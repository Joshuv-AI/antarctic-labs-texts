Causal Inference for Qualitative Outcomes Thanks: The R package for implementing the methodologies developed in this paper is available on GitHub at https://github.com/riccardo-df/causalQual .
Riccardo Di Francesco, Giovanni Mellace

Abstract
Causal inference methods such as instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, and difference-in-differences are widely used to estimate treatment effects. However, their application to qualitative outcomes poses fundamental challenges, as standard causal estimands are ill-defined in this context. This paper highlights these issues and introduces an alternative framework that focuses on well-defined and interpretable estimands that quantify how treatment affects the probability distribution over outcome categories. We establish that standard identification assumptions are sufficient for identification and propose simple, intuitive estimation strategies that remain fully compatible with conventional econometric methods. To facilitate implementation, we provide an open-source R package, causalQual , which is publicly available on GitHub. Keywords: Probability shift, multinomial outcomes, ordered non-numeric outcomes. JEL Codes: C21, C26, C31, C35.

Introduction

Causal inference has become a cornerstone of applied econometrics, with methods such as
instrumental variables
,
difference-in-differences
, and
regression discontinuity
widely employed to identify and estimate the effects of policies or interventions
(
Imbens 2022
;
Angrist 2022
)
. The widespread adoption of these methodologies has been central to the so-called
credibility revolution
, which emphasizes credible and transparent identification strategies to improve the reliability of empirical findings
(
Angrist and Pischke 2010
;
Lechner 2023
)
.

These methodologies are primarily developed for numerical outcomes, whether continuous or discrete. Yet, many empirical applications across disciplines involve qualitative outcomes, which take the form of ordered or unordered categories. In political science, voter ideology is often recorded on an ordinal scale ranging from “very liberal” to “very conservative”
(
Mason 2015
)
, and attitudes toward policy issues are commonly measured using Likert scales
(
Kuziemko et al. 2015
)
. In health, researchers analyze life satisfaction
(
Frey and Stutzer 2002
)
, subjective well-being
(
Reisinger 2022
)
, and self-reported health status
(
Peracchi and Rossetti 2012
;
Peracchi and Rossetti 2013
)
– all of which lack a natural cardinal interpretation. In consumer research, perceived product quality and satisfaction are often captured through consumer ratings and review scores
(
Mayzlin et al. 2014
)
, which are inherently ordinal. In such cases, widely used causal estimands, such as the
average treatment effect
and its conditional versions, are ill-defined, as arithmetic operations like averaging or computing differences between categories lack meaningful quantitative interpretation.

This article highlights the fundamental challenges that arise when conventional causal inference methods are applied to qualitative outcomes and introduces a principled approach to addressing these issues. Standard causal methodologies, when employed without fully accounting for the qualitative nature of the outcome, suffer from inherent limitations, as conventional estimands are meaningless in this context. A common approach among applied researchers is to dichotomize the outcome based on an arbitrary threshold to enable the use of methods designed for binary outcomes. However, results may be highly sensitive to the choice of threshold, and the validity of identifying assumptions for one dichotomization does not necessarily imply identification under an alternative thresholding scheme
(
Yamauchi 2020
)
. This paper advocates for a fundamental shift toward alternative estimands that are not only meaningful and interpretable but also identifiable within standard research designs.

Specifically, we demonstrate that the naive application of widely used research designs – including instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, and regression discontinuity – to qualitative outcomes leads to the identification of quantities whose magnitude and sign depend on arbitrary coding choices, rendering them devoid of economic meaning. To address this issue, we shift the focus to well-defined and interpretable estimands that characterize how treatment affects the probability distribution over outcome categories. This approach allows us to quantify how treatment alters the likelihood of observing different responses, offering an economically meaningful alternative to standard causal parameters.

We establish that the assumptions underpinning conventional identification strategies – or slight modifications thereof – are sufficient to identify the new estimands, ensuring their compatibility with standard research designs. For estimation, we rely on conventional methods while explicitly modeling category probabilities rather than the outcome itself. This enables applied researchers to continue using familiar econometric tools while targeting causal parameters that are both meaningful and interpretable. To facilitate implementation, we provide the open-source R package
causalQual
, which offers a straightforward and user-friendly approach to estimation.

2
Causal framework

We are interested in the causal effect of a binary treatment
D_{i}\in\{0,1\}
on a qualitative outcome
Y_{i}\in\{1,2,\dots,M\}
. We denote by
\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}=(X_{i1},\dots,X_{ik})^{\top}\in\mathcal{X}
the
k\times 1
vector of pre-treatment covariates and define
p_{m}(d,\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}):=\EX[\mathbb{1}(Y_{i}=m)|D_{i}=d,\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}]=\PX(Y_{i}=m|D_{i}=d,\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})
as the conditional class probability and
\pi(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}):=\EX[D_{i}|\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}]=\PX(D_{i}=1|\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})
as the conditional treatment probability or propensity score.
4
4

4

We use boldface characters to distinguish vectors from scalars. Additionally, we represent random variables by uppercase letters and their realizations by lowercase letters.

Although the
M
outcome categories may exhibit a natural ordering – where higher values correspond to stronger responses – they are not measured on a cardinal scale. Therefore, arithmetic operations such as averages of
Y_{i}
or differences between its categories lack meaningful quantitative interpretation.

We adopt the potential outcomes framework
(
Neyman 1923
;
Rubin 1974
)
and posit the existence of two potential outcomes
Y_{i}(1)
and
Y_{i}(0)
, representing the outcome that unit
i
would experience under each treatment level. The realized outcome for unit
i
then follows the standard observational rule:
5
5

5

The definition of potential outcomes and the observational rule linking them to observed outcomes implicitly impose SUTVA
(
Laffers and Mellace 2020
, see, e.g.,)
.

Y_{i}=D_{i}Y_{i}(1)+(1-D_{i})Y_{i}(0).

(1)

If
Y_{i}
were a cardinal numeric variable, the individual treatment effect could be naturally defined as the difference in potential outcomes,
Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)
. In that case, standard causal estimands such as the Average Treatment Effect (ATE)
\tau:=\EX[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)]
and the Average Treatment Effect on the Treated (ATT)
\tau_{T}:=\EX[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)|D_{i}=1]
would be well-defined and directly identifiable under additional assumptions.

However, because
Y_{i}
is a qualitative variable, such differences are not meaningful. Instead, we define causal effects in terms of changes in the probability distribution over outcome categories, allowing us to quantify how the treatment shifts the likelihood of observing different responses.

Specifically, we define the Probability of Shift (PS) as the change in the probability of belonging to category
m
due to treatment:

\begin{split}\delta_{m}:=\PX(Y_{i}(1)=m)-\PX(Y_{i}(0)=m).\end{split}

(2)

For instance,
\delta_{m}>0
indicates that the treatment increases the likelihood of observing outcome category
m
, while
\delta_{m}<0
implies a decrease in the probability mass assigned to
m
caused by the treatment. By construction,
\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{m}=0
, reflecting the intuitive trade-off that an increase in the probability of some outcome categories must be offset by a decrease in others.

{remarkk}

A naive approach is to treat
Y_{i}
as numeric, in which case the ATE would write as

\tau=\sum_{m=1}^{M}m\delta_{m}.

(3)

This expression shows that the average treatment effect corresponds to a weighted sum of the probability shifts
\delta_{m}
, where the weights are simply the category labels
m
. However, this approach is fundamentally flawed for at least two reasons.

First, the weights
m
do not carry meaningful information about the treatment effect; rather, they are an artifact of arbitrary labeling. Since these labels do not represent numerical distances but merely indicate distinct classes, they can be reindexed without altering the underlying outcome – yet doing so would completely change the magnitude of the ATE. This lack of invariance makes the ATE highly sensitive to arbitrary coding choices.

Second, even if one accepts this flawed weighting scheme, the sign of
\tau
behaves erratically and lacks meaningful interpretation. Since
\sum_{m=1}^{M}\delta_{m}=0
, any treatment with a nonzero effect necessarily implies that
\delta_{m}>0
for some categories and
\delta_{m}<0
for others. Consequently, the sign of
\tau
is determined by the relative magnitudes of these probability shifts, weighted in an arbitrary manner. Small shifts in higher-indexed categories may dominate larger shifts in lower-indexed ones, leading to an unreliable and misleading sign. Moreover, because category labels are arbitrary, reindexing them can completely alter both the magnitude and sign of
\tau
, further underscoring its lack of economic significance.

We further define two conditional versions of PS to provide a more granular characterization of treatment effects: the Probability of Shift on the Treated (PST)

\delta_{m,T}:=\PX(Y_{i}(1)=m|D_{i}=1)-\PX(Y_{i}(0)=m|D_{i}=1),

(4)

which measures the probability shift among the treated population, and the Conditional Probability of Shift (CPS)

\delta_{m}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}):=\PX(Y_{i}(1)=m|\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})-\PX(Y_{i}(0)=m|\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}),

(5)

which measures the probability shift for units with covariate values
x
.

3
Selection-on-observables

Selection-on-observables research designs are used to identify causal effects in observational settings where units are observed at a specific point in time, some receive treatment, and data on both pre-treatment characteristics and post-treatment outcomes are available. This approach is based on the following standard assumptions
(
Imbens and Rubin 2015
, see, e.g.,)
:

Assumption 1
.

(Unconfoundedness):
\{Y_{i}(1),Y_{i}(0)\}\perp\!\!\!\perp D_{i}|\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}
.

Assumption 2
.

(Common support):
0<\pi(\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i})<1
.

Assumption
1
states that, after conditioning on
\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}
, treatment assignment is as good as random, meaning that
\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}
fully accounts for selection into treatment. Assumption
2
ensures that for every value of
\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}
, there are both treated and untreated units, preventing situations where no valid counterfactual exists.

Together, these assumptions allow for the identification of
\tau
and its conditional versions by ensuring meaningful comparisons between treated and control units with similar observed characteristics. However, the qualitative nature of
Y_{i}
renders
\tau
ill-defined. Therefore, we shift our focus to the identification of PS and its conditional counterparts.

Specifically, observe that

\begin{split}p_{m}(1,\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})&-p_{m}(0,\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})=\\
&=\PX(Y_{i}(1)=m|D_{i}=1,\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})-\PX(Y_{i}(0)=m|D_{i}=0,\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\,\,\,\,\,\,\,(\textit{by equation \eqref{equation_observational_rule}})\\
&=\PX(Y_{i}(1)=m|\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})-\PX(Y_{i}(0)=m|\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}=\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}})\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,(\textit{by Assumption \ref{assumption_unconfoundedness}})\\
&=\delta_{m}(\mbox{\boldmath{$x$}}),\end{split}

Assumption
2
ensures that all conditional probabilities are well-defined at
x
, establishing that the standard selection-on-observables assumptions are sufficient to identify the CPS at any given
x
. Moreover, since
\delta_{m}=\EX[\delta_{m}(\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i})]
and
\delta_{m,T}=\EX[\delta_{m}(\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i})|D_{i}=1]
, the PS and PST are also identified. Thus, even though standard causal parameters are ill-defined for qualitative outcomes, selection-on-observables remains a valid identification strategy. The key insight is to shift the focus toward meaningful and interpretable estimands, namely the PS and its conditional versions.

For estimation, note that

\begin{split}\delta_{m}=\EX[\mathbb{1}(Y_{i}(1)=m)-\mathbb{1}(Y_{i}(0)=m)].\end{split}

(6)

This suggests that we can construct an estimate
\hat{\delta}_{m}
by applying standard estimation strategies to the binary variable
\mathbb{1}(Y_{i}=m)
and estimating the ATE on that variable.
6
6

6

Applying such estimation strategies to the subsample of treated units would yield an estimate
\hat{\delta}_{m,T}
of
\delta_{m,T}
.

4
Instrumental variables

In some applications, the observed covariates
\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i}
may not fully account for selection into treatment, making a selection-on-observables design unsuitable. Instrumental variables (IV) designs provide a popular identification strategy to address this issue. The key idea behind IV is to exploit a variable – an instrument – that is as good as randomly assigned, influences treatment assignment, but has no direct effect on the outcome except through treatment. This exogenous source of variation enables the identification of causal effects by isolating changes in treatment status that are not driven by unobserved factors.

Although our results can be readily extended to more complex settings, we focus on the simple case of a single binary instrument
Z_{i}\in\{0,1\}
for ease of illustration. We build upon the framework introduced in Section
2
by defining the potential treatment statuses
D_{i}(1)
and
D_{i}(0)
, which represent the treatment that unit
i
would receive under each level of the instrument. Potential outcomes are now indexed by both treatment status
d
and instrument assignment
z
, denoted by
Y_{i}(d,z)
.

Within this framework, units can be classified into four distinct compliance types based on their potential treatment status as a function of the instrument
(
Angrist et al. 1996
)
:
always-takers
(
at
) receive treatment regardless of assignment (
D_{i}(1)=D_{i}(0)=1
),
never-takers
(
nt
) never receive treatment regardless of assignment (
D_{i}(1)=D_{i}(0)=0
),
compliers
(
co
) take the treatment only if assigned (
D_{i}(1)=1,D_{i}(0)=0
), and
defiers
(
de
) take the treatment only when not assigned (
D_{i}(1)=0,D_{i}(0)=1
). To shorten notation, we denote an unit’s compliance type by
T_{i}\in\{at,nt,co,de\}
.

A standard approach in IV settings is to target identification and estimation of the Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE)
\tau_{L}:=\EX[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)|T_{i}=co]
, which represents the average treatment effect for compliers. Identification relies on the following standard assumptions
(
Imbens and Angrist 1994
;
Angrist et al. 1996
)
:

Assumption 3
.

(Exogeneity):
\{Y_{i}(D_{i},1),Y_{i}(D_{i},0),D_{i}(1),D_{i}(0)\}\perp\!\!\!\perp Z_{i}
.

Assumption 4
.

(Exclusion restriction):
Y_{i}(d,1)=Y_{i}(d,0)=Y_{i}(d),\,d=0,1
.

Assumption 5
.

(Monotonicity):
\PX(D_{i}(1)\geq D_{i}(0))=1
.

Assumption 6
.

(Relevance):
\PX(T_{i}=co)>0
.

Assumption
3
mandates that the instrument is as good as randomly assigned. Assumption
4
requires that the instrument affects the outcome only through its influence on treatment, ruling out any direct effect. Assumption
5
imposes that the instrument can only increase the likelihood of treatment, ruling out defiers.
7
7

7

This assumption is made without loss of generality; one could alternatively assume that the instrument can only decrease the probability of treatment.
Finally, Assumption
6
states that the instrument has a nonzero effect on the treatment, thereby generating exogenous variation in the latter.

However, the qualitative nature of
Y_{i}
implies that
\tau_{L}
is ill-defined. We thus shift our focus toward the identification of the Local Probability Shift (LPS):

\delta_{m,L}:=\PX(Y_{i}(1)=m|T_{i}=co)-\PX(Y_{i}(0)=m|T_{i}=co).

(7)

Specifically, Appendix
A
shows that, under Assumptions
3
–
6
,

\delta_{m,L}=\frac{\PX(Y_{i}=m|Z_{i}=1)-\PX(Y_{i}=m|Z_{i}=0)}{\PX(D_{i}=1|Z_{i}=1)-\PX(D_{i}=1|Z_{i}=0)}.

(8)

This establishes that the standard IV assumptions are sufficient to identify a well-defined and interpretable causal parameter for qualitative outcomes – the LPS.

For estimation, note that

\delta_{m,L}=\frac{\EX[\mathbb{1}(Y_{i}=m)|Z_{i}=1]-\EX[\mathbb{1}(Y_{i}=m)|Z_{i}=0]}{\PX(D_{i}=1|Z_{i}=1)-\PX(D_{i}=1|Z_{i}=0)}.

(9)

This suggests that we can construct an estimate
\hat{\delta}_{m,L}
by applying standard IV estimation strategies to the binary variable
\mathbb{1}(Y_{i}=m)
and estimating the LATE on that variable.

5
Regression discontinuity

Regression Discontinuity (RD) designs are employed to identify causal effects when treatment assignment is determined by whether a continuous variable crosses a known threshold or cutoff. The key assumption is that units just above and just below the cutoff are comparable, meaning that any discontinuity in outcomes at the threshold can be attributed to the treatment rather than to pre-existing differences
(
Lee and Lemieux 2010
, see, e.g.,)
.

Although our results can be readily extended to more complex settings, we focus on the standard
sharp
RD design for ease of illustration. In this setup, we consider a single observed covariate
X_{i}
, referred to as the “running variable”. By construction,
X_{i}
fully determines treatment assignment. Specifically, units receive treatment if and only if their running variable exceeds a predetermined threshold
c
. Thus,
D_{i}=\mathbb{1}(X_{i}\geq c)
.

This setup leads to two key observations that draw direct analogies to selection-on-observables designs. First, Assumption
1
holds trivially since conditioning on
X_{i}
fully determines
D_{i}
. Second, Assumption
2
is necessarily violated, as
\pi(x)
is either zero or one for all
x

(
Imbens and Lemieux 2008
, see, e.g.,)
.

A standard approach in RD settings is to replace Assumption
2
with the following continuity assumption for identification of the Average Treatment Effect at the Cutoff (ATC)
\tau_{C}:=\EX[Y_{i}(1)-Y_{i}(0)|X_{i}=c]
.

Assumption 7a
.

(Continuity of mean outcomes):
\EX[Y_{i}(d)|X_{i}=x]
is continuous in
x
for
d=0,1
.

Assumption
7a
ensures that expected potential outcomes evolve smoothly in
x
. This allows for a fair comparison between treated and control units arbitrarily close to
c
, thereby enabling the identification of
\tau_{C}
.

However, in the presence of qualitative outcomes,
\tau_{C}
is ill-defined. We thus shift our focus to the identification of a meaningful parameter – the Probability of Shift at the Cutoff (PSC):

\delta_{m,C}:=\PX(Y_{i}(1)=m|X_{i}=c)-\PX(Y_{i}(0)=m|X_{i}=c).

(10)

Assumption
7a
does not suffice to identify the PSC. To address this, we introduce an alternative continuity assumption that enables its identification:

Assumption 7b
.

(Continuity of mass functions):
\PX(Y_{i}(d)=m|X_{i}=x)
is continuous in
x
for
d=0,1
.

Assumption
7b
requires that the conditional probability mass functions of potential outcomes evolve smoothly with
x
. This ensures that class probabilities – rather than the expected outcomes – remain comparable in a neighborhood of
c
, enabling the identification of
\delta_{m,C}
through a fair comparison of class probabilities on either side of the threshold.

Under Assumptions
1
and
7b
, we obtain

\begin{split}\lim_{x\downarrow c}&\PX(Y_{i}=m|D_{i}=1,X_{i}=x)=\\
&=\lim_{x\downarrow c}\PX(Y_{i}(1)=m|D_{i}=1,X_{i}=x)\,\,\,\,\,\,\,(\textit{by equation \eqref{equation_observational_rule}})\\
&=\lim_{x\downarrow c}\PX(Y_{i}(1)=m|X_{i}=x)\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,(\textit{by Assumption \ref{assumption_unconfoundedness}})\\
&=\PX(Y_{i}(1)=m|X_{i}=c),\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,\,(\textit{by Assumption \ref{assumption_continuity_probabilities}})\end{split}

and similarly

\lim_{x\uparrow c}\PX(Y_{i}=m|D_{i}=0,X_{i}=x)=\PX(Y_{i}(0)=m|X_{i}=c).

Thus,

\lim_{x\downarrow c}\PX(Y_{i}=m|D_{i}=1,X_{i}=x)-\lim_{x\uparrow c}\PX(Y_{i}=m|D_{i}=0,X_{i}=x)=\delta_{m,C}.

(11)

This establishes that Assumptions
1
and
7b
are sufficient to identify the PSC.

For estimation, note that

\delta_{m,C}=\lim_{x\downarrow c}\EX[\mathbb{1}(Y_{i}=m)|D_{i}=1,X_{i}=x]-\lim_{x\uparrow c}\EX[\mathbb{1}(Y_{i}=m)|D_{i}=0,X_{i}=x].

(12)

This suggests that we can construct an estimate
\hat{\delta}_{m,C}
by applying standard RD estimation strategies to the binary variable
\mathbb{1}(Y_{i}=m)
and estimating the ATC on that variable.

6
Difference-in-differences

Difference-in-Differences (DiD) designs are employed to identify causal effects when units are observed over time and treatment is introduced only from a certain point onward for some units. The key assumption is that, in the absence of treatment, the change in outcomes for treated units would have mirrored the change observed in the control group
(
Roth et al. 2023
;
de Chaisemartin and D’Haultfœuille 2023
, see, e.g.,)
.

Although our results can be readily extended to more complex settings, we focus on the standard two-period DiD framework for ease of illustration. In this setup, we observe units in two time periods
s\in\{t,t-1\}
. The potential outcomes framework introduced in Section
2
extends naturally to this setting by incorporating potential outcomes indexed by both treatment status
d
and time
s
, denoted as
Y_{is}(d)
. By construction, no units receive treatment in the pre-treatment period
t-1
, while in the post-treatment period
t
a subset of units is treated (
D_{i}=1
). The observed outcomes thus follow the assignment rule:
8
8

8

This formulation assumes no-anticipation, meaning that treatment effects do not materialize before treatment is actually received.

Y_{it-1}=Y_{it-1}(0),\quad Y_{it}=D_{i}Y_{it}(1)+(1-D_{i})Y_{it}(0).

(13)

In this context, the PST is defined as

\delta_{m,T}:=\PX(Y_{it}(1)=m|D_{i}=1)-\PX(Y_{it}(0)=m|D_{i}=1).

(14)

A standard approach in DiD settings is to impose the following parallel trends assumption for identification of the ATT, now defined as
\tau_{T}:=\EX[Y_{it}(1)-Y_{it}(0)|D_{i}=1]
.

Assumption 8a
.

(Parallel trends on mean outcomes):

\EX[Y_{it}(0)-Y_{it-1}(0)|D_{i}=1]=\EX[Y_{it}(0)-Y_{it-1}(0)|D_{i}=0]
.

Assumption
8a
states that, in the absence of treatment, the change in outcomes for the treated group would have mirrored that of the control group, effectively ensuring that any systematic differences between the groups remain constant over time. This allows for a fair comparison of how outcomes evolve across groups, thereby enabling the identification of
\tau_{T}
.

However, the qualitative nature of
Y_{i}
renders
\tau_{T}
meaningless, and Assumption
8a
does not enable the identification of the PST. To address this, we introduce an alternative parallel trends assumption that enables its identification.

Assumption 8b
.

(Parallel trends on mass functions):

\PX(Y_{it}(0)=m|D_{i}=1)-\PX(Y_{it-1}(0)=m|D_{i}=1)=\PX(Y_{it}(0)=m|D_{i}=0)-\PX(Y_{it-1}(0)=m|D_{i}=0)
.

Assumption
8b
requires that the probability time shift of
Y_{is}(0)
for class
m
follows a similar evolution over time in both the treated and control groups. Essentially, this imposes parallel trends on the probability mass functions of
Y_{is}(0)
, rather than on its mean, ensuring that categorical shifts – rather than differences – remain comparable across groups.

Under Assumption
8b
, we obtain

\begin{split}\PX(Y_{it}(0)=m|D_{i}=1)&=\PX(Y_{it-1}(0)=m|D_{i}=1)-\\
&\PX(Y_{it}(0)=m|D_{i}=0)-\PX(Y_{it-1}(0)=m|D_{i}=0).\end{split}

(15)

Replacing this in (
14
) and using (
13
) to replace potential outcomes with observable outcomes, we obtain

\begin{split}\delta_{m,T}=\PX(Y_{it}=m|D_{i}=1)&-\PX(Y_{it-1}=m|D_{i}=1)-\\
[\PX(Y_{it}=m|D_{i}=0)&-\PX(Y_{it-1}=m|D_{i}=0)].\end{split}

(16)

This establishes that Assumption
8b
is sufficient to identify the PST.

For estimation, note that

\delta_{m,T}=\EX[\mathbb{1}(Y_{it}=m)-\mathbb{1}(Y_{it-1}=m)|D_{i}=1]-\EX[\mathbb{1}(Y_{it}=m)-\mathbb{1}(Y_{it-1}=m)|D_{i}=0].

(17)

This suggests that we can construct an estimate
\hat{\delta}_{m,T}
by applying standard DiD estimation strategies to the binary variable
\mathbb{1}(Y_{is}=m)
and estimating the ATT on that variable.

Simulation

7.1
Selection-on-observables

Potential outcomes are modeled using two distinct approaches, depending on whether the outcome is multinomial or ordered. For multinomial outcomes, we construct the probability mass functions by applying a softmax transformation to linear predictors. Specifically, for each class
m

\{1,2,3\}
according to these mass functions. For ordered outcomes, we first generate underlying latent potential outcomes
Y_{i}^{*}(d)
and then obtain the observable potential outcomes
Y_{i}(d)
by discretizing
Y_{i}^{*}(d)
using fixed thresholds.

Conclusion

Causal inference methods have become central to empirical research in economics and the social sciences, enabling researchers to estimate the effects of policies and interventions with increasing credibility. However, the applicability of these methodologies has largely been confined to contexts where outcomes are numerical. This paper highlights a fundamental yet often overlooked issue: when applied to qualitative outcomes – whether multinomial or ordered – standard causal inference methods yield results that are entirely meaningless.

We demonstrate that the naive application of widely used identification strategies – including instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, and difference-in-differences – to qualitative outcomes leads to the estimation of quantities whose magnitude and sign depend on arbitrary coding choices. This renders conventional causal estimands devoid of economic meaning and calls into question their practical relevance. To address this issue, we propose an alternative framework centered on well-defined and interpretable estimands that characterize how treatment affects the probability distribution over outcome categories, thereby providing economically meaningful alternatives to conventional causal parameters.

We show that the assumptions underpinning standard identification strategies – or slight modifications thereof – are sufficient to identify the new estimands, ensuring their compatibility with conventional research designs. For estimation, we advocate for leveraging standard econometric tools while modeling category probabilities rather than outcome levels, thereby allowing researchers to continue using familiar methodologies while targeting causal parameters that are both interpretable and well-defined.

While we establish identification and estimation within standard cases for ease of illustration, our framework naturally extends to more complex settings, including instrumental variables with multiple instruments
(
van ’t Hoff et al. 2023
;
Mogstad et al. 2018
;
Mogstad and Torgovitsky 2018
;
Goff 2024
, see, e.g.,)
, difference-in-differences with heterogeneous effects and staggered treatment adoption
(
Callaway and Sant’Anna 2021
;
Sun and Abraham 2021
;
Borusyak et al. 2024
;
Goodman-Bacon 2021
, see, e.g.,)
, and fuzzy regression discontinuity designs
(
Cattaneo and Titiunik 2022
, see, e.g.,)
. We plan to continuously update our R package,
causalQual
, to incorporate these extensions as they develop.

Appendix B
Additional simulation details

For multinomial outcomes, we construct conditional probability mass functions by first computing linear predictors based on the covariates:

\eta_{mi}(d)=\beta_{m1}^{d}X_{i1}+\beta_{m2}^{d}X_{i2}+\beta_{m3}^{d}X_{i3},\quad d=0,1,

and then transforming these predictors into valid probability distributions using the softmax function:

\PX(Y_{i}(d)=m|\mbox{\boldmath{$X$}}_{i})=\frac{\exp(\eta_{mi}(d))}{\sum_{m^{\prime}}\exp(\eta_{m^{\prime}i}(d))},\quad d=0,1.

The coefficients used in the linear predictor models are specified as:

\beta_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&0.3&-0.2\\
-0.2&0.4&0.1\\
0.1&-0.3&0.5\end{bmatrix},\quad\beta_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}0.7&0.7&-0.2\\
-0.2&0.4&0.1\\
-0.2&-0.5&0.1\end{bmatrix}.

For ordered outcomes, we first generate underlying latent potential outcomes

Y_{i}^{*}(d)=\tau d+\sum_{j}X_{ij}+\mathcal{N}(0,1),\quad d=0,1,

and then obtain the ordered potential outcomes by discretizing
Y_{i}^{*}(d)
using fixed thresholds:

Y_{i}(d)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}m\mathbb{1}(\zeta_{m-1}<Y_{i}^{*}(d)\leq\zeta_{m}),\quad d=0,1,

with
\zeta_{1}=2
,
\zeta_{2}=3
, and
\tau=2
.
