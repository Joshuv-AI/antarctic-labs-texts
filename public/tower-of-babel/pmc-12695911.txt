A probabilistic GIS-based framework for urban flood risk assessment in Chengdu metro network
Authors: Qiuping Li, Ting Ni, Lei Li, Hao Wen, Jiuping Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Urban metro network is highly vulnerable to flooding, yet most existing risk assessments rely on deterministic weighting methods that inadequately address uncertainty. To address this limitation, this study develops a probabilistic GIS-based flood risk assessment framework that integrates the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) with its Monte Carlo extension (MC-AHP). Unlike conventional studies, the framework (i) explicitly quantifies uncertainty in expert judgments through β-PERT simulations, (ii) incorporates a comprehensive indicator system covering hazard, exposure, and vulnerability—including passenger flow and under-construction lines often overlooked in prior work, and (iii) provides spatially explicit risk mapping for both operational and planned infrastructure. Application to the Chengdu metro network reveals a concentric flood risk distribution, with the central urban core exhibiting the highest vulnerability despite moderate hazard levels. Compared to AHP, MC-AHP identifies a greater proportion of high-risk areas and provides probability distributions of indicator weights, thereby reducing the influence of subjective bias. Validation against historical flood events confirms the framework’s predictive reliability. By explicitly incorporating uncertainty and extending analysis to planned infrastructure, this study advances the methodological rigor of urban flood risk assessment and provides generalizable insights for strengthening metro network resilience worldwide in the face of accelerating climate change and rapid urbanization.

Introduction

In recent decades, the frequency and intensity of urban flooding have increased significantly due to rapid urbanization and global climate change1, making floods one of the most economically destructive natural hazards worldwide2. Densely populated cities, especially those with aging infrastructure and inadequate drainage systems, are particularly vulnerable3. To address growing traffic demand and relieve surface congestion, metro network has become essential components of sustainable urban transportation4,5. These systems, however, are particularly susceptible to flood-related disruptions because of their underground nature6,7. Flooding of metro stations or tunnels can paralyze urban transit, endanger human lives, and cause substantial economic losses8. A notable example is the 2021 Zhengzhou metro flood disaster, where excessive rainfall inundated tunnels, causing 14 deaths and trapping hundreds of passengers9. This event underscores the vulnerability of rapidly expanding underground rail networks to extreme rainfall. Over the past decade, China has undergone one of the fastest metros build-outs worldwide5. By the end of 2024, nearly 50 cities operated metro network totalling more than 10,000 km. This rapid national expansion has greatly increased the spatial footprint and societal dependence on underground rail infrastructure, thereby raising both the likelihood that metro assets intersect flood-prone areas and the potential human/economic consequences of station or tunnel inundation. Consequently, flood risk assessment of metro network is essential for prioritizing retrofitting and early-warning measures. However, resilience investments have not kept pace with system expansion, particularly in regions prone to extreme precipitation10,11. Against this backdrop, Chengdu, a sprawling megacity in the Sichuan Basin with intricate watercourses, faces escalating flood risk as heavy rainfall intensifies alongside continued urban growth.

To strengthen urban resilience and safeguard metro network, comprehensive flood risk evaluation methods are crucial12. These should account for hazard intensity, exposure levels, and system vulnerability while providing detailed spatial data to inform planning and crisis management13–16. Existing flood risk assessment methods fall into four broad categories: historical disaster statistics (HDMS), scenario-based simulations (SSA), remote sensing and GIS-based assessments, and multi-criteria decision analysis (MCDA)11,17–19. Integrating MCDA into GIS has advanced spatial decision support by combining geospatial analysis with both empirical data (e.g., rainfall, land use, slope) and expert judgment, enabling more comprehensive and value-informed assessments12,20,21.

Among MCDA techniques, the Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP) is widely applied due to its structured approach to factor weighting and its capacity to incorporate expert judgment22,23. However, AHP is constrained by its deterministic framework and susceptibility to subjective bias, reducing its reliability in decision-making under uncertainty and imperfect information24,25. To address this issue, researchers have introduced probabilistic extensions to AHP. One such approach is the Monte Carlo Analytic Hierarchy Process (MC-AHP), which uses probabilistic distributions, typically β-PERT to simulate variability in expert judgments and generate more robust weight estimates26,27. Compared to traditional AHP, MC-AHP provides a deeper understanding of uncertainty and reduces the influence of extreme or inconsistent evaluations28.

Despite the growing attention to flood risks in urban infrastructure, existing research still presents several gaps. First, few studies have systematically applied advanced multi-criteria methods such as the Monte Carlo Analytic Hierarchy Process (MC-AHP) to metro network risk assessment. Second, many assessments lack spatial precision or fail to incorporate comprehensive vulnerability indicators such as passenger flow, population density, and road proximity. Moreover, existing studies often overlook the future risks faced by infrastructure under construction. To address these limitations, this study develops this study develops a probabilistic GIS-based flood risk assessment framework that integrates AHP with its Monte Carlo extension (MC-AHP). Specifically, a multi-level indicator system is established based on the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change29 risk framework, incorporating 16 indicators across the three dimensions of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. The framework integrates traditional AHP with MC-AHP to evaluate and compare the spatial distribution of flood risks across metro lines and stations.

This study advances existing research in three key aspects, enhancing both the methodological value and practical relevance of metro flood risk assessment. First, it introduces MC-AHP to explicitly quantify uncertainty in expert judgments, a critical limitation often overlooked in conventional AHP-based studies. Second, by integrating the triad of disaster formation mechanics—hazard-causing factors, hazard-pregnant environment, and hazard-bearing carriers—the framework enables a more comprehensive evaluation. Third, by simultaneously assessing both operational and under-construction metro lines and stations, this study extends the scope of prior research and provides forward-looking insights for infrastructure planning and resilience building.

The objectives of this study are fourfold: (1) to construct a spatially explicit and probabilistic flood risk assessment model for metro network using GIS, AHP, and MC-AHP; (2) to identify high-risk metro lines and stations in Chengdu and examine their spatial distribution; (3) to compare the performance of deterministic AHP and probabilistic MC-AHP in capturing uncertainty in urban flood risk evaluation; and (4) to provide practical recommendations for infrastructure planning, early-warning systems, and resilience enhancement.

By combining methodological innovation with empirical validation in Chengdu, this study not only addresses the limitations of conventional flood risk assessments but also offers generalizable insights for strengthening metro network resilience in rapidly urbanizing and climate-stressed cities worldwide.

Index system

According to the formation mechanism of flood disasters, a regional flood disaster system consists of three components: disaster-causing factors, disaster-pregnant environment, and disaster-bearing carriers30,31. Based on this framework, a metro flood risk assessment index system is developed by incorporating the dimensions of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability. The index system includes:

(1) Hazard index. Hazard refers to the potential occurrence of a natural or human-induced physical event, trend, or impact that may cause loss of life, damage to property, ecosystems, or disruption of services29,32. In this study, hazard denotes the probability, frequency and intensity of flood-producing processes (e.g., heavy rainfall, river overflow) that can cause inundation. Hazard of disaster-causing factors index include annual average rainfall (H11), annual number of heavy rain days (H12), and maximum daily rainfall (H13). And Hazard of disaster-pregnant environment index include elevation (H21), slope (H22), river density (H23), and river proximity (H24). River density (H23) refers to the length of rivers per unit area. River proximity (H24) refers to the spatial relationship between rivers and surrounding environments.

(2) Exposure index. Exposure refers to the presence and distribution of people, assets, and ecosystems in areas that may be adversely affected by natural hazards, reflecting the extent to which these receptors are subject to the frequency, intensity, and spatiotemporal characteristics of hazardous events29,33. In this study, the exposure of disaster-bearing carriers index includes the number of metro exits (E1), metro density (E2), metro proximity (E3), and the number of transfer stations (E4).

(3) Vulnerability index. Vulnerability refers to the inherent characteristics of receptors that determine their susceptibility to harm and their capacity to cope with, resist, and recover from the impacts of natural hazards29,34. The vulnerability of disaster-bearing carriers index includes five indicators: passenger flow intensity (V1), station intensity (V2), population density (V3), road network density (V4), and road network proximity (V5).

Monte Carlo analytic hierarchy process (MC-AHP)

Compared to the AHP method, the application of MC-AHP demonstrates two major advantages. First, MC-AHP integrates the uncertainty inherent in expert judgments during the decision-making process36. To incorporate uncertainty in expert judgment, MC-AHP treats pairwise comparisons as β-PERT distributions, using minimum, most likely, and maximum values. Second, MC-AHP provides more detailed output, helping decision-makers better understand the uncertainty behind rankings28. Case study: chengdu

Overview of the study area

Chengdu is located in the western part of the Sichuan Basin, situated between longitudes 102°54′ to 104°53′ and latitudes 30°05′ to 31°26′39, the geographical location is shown in Fig. 2. The topography is higher in the northwest and lower in the south. The city mainly consists of plains, located in the middle reaches of the Min River, where rivers are crisscrossed, forming a multi-river plain characteristic. As of 2023, Chengdu governs 12 urban districts, 3 counties, and 5 county-level cities, covering a total area of 14,335 square kilometers, with a permanent population of 21.403 million. Since the opening of the Chengdu Metro in 2010, it has become an integral part of the city’s transportation system. By December 2024, the Chengdu Metro operated 14 lines, with a total operating length of 594.14 km (excluding trams and suburban railways). There are 5 metro lines under construction, with a total length of 269.9 km. In 2024, the average daily ridership of the Chengdu Metro reached 6.0025 million trips. These figures reflecting a large spatial footprint and substantial human exposure. Figure 3 shows the metro network of the study area.

Hazard analysis

Based on publicly available data, this study collected annual average rainfall data from 2001 to 2023, as well as the number of annual heavy rainfall days and the maximum daily rainfall from 2001 to 2022. Validation of the assessment results

To verify the practical effectiveness and accuracy of the flood risk assessment model, a spatial overlay analysis was conducted using historical flood event records. Information on ten metro stations that experienced waterlogging during past extreme rainfall events (e.g., the rainstorms on July 2, 2018; August 16, 2020; and July 3, 2025) was collected from official reports and reliable media sources. These historically affected stations served as reference points for validation.

Using the QGIS platform, the spatial locations of these ten stations were overlaid onto the flood risk classification map of the metro network (Fig. 12(b)), which was generated based on the Monte Carlo Analytic Hierarchy Process (MC-AHP). The corresponding risk level for each historical flood point was then extracted and statistically analyzed.

The results show that most of the historically affected metro stations fall within the medium- to very high-risk zones identified in this study, with five stations located in the very high-risk zone and four stations located in the high-risk zone. For example, Shufang Station, which experienced waterlogging during the August 16, 2020 event, lies in a high-risk area on the risk map. Similarly, Fuhuayuan Station, which accumulated water during the July 3, 2025 rainstorm, is situated in a very high-risk area. Overall, the historical flood points align closely with the medium- and higher-risk areas delineated in this assessment, indicating strong consistency between observed disaster impacts and the model’s predictions. This confirms that the proposed assessment framework can effectively and accurately identify sections of the metro network that are genuinely susceptible to flooding.

Discussion

Interpretation of results and comparison with previous studies

This assessment reveals a concentric spatial distribution of flood risk, radiating from the high-risk urban core toward the lower-risk suburbs. This pattern is consistent with observations in other megacities with extensive metro network, such as Beijing15 and Shanghai7, where urban cores are also identified as flood risk hotspots due to concentrated infrastructure and population. However, this study deepens that understanding by dissecting the contributing factors: in Chengdu’s city center, the extreme risk is predominantly driven by high vulnerability (e.g., immense passenger flow, high station density), even though the hazard level is relatively moderate. This contrasts with areas such as Dujiangyan, where high risk is mainly attributable to hazard factors (e.g., extreme rainfall). This dichotomy underscores the critical importance of integrating the three dimensions of hazard, exposure, and vulnerability in metro flood risk assessments. Assessments based solely on a single dimension may yield incomplete or potentially misleading conclusions.

Uncertainty analysis

Despite the establishment of a robust assessment framework, several sources of uncertainty should be acknowledged. First, the hazard analysis relies on historical rainfall data and static environmental factors. Future climate change scenarios, which are expected to increase the frequency and intensity of extreme rainfall events, were not incorporated, potentially leading to an underestimation of future hazards. Second, although the vulnerability indicators are comprehensive, more precise physical vulnerability data—such as the actual elevations of station entrances—were not included in this study, though such data could further enhance the model’s accuracy. Third, while the MC-AHP method effectively handles uncertainty in weight assignment, it remains dependent on the initial construction of the hierarchy and the selection of indicators, both of which are based on expert opinion and literature. Although uncertainty in the weighting process was quantified, the structural uncertainty of the model itself warrants further exploration.

Future research

Building upon this study, future research could explore more advanced computational techniques to further enhance the accuracy of flood risk assessments. Data-driven methods, including probabilistic frameworks and optimization algorithms, can be employed to mine complex nonlinear relationships between risk indicators and historical flood events, potentially uncovering hidden patterns that traditional MCDA methods might miss43. For instance, Sadeghfam et al.44 developed a probabilistic framework that integrated frequency analysis and Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) to objectively delineate climate suitability for agriculture, significantly reducing subjectivity and demonstrating the potential of data-driven approaches to enhance assessment accuracy. Furthermore, more sophisticated uncertainty analysis frameworks, such as the Generalized Likelihood Uncertainty Estimation (GLUE) method —which has been successfully applied to quantify uncertainty and address equifinality in environmental models, such as groundwater vulnerability assessments45—could be introduced to the field of metro flood risk. Applying these advanced data-driven methods and GLUE techniques represents a promising frontier for future research.

Practical implications and mitigation strategies

The spatially explicit risk maps generated in this study have significant practical value, providing actionable decision-support information for urban planners and metro operators. The high-resolution risk classification facilitates the development of tiered emergency response strategies, enabling prioritized resource allocation based on the specific risk profile of each metro line and station. Grounded in these practical implications, the following concrete strategies are proposed according to operational domains:

First, from the perspective of early warning systems, for metro lines and stations with very high flood risk, such as Line 1, Line 2, Line 4, Line 7, and stations like Shufang Station, Guangfu Station, Fuhuayuan Station, South Railway Station, Taipingyuan Station, and TCM University Provincial Hospital Station, where passenger flow is high and transfer coefficients are significant, the metro flood disaster early warning system should be further improved. This will ensure that disaster information is received promptly and effective warnings are issued to the public. Additionally, a rapid response mechanism should be established so that once early warning information is received, the metro network can quickly activate emergency plans and take necessary flood prevention measures.

Second, from the perspective of facility upgrades, the construction of waterproof facilities at the entrances and tunnels of metro stations such as Shufang Station, which suffered severe water accumulation during the ‘8.16’ rainstorm, and Shengxian Lake Station, which was submerged during the ‘7.3’ rainstorm, should be strengthened. In addition to raising exit heights and installing waterstops or waterproof doors, consideration should be given to adding waterproof layers in tunnels and enhancing drainage facilities. Furthermore, for older metro lines and facilities, such as Line 1, regular inspections and maintenance should be conducted to ensure they remain in good working condition.

Third, from the perspective of urban planning, flood prevention planning for underground spaces should be strengthened in high-risk areas such as Jinjiang District, Wuhou District, Jinniu District, Chenghua District, Qingyang District, Shuangliu District, Longquanyi District, Wenjiang District, and Pidu District. These areas have high annual rainfall or impermeable surface rates. In urban development, flood prevention requirements for underground spaces should be fully considered, and drainage systems and flood control facilities should be appropriately planned. It is important to avoid constructing metro stations and other critical infrastructure in flood-prone areas to reduce the impact of flood disasters on the metro network.

By implementing this tiered strategy—ranging from advanced early warning responses and structural upgrades to forward-looking planning—urban planners, metro operators, and emergency management departments can move beyond reactive measures and systematically enhance the resilience of the Chengdu metro network. This study holds critical significance for maintaining urban operational continuity, ensuring passenger safety, and ultimately strengthening overall urban resilience.

Conclusion

This study developed a GIS-based integrated framework combining AHP and MC-AHP methodologies to assess flood risk in the Chengdu metro network. By incorporating 16 indicators across hazard, exposure, and vulnerability dimensions, the framework effectively identified high-risk zones and provided a robust tool for urban infrastructure resilience planning.

The main findings include four aspects. First, the flood risk distribution exhibits a concentric pattern, diminishing from the high-risk urban core to the lower-risk suburbs. Second, the MC-AHP method proved superior to the traditional AHP, identifying a greater proportion of high-risk areas (8% vs. 7% for very high-risk) and effectively quantifying uncertainties in expert judgment through β-PERT distributions, thereby yielding more reliable and accurate risk maps. Third, the assessment of 14 operational and 5 under-construction lines revealed that Lines 7, 4, and 2 contain the highest concentrations of very high-risk stations, necessitating prioritized mitigation efforts. Fourth, At the station level, 54 stations (≈ 15.3%) are classified as very-high risk and 124 stations (≈ 35.0%) as high risk, with ~ 72.6% of stations at medium or higher risk, indicating substantial system vulnerability.

The validation of results against historical flood events confirmed the model’s practical accuracy. The findings offer critical insights for policymakers and urban planners, highlighting the urgency of implementing tiered emergency response strategies, upgrading waterproofing infrastructure in high-risk stations, and integrating flood resilience into future urban planning. The framework can serve as a methodological benchmark for flood risk assessment in other metropolises facing similar challenges of rapid underground expansion and escalating climate risks.
