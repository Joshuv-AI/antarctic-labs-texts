Classification of Discrete-Time Queues
Muhammad El-Taha

Abstract




Abstract.

In this article we classify discrete-time queues based on scheduling rules and observation epochs combinations. This classification leads to
coherent
,
sub-coherent
, and
super-coherent
systems when
observed
waiting times are, respectively equal to, less than, or larger than actual waiting times. We then explore the consequences of this classification. Specifically,
we discuss invariant properties of
coherent
systems including queue-lengths, waiting times, servers’ busy times, busy periods, Pollaczek-Khinchine formula, and other common characteristics. An important consequence is that a performance characteristic of a system with specific scheduling rule and observation epoch combination extends to the entire class.
An unresolved issue in the literature is the assertion that Little’s law does not apply for discrete-time queues that incorporate certain scheduling rules. Using this classification, we reconcile the generality of Little’s law and its applicability to all discrete-time queues regardless of scheduling rules.

Keywords:
Classification of discrete-time queues, coherent queues, sample-path analysis, Little’s law, invariant characteristics

Introduction

Consider a discrete-time queueing model where time
\tau=0,1,2,\ldots
is discrete, and where time slots are of equal unit length. Arrivals and departures can occur at the same discrete time instants. We refer to this as the actual system.
To keep track of the system state and transitions between states one needs to order the arrivals and departures. This leads to several scheduling rules (
SR
) like the early arrival (
EAS
) and late arrival (
LAS
) systems. Moreover, to track the system state, (e.g., queue-length) one observes the system at slot edges or slot centers. Scheduling rules and observation epochs will be discussed in Section
2
. When considered together
SR
and observation epochs generate systems that are
coherent
and others that are
incoherent
in the sense that the customers’ observed waiting times in these
incoherent
systems are unequal to the actual system waiting times.

It is well-known that Little’s law (
LL
) applies to discrete-time systems at great level generality. However,
applying discrete-time
LL
to queues with scheduling rules (
SR
) has not been fully explored. There are assertions in the literature that there are instances when
LL

does not apply even when all assumptions are met. A primary motive in this article is to explore this issue and provide recommendations that remedy this situation.

Hunter
[
18
]

He shows that for

SR
for G/Geom/1 and B/Geom/1 FCFS models. But to derive his results, Hunter adopts the convention that (see page 228) for systems with

he “counts the number of service time positions spent in the system”; for

Desert and Daduna

One can identify five popular scheduling rules and six observation epochs (Section

). This creates a large number of potential instances that may require attention. This article creates a new framework that addresses these issues and provides a resolution. Specifically, the contributions of this article are:

1. Classify discrete-time queues based on actual and observed waiting times. This is a novel idea that has not been utilized before in the literature. We examine all
SR
and observation epochs arrangements and classify them into

2
Preliminaries: Queues with Scheduling Rules

In this section, we discuss preliminaries that are needed in Section
3
. Specifically, we discuss what we mean by the actual/standard system, introduce scheduling rules and observation epochs, and discuss our motivation and known issues that arise with
LL
when applied to queues with
SR
. We also give examples that compare actual vs observed waiting times.

2.1
The Actual/Standard System

We define a discrete-time
actual
or
standard
system as one where arrivals and departures take place at discrete-times
\tau
(slot edges) exactly. This means that more than one event type can take place at the same time instant. Sometimes this is all we need to obtain certain results as we do with
LL
in Section
4
. However, quite often, we need to order the events (arrivals and departures) such as when we need to track state transition probabilities. This leads to a variety of
SR
that are described next.

2.2
Scheduling Rules

Here we describe discrete-time queueing models where time is divided, w.l.o.g., into slots of unit length. We assume a discrete-time scale
\{0,1,\ldots,\tau,\ldots\}
so that a time slot is an interval of the time

(\tau,\tau+1]
.
The system is driven by two event types: arrivals and departures (service completions). We assume that the events, i.e., arrivals and or departures occur at the boundary of the time slots. The order of scheduling arrivals and departures in a time slot (equivalently at a time instant) leads to different
SR
that we describe below.
Depending on the behavior of the physical system the order of potential arrivals and departures at any given slot vary significantly. This results in various
SR
, also referred to as scheduling systems or waiting room management policy. Let
A
and
D
denote a potential arrival or departure, respectively, at time
\tau
. We follow the notation setup as in Hunter
[
18
]
, Chaudhry et al
[
4
]
and Desert and Daduna
[
8
]
.

In the early arrival system (
EAS
), potential arrivals in a time slot are scheduled to occur before potential departures.
Specifically, a potential arrival at time
\tau
occurs in
(\tau,\tau+)
, and a potential departure at time
\tau
occurs in
(\tau-,\tau)
.
That is,
\tau-<D<\tau<A<\tau+
.
Moreover, if an arrival finds an idle server, it goes into service immediately and can potentially depart in the same time slot.
In the late arrivals system (
LAS
) the order of potential arrivals and departures is reversed so that potential departures occur early in a time slot and potential arrivals occur at the end of the slot.
More specifically, a potential departure at time
\tau
occurs in
(\tau,\tau+)
, and a potential arrival at time
\tau
occurs in
(\tau-,\tau)
.
That is
\tau-<A<\tau<D<\tau+
.
Moreover, if an arrival at
\tau-
finds an idle server and starts service immediately, it can potentially depart at the beginning of the next time slot at
\tau+
, the system is called immediate access (
IA
).
The late arrival with delayed access (
LAS-DA
) scheduling system is similar to the
LAS-IA
except that
an arrival at
\tau-
waits until the next slot to start service at
\tau+1
, then the system is called delayed access (
DA
).
In the late arrivals departures-first (
LA-DF
) system both potential arrivals and departures occur late in the slot, so that
\tau--<D<\tau-<A<\tau
. An arrival that finds an idle server stars service at
\tau
.
Finally, in the late arrivals arrivals-first (
LA-AF
) system both potential arrivals and departures occur late in the slot, so that
\tau--<A<\tau-<D<\tau
. An arrival that finds an idle server stars service at
\tau
. These scheduling rules are depicted in Figure 1.

,

LAS-DA

2.3

2.4
Motivation

In this subsection we point out instances in the literature where it is stated that
LL
does not appear to hold.
Consider a stable B/Geom/1 model. Arrivals follow a Bernoulli process such that the probability of an arrival in any slot is
0<\alpha<1
. Service times are geometric with parameter

0<\beta<1
, so that

P(S=n)=(1-\beta)^{n-1}\beta\;\;(n=1,2,\ldots)\;,

and
E[S]=1/\beta\;
. Let
\rho=\alpha/\beta<1
.
Consider system characteristics at the slot edges (random observer) and slot centers (outside observer).
The superscripts
R
and
C
will be used to represent random observer (slot edges) and outside observer (slot centers) respectively.
Note that for this system, and regardless of scheduling rule, it is well established (Hunter
[
18
]
) that the mean waiting time in the system is given by
W=\frac{1-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha}.

Now, as noted in Desert and Daduna
[
8
]
, apply
LL
with
\lambda=\alpha
to
EAS
and
LAS-IA
systems. Consider the
EAS
at the random observer epochs, then it is well-known (e.g., El-Taha
[
9
]
) that
L^{R}=\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)}{\beta-\alpha}
. But
L^{R}\not=\lambda W
. Similarly, consider the
LAS-IA
system observed at the slot centers. Then
for this system
L^{C}=\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)}{\beta-\alpha}
. However,
L^{C}\not=\lambda W
.The conclusion is that
LL
does not appear to apply for
EAS
system at the random observer epochs and
LAS-IA
at the outside observer epochs. On the face of it this appears, as noted in the literature, to contradict
LL
.

Some combinations of
SR
/observation epochs have an unexpected effect on the application
of
LL
. In the next subsection we examine three examples to shed light on issues with evaluating waiting times in queues when we factor in
SR
and observation epochs.

2.5
Waiting Times in Queues with Scheduling Rules

We continue our motivation and consider three examples that illustrate the issue with waiting times in discrete-time queues.
We assume that in these queueing models service times are greater or equal to one, i.e., no zero service times.
Let
A_{k},D_{k}
be the actual system’s
k^{th}
arrival and departure epochs, so that

W_{k}=\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}{\bf 1}\{A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}\}=D_{k}-A_{k}\;.

(1)

Now, let
A^{\prime}_{k},D^{\prime}_{k}
be the scheduled
k^{th}
arrival and departure epochs associated with selected
SR
. For a given
SR
, arrival and departure instants are related to the actual arrival and departure instants as follows:

A^{\prime}_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}A_{k}^{-}&\mbox{for LAS-IA, LAS-DA, LA-DF}\;;\\
\\
A_{k}^{--}&\mbox{for LA-AF}\;;\\
\\
A_{k}^{+}&\mbox{for EAS}\;.\end{array}\right.

Similarly,

D^{\prime}_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}D_{k}^{-}&\mbox{for EAS, LA-AF}\;;\\
\\
D_{k}^{--}&\mbox{for LA-DF}\;;\\
\\
D_{k}^{+}&\mbox{for LAS-DA}\;;\\
\\
(D_{k}-1)^{+}&\mbox{for LAS-IA}\;.\end{array}\right.

Let the observed waiting time at slot edges of
k^{th}
arrival be
W_{k}^{o}=\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}{\bf 1}\{A^{\prime}_{k}<\tau\leq D^{\prime}_{k}\}
.

Example 1.

Consider a single server queue where, say
k^{th}

S_{k}=1

\tau

W_{k}=1
. Let us consider the random observer (slot edges) epochs. Consider the
LAS-IA

A^{\prime}_{k}=\tau^{-}

D^{\prime}_{k}-A^{\prime}_{k}=0\not=W_{k}=1
.
However, the observed waiting time

giving the correct time in the system. Now, consider the

A^{\prime}_{k}=\tau^{+}

W^{o}_{k}=0\not=W_{k}=1

LAS-DA

D^{\prime}_{k}-A^{\prime}_{k}=1=W_{k}

.
Both definitions

Example 2.

Consider a

\tau=1

3
Classification of Discrete-Time Queues

In this section we classify discrete-time systems with respect to
SR
and observation epochs combinations.
For our purposes, a discrete-time queueing system has three basic features. The first feature is the
SR
which can be any of
EAS
,
LAS-IA
,
LAS-DA
,
LA-AF
,
LA-DF
. One can think of others, but these are the ones considered in the literature. The second feature is the state observation epoch needed to obtain the time-average system characteristics. In continuous time one observes the system continuously over time. In discrete-time queues with
SR
, we have the random observer epochs (slot edges), outside observer epochs (slot centers), and potential pre-arrival, post arrival, pre-departure, and post departure epochs. The third feature is how to measure time in the system including time in queue and time in service. In the literature, regardless of the observation epochs, there are two methods to measure time in system by either counting time positions (e.g., slot edges or slot centers), or counting service slots. More importantly, the service position/slot is not always the selected observation epoch, contributing to the issues mentioned earlier. By contrast we count time in the system at the observation epochs.

3.1
Assumptions

With
SR
that schedule arrivals and departures around slot boundaries, observed and actual waiting times are not always equivalent. To resolve this issue, we introduce the concept of
coherent
systems.
Consider any discrete-time queueing system with possible multiple
SR
and observation epochs. Let
u(\tau)\in\{\tau,\tau-.5,\tau^{--},\tau^{-},\tau^{+},\tau^{++}\}
be an observation epoch, and
\{(A_{k},D_{k}),k\geq 1\}
be the input data.
At the heart of it, it turns out that
the actual customer waiting

W_{k}=D_{k}-A_{k}
and the observed waiting time (waiting time as observed at the observation epochs) are not equal for all
SR
/observation epochs combinations.
This observation motivates our next definition.

Definition 1.

A discrete-time queueing system is said to be
coherent
if for all customers,
k=1,2,\ldots
,

\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}{\bf 1}\{A^{\prime}_{k}<u(\tau)\leq D^{\prime}_{k}\}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}{\bf 1}\{A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}\}\;.

(2)

Otherwise, the system is said to be
incoherent
.

This definition says that a discrete-time queueing system is
coherent
if, for every customer, the observed waiting time is equal to the actual waiting time. In other words, when you measure how long a customer has been in the system based on a specific scheduling rule and observation epoch, the result matches the actual time elapsed between their arrival and departure. An incoherent system is one where the observed waiting time does not equal the actual waiting time.

Remarks.

(i) Let

W^{o}_{k}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}{\bf 1}\{A^{\prime}_{k}<u(\tau)\leq D^{\prime}_{k}\}

be the
observed
waiting time associated with some
SR
/observation epochs combinations. Then (
2
) says that a system is
coherent
if for all arrivals
k=1,2,\ldots
,

W^{o}_{k}=W_{k}\;.

Otherwise, the system is said to be
incoherent
.

(ii) Note that for the
actual
system for all
k=1,\ldots

\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}{\bf 1}\{A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}\}=\sum_{\tau=A_{k}}^{D_{k}}{\bf 1}\{A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}\}=D_{k}-A_{k}:=W_{k}

(iii) For systems with
SR
and random observation epochs, i.e.,
u(\tau)=\tau
we have

\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}{\bf 1}\{A^{\prime}_{k}<\tau\leq D^{\prime}_{k}\}=\sum_{\tau=A_{k}}^{D_{k}}{\bf 1}\{A^{\prime}_{k}<\tau\leq D^{\prime}_{k}\}

This sum equals the actual
W_{k}
of corresponding actual system for only
coherent
systems.

Lemma 3.1

The waiting time distribution function is invariant with respect to SR for all coherent systems.

Proof.

The proof follows from observing that for any sample path, all
\{W_{k},k\geq 1\}
for any
coherent
system are the same as that of the actual system.

Incoherent systems have been recognized and studied in the literature, see for example Hunter
[
18
]
.
The examples in Subsection
2.5
suggest that there are two types of
incoherent
systems. One is where the observed waiting time is shorter the actual waiting time, and the other is where the observed waiting time is longer the actual waiting time.

Definition 2.
Consider any discrete-time queueing system with possible multiple
SR

4
Little’s Law for Discrete Systems

To pinpoint the issues surrounding the application of
LL
to discrete-time queues we need a general rigorous proof of
LL
.
However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no proof of discrete-time
LL
at the level of generality of the proof given by Stidham
[
20
]
and El-Taha and Stidham
[
11
]
for the continuous time
LL
. Moreover, the definitions of
L
and
W
play a role in how

functions when
SR
are invoked. In this section we review the discrete-time

and give a sample-path proof at a level of generality comparable to Stidham
[
20
]
. It turns out that the proof of

for discrete-time systems is similar to that of the continuous time counterpart.

Consider a discrete-time scale
\tau=0,1,\ldots
. The input
data are
\{(A_{k},D_{k}),k\geq 1\}
, where
0\leq A_{k}\leq A_{k+1}<\infty
,
A_{k}\leq D_{k}<\infty
,

, and
A_{k}
and
D_{k}
are
interpreted as the arrival
time and the departure time, respectively, of customer
k
. We assume
\{A_{k},k\geq 1\}
is a deterministic point process, i.e., a sample path of a stochastic process.
Note that our definition allows more than one event to occur at any given time point, i.e., batch arrivals.
We also assume that

A_{k}\rightarrow\infty
, as
k\rightarrow\infty
, so that there are
only a finite number of arrivals in any finite time interval. Let
A(\tau):=\#\{k:A_{k}\leq\tau\}
,
D(\tau):=\#\{k:D_{k}\leq\tau\},\tau\geq 0
, so that
A(\tau)

and

count the number of arrivals and departures, respectively, in the
interval
(0,\tau]
. Note that, since

for all

,

as
\tau\rightarrow\infty
. Note also that
A(\tau)=\max\{k:A_{k}\leq\tau\}
, since
\{A_{k},k\geq 1\}
is a non-decreasing sequence.
But in general, we cannot write
D(\tau)=\max\{k:D_{k}\leq\tau\}
, because

\{D_{k},k\geq 1\}
is not necessarily non-decreasing. It is
non-decreasing
if the discipline is
first-in, first-out
(FIFO), that is, if departures
occur in the same order as arrivals.

Informally, one can see that,

\displaystyle L(\tau)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\#\{k:A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}\}=A(\tau)-D(\tau)\;,\;\tau\geq 0\;,

(4)

\displaystyle W_{k}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle D_{k}-A_{k}\;,\;k\geq 1\;,

(5)

is the waiting time in the system of customer

Formally, let
{\bf 1}\{E\}

\displaystyle L(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}{\bf 1}\{A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}\}\;,

(6)

\displaystyle W_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}{\bf 1}\{A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}\}\;.

(7)

In this subsection, all we say is that the same arrival and departure instants are used to compute both

and

Our result below is a discrete-time counterpart of the original sample-path version of

contained in Stidham

Theorem 4.1

Suppose

\tau^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}L(j)\rightarrow L
, as

.

The proof is given in the Appendix. Theorem

is general enough to work in almost all practical situations in discrete-time queues. One can construct discrete-time versions of

that works under weaker conditions than those given here as in El-Taha and Stidham
[

Note that our definitions of

Example.
Consider a discrete system where arrivals and departures occur at

Now, consider equality on the left and inequality on the right, that is let

We see that

4.1
Applying Little’s Law to Queues with Scheduling Rules

When it exists,

If you calculate

Similarly, if you calculate

This approach reconciles the apparent contradictions found in the literature. The important thing is to use the same set of arrival and departure instants consistently to evaluate

Little’s Law holds universally for all discrete-time queues, regardless of the scheduling rule, as long as this consistency in measurement is maintained.

Consider a B/Geom/1 queue as described above. Then

\\
\gamma(0)\pi(0)\;;&n=1\;;\end{array}\right.

(20)

and

\pi(0)=\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\gamma+\gamma(0)-\gamma(0)\gamma+\gamma(0)\gamma(1)}\;,

where
for coherent systems
\gamma(0)=\frac{\alpha}{\beta(1-\alpha)}
, and
\gamma(1)=\gamma

for sub-coherent systems
\gamma(0)=\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)}{\beta(1-\alpha)}

\gamma(1)=\gamma

\gamma(0)=\frac{\alpha(1+\beta)}{\beta(1-\alpha)^{2}}

Using Theorem

\displaystyle L=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\pi(n)=\frac{[\gamma(0)\gamma(1-\gamma)^{2}+\gamma(0)\gamma(1)(1-\gamma(1-\gamma)^{2})](1-\gamma)}{\gamma^{(}1-\gamma)^{2}[1-\gamma+\gamma(0)-\gamma(0)\gamma+\gamma(0)\gamma(1)]}\;.

We specialize Theorem

for the three classes in the following result.

Theorem 5.2

Let

and
\rho=\alpha/\beta

(ii) for sub-coherent systems

\pi(n)=\left.\begin{array}[]{lll}(1-\gamma)\gamma^{n}\;;&n=0,1,\ldots\;;\end{array}\right.

(22)

(iii) for super-coherent systems

\pi(n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\rho^{2}(1-\gamma)\gamma^{n-2}\;;&n=2,3,\ldots\;;\\

5.2
Coherent and Sub-Coherent Systems

It is more common in the literature to see studies of instances of
coherent
and/or
sub-coherent
systems as the majority of systems
fall under these two categories. For these systems
\gamma(1)=\gamma
, and
\gamma(0)=\frac{\alpha(1-\beta(0))}{\beta(1-\alpha)}
where
\beta(0)=0
for
coherent
systems and
\beta
for
sub-coherent
systems. Therefore,

\pi(n)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\gamma(0)(1-\gamma)\gamma^{n-1}}{1-\gamma+\gamma(0)}\;;&n=1,2,\ldots\;;\\
\\
\frac{1-\gamma}{1-\gamma+\gamma(0)}\;;&n=0\;;\end{array}\right.

(24)

and

L=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\pi(n)=\frac{(1-\gamma)\gamma(0)}{1-\gamma+\gamma(0)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}n\gamma^{n-1}=\frac{(1-\gamma)\gamma(0)}{1-\gamma+\gamma(0)}\frac{1}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}\;;

then simplify to obtain,

L=\frac{\gamma(0)}{(1-\gamma+\gamma(0))(1-\gamma)}\;.

Using the input parameters
\alpha
and
\beta
, we get

L=\frac{\alpha-\alpha\beta(0)}{\beta-\alpha\beta(0)}\times\frac{\beta(1-\alpha)}{\beta-\alpha}\;;

so that

L=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\alpha(1-\alpha)}{\beta-\alpha}\;;&\beta(0)=0\;($coherent systems$)\;;\\
\\
\frac{\alpha(1-\beta)}{\beta-\alpha}\;;&\beta(0)=\beta\;($sub-coherent systems$)\;.\end{array}\right.

(25)

Now we focus on
\pi(0)
. Note that
1-\gamma+\gamma(0)=\frac{\beta-\alpha\beta(0)}{\beta(1-\alpha)}
, and

\pi(0)=\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha\beta(0)}\;.

Moreover, the probability that there is one or more customers in the system is given by

1-\pi(0)=\frac{\gamma(0)}{1-\gamma+\gamma(0)}=\frac{\alpha-\alpha\beta(0)}{\beta-\alpha\beta(0)}\;.

(26)

Equation (
26
) is interesting in that one cannot say, at this level of generality, that
1-\pi(0)
is the probability the server is busy, or it represents the sever utilization, only that it represents the probability that one or more customers are in the system. This is a radical shift from our understanding of

1-\pi(0)
is continuous time systems.
Note how we adjust for customers that enter an empty system and leave immediately, i.e., before the next observation instant.

Remark.

In the continuous setting we apply Little’s law to obtain the server’s utilization where
L=U
,
\lambda
is the arrival rate and
W
is the mean service time. Applying
LL
to the discrete-time
actual
system, we obtain

U=\alpha/\beta

as the probability that the server is busy. This is true only for
coherent
systems.
Similarly, the probability the server is idle is
I=1-\rho
. Now, note that,

\pi(0)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta}=1-\rho\;;&\beta(0)=0\;($coherent systems$)\;;\\
\\
\frac{\beta-\alpha}{\beta-\alpha\beta(0)}=1-\gamma\;;&\beta(0)=\beta\;($sub-coherent systems$)\;.\end{array}\right.

(27)

So, the time average probability that the server is idle is consistent with the case
\beta(0)=0
. For
sub-coherent
systems where

\beta(0)=\beta
, we cannot interpret
\pi(0)
as the probability the server is idle, only that there are
0
customers in the system at the observed epochs. This is an adjustment for customers that enter an empty system and leave immediately, i.e., before the next observation instant. Obviously, those customers kept the server busy for one slot, but that information is not captured in the state description.

Additional Invariant Characteristics of Coherent Systems

In this Section we focus on
coherent

\displaystyle=

G_{k}
is the total cost
incurred by customer
k
. Assume that
H(\tau)
and
G_{k}
are well defined for all
\tau
and
k
.

Like the continuous time case,
LL
has an economic interpretation that suggests the current extension. Let
f_{k}(\tau):={\bf 1}\{A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}\}
, i.e., customer
k
incurs a cost of one dollar per unit time while
in the system (i.e., while
A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}
) and zero cost otherwise.
Then we can interpret the
function
f_{k}(\tau)
as the cost rate of customer
k
at time
\tau
. Under this
interpretation,

L(\tau)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\tau)
is the total cost rate at time
\tau
and

W_{k}=\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}f_{k}(\tau)
is the total cost incurred by customer
k
, so that

L=\lambda W

says that the long-run average cost per unit time
equals the arrival rate of customers times the long-run average cost per
customer.
The generalization to
H=\lambda G
arises
naturally if one allows a
more general cost-rate function than the indicator of the event

\{A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}\}
.
With
H(\tau)
and
G_{k}
defined by (
44
) and (
45
),
respectively, define the following limiting averages, when they exist:

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}N(\tau)\;,

(46)

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau}H(j)\;,

(47)

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}G_{k}\;.

(48)

We seek conditions under which
H=\lambda G
.
Following Stidham
[
20
]
and Heyman and Stidham
[
17
]
, suppose
that the bivariate sequence
\{(A_{k},f_{k}(\cdot)),k\geq 1\}
satisfies the
following condition:

Condition L1.
There exists a sequence
\{W_{k},k\geq 1\}
such that,

(i)
W_{k}/A_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
; and

(ii)
f_{k}(\tau)=0
for
\tau\notin(A_{k},A_{k}+W_{k}]
.

Condition
L1
says that all the cost associated with the

k^{th}
point (e.g., the
k^{th}
customer) is incurred in a finite time
interval beginning at the point (e.g., the arrival of the customer), and that
the lengths of these intervals cannot grow at the same rate as the points
themselves, as
k\to\infty
. This is a stronger-than-necessary condition
for
H=\lambda G
(See El-Taha and Stidham
[
11
]
for details), but it is
satisfied in most applications to queueing systems, in which the time points

A_{k}
and
A_{k}+W_{k}
correspond to customer arrivals and departures,
respectively, and it is natural to assume that customers can only incur cost
while they are physically present in the system.

The proof of the discrete-time
H=\lambda G
follows the same steps as the continuous-time case given by El-Taha and Stidham
[
11
]
.

Theorem 7.1

Suppose
\tau^{-1}A(\tau)\rightarrow\lambda
as
\tau\rightarrow\infty
, where

0\leq\lambda<\infty
, and Condition L1 holds. Then if
n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}G_{k}\rightarrow G
as
n\rightarrow\infty
,
where
0\leq G<\infty
, then
\tau^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\tau}H(j)

\rightarrow H
as

\tau\rightarrow\infty
, and
H=\lambda G
.

Proof.
Similar to (
56
), one can show that for all
\tau\geq 1,

\sum_{k:A_{k}\leq\tau}G_{k}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}H(j)\geq\sum_{k:A_{k}+W_{k}\leq\tau}G_{k}\;,\;\tau\geq 1\;.

(49)

Now, utilizing Lemma
9.1
, the proof is similar to the argument in the proof of Theorem
4.1
.

7.1
Multi-Server Queues: Relations between Workload and
Waiting Time

The results in this Subsection are derived using the
actual
system, i.e., we assume arrivals and departures take place at the integer instants
\tau
. By condition
L2
(see below), the results are valid for all
coherent
systems.
We now use
H=\lambda G
to derive a relation between the
time-average workload and the customer-average waiting time in the queue in
a multi-server system with a non-preemptive queue discipline.
Consider the
G/G/c
queue. The
input data consists of the sequence

\{(A_{k},S_{k}),k\geq 1\}
, where
A_{k}
is the arrival instant and
S_{k}
the
work requirement of customer
k
.
Assume that each the queue discipline is a non-preemptive, i.e.,
the server is never idle
when customers are waiting, and that the server works at unit rate.

Let

W_{k}^{q}
denotes the
k^{th}
customer waiting time in queue (excluding service time).
Assume the following limits exist and are finite:

\displaystyle ES

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}S_{k}\;,

\displaystyle ES^{2}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}S_{k}^{2}\;,

\displaystyle EW^{q}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}W_{k}^{q}\;,

\displaystyle ESW^{q}

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}S_{k}W_{k}^{q}\;.

Here,
ES
is the long-run average service time,
ES^{2}
is the long-run empirical second moment of service times,
EW_{q}
is the long-run average waiting time in queue. Note that these are sample-path averages, even though we use a notation
suggestive of expectations.

Let
f_{k}(\tau)
be the work remaining to be done for the
k^{th}

customer at time epoch
\tau
. Then for the
actual
system

\displaystyle f_{k}(\tau)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{k}{\bf 1}\{A_{k}<\tau\leq A_{k}+W_{k}^{q}\}+

(50)

\displaystyle(S_{k}-(\tau-A_{k}-W_{k}^{q})){\bf 1}\{A_{k}+W_{k}^{q}<\tau\leq A_{k}+W_{k}^{q}+S_{k}\}\;.

Implicit in (
50
) is the assumption that all arrivals and departures take place at the discrete-time instants
\tau
.
Let

V(\tau)=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\tau),

so that
V(\tau)
is the total amount of work in the system at potential arrival times
\tau
. That is this is the total work in the system a potential arrival at time
\tau
would see upon arrival.
Let

EV:=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}V(j)\;,

when the limit exists.
The following result is valid for multi-server discrete-time queues.

Corollary 7.2

Consider a multi-server queue with FIFO queueing discipline. Suppose the sequences
\{S_{k},k\geq 1\}
and
\{W_{k}^{q},k\geq 1\}
, are
asymptotically pathwise uncorrelated, that is,

ESW^{q}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}S_{k}W_{k}^{q}=ES\cdot EW^{q}\;.

(51)

Then

EV=\lambda ESEW^{q}+\lambda(ES^{2}-ES)/2\;.

(52)

Proof.

Let
H(\tau)=V(\tau)
. Using (
50
),

G_{k}=\sum_{\tau=0}^{\infty}f_{k}(\tau)=S_{k}W_{k}^{q}+(S_{k}^{2}-S_{k})/2\;;

G=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{n}[S_{k}W_{k}^{q}+(S_{k}^{2}-S_{k})/2]=ESW^{q}+(ES^{2}-ES)/2\;.

Since
\lambda
,
ES
, and
EW^{q}
are well defined
and finite, Condition
L1
holds with
W_{k}=W_{k}^{q}+S_{k}
, the waiting time
of the
k^{th}
customer in the system. Applying
H=\lambda G
, we conclude that

EV=\lambda ESW^{q}+\lambda(ES^{2}-ES)/2\;.

(53)

Now, use condition (
51
) to obtain (
52
).

Condition (
51
) is true a.s. for stochastic models with
service-time
independent
queueing disciplines, that is, models in which the rule for selecting
the next job to process does depend on the service times
of jobs. The
FIFO
queue discipline is an example of such a rule.

The first term of (
52
) is the total amount of work associated with
customers waiting in the queue, and the second term is the
residual service time. In contrast, for continuous time models the residual service time is given by
\lambda ES^{2}/2
.
Relationship (
52
) is valid for systems where customers depart in the order of arrival like the
G/D/c
and
G/G/1-FIFO
queues.

Note that
EV

coincides with the

virtual
waiting time, that is, the amount of time that a customer would have
to wait in the queue if that customer arrived at time
\tau
. Thus, in the

FIFO
case, (
52
) also gives a relation between the
time average virtual
waiting time and the customer average actual waiting time in queue.

Single-Server Queues: The Pollaczek-Khinchine Formula

Here we give the well-known Pollaczek-Khinchine Formula for the
actual
discrete-time queues with Bernoulli arrivals.

Corollary 7.3

Consider a stable FIFO single-server queue with service time independent discipline.
Assume
ASTA
holds, then,

EW^{q}=\frac{\lambda(ES^{2}-ES)}{2(1-\rho)}\;,

(54)

where
\rho=\lambda ES<1
.

Proof.

Let
EV^{A}
be the workload in the system at (actual) arrival times.
With
FIFO
discipline, the workload at arrival instants
EV^{A}
coincides with the actual waiting time in the queue
W^{q}
.
Moreover, by
ASTA
(El-Taha
[
10
]
), we obtain
EV^{A}=EV
(note that EV is the workload at
potential
arrival time instants).
Therefore

EW^{q}=\lambda ESEW^{q}+\lambda(ES^{2}-ES)/2\;.

Simplify to obtain (
54
).

Corollary
7.3
is a
sample-path version of the Pollaczek-Khinchine (PK) formula for a
FIFO
single-server
queue where
ASTA
holds. In a stochastic setting, the most common situation where
ASTA
holds is when arrivals are Bernoulli.

7.2
H=
\lambda
G and Coherent Queues

Here we explore how H=
\lambda
G applies to queues with
SR
. We first extend our definition of
coherent
systems to queues with cost function
f_{k}(\tau)
.
Let
A_{k},D_{k}=A_{k}+W_{k}
be the actual system
k^{th}
arrival and departure epochs. Moreover, let
A^{\prime}_{k},D^{\prime}_{k}=(A_{k}+W_{k})^{\prime}
be the
k^{th}
scheduled arrival and departure epochs associated with selected
SR
. Now, we modify condition
L1
to
L2
.

Condition L2.

There exists a sequence
\{W_{k},k\geq 1\}
such that,

(i)
W_{k}/A^{\prime}_{k}\rightarrow 0
as
k\rightarrow\infty
; and

(ii)
f_{k}(u(\tau))=0\mbox{ for }u(\tau)\notin(A^{\prime}_{k},(A_{k}+W_{k})^{\prime}]

where depending on the
SR
we always have
A^{\prime}_{k}
equals
A_{k}^{-},A_{k}^{--}
or
A_{k}^{+}
. Similarly,
D^{\prime}_{k}=(A_{k}+W_{k})^{\prime}
equals
D_{k}^{-},D_{k}^{--}
,
D_{k}^{+}
or
(D_{k}-1)^{+}
.

Definition 1.
Consider a discrete-time queueing system with any
SR
and observation epoch combination.
Then, the system is said to be
coherent
if for all arrivals
k=1,2,\ldots
,

\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\tau)=\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(u(\tau))\;.

Otherwise, the system is said to be
incoherent
.

Note that for all
k=1,\ldots

\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\tau)=\sum_{\tau=A_{k}+1}^{A_{k}+W_{k}}f_{k}(\tau),

and

\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(u(\tau))=\sum_{\tau=A_{k}+1}^{A_{k}+W_{k}}f_{k}(u(\tau)).

Similar to Theorem
4.2
one can show that
H=\lambda G
applies to all
coherent
systems.

Corollary 7.4

Under condition L2,
H=\lambda G
applies to all coherent systems.

Corollary 7.5

Relation (
50
) applies to all coherent systems. In particular,
the results in Subsection
7.1
apply to all coherent queues.

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle f_{k}(u(\tau))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S_{k}{\bf 1}\{A^{\prime}_{k}<u(\tau)\leq(A_{k}+W_{k}^{q})^{\prime}\}+

(55)

\displaystyle(S_{k}-(\tau-A_{k}-W_{k}^{q})){\bf 1}\{(A_{k}+W_{k}^{q})^{\prime}<u(\tau)\leq(A_{k}+W_{k}^{q}+S_{k})^{\prime}\}\;.

The result follows by
noting that for all
coherent
systems we have,

\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(u(\tau))=\sum_{\tau=1}^{\infty}f_{k}(\tau)=G_{k}\;;

where
f_{k}(\tau)
and
f_{k}(u(\tau))
are given by (
50
) and (
55
) respectively.

Remarks.

(i) Consider the
B/Geom/1

coherent
model. Assume arrival probability is
\alpha
and mean service time is
1/\beta
. Then by (
54
), we have

W_{q}=\rho(1-\beta)/(\beta-\alpha)\;,

and

W=(1-\alpha)/(\beta-\alpha)

(ii) The workload process goes up by
S_{k},k=1,2,\ldots,
at arrival instants, then it goes down by one unit at a time. This this equivalent to a model with batch arrivals where the batch size is equal to
S
and service times are deterministic with one unit per customer. Then the number of customers in the system at any given time is equal to corresponding workload
V(\tau)
. Therefore, the mean queue-length is the same as the mean work in the system and can be computed from the
PK
formula given in this section.

(iii) The
PK
formula given in (
54
) has been derived for one
SR
rule at a time. See for instance Hunter
[
18
]
who gives the result for
LAS-DA
observed at departure instants, and Chan et al.
[
5
]
who gives the same result for discretized
M/G/1
queue using the equivalent of the
EAS
scheduling rule. In contrast, our approach applies to all
coherent
systems.

8
Concluding Remarks

The results in this article open the door to study discrete-time queues as classes.
By studying one model within a class (e.g.,
coherent
systems), one can draw conclusions about the entire class. More research is needed to study the invariant characteristics of each class, with emphasis on the
coherent
class.
When using
SR
, one also needs to be aware of the corresponding observation epoch.
Although most of systems studied in the literature are
coherent
, there are instances when
incoherent
systems can be useful as they can give insights about the behavior of a
coherent
system. An example is when we apply
BASTA
(El-Taha
[
10
]
) for discrete-time systems with
SR
.

9
Appendix: Proof of Little’s Law

We shall need the following lemma, which is a discrete-time sample-path analogue of the elementary renewal theorem, was proved in El-Taha and Stidham
[
11
]

in continuous time.

Lemma 9.1

Let
0\leq\lambda\leq\infty
.
Then
\tau^{-1}A(\tau)\rightarrow\lambda
as
\tau\rightarrow\infty

if and only if
k^{-1}A_{k}\rightarrow\lambda^{-1}
as

k\rightarrow\infty
.

For a proof of Lemma
9.1
refer to the proof Lemma 2.1 of El-Taha and Stidham
[
11
]
.
Now, we obtain the basic inequality,

Lemma 9.2

For all
\tau\geq 1

\sum_{k:A_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}L(j)\geq\sum_{k:D_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}\;,\;\tau\geq 1\;.

(56)

Proof.

Using the above notation, we obtain the basic equalities,

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}L(j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k:A_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}-\sum_{k:A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}}(D_{k}-\tau)\;;

(57)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}L(j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k:D_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}+\sum_{k:A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}}(\tau-A_{k})\;;

(58)

\displaystyle\sum_{k:A_{k}<\tau\leq D_{k}}W_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k:A_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}-\sum_{k:D_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}\hbox{ .}

(59)

Note that the third equality follows from the first two. The Lemma then follows.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

Since
W<\infty
,
it follows that
n^{-1}W_{n}\rightarrow 0
.
Moreover,
W_{n}/A_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. To see this write
W_{n}/A_{n}=(W_{n}/n)(n/A_{n})
and use Lemma
9.1
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be given. Since
W_{n}/A_{n}\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, there exists an integer
N
such that,
k\geq N

implies
W_{k}\leq A_{k}\epsilon
. Therefore, for all
\tau\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{k:D_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k:A_{k}+W_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq N:A_{k}(1+\epsilon)\leq\tau}W_{k}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{k:A_{k}(1+\epsilon)\leq\tau}W_{k}-\sum_{k\leq N-1}W_{k},

which, together with the basic inequality (
56
), implies

\displaystyle\sum_{k:A_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}\geq\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}L(j)\geq\sum_{k:A_{k}(1+\epsilon)\leq\tau}W_{k}-\sum_{k\leq N-1}W_{k}\;.

(60)

Moreover,

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{k:A_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}A(\tau)A(\tau)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{A(\tau)}W_{k}=\lambda W\;.

and

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{k:A_{k}(1+\epsilon)\leq\tau}W_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)^{-1}\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}[\tau(1+\epsilon)^{-1}]^{-1}\sum_{k:A_{k}\leq\tau(1+\epsilon)^{-1}}W_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)^{-1}\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{k:A_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)^{-1}\lambda W\;.

Now,

\displaystyle\lambda W=\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{k:A_{k}\leq\tau}W_{k}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\limsup_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}L(j)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\liminf_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}L(j)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{k:A_{k}(1+\epsilon)\leq\tau}W_{k}-\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{k\leq N-1}W_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+\epsilon)^{-1}\lambda W\;.

Since
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, using the fact that
\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{k\leq N-1}W_{k}=0
, we conclude that these inequalities hold in the limit as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. Therefore, the limit
\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}L(j)
exists, and

\lambda W\geq\lim_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\tau^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{\tau}L(j)\geq\lambda W\;.

This completes the proof.
