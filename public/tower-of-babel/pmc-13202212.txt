Clinical Characteristics, Antibiotic Resistance Trends, and Associated Molecular Epidemiology Traits in Escherichia coli Isolates Producing Neonatal Bacteremia
Authors: Keerti Ivaturi, Christine Symes, Mina Farahbakhsh, Sanjula Bhuri, Joshua L Wheatley, Rangaraj Selvarangan, Daniel P Heruth, Susana Chavez-Bueno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Escherichia coli is a leading cause of neonatal sepsis. This study's objective was to investigate the clinical characteristics of newborns with E. coli bacteremia, and recent antibiotic resistance trends and molecular epidemiologic features of these invasive strains.MethodsClinical data and blood E. coli isolates collected from 54 neonates with bacteremia were studied. E. coli antibiotic susceptibility trends over time, and whole-genome sequencing data relevant to the genomic relatedness and capsule genotype of the isolates were analyzed.ResultsThirty percent of bacteremic newborns were preterm. Mortality was 19% in preterm versus 5% in term infants. Cerebrospinal fluid was collected in 43 (80%); meningitis was diagnosed in 28%. Necrotizing enterocolitis occurred in 2 neonates, both born preterm. Fifty-four percent of isolates were nonsusceptible to ampicillin and 7% to ceftriaxone. Nonsusceptibility to cefazolin increased over time; non-susceptibility rates for gentamicin and tobramycin were 11% for each antibiotic. Whole-genome sequencing showed multiple sequence types (STs), of which ST95 (17%) was the most prevalent, followed by ST69 (11%), ST131 (7%), and ST12 (7%). K1 (43%) and K5 (20%) capsule genes were most prevalent.ConclusionsMortality from E. coli sepsis remains high, especially in preterm newborns. Resistance to β-lactams and aminoglycosides, the most common empiric treatments for E. coli sepsis, is worsening. Treatment regimens against neonatal E. coli sepsis need to be tailored according to evolving antibiotic resistance trends. Neonatal sepsis isolates are characterized by specific genomic traits indicative of virulent phenotypes that need continued surveillance.Keywords: antibiotic resistance, bacteremia, E. coli, neonatal sepsis, whole genome sequencing

E. coli is a major neonatal pathogen in the United States, where it is a leading cause of sepsis and associated mortality of up to 30%–40%, particularly among premature newborns [1–3]. Antibiotic resistance has become prevalent in E. coli strains causing neonatal sepsis. Antibiotics commonly prescribed for empiric treatment of neonatal E. coli sepsis are now less reliable in providing adequate coverage [1, 2]. Neonatal E. coli strains are showing reduced susceptibility to ampicillin and gentamicin—key antibiotics in the treatment of neonatal early-onset sepsis [4]. Neonates (children up to 28 days old) diagnosed with sepsis later in their hospital course or with frequent antibiotic exposure have also been infected with multidrug-resistant E. coli strains [1]. Continued monitoring of antibiotic resistance trends among neonatal E. coli invasive strains is necessary. Moreover, there is a scarcity of studies detailing the clinical characteristics and risk factors specific to neonates with E. coli bacteremia in the United States. This knowledge is crucial to develop effective preventative and treatment strategies for this devastating disease.

Neonatal E. coli sepsis isolates harbor characteristic virulence-associated genes that define them as extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli [5]. These extraintestinal pathogenic E. coli isolates belong to common multilocus sequence types (MLST) that cause invasive diseases in children. Multilocus sequence typing is a molecular genotyping method to investigate the genetic relatedness and evolution of E. coli strains, with each sequence type (ST) defined by a specific gene allele combination [6]. MLST-based E. coli typing is also useful for tracking the distribution of E. coli STs with similar pathogenicity and virulence gene content. For example, ST131 E. coli strains predominate among those causing bacteremia in both children and adults [7]. We and others originally described the MLST-based molecular epidemiology of neonatal invasive E. coli in the United States, highlighting the predominance of ST131 and ST95, and the recent emergence of ST1193, also seen in other countries [8–12]. However, continued surveillance of predominant E. coli STs causing neonatal invasive disease in different areas of the United States is needed to understand epidemiological trends and to detect the potential emergence of virulent clones.

Among the virulence factors relevant to neonatal E. coli disease, the K1 capsule is prevalent among invasive strains, and plays a crucial role in pathogenesis by protecting the bacteria against opsonophagocytosis and complement-mediated lysis. Up to 80% of neonatal meningitis isolates are K1-positive; however, small studies report a lower prevalence among isolates causing neonatal bacteremia only [8, 10, 13]. A previous US study of 43 neonatal E. coli bacteremia isolates published by our group showed a K1 capsule prevalence of 46% [9]. Recent data on the prevalence of the K1 capsule in neonatal E. coli bacteremia isolates in the United States is limited, which highlights the need for ongoing surveillance, particularly given the potential role of the E. coli capsular antigen as a target for future preventive strategies.

This study aimed to investigate the clinical characteristics of US neonates with E. coli bacteremia, and the pattern of antibiotic resistance of these clinical isolates over time, including those isolated in recent years. We also sought to characterize the molecular epidemiologic features of these bacterial isolates, using whole-genome sequencing (WGS) to determine MLST classification and to characterize the K1 capsule encoding genes of these invasive strains.

METHODS

This is a descriptive study of neonatal E. coli invasive strains collected at a tertiary pediatric hospital in the United States. Blood cultures from hospitalized neonates ages 0–30 days old that had growth of E. coli were identified by review of electronic medical records. E coli isolates were stored at – 80° C in 20% glycerol until further studies were performed. All isolates were collected from January 2006 through May 2021. The collection and storage process previously described by our group was used for the current study [9]. In this study, 54 samples were collected at Children's Mercy Kansas City, a large free-standing pediatric hospital in the Midwest United States. Clinical data were retrieved by review of the electronic medical records under institutional review board approval (IRB number STUDY00000195). Informed consent was waived.

E. coli bacteremia was defined as isolation of only this pathogen from a blood culture specimen. Age at bacteremia diagnosis was based on the date and time when the blood culture sample was collected. Only isolates from each neonate's first positive blood culture were included. In 4 neonates, a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) E. coli isolate collected on the first day of bacteremia was retrieved instead of the blood isolate. E. coli identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing were performed by the microbiology clinical laboratory following guidelines by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute for established minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) breakpoints that classified each isolate as susceptible, intermediate or resistant to the antibiotics tested. Isolates demonstrating resistance or intermediate susceptibility to a given antibiotic were designated as nonsusceptible. Testing for extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production was performed with the VITEK 2 system, including ESBL testing as needed. Multidrug-resistant (MDR) E. coli was defined as nonsusceptibility to at least 3 antibiotic classes as determined by Magiorakos et al [14]. To study antibiotic susceptibility trends over time, we compared isolates recovered in the first half of the study (years 2006–2013) with those identified in the second half (years 2014–2021).

WGS data from each E. coli isolate was obtained using Illumina HiSeq technology as previously described [15]. Reads were assembled de novo with the A5 assembler pipeline [16]. Sequences were analyzed using the bioinformatics resources available at the Centre of Genomic Epidemiology from the Technical University of Denmark (https://www.genomicepidemiology.org/). MLST 2.0 was used to assign the ST of each strain according to the Achtman scheme [17–20]. VirulenceFinder 2.0 was used to determine the presence of capsule synthesis genes using the default threshold percent identity of 90%.

For data statistical analyses, chi-square of Fisher exact tests were used to compare proportions as appropriate; a P value < .05 was considered significant.

RESULTS

Demographic and Birth Characteristics

A total of 54 neonatal E. coli bacteremia isolates were identified from 2006 to 2021. The average number of isolates collected per year was 3.3 (standard deviation ± 1.7). N = 21 (39%) newborns were female. Caucasian was the most frequent race with n = 31 (57%), followed by Black newborns, n = 11 (20%). N = 7 (13%) were Hispanic or Latino (Table 1). Term newborns comprised a total of 38 (70%). The gestational age distribution of all infants is shown in Table 1. Birthweight was available in 50 newborns. The 4 newborns with unavailable birthweight were all term. Birthweight distribution is shown in Table 1.

Patient Characteristics of Newborns With E. coli Bacteremia

Delivery and Newborn Clinical Characteristics

Most newborns were delivered vaginally (Table 1). Duration of rupture of membranes before delivery was recorded in 28 patients, and ranged from 0 to 68 hours, with a median of 4 hours and interquartile range (IQR) of 9.2 hours. Prolonged rupture of membranes (≥ 18 hours) was observed in 4 (14%) of these deliveries.

Congenital anomalies were present in 24 newborns (44%) (Table 1). For a complete list of congenital anomalies and additional clinical characteristics, refer to Supplementary Table 1

Clinical Factors Associated With Bacteremia Episode

Median age of bacteremia onset was 13 days old (IQR 1 day). Overall, n = 7 (13%) newborns had sepsis onset at age ≤ 72 hours old and were classified as early-onset sepsis (EOS) cases. Among the 7 neonates with EOS, 4 were born preterm (born < 37 weeks of gestation). More than half of the newborns required treatment in the intensive care unit (30 of 54, 55.5%), including all the 16 preterm babies in the study, whereas only 14 of the 38 term newborns required intensive care (P < .0001).

Regarding complications associated with E. coli bacteremia, CSF was collected to evaluate meningitis in 43 (79.6%) neonates. N = 12 (28%) were treated for E. coli meningitis, including 5 with positive CSF cultures (4 of these CSF isolates were characterized in this study), 1 with a CSF polymerase chain reaction demonstrating E. coli, and 6 who were treated for meningitis despite negative microbiologic CSF testing (these 6 infants received antibiotics before CSF sampling). Two newborns with positive CSF cultures died, both were born prematurely and had late-onset sepsis (LOS). Of the 11 newborns without CSF collected, 6 received empiric antibiotic treatment for meningitis.

Necrotizing enterocolitis occurred in 2 newborns, both born preterm, representing a necrotizing enterocolitis rate of 12.5% among the preterm infants in our study.

Among all 54 newborns, 5 (9.3%) died: n = 3 were born preterm versus n = 2 born term. Mortality rate was 19% among preterm newborns versus 5% among term newborns (P = .15) The mean number of days between blood culture collection and death was 3 days (standard deviation ± 2.7).

The median length of hospital stay was 13.5 days (IQR 34 days); median length of stay of only those who survived was 19 days (IQR 38 days). A summary of selected clinical characteristics of all newborns is presented in Supplementary Table 1.

Antibiotic Susceptibility Trends

Among all isolates, 54% were nonsusceptible to ampicillin and 11% were nonsusceptible to gentamicin. Six isolates (11%) were nonsusceptible to both ampicillin and gentamicin. Nonsusceptibility rates to these antibiotics did not significantly change between the first study period (years 2006–2013) and the latter years of the study (2014–2021) (Figure 1). One isolate in the second period of the study was nonsusceptible to amikacin. We did observe a significant increase in cefazolin nonsusceptibility from 8% to 36% (P < .03).

Antibiotic nonsusceptibility rates in neonatal E. coli bacteremia isolates. A significant increase in cefazolin nonsusceptibility rates was observed over time (*P < .03).

A total of 17 isolates (31%) were MDR, n = 5 during the first part of the study and n = 12 in the second period (P = .08). Overall, 3 (5.5%) isolates showed ESBL production (Table 2). We found no carbapenem resistance.

Whole-genome Sequencing- Based Classification by MSLT and Capsule Genes Typing

To study the molecular characteristics of the invasive neonatal E. coli strains, WGS was performed to determine their MLST classification and the presence of capsule genes. WGS data analyses showed that the strains belonged to multiple STs. The most prevalent ST overall was ST95 (17%), followed by ST69 (11%), ST 131 (7%), ST12 (7%), and ST404 (7%). ST1193 and ST405 were present each in 6% of isolates. Other STs were found at a lower prevalence, with ≤ 2 (≤ 3.7%) isolates in each MLST group (Figure 2A and Supplementary table 2). Changes in the proportion of MLST distribution among the strains over the 2 study periods are shown in Figure 2B and 2C, which highlight STs with shifts in proportion over time. Notably, ST95 strains became less prevalent, whereas the frequency of other STs increased, including ST69 (from 0% to 21.4%, P < .03) and ST1193 (from 0% to 11%). The distribution of each ST was no different between isolates causing EOS versus LOS.

Multilocus sequence type distribution of neonatal E. coli bacteremia isolates. (A) Overall prevalence of sequence types (STs) among all strains. (B, C) Changes in the distribution of STs between the 2 study periods.

To determine the distribution and relationships of STs among the strains, an MLST-based phylogenetic tree was constructed. The alleles for the 7 housekeeping genes, adk, fumC, gyrB, icd, mdh, purA, and recA, of each strain were concatenated and aligned using CLUSTALW. The phylogenetic tree shown in Figure 3 highlights 6 of the most common STs identified. Clustered with the prevalent ST95 strains, the tree shows the only ST2619 strain, SCB71. ST2619 has been reported as a novel ST within the ST95 complex of strains causing extraintestinal invasive E. coli infections [21]. The genomic sequences of all alleles from the 7 genes in each strain used for MLST classification and phylogenetic tree construction are included in Supplementary File 1.

Multilocus sequence type–based phylogenetic tree of neonatal E. coli bacteremia isolates. The names of the most common sequence types among neonatal invasive strains are indicated.

Given the importance of the capsule in the pathogenesis of neonatal invasive E. coli disease, the presence of genes associated to capsule type in each strain was studied. The K1 capsule genes were present in 43% of all isolates; K5 capsule genes were present in 20%. Additional capsule types detected included K4, K23, K52 and K96, which were found each in 2%–5% of strains. These capsule types belong to group 2 capsules (bearing kpsM II alleles), except for type K96, which belongs to capsule group 3 (kpsM III) [22] (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Among the remaining strains, 7 had kpsM II genes without a specific type identified, and no kps genes were identified in 6 strains (Supplementary Fig. 1B). In the subgroup of isolates detected in CSF (n = 6), the K1 capsule genes were found in 67%. There was no difference in the prevalence of K1 capsule genes in strains that caused EOS versus LOS, or in those infecting newborns that survived versus those who did not.

DISCUSSION

This study presents recent data on the clinical characteristics of neonates with E. coli sepsis, and the molecular traits of the causative strains. This is the largest retrospective study of neonatal E. coli invasive strains in the United States examining antibiotic resistance and applying WGS data analyses to characterize relevant bacterial genomic properties.

The clinical features of the newborns included are characteristic of neonatal E. coli sepsis. In this sample, 30% of neonates were preterm, compared with a 10% preterm birth rate in the United States, indicating overrepresentation of preterm infants in cases of neonatal E. coli sepsis as it has been seen in other studies [1, 2, 23].

E. coli meningitis is a major concern in newborns with E. coli bacteremia. In this study, 80% of neonates received a lumbar puncture, with 14% showing E. coli in CSF. E. coli meningitis rates in neonatal E. coli sepsis are not well defined in the United States, where only 30%–66% of these very ill infants undergo lumbar punctures, likely contributing to underestimating meningitis incidence [2, 24, 25]. Considering the possibility of concurrent meningitis when choosing empiric treatment for neonatal E. coli sepsis based on up-to-date resistance data is important, and obtaining a CSF sample in every case is crucial. In this study, mortality E. coli occurred predominantly in preterm newborns, which is consistent with data from high-income countries and underscores the need for close monitoring of antibiotic resistance to improve empiric treatment strategies, particularly in this population [23, 26, 27].

Widespread antibiotic resistance among neonatal E. coli strains is of major concern. This study demonstrated an overall 54% nonsusceptibility rate to ampicillin, with an increase between the study periods. In the United States, and other high-income countries such as France, recent studies reported similar nonsusceptibility rates to ampicillin or amoxicillin between 67% and 74% [1, 23, 28]; greater nonsusceptibility rates up to 85% are reported in China [29]. In this study, nonsusceptibility rates to gentamicin were similar to those reported in the United States and France [1, 28]. In contrast, greater gentamicin resistance rates of 20%–30% are reported in China [29, 12]. Regarding amikacin, our study demonstrates a 1.8% nonsusceptibility rate, similar to data from the United States and China [1, 12, 29]. However, in India, amikacin nonsusceptibility rate is up to 26% [30]. Continued monitoring of E. coli resistance to the most commonly used antibiotics in neonates in the United States and abroad is crucial because such data should guide selection of the most appropriate empiric antibiotics against neonatal sepsis.

This study demonstrates for the first time in the United States a statistically significant increase in cefazolin nonsusceptibility. Cefazolin is not indicated for empiric treatment against neonatal sepsis, but monitoring resistance trends is important given its increased use as alternative therapy in newborns [31, 32]. Regarding third-generation cephalosporins, previous US data showed resistance rates of 2%–5% [1, 9, 10], which concurs with our overall findings. However, we did observe an increase in ceftriaxone nonsusceptibility between our 2 study periods. In France, third-generation cephalosporin resistance is already higher at 14.5%, and even greater in China, up to 40%–60% [12, 28, 29, 33]. We also found that 5.5% of neonatal E. coli isolates showed ESBL production, similar to another US study [1]. Higher ESBL production rates in neonatal E. coli sepsis isolates have been found in France (14%) and China (up to 32%) [28] [34]. We found no resistance to carbapenems; however, in non-US regions such as China, resistance to meropenem was 3% [12], and as high as 26% in India [30,35]. Close surveillance of resistance to cephalosporins and carbapenems is necessary, given their importance as alternative agents against neonatal sepsis.

Current empiric treatment for neonatal sepsis in the United States is ampicillin plus gentamicin [36]. In our study, 11% of neonatal E. coli bacteremia isolates were nonsusceptible to both antibiotics. A previous study found no increased mortality risk in newborns with EOS caused by E. coli resistant to both ampicillin and gentamicin, which was present in 10% of the isolates reported [1]. Studies in the United States to date have been small, and a threshold resistance rate to these 2 antibiotics that would warrant use of an alternative regimen is uncertain. Empiric antibiotic therapy with broad-spectrum β-lactams, such as third- or fourth-generation cephalosporins or carbapenems, is a consideration in premature newborns at increased risk for severe infection [37], but indiscriminate use of broader empiric therapies is associated to rapid development of resistance and adverse outcomes [38–41]. Using amikacin in empiric regimens against neonatal sepsis is an option that remains viable in the United States, and is used for treatment of EOS and LOS in other high-income countries [42]. Ongoing surveillance of resistance trends in larger US populations is essential to inform clinicians in selecting effective empiric regimens for neonatal sepsis.

This study also presents novel data on the molecular epidemiology of neonatal E. coli bacteremia strains, which has been studied only in limited detail in the United States. We have previously demonstrated a predominance of ST95, ST131, and ST1193 among invasive strains in earlier years [8, 9]. The current study, which includes recent strains from the Midwest United States, provides further detail of molecular epidemiologic trends demonstrating greater diversity among invasive neonatal E. coli strains. ST95 strains have persisted for decades as a cause of invasive neonatal E. coli infections, particularly meningitis, and are typically not MDR [43, 44]. The increasing prevalence trend of ST69 and ST1193 strains is relevant, given their rising predominance as a cause of invasive disease, and the rapid expansion of MDR ST1193 strains worldwide [11, 45, 46]. ST69 strains are common among cases of adult septicemia and invasive neonatal infections in other countries, and are not commonly MDR, although some recent studies have reported the emergence of ESBL producers among them [47–51]. ST1193 strains dominate among neonatal invasive isolates that cause sepsis and meningitis in other countries [12, 52–54]. In contrast to ST95 and ST69 strains, ST1193 strains are commonly MDR, with 92% showing resistance to amoxicillin, 42% to gentamicin, and up to 33% harboring EBSL genes [12, 53, 55]. Of note, our study shows that the prevalence of ST131, also strongly associated with an MDR phenotype, did not increase in recent years. ST131 strains predominate among neonatal septicemia cases in India and other countries, where they have been associated to mortality as high as 40% [44, 56, 57]. ST131 strains are commonly ESBL producers and are also highly resistant to other antibiotics including fluroquinolones, aminoglycosides, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole [57]. In China, ST131 neonatal invasive strains showed 67% resistance to amoxicillin and 100% to gentamicin [53]. Monitoring trends of invasive E. coli clones that are associated with specific virulence potential and antibiotic resistance properties is relevant in choosing appropriate preventative and treatment strategies against neonatal E. coli sepsis.

We found a prevalence of K1 capsule genes in 43% of all neonatal E. coli bacteremia isolates, which is in contrast to the ∼80% prevalence in neonatal meningitis isolates traditionally reported [58, 54]. Smaller studies of neonatal E. coli bacteremia showed a K1 capsule prevalence of 53% in United States and of 59% in France [10, 52]. The K1 capsule belongs to group 2 capsules, found in strains carrying the kpsM II gene, which include other K capsules of strains causing severe invasive infections [43]. The lower prevalence of K1 capsule in our collection may be related to the emergence of strains carrying other group 2 capsules, such as ST131 and ST1193 strains, which can have K1 or K5 capsules [59]. Interestingly, we did find evidence of the K1 capsule in 67% of CSF isolates, which highlights the more specific role of the K1 capsule in the pathogenesis of neonatal meningitis [60]. This information is clinically relevant given that available polymerase chain reaction–based tests that detect E. coli in the CSF identify only K1 strains. Further understanding of the epidemiology of K antigens among invasive neonatal E. coli strains is crucial to elucidate the role of specific capsule structures and functions in pathogenesis, and as potential diagnostic and therapeutic targets.

A limitation of this study is its retrospective nature, which limits the extent of clinical data available for analyses. This includes the absence of MIC data for the antibiotics tested. Despite this, the information retrieved allows meaningful interpretation of several relevant clinical endpoints, including important antibiotic resistance data of a large collection of E. coli strains from bacteremic neonates. Moreover, the current study provides novel information on the molecular epidemiology shifts of these invasive strains. Another caveat of the data presented is the study of isolates within a single center in the United States, which limits generalization of the results to other populations. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to monitor antibiotic resistance trends in neonatal E. coli invasive strains, and we have launched a collaborative project to do so [61]. These efforts are essential to tailor effective therapies against neonatal sepsis. Understanding genomic traits and molecular epidemiology trends of neonatal E. coli invasive strains is also crucial to develop novel preventative and treatment strategies that are urgently needed.
