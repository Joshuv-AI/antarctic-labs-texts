Vasomotor symptoms (VMS), including hot flashes and night sweats, affect 15% to 80% of people during perimenopause and menopause, and can impair sleep, daily functioning, mood, and quality of life.1–3 Menopausal hormone therapy remains the gold-standard treatment for VMS but is contraindicated for patients with hormone-sensitive breast cancer who are on endocrine therapy, and for patients with breast cancer experiencing VMS due to spontaneous or iatrogenic perimenopause or menopause.1,4 People with hormone-sensitive breast cancer may be on endocrine therapy, such as tamoxifen or aromatase inhibitors, for 5 to 10 years as adjuvant treatment after undergoing surgery. Nonhormonal alternatives — such as off-label selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), gabapentin, clonidine, and oxybutynin — exist, but their efficacy is modest and limited by adverse effects.1 Neurokinin-3 (NK3) receptor antagonists have emerged as a targeted option for patients with VMS.

What are NK3 receptor antagonists and in whom are they used?

Neurokinin-3 receptor antagonists are oral, nonhormonal therapies for VMS that target hypothalamic thermoregulatory pathways involved in the generation of hot flashes. States of estrogen deficiency (i.e., during menopause or endocrine therapy) narrow the thermoregulatory zone through hyperactivity of kisspeptin–neurokinin B–dynorphin (KNDy) neurons, resulting in increased neurokinin B and substance P signalling. Neurokinin B activates NK3 receptors on KNDy neurons and thermoregulatory neurons. These mechanisms allow small changes in core temperature and trigger flushing, sweating, and vasodilation. Substance P signalling through neurokinin-1 (NK1) receptors results in peripheral vasodilation and sleep disturbance. Neurokinin-3 receptor antagonists modulate these pathways to reduce the frequency and severity of hot flashes.1,2

The 2 agents currently approved by Health Canada are fezolinetant, a selective NK3 receptor antagonist, approved in 2024, and elinzanetant, a dual NK1 and NK3 receptor antagonist approved in 2025 (Table 1).5–8 Both agents were approved for the treatment of moderate-to-severe VMS associated with menopause. These agents can be prescribed and managed by primary care providers.1,9

Neurokinin-3 receptor antagonists are efficacious for patients who cannot or prefer not to use menopausal hormone therapy, such as patients with hormone-sensitive breast cancer on endocrine therapy.1,7 Vasomotor symptoms affect 78% to 93% of patients receiving endocrine therapy for breast cancer and can contribute to rates of treatment discontinuation exceeding 20%, with negative consequences for risk of cancer recurrence and survival. Existing nonhormonal options (SSRIs, SNRIs, gabapentin, and oxybutynin) are limited by incomplete symptom control and adverse effect profiles.1,10

Elinzanetant is the only NK3 receptor antagonist to date with phase III evidence in patients with hormone-sensitive breast cancer who are receiving endocrine therapy. Lke venlafaxine and gabapentin, it is an efficacious nonhormonal option; selection is guided by patient-specific factors (e.g., tolerability, concomitant medications, symptom profile).1,11 Fezolinetant is not contraindicated for patients with breast cancer with symptoms related to endocrine therapy;9 however, its use remains off label while its application is actively being studied in this population.

What is the evidence for their benefit?

Other nonhormonal therapies (SSRIs, SNRIs, gabapentin, clonidine and oxybutynin) for VMS have been shown to reduce the frequency of hot flashes by 38% to 58% in patients with breast cancer, with a placebo response ranging from 15% to 50%.1

Evidence supporting NK3 receptor antagonists for VMS is grounded in multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving postmenopausal women with 7 or more hot flashes daily.5–7,9 In an RCT comparing the frequency of VMS in 527 participants (175 on placebo, 176 on fezolinetant 30 mg, 176 on fezolinetant 45 mg), those receiving 30 mg or 45 mg of fezolinetant had a placebo-adjusted frequency of 2.39 (standard error [SE] 0.44) or 2.55 (SE 0.43) fewer events per day at 12 weeks than at baseline, respectively (p < 0.001).5 A 52-week RCT safety study of 1830 participants — whereby 610 received placebo, 611 received fezolinetant 30 mg and 609 received fezolinetant 45 mg — showed that the frequency of treatment-emergent adverse effects was 64.1%, 67.9%, and 63.9%, respectively, with no significant differences identified. In an RCT of 400 participants, whereby 200 received placebo and 200 received elinzanetant 120 mg daily, those receiving elinzanetant had 3.2 fewer (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.9 to 4.6, p < 0.001) events per day at 12 weeks than at baseline than those receiving placebo.5,9

Among 474 women (aged 18 to 70 yr) with moderate-to-severe VMS associated with endocrine therapy for hormone-sensitive breast cancer or its prevention enrolled in an RCT, 158 received placebo for 12 weeks followed by elinzanetant 120 mg daily, while 316 received elinzanetant 120 mg upfront. At 12 weeks, the frequency of VMS decreased by 4.2 (95% CI 3.2 to 5.2) events per day in the placebo group and 7.8 (95% CI 7.1 to 8.5) events per day in the elinzanetant group (p < 0.001).7,9 Breast cancer–specific outcomes were not reported. The efficacy of switching between NK receptor antagonists after an inadequate response has not been studied.

Beyond symptomatic impact, frequent or severe VMS have been associated with cardiovascular risk markers and bone loss, although causality remains uncertain. These associations may reflect direct physiologic effects of VMS, including repeated cortisol surges, sympathetic activation, oxidative stress, and nocturnal nondipping blood pressure, as well as lower circulating estrogen, which independently affects cardiovascular and skeletal health.1,2,4 Unlike menopausal hormone therapy, neither fezolinetant nor elinzanetant has demonstrated an effect on bone mineral density or trabecular bone score, and the impact of these agents on cardiovascular risk or thromboembolic outcomes has not yet been evaluated.5–7

What are the harms of these medications?

Neurokinin-3 receptor antagonists have demonstrated favourable short-term safety profiles, with most adverse effects being mild to moderate. Commonly reported adverse events with fezolinetant include abdominal pain, diarrhea, insomnia, and back pain. Elinzanetant is generally well tolerated, with adverse effects predominantly including headache and prominent central nervous system symptoms. Somnolence, fatigue, vertigo, dizziness, and presyncope occurred in 11.9% of elinzanetant-treated patients compared with 3.5% receiving placebo.6,7,9 These effects may relate to elinzanetant’s NK1 receptor activity; patients should be advised to take elinzanetant at bedtime and to avoid driving or hazardous activities until adverse effects resolve.9,11 Rare events — including pulmonary embolism, transient ischemic attack, and myocardial infarction — have been reported in RCTs of both agents, but occurred at low frequency, at similar rates to placebo groups and without established causality.6,7,9

Hepatic safety is an important consideration with fezolinetant. Elevated transaminase more than 3 times the upper limit of normal occurred in approximately 2.3% of patients, compared with 0.9% in those taking placebo.5,9 Postmarketing cases of drug-induced liver injury with jaundice have also been reported. Health Canada recommends hepatic laboratory testing monthly for the first 3 months and then at 6 and 9 months after initiation of therapy. Fezolinetant is contraindicated in patients with cirrhosis, severe renal impairment, or end-stage renal disease, or concomitant use of cytochrome P450 (CYP) 1A2 inhibitors (such as fluvoxamine and ciprofloxacin).8,9

Elinzanetant has not demonstrated a meaningful risk to hepatic safety. Clinical trials found elevated liver enzymes in 1.2% of participants treated with elinzanetant, compared with 1.0% of those on placebo.6,7,9 The independent liver safety monitoring board concluded elinzanetant did not result in a hepatotoxic signal, and routine serial liver monitoring is not recommended by Health Canada.8

Unlike fezolinetant, which is metabolized by CYP1A2, elinzanetant is metabolized by CYP3A4. Concomitant use of elinzanetant with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (such as ritonavir, cobicistat, itraconazole, and clarithromycin) should be avoided, and the dose should be reduced to 60 mg daily when used alongside moderate inhibitors (such as ciprofloxacin, fluconazole, verapamil, grapefruit juice, and other CYP3A4-inhibiting foods). Strong and moderate CYP3A4 inducers such as carbamazepine should also be avoided. As a weak CYP3A4 inhibitor itself, elinzanetant may increase exposure to other CYP3A4 substrates. Elinzanetant should be used with caution in patients with a history of seizures or conditions that lower the seizure threshold, as seizure was reported in 1 patient.1,2,9

What are the resource implications?

Cost and coverage are the most substantial barriers to uptake of NK3 receptor antagonists in Canada. Fezolinetant and elinzanetant are not currently listed on provincial public drug formularies. Access requires private insurance coverage or out-of-pocket payment. The retail price for elinazetant is $165 per month. Fezolinetant ranges from $180 to $190 per month.

Hepatic monitoring for fezolinetant adds further resource considerations. These requirements increase health care utilization and may temper usage, given the added cost and inconvenience of serial laboratory monitoring.2,8

What can be expected in the foreseeable future?

Neurokinin-3 receptor antagonists represent an advance in the nonhormonal management of VMS. Their role will likely expand as evidence accumulates and clinical experience grows. Ongoing studies such as the FLASH-Breast trial12 are evaluating fezolinetant in people with stage I to III hormone-sensitive breast cancer who are receiving endocrine therapy.5

As access to fezolinetant and elinzanetant grows, postmarketing studies can be undertaken to evaluate the effects of these agents on bone health, long-term hepatic and cardiovascular safety, and breast cancer recurrence. Studies using surrogate cardiovascular markers, including lipid profiles, endothelial function, and blood pressure, could further investigate connections between symptom improvement and broader long-term health effects.4
