A Banach space with L L -orthogonal sequences but without L L -orthogonal elements
Antonio Avilés, Gonzalo Martínez-Cervantes, Alejandro Poveda, Luis Sáenz

Abstract
. We prove that the existence of Banach spaces with L L -orthogonal sequences but without L L -orthogonal elements is independent of the standard foundation of Mathematics, ZFC. This provides a definitive answer to [ AMCRZ23 , Question 1.1] . Generalizing classical Q Q -point ultrafilters, we introduce the notion of Q Q -measures and provide several results generalizing former theorems by Miller [ Mil80 ] and Bartoszynski [ Bar95 ] for Q Q -point ultrafilters.

Primary 03E35, 46B04, 54A20, 54A35; Secondary: 46B03, 57N17, 03E75, 54D80.

1.
Introduction

The presence of isomorphic copies of
\ell_{1}
in a Banach space and their relation to orthogonality conditions in the corresponding bidual has been a central topic of research in Banach space theory. The theory was developed by G. Godefroy, N. J. Kalton, and B. Maurey, among others top-notch experts, in the late 1980s. A fundamental result due to Godefroy
[
God89
, Theorem II.4]
states that a Banach space
X
contains an isomorphic copy of
\ell_{1}
if, and only if,
X
admits an equivalent renorming with an element
x^{**}\in X^{**}
such that for every
x\in X
,

\|x+x^{**}\|=1+\|x\|.

These elements are termed
L-orthogonal
as per the notation in
[
LPZ21
]
.

A similar characterization given by V. Kadets, V. Shepelska, and D. Werner
[
KSW11
, Theorem 4.3]
establishes that a Banach space
X
contains an isomorphic copy of
\ell_{1}
if, and only if, there exists an equivalent renorming of
X
with a sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\omega}
in its unit ball such that for every
x\in X

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|x+x_{n}\|=1+\|x\|.

Sequences with this property are termed
L
-orthogonal
in
[
AMCRZ23
]
.
For an
L
-orthogonal sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\omega}
, the function

\tau(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|x+x_{n}\|

defines a type of Maurey satisfying
\tau(x)=1+\|x\|
for every
x\in X

[
Mau82
]
. Accordingly,
L
-orthogonal sequences are called
canonical
\ell_{1}
-types
in
[
KSW11
]
.

Examples of nonseparable Banach spaces with
L
-orthogonal elements but with no
L
-orthogonal sequences are known. Whether there is a Banach space with
L
-orthogonal sequences yet without
L
-orthogonal elements in its double dual was asked in
[
AMCRZ23
]
. In the mentioned paper, the authors provide a positive answer under the existence of selective ultrafilters on the set of natural numbers,
\omega
. The existence of selective ultrafilters on
\omega
is consistent with the ZFC axioms –for instance, selective ultrafilters exist under the Continuum Hypothesis– and so is its negation
[
Kun71
]
.

The above-mentioned result was later improved in
[
HS24
]
by the last author and Hrušák, who showed that a free ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
is a
Q
-point if, and only if, for every Banach space
X
and every
L
-orthogonal sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\omega}
in
X
,
\mathcal{U}
-
\lim x_{n}\in X^{**}
is an
L
-orthogonal element. (
\mathcal{U}
-
\lim x_{n}
is the standard notation for the limit, under the ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
, in the weak*-topology of the sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\omega}
.) In effect, this refines the result of
[
AMCRZ23
]
in that every selective ultrafilter is a
Q
-point.

In
[
AMCRZ23
]
, the authors left open the question of whether, consistently, there exists a Banach space with
L
-orthogonal sequences but without
L
-orthogonal elements. This manuscript settles this question affirmatively:

Theorem 1

(Independence of
L
-elements)
.

The existence of a Banach space with an
L
-orthogonal sequence but without
L
-orthogonal elements in its bidual is independent of the
\mathrm{ZFC}
axioms.

The key idea behind this result is the generalization of the notion of
Q
-point to measures, something already considered in
[
Ś24
]
, and for
P
-measures in
[
BNS23
,
BNCMM24
]
. Remember that
\mathcal{U}
is a
Q
-point if for every partition of
\omega
into finite sets there is a set in
\mathcal{U}
that is a selector for the partition, that is, a set that intersects each set of the partition in at most one point. There are several ways to extend this definition to measures, the most relevant for us are the following:

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\mu:\mathcal{P}(\omega)\longrightarrow[0,+\infty)
be a finitely additive measure defined on the subsets of
\omega
and vanishing on finite sets.

(1)

\mu
is a
Q^{+}(\omega)
-measure
if every partition of
\omega
into finite sets has a selector of positive measure.

(2)

\mu
is a
fit
Q
-measure
if for every partition of
\omega
into finite sets and every
\delta<\frac{1}{2}
there is a finite union of selectors of measure greater than
\delta\cdot\mu(\omega)
.

(3)

\mu
is a
strong
Q
-measure
if every partition of
\omega
into finite sets has a selector of full measure.

It is immediate that the Dirac measure of a
Q
-point is a strong
Q
-measure. Moreover, every strong
Q
-measure is a fit
Q
-measure and every fit
Q
-measure is a
Q^{+}(\omega)
-measure.

Theorem
1
follows from the combination of the following two facts.

Theorem 1.2
.

It is consistent with ZFC that there are no
Q^{+}(\omega)
-measures.

Theorem 1.3
.

If no fit
Q
-measures exist, then there is a Banach space with an
L
-orthogonal sequence and no
L
-orthogonal elements.

The first part of this paper (§
3
) is a purely set-theoretic investigation. In the first place in §
3.1
we make sure that these generalizations of
Q
-points are meaningful. Extending classical results in the area (see
[
Bar95
, Theorem 4.6.6]
), we prove that
\mathfrak{d}=cov(\mathcal{M})
is equivalent to the fact that every atomless measure of density less than
\mathfrak{d}
extends to a strong
Q
-measure. So in some models these objects are indeed abundant. Then, in §
3.2
we prove Theorem
1.2
. We generalize the proof that there are no
Q
-points in Laver’s model
[
Mil80
]
, which applies to other models obtained by iterated forcing as well. A more general statement is that no
\sigma
-bounded-cc ideal is rapid in these models.

The second part of the paper (§
4
) contains the proof of Theorem
1.3
. This section focuses more on Banach spaces and less on set theory. We adapt the proof from
[
AMCRZ23
]
that if no
Q
-points exist, then an
L
-orthogonal sequence can be constructed with no
L
-orthogonal elements in its weak
∗
-closure. There are two steps: First we prove that any measure
\mu:\mathcal{P}(\omega)\to[-1,1]
whose positive and negative parts are not fit
Q
-measures yields a Banach space
X_{\mu}
with an
L
-orthogonal sequence
(x_{n})_{n\in\omega}\subseteq X_{\mu}
such that
T^{**}(\mu)
is not an
L
-orthogonal element of
X_{\mu}
. (Here
T\colon\ell_{1}\rightarrow X
is the operator defined as
T(e_{n}):=x_{n}
for
n\in\omega
.) This is accomplished in §
4.2
. Afterwards, in §
4.3
, we amalgamate all of these spaces
X_{\mu}
into a single one that serves as a witness for Theorem
1.3
. In addition to this proof, there is also in §
4.1
a generalization of a result from
[
HS24
]
that goes in the converse direction: for an
L
-orthogonal sequence,
T^{**}(\mu)
is always an
L
-orthogonal element if
\mu
is a strong
Q
-measure.

The paper is concluded with Section
5
by presenting a few open questions. Our notations are standard both in Set Theory and Banach Space Theory. Relevant preliminaries will be also provided next in §
2
.

2.
Preliminaries

As it is customary in Set Theory we denote by
\omega
the set of natural numbers.
A set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\omega)
is called a
filter
if
\emptyset\notin\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}
is closed under taking supersets and finite intersections. We shall assume from the outset that all of our filters contain the complements of finite subsets of
\omega
.

Ideals provide the dual notion of a filter. Specifically,
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\omega)
is an
ideal
if, and only if,
\mathcal{I}^{*}=\{\omega\setminus I\mathrel{|}\allowbreak I\in\mathcal{I}\}
is a filter; to wit,
\omega\notin\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{I}
contains all finite subsets of
\omega
and it is closed under finite unions and under taking subsets. The collection of
\mathcal{I}
-positive subsets of
\omega
is

\mathcal{I}^{+}:=\{J\mathrel{|}\allowbreak J\notin\mathcal{I}\}.

The
restriction
of an ideal (resp. a filter) to a set
X\subseteq\omega
is

\mathcal{I}\lvert_{X}:=\{I\cap X:I\in\mathcal{I}\}.

Maximal filters (also known as
ultrafilters
) are of paramount importance. An ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathscr{P}(\omega)
is called a
Q
-point
if for every partition
\{F_{n}\mathrel{|}\allowbreak n\in\omega\}
of
\omega
into finite sets there exists
X\in\mathcal{U}
such that
\lvert X\cap F_{n}\lvert\leq 1
for all
n\in\omega
. More generally (and dually), we call an ideal
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\omega)
a
Q^{+}(\omega)
-ideal
if for every partition
\{F_{n}\mathrel{|}\allowbreak n\in\omega\}
of
\omega
into finite sets there exists
X\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
such that
\lvert X\cap F_{n}\lvert\leq 1
for all
n\in\omega
. A natural extension of
Q^{+}(\omega)
-ideals are rapid ones. An ideal
\mathcal{I}
is rapid
if for every partition
\{F_{n}\mathrel{|}\allowbreak n<\omega\}
of
\omega
into finite sets there is
X\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
such that, for each
n<\omega
,
|X\cap F_{n}|\leq n
.

Observe that in all the definitions above, it is equivalent to say “for every partition into finite sets” and “for every partition into finite intervals”. To see this, if
\mathcal{I}
satisfies any of this notions for partitions into finite intervals and you have a general partition
\{F_{n}\}
into finite sets, consider the partition
\{[m_{n},m_{n+1})\}
into intervals where

m_{n+1}=\max\left(F_{2n+2}\cup\bigcup\{F_{k}:\min(F_{k})<m_{n}\}\right).

Notice that if
X\in\mathcal{I}^{+}
with
|X\cap[m_{n},m_{n+1})|\leq 1
for all
n
, then
|X\cap F_{n}|\leq 2
for all
n
, but then
X
can be divided into two pieces, one of which is in
\mathcal{I}^{+}
.

In what follows all Boolean algebras
\mathbb{B}
are considered to be subalgebras of
(\mathcal{P}(\omega),\subseteq,\cup,\cap,\setminus)
and all of our measures on
\mathbb{B}
will be assumed to be finitely-additive, of bounded variation and vanishing on all finite sets.
For the record, recall that
\mu
is called
atomless
if given any
A\in\mathbb{B}
with
\mu(A)>0
there is
B\subseteq A
such that
0<\mu(B)<\mu(A)
.

Definition 2.1
.

For
\varepsilon>0
, a measure
\mu\colon\mathbb{B}\to[0,1]
is called an
\varepsilon
-strong
Q
-measure
if for any
\{A_{n}\mathrel{|}\allowbreak n\in\omega\}
partition of
\omega
into finite sets there is
A\subseteq\omega
such that
\mu(A)\geq\varepsilon
and
\lvert A_{n}\cap A\lvert\leq 1
for any
n\in\omega
. Those measures that are
\mu(\omega)
-strong
Q
-measures will be referred just as strong
Q
-measures.

Remark 2.2
.

Notice that any
Q
-point naturally defines a strong
Q
-measure.

Measures induce ideals in a natural way:

Definition 2.3
.

An ideal
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\omega)
is called a
measure ideal
(on
\mathbb{B}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\omega)
) if there is a measure
\mu\colon\mathbb{B}\to[0,1]
such that
\mathcal{I}=\Null(\mu)=\ker(\mu)=\{A\in\mathbb{B}\mathrel{|}\allowbreak\mu(A)=0\}
. We say that

\mu
is a
Q^{+}(\omega)
-measure
if
\ker(\mu)
is a
Q^{+}(\omega)
-ideal.

The following notation will be used at several places in this paper:

Notation 2.4
.

Consider
(x_{n})_{n\in\omega}
a sequence in a Banach space
X
, and a measure of bounded variation
\mu\colon\mathcal{P}(\omega)\to\mathbb{R}
. Let
T\colon\ell_{1}\to X
be defined as
T(e_{n}):=x_{n}
for
n\in\omega
. Define the
\mu
-limit of
(x_{n})_{n\in\omega}
as

\mu\text{-}\lim x_{n}:=T^{**}(\mu).

Here
T^{**}\colon\ell_{1}^{**}\rightarrow X^{**}
and remember that
\ell_{1}^{**}=\ell_{\infty}^{*}
is naturally identified with the set of finitely additive signed finite measures on
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
. In the case where
\mu
stems from an ultrafilter
\mathcal{U}
this coincides with the classical
\mathcal{U}
-limit.

We consider
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
equipped with the natural topology inherited from the product topology of
2^{\omega}=\{f\lvert f:\omega\to\{0,1\}\}
via characteristic functions. At any point we say that a subset of
\mathcal{P}(\omega)
has certain topological property (e.g., closed, Borel, or analytic) we shall be referring to this underlying topology.

In what follows we will consider several
cardinal invariants
. A family
\mathcal{D}
of functions in
\omega^{\omega}=\{f\lvert f:\omega\to\omega\}
is called
dominating
if for every
f\in\omega^{\omega}
there is
g\in\mathcal{D}
such that
\{n\in\omega\mathrel{|}\allowbreak g(n)\leq f(n)\}
is finite. The cardinal
\mathfrak{d}
, known as the
dominating number
, is defined as follows:

\mathfrak{d}=\min\{\lvert\mathcal{D}\lvert:\mathcal{D}\subseteq\omega^{\omega}\text{ is a dominating family}\}.

The dominating number
\mathfrak{d}
admits several characterizations – we will exploit a classical one, cf.
[
Bla10
]
. Given partitions
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{n}:n\in\omega\}
and
\mathcal{Q}=\{Q_{n}:n\in\omega\}
of
\omega
into finite intervals, we say that
\mathcal{P}

dominates

\mathcal{Q}
(in symbols,
\mathcal{P}\geq\mathcal{Q}
) if for all but finitely many
n\in\omega
there is
m\in\omega
such that
Q_{m}\subseteq P_{n}
. Denote the set of interval partitions of
\omega
as
\Part
. A family
\mathcal{D}
is
dominant in
\Part
if it is dominant in the order just introduced, meaning that for any
\mathcal{Q}\in\Part
there is
\mathcal{P}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
\mathcal{Q}\leq\mathcal{P}
. It is a classical result that the least cardinality of such a dominating family is
\mathfrak{d}

[
Bla10
]
.

Another central cardinal invariant is
\cov(\mathcal{M})
. A family
\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{\omega}
is a covering family of meager sets if each set in
\mathcal{C}
is meager and
\bigcup\mathcal{C}=2^{\omega}
. The covering number
\cov(\mathcal{M})
is defined as the smallest cardinal of a covering family of meager sets; namely,

\cov(\mathcal{M})=\min\{\lvert\mathcal{C}\lvert:\mathcal{C}\subseteq 2^{\omega}\text{ is a covering family of meager sets}\}.

A
reaping family

\mathcal{R}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\omega)
is a family such that for any
A\in\mathcal{P}(\omega)
there is
R\in\mathcal{R}
such that
R\cap A
or
A^{c}\cap R
is finite. The least size of such a family is
\mathfrak{r}
, and it is known that
\cov(\mathcal{M})\leq\mathfrak{r}
, see
[
Bla10
]
.

3.
On
Q^{+}(\omega)
-measures.

3.1.
Models with many strong
Q
-measures.

A classical result in cardinal invariant theory asserts that any filter
\mathscr{F}
of character less that
\mathfrak{d}
can be extended to a
Q
-point if, and only if,
\cov(\mathcal{M})=\mathfrak{d}

[
Bar95
, Theorem 4.6.6]
. Recall that if
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathscr{B}
are families of sets, we say that
\mathscr{A}
is dense in
\mathscr{B}
if for any
B\in\mathscr{B}
there is
A\in\mathscr{A}
such that
A\subseteq B
. The least cardinality of a dense subfamily of a filter is called the character of that filter. For an algebra
\mathbb{B}
of subsets of
\omega
, the density of
\mathbb{B}
will be the least cardinality of a dense family of the infinite sets in
\mathbb{B}
.

The goal of this subsection is to prove the following result:

Theorem 3.1
.

The following statements are equivalent:

(1)

\mathfrak{d}=cov(\mathcal{M})
.

(2)

For any measure
\mu:\mathbb{B}\to[0,1]
, such that
[\omega]^{<\omega}\subseteq\mathbb{B}
,
\mu
vanishes on finite sets, and has density less than
\mathfrak{d}
, there exists an atomless strong
Q
-measure
\nu:\mathcal{P}(\omega)\to[0,1]
extending
\mu
.

Before the proof, some notation and an observation. A
partial selector
for
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{n}:n\in\omega\}
is a set
S
such that
|S\cap P_{n}|\leq 1
for all but finitely many
n
. And we say that
S
is
m
-skipping for
\mathcal{P}
if for any
n\in\omega
, there is at most one

such that
\lvert P_{i}\cap S\lvert>0
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{n}:n\in\omega\}
be a partition of
\omega
in finite intervals, and

with the property that if

\mathcal{Q}
then,

is a partial selector for
\mathcal{P}
that is

-skipping.

Proof.

Just define
Q_{n}=\bigcup_{i\in[(m+1)n,(m+1)(n+1))}P_{i}
.

Given two filters

“If

Proposition 3.3

Assume the filter dichotomy holds and there are no

-points, then there are no

-measures.

Proof.

Consider a measure

finite to one such that for any

By the filter dichotomy there must be

The goal of this subsection is to complete the proof of Theorem

Suppose no fit

We commence with a series of auxiliary lemmata about

\sup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}\|x_{i}\|_{i}<\infty
endowed with the natural norm. The (canonical) projection map between
\prod_{i\in\mathcal{I}}X_{i}
and

will be denoted by

. In cases where

X_{1}\oplus_{\infty}\dots\oplus_{\infty}X_{n}

Lemma 4.11
.

Suppose that
\{X_{i}:i\in\mathcal{I}\}
is an indexed family of Banach spaces. Suppose also that for each

we are given an
L
-orthogonal sequence

. Then
(x_{n})_{n}

yields an
L
-orthogonal sequence in the
\ell_{\infty}
-sum space
\left(\bigoplus_{i\in\mathcal{I}}X_{i}\right)_{\infty}.

Proof.

Notice that for any

we have

\lim_{n}\|x+x_{n}\|\geq\lim_{n}\|x_{i}+x_{i,n}\|_{i}=1+\|x_{i}\|_{i}

for every

, so

1+\|x\|\geq\lim_{n}\|x+x_{n}\|\geq\sup_{i\in\mathcal{I}}(1+\|x_{i}\|_{i})=1+\|x\|

as desired.

Proof.

Proof of claim.

On the one hand we have

5.
Open questions

After our investigations, one question still remains elusive:

Question 1
.

Is there a model of
\mathrm{ZFC}
with
Q^{+}(\omega)
-measures but without
Q
-points?

This seems plausible, yet hard to arrange. The basic issue is the tension between the existence of
Q^{+}(\omega)
-measures and
Q
-points. Namely, in all the classical models where there are no
Q
-points there are no
Q^{+}(\omega)
-measures either. We also ask whether a quantitative version version of Theorem
4.3
is possible:

Question 2
.

Assume
\mu
is an
\varepsilon
-strong
Q
-measure and
(x_{n})_{n\in\omega}
is an
L
-orthogonal sequence in a Banach space
X
. Does

\left\lVert x+\mu\text{-}\lim(x_{n})\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert x\right\rVert+\varepsilon\left\lVert\mu\text{-}\lim(x_{n})\right\rVert

hold for
x\in X
?

In fact, we do not have examples that separate any of the various generalizations of
Q
-points that have arisen in our work.

Question 3
.

Are any of the notions of
Q^{+}(\omega)
-measure,
\delta
-fit
Q
-measure, fit
Q
-measure,
\varepsilon
-strong
Q
-measure, and the like, equivalent to each other?

And finally, we wonder if any of these codes the transmission of
L
-orthogonality from a sequence to an element of the bidual.

Question 4
.

Is there a combinatorial characterization of those measures
\mu
such that the
\mu\text{-}\lim
through any
L
-orthogonal sequence is an
L
-orthogonal element?
