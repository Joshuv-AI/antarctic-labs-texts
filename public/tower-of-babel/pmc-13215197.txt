Membrane-separated electrodes enable high-rate low-energy electrochemical carbon capture
Authors: Kaige Sun, Mike Tebyetekerwa, Hongxia Zhang, Timothy T. Duignan, Rizal Evans, Lei Ge, Yi Sun, Yuhui Ge, Zhuyuan Wang, Chao Xing, Jindi Yang, Xiangkang Zeng, Darren Martin, Xiwang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Electrochemical carbon dioxide (CO2) capture using supercapacitive systems is a promising green technology but remains limited by low uptake rates and high energy requirement. Here, we present a membrane-integrated supercapacitor system that addresses these challenges by decoupling electrode environments with a cation exchange membrane. This configuration sustains high hydroxide concentration at the gas-facing negative electrode, generated through dynamic water dissociation within the electric double layer. The resulting localized alkaline interface enhances CO2 capture by driving its conversion into (bi)carbonate species via a pH-swing mechanism. The system achieves a CO2 uptake of up to 893 mmol/kg with a fast rate of 1281 mmol/kg/hour at −1.4 V under 20% CO2. Energy consumption as low as 32 kJ/mol is obtained at −0.8 V under 20% CO2 together with a long lifetime over 200 hours at −1.0 V, 10% CO2. These findings establish a robust platform for electrochemical CO2 capture and underscore the importance of localized chemical environments in supercapacitive swing adsorption.

INTRODUCTION

Capturing carbon dioxide (CO2) from dilute gas streams is central to climate mitigation (1–3). Among emerging strategies, electrochemical CO2 capture offers a modular and potentially low-energy alternative to conventional thermal and solvent-based approaches (4–8). In particular, supercapacitive swing adsorption (SSA) (9) exploits capacitive charge storage in aqueous systems to reversibly capture CO2 at carbon electrodes, avoiding the use of chemical sorbents or redox mediators (10–12). Its operational simplicity, low-cost materials, excellent cycling stability, and compatibility with intermittent renewable energy sources make SSA an attractive approach for sustainable carbon capture (4, 13, 14). To date, substantial progress has been made in SSA area, including capture capacity up to ~780 mmol CO2 per kilogram of electrode (mmol/kg) (15), adsorption rates over 300 mmol CO2 per kilogram of electrode per hour (mmol/kg/hour) (15, 16), and energy requirements as low as 0.3 kilojoule per mol of captured CO2 (kJ/mol) (17). Yet, despite its promise, these peak values are typically achieved under conditions sacrificing other performance dimensions. Most reported SSA performance remains limited, typically capturing less than 200 mmol/kg/hour with high energy consumption (9, 15–21). These limitations stem from an incomplete understanding of the underlying capture mechanism, hindering efforts to design systems that fully exploit its potential.

CO2 speciation in aqueous systems is governed by a set of pH-dependent equilibria (4, 7, 14): Dissolved CO2 hydrates to form H2CO3, which dissociates into HCO3− and CO32−, with the dominant species determined by pH. The Bjerrum plot illustrates this sensitivity, showing that small pH shifts can substantially alter the distribution of carbon species (fig. S1). Theoretically, creating a localized alkaline environment can enhance CO2 uptake by stabilizing (bi)carbonate ions. However, conventional SSA cells use symmetric carbon electrodes in a shared electrolyte, limiting the formation of strong pH gradients. Efforts to break this symmetry, such as replacing one carbon electrode with metallic zinc, have improved capture performance by introducing intentional redox reactions, but potentially reduce long-term stability and increase system complexity (22). This points to the need for a more spatial and stable approach to decouple electrode environments and control local pH during operation.

Here, we report a membrane-integrated SSA architecture that spatially separates electrode environments to engineer local alkalinity generated via interfacial water dissociation at the CO2-facing electrode. By incorporating a cation exchange membrane (CEM), we isolate ion transport and suppress reaction crossover, enabling a high-pH microenvironment during charging. This pH-swing shifts dissolved CO2 toward (bi)carbonate ion formation, enhancing capture kinetics while avoiding side reactions at the positive electrode. The resulting system achieves a maximum CO2 capture capacity of 893 mmol/kg and a rate of 1281 mmol/kg/hour at a wide −1.4 V cell voltage under 20% CO2 conditions, and delivers an energy consumption as low as 32 kJ/mol when using −0.8 V. The operational stability exceeds 200 hours cycled at −1.0 V under 10% CO2. Our findings demonstrate that electrochemical control of local chemical environments, enabled by membrane design, offers a compelling route to high-efficiency carbon capture.

RESULTS

Cell configuration

As illustrated in Fig. 1, our CO2 capture cell consists of two symmetric activated carbon electrodes, with the activated carbon supported on a hydrophobic gas diffusion layer (GDL) for direct contact with humidified simulated flue gas. The positive electrode is isolated from the gas stream and maintained in a moist environment via a sodium chloride–soaked separator. NaCl was used as the electrolyte. A CEM separates the electrodes, permitting sodium ions (Na+) transport while blocking anion crossover. Upon negative polarization, Na+ accumulates at the negative electrode, forming an electric double layer (EDL) that induces interfacial water dissociation. While the resulting protons are reversibly adsorbed by the negative carbon electrode, the remaining hydroxide ions (OH−) are retained near the negative electrode, establishing a steep local pH gradient that drives dissolved CO2 toward (bi)carbonate formation. This membrane-enabled spatial control over ion transport and interfacial chemistry underpins the system’s high-rate, low-energy CO2 capture using nontoxic, abundant materials.

Schematic illustration of the membrane-based electrochemical CO2 capture cell.

The cell comprises symmetric activated carbon electrodes, with the negative electrode supported on a hydrophobic GDL to enable direct contact with humidified simulated flue gas. A CEM separates the negative and positive electrode, while an ion-conductive, electronically insulating separator maintains ionic continuity at the positive electrode, which is kept moist using a NaCl-dampened support. The capture process includes three stages: During charging, Na+ ions migrate across the CEM and accumulate at the negative electrode, forming EDL (the first step). The resulting high electric field strength within the EDL promotes interfacial water dissociation (the second step), generating hydroxide ions and protons, while protons are reversibly adsorbed by the negative carbon electrode. The remaining hydroxide ions raise the local pH and facilitate CO2 speciation into (bi)carbonate species at the negative electrode (the third step). Note that negative electrode refers to the electrode that receives electrons during charging.

Performance benchmarking of the membrane-integrated SSA cell

To validate the design concept, we designed a CEM-integrated SSA architecture with a gas flow system (fig. S2). Operando gas analysis confirmed reversible uptake and release across charge-discharge cycles (fig. S3). No capture was observed under pure N2 and using a bare GDL (fig. S4), affirming that CO2 selective uptake occurs and that it is from electrochemical processes at the carbon interface. Capture performance increased with cell voltage and feed concentration. Under 10% CO2 (volume concentration), increasing the cell voltage from −0.8 V to −1.4 V enhanced capture from 186 to 813 mmol/kg, with a corresponding rise in adsorption rate (Fig. 2A). This voltage-dependent behavior arises from electric field–enhanced water dissociation and the resulting proton storage, which collectively increase local alkalinity. A minimum energy consumption of 35 kJ/mol was achieved at the lowest voltage of −0.8 V. At 20% CO2, capacity peaked at 893 mmol/kg and rates reached 1281 mmol/kg/hour at a cell voltage of −1.4 V (Fig. 2B), corresponding to a CO2 capture ratio of ~3.5% per cycle and with an energy input of 207 kJ/mol (Fig. 2B).

The optimal capture rate reported was ~432 mmol/kg/hour with an energy consumption of ~300 kJ/mol (15), while the lowest energy consumption was reported to be ~20 kJ/mol alongside with a capture rate of 310 mmol/kg/hour (17). This work achieved a capture rate of 1281 mmol/kg/hour with 207 kJ/mol and an optimistic energy consumption of 32 kJ/mol with 361 mmol/kg/hour capture rate.

Although higher voltages increased energy input, the system further achieved a minimum energy cost of 32 kJ/mol with capture rate of 361 mmol/kg/hour and capacity of 196 mmol/kg under −0.8 V cell voltage (Fig. 2B). The CO2 capture process is fully reversible, with all captured CO2 being released during discharge, giving an optimal CO2 productivity of 61.6 mmol/hour/m2 (738 mmol/hour/kg) at −1.4 V and 20% CO2 conditions (fig. S5). In addition, the related coulombic efficiencies reached around 100% at −0.8 V, −1.0 V, and −1.2 V at 20% CO2 (fig. S6), while it reduced to 75% at −1.4 V, which may be ascribed to some irreversible electrochemical reactions (16). The charge efficiency reached above 50% at all tested voltage range under 20% CO2 and up to 87.7% at −0.8 V (fig. S6), which is higher than all the previously reported values (15, 16, 18). The improved charge efficiencies are attributed to the CEM, which decouples the two electrodes and suppresses ion crossover (16, 23). Table S1 summarized key performance data for capturing CO2 from 10% and 20% feed concentration under different cell voltages, showing improved adsorptive metrics with increasing voltage but compromise energetic efficiency. Compared with the membrane-free configuration, where both carbon electrodes operate in a shared electrolyte that permits co-ion migration and reaction crossover, the CEM-integrated cell achieved a 29.6% higher capture rate (389 versus 300 mmol/kg/hour) and reduced the energy consumption by over 40% (from 216 kJ/mol to 128 kJ/mol; fig. S7) under the same test conditions (−1.0 V, 10% CO2), confirming that incorporating a CEM effectively suppresses reaction crossover and isolates ion transport, enhancing capture performance while minimizing energy dissipation.

We further assessed the long-term stability of CEM-integrated SSA system. No substantial degradation over 228-hour testing under 10% CO2 and −1.0 V voltage confirmed stable capture and release performance (Fig. 2, C and D, and figs. S8 to S10), demonstrating durability and repeatability. Even at a high cell voltage (−1.4 V, 20% CO2), no noticeable fading was observed after over 80-hour cycling (fig. S11). Techno-economic viability was conducted by estimating the cost of CO2 removal under the feed conditions (table S2). The results showed a decreased cost as the increase of cell voltage and CO2 feed concentration, while the lowest CO2 capture cost is achieved at 20% CO2 and −1.4 V cell voltage, which is $368 per ton captured CO2 (fig. S12, A and B), higher than typical thermal-based carbon capture methods (24–27). This high cost is primarily due to the current high price of the CEM. A CEM-free cell reduces the capture cost by a factor of ~3.9 under the same testing conditions (fig. S12C), although its CO2 capture performance is lower than that of the CEM-based cell. Economically, while the CEM plays a critical role in enhancing capturing performance, its overall cost-effectiveness depends on balancing the associated material cost with the performance gains achieved. Continued optimization (e.g., improved capture rate and reduced energy consumption) is expected to decrease the capture cost to a more competitive level ($75 per ton of CO2; fig. S12D and text S1). Compared to other electrochemical CO2 capture methods relying on specialized sorbents and materials (8, 10, 11, 28–38), SSA offers green solutions to capture CO2 using only inexpensive and durable materials (table S3). Nevertheless, despite variations in SSA cell designs, electrode materials, and operational parameters across reported SSA systems (9, 15–21, 39, 40), most systems exhibited either low capturing rates or high energy penalties; in contrast, the CEM-integrated SSA outperforms the current leading SSA systems, delivering high-rate, low-energy CO2 capture (Fig. 2E and fig. S13). Overall, this work establishes membrane-integrated SSA as a scalable and efficient platform for electrochemical separation of CO2 from dilute gas streams.

Understanding the local capture environment: CO2 gas access and electrode polarity

With the membrane-enabled architecture designed to promote local alkalinity at the CO2-facing electrode, we first investigated how the interfacial environment governs capture performance. Specifically, we examined how gas accessibility and electrode polarity influence CO2 uptake, aiming to validate the hypothesis that capture is driven by spatially resolved electrochemical conditions rather than bulk electrolyte properties.

To evaluate the role of gas-phase availability, we constructed and compared three distinct electrode configurations within a batch-mode SSA cell (fig. S14). These included (i) a gas diffusion electrode (GDE) composed of activated carbon supported on a porous hydrophobic layer (figs. S15 to S19), enabling direct contact with gaseous CO2; (ii) a partially gas-accessible electrode (PGAE), fabricated by depositing activated carbon on a dense, nonporous graphite substrate (fig. S20); and (iii) a fully immersed electrode with unrestricted electrolyte access but no gas-phase exposure. Under a 10% CO2 atmosphere and polarization to −1.0 V, the GDE exhibited a substantial CO2 uptake of 112 mmol/kg (Fig. 3, A and B). The PGAE showed only 31 mmol/kg, while the fully immersed electrode demonstrated no measurable capture even though it exhibited the highest electrochemical capacitance (fig. S21). These findings indicate the presence and critical role of a gas-electrode-electrolyte interface, or triple-phase boundary (fig. S22) for enabling electrochemical CO2 capture.

To further explore the conditions necessary for capture, we examined the influence of electrode polarity using a deliberately asymmetric cell configuration. One electrode maintaining gas access was partially immersed, while the opposing electrode was fully submerged to eliminate direct gas exposure. When the gas-exposed electrode was negatively polarized from open circuit to −1.0 V, reversible CO2 uptake and release were observed, as confirmed by reproducible pressure swings during charging and discharging (Fig. 3C and fig. S23A). In contrast, applying a positive voltage to the same electrode did not result in any detectable CO2 capture, despite identical materials and operating conditions (Fig. 3D and fig. S23B). The lack of response under positive polarization was also reproduced in the membrane-free gas flow configuration (fig. S23C). Landskron and Forse reported a seemingly contradict behavior of CO2 desorption/release under positive polarization of the gas-facing electrode (9, 17, 39). This appears to depend strongly on the concentration of dissolved CO2-derived species. A slight CO2 release was also observed in our system upon positive polarization when the CO2 feed concentration was increased to 45%, due to the higher amount of dissolved CO2 species at elevated CO2 partial pressure, followed by re-capture during negative polarization (fig. S24). These results confirm that electron enrichment at the triple-phase boundary is a prerequisite for initiating the capture process. It is not simply the material or the potential applied, but the directional electron flux at a gas-accessible surface that activates the local chemical environment necessary for CO2 capture.

Collectively, these experiments demonstrate that high-rate electrochemical CO2 capture relies on a finely tuned interfacial environment defined by gas-phase access and cathodic polarization. Capture does not proceed through passive electroadsorption of ions from the bulk electrolyte, but rather through targeted activation of CO2 at the triple-phase boundary, where electron-rich carbon, water, and gaseous CO2 converge. Earlier studies noticed that SSA behaviors may be related to the formation of a three-phase boundary among unwetted carbon pores, gas, and electrolyte-infiltrated pores (9, 20, 41). The critical role of this interface in facilitating CO2 conversion has not been provided with a more in-depth explanation. This localized reactivity, enabled by the GDE architecture and reinforced through spatial ion control via the CEM, underpins the high-rate, low-energy performance of our system.

Controlling capture chemistry through membrane-induced electrolyte asymmetry

To understand how the electrolyte environment contributes to CO2 capture, we examined the influence of ionic strength and ion composition on system performance. In a batch-mode SSA configuration used in Fig. 3A with two identical GDEs under 10% CO2, we systematically varied the electrolyte ionic strength from 0.01 to 3 M using a range of monovalent and divalent salts. A linear increase in CO2 capture capacity was observed with rising ionic strength, reaching 165 mmol/kg at 3 M, more than doubling the capture capacity at 0.01 M (Fig. 4A). This enhancement occurred largely independent of cation identity, indicating that the effect is driven by total ionic content rather than specific ion-electrode interactions, which aligns with the earlier findings reported by Landskron (19, 20). Notably, in a 10% CO2-saturated but solute-free electrolyte (0 M ionic strength), no measurable capture was detected, despite the dissolved CO2 concentration being 2.91 mM (fig. S25). While electrodes displayed poor capacitance and high resistance in low ionic strength regime (0 M and 0.01 M NaCl systems; fig. S26), CO2 capture capacity jumped to 71 mmol/kg at 0.01 M NaCl electrolyte and further enhanced to 112 mmol/kg with 0.1 M NaCl electrolyte. These results suggest a field-induced interfacial effect: Stronger ionic strength leads to denser EDL and enhanced interfacial field strength, which promote CO2 capture. In the absence of supporting ions to compress the EDL, the interfacial field remains too weak to drive CO2 capture (text S2).

To decouple charge storage from CO2 capture behavior, we compared EDL capacitance and CO2 uptake across a range of electrolytes. While a positive correlation was initially observed, the relationship appears to be scattered at higher capacitance values, with CO2 capture capacities ranging widely even at similar capacitance levels (Fig. 4B and fig. S27). For example, at approximately 30 F/g, capture ranged from 98 to 159 mmol/kg, suggesting that factors beyond total charge, such as local pH, are critical. A similar behavior has also been observed recently, providing strong support evidence (42).

We further explored the role of pH by varying the NaCl-to-HCl ratio while keeping ionic strength constant at 0.1 M. Increasing the acid content led to a sharp drop in capture capacity, from 112 to 12 mmol/kg, despite a 71.4% increase in capacitance (fig. S28). This behavior was further confirmed by a recent study (42). According to the Bjerrum plot, molecular CO2 and H2CO3 dominate in strongly acidic medium, which suppresses conversion to (bi)carbonate species. These findings reinforce that CO2 capture is strongly pH-dependent and that local alkalinity, rather than general electroadsorption, governs system performance. The addition of NaCl appears to enhance capture not through direct ion-CO2 interaction but by creating favorable pH conditions at the electrode surface.

To directly quantify local alkalinity at the carbon interface, we used a rotating ring-disk electrode (RRDE) technique to measure the near-surface (diffusion-layer) pH adjacent to the electrode (figs. S29 to S30). As the applied potential shifted negatively, the local pH increased, reaching 9.8 at −1.0 V (Fig. 4C). This observation confirms that OH− is generated under cathodic polarization. Ex situ time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) further revealed notable accumulation of HCO3− and CO32− species on the gas-facing negative electrode, confirming that CO2 is captured primarily in ionic form (Fig. 4D). These results agree with the predicted speciation from the Bjerrum plot and indicate that reversible CO2 conversion to (bi)carbonate is the dominant capture pathway.

To investigate the origin of OH− generation, we performed in situ infrared (IR) spectroscopy on electrode (fig. S31). Upon increasing the potential from open circuit to −1.0 V, the vibrational band of adsorbed water (H2O*) at 3525 cm−1 (43, 44) shifted toward that of OH* (43, 45, 46), accompanied by a rise in signal intensity (Fig. 4E). These spectral changes indicate progressive water dissociation at the interface. Modified Poisson-Boltzmann model (text S3) and machine learning interatomic potential (MLIP) calculations (text S4) were used to reveal water dissociation on carbon electrode. The calculated results showed the proton transfer and adsorption from the bulk are intrinsically kinetically constrained in near-neutral electrolytes, demonstrating that electric field–induced water dissociation into H+ and OH− locally is required (fig. S32 and text S3). The electric field strength reaches 0.1574 GV/m at 0.01 M ionic strength (fig. S33A), reaching the field requirement to dissociate water (~107 V/m) (47, 48). This field increases logarithmically with the ionic strength and is positively correlated to the applied potential (fig. S33), strongly supporting electric field–driven CO2 capture process. MLIP calculations indicated that a strong electric field enables barrierless proton transfer from interfacial water molecules to carboxylate functional group on the carbon electrode surface (figs. S34 and S35), which is further confirmed by the increased C-OH peak observed in the in situ IR spectra (fig. S36 and text S5). Collectively, these results demonstrated that under strong electric field within EDL, interfacial water molecules dissociate into H+ and OH− [the “Wien effect” (47–54)]. The generated H+ are subsequently adsorbed by the activated carbon electrode via “ionic liquid-solid” mechanism (16, 20), while the remaining OH− ions accumulate and generate local alkalinity (text S6). The OH* signal decreased as the potential returned to 0 V, confirming the reversibility of water dissociation process and CO2 capture. Together, the RRDE, TOF-SIMS, IR, and computational modeling provide converging evidence that the high local pH required for CO2 capture is induced by field-driven water dissociation rather than bulk solution chemistry.

The role of the CEM is central to sustaining this localized alkaline environment. By allowing only cation transport, the membrane prevents OH− from diffusing away from the negative electrode, preserving the steep pH gradient essential for efficient capture. TOF-SIMS analysis of membrane-covered electrode coupled with ion selectivity experiment revealed elevated Na+ and suppressed Cl− concentrations at the negative electrode interface (fig. S37), consistent with the membrane’s cation selectivity (fig. S38, A and B). The local acidic environment at the positive electrode (fig. S39) was isolated by the CEM (fig. S38, C and D), preventing mutual influence with the capturing electrode. The acidic pH at the positive electrode facilitates CO2 release, offering further mechanistic insight into the CO2 desorption observed during the charging process (17, 22, 39, 55). Using an anion exchange membrane (AEM) to isolate the positive electrode [AEM-covered positive electrode (AEM-Pos.); fig. S40A], the interference between the two electrodes is partially reduced, enabling a similar performance enhancement to CEM cell (224 mmol/kg, 387 mmol/kg/hour in AEM-Pos. versus 233 mmol/kg, 396 mmol/kg/hour in CEM cell; fig. S40C). However, the energy consumption of AEM-Pos. cell increased to 152 kJ/mol compared to 115 kJ/mol of CEM cell, accompanied with a substantial decline in charge efficiency (reduced to 38% from 51%) under the same test conditions (100 mA/g, −1.0 V, 10% CO2; fig. S40, C and D). While AEM minimizes the reaction crossover by isolating the positive electrode, the capturing electrode remains in direct contact with the bulk electrolyte, imposing a co-ion expulsion penalty (23, 56, 57), leading to additional energy dissipation during charging and reducing overall capture efficiency. In contrast, replacing the CEM with an AEM at the same position, namely, AEM-covered negative electrode (AEM-Neg.; fig. S40A), abolished electrochemical performance and CO2 uptake under identical conditions (fig. S40, B and E). Although the cell symmetry was broken, the negative electrode is deprived of cations necessary to generate local alkalinity. These results confirm that the CEM not only enables pH control but also suppresses unwanted ion shuttling and parasitic reactions (fig. S41).

DISCUSSION

This work establishes a membrane-integrated supercapacitive architecture that enables high-rate, low-energy electrochemical CO2 capture through precise control of interfacial conditions. By introducing a CEM, we achieve spatial separation of electrode environments and direct ion transport pathways, allowing stable local alkalinity at the gas-facing negative electrode without introducing redox-active components. The result is a robust pH-swing system that captures CO2 via reversible conversion to (bi)carbonate, with performance metrics that rival or exceed state-of-the-art electrochemical methods in terms of capacity, rate, and energy efficiency.

Mechanistic investigations reveal that CO2 capture in this system is not governed by bulk electrolyte properties or passive electrosorption of preexisting ionic species. Instead, capture occurs at the gas-electrode-electrolyte triple-phase boundary under negative polarization, where field-induced interfacial water dissociation generates OH−, increasing the local pH. This elevated alkalinity shifts CO2 speciation toward (bi)carbonate formation, producing ionic species that are captured locally. The process depends critically on gas accessibility and electrode polarity and is sustained by membrane-mediated ion asymmetry that preserves the local chemical environment. Electrochemical, spectroscopic, mass spectrometric, and computational evidence collectively confirm that CO2 capture proceeds through interfacial chemical modulation that governs the formation and adsorption of reactive carbon species, rather than being driven solely by charge storage in the bulk.

The use of commercially available materials and aqueous electrolytes positions this system as an accessible and scalable solution for post-combustion CO2 separation. Capture rates exceeding 1200 mmol/kg/hour and capacity as high as 893 mmol/kg were achieved under simulated flue gas CO2 concentration. These results compare favorably to both amine scrubbing and redox-mediated electrochemical approaches while offering advantages in safety, stability, and modularity. Remaining challenges include optimizing CO2 capture from realistic flue gases, which typically contain O2, SO2, and NOx that may induce electrode corrosion, parasitic side reactions, and long-term performance decay. In parallel, further improvements in CO2 capture performance are needed to lower the overall cost of capture. Optimizing carbon type and electrode structure, particularly through advanced porous carbons with high surface area and tailored porosity, could enhance CO2 capture capacity (17, 18, 58). At last, translating cell-level demonstrations into practical systems will introduce engineering constraints, such as gas diffusion limitations and pressure drop in larger modules, and require other engineering advances including stacked cell architecture, roll-to-roll designs, and other scalable manufacturing strategies to enable industrial-scale deployment.

Overall, this study illustrates how membrane-enabled electrochemical architectures can be used to tailor reaction environments at the nanoscale, opening directions for ion-selective separations, carbon utilization, and gas-phase electrocatalysis. By breaking symmetry in supercapacitive systems and activating interfacial water chemistry, we demonstrate a design principle that may extend beyond CO2 capture to other applications requiring dynamic local pH control and selective ion management.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

GDE fabrication

GDEs for both flow-type and batch-mode electrochemical CO2 capture reactors were fabricated by coating a slurry consisting of activated carbon (Acticarb PS1000F, Activated Carbon Technologies Pty Ltd.), polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF; Sigma-Aldrich) binder, and super P carbon black (MTI Corp.) with a mass ratio of 8:1:1 on Toray carbon paper 5% wet proofing (Fuel Cell Store, USA), which was dried in vacuum at 110°C overnight.

Materials characterization

A field-emission scanning electron microscope (FE-SEM; JEOL JSM-7100F) was used to characterize the sample morphologies. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) technique to measure surface chemical compositions of the carbon was done on a Kratos Axis Ultra photoelectron spectrometer. Nitrogen adsorption/desorption isotherms were measured on a Tristar II 3020 instrument at 77 K. The specific surface area of the carbon was calculated using the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) equation. The pore size distribution was obtained using the density functional theory (DFT) method. A contact angle goniometer (OCA 15EC) was used to detect the surface wettability with deionized water.

Electrochemical measurements

Basic electrochemical properties were measured in a conventional three-electrode system using a Biologic electrochemical workstation (SP-150) with a GDE, Pt plate, Ag/AgCl electrode, and 1 M NaCl aqueous solution as the working, counter, reference electrode, and electrolyte, respectively. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves were swept between potential −1.0 V and 0 V [versus Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl)] at scan rates of 1 to 100 mV/s, gravimetric charge-discharge (GCD) measurements were performed at the same potential range with different current densities, and the specific capacitance was obtained from the GCD profiles.

Electrochemical CO2 capture using flow cells

In flow-type SSA tests, two carbon electrodes and one separator (Whatman) damped with 3 M NaCl electrolyte were used. The moist mimic flue gas (containing 10% or 20% CO2 in volume concentration) flowed into the cell with a controlled flow rate by mass flow controller, and the CO2 concentration was recorded in the outlet using a CO2 sensor (SprintIR-60% CO2 sensor, Gas Sensing Solutions). A cation exchange membrane (CEM, Fumasep, FKE-50, Fuel Cell Store, USA) was used for the membrane-integrated SSA. The CEM was pretreated using 0.5 M NaCl before use. Before the test, the mixture gas flowed through the system for some time without any bias to achieve saturation of the electrode and electrolyte. During the test, a constant current of 100 mA/g (based on the gas-exposed negative electrode) was supplied until a target cell voltage was reached, after which the voltage was held for 30 min to allow adsorption equilibrium to be achieved. Electrochemical CO2 capture using batch-mode cells

Batch-mode electrochemical gas adsorption measurements were carried out in a custom-designed gastight reactor. Briefly, two activated carbon electrodes were placed into the electrochemical reactor with 30 ml of electrolyte. After assembly, the electrochemical reactor was then placed in a temperature-controlled chamber (298.15 K) and filled with a mixture of CO2/N2 gas (CO2/N2 = 10: 90), which represents a mimic of flue gas. An electrochemical workstation (Biologic, SP-150) was connected to the two current collectors to apply voltage across the electrodes. Before the charge/discharge operation, the gas reactor was equilibrated for several hours to allow for the saturation of the electrodes and electrolyte with the gas. The same electrochemical operation as the flow cell test was used. Specifically, a constant current of 100 mA/g (based on the gas-exposed negative electrode) was supplied until −1.0 V target cell voltage was reached, after which the voltage was held for 30 min to allow adsorption equilibrium to be achieved. The discharge step used the same current density (100 mA/g) and discharged to 0 V holding for 30 min. The charge/discharge operation was kept the same throughout the batch-mode cell study. A pressure transducer (ESI USB pressure transducer, GD4200-USB02.5AB) was connected to the device and used to monitor the gas reservoir’s pressure change during the electrochemical operation. For each charge/discharge cycle, the amount of CO2 adsorbed during each charge/discharge cycle was calculated by measuring the difference in pressure between the start and end of the charging phase. The ideal gas equation pV = nRT was used to transfer the pressure difference to moles of CO2 (9, 17, 39, 40, 59). The volume of the gas reservoir was calculated based on Boyle’s law (39, 40), which is 72 ml in our case. The CO2 adsorption capacity was calculated from the fifth pressure cycle due to the relatively stable change. In addition, the corresponded full-cell electrochemical capacitance was calculated from the voltage curve on galvanostatic charging of the cell using (20)C=4×I×Δtmtotal×ΔU(11)where C is the specific capacitance (F/g), I is the constant current (A), mtotal is the total mass of the activated carbon in the two electrodes (g), ∆U is the cell voltage difference (V), and ∆t is the negative charge duration (s). Note that only the galvanostatic charging step contributes to the electrochemical capacitance, while the voltage holding step does not affect the capacitance.

Bikerman steric correction for high potentials

The standard Poisson-Boltzmann equation predicts ion concentrations via the Boltzmann factor ρ+=ρ+bexp(−ϕ), which diverges at high potentials. At ψ0=−1.0 V (ϕ0≈−39), the Boltzmann factor e39 ≈ 1017 would predict absurdly high ion densities, violating the physical constraint that ions cannot overlap.

To extend the model to high potential, we incorporate the Bikerman (lattice-gas) steric correction, which caps ion concentrations at a maximum close-packing density ρmax=1/a3, where a is the hydrated ion diameter. The modified ion densities becomeρ±(z)=ρ±be∓ϕ−U±1+ν(e−ϕ−U++e+ϕ−U−−2)(30)where ν=ρb/ρmax is the bulk packing fraction. At low potentials, the denominator is approximately unity and standard Boltzmann statistics are recovered. At high potentials, the denominator grows proportionally to the Boltzmann factor, causing the concentration to saturate at ρmax rather than diverge.

Using a hydrated ion size of a = 3.5 Å gives ρmax=2.3×1028 ions/m3 (≈38 M). With this correction, the model remains numerically stable and physically reasonable up to ∣ψ0∣=1.4 V and beyond. At moderate potentials (∣ψ0∣≤200 mV), the Bikerman correction has negligible effect (<1% difference from standard Poisson-Boltzmann), confirming that the log c scaling results are robust.

Physically, the calculated interfacial electric field represents the electrostatic force per unit charge acting on interfacial ions and polar water molecules. We evaluate the field at a fixed probe position z∗=2.6 Å, corresponding to the characteristic hydrogen-bond distance between the carboxylate oxygen (COO−) and the nearest water hydrogen. This position lies within the Stern-layer region, where the potential drop is most concentrated. The field is therefore spatially confined to the angstrom- to nanometer-scale EDL and does not represent a macroscopic field across the electrode gap. It relies on a continuum description of the solvent dielectric; a more precise quantitative evaluation would require fully explicit molecular dynamics simulations.

Computational methods: Proton transfer calculations

System preparation

A model system consisting of a graphene nanoribbon segment (3 × 2 unit cells, armchair edge) functionalized with a carboxylic acid group (COOH) was constructed using the atomic simulation environment (ASE) (68). The graphene edges were saturated with hydrogen atoms, and a carboxyl group was attached to an edge carbon atom. The initial structure was geometry optimized using the orbital materials (ORB) machine learning force interatomic potential (MLIP) (69) with the OMOL model variant, which is specifically parameterized for organic molecules and materials.

To model the aqueous environment, nine water molecules were positioned around the COOH functional group to create a solvation shell. A hydroxide anion (OH−) was placed approximately 4.5 Å from the carboxylic acid group (fig. S34). The complete solvated system comprised 65 atoms with a composition of C25H28O12 and a net charge of −1.

Geometry optimization

All geometry optimizations were performed using the Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (BFGS) algorithm as implemented in ASE, with the ORB-OMOL force field serving as the energy and force calculator. The system charge was set to −1 (accounting for the OH− anion) with a spin multiplicity of 1. Convergence criteria were set to a maximum force of 0.05 eV/Å. Periodic boundary conditions were disabled, treating the system as a molecular cluster.

Constrained optimization

To maintain the protonated COOH state during geometry optimization and prevent spontaneous deprotonation, a Hookean constraint was applied to the hydroxyl O-H bond. The constraint was implemented using ASE’s Hookean spring potential with a spring constant of k = 50 eV/Å2 centered at the target O-H distance of approximately1.0 Å. This constraint force was sufficient to prevent proton transfer while allowing relaxation of the surrounding structure.

Energy profile calculation

The potential energy surface for proton transfer was mapped by performing constrained geometry optimizations at fixed O-H distances ranging from 0.95 to 3.50 Å (20 points). At each distance, a Hookean constraint (k = 100 eV/Å2) was applied to maintain the target O-H separation, and the remaining degrees of freedom were optimized to a force convergence of 0.1 eV/Å (maximum 200 steps). After optimization with the constraint, the constraint was removed and a single-point energy calculation was performed to obtain the unbiased potential energy. The relative energies were referenced to the minimum energy configuration (deprotonated state).
