Seasonal activity and phoresy rates of Nitidulid beetles (Coleoptera: Nitidulidae) captured in stands with oak wilt infections in northern Michigan, USA
Authors: Olivia R Morris, Karandeep Chahal, Bert Cregg, Nancy Sharma, James Wieferich, Monique L Sakalidis, Deborah G McCullough
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNitidulid beetles (Coleoptera: Nitidulidae), the overland vectors of the Bretziella fagacearum fungus that causes oak wilt, were monitored in infection centers in Quercus rubra stands in northern Michigan, USA using baited, wind-oriented traps for 2 years. First nitidulid captures, accounting for <1.5% of total annual captures, occurred in late April in both years (<50 cumulative degree days [DDs]; base 10°C). A total of 1,188 and 735 beetles representing 19 and 18 species were captured in 2018 (18 traps; 3 sites) and 2019 (16 traps; 4 sites), respectively. Ground traps (1.5 m high) captured more beetles and species than traps on canopy branches of red oaks. Most nitidulids (81–86%) were captured in May and June, but frequent precipitation and cool spring temperatures extended activity into early July in 2019. In 2018, 336 beetles representing 12 species were screened for B. fagacearum spores, but only 20 beetles from 4 species bore viable spores. Mycelial mats on red oaks killed were 4-fold more abundant in 2019 than in 2018. Of the 225 beetles screened in 2019, 56 beetles representing 6 species had viable spores. Nearly all (96%) spore-bearing beetles in both years were captured in late May or June. In bimonthly xylem samples collected from healthy trees, large earlywood vessels, presumably more vulnerable to infection than latewood, were present from May to June in 2018 and until early July in 2019. Results are consistent with mid-May to mid-July high-risk periods designated in current state and regional guidelines for oak wilt management.

Oak wilt, a vascular disease caused by the fungus Bretziella fagacearum (Bretz) (formerly Ceratocystis fagacearum; De Beer et al. 2017), was first recognized as an important forest pathogen in 1944 in Wisconsin (Henry et al. 1944, Juzwik et al. 2008). Infection occurs when fungal hyphae grow into xylem vessels, impeding water transport from roots to tree canopies. Although the origin of B. fagacearum is unknown, Juzwik et al. (2008) speculated it may have been introduced from Central America, where oak (Quercus spp.) diversity is notably high. Oak wilt infections have been confirmed in at least 24 states to date, including 51 of the 83 counties in Michigan (USDA Forest Service 2016, EDDMapS 2024, MI DNR 2024).

Virtually all North American oaks can be infected by B. fagacearum, but vulnerability varies among groups and species (Juzwik et al. 2004, Gearman and Blinnikov 2019). Red oaks (Sect. Lobatae) are highly vulnerable and trees can be killed in as little as 3 wks following infection (Gibbs and French 1980, Appel et al. 1986, Hayslett et al 2008). In the white oak group (Sect. Quercus), infection is less severe and rarely results in mortality, with the exception of bur oak (Q. macrocarpa) which may succumb within 7 years (Juzwik et al. 2004, 2011, Hayslett et al 2008). White oaks, which have small xylem vessels relative to red oaks, can produce tyloses in xylem vessels, which inhibit movement of the fungal hyphae through the tree (Hayslett et al. 2008, Gearman and Blinnikov 2019). In red oaks, this response is weak and the fungus typically spreads faster than trees are able to occlude hyphal growth (Jacobi and MacDonald 1980). Ongoing spread of oak wilt in forests and residential areas continues to generate substantial concern in much of the eastern United States, including the Great Lakes region encompassing Michigan, Minnesota, and Wisconsin, as well as Texas, where live oaks (Quercus sect. Virentes) are affected (Juzwik et al. 2008, 2011, Jagemann et al. 2018, DiGirolomo et al. 2020).

Oak wilt can be transmitted from an infected red oak to healthy oaks via underground root grafts, leading to expanding centers of disease, particularly in stands where oaks are dominant or relatively abundant (Juzwik 2008, Blaedow et al. 2010, Juzwik et al. 2011). Efforts to contain oak wilt can involve mechanical disruption of root grafts using deep trenching or vibratory plowing combined with removal of infected and potentially infected trees and stumps (Bruhn and Heyd 1992, Cummings-Carlson and Martin 2001, Koch et al. 2010, Juzwik et al. 2011, Yang et al. 2024). These activities, however, are costly, difficult to implement, often require the sacrifice of many healthy red oaks and may need to be repeated over time (e.g., Koch et al. 2010, Haight et al. 2011, Juzwik et al. 2011). Field evaluations of herbicide or fungicide applications to infected oaks to prevent oak wilt spread via grafted roots are underway but have so far yielded inconsistent results (Bruhn et al. 2003, Cook 2018).

A major focus of oak wilt management, therefore, involves preventing establishment of new infection centers, which occur as a result of overland spread of the fungus by insect vectors (Dorsey and Leach 1956, Juzwik and French 1983, Appel et al. 1986, Juzwik et al. 2004, Ambourn et al. 2005). Studies in red oak stands in Wisconsin and Minnesota identified native sap beetles (Coleoptera: Nitidulidae) as the primary vectors of oak wilt (Cease and Juzwik 2001, Juzwik et al. 2004, 2011, Ambourn et al. 2005). Tiny nitidulid beetles, primarily species within the subfamilies Carpophilinae, Cryptarchinae, and Nitidulinae, feed on fungi and decaying organic matter (Skalbeck 1976, Gazit et al 1982), as well as sap emanating from fresh wounds on oaks (Dorsey et al. 1953, Norris 1956). Many nitidulid species are also attracted to mycelial mats produced under the bark on trunks and large branches of red oaks recently killed by oak wilt (e.g., Norris 1956, Skalbeck 1976, Juzwik 1983, Juzwik and French 1983). Pressure from these mats causes the outer bark to crack, facilitating emission of volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which attract nitidulid beetles (Shelstad et al. 1991, Lin and Phalen 1992, Kyhl et al. 2002). Nitidulid adults may feed, mate, and oviposit on mats, and larvae of at least some species feed on mats (Cease and Juzwik 2001, Kyhl et al. 2002, Juzwik et al 2004, Ambourn et al. 2005, Loughner et al. 2014).

Mycelial mats are commonly produced in spring on trees killed by oak wilt in the previous year, although fresh mats can also be found in summer and fall (Cummings Carlson and Martin 2001, Kyhl et al. 2002, Chahal 2024). Hydrophobic spores, which are produced on fresh mycelial mats for 2–3 wks, readily adhere to insect exoskeletons, thus contaminating nitidulids upon contact (Juzwik and French 1983, Malloch and Blackwell 1993, Wilson 2005). Healthy oak trees become infected by oak wilt when nitidulids bearing viable spores visit fresh wounds with exposed xylem (Gibbs and French 1980). Previous studies indicated wounds may be highly vulnerable to infection for approximately 2–4 days, but vulnerability declines over the next few days as wounds are compartmentalized (Zuckerman et al. 1953, Kuntz and Drake 1957, Juzwik et al. 2004, 2011, Hayslett et al. 2008). Rapidly covering fresh wounds with latex paint or tree paint can reduce nitidulid attraction or introduction of B. fagacearum spores (Koch et al. 2010, Juzwik et al. 2011), but is often not practical, particularly in forests.

Vulnerability of red oaks to infection may vary seasonally, as xylem formation transitions from earlywood to latewood. Earlywood vessels with relatively large lumens facilitate transport of high volumes of sap during the early portion of the growing season as buds break and leaves expand. Latewood vessels, produced after leaves are fully expanded, may offer some protection against embolisms caused by mid or late summer drought (Kitin and Funada 2016). Red oaks may be especially susceptible to oak wilt infection in spring and early summer when xylem is comprised of large-diameter earlywood vessels that transport water to the growing canopy, compared with later in the summer when thick-walled latewood vessels with relatively small diameters are present (Cartenì et al 2018, Torbenson et al. 2016).

To reduce the likelihood of new infections, management guidelines often recommend limiting activities that could potentially wound healthy red oak trees, such as pruning, utility right-of-way or fuel break maintenance, thinning, and harvesting during periods when overland transmission by nitidulid beetles is likely to occur. Following field studies, state resource agencies in Minnesota and Wisconsin issued guidelines that call for restricting such activities between mid-April and early or mid-July (e.g., MI DNR 2017, Jagemann et al. 2018, Juzwik et al 2018, Bonello 2019, WI DN 2019). State and local directives similarly aimed at limiting activities that could potentially attract spore-bearing beetles to wounds on otherwise healthy oaks have also been issued in Michigan (MI DNR 2017, Cook 2018, Sakalidis et al. 2020). These recommendations have been challenged, however, by utilities, foresters, arborists, and private landowners because relevant data to support such guidelines are lacking. Opponents have pointed out that oak wilt guidelines in Missouri, which originally identified a high-risk period of infection from April through June, had to be revised when studies in Missouri sites showed risk of infection was relatively low in June (Hayslett et al. 2008). Moreover, previous studies have often focused on 2 nitidulid species, Carpophilus sayi (Parsons 1943) and Colopterus truncatus (Randall 1838) (e.g., Khyl et al. 2002, Juzwik et al. 2004, Ambourne et al. 2005, Jagemann et al. 2018). Both species were collected from fresh wounds on red oaks in 2 sites in Minnesota (Juzwik et al. 2004). Beetles in other genera, including Glischrochilus and Epuraea, however, have also borne viable B. fagacearum spores when captured (e.g., Juzwik and French 1983, Kyhl et al. 2002). Less is known about these and other nitidulid species that may be present in red oak stands, their activity periods, and their propensity for collecting B. fagacearum spores.

To address these questions, we monitored nitidulid species from spring through autumn over 2 years using baited traps in oak wilt infection centers in mature red oak stands in northern Michigan. We documented the onset and peak of nitidulid activity, species abundance, and diversity, along with the proportion of captured beetles bearing viable B. fagacearum spores. Abundance of infected trees and a number of mycelial mats in the sites were quantified annually. Seasonal xylem development of red oaks was also tracked in both years to identify the onset and duration of earlywood production and transition to latewood production.

Methods

Study Sites

We selected 3 research sites, Nessen Road, Bunker Hill, and Coyote Road, in mature red oak stands on state forest land in northern Lower Michigan (Table 1), in cooperation with personnel from the Michigan Dept. of Natural Resources in 2017. A few trees killed by oak wilt infections were previously identified in all 3 sites, but no management activities or disturbances had occurred. We selected, tagged, and measured diameter at breast height (DBH) (1.3 m aboveground) of at least 80 apparently healthy red oak trees (DBH > 10 cm) in each site in early fall 2017. In 2019, an additional, smaller red oak stand at Bass Lake was included in the study (Table 1). At the Bass Lake site, we selected and measured DBH of 20 healthy red oaks in early spring 2019.

Monitoring Nitidulids

In spring 2018, satellite images were overlaid with a grid to divide each site (Table 1) into 6 areas of approximately equal size and a similar number of red oaks. A wind-oriented funnel trap designed to capture nitidulid beetles (Dowd et al. 1992) was installed in the center of each of the 6 areas. Three traps were hung from a lower canopy branch on a healthy red oak, approximately 20 m high, while the other 3 traps were hung 1.5 m high from a bent length of rebar embedded in the ground. Canopy and ground traps were alternated among the 6 areas within each site. Captured beetles were collected in glass vials (20 ml) in each trap. Vials were filled with crinkled brown craft paper (Wrap N Style, Michaels Stores, Irving, TX) limiting any contact and potential exchange of B. fagacearum spores between beetles (Dowd et al. 1992, Ambourn et al. 2005, Juzwik et al. 2006).

Each trap was baited with approximately 10 ml of fermenting whole wheat bread dough (WWBD), along with a lure consisting of the male-produced sex-aggregation pheromone for either Carpophilus sayi ([2E,4E,6E,8E]-3,5-dimethyl-7-ethyl-2,4,6,8-undecatetraene and [2E,4E,6E,8E]-3,5,7-trimethyl-2,4,6,8-undecatetraene), or Colopterus truncatus ([2E,4E,6E]-3,5-dimethyl-2,4,6-octatriene, [2E,4E,6E]-4,6-dimethyl-2,4,6-nonatriene, and [2E,4E,6E,8E]-3,5,7-trimethyl-2,4,6,8-decatetraene), or both (Great Lakes IPM, Vestaburg, MI) (Bartelt et al. 2004, Cossé and Bartelt 2000, Kyhl et al. 2002). At any given time, 1 canopy and 1 ground trap at each site had a Ca. sayi lure, a Co. truncatus lure, or both lures, in addition to the WWBD. Lures in traps were replaced on each trap check date, cycling through a Ca. sayi lure, followed by a Co. truncatus lure, followed by a combination of the 2 lures.

Traps were deployed and baited on 6–8 March 2018. Vials with captured nitidulids, lures, and WWBD were replaced at 2 wk intervals from 22 March to 3 November 2018.

In 2019, trapping methods were modified following evaluation of 2018 results and because of the resources and time needed for trapping at the additional Bass Lake site. Four ground traps were deployed in each of the 4 sites but given that no unique species were captured in canopy traps in 2018 (see Results), we did not deploy canopy traps in 2019. Trap locations were determined by dividing each site into 4 approximately equal areas with similar numbers of red oaks and installing the trap near the center of each area. All traps were baited with WWBD, along with either a Ca. sayi pheromone lure (2 traps) or the Co. truncatus pheromone lure (2 traps). As in 2018, traps were checked at 2 week intervals to replace beetle collection vials, lures, and bread dough. The Ca. sayi lures were replaced with Co. truncatus lures and vice versa on each trap check date. Traps were baited and deployed on 26–27 March 2019 and captured nitidulids were collected at the 4 sites from 7 April through 26 October.

DDs associated with trap check dates in 2018 and 2019 were acquired from the MSU Enviroweather station in Williamsburg, MI, located 14–85 km from the 4 sites. Degree days were calculated with the Baskerville–Emin method with a start date of 1 January and a base threshold of 10°C (MAWN 2022) and are presented as DD10C.

In both 2018 and 2019, vials with captured beetles were transported in coolers with ice from field sites to the Michigan State University (MSU) Forest Entomology lab in East Lansing, Ingham County, Michigan. Beetles were carefully removed from vials with forceps, placed in individual, sterilized microcentrifuge tubes, and then stored at −20°C until they could be identified. To avoid cross contaminating individuals within or between vials, forceps were dipped in 70% ethanol and then flamed after each beetle was removed. After beetles were identified to species, they were returned to the same individual tubes. Up to 10 beetles of each nitidulid species captured were mounted and submitted as voucher specimens to the Albert J. Cook Arthropod Research Collection at MSU.

Evaluating Bretziella fagacearum on Beetles

After nitidulids were identified to species, up to 10 individuals per species and trap, collected in each site on each trap check date, were screened for B. fagacearum. When fewer than 2 beetles of a species were captured in a site, specimens were mounted for identification and/or submission as voucher specimens, which precluded screening for B. fagacearum.

The presence of B. fagacearum fungus on captured beetles was determined by plating beetle “rinsate” using serial dilution plating techniques. Beetles collected in 2018 were processed following methods of Ambourn et al. (2005). A modified and slightly more rigorous protocol was used to process beetles captured in 2019 (Chahal 2024). Five hundred microliters of sterile water was added to each microcentrifuge tube containing a beetle specimen. Each specimen was crushed for 3–5 s using a tip sonicator to dislodge fungal propagules. The resulting suspension was serially diluted to make 2 additional 10-fold dilutions, and then 900 µl aliquots of each dilution was spread onto duplicate lactic acid-amended potato dextrose agar. One additional Petri dish was plated with 100 µl of undiluted “rinsate” of each beetle. Original suspensions (sonicated beetle in 500 µl water) and further 10-fold dilutions were vortexed for 3–5 s to homogenize samples before collecting aliquots for subsequent 10-fold dilutions and plating. A total of 5 Petri dishes were cultured from “rinsates” of each beetle. Sonicator tips were sterilized after processing each beetle by dipping the tips into 70% ethanol for 5 s, then rinsing with sterile water. Cultured Petri dishes were incubated at 25°C in the dark and inspected periodically for 14 days. Colony morphology and endoconidia characteristics were used to identify B. fagacearum colonies (de Beer et al. 2017). Additionally, axenic cultures from all spore-bearing beetles were confirmed using real-time PCR (Bourgault et al. 2022). The identity of 2 representative pure cultures was also confirmed by sequencing the internal transcribed spacer (ITS1F and ITS4R), following Chahal et al. (2022a). Colony forming units (CFUs) in each dish were quantified and averaged by species and date in 2018 and 2019.

Mycelial Mats

As part of a related study, 1 tree in the Nessen Road, Bunker Hill, and Coyote Road sites was inoculated monthly with 2 spore suspensions, each containing 1 × 106B. fagacearum endoconidia from August to October 2017, and again from April to November in 2018 and 2019 (Chahal 2022b, 2024). At the Bass Lake site, all infections were natural; no trees were inoculated. Tagged oaks in each site were surveyed on each visit and mycelial mats produced on either inoculated trees or naturally infected (noninoculated) trees were recorded in 2018 and again in 2019 (Chahal et al. 2021, Chahal 2024). Tree trunks were visually assessed and tapped with the back of a hatchet around the circumference from approximately 15 cm up to 3 m high. A distinct hollow sound is produced when bark above a mat is tapped. Trees were also examined with binoculars to locate distinctive splits or ruptures in the bark, indicative of mats. Mats below 3 m were marked with paint and observations for each tree were recorded to prevent double-counting on subsequent field visits.

Xylem Development

An increment borer (5.15 mm diameter) was used to collect cores with at least 4–5 years of xylem tissue from healthy red oaks that had not been inoculated or otherwise disturbed. Cores were collected 1.3 m above ground on the north-facing side of the trunk of 2 trees at each site every 2 wks when traps were checked in 2018 and 2019. After a core was extracted, the hole in the trunk was immediately sealed with silicone. Cores were stored with 50% ethanol in individual glass vials. Each core was dried, and then examined under a stereomicroscope to assess the presence of earlywood or latewood vessels. Cores continued to be collected (from different trees on each occasion) until latewood vessels were observed on all sampled trees on 2 consecutive samples. Cores were collected from 22 March through 20 June 2018 and from 4 April through August 8 in 2019.

Statistical Analysis

Total nitidulid captures on each date in the sites were analyzed using a Type III 2-way ANOVA (PROC MIXED; SAS 9.4) with collection date and site as fixed factors to identify periods when nitidulid activity was highest and to evaluate potential differences in captures among sites. Nitidulid data from 2018 and 2019 were analyzed separately. Assumptions of normality were tested with the Shapiro–Wilks test and residual plots, while homogeneity of variance was evaluated with Levene’s test. Nitidulid counts in 2018 and in 2019 were log transformed to meet ANOVA assumptions. When ANOVA results were significant (P < 0.05), the Tukey–Kramer adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied.

We further analyzed seasonal activity of the 5 most abundant nitidulid species captured in 2018 and 2019. Captures were log-transformed and then analyzed with a 2-way ANOVA to evaluate differences among sites and trap check dates for each species.

Binary logistic regression was used to analyze the presence–absence of viable oak wilt spores on subsets of the nitidulid beetles captured on each collection date using PROC GLIMMIX (SAS 9.4). Model terms included collection date and site, with the Satterthwaite approximation for calculating denominator degrees of freedom. In 2018, spore-bearing beetles were captured on consecutive dates and further contrasts were unnecessary. In 2019, an a priori contrast was performed to compare rates of spore-bearing beetles captured during the first group of dates (22 May to 5 June; 5–21 June) and the second group (14–29 August).

Potential differences in nitidulid captures among traps baited with different lures or between trap locations (i.e., ground vs. canopy traps) in 2018 were also evaluated. Indicator Species Analysis (ISA) was used to determine whether baiting traps with the Ca. sayi, Co. truncatus or combination of both lures in 2018 affected nitidulid captures. An indicator value, which can range from 0 to 100, was calculated to evaluate associations between nitidulid species captured with each lure and the combination of lures. Principle components analysis (PCA) was conducted in R using the vegan package (R Core Team 2023; Version 4.3.2) (Oksanen et al. 2022; Version 2.6.4) to compare beta diversity of nitidulid species captured in ground traps versus canopy traps at the 3 sites in 2018. Traps in which no beetles were caught were removed from analysis. For the PCA, species count data was Hellinger transformed to account for species relative abundances (Legendre et al. 2001).

Results

Nitidulid Captures in 2018

A total of 1,188 nitidulids representing 19 species were captured in the 18 traps at the 3 sites between 24 April (12 DD10C) and 3 November (1490 DD10C) in 2018 (Table 1). Number of nitidulid captures differed among dates of trap checks (F = 5.80; df = 14,137; P < 0.0001) (Fig. 1A). Baited traps were deployed in all 3 sites on 8 March but no nitidulids were captured until 24 April trap check, when single beetles were captured at the Coyote Road and Nessen Road sites. More than half (58%) of all nitidulid captures occurred between 8 May (61 DD10C) and 6 June (247 DD10C). Captures peaked on 22 May 2018 (112 DD10C) when 327 nitidulids (27.5% of the total) were collected in traps, which was significantly more beetles than on other trap check dates (Fig. 1A). Captures on 24 April and 6 June were higher than captures between 19 June and 7 August, which were higher than captures from mid-August through 3 November (Fig. 1A).

At all 3 sites, Ca. sayi was the most frequently captured species in 2018, comprising 39%, 34%, and 37% of total nitidulid captures at Bunker Hill, Coyote, and Nessen sites, respectively (Table 2). Captures of Ca. sayi differed among trap check dates (F = 4.55; df = 13, 75; P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2A) and among the 3 sites (F = 6.02; df = 2, 75; P = 0.0038). Captures of Ca. sayi were higher from 22 May through 24 July, when 87% of these beetles were trapped, than on other dates. This species was captured more frequently at Bunker Hill (t = 3.44; df = 2, 75; P = 0.0009) than at the Coyote site, but captures at Bunker Hill and Nessen did not differ (t = 1.46; df = 2, 75; P = 0.1482). Traps at the Nessen site captured more Ca. sayi than at the Coyote Road site (t = -2.14; df = 2, 75; P = 0.0360). At the Coyote and Nessen sites, approximately half (52–53%) of the Ca. sayi beetles were captured in canopy traps while 21% of the Ca. sayi collected at Bunker Hill were in canopy traps.

The next most captured species in 2018 was Carpophilus brachypterus, which accounted for 16% of the total (Table 2). Captures of this species did not differ among collection dates (F = 1.70; df = 13, 40; P = 0.0983) (Fig. 2A) or sites (F = 3.16; df = 2, 40; P = 0.533), but 79% of Ca. brachypterus beetles were collected in ground traps (IV = 21.1; P = 0.0034) (Fig. 2A).

Colopterus truncatus also represented 16% of the total captures (Table 2) and differed among collection dates (F = 2.18; df = 11, 42; P = 0.0344) (Fig. 2A) and sites (F = 10.83; df = 2, 42; P = 0.0002) in 2018. This species was captured more frequently at the Bunker Hill site than at the Coyote (t = 4.47; df = 2, 42; P < 0.0001) and Nessen sites (t = 3.32; df = 2, 42; P = 0.0024), which did not differ from each other (t = −1.33; df = 2,42; P = 0.1911). Most (68%) of the Co. truncatus beetles were collected between 8 May and 6 June, peaking on the 22 May 2018 trap check (Fig. 2A) and 89% were captured in ground traps (IV = 29.6; P = 0.0002).

Captures of Glischrochilus fasciatus represented 14% of total captures in 2018 (Table 2), did not differ among collection dates (F = 0.72; df = 11, 25; P = 0.7131) (Fig. 2A) or sites (F = 0.88; df = 2, 25; P = 0.4285) and 92% of these beetles were captured in ground traps (IV = 19.8; P = 0.0002). Similarly, captures of Glischrochilus sanguinolentus represented 6% of total captures in 2018 (Table 2), did not differ among collection dates (F = 0.42; df = 10, 23; P = 0.9217) (Fig. 2A) or sites (F = 1.02; df = 2, 23; P = 0.3746) and nearly all (93%) were captured in ground traps (IV = 22.0; P = 0.0002).

Canopy Versus Ground Traps in 2018

Species diversity and abundance of nitidulids captured in canopy versus ground traps were evaluated using 2018 data from all 3 sites. Ground traps caught a significantly higher diversity and number of beetles than canopy traps (F = 7.80; P = 0.0006). Canopy traps captured an average of 2 (±0.22) beetles per trap per collection date over the 8-month trapping season, compared to ground traps, which averaged 7 (±0.39) beetles per trap per collection date. Across the 2018 trapping season, canopy traps captured 14 species while ground traps captured 19 nitidulid species and 78% of all nitidulids. Results from PCA were similar, indicating that nitidulid species captured by canopy traps were also represented in ground trap captures (Fig. 3). ISA results showed that of the 21 species captured in 2018, only Colopterus maculatus (IV = 3; P = 0.1252) and Cryptarcha concinna (IV = 3.4; P = 0.5613) were indicators for canopy traps. Discussion

Establishment of a new oak wilt infection center requires a series of events to occur. One or more nitidulids must locate a tree previously infected and killed by oak wilt. Beetles must access a mycelial mat that is actively sporulating and viable B. fagacearum spores must adhere to their exoskeleton. While spores remain viable, those beetle(s) must then locate and visit, presumably to feed on sap, a relatively fresh wound that is deep enough to expose xylem on a healthy tree. Our study, which included monitoring activity of nitidulid species in stands with active oak wilt infections, determining the proportion of captured beetles bearing viable B. fagacearum fungal spores, and documenting the temporal availability of vulnerable earlywood tissue in red oaks, collectively provides information for identifying relative risk periods for overland oak wilt transmission over the growing season.

Our data are consistent with current recommendations issued by the Michigan Dept. of Natural Resources and similar to those of other resource management agencies in the region (e.g., Sagor et al. 2024) that call for limiting activities that could potentially wound healthy oaks and attract nitidulids between mid-April and mid-July (MI DNR 2017). Although baited traps in our sites were deployed in March in both 2018 and 2019, no nitidulid beetles were collected until the 24 April trap check in either year (approximately 10–40 DD10C) when a total of 2 and 9 beetles, representing less than 1.5% of the annual captures, were trapped in 2018 and 2019, respectively. Degree day accumulation can begin earlier in southern Michigan than in our study sites in northern Michigan, but the first nitidulid captures are perhaps best regarded as an early warning sign to wrap up activities that could expose xylem on live red oaks. A few nitidulids may be warm enough to fly to a fresh wound on a sunny day in spring (e.g., in March), but they are not necessarily bearing viable B. fagacearum spores unless they were also able to fly to a sporulating mycelial mat before visiting the new wound (e.g., Juzwik et al. 2004). Monitoring local degree day accumulations through websites such as MSU’s Enviro-weather in Michigan, the National Phenology Network (usanpn.org), or Cornell University’s climate smart farming (climatesmartfarming.org/tools/csf-growing-degree-day-calculator) can more precisely inform individual decisions by foresters or arborists.

While the onset of the high-risk period in spring is of interest, risk of overland spread to freshly wounded oaks will be greatest when active nitidulids and mats are relatively abundant in a stand. In our sites, captures of nitidulids varied seasonally in both years but were consistently highest in May and June when 86% and 81% of all nitidulids were collected in 2018 and 2019, respectively. Beetle captures peaked 3 wks later in 2019, corresponding to approximately 212 cumulative DD10C, compared with 112 DD10C in 2018. Delayed and extended nitidulid activity in 2019 likely reflects higher, more frequent precipitation along with substantially cooler temperatures in spring 2019, both of which would have limited or slowed beetle flight and activity. Studies in Minnesota and Wisconsin have similarly reported relatively high nitidulid activity, as evidenced by trap captures, in May and June (Juzwik et al. 2004, Ambourn et al. 2005, Jagemann et al. 2018). Moreover, despite the presence of fresh mycelial mats on either inoculated or naturally infected trees in our sites throughout much of the growing season, particularly in 2019, nearly all (96%) nitidulids with viable B. fagacearum spores were captured in either May or June in both years.

A notable increase in the percentage of beetles that yielded viable B. fagacearum spores between 2018 (6% of screened beetles) and 2019 (25% of screened beetles) reflects the abundance of mycelial mats on naturally infected trees at the Bass Lake site plus mat production by trees inoculated in 2018 in the other 3 sites (Table 4) (Chahal 2024). Presumably, the increased abundance of mycelial mats in 2019, particularly in May and June, increased the local availability of inoculum while VOCs associated with mats may also have attracted nitidulids from surrounding stands. Low rates of spore-bearing beetles in August 2019 reflected the reduced availability of fresh mycelial mats produced in July or August by trees that died earlier in the year (Chahal 2024). Of all the beetles screened for fungal spores, only 14% were captured in August and the 3 beetles that bore B. fagacearum spores represented only 3% of the total beetles screened in 2019. Additionally, while nitidulids were active in July, the risk for vectoring B. fagacearum in July was low, given that no mycelial mats were detected in any sites during that month and none of the screened beetles captured in July bore viable B. fagacearum spores. Oaks that visibly wilt and decline in early summer (through mid-July) may produce mats in August or September of the same year, while oaks that wilt later in the summer produce mats the following spring (Gibbs and French 1980, Chahal 2024). Thus, artificial inoculations, and perhaps the progression of the Bass Lake infection center, likely facilitated mat formation in August.

Although mycelial mats can be produced from spring through fall (Juzwik 1983, Chahal 2024), they are more likely to be present in spring when relatively high sap flow and wood moisture can adequately support mat formation (Tainter and Gubler 1973, Chahal 2024) than in fall. Given that only 19% of all nitidulids captured in 2019 were collected after 16 July, along with the infrequent production of mycelial mats between July and November, we conclude that while risk of overland spread of oak wilt is not zero in mid and late summer (e.g., July to September), the risk is much lower than in spring and early summer (May through June). Recent guidelines issued in Minnesota (Sagor et al. 2024) were similar except their high-risk period extended from May through July.

Numbers of spore-bearing beetles accessing fresh wounds to feed on sap emanating from healthy red oaks will vary among trees, sites, and over time, but the spore load borne by individual beetles may also affect the likelihood of infection and perhaps the rate of fungal growth. Spore loads on individual beetles likely reflect both availability of actively sporulating mycelial mats along with the time beetles spend on the mats. Fresh wounds on trees in Minnesota were visited by anywhere from zero to 228 beetles over a 6-day period, with <800 up to 18,000 CFUs per beetle (Juzwik et al. 2008). In contrast, average CFUs on nitidulids collected from fresh wounds in Missouri were consistently lower (≤3000 CFUs) (Hayslett et al. 2008). Variability in CFU loads among individual beetles captured in our sites was substantial and even when averaged by capture date or species, rates ranged from 484 CFUs per beetle up to more than 13,000 CFUs on 1 beetle. Given the relatively high CFUs borne by many of the beetles we screened, relatively few beetles may be needed for infection to occur.

Development of earlywood vessels in xylem of ring-porous trees, such as red oaks, typically begins 1–4 wks before bud break, with the first vessels fully expanded at bud break (Zsada and Zahner 1969, Kitin and Funada 2016). Earlywood development in relation to DDs was similar at all sites in both 2018 and 2019, although the duration of earlywood production differed between years. Red oaks in our sites produced earlywood throughout May in both years, but earlywood production extended into early July 2019, reflecting the slow warmup and frequent precipitation in May 2019. Large diameter (60–80 µm) earlywood vessels support high sap flow in spring when buds and leaves are expanding (Zasada and Zahner 1969). Earlywood cells may be more vulnerable to oak wilt infection because their size allows for easier movement of oak wilt conidia through the xylem compared to later in the summer when narrow-diameter latewood vessels (20–40 µm) are produced (Kitin and Funada 2016, Sousa et al. 2016, Massart 2017, Chahal 2024). Latewood vessel formation begins as leaf expansion is completed and is negatively correlated with mean maximum monthly temperatures (Kitin and Funada 2016). Although it seems reasonable to expect red oaks are more vulnerable to B. fagacearum before earlywood vessels give way to latewood formation, confirmation of this relationship requires further research. If earlywood vessels are associated with higher B. fagacearum infection rates or if fungal infection progresses more rapidly in earlywood than in latewood, red oaks will be especially vulnerable in May and possibly longer, depending on weather conditions.

We were particularly interested in relative abundance and activity of Ca. sayi, and Co. truncatus, given their previously reported role as key vectors of B. fagacearum and the previous studies in Minnesota and Wisconsin that focused on these 2 species (Cease and Juzwik, 2001, Juzwik et al. 2004, 2011, Ambourn et al. 2005, Jagemann et al. 2018). Peak activity of Ca. sayi, and Co. truncatus, along with Ca. brachypterus, which was also relatively abundant, corresponded to similar degree day accumulations in 2018 and 2019, although calendar dates differed by at least 2 wks. Most Ca. sayi and Co. truncatus at our sites were trapped between early May and early June 2018, with a distinct second peak occurring in July. In 2019, these 2 species were most abundant between mid-May and early July, but there was no prominent second peak later in the summer. The slight lag in captures in 2019 compared to 2018 was likely attributable to cool May temperatures and much greater spring precipitation in 2019. In Wisconsin, the first captures of Ca. sayi corresponded to approximately 200 DD10C (18 April 2016), lagging 3–4 wks behind the first captures of Co. truncatus, which corresponded to 50 DD10C (14 March 2016) (Jagemann et al. 2018). In our sites, however, while Ca. sayi was captured in much greater numbers than Co. truncatus, both species were relatively abundant in May and June and then continued to be collected through the summer. Variation in nitidulid activity within a growing season is not surprising given the extent of microclimatic variation within a forest stand and differences among years and between sites may be even more pronounced.

High captures of Ca. sayi and Co. truncatus in traps or from artificial wounds in spring have been linked with the relative abundance of mycelial mats in May and June in previous studies (e.g., Cease and Juzwik 2001, Juzwik et al. 2004). In 2019, we observed fresh mats throughout much of the summer on inoculated trees, along with naturally infected trees in our sites. While life cycles of most nitidulid species have not been determined, seasonal patterns of beetle captures suggest overwintered adults were especially attracted to baited traps in spring. Lower captures continued through much of the summer, perhaps as beetles dispersed to utilize other food sources that became available or reflecting an extended period of nitidulid reproduction and emergence of new adult beetles.

In previous studies in the Great Lakes region of the United States, number and proportion of nitidulid beetles bearing viable B. fagacearum spores were consistently higher in May and June than later in the summer (Juzwik et al. 2004, Ambourn et al. 2005). In 2018, 4% of Ca. sayi, and 7% of Co. truncatus captured in our traps, which were systematically distributed in stands, were bearing spores. Not surprisingly, these rates were lower compared with niditulids collected directly from mycelial mats (Juzwik and French 1983, Cease and Juzwik 2001, 2004) but were almost identical to data from Wisconsin where traps were intentionally placed near mat-producing trees (Jagemann et al. 2018). In 2019, however, overall rates of beetles bearing spores captured in our sites jumped substantially; 31% of the Ca. sayi, 46% of the Co. truncatus, and 20% of the Ca. brachypterus beetles we screened carried viable B. fagacearum spores. Captures of spore-bearing nitidulids consistently aligned with peak production of mycelial mats on dead trees from mid-May to early June in our sites (Chahal 2024), reflecting positive associations between production of B. fagacearum spores and spring weather (Curl 1955).

We identified all captured nitidulids to species, regardless of whether the species was previously reported to be a vector of B. fagacearum. Other studies have focused specifically on Ca. sayi and Co. truncatus (e.g., Ambourn et al. 2005, Jagemann et al. 2018); spore-bearing individuals of both species were collected from artificial wounds on red oaks in Minnesota (Juzwik et al. 2004). However, this focus risks overlooking potentially important vectors that are at least somewhat attracted to volatiles emanating from mycelial mats and fresh wounds on oaks. In addition to Ca. sayi and Co. truncatus, we captured 4 other nitidulid species with viable B. fagacearum spores, including Glishchrochilus sanguinolentus (Oliv.) and G. fasciatus (Oliv.), along with Ca. brachyperus, Ca. dimidiatus. In Minnesota, these same 2 Glischrochilus species, along with G. quadrisignatus (Say) and Epuraea corticina (Erichson) were collected directly from mycelial mats (Cease and Juzwik 2001). A 2017 study in 3 northeastern United States sites deployed funnel, modified pitfall, and wind-oriented traps baited with the same pheromone lures used in our wind-oriented traps, plus fermenting liquid similar to the fermenting whole wheat bread dough used in our traps (DiGirolomo et al. 2020). They captured 34 nitidulid species across the 3 sites, indicative of the substantial diversity of this family. We note that 5 of the 6 species carrying B. fagacearum spores that were captured in our sites were among the most abundant species captured by DiGirolomo et al. (2020).

Since lures with the Ca. sayi and Co. truncatus pheromones became commercially available in 2005, most studies of B. fagacearum vectors have employed these lures, along with fermenting dough or liquid, to attract nitidulids to traps (e.g., Ambourn et al. 2005, Hayslett et al. 2008, Jagemann et al. 2018, DiGirolomo et al. 2020). Bartelt et al. (2004) reported these pheromone compounds were broadly attractive to multiple nitidulid species, which is supported by the diverse assemblage of nitidulids captured by DiGirolomo et al. (2020). High numbers of Ca. sayi captured in traps in our sites that were baited with the Ca. sayi aggregation pheromone could indicate that this species is more abundant than other species, reflect the strong attraction of these beetles to this pheromone, or both. For other nitidulid species, however, including Co. truncatus, number of captures did not differ among traps baited with the Ca. sayi lure, the Co. truncatus lure, or both lures. Moreover, no unique species were attracted to traps baited with 1 or both lures. In the future, it would be interesting to compare nitidulid abundance and species assemblages in traps baited only with fermenting dough versus traps baited with only the Ca. sayi pheromone and no fermenting material.

We originally hypothesized that different nitidulid species might be captured in traps suspended in the canopy of mature red oaks versus ground traps. An early study with nitidulids (Skalbeck 1976) and work with other forest insects such as cerambycid beetles have shown species assemblages can differ substantially between baited traps hung on branches in tree canopies and traps hung on rebar poles at ground level, even when they are directly below the canopy traps (e.g., Graham et al. 2012, Engelken and McCullough 2020). In our sites, however, ground traps captured more beetles and nitidulid species than canopy traps, and all species collected by the canopy traps were also collected in ground traps. Nitidulid beetles are generalist feeders capable of utilizing a range of food sources, including decaying fruit, nectar, woody material, detritus, and fungal exudates (Gaszit et al. 1982, Lin and Phelan 1992, Zeran et al. 2006). Several such food sources likely occur most often on or near the ground, which would limit the need for beetles to access the canopies of large trees. Overwintering sites or larval development may also affect the spatial distribution of nitidulids in a given site. If adult beetles emerge from leaf litter or down woody material in spring, they may be more likely to detect or be attracted to lures on nearby ground traps compared to canopy traps. In agricultural fields, where nitidulid beetles are sometimes considered to be pests, adults reportedly overwinter in leaf litter and uncultivated areas bordering agricultural fields (Noronha and Mason 2017). Relatively little information on nitidulid behavior and development in forested sites is available, however, presumably because of the difficulty of attempting to monitor these tiny beetles in a natural environment (Bai et al. 2017, Eliceche et al. 2017).

Whether species captured in the baited traps accurately represent the species assemblage and relative abundance of nitidulids in our sites and more broadly, in red oak forests in the Great Lakes region (Cossé and Bartelt 2000), is largely unknown. Many species were captured in low numbers in our traps and those of DiGirolomo et al. (2020), possibly because they were only attracted to the fermenting whole wheat bread dough or even somewhat repelled by pheromones of sympatric nitidulids. For example, although we captured only a few Ca. dimidiatus in our traps, 33–50% of those beetles were bearing B. fagacearum spores. Spore-bearing Ca. dimidiatus were also captured in Minnesota (Juzwik and French 1983), indicating these beetles are attracted to mycelial mats, whether or not they are attracted to baited traps. Additionally, Cease and Juzwik (2001) reported species composition of nitidulids visiting mats changed over a matter of a few days as mats aged. These results suggest nitidulid species can be underrepresented in trap collections but still play important roles as vectors of B. fagacearum, while other species that are strongly attracted to baited traps may be less likely to carry high loads of viable spores. Further research to identify and assess nitidulid attraction to key VOCs associated with sporulating mats and fresh wounds on healthy red oaks would be helpful for vector monitoring and facilitate more specific assessments of relative risks of overland spread throughout the year.
