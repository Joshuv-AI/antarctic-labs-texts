Learning Variational Inequalities from Data: Fast Generalization Rates under Strong Monotonicity
Eric Zhao, Tatjana Chavdarova, Michael Jordan

Abstract
Variational inequalities (VIs) are a broad class of optimization problems encompassing machine learning problems ranging from standard convex minimization to more complex scenarios like min-max optimization and computing the equilibria of multi-player games. In convex optimization, strong convexity allows for fast statistical learning rates requiring only Θ ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) \Theta(1/\varepsilon) stochastic first-order oracle calls to find an ε \varepsilon -optimal solution, rather than the standard Θ ⁡ ( 1 / ε 2 ) \Theta(1/\varepsilon^{2}) calls. This note provides a simple overview of how one can similarly obtain fast Θ ⁡ ( 1 / ε ) \Theta(1/\varepsilon) rates for learning VIs that satisfy strong monotonicity , a generalization of strong convexity. Specifically, we demonstrate how standard stability-based generalization arguments for convex minimization extend directly to VIs when the domain admits a small covering, or when the operator is integrable and suboptimality is measured by potential functions such as when finding equilibria in multi-player games.



1
Introduction

Variational inequalities

Stampacchia (1964)
model a broad range of optimization and fixed-point problems. They are concerned with finding a solution
{\bm{z}}^{\star}
from a continuous domain
\mathcal{Z}
, such that

\langle{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\star},F({\bm{z}}^{\star})\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}\,,\vskip-1.99997pt

(VI)

where
F\colon\mathcal{Z}\mapsto\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a continuous map, and
\mathcal{Z}
is a subset of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The operator
F
can be understood as encoding both the optimality condition and first-order information of a VI.

Variational inequalities (
VI
s) are a popular framework primarily due to their generality—they encompass convex minimization problems (when
F\equiv\nabla f
), min-max optimization problems, complementarity problems
(
Cottle and Dantzig, 1968
)
, and equilibrium computation problems for general games.
VIs therefore provide a language for proving results that are applicable to a wide range of optimization problems.
As an example, consider a game with
k
agents, each with a strategy
{\bm{z}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{i}}
and aiming to optimize their own objective
f_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Denoting the players’ joint strategy with
{\bm{z}}\equiv[{\bm{z}}_{1}^{\intercal},\dots,{\bm{z}}_{k}^{\intercal}]^{\intercal}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},d=\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{i}
, we can write the problem of finding an equilibrium in this game as equivalently solving a
VI
whose operator
F
represents the gradients of each player’s objective:

F({\bm{z}})\equiv\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{{\bm{z}}_{1}}f_{1}({\bm{z}})\\
\dots\\
\nabla_{{\bm{z}}_{k}}f_{k}({\bm{z}})\\
\end{bmatrix}\,.

We refer the reader to
Facchinei and Pang (2003)
for more examples.
Due to their generality, VIs also find applications in economics, game theory, and machine learning, to multi-agent reinforcement learning, GANs, and other problems involving trade-offs
Nagurney and Zhang (1995)
;
Martínez-Legaz (1990)
;
Scutari et al. (2010)
;
Lowe et al. (2017)
;
Sidahmed and Chavdarova (2024)
;
Gidel et al. (2019)
.

In many applications of variational inequalities, the operator
F
may not be known a priori.
Instead, one may only have access to historical datapoints for the operator or other noisy finite estimates of operator evaluations.
Indeed, this is often the case in many practical applications of VIs:

(i)

auction games
—where an auctioneer and bidders interact so as to allocate resources
(
McAfee and McMillan, 1987
;
Riley, 1989
;
Milgrom, 2004
;
Krishna, 2009
)
;

(ii)

various
network games

(
Galeotti et al., 2010
;
Parise and Ozdaglar, 2019
)
—which model the interaction between parts of information systems controlled by different parties; or

(iii)

alternating multi-agent games
—where the goal is to perform well on an unseen game
(
Vinyals et al., 2017
, e.g., mini-games in)
.

The key challenge with learning VI solutions in these applications is contending with uncertainty due to noisy observation model.
This noise can originate from different sources depending on the application, such as stochasticity in agents’ decisions, noisy mini-batch evaluation of the operator, or having access only to unbiased variations of the “ground-truth” operator
F
.
One approach to learning VI solutions from noisy data is to run a conventional algorithm, such as gradient descent, on an empirical estimate of the operator and then bound generalization error, i.e., prove that a near-optimal solution for the empirical operator estimate is also near-optimal for the true operator.

One classical approach to bounding generalization error exploits the fact that learning algorithms that exhibit
algorithmic stability

(
Rogers and Wagner, 1978
;
Devroye and Wagner, 1979
)
—a measure of how sensitive an algorithm’s outputs are to small perturbations in the input dataset—must have limited generalization error.
In strongly convex optimization problems, the key advantage of stability-based generalization analysis is that—even for simple first-order methods—they provide tighter sample complexity rates of order
O(1/\varepsilon)
where
\varepsilon
denotes the error tolerance
Bousquet and Elisseeff (2002)
.
This is in contrast to the
O(1/\varepsilon^{2})
samples needed by stochastic approximation methods
Nemirovski and Yudin (1983)
.
Recent papers have extended these stability-based generalization results to strongly-convex strongly-concave zero-sum games
Li and Liu (2022)
;
Farnia and Ozdaglar (2021)
;
Zhang et al. (2020)
.

In this note, we demonstrate how to directly extend stability-based generalization proofs for convex minimization to variational inequalities.
In particular, we demonstrate how fast sample complexity rates of
O(1/\varepsilon)
are readily obtainable in strongly monotone VIs,
which are the natural VI generalization of strongly convex optimization (see Section
2
) and include strongly-convex-concave min-max optimization as a special case.
A technical issue that we address is that the conventional measure of sub-optimality in VIs, the
gap function
, can be unstable.
This is a property of gap functions that is not unique to VIs and can be bypassed in two ways.
First, if a VI’s domain admits a small covering, the generalization error of the gap function is stable and fast generalization rates can be shown accordingly.
Second, if the operator is integrable and sub-optimality is measured in terms of potential functions, one can still prove fast generalization rates by analyzing the stability of a surrogate for the gap function;
a common example is computing the Nash equilibrium of a multi-player game whose players have strongly convex/concave utilities.

Related works.

Algorithmic stability is a standard approach to characterizing the generalization of a learning algorithm
(

;

.

.
These results prove fast sample complexity rates of

Recently, a number of works have proven similar fast rates for strongly-convex-concave games

Farnia and Ozdaglar (2021)

.
This note generalizes and streamlines these results by showing that, in fact, the same stability arguments used in convex minimization can be directly extended to VIs.

We also note that there is a long history of results studying the convergence of first-order methods in VIs.
In deterministic problems, the development of efficient algorithms with convergence guarantees has recently been the focus of interest in machine learning and optimization in both unconstrained
(
Tseng, 1995
;
Daskalakis et al., 2018
;
Mokhtari et al., 2019
;
Mokhtari et al., 2020
;
Golowich et al., 2020
;

, see, e.g.,)
, and constrained settings

.
For stochastic problems, the seminal work of
Juditsky et al. (2011)

algorithms.

2
Preliminaries

We will use bold small letters denote vectors
and curly capital letters denote sets.
We also use
\mathcal{Z}
to denote a convex and compact set in the Euclidean space
\mathcal{E}
, with inner product
\left<\cdot,\cdot\right>
and norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
.

2.1
Variational inequalities (VIs)

A
VI
is represented by the tuple
(F,\mathcal{Z})
containing its operator
F
and domain
\mathcal{Z}
.
We use
D
to denote the diameter of the domain
\mathcal{Z}
where

D\coloneqq\max_{{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert

will be treated as a constant; for example, if
\mathcal{Z}
is the probability simplex
\Delta_{d}
and
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
is the
\ell_{1}
norm, then
D=2
.

The most common solution concept for a variational inequality is the
Stampacchia solution
, which is defined as a solution
{\bm{z}}_{\star}\in\mathcal{Z}
where
\left<F({\bm{z}}_{\star}),{\bm{z}}_{\star}-{\bm{z}}\right>\leq 0
for all
{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}
.
A standard quantity for measuring how close a solution
{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}
is to being a Stampacchia solution is the
gap function
:

\displaystyle{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}_{F}({\bm{z}})\triangleq\max_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{Z}}\ \left<F({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-{\bm{u}}\right>\,.

When
F
is clear from context, we will omit the subscript and write
{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}
.

When we have a VI with a conservative operator
F
—
F
is the gradient(s) of a potential function(s)—another common numerical quantification of suboptimality is the gap between the best attainable potential versus the iterate’s potential, which we will refer to as the
potential gap
.
Specifically, if the domain has a product structure
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}_{1}\times\dots\times\mathcal{Z}_{k}
, we can interpret the VI as a game with
k
players.
For each player
i\in[d]
, the restriction of the operator
F
to subspace
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
, denoted by
F_{i}
, can be interpreted as the gradient of their potential/utility function
f_{i}
.
We can then define the suboptimality of a solution
{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}
as sum of each player’s suboptimality:

\mathrm{Err}_{F}({\bm{z}})\triangleq\sum_{i\in[k]}f_{i}({\bm{z}})-\min_{{\bm{z}}_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}}f_{i}({\mathbf{z}}_{i}^{*},{\mathbf{z}}_{-i}).

Throughout, we will adopt the standard assumptions that an operator
F
is
L
-
Lipschitz
and
monotone
.
These conditions ensure that a (weak) solution to the VI exists
(
Facchinei and Pang, 2003
, Chapter
2
)
.

Assumption 2.1

(
L
-Lipschitz operator)
.

An operator
F\colon\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
is
L
-Lipschitz if and only if:

\displaystyle\left\lVert F({\bm{z}})-F({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\right\rVert\leq L\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert,\quad\forall{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}\,.

Assumption 2.2

(Monotone operator)
.

An operator
F\colon\mathcal{Z}\rightarrow\mathcal{E}
is monotone if and only if:

\displaystyle\left<{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime},F({\bm{z}})-F({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\right>\geq 0,\quad\forall{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}\,.

Monotonicity can be understood as an analog of the assumption of convexity.
In some cases, we may assume the operator is strongly monotone, which is a stronger assumption as it implies monotonicity. One can understand strong monotonicity as generalizing strong convexity.

Assumption 2.3

(
\mu
-strongly monotone operator)
.

An operator
F\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{E}
is said to be
\mu
-strongly monotone if and only if there exists
\mu>0
such that:

\displaystyle\left<{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime},F({\bm{z}})-F({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\right>\geq\mu\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2},\quad\forall{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}\,.

In strongly monotone settings, the ratio
L/\mu
is known as the
condition number
of the problem.
For ease of presentation, we will focus on how generalization rates scale with dimension, sample complexity, and error tolerance.
As such, the condition number should be understood to be a constant quantity on which we do not necessarily seek a tight characterization, although we will always state dependence on the condition number for completeness.

Finally, we will assume that the VIs we study are in an unconstrained setting.

Assumption 2.4

(Unconstrained setting)
.

There exists
{\bm{z}}^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}
such that
F({\bm{z}}^{*})=0
.

2.2
Generalization and algorithmic stability

We are interested in solving a variational inequality problem without direct access to an operator oracle
{\bm{z}}\mapsto F({\bm{z}})
by learning from data.
In this setting, we are restricted to only knowing the domain
\mathcal{Z}
and having access to a noisy operator oracle
\Xi
and a series of noise inputs
{\bm{\zeta}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{\zeta}}_{n}
drawn i.i.d. from some data distribution
\mathcal{D}
whose support we will denote as
\mathcal{X}
.
Formally,
\Xi
is a Borel function that given a sample
{\bm{\zeta}}\sim\mathcal{D}
provides the unbiased estimate

\displaystyle\forall{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}:\Exp_{{\bm{\zeta}}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\Xi({\bm{z}},{\bm{\zeta}})\right]=F({\bm{z}}).

Each
{\bm{\zeta}}_{i}
can be understood as a datapoint in a dataset
X
of size
n
.
Given a dataset
X\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
, we will use the shorthand
\widehat{F}_{X}({\bm{z}})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Xi({\bm{z}},{\bm{\zeta}}_{i})
and
\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}({\bm{z}})=\Xi({\bm{z}},{\bm{\zeta}}_{i})
.
Two datasets
X,X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
are
neighboring
if they disagree in at most one entry.

We assume that noisy operator estimates satisfy the same assumptions as the true operator
F
(i.e., Assumptions
2.2
,
2.1
), as is standard in generalization literature, e.g.
Hardt et al. (2016)
;
Farnia and Ozdaglar (2021)
.
We will also assume that operator samples are of bounded norm to control the variance of the noise.

Assumption 2.5

(Bounded Operator Norm)
.

The operator
F
has bounded norm at every
{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert F({\bm{z}})\right\rVert^{2}\leq K\,,\qquad\forall{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}\,.

An empirical variant of an optimization algorithm, e.g. gradient descent (
GD
), then follows from running the algorithms on the empirical operator
\widehat{F}_{X}
computed from a dataset
X\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
rather than the true operator
F({\bm{z}})
.
We aim to understand the associated generalization error of using our empirical operator
\widehat{F}_{X}
instead of the true one,
F
.
Given a candidate solution
{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}
produced by a learning algorithm trained on a dataset
\mathcal{X}_{n}
, we define its empirical gap function value as

\widehat{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}_{X}({\bm{z}})\coloneqq\max_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left<\widehat{F}_{X}({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-{\mathbf{w}}\right>

and its generalization gap as
{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}({\bm{z}})-\widehat{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}_{X}({\bm{z}})
.
In this work, we will mainly focus on settings where we drive the empirical gap function
\widehat{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}_{X}({\bm{z}})
to zero by training on the empirical operator
\widehat{F}_{X}
for many iterations, and only seek to control the generalization gap.
We analogously define the empirical potential gap
\widehat{\mathrm{Err}}_{X}({\bm{z}})
and the generalization potential gap
\mathrm{Err}({\bm{z}})-\widehat{\mathrm{Err}}_{X}({\bm{z}})
.

Stability.

We say that a mapping is
uniformly stable
if changing one record
{\bm{\zeta}}_{i}
in its input (a dataset) does not significantly change the output.

Definition 2.6

(Uniformly Stable Algorithm)
.

An algorithm
A\colon\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Z}
is
\gamma
-uniformly stable if, for all datasets
X\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
and
X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
that differ in only one element it holds that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert A(X)-A(X^{\prime})\right\rVert\leq\gamma\,.

Composing a uniformly stable algorithm with a Lipschitz loss results in a uniformly bounded function.
Uniformly bounded functions admit powerful generalization bounds
(
Bousquet and Elisseeff, 2002
)
that, for instance, can allow for obtaining dimension-free rates or fast rates.

Definition 2.7

(Uniformly Bounded Functions)
.

A mapping
f\colon\mathcal{X}^{n}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
is
\gamma
-uniformly bounded if, for all datasets
X\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
and
X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}^{n}
that differ in only one element, and all evaluation data points
{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}
, it holds that:

\displaystyle\left|f(X,{\bm{x}})-f(X^{\prime},{\bm{x}})\right|\leq\gamma\,.

2.3
VI methods

Among the most commonly used first-order algorithms for solving
VI
s is the (stochastic)
gradient descent
method.
Gradient descent naturally extends to the setting of VI optimization as follows:

{\bm{z}}_{t+1}={\bm{z}}_{t}-\eta F({\bm{z}}_{t})\,,

(GD)

where
\eta\in[0,1]
denotes a step size.

The results we demonstrate for (
GD
) can also be applied to obtain corresponding sample complexity bounds for other first-order VI methods, such as
optimistic
GD
(
Popov, 1980
)
.

Stability.

It is known that the iterates produced by gradient descent are stable in norm in settings with smoothness (Assumption
2.1
), strong monotonicity (Assumption
2.3
) and bounded operator norms (Assumption
2.5
)
Hardt et al. (2016)
.

Lemma 2.8

(GD stability)
.

Consider a smooth strongly monotone variational inequality
(F,\mathcal{Z})
(Assumption
2.1
,
2.3
,
2.5
).
Given neighboring datasets
X\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
and
X^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}_{n}
, let
{\bm{z}}_{T}
and
{\bm{z}}_{T}^{\prime}
denote the
T
th iterates of gradient descent (Algorithm (
GD
)) on the empirical operators given by
X
and
X^{\prime}
respectively.
For step sizes
0<\eta<\frac{2\mu}{L^{2}}
, uniform stability holds with

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\bm{z}}_{T}-{\bm{z}}_{T}^{\prime}\right\rVert\leq\frac{2K}{n(2\mu-\eta L^{2})}.

3
Fast Gap Function Bounds for Small Domains

The primary challenge with performing stability-based generalization analysis for variational inequalities is that the variational term for gap functions,
\argmax_{{\bm{z}}^{\star}\in Z}\left<F({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\star}\right>
,
is unstable.
This challenge is not unique to variational inequalities but rather an artifact of the gap function.
From another perspective, stability proofs involve a symmetrization step that typically assume that the training loss is a linear combination of losses computed on individual datapoints. In contrast, the term
\argmax_{{\bm{z}}^{\star}\in Z}\langle\widehat{F}_{X}({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\star}\rangle
may depend non-linearly on the dataset
X
.

A natural approach is to take a union bound over the possible values of this variational term.
Specifically, we can define for every point in the solution set
w\in\mathcal{Z}
, the loss

\left<\widehat{F}({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-w\right>=\tfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<\Xi({\bm{z}},{\bm{\zeta}}),{\bm{z}}-w\right>,

(1)

which is linear in the datapoints
\left\{{\bm{\zeta}}_{i}\right\}_{i\in[n]}
.
This reduces the problem of bounding the generalization error of the gap function to bounding the generalization error of a linear loss function (
1
) and then taking a union bound over the possible values of
w
in the domain
\mathcal{Z}
.
Indeed, for strongly monotone VIs with small domains, this approach provides fast in-expectation generalization error bounds of
O(1/n)
, where
n
is the size of the dataset.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(F,\mathcal{Z})
be a smooth strongly monotone VI (Assumptions
2.1
,
2.3
,
2.5
).
The expected generalization gap for a
\gamma
-stable algorithm
A\colon\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Z}
given
n
datapoints is, for
r>0
,

\displaystyle\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}_{{F}}}}(A(X))-{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}_{{\widehat{F}}}}}(A(X))\right]\leq O\bigg(Kr+(LD+K)\gamma\log(N(\mathcal{Z},r,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert))\bigg),

where
N(\mathcal{Z},r,\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert)
is the
r
-covering number for
\mathcal{Z}
.

Proof.

Let us first fix a choice of
{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{Z}
.
This stability proof begins with a standard symmetrization argument.
Let us define
X^{(i)}
as a copy of the dataset
X
but whose
i
th record
X_{i}
is replaced with that of dataset
X^{\prime}
, i.e.
X^{\prime}_{i}
.
We can thus write

\begin{split}&\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\left<F(A(X)),A(X)-{\mathbf{w}}\right>\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Exp_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}\\
X^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left[\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}(A(X^{(i)})),A(X^{(i)})-{\mathbf{w}}\right>\right].\end{split}

(2)

Next, recall that, due to the
\gamma
-stability of the algorithm
A
, for any realization of the datasets
X
and
X^{\prime}
, we have by definition that

\left\lVert A(X)-A(X^{(i)})\right\rVert\leq\gamma
.
Applying Hölder’s inequality and the
L
-smoothness of
\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}
, we can bound the symmetrization gap by

\begin{split}&{\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}(A(X^{(i)})),A(X^{(i)})-{\mathbf{w}}\right>-\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}(A(X)),A(X)-{\mathbf{w}}\right>}\\
&\leq\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}(A(X^{(i)}))-\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}(A(X)),A(X^{(i)})-{\mathbf{w}}\right>+\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}(A(X)),A(X)-A(X^{(i)})\right>\\
&\leq\;LD\gamma+K\gamma.\end{split}

As a result, the random variable

\displaystyle\Delta(X,X^{\prime},{\mathbf{w}})\coloneqq\;

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}(A(X)),A(X)-{\mathbf{w}}\right>-\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}(A(X^{(i)})),A(X^{(i)})-{\mathbf{w}}\right>,

is always bounded by
\Delta(X,X^{\prime},{\mathbf{w}})\leq 2(LD+K)\gamma
.
This implies that
\Delta(X,X^{\prime},{\mathbf{w}})
is a
(LD+K)\gamma
-sub-Gaussian random variable.
For any finite subset
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}\subset\mathcal{Z}
of our convex set, we can thus apply the maximal inequality to obtain a uniform bound over all potential values of the variational term
{\mathbf{w}}
:

\Exp_{X,X^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\max_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\Delta(X,X^{\prime},{\mathbf{w}})\right]\leq\sqrt{2}(LD+K)\gamma\log(|\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}|).

We can thus apply Jensen’s inequality and (
2
) to bound the generalization gap when
{\mathbf{w}}
is restricted to the subset
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
:

\sqrt{2}(LD+K)\gamma\log(|\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}|)\geq\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\max_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}}\left<F(A(X)),A(X)-{\mathbf{w}}\right>\right]-\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}_{{\widehat{F}}}}}(A(X))\right].

(3)

We now choose the subset
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}
to cover the domain
\mathcal{Z}
, proceeding with a one-step discretization for ease of exposition.
Let
\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}
be the smallest
\delta
-covering of the set
\mathcal{Z}
in its norm
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert
; i.e., for each
{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}
, there exists
{\bm{z}}_{\delta}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}
such that
\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}_{\delta}\right\rVert\leq\delta
.
Fixing
\delta>0
, we can bound the generalization error by

\displaystyle\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}_{F}(A(X))-{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}_{\widehat{F}}(A(X))\right]

\displaystyle=\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\max_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}}\left<F(A(X)),A(X)-{\mathbf{w}}\right>\right]-\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}_{\widehat{F}}(A(X))\right]

\displaystyle+\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}_{F}(A(X))\right]-\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\max_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}}\left<F(A(X)),A(X)-{\mathbf{w}}\right>\right].

We can upper bound the first two summands by (
3
) to be at most

\displaystyle\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\max_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{Z}_{\Delta}}\left<F(A(X)),A(X)-{\mathbf{w}}\right>\right]-\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}_{\widehat{F}}(A(X))\right]\leq\sqrt{2}(LD+K)\gamma\log(|\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}|).

As
\mathcal{Z}_{\delta}
is a
\delta
-cover, the next two summands are at most

\displaystyle\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}_{F}(A(X))\right]-\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\max_{{\mathbf{w}}\in\mathcal{Z}_{2^{-M}}}\left<F(A(X)),A(X)-{\mathbf{w}}\right>\right]\leq K\delta.

∎

Due to the linear structure of the inner product in the gap function, it is possible to control the generalization error by taking a union bound only over the extremal points of the domain.
This means that, in certain settings, we can improve on the rates of Theorem
3.1
.
For example, let us consider the (
d+1
)-dimensional probability simplex
\Delta_{d}
equipped with the L1 norm.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
(F,\Delta_{d})
be a smooth strongly monotone variational inequality (Assumptions
2.1
,
2.3
,
2.5
).
The expected generalization gap for a
\gamma
-stable algorithm
A\colon\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\Delta_{d}
is:

\displaystyle\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}_{{F}}}}(A(X))-{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}_{{\widehat{F}}}}}(A(X))\right]\in O\big((L+K)\gamma\log d\big).

Proof.

Because linear functions are maximized on the vertices of a simplex,
we have that

\min_{w\in\Delta_{d}}\left<F(A(X)),w\right>=\min_{i\in[d+1]}\left<F(A(X)),e_{i}\right>,

where
e_{i}
is the
i
th standard basis vector.
It thus suffices to only vary
w
over the set
\mathcal{Z}^{\prime}=\left\{e_{i}\right\}_{i\in[d+1]}
.
The claim then follows by (
3
).
∎

We can, for example, plug the
\gamma=\frac{2K}{n(2\mu-\eta L^{2})}
stability of the gradient descent algorithm (Lemma
2.8
) into Theorem
3.2
to obtain a fast
O(1/n)
generalization error bound in strongly monotone VIs.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
(F,\Delta_{d})
be a smooth strongly monotone variational inequality (Assumptions
2.1
,
2.3
,
2.5
).
The expected generalization error of the gradient descent algorithm
A\colon\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\Delta_{d}
with sufficiently small learning rate
\eta\to 0
and large time horizon
T\to\infty
is:

\displaystyle\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[{\mathop{\hbox{\rm Err}}}_{F}(A(X))\right]\leq\widetilde{O}\bigg(\frac{K\cdot(L+K)}{n\mu}\bigg),

where
\widetilde{O}
hides logarithmic factors.

4
Fast Potential Gap Bounds for Games

We now turn to the case of variational inequalities that describe multi-player games, where we assume that the operator
F
is conservative and the domain has the product structure
\mathcal{Z}=\mathcal{Z}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{Z}_{k}
.
Throughout this section, we let
F_{i}
denote the restriction of the operator
F
to player
i
’s strategy space
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
, and use
\mu_{i}
and
L_{i}
to denote player
i
’s strong monotonicity constant and the smoothness constant respectively; note that
\mu_{i}\geq\mu
and
L_{i}\leq L
.

Another method of managing the instability of the gap function is to avoid bounding the generalization error of the gap function itself and only seek to bound the
potential gap
.
In many applications, such as finding Nash equilibrium in multi-player games, only the latter is of interest and the gap function serves only as a means to upper bound the potential gap.
In these cases, we can define a surrogate gap function that replaces the unstable term
\argmax_{{\bm{z}}^{\star}\in Z}\left<F({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\star}\right>
with

w^{*}({\bm{z}})=\bigg[\argmin_{{\bm{z}}_{i}^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}}f_{i}({\bm{z}}_{i}^{*},{\bm{z}}_{-i})\bigg]_{i\in[k]}.

(4)

We denote this weak form of the gap function as

\mathrm{WeakGap}_{F}({\mathbf{z}})\triangleq\left<F({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-w^{*}({\mathbf{z}})\right>,

and note that it also upper bounds the potential gap by

\mathrm{Err}_{F}({\mathbf{z}})\leq\mathrm{WeakGap}_{F}({\mathbf{z}})
.
Unlike the gap function, this weaker gap function is stable due to the stability of the term
w^{*}({\mathbf{z}})
, as noted by
Lin et al. (2020)
in the min-max settings.
We can also observe that the empirical form of this weak gap function,
\widehat{\mathrm{WeakGap}}_{X}({\mathbf{z}})=\left<\widehat{F}({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-w^{*}({\mathbf{z}})\right>
,
is linear in the dataset
X
since the term
w^{*}({\mathbf{z}})
is fixed.
This allows us to reduce the problem of upper bounding the potential gap to the familiar problem of bounding the generalization error of an expected loss function.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
(F,\mathcal{Z})
be a smooth strongly monotone variational inequality (Assumptions
2.1
,
2.3
,
2.5
) corresponding to a
k
-player game.
The expected generalization gap for a
\gamma
-stable algorithm
A\colon\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Z}
is

\displaystyle\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\mathrm{WeakGap}_{F}(A(X))-\mathrm{WeakGap}_{\widehat{F}}(A(X))\right]\in O\bigg(\gamma(2DL+K\sum_{i\in[k]}\tfrac{L_{i}}{\mu_{i}})\bigg).

Proof.

The usual symmetrization argument implies that expected generalization gap is bounded by

\begin{split}\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\mathrm{WeakGap}_{F}(A(X))\right]=&\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Exp_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}\\
X^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left[\left<\widehat{F}_{X^{\prime}_{j}}(A(X)),A(X)-w^{*}(A(X))\right>\right]\\
=&\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Exp_{\begin{subarray}{c}X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}\\
X^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}\end{subarray}}\left[\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{j}}(A(X^{(j)})),A(X^{(j)})-w^{*}(A(X^{(j)}))\right>\right]\\
\leq&\;\Delta+\Exp_{X\sim\mathcal{D}^{n}}\left[\mathrm{WeakGap}_{\widehat{F}_{X}}(A(X))\right],\end{split}

where
\Delta
is the quantity

\displaystyle\Delta=

\displaystyle\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{j}}(A(X^{(j)})),A(X^{(j)})-w^{*}(A(X^{(j)}))\right>-\mathrm{WeakGap}_{\widehat{F}_{X}}(A(X)),

that we can decompose into the terms

\displaystyle(1)=

\displaystyle\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{j}}(A(X^{(j)})),A(X^{(j)})\right>-\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{j}}(A(X)),A(X)\right>,

and

\displaystyle(2)=

\displaystyle\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{j}}(A(X)),w^{*}(A(X))\right>-\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{j}}(A(X^{(j)})),w^{*}(A(X^{(j)}))\right>.

We can control first term using Hölder’s inequality and the smoothness of
\widehat{F}_{X_{j}}
to obtain

\displaystyle(1)

\displaystyle=\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}(A(X^{(i)}))-\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}(A(X)),A(X^{(i)}))\right>+\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{i}}(A(X)),A(X^{(i)})-A(X)\right>

\displaystyle\leq DL\gamma+K\gamma.

(5)

We can similarly bound the second term by

\displaystyle(2)

\displaystyle=\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{j}}(A(X))-\widehat{F}_{X_{j}}(A(X^{(j)})),w^{*}(A(X^{(j)}))\right>+\left<\widehat{F}_{X_{j}}(A(X)),w^{*}(A(X))-w^{*}(A(X^{(j)}))\right>

\displaystyle\leq DL\gamma+K\left\lVert w^{*}(A(X))-w^{*}(A(X^{(j)}))\right\rVert.

(6)

We now bound
\left\lVert w^{*}(A(X))-w^{*}(A(X^{(j)}))\right\rVert

in terms of
\left\lVert A(X)-A(X^{(j)})\right\rVert
using the technique of
Lin et al. (2020)
.
This is the step that was not possible with the original gap function.
We proceed by bounding each player’s contribution to this norm separately.
Due to the optimality of
w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i}
, the restriction of
w^{*}({\bm{z}})
to the
i
th player’s strategy space
\mathcal{Z}_{i}
, the first-order optimality condition of the strongly convex function
f_{i}
implies that, for all
{\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}_{i}
,

\left<F(w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i},{\bm{z}}_{-i})_{i},w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i}-{\bm{x}}\right>\leq 0\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\left<F(w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}_{-i})_{i},w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i}-{\bm{x}}^{\prime}\right>\leq 0.

Choosing
{\bm{x}}=w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i}
and
{\bm{x}}^{\prime}=w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i}
and summing the two inequalities gives

\left<F(w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i},{\bm{z}}_{-i})_{i}-F(w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}_{-i})_{i},w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i}-w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i}\right>\leq 0.

In concert with the strong monotonicity of
F_{i}
, which implies

\left<F(w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i},{\bm{z}}_{-i})_{i}-F(w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i},{\bm{z}}_{-i})_{i},w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i}-w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i}\right>-\mu_{i}\left\lVert w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i}-w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i}\right\rVert^{2}\geq 0,

we have

\begin{split}\mu_{i}\left\lVert w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i}-w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i}\right\rVert^{2}&\leq\left<F(w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}_{-i})_{i}-F(w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i},{\bm{z}}_{-i}),w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i}-w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i}\right>\\
&\leq L_{i}\left\lVert{\bm{z}}^{\prime}_{-i}-{\bm{z}}_{-i}\right\rVert\left\lVert w^{*}({\bm{z}})_{i}-w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{\prime})_{i}\right\rVert.\end{split}

(7)

Choosing
{\bm{z}}=A(X)
and
{\bm{z}}^{\prime}=A(X^{(j)})
and subsituting the inequality into (
6
) gives

\displaystyle(2)\leq DL\gamma+K\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{L_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\left\lVert A(X)_{i}-A(X^{(j)})_{i}\right\rVert.

(8)

Summing (
8
) with (
5
) completes the proof.
∎

In this approach, we reduce the problem of bounding the potential gap to bounding the generalization error of an expected loss.
One implication is that we can directly apply—rather than reinventing—the many technical tools developed for the stability analysis of convex minimization.
For example, we can improve Theorem
4.1
to the following high-probability guarantee due to
Klochkov and Zhivotovskiy (2021)
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
(F,\mathcal{Z})
be a smooth strongly monotone variational inequality (Assumptions
2.1
,
2.3
,
2.5
) corresponding to a
k
-player game.
The expected generalization gap for a
\gamma
-stable algorithm
A\colon\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\mathcal{Z}
when training error is small; i.e.,
\mathrm{WeakGap}_{\widehat{F}}(A(X))\leq\varepsilon
, is, with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{WeakGap}_{F}(A(X))\leq 2\varepsilon+O\Big(\log(\tfrac{1}{\delta})\Big(\big(DL+K\sum_{i\in[k]}\tfrac{L_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\big)\gamma\log n+\tfrac{\big(DL+K\sum_{i\in[k]}\tfrac{L_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\big)^{2}}{n}\Big)\Big).

This provides a fast-rate high-probability potential gap bound of order
O(1/n)
for gradient descent in games.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
(F,\mathcal{Z})
be a smooth strongly monotone variational inequality (Assumptions
2.1
,
2.3
,
2.5
) corresponding to a
k
-player game.
Running the gradient descent algorithm
A\colon\mathcal{X}^{n}\to\Delta_{d}
with sufficiently small learning rate
\eta\to 0
and large time horizon
T\to\infty
results in an iterate
A(X)
that with probability at least
1-\delta
has a potential gap bounded by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}_{F}(A(X))\leq\widetilde{O}\Big(\tfrac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}\Big(\tfrac{K}{\mu}\big(DL+K\sum_{i\in[k]}\tfrac{L_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\big)+\big(DL+K\sum_{i\in[k]}\tfrac{L_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\big)^{2}\Big)\Big),

where
\widetilde{O}
hides logarithmic factors.

Theorem
4.2
follows directly from proving that a Bernstein noise condition
(
Koltchinskii, 2006
;
Klochkov and Zhivotovskiy, 2021
)
is satisfied by the weak gap function.

Definition 4.4

(Generalized Bernstein Noise Condition for Minimization)
.

We say that an algorithm
A\colon\mathcal{X}_{n}\times\mathcal{X}\to[0,M]
satisfies the generalized Bernstein assumption for some parameter if, for any
B>0
,
{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}
, there is
{\bm{z}}^{*}\in\mathcal{Z}^{*}
such that:

\displaystyle\Exp_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\big(f({\bm{z}},x^{\prime})-f({\bm{z}}^{*},x^{\prime})\right]^{2}\leq B\big(\Exp_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[f({\bm{z}},x^{\prime})\right]-\Exp_{x^{\prime}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[f({\bm{z}}^{*},x^{\prime})\right]\big)\,.

Indeed, this noise condition is satisfied in VIs when strong monotonicity holds.

Fact 4.5
.

The weak gap function
\left<F({\mathbf{z}}),{\mathbf{z}}-w^{*}({\mathbf{z}})\right>
of a strongly monotone VI
(F,\mathcal{Z})
satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
4.2
fulfills the Bernstein condition with

B=(LD+K(1+\sum_{i\in[k]}\tfrac{L_{i}}{\mu_{i}}))^{2}
.

Proof.

We want to upper bound the variance of the weak gap function:

\displaystyle\mathrm{GapVar}

\displaystyle\triangleq\mathbb{E}\big[\big(\left<\widehat{F}({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-w^{*}({\bm{z}})\right>-\left<\widehat{F}({\bm{z}}^{*},{\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}^{*}-w^{*}({\bm{z}})\right>\big)^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big(\left<\widehat{F}({\bm{z}})-\widehat{F}({\bm{z}}^{*},{\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-w^{*}({\bm{z}})\right>-\left<\widehat{F}({\bm{z}}^{*},{\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}^{*}-{\bm{z}}-w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{*})+w^{*}({\bm{z}})\right>\Big)^{2}\Big].

To control the first summand, we use Hölder’s inequality to bound

\displaystyle\left<\widehat{F}({\bm{z}})-\widehat{F}({\bm{z}}^{*},{\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-w^{*}({\bm{z}})\right>\leq\left\lVert\widehat{F}({\bm{z}})-\widehat{F}({\bm{z}}^{*},{\bm{z}}_{-i})\right\rVert\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-w^{*}({\bm{z}})\right\rVert\leq LD\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{*}\right\rVert.

To control the second summand, we can again use Hölder’s inequality to bound

\displaystyle\left<\widehat{F}({\bm{z}}^{*},{\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}^{*}-{\bm{z}}-w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{*})+w^{*}({\bm{z}})\right>\leq K(\left\lVert{\bm{z}}^{*}-{\bm{z}}\right\rVert+\left\lVert w^{*}({\bm{z}})-w^{*}({\bm{z}}^{*})\right\rVert)

\displaystyle\leq K(1+\sum_{i\in[k]}\tfrac{L_{i}}{\mu_{i}})\left\lVert{\bm{z}}^{*}-{\bm{z}}\right\rVert,

where the last step bounds the stability of the variational term surrogate
w^{*}
in the same fashion as (
7
).
Summing these bounds, we have as desired

\displaystyle\mathrm{GapVar}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[(LD+K(1+\sum_{i\in[k]}\tfrac{L_{i}}{\mu_{i}}))^{2}\left\lVert{\bm{z}}^{*}-{\bm{z}}\right\rVert^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[(LD+K(1+\sum_{i\in[k]}\tfrac{L_{i}}{\mu_{i}}))^{2}\left<F({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{*}\right>\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Big[(LD+K(1+\sum_{i\in[k]}\tfrac{L_{i}}{\mu_{i}}))^{2}\left<F({\bm{z}}),{\bm{z}}-w^{*}({\bm{z}})\right>\Big].

∎

The high-probability bound then follows directly from
Klochkov and Zhivotovskiy (2021)
which provides a high-probability generalization error bound of order
O((\gamma\log(n)+B/n)\log(1/\delta))
when the Bernstein condition holds with parameter
B
.
We defer the complete proof of Theorem
4.2
to the Appendix.

5
Discussion

This note aims to provide an accessible exposition for deriving fast-rate generalization bounds in strongly monotone variational inequalities.
These derivations, which are direct extensions of stability arguments in convex optimization settings, demonstrate that the generality of working with VIs comes essentially “for free” when it comes to stability-based analyses of first-order methods.
There remain, however, gaps in our understanding of learning in strongly monotone VIs.
First, there seems to be an inherent hardness to minimizing gap functions with fast rates.
Our results on bounding the gap function relied on either assuming a small domain (Theorem
3.1
), which can introduce dimension dependence, or assuming domain structure (Theorem
3.2
).
Second, the generalization rates we derive are likely not optimal in their dependence on condition numbers and especially their dependence on
D
, the diameter of the domain.
We hypothesize that the latter dependence on
D
may be inevitable when working with gap functions, but it is unclear if this should also be the case for potential gaps.

Appendix A
Additional Preliminaries

A.1
Additional definitions

Contractive operators are defined as follows.

Definition A.1

(Contractiveness and non-expansiveness)
.

We say a mapping
G\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
, is non-expansive or
c
-contractive on
\mathcal{Z}
if there exists
c\in(0,1]
or
c\in(0,1)
, respectively, such that:

\left\lVert G({\bm{z}})-G({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\right\rVert\leq c\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert\,,\quad\forall{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}\,.

Appendix B
Proofs

B.1
Growth lemma and proof of Lemma
2.8

The growth lemma formalizes the notion of running a contractive iterative algorithm (as per Def.
A.1
) on two different but similar datasets.

Lemma B.1

(Generalized growth lemma
Farnia and Ozdaglar (2021)
Lemma 2)
.

Consider two sequences of updates,
\widetilde{P}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{P}_{T}
and
\widetilde{P}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\widetilde{P}_{T}^{\prime}
, where each update is a mapping of form
\widetilde{P}_{i}\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
.
Let
P\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathcal{Z}
be some mapping, such that
P+\widetilde{P}_{1},\dots,P+\widetilde{P}_{T}
and
P+\widetilde{P}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,P+\widetilde{P}_{T}^{\prime}
are two sequences of
\xi
-contractive mappings that each take
\mathcal{Z}
to
\mathcal{Z}
.
Fix some
{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}
and define
{\bm{z}}_{1},\dots,{\bm{z}}_{T}\in\mathcal{X}
to be the trajectory unrolled by applying the updates
P+\widetilde{P}_{1},\dots,P+\widetilde{P}_{T}
in sequence, starting at
{\bm{z}}
.
Analogously define
{\bm{z}}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,{\bm{z}}_{T}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{X}
with respect to
P+\widetilde{P}_{1}^{\prime},\dots,P+\widetilde{P}_{T}^{\prime}
.
For timesteps
t
where
\widetilde{P}_{t}=\widetilde{P}_{t}^{\prime}
, we have
\left\lVert{\bm{z}}_{t+1}-{\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{\prime}\right\rVert\leq\xi\left\lVert{\bm{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime}\right\rVert
.
For all timesteps
t
, the following holds for every constant
r>0
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\bm{z}}_{t+1}-{\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{\prime}\right\rVert\leq\xi\left\lVert{\bm{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime}\right\rVert+\sup_{{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\lVert r{\bm{z}}-\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}})\right\rVert+\sup_{{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\lVert r{\bm{z}}-\widetilde{P}_{t}^{\prime}({\bm{z}})\right\rVert\,.

Proof of Lemma
B.1
.

The first claim holds by definition of contractiveness.
The second claim holds by fixing any
r>0
and observing that

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\bm{z}}_{t+1}-{\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{\prime}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert P({\bm{z}}_{t})-P({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})+\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}}_{t})-\widetilde{P}_{t}^{\prime}({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert P({\bm{z}}_{t})-P({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})+\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}}_{t})-\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})+\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})-\widetilde{P}_{t}^{\prime}({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert P({\bm{z}}_{t})+\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}}_{t})-P({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})-\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})\right\rVert+\left\lVert\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})-\widetilde{P}_{t}^{\prime}({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\xi\left\lVert{\bm{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})-r{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime}\right\rVert+\left\lVert r{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime}-\widetilde{P}_{t}^{\prime}({\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\xi\left\lVert{\bm{z}}_{t}-{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime}\right\rVert+\sup_{{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\lVert\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}})-r{\bm{z}}\right\rVert+\sup_{{\bm{z}}\in\mathcal{Z}}\left\lVert r{\bm{z}}-\widetilde{P}_{t}^{\prime}({\bm{z}})\right\rVert.

The first equality holds by definition of the iterate
{\bm{z}}_{t+1}=\left\lVert P+\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}}_{t})\right\rVert
and likewise for
{\bm{z}}_{t+1}^{\prime}
.
The first inequality is an application of the triangle inequality.
The second inequality uses the
\xi
-contractiveness of
P+\widetilde{P}_{t}
.
The third inequality relaxes
{\bm{z}}_{t}^{\prime}
to be a supremum over the whole domain
\mathcal{X}
.
∎

For completeness, we retrace below a standard proof of the contractiveness of gradient descent on strongly convex functions but in the more general setting of strongly monotone VIs—observing that the proof goes through without modification.

Lemma B.2

(
GD
contractiveness)
.

Let
F\colon\mathcal{Z}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a
\mu
-strongly monotone (Assumption
2.3
) and
L
-Lipschitz (Assumption
2.1
) operator.
For any learning rate
\gamma>0
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert({\bm{z}}-\gamma F({\bm{z}}))-\big({\bm{z}}^{\prime}-\gamma F({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\big)\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq(1-2\gamma\mu+\gamma^{2}L^{2})\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}_{2},\qquad\forall{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}\,.

Moreover, if the learning rate satisfies
0<\gamma<\frac{2\mu}{L^{2}}
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\big({\bm{z}}-\gamma F({\bm{z}})\big)-\big({\bm{z}}^{\prime}-\gamma F({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\big)\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq\left(1-\frac{\mu^{2}}{L^{2}}\right)\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}_{2},\qquad\forall{\bm{z}},{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{Z}\,.

Proof of Lemma
B.2
.

Developing the left-hand-side of our first claim:

\displaystyle\left\lVert({\bm{z}}-\alpha F({\bm{z}}))-\big({\bm{z}}^{\prime}-\alpha F({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\big)\right\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}-\alpha\big(F({\bm{z}})-F({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\big)\right\rVert^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}-2\alpha\underbrace{\langle F({\bm{z}})-F({\bm{z}}^{\prime}),{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\rangle}_{\geq\mu\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}+\alpha^{2}\underbrace{\left\lVert F({\bm{z}})-F({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}_{\leq L^{2}\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq(1-2\alpha\mu+\alpha^{2}L^{2})\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}.

The second equality just expands the square, while the first inequality uses the
\mu
-strong monotonicity of
F
to bound
\langle F({\bm{z}})-F({\bm{z}}^{\prime}),{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\rangle\geq\mu\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}
and the
L
-Lipschitzness of
F
to bound
\left\lVert F({\bm{z}})-F({\bm{z}}^{\prime})\right\rVert^{2}_{2}\leq L^{2}\left\lVert{\bm{z}}-{\bm{z}}^{\prime}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}
. For given
\mu,L
we can select
\alpha
such that
1-2\alpha\mu+\alpha^{2}L^{2}<1\,.

∎

\displaystyle P({\bm{z}})={\bm{z}}-\frac{\gamma}{n}\sum_{i\in[n]\setminus\left\{j\right\}}\Xi({\bm{z}},\xi_{i}),\qquad\widetilde{P}_{t}({\bm{z}})=\frac{\gamma}{n}\Xi({\bm{z}},\xi_{j}).

.
