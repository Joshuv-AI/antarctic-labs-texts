Retinopathy of Prematurity Is Associated With Reduced Thalamic Volume and Impaired Neurodevelopment. A Cohort Study
Authors: Jose Uberos, Marta Carrasco‐Solis, Carolina Laynez‐Rubio, Ana Nieto‐Ruiz, Aida Ruiz‐López, Francisco Contreras‐Chova, Elizabeth Fernández‐Marin, Ana Campos‐Martínez, Jose Luis García‐Serrano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRetinopathy of prematurity (ROP) is a neurovascular disorder that may affect not only the retina but also the brain structures involved in visual processing. The relationship between ROP severity and the volumetric development of these regions remains unclear.MethodsA total of 157 children aged 6–16 years, born preterm (24–36 weeks gestational age), were assessed. Perinatal data and comorbidities were collected. Brain MRI (3 T) data were analysed using FreeSurfer to obtain the volumes of the thalamus, fusiform gyrus, cuneus, lingual gyrus, lateral occipital gyrus, optic chiasm and calcarine sulcus. Cognitive performance was evaluated using the WISC‐V. Multivariate regression analyses were adjusted for significant perinatal variables.ResultsThe children with severe ROP had significantly smaller bilateral thalamic volumes (left Th: 6665 ± 672 mm3 vs. 7492 ± 820 mm3; p < 0.005) and a reduced right fusiform gyrus volume (p = 0.041) compared with those without ROP or only mildly affected. Severe ROP was also associated with lower full‐scale IQ scores and reduced verbal comprehension, visuospatial reasoning, and working memory indices (p < 0.008).ConclusionsSevere ROP is associated with reduced thalamic and fusiform gyrus volumes as well as with lower cognitive performance, suggesting that ROP reflects a broader neurovascular disruption affecting central visual pathways.

Introduction

Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) is a vascular disease of the retina that can cause vision loss or blindness, primarily in newborns before 32 weeks of gestational age (GA). The condition is caused by an interruption in the normal vascularization of the retina, followed by an abnormal proliferative phase [1]. Although ROP initially affects the retina, its consequences can extend to the central visual system, and it could be considered a marker of broader neurological vulnerability, given that it shares risk factors described for neonatal brain injuries (hypoxia‐ischaemia, oxidative stress and nutritional deficiency, among others) [2].

The brain structures involved in visual processing include: (a) the fusiform gyrus, a cerebral convolution located in the lower part of the temporal and occipital lobes, which is crucial for visual processing; (b) the cuneus, an area of the cerebral cortex located in the medial portion of the occipital lobe, which receives visual information from the superior quadrant of the retina on the same side; (c) the lateral occipital cortex, the outer surface of the occipital lobe and the brain region responsible for vision, which is located at the back of each cerebral hemisphere; this structure is the most superficial area of the occipital lobe, and is where initial visual information is processed; (d) the lingual gyrus, a fold or convolution of the brain located on the medial surface of the occipital lobe, situated inferior to the calcarine sulcus and continuous with the parahippocampal gyrus in the temporal lobe; the lingual gyrus is part of the primary visual cortex and plays an important role in the perception and integration of visual information; (e) the optic chiasm, the region of the brain where the optic nerve fibres partially cross; this structure, located at the base of the brain, is fundamental for binocular vision and the brain's interpretation of images; (f) the thalamus, which constitutes an intermediate station for sensory information from the spinal cord and retina that travels to the cerebral cortex; the thalamus also has motor functions and connects with the cerebellum; furthermore, it is involved in higher functions, such as attention, language, memory and executive function; (g) the calcarine sulcus, one of the fissures on the surface of the brain, is located on the medial surface of each occipital lobe, following a path from back to front and from bottom to top; the primary visual cortex is located on either side of this sulcus and deep within it [3].

Several studies have considered possible relationships between ROP and structural brain abnormalities, specifically analysing the cerebellum, brainstem and fusiform gyrus. However, to our knowledge none have focused on the association between ROP and the development of other fundamental structures such as the thalamus [4, 5]. The thalamus is a key subcortical structure in the transmission of sensory signals (including visual ones) to the cerebral cortex. During early development, the thalamus and visual cortex are highly interconnected, so in theory, any lesion or alteration in visual input (such as that caused by ROP) could modify thalamic maturation. In this regard, some studies have established a relationship between thalamic volume in premature newborns and neurodevelopment [6].

Although at present ROP is classified solely according to neovascular changes in the retina, the pathology affects both neural and vascular tissue, which is why some authors have termed it a ‘neurovascular disease of the central nervous system’ [7]. Retinal ganglion cells are known to form synapses in the lateral geniculate nuclei of the thalamus, so it is likely that changes occur simultaneously with the development of ROP in related areas of neural and vascular brain tissue [2].

In the understanding that diverse growth factors are involved in both retinal and brain development during neurovascular maturation [8, 9] the main objective of the present study is to explore the relationships between ROP and brain structures related to visual processing, and between ROP and long‐term neurodevelopment.

Method

Study Population

A prospective study was conducted of a group of children with the following characteristics: age 6–16 years, history of premature birth, postmenstrual age of 24–36 weeks, and admitted to the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) of the San Cecilio University Hospital in Granada (Spain) between January 2008 and December 2017. The initial cohort of very low birth weight (VLBW) premature infants consisted of 402 children, of whom 55 died before 1 month of age. Of the remaining 347 children, 74 were diagnosed with motor disorder/cerebral palsy, moderate to severe cognitive impairment, genopathy and/or multiple disabilities and were excluded. Thus, 275 children were eligible for inclusion and were invited to participate, via a psychometric analysis using the Weschsler Intelligence Scale for Children (WISC‐V), published by Pearson in 2015, and a functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) study of the brain. However, participation was declined by the parents or guardians in 118 cases. Hence, 157 children were included in the cognitive performance (WISC‐V) stage of the study. MRI was applied to 117 of these children, but analysis of the imaging test results revealed cerebellar malformation in one child and agenesis of the corpus callosum in another, and these children were excluded from the subsequent analysis (Figure 1).

Neonatal Comorbidity

The term chorioamnionitis describes acute inflammation of the chorion with or without involvement of the amnion, reflecting a maternal immunological response to infection, with a history of foul‐smelling amniotic fluid, fever, increased acute‐phase reactants and/or spontaneous onset of preterm labour [10] In the present study, the following classifications of early onset sepsis (EOS) were considered: ‘no sepsis’ for asymptomatic newborns with negative cultures; ‘confirmed EOS’ for newborns with clinical symptoms suggestive of EOS up to 7 days of age, unexplained by any other aetiology, and with positive cultures; ‘clinical EOS’ for newborns with symptoms but no growth in cultures.

Late onset sepsis (LOS) is defined as that which occurs beyond 72 h of postpartum life and meets the criteria for infectious symptoms (fever, irritability, apathy, apnoea, failure to thrive, refusal to feed, tachycardia or tachypnoea) and for the presence of acute phase reactants (elevated interleukin 6 or C‐reactive protein), neutrophilia with left shift and/or leukopaenia with positive blood culture [11] Newborns with clinical manifestations of sepsis and an increase in acute phase reactants but with a negative blood culture are classified as presenting clinical sepsis ‘without microbiological confirmation’. Early‐onset sepsis is established when a vertical mother–child transmission mechanism can be demonstrated [12].

The Clinical Risk Index for Babies (CRIB II) is derived from the following variables: sex, gestational age, birth weight and excess base in blood gases obtained in the first 12 h. In the present study, the total CRIB II score (range 0 to 27) was calculated [13] The presence and degree of bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) was recorded following the recommendations of NIHCD [14] and Jobe and Bancalari [15] BPD is defined as the need for supplemental oxygen > 21% at 28 days of life and/or the need for supplemental oxygen > 21% or positive airway pressure at 36 weeks of corrected gestational age. BPD is classified as mild, moderate or severe. Patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) is diagnosed by Doppler ultrasound and treatment is indicated when there are clinical repercussions or when the diameter is greater than 2 mm. The diagnosis and staging of intraventricular haemorrhage (IVH) are based on the Papile classification [16]. To obtain this diagnosis, all newborns are given a brain ultrasound scan on the third day of life and weekly thereafter. The diagnosis and staging of necrotising enterocolitis (NEC) are performed using Bell's criteria [17].

Assessment of ROP

Screening programmes for the early detection of ROP began in 1988 after the publication of a multicentre study of cryotherapy for ROP [18]. This study defined the presence of one or more of the following as an unfavourable structural outcome: retinal fold involving the macula, retinal detachment affecting zone I in the posterior pole, or retrolental tissue or mass. The aim of these programmes was to identify preterm infants who needed treatment for ROP, and on this basis to facilitate short and long‐term follow‐up [19, 20]. It has been suggested that the initial examination should be based on the child's postmenstrual age, since the onset of ROP correlates better with this parameter than with GA. Accordingly, the Ophthalmology Service and the Neonatal Unit at the San Cecilio University Hospital (Granada, Spain) jointly prepared a schedule to begin examining premature infants weighing ≤ 1500 g at birth or with GA ≤ 30 weeks. Infants weighing 1500–2000 g or with GA > 30 weeks were also screened if they presented an unstable clinical course (including those needing cardiorespiratory support) or who, according to paediatric criteria, were considered at high risk of developing ROP [21]. A weekly examination was recommended if any of the following were observed: the presence of immature vascularisation in zone I, the absence of vascularisation in the retina, the vessels only reaching central zone II or zone I, stage 1 or 2 ROP in zone I, stage 3 ROP in zone II, or the suspicion or presence of aggressive posterior ROP. Finally, examinations were performed every 2–3 weeks if stage 1 or 2 or signs of ROP regression were observed in zone III [22]. We define severe ROP as the existence of stage 3–5 and/or Plus disease in zone I or II.

MRI Data Acquisition and Neuroimage Processing

Prior to the neuroimaging session, the children were familiarised with the sounds of the scanner and with the MRI environment. Brain data and images were acquired with a 3 T MRI scanner equipped with a 32‐channel receiving phased array head coil (Magnetom Trio Siemens Medical System, ERLANGEN, Germany), at the Mind, Brain and Behaviour Research Centre (CIMCYC) of the University of Granada, Spain. A high resolution T1‐weighted 3D magnetisation‐prepared rapid gradient‐echo (MPRAGE) sequence was acquired for each participant, with the following parameters: repetition time (RT) 2.3 ms, echo time (ET) 3.1 ms, flip angle 9°, field of view (FOV) 256 × 256 mm, matrix size 320 × 320, number of slices 208. With these parameters, an isotropic resolution of 0.8 × 0.8 × 0.8 mm was obtained. Total acquisition time for the T1 sequence was 6 min 35 s. Head movements were minimised using a foam system fitted around the participant's head. Furthermore, a cartoon film was projected to reassure the child during the MRI scanning.

Magnetic resonance images were processed using FreeSurfer version 6.0 (Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging, Massachusetts General Hospital, Boston, MA, USA), an automated and widely used software package for cortical and subcortical segmentation and morphometric analysis. Image processing was performed using the automated processing ‘recon‐all’ pipeline in FreeSurfer software (version 6.0, http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/) on the Alhambra Cluster of the University of Granada (Spain). Preprocessing steps involved intensity normalisation, registration to the Talairach space, skull stripping, segmentation of white matter (WM), tessellation of the WM boundary and automatic correction of topological defects. The validity and feasibility of FreeSurfer‐based analyses in paediatric populations, including children within the age range evaluated in the present study, have been previously demonstrated in developmental neuroimaging studies [23] and more recent paediatric quality‐control studies [24]. In addition, all MRI scans underwent systematic visual inspection before and after processing to identify motion‐induced artefacts, segmentation inaccuracies, or reconstruction failures. Images with severe artefacts or inadequate segmentation quality were excluded from the final analyses. Cortical reconstructions and tissue boundaries generated by FreeSurfer were also visually reviewed as part of the quality‐control procedure.

The cerebral cortex was then parcelled into regions of interest (ROIs) based on gyral and sulcal structures from the Destrieux atlas [25, 26, 27]. FreeSurfer outputs were also visually inspected to check for correct segmentation and parcellation. In addition, a segmentation was performed of the following structures: ox: Optic chiasm; Th: Thalamus; FG: fusiform gyrus; Cun: Cuneus; LOG: Lateral occipital gyrus; LgG: Gyrus lingualis; cs: Calcarine sulcus.

Cognitive Performance

The participants were seen at the neuropsychology clinic in the Paediatric Neurology Unit of our hospital. During the first evaluation session, cognitive performance was measured using the Weschsler Intelligence Scale for children (WISC‐V), edited by Pearson 2015. This test obtains an IQ assessment and generates a performance score on five major indices corresponding to the domains of verbal comprehension, fluid intelligence, visual–spatial perception, working memory and processing speed [28]. The neuropsychological study generally preceded the MRI study, the interval between the two being less than 6 months in all cases.

Statistical Analysis

Application of the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test confirmed that the study data presented a normal distribution. The descriptive data obtained were summarised by the mean and standard deviation (SD) for the continuous values, and by frequency distribution (%) for the categorical ones. Univariate comparisons were made using ANOVA and post hoc comparisons using the Bonferroni test. The 2‐by‐2 comparisons were performed using the t‐test in blocks of six variables. The Bonferroni adjustment was made, assuming that a p‐value < 0.008 was required to consider differences significant. The chi‐square test was used for the categorical values. The association analysis was performed first by univariate and then by multivariate logistic regression. Collinearity in the linear regression analyses was assessed by the variance inflation factor (VIF), adjusting for variables that did not present multicollinearity with the remaining predictor variables when the VIF was less than 5 [29]. All statistical analyses were performed with IBM SPSS 28.0 for Windows (IBM, Armonk, NY).

We then considered possible differences between the children who developed ROP and those who did not (Table 2). The former had a lower postmenstrual age (27.4 ± 1.4 vs. 30.4 ± 2.2). Moreover, their birth weight was significantly lower (985 ± 254 g) and they required more days of oxygen therapy (54.5 ± 47.4 vs. 21.7 ± 23.3). In addition, these infants more frequently presented with the following associated comorbidities: BPD (52.3% vs. 27.6%), EOS (19.0% vs. 4.2%), LOS (33.3% vs. 11.7%) and NEC > stage 2 (23.8% vs. 5.3%). At the time of inclusion in the study and of receiving the fMRI, the chronological age of these children was greater than that of those with no history of ROP (12.8 ± 2.6 years vs. 10.2 ± 2.6 years); consequently, the weight and height values of this group of children were also significantly greater. We believe these observed differences are important and should be taken into account for adjustments in the analysis phase. We observed no socioeconomic differences between the two groups studied.

Table 4 shows the mean (unadjusted) values corresponding to the surface area, thickness and volume of brain regions related to visual processing. Although the mean chronological age of patients with a history of ROP was 2.6 years greater, the volumes of left Th, Cun, ox and LgG, and of right Th, FG and LOG, were all significantly smaller. After applying the Bonferroni correction (p < 0.008), only the left Cun thickness and the right FG thickness and volume were considered significantly smaller in the children with a history of ROP. Table 5 shows the mean (unadjusted) volumes of the studied regions. After applying the Bonferroni correction, we conclude that the left Th is significantly smaller in children with severe ROP (6665 ± 672 mm3 vs. 7492 ± 820 mm3; p < 0.005). Additional exploratory analyses of global brain morphometric measures derived from FreeSurfer did not demonstrate widespread differences in brain volumes between children with and without ROP. Likewise, no significant global volumetric reductions were observed in the severe ROP subgroup. The only significant difference identified was a lower volume of the right pars orbitalis in children with severe ROP compared with children without ROP (2815 ± 313 mm3 vs. 3345 ± 535 mm3, p = 0.004).

We have also evaluated the relationship between structural neuroimaging measures and cognitive performance. We observed a significant association between thalamic volume and full‐scale IQ scores (r = 0.251; p = 0.007), with smaller thalamic volumes being associated with lower cognitive performance. Furthermore, reduced thalamic volume was also associated with poorer performance in specific WISC‐V domains. These findings suggest a relationship between thalamic structural development and subsequent neurocognitive outcomes in this cohort.

In the multivariate regression analysis, the adjustment variables considered were those that presented statistically significant differences between the study groups (Table 2). Discussion

In our study, the VLBW infants with severe ROP exhibited alterations in brain structures related to visual processing—specifically, thalamic volumes were reduced in both hemispheres and in the right fusiform gyrus. Previous studies have consistently reported a decrease in brain volume in brain regions related to visual processing in individuals with a history of ROP, especially in severe cases or those requiring treatment. Moreover, there is evidence of a significant reduction in occipital volume [30] lateral geniculate body [31] in the myelination of the optic radiations and in visual white matter [32] in children with a history of ROP.

The external configuration of the thalamus is relatively simple, with the exception of two protuberances on its posteroventral aspect: these are the medial and lateral geniculate bodies, which are responsible for processing auditory and visual impulses, respectively. Uggetti et al. [33] performed MRI and examined thalamic regions (specifically, the lateral geniculate bodies) in children who were blind but had no history of ROP. In this regard, Ingvaldsen et al. [34] proposed that ROP could be just the tip of an iceberg that we call ‘visual impairment of prematurity’ (VOP). The VOP paradigm comprises abnormal retinal vascularisation, alterations in retinal cellular architecture, choroidal degeneration and abnormalities in the visual pathway, including cortical areas and, according to our data, the thalamic region. In their cohort study, Ball et al. [5] observed that premature birth was associated with a reduction in volume in the thalamus, hippocampus, orbitofrontal lobe, posterior cingulate cortex and semioval centre when assessed at a term‐equivalent age, but these authors did not quantify the contribution of ROP to this process. In related work, Drost et al. [4] reported that severe ROP was associated with smaller cerebellar and brainstem volumes, but did not examine whether thalamic volume was affected by this condition. Finally in this respect, Roy et al. examined [30] a cohort of 395 premature newborns with less than 32 weeks of gestational age and observed that ROP of any degree was associated with high scores on a ‘global brain abnormality score’ that included the basal ganglia; however, they did not directly quantify thalamic volume in children with ROP.

In the present study, we also observed significantly smaller volumes of the right fusiform gyrus in premature infants with a history of severe ROP. The fusiform gyrus is located on the inferior surface of the temporal lobe, between the parahippocampal gyrus and the inferior temporal gyrus. It is involved in visual recognition of faces and objects, visuospatial integration processes and visual memory, and provides the ventral pathway for visual processing. This structure also plays a fundamental role in language processing and memory. We believe that reduced retinal stimulation (due to severe ROP) during critical periods could alter the development of the ventrotemporal gyrus (including the fusiform gyrus) through disuse or compensatory reorganisation.

The specific relationship between decreased FG volume and ROP is not widely documented as a direct and consistent association in the literature; however, there is indirect evidence and several studies have been conducted of the association between prematurity, ROP and FG alterations. Kline et al. [35] analysed a cohort of 110 VLBW and 51 full‐term newborns and observed a relationship between ROP and general cortical alterations. However, these authors did not specifically analyse the relationship between FG volume and ROP grade. Other studies have shown that ROP, especially severe ROP, is associated with a higher likelihood of structural brain abnormalities assessed by MRI, and with regional alterations such as changes in the cerebellum and deep nuclei; these associations generally persist after adjusting for perinatal confounding factors [30, 36].

The Extremely Low Gestational Age Newborn Study (ELGAN study) [37] concluded that children with ROP were more likely than their peers to have below‐average cognitive and psychomotor development at 2 years of age, with a higher risk of developing attention deficit hyperactivity disorder. Similarly, Schmidt et al. [38] observed that children with a history of ROP had more neurodevelopmental disorders at 5 years of age, which persisted between the ages of 10 and 13. These results are consistent with our observations regarding lower verbal comprehension, fluid reasoning and IQ scores in children with a history of ROP. The study results led us to reject the possible influence of hypoxic‐ischaemic or inflammatory lesions on thalamic development and future neurodevelopment. However, systemic factors such as the duration of oxygen therapy, which was greater in the group of children with ROP, could be related to decreased Th volume and impaired neurodevelopmental outcomes. In view of these considerations, we believe that sensory deprivation secondary to ROP may contribute to the smaller thalamic volume in these children and to the lower scores obtained for the WISC test subdomains. In this respect, a study by Trimarco et al. [6] on 116 premature newborns reported that the growth trajectory of thalamus volume in the early postnatal period was related to cognitive, motor and language outcomes at age 2.

To further explore whether the neuroimaging findings associated with ROP reflected selective visual pathway involvement or more diffuse neurodevelopmental impairment, we analysed additional global morphometric measures obtained from FreeSurfer. Interestingly, we did not identify generalised reductions in brain volumes in children with ROP or severe ROP. The only significant finding was a reduced volume of the right pars orbitalis in the severe ROP group. Although the functional significance of this isolated regional difference remains uncertain, the absence of widespread volumetric abnormalities may suggest that the structural alterations observed in our cohort are not solely explained by diffuse global brain injury. Instead, these findings could support the hypothesis of selective regional vulnerability associated with severe prematurity and ROP‐related neurodevelopmental processes. Nevertheless, given the exploratory nature of these analyses and the relatively limited sample size, these results should be interpreted cautiously and require confirmation in larger studies.

Interestingly, smaller thalamic volumes were associated with lower full‐scale IQ scores and poorer performance across specific WISC‐V domains. The thalamus plays a central role in cortical connectivity, sensory integration, and higher‐order cognitive processing, and altered thalamic development has previously been linked to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes in preterm populations. Our findings therefore support the hypothesis that the structural alterations observed in children with ROP may extend beyond isolated visual pathway abnormalities and could reflect broader disturbances in neurodevelopmental maturation. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, causality cannot be established; however, these results suggest a possible link between neonatal retinal pathology, impaired brain development, and later cognitive function. Further longitudinal studies integrating ophthalmologic, neuroimaging, and neuropsychological assessments are warranted to better clarify these relationships.

The main limitation of the present study concerns the significant number of newborns for whom permission to participate was refused, and the potential selection bias associated with this circumstance. However, the neonatal comorbidities and obstetric histories of this group of children did not differ from those recorded among the children we were able to recruit. Twenty‐five percent of those who were recruited did not undergo the MRI study, but this did not alter the percentage of children with ROP within our analyses, and therefore, in our opinion, the possibility of selection bias in the sample is not of great concern.

As a pointer for future research, our findings raise the question of whether the systematic analysis of thalamic volumes in children with ROP could be a neurovisual and cognitive biomarker in premature infants with ROP, allowing for the planning of early interventions, both visual (visual rehabilitation) and neurosensory stimulation. Furthermore, future studies that include tractography data and diffusion analysis can assess the integrity of the pathways and provide complementary information to the present work.

Conclusions

Severe ROP is associated with reduced volume in Th and FG gyrus, as well as poorer cognitive performance, suggesting neurostructural involvement of the central visual system. These findings support the consideration of ROP as a neurovascular disease of the central nervous system and highlight the potential value of Th volume as a neurovisual and cognitive biomarker in preterm infants.
