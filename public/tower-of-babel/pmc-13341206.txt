Comparison between Short-Segment versus Long-Segment Screw Fixation in Osteoporotic Vertebral Compression Fractures in Thoracolumbar Junction with Myelopathy
Authors: Chan Yang Noh, Jin Hoon Park, Jae Hwan Cho, Sung Bum Kim, Chongman Kim, Sun Woo Jang, Danbi Park, Sang Hoon Hwang, Min Han Kim, So Jin Song, Il Young Lee, The Korean Spinal Osteoporosis Society
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOsteopenic and osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF) is a prevalent clinical problem, particularly in the elderly. Although the majority of compression fractures do not result in myelopathy and are managed nonoperatively, surgical intervention, including screw fixation, may be necessary in select cases of OVCF associated with myelopathy. This retrospective study compared the clinical outcomes, radiographic results, and complication rates between short-segment and long-segment screw fixation in patients with OVCF at the thoracolumbar junction (TLJ) and myelopathic symptoms due to a spinal cord-compressing lesion.MethodsA total of 48 patients with OVCFs at the TLJ and myelopathic symptoms, who underwent either short-segment (two-level, n=17) or long-segment (>2 levels, n=31) screw fixation were studied. Postoperative outcomes including clinical parameters, radiographic parameters, and incidence of complications were evaluated.ResultsPostoperative improvement in Frankel scale was significantly greater in the short-segment versus long-segment fixation group (p=0.01). Long-segment fixation resulted in superior preservation of the postoperative correction angle (p<0.01). No difference in incidence of mechanical failure was demonstrated between the two groups (p=0.99). ConclusionShort-segment fixation demonstrated greater postoperative neurological improvement and less invasiveness, whereas long-segment fixation was associated with superior preservation of the correction angle. It is also noteworthy that short-segment screw fixation is not predisposed to instrument failure compared to long-segment screw fixation. These findings suggest that short-segment screw fixation may be a viable option for many patients with OVCFs, depending on clinical condition.Keywords: Osteoporosis; Fractures, compression; Spinal fusion; Spinal cord diseases

INTRODUCTION

Osteopenic and osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCFs) present significant clinical difficulties, particularly in the elderly, in which such fractures are prevalent [1,15]. Moreover, vertebral compression fractures represent the most prevalent complication of spinal osteoporosis, and surgical intervention for OVCFs may pose several challenges [13]. OVCFs can result in localized pain at the fracture site and loss of vertebral body height which may subsequently contribute to the development of kyphosis and myelopathy [6]. Several management strategies are available, with surgical intervention involving screw fixation of vertebral bodies rarely employed to address instability and alleviate associated myelopathy. Due to its high fusion rate, durable axial loading, and safe procedure, posterior lumbar interbody fusion and/or posterior screw fixation is widely employed in lumbar spinal lesions where decompressive laminectomy and bone fusion are required [14]. Although surgical interventions for OVCFs are employed in certain cases, there are no established guidelines as to surgical approaches, fusion levels, or the extent of kyphosis correction [12].

The debate centers on the comparison between short-segment and long-segment screw fixation techniques. Both approaches aim to restore vertebral alignment, provide stability, and alleviate clinical symptoms. Thoracolumbar fractures were conventionally treated with long constructs with four screws proximal and distal to the fracture level. Long-segment fixation imparts greater stability and support with less chance of implant failure than short-segment fixation does, but at the cost of sacrificing motion segments [3]. To overcome the limitations of long-segment fixation, many authors suggested a short-segment fixation technique, incorporating the pedicles of fractured vertebrae, well over a decade ago [9]. Yet, clarification of the superiority of one method over the other remains elusive, prompting the need for further comparative analysis [4].

By conducting a retrospective study in 48 patients with OVCFs with myelopathy who underwent either short-segment or long-segment screw fixation, we aimed to address the differences between the two methods in clinical outcomes, radiographic results, and complication rates.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study protocol was approved by Asan Medical Center Institutional Review Board (AMC IRB) and written informed consent was waived due to its retrospective nature (IRB 2020-1342).

Patient selection

All patients diagnosed with osteopenia or osteoporosis showing one or more compression fractures of vertebral bodies at the thoracolumbar junction (T10-L2) and who underwent screw fixation, including at the fracture level, at a single institution from January 2012 to December 2023, were screened. In accordance with the World Health Organization, osteopenia was defined as a mean T-score ranging between -1.0 and -2.5, based on dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry measurements of two or more vertebrae. In contrast, osteoporosis was defined by T-scores of less than -2.5. From the screened cohort, patients with osteopenia or osteoporosis showing vertebral compression fracture and myelopathic symptoms were included. A total of 48 consecutive cases were enrolled after applying the following exclusion criteria : <5 months of postoperative follow-up; incomplete medical or radiographic records; or loss to follow-up. Demographics

The short-segment fixation group was defined as patients with fixation of two vertebral levels and in whom pedicle screws were inserted one level above and below, and at the index level of the OVCF. The long-segment fixation group was defined as patients with any fixation procedure involving more vertebral levels than the short-segment fixation group. The two groups were compared based on demographic characteristics (sex, age), Bone mineral densitometry (BMD) T-scores, the number of fused levels, vertebral fracture etiology and index level, follow-up duration, and preoperative osteoporotic medication use.

Surgical indication and technique

All patients in this study had an OVCF at the thoracolumbar junction and myelopathic symptoms that correlated with the affected spinal level. All surgeries were conducted by one of four board-certified spinal surgeons. In this study, surgeons’ choice between short- and long-segment fixation was guided by two distinct biomechanical theories. One theory employed long-segment fixation for enhanced biomechanical stability of the spine. The other theory was based on minimizing bending moment of the operated segment through short-segment fixation, while employing bicortical screw insertion for biomechanical stability.

Short-segment fixation

For short-segment fixation, patients were positioned prone on a Jackson spinal table under general anesthesia. Subsequently, a midline skin incision was performed, extending one vertebral level superiorly and inferiorly to the targeted vertebra. Using a navigation system, pedicle screws 5.5–7.5 mm in diameter, based on pedicle size, were inserted into the pedicles one level above and below the targeted vertebra using the Solera® system (Medtronic Sofamor Danek USA, Inc.; Memphis, TN, USA). At the targeted vertebra, unilateral pedicle screws were inserted. The decision regarding the extent of decompression was based on magnetic resonance imaging while the size of screws was decided based on computed tomography (CT) findings. Unilateral laminectomy and pediculectomy were performed to decompress the spinal cord from fractured vertebral body. The unilateral pediculectomy provided adequate access to the ventral aspect of the spinal canal and vertebral body, allowing removal of compressive lesion using an osteotome and reverse-angled curette. For interbody lumbar fusion, titanium cage and 15 mL of allograft bone chips were inserted into intervertebral space. Posterolateral fusion was carried out on the remaining unilateral laminae using 15 mL of allograft bone chips. Intraoperative plain radiographs and postoperative CT scans confirmed accurate and bicortical insertion of screws for biomechanical stability.

Long-segment fixation

For long-segment fixation, patients were positioned prone on a Jackson spinal table and administered general anesthesia. A midline skin incision was made extending targeted levels adjacent to the index level of compressive lesion due to OVCF. Subperiosteal dissection was carried out to expose the spinous processes and laminae. Pedicle screws were inserted at the vertebrae including the index level and augmented screws with bone cement were used depending on the surgeon’s decision. Posterior decompressive laminectomy of compressive lesion using high speed drill followed by interbody fusion using titanium cage and 15 mL of allograft bone chips was performed. Subsequently bilateral posterolateral fusion was performed on the remaining facet joints and/or transverse processes. Intraoperative plain radiographs were subsequently obtained to confirm accurate screw placement.

Radiographic assessments

All radiographic parameters were measured on X-ray films to assess the degree of vertebral alignment restoration and maintenance. Local kyphosis angle (LKA) was defined as the angle formed by the superior endplate of the upper vertebra and the inferior endplate of the lower vertebra. A representative measurement of LKA is demonstrated in Fig. 2. Correction angle was defined as the difference between preoperative LKA and immediate postoperative LKA. Loss of correction angle was defined as the difference between immediate postoperative LKA and LKA at the final postoperative follow-up.

An illustration of local kyphotic angle (LKA) measurement. A representative case of compression fracture of L1. The angle formed between the two hypothetical perpendicular lines of the superior endplate of the upper vertebra and the inferior endplate of the lower vertebra is measured and defined as LKA.

Screw angle change (SAC) was defined as an alteration >10° in the angle between the pedicle screw and the inferior endplate when comparing immediate postoperative lateral lumbar X-rays with those obtained at the 6-month postoperative follow-up. Cases of screw pull-out were identified by noticeable changes in the position of the screw or screw head between immediate postoperative lateral lumbar X-rays and the final postoperative follow-up images.

Clinical assessments

Clinical outcomes were evaluated using various modalities, including Visual analog scale (VAS) scores for pain assessment, the Frankel scale for evaluation of neurologic function, and modified Japanese Orthopedic Association (mJOA) scores for functional assessment. Each alphabet in Frankel scale was assigned in numbers in numerical order (A=1, B=2, … E=5) and statistically analyzed. Each clinical parameter was evaluated preoperatively, immediately postoperatively and at 6-month follow-up. The differences in each parameter from preoperative state to final operative state were designated as ΔVAS, ΔFrankel, and ΔmJOA.

Complications

Complications related to postoperative wound infection, surgical wound dehiscence, and revision due to neurological deterioration was recorded. Cases with documented medical record of surgical wound infection or prolonged use of postoperative antibiotics over 3 days were included in wound infection. Cases in which patients had to undergo revision surgery due to surgical wound dehiscence were included in revision due to wound dehiscence. Cases requiring revision surgery due to postoperative deterioration of myelopathy were also evaluated. Postoperative complications associated with anesthesia were assessed. These included hypotensive shock, deep vein thrombosis, and postoperative transfusion requirements. Hypotensive shock was specifically defined as requiring intravenous inotropic agents postoperatively, while deep vein thrombosis was defined as a confirmed thrombosis on lower extremity CT.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 20; SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The statistical significance was tested using a chi-squared test or Fisher exact test for categorical variables, and an unpaired Student’s t-test or Welch’s t-test with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for continuous variables. All values were reported as a mean±standard deviation. p-value of <0.05 denoted statistical significance.

Data availability

The data of this study are available from the corresponding author, upon reasonable request.

Complications

Incidence of complications regarding operation and general anesthesia is summarized in Table 3. No complication related to wound infection or revision operation due to dehiscence was found in either group. One patient in the short-segment group underwent revision surgery due to postoperative neurologic deterioration, whereas no cases of revision surgery were observed in the long-segment group (p=0.17). Complications, including hypotensive shock (p=0.21) and deep vein thrombosis (p=0.23), did not differ significantly between the groups. Intraoperative transfusion was significantly more frequent in the long-segment group (14 cases) compared to the short-segment group (two cases; p=0.02). However, postoperative transfusion rates showed no statistically significant difference between the two groups.

DISCUSSION

This retrospective study was designed to weigh the pros and cons of short-segment versus long-segment fusion by comparing 48 consecutive operative cases with OVCFs at the thoracolumbar junction and myelopathic symptoms. The findings of this study indicate that both short-segment and long-segment fixation demonstrate improvements in postoperative VAS scores, with comparable outcomes between the two groups. However, differences emerge upon closer examination, particularly regarding improvements in neurologic function and the radiographic preservation of correction angle. Notably, short-segment fixation exhibits significant advantages in certain aspects, such as postoperative improvements in Frankel scale, while long-segment fixation demonstrates superior preservation of postoperative correction angle. Additionally, operation time, postoperative hospital stay, estimated blood loss, and intraoperative transfusion requirements also showed statistically significant superiority in the short-segment group. These results may endorse short-segment fixation in OVCF at the thoracolumbar junction as a cost-efficient, risk-minimizing, and less invasive technique.

In patients with OVCF at the thoracolumbar junction and myelopathic symptoms, surgical treatment can restore vertebral body height, correct angular deformity, decompress neural tissue, and facilitate rapid mobilization and rehabilitation [8]. However, contention remains about the extent of fixation in such patients. Short-segment fusion offers benefits such as reduced invasiveness, making such fusion more suitable for elderly patients, and preserving a greater number of motion segments. Conversely, short-segment fusion raises concerns about the potential for increased mechanical load on the constructs, which may result in early implant failure and the loss of sagittal alignment [10]. Peters et al. [11] showed, in thoracolumbar vertebral fracture, that a two-level above and below fusion technique provided more stability than a one-level above and below fusion technique. Altay et al. [2] demonstrated that short-segment and long-segment fixation yielded comparable clinical outcomes; however, sagittal radiographic parameters were superior with long-segment fixation. Tezeren and Kuru [16] reported a high failure rate with short-segment fixation, whereas long-segment fixation was associated with an extended operative time and a significant increase in blood loss. Conversely, Jeong et al. [5] concluded that short-segment fixation including the fractured vertebra in thoracolumbar and lumbar burst fractures demonstrated comparable clinical and radiologic results to those of long-segment fixation.

This study contributes to the ongoing debate by presenting comparable postoperative clinical outcomes such as VAS, mJOA scores, mechanical failure rates, and initial correction angle between the two groups. However, results of this study suggests that short-segment fixation provides superior postoperative neurologic improvements compared to long-segment fixation. This characteristic of short-segment group can potentially be attributed to the surgical technique of removal of ventrally compressive bone fragments due to retropulsion of osteoporotic vertebral body [7]. This result warrants further discussion, particularly considering the inherent differences in the surgical approach to neural decompression between these two strategies. In long-segment fixation, which involves three or more instrumented levels in this study, the extended construct accommodates longer decompressive laminectomy spanning multiple vertebral levels. However, in short-segment fixation, where only two levels are manipulated, the longitudinal extent of posterior laminectomy is limited by its surgical level. Consequently, to achieve adequate neural decompression in cases of myelopathy caused by ventral spinal cord compression, the short-segment fixation frequently necessitates ventral decompression. This can be interpreted as an integral adaptation inherent to the short-segment instrumentation in myelopathic symptoms.

This study has several limitations due to its retrospective design and stringent exclusion criteria, resulting in a relatively small sample size. Since patient data were pooled from four surgeons within a single institution, there was potential for heterogeneous surgical techniques such as use of augmented screws. Furthermore, varied follow-up durations might have influenced the documented clinical and radiological outcomes. Lastly, inconsistent policies on obtaining radiographic images between surgeons prevented a comprehensive evaluation of changes in sagittal and coronal balance, which could have strengthened the comparison between the two groups.

CONCLUSION

This comparative study seeks to contribute to the ongoing debate surrounding the optimal management of OVCFs. While both short-segment and long-segment screw fixation techniques offer viable options, our findings suggest that short-segment fixation may be particularly advantageous in certain clinical scenarios, offering comparable clinical outcomes while preserving the motion segment.
