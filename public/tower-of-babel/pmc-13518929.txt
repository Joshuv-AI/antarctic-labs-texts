Protective roles of green plant extracts as functional foods and N-acetylcysteine against CCL4-induced hepatic toxicity on AML-12 cell line
Authors: Safa H. Qahl, Fatimah Amer, Hajer A. Alfarteesh, Ahlam A. Harasani, Nouf A. Babteen, Rawan Altalhi, Rasha A. Al-Eisa, Mudi H. Alharbi, Maryam M. Alghamdi, Nora H. J. Alharbi, Alaa H. ABSTRACT
The current research work examined the protective effects of some green plant extracts compared with N-acetylcysteine against liver toxicity caused by CCL4 in hepatic Alpha Mouse Liver 12 (AML-12) cells. Using standard biochemical assays, oxidative stress indicators, inflammatory cytokines, and redox status were measured. Plant extracts boosted the survival rate to reach 86.05% (moringa), 83.64% (arugula), 79.45% (broccoli), 78.19% (mint), and 77.21% (celery). Moringa demonstrated the strongest hepatoprotection, with enzyme levels such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) decreased to 46.66, 56.9, 77.61, and 156.51 U/L. N-acetylcysteine reduced malondialdehyde (MDA) to 3.44 nmol/mg and among plant extracts, moringa showed the greatest reduction at 3.65 nmol/mg. At 35.49, 31.25, and 24.04 U/mg, moringa demonstrated the highest plant-derived recovery for catalase (CAT), superoxide Dismutase (SOD), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx). The strongest plant-derived protection was offered by moringa, which had a reduced glutathione (GSH) to oxidized glutathione (GSSG) ratio of 4.56. The highest reductions in inflammatory markers have been observed in arugula and moringa for tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) 38.05, 42.2 pg/mL, while Interleukin-6 (IL-6) reached 33.25, 28.54 pg/mL, respectively. Via enhancing the activity of antioxidant enzymes (CAT, SOD, and GPx), maintaining GSH/GSSG balance, lowering MDA levels, and reducing cytokines related to inflammation (TNF-α and IL-6), broccoli, mint, and celery provided moderate and significant defense against CCL4-induced liver damage. Moringa and arugula, among the green extracts, were suggested as the most effective hepatoprotective agents.

Introduction

The liver is a dynamic organ that plays an important role in macromolecule metabolism, generating energy, bile acid production, producing protein, and innate immunity response1. Numerous conditions can induce liver damage, such as repeated drug exposure, medical treatment, and hazardous compounds in the environment. It can also be caused by certain conditions such as non-insulin dependent diabetic symptoms, chronic kidney disease, and heart failure. Many cases of untreated liver injury develop into fibrosis, cirrhosis, and cancer2. Liver injury is associated with cellular necrosis, increased tissue lipid peroxidation, and glutathione depletion. Furthermore, various biochemical indicators and such diseases provide a significant challenge to international public health3. Excessive exposure to hepatotoxins and medications can cause acute liver injury, potentially leading to serious complications. Carbon tetrachloride (CCL4), known as tetra chloromethane, is a colorless chlorinated hydrocarbon that has numerous industrial applications in the degreasing process and is found in refrigerant and fire extinguisher structures4. However, prolonged daily exposure to CCL4 causes cell death and necrosis5.

Furthermore, yet there is no specific treatment in medical science that protects the liver from harm or helps to regenerate hepatic cells. More than half of all contemporary clinical drugs are natural compounds, and they are crucial to pharmaceutical drug research initiatives. The consumption of a range of indigenous herbs and vegetables is can contribute greatly to the advancement of human health in terms of illness prevention and treatment, since plants have long served as a useful and sensible supply of medicinal substances6. The use of plants for medicinal purposes for curing various kinds of liver diseases and dysfunctions has grown in popularity and gained wide acceptance. However, a wide range of medicinal plants have been shown to provide some hepatoprotection. Celery (Apium graveolens) extract has hepatoprotective properties, lowering oxidative stress and restoring antioxidant enzyme levels in CCL4-induced liver damage7. Arugula (Eruca sativa) leaves protect the liver from CCL4-induced injury by increasing glutathione levels and reducing lipid peroxidation8. Moringa (Moringa Oleifera) extract reduces CCL4-induced hepatotoxicity by lowering oxidative stress and enhancing liver enzyme profiles9. Broccoli (Brassica Oleracea var. italica) contains antioxidants that reduce CCL4-induced liver damage and promote detoxification pathways10. Via scavenging free radicals and stabilizing cell membranes, mint (Mentha Spicata) extract shields the liver against CCL4 toxicity11.

The acetylated amino acid L-cysteine is the source of N-acetylcysteine, a sulfhydryl molecule. Respiratory, neurological, renal, and cardiac diseases are among its many biological impacts12. N-acetylcysteine helps in protecting the liver from harm caused by CCL4 and reducing the oxidative stress, restoring intracellular antioxidant capacity by acting as a precursor to glutathione13 (Fig. 1).Fig. 13D molecular structure of N-acetylcysteine.

3D molecular structure of N-acetylcysteine.

The current research work is designed to find out the hepatoprotection of CCL4 exposure on AML-12 cell survival rate, biochemical enzymes, lipid peroxidation, liver function indicators, antioxidant enzyme activities, glutathione redox system, and pro-inflammatory cytokines. It investigated the probable protection against liver toxicity by some selected green plant extracts, such as celery, arugula, moringa, broccoli, and mint, compared to N-acetylcysteine.

Materials and methods

Sample extraction

Fresh plant materials (celery, moringa, and mint leaves), (arugula leaves), and (broccoli florets) were purchased from Al-Madinah Al-Munawwarah, Taif, and Al-Jouf, Saudi Arabia, respectively. The plants were identified by Department of Biotechnology, University of Taif. Plants were washed with distilled water and allowed to dry until they reached a consistent weight at the ambient temperature. The dry materials were ground finely using a mechanical grinder (MF-10, Jingxin Co., Ltd., China). Each plant powder was extracted using aqueous or hydroalcoholic solvents, depending on the plant. In brief, 10 g of each powder was weighed and combined with 100 mL of 70% ethanol except arugula which combined with 100 mL of distilled water and incubated at 25 °C with continual stirring for 24 h2. The extracts were filtered using Whatman No.1 filter paper and concentrated under decreased pressure in a rotary evaporator (YRE-2000E, Gongyi, China) at 40 °C.

Cell culture and toxicity induction

Alpha Mouse Liver 12 (AML-12) cells from the Shanghai Institute of Biological Sciences in China were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 (DMEM/F12) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% insulin–transferrin–selenium (ITS) liquid media supplement (Sigma, St. Louis, Michigan, USA), and 40 ng/mL dexamethasone (Sorlabo, Beijing, China). All extracts were diluted in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to the appropriate quantities before treatment, with a final DMSO content of 0.1% to reduce cytotoxicity. Cells were cultivated under normal conditions at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2. When the cells achieved 80–90% confluence, they were trypsinized and plated in a 96-well microplate, 1 × 106 cells per well. Carbon tetrachloride (CCL4, Sigma-Aldrich, 02,671) was dissolved in DMSO and then diluted into Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) with 10% FBS. AML-12 cells were treated with 0.2% CCL4 for 2 h to induce hepatocyte damage. Plant extracts were added to CCL4 to evaluate their protective effects14. N-Acetylcysteine was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (A9165-N-Acetyl-L-cysteine, Louis, Missouri, USA). N-acetylcysteine at 1 mM served as a reference protecting compound. Treatments lasted 24 h, following which cells were taken for viability measures and biochemical analysis. Controls included untreated cells (negative control) and CCL4-treated cells (positive control). Cytoprotection assay

The assay depended on live cells’ ability to convert the yellow tetrazolium salt MTT (0.5 mg/mL) for 2 h (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide) with 200 µg/mL of extracts, and it was transferred into purple formazan crystals using NAD(P)H-dependent oxidoreductase enzymes. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates with a total of 104 cells/well and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C in a humidified condition with 5% CO2. After incubation, the formazan was dissolved with 200 μL/well of DMSO, and the absorbance was measured at 570 nm with a microplate reader (RT-2100C, Shenzhen, China). The viability of treated cells was measured as a percentage of untreated control cells based on their absorbance15.

Cellular extraction for biochemical assays

Cells were washed two times with cold PBS and lysed in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris (hydroxymethyl) aminomethane hydrochloride (pH 7.4), 150 mM sodium chloride, 1% Triton X-100, and protease inhibitors. Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min before centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C16.

Biochemical enzyme assays

Sigma-Aldrich kits as Alanine aminotransferase (ALT, MAK052); Aspartate aminotransferase (AST, MAK055); Alkaline phosphatase (ALP, MAK447); Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH, MAK529), were used to test biochemical enzyme activities, following manufacturer’s protocol. In each test, 50 µL of sample or standard was applied to the reaction wells in duplicate. After removing the supernatant from the wells, enzyme activities were immediately detected. A microplate reader was used to quantify ALT at 570 nm, AST at 450 nm, ALP at 405 nm, and LDH at 490 nm. Enzyme activity was estimated and expressed in U/L.

Lipid peroxidation assay

Cell lysates (50 µL of each sample) were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. As directed by the manufacturer, lipid peroxidation was evaluated using Sigma-Aldrich kits. Malondialdehyde, a sign of lipid peroxidation, was identified by using MDA (MAK085). A thiobarbituric acid reactive material (TBARS) was used to investigate MDA. Following treatment, the medium was taken out of the wells, adherent cells were scraped off, floated in cold PBS, sonicated, and chilled. Sodium dodecyl sulfate, color reagent, and lysed cell or standard supernatant were present in every vial. The vial was centrifuged for detection after being heated to 100 °C for an hour and then cooled in an ice bath17. Using a microplate reader, the absorbance of MDA was measured at 532 nm.

Antioxidant enzyme activity assays

CAT (MAK080) assessed catalase activity, an enzyme that degrades hydrogen peroxide. AML-12 cell lysates were produced as indicated, and 50 µL of each sample was transferred to a 96-well microplate with 150 µL phosphate buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.8). The reaction was started by adding 50 µL of 100 mM hydrogen peroxide substrate, and catalase decomposed the hydrogen peroxide, producing a chromophore via an associated reaction18. SOD (MAK196) tested superoxide dismutase activity, which transforms superoxide radicals to oxygen and hydrogen peroxide. This test uses a tetrazolium salt to identify superoxide radicals generated by xanthine oxidase and hypoxanthine. After treatment, the medium was removed from the wells, the adhering cells were removed, and the cells were suspended in 20 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid) buffer (HEPES) (pH 7.2, containing 1 mM EGTA, 210 mM mannitol, and 70 mM sucrose), sonicated, and placed on ice. A diluted radical detector, lysed cell or standard supernatant, and xanthine oxidase were added to each well of a 96-well plate15. Using a microplate reader, absorbances of antioxidant enzyme activities were measured; CAT at 520 nm, and SOD at 450 nm.

Glutathione redox system assays

GPx (MAK437) measured glutathione peroxidase activity, which lowers hydrogen peroxide and organic peroxides with glutathione. AML-12 cell lysates were seeded ina 96-well microplateat 50 µL for each sample. The reaction mixture included glutathione, glutathione reductase, and Nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) in phosphate buffer (pH 7.4). The enzymatic reaction was initiated with H2O2 substrate, and the oxidation of NADPH to NADP⁺ was measured through a decrease in absorbance18. Using a microplate reader, the absorbance of GPx was measured at 340 nm.

GSH/GSSG (CS0260) is a kit for measuring reduced glutathione (GSH), oxidized glutathione (GSSG), and GSH/GSSG ratios in samples. AML-12 cell lysates were deproteinized with 5% sulfosalicylic acid (SSA) and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove precipitated proteins. The supernatant was collected and analyzed. 50 µL of the sample was combined with the assay buffer and the DTNB recycling reagent, which contained NADPH and glutathione reductase. The process produced a yellow-colored product, 5-thio-2-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB). Samples were pretreated with 2-vinylpyridine, which derivatized GSH and allowed for GSSG detection. GSH levels were estimated by subtracting GSSG values from total glutathione. Since GSH is one of the most significant endogenous antioxidant defense systems that eliminates lipid and hydrogen peroxides. Using a microplate reader, absorbances of GSH and GSSG were measured at 412 nm and at 450 nm, respectively. GSH:GSSG ratios is essential for cell survival, while the GSH/GSSG ratio was used to evaluate cellular redox state. The concentration of reduced GSH was divided by the concentration of GSSG to determine the GSH/GSSG ratio19.

Pro-inflammatory cytokines assays

Using ELISA kits (RAB0308, RAB0477, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) in the cell culture supernatants were measured in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. 96-well plates were seeded with 100 µL of each sample, and the plates were allowed to stand at room temperature for two hours. Following washing, biotin-conjugated detection antibodies, and streptavidin–horseradish peroxidase (HRP) solution were added to the wells. Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate was used to induce the colorimetric reaction, and 2 N sulfuric acid was used to prevent the reaction. The color developed as a result of enzymatic processes, which produced a blue substance that changed yellow when the stop solution was applied by using an ELISA microplate reader (BK-EL10A, Shandong, China), absorbance was measured at 450 nm and expressed as pg/mL12.

Statistical analyses

Each experiment was conducted in triplicate. Standard statistical methods were used to generate means and standard deviations. GraphPad Prism (version 6), SPSS Version 25, and Microsoft Excel 2020 were used for statistical analyses. Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons and one-way ANOVA were used to examine intergroup differences across various groups (P < 0.05).

Results and discussion

The influence of CCL4 toxicity and protective treatments on AML-12 cell survival

The current study investigated the preventive effects of N-acetylcysteine and some green plant extracts, including celery, arugula, moringa, broccoli, and mint, against liver toxicity caused by CCL4 in AML-12 cells. AML-12 cells were used in the CCl4-induced damage model because of their capacity to retain liver specialized activities. AML-12 cells express functioning CYP2E1, which is necessary for the bioactivation of CCl4 into reactive trichloromethyl radicals, in contrast to several other liver cell lines. By interacting with cellular macromolecules and starting lipid peroxidation a process that closely resembles the molecular processes of clinical hepatotoxicity these radicals cause oxidative damage. Normal cellular viability under baseline conditions was validated by the (-) control group, which represented the untreated cells and demonstrated 100% survival rate. Cell survival was considerably lowered to 41.12% after exposure to CCL4, demonstrating that the hepatotoxin induced high cytotoxicity, Fig. 3. Cell survival rate increased and reached 92.47% when N-acetylcysteine reduced the cytotoxicity. Moringa extract showed the highest (86.05%) cell survival rate, followed by arugula (83.64%), broccoli (79.45%), mint (78.19%), and celery (77.21%). Moringa has significant levels of chlorogenic acid and quercetin, which may have increased its ability to scavenge free radicals20. Cruciferous vegetables, including broccoli and arugula, are known to contain sulforaphane and other isothiocyanates, which induce phase II detoxifying enzymes and minimize oxidative stress10. Celery and mint have terpenoid and polyphenolic components, which alter cellular antioxidant defenses21,22. The finding reported the possibility of functional food-based protection against hepatic oxidative stress through supplementation with green plant extracts. The influence of CCL4 toxicity and protective treatments on AML-12 cell enzyme activities

ALT is the most sensitive liver enzyme to detect damage to liver cells, and LDH, ALP, and AST are accurate markers of hepatic disease5. The hepatic enzyme ALT is necessary to be released into the bloodstream by converting proteins into energy21. AST aids in the metabolism of amino acids. Additionally, the liver and bone contain the enzyme ALP, which helps break down proteins and block the bile duct10. Finding the location and the extent of tissue damage within the organism is the primary goal of the LDH test9.

The CCL4-treated positive control showed significant increases (ALT = 125.36 U/L, AST = 133.65 U/L, ALP = 144.36 U/L, and LDH = 314.24 U/L) in comparison to the negative control, which had normal enzyme activities (ALT = 24.55 U/L, AST = 29.18 U/L, ALP = 61.25 U/L, and LDH = 100 U/L). Cai et al.23 reported that N-acetylcysteine decreased ALT and AST levels, which might directly decrease hepatocyte damage.

Moringa extract detected similar values to the N-acetylcysteine-treated group; 46.66, 56.90, 77.61, and 156.51 U/L for ALT, AST, ALP, and LDH, respectively. Mint and celery extracts exhibited slight effects, while arugula and broccoli extracts provided substantial protection. Our result for celery extract was in agreement with the enzymatic activities against acetaminophen-induced acute liver injury24. These findings were consistent with a previous study that showed mint essential oil reversed the increases observed in the CCL4-treated group, hence reducing elevated AST and ALT levels, signs associated with the fibrosis progression11 (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Effect of green plant extracts and N-Acetylcysteine on enzyme activities in AML-12 cell line; different letters mean significant differences among groups.

The influence of CCL4 toxicity and protective treatments on AML-12 cell malondialdehyde

The unstable lipid hydroperoxides may be changed into metabolites, including MDA, 4-HNE, and conjugated dienes11. Mint extract had the lowest MDA reduction (5.32 nmol/mg), while moringa extract had the largest (3.65 nmol/mg), Fig. 5. According to El-Beltagi et al.9, moringa can stop the lipid oxidation process and rapidly detoxify the reactive hazardous metabolites of CCL4. The liver MDA content of the CCL4 group was substantially greater than that of the mint samples11. All samples significantly decreased MDA when compared to the CCL4-treated control. This demonstrates their ability to inhibit oxidative damage using naturally occurring antioxidants, including flavonoids and polyphenols. The influence of CCL4 toxicity and protective treatments on AML-12 cell antioxidant enzyme activities

An indirect way to identify cell damage caused by free radicals is to measure intracellular enzyme activity18. SOD, CAT, and GPx are cooperative defense mechanisms used to protect cells from damage caused by free radicals25,26. The CCL4-treated positive control showed a notable decrease (CAT = 17.25 U/mg, SOD = 11.06 U/mg, GPx = 11.23 U/mg) in comparison to the negative control (CAT = 39.02 U/mg, SOD = 33.25 U/mg, GPx = 26.11 U/mg), Fig. 6. The results of antioxidant enzyme activities were in agreement with Gonzalez et al.15, who studied the evaluation of hepatoprotective drugs against damage caused by acetaminophen and CCL4 in-vitro. N-acetylcysteine treatment returned these enzyme activities back to approximately normal levels (CAT = 36.06 U/mg, SOD = 31.2 U/mg, GPx = 23.6 U/mg), demonstrating its potent protective and antioxidant properties. Moringa had the greatest repair of enzymatic activity among the investigated green plant extracts (CAT = 35.49 U/mg, SOD = 31.25 U/mg, GPx = 24.04 U/mg), followed by arugula (CAT = 33.68 U/mg, SOD = 29.74 U/mg, GPx = 19.25 U/mg). Intoxication of rats with CCL4 demonstrated an increase in MDA leveland a decrease in antioxidants (GSH and CAT) levels in liver tissue, while the use of moringa seed oil improved the functions of liver enzymes27. The current results were in agreement with Khalaf et al.8, who extracted the arugula plant and reported the excessive free radical production and a deficiency of antioxidant substances, such as GSH and SOD, altering cellular redox balance. Green plant extracts, including moringa and arugula, reduced oxidative damage and restored the activity of antioxidant enzymes in CCL4-exposed AML-12 cells, highlighting their possibility as functional foods with liver-protective and antioxidative benefits.

The influence of CCL4 toxicity and protective treatments on AML-12 cell glutathione redox system

The liver is one of the tissues with the largest concentration of the endogenous tripeptide (-L-glutamyl-L-cysteinylglycine, or GSH)19. Specifically, it is the main tissue that contributes to its biosynthesis23. GSH is a crucial cellular antioxidant that protects cells from liver damage caused by reactive oxygen species (ROS). In order to explain the cellular redox environment, it has been proposed that the effective conversion of GSH to GSSG, which is then reduced back to GSH by GPx, is a marker of redox capacity8,29. The NADPH-dependent enzyme called GSSG reductase maintains GSH in its thiol-reduced state inside the cell. GSSG and glutathione conjugates (GS-R) are two more forms of GSH. Since GSH is one of the most significant endogenous antioxidant defense systems that eliminates lipid and hydrogen peroxides, maintaining optimum GSH : GSSG ratios is essential for cell survival19,22.

GSH, GSSG, and the GSH/GSSG ratio in the AML-12 cell line were measured to determine the protective effects of different green plant extracts and N-acetylcysteine against CCL4-induced liver damage, Figs. 7, 8. A healthy redox balance was indicated by the (-) control group’s high GSH level (7.28 µmol/mg) and low GSSG level (1.02 µmol/mg), which led to a high GSH/GSSG ratio of 7.14. Whereas GSH (3.12 µmol/mg) significantly decreased and GSSG (5.14 µmol/mg) significantly increased in the (+) control group, which was exposed to CCL4 without medication. The GSH/GSSG ratio significantly dropped to 0.61, indicating oxidative stress and decreased antioxidant defense. With a ratio of 5.63, N-acetylcysteine treatment dramatically reduced GSSG to 1.36 µmol/mg and raised GSH to 7.66 µmol/mg, demonstrating its powerful antioxidant activity. In the same way, as compared to the (+) control, green plant extracts raised GSH levels and lowered GSSG levels. After CCL4 was administered, the amount of GSH in the liver was reduced. This could be because GSH is essential for eliminating hypochlorous acid and CCL3OO• radicals, and GSH is a major mediator in the body’s detoxification process12. N-acetylcysteine can cross the hepatic cell membrane and be catabolized into cysteine, which is a precursor to GSH30. With GSH of 7.11 µmol/mg, GSSG of 1.56 µmol/mg, and a GSH/GSSG ratio of 4.56, moringa showed the strongest protective effect among the plants, followed by arugula with a ratio of 3.16. Prior research has shown that raising GSH plasma levels has positive systemic effects31, which is consistent with the reported GSH depletion in patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease as compared to control32. GSH compounds have been reported to be able to cross cell membranes and increase oral availability, although oral GSH treatment does not considerably increase GSH in plasma. Via preventing the production of free radicals, consuming minthapiperite preserves GSH levels in tissue injured by CCL411 and arsenic33. While celery seed extract was used to protect rats’ livers from DEHP-induced toxicity34. These results observed the potential uses as functional foods against the CCL4 hepatotoxicity.

The influence of CCL4 toxicity and protective treatments on AML-12 cell pro-inflammatory cytokines

The pro-inflammatory cytokines as TNF-α and IL-6, can play a crucial role in the immune system10. In AML-12 cells exposed to CCL4, the effects of green plant extracts and N-acetylcysteine on inflammatory markers were evaluated by measuring the cytokines TNF-α and IL-6. The basal levels of TNF-α (17.25 pg/mL) and IL-6 (11.09 pg/mL) were low in the (–) control group, suggesting a typical state of inflammation. Conversely, both cytokines (TNF-α: 72.36 pg/mL and IL-6: 66.57 pg/mL) were significantly elevated in the (+) control group, indicating that CCL4 triggered substantial inflammatory responses. Treatment with N-acetylcysteine dramatically reduced these levels to 21 pg/mL for IL-6 and 26.14 pg/mL for TNF-α, demonstrating its strong anti-inflammatory effects. N-acetylcysteine reduced liver damage by eliminating TNF-α and IL-6, Fig. 9. N-acetylcysteine interferes with nuclear factor kappa-B phosphorylation and suppresses the production of adhesion molecules, which attract neutrophils to the site of inflammation12. The biggest decreases occurred with arugula and moringa, which reduced TNF-α to 38.05 and 42.2 pg/mL and IL-6 to 33.25 and 28.54 pg/mL, respectively, indicating significant protective benefits. Celery, broccoli, and mint similarly reduced cytokine levels in comparison to the (+) control. Results of inflammatory markers as TNF-α and IL-1β, were in agreement with clear extraction in a concentration of (100 mg/ml) on toxic rat liver23. Nayif et al.35 reported a dose-dependent protective effect of the mint extract (100 mg/kg), with the higher dose providing more protection against TNF-α and IL-6, in mice caused by the ethanolic extract. The efficiency of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities in Mentha piperita can be in charge to the decrease in IL-6 and IL-1β biomarkers36. The phytochemical components perhaps have an effect on the inflammatory signaling pathway, reducing the oxidative damage, and modifying the immunological responses37. In general, these findings established that both N-acetylcysteine and the studied green plant extracts significantly decreased the inflammatory responses carried on by the toxic CCL4, demonstrating their possible uses as functional foods or even therapeutic agents in the reduction of hepatic inflammation.Fig. 9Effect of green plant extracts and N-Acetylcysteine on pro-inflammatory cytokines in AML-12 cell line; different letters mean significant differences among groups.

Conclusion

The purpose of the current research work was to compare the hepatoprotective potential of N-acetylcysteine and various green plant extracts against CCL4-induced liver damage in the AML-12 cell line. The detected green plant extracts possess the potential to be natural functional foods for hepatic activity; broccoli, mint, and celery showed moderate and considerable protection, while moringa and arugula have been found to have the strongest hepatoprotective advantages. Additional examination is required to determine the crucial mechanisms and in-vivo efficacy of various green plant extracts for liver protection.

Limitations

The use of crude aqueous and hydroalcoholic extracts without direct analytical characterization (e.g., HPLC or GC–MS) is one of the study’s main limitations; as a result, although the hepatoprotective effects are strong, the precise bioactive molecules causing these effects need to be experimentally confirmed in subsequent studies.
