Firearm classification from acoustic signals using combined mel spectrogram, MFCC, LFCC, and CRNN networks
Authors: Lamine Elkarous, Mohamed Hechmi Jeridi, Montassar Dhouibi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Discriminating the source of gunfire and accurately identifying the type of firearm used present a complex multifaceted challenge, requiring the integration of advanced acoustic signal processing techniques with cutting-edge deep learning methodologies. This research addresses the inherent difficulties in achieving reliable gunshot classification, which stem from the variability of acoustic signatures, environmental noise interference, and the need for real-time processing capabilities. The proposed system captures gunfire sounds using MEMS, processes the signals to extract discriminative features, and leverages deep learning models to classify firearm types. This work is conducted after addressing and emphasizing the challenges associated with acoustic principles, frequency modulation, acoustic pressure variations, and the acoustic fingerprints of different firearms. The system design phase highlights the technical complexities, the analysis of stringent requirements for real-world deployment, and the proposal and optimization of the model. The implementation involves collection of representative datasets, advanced data preprocessing techniques to mitigate noise and enhance signal quality, and the training of deep learning architectures using a novel feature set, termed MLFCC, which combines Mel spectrograms, MFCCs, and LFCCs. The proposed model demonstrates performance in real-time applications, effectively balancing accuracy and computational efficiency. Experimental results reveal the system’s ability to handle the complexities of real-world scenarios while also exposing its limitations, particularly its sensitivity to extreme environmental conditions. This work underscores the significant technical challenges involved in developing gunfire discrimination system and highlights the critical role of interdisciplinary approaches in overcoming these obstacles. By pushing the boundaries of acoustic signal processing and deep learning, this research contributes to the advancement of public safety technologies, offering a foundation for innovations in this demanding field.

Introduction

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) and acoustic signal processing has opened new frontiers in security and forensic sciences, particularly in the identification and classification of gunshot sounds. Law enforcement, public safety, and emergency response systems rely on the accurate and timely detection of firearm discharges to enhance situational awareness, facilitate forensic investigations, and enable rapid responses to shooting incidents. However, the task of identifying gunshots from acoustic data remains inherently complex due to variations in acoustic signatures, environmental noise interference, and the need for real-time processing.

Traditional sound classification methods have relied on spectral analysis to differentiate acoustic signatures. However, deep learning techniques-particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Convolutional Recurrent Neural Networks (CRNNs)-have revolutionized the field by enabling the extraction of intricate patterns from audio data.

These models excel at capturing both spatial and temporal features, making them well-suited for applications such as gunshot recognition, where distinguishing firearm discharges from background noise is critical. Despite recent progress, the lack of high-quality, annotated gunshot datasets remains a challenge for training robust deep learning models.

To address this, researchers have developed specialized datasets, such as the Forensic dataset, which comprises carefully recorded gunshot samples in controlled environments. These resources aim to improve model performance and the reliability of automated systems in real-world scenarios.

In this work, we propose a novel approach to firearm classification using a composite feature set called MLFCC (Mel-Linear Frequency Cepstral Coefficients), which integrates Mel spectrograms, Mel Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs), and Linear Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (LFCCs). This comprehensive acoustic fingerprint captures complementary information across multiple frequency bands and cepstral domains, improving the discriminability of acoustic features. The proposed method leverages a CRNN architecture, combining CNN-based spatial feature extraction with the temporal modeling capabilities of RNNs to effectively capture the spectral and sequential characteristics of gunshot sounds.

This work aims to overcome the challenges associated with reliable gunshot classification by pursuing three key objectives.

First, it seeks to enhance feature discriminability through the development and validation of a novel composite representation, the MLFCC (Mel–Linear Frequency Cepstral Coefficients), which fuses Mel spectrograms, MFCCs, and LFCCs to jointly capture complementary spectral and cepstral cues across multiple frequency bands.

Second, it focuses on designing a robust hybrid CRNN architecture that combines convolutional layers for spatial feature extraction with recurrent layers for temporal sequence modeling, thereby enabling the effective characterization of both spectral and temporal dynamics of gunshot events.

Finally, the system aims to achieve high real-time classification accuracy while maintaining computational efficiency suitable for embedded deployment in practical acoustic monitoring environments.

The methodology follows a pipeline of data acquisition, preprocessing, feature extraction, and model training.

Advanced data augmentation techniques-including time-shifting, pitch-shifting, and noise reduction-are applied to address dataset imbalances and enhance model robustness.

The system is designed for real-time operation, making it suitable for public safety applications.

Experimental results demonstrate the system’s effectiveness, achieving an average accuracy of 96.3% with a loss of 0.103. However, challenges persist, particularly in distinguishing between acoustically similar firearms, such as the M4 and M16 rifles, which share sound characteristics due to their common AR-15 chassis design. Future research will focus on integrating multi-modal sensing technologies, exploring advanced signal processing techniques, and expanding the diversity of training datasets to further improve performance.

By pushing the boundaries of deep learning and acoustic signal processing, this study contributes to advancements in public safety technology, laying the groundwork for future innovations in firearm detection and classification.

The proposed approach represents a significant step toward addressing the technical challenges of gunshot recognition, with the potential to enhance situational awareness and response in critical scenarios.

This paper is structured to provide a logical progression from methodology to experimental validation. The first section outlines the proposed approach, detailing the theoretical foundations of CRNNs and the MLFCC feature set for gunshot classification. It covers spatial and temporal processing models, data collection, preprocessing, feature extraction, and model training. The second section presents experimental results, including performance metrics and real-time testing outcomes, followed by an analysis of the system’s limitations and broader implications.

Proposed approach

Proposed solution consists of an acoustic identification system for small-calibre weapons that uses acoustic fingerprints captured by a microphone and and analyzes them by leveraging an advanced algorithmic deep learning pipeline. This predicts the type of firearm used. The acoustic firearms identification system is based on several key stages, capture, process and analyse the acoustic signal generated by a gunshot, culminating in the inference of the ballistic signature’s classification.

The first stage consists of acquiring the acoustic signal using an appropriate capture device.

Once the acoustic signal has been processed, the next step is to use an artificial intelligence (AI) model to predict the type of firearm based on the data. Theoretical model

Spatial feature extraction architectures and sequential memory-based models are fundamental approaches in acoustic signal processing7. The former excels at capturing local and hierarchical patterns from spectral data through multi-layer filtering operations, while the latter models temporal dependencies via recurrent state transitions.

A hybrid integration of these paradigms enables advanced acoustic analysis, significantly enhancing the characterization and identification of transient signals, including small-caliber firearm discharges.

Spatial feature extraction

The architecture employs a multi-stage hierarchical processing pipeline optimized for high-dimensional spectro-temporal data analysis. Each processing stage implements specialized transformations that progressively extract increasingly abstract and discriminative feature representations.

The system’s shift-invariant properties emerge from its carefully designed local connectivity patterns and weight sharing mechanisms. The underlying computational architecture employs learnable finite impulse response (FIR) filters arranged in deep filter-bank structures. This design enables localized convolutional operations across the input representation, facilitating the extraction of both spectral and spatial features.

By interpreting the spectrogram as a two-dimensional image, the approach leverages principles from advanced machine vision methodologies. Techniques originally developed for industrial visual inspection, which emphasize the joint extraction of global and local features to produce abstract and discriminative representations8, reinforce the relevance of such hierarchical processing pipelines for capturing fine-grained acoustic patterns essential to robust classification.

Recurrent Neural Networks have the same input and output architecture as any other deep neural network. However, differences arise in how information flows from input to output.

According to9,10, the most effective method for image classification is the use of Convolutional Neural Networks. The CNN model11,12, has demonstrated its efficiency in the classification of audio data by leveraging the spatio-temporal features of acoustic signals. According to this research13, Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) are designed to detect patterns in sequences of data, such as images.

Unlike traditional neural networks, RNNs can remember past events and use a “memory” to capture computed information up to that point. LSTM (Long Short-Term Memory) is the most commonly used RNN, particularly in linguistic translation and speech processing to extract features from a sequence of data, such as videos.

Hybrid architectures for spatiotemporal feature learning

Hybrid neural architectures that integrate convolutional and recurrent components-collectively known as Convolutional Recurrent Neural Networks (CRNNs)-offer a robust framework for joint spatial and temporal feature learning. In these architectures, convolutional layers operate as spatial encoders, extracting hierarchical feature representations from input data, while recurrent layers, such as LSTMs or GRUs, model temporal dynamics by capturing dependencies across sequential frames or observations.

This architectural (Fig. 2) synergy enables CRNNs to effectively learn complex spatiotemporal correlations, making them highly suitable for tasks such as video sequence analysis, scene understanding, and time-series forecasting. By leveraging convolutional feature hierarchies in conjunction with recurrent state transitions, CRNNs achieve superior representational efficiency and improved generalization in domains characterized by both spatial structure and temporal continuity.Figure 2CRNN hybrid architecture for spatio-temporal feature learning.

Acoustic fingerprint

Features used for acoustic pattern recognition, such as Mel Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) and the Mel-spectrogram, were initially developed for speech recognition14. However, these features have been extended to different acoustic scenarios, including gunshot detection15.

The Mel-spectrogram is a visual representation of frequency changes in a signal over time. For the human ear, frequencies of 600 Hz and 1000 Hz may sound different, but frequencies of 7600 Hz and 8000 Hz may sound similar. Due to the non-linear transformations of the frequency scale, frequencies that seem less distant appear closer together on the Mel scale.

In the Mel spectrogram, the y-axis represents the Mel scale, and the x-axis represents time. The Mel spectrogram is calculated by dividing the audio signal into windows of 2048 samples with a 512 sample overlap. Next, a Fast Fourier Transform is applied to each window, and the given audio spectrum is divided into evenly spaced frequencies; 128 Mel-filter banks are taken for this purpose. Finally, for each window, based on the Mel scale frequencies, the magnitude of the audio signal is decomposed into its components (Fig. 3).Figure 3M4 gunshot mel spectrogram.

M4 gunshot mel spectrogram.

Mel Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) help us understand the periodic structures in frequency that provide us with information related to echoes. In MFCC, frequency bands are evenly spaced on the Mel scale, allowing for a better audio representation. MFCC are the coefficients of the Mel Frequency Cepstrum.

To obtain a sequence of MFCC, a sampling rate of 44,100 Hz was used, the number of output MFCC features is set to 44, the FFT window length is 2048 samples, the number of samples between successive frames is 512, and a Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) was employed (Fig. 4 illustrates the M4 Gunshot MFCC Spectrogram).Figure 4M4 gunshot MFCC spectrogram.

M4 gunshot MFCC spectrogram.

Linear Prediction Coefficients (LPC)11 widely used technique in acoustic pattern recognition is based on the principle of modeling the audio signal using a linear prediction model. They are designed to capture the spectral properties of a signal by approximating the linear relationship between past and future samples of the signal.

In the context of gunshot recognition, LPC coefficients can be utilized to extract relevant information about the specific acoustic characteristics of gunshots. By adjusting the parameters of LPC, such as the analysis window length and the number of coefficients, it is possible to obtain a compact yet informative representation of signal features related to gunshots (Fig. 5).Figure 5Linear prediction coefficient spectral modeling of M4 gunshot (12-coefficient configuration).

The extraction of MFCC coefficients

In proposed acoustic identification system, the extraction of MFCC coefficients is carried out following several key steps. First, the audio signal is pre-emphasized to amplify high frequencies and improve signal quality. The signal is then divided into fixed-size frames so that different signal segments can be processed independently. After that, each frame is windowed, usually using a Hamming window, to minimize undesired edge effects. After this, triangular band-pass filters are applied to divide the power spectrum of each frame into specific frequency bands, corresponding to human auditory filters. Once the frequency bands are defined, the Discrete Cosine Transform DCT16 is applied to the power spectrum of each frame. This transformation converts the spectral information into MFCC coefficients, emphasizing frequency variations that are significant for human sound perception.The extraction pipeline is visualized in the Fig. 6.Figure 6Acoustic feature extraction pipeline for MFCCs.

The extraction of LFCC coefficients

The steps to compute LFCC coefficients are similar to those used to compute MFCCs and the Mel spectrogram. First, the audio signal is divided into short segments. Next, a Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is applied to each segment to obtain the short-term spectrum. The magnitude spectrum is then filtered using a bank of linear gamma-tone filters, similar to the Mel spectrogram computation. The outputs of the band-pass filters are then processed to obtain a logarithmic energy value. Finally, the LFCC coefficients are computed using a Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT), similar to the computation of MFCCs.

Extracting acoustic features

Mel spectrogram, MFCC, and LFCC are effective for extracting acoustic features to form acoustic fingerprints11,14,15).

Combining these methods to extract sound characteristics for gunshot identification and classification aims to create a comprehensive acoustic fingerprint encompassing various aspects of gunshot sounds. The Mel spectrogram provides a frequency-based representation, while MFCC and LFCC offer spectral and cepstral insights. Methodology

Focusing on gunshot identification and calibre classification using a combined CNN and RNN-LSTM approach, a comprehensive set of audio preprocessing operations was conducted to enhance the quality of the acoustic data. Additionally, real-time audio processing techniques were implemented to facilitate live listening for gunshot analysis. Furthermore, extensive model training was carried out on the CRNN architecture using a carefully curated dataset, ensuring that the model was robustly trained to accurately classify small firearm calibers based on their distinct acoustic profiles.

Audio pre-processing and feature extraction duties were made easier by Librosa, designed specifically for audio signal analysis. This allowed us to extract valuable insights from audio datasets. TensorFlow, made it easier to train and implement complex deep learning models, while Keras provide intuitive user interface. PyAudio facilitated real-time audio processing, Torch provided a robust platform for building and training neural networks, and Audiomentations enriched dataset diversity through advanced audio data augmentation techniques.

The dataset was thoughtfully curated, containing audio files organized into ten distinct classes. With classes including AK-12, AK-47, IMI Desert Eagle, M4, M16, M249, MG-42, MP5, Zastava M92, and other noises, we were particularly interested in classifying firearm noises.

The scope of the data set was expanded by adding the “Other Sounds” class, which included a wide variety of urban sounds outside of categories connected to firearms. To mitigate dataset imbalance and enhance model generalization, an extensive audio data augmentation protocol was applied. Augmentation operations simulated realistic acoustic variability while preserving signal integrity. The adopted transformations included time-shifting, time masking, pitch-shifting, Gaussian noise injection, and spectrogram-domain masking.

Time-shifting displaced the waveform along the temporal axis to improve invariance to alignment variations. Time masking muted short temporal segments, promoting robustness to missing data. Pitch-shifting modified spectral pitch while maintaining temporal consistency, reproducing natural frequency variations. Gaussian noise injection introduced stochastic perturbations, improving resilience to environmental noise. Spectrogram-based time and frequency masking enhanced feature invariance across temporal and spectral domains.

Pitch-shifting was randomized within ±4 semitones to emulate variations in resonance characteristics. Data processing

Raw firearm audio signals were resampled to 44.1 kHz, converted to mono, amplitude-normalized, and pre-emphasized to enhance high-frequency transients. The processed signals were segmented using a Short-Time Fourier Transform with 25 ms frames and 10 ms overlap to obtain time–frequency representations. From these, Mel Spectrograms, MFCCs, and LFCCs were extracted to capture complementary perceptual and spectral characteristics. All features were standardized and structured into multi-representational tensors.

Data loading

Audio recordings were ingested and managed using the Python libraries Librosa and PySoundFile, which support multiple formats, including WAV and MP3. Librosa was employed for waveform reading, resampling, and conversion to mono channels, while PySoundFile provided precise low-level access for frame-accurate decoding and metadata retrieval. All recordings were standardized to a uniform sampling rate of 44.1 kHz to maintain temporal consistency across the dataset.

The audio signals were stored as one-dimensional time-series arrays representing amplitude variations over time, preserving transient and high-frequency content critical for firearm acoustic analysis. This representation is illustrated for an AK-47 signal in Fig. 10.

To optimize memory usage and enable efficient training, audio data were loaded in buffered batches, allowing on-the-fly preprocessing without exceeding system memory constraints. Each waveform was checked for clipping or silence, and normalization routines were applied to maintain consistent amplitude ranges across recordings. This standardized and memory-efficient representation facilitated subsequent segmentation, windowing, spectral transformations, and augmentation pipelines.

Resampling

Acoustic sequences exhibit variability in their sampling frequencies, posing challenges for consistent analysis and interoperability across subsequent stages of the processing pipeline. To address this, standardization of the sampling frequency is implemented as a necessary preprocessing step to ensure methodological coherence and compatibility.

Within this context, resampling adjusts the temporal resolution of acoustic signals, thereby enabling uniformity and facilitating integration throughout the analytical framework.

The algorithmic basis of resampling involves either interpolation or decimation, depending on whether the target sampling frequency is higher or lower than that of the original signal.

Interpolation entails the generation of additional data points between existing samples, thereby increasing the sampling rate, while decimation involves the systematic removal of samples to achieve a lower sampling rate. This resampling procedure is essential for producing temporally consistent acoustic representations, which in turn ensures the reliability and comparability of features extracted for subsequent deep learning-based analyses.

Suppose we have an original audio signal with a sampling frequency of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{f}_\textit{s}$$\end{document} (in Hz) and a total duration of T (in seconds). To resample the signal to a new sampling frequency \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{f}_\textit{s}'$$\end{document}, we can use the following formula:

New number of samples8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\textbf {N}}' = \left\lfloor N \times \frac{\textit{f}_\textit{s}'}{\textit{f}_\textit{s}} \right\rfloor \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where N is the original number of samples,\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{f}_\textit{s}'$$\end{document} is the new sampling frequency, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{f}_\textit{s}$$\end{document} is the original sampling frequency5.

In this study, all audio recordings were resampled to a standardized sampling rate of 44.1 kHz to ensure consistency in temporal and frequency resolution across the dataset.

The resampling procedure was implemented using the resample and resamplepoly functions from the SciPy and NumPy signal processing libraries, which apply polyphase filtering to mitigate aliasing artifacts and preserve signal integrity. Prior to decimation or interpolation, an anti-aliasing finite impulse response (FIR) low-pass filter was applied to suppress frequency components above the Nyquist threshold of the target sampling rate.

This preprocessing step was essential for harmonizing the dataset and ensuring the reliability of subsequent time-frequency analyses and feature extraction procedures employed in the deep learning pipeline..

Converting stereo channels to mono

We converted all audio files to mono format to match the single-channel input of our audio sensor.

Converting stereo audio files to mono involves combining the left and right channels into a single channel17. This results in a mono representation of the audio signal, where all audio information is present in a single audio stream.

Converting stereo channels to mono can be done using a simple formula:9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \text {Mono} = \frac{{Left Channel} + {Right Channel}}{2} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}This formula takes each sample from the left channel, adds it to the corresponding sample from the right channel, and then divides the result by two to get the mono sample. Short-time fourier transform (STFT)

Once the audio is processed, a Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) is applied to each frame to obtain the frequency spectrum. The Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT) serves as a core analytical tool in audio processing, enabling time-frequency representation of sound signals. By decomposing the signal into localized frequency components, STFT reveals spectral evolution over time. The transform is mathematically expressed as:14\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {\textbf {X}}_{{STFT}}[\textit{k, n}] = \frac{1}{N} \sum _{\textit{m=0}}^{\textit{N-1}} {\textbf {x}}[\textit{m + nH}] \cdot {\textbf {w}}[\textit{m}] \cdot W^{km} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}An STFT is the Fourier transform of a signal x[m], multiplied by a windowing function w[m]. In this definition, N of the windowing length, n is the temporal frame index, and k refers to the frequency index. H stands in for the hop length, which determines the overlap of adjacent frames as N-H. The overlap ratio between frames is (N-H)/N.

Benchmarking Performance: acoustic feature fusion and CRNN architecture

This work details a specialized framework for firearm classification, leveraging a Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (CRNN) architecture combined with the consolidated MLFCC (Mel-Linear Frequency Cepstral Coefficients) feature set to enhance feature discriminability (Fig. 21). This system achieved a classification accuracy of 96.3% with a cross-entropy loss of 0.103 on the optimized test dataset, representing an observable improvement when benchmarked against internal models, such as the initial CRNN (94% accuracy) and the standard CNN (84% accuracy).

The improved performance can be attributed to the MLFCC feature set, which integrates Mel spectrogram, MFCC, and LFCC to capture complementary spectral and cepstral information across different frequency scales, enhancing the model’s capacity to distinguish subtle acoustic variations in firearm discharges.

The CRNN architecture further contributes by combining convolutional layers for local spectral-temporal feature extraction with recurrent layers that model long-term temporal dependencies, enabling the learning of detailed and structured spatiotemporal representations. Nonetheless, the model has difficulty separating certain firearm classes with highly similar acoustic profiles, such as the M4 and M16 rifles, due to their nearly identical firing characteristics. Moreover, external factors such as ambient noise, reverberation, and variations in source-to-microphone positioning can alter the acoustic signature, limiting system robustness and highlighting the need for advanced signal enhancement, feature normalization, and augmented training data to support deployment in real-world conditions.

To address current limitations and enhance system performance, several strategic improvements merit investigation.

First, expanding the training set to incorporate greater diversity of firearms, recording conditions, and environmental scenarios would strengthen the model’s generalization and robustness to real-world variability.

Second, exploring advanced signal processing techniques-such as adaptive noise cancellation and blind source separation-could improve performance in noisy or complex acoustic environments by isolating target signals more effectively.

Finally, integrating this technology with complementary sensors may enable a multimodal system that improves detection accuracy and situational awareness through sensor fusion18.

Benchmark results establish the technical merit of combining acoustic feature extraction with CRNN models for precise firearm classification. Our system achieves high accuracy and robust real-time performance, validating its practical potential. While acknowledging current limitations and ethical considerations, this work provides a foundation for advancing acoustic-based firearm detection technology-a critical step toward enhancing public safety and security. Future refinements in dataset diversity, signal processing, and multimodal integration could further elevate the system’s reliability in real-world scenarios.

Conclusion

The research presented here established a specialized framework for small-caliber firearm classification, leveraging a novel feature extraction approach, MLFCC (Mel-Linear Frequency Cepstral Coefficients), which integrates Mel spectrograms, MFCCs, and LFCCs to enhance feature discriminability in the cepstral domain.

The core architecture employed is a Convolutional Recurrent Neural Network (CRNN), designed to simultaneously capture localized spectro-temporal patterns through convolutional operations and model long-range temporal dependencies via recurrent layers. Experimental results demonstrated the efficacy of the optimized system, which achieved a classification accuracy of 96.3% with a cross-entropy loss of 0.103 on the test dataset.

The system’s robustness was supported by comprehensive preprocessing stages, including spectral subtraction for noise reduction, dynamic range compression, and adaptive resampling to 44.1 kHz.

Data augmentation techniques, such as time-domain warping and additive Gaussian noise, were additionally employed to improve model robustness against environmental variability. Crucially, implementation on embedded hardware confirmed the feasibility of real-time operation, exhibiting an average inference latency of 187ms per 2-second audio segment.

While the model exhibited particular efficacy in distinguishing firearm classes with distinct acoustic signatures , it encountered specific challenges that must be explicitly acknowledged. Performance degradation was observed when classifying weapons sharing common mechanical platforms, notably the M4 and M16 rifles.

This limitation primarily stems from the inherent spectral similarity in their muzzle blast waveforms and the overlapping characteristics of their shockwave propagation patterns.

Furthermore, operational applicability is challenged by the system’s sensitivity to extreme environmental conditions, where external factors like ambient noise, reverberation, and variations in source-to-microphone positioning can alter the acoustic signature, limiting overall robustness.

Technically, the current feature extraction pipeline does not fully account for nonlinear propagation effects in outdoor environments, and the temporal resolution of the STFT-based features may be insufficient to accurately capture transient ballistic signatures in certain scenarios.

Future work is required to mitigate these constraints. Subsequent investigations should explore wavelet-based time-frequency representations and consider incorporating physical models of muzzle blast formation to enhance feature discriminability . Moreover, research directions include the development of multi-sensor fusion architectures and the investigation of attention mechanisms to improve classification performance, particularly on the acoustically similar firearm classes.

This work contributes significantly to the ongoing development of robust acoustic classification systems for security applications, although further validation under different operational conditions is explicitly required to confirm reliability in uncontrolled settings.
