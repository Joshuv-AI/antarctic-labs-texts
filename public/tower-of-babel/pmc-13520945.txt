Stand‐alone graph‐based lung airway labeling with quantitative validations
Authors: Hairong Chen, Arezoo Modiri, Amit Sawant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have shown that protecting airways connected to high‐functioning lung parenchyma reduces the risk of post‐treatment radiation‐induced lung injury. To account for airway sparing in radiotherapy planning, a critical requirement is to characterize the geometry and radiation response of individual airway segments.PurposeTo report on automated individual airway labeling framework with branch dimensions extracted from CT‐derived virtual bronchoscopy images.MethodsWe developed an automated graph‐based topological labeling algorithm with bifurcation detections and context‐based denoising. In‐house modules for estimating airway diameters and tube length were also developed. Both labeling and meta‐information estimation were validated against a commercial virtual bronchoscopy software in data from 20 lung cancer patients. Labeling metrics were segmentation surface scores: i.e., average symmetric surface distance (ASSD), normalized surface distance (NSD), and 95th percentile surface Hausdorff (HD95surf). Meta‐information metrics were absolute errors of branch major diameter, minor diameter, and tube length. Mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) were calculated to assess meta‐information deviations in both validations.ResultsMean and standard deviations were 0.56 ± 0.21 mm for ASSD, 87.66 ± 3.20% for NSD, and 2.51 ± 0.68 mm for HD95surf. Deviations of meta‐information metrics in labeling validation were MAE/RMSE: 0.33 ± 0.09/0.67 ± 0.16 mm for major diameter, 0.24 ± 0.07/0.53 ± 0.15 mm for minor diameter, and 3.27 ± 0.82/5.53 ± 1.53 mm for tube length. Deviations of meta‐information metrics in meta‐information validation were MAE/RMSE: 1.81 ± 0.30/2.31 ± 0.41 mm for major diameter, 1.25 ± 0.27/1.14 ± 0.26 mm for minor diameter, and 2.20 ± 0.32/3.24 ± 0.49 mm for tube length.ConclusionThe proposed open‐source framework provides stand‐alone, fully‐automated airway labeling with very good agreement with a commercial virtual bronchoscopy system. Our method shows strong potential for use in functionally‐guided lung sparing in radiation treatment plan optimization.

INTRODUCTION

Radiation therapy alone or combined with systemic therapy (chemo‐ and/or immune therapy) is the current standard of care for locally advanced lung cancer (stage III per American Joint Committee on Cancer Version 9 guidelines). 1 Lung cancer patients come with an already compromised pulmonary system with ∼70% having chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. 2 , 3 Many investigations have focused on strategies to spare healthy lung tissue during the radiotherapy (RT) plan optimization process. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 These techniques aim to improve post‐RT lung function by spatially mapping highly functional regions within the lung parenchyma and creating treatment plans that redirect radiation dose from these high‐functioning subvolumes to relatively low‐functioning subvolumes. Although multiple early‐stage clinical trials have assessed the feasibility of this approach, the technique overall has shown only modest benefits in post‐RT functional sparing. 5 , 6 , 11 , 12 In this context, Vicente et al. postulated that it is crucial to protect not only healthy lung parenchyma but also the connected elements of the airway tree that supply air to the healthy lung tissue. 13 They showed through modeling that this approach achieved superior post‐treatment preservation of ventilation (on average 14.5% higher) compared to state‐of‐the‐art methods. It used BHCT due to its higher resolution for a better chance of extracting diameters and lengths per individual airway. That information was then used to calculate the radiation sensitivity of each airway for treatment planning. The average CTs were used for treatment planning as it is in clinical practice. The airway tree contour was registered on the treatment plan CT using deformable image registration.

To meet requirements for such RT planning, individual airway branch labeling and metadata (especially branch diameters) extraction are indispensable prerequisites. Commercial virtual bronchoscopy tools such as LungQ (Thirona; Nijmegen, The Netherlands), LungPoint (Broncus Technologies, Inc.; Mountain View, CA), CT Respiratory Analysis (Canon Medical Systems USA; Tustin, CA), and VIDA Vision (VIDA Diagnostics Inc.; Coralville, IA) are available for this purpose. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 However, the use of commercial virtual bronchoscopy systems for lung RT planning presents two challenges. First, these platforms are proprietary and, therefore, expensive to acquire and maintain in the clinic. Second, these systems are designed for interventional pulmonology and, therefore, lack important features needed for RT planning, such as segmenting individual airways, extracting information on individual airways, and exporting data in a standardized image format (in the radiotherapy context, DICOM‐RT). To develop a clinically translatable, noncommercial solution for incorporation of airways as organs at risk (OARs) into the RT planning process, we need: (a) open‐source airway segmentation algorithms, and (b) robust algorithms for individual airway extraction and labeling. In this work, we focus on the second part of the problem.

Several studies have focused on the first problem, i.e., development of open‐source algorithms for whole‐airway tree segmentation. During the Extraction of Airways from CT (EXACT’09) challenge, approximately 15 algorithms were evaluated spanning region growing, morphology‐based segmentation, gradient vector flow, and template matching. 18 Additional machine learning strategies were explored through registration and hidden Markov tree models. 19 , 20 Another line of research focused on deterministic airway generation. For example, Stavros’ AVATREE platform reconstructed airway branches by iteratively segmenting initial point clouds using planes derived from principal component analysis. 21 , 22 Although clear branching rules provided useful structural guidance, uncertainty inevitably increased toward the distal branches, where point cloud density became sparse. It is noteworthy that the above‐mentioned efforts are all dedicated to segmenting the airway tree as a single structure without labeling and data extraction at individual airway level.

Relatively fewer studies have focused on the second problem, i.e., individual airway extraction and characterization. Recent advances in deep learning have improved airway segmentation, enabling greater automation, higher accuracy, and enhanced anatomical detail. 23 , 24 , 25 These models are typically trained on large‐scale datasets to infer end‐to‐end features directly from imaging data. However, most deep learning approaches yield a limited number of identifiable airway branches (e.g., the trachea, five lobe branches, 19 segmental branches, and 127 subsegmental branches in the work of Li et al.). 25 The presence and branching patterns of subsegmental airways exhibit considerable inter‐individual variability and may evolve with the progression of lung diseases, further complicating consistent labeling. The AirMorph strategy introduces a topology‐preserving deep learning pipeline that supports fully automatic anatomical labeling across lobar, segmental, and subsegmental hierarchies. 24 Although subsegmental branches at distal terminals are labeled in clustered groups, individual airways remain unresolved in these techniques.

Graph‐based algorithms have been explored in the field of airway labeling with promising results at individual airway level by introducing graphs, structures made of nodes (vertices) connected by links (edges). 26 The study by Tschirren et al. yielded 32 anatomical labels with graph theory by integrating the concepts of population average tree, parallel edges, and max‐weighted clique. 27 This number of labels is still insufficient for radiation treatment planning using the approach introduced by Vicente et al., where ∼200 individual airway segments (corresponding to 9–13 airway generations) are used in the treatment planning process. 13 , 28 Another hybrid deep learning model incorporated structural and positional features inspired by graph theory and achieved 39 anchor branches, 23 still insufficient for our purpose.

We have developed a purely graph‐based algorithm enabling comprehensive airway segmentation, labeling, and metadata extraction from CT images. We used a stacking strategy for bifurcation detection, ensued by topological ordering of detected bifurcations to ensure correct continuity and hierarchy structure of airways. The goal was to enable individual airway branch labeling and metadata extraction required for airway‐aware RT planning where hundreds of airway branches should be included. Our labeling application is stand‐alone and can be used in conjunction with any DICOM‐compliant commercial or open‐source airway segmentation software. Using the airway tree as input, our algorithm generates individual airway labels and metadata (diameters and lengths). We validated our results with those from a reference tool: the research version of LungPoint, a commercial virtual bronchoscopy software. 29 For evaluation, we performed a multistage quantitative analysis with examination of outliers.

Datasets

Under IRB‐approval, diagnostic‐quality breath‐hold CT images from 20 lung cancer patients treated with RT were retrospectively studied. Slice thickness ranged from 0.3 to 1.0 mm, and pixel resolution ranged from 0.39 to 0.97 mm. Further specifications appear in—supplemented Table S1. Airway number achieved by the reference method ranged from 113 to 323, with a total of 4681 individual branches.

Graph‐based topological labeling steps

Whole‐airway tree segments

The airway tree mask was treated as an independent input that could be derived from different tools/algorithms. We used LungPoint to produce DICOM‐RT files containing airway contours annotated with topological names and relevant metadata. LungPoint assigned branch labels based on the sorted order of airway names. Multi‐labeled volumes with branch diameters and tube length served as references for validations.

Skeletonization

Skeletonization achieves a 1‐voxel connectivity backbone with conserved topological structure. 30 Before the skeletonization procedure, a morphological close operation was applied to remove potential holes from the given airway mask. Skeleton points were classified as bifurcations, terminals, and connecting points. Bifurcation stands for the dividing point on the airway path. Terminals have only one connection. Connecting points distribute between every two bifurcations, or between bifurcation and terminal.

Airway inlet detection

Despite morphological preprocessing, the main trachea skeleton often exhibited zigzag patterns and cyclic structures, which led to false bifurcation detection at the beginning. To mitigate this issue, a filtering process was applied to determine the trachea inlet. An illustrative pseudo code is shown in Table S2.

Bifurcation detection

Within a skeleton graph, a bifurcation point has more than two connections under certain connectivity rules (such as 6‐neighbor or 26‐neighbor). However, this condition faces challenges at compact fine branches, where even split branch connecting points could be falsely categorized as bifurcations. Instead of filtering out false bifurcations through registration and vertex‐edge restrictions, 21 we used a stacking method to distinguish them. To simulate lung airway branching, the segment path before bifurcation should remain identical when defining the shortest paths from a single inlet to two terminal points. Given unweighted graph, breath‐first search (BFS) was used to traverse the shortest paths with an ordered 26‐neighborhood, defined by directional offsets ranging from −1 to 1 for all three axes. BFS is a method for exploring a network or tree by moving outward in layers. Instead of diving deep along one path, BFS examines everything that is immediately reachable from the starting point, then everything one step beyond that, and so on. This creates a level‐by‐level sweep through the structure (Figure S6 in supplement file for illustration of BFS). The bifurcation number was the terminal number minus one (Figure 3).

Illustration of stacking strategy for bifurcation detection.

Topological ordering

All shortest paths were represented by bifurcations plus inlet and terminals with their local order. Topological ordering was necessary to find the global order of all bifurcations from Kahn's algorithm (pseudo code shown in Table S3). 31

Denoising

Context‐based denoising was employed by introducing lung lobes segmented by TotalSegmentator. 31 Any terminal branches detected outside them were assumed to be spurs and eliminated due to human physiology. 32 In addition, the conventional path length thresholding (criteria as 2) was used to remove spurs or noisy skeleton points within the lobes. 27

Naming and labeling

This process worked on bifurcation points to assign names and integer labels based on topological order. Naming was carried out using a BFS that considered spatial coordinates and angular deviation from the branch direction, ensuring that branch names reflected the hierarchical tree structure. Labeling for gradient visualization followed depth‐first search traversal. Finally, the inlet and connecting skeleton points were labeled according to their subsequent bifurcations or terminal endpoints (Figure 3).

Branch volume partitioning

Voronoi partitioning, a way to divide a plane into regions based on proximity to a set of seeds, 33 was implemented to generate labeled airway branches. All labeled skeleton points served as seeds to divide the given airway tree mask in a greedy manner based on the closest distance to them.

In‐house meta‐information estimation

The required meta‐information included the means of major and minor diameters (dMeanMajor and dMeanMinor, respectively) and tube length to match the given information from the reference DICOM files. Major and minor diameters, as used here, correspond to long and short axes of the elliptical contour forming the cross‐section of the tube volume. Given individual branch volume and corresponding skeleton points, the estimation was implemented as Table S4 in supplement file.

Validation

Three aspects were validated against the reference tool's outputs: airway continuity and hierarchy, labeling, and the underlying meta‐information derivation.

Continuity validation

This validation acted as a sanity check to ensure continuous connectivity from the tracheal inlet to the terminal branches. The hierarchy map was generated through the steps in Table S5.

Labeling validation

Our developed naming and labeling scheme and that of the reference tool followed different topological orders for mapping anatomical structures. Meta‐information extraction in the two methods was also different (the method used by the reference commercial tool is not publicized). Therefore, we devised a two‐step process. First, branch volume matching was performed to identify corresponding segments based on either name or label. By fixing a reference branch, our labeled segment was iteratively evaluated to determine the one with the greatest volumetric overlap, which was then selected as its paired counterpart. Second, metrics were evaluated. Surface metrics included average symmetric surface distance (ASSD), normalized surface distance (NSD,) and 95th percentile Hausdorff (HD95surf). 34 Surface thickness was defined as the spatial diagonal of individual image spacing. Regarding surface scores, second‐order statistics were computed across 20 cases, based on first‐order measures such as mean and median. Meta‐information was estimated for matched branch pairs with our developed method. Deviations were quantified using mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE), with summary statistics reported for both metrics.

Quantitative meta‐information derivation validation

Using the reference‐labeled branches, we estimated diameters and tube lengths using our algorithm. These results were then compared against the branch data from the reference tool in terms of absolute errors. Performance was evaluated using MAE and RMSE as quantitative metrics.

Implementation

This algorithm was developed using C++17 via Microsoft Visual Studio C++ 2022 in a Windows system with third‐party libraries, such as ITK, 35 Plastimatch, 36 and DCMTK. 37

DISCUSSION

Previous studies have shown that protecting airways (supply chain for functional lungs) from radiation damage improves ventilation preservation, and thus patient quality of life, after radiotherapy in lung cancer patients. 13 Implementing airway protection in radiotherapy planning depends on developing algorithms that allow dealing with airways at individual level. We have developed and presented a stand‐alone, flexible interface to fulfill automated comprehensive airway labeling enriched with detailed branch‐level information (i.e., diameters and tube lengths) through graph‐based algorithms.

Our interface accepts airway tree mask as input. Several open‐source autosegmentation tools can produce that input. 29 , 31 Quantitative validations were performed to assess our results. As an analytical approach, our method doesn't require large dataset for validation as other deep learning models demanded.

Multiple procedures were implemented to produce robust labeling and partitioning, such as morphological preprocessing, trachea inlet detection, one‐connectivity skeletonization, bifurcation detection, and topological ordering. Second order analysis for the mean values of ASSD, NSD, and HD95surf were < 1 mm, > 85%, and < 4 mm, respectively, making our method's accuracy acceptable based on the literature‐reported measures. 34 The mean values of MAE and RMSE for meta‐information were within 1 mm for diameters and 6 mm for tube length. The independent meta‐information validation achieved average deviations within 3 mm for diameters and 4 mm for tube length. Regarding generation analysis in labeling validation, the RMSE for diameters was within 1.5 mm and for tube lengths was around 17.5 mm against generation, overall indicating the potential utility of our method for airway‐aware treatment planning. 13

Some inconsistencies between the results from the reference tool (LungPoint, a commercial, clinically validated tool) and those of our method appeared in the following cases:

(1) Overlapping: The most common reason for labeling errors was overlapping (i.e., one of the paired branches was a subset of its pair). This resulted in differences in tube lengths. Overlapping was caused by divergences in the detection of spurs and bifurcations as detailed below (Figure 13). Spur detection: Undetected spurs were treated as terminal branches and assigned subvolumes accordingly. Conversely, overdetected spurs lost their terminal seeds during spatial partitioning and were labeled based on proximity to the nearest seed. However, these branches were paired with more than one reference branch.Sensitive bifurcations: Near fine terminal regions, bifurcations were more readily detected with pronounced turning angles in small branches that exceeded a spur threshold. In these cases, more bifurcations could be detected by our method than by the reference tool.Shifted bifurcations: Due to BFS's sensitivity to local connectivity and queuing order, nodes at the same depth can be visited in varying sequences, especially in dense regions like bifurcations. 38 This traversal variability altered shortest path construction, causing bifurcation points—shared by multiple paths from inlet to terminals—to appear displaced.

The overlapping challenges were mainly reflected in the labeling validation results in terms of reduced NSD and elevated ASSD and HD95surf values in some airway generations. A comparable pattern was observed in Stavros’ diameter‐generation outcomes with generation between 12 and 16, despite employing different derivation techniques. 21

(2) Inconsistencies in reference meta‐information: We observed that potentially inaccurate diameter and tube length values were assigned to some branches by our reference tool. Manual measurements using third‐party tools such as ITK‐SNAP showed strong alignment with our estimations but notable deviation from the reference records—suggesting potential errors in the original meta‐info (Figure S3 in supplement file). The manual evaluation was an exploratory step performed by a single observer for some of the outliers to understand the reason for the errors. These inconsistencies were most observed in lower‐generation branches, where diameter calculations appeared to rely on broader segments inherited from preceding branches instead of average diameters as illustrated in Figure 11, with larger absolute error for generations between 2 and 5. Figures 10 and 11 revealed trends across all 4,681 branches, showing diameter error magnitudes tilting up at generations 2 to 4 and becoming stable after generation 6.

(3) In our method, following 3D alignment, 2D contours for each slice were extracted using a level‐set‐based library function. A default value of 1 mm was assigned to diameter if partial contours, especially in sharp‐edged shapes like rectangles or squares (e.g., in fine terminal branches), were detected. This is the reason for an upward trend in diameter errors with increasing airway generation, as illustrated in Figure 11.
