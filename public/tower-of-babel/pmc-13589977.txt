Lipid Variability Predicts Long-Term Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events in MINOCA: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Yanlin Wang, Mingrui Diao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigates the predictive value of long-term variability in low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in patients with myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA).MethodsIn this retrospective cohort study, 254 consecutive MINOCA patients (80 with MACEs, 174 without) were analyzed over a median 41-month follow-up. Lipid variability (LV) was quantified using standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), and variability independent of the mean (VIM) from ≥ 3 measurements. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. Collinearity among variability metrics was assessed via variance inflation factor (VIF), and separate Cox models were constructed for each metric to avoid multicollinearity. The independent association between LV and MACE risk was analyzed via multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models, adjusted for established clinical covariates including age, sex, BMI, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, baseline lipid levels, and statin use during follow-up. The predictive performance was evaluated with time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves.ResultsMACE incidence was 31.5% during follow-up. While all variability metrics were higher in the MACE group (P < 0.05), multivariate analysis identified only HDL-C SD (HR = 3.751, 95% CI: 2.088–6.740, P < 0.001), HDL-C VIM (HR = 1.620, 95% CI: 1.055–2.486, P = 0.027), and Lp(a) VIM (HR = 2.229, 95% CI: 1.334–3.725, P = 0.002) as independent predictors of MACEs, after adjusting for baseline confounding factors, the results were consistent with it. Time-dependent ROC curve analysis indicated that a composite score offered moderate predictive value at 2.5 and 3.5 years of follow-up (AUC of 0.751 and 0.714, respectively), but its performance declined at 4.5 years (AUC = 0.696).ConclusionIn MINOCA, variability in HDL-C and long-term variability in Lp(a) independently predict MACE risk. Beyond achieving lipid targets, maintaining long-term stability may be clinically important, offering a novel perspective for risk stratification and personalized management.Keywords: myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries, lipid variability, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, lipoprotein(a), major adverse cardiovascular events, prognosis

Introduction

Myocardial infarction with non-obstructive coronary arteries (MINOCA) constitutes a distinct clinical syndrome characterized by definitive evidence of acute myocardial infarction (MI) despite the absence of flow-limiting obstructive lesions (stenosis degree < 50%, ie, normal or near-normal coronary arteries) in all major epicardial coronary arteries on angiography.1 The diagnosis requires adherence to a rigorous and integrative pathway. Firstly, the universal biochemical criteria for acute MI must be met, involving a serial rise and/or fall in cardiac troponin levels. Secondly, objective evidence of myocardial ischemia is required, encompassing typical ischemic symptoms, electrocardiographic ischemic changes or pathological Q waves, imaging evidence of new loss of viable myocardium or regional wall motion abnormality, or an angiographically identified coronary thrombus. A key prerequisite is the angiographic confirmation of the absence of significant stenosis (< 50% in all major vessels). Finally, other clinical conditions that could explain the acute presentation (eg, pulmonary embolism, myocarditis) and non-ischemic causes of myocardial injury [eg, severe renal failure, acute decompensation of chronic heart failure (HF)] must be systematically ruled out before establishing the diagnosis.2,3 Globally, MINOCA accounts for approximately 5%–15% of all MIs, with the prognosis paralleling that of obstructive MI, and its mortality risk parallels that of obstructive MI, indicating that it is not a benign condition but rather carries similarly severe prognosis.4–7 Therefore, early accurate identification and effective management of MINOCA are crucial for improving patient outcomes.

Dyslipidemia is a well-established risk factor for various cardiovascular diseases, including MI, and is closely associated with patient mortality risk.8–10 Consequently, effective control of lipid levels is a cornerstone for preventing cardiovascular events and reducing mortality. Emerging research suggests that lipid variability (LV), commonly defined as the degree of fluctuation in lipid parameters over time, can serve as a supplementary metric for assessing cardiovascular risk and mortality. Although no universally standardized definition exists, LV is typically quantified in research using statistical metrics such as standard deviation (SD), coefficient of variation (CV), and variability independent of the mean (VIM).11,12 To date, LV has been increasingly validated as an independent predictor of cardiovascular risk and mortality across various patient populations.12 However, its prognostic value in the specific context of MINOCA has not been systematically evaluated. Methodological challenges in assessing longitudinal LV, such as the influence of measurement frequency, follow-up duration, and time-dependent exposure assessment, warrant careful consideration when interpreting variability estimates.

Based on this, this retrospective cohort study seeks to systematically assess the associations of various variability metrics (including SD, CV, and VIM) for low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] with the risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACEs) in patients with MINOCA. We aim to provide novel evidence-based insights for long-term risk stratification and management in this unique population.

Materials and Methods

Participants

This was a single-center retrospective cohort study. To assess sample size adequacy, a statistical power analysis was conducted based on previous literature and preliminary data. Assuming a hazard ratio (HR) of 2.0 for MACEs between high and low LV groups, with a two-sided α of 0.05, a power of 80%, and an estimated total event rate of 31.5%, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 230 patients. Accounting for a potential 10% loss to follow-up, 254 consecutive patients hospitalized for acute MI and subsequently diagnosed with MINOCA between June 1, 2020 and June 30, 2023, were ultimately included. Based on follow-up data until June 30, 2025, patients were divided into two groups according to the occurrence of MACEs: MACE group (n = 80) and non-MACE group (n = 174). MACEs were defined as a composite endpoint including cardiac death, hospitalization for new-onset or worsening HF, non-fatal recurrent MI, ischemic stroke, and re-hospitalization for unstable angina or worsening anginal symptoms. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xindu District People’s Hospital of Chengdu (Approval No.: [IIT]PJ2025038), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Inclusion criteria: The diagnosis of MINOCA strictly adhered to international consensus criteria:13 (1) Diagnosis of acute MI according to the Fourth Universal Definition of MI (serial rise and/or fall of cardiac troponin levels with at least one value above the 99th percentile upper reference limit); (2) Evidence of myocardial ischemia based on clinical presentation, electrocardiogram, imaging, or angiographic findings; (3) Coronary angiography showing stenosis < 50% in all major epicardial arteries (diameter ≥ 2.0 mm); (4) Systematic exclusion of other diseases that could explain the myocardial injury (eg, myocarditis, pulmonary embolism, Takotsubo syndrome) and non-ischemic causes of myocardial injury (eg, severe renal failure, sepsis).

Exclusion criteria: (1) Incomplete follow-up data or loss to follow-up; (2) Death from non-cardiovascular causes during follow-up; (3) Comorbid non-cardiovascular diseases that could significantly affect prognosis, such as malignancy, end-stage renal disease, or severe liver disease; (4) Less than three valid lipid profile measurements during baseline and follow-up periods, precluding calculation of variability metrics.

Data Collection

Patient data were collected from the hospital electronic medical record system (EMRS) and the cardiology follow-up database. (1) Baseline data included demographic characteristics [age, sex, body mass index (BMI)], cardiovascular risk factors (smoking history, alcohol consumption history, history of hypertension, history of diabetes), clinical features at admission, and medication use (particularly statin therapy). (2) Lipid testing and LV data: All fasting lipid profile results for LDL-C, HDL-C, and Lp(a) were extracted from the date of initial MINOCA diagnosis until the occurrence of an endpoint event or the follow-up cutoff date (June 30, 2025). To ensure reliability of variability calculations, only patients with at least three valid lipid measurements were included. The median number of lipid measurements during follow-up was 4 (interquartile range [IQR]: 3–6), with no significant difference between the MACE and non-MACE groups. SD was expressed in the original units (mmol/L for LDL-C and HDL-C; mg/L for Lp(a)), while CV and VIM are unitless. Statin use during follow-up was recorded at each outpatient visit, and medication adherence was assessed through patient self-report and prescription refill records. All fasting venous blood samples were collected in the early morning and analyzed using a Hitachi 7600-020 fully automated biochemical analyzer. LDL-C was measured using a selective dissolution direct method (reagent kit: Ningbo Medical System Biotechnology, inter-assay coefficient of variation [CV] ≤ 3.0%); HDL-C was measured using a direct method (reagent kit: Ningbo Medical System Biotechnology, inter-assay CV ≤ 2.8%); and Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] was measured using an immunoturbidimetric assay (reagent kit: Desai Diagnostic Systems, inter-assay CV ≤ 4.5%). All assays were calibrated daily using matched calibrators, and the laboratory regularly participated in the external quality assessment program of the National Center for Clinical Laboratories (NCCL) of the Ministry of Health, with quality control results consistently falling within the acceptable range. The detection ranges for LDL-C, HDL-C, and Lp(a) were 0.08–20.0 mmol/L, 0.08–5.0 mmol/L, and 30–1000 mg/L, respectively. The lipid results included in this study were derived from real-world clinical follow-up testing, and pre-analytical variations were strictly controlled according to the standard operating procedures of our institution. LV was quantified using the following metrics: SD reflecting the absolute magnitude of fluctuation; CV calculated as CV = (SD/mean) × 100% to assess relative fluctuation scaled to the mean level; VIM calculated as VIM = (SD/meank), where the exponent k was derived by fitting the regression equation ln(SD) = a + k × ln(mean), providing a more robust measure of relative variability.

Follow-Up and Endpoint Definition

All enrolled MINOCA patients underwent regular follow-up from the date of initial diagnosis until the prespecified cutoff date of June 30, 2025. Follow-up was conducted through systematic review of the hospital EMRS and cardiology outpatient clinic notes and telephone interviews to ensure completeness and accuracy of endpoint information.

The primary endpoint was the occurrence of MACEs during the follow-up period. MACE was a pre-defined composite endpoint comprising any of the following events: cardiac death, hospitalization for new-onset or worsening HF, non-fatal recurrent MI, and ischemic stroke, or re-hospitalization for unstable angina or worsening anginal symptoms. To minimize ascertainment bias for the softer endpoint of re-hospitalization for unstable angina, predefined strict diagnostic criteria (typical chest pain plus dynamic ECG changes or positive troponin) were applied and adjudicated by the independent endpoint committee.

To accurately adjudicate endpoint events, clear confirmation standards and procedures were established. Cardiac death required a death certificate or family-confirmed cause of death. Hospitalization for HF required detailed hospitalization records including a definitive diagnosis, relevant clinical manifestations, and records of targeted therapy. Diagnosis of non-fatal recurrent MI was based on a comprehensive review of admission notes, progress logs, dynamic electrocardiographic changes, and serial cardiac biomarker (primarily troponin) levels. Ischemic stroke required supporting documentation from neurology consultation notes, hospitalization records, and head CT/MRI reports. Re-hospitalization for angina necessitated corresponding hospitalization records detailing anginal symptoms and relevant pharmacological treatment or evaluation. To minimize adjudication bias, an independent Endpoint Adjudication Committee was established, consisting of two senior cardiologists not involved in data collection or group assignment. All source documents (medical records, laboratory reports, imaging reports, etc.) related to potential endpoint events were anonymized regarding patient group allocation before being reviewed by the committee. The two experts independently adjudicated each event based on the aforementioned criteria. Agreement in the initial adjudication confirmed the event. In case of disagreement, a third senior cardiologist was consulted for final arbitration.12

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 and IBM SPSS Statistics 27 software. Continuous variables conforming to a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using the independent samples *t*-test. Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables were presented as number (percentage) and compared using the Chi-square (χ2) test or Fisher’s exact test (if the expected frequency was < 5). Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method, and differences between groups were compared using the Log rank test. The proportional hazards assumption for the Cox model was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and no significant violations were detected (all P > 0.05). Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were employed to analyze independent factors influencing the long-term prognosis of MINOCA patients. In the primary analysis, each lipid variability metric was entered into a separate Cox model along with clinically relevant covariates: age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol consumption, diabetes, hypertension, statin use during follow-up, and the baseline level of the corresponding lipid parameter. To test the robustness of the findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed by constructing a single Cox model that simultaneously included all lipid variability metrics and all clinical covariates (age, sex, BMI, smoking, alcohol, diabetes, hypertension, statin use, and baseline HDL-C, Lp(a), and LDL-C). Collinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), with VIF > 5 indicating significant multicollinearity. To evaluate the long-term dynamic predictive value of LV metrics for MACEs, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted using R 4.3.2. A composite prediction score, constructed from the LV indicators identified as independent predictors in the Cox regression analysis, was used as the marker. Time-dependent ROC analysis was performed at three clinically meaningful time points during follow-up (2.5, 3.5, and 4.5 years). The inverse probability of censoring weighting method was used to handle censored data. The time-dependent area under the curve (AUC) and its 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated at each time point to quantify the discriminatory ability of this composite score for MACE risk at different time horizons. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Incidence of MACEs in MINOCA Patients During Follow-Up

Among the 254 MINOCA patients, 80 (31.50%) experienced MACEs during the median follow-up of 41 months. Cardiac death was the most frequent event (40 cases, accounting for 50.00% of MACEs and 15.75% of the total cohort), followed by non-fatal recurrent MI (12 cases, 15.00%), hospitalization for new-onset or worsening HF (10 cases, 12.50%), and ischemic stroke (10 cases, 12.50%). Re-hospitalization for unstable angina or worsening anginal symptoms occurred in 8 cases (10.00%) (Table 1).

In a sensitivity analysis with full adjustment for all clinical covariates and other lipid variability metrics simultaneously, HDL-C SD, HDL-C VIM, and Lp(a) VIM remained independent predictors (Supplementary Table S1). Discussion

Through a median-term follow-up of 254 MINOCA patients, this study is the first to systematically evaluate the associations of long-term variability (assessed via SD, CV, and VIM) in different lipid components including LDL-C, HDL-C, and Lp(a) with MACE risk. Our central finding is that, after adjusting for multiple potential confounders, variability in HDL-C (HDL-SD and HDL-VIM) and long-term variability in Lp(a) [Lp(a)-VIM] are independent and significant predictors of MACEs in MINOCA patients. This finding extends the focus of traditional lipid management from “level control” to “stability management”, offering a novel potential target for risk stratification and precise intervention in this unique population.

First, the most potent predictor identified in this study was the HDL-SD, with an HR as high as 3.751. Traditionally, HDL-C is regarded as “good cholesterol”, and its low level is a well-established cardiovascular risk factor.14,15 However, accumulating recent evidence suggests that the functional quality of HDL may be more critical than its absolute quantity.16 Our study provides novel confirmation from a different perspective: even “good cholesterol” can become a “bad signal” if its levels exhibit marked fluctuations over time. Such instability in HDL levels may reflect underlying systemic metabolic dysregulation, unstable inflammatory states, or fluctuating endothelial function, all of which are promoters of atherosclerosis progression and plaque instability.17 HDL particles are responsible for reverse cholesterol transport. Pronounced fluctuations in their levels might indicate an unstable efficiency of this crucial protective mechanism, potentially compromising the consistent and effective removal of cholesterol from the vascular wall, thereby elevating cardiovascular event risk.5

Second, Lp(a)-VIM was identified as another independent risk factor. Lp(a) itself, due to its pro-atherogenic and pro-thrombotic properties, is already recognized as an important residual risk factor.18–20 Our findings further reveal that long-term trend-like fluctuations in Lp(a) levels constitute a significant component of its risk profile. However, a key issue that must be acknowledged is that Lp(a) levels are largely genetically determined and remain relatively stable within individuals over time.21 Some researchers have therefore questioned whether its “variability” has any clinical significance. In response, we argue that although the heritability of Lp(a) is as high as 70–90%, several recent longitudinal cohort studies have demonstrated that Lp(a) levels still exhibit a certain degree of biological variation during long-term follow-up (intra-individual coefficient of variation approximately 8–15%). Such variation may be related to inflammatory status, fluctuations in renal function, intermittent use of lipid-lowering therapy (especially PCSK9 inhibitors), or changes in treatment adherence.21–26 Our sensitivity analysis further revealed that baseline Lp(a) was only borderline significant (P = 0.064) whereas Lp(a) VIM remained strongly significant (P = 0.005) after full adjustment. We speculate that long-term fluctuations in Lp(a) may indirectly reflect changes in the patient’s metabolic stability or treatment adherence, rather than being determined solely by genetic fixation. Of course, given the high heritability of Lp(a), the clinical utility of its variability still needs to be validated in prospective studies with more intensive sampling protocols and genetic correction. Compared to a single measurement, long-term variability might better reflect an individual’s dynamic risk status under the combined influence of genetic background, inflammatory burden, and metabolic stress. The mechanism may involve a wave-like attack of toxic and pro-inflammatory effects on the vascular endothelium by fluctuating Lp(a) levels, hindering endothelial repair processes and exacerbating plaque instability.

Of particular interest is the observation that while all LDL variability metrics and Lp(a)-SD showed significant associations with MACEs in univariate analysis, these associations lost their independence in the multivariate Cox regression. This result could be explained by two non‑mutually exclusive possibilities. First, the contribution of LDL and Lp(a) variability to MACE risk may not be partly mediated through, or interconnected with, more fundamental pathophysiological processes such as HDL variability or inflammatory pathways.27 For instance, marked LDL fluctuations might exacerbate oxidative stress, which concurrently impacts HDL function and stability.28 Therefore, when all LV metrics are considered simultaneously, HDL variability stands out as a stronger and more direct risk marker. This suggests that in clinical practice, monitoring HDL stability might hold higher priority value than monitoring LDL fluctuations. Furthermore, the non-significance of Lp(a)-CV in multivariate analysis, in contrast to the significance of its SD and VIM, may be because CV primarily reflects relative fluctuation around the mean between visits, while SD and VIM (especially VIM) are better at capturing long-term and trend-like changes. For an indicator like Lp(a), which has a relatively long half-life and is strongly influenced by genetics, its long-term trajectory may carry more pathological significance than its short-term relative variability.

Third, further clarification is needed regarding the MACE incidence and population characteristics in this study. The MACE incidence in our study was 31.5% (median follow-up of 41 months), which is higher than the ranges reported in some previous MINOCA studies (eg, the Lp(a) study reported a MACE rate of 9.5%–18.5% during a 41.7‑month follow-up29 while another study showed approximately 25%.30 This discrepancy may be attributable to the higher burden of cardiovascular risk factors in our patient population, such as the slightly higher proportions of diabetes, smoking, and alcohol consumption compared to other studies. To some extent, this reflects the characteristics of our center as a regional cardiovascular referral center that admits high‑risk transferred cases. However, it must be acknowledged that the characteristics of our population do not fully match those of the “typical Chinese population”, and the generalizability of our findings is therefore somewhat limited. Finally, this study employed time-dependent ROC curve analysis to assess the long-term predictive value of LV for MACEs in MINOCA patients. The results showed good predictive performance at intermediate-term follow-up (2.5 and 3.5 years), suggesting that dynamic lipid fluctuations may be an important factor influencing mid-term cardiovascular risk. However, its predictive value attenuated at a longer-term follow-up (4.5 years), indicating relatively limited discriminative ability for long-term risk. This finding implies that LV might be more suitable for intermediate-term risk assessment, while predicting longer-term prognosis may require integration with other stable or cumulative biomarkers or clinical features.

Limitations and Future Perspectives

This study still has several limitations. Firstly, as an observational study, despite multivariate adjustment, residual confounding from unmeasured factors (eg, variability in inflammatory markers, detailed lifestyle factors) cannot be entirely ruled out. Secondly, the single-center retrospective design with a limited sample size (80 MACEs) may reduce the power to detect weaker associations and affect the generalizability of the findings. The modest number of events also increases the risk of overfitting in the multivariate models, underscoring the need for validation in larger, multi‑center cohorts. Thirdly, the study focused only on LV magnitude without delving into differences in fluctuation patterns (eg, frequent small fluctuations vs occasional large fluctuations). Moreover, the relatively small number of lipid measurements (minimum three, median four) may not fully capture true long‑term fluctuation patterns; more frequent sampling over extended follow‑up would provide more robust estimates. Fourthly, the time‑dependent nature of LV assessment—where measurements were collected until MACE occurrence or end of follow‑up—may introduce bias because patients with shorter follow‑up had fewer measurements. Fifthly, the inclusion of re‑hospitalization for unstable angina as a soft endpoint may introduce ascertainment bias, despite our efforts to standardize adjudication. Finally, the study primarily reveals an association, and the underlying biological mechanisms (eg, how exactly lipid fluctuations affect plaque stability or endothelial function) remain insufficiently explored. Based on these limitations, future research could focus on the following directions: (1) Conducting studies to elucidate the mechanisms by which LV affects vascular cell function and plaque characteristics. (2) Performing multi-center, prospective cohort studies to validate the predictive value of LV and to optimize risk prediction models. (3) Exploring the effects of different lipid-lowering treatment regimens on stabilizing lipid levels and their association with clinical outcomes. (4) Conducting similar investigations in other patient populations, such as those with obstructive MI, to compare similarities and differences in the predictive value of LV. (5) Future studies with larger sample sizes should specifically examine whether the prognostic value of LV differs between men and women, given the known sex differences in cholesterol metabolism and MINOCA etiology.

Conclusion

This study systematically evaluates the association between LV and the long-term prognosis of MINOCA patients. The key finding is that variability in HDL-C (as measured by SD and VIM) and long-term variability in LP(a) (as measured by VIM) are independent risk factors for MACEs in MINOCA patients. These metrics demonstrate modest to moderate predictive value at intermediate-term follow-up (2.5–3.5 years). These results suggest that for this specific population, lipid management should extend beyond merely achieving target levels to also emphasize maintaining stability. Monitoring the long-term stability of HDL-C and Lp(a) may provide novel and important insights for identifying high-risk patients, optimizing risk stratification, and formulating individualized management strategies.
