Low-dose radiation exposure and health outcomes among healthcare workers: a multi-center prospective cohort study
Authors: Xiaobin Liu, Qian Su, Jing Zhang, Yan Chen, Fei Li, Caixia Xie, Li He, Shiyu Liu, Yu Lv
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe long-term effects of chronic low-dose radiation exposure remain a subject of debate, with studies suggesting a range of outcomes from negligible to significant health impacts.MethodsThis multi-center cohort study followed 210 newly hired healthcare workers in radiation-related occupations for 9 years, from January 2015 to December 2023. Health effects were evaluated using Repeated Measures Analysis of Variance (rMANOVA). Significant interactions were explored using Bonferroni’s post hoc analysis and Bayesian methods. Repeated-measures regression models and Restricted Cubic Splines (RCS) models were employed to further assess the statistical significance and strength of evidence. Sensitivity analyses were performed employing Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) to ensure robustness of the results.ResultsThe per-protocol set included 111 subjects after accounting for exclusions and losses to follow-up. The rMANOVA models identified subtle but statistically significant differences across multiple follow-up intervals for various health indices. Parameters with Bayes factors (BF10) greater than 100, indicating extremely strong evidence for the alternative hypothesis, included RBC, MCV, MCH, MCHC, PH, SG, TP, ALB, GLB, and A/G. Per 1-mSv increment in instantaneous Hp(10), MCV decreased by 5.96 fL (95%CI -8.92 to -3.00, P < 0.001), MCH by 2.31 pg (95%CI -3.54 to -1.08, P < 0.001), and urine PH by 1.02 units (95%CI -1.65 to -0.40, P = 0.001). Conversely, each additional 1 mSv of cumulative Hp(10) was associated with an increase of 8.22 fL in MCV (95%CI 6.54–9.91, P < 0.001) and 2.37 pg in MCH (95%CI 1.67–3.07, P < 0.001), but also with reductions in hemoglobin (-3.48 g/L, 95%CI -6.36 to -0.59, P = 0.018), plateletcrit (-0.022%, 95%CI -0.037 to -0.008, P = 0.002), large-platelet ratio (-8.40%, 95%CI -14.35 to -0.25, P = 0.006), total protein (-2.10 g/L, 95%CI -3.67 to -0.52, P = 0.009), and globulin (-1.56 g/L, 95%CI -2.80 to -0.31, P = 0.014), together with a rise in the albumin/globulin ratio (0.123, 95%CI 0.01–0.23, P = 0.031). RCS models after adjusting for age, gender and profession revealed statistically significant dose-response relationships between Ln-transformed Hp(10) and the health indices of RBC, MCHC, and A/G.ConclusionsThis pioneering longitudinal cohort study in western China demonstrates that chronic low-dose radiation exposure among healthcare workers can induce subtle yet significant health effects, underscoring the necessity of adhering to the ALARA principle for radiation exposure management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-025-01133-5.Keywords: Personal dose equivalent, Health effects, Healthcare radiation workers.

Introduction

Radiation exposure remains a critical concern in occupational health, particularly for healthcare radiation workers who are routinely exposed to low-dose radiation, such as radiologists and surgeons [1, 2]. Despite stringent safety protocols and protective measures, such as lead aprons and thyroid shields, the cumulative effect of low-dose radiation exposure over a career span raises concerns about potential health risks [3]. The long-term health effects of radiation exposure have been the subject of debate and research for many years [4]. While high doses of radiation are unequivocally linked to a range of adverse health outcomes, including cancer and acute radiation syndrome, the impact of chronic low-dose exposure is less clear [5, 6]. This ambiguity poses a significant challenge for the development of safety standards and protective measures for radiation workers.

Historically, the linear no-threshold hypothesis (LNTH) has traditionally been used to estimate the risks of low-dose radiation exposure, positing that any amount of radiation, no matter how small, could increase the risk of cancer and other diseases [7]. However, this hypothesis was not without controversy, as some studies suggested that low-dose radiation exposure may have minimal or even beneficial effects, known as hormesis, where low doses could stimulate protective biological responses [8, 9]. This controversy underscores the importance of long-term, prospective cohort studies in providing empirical evidence to inform safety standards and health policies for radiation workers. Notably, the National Academy of Sciences (NAS) identified as one of the top ten research needs “Occupational Radiation Studies for the Future”, which should include investigations designed to evaluate the impacts of prolonged, low-dose radiation exposure on individuals [10].

The health effects of chronic low-dose radiation are multifaceted and can include a range of outcomes from subclinical changes in blood parameters to the development of malignancies and cardiovascular diseases [11, 12]. Previous studies have primarily focused on deterministic effects, such as cancer incidence, leaving a gap in our understanding of the broader health impacts [13, 14]. According to the UNSCEAR’s (the United Nations Scientific Committee on the Effects of Ionizing Radiations) stance, it strongly advises a shift towards investigating a wider range of ailments not limited to cardiovascular conditions, advocating for studies designed to probe clinical and sub-phenotypic outcomes, alongside biological markers, given their likelihood to provide crucial insights [15]. Additionally, most of these studies have been retrospective or cross-sectional, which are limited by recall bias and the inability to establish causality [16, 17]. The present study aims to address these limitations by adopting a longitudinal cohort design, following healthcare radiation workers across multiple centers over a nine-year period to systematically assess a wide spectrum of the health risks associated with chronic low-dose radiation exposure.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

The present cohort study, conducted from January 2015 to December 2023 and following the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, [18] was implemented across three centers: Qingyang Hospital, Caotang Hospital, and Chengdong Hospital, all affiliated with the Sichuan Academy of Medical Sciences (Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital). Initial cohort consisting of medical personnel newly hired in radiology-related professions since January 2015 through December 2017, underwent baseline physical examinations before assuming their duties. The participants identified as unsuitable for work involving radiation exposure during the pre-employment physical examinations were excluded, according to the occupational health standards of the People’s Republic of China (PRC), specifically the “Health requirements and surveillance specifications for radiation workers”. [19] The exclusion criteria encompassed several conditions: severe visual or auditory impairments; serious and recurrent diseases causing partial loss of work capacity, such as severe disorders of the hematopoietic system, malignant tumors, chronic cardiopulmonary diseases resulting in a significant decline in cardiopulmonary function, uncontrolled epilepsy, and severe skin diseases at the site of exposure; as well as radiation related illnesses that were not completely recovered. Additionally, underscoring the study’s alignment with mandatory legal requirements, [20–22] the medical staff engaged in radiological work have signed an “Occupational Hazard Notification” as part of their employment contract with the hospital.

Cohort follow-up and quality control

The participants enrolled in the initial cohort adhered to a biennial follow-up regimen from their inception. By December 2023, they were mandatorily scheduled for four consecutive assessments, including pre-employment physical examinations. Assessment of personal external radiation carried out by Sichuan Lanruixin Health Testing Technology Service Co., Ltd., along with regular radiation personnel physical examinations conducted by West China School of Public Health and West China Fourth Hospital, together composed the major components of the follow-up procedure. Both the Department of Ecology and Environment of Sichuan Province and the Sichuan Province Health and Family Planning Supervision and Law Enforcement Corps provided technical guidance and actively participated in on-site quality control, independently organizing annual radiation worker training initiatives and conducting effectiveness assessments.

Data availability

The datasets relevant to this study are now available upon reasonable request. Appropriate permission has been obtained from the Sichuan Academy of Medical Sciences, Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital ethics committee. All data and codes that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Measurement of personal dose equivalent

The dosimetry for radiation exposure assessment was based on routine survey data required for compliance with occupational radiation exposure regulations. The survey frequency was set at once per quarter, following the principle that the observation period should not exceed 90 days. The personal dosimeters for all subjects were replaced during the first week of the second quarter, following the end of each quarter. As in previous studies [23, 24], Hp(10) was adopted as the benchmark for personal external radiation exposure, representing the dose at a depth of 10 mm below the skin surface. This quantity is used to estimate the personal equivalent dose, which is relevant for assessing effective dose under specific exposure conditions, and is typically measured in microsieverts. Measurement of health effects

To capture both the early, potentially reversible perturbations (e.g., oxidative-stress-mediated shifts in blood-cell kinetics or renal handling) and the sub-clinical harbingers of later chronic disease that accrue with cumulative dose, we deliberately selected a panel of routine but sensitive indicators: vital signs for immediate autonomic response, complete blood counts for hematopoietic turnover, urinalysis for proximal tubular function, hepatic/renal chemistries for metabolic and excretory equilibrium, and chromosome aberrations as a direct, integration biomarker of cumulative genotoxic burden at low-dose rates. The main explanations of all health effects were presented in etable1.

Three fasting venous blood (approximately 3 mL), and a single fasting morning urine specimen (approximately 10 mL) were collected from each subject. Urine specimens underwent urinalysis assessment by using Automated Urine Analysis System (US1680P, Roche Diagnostics, USA). One blood sample was measured for blood cell analysis using the Automatic Blood Analysis System (CAL8000-2.0, Mindray, PRC), another was measured for hepatic and renal function using the Automated Biochemical Analysis (ADVIA2400, Siemens, Germany), and the last sample was measured for unstable chromosomal aberrations (UCAs) indices and micronucleus test (MNT) in peripheral blood lymphocytes using an optical microscope (CX23, OLYMPUS, Japan).

Sensitivity analysis

In adherence to the intent-to-treat philosophy, [26, 27] we conducted both a per-protocol set (PPS) and a full-analysis set (FAS) analysis simultaneously. The PPS comprised only participants who completed all four scheduled examinations without protocol deviations, whereas the FAS included every worker ever enrolled regardless of missed visits or dropout. By cross-validating these two datasets we not only strengthen the reliability of the findings but, crucially, quantify the extent to which loss to follow-up could have biased the estimated dose-response relationships.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R software (Version 4.2.2) under RStudio (Version 2023.12.1 + 402). Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation for normally distributed continuous variables, and as median [Inter Quartile Range, (IQR)] for those with skewed distribution, and as frequency [corresponding proportion (%)] for categorical variables, respectively. Wilcoxon rank sum test, Student’s-T test and χ2 test were used to analyze differences in demographic characteristics according to the data property.

Health effects were evaluated with three complementary longitudinal approaches: (i) repeated-measures ANOVA (rMANOVA) applied to the per-protocol set, (ii) Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) used as a sensitivity analysis on the full-analysis set, and (iii) repeated-measures regression models that incorporated the annual follow-up structure (T0→T1→T2→T3, 1-year intervals) to estimate both instantaneous and cumulative dose-response relationships while controlling for within-subject correlation. The cumulative Hp(10) for each participant was calculated as the sum of all quarterly dosimeter readings up to the time of each follow-up examination. Significant interactions were examined using Bonferroni’s post hoc analysis [28]. To further evaluate the statistical significance and the strength of the evidence, Bayesian methods were additionally employed. The likelihood of the data under the alternative hypothesis compared to the null hypothesis was quantified using Bayes factors (BF10). These Bayes factors were interpreted in accordance with the criteria proposed by Wagenmakers et al.,29 where a BF10<1 suggests evidence in favor of the null hypothesis, and values of 1 ≤ BF10<3 are considered anecdotal, 3 ≤ BF10<10 indicate moderate evidence, 10 ≤ BF10<30 suggest strong evidence, 30 ≤ BF10<100 imply very strong evidence, and BF10 ≥ 100 is indicative of extremely strong evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis. Correlations with health effects were estimated using Pearson correlation for Hp(10) to assess linear associations. Restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to explore potential non-linear associations, while controlling for age, gender and profession. Log transformations were applied to account for skewed distributions prior to regression modeling. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance level of 0.05.

Results

The initial cohort identified 210 newly hired healthcare radiation workers from 2015 to 2017. We excluded 9 volunteers who were deemed unsuitable for radiation work during pre-employment physical examinations. 45, 19, and 26 volunteers were lost in the subsequent 3 follow-ups, respectively, leaving 111 radiation workers ultimately completed all surveys for analysis. The detailed reasons for the loss of follow-up were listed in Fig. 1. Age, gender, and profession type showed no statistical difference initially versus ultimately; however, there was a significant difference in departmental distribution (χ2 = 59.917, P < 0.001). In light of the hospital discontinuing 125I programs over the 9-year follow-up period, a subset from the initiating cohort, particularly those in departments of Hepato-Bilious Surgery, Organ Transplant and Thoracic Surgery was removed due to these program alterations (Table 1).

All unstable chromosomal aberrations (UCAs) indices and micronucleus test (MCN) were 0.00 (IQR 0.00). For statistical rigor and interpretability, certain indicators were omitted from subsequent analyses given their limited analytical relevance. Instead, significant disparities emerged across multiple follow-up intervals in the remaining parameters. The results from the Bayesian methods were largely consistent with those from Bonferroni’s post hoc analysis. Specifically, parameters with Bayes factors (BF10) greater than 100, indicating extremely strong evidence for the alternative hypothesis, included RBC, MCV, MCH, MCHC, PH, SG, TP, ALB, GLB, and A/G. BF10 between 3 and 10, suggesting moderate evidence, included WBC. BF10 between 1 and 3, indicating anecdotal evidence, included BASO, BASO rate, PLT, and PCT. The remaining parameters had BF10 less than 1, favoring the null hypothesis (eTable 2). Age, gender, and profession have a significant impact on a variety of indices. For instance, the Bayes factor (BF10) for gender differences in DBP, SBP, MONO, RBC, HGB, HCT, MCHC, LPLT, LEU_sediment, LGB, A/G, and CR all exceed 100, suggesting extremely strong evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis, as shown in eTable 2.

Repeated-measures regression analyses of the FAS trajectories showed that only a subset of hematological parameters were significantly associated with either instantaneous or cumulative Hp(10) dose (Table 3). Per 1-mSv increment in instantaneous Hp(10), MCV decreased by 5.96 fL (95%CI −8.92 to −3.00, P < 0.001), MCH by 2.31 pg (95%CI −3.54 to −1.08, P < 0.001), and urine PH by 1.02 units (95%CI −1.65 to −0.40, P = 0.001). Conversely, each additional 1 mSv of cumulative Hp(10) was associated with an increase of 8.22 fL in MCV (95%CI 6.54–9.91, P < 0.001) and 2.37 pg in MCH (95%CI 1.67–3.07, P < 0.001), but also with reductions in hemoglobin (−3.48 g/L, 95%CI −6.36 to −0.59, P = 0.018), plateletcrit (−0.022%, 95%CI −0.037 to −0.008, P = 0.002), large-platelet ratio (−8.40%, 95%CI −14.35 to −0.25, P = 0.006), total protein (−2.10 g/L, 95%CI −3.67 to −0.52, P = 0.009), and globulin (−1.56 g/L, 95%CI −2.80 to −0.31, P = 0.014), together with a rise in the albumin/globulin ratio (0.123, 95%CI 0.01–0.23, P = 0.031). Cumulative dose was additionally inversely related to total white blood-cell count (β=−0.637 × 10⁹/L, 95%CI −1.13 to −0.14, P = 0.012). No statistically significant associations were detected for blood pressure, heart rate, hepatic or renal indices, or remaining urinalysis parameters (Table 2).

Discussion

This is the largest multiple-center longitudinal cohort study in western China examining the health effects of chronic low-dose radiation exposure among healthcare radiation workers over a nine-year period. The findings from the present study provide valuable insights into the ongoing debate surrounding the potential stochastic effects of low-dose radiation on human health, encompass a broad spectrum of health outcomes from blood parameters to unstable chromosomal aberrations, and offer a comprehensive assessment that moves beyond the traditional focus on deterministic effects alone. The meticulous 9-year follow-up in this multi-center study maximized comparability, ensuring that our final estimates of the health effects were unbiased across the cohort, while using both FAS and PPS for analysis made the conclusion more credible.

The statistical associations observed between Ln-transformed Hp(10) and specific health examination indices, such as red blood cell, mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, and Albumin/Globulin, suggest that even low levels of radiation exposure may have subtle, yet measurable, impacts on health. The present study extended this observation by showing that the direction of effect depends on the temporal architecture of exposure: instantaneous spikes acutely reduce erythrocyte size indices, whereas the same dose accumulated over years produces larger but fewer cells together with lower leukocyte and platelet counts. This dual behaviour indicated that dose-rate and dose-fractionation must be modelled simultaneously before any biological inference is drawn. The mirror-image trajectories documented in regression analyses—acute shrinking MCV while cumulative exposure enlarges it—mirror experimental data in which rapid cell-cycle arrest is followed by clonal selection of radioresistant precursors, emphasising that low-dose biology is governed by competing time-dependent processes rather than a single linear slope. Many indicators of hemogram, urinalysis, and hepatic and renal function show subtle but statistically significant fluctuations within the normal range, with some increasing and others decreasing. Our results highlighted the disorder of this association, which may be the reason for the completely opposite conclusions in previous studies [30, 31]. This suggests that individual susceptibility to radiation might vary greatly, influenced not only by cumulative doses but also by other factors like genetic predisposition, overall health status, or even timing of exposure. The fact that these changes occur at relatively lower levels could imply that subclinical damage accumulates over time, contributing to potential long-term health consequences. Despite the potential for red blood cell or hemoglobin levels to become abnormal in radiation workers over time, these findings from hemogram tests often take a back seat to lymphocyte results in health monitoring protocols. This oversight is notable given that chronic exposure to low-dose ionizing radiation could significantly affect not only RBC levels and hemoglobin but also the concentrations of 2,3-diphosphoglycerate (2,3-DPG) and glutathione (GSH) in these workers [32]. The importance of RBCs to human health cannot be understated, making their regular monitoring in radiation workers a critical component of health surveillance programs. The fact that cumulative Hp(10) predicts a gradual hemoglobin decline even while individual cell volume rises implies an ongoing loss of total erythrocyte mass, a finding that would be missed if surveillance protocols focused only on lymphocyte counts. However, the precise impact of these hematological changes on the overall health of radiation workers is not yet fully understood and warrants further investigation. The present study has observed that low-dose radiation exerts an inhibitory effect on red blood cells up to a certain threshold, specifically at HP(10) 0.135 mSv (e−2 mSv), beyond which an adaptive response is triggered. Concurrently, there is a noted decrease in mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration. While low-dose radiation is capable of activating certain protective mechanisms within the cells, it may also induce adverse effects [33]. This intricate balance between cellular response and damage underscores the need for further research to elucidate the comprehensive effects of low-dose radiation on red blood cell health.

Instantaneous doses transiently impair cellular maturation, whereas cumulative doses re-shape the stem-cell repertoire and shift the haematopoietic set-point. An acute spike appears to acutely perturb the bone-marrow micro-environment: transient cell-cycle arrest in erythroblasts and an immediate release of smaller, prematurely truncated reticulocytes could explain the sudden drop in mean corpuscular volume and hemoglobin content. The absence of a parallel drop in total red-cell count at the same time-point suggests that the marrow still succeeds in maintaining numeric output, but at the cost of cellular quality, an observation reminiscent of experimental models in which a single high-LET exposure produces a wave of micronucleated normoblasts without yet depleting the committed progenitor pool [34]. When the same increment of dose is accumulated slowly over years, however, the marrow faces a qualitatively different challenge: chronic oxidative stress, repetitive endothelial injury and repeated selection of clones with more robust antioxidant capacity. The eventual emergence of larger, hemoglobin-rich cells together with lower overall erythrocyte counts is compatible with a Darwinian selection process in which precursors that survive low-grade genotoxic stress are characterised by delayed maturation, extra cell divisions and ultimately larger cell size, a phenotype previously described in residents of high-background-radiation areas [35]. The platelet compartment deserves special emphasis. While classical radiopathology focuses on thrombocytopenia after high doses, our results reveal that chronic low-rate exposure reduces the proportion of young, large platelets long before numeric thrombocytopenia becomes manifest. Such qualitative platelet ageing has been linked to endothelial dysfunction and accelerated vascular inflammation in non-radiation settings.

Although proteins are relatively less sensitive to radiation, low-dose radiation may not significantly affect the spatial structure of proteins, it can indirectly impact protein function through mechanisms such as oxidative stress [36]. Our results suggest that the dose-response relationship between low-dose radiation and the A/G ratio observed below HP(10) 0.135 mSv may be attributed to the decrease in globulin levels following irradiation. The present study shows that the fall in globulin is not an isolated artefact; total protein moves in parallel, suggesting a genuine reduction in hepatic synthetic capacity or chronic low-grade protein loss rather than a simple redistribution between albumin and globulin fractions.

While some studies have reported negligible or even hormetic effects of low-dose radiation, [37] others have identified potential risks, including increased incidence of cardiovascular diseases and subtle changes in blood parameters [38, 39]. The lack of significant differences in dose-response relationships for monocyte rate and mean corpuscular volume, alongside the observed increase in SBP with higher levels of Ln-transformed Hp(10), points to the need for a nuanced understanding of radiation’s health impacts. The small but statistically significant effect of radiation on the longitudinal trends of BP is compatible with the degenerative impact of radiation on blood vessels [40]. Radiation-induced vascular injury, widely recognized as a significant contributor to radiation-therapy complications, often presents as atherosclerotic changes in major vessels [41, 42]. These results suggest that cardiovascular system may be more sensitive to radiation exposure than others, highlighting the importance of a multifaceted approach to health monitoring for medical personnel exposed to radiation.

The observed health impacts, albeit subtle, underscore the importance of adhering to the principle of “as low as reasonably achievable” (ALARA) in managing radiation exposure. Healthcare workers can’t fear life-saving radiation, but must be afraid of radiation unawareness and negligence [43]. Furthermore, the results highlight the need for comprehensive health monitoring programs that include a range of health outcomes, beyond deterministic effects, to adequately assess the risks associated with chronic low-dose radiation exposure. In light of these findings, occupational health policies may need to be revisited to ensure that they adequately protect workers from the potential health risks of low-dose radiation. This may include the implementation of more stringent exposure limits, the adoption of advanced protective technologies, and the provision of regular health screenings that specifically target the health outcomes identified as sensitive to radiation exposure. Notably, in contrast to international practices, China’s radiation safety programs are meticulously supervised through a dual administration, the Department of Ecology and Environment and the Health and Family Planning Supervision and Law Enforcement Corps, ensuring meticulous oversight for maximum effectiveness [44, 45].

Chromosome aberration and micronucleus analyses in peripheral blood lymphocytes are common methods for assessing radiation damage in workers. Typically, these measurements fall within normal range [46]. However, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) technology, with its high-resolution DNA probes, offers more precise detection and identification of specific chromosomal abnormalities, showing advantages in low-dose radiation scenarios due to its high resolution, sensitivity, and data comparability [47]. According to national standards, the detection of unstable chromosome aberrations in peripheral blood lymphocytes is the standard method for health checks of radiation workers in China [23]. This method meets the sensitivity requirements for routine monitoring of these workers, and thus, we have adopted it in our study to comply with national regulations and legal standards.

This study adds significant value by being the largest multiple-center longitudinal cohort study in western China, providing a comprehensive assessment of the health effects of chronic low-dose radiation exposure over a 9-year period. The study goes beyond the traditional focus on deterministic effects and addresses limitations of previous studies by adopting a longitudinal cohort design, which allows for a more accurate assessment of the health risks associated with chronic low-dose radiation exposure. While this study provides important insights, several limitations should be considered. Firstly, the current study focuses on the design of epidemiological and statistical methods for repeated measurements, overlooking some biological markers that could potentially link low-dose radiation exposure to changes in routine health examination markers. For instance, several biodosimetry tools are currently in development that utilize cytogenetics, proteomics, and genomics endpoints [48]. Secondly, the loss of participants over the nine-year follow-up period—coupled with the stringent pre-employment medical screening mandated by Chinese occupational-health law (which legally excludes applicants with hematologic, cardiopulmonary, or other chronic conditions from radiation work)—may have introduced bias and further restricted the baseline health range of the cohort, potentially limiting the generalizability of our findings to populations without such rigorous health selection. Thirdly, the study did not collect information on occupational or medical radiation received before enrolment. Consequently, cumulative dose could be underestimated for workers who had prior interventional, industrial or radiological employment. Nevertheless, the cohort underwent multiple measurements over a 9-year period; this long within-person series should attenuate baseline-exposure misclassification and permits assessment of dose–response slopes based on increments acquired after T0. Additionally, the reliance on Hp(10) as a measure of radiation exposure does not account for internal exposure or variations in individual sensitivity to radiation. Some well-established confounding factors in previous studies, such as smoking, [49] were not fully considered. Furthermore, while the participating centers were not designated coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) hospitals, the potential influence of undetected subclinical SARS-CoV-2 infections or other pandemic-related systemic stressors during the 2020–2023 period on the studied health parameters cannot be entirely ruled out. However, the consistent, dose-dependent trajectories of key outcomes observed throughout the entire study period, beginning years before the pandemic, suggest that our core findings are robust to this potential confounding. Future research should aim to address these limitations by incorporating more comprehensive measures of radiation exposure, including internal dosimetry, pathophysiological outcomes, and biological markers of radiation effect. Longitudinal studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods are also needed to further elucidate the long-term health effects of chronic low-dose radiation exposure. Finally, the present study exploring the mechanisms underlying the observed health impacts could provide valuable insights into how low-dose radiation influences human health, informing the development of targeted interventions to mitigate these effects.

Conclusions

This is the largest multiple-center longitudinal cohort study in western China, shedding light on the health effects of chronic low-dose radiation exposure among healthcare radiation workers, finding that even low levels of radiation exposure can have subtle but measurable impacts on health, emphasizing the importance of adhering to ALARA principle in managing radiation exposure. In particular, the present study underscores the need for comprehensive health monitoring programs that assess a broad range of health outcomes, including but not limited to deterministic effects. This could lead to a reevaluation of occupational health policies to more effectively safeguard workers from the potential health risks associated with chronic exposure to low-dose radiation.
