Effects of Bio-Slurry and Chemical Fertiliser Application on Soil Physicochemical Properties and Selected Heavy Metal Concentrations in Maize and Brassica Vegetables in Malawi
Authors: Grace Thole Katola, Ephraim Vunain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluated the effects of bio-slurry and chemical fertiliser application, relative to an unamended control, on soil physicochemical properties and the concentrations of selected heavy metals (Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, Pb, Cd, Cr, and Hg) in soil and in maize (Zea mays L.), rape (Brassica napus), and Chinese cabbage (Brassica pekinensis) grown in Zomba, Malawi. A randomised complete block design with three treatments (100% bio-slurry, 100% chemical fertiliser, and an unamended control) and three replications was used. Soil samples were collected before planting and after harvest, and soil, bio-slurry, and crop samples were analysed for heavy metals by atomic absorption spectrometry. Bio-slurry significantly increased soil organic carbon, organic matter, and exchangeable bases (Ca, Na, Mg, K) relative to chemical fertiliser and the control, whereas chemical fertiliser and the control were both associated with a significant decline in soil pH. All measured heavy metal concentrations in soil and crops remained below FAO/WHO permissible limits, with iron (Fe) the most abundant and cadmium (Cd) the least abundant metal overall. However, the bio-slurry itself exceeded CODEX microbiological limits for mesophilic aerobic bacteria, moulds, and coliforms, indicating a microbiological food-safety concern that requires further treatment or hygienic handling before field application. The findings indicate that bio-slurry improves soil fertility more effectively than chemical fertiliser without increasing heavy-metal risk in the crops tested, but that its microbiological quality must be addressed before it can be recommended as an unqualified safe soil amendment in Malawi.Keywords: anaerobic digestate, atomic absorption spectrometry, soil amendment, microbial contamination, Zomba, Malawi

1. Introduction

Soils are a fundamental component of the Earth’s natural capital and underpin terrestrial food systems by supporting the production of food, feed, fiber, and fuel.1,2 Soil health, defined as the capacity of soil to function as a dynamic living system, is essential for sustaining plant productivity, regulating nutrient cycling, and supporting biological activity and ecosystem services.3,4 Healthy soils contribute to water regulation, carbon sequestration, suppression of pathogens, detoxification of harmful substances, and efficient recycling of nutrients and energy, thereby ensuring long-term agricultural productivity and environmental sustainability.4,5 Key chemical properties such as soil pH, cation exchange capacity, and nutrient availability are widely used indicators of soil health, as they determine nutrient supply and the soil’s ability to immobilize toxic elements. 6

Maize (Zea mays L.) is one of the most important cereal crops globally and serves as a staple food for millions of people in Africa, Latin America, and Asia, contributing substantially to caloric intake in many developing countries. 7 In Malawi, maize is the dominant staple crop, accounting for approximately 60% of daily caloric intake and nearly 80% of cultivated land.8,9 Vegetables belonging to the Brassica genus, including rape (Brassica napus) and Chinese cabbage (Brassica pekinensis), are among the most widely cultivated leafy vegetables worldwide and are valued for their high nutritional content, including vitamins, minerals, and health-promoting phytochemicals.10,11 In Malawi, these crops are important sources of household nutrition and income for smallholder farmers. 12

Despite their importance, the productivity of maize and Brassica vegetables is increasingly constrained by soil degradation. Globally, soil degradation driven by nutrient depletion, loss of organic matter, contamination, and accumulation of toxic elements threatens the sustainability of agricultural systems. 13 In Malawi, highly weathered soils with low organic matter and nutrient deficiencies have resulted in declining crop yields and heightened food insecurity. 14 Smallholder farming systems are particularly vulnerable due to reliance on rain-fed agriculture, limited fertiliser use, and low-input management practices. 15

To counter declining soil fertility, farmers rely on mineral fertilisers and organic amendments such as manure, compost, and biosolids. 16 Chemical fertilisers provide readily available nutrients, particularly nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, and are effective in improving crop yields.17,18 However, prolonged and excessive application can adversely affect soil structure, reduce organic matter content, disrupt biological activity, and contribute to environmental pollution.19,20 In addition, many inorganic fertilisers contain trace heavy metals that may accumulate in soils and crops, posing risks to food safety and human health due to their persistence and bioaccumulation potential.21-23

Organic fertilisers derived from biological materials improve soil organic matter, nutrient retention, microbial activity, and cation exchange capacity, thereby enhancing long-term soil fertility and structure.2,16,20 Among these, bio-slurry is a nutrient-rich by-product of anaerobic digestion during biogas production and has gained increasing attention as a sustainable alternative to chemical fertilisers. 24 Bio-slurry supplies essential macro- and micronutrients, improves soil water-holding capacity, and enhances nutrient retention, often reducing the need for chemical fertilisers and improving crop yields.25,26 When derived from wastewater treatment systems, bio-slurry can also provide trace elements necessary for plant growth; however, its use requires careful evaluation due to potential heavy metal content.

The excessive use of chemical fertilisers has raised significant environmental and food safety concerns, including soil degradation, nutrient imbalances, and potential contamination of crops with heavy metals.27,28 As a result, there is an increasing need for sustainable fertiliser management strategies that can enhance soil fertility while minimizing environmental risks. Bio-slurry, a nutrient-rich byproduct of anaerobic digestion, has been identified as a cost-effective and environmentally friendly soil amendment capable of improving soil health, reducing reliance on chemical inputs, and promoting sustainable agricultural production. 29 Despite these potential benefits, the adoption of bio-slurry in Malawi remains limited. This is largely due to insufficient locally generated empirical evidence comparing its effectiveness with conventional chemical fertilisers, particularly in relation to soil physicochemical properties and heavy metal accumulation in key food and horticultural crops. Agriculture plays a central role in Malawi’s economy and food security; however, declining soil fertility and rising fertiliser costs continue to constrain agricultural productivity and threaten sustainable crop production.8,30 Therefore, there is a critical need to evaluate the effects of bio-slurry relative to chemical fertilisers under local conditions to inform sustainable soil fertility management practices and ensure food safety. Evaluating the effects of bio-slurry relative to chemical fertilisers is essential for developing sustainable soil fertility management practices that enhance productivity while safeguarding environmental and human health. This study provides empirical evidence on the impacts of bio-slurry and chemical fertiliser applications on soil physicochemical properties and food safety in key crops, including maize (Zea mays L.), rape (Brassica napus), and Chinese cabbage (Brassica pekinensis), under Malawian conditions. 2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Description of the Study Area

The experiment was conducted at Kalimbuka, Traditional Authority Mwambo, in Zomba District, southern Malawi, approximately 284.3 km south of Lilongwe. 31 The study site is located at 15.4001° S latitude and 35.3060° E longitude, at an altitude ranging from 700 to 1000 m above sea. 32 The area experiences a unimodal rainfall pattern, with the cropping season extending from November to April. Mean annual rainfall is approximately 2000 mm, making the area one of the wettest regions in Malawi. 33 During the experimental period, mean monthly rainfall was 186.8 mm, with the highest rainfall recorded in January (283 mm) and the lowest in April (36 mm). The recorded maximum and minimum temperatures were 30.5 °C and 16.7 °C, respectively. 34 This study area was selected for its relevance to smallholder agricultural systems in Malawi, where soil fertility decline and fertiliser access are major constraints on crop production.

2.2. Agricultural Practices and Soil Characteristics

Agriculture in the study area is predominantly rain-fed. Major crops cultivated include maize, sweet potatoes, rice, cassava, groundnuts, beans, and pigeon peas. Horticultural production is common in the region, with a focus on leafy vegetables such as rape and Chinese cabbage. Livelihood activities mainly involve crop production and livestock rearing, with poultry being the most common livestock enterprise. The study area is dominated by Ferralsols exhibiting a range of textures from sandy loam to clay.

2.3. Experimental Materials

The experimental materials included bio-slurry and chemical fertilisers (NPK and urea), maize seeds (SC 403 Kanyani variety), and two vegetable seed varieties. The selected crop varieties were chosen based on their adaptability to local soil and climatic conditions, market demand, and resistance to pests and diseases. Field and laboratory equipment used in the study included a soil auger, measuring tape, string, kitchen knife, panga knife, plastic bags, and a watering can. The bio-slurry used in this study was obtained from The Zingwangwa (Soche) Wastewater Treatment Facility in Blantyre, Malawi, which receives wastewater through the municipal sewerage network as well as fecal sludge and septage delivered by vacuum tankers from pit latrines and septic tanks. The treatment system operates under ambient tropical conditions that generally fall within the mesophilic temperature range (approximately 30–40 °C). The bio-slurry was collected on 10th December and was used 2 weeks after the day of collection.

2.4. Experimental Design, Treatments and Procedures

The experiment was laid out in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three treatments and three replications. This design was employed to control for variability within the experimental field and improve the precision of treatment comparisons. The treatments consisted of 100% bio-slurry, 100% chemical fertilizer, and a control with no soil amendments. Each experimental plot measured 10.5 m × 6.5 m, giving a total of 27 plots. Six rows were established per plot, with row spacing of 75 cm and plant spacing of 25 cm, while blocks were separated by 0.55 m. The total plant population across the experimental area was approximately 9,828 plants (68.25 m2 plot-1 ÷ 0.1875 m2 plant-1 × 27 plots.

Seedbeds were prepared manually using hoes by loosening the soil to a depth of 0–20 cm. The experiment comprised 27 plots, with nine plots allocated to each treatment (bio-slurry, chemical fertiliser, and control).

For maize plots receiving bio-slurry, 300 g of bio-slurry was applied per planting hole (10–15 cm depth) and covered with soil two weeks before planting. Planting holes were spaced 75 cm × 25–30 cm, and one maize seed was sown per hole. A second application of bio-slurry (300 g per plant) was carried out 21 days after planting. In vegetable plots amended with bio-slurry, the bio-slurry was evenly incorporated into the soil two weeks before transplanting. Seedlings were transplanted after eight days at 25 cm spacing and a second bio-slurry application was uniformly applied 21 days after transplanting (Table 1).

After harvest, post-experimental soil samples were collected from each of the 27 plots using a soil auger. All samples were air-dried under shade, stored in Ziplock bags, and transported to the University of Malawi (UNIMA) Chemistry Laboratory. In the laboratory, soil samples were ground, sieved through a 2.0 mm mesh, and analyzed for selected physicochemical properties, including soil pH, organic carbon (OC), organic matter (OM), and exchangeable bases (Ca, K, Na, and Mg), as well as selected heavy metals Cadmium (Cd), Mercury (Hg), Lead (Pb), Copper (Cu), Chromium (Cr), Iron (Fe), Manganese (Mn), and Zinc (Zn).

Soil pH was determined by mixing air-dried, sieved soil with distilled water at a 1:2 soil-to-water ratio, then shaking and measuring with a calibrated HI-991300N waterproof pH/EC/TDS/SAL/temperature meter (Hanna Instruments Limited), as described by Dhyan Singh. 35

Soil organic carbon was determined using the Walkley–Black wet oxidation method, and organic matter content was estimated using a conversion factor of 1.724. 37

Exchangeable calcium (Ca), magnesium (Mg), potassium (K), and sodium (Na) were extracted using 1.0 M ammonium acetate (NH4OAc) at pH 7, according to the method described by Walkley. 37 Ten grams of air-dried soil (2 mm sieved) were extracted with 100 mL of NH4OAc (1:10 ratio) and shaken for 30 minutes. The suspension was centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 minutes, and the supernatant was filtered to obtain a clear extract. The extracted cations were quantified using atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS). Quality control measures included procedural blanks, duplicates, and certified reference materials; extracts were stored at 4 °C until analysis to maintain sample integrity. The identical quality control method was used for the heavy-metal digests. Samples were digested and analyzed in triplicate, with procedural blanks incorporated into each analytical batch. Additionally, a certified reference material was analyzed alongside the samples, yielding recoveries between 94% and 98%. Calibration was performed using certified single-element standards, and instrument response was verified before analysis. The analytical sensitivity of the FAAS method was consistent with values reported in the literature, with typical limits of detection (LOD) of 0.08-0.11 mg L-1 for Pb, 0.01 mg L-1 for Cd, 0.021 mg L-1 for Zn, and 0.002-0.022 mg L-1 for Cr, 0.005-0.19 mg L-1 for Fe, 0.0015-0.1 mg L-1 for Mn and 0.0015-0.1 mg L-1 for Cu, while corresponding limits of quantification (LOQ) ranged from 0.16-2.0 mg L-1 for Pb, 0.02-0.30 mg L-1 for Cd, 0.005 mg L-1 for Zn, and 0.03-0.10 mg L-1 for Cr. These detection limits are well below the calibration ranges used in the analysis, confirming that the method provides sufficient sensitivity to quantify trace metal concentrations in soil samples.

Heavy metal concentrations (Fe, Zn, Cu, Pb, Cd, Cr, Hg, and Mn) in pre- and post-experimental soils were determined following Horwitz, 38 with slight modifications. Wet acid digestion using a nitric acid–perchloric acid (HNO3–HClO4) mixture was employed to ensure complete dissolution of soil-bound metals for accurate quantification. A 1.0000 g soil sample was digested using nitric acid and di-acid mixture under controlled heating conditions.

The digested samples were filtered, diluted to volume, and analysed using Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (AAS; AA240FS model, Agilent Technologies, Australia). All metals except mercury were determined by flame AAS using an air-acetylene flame, at the following analytical wavelengths: Fe 248.3 nm, Mn 279.5 nm, Zn 213.9 nm, Cu 324.8 nm, Pb 217.0 nm, Cd 228.8 nm, and Cr 357.9 nm. Mercury was determined by cold-vapour atomic absorption spectrometry (CVAAS) at 253.7 nm, following the reduction reagent/standard method, e.g., stannous chloride reduction. Calibration was performed using matrix-matched certified single-element standards, as described above for the cation analysis. All samples were analysed in triplicate alongside blanks to ensure analytical accuracy and reproducibility.

2.5.2. Bio-Slurry Sampling and Analysis

Microbiological activity in bio-slurry was assessed in accordance with ISO 21527 Part B 39 using the surface plating method. This method was chosen because it is a standardised and widely accepted approach for quantifying microbial populations in organic materials. The bio-slurry sample was serially diluted, and a 1 mL aliquot of the 10-6 dilution was spread onto agar plates in triplicate. Mesophilic aerobic bacteria were enumerated on Plate Count Agar (PCA), moulds on Dichloran Rose Bengal Chloramphenicol Agar (DRBC), and coliforms on Violet Red Bile Agar (VRBA). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 hours to allow colony development. Colony-forming units (CFU/ml) were then counted from plates containing 30–300 colonies to estimate microbial populations. This analysis was important for evaluating the biological quality and nutrient mineralisation potential of the bio-slurry.

2.5.3. Vegetable Sampling and Analysis

Vegetable samples were randomly collected from each treatment across all replications to ensure representativeness. Samples were washed, chopped, sun-dried, and transported to the laboratory. They were then ground, sieved (2.0 mm), and stored in desiccators prior to analysis to prevent contamination and moisture absorption.

Heavy metal concentrations (Fe, Zn, Cu, Pb, Cd, Cr, Hg, and Mn) in maize, Chinese cabbage, and rape were determined using wet acid digestion with a nitric acid–perchloric acid mixture, followed by quantification using Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (AAS), using the same instrumental wavelengths and CVAAS mercury method described in section 2.5.1, as described by Horwitz. 38 The analysis was conducted to evaluate the potential transfer and accumulation of heavy metals from soil amendments into edible crop parts, thereby assessing food safety risks.

2.5.4. Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Differences among treatments were considered statistically significant at p ≤ 0.05. Descriptive statistics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or as percentages, where appropriate. Independent t-tests and one-way ANOVA were used to assess differences in soil physicochemical properties and heavy metal concentrations before and after treatment, and among maize, rape, and Chinese cabbage samples. Heavy metal concentrations in soil, bio-slurry, and vegetables were compared with FAO/WHO 40 permissible limits.

3.2. Effects of Bio-Slurry and Chemical Fertiliser Application on Soil Physico-chemical Properties

3.2.1. Soil pH

Table 3 shows that the application of bio-slurry did not significantly increase soil pH in soils cultivated with maize, rape, and Chinese cabbage. This finding agrees with,36,42 who reported that bio-slurry has only a minimal and statistically insignificant effect on soil pH, likely due to the slow rate of mineralisation. Similarly, 43 observed only a moderate increase in soil pH following bio-slurry application.

The limited effect of bio-slurry on soil pH can be attributed to its high organic matter content, which acts as a buffering agent. Organic matter moderates pH fluctuations through the release and exchange of hydrogen ions, thereby stabilising soil chemical conditions. 44 As a result, bio-slurry may cause only slight changes in soil pH depending on existing soil conditions.

In contrast, a significant decrease in soil pH was observed in soils treated with chemical fertilisers and in the control. This finding agrees with,45,46 who reported that chemical fertiliser application leads to soil acidification. As noted by, 47 this effect is primarily due to nitrification of ammonium-based fertilisers, which releases hydrogen ions and increases soil acidity.

3.2.2. Soil Organic Carbon

Results in Table 3 show that bio-slurry application significantly increased organic carbon (OC), while chemical fertiliser significantly reduced OC. These differences were significant in soils cultivated with maize and Chinese cabbage, but no significant effect was observed in soils planted with rape (Brassica napus). The increase in OC under bio-slurry agrees with,48,49 who reported higher organic carbon levels in soils treated with bio-slurry compared to chemical fertilisers and control treatments. This improvement is attributed to the high organic matter content of bio-slurry, which enhances soil carbon through organic inputs and mineralisation processes. 50

In contrast, the decrease in OC under chemical fertiliser application agrees with, 51 who associated this effect with soil acidification and reduced enzyme activity. Similarly, 52 reported that chemical fertilisers alter soil microbial communities, thereby disrupting soil organic carbon dynamics and contributing to lower OC levels.

3.2.3. Soil Organic Matter

Table 3 shows a significant increase in organic matter (OM) in soils treated with bio-slurry compared to chemical fertiliser and control treatments. This finding agrees with, 53 who also reported higher OM levels under bio-slurry application. The increase is attributed to the carbon-rich nature of bio-slurry, which directly contributes organic matter to the soil. A notable rise in OM was observed in soils cultivated with maize and rape. Similarly, 54 highlighted the role of organic fertilisers in enhancing soil organic matter and overall soil health.

In contrast, chemical fertiliser application resulted in a significant reduction in OM, particularly in soils under maize cultivation. This finding agrees with, 55 who reported a decline in soil organic matter following NPK fertiliser application. The reduction is likely due to the effects of chemical fertilisers on soil nitrogen cycling and microbial activity, which can disrupt organic matter accumulation.

3.2.4. Exchangeable Bases (Ca, K, Mg, and Na)

The application of bio-slurry significantly increased exchangeable calcium (Ca), particularly in soils cultivated with maize and rape, while chemical fertiliser reduced Ca levels, especially under maize. This finding agrees with, 48 who reported higher Ca levels in bio-slurry-treated soils. The increase is attributed to the high calcium content of bio-slurry and its contribution to soil organic matter, which enhances cation exchange capacity and calcium retention.56,57

Similarly, bio-slurry significantly increased exchangeable potassium (K) in most soils, except those cultivated with Chinese cabbage. This agrees with 58 and, 43 who reported increased soil K following bio-slurry application. The increase is mainly due to the inherent potassium content of bio-slurry and interactions among exchangeable cations that favour K availability. 59

Exchangeable magnesium (Mg) and sodium (Na) also increased significantly in bio-slurry-treated soils, whereas reductions were observed under chemical fertiliser and control treatments. This finding agrees with, 48 who reported similar increases in Mg and Na following bio-slurry application.

3.3. Effects of Bio-Slurry and Chemical Fertiliser Application on Heavy Metals Concentration in Soils

3.3.1. Heavy Metal Concentration in Bio-Slurry

Results in Table 4 show that the mean concentrations of all analysed heavy metals before field application were below the established maximum permissible limits (MPL), with Pb and Hg not detected. Although cadmium (1.03 mg/kg) and chromium (10.35 mg/kg) were present, their levels remained within safe thresholds. The order of metal concentrations was Fe > Mn > Cu > Cr > Zn > Cd. The relatively high iron (Fe) levels may be attributed to corrosion of iron and steel components in municipal wastewater treatment systems, which contributes to iron accumulation in sludge-derived bio-slurry. 60 In contrast, the low cadmium (Cd) levels agree with, 61 who reported that controlled wastewater systems with limited industrial discharge reduce cadmium contamination.

3.3.3. Microbial Load in Bio-Slurry

Results in Table 6 indicate that the bio-slurry exhibited high microbial counts, with mesophilic aerobic bacteria (4.4 × 107 CFU ml-1), moulds (1 × 108 CFU ml-1), and coliforms (1 × 105 CFU ml-1), all exceeding CODEX acceptable limits. The elevated mould levels likely reflect poor storage conditions, while the high coliform count indicates possible faecal contamination and the presence of potential pathogens. Coliforms serve as standard indicators of faecal contamination and treatment efficiency. 69

Although anaerobic digestion reduces pathogen loads compared with raw manure, it does not eliminate them completely. 70 Indicator bacteria may therefore be transferred to crops fertilized with bio-slurry. 71 Mesophilic digestion (30–40 °C), commonly used in small-scale biogas systems, is less effective at pathogen inactivation than thermophilic digestion (50–60 °C).72,73 Consequently, pathogens may persist in bio-slurry and pose contamination risks, particularly for vegetables consumed raw, which can serve as direct transmission routes for foodborne pathogens. 27

3.3.4. Heavy Metal Concentration in Pre- and Post-experimental Soils

Figure 1 presents the mean concentrations of heavy metals in soils before and after treatment. Across all treatments (bio-slurry, chemical fertiliser, and control), heavy metal levels remained below the maximum permissible limits (MPLs) set by Food and Agriculture Organization and World Health Organization, 19 indicating a low risk of contamination. Pb and Hg were not detected in both pre- and post-experimental soils, suggesting minimal background levels or limited anthropogenic inputs. Similarly, Cd was undetectable in chemical fertiliser-treated soils, indicating low input or limited mobility. The order of heavy metal concentrations changed slightly after treatment but remained dominated by Fe, followed by Mn and Cr. Iron (Fe) recorded the highest concentrations, with significant increases after treatment, particularly in bio-slurry (744.10±7.92 mg/kg) and chemical fertiliser-treated soils (710.03±5.32 mg/kg), compared to the control (321.27±2.78 mg/kg). This agrees with, 74 and is likely due to iron-rich parent material and possible anthropogenic contributions such as runoff and soil disturbance.75,76

3.3.5. Effects of Bio-Slurry Application and Chemical Fertiliser on Heavy Metal Concentration in the Soil

Figure 1 shows that bio-slurry application significantly increased Fe (357.02±2.31 to 744.10±7.92 mg kg-1), Zn (2.06±0.21 to 3.35±0.41 mg kg-1), and Cr (8.53±0.97 to 32.10±1.02 mg kg-1) concentrations. This increase agrees with, 78 who attributed elevated heavy metal levels to the characteristics of sewage sludge–derived bio-slurry. Similarly, 79 reported that wastewater sources, including domestic and industrial effluents and sewer corrosion, contribute to heavy metal accumulation in sludge. The increase in Cr bioavailability also agrees with, 47 while the rise in Fe and Zn is consistent with findings by.29,48,53 These increases may further be influenced by organic matter in bio-slurry, which enhances metal mobility and availability through chelation with humic substances. 80

In contrast, chemical fertiliser application significantly increased Fe (232.10±1.99 to 710.03±5.32 mg kg-1) but decreased Zn (4.10±0.10 to 2.88±0.61 mg kg-1) relative to the control. This finding agrees with, 48 who reported reduced Zn levels following chemical fertiliser application. The decline in Zn may be attributed to alkaline soil conditions, where zinc forms insoluble compounds, reducing its availability. 81

3.4 Effects of Application of Bio-Slurry and Chemical Fertiliser on Heavy Metal Concentration in Maize, Rape, and Chinese Cabbage

3.4.1. Cadmium

A significant difference in Cd concentrations was observed in Chinese cabbage, with higher levels in bio-slurry-treated soils (1.70±0.11 mg kg-1) compared to chemical fertiliser and control treatments. This finding agrees with, 53 who reported elevated Cd levels in bio-slurry-amended crops. The increased availability of Cd may be linked to organic matter inputs from bio-slurry, particularly sewage sludge, which can contain Cd and enhance its mobility and plant uptake.82-84 Despite this, Cd concentrations in crops remained below the maximum permissible limits (MPLs) set by Food and Agriculture Organization and World Health Organization, 19 indicating safe consumption levels.

3.4.2. Iron (Fe)

Fe concentrations did not exceed MPLs but showed significant differences among treatments, with higher levels in maize (346.50 ± 6.52 mg kg-1) and rape (260.00 ± 5.49 mg kg-1) under bio-slurry application. This agrees with49,90, who reported increased Fe uptake in crops grown in bio-slurry-amended soils. The elevated Fe levels are likely due to high baseline soil Fe and its availability under suitable pH and redox conditions. 85 Overall, Fe levels remained within safe limits for consumption.

3.4.3. Lead (Pb) and Mercury (Hg)

Pb and Hg were not detected in any crop samples across all treatments. This indicates minimal contamination and confirms that maize, Chinese cabbage, and rape are safe for consumption with respect to Pb and Hg toxicity.

3.4.4. Manganese (Mn) and Zinc (Zn)

No significant differences in Mn and Zn concentrations were observed across treatments. However, the highest Zn level was recorded in Chinese cabbage under chemical fertiliser (11.7 ± 0.17 mg kg-1), while higher Mn was observed in rape (39.03 ± 1.34 mg kg-1) under the same treatment. These findings agree with,53,79 who reported increased Mn and Zn under chemical fertilisation. Despite this, all values remained below MPLs, indicating no risk to human health.

3.4.5. Copper and Chromium

Copper (Cu) and chromium (Cr) concentrations showed no statistically significant differences across treatments. The highest mean values were recorded in Chinese cabbage and rape grown in bio-slurry-amended soils, but these concentrations were well below WHO/FAO (2011) permissible limits. 40 Organic amendments can enhance Cu and Cr mobility through complexation with humic substances, potentially increasing plant uptake. 80 Although Cu is an essential micronutrient, excessive dietary intake may pose health risks. 86 In the present study, Cu and Cr levels in all crops remained within safe thresholds, indicating no adverse implications for human consumption.

Among the crops studied, heavy metal accumulation followed the order rape > Chinese cabbage > maize, reflecting the greater metal uptake capacity of leafy vegetables. Leafy crops tend to accumulate higher concentrations of heavy metals due to their larger surface areas and enhanced root–shoot translocation mechanisms.87,88 Similar trends have been reported by 89 who observed higher heavy metal concentrations in spinach compared to other vegetables. According to Singh et al (2010) 87 heavy metals may adhere to leaf surfaces and subsequently be absorbed into plant tissues, contributing to elevated concentrations in edible parts. Variations in metal accumulation among vegetable species are therefore largely attributed to differences in metal absorption efficiency and physiological characteristics 65 .

Across treatments, the descending order of heavy metal concentrations in crops was Fe > Cu > Cr > Mn > Zn > Cd for bio-slurry-treated soils, and Fe > Mn > Cr > Cu > Zn > Cd for both chemical fertiliser and control treatments, indicating iron as the dominant metal accumulated irrespective of treatment type (Figure 2).

3.5. General Discussion

Taken together, these results show that bio-slurry was consistently more effective than chemical fertiliser at maintaining soil organic carbon, organic matter, and exchangeable base cations, while both chemical fertiliser and the unamended control were associated with a decline in soil pH over the cropping season. This pattern aligns with the wider literature on organic-versus-mineral amendment comparisons discussed in section 3.2, and supports bio-slurry’s agronomic case as a soil-fertility amendment under smallholder conditions in Malawi.

However, two caveats temper a straightforward food-safety conclusion, and the contrast with comparable studies elsewhere is instructive. Across the four most relevant comparator studies, bio-slurry or biosolid application has, in three of four cases, resulted in at least one heavy metal exceeding food-safety thresholds in the harvested crop: chromium in maize shoots regardless of biosolid source in Uganda 78 ; nickel and cobalt in maize grain in Ethiopia 79 ; and arsenic, lead, and cadmium in lettuce, also in Ethiopia. 80 Against this backdrop, our finding that all measured heavy metals in soil and crops remained below FAO/WHO permissible limits is a comparatively favourable outcome, but not one that should be over-generalised, for two reasons.

First, although absolute concentrations stayed within permissible limits, bio-slurry application measurably increased Fe, Zn, and Cr in soil relative to chemical fertiliser and the control (section 3.3.5). This directional increase, even while remaining sub-threshold, mirrors patterns reported elsewhere for sludge- and digestate-derived organic amendments81,82 and warrants monitoring under repeated or longer-term application, since cumulative loading rather than single-season concentration is the more relevant risk metric for soils receiving organic amendments year after year.

Second, and more importantly, the bio-slurry itself exceeded CODEX microbiological limits for mesophilic aerobic bacteria, moulds, and coliforms (Table 6), indicating a microbiological food-safety hazard that is independent of, and not mitigated by, the low heavy-metal risk. This is particularly relevant for rape and Chinese cabbage, which are frequently consumed with minimal cooking. This result is consistent with reports that mesophilic anaerobic digestion does not fully inactivate pathogens,83,84 and it means that bio-slurry’s food-safety profile in this study should be described as favourable with respect to heavy metals but conditional with respect to microbiological quality, pending further treatment (e.g., extended curing, thermophilic digestion, or a defined pre-harvest interval) or hygienic handling at the point of crop consumption.

Table 7 situates these findings against previous local and regional studies of bio-slurry and chemical fertiliser effects on soil and crop heavy metals. The studies vary considerably in amendment source, crop type, and the suite of metals assessed, and not all report a formal food-safety comparison, With that caveat, the broader pattern across this literature that organic amendments derived from biological waste streams carry a real, if variable, risk of heavy-metal transfer to edible tissue argues for caution in extrapolating our single-season, single-site result to other bio-slurry sources or longer application histories.

We therefore recommend that future work apply a quantitative dietary-exposure framework, such as the estimated daily intake (EDI) and target hazard quotient (THQ) approach used by Lucic et al (2022) 89 for vegetable heavy-metal exposure, to move beyond simple comparison with permissible limits toward a formal risk estimate for Malawian dietary patterns. Such an approach would also allow the heavy-metal and microbiological findings to be weighed within a single, comparable risk framework, rather than assessed as separate and seemingly contradictory conclusions about the same amendment.

4. Conclusion

Bio-slurry and chemical fertiliser had distinct effects on soil physicochemical properties. Bio-slurry increased organic carbon, organic matter, and exchangeable base cations (Ca, Na, Mg, K), consistent with improved fertility and nutrient retention, while chemical fertiliser raised soil pH but reduced these same parameters, pointing to a risk of soil degradation under continuous use. All measured heavy metals in soil and crops remained below FAO/WHO permissible limits. Bio-slurry increased soil Fe, Zn, and Cr relative to chemical fertiliser, which increased Fe but reduced Zn; despite these shifts, Cd, Fe, Zn, Mn, Cu, and Cr in maize, rape, and Chinese cabbage stayed within permissible limits, and Pb and Hg were not detected in any treatment. Leafy vegetables accumulated more metal than maize, likely reflecting their greater uptake capacity. These results are limited to a single cropping season and to the metals and crops measured, and should not be extrapolated to a general claim of food safety — particularly given the elevated microbial counts recorded in the bio-slurry itself (Table 6), which indicate that its food-safety benefits cannot be assumed without attention to hygienic production and handling. We recommend that fertiliser sources be monitored to prevent heavy-metal contamination, that bio-slurry’s microbiological quality be addressed (e.g., through extended curing, thermophilic digestion, or hygienic handling) before wider recommendation, and that bio-slurry be integrated with reduced rates of chemical fertiliser to improve soil organic matter and nutrient retention. Longer-term studies are needed to assess cumulative effects on soil quality and heavy metal dynamics under varying soil and cropping conditions.

4.1. Study Limitations

This study was limited to a single cropping season and a short duration, which may not adequately capture long-term effects or seasonal variations in soil properties and heavy metal accumulation. Additionally, the research was conducted within a specific geographic area and focused on selected crops, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other regions, environmental conditions, and cropping systems.
