Proteomics of malignant pleural mesothelioma under hypoxic and normoxic conditions in a large animal (porcine) tumor model
Authors: Weirun Min, Donggang He, Xuanbo Liu, Xiaokang Zhu, Baiqiang Cui, Wei Cao, Zhuang Zuo, Jin Wang, Zhibo Ye, Xu Tang, Sheng Yong, Shengyuan Xiong, Dacheng Jin, Shengguo Zhao, Yunjiu Gou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMalignant Pleural Mesothelioma (MPM) is a rare but highly aggressive malignant tumor, whose occurrence is closely associated with asbestos exposure. Clinical treatment options are limited and the prognosis is poor. The tumor microenvironment, especially hypoxia, plays a key regulatory role in the occurrence and development of MPM, yet its specific molecular mechanism remains unclear. To explore the effects of different oxygen metabolic states on the protein expression profiles and functional characteristics of MPM, this study first cultured MPM cells under normoxic and hypoxic conditions and conducted proteomic analysis. Furthermore, it explored MPM animal models simulating normoxic and hypoxic conditions, aiming to provide an experimental platform closer to the physiological state for MPM mechanism research and targeted intervention.MethodsIn in vitro studies, MSTO-211 H cells were cultured under 1% O₂ concentration (experimental group) and 21% O₂ concentration (control group) for 48 h, passaged for 3 generations, with 3 biological replicates in each group. TMT quantitative proteomics was used to screen differential proteins, and GO and KEGG analyses were performed to explore related signaling pathways. In animal experiments, the hypoxic group and normoxic group were injected with benzo(a)pyrene solution via a closed thoracic device, while the blank control group was injected with normal saline. Clinical symptoms and multiple biological indicators of the animals were monitored, and tumor characteristics were described based on CT, histopathological and immunohistochemical results.​ResultsIn in vitro studies, a total of 42 differential proteins were identified between the hypoxic group and the normoxic group. GO and KEGG analyses revealed that the two groups shared differences in three signaling pathways: protein digestion and absorption, neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction, and Staphylococcus aureus infection. In in vivo studies, MPM occurred in both the hypoxic group (Tibetan pigs) and the normoxic group (Landrace pigs) after intrathoracic injection of benzo(a)pyrene, while the normoxic group was accompanied by benign pleural hyperplasia. Only inflammatory cell infiltration was observed in the normal saline control group. The tumor tissue was composed of well-differentiated epithelioid cells and fibrovascular stroma. IHC detection showed that the tumor cells expressed specific proteins consistent with MPM, and the tumor microenvironment contained abundant CD31-positive small vascular endothelial cells. During the follow-up period of 224–238 days, the experimental pigs remained in good health.​ConclusionBased on the study of differentially expressed proteins in malignant pleural mesothelioma under hypoxic and normoxic conditions, the successfully constructed large-scale porcine tumor model can pre-verify the effect of drugs on tumors in different oxygen environments during new drug development, improving screening efficiency and success rate. It can also accurately simulate clinical scenarios, providing experimental support for the efficacy evaluation and scheme optimization of intervention strategies such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune targeting, thereby effectively promoting the development of clinical treatment for malignant pleural mesothelioma.​Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15256-9.

Background

Mesothelioma is a rare tumor originating from mesothelial cells in the pleura or other sites, with approximately 81% arising from the pleura and the remaining from locations including the peritoneum, pericardium, and vaginal tunic [1–5]. Malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) is a rare cancer [6, 7], with an incidence projected to continue increasing in the coming decades. Clinically, it is usually diagnosed at an advanced stage and is easily misdiagnosed due to overlapping symptoms with tuberculous pleurisy and pleural metastasis of lung cancer. Current treatments such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy yield suboptimal outcomes [8–10]. Oxygen is one of the key features of the tumor microenvironment, and is closely related to tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, metabolic reprogramming, and immune escape. A large number of studies have shown that hypoxic status can promote tumor progression, enhance its invasiveness and metastatic potential, and significantly affect patient prognosis [11, 12]. In malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM), studies have reported that hypoxia can enhance its invasive behavior at both molecular and phenotypic levels [13]. However, there are systematic differences in the biological behaviors of tumor cells under different oxygen tension conditions, and these changes can often be reflected by differences in the expression profiles of the proteome.​Thus, the in vitro component of this study focuses on the protein level, leveraging tandem mass tag (TMT)-based quantitative proteomics—a cutting-edge technique—tocomprehensively and in-depth analyze protein expression profiles of MPM cells under hypoxic and normoxic environments. To validate the scientific rigor and reliability of in vitro findings, corresponding in vivo animal experiments are urgently necessary.​Currently, most in vivo animal models used for basic MPM research are mice or rats, which exhibit significant differences in cancer biology compared to human MPM [14]. Preclinical models that accurately recapitulate the genomic and pathological characteristics of MPM not only expand our understanding of MPM biology but also provide an excellent platform for investigating therapeutic strategies [15]. Pigs, however, share striking similarities with humans in terms of body size, anatomical structure, physiology, and pathophysiological responses [16]. Studies have demonstrated high homology between porcine and human gene sequences [17, 18]. Adamet et al. reported a porcine cancer model based on primary porcine cells, revealing crucial similarities in tumorigenesis between pigs and humans [19]. Additionally, pigs have a relatively long lifespan, enabling longitudinal studies to simulate disease onset and progression under different conditions [17, 18, 20]. In constructing the animal model for this study, based on in vitro findings, different types of experimental animals were selected to simulate hypoxic and normoxic microenvironments. Tibetan pigs, which have long inhabited high-altitude regions, possess inherent adaptability to hypoxic environments. They exhibit lower arterial oxygen content and oxygen saturation, coupled with heavier lungs and denser pulmonary arteries and arterioles to ensure respiration under hypoxia [21], making them ideal for simulating the hypoxic tumor microenvironment. In contrast, Landrace pigs serve as representatives of the normoxic environment.​This study aims to establish a safe, stable, and reproducible protocol for constructing a porcine model of malignant pleural mesothelioma.

Materials and methods

In vitro study

MSTO-211 H cells were cultured under two oxygen environments

The experimental group was cultured in a hypoxic environment (oxygen concentration 1%), and the control group was cultured in a normoxic environment (oxygen concentration 21%). For the experimental group, MSTO-211 H cells were cultured at 1% oxygen concentration for 48 h; for the control group, MSTO-211 H cells were cultured at 21% oxygen concentration for 72 h. After passaging to the 3rd generation, each group was subjected to sequencing with 3 biological replicates.

Cell line

Human pleural mesothelioma cells (MSTO-211 H) were purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd.​.

Instruments and reagents​

Instruments: Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer, Easy-nLC system, Eppendorf 5430R centrifuge, GE Healthcare EPS601 gel electrophoresis apparatus, Thermo Scientific microplate reader, MP Fastprep-24 homogenizer, Scientz JY92-II ultrasonic liquid processor, Nanodrop 2000c, MASCOT 2.2 software.​.

Reagents: TMT labeling kit, BCA kit (P0012), formic acid (FA), acetonitrile (ACN), glycerol, BSA, bromophenol blue (Sangon), SDS, Urea, dithiothreitol (DTT), iodoacetamide (IAA, Bio-Rad), KH₂PO₄, KCl, HCl (Sinopharm), C18 Cartridge, trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), Tris, NH₄HCO₃ (Sigma), 1640 medium, fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin-streptomycin double antibody, PBS, trypsin (Gibco).

Proteomic analysis​

TMT proteomics and functional and pathway enrichment analysis

Proteins were extracted using SDT lysis method. After quantification by BCA assay, an appropriate amount of protein was subjected to trypsin digestion by FASP method. The digested peptides were desalted by C18 Cartridge, lyophilized, reconstituted with 0.1% formic acid solution, and quantified by OD280. Meanwhile, 20 µg of protein from each sample was mixed with 5X loading buffer, boiled for 5 min, separated on 12.5% SDS-PAGE gel at a constant current of 14 mA for 90 min, and stained with Coomassie Brilliant Blue R-250 to visualize protein bands.​Subsequently, 100 µg peptide mixture from each sample was labeled with iTRAQ reagent (Applied Biosystems) and TMT reagent (Thermo Scientific) respectively according to the manufacturers’ instructions. The labeled peptides were separated using a high pH reversed-phase peptide separation kit (Thermo Scientific). The dried peptide mixture was reconstituted and acidified with 0.1% TFA solution, then loaded onto an equilibrated high pH reversed-phase spin fractionation column. The bound peptides were eluted with a step gradient of volatile high pH elution solution containing different concentrations of acetonitrile to obtain 10 different fractions. The fractions were desalted by C18 column and concentrated by vacuum centrifugation.​Then, 60/90-minute LC-MS/MS analysis was performed using a Q Exactive mass spectrometer coupled with an Easy nLC system. Peptides were separated with a specific buffer gradient in reversed-phase trap column and analytical column. The mass spectrometer acquired data in positive ion mode using data-dependent top 10 method. The obtained MS raw data were used for protein identification and quantification via the MASCOT engine (version 2.2) embedded in Proteome Discoverer 1.4 software.​Hierarchical clustering analysis was performed using Cluster 3.0 and Java Treeview software with Euclidean distance algorithm and average linkage clustering algorithm (based on the centroid of observations). Subcellular localization of proteins was predicted by CELLO method (machine learning based on multiple support vector machines). The sequence was scanned using InterProScan software package to obtain domain annotation information of target proteins in the Pfam database.​For functional and pathway analysis, NCBI BLAST + client software and InterProScan were used to search for homologous sequences of differentially expressed proteins, and then Blast2GO software was used for GO functional annotation, and R software was used to draw annotation results. Proteins were compared with KEGG database to retrieve KEGG homology identification and map to corresponding pathways. With the entire quantitative protein as the background dataset, enrichment analysis was performed based on Fisher’s exact test, and p-values were adjusted by Benjamini-Hochberg correction. Functional categories and pathways with p-values less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Based on information from IntAct or STRING database, Cytoscape software (version 3.2.1) was used to construct and analyze protein-protein interaction networks.​

In vivo study

By intrathoracic injection of benzo(a)pyrene, a known carcinogen, into experimental animals, a series of complex biological processes ultimately resulted in the successful induction of MPM animal models that grow in accordance with actual tumor conditions under different oxygen environments.

Experimental animal

Three male Tibetan pigs (acclimatized to a hypoxic environment) with a body weight of approximately 5–10 kg were randomly selected for the experimental group. For the control group, three male Landrace pigs of comparable weight (acclimatized to a normoxic environment) were randomly chosen. Both groups received an administration of 2 mg benzopyrene inducer once every two weeks. In addition, one male Tibetan pig, weighing approximately 5–10 kg, was selected and administered the same volume of normal saline to serve as the blank control group (as shown in Fig. 1). All experimental animals were purchased from Gansu Bailv Grass Industry Development Co., Ltd. The three groups were housed in separate cages and fed the same diet under standard rearing condition.

Experimental reagents and equipment

3,4-Benzopyrene (3,4-BaP), procured from Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., was stored sealed at 4 °C. Veterinary-grade ventilators and electrocardiographs were provided by the College of Veterinary Medicine, Gansu Agricultural University. Additional materials included corn oil, Propofol medium/long-chain triglyceride emulsion injection, sevoflurane for inhalation, sufentanil injection, cisatracurium, lidocaine, enrofloxacin injection, sodium pentobarbital, phenytoin sodium, veterinary laryngoscope, breathing mask, disposable tracheal tubes, anesthesia double-lumen tubes, and disposable thoracostomy kits.

Porcine tumor model experimental procedures

Anesthesia and tracheal intubation

Two weeks post-acclimatization, an assistant in protective clothing guided the pigs to the operation table, ensuring gentle handling to prevent resistance. The pigs were then appropriately positioned on the table, and the anesthesia assistant adjusted the ventilator to a flow rate of 5 L/min with 8% sevoflurane for inhalation, providing assisted ventilation via a breathing mask. Upon achieving sufficient sedation, an indwelling needle was inserted into the auricular vein, sevoflurane was discontinued, and anesthesia was rapidly induced with 2 mg/kg Propofol medium/long-chain triglyceride emulsion injection, 1 µg/kg sufentanil injection, and 0.15 mg/kg rocuronium. Two minutes later, satisfactory anesthesia was confirmed by muscle relaxation, stable respiration, and absence of pain response. Tracheal intubation was performed using a veterinary laryngoscope, with the tube number and depth recorded. Manual ventilation was provided, and bilateral lung sounds were auscultated as clear and symmetrical. The tracheal tube was secured, connected to the ventilator, with tidal volume adjusted to 8–10 ml/kg and respiratory rate set to 15–20 breaths/min. Following successful intubation, the pigs were prepared for the subsequent steps of the closed chest drainage perfusion implantation.

Establishment of a continuous closed chest drainage perfusion model in experimental pigs

Once satisfactory anesthesia and successful tracheal intubation were achieved, the pigs were positioned in left lateral decubitus. The right thoracic wall of the pigs was marked at the seventh intercostal space for puncture, and the area around the surgical incision was prepared by an assistant. The surgeon performed hand hygiene, donned sterile surgical attire, and disinfected a 15 cm area centered on the puncture site three times. Local infiltration anesthesia was administered with 20 mg/ml lidocaine. Using curved hemostatic forceps, the surgeon alternated blunt dissection of the thoracic wall muscle layers to reach the upper margin of the rib, piercing the parietal pleura to enter the pleural cavity, indicated by a breakthrough sensation and immediate outflow of liquid or gas. The drainage tube was clamped and inserted 1–2 cm into the pleural cavity through the intercostal incision. After confirming the appropriate depth with a guidewire, the skin at the incision site was sutured, and the drainage tube was secured and covered with sterile gauze and adhesive tape, as shown in Fig. 2. If the pigs exhibited movement or resistance to the ventilator during the procedure, an additional 2 mg of rocuronium was administered. After the procedure and inducer injection, the tracheal tube was removed once spontaneous breathing, bilateral pupillary light reflex, and limb muscle strength were fully recovered. The pigs were monitored for directional recovery and normal activity, provided with liquid feed two hours later, and returned to normal feeding four hours post-procedure. The animals were closely monitored for three days post-procedure, and if no significant abnormalities were observed, the experimental and control groups were injected with the inducer. The drainage device was connected to the benzopyrene-corn oil mixture, and 2 ml of the inducer (containing 25 mg benzopyrene) was injected, while the blank control group received 2 ml of saline. Post-injection, the pigs were observed for signs of redness, swelling, or purulent discharge at the tube insertion site. If these symptoms occurred, enrofloxacin injection was administered intramuscularly (2.5 mg/kg, once or twice daily for 2–3 days until wound inflammation subsided). The drainage device’s tubing was flushed weekly with an appropriate amount of saline, and the device was checked for blockages or dislodgement, addressing any issues promptly. The inducer or saline was injected every two weeks, and the animals’ body weight changes were recorded. Details of the experimental procedures are presented in Table 1.

Imaging data collection

Following the establishment of the continuous closed chest drainage perfusion model in the experimental pigs, CT scans were performed to verify the appropriate placement of the chest drainage perfusion device and tracheal intubation, as shown in Fig. 3. Nine weeks after the first inducer injection, a lung CT scan was conducted to observe pleural lesions. Upon the initial detection of tumor imaging, the pigs’ physiological indicators and behavior were continuously monitored, with CT scans performed every 12 weeks to observe tumor growth and metastasis.

Follow-up and euthanasia

Throughout the follow-up period, clinical monitoring of the animals was conducted daily, with particular attention to any signs of pain or pneumonia. All animals underwent non-contrast chest CT scans post-inducer injection. If no pleural hyperplasia or lung tumors were detected at 9 weeks, the animals were euthanized. If tumors were detected, animals were re-scanned at 12 weeks to assess tumor growth. On the day of euthanasia, peripheral venous blood samples were collected to observe abnormal blood counts. The animals were euthanized by intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital (80 mg/kg) and phenytoin sodium (10 mg/kg).

Pathological examination

Following euthanasia, necropsies were performed by researchers from the College of Animal Science and Technology, Gansu Agricultural University. The thickened pleural and lung tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (NBF) for 2 to 3 days. Using disposable biopsy knives, the pleura and lung tissues were sectioned at intervals of approximately 5 mm for macroscopic examination of tumor presence. Additionally, a comprehensive examination of the thoracic lymph nodes, chest wall, and abdominal cavity was conducted during the necropsy, with all gross lesions fixed in NBF. The selected pleural and lung tissue sections were processed in alcohol and xylene, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm, and stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E). Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining for tumor samples included cytokeratin 7 (CK7), vimentin, calretinin, D2-40, WT-1, MC, Ki-67, NapsinA, and CD31, using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen and hematoxylin as the counterstain. IHC staining procedures were as outlined in Supplementary Tables 3 and validated using blank control pig pleural tissue.

Safety

All experimental animals remained in good health during the follow-up period. Comparison of peripheral venous blood samples before induction and at euthanasia showed that only inflammatory indicators and tumor markers tended to increase, while other indicators were not significantly abnormal. Only one case of catheter-related complication (small right pneumothorax) occurred in Tibetan pigs, which recovered quickly after catheter drainage. The skin at the catheterization site showed no abnormalities, with no pain on palpation, and the animals maintained normal daily activities.

In vivo study

By injecting benzopyrene, a known carcinogen, into the chest cavities of these pigs, we successfully induced malignant pleural mesothelioma through a series of complex biological processes, resulting in models that accurately reflect tumour conditions in varying oxygen environments.

Results of imaging and body weight changes

Before the initiation of the inducer injection, peripheral venous blood samples were collected for baseline tests and to preserve normal blood samples. Following the first inducer injection, blood samples were again drawn for analysis of complete blood count, chemical components, and tumor markers, in addition to conducting chest CT scans. Special attention was given to pre-existing pleural thickening and abnormal density shadows at the pleura-lung interface (including lung consolidation and ground-glass opacities) to distinguish them from new abnormalities post-induction. At week 9 post-induction, chest CT scans were performed on all experimental animals, which revealed subtle pleural thickening in some pigs, minimal pleural effusion in others, and abnormal density shadows at the pleura-lung interface in some instances (as shown in Fig. 11). Following biweekly injections of 2 ml of the inducer or saline, the animals’ body weights were recorded. Notably, the inducer-injected animals exhibited a marked decrease in weight gain by week 16 post-induction, in contrast to the control group, which exhibited no abnormal weight trends, as illustrated in Fig. 12. The longest tumor diameter changes measured by CT images are shown in Fig. 13. By week 28 post-induction, animals injected with the inducer began exhibiting significantly restless behavior, prompting further peripheral venous blood sampling and close monitoring for abnormalities and physiological changes. At week 30 post-induction, blood analysis indicated slightly elevated counts, and chest CT scans revealed pleural hyperplasia and minimal pleural effusion in six animals injected with benzopyrene, with cytological analysis of the pleural effusion showing numerous white blood cells and a few atypical cell components. Blood sampling at 32 weeks post-induction continued to show slightly elevated blood counts and tumor markers, with an increasing trend in atypical cells in the pleural effusion compared to earlier findings. Chest CT scans consistently demonstrated clear pleural thickening, with images revealing abnormal density shadows at the pleura-lung interface, as detailed in Table 2; no enlargement of thoracic lymph nodes was observed during the follow-up period. Pathological findings

Tumors developed at the injection site in five cases (5/7, 71%), and one tumor (1/7, 14%) was located at the pleura-lung interface. The induced tumors included three cases of epithelioid pleural mesothelioma (3/5, 60%, as shown in Figs. 14, 15, and 16) and two cases of biphasic pleural mesothelioma (2/5, 40%, as shown in Figs. 17 and 18). In the Landrace pig group, two cases (2/3, 66%, as shown in Figs. 14 and 15) developed tumors post-induction, with one case showing pleural mesothelial cell hyperplasia (as shown in Fig. 19); all three Tibetan pigs (3/3, 100%, as shown in Figs. 14, 17, and 18) developed tumors post-induction. Discussion

Malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) is generally diagnosed at an advanced stage. Clinically, the main treatments for MPM are maintenance chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Compared with surgical treatment, chemotherapy and immunotherapy have significant advantages in prolonging the survival of MPM patients. However, most chemotherapeutic and immunotherapeutic drugs have considerable side effects, and there is a clinical need for more effective chemotherapeutic and immunotherapeutic agents with fewer side effects.​

The hypoxic microenvironment can affect tumor progression through multi-dimensional regulation. At the molecular level, it activates HIF-1α, upregulates GLUT1 and HK2 to enhance glycolysis, and activates the VEGF pathway to promote abnormal angiogenesis, thereby exacerbating oxygen supply imbalance [22, 23]. In signal crosstalk, it synergizes with the protein digestion and absorption pathway through the PI3K-AKT and NF-κB pathways, promotes proliferation via ASCT2/mTOR, mediates drug resistance through LAT1 (similar to the mechanism in lung cancer), and regulates Rac1 through the neuroactive ligand-receptor pathway to enhance invasiveness (consistent with the mechanism in breast cancer) [24–26]. At the microenvironmental level, it promotes tumor cells to secrete CCL2, which recruits tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and induces M2 polarization to form an immunosuppressive microenvironment, increases vascular permeability to facilitate metastasis, and the Staphylococcus aureus infection pathway may amplify this effect via TLR2 [27, 28]. Our in vitro study revealed 42 differentially expressed proteins between the normoxic and hypoxic groups, among which 8 were upregulated. The top 5 upregulated proteins were PSMA5, UBE2E1, SERPING1, TMEM230, and POLR2K, while the top 5 of the 34 downregulated proteins were NPDC1, DSTN, COX6C, KRT72, and ENO2.

The core mechanisms of action of the upregulated proteins are as follows: As a core subunit of the 20 S proteasome, PSMA5 is highly expressed in liver cancer, lung cancer, and MPM. It affects the cell cycle by regulating cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), and promotes proliferation, inhibits apoptosis, and enhances invasiveness through the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway as well as signaling pathways such as PI3K-AKT and NF-κB. Based on its functional characteristics, specific inhibitors (such as optimized bortezomib structures) can be developed to maintain the stability of tumor suppressor proteins by blocking proteasome activity, and restore protein metabolic balance to induce tumor cell apoptosis [29–31]. UBE2E1 is a key molecule in the ubiquitin-proteasome system. It activates pro-cancer pathways by mediating the ubiquitination and degradation of tumor suppressor proteins such as p53 and PTEN, and simultaneously regulates the ubiquitination of cyclins to accelerate the cell cycle process. It can also interfere with DNA damage repair to cause drug resistance and promote immune escape by remodeling the tumor microenvironment, playing a pro-cancer role in various cancers such as breast cancer, lung cancer, and colorectal cancer [32–35]. In addition to being related to hereditary angioedema, SERPING1 (C1 esterase inhibitor) is involved in the remodeling of the immune microenvironment in cancer by inhibiting complement-mediated killing and regulating angiogenesis. Chronic inflammation caused by its dysfunction can drive tumor proliferation, and indirectly activate the PI3K-AKT pathway by regulating extracellular proteases to promote tumor progression [36–38]. Although TMEM230 is associated with Parkinson’s disease, in cancer, it can regulate proliferation and survival through the PI3K-AKT pathway, interfere with the cell cycle and apoptotic processes, and at the same time affect the tumor microenvironment and cellular metabolism, promoting carcinogenesis in multiple dimensions [39–45]. As a subunit of RNA polymerase II, POLR2K enhances oncogene expression and inhibits tumor suppressor genes through transcriptional dysregulation, interferes with the transcription of genes related to DNA damage repair, and regulates the expression of cyclins leading to uncontrolled proliferation, maintaining the malignant phenotype of tumor cells [46, 47]. KRT72 is a member of the keratin gene family, and research on it in tumors is relatively limited. Its specific functions in tumor cells and its impact on tumor development require more studies to explore and reveal.

The core mechanisms of action of the downregulated proteins are as follows: NPDC1 was initially discovered in the nervous system, where it regulates the proliferation and differentiation of neural precursor cells. In terms of tumor cells, NPDC is associated with tumor infiltration in colon cancer and functions to inhibit tumor cell proliferation [48, 49]. The protein encoded by DSTN belongs to the ADF family of actin-binding proteins. In cancers such as lung cancer and pancreatic cancer, DSTN expression is upregulated, which is closely related to the enhanced proliferation, invasion, and metastasis abilities of tumor cells. However, in colorectal cancer, compared with normal mucosa, DSTN expression in tumor tissues is significantly downregulated, and the degree of its decreased expression is related to disease prognosis [50–52]. COX6C is a component of the cytochrome c oxidase complex. In lung cancer, copy number amplification in the 8q22.2 region drives the upregulation of COX6C expression, which may mediate mitosis through the ROS-AMPK signaling pathway, thereby regulating cell proliferation. Studies have found that knocking down COX6C may inhibit cell apoptosis and promote cancer development, suggesting that the role of COX6C in tumorigenesis and development is relatively complex and may play different functions at different stages [53–55]. ENO2 (enolase 2) plays an important role in tumor cells. High expression of ENO2 may promote glycolytic metabolism in tumor cells, providing sufficient energy for their rapid proliferation. At the same time, ENO2 may be involved in the invasion and metastasis of tumor cells, enhancing the migration ability of tumor cells by regulating cytoskeletal rearrangement and cell adhesion. In prostate cancer, the expression level of ENO2 is related to tumor stage and prognosis, and high expression of ENO2 often indicates poor prognosis, suggesting its key role in promoting the malignant progression of tumors [56–59]. The above differential proteins were first discovered in MPM cells cultured in hypoxic and normoxic microenvironments. These upregulated proteins may be key factors for malignant pleural mesothelioma cells to adapt to environmental changes and drive disease progression. In subsequent experiments, based on these clues, for the upregulated proteins, studying the signaling pathways they activate in hypoxic environments and their regulatory effects on downstream gene expression may lead to the discovery of key molecules related to tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, or anti-apoptosis. The study of downregulated proteins will help reveal the tumor suppressor pathways or normal cell function regulation mechanisms that are inhibited under normoxic conditions. Once these potential molecular targets are accurately identified, they will provide a direct theoretical basis for the development of specific targeted therapeutic drugs.​

GO and KEGG analyses revealed that the two groups were involved in 3 differential signaling pathways: In colorectal cancer, the protein digestion and absorption pathway activates mTOR via ASCT2 to promote proliferation; in breast cancer, it activates Rac1 to enhance invasion and metastasis. Additionally, it can regulate the microenvironment to promote angiogenesis and immunosuppression, participate in drug resistance (e.g., high expression of LAT1 in lung cancer leading to cisplatin resistance), and regulate the characteristics of cancer stem cells (inhibition of its enzymes can suppress the growth of glioma) [60–65]. The neuroactive ligand-receptor interaction pathway promotes cancer progression through multiple mechanisms, including activating signal transduction to enhance proliferation, regulating the cytoskeleton to facilitate invasion, modulating the microenvironment, participating in drug resistance, and regulating the properties of cancer stem cells [66, 67]. The Staphylococcus aureus infection pathway participates in cancer progression through multiple dimensions, such as activating inflammation via TLR2, regulating immune balance, activating related pathways to promote metastasis, and affecting macrophage functions [68–71]. These pathways provide clues for exploring key molecular targets in MPM, help clarify the regulatory mechanisms under different oxygen environments, and are of great significance for MPM research. They can provide new directions and theoretical references for the development of efficient and low-toxicity therapeutic agents, improvement of patient prognosis, and resolution of clinical dilemmas.

To systematically verify the scientific rigor and biological reliability of the conclusions from in vitro cell studies, conducting in vivo animal model experiments that match the in vitro experimental design is not only a key link in improving the research evidence chain but also a necessary prerequisite for promoting the translation of basic research to clinical practice, which holds important scientific value and practical urgency. Large animal models can effectively evaluate the responses of immune cells to new anticancer drugs in MPM cells, thereby reducing the side effects of new drugs, and the development of new drugs also requires the support of more large animal models [72]. Animal models, as in vivo preclinical models, are helpful for understanding the pathogenesis, immune cell interactions, and biological roles of MPM in living animals. In addition, animal models provide an excellent tumor microenvironment (TME) for the development and preclinical trials of new therapeutic drugs [73, 74]. For example, most antibiotics are tested on animals before being used in humans, and insulin was discovered using dogs as animal models [75]. Ghosn [76] reported the successful induction of neoplastic pulmonary nodules in a large animal (porcine) model. Benzopyrene, as one of the strongest carcinogens, is often used to construct animal lung tumor models [77–83]. In the porcine MPM model constructed in this study, Tibetan pigs have a unique hypoxic physiological mechanism to ensure the hypoxic environment for tumor cells. There is no difference in tumor histology induced by hypoxic and normoxic tumor microenvironments, but the malignancy of tumors under hypoxia is higher, which is consistent with the growth law of cancer cells in the human tumor microenvironment. Tumors generated under hypoxic microenvironment are mainly biphasic type with higher malignancy, while those generated under normoxic microenvironment are mainly epithelioid subtype with lower malignancy, which also conforms to the growth trend of tumor cells in the tumor microenvironment [84–86]. Most of the successfully induced tumors (3/5, 60%) are epithelioid subtype, which is similar to the WHO classification of human MPM: epithelioid subtype (approximately 60–80%), biphasic type (approximately 10–15%), and sarcomatoid subtype (approximately 10%) [87–89]. The tumor models in this study are mostly epithelioid subtype, and the epithelioid subtype is sensitive to chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and radiotherapy [8, 90], which can provide a preclinical model for the development of new drugs and a suitable large animal model for exploring MPM treatment strategies.​.

The large porcine tumor model constructed in this study has significant advantages: tumor tissues can be used for cell line culture, drug response detection, and research on characteristics and markers; allograft rejection is weak, and molecular characteristics and various biological properties are similar to humans, providing a reliable basis for preclinical verification of new drugs and treatment strategies, and also facilitating the exploration of minimally invasive surgery and research on immunotherapy. Its high tumor microvessel density is more conducive to innovation in vascular system treatment, and it has prominent application value in the screening and evaluation of intervention measures.​.

The innovation of this model lies in: it is the first to be constructed based on MPM differential proteins under hypoxia and normoxia, breaking through the limitations of traditional small animal models and being more close to human pathological characteristics; using the species differences between Tibetan pigs and Landrace pigs to simulate oxygen environment specificity, providing a new tool for analyzing the regulatory mechanism of the microenvironment; induced by benzo(a)pyrene, it has the characteristics of rapid growth and simple operation, which can provide strong support for related research.​.

This study has certain limitations: the dynamic interaction network of differential proteins remains to be elucidated; there are biological differences between the porcine model and humans; the sample size and observation period are limited; and in-depth model-based therapeutic research has not been carried out. In the future, combining single-cell sequencing, multi-omics analysis, and gene editing technology to improve the accuracy of the model can promote its translation in new drug development and optimization of treatment strategies, providing a more clinically relevant experimental platform for MPM research.

Conclusions

This study innovatively constructed a large porcine tumor model based on differentially expressed proteins of malignant pleural mesothelioma under hypoxic and normoxic conditions. Its innovative value is reflected in two aspects: on the one hand, in new drug development, it can specifically verify the effect of drugs on tumors in different oxygen environments, greatly improving the screening efficiency and success rate; on the other hand, it can accurately simulate clinical diagnosis and treatment scenarios, providing more practical experimental support for the efficacy evaluation and scheme optimization of intervention strategies such as chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune.

targeting. This model breaks through the limitations of traditional models and injects new impetus into promoting the development of clinical treatment for malignant pleural mesothelioma.
