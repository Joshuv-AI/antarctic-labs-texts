Nurse-led geriatric syndrome screening and intervention program for frail older inpatients in Singapore: a study protocol on implementation research and program evaluation
Authors: C. Lim, M. L. Ginting, A. Subramaniam, J. Y. Tang, E. E. Rodrigues, J. Q. Chia, S. Y. Chua, W. S. Tan, W. S. Lim, Y. Y. Ding, J. Chew, G. Sum, J. Y. J. Lim, J. Y. J. Lim, J. Y. Li, A. Mah, J. Kang, S. W. Gui, S. J. Vadassery, K. Y. How, H. N. Tan, R. W. M. Choo, C. R. Chang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFrailty confers greater risks of negative health outcomes in hospitalised older adults. To improve care for this vulnerable population, Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) is recommended for frail older persons. However, implementing CGA outside of specialized geriatrician-led settings is limited, and few care models use frailty to identify and target older persons for CGA in the hospital-wide context.MethodsThis paper describes the study protocol of the implementation research and program evaluation of the Inpatient Nursing Frailty Assessment (INFA) program. The INFA program is designed to address these gaps by improving the identification and management of frailty and geriatric syndromes at a local hospital. Participants will be patients aged 65 and above, admitted to medical and surgical wards, who are mildly to moderately frail (Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) 4–6) and not receiving inpatient geriatric care. There are two phases to the study: Pre-implementation and implementation. In the pre-implementation phase, we will elicit anticipated implementation barriers and enablers, and the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) and its Outcomes Addendum as the implementation research tool to select, specify and develop implementation strategies to address contextual barriers. In the implementation phase, we apply a hybrid effectiveness-implementation study design and multi-methods approach. Implementation research outcomes include adoption, implementation, sustainability, and scalability. A quasi-experimental approach with intervention and control arms will compare the effects of the INFA program to usual care. The primary outcome is functional status (activities of daily living) at six months post-discharge. Secondary outcomes include patient outcomes (e.g., health-related quality of life), health service utilisation and cost, and a cost-effectiveness analysis.DiscussionThe INFA program is a novel approach that empowers nurses to provide early frailty assessment to frail older adults warded in acute hospitals under non-geriatrician care. This study uses implementation research to develop implementation strategies, assess implementation outcomes and evaluate the effectiveness of INFA. It aims to be a valuable blueprint to enhance the quality of care for frail older adults within the inpatient setting.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT06469723. Background

Frailty is a state of increased vulnerability due to an age-associated decline in physiological reserves, compromising the individual’s ability to cope with acute stressors and resulting in adverse outcomes including disability, hospitalization, institutionalization, and mortality [1, 2]. In the acute inpatient setting, the prevalence of frailty is up to 59% in General Medicine wards, and can range 19–40% in surgical wards [3, 4]. Frailty at admission is also associated with longer hospital length of stays (LOS), higher readmission rates, and increased healthcare costs [5, 6]. Additionally, frail older inpatients have a higher risk of presenting with geriatric syndromes [7]. Notably, the presence of geriatric syndromes in conjunction with greater frailty severity is associated with prolonged LOS and increased 30-day readmissions [8].

Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) is a holistic, multidimensional, and multidisciplinary diagnostic approach and is the gold standard for the management of frailty in older persons [9]. CGA can also uncover geriatric syndromes that may otherwise go undetected [10], and evidence indicates that older patients who receive CGA upon hospital admission are more likely to be alive and in their own homes at follow‐up [11]. However, CGA is often considered complex, labour-intensive, and time-consuming [12], requiring significant resources that have restricted its implementation to specialized settings with access to geriatric specialists and multidisciplinary teams [13–16]. Furthermore, systematic procedures for identifying frailty and initiating CGA-based interventions in the inpatient setting remain lacking, and existing models of care do not routinely stratify individuals for interventions based on frailty status [17].

Existing international literature reported barriers to implementing CGA in hospitals and the emergency departments that were related to organisational structures, internal workflows, healthcare provider-level and patient-level hindrances [11, 18–22]. There are also mixed findings on the effect of CGA in inpatient settings on health outcomes, including frailty, quality of life, functional status, healthcare utilisation, and mortality [11, 18–22]. This has been attributed to poor implementation strategies [23, 24].

Additionally, many evidence-based interventions fail to be implemented with high fidelity and deliver the expected outcomes in real-world settings [25]. There has been a growing call to action to consider contextual barriers and enablers even prior to implementation and evaluate implementation outcomes during implementation and to assess implementation outcomes like adoption and sustainability [26]. By considering contextual factors, which includes social, cultural, physical environment and organisational settings [27], we can better develop, evaluate and refine evidence-informed implementation strategies to enhance the adoption, scalability, and sustainability of CGA-based models of care within healthcare systems [28].

This paper describes the study protocol of the implementation research and program evaluation of the Inpatient Nursing Frailty Assessment (INFA) program, which aims to support a ‘frailty-ready hospital’ by embedding a structured, nurse-led frailty intervention model integrated directly into routine inpatient care. Building on evidence that frailty assessment is feasible and prognostically meaningful in acute hospital settings [29, 30], and that Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment (CGA) improves outcomes for older inpatients [20], INFA’s novelty lies in applying routine frailty stratification early in the admission pathway using the CFS, combined with nurse-led geriatric syndrome screening at the bedside. This stratification then informs a tiered escalation approach, directing patients to the level of geriatric input appropriate to their needs. In doing so, INFA shifts frailty care upstream into routine ward processes, extends geriatric principles across non-geriatrician specialties, and offers a scalable model that can be adapted across hospital settings.

However, given the complexities of and gaps on successful implementation of frailty programs in real world inpatient contexts, a structured approach to designing and evaluating INFA’s implementation is essential. Thus, in this study, we also apply implementation science methodology to assess the contextual factors that influence uptake and implementation outcomes, both prior to and during implementation. The application of implementation research will hopefully facilitate a more seamless and successful implementation of INFA into routine inpatient clinical practices [26].

INFA program

The following section describes the INFA program according to the Aims, Ingredients (Inputs), Mechanism, and delivery (AIMD) framework [31]. The updated AIMD framework comprises four components (1) Aims: what do you want your intervention to achieve and for whom? (2) Ingredients: what comprises the intervention? (3) Mechanisms: how do you propose the intervention will work? And (4) Delivery: how will you deliver the intervention?

Aims

The INFA program aims to enhance the care of older adults in acute hospital settings by adopting a systematic approach that includes early identification of frailty, geriatric syndrome screening and targeted interventions, alongside a structured process to identify those with complex care needs for CGA. By integrating geriatric assessments and interventions into the hospital workflow, the INFA program ensures timely detection and management of geriatric syndromes, facilitates access to specialized geriatric expertise, and initiates early discharge planning to achieve optimal health outcomes for frail older inpatients.

Ingredients

The INFA program consists of a systematic risk stratification model that uses the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS) [32] to identify very mild to moderately frail patients (CFS 4–6) patients admitted to acute inpatient wards under non-geriatrician care who will be included and assessed under the INFA program. The INFA program includes several core components to deliver comprehensive care for frail older adults in acute hospital settings, as shown in Table 1. These patients will receive a standardized screening for geriatric syndromes (akin to a ‘brief-CGA”) [33] which triggers various resources, including nurse-led interventions, multidisciplinary team care, discharge planning and community care referrals. Patient journey diagram

With the aim to create a systematized pipeline to ensure the right patient group receives a higher level of assessment and geriatric involvement, the INFA also uses a triage system to facilitate the identification of patients with more complex needs (screened positive on 4AT [34], a brief validated delirium screening tool, that may suggest delirium or > 2 falls in the past 1 year) to be referred to the Geriatric Comprehensive Assessment and Rehabilitation for the Elderly (GeriCARE) service, an Advance Practice Nurse (APN)-led mobile geriatric assessment team that performs CGA for frail elderly inpatients. The APN will also conduct case discussions for care planning and advocate non-pharmacological behavioural interventions. If there are identified medical needs (i.e. delirium requiring pharmacological management), the APN will review the case with a geriatrician.

Post-discharge care plans such as referrals to community hospital, transitional care services, home-based and centre-based services or outpatient clinic appointments can also be planned from the multidisciplinary or geriatric care involvement.

Delivery

The INFA program is primarily nurse-led, with interventions and referrals based on the geriatric syndromes detected. Within each ward implementing the INFA program, there are ward nurses (Geriatric Resource Nurses) who are trained with the institutional Care of the Elderly certification, a hospital-level geriatrics nursing skills training program based on the Nurses Improving Care for Healthsystem Elders (NICHE) program [38] to ensure the readiness to care for frail older adults. In addition, a multidisciplinary team of healthcare professionals, including physiotherapists, occupational therapists, dietitians, speech therapists, and medical social workers deliver the intervention. Other specialist consultations, including Otorhinolaryngology and Ophthalmology services for sensory impairments, can also be obtained when detected.

The INFA program also allows for further risk stratification and identification of cases with complex care needs. These patients are additionally managed by a team with specialised geriatric medicine expertise, the Advanced Practice Nurse (APN)-led GeriCARE service, supported by geriatricians, with capabilities for performing CGA and guiding discharge planning. Discharge planning is coordinated by Ward Resource Managers, a role fulfilled by senior nurses. Underpinning the INFA program, training for physicians and nurses is supported by the institution’s Nursing Education department and physician leads for training initiatives.

Implementation research framework

Implementation research in both phases will be anchored in the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [39] and its Outcomes Addendum [40] The CFIR has been one of the more widely used determinant frameworks to assess contextual factors for successful implementation, inform choice of implementation strategies that best mitigate contextual determinants and guide the evaluation of implementation outcomes [40]. This study will use the most recent version with updated domains and constructs [39].

Pre-implementation phase

Study component 1: qualitative study (implementation research)

Study objectives

We aim to identify anticipated contextual barriers and facilitators to implementation. Specifically, we aim to elicit CFIR determinants of antecedent assessments (acceptability, appropriateness, feasibility, implementation climate and implementation readiness) and the anticipated implementation outcome on adoptability and implementability. We will include insights on sustainability and scalability if they arise. The definitions of these terminologies are in Supplementary Appendix Table 1. Subsequently, we will select, specify, and develop implementation strategies to mitigate the barriers and leverage facilitators.

Study sample

Purposive sampling will be employed, and sample size will be guided by the concept of sufficiency [41]. Study participants will be frontline implementers across levels of seniority and inter-professional groups (nurses, doctors, allied health professionals), including healthcare administrators who are part of the hospital leadership team and are decision-makers for implementing INFA. They will be selected based on their anticipated involvement in the implementation of INFA and have sufficient inpatient work experience to share anticipated barriers and facilitators. The authors do not belong to the specialty departments or wards of the frontline implementers which enables an independent evaluation by the authors. Nevertheless, some authors may have had prior encounters with some study participants for the purpose of ground-sensing and pre-engagements to inform the development and planning of the programme and its implementation.

Data collection

Face-to-face in-depth interviews (IDIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) will be conducted by trained qualitative researchers (CL, MLG, AS, JYT, SYC). These data collection sessions will be held in meeting rooms within the hospital premises, ensuring privacy and minimal disturbance. All participants only participated once via an IDI or an FGD. CL (MRCP, Mmed) and SYC (MD) are female geriatricians who work in the acute hospital setting; MLG (MD, MPH) is a female researcher with clinical background alongside a master’s degree in public health; AS (MGer) is a male researcher with social sciences background and has a master’s degree in gerontology; JYT (B.Sc) is a female researcher with a background in life sciences. MLG, AS, JYT, and SYC have accumulated experience in qualitative research. CL has received qualitative training prior to conducting the qualitative research. All IDIs and FGDs will be audio recorded and field notes will be captured during the data collection sessions and as part of de-briefs among researchers after completion of each IDI and FGD. Each IDI and FGD will last around 60–120 min. Only the participants and researchers will be present during each session.

The updated CFIR and its Outcomes Addendum (antecedent assessments, anticipated implementation outcomes) will inform the semi-structured topic guides [39]. Supplementary Appendix Table 1 summarises the qualitative topics elicited from each stakeholder group. The topic guides will be pilot tested with researchers and professionals to gather feedback and inform refinements prior to data collection. A patient journey diagram will also be used to facilitate participants’ understanding of the INFA program.

Data analysis

This study will apply rapid qualitative inquiry (RQI) through a deductive-inductive analytical approach to facilitate timely reporting of actionable findings to decision makers on adaptations to the intervention and implementation [42]. Firstly, deductive analysis will be guided by the updated CFIR and its Outcomes Addendum. We will conduct directed deductive coding from audiotapes (Rapid Identification of Themes from Audio Recordings (RITA)) while referencing a codebook that lists and defines a-priori codes including selected CFIR constructs, anticipated implementation outcomes and antecedent assessments. Thereafter, we will employ mind-mapping to facilitate inductive sense-making for deriving themes and to categorise findings by CFIR’s antecedent assessments and implementation outcomes. The Rapid Research, Evaluation and Appraisal Lab (RREAL) sheet would be used as a table-based approach to document and narrate the findings from each session [42, 43]. These analytical processes will be repeated for each IDI and FGD, before employing framework analysis to triangulate and synthesise findings across the RREALs [44]. Microsoft Excel will be used for data management.

For analytical rigor, data from each session will be coded by two trained researchers independently before being reviewed by an independent third analyst to enable investigator triangulation. Informant feedback will be obtained to ensure credibility of findings via circulation of the RREAL sheet. We will test the dependability of the rapid analysis technique by comparing the codes against the coding of a small subset of FGDs and IDIs using full verbatim transcripts [42, 43, 45].

We will adhere to methodological guidance provided by experts on rapid qualitative techniques [42, 43, 45]. See Supplementary Appendix Table 1 on tools employed under the RQI.

Study component 2: select and specify implementation strategies

Implementation strategies are defined as “methods or techniques used to enhance the adoption, implementation, and sustainability of a clinical program or practice” [46]. Anticipated barriers and facilitators will be mapped to determinant constructs in the updated CFIR across five domains (innovation, outer setting, inner setting, individuals, implementation process) [39, 40]. Subsequently, we will match CFIR-identified determinants to Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change compilation (ERIC) strategies using the CFIR-ERIC Matching Tool. Examples of implementation strategies include the development of educational materials and conduct of training sessions, patient and caregiver education, and the development of IT tools (e.g., INFA EPIC tool). Using a collaborative approach, stakeholders (researchers, implementation leads, frontline healthcare providers) will select ERIC implementation strategies and specify them across 6 domains (actor, action, action target, dose, temporality, implementation outcomes) [47]. The selected implementation strategies will be developed according to their specifications for the implementation phase to mitigate anticipated contextual barriers and leverage facilitators.

Implementation phase (15 months)

Objectives

First, we aim to conduct implementation research to evaluate implementation outcomes (adoption, implementation, sustainability, scalability), as well as fidelity of selected implementation strategies and their attribution to implementation outcomes. Second, we will evaluate the effectiveness of the core functions of the programme. Third, the logic model and theory of change will be refined from the current version (Fig. 2).

Study design and participants

The implementation phase employs a hybrid effectiveness-implementation design [48–52], guided by the updated Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) and its Outcomes Addendum [39, 40]. Effectiveness pertains to study components 1 to 4 and implementation research pertains to study component 5. We utilize a mixed-methods approach based on the principle of complementarity to achieve our study objectives. Both qualitative and quantitative data are collected concurrently, given equal importance, analyzed separately, and integrated during the interpretation of the findings. By using mixed methods, we can explore the contextual and programmatic factors affecting implementation outcomes through qualitative approaches, while quantitative methods allow us to measure the processes and outcomes of the INFA program.

Study sample

The INFA program will be implemented in three wards: General Medicine, General Surgery and Orthopaedic Surgery, with corresponding wards in the same hospital serving as controls and continuing with usual care. All participants will be acute admissions through the Emergency Department (ED). As part of routine care, ED nurses will screen patients aged 65 years or older using the Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS). Those with a CFS score of 4 to 6 who are subsequently admitted to one of the designated study wards will be considered for inclusion. Admission to a ward is determined solely by the hospital’s usual ED and bed management processes, independent of the study. Recruitment will take place in the ward, where study personnel will confirm eligibility and obtain informed consent before enrolment.

Study component 1: process indicators

Objectives

This component aims on evaluating the process of care within the INFA program and assessing the effectiveness of the implementation strategies employed.

Data collection and analysis

We will use a comprehensive list of indicators to monitor and track the fidelity of executing the specified dimensions (actor, action, action target, dose, temporality) of each selected ERIC implementation strategy [53]. Study component 2: patient and caregiver outcomes

Objectives

This study employs a quasi-experimental, controlled pre-post study design to evaluate the impact of the INFA program on patient and caregiver outcomes. A quasi-experimental study design is adopted due to practical constraints that preclude individual-level randomization. The INFA program is implemented at the ward level, making it challenging to randomize patients within the same ward to different care models without introducing contamination effects and disrupting routine care processes. Additionally, ward-level randomization is also not feasible due to hospital operational constraints, such as bed management policies and variability in patient case mix. Participants are therefore recruited based on admission to either an intervention or control ward, which reflect real-world service delivery contexts.

The primary outcome of interest will be the participants’ functional status at six months post-discharge, assessed using the Modified Barthel Index (MBI) [54], a widely used 10-item measure of basic activities of daily living with scores ranging from 0–100 (higher scores indicate greater independence). Secondary outcomes will include health-related quality of life (HRQoL) using the five-level version of the EQ-5D-5L [55] instrument, a five-dimension, five-level descriptive instrument that generates health utility values and caregiver burden using the Zarit Burden Index [56], a validated questionnaire commonly used to measure perceived burden among caregivers.

Sample size

Our sample size calculation is based on the primary outcome, the Modified Barthel Index (MBI) score, measured six months post-discharge. We use the minimally clinically important difference (MCID) of a 10-point change in the mean MBI score [57], but chose a more conservative estimate of a 7-point change (standard deviation = 20) [58] as the effect size may be attenuated over six months in a frail inpatient cohort. With a two-sided 5% significance level and 80% power, 150 participants per arm are required, allowing for an estimated 20% dropout rate.

Data collection

Data on functional status and caregiver burden will be collected face-to-face or via phone call at three time points by trained research staff: at admission, at discharge, and six months post-discharge. HRQoL will be collected at discharge and six-months post-discharge. A six-month duration [59, 60] for our study is likely sufficient for us to see meaningful effects of the INFA program on these outcomes, while minimising drop-outs that could affect statistical power.

Data analysis

For the primary outcome of functional status, measured by the MBI, longitudinal analyses using mixed effects models will be conducted to assess changes over time. Given that mortality may lead to non-random dropout and bias in longitudinal estimates, we will also explore joint modeling [61, 62] approaches that simultaneously evaluate longitudinal changes in MBI and account for mortality as a competing risk. This will allow us to assess for functional decline while adjusting for survival-related bias, where appropriate.

Caregiver burden (Zarit Burden Index) will be analysed separately using linear mixed models for repeated measures, adjusting for baseline covariates. HRQoL will be assessed using utility values from the EQ-5D five-level version (EQ-5D-5L), derived using the crosswalk methodology to map onto health-state preference values locally validated using the three-level version [63, 64]. Changes in HRQoL will be analysed using regression models adjusted for baseline covariates.

Study component 3: healthcare utilization

Objectives

To evaluate the impact of INFA participation on the number of unplanned hospital readmissions and length of stay, and number of emergency department visits, specialist outpatient clinic (SOC) visits, and public primary care visits at 6-months discharge.

Data collection

The data will be collected by trained research assistants during the admission and by phone call post-discharge. Where possible, patients will be the primary informants. LOS data, readmissions and ED visits will be obtained from hospital records. Number of unplanned hospital readmissions and length of stay will be from administrative data (hospital records). Counts of healthcare use for SOC and primary care will be based on self-reported data collected via surveys at INFA enrolment and 6-months post-discharge from INFA.

Data analysis

Healthcare utilization outcomes, including unplanned hospital readmissions, ED visits, and LOS, will be regarded as count variables and analysed using a difference-in-differences (DiD) approach. This method will compare the average change in effect for the INFA intervention group to the average change in effect for the control group over time, specifically from six months before admission to six months after discharge. To ensure the robustness of the analysis, we will check that key DiD assumptions hold. These include parallel trends (visually inspecting the data to ensure both groups exhibit similar trends in healthcare utilisation before the intervention), no anticipation effect (allocation to the intervention is not associated with the outcomes), and stable composition (the characteristics of both groups should remain comparable over the study timeframe). The results will be presented as incidence-rate ratios (IRRs) and their corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses will be conducted using Stata/SE 16.1, with a significance level of 5%.

Study component 4: cost-effectiveness analyses

Objectives

Study Component 4 aims to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of the INFA program compared to usual care, adopting a societal perspective. The analysis will use QALYs derived from EQ-5D-5L scores and costs measured over the six-month period before admission and the six months following discharge. “Usual care” refers to the existing medical, nursing, and allied health care delivered during the inpatient stay, largely driven by current practices without the structured screening and interventions provided through the INFA program.

Study source and cost components

Trained research assistants will perform data collection. Data will be obtained using the Client Service Receipt Inventory (CSRI), a comprehensive tool designed to describe and measure service utilization patterns and estimate associated costs across healthcare, social care, and community settings [65, 66]. Additional data will be obtained from patients and caregiver surveys and hospital administrative records. Supplementary Appendix Table 2 will summarise the key cost categories, variables, and data sources used for cost estimation.

Additionally, the program cost per patient will be estimated. This will include (1) INFA screening; (2) Nurse-led post-screening intervention protocols; (3) GeriCARE and geriatrician referrals; and (4) Discharge and follow-up planning. These costs will be aggregated from financial statements, covering operating expenses, manpower, and equipment costs.

Data analysis

Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) will be calculated using the EQ-5D-5L. Data will be collected at discharge and 6-months post-discharge. Utility values for the EQ-5D-5L will be derived using Singaporean value sets [63, 64], ranging from less than 0 (poor health) to 1 (perfect health). The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) will be calculated by taking the ratio between incremental cost and incremental QALYs, comparing values six months before and after INFA enrolment.

Data collection and analysis

Study component 5 is not part of the quasi-experimental study design described above that evaluates quantitative effectiveness outcomes using a control arm and an intervention arm. This component here only involves the intervention arm that will deliver both INFA’s core functions and have implementation strategies. Ideally, a comparator arm for this study component could be one with the INFA intervention but without any implementation strategies. It will not be feasible to construct such an arm for this study due to resource constraints. Next, we will not apply a before-after study design within the intervention arm to test the effects of strategies. This is because we conceptualise the INFA program (core functions, intervention components) to be tied closely to implementation strategies. For instance, it may be unethical to deliver the screening and care without the strategies on education and training. It may also be impractical to execute the programme without the strategies on changes to IT systems and workflows.

First, to assess fidelity of implementation strategies, we will use a comprehensive list of indicators to monitor and track the fidelity of executing the specified dimensions (actor, action, action target, dose, temporality) of each selected ERIC implementation strategy [47]. In other words, we review if each strategy was delivered in the implementation phase as it was intended in the pre-implementation phase [46]. Indicators will be collected by implementation leads, frontline implementers and the study team on a regular basis, and reviewed by the study team. Deviations will be documented and discussed among the study team and implementers. Adaptations to how the strategies are specified and executed during the implementation phase will be documented [47].

Second, we will evaluate the effectiveness of the strategies and their attribution to implementation outcomes utilising information from both the qualitative component and the quantitative process indicators on implementation of strategies. We will assess this using a combination of surveys with implementers of the intervention arm, as well as FGDs/IDIs with deliverers and recipients of the intervention arm. To elaborate, for example, to assess the ERIC strategy on conducting educational meetings, we may gather feedback (duration and frequency, usefulness to encourage adoption, suggestions for improvement, etc.) from frontline healthcare workers who attended them [47]. Qualitative data collection (FGDs, IDIs) will be from both implementers and/or older adults depending on the actors and recipients of the selected strategy. We will employ similar qualitative methods described in the preceding section.

Third, we will conduct qualitative research to elicit the perspectives on the extent of attainment of implementation outcomes, as well as barrier and facilitators of achieving the implementation outcomes. The qualitative perspectives will be from implementers and older adults. Sampling methods for the qualitative data collection will be similar to that described in the pre-implementation phase. Study participants will comprise frontline healthcare providers (nurses, doctors, allied health professionals) and the nursing leadership. Ward nurses would need to have administered the INFA geriatric syndrome screening to at least five patients. Unlike the preceding phase, we will interview older adults. They will be selected based on being communicative and able to share their experience on receiving the INFA care model. Data analysis will also be similar to that described under the pre-implementation phase and underpinned by the updated CFIR and Outcomes Addendum. In contrast to the pre-implementation phase where we elicit anticipated contextual determinants, we will transition to eliciting perspectives via face-to-face FGDs and IDIs on actual contextual determinants in achieving implementation outcomes (see Supplementary Appendix Table 1). Pragmatic qualitative techniques will be employed in this phase for timely reporting of findings to inform scalability to more wards within the study’s hospital and potentially to other hospitals in Singapore.

Overall, data from the 3 sub-components will be triangulated to present a narrative on the fidelity of the strategies, their attribution towards implementation outcomes, level of attainment of implementation outcomes, and hindrances/enablers to implementation outcomes. A summary of the unit of analysis of implementation outcomes will be included in Supplementary Appendix Table 3.

Discussion

The INFA program is a novel intervention that expands the scope of current inpatient models of care by using frailty status as a means of identifying patients for geriatric syndrome screening and facilitating access to specialized geriatric specialist care, thus expanding the benefits of CGA to a larger patient population. Existing health service delivery models may not be sufficient to adequately address the needs of frail older adults in the hospital. Furthermore, efficient workflows in the inpatient setting to identify frailty and conduct CGA-based interventions is lacking. Hence, the INFA program is intentionally designed to have a set of core principles for frailty care to allow for a more systematic approach to patient care that is grounded in patient-specific needs and to optimize the allocation of resources to frail patients most at risk for adverse outcomes from hospitalization. Using implementation research, contextual barriers and enablers elicited prior to implementation and assessing the fidelity and effectiveness of implementation strategies can help improve the implementation of the INFA program into routine inpatient clinical practice.

Strengths and limitations

The strength of the implementation phase is the use of a hybrid effectiveness-implementation design. This design allows for the evaluation of both the effectiveness of the intervention and the implementation process. By combining these aspects, the study can provide a comprehensive understanding of not only how well the program works but also the contextual factors that influence its success. The mixed-methods approach, which integrates both qualitative and quantitative data, enhances the robustness of the study. This approach allows for the triangulation of data, where qualitative insights can explain and contextualize quantitative findings, leading to a more nuanced and comprehensive understanding of the intervention’s impact.

In addition, the integration of a pre-implementation phase as part of assessing the implementation context helps identify anticipated barriers and facilitators prior to the actual implementation of the program. This in turn aids with informing, the selection, specification, and sequencing of implementation strategies to address the pre-empted barriers and facilitators, as well as the adaptations of the innovation prior to implementation. These collectively promote implementation success and adoption among healthcare providers, patients, and caregivers [67]. In doing so, our study would also contribute towards demonstrating the utility of leveraging the updated CFIR alongside the CFIR-ERIC matching process for the development of implementation strategies in addressing contextual barriers and facilitators. The reporting of these implementation strategies and its associated specifications would further contribute to bridging current knowledge gaps in extant literature in this regard [68].

Furthermore, the adoption of the maximum variation purposive sampling strategy for the implementation research component enables the engagement of a broad range of pertinent stakeholders situated across various levels of the implementation context (i.e., healthcare providers from different disciplines, seniority levels, and leadership positions, as well as patient participants). This facilitates capturing diverse and integral perspectives alongside enhancing the relevance and applicability of the findings.

Lastly, by employing pragmatic and rapid qualitative research techniques underpinned by a team-based approach of trained and qualified analysts for data collection and analysis, enables both study rigor and resource efficiency to be attained. As a result, timely reporting of useful and actionable findings to the implementation team as well as to inform the development of implementation strategies is enabled. Further, leveraging multiple analysts to verify coding of findings to the CFIR constructs and establishing coding consensus helps ensure consistency, thereby enhancing rigor and dependability of data interpretations. Through such collaborative analysis, investigator triangulation is enabled by counteracting potential presumptions of a single researcher investigation [69], alongside enhancing the researchers’ reflexive engagement with the data.

The study protocol for the INFA program has several limitations. First, the quasi-experimental design may lead to selection bias and unmeasured confounding. However, we will adjust for baseline differences and control for key covariates to minimize these effects. Second, relying on wards within a single hospital could limit generalizability to other settings with different resources or workflows. To mitigate this, we will document context-specific factors in detail and compare findings with similar studies to identify commonalities and differences. Third, rapid qualitative research conducted may constrain the depth of contextual insights, but we will use multiple data collection methods, triangulate sources, and train interviewers thoroughly. The evidence to date has shown that rapid deductive CFIR analysis allows for timely data collection and analysis while using fewer resources compared to traditional methods without compromising rigor. Finally, self-reported data introduce the possibility of recall bias; we will cross-verify with administrative records and other objective measures wherever feasible to improve accuracy.

Conclusions

In summary, the INFA program represents a comprehensive and systematic approach to identifying and managing frailty among older adults in acute hospital settings, and provides a means for integrating care between acute care settings, community and social care partners through comprehensive discharge planning. By integrating routine frailty screening, geriatric syndrome assessments, and targeted interventions within the existing hospital workflow, the program aims to improve health outcomes and optimize healthcare utilization. With the core principles of frailty care, a menu of implementation strategies, the rich repository of contextual barriers and enablers and a methodological framework on how to roll out and evaluate a frailty program, this evaluation study aims to be a valuable blueprint for other health systems aiming to become ‘frailty-ready’. This will be crucial for implementers, hospital administrators and policymakers as it offers evidence-based guidance for scaling and sustaining similar frailty management programs in the inpatient healthcare setting, adapting to their own resources and workflows. Lessons learned from this study will contribute to the broader understanding of how to effectively integrate frailty care into inpatient practice, reinforcing the essential role of acute hospitals in the continuum of care for frail older adults. These insights will potentially inform future research and policy initiatives, ultimately improving frailty care for this vulnerable population.
