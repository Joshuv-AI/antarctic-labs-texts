Dysmenorrhea patients’ experience with work productivity impact and treatment in Japan
Authors: Osamu Hiraike, Daisuke Goto, Masahiko Uchiyama, Shoko Takahashi, Yutaka Osuga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNearly 50% of Japanese women report impaired work performance due to menstrual symptoms. Our objectives were to describe the real-world experiences of patients living with dysmenorrhea with a focus on work productivity and its association with treatments. Our exploratory objective was to understand the coping methods for menstrual pain in Japanese women.MethodsUsing an anonymized database consisting of linked Japanese health insurance claims and Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire - General Health (WPAI-GH) responses for women aged between 18 and 50 years, we conducted descriptive analyses of work productivity impact among patients with dysmenorrhea, other chronic diseases and individuals without medical records. Analysis of work productivity impact was conducted per dysmenorrhea treatment. The analyses were descriptive and unadjusted thus the generalizability and external validity of differences between conditions and treatments are limited.ResultsAmong 9,235 individuals in the employer-sponsored insurance claims database, 956 (10.4%) had diagnosed dysmenorrhea. The unadjusted mean WPAI-GH scores for the dysmenorrhea patient population demonstrated absenteeism at 2.5 ± 0.3% (mean ± standard error) and presenteeism at 26.8 ± 0.9%, similar to groups with mental disorders, headaches, or low back pain. Dysmenorrhea patients receiving low-dose estrogen progestin, also known as combined oral contraceptives, was the largest treatment group and had the lowest mean absenteeism and presenteeism scores among treatment groups in this unadjusted descriptive analysis. In 5,471 respondents who responded on coping methods for menstrual pain, 2,250 (41.1%) used over-the-counter medications, while 329 (6.0%) sought medical care.ConclusionThe productivity impact observed in patients with dysmenorrhea was substantial and found to be similar to that of patient groups with major chronic conditions such as mental disorders, headaches, and low back pain. We further found that dysmenorrhea patients’ productivity outcomes varied across treatment groups. Whilst prospective studies with adjustment for confounding factors are needed to establish the generalizability of differences between treatment- and condition-based subgroups, our findings underscore the need for greater awareness of dysmenorrhea as a condition that deserves medical attention, and timely medical care may help reduce the burden and improve the quality of life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04588-8.Keywords: Dysmenorrhea, Low-dose estrogen–progestin, Combined oral contraceptives, Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire - General Health (WPAI), Menstrual pain, Treatment pattern, Presenteeism, Absenteeism, Work productivity loss, Activity impairment

Background

Approximately eight million Japanese women are estimated to have dysmenorrhea, and only about 11%-20% receive treatment [1–4]. Despite Japan’s universal health insurance system that provides access to care with limited out-of-pocket expenses for all residents, the vast majority of women live with symptoms without receiving a diagnosis or treatment [4, 5].

Dysmenorrhea is a condition that occurs during menstruation, characterized mainly by lower abdominal and back pain, and may include bloating, nausea, headache, fatigue, loss of appetite, irritability, diarrhea, and depressive symptoms [6]. It affects work productivity, particularly the percentage of reduced work efficiency while present at work due to health reasons (presenteeism) [4, 5, 7–10]. Although more than 50% of Japanese women are aware of menstrual abnormalities, the majority remain untreated [5]. A survey of 3,324 Japanese working women reported that 44.6% of patients who did not seek medical attention did so because they believed menstruation was something that should be endured [11]. Reasons for not seeking treatment include the idea that women should endure menstrual pain, the low recognition of menstrual-related symptoms, such as menstrual pain and premenstrual syndrome (PMS) as medical conditions, a lack of awareness about treatment’s effectiveness, reluctance to use low-dose estrogen–progestin, also known as combined oral contraceptives, and seek treatment from a gynecologist [12–14]. Among patients who regularly visit gynecologists, many began doing so based on recommendations from health check-ups and/or non- gynecologist physicians including occupational health physicians who serve as consultants for employers. The rate of receiving treatment is at an approximately two-fold than patients who initiated visits on their own [5]. Recommendations from healthcare professionals often serve as a trigger for women to seek care, highlighting the important role that healthcare providers play.

In Japan, treatment options for dysmenorrhea include hormonal agents, analgesics, hemostatic agents, Kampo medicine (a traditional herbal medicine with origins in Chinese medicine, subsequently adapted and developed in Japan), and gynecologic surgery [15]. Pharmacological treatments, such as levonorgestrel intrauterine system (LNG-IUS), low-dose estrogen progestin, have been reported to improve work productivity in Japanese women [16]. Therefore, when selecting a treatment for dysmenorrhea, it is important to assess patients’ daily life challenges, work productivity impact. In Japan, the Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare have also identified the promotion of women’s health in the workplace as a priority issue [17–19]. Evidence that demonstrates the relationship between treatment and work productivity may contribute to better care for women with dysmenorrhea through improved treatment guidelines and medical policies based on scientific evidence.

Objectives

The primary objectives of the current study were to describe the real-world experiences of patients living with dysmenorrhea, focusing on work productivity and its correlation with treatments as appeared in Japanese survey and claims data. Our exploratory objective was to understand the coping methods for menstrual pain in Japanese women using separately administered survey responses.

Materials and methods

Primary objectives: data source, study population and inclusion criteria

The present study used an existing database that was previously developed by DeSC Healthcare, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan; hereafter referred to as DeSC). This database consists of medical insurance claims data from selected Japanese health insurance societies linked with the results of online surveys conducted via DeSC’s smartphone application “kencom”; the survey instruments are discussed in detail later in the section entitled Survey items for the primary objectives. The database for this study comprised insurance claims data from employer-sponsored plans, and included all enrollees from whom questionnaire responses were obtained regardless of whether they have any specific disease. The database contained demographic characteristics, such as sex and age, and medical insurance claims data, including diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), medical procedures, and prescription drug information.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: from the database, we included records of individuals who had responded to the survey at least once between July 2020 and July 2022 and were aged 18 to 50 years. Insurance claims of included patients were obtained for the time period between July 1, 2020 and June 30, 2022 (See Figure 1a).

Exploratory objective: Data source, study population and inclusion criteria

We analyzed participants’ responses to a question on coping methods with menstrual pain; the responses were part of the DeSC dataset. We analyzed responses from those who responded to the menstrual pain coping question regardless of whether they had a diagnosis of dysmenorrhea. The inclusion criteria that we used for the primary endpoint (described in the previous subsection) were also used for this analysis (See Fig. 1a).

Ethics approval

The data used in this study were sourced from existing anonymized databases; the authors received permission for secondary use from the owner of the data. In accordance with the Ethical Guidelines for Life Sciences and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects in Japan [20], this study was classified as exempt from review and approval by an institutional ethics committee.

Primary objectives: survey data

The survey items included age, body mass index (BMI;kg/m2), occupation, the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment Questionnaire-General Health (WPAI-GH) [21], EuroQol 5 dimensions 5-level (EQ-5D-5L)[22, 23]. The medical diagnoses for which medical consultations were sought during the study period, prescribed medications, and surgeries. Age was calculated based on the first day of the month of birth, and the participant’s age was defined as that on the date of the questionnaire response.

We note that the WPAI-GH instrument’s recall period is 7 days. With a large number of samples in the dataset, the survey timing is assumed to be equally distributed over women’s menstruation cycles, and the WPAI-GH scores therefore reflect the average productivity impact of dysmenorrhea across the menstrual cycle, including both symptomatic and asymptomatic phases, capturing the impact of the chronic nature of the disease.

The questionnaire surveys (WPAI-GH and EQ-5D-5L) were conducted twice a year, in June and December, over the two-year study period, resulting in four survey waves with a response deadline extending to the following month (Figure 2a). No participant responded more than once, and individuals who responded at either time point were included in the analysis. WPAI-GH scores were calculated using responses to the questionnaire that asked about the 7 days preceding the survey, and were calculated for the following four domains: 1) the percentage of work time missed due to health reasons (absenteeism), 2) the percentage of reduced work efficiency while present at work due to health reasons (presenteeism), 3) the percentage of overall work productivity loss due to health reasons (work productivity loss), and 4) the percentage of reduced activity due to health reasons (activity impairment) (see Supplementary Material 1 for details). WPAI-GH outcomes were calculated using the validated scoring algorithm described by Reilly et al. (1993), with absenteeism calculated as Q2/(Q2+Q4), presenteeism as Q5/10, overall work productivity loss as Q2/(Q2+Q4)+[(1−Q2/(Q2+Q4))×(Q5/10)], and activity impairment as Q6/10. The analytic population differed across WPAI-GH domains depending on employment status and the availability of valid questionnaire responses.

Primary objectives: linkage between survey data and insurance claims records, and diagnosis-based sub-grouping of patients

The prescribed medications were identified for each participant from insurance claim records as the drug(s) most recently prescribed within the 120 days prior to the date of responding to the questionnaire. In our dataset, the “progestin” category refers to oral progestins only (e.g., medroxyprogesterone acetate, dydrogesterone) (see Supplementary Material 2). Patients with any gynecologic surgery procedures for the treatment of dysmenorrhea performed prior to the questionnaire survey date were categorized into the surgery group regardless of concurrent pharmacological treatments.

Patients with dysmenorrhea were defined as those who had at least one outpatient visit with an ICD-10 code for primary dysmenorrhea (N944), secondary dysmenorrhea (N945), or unspecified dysmenorrhea (N946) during the study period. The survey identified the following conditions among participants in addition to dysmenorrhea; malignancies, diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders, cardiovascular diseases, mental disorders, such as depression and schizophrenia, sleep disorders, migraine, non-migraine headaches, allergic rhinitis (including hay fever), low back pain, constipation, collagen diseases (including systemic lupus erythematosus, rheumatoid arthritis, and Sjögren’s syndrome), respiratory diseases, including asthma, and PMS; all defined as having at least one outpatient consultation for the condition during the study period. Detailed definitions are listed in Supplementary Material 3. In addition, complications related to the female reproductive system were recorded. Patients could have multiple conditions.

Exploratory objective: survey data

A survey on coping methods for menstrual pain was conducted in June 2022. The participants were asked to recall how they had managed menstruation over the one-year period from January to December 2021 (Fig. 2b). This was a non-validated single-question multiple-choice survey for which respondents were asked to choose their coping methods from the choices shown in Supplementary Material 4.

Primary objectives: analytical methods and statistical analyses

For the primary objectives, first, all female respondents aged 18–50 years were classified as either “individuals with medical records” (at least one outpatient visit with a corresponding claim during the 2-year observation period) or “individuals without medical records” (no recorded outpatient visits). Second, among individuals with medical records, participants were classified according to the presence or absence of each condition (including dysmenorrhea and other chronic diseases), and the mean WPAI-GH and EQ-5D-5 L scores were calculated for those with and without each condition (Fig. 1a). Third, within patients with dysmenorrhea, patients were classified into treatment subgroups based on the latest prescription within 120 days prior to the WPAI-GH survey to describe WPAI-GH and EQ-5D-5 L outcomes by treatment category (Fig. 1b). It is noted that unadjusted analyses of condition-based groups should not be used to compare differences between conditions as differences between groups are not generalizable to differences between conditions primarily because no control of patient characteristics such as severity adjustment was completed.

Our data included claims submitted to Japan’s universal health insurance system, which covers virtually all medically necessary procedures and prescriptions rendered and given to the residents of the country. Therefore, in this study, individuals without medical records were assumed to have had no illnesses or health conditions requiring medical attention during the relevant period. Participants were classified according to the presence or absence of each condition, irrespective of coexisting conditions (Please see Fig. 1a for a list of the conditions). The unadjusted mean WPAI-GH domain scores (absenteeism, presenteeism, work productivity loss, and activity impairment) were calculated for each condition-based group. Further analyses calculated differences in the unadjusted WPAI-GH scores between those with a condition and without for each condition individually. The same set of analyses was completed for the two broader subgroups; those without any medical records and all others with any medical records. (Please note that the aforementioned groups with medical records were not necessarily mutually exclusive.) In addition, summary statistics were visualized for the aforementioned groups to allow a comprehensive view of WPAI-GH scores.

The individuals without medical records were intended to serve as a reference group to conduct unadjusted descriptive assessment of WPAI-GH scores. Student’s t test was used to describe differences between groups; we note that the statistics were conducted using unadjusted data. We conducted statistical analysis using R (version 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria [https://www.R-project.org/]).

Statistical tests between participants with and without conditions were performed for each WPAI-GH and EQ-5D-5 L measure using the Student’s t-test. WPAI-GH and EQ-5D-5 L measures in each treatment group of patients with dysmenorrhea were evaluated against those of individuals without medical records using the Student’s t-test. The t-test was conducted two-sided at the 5% level. Numerical variables are presented as the mean ± standard error, and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). No adjustment for confounders or multiple testing were performed; p-values are reported without correction for multiplicity and confounding; due to the risk of false positive results, the results should be interpreted as descriptive rather than comparative.

Exploratory objective: analytical methods and statistical analysis

We conducted an unadjusted summary analysis of responders’ coping methods for menstrual pain; the survey was conducted in the female population regardless of dysmenorrhea diagnosis or lack thereof including those without reported menstrual pain. Individuals who satisfied this study’s inclusion criteria (Please see Fig. 1a), had also responded to the survey. The survey respondents in this analysis were aged 18 to 50 years old; thus, this survey reflects coping behaviors in a general working-age female population. For the summary analysis, we counted the number and percentage of patients for each frequently observed coping method (including over-the-counter medication, resting, and consultation with and treatment by a physician). Since the sample is not limited to dysmenorrhea patients, the findings are not meant to represent dysmenorrhea patients’ coping methods or treatment seeking behavior.

Discussion

WPAI-GH in dysmenorrhea

Work productivity loss may be divided into two components: absenteeism, which is absence from work, and presenteeism, which is reduced productivity at work due to health issues. Previous studies indicated that approximately 60% of economic losses caused by illness were attributable to presenteeism, making it a serious issue [24, 25].

Our findings indicated high presenteeism in patients with dysmenorrhea. The same was also found for the patient group with PMS. These results are supported by previous studies that also reported an association between menstrual-related symptoms and high presenteeism [26], and our results imply that many individuals continued to work despite feeling unwell. The severity of presenteeism in the dysmenorrhea patient group was similar to that in groups with mental disorders, headaches, or low back pain. In contrast, the patient group with dysmenorrhea had a 2.5% absenteeism score in our study, the second smallest in the conditions analyzed.

This discrepancy between absenteeism and presenteeism, and the findings in productivity loss and activity impairment suggest that dysmenorrhea patients continued to work despite the sizable impact that they experienced. There are a number of previous studies that indicate that Japanese women tend to endure menstrual symptoms; some of the reasons include lack of disease awareness and hesitancy in requesting sick-leave for menstruation-related conditions [27–30], and lack of sympathy from many of those who do not suffer from menstrual symptoms including both women and men [31].

In the present study, work productivity loss and activity impairment found in the dysmenorrhea population were 28.0% and 27.5% according to WPAI-GH results, respectively; demonstrating that the patient group with dysmenorrhea experienced sizable effects to both work-related and other daily activities. Our analysis found that patient groups with conditions with high impact such as those related to premenstrual syndrome, sleep disorders and migraine in these measures also generally showed high presenteeism. It is noted that unadjusted analyses of condition-based groups should not be used to compare differences between conditions as differences between groups are not generalizable to differences between conditions primarily because no control of patient characteristics such as severity adjustment was completed. Yet, our findings of high presenteeism in groups with conditions such as sleep disorders, migraine, mental disorders, headaches, and low back pain, are consistent with previous findings [32–35]. Our findings demonstrated that the patients with dysmenorrhea experienced markable loss of productivity, activity impairment, and presenteeism. We note that the EQ-5D-5 L, being a short generalized instrument, was unable to capture differences in health-related impact across different conditions (Supplementary Material 8).

Dysmenorrhea’s impact by treatment as measured in WPAI-GH

In our unadjusted descriptive analysis of treatment for dysmenorrhea and WPAI-GH outcomes, the mean absenteeism scores varied across treatment groups. Patients who were treated with the Kampo medicine alone, analgesic agents alone, or progestin alone had a higher mean absenteeism score than individuals without medical records whereas, patients who were treated with LNG-IUS and low-dose estrogen progestin had a lower mean absenteeism score.

The mean presenteeism, work productivity loss and activity impairment scores were highest in the LNG-IUS group, followed by the analgesic agents alone and progestin groups, with lower mean scores observed in the low-dose estrogen progestin alone, no intervention and Kampo medicine alone groups. In this unadjusted analysis, all but the Kampo medicine alone groups demonstrated statistically higher mean presenteeism scores than the individuals without medical records. Similarly, all treatment groups except the Kampo medicine alone groups demonstrated statistically higher mean work productivity loss scores than the individuals without medical records, demonstrating consistency between presenteeism and work productivity loss (Fig. 5c).

These unadjusted and descriptive findings suggested the productivity impact experienced by patients with dysmenorrhea differed across treatment groups as measured by WPAI-GH. While the effectiveness of treatment options cannot be compared as our analyses were unadjusted and did not control for confounding factors, the lower WPAI-GH impact observed in patient groups that used hormonal treatments such as low-dose estrogen progestin and progestin is descriptively consistent with a previous study in which low-dose estrogen progestin treatment was significantly more effective in improving presenteeism, work productivity loss, and activity impairment [16].

Our findings described real-world differences in productivity impact among dysmenorrhea patients who were treated with different treatments while a well-controlled study as opposed to observational unadjusted descriptive studies will be needed to understand the source of differences in productivity impact.

Dysmenorrhea and comorbidities

Our analysis found that 956 professionally diagnosed dysmenorrhea patients out of 9,235 patients who met the inclusion criteria; this rate is lower than that reported in studies using self-reported menstrual pain in school- or community-based samples (de Arruda GT et al.,71.3%;Wang L et al.,66.1%) [36, 37]. This difference demonstrates that a large number of patients are not diagnosed and treated in clinical settings.

In the present study, 29.4% of patients with dysmenorrhea had comorbid mental disorders, such as depression or schizophrenia, and 24.6% had low back pain. This finding is consistent with a study that reported that more than 80% of young women with dysmenorrhea and menorrhagia experienced both physical and psychological issues, including pelvic pain, sleep disorders, mood disorders, diarrhea, and nausea [38] and with other studies reporting psychological impact such as depression and anxiety and heightened susceptibility to other chronic conditions later in life [39–41]. Our findings along with existing evidence show dysmenorrhea patients have high burden from diseases beyond dysmenorrhea. Proactive treatment of dysmenorrhea would be important to alleviate some of their increased disease burden. Because primary, secondary, and unspecified dysmenorrhea were analyzed together based on ICD-10 codes, potential clinical heterogeneity within the dysmenorrhea group might mean that individual impact can significantly vary.

Coping strategy with menstrual pain

The question on coping with menstrual pain was administered to the entire female sample irrespective of their clinical diagnosis with dysmenorrhea, and we found that 91.4% of respondents appeared to manage their symptoms without seeking medical care. In a previous study that compared women who saw a gynecologist with those who did not, the former group showed a significant reduction in the impact on daily life due to their menstrual symptoms, whereas the latter group showed no change [42]. Therefore, while we recognize that the findings from this question might not directly apply to patients with dysmenorrhea diagnosis, the finding highlights the importance of proactive treatment for menstrual pain. Not seeking medical care may be attributable to lack of information regarding dysmenorrhea and treatment. A systematic review reported that the majority of women (> 62%) cited their mother as their primary source of information on dysmenorrhea, with friends being a secondary source (10–65%) [38], suggesting professional sources are not primary. It was reported that women in their 20s to 30s, without a spouse or children and working full-time, showed high expectations for research on both “mental health” and “menstruation” [43]; by communicating that dysmenorrhea is a treatable medical condition and that appropriate management options are available, more patients would be encouraged to seek treatment; thereby relieving the negative impact of dysmenorrhea.

As awareness of dysmenorrhea as a medical condition increases and more individuals seek medical attention, this gap will likely narrow.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The presence or absence of medical conditions was based solely on diagnoses recorded in health insurance claims data and, the severity was not known. Furthermore, only treatments prescribed by physicians and reimbursed by insurance were included in the analysis; therefore, other treatments such as OTC drugs were not captured. In addition, we might have counted the same patients twice or more when they changed their health insurance plans, which primarily occurred when participants changed their jobs but stayed in the survey. The study population was limited to individuals in Japan who held an account in the kencom platform and were covered by health insurance societies participating in the system. The sample did not include those enrolled in Japan’s national health insurance programs that cover individuals who do not receive insurance through their employers. Additionally, responses to WPAI-GH surveys represented fewer than 10% of the approximately 150,000 users in the kencom users. The questionnaire on coping with menstrual pain relied on retrospective reporting of over a one-year period prior to the survey, which may have compromised accuracy due to participants’ difficulty to remember the distant past. The condition-level analyses assessed differences in patients with a given condition to those without, regardless of other comorbidities; thus, the comparison group may have included individuals with multiple conditions. The participants with no medical records may potentially include individuals with undiagnosed dysmenorrhea. We further note that any differences between condition-based groups are not to be seen as differences between conditions as no confounding factors including baseline severity were controlled for between groups. In addition, we performed a large number of unadjusted pairwise comparisons across conditions and treatment groups. Because no adjustments for confounding were applied and our analysis is unadjusted and descriptive, the statistical results of this study should not be interpreted as establishing evidence for comparative assessment of treatment options. We note that given the relatively young study population (mean age approximately 38 years) and the similarity in baseline characteristics between participants with dysmenorrhea and those without medical records, differences in impact observed between the dysmenorrhea population and individuals without medical records serve as a useful benchmark for understanding the current state of disease impact among patients under care for dysmenorrhea.

Conclusions

Patients with dysmenorrhea were observed with impaired work productivity and increased presenteeism and activity impairment; the impact on productivity observed in patients with dysmenorrhea was substantial and comparable impacts were observed in patient groups with major chronic conditions such as mental disorders, headaches, and low back pain. We further found that dysmenorrhea patients’ productivity outcomes varied across treatment groups. These findings on differences between treatment- and condition-based subgroups were based on unadjusted analyses and prospective studies with adjustment for confounding factors are needed to establish generalizability. Yet, underdiagnosis and a large number of comorbidities along with our findings suggest that continued efforts to improve the diagnosis and treatment for dysmenorrhea would be critical to better alleviate the productivity impact. One critical component to improve the quality of life in women with dysmenorrhea would be to increase the awareness that dysmenorrhea is a treatable medical condition and encourage women to seek appropriate medical care rather than enduring dysmenorrheal pain and its impact.
