A Tripartite Framework of Conflict-Induced Stress, Acoustic Hypervigilance, and Ramadan Observance as Convergent Determinants of Circadian Decoherence in Athletes
Authors: Wissem Dhahbi, Karim Chamari, Ismail Dergaa, Khaled Trabelsi, Meriem Gaddas, Achraf Ammar, Ahmed S BaHammam, Helmi Ben Saad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAthletes living and training in active conflict zones face a combination of distinct biological pressures that disrupt the body’s internal 24-hour clock and stress-hormone systems. The constant psychological threat of conflict keeps the body’s stress-hormone axis chronically active, raising cortisol levels, heightening alertness, and making it harder to fall and stay asleep. A key problem in the existing research is that nighttime sleep disruption in conflict zones has often been incorrectly described as ordinary screen-related sleep loss. In reality, government-issued missile warning sirens and emergency phone alerts force athletes fully awake, trigger an acute surge of stress hormones, and, over time, condition the nervous system to treat all nighttime sounds as threats. This pathway, which we call conflict-specific acoustic hypervigilance, is biologically distinct from the sleep disruption caused by voluntary smartphone use. For Muslim athletes in these regions, Ramadan fasting adds further strain: eating and drinking only after sunset shifts the body’s internal timing, causes progressive daytime dehydration, and may delay the hormonal signals that govern sleep. This review argues that when all three pressures operate at the same time, their combined effect on sleep, hormones, and recovery may exceed what any one pressure would cause alone, a state we call circadian decoherence. Our proposed Tripartite Athletic Stress Framework maps how these three stressors interact and amplify each other. We propose two measurable cortisol markers as practical tools for monitoring biological overload in affected athletes. Practical countermeasures, including noise management, adjusted meal timing, strategic napping, and psychological resilience training, are outlined with direct reference to the framework’s intervention targets.

Introduction

Armed conflict constitutes one of the most physiologically severe environmental stressors to which human populations are chronically exposed.1,2 Since 2022, the geographic scope of active armed conflict has expanded substantially across multiple regions, collectively placing tens of millions of individuals under conditions of chronic threat exposure.1 The participation of athletes in high-level competition while residing in an active war zone constitutes one of the most extreme and understudied physiological situations in sports science.3 Unlike classical overtraining or isolated psychological stress, this condition simultaneously combines three mutually reinforcing biological stressors: a chronic life-threatening stress that maximally activates neuroendocrine systems,4,5 an intense physical training-program maintaining permanently high hormonal demand,6 and severe sleep deprivation that destroys the biological architecture essential for recovery.3,7 Conflict exposure activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, leading to sustained cortisol elevation, amygdala hyperactivation, and prefrontal cortex suppression, together with hypervigilance states that profoundly disrupt sleep onset and maintenance.4,8 As noted in the literature, among the hormonal changes induced by sleep loss, there is an increase in cortisol and corticosterone secretion, and a reduction in testosterone and insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1), favoring the establishment of a highly proteolytic environment.3,7,9 Although conflict-related stress has been extensively studied in military and civilian trauma populations, its implications for athlete recovery and performance remain insufficiently examined. Physical activity and sport participation surveys across the Eastern Mediterranean Region (EMR) nevertheless document an established athletic infrastructure within these populations,10 indicating that many athletes continue to train and compete under conditions of acute and chronic conflict exposure that conventional periodization and recovery models do not adequately accommodate.

This review is written with several audiences in mind. Sports scientists and chronobiologists will find a mechanistic framework for studying environments where multiple stressors overlap. Sports medicine physicians and other healthcare providers can use the candidate monitoring markers and intervention targets to track and support athletes affected by conflict. For coaches and performance support staff, the framework informs training load and recovery decisions when athletes train under threat. Athletes themselves may use it to make sense of their own symptoms and to adopt simple self-management steps, such as filtering nighttime alerts or napping at strategic times. It also speaks to sport governing bodies and policy-makers, whose scheduling and welfare provisions have yet to accommodate the demands of religious observance during active conflict. A mechanistically distinct stressor may further compound this neuroendocrine burden. In active conflict-affected regions, athletes rely on continuous digital communication for security monitoring while simultaneously being subject to state-broadcast emergency acoustic alert systems, including missile warning sirens, civil defense broadcasts, and military public-address notifications that deliver sudden, high-intensity acoustic stimuli during nocturnal recovery windows.11,12 These systems produce neurobiologically distinct effects from those generated by voluntary recreational device use.13,14 They precipitate acute sympathetic-adrenomedullary activation, force complete arousal from sleep, mandate behavioral evacuation or shelter-seeking responses that extend wakefulness by 30 to 90 minutes or more per episode, and condition a generalized acoustic hypervigilance such that all subsequent auditory stimuli acquire threat-signal properties through associative learning.8,15 This pathway, designated here as conflict-specific acoustic hypervigilance (CSAH), is mechanistically and clinically distinct from the voluntary, behaviorally mediated pathway of behavioral digital sleep fragmentation (DSF-B), which includes notification-driven micro-arousals and pre-sleep blue-light exposure from recreational screen use.16,17 Conflating these two pathways may mischaracterize the primary mechanism operative in conflict-zone athletes, underestimate the physiological severity of the condition, and misidentify appropriate intervention targets.

For athletes in conflict-affected Muslim-majority nations, a concurrent religious observance introduces distinct physiological demands: approximately two billion Muslims observe Ramadan annually, abstaining from all food and fluid intake between dawn (Fajr) and sunset (Maghrib) for 29 to 30 consecutive days.18 For competitive athletes, this observance entails sustained daytime caloric restriction, progressive hypohydration, and temporally structured meal timing that coincides with ongoing training and competition demands.19 Meta-analytic evidence documents moderate decrements in anaerobic power, sprint performance, and sleep-wake cycle integrity under RIF.20–23 The existing literature, however, has predominantly examined Ramadan intermittent fasting (RIF) in stable, non-conflict contexts, disregarding the broader psychosocial and environmental conditions under which fasting coincides with armed conflict for a proportion of Muslim athletes globally.24 RIF may modify the coupling of peripheral tissue oscillators to the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) through temporal inversion of the feeding-fasting cycle and potential nocturnal melatonin phase delay, a circadian displacement that, when coinciding with conflict-induced chronic psychological stress (CCPS) and CSAH, may produce convergent disruption of a magnitude not yet addressed by existing frameworks.

The convergence of CCPS, the compound sleep-disruption domain encompassing CSAH and DSF-B, and RIF constitutes a challenge to the circadian timing system. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, no prior framework has comprehensively integrated these interacting factors within an athlete-specific performance and recovery model, although a preliminary conceptual outline of this convergence was introduced by Dhahbi et al.25 The resulting hormonal disruption operates on all major endocrine axes simultaneously, creating a state of cascading dysfunction that affects muscular, neurological, metabolic, and cognitive performance.4,26,27 Accordingly, the aims of this narrative review were threefold to (i) synthesize evidence on the independent pathophysiological mechanisms through which each stressor disrupts circadian architecture, hormonal homeostasis, and athletic performance; (ii) advance the triple-stressor convergence hypothesis (TSCH), which posits that the simultaneous operation of these stressors may produce emergent, nonlinear degradation of the circadian timing system and downstream physiological functions; and (iii) introduce the tripartite athletic stress framework (TASF), a novel conceptual model mapping the mechanistic pathways and bidirectional feedback loops through which these stressors interact, with candidate biomarkers for monitoring allostatic overload in affected athletes. A comprehensive literature search (April 25, 2026) across PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus identified no equivalent integrative framework in the published literature.

This narrative review was prepared in accordance with the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) framework, which provides quality criteria for narrative reviews, including justification of importance, statement of aims, description of literature search, referencing, scientific reasoning, and appropriate presentation of data. A completed SANRA self-assessment is available from the corresponding author upon request.28

The Circadian Timing System and Its Vulnerability to Convergent Disruption

The mammalian circadian timing system operates through a hierarchical architecture in which the SCN of the anterior hypothalamus functions as the master pacemaker, entraining peripheral oscillators in virtually every organ system through neuronal, hormonal, and behavioral signals.29,30 The SCN receives photic input from melanopsin-expressing intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells and generates approximately 24-hour oscillations in clock gene expression within the circadian locomotor output cycles kaput (CLOCK), brain and muscle ARNT-like protein 1 (BMAL1), period (PER), and cryptochrome (CRY) gene families, which are transmitted to downstream tissues. Peripheral clocks in the liver, skeletal muscle, pancreas, and adipose tissue are synchronized primarily through feeding-fasting rhythms and glucocorticoid signaling, creating a system in which temporal alignment between central and peripheral oscillators is essential for metabolic coherence.29 Pineal melatonin secretion, dependent on light-dark cycles, is, however, doubly compromised in conflict environments: by chronic cortisol, which partially blocks melatonin receptors, and by the artificial lighting and permanent alert conditions of war zones.31,32

The vulnerability of this system to desynchronization was demonstrated experimentally by Scheer et al,33 who used a forced desynchrony protocol to shift participants’ behavioral schedules by approximately 12 hours relative to their endogenous circadian phase. Under misalignment conditions, participants exhibited a complete reversal of the diurnal cortisol rhythm, a 17% decrease in leptin, a 22% increase in insulin with a concurrent 6% elevation in glucose, and a 20% reduction in sleep efficiency; three of eight participants showed postprandial glucose responses in the prediabetes range.33 These findings indicate that circadian misalignment alone, in the absence of caloric restriction or external stressors, may produce clinically meaningful metabolic and endocrine disruption. In the conflict-zone athlete, this disruption evolves into a “circadian collapse”, characterized by a flattening of the cortisol diurnal gradient4,5 and a state where the homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) index rises measurably from 4–6 weeks of chronic exposure,4,34 further blocking the anabolic gate.35

The skeletal muscle circadian clock carries direct implications for athletic performance. Andrews et al,36 in a murine genetic model, demonstrated that CLOCK and BMAL1 regulate myogenic differentiation 1 (MyoD), the master transcription factor governing myogenesis, through direct binding to the MyoD core enhancer. Mice with disrupted clock function displayed approximately 30% reductions in maximal contractile force, disrupted myofilament organization, decreased structural protein expression, and a 40% reduction in mitochondrial volume. These findings suggest that circadian clock integrity is essential for maintaining skeletal muscle structure and contractile function, a principle directly relevant to athletes whose circadian systems are challenged by the convergent stressors addressed in this review.

The concept of social jetlag, introduced by Roenneberg et al37 through analysis of more than 65,000 participants, provides additional context for understanding chronic circadian misalignment. Social jetlag, defined as the discrepancy between endogenous circadian timing and socially imposed schedules, was independently associated with increased body mass index beyond the effects of sleep duration alone.37 During Ramadan, the temporal restructuring of social, religious, and feeding schedules creates a form of imposed social jetlag that compounds any pre-existing chronotype mismatch. For athletes in conflict zones, where schedules are further disrupted by security alerts, curfews, and infrastructure instability, the misalignment between endogenous circadian preferences and externally imposed temporal constraints is particularly pronounced. This state manifests as a persistent pro-inflammatory response in the peripheral and central nervous systems,27 where pro-inflammatory cytokines like interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α remain significantly elevated, independently disrupting both sleep architecture and insulin signaling.27,34,38

Conflict-Induced Chronic Psychological Stress and Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal Axis Dysregulation

The neuroendocrine stress response, centered on the HPA axis, is a conserved adaptive system that, under acute activation, mobilizes energy substrates, enhances vigilance, and suppresses nonessential physiological processes to promote survival.39 Chronic activation of this system, as observed in populations exposed to ongoing armed conflict, political violence, or forced displacement, produces a state of allostatic overload characterized by sustained glucocorticoid elevation, hippocampal dendritic atrophy, amygdala hypertrophy, and prefrontal cortex functional impairment.4,40 Under chronic war stress, this axis undergoes hypercortisolaemia without the evening physiological nadir that normally allows endocrine recovery,4,5 leading to a circadian collapse and flattening of the cortisol diurnal gradient.4

McEwen4 provided a comprehensive account of the brain as both a target and an orchestrator of the stress response, describing structural remodeling in three critical regions: dendritic retraction and reduced neurogenesis in the hippocampus; dendritic expansion and enhanced synaptic connectivity in the amygdala; and dendritic simplification in the medial prefrontal cortex. These structural changes may translate directly into functional consequences for athletes, including impaired declarative memory and contextual processing, heightened threat detection and emotional reactivity, and reduced executive function, decision-making capacity, and emotion regulation. Sapolsky41 also showed that prolonged glucocorticoid exposure disrupts hippocampal synaptic plasticity and increases neuronal vulnerability, with measurable hippocampal volume reductions documented in post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), recurrent depression, and Cushing syndrome. Beyond structural atrophy, such as the 8–12% hippocampal shrinkage observed in combat veterans,42 chronic stress leaves a lasting epigenetic signature through nuclear receptor subfamily 3 group C member 1 (NR3C1) gene methylation. These molecular changes can effectively “lock” the stress response, persisting for years even after the conflict has ended.43

The HPA axis dysregulation observed in conflict-exposed populations follows characteristic patterns described by Yehuda8 and Herman et al.44 Rather than uniformly producing hypercortisolism, PTSD is characterized by enhanced negative feedback sensitivity of the HPA axis, resulting in lower basal cortisol levels, elevated catecholamines, and an augmented startle response. Herman et al44 detailed the neural circuitry governing HPA regulation, noting that anticipatory stress responses are largely driven by GABAergic (GABA for gamma-aminobutyric acid) disinhibition in the amygdala. At the same time, the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex provide transsynaptic inhibition through negative feedback. Under chronic stress, these regulatory circuits undergo adaptation that may manifest as basal hypersecretion, sensitized stress responses, or adrenal exhaustion; the expressed pattern likely varies across individuals and levels of conflict exposure severity. Sustained exposure to the stressors of conflict eventually erodes the brain’s internal regulatory brakes. After approximately four to six weeks of chronic pressure, the downregulation of hippocampal glucocorticoid receptors weakens the HPA axis’s negative feedback loop, effectively trapping the neuroendocrine system in a state of permanent arousal.4,45 An acute collapse in allopregnanolone levels further compounds this physiological entrapment; the loss of this neuroactive progesterone metabolite depletes the brain’s GABAergic inhibitory shield, leaving the athlete vulnerable to profound nocturnal anxiety and persistent insomnia.5,32

Chrousos39 described how chronic HPA activation suppresses the gonadal axis through corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH)-mediated inhibition of gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) secretion, the somatotropic axis through suppression of growth hormone (GH) and IGF-1, and the thyroid axis through reduced thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) release. For athletes, these neuroendocrine consequences translate into reduced testosterone availability, impaired anabolic signaling, compromised tissue repair, and decreased metabolic rate, all of which are directly antagonistic to training adaptation. Furthermore, locus coeruleus hypersensitivity to input from the salience network, a well-established neurobiological feature of chronic threat exposure, may maintain continuous sympathetic tone that prolongs sleep-onset latency, elevates nocturnal sympathetic activation, and reduces slow-wave sleep (SWS) duration even during periods of physical fatigue.15 When stress-induced sleep disruption coincides with CSAH/DSF-B fragmentation and RIF-related schedule adjustments, the cumulative impact on sleep architecture may be multiplicative rather than merely additive. The neurobiological architecture of sleep is largely governed by the locus coeruleus, which serves as the essential gatekeeper for transitioning between non-rapid eye movement (NREM) and rapid eye movement (REM) stages. Under the burden of conflict, stress-enriched wakefulness keeps the locus coeruleus in a state of chronic hyper-responsivity, triggering frequent microarousals and systematically suppressing restorative REM sleep.46,47 For the elite athlete, this disruption is profoundly accelerated. Evidence from animal models indicates that the “double hormonal load”—the synergistic impact of high-intensity training superimposed on war-zone trauma—hastens the entry into Phase 3 epigenetic irreversibility by approximately 40%. This suggests that for these individuals, the critical window for intervention may shrink from three months to as little as six to eight weeks.48

Sleep Disruption in Conflict Zones: Behavioral Digital Sleep Fragmentation and Conflict-Specific Acoustic Hypervigilance

Sleep disruption in conflict-zone athletes occurs through two mechanistically distinct pathways that require formal differentiation to construct an accurate physiological model and to identify appropriate intervention targets. The first, DSF-B, encompasses voluntary technology-mediated behaviors that degrade sleep architecture through blue-light melatonin suppression, time displacement, and cognitive-emotional hyperactivation. The second, CSAH, is driven by involuntary exposure to state-broadcast emergency alert systems and represents a categorically more severe and mechanistically distinct physiological disruption. The chronic sleep debt endemic to conflict environments fundamentally dismantles the biological scaffolding required for systemic recovery. This disruption precipitates a state of catabolic dominance: as restorative markers like testosterone and IGF-1 plummet, cortisol levels surge, creating a highly proteolytic environment that effectively erodes the endocrine foundations of athletic performance.7,9 The following section characterizes the three distinct acoustic threat categories operative in current EMR conflict-affected regions and the differential physiological pathways through which they compound CSAH-mediated circadian disruption.

Conflict-Specific Acoustic Hypervigilance

The Conflict-Zone Acoustic Threat Taxonomy: Explosive Events, State-Broadcast Sirens, and Government-Issued Smartphone Alerts

The acoustic threat landscape confronting athletes in active conflict zones is not a single homogeneous stimulus.49,50 It comprises three mechanistically and acoustically distinct categories, each producing a distinct physiological signature and each operating across a different temporal window of the nocturnal recovery period.51 Because direct empirical evidence from athlete populations exposed to conflict-zone nocturnal alert systems remains limited, the CSAH construct proposed herein should be considered a mechanistically informed conceptual model derived from adjacent sleep, trauma, and environmental-noise literatures. Failure to distinguish these categories may underestimate both the cumulative acoustic stress and the diversity of neural pathways through which such stress may influence recovery physiology.

The first category encompasses the direct acoustic consequences of offensive military activity: supersonic aircraft overflights, ballistic missile transits, aerial bombardment, artillery exchanges, and airborne explosion events. These stimuli share several critical properties that place them entirely outside the civilian noise pollution framework. They occur without any preceding warning signal reaching the sleeping individual. Their intensity at ground level routinely exceeds 140 decibels (dB) for proximate explosive events and 120 to 130 dB for supersonic overflights, surpassing the threshold for acute cochlear trauma and far exceeding the human pain threshold. Particularly intense and frightening noise stimuli trigger the sympathetic fight-flight reaction with secretion of adrenaline and noradrenaline from the adrenal medulla, alongside a defeat reaction involving plasma cortisol elevation; critically, the activation of these neuroendocrine cascades does not require cortical involvement, meaning cognitive perception of the noise is unnecessary for its cardiovascular and HPA consequences to manifest fully.52 For the sleeping athlete, this means the subcortical amygdala-locus coeruleus circuit is fully activated before conscious threat appraisal can occur, producing a complete catecholamine and cortisol surge that bypasses voluntary attentional processes entirely. Decades of neurophysiological research have pinpointed a hyper-responsive locus coeruleus–noradrenaline system as a key driver of stress vulnerability. This persistent noradrenergic overdrive acts as a constant biological alarm, fueling a state of chronic anxiety, hypertension, and systemic cardiovascular strain.53

The second category is state-broadcast public warning infrastructure: fixed outdoor sirens deployed across residential and military zones throughout the EMR conflict landscape. Across multiple conflict-affected EMR nations, state-operated public warning systems activate automatically upon detection of incoming projectiles, broadcasting pre-recorded alert tones across all public-address infrastructure in the threatened zone through comparable broadcast architectures. These systems are engineered to a single operational specification: they must be physically impossible to sleep through. Outdoor sirens typically operate between 85 and 120 dB at street level. Even after wall attenuation of approximately 25 to 35 dB, the resulting indoor level substantially exceeds the World Health Organization (WHO) nighttime indoor bedroom limit of 30 dB, below which sleep of good quality can be maintained.54,55 Physiological reactions, including autonomic, motor, and cortical arousals, can be triggered by sound pressure levels as low as 30 dB during sleep, and the threshold for noise-induced cardiometabolic stress reactions is substantially lower during sleep than during wakefulness.55 A siren penetrating residential walls at 60 to 80 dB is likely to activate threat-processing circuits at a level approximately 30 to 50 dB above the biological arousal floor for a sleeping nervous system.

The third category is particularly important for the TASF: government-issued smartphone alert applications that deliver mandatory threat notifications directly to the sleeping individual’s device. Across conflict-affected EMR nations, government-issued smartphone applications relay real-time threat notifications encompassing rocket fire, hostile aircraft intrusion, terrorist infiltration, and other emergency events. A defining hardware feature distinguishes these systems from all other smartphone notifications and creates a critical physiological bridge between the CSAH and DSF-B pathways. These applications are explicitly designed to override the device’s silent and vibration modes, sounding a high-intensity alarm regardless of any user-configured notification settings. This silent-mode override eliminates the primary behavioral countermeasure recommended by the DSF-B intervention literature for nocturnal notification management. An athlete who has correctly implemented a structured notification-filtering protocol to manage DSF-B remains fully exposed to government-issued alert intrusions, which cannot be silenced by any behavioral digital hygiene strategy short of disabling the application entirely, a decision that may carry direct personal safety consequences in an active conflict environment.

The temporal architecture of multi-stage alert protocols operative in conflict-affected EMR nations illustrates how a single nocturnal threat event produces multiple sequential acoustic disruptions: a pre-alert application notification 15 to 30 minutes before an expected salvo; a location-based short message service with loud audio approximately 10 minutes before launch; a public siren approximately 90 seconds before impact; and an all-clear notification after the threat has passed. Each of the four stages independently activates the amygdala-locus coeruleus arousal circuit, resets the sympathoadrenomedullary discharge cycle, and restarts the clock on post-arousal cortisol elevation. A single threat event, therefore, produces a minimum of four discrete acoustic arousals separated by intervals of 10 to 30 minutes, precisely the duration required for the athlete to re-enter light NREM sleep before the next alert fires. This architecture may prevent descent into SWS and may eliminate the physiological window for pulsatile GH secretion across the entire threat sequence.

A further mechanistic dimension concerns cortisol kinetics during repeated sub-awakening acoustic events. Noise activates subcortical processing centers, including the amygdala and the HPA axis, inducing cortisol secretion even below the awakening threshold; repeated noise events accumulate cortisol in the blood because the time constant for cortisol decay is approximately 50 to 100 times longer than that for adrenaline and noradrenaline.56 This kinetic asymmetry means that on conflict nights, when some alerts produce full arousal while others do not fully penetrate sleep, sub-awakening events continue to load the cortisol system. The athlete who reports incomplete wakefulness during a distant alert is still experiencing progressive neuroendocrine loading. Over a multi-week Ramadan period with nightly alert exposure across all three acoustic categories, sub-awakening cortisol accumulation compounds the overt HPA dysregulation of CCPS and the direct nocturnal secretion associated with full CSAH arousals, constituting a third cortisol input pathway within the TSCH that has not previously been recognized.

The three acoustic categories also differ fundamentally in their habituation trajectories. The civilian environmental noise literature documents that autonomic arousals habituate to repeated noise exposure, albeit more slowly than cortical arousals, allowing populations near airports or traffic corridors to develop partial tolerance over time, habituation is not complete, but it attenuates.52 CSAH acoustic events operate through the opposite trajectory. Each siren-forced arousal paired with mandatory shelter-seeking constitutes a conditioning trial in which the acoustic stimulus is followed by a genuine aversive behavioral consequence, satisfying the contingency requirements for classical threat conditioning rather than habituation. Through the amygdala-bed nucleus of the stria terminalis pathway, CSAH stimuli sensitize rather than attenuate with repeated exposure. Each Ramadan night of conflict-zone acoustic exposure adds conditioning trials that progressively lower the awakening threshold for all subsequent nocturnal stimuli, including the alert application notifications of the following stage. The athlete completing three weeks of Ramadan in an active conflict zone is neurobiologically more sensitive to nocturnal acoustic stimuli at the end of the month than at the beginning. Together, the three acoustic threat categories operate across a severity gradient from behavioral disruption through physiologically mandated awakening to acute trauma, bypassing voluntary processing, all engaging the shared HPA-sympathoadrenomedullary convergence pathway and producing a nightly neuroendocrine burden that substantiates the nonlinear stressor interaction the TSCH predicts.

Neurobiological Cascade and Conditioning Mechanisms

Each acoustic category described in The conflict-zone acoustic threat taxonomy: explosive events, state-broadcast sirens, and government-issued smartphone alerts ultimately converges on a common subcortical processing circuit whose operations define the CSAH neurobiological signature. CSAH constitutes a categorically distinct and physiologically more severe sleep disruption mechanism operative in populations exposed to active armed conflict. State-broadcast emergency alert systems deployed across conflict-affected EMR nations, including missile warning sirens, civil defense broadcasts, and military public-address systems, are currently active across multiple conflict settings within the region and deliver sudden, high-intensity acoustic stimuli often exceeding 85 dB during nocturnal hours, with no pre-stimulus warning and mandatory behavioral response requirements. The neurobiological consequences of such stimuli differ substantially from those of smartphone notifications.

A high-intensity unexpected acoustic stimulus during sleep activates the amygdala-locus coeruleus circuit, triggering acute sympathetic-adrenomedullary discharge with rapid norepinephrine and epinephrine release before any cortical processing of threat content.15 This acute surge elevates heart rate, blood pressure, and metabolic demand within seconds, generating a complete arousal from sleep rather than a micro-arousal. The neurobiological transition into REM sleep is governed by the locus coeruleus, which serves as the essential gatekeeper of the sleep cycle. In conflict-saturated environments, the high-locus coeruleus activity associated with hypervigilance persists into the night, triggering frequent microarousals and the systematic suppression of restorative REM stages.46 The scale of this deficit is staggering; evidence from combat veterans indicates that REM duration can plummet from the healthy average of 90–110 minutes per night to a mere 20–45 minutes, representing a loss of 55% to 75%.32,42 For the elite athlete, this destruction of REM sleep represents a fundamental breakdown in technical development. Because REM is the primary window for replaying and consolidating motor sequences, its absence prevents technical gains from intensive training sessions from being truly encoded into the neural circuitry.57

In active conflict environments, this arousal is accompanied by mandatory behavioral displacement: shelter-seeking or evacuation procedures extend wakefulness by 30 to 90 or more minutes per episode. Where multiple alerts occur within a single night, a documented pattern during periods of sustained conflict operations, total involuntary sleep loss per night may approach or exceed two hours, independent of any voluntary digital behavior. During the concurrent RIF, these alert-forced arousals frequently occur within the already compressed nocturnal feeding window, directly interrupting the Suhoor meal, attenuating post-meal anabolic signaling, and precipitating acute HPA activation precisely when parasympathetic recovery and GH secretion are most physiologically required. The fragmentation of SWS architecture essentially severs the vital link to anabolic repair. Since peak GH pulses are intrinsically coupled with the deep stages of SWS, the destruction of this architectural stage precipitates a collapse in the GH–IGF-1 axis.3,58 For the elite athlete, this results in a state of physiological futility: while training intensity may remain high, the absence of these endocrine signals prevents structural adaptation, effectively deconstructing the body rather than rebuilding it.3,7

Repeated CSAH exposures introduce an additional mechanism absent from DSF-B: conditioned acoustic hypervigilance. Through repeated pairing of acoustic stimuli with genuine threat and forced arousal, all subsequent auditory stimuli in the nocturnal environment, including lower-intensity sounds from digital devices, environmental noise, and normal household activity, acquire heightened salience through classical conditioning, maintaining elevated amygdala activation and locus coeruleus arousal readiness throughout the sleep period.8,44 This conditioned state may prevent the progressive elevation of the arousal threshold required for descent into SWS, so that SWS fragmentation may persist across nights even when no alert is broadcast. The resulting nocturnal physiology is largely consistent with the hypervigilant sleep architecture characteristic of PTSD, for which Van Someren15 has detailed the underlying central mechanisms. The erosion of SWS effectively shuts down the brain’s nocturnal “sanitation” protocol. Without the vital glymphatic clearance that occurs exclusively during this deep stage of sleep, the central nervous system loses its primary mechanism for flushing out metabolic byproducts.59 This failure produces a persistent state of neuroinflammation, in which the accumulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines (specifically interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-α) degrades neural health and impairs recovery.27,38 For athletes in current EMR conflict-affected regions, CSAH can therefore function as a persistent architectural disruptor of nocturnal recovery, with severity determined by alert frequency, perceived threat immediacy, and individual conditioning history rather than by voluntary behavioral choices.

Behavioral Digital Sleep Fragmentation

Unlike overt sleep deprivation, DSF-B operates through pathways that may preserve total sleep time while selectively degrading the restorative microstructure of sleep, particularly SWS and REM sleep.60 Three principal mechanisms have been identified: time displacement, whereby device use delays sleep onset; physiological arousal from screen content; and melatonin suppression from short-wavelength blue-enriched light emission.61 Chang et al,16 in a controlled crossover laboratory study, demonstrated that four hours of evening reading from a light-emitting eReader suppressed melatonin secretion, delayed circadian phase by approximately 1.5 hours, increased sleep latency by 10 minutes, reduced REM sleep duration, and impaired next-morning alertness compared to reading a printed book.

In conflict-saturated environments, the elite athlete’s melatonin system is under a dual siege. While exposure to artificial blue light during nocturnal hours suppresses the hormone’s secretion at the source, chronic hypercortisolemia further compounds the disruption by partially obstructing melatonin receptors. This dual mechanism effectively dismantles the body’s primary hormonal signal for biological darkness, leading to the persistent circadian desynchronization observed in populations exposed to prolonged high-stress environments.4,31,32 The photic mechanism involves melanopsin-containing retinal ganglion cells that are maximally sensitive to blue light at approximately 480 nm, providing a direct retinohypothalamic pathway to the SCN that overrides darkness-related melatonin induction. The significance of this mechanism within the Ramadan context is amplified by the common practice of extended nighttime smartphone and television use during the socially active post-Iftar period.

Population-level evidence from Hysing et al,17 who studied 9846 Norwegian adolescents, established dose-response relationships between electronic device use and sleep outcomes. For example, computer use was associated with a 2.70-fold increase in the odds of sleeping fewer than five hours.17 Exelmans and Van den Bulck62 extended these findings to adults, demonstrating that bedtime mobile phone use predicted longer sleep latency, worse sleep efficiency, greater sleep disturbance, and increased daytime dysfunction. Levenson et al,63 in a nationally representative sample of 1788 young adults, found that those in the highest quartile of social media checking frequency had an adjusted odds ratio of 2.92 for sleep disturbance, with checking frequency a stronger predictor than total usage duration. Beyond melatonin suppression and time displacement, notification-induced acoustic micro-arousals represent a further neurophysiological mechanism within DSF-B. Research on arousal thresholds during sleep stages indicates that intense or highly salient acoustic stimuli can penetrate even the elevated arousal threshold characteristic of deep SWS, inducing cortical re-engagement without full conscious awakening.64 Such micro-arousals generate a hidden fragmentation problem in which total sleep time appears nominally preserved on self-report instruments, yet the underlying sleep microarchitecture is substantially compromised. Tasali et al60 used acoustic stimuli to selectively suppress SWS in healthy young adults for three consecutive nights. They observed a 25% decrease in insulin sensitivity without compensatory insulin secretion, with the magnitude of reduction strongly correlated with the degree of SWS suppression (r = 0.89). This finding is directly relevant to understanding how notification-induced micro-arousals degrade metabolic recovery in athletes who perceive that they have obtained adequate sleep.

Ramadan Intermittent Fasting: Physiological Concomitants and Circadian-Metabolic Implications

The physiological impact of RIF on athletic populations extends beyond simple caloric restriction. During Ramadan, the feeding-fasting cycle is effectively inverted relative to the natural light-dark cycle: daytime hours that would normally coincide with peak metabolic activity become a prolonged fasting window of 14 to 18 hours, depending on latitude and season, while the compressed nocturnal window between Iftar (the sunset meal) and Suhoor (the pre-dawn meal) represents the only period for nutrient and fluid intake.65,66 This temporal inversion may constitute a significant modification of peripheral circadian oscillator entrainment, particularly in the liver, pancreas, and skeletal muscle, which rely on feeding-fasting cycles as their primary non-photic zeitgeber.29 In the specific polystressor context of conflict combined with concurrent Ramadan observance, a “silent” metabolic brake in the form of functional hypothyroidism, or “low T3 syndrome”, may emerge. Under the weight of chronic stress, the body prioritizes energy conservation by shunting peripheral conversion of T4 toward inactive reverse T3 rather than its active T3 counterpart.6 This shift results in a depressed basal metabolic rate, stalled protein synthesis, and impaired thermoregulation, all of which substantially erode exercise tolerance—even as standard clinical tests showing normal TSH levels keep the underlying pathology invisible to routine screening.6

The meta-analysis by Abaïdia et al,20 based on 11 controlled studies, reported that RIF was associated with significant decrements in mean and peak power during Wingate and repeated-sprint tests, whereas aerobic capacity, maximal strength, and jump height were not significantly affected. A 2024 umbrella review by Trabelsi et al67 further demonstrated that RIF is associated with performance decline during high-intensity exercise testing in athletes. These findings indicate that the highest-intensity, glycolytically dependent efforts are most vulnerable to the fasting state, consistent with progressive hepatic and muscle glycogen depletion across the daytime fasting window.66 The physiological cost of sleep debt is not merely neurological; it manifests as a literal draining of the body’s metabolic reservoirs. Evidence indicates that sleep deprivation, when paired with high physical demands, can plummet pre-exercise muscle glycogen levels by as much as 33%—dropping from 310 to 209 mmol/kg dry weight —effectively stripping the athlete of the essential fuel required for high-intensity performance.68 A collapse in leptin signaling further compounds this energy crisis, the primary indicator of the body’s energy status. As leptin levels fall, the resulting “energy alarm” independently suppresses the gonadal axis, creating a secondary pathway to anabolic failure that traps the athlete in a self-reinforcing catabolic spiral.6,34

In professional Algerian soccer players, Zerguini et al69 documented significant declines in speed, agility, dribbling accuracy, and endurance capacity during Ramadan, with approximately 70% of players reporting a perceived performance impairment that persisted for at least 2 weeks after the fasting period. While RIF in healthy, non-athletic adults under stable, controlled conditions produces minimal absolute hormonal changes,70 the physiological context of concurrent conflict exposure may amplify these responses in competitive athletes sustaining high training loads, as the additive neuroendocrine burden of CCPS and CSAH substantially narrows the biological tolerance for any additional schedule-related circadian displacement.

The endocrine consequences of RIF are particularly relevant to the TSCH. Chennaoui et al71 studied eight middle-distance runners and identified significant reductions in melatonin concentrations, elevated catecholamine levels, increased interleukin-6, and a tendency toward reduced testosterone-to-cortisol (T:C) ratios at the end of Ramadan, with recovery within seven days post-Ramadan. The reduction in melatonin is of particular mechanistic significance because melatonin serves as the primary hormonal signal of biological darkness, synchronizing peripheral clocks to the central SCN pacemaker.30 When melatonin secretion is suppressed or phase-delayed by altered light exposure during Ramadan, including the common practice of extended nocturnal social activity under artificial lighting, the coherence between central and peripheral clocks may be weakened. Sustained cortisol elevation serves as a primary driver of systemic insulin resistance. Through the inhibition of the glucose transporter type 4 and the stimulation of hepatic gluconeogenesis, this hypercortisolemic state induces a degree of muscular glucose impermeability.35 This shift is objectively reflected in a measurable rise in the HOMA-IR index, signaling a disruption in cellular energy-storage capacity.34 For athletes following Ramadan feeding schedules, this molecular impedance impedes efficient glycogen replenishment, as skeletal muscle remains physiologically resistant to glucose uptake even during the nocturnal feeding window.

Sleep disruption during Ramadan is well documented and represents a critical mediating variable between fasting and performance. The systematic review and meta-analysis by Trabelsi et al,21 which included 13 studies of athletes observing Ramadan, demonstrated a moderate reduction in total sleep time (standardized mean difference = 0.97, p = 0.01) alongside decreased self-reported sleep quality in three of the four included studies. Athletes consistently reported later bedtimes, fragmented nocturnal sleep due to Suhoor meal preparation, and increased reliance on daytime napping. The resulting reductions in SWS and REM sleep carry downstream consequences for hormonal secretion that are examined in The Neuroendocrine Convergence Point: Mechanisms of Triple-Stressor Interaction. Evidence-based chrononutrition strategies, including scheduling training sessions to the post-Iftar period, prioritizing complex carbohydrates and protein at Suhoor, and maintaining adequate hydration throughout the nocturnal window, represent the most directly modifiable countermeasures within the RIF domain.65,72–74 Sleep restriction fundamentally distorts the metabolic signaling required for precise appetite regulation, inducing an 18% decrease in leptin alongside a 28% surge in ghrelin.34 This endocrine shift acts as a faulty internal fuel gauge, obscuring the athlete’s true energy status and preventing an accurate perception of caloric requirements. The resulting risk of chronic under-fueling is particularly critical in a conflict environment, where the dual pressures of high-intensity training and environmental threat push metabolic demands to their absolute physiological limits.6,34,75

The Neuroendocrine Convergence Point: Mechanisms of Triple-Stressor Interaction

The TSCH posits that the three stressors identified in preceding sections, with CCPS and CSAH constituting the primary conflict-endemic stressor domains and RIF constituting a concurrent circadian-metabolic domain, converge on a shared neuroendocrine pathway producing emergent physiological dysfunction that may exceed the predicted sum of individual stressor effects. The central convergence point is the circadian regulation of the cortisol-melatonin axis, which governs the temporal organization of anabolic and catabolic processes, immune function, and cognitive restoration.

Spiegel et al,76 in a landmark study, demonstrated that six nights of sleep restriction to four hours per night in healthy young men produced glucose clearance rates 40% slower than the rested condition, elevated evening cortisol levels, and a shift toward sympathetic nervous system dominance; the authors noted that the resulting metabolic and endocrine profile resembled features of accelerated aging. Leproult and Van Cauter9 subsequently reported, in a sample of ten young men, that one week of sleep restriction to fewer than five hours per night produced a 10 to 15% decrease in daytime testosterone levels, reductions of a magnitude equivalent to 10 to 15 years of aging, with the lowest values occurring during the afternoon hours when athletic training typically occurs. Extending these findings to proximate conflict-zone conditions, Mantua et al77 reported that total sleep deprivation combined with military field stress reduced morning free testosterone concentrations by 28% (from 105.91 to 79.87 pg/mL) in one cohort and by 25.4% (from 115.31 to 89.29 pg/mL) in a second cohort, providing a potential military analog for the neuroendocrine consequences experienced by athletes under conditions of conflict-related sleep deprivation. Within the CSAH framework, the repeated acute sympathetic-adreno-medullary surges generated by nightly emergency alert exposures constitute an additional abrupt catabolic insult superimposed on the chronic HPA dysregulation of CCPS, may produce a dual-axis activation pattern not adequately captured by either the DSF-B literature or the chronic stress literature in isolation.

The consequences of disrupted sleep extend beyond testosterone suppression to directly impair muscle protein synthesis. Lamon et al78 demonstrated that acute sleep deprivation independently blunted the postprandial skeletal muscle fractional synthesis rate by approximately 18% (from 0.072 to 0.059% per hour) while promoting a catabolic hormonal profile marked by a 21% increase in plasma cortisol. This finding had direct implications for RIF athletes: the compressed nocturnal window available for protein consumption is often the window most degraded by CCPS-induced hypervigilance and CSAH-forced arousals, producing a state in which the only feeding opportunity coincides with maximally impaired anabolic signaling. In addition, the GH/IGF-1 axis may be adversely affected under conditions of repeated slow-wave sleep disruption, given the dependence of pulsatile GH secretion on consolidated slow-wave sleep architecture. Recurrent CSAH-related sleep fragmentation may reduce the duration or continuity of sleep stages typically associated with peak nocturnal GH secretion and, consequently, may impair recovery-related processes such as satellite cell activation and tissue repair.

The catecholamine-cortisol kinetic disparity described in The conflict-zone acoustic threat taxonomy: explosive events, state-broadcast sirens, and government-issued smartphone alerts introduces a third cortisol input pathway within the TSCH: sub-awakening acoustic events may progressively load systemic cortisol independent of conscious arousal frequency,56 compounding both overt CCPS-driven HPA dysregulation and full CSAH-forced arousal responses subclinically.

The appetite-regulatory consequences of sleep disruption further compound the metabolic challenge of RIF. Spiegel et al34 demonstrated that two nights of sleep curtailment produced an 18% decrease in leptin, a 28% increase in ghrelin, a 24% increase in subjective hunger, and a 33 to 45% increase in appetite for calorie-dense, high-carbohydrate foods. During Ramadan, when the feeding window is already compressed and caloric intake may be suboptimal, additional appetite dysregulation produced by CSAH and DSF-B may potentially produce a state in which athletes experience heightened cravings for high-glycemic foods during the Iftar window while simultaneously struggling to meet protein and complex carbohydrate targets for recovery. Nedeltcheva et al79 clarified the body composition implications: sleeping 5.5 versus 8.5 hours per night during a caloric restriction protocol reduced fat loss by 55% while increasing lean mass loss by 60%. For RIF athletes who are simultaneously sleep-restricted by CCPS, CSAH, and DSF-B, the caloric deficit created by daytime fasting may promote preferential catabolism of lean tissue rather than adipose stores.

McEwen and Karatsoreos provided a theoretical framework linking circadian disruption and sleep deprivation to the allostatic load framework, arguing that circadian disruption functions as both a direct stressor and an amplifier of other stressors, contributing to cumulative allostatic overload through elevated inflammatory cytokines, autonomic imbalance, evening cortisol elevation, and impaired glucose regulation.80 Within the TSCH, this amplification effect is hypothesized to operate bidirectionally across all three domains: RIF-induced circadian desynchronization amplifies the HPA axis dysregulation produced by CCPS, which in turn exacerbates the sleep architecture degradation initiated by CSAH and DSF-B, potentially creating a self-reinforcing cycle of progressive physiological erosion. The net result is a shift in the T:C ratio toward sustained catabolism, a progressive blunting of melatonin amplitude, and the development of a state termed circadian decoherence, in which the temporal coordination among organ systems breaks down, and anabolic-catabolic processes become disordered in their timing.

The Systematic Anabolic Collapse and Neuroinflammatory Loop

The hormonal disruption operative in conflict zones is not isolated; it represents a comprehensive failure of the body’s regulatory networks across all major endocrine axes. This systemic collapse is most clinically evident in the reversal of the testosterone-to-cortisol ratio, the primary marker of an athlete’s recovery status. For the elite competitor, even a single week of sleep restriction results in a 10–15% decline in testosterone—a hormonal blow that effectively forces the body through 10 to 15 years of physiological aging in a matter of days.9,81,82

This state of “accelerated aging” is further compounded by the loss of structural repair. Because peak GH pulses are intrinsically coupled with consolidated SWS, the destruction of sleep architecture by conflict-related stressors precipitates a collapse in the GH–IGF-1 axis.3,58 The human consequence is a state of physiological futility: the athlete continues to undergo intensive training, yet the body is stripped of the anabolic signals required for tissue repair. Rather than adapting to the training load, the athlete physiologically deconstructs themselves.3,7

Beyond the muscle, the brain’s internal chemistry is equally compromised. Extreme stress triggers an acute decline in allopregnanolone, a vital neuroactive metabolite of progesterone. This deficit removes the brain’s primary GABAergic inhibitory buffer, leaving the athlete neurobiologically defenseless against nocturnal anxiety and the consolidation of traumatic memories during fragmented rest.32 Finally, chronic stress initiates a persistent neuroinflammatory response within the central nervous system. Pro-inflammatory cytokines (specifically interleukins 6 and 1β, and tumor necrosis factor-α) establish a self-reinforcing “toxic loop”: they independently degrade sleep architecture and insulin signaling, which in turn fuels further cortisol elevation, locking the athlete into an amplifying cycle of systemic erosion.27,38

Temporal Dose-Dependence and Epigenetic Acceleration

The neuroendocrine erosion experienced by the athlete follows a non-linear temporal trajectory, progressing through three distinct physiological phases. During the initial adaptive phase (0–4 weeks), the HPA axis responds to the conflict environment with full reversibility; though performance may be challenged, the system can return to baseline with adequate recovery.83 However, sustained exposure leads to the saturation phase (4 weeks–3 months), where the circadian cortisol gradient flattens and insulin resistance becomes objectively measurable. In this stage, the window for recovery narrows significantly as the system begins to “lock” into a state of chronic arousal.34,84 The transition into phase 3 (>3 months) represents a critical “point of no return”. At this juncture, the physiological burden translates into structural and molecular damage, including an 8–12% reduction in hippocampal volume and the appearance of persistent epigenetic “scars”. Specifically, methylation of the nuclear receptor subfamily 3, group C, member 1 gene acts as a cellular memory of the trauma, potentially inducing long-term irreversibility that persists even after the stressor is removed.42,43

For the elite athlete, the most urgent finding is the “double hormonal load” paradox. Evidence from animal models—specifically those combining restraint stress with forced exercise—reveals that training stress does not merely add to war stress; it multiplies it. This synergistic load accelerates entry into Phase 3 by approximately 40%. In these models, epigenetic markers of irreversibility were reached in as little as 3 to 6 weeks, compared to 8 weeks for stress alone.85 This suggests that for athletes maintaining intensive training in conflict zones, the biological window for effective intervention may be nearly halved, moving the threshold of partial irreversibility to just 6 to 8 weeks.48

Cognitive and Psychological Consequences of Compound Stressor Exposure

The cognitive dimensions of athletic performance, including reaction time, decision-making speed, attentional allocation, and effort perception, are among the first casualties of compound stressor exposure.86–88 Ben Cheikh et al86 concluded that one-night sleep deprivation affects both the activation processes of selective attention and maximal isometric strength, two key skills in combat sports. Taheri and Arabameri87 demonstrated that a single night of total sleep deprivation significantly impaired choice reaction time in college athletes (p = 0.003) while leaving anaerobic power unaffected, supporting the functional role of sleep in cognitive restitution, with cognitive-perceptual capacities demonstrating greater vulnerability to sleep loss than gross motor output. Tian et al88 examined cognitive performance in national-level pencak silat athletes during RIF and highlighted domain-specific impairments: psychomotor speed and vigilance were maintained at 09:00 h but deteriorated significantly by 16:00 h, while verbal learning and memory showed significant afternoon decrements (d = 0.8, p = 0.03). These circadian-dependent cognitive fluctuations may further compound the general performance decrements documented across the Ramadan fasting day.

The systematic review by Craven et al89 confirmed that acute sleep loss produces significant increases in rating of perceived exertion (RPE) at equivalent absolute workloads, meaning that athletes perceive identical training stimuli as harder when sleep-deprived. This perceptual amplification of effort, combined with the motivational challenges of training during caloric restriction and psychological stress, may threaten the intrinsic motivation that Ryan and Deci90 identified as fundamental to sustained effortful engagement. Self-determination theory posits that competence, autonomy, and relatedness are essential psychological nutrients. In a polystressor crisis environment, the athlete’s perceived competence is threatened by declining performance, autonomy is constrained by religious and environmental obligations, and relatedness may be disrupted by conflict-related social fragmentation. A clinically perilous “performance illusion” manifests as noradrenergic hypervigilance and chronic hypercortisolemia combine to generate a deceptive subjective state of energy and alertness.4,91 During this phase, athletes may perceive significantly less fatigue, as stress-induced endorphins and elevated cortisol modulate pain thresholds and mimic the high-arousal states of official competition.91 This subjective resilience encourages the maintenance—or even escalation—of intensive training loads, even as objective biological indicators are in a state of silent, systematic collapse.85,91 By obscuring the underlying state of homeostatic depletion, this mechanism facilitates a high-risk trajectory toward severe overtraining and musculoskeletal injury, ultimately converting a subclinical neuroendocrine condition into a self-perpetuating state of progressive systemic dysfunction.85,91

The psychological burden is further intensified by the hypervigilance and anticipatory arousal generated by both CSAH and DSF-B. Research indicates a high prevalence of severe nomophobia among elite competitors, strongly correlated with severe anxiety and clinical insomnia.92,93 In conflict environments, compulsive device-checking acquires an additional rational dimension that distinguishes it from recreational nomophobia: the athlete monitors their device for genuine security information, creating an additive interaction between conflict-conditioned threat sensitivity and device-mediated autonomic over-responsiveness that continuously depletes cognitive reserves and prevents the psychological detachment from stressors essential for central nervous system recovery. The resulting deterioration in emotional regulation and self-efficacy accelerates early-stage clinical burnout, as evidenced by monitoring data from RIF athletes: Hooper Index monitoring in RIF athletes94 documented increases from pre-Ramadan to end-of-Ramadan values in fatigue (3.3 to 4.3), sleep disruption (3.1 to 4.6), and perceived stress (2.9 to 3.5), alongside a reduction in total sleep duration of approximately 45 minutes and increased insomnia symptoms.20,95,96 Rabbani et al97 validated the Hooper Index in professional soccer, confirming its utility as a minimally invasive screening tool for detecting early allostatic overload in athletes experiencing simultaneous fasting, stress, and sleep disruption.

Chronic stress fosters a state of amygdalar hyperactivation that systematically inhibits the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, the neural epicenter for strategic anticipation and executive management under competitive pressure.4,26 Consequently, the athlete is effectively precluded from accessing optimal performance states, such as “flow” and the individual zones of optimal functioning, as the neurobiological prerequisite for these states—cognitive-emotional equilibrium is dismantled.26 This neuroplastic shift prioritizes emotional reactivity over analytical processing, triggering contrasting patterns of structural remodeling that impair executive function while heightening emotionality, ultimately destabilizing the neural circuits dedicated to learning, memory, and emotional regulation.4,26

The Cortisol Awakening Response and Diurnal Slope as Biomarkers of Triple-Stressor Overload

The cortisol awakening response (CAR), defined as the marked increase in cortisol secretion occurring in the first 30 to 45 minutes after morning awakening, has been proposed as a discrete neuroendocrine marker of anticipatory cognitive and metabolic preparation for daily demands.98,99 The CAR is regulated by the SCN through an extra-pituitary adrenal pathway and is modulated by hippocampal and prefrontal cortical inputs related to prospective memory and anticipated daily stress.99 In a comprehensive review, Stalder et al100 proposed that the CAR operates through a dual-control system integrating circadian, environmental, and neurocognitive processes, with both rapid nongenomic and slower genomic glucocorticoid signaling pathways activated at awakening.

Within the TASF, the CAR is proposed as a potentially sensitive biomarker of the degree of circadian-neuroendocrine disruption produced by triple-stressor convergence. When inverted feeding schedules degrade SCN-peripheral clock alignment, hippocampal inputs are impaired by stress-related atrophy, and anticipatory cognitive processes are distorted by hypervigilance conditioned through repeated CSAH exposures, the CAR may become either blunted, reflecting HPA axis exhaustion, or exaggerated, reflecting anticipatory threat overestimation. Both patterns have been associated with adverse health outcomes in non-athletic populations. The diurnal cortisol slope, which represents the rate of decline from the morning peak to the evening nadir, provides complementary information about the integrity of circadian HPA axis regulation. Adam et al,101 in a systematic review and meta-analysis of 179 associations across 80 studies, identified that flatter diurnal cortisol slopes were associated with poorer health outcomes (average r = 0.147), with the strongest effects observed for inflammatory and immune endpoints (r = 0.288). For athletes under triple-stressor load, serial monitoring of the diurnal cortisol slope provides an objective, minimally invasive index of progressive allostatic overload that complements subjective monitoring instruments such as the Hooper Index. Together, the CAR magnitude, diurnal cortisol slope, and salivary T:C ratio represent promising candidate biomarkers for a prospective monitoring approach, pending empirical validation in affected athlete populations.

The Tripartite Athletic Stress Framework: A Novel Conceptual Model

The TASF integrates the evidence presented in preceding sections into a unified conceptual model organized around three interacting stressor domains, a shared neuroendocrine convergence pathway, and a set of downstream performance and health outcomes. The three input domains are structured as follows. Domain 1 (psychoneuroendocrine) encompasses CCPS-induced HPA axis activation, amygdala-mediated hypervigilance, prefrontal cortex suppression, and chronic glucocorticoid exposure. Domain 2 (sleep-architectural) comprises two mechanistically distinct sub-pathways: CSAH, operating through acute acoustic arousals generated by explosive events, state-broadcast sirens, and government alert applications with mandatory silent-mode override; and DSF-B, operating through SWS micro-fragmentation, blue-light melatonin phase delay, and notification-triggered cortical micro-arousals. Domain 3 (circadian-metabolic) encompasses the physiological concomitants of concurrent RIF, including feeding schedule inversion, daytime caloric restriction, progressive hypohydration, hepatic glycogen depletion-repletion cycling, and potential melatonin suppression from extended nocturnal light exposure (Figure 1). The convergence pathway is proposed to operate through the circadian cortisol-melatonin axis. Each stressor independently perturbs this axis: RIF inverts the feeding-entrained peripheral clock signal; CCPS flattens the diurnal cortisol slope and dysregulates the CAR; and the compound sleep-disruption domain suppresses melatonin and fragments SWS continuity through acute full arousals (CSAH) and chronic conditioned hypervigilance alongside photic and micro-arousal mechanisms (DSF-B). When all three domains operate simultaneously, the resulting central-peripheral clock desynchronization may exceed any single domain’s disruptive capacity, potentially producing circadian decoherence, a state in which the temporal coordination among organ systems breaks down and anabolic-catabolic processes become disordered in their timing. The bidirectional feedback loops that distinguish this framework from simple additive models include: CCPS-mediated glucocorticoid excess exacerbating the catabolic environment of RIF by inducing peripheral insulin resistance that prevents efficient glycogen storage at Iftar; CSAH-induced nocturnal sympathetic surges feeding directly back into CCPS-conditioned hyperarousal, ensuring the athlete remains in a hypervigilant state throughout the night; and late-night Suhoor feeding further phase-delaying peripheral metabolic clocks already dysregulated by conditioned acoustic vigilance, DSF-B micro-arousals, and blue-light exposure.

The downstream consequences of circadian decoherence manifest across four performance-relevant domains. In the hormonal domain, the T:C ratio shifts toward catabolism through combined testosterone suppression and cortisol dysrhythmia. In the metabolic domain, insulin sensitivity declines, appetite regulation is distorted, and body composition shifts toward lean mass erosion and visceral fat accumulation. In the cognitive domain, reaction time, decision-making speed, and perceived exertion are degraded. In the immunological domain, elevated inflammatory cytokines and suppressed immune surveillance increase vulnerability to infection and delay tissue repair, thereby increasing the risk of soft-tissue injury. To the best of the authors’ knowledge, published literature addressing individual components of this framework has not integrated conflict-zone CCPS and CSAH as the primary stressor domains alongside RIF as a co-occurring, context-specific circadian-metabolic domain within a unified athletic performance model.19,23,67,80

The Olympic Equity Gap: Biological Disadvantage

The documented constellation of neuroendocrine disruptions represents a quantifiable and involuntary biological impediment unique to the war context, effectively creating an unaddressed competitive disadvantage.103,104 Although fundamental principles 4 and 6 of the Olympic charter designate sport as a human right to be secured without discrimination, current institutional safeguards remain focused on gender identity and anti-doping while neglecting the profound physiological inequality imposed by armed conflict.104,105 The Ukrainian participation in the Paris 2024 Games provides a sobering empirical baseline of this disparity: with its smallest historical delegation of 140 athletes and more than 500 sports facilities destroyed, the nation’s performance fell to 12 medals—a marked decline from the pre-war average of 19–27.103 Ultimately, war-induced endocrine disruption fundamentally erodes the principle of competitive legitimacy, as athletic results increasingly mirror the magnitude of arbitrary external trauma rather than the intrinsic benchmarks of talent, preparation, and effort.104

Evidence-Based Countermeasures and Applied Interventions

The TASF implies a corresponding set of intervention targets organized by stressor domain, and the most effective protocols address all three domains simultaneously rather than in isolation. However, because no intervention studies have directly evaluated mitigation strategies in athletes exposed to this combined stressor profile, the recommendations presented below should be interpreted as theory-informed extrapolations derived from adjacent evidence bases.

Acoustic Environment Management and Digital Sleep Hygiene

Given the dual-pathway architecture of sleep disruption in conflict-zone athletes, countermeasures must address CSAH and DSF-B through targeted strategies. For CSAH, the primary intervention target is to reduce conditioned nocturnal arousal readiness and attenuate the impact of emergency acoustic stimulus during sleep. Where infrastructure permits, low-frequency sound masking within the sleeping environment can attenuate the perceived salience of distant alert stimuli below full arousal threshold, consistent with the acoustic arousal threshold evidence of Neckelmann and Ursin.64 Structured psychological decompression protocols conducted in the 60 minutes preceding sleep, including diaphragmatic breathing and progressive muscle relaxation, may reduce baseline sympathetic tone and lower amygdala reactivity to subsequent acoustic stimuli, thereby potentially raising the effective arousal threshold. Establishing and rehearsing a cognitively pre-planned emergency-response protocol reduces the cognitive-emotional processing demand of each alert event and shortens post-arousal return-to-sleep latency by converting an unpredictable threat response into a procedurally automatized behavior.

For DSF-B, athletes should implement device-level notification filtering during biologically critical sleep windows, utilizing automated “Do Not Disturb” configurations that permit only verified emergency contacts to bypass the filter, thereby eliminating nonessential social media and news alerts while preserving access to genuine security notifications. A structured device-free pre-sleep period of at least 90 minutes is recommended to allow the onset of endogenous melatonin secretion without photic interference. When evening screen exposure is unavoidable due to communication obligations, short-wavelength-blocking eyewear provides a practical mitigation measure. These protocols are particularly actionable in the context of Ramadan, where extended post-Iftar social digital activity is normative but directly antagonistic to sleep architecture.

Chrononutrition

Chrononutrition strategies for RIF athletes should be calibrated to counteract the catabolic hormonal environment and support rapid recovery within a compressed feeding window. Maughan et al recommended shifting training sessions to the post-Iftar period to provide nutritional support during the recovery window.72 Burke and King65 emphasized that the timing of protein intake relative to exercise is critical for muscle protein synthesis during periods of restricted feeding opportunity. Chamari et al,106 reporting the Aspetar Orthopedic and Sports Medicine Hospital clinical guidelines, provided specific macronutrient targets for elite athletes during Ramadan. Strategic Napping

Strategic napping has emerged as one of the most evidence-supported countermeasures for sleep-restricted athletes during Ramadan.74,107 Lastella et al,107 in a systematic review of 37 studies encompassing 3489 participants, noted that naps of 20 to 90 minutes improved sprint performance, repeated high-intensity efforts, and alertness while reducing perceived fatigue, with optimal timing between 13:00 and 16:00 hours. Boukhris et al108 demonstrated in RIF athletes that a 45-minute nap produced greater improvements in shuttle run performance measures than a 25-minute nap. Waterhouse et al109 confirmed that a 30-minute post-lunch nap following partial sleep deprivation improved sprint times, short-term memory, alertness, and reaction time accuracy. Within the TASF, post-Iftar naps of 20 to 45 minutes represent a practical micro-recovery dose that may partially compensate for nocturnal SWS deficits produced by both CSAH and DSF-B without inducing further circadian phase displacement.

Autonomic Rebalancing

Voluntary, slow diaphragmatic breathing at approximately 6 breaths per minute is a practical, low-cost intervention for managing sympathetic dominance driven by CCPS and CSAH. Laborde et al,110 in a meta-analysis of 223 studies, found significant increases in the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) of heart rate variability (HRV) during breathing sessions, immediately after single sessions, and following multi-session interventions. Enhanced parasympathetic activity may partially counteract the sympathetic dominance produced by chronic stress and sleep restriction, supporting cardiovascular recovery and reducing nocturnal autonomic arousal. Yoga Nidra, a guided meditation technique that induces a hypnagogic physiological state, has demonstrated preliminary efficacy in improving perceived recovery quality and sleep in elite karate athletes, suggesting potential utility as a complement to breathing-based protocols.111 However, the evidence base remains limited to case-level studies.

Stress Inoculation Training

Stress inoculation training (SIT), as formalized by Meichenbaum and Deffenbacher,102 offers a structured psychological intervention for building coping resilience under chronic stress conditions. The three-phase model, comprising conceptualization, skills acquisition, and graduated application, has demonstrated beneficial outcomes for anxiety reduction and performance maintenance in sport contexts. For athletes in conflict-affected regions, SIT adapted to the realities of geopolitical instability may help reframe the compound challenge of Ramadan training under psychological duress as a controllable, skill-based endeavor, reducing threat appraisal severity and thereby attenuating CCPS-driven HPA activation. HRV-guided training load management, with the autonomy to reduce mechanical external load when subjective stress, delayed onset muscle soreness, and sleep disruption peak simultaneously, provides the objective monitoring infrastructure within which psychological interventions can be applied with precision.

Limitations and Directions for Future Research

Several limitations of the present review and the proposed TASF require acknowledgment. First, the evidence synthesis is narrative rather than systematic, and the framework is conceptual rather than empirically validated. No published study has simultaneously measured all three stressor domains and their interactive effects on circadian biomarkers and athletic performance outcomes within a single cohort, and the nonlinear synergistic predictions of the TSCH therefore remain inferential pending prospective testing. Second, the populations of greatest current relevance, athletes in conflict-affected EMR nations, are among the most difficult to study due to logistical, ethical, and security constraints, resulting in a paucity of controlled athletic data from these settings.10 Third, the CSAH construct introduced in this review, while mechanistically grounded in established neurobiological principles governing acoustic arousal, HPA axis activation, and conditioned threat response, currently lacks a dedicated empirical literature in the athletic performance domain. Its differentiation from DSF-B rests on mechanistic inference from adjacent literatures rather than direct experimental evidence from conflict-zone athlete populations, and prospective empirical validation is required. Fourth, the DSF-B literature, while rapidly expanding, has relied heavily on self-reported screen use and cross-sectional designs, limiting causal inference regarding the specific mechanisms through which smartphone notifications disrupt sleep microarchitecture. Fifth, the TASF currently treats the three-stressor domains as broadly equivalent in their disruptive potential. In contrast, their relative contributions likely vary substantially across individuals, geographic contexts, conflict intensity, and Ramadan timing. Approximately 89% of participants in the sleep deprivation and athletic performance literature are men,89 creating a substantial sex-based evidence gap that limits generalizability to women athletes. Future research should prioritize longitudinal, multi-site studies that concurrently capture circadian phase markers (dim-light melatonin onset [DLMO]), cortisol profiles (CAR, diurnal slope), actigraphy-derived sleep architecture indices, objective acoustic environment logs, validated stress inventories, and sport-specific performance outcomes in Muslim athletes across varying contexts of psychosocial adversity. Ecological momentary assessment deployed via secure mobile applications represents a particularly promising methodology for capturing real-time subjective and behavioral data while minimizing recall bias, and for mapping the temporal dynamics of the TASF’s bidirectional feedback loops.

Among the most important testable predictions generated by the TASF is the hypothesis that athletes exposed to all three stressors simultaneously are expected to exhibit greater CAR blunting, steeper reductions in the T:C ratio, more pronounced SWS suppression, and larger cognitive performance decrements than athletes exposed to any one or two stressors in combination. Establishing dose-response relationships and identifying moderating variables, including chronotype, training status, genetic polymorphisms in clock genes, prior stress exposure history, CSAH alert frequency, and digital boundary-setting capacity, represent priorities for advancing the framework from a conceptual proposition to an empirically validated model. Prospective intervention trials should use the TASF as an organizing scaffold, isolating the efficacy of specific acoustic environment management protocols, customized chrononutritional adjustments, and psychological resilience programs in mitigating the downstream outputs of the polystressor crisis environment. A further limitation concerns the geographic and temporal specificity of the RIF domain: the physiological interactions attributed to Ramadan observance are analyzed exclusively within the context of its 2026 coincidence with the EMR conflict, and the framework does not generalize to Muslim-majority populations in stable, non-conflict settings, nor to the global minority of healthcare or sport systems that adjust institutional working hours during Ramadan. The applicability of Domain 3 is therefore explicitly bounded by the conflict-coincident context and should not be interpreted as attributing intrinsic performance-impairing properties to Ramadan observance.

Conclusion

The TASF presented in this review addresses an important gap in the sports science and chronobiology literature by integrating the primary stressors endemic to armed conflict, specifically CCPS and the compound sleep-disruption domain encompassing CSAH and DSF-B, with RIF as a concurrent physiological domain operative in Muslim-majority conflict-affected populations, into a unified conceptual model of circadian decoherence and neuroendocrine dysregulation. The evidence reviewed suggests that each stressor domain may independently perturb the circadian cortisol-melatonin axis through distinct mechanisms: feeding-schedule restructuring modifies the entrainment of peripheral clocks relative to the SCN; chronic HPA axis activation flattens the diurnal cortisol slope and impairs hippocampal regulation of the CAR; and CSAH generates acute complete sleep arousals with conditioned hypervigilance, while DSF-B suppresses melatonin and fragments SWS through photic signaling and micro-arousals. When these mechanisms operate simultaneously within the polystressor crisis environment, the TSCH predicts emergent, nonlinear degradation that exceeds the additive sum of individual effects.

The reconceptualization of the third stressor domain as a dual-pathway construct, CSAH and DSF-B, addresses a fundamental misclassification in the prior framing and in the broader Ramadan-athletic performance literature, which has not distinguished between the neurobiological consequences of a social media notification and those of a state-issued missile-warning siren. This distinction carries direct implications for intervention design, as the countermeasures appropriate for DSF-B address only the voluntary pathway and are likely insufficient to address the acute and conditioned disruptions produced by CSAH. The practical and ethical dimensions of this framework extend beyond sport science. Athletes in conflict-affected Muslim-majority regions represent a doubly underserved population: underserved by a research enterprise that has studied RIF primarily in stable, well-resourced contexts, and underserved by sport governing bodies whose scheduling and support structures rarely accommodate the compound demands of religious observance under conditions of active armed conflict. The identification of the CAR and diurnal cortisol slope as candidate monitoring biomarkers, alongside domain-differentiated countermeasures such as acoustic environment management, digital sleep hygiene, strategic napping, chrononutrition optimization, diaphragmatic breathing, and SIT, provides an actionable pathway from conceptual model to applied intervention. The framework invites empirical testing through prospective cohort designs capable of capturing the temporal dynamics of circadian decoherence across the month of Ramadan in real conflict-affected environments, and it calls for research partnerships with institutions in the EMR that can provide access to populations most directly affected.

As armed conflicts across multiple conflict-affected regions persist without foreseeable resolution, this framework is positioned to serve as an enduring reference for sports medicine practitioners, military performance scientists, and sport governing bodies operating in such contexts. Athletes who train and compete under these conditions face a unique, simultaneous disruption profile spanning five axes: hypercortisolaemia, noradrenergic hyperactivation, anabolic hormone collapse, metabolic failure, and neuroinflammatory amplification. They remain markedly underrepresented in the sport science and chronobiology literature despite confronting recovery challenges of exceptional complexity, and they warrant scientific and regulatory attention commensurate with the severity of their circumstances.
