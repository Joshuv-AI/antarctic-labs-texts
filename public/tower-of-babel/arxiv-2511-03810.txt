On the Existence of Fair Allocations for Goods and Chores under Dissimilar Preferences
Egor Gagushin, Marios Mertzanidis, Alexandros Psomas

Abstract
We study the fundamental problem of fairly allocating a multiset ℳ \mathcal{M} of t t types of indivisible items among d d groups of agents, where all agents within a group have identical additive valuations. Gorantla et al. [ 34 ] showed that for every such instance, there exists a finite number μ \mu such that, if each item type appears at least μ \mu times, an envy-free allocation exists. Their proof is non-constructive and only provides explicit upper bounds on μ \mu for the cases of two groups ( d = 2 d=2 ) or two item types ( t = 2 t=2 ). In this work, we resolve one of the main open questions posed by Gorantla et al. [ 34 ] by deriving explicit upper bounds on μ \mu that hold for arbitrary numbers of groups and item types. We introduce a significantly simpler, yet powerful technique that not only yields constructive guarantees for indivisible goods but also extends naturally to chores and continuous domains, leading to new results in related fair division settings such as cake cutting.



1
Introduction

Fairly allocating resources is a central problem in computer science and economics. What “fairness” means, however, admits multiple formal definitions. A classic notion is
proportionality
: an allocation among
n
agents is proportional if each agent receives at least a
1/n
fraction of its total value for all items. Arguably the gold standard is
envy-freeness
, which asks that no agent prefers another agent’s bundle to their own.

Envy-free allocations are not guaranteed to exist in the worst case; consider, for example, a single indivisible item and two agents with a positive value for it. Motivated by such inherent limitations, a substantial body of work tries to identify conditions and settings under which fair allocations become possible. This line of work has culminated in numerous relaxations of envy-freeness, including
envy-freeness up to one item
(EF1) and
envy-freeness up to any item
(EFX); the former is known to exist quite generally, while the latter remains elusive.
In stochastic settings envy-free allocations can emerge with high probability; e.g., when agents have additive preferences, drawn i.i.d. from a distribution with a bounded probability density function, Manurangsi and Suksompong
[
48
]
show that, for the case of goods, an envy-free allocation exists with high probability when the number of items satisfies
m\in\Omega(n\log n/\log\log n)
.

A complementary, structural route asks whether
multiplicity alone
can guarantee fairness. In this direction, Gorantla et al.
[
34
]
investigate whether envy-free allocations exist when items are available in multiple identical copies.
Formally, they consider the problem of fairly allocating a multiset
\mathcal{M}
consisting of
t
types of indivisible items among
d
groups of agents, where all agents within a group have identical additive valuations.
[
34
]
show that, for every such instance, there exists a finite number
\mu
such that, if each item type has at least
\mu
copies, then an envy-free allocation exists. Their proof relies on elegant but intricate arguments demonstrating that a certain linear program has a sufficiently large feasibility space to guarantee the existence of an integral feasible solution.

However, this approach has some key limitations: (i) it does not yield an efficient algorithm for finding such allocations when they are guaranteed to exist, (ii) it is too specialized to easily yield results for other fair-division settings, and, more importantly, (iii) it does not provide explicit bounds on
\mu
, as the argument is purely existential by nature. Gorantla et al.
[
34
]
derive upper bounds on
\mu
only for the special cases where either the number of item types is two (
t=2
) or the number of agent groups is two (
d=2
).

In this paper, we resolve one of the main questions left open by
[
34
]
by providing explicit bounds on
\mu
for arbitrary instances and developing a technique that addresses the aforementioned limitations.

1.1
Our Contributions

We provide the first
explicit
upper bounds on
\mu
, the number of copies required per item type to guarantee the existence of an envy-free allocation. Specifically, we prove that

\mu\in O\!\left(\tfrac{tn^{3}}{g\delta^{2}}\right)
,
where
g
denotes the greatest common divisor of the agent group sizes, and
\delta
quantifies the degree of dissimilarity between the valuations of different groups.
For comparison, Gorantla et al.
[
34
]
established that, when
d=2
, one has

\mu\in O\!\left(\tfrac{n^{2}\sqrt{t}}{g\delta}\right)

and
\mu\in\Omega\!\left(\tfrac{n^{2}}{g\delta}\right)
,
while for the case of
t=2
they obtained

\mu\in O\!\left(\tfrac{n^{2}}{g\delta}\right)
.
Interestingly, in the special case where each group consists of a single agent, our bound simplifies to

\mu\in O\!\left(\tfrac{n^{3}}{\delta^{2}}\right)
,
showing that, unlike in
[
34
]
, our result is independent of the number of item types
t
.

At the heart of our analysis is a dissimilarity condition that implies the existence of envy-free allocations. Our condition formalizes a natural intuition:
fairness should be possible if agents’ preferences are sufficiently dissimilar
. We quantify this dissimilarity between agents’ valuations using well-established measures of distance, including the Euclidean distance and various
f
-divergences such as the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence and the
\chi^{2}
-divergence.

Our technique is simple and naturally extends across models, yielding results in a broad range of fair-division settings.

Chores and envy-freeness.

We extend our analysis to the setting where items represent
chores
: indivisible tasks that impose costs rather than provide value to agents. By following the same framework developed for the case of goods, we derive explicit upper bounds on
\mu
that guarantee the existence of envy-free allocations in this setting as well.

Cake Cutting.

We consider the classic envy-free cake cutting problem, in the query model of Robertson and Webb. It is known that there exists a protocol for finding an envy-free allocation that uses at most
O(n^{n^{n^{n^{n^{n}}}}})
queries
[
10
]
(the best known lower bound in
\Omega(n^{2})

[
51
]
). If agents’ valuation functions can be represented as polynomials of degree at most
d
, Brânzei
[
21
]
provides a protocol for finding an envy-free allocation using
O(n^{2}\,d)
queries.
As an application of our condition for the existence of envy-free allocations, we prove that if (i) valuation functions are
k
-Lipschitz continuous for some constant
k
, and (ii) the
\ell_{2}
distance between the valuations of any pair of agents is at least a constant, then we can find a strongly envy-free allocation using
O(n\sqrt{n})
queries.

Proportionality for Goods.

Using the same technical framework, we also derive conditions under which proportional allocations exist for both goods and chores. For proportionality, the intuitive interpretation of our condition is that
each agent must be sufficiently far from society’s aggregate valuation
. In the case of goods, society’s valuation for an item is defined as the average of the agents’
\ell_{1}
-normalized valuations, and an agent’s distance from society is measured via the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between her
\ell_{1}
-normalized valuation vector and society’s valuation vector (see
Theorem

7
for the formal statement). In the case of chores, society’s cost for an item is given by the harmonic mean of the agents’
\ell_{1}
-normalized costs.

Leveraging the variational characterization of the
\chi^{2}
-divergence, we show that in a stochastic setting, where each value (or cost) is independently drawn from
U[0,1]
, with high probability all agents are sufficiently far from society when
m\in\Omega(n)
. By combining these results, we recover the state-of-the-art existence guarantees (up to constant factors) for proportional allocations in the stochastic setting.

Our Techniques

The proofs of all aforementioned results follow the same high-level structure. Here, we outline the main arguments for the case of envy-freeness and goods.

First, we identify fractional mechanisms that admit closed-form guarantees on the envy gap: the worst-case margin by which a group values its own bundle over another’s. We introduce a new mechanism, the
Relative Norm
mechanism (and its counterpart, the
Log Relative Norm
mechanism, for chores), and show that under this mechanism, the envy gap between agents belonging to different groups is lower bounded by a simple, interpretable quantity that depends on a suitable measure of dissimilarity between the groups.

Second, we formulate a linear program that computes a
fractional
allocation maximizing the minimum envy gap. By definition, the envy gap achieved by the optimal fractional solution is at least as large as that guaranteed by our
Relative Norm
mechanism. We then develop a rounding procedure that transforms this fractional solution into an integral one while ensuring that (i) each agent receives an integral bundle of items, (ii) all agents within the same group receive identical bundles, and (iii) only a small number of fractional items need to be transferred between groups.

A central technical challenge arises from the requirement that all agents in the same group must receive identical bundles
[
34
]
. This constraint complicates the redistribution of fractional items when the total number of copies to be reallocated cannot be expressed as an integer linear combination of group sizes. For example, suppose we need to reassign
11
copies of an item among two groups of sizes
n_{1}=5
and
n_{2}=7
. In this case, it is impossible to distribute the items while maintaining identical allocations within each group. To overcome this difficulty, we leverage a result of Brauer
[
22
]
for the Frobenius coin problem.

Combining the above observations, we show that if the total value of items transferred from one group of agents to another is smaller than the original envy gap, then the resulting integral allocation remains envy-free. The total transferred value can be upper bounded by the product of (i) the total fraction of items exchanged among groups and (ii) the maximum value assigned to any single item. Consequently, we derive a sufficient condition ensuring envy-freeness of the rounded allocation: if the maximum item value is smaller than a threshold that depends on the pairwise distances between agents’ valuation vectors, then the final integral allocation is guaranteed to be envy-free.

Finally, we show that as the number of item copies increases, the normalized value of each individual item decreases, while the pairwise distances between agents’ valuation vectors remain essentially unchanged. Combining these ingredients, we obtain an explicit bound on the number of copies required to ensure that our condition on the maximum item value is satisfied, guaranteeing the existence of an envy-free allocation.

Future Directions.

Beyond the immediate consequences of our work, we believe that our framework provides a promising new lens for understanding the tractability of fairness notions for indivisible items, such as the maximin share guarantee (MMS) and envy-freeness up to any item (EFX). One possibly useful interpretation of our results is that they identify structural features of instances, namely dissimilarity among the agents, that determine when these problems are easy.
1
1

1

Every proportional allocation is MMS, and every envy-free allocation is EFX, so our results imply the existence of MMS/EFX as well.

Using this perspective, our results complement well-known tractable cases for MMS and EFX, such as when agents have identical valuations.

Motivated by this observation, we also explore the problem of allocating indivisible goods to agents that are similar, but not identical. We measure similarity using an
f
-divergence between normalized valuations. As a proof of concept, in
Theorem

11
we prove that when the TV distance between the
\ell_{1}
-normalized valuations of any pair of agents is upper bounded, then a relaxation of EFX called transfer-EFX (tEFX) can be guaranteed for any number of agents.

Relaxations of Proportionality and Envy-Freeness.

Faced with the non-existence of proportional and envy-free allocations for indivisible items, a natural approach is to consider relaxed notions of fairness.

A well-studied relaxation of proportionality is maximin share (MMS) fairness
[
23

n

[

[

n\leq 3
additive agents
[
25

1
]

n\geq 4

For general additive valuations, a long series of works in fair division strives to pin down the exact constant for which
\alpha
-MMS allocations exist (for goods, we know that
\alpha\leq\frac{39}{40}

[
30
]
and
\alpha\geq\frac{10}{13}

[
38

[

]
and

[
30
]

[

Closer to our work, another major line of work tries to attack the MMS and EFX problems by restricting agents’ valuations. For the case of goods, it is known that EFX allocations exist for three types of agents
[

For the case of chores, EFX allocations are known to exist under leveled preferences

Hummel

Fair allocations in stochastic settings.

Dickerson et al.

Preliminaries

There is a set
\mathcal{N}
of
n
agents. Each agent
i
belongs to one of
d
possible groups: group
i
has
n_{i}
agents that have identical, additive preferences. Without loss of generality, we assume that
n_{1}\leq n_{2}\leq\cdots\leq n_{d}
. We have that
\sum_{i=1}^{d}n_{i}=n
.

Our arguments hinge on the ability to express large integers as nonnegative combinations of the group sizes. Towards this, we rely on the following theorem, due to Brauer
[
22
]
, and the ensuing corollary.

Theorem 1

(
[
22
]
)
.

Let
n_{1}\leq n_{2}\leq\cdots\leq n_{d}
, be positive integers such that
\gcd(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})=1
. Then, any integer
k\geq(n_{1}-1)(n_{d}-1)
can be written as
k=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\cdot n_{i}
, where
x_{i}\geq 0
is an integer for all
i\in[d]
.

Corollary 1
.

Let
n_{1}\leq n_{2}\leq\cdots\leq n_{d}
, be positive integers such that
\gcd(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})=g
. Then, any integer
k\geq g\cdot(n_{1}/g-1)\cdot(n_{d}/g-1)
, such that
k\equiv 0\pmod{g}
, can be written as
k=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\cdot n_{i}
, where
x_{i}\geq 0
is an integer for all
i\in[d]
.

For the remainder of this paper, we will use
g
for the greatest common divisor of
n_{1},\dots,n_{d}
, and
\theta
for the threshold from Corollary
1
.

Definition 1

(gcd
g
and threshold
\theta
)
.

Let
g\;\coloneqq\;\gcd(n_{1},\dots,n_{d})
, and
\theta\;\coloneqq\;g\Big(\tfrac{n_{1}}{g}-1\Big)\Big(\tfrac{n_{d}}{g}-1\Big).

There is also a multiset
\mathcal{M}
of
m
items. There are
t
types of items, with
k_{j}\geq 1
items of type
j
. We have that
\sum_{j=1}^{t}k_{j}=m
. We denote by
\mathbf{k}=[k_{1},\dots,k_{t}]^{T}
the vector of the number of copies for each item type. The practical motivation for this problem is the fair allocation of food donations
[
2

50
,
45
]
, where there are many types of items, corresponding to different brands, packages, and so on, and for each type there are many identical copies.

The items can be either
all goods
or
all chores
. For the case of goods, each group of agents
i\in[d]
has a vector of values
v_{i}(\mathbf{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
where for each
j\in\mathcal{M}
,
v_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})\geq 0
denotes the value that each agent in group
i
has for item
j
. The first
k_{1}
entries of

are all equal to the value of an agent in group
i
for the first type of items, the next
k_{2}
entries of
v_{i}(\mathbf{k})
are all equal to the value for the second type of items, and so on. The value of an agent in group
i
for a subset of items
S\subseteq\mathcal{M}
is
v_{i}(\mathbf{k},S)=\sum_{j\in S}v_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})
. For the case where
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{1}
(i.e., all types of items have one copy), we drop the parentheses and let
v_{i}(\mathbf{1})=v_{i}
.

For the case of chores, each group of agents
i\in[d]
has a vector of costs
c_{i}(\mathbf{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
where for each
j\in\mathcal{M}
,
c_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})>0
denotes the cost that each agent in group
i
has for receiving item
j
. The first
k_{1}
entries of
c_{i}(\mathbf{k})
are all equal to the cost for the first type of items, the next
k_{2}
entries of
c_{i}(\mathbf{k})
are all equal to the cost for the second type of items, and so on. The cost of an agent in group
i
for a subset of items
S\subseteq\mathcal{M}
is
c_{i}(\mathbf{k},S)=\sum_{j\in S}c_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})
. Similarly we let
c_{i}(\mathbf{1})=c_{i}
.

Throughout the paper, it will be convenient to work with normalized preferences. For ease of notation, we use
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{k})\in[0,1]^{m}
to refer to the
\ell_{1}
-
normalized
valuation of an agent in group
i
, that is,
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{v_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})}{v_{i}(\mathbf{k},\mathcal{M})}=\frac{v_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})}{\|v_{i}(\mathbf{k})\|_{1}}
for
j\in\mathcal{M}
. We use notation
\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i}(\mathbf{k})
to refer to the
\ell_{2}
-
normalized
valuation of an agent in group

, that is,
\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{v_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})}{\sqrt{\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}(v_{i,j}(\mathbf{k}))^{2}}}=\frac{v_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})}{\|v_{i}(\mathbf{k})\|_{2}}
for
j\in\mathcal{M}
.
Similarly,
\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i}(\mathbf{k})\in[0,1]^{m}
is defined as
\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})=\frac{c_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})}{c_{i}(\mathbf{k},\mathcal{M})}=\frac{c_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})}{\|c_{i}(\mathbf{k})\|_{1}}
, for all
j\in\mathcal{M}
; we will not use
\ell_{2}

We write

(resp.

) for the minimum and maximum value (resp. cost) of an item. That is,

-normalized minimum and maximum values/costs,

Allocations.

A fractional allocation
x\in[0,1]^{d\,m}
defines, for every agent group
i\in[d]
and item
j\in\mathcal{M}
, the fraction
x_{i,j}\geq 0
of the item
j
that each agent in group
i
receives. An allocation
x
is feasible if, for all items
j\in\mathcal{M}
,
\sum_{i\in[d]}n_{i}\cdot x_{i,j}\leq 1
and
x_{i,j}\geq 0
for all
i\in[d]
,
j\in\mathcal{M}
. Throughout the paper, unless explicitly stated, all agents in the same group will receive the exact same bundle of items, and therefore (for the purposes of discussing envy-freeness) it suffices to refer to groups instead of individual agents.
We are interested in
integral

A
for integral allocations and
x
for fractional allocations. An integral allocation
A=(A_{1},\ldots,A_{d})
is a partition of
\mathcal{M}
, where
A_{i}
denotes the set of items allocated to each agent in group
i\in[d]
, and
A_{i,z}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
indicates the number of copies each agent in group
i
received from item type
z
. Throughout the paper, we only consider
complete
allocations: an allocation is complete if every item gets allocated.

Fairness Notions

An (integral or fractional) allocation of goods
x
is
proportional
if, for all groups
i\in[d]
,
v_{i}(\mathbf{k},x_{i})\geq n_{i}\cdot\frac{v_{i}(\mathbf{k},\mathcal{M})}{n}
; if the inequality is strict, then the allocation is called strongly proportional.
An (integral or fractional) allocation of goods
x
is
envy-free
if, for all pairs of groups
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
,
v_{i}(\mathbf{k},x_{i})\geq v_{i}(\mathbf{k},x_{i^{\prime}})
; if the inequality is strict, then the allocation is called strongly envy-free.
Let
\textsc{Gap}_{i,i^{\prime}}(x)=v_{i}(x_{i})-v_{i}(x_{i^{\prime}})
be the advantage (or “negative envy”) of an agent in group
i
for an agent in group
i^{\prime}
, in an allocation
x
of goods.

An (integral or fractional) allocation of chores
x
is
proportional
if, for all agents
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
c_{i}(x_{i})\leq\frac{c_{i}(\mathcal{M})}{n}
; if the inequality is strict, then the allocation is called strongly proportional.
An (integral or fractional) allocation of chores
x
is
envy-free
if, for all pairs of agents
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
,
c_{i}(x_{i})\geq c_{i}(x_{i^{\prime}})
; if the inequality is strict, then the allocation is called strongly envy-free.
For chores, we similarly define
\textsc{CGap}_{i,i^{\prime}}(x)=c_{i}(x_{i^{\prime}})-c_{i}(x_{i})
as the advantage of an agent in group
i
for an agent in group
i^{\prime}
in allocation
x
.

Statistical Distances

An
f
-divergence
D_{f}(P||Q)
measures the distance between two probability measures
P

. For the most part, in this paper, we will consider
f
-divergences for probability mass functions (i.e., discrete distributions), and specifically, we will be studying the
f
-divergence between
\ell_{1}
-normalized preference vectors.

Let
P=(p_{1},\dots,p_{z})
and
Q=(q_{1},\dots,q_{z})
be two probability mass functions (
p_{i},q_{i}\geq 0
for all
i
, and
\sum_{i=1}^{z}p_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{z}q_{i}=1
).
The
\chi^{2}
-divergence between
P
and
Q
is defined as
D_{\chi^{2}}(P||Q)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{z}\frac{(P_{i}-Q_{i})^{2}}{Q_{i}}
. The KL-divergence between
P
and
Q
is defined as
D_{KL}(P||Q)\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{z}P_{i}\ln\left(\frac{P_{i}}{Q_{i}}\right)
. Finally, the Total variation (TV) distance between
P
and
Q
is defined as
D_{TV}(P||Q)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{z}|P_{i}-Q_{i}|
.

3
Conditions Under Which Envy-Free Allocations Exist

In this section, we study the existence of envy-free allocations. Missing proofs are deferred to
Appendix

A
.

We start by defining a mechanism for constructing a good
fractional
allocation: the
Relative Norm
mechanism, given valuations
v_{1},\dots,v_{d}
, outputs an allocation
x^{\textsc{RN}}
such that

x^{\textsc{RN}}_{i,j}=\frac{\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})}{n\,\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}+\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[d]}n_{i^{\prime}}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})}{n^{2}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}

where
x^{\textsc{RN}}_{i,j}
is the fraction of item
j\in\mathcal{M}
that each agent of group
i
receives,
i\in[d]
. Recall that
\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})
is the
\ell_{2}
-normalized value of an agent in group
i
for item
j
, and
\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})
is the
\ell_{2}
-normalized maximum value.

Although the above mechanism may initially appear complicated or arbitrary, it is in fact derived from simple and natural principles. Our goal is to design a mechanism in which the fraction of an item allocated to an agent increases with how much the agent values that item. Since our target property (i.e. envy-freeness) is scale-free, we must employ a normalized notion of value so that agents with uniformly higher valuations across all items do not gain an unfair advantage. So, the most natural idea is to let the allocation scale linearly with the agent’s normalized valuation, i.e.,
x_{i,j}=a\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})+b
for some choice of
a,b
. The parameters
a
and
b
are chosen to ensure that all fractional allocations are nonnegative and that every item is fully allocated. Following this reasoning, we obtain our
Relative Norm
mechanism.

First, we argue that this mechanism is feasible.

Lemma 1
.

The
Relative Norm
mechanism is feasible. That is, (i)
x^{\textsc{RN}}_{i,j}\geq 0
for all
i\in[d]
and
j\in\mathcal{M}
, and (ii)
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}n_{i}\cdot x^{\textsc{RN}}_{i,j}=1
for all
j\in\mathcal{M}
.

Proof.

Towards proving that
x^{\textsc{RN}}_{i,j}\geq 0
we have:

\displaystyle x^{\textsc{RN}}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})}{n\,\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}+\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[d]}n_{i^{\prime}}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})}{n^{2}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[d]}n_{i^{\prime}}\cdot\frac{\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})}{\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[d]}n_{i^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n}=0

Towards proving that
\sum_{i\in[d]}n_{i}\cdot x^{\textsc{RN}}_{i,j}=1
we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[d]}n_{i}\cdot x^{\textsc{RN}}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[d]}n_{i}\cdot\left(\frac{\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})}{n\,\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}+\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[d]}n_{i^{\prime}}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})}{n^{2}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in[d]}n_{i}\cdot\left(\frac{1}{n}+\frac{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[d]}n_{i^{\prime}}\left(\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})-\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})\right)}{n^{2}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{1}{n^{2}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}\underbrace{\sum_{i\in[d]}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in[d]}n_{i}\cdot n_{i^{\prime}}\cdot\left(\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})-\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})\right)}_{0}

\displaystyle=1.

∎

Let
\textsc{Gap}_{i,i^{\prime}}(x)\coloneqq v_{i}(x_{i})-v_{i}(x_{i^{\prime}})
denote the advantage (or, equivalently, negative envy) of an agent of group
i
for an agent of group
i^{\prime}
under allocation
x
. The next lemma gives a lower bound on
\textsc{Gap}_{i,i^{\prime}}(x^{\textsc{RN}})

Lemma 2
.

For all
i,i^{\prime}\in[d]
,
\textsc{Gap}_{i,i^{\prime}}(x^{\textsc{RN}})\geq\frac{\|v_{i}(\mathbf{k})\|_{2}}{2n\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{\max}(\mathbf{k})}\cdot\|\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i}(\mathbf{k})-\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\|_{2}^{2}
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\textsc{Gap}_{i,i^{\prime}}(x^{\textsc{RN}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}v_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})(x^{\textsc{RN}}_{i,j}-x^{\textsc{RN}}_{i^{\prime},j})

\displaystyle=\|v_{i}(\mathbf{k})\|_{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{v_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})}{\|v_{i}(\mathbf{k})\|_{2}}\left(\left(\frac{\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})}{n\,\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}+\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}\in[d]}n_{i^{\prime\prime}}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})}{n^{2}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\hskip 200.0pt\left.-\left(\frac{\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})}{n\,\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}+\frac{1}{n}-\frac{\sum_{i^{\prime\prime}\in[d]}n_{i^{\prime\prime}}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})}{n^{2}\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\|v_{i}(\mathbf{k})\|_{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})\left(\frac{\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})-\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})}{n\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{max}(\mathbf{k})}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|v_{i}(\mathbf{k})\|_{2}}{n\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{\max}(\mathbf{k})}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\left(\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})\right)^{2}-\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|v_{i}(\mathbf{k})\|_{2}}{n\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{\max}(\mathbf{k})}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k}))^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k}))^{2}-\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|v_{i}(\mathbf{k})\|_{2}\cdot\|\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i}(\mathbf{k})-\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime}}(\mathbf{k})\|_{2}^{2}}{2n\cdot\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{\max}(\mathbf{k})},

where in the penultimate equation we used the fact that
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}k_{j}(\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k}))^{2}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}k_{j}(\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime},j}(\mathbf{k}))^{2}=1
.
∎

\begin{array}[]{ll@{}ll}\text{maximize}&\alpha&\\
\text{subject to}&\alpha\leq\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\tilde{v}^{(2)}_{i,j}(\mathbf{k})\cdot(x_{i,j}-x_{i^{\prime},j})&\forall i,i^{\prime}\in[d],i^{\prime}\neq i\\

&\alpha,x_{i,j}\geq 0&\forall i\in[d],\forall j\in\mathcal{M}\end{array}

Lemma 3
.

If
k_{z}\geq\theta

Proof.

LP (
1
) has

items are shared between groups of agents.

Let
x^{*}
be an optimal solution of LP (
1
) where at most

For each group
i

, and

. Therefore, at the end of Phase 1, we have that

Phase 2.

of type
z

B_{i,z}
is an integer, for all
i\in[d]
. Let

.

Phase 3.
For every
z\in[t]
while

We have that

. Thus, after Phase 3, every agent group has received at most

).
All agents of the same group have received exactly the same integral bundle. Also, compared to the allocation

.
∎

x^{\textsc{RN}}

4.2
Envy-free Cake Cutting

In this section, we consider the classic problem of cake-cutting in the query model of Robertson and Webb.

In this setting, each group has one agent (
n_{1}=\dots=n_{d}=1
) and every agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
has a valuation function
U_{i}
that assigns a non-negative value to any subinterval of
[0,1]
. We can think of these values as induced by a density
u_{i}
, i.e., for an interval
I=[a,b]
,
U_{i}(I)=\int_{x\in I}u_{i}(x)dx
. In the standard model, valuations are normalized, i.e.,
U_{i}([0,1])=\int_{x\in[0,1]}u_{i}(x)dx=1
, additive, i.e., for a set of disjoint intervals
I_{1},\dots,I_{z}
,
U_{i}(\cup_{j=1}^{z}I_{j})=\sum_{j=1}^{z}U_{i}(I_{j})
, and non-atomic, i.e.,
\forall x,y\in[0,1],\lambda\in[0,1],\exists z\in[x,y]
where
U_{i}([x,z])=\lambda\cdot U_{i}([x,y])
. We will additionally assume that (i) for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
u_{i}
is
k
-Lipschitz continuous, and (ii) for all
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
,
\|u_{i}-u_{i^{\prime}}\|_{2}\geq\sqrt{\delta}
, where
\|u_{i}\|_{2}=\sqrt{\int_{x\in[0,1]}u_{i}(x)^{2}dx}
and
\delta
is a positive constant.

In the Robertson and Webb
[
52
]
model (henceforth, RW) there are two types of queries: (i)
\textsc{Eval}_{i}(I)
: given an agent
i
and an interval
I\subseteq[0,1]
, return
U_{i}(I)
, and (ii)
\textsc{Cut}_{i}(x,z)
: given an agent
i
, a point
x\in[0,1]
, and a value
z\in[0,1]
, return the smallest
y\in[x,1]
such that
U_{i}([x,y])=z
.

It will be convenient to work with
\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x)=\frac{u_{i}(x)}{\|u_{i}\|_{2}}
. By definition,
\int_{x\in[0,1]}\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x)^{2}dx=1
. Furthermore,
\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}
is
\frac{k}{\|u_{i}\|_{2}}
-Lipschitz continuous; since
\|u_{i}\|_{2}\geq 1
(by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality),
\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}
is
k
-Lipschitz continuous, for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
. Similarly, for all
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
,
\|\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}-\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime}}\|_{2}\geq\sqrt{\delta}
.

Lemma 11
.

For all
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x)\leq\max\{(8k)^{1/3},2\}
, for all
x\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

Let
z^{*}=max_{x\in[0,1]}\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x)
, and let
x^{*}
be such that
\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x^{*})=z^{*}
. Then,
\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x)\geq z^{*}-k|x-x^{*}|
. Wlog,
x^{*}\leq 1/2
; let
I=[x^{*},x^{*}+1/2]
. For all
x\in I
, it holds that
\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x)\geq z^{*}-\frac{k}{2}
. We have that
1=\int_{x\in[0,1]}\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x)^{2}dx\geq\int_{x\in I}(z^{*}-\frac{k}{2})^{2}dx=\frac{1}{2}(z^{*}-\frac{k}{2})^{2}
. Re-arranging, we have that

z^{*}\leq\frac{k}{2}+\sqrt{2}
.
For
k\in[1,2\sqrt{2}]
, it is easy to confirm that
\frac{k}{2}+\sqrt{2}\leq(8k)^{1/3}
, and for
k\leq 1
, we have that
z^{*}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{2}<2
.

Otherwise, if
k>2\sqrt{2}
, we have that
\frac{z^{*}}{2k}\leq\frac{1}{4}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2k}\leq\frac{1}{2}
. Then,
x^{*}+\frac{z^{*}}{2k}\leq 1
. Let
I^{\prime}=[x^{*},x^{*}+\frac{z^{*}}{2k}]
. Repeating the same argument as above, we have that for all
x\in I^{\prime}
,
\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x)\geq z^{*}-k\frac{z^{*}}{2k}=\frac{z^{*}}{2}
. We have
1=\int_{x\in[0,1]}\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x)^{2}dx\geq\int_{x\in I^{\prime}}(\frac{z^{*}}{2})^{2}dx=\frac{z^{*}}{2k}\cdot\frac{(z^{*})^{2}}{4}=\frac{(z^{*})^{3}}{8k}
. Re-arranging, we have that
z^{*}\leq(8k)^{1/3}
.
∎

We are ready to prove our main result for this section.

G_{i,i^{\prime}}=\sum_{j=0}^{\frac{1}{\epsilon}-1}\left(\frac{\int_{\epsilon j}^{\epsilon(1+j)}\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x)dx}{\sqrt{\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{1/\epsilon-1}\left(\int_{\epsilon j^{\prime}}^{\epsilon(j^{\prime}+1)}\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i}(x)dx\right)^{2}}}-\frac{\int_{\epsilon j}^{\epsilon(1+j)}\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime}}(x)dx}{\sqrt{\sum_{j^{\prime}=0}^{1/\epsilon}\left(\int_{\epsilon j^{\prime}}^{\epsilon(j^{\prime}+1)}\tilde{u}^{(2)}_{i^{\prime}}(x)dx\right)^{2}}}\right)^{2}

For all

4.3
Proportional Allocations of Goods and Chores

In this section, we study conditions under which proportional allocations exist for goods and chores. We also give applications of our existence results.

We will no longer assume that agents are organized into groups. This relaxation does not make the setting any more restrictive, as our bounds will not depend on the pairwise distances between agents. Consequently, the results remain meaningful even when different agents have identical or nearly identical valuations. Moreover, since proportionality is a significantly weaker notion of fairness than envy-freeness, we will not require that agents with identical valuations receive identical bundles. Treating each agent as an individual group therefore simplifies our exposition.

Throughout this section, we also assume that
\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{1}
. While it is possible to replicate the analysis and derive slightly stronger bounds on the number of copies required to guarantee proportionality (as opposed to envy-freeness), such an analysis would closely mirror that of the previous sections and would not yield additional technical insights. Instead, our focus is on exploring how the connection between fair allocations and
f
-divergences can be leveraged to obtain results by drawing on the heavy machinery developed in information theory for these divergences. In particular, we employ the variational characterization of
f
-divergences to establish bounds that we then use to recover, up to constant factors, state-of-the-art results in stochastic fair division.

All missing proofs are deferred to
Appendix

D
.

4.3.1
Allocating Goods via the Trading Post Mechanism

We define
S_{j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\frac{v_{i,j}}{v_{i}(\mathcal{M})}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j}
as society’s valuation for item
j\in\mathcal{M}
. Equivalently,
S_{j}
is the average of agents’
\ell_{1}
-normalized valuations. The
Trading Post
mechanism outputs an allocation
x^{\textsc{TP}}
such that
x^{\textsc{TP}}_{i,j}=\frac{\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j}}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}}\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j}}
, for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
and
j\in\mathcal{M}
.

We start by observing that the utility of an agent in the Trading Post mechanism has a nice form, as a function of the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between her valuation and society’s valuation.

Lemma 13
.

For all
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
\frac{v_{i}(x^{\textsc{TP}}_{i})}{v_{i}(\mathcal{M})}=\frac{1}{n}\left(1+D_{\chi^{2}}(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}||S)\right)
.

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{v_{i}(x^{\textsc{TP}}_{i})}{v_{i}(\mathcal{M})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{{v_{i}(\mathcal{M})}}\sum_{j\in[m]}v_{i,j}\frac{\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j}}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}}\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j})^{2}}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}}\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j})^{2}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}}\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(1+\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j})^{2}}{S_{j}}-2\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j}+S_{j}\right)

(
\|\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}\|_{1}=\|S\|_{1}=1
)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(1+\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j})^{2}-2\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j}S_{j}+S^{2}_{j}}{S_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(1+\sum_{j\in[m]}\frac{(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j}-S_{j})^{2}}{S_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(1+D_{\chi^{2}}(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}||S)\right).

∎

Now, consider the following LP that maximizes the
\ell_{1}
-normalized minimum utility of an agent. An allocation is proportional if its value with respect to this LP is at least
1/n
.

\begin{array}[]{ll@{}ll}\text{maximize}&\alpha&\\
\text{subject to}&\alpha\leq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i,j}x_{i,j}&\forall i\in\mathcal{N}\\
&\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i,j}\leq 1&\forall j\in\mathcal{M}\\
&\alpha,x_{i,j}\geq 0&\forall i\in\mathcal{N},\forall j\in\mathcal{M}\end{array}

(3)

The next lemma allows us to argue about the existence of an integral allocation that has high objective value (with respect to the LP above).

Lemma 14
.

[Essentially Theorem 3 of
[
28
]
]
Let
x^{*}
be an optimal solution to LP (
3
). There exists an integral allocation
A
, such that, for all agents
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
v_{i}(A_{i})\geq v_{i}(x^{*}_{i})-\max_{j\in\mathcal{M}}v_{i,j}
.

Proof.

LP (
3
) maximized the normalized
p
-mean of utilities, for
p\rightarrow-\infty
. It is known
[
28
]
that there exist prices
p^{*}_{j}\geq 0
for each item
j\in\mathcal{M}
, and budgets
b^{*}_{i}
for each agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
, such that
(x^{*},p^{*},b^{*})
is a Fisher market equilibrium.

[
16
]
give an algorithm that, given a Fisher market equilibrium
(x,p,b)
, in polynomial time, find an integral allocation
A
and budgets
b^{\prime}
(that satisfy certain conditions), such that
(A,p,b^{\prime})
is a market equilibrium.
[
28

prove that, for this integral allocation
A
, for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
v_{i}(A_{i})\geq v_{i}(x^{*}_{i})-v_{i,j}
, for some item

in the maximum bang-per-buck bundle of agent
i
. The lemma follows.
∎

Given
Lemmas

13
and
14
, we are ready to prove our main result in this section.

Theorem 7
.

Let
D^{*}=\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\left\{D_{\chi^{2}}(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}||S)\right\}
. If
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{max}\leq\frac{D^{*}}{n}
, then a proportional allocation exists. When the inequality is strict, then a strongly proportional allocation exists.

Proof.

Let
\alpha^{*}
be the value of an optimal solution to LP (
3
), and let
A
be the integral allocation from
Lemma

14
.
We have that
v_{i}(A_{i})\geq\alpha^{*}\,v_{i}(\mathcal{M})-\max_{j\in\mathcal{M}}v_{i,j}
. Let
x^{\textsc{TP}}
be the allocation output by the Trading Post mechanism. Since
x^{\textsc{TP}}
is a feasible solution for LP (
3
), we have that
\alpha^{*}\geq\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}(x^{\textsc{TP}}_{i})=\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\frac{v_{i}(x^{\textsc{TP}}_{i})}{v_{i}(\mathcal{M})}
. Therefore, using
Lemma

13
, we have that
\alpha^{*}\geq\frac{1}{n}\left(1+D^{*}\right)
, where
D^{*}=\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\left\{D_{\chi^{2}}(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}||s)\right\}
. Therefore, we overall have
u_{i}(A)\geq\frac{1}{n}\left(1+D^{*}\right)v_{i}(\mathcal{M})-\max_{j\in\mathcal{M}}v_{i,j}\geq 1/n
, where in the last inequality we used the fact that, for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
and
j\in\mathcal{M}
,
\frac{v_{i,j}}{v_{i}(\mathcal{M})}\leq\frac{D^{*}}{n}
.
∎

4.3.2
Proportional Allocations of Goods in the Stochastic Setting

As an application of
Theorem

7
, we consider a setting with stochastic valuations. Specifically, we consider the setting where
v_{i,j}
is sampled i.i.d. from the
U[0,1]
distribution.
[
48
]
prove that a proportional allocation exists with high probability for
m\geq n
(their result, in fact, holds as long as the PDF of the distribution is upper and lower bounded by constants). Here, we re-prove this result in a “modular” way, but with a worse dependence on
n
. Specifically, we show that in this model
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{max}\leq\frac{\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\left\{D_{\chi^{2}}(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}||S)\right\}}{n}
holds with high probability, for
m\in\Omega(n)
. This readily implies the existence of a proportional allocation.

Lemma 15
.

In the i.i.d.
U[0,1]
model
(v_{i,j}\sim U[0,1])
, for
m\in\Omega(n)
, it holds that
D_{\chi^{2}}(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}||S)\geq\frac{n-3}{3n}
, for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
, with high probability
(1-O(1/n))
.

Theorem 8
.

In the i.i.d.
U[0,1]
model
(v_{i,j}\sim U[0,1])
, a (strongly) proportional allocation exists with high probability
(1-O(1/n))
, when
m\in\Omega(n)
.

Proof.

Lemma

15
implies that, with high probability,
D^{*}=\min_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\left\{D_{\chi^{2}}(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}||S)\right\}\geq\frac{n-3}{3n}
. Also with high probability, for all
i\in\mathcal{N},j\in\mathcal{M}
,
\frac{v_{i,j}}{v_{i}(\mathcal{M})}\leq\frac{1}{v_{i}(\mathcal{M})}\leq\frac{1}{m}\left(\frac{1}{1/2-\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{m}}}\right)
.
Theorem

7
implies that if
\frac{1}{m}\left(\frac{1}{1/2-\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{m}}}\right)\leq\frac{D^{*}}{n}
then a proportional allocation exists. Therefore, if
\frac{1}{m}\left(\frac{1}{1/2-\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{m}}}\right)\leq\frac{n-3}{3n^{2}}
a proportional allocation exists; assuming that
m\geq c^{\prime}n
, for some positive constant
c^{\prime}
, the strict inequality holds for all sufficiently large
n
.
∎

4.3.3
Allocating Chores via the Inverse Trading Post Mechanism

Let
H_{j}=\frac{n}{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}1/\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}}
be society’s cost for item
j\in\mathcal{M}
, defined as the harmonic mean of normalized agent costs. We define a variation of the Trading Post mechanism where weights are inversely proportional to agents’ costs. Formally, given costs
c_{1},\dots,c_{n}
, the
inverse Trading Post
outputs an allocation
x^{\textsc{ITP}}_{i,j}=\frac{1/\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}}1/\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j}}
. The cost of agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
when participating in the inverse Trading Post mechanism is
c_{i}(x^{\textsc{ITP}}_{i})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}c_{i,j}\frac{1/\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}}1/\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j}}=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{c_{i}(\mathcal{M})}{\sum_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}}1/\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j}}=n\,c_{i}(\mathcal{M})\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}H_{j}
.
We start by expressing this cost as a function of the distance between an agent and society.

Lemma 16
.

For all
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
\frac{c_{i}(x^{\textsc{ITP}}_{i})}{c_{i}(\mathcal{M})}=\frac{1}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}-H_{j})^{2}}{\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}}\right)
.

Interestingly, the expression for the normalized cost of agent
i
in
Lemma

16
does not depend on
i
’s identity, i.e., all agents have the same normalized cost.

Next, consider the following LP that minimizes the maximum
\ell_{1}
-normalized cost of an agent.

\begin{array}[]{ll@{}ll}\text{minimize}&\alpha&\\
\text{subject to}&\alpha\geq\sum_{j=1}^{m}\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{ij}x_{ij}&\forall i\in\mathcal{N}\\
&\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}=1&\forall j\in\mathcal{M}\\
&\alpha,x_{ij}\geq 0&\forall i\in\mathcal{N},\forall j\in\mathcal{M}\end{array}

(4)

Similarly to
Lemma

14
, the following lemma argues about the existence of a good integral allocation.

Lemma 17
.

[Essentially Theorem 11 of
[
28
]
]
Let
x^{*}
be an optimal solution to LP (
4
). There exists an integral allocation
A
, such that, for all agents
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
c_{i}(A_{i})\leq c_{i}(x^{*}_{i})+\max_{j\in\mathcal{M}}c_{i,j}
.

Proof.

Similarly to
Lemma

14
, we use the market interpretation of LP (
4
) by
[
28
]
, coupled with a rounding algorithm of
[
20
]
. Specifically, LP (
4
) minimizes the minimizing the normalized
p
-mean of disutilities, for
p\rightarrow\infty
. Letting
x^{*}
be an optimal solution to LP (
4
),
[
28
]
prove that there exist rewards
r
and budgets
e
such that
(x,r,e)
is a market equilibrium.
[
20
]
give an algorithm (based on the algorithm of
[
16
]
) that, given such a market equilibrium, computes another market equilibrium
(A,r,e^{\prime})
, where
A
is an integral allocation and
e^{\prime}
satisfies certain properties. Relevant to us here,
[
28
]
prove that
c_{i}(A_{i})\leq c_{i}(x^{*}_{i})+\max_{j\in\mathcal{M}}c_{i,j}
; the lemma follows.
∎

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Theorem 9
.

If
\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{max}\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}-H_{j})^{2}}{\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}}
, then a proportional allocation exists. When the inequality is strict, then a strongly proportional allocation exists.

Proof.

The allocation of
Lemma

17
satisfies, for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
c_{i}(A_{i})\leq c_{i}(x^{*}_{i})+\max_{j\in\mathcal{M}}c_{i,j}
. Furthermore,
\frac{c_{i}(x^{*}_{i})}{c_{i}(\mathcal{M})}\leq\alpha^{*}
, where
\alpha^{*}
is the optimal value of LP (
4
).
The allocation of the inverse Trading Post mechanism,
x^{\textsc{ITP}}
, is a feasible solution for LP (
4
), therefore,

\alpha^{*}\leq\min_{z\in\mathcal{N}}\frac{c_{z}(x^{\textsc{ITP}}_{z})}{c_{z}(\mathcal{M})}
.
By
Lemma

16
, the RHS is equal to
\frac{1}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}-H_{j})^{2}}{\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}}\right)
.
Combining, we have that

\displaystyle c_{i}(A_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{i}(\mathcal{M})}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}-H_{j})^{2}}{\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}}\right)+\max_{j\in\mathcal{M}}c_{i,j}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{c_{i}(\mathcal{M})}{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}-H_{j})^{2}}{\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}}\right)+\frac{c_{i}(\mathcal{M})}{n^{2}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}-H_{j})^{2}}{\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell,j}}

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{i}(\mathcal{M})}{n}.

∎

We note that there exists an equivalent version of this theorem, where the bound on
\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{max}
involves the
\chi^{2}
-divergence between
\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i}
and
H/\|H\|_{1}
. Specifically, if
\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{max}\leq\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}\left((1-\|H\|_{1})^{2}+\|H\|^{2}_{1}D_{\chi^{2}}(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{\ell}||\frac{H}{\|H\|_{1}})\right)
, then a proportional allocation exists.

4.3.4
Proportional Allocations of Chores in the Stochastic Setting

As an application of
Theorem

7
, similarly to the case of goods, we consider a setting with stochastic preferences:
c_{i,j}
is sampled i.i.d. from the
U[0,1]
distribution. In recent work,
[
49
]
prove that, for all distributions whose PDF is bounded by constants, a proportional allocation exists with high probability for

. Here, we re-prove a weaker version of this result, by showing that the condition in

(and therefore, by

9
, a proportional allocation exists).

We start by showing a couple of technical lemmas.

Lemma 18
.

\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}-H_{j})^{2}}{\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}}\geq\langle H-\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i},g\rangle-\frac{\langle\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i},g^{2}\rangle}{4}
, where

is any vector in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
g^{2}=[g_{1}^{2},g_{2}^{2},\cdots,g_{m}^{2}]
.

Lemma 19
.

In the i.i.d.
U[0,1]
model
(c_{i,j}\sim U[0,1])
, for
m\in\Omega(n)
, it holds that
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}-H_{j})^{2}}{\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}}\geq\frac{5}{16}
, for all

, with high probability
(1-O(1/n))
.

We are now ready to state our result about the existence of proportional allocations of chores.

Theorem 10
.

In the i.i.d.
U[0,1]
model
(c_{i,j}\sim U[0,1])
, a (strongly) proportional allocation exists with high probability
(1-O(1/n))
, when

.

Proof.

Lemma

19
gives that, with high probability,
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}-H_{j})^{2}}{\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}}\geq\frac{5}{16}
, for all

\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{N}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}-H_{j})^{2}}{\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}}\geq\frac{5n}{16}

∎

\displaystyle\geq\langle H-\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i},g\rangle-\frac{\langle\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i},g^{2}\rangle}{4}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}-H_{j})-\frac{1}{4}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\frac{1}{4}\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{16}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}H_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{16}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}H_{j}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{7}{16}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\prod_{i\in[n]}\tilde{c}^{(1)}_{i,j}\right)^{1/n}

(HM-GM inequality)

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{16}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\prod_{i\in[n]}\frac{c_{i,j}}{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}}c_{i,j^{\prime}}}\right)^{1/n}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{7}{16}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\prod_{i\in[n]}\frac{c_{i,j}}{\sum_{j^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{j\}}c_{i,j^{\prime}}}\right)^{1/n}

(
0\leq c_{i,j}\leq 1
)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{7}{16}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\prod_{i\in[n]}\frac{c_{i,j}}{\frac{m}{2}-1-\sqrt{m\log n}}\right)^{1/n}

(Hoeffding high-probability bound)

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{16}-\frac{1}{m-2-2\sqrt{m\log n}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}}\left(\prod_{i\in[n]}c_{i,j}\right)^{1/n}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{7}{16}-\frac{m\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n}+\sqrt{m\log n}}{m-2-2\sqrt{m\log n}}

(Hoeffding high-probability bound)

\displaystyle=\frac{7}{16}-\frac{\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n}+\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{m}}}{1-\frac{2}{m}-2\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{m}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{7}{16}-\frac{\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n}+\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{m}}}{1-3\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{m}}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{7}{16}-\frac{\frac{4}{9}+\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{m}}}{1-3\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{m}}}

(
\left(\frac{n}{n+1}\right)^{n}

is decreasing
)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{5}{16}.

∎

Appendix E
Allocating Goods to Similar Agents

So far, we have considered the situation where agents’ valuation vectors are dissimilar and proved that fair allocations exist. It is well understood that when agents are identical, EFX allocations exist. As a proof of concept, in this section we prove that when agents are similar — but not identical — EFX allocations of goods exist, complementing our previous results.

We consider the fairness notion of
tEFX
, or “transfer EFX”. An integral allocation
A
is tEFX if, for all
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
, for all goods
j\in A_{i^{\prime}}
, such that
v_{i}(A_{i})+v_{i,j}\geq v_{i}(A_{i^{\prime}})-v_{i,j}
. Note that this notion is stronger than (i.e., it implies) EF2x, which demands that the removal of any two distinct goods eliminates envy, and was recently shown to exist for four additive agents
[
8
]
. Here, we prove that tEFX allocations exist for any number of agents that are “pairwise similar.” Formally, we require a bound on the TV distance between
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}
and
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime}}
for any pair of agents
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
.

We use the following greedy algorithm.
Algorithm

1
allocates the item that is the least desirable to agent
1
to the agent with the smallest utility from the point of view of agent
1
; note that if
v_{1}(S)\geq v_{1}(S^{\prime})
, then
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(S)\geq\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(S^{\prime})
, for any two bundles
S
and
S^{\prime}
. This algorithm produces an EFX allocation for identical agents. Here we prove that it also produces a tEFX allocation for non-identical, but “similar,” agents.

Algorithm 1

Greedy Allocation

Initialize
A=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
, with
A_{i}=\emptyset
.

Rename the items by decreasing order in terms of
v_{1,j}
(i.e.,
v_{1,1}\geq v_{1,2}\geq\dots
).

for
each item
j
in the sorted order
do

i\leftarrow\arg\min_{i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}}v_{1}(A_{i^{\prime}})

A_{i}\leftarrow A_{i}\cup\{j\}

end

for

Theorem 11
.

Let
D^{*}=\max_{i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}}D_{TV}(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}||\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime}})
. If
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{min}\geq 8D^{*}
, then a tEFX allocation exists.

Proof.

Let
A
be the allocation of
Algorithm

1
, and let
i,i^{\prime}\in\mathcal{N}
be any pair of agents such that
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(A_{i})\geq\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(A_{i^{\prime}})
. By the definition of TV,
|\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(A_{i})-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}(A_{i})|\leq 2D_{TV}(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}||\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i})\leq 2D^{*}
, and
|v_{1}(A_{i^{\prime}})-v_{i}(A_{i^{\prime}})|\leq 2D^{*}
. Combining the inequalities, we have that
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}(A_{i^{\prime}})-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}(A_{i})=\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}(A_{i^{\prime}})+\left(\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(A_{i^{\prime}})-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(A_{i^{\prime}})\right)+\left(-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(A_{i})+\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(A_{i})\right)-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i}(A_{i})\leq 4D^{*}\leq\frac{\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{min}}{2}
; therefore, since envy is scale-free, the removal of any item from
A_{i^{\prime}}
eliminates
i
’s envy.

Next, let
j
be the last item
i
received in
Algorithm

1
; then, it must be that
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(A_{i})-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1,j}\leq\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(A_{i^{\prime}})
. By the definition of TV, we have
|\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(A_{i})-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime}}(A_{i})|\leq 2D^{*}
and
|\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1}(A_{i^{\prime}})-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime}}(A_{i^{\prime}})|\leq 2D^{*}
. Combining, we have that
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime}}(A_{i})-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime}}(A_{i^{\prime}})\leq 4D^{*}+\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1,j}
. Let
j^{*}\in argmin_{\ell\in A_{i}}\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},\ell}
. By the definition of TV, we have
|\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j^{*}}-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1,j^{*}}|\leq 2D^{*}
and
|\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j}-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1,j}|\leq 2D^{*}
. Since
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j^{*}}\leq\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j}
(
j\in A_{i}
and
j^{*}\in argmin_{\ell\in A_{i}}\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},\ell}
), and
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1,j}\leq\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1,j^{*}}
(
j^{*}
was included before
j
in the bundle
A_{i}
), we have that
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{1,j}\leq 4D^{*}+\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j^{*}}
.
Combining, we have that
\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime}}(A_{i})-\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime}}(A_{i^{\prime}})\leq 8D^{*}+\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j^{*}}\leq\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{min}+\tilde{v}^{(1)}_{i^{\prime},j^{*}}
. Therefore, transferring any item from
A_{i}
to
A_{i^{\prime}}
eliminates the envy of
i^{\prime}
.
∎
