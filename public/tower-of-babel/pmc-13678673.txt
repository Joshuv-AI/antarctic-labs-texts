Comparative biochemical analysis of tea (Camellia sinensis) and coffee (Coffea arabica) extracts identifies the ethyl acetate fraction of green tea as a potential dual digestive enzyme inhibitor: an in vitro and in silico study
Authors: Youmna E. El-Saied, Abdullah A. Elgazar, Fardous F. El-Senduny, Nada E. Hammouda, Omali Y. El-Khawaga, Farid A. Badria
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic diseases, including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), have prompted interest in identifying natural products capable of modulating digestive enzymes. This study comparatively evaluated the phytochemical composition, antioxidant activity, and digestive enzyme inhibitory activities of green tea (GT), black tea (BT), and green coffee (GC) extracts. Phytochemical screening, antioxidant assays (DPPH, ABTS, and SOD-like), and quantitative phenolic analysis indicated that GT contained the highest phenolic content and exhibited the greatest antioxidant activity, whereas GC showed the highest alkaloid content. Among the fractionated extracts, the ethyl acetate fraction of green tea (EtOAc fraction of GT) was selected for further investigation based on its phenolic content, pancreatic lipase inhibitory activity, and overall biological profile. HPLC analysis identified ferulic acid and chlorogenic acid as major constituents, together with syringic acid, resveratrol, myricetin, and rosmarinic acid. Both the crude BT extract and EtOAc fraction of GT exhibited α-amylase inhibitory activity comparable to that of quercetin. Kinetic analysis indicated competitive inhibition by the EtOAc fraction of GT (IC50 = 0.662 mg mL−1), whereas BT displayed mixed-type inhibition with a strong uncompetitive component. The EtOAc fraction of GT also showed the lowest IC50 against pancreatic lipase (0.264 mg mL−1) and followed a non-competitive inhibition mechanism. The EtOAc fraction maintained 98.94% of human skin fibroblast viability after 48 h at 500 µg mL−1 in a preliminary cytotoxicity screen. Molecular docking was consistent with the experimental findings, suggesting that hesperidin and rutin may contribute to competitive α-amylase inhibition and allosteric pancreatic lipase modulation. These findings indicate that the ethyl acetate fraction of green tea is a phenolic-rich fraction with dual digestive enzyme inhibitory activity that merits further evaluation in appropriate biological models.

Introduction

Metabolic diseases, including obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), are a growing global health burden. These disorders are closely linked by insulin resistance and chronic low-grade inflammation and are driven by genetic susceptibility and lifestyle factors, including physical inactivity and energy-dense diets.1,2

Current management of metabolic diseases relies on pharmacological therapies and lifestyle modification. However, medications may be associated with adverse effects or reduced long-term efficacy, while sustained adherence to dietary and physical activity interventions remains challenging, highlighting the need for complementary therapeutic strategies.3 As natural products represent a major reservoir for compounds with polypharmacological activities,4,5 an opportunity emerges for novel interventions, where functional foods and their constituent bioactive compounds are gaining considerable attention for their therapeutic potential.6,7

Among emerging strategies, inhibition of the digestive enzymes α-amylase and pancreatic lipase has attracted considerable interest as an approach to reducing dietary carbohydrate and fat absorption. Inhibition of α-amylase slows starch digestion and attenuates postprandial glucose excursions, whereas inhibition of pancreatic lipase limits triglyceride hydrolysis and reduces intestinal lipid absorption.8 Together, these complementary mechanisms may help decrease metabolic overload associated with obesity, insulin resistance, and hepatic lipid accumulation, providing a rationale for investigating dual digestive enzyme inhibitors as potential nutraceuticals for metabolic disease management.9–11

Many of the natural compounds capable of this inhibition are phenolics,12–14 whose relevance is amplified by pleiotropic effects beyond the gut. In vivo, studies demonstrate that absorbed phenolics exert direct hepatoprotective effects. For instance, compounds like quercetin and curcumin have been shown in animal models to reduce liver fat and inflammation by activating key cellular pathways such as AMPK and enhancing autophagy.15,16 This is confirmed by clinical evidence, where supplementation with phenolics like silymarin, curcumin, hesperidin, and quercetin has led to significant improvements in NAFLD markers, including reductions in liver enzymes and intrahepatic lipid content.16,17 Indicating that phenolic enzyme inhibitors function through a dual-action mechanism: they act as upstream gatekeepers to reduce metabolic load and as downstream agents that directly ameliorate the pathological processes of fat accumulation and inflammation within the liver.18

A primary source of such bioactive phenolics is green tea, derived from the leaves of Camellia sinensis. Its rich phytochemical profile comprises a diverse range of bioactive constituents, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, catechin, alkaloids and other polyphenolic compounds which collectively contribute to its well-documented antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic regulatory properties. These phytochemicals have been reported to modulate key cellular signaling pathways involved in lipid and glucose homeostasis, including AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and sterol regulatory element-binding proteins (SREBPs). Thereby contributing to the prevention and management of metabolic disorders.19–22

The therapeutic potential of green tea has been extensively investigated in both in vitro and in vivo models. Previous studies have demonstrated that green tea phytochemicals can attenuate lipid accumulation, enhance fatty acid oxidation, improve glucose tolerance, and alleviate hepatic steatosis through multiple complementary mechanism. Although catechins, particularly epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), have been widely recognized as important contributions to these biological effects, the overall pharmacological activity of green tea is considered to result from the synergistic interactions among its diverse phytochemical constituents.23,24

While compelling, clinical trials in humans have yielded more varied results, with some reporting significant improvements in NAFLD markers, whereas others have demonstrated only modest benefits. These inconsistencies may be attributed to differences in study design, treatment duration, dosage, phytochemical composition of green tea preparations, and the bioavailability of their bioactive constituents.

This study aimed to compare the phytochemical profiles, antioxidant capacity, α-amylase and pancreatic lipase inhibition, and biocompatibility of green tea, black tea, green coffee, roasted coffee and roasted coffee marc and their solvent fractions to identify promising candidates for functional food development. We hypothesised that the phenolic-rich ethyl acetate fraction of green tea would combine dual enzyme inhibition with favourable biocompatibility. Molecular docking was used to explore potential interactions underlying the observed inhibition. These in vitro and in silico assessments were intended to guide future in vivo studies rather than establish clinical efficacy.

Results and discussion

Phytochemical profile of plant extracts

The phytochemical investigation began with a qualitative screening of the crude methanolic extracts, which revealed a diverse array of secondary metabolites across the different samples (Table 1). Notably, the black tea (BT) extract showed a strong presence of flavonoids and coumarins, while green tea (GT) was particularly rich in phenolic compounds. Green coffee (GC) was distinguished by its high content of alkaloids.

Antioxidant activity

In order to obtain insights on the antioxidant capacities of the five crude methanolic extracts, DPPH, ABTS radical scavenging assays, as well as an SOD-like activity assay were conducted as presented in Fig. 1. The GT extract demonstrated superior antioxidant activity across all three assays, with an ABTS inhibition of 86.47 ± 0.58% and SOD-like activity of 84.8 ± 0.75%. The GC extract also showed potent radical-scavenging properties, nearly matching GT in the ABTS and DPPH assays. The high antioxidant potential of green tea is well-documented and is primarily attributed to its diverse phytochemical composition, including phenolic acids, flavonoids, catechins, alkaloids, and other polyphenolic constituents, which collectively act as hydrogen donors and radical scavengers.25 Similarly, the potent activity of green coffee is attributed to its abundance of chlorogenic acids, which are largely degraded during the high temperatures of the roasting process. This degradation explains the significantly lower antioxidant activity observed in the roasted coffee (RC) and marc of roasted coffee (MRC) crudes.26 The pronounced antioxidant activities of the green tea and green coffee extracts identified them as prime candidates for further investigation in metabolic disease. Consequently, these crudes were subjected to fractionation and detailed chemical analysis to determine their phenolic and alkaloid profiles, thereby aiming to establish a direct correlation with their potent antioxidant capacity. The antioxidant assays were intended as comparative screening experiments performed under identical experimental conditions, allowing relative evaluation of antioxidant activity among the tested extracts rather than quantitative determination of antioxidant potency.

Following this, the total phenolic (TPC) and total alkaloid (TAC) contents were quantified for the crude extracts and the subsequent fractions of GT and GC (Table 2). The ethyl acetate fraction of green tea (EtOAc fraction of GT) exhibited the highest phenolic content by a significant margin, measuring 2635 ± 3.3 µg mL−1. This is consistent with established phytochemical principles, where the medium polarity of ethyl acetate makes it highly effective for extracting polyphenols such as catechins and their derivatives from an initial aqueous methanolic extract. Conversely, the aqueous residue of green tea (Aqu. R GT) contained the highest concentration of alkaloids (3.8 ± 0.0577 µg mL−1). This suggests that the major alkaloids present in green tea, such as caffeine and theophylline, are highly polar or exist as salts, leading to their retention in the final aqueous phase after partitioning against less polar organic solvents.

Inhibition of enzymes involved in metabolic disorders

α-Amylase inhibition

Inhibition of α-amylase is a key therapeutic strategy for managing postprandial hyperglycemia.27,28 In this study, the crude BT extract and the EtOAc fraction of GT fraction emerged as the most potent inhibitors of α-amylase, with inhibition percentages of 89.39 ± 4.21% and 81.26 ± 3.43%, respectively, which were comparable to the positive control, quercetin (92.64 ± 2.48%) as shown in Fig. 2. This could be explained in the light of phytochemical investigations which revealed the enrichment of BT and GT with flavonoids and phenolics respectively. This is also in agreement with previous studies reporting the inhibitory action of this class of compounds on α-amylase.29

To elucidate the underlying inhibitory mechanisms, enzyme kinetic studies were conducted on the BT and EtOAc fraction of GT extracts. The IC50 values were determined to be 0.574 mg mL−1 for BT, 0.662 mg mL−1 for EtOAc fraction of GT, and 0.476 mg mL−1 for quercetin (Fig. 3A). The Lineweaver–Burk analysis for EtOAc fraction of GT revealed a competitive mode of inhibition. In the presence of the extract, the apparent Michaelis constant (Km, app) increased from 5.7 mg mL−1 to 7.5 mg mL−1, while the maximum velocity (Vmax) remained constant (Fig. 3B). This profile indicates that compounds within the EtOAc fraction of GT fraction compete with the starch substrate for the α-amylase active site. Accordingly, the inhibition constant (Ki) for this competitive inhibitor was calculated to be 2.1 mg mL−1. This finding is consistent with studies demonstrating that polyphenols can bind to the enzyme's active site, forming hydrogen bonds with key residues such as Asp197 and Glu233, thereby blocking substrate access.30

(A) IC50 of the enzyme inhibitor activity for EtOAc fraction of GT, BT and Quercetin. (B) Lineweaver–Burk plots of the activities of α-amylase with or without EtOAc fraction of GT. (C) Lineweaver–Burk plots of the activities of α-amylase with or without BT.

In contrast, the BT extract exhibited mixed-type inhibition, as evidenced by simultaneous decreases in both the apparent Km and Vmax in the presence of the inhibitor (Fig. 3C). This kinetic behavior indicates that the inhibitory constituents of BT are capable of binding to both the free enzyme (E) and the enzyme–substrate (ES) complex. To quantify these interactions, two inhibition constants were determined. The inhibition constant for binding to the ES complex , calculated using the uncompetitive inhibition model, was 0.34 mg mL−1, whereas the inhibition constant for binding to the free enzyme (Ki) was 10.25 mg mL−1. The substantially lower demonstrates that the inhibitory compounds have a much higher affinity for the ES complex than for the free enzyme, indicating that the mixed inhibition is dominated by an uncompetitive component. This preferential binding to the ES complex suggests that the inhibitors interact with an allosteric site that becomes more favorable or accessible following substrate binding and the associated conformational changes in the enzyme. These findings are consistent with a previous study reporting mixed inhibition with preferential ES binding for black tea polyphenols against pancreatic lipase.31,32

Pancreatic lipase inhibition

Since the inhibition of pancreatic lipase is a primary strategy for reducing dietary fat absorption and improving the level of cholesterol and overall cardiovascular health, the effect of different extracts and fractions of tea and coffee on pancreatic lipase was studied. Among them, the EtOAc fraction of GT fraction was the most potent inhibitor by a significant margin, with an inhibition of 93 ± 0.577%, which was statistically similar to the positive control, orlistat (94 ± 0.577%). Other extracts and fractions showed variable inhibitory effect but less than 85% inhibition (Fig. 4). Hence, the inhibitory effect of EtOAc fraction of GT and its inhibitory mode was further studied.

The IC50 calculation showed that EtOAc fraction of GT and orlistat were 0.264 mg mL−1 and 0.237 mg mL−1 (Fig. 5A). Kinetic analysis of the EtOAc fraction of GT fraction against pancreatic lipase revealed a non-competitive mode of inhibition (Fig. 5B). Here, Vmax decreased from 5.2 × 10−3 min−1 to 3.4 × 10−3 min−1, while Km remained unchanged. This indicates that the inhibitor binds to an allosteric site on the lipase enzyme, a site distinct from the active site where the triglyceride substrate binds. This binding induces a conformational change that reduces the enzyme's catalytic efficiency without affecting its ability to bind the substrate. Consequently, using the kinetic equation for non-competitive inhibition, the apparent inhibition constant (Ki) was calculated to be 0.498 mg mL−1. Interestingly, the non-competitive inhibition of lipase by different cultivar and processed teas is well-supported in the literature.33–35

Pearson's correlation analysis was performed to further investigate the relationships among phytochemical composition, antioxidant capacity, and enzyme inhibitory activities of the crude extracts. A significant positive correlation was observed between total alkaloid content and pancreatic lipase inhibition (r = 0.945, p = 0.015). Likewise, DPPH radical scavenging activity was strongly correlated with SOD-like activity (r = 0.962, p = 0.009), confirming the consistency of the antioxidant assays. Positive trends were also observed between total phenolic content and SOD-like activity (r = 0.833, p = 0.080), as well as between total phenolic content and pancreatic lipase inhibition (r = 0.862, p = 0.060); however, these associations did not reach statistical significance, likely due to the limited number of crude extracts evaluated. Pearson's correlation analysis of the fractionated extracts revealed no statistically significant relationships between total phenolic content, total alkaloid content, α-amylase inhibition, and pancreatic lipase inhibition (all p ≥ 0.154). This finding suggests that the inhibitory activity of the individual fractions is more likely influenced by the qualitative composition and interactions of specific phytochemicals than by their total phenolic or alkaloid contents alone.

Chemical characterization of bioactive extracts

The crude black tea extract and the green tea EtOAc fraction were selected for targeted HPLC characterization based on their enzyme-inhibitory profiles. Black tea exhibited the strongest α-amylase inhibition (IC50 = 0.574 mg mL−1), whereas the green tea EtOAc fraction exhibited the strongest pancreatic lipase inhibition (IC50 = 0.264 mg mL−1) and substantial α-amylase inhibition (IC50 = 0.662 mg mL−1). This analysis aimed to characterize selected constituents of these active preparations. Establishing relationships between individual phenolic constituents and inhibitory activity across all preparations would require broader comparative profiling and subsequent activity-guided investigation.

HPLC analysis

Quantitative HPLC analysis confirmed that both extracts were rich in phenolic compounds and caffeine (Fig. S1). The EtOAc fraction of GT fraction contained a substantially higher concentration of caffeine (17 370.9 mg kg−1) compared to the BT extract (12 747.2 mg kg−1). Comprehensive phenolic profiling identified 12 compounds in EtOAc fraction of GT and 10 in BT (Table 3). Although the aqueous residual fraction exhibited the highest total alkaloid content in the colorimetric assay (Table 2), the HPLC analysis showed enrichment of caffeine within the ethyl acetate fraction. This apparent discrepancy reflects the different analytical principles of the two methods. The total alkaloid assay provides an estimate of the cumulative alkaloid content without distinguishing individual compounds, whereas HPLC selectively quantifies individual constituents. Consequently, enrichment of caffeine in the ethyl acetate fraction does not necessarily correspond to the highest total alkaloid content, as other more polar alkaloids and nitrogen-containing constituents may remain preferentially distributed in the aqueous residual fraction following solvent partitioning. Therefore, the findings obtained by the two analytical approaches are complementary rather than contradictory. EtOAc fraction of GT was found to be particularly rich in ferulic acid (6608.2 mg kg−1) and chlorogenic acid (5310.2 mg kg−1), while the major phenolic in BT was chlorogenic acid (6757.1 mg kg−1). The presence of these compounds is highly relevant to a biochemical basis for future in vivo studies such as NAFLD, as both ferulic acid and chlorogenic acid have been shown to prevent hepatic lipid accumulation and reduce liver injury in preclinical models, partly through the activation of PPARα36 and modulation of gut microbiota.37

Also, the EtOAc fraction of GT fraction contained four additional phenolic compounds not detected in BT: syringic acid, resveratrol, myricetin, and rosmarinic acid. Each of these compounds has well-documented benefits in metabolic diseases. For instance, resveratrol is known to ameliorate hepatic steatosis by activating SIRT1,38 myricetin has anti-obesity effects through SIRT3 upregulation,39 syringic acid protects against NAFLD via the Nrf2/HO-1 pathway,40 and rosmarinic acid repairs mitochondrial damage.41 This richer and more diverse phenolic profile in the unfermented green tea fraction likely contributes to its pronounced bioactivity in management of complex metabolic disorder like metabolic syndrome. Hence, the observed amylase lipase-inhibition gives broader therapeutic potential of the ethyl acetate fraction of green tea. Regarding the alkaloid content, BT extract was found to be rich with caffeine by concentration 12 747.180 mg kg−1 at RT 2.38 min. While EtOAc fraction of GT extract by concentration 17 370.909 mg kg−1 at RT 2.39 min (Fig. 2S). Caffeine has been associated with regulation of energy expenditure and improving lipolysis42–44 and more importantly, inhibiting liver fibrosis via upregulation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) and reduction of SMAD2/3.45

In vitro cell viability assessment

The four green-tea fractions were evaluated in normal human skin fibroblasts (HSF) as a preliminary comparative cytotoxicity screen to support fraction selection. After 48 h of exposure at 500 µg mL−1, the EtOAc fraction maintained 98.94% cell viability relative to the control (Fig. 6). Previous animal and human studies provide contextual evidence of tolerability for specific green-tea preparations. Takami et al.46 reported no-observed-adverse-effect levels of 764 and 820 mg per kg body weight/day for green-tea catechins in male and female rats, respectively, following 90-day dietary administration. Shen et al.47 reported that green-tea polyphenol supplementation at 500 mg per day for 24 weeks did not adversely affect monitored liver or kidney function parameters in postmenopausal women, with no treatment-related adverse events reported. These findings support further investigation of green-tea preparations but do not establish the safety of the present EtOAc fraction. Our result indicates limited cytotoxicity under the tested conditions; broader assessment requires concentration-dependent testing, an appropriate positive control and additional relevant cell models.

In silico investigation of molecular mechanism of ethyl acetate fraction of green tea

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms responsible for the inhibitory activity of the green tea ethyl acetate (EtOAc fraction of GT) fraction, a comprehensive in silico molecular docking study was conducted. This analysis focused on key phytochemicals identified through HPLC profiling of the EtOAc fraction, correlating their predicted molecular interactions with experimental enzyme kinetics data for α-amylase and pancreatic lipase. From the full compound library, four major phytoconstituents—hesperidin, rutin, chlorogenic acid, and rosmarinic acid—were selected based on their strong binding affinities (Table 4) and relevance to observed biological activity. The binding energies of these and other compounds were used to support mechanistic hypotheses and are discussed in relation to structural interaction profiles.

α-Amylase: competitive active site blockade

Hesperidin exhibited the highest binding affinity toward α-amylase (−8.21 kcal mol−1), forming a stable complex within the catalytic pocket (Fig. 7A). The neohesperidose moiety played a central role in ligand anchoring, with hydroxyl groups forming hydrogen bonds with the catalytic residues Asp197 and Glu233, while an additional hydrogen bond was established with Gln63. The flavanone aglycone further stabilized the complex through complementary interactions. The oxygen atom of the pyran ring formed a hydrogen bond with Lys200, whereas the A-ring hydroxyl group interacted with His305. The aromatic rings were additionally stabilized by hydrophobic π–alkyl interactions with Ile235, Ala307, and the catalytic residue Asp300. The simultaneous engagement of multiple catalytic residues together with extensive hydrophobic contacts explains the favorable binding affinity of hesperidin and supports its contribution to the competitive inhibition of α-amylase.

Two-dimensional interaction diagrams of selected phytochemicals from the ethyl acetate fraction of green tea docked within the catalytic site of α-amylase (PDB ID: 4W93). The panels show: (A) Hesperidin, (B) Chlorogenic acid, (C) Rutin, and (D) Rosmarinic acid.

Rutin also displayed high binding affinity (−7.73 kcal mol−1) through the cooperative contribution of both its quercetin aglycone and rutinose disaccharide (Fig. 7B). The sugar moiety directly interacted with the catalytic residues Asp197 and Glu233, while additional hydrogen bonds with Tyr62 and His201 further stabilized the complex. The quercetin nucleus contributed several hydrogen bonds involving Lys200, Leu237, Tyr151, and His305, together with hydrophobic π–alkyl interactions with Ile235 and Ala307. A π–π T-shaped interaction with Tyr151 further reinforced ligand stabilization. The combination of hydrogen bonding, aromatic stacking, and hydrophobic interactions indicates efficient occupation of the catalytic pocket and supports the observed α-amylase inhibitory activity.

Chlorogenic acid bound within the α-amylase catalytic pocket with a binding affinity of −5.91 kcal mol−1 (Fig. 7C). The quinic acid moiety primarily mediated recognition of the catalytic center, forming hydrogen bonds with Asp197 and Glu233, in addition to His299. The caffeic acid portion contributed an additional hydrogen bond with Lys200, while its aromatic ring established hydrophobic interactions with Ile235 and Tyr151. Although chlorogenic acid formed fewer interactions than hesperidin and rutin, its direct engagement with two catalytic residues provides a structural basis for competitive inhibition and is consistent with its moderate binding affinity.

Rosmarinic acid exhibited a comparable binding affinity (−5.85 kcal mol−1), occupying the α-amylase active site through a combination of hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 7D). Its 3,4-dihydroxyphenyllactic acid moiety formed hydrogen bonds with the catalytic residue Glu233 and His305, while the aromatic ring established π–alkyl interactions with Asp300 and Tyr62. The caffeic acid moiety further contributed hydrophobic contacts with Lys200, Ile235, and His201, providing additional stabilization within the binding pocket. Although its interaction network was less extensive than those of hesperidin and rutin, the observed contacts support its ability to bind within the catalytic site and contribute to α-amylase inhibition.

Overall, the docking results demonstrate that flavonoid glycosides, particularly hesperidin and rutin, establish more extensive hydrogen-bonding and hydrophobic interaction networks than the phenolic acids chlorogenic acid and rosmarinic acid. Importantly, direct interactions with the catalytic residues Asp197, Glu233, and Asp300 provide a molecular explanation for the experimentally observed competitive inhibition of α-amylase by the EtOAc green tea fraction. This level of interaction is consistent with their higher docking scores and likely contributes to the overall inhibitory activity of the extract.

Predicted interactions at a previously reported pancreatic lipase allosteric pocket

Hesperidin exhibited the highest binding affinity toward pancreatic lipase (−9.60 kcal mol−1), occupying an allosteric binding pocket distinct from the catalytic site (Fig. 8A). The neohesperidose disaccharide played a central role in ligand stabilization by forming an extensive hydrogen-bonding network with Arg368, Asp390, and Arg398. Additional hydrogen bonds involving Leu36 and Arg38, together with hydrophobic π–alkyl interactions with Ile249, Ala40, and Leu41, further stabilized the flavanone scaffold. These predicted contacts suggest a plausible binding pose for hesperidin within the selected pocket; its effect on enzyme conformation and activity requires experimental verification.

Rutin also demonstrated high binding affinity (−8.70 kcal mol−1) within the same allosteric pocket (Fig. 8B). Both the quercetin aglycone and rutinose disaccharide contributed to ligand stabilization. The aglycone established amide–π stacking interactions with Glu13 and Ala333, a salt bridge with Asp332, hydrophobic interactions with Lys240 and Ala40, and a hydrogen bond with Arg338. The sugar moiety further reinforced binding through hydrogen bonds with Asp31 and Leu41, accompanied by additional hydrophobic contacts. These contacts describe the predicted accommodation of rutin within the selected pocket.

Chlorogenic acid bound favorably within the allosteric pocket with a binding affinity of −7.50 kcal mol−1 (Fig. 8C). The quinic acid moiety contributed multiple hydrogen bonds with Gln29, Tyr59, Cys61, and Leu41, while the ester carbonyl formed additional hydrogen bonds with Arg368 and Leu41. The caffeic acid moiety complemented these interactions through hydrogen bonding with Arg38 and a π–alkyl interaction with Ala40, resulting in stable accommodation of the ligand within the allosteric site.

Similarly, rosmarinic acid exhibited a binding affinity of −7.30 kcal mol−1 and occupied the allosteric pocket through complementary polar and hydrophobic interactions (Fig. 8D). The caffeic acid moiety formed hydrogen bonds with Gln29, Arg38, and Leu41, together with π–alkyl interactions involving Arg38, Ala40, and Ile249. The 3,4-dihydroxyphenyllactic acid moiety further stabilized the complex through hydrogen bonds with Ala333, Arg398, and Arg368. These interactions indicate that These contacts suggest that rosmarinic acid can be accommodated within the selected pocket in the docking model.

Among the compounds docked to the selected pancreatic-lipase pocket, hesperidin and rutin yielded the most favourable docking scores and extensive predicted interaction networks. These findings suggest possible binding interactions at a previously reported allosteric region. However, because docking was directed to this pocket, the results do not establish preferential binding over the catalytic site. They also do not independently demonstrate noncompetitive inhibition or identify the constituents responsible for the activity of the complete fraction. The proposed interactions require experimental verification.

In summary, the integrated analysis for α-amylase provides computational support for a competitive inhibition mechanism. The kinetic data for the EtOAc fraction of GT fraction indicated a competitive profile, and the docking simulations offer a plausible structural basis for this observation. The models show that selected phytochemicals, particularly hesperidin and rutin, occupy the catalytic cleft and interact directly with the catalytic triad residues (Asp197, Glu233, and Asp300). This observation is consistent with previous literature. For instance, mutagenesis studies established the essential role of these specific residues in α-amylase catalysis.48 Furthermore, subsequent work by ref. 49 on flavonoid inhibitors concluded that potent, competitive inhibition is achieved through the formation of hydrogen bonds with this same catalytic triad. Our structural findings for hesperidin and rutin are therefore in agreement with this established model of active site blockade.

For pancreatic lipase, the findings strongly suggest a non-competitive, allosteric mechanism of inhibition. The non-competitive kinetic profile of the extract implies that the inhibitors act at a site distinct from the substrate-binding pocket. The docking analysis supports this by identifying a high-affinity allosteric pocket as the most favorable binding location for compounds like hesperidin and rutin. This proposed mechanism is consistent with the established model of lipase regulation, which is critically dependent on the conformational change of a mobile “lid” domain for interfacial activation, a feature first detailed in its X-ray structure.49 The allosteric site identified in our models is located near the hinge of this lid, suggesting that inhibitor binding could restrict its necessary movement. This mode of allosteric lid immobilization has been reported for other non-competitive inhibitors; for example, recent studies identified a similar allosteric pocket for a series of salicylanilide inhibitors.50 Our findings therefore contribute to the growing body of evidence supporting allosteric modulation as a key inhibitory strategy against pancreatic lipase. Still, The docking results are presented as a computational hypothesis to support the experimental kinetic findings and do not constitute definitive proof of binding mode or mechanism.

The present study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the investigation was limited to biochemical characterization, antioxidant evaluation, digestive enzyme inhibition assays, cytotoxicity screening, and molecular docking analyses; therefore, the findings should be considered preliminary and do not constitute direct evidence of therapeutic efficacy against NAFLD or other metabolic disorders. Second, antioxidant activity was evaluated at a single concentration, precluding IC50 determination, while the cytotoxicity assessment was limited to a single normal human fibroblast (HSF) cell line. The inclusion of an appropriate positive control is also recommended to further validate the biocompatibility and safety profile of the investigated fractions. In addition, the phytochemical characterization did not include quantitative determination of the major green tea catechins (EGCG, EGC, ECG, and EC) as it was beyond the defined scope of the investigation, which focused on characterizing the phenolic acids and flavonoids for which reference standards were available. Future studies employing validated chromatographic methods, concentration-dependent antioxidant analyses, hepatic cell and animal models, and clinical investigations are warranted to further characterize the active fraction and confirm its biological and translational potential.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence for the potential of green tea's ethyl acetate fraction (EtOAc fraction of GT) as a valuable nutraceutical intervention for managing metabolic diseases and preventing non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Our bio-phytochemical characterization revealed a rich phenolic composition in EtOAc fraction of GT, highlighted by the presence of ferulic acid, chlorogenic acid, syringic acid, resveratrol, myricetin, and rosmarinic acid. Although the black tea crude extract exhibited a lower IC50 toward α-amylase than the EtOAc fraction of GT fraction, the latter was selected for further investigation because of its balanced inhibitory activity against both digestive enzymes, together with its favorable phytochemical profile, and antioxidant capacity. Crucially, EtOAc fraction of GT demonstrated significant inhibitory activities against both α-amylase and pancreatic lipase, key enzymes in carbohydrate and lipid digestion, respectively. The observed competitive inhibition for α-amylase and non-competitive inhibition for pancreatic lipase, was also supported by in silico molecular docking studies. Furthermore, The antioxidant activity and maintenance of human skin fibroblast viability at the tested concentration provide additional grounds for further investigation, although comprehensive toxicological evaluation is required before safety or therapeutic suitability can be established. Collectively, these findings identify the ethyl acetate fraction of green tea as a promising source of bioactive phytochemicals with dual inhibitory activity against α-amylase and pancreatic lipase. Although these findings provide a biochemical basis for further investigation, additional pharmacological, pharmacokinetic, and in vivo studies are required before therapeutic applications can be considered.

Material and methods

Plant materials

Five plant-based materials were investigated: black tea (BT), green tea (GT), green coffee beans (GC), roasted coffee beans (RC), and roasted coffee marc (MRC). Black tea (Camellia sinensis), commercially available as Lipton tea beads, was purchased from a local market in Mansoura, Dakahlia, Egypt. Green tea leaves of Chinese origin and green and roasted coffee beans (Coffea arabica L.) of Brazilian origin were purchased from El-Nekeety Herbal Store, Mansoura, Dakahlia, Egypt. Geographical origins were based on information provided by the supplier. The botanical identity of the tea and coffee materials, belonging to the Theaceae and Rubiaceae families, respectively, was authenticated by the Department of Pharmacognosy, Kafrelsheikh University.

Roasted coffee marc was collected immediately after brewing, spread in a thin layer, and dried in a forced-air oven at 40–45 °C for 24 h. The dried residue was ground into a fine powder, sieved, and stored in airtight amber containers at room temperature, protected from light, until extraction. The powdered marc was extracted with 70% aqueous methanol for 1 h at 25 °C under magnetic stirring. The mixture was filtered, and the filtrate was concentrated under reduced pressure at 40 °C. The resulting crude extract was stored at −20 °C until analysis.

Place the marc extraction procedure in Section 4.2 and identify it as an exception to the 48 h extraction procedure currently described for all five materials.

Preparation of extracts and fractionation

Each of the five plant materials (250 g) was ground into a powder and extracted by maceration with 70% aqueous methanol (PioChem™, Egypt, Cat. No. MET0300) (3 × 1.5 L) at regular intervals for 48 hours at 25 °C. The combined methanolic filtrates were concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator at 45 °C to yield the crude methanolic extracts. The extraction yields were as follows: BT (21 g, 8.40%), GT (25 g, 10.00%), GC (18 g, 7.20%), RC (22 g, 8.80%), and MRC (17 g, 6.80%). The extracts exhibiting the highest antioxidant activity were selected for further fractionation. The selected crude extract was dissolved in a minimal amount of methanol, suspended in distilled water, and partitioned sequentially in a separating funnel with petroleum ether (PioChem™, Egypt, Cat. No. P011), methylene chloride (El-Nasr Pharmaceutical Chemicals™, Egypt, Cat. No. M0158111), and ethyl acetate (Merck KGaA, Germany, Cat. No. K2397923). Each solvent fraction and the final aqueous residue were collected and evaporated to dryness for further analysis. The fractionation yields from GT crude extract (25 g) were: P.E fraction (0.21 g, 0.84%), M.C fraction (1.00 g, 4.00%), EtOAc fraction (4.20 g, 16.80%), and aqueous residue (7.70 g, 30.80%). The fractionation yields from GC crude extract (18 g) were: P.E fraction (0.80 g, 4.44%), M.C fraction (1.80 g, 10.00%), EtOAc fraction (1.80 g, 10.00%), and aqueous residue (10.20 g, 56.67%). All dry crude extracts and fractions were stored at −20 °C in sealed amber glass vials under desiccation to prevent photodegradation and oxidative degradation of polyphenolic constituents. Working stock solutions were prepared immediately prior to each experiment by dissolving a precisely weighed quantity of dry extract in suitable solvent according to the assassin application. A single homogeneous batch of each extract was used throughout all biological experiments to ensure inter-assay reproducibility.

Phytochemical investigation

The phytochemical profile of the extracts was investigated through both qualitative and quantitative analyses. Initially, the crude extracts were screened for the presence of major secondary metabolite classes using standard colorimetric tests as described by.51 This screening included tests for carbohydrates and glycosides (Molish's test), phenolic compounds (ferric chloride test), flavonoids (alkaline reagent test), alkaloids (Dragendorff's reagent), and amino acids/proteins (Biuret test), among others. Following the qualitative assessment, quantitative analysis was performed to determine the total phenolic and alkaloid contents. The total phenolic content (TPC) was estimated using the Folin–Ciocalteu colorimetric method, with results expressed as gallic acid equivalents per milliliter (µg GAE per mL) of the analyzed extract solution.52 A calibration curve was prepared using gallic acid standard solutions at concentrations ranging from 0 to 150 µg mL−1 (Fig. 9).The total alkaloid content (TAC) was determined using a bromocresol green (BCG) complexometric method, with atropine as the standard (Fig. 10).53 The quantification was performed in three independent biological replicates with triplicate absorbance readings per sample.

Biological and biochemical evaluation

Antioxidant activity assays

For DPPH free radical acavenging activity:54 this assay was performed according to the method of Gulcin et al. (2023) using 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA, Cat. No. D9132). Briefly, 200 µL of each sample was added to 1 mL of a 0.1 mM methanolic solution of DPPH. The mixtures were incubated at 25 °C for 30 minutes in the dark, and the absorbance was measured at 517 nm. Vitamin C (1 mg mL−1) was used as a positive control.

While ABTS (2,2′-azino-bis (3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)) radical cation decolorization assay: this was conducted following the protocol of Daniel C. Moreira (protocols.io, 2019). The ABTS radical cation was generated by reacting to a 7 mM ABTS solution with 2.45 mM potassium persulfate (Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA, Cat. No. 906735) and allowing the mixture to stand in the dark for 12–16 hours. The solution was diluted to an absorbance of 0.7 at 734 nm. 50 µL of each sample was mixed with 950 µL of the diluted ABTS solution, and the absorbance was read after 5 minutes. Ascorbic acid (1 mg mL−1) served as the standard.

Finally, superoxide dismutase (SOD)-like activity: the activity was measured using a commercial kit (Bio-diagnostic, Egypt, Cat. No. SD 25 21) according to the manufacturer's manual, based on the method of ref. 55. The assay measures the inhibition of formazan dye formation, with the optical density recorded at 560 nm. All tested extracts and the vitamin C positive control were evaluated at a final assay concentration of 1 mg mL−1. Antioxidant activity was expressed as percentage inhibition relative to the corresponding assay control containing the reaction reagents and sample vehicle without an antioxidant. Percentage inhibition was calculated using the following equation:where Acontrol represents the absorbance of the uninhibited control reaction and Asample represents the absorbance in the presence of the tested extract or vitamin C. For DPPH and ABTS, this percentage represents radical scavenging; for the SOD-like assay, it represents inhibition of superoxide-mediated formazan formation. Vitamin C was evaluated as a positive reference using the same calculation and was not assigned an activity of 100%.

Enzyme inhibitory assays

For α-amylase inhibition assay: the α-amylase inhibitory activity was determined based on the method described by Suthindhiran et al. (2009).56 The assay utilized α-amylase from Aspergillus oryzae (∼30 U mg−1, Sigma-Aldrich, MO, USA, Cat. No. 10065). In a 96-well microplate, 50 µL of each sample solution (1 mg mL−1) was pre-incubated with 50 µL of α-amylase solution for 10 min at 37 °C. The reaction was initiated by adding 50 µL of 1% soluble starch solution, and the mixture was incubated for 15 min at 37 °C. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 µL of 2% 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS; Glentham Life Sciences, England, Cat. No. GT6720) coloring reagent. The plate was heated at 95–100 °C for 5 min, cooled to room temperature, and the absorbance was measured at 540 nm. Quercetin at a final concentration of 50 µg mL−1 was used as the positive control. Each extract and fraction was tested in three independent biological replicates.

While in case of pancreatic lipase inhibition assay: the procedure was adapted from Zhang et al. (2022).57 The assay used pancreatic lipase (activity 30–90 U mg−1, Solarbia Life Sciences, China, Cat. No. L8070) and p-nitrophenyl phosphate (Laboratory Reagents and Fine Chemicals Company, India, CAS No. 133-13-2) as the substrate. In a 96-well plate, 100 µL of each extract solution (1 mg mL−1) was mixed with 90 µL of pancreatic lipase solution, and the mixture was pre-incubated for 20 min at 37 °C. The reaction was initiated by adding 10 µL of p-nitrophenyl palmitate (p-NPP) solution (10 mM), giving a total reaction volume of 210 µL. Following incubation for 20 min at 37 °C, absorbance was measured at 400 nm. Orlistat solution (500 µg mL−1) served as the positive reference. The uninhibited control contained an equivalent volume of the sample vehicle instead of the extract. Experiments were conducted in three independent runs, each with technical triplicate measurements. After incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance was determined at 400 nm. Orlistat (500 µg ml−1) (Solarbia Life Sciences, China, Cat. No. SO8620) was used as a positive control. Identical replication design three independent biological replicates, each in technical triplicate.

Enzyme inhibition kinetics

Enzyme inhibition kinetics were investigated for the black tea extract and the ethyl acetate fraction of green tea against α-amylase, and for the ethyl acetate fraction of green tea against pancreatic lipase. Initial reaction velocities were determined under the assay conditions described in Section 4.4.2, using the linear reaction period established in preliminary enzyme-assay experiments. For α-amylase, soluble starch concentrations of 1, 5, 10, and 20 mg mL−1 were evaluated without inhibitor and with black tea extract or the ethyl acetate fraction of green tea at 0.574 and 0.662 mg mL−1, respectively. For pancreatic lipase, substrate concentrations of 1, 5, 10, and 15 mg mL−1 were evaluated without inhibitor and with the ethyl acetate fraction of green tea at 0.264 mg mL−1. All kinetic measurements were performed in triplicate. Lineweaver–Burk plots were constructed by plotting the reciprocal of the initial reaction velocity against the reciprocal of substrate concentration, and changes in apparent Km and Vmax were used to assess inhibition patterns. Apparent inhibition constants (Ki and, where applicable, ) were calculated using GraphPad Prism 8 from the kinetic relationships corresponding to the assigned inhibition models. Substrate concentrations and apparent Km values were expressed in mg mL−1, while apparent inhibition constants were expressed in mg mL−1 of the tested preparation. The assigned inhibition patterns and apparent inhibition constants describe the kinetic behaviour of the multicomponent preparations under the experimental conditions and do not represent binding constants for individual constituents.58–62

Molecular docking studies

To investigate the binding interactions between the identified phytochemicals and the target enzymes, molecular docking studies were performed using AutoDock Vina.66,67 Firstly, 2D structures of the major compounds were obtained from the PubChem database. The structures were converted to 3D format and subjected to energy minimization using the MMFF94 force field to obtain stable, low-energy conformations suitable for docking. Then, crystal structures of α-amylase (PDB ID: 4W93) and human pancreatic lipase (PDB ID: 1ETH) were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Prior to docking, the protein structures were prepared by removing all water molecules, co-crystallized ligands, and cofactors. Polar hydrogen atoms were added, and Kollman charges were assigned to the protein atoms using AutoDock Tools. Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina. A grid box was defined to encompass the catalytic in case of amylase, where the coordinate was (x, y, z): −11.95, 3.633, −22.995 while an allosteric site was selected in case for lipase (the interface between colipase and the C-terminal domain of lipase)50 with coordinate (x, y, z): 60, 32, 112. The grid box dimensions were set to 25 Å × 25 Å × 25 Å to allow sufficient space for the ligand to move freely. The exhaustiveness parameter, which controls the extent of the conformational search, was set to 8. The resulting docked poses were ranked based on their binding affinity scores (kcal mol−1). The lowest energy (most favorable) conformation for each compound was selected for further analysis. The interactions between the ligand and the amino acid residues in the enzyme's active site were visualized and analyzed using BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer to identify key binding interactions, such as hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and π–π stacking.68

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8. All data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from triplicate experiments. Antioxidant activities (DPPH, ABTS, and SOD-like activity) and enzymes inhibition assays were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test, with vitamin C as the positive control. Group comparisons were performed by One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's multiple comparisons test for all pairwise post-hoc analysis.

An unpaired Student's t-test was applied for comparisons between two independent groups. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess associations between phytochemical content and biological activity. IC50 values were determined by non-linear regression analysis using the four-parameter logistic sigmoidal dose–response model. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. In addition, Pearson's correlation analysis was performed to evaluate the relationships among total phenolic content (TPC), total alkaloid content, antioxidant activities, and digestive enzyme inhibitory activities. Correlation coefficients (r) and corresponding two-tailed p-values were calculated.
