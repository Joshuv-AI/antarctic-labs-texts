Glenohumeral allograft capsular reconstruction for recalcitrant instability of reverse total shoulder arthroplasty: a novel technique
Authors: Scott Huff, Margaret A Sinkler, Robert Gillespie, Peter Johnston
Published: 2026

Instability following reverse total shoulder arthroplasty (rTSA) is an uncommon but devastating post-operative complication. Instability rates increase in cuff-deficient and obese patients, as well as in the revision setting.4,8,9,12 Proximal humeral bone loss, particularly of the greater tuberosity in traumatic or revisions settings, portends a higher risk of instability as well.3 Persistent or recalcitrant instability has also been correlated with deltoid dysfunction, acromial fractures, and soft-tissue impingement.1 Single dislocations may be treated with closed reduction and immobilization and may avoid revision surgery in roughly 1/3 of cases.6 Recurrent dislocations are typically managed with operative intervention aimed at retensioning the deltoid either through humeral lateralization or distalization. This often includes upsizing of the glenosphere, humeral tray, or both. After first revision, if a prosthesis remains unstable, a second revision has been shown to have very high continued instability rates of nearly 48%.6 Continued increasing of length and offset of components also comes with the risk of overdistalizing the humerus and overtensioning the deltoid, which can lead to a myriad of separate complications including axillary nerve tension neuropraxia, scapular spine, or acromial stress reaction or fracture.11 In addition to the above complications, rTSA systems all have an intrinsic maximum component size that is available, such that continued increase in the glenosphere or humeral tray is not always possible. As such, an all-soft-tissue stabilizing procedure is a desirable option for increasing stability when component size augmentation is not possible or desired. To date, there have been few reports of soft-tissue capsular reconstruction for recalcitrant instability, with a single case of allograft reconstruction.2 We present a case series of recurrent rTSA recurrent dislocations managed with capsular reconstruction with an Achilles allograft glenohumeral yoke procedure.

Case descriptions

Patient 1: Patient is a 72-year-old male referred for evaluation of recurrent anterior dislocations of rTSA prosthesis. Index surgery was performed a little over a year previously for advanced glenohumeral arthritis that had failed conservative measures. Thirteen months post-operatively, the patient experienced an atraumatic dislocation while sleeping. The patient underwent revision with increase in humeral tray spacer and glenosphere offset. Cultures were obtained which were negative for growth. Over the next several months, the patient suffered 6 repeat dislocations managed with closed reduction. Following referral, the patient was taken for repeat open evaluation where cloudy fluid was encountered. Repeat cultures taken, irrigation and débridement were performed, and antibiotic spacer placed. Cultures returned positive for Cutibacterium acnes and patient was started on 6 weeks intravenous antibiotics. Following completion of the antibiotic course, the patient was indicated for revision surgery. Electromyography was also obtained to confirm axillary nerve function remained intact. The patient was taken for revision rTSA with allograft glenohumeral capsular reconstruction as described in our technique section. He is now over 1-year post-operative with no recurrent instability events.

Patient 2: 64-year-old morbidly obese female presented with acute right shoulder pain following a fall 3 weeks prior. Past surgical history was significant for previous right anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty 5 years ago. The patient was found to have locked anteriorly dislocated humeral component. Given extremely high instability risk, the patient was offered both conversion to reverse as well as observation and elected to undergo conversion to reverse prosthesis. Conversion to reverse with a 33+3-mm lateralized glenosphere with neutral humeral tray and polyethylene liner was performed. No subscapularis or anterior capsular tissue was reparable. Unfortunately, the patient was found to be dislocated at 2-week follow-up appointment, and a closed reduction attempt the next day in the operating room under full sedation was unsuccessful. The patient was then taken for second revision with an increase in the glenosphere to 33+6-mm lateralization and change to a 5-degree constrained polyethylene liner. The patient was stable at initial follow-up appointments but was again found to be anteriorly dislocate at her 6-week follow-up. At this time, the patient was taken for a third revision with Achilles allograft glenohumeral capsular reconstruction. At her most recent follow-up, the patient is now over 6 months post-operatively with no recurrent instability, 150° forward elevation, and 50° external rotation.

Technique

Standard deltopectoral revision exposure is performed. A circumferential release of the proximal humerus is performed to allow for adequate mobilization and passing of the graft. The glenosphere is removed and the baseplate and surrounding native glenoid are exposed. The baseplate may be removed or remain in place if adequate posterior glenoid is visualized and accessible. An Achilles allograft is utilized for the capsular reconstruction and prepared on the back table with suture tape whipstitched to the distal aspect, while the proximal aspect is split in the midline (Fig. 1). Two suture anchors are utilized to secure the distal aspect of the graft to the posterior aspect of the glenoid. This may be on the face of the glenoid posterior to the baseplate if adequate bone stock is available, or otherwise into the posterior neck of the glenoid. This can be performed through a small percutaneous posterior incision if the angle requires it. The broad proximal end of the graft is split, and one limb is passed anterior and the other posterior about the proximal humerus (Fig. 2). The humeral tray is reduced onto the glenosphere and the arm positioned with the greater tuberosity at an apex distance from the glenosphere as to not overconstrain rotation of the joint post-operatively. The tails are tied in a simple knot anteriorly at the level of the bicipital groove, and the knot further secured with suture (Fig. 3). Finally, the two limbs are secured to the anterior glenoid with two all-suture anchors at the 2- and 5-o'clock positions (Fig. 4).

(A and B) While the humeral tray remains dislocated, one split limb is passed each anteriorly and posteriorly to the humerus.

The humeral tray is reduced, and the Achilles limbs are tied in a simple knot and secured over the lateral humerus.

(A and B) Final construct after the knot has been sutured and the two limbs secured to the superior and inferiors aspects of the anterior glenoid with anchors.

Discussion

Management of unstable rTSA can be a particularly frustrating challenged faced by orthopedic surgeons. Component undersizing or malposition may be an identifiable cause. Both bony mechanical impingement and soft-tissue deficiencies can result in recurrent instability.7 Infection, polyethylene wear, and axillary nerve dysfunction can all also be actors in recurrent dislocations.5,7 When other causes of instability have been eliminated and component upsizing or position revision is impossible or impractical due to lack of available implants or concern over further bone-stock loss, a soft-tissue capsular reconstruction may provide a viable soft-tissue option to enhance stability. There have been several reports of all-suture glenohumeral cerclage.10,13 This option is cost effective and likely functions by temporarily constraining the shoulder to allow contraction and healing of native soft-tissue. We present a novel technique of glenohumeral capsular reconstruction using Achilles allograft and familiar anchor fixation. This option may be preferable in several instances, e.g., when there has been attritional loss of all anterior tendon and capsular tissue. Allograft capsular reconstruction may also be preferable to allograft prosthetic composite with allograft soft-tissue attachments when the surgeon prefers to retain the humeral stem or there is not substantial proximal humeral bone loss. Our small case series highlights particularly challenging cases of instability managed successfully with an all-soft-tissue reconstruction. It likely provides better long-term function and range of motion than a cuff tear arthropathy hemiarthroplasty or resection arthroplasty as a salvage procedure for recurrently dislocating reverse prosthesis.

Conclusion

Achilles allograft capsular reconstruction is a viable solution for patients with the difficult problem of recurrent rTSA instability. It can be performed while retaining both the glenoid baseplate and humeral stem and can result in reasonable range of motion post-operatively while maintaining stability.
