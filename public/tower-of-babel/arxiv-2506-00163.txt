Computing Witnesses Using the SCAN Algorithm (Extended Preprint)
Fabian Achammer, Stefan Hetzl, Renate A. Schmidt

Abstract
Second-order quantifier-elimination is the problem of finding, given a formula with second-order quantifiers, a logically equivalent first-order formula. While such formulas are not computable in general, there are practical algorithms and subclasses with applications throughout computational logic. One of the most prominent algorithms for second-order quantifier elimination is the SCAN algorithm which is based on saturation theorem proving. In this paper we show how the SCAN algorithm on clause sets can be extended to solve a more general problem: namely, finding an instance of the second-order quantifiers that results in a logically equivalent first-order formula. In addition we provide a prototype implementation of the proposed method. This work paves the way for applying the SCAN algorithm to new problems in application domains such as modal correspondence theory, knowledge representation, and verification.

Introduction

Second-order quantifier elimination (SOQE)

[
22
,
23
]
is the problem
of, given a formula with second-order quantifiers

\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
where
\varphi
is first-order, find a first-order formula

such that

\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\equiv\psi.

For example, one can show that

is equivalent to

.
An algorithm for eliminating existential quantifiers can
be used to eliminate universal quantifiers by writing

\forall\overline{X}\,
as
\neg\exists\overline{X}\neg
.

SOQE is an important topic in logic, automated reasoning and artificial
intelligence that has real-world applications in diverse areas.
It has been used to automate correspondence theory for modal

[

]
, which has led
to new attention and results in correspondence theory of various
algebras and logics
[

,

,
10
,

]
.
SOQE can be used to transform a knowledge base (or set of formulas, given by
\varphi
) into
a restricted view

of the knowledge base in which all occurrences
of the predicate symbols
\overline{X}
have been eliminated.
This reduction has also been referred to as

[
36
]
or projection and studied in the form
of uniform interpolation or strongest necessary condition in knowledge
representation
[

,
33

,

,

,

]

and answer set programming
[

Forgetting offers solutions to several important applications and manipulations of

,

.
In automated reasoning an application of SOQE is (predicate) symbol

.
Another very promising application domain is agent communication,
requiring knowledge sharing among agents

Two prominent approaches to computing SOQE-solutions
are the SCAN algorithm introduced
in
[

]
and the DLS algorithm
introduced in
[

]
and extended

[

SCAN is based on the idea of computing all logical
consequences of
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
and omit those formulas that contain
predicate variables from
\overline{X}
.
It transforms the first-order part
\varphi
into clausal
normal form and computes the closure under a constraint resolution calculus,
performing inferences only on
\overline{X}
-literals. During this process
clauses with
\overline{X}
-literals for which sufficiently many inferences
have been performed are deleted
[
20
,

]
.
If the algorithm terminates, it returns a set of first-order consequences
which is logically equivalent
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi

Reverse Skolemization might be applied,
if during the clause form transformation Skolemization was performed.
SCAN has been shown complete for computing frame correspondence properties
for the class of modal Sahlqvist axioms
[
25
]
.
The SCAN algorithm is closely related to hierarchic
superposition
[
3
,
4
]
.

The other prominent approach, the DLS algorithm, is based on a result of
Ackermann
[
1
]
.
Known as Ackermann’s Lemma, it
states a condition under which a predicate variable is eliminable from a second-order formula
\exists X\varphi
.
The idea of the DLS algorithm is to use equivalence preserving
operations to bring the problem into a form
where Ackermann’s Lemma is applicable.

In this paper we are interested in a more general problem which we call

witnessed second-order quantifier elimination (WSOQE)
: Given a formula
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
with first-order
\varphi
, find first-order predicates
(formulas)

satisfying this

\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\equiv\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}].

(
\ast

That is, the goal is to find a first-order instantiation
\overline{\alpha}
(in the same language as
\varphi
) of variables
\overline{X}

such that an equivalent first-order formula is obtained.
We call
\overline{\alpha}
a

\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
.
Recall that the second-order formula
\varPhi
from the beginning of the introduction is equivalent to
B(a)
.
One can check that
\alpha=\lambda u.u\simeq a
and
\beta=\lambda u.B(u)
are WSOQE-witnesses for
\varPhi
.

The contribution of this paper is an algorithm to solve WSOQE and compute such witnesses.
Our algorithm, called
WSCAN

a post-processing step which extracts a witness from the derivation
of a terminating SCAN run.
The witness construction proceeds in a bottom-up way, computing

A prototype has been implemented in version 2.18.1 of the GAPT software package

In addition to solving the more general WSOQE problem, the concepts
introduced in this paper provide a new correctness proof for the

The WSOQE problem bridges the gap between SOQE and another important problem,
namely,

is first-order find a tuple

Since the earlier example
\varPhi
is not valid it does not have an FEQ-solution.
However, one can check that the witnesses

\varPhi^{\prime}=\exists X\,(B(a)\rightarrow(X(a)\land\forall u\,(X(u)\rightarrow B(u))))

Going back to studies in the 19th century by
[
45

, solving formula equations is one of the oldest and most central problems of logic.
The book
[

]
comprehensively considers the problem in the setting of Boolean algebra.
Solving Boolean equations is closely related to Boolean unification, a subject of thorough study in
computer science, see, e.g.,
[

The generalization of this problem from propositional to first-order logic has been made explicit

[

6

Today, FEQ is the central problem underlying several areas of computational logic, even though
this is often not made explicit.
For example, solving constrained Horn clauses

More generally, the problem of inductive theorem proving, e.g., in Peano arithmetic, can be

One can naively solve FEQ for

\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}]

An algorithm for WSOQE, like the one we introduce in this paper, can be used to solve both SOQE and FEQ:
A WSOQE-witness

satisfies (
*

However, note that WSOQE is not complete for SOQE as there are formulas that have a SOQE-solution, but no WSOQE-witness (see

Furthermore, if one has a WSOQE-witness

by checking whether
\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}]

The use of solutions to WSOQE for solving SOQE or FEQ is widespread in the literature:
it is the basis of Ackermann’s Lemma
[

]
, which provides the foundation for the DLS

[

for SOQE.
It is central for the decidability of quantifier-free Boolean unification with predicates

and for the fixed-point theorem for Horn formula equations of

,

]

Solutions to WSOQE are called ELIM-witnesses in

3
The SCAN Algorithm

The SCAN algorithm takes as input a formula
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
, where
\varphi
is first-order
and then transforms
\varphi
into its clausal normal form
N
, which might include a Skolemization step.
Next,
N
is saturated by a
purification
process:
SCAN picks a clause
C
and a designated
\overline{X}
-literal
L\in C
.
All non-redundant constraint resolution inferences (defined below) between
C
(on that literal
L
) and the rest of the clauses are performed.
In addition constraint factoring inferences (defined below) on
C
, constraint elimination and redundancy criteria are used to simplify the resulting clauses.
If all non-redundant inferences have been generated,
C
is deleted from the clause set and the process is repeated with a new clause and designated
\overline{X}
-literal.
This process might not terminate, but if we reach a point where no clause contains an
\overline{X}
-literal the result is a clause set
N^{\ast}
such that
\exists\overline{X}\,N\equiv N^{\ast}
.
SCAN attempts to reverse the Skolemization step from the beginning, if performed, and returns a first-order formula logically equivalent to
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
.
As we present our results for clause sets, we do not deal with reverse Skolemization.

Note that during the purification process, SCAN not only chooses a clause, but also a literal within this clause.
To give a name to this concept we introduce the notion of
pointed clause
(in analogy to

Definition 1

A

We call

If

is an
X

is an

Witness Extraction from SCAN Derivations

In this section we introduce the WSCAN algorithm (‘W’ for witness), as an extension of SCAN on clause sets by providing a witness for the second-order quantifier elimination on termination.
At first we also consider solutions to WSOQE that are not necessarily first-order and give them a name: A tuple of predicate expressions
\overline{\alpha}
is called an
elimination witness
, or simply a
witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
, if it satisfies the WSOQE-condition (

∗

‣
1
) for
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
.

An illustration of how WSCAN works is given in the following diagram:

\begin{array}[]{rcccccccl}N=N(D)_{0}&\xrightarrow{D_{1}}&N(D)_{1}&\xrightarrow{D_{2}}&\quad\dots&N(D)_{m-1}&\xrightarrow{D_{m}}&\ N(D)_{m}\\[3.00003pt]
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D)}=\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{0}(D)}&\xleftarrow{T_{D_{1}}}&\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{1}(D)}&\xleftarrow{T_{D_{2}}}&\quad\dots&\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{m-1}(D)}&\xleftarrow{T_{D_{m}}}&\ \overline{\mathrm{wit}_{m}(D)}\end{array}

We start by saturating a given clause set

using the derivation steps of the calculus and collect them in a derivation

.
If saturation terminates we construct a witness
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D)}
for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.
To this aim we iteratively compute witnesses
\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{i}(D)}
for
\exists\overline{X}\,N(D)_{i}
for all
0\leq i\leq m
starting from the back with
\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{m}(D)}
.
Since
D
resulted from saturating the input clause set
N
we have that
N(D)_{m}
, i.e., the conclusion of
D
from
N
, contains none of the variables in
\overline{X}
, otherwise a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation step would be applicable.
We now need an initial witness
\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{m}(D)}
for
N(D)_{m}
.
Since
N(D)_{m}
does not contain any of the variables of
\overline{X}
choosing for
\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{m}(D)}
any tuple of predicate expressions (compatible with the arities of
\overline{X}
) produces a witness.
We then iteratively transform witness
\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{i}(D)}
of
N(D)_{i}
to a witness
\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{i-1}(D)}
of
N(D)_{i-1}
using an operation
T_{D_{i}}
.
Finally, the tuple of predicates
\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{0}(D)}
is a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.

We now want to define the transformation
T_{S}
for the different derivation steps
S
in
\mathcal{C}
.
It has to satisfy the following property:

Lemma 1
(Witness preservation lemma)

If
S
is a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation step from
N
to
N^{\prime}
and
\overline{\alpha}
is a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N^{\prime}
,
then
T_{S}(\overline{\alpha})
is a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.

Before we give the formal definition of
T_{S}
, we give some intuition on how to satisfy
Lemma

1
.
For
S\in\left\{\mathrm{Res},\mathrm{Fac},\mathrm{ConstrElim},\mathrm{RedElim}\right\}
we have
N\equiv N^{\prime}
.
Therefore we can use the same witness, i.e., we set
T_{S}(\overline{\alpha})~=~\overline{\alpha}
.
If we have
S=\mathrm{ExtPurDel}_{X}^{+}
we see that
N^{\prime}\equiv N[X\leftarrow\lambda\overline{u}.\top]
since every clause in
N
that contains
X
has a positive occurrence of
X
, making those clauses logically equivalent to
\top
under
[X\leftarrow\lambda\overline{u}.\top]
.
If
\overline{\alpha}
is a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N^{\prime}
,
then setting the
X
-component of
\overline{\alpha}
to
\lambda\overline{u}.\top
and leaving the other components the same results in a witness for
N
.
Similarly, if
S=\mathrm{ExtPurDel}_{X}^{-}
we set the
X
-component of
T_{S}(\overline{\alpha})
to
X
to
\lambda\overline{u}.\bot
. Then
N^{\prime}\equiv N[X\leftarrow\lambda\overline{u}.\bot]
.

Defining the transformation
T_{S}
for
S=\mathrm{PurDel}_{P}
needs a bit of groundwork.
A central piece is the resolution closure of a clause set with respect to a pointed clause:

Definition 4

Let
P
be a pointed clause with designated literal
L
and let
N
be a clause set.
We define
\mathrm{Res}_{P}({N})
to be the smallest set such that
(i)
N\subseteq\mathrm{Res}_{P}({N})
, and (ii) if
C\in\mathrm{Res}_{P}({N})
and
R
is a resolvent between
C
and
P
upon
L
, then
R\in\mathrm{Res}_{P}({N})
.

For a clause
C
we define the abbreviation
\mathrm{Res}_{P}({C})~:=~\mathrm{Res}_{P}({\left\{C\right\}})

and for a literal
L
we set
\mathrm{Res}_{P}({L})~:=~\mathrm{Res}_{P}({\left\{L\right\}})
.

Furthermore for
P=\underline{L(\overline{t})}\lor C_{P}
we define the set
\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})~:=~\mathrm{Res}_{P}({L(\overline{c})^{\perp}})
, where
\overline{c}
is a tuple of fresh constants.
The ‘
\mathrm{U}
’ stands for unit and signifies that we consider the resolution closure with respect to
P
starting with a unit clause dual to the designated literal of
P
.

To simplify the presentation of the set
\mathrm{Res}_{P}({N})
we often tacitly replace it by a logically equivalent clause set, for example by replacing a clause
C\in\mathrm{Res}_{P}({N})
by a logically equivalent clause
C^{\prime}
, which results from
C
by constraint elimination.

Some examples of
\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})
are given in the following:

Example 2

Let
P=\underline{X(a)}
, then we have

\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(c)=\left\{\neg X(c),a\not\simeq c\right\}
and for
P^{\prime}=\underline{\neg X(a)}\lor\neg X(b)
we get

\mathrm{ResU}_{P^{\prime}}(c)=\left\{X(c),a\not\simeq c\lor\neg X(b)\right\}
.

For
P^{\prime\prime}=B(u,v)\lor\underline{\neg X(u)}\lor X(v)
we get an infinite set:

\displaystyle\mathrm{ResU}_{P^{\prime\prime}}(c)=\{

\displaystyle X(c),\ B(c,v)\lor X(v),

\displaystyle B(c,v)\lor B(v,v^{\prime})\lor X(v^{\prime}),

\displaystyle B(c,v)\lor B(v,v^{\prime})\lor B(v^{\prime},v^{\prime\prime})\lor X(v^{\prime\prime}),\dots\}.

In the definition of the clause set
\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})
we use a tuple of constants
\overline{c}
instead of variables as we want to avoid the implicit universal quantification over the clauses for these placeholders.
These constants are intended as arguments for a predicate expression
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
(‘
\mathrm{p}
’ for predicate) given by:

Definition 5

Let
P
be an
X
-pointed clause. We set

\mathrm{pResU}_{P}:=\begin{cases}\lambda\overline{u}.\bigwedge_{C^{\prime}(\overline{c},\overline{v})\in\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})}\forall\overline{v}\,C^{\prime}(\overline{u},\overline{v})&\text{ if }P=\underline{\neg X(\overline{t})}\lor C\text{ and}\\
\lambda\overline{u}.\bigvee_{C^{\prime}(\overline{c},\overline{v})\in\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})}\exists\overline{v}\,\neg C^{\prime}(\overline{u},\overline{v})&\text{ if }P=\underline{X(\overline{t})}\lor C,\end{cases}

where
\overline{u}
is a tuple of fresh variables of the same size as
\overline{c}
.

Example 3

For
P=\underline{X(a)}
and
P^{\prime}=\underline{\neg X(a)}\lor\neg X(b)
as in
Example

2
we get
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}=\lambda u.X(u)\lor u\simeq a
and
\mathrm{pResU}_{P^{\prime}}=\lambda u.X(u)\land(u\not\simeq a\lor\neg X(b))
.
If
\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})
is infinite, as for
P^{\prime\prime}
of
Example

2
, then
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
is an infinite predicate expression.

A crucial property of
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
is the following:

Lemma 2

Let
P
be an
X
-pointed clause and let
C
be a clause.
Then we have
\models P[X\leftarrow\mathrm{pResU}_{P}]
and
\models\mathrm{Res}_{P}({C})\rightarrow C[X\leftarrow\mathrm{pResU}_{P}]
.

A way to view this lemma is (i)
X\leftarrow\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
is always a satisfying assignment for
P
and (ii)
X\leftarrow\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
is a satisfying assignment for
C
, given that there is an assignment of
X
that satisfies all clauses in
\mathrm{Res}_{P}({C})
(note that
X
can occur freely in
\mathrm{Res}_{P}({C})
and
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
).
The reader might gain an intuition for the lemma by applying it to the pointed clauses from the previous example.

Using
Lemma

2
we can show the witness preservation lemma (
Lemma

1
) for purified clause deletion steps using
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
:

Lemma 3

Let
N
be a clause set, let
P
be
X_{i}
-pointed and purified in
N
and suppose
\overline{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
is a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.
Then a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,(N\cup\left\{P\right\})
is given by
(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},\mathrm{pResU}_{P}[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}],\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
.

Proof
(Proof sketch)

To avoid notational overhead we give a proof sketch under the simplifying assumption
d=1
, i.e., we only consider a single variable
X
.

To show that
\alpha_{P}:=\mathrm{pResU}_{P}[X\leftarrow\alpha]
is a witness for
N_{P}:=N\cup\left\{P\right\}
we need to show that both directions of the WSOQE-condition (

∗

‣
1
) hold.
The direction
\models N_{P}[X\leftarrow\alpha_{P}]\rightarrow\exists X\,N_{P}
holds as
\alpha_{P}
is an instance of the existential quantifier.
Now it suffices to show
\models\exists X\,N_{P}\rightarrow N_{P}[X\leftarrow\alpha_{P}]
.
Since
N\subseteq N_{P}
we have
\models\exists X\,N_{P}\rightarrow\exists X\,N

and because
\alpha
is a witness for
\exists X\,N
we have
\models\exists X\,N\rightarrow N[X\leftarrow\alpha]
.
It remains to show
\models N[X\leftarrow\alpha]\rightarrow N_{P}[X\leftarrow\alpha_{P}]
.
We reduce this to
\models P[X\leftarrow\alpha_{P}]
and
\models N[X\leftarrow\alpha]\rightarrow C[X\leftarrow\alpha_{P}]
for all
C\in N
.

Using the first statement of
Lemma

2
we get
\models P[X\leftarrow\mathrm{pResU}_{P}]
.
Applying the substitution which sends
X
to
\alpha
results in
\models P[X\leftarrow\mathrm{pResU}_{P}][X\leftarrow\alpha]
.
Note that applying
[X\leftarrow\alpha]
after
[X\leftarrow\mathrm{pResU}_{P}]
is the same as applying
[X\leftarrow\mathrm{pResU}_{P}[X\leftarrow\alpha]]
.
Thus
\models P[X\leftarrow\alpha_{P}]
.

To show
\models N[X\leftarrow\alpha]\rightarrow C[X\leftarrow\alpha_{P}]
we use that
P
is purified in
N
to get that all clauses in
\mathrm{Res}_{P}({N})
,
and in particular all clauses in
\mathrm{Res}_{P}({C})\subseteq\mathrm{Res}_{P}({N})
, are redundant in
N
.
Therefore
\models N\rightarrow\mathrm{Res}_{P}({C})
and we can apply the second statement of
Lemma

2
to get
\models N\rightarrow C[X\leftarrow\mathrm{pResU}_{P}]
.
Again, applying
[X\leftarrow\alpha]
results in the desired statement
\models N[X\leftarrow\alpha]\rightarrow C[X\leftarrow\alpha_{P}]
.

We now give the formal definition of the witness transformation:

Definition 6

Let
S
be a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation step and let
\overline{X}=(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
be a tuple of predicate variables.
For
1\leq i\leq d
, let
\overline{u_{i}}
be a tuple of distinct variables, the size of which is the arity of
X_{i}
.
Also, let
\overline{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{d})
be a tuple of predicate expressions such that
\alpha_{i}
has the same arity as
X_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq d
.
We define the predicate expression
T_{S}(\overline{\alpha})
by a case distinction on
S
as follows:

\begin{array}[]{rll}T_{S}(\overline{\alpha})&:=&\overline{\alpha}\text{ for $S\in\left\{\mathrm{Res},\mathrm{Fac},\mathrm{ConstrElim},\mathrm{RedElim}\right\}$},\\
T_{\mathrm{ExtPurDel}^{+}_{X_{i}}}(\overline{\alpha})&:=&(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},\lambda\overline{u_{i}}.\top,\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{d}),\\
T_{\mathrm{ExtPurDel}^{-}_{X_{i}}}(\overline{\alpha})&:=&(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},\lambda\overline{u_{i}}.\bot,\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{d}),\text{ and}\\
T_{\mathrm{PurDel}_{P}}(\overline{\alpha})&:=&(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},\mathrm{pResU}_{P}[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}],\alpha_{i+1},\dots,\alpha_{d}),\\
&&\text{if $P$ is $X_{i}$-pointed}.\end{array}

Above we explained why the witness preservation lemma holds for these
T_{S}(\overline{\alpha})
.
We now construct witnesses iteratively given a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation
(D_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
starting from the back:

Definition 7

Let
N(X_{1},\dots,X_{d})
be a clause set and let
D=(D_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
be a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
.
For
1\leq i\leq d
let
W_{i}
be a predicate variable with the same arity as
X_{i}
.
We inductively define a tuple
\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{i}(D)}
of predicate expressions, for
0\leq i\leq m
, starting with
\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{m}(D)}
as the base case by

\displaystyle\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{m}(D)}

\displaystyle:=(W_{1},\dots,W_{d})\quad\text{and}\quad\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{i-1}(D)}

\displaystyle:=T_{D_{i}}(\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{i}(D)}).

Finally, the witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
is given by
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D)}:=\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{0}(D)}
.

If
D=(D_{i})_{1\leq i\leq m}
is an
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
,
the conclusion of
D
does not contain any of the predicate variables in
\overline{X}
.
Thus, any tuple of predicate expressions (compatible with the arities in
\overline{X}
) provides a witness for the conclusion of
D
.
The predicate variables
W_{i}
in the above definition serve as placeholders for this arbitrary choice of witness and any instantiation of these predicate variables provides a witness.
We call
W_{i}
the
witness parameter corresponding to
X_{i}
.
The
\overline{\mathrm{wit}_{i}(D)}
are witnesses for the intermediate clause sets
N(D)_{i}
which can be proved by an inductive argument on the length of
D
using
Lemma

1
.

Our main result is:

Theorem 4.1

If
D
is an
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
, then
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D)}
is a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.

Here is an example illustrating the algorithm:

Example 4

Remember the clause set
N
from
Example

1
:

\displaystyle(1)\ B(a,v)\qquad(2)\ X(a)\qquad(3)\ B(u,v)\lor\neg X(u)\lor X(v)\qquad(4)\ \neg X(c)

and suppose we want to find a witness for
X
.
Some resolvents from
N
are

\displaystyle(5)\ a\not\simeq u\lor B(u,v)\lor X(v)\quad\text{($2$ with $3$)}\quad\text{and}\quad(6)\

\displaystyle a\not\simeq c

\displaystyle\text{($2$ with $4$)}.

Also recall the derivation
D
:

\mathord{\left\{1,2,3,4\right\}}\mathord{}

\mathrm{Res}_{2.1,4.1}

\mathord{\left\{1,2,3,4,6\right\}}\mathord{}

\mathrm{PurDel}_{2.1}

\mathord{\left\{1,3,4,6\right\}}\mathord{}

\mathrm{ExtPurDel}_{X}^{-}

\mathord{\left\{1,6\right\}}\mathord{}

In computing
\mathrm{wit}_{i}(D)
,
we freely use equivalences of the form
\bot\lor\varphi\equiv\varphi
,
\top\land\varphi\equiv\varphi
and
\neg\neg\varphi\equiv\varphi
as well as commutativity and associativity of
\land
and
\lor
to simplify the constructed witnesses.
We use
W_{X}
to denote the witness parameter corresponding to
X
.
The steps to construct
\mathrm{wit}(D)
are:

\begin{array}[]{r@{\quad}l@{\quad}l@{\quad}l@{\quad}l}i&N(D)_{i}&D_{i+1}&T_{D_{i+1}}(\alpha)&\mathrm{wit}_{i}(D)\\
\hline\cr 3&\left\{1,6\right\}&-&-&W_{X}\\
2&\left\{1,3,4,6\right\}&\mathrm{ExtPurDel}_{X}^{-}&\lambda u.\bot&\lambda u.\bot\\
1&\left\{1,2,3,4,6\right\}&\mathrm{PurDel}_{2.1}&\mathrm{pResU}_{2.1}[X\leftarrow\alpha]&\lambda u.u\simeq a\\
0&\left\{1,2,3,4\right\}&\mathrm{Res}_{2.1,4.1}&\alpha&\lambda u.u\simeq a\end{array}

Note that
\mathrm{ResU}_{2.1}(d)=\left\{\neg X(d),d\not\simeq a\right\}
and thus
\mathrm{pResU}_{2.1}=\lambda u.X(u)\lor u\simeq a
.
Therefore
\mathrm{wit}(D)=\lambda u.u\simeq a
is a WSOQE-witness for
\exists X\,N
.

5
First-order Witnesses

At this point it is not clear that the components of
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D)}
are always first-order predicates as the expression
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
can be infinite.
In fact, there are
\overline{X}
-eliminating derivations where the predicate expressions in
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D)}
are not equivalent to first-order predicates.
This can be avoided, if for all purified clauses
P
in
D
there are first-order predicates
F_{P}
equivalent to
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
:

Definition 8

Let
D
be a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
.
Let
F
be a mapping that assigns to all purified pointed clauses
P
of
D
a first-order predicate
F_{P}
with
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}\equiv F_{P}
.
Then
F
is called a
first-order annotation for
D
.
In that case, denote by
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D,F)}
the tuple of first-order predicate expressions resulting from
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D)}
where every occurrence of
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
is replaced by
F_{P}
.

Now the first-order version of our main result is:

Corollary 1

If
D
is an
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
and
F
is a first-order annotation for
D
,
then
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D,F)}
is a first-order witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.

To find such a first-order annotation
F
we compute for all purified pointed clauses
P
a finite clause set
N_{P}
logically equivalent to
\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})
.
Then replacing
\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})
by
N_{P}
in the definition of
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
results in a first-order predicate expression
F_{P}
equivalent to
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
.
We use SCAN’s purification process to find such finite clause sets
N_{P}
:
Let
P=\underline{L(\overline{t})}\lor C
.
Start with the clause set
\left\{L(\overline{c})^{\perp}\right\}
and saturate it with respect to constraint resolution with
P
.
In addition we use constraint and redundancy elimination to simplify the intermediate clause sets.
If at some point all constraint resolvents of the clause set with
P
are redundant, we obtain a finite clause set
N_{P}
which is logically equivalent to
\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})
.
However, this process might not terminate as is the case for
P^{\prime\prime}
from
Example

2
.
Thus it is unclear whether computing a first-order annotation
F
is always possible.

To get a concrete strategy for constructing
\mathcal{C}
-derivations where
Corollary

1
is applicable we direct our search to a decidable property which is sufficient to guarantee first-order annotations.
One such property is:

Definition 9

An
X
-pointed clause
P
is called
one-sided
if
X
occurs only positively or only negatively in
P
.
A
\mathcal{C}
-derivation
D
is called
one-sided
if all purified pointed clauses in
D
are one-sided (not necessarily with the same polarity).

Derivation
D
in
Example

4
is one-sided as its only purified pointed clause
2.1
is one-sided.
In general, if
P=\underline{L(\overline{t})}\lor C
is one-sided,
then we have
\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})=\left\{L(\overline{c})^{\perp},\overline{t}\not\simeq\overline{c}\lor C\right\}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})
is finite.
Thus
P\mapsto\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
is a first-order annotation for one-sided
\mathcal{C}
-derivations.
One-sidedness of a pointed clause
P
is not necessary for the existence of a first-order predicate equivalent to
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
, e.g., for
P=\underline{\neg X(u,v)}\lor X(v,u)
we have
\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(c,d)\equiv\left\{X(c,d),X(d,c)\right\}
, therefore
F_{P}=\lambda u\lambda v.X(u,v)\land X(v,u)
satisfies
F_{P}\equiv\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
.

As a special case of
Corollary

1
we get:

Corollary 2

If
D
is a one-sided
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
,
then
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D)}
is a first-order witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.

Since one-sidedness is a decidable property, this suggests a strategy to construct
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivations which induce WSOQE-witnesses: Run SCAN in such a way that only one-sided pointed clauses
P
are purified.
If SCAN terminates, the resulting derivation will also be one-sided, ensuring a first-order witness
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D)}
.
However, it is open whether this affects completeness of the calculus (see Problem
1
).

Furthermore for one-sided
\overline{X}
-eliminating derivations
D
one can show a tight exponential upper bound on the size of
\overline{\mathrm{wit}(D)}
in terms of the length of
D
.
If every purified pointed clause in
D
contains at most one
\overline{X}
-literal, the bound is even linear.

6
Implementation

The presented method is implemented in the Scala programming language
[
41
]
as part of the General Architecture for Proof Theory (GAPT) software package
[
18
]
which provides useful data structures to develop algorithms in computational logic, including terms, formulas and clauses.
The prototype is available in version 2.18.1 of GAPT (
https://www.logic.at/gapt/release_archive.html
).
Section 8.4 of the GAPT user manual details how to use the prototype.

To evaluate our witness construction method we have implemented the SCAN algorithm which, in addition to finding a logically equivalent first-order clause set, provides a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation which is then used by our method to extract a witness.

The saturation loop proceeds by picking a pointed clause
P=\underline{L(\overline{t})}\lor C
from the active clause set and performs purification, i.e., adds all non-redundant resolvents between
P
and the rest of the clause set while eagerly applying constraint elimination, tautology deletion, subsumption deletion, as well as inferring all non-redundant constraint factors of newly added clauses.
Once
P
is purified, it is deleted from the active clause set.
Since this process might not terminate we provide parameters that limit the inferences to be performed.
If the input clause set is not saturated within this limit the procedure reports an error.
Since one-sided derivations guarantee first-order witnesses, we also provide an option to only apply the purification process to one-sided pointed clauses.
During the saturation process all performed steps are stored in a
\mathcal{C}
-derivation, modelled as the initial clause set together with a list of
\mathcal{C}
-derivation steps.

The choice of pointed clause determines the outcome of the saturation process.
Different choices can lead to termination or non-termination.
This also affects which
\mathcal{C}
-derivations are found.
To find all possible
\mathcal{C}
-derivations from a given clause set,
we have implemented backtracking on top of the saturation process to be able to get
\mathcal{C}
-derivations based on the different choices of pointed clauses.
This backtracking can be performed on-demand, and allows to find multiple derivations and therefore multiple witnesses.

The witness extraction method recurses on the produced
\mathcal{C}
-derivation following
Definition

7
.
We implemented
T_{S}
according to
Definition

6
where
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
is computed by using the same purification process as described above, but starting with the clause set consisting of the single literal
L(\overline{c})^{\perp}
.
This means we also use redundancy criteria and constraint factoring inferences to simplify the clause set
\mathrm{ResU}_{P}(\overline{c})
.
Since
\mathrm{pResU}_{P}
is potentially infinite we also provide parameters that limit the inference steps performed in its computation.
If the witness computation succeeds, it returns a substitution that maps the eliminated symbols to the computed first-order witness predicates.

Our prototype implementation has been tested on 26 examples from the file

examples/predicateEliminationProblems.scala
included in GAPT 2.18.1.
These examples were either created by us or selected from the literature and
possibly modified. Tests were run using the GAPT shell with the Azul Zulu
Java JDK 21.0.2 on a MacBook Pro with an M2 Pro CPU and 32GB RAM. We run the
wscan
method with default parameters, measuring execution
time, input clause set size, and resulting witness size (if found).
We measure the size of a literal by the number of non-logical symbols it contains.
The size of a clause is given by the sum of the sizes of its literals and the size of a clause set is given by the sum of the sizes of its clauses.
The size of a witness is measured by the number of logical and non-logical symbols it contains.
See the
wscanTest
method in the file
examples/predicateEliminationProblems.scala
for the exact test protocol.

The results are the following: The input size ranged from 2 to 177 with an average of 21.58.
For 21 of the 26 examples a witness could be computed.
In the remaining five cases a user-set timeout based on the number of derivation steps was reached
without finding a derivation.
For the 21 terminating examples the running times ranged from 0.03 milliseconds to 150.60
milliseconds with an average of 14.96 milliseconds.
The witness sizes ranged from 0 (this example had no second-order quantifiers) to 220 with an average of 21.50.

We have not optimized our implementation for large clause sets.
Furthermore, we are not aware of a sufficiently large database of examples to test our method on.
This means a thorough empirical evaluation is left as future work.

7
Discussion and Future Work

Constructing multiple witnesses.

Different
\mathcal{C}
-derivations can result in different, non-equivalent witnesses. Thus one can use our method to construct multiple witnesses for a given input clause set.
As already mentioned, to get different
\mathcal{C}
-derivations our prototype performs backtracking on different choices of pointed clauses in the purification process of SCAN.

Another approach to finding multiple witnesses would be to instantiate the free predicate variables
W_{1},\dots,W_{d}
in the definition of
\mathrm{wit}(D)
.
These are contained in the witness if
D
does not use extended purity deletion.

In practice certain witnesses might be preferred over others, e.g., based on size.
Accounting for additional constraints like these when constructing witnesses is an avenue for future work.

Limitations for finding witnesses.

The original SCAN algorithm uses Skolemization if the input formula is not in clause form and after the saturation process tries to undo the Skolemization to find a logically equivalent first-order formula.
While reverse Skolemization is undecidable in general, it does work in some cases, for example, if the input formula has the form
\exists\overline{u}\,\varphi
where
\varphi
is quantifier-free.
We discuss such an example where the SCAN algorithm terminates and finds a SOQE-solution, but where no WSOQE-witness exists.

The input formula is
\Phi=\exists X\,\exists u\,\exists v\,(X(u)\land\neg X(v))
.
Skolemization of the first-order part yields the clause set
N=\left\{X(a),\neg X(b)\right\}
with Skolem constants
a
and
b
.
An
X
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
is given by
D=

\mathord{\left\{X(a),\neg X(b)\right\}}\mathord{}

\mathrm{Res}_{\underline{X(a)},\underline{\neg X(b)}}

\mathord{\left\{X(a),\neg X(b),a\not\simeq b\right\}}\mathord{}

\mathrm{PurDel}_{\underline{X(a)}}

\mathord{\left\{\neg X(b),a\not\simeq b\right\}}\mathord{}

\mathrm{ExtPurDel}_{X}^{-}

\mathord{\left\{a\not\simeq b\right\}}\mathord{}

The original SCAN algorithm now reverses the Skolemization on the resulting formula
a\not\simeq b
, to get the logically equivalent formula
\exists u\,\exists v\,u\not\simeq v
, i.e., we have
\Phi\equiv\exists u\,\exists v\,u\not\simeq v
.
The computed witness
\mathrm{wit}(D)=\mathrm{pResU}_{\underline{X(a)}}[X\leftarrow\lambda u.\bot]
is equivalent to
\lambda u.u\simeq a
.
However, this witness contains a Skolem constant which is not in the language of the input formula.
In fact, no WSOQE-witness exists in the input language:
Assume
\Phi
had a WSOQE-witness
\alpha=\lambda u.\varphi(u)
.
Then
\alpha
is a first-order predicate in the empty language and in every structure
\mathcal{M}
it defines the subset
\alpha^{\mathcal{M}}
given by
\left\{m\in M\ |\ \mathcal{M}\models\varphi(u)\right\}
.
For every two-element model
\mathcal{M}=\left\{m_{1},m_{2}\right\}

we have
\mathcal{M}\models\exists X\,\exists u\,\exists v\,(X(u)\land\neg X(v))
.
Since
\alpha
is a witness we get

\mathcal{M}\models\exists u\,\exists v\,(\varphi(u)\land\neg\varphi(v))
.
Thus
\alpha^{\mathcal{M}}
is neither the empty set nor the whole set
M
.
However, in the empty language the only first-order parameter-free definable sets in a structure

\mathcal{M}
are the empty set and
M
itself, see
[
29
, Theorem 2.1.2]
, which is a contradiction.

This shows a fundamental limitation for finding witnesses, even if SOQE-solutions exist.
For practical applications it would be useful to add Skolemization and reverse Skolemization steps to the implementation and investigate classes of formulas where witness construction succeeds in the presence of Skolemization.

Quantifier alternations

We have discussed the WSOQE problem in the context of existential quantifiers, but one can also consider the dual problem for universal quantifiers:
Given a formula
\forall\overline{X}\,\varphi
with first-order
\varphi
, find first-order predicates
\overline{\alpha}
such that

\forall\overline{X}\,\varphi\equiv\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}].

One can reduce this problem to the existential case since for all first-order predicates
\overline{\alpha}
we have
\forall\overline{X}\,\varphi\equiv\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}]
if and only if
\exists\overline{X}\,\neg\varphi\equiv\neg\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}]
by writing
\forall\overline{X}\,
as
\neg\exists\overline{X}\,\neg
.
This way, one can solve the problem for an arbitrary quantifier prefix by successively eliminating alternating blocks of existential and universal quantifiers starting with the innermost block.

While the reduction above works if one has a general WSOQE method that can handle any input formula,
our submission only
works when the input is a clause set,
i.e., the domain variables are universally
quantified.
The above reduction introduces a negation on the input formula,
meaning that we now have to deal with existential quantifiers on
the domain variables as well, e.g., via Skolemization.
However, as stated before there are fundamental limitations when introducing Skolemization.
For input formulas where the first-order part is quantifier-free our method is applicable though.

Completeness of one-sided
\overline{X}
-eliminating derivations.

To ensure that SCAN produces a one-sided derivation, if terminating, one can restrict the application of
\mathrm{PurDel}_{P}
to one-sided
P
.
However, this might restrict the number of clause sets for which an
\overline{X}
-eliminating derivation can be found.
It is open whether one-sided
\overline{X}
-eliminating derivations are complete in this sense:

Problem 1

If there is an
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
, is there always
a one-sided
\overline{X}
-eliminating
\mathcal{C}
-derivation from
N
?

A positive answer would justify using the strategy of restricting
\mathrm{PurDel}_{P}
to one-sided
P
during a run of the SCAN algorithm which, if terminating, guarantees a first-order witness by
Corollary

2
.

Computing witnesses with DLS(*).

As this work extends a known algorithm for solving SOQE to solve the more general WSOQE problem, the question arises whether other algorithms like DLS
[
13
]
and DLS*
[
14
,
40
]
can be equally modified to solve WSOQE and how the resulting witnesses compare.

Improvement of Ackermann’s Lemma.

Ackermann’s Lemma provides a method for solving WSOQE as well.
On clause sets, our method is an improvement on Ackermann’s Lemma in the following sense:
If Ackermann’s Lemma applies to
\exists X\,N
, then there is a one-sided
\mathcal{C}
-derivation
D
from
N
, such that
\mathrm{wit}(D)
is equivalent to the witness produced by Ackermann’s Lemma.
Furthermore our method can construct a witness for
Example

4
where Ackermann’s Lemma is not applicable.
This result makes no claim about whether our method improves on other Ackermann-based algorithms, such as DLS and DLS*, since they employ other transformation steps besides the application of Ackermann’s Lemma.

Equality reasoning.

A desirable extension is an implementation with
equality reasoning.
The original SCAN algorithm works in the presence of
equality
[
3
]
, so we expect the witness construction to carry over
to first-order logic with equality.

Solving FEQ.

As discussed in the Introduction, any WSOQE algorithm can be applied to solving formula equations, by appending a validity check with a first-order theorem prover.
In some cases our method can skip the additional validity check: If SCAN terminates with the empty set (which implies that
\exists\overline{X}\,N
is valid) and the resulting derivation is one-sided, the witness found is also a solution to the FEQ problem.
However, SCAN might output a valid clause set which is not the empty set, in which case a validity check is still required to solve FEQ.

This opens the door to apply this method in Boolean unification (which is a restriction of FEQ to nullary predicate variables) and in the context of software verification for solving sets of constrained Horn clauses
[
7
]
.

Application to Forgetting.

Methods for computing forgetting solutions and uniform
interpolants have been developed for description logics and modal
logics
[
33
,
32
,
34
,
2
]
.
For applications, e.g., knowledge processing, agent applications and
Boolean unification, the possibility of extending these to compute also
witnesses for eliminated variables would be attractive.
Using a WSOQE-solution instead of a SOQE-solution has the advantage that the structure of the
existing knowledge base is left largely intact.
Functionality to return witnesses could
provide a promising avenue to develop methods for such applications, but
such development is subject to future work.

Expressivity.

Investigating SOQE, and related problems, e.g., uniform
interpolation, one quickly encounters expressibility challenges.
These carry over to WSOQE, as we have seen in this paper, but also to FEQ
and Boolean unification as a special instance of FEQ.
However, applications might be able to cope with language extensions, e.g.,
the extent to which infinite witnesses can be expressed using fixpoints is worth investigating.
Enhancing the expressivity of the target language might increase success rates.

8
Conclusion

We introduced WSCAN, an extension of the SCAN algorithm on clause sets, to compute witnesses for existential second-order quantifiers, thereby solving the more general WSOQE-problem.
Given an
\overline{X}
-eliminating derivation
D
from a clause set
N
we can produce a witness for
\exists\overline{X}\,N
.
That witness might be infinite in general, but if in addition all purified pointed clauses of
D
have a certain finiteness property, like one-sidedness, we can guarantee that the witness is first-order.
To the best of our knowledge there are currently no other WSOQE-algorithms applicable to arbitrary clause sets.
This work paves the way to new applications of the SCAN algorithm in a variety of different areas,
ranging from modal correspondence theory and knowledge representation to verification.

On a more abstract level, we see this work as a contribution to bridging the gap between
second-order quantifier elimination (SOQE) and solving formula equations (FEQ) which will be of interest to the
respective communities investigating these problems.
SOQE has been studied mostly in the context of modal logic, description logics, knowledge representation and answer set programming.
On the other hand, FEQ is studied (usually in different formalisms and under different names)
in the verification and automated (inductive) theorem proving communities.
We believe that the complex of problems consisting of SOQE, WSOQE, and FEQ provides a suitable

common logical foundation
for work that is done on all of these topics, as has also been observed in
[
47
]
.
This perspective not only allows to relate these, at first sight different, problems to one another
on a logical level, but also stimulates a cross-fertilization in terms of practical solution
ideas, techniques, and algorithms.

Appendix 0.A
Relationship between WSOQE and FEQ

We give a proof of a relationship between WSOQE and FEQ which justifies using WSOQE algorithms for solving FEQ by appending a validity check using, e.g., a first-order theorem prover.

Proposition 2

Consider a formula of the form
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
where
\varphi
is first-order.
Then
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
has an FEQ-solution iff it is valid and has a WSOQE-witness.

Proof

For the left-to-right direction, let
\overline{\alpha}
be a tuple of first-order predicates
such that
\models\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}]
.
Then
\models\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
and thus
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\equiv\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}]
.

For the right-to-left direction, there is a tuple
\overline{\alpha}
of first-order predicates
such that
\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi\equiv\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}]
.
Since also
\models\exists\overline{X}\,\varphi
, we obtain
\models\varphi[\overline{X}\leftarrow\overline{\alpha}]
.

\displaystyle\left|E\right|

\displaystyle=1,\text{ if $E$ is an individual constant, variable, $\bot$ or $\top$}

\displaystyle\left|f(\overline{t})\right|

\displaystyle=1+\left|\overline{t}\right|\text{ for function symbols $f$ and terms $\overline{t}$}

\displaystyle\left|R(\overline{t})\right|

\displaystyle=1+\left|\overline{t}\right|,\text{ for predicate symbols $R$ and terms $\overline{t}$}

\displaystyle\left|X(\overline{t})\right|

\displaystyle=1+\left|\overline{t}\right|,\text{ for predicate variables $X$ and terms $\overline{t}$}

\displaystyle\left|\neg\varphi\right|

\displaystyle=1+\left|\varphi\right|,\text{ for a formula $\varphi$}

\displaystyle\left|\bigoplus_{i\in I}\varphi_{i}\right|

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\sum_{i\in I}\left|\varphi_{i}\right|,&\text{ for $\oplus\in\left\{\land,\lor\right\}$ and formulas $\varphi_{i}$, if $I$ is finite}\\
\infty&\text{ for $\oplus\in\left\{\land,\lor\right\}$ and formulas $\varphi_{i}$, if $I$ is infinite}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\left|\varphi\rightarrow\psi\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\varphi\right|+\left|\psi\right|,\text{ for formulas $\varphi$ and $\psi$}

\displaystyle\left|\varphi\leftrightarrow\psi\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\varphi\right|+\left|\psi\right|,\text{ for formulas $\varphi$ and $\psi$}

\displaystyle\left|\forall u\,\varphi\right|

\displaystyle=1+\left|\varphi\right|,\text{ for a formula $\varphi$}

\displaystyle\left|\exists u\,\varphi\right|

\displaystyle=1+\left|\varphi\right|,\text{ for a formula $\varphi$}

\displaystyle\left|\forall X\,\varphi\right|

\displaystyle=1+\left|\varphi\right|,\text{ for a formula $\varphi$}

\displaystyle\left|\exists X\,\varphi\right|

\displaystyle=1+\left|\varphi\right|,\text{ for a formula $\varphi$}

\displaystyle\left|\lambda u.E\right|

\displaystyle=\left|E\right|,\text{ for an expression $E$}

\displaystyle\left|(E_{1},\dots,E_{d})\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\left|E_{i}\right|,\text{ for expressions $E_{1},\dots,E_{d}$.}
