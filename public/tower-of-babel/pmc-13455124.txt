Heterotopic Ossification at Tracker Pin Sites Following Robotic‐Assisted Total Knee Arthroplasty
Authors: Jun Wei Lim, Chloe Jane Lowe, Martin Mitchell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CaseA 72‐year‐old Caucasian man presented with left knee osteoarthritis and underwent Mako robotic‐assisted total knee arthroplasty (TKA). Two months postoperatively, he complained of mild discomfort in his left quadriceps and limited flexion, with an active range of motion (ROM) from 0° to 60°. At 10 months, his ROM had not improved, and plain radiographs revealed a large, calcified mass in the distal femur, which appeared to be emanating from the tracker pin sites used during Mako robotic‐assisted TKA, raising concern for heterotopic ossification (HO). He subsequently underwent excision of the HO and was started on indomethacin.ConclusionSurgeons performing robotic‐assisted TKAs should be aware of the potential for HO formation at tracker pin sites as a possible complication, particularly in patients presenting with postoperative stiffness.

1. Introduction

Heterotopic ossification (HO) is a pathological process characterised by the formation of extraskeletal bone within soft tissues. HO may occur secondary to hereditary conditions but more commonly develops following local musculoskeletal or neurological trauma [1–3]. The reported incidence of HO after total knee arthroplasty (TKA) varies widely in the literature, but it commonly occurs around the prosthesis [4, 5].

Recent advances have improved our understanding of the mechanisms involved in HO formation. Current evidence suggests that HO is driven by a complex interaction between inflammation, immune dysregulation, mesenchymal stem cell differentiation, angiogenesis and aberrant osteogenic signalling following tissue injury. A number of translational studies have also investigated potential strategies for earlier diagnosis and targeted treatment, including therapies aimed at reducing oxidative stress and regulating stem cell activity. Despite that, the exact pathogenesis of arthroplasty‐associated HO remains unclear, and its prevention and early management continue to present clinical challenges [6–12].

The use of robotic‐assisted TKA has increased in recent years, aiming to improve component positioning and reduce alignment outliers [13]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case report describing an unusual complication of HO originating from the tracker pin sites following Mako robotic‐assisted TKA (Stryker, Mahwah). The patient was informed that data concerning the case would be submitted for publication, and he provided a written consent.

2. Case Presentation

A Caucasian man in his 70s presented to the orthopaedic elective clinic with increasing left knee pain and limited mobility, preoperative range of motion (ROM) was 10°–100°. His past medical history included atrial fibrillation (on warfarin), vasculitis, right total hip arthroplasty (THA), right TKA, hypertension, transient ischaemic attack and well controlled type II diabetes mellitus. The patient′s body mass index (BMI) was 29.1 kg/m2. He had experienced no complications following his previous right THA and TKA.

Plain radiographs of his left knee demonstrated bone‐on‐bone medial compartment osteoarthritis (Figure 1), and the patient was subsequently listed for left TKR. All elective patients underwent preoperative assessment within 3 months prior to surgery. Routine preoperative investigations, including full blood count (FBC), urea and electrolytes (U&E), coagulation profile and group and save testing, were performed and were largely unremarkable (Table 1). In accordance with local perioperative anticoagulation guidelines, warfarin was discontinued 5 days before surgery, and dalteparin was administered according to the regimen outlined in Table 2.

The patient subsequently underwent Mako robotic‐assisted TKA with Stryker Triathlon cruciate retaining (CR) implants (Figure 2). The surgery was performed using standard techniques, including prophylactic antibiotic with cefuroxime, a thigh tourniquet, intraoperative infiltration of 2 mg/mL ropivacaine for postoperative analgesia and administration of tranexamic acid just prior to tourniquet release. There was no unusual or excessive intraoperative bleeding. Warfarin was restarted on the first postoperative day, as there were no concerns regarding wound haemostasis. The patient subsequently underwent standard postoperative knee replacement rehabilitation based on Enhanced Recovery After Surgery (ERAS) programme, with an emphasis on early mobility and structured physiotherapy exercises, including knee extension, heel slides, quadricep sets and straight leg raises [14].

Anteroposterior and lateral views of the postoperative left knee plain radiographs demonstrating drilling marks (arrows) for tracker pins on the femoral diaphysis.

The patient was reviewed in the routine arthroplasty clinic at 2 months postoperatively, at which time there were no concerns regarding wound healing. He reported mild discomfort in his left quadriceps and had an active knee ROM from 0° to 60°. He was advised to continue with ROM exercises and physiotherapy. Due to persistent knee stiffness, he was re‐reviewed in the consultant clinic at 10 months postoperatively, where he was found to have a diffuse, solid swelling in his left distal femur and continued limited ROM of 0° to 60°.

Repeated plain radiographs demonstrated a large, diffuse area of heterotopic bone formation, appearing to originate from the tracker pin sites used during Mako robotic‐assisted TKA (Figure 3). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computerised tomography (CT) scans were obtained to further characterise the mass. These revealed a large calcific lesion on the anterior cortex of the distal femur, measuring 5.6 × 4.3 × 8.9 cm (Figures 4 and 5), consistent with HO. Blood tests, including alkaline phosphate and calcium levels, were normal.

Given the limitation in ROM, a joint decision was made to proceed with surgical excision of the mass. The patient was positioned supine under general anaesthesia, with a sterile thigh tourniquet applied. The proximal end of the previous incision was utilised and extended proximally. The skin, fascia and quadriceps tendon were incised to expose the mass (Figure 6). It was carefully dissected from the surrounding muscle and excised en bloc using osteotomes. The debrided femoral cortex was covered with bone wax, and the wound was closed using standard techniques. The excised mass was sent for histopathological evaluation. After excision, the patient′s intraoperative passive ROM improved to 0°–100° (Figure 7). He was commenced on 75 mg of indomethacin daily for 6 weeks with omeprazole for gastric protection. Prophylactic radiotherapy was not administered in this case. The excised lesion was sent for histopathological analysis, which confirmed the diagnosis of HO.

Postoperatively, the patient followed a standard TKA rehabilitation ERAS programme, focusing on ROM and quadriceps strengthening. At 6 weeks postoperatively, plain radiographs showed the absence of ossified tissue, and his ROM had improved to 0°–110°. At 1 year postoperatively, there were no signs on x‐ray of HO recurrence.

3. Discussion

There are many causes of stiffness following TKA, and it can be debilitating for patients [15]. In this case, intraoperative technical error was considered unlikely given the use of robotic‐assisted TKA. However, previous studies have demonstrated an association between warfarin use and postoperative stiffness. Kahlenberg et al. [16] reported a significantly increased risk of manipulation under anaesthesia following TKR in patients who used preoperative warfarin, possibly related to increased postoperative bleeding and haematoma formation. Although there were no postoperative wound concerns suggestive of clinically significant haematoma formation in our patient, prolonged warfarin therapy may still have contributed to the development of HO.

Reported risk factors for HO development following TKA include male sex, obesity, ankylosing spondylitis, prior HO, previous trauma or surgery, a history of septic arthritis, hypertrophic arthrosis, haematoma formation, difficulty in regulating warfarin dosage, postoperative knee manipulation, additional surgical dissection to expose the distal femur and notching of the anterior femoral cortex [17–19]. With the combination of preoperative CT scanning and optical motion capture technology, which allows for real‐time intraoperative data collection, the optimal bone resection window can be calculated by computer software. This process conceptually helps to limit bone resection and soft tissue releases [20, 21]. Therefore, the surgical dissection in this case would have been less extensive compared with standard manual TKA.

Despite lacking many of the classical risk factors for HO, our patient developed extensive HO predominantly at the anterior aspect of the distal femur, affecting the extensor mechanism and thereby limiting knee flexion. The local treating team has subsequently considered the efficacy of using bone wax prophylactically in the pin site holes to prevent this complication through use of its haemostatic tamponade effect. However, bone wax was not utilised in the present case because of concerns regarding its potential complications, including foreign body reaction, impaired bone healing, chronic inflammation and increased susceptibility to infection, all of which have been reported in the literature [22].

HO is increasingly recognised as a complication following trauma and major orthopaedic surgeries [23]. Similar mechanisms have been proposed in cases of HO following anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, where bone debris and hematoma believed to contribute to progressive bone formation over time [24]. Sandilands et al. [16] described HO formation following the placement of a pelvic external fixator in a patient with an unstable pelvic fracture, which resulted in anterior inferior iliac spine (AIIS) hip impingement. However, that patient had multiple risk factors for HO formation, including head injury, spinal cord injury, prolonged intubation, multiple surgeries, male sex and obesity [25]. In contrast, our patient had relatively few established risk factors apart from warfarin use and possible local bleeding related to tracker pin insertion. The periosteal reaction associated with pin insertion, pin‐site haematoma and cortical bleeding may have acted as local stimuli for ectopic bone formation.

The use of robotic‐assisted TKA has increased substantially in recent years [26]. At our institute alone, 214 Mako TKA were successfully performed in the past year. For robotic‐assisted TKA, additional incisions are required for the insertion of tracker pins, which are known to carry risks such as pin‐related fractures [27], whereas classic Mako robotic‐assisted TKA procedures commonly require bicortical diaphyseal pin insertion [28] further increasing this risk. However, to the best of our knowledge, HO formation at tracker pin sites following robotic‐assisted TKA, as presented in this case, has never been reported. Although robotic‐assisted systems are preferable for TKAs to minimise soft‐tissue infection, the local effects of pin‐site insertion may introduce new potential risk points for the development of HO, highlighting the need for careful postoperative assessment in patients with unexpected stiffness.

3.1. Limitations

Although this case highlights an important potential side effect of Mako robotic‐associated TKAs, this case is limited by the number of reported cases of HO at pin sites postoperatively as it is likely underreported.

4. Conclusion

This case report highlights that HO can develop at tracker pin sites following robotic‐assisted TKA. Although rare, surgeons performing robotic‐assisted TKAs should be aware of this important potential complication, particularly in patients presenting with stiffness postoperatively.
