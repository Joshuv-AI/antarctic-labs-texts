State-dependent neuronal and network dynamics in the lateral hypothalamus across sevoflurane anesthesia–emergence revealed by microelectrode arrays
Authors: Qi Li, Qianli Jia, Yilin Song, Jian Miao, Ying Wang, Yu Liu, Yiming Duan, Bomin Sun, Jinping Luo, Qianzi Yang, Xinxia Cai, Yan Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGeneral anesthetics can induce a safe, reversible state of unconsciousness, yet their neural mechanisms remain incompletely understood. The lateral hypothalamus (LH) contains orexinergic and other arousal-promoting neurons and projects broadly to anesthesia–arousal–related nuclei such as the ventral tegmental area (VTA) together with locus coeruleus (LC), being critically involved in anesthetic-induced loss of consciousness. However, high spatiotemporal-resolution in vivo evidence characterizing the dynamic electrophysiological features of LH during the anesthesia–emergence transition is lacking. To fill this gap, we developed multi-channel microelectrode arrays and optimized the electrode surface using PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS to lower impedance and improve signal-to-noise ratio. Under a mouse paradigm spanning wakefulness, sevoflurane anesthesia, and post-anesthetic emergence, we simultaneously recorded, in vivo, cortical electroencephalography (EEG), local field potentials (LFPs), and single-unit activity (spikes) within the LH. At the single-neuron level, 78% of LH neurons were suppressed during anesthesia, exhibiting distinct and recognizable spike waveform features, whereas 11% were activated and 11% remained unchanged. In a subset of suppressed and activated units, spike amplitude varied in parallel with firing rate under sevoflurane. At the population level, sevoflurane produced low-frequency-dominated spectra accompanied by elevated spectral edge frequency (SEF95) in both cortical EEG and LH LFPs; the LH displayed a higher fraction of delta power, whereas EEG showed larger SEF95 shifts relative to wake, suggesting greater cortical sensitivity to anesthetic depth while the LH exhibited more pronounced synchrony. Moreover, sevoflurane anesthesia was associated with a state-dependent enhancement of low-frequency functional coupling between LH LFPs and cortical EEG, which was reversible upon emergence. Collectively, these findings delineate a multi-scale electrophysiological profile of the LH across anesthesia–emergence and lay the groundwork for elucidating the circuit mechanisms through which inhaled anesthetics lead to loss of consciousness. Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Nanoparticles, Biosensors

Introduction

Although general anesthetics are extensively applied in clinical practice to induce a reversible loss of consciousness1,2, their molecular and circuit-level mechanistic processes within the central nervous system remain incompletely understood. Accumulating evidence indicates that loss of consciousness caused by anesthesia and normal sleep–wake state transitions may partially rely on shared neural networks3–5, involving key regions including the ventral tegmental area (VTA), preoptic area (POA), locus coeruleus (LC), and the lateral hypothalamus (LH).

The LH is critically involved in both anesthesia and sleep–wake regulation. At the circuit level, LH neurons innervate multiple arousal-related regions, including the VTA, BF, and LC5,6. At the cellular scale, the LH comprises heterogeneous neuronal populations, including orexin neurons, GABAergic neurons, melanocyte-concentrating hormone (MCH) neurons, and glutamatergic neurons7. Orexin neurons are critical for maintaining wakefulness, and their deficiency leads to narcolepsy8; in contrast, MCH neurons show elevated activity during rapid eye movement sleep and contribute to the stabilization of sleep states9. Thus, elucidating how general anesthetics act on the LH is of considerable importance.

Current research on the involvement of the LH in sleep and anesthesia mechanisms predominantly relies on chemogenetics, optogenetics, and in vivo calcium imaging approaches10,11. Previous studies have shown that chemogenetic stimulation of orexin-expressing cells within the lateral hypothalamus significantly shortens the time required for anesthetic emergence10, whereas optogenetic inhibition combined with patch-clamp recordings has demonstrated that suppressing these neurons can induce sleep11. Although these techniques offer cell-type specificity, they are unable to provide electrophysiological information with sub-millisecond temporal resolution and therefore cannot accurately capture the instantaneous firing properties and population-level dynamics of LH neurons during state transitions.

A limited number of in vivo electrophysiological studies on the LH have employed glass micropipettes or low-channel-count stainless steel microwires to record single-unit or multi-unit activity (MUA)12–14. Recordings with glass micropipettes have shown that identified orexin neurons fire at high rates during wakefulness but are nearly silent during sleep12. A study using microwires in the PF-LH has revealed distinct firing patterns of LH neurons across non–rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep, rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, and the waking state13. Moreover, a study using simultaneous recordings of MUA and EEG reported that population activity in different LH subregions was markedly increased after sleep deprivation but exhibited region-specific suppression during recovery sleep14.

However, these conventional electrophysiological methods are limited by sparse sampling and restricted spatial coverage, making it difficult to simultaneously obtain single-cell activity and local network activity within the LH, together with cortical EEG signals, thereby preventing a cross-scale characterization of the coordination between LH activity and cortical rhythms. Moreover, existing experimental paradigms have focused primarily on natural sleep and circadian regulation, and systematic recordings across the continuous and dynamic anesthesia–emergence process remain lacking. Therefore, high-spatiotemporal-resolution, multi-channel electrophysiological techniques are urgently needed to acquire LH multi-channel spike activity, LFPs, and cortical EEG signals throughout the awake–anesthetized–emergent sequence, enabling a more comprehensive and systematic characterization of LH neuronal activity.

Here, we developed implantable multielectrode arrays (MEAs) based on silicon substrates with high spatiotemporal precision using microelectromechanical systems (MEMS) technology, enabling in vivo recording of neuronal electrophysiological activity in the LH with minimal implantation damage. The probe geometry was designed to match the implantation depth and anatomical scale of the mouse LH, enabling anatomically constrained sampling within the targeted deep nucleus. To improve electrode performance, recording sites were further modified with PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS15,16. During the awake–anesthetized–emergent transition induced by sevoflurane, we simultaneously recorded LH single-unit activity, LFPs, and cortical EEG signals. By systematically analyzing electrophysiological data across these stages, we delineated the effects of sevoflurane on LH neurons and cortical electrical activity, revealed the critical role and distinctive electrophysiological features of the LH during the anesthesia–emergence transition, and provided new theoretical underpinnings and methodological approaches for further probing the neural mechanistic pathways underlying loss of consciousness produced by inhaled anesthetics.

Materials and methods

Structural design and electrochemical modification of MEAs

MEAs were fabricated using MEMS technology and featured a three-layer architecture comprising a Si/SiO₂ substrate, a Ti/Pt conductive layer, and a SiO₂/Si₃N₄ insulating top layer, with a total thickness of approximately 25 μm (Fig. S1a). The device consisted of two thin-profile, mechanically robust shanks that facilitated stable insertion into deep-brain structures while minimizing tissue displacement (Fig. S1b). Each shank had a length of 6 mm and a width of 280 μm, dimensions sufficient for targeting the lateral hypothalamus in mice. The two shanks were separated by 60 μm at the base, a configuration designed to enable broader mediolateral coverage once positioned within deep nuclei. These structural features provided the geometric basis for wide-field neural sampling in downstream electrophysiological recordings.

To improve the recording performance, all recording sites were modified using an electrochemical workstation (Princeton Applied Research, USA). Two modification strategies were compared: PtNPs modification and PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS composite modification. The PtNPs precursor was generated by combining aqueous solutions of H₂PtCl₆ (48 mM) and Pb(CH₃COO)₂ (4.2 mM) in equal volumes, after which the mixture was allowed to age overnight and subsequently clarified by filtration. The PEDOT:PSS solution was prepared by introducing 20 mM EDOT into a 0.1 M PSS solution and subjecting the mixture to ultrasonic treatment for 30 min. PtNPs were electrodeposited using a two-electrode system by chronoamperometry (−1 V, 30 s), while PEDOT:PSS was electrodeposited using a three-electrode system by cyclic voltammetry (CV, 0–0.85 V, 5 cycles).

Surface morphology of the bare and modified electrode sites was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) at magnifications of 5 µm and 500 nm. Electrochemical impedance measurements were performed in phosphate-buffered saline with a three-electrode configuration over a frequency range of 10–105 Hz.

Animal

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks were utilized for all experiments and were sourced from Vital River (Beijing, China). Animals were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. Housing was conducted in a controlled environment with a 12-h light/dark cycle, ambient temperature of 20–22 °C, and relative humidity of approximately 50%.

In vivo multi-channel electrophysiological recordings

Mice were induced and maintained under 1.5% isoflurane and positioned in a stereotaxic frame (RWD Life Science Co., Ltd., China). Using a microdrive (Model 2662, David Kopf Instruments, USA), MEAs were implanted into the lateral hypothalamus (LH; AP: −1.34 ± 0.05 mm; ML: +1.00 ± 0.05 mm; DV: −5.00 ± 0.05 mm). The ground and EEG electrodes were attached to skull screws placed anterior and posterior to the bregma, while electromyogram (EMG) electrodes were inserted into the neck muscles. All implants were secured with dental cement, and animals were individually housed for postoperative recovery for no less than 7 days.

For recordings, mice were housed in a transparent, gas-tight flow-through chamber that allowed free movement, with separate inlet and outlet ports for continuous gas exchange and anesthetic scavenging. A gas mixture containing 100% O₂ as the carrier gas was delivered at 1 L/min through the inlet, and excess gas was continuously removed through the outlet. After a 30-min habituation period, baseline signals were recorded for 15 min. During the anesthesia phase, sevoflurane was delivered in the O₂ carrier gas via a vaporizer to achieve 1.5% sevoflurane for 30 min, followed by a 30-min recovery period after discontinuation of sevoflurane. The disappearance and recovery of the righting reflex were recorded concurrently.

Neural signals from the LH were recorded using a custom-built electrophysiological recording system (AIRCAS-128, China) at a sampling rate of 30 kHz for spikes and 1 kHz for LFPs, EEG, and EMG signals. The spike duration window was set to 3 ms. The MEA was connected to the recording system using a custom-made 16-channel headstage that provided signal amplification and filtering, together with a lightweight cable of sufficient length to permit unrestricted movement of the animal.

Spike analysis and neuron classification

Spike sorting was conducted with Offline Sorter (Plexon Inc., Dallas, TX, USA) to remove artifacts and isolate single-unit activities on each channel. The resulting spike trains and waveforms were then transferred to NeuroExplorer (V4 x64, NeuroNexus, Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Subsequent analyses of spikes, LFPs, and electroencephalograms (EEGs) were performed in MATLAB (Version R2023b; MathWorks, USA).

To classify LH neurons according to their firing responses to sevoflurane anesthesia, we compared the firing rates between wakefulness and anesthesia periods. For each neuron, spike timestamps from the first 600 s of the awake period and the last 600 s of the anesthesia period were binned at 1-min intervals (10 bins in total) to calculate firing rates. Differences across the two periods were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Based on these results, neurons were then categorized into three types: suppressed neurons (firing rate significantly lower during anesthesia, p < 0.05); activated neurons (significantly higher, p < 0.05); and neutral neurons (no significant difference, p > 0.05).

Spectral analyses of LFP and EEG signals were performed across predefined frequency ranges, including slow oscillations (0.5–1 Hz), delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (30–80 Hz)17,18.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis and statistical testing were performed with GraphPad Prism (version 9.5; GraphPad Software, USA) and MATLAB Version R2023b (MathWorks, USA).

Group differences in signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) between modification conditions were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Differences in firing rates and band powers among the three experimental stages were evaluated with Friedman’s test for repeated measures, followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparison post hoc comparisons. Waveform parameters of different neuron types were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc correction and false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment. Paired differences in SEF95 values were evaluated with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, and multiple comparisons were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR procedure.

To assess the correlation between LH LFPs and cortical EEG activity, correlation analyses were performed on band power signals. For each animal, 15-min data segments were selected for each behavioral stage, including the awake period, the anesthesia period following LORR, and the emergence period following RORR. Signals were divided into non-overlapping 10-s time windows, and Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated between LH LFPs and EEG band power within each stage. To enable group-level statistical comparisons, correlation coefficients were converted to Fisher’s z values. Differences in Fisher z values across the three experimental stages were assessed using Friedman’s test, followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc pairwise comparisons.

All data are summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Significance levels: ns, not significant; *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001.

The SNR was quantified by calculating the peak-to-peak amplitude of individual spikes and estimating baseline noise as the root-mean-square (RMS) of the raw signal. Spike amplitudes were subsequently normalized to the noise level by dividing them by twice the RMS noise, yielding the SNR19,20.

Results

Design of MEAs and performance improvement by PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS modification

To enable real-time single-neuron recordings in the LH, we designed and fabricated MEAs with 16 recording sites (20 μm in diameter) distributed along the distal tips of two shanks (Figs. 1a and S1a). The edge-to-edge spacing of 620 μm between the two shanks allowed simultaneous sampling of neuronal ensembles across the mediolateral extent of the LH. In addition, the distal ends of the two shanks were designed with an angled tip geometry, and the recording sites were correspondingly arranged along this oblique edge, enabling the probe to cover a broader range of dorsoventral depths during insertion. This configuration allowed the capture of heterogeneous neuronal activity from multiple LH subregions.

Schematic illustration of the design, implantation site, and in vivo recording protocol of the multi-channel MEAs. a Optical microscope image of the entire MEA and a magnified view of the sensing sites at the tip. b Representative in vivo electrophysiological traces recorded using MEAs, illustrating differences in SNR between the two modification conditions. c Comparison of average SNR between PtNPs and PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS modifications (n = 6, Mann–Whitney U test, **P < 0.01). d Schematic illustration of MEA implantation targeting the lateral hypothalamic area (LH) in mice. Optical images of coronal brain slices after MEA implantation, e the electrode track and its endpoint in the LH region, f magnified view of the electrode tip within the LH region shown in (e). g Schematic illustration of the experimental procedure from MEA implantation to in vivo electrophysiological recording, including awake, sevoflurane anesthesia, and emergence phases

The packaged MEA was further integrated with two EEG and two EMG leads (Fig. S1c), allowing synchronous acquisition of single-unit spikes, LFPs, cortical EEG, and muscular EMG signals. This multi-scale recording configuration provided substantially broader spatial coverage and richer electrophysiological information than conventional single-wire electrodes, supporting population-level characterization of LH dynamics during the awake–anesthetized–emergent transition.

To optimize the electrophysiological recording performance of the MEAs, the electrode sites were modified using either conventional platinum nanoparticles (PtNPs) or a composite material of PtNPs and PEDOT:PSS. SEM analysis revealed pronounced differences in surface morphology among the three electrode types (Fig. S2a–c). The bare electrode exhibited a relatively smooth surface (Fig. S2a), whereas PtNPs modification produced a granular and porous nanostructure with an increased effective surface area (Fig. S2b). In contrast, the PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS composite layer filled the interparticle gaps and formed a more continuous and uniform surface morphology (Fig. S2c), indicating improved structural integrity at the electrode–electrolyte interface.

Consistent with these morphological changes, electrochemical impedance measurements demonstrated substantial improvements in electrode performance following surface modification (Fig. S2d–f). For bare electrodes, the impedance magnitude and phase angle were 1245.58 ± 313.28 kΩ and 64.25 ± 7.57°, respectively. These values decreased markedly to 28.74 ± 2.24 kΩ and 51.18 ± 5.15° after PtNPs modification, and were further reduced to 12.56 ± 1.91 kΩ and 23.03 ± 1.99° following PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS composite modification, reflecting a progressive enhancement in electrochemical properties.

Consistent with these electrochemical advantages, the PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS-modified electrodes exhibited superior in vivo recording quality compared with PtNPs-modified electrodes alone. Representative electrophysiological traces obtained from the two electrode modification conditions are presented in Fig. 1b. The maximum signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) reached 6.86 in the PtNPs group and increased to 12.63 in the PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS group. Statistical analysis of the mean SNR across animals confirmed a significant enhancement in recording performance with PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS modification (Fig. 1c, p = 0.0022). In addition, a longitudinal assessment of post-implantation recording quality based on the maximum spike SNR at 1, 2, 4, and 8 weeks showed a significant decrease only at 8 weeks compared with 1 week; however, the maximum SNR at 8 weeks remained above 10 across recording sites (Fig. S6).

In summary, we successfully fabricated structurally robust and functionally stable MEAs and demonstrated that PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS composite modification markedly improved their electrochemical and recording performance. The modified electrodes exhibited excellent conductivity, low impedance, and enhanced signal fidelity during in vivo recordings, providing a reliable basis for stable acquisition of multi-channel neuronal activity and network oscillations in subsequent experiments.

Recording paradigm and representative electrophysiological signals from the LH

To investigate the changes in neuronal activity within the LH during the anesthesia–emergence transition, multi-channel MEAs were implanted into the LH region. The electrode implantation procedure is schematically depicted in Fig. 1d, and optical images of post-perfusion brain slices are presented in Fig. 1e, f, confirming that the recording sites were precisely located within the LH. The overall recording paradigm is illustrated in Fig. 1g, which follows the awake–anesthetized–emergent sequence described in the “Materials and methods.” Throughout the experiment, the loss and recovery of righting reflex (LORR and RORR) were simultaneously monitored to define behavioral state transitions21.

As shown in Fig. 2, continuous electrophysiological recordings from a representative mouse throughout the entire 75-min awake–anesthesia–emergence period included EMG signals (Fig. 2b), EEG signals (Fig. 2c), and multi-channel neuronal spikes and LFP signals (Fig. 2d). The multi-channel recordings revealed diverse patterns of neuronal dynamics during anesthesia and emergence. Among the 11 neurons illustrated in this representative example, 8 neurons (72.7%) exhibited marked suppression of firing during sevoflurane anesthesia and gradual recovery during emergence (e.g., channels 4 and 6). One neuron (9.1%) exhibited the opposite firing pattern, showing lower discharge rates during wakefulness but a marked increase in firing during anesthesia, returning to baseline levels during recovery (e.g., channel 9), while 2 neurons (18.2%) maintained relatively stable activity across all stages (e.g., channel 2). Notably, neurons whose firing decreased during anesthesia generally exhibited higher spike amplitudes during wakefulness than neurons whose firing increased or remained relatively stable, whereas the latter two types of responses tended to show smaller amplitudes, as visually evident from the vertical scales of the spike traces in Fig. 2d. Meanwhile, EEG and EMG signals, together with LH spiking and LFP activity, displayed coordinated state-dependent changes across the anesthesia–emergence transition, with robust spiking and relatively desynchronized EEG during wakefulness, suppressed firing in the majority of units and stronger low-frequency activity around LORR under sevoflurane, and a gradual return toward wake-like patterns around RORR.

Taken together, the representative recordings in Fig. 2 show that LH neurons exhibit heterogeneous and state-dependent firing responses during sevoflurane anesthesia, including neurons with markedly reduced firing, neurons with increased activity, and neurons that remained relatively stable across states. These observed response patterns, along with the associated amplitude differences, provide the basis for subsequent population-level analyses of LH electrophysiological dynamics during the anesthesia–emergence transition.

Classification of LH neurons based on firing patterns across anesthesia–emergence transition

To characterize the firing-rate dynamics of LH neurons across the anesthesia–emergence transition, we first visualized all recorded neurons using heatmaps aligned to the experimental timeline (Fig. 3a). The heatmap revealed three distinct response patterns consistent with the state-dependent firing patterns observed in individual animals (Fig. 2): a large proportion of neurons showed a marked decrease in firing after the onset of sevoflurane anesthesia, while a smaller group exhibited increased firing, and another group showed little change across states.

Top, firing rates of individual neurons and group means during awake, sevoflurane anesthesia, and emergence; bottom, representative spike waveforms (mean ± SD) for each neuron type, calculated from n = 50 randomly selected spikes during the awake state. c Proportion of suppressed, activated, and neutral neurons among all recorded neurons. To quantify the heterogeneity of neuronal responses during the sevoflurane-induced anesthesia–emergence transition, we compared the firing rates of each neuron between the awake period and the sevoflurane period (after the LORR). Based on these comparisons (as defined in the “Materials and methods”), neurons were classified into three groups: suppressed, activated, and neutral. To characterize the firing properties of each neuronal group after classification, we examined their activity across the awake, sevoflurane anesthesia, and emergence stages. Comparison of firing rates across stages (Fig. 3b) revealed that suppressed neurons exhibited a significant reduction in firing during stable anesthesia compared with the awake state, whereas activated neurons showed increased firing during anesthesia, rising from a relatively low baseline firing level in the awake state. In contrast, neutral neurons maintained relatively stable firing rates across all three stages. Representative spike raster plots illustrating these stage-dependent firing patterns are shown in Fig. S3a–c, together with representative spike waveforms for each neuronal group (Fig. 3b). We then quantified the overall proportion of each neuronal type among all recorded neurons (n = 47 neurons from 5 mice). As shown in Fig. 3c, suppressed neurons constituted the largest proportion of the recorded population (78%), whereas activated and neutral neurons each accounted for 11%. This distribution indicates that more than two-thirds of LH neurons exhibit a significant reduction in firing during anesthesia.

To minimize the influence of inter-animal variability in behavioral transition timing, the LORR and RORR time points were aligned across animals. Heatmaps of z-scored firing rates (Figs. 3d, e and S3d) further illustrated the temporal evolution of these firing-rate changes during the anesthesia–emergence transition. In particular, the peri-event representations made the gradual suppression and subsequent recovery of suppressed neurons (Fig. 3d), as well as the transient elevation of activated neurons around the LORR period (Fig. 3e), more clearly apparent at the population level. Neutral neurons showed no consistent peri-event modulation in firing (Fig. S3d).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that LH neurons comprise three functionally distinct subpopulations—suppressed, activated, and neutral—that exhibit robust and state-dependent firing patterns across the anesthesia–emergence transition. This tripartite organization provides a clear population-level framework for understanding LH neural dynamics during anesthesia and emergence.

Spiking phenotypes and state-dependent modulation of LH neurons during the anesthesia–emergence process

In the awake state, we examined the spike waveforms of neurons in the LH, with representative examples shown in Fig. 4a. Clear differences in amplitude, width, and overall shape were evident across neurons, suggesting that neurons in deep nuclei may likewise exhibit distinguishable firing signatures. To evaluate this observation, we quantified waveform features for each unit during the awake baseline, including the peak-to-valley ratio (P/V), trough-to-peak time (TTP), half-valley width (HVW), firing rate, and amplitude (Fig. 4b).

The analysis showed no statistically significant differences in waveform features between activated and neutral neurons (Figs. 4c–e and S3). However, activated neurons displayed a noticeably lower baseline firing rate (Figs. 4a–c and S3a). By contrast, suppressed neurons differed significantly from the other types on multiple metrics, exhibiting larger spike amplitudes (Fig. 4c), shorter TTP values (Fig. 4d), and higher P/V ratios (Fig. 4e). These results indicate that, beyond dynamic differences in firing rate, suppressed neurons also possess distinguishable spike-shape characteristics.

During waveform analysis, we further noted that spike amplitude varied markedly across states for a subset of neurons, suggesting state-dependent modulation of action potential shape during anesthesia–emergence. To verify this, we compared amplitude across the awake, sevoflurane, and emergence stages for each neuron (Fig. 4f–h). Most neurons showed stable amplitudes across stages (Fig. 4f), whereas a minority exhibited clear stage-dependent fluctuations. Among them, some suppressed neurons showed pronounced amplitude reductions during anesthesia that returned to baseline upon emergence (Fig. 4g); conversely, some activated neurons displayed increased amplitude under anesthesia that fell back during emergence (Fig. 4h). In these neurons, amplitude changes closely tracked their firing rate (FR) dynamics.

To quantify this relationship, we plotted FR and amplitude trajectories over the entire anesthesia–emergence sequence (Fig. S4a, b) and performed Pearson correlation analyses for representative units with robust amplitude fluctuations (Fig. 5a, b). FR and amplitude were significantly positively correlated (Fig. 5a, suppressed units: r = 0.366, p < 0.0001; Fig. 5b, activated units: r = 0.695, p < 0.0001), whereas the correlation disappeared in time-shuffled controls (Fig. 5c, suppressed units: r = 0.013, p = 0.880; Fig. 5d, activated units: r = 0.067, p = 0.423). Spearman analyses yielded consistent results (Fig. S4c, d).

Taken together, LH neurons exhibit type-specific dynamics in firing patterns during anesthesia–emergence, along with state-dependent plasticity in spike shape. These findings indicate that sevoflurane may shape the neuronal firing patterns in deep nuclei through joint modulation of intrinsic excitability and network inputs.

Sevoflurane anesthesia induces divergent spectral dynamics between cortex and lateral hypothalamus

Beyond the single-neuron level, we further analyzed population-level oscillatory characteristics from two perspectives: the LH LFPs and cortical EEG, and compared their spectral profiles across behavioral states and recording modalities. As shown in Fig. 6a and S5c, the power spectral densities (PSD) of cortical EEG (Fig. S5c) and LH LFPs (Fig. 6a) under different states are illustrated. During the wakefulness and recovery phases, the overall spectral distributions were largely similar, whereas during sevoflurane anesthesia, a marked increase in power emerged in the low-frequency range (slow-delta and delta < 4 Hz), accompanied by a slight upward shift in the mid-to-high-frequency bands. To directly compare spectral differences between modalities during anesthesia, the PSDs of cortical EEG and LH LFPs during the sevoflurane stage were overlaid (Fig. 6b). Notably, comparison during the sevoflurane stage revealed that LH LFPs exhibited relatively higher power in the low-frequency range, whereas cortical EEG showed comparatively greater power in the higher-frequency bands.

Further statistical analyses of power across frequency bands (Fig. S5d, e) revealed that, in LH LFPs, power in the slow-delta and delta bands was significantly elevated during sevoflurane anesthesia, while theta and alpha power decreased markedly. Beta power showed an increasing trend without reaching statistical significance, and gamma power remained unchanged. In the cortical EEG, only beta power exhibited a significant increase, while the trends in other bands were largely consistent with those observed in LFPs.

We next compared delta power between EEG and LH LFPs under baseline and anesthetized conditions (Fig. 6f, g), as this frequency range is closely associated with neuronal synchronization and arousal state22–24. The results showed that delta power in LH LFPs was significantly higher than that in cortical EEG under both wakeful and anesthetized conditions (Fig. 6f, p = 0.0156; Fig. 6g, p = 0.0156), a finding that is consistent with the modality-specific spectral differences observed during sevoflurane anesthesia (Fig. 6b), suggesting a higher degree of synchronization in the LH compared with the cortex.

We also compared the magnitude of power changes in cortical EEG and LH LFPs from wakefulness to anesthesia. Although the inter-band differences did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 6e), the EEG exhibited generally greater changes than LFPs. To further characterize the overall trend of spectral power, we analyzed the SEF95, which corresponds to the frequency encompassing 95% of the total power25. Consistent with the patterns observed in the PSD (Figs. 6a, b and S5c) and spectrograms (Fig. 6c), SEF95 values in both EEG and LH LFPs were significantly higher during sevoflurane anesthesia than during wakefulness (Fig. 6d; EEG: p = 0.0313; LH LFPs: p = 0.0313). This indicates that, although anesthesia is characterized by predominant low-frequency activity, it is associated with a moderate increase in high-frequency components.

Moreover, SEF95 in EEG was significantly higher than in LFPs, indicating a broader spectral distribution in the cortex, whereas LH activity was more confined to the low-frequency range. This difference aligns with the previous findings (Fig. 6e), suggesting that cortical EEG exhibited greater spectral changes during the transition from wakefulness to anesthesia, while LH maintained a higher level of low-frequency synchronization.

In summary, sevoflurane anesthesia induced low-frequency-dominated oscillatory activity in both the cortex and the lateral hypothalamus. However, the two regions displayed distinct response patterns: the cortex showed more pronounced spectral modulation, particularly enhanced activity in higher-frequency ranges, whereas the lateral hypothalamus maintained a relatively stable low-frequency synchronized rhythm.

Sevoflurane anesthesia increases low-frequency correlation between LH LFPs and cortical EEG

Given the prominent low-frequency oscillatory activity observed in both the LH and the cortex during sevoflurane anesthesia, we next examined whether these signals exhibited coordinated changes across brain regions by assessing the correlation between LH LFPs and cortical EEG activity. Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were first calculated between LH LFPs and EEG band power within each behavioral stage, and group-level comparisons were performed on Fisher z-transformed correlation values, as defined in the “Materials and methods”.

As shown in Fig. 7a–c, LH–EEG correlations in the slow-delta, delta, and theta bands exhibited significant state-dependent modulation. Specifically, for all three bands, correlation coefficients were significantly higher during sevoflurane anesthesia than during wakefulness (slow-delta, p = 0.048; delta, p = 0.0040; theta, p = 0.0226) and emergence (slow-delta, p = 0.0040; delta, p = 0.048; theta, p = 0.00985). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were detected between the wakefulness and emergence stages.

Taken together, our results indicate that sevoflurane anesthesia is accompanied by a reversible enhancement of low-frequency functional connectivity between LH LFPs and cortical EEG activity.

Discussion

The neural mechanisms by which general anesthetics give rise to loss of consciousness (LOC) remain incompletely defined. An increasing body of work indicates that sleep–wake regulation and anesthetic-induced LOC may share portions of their neural circuitry and key nuclei3,4,26. For example, general anesthetics are thought to induce LOC by suppressing arousal-promoting systems or activating sleep-promoting pathways27–29. Among these, the LH has been extensively validated as a critical center for maintaining wakefulness30,31 and has been implicated in consciousness control under multiple anesthetics32,33. For example, isoflurane has been shown to suppress orexin neuron activity within the LH, thereby reducing arousal levels34. However, direct evidence with high spatiotemporal resolution describing LH electrophysiology during the anesthesia–emergence transition is still lacking. To bridge this gap, we established a multi-scale electrophysiological recording framework tailored for deep-brain nuclei, enabling simultaneous assessment of LH neuronal firing and cortical dynamics across anesthesia and emergence. Building on this framework, we tracked LH activity together with cortical EEG throughout the anesthesia-induced state transition, thereby delineating the cross-scale impact of sevoflurane on deep nuclei and cortical networks.

We employed a 16-channel microelectrode array designed for deep-LH targeting and anatomically constrained sampling. High-density commercial probes are widely available; however, our probe geometry and site distribution were tailored to support consistent LH targeting and to maintain a confined sampling footprint during the sevoflurane anesthesia–emergence paradigm. Together with the low-impedance PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS interface, this design enabled stable single-unit and LFP recordings across baseline, anesthesia, and recovery. We acknowledge that the current channel count limits fine-scale spatial coverage, and future iterations may increase site density and channel count while preserving targeting specificity for the intended brain regions.

Our EIS results showed a progressive decrease in phase angle after surface modification (Fig. S2d–f), from 64.25° for bare sites to 51.18° after PtNPs deposition and further to 23.03° following PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS composite modification, together with a marked reduction in impedance magnitude. The phase angle reflects the balance between capacitive polarization and resistive as well as charge-transfer contributions at the electrode–electrolyte interface; therefore, the observed decrease is consistent with a less polarization-dominated response and increased resistive and charge-transfer contributions within the measured frequency range35. This trend is consistent with the increased electrochemically accessible surface area introduced by PtNPs and the mixed ionic–electronic transport provided by PEDOT:PSS, which may facilitate interfacial charge exchange and charge compensation. Similar reductions in phase angle after PEDOT-based coatings have been reported for neural microelectrodes36.

In prior studies, spike waveform parameters such as P/V, HVW, amplitude, and TTP are commonly used at rest to distinguish neocortical regular-spiking (RS) from fast-spiking (FS) neurons37–41. In deep nuclei, however, waveform features are often more diverse and complex42,43. In the present work, we quantitatively compared LH neurons using the above waveform metrics and found that suppressed neurons differed significantly from other types in measures including amplitude and P/V, suggesting relatively specific electrophysiological signatures. By contrast, we were unable to separate activated from neutral neurons based on waveform features, which may reflect the small sample size (n = 5). Overall, our results provide an initial reference and direction for using waveform parameters to aid in inferring neuronal classes within deep nuclei.

Previous work using a custom four-microwire microelectrode array reported that, under sevoflurane anesthesia, action-potential amplitude is markedly reduced in regular-spiking (RS) medial prefrontal cortical pyramidal neurons (mPFC)44. Similarly, during the anesthesia–emergence transition, we observed that, in a subset of neurons, spike amplitude covaried with firing rate. The mechanisms by which anesthetics influence action-potential waveforms remain unclear; potential contributors include changes in membrane ion-channel activity and differential modulation of GABAergic or glutamatergic transmitter systems45,46. Future studies combining ion-channel–specific blockade with transmitter-targeted manipulations may help delineate how different neuronal types respond to anesthetics at both the electrophysiological and molecular levels.

Delta oscillations are generally considered a signature of population synchrony and are closely linked to reduced consciousness during sleep and anesthesia22–24. In this study, we observed that sevoflurane anesthesia significantly increased delta power in both EEG and LH LFPs, with delta power consistently higher in LH LFPs than in EEG. Because LFPs reflect local population-level synchronous activity47,48, these results suggest that local LH networks may exhibit synchrony exceeding the cortical average during slow-wave oscillations. In other words, the LH may not act merely as a simple arousal “switch” 49, but rather as a downstream subcortical hub participating in the generation and regulation of slow waves, helping to stabilize global low-frequency rhythms under anesthesia. This provides a new perspective on the low-frequency dominance induced by anesthesia.

The observed enhancement of low-frequency coupling between the lateral hypothalamus and the cortex during sevoflurane anesthesia suggests that anesthetic-induced unconsciousness involves not only regional changes in neural activity, but also altered coordination across subcortical–cortical networks. Given the established association between low-frequency oscillations and large-scale neuronal synchronization, strengthened LH–EEG coupling may reflect increased integration of arousal-related subcortical nuclei into global slow-wave dynamics under anesthesia. In this context, the LH may contribute to stabilizing anesthetic-induced brain states by participating in the coordination of widespread low-frequency activity rather than functioning solely as a passive target of cortical suppression.

This study has several limitations. First, we characterized LH electrophysiology only under sevoflurane; without comparisons to other anesthetics (e.g., isoflurane or the intravenous agent propofol), we cannot determine whether the observed neurodynamic features are drug-specific. In addition, our sevoflurane paradigm used a fixed target concentration and stage-level comparisons across awake baseline, anesthesia, and recovery as the primary analytical framework; therefore, we did not systematically examine how LH neuronal dynamics vary across finer phases within anesthesia around behavioral state transitions such as loss and recovery of the righting reflex, nor did we compare neuronal dynamics across graded anesthetic concentrations. Second, we could not assign the recorded firing-pattern classes to specific molecular or neurotransmitter phenotypes. Future work should combine chemogenetic or optogenetic approaches to selectively manipulate distinct LH neuronal types to elucidate sevoflurane’s cell-type-specific actions, and—building on this—implement multi-region simultaneous recordings to map the dynamic interaction networks between the LH and other key brain areas.

In summary, using custom high-resolution multi-channel MEAs and concurrent cortical EEG, we systematically characterized LH neuronal dynamics across the awake–anesthetized–emergent sequence at multiple levels (LFPs, spikes, and waveform features). These findings provide new experimental evidence and conceptual avenues for understanding the LH’s role in regulating brain states during anesthesia. From a translational perspective, because clinical anesthesia depth monitoring relies largely on cortical EEG signatures, our multi-scale observations may provide complementary circuit-level context for interpreting established EEG depth markers. This framework may also be informative for future studies of emergence dynamics, including delayed emergence.

Conclusion

This study used custom multi-channel MEAs to perform multimodal electrophysiological recordings of neuronal activity in the mouse LH, systematically revealing the dynamic characteristics of neurons and brain networks during the sevoflurane anesthesia–emergence transition. Electrode performance tests showed that PtNPs/PEDOT:PSS modification significantly reduced impedance and improved the SNR, thereby enabling stable and reliable in vivo neural signals. At the single-neuron level, LH neurons exhibited type-specific firing changes: most neurons were markedly suppressed during anesthesia, a minority were activated, and in some neurons the spike amplitude showed dynamic changes with firing rate under sevoflurane, whereas another subset remained neutral. At the population level, sevoflurane anesthesia caused both cortical EEG and LH LFPs to show increased SEF95 but low-frequency–dominated oscillatory features; among these, the proportion of delta power was higher in the LH, whereas EEG displayed larger differences across the wake–anesthesia transition, suggesting that the LH maintains stable slow-wave rhythms under anesthesia. Beyond changes in local oscillatory power, sevoflurane anesthesia was associated with a reversible enhancement of low-frequency functional coupling between the LH and the cortex, indicating increased interregional coordination during the anesthetized state. In sum, based on MEA technology, this study systematically delineated the multi-scale electrophysiological characteristics of the LH during the anesthesia–emergence transition.
