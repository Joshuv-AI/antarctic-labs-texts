Injectable platelet-rich fibrin (iPRF) and postoperative outcomes in mandibular impacted third molar surgery: a split-mouth randomized controlled trial
Authors: Alanur Çiftçi Şişman, Zeliha Betül Özsağir, Mert Öke, Aleyna Duru, İbrahim Mollaalioğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimTo compare phase-specific soft tissue healing (STH) and periodontal changes after mandibular impacted third molar (MITM) extraction with and without iPRF.Materials and methodsThis single-blind, single-center, split-mouth, randomized controlled trial included patients with bilateral MITMs. Extraction sites were randomly assigned using simple randomization to either the iPRF group (test; irrigation with iPRF) or the control group (irrigation with saline). The primary outcome was STH, using Inflammatory Proliferative Remodeling (IPR) scale on days 3 and 14 and at week 6. Secondary outcomes included complete wound epithelialization (CWE), (days 7 and 14); postoperative average pain (PAP) during the first 3 days; and periodontal parameters—gingival recession (GR), probing pocket depth (PPD), and clinical attachment level (CAL)— measured at baseline and 6 weeks at the distal aspect of the second molar.ResultsA total of 32 patients (mean age: 25.09 ± 5.16 years) were included. iPRF group showed significantly higher STH scores in the inflammatory and proliferative phases and lower PAP. No significant intergroup differences were observed in CWE. Both groups showed significant reductions in PPD and significant increases in GR at 6 weeks, while CAL improved significantly only in iPRF group. Intergroup comparisons revealed significantly higher baseline PPD and CAL values in iPRF group; however, no differences were observed between groups at 6 weeks. Change analysis demonstrated a significantly greater CAL gain in iPRF group.ConclusionThe application of iPRF appears to enhance early STH, reduce PAP, and promote favorable changes in CAL following MITM extraction.Trial registrationNCT07257328, registered retrospectively on November 20, 2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08593-z.

Introduction

Impacted third molars (ITMs) are a common finding in adults, and surgical removal—particularly of mandibular impacted third molars (MITMs)—is among the most frequently performed procedures in oral and maxillofacial surgery (OMFS) [1]. Healing of the extraction socket is a critical outcome, as impaired healing may lead to complications and postoperative sequelae such as delayed recovery, persistent pain, infection, swelling, trismus, and local osteitis (dry socket) [1, 2]. In particular, MITMs are prone to unpredictable healing outcomes [3]. Therefore, minimizing these sequelae is essential for optimal postoperative recovery and overall surgical success [4, 5]. Given the need to improve patient well-being and advance dental practice, exploring different approaches to enhance the postoperative experience after tooth extraction has attracted increasing interest [6].

One such approach is the use of injectable platelet-rich fibrin (iPRF), which has recently gained attention in oral surgery, particularly after MITM extraction [2]. PRF was promoted by Dr. Joseph Choukroun as a platelet concentrate for application in dental extraction sockets to enhance wound healing (WH) and alleviate postoperative discomfort [7]. The beneficial effects of PRF are primarily attributed to its reservoir of growth factors, which support tissue repair, thereby promoting faster socket healing and reducing postoperative pain [8].

iPRF is a type of autogenous platelet concentrate developed using the low-speed centrifugation concept (LSCC) [9], designed to retain higher levels of immune cells, growth factors, and cytokines [10]. This bioactive product, typically obtained in liquid form, possesses regenerative properties [11, 12] and contributes to accelerated vascularization and enhanced WH [6]. It appears to enhance healing by promoting tissue repair and reducing inflammation, leading to faster recovery and greater patient satisfaction [13]. However, evidence regarding its effectiveness in reducing postoperative complications following third molar extractions remains inconsistent [2].

Given the potential of iPRF to modulate WH and the limited and conflicting evidence in the context of MITM extraction, further investigation is warranted. This study aimed to evaluate soft tissue healing (STH) after MITM extraction performed with and without iPRF application using the Inflammatory Proliferative Remodeling (IPR) scale, which assesses WH across its phases, and to investigate the effects of iPRF on changes in the adjacent periodontal tissues. The null hypothesis was that the application of iPRF to MITM extraction sockets would not improve postoperative outcomes, including STH, nor affect the adjacent periodontal tissues, compared with conventional saline irrigation.

Materials and methods

Study design

The study was designed as a single-blind, single-center, single-operator, parallel-group, split-mouth prospective randomized controlled clinical trial. The study was conducted in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the University of Health Sciences Hamidiye Clinical Research Ethics Committee (document number 22/104), and the trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov under the identifier NCT07257328 on November 20, 2025.

Sampling method and patient characteristics

The sample size estimation was based on visual analogue scale (VAS) pain scores reported in a previous study [11] and was performed using G*Power 3.1.9.2 software (University of Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, NRW, Germany). Assuming a significance level of α = 0.05, a power of 95% (1 − β = 0.95), and an anticipated effect size of 0.90, the required sample size was calculated as 29 sites per group using a two-tailed paired-samples t test. To account for a potential dropout rate of 10%, the final sample size was set at a minimum of 32 sites per group. The inclusion criteria comprised healthy (ASA I) adults aged over 18 years who required the removal of bilateral MITMs. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy or lactation; use of anticoagulant drugs, steroids, or oral contraceptives; bleeding disorders; autoimmune or immunoproliferative disorders; smoking; poor oral hygiene or lack of motivation; and the presence of acute inflammation or local pericoronal infection. Patients were enrolled regardless of socio-economic status, religion, caste, or sex. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study groups, randomization and blinding

The extraction sites were randomly allocated to two groups (test/iPRF group and control/saline group) using simple random sampling, with each patient’s right or left MITM assigned by coin toss. To ensure allocation concealment, the coin toss was performed immediately prior to surgery by a volunteer resident who was not involved in the surgical procedure or outcome assessment, preventing the operator from knowing the assignment in advance. In the control group (n = 32), extraction sockets were allowed to heal naturally following irrigation with 0.9% saline. In the test group (n = 32), sockets were irrigated with iPRF following routine surgical extraction of MITM. All patients followed a standard oral hygiene regimen prior to surgery to ensure an optimal environment for WH, including instruction on proper brushing and flossing techniques. All extractions were performed by the same operator. To minimize bias, the operator was not involved in outcome assessment, data analysis, or interpretation of the results. Investigators responsible for outcome measurements were not involved in the surgical procedures and were blinded to the treatment allocation of each tooth. Extraction procedure

All surgical procedures were performed under standardized conditions by the same experienced oral surgeon to ensure consistency. Prior to extraction, panoramic radiographs were evaluated, and MITM selection was standardized in accordance with a previous study [14]. To ensure comparability, surgical difficulty was standardized by confirming similarity between the right and left MITMs within each patient and across all patients. Only MITMs classified as Pell and Gregory Class I or II in relation to the mandibular ramus, with Level A or B depth and either mesial or vertical angulation, were included. As surgical difficulty can influence postoperative outcomes [15], teeth meeting the initial inclusion criteria were further assessed using the Pederson difficulty index [16]. This index assigns scores ranging from 3 to 10, categorizing surgical difficulty as minimal, moderate, or very difficult (Table 1). Only teeth classified as having moderate surgical difficulty (scores 5–6) were included. Teeth with root malformations, teeth located near mandibular or accessory canals (due to their neurovascular content, as described by Cuozzo et al., and teeth associated with pathological conditions such as dentigerous cysts were excluded from the study [17].

Pederson difficulty index

Local anaesthesia of the inferior alveolar, lingual, and buccal nerves was achieved using 4% articaine hydrochloride with epinephrine (1:100.000). Prior to the procedure, anaesthetic effectiveness was confirmed by assessing patient pain perception through gentle tapping of the tongue, lower lip, chin, and gingiva surrounding the relevant tooth. A modified full-thickness mucoperiosteal triangular flap was raised to access the tooth, as this design has been associated with reduced periodontal complications in adjacent teeth [18]. Following extraction, the socket was irrigated with either saline or iPRF, according to the assigned group. Primary mucosal closure was achieved in all patients using 3–0 polyglactin absorbable sutures. All patients received a standardized postoperative medication regimen consisting of amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (875/125 mg; Klamoks BID, Bilim Pharmaceuticals, Istanbul, Turkiye) at a dose of 1000 mg twice daily for 7 days and paracetamol (Parol, Atabay Inc., Istanbul, Turkiye) at 500 mg three times daily for up to 7 days for pain control. Patients with penicillin sensitivity were prescribed clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily for the same duration, in accordance with the current literature [19]. In addition, patients were instructed to rinse with 0.2% chlorhexidine mouthwash twice daily for 7 days. Adherence to medication and postoperative care was assessed during regular follow-up appointments.

iPRF protocol

For the test group, venous blood was collected from the antecubital vein of all patients into sterile, noncoated 9-mL plastic blood collection tubes (Vacuette, Greiner Bio-One GmbH, Kremsmünster, Austria) that did not contain anticoagulants or a gelling agent. The tubes were centrifuged using a benchtop centrifuge (Hettich, Tuttlingen, Germany) at 700 rpm for 3 min to obtain iPRF, following the manufacturer’s instructions (Fig. 2). Centrifugation separated the blood into two fractions: a superficial layer of injectable iPRF and a deeper layer of red blood cells. Since the amount of iPRF obtained may vary between patients [20], a minimum volume of 5 mL was collected for each application. The iPRF was aspirated from the upper layer of each tube using a sterile 5-mL syringe fitted with a 21-gauge needle, and used to gently rinse the extraction socket by allowing excess iPRF to overflow. To ensure maximum preservation of bioactive components, the time from the end of centrifugation to application was kept under 2 min. After application, iPRF was left in situ within the socket, and a 10-minute waiting period was observed to allow it to polymerize into a gel. The protocol was implemented based on previous studies [2, 21, 22].

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was STH. Secondary outcome measurements included complete wound epithelization (CWE), postoperative average pain (PAP) and periodontal measurements including gingival recession (GR), probing pocket depth (PPD) and clinical attachment level (CAL) at the distal aspect of the adjacent second molar. Two independent investigators, who were not involved in the surgical procedures or randomization and were blinded to group allocation, performed all outcome measurements. To ensure consistency of the findings, interrater and intrarater agreement between the two investigators was assessed according to the type of outcome measure. For ordinal healing scores (phase-specific IPR scores and total IPR scores), weighted Cohen’s kappa (κw) was used to evaluate both intra- and interrater agreement, a common method for 2 raters [23]. Kappa values were interpreted as follows: values ≤ 0 indicated no agreement; 0.01–0.20 indicated none to slight agreement; 0.21–0.40 fair agreement; 0.41–0.60 moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80 substantial agreement; and 0.81–1.00 almost perfect agreement [24]. For continuous variables, including GR, PPD and CAL measured in mm, intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model with absolute agreement (ICC(3,1)) [25]. The ICC values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater reliability. The 95% confidence interval (CI) of the ICC estimate was used to interpret reliability according to the following guidelines: values < 0.50 indicate poor reliability, 0.50–0.75 moderate reliability, 0.75–0.90 good reliability, and > 0.90 excellent reliability. Reliability analyses were performed separately for each follow-up time point (postoperative day 3, day 14, and week 6).

Evaluation of postextraction soft tissue healing (STH)

Inflammatory Proliferative Remodeling (IPR) scale was used to evaluate STH. The IPR scale is designed to represent the biological progression of WH by dividing the process into 3 subscales corresponding to the inflammatory, proliferative, and remodeling phases [26]. The individual phase scores and the overall IPR score provide a structured evaluation of healing responses specific to each phase [19]. In this study, follow-up visits were scheduled in alignment with IPR scale to assess healing during its respective phases. The inflammatory phase (IPR 1) was assessed 3 days post-surgery based on eight parameters: bleeding, granulation tissue, hematoma, tissue color, incision margins, edema, pain, and suppuration. A score of 5–8 indicated successful inflammatory phase. The proliferative phase (IPR 2) was assessed 14 days post-surgery using five parameters: re-epithelization, tissue color, scar formation, pain, and suppuration. A score of 3–5 indicated successful healing. The remodeling phase (IPR 3) was assessed 6 weeks post-surgery based on three parameters: scar formation, tissue color, and pain. A score of 2–3 indicated successful healing in this phase. The total score (IPR total) ranges from 0 to 16, with 0–4 indicating poor healing, 5–10 acceptable healing, and 11–16 excellent healing (Table 2).

Evaluation of postoperative average pain (PAP)

Patients were asked to rate their average pain (PAP) over the first 3 postoperative days using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS), ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain); this single composite score was used for comparative analysis.

Evaluation of complete wound epithelization (CWE)

H₂O₂ bubbling test was used to evaluate CWE clinically. The test is based on the principle that if the epithelium is discontinuous, H₂O₂ will diffuse into the connective tissue and catalase will act on the H₂O₂ to release water and oxygen, producing bubbles in the wound [11]. To evaluate CWE, the area was dried and 3% H₂O₂ was applied to the wound using a syringe at postoperative days 7 and 14 (Fig. 3). The appearance of bubbles suggests that the surgical site is not completely epithelialized. If there is no bubble, it is assumed that CWE has occurred.

Evaluation of periodontal measurements

Preoperatively (baseline) and at 6 weeks postoperatively, GR, and CAL were assessed at the distal aspect of the second molar adjacent to the extraction site. GR was quantified as the change in the distance from the cementoenamel junction (CEJ) to the free gingival margin at the distobuccal site of the second molar. PPD was quantified as the distance from the gingival margin to the base of the periodontal pocket at the distal aspect of the adjacent second molar and measured using a periodontal probe. CAL was quantified as the distance from CEJ to the base of the periodontal pocket. Comparisons were made by calculating the difference between preoperative and postoperative values in the control and iPRF groups.

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as the frequency, ratio, mean, standard deviation, minimum and maximum values. Group comparisons were performed using the paired-samples t-test for normally distributed variables. Independent t-test was used to evaluate changes in the variables over time within the study groups. To determine correlations between variables, Pearson and Spearman rank correlation coefficients were used. The data were statistically analyzed using SPSS v.25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) with a significance level of 5%.

Results

A total of 40 patients were recruited for the study. Six did not meet the eligibility criteria and 34 were selected for the intervention. One dropped out owing to withdrawal consent and 1 was excluded owing to lost follow-up. No cases of local osteitis were observed in any of the patients. The final sample was composed of 32 patients with a mean age of 25.09 [± 5.16] years. Table 3 presents basic characteristics of the study sample.

Interrater and intrarater reliability analyses demonstrated good agreement for the assessment of scores at all follow-up time points. Interrater agreement for phase-specific and total healing scores was high on postoperative day 3 (IPR1, κw = 0.83), day 14 (IPR2, κw = 0.82), week 6 (IPR3, κw = 0.83), and total score (IPR total, κw = 0.81). For continuous variables, including GR, PPD and CAL, interrater agreement was found good, with ICC of 0.82 for GR, 0.87 for PPD and 0.84 for CAL. Intrarater reliability for these measurements demonstrated high consistency, with ICC values of 0.92 and 0.94 for the two raters, respectively. Overall, these findings indicate high measurement reliability for both ordinal healing scores and continuous periodontal parameters across all follow-up assessments.

Total IPR score ranged from 10 to 14 ± 0.99 in the control group and 11–15 ± 1.30 in iPRF group. Statistically significant differences were observed between control and iPRF groups for IPR-1, IPR-2, and the total IPR score, with higher scores observed in the iPRF group. No significant difference was detected between the groups in IPR Phase 3, reflecting the remodeling stage. PAP scores ranged from 6 to 9 in both groups. Mean PAP scores were 7.15 ± 1.00 in the control group and 5.82 ± 0.98 in the iPRF group. A statistically significant difference in PAP scores was observed between two groups, with lower pain scores in iPRF group (Table 4).

Comparison of various parameters by groups

In the H₂O₂ test conducted on postoperative day 7, CWE was detected in 69% (n = 22) of patients in the control group, increasing to 78% (n = 25) on day 14. In iPRF group, CWE was present in 72% (n = 23) on day 7 and 97% (n = 31) on day 14. No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups regarding CWE at either time point (McNemar test, p = 1.00 and p = 0.180, respectively).

Periodontal parameters were evaluated at baseline and at the 6-week follow-up in both groups. At baseline, PPD and CAL values ranged from 2 to 8 mm in both groups, while GR was absent (0 mm) in all cases. At 6 weeks, PPD values ranged from 2 to 7 mm in both groups, GR ranged from 0 to 2 mm, and CAL values ranged from 2 to 8 mm in the control group and from 2 to 7 mm in the iPRF group.

In intragroup comparisons, PPD showed a statistically significant reduction from baseline to 6 weeks in both groups (p = 0.005 and p = 0.001, respectively). GR also showed a statistically significant change in both the control and iPRF groups (p = 0.001 and p = 0.006, respectively). For CAL, no statistically significant difference was observed in the control group (p = 0.160); however, the iPRF group exhibited a significant improvement (p = 0.001). Discussion

Within the limitations of this study, irrigating the sockets with iPRF was found to positively affected the inflammatory and proliferative phases of STH following MITM extraction, reduced PAP and improved CAL at the distal aspect of the second molar, thereby refuting the null hypothesis.

Various types of platelet-rich fibrin (PRF) have been investigated in several studies for their potential to improve WH following tooth extraction. In their randomized controlled trial (RCT), Ustaoğlu et al. evaluated STH and CWE following tooth extraction in test groups treated with leukocyte-rich PRF (LPRF) or titanium-prepared PRF (TPRF), as well as in a control group (n = 19) without PRF application [11] Both test groups demonstrated 100% CWE at 2 weeks postoperatively, a rate comparable to that observed in the present study (97%). In contrast to our findings, they did not identify statistically significant differences in STH when healing was assessed using the Landry WH Index (LWHI). Similarly, Pereira et al. conducted a split-mouth RCT (n = 16) and reported that advanced platelet-rich fibrin (APRF⁺) had no additional effect on the healing of post-extraction sockets of maxillary third molars when healing was assessed clinically using a VAS [27]. Gatti et al. evaluated LPRF in the context of post-operative wound outcomes at palatal donor sites after free gingival graft harvesting, and reported that LPRF did not produce statistically significant differences in postoperative pain or overall discomfort [28]. Akpınar et al. investigated the effect of iPRF application on postoperative outcomes in third molar surgery in a split-mouth RCT (n = 35) and reported that iPRF had a limited effect on postoperative pain but was effective in reducing postoperative edema [29]. Conversely, Aliberti et al. reported in their RCT (n = 28) investigating recovery after MITM extraction, that the iPRF group demonstrated superior early postoperative outcomes, including reduced postoperative pain and enhanced WH [2]. Consistantly, the present study demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in healing in favor of the iPRF group during the inflammatory and proliferative phases, extending up to 14 days postoperatively, whereas no significant difference was observed between the groups at the 6-week follow-up. This effect may be explained by the capacity of iPRF to promote early healing via growth factor release [30], as well as by the application of the IPR scale for phase-specific evaluation.

Postoperative pain is one of the most common complications following third molar extraction and has been reported to worsen during the first 72 h postoperatively [31]. Accordingly, in the present study, patients were asked to rate the overall pain they experienced during the first 3 postoperative days, as this period is expected to encompass peak pain intensity and allows assessment within a timeframe that minimizes potential bias related to patient self-reporting and recall. In line with findings reported in the literature [2, 11], postoperative pain was found significantly lower in the iPRF group compared with the control group in the present study.

A wide range of indications and contraindications for ITM extraction has been described in the literature [32], including the prevention or management of periodontal defects affecting the adjacent second molar [33]. Over the past four decades, numerous studies have explored the relationship between ITMs and periodontal health, as well as the periodontal outcomes following their removal [34].

Studies have demonstrated an increased risk of periodontal deterioration in fully erupted second molars positioned adjacent to ITMs [35]. Consequently, surgical removal of ITMs should be directed not only toward preserving periodontal integrity but also toward enhancing periodontal tissue regeneration at the distal aspect of the neighboring second molar [34]. In a split-mouth RCT involving 17 patients, Torumtay et al. compared the effects of saline and iPRF on deep periodontal pockets and reported significant improvements in PPD reduction and CAL gain in the iPRF-treated sites [36]. In the present study, as group allocation was randomized, baseline values were not identical between the groups. Notably, the iPRF group exhibited higher initial values in terms of PPD and CAL; despite this disadvantage, it demonstrated greater improvement over time, suggesting a potentially enhanced effect on healing. Consistent with previous findings, the present study also observed CAL gains in iPRF-treated sockets. The regenerative properties of iPRF, including its growth factors and cytokines, are thought to support accelerated healing and contribute to improved periodontal outcomes. Systemic antibiotics have been reported to reduce postoperative infection rates and may influence aspects of postoperative inflammatory responses [37–39]. In the present study, all patients received the same standardized postoperative antibiotic regimen, ensuring a uniform antimicrobial effect across all groups. Additionally, no cases of local osteitis or postoperative infection were observed. Therefore, it is unlikely that the antibiotic regimen influenced the differences observed between groups.

It is well established that WH is a multifactorial process [40]. The study sample consisted of systemically healthy individuals, selected to mitigate the influence of systemic healing factors on socket healing following tooth extraction. The mean age was 25.09 [± 5.16] years, reflecting both the typical demographic of and the generally healthy patient population undergoing MITM extraction in routine clinical practice [41, 42]. This distribution was comparable to that reported in other oral surgery cohorts [41, 43], supporting the real-world relevance of the findings. The role of age in healing outcomes remains controversial. Although some studies have identified age as an important determinant of healing outcomes [44], no association between age and STH was observed in the present study, in line with previous reports [19, 20]. Consistently, Phillips et al. reported that WH outcomes were not associated with age, even after controlling for the complexity of third molar surgery [45].

The study was designed as a prospective, controlled, split-mouth investigation with three follow-up assessments and multiple outcome measures to enhance the robustness of the findings. Split-mouth designs enable direct intra-patient comparisons, thereby reducing inter-individual variability. By using each patient as both the control and experimental subject, potential bias is minimized, strengthening the internal validity and reliability of the outcome assessments. Despite efforts to minimize confounding factors, the study has several limitations. First, it was conducted at a single center, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings with differing surgical techniques or patient populations. Additionally, while restricting the study sample to patients meeting strict inclusion criteria enhanced internal validity and helped control for confounding factors affecting wound healing, it may limit the generalizability of the results to the broader population undergoing third molar surgery. Although the sample size was sufficient and consistent with previous studies, it was influenced by the requirement for multiple control sessions related to phase-specific evaluation, strict inclusion criteria for standardization, and the potential for participant attrition. Second, several variables may influence postoperative outcomes. In various medical specialties, prolonged operative time (> 2 h) has been associated with an increased risk of surgical site infection [46]. In oral surgery, operation duration has been reported as a predictive factor for postoperative pain, trismus, and edema [47]. To the best of our knowledge, no studies have directly examined the effect of operation time on oral STH. Moreover, it is unlikely that a routine wisdom tooth extraction would require more than 2 h. As the primary outcome of the present study was STH, variables such as operation time and the number of analgesics consumed were not recorded. In addition, despite efforts to ensure methodological consistency through blinded evaluations, complete blinding was not feasible. Although the evaluators responsible for clinical measurements and the statistician remained blinded to procedural allocation, the surgeon and the patients could not be blinded due to the nature of the intervention. As a result, patients may have perceived enhanced care or anticipated improved outcomes on the side receiving the adjunctive intervention. Besides, pain is a subjective and recall-dependent outcome that was assessed through patient self-report, which may introduce reporting bias. Lastly, it has been reported that errors during periodontal probing may account for up to 30–50% of the final estimated value [48]. Despite the high interrater and intrarater agreement in this study, the measurement variability during probing cannot be entirely excluded.

Conclusion

Healing of the extraction socket after MITM removal is an important outcome in OMFS. Numerous approaches have been proposed to enhance socket healing and postoperative outcomes. Among these, iPRF represents a simple and biologically based adjunctive method. Within the limitations of the present study, the application of iPRF to extraction sockets appears to contribute to improved STH, reduced PAP, and beneficial effects on the periodontal tissues of the adjacent second molar. The findings of this study support the potential role of iPRF as a useful adjunct in routine clinical practice.
