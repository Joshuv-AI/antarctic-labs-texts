Evaluation of an ultraviolet C device for hospital pharmacy compounding
Authors: Amedio Tabbakh, Max Colombel, Isabelle Fredenucci, Pierre Cassier, Thomas Briot
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aseptic pharmaceutical compounding is a high-risk activity in hospital pharmacies requiring strict environmental control. Ultraviolet-C (UVC) irradiation offers a “non-touch” and chemical-free method of disinfection for materials entering in white rooms. The present study aimed to investigate the efficacy of a UVC disinfection device (Artemisia, Byola, Faulquemont, France) for inactivating common hospital pathogens in vitro. Stock solutions of Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus spizizenii, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Candida albicans were prepared and spread on Petri dishes. Plates were placed in the disinfection device and exposed to UVC for 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 min (2, 5, and 10 min for B. spizizenii). After UVC exposure, culture plates were incubated at 32 ± 2 °C and colony forming unit were quantified 24- and 120-hours post-exposure. Microorganisms were chosen as they have different cell wall compositions and are associated with healthcare-associated infections; B. spizizenii spores were included as a surrogate microorganism for pathogenic spore species and strains. For all microorganisms, after 2 min of exposure a reduction > 7 log10 was observed at 24- and 120-hours post-exposure. The Artemisia tested herein thus demonstrates potential to be used for sterilisation, at least against the microorganisms tested.

Introduction

Aseptic pharmaceutical compounding is a high-risk activity in hospital pharmacies that requires strict environmental control to ensure the microbiological safety of sterile preparations and, ultimately, patient safety. To minimize the risk of microbiological contamination, one key step in this process is the disinfection of incoming materials such as packaging, paper and labels before entering white rooms, to avoid contamination by common microorganisms. This is required by guidelines, such as the Good Manufacturing Practices (GMPs)1 or the French Good Compounding Practices for Hospital and Community Pharmacies (Bonnes Pratiques de Préparation, BPP)2, which define appropriate control and prevention measures for maintaining white room aseptic handling safety including cleaning, disinfecting and sterilising all potential sources of microbial contamination.

To address this critical need, various surface disinfection methods are available, such as the use of hydrogen peroxide and isopropyl alcohol3. Ultraviolet-C (UVC) irradiation offers as a “non-touch” and chemical-free alternative. While its efficacy has been recognised by health authorities such as the Center for Disease Control3, there are no universal guidelines concerning the application of UVC disinfection methods in hospital environments. UVC exerts its germicidal activity by disrupting the DNA or RNA of microorganisms by dimerization of the nucleic acid bases4. This prevents their replication and functionality, and has been demonstrated to be effective in eliminating common pathogens in hospitals, notably on fomites5–7. The disinfection efficacy of UVC depends primarily on the dose of radiation that is determined by its intensity and duration of exposure8. The Artemisia (FR3136173A19, is a prototype UVC disinfection device that was developed by Byola (Faulquemont, France). This device emits 254-nm UVC and delivers a dose of UVC that can be adjusted by modifying the duration of exposure time as the intensity constant. Its two-sided door design is particularly interesting to be used in units for aseptic compounding as it eases the transfer of material from one room to another. Validating the performance of this device is essential prior to it being implemented into routine aseptic compounding. Furthermore, establishing a time-efficacy relationship of this device needs to be explored, as a shorter duration of exposure can offer a significant time-saving advantage for routine implementation.

The present study therefore aimed to investigate the efficacy of the Artemisia disinfection device, and determine the optimal duration of 254-nm UVC exposure for inactivating common hospital pathogens in vitro.

Materials and methods

UVC device and setup

The Artemisia (Byola) is a disinfection device that is composed of front and rear doors to facilitate the transfer of materials. The disinfection device contains 12 UVC lamps that provide 360° coverage (Fig. 1). These lamps emit UVC radiation at 254 nm, with a total lamp power of 336 W (Table 1). On each shelf, the intensity is 4.39 mW/cm² (measured using a UVC light meter (UVC254 UVC light meter; Shenzhen Sanpo Instrument, Shenzhen, China). The disinfection device was kindly provided by Byola.

Assessment of the impact of UVC exposure on the growth-promoting capacity of Trypcase Soy Agar plates (applicability of the method)

To determine whether UVC irradiation could alter the growth promoting properties of Trypcase Soy Agar (TSA; BioMérieux, Marcy l’Étoile, France), microbial growth was compared between TSA plates pre-exposed to UVC (UVC-TSA) versus non-exposed-UVC TSA (C-TSA) plates. Calibrated beads (Bioball; BioMérieux) of Staphyloccocus aureus, Pseudonomas aeruginosa, Escherichia Coli, Bacillus spizizenii, and Candida alblicans containing 30 colony forming units (CFUs) were rehydrated using 100µL of purified water (Bio-Rad, Marnes-la-Coquette, France). The resulting suspensions were plated onto UVC-TSA (for 5 min; except for B. spizizenii that was exposed for 10 min), as well as C-TSA (n = 2 per condition and microorganism). After incubation at 32 ± 2 °C for 120 h, CFU were enumerated and growth ratios (CFU on UVC-TSA/CFU on C-TSA) were calculated to assess potential impairment of TSA growth capacity following UVC exposure.

When inoculation yielded inconclusive results for a given microorganism (e.g. absence of growth), an extended evaluation was performed using a MacFarland-adjusted stock suspension. Ten-fold serial dilutions were prepared (down to 103 CFU/mL), and 10µL of each dilution was plated onto both UVC-TSA and C-TSA plates (n = 2 per condition and microorganism). After incubation at 32 ± 2 °C for 120 h, CFU counts were compared to determine whether UVC pre-exposure affected the ability of TSA to support microbial growth across a range of inoculum concentrations.

Microorganisms and sample preparation

The microorganisms used were Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus [National Collection of Type Cultures - NCTC 10788], B. spizizenii [NCTC 10400]), Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli [NCTC 12923], P. aeruginosa [NCTC 12924]), and yeast (C. albicans [National Collection of Pathogenic Fungi - NCPF 3179]).

For S. aureus, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and C. albicans samples were prepared by using Bioball; that were rehydrated directly in a Trypcase Soy Broth (BioMérieux) for 24 h at 36 ± 2 °C. After rehydration bacterial cultures grown in the Trypcase Soy Broth were used to inoculate TSA plates and incubated for another 24 h at 36 ± 2 °C.

After growth, CFUs were collected using an inoculation loop and transferred into tubes of purified water to obtain stock solutions. Optical density was adjusted to a 2 McFarland standard for bacteria and 3 for C. albicans.

Immediately after preparation and adjustment to the predefined MacFarland standard, the same stock solution was used in parallel for inoculum verification and preparation onto plates intended for UVC exposure. Ten-fold serial dilutions were prepared from the stock suspension repeatedly, 5 times to achieve a final concentration of 103 CFU/mL for bacteria and 102 CFU/mL for C. albicans, corresponding to countable CFU ranges. Briefly, 100 µL of the final suspension was inoculated onto each of 3 TSA plate per microorganism and placed at 32 ± 2 °C for 24 h for visual CFU enumeration and determination of stock solutions inocula. In parallel, 0.5 mL of the same undiluted stock solution was spread on each of the 15 TSA plates destined for subsequent UVC irradiation.

For B. spizizenii, the samples were prepared by using a calibrated bead containing 108 CFU of sporulated organisms. To obtain the stock solution, the beads were rehydrated using the Re-Hydration Fluid according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Bioball; BioMérieux). A volume of 0.5 mL of the stock solution was then spread on 9 different TSA plates.

The selection of test microorganisms, incubation temperatures, and incubation times before enumeration were performed in accordance with the requirements of the European Pharmacopeia monograph 2.6.1210.

Microorganism exposure to UVC

Inoculated TSA plates were placed at the centre of the disinfection device and the doors were closed. Plates of P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, C. albicans, and E. coli, were exposed to UVC for 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 min (3 plates per duration); the B. spizizenii spores were exposed for 2, 5, and 10 min (3 plates per duration) as bacterial spores are described to be more UVC-resistant than vegetative cells11.

After UVC exposure, culture plates were incubated at 32 ± 2 °C and visually quantified 24- and 120-hours post-exposure. For each exposure duration, CFU count was enumerated visually on culture plates.

Results

Impact of UVC exposure on TSA growth capacity

In the direct evaluation using inocula standardised at 30 CFU, comparable growth was observed on UVC-TSA and C-TSA plates for P. aeruginosa, B. spizizenii, E. coli, and C. albicans. Growth ratios were 1.06 for P. aeruginosa, 0.86 for B. spizizenii, 0.85 for E. coli, and 1.00 for C. albicans, indicating no relevant impairment of the growth-supporting properties of TSA following UVC irradiation under the tested conditions (Table 2).

Effect of 254-nm UVC irradiation on bacterial cells and yeast

For all microorganisms tested, the initial concentration of stock solutions > 1.0 × 107 CFU/mL was achieved (Table 4). At both 24 (Fig. 2a) and 120 h post-exposure (Fig. 2b), UVC irradiation resulted in reductions >7log10 for all microorganisms and across all exposure durations. At 24 h, residual CFU of E. coli were detected after a 1-minute UVC exposure (Fig. 2a); at 120 h, residual CFU were detected for E. coli, C. albicans, and P. aeruginosa (Fig. 2b).

Discussion

The present study demonstrates the germicidal efficacy of the novel 254-nm UVC irradiation device, Artemisia, against several bacteria (vegetative cells and spores) as well as C. albicans as this obtained systematically >7log10 reduction for all the tested microorganisms. Growth promotion testing also confirmed that UVC pre-exposure did not impair the ability of TSA to support microbial growth. Since sterility is the absence of viable microorganisms, as defined by a sterility assurance level equal to or less than 10− 612, the Artemisia device tested herein demonstrates potential to be used for sterilisation, at least against the microorganisms tested and after 2 min UVC exposure. Moreover, the Log reduction on tested strains are in accordance with the definition of UVC efficacy as described in the British Standard BS8628:2022, which state that reduction rates must be ≥5log10to validate bacterial activity, ≥ 4 log10to validate sporicidal activity and ≥4log10 to validate yeasticidal activity under the conditions specified in this norm13.

The microorganisms used herein were chosen as they have different cell wall compositions and are associated with healthcare-associated infections14; B. spizizenii spores were included as a surrogate microorganism for pathogenic spore species and strains. In the operative conditions tested, the device was able to reduce the bioburden by >7log10 after 1 min (0.263 J/cm²) for S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, E. coli, and > 6 log10 for C. albicans; for B. spizizenii spores the bioburden was reduced >7log10 after 2 min (0.526 J/cm²). A lower reduction was observed for C. albicans compared with vegetative bacteria. For yeast, multiple protective factors have been identified, beyond cell wall structure alone, including larger cell size, presence of a nuclear membrane, and other repair mechanisms that are not present in bacteria15. As B. spizizenii spores are known to be 10- to 50 fold more resistant than are growing cells to UV radiation at 254-nm, it is reasonable to expect similar or greater efficacy against the other microorganisms of interest when using the same device16.

Other UVC devices designed for surface disinfection, which could thus be used for the disinfection of medical material, have been evaluated. For example, Tamires dos Santos et al.. evaluated the Biosept Home device (Bioset, Rio Claro, Brazil), a portable UVC device designed to disinfect surfaces6. As does the Artemisia, it operates at a wavelength of 254 nm, however, the Artemisia device is designed as a closed unit whereas the Biosept Home is a portable, hand-held, device used to irradiate the desired surfaces; operators are therefore at risk of exposure to UVC light, known to cause harm to mammalian epidermal cells17. Concerning the efficacy evaluation protocol, in the study testing the Biosept Home, culture plates were exposed to UVC for 20 s at a distance of 1 cm, delivering a light dose of 0.912 J/cm²6. Under these conditions, no bacterial growth was detected for E. coli, P. aeruginosa, S. typhimurium, S. aureus and S. epidermidis, confirming the device’s efficacy in reducing bacterial bioburden. However, for C. albicans, colonies persisted at 10² CFU/mL post-exposure, corresponding to a 4log10 reduction under experimental conditions. A second portable hand-held, UVC device (MMOptics, Sao Carlos, Brazil) was evaluated by Quatrini Corrêa et al.18. It also operates at 254 nm and a light dose of 0.78 J/cm2 was applied to Petri dishes containing S. aureus, S. mutans, S. pneumoniae, E. coli, P. aeruginosa, and C. albicans. A >6log10 reduction for S. aureus, S. mutans and S. pneumoniae was obtained. However, for S. pneumonaie, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa the reduction of initial bioburden never exceeded the >6log10 reduction. The differences in efficacy between these devices can be attributed to variation in UVC exposure conditions. The Artemisia device provides 360° UVC exposure, eliminating issues such as shadowing and lamp orientation, which can otherwise reduce sterilisation efficacy19. Moreover, unlike hand-held devices, the Artemisia device ensures standardised exposure time, minimising the risk of random errors, as human intervention is a major risk for incomplete disinfection20. However, in the present study microorganisms were tested under favourable clean conditions, on plates, which differs from settings where material could be soiled. UVC efficacy is inherently surface-dependant, as organic matter may create shadowed areas that limit irradiation21. Consequently, UVC disinfection should be applied to pre-cleaned materials to ensure optimal performance22.

The present study has, however, some limitations. First, bioburden reduction was assessed exclusively using in vitro microorganism cultures and with a limited number of samples. Nonetheless, given that culture plates provide an ideal growth medium for microorganisms, the results are expected to be similar, if not superior, on routine decontamination surfaces. Second, the effects of UVC on viruses were not investigated; however, a study reported by Narita et al. found that UVC irradiation at 222 nm and 254 nm effectively inhibited viral activity for influenza A virus. In their study, a 5 mW/cm2 222 nm UVC lamp at 10 mm from the emission window and a 3mW/cm2 254 nm lamp at 20 mm from the window were generated by using the SafeZoneUVC device (Ushio Inc. Tokyo, Japan)23. Influenza A virus was inactivated by a dose of 6 mJ/cm² of UVC at 254-nm, which is largely inferior to the dose delivered by the Artemisia device. Finally, further research is also needed to evaluate UVC time-efficacy on various materials, such as labels and packaging, to ensure their sterility after exposure.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that the 254-nm UVC device Artemisia achieves a high-level bioburden reduction under tested conditions. A reduction >7log10 after 2 min of exposure to UVC for all microorganisms tested in this study with the Artemisia was obtained, which suggests that following adequate exposure time, the device can reach sterilisation for the tested microorganisms. When applied to pre-cleaned material, as is routinely practice in pharmacy compounding settings, the Artemisia is a promising standardised and operator-safe device for surface decontamination. However, further explorations should be conducted to evaluate its effectiveness across a broader range of materials and microorganisms.
