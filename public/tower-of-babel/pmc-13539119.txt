Voices for Change: Stakeholder Perspectives on Childhood Diagnostic Delays in Western Australia
Authors: Shane Grundy, Declan R. Walter, Lucy M. Macloughlin, Melinda Bridges, Frank D. Baughman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundTimely access to developmental assessment services for children is essential, yet diagnostic delays persist even in nations with advanced healthcare systems. In Western Australia (WA), a recent parliamentary inquiry revealed over 17 000 children awaiting developmental assessments, with median wait times for specialist appointments reaching 15.1 months.MethodUsing a qualitative design underpinned by a social constructionist epistemology, this study examined the perspectives of parents (n = 17), educators (n = 10) and healthcare professionals (n = 9) in WA through open‐ended survey questions analysed via mixed content analysis. We aimed to identify commonalities in stakeholder experiences with diagnostic delays and potential solutions.ResultsStakeholders consistently identified insufficient funding (22 instances), extended wait times (44 instances) and lack of support (110 instances) as primary concerns. Parents reported significant stress navigating the system and frustration with ‘wait and see’ approaches; educators highlighted inadequate staffing and challenges supporting undiagnosed students; and healthcare professionals emphasised the need for more efficient referral processes and increased specialist availability.ConclusionFindings underscore the need for increased funding and workforce incentives, improved inter‐stakeholder communication and referral processes and clearer diagnostic pathway information. These cross‐sector perspectives offer actionable recommendations for policy reform aimed at reducing diagnostic delay for children in WA.

The prevalence of developmental challenges among children is substantial and represents a significant public health concern both globally and within Australia. In the United States, approximately 17.8% of children aged 3–17 years have received a developmental disability diagnosis (Zablotsky et al. 2019), while in Australia, 7.4% of children under 14 years of age have a disability, with 3.7% experiencing severe or profound core activity limitations (Australian Bureau of Statistics 2013). Autism spectrum disorder affects approximately 1 in 70 Australians (Aspect 2018), and 13.9% of 4‐ to 17‐year‐olds experience mental health problems within a 12‐month period (Lawrence et al. 2015), a figure consistent with global estimates of 13.4% (Polanczyk et al. 2015). The Australian Early Development Census (2021) further reported that 21.7% of children were developmentally vulnerable in one or more domains. Early intervention programmes are founded on the understanding that the first years of life represent a critical window for development across cognitive, social, physical and emotional domains (Estes et al. 2015). Consequently, public health and educational systems prioritise the timely identification and support of children with developmental challenges. However, mounting evidence suggests these systems are struggling to deliver timely diagnoses and assessments.

Diagnostic delay (DD)—the interval between initial parental concerns about child development and formal assessment or diagnosis—is becoming an urgent issue within Australia and worldwide, carrying significant implications for children, families, educators and healthcare professionals. In Western Australia (WA) alone, recent reports revealed over 17 000 children awaiting developmental assessments, with median wait times for specialist appointments reaching 15.1 months (McNeill 2022). The situation is estimated to be even more severe in regional and remote areas, where wait times can exceed 2 years. These extended delays are particularly concerning given the growing recognition of early intervention's importance and the potential negative outcomes of delayed intervention (McGorry and Mei 2018).

The impact of DD extends across multiple stakeholders, with each experiencing distinct challenges. Children awaiting assessment risk missing critical windows for early intervention, potentially compromising their developmental trajectory and long‐term well‐being (Estes et al. 2015; Oredipe et al. 2023). Parents and guardians frequently report significant psychological burden, experiencing stress, fatigue and frustration throughout the diagnostic process (Crane et al. 2016; Seymour et al. 2013). Within educational settings, teachers—particularly those in special education—encounter increased challenges in supporting children with undiagnosed developmental needs (Hinds et al. 2015). Healthcare providers, while central to assessment and treatment, often struggle with early identification and appropriate screening protocols (Garg et al. 2018; Zuckerman et al. 2015).

While research examining factors contributing to DD across broad developmental domains remains limited, condition‐specific studies offer relevant insights. For example, Augusto et al. (2025) found an average interval of 6.6 years for autism and 6.8 years for intellectual disability between parents' first concerns and formal diagnosis, attributing this delay largely to limited early‐identification capacity among primary‐care professionals. In the context of cancer, Renzi et al. (2023) identified systemic and socioeconomic factors that contributed to DD. These factors included regional disparities in screening participation and slower diagnostic pathways among older patients and those with mental health conditions. Consistent with these findings, barriers commonly reported across the broader literature include resource limitations, insufficient healthcare provider expertise, reluctance to diagnose early and persistent stigma surrounding developmental disorders (Mitchell and Holdt 2014; Zuckerman et al. 2014). Conversely, facilitating factors encompass higher socioeconomic status, family history of developmental disabilities and robust community support networks (Marshall et al. 2020; Sapiets et al. 2021).

The current study examines the experiences of key stakeholder groups affected by DD in WA, specifically focusing on parents, educators and healthcare professionals. Through examining their perspectives, we aim to identify both contributing factors to DD and potential mitigation strategies. The primary research question is: ‘What are the perceived contributors and alleviators to DD that are common among health professionals, educators and parents concerned about the development of their child/ren?’ By synthesising stakeholder perspectives, we aim to generate insights that may inform policy development and enhance the diagnostic journey for children and families.

Methods

Design and Measures

We employed a qualitative methodology with a social constructionist epistemological approach. This approach was selected for its capacity to examine participants' personal experiences and their interpretation of the phenomenon under study (Willig 2013), recognising that stakeholders' understanding of DD is shaped by their social and professional contexts. Data were collected via Qualtrics, an online survey platform, using open‐ended, short‐response questions. Three stakeholder‐specific questionnaires were developed for parents/guardians, educators and health professionals, respectively, each containing nine open‐ended questions adapted to the respondent's role (see Table 1). The reporting of this study was guided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist (Tong et al. 2007; see Supporting Information S1) to ensure transparent reporting.

Participants

An initial 47 participants commenced the survey. Eleven of these completed only the demographic items and did not provide responses to the open‐ended questions and were therefore not included in the analysis. Participants were recruited through social media advertisements (Facebook parenting and education groups), flyers distributed to childcare centres and local health clinics in the Perth metropolitan area, and via snowball sampling whereby initial participants shared the study with eligible contacts. Eligible participants were adults (aged 18+) residing in WA who were either parents/guardians of a child with developmental concerns, educators working with such children, or healthcare professionals involved in developmental assessment.

Procedure

Following university ethical approval (HRE2023‐0399), participant recruitment and data collection commenced through social media platform advertisements and community distribution of study flyers. Potential participants self‐selected by following a link to the Qualtrics survey. Upon accessing the survey, participants reviewed the project information sheet detailing the study's purpose, procedures, risks, how their data would be used and stored, that no financial incentive was available and their rights (including the right to withdraw at any time without consequence). Participants provided informed consent electronically before completing one of three stakeholder‐specific questionnaires. The average completion time was approximately 20 min. Upon completion, participants received a debriefing page that reiterated information regarding data usage, restated the research objectives, provided relevant support services and acknowledged their contribution to the research.

Analysis

Participant responses were exported from Qualtrics and imported into NVivo 12 (QSR International) for analysis, with all identifying information removed prior to coding (Schreier 2012). We conducted a mixed content analysis combining summative and conventional approaches. The summative content analysis involved systematic word frequency counts across the dataset to identify prominent terms and quantify their prevalence, providing an initial mapping of key concerns. The conventional content analysis involved an inductive, data‐driven coding process in which categories emerged directly from participant responses rather than from pre‐existing theoretical frameworks (Hsieh and Shannon 2005). This dual approach was selected because the summative analysis provided a quantitative overview of the most salient issues, while the conventional analysis enabled a richer, contextualised understanding of stakeholders' experiences and recommendations.

The analysis proceeded through three primary stages. First, during data immersion, two members of the research team (S.G. and D.R.W.) independently read and re‐read all responses to develop familiarity with the data. Second, during initial coding, both researchers independently generated preliminary codes by highlighting meaningful segments of text and assigning descriptive labels. In the first instance, both researchers independently coded an initial subset of questionnaires and then met to compare their coding frameworks, discuss discrepancies and agree to a unified coding scheme, which was subsequently applied across the remaining responses. Inter‐coder agreement was high, with disagreements resolved through discussion and reference to the original data. Third, during theme development, the agreed codes were grouped into broader thematic categories through an iterative process of comparison and refinement. Themes were reviewed to ensure they were internally coherent and externally distinct and were finalised through discussion among the broader research team. Throughout this process, the researchers maintained reflexive journals to document analytical decisions and potential biases.

Our analysis yielded results from two complementary approaches. The summative content analysis identified key terms and their frequency across the dataset, revealing the relative prominence of different concerns. The conventional content analysis then explored the meaning and context behind these concerns, with themes derived inductively from participant responses through the coding process described above. Results are organised below by analytic approach, with the summative analysis presented first to provide a quantitative overview, followed by the conventional analysis, which contextualises these findings through stakeholder narratives.

Summative Content Analysis

Word frequency analysis revealed four primary themes across all participant groups (see Table 3). Support emerged as the most frequently discussed theme (110 instances, 3.5% coverage), with financial support representing a critical concern across all groups. Parents consistently sought personal financial assistance for medical expenses, while educators advocated for increased school funding for staff and resources. Healthcare professionals emphasised the need for funding to expand the workforce to meet growing demands. Within this theme, funding (22 instances, 3.51% coverage) and service needs (13 instances, 2.51% coverage) were especially prominent, reflecting gaps between service availability and demand, and the need for centralised service information to address access barriers for families.

Time‐related concerns (38 instances, 1.51% coverage) manifested differently across stakeholder groups. Parents expressed significant frustration with appointment wait times and diagnosis delays, while families reported ongoing challenges in balancing care requirements with other commitments. Healthcare professionals and educators highlighted serious concerns about missed developmental milestones, with educators particularly noting increased time demands for supporting children awaiting diagnosis.

The theme of waiting (44 instances, 1.36% coverage) revealed widespread concerns. Parents strongly criticised the ‘wait and see’ approach often adopted by healthcare providers, while families described considerable uncertainty during waitlist periods. Educators observed substantial negative effects of delays on their students, and healthcare professionals acknowledged the significant impact of delays on families. More specifically, wait times (29 instances, 4.15% coverage) were identified across all groups as detrimental to child development, while concerns about the duration of the process (13 instances, 1.91% coverage) reflected the cumulative burden of documentation and repeated delays across the diagnostic journey.

Discussion of diagnosis (65 instances, 2.21% coverage) centred on comparative experiences. Parents and educators frequently compared pre‐ and post‐diagnosis experiences, noting that access to support services improved significantly after diagnosis. Healthcare providers emphasised their limited intervention options before formal diagnosis.

Parents and Guardians

Parents and guardians emphasised the importance of proactive information seeking. They consistently noted the value of seeking support early and the benefits of accessing multiple support channels. Their recommendations highlighted the need for more accessible information about available services and clearer guidance on navigating the diagnostic pathway.

Educators

Educators primarily focused on classroom support and inclusion challenges. They expressed strong needs for better information about available support services and emphasised the importance of early intervention resources. Their recommendations centred on enhanced training for identifying developmental indicators and improved communication channels with both parents and healthcare providers.

Healthcare Professionals

Healthcare professionals identified service availability as a critical concern. They offered criticism of ‘wait and see’ approaches while acknowledging the systemic pressures that perpetuate them. Their recommendations focused on the need for greater school presence, recognition of diverse presentation patterns, and more efficient referral processes to reduce bottlenecks in the diagnostic pathway.

Factors Adding to Delay

Communication gaps between healthcare providers, educators and families emerged as a prominent contributor to DD. Parents and guardians frequently reported feeling ‘lost’ in the initial help‐seeking stages, with insufficient information about where to seek assessment or what to expect from the process. Some practitioners' persistence with ‘wait and see’ approaches further complicated the journey, leaving families uncertain about when to escalate concerns. Educators described challenges in communicating developmental concerns to parents sensitively, while healthcare professionals noted the difficulty of coordinating across services.

Financial and resource constraints further compounded these barriers. Healthcare funding limitations, particularly regarding bulk billing, created significant access barriers. Participants reported wait times ranging from 6 months to 2 years for specialist appointments, with these delays described consistently as ‘unacceptable.’ Waitlist length surfaced as a primary barrier, compounded by insufficient healthcare professional availability. Financial, emotional and practical challenges were reported across all stakeholder groups.

Consequences of Delay

The consequences of delay were particularly significant. Participants reported missed developmental and academic milestones, with several parents noting increased behavioural challenges. One parent shared, ‘My son became really aggressive… I truly believe this wouldn't have happened if we got help earlier.’ Educators consistently reported reduced academic prospects with later diagnosis, while healthcare professionals emphasised lost intervention opportunities during critical developmental periods.

Stakeholder Critiques and Recommendations

Critiques and recommendations were expressed at the systemic level and by each stakeholder group. At the systemic level, participants called for increased funding for healthcare professional training, enhanced school resources for special‐needs support, action on private/public healthcare cost disparities and improved staff‐to‐student ratios, including a centralised service database (e.g., via MyGov) to streamline access to services and assessment forms. Parents and guardians emphasised patience during the diagnostic process alongside early help‐seeking and community support, and called for a more accessible information and clearer guidance on navigating the diagnostic pathway. Health professionals advocated for improved appointment availability, more efficient referral processes and enhanced autism‐specific training. Educators called for improved communication with parents and healthcare providers, and enhanced developmental screening training.

Discussion

This study provides a cross‐sector analysis of DD in childhood development, examining perspectives from parents, educators and healthcare professionals in WA. Our findings reveal that DD significantly impacts all stakeholder groups, with common contributors including insufficient funding, extended wait times, unclear diagnostic pathways, communication barriers and healthcare professional shortages. While participants readily identified factors contributing to DD, proposed solutions varied considerably, highlighting the complexity of addressing this multifaceted issue.

Stakeholder Perspectives in Context

Parents reported substantial stress and financial burden while pursuing diagnoses, describing significant challenges navigating the healthcare system and insufficient long‐term support. Many expressed frustration with limited information about available resources and the ‘wait and see’ approach adopted by healthcare providers. These experiences align closely with findings from Crane et al. (2016), whose survey of over 1000 UK parents found that the diagnostic process was frequently described as stressful and protracted. Similarly, the parental belief that earlier diagnosis would have improved their children's outcomes is consistent with longitudinal evidence demonstrating the efficacy of early intervention (Estes et al. 2015; Oredipe et al. 2023; Zuckerman et al. 2017). The frustration with ‘wait and see’ approaches mirrors findings by Mitchell and Holdt (2014), who documented the emotional toll of delayed recognition of developmental concerns by healthcare providers.

Educators identified systemic challenges including inadequate staffing and funding for supporting children with developmental needs. They described witnessing the academic and social consequences of delayed diagnosis and expressed uncertainty about communicating developmental concerns to parents. These findings are consistent with Hinds et al. (2015), who found that teachers experienced significant distress when unable to provide adequate support, and extend the literature by documenting the specific challenges educators face in the WA context.

Healthcare providers acknowledged systemic barriers including extended wait times, inefficient referral processes and the need for increased specialist training and employment. These findings are consistent with broader Australian research by Garg et al. (2018), who identified that healthcare professionals in multicultural settings face additional challenges in developmental surveillance. The healthcare professionals' emphasis on improved communication channels resonates with Sapiets et al. (2021), who identified fragmented service coordination as a key barrier to timely access to early intervention across multiple countries.

Common Themes and Implications

The convergence of concerns across all three stakeholder groups—particularly regarding funding, wait times and communication—suggests that DD is a systemic issue requiring coordinated, multi‐level intervention rather than isolated reforms. This view aligns with Marshall et al. (2020), who emphasised that barriers to help‐seeking for families of children with developmental delays are interconnected and span individual, interpersonal and structural levels. The universal recognition of early intervention's value across our participant groups further supports the growing evidence base for proactive, rather than reactive, approaches to developmental assessment (McGorry and Mei 2018).

Several practical implications emerge from these findings. Increased funding for diagnostic services and early intervention programmes could help reduce wait times and improve access to care. Implementing comprehensive training programmes for educators could facilitate earlier identification of developmental issues, a recommendation also supported by Augusto et al. (2025) in the context of autism diagnosis. Developing clearer pathways and information resources for parents could alleviate some of the stress and confusion reported by families. The participant suggestion of a centralised service database through MyGov represents an innovative approach to streamlining appointment booking and reducing wait times, and warrants further investigation.

Limitations and Future Directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these results. The predominantly female, relatively small sample size (N = 36) and focus on the WA population may limit generalisability to other regions or healthcare systems. Selection bias may have occurred, as those most affected by DD may have been more likely to participate. The use of written survey responses, while enabling broad participation, may have limited the depth of data compared to in‐depth interviews. Accordingly, data saturation was not formally assessed, as the fixed survey design precluded the iterative sampling and probing through which saturation is typically established. While recurring themes across stakeholder groups suggest the main concerns were captured, this remains a limitation that future interview‐based research could address. Additionally, the absence of children's and adolescents' perspectives represents a significant gap in understanding the full impact of the diagnostic process.

Future research should address these limitations by employing larger, more diverse samples and incorporating interview‐based methodologies to capture richer narratives. Including perspectives from children and adolescents who have experienced the diagnostic process could inform more child‐centred approaches. Longitudinal studies tracking outcomes of early versus late diagnosis would provide compelling evidence for the importance of timely interventions. Comparative studies across different Australian states and healthcare systems could identify transferable best practices. Economic impact studies examining the costs of DD to healthcare and education systems could provide additional motivation for policy reform.

Conclusion

This study provides cross‐sector insights into DD in childhood development in WA, revealing shared concerns among parents, educators and healthcare professionals regarding funding, wait times, communication and systemic coordination. Our findings suggest that reducing DD requires a comprehensive, coordinated approach combining increased resource allocation, improved inter‐stakeholder communication, streamlined referral processes and enhanced training for both educators and healthcare professionals. The convergence of stakeholder perspectives documented here offers a foundation for evidence‐informed policy reform aimed at ensuring children receive timely diagnoses and access to early intervention.
