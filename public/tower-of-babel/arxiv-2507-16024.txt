Structure-preserving deflation of critical eigenvalues in quadratic eigenvalue problems associated with damped mass-spring systems
Rafikul Alam, Volker Mehrmann, Ninoslav Truhar

Abstract
For a quadratic matrix polynomial associated with a damped mass-spring system there are three types of critical eigenvalues, the eigenvalues ∞ \infty and 0 0 and the eigenvalues on the imaginary axis. All these are on the boundary of the set of (robustly) stable eigenvalues. For numerical methods, but also for (robust) stability analysis, it is desirable to deflate such eigenvalues by projecting the matrix polynomial to a lower dimensional subspace before computing the other eigenvalues and eigenvectors. We describe structure-preserving deflation strategies that deflate these eigenvalues via a trimmed structure-preserving linearization. We employ these results for the special case of hyperbolic problems. We also analyze the effect of a (possibly low rank) parametric damping matrix on purely imaginary eigenvalues.



Keywords:
Mass-spring system, damping, inertia, hyperbolic eigenvalue problem, trimmed linearization, definite matrix pencil.

AMS subject classification:
65F15, 15A57, 15A18, 65F35

1
Introduction

We consider the quadratic eigenvalue problem

P(\lambda)v=0,

(1)

for regular matrix polynomials
P(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+\lambda D+K
, with Hermitian coefficients
M=M^{*},\ D=D^{*},\ K=K^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
, eigenvalues
\lambda
and right eigenvectors
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
. (Here
M^{*}
denotes the conjugate transpose of the matrix
M
.)
Such quadratic eigenvalue problems are well studied
[
7
,
13
,
25
]

and arise in a multitude of applications
see
[
4
,
23
,
30
]
.
Our main motivation to revisit this problem is the class of damped mass-spring systems, see
[
31
]
, where
M
is a positive definite or positive semidefinite mass matrix,
D
is a semidefinite damping matrix and
K
is a positive (semi-)definite stiffness matrix.
Most of our results hold regardless of whether
D
is positive or negative semidefinite. In this paper we focus on the case that
D
is positive semidefinite.

Homogeneous damped mass-spring systems have the structure

M\ddot{x}+D\dot{x}+Kx=0,

(2)

and the ansatz
x=e^{t\lambda}v
leads to the quadratic eigenvalue problem (
1
).
Numerical methods for the solution of
the eigenvalue problem are widely available
see
[
9
,
23
]
. They are mainly based on the reformulation (
linearization
) of (
1
) as a linear eigenvalue problem.
The construction of such linearizations has been a very important research topic (see e.g.

[
6
,
16
]
) in particular when it is essential to preserve the structure, see
[
10
,
15
]
.

One of the difficulties with such structured linearizations arises when the leading coefficient
M
and/or the trailing coefficient
K
is singular, which may mean that the classical structured linearizations do not exist
[
15
]
, and the occuring eigenvalues at
\infty
or
0
may have Jordan blocks of size
2
, or the problem is non-regular, see
[
18
,
19
]
.

In such a situation it is advisable to first deflate the part of the matrix polynomial associated with these eigenvalues. This can, for example, be done via
trimmed
linearizations
[
5
]
. However, it is common practice in industrial applications to introduce small perturbations to move the eigenvalues away from
0
,
\infty
but this may lead to drastically wrong results, see the analysis in the context of brake squeal
[
8
]
. It has been shown there, that from a numerical point of view it is better to consider the nearby problem with exact eigenvalues
0
or
\infty
and to apply numerical methods to a problem where these eigenvalues have been deflated.
Let us illustrate the situation of eigenvalues near
\infty
or
0
with a very simple example, see
[
22
]
.

Example 1

Consider the standard model of a damped mass-spring system

m\ddot{q}+d\dot{q}+kq=f
,
with position mass
m
, stiffness
k
and damping
d
, forcing function
f
and kinetic plus potential energy
\mathcal{H}(q,p)=\frac{1}{2}kq^{2}+\frac{p^{2}}{2m}
. The classical first order formulation (by introducing
p=\dot{q}
) is given by

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}m&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\dot{p}\\
\dot{q}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}-d&-k\\
1&0\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}p\\
q\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}f\\
0\end{array}\right].

The associated eigenvalue problem has the matrix pencil

P(\lambda)=\lambda\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}m&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}d&k\\
-1&0\end{array}\right],

with eigenvalues

-\frac{d}{2m}\pm\sqrt{(\frac{d}{2m})^{2}-\frac{k}{m}}

If the size of the mass
m
is negligible compared to the damping and stiffness, it is common practice to set
m=0
, which then gives the matrix pencil

P(\lambda)=\lambda\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&0\\
0&1\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}d&k\\
-1&0\end{array}\right],

with eigenvalues
\infty
and
-k/d
or even a Jordan block at
\infty
if
d=0
.

Another common technique is to replace stiff springs (with very large
k
) by rigid connections. In our example this corresponds to the limit
k\to\infty
. To be able to take this limit we can rewrite the system in new coordinates and obtain an equivalent matrix pencil with

P(\lambda)=\lambda\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}m&0\\
0&\frac{1}{k}\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}d&1\\
-1&0\end{array}\right].

For
k\to\infty
this pencil has a Jordan block of size
2
at the eigenvalue
\infty
.
One may also take the limits
m\to 0
and
k\to\infty
simultaneously. This gives a double semisimple eigenvalue at
\infty
.

To further illustrate the difficulties that may arise consider the linearization of (
1
) as

Q(\lambda):=\lambda\left[\begin{matrix}M&\\
&I_{n}\end{matrix}\right]+\left[\begin{matrix}D&I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&0\end{matrix}\right]\left[\begin{matrix}I_{n}&\\
&K\end{matrix}\right],

(3)

with
I_{n}
denoting the identity matrix of size
n
. This has the structure of a
dissipative Hamiltonian pencil
and the spectral properties for such pencils have been
analyzed in detail in

[
18
]
. There it has been shown that if the pencil is regular, then all finite eigenvalues are in the closed left half of the complex plane, the eigenvalues at
\infty
and
0
may have Jordan blocks of size at most two, which in the case that
0
has such a Jordan block means that the system is unstable and if
\infty
has such a Jordan block then arbitrarily small perturbations may move the eigenvalues anywhere in the complex plane
[
2
]
. Furthermore, the nonzero purely imaginary eigenvalues are
semisimple
, i.e., have Jordan blocks of size at most one.
If
M
(or
K
) is invertible, the case that we are considering in this paper, then the Jordan blocks at
0
(resp.,
\infty
) are of size at most one. Further properties of the dynamical system are studied in
[
1
,
21
]
.

In view of the possible Jordan structures that may arise when the matrix polynomial has eigenvalues at
0
and/or
\infty
, one of the goals of this paper is to derive deflation methods for these eigenvalues. This is done for general damped mass-spring systems in Section
2
and for the specific case of
hyperbolic problems
in Section
3
.

Besides the eigenvalues
0
and
\infty
, we also study the nonzero purely imaginary eigenvalues which correspond to undamped oscillatory solutions. We discuss deflation procedures for these eigenvalues in Section
4
and analyze the effect of (low rank) damping on these eigenvalues. We develop a constructive method for low rank perturbations of the damping matrix that move all or a selected set of purely imaginary eigenvalues of
P(\lambda)
from the imaginary axis.

1.1
Notation and preliminaries

We denote the set of all
m\times n
matrices with entries in
\mathbb{C}
by
\mathbb{C}^{m,n}.

The nullspace of a matrix
X
is denoted by
\mathcal{N}(X)
. The dimension of the nullspace is called the
nullity
of
X
.

If
X
and
Y
are
n\times n
Hermitian matrices, then we write
X>Y
(resp.,
X\geq Y
) if
X-Y
is positive definite (resp., positive semidefinite).

The
inertia of a Hermitian matrix

H
is denoted by
\imath(H):=(\imath_{+}(H),\,\imath_{-}(H),\,\imath_{0}(H))
, where
\imath_{+}(H),\,\imath_{-}(H)
and
\imath_{0}(H)
denote the number of positive, negative and zero eigenvalues of
H
, respectively.

We assume that the matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)
in (
1
) is
regular
, i.e. there exists
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\det P(\lambda_{0})\neq 0
. The
spectrum
of
P(\lambda)
, denoted by
\Lambda(P),
is given by

\Lambda(P):=\{\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}:\det(P(\lambda))=0\}.

Let

\mathbf{S}_{P}(\lambda)=\mathrm{diag}(\phi_{1}(\lambda),\cdots,\phi_{n}(\lambda))

be the
Smith form
, see e.g.
[
26
]
, of
P(\lambda)
which is obtained under unimodular equivalence transformations, where
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}
are unique monic polynomials and
\phi_{i}
divides
\phi_{i+1}
for
i=1,\ldots,n-1
.
Then
\phi_{i}(\lambda)=(\lambda-\mu)^{m_{i}}\rho_{i}(\lambda)
with
\rho_{i}(\mu)\neq 0
and
m_{i}\geq 0
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. The tuple

\textrm{Ind}(\mu,P):=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})
is called the
multiplicity index
of
\mu
and satisfies the condition
0\leq m_{1}\leq m_{2}\leq\cdots\leq m_{n}.
The nonzero components in
\textrm{Ind}(\mu,P)
are called the
partial multiplicities
of
\mu
as an eigenvalue of
P(\lambda)
. The factors
(\lambda-\mu)^{m_{i}}
with
m_{i}\neq 0
are called the
elementary divisors
of
P(\lambda)
at
\mu
. The
algebraic multiplicity
of
\mu
is then given by
m_{1}+\cdots+m_{n}
.

Definition 2

Let
P(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}A+\lambda B+C
be an
n\times n
matrix polynomial, where
A
is nonsingular and
\mathrm{rank}(C)<n.
An
m\times m
matrix pencil
L(\lambda):={L_{0}}+\lambda{L_{1}}
, where
L_{1}
is nonsingular, is said to be a trimmed linearization of
P(\lambda)
if
m<2n,\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}=\Lambda(L)\setminus\{0\}
and
\textrm{Ind}(\mu,P)=\textrm{Ind}(\mu,L)
for all
\mu\in\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}.

For
P(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}A+\lambda B+C
with
\mathrm{rank}(A)<n
and
C
nonsingular, a trimmed linearization of
P(\lambda)
is defined similarly. In such a case, an
m\times m
pencil
L(\lambda):=L_{0}+\lambda L_{1}
is a trimmed linearization of
P(\lambda)
if
m<2n,\Lambda(P)\setminus\{\infty\}=\Lambda(L)\setminus\{\infty\}
and
\textrm{Ind}(\mu,P)=\textrm{Ind}(\mu,L)
for all
\mu\in\Lambda(P)\setminus\{\infty\}
.

Let
G(\lambda)
be an
n\times n
regular rational matrix. Then the Smith-McMillan form
[
26
]
of
G(\lambda)
, which is obtained under unimodular equivalence transformations, is given by

\mathbf{SM}(\lambda):=\mathrm{diag}(\phi_{1}(\lambda)/{\psi_{1}(\lambda)},\cdots,\phi_{n}(\lambda)/{\psi_{n}(\lambda)}),

where
\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{n}
and
\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n}
are unique monic polynomials such that
\phi_{i}
and
\psi_{i}
are pairwise coprime,
\phi_{i}
divides
\phi_{i+1}
and
\psi_{i+1}
divides
\psi_{i}.

A matrix polynomial
P(\lambda)
as in (
1
) is said to be
hyperbolic
if
M>0
and

(x^{*}Dx)^{2}-4x^{*}Mx\,x^{*}Kx>0

(4)

for all nonzero
x
, or equivalently, if
-P(\mu)>0
for some real
\mu
, see
[
11
]
. If
P(\lambda)
is hyperbolic with
D>0
and
K\geq 0
then
P(\lambda)
is called
overdamped.

An
n\times n
Hermitian matrix pencil
A+\lambda E
is said to be a
definite pencil
if
A+\mu E>0
for some
\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

The
reverse polynomial
to
P(\lambda)
in (
1
) is
\mathrm{rev}P(\lambda):=\mu^{2}K+\mu D+M
.

For
M=M^{*}>0
, a matrix
X\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}
is called
M
-unitary
if
X^{*}MX=I_{n}
.

2
Deflation of zero and infinite eigenvalues

In this section we design a method for deflating zero (infinite) eigenvalues of
P(\lambda)
and we assume that either
M
or
K
is invertible. Our strategy is to construct
trimmed
structured linearizations of
P(\lambda)
in which the zero or infinite eigenvalues of
P(\lambda)
have been deflated. We treat only the quadratic matrix polynomial
P
but the proposed method can be extended to general even order Hermitian matrix polynomials.

Since
\infty
is an eigenvalue of
P(\lambda)
if and only if
0
is an eigenvalue of
\mathrm{rev}P(\lambda):=\mu^{2}K+\mu D+M
, without loss of generality we describe the procedure for deflating the zero eigenvalues, i.e., we discuss the case that
M>0
and that
K\geq 0
has rank
r<n
.
Although all results in this section hold for
P(\lambda)
when
M=M^{*}
is nonsingular and
D=D^{*}
is semidefinite, to not overload the presentation, we only consider
M>0
and
D\geq 0
. Furthermore, we assume in the following that we have a full rank factorization
K=GG^{*}
, i.e.,
G\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n\times r}
has full column rank.
In many applications this is directly available,
[
31
]
, otherwise it may be obtained, e.g., by a low rank Cholesky factorization of
K
.

When this factorization of
K
is available, then we may consider the Hermitian matrix pencil

S(\lambda):=\left[\begin{matrix}\lambda M+D&G\\
G^{*}&-\lambda I_{r}\end{matrix}\right]=\lambda\left[\begin{matrix}M&\\
&-I_{r}\end{matrix}\right]+\left[\begin{matrix}D&G\\
G^{*}&0\end{matrix}\right]

(5)

of size
n+r
where both coefficients are indefinite.

Remark 3

As an alternative to (
5
), one may consider the
equivalent
dissipative Hamiltonian pencil

L(\lambda):=\lambda\left[\begin{matrix}M&\\
&I_{r}\end{matrix}\right]+\left[\begin{matrix}D&G\\
-G^{*}&0\end{matrix}\right].

(6)

Note that in this formulation both coefficients have a Hermitian part that is positive definite and positive semidefinite, respectively.

Our first result characterizes the Smith form of
S(\lambda)
in relation to the Smith-McMillan form of the rational matrix function
T(\lambda):=P(\lambda)/\lambda
.

Lemma 4

Let
P(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+\lambda D+GG^{*}
, where
0<M=M^{*},\ 0\leq D=D^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
, and
G\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times r}
is of rank
r
. If the
Smith-McMillan form
of the rational matrix
T(\lambda)
is

\mathrm{diag}(\phi_{1}(\lambda)/{\psi_{1}(\lambda)},\cdots,\phi_{n}(\lambda)/{\psi_{n}(\lambda)})
,
then the
Smith form
of the matrix pencil
S(\lambda)
in (
5
) is given by

\left[\begin{matrix}I_{r}&\\
&\mathrm{diag}(\phi_{1}(\lambda),\cdots,\phi_{n}(\lambda))\end{matrix}\right].

(7)

In particular,

\Lambda(S)\setminus\{0\}=\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}
and
\mathrm{Ind}(\mu,S)=\mathrm{Ind}(\mu,P)
for all
\mu\in\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}
.

For any
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, the map

\mathcal{N}(P(\lambda_{0}))\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}(S(\lambda_{0})),\ u\longmapsto\left[\begin{matrix}\lambda_{0}u\\
G^{*}u\end{matrix}\right],

is an isomorphism.

Furthermore, if
D
is invertible, then
0\in\Lambda(S)
if and only if
G^{*}D^{-1}G
is singular. In particular, if
D
is definite then
\Lambda(S)=\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}
.

Proof
. We have

\displaystyle\det(S(\lambda))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det(-\lambda I_{r})\det(\lambda M+D+G(\lambda I_{r})^{-1}G^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(-1)^{r}\lambda^{r}\det(P(\lambda)/\lambda)=(-1)^{r}\det(P(\lambda))/{\lambda^{n-r}},

which shows that
\Lambda(S)\setminus\{0\}=\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}
. If
\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}
, then it follows that the algebraic multiplicity of
\lambda_{0}
as an eigenvalue of
S(\lambda)
is the same as the algebraic multiplicity of
\lambda_{0}
as an eigenvalue of
P(\lambda)
. It is obvious that the map

\mathcal{N}(P(\lambda_{0}))\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}(S(\lambda_{0})),\hskip 11.74988ptu\longmapsto\left[\begin{matrix}\lambda_{0}u\\
G^{*}u\end{matrix}\right]

is an isomorphism. Hence the geometric multiplicity of
\lambda_{0}
as an eigenvalue of
S(\lambda)
is the same as the geometric multiplicity of
\lambda_{0}
as an eigenvalue of
P(\lambda)
.

Since

\mathrm{rank}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}G^{*}&-\lambda I_{r}\end{array}\right]\right)=r=\mathrm{rank}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}G\\
-\lambda I_{r}\end{array}\right]\right)

for all
\lambda\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
it follows that

T(\lambda)=\lambda M+D+G(\lambda I_{r}-0)^{-1}G^{*}

is a
minimal realization
, i.e., a realization of minimal degree (see, e.g.,
[
3
]
) of the rational matrix
T(\lambda)
.
As
T(\lambda)
is the transfer function associated with the system matrix
S(\lambda)
and has Smith-McMillan form
\mathrm{diag}(\phi_{1}(\lambda)/{\psi_{1}(\lambda)},\cdots,\phi_{n}(\lambda)/{\psi_{n}(\lambda)})
, the Smith form of
S(\lambda)
is given by (
7
), see,
[
26
, Theorem 4.1, p.111]
.
Hence it follows that
\mathrm{Ind}(\mu,S)=\mathrm{Ind}(\mu,P)
for all
\mu\in\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}
.

Next, we have
\det(S(0))=\det(D)\det(-G^{*}D^{-1}G)
showing that
S(0)
is singular if and only if
G^{*}D^{-1}G
is singular.
Therefore,
0
is an eigenvalue of
S(\lambda)
if and only if
G^{*}D^{-1}G
is singular. In particular, if
D
is definite then
G^{*}D^{-1}G
is nonsingular and the assertion follows.

Remark 5

Lemma
4
shows that
S(\lambda)
in (
5
) is a trimmed linearization of
P(\lambda)
in the sense of Definition
2
. For
D
definite, we have

\Lambda(S)=\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}\text{ and }\mathrm{Ind}(\mu,P)=\mathrm{Ind}(\mu,S)

for all
\mu\in\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}
. Hence, in this case, the linearization
S(\lambda)
deflates the zero eigenvalue of
P(\lambda)
and preserves the nonzero eigenvalues of
P(\lambda)
including their partial multiplicities.

For the case that
D
is only semidefinite
we need the following lemma.

Lemma 6

Let
\mathcal{D}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}D_{11}&D_{12}&D_{13}\\
D_{21}&D_{22}&0\\
\hline\cr D_{31}&0&0\end{array}\right]
, where
D_{13},D_{31}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r,r}
are nonsingular. For
\mathcal{\widehat{D}}:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}D_{12}&D_{13}\\
D_{22}&0\end{array}\right]
then

\mathrm{nullity}(\mathcal{D})=\mathrm{nullity}(D_{22})=\mathrm{nullity}(\mathcal{\widehat{D}})
.

Moreover, the maps

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(D_{22})\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}),\hskip 11.74988ptx\longmapsto\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
-D_{13}^{-1}D_{12}x\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}})\longrightarrow N(\mathcal{D}),\hskip 11.74988pt\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
y\end{array}\right]\longmapsto\left[\begin{array}[]{c}0\\
x\\
y\end{array}\right]

are isomorphisms. Furthermore,
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{\widehat{D}})=r+\mathrm{rank}(D_{22})
and
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{D})=2r+\mathrm{rank}(D_{22})
.

Proof
. We have
\widehat{\mathcal{D}}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{r}&-D_{13}^{-1}D_{12}\\
0&I_{n-r}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{r}&0\\
0&D_{22}\end{array}\right]
. Hence
\mathrm{rank}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}})=r+\mathrm{rank}(D_{22})
and
\mathrm{nullity}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}})=\mathrm{nullity}(D_{22})
. It is easily seen that the map

\mathcal{N}(D_{22})\longrightarrow\mathcal{N}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}}),\hskip 11.74988ptx\longmapsto\left[\begin{array}[]{c}x\\
-D_{13}^{-1}D_{12}x\end{array}\right]

is well-defined and an isomorphism.

By applying block Gaussian elimination
\mathcal{D}
can be transformed to

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}0&0&D_{13}\\
0&D_{22}&0\\
\hline\cr D_{31}&0&0\end{array}\right],

which shows that
\mathrm{rank}(\mathcal{D})=2r+\mathrm{rank}(D_{22})
and
\mathrm{nullity}(\mathcal{D})=\mathrm{nullity}(D_{22})
. Finally, the map from
\mathcal{N}(\widehat{\mathcal{D}})
to
\mathcal{N}(\mathcal{D})
is easily seen to be well-defined and is an isomorphism.

As an immediate consequence of Lemma
6
, we show that the projection (restriction) of the damping matrix
D
onto the nullspace of
K:=GG^{*}
plays a vital role in the deflation of zero eigenvalues of
P(\lambda)
.

Lemma 7

Consider
P(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+\lambda D+GG^{*}
with
D=WW^{*}
, where
0<M=M^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
,
G\in\mathbb{C}^{n,r}
is of rank
r
, and
W\in\mathbb{C}^{n,m}
is of rank
m
and the associated pencil

S(\lambda)
in (
5
).
Let
Q^{*}G=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}R\\
0\end{array}\right]
be a QR factorization of
G
, where
R\in{\mathbb{C}}^{r,r}
is nonsingular and

Q:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Q_{1}&Q_{2}\end{array}\right]
is unitary with
Q_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n-r}
. Then
\Lambda(S)=\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}
if and only if
Q_{2}^{*}DQ_{2}
is nonsingular.
In particular,
\Lambda(S)=\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}
if and only if
\mathrm{rank}(W^{*}Q_{2})=n-r.

Proof
. We have

\displaystyle\mathrm{diag}(Q^{*},I_{r})S(\lambda)\mathrm{diag}(Q,I_{r})

\displaystyle\hskip 11.74988pt=\lambda\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}M_{11}&M_{12}&0\\
M_{21}&M_{22}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&-I_{r}\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}[]{cc|c}D_{11}&D_{12}&R\\
D_{21}&D_{22}&0\\
\hline\cr R^{*}&0&0\end{array}\right],

where
D_{22}=Q_{2}^{*}DQ_{2}
. By Lemma
6
,
0\in\Lambda(S)
if and only if
D_{22}
is singular. Hence
\Lambda(S)=\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}
if and only if
D_{22}
is nonsingular. Finally, note that
Q_{2}^{*}WW^{*}Q_{2}
is nonsingular if and only if
\mathrm{rank}(W^{*}Q_{2})=n-r
.

Lemma
7
shows that if
D
is positive semidefinite, to deflate the zero eigenvalues of
P(\lambda)
, the rank of
D
must be greater than or equal to the nullity of
K=GG^{*}
. The next lemma characterizes the deflation of zero eigenvalues of
P(\lambda)
under the condition that
D
is semidefinite and
\mathrm{rank}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}D&G\end{array}\right]\right)=n
.

Lemma 8

Consider
P(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+\lambda D+GG^{*}
, where
0<M=M^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
,
0\leq D=D^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
,
G\in\mathbb{C}^{n,r}
is of rank
r
, and the associated pencil

S(\lambda)
in (
5
). If
\mathrm{rank}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}D&G\end{array}\right]\right)=n
then
0\notin\Lambda(S)
and we have
\Lambda(S)=\Lambda(P)\setminus\{0\}.

Proof
. If

2.1
Computational methods.

We now briefly discuss two methods for the implementation of the result in Theorem
9
.
Matlab
implementations are presented in the appendix. Consider the matrices
0<M\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}
,
D=D^{*}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}
, and

D
is semidefinite and that
\mathrm{rank}(G)=r
. Also, suppose that

For the first method, consider a rank revealing QR factorization

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}D&G\end{array}\right]P=Q\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}R_{11}&R_{12}\\
0&0\end{array}\right],

(15)

where

is nonsingular,

is unitary and

is a permutation matrix. Partition

, where
Q_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,m}.
Then it follows that
Q_{2}^{*}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}D&G\end{array}\right]=0

\mathrm{span}(Q_{2})=\mathcal{N}(D)\cap\mathcal{N}(G^{*})

Q_{1}^{*}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}D&G\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}R_{11}&R_{12}\end{array}\right]P^{*}

P(\lambda)
.

1.

Compute

2.

Compute a rank revealing QR factorization as in (

3.

Partition

conformably and compute
\widehat{D}:=Q_{1}^{*}DQ_{1},
where
Q_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,m}
.

4.

Compute
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}M_{11}&M_{12}\\

and the Schur complement
\widehat{M}:=M_{11}-M_{12}M_{22}^{-1}M_{12}^{*}
, where
M_{11}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m,m}
.

5.

Compute
R:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}R_{11}&R_{12}\end{array}\right]P^{*}
and define
\widehat{G}
to be the last
r
columns of
R
, where
r=\mathrm{rank}(G)
.

6.

Construct the pencil
\lambda\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\widehat{M}&0\\

\widehat{G}^{*}&0\end{array}\right]
in which the zero eigenvalues are deflated.

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}D&G\end{array}\right]=U\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{m}&0\\

where

, where

. Then it follows that

. Also,
U_{1}^{*}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}D&G\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\Sigma_{m}&0\end{array}\right]V^{*}
has full row rank. This yields the following algorithm.

Algorithm 2

SVD based method for deflating zero eigenvalues of
P(\lambda)
.

1.

Compute

If
m=n
then STOP as the eigenvalue
0

m<n

2.

Compute the SVD as in (

3.

where

4.

Compute
\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}M_{11}&M_{12}\\
M_{12}^{*}&M_{22}\end{array}\right]:=U^{*}MU
and
\widehat{M}:=M_{11}-M_{12}M_{22}^{-1}M_{12}^{*}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m,m}
.

2.2
Numerical Example

In this subsection we illustrate the two methods described in the previous subsection via a numerical example.

Consider the free vibration of a rod as presented in
[
29
]
modeled by a following partial differential equation (PDE) on the spatial domain
\Omega=(0,1)
and time domain

given by

\rho(x)u_{tt}+c(x)u_{t}-\frac{d}{dx}\left(k(x)\frac{du}{dx}\right)+\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}\left(a(x)\frac{d^{2}u}{dx^{2}}\right)=0\,,

(17)

subject to the boundary conditions

Here,

denotes the mass density with units [kg/m],

is the damping coefficient [kg/(m·s)],

is the axial stiffness [N], and

2

To approximate the solution, we introduce a finite-dimensional subspace spanned by a set of basis (shape) functions

(18)

where

are time-dependent coefficients. As the PDE involves up to fourth derivatives, the shape functions are assumed to be at least
C^{1}
-continuous. Therefore, Hermite cubic elements or other higher-order elements are appropriate for this problem
[
12
,
32
]
.

To apply the finite element method to the weak form, we multiply equation (
17
) by a test function

and integrate over the domain. After integrating by parts where necessary (and using the natural boundary conditions), we obtain

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\rho(x)u_{tt}v\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}c(x)u_{t}v\,dx+\int_{0}^{1}k(x)u_{x}v_{x}\,dx

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}a(x)u_{xx}v_{xx}.

Substituting the ansatz (

) for

and choosing

, we arrive at the system of second-order ordinary differential equations

where the unknown coefficients

are collected in a column vector

. For

, the mass and damping matrices, and the two stiffness matrices

are defined by the integrals

\displaystyle M_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\rho(x)\phi_{j}(x)\phi_{i}(x)\,dx,

\displaystyle C_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}c(x)\phi_{j}(x)\phi_{i}(x)\,dx,

\displaystyle(K_{1})_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}k(x)\phi_{j}^{\prime}(x)\phi_{i}^{\prime}(x)\,dx,

\displaystyle(K_{2})_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}a(x)\phi_{j}^{\prime\prime}(x)\phi_{i}^{\prime\prime}(x)\,dx,

where
K_{1}
is associated with the second derivative (axial deformation) and
K_{2}
is associated with the fourth derivative (bending stiffness).

These matrices are computed element-by-element using appropriate quadrature formulas, and then assembled into global matrices.

As a concrete example consider

Using

4
Deflation and damping of purely imaginary eigenvalues

In this section we discuss the deflation of nonzero purely imaginary eigenvalues and we also discuss the influence of (additional)
damping on these eigenvalues.

Consider the two matrix polynomials
P_{0}(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+K
and
P(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+\lambda D+K
, where
M=M^{*}>0
,
K=K^{*}\geq 0
, and
D=D^{*}\geq 0
. The eigenvalues of

are all purely imaginary and typically damping is used to move them from the imaginary axis. In other applications damping is already active but this has not yet moved all eigenvalues from the imaginary axis. In this case one often uses design changes in the physical system or feedback to move these purely imaginary eigenvalues. Another application in industrial practice is to compute the eigenvectors of
P_{0}(\lambda)
and use these for modal truncation in

to produce a small size quadratic problem which is assumed to give good approximations to relevant eigenvalues, see
[
8
,
20
]
for an analysis of this approach in the context of brake squeal. The reduced model is then used for the calculation of extra damping to move the imaginary eigenvalues.

In the following, we introduce a deflation method for the purely imaginary eigenvalues. All results in this section hold for

when

,

is semidefinite and

. However, for simplicity of presentation, we only discuss the case that

,

, and

. Consider first the spectral properties of

[

\hline\cr&(\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2})I_{p}\end{array}\right]

x^{*}Dx=0
as
x^{*}P_{0}(i\omega)x
real and
\omega\neq 0

i\omega\in\Lambda(P_{0}),

\mathcal{N}(P(i\omega))\subset\mathcal{N}(P_{0}(i\omega))

\mathrm{rank}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{0}(i\omega)&D\end{array}\right]\right)=\mathrm{rank}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{c}P_{0}(i\omega)\\
D\end{array}\right]\right)<n.

Conversely, suppose that

.

By part (a), we have

if and only if

if and only if

p
is the geometric multiplicity of
i\omega
as an eigenvalue of
P(\lambda)
. Observe that

. Hence

and

form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{N}(P(i\omega))
.

By construction, we have
X^{*}MX=I_{n}
. Note that
DX_{2}=0
and
\omega^{2}MX_{2}=KX_{2}

then yields that

Since the geometric multiplicity of

Theorem
18
shows the effect of the semidefinite damping matrix on the purely imaginary eigenvalues of
P_{0}(\lambda)
. Observe that for any semidefinite damping

, we have
\Lambda(P)\cap i{\mathbb{R}}\subset\Lambda(P_{0})\cap i{\mathbb{R}}
. This means that the purely imaginary eigenvalues of

) that remain stationary in

after the damping

Furthermore, when the damping

is applied, an eigenvalue

(counting multiplicity) will leave the imaginary axis if and only if

. Thus, if

\Lambda(P_{0})=\{\pm i\omega_{1},\ldots,\pm i\omega_{\ell}\}
, then
all the undamped eigenvalues will leave the imaginary axis when the damping

is applied if and only if

\mathrm{rank}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{0}(i\omega_{j})&D\end{array}\right]\right)=n
for

. In particular, if the damping matrix

have negative real part when

We now analyze in more detail how to construct damping matrices
D
that remove undamped eigenvalues from the imaginary axis. Consider
P_{0}(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+K
with
M>0
and
K>0
. Then for any positive semidefinite damping matrix
D
and

, we have
\Lambda(P)\cap i{\mathbb{R}}\subset\Lambda(P_{0})
.
More precisely, let

i\omega\in\Lambda(P_{0})
with
\omega\neq 0
, then by Theorem
18
there are three possible cases:

(a)

\pm i\omega\notin\Lambda(P)
or

(b)

\pm i\omega\in\Lambda(P)
and
\mathcal{N}(P(i\omega))\subsetneqq\mathcal{N}(P_{0}(i\omega))
or

(c)

\pm i\omega\in\Lambda(P)
and
\mathcal{N}(P(i\omega))=\mathcal{N}(P_{0}(i\omega))

In case (a), the damping

removes
\pm i\omega
from the imaginary axis. In case (b),

is a multiple eigenvalue and the damping

removes a few copies of the eigenvalue
\pm i\omega
(counting multiplicity) from the imaginary axis and a few copies of
\pm i\omega
remain purely imaginary with reduced geometric multiplicity. Finally, in case (c),
\pm i\omega
remains stationary and is completely unaffected by the damping

.

Our next result shows that if
i\omega
is an undamped eigenvalue of geometric multiplicity

then a semidefinite damping matrix that removes

completely from the imaginary axis must have rank at least

.

Theorem 20

Consider a matrix polynomial
P(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+\lambda D+K
, where
0<M=M^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
,
0\leq D=D^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
, and
0\leq K=K^{*}\in\mathbb{C}^{n,n}
. Let
P_{0}(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+K
and let
i\omega\in\Lambda(P)
with geometric multiplicity
p
. Consider the QR factorization
Q^{*}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{0}(i\omega)&D\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}R\\
0_{p,{2n}}\end{array}\right]
, where
R
has full row rank and
Q:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Q_{1}&Q_{2}\end{array}\right]
is unitary with
Q_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,p}.

Then the columns of
Q_{2}
span
\mathcal{N}(P(i\omega))
.

Let
0<T=T^{*}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{p,p}
. Consider the family of matrix polynomials
\widehat{P}_{t}(\lambda):=P(\lambda)+\lambda\,tMQ_{2}TQ^{*}_{2}M
for all
t>0
. Then there exists a nonsingular matrix
X:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X_{1}&X_{2}\end{array}\right]
with
X_{2}:=Q_{2}(Q_{2}^{*}MQ_{2})^{-1/2}
such that for all
t>0
,

\displaystyle X^{*}P(\lambda)X

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\widetilde{P}(\lambda)&\\
\hline\cr&(\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2})I_{p}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle X^{*}\widehat{P}_{t}(\lambda)X

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\widetilde{P}(\lambda)&\\
\hline\cr&\lambda^{2}I_{p}+\lambda\,t\widehat{T}+\omega^{2}I_{p}\end{array}\right],

where
\widehat{T}:=(Q_{2}^{*}MQ_{2})^{1/2}T(Q_{2}^{*}MQ_{2})^{1/2}
and
\widetilde{P}(\lambda):=X_{1}^{*}P(\lambda)X_{1}
.
Furthermore, we have
\Lambda(\widetilde{P})=\Lambda(P)\setminus\{\pm i\omega\}
and the eigenvalues of
\lambda^{2}I_{p}+\lambda\,t\widehat{T}+\omega^{2}I_{p}
have negative real parts for all
t>0
. Thus, in the damped system
\widehat{P}_{t}(\lambda)
, for all
t>0
,
\pm i\omega\notin\Lambda(\widehat{P}_{t})
.

Let
W
be an
n\times m
matrix such that
\mathrm{rank}(W)=m<p
. Then for any
t>0
the damped system
\widehat{P}_{t}(\lambda):=P(\lambda)+\lambda\,tWW^{*}

has undamped eigenvalues
\pm i\omega
, i.e.,
\pm i\omega\in\Lambda(\widehat{P}_{t})
for all
t>0
.

Proof
. Set
\widehat{D}:=MQ_{2}TQ_{2}^{*}M=MX_{2}\widehat{T}X_{2}^{*}M
. Note that the columns of
X_{2}
form an
M
-orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{N}(P(i\omega))
. For the given
X_{2}
construct
X_{1}
as in Theorem
18
(b), so that by construction
X^{*}MX=I_{n}
. We have
DX_{2}=0
and
\omega^{2}MX_{2}=KX_{2}
which together with
X^{*}MX=I_{n}
yields
X_{1}^{*}P(\lambda)X_{2}=0
and
X_{2}^{*}P(\lambda)X_{2}=(\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2})I_{p}
. Next, observe that
\widehat{D}X_{1}=0
and
X_{2}^{*}\widehat{D}X_{2}=\widehat{T}
. Hence by Theorem
18
, for all
t>0
we have

\displaystyle X^{*}P(\lambda)X

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\widetilde{P}(\lambda)&\\
\hline\cr&(\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2})I_{p}\end{array}\right],

\displaystyle X^{*}\widehat{P}_{t}(\lambda)X

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\widetilde{P}(\lambda)&\\
\hline\cr&\lambda^{2}I_{p}+\lambda\,t\widehat{T}+\omega^{2}I_{p}\end{array}\right]

and
\Lambda(\widetilde{P})=\Lambda(P)\setminus\{\pm i\omega\}
. Since
\widehat{T}
is positive definite, again by Theorem
18
, the eigenvalues of
\lambda^{2}I_{p}+\lambda\,t\widehat{T}+\omega^{2}I_{p}
lie in the open left half complex plane.

Finally, set
\widehat{D}_{t}:=D+tWW^{*}
. Then
W=Q_{1}Y_{1}+Q_{2}Y_{2}
for some
(n-p)\times m
matrix
Y_{1}
and
p\times m
matrix
Y_{2}
. Since
m<p
, there exists a nonzero vector
u
such that
u^{*}Y_{2}=0
. With
v:=Q_{2}u
, then
v^{*}W=u^{*}Y_{2}=0
. Consequently,
v^{*}\widehat{D}_{t}=v^{*}(D+tWW^{*})=0
and
P_{0}(i\omega)v=0
which shows that

\mathrm{rank}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{0}(i\omega)&\widehat{D}_{t}\end{array}\right]\right)<n
for all
t>0
. Hence by Theorem
18
, we have
\pm i\omega\in\Lambda(\widehat{P}_{t})
for all
t>0
.

Remark 21

Proposition 23

Let

, where
0<M=M^{*},\ 0<K=K^{*}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}

X
be

-unitary such that

, where

u_{j}\neq 0

. If all the eigenvalues of

, the eigenvalues of

have negative real part.

Proof
. Note that the columns of
X
are eigenvectors of
P_{0}(\lambda)
. Let
x_{j}
be the
j
-th column of
X
, that is,
x_{j}:=Xe_{j}
. Then
P_{0}(i\omega_{j})x_{j}=0
for
j=1,\ldots,n
.
Since

is simple,
\pm i\omega_{j}\in\Lambda(P_{t})
if and only if
tvv^{*}x_{j}=0
if and only if
v^{*}x_{j}=0
. Now
v^{*}x_{j}=u^{*}X^{*}Mx_{j}=u^{*}X^{*}MXe_{j}=u_{j}\neq 0
. This shows that
tvv^{*}x_{j}\neq 0
for
j=1,\ldots,n
and
t>0
. Since
\Lambda(P_{t})\cap i{\mathbb{R}}\subset\Lambda(P_{0})
for all

have negative real part.

The damping matrix
\widehat{D}:=MQ_{2}TQ_{2}^{*}M
in Theorem

removes the imaginary eigenvalues
\pm i\omega
of

from the imaginary axis leaving the other eigenvalues of

unchanged. On the other hand, the damping matrix

can also be used to remove the eigenvalues

from the imaginary axis even though a decomposition of

Proposition 24

Consider
P(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+\lambda D+K
and
P_{0}(\lambda)=\lambda^{2}M+K
, where
0<M=M^{*},\ 0\leq D=D^{*},\ 0\leq K=K^{*}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}
. Let
i\omega\in\Lambda(P)
have geometric multiplicity
p
and let
Q_{2}
and
T
be as in Theorem
20
. Consider
\widehat{P}_{t}(\lambda):=P(\lambda)+\lambda\,tQ_{2}TQ^{*}_{2}
. Then for all

,
\pm i\omega\notin\Lambda(\widehat{P}_{t})
.

Proof
. Set
\widehat{D}_{t}:=D+tQ_{2}TQ_{2}^{*}.

It is easy to see that
\mathcal{N}(\widehat{P}_{t}(i\omega))\subset\mathcal{N}(P(i\omega))
for
t>0
. Indeed, by Theorem
18
, we have
\mathcal{N}(\widehat{P}_{t}(i\omega))=\mathcal{N}(P_{0}(i\omega))\cap\mathcal{N}(\widehat{D}_{t})
. Since

and

, we have that

if and only if
Dx+t(Q_{2}TQ_{2}^{*})x=0

and
Q_{2}TQ_{2}^{*}x=0
. Hence
\mathcal{N}(\widehat{P}_{t}(i\omega))\subset\mathcal{N}(P(i\omega))

Note that the columns of
Q_{2}
form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{N}(P(i\omega))
and
DQ_{2}=0
. Let

. Then

for some

. Now
\widehat{D}_{t}x=DQ_{2}y+tQ_{2}Ty=tQ_{2}Ty
. Hence
\widehat{D}_{t}x=0
implies that
Q_{2}Ty=0
and thus

and

. This shows that
x^{*}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{0}(i\omega)&\widehat{D}_{t}\end{array}\right]=0
implies that

. Hence, we have that
\mathrm{rank}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{0}(i\omega)&\widehat{D}_{t}\end{array}\right]\right)=n
for all

\hline\cr&\widetilde{P}_{0}(\lambda)\end{array}\right]\,\mbox{ for all }\,t>0,

\widetilde{P}_{0}(\lambda):=\mathrm{diag}((\lambda^{2}+\omega_{1}^{2})I_{p_{1}},\,\cdots,\,(\lambda^{2}+\omega_{\ell}^{2})I_{p_{\ell}})
and
\widetilde{P}_{t}(\lambda):=X_{1}^{*}P_{t}(\lambda)X_{1}
.
Furthermore, all eigenvalues of
\widetilde{P}_{t}(\lambda)
have negative real parts,
\Lambda(\widetilde{P}_{t})=\Lambda(P_{t})\setminus\sigma_{0}
and
\Lambda(\widetilde{P}_{0})=\sigma_{0}
for all
t>0
. Thus the geometric multiplicity of a purely imaginary eigenvalue of
P_{t}(\lambda)
remains the same for all
t>0
.

Proof
. Note that
\mathrm{rank}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{0}(i\omega)&tD\end{array}\right]\right)=\mathrm{rank}\left(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{0}(i\omega)&D\end{array}\right]\right)
for all
t>0
. Hence the conclusions in (a) follow. The matrix
X
as constructed in Theorem
18
depends on an
M
-orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{N}(P_{1}(i\omega_{j}))
. Since
\mathcal{N}(P_{1}(i\omega_{j}))=\mathcal{N}(P_{t}(i\omega_{j}))
for all

.

4.1
Numerical methods for the deflation of purely imaginary eigenvalues.

Let
0<M,\ 0\leq K,\ D=D\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,n}
with
D
semidefinite. Consider
P_{0}(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+K
and
P(\lambda):=\lambda^{2}M+\lambda D+K.
We describe two numerical methods for deflating the purely imaginary eigenvalues of
P(\lambda)
. The first method is based on an
M
-unitary QR factorization.

Let
i\omega\in\Lambda(P)
and
\omega\neq 0
. For
m:=\mathrm{rank}(\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{0}(i\omega)&D\end{array}\right])<n
, set
p:=n-m
. Consider a rank revealing QR factorization

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{0}(i\omega)&D\end{array}\right]P=Q\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}R_{11}&R_{12}\\
0&0\end{array}\right],

where
Q^{*}Q=I_{n}
,
R_{11}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{m,m}
is nonsingular and
P\in{\mathbb{C}}^{2n,2n}
is a permutation matrix. Partition
Q=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}Q_{1}&Q_{2}\end{array}\right]
, where
Q_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,m}
are the leading columns of
Q
and set
X_{2}:=Q_{2}
. Then it follows that
Q_{2}^{*}\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}P_{0}(i\omega)&D\end{array}\right]=0
which shows that
\mathrm{span}(Q_{2})=\mathcal{N}(D)\cap\mathcal{N}(P_{0}(i\omega))
.

Next, compute a Cholesky factorization
Q_{2}^{*}MQ_{2}=C_{2}C_{2}^{*}
and define

X_{2}:=Q_{2}(C_{2}^{*})^{-1}
by solving the linear system
X_{2}C_{2}^{*}=Q_{2}
for
X_{2}
.
Then we have
X_{2}^{*}MX_{2}=C_{2}^{-1}Q_{2}2^{*}MQ_{2}C_{2}^{-*}=C_{2}^{-1}C_{2}C_{2}^{*}C_{2}^{-*}=I_{p}
and
\mathrm{span}(X_{2})=\mathrm{span}(Q_{2})=\mathcal{N}(D)\cap\mathcal{N}(P_{0}(i\omega))
. Note that
P_{0}(i\omega)X_{2}=0
which implies that
\omega^{2}MX_{2}=KX_{2}
which in turn shows that
X_{2}^{*}KX_{2}=\omega^{2}I_{p}
. Since
DX_{2}=0
, we have

X_{2}^{*}P(\lambda)X_{2}=\lambda^{2}X_{2}^{*}MX_{2}+\lambda X_{2}^{*}DX_{2}+X_{2}^{*}KX_{2}=(\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2})I_{p}.

Next, compute a QR factorization
MX_{2}=U\left[\begin{array}[]{c}R\\
0\end{array}\right]
, where
R\in{\mathbb{C}}^{p,p}
is nonsingular. Partition
U=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}U_{1}&U_{2}\end{array}\right]
, where
U_{1}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n,p}
.
Compute a Cholesky factorization
Q_{2}^{*}MQ_{2}=C_{3}C_{3}^{*}
and define
X_{1}:=U_{2}(C_{3}^{*})^{-1}
by solving the linear system
X_{1}C_{3}^{*}=U_{2}
for
X_{1}
. Then
X_{1}^{*}MX_{1}=C_{3}^{-1}U_{2}^{*}MU_{2}(C_{3}^{*})^{-1}=C_{3}^{-1}C_{3}C_{3}^{*}(C_{3}^{*})^{-1}=I_{m}
and
X_{1}^{*}MX_{2}=0
. Since
\omega^{2}MX_{2}=KX_{2}
, it follows that
X_{1}^{*}KX_{2}=0
.

Then, setting
X:=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X_{1}&X_{2}\end{array}\right]
, by construction we have
X^{*}MX=I_{n}
and

X^{*}P(\lambda)X=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X_{1}^{*}P(\lambda)X_{1}&X_{1}^{*}P(\lambda)X_{2}\\
X_{2}^{*}P(\lambda)X_{1}&X_{2}^{*}P(\lambda)X_{2}\end{array}\right]=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}X_{1}^{*}P(\lambda)X_{1}&0\\
0&(\lambda^{2}+\omega^{2})I_{p}\end{array}\right].

Since
p
is the geometric multiplicity of
i\omega,
it follows that
\pm i\omega
is not an eigenvalue of
\widehat{P}(\lambda):=X_{1}^{*}P(\lambda)X_{1}
.

This construction leads to the following algorithm for the deflation of the eigenvalue
\pm i\omega
.

4.2
A numerical example

To illustrate our results, we construct a second-order linear mechanical system of the form

M\ddot{x}(t)+D\dot{x}(t)+Kx(t)=0,

where
M,D,K\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
are the
mass
,
damping
, and
stiffness
matrices, respectively, see
[
28
]
.

In particular, we choose
n=10
, the mass matrix
M=I_{n}
, and
p=2
modal vectors to create
two purely oscillatory modes
, leading to eigenvalues
\pm i\omega_{1}
and
\pm i\omega_{2}
, where
\omega_{1}=5
and
\omega_{2}=7
.

We then construct a real orthonormal matrix
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, whose columns span the desired modal subspace. Based on this, we define the initial stiffness matrix

K_{0}=X_{0}\,\mathrm{diag}(\omega_{1}^{2},\omega_{2}^{2})\,X_{0}^{T}.

To form the complete stiffness matrix
K
of rank
r=6<n
, we add a low-rank correction

K_{1}=GG^{T},

where
G\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r-p)}
and the columns of
G
are orthogonal to those of
X_{0}
. Then

K=K_{0}+K_{1},

is a symmetric, positive semidefinite matrix of the given rank
r
.

The corresponding quadratic eigenvalue problem has two pairs of purely imaginary eigenvalues,
\pm 7i
and
\pm 5i
, which are computed via
polyeig(K, D, M)
in
Matlab

as

\{2.9424\times 10^{-15}+7i,\ 2.9424\times 10^{-15}-7i,\ -2.5853\times 10^{-15}+5i,\ -2.5853\times 10^{-15}-5i\},

along with two further (numerically zero) eigenvalues

\{1.9334\times 10^{-15}+0i,\ -1.1545\times 10^{-16}+0i\}.

To deflate the pair
\pm i\omega_{1}=\pm 5i
, we apply Algorithm
3
and obtain a reduced system
\widehat{M},\widehat{D},\widehat{K}
, with eigenvalues

\displaystyle-3.27\!\times\!10^{-15}\pm 7i,\quad-0.63,\ -0.43,

\displaystyle-0.13\pm 0.99i,\quad-0.19\pm 0.97i,\quad-0.49\pm 0.87i,

\displaystyle-0.44\pm 0.90i,\quad-0.29\pm 0.95i,\quad-0.33\pm 0.94i,

\displaystyle 1.49\!\times\!10^{-15},\ -3.17\!\times\!10^{-15}.

while with Algorithm
4
we obtain the eigenvalues

\displaystyle 6.13\!\times\!10^{-15}\pm 7i,\quad-0.63,\ -0.43,

\displaystyle-0.13\pm 0.99i,\quad-0.19\pm 0.97i,\quad-0.49\pm 0.87i,

\displaystyle-0.44\pm 0.90i,\quad-0.29\pm 0.95i,\quad-0.33\pm 0.94i,

\displaystyle 2.32\!\times\!10^{-16},\ -1.84\!\times\!10^{-15}.

These results confirm that the purely imaginary pair
\pm 7i
is preserved in both reduced systems, while the pair
\pm 5i
has been successfully removed.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we have proposed a trimmed linearization of a quadratic matrix polynomial
arising from a damped mass-spring-system,
in which the eigenvalues of
P
at infinity, resp., zero and also the other purely imaginary eigenvalues are deflated. Also,
we have presented results on how one can assess whether the problem is hyperbolic or not, and what is the inertia of the hyperbolic eigenvalue problem.
Finally, we thoroughly described the movement of purely imaginary eigenvalues under the influence of semidefinite parametric damping matrices.
