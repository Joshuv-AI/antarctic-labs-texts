Complete asymptotics in the formation of quiescent big bang singularities
Andrés Franco-Grisales and Hans Ringström

Abstract
. There are three categories of mathematical results concerning quiescent big bang singularities: the derivation of asymptotics in a symmetry class; the construction of spacetimes given initial data on the singularity; and the proof of big bang formation in the absence of symmetries, including the proof of stable big bang formation. In a recent article, the first author demonstrated the existence of developments corresponding to a geometric notion of initial data on a big bang singularity. Moreover, this article, combined with previous articles by the second author, gives a unified and geometric perspective on large classes of seemingly disparate results in the first two categories. Concerning the third category, Oude Groeniger et al recently formulated a general condition on initial data ensuring big bang formation, including curvature blow up. This result, among other things, generalises previous results on stable big bang formation. However, it does not include a statement saying that the solutions induce initial data on the singularity. Here we tie all three categories of results together by demonstrating that the solutions of Oude Groeniger et al induce data on the singularity. However, the results are more general and can potentially be used to derive similar conclusions in other gauges.



1.
Introduction

The subject of this article is quiescent big bang singularities. To put this topic into context, it is natural to return to the work of
Belinskiǐ, Khalatnikov and Lifschitz (BKL), see, e.g.,
[
11
,
12
]
as well as more modern refinements, e.g.,
[
16
,
17
,
25
,
26
]
.
Very crudely, the idea is to study the asymptotics in the direction of a big bang singularity using a foliation by spacelike hypersurfaces, the
mean curvatures of which diverge; i.e., if
K
denotes the Weingarten map of the leaves of the foliation,
\theta:=\mathrm{tr}K
diverges. In order
to extract a finite limit, it is therefore natural to expansion normalise by dividing by the
mean curvature:
\mathcal{K}:=K/\theta
. We refer to
\mathcal{K}
as
the expansion normalised Weingarten map
. One suggestion of BKL is that, along a
causal curve going into the singularity, the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}
should oscillate according to a chaotic one-dimensional map referred to as
the
Kasner map
or
BKL map
. This is referred to as the oscillatory setting. However, for certain matter models and for
certain symmetry classes, the oscillations can be neutralised and the resulting behaviour is
quiescent
in the sense that the eigenvalues
converge. Oscillatory solutions are very interesting, but, to the best of our knowledge, there are only results in the spatially homogeneous
setting; see, e.g.,
[
54
,
36
,
37
,
6
,
31
,
32
,
13
,
7
]
. There is a vast literature on spatially homogeneous solutions, and
most of the results concern the quiescent setting. There are also some results in the case of
\mathbb{T}^{3}
-Gowdy symmetry; see, e.g.,

[
14
,
38
,
40
]
. However, going beyond these results has turned out to be difficult. One way to avoid this difficulty is to
turn the problem around, to start with “data at the singularity” and to prove that there are corresponding solutions. Over the last 20–30
years, a substantial body of work on this topic has appeared; see, e.g.,
[
29
,
27
,
34
,
4
,
28
,
15
,
53
,
1
,
30
,
2
,
21
,
5
]
.
Unfortunately, these results have certain deficiencies. One deficiency is that many of the results are in the real analytic setting, a regularity
class incompatible with finite speed of propagation, a central notion in general relativity. A second deficiency is that the notions of initial
data in the different results are strongly connected to the particular choice of gauge. It is therefore not easy to see how the conditions of the
different articles relate. Finally, viewing the constructed spacetimes as solutions to the initial value problem given initial data on the
singularity, it would be highly desirable to have a geometric formulation of the initial value problem; isometric data on the singularity should give
rise to isometric developments etc. In
[
45
]
, an article based on
[
42
,
44
]
, the second author developed such a geometric
notion of initial data. In
[
23
]
, the first author demonstrated that this notion of initial data can be used to formulate a geometric
initial value problem for the Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations with data on the singularity. In particular, given initial data there is,
up to isometry, a unique corresponding development, and isometric initial data on the singularity give rise to isometric developments. We expect
this result to give a unified perspective on the results starting with initial data on the singularity. In the spatially homogeneous Bianchi class
A setting, this is verified in
[
46
,
47
]
. In the
\mathbb{T}^{3}
–Gowdy setting, it is verified in
[
46
]
. In particular, this
means that the notion of initial data used in
[
29
,
34
]
is a special case of the initial data used in
[
45
,
23
]
. That the notion
of initial data used in
[
4
]
is a special case of the notion introduced in
[
45
]
is verified in
[
45
]
. That the notion
of initial data used in
[
30
,
21
,
5
]
is a special case of the notion introduced in
[
45
]
is also verified in
[
45
]
.

Studying the stability of big bang formation is an alternative approach which has been very active in the last 5–10 years. The results
began with the work of Rodnianski and Speck, demonstrating stability of spatially homogeneous, isotropic and spatially flat solutions in the
Einstein-scalar field and the Einstein-stiff fluid setting; see
[
49
,
50
]
. Speck demonstrates a similar result in the case of

\mathbb{S}^{3}
-spatial topology; see
[
52
]
. In
[
51
]
, the authors consider the vacuum equations in higher dimensions in situations
with intermediate anisotropy; the conditions are more general than requiring proximity to isotropy, but they are still quite far from covering
the full subcritical range in which stability is expected. In
[
19
]
, Fajman and Urban prove a stability result in the case of spatial
local homogeneity and isotropy and negative spatial curvature. This result was later generalised to allow the presence of Vlasov
matter and any sign of the sectional curvature in
[
20
]
. Finally, Fournodavlos, Rodnianski and Speck demonstrate stable big bang formation
for the full subcritical range of Kasner-scalar field spacetimes in
[
22
]
. This is a very important result for many reasons. First, because
it covers the maximal range in which one can obtain stability. Second, because the authors introduce a new formulation of the equations using a
Fermi-Walker propagated frame which has turned out to be very fruitful. The extension of this result to stability of the big
bang singularity for the Einstein-Maxwell-scalar field-Vlasov system in the full strong subcritical range is discussed in
[
3
]
.

All of the stability results mentioned so far
are obtained using constant mean curvature (CMC) foliations. Such foliations are natural for several reasons. To begin with, they are uniquely
determined by the spacetime for large classes of matter models. Moreover, they synchronise the singularity, something which is not to be
expected for most gauges. On the other hand, CMC foliations have deficiencies. In particular, the corresponding gauge fixed system
includes elliptic equations. This means that it is not possible to localise the analysis in space. This is contrary to the expected spatial
localisation of the causal structure. Moreover, thinking ahead, going beyond stability of spatially homogeneous solutions, quite violent
spatial variations are to be expected; this
can be seen already in the spikes appearing in the
\mathbb{T}^{3}
-Gowdy symmetric setting, see, e.g.,
[
35
]
. In order to obtain a clear picture
of the behaviour of solutions near the big bang, it can therefore be expected to be necessary to localise the analysis. In the spatially
homogeneous, isotropic and spatially flat setting, localisation is achieved by Beyer and Oliynyk in
[
8
]
, a result which is generalised
to include a fluid in
[
9
]
. This requires the development of a new, local, gauge. The authors achieve this by using the scalar field
as a time coordinate. In
[
10
]
, Beyer, Oliynyk and Zheng generalise the result to cover the full subcritical range in
3
-dimensions,
a result which is generalised to higher dimensions in
[
55
]
. As in
[
22
]
, the authors use a Fermi-Walker transported frame in order
to obtain their results.

In spite of the importance of the above results, they have one fundamental weakness, namely that they only demonstrate stability of specific
spatially locally homogeneous solutions. Due to the ideas of BKL and the results
[
4
,
23
]
concerning data on the singularity, it
is natural to expect that solutions should exhibit quiescent asymptotics more generally. Moreover, due to the perspective developed in

[
42
,
44
,
45
]
, it is natural to expect that expansion normalised initial data should play an important role in phrasing the corresponding
condition. In fact, in
[
33
]
, Oude Groeniger, Petersen and the second author provide a general condition on initial data, without any
reference to a background solution (spatially homogeneous or otherwise) such that the corresponding solutions exhibit big bang formation with a
crushing CMC foliation and curvature blow up. Moreover, the result gives partial asymptotics, corresponding to some of the data on the singularity,
as formulated in
[
45
]
or
[
23
]
. In addition, the results of
[
33
]
not only imply the previous stability results in the
Einstein–scalar field setting with CMC foliations, they substantially extend them. Since
[
33
]
concerns the Einstein–nonlinear scalar field
setting, the results also yield future and past global nonlinear stability results for large classes of spatially locally homogeneous solutions.

Considering the above circle of ideas, the only thing missing to tie the results given data on the singularity together with the stability results
is a proof that the solutions constructed in
[
33
]
induce data on the singularity in the sense of
[
45
]
or
[
23
]
. In this article,
we provide such a proof. Moreover, we do so by providing tools we expect to be applicable more generally. More specifically, we prove that on the
basis of appropriate
C^{2}
-assumptions concerning solutions to the FRS equations used in
[
33
]
, it is possible to deduce
C^{k}
-conclusions for
any
k
. We also prove that, given an appropriate foliation (not necessarily a CMC foliation) and appropriate assumptions concerning a Fermi-Walker
propagated orthonormal frame of the leaves of the foliation as well as concerning basic geometric quantities expressed with respect this Fermi-Walker
propagated orthonormal frame, it follows that the solution induces data on the singularity in the sense of
[
45
]
or
[
23
]
.

1.1.
The Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations

In this article, we are interested in the Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations. They are determined once we specify a

potential
; i.e., a function
V\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. A solution consists of a time oriented Lorentz manifold
(M,g)
, i.e. a
spacetime
,
and a function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(M)
, referred to as the
scalar field
, satisfying

(1a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Ric}_{g}-\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{Scal}_{g}g

\displaystyle=T,

(1b)

\displaystyle\Box_{g}\varphi

\displaystyle=V^{\prime}\circ\varphi.

Here
\mathrm{Ric}_{g}
and
\mathrm{Scal}_{g}
denote the Ricci and scalar curvatures of
g
respectively,
\Box_{g}
is the wave operator associated with
g
, and

(2)

T={\mathrm{d}}\varphi\otimes{\mathrm{d}}\varphi-\big(\tfrac{1}{2}|{\mathrm{d}}\varphi|_{g}^{2}+V\circ\varphi\big)g

is the energy-momentum tensor of
\varphi
. Note that adding a cosmological constant term to the left hand side of (
1a
) would not
make the system more general, as the cosmological constant can be absorbed in the potential.

There is a Cauchy problem associated with (
1
).

Definition 1
.

Initial data for the Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations
(
1
) consist of a Riemannian manifold
(\Sigma,h)
, a symmetric
covariant
2
-tensor field
k
and two smooth functions
\phi
and
\psi
satisfying

(3a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Scal}_{h}-|k|^{2}_{h}+(\mathrm{tr}_{h}k)^{2}

\displaystyle=\psi^{2}+|{\mathrm{d}}\phi|_{h}^{2}+2V\circ\phi,

(3b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}_{h}k-{\mathrm{d}}\mathrm{tr}_{h}k

\displaystyle=\psi{\mathrm{d}}\phi.

If
\theta:=\mathrm{tr}_{h}k
is constant, the initial data are said to be
constant mean curvature
(CMC) initial data.
Given initial data for the Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations, say
(\Sigma,h,k,\phi,\psi)
a corresponding
development
is a
solution
(M,g,\varphi)
to (
1
), and an embedding
i:\Sigma\rightarrow M
such that the following hold. First,
i^{*}g=h
, so that,
in particular,
i(\Sigma)
is a spacelike hypersurface in
(M,g)
. Second, if
\bar{k}
is the second fundamental form induced on
i(\Sigma)
and
U
is the
future directed unit normal to
i(\Sigma)
, then

i^{*}\bar{k}=k,\ \ \ \varphi\circ i=\phi,\ \ \ (U\varphi)\circ i=\psi.

Remark 2
.

Equation (
3a
) is called the
Hamiltonian constraint equation
and (
3b
) is called the

momentum constraint equation
.

Remark 3
.

Due to arguments going back to the work of Yvonne Choquet-Bruhat and Robert Geroch
[
24
]
, there is a unique maximal globally hyperbolic
development associated with initial data; see also
[
41
, Chapter 23]
for a textbook presentation.

Remark 4
.

Let
(M,g,\varphi)
be a solution to (
1
) and let
\Sigma
be a spacelike hypersurface in
(M,g)
with future directed unit normal
U
.
Define
h
and
k
to be the associated induced first and second fundamental forms. Let, moreover,
\phi:=\varphi|_{\Sigma}
and

\psi:=U(\varphi)|_{\Sigma}
. Then
(\Sigma,h,k,\phi,\psi)
are initial data for the Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations; see, e.g.,

[
39
, Chapter 13]
.

Given initial data such that the associated mean curvature is strictly positive, it is possible to define associated expansion normalised initial
data, a concept which is of central importance in the definition of initial data on the singularity.

Definition 5
.

Let
(\Sigma,h,k,\phi,\psi)
be initial data for the Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations and
\theta:=\mathrm{tr}_{h}k
. Assume that
\theta>0
.
Let
K
be the
(1,1)
-tensor field obtained by raising one index in
k
using
h
. Then
\mathcal{K}:=\theta^{-1}K
is called the

expansion normalised Weingarten map
. Define

\mathcal{H}(X,Y):=h(\theta^{\mathcal{K}}X,\theta^{\mathcal{K}}Y),\ \ \ \Psi:=\theta^{-1}\psi,\ \ \ \Phi:=\phi+\Psi\ln\theta,

for all
X,Y\in T_{p}\Sigma
and all
p\in\Sigma
, where

\theta^{\mathcal{K}}X:=\textstyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\tfrac{(\ln\theta)^{m}}{m!}\mathcal{K}^{m}X.

Then
(\Sigma,\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K},\Phi,\Psi)
are referred to as
expansion normalised initial data
associated with the initial data
(\Sigma,h,k,\phi,\psi)
.

1.2.
Initial data on the singularity

Next, we define the notion of initial data on the singularity.

Definition 6
.

For integers
\ell\geq 1
and
n\geq 3
, let
(\Sigma,\mathring{\mathcal{H}})
be an
n
-dimensional
C^{\ell}
Riemannian manifold,
\mathring{\mathcal{K}}
a
C^{\ell}

(1,1)
-tensor field on
\Sigma
and
\mathring{\Phi},\mathring{\Psi}\in C^{\ell}(\Sigma)
. Assume that

(1)

\mathrm{tr}\mathring{\mathcal{K}}=1
and
\mathring{\mathcal{K}}
is self adjoint with respect to
\mathring{\mathcal{H}}
.

(2)

\mathrm{tr}\mathring{\mathcal{K}}^{2}+\mathring{\Psi}^{2}=1
and
\mathrm{div}_{\mathring{\mathcal{H}}}\mathring{\mathcal{K}}=\mathring{\Psi}{\mathrm{d}}\mathring{\Phi}
.

(3)

The eigenvalues
\mathring{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathring{p}_{n}
of
\mathring{\mathcal{K}}
are everywhere distinct, and satisfy

(4)

\mathring{p}_{I}+\mathring{p}_{J}-\mathring{p}_{K}<1

for all
I,J
and
K
such that
I\neq J
.

Then
(\Sigma,\mathring{\mathcal{H}},\mathring{\mathcal{K}},\mathring{\Phi},\mathring{\Psi})
are
C^{\ell}

robust nondegenerate quiescent initial data on the singularity for the
Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations
. If they are
C^{\ell}
for any
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, they are said to be smooth.

Remark 7
.

It is possible to omit the nondegeneracy requirement; i.e., the requirement that the eigenvalues be distinct. However, we are mainly
interested in the nondegenerate setting in this article. It is also possible to omit the requirement that (
4
)
hold. However, it is then necessary to introduce separate conditions on the structure coefficients associated with an eigenframe of
\mathring{\mathcal{K}}
;
see, e.g.,
[
45
, Definition 10, p. 9]
. Moreover, the solutions inducing initial data violating (
4
) are
expected to be unstable. Since we are here interested in the asymptotics induced by solutions arising in stability results, such a
generalisation is therefore not of great interest here.

In order to relate a solution as in Subsection
1.1
to initial data on the singularity in the sense of
Definition
6
, we first need to introduce a foliation. Let
(M,g,\varphi)
be a globally hyperbolic solution to the Einstein–nonlinear
scalar field equations. Then
M
is diffeomorphic to
\Sigma\times\mathcal{I}
for some
n
-manifold
\Sigma
and some open interval
\mathcal{I}=(t_{-},t_{+})
, where

\Sigma_{t}:=\Sigma\times\{t\}
are spacelike Cauchy hypersurfaces. When speaking of big bang singularities, we have
crushing singularities in mind. In other words, we here assume the mean curvature of the leaves
\Sigma_{t}
of the foliation to diverge uniformly to

\infty
as
t\downarrow t_{-}
. The metric
g
and scalar field
\varphi
induce initial data, cf. Remark
4
, on each
leaf:
(\Sigma,h(t),k(t),\phi(t),\psi(t))
; we think of
the data as being defined on
\Sigma
. Moreover, these data induce expansion normalised initital data
(\Sigma,\mathcal{H}(t),\mathcal{K}(t),\Phi(t),\Psi(t))
as explained
in Definition
5
. We say that the solution induces initial data on the singularity along the foliation
\{\Sigma_{t}\}_{t\in\mathcal{I}}

if there are
t_{0}\in\mathcal{I}
; constants
\delta>0
and
C_{\ell}
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
; and robust quiescent initial data on the singularity for the
Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations, say
(\Sigma,\mathring{\mathcal{H}},\mathring{\mathcal{K}},\mathring{\Phi},\mathring{\Psi})
, such that

(5a)

\displaystyle\|\theta^{\delta}\bar{D}^{\ell}(\mathcal{H}(t)-\mathring{\mathcal{H}})\|_{C^{0}(\Sigma)}+\|\theta^{\delta}\bar{D}^{\ell}(\mathcal{K}(t)-\mathring{\mathcal{K}})\|_{C^{0}(\Sigma)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\ell},

(5b)

\displaystyle\|\theta^{\delta}\bar{D}^{\ell}(\Phi(t)-\mathring{\Phi})\|_{C^{0}(\Sigma)}+\|\theta^{\delta}\bar{D}^{\ell}(\Psi(t)-\mathring{\Psi})\|_{C^{0}(\Sigma)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{\ell}

for all
t\leq t_{0}
and all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Here
\bar{D}
is the Levi-Civita connection associated with a fixed reference Riemannian metric on
\Sigma
,
say
h_{\mathrm{ref}}
, and the
C^{0}
-norm of a tensor field
T
is defined as follows:

\|T\|_{C^{0}(\Sigma)}:=\sup_{x\in\Sigma}|T(x)|_{h_{\mathrm{ref}}}.

In case
\Sigma
is non-compact, one could relax (
5
) to the requirement that for each compact subset
K
of
\Sigma
, there are
constants
\delta
and
C_{\ell}
such that (
5
) holds with
\Sigma
replaced by
K
.

In
3+1
-dimensions, the step from data on the singularity to corresponding developments is discussed in
[
23
]
. In particular, due
to
[
23
, Theorem 1.21, p. 10]
, initial data on the singularity give rise to unique (up to isometry) maximal globally hyperbolic
developments with a local Gaussian foliation close to the singularity. Next, we turn to the opposite direction. In particular, we prove that
the general condition on initial data introduced in
[
33
]
, ensuring that the corresponding maximal globally hyperbolic developments
have crushing CMC singularities with curvature blow up, in fact yields solutions inducing data on the singularity along the CMC foliation.

1.3.
The main result

In this subsection, we state the main result, namely that the solutions constructed in
[
33
]
induce data on the
singularity. However, to begin with, we need to introduce some terminology.

Definition 8
.

Let
\mathfrak{I}=(\Sigma,h,k,\phi,\psi)
be CMC initial data with mean curvature
\theta>0
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be the associated expansion normalised Weingarten
map, see Definition
5
, and let
{\sigma_{p}}\in(0,1)
. Then
\mathfrak{I}
and the eigenvalues
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
of
\mathcal{K}
are
said to be
{\sigma_{p}}
-
admissible

if they satisfy

(6)

p_{I}+p_{J}-p_{K}<1-{\sigma_{p}},

for all
I,J,K
, such that
I\neq J
.

Concerning the potential, we make the following assumption.

Definition 9
.

Fix
\sigma_{V}\in(0,1)
. If
V\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
has the property that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
there is a constant
c_{k}>0
such that

(7)

\textstyle{\sum}_{\ell\leq k}|V^{(\ell)}(x)|\leq c_{k}e^{2(1-\sigma_{V})|x|}

for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
, then
V
is said to be a
\sigma_{V}
-admissible potential
.

Remark 10
.

In comparison with
[
33
, Definition 1, p. 5]
, we have here omitted the requirement that
V
be non-negative. We include this requirement
explicitly in Theorem
11
instead.

Theorem 11

(The main theorem)
.

Fix
3\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
and admissibility thresholds
\sigma_{V}
,
{\sigma_{p}}\in(0,1)
. Let
(\Sigma,h_{\mathrm{ref}})
be a closed Riemannian manifold of dimension

n
and let
V\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
be a non-negative
\sigma_{V}
–admissible potential. Then there is a
\kappa\in\mathbb{N}
, depending only on
n
,
\sigma_{V}

and
{\sigma_{p}}
, such that the following holds. For any
\zeta_{0}>0
, there is a
\zeta_{1}>0
such that:

If
\mathfrak{I}
are
{\sigma_{p}}
-admissible CMC initial data on
\Sigma
for the Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations with potential
V
, such that the
associated expansion-normalized initial data
(\Sigma,\mathcal{H}_{0},\mathcal{K}_{0},\Phi_{0},\Psi_{0})
satisfy

(8)

\|\mathcal{H}^{-1}_{0}\|_{C^{0}_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Sigma)}+\|\mathcal{H}_{0}\|_{H^{\kappa}_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Sigma)}+\|\mathcal{K}_{0}\|_{H^{\kappa}_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Sigma)}+\|\Phi_{0}\|_{H^{\kappa}_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Sigma)}+\|\Psi_{0}\|_{H^{\kappa}_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Sigma)}<\zeta_{0};

|p_{I}-p_{J}|>\zeta_{0}^{-1}

, where

CMC foliation:

There is a diffeomorphism
\Xi

Asymptotic data:

There are smooth robust nondegenerate quiescent initial data on the singularity for the Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations,

(\Sigma,\mathring{\mathcal{H}},\mathring{\mathcal{K}},\mathring{\Phi},\mathring{\Psi})

\delta>0

\Sigma_{t}
.

Let
\mathring{p}_{I}
denote the eigenvalues of
\mathring{\mathcal{K}}

,

\mathfrak{R}_{g}:=\mathrm{Ric}_{g,\mu\nu}\mathrm{Ric}_{g}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\big\|t^{4}\mathfrak{K}_{g}(t,\cdot)-4\big[\textstyle{\sum}_{I}\mathring{p}_{I}^{2}(1-\mathring{p}_{I}^{2})+\textstyle{\sum}_{I<J}\mathring{p}_{I}^{2}\mathring{p}_{J}^{2}\big]\big\|_{C^{\ell}_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Sigma)}\leq C_{\ell}t^{\delta},

(10b)

\displaystyle\|t^{4}\mathfrak{R}_{g}(t,\cdot)-\mathring{\Psi}^{4}\|_{C^{\ell}_{\mathrm{ref}}(\Sigma)}\leq C_{\ell}t^{\delta}

for all
t\in(0,t_{0}]
and all

, so that
(M,g)
is
C^{2}
past inextendible. Moreover, every past directed causal geodesic
in

The main result of this article is Theorem

In

tends to infinity, the number of derivatives you need to control initially tends to infinity.
This is unfortunate. In the present article, we therefore begin by deriving partial information concerning the asymptotics for all derivatives, given
assumptions concerning the initial data in a fixed finite degree of regularity. This is the purpose of Theorem
19
. The basic idea is to prove energy
estimates inductively on the order
\ell
of the energy
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{tot},\ell}
introduced in (
43
). For the first order energy, we have estimates by
assumption. For a higher order, say
\ell
, the goal is to prove an estimate of the form (
108
). In this estimate, we effectively control the
last term on the right hand side due to the induction hypothesis and we control the second term on the right hand side because the factor in front of the
energy is integrable. For these reasons, we can effectively ignore the last two terms on the right hand side of (
108
), with a slight loss.
This means, inductively, that the energy only grows at the rate
t^{-9}
in the direction of the singularity; see
Corollary
36
. Moreover, the rate is independent of the number of derivatives and of the dimension. Combining this estimate
with interpolation yields good enough estimates for most of the quantities of interest. However, the estimates for
k_{IJ}
and
e_{0}\varphi
are not
immediately optimal. Nevertheless, combining our improved estimates with the equations, optimal estimates for these quantities can also be derived.
Moreover, we obtain the conclusions of Theorem
19
.

To conclude, the main point is to derive an estimate of the form (

). We begin by deriving some basic consequences of the

It is of particular importance to note that we include additional time dependent weights (that tend to infinity in the direction of the singularity)

we have the extra time dependent weights in the energies for the frame and co-frame. With the algorithm at hand, we derive elliptic

first sight. However, it turns out to be important since the second term on the left hand side appears in the energy estimates. We also need
(

Asymptotics.
In the context of quiescent big bang formation, one would like to show that solutions induce data on the singularity as in
[
45
]
or
[
23
]
. In particular, if the solution is expected to exhibit stable big bang formation, one would like to show that it induces data on the singularity as in Definition
6
. In
[
33
]
, the authors obtain some of these data. Specifically, if
(\Sigma,\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K},\Phi,\Psi)
denotes the expansion normalized initial data along the foliation, the authors prove the convergence of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}
, of
\Phi
and of
\Psi
, in
C^{k_{0}}
, for some fixed
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, to nontrivial limits satisfying the expected constraints, (
33
) and (
34
). However, they fail to obtain convergence of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
, due to limited information on the asymptotic behavior of the Fermi-Walker frame
\{e_{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
. The authors obtain estimates of the form

\|e_{I}\|_{C^{k_{0}}(\Sigma)}\leq Ct^{-1+3\sigma}

for some
\sigma>0
, along with similar estimates for the corresponding dual frame and structure coefficients, which are not sharp. That this is enough information to prove big bang formation is a remarkable property of the Fermi-Walker frame. One does not need to have full knowledge about the asymptotics to prove curvature blow-up.

Following the results of
[
33
]
, it makes sense to set as our starting point some
C^{k_{0}}
bounds on a Fermi-Walker frame and on the components of geometric quantities in terms of this frame, along with
C^{k_{0}}
convergence of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}
, of
\Phi
and of
\Psi
. These are precisely the estimates (
36
) and (
35
). Note that these estimates correspond to the conclusions obtained in
[
33
]
with two exceptions. First, since we do not assume the CMC condition, we need a way to control the mean curvature. This is done through the quantity
\Theta=N\theta
, which is assumed to be asymptotic to
t^{-1}
as
t\downarrow 0
. Second, in
[
33
]
, the authors obtain convergence of the lapse,
N\to 1
in
C^{k_{0}}
as
t\downarrow 0
. By contrast, here we only assume that
N
is bounded in
C^{k_{0}}
, and that
N^{-1}
is bounded in
C^{0}
. However, we point out that if the CMC condition is assumed, then the assumption that
t\Theta-1\to 0
becomes
N-1\to 0
. We begin by deriving some basic consequences of the assumptions in Subsection
3.1
.

The idea to derive the remaining asymptotics, is to introduce an eigenframe
\{\widehat{e}_{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
for
\mathcal{K}
. That is, an orthonormal frame for
h
satisfying
\mathcal{K}(\widehat{e}_{I})=p_{I}\widehat{e}_{I}
. This is done in Subsection
3.2
. Here the nondegeneracy assumption is important, since it ensures that one can take this frame to be smooth. Since the eigenframe diagonalizes both
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
simultaneously, and we already know the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{K}
to converge, if we can show that appropriate normalizations of the
\widehat{e}_{I}
converge to nontrivial limits as
t\downarrow 0
, it will follow that
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
also converge. This is the purpose of Theorem
56
, of which Theorem
24
is a direct consequence. To begin with, it is thus necessary to relate the Fermi-Walker frame to the eigenframe. This is done through a family of orthogonal matrices
A
satisfying
\widehat{e}_{I}=A_{I}^{J}e_{J}
. Einstein’s equations plus the assumptions can then be used to deduce that the
A_{I}^{J}
are globally bounded in
C^{k_{0}-1}
. In fact, one can prove that there is a family of orthogonal matrices
\mathring{A}
such that
A_{I}^{J}\to\mathring{A}_{I}^{J}
in
C^{k_{0}-1}
as
t\downarrow 0
; see Proposition
47
. Once this has been done, one can translate the estimates for the Fermi-Walker frame, the corresponding dual frame, and the structure coefficients, to the eigenframe and the associated objects. Of course, the resulting estimates are not optimal yet, but it is possible to improve on them.

Once estimates for the eigenframe have been obtained, we proceed, in Subsection
3.3
, to consider the expansion normalized eigenframe
\{{\check{e}}_{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
given by
{\check{e}}_{I}=\theta^{-p_{I}}\widehat{e}_{I}
(so that
\mathcal{H}({\check{e}}_{I},{\check{e}}_{I})=1
), along with the corresponding dual frame and structure coefficients. These objects are now expected to converge as
t\downarrow 0
. They also inherit some estimates from the Fermi-Walker frame. However, at this point, the bound that we obtain blows up as
t\downarrow 0
. Einstein’s equations then imply a system of evolution equations for the
{\check{e}}_{I}
and related objects, which we use in Subsection
3.4
to derive asymptotics. It turns out that these equations can be integrated repeatedly as ODEs to improve on the estimates, at the price of losing two derivatives on each iteration. Thus, given an
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
one concludes that the
{\check{e}}_{I}
and related objects are globally bounded in
C^{\ell}
after a finite number of steps, provided that
k_{0}
is large enough. Finally, one more integration of the evolution equations yields the existence of nontrivial
C^{\ell}
limits for the
{\check{e}}_{I}
and related objects. These limits can then be used to define
\mathring{\mathcal{H}}
and
\mathring{\mathcal{K}}
, the limits of
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
, and it can be shown that
(\Sigma,\mathring{\mathcal{H}},\mathring{\mathcal{K}},\mathring{\Phi},\mathring{\Psi})
satisfies the conditions of Definition
6
.

Once we have Theorems
19
and
24
at our disposal, we can combine them with
the arguments in
[
33
]
in order to deduce that Theorem

holds. The details are to be found in

to be half the minimum of
|a_{i}-m_{i}\delta|
for
i\geq K+1
. In fact, the inequality

|a_{i}-m\delta-\alpha|\geq\alpha

holds for every integer
m
.

For every
x\in\Sigma
, we think of the
a_{i}
as the numbers
1-\mathring{p}_{I}(x)-\mathring{p}_{J}(x)+\mathring{p}_{K}(x)
for
I\neq J
, and the
1+\mathring{p}_{I}(x)
. Then by the previous paragraph, there is an
0<\alpha(x)\leq\delta/4
such that

\begin{split}|1-\mathring{p}_{I}(x)-\mathring{p}_{J}(x)+\mathring{p}_{K}(x)-m\delta-\alpha(x)|&\geq\alpha(x),\\
|1+\mathring{p}_{I}(x)-m\delta-\alpha(x)|&\geq\alpha(x).\end{split}

By continuity, there is an open set
U_{x}\subset\Sigma
containing
x
, such that

\begin{split}|1-\mathring{p}_{I}(y)-\mathring{p}_{J}(y)+\mathring{p}_{K}(y)-m\delta-\alpha(x)|&\geq\tfrac{\alpha(x)}{2},\\
|1+\mathring{p}_{I}(y)-m\delta-\alpha(x)|&\geq\tfrac{\alpha(x)}{2},\end{split}

for all
y\in U_{x}
. Finally, since the
U_{x}
form an open cover of
\Sigma
, there are finitely many of them that cover
\Sigma
.
∎

3.2.
The eigenframe

Due to the assumptions, the eigenvalues
p_{I}
of
\mathcal{K}
are distinct everywhere. Due to
[
42
, Lemma A.1, p. 223]
, we can, by taking a finite
covering space of
\Sigma
, if necessary, assume that there are smooth vector fields
\widehat{e}_{I}
on
M
, for
I=1,\ldots,n
, tangential
to the hypersurfaces
\Sigma_{t}
and such that
\mathcal{K}(\widehat{e}_{I})=p_{I}\widehat{e}_{I}
and
h(\widehat{e}_{I},\widehat{e}_{J})=\delta_{IJ}
. In particular,
\{\widehat{e}_{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
is then an
orthonormal frame for
h
on each
\Sigma_{t}
. Denote the corresponding dual frame
\{\widehat{\omega}^{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
. Next, we deduce evolution equations for the
\widehat{e}_{I}
.

Lemma 43
.

Let
3\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,
r>0
,
\delta\in(0,1)
and fix a
\delta
-admissible potential
V\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Given that

(M,g,\varphi)
is a solution to the Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations satisfying the
(\delta,n,k_{0},r)
-assumptions, assume that there is
an eigenframe
\{\widehat{e}_{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
with the properties listed above. Then
\{\widehat{e}_{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
and the eigenvalues
p_{I}
satisfy the system of equations

(119a)

\displaystyle[e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}]

\displaystyle=\widehat{e}_{I}(\ln N)e_{0}-\theta p_{I}\widehat{e}_{I}+\textstyle\sum_{J\neq I}\tfrac{1}{\theta(p_{I}-p_{J})}h\big((\mathcal{L}_{e_{0}}K)(\widehat{e}_{I}),\widehat{e}_{J}\big)\widehat{e}_{J},

(119b)

\displaystyle e_{0}p_{I}

\displaystyle=-\theta^{-1}e_{0}(\theta)p_{I}+\theta^{-1}g\big((\mathcal{L}_{e_{0}}K)(\widehat{e}_{I}),\widehat{e}_{I}\big)

(no summation on
I
). In particular,

\partial_{t}\widehat{e}_{I}^{\;i}=-\Theta p_{I}\widehat{e}_{I}^{\;i}+\textstyle\sum_{J\neq I}\tfrac{N^{2}}{\Theta(p_{I}-p_{J})}g\big((\mathcal{L}_{e_{0}}K)(\widehat{e}_{I}),\widehat{e}_{J}\big)\widehat{e}_{J}^{\;i}

(no summation on
I
), where
\widehat{e}_{I}=\widehat{e}_{I}^{\;i}E_{i}
.

Remark 44
.

Recall from Subsection
A.3
, that with respect to the frame
\{E_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and dual frame
\{\eta^{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
,

\mathcal{L}_{e_{0}}K=N^{-1}\partial_{t}(K^{i}_{\phantom{i}j})E_{i}\otimes\eta^{j}.

Proof.

Compute

0=e_{0}\big(g(\widehat{e}_{I},\widehat{e}_{J})\big)=(\mathcal{L}_{e_{0}}g)(\widehat{e}_{I},\widehat{e}_{J})+g\big([e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}],\widehat{e}_{J}\big)+g\big(\widehat{e}_{I},[e_{0},\widehat{e}_{J}]\big).

Hence

2\theta p_{I}\delta_{IJ}=-g\big([e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}],\widehat{e}_{J}\big)-g\big(\widehat{e}_{I},[e_{0},\widehat{e}_{J}]\big)

(no summation on
I
on the left hand side). In particular, it follows that

g\big([e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}],\widehat{e}_{I}\big)=-\theta p_{I}

(no summation on
I
on the left hand side). For
X\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, denote by
\overline{X}
the part of
X
tangential to the
\Sigma_{t}
. Then, applying

\overline{[e_{0},\,\cdot\,]}
to both sides of
K(\widehat{e}_{I})=\theta p_{I}\widehat{e}_{I}
(no summation) yields

(\mathcal{L}_{e_{0}}K)(\widehat{e}_{I})+K\big(\overline{[e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}]}\big)=e_{0}(\theta p_{I})\widehat{e}_{I}+\theta p_{I}\overline{[e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}]}

(no summation). Note that
K\big(\overline{[e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}]}\big)=\sum_{J}g\big([e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}],\widehat{e}_{J}\big)\theta p_{J}\widehat{e}_{J}
. The equation for
p_{I}
follows by
applying
g(\widehat{e}_{I},\,\cdot\,)
to the resulting equation. On the other hand, applying
g(\widehat{e}_{J},\,\cdot\,)
with
J\neq I
, yields

g\big([e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}],\widehat{e}_{J}\big)=\tfrac{1}{\theta(p_{I}-p_{J})}g\big((\mathcal{L}_{e_{0}}K)(\widehat{e}_{I}),\widehat{e}_{J}\big).

Next,

-g\big([e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}],e_{0}\big)=g\big([\widehat{e}_{I},N^{-1}\partial_{t}],e_{0}\big)=g\big(-N^{-2}\widehat{e}_{I}(N)\partial_{t},e_{0}\big)=g\big(-\widehat{e}_{I}(\ln N)e_{0},e_{0}\big)=\widehat{e}_{I}(\ln N).

Finally, we can write

[e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}]=-g\big([e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}],e_{0}\big)e_{0}+\textstyle\sum_{J}g\big([e_{0},\widehat{e}_{I}],\widehat{e}_{J}\big)\widehat{e}_{J}.

The lemma follows.
∎

The idea is to translate estimates for the Fermi-Walker frame
\{e_{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
, its dual frame
\{\omega^{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
and its structure coefficients,
to the eigenframe
\{\widehat{e}_{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
. To this end, we consider the family of matrices
A
whose elements are defined by

\widehat{e}_{I}=A_{I}^{J}e_{J}.

Note that
A
is a family of orthogonal matrices, so that
e_{I}=A_{J}^{I}\widehat{e}_{J}
. For the dual frames,
\widehat{\omega}^{I}=A_{I}^{J}\omega^{J}
. Moreover,

A_{I}^{J}=g(\widehat{e}_{I},e_{J})
, so that
A_{I}^{J}
is smooth, and the bound
|A_{I}^{J}|\leq 1
holds everywhere on
M
. Therefore, in order to translate estimates
for the Fermi-Walker frame, we only need to control the derivatives of
A
. To this end, we deduce an evolution equation for
A
.

Lemma 45
.

Let
3\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,
r>0
,
\delta\in(0,1)
and fix a
\delta
-admissible potential

\{\widehat{e}_{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
with the properties listed at the beginning of the present subsection. Then the elements of
A
satisfy the evolution
equation

(120)

\partial_{t}A_{I}^{J}=\textstyle\sum_{K\neq I}\tfrac{N^{2}}{\Theta(p_{I}-p_{K})}g\big((\mathcal{L}_{e_{0}}K)(\widehat{e}_{I}),\widehat{e}_{K}\big)A_{K}^{J}.

Proof.

First, note that

(121)

\partial_{t}\widehat{e}_{I}^{\;i}=\big(\partial_{t}A_{I}^{J}\big)e_{J}^{i}+A_{I}^{J}\partial_{t}e_{J}^{i}.

From (
32

\partial_{t}e_{I}^{i}=-Nk_{IJ}e_{J}^{i}.

Therefore,

(122)

\begin{split}A_{I}^{J}\partial_{t}e_{J}^{i}=&-NA_{I}^{J}k_{JK}e_{K}^{i}=-NA_{M}^{K}k(\widehat{e}_{I},\widehat{e}_{M})e_{K}^{i}\\
=&-NA_{I}^{K}\theta p_{I}e_{K}^{i}=-N\theta p_{I}\widehat{e}_{I}^{\;i}\end{split}

(no summation on

). We can then conclude from Lemma
43
that

\big(\partial_{t}A_{I}^{J}\big)e_{J}=\textstyle\sum_{K\neq I}\tfrac{N^{2}}{\Theta(p_{I}-p_{K})}g\big(\mathcal{L}_{e_{0}}K(\widehat{e}_{I}),\widehat{e}_{K}\big)\widehat{e}_{K}.

Since
\widehat{e}_{K}=A_{K}^{J}e_{J}
, the result follows.
∎

Since we only have information about the Fermi-Walker frame, we rewrite the evolution equation for
A_{I}^{J}
as follows. Define
B_{IKLM}
by

B_{IKLM}:=\tfrac{N^{2}}{\Theta(p_{I}-p_{K})}\big(-\mathrm{Ric}_{h}(e_{L},e_{M})+N^{-1}(\overline{\nabla}^{2}N)(e_{L},e_{M})+e_{L}(\varphi)e_{M}(\varphi)\big),

for

) implies that

\partial_{t}A_{I}^{J}=\textstyle\sum_{K}B_{IKLM}A_{I}^{L}A_{K}^{M}A_{K}^{J}.

It is thus of interest to deduce estimates for

Lemma 46
.

Let
3\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
,

,

,

Proposition 47
.

Let
3\leq n\in\mathbb{N}
,
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
,
r>0
,
\delta\in(0,1)
and fix a
\delta
-admissible potential
V\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Given that

(M,g,\varphi)
is a solution to the Einstein–nonlinear scalar field equations satisfying the
(\delta,n,k_{0},r)
-assumptions, assume that there is
an eigenframe
\{\widehat{e}_{I}\}_{I=1}^{n}
with the properties listed at the beginning of the present subsection. Given
t_{0}\in(0,T)
, there is a constant

C
and a
C^{k_{0}-1}(\Sigma)
family
\mathring{A}
of orthogonal matrices such that, for
t\leq t_{0}
,

(125)

\|A_{I}^{J}(t)-\mathring{A}_{I}^{J}\|_{C^{k_{0}-1}(\Sigma)}\leq Ct^{2\delta}.

Remark 48
.

When we speak of
t_{0}
in what follows, we take it for granted to be the number fixed in this proposition.

Proof.

For
m<k_{0}
, define the functions

\rho_{m}:=\textstyle\sum_{I,J}\textstyle\sum_{|\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}|=m}\big(E_{\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}}A_{I}^{J}\big)^{2}.

Since
|A_{I}^{J}|\leq 1
everywhere, it follows that
\rho_{0}\leq n^{2}
. We derive uniform bounds for the remaining
\rho_{m}
inductively.
Fix
m<k_{0}
. Suppose that there is a constant
C
, such that
\rho_{k}\leq C
for all
k\leq m-1
. Then, by Lemma
46
and
(
123
),

\begin{split}|\partial_{t}\rho_{m}|&=2\textstyle\sum_{I,J}\textstyle\sum_{|\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}|=m}\big(E_{\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}}A_{I}^{J}\big)\big(E_{\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}}\partial_{t}A_{I}^{J}\big)\leq 2\rho_{m}^{1/2}\big(\textstyle\sum_{I,J}\textstyle\sum_{|\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}|=m}\big(E_{\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}}\partial_{t}A_{I}^{J}\big)^{2}\big)^{1/2}\\
&\leq 2\rho_{m}^{1/2}\big(Ct^{-1+2\delta}\rho_{m}^{1/2}+Ct^{-1+2\delta}\big)\leq Ct^{-1+2\delta}\rho_{m}+Ct^{-1+2\delta}\rho_{m}^{1/2}\\
&\leq Ct^{-1+2\delta}\rho_{m}+Ct^{-1+2\delta},\end{split}

where we used Young’s inequality. Since
t^{-1+2\delta}
is integrable on
(0,t_{0}]
, it follows that

\rho_{m}(s)=\rho_{m}(t_{0})+\textstyle\int_{s}^{t_{0}}-\partial_{t}\rho_{m}(t){\mathrm{d}}t\leq C+C\textstyle\int_{s}^{t_{0}}t^{-1+2\delta}\rho_{m}(t){\mathrm{d}}t

for all
s\in(0,t_{0}]
. By Grönwall’s lemma, we conclude that
\rho_{m}(t)\leq C
for some constant
C
and all
t\in(0,t_{0}]
.

By the above,
\|A_{I}^{J}(t)\|_{C^{k_{0}-1}(\Sigma)}\leq C
for all
t\in(0,t_{0}]
. To find the matrix
\mathring{A}_{I}^{J}
, note that for
u<s
and

|\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}|\leq k_{0}-1
,

\big|E_{\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}}A_{I}^{J}(s)-E_{\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}}A_{I}^{J}(u)\big|\leq\textstyle\int_{u}^{s}\big|E_{\mathrm{\mathbf{I}}}\partial_{t}A_{I}^{J}(t)\big|{\mathrm{d}}t\leq C(s^{2\delta}-u^{2\delta}).

Hence, there are functions
\mathring{A}_{I}^{J}\in C^{k_{0}-1}(\Sigma)
such that (
125
) holds. Finally,
\mathring{A}_{I}^{J}
is
orthogonal, since it is the pointwise limit of
A_{I}^{J}
, which is orthogonal for all
t\in(0,T)
.
∎

Now that we know

Hence, appealing to the Jacobi identity,

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\Lambda_{IJ}^{K}&=\partial_{t}(\theta^{2p_{K}})g\big([{\check{e}}_{I},{\check{e}}_{J}],{\check{e}}_{K}\big)+N\theta^{2p_{K}}\Big(2k\big([{\check{e}}_{I},{\check{e}}_{J}],{\check{e}}_{K}\big)\\
&\quad+g\big([[e_{0},{\check{e}}_{I}],{\check{e}}_{J}],{\check{e}}_{K}\big)+g\big([{\check{e}}_{I},[e_{0},{\check{e}}_{J}]],{\check{e}}_{K}\big)+g\big([{\check{e}}_{I},{\check{e}}_{J}],[e_{0},{\check{e}}_{K}]\big)\Big).\end{split}

Note that

[e_{0},{\check{e}}_{I}]=N^{-1}[\partial_{t},{\check{e}}_{I}]+{\check{e}}_{I}(\ln N)e_{0}=N^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{I}^{L}{\check{e}}_{L}+{\check{e}}_{I}(\ln N)e_{0},

so that

\begin{split}[[e_{0},{\check{e}}_{I}],{\check{e}}_{J}]&=N^{-1}\mathcal{B}_{I}^{L}[{\check{e}}_{L},{\check{e}}_{J}]+N^{-1}{\check{e}}_{J}(\ln N)\mathcal{B}_{I}^{L}{\check{e}}_{L}-N^{-1}{\check{e}}_{J}(\mathcal{B}_{I}^{L}){\check{e}}_{L}\\
&\quad+{\check{e}}_{I}(\ln N)[e_{0},{\check{e}}_{J}]-{\check{e}}_{J}{\check{e}}_{I}(\ln N)e_{0}.\end{split}

Going back to
\partial_{t}\Lambda_{IJ}^{K}
, our previous observations yield (no summation on
K
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\Lambda_{IJ}^{K}

\displaystyle=2\partial_{t}(p_{K}\ln\theta)\Lambda_{IJ}^{K}+2\Theta p_{K}\Lambda_{IJ}^{K}

\displaystyle\quad+\theta^{2p_{K}}\Big(\mathcal{B}_{I}^{L}g\big([{\check{e}}_{L},{\check{e}}_{J}],{\check{e}}_{K}\big)+{\check{e}}_{J}(\ln N)\mathcal{B}_{I}^{L}g({\check{e}}_{L},{\check{e}}_{K})-{\check{e}}_{J}\big(\mathcal{B}_{I}^{L}\big)g({\check{e}}_{L},{\check{e}}_{K})

\displaystyle\quad+N{\check{e}}_{I}(\ln N)g\big([e_{0},{\check{e}}_{J}],{\check{e}}_{K}\big)-\mathcal{B}_{J}^{L}g\big([{\check{e}}_{L},{\check{e}}_{I}],{\check{e}}_{K}\big)-{\check{e}}_{I}(\ln N)\mathcal{B}_{J}^{L}g({\check{e}}_{L},{\check{e}}_{K})

\displaystyle\quad+{\check{e}}_{I}\big(\mathcal{B}_{J}^{L}\big)g({\check{e}}_{L},{\check{e}}_{K})-N{\check{e}}_{J}(\ln N)g\big([e_{0},{\check{e}}_{I}],{\check{e}}_{K}\big)+\mathcal{B}_{K}^{L}g\big([{\check{e}}_{I},{\check{e}}_{J}],{\check{e}}_{L}\big)\Big)

\displaystyle=-2\mathcal{B}_{K}^{K}\Lambda_{IJ}^{K}+\mathcal{B}_{I}^{L}\Lambda_{LJ}^{K}-{\check{e}}_{J}\big(\mathcal{B}_{I}^{K}\big)-\mathcal{B}_{J}^{L}\Lambda_{LI}^{K}+{\check{e}}_{I}\big(\mathcal{B}_{J}^{K}\big)+\textstyle\sum_{L}\theta^{2(p_{K}-p_{L})}\mathcal{B}_{K}^{L}\Lambda_{IJ}^{L},

where we used that
-\mathcal{B}_{K}^{K}=\partial_{t}(p_{K}\ln\theta)+\Theta p_{K}
(no summation). The lemma follows.
∎

(127)
