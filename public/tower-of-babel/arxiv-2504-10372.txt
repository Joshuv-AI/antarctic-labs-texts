Simple physical systems as a reference for multivariate information dynamics
Alberto Liardi, Madalina I Sas, George Blackburne, William J Knottenbelt, Pedro A.M. Mediano, Henrik Jeldtoft Jensen

Abstract
Understanding a complex system entails capturing the non-trivial collective phenomena that arise from interactions between its different parts. Information theory is a flexible and robust framework to study such behaviours, with several measures designed to quantify and characterise the interdependencies among the system’s components. However, since these estimators rely on the statistical distributions of observed quantities, it is crucial to examine the relationships between information-theoretic measures and the system’s underlying mechanistic structure. To this end, here we present an information-theoretic analytical investigation of an elementary system of interactive random walkers subject to Gaussian noise. Focusing on partial information decomposition, causal emergence, and integrated information, our results help us develop some intuitions on their relationship with the physical parameters of the system. For instance, we observe that uncoupled systems can exhibit emergent properties, in a way that we suggest may be better described as “statistically autonomous”. Overall, we observe that in this simple scenario information measures align more reliably with the system’s mechanistic properties when calculated at the level of microscopic components, rather than their coarse-grained counterparts, and over timescales comparable with the system’s intrinsic dynamics. Moreover, we show that approaches that separate the contributions of the system’s dynamics and steady-state distribution (e.g. via causal perturbations) may help strengthen the interpretation of information-theoretic analyses.

I
Introduction

Information theory has become a convenient and powerful methodology for the characterisation of multivariate dynamical systems
Cover and Thomas 1991
;
MacKay 2003
. The formalism initiated by Shannon
Shannon 1948
has evolved into a sophisticated field able to reveal from observable data the fundamental organisation and information-processing properties of complex systems.

Present-day information theory is a broad and lively research subject with applications spanning many fields, such as computer science and machine learning
Tishby et al. 2000
;
Shwartz-Ziv and Tishby 2017
;
Proca et al. 2024
, neuroscience
Timme and Lapish 2018
;
Stone 2018
;
Luppi et al. 2024
, biology
Smith 2000
;
Adami 2004
, ecology
Margalef 1973
;
Rajpal et al. 2023
and beyond
Ben-Naim and Dufour 2024
.
Information theory offers a variety of metrics to assess the interdependencies and complexity of dynamical processes, with mutual information and transfer entropy being among the most popular.
Notably, recent advances in the field now allow for more fine-grained and profound characterisation of multivariate systems
Williams and Beer 2010
;
Mediano et al. 2019
;
Ince 2017a
;
Varley et al. 2023
;
Varley 2024a
, including the interrogation of relationships between microscopic and macroscopic scales
Rosas et al. 2018a
;
Hoel et al. 2013
;
Rosas et al. 2024
.
Nonetheless, linking experimental results using these information-theoretic quantities to our intuitions about the mechanisms behind complex phenomena remains a challenging problem.

One common suspect that often leads to spurious conclusions is the so-called “ghost of causality” in statistical models
Pearl 2009a
;
Pearl 2009b
;
Pearl et al. 2016
. Specifically, while many works have investigated structural causal inference using information flow
Wiener 1956
;
Duan et al. 2013
;
Eichler 2013
;
Martínez-Sánchez et al. 2024
, it has also been shown that this flow is not always faithful to the underlying causal structure
Janzing et al. 2013
.
Within complexity science, an increasingly popular view is to distinguish between “mechanisms” (i.e. the underlying structure of the system) and “behaviours” (i.e. the properties of the system’s trajectories in its state space)
Rosas et al. 2022
. Elucidating the relationship between the two, however, is complicated – even more so
when analysing real-world complex systems, where the high number of degrees of freedom and the complexity of interactions make it extremely difficult to interpret the underlying mechanical structure.

While the field has thoroughly explored the formal properties of elementary information-theoretic quantities, less attention has been given to more recent measures – those which we likely stand most to gain from for understanding and engineering complex systems.
To fill this gap, here we investigate how specific information-theoretic relationships are related to functional or mechanistic properties by analytically studying a simple system of interacting random walkers, for which physical intuitions and mathematical tools are available.
With this model at hand, we study how information-theoretic metrics vary when the interaction strength changes, and how this affects the information dynamics of the system.
Importantly, we show that in general information-theoretic behaviours differ from the underlying mechanisms,
and outline some conditions that may help strengthen the interpretation of such measures applied to empirical data.

The rest of this paper is organised as follows: in Sec.
II
we provide a simple and known example of a non-intuitive property of information-theoretic quantities, which justifies this study. Then, we present the one-dimensional model of interacting random walkers that will be our main case study (Sec.
III
). In Sec.
IV
, we report the analytical and numerical results for various information-theoretic metrics, and we discuss their implications for the study of real data in Sec.
V
.
In the supplementary material, we report the detailed calculations of all quantities considered, additional investigations of their behaviours, and a brief comparative study of theoretical and numerical results.

II
A simple example

To illustrate how interpreting the behaviour of statistical quantities can be subtle, let us focus on the behaviour of mutual information (MI) in a stochastic system.
Given two random variables
X,Y
, the mutual information between them assesses the amount of information that we gain about
X
when
Y
is observed. In other words, it measures the knowledge that
Y
brings about
X
. Denoting Shannon’s entropy by
H
, the MI between
X
and
Y
can be written as

I(X;Y)=H(Y)-H(Y|X)\,.

(1)

For further discussion and mathematical details, we refer to App.
A
.

An interesting property of MI is that it can vanish in two different scenarios:

1.

if
X
and
Y
are independent variables, and thus
{H(Y)=H(Y|X)>0}
,

2.

if
Y
is a deterministic function of
X
, and thus
{H(Y)=H(Y|X)=0}
.

Therefore, MI depends on properties of both the channel (i.e. the relation between
X
and
Y
) and the input distribution (in this case
Y
), and hence a change in MI can’t be easily attributed to either. This may complicate the interpretation of the behaviour of the mutual information.

To better illustrate this point, consider a time-dependent signal
f(t)
which can take the two values
+A
and
-A
. We set the initial condition
f(0)=A
with probability
\alpha
, and let the signal evolve in time, swapping between
\pm A
at a constant rate
\lambda
.
We want to compute the mutual information
I(f(0);f(t))
between the signal at time zero and a later time
t
, i.e. we measure how much information the starting state provides about the one at time
t
. This can be easily achieved by working directly on the probabilities
p(f(0))
,
p(f(t))
, and
p(f(0);f(t))
. We refer to App.
B
for the mathematical details.
To get a feeling of how the mutual information behaves when the interdependencies among channels and the property of the input distribution are changed, we let
\alpha
vary from 0 to 1 while
\lambda
is fixed (Fig.
1
). Interestingly, the MI follows a non-monotonous behaviour: it is positive for
0<\alpha<1
, then vanishes for
\alpha=0,1
, since the value at
f(0)
is assumed with probability 1. This is due to a vanishing entropy in the marginal distribution, as the system becomes fully deterministic (case 2 above).
Similarly, for fixed
\alpha
, the increased stochasticity induced by a larger
\lambda
drives the mutual information to zero (case 1 above).

This behaviour is of course well known in information theory and stochastic processes. Nonetheless, it is a useful reminder that a decrease in the mutual information may be either indicative of a weaker interdependence between variables or a more deterministic nature of the input distribution – two very different mechanical properties of the system.

for details.

We will now elaborate on this theme by focusing on independent and coupled random walkers, both in a stationary and non-stationary case. We present analytic and numerical results for a variety of measures that are currently commonly employed to discuss higher-order properties in complex systems.

III
Model

Our mathematical laboratorium consists of a set of
N
random walkers (RWs) that move in a continuous 1-dimensional space in discrete time.
We consider an equation of motion that follows an Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process

Starting from the Langevin equation in continuous time, for the central walkers

\displaystyle m\frac{d^{2}x_{i}}{dt^{2}}=

\displaystyle-\nu\frac{dx_{i}}{dt}-\theta[x_{i}(t)-ia]+\gamma[x_{i+1}(t)-x_{i}(t)-a]+

(2)

\displaystyle-\gamma[x_{i}(t)-x_{i-1}(t)-a]+\eta_{i}(t)\,,

a stochastic Gaussian term sampled from

Restricting ourselves to the overdamped dynamics regime (
m\rightarrow 0
) in discrete time, we have:

\displaystyle x_{i}(t+dt)=

\displaystyle x_{i}(t)-\frac{dt}{\nu}\theta[x_{i}(t)-ia]+

(3)

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma dt}{\nu}(x_{i+1}(t)+x_{i-1}(t)-2x_{i}(t))+\frac{dt}{\nu}\eta_{i}(t)\,.

In other words, the dynamics of a RW is characterised by the interactions with its two nearest neighbours mediated by
\gamma
, and a restoring force due to
\theta
.
Finally, we can rewrite the equations in terms of the deviations from the resting positions of the springs
{\Delta_{i}(t)\equiv x_{i}(t)-ia}
, obtaining

\displaystyle\Delta_{i}(t+1)=

\displaystyle(1-\theta-2\gamma)\Delta_{i}(t)+\gamma(\Delta_{i+1}(t)+\Delta_{i-1}(t))+

(4)

\displaystyle+\eta_{i}(t)\,,

where for ease of notation we let

1

1

Setting

defines the system’s timescale as one timestep, with arbitrary unit of time. Accordingly, references to short or long timescales should be understood relative to the system’s intrinsic dynamics, which can correspond to various actual durations across different physical processes.
.
With a small abuse of language, throughout this work, we will refer to

as both deviations and positions of the RWs.
Focusing now on the edges

, similar considerations lead to the following equations of motion:

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(t+1)=(1-\theta-\gamma)\Delta_{1}(t)+\gamma\Delta_{2}(t)+\eta_{1}(t)\,,

\displaystyle\Delta_{N}(t+1)=(1-\theta-\gamma)\Delta_{N}(t)+\gamma\Delta_{N-1}(t)+\eta_{N}(t)\,.

(5)

We remind that the OU process taken into consideration is stationary only for
{\theta\in(0,1)}
. Thus, for our analyses, we restrict to this range of the parameter.

Eqs. (

\mathbf{\Delta}(t+1)=\mathbf{M\Delta}(t)+\bm{\eta}(t)\,,

(6)

where we introduced the vectors

{\bm{\eta}(t)=(\eta_{1}(t),\ldots,\eta_{N}(t))}
, the shorthand
{\alpha\equiv 1-\theta-\gamma}

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\gamma&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\gamma&\beta&\gamma&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\

Assuming as initial conditions

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}(t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\bm{M}^{t^{\prime}-t}\bm{\Delta}(t)+\bm{{\mathchoice{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\displaystyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\textstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptstyle\chi$}}{\raisebox{0.0pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle\chi$}}}}_{tt^{\prime}}\,,

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &\bm{M}^{t^{\prime}-t-1}\bm{\eta}(t)+\bm{M}^{t^{\prime}-t-2}\bm{\eta}(t+1)+\ldots+\\
&+\bm{M}\bm{\eta}(t^{\prime}-2)+\bm{\eta}(t^{\prime}-1)\,.\end{aligned}

(9)

In Eq. (
8

Importantly, the knowledge of

;

;

.

To keep the problem analytically tractable and preserve an intuitive understanding, in this work we solve Eqs. (
8
)-(
9
) under two important assumptions:

1.

we use a perturbative approach expanding the equations of motion to first order in

, meaning that the dynamics of the walkers are only slightly affected by the coupling. In practice, this accounts for

2.

Hence, our results will be exact in the limit of vanishing

In other words,

The simplicity of this setup allows for analytical calculations of information measures. Thus, the interplay between the interaction terms

IV
Results

IV.1
Mutual Information

We begin our analysis by looking at the time-delayed mutual information (TDMI) between various elements of the system of
N>2
RWs.
Since its introduction by Fraser and Swinney
Fraser and Swinney 1986
, TDMI has become a crucial quantity in the study of the information dynamics of complex systems.
TDMI quantifies the statistical dependence between a variable and a time-delayed version of another, reflecting how information from a past state contributes to predicting a future state.
This metric is commonly used to reveal underlying temporal structures, finding applications in numerous fields
James et al. 2011
.
In our case, given two timepoints
t,t^{\prime}
, with
t^{\prime}>t
, we compute the TDMI between RWs, c.o.m., and RWs and c.o.m.
Referring to App.
C.3
for the mathematical details of the derivation, the functional expressions read

\displaystyle I(\Delta_{i}(t);\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma t+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})\,,

(12)

\displaystyle I(\Delta_{i}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})\,,\quad i\neq j

(13)

\displaystyle I(V(t);V(t^{\prime}))\,\,\,

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})\,,

(14)

\displaystyle I(\Delta_{i}(t);V(t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{N\tau}{N\tau-1}\right)}+\gamma\frac{t-1}{N\tau-1}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})\,,

(15)

\displaystyle I(V(t);\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{N\tau}{N\tau-1}\right)}+\gamma\frac{t^{\prime}-1}{N\tau-1}+\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})\,,

(16)

where
\tau\equiv t^{\prime}/t
.
Eqs. (
12
)-(
16
) showcase the dependency of TDMI on the interaction strength of the system: for larger
\gamma
,
{I(\Delta_{i}(t),\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))}
decreases, as the single RW predicts less of itself as its motion is increasingly affected by the other walkers, whereas
I(V(t),V(t^{\prime}))
is independent of
\gamma
, as expected since
V
behaves as an autonomous RW (c.f. Eq. (
11
)).
Both
I(\Delta_{i}(t),V(t^{\prime}))
and
I(V(t),\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))
increase with
\gamma
, as stronger interactions make the different parts more interdependent and therefore more explainable if one of them is known.
We note that the exact calculations for the
N=2
case lead to the same conclusions (App.
D.2
).
Finally, the TDMI between different walkers vanishes. This is a spurious consequence of the perturbative approach at first order in
\gamma
. In fact, in the
N=2
RW case
I(\Delta_{i}(t),\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))>0
for

, increasing with the interaction strength as one would expect (see Eq. (

IV.2
Transfer Entropy

Transfer entropy (TE) is an information-theoretic metric used to quantify how the past of one variable affects the future of another variable
Schreiber 2000
.
It is often seen as a measure of the information transfer between two stochastic processes, capturing the directed flow of information over time from one variable to another.

More precisely, given two stochastic processes
X
and
Y
, TE quantifies the additional information that the past of
X
contributes to predicting the future of
Y
, beyond what is already explained by the past of
Y
alone.
For a system with Markovian dynamics – such as the one discussed here – TE can be expressed in terms of Shannon entropies as

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(X(t);Y(t+1))=

\displaystyle H(Y_{t+1}|Y_{t})-H(Y_{t+1}|Y_{t},X_{t})\,.

(17)

Referring to Apps.
C.4
-
D.3
for the calculations, and denoting the future timestep as
t^{\prime}=t+1
,
we obtain that the transfer entropy of the RWs and the c.o.m. vanish at first order in
\gamma
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}(\Delta_{i}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}(\Delta_{j}(t);V(t^{\prime}))

(18)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{T}(V(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))=\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})\,.

This is related to the behaviour of Eq. (
13
),
and it is a consequence of TE being a second-order quantity in
\gamma
(as was the case for
{I(\Delta_{i}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})),\,\,i\neq j}
).
From a practical point of view, however, this suggests that TE may struggle to detect very weak interactions – although this is an expected result of the geometric nature of TE
(Sec.
V.4
).

As expected, if we consider the exact results for the system of
N=2
random walkers, in which higher-order contributions in
\gamma
are included, we obtain that the transfer entropies
\mathcal{T}(\Delta_{i}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))
become non-zero (see Eqs. (
159
)-(
162
)).
Moreover, we note that the information flow between the random walkers increases for larger coupling parameter
\gamma
and for any timepoint (Fig.
2
).

Additionally, this is also equal to the TE between the c.o.m. and the single walker, since the knowledge of the position of the two walkers completely determines the position of the c.o.m., i.e.
{H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t))=H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t),V(t))}
. This behaviour is expected, as it resembles the increase in the interconnectedness of the RWs due to stronger interactions.

Finally, the TE from a RW to

does not provide any more information about

than what is given by

\mathcal{T}(\Delta_{j}(t);V(t^{\prime}))=0\quad\forall\gamma\,.

(20)

This result emphasises the statistically autonomous dynamics of the c.o.m. as a free random walker. This property is also known as

Bertschinger et al. 2006

IV.3
Emergence estimators

One promising aspect of information theory is that it allows us to quantify how
emergent
a system is, i.e. how collective phenomena arise from the interaction of a group of elementary constituents.
One novel approach to estimating causal emergence has been suggested by Rosas, Mediano
et al.

Rosas et al. 2020a
;
Mediano et al. 2022
, who provide a mathematical framework and practical criteria to evaluate the presence and type of emergence. Here we briefly revisit some of the key concepts of the theory, slightly changing the original notation to match our scenario.

Given a macroscopic feature of the system
V
and
N
microscopic components
\{\Delta_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
, three measures
are introduced to investigate whether
V
is an emergent property of the system
(c.f. Eqs. 10a-10c in
Rosas et al. 2020a
):

\displaystyle\Psi_{t,t^{\prime}}^{(1)}(V)

\displaystyle=I(V(t);V(t^{\prime}))-\sum_{j=1}^{N}I(\Delta_{j}(t);V(t^{\prime}))

(21)

\displaystyle\Delta^{(1)}_{t,t^{\prime}}(V)

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} \max_{i}\Bigl(I(V(t);&\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))+\\
&-\sum_{j=1}^{N}I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))\Bigr)\end{aligned}

(22)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{(1)}_{t,t^{\prime}}(V)

\displaystyle=\max_{i}\Bigl(I(V(t);\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))\Bigr)\,.

(23)

For ease of notation, we refer to these quantities as
\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}
,
\Delta_{tt^{\prime}}
,
\Gamma_{tt^{\prime}}
, respectively.
Based on these estimators, the theory
Rosas et al. 2020a
provides three sufficient conditions to determine the emergent nature of
V
:

(1)

\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}>0
is sufficient for
V
to be causally emergent.

(2)

\Delta_{tt^{\prime}}>0
is sufficient for
V
to have downward causation (i.e. influence on the microscopic components
\Delta_{j}
).

(3)

\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}>0
and
\Gamma_{tt^{\prime}}=0
are sufficient for
V
to be causally decoupled (i.e.
V
has information about itself that is not present in the individual
\Delta_{j}
).

Given the mutual information above (Eqs. (
12
)-(
16

\Psi_{tt^{\prime}},\Delta_{tt^{\prime}},\Gamma_{tt^{\prime}}
using the c.o.m. as

.
Referring for the full calculations to App.

\displaystyle\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}

On the other hand,

Now turning on the interactions (
\gamma>0

\Delta_{tt^{\prime}}

Overall, this is a clear example in which higher-order behaviours do not correspond to higher-order mechanisms:
\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}

The relationship between high-order interactions, emergence, and autonomy is a nuanced open problem, crucial for understanding results from real complex systems (Sec.

Partial Information Decomposition

Partial Information Decomposition (PID)

is a novel framework that provides a fine-grained characterisation of how information is distributed among multiple components of a system.
PID has been applied widely to the study of artificial neural networks

Formally, given three stochastic variables, of which two are denoted as sources (

\displaystyle I(X;Z)

\displaystyle=\text{Un}(X;Z)+\text{Red}(X,Y;Z)\,,

(27)

\displaystyle I(Y;Z)

\displaystyle=\text{Un}(Y;Z)+\text{Red}(X,Y;Z)\,,

(28)

\displaystyle I(X,Y;Z)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &=\text{Un}(X;Z)+\text{Un}(Y;Z)+\\
&+\text{Red}(X,Y;Z)+\text{Syn}(X,Y;Z)\,,\end{aligned}

(29)

where
\text{Un}(X;Z)
and
\text{Un}(Y;Z)
are the unique information about

X
and

\displaystyle\text{Red}(X,Y;Z)

V
Discussion

V.1
Mechanistic interpretations of information-theoretic measures

In this study, we analysed how various information metrics depend on the interactions present in a simple model of Gaussian random walkers (RWs), focusing on how these measures are related to the underlying interaction strength
\gamma
.
When measured over a few timesteps, we found that both transfer entropy and integrated information which only include RWs positively correlated with the coupling
\gamma
. Somewhat counter-intuitively, we also found that synergy (measured with Minimum Mutual Information (MMI)
Barrett 2015
) negatively correlated with interaction strength – suggesting that, in some conditions, MMI synergy may be thought of as quantifying complementarity
Milinkovic et al. 2024
more than high-order interactions. Interestingly, however, synergy became positively correlated with interaction strength under a causal intervention, showing that the original negative correlation was due to the instantaneous interdependencies between walkers.

Taken together, our results suggest that, in this simple physical scenario, information measures correlate with the mechanistic properties of the system under specific conditions. More specifically, this seems to occur when:

1.

the measures are computed at timescales close to the intrinsic timescale of the system;

2.

the informational contributions of steady-state and dynamics are disentangled, for example using causal intervention; and

3.

the measures are calculated solely on the RWs, ensuring that microscopic and macroscopic quantities are not mixed.

Condition 1 is motivated by the results in Figs.
4
-
5(a)
showing clearer correspondence with
\gamma
at short timescales (as well as by a broader line of work exploring the behaviour of information measures across timescales
Barnett and Seth 2017
).
Condition 2 is supported by the results in Fig.
4
, showing that under a causal intervention the system is more synergistic for stronger interactions – an effect which is usually concealed by the redundant contribution of the steady-state distribution of the RWs.
Finally, condition 3 is motivated by the results in Figs.
3
-
5(b)
, where introducing the centre of mass
V
in the calculations inverted the relationship between information measures and coupling strength.

Although informed by our synthetic study, we do not know to what extent these conditions generalise to other systems. Verifying these properties in broader settings is a promising avenue for future work.

V.2
Coarse-grainings and implications for empirical applications

In our analysis, we showed that macroscopic variables can have different information dynamics than their microscopic constituents. Our ability to interpret our findings was enabled by the fact that we are operating within a fully accounted for mathematical model that dictates the system’s mechanics, and therefore the micro-macro relationship. However, in empirical scenarios, one is likely not afforded this luxury.

Specifically, we saw that the TDMI, PID, and
\Phi
measures behave differently with respect to the interaction strength
\gamma
depending on whether the walkers or the c.o.m. are considered.
This phenomenon has direct implications for the application of such estimators to empirical data. For example, in electroencephalography (EEG) data, it can be the case due to volume conduction or electrolyte bridging that the signal read by a particular electrode is an average or mixed input from surrounding electrodes, rather than being the desired localised signal
Van den Broek et al. 1998
. Consequently, when analysing the information dynamics between two arbitrary electrodes, it is hard to tell whether these should be considered microscopic or macroscopic variables. Thus, such effects in empirical recordings can produce information-theoretic results leading to precisely opposing interpretations regarding the underlying mechanisms of the system (in this case, the activity of individual neurons).

More broadly, however, while remaining vigilant about the incidental mixing of scales may be considered a relatively easy task, relating results from reliably known scales is far more difficult
Barack and Krakauer 2021
. In fact, computing information-theoretic quantities on macroscopic structures whose generative processes are unknown might conceal unexpected statistical interdependencies, which will eventually be reflected in the measured information dynamics.
For example, it remains an open problem whether the BOLD signal obtained by functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) is a faithful reflection of neural activity happening in a particular voxel
Logothetis 2003
;
Ma et al. 2022
. Accordingly, work linking BOLD signal fluctuations with features like ongoing calcium dynamics remains highly non-trivial, with evidence for both convergent and divergent functional organisations
Vafaii et al. 2024
.

However, even if one can assume understanding of the data generative process, for instance that a local field potential (LFP) is the net voltage of the superposition of all surrounding synchronous membrane conductances
Reimann et al. 2013
, our results suggest that one should explicitly expect different information dynamics between these scales. For instance, the TDMI of the synaptic potentials may decrease with increased coupling, and the TDMI of the LFP may be independent of it (c.f. Eqs. (
12
)-(
14
)).

Therefore, our findings highlight the difficulty of bridging empirical results from disciplines operating on complex systems at different levels of organisation.

V.3
Causal emergence and autonomy

In Sec.
IV
we showed that, according to Ref.
Rosas et al. 2020a
, the centre of mass (c.o.m.) of the random walkers satisfies the criteria for causal decoupling, and thus could be considered an emergent property of the system. This was shown by a positive value of the emergence criterion,
\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}>0
.
In other words, this indicates that the c.o.m. strongly determines its own dynamics, with a vanishingly small contribution of each RW taken individually.

This behaviour naturally matches the statistically autonomous nature of the c.o.m., whose dynamics depend only on its previous states (Eq. (
11
)), but clashes with the intuition of
\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}
as quantifying higher-order mechanisms (since there are none, especially in the non-interacting system). This suggests that causal emergence – defined by
\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}>0
– does not always depict higher-order mechanisms, as it can also be seen as a measure of autonomy
Rosas et al. 2024
.
This result establishes an interesting connection between approaches to emergence based on synergy
Rosas et al. 2020a
;
Mediano et al. 2022
and others based on dynamical independence
Barnett and Seth 2023
;
Milinkovic et al. 2024
, opening a promising avenue for future research.

Moreover, we found that
\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}
is larger when the walkers do not interact.
4
4

4

Formally, this is due to the c.o.m.
V
acting as a free random walker, with
I(V(t);V(t^{\prime}))
being independent of
\gamma
, whereas the term
I(\Delta_{j}(t);V(t^{\prime}))
grows, thus decreasing the value of
\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}
(Eq. (
21
)).

Although this result may seem counter-intuitive, it highlights an important aspect of the original theory
Rosas et al. 2020a
: although
\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}>0
is a sufficient condition for causal emergence, the magnitude of
\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}
is not by itself indicative of the “amount” of emergence (or autonomy) in the system.

Hence, the notion of emergence provided by
\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}
should not be interpreted in a mechanistic sense.
In this case, thanks to the analytical tractability of the system, we could explain the emergent nature of the c.o.m. through its autonomy. Nonetheless, developing a measure that is sensitive only to emergence due to higher-order mechanisms (and not to autonomous dynamics) offers an interesting direction for future work.

V.4
Technical considerations on stationarity and geometry

Although the model treated in the main text is non-stationary, the results presented above are qualitatively the same for the stationary Ornstein-Uhlenbeck (OU) process studied in Apps.
C
-
D
.
Interestingly, it is possible to show that the steady-state limit of a stationary OU process corresponds to a Vector Autoregressive (VAR) model, which has been studied extensively in the information theory literature
Barnett and Seth 2011
;
Mediano et al. 2018
;
Chicharro 2011
. Solving the VAR model at first order in
\gamma
yields qualitatively similar results to the ones presented above, and shows behaviours consistent with prior literature, both on transfer entropy
Barnett and Seth 2011
and integrated information
Mediano et al. 2018
.

Finally, it is worth discussing the implications of the fact that mutual information between RWs and transfer entropy are second-order quantities in the interaction parameter
\gamma
.
This result has a clear geometric interpretation: for any differentiable information measure with a minimum at
\gamma=0
,
elementary calculus theorems guarantee that the first non-zero term in its expansion is of order
\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})
.
Thus, this also implies that any measure that can be written in terms of the Kullback-Leibler divergence (such as mutual information and transfer entropy) is a second-order measure in
\gamma
.
As a consequence, TE and MI might struggle to detect weak interactions, as their second-order dependence on the interaction strength increases the statistical power needed to distinguish weak interactions from noise.

Following this reasoning, we hypothesise that information measures based on other probability distances (e.g. total variation distance
Gibbs and Su 2002
) may scale better with low values of
\gamma
, potentially resulting in estimators that are more reliable for large, very weakly interacting systems, which are common across the complex systems literature
Garas et al. 2008
;
Csermely 2004
;
Berlow 1999
;
Karlin and McGregor 1972
;
Pichler et al. 2020
.

VI
Conclusion

In this work, we provided an in-depth study of the relationship between information-theoretic measures and the mechanistic properties of a system of random walkers, investigating how the latter might be related to the former and developing some intuitions based on the model’s physical parameters.
To do this, we analysed some of the currently most established as well as novel frameworks in information theory, including transfer entropy
Schreiber 2000
, PID
Williams and Beer 2010
and emergence
Rosas et al. 2020a
.
The simplicity of the 1D Gaussian random walkers enabled us to derive analytical approximations of all information measures based on the model’s dynamics, highlighting their functional dependence on the system’s physical parameters, such as the interaction strength.

Our results indicate that even in a simple scenario such as the one investigated here, establishing a direct link between information-theoretic quantities and the causal make-up of a complex system is challenging.
However, we noticed that these statistical measures reflect more faithfully the system’s mechanistic features when only microscopic quantities are used in the computations, when short timescales are considered, and when the system is subject to a maximum-entropy causal perturbation. In particular, we believe that future work on causal perturbations could help establish a deeper connection between behaviours and mechanisms
Rosas et al. 2022
.

This study suffers from two important limitations: in the case of
N>2
random walkers, we only consider interactions to the first order, effectively approximating the information measures to a regime of weak coupling, whereas with
N=2
random walkers we obtain exact results, but lack a meaningful number of system components. Although we mitigated these shortcomings by investigating both scenarios together, future work should continue developing numerical and analytical treatments of information quantities.

We believe this study is a small step in a larger effort to link physical systems to statistical models, in the hope that physics can provide meaningful intuitions to guide the use and development of information-theoretic measures.
We hope this analysis helps inform the interplay between statistical interdependence and causal mechanisms, and motivates future work pushing the boundaries of the promising interaction between physics and information theory.

Appendix A
Information theory background

In this section, we review some of the fundamental quantities of information theory used in this work.

A.1
Mutual information

Mutual information (MI) is a crucial measure in information theory
Cover and Thomas 1999
, being widely used by itself or as a building block for more sophisticated metrics
Beer and Williams 2015
;
Mediano et al. 2019
.
MI measures the pairwise interdependence between two stochastic variables
X,Y
: assuming
X,Y
are distributed according to the probabilities densities
p_{X}(x)
,
p_{Y}(y)
, and
p_{X,Y}(x;y)
, the mutual information between
X
and
Y
is defined as

I(X;Y)=\sum_{x}\sum_{y}p_{X,Y}(x;y)\ln\left(\frac{p_{X,Y}(x;y)}{p_{X}(x)p_{Y}(y)}\right)\,

(45)

where the two sums run over all possible outcomes
x,y
of the random variables
X
and
Y
, respectively.
Given the definition of the Shannon entropy of a random variable
X

H(X)=\sum_{x}p_{X}(x)\ln\frac{1}{p_{X}(x)}\,,

(46)

it is illuminating to express MI in terms of entropies

\displaystyle I(X;Y)

\displaystyle=H(X)-H(X|Y)

(47)

\displaystyle=H(X)+H(Y)-H(X,Y)

where
H(X|Y)
is the conditional entropy of
X
given
Y
, and
H(X,Y)
is the joint entropy of the two.
In particular, if
X,Y
are jointly Gaussian distributed with mean
\mu=0
and covariance matrix
\Sigma
, then the entropy in Eq. (
46
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle H(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(|2\pi e\Sigma_{X}|\right)

(48)

\displaystyle H(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(|2\pi e\Sigma|\right)\,,

(49)

where
\Sigma_{X}
is the covariance matrix of
X
. An analogous expression holds for
H(Y)
.
Thus, calculating the mutual information between Gaussian processes only involves estimating the covariance matrices of the distributions, and then using Eqs. (
47
)-(
49
) to obtain

I(X;Y)=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{|\Sigma_{X}|\,|\Sigma_{Y}|}{|\Sigma|}\right)\,.

(50)

A.2
Transfer entropy

Transfer entropy was introduced by Schreiber
Schreiber 2000
as a non-parametric statistical measure based on the concept of conditional mutual information.
Within the study of complex systems, TE aims to uncover relationships between the different components, potentially probing causal and dynamical interactions
Lizier and Prokopenko 2010
;
Hahs and Pethel 2011
, and is equivalent to Granger Causality for Gaussian variables
Barnett et al. 2009
;
Granger 1963
;
Geweke 1982
.
Its applications span various fields, including neuroscience
Wibral et al. 2014
;
Vakorin et al. 2010
, finance
Dimpfl and Peter 2013
;
Korbel et al. 2019
, climate science
Stramaglia et al. 2024
;
Runge et al. 2012
, and engineering
Bauer et al. 2006
;
Overbey and Todd 2009
, in which TE has profoundly contributed to the understanding of how information propagates through networks and affects the behaviour of interconnected elements.

Unlike simpler correlations, transfer entropy captures non-linear dependencies and is particularly effective in identifying causal relationships in complex and coupled systems.
Specifically, it assesses how much the future state of one system (the target) can be predicted by knowing the past state of another (the source), beyond what the target’s past alone provides.
Similarly to the expression of MI in Eq. (
47
), it is convenient to look at how TE can be expressed in terms of Shannon entropies:

\mathcal{T}(X(t-L);Y(t))=H(Y_{t}|Y_{t-1:t-L})-H(Y_{t}|Y_{t-1:t-L},X_{t-1:t-L})\,,

(51)

where
{X_{t-1:t-L}\equiv(X_{t-1},X_{t-2},...,X_{t-L})}
. This expression highlights that TE is a measure of how much one time series
Y
depends on the past history of another time series
X
: if
Y
is independent of
X
, the two conditioned entropies on the right-hand side will be equal, and TE vanishes. Instead, if the past of
X
fully determines
Y_{t}
, then
H(Y_{t}|Y_{t-1:t-L},X_{t-1:t-L})=0
and the transfer entropy is maximum.

Appendix B

We briefly present the details of the simple swapping signal used in the Sec.

We consider a function

-A&\mbox{with probability $1-\alpha$.}\end{array}\right.

(52)

-A

and

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(\,p\{0\;{\rm swaps}\}+p\{2\;{\rm swaps}\}+p\{4\;{\rm swaps}\}+\ldots\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(e^{-\lambda t}+\frac{(\lambda t)^{2}}{2!}e^{-\lambda t}+\frac{(\lambda t)^{4}}{4!}e^{-\lambda t}+\cdots\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha e^{-\lambda t}\cosh(\lambda t)

The other joint probabilities are computed the same way, reading

\displaystyle p(f(0)=-A;f(t)=A)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)e^{-\lambda t}\sinh(\lambda t)

(55)

\displaystyle p(f(0)=A;f(t)=-A)

\displaystyle=\alpha e^{-\lambda t}\sinh(\lambda t)

(56)

\displaystyle p(f(0)=-A;f(t)=-A)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)e^{-\lambda t}\cosh(\lambda t)\,.

(57)

Finally,

\displaystyle p(f(t)=A)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &=p(f(0)=A;f(t)=A)+p(f(0)=-A;f(t)=A)\\

(58)

\displaystyle p(f(t)=-A)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &=p(f(0)=A;f(t)=-A)+p(f(0)=-A;f(t)=-A)\\
&=\alpha e^{-\lambda t}\sinh(\lambda t)+(1-\alpha)e^{-\lambda t}\cosh(\lambda t)\\
&=e^{-\lambda t}\cosh(\lambda t)-\alpha e^{-2\lambda t}\end{aligned}

(59)

Now using Eq. (
45
) and doing some algebra, we obtain the mutual information between

and
f(t)

\displaystyle I(f(0);f(t))=\frac{1}{2}\left[a\log a+b\log b-(a-c)\log(a-c)-(b+c)\log(b+c)\right]\,,

(60)

where we introduced the shorthand notation

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=1+\exp(-2\lambda t)

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=1-\exp(-2\lambda t)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=2\alpha\exp(-2\lambda t)\,.

We use this expression to illustrate the behaviour of the mutual information with
\alpha
fixed and varying the stochastic element, i.e. the rate of fluctuations
\gamma
, and for fixed

(Fig.

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck processes are a type of stochastic process used to model systems that tend toward a stable equilibrium over time.
Unlike a simple Brownian motion, which drifts indefinitely, the OU process introduces a restoring force that pulls values back toward a long-term mean, making it especially useful for modelling mean-reverting behaviour in time series data, such as interest rates in finance

Björk 2009

\eta_{t}
a white noise term, and

to 1.

We employ OU processes since their mathematical tractability allows us to study in detail how information-theoretic measures behave on a simple coupled system with stationary dynamics.

We are now finally ready to calculate the mutual information between the different terms. The following manipulations consist of substituting the variances and covariances found above in Eq. (

We begin with the MI of a walker between its positions at time

\begin{split}&I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\dfrac{\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\Delta_{j}(t)\Bigr)\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})\Bigr)}{|\Sigma_{\Delta_{j}\Delta_{j}^{\prime}}|}}\\

-\Biggl(\sigma^{2}\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\alpha^{t^{\prime}-t}\left[1-2\gamma\left(\dfrac{(t^{\prime}-t)}{\alpha}+\dfrac{2\alpha\left(1-t\alpha^{2(t-1)}+(t-1)\alpha^{2t}\right)}{(1-\alpha^{2})(1-\alpha^{2t})}\right)\right]\Biggr)^{2}\end{multlined}}\right)}\\

-\left(\dfrac{4\gamma\alpha}{1-\alpha^{2}}\left(\dfrac{\left(1-t\alpha^{2(t-1)}+(t-1)\alpha^{2t}\right)}{1-\alpha^{2t}}+\dfrac{\left(1-t^{\prime}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-1)}+(t^{\prime}-1)\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}\right)}{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}\right)\right)\Biggr]\end{multlined}}{\dfrac{(1-\alpha^{2t})(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})}{(1-\alpha^{2})^{2}}-\left(\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\right)^{2}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}\right)}\\

&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{N}\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\cdot\sigma^{2}\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\left(1-\dfrac{4\gamma\alpha\left(1-t^{\prime}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-1)}+(t^{\prime}-1)\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}\right)}{(1-\alpha^{2})(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})}\right)}{\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{N}\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\cdot\sigma^{2}\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\left(1-\dfrac{4\gamma\alpha\left(1-t^{\prime}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-1)}+(t^{\prime}-1)\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}\right)}{(1-\alpha^{2})(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})}\right)-\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{N}\right)^{2}\left(\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\right)^{2}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}\right)}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}\cdot\left(1+\dfrac{4\gamma\alpha\dfrac{\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t}}{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}\left(1-t^{\prime}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-1)}+(t^{\prime}-1)\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}\right)}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}\right)\right)}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}\right)}+\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(1+\dfrac{4\gamma\alpha\dfrac{\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t}}{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}\left(1-t^{\prime}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-1)}+(t^{\prime}-1)\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}\right)}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}\right)}\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}\right)}+\dfrac{2\gamma\alpha\dfrac{\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t}}{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}\left(1-t^{\prime}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-1)}+(t^{\prime}-1)\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}\right)}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}\end{split}\,.

(83)

Although these expressions could seem quite intricate, the key takeaway is simple: stronger restoring forces, i.e. smaller values of
\alpha\in(0,1]
, suppress the magnitude of the mutual information by erasing the memory of the system, and thus decrease the information transfer.

C.3.1
\theta\rightarrow 0
limit

We can recover the expressions outlined in the main part of the work by taking the limit
\theta\rightarrow 0
(or equivalently
\alpha\rightarrow 1
) in the equations above.
This can be achieved by simply noting that:

\displaystyle 1-\alpha^{2x}=1-(1-\theta)^{2x}\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits_{\theta\to 0}}2x\theta+o(\theta)

(84)

\displaystyle 1-x\alpha^{2(x-1)}+(x-1)\alpha^{2x}=1-x(1-\theta)^{2(x-1)}+(x-1)(1-\theta)^{2x}\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits_{\theta\to 0}}2x(x-1)\theta^{2}+o(\theta^{2})

(85)

Thus, Eqs. (
80
)-(
83
) become

\displaystyle\begin{split}I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))&\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits_{\alpha\to 1}}\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{2t^{\prime}}{2(t^{\prime}-t)}\right)}-\dfrac{2\gamma\,2t}{2(t^{\prime}-t)}\left(t^{\prime}-t+\dfrac{2t(t-1)}{4t}-\dfrac{2t^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-1)}{4t^{\prime}}\right)\\
&\,\,\,=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{t^{\prime}}{t^{\prime}-t}\right)}-\dfrac{2\gamma t}{t^{\prime}-t}\left(\dfrac{t^{\prime}-t}{2}\right)\\
&\,\,\,=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma t\,,\end{split}

(86)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I(V(t);V(t^{\prime}))&\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits_{\alpha\to 1}}\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{2t^{\prime}}{2(t^{\prime}-t)}\right)}\\
&\,\,\,=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}\,,\end{split}

(87)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I(\Delta_{j}(t);V(t^{\prime}))&\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits_{\alpha\to 1}}\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N\,2t^{\prime}}{N\,2t^{\prime}-2t}\right)}+\dfrac{2\gamma\dfrac{2t(t-1)}{2}}{N\,2t^{\prime}-2t}\\
&\,\,\,\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{N\tau}{N\tau-1}\right)}+\gamma\frac{t-1}{N\tau-1}\,,\end{split}

(88)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I(V(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))&\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits_{\alpha\to 1}}\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N\,2t^{\prime}}{N\,2t^{\prime}-2t}\right)}+\dfrac{2\gamma\dfrac{2t}{2t^{\prime}}\dfrac{2t^{\prime}(t^{\prime}-1)}{2}}{N\,2t^{\prime}-2t}\\
&\,\,\,=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{N\tau}{N\tau-1}\right)}+\gamma\frac{t^{\prime}-1}{N\tau-1}\,,\end{split}

(89)

where
\tau\equiv t^{\prime}/t
.

C.4
Transfer entropy

Here we report the calculations for TE between walkers and between one walker and the c.o.m.  in both directions.
Since the calculations for TE in the general OU case would be somewhat cumbersome, we focus on the simpler case in which
\theta\to 0
.
Making use of Eq.(
51
), the TE from one walker to another reads

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}(\Delta_{i}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))&=H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t))-H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t(\tau-1)(1-2\gamma(t^{\prime}-t-1))\right)+\\
&\quad-\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t(\tau-1)(1-2\gamma(t^{\prime}-t-1))\right)\\
&=0\,,\end{split}

(90)

where we employed

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t))&=H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))-I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t^{\prime}(1-2\gamma(t^{\prime}-1))\right)-\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}(1-2\gamma t)\right)}\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t(\tau-1)(1-2\gamma(t^{\prime}-t-1))\right)\end{split}

(91)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t))&=H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t))-H(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}\right)^{3}t^{3}(\tau-1)(1-2\gamma(t^{\prime}+t-3))\right)-\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}\right)^{2}t^{2}(1-2\gamma(2t-2))\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t(\tau-1)(1-2\gamma(t^{\prime}-t-1))\right)\,.\end{split}

(92)

The transfer entropy from the c.o.m. to one walker is to first order in
\gamma

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}(V(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))&=H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t))-H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t),V(t))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t(\tau-1)(1-2\gamma(t^{\prime}-t-1))\right)+\\
&\quad-\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t(\tau-1)\left(1-2\gamma(t^{\prime}-t-1)\right)\right)\\
&=0\,,\end{split}

(93)

where we used

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t),V(t))&=H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t),V(t))-H(\Delta_{j}(t),V(t))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}\right)^{3}\left(\dfrac{t}{N}\right)^{3}N(\tau-1)\Bigl(N(1-2\gamma(t^{\prime}-2))-1+2\gamma(t^{\prime}-t-1)\Bigr)\right)+\\
&\quad-\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}\right)^{2}\left(\dfrac{t}{N}\right)^{2}(N(1-2\gamma(t-1))-1)\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t(\tau-1)\dfrac{N(1-2\gamma(t^{\prime}-2))-1+2\gamma(t^{\prime}-t-1)}{N(1-2\gamma(t-1))-1}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t(\tau-1)\dfrac{(N-1)\left(1-2\gamma\dfrac{N}{N-1}(t^{\prime}-2)+2\gamma\dfrac{1}{N-1}(t^{\prime}-t-1)\right)\left(1+2\gamma\dfrac{N}{N-1}(t-1)\right)}{N-1}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t(\tau-1)\left(1-2\gamma(t^{\prime}-t-1)\right)\right)\,.\end{split}

(94)

Finally, the TE from one random walker to the centre of mass reads

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}(\Delta_{j}(t);V(t^{\prime}))&=H(V(t^{\prime})|V(t))-H(V(t^{\prime})|V(t),\Delta_{j}(t))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t\dfrac{\tau-1}{N}\right)-\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t\dfrac{\tau-1}{N}\right)\\
&=0\,,\end{split}

(95)

where we used

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(V(t^{\prime})|V(t))&=H(V(t^{\prime}))-I(V(t);V(t^{\prime}))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}t^{\prime}\right)-\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\frac{\sigma^{2}}{N}\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t\dfrac{\tau-1}{N}\right)\end{split}

(96)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(V(t^{\prime})|V(t),\Delta_{j}(t))&=H(V(t^{\prime}),V(t),\Delta_{j}(t))-H(V(t),\Delta_{j}(t))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}\right)^{3}\left(\dfrac{t}{N}\right)^{3}(\tau-1)(N(1-2\gamma(t-1))-1)\right)+\\
&\quad-\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}\right)^{2}\left(\dfrac{t}{N}\right)^{2}(N(1-2\gamma(t-1))-1)\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t\dfrac{\tau-1}{N}\right)\end{split}\,.

(97)

In all three cases above, we obtained that the transfer entropy vanishes. This result could have been expected as TE is a second-order quantity in
\gamma
.

C.5
Causal emergence quantities

In this section we provide the results for the
\Psi,\Gamma,\Delta
causal emergent quantities in the more general case in which
\theta\neq 0
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Psi_{tt^{\prime}}&=I(V(t);V(t^{\prime}))-\sum_{j=1}^{N}I(\Delta_{j}(t);V(t^{\prime}))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}\right)}-\frac{N}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}\right)}-N\dfrac{2\gamma\alpha\dfrac{\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\left(1-t\alpha^{2(t-1)}+(t-1)\alpha^{2t}\right)}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}\\
&\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits_{N\to+\infty}}\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}\right)}-\dfrac{\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}{(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})}-\dfrac{2\gamma\alpha\,\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}\left(1-t\alpha^{2(t-1)}+(t-1)\alpha^{2t}\right)}{(1-\alpha^{2})(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})}\end{split}

(98)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Delta_{tt^{\prime}}&=\max_{i}\Bigl(I(V(t);\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))-\sum_{j=1}^{N}I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))\Bigr)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}\right)}+\dfrac{2\gamma\alpha\dfrac{\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t}}{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}\left(1-t^{\prime}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-1)}+(t^{\prime}-1)\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}\right)}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}-\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}\right)}+\\
&\quad\quad+\dfrac{2\gamma(1-\alpha^{2t})\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}{1-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}\left(\dfrac{t^{\prime}-t}{\alpha}+\dfrac{\alpha\left(1-t\alpha^{2(t-1)}+(t-1)\alpha^{2t}\right)}{1-\alpha^{2t}(1-\alpha^{2})}-\dfrac{\alpha\left(1-t^{\prime}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-1)}+(t^{\prime}-1)\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}\right)}{(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})(1-\alpha^{2})}\right)\\
&\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits_{N\to+\infty}}-\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}\right)}+\\
&\quad\quad+\dfrac{2\gamma(1-\alpha^{2t})\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}{1-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}\left(\dfrac{t^{\prime}-t}{\alpha}+\dfrac{\alpha\left(1-t\alpha^{2(t-1)}+(t-1)\alpha^{2t}\right)}{1-\alpha^{2t}(1-\alpha^{2})}-\dfrac{\alpha\left(1-t^{\prime}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-1)}+(t^{\prime}-1)\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}\right)}{(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})(1-\alpha^{2})}\right)\end{split}

(99)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Gamma_{tt^{\prime}}&=\max_{i}\Bigl(I(V(t);\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))\Bigr)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}\right)}+\dfrac{2\gamma\alpha\dfrac{\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}}{1-\alpha^{2}}\dfrac{1-\alpha^{2t}}{1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}}\left(1-t^{\prime}\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-1)}+(t^{\prime}-1)\alpha^{2t^{\prime}}\right)}{N(1-\alpha^{2t^{\prime}})-\alpha^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}(1-\alpha^{2t})}\\
&\mathrel{\mathop{=}\limits_{N\to+\infty}}0\end{split}

(100)

C.6
Partial Information Decomposition

We present here the computations for the three PID cases outlined in Sec.
IV.4
.

(
1
)

Recalling

\begin{split}X&=\Delta_{j}(t),\quad Y=\Delta_{i}(t),\quad\text{and}\\
Z&=V(t^{\prime}),\,i\neq j\,,\end{split}

(104)

We compute the necessary mutual information:

\begin{split}I(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t);V(t^{\prime}))&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{|\Sigma_{\Delta_{j}\Delta_{i}}|\,\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(V(t^{\prime})\Bigr)}{|\Sigma_{\Delta_{j}\Delta_{i}V^{\prime}}|}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\left(\sigma^{2}t\right)^{2}\left(1-4\gamma(t-1)\right)\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t^{\prime}}{N}}{\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t}{N}\right)^{3}N\left(N\tau-2-2\gamma(t-1)\left(2N\tau-3\right)\right)}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{N\tau(1-4\gamma(t-1))}{(N\tau-2)\left(1-2\gamma(t-1)\dfrac{2N\tau-3}{N\tau-2}\right)}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{N\tau}{N\tau-2}\right)+\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left((1-4\gamma(t-1))\left(1+2\gamma(t-1)\dfrac{2N\tau-3}{N\tau-2}\right)\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{N\tau}{N\tau-2}\right)+\gamma\dfrac{t-1}{N\tau-2}\,.\end{split}

(105)

Using Eqs. (
15
)-(
105
) we then obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Red}(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t);V(t^{\prime}))&=I(\Delta_{j}(t);V(t^{\prime}))\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{N\tau}{N\tau-1}\right)}+\gamma\frac{t-1}{N\tau-1}\end{split}

(106)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Syn}(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t);V(t^{\prime}))&=I(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t);V(t^{\prime}))-I(\Delta_{j}(t);V(t^{\prime}))\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{N\tau-1}{N\tau-2}\right)}+\gamma\frac{t-1}{N\tau-2}-\gamma\frac{t-1}{N\tau-1}\end{split}

(107)

To obtain the normalised quantities, we simply divide each atom by the joint mutual information. We indicate these normalised quantities as
NMI
(Normalised by Mutual Information).

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Red}_{\text{NMI}}(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t);V(t^{\prime}))&\equiv\dfrac{\text{Red}(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t);V(t^{\prime}))}{I(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t);V(t^{\prime}))}\\
&=\dfrac{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N\tau}{N\tau-1}\right)}+\gamma\dfrac{t-1}{N\tau-1}}{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{N\tau}{N\tau-2}\right)+\gamma\dfrac{t-1}{N\tau-2}}\\
&=\dfrac{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N\tau}{N\tau-1}\right)}+\gamma(t-1)\left(\dfrac{1}{N\tau-1}-\dfrac{1}{N\tau-2}\dfrac{\ln{\left(\dfrac{N\tau}{N\tau-1}\right)}}{\ln{\left(\dfrac{N\tau}{N\tau-2}\right)}}\right)}{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N\tau}{N\tau-2}\right)}}\end{split}

(108)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Syn}_{\text{NMI}}(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t);V(t^{\prime}))&\equiv\dfrac{\text{Syn}(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t);V(t^{\prime}))}{I(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t);V(t^{\prime}))}\\
&=\dfrac{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N\tau-1}{N\tau-2}\right)}+\gamma\dfrac{t-1}{N\tau-2}-\gamma\dfrac{t-1}{N\tau-1}}{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{N\tau}{N\tau-2}\right)+\gamma\dfrac{t-1}{N\tau-2}}\\
&=\dfrac{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{N\tau-1}{N\tau-2}\right)}-\gamma(t-1)\left(\dfrac{1}{N\tau-1}+\dfrac{1}{N\tau-2}\dfrac{\ln{\left(\dfrac{N\tau-1}{N\tau-2}\right)}}{\ln\left(\dfrac{N\tau}{N\tau-2}\right)}-\dfrac{1}{N\tau-2}\right)}{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{N\tau}{N\tau-2}\right)}\,,\end{split}

(109)

where we factorised the terms in
\gamma
so that the multiplying parentheses are positive. Thus
\text{Red}_{\text{NMI}}
increases with the interaction strength, while
\text{Syn}_{\text{NMI}}
decreases.

(
2
)

In this case we look at

\begin{split}X&=\Delta_{j}(t),\quad Y=V(t),\quad\text{and}\\
Z&=\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\}\,.\end{split}

(110)

In this situation we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}I(\Delta_{j}(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\Delta_{j}(t)\Bigr)\,|\Sigma_{\Delta^{\prime}V^{\prime}}|}{|\Sigma_{\Delta\Delta^{\prime}V^{\prime}}|}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t}{N}\right)t(1-2\gamma(t-1))\,\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t}{N}\right)^{2}\tau^{2}(N-1)\left(1-2\gamma\dfrac{N(t^{\prime}-1)}{N-1}\right)}{\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t}{N}\right)^{3}\tau(\tau-1)N(N-1)\left(1-2\gamma\dfrac{N(t^{\prime}-2)+1}{N-1}\right)}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)+\ln\left(\left(1-2\gamma(t-1)\right)\left(1-\dfrac{2\gamma N(t^{\prime}-1)}{N-1}\right)\left(1+\dfrac{2\gamma(N(t^{\prime}-2)+1)}{N-1}\right)\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)+\ln\left(1+\dfrac{-2\gamma(t-1)(N-1)-2\gamma N+2\gamma}{N-1}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma t\\
&=I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))\end{split}

(111)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I(V(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(V(t)\Bigr)\,|\Sigma_{\Delta^{\prime}V^{\prime}}|}{|\Sigma_{V\Delta^{\prime}V^{\prime}}|}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t}{N}\right)\,\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t}{N}\right)^{2}\tau^{2}(N-1)\left(1-2\gamma\dfrac{N(t^{\prime}-1)}{N-1}\right)}{\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t}{N}\right)^{3}\tau(\tau-1)(N-1)\left(1-2\gamma\dfrac{N(t^{\prime}-1)}{N-1}\right)}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}\\
&=I(V(t);V(t^{\prime}))\end{split}

(112)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I(\Delta_{j}(t),V(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{|\Sigma_{\Delta V}|\,|\Sigma_{\Delta^{\prime}V^{\prime}}|}{|\Sigma_{\Delta V\Delta^{\prime}V^{\prime}}|}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t}{N}\right)^{2}\left(1-2\gamma\dfrac{N(t-1)}{N-1}\right)\,\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t}{N}\right)^{2}\tau^{2}(N-1)\left(1-2\gamma\dfrac{N(t^{\prime}-1)}{N-1}\right)}{\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t}{N}\right)^{4}(\tau-1)^{2}(N-1)^{2}\left(1-2\gamma\dfrac{N(t^{\prime}-2)}{N-1}\right)}\right)\\
&=\ln\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)+\ln\left(1-\dfrac{2\gamma N(t-1)+2\gamma N(t^{\prime}-1)-2\gamma N(t^{\prime}-2)}{N-1}\right)\\
&=\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma\frac{Nt}{N-1}\,.\end{split}

(113)

Redundancy and synergy then read

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Red}(\Delta_{j}(t),V(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})=&I(\Delta_{j}(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma t\end{split}

(114)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Syn}(\Delta_{j}(t),V(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})=&I(\Delta_{j}(t),V(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})-I(V(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma\frac{Nt}{N-1}\,.\end{split}

(115)

The normalised quantities can be computed as

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Red}_{\text{NMI}}(\Delta_{j}(t),V(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})&\equiv\dfrac{\text{Red}(\Delta_{j}(t),V(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})}{I(\Delta_{j}(t),V(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})}\\
&=\dfrac{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma t}{\ln{\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma\dfrac{Nt}{N-1}}\\
&=\dfrac{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma t\left(1-\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{N}{N-1}\right)}{\ln{\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}}\end{split}

(116)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Syn}_{\text{NMI}}(\Delta_{j}(t),V(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})&\equiv\dfrac{\text{Syn}(\Delta_{j}(t),V(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})}{I(\Delta_{j}(t),V(t);\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),V(t^{\prime})\})}\\
&=\dfrac{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma\dfrac{Nt}{N-1}}{\ln{\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma\dfrac{Nt}{N-1}}\\
&=\dfrac{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{Nt}{N-1}}{\ln{\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}}\,,\end{split}

(117)

showing that both normalised redundancy and synergy decrease with
\gamma
.

(
3
)

Finally, we focus on

\begin{split}X&=\Delta_{j}(t),\quad{Y=\Delta_{i}(t)},\quad\text{and}\\
Z&=\{\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime})\},\,i\neq j\,.\end{split}

(118)

The mutual information read

\displaystyle\begin{split}I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\Delta_{j}(t)\Bigr)|\Sigma_{\Delta_{j}^{\prime}\Delta_{i}^{\prime}}|}{|\Sigma_{\Delta_{j}\Delta_{j}^{\prime}\Delta_{i}^{\prime}}|}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\sigma^{2}t(1-2\gamma(t-1))\cdot(\sigma^{2}t^{\prime})^{2}(1-4\gamma(t^{\prime}-1))}{(\sigma^{2}t)^{3}\tau(\tau-1)(1-2\gamma(2t^{\prime}-3))}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)+\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\Bigl(1-2\gamma(t-1)-4\gamma(t^{\prime}-1)+2\gamma(2t^{\prime}-3)\Bigr)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)-\gamma t\\
&=I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))\end{split}

(119)

\displaystyle\begin{split}I(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}))&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{|\Sigma_{\Delta_{j}\Delta_{i}}||\Sigma_{\Delta_{j}^{\prime}\Delta_{i}^{\prime}}|}{|\Sigma_{\Delta_{j}\Delta_{i}\Delta_{j}^{\prime}\Delta_{i}^{\prime}}|}\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\dfrac{(\sigma^{2}t)^{2}(1-4\gamma(t-1))\cdot(\sigma^{2}t^{\prime})^{2}(1-4\gamma(t^{\prime}-1))}{(\sigma^{2}t)^{4}(\tau-1)^{2}(1-4\gamma(t^{\prime}-2))}\right)\\
&=\ln\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)+\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\Bigl(1-4\gamma(t-1)-4\gamma(t^{\prime}-1)+4\gamma(t^{\prime}-2)\Bigr)\\
&=\ln\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)-2\gamma t\\
&=2I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))\,.\end{split}

(120)

Thus, redundancy and synergy are

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Red}(\Delta_{i}(t),\Delta_{j}(t);\{\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})\})&=I(\Delta_{j}(t);\{\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})\})\\
&=I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})\})\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma t\end{split}

(121)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Syn}(\Delta_{i}(t),\Delta_{j}(t);\{\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})\})&=I(\Delta_{i}(t),\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))-I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))\\
&=2I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))-I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))\\
&=I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\frac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma t\,.\end{split}

(122)

Finally, we turn to the normalised atoms:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Red}_{\text{NMI}}(\Delta_{i}(t),\Delta_{j}(t);\{\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})\})&\equiv\dfrac{\text{Red}(\Delta_{i}(t),\Delta_{j}(t);\{\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})\})}{I(\Delta_{i}(t),\Delta_{j}(t);\{\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})\})}\\
&=\dfrac{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)}-\gamma t}{\ln\left(\dfrac{\tau}{\tau-1}\right)-2\gamma t}\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\end{split}

(123)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\text{Syn}_{\text{NMI}}(\Delta_{i}(t),\Delta_{j}(t);\{\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})\})&=\text{Red}_{\text{NMI}}(\Delta_{i}(t),\Delta_{j}(t);\{\Delta_{i}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})\})\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\,.\end{split}

(124)

We obtained that both atoms are constant in
\gamma
, which is a consequence of the first order approximation in
\gamma
. Since for small coupling strength there is complete symmetry between synergy and redundancy, each contributes to half the information in the system, hence resulting in
\text{Red}_{\text{NMI}}=\text{Syn}_{\text{NMI}}=1/2
.

C.7
Useful identities

We report here two identities which are helpful when computing the mutual information at first order.
Considering
F_{v},F_{c}
as the terms coming from variance and covariance expressions which
do not
contain
\gamma
, and
\epsilon_{v},\epsilon_{c}
the parts that
do
, the following holds

\begin{split}\dfrac{F_{v}(1+\epsilon_{v})}{F_{v}(1+\epsilon_{v})-F_{c}(1+\epsilon_{c})}&=\dfrac{F_{v}(1+\epsilon_{v})}{(F_{v}-F_{c})\left(1+\dfrac{F_{v}\epsilon_{v}-F_{c}\epsilon_{c}}{F_{v}-F_{c}}\right)}\\
&=\dfrac{F_{v}(1+\epsilon_{v})\left(1-\dfrac{F_{v}\epsilon_{v}-F_{c}\epsilon_{c}}{F_{v}-F_{c}}\right)}{F_{v}-F_{c}}+o(\epsilon)\\
&=\dfrac{F_{v}}{F_{v}-F_{c}}\cdot\left(1+\epsilon_{v}-\dfrac{F_{v}\epsilon_{v}-F_{c}\epsilon_{c}}{F_{v}-F_{c}}\right)+o(\epsilon)\\
&=\dfrac{F_{v}}{F_{v}-F_{c}}\cdot\left(1+\dfrac{F_{v}\epsilon_{v}-F_{c}\epsilon_{v}-F_{v}\epsilon_{v}+F_{c}\epsilon_{c}}{F_{v}-F_{c}}\right)+o(\epsilon)\\
&=\dfrac{F_{v}}{F_{v}-F_{c}}\cdot\left(1+\dfrac{F_{c}(\epsilon_{c}-\epsilon_{v})}{F_{v}-F_{c}}\right)+o(\epsilon)\,.\end{split}

(125)

where we expanded everything at first order in
\epsilon_{v},\epsilon_{c}
(i.e.
\gamma
).
By setting
\epsilon_{c}=0
, we obtain another useful expression

\dfrac{F_{v}(1+\epsilon_{v})}{F_{v}(1+\epsilon_{v})-F_{c}}=\dfrac{F_{v}}{F_{v}-F_{c}}\cdot\left(1-\dfrac{F_{c}\epsilon_{v}}{F_{v}-F_{c}}\right)+o(\epsilon)\,.

(126)

Appendix D
N=2
random walkers

In this section, we focus on a simpler system composed of only 2 random walkers. Although the dynamic is the same as the one studied in the
N
case, the presence of only two particles allows us to perform exact calculations at all orders in
\theta,\gamma
.
Clearly, no multi-component mechanical interactions can be present in such a system. However, it is still interesting to see how information theoretic quantities depend on multivariate terms, and if their behaviour changes when a larger number of components is then considered.
Drawing frequent connections with the
N
case studied above, we show this scenario of 2 RWs complements the previous analysis by tackling information measures from a slightly different angle.

D.1
Model

\displaystyle x_{1}(t+1)=\bigl(1-\theta\bigr)x_{1}(t)+\gamma\bigl(x_{2}(t)-x_{1}(t)-a\bigr)+\eta_{1}(t)

(127)

\displaystyle x_{2}(t+1)=\bigl(1-\theta\bigr)x_{2}(t)-\gamma\bigl(x_{2}(t)-x_{1}(t)-a\bigr)+\eta_{2}(t)\,,

(128)

as before, we express the equations of motion in terms of the displacement from their resting position
\Delta_{i}(t)=x_{i}(t)-a
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(t+1)=\bigl(1-\theta\bigr)\Delta_{1}(t)+\gamma\bigl(\Delta_{2}(t)-\Delta_{1}(t)\bigr)+\eta_{1}(t)

(129)

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(t+1)=\bigl(1-\theta\bigr)\Delta_{2}(t)-\gamma\bigl(\Delta_{2}(t)-\Delta_{1}(t)\bigr)+\eta_{2}(t)\,.

(130)

We can decouple the system by using the coordinates in the center-of-mass frame

\displaystyle\nu(t)\equiv\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\Delta_{1}(t)+\Delta_{2}(t))=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x_{1}(t)+x_{2}(t)-a)

(131)

\displaystyle\rho(t)\equiv\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\Delta_{2}(t)-\Delta_{1}(t))=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(x_{2}(t)+x_{1}(t)-a)\,,

(132)

and their inverse

\displaystyle\Delta_{1}(t)=\dfrac{\nu(t)-\rho(t)}{\sqrt{2}}

(133)

\displaystyle\Delta_{2}(t)=\dfrac{\nu(t)+\rho(t)}{\sqrt{2}}\,.

(134)

Note that
\rho
and
\nu
are not exactly the relative positions and center of mass of the displacements
\Delta
, as they differ by a constant and additive factors:
{V(t)=\nu(t)/\sqrt{2}+a}
and
{r(t)=\sqrt{2}\rho(t)+a}
.

However, using the new coordinates, the equations of motion become

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}\nu(t+1)&=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\Delta_{1}(t+1)+\Delta_{2}(t+1))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigl(\bigl(1-\theta\bigr)\Delta_{1}(t)+\gamma\bigl(\Delta_{2}(t)-\Delta_{1}(t)\bigr)+\eta_{1}(t)+\bigl(1-\theta\bigr)\Delta_{2}(t)-\gamma\bigl(\Delta_{2}(t)-\Delta_{1}(t)\bigr)+\eta_{2}(t)\Bigr)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigl((1-\theta)\bigl(\Delta_{1}(t)+\Delta_{2}(t)\bigr)+\eta_{1}(t)+\eta_{2}(t)\Bigr)\\
&=(1-\theta)\nu(t)+\eta_{+}(t)\,,\end{split}

(135)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho(t+1)&=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\Delta_{2}(t+1)-\Delta_{1}(t+1))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigl(\bigl(1-\theta\bigr)\Delta_{2}(t)-\gamma\bigl(\Delta_{2}(t)-\Delta_{1}(t)\bigr)+\eta_{2}(t)-\bigl(1-\theta\bigr)\Delta_{1}(t)-\gamma\bigl(\Delta_{2}(t)-\Delta_{1}(t)\bigr)-\eta_{1}(t)\Bigr)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Bigl((1-\theta-2\gamma)(\Delta_{2}(t)-\Delta_{1}(t))+\eta_{2}(t)-\eta_{1}(t)\Bigr)\\
&=(1-\theta-2\gamma)\rho(t)+\eta_{-}(t)\,,\end{split}

(136)

where
{\eta_{+}(t)=(\eta_{1}(t)+\eta_{2}(t))/\sqrt{2}}
and
{\eta_{-}(t)=(\eta_{2}(t)-\eta_{1}(t))/\sqrt{2}}
are Gaussian variables with mean zero and variance
\sigma^{2}
. In other words,
\eta_{\pm}(t)
behaves just like the usual terms
{\eta_{i}(t),i=1,2,...,N}
employed in the N-case.
Moreover, we have
{\mathrm{Cov}\Bigl(\eta_{j}(t_{m});\eta_{i}(t_{n})\Bigr)=\sigma^{2}\delta_{ij}\delta_{mn}},\,\,\text{with }i,j=\pm,\,m,n>0
, where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta, confirming that
\nu
and
\rho
are indeed decoupled.

Recursively applying Eqs. (
135
)-(
136

(137)

(138)

where we introduced the shorthands
{\alpha\equiv(1-\theta)}
and
{\beta\equiv(1-\theta-2\gamma)}
.
Choosing
{\Delta_{1,2}(0)=0}

(142)

In this section, we report the computations of transfer entropy across walkers and between walkers and centre of mass.

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}(\Delta_{i}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))&=H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t))-H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\left(\left(t+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)\left(t^{\prime}+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)-\left(t+\beta^{t^{\prime}-t}\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)}{t+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}}\right)+\\
&\quad-\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\cdot\left(t^{\prime}-t+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\beta^{2}}-\beta^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)\right)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\left(\left(t+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)\left(t^{\prime}+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)-\left(t+\beta^{t^{\prime}-t}\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)^{2}\right)}{\left(t+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)\left(t^{\prime}-t+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\beta^{2}}-\beta^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)}\right)\,,\end{split}

(159)

where we used

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t))&=H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))-I(\Delta_{j}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(t^{\prime}+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)\right)+\\

(160)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t))&=H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}),\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t))-H(\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left(2\pi e\,\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)^{3}4t\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\left(t^{\prime}-t+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t^{\prime}}}{1-\beta^{2}}-\beta^{2(t^{\prime}-t)}\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)\right)+\\

(161)

We observe that, in this simple case of 2 particles,
H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t),\Delta_{i}(t))=H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t),V(t))
, as measuring the position of one walker and the c.o.m. or the positions of both walkers provides the same information.

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}(V(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))&=H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t))-H(\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime})|\Delta_{j}(t),V(t))\\

&=\mathcal{T}(\Delta_{i}(t);\Delta_{j}(t^{\prime}))\,.\end{split}

(162)

Finally, the transfer entropy from one walker to the c.o.m. reads

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}(\Delta_{j}(t);V(t^{\prime}))&=H(V(t^{\prime})|V(t))-H(V(t^{\prime})|V(t),\Delta_{j}(t))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t\dfrac{\tau-1}{2}\right)-\dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\left(2\pi e\,\sigma^{2}t\dfrac{\tau-1}{2}\right)}\\
&=0\,,\end{split}

(163)

where we used

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(V(t^{\prime})|V(t))&=H(V(t^{\prime}))-I(V(t);V(t^{\prime}))\\

(164)

\displaystyle\begin{split}H(V(t^{\prime})|V(t),\Delta_{j}(t))&=H(V(t^{\prime}),V(t),\Delta_{j}(t))-H(V(t),\Delta_{j}(t))\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left(2\pi e\,\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)^{3}(\tau-1)\,t^{2}\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)-\dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left(2\pi e\,\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}t\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)\\

(165)

The joint entropies above are calculated using Eq. (
49

We see that TE between walkers increases with the interaction strength
\gamma

obtained in the N case was a confound due to the calculations at first order in

This also accounts for the transfer entropy from the center of mass to one random walker.
On the other hand, we obtained that

D.7
Causal intervention

In this section, we complement the results showed in Sec.
IV.4
employing the intervention method as a possible approach to investigate the causal interdependencies within a system.
We achieve this by implementing a perturbative analysis in which the transition from one state to another follows the equation of motion of the original system, but the distribution of
\mathbf{\Delta}(t)
at time
t
of intervention is replaced by a configuration of maximum entropy
Pearl 1995
;
Tononi 2001
;
Tononi and Sporns 2003
;
Tononi 2004
;
Ay and Polani 2008
.
This way, the information generated in the system is only due to its evolution through different states.
In the specific scenario of
N=2
RWs, this consists of investigating the dynamics of the two independent walkers that evolve following Eqs. (
129
)-(
130
).
Denoting with
p
the distributions of the original system and with
q
the ones undergoing intervention, we have:

q(\mathbf{\Delta}(t^{\prime}),\mathbf{\Delta}(t))=p(\mathbf{\Delta}(t^{\prime})|\mathbf{\Delta}(t))q(\mathbf{\Delta}(t))\,,

(173)

where
{\mathbf{\Delta}\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\Delta_{1}&\Delta_{2}\end{pmatrix}^{T}}
,
{p(\mathbf{\Delta}(t^{\prime})|\mathbf{\Delta}(t))}
is the conditional distribution of the original system, and
{q(\mathbf{\Delta}(t))=p(\Delta_{1}(t))\,p(\Delta_{2}(t))}
is the input distribution modelled as a maximum entropy distribution with the marginals equal to the original system.
Therefore, this analysis allows one to examine the ensemble of states
{q(\mathbf{\Delta}(t^{\prime}))}
that are reached solely due to the interactions in the evolution of the dynamics.

Considering for simplicity the case in which
\theta=0
, the equations of motions between two timepoints
t,t^{\prime}
,
t^{\prime}>t
read

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}(t^{\prime})=A\mathbf{\Delta}(t)+V,\quad\text{with }\quad A=\left(\begin{matrix}\displaystyle\frac{1+\beta^{\Delta t}}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{1-\beta^{\Delta t}}{2}\vskip 4.2679pt\\
\displaystyle\frac{1-\beta^{\Delta t}}{2}&\displaystyle\frac{1+\beta^{\Delta t}}{2}\end{matrix}\right),\quad V=\left(\begin{matrix}\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{t}=t}^{t^{\prime}-1}\Bigl(\eta_{+}(\tilde{t})+\beta^{t^{\prime}-t-1}\eta_{-}(\tilde{t})\Bigr)\vskip 4.2679pt\\
\displaystyle\sum_{\tilde{t}=t}^{t^{\prime}-1}\Bigl(\eta_{+}(\tilde{t})-\beta^{t^{\prime}-t-1}\eta_{-}(\tilde{t})\Bigr)\end{matrix}\right),

(174)

where we introduced
\Delta t\equiv t^{\prime}-t
and used
\beta=1-2\gamma
.
With this and Eqs. (
143
)-(
146
) at hand, we start constructing the probability distributions needed in Eq. (
173
).
We have that

\mathbf{\Delta}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0\,,\,\Sigma_{t}\right),\,\quad\text{with}\quad\Sigma_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(t+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)&0\\
0&\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(t+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\right)\end{pmatrix}

(175)

and

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}(t^{\prime})|\mathbf{\Delta}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(A\mathbf{\Delta}(t)\,,\,\Sigma_{t^{\prime}|t}\right),\quad\text{with}\vskip 8.53581pt

(176)

\displaystyle\Sigma_{t^{\prime}|t}\equiv\tilde{\Sigma}_{t^{\prime}}-\tilde{\Sigma}_{t;t^{\prime}}^{T}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t}^{-1}\tilde{\Sigma}_{t;t^{\prime}}=\dfrac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\left(\begin{matrix}\Delta t+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2\Delta t}}{1-\beta^{2}}&\Delta t-\dfrac{1-\beta^{2\Delta t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\vskip 5.69054pt\\
\Delta t-\dfrac{1-\beta^{2\Delta t}}{1-\beta^{2}}&\Delta t+\dfrac{1-\beta^{2\Delta t}}{1-\beta^{2}}\end{matrix}\right),

where the
\,\tilde{}\,
indicates that the covariances belong to the distributions
p
of the original system.
Finally, we can calculate the joint distribution by multiplying the Gaussian distributions of Eqs. (
175
)-(
176
), which provides the full covariance of the system:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\Delta}(t^{\prime}),\mathbf{\Delta}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}\left(0\,,\,\Sigma\right),\quad\text{with}\vskip 5.69054pt

(177)

\displaystyle\Sigma\equiv\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{t^{\prime}}&\Sigma_{t;t^{\prime}}^{T}\\

Appendix E
Numerical tests

In this section, we report the numerical calculations of information-theoretic measures performed on the various systems studied above.
Since these measures rely on estimating joint and marginal probability distributions, their accuracy is inevitably heavily dependent on numerics and data availability.
Therefore, by comparing them with the theoretical formulas found in previous sections, the objective here is to simultaneously validate our theoretical results and test the robustness of numerical procedures of these metrics.
Finally, we assess and discuss how numerical approximations and round-up errors can affect the assessment of emergent properties of the system.

For brevity, we focus exclusively on the estimation of the mutual information in the case of

Lizier 2014
