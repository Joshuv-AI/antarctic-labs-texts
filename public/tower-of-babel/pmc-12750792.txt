Activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor relieves acute pancreatitis via the RBX1/HSF1 pathway
Authors: Yu Fang, Chuanhai Zhang, Jihai Yu, Xiayong Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute pancreatitis (AP) represents one of the most prevalent acute gastrointestinal disorders. Research has indicated a significant correlation between aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) signaling and pancreatic injury associated with AP. Nevertheless, the specific role of AhR signaling in tight junction injury induced by AP remains to be fully understood.MethodsAn experimental model of severe AP was established through repeated cerulein injections in conjunction with a single LPS injection. We evaluated pancreatic injury by quantifying serum amylase activity, LDH levels, pancreatic MPO levels, histopathological changes, tight junction protein expression, and the infiltration and polarization of macrophages. The regulatory interactions between AhR and HSF1 were analyzed through experimental validation and predictions derived from the BioGRID and Ubibrowser databases.ResultsAhR exhibited low expression and a negative correlation with the M1 polarization of pancreatic resident macrophages (PRM). In primary acinar cells treated with cerulein, AhR enhanced the activation of HSF1 signaling and mitigated the impairment of tight junction integrity. Conditioned medium from primary acinar cells overexpressing AhR resulted in the M2 polarization of PRM. RBX1 was identified as a potential mediator of the interaction between AhR and HSF1. RBX1 was found to facilitate HSF1 ubiquitination through its binding to HSF1, thereby promoting its degradation. Finally, AhR overexpression reduced cerulein/LPS-induced damage to pancreatic tight junction integrity in AP mice, while triptolide inhibited the therapeutic effects.ConclusionAhR facilitates the ubiquitination modification of HSF1 through its interaction with RBX1. This results in the suppression of HSF1 expression and activation, thereby mitigating cerulein/LPS-induced damage to pancreatic tight junctions in AP miceClinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12950-025-00481-7.Keywords: Aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR), HSF1, RBX1, Tight junction, Acute pancreatitis

Introduction

Acute pancreatitis (AP) is a prevalent inflammatory condition affecting the gastrointestinal tract, with its incidence increasing globally [1]. The histopathological progression of AP is characterized by structural damage, infiltration of inflammatory cells, and the regeneration of stromal formation and fibrosis. An early event in experimental AP is the disruption of pancreatic epithelial tight junctions, which exacerbates edema within the pancreatic parenchyma [2, 3]. Consequently, the integrity of tight junctions may be crucial in influencing the progression and severity of AP.

In recent years, the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) has emerged as a significant regulator of host-environment interactions, particularly concerning immune and inflammatory responses [4, 5]. Naringenin has been shown to mitigate severe intestinal injury associated with AP by inhibiting the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome, which are specifically triggered by the AhR signaling pathway [6]. Furthermore, human β-defensin-3 has been found to reduce tight junction barrier damage and alleviate inflammation resembling atopic dermatitis through the activation of keratinocyte autophagy, which also involves the activation of AhR signaling [7]. Thus, it is hypothesized that AhR signaling may play a pivotal role in the inflammatory response and tight junction impairment in AP.

Heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) serves as a master transcriptional regulator that orchestrates the induction of heat shock protein chaperones, thereby ensuring quality control of the proteome and maintaining protein homeostasis as a protective response to stress [8]. Systemic activation of HSF1 has been documented in patients with AP when compared to healthy individuals [9]. Additionally, AP has been associated with a marked increase in the expression of 70-kDa heat shock protein (HSP70) and HSP27, which correlates with heightened HSF1 binding activity and may provide a protective effect on residual acini following AP [10]. HSF1 has been reported to confer protection to AR42J pancreatic acinar cells from AP induced by cerulein through the inhibition of SOCS3 expression [11].

Ubiquitination and deubiquitination are reversible biochemical processes that can significantly modify the characteristics of target proteins, influencing their stability, intracellular localization, and enzymatic activity [12]. In the current study, analyses utilizing the BioGRID and Ubibrowser databases indicate that the E3 ubiquitin ligase RBX1 may mediate interactions between the AhR and HSF1. However, the regulatory role of RBX1 in the context of AP has not been previously documented. This study aims to explore the involvement of AhR binding to E3 ubiquitin ligase RBX1 in modulating HSF1 signaling during AP-related inflammatory injury and acinar cell tight junction disruption, with the objective of establishing a novel foundation for the treatment of AP.

Materials and methods

Experimental AP model construction

Male C57BL/6 mice, aged 6 to 8 weeks, were obtained from Hunan Slake Jingda Laboratory Animal Co. (Changsha, China). All mice were bred and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Animal Laboratory Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of USTC. To construct severe AP animal model, mice received seven intraperitoneal injections of cerulein (HY-A0190, MedChem Express, Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA) at a dosage of 50 µg/kg body weight at 1-hour intervals. Concomitant with the 7th cerulein injection, mice received a single intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 10 mg/kg). Control mice received equivalent volumes of 0.9% saline solution following the same injection schedule (including a saline injection during the 7th time point). Twenty-four hours following the final administration of cerulein and saline, the mice were anesthetized using isoflurane. Blood samples were collected from the inferior vena cava, after which the mice were euthanized, and the pancreas was promptly excised for further analysis.

To overexpress AhR protein, 200 µL of AAV vector plasmid (2 × 10⁹ PFU/mL) was injected into the tail vein 6 h after the establishment of the severe AP model [13]. HSF1 inhibitor triptolide (A3891, APExBIO) was prepared by dissolving it in 1 M NaOH containing β-cyclodextrin, followed by further dilution with PBS to achieve a total injection volume of 50–55 µL, corresponding to a final concentration of 0.4 mg/kg body weight, and was administered intraperitoneally on a daily basis [14]. Mice from other groups received treatment with a carrier solution as a control. Subsequently, mice were induced to AP after 7 days of intervention with triptolide.

Blood sample collection and biochemical analysis

Blood samples from each group were obtained and subjected to centrifugation at 250 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The enzymatic activity of serum amylase was evaluated through enzyme dynamics chemistry employing an α-Amylase assay kit (C016-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Additionally, the release of LDH in serum was quantified using the LDH assay kit (A020-2, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute), in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Myeloperoxidase (MPO) assay

For the assays, pancreatic tissue samples were homogenized. The resulting tissue homogenate was then analyzed for MPO activity using commercially available test kits (A044-1-1). All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions provided by Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute.

Histopathological scoring of the pancreas

Pancreatic tissue specimens were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution and subsequently embedded in paraffin. Sections of 5 μm in thickness were prepared from these specimens. The slides underwent a dewaxing and rehydration process. For the purpose of histopathological assessment, the slides were stained using hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). The extent of tissue damage was evaluated using a scoring system ranging from 0 to 3, as previously described [15]. Specifically, the scoring criteria for edema were as follows: 0 indicates absence; 1 denotes interlobular diffuse dilatation; 2 represents the same as 1 plus intermolecular diffuse dilatation; and 3 indicates the same as 2 plus intercellular diffuse dilatation. For inflammatory infiltrate, the scores were: 0 for absence; 1 for peripancreatic duct involvement; 2 for interlobular or perivascular infiltration affecting less than 50% of lobules; and 3 for interlobular or perivascular infiltration affecting more than 50% of lobules. Lastly, necrosis was scored as follows: 0 for absence; 1 for periductal necrosis involving less than 10% of cells; 2 for focal vesicular cell necrosis affecting 30%-50% of cells; and 3 for diffuse vesicular cell necrosis affecting more than 50% of cells.

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from pancreatic tissues and cell samples using TRIzol (15596026, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The total mRNA was subsequently reverse transcribed utilizing the HiFiScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (CW2569, CWBIO, Taizhou, China). cDNA amplification and quantification were performed with the PikoReal Real-Time PCR System (PIKOREAL96, Thermo Fisher Scientific), employing UltraSYBR Mixture (CW2601, CWBIO) for detection. β-actin served as the housekeeping gene, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was employed to determine relative gene expression levels. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Mouse pancreatic tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution (AWI0056a, Abiowell, Changsha, China). The sample sections were baked at 60 °C for 12 h. Subsequently, sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series to water. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed using either 0.01 M citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Endogenous enzymes were inactivated by applying 1% periodic acid at room temperature for 15 min. Primary antibodies, including F4/80 (1: 200; AWA10493, Abiowell), claudin 5 (1: 100; 29767-1-AP, Proteintech), occludin (1: 100; 27260-1-AP, Proteintech), and ZO-1 (1: 100; 21773-1-AP, Proteintech), were applied and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, sections were incubated with 50–100 µL of anti-rabbit IgG-HRP polymer secondary antibody (AWI0629, Abiowell) at 37 °C for 30 min. Chromogenic development was performed by applying 50–100 µL of DAB (3,3’-diaminobenzidine) working solution at room temperature for 1–5 min. Sections were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series (60% to 100%), with 5 min per concentration. After dehydration, sections were cleared in xylene (twice, 10 min each). Finally, sections were mounted with neutral balsam and examined under a microscope (BA410T, Motic, Xiamen, China).

Image analysis and quantification were performed using ImageJ software. For F4/80 staining, positively stained macrophages were counted in five randomly selected high-power fields (×400 magnification) per sample, and the average number was calculated. For the tight junction proteins claudin5, occludin, and ZO-1, the expression levels were quantified by measuring the integrated optical density (IOD) normalized to the area of interest (IOD/area) in five randomly selected fields per section.

Isolation of pancreatic resident macrophages (PRM)

Pancreatic tissue samples were rinsed with PBS to remove blood contaminants and adipose tissue, then minced into approximately 1 mm³ fragments using scissors. The tissue fragments were further mechanically dissociated in a small volume of PBS and subsequently transferred into pre-warmed (37 °C) DMEM high-glucose culture medium containing 0.2% collagenase I and 10% FBS for enzymatic digestion at 37 °C for 2 h. After digestion, the reaction was stopped by adding 3 mL of complete medium. The cell suspension was filtered through a 100 μm strainer to remove undigested tissue fragments. The filtrate was centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in pre-cooled sorting buffer for subsequent use.

Macrophages were further purified using magnetic bead-based sorting. The cell suspension obtained above was counted (approximately 1.8 × 107 cells). An aliquot of the cell suspension was centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellet was resuspended in 180 µL of sorting buffer, and 20 µL of anti-F4/80 magnetic beads (130-110-443, Miltenyi) were added. The mixture was thoroughly vortexed and incubated at 2–8 °C for 15 min. Then, 1 mL of sorting buffer was added, and the cells were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 10 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended in 500 µL of sorting buffer. The separation column was placed in the magnetic separator and equilibrated with 3 mL of sorting buffer. The cell suspension was slowly applied to the column, and the flow-through (unlabeled cells) was collected in a clean 50 mL centrifuge tube. The column was washed with 5 mL of sorting buffer, and this step was repeated three times. Finally, 3 mL of sorting buffer was applied to the column, and the buffer was rapidly flushed through using the column plunger. The eluted cells (F4/80-positive cells) were collected in a new 15 mL centrifuge tube. The cells were centrifuged to obtain a pellet and resuspended in RPMI-1640 complete medium for subsequent experiments. The resulting cells were identified as macrophages by flow cytometry and maintained in primary macrophage medium for further culture.

Flow cytometry for macrophage subset analysis

To evaluate the impact of various treatments on the polarization of PRM M1 and M2 subpopulations, we employed iNOS as a marker for the M1 subpopulation and CD206 for the M2 subpopulation. Initially, the cells were washed twice with PBS and subsequently incubated with the iNOS antibody (740025TP488, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for 30 min in a light-protected environment. For the assessment of the M2 phenotype, the cells were treated with a membrane-permeabilizing agent (00-5523-00, eBioscience, San Diego, CA, USA) at room temperature for 30 min, followed by a 30-minute incubation with the CD206 antibody (53-2061-82, eBioscience), also shielded from light. The cells were then resuspended in PBS containing 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Yancheng Saibao Biotech, Yancheng, China) and analyzed using flow cytometry (A00-1-1102, Beckman Coulter, Miami, FL, USA).

Western blot analysis

Tissue and cell samples were homogenized in ice-cold RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), which contained a protease inhibitor cocktail (583794, Jintai Hongda Biotec, Beijing, China) and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (P1260, ApplyGen, Beijing, China). Following the manufacturer’s instructions, protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Protein samples were then separated via 10% SDS-PAGE gel and transferred onto a nitrocellulose membrane. After adequate blocking with a 5% fat-free milk solution, the designated primary antibodies were applied to the membranes and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated IgG secondary antibody for 1.5 h at room temperature. The resulting bands were visualized using Chemiluminescent imaging system (ChemiScope6100, CLINX, Shanghai, China). All antibodies used in this study are listed in Table 2. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Pancreatic tissue was subjected to weighing and subsequently immersed in 900 mL of normal saline. This mixture underwent ultrasonic trituration followed by centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min to yield a pancreatic tissue homogenate. The concentrations of various cytokines, specifically IL-6 (EM0121, FineTest, Wuhan, China), TNF-α (EM0183, FineTest), and IL-1β (EM0109, FineTest), within the pancreatic tissue homogenate and PRM cell supernatant were quantified using ELISA kits in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. The samples were incubated for 90 min at room temperature, after which biotin labelled antibodies were introduced for an additional hour. Subsequently, streptavidin conjugated with horseradish peroxidase was added, and after a 30-minute incubation period, the substrate was introduced. The absorbance values were then measured using a microplate reader (MB-530, HEALES, Shenzhen, China), and the concentrations of the cytokines were determined based on the established standard curves.

Primary pancreatic acinar cell isolation and in vitro cultures

Pancreatic tissues were extracted from 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice and subsequently minced in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) supplemented with 10 mmol/L HEPES and 0.1% BSA. The minced pancreatic segments were then subjected to incubation in 10 mL of a collagenase IA solution, which contains 10 mmol/L HEPES, 0.25 mg/mL trypsin inhibitor, and 200 U/mL collagenase IA, for 20–30 min at 37 °C. Following this incubation, the collagenase solution was removed and replaced with Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; 10099141, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), 1% penicillin and streptomycin, 25 ng/mL recombinant mouse epidermal growth factor (Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 0.25 mg/mL trypsin inhibitor, maintained at 37 °C, 95% humidity, and 5% CO2. The identity and purity of the isolated primary mouse pancreatic acinar cells were confirmed by α-amylase immunostaining (Figure S1).

To induce in vitro AP model, primary mouse pancreatic acinar cells were treated with 10− 8 M cerulein for 24 h, while the Control group received an equivalent volume of PBS [16].

In order to evaluate the stability of the RBX1 protein, acinar cells were subjected to treatment with cycloheximide (CHX) at a concentration of 5 µM, thereby inhibiting protein synthesis. To investigate the potential degradation of the RBX1 protein via the ubiquitin-proteasome system, cells that had been engineered to overexpress RBX1 were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (20 µg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) for 4 h [17].

Cell transfection for gene silence and overexpress

The short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sourced from HonorGene (Changsha, China) were transfected into primary pancreatic acinar cells utilizing Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Invitrogen). RBX1 shRNA (sh-RBX1; HG-Mi019712) was employed to generate stable cells for the purpose of downregulating RBX1 expression. Plasmids containing cDNA for AhR (HG-MO013464) and RBX1 (HG-MO019712), also provided by HonorGene, were utilized to enhance the expression levels of AhR and RBX. All transfection protocols were conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Primary pancreatic acinar cells were harvested and subsequently lysed through the addition of ice-cold IP Lysis Buffer (AWB0144, Abiowell). The resulting samples underwent IP with either AhR or RBX1 antibody that was immobilized on agarose beads. Following elution, the samples were subjected to separation via 10% SDS-PAGE, followed by western blotting.

Ubiquitination assay

The cells were subjected to washing with PBS and subsequently lysed using IP lysis buffer supplemented with protease inhibitor (AWH0645, Abiowell) and phosphatase inhibitor (AWH0650, Abiowell) while maintained on ice for 30 min. The resulting lysates were quantified, and an equivalent volume of each lysate was utilized for IP with protein A/G agarose that had been pre-coated with the designated antibodies. The resin beads were then washed with the lysis buffer, and the samples were eluted. The eluted fractions were analyzed through SDS-PAGE electrophoresis. Following this, immunoblotting was conducted using the specified antibodies, HSF1 (1: 5000; ab2923, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA) and ubiquitin (1: 5000; 10201-2-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China).

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Cells were fixed using a 4% paraformaldehyde solution (AWI0056a, Abiowell) and permeabilized with PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 (AWI0603a, Abiowell). The samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against α-amylase (1: 50; AWA60917, Abiowell), claudin 5 (1: 50; bs-10296R, Bioss), occludin (1: 50; 27260-1-AP, Proteintech) and ZO-1 (1: 50; 21773-1-AP, Proteintech). Following this, the samples were treated with secondary antibody, specifically Alexa Fluor 488-labeled Goat anti-rabbit IgG (AWS0005a, Abiowell), for 90 min at ambient temperatures. The samples were then incubated for 10 min with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; AWC0293a, Abiowell), to facilitate nuclear staining. Fluorescent images were acquired and analyzed using a laser confocal microscope (BA210T, Motic).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed utilizing GraphPad Prism software. The distribution of variables was evaluated through the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation. The relationship between the two protein expression levels was examined using the Pearson correlation coefficient. A two-tailed Student’s t-test was employed to assess differences between groups. For multiple comparisons, one-way analysis of variance was conducted, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. A P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

AHR expression downregulation in AP mice is associated with macrophage polarization

A model for severe AP was developed using a protocol that involved multiple cerulein injections in conjunction with a single injection of LPS. The ratio of pancreas weight to body weight was significantly elevated in the AP group compared to the Sham group (Fig. 1A), indicating the successful induction of AP. AP is distinguished by markedly increased serum levels of LDH, as well as elevated activity of amylase [18]. Therefore, systemic manifestations of AP were evaluated through serum biochemical analyses measuring amylase activity and LDH levels. The findings revealed a significant increase in both amylase activity and LDH levels in the serum of mice following cerulein/LPS treatment (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, elevated MPO levels in the pancreas suggested an increase in neutrophil infiltration in the AP mice (Fig. 1B). Histological examination revealed no significant abnormalities in the pancreatic ducts and acini of the Sham group; however, the AP group exhibited considerable destruction of pancreatic tissue, characterized by disorganized lobular architecture, interstitial congestion, hemorrhage, and areas of coagulative necrosis, accompanied by inflammatory cell infiltration surrounding the necrotic regions (Fig. 1C and D). Additionally, qRT-PCR and IHC staining analysis demonstrated a significant reduction in the expression levels of tight junction-associated proteins, including claudin 5, occludin, and ZO-1, in the pancreatic tissues of AP mice (Fig. 1E-G), indicating that cerulein/LPS injections resulted in tight junction impairment within the pancreas. Collectively, these data confirm the successful establishment of the experimental AP model and the presence of pancreatic tight junction injury.

Macrophages are known to play a critical role in the progression of AP [19]. IHC analysis using the F4/80 marker revealed increased macrophage infiltration in the pancreas of AP mice (Fig. 1H), with the majority of resident macrophages exhibiting an M1 phenotype (Fig. 1I). Previous studies have indicated that activation of the AhR inhibits M1 polarization while promoting M2 polarization [20]. Consequently, we assessed the expression levels of AhR in the pancreatic tissues of AP mice and observed a significant decrease (Fig. 1J). Notably, AhR expression levels in the pancreatic tissues of AP mice were negatively correlated with the proportion of M1 macrophages and positively correlated with the proportion of M2 macrophages (Fig. 1K). Additionally, levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β, were significantly elevated following cerulein/LPS induction (Fig. 1L). These findings suggest that macrophages in the pancreas of AP mice are polarized towards the M1 phenotype, potentially linked to the inhibition of AhR activation.

AhR overexpression alleviates cerulein-induced tight junction damage in acinar cells through HSF1 signaling

Systemic activation of the stress response regulator HSF1 has been observed during the progression of AP [9], potentially providing a protective effect on the remaining acini following AP [10]. In our study, we investigated the expression levels of HSF1 in pancreatic tissues from mice with AP. Western blot analysis indicated a reduction in both the expression and phosphorylation levels of HSF1 following cerulein/LPS injections (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, activation of AhR and HSF1 was significantly inhibited in an in vitro model of cerulein-induced acinar cell damage (Fig. 2B). These findings imply that there may be a threshold to the protective effects of HSF1 on the acinar cells. Subsequently, we overexpressed AhR in acinar cells and confirmed the efficiency of this overexpression through qRT-PCR and western blotting (Fig. 2C). The cells were treated with cerulein for 24 h after transfection with overexpression plasmids. The results demonstrated that the cerulein-induced inhibition of AhR and HSF1 activation was significantly alleviated by AhR overexpression (Fig. 2D). Additionally, AhR overexpression markedly rescued cerulein-induced tight junction damage in the acinar cells (Fig. 2E). Next, we treated PRM cells with conditioned medium from primary pancreatic acinar cells and found that AhR overexpression significantly inhibited the polarization of M1 macrophages while promoting the polarization of M2 macrophages (Fig. 2F). Correspondingly, the secretion levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines were diminished by AhR overexpression (Fig. 2G). Collectively, these data suggest that AhR overexpression mitigates cerulein-induced tight junction injury in the acinar cells and the polarization of M1 macrophages, potentially through the activation of HSF1 signaling pathways.

AhR mediates the downregulation of RBX1 expression via its interaction with RBX1

To investigate the underlying mechanism by which AhR regulates HSF1, a total of 129 potential AhR-interacting molecules were identified through the BioGRID database (https://thebiogrid.org/). Additionally, 72 E3 ubiquitin ligases that may facilitate the ubiquitination of downstream proteins, specifically HSF1, were sourced from the Ubibrowser database (http://ubibrowser.ncpsb.org.cn). An intersection of these datasets revealed two significant ligases, STUB1 and RBX1 (Fig. 3A). In pancreatic tissues from AP mice, the expression levels of both STUB1 and RBX1 were markedly increased, with RBX1 exhibiting a more pronounced elevation (Fig. 3B). Similar findings were observed in cerulein-treated acinar cells (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, it was determined that overexpression of AhR significantly restored the elevated expression levels of RBX1 induced by cerulein treatment (Fig. 3D). Co-IP assays confirmed the interactions between AhR and RBX1 (Fig. 3E). Collectively, these findings suggest that AhR downregulates the expression of RBX1 through binding.

RBX1 promotes ubiquitination modification of HSF1 and induces proteasome-mediated degradation

The regulatory function of the E3 ubiquitin ligase RBX1 on the HSF1 protein was further investigated. We transfected a plasmid designed for the overexpression of RBX1 into primary mouse acinar cells and confirmed the efficiency of this overexpression (Fig. 4A). The overexpression of RBX1 was found to enhance the cerulein-induced upregulation of RBX1 and inactivation of HSF1 (Fig. 4B). Co-IP assays demonstrated the interaction between RBX1 and HSF1 (Fig. 4C). Furthermore, treatment of primary acinar cells with the protein synthesis inhibitor CHX indicated that RBX1 overexpression compromised the protein stability of HSF1 (Fig. 4D). To ascertain whether RBX1 facilitates HSF1 degradation via the ubiquitin-proteasome system, cells were treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG132, which completely reversed the degradation of HSF1 induced by RBX1 overexpression (Fig. 4E). Additionally, RBX1 overexpression was shown to significantly enhance the ubiquitination of HSF1 (Fig. 4F). These findings suggest that the E3 ubiquitin ligase RBX1 promotes the degradation of the target protein HSF1 by facilitating its ubiquitination.

Subsequently, we identified optimal silencing targets for RBX1 through qRT-PCR and western blotting (Fig. 4G), which were utilized in subsequent experiments. Silencing RBX1 significantly restored the expression and activation of HSF1 that had been suppressed by cerulein treatment (Fig. 4H). Moreover, the downregulation of RBX1 resulted in increased expression levels of claudin 5, occludin, and ZO-1 (Fig. 4I and J), indicating a mitigation of cerulein-induced tight junction damage in the acinar cells. Treatment of PRM cells with conditioned medium from RBX1-silenced acinar cells revealed a notable reduction in the proportion of M1-type macrophages and the secretion levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, while the proportion of M2-type macrophages was significantly elevated (Fig. 4K and L). In conclusion, RBX1 inhibits the expression and activation of HSF1 by promoting its degradation, which subsequently exacerbates cerulein-induced tight junction damage in acinar cells and enhances M1 macrophage polarization.

AhR upregulates HSF1 expression by binding to RBX1, alleviating acinar cell injury

Next, Co-IP analyses indicated a reduction in the binding affinity between RBX1 and HSF1 following the overexpression of AhR (Fig. 5A). This observation implies that AhR competes with HSF1 for binding to RBX1. The ubiquitination levels of HSF1 were then examined. Results revealed that AhR overexpression markedly suppressed RBX1-mediated HSF1 ubiquitination (Fig. 5A). To further investigate the intrinsic mechanism, we transfected acinar cells overexpressing AhR with a plasmid designed for RBX1 overexpression, which resulted in a significant restoration of RBX1 protein levels that had been diminished due to AhR overexpression (Fig. 5B). Additionally, the overexpression of RBX1 was found to partially reverse the increases in HSF1 protein levels and phosphorylation induced by AhR overexpression (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, the therapeutic effects of AhR overexpression on tight junction integrity in cerulein-treated acinar cells were also partially negated by RBX1 overexpression (Fig. 5C and D). In PRM cells treated with conditioned medium from primary acinar cells, RBX1 overexpression significantly mitigated the polarization of M1 macrophages and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, while also promoting the polarization of M2 macrophages that had been inhibited by AhR overexpression (Fig. 5E and F). These findings suggest that AhR enhances HSF1 expression through its interaction with RBX1, thereby contributing to the alleviation of tight junction injury in the acinar cells and the modulation of PRM polarization.

AhR overexpression attenuates pancreatic injury and preserves the integrity of tight junctions in AP mice via the RBX1/HSF1 axis

To assess the in vivo modulation of AhR activation, AP mice were administered the AAV vector plasmid for AhR overexpression. Additionally, the function of HSF1 was pharmacologically inhibited in AP mice through triptolide treatment. Western blot analysis of pancreatic tissues revealed that AhR overexpression led to an upregulation of AhR protein levels, as well as an increase in both the protein and phosphorylation levels of HSF1, which correspondingly resulted in a downregulation of RBX1 protein levels. Conversely, triptolide treatment resulted in a decrease in both the protein and phosphorylation levels of HSF1 (Fig. 6A). Compared to the AP mice, AhR overexpression resulted in a reduction of the pancreas-to-body weight ratio, while triptolide treatment led to an increase in this ratio (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, AhR overexpression was associated with a decrease in serum amylase activity and LDH levels, as well as a reduction in neutrophil infiltration within the pancreatic tissue of AP mice; in contrast, triptolide treatment exhibited the opposite effects (Fig. 6C). Histological examination using H&E staining indicated that AhR overexpression mitigated edema, inflammatory infiltration, and necrotic conditions in pancreatic tissues, whereas triptolide treatment exacerbated these injuries (Fig. 6D and E). Additionally, tight junction integrity in the pancreatic tissues of AP mice was preserved by AhR overexpression, while it was compromised by triptolide (Fig. 6F-H). Notably, triptolide treatment was found to inhibit the therapeutic effects of AhR overexpression on pancreatic injury in AP mice, and the exacerbation of pancreatic injury by triptolide in AP mice was also alleviated to some extent by AhR overexpression (Fig. 6B-H). Moreover, AhR overexpression significantly reduced macrophage infiltration, M1 macrophage polarization, and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in the pancreatic tissues of AP mice, whereas triptolide treatment exacerbated these conditions. Additionally, triptolide was found to negate the therapeutic effects of AhR overexpression on macrophage infiltration, M1 macrophage polarization, and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion (Fig. 6I-K). Collectively, these findings suggest that the AhR overexpression alleviates pancreatic injury in AP mice through the RBX1/HSF1 signaling axis, while the HSF1 inhibitor triptolide partially counteracts this protective effect.

Discussion

AP is a prevalent disorder within the digestive system; however, its underlying pathogenesis remains inadequately understood. A significant obstacle in the management of AP is the lack of a targeted therapeutic strategy for this condition [21]. Advancing our comprehension of AP has the potential to enhance clinical outcomes, as well as decrease rates of organ failure and mortality [22]. Consequently, it is imperative to conduct a comprehensive investigation into the pathogenesis of AP, identify critical therapeutic targets, and explore potential therapeutic strategies for the effective prevention and treatment of this condition. In the present study, we report for the first time that the activation of AhR mitigates the injury to pancreatic tight junction integrity in a murine model of AP, potentially via the RBX1/HSF1 signaling pathway (Fig. 7). This indicates that the activation of AhR could represent a new therapeutic approach for the treatment of AP.

AHR relieves acute pancreatitis via the RBX1/HSF1 pathway

Numerous studies have elucidated the involvement of AhR signaling in a variety of inflammatory conditions, such as osteoarthritis [23], atopic dermatitis [24, 25], inflammatory bowel diseases [26], and periodontitis [27]. The present investigation revealed that AhR was significantly underexpressed in pancreatic tissues from AP models and in cerulein-treated primary acinar cells, exhibiting a negative correlation with the levels of M1 polarization. Furthermore, the overexpression of AhR was found to mitigate cerulein-induced damage to the tight junctions of acinar cells, as well as the M1 polarization of PRM and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines. In AP mice, the overexpression of AhR protein markedly reduced pancreatic tissue damage and preserved tight junction integrity following cerulein/LPS injections. In a related study, Yang et al. demonstrated that AhR activation lessened renal inflammation and nephrocalcinosis induced by calcium oxalate by diminishing M1 macrophage polarization and enhancing M2 macrophage polarization [20]. Additionally, AhR activation has been shown to inhibit macrophage pyroptosis, thereby alleviating intestinal inflammation [28].

HSF1, a conserved transcription factor activated by stress, has been identified as playing a crucial role in AP [9, 11, 29]. In the present study, we demonstrated that HSF1 signaling was markedly activated in pancreatic tissues and in cerulein-treated primary acinar cells derived from AP mice. Additionally, we established for the first time a regulatory relationship between AhR and HSF1. Specifically, it was observed that AhR enhances both the expression and phosphorylation of HSF1. In AP mice, the administration of HSF1 inhibitors significantly counteracted the therapeutic effects of AhR overexpression.

Numerous studies have established that ubiquitination plays a crucial regulatory role in the progression of AP. For example, ubiquitin-specific protease 25 (USP25) has been associated with the aggravation of AP and the resultant multiorgan damage through the activation of the STAT3 pathway and TBK1-NF-κB signaling pathways [16, 30]. In the present study, an analysis utilizing the BioGRID platform revealed the regulation of AhR by various E3 ubiquitin ligases, including STUB1, HECTD1, RBX1, UBLCP1, and UCHL3. This finding intersects with predictions from the Ubibrowser database regarding E3 ligases that may mediate the ubiquitination of HSF1, indicating that STUB1 and RBX1 could facilitate interactions between AhR and HSF1. Notably, the expression levels of both STUB1 and RBX1 were found to be upregulated in pancreatic tissues from AP mouse models and in cerulein-treated primary acinar cells, with RBX1 exhibiting significantly higher expression levels. Furthermore, it was observed that AhR downregulates its own expression by binding to RBX1, which in turn promotes the ubiquitination and subsequent degradation of HSF1 through its interaction with HSF1. Conversely, research by Maxime J Kinet has indicated that HSF1 activates the ubiquitin-proteasome system, which encompasses the E3 ligase RBX1 [31]. Additionally, there may be potential competitive binding dynamics between AhR and HSF1 for RBX1. These findings suggest a complex intrinsic regulatory relationship among these three proteins, which may warrant further investigation in future research endeavors.

It is important to acknowledge certain limitations in our current study. While our findings highlight the role of the AhR-RBX1-HSF1 axis in pancreatic acinar cells, its potential effects on other pancreatic cell types, such as ductal cells and stellate cells, remain unclear and warrant further investigation. For instance, previous studies have suggested that AhR activation may exacerbate pancreatic fibrosis in chronic pancreatitis, potentially through IL-22-mediated activation of pancreatic stellate cells [32]. Thus, the cell type-specific functions of the AhR-RBX1-HSF1 signaling pathway across different pancreatic compartments need to be more systematically evaluated in future work.

Conclusion

This study provides inaugural evidence that AhR mitigates pancreatic tight junction damage and M1 polarization of PRM via the RBX1/HSF1 signaling pathway. Therefore, AhR represents a promising therapeutic target for AP and other inflammatory conditions associated with dysregulated macrophage polarization.
