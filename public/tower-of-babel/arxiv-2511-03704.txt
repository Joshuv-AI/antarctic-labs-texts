Long-Lasting and Slowly Varying Transient Dynamics in Discrete-Time Systems
Anthony Pasion a, b and Felicia Maria Magpantay a

Abstract
Analysis of mathematical models in ecology and epidemiology often focuses on asymptotic dynamics, such as stable equilibria and periodic orbits. However, many systems exhibit long transient behaviors where certain aspects of the dynamics remain in a slowly evolving state for an extended period before undergoing rapid change. In this work, we analyze long-lasting and slowly varying transient dynamics in discrete-time systems based on extensions of previous theoretical frameworks developed for continuous-time systems. This involves clarifying the conditions under which we say an observable of the system exhibits prolonged transients, and deriving criteria for characterizing these dynamics. Our results show that specific points in the state space, analogous to previously defined transient centers in continuous-time systems, can generate and sustain long transients in discrete-time models. We demonstrate how these properties manifest in predator-prey models and epidemiological systems, particularly when populations or disease prevalence remain low for extended intervals before sudden shifts.

Introduction

The dynamics of ecological and epidemiological systems are often described with a focus on their long-term behavior. However, recent advances have highlighted that many such systems exhibit transient behavior that can persist for ecologically relevant time spans before transitioning to a qualitatively different regime. These long transients can have significant implications for forecasting, management, and conceptual understanding of population persistence, extinction, and disease dynamics. As such, there is growing interest in developing a systematic mathematical framework to characterize and predict long-lasting transient dynamics.

A foundational body of work led by Hastings et al. has emphasized the importance of transient dynamics in ecology
[
5
,
4
,
12
,
13
]
. They highlighted that system trajectories may remain far from their attractors for a prolonged period, potentially leading to misleading conclusions if only asymptotic dynamics are considered. Long transients have been observed in models of predator-prey systems, population models with dispersal-driven dynamics, and models of epidemic outbreaks. These behaviors may arise from mechanisms such as crawl-by dynamics, ghost attractors, chaotic repellers, chaotic saddles or invariant manifolds
[
5
,
4
,
12
,
13
,
15
,
14
]
.

In an attempt to give these ideas a more technical mathematical framework, Liu and Magpantay (2022) introduced the notions of transient points, transient centers, and reachable transient centers for continuous-time systems governed by ordinary differential equations (ODEs)
[
9
]
. These are points in state space around which an observable quantity evolves very slowly for a long time. These ideas were further developed in
[
10
]
and used to gain insight into transient phenomena in ecology such as the honeymoon period of a disease
[
11
,
8
]
or the temporary collapse of the population in predator-prey interactions as seen in
[
4
]
.

The Liu et al. framework
[
9
,
10
]
was developed in the setting of model systems governed by ODEs. However, many biological systems are more naturally modeled using discrete-time dynamical systems governed by difference equations or maps. These include systems with discrete generations, seasonal forcing terms, or pulsed interventions. Discrete-time models are also known to exhibit rich and varied transient dynamics, which may not have clear analogues in continuous-time, especially when governed by discontinuous or piecewise-defined maps
[
15
,
14
]
.

The purpose of this work is to extend the framework developed by Liu et al.
[
9
,
10
]
to systems governed by discrete-time maps. Here we define and characterize points in the state space that generate slowly varying and long-lasting transient dynamics in discrete-time systems. Our results show that, just as in continuous-time models, discrete-time systems can feature special points in the state space where the evolution of an observable of the system is nearly stationary for extended periods before undergoing sudden transitions. These theoretical results are applied to various ecological and epidemiological models. By grounding our results in a rigorous mathematical setting and linking them to some well-documented transient phenomena in the literature, we hope that this work will contribute to the ongoing development of a unified mathematical theory of long transients across modeling frameworks.

The paper is divided into five (5) sections. In Section 2, we introduce precise mathematical definitions of concepts such as transient points and transient centers adapted specifically for discrete-time systems. We also establish foundational results on their properties and derive necessary conditions for their existence. In Section 3, we examine conditions under which fixed points can serve as transient centers and provide clear criteria for their identification based on system stability and observable properties. In Section 4, we apply the theoretical framework we have developed to specific ecological and epidemiological models. Transient phenomena such as prolonged population collapses and honeymoon periods observed in disease outbreaks are explicitly illustrated. We end the paper by summarizing its main contributions and lay out our future directions that build on these results.

Definition 2.1

(Transient Points)
.

Let

\displaystyle v\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle s>0

\displaystyle T\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta v(x)=v(f(x))-v(x).

\displaystyle\displaystyle T_{s}(\xi)=\begin{cases}\inf\{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}~:~|\Delta v(f^{t}(\xi))|>s\},&\text{if the set is non-empty,}\\
\infty,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We call
\displaystyle T_{s}(\xi)

\displaystyle(v,s)
-transient time of
\displaystyle\xi

\displaystyle\xi
is a
\displaystyle(v,s,T)
-transient point if
\displaystyle T<T_{s}(\xi)<\infty

Definition 2.2

(Transient Centers)
.

Let
\displaystyle v\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle x_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle S>0
such that for all
\displaystyle 0<s<S

\displaystyle T\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

\displaystyle(v,s,T)
-transient point in every neighborhood of
\displaystyle x_{*}
.

It is common to model systems wherein not all the states are observed or measured. We define an
observable function

\displaystyle v:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, which captures some quantity of interest evolving along system trajectories.
Examples of such observables include the number of infected individuals in epidemic models, the density of prey or predator populations in ecological predator-prey models, total biomass in food-web models, or the fraction of vaccinated individuals in disease models with vaccination. Throughout the remainder of this paper, we assume that
\displaystyle v\in C(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
unless stated otherwise. We are interested in long and slow transient behaviours displayed by the observable
\displaystyle v
. A
\displaystyle(v,s,T)
-transient point is a state
\displaystyle\xi
where the rate of change of the observable
\displaystyle v
remains small for a long time. Specifically,
\displaystyle\Delta v
stays below a threshold
\displaystyle s
for at least time
\displaystyle T
. This means that the system exhibits slow evolution in terms of the observable
\displaystyle v
before eventually experiencing a more significant rate of change. On the other hand, a transient center is a point in state space where arbitrarily slow transient behaviors can occur arbitrarily close to that point. Specifically, this means for any threshold
\displaystyle s
, no matter how small, there are states arbitrarily close to
\displaystyle x_{*}
where
\displaystyle v
changes slower than
\displaystyle s
for longer than any given time interval
\displaystyle T
, no matter how long. Transient centers act as organizing structures in the dynamics, where the system’s observable experiences prolonged phases of near-stationary behavior before transitioning to different regimes. Intuitively, if
\displaystyle x_{*}
is a transient center, then the system exhibits long transient dynamics near
\displaystyle x_{*}
, meaning that trajectories of the observable originating close to
\displaystyle x_{*}
remain in a slowly evolving phase for extended durations before eventually undergoing more noticeable changes.

Several foundational results we establish in this section, including necessary conditions and invariance properties, are direct discrete-time analogues of the continuous-time results presented by Liu et al. in
[

]

These new results provide simplified and useful criteria for identifying transient centers, significantly facilitating their verification in discrete-time models. We now proceed to rigorously present these theoretical results.

Theorem 2.3
.

If
\displaystyle x_{*}
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center, then

(i)

\displaystyle x_{*}
is also an
\displaystyle(\alpha v+\beta)
-transient center for any
\displaystyle\alpha\neq 0
and
\displaystyle\beta\in\mathbb{R}
,

(ii)

\displaystyle\Delta v(x_{*})=v(f(x_{*}))-v(x_{*})=0
, and

(iii)

\displaystyle f^{t}(x_{*})
is also a
\displaystyle v
-transient center for all
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}
.

Proof.

Proof of (i).

We use the notation
\displaystyle T^{v}_{s}(\xi)
to denote the
\displaystyle(v,s)
-transient time of
\displaystyle\xi
.
Let
\displaystyle\tilde{v}=\alpha v+\beta
where
\displaystyle\alpha\neq 0
and
\displaystyle\beta\in\mathbb{R}
.
If
\displaystyle\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
\displaystyle(v,s,T)
-transient point then
\displaystyle T<T^{v}_{s}(\xi)<\infty
. Clearly,
\displaystyle\xi
is also a
\displaystyle(\tilde{v},s/|\alpha|,T)
-transient point since its
\displaystyle(\tilde{v},s/|\alpha|)
-transient time
\displaystyle T^{\tilde{v}}_{s/|\alpha|}(\xi)=T^{v}_{s}(\xi)
from the fact that
\displaystyle\Delta\tilde{v}=\alpha\Delta v
. It is now straightforward from Definition
2.2
that
\displaystyle x_{*}
is also a
\displaystyle\tilde{v}
-transient center.

Proof of (ii).

Let
\displaystyle|\Delta v(x_{*})|=|v(f(x_{*}))-v(x_{*})|=c
and assume that
\displaystyle c\neq 0
. Since
\displaystyle v
and
\displaystyle f
are both continuous, the function
\displaystyle\Delta v=v(f(x))-v(x)
is also continuous on
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By the definition of continuity, there exists a neighborhood
\displaystyle U
of
\displaystyle x_{*}
such that for all
\displaystyle x\in U
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Delta v(x)-\Delta v(x_{*})|<\frac{c}{2}.

Rearranging gives us
\displaystyle|\Delta v(x)|>\frac{c}{2}
for all
\displaystyle x\in U
.
Now, set
\displaystyle s=\frac{c}{2}
. By the definition of a transient center, there must exist a
\displaystyle(v,s,T)
-transient point arbitrarily close to
\displaystyle x_{*}
. However, in the neighborhood
\displaystyle U
, we have
\displaystyle|\Delta v(x)|>s
or in other words,
\displaystyle T_{s}(x)=0
which contradicts this requirement. Thus we must have
\displaystyle c=0
.

Proof of (iii).
We proceed by induction on
\displaystyle t
. By assumption,
\displaystyle x_{*}
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center, so the statement holds for
\displaystyle t=0
since
\displaystyle f^{0}(x_{*})=x_{*}
. We now assume that for some
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}

\displaystyle f^{t}(x_{*})
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center and show that
\displaystyle f^{t+1}(x_{*})
is also a
\displaystyle v
-transient center. Let
\displaystyle C
be an arbitrary neighborhood of
\displaystyle f^{t+1}(x_{*})

The continuity of
\displaystyle f
implies the existence of a neighborhood
\displaystyle B

\displaystyle f^{t}(x_{*})

\displaystyle f(B)\subset C

\displaystyle(v,s,T+1)
-transient point
\displaystyle x

\displaystyle B

-transient point. Since

\displaystyle f^{t+1}(x_{*})

The previous theorem provides a characterization of transient centers in discrete-time systems. Theorem
2.3
)(i) tells us that the property of being a transient center does not depend on the particular units or origin of the observable
\displaystyle v
, as long as you are only scaling or shifting
\displaystyle v
. For example, if
\displaystyle v
represents a certain population component of a model system, then it does not matter whether the population is in hundreds, thousands or even subtracted from some baseline value. Theorem
2.3
)(ii) states that if
\displaystyle x_{*}
is a transient center, then the observable function
\displaystyle v
does not change at
\displaystyle x_{*}
under iterations of the system, that is,
\displaystyle v(f(x_{*}))=v(x_{*})
. Note that the converse does not hold in general. This is easy to see by considering the case where
\displaystyle x_{*}
is a fixed point of
\displaystyle f
, i.e.,
\displaystyle f(x_{*})=x_{*}
. In this case, we have
\displaystyle\Delta v(x_{*})=v(f(x_{*}))-v(x_{*})=v(x_{*})-v(x_{*})=0
. However, this does not imply that transient points accumulate around
\displaystyle x_{*}
in the manner required by the definition of a transient center. In the next section, we shall develop rigorous criteria for identifying which fixed points act as transient centers. Finally, Theorem
2.3
)(iii) shows that if
\displaystyle x_{*}
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center, then every forward iterate
\displaystyle f^{t}(x_{*})
for all
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}
remains a transient center. This means that once a point exhibits transient center behavior, this property persists along its forward trajectory under
\displaystyle f
. Combining this result with Theorem
2.3
)(ii), it follows that
\displaystyle v
remains constant along the entire trajectory
\displaystyle\{f^{t}(x_{*})\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}}
. Although transient centers are not necessarily fixed points of
\displaystyle f
, they behave similarly with respect to the observable
\displaystyle v
. This implies that transient centers form an invariant set under
\displaystyle f
where
\displaystyle v
does not change significantly over time. If
\displaystyle v
represents our quantity of interest, then neighborhoods of transient centers are regions where this quantity can change very slowly over time.

Let us now define the following set,

\displaystyle\displaystyle X^{v}=\{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}~|~\Delta v(f^{t}(\xi))=0~\text{for all}~t\in\mathbb{Z}_{0}^{+}\}

(2)

consisting of all points where the observable function
\displaystyle v
remains constant under iteration. The set
\displaystyle X^{v}
contains candidate
\displaystyle v
-transient centers. The following theorems provide useful criteria to determine which elements of the set
\displaystyle X^{v}
defined in (

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\displaystyle x_{*}\in X^{v}
. If there exists
\displaystyle S>0
such that for all
\displaystyle s\in(0,S)
, in any neighborhood of
\displaystyle x_{*}
, there is an
\displaystyle x
such that
\displaystyle T_{s}(x)<\infty
, then
\displaystyle x_{*}
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center.

Proof.

Let
\displaystyle s\in(0,S)
and
\displaystyle T\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Let
\displaystyle U
be a neighborhood of
\displaystyle x_{*}
. We will show that
\displaystyle U
contains a
\displaystyle(v,s,T)
-transient point. Since
\displaystyle x_{*}\in X^{v}
, we have
\displaystyle\Delta v(f^{t}(x_{*}))=0
for all
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}
. For each
\displaystyle t\in 0\mathbin{:}T
, from the continuity of
\displaystyle\Delta v
and
\displaystyle f^{t}
, we can choose
\displaystyle r_{t}>0
such that for all
\displaystyle x\in B_{r_{t}}(x_{*})
we have
\displaystyle|\Delta v(f^{t}(x))|<s
. Here, we also pick
\displaystyle r_{0}
small enough so that
\displaystyle B_{r_{0}}(x_{*})\subset U
.
Now set
\displaystyle r=\min_{t\in 0\mathbin{:}T}\{r_{t}\}
and
\displaystyle W=B_{r}(x_{*})
.
Clearly
\displaystyle W\subset B_{r_{0}}(x_{*})\subset U
. By construction,
\displaystyle|\Delta v(f^{t}(x))|<s
for all
\displaystyle x\in W
and
\displaystyle t\in 0\mathbin{:}T
. By assumption, there exists a point
\displaystyle x\in W
such that
\displaystyle T_{s}(x)<\infty
. Since
\displaystyle x\in W
, we know that
\displaystyle|\Delta v(f^{t}(x))|<s
for all
\displaystyle t\in 0\mathbin{:}T
, ensuring that
\displaystyle T_{s}(x)\geq T
. Thus,
\displaystyle x
satisfies the conditions for being a
\displaystyle(v,s,T)
-transient point. It follows that
\displaystyle x_{*}
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center.
∎

Theorem 2.5
.

A point
\displaystyle x_{*}\in X^{v}
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center if and only if there exists
\displaystyle S_{*}>0
such that in any neighborhood of
\displaystyle x_{*}
, there is an
\displaystyle x
such that
\displaystyle T_{S_{*}}(x)<\infty
.

Proof.

Assume that
\displaystyle x_{*}
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center. Then, by definition, there exists some
\displaystyle S>0
such that for all
\displaystyle s\in(0,S)
and all
\displaystyle T\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, any neighborhood
\displaystyle U
of
\displaystyle x_{*}
contains a
\displaystyle(v,s,T)
-transient point. Choosing
\displaystyle S_{*}=S/2
, we see that there must exist an
\displaystyle x\in U
which is a
\displaystyle(v,S_{*},T)
-transient point for any
\displaystyle T\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
since
\displaystyle x_{*}
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center. This means
\displaystyle T_{S_{*}}(x)<\infty
, which proves the forward implication. Conversely, suppose that there exists
\displaystyle S_{*}>0
such that for every neighborhood of
\displaystyle x_{*}
, there is an
\displaystyle x
with
\displaystyle T_{S_{*}}(x)<\infty
. For any
\displaystyle s\in(0,S_{*})
, clearly
\displaystyle T_{s}(x)\leq T_{S_{*}}(x)<\infty
.
From the previous theorem we conclude that
\displaystyle x_{*}
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem
2.5
reduces the problem of verifying transient centers from a universal condition (e.g., for all
\displaystyle 0<s<S
) to an existential one (e.g., there exists
\displaystyle S_{*}
). Additionally, we can also restate Theorem
2.5
in an equivalent double supremum form since the condition that there exists
\displaystyle S_{*}>0
so that every neighborhood of
\displaystyle x_{*}
contains some
\displaystyle x
with
\displaystyle T_{S_{*}}(x)<\infty
is equivalent to the existence of
\displaystyle S_{*}>0
such that for every
\displaystyle r>0
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sup_{x\in B_{r}(x_{*})}~\sup_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}}\left|\Delta v(f^{t}(x))\right|\geq S_{*}.

In this form, no matter how small the ball
\displaystyle B_{r}(x_{*})
, the maximum absolute observable increment
\displaystyle\left|\Delta v(f^{t}(x))\right|
achieved by some forward iterate of some point in that ball always reaches at least
\displaystyle S_{*}
. On another note, reformulating Theorem
2.5
in terms of its negation, we observe that a point
\displaystyle x_{*}\in X^{v}
is not a
\displaystyle v
-transient center precisely when for every possible threshold
\displaystyle S_{*}>0
, there is some neighborhood
\displaystyle U
around
\displaystyle x_{*}
whose points
\displaystyle x\in U
satisfy
\displaystyle T_{S_{*}}(x)=\infty
. In other words, all orbits starting in this neighborhood
\displaystyle U
never exceed some threshold in the rate of change of the observable
\displaystyle v
.

Example 2.6

Consider the following model system given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}x(t+1)&=x(t)[1-hy(t)]\\
y(t+1)&=y(t)+h[x(t)-1]\end{cases}

\displaystyle\{f^{t}(\xi)\}_{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}}

\displaystyle|\Delta v(x_{0},y_{0})|=0<S_{*}

\displaystyle t_{*}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}

\displaystyle|\Delta v(x_{t_{*}},y_{t_{*}})|>S_{*}

Note that as long as
\displaystyle x_{t}<1

\displaystyle\displaystyle y_{t+1}=y_{t}+h(x_{t}-1)<y_{t}.

\displaystyle t_{*}=\min\{t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}~:~x_{t}\geq 1\}

\displaystyle y_{t}<y_{t-1}<\dots<y_{1}

\displaystyle t\in 1\mathbin{:}t_{*}

\displaystyle y_{t_{*}}<y_{1}=-h(1-\varepsilon)<0

\displaystyle y_{t}<y_{1}<0

\displaystyle\displaystyle x_{t+1}=x_{t}(1-hy_{t})>x_{t}(1-hy_{1})=(1+\alpha)x_{t}

\displaystyle\displaystyle x_{t}\geq x_{1}(1+\alpha)^{t-1}=\varepsilon(1+\alpha)^{t-1}

\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{Z^{+}}

\displaystyle y_{t}<y_{t-1}<\dots<y_{1}

Example 3.6
.

Consider the following discrete-time system

\displaystyle\displaystyle x(t+1)=g\left((x(t),y(t)\right),\quad y(t+1)=h\left(x(t),y(t)\right)

\displaystyle g(x,y)=\frac{ay}{1+x^{2}}

\displaystyle h(x,y)=\frac{bx}{1+y^{2}}

\displaystyle ab>1

\displaystyle(0,0)

\displaystyle\displaystyle g_{x}=-\frac{2axy}{(1+x^{2})^{2}},\quad g_{y}=\frac{a}{1+x^{2}},\quad h_{x}=\frac{b}{1+y^{2}},\quad h_{y}=-\frac{2bxy}{(1+y^{2})^{2}}.

Hence, the Jacobian at the origin reduces to
\displaystyle A=\begin{bmatrix}0&a\\
b&0\end{bmatrix}
which is nonnegative, irreducible and has an eigenvalue
\displaystyle\lambda=\sqrt{ab}>1
.
If we consider the observable function
\displaystyle v(x,y)=(1,1)\cdot(x,y)=x+y
, then Corollary
3.5
implies that the origin is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center of model system (

Note that Theorem

because then

for any eigenvector

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\displaystyle f\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\displaystyle v\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathbb{R})
. If
\displaystyle x^{*}
is an unstable fixed point of the nonlinear system (
1
) such that the gradient
\displaystyle\nabla\Delta v(x^{*})=0
and the Hessian matrix
\displaystyle H_{\Delta v}(x^{*})
is positive (or negative) definite, then
\displaystyle x^{*}
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle\Delta v
is twice continuously differentiable at
\displaystyle x^{*}
, we can expand
\displaystyle\Delta v(x)
using a Taylor series around
\displaystyle x^{*}
. Since
\displaystyle\Delta v(x^{*})=0
and
\displaystyle\nabla\Delta v(x^{*})=0
, we have

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta v(x)=\frac{1}{2}(x-x^{*})^{\top}H_{\Delta v}(x^{*})(x-x^{*})+o(\|x-x^{*}\|^{2})

(19)

where the nonzero Hessian matrix
\displaystyle H_{\Delta v}(x^{*})
is a symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues are real. Additionally, the positive or negative definiteness of
\displaystyle H_{\Delta v}(x^{*})
implies that all of its eigenvalues are either positive or negative, respectively. This assumption also gives us the bound

\displaystyle\displaystyle|(x-x^{*})^{\top}H_{\Delta v}(x^{*})(x-x^{*})|\geq\lambda\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}

where
\displaystyle\lambda=\min\{|\lambda_{i}|~:~\lambda_{i}~\text{is an eigenvalue of $\displaystyle H_{\Delta v}(x^{*})$}\}
. Now, by definition of little-o, there is an
\displaystyle r>0
so small such that

\displaystyle\displaystyle\left|o(\|x-x^{*}\|^{2})\right|\leq\frac{\lambda}{4}\|x-x^{*}\|^{2}

whenever
\displaystyle x\in\overline{B_{r}(x^{*})}=\{x~:~\|x-x^{*}\|\leq r\}
.
Hence, we have from (
19
) that

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Delta v(x)|\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}\|x-x^{*}\|^{2},\quad\forall~x\in\overline{B_{r}(x^{*})}.

(20)

Additionally, since
\displaystyle x^{*}
is unstable, there exists some
\displaystyle\rho\in(0,r]
such that for any neighborhood
\displaystyle U
of
\displaystyle x^{*}
, there exists a
\displaystyle y\in U\setminus\{x^{*}\}
and a time
\displaystyle t_{\rho}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
\displaystyle\|f^{t_{\rho}}(y)-x^{*}\|>\rho
. Consider the continuous function
\displaystyle g:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\displaystyle g(k)=\|f^{t_{\rho}}\left(x^{*}+k(y-x^{*})\right)-x^{*}\|.

Note that
\displaystyle g(0)=\|f^{t_{\rho}}(x^{*})-x^{*}\|=0<\rho
and
\displaystyle g(1)=\|f^{t_{\rho}}(y)-x^{*}\|>\rho
. Hence, we can apply the Intermediate Value Theorem and conclude that there is some
\displaystyle k^{*}\in(0,1)
satisfying
\displaystyle g(k^{*})=\rho
. This implies that if we initialize at
\displaystyle x=x^{*}+k^{*}(y-x^{*})\in U
, then we have
\displaystyle\|f^{t_{\rho}}(x)-x^{*}\|=\rho
.
We now choose
\displaystyle S_{*}\in\left(0,\frac{\lambda}{2}\rho^{2}\right)
and deduce from (
20
) that

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\Delta v(f^{t_{\rho}}(x))|\geq\frac{\lambda}{2}\|f^{t_{\rho}}(x)-x^{*}\|^{2}=\frac{\lambda}{2}\rho^{2}>S_{*}.

This shows that
\displaystyle T_{S_{*}}(x)\leq t_{\rho}<\infty
for such
\displaystyle x\in U
. Applying Theorem
2.5
, we conclude that
\displaystyle x^{*}
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center.
∎

Example 3.8
.

Consider the following one-dimensional example given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle x(t+1)=2x(t)+[x(t)]^{3}.

Let
\displaystyle f(x)=2x+x^{3}
and
\displaystyle v(x)=x^{2}
. Then

\displaystyle\displaystyle\Delta v(x)=v(f(x))-v(x)=3x^{2}+4x^{4}+x^{6}.

Note that
\displaystyle x^{*}=0
is an unstable fixed point since
\displaystyle f^{\prime}(0)=2>1
. Also, Theorem
3.4
does not apply here since
\displaystyle v^{\prime}(0)=0
. Nevertheless, we compute that
\displaystyle[\Delta v]^{\prime}(0)=0
, and
\displaystyle[\Delta v]^{\prime\prime}(0)=6>0
. Thus, Theorem
3.7
applies and
\displaystyle x^{*}=0
is a
\displaystyle v
-transient center.

It is tempting to use the ideas behind the proof of Theorem
3.7
in order to obtain a similar first-order criterion by assuming that
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle v
are
\displaystyle C^{1}
, and the gradient
\displaystyle\nabla\Delta v(x^{*})\neq 0
. However, this seemingly straightforward adaptation runs into a problem. Unlike in the case of Theorem
3.7
where we obtained a uniform quadratic lower bound in every direction around the fixed point
\displaystyle x^{*}
, imposing the condition
\displaystyle\nabla\Delta v(x^{*})\neq 0
can only control the directional growth of
\displaystyle\Delta v
along the vector
\displaystyle\nabla\Delta v(x^{*})
itself, and not in orthogonal directions. If we Taylor expand
\displaystyle\Delta v
around
\displaystyle x^{*}
, then its nonzero linear term satisfies

\displaystyle\displaystyle|\nabla\Delta v(x^{*})\cdot(x-x^{*})|=\|\nabla\Delta v(x^{*})\|\,\|x-x^{*}\|\,|\cos\theta|

where
\displaystyle\theta
is the angle between
\displaystyle\nabla\Delta v(x^{*})
and
\displaystyle\|x-x^{*}\|
. As
\displaystyle\theta\to\frac{\pi}{2}
,
\displaystyle\cos\theta\to 0
, and thus, no uniform linear bound of the form
\displaystyle|\Delta v(x)|\geq c\|x-x^{2}\|
,
\displaystyle\forall~x\in\overline{B_{r}(x^{*})}
can hold no matter how small
\displaystyle r
is. The only way to obtain such estimate is to restrict our attention to directions in which we assume (or perhaps know) the directional derivative will not vanish similar to what we did in Theorems
3.3
and
3.4
.

4
Application to Models in Ecology and Epidemiology

The theoretical framework and criteria for identifying long-lasting and slowly varying transient dynamics developed in the previous sections provide a systematic basis for analyzing a wide class of discrete-time models. In this section, we present concrete examples drawn from ecology and epidemiology to illustrate how the concepts of transient points and transient centers manifest in real-world biological systems. By applying the main results to specific models, we demonstrate how these mathematical tools can be used to identify and predict long transients in populations and disease dynamics. We emphasize through these examples the relevance of long transient dynamics for interpreting and managing ecological and epidemiological systems.

4.1
Predator-Prey Model

In 2022, Streipert et al.
[
16
]
presented a new derivation and thorough analysis of a discrete-time predator–prey model, constructed directly from first principles rather than as a discretization of an existing continuous-time model. The model assumes that the prey population exhibits logistic growth in the absence of predators, converging to a carrying capacity, while the predator population requires prey to persist. Most importantly, their approach ensures biologically meaningful dynamics, such as the non-negativity of populations and satisfaction of the axiom of parenthood, that is, no population can arise from zero. The discrete-time model they proposed is given by

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{cases}x(t+1)=\frac{(1+r)x(t)}{1+\frac{r}{K}x(t)+\alpha y(t)}\\[8.61108pt]
y(t+1)=\frac{[1+\gamma x(t)]y(t)}{1+d}\end{cases}

(21)

where
\displaystyle x(t)
and
\displaystyle y(t)
are the are the prey and predator populations at time
\displaystyle t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{0}
,
\displaystyle r>0
is the intrinsic growth rate of the prey,
\displaystyle K>0
is the prey population carrying capacity,
\displaystyle\alpha>0
is the predation rate,
\displaystyle\gamma>0
is the consumption-energy rate for the predator, and
\displaystyle d>0
is the predator’s natural death rate.

Consider first the observable function
\displaystyle v_{1}(x,y)=x
which depicts the prey population. We first formally prove that the fixed point
\displaystyle E_{0}=(0,0)
is a
\displaystyle v_{1}
-transient center and therefore cause arbitrarily slow dynamics for arbitrarily long periods of times. We apply Theorem

. At

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{E_{0}}=\begin{bmatrix}1+r&0\\
0&\frac{1}{1+d}\end{bmatrix}.

The eigenvalues are
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=1+r>1
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{1+d}<1
. The corresponding eigenvector for
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=1+r
is
\displaystyle w_{1}=(1,0)
. Since
\displaystyle v_{1}(x,y)=x
, we have
\displaystyle\nabla v_{1}(E_{0})=(1,0)

\displaystyle\nabla v_{1}(E_{0})\cdot w_{1}=1\neq 0
. Thus, all the hypotheses of Theorem
3.4
are satisfied, and
\displaystyle E_{0}
is a
\displaystyle v_{1}
-transient center. In a similar manner, if we set
\displaystyle v_{2}(x,y)=y
and assume that
\displaystyle d<\gamma K

\displaystyle E_{K}=(K,0)
is a
\displaystyle v_{2}
-transient center. The Jacobian matrix

\displaystyle\displaystyle A_{E_{K}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{1+r}&-\frac{\alpha K}{1+r}\\

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}=\frac{1}{1+r}<1
and
\displaystyle\lambda_{2}=\frac{1+\gamma K}{1+d}>1

\displaystyle w_{2}=\left(\frac{\alpha K}{(1+r)(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})},~1\right)

\displaystyle\nabla v_{2}(E_{K})\cdot w_{2}=1\neq 0

\displaystyle v_{2}
-transient center of model system (

\displaystyle v_{1}

Our findings have a clear biological interpretation. The existence of a
\displaystyle v
-transient center for an observable
\displaystyle v
corresponding to a population component such as the prey or predator means that, for initial conditions close to the transient center, the population can remain at a constant level for an extended but finite duration before eventually increasing either due to underlying instabilities or as a result of other features in the system’s dynamics. In particular, we have observed that although environmental conditions eventually allow population recovery due to the instability of fixed points, the local dynamics near these repelling fixed points can keep populations trapped in low-density states for prolonged periods. From a management perspective, this means that relying solely on local growth rates around a fixed point may significantly underestimate the true recovery time. Populations near such transient centers might appear permanently collapsed, even when long-term recovery is possible. Moreover, we have demonstrated in model system (
21
) that it is not just the isolated fixed-points
\displaystyle E_{0}
and
\displaystyle E_{K}
that exhibit this transient behavior, but the entire line
\displaystyle\left\{(x,0)~:~x\geq 0\right\}
is made of transient centers for the predator observable. Concretely, any state within this threshold forces the predator population to linger at near-zero levels for a considerable amount of time before eventually rebounding. Ecologically, this provides a rigorous explanation for why predator populations can remain persistently low even when prey densities would otherwise support recovery.

In ecological models, the mechanism behind transient centers provides a rigorous mathematical explanation for temporary population collapse. As seen in model system (
21
), a predator population might seem entirely wiped out for seasons or years only to reappear later, or why a prey population can hang on at vanishingly low densities before suddenly exploding. We shall see in the next subsection that similar behavior occurs in epidemic models. Disease prevalence can remain at minimal levels, nearly unnoticed, before suddenly escalating into a significant outbreak.

5
Summary and Future Work

In this paper, we developed a comprehensive mathematical framework to identify, characterize, and analyze long-lasting and slowly varying transient dynamics in discrete-time model systems. While many previous studies have emphasized stable long-term dynamics like equilibria and periodic cycles, we focused on a specific type of long transient dynamics where a system’s observable remains nearly constant for extended periods before experiencing a sudden change. By extending existing theories from continuous-time models, we introduced precise definitions of transient points and transient centers tailored to discrete-time systems. We also established rigorous conditions and criteria for detecting these structures. In particular, we provided some characterizations of fixed points as transient centers. Lastly, the practical application of the theoretical framework is demonstrated through a detailed analysis of specific examples from predator-prey and epidemic models. The results we obtained provide some important biological insights. They help explain some well-known transient phenomena such as temporary population collapses and the honeymoon period of a disease.

Our current research efforts are concentrated on two main aspects. The first is adapting the concept of reachability which is an important property that makes the transient dynamics generated by transient centers attainable from other points in the state space to discrete-time dynamical systems. In the original formulation of Liu and Magpantay
[
9
]
, reachable transient points and reachable transient centers for ODE systems are defined using the backward-time trajectories of the ODE. In this way, if a point in the state space arbitrarily close to a
\displaystyle v
-transient center is a
\displaystyle(v,s,T)
-transient point when you run time forwards and when you run time backwards, then this would enable you to construct an entrance point outside any neighborhood of the given
\displaystyle v
-transient center. The formal, rigorous definition of a reachable
\displaystyle(v,s,T)
-transient point and of a reachable
\displaystyle v
-transient center for ODE systems can be found in
[
9
,
10
]
. Several mathematical properties of these points are also established in
[
10
]
. Of course, unlike invertible flows generated by ODEs, we know that discrete-time maps may not admit a global inverse. Thus, a direct translation to discrete-time systems of this reverse flow requirement is impossible. We are currently developing and investigating a new and purely forward-time definition of reachability that captures the same spirit of attainable transient centers. We emphasize that establishing reachability in discrete-time systems is not merely a formal extension. It ensures that realistic trajectories starting from generic initializations can enter and stay in slow regions for a long duration of time.

The second primary aspect we intend to explore in future research involves transient centers that are not fixed points. Even within the continuous-time setting, the theory on non-equilibrium transient centers remains underdeveloped. Currently, our primary tool for characterizing such transient centers is given in Theorem
2.5
. Despite the usefulness of this result in proving various theoretical criteria, it exhibits several drawbacks when applied to concrete examples as demonstrated in the last section. Specifically, verifying the finite transient time condition often requires intricate and careful constructions within the system dynamics which makes the application of this result challenging.
This motivates a parallel line of work to improve and develop simple, easily verifiable criteria for transient centers, both equilibrium and non-equilibrium, so that identification does not rely on delicate constructions and is accessible to applications.
