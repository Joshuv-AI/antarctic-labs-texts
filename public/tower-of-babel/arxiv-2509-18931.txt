Direct Paths in the Temporal Hypercube
Austin Eide, Martijn Gösgens, Paweł Prałat

Abstract
We consider the n n -dimensional random temporal hypercube , i.e., the n n -dimensional hypercube graph with its edges endowed with i.i.d. continuous random weights. We say that a vertex w w is accessible from another vertex v v if and only if there is a path from v v to w w with increasing edge weights. We study accessible “direct” paths from a fixed vertex to its antipodal point and show that as n → ∞ n\to\infty , the number of such paths converges in distribution to a mixed Poisson law with mixture given by the product of two independent exponentials with rate 1 1 . Our proof makes use of the Chen-Stein method, coupling arguments, as well as combinatorial arguments which show that typical pairs of accessible paths have small overlap.



1. Introduction

In a temporal graph, each edge is endowed with a weight that we interpret as a time-stamp; an edge is inactive before the time-stamp is reached, after which point it is active. We say that a path in a temporal graph is
accessible
if it is increasing with respect to the edge weights, and a vertex
w
is accessible from
v
if there is some accessible path from
v
to
w
in the graph. The graph is
temporally connected
if all vertices are pairwise accessible from one another. Temporal graphs provide a natural and perhaps more realistic model for a variety of dynamic network processes. For instance, in a dynamically evolving social network, information can only pass from one node to another after they have formed a connection in the network. On the theoretical side, extending basic graph theoretic properties such as connectivity and diameter to the temporal setting is an interesting technical challenge. We refer to
michail2016introduction
for a broad overview of the topic.

In this paper, we are interested in the setting in which edge weights are assigned randomly. We remark that when edge weights are sampled i.i.d. from any continuous distribution, the model is equivalent to one in which the edges are ordered by a random permutation (an equivalence we exploit throughout the paper). Of particular interest in the literature has been the length of a longest accessible path in a randomly weighted temporal graph. Using the second moment method,
lavrov2016increasing
showed that a randomly edge-ordered complete graph
K_{n}
contains an accessible Hamiltonian path with probability at least
\frac{1}{e}+o(1)
, and additionally that it contains accessible paths of length
0.85n
with high probability
1

An event holds
with high probability
(
w.h.p.
) if it holds with probability tending to one as
n\to\infty

. They conjecture that an accessible Hamiltonian path also exists w.h.p., a problem which remains open. We note that on the deterministic side,
graham1973increasing
showed that for
any

there exists an accessible path of length at least
\sqrt{n-1}
, and
calderbank1984increasing

(1+o(1))\frac{n}{2}

There are also significant recent results on temporal

It is easy to see that the hypercube cannot be w.h.p. temporarily connected. For example, we expect one vertex with all incident edges with weights more than
\tfrac{1}{2}
and one with all weights less than
\tfrac{1}{2}
. With probability bounded away from zero we have at least one vertex of each type which implies that there is no accessible path from one of them to the other. Hence, in this work we study the following variant of the problems mentioned above. Consider the
n
-dimensional hypercube graph, with vertex set given by the power set of
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
and edge set given by pairs
v,v^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
with
|v\Delta v^{\prime}|=1
. We say that a path from
v
to
v^{\prime}
in the hypercube is
direct
if it has length equal to the graph distance of
v
and
v^{\prime}
. Assign i.i.d.
\text{Unif}[0,1]
weights to the edges, and define
X_{n}
to be the random variable which counts the number of accessible direct paths from
\emptyset
to
[n]
. We remark that
X_{n}
is a particularly critical random variable: there are
n!
direct paths from
\emptyset
to
[n]
, and each of them is accessible with probability exactly
\frac{1}{n!}
, which means
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]=1
. If the correlations between different paths are negligible, then it is reasonable to expect
X_{n}
to converge to a Poisson random variable with parameter
1
. On the other hand, the presence of one directed path increases chances for other overlapping direct paths. If this correlation is strong, we may have
X_{n}=0
w.h.p. and
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}\ |\ X_{n}>0]\to\infty
, so that
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]=1
. These correlations, however, are highly non-trivial. Understanding this critical scenario was proposed as one of the open problems during the 17th Annual Workshop on Probability and Combinatorics, McGill University’s Bellairs Institute, Holetown, Barbados (April 7–14, 2023). Our main result,
Theorem

1.1
, shows that both conjectures above are false: it establishes that
X_{n}
converges in distribution to a mixed Poisson random variable
\text{Poi}(Z\cdot Z^{\prime})
, where
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
are independent exponential random variables of rate
1
. In particular,
\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=0)\to\delta
, where
\delta=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-z}}{1+z}\,dz\approx 0.596347
is the
Gompertz
constant
(
gompertz1825mortality
)
.

While there is some previous work on the temporal hypercube (e.g.,
de2015increasing
), our result shares more significant similarities with the
vertex-weighted
version of the problem, which has been studied extensively
(
berestycki2014accessibility
;
berestycki2016number
;
kauffman1987towards
;
kingman1978simple
;
martinsson2015accessibility
)
. Interest in the vertex-weighted hypercube comes from the field of evolutionary biology, where each vertex of the hypercube corresponds to a potential genetic code. One typically fixes the weight of vertex
[n]
to
1
and the weight of
\emptyset
to
x\in[0,1)
, and considers the distribution of the number of accessible paths from one to the other. This process is sometimes also referred to as
accessibility percolation

(
martinsson2015accessibility
)
. As in the edge-weighted case, there are
n!
direct paths from
\emptyset
to
[n]
; in order for such a path to be increasing, all of the
n-1
interior vertex weights must be at least
x
and be in increasing order, which occurs with probability
\frac{(1-x)^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}
. Thus we expect
n(1-x)^{n-1}
accessible paths.

hegarty2014existence
establish a phase transition
x=\frac{\log n}{n}
, the point at which the expected number of increasing paths is
1+o(1)
: for
\omega=\omega(n)
tending to infinity arbitrarily slowly, if
x=\frac{\log n-\omega}{n}
then there is no accessible direct path w.h.p., and if
x=\frac{\log n+\omega}{n}
then an accessible direct path exists w.h.p.
li2018phase
proves a similar result when backtracking is allowed. When
x=c/n
for constant
c\geq 0
,
berestycki2016number
show that the number of accessible direct paths from
\emptyset
to
[n]
, rescaled by
n
, converges in distribution to
e^{-c}Z\cdot Z^{\prime}
, where
Z
and
Z^{\prime}
are independent exponential random variables of rate
1
. Our main result and proof techniques bear some similarity to this one, a connection we explore in more detail in
Section

1.3
.

Our proof strategy makes use of the
Chen-Stein method
, which bounds the difference between the distribution of a sum of indicators and the Poisson distribution
(
arratia1990poisson
)
. The Chen-Stein method is typically used to prove Poisson limit laws, but has also been used to prove
mixed
Poisson limits
(
reinert2007length
)
like our main result.

1.1. Notation

We consider a hypercube on
2^{n}
vertices. Let
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
denote the set of dimensions. Each of the
2^{n}
vertices of the hypercube can be identified with a subset of
[n]
, corresponding to the set of nonzero coordinates of the vertex. This way, the vertex-set corresponds to the
power set

V=\mathcal{P}([n])
of
[n]
. For
v\in V
, we refer to
|v|
(the number of nonzero coordinates of
v
) as the
level
of
v
, and we denote the set of vertices with level
k
by
V_{k}
. We are interested in paths between the two endpoints
\emptyset
and
[n]
. Two vertices
v,v^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
are connected if they differ by a single coordinate, i.e., if
|v\triangle v^{\prime}|=1
, where
A\triangle B=(A\setminus B)\cup(B\setminus A)
is the symmetric difference of two sets,
A
and
B
. Because every vertex has degree
n
, the hypercube has
2^{n-1}n
edges in total. If
v\subset v^{\prime}\subseteq[n]
, then there exists a (not necessarily accessible) direct path from
v
to
v^{\prime}
. We denote the set of direct paths from
v
to
v^{\prime}
by
\Pi(v,v^{\prime})
. Each of these paths corresponds to a permutation of the directions
v^{\prime}\setminus v
, so that
|\Pi(v,v^{\prime})|=(|v^{\prime}|-|v|)!
if
v\subseteq v^{\prime}
and
|\Pi(v,v^{\prime})|=0
otherwise. For
U,U^{\prime}\subseteq V
, we write
\Pi(U,U^{\prime})=\bigcup_{u\in U,u^{\prime}\in U^{\prime}}\Pi(u,u^{\prime})
to denote the collection of direct paths from
U
to
U^{\prime}
.

We denote the set of hypercube edges by
E
. We say that an edge has level
k
if it connects a vertex of level
k-1
to a vertex of level
k
, and let
E_{k}
denote the set of hypercube edges at level
k
. We let
W(e)
denote the weight of edge
e\in E
. Each of the weights are independently drawn from a uniform distribution on
[0,1]
. For a path
\pi
, let
v_{k}(\pi)\in V_{k}
denote the level-
k
vertex of the path
\pi
. Similarly, let
W_{k}(\pi)
denote the weight of the
k
-th edge along this path, and let
|\pi|
denote the length of the path (i.e., the number of edges). We say that a path
\pi
is
accessible
if

W_{1}(\pi)<\dots<W_{|\pi|}(\pi),

and we denote the indicator of this event by
I(\pi)
. We are interested in the number of accessible paths from
\emptyset
to
[n]
. That is, we study the distribution of

X_{n}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(\emptyset,[n])}I(\pi).

We use standard asymptotic notation throughout. For a real sequence
a_{n}
and nonnegative real sequence
b_{n}
, we write
a_{n}=\mathcal{O}(b_{n})
if
|a_{n}|\leq Cb_{n}
for some
C>0
. Similarly,
a_{n}=\Omega(b_{n})
if
|a_{n}|\geq cb_{n}
for some
c>0
and
n
sufficiently large. If both
a_{n}=\mathcal{O}(b_{n})
and
a_{n}=\Omega(b_{n})
hold, then we write
a_{n}=\Theta(b_{n})
. If
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}/b_{n}=0
, then we write
a_{n}=o(b_{n})
or
a_{n}\ll b_{n}
. Finally, if
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}b_{n}/a_{n}=0
, then we write
a_{n}=\omega(b_{n})
or
a_{n}\gg b_{n}
.

1.2. Main results

Theorem 1.1
.

The number of accessible direct paths in the temporal hypercube converges in distribution to a mixed Poisson distribution, where the mixture corresponds to the product of two independent exponential random variables with rate
1
. That is,

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=x)\to\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(Z\cdot Z^{\prime})^{x}}{x!}e^{-Z\cdot Z^{\prime}}\right],

for
x\in\mathbb{N}
and
Z,Z^{\prime}\sim\text{Exp}(1)
independently.
More explicitly, the limiting distribution is given by

\mathbb{P}(X=x)=\sum_{k=0}^{x}{x\choose k}\frac{\delta-\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{r}r!}{k!},

(1)

where
\mathbb{P}(X=0)=\delta=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-z}}{1+z}\,dz\approx 0.596347
is the
Gompertz
constant
(
gompertz1825mortality
)
.

Similarly to the result about the supercritical vertex-weighted hypercube from

While

\emptyset

any

v

are contained in a

Theorem

also applies to

and

is concentrated around

We note that the convergence in distribution of
Theorem

1.1
does not necessarily imply convergence in moments, although we can obtain an explicit formula for the moments of the limiting distribution. Indeed, if
Y
is a random variable and
X\sim\text{Poi}(Y)
, then by Proposition 1 of
kuba2016moment
, the moments of
X
are given by

\mathbb{E}[X^{k}]=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{j}\mathbb{E}[Y^{j}]

where
\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{j}
is a
Stirling number of the second kind
, that is, the number of ways to partition a set of
k
objects into
j
nonempty parts. In our case, the mixing distribution has moments given by
\mathbb{E}[(Z\cdot Z^{\prime})^{k}]=\mathbb{E}[Z^{k}]^{2}=(k!)^{2}
, so that

\mathbb{E}[X^{k}]=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{k}{j}(j!)^{2}.

The sequence above is the
Stirling transform
of the sequence
(k!)^{2}.
At present, we can only prove that
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{k}]\to\mathbb{E}[X^{k}]
for
k\in\{1,2\}
. As mentioned above,
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]=1=\mathbb{E}[X]
for all
n
, and
\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{2}]\to\sum_{j=0}^{2}\genfrac{}{}{0.0pt}{}{2}{j}(j!)^{2}=5
, as summarized in the following lemma.

Lemma 1.2
.

The second moment of
X_{n}
converges to the second moment of
X\sim\text{Poi}(Z\cdot Z^{\prime})
. That is,

\mathbb{E}[X_{n}^{2}]\to\mathbb{E}[X^{2}]=5.

The limiting distribution is
heavy-tailed
, in the sense that its tail decays slower than any exponential
(
nair2022fundamentals
)
. A nonnegative random variable is heavy-tailed whenever

\lim_{x\to\infty}e^{tx}\mathbb{P}(X>x)\to\infty,

for all
t>0
. For
X\sim\text{Poi}(Z\cdot Z^{\prime})
, this can be seen from

\mathbb{P}(X>x)\geq\mathbb{P}(Z,Z^{\prime}>\sqrt{x})\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(X>Z\cdot Z^{\prime}\ \middle|\ Z,Z^{\prime}>\sqrt{x}\right)\sim\frac{1}{2}e^{-2\sqrt{x}}=e^{-o(x)}.

1.3. Proof overview

Here we give an overview of the proof of
Theorem

1.1
, beginning with an observation which helps us understand the structure of the union of accessible direct paths from
\emptyset
to
[n]
. A byproduct of our second moment computations in
Section

2
and
Appendix

A
is the following result, which we do not prove explicitly but which is easily derived from
Lemma

2.2
. Let
k=k(n)\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
grow to infinity arbitrarily slowly. W.h.p., any pair of accessible direct paths has the following structure: the paths travel together from
\emptyset
until they diverge at some level
0\leq\ell\leq k
, and are edge-disjoint until they meet again at some level
n-k\leq\ell^{\prime}\leq n
, after which point they travel together again to the terminal vertex
[n]
. In fact, a mild extension of our arguments shows that “edge-disjoint” can be replaced with “vertex-disjoint” in the preceding statement. The same structure was observed for the vertex-weighted case in
berestycki2016number
and
hegarty2014existence
, where similar second moment calculations are carried out.

Thus, the set of edges which are part of an accessible direct path from
\emptyset
to
[n]
induces a tree on the first
k
levels and another tree on the last
k
levels w.h.p., for any
k=k(n)\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
tending to infinity. Moreover, no accessible direct paths intersect between levels
k
and
n-k
w.h.p. This motivates splitting our analysis into two parts: one dealing with the “tree segments” at the ends of the hypercube, and one dealing with the “middle segment” in which leaves of the two trees are joined by compatible paths. We treat each part separately in Sections
3
and
4
.

Motivated by the observations above, for the tree segments our strategy is to greedily find a large tree in the first
k=k(n)
levels of the hypercube, with root vertex
\emptyset
, such that every path from
\emptyset
to a leaf is increasing, and such that the weights along the tree paths are small. We also find a symmetric counterpart to this tree in the last
k
levels rooted at
[n]
, this time with all paths decreasing and consisting of large weights. Each non-leaf vertex in these trees has
r
children. The children of a given vertex
v
in the bottom tree are selected by choosing the
r
directions with smallest weight which (i) have weight larger than the weight on the edge leading into
v
in the tree, and (ii) have not already been chosen by an ancestor of
v
in the tree. We use an analogous rule to construct the top tree. In
Lemma

3.2
, we show that if
k\to\infty
and
k^{4}r^{2}\log r\ll n
, then (a transformation of) the weights along the tree edges converge almost surely to sums of exponential random variables. A similar tree construction for the vertex-weighted hypercube appears in
berestycki2016number
, but instead of a fixed number of offspring they use a weight threshold to select children at each vertex. The fixed-offspring trees have additional structure, which we use in our proofs. We note that
berestycki2016number
proves a scaling limit while we characterize the exact limiting distribution, which requires more precise asymptotics for these tree segments.

For our purposes, choosing
r=\lceil\frac{3}{2}\log k\rceil
will be enough to ensure that the trees are large enough to capture all accessible direct paths with high probability. With this choice for
r
, we let
\Pi^{*}_{k}\subset\Pi(\emptyset,[n])
denote the set of paths where the first and last
k
edges correspond to paths in the trees. Note that
\Pi_{k}^{*}
is a random set, which depends only on the weights in the first and last
k
levels. We then define the random variable

X_{k,n}^{*}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi_{k}^{*}}I_{\pi}.

Clearly,
X_{k,n}^{*}\leq X_{n}
. We will prove in
Section

3
that the paths outside
\Pi_{k}^{*}
are negligible:

Lemma 1.3
.

For
1\ll k\ll\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\to\infty
, it holds w.h.p. that
X_{n}=X_{k,n}^{*}
.

Thus it will suffice to consider only the “tree paths” counted by
X_{k,n}^{*}
. Let
E_{k}^{*}
denote the set of edges in the first and last
k
levels of the hypercube, that is,

E^{*}_{k}=\bigcup_{\ell\in[k]}(E_{\ell}\cup E_{n-\ell+1}).

Let
\lambda_{k,n}=\mathbb{E}[X_{k,n}^{*}|(W(e))_{e\in E_{k}^{*}}]
. (The definitions of the trees and the random variable
X_{k,n}^{*}
will be made precise in
Section

3
.) We remark that
\lambda_{k,n}
is a random variable which depends only on the edge weights in the first and last
k
levels. The main result of
Section

3
is the following:

Lemma 1.4
.

There exists a coupling so that,
\lambda_{k,n}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}ZZ^{\prime}
as
1\ll k\ll\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\to\infty
, where
Z,Z^{\prime}\sim\text{Exp}(1)
independently.

The interpretation of
Lemma

1.4
is that each side of the hypercube contributes an exponentially distributed factor to the conditional expectation of
X_{k,n}^{*}
.

Using the Chen-Stein method, we prove the following lemma about the middle segment in
Section

4
:

Lemma 1.5
.

Let
1\ll k\ll n
and define
\lambda_{k,n}=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{k,n}^{*}\ \middle|\ (W(e))_{e\in E_{k}^{*}}\right]
. There exists a coupling between
X_{k,n}^{*}
and a (mixed) Poisson random variable
Y_{k,n}
with parameter
\lambda_{k,n}
, so that

\mathbb{P}(X_{k,n}^{*}\neq Y_{k,n})\to 0.

The above lemmas can be combined to prove our main result:

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

We first use
Lemma

1.3
to write

\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=x)=\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=x=X_{k,n}^{*})+\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=x,X_{k,n}^{*}\neq x)=\mathbb{P}(X_{k,n}^{*}=x)+o(1).

Then, we use
Lemma

1.5
to write

\mathbb{P}(X_{k,n}^{*}=x)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\lambda_{k,n}}\frac{\lambda_{k,n}^{x}}{x!}\right]+o(1).

Since
e^{-\lambda}\lambda^{x}
is bounded, we can apply
Lemma

1.4
and the continuous mapping theorem to obtain

\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-\lambda_{k,n}}\frac{\lambda_{k,n}^{x}}{x!}\right]\to\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-ZZ^{\prime}}\frac{(ZZ^{\prime})^{x}}{x!}\right],

which completes the proof. The alternative expression (
1
) is derived in
Lemma

C.1
.
∎

2. The second moment

Throughout this section, we let
\pi_{n}\in\Pi(\emptyset,[n])
denote the
canonical
direct path from
\emptyset
to
[n]
given by

\emptyset,[1],[2],\dots,[n].

Consider any
\pi\in\Pi(\emptyset,[n]).
We will encode
\pi
relative to
\pi_{n}
. First, we specify the
gap vector

\bm{a}
of
\pi
with respect to
\pi_{n}
. If
\pi
and
\pi_{n}
do not share any edges, then
\bm{a}
has only 1 coordinate,
a_{0}=n
. Assume then that these paths overlap at some point. The coordinates of
\bm{a}
are defined as follows: let
a_{0}
be the size of the gap, measured by number of edges of
\pi
(or
\pi_{n}
), between vertex
\emptyset
and the bottom vertex on the first edge
e_{1}
which is shared by
\pi
and
\pi_{n}
. For
i\geq 1
, let
a_{i}
be the gap between the top vertex of the
i
th shared edge
e_{i}
and the bottom vertex of the
(i+1)
st shared edge
e_{i+1}
, or
[n]
if there are no more shared edges. If
\pi
shares exactly
s
edges with
\pi_{n}
, then
\bm{a}
has exactly
s+1
coordinates, and these coordinates sum to
n-s
. Moreover,
a_{i}\neq 1
for all
i
, but we can have
a_{i}=0
. In particular,
\pi_{n}
itself has a gap vector of length
n+1
and consists of only zeros. See Figure

We can express the probability that a pair of paths is accessible using the gap vector
\bm{a}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\pi
be a path which has gap vector
\bm{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{s})
relative to
\pi_{n}
. Then the probability that both
\pi
and
\pi_{n}
are accessible is

\mathbb{E}[I_{\pi}I_{\pi_{n}}]=\frac{\prod_{i=0}^{s}\binom{2a_{i}}{a_{i}}}{(2n-s)!}.

Proof.

The edge weights induce a random permutation of the edges of
\pi_{n}\cup\pi
, and either path is increasing with respect to the edge weights if and only if it is increasing with respect to the order of the corresponding permutation. There are
(2n-s)!
possible permutations of the edges of
\pi_{n}\cup\pi
. Thinking of a permutation as a labeling of the edges with labels from the set
\{1,2,\dots,2n-s\}
, we observe that in any permutation for which both
\pi_{n}
and
\pi
are increasing, the labels of the
s
shared edges are completely determined, i.e., there is exactly
1
way these edges can be labeled. Similarly, the set of labels which can be assigned to edges in the
i
th gap (of both
\pi
and
\pi_{n}
) is also determined for
i=0,1,\dots,s
. For each
i
, there are
2a_{i}
labels to assign to the edges in gap
i
, and precisely
\binom{2a_{i}}{a_{i}}
assignments result in both
\pi_{n}
and
\pi
being increasing across the gap. Indeed, once we distribute labels between
\pi_{n}
and
\pi
, there is only one way to assign them to edges. Hence there are
\prod_{i=0}^{s}\binom{2a_{i}}{a_{i}}
permutations for which both
\pi_{n}
and
\pi
are increasing.
∎

The nonzero coordinates of a gap vector
\bm{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{s})
correspond to the
(nontrivial) gaps
between
\pi
and
\pi_{n}
, i.e., segments where
\pi
and
\pi_{n}
diverge from one another. If
\pi
has a gap vector of length
s+1
with
g
nonzero coordinates, we say that
\pi
and
\pi_{n}
share
s
edges with
g

(\pi,\pi^{\prime})
from
\Pi(\emptyset,[n])
which share
s
edges with
g
gaps. For a path
\pi
, we define

\mathcal{P}_{s,g}(\pi)=\{\pi^{\prime}\,:\,\pi\in\Pi(\emptyset,[n]),\,\pi\text{ and }\pi^{\prime}\text{ share }s\text{ edges with }g\text{ gaps}\}

and we let

\mathcal{P}_{s,g}=\bigcup_{\pi\in\Pi(\emptyset,[n])}\bigcup_{\pi^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{s,g}(\pi)}\Big\{(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\Big\}.

(2)

So
\mathcal{P}_{s,g}
is the set of all pairs of paths
(\pi,\pi^{\prime})
which share
s
edges with
g

\mathcal{P}_{s,g}
is nonempty precisely when
s\in\{0,1,2,\dots,n-2\}
and

, or
s=n
and
g=0
. (It is impossible for a pair of paths to share exactly
n-1
edges.)

For
s\in\{0,1,\dots,n-2\}
and
1\leq g\leq\min\left\{s+1,\left\lfloor\frac{n-s}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}
, or
s=n
and
g=0
, define

c_{s,g}=\sum_{(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}_{s,g}}\mathbb{E}[I_{\pi}I_{\pi^{\prime}}].

(3)

Note that by symmetry we may write

c_{s,g}=n!\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{s,g}(\pi_{n})}\mathbb{E}[I_{\pi_{n}}I_{\pi}]=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{s,g}(\pi_{n})}\mathbb{E}[I_{\pi}\,|\,I_{\pi_{n}}=1].

(4)

Thus we have the following simple interpretation of the term
c_{s,g}
: conditioned on the path
\pi_{n}
being accessible, we expect
c_{s,g}
accessible paths which share
s
edges and have
g
gaps with
\pi_{n}
.
Lemma

2.2
below computes the second moment of
X_{n}
by carefully summing the terms
c_{s,g}
over all valid

and

. In light of the above, we may interpret
Lemma

2.2
as stating that, conditioned on
\pi_{n}
being accessible, for any
k=k(n)
tending to infinity, in expectation there are
4+o(1)
accessible paths that share at most
k=k(n)
edges with
\pi_{n}
and have a single gap. Moreover, we show that, w.h.p., there are no accessible paths which have more than one gap, or which share more than
k
edges with
\pi_{n}
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The following three properties hold:

1.

\displaystyle\sum_{s=1}^{n-2}\sum_{g=2}^{\min\left\{s+1,\left\lfloor\frac{n-s}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}}c_{s,g}=o(1)
;

2.

for any

\displaystyle\sum_{s=k}^{n-2}c_{s,1}=o(1)

3.

for any

\displaystyle\sum_{s=0}^{k}c_{s,1}=4+o(1)

3. Tree segments

In this section, we will prove
Lemma

1.4
in two main steps. First, we construct trees consisting of the edges that are most likely to be part of accessible paths near each endpoint. In
Lemma

3.1
, we will introduce a coupling that proves that the weights of these tree edges converge almost surely to sums of exponential random variables. Second, we will construct two random variables
Z_{k},Z_{k}^{\prime}
that are functions of the weights in the first and last
k
levels, respectively. We will prove in
Lemma

3.6
that
Z_{k},Z_{k}^{\prime}
converge in probability to two independent exponentially distributed random variables.
Lemma

3.7
additionally proves
\lambda_{k,n}-Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}0
, which will lead to the proof of
Lemma

1.4
.

3.1. Tree coupling

In this section, we construct a coupling between exponential random variables and the ‘best’ weights in the first few levels. To do so, we first transform the uniform weights to exponential random variables. Notice that if
U\sim\text{Unif}([0,1])
, then

-\log(1-U)\sim\text{Exp}(1).

Let us denote these transformed weights by

\widetilde{W}(e)=-\log(1-W(e)).

(5)

A nice advantage of these transformed weights is that among
\ell
edges, the smallest weight will follow the
\text{Exp}(\ell)
distribution. Moreover, the difference between the smallest and second smallest weight is
\text{Exp}(\ell-1)
and so forth. This can be seen from the fact that the remaining
\ell-1
weights are each
\text{Exp}(1)
distributed conditioned on being larger than the smallest weight. By the memorylessness property of the exponential distribution, the difference between each of these weights and the smallest weights is independently
\text{Exp}(1)
distributed, so that the minimum of them is indeed
\text{Exp}(\ell-1)
distributed.

To construct a tree from the most important edges, we use an approach similar to
berestycki2016number
and exclude directions that have been taken by an ancestor node. The main difference is that we consider a fixed number of offspring
r
, while they consider weight thresholds. For
i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}\in[r]
, we denote by
\pi(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell};r)
a path from
\emptyset
to some
v\in V_{\ell}
where, in the first step, we make a step in the direction with the
i_{1}
-th smallest weight. After that, the path makes a step in the direction of the
i_{2}
-th smallest weight that is larger than the current weight and excludes the
r
directions that had the smallest weights in the first step. In the third step, we consider all weights larger than the current weight, excluding the
2r
‘best’ directions of its ancestors and pick the
i_{3}
-rd smallest among those, and so forth. Note that such a path
\pi(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell};r)
may not exist for all
r,\ell
and
i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell}
. In particular, such a path does not exist if
(\ell-1)r>n
.

Let
\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell})
denote the weight of this path (i.e.,
\widetilde{W}(e)
of the last edge
e
in this path) if the path
\pi(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell};r)
exists and
\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell})=\infty
otherwise. We will omit the subscript
n,r
whenever the relevant values are clear from the context. We will abbreviate
\bm{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell})
and will denote the
predecessor
of
\bm{i}\neq(1)
as

\bm{i}^{-}=\begin{cases}(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1},i_{\ell}-1)&\text{ if $i_{\ell}\geq 2$,}\\
(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1})&\text{ if $i_{\ell}=1$,}\end{cases}

and define the set of predecessors of
\bm{i}
as

P(\bm{i})=\{(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell^{\prime}-1},j)\ :\ \ell^{\prime}\in[\ell],j\in[i_{\ell^{\prime}}]\}.

We define

\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i})=\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i})-\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i}^{-}),

so that

\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i})=\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{j}).

(6)

Given
k
and
r
, if the path corresponding to
\bm{i}=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k})\in[r]^{k}
exists, then after transforming back to uniform edge weights using (
5
), the weight on the last edge
e
of
\bm{i}
is distributed as

W_{n,r}(\bm{i})=1-\exp\left(-\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{j})\right).

We may built a symmetric tree rooted at
[n]
in the top of the cube in a similar manner as above, except that at each step we choose the largest available weight rather than the smallest. By our construction, if the path
\bm{i}\in[r]^{k}
exists in the top cube, then for the last edge
e
on
\bm{i}
we have

W_{n,r}(e)=\exp\left(-\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta\widetilde{W}^{\prime}_{n,r}(\bm{j})\right)

where the
\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}^{\prime}(\bm{j})
’s are the increments of the top tree. We remark that
\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}^{\prime}(\bm{j})
has the same distribution as
\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{j})
for all
\bm{j}
. Moreover, if
k\leq\lfloor n/2\rfloor
, then the bottom tree and the top tree are independent, as they depend on disjoint edge sets. Most of our attention in this section is devoted to understanding the behavior of the sums
\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{j})
for
\bm{i}\in[r]^{k}
. To this end, below we describe a useful coupling between for the increments
\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{j})
.

We will couple
\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{j})
to independent
\text{Exp}(1)
random variables
(\Delta(\bm{i}))_{\bm{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{\infty}}
, where
\mathbb{N}^{\infty}=\bigcup_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{N}^{\ell}
is the set of integer sequences. For
k,r\in\mathbb{N}
our coupling is as follows. For
i_{1}\in[r]
, we couple

\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(i_{1})=\frac{1}{n-(i_{1}-1)}\Delta(i_{1}),

(Note that
\frac{1}{n-(i_{1}-1)}\Delta(i_{1})
is distributed as
\text{Exp}(n-(i_{1}-1))
for any
i_{1}\in[r]
.) To couple
\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i})
for
\bm{i}\in[r]^{\ell}
with
\ell\geq 2
, we first sample a binomial random variable for its parent
\bm{i}^{\prime}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1})
as

N_{n,r}(\bm{i}^{\prime})\sim\text{Bin}(n-(\ell-1)r,1-e^{-\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i}^{\prime})}),

(7)

and then couple

\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i})=\begin{cases}\frac{\Delta(\bm{i})}{n-(\ell-1)r-(i_{\ell}-1)-N_{n,r}(\bm{i}^{\prime})}&\text{ if $N_{n,r}(\bm{i}^{\prime})<n-(\ell-1)r-(i_{\ell}-1)$,}\\
\infty&\text{ else.}\end{cases}

(8)

(Note that the parent
\bm{i}^{\prime}
is not (necessarily) the same as the predecessor
\bm{i}^{-}
.)

Lemma 3.1
.

The coupling defined by (
7
) and (
8
) is valid.

Proof.

We start with level
\ell=1
and consider
i_{1}\in[r]
. If
i_{1}=1
, then
\Delta\widetilde{W}(1)=\widetilde{W}(1)
and

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{W}(1)\geq w)=e^{-wn},

which corresponds to the tail probability of an exponential random variable with rate
n
, so that
n\Delta\widetilde{W}(1)\sim\text{Exp}(1)
. Now, for
i_{1}\geq 2
, we know that there are
n-(i_{1}-1)
edges from
\emptyset
to
V_{1}
with weight larger than
\widetilde{W}(i_{1}-1)
, so that

\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{W}(i_{1})\geq\widetilde{W}(i_{1}-1)+w)=e^{-w(n-(i_{1}-1))},

so that indeed
(n-(i_{1}-1))\Delta\widetilde{W}(i_{1})\sim\text{Exp}(1)
.

For
\ell\geq 2
, we first note that among the
n
directions of the hypercube,
(\ell-1)r
need to be excluded as each of the
\ell-1
ancestors have expanded in
r
unique directions that we exclude for the remainder of the subtree.
Among the
n-(\ell-1)r
remaining directions, we need to find the
i_{\ell}
-th smallest weight that is larger than
\widetilde{W}(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1})
. Let
N(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1})
denote the number of edges that are smaller than this. Then, given
\widetilde{W}(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1})=w
, we have

N(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1})\sim\text{Bin}(n-(\ell-1)r,1-e^{-w}).

Then, given
N(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1})<n-(\ell-1)r-(i_{\ell}-1)
,
\widetilde{W}(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell})
is the
i_{\ell}
-th smallest among the
n-(\ell-1)r
weights that are each at least
\widetilde{W}(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1})
, so that

(n-(\ell-1)r-(i_{\ell}-1)-N(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1}))\cdot\Delta\widetilde{W}(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell})\sim\text{Exp}(1),

as required. Whenever
N(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell-1})\geq n-(\ell-1)r-(i_{\ell}-1)
, the path
\pi(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell};r)
does not exist, so that
\widetilde{W}(i_{1},\dots,i_{\ell})=\infty
.
∎

From (
8
), it is clear that we have
n\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i})\geq\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta(\bm{j})
for all
\bm{i}\in[r]^{k}
. In the next lemma, we show a nearly-matching upper bound for the
n\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i})
’s which holds with high probability provided
1\ll r\ll k
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
1\ll r\ll k
. There exists a coupling and a sequence
n_{k,r}
, so that with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{k^{2}}
,

n\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i})\leq\frac{1}{k}+\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta(\bm{j})

holds for all
\bm{i}\in[r]^{k}
and
n\geq n_{k,r}
. Equivalently,

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\bm{i}\in[r]^{k},n\geq n_{k,r}:\ n\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i})>\frac{1}{k}+\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta(\bm{j})\right)<\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

(9)

Proof.

We will use induction on
k
to prove that for any
k,r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\varepsilon>0
, there is a random variable
M_{k,r}(\varepsilon)
with
\mathbb{P}(M_{k,r}(\varepsilon)<\infty)=1
so that

n\geq M_{k,r}(\varepsilon)\Rightarrow\forall\bm{i}\in[r]^{\underline{k}}:\ n\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i})\leq\Delta(\bm{i})+\varepsilon,

(10)

where
[r]^{\underline{k}}=\bigcup_{\ell=1}^{k}[r]^{\ell}
.
For
k=1
, we have

n\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(i_{1})=\frac{\Delta(i_{1})}{1-\frac{i_{1}-1}{n}}=\Delta(i_{1})+\frac{i_{1}-1}{n-(i_{1}-1)}\Delta(i_{1})<\Delta(i_{1})+\frac{r}{n-r}\max_{i_{1}\in[r]}\Delta(i_{1}).

We solve

\frac{r}{n-r}\max_{i_{1}\in[r]}\Delta(i_{1})\leq\varepsilon\Rightarrow n\geq r+\frac{r}{\varepsilon}\max_{i_{1}\in[r]}\Delta(i_{1}),

so that the induction hypothesis (
10
) holds for
k=1
and any
r\in\mathbb{N}
with the random variable
M_{1,r}(\varepsilon)=r+\frac{r}{\varepsilon}\max_{i_{1}\in[r]}\Delta(i_{1})
.

For the induction step, we assume (
10
) holds up to
k
and prove that it also holds for
k+1
. Let us pick
\bm{i}\in[r]^{k+1}
and denote its parent by
\bm{i}^{\prime}\in[r]^{k}
. For
n\geq M_{k,r}(\varepsilon)
, the induction hypothesis tells us that

n\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{j})\leq\Delta(\bm{j})+\varepsilon

for all
\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i}^{\prime})\subset[r]^{\underline{k}}
. Summing over
\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i}^{\prime})
, we obtain

n\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i}^{\prime})\leq|P(\bm{i}^{\prime})|\varepsilon+\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i}^{\prime})}\Delta(\bm{i}),

so that the binomial random variable
N_{n,r}(\bm{i}^{\prime})
defined in
Eq.

7
is stochastically dominated by the Poisson random variable

Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i}^{\prime})\sim\text{Poi}\left(|P(\bm{i}^{\prime})|\varepsilon+\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i}^{\prime})}\Delta(\bm{j})\right).

Note that
Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i}^{\prime})
does not depend on
n,k,r
. This means that for any
n\geq M_{k,r}(\varepsilon)
, we can couple
N_{n,r}(\bm{i}^{\prime})
to
Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i}^{\prime})
in a way that ensures
N_{n,r}(\bm{i}^{\prime})\leq Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i}^{\prime})
.
This means that

\displaystyle n\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta(\bm{i})}{1-\frac{kr+i_{\ell}-1}{n}-\frac{1}{n}N_{n,r}(\bm{i}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\Delta(\bm{i})}{1-\frac{(k+1)r}{n}-\frac{1}{n}Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i}^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\Delta(\bm{i})+\frac{(k+1)r+Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i}^{\prime})}{n-(k+1)r-Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i}^{\prime})}\Delta(\bm{i})

\displaystyle\leq\Delta(\bm{i})+\varepsilon,

for
n\geq((k+1)r+Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i}^{\prime}))(1+\Delta(\bm{i})/\varepsilon)
. This leads to

M_{k+1,r}(\varepsilon)=\max\left\{M_{k,r}(\varepsilon),\max_{\bm{i}\in[r]^{k+1}}\left\{((k+1)r+Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i}^{\prime}))(1+\Delta(\bm{i})/\varepsilon)\right\}\right\},

which is the maximum of a finite number of random variables, so that
\mathbb{P}(M_{k+1,r}(\varepsilon)<\infty)=1
, which completes the induction proof.

The random variable
M_{k,r}(\varepsilon)
can be rewritten as follows:

M_{k,r}(\varepsilon)=\max_{\ell\in[k],\bm{i}\in[r]^{\ell}}\left\{(\ell r+Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i}^{\prime}))\left(1+\frac{\Delta(\bm{i})}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\}.

To complete the proof, we consider the random variable
M_{k,r}(k^{-2}r^{-1})
and pick
n_{k,r}
such that

\mathbb{P}(M_{k,r}(k^{-2}r^{-1})>n_{k,r})<\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

If there is some
n\geq n_{k,r}
such that

\exists\bm{i}\in[r]^{k}:\ n\widetilde{W}(\bm{i})>\frac{1}{k}+\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta(\bm{j}),

then there must be some
\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})
with
n\Delta\widetilde{W}_{n,r}(\bm{j})>\Delta(\bm{j})+k^{-2}r^{-1}
. But by (
10
), this
implies
M_{k,r}(k^{-2}r^{-1})>n
.
Hence,

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists\bm{i}\in[r]^{k},n\geq n_{k,r}:\ n\widetilde{W}(\bm{i})>\frac{1}{k}+\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta(\bm{j})\right)\leq\mathbb{P}(M_{k,r}(k^{-2}r^{-1})>n_{k,r})<\frac{1}{k^{2}},

as stated in the lemma.
∎

The following lemma derives an admissible sequence
n_{k,r}
, which we prove in
Appendix

B
.

Lemma 3.3
.

For
1\ll r\ll k
, there exists a coupling and a sequence
n_{k,r}\sim 2k^{4}r^{2}\log r
that achieves (
9
).

In particular,
Lemma

3.3
tells us that
r_{k}=\lceil\frac{3}{2}\log k\rceil
and
k\ll\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
is sufficient to ensure

.

3.2. Tree functionals

In this section, we introduce the functional
Z_{k}=Z_{n,k,r_{k}}
, which is a function of the tree weights
(\widetilde{W}_{n,r_{k}}(\bm{i}))_{\bm{i}\in[r_{k}]^{k}}
and its inverted counterpart
Z_{k}^{\prime}
, which is a function of the tree weights from the other endpoint of the hypercube.

Corollary 3.4
.

Define

Z_{n,k,r}=\sum_{\bm{i}\in[r]^{k}}e^{-n\widetilde{W}(\bm{i})},\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{\infty,k,r}=\sum_{\bm{i}\in[r]^{k}}\exp\left(-\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta(\bm{j})\right).

Then, there is some sequence
n_{k}\geq k
as
k\to\infty
so that

\frac{Z_{n_{k},k,r_{k}}}{Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{a.s.}}}{{\to}}1,

as
k\to\infty
, where
r_{k}=\lceil\frac{3}{2}\log k\rceil
. Moreover,
\mathbb{E}[Z_{n_{k},k,r_{k}}]\to 1
.

Proof.

We pick
n_{k}=n_{k,r_{k}}
, for
n_{k,r}
as given by
Lemma

3.2
.
This means that with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{k^{2}}
,

0\leq n\widetilde{W}_{n_{k},r_{k}}(\bm{i})-\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta(\bm{j})\leq\frac{1}{k},

holds for all
\bm{i}\in[r_{k}]^{k}.

By monototonicity of
Z_{n,k,r}
, we get

e^{-\frac{1}{k}}Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}\leq Z_{n_{k},k,r_{k}}\leq Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}.

The remainder of the convergence proof follows from the Borel-Cantelli lemma.

For the expectation, we use the dominated convergence theorem with bound
Z_{n_{k},k,r_{k}}\leq Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}
and compute

\mathbb{E}[Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}]=\sum_{i\in[r_{k}]}2^{-i}\mathbb{E}[Z_{\infty,k-1,r_{k}}],

by independence of the
\Delta(\bm{i})
and
\mathbb{E}[e^{-\Delta(\bm{i})}]=\frac{1}{2}
. Solving this recursion leads to

\mathbb{E}[Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}]=\left(1-2^{-r}\right)^{k}=1-\mathcal{O}(k2^{-r_{k}}).

Our choice
r_{k}=\lceil\frac{3}{2}\log k\rceil
ensures
k2^{-r_{k}}\leq k^{1-\frac{3}{2}\log 2}\to 0
since
1-\frac{3}{2}\log 2\approx-0.0397<0
.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

There exists a random variable
Z\sim\textup{Exp}(1)
, so that

Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}Z,

as
k\to\infty
and
{r_{k}=\lceil\frac{3}{2}\log k\rceil}.

Proof.

For
i\in[r]
, define

Z_{k-1,r}^{(i)}=\sum_{(i_{2},\dots,i_{k})\in[r]^{k-1}}\exp\left(-\sum_{\ell=2}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{i_{\ell}}\Delta(i,i_{2},\dots,i_{\ell-1},j)\right).

Then note that
Z_{k-1,r}^{(1)},\dots,Z_{k-1,r}^{(r)}
are independent copies of
Z_{\infty,k-1,r}
, since they are functions of disjoint sets of
\Delta(\bm{i})
’s.
Moreover,

Z_{\infty,k,r}=\sum_{i=1}^{r}e^{-\sum_{j=1}^{i}\Delta(j)}Z_{k-1,r}^{(i)}.

This tells us that

\mathbb{E}[Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}]=(1-2^{-r_{k}})\mathbb{E}[Z_{\infty,k-1,r_{k}}]=(1-2^{-r_{k}})^{k}\to 1.

We now show that the limiting random variable

Z=\lim_{k\to\infty}\lim_{r\to\infty}Z_{\infty,k,r}

exists.
To show that the inner limit exists, note that
[r]^{k}\subset[r+1]^{k}
, so that
Z_{\infty,k,r+1}-Z_{\infty,k,r}>0
. Moreover, the Markov inequality allows us to bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\infty,k,r+1}-Z_{\infty,k,r}>r^{-2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle r^{2}\mathbb{E}[Z_{\infty,k,r+m}-Z_{\infty,k,r}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{2}\left((1-2^{-r-1})^{k}-(1-2^{-r})^{k}\right)=\mathcal{O}(r^{2}k2^{-r}).

This yields

\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{\infty,k,r+1}-Z_{\infty,k,r}>r^{-2}\right)=k\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{O}(r^{2}2^{-r})<\infty.

We then use the Borel-Cantelli lemma to conclude that
Z_{\infty,k,r}
converges almost surely as
r\to\infty
to some random variable
Z_{\infty,k,\infty}
.

To prove that
Z_{\infty,k,\infty}
converges as
k\to\infty
, we show that it is a martingale:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Z_{\infty,k+1,\infty}\ \middle|\ \left(\Delta(\bm{i})\right)_{\bm{i}\in\bigcup_{1\leq\ell\leq k}\mathbb{N}^{\ell}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}}\exp\left(-\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta(\bm{j})\right)\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}\exp\left(-\sum_{1\leq b\leq a}\Delta(\bm{i},b)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}}\exp\left(-\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta(\bm{j})\right)\cdot\sum_{a=1}^{\infty}2^{-a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}}\exp\left(-\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})}\Delta(\bm{j})\right)\cdot 1=Z_{\infty,k,\infty}.

In the second line, we used that if
\Delta\sim\text{Exp}(1),
then
\mathbb{E}[\exp(-\Delta)]=\frac{1}{2}
, and thus by independence
\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{b=1}^{a}\Delta(\bm{i},b)\right)\right]=2^{-a}
. Since
Z_{\infty,k,\infty}
is a nonnegative martingale, Doob’s martingale theorem ensures that it converges almost surely to some random variable
Z
.

Next, we show that
Z\sim\text{Exp}(1)
.
To do so, we first rewrite
Z_{\infty,k,\infty}
. Note that
1\in P(\bm{i})
for all
\bm{i}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle Z_{\infty,k,\infty}

\displaystyle=e^{-\Delta(1)}\sum_{\bm{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{k}}\exp\left(-\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})\setminus\{1\}}\Delta(\bm{j})\right)

\displaystyle=e^{-\Delta(1)}\sum_{\bm{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{k}\ :\ i_{1}=1}\exp\left(-\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})\setminus\{1\}}\Delta(\bm{j})\right)+e^{-\Delta(1)}\sum_{\bm{i}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{k}\ :\ i_{1}>1}\exp\left(-\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i})\setminus\{1\}}\Delta(\bm{j})\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{=}}e^{-\Delta(1)}Z_{\infty,k-1,\infty}+e^{-\Delta(1)}Z_{\infty,k,\infty}.

Since
\Delta(1)\sim\text{Exp}(1)
, we have
e^{-\Delta(1)}\sim\text{Unif}([0,1])
. Taking the limit
k\to\infty
on both sides tells us that

Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle D}}{{=}}U\cdot(Z_{1}^{\prime}+Z_{2}^{\prime}),

where
U\sim\text{Unif}([0,1])
and
Z_{1}^{\prime},Z_{2}^{\prime}
are independent copies of
Z
. We now derive the moment-generating function (MGF) of
Z
.

\displaystyle M(t)=\mathbb{E}[e^{-tZ}]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{E}\left[e^{tU\cdot(Z_{1}^{\prime}+Z_{2}^{\prime})}\ \middle|\ Z_{1}^{\prime},Z_{2}^{\prime}\right]\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{1}e^{tu\cdot(Z_{1}^{\prime}+Z_{2}^{\prime})}du\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e^{t\cdot(Z_{1}^{\prime}+Z_{2}^{\prime})}-1}{t\cdot(Z_{1}^{\prime}+Z_{2}^{\prime})}\right].

Taking the derivative with respect to
t
yields

\displaystyle M^{\prime}(t)=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{(Z_{1}^{\prime}+Z_{2}^{\prime})\cdot e^{t\cdot(Z_{1}^{\prime}+Z_{2}^{\prime})}}{t\cdot(Z_{1}^{\prime}+Z_{2}^{\prime})}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{e^{t\cdot(Z_{1}^{\prime}+Z_{2}^{\prime})}-1}{t^{2}\cdot(Z_{1}^{\prime}+Z_{2}^{\prime})}\right]=\frac{M(t)^{2}-M(t)}{t}.

This is a separable differential equation:

\frac{M^{\prime}(t)}{M(t)^{2}-M(t)}=\frac{1}{t}.

Integrating both sides yields

\log(1-M(t))-\log M(t)=\log(t)+c\\
\Rightarrow\frac{1-M(t)}{M(t)}=te^{c}\\
\Rightarrow M(t)=\frac{1}{1+te^{c}}.

The constant
e^{c}
is determined by
1=\mathbb{E}[Z]=M^{\prime}(0)=-e^{c}
, so that
e^{c}=-1
. We conclude that

\mathbb{E}[e^{tZ}]=(1-t)^{-1},

so that indeed
Z\sim\text{Exp}(1)
.

Finally, we prove
Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}Z
for our choice of
r_{k}
.
Since
Z_{\infty,k,r}
is increasing in
r
, we have
Z_{\infty,k,\infty}-Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}>0
.
The Markov inequality allows us to bound

\mathbb{P}(Z_{\infty,k,\infty}-Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}>\varepsilon)\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left(1-(1-2^{-r_{k}})^{k}\right)=\mathcal{O}(k2^{-r_{k}})\to 0.

This proves
Z_{\infty,k,\infty}-Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}0
, so that
Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}Z
.
∎

Lemma 3.6
.

Consider
Z_{k}=Z_{n,k,r_{k}}
and its inverted counterpart
Z_{k}^{\prime}
for
r_{k}=\lceil\frac{3}{2}\log k\rceil
.
There exists a coupling so that

Z_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}Z,\quad\text{and}\quad Z_{k}^{\prime}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}Z^{\prime},

as
1\ll k\ll\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\to\infty
,
where
Z,Z^{\prime}\sim\textup{Exp}(1)
are independent exponential random variables.

Proof.

We take
Z_{k}=Z_{n_{k},k,r_{k}}
as defined in
Corollary

3.4
, which is a function of the weights of the first
k
levels. For
Z_{k}^{\prime}
, we take the inverted variant of
Z_{n,k,r_{k}}
. That is, we invert the hypercube by mapping every vertex
v\in V
to its opposite
[n]\setminus v
, and replacing every edge weight
W(e)
by
1-W(e)
. Let
Z^{\prime}_{n,k,r_{k}}
denote the function
Z_{n,k,r_{k}}
applied to this inverted hypercube. Hence,
Z^{\prime}_{n,k,r_{k}}
is a function of the last
k
levels of the original hypercube.

We take the limit
Z
from
Lemma

3.5
and use
Corollary

3.4
to write

\frac{Z_{k}}{Z}=\frac{Z_{n_{k},k,r_{k}}}{Z}=\frac{Z_{n_{k},k,r_{k}}}{Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}}\frac{Z_{\infty,k,r_{k}}}{Z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}1,

so that indeed
Z_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\to}}Z
.
The proof for
Z_{k}^{\prime}
is identical.
∎

To prove
Lemma

1.3
and
Lemma

1.4
, we will make use of the following lemma, which is proven in
Appendix

B
. Recall that
\lambda_{k,n}=\mathbb{E}[X_{k,n}^{*}|(W(e))_{e\in E_{k}^{*}}]
.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
Z_{k}=Z_{n,k,r_{k}}
as defined in
Corollary

3.4
and its inverted counterpart
Z_{k}^{\prime}
. Then

\mathbb{E}[|\lambda_{k,n}-Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\prime}|]\to 0,

for
1\ll k\ll\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}\to\infty
.

Proof of
Lemma

1.3
.

From
\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\prime}]\to 1
(
Corollary

3.4
) and
\mathbb{E}[|\lambda_{k,n}-Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\prime}|]\to 0
(
Lemma

3.7
), it follows that
\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{k,n}]\to 1
. This means that the nonnegative random variable
X_{n}-X_{k,n}^{*}
has vanishing expectation, so that it follows from the Markov inequality that
X_{n}=X_{k,n}^{*}
with high probability.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

1.4
.

The result follows directly by combining
Lemma

3.6
and
Lemma

3.7
.
∎

4. Middle part

To prove the coupling result from
Lemma

1.5
, we use the Chen-Stein method
(
arratia1990poisson
)
to prove that there exists a coupling between
Y_{k,n}
and
X_{k,n}^{*}
where
\mathbb{P}(Y_{k,n}\neq X_{k,n}^{*})
is small. The Chen-Stein method is typically formulated in terms of the
Total Variation
distance. However, for our purpose, it is not necessary to formally introduce the concept of total variation.
We use the following ‘local’ version of the Chen-Stein method, as presented in
ross2011fundamentals
:

Theorem 4.1

(Theorem 4.7 of
ross2011fundamentals
)
.

Let
(I_{\theta})_{\theta\in\Theta}
be a set of indicators, and let
\Gamma(\theta)\subset\Theta
be the set of indicators that are dependent on
I_{\theta}
. Define
X=\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}I_{\theta}
,
\lambda=\mathbb{E}[X]
and
Z\sim\text{Poi}(\lambda)
. There exists a coupling between
X
and
Z
so that

\mathbb{P}(X\neq Z)\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{\lambda}\right\}\sum_{\theta\in\Theta}\sum_{\theta^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\theta)}\left(\mathbb{E}[I_{\theta}]\mathbb{E}[I_{\theta^{\prime}}]+{\mathbf{1}}_{[\theta\neq\theta^{\prime}]}\mathbb{E}[I_{\theta}I_{\theta^{\prime}}]\right).

Let us define
\Pi^{*}_{k}
as the set of paths
\pi
from
\emptyset
to
[n]
so that the first
k
steps of
\pi
are contained in the
r_{k}
-ary tree rooted at
\emptyset
and the last
k
steps are contained in the tree rooted at
[n]
.
We consider

X_{k,n}^{*}=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi^{*}_{k}}I_{\pi}\leq X_{n}.

To simplify the notation, we abbreviate

\mathbb{P}_{k}(\cdot)=\mathbb{P}(\cdot|(W(e))_{e\in E_{k}^{*}}),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{k}[\cdot]=\mathbb{E}[\cdot|(W(e))_{e\in E_{k}^{*}}].

Proof of
Lemma

1.5
.

We consider the equivalent statement

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbb{P}_{k}(X_{k,n}^{*}\neq Y_{k,n})\right]\to 0,

which we prove by bounding
\mathbb{P}_{k}(X_{k,n}^{*}\neq Y_{k,n})
using the Chen-Stein method.
To apply
Theorem

4.1
, we need to construct a set of dependent events for each
\pi
. Two indicators
I_{\pi},I_{\pi^{\prime}}
are dependent in
\mathbb{P}_{k}
if they share an edge in the middle levels. For each
\pi\in\Pi^{*}_{k}
, we define
\Gamma(\pi)
as the set of such overlapping paths.
Applying
Theorem

4.1
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{k}(X_{k,n}^{*}\neq Y_{k,n})

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,\frac{1}{\lambda_{k,n}}\right\}\sum_{\pi\in\Pi^{*}_{k}}\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)}\left(\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi}]\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi^{\prime}}]+{\mathbf{1}}_{[\pi\neq\pi^{\prime}]}\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi}I_{\pi^{\prime}}]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\pi\in\Pi^{*}_{k}}\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)}\left(\frac{1}{\lambda_{k,n}}\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi}]\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi^{\prime}}]+{\mathbf{1}}_{[\pi\neq\pi^{\prime}]}\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi}I_{\pi^{\prime}}]\right).

(11)

We start by bounding the first part of the sum. We write

\sum_{\pi\in\Pi^{*}_{k}}\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)}\frac{1}{\lambda_{k,n}}\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi}]\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi^{\prime}}]\leq\max_{\pi\in\Pi^{*}}\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)}\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi^{\prime}}].

Let
v_{k}(\pi)\in V_{k}
denote the
k
-th vertex of
\pi
. Define

V_{k}^{*}=\{v_{k}(\pi)\ :\ \pi\in\Pi_{k}^{*}\},

and define
V_{n-k}^{*}
similarly. For a path
\pi
,
\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi}]
depends on the gap between the tree weights. This gap only depends on the endpoints
u=v_{k}(\pi)
and
u^{\prime}=v_{n-k}(\pi)
. Let us denote this weight gap by
w(u,u^{\prime})
. Then

\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi}]=\frac{w(u,u^{\prime})^{n-2k}}{(n-2k)!}.

For
u\in V_{k},u^{\prime}\in V_{n-k}
, we define

\Gamma(\pi;u,u^{\prime})=\{\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)\ :\ v_{k}(\pi^{\prime})=u,v_{n-k}(\pi^{\prime})=u^{\prime}\}.

This allows us to write

\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)}I_{\pi^{\prime}}\right]=\sum_{u\in V_{k}^{*}}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in V_{n-k}^{*}}w(u,u^{\prime})^{n-2k}\frac{|\Gamma(\pi;u,u^{\prime})|}{(n-2k)!}.

We now bound
|\Gamma(\pi;u,u^{\prime})|/(n-2k)!
uniformly over
u\in V_{k}^{*},u^{\prime}\in V_{n-k}^{*}
, and
\pi\in\Pi_{k}^{*}
. We do this by a union bound. Let
\Gamma_{\ell}(\pi;u,u^{\prime})
be the subset of
\Gamma(\pi;u,u^{\prime})
containing the paths that share an edge at level
k+\ell
with
\pi
. By the union bound,

|\Gamma(\pi;u,u^{\prime})|\leq\sum_{\ell=1,\dots,n-2k}|\Gamma_{\ell}(\pi;u,u^{\prime})|.

Then

\displaystyle|\Gamma_{\ell}(\pi;u,u^{\prime})|

\displaystyle=(\ell-1)!\cdot(n-2k-\ell)!{\mathbf{1}}_{[w(u,u^{\prime})>0,u\subset v_{k+\ell}(\pi)\subset u^{\prime}]}

\displaystyle\leq(\ell-1)!\cdot(n-2k-\ell)!

\displaystyle=\frac{(n-2k-1)!}{{n-2k-1\choose\ell-1}}.

This leads to

\frac{|\Gamma(\pi;u,u^{\prime})|}{(n-2k)!}\leq\frac{1}{n-2k}\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-2k}{n-2k-1\choose\ell-1}^{-1}\sim\frac{2}{n}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)}I_{\pi^{\prime}}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\in V_{k}^{*}}\sum_{u^{\prime}\in V_{n-k}^{*}}w(u,u^{\prime})^{n-2k}\frac{|\Gamma(\pi;u,u^{\prime})|}{(n-2k)!}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}_{k}[X_{k,n}^{*}]}{n-2k}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-2k-1}{n-2k-1\choose\ell}^{-1}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2\lambda_{k,n}}{n}.

Since this bound holds for all
\pi\in\Pi^{*}_{k}
uniformly, we have obtained

\max_{\pi\in\Pi^{*}}\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)}\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi^{\prime}}]=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\lambda_{k,n}}{n}\right).

We now bound the second part of the sum in (
11
).
We first take the expectation:

\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{\pi\in\Pi^{*}_{k}}\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)}{\mathbf{1}}_{[\pi\neq\pi^{\prime}]}\mathbb{E}_{k}[I_{\pi}I_{\pi^{\prime}}]\right]=\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(\emptyset,[n])}\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi(\emptyset,[n])}{\mathbf{1}}_{[\pi\neq\pi^{\prime}]}\mathbb{E}[I_{\pi}I_{\pi^{\prime}}{\mathbf{1}}_{[\pi\in\Pi^{*}_{k},\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)]}].

The condition
\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)
implies that
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
share an edge in
\bigcup_{\ell=k+1}^{n-k}E_{\ell}
.
This implies that
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
have
g\geq 2
gaps or that they share more than
k
edges. (See
Section

2
.) Therefore, we can bound by simply summing over all pairs

(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}_{k}^{*}=\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\geq 1\\
g\geq 2\end{subarray}}\mathcal{P}_{s,g}\right)\cup\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}n-2\geq s>k\\
g\geq 1\end{subarray}}\mathcal{P}_{s,g}\right),

where
\mathcal{P}_{s,g}
is the set of ordered pairs of paths sharing exactly
s
edges with
g
gaps as defined in (
2
). We also recall the definition
c_{s,g}=\sum_{(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}_{s,g}}\mathbb{E}[I_{\pi}I_{\pi^{\prime}}]
in (
3
). We have the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\Pi(\emptyset,[n])}\sum_{\pi^{\prime}\in\Pi(\emptyset,[n])}{\mathbf{1}}_{[\pi\neq\pi^{\prime}]}\mathbb{E}[I_{\pi}I_{\pi^{\prime}}{\mathbf{1}}_{[\pi\in\Pi^{*}_{k},\pi^{\prime}\in\Gamma(\pi)]}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{(\pi,\pi^{\prime})\in\mathcal{P}_{k}^{*}}\mathbb{E}[I_{\pi}I_{\pi^{\prime}}]=\sum_{s=k+1}^{\infty}c_{s,1}+\sum_{g=2}^{\infty}\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}c_{s,g}=o(1),

by
Lemma

2.2
.
In conclusion, we can bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X_{k,n}^{*}\neq Y_{k,n})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{k,n}]}{n}\right)+o(1)\to 0,

which finishes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Appendix A
Proof of
Lemma

2.2

Here we prove
Lemma

2.2
. We break up the proof into a series of smaller lemmas for readability. We begin by deriving an explicit expression for the term
c_{s,g}
. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, let
w_{n}
denote the number of paths in
\Pi(\emptyset,[n])
which are edge disjoint from
\pi_{n}
. We take
w_{0}=1
by convention,
w_{1}=0
, and in general we have
w_{n}\leq n!
. For
N,j,b\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, define

\mathcal{C}(N,j;b)=\left\{(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{j})\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}^{j}\,:\,\sum_{i=1}^{j}x_{i}=N,\,x_{i}\geq b\,\forall\,i\right\}.

In other words,
\mathcal{C}(N,j;b)
is the set of natural number compositions of
N
with
j
parts of at least
b
. We write
\mathcal{C}(N,j)
for
\mathcal{C}(N,j;0)
. For future reference, we observe

|\mathcal{C}(N,j;b)|=\binom{N-(b-1)j-1}{j-1}

(where the binomial coefficient on the right is
0
whenever
N-(b-1)j-1<j-1
).

Lemma A.1
.

For
s\in\{0,1,\dots,n-2\}
and
1\leq g\leq\min\left\{s+1,\left\lfloor\frac{n-s}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}
,

c_{s,g}=\binom{s+1}{g}\frac{n!}{(2n-s)!}\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g;2)}\prod_{i=1}^{g}w_{x_{i}}\binom{2x_{i}}{x_{i}}.

Proof.

We encode paths
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{s,g}(\pi_{n})
by the pairs
(\bm{a},\bm{G})
as outlined at the beginning of
Section

2
. By definition, the vector
\bm{a}
has length
s+1
and exactly
g
nonzero coordinates, all at least
2
, summing to
n-s
. To specify one uniquely we may thus choose a composition
\bm{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{g})\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g;2)
, then choose
g
coordinates out of
s+1
total in
a
for
\bm{x}
to occupy. If some
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{s,g}(\pi_{n})
has gap vector
\bm{a}=(a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{s})
with nonzero coordinates
(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{g})\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g;2)
, then by
Lemma

2.1
we have

\mathbb{E}[I_{\pi_{n}}I_{\pi}]=\frac{1}{(2n-s)!}\prod_{i=0}^{s}\binom{2a_{i}}{a_{i}}=\frac{1}{(2n-s)!}\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{2x_{i}}{x_{i}}

since
\binom{2a_{i}}{a_{i}}=1
if
a_{i}=0
. Moreover, given
\bm{a}
, there are exactly

\prod_{i=0}^{s}w_{a_{i}}=\prod_{i=1}^{g}w_{x_{i}}

choices for
\bm{G}
. It follows that

\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{s,g}(\pi_{n})}\mathbb{E}[I_{\pi_{n}}I_{\pi}]=\binom{s+1}{g}\frac{1}{(2n-s)!}\prod_{i=1}^{g}w_{x_{i}}\binom{2x_{i}}{x_{i}},

and the proof of the lemma is finished.
∎

We also establish a convenient upper bound on
c_{s,g}
which we will use repeatedly.

Lemma A.2
.

For any
0\leq s\leq n-2
and
1\leq g\leq\min\left\{s+1,\left\lfloor\frac{n-s}{2}\right\rfloor\right\}
, we have

c_{s,g}\leq\frac{\binom{2(n-s)}{n-s}}{\binom{2n-s}{n}}\binom{s+1}{g}\frac{2^{g-1}}{(g-1)!(n-s-g)^{g-1}}

Proof.

We begin by writing

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g;2)}\prod_{i=1}^{g}w_{x_{i}}\binom{2x_{i}}{x_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g;2)}\prod_{i=1}^{g}(x_{i})!\binom{2x_{i}}{x_{i}}

(12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(n-s)!\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g;2)}\binom{n-s}{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{g}}^{-1}\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{2x_{i}}{x_{i}}

where we use
w_{x}\leq x!
for any
x
in the first line. It is straightforward to show that

\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g;2)}\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{2x_{i}}{x_{i}}\leq\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g)}\prod_{i=1}^{g}\binom{2x_{i}}{x_{i}}=\binom{2(n-s)}{n-s},

(13)

i.e., the maximum of the product in the middle above occurs at the point
\bm{x}=(n-s,0,0,\dots,0)\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g)
. Similarly,

\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g;2)}\binom{n-s}{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{g}}=\binom{n-s}{n-s-2(g-1),2,\dots,2}=\frac{(n-s)!}{2^{g-1}(n-s-2(g-1))!}.

Since
|\mathcal{C}(n-s,g;2)|=\binom{n-s-g-1}{g-1}
, we see

\displaystyle\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g;2)}\binom{n-s}{x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{g}}^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\binom{n-s-g-1}{g-1}\cdot\frac{2^{g-1}(n-s-2(g-1))!}{(n-s)!}

(14)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2^{g-1}}{(g-1)!}\cdot\frac{(n-s-g-1)!}{(n-s)!}\cdot\frac{(n-s-2(g-1))!}{(n-s-2g)!}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2^{g-1}}{(g-1)!}\cdot\frac{(n-s-2g+2)(n-s-2g+1)}{(n-s-g)^{g+1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{2^{g-1}}{(g-1)!}\frac{1}{(n-s-g)^{g-1}}\ .

For the final inequality we need that
g\geq 2
, which implies
n-s-2g+1,n-s-2g+2\leq n-s-g
. Even so, the inequality (
14
) clearly still holds (with equality) for
g=1
, since in this case there is only the single summand
\binom{n-s}{n-s}^{-1}=1
. Substituting the bounds (
13
) and (
14
) into (
12
) yields

\sum_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{C}(n-s,g;2)}\prod_{i=1}^{g}w_{x_{i}}\binom{2x_{i}}{x_{i}}\leq(n-s)!\binom{2(n-s)}{n-s}\frac{2^{g-1}}{(g-1)!(n-s-g)^{g-1}}

from which point the result easily follows using the expression for
c_{s,g}
in
Lemma

A.1
.
∎

Lemma A.3
.

For
s\geq 0
satisfying
n-s\gg 1
, we have

\frac{\binom{2(n-s)}{n-s}}{\binom{2n-s}{n}}=\mathcal{O}(1)e^{-nf(s/n)}

where
f(x)=\log\left(\frac{(2-x)^{2-x}}{(4(1-x))^{1-x}}\right)
for
x\in[0,1]
. The function
f
satisfies
f(0)=f(1)=0
,
f
is strictly increasing on
[0,2/3]
and strictly decreasing on
[2/3,1]
, and
f(x)\geq\frac{x}{2}
for
0\leq x\leq\frac{1}{3}
.

Proof.

We will obtain the asymptotic expression for the ratio with Stirling’s formula (
s!=(1+o(1))\sqrt{2\pi s}(s/e)^{s}
). We first simplify the ratio as

\frac{\binom{2(n-s)}{n-s}}{\binom{2n-s}{n}}=\frac{(2(n-s))!n!}{(2n-s)!(n-s)!}.

We apply Stirling’s formula to each factorial on the right-hand side individually. The total sub-exponential factor is given by

\sqrt{\frac{4\pi(n-s)\cdot 2\pi n}{2\pi(2n-s)\cdot 2\pi(n-s)}}\leq\sqrt{2}

where we used
2n-s\geq n
. The exponential factor is

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\frac{2(n-s)}{e}\right)^{2(n-s)}\left(\frac{n}{e}\right)^{n}}{\left(\frac{2n-s}{e}\right)^{2n-s}\left(\frac{n-s}{e}\right)^{n-s}}=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{(2(1-s/n))^{2(1-s/n)}}{(2-s/n)^{2-s/n}(1-s/n)^{1-s/n}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{(4(1-s/n))^{1-s/n}}{(2-s/n)^{2-s/n}}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e^{-nf(s/n)}

This establishes the asymptotic expression in the statement of the lemma.

We can evaluate
f(0)=0
, and we use the convention that
f(1)=\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1}f(x)=0
. For
x\in[0,1)
, we have

f^{\prime}(x)=\log(1-x)-\log(2-x)+\log 4\quad\text{ and }\quad f^{\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{1}{2-x}-\frac{1}{1-x}.

It is easily checked that
f^{\prime}(2/3)=0
. Since
f^{\prime\prime}(x)<0
for
x\in[0,1)
,
f
is concave on
[0,1)
and hence
f
must be strictly increasing on
[0,2/3]
and strictly decreasing on
[2/3,1]
. Moreover, by the concavity of
f
, for
x\in[0,1/3]
we have that
f(x)\geq\frac{f(1/3)}{1/3}\cdot x\geq\frac{x}{2}
.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

We have

\sum_{s=1}^{0.99n}\sum_{g=2}^{\min\left\{s+1,\frac{n-s}{2}\right\}}c_{s,g}=o(1)

and, for any
k=k(n)\to\infty
,

\sum_{s=k}^{0.99n}c_{s,1}=o(1).

Proof.

Note that for
s\leq 0.99n
and
g\leq\min\left\{s+1,\frac{n-s}{2}\right\}
, we have

s+g\leq s+\min\left\{s+1,\frac{n-s}{2}\right\}=\min\left\{2s+1,\frac{n+s}{2}\right\}\leq\frac{1.99n}{2},

and so
n-s-g\geq 0.005n=\frac{n}{200}
. Using Lemmas
A.2
and
A.3
, we may write for any
1\leq s\leq 0.99n
:

\displaystyle\sum_{g=2}^{\min\left\{s+1,\frac{n-s}{2}\right\}}c_{s,g}=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(e^{-nf(s/n)})\sum_{g=2}^{\min\left\{s+1,\frac{n-s}{2}\right\}}\binom{s+1}{g}\frac{1}{(g-1)!}\left(\frac{400}{n}\right)^{g-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(se^{-nf(s/n)})\sum_{g=2}^{\min\left\{s+1,\frac{n-s}{2}\right\}}\binom{s}{g-1}\frac{1}{(g-1)!}\left(\frac{400}{n}\right)^{g-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(se^{-nf(s/n)})\sum_{g=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(g-1)!^{2}}\left(\frac{400s}{n}\right)^{g-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{s^{2}}{n}e^{-nf(s/n)}\right)\sum_{g=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(g-1)!^{2}}\left(\frac{400s}{n}\right)^{g-2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{s^{2}}{n}e^{-nf(s/n)}\right)\sum_{g=2}^{\infty}\frac{400^{g-2}}{(g-1)!^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{s^{2}}{n}e^{-nf(s/n)}\right).

Thus, to show the first statement of the Lemma, it suffices to show that the sum
\sum_{s=1}^{0.99n}\frac{s^{2}}{n}e^{-nf(s/n)}
is
o(1)
. For
1\leq s\leq\frac{n}{3}
, by
Lemma

A.3
we have that
f(s/n)\geq s/2n
and so we may write

\sum_{s=1}^{n/3}\frac{s^{2}}{n}e^{-nf(s/n)}\leq\sum_{s=1}^{n/3}\frac{s^{2}}{n}e^{-s/2}=\mathcal{O}(1/n)=o(1).

For
\frac{n}{3}\leq s\leq 0.99n
, we have that
f(s/n)\geq\min\{f(1/3),f(0.99)\}=f(0.99)=0.04223...\geq 0.04
, and hence

\sum_{s=n/3}^{0.99n}\frac{s^{2}}{n}e^{-nf(s/n)}=\mathcal{O}(n^{2}e^{-0.04n})=o(1).

This proves the first statement in the Lemma. For the second, again using Lemmas
A.2
and
A.3
we can write

c_{s,1}\leq\mathcal{O}\left(se^{-nf(s/n)}\right)

for
1\leq s\leq 0.99n.
Note that, similar to above, we have

\sum_{s=n/3}^{0.99n}se^{-nf(s/n)}\leq\mathcal{O}(n^{2}e^{-0.04n})=o(1).

So the second statement holds for any
k=k(n)
in
[n/3,0,99n].
Suppose
\omega(1)=k\leq n/3
. Then, we have
\sum_{s=k}^{n/3}se^{-nf(s/n)}\leq\sum_{s=k}^{n/3}se^{-s/2}
. Note that the ratio of successive terms in the sum is
(s+1)e^{-(s+1)/2}/se^{-s/2}=\frac{s+1}{s}e^{-1/2}=(1+o(1))e^{-1/2}<0.61
, since
s\geq k\gg 1
. Thus,

\sum_{s=k}^{\infty}se^{-s/2}\leq ke^{-k/2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(0.61)^{\ell}=\mathcal{O}\left(ke^{-k/2}\right)=o(1),

which completes the proof of the second statement.
∎

Lemma A.5
.

We have

\sum_{s=0.99n}^{n-2}\sum_{g=1}^{\min\left\{s+1,\frac{n-s}{2}\right\}}c_{s,g}=o(1).

Proof.

Clearly we have
\min\left\{s+1,\frac{n-s}{2}\right\}=\frac{n-s}{2}
for
0.99n\leq s\leq n-2
. We write

\frac{\binom{2(n-s)}{n-s}}{\binom{2n-s}{n}}\leq\frac{4^{n-s}}{(n+n-s)_{(n-s)}/(n-s)!}\leq\left(\frac{4}{n}\right)^{n-s}(n-s)!,

where
(n)_{k}=n\cdot(n-1)\cdots(n-k+1)\geq(n-k)^{k}
denotes the falling factorial.
For
s\leq n-2
and
g\leq\frac{n-s}{2}
, we have
n-s-g\geq 1
. Thus by
Lemma

A.2
and the above inequality,

\displaystyle\sum_{g=1}^{\frac{n-s}{2}}c_{s,g}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{4}{n}\right)^{n-s}(n-s)!\sum_{g=1}^{\frac{n-s}{2}}\binom{s+1}{g}\frac{2^{g-1}}{(g-1)!}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{4}{n}\right)^{n-s}(n-s)!\binom{n}{\frac{n-s}{2}}\sum_{g=1}^{\infty}\frac{2^{g-1}}{(g-1)!}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{2}\left(\frac{4}{n}\right)^{n-s}(n-s)!\frac{n^{\frac{n-s}{2}}}{\left(\frac{n-s}{2}\right)!}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle e^{2}\left(\frac{16(n-s)}{n}\right)^{\frac{n-s}{2}}.

Then we have

\sum_{s=0.99n}^{n-2}c_{s,g}\leq e^{2}\sum_{s=0.99n}^{n-2}\left(\frac{16(n-s)}{n}\right)^{\frac{n-s}{2}}=e^{2}\sum_{\ell=2}^{0.01n}\left(\frac{16\ell}{n}\right)^{\ell/2}=o(1),

and the proof of the lemma is finished.
∎

Lemma A.6
.

For any
k=k(n)\to\infty
,

\sum_{s=0}^{k}c_{s,1}=4+o(1).

Proof.

We remark that
c_{s,1}
is given by

c_{s,1}=(s+1)\frac{n!}{(2n-s)!}\binom{2(n-s)}{n-s}w_{n-s}.

(15)

by
Lemma

A.1
. It will suffice to prove the result for
k=o(\sqrt{n})
. Indeed, given this, for arbitrary
k\to\infty
we may choose
h\leq k
with
h=o(\sqrt{n})
; then

\sum_{s=0}^{k}c_{s,1}=\sum_{s=0}^{h}c_{s,1}+\sum_{s=h+1}^{k}c_{s,1}=4+o(1),

where we use
Lemma

A.4
to conclude that
\sum_{s=h+1}^{k}c_{s,1}=o(1).

Now, let
k=o(\sqrt{n})
. We claim that
w_{n-s}=(1+o(1))(n-s)!
, uniformly over
1\leq s\leq k.
To see this, first observe that
w_{n-s}
is lower bounded by the number of paths of length
n-s
from
\emptyset
to
[n-s]
which are
vertex disjoint
from
\pi_{n-s}
on the interior of the
n-s
-dimensional hypercube, i.e., vertex disjoint except at
\emptyset
and
[n-s]
. Suppose we sample a path of length
n-s
from
\emptyset
to
[n-s]
uniformly at random. The probability that the path goes through any particular vertex on level
h
of the hypercube is
\binom{n-s}{h}^{-1}
. Hence, by a union bound, the probability that the path intersects with
\pi_{n-s}
on the interior of a
n-s
-dimensional hypercube is at most
\sum_{h=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-s}{h}^{-1}=\mathcal{O}((n-s)^{-1})=\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}).
It follows that there are
(1-\mathcal{O}(n^{-1}))(n-s)!
paths which are vertex disjoint from
\pi_{n-s}
on the interior, so we have
(1+o(1))(n-s)!\leq w_{n-s}
. Trivially,
w_{n-s}\leq(n-s)!
, and this establishes the claim.

We plug
w_{n-s}=(1+o(1))(n-s)!
into (
15
) to reach

c_{s,1}=(1+o(1))(s+1)\frac{n!}{(n-s)!}\cdot\frac{(2(n-s))!}{(2n-s)!}

for
1\leq s\leq k
. Since
k=o(\sqrt{n})
, we have
\frac{n!}{(n-s)!}=(1+o(1))n^{s}
and
\frac{(2(n-s))!}{(2n-s)!}=(1+o(1))\frac{1}{(2n)^{s}}.
Thus in total we get
c_{s,1}=(1+o(1))\frac{s+1}{2^{s}}
uniformly over
1\leq s\leq k
, so that finally

\sum_{s=0}^{k}c_{s,1}=(1+o(1))\sum_{s=0}^{k}\frac{s+1}{2^{s}}=4+o(1),

and the proof of the lemma is finished.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

3.3
.

We bound
M_{k,r}(\varepsilon)
in order to derive an admissible sequence
n_{k,r}
.
First we bound

\displaystyle M_{k,r}(\varepsilon)\leq\max_{\ell\in[k],\bm{i}\in[r]^{\ell}}\left\{(\ell r+Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i}^{\prime}))\right\}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\max_{\ell\in[k],\bm{i}\in[r]^{\ell}}\Delta(\bm{i})\right).

The parameter of the Poisson random variable
Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i})
is
|P(\bm{i})|\varepsilon
plus a sum of exponential random variables. Recall that a mixed Poisson distribution with exponential mixture follows the geometric distribution, while a sum of geometric distributions follows a negative binomial distribution. This allows us to decompose
Y_{\varepsilon}(\bm{i})
into
Y(\bm{i})\sim\text{Poi}(|P(\bm{i})|\varepsilon)
and
B(\bm{i})\sim\text{NB}(|P(\bm{i})|,\frac{1}{2})
, where NB denotes the negative binomial distribution; the number of failed trials until the
|P(\bm{i})|
-th success.
This leads to

\displaystyle M_{k,r}(\varepsilon)\leq\max_{\ell\in[k],\bm{i}\in[r]^{\ell}}\left\{(\ell r+Y(\bm{i}^{\prime})+B(\bm{i}^{\prime}))\right\}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\max_{\ell\in[k],\bm{i}\in[r]^{\ell}}\Delta(\bm{i})\right).

We will pick

n_{k,r}=(kr+y_{k}+b_{k})(1+\varepsilon^{-1}d_{k}),

where
y_{k},b_{k},d_{k}
are chosen in order to guarantee

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\ell\in[k],\bm{i}\in[r]^{\ell}}Y(\bm{i}^{\prime})>y_{k}\right),\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\ell\in[k],\bm{i}\in[r]^{\ell}}B(\bm{i}^{\prime})>b_{k}\right),\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{\ell\in[k],\bm{i}\in[r]^{\ell}}\Delta(\bm{i})>d_{k}\right)\leq\frac{1}{3k^{2}},

so that

\mathbb{P}(M_{k,r}(\varepsilon)>n_{k,r})\leq\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

We will now derive the desired asymptotics of
y_{k},b_{k},d_{k}
.
Firstly note that

\left|\bigcup_{\ell\in[k]}[r]^{\ell}\right|=\frac{r^{k+1}-1}{r-1}\sim r^{k}.

For
y_{k}
, we use the fact that
Y(\bm{i}^{\prime})
is stochastically dominated by
\text{Poi}(rk\varepsilon)
and use the Chernoff inequality to bound

\mathbb{P}(Y(\bm{i}^{\prime})>y_{k})\leq\left(\frac{erk\varepsilon}{y_{k}}\right)^{y_{k}}e^{-rk\varepsilon}.

Then, by the union bound, it suffices to have

\frac{r^{k+1}-1}{r-1}\left(\frac{erk\varepsilon}{y_{k}}\right)^{y_{k}}e^{-rk\varepsilon}\leq\frac{1}{3k^{2}}.

We pick
y_{k}
to satisfy this constraint with equality. We take the log to determine the asymptotics of
y_{k}
.

k\log r+o(1)+y_{k}\left(1+\log r+\log k-\log y_{k}\right)-rk\varepsilon=-\log 3-2\log k.

The main asymptotics are determined by

k\log r\sim y_{k}\left(\log y_{k}-\log k\right)\Rightarrow y_{k}\sim k\frac{\log r}{\log\log r}.

For
b_{k}
, we make use of the fact that each of the
B(\bm{i}^{\prime})
is stochastically dominated by
\text{NB}(rk,\frac{1}{2})
. In addition, the event that the
rk
-th failure occurs before the
a_{k}
-th trial is equivalent to the event that the first
a_{k}
trials contain less than
rk
successes. This allows us to use Hoeffding’s inequality to bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(B(\bm{i}^{\prime})+rk>a_{k})\leq\mathbb{P}(\text{Bin}(a_{k},\tfrac{1}{2})<rk)\leq e^{-2a_{k}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{rk}{a_{k}}\right)^{2}}.

Again, by the union bound it suffices to have

\frac{r^{k+1}-1}{r-1}e^{-2a_{k}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{rk}{a_{k}}\right)^{2}}=\frac{1}{3k^{2}}.

We pick
a_{k}
to satisfy this constraint with equality and set
b_{k}=a_{k}-rk
. We inspect the asymptotics of
a_{k}
:

k\log r+o(1)-2a_{k}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{rk}{a_{k}}\right)^{2}=\mathcal{O}(1)-2\log k.

We rewrite this to

\frac{a_{k}}{2}+\frac{2r^{2}k^{2}}{a_{k}}=k\log r+2\log k+2rk+\mathcal{O}(1)\sim 2rk,

which leads to
a_{k}\sim 2rk
, so that
b_{k}\sim rk
.

Finally, we choose
r_{k}
so that

\frac{r^{k+1}-1}{r-1}e^{-d_{k}}=\frac{1}{3k^{2}},

which has asymptotics
d_{k}\sim k\log r
.
Putting everything together, we obtained

n_{k,r}=(kr+y_{k}+b_{k})(1+\varepsilon^{-1}d_{k})\sim 2kr\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}k\log r=2k^{4}r^{2}\log r,

for
\varepsilon=k^{-2}r^{-1}
.
∎

\varepsilon_{1}=Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\prime}-r_{k}^{2k}\mathbb{E}_{k}[{\mathbf{1}}_{[U\subset U^{\prime},w(U)+w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\leq 1]}\cdot\left(1-w(U)\right)^{n}\left(1-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\right)^{n}]\geq 0.

Secondly, we use

(1-w(U))(1-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))=1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})+w(U)w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\geq 1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})

and define

\varepsilon_{2}=r_{k}^{2k}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[{\mathbf{1}}_{[U\subset U^{\prime},w(U)+w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\leq 1]}\cdot\left(\left(1-w(U)\right)^{n}\left(1-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\right)^{n}-\left(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\right)^{n}\right)\right]\geq 0.

Finally, we consider the change in exponent

\varepsilon_{3}=r_{k}^{2k}\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[{\mathbf{1}}_{[U\subset U^{\prime},w(U)+w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\leq 1]}\left(\left(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\right)^{n-2k}-\left(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\right)^{n}\right)\right]\geq 0,

so that

\lambda_{k,n}-Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\prime}=\varepsilon_{3}-\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}.

Since each of these errors is nonnegative, we have

|\lambda_{k,n}-Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\prime}|\leq\varepsilon_{1}+\varepsilon_{2}+\varepsilon_{3},

so that it suffices to prove that each of these errors vanish in expectation.

Vanishing
\varepsilon_{1}
.

Note that
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{1}]\leq\mathbb{E}[Z_{k}Z_{k}^{\prime}]\leq 1
, so that by the dominated convergence theorem, we only need to show that
\mathbb{P}(U\not\subset U^{\prime}\text{ or }w(U)+w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})>1)\to 0
.
By the independence of
U
and
U^{\prime}
, we have

\mathbb{P}(U\subset U^{\prime})=\frac{{n-k\choose k}}{{n\choose k}}\sim\frac{(n-k)^{k}/k!}{n^{k}/k!}=\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)^{k}\to 1

for
k\ll\sqrt{n}
.
Furthermore, by
Lemma

3.2
, we have

-n\log(1-w(u))\leq\frac{1}{k}+\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i}(u))}\Delta(\bm{j}),

for all
u\in V_{k}^{*}
with probability at least
1-k^{-2}
, where
\bm{i}(u)
is the index corresponding to
u\in V_{k}^{*}
. Together with
-n\log(1-w(u))\geq nw(u)
and the Markov inequality, we obtain the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(w(U)>\tfrac{1}{2})

\displaystyle\leq k^{-2}+\mathbb{P}\left(\frac{1}{k}+\sum_{\bm{j}\in P(\bm{i}(U))}\Delta(\bm{j})>\frac{n}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq k^{-2}+\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{k}+|P(\bm{i}(U))|\right]

\displaystyle\leq k^{-2}+\frac{2}{kn}+\frac{2kr}{n},

which vanishes for
1\ll k\ll\sqrt{n}
.
We conclude

\mathbb{P}(U\not\subset U^{\prime}\text{ or }w(U)+w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})>1)\leq\mathbb{P}(U\not\subset U^{\prime})+\mathbb{P}(w(U)>\tfrac{1}{2})+\mathbb{P}(w(U^{\prime})>\tfrac{1}{2})\to 0,

so that
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{1}]\to 0
.

Vanishing
\varepsilon_{2}
.

We rewrite

\displaystyle(1-w(U))^{n}(1-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n}-(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-w(U))^{n}(1-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n}\left(1-\left(1-\frac{w(U)}{1-w(U)}\frac{w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})}{1-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})}\right)^{n}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}\left(nw(U)(1-w(U))^{n-1}w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})(1-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n-1}\right).

Now, note that
w(U)(1-w(U))^{n-1}
is maximized at
w(U)=1/n
, with value
\tfrac{1}{n}(1-\tfrac{1}{n})^{n-1}\leq\tfrac{1}{n}
, so that the above is
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1})
.
This tells us that

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{2}]=\mathcal{O}(r_{k}^{2k}/n),

which vanishes for
k\ll\frac{\log n}{\log\log n}
.

Vanishing
\varepsilon_{3}
.

We write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{3}]

\displaystyle=r_{k}^{2k}\mathbb{E}\left[{\mathbf{1}}_{[U\subset U^{\prime},w(U)+w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\leq 1]}\left(\left(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\right)^{n-2k}-\left(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\right)^{n}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq r_{k}^{2k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\right)^{n-2k}-\left(\left(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime})\right)^{n-2k}\right)^{\frac{n}{n-2k}}\right)\right].

Using concavity of
y\mapsto-y^{\frac{n}{n-2k}}
, Jensen’s inequality yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{3}]

\displaystyle\leq r_{k}^{2k}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n-2k}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n-2k}\right]^{\frac{n}{n-2k}}\right)

\displaystyle=r_{k}^{2k}\mathbb{E}\left[(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n-2k}\right]\left(1-\mathbb{E}\left[(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n-2k}\right]^{\frac{2k}{n-2k}}\right).

Next, using
1-e^{-x}\leq x
for
x=-\frac{2k}{n-2k}\log\mathbb{E}_{k}\left[(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n-2k}\right]\geq 0
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{3}]

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{2kr_{k}^{2k}}{n-2k}\mathbb{E}\left[(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n-2k}\right]\log\mathbb{E}\left[(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n-2k}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{4k^{2}\log r_{k}}{n-2k}-\frac{2k}{n-2k}q\log q,

for
q=r_{k}^{2k}\mathbb{E}\left[(1-w(U)-w^{\prime}(U^{\prime}))^{n-2k}\right]
.
The nonnegativity of
\varepsilon_{3}
implies
q\log q\leq 2k\log k
, so that we only need the first term to vanish, which occurs whenever
k^{2}\log r_{k}\ll n
,
and the proof of the lemma is finished.
∎

Appendix C
The limiting distribution

Lemma C.1
.

The limiting distribution is given by

\mathbb{P}(X=x)=\sum_{k=0}^{x}{x\choose k}\frac{\delta-\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{r}r!}{k!},

where
\delta=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-z}}{1+z}\,dz
is the
Gompertz
constant.

Proof.

We will prove this theorem by rewriting

\mathbb{P}(X=x)=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-ZZ^{\prime}}\frac{(ZZ^{\prime})^{x}}{x!}\right]

to the desired formula. We first consider the conditional expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-ZZ^{\prime}}\frac{(ZZ^{\prime})^{x}}{x!}\ \middle|\ Z=z\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[e^{-zZ^{\prime}}\frac{(zZ^{\prime})^{x}}{x!}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{x}}{x!}\int_{0}^{\infty}(z^{\prime})^{x}e^{-zz^{\prime}-z^{\prime}}dz^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{x}}{(z+1)^{x+1}},

where the last step follows by partial integration and solving the resulting recurrence.
Taking the expectation over
Z
, we obtained,

\mathbb{P}(X=x)=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{Z^{x}}{(1+Z)^{x+1}}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{Z+1}\left(1-\frac{1}{Z+1}\right)^{x}\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{x}{x\choose k}(-1)^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[(Z+1)^{-k-1}\right].

(16)

We thus need to compute negative moments

m_{k}=\mathbb{E}\left[(Z+1)^{-k}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-z}dz}{(1+z)^{k}},

for
k\geq 1.
Note that
m_{1}=\delta
is Gompertz constant.
We apply integration by parts to obtain the recursion

m_{k+1}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-z}dz}{(1+z)^{k+1}}=\left[-\frac{1}{k}\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+z)^{r}}\right]_{z=0}^{\infty}-\frac{1}{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{e^{-z}dz}{(1+z)^{k}}=\frac{1-m_{k}}{k}.

The above can be rewritten to
k!m_{k+1}=(k-1)!-(k-1)!m_{k}
. Repeating this relation leads to

k!m_{k+1}=\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{r}(k-1-r)!+(-1)^{k}\delta,

so that

m_{k+1}=\frac{(-1)^{k}\delta+\sum_{r=0}^{k-1}(-1)^{k-1-r}r!}{k!}=(-1)^{k}\frac{\delta-\sum_{r=0}^{k-1-r}(-1)^{r}r!}{k!}

Plugging this into (
16
) yields the desired expression.
∎
