A mediation model on how PTSD symptoms are related to psychotic experiences in survivors of the ongoing Gaza war 2023–2024
Authors: Feten Fekih-Romdhane, Tatiana Nemr, Kamel Jebreen, Tasnim Swaitti, Mohammed Jebreen, Eqbal Radwan, Wafa Kammoun-Rebai, Inad Nawajah, Omar Shamsti, Sahar Obeid, Souheil Hallit
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundResearch allowing the recognition of significant associations between post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms and psychotic experiences (PEs), and the identification of potential modifiable factors affecting their trajectories of co-occurrence is paramount for improving mental health of mental health of war victims. However, there remains a dearth of evidence regarding trauma, PTSD and psychosis in regions exposed to prolonged conflict and oppression. Our study aimed to: (1) investigate the direct bidirectional connections between PTSD symptoms and PEs in a sample of adults living in Gaza amidst the ongoing 2023–2024 war, and (2) test the roles of depression and anxiety symptoms as mediators in these connections.MethodsThe study was performed among adult civilians in Gaza during the month of September 2024. At the time of assessment, the study population has been enduring more than 11 months of mass violence, bombing, massacres and starvation.ResultsAfter adjusting for confounding variables, results indicated that depression and anxiety symptoms fully mediated the bidirectional relationship between PTSD symptoms and PEs. Specifically, Gazan participants reporting higher levels of PTSD symptoms also reported higher levels of anxiety/depression, which in turn was related to more severe PEs; and those who had greater PEs also displayed greater anxiety/depression, which in turn was related to higher levels of PTSD symptoms. It is noteworthy that PTSD was not directly associated with PEs in all models.ConclusionThe relationship between PTSD and PEs in extremely traumatizing environments seem to be complex. Our results highlight the crucial need for screening and early detection of one condition when the other is present, specifically in war-torn populations. The full mediation effect found may indicate the importance of depression/anxiety symptoms in reducing PEs in war-affected individuals with PTSD, and in alleviating PTSD symptoms in those experiencing PEs as well.Keywords: PTSD, Psychotic experiences, Depression, Anxiety, War, Gaza

Introduction

A compelling body of literature indicated that post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms and psychotic phenomena are intimately connected in both non-clinical and clinical populations [9, 19] in a dose-response fashion, such that a higher number of PTSD symptoms is associated with higher odds of experiencing psychotic experiences (PEs; such as paranoia, strange experiences, and hallucinations) [2]. On the one hand, previous research found that 38% of patients with an at-risk mental state for developing psychosis or a psychotic disorder met DSM‐5 criteria for PTSD diagnosis (for > 95%, the PTSD diagnosis was not detected by their clinical teams and not recorded in their notes), and 37% met criteria for sub‐threshold PTSD [36]. A systematic review showed that an estimated 31% of patients with psychotic disorders screened positive for PTSD, among them 28% were clinically undetected (i.e. PTSD non documented in the clinical records) [65]. On the other hand, trauma-exposed individuals who develop PTSD were found to have an increased risk of subsequently developing delusional experiences (Relative Risk of 9.24) [51] and all positive psychotic experiences (odds ratios 1.83) [49] than those who do not develop PTSD.

In the particular context of war and conflicts, a study by Soosay et al. [53] reported that PTSD was associated with psychotic symptoms in a population severely affected by conflict and subsequent humanitarian crisis in post-war Timor Leste. The co-occurence PTSD and psychotic symptoms has also been observed in victims of the Pol Pot regime in Cambodia [29], in Vietnam combat veterans [22], and in trauma-affected refugees in Denmark [42]. The World Health Organization World Mental Health (WMH) Surveys including a large multi-national sample of general population adults showed that lifetime PEs were associated with up to 2-fold increased risk of subsequent onset of PTSD, and that PTSD was a significant was a significant predictor of the development of later PEs, with an odds ratio of 1.8 [37]. Undiagnosed and untreated PTSD in people with psychosis may exacerbate positive symptoms of psychosis, prevent access to adequate treatment, and lead to additional mental health complications [41]. Altogether, these observations raise the question of what variables might be modulating the connection between psychosis and PTSD. Although there is strong conclusive evidence to support reciprocal influences between PTSD and psychotic symptoms, only very limited research has previously been undertaken on the presence of third variables that might account for the relationship between the two types of psychopathologies. This study focuses on depression and anxiety as possible mediators in the bidirectional pathways linking PTSD to PEs (see conceptual framework in Fig. 1).

Pathways between depressive/anxiety symptoms and PEs

There is evidence to indicate that PEs can trigger anxiety and depression symptoms, and conversely, anxiety/depression can lead to the emergence or exacerbation of PEs [23, 26, 44, 55, 57]. From one perspective, research reported that preceding anxiety and depression may provoke the onset or worsening of PEs (e.g [59, 62]. A longitudinal cohort study in a large Dutch general population sample found that preceding depressive and anxiety symptoms were directly associated with later PEs incidence across measurement waves, thus endorsing the presence of an ‘affective pathway’ to psychosis [45]. Other longitudinal studies provided similar results demonstrating that anxiety and/or depression symptoms assessed at baseline prospectively predicted the development of PEs over time [16, 20, 39, 63]. In this regard, it has been suggested that anxiety may generate an anticipation of threat and depression may fuel a sense of vulnerability, thus contributing to increased risk for psychotic symptoms [16]. From another perspective, there is strong longitudinal evidence that individuals who endorse PEs have a greater likelihood of developing either single or multi-morbid mental conditions, with the most prevalent diagnosed disorders being major depression and anxiety disorders [8]. Some research findings have also reported reciprocal longitudinal relationships between depression/anxiety and PEs that go in both directions [37]. For instance, a prospective longitudinal study conducted among Tunisian high-school students aged 13–18 years reported a bidirectional relationship between psychological distress (i.e. depression, anxiety, stress) and PEs moderated by sex; boys initially presenting with psychological distress were an increased risk of experiencing new onset PEs at 12-month follow-up, whereas girls who firstly endorsed PEs had a higher risk of developing psychological distress over time [13].

Pathways between depressive/anxiety symptoms and PTSD

Several sources of empirical evidence pointed to bidirectional patterns of relations over time between depression/anxiety and PTSD. For instance, Cheng et al. [10] found that preceding PTSD symptoms predicted later depressive symptoms, but not vice versa, among children after a natural disaster. A US study revealed that PTSD symptom severity in trauma victims at baseline were prospectively and positively associated with depression and state anxiety [48]. In the other direction of association, Ying et al. [64] observed that depression was a significant predictor of PTSD during a 24-month follow-up period among children after the Wenchuan earthquake. In terms of bidirectionality, a prospective longitudinal study among 321 family surrogates of intensive care unit decedents found that depressive symptoms predicted PTSD symptoms in the first-year post-loss, anxiety predicted PTSD symptoms within 13- and 24-month after bereavement, whereas PTSD symptoms predicted both depressive and anxiety symptoms in the second bereavement year [61].

Rationale of this study

Palestinians have been victims of occupation, violence and gross violations of human rights for decades. Since October 7, 2023, war dramatically escalated in Gaza, turning to the deadliest Israeli assault ever seen since 1948. Since then, it was estimated that more than 186 000 Gazans were killed in the Strip by July 2024 [28]. This study proposes to shed light on the paths between PTSD and psychosis in Palestinian civilians in Gaza who are currently facing mass killing, daily horrors and massacres. This choice was motivated by several reasons. First, despite a sizeable amount of research literature has been undertaken on the relationship between childhood trauma and the development of psychotic symptoms, research examining the impact of current trauma, such as exposure to war atrocities, is still under-researched and poorly understood [58]. Moreover, even the few existing studies rather focused on Western countries, while there remains a dearth of evidence regarding trauma, PTSD and psychosis in regions exposed to prolonged conflict and oppression, such as Middle East and North Africa (MENA). There is some empirical evidence suggesting that war-affected people from non-Western countries present higher rates of PTSD relative those from western countries such as the US (10–30% versus 7%) [4, 27, 42], and that psychotic symptoms co-morbid with PTSD are higher in people from war-torn nations compared to those from Western regions [32, 53]. In addition, beliefs in supernatural phenomena such as jinn, land-dwelling demon and and evil spirits, is normative in the region, in particular in Palestine [1, 15]. Furthermore, earlier research has separately studied the effects of PTSD and depression/anxiety on psychotic symptoms, but none of these have considered all variables together in a war-affected population to our knowledge. Such an approach can provide new insights into mechanisms underpinning the complex and intricate relationship between PTSD and psychosis in the context of exposure to severe war-related trauma, and the way how to early treat or prevent the onset of clinical psychosis in war-affected populations [25, 30]. Our study builds upon gaps in the previous literature by: (1) investigating the direct bidirectional connections between PTSD symptoms and PEs in a sample of adults living in Gaza amidst the ongoing war, and (2) testing the roles of depression and anxiety symptoms as mediators in these connections.

Methods

Participants and procedure

The study was performed among adult civilians in Gaza during the month of September 2024. At the time of assessment, the study population has been enduring more than 11 months of mass violence, bombing, massacres and starvation. Inclusion criteria consisted of being aged 18 years and over, living in the Gaza strip and expressing their willingness to participate. Ethical clearance to conduct this research was obtained from the scientific and ethics committee of Deanship of scientific research, Palestine Technical university – Kadoorie, Palestine. Potential eligible participants were electronically invited via an online survey platform (Google Forms), and was promoted through social media networks (e.g. Facebook, Instagram, Telegram, LinkedIn, Viber, Twitter). Based on the snowball sampling method, each participant was encouraged to invite new participants from their personal networks and contacts. Following ethical considerations, the first section of the questionnaire contained a detailed explanation of the study objectives. At the end of this section, participants were asked to provide their written informed consent to take part in the study by responding to the question “Do you agree to participate in this study”. A “yes” response gave the respondent access to the next sections of the survey, whereas a “no” response allowed them to leave the survey and not to take part in the study. The data collected were anonymized, and confidentiality was ensured throughout the study process. Twelve individuals (0.4%) accessed the survey link, but clicked ‘no’ for the consent question at the beginning of the questionnaire, thus indicating unwillingness to participate and withdrawing directly from the survey. At last, a total of 534 valid responses were received and analyzed.

Measurements

The survey included information related to participants’ socio-demographic characteristics (e.g. sex, age, marital status, educational level, and place of residency). PTSD symptoms were assessed using the Impact of Event Scale-6 (IES-6), PEs were assessed using the Prodromal Questionnaire- Brief (PQ-B), depression symptoms were assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), and anxiety symptoms were assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7).

The IES-6 [56]

The Arabic validated version of the IES-6 was used in the present study [24]. The scale represents the short form of the Impact of Event Scale – Revised [60]. The IES-6 measures the severity of PTSD symptoms via 6 items and 3 components: avoidance (2 items), intrusion (2 items), and hyper-arousal (2 items). Items are rated on a five-point scale that ranges from 0 (not at all), to 1 (a little bit), 2 (moderately), 3 (quite a bit) and 4 (extremely). More elevated scores indicate higher levels of PTSD responses. In this sample, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.83.

The PQ-B [33, 34]

The Arabic validated version of the PQ-B was adopted [14]. The questionnaire is composed of 21 items assessing the severity of attenuated psychotic symptoms (delusions and hallucinations) in general population adults. All items have a yes/no format of response. Sample items are “Have you heard unusual sounds like banging, clicking, hissing, clapping or ringing in your ears?” and “Do familiar surroundings sometimes seem strange, confusing, threatening or unreal to you?”. In our sample, Cronbach’s alpha was of 0.98.

The GAD-7 [54]

The Arabic validated version was used [12, 50]. The GAD-7 is a self-report tool that contains 7 items. It consists of asking respondents whether they have experienced, over the past 2 weeks, one of the generalized anxiety symptoms included in the questionnaire (e.g., “Worrying too much about different things” or “Not being able to stop or control worrying”). Items are rated on a four-point scale that ranges from 0 (Not at all), 1 (Several days), 2 (More than half the days), to 3 (Nearly every day). Greater scores reflect higher levels of anxiety. Cronbach’s alpha in this study was 0.94.

The PHQ-9 [31]

The Arabic validated version of the PHQ-9 was used [11, 50]. The PHQ-9 contains 9 items intended to assess levels of depression symptoms experienced over the past two weeks (e.g., “Thoughts that you would be better off dead, or of hurting yourself” or “Feeling bad about yourself or that you are a failure or have let yourself or your family down”). Items are rated on a four-point scale that ranges from 0 (Not at all), to 1 (Several days), 2 (More than half the days), and 3 (Nearly every day). Higher total scores refer to more severe depressive symptoms. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was 0.93.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS v.27 software was used for the statistical analysis. The PQB and PTSD scores were considered normally distributed since the skewness and kurtosis values varied between − 1 and + 1. Student’s t test was used to compare a continuous variable and a dichotomous variable, unidirectional ANOVA to compare a continuous variable and a categorical variable, and Pearson’s test to correlate two continuous variables. The mediation analysis was performed using PROCESS MACRO (a SPSS add-on) v3.4 Model 4, with the number of bootstrap samples set at 5000 and a 95% confidence interval. Four routes result from this analysis: pathway A of the independent variable to the mediator, pathway B of the mediator to the dependent variable, pathways C and C’ indicating the total and direct effects of the independent variable to the dependent variable. Covariates entered in the model were those that showed a p < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis. We considered the mediation analysis to be significant if the confidence interval did not pass by zero. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Research allowing the recognition of significant associations between PTSD symptoms and PEs and the identification of potential modifiable factors affecting their trajectories of co-occurrence is paramount for improving mental health of mental health of war victims. One potential mechanism connecting PTSD to psychosis that this study aimed to investigate is depression and anxiety symptoms. Findings supported a full mediation effect of both depression and anxiety between PTSD symptoms and PEs, in both directions. These findings offer some clues to explain the processes through which the relationship between PTSD and PEs operates in the context of current war.

Our results align with those of several previous studies underscoring a positive association between the presence or intensity of positive psychotic symptoms and PTSD [5, 35], and establishing a bidirectional causality between both pathologies [17]. The two-directional associations between PTSD and psychotic symptoms observed in our study can be looked at and interpreted through the theoretical frameworks of unidirectional theory and theory of common risk factors [17]. Following the unidirectional theory, one of the two conditions could arise as a vulnerability variable for the other. In support to this approach, there exists empirical research indicating that individuals with PTSD have at least twice increased risk for the development of psychotic symptoms compared to the general population [7, 19, 21, 49, 51]. As such, a model of continuity between psychosis and PTSD has been hypothesized in a manner that the psychotic pathology would be an aggravating (or worsening) factor for a former PTSD [3, 7]. Trauma-specialist clinicians stipulated that exposure to high-level, prolonged human rights violations may result in phenomena that are not encompassed by PTSD criteria among affected people, such as an attenuated sense of security, loss of trust and suspiciousness of others [52]. In some individuals, such trauma reactions can be adaptive, whereas in others suspicion and fear can magnify or create a vulnerability to paranoia and other psychotic manifestations [53]. The presence of psychotic phenomena in survivors of mass conflict and human rights abuses lends more support to the theory and evidence that more severe and complex forms of traumatic stress occur and should be recognized in survivors of repeated long-term trauma [38].

Within the theory of common risk factors, which aligns more with our findings, interactions between PTSD and psychosis would be explained by certain factors that increase the risk of developing one or the other disorder independently. Among the plausible factors involved in the etiopathogenic mechanisms of the link between PTSD and psychosis are depression and anxiety. This study’s results indicated that depression and anxiety symptoms fully mediated the bidirectional relationship between PTSD symptoms and PEs. Specifically, Gazans reporting high levels of PTSD symptoms also reported higher levels of anxiety/depression, which in turn was related to more severe PEs; and those who had greater PEs also displayed greater anxiety/depression, which in turn was related to higher levels of PTSD symptoms. Broadly in line with our results, a systematic review showed that 14 to 47% people with psychosis present with PTSD symptoms, and that depression was consistently linked to psychosis in those with PTSD [6]. Similarly, another systematic review and meta-analysis highlighted high prevalence estimates of clinically relevant PTSD symptoms (42%) and PTSD diagnosis (30%) in people with first-episode psychosis, with depression and anxiety being implicated as potential risk factors for co-occurrence [47]. However, in both reviews a very low number of studies could be identified, reflecting the scarcity of literature on this topic.

Study limitations

Although our study adds knowledge on the topic, some limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on self-report measures, which could have contributed to a lack of precision in responses. Thus, there is a need for comprehensive assessments using structured interviews in future studies to enhance the current findings. Second, the cross-sectional nature of our data precludes from making conclusions on causality. Future longitudinal studies with multiple measurement points are warranted to evaluate the interplay between PTSD, depression, anxiety and PEs to clarify the direction of the relations between these constructs. Third, data were gathered using snowball sampling, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to the wider war-affected population.

Practical implications

Our findings contribute to the literature in the field, and may have important clinical implications for prevention or management/treatment of PTSD and psychosis in the context of war. The relationship between PTSD and psychosis in such extremely traumatizing environments seem to be complex, with findings preliminarily suggesting that one factor can be triggering or exacerbating for the other. Psychotic symptoms occurring in trauma and war survivors with PTSD may be difficult to capture, especially since PTSD and psychosis symptoms share many similarities [43]. In addition, individuals with psychosis are often not evaluated for symptoms of PTSD [46]. Our results highlight the crucial need for screening and early detection of one condition when the other is present, specifically in war-torn populations. The full mediation effect we found may indicate the importance of depression/anxiety symptoms in reducing PEs in war-affected individuals with PTSD, and in alleviating PTSD symptoms in those experiencing PEs as well. Therefore, along with evidence-based trauma‐focused therapy [18, 40], treating anxiety and depression early in war trauma survivors could be an effective way to reduce or prevent psychotic phenomena from emerging. Since this investigation was an initial study performed in Gaza during war on the link between PTSD and PEs, future longitudinal and experimental studies are warranted to replicate and confirm the mediation model. Additionally, further research can explore other potential explaining mechanisms in the relations between PTSD and PEs in war and mass violence contexts.

Conclusion

Our study helps elucidate, for the first time, the important role of depression and anxiety in the paths between PTSD and PEs in Palestinian adults living in the Gaza Strip, through the most devastating war in their history. We found that PTSD and PEs did not have a direct but a mediated impact on one another, with the association being fully explained by depression and anxiety. Findings imply that, treating depression/anxiety symptoms can be considered an integral part of interventions to reduce onset of psychosis in war survivors with PTSD. This field can benefit from future longitudinal research to permit stronger conclusions concerning bidirectional effects between the variables.
