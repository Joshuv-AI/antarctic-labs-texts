Perception, Knowledge and Clinical Use of Silver Diamine Fluoride Among Dental Professionals in Saudi Arabia: A Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Maha Eid, Doaa M Banaamah, Lujain Bajsair, Nedaa M Banaamah, Khadijah M Saidi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSilver diamine fluoride (SDF) is a non-invasive, cost-effective agent proven effective in arresting and preventing dental caries. Despite growing global evidence supporting its use, significant gaps exist in self-reported knowledge and clinical adoption among dental professionals. Previous studies in Saudi Arabia focused exclusively on general dentists, leaving perception and knowledge among a diverse dental workforce largely unexplored.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the perception, knowledge, and clinical use of SDF among a diverse group of dental professionals in Saudi Arabia, including pediatric dentists, restorative dentists, postgraduate and undergraduate students across both governmental and private practice settings.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted using an online self-administered questionnaire distributed via social media and Email between December 2020 and March 2021, using convenience sampling. A total of 320 dental professionals participated. Responses were analyzed using chi-square tests with significance set at p≤0.05.ResultsWhile 61.3% of participants reported awareness of SDF for arresting caries in children, only 20% reported awareness of the correct application frequency and only 25.9% reported knowledge of its cost-effectiveness. The majority (76.9%) had never used SDF clinically. Females demonstrated significantly higher awareness than males across most domains. Self-reported knowledge and willingness to use SDF increased significantly with higher educational qualification and greater clinical experience.ConclusionSignificant gaps in self-reported knowledge persist regarding SDF application frequency, cost-effectiveness, and clinical use among dental professionals in Saudi Arabia. The findings suggest a potential role for integrating SDF into undergraduate dental curricula and providing continuing professional development programs for practicing clinicians. Generalizability is limited by the convenience sampling approach.Keywords: silver diamine fluoride, dental professionals, knowledge gap, caries prevention, Saudi Arabia, pediatric dentistry, cross-sectional study, clinical use, awareness

Introduction

The use of silver diamine fluoride (SDF) for managing dental caries has earned considerable attention worldwide. SDF is a unique material that has the ability to non-invasively achieve the clinically significant goal of caries arrest.1 SDF has proven efficacy in prevention and in decreasing the incidence of new caries lesions, with its preventive effect appearing immediate and maintained over time.1 At concentrations of 30% and 38%, SDF shows remarkable potential as an alternative treatment option for arresting caries lesions in primary dentitions and permanent first molars.2

SDF exerts its cariostatic effect through two principal mechanisms: the antimicrobial action of silver ions, which inhibit the growth of cariogenic bacteria and penetrate dentinal tubules to arrest carious progression, and the remineralizing effect of fluoride, which promotes fluorapatite formation and creates a caries-resistant tooth structure.3,4 SDF is approved for clinical use in Saudi Arabia by the Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA). A notable clinical consideration is the aesthetic limitation of SDF — the agent permanently stains arrested carious lesions dark brown or black, creating what has been termed the “aesthetic paradox” of an effective caries management tool with an unacceptable cosmetic outcome for some patients.5 This cosmetic concern has been identified as one of the primary practical challenges limiting broader adoption in clinical practice, particularly in anterior teeth.

Current evidence and clinical recommendations support the appropriate use of SDF as part of a comprehensive caries management program.5 SDF is known for arresting caries on cavitated lesions in primary teeth and root caries in the elderly population.5,6 Clinically, the application is easy, non-invasive, affordable, and safe. The clinical use of SDF aligns with the contemporary philosophy of Minimally Invasive Dentistry (MID), which advocates for preserving tooth structure through early, non-invasive intervention rather than traditional “drill-and-fill” restorative approaches. Positioning SDF within this framework highlights its scientific relevance and potential role in modern caries management.7

Studies have shown that SDF applied once yearly is more effective than other topical fluorides applied two to four times per year for specific indications, and may be more cost-effective than dental sealants in high-risk populations.1,8 Annual application of SDF to high-risk surfaces has been identified as a cost-effective approach to caries prevention.1 SDF offers an alternative care perspective particularly for patients for whom conventional restorative treatment is not immediately available or feasible.7

For the purposes of this study, “perception” refers to the overall attitude towards SDF use in clinical practice; “knowledge” refers to self-reported familiarity with SDF’s clinical applications, benefits, application technique, frequency of use, and cost-effectiveness; and “clinical use” refers to the reported frequency of SDF application in daily practice.

Despite growing global evidence supporting SDF use, self-reported knowledge and clinical utilization among dental professionals remained variable. While previous studies examined SDF awareness among general dentists in Saudi Arabia,9,10 these studies revealed high theoretical awareness but persistently low clinical application and identified educational gaps among practicing clinicians. Limited data existed on the broader dental workforce, including pediatric dentists, restorative dentists, postgraduate and undergraduate students across different practice settings. Furthermore, no study had examined the influence of gender, type of practice, and clinical experience on SDF knowledge and clinical use in the Saudi context. The rationale for including a diverse sample of dental professionals — spanning undergraduate students to board-certified specialists across governmental and private settings — was to provide a comprehensive baseline assessment of SDF knowledge across the full spectrum of the dental workforce in Saudi Arabia. The objective of this study was therefore to assess the perception, self-reported knowledge, and clinical use of SDF among this diverse group of dental professionals.

Materials and Methods

A cross-sectional study was conducted using an online self-administered questionnaire distributed from December 2020 to March 2021. The questionnaire was circulated via social media platforms (WhatsApp and Twitter) and via institutional Email lists to dental professionals across Saudi Arabia.

The questionnaire consisted of two parts. The first part captured demographic data including age, gender, educational qualification, title, specialty, type of practice, and clinical experience. The second part assessed the general perception and self-reported knowledge of SDF use in clinical practice through eight questions addressing awareness, knowledge of benefits and risks, application technique, cost-effectiveness, application frequency, willingness to use SDF, and frequency of current use. The complete questionnaire is provided in Appendix 1.

The questionnaire was developed by the authors based on a review of the relevant SDF literature. Face validity was assessed by three experienced pediatric dentists prior to distribution. The questionnaire was administered in English. Formal psychometric validation, including pilot testing and reliability assessment (eg, Cronbach’s alpha for internal consistency), was not conducted prior to the study; this is acknowledged as a limitation. The questionnaire used a Yes/No/Maybe response format. Responses of “Maybe” were recorded as a separate uncertainty category and reported alongside “Yes” and “No” responses throughout the analysis.

Inclusion criteria comprised dental students, interns, residents, and practicing dental professionals above 18 years of age. Exclusion criteria included dental professionals whose scope of practice excludes clinical dentistry, such as non-clinical researchers or retired dentists. Participants who had never used SDF clinically were not excluded, as the study aimed to assess awareness and perceptions across the full spectrum of the dental workforce. Convenience sampling was used. The final sample of 320 participants is consistent with sample sizes reported in previous similar knowledge, attitude, and practice surveys in dental literature. No formal sample size calculation was performed; this is acknowledged as a limitation. The total number of individuals invited to participate could not be recorded, and therefore the response rate could not be calculated.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Research Ethics Committee, Faculty of Dentistry, King Abdulaziz University, Jeddah, Saudi Arabia (Approval No. 185–12-20), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants prior to completing the survey. Participants were presented with a consent statement at the beginning of the online questionnaire explaining the study purpose, voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality of their responses. Proceeding with the questionnaire was considered confirmation of written electronic consent.

Data were presented as frequency and percentages. Chi-square test was used to compare responses based on gender, educational qualification, type of practice, and clinical experience. Microsoft Excel was used to generate representative figures. Significance level was set at p≤0.05. Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS version 18.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

A total of 320 dental professionals participated in this study. The age of participants ranged from 20 to 65 years, with a mean of 30.03±5.6 years. Females constituted 53.1% of the sample. Regarding educational qualification, 70.7% held a bachelor’s degree, 13.1% were undergraduate students, and 7.5% were postgraduate students. The majority held the title of dentist (97.3%). Regarding specialty, 68.2% were general dentists or interns, 15.6% were students, 8.8% were pediatric dentists, and 5.6% were restorative dentists. The type of practice was governmental in 45.3% and private in 54.7% of participants. Clinical experience was less than five years in 63.4%, between five and ten years in 28.8%, and over ten years in 7.8% of participants. It is noted that undergraduate students (13.1% of the sample) were categorized within the “less than five years” experience group, as they reported zero or minimal clinical experience (Table 1).

Regarding general self-reported awareness and knowledge of SDF, 61.3% reported awareness of SDF for arresting caries lesions in children, while 33.8% were not aware and 5% were unsure. Self-reported awareness of the clinical benefits and risks of SDF was reported by 58.1%, while 31.9% were unaware and 10% were unsure. Discussion

This study assessed the self-reported perception, knowledge, and clinical use of SDF among a diverse group of dental professionals in Saudi Arabia. The findings revealed that while general awareness of SDF was reasonably high, significant gaps in self-reported knowledge existed particularly regarding application frequency and cost-effectiveness, and actual clinical use remained low among the majority of participants.

The overall self-reported awareness rate of 61.3% for SDF use in arresting caries lesions in children was consistent with findings from other regional studies. A national survey conducted among general dentists in Saudi Arabia reported that 92.45% were aware of SDF as a material, though awareness of its specific clinical applications varied considerably and clinical adoption remained low.9 Similarly, a study conducted among dental practitioners in Riyadh public hospitals reported only average SDF knowledge scores, highlighting persistent gaps despite general familiarity with the material.10 The present study extended these findings by including a broader and more diverse sample encompassing pediatric dentists, restorative dentists, postgraduate and undergraduate students, and practitioners from both governmental and private settings across the western region of Saudi Arabia, thereby providing a more comprehensive picture of SDF perception across the dental workforce.

Notably, only 20% of participants reported awareness of the SDF application frequency, and only 25.9% reported knowledge of its cost-effectiveness compared to other preventive treatments. It is important to note that these figures reflect self-reported awareness — the questionnaire asked participants whether they believed they knew the frequency and cost-effectiveness, and did not objectively test this knowledge. Nonetheless, these findings highlight that practical knowledge of application protocols and economic advantages remains limited, which may contribute to the significant implementation gap observed.

The discrepancy between moderate general awareness (61.3%) and very low clinical use (76.9% never used SDF) represents the most critical finding of this study. This implementation gap likely reflects multiple interacting factors identified in the literature, including concerns about tooth staining, lack of formal training, uncertainty regarding application protocols, and limited availability of SDF products in some clinical settings.7,8 The study was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic (December 2020 to March 2021), a period during which non-aerosol treatment options such as SDF were actively recommended by international dental bodies to reduce viral transmission risk. Despite this context, the majority of participants had still never used SDF clinically, underscoring the depth of the implementation gap and the need for structured educational interventions.

The significantly higher self-reported awareness and willingness to use SDF among female participants across multiple domains was an observed finding of this study. The reasons for this gender difference could not be determined from the current cross-sectional design, and this association may be confounded by other variables such as specialty distribution, educational level, or clinical experience. Future studies incorporating multivariable analyses would be needed to clarify the independent contribution of gender to SDF knowledge.

As expected, higher educational qualification and greater clinical experience were both strongly associated with increased self-reported SDF knowledge and willingness to use it in practice. Postgraduate degree holders demonstrated near-complete awareness across most domains, while undergraduate students reported critically low knowledge levels. These findings underscore the importance of integrating SDF into undergraduate dental curricula in Saudi Arabia, ensuring that future dental graduates enter clinical practice with adequate knowledge of this evidence-based caries management tool. Specifically, integrating SDF into preclinical phantom-head laboratory training and clinical pediatric dentistry requirements would provide structured exposure. Additionally, educational interventions should be targeted particularly toward general practitioners, interns, and early-career clinicians (those with less than five years of experience), who demonstrated the greatest knowledge gaps in this study.

The type of practice had minimal influence on self-reported SDF knowledge, with the exception of self-reported awareness of SDF efficacy compared to other treatments, which was significantly higher among governmental practitioners. This may reflect greater exposure to evidence-based preventive dentistry programs within governmental healthcare settings in Saudi Arabia, including national oral health initiatives. However, this interpretation is speculative and not supported by direct evidence from the study.

Furthermore, the introduction would benefit from including a brief comparison with international literature on SDF adoption barriers, which could further contextualise the findings from Saudi Arabia within the global evidence base.

This study had several limitations. The convenience sampling method and online distribution via social media and Email introduced potential selection bias, likely overrepresenting younger, more digitally connected, and more educated practitioners. The total number of individuals reached could not be recorded, and the response rate therefore could not be calculated, making it impossible to formally assess non-response bias. The questionnaire was not formally validated prior to use, and responses reflect self-reported awareness rather than objectively measured knowledge; this distinction is important when interpreting findings. Several educational subgroups contained very small numbers of participants, particularly board-certified specialists (n=2) and PhD holders (n=9), which limits the reliability of subgroup comparisons for these groups. Multiple statistical comparisons were performed without correction for Type I error, which should be considered when interpreting the results, as some significant associations may reflect chance findings. The study was conducted primarily through networks based in the western region of Saudi Arabia (Jeddah), which may limit generalizability of findings to other regions of the Kingdom and to Saudi Arabia as a whole. Additionally, the data were collected during the COVID-19 pandemic, which may have influenced both dental practice patterns and survey participation. Finally, the data were collected in 2020–2021, and the delay between data collection and publication means that findings may not fully reflect the current state of SDF awareness and clinical practice in Saudi Arabia, given the increasing international literature and educational efforts on SDF in recent years.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that while general self-reported awareness of SDF among dental professionals in Saudi Arabia was moderate, significant gaps in practical knowledge persisted regarding application frequency, cost-effectiveness, and clinical use. The majority of participants had never used SDF in clinical practice, highlighting a substantial gap between awareness and adoption. Female practitioners, those with higher educational qualifications, and those with greater clinical experience reported significantly better knowledge and willingness to use SDF. These findings suggest a potential role for integrating SDF into undergraduate dental curricula and for providing structured continuing professional development programs for practicing clinicians, particularly targeting general practitioners, interns, and early-career dentists who demonstrated the greatest knowledge gaps. Raising awareness of SDF’s clinical benefits, application protocols, and cost-effectiveness may contribute to improving clinical adoption in Saudi Arabia. These conclusions should be interpreted in the context of the convenience sampling approach used, which may limit the generalizability of findings to all dental professionals in the Kingdom.
