Corrosion resistance behavior of additively manufactured 316L stainless steel in nuclear environments
Authors: Ahmed Alameri, Chandrabhan Verma, Imad Barsoum, Akram Alfantazi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdditive manufacturing (AM) offers several unique advantages, including greater efficiency, design flexibility, and microstructural refinement. However, the corrosion resistance of AM-produced alloys remains a field of debate owing to the effects of printing parameters on porosity, microstructural properties, and passive film stability. The present study reports the printing of 316 L stainless steel (SS316L) specimens using the laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) technique and the subsequent testing of their corrosion resistance in 0.6 M NaCl, H2O2 (0.01 M and 0.5 M), and Na2SO4 (25 mM) solutions. The specimens were produced under a wide range of printing parameters, including scanning speed (500–1100 mm/s), laser power (150–330 W), and hatch distance (0.09–0.125 mm), with varying hardness, electrochemical behavior, porosity, and grain morphology and size. The results showed that an appropriate combination of scanning speed and laser power is crucial for achieving a refined microstructure and reduced porosity. The outcomes of electrochemical (EIS and PDP) studies suggest that AM-printed alloys exhibit better corrosion resistance than wrought 316 L, with specimen #19 demonstrating the best polarization resistance (Rp) and the lowest current density (Icorr). The specimen was fabricated at a laser power of 230 W, a scan speed of 1100 mm/s, and a hatch distance of 0.09 mm, respectively. The results further show that, in NaCl solution, porosity is a crucial factor in pitting initiation. On the other hand, in H2O2 solution, an increase in peroxide concentration stabilizes the passive films.Keywords: Additive manufacturing, Corrosion resistance, Passive film stability, Nuclear and marine environments, Microstructural refinementsSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Recently, additive manufacturing (AM) has emerged as a novel production process, where products are built in a layer-by-layer fashion1. The emergence of AM has shown great promise due to the advantages, including greater design flexibility, reduced waste production, and time-effective prototype production2–4. Due to the benefits AM offers, namely the ability to produce objects with complex geometries and reduced lead times, it was considered a promising manufacturing method for the manufacturing of advanced nuclear reactor components by the U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC)5. Stainless steel 316L is a grade of austenitic stainless steel known for its excellent mechanical properties and corrosion resistance, making it suitable for use in harsh conditions across various industries, including the petroleum, nuclear, and marine industries6–8. Stainless steel316L, manufactured using AM techniques such as laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) and direct energy deposition (DED), is one of the most studied AM alloys due to its weldability and the ability to build parts with great printing accuracy and finish9–12. However, due to the difference in manufacturing and cycles of melting and cooling during the printing process, AM metals typically exhibit a unique microstructure compared to traditional metals13–15. Due to this, multiple cases in research showcased changes in mechanical and corrosion resistance properties compared to traditionally manufactured 316 L16–20. The AM of 316L also introduces unique features, such as porosity21–23 and residual stress24–27, which may be detrimental to the mechanical and corrosion resistance performance of the printed material.

The process of fabricating using LPBF involves various processing parameters that influence the quality and the presence of defects in manufactured parts28. Although the quality of parts fabricated using LPBF is affected by many parameters29. The most influential processing parameters are laser power, scanning speed, hatch distance, and layer thickness30. Although the use of energy density as a variable doesn’t fully explain the complex process occurring during printing, it is commonly utilized in the literature to establish a foundation for the influence of processing parameters. Tucho et al.32 investigated the effects of processing parameters, including laser power, scanning speed, and hatch distance, on the quality and hardness of printed 316 L. Results from examining several printed 316 L specimens with varying processing parameters revealed that a higher energy density generally results in fewer pores, and that hatch distance is inversely proportional to porosity. Liu et al.21 state that increasing the scan speed at a given laser power results in higher porosity. In contrast, Kamath et al.33 have shown that the effect of scan speed is lower at higher laser powers, offering greater design flexibility. Kamath et al.32 have also demonstrated that near-full dense parts were able to be built using higher laser powers, upward of 400 W. Liverani et al.33 investigated the role of processing parameters on the relative density of printed 316 L specimen, results reveal that laser power and scanning speed play the most influential role on the presence of pore defects, and must be adjusted appropriately to ensure sufficient melting and fusion. Evangelou et al.34 examined the influence of processing parameters on the densification and hardness of printed 316 L using LPBF. Results show that a relative density of 95% or higher can be achieved with an energy density of at least 100 J/mm³. The hardness of the printed 316 L specimen has been shown to increase with increasing energy density. Ziri et al.35 have presented similar findings, showing a reduction in porosity with increasing energy density, up to a threshold of 83.3 J/mm³. Hardness examination has also been shown to increase with decreasing porosity.

Considerable work has been done to explore the unique microstructural features and corrosion resistance of AM 316L under different testing conditions. One of the most frequently reported microstructural features in AM 316L is the absence of MnS inclusions, which is attributed to the rapid cooling rates associated with the manufacturing process36. Detrimental inclusions, such as MnS, in austenitic stainless steel are considered pitting initiators, as their dissolution reveals the metal’s surface and propagates the corrosion attack in that area37–39. Investigation done by Chao et al.40 revealed greater corrosion resistance of AM 316L in chloride-containing solution compared to wrought 316 L, due to the absence of MnS inclusions and Cr-depleted areas in AM 316 L. The lack of MnS inclusions was attributed to the fast-cooling rates during the LPBF process, which enabled solidification without sulfide inclusions. Sander et al.41 reported similar findings under similar testing conditions, showcasing AM 316 L’s greater corrosion resistance performance compared to wrought 316 L, attributed to the size distribution or absence of pit-inducing inclusions. Kale et al.12 have also demonstrated the superior corrosion resistance performance of AM 316 L manufactured using LPBF compared to wrought 316 L and 316 L manufactured using Selective Laser Sintering (SLS). The results highlighted the influence of surface defects, such as pores, which are prevalent in SLS-manufactured AM 316 L, leading to lower corrosion resistance and the formation of a non-uniform passive layer that promotes pit formation. Multiple studies have examined the corrosion resistance performance of AM 316 L in acidic and basic environments. Miller et al.42 compared the corrosion resistance properties of LPBF 316 L with wrought 316 L exposed to sulfuric acid media. AM 316 L experienced greater metal loss after prolonged exposure than wrought 316 L. Greater surface roughness was observed on AM 316 L, suggesting potential corrosive attack along the melt edges. Trelewicz et al.43 also investigated the corrosion resistance of LPBF 316 L using electrochemical testing in 0.1 M HCl. The examination demonstrated reduced corrosion resistance of LPBF 316 L compared to wrought 316 L in an HCl-containing solution, attributed to the non-homogeneous distribution of solutes crucial for passive-layer formation. Karimi et al.44 have explored the corrosion performance of LPBF-manufactured 316 L in alkaline solutions containing chloride ions. Electrochemical results demonstrate the superior corrosion resistance of LPBF 316 L, attributed to the faster cooling rates associated with the manufacturing process, which yields a microstructure with enhanced phase stability and the elimination of detrimental inclusions.

Electrochemical corrosion investigations of LPBF 316 L in the literature yield contradictory results, suggesting that, due to the novelty and complexity of AM of stainless steel, LPBF 316 L may exhibit varying microstructural features depending on the manufacturing process parameters and conditions. Optimization of processing parameters to produce highly dense 316 L has been extensively explored in the literature, demonstrating the ability to produce LPBF 316 L with minimal porosity defects and superior mechanical properties across a range of processing parameter combinations. Sander et al.41 investigated the effect of varying laser power and scanning speed on the corrosion performance of LPBF 316 L. Results revealed that all LPBF 316 L specimens performed exceptionally compared to wrought 316 L, regardless of the presence of pore defects. Although the impact and the slight differences between LPBF 316 L specimens with varying processing parameters have not been discussed extensively. Laleh et al.45 have also investigated a similar topic, focusing on the role of porosity in the corrosion resistance performance of LPBF 316 L in chloride-containing solutions, printed using varying laser power and scanning speeds. Electrochemical and optical examinations revealed that irregular pore defects, particularly a lack of fusion porosity, were detrimental to the corrosion resistance of LPBF 316 L. The presence of lack of fusion pores is caused by a mismatch in process parameter selection, primarily due to variations in laser power and scanning speed.

Wang et al.46 have also examined the influence of porosity on the corrosion resistance of LPBF 316 L by printing multiple parts with varying laser powers. The findings indicate that porosity plays a significant role in determining corrosion resistance performance. However, pitting initiated in areas with subsurface pores, suggesting that it was caused by residual stress and stress concentration. Puga et al.47 explored a similar topic, investigating the role of LPBF processing parameters, laser power, and hatch distance, on corrosion resistance and microstructure of LPBF 316 L in nitric acid solution. Findings show an increase in corrosion rates for LPBF 316 L printed with a low hatch distance; preferential corrosion attack was observed along melt pools and grain boundaries. The preferential attack was hypothesized to be due to the segregation of alloying elements and the presence of balling porosity. Despite several reports on the corrosion behavior of LPBF-derived 316 L SS, most previous studies focused on either a single electrolyte, such as chloride-containing solutions, or limited processing parameters. Generally, these studies mainly presented the composition between additively manufactured and wrought counterparts. Conversely, the present study explores and introduces several significant advancements. First, the present study provides systematic details on the evaluation of LPBF processing parameters, such as hatch distance, laser power, and scan speed, across 20 distinct combinations. These enable a more comprehensive and systematic understanding of synergistic effects on corrosion behavior, porosity, and microstructure. Secondarily, unlike previous studies, the present study directly compares the corrosion behavior of additively manufactured 316 L in marine (NaCl) and nuclear (Na2SO4 + H2O2) environments relevant to marine and nuclear applications. Thirdly, this study also establishes a correlation between environmental dependence on corrosion responses and specific process-induced defects, mainly porosity. These criteria demonstrate that porosity governs the effect of oxidizing conditions on passive film stabilization and pitting in chloride electrolytes. Lastly, this study also identified optimized LPBF parameter windows that simultaneously maximize corrosion resistance and porosity, thereby directly linking process optimization to functional performance.

The present study investigates the corrosion resistance of 316L produced by LPBF in two electrolytes: marine (NaCl) and nuclear (H2O2 + Na2SO4). Some previous studies documented the corrosion behavior of LPBF-derived 316 L ; however, the novelty of the present investigation lies in the combined, systematic testing of processing-structure-corrosion relationships under oxidizing and chloride-related conditions relevant to nuclear and marine environments. This study investigates 20 LPBF parameter combinations, providing a broad spectrum of information, unlike previous studies that focus mainly on limited processing windows or single-electrolyte systems. The present study directly establishes relationships among microstructural features, porosity, and electrochemical responses under different environments. Moreover, this investigation provides new insight into the environment-mediated role of porosity, showing that, though porosity controls pit initiation under chloride-based conditions, oxidizing conditions favor passivation. The dual-electrolyte comparison and the characterization of the optimized processing window, linking printing parameters to corrosion behavior, represent a distinct advancement over existing studies. Though some studies have already established the effects of LPBF process parameters on corrosion behavior and porosity, most previous studies use these relationships in isolation or within a single environmental condition. Notably, the experiments employed in the present study are not intended to fully replicate the complex nuclear environments, which generally involve high pressure, elevated temperature, and radiation effects. Rather than H2O2 and Na2SO4-based environments, environments that provide a simplified illustration of the oxidizing species produced through water radiolysis were selected. This implies that the present work focuses on testing fundamental corrosion pathways and passive film stability under controlled laboratory conditions, providing basic insights relevant to nuclear applications rather than directly replicating reactor conditions.

Though the electrochemical studies in the present study were conducted in the short term for laboratory testing, the revised results provide valuable insights into mechanisms relevant to industrial applications, especially nuclear-industry applications. This study clearly establishes a strong relationship between LPBF processing parameters, porosity, and stability of passive films, which are the principal parameters controlling corrosion behavior in service environments. In industrial systems, metallic materials are exposed to highly aggressive conditions, including high pressure, high flow rates, elevated temperatures, and radiation. The selected electrolytes, NaCl and Na2SO4 + H2O2, although they do not replicate the actual factors, are designed to provide insights into degradation mechanisms such as chloride-mediated pitting and the radiolysis of water, which produces oxidizing effects. The outcomes show that porosity plays a crucial role in pit initiation, and oxidizing conditions favor the passivation. It was also established that reducing porosity increases polarization resistance. These outcomes can inform process qualification strategies, where controlling defect populations is critical to ensuring long-term performance. The present study can be considered a fundamental step in correlating processing-structure-corrosion relationships, and their direct translation to industrial conditions needs further validation.

Materials and methods

Materials and solutions

The LBPF process was performed using an EOS M400-4 AM, with 316 L alloy powder having a particle size distribution of 20 to 60 μm and the chemical composition listed in Table 1. A total of three solutions were tested: a chloride-containing solution of concentration 0.6 M NaCl, and two solutions containing hydrogen peroxide and sodium sulfate with the following concentrations: 0.01M H2O2 and 25mM Na2SO4, and 0.5M H2O2 and 25mM Na2SO. The Na2SO4+H2O2 solutions are used to simplify oxidizing conditions to describe the availability of radiolysis-induced species frequently observed in nuclear systems. Notably, these conditions do not directly replicate the exact complexity of the reactor environment, mainly with respect to pressure, temperature, and radiation effects. As part of this work, a wide range of processing parameter combinations was selected, including laser power from 150 W to 330 W, scanning speed from 500 mm/s to 1100 mm/s, and hatch distance from 0.09 mm to 0.125 mm. The selected range of laser power, scanning speed, and hatch distance was based on the commonly used LPBF processing windows for 316 L SS operation on the EOS M400-4 system. These windows were chosen to cover low-, intermediate-, and high-energy input conditions, enabling efficient testing of optimized densification, insufficient melting, and potential instability in the melt pool. A total of 20 processing parameter combinations were used to build cubic specimens with an edge length of 10 mm, a constant layer thickness of 0.04 mm, and a bi-directional scanning strategy. A schematic of the cubic specimen is shown in Fig. 1. Table 2 showcases the selected processing parameter combinations of each built specimen. Specimens were prepared for further testing by being cut perpendicular to the building direction using a low-speed cutter. Area fraction, microstructural characterization, and electrochemical corrosion testing were performed on the exposed surface perpendicular to the building direction.

Microstructural characterization

Surfaces perpendicular and parallel to the building direction were prepared for microstructural characterization, using the same grinding and polishing protocol as for determining the area fraction. Electrolytic etching with a 10% oxalic acid solution was performed on mirror-finish surfaces at a current density of 1 A/cm² to reveal the microstructure. The etched surfaces were then examined using an OLYMPUS GX51 optical microscope, with images captured at different magnifications. The grain structure and grain size were analyzed using ImageJ software, utilizing the morphological segmentation tool and analysis tool to determine grain size. Notably, porosity characterization in the study is based on 2D optical image measurements of polished cross-sections. Though this approach enables consistent comparison of several specimens, it does not capture the full 3D distribution, morphology, and size of pores. It should be noted that porosity characterization in this study is based on 2D optical image analysis of polished cross-sections. Thus, the reported values should not be considered as absolute volumetric measurements but interpreted as relative indicators.

Electrochemical evaluation

Specimens were prepared for electrochemical corrosion testing by connecting a copper wire, mounting the specimen, and testing the cross-sectional surface perpendicular to the building direction. Following the grinding to a P4000 grit finish, electrochemical corrosion testing was performed using a standard three-electrode setup connected to a Gamry Reference 3000 Potentiostat. To minimize the effect of surface preparation on the specimen’s corrosion behavior, all specimens underwent identical cleaning, grinding, and polishing procedures before electrochemical testing. This ensured that all preparation-driven artifacts and surface roughness were consistent across the specimens. Therefore, the observed trends are mainly attributed to processing parameters rather than surface roughness or preparation-induced artifacts. However, the role of residual stress and other microstructural factors can be only partially excluded. The tested specimen was the working electrode, and an Ag/AgCl (SCE) electrode and a graphite rod served as the reference and counter electrodes, respectively. The specimen was initially immersed in the solution at room temperature for 30 min, until the resting potential stabilized, followed by an EIS experiment set up with an initial frequency of 100,000 Hz and a frequency range of 0.01 Hz. Followed by Potentiodynamic Polarization tests conducted at −0.4 V and + 1.2 V about the resting potential obtained during OCP monitoring, at a scan rate of 1 mV/s. The wide range of potentials was chosen to explore the passive region and the breakdown potential of the passive film. To ensure data reproducibility, all electrochemical experiments were conducted at least twice. Variation across repeated experiments was within the accepted range for electrochemical testing, with consistent trends in key parameters such as Rp and Icorr. Due to the limited number of repetitions, a comprehensive statistical analysis was not conducted; therefore, the outcomes are interpreted primarily in terms of comparative trends rather than absolute quantitative differences. Electrochemical evaluation was performed under various testing conditions, including neutral, chloride-containing and conditions containing oxidizing species. Testing in different solutions enables the evaluation of the corrosion resistance of LPBF-built specimens under varying conditions. When analyzing the effect of processing parameters on corrosion resistance under different conditions, the specimen may be subject to. An as-received wrought stainless steel of grade 316 L was also tested in the solutions to compare its corrosion resistance with that of its LPBF counterpart.

Results and discussions

Effects of processing parameters on pore defects

Using area-fraction determination on the revealed surface, perpendicular to the building direction, revealed the presence of surface defects, such as pores. Figure 2(a) showcases optical images of one of the specimen’s surfaces and highlighting the presence of pores in Fig. 2(b). A total of 5 optical images were taken, and the average area fraction/porosity percentage was determined after pores were identified using contrast thresholding. The obtained area fraction values have been plotted with respect to each specimen’s LPBF processing parameters and are presented in Fig. 2.

(a) Optical image of polished surface and (b) Edited optical image using contrast thresholding, highlighting pores.

Greater porosity defects were observed in specimens manufactured at low laser power and scanning speed, specifically those in Group A, which were produced with a laser power of 150 W and a scanning speed of 500 mm/s. Keeping laser power and scanning speed constant, a greater number of pores was observed with increasing hatch distance in group A, as specimen 3, manufactured with the most considerable hatch distance, exhibited a significant increase in porosity. A greater area fraction has been achieved by increasing laser power and scanning speed. Multiple specimens were successfully manufactured with minimal porosity by varying different processing parameters, specifically laser power and scanning speed. For instance, numerous specimens in Groups C, F, and G were observed to have an area fraction of 99.95% and upwards. The highest area fraction, 99.97%, was observed for specimen #19 in group G, which was produced with a laser power of 230 W, a scanning speed of 1100 mm/s, and a layer thickness of 0.09 mm. Scanning speed has also been shown to play a synergistic role in reducing porosity, indicating compatibility between laser power and scanning speed; a mismatch results in a reduced area fraction. Comparing specimens with fixed laser power and hatch distance but varying scanning speed, for instance, specimens #5, #16, and #20 were manufactured using a laser power of 200 W and a hatch distance of 0.1 mm, but varying scanning speeds of 700 mm/s, 800 mm/s, and 1100 mm/s, respectively. Specimen #5 experienced thegreatest porosity presence, followed by specimen #16, while specimen #20 showed the least presence of porosity. A similar case can also be observed; although less significant, between specimens #13, #18, and #19, a greater scanning speed value resulted in fewer pore defects. Another more apparent observation is the influence of hatch distance selection on the area fraction of the manufactured specimen. A trend was observed across groups A, C, and D, which consisted of specimens manufactured at fixed laser power and scanning speed but with varying hatch distances. Manufactured specimens at a higher hatch distance exhibited greater porosity. The specimens with the lowest area fraction were manufactured at a hatch distance of 0.125 mm, yielding the highest value among the studied processing parameters. It is important to note that the porosity discussed in the present study is derived from 2D surface analysis and may not fully reflect subsurface pore properties. However, consistent trends observed across all specimens provide a reliable basis for correlating processing parameters with relative corrosion behavior and porosity.

Microstructural analysis

Figure 3 shows optical images taken of etched surfaces perpendicular and parallel to the building direction. This figure shows only representative specimens. They were chosen to capture the range of processing conditions, including low, intermediate, and optimized parameter combinations, and to illustrate corresponding variations in porosity and melt pool morphology. The complete dataset and overall trends are presented in the quantitative analysis and contour plots. The revealed microstructure is typical of metals produced using laser technologies, showcasing the thermal history of the melting process. Melt tracks caused by the laser beam, as well as the overlap between melt tracks from different layers, are visible in the images. The scanning track is highlighted in Fig. 3(a). At low laser power and scan speeds, the observed melt tracks were discontinuous when compared to the rest of the specimen. This may indicate that insufficient power and scan speeds have prevented significant overlap of melt tracks between layers. A difference in hatch distance led to differences in melt-track overlap. Higher hatch distances increase the spacing between consecutive melting tracks, reducing overlap. At lower hatch distances, melt tracks are essentially melted twice: once during initial scanning and again when melting occurs at an adjacent track, fusing them. This is illustrated in Figs. 3(c) and 3 (d). Figure 3(c) shows specimen #4 built using the lowest hatch distance, while Fig. 3(d) shows specimen #6 built using the same processing parameters as specimen #4 but at a higher distance. Less overlap of melt tracks is observed in the specimen with higher hatch distances.

Images showing optical images of etched surfaces perpendicular (a-b) and parallel (c-d) to the building direction.

Austenite grain boundaries are also visible alongside the melt tracks; the grain structure is complex, with grains varying in size and boundaries that are bent and twisted. The light optical images were analyzed in ImageJ to determine the average grain size perpendicular to the building direction. ImageJ analysis successfully segmented the grain boundaries, as illustrated in Fig. 4. The average grain diameter was then analyzed and is shown in Table 3. The obtained average grain sizes exhibit significant variation, as indicated by the high standard deviations. This is due to the complex thermal history the layers undergo during the manufacturing process. Kale et al.12 reported similar grain sizes with high variance from EBSD analysis of 316 L specimens manufactured using LPBF. Although the variance in the present work is significantly higher compared to the literature, image analysis is likely to be less accurate than EBSD analysis. The presence of melt tracks may also have affected the segmentation process during analysis, particularly at grain boundaries located between or near the melt tracks. Specimens fabricated at low laser power and scan speeds exhibit smaller grain sizes, indicating a finer grain structure and increased grain boundaries. Specimens fabricated at 250 W and 900 mm/s exhibit a coarser grain structure, with grain sizes averaging approximately 30 μm. Notably, the grain size derived from optical micrographs using ImageJ is subject to inherent limitations, including limited resolution and challenges in segmenting complex melt-pool structures. The heterogeneity of microstructure results in relatively larger standard deviations. This suggests that the grain size distribution documented in the present study should be interpreted as an approximate indicator of microstructural trends rather than precise qualitative measurements. Therefore, the relationship between corrosion behavior and grain size should be considered qualitative, and porosity remains the dominant factor affecting corrosion resistance in the present study.

Hardness analysis

Table 4 outlines the average hardness measured for each fabricated specimen on surfaces perpendicular to the building direction. In the present study, hardness measurements are also included to provide complementary insight into the effect of LPBF processing parameters on microstructural properties. The primary focus of the present study is to measure the relative corrosion behavior of AM-fabricated and wrought 316 L. Hardness is highly sensitive to several characteristics, such as grain structure, local heterogeneity, and porosity, which in turn affect corrosion behavior. Therefore, in the present investigation, hardness is used as a supporting indicator to better understand the relationships among microstructure, printing parameters, and corrosion behavior. The highest hardness was measured in specimen #12 at 239 HV. The lowest hardness was observed in specimen #13 at 216 HV. Consistent with the literature, many studies48–50 suggest that increased porosity decreases strength and hardness. This is due to the collapse of pores under load, particularly at loads below those that the material can typically withstand. In the experimental results, specimens with low porosity exhibit lower hardness. The specimen with significantly higher porosity exhibited greater hardness. Conventional metallurgical analysis indicates that increased porosity is consistent with decreased hardness due to pore collapse and stress concentration. However, the present investigation manifests a slight deviation from this trend. These deviations can be attributed to several attributes. Firstly, the porosity assessed and reported in this investigation is based on 2D surface analysis, which may not fully reflect the true subsurface or volumetric distribution, thereby influencing the indentation response. Secondarily, hardness derived from micro-indentation is highly localized and may be influenced by the specific indentation region, particularly in heterogeneous LPBF microstructures containing grain structures and melt pool boundaries. Thirdly, the absence of a pore directly beneath the indenter or localized densification around the indentation site may lead to higher harness values, even in specimens with overall higher porosity. Therefore, the hardness and porosity reported in this study should be considered as qualitative rather than strictly quantitative. Image analysis and contrast thresholding provide information on the presence of porosity on the captured surface. However, the obtained porosity results do not accurately reflect the amount and distribution of porosity relative to the volume of the fabricated specimen. More advanced techniques and methodologies can be employed to provide this information, offering a clearer picture and explanation of the fluctuations present in the hardness measurements.

Electrochemical corrosion studies

Corrosion behavior analysis of LPBF-fabricated SS316L in 0.6 M NaCl

The Potentiodynamic polarization curves of the specimen are presented in Fig. 5. A careful observation of the Tafel curves shows distinct variations in the passivation and corrosion kinetics among the tested specimens. The specimens fabricated under suboptimal processing conditions, including low scan speed and laser power, exhibit more negative corrosion potentials and higher anodic current densities, suggesting reduced passive film stability and enhanced corrosion rates. Conversely, in optimized specimens such as specimen #19, significantly lower anodic current densities and more extensive passive regions are observed, suggesting enhanced corrosion resistance and greater stability of passive films. The shift in breakdown potential and passive region further shows that these species are associated with highly stable passive films, reduced porosity, and enhanced protection (corrosion resistance). The change in cathodic behavior is attributed to differences in surface reactivity, which are associated with defect distribution and microstructural heterogeneity. These findings suggest that printing parameters not only affect corrosion current density but also affect passive film stability and breakdown potential. The curves are analyzed using the Tafel fit, and numerous parameters, including the corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (Icorr), were derived (Table 5). The formation of the passive film is observed in the curves, where the current density remains constant. The passivation potential (Epp) denotes the potential at which the passive film is formed. The passive region persists until a breakdown is observed, at which point the anodic curve shifts into an active area. Breakdown potential (Ed) denotes the potential where the breakdown of the passive film occurs; Epp and Ed are highlighted in Fig. 5. Specimens from group A have been shown to exhibit poorer corrosion resistance than the other tested specimens, as indicated by the higher Icorr values. Notably, specimen #2 shows a relatively high Icorr compared to other specimens and shows slight deviation from the generated trends. The behavior may be due to intermediate processing parameters, leading to microstructural heterogeneity and a lack of fusion defects. Such a feature can serve as localized sites for electrochemical reactions, increasing Icorr and anodic dissolution. Moreover, melt-pool boundaries and subsurface porosity irregularities may accelerate corrosion by locally breaking down passive films. This may be due to the increased amount of pore defects in those specimens, which can significantly accelerate pitting corrosion. Specimen #19 exhibits the best corrosion resistance properties among the fabricated specimens, with the lowest Icorr value of 0.02 µA/cm². The breakdown potential (Ed) varied among specimens, and specimen #1 showed pitting at lower potentials, indicating poor passivating properties. Specimens #17 and #18 have demonstrated excellent passivation properties, with breakdown potentials of 1072 and 956 mV, respectively. The passive layer in those specimens has also lasted the longest, as indicated by the Ed-Epp values, demonstrating excellent corrosion resistance. The results presented in Table 5 also suggest that corrosion resistance is greatly dependent on the passive film stability and processing-induced porosity. The specimen #19 exhibits stable passivation behavior and the lowest Icorr, validating its superior performance in chloride-containing environments.

Figure 6 showcases the influence of processing parameters on the corrosion resistance properties of the printed specimen. The contour plots indicate an increase in current density at lower laser powers and scanning speeds, suggesting reduced corrosion resistance. This is most likely due to the higher porosity exhibited by these specimens, resulting from insufficient melting caused by unoptimized processing parameters. Another trend observed is the influence of hatch distance on corrosion resistance. Specimens fabricated at high hatch distances have generally exhibited better corrosion resistance compared to those manufactured at lower hatch distances. This trend is also apparent when observing the passivation range of specimens built with varying hatch distances. Specimens fabricated at higher hatch distances have shown an increase in passive range, and breakdown occurs at high potentials. While specimens fabricated at low hatch distance experience breakdown at much earlier potentials. This is apparent in the following groups: A, B, C, and D, each of which is a specimen fabricated at identical laser power and scanning speeds, but varying hatch distances. This may be due to increased melt pool and track boundaries, as lower hatch distances result in greater overlap between melt pools, thereby increasing the number of boundaries. Generally, an increase in hatch distance is associated with higher porosity due to reduced overlap between melt tracks; the increase in corrosion resistance at higher hatch distance indicates the involvement of additional factors. Microstructural homogeneity, melt pool geometry, and track boundaries are influenced by the hatch distance, which, in turn, affects passive film formation, thickness, and stability. It is possible that, at higher hatch distances, minimized overall results in melt pool boundary regions or compositional heterogeneity that can serve as preferential corrosion sites. These discussions show that the corrosion behavior of 316 L is not attributed solely to porosity, but rather to a combination of microstructural homogeneity, porosity, and passive film properties. This explores the complex and competing effects of processing parameters on the corrosion performance of additively manufactured specimens.

Contour plot examining the influence of the following parameters on thecorrosion rate in 0.6 M NaCl: (a) Laser power and scanning speed, (b) Laser power and hatch distance, and (c) Scanning speed and hatch distance.

The enhanced corrosion resistance of AM-derived 316 L specimens as compared to wrought materials can be attributed to the combined effects of passive film and microstructural properties. Firstly, the well-refined columnar/cellular microstructure and the unavailability of MnS as inclusions in LPBF minimize the number of preferential pitting initiation sites, which is reflected by an increase in breakdown potential and a decrease in Icorr in PDP tests. Secondarily, although porosity can serve as pit initiation sites and as a local stress concentrator, AM-fabricated specimens with reduced porosity (e.g., specimen #19) exhibit an increase in Rp due to the formation of a stable, firm passive film, as evidenced by the larger semicircle diameter in the Nyquist plots. Lastly, the rapid solidification associated with LPBF processing promotes a more homogeneous and uniform distribution of alloying elements, including Mo and Cr, thereby contributing significantly to the formation and stabilization of passive films. This results in enhanced resistance to breakdown and greater protection than wrought 316 L SS. This suggests that the better corrosion resistance of AM specimens is due to the synergistic effects of enhanced passive film stability, increased passive film density, and optimized porosity, which collectively contribute to the observed electrochemical response.

The Nyquist and Bode plots for printed specimen corrosion are presented in Figs. 8and 9, respectively, and describe the properties of the passive layer formed in the tested solutions. The plots have a semicircular shape and, in some cases, are incomplete; the semicircle’s diameter describes the properties of the passive layer. A larger semi-circle diameter indicates that the passive layer formed is more corrosion-resistant. Generally, larger Nyquist plot diameters are associated with higher corrosion resistance and Rp values, and their proper interpretation can provide indirect insights into passive film properties. Though complete stability and thickness information cannot be derived without advanced characterization, such as SEM and XPS analyses, electrochemical studies can provide indicative rather than definitive information. The plots were fitted using a constant phase element (CPE) equivalent circuit; a schematic of the equivalent circuit is shown in Fig. 7 , and the fitting parameters are listed in Table 6.

Corrosion behavior analysis of LPBF fabricated SS316L in sulphate solutions containing hydrogen peroxide

The best-performing specimens were selected for testing in sulfate solutions containing hydrogen peroxide: specimens #10 , #12 , and #19 . The selection of these three specimens is based on their performance, as determined earlier through electrochemical measurements. Particularly, these samples correspond to the distinct performance groups, including high, intermediate, and optimized corrosion resistance, allowing for a comparative assessment under oxidizing conditions. This strategy was adopted to ensure that the present investigation captures a range of material responses while maintaining experimental feasibility. Notably, the study’s objective was not to re-evaluate all specimens under processing conditions, but to demonstrate the influence of oxidizing environments on representative microstructures. All specimens were tested in 0.6 M NaCl, H2O2 (0.01 M and 0.5 M), and Na2SO4 (25 mM) solutions. The Potentiodynamic curves of the selected specimen in both 0.01 and 0.5 M H2O2 containing 25mM Na2SO4 are showcased in Fig. 10(a-b). The curves were fitted using Tafel extrapolation, and the fitting parameters are shown in Table 7. The parameters of the corresponding solutions are also provided, indicating which solutions each specimen performed best in. It is observed that the corrosion potential of the specimen in a 0.01 M H2O2 solution has increased significantly when compared to testing in other solutions. This may indicate the formation of a passive layer upon the addition of H2O2. A further increase in corrosion potential is observed when testing with 0.5 M H2O2, indicating the formation of a passive layer and an increase in thickness with increasing H2O2 concentration. This may be due to reduced H2O2 at the exposed metal surface, promoting passive film formation. The passive region in the 0.5 M H2O2 solution extends and remains stable at higher potentials compared to the 0.01 M H2O2 solution. The careful examination of Table 7 shows that an increase in H2O2 concentration enhances passivation, as reflected in higher Ecorr values. Though specimen #12 performs effectively under strongly oxidizing (high H2O2 concentration) conditions, specimen #19 maintains consistent corrosion resistance in various tested environments.

Conclusions

The present investigation describes the printing of 316 L stainless steel specimens using the laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) technique and the subsequent testing of their corrosion resistance behavior in marine and nuclear environments. The following conclusions were drawn:

Low scan speed and low laser power produced 316L alloys of increased pore formation, and conversely, this was true for high scan speed and high laser power.
