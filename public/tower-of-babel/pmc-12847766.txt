Cannabidiol perturbs macrophage polarization by interfering with the metabolic flux and PI3K/Akt pathway
Authors: Thadaphong Sukdee, Benjawan Wongprom, Thitiporn Pattarakarnkul, Asada Leelahavanichkul, Awirut Charoensappakit, Kritsanawan Sae‑khow, Phanomsak Yukhet, Tirayut Vilaivan, Tanapat Palaga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cannabinoids are increasingly used for medical application, yet the impact of specific cannabinoids on innate immune cells and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain controversial. In this study, we investigated the effect of cannabinoid, cannabidiol (CBD) on macrophage polarization toward M1 and M2 using murine bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM). CBD markedly inhibited IL-6 production and nitric oxide production without affecting TNF-α production in M1 polarized (LPS + IFN-γ-treated) macrophages. CBD treatment of M1 macrophages, however, increased expression of MHC II and CD86. On the other hand, CBD decreased key M2 markers, including CD206 and arginase-1, in IL-4-treated M2 macrophages. Mechanistically, CBD did not alter signaling downstream of TLR4 or IFN-γR (NF-κB, MAPK, and STAT1 pathways) but inhibited Akt phosphorylation in M1 macrophages. Metabolic flux analysis further revealed that CBD significantly enhanced glycolysis in M1 macrophages while reducing oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) in M2 macrophages. Collectively, our findings revealed the multi-faceted effect of CBD on macrophage polarization by interfering with PI3K/Akt signaling and altering the metabolic flux. Taken together, our findings provide mechanistic insights into how CBD interferes with innate immune functions during macrophage polarization that may inform future therapeutic strategies utilizing cannabinoids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33360-5.

Introduction

Cannabinoids are a class of terpene-phenolic compounds primarily derived from Cannabis species. The cannabinoids have been reported to have broad spectrum of pharmacological effects in preclinical models and in human diseases, including pain, neuropsychiatric disorders, neurodegenerative and inflammatory diseases among others1–3. Therefore, they have high therapeutic potential in medical applications for a wide range of diseases. Cannabinoids exert their regulatory effects on cellular functions and responses by primarily stimulating the cannabinoid receptor type 1 (CB1R) and type 2 (CB2R), both are members of the G protein-coupled receptor family4. Among the most recognized cannabinoids are ∆9-tetrahydrocannabinol phytocannabinoids (∆9-THC) and cannabidiol (CBD). ∆9-THC is primarily responsible for the psychoactive effects of the cannabis, whereas CBD does not exhibit these effects. These psychoactive responses are chiefly mediated via the CB1R that was initially identified in the central nervous system. In contrast, CB2R is predominantly expressed in the immune cells and peripheral tissues5, suggesting that it may play important role in immunomodulation via engaging with cannabinoids. However, the precise effects of cannabinoids on each subset of the immune cells remain poorly understood.

Recent studies demonstrated the beneficial effects of cannabinoids as immunomodulators that influence the immune cell functions, both innate and adaptive immunity6,7. In the hyperinflammatory conditions, CBD improves the wound healing process by preventing excessive inflammation during early wound closure in normal mice and promotes tissue repair in diabetic mice8. In addition, the combination of THC with CBD at 1:1 ratio, attenuates expression of the inflammatory mediators in TLR7/8-stimulated monocytes and primary human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs)9. This data, and others, indicate that cannabinoids may dampen the viral sensing and inflammatory process in immune cells. However, how cannabinoids influence the immune response-related signaling pathways in specific immune cell subsets remain unclear. Understanding the mechanisms of how cannabinoids interfere with the immune response will allow for better use of these compounds in clinical settings.

Macrophage polarization is driven by stimuli from microenvironment, resulting in distinct and diverse functional phenotypes. At least in an in vitro model, two well-established subtypes of activated macrophages are classically activated macrophages (M1) and alternatively activated macrophages (M2)10,11. M1 macrophages are induced by exposure to pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and/or pro-inflammatory cytokines which are responsible for inflammatory responses and eliminating pathogens. On the other hand, IL-4-treated M2 macrophages suppress inflammation and promote tissue regeneration after the resolution of inflammation12. To accommodate the energy requirement of polarized macrophages, metabolic regulation plays crucial roles in dictating the polarization and biological functions of macrophages13. In disease settings, macrophage polarization may have negative impact on the outcomes such as in tumor where tumor-associated macrophages are linked with tumor progression and metastasis11. Thus, the signaling pathways leading to macrophage activation and polarization must be precisely and tightly regulated.

The impact of cannabinoids on macrophage activation and function has been reported in various studies. CBD acts as a molecular switch that stimulates the biosynthesis of inflammation-resolving lipid mediators and suppresses pro-inflammatory leukotriene and by suppressing secretion of TNF-α14,15. Similarly, stimulation via CB2R attenuates inflammation by inhibiting M1 polarization during skin wound healing16. In contrast, CB1R is reported to inhibit M2 polarization and promotes M1 polarization by G(α)i/o/RhoA/NF-κB p65 signaling pathway17,18. CBD treatment skews polarization of macrophages toward M2b in inflammatory conditions and reduces the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α19. To apply the knowledge on CBD and macrophage polarization, the use of hydrogel loading CBD was reported to have anti-inflammatory effect for treatment against chronic enteritis20. Therefore, the effects of cannabinoids on macrophage polarization and function remain controversial and may vary depending on the context. Despite extensive studies on the effects of CBD on macrophage biological functions, the detailed signaling pathways affected by CBD, as well as how CBD treatment influences metabolic pathways in M1 or M2 macrophages, have not been carefully addressed.

In this study, we investigate the effects of CBD on M1 and M2 polarization using bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs), focusing on the immediate signaling pathways triggered by M1 or M2 stimuli and the metabolic shift underlying their polarization. The findings presented here may provide insights into the biological effects of CBD on specific innate immune responses and potentially inform therapeutic strategies utilizing cannabinoids for treatment of cancer, infectious diseases, and other immune-mediated diseases.

Effect of cannabinoids on the viability of macrophages

To assess the effect of synthetic cannabinoids on macrophage cell viability, BMDMs were treated with cannabinoids at concentrations of 10, 20, and 100 µM for 24 h (Fig. 1A) and the cell viability was determined by MTT assay. The results demonstrated that all cannabinoids at 100 µM were toxic to BMDMs, reducing macrophages total viability to below 80%. In contrast, the treatment with 10 or 20 µM did not have significant impact on macrophage viability, as shown in Fig. 1B. Interestingly, certain cannabinoids, CBD, CBN, and ∆9-THC, appeared to enhance cell proliferation of BMDMs. For CBD, concentrations above 40 µM began to affect cell viability (Supplementary Figure S1). In addition, previous in vitro studies examining the effects of cannabinoids on macrophage functions have primarily used these compounds at the concentrations ranging from 5 to 25 µM 15,21–23. Therefore, cannabinoid concentrations of 10 and 20 µM were selected for subsequent experiments.

Effect of synthetic cannabinoids on cytokine and NO production in LPS+IFN-γ-stimulated BMDMs

To investigate the immunomodulatory effects of cannabinoids, BMDMs were pretreated with synthetic cannabinoids at 10, 20 µM for 1 h, and stimulated with LPS (100 ng/ml) and IFN-γ (10 ng/ml) to induce an M1 polarization. After 24 h of incubation, secreted TNF-α and IL-6 were measured using sandwich ELISA (Fig. 2A). The results showed that all synthetic cannabinoids, except ∆9-THC, significantly reduced the amount of secreted IL-6 (Fig. 2B), while the TNF-α levels remained unaffected (Fig. 2C). Notably, CBD at 20 µM exhibits the strongest inhibitory effect against IL-6 production, as shown in Fig. 2B. Although some cannabinoids at the tested concentrations increased cell proliferation (Fig. 1), the overall reduction in cytokine and nitric oxide production following cannabinoid treatment supports a negative impact of these compounds on M1 macrophage functions. For TNF-α where no difference was observed among treatment groups, potential variations in cell numbers could influence the interpretation of the results. These findings suggest that some cannabinoids have unique characteristics in regulating cytokine production in M1 macrophages.

Next, we further tested the effect of cannabinoids on enzymatic function of iNOS, which catalyzes the production of NO from L-arginine. Our results showed that only CBN and CBD significantly suppressed NO production from M1 BMDMs, especially CBD at 20 µM, exhibited the highest inhibitory activity on NO production to almost the baseline level, as shown in Fig. 2D. Because CBD showed the most potent impact on M1-related functions, the impact of CBD on M1 and M2 macrophages was further investigated.

Effect of CBD on M1 markers

To investigate the effect of CBD on expression of M1 signature genes, BMDMs were pretreated with CBD (20 µM) for 1 h and stimulated with LPS (100 ng/ml) and IFN-γ (10 ng/ml) for 6 h. After stimulation, the expressions of M1 signature genes (Il1b, Il12b, and Nos2) were detected by RT-qPCR (Fig. 3A). The results show that treatment with CBD alone did not alter the expression of these genes (Fig. 3B). The levels of Il1b and Il12b mRNA were markedly inhibited in the presence of CBD in M1 BMDMs, indicating an inhibitory effect of CBD on pro-inflammatory M1 marker genes (Fig. 3B). In contrast, CBD had no effect on Nos2 expression. Collectively, the results suggested that CBD selectively modulates expression of M1 signature genes.

To assess the impact of CBD on the surface markers of M1 macrophages that play pivotal roles in communicating with adaptive immune cells, BMDMs were pretreated with CBD (10, 20 µM) and stimulated with LPS (100 ng/ml) and IFN-γ (10 ng/ml). After 24 h of stimulation, BMDMs were harvested and stained with fluorescence-conjugated specific antibodies to MHC-II and CD86 (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Figure S2). Our results showed that MHC class II and CD86 were further upregulated by CBD treatment in a dose-dependent manner in M1 macrophages, as shown in Fig. 4B. In contrast to CD86 and MHC-II, the level of CD40 increased following M1 stimulation and remained unchanged in the presence of CBD (Fig. 4B). This finding suggested that CBD selectively affects the expression of surface markers of M1 macrophages that may influence activation of adaptive immune cells.

Effect of CBD on arginase-1 and iNOS expression in M1 BMDMs

From previous experiments, CBD treatment reduced the production of NO production, while it showed no effect on iNOS expression in M1 macrophages. Because of the competitive substrate usage between iNOS and arginase-1, we hypothesized that arginase-1 may be upregulated upon CBD treatment and compete for the same substrate that results in lower NO output. To test this hypothesis, we examined the expression of arginase-1 in LPS+IFN-γ-stimulated M1 BMDMs (Fig. 5A-B). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that Arg1 mRNA expression was slightly elevated in the presence of CBD, however, this increase did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 5B) and the protein of arginase-1 was not detectable by Western blot (Fig. 5C-D). We confirmed that IL-4 treatment induced arginase-1 expression and CBD did not have any impact on this expression. Overall, these findings suggest that the inhibitory effect of CBD on NO production is not directly mediated by suppressing iNOS expression or competitive substrate utilization between iNOS and arginase-1 in macrophages.

Effect of CBD on downstream signaling pathways of TLR4 in M1 BMDMs

To investigate whether CBD treatment affects NF-κB signaling pathways during M1 polarization, BMDMs were pretreated with CBD (20 µM) for 1 h and stimulated with LPS (100 ng/ml) and IFN-γ (10 ng/ml) and were subjected to Western blot to examine the phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 (Supplementary Figure S3A and B). The results showed that p-NF-κB levels increased following LPS + IFN-γ stimulation, and its levels did not alter upon CBD treatment, suggesting that CBD did not interfere with the early activation of NF-κB signaling in M1 macrophages.

To determine whether CBD influences the MAPK signaling pathway in M1 macrophages, the cell lysate from BMDMs treated as described above was examined for the activation of MAPK pathways (SAPK/JNK, p38, and p44/42) (Supplementary Figure S3C-E). Our results showed that the phosphorylation of all MAPK proteins increased following LPS + IFN-γ stimulation (Supplementary Figure S3C-E). The level of SAPK/JNK p-SAPK/JNK was slightly increased during CBD treatment at 30 and 60 min following LPS + IFN-γ stimulation. However, this increase did not reach a statistically significant level when compared with vehicle control (0.1% DMSO) treatment (Supplementary Figure S3C). The phosphorylation of Erk1/2 and p38 were also not affected by CBD treatment, as shown in the representative Western blot in Figures S3D and S3E. Together, these results indicated that the treatment with CBD in M1 macrophages did not interfere with the downstream signaling pathways of TLR4, including NF-κB, SAPK/JNK, p38 and Erk1/2.

Next, the effect of CBD on the activation of PI3K/Akt was determined in M1 BMDMs. As shown in Fig. 5E, Akt phosphorylation was immediately induced upon LPS+IFN-γ stimulation as early as 5 min and remained high by 30 min. CBD treatment clearly reduced the activation of Akt in M1 BMDMs after 5 min, indicating that activation of PI3/Akt signaling is compromised by CBD treatment in M1 macrophages.

Effect of CBD on IFN-γ/STAT1 signaling pathway in M1 BMDMs

To evaluate whether CBD affects IFN-γ/STAT1 signaling pathway, BMDMs were pretreated with CBD (20 µM) for 1 h and stimulated with LPS + IFN-γ and the level of p-STAT1 were examined by Western blot (Supplementary Figure S4A and B). The results showed that p-STAT1 levels increased in a time-dependent manner and reached a maximum at 30 min after stimulation. CBD treatment did not change the activation dynamic of STAT1 phosphorylation (Supplementary Figure S4B). These results suggest that the delayed STAT1 phosphorylation may limit macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype.

Effect of CBD on M2 BMDMs

In the previous experiment, ariganse-1 expression in IL-4-stimulated BMDMs (M2 macrophages) was decreased during CBD treatment (Fig. 5D). To further explore the impact of CBD on M2 macrophage polarization, BMDMs were pretreated with CBD (20 µM) for 1 h and stimulated with IL-4 (100 ng/ml) and the expression of M2 signature genes was detected (Fig. 6A). Surface expression of CD206 was detected at 24 h after IL-4 stimulation by flow cytometry. The results showed the inhibitory effect of CDB on CD206 upregulation (Fig. 6B and Supplementary Figure S2). When the expression of other M2 signature genes was evaluated 6 h after IL-4 stimulation, the result showed that Arg1 and Retnla, were markedly suppressed (Fig. 6C). Collectively, these findings suggest that CBD treatment interferes with M2 polarization by downregulating the expression of key M2 marker genes.

Effect of CBD on metabolic flux in M1 BMDMs

To assess the impact of CBD on metabolic pathways in M1 macrophages, BMDMs were pretreated with CBD (20 µM) for 1 h and stimulated with LPS (100 ng/ml) and IFN-γ (10 ng/ml) for 1 h before subjecting to glycolysis flux analysis. The basal respiration was measured, followed by the sequential addition of glucose (10 mM), oligomycin (1 µM), and 2-DG (50 mM) at the indicated time (Fig. 7A). Our results showed that CBD treatment of BMDMs enhanced the basal respiration of BMDMs as shown in extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) (Fig. 7B). In M1 BMDMs, CBD treatment significantly elevated the glycolytic capacity when compared with vehicle control, indicating an increased ability of cells to meet energetic demands via glycolysis (Fig. 7C). The glycolysis reserve was also increased by CBD treatment, as shown in Fig. 7C. This result suggested that CBD treatment promotes cellular metabolism along the glycolytic pathway during M1 polarization.

Effect of CBD on metabolic flux in M2 BMDMs

To test whether CBD affects the mitochondrial function via OXPHOS in M2 macrophages, BMDMs were pretreated with CBD (20 µM) for 1 h and stimulated with IL-4 for 24 h (Fig. 8A). After basal respiration was measured, oligomycin (1.5 µM), FCCP (1 µM), and a mixture of Rot/AA (0.5 µM) were sequentially added at the indicated time, and the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was monitored (Fig. 8B). The results showed that CBD treatment lowered the OCR at the basal stage and reduced OCR at all timepoints, indicating that ATP production was dampened by CBD treatment (Fig. 8C). Overall, these results suggested that CBD treatment limits the mitochondrial function and metabolic flexibility especially during M2 polarization, thereby reducing the capacity to generate energy for M2 polarization.

Discussion

Emerging evidence indicates that cannabinoids possess anti-inflammatory properties and function as immunomodulators in diverse therapeutic contexts24,25. Various studies have reported on the effect of cannabinoids, either the phytocannabinoids or synthetic cannabinoids, on macrophage functions both in an in vitro and in vivo models19,23,26–28. In most studies, cannabinoids are found to possess anti-inflammatory activities against pro-inflammatory macrophages, including M1 polarizing macrophages. Our findings are consistent with previous reports, however, we also provide additional evidence suggesting that CBD may enhance antigen-presenting functions in M1 macrophages.

Goodman et al.. demonstrate that ∆9-THC and CBD elicit distinct effects on RAW264.7 macrophage functions. They showed that ∆9-THC decreases phagocytosis in a dose-dependent manner, which did not correlate with the level of TNF-α expression. In contrast, CBD exhibited a biphasic effect, has an inhibitory effect at a low dose of CBD at 15–47 µM, where it decreases phagocytosis, but at a higher dose (80 µM), it enhances phagocytosis in live bacteria-infected macrophages29. Kozela et al.. also demonstrated that the pretreatment of THC and CBD for 2 h has different outcomes on LPS-stimulated BV-2 microglia. Specifically, they reported that CBD at low dose (10 µM) but not THC, effectively suppressed IL-6 expression by inhibiting NF-κB activation within 15 min post-stimulation24. Our study used the doses of 10–20 µM for the investigation based on the results on cell viability and previous studies which examined the effects of cannabinoids on macrophage functions15,21,22. We observed a similar inhibitory effect on IL-6 and NO production, but not TNF-α, by certain cannabinoids, particularly CBD and ∆9-THC. Because some cannabinoids at the concentrations used in this study exhibited stimulatory effects on cell growth, differences in cell numbers could influence the interpretation of the results. In particular, cannabinoids with pronounced growth-promoting effects, such as ∆9-THC and CBD, may nonetheless reduce TNF-α production.

In M1 macrophages, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS, encoded by Nos2) catalyzes arginine to produce nitric oxide (NO) and citrulline, which have potent mediating antimicrobial and pro-inflammatory activities. Conversely, M2 macrophages upregulate Arg-1, which converts arginine into urea and ornithine, a precursor for polyamine synthesis30. Our results found that CBD reduced NO levels in macrophages following LPS + IFN-γ stimulation but did not affect iNOS expression at either the transcriptional or protein level. Because CBD in M1 macrophages did not induce Arg-1, it is unlikely that the decrease in NO production is caused by competition for substrate between iNOS and Arg-1 and iNOS. It remains unclear how CBD treatment reduces NO production without reducing iNOS or increasing arginase 1 expression.

However, not all M1-related markers were suppressed by CBD treatment. We found the upregulation of antigen presentation molecules in the presence of CBD treatment. MHC-II molecules and CD86 are important cell surface markers of professional antigen-presenting (APC) cells, dendritic cells, and B cells, including macrophages. These molecules play a pivotal role in the presentation of processed antigens to CD4 + T helper cells for activation of adaptive immune responses31. Yekhtin et al.. reported the unexpected finding that CBD treatment induced MHC-II expression in peritoneal macrophages, providing a novel insight that combined cannabinoid and LPS treatments may polarize these macrophages towards an M2b phenotype19. M2b is a subset of M2 macrophage, also known as a regulatory macrophage (Mregs), that can be polarized by TLR agonist (e.g., IL-1β or LPS) in the presence of IgG immune complex. These Mregs highly express the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10, secreting a low level of IL-12. Unlike IL-4-induced macrophages, Mregs exhibit a marked upregulation of MHC-II and co-stimulatory molecules (CD80, CD86), potentially influencing T-cell responses32,33. The population of Mregs arises during the later stage of adaptive immune response, involved with a regulatory role in immune response and limiting inflammation33. More studies are needed to determine whether CBD treatment of M1 macrophages switch the phenotypes toward M2b. Previous study in the effect of CBD on autoimmune reactive T cells showed that CBD treatment regulates memory T cell functions. Interestingly, this study found that CBD promotes T cell exhaustion or tolerance34.

In contrast to the findings by Kozela et al. that reported the inhibitory effect of CBD on NF-κB activation, we did not observe any effect of CBD on NF-κB, MAKP and STAT1 pathways. The discrepancy on the effect of CBD on NF-κB in our study and Kozela et al. may be due to the cells used or the stimuli (LPS vs. LPS + IFN-γ) or both. Interestingly, our results demonstrated that CBD treatment is inhibitory against PI3K/Akt pathways in M1 macrophages. Previous study indirectly linked CBD to Akt in a rat model of myocardial ischemia reperfusion but the target cells were not identified35. In agreement with our work, CBD is reported to alter the inflammatory profiles of LPS-stimulated macrophages partially via inhibiting phosphorylation of mTOR and autophagy23. Because PI3K/Akt and mTOR pathways are tightly linked, it is possible that CBD may interfere with M1 functions via this PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis13. Akt is known to be tightly linked with macrophage metabolism and activation13. Because CBD did not directly interfere with activation of the key M1 signaling pathways (NF-κB, MAPK) or M2 signaling cascade (STAT1), it is possible that CBD may indirectly influence Akt activation via the effect on metabolic pathway.

We also found that CBD also suppressed signature gene of the wound healing M2 macrophages. Thus, the impact of cannabinoids, especially CBD, may be more complicated than just anti-inflammatory effect. Our findings on the effect of IL-4-stimulated macrophages are indicative of the broader impact of CBD on macrophage polarization spectrum. Three markers for wound healing M2 macrophages were markedly reduced in CBD-treated M2 macrophages. In one study, CBD treatment skewed human monocytes toward M2 macrophages36. How CBD suppressed IL-4-induced M2 polarization remains unresolved.

Activated macrophages used a distinct metabolic pathway to control energy production for their effector functions. M1 macrophages rely mainly on glycolysis by upregulation of glycolytic enzymes for rapid energy production to promote inflammatory response and host defense37. Our findings on enhanced glycolysis in M1 macrophages after CBD treatment are surprising. CBD treatment may trigger glycolysis overdrive that may cause the high glucose consumption rate that limits glucose availability, leading to the reduction of the inflammatory phenotype of M1 macrophages. Because some M1 markers are increased upon CBD treatment (MHC class II, CD86), it is likely that CBD may trigger glycolysis to enhance M1 functions, but the negative effect of CBD on other pathways, such as PI3K/Akt, may result in a mixed M1 phenotype.

Unlike M1 polarization, M2 macrophages have fully intact TCA cycle to meet the energy demand for tissue homeostasis and repair38. It facilitates the utilization of glucose and alternative carbon sources, such as lipid metabolites derived from β-oxidation, to fuel the TCA cycle and drive energy production through OXPHOS38. Drummond-Main et al.. reported that CBD at a range of 7–15 µM disrupts the mitochondrial membrane potential by reducing oxygen consumption and ATP production in isolated rat brain mitochondria and glioblastoma cell line39. These findings are in line with Sun and co-workers that demonstrated that CBD modulates mitochondrial function in a dose-dependent manner upon IL-4 stimulation. This modulation leads to a decrease in M2-related markers and enhances tumoricidal effects40. Consistent with these observations, we found that CBD treatment inhibited mitochondrial respiration by decreasing oxygen consumption in oxidative metabolism in M2 macrophages.

A limitation of this study is that the mechanisms by which CBD modulates macrophage polarization were not fully investigated. Cannabinoids primarily signal through two receptors, CB1R and CB2R. In our preliminary analysis, Cnr2, which encodes CB2R, was expressed at higher levels than Cnr1 (CB1R) in BMDMs (Supplementary Figure S6), suggesting that CB2R may serve as the main receptor mediating CBD’s effects in macrophages. Further studies using gene knockdown or knockout approaches are needed to confirm this hypothesis. Furthermore, in vivo studies are warranted to assess the biological impact of CBD, particularly in disease models where pronounced macrophage polarization occurs.

In summary, this study demonstrates the multi-faceted effect of cannabinoids on macrophage polarization and the associated biological mechanisms in BMDMs that are activated towards M1 or M2 phenotypes. However, further investigation is needed to elucidate the specific regulatory roles and functions of cannabinoids in BMDM modulation. Understanding the molecular mechanisms underlying these effects of cannabinoids on macrophage polarization will facilitate the precision use of cannabinoids for medical use in the future.

Materials and methods

Generation of bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs)

Murine BMDMs were generated from hematopoietic stem cells from bone marrow. Briefly, eight-to-ten-week-old C57/BL6 female mice (weight 20–25 g) were purchased from Nomura Siam International (Thailand) for the bone marrow source and maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University. Mice were sacrificed by overdose of isoflurane inhalation and confirmed by cervical dislocation before bone marrow isolation. Bone marrow cells were isolated from the tibia and femur and filtered using a 70 µM cell strainers. For macrophage differentiation, bone marrow cells were cultured in bone marrow macrophage (BMM) media (Dulbecco’ modified Eagle’ medium (DMEM) (Hyclone™, USA) supplemented with 20% (v/v) L929 fibroblast cell line (RIKEN BioResource Center, Ibaraki, Japan) conditioned medium, 5% (v/v) horse serum (Hyclone™, USA), 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Gibco™, USA), 1% (v/v) HEPES (Hyclone™, USA), 1% (v/v) sodium pyruvate (Hyclone™, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (HycloneTM, USA).) On day 4, fresh media (3 ml) was added to the bone marrow cells. Cells were harvested using cold PBS and used for experiments or stored at 80 °C until used on day 7 of differentiation. Differentiated BMDMs were confirmed by staining with anti-CD11b and anti-F4/80 antibody (Biolegend, USA). All experimental procedures involving laboratory animals were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the Faculty of Medicine, Chulalongkorn University, Bangkok, Thailand (approval protocol No. 2491003). All experiments were performed according to the guidelines issued by the IACUC. Animals were monitored daily for health and behavior throughout the study. Humane endpoints were established to lessen the pain and distress of the animals according to ARRIVE and IACUC recommendations. The monitoring was for assessment of body weight, posture, mobility, grooming behavior, food and water intake, and signs of distress. The study is reported in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines.

Cell culture and cannabinoid treatment

BMDMs were seeded in BMM media at a density of 4 × 105 cells/well. For cannabinoid treatment and stimulation, BMM media was discarded and replaced with fresh DMEM complete media supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Hyclone™, USA), 1% (v/v) HEPES (Hyclone™, USA), 1% (v/v) sodium pyruvate (Hyclone™, USA), and 1% penicillin (Hyclone™, USA). Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator (Thermo Electron Corporation, USA) at the indicated time. The cannabinoid structure and chemical formula are shown in Supplementary Table 1. All cannabinoids used in this study were synthesized according to established literature protocols41–45. The identity of all compounds was confirmed by spectroscopic data, including NMR (1H and13C) and mass spectrometry. The purities were determined by reverse-phase HPLC as follows: CBD (> 99%), ∆9-THC (> 87%), ∆8-THC (> 99%), CBG (> 99%), and CBN (> 90%). These compounds were dissolved in Hybrid-MAX™ DMSO (Merck, Germany) to make the final concentration up to 100 mM and aliquoted into small volumes to limit the freeze-thaw cycle and stored at -20 °C until used for cell treatment in further experiments.

MTT assay

To test the cell viability of macrophages during cannabinoid treatment, BMDMs at a density of 2 × 104 cells were cultured in a complete DMEM medium and treated with synthetic cannabinoids at indicated concentrations (10–100 µM) for 1 h. MTT solution (5 mg/ml) was added to each condition at 20 h after cannabinoid treatment. After 4 hours of incubation, the MTT formazan crystals from viable cells were dissolved in 200 µl of DMSO, and the optical density at wavelength 540 nm was measured using a Multi-Skan™ UV-visible microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA).

Cytokine quantification by sandwich ELISA

To investigate the effect of cannabinoids on cytokine production, BMDMs were seeded at density of 4 × 105 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight prior to treatment. BMDMs were pretreated with indicated cannabinoids for 1 h before stimulation with LPS (100 ng/ml, Sigma- Aldrich, USA) and IFN-γ (10 ng/ml, BioLegend, USA). After 24 h of treatment, cell-free supernatants were collected, and the amounts of TNF-α and IL-6 were measured using the ELISA-MAX™ standard set (BioLegend, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction was stopped using 1M H2O2, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multi-Skant™ microplate reader (Thermo Fisher, USA.)

Western blot

BMDMs were pretreated and stimulated as indicated in each experiment. The cells were washed with 1X PBS, pH 7.4, to remove residual serum in the culture medium, followed by aspiration with 1X PBS. Cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% Nonidet P-40, 0.5% Sodium deoxycholate, and 0.1% Sodium dodecyl sulfate) supplemented with 1X protease and 1X phosphatase inhibitor (Cell Signaling Technology, USA). The protein concentration was determined by the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay using Pierce™ protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher, USA). The protein samples were separated on SDS-PAGE, followed by transferring to PVDF membrane (Merck, Germany). The membrane was probed with primary antibodies specific to iNOS, Arg-1, NF-κB, p44/42 Erk, SAPK/JNK, Akt and STAT1 (all were from Cell Signaling Technology, USA). Signals were detected by the chemiluminescence method, and the relative intensity was analyzed by ImageJ. All data of the uncropped images of Western blots are supplied as Supplementary Figure S4.

RT-qPCR

BMDMs were pretreated and stimulated as indicated. Total RNA was extracted by TRIzol™ (Thermo Fischer, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Five hundred nanograms of RNA were converted into cDNA for quantitative PCR. For gene expression analysis, the cDNA template was mixed with iQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA). The specific primers used in this study were listed in Supplementary Table 2. Realtime PCR was performed using CFX connect Real-time system (Bio-Rad, USA). The relative expression of target genes was normalized with Actb using 2(−∆∆CT) method.

Flow cytometry

BMDMs were pretreated and stimulated as indicated. After 24 h of stimulation, cells were harvested using cold 1X PBS and centrifuged at 3,000 g, 4 °C for 5 min. Non-specific binding to Fc receptor was blocked by CD16/32 Fc receptor blocker (BioLegend, USA) at a final concentration of 1 µg per 1 × 106 cells for 15 min. After washing with FACS buffer, the cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies as follows the manufacturer’s instructions for 30 min to target the M1 markers (CD86, MHC-II, and CD40) or the M2 marker (CD206). All antibodies were purchased from BioLegend (USA). The stained cells were analyzed by FC500 flow cytometry (Beckman Coulter, USA), and data were visualized and analyzed using FlowJo v.10 software (Tree Star Inc., USA).

Real-time metabolic profiling

Approximately 2.4 × 104 cells of BMDMs were seeded in a Seahorse XFp cell culture miniplate (Agilent Technology, USA.) in BMMs media overnight. The metabolic profiles of BMDMs were detected using Seahorse XFp Glycolysis Stress for glycolysis and Mito Stress Test Kit for OXPHOS, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. ECAR for glycolysis and OCR for mitochondrial profiles were measured using an Agilent Seahorse XFp analyzer (Agilent Technology, USA).

To assess glycolysis function, BMDMs were pretreated with CBD (20 µM), followed by stimulation with LPS (100 ng/ml) and IFN-γ (10 ng/ml) and the baseline non-glycolysis acidification rate was detected. Following this step, the first injection of concentrated glucose (10 mM) was performed to induce glycolysis. Oligomycin (50 mM) and 2-DG (50 mM) were successively added to confirm that the ECAR signal in this experiment represents the glycolytic function. For mitochondrial function, BMDMs were pretreated with CBD (20 µM), followed by stimulation with IL-4 (100 ng/ml). Oligomycin (1.5 µM), FCCP (1 µM) was added to disrupt the proton gradient and mitochondrial membrane potential, leading to the determination of ATP-linked respiration and maximal respiration, respectively. Finally, a mixture of rotenone and antimycin A (0.5 μm) was added to shut down electron flow in ETC and allowed the calculation of OCR from non-mitochondrial respiration. The data was analyzed using Agilent Seahorse Wave (Agilent Technology, USA).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software version 10. The statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA or unpaired t-test, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.
