En bloc resection of multilevel cervicothoracic junction tumors via a combined posterior and osteomuscular-sparing transmanubrial anterior approach: a technical note and case series
Authors: Jun-Xiao Li, Shang-Bin Zhou, Mei-Ling Xu, Bo Li, Jian-Ru Xiao, Wei Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultilevel localized tumors of the cervicothoracic junction (CTJ) pose significant surgical challenges due to the complex regional anatomy and proximity to vital neurovascular structures. Building upon the pioneering work established the combined transmanubrial and posterior approach for CTJ tumor resection in the 1990s, this study reports our institutional experience with this combined approach for the en bloc resection of multilevel localized primary CTJ tumors.Case presentationThree patients with multilevel localized CTJ tumors underwent the combined posterior and transmanubrial osteomuscular-sparing anterior approach (TMA). The patients included a male with a C7–T2 giant cell tumor, a male with a large osteosarcoma involving the left chest wall at the C6–T2 level, and a female with recurrent C7–T3 epithelioid hemangioendothelioma. Postoperatively, all three cases preserved osteomuscular integrity and one patient exhibited postoperative intrinsic hand muscle weakness secondary to obligatory C8/T1 nerve root sacrifice; the other two patients maintained normal neurological function and spinal stability, with no evidence of spinal dysfunction during follow-up.ConclusionThe combined posterior and transmanubrial osteomuscular-sparing anterior approach is a viable surgical option for patients with multilevel CTJ tumors, especially those with a narrow cervicothoracic angle and anterolateral tumor extension requiring en bloc excision.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04386-4.Keywords: Transmanubrial osteomuscular-sparing anterior approach, Cervicothoracic junction tumors, Multilevel vertebrectomy, En bloc resection

Introduction

The cervicothoracic junction (CTJ) represents a critical anatomical transition zone, spanning from the bilateral transverse processes of C7–T4 to the first rib and sternum [1]. Vertebral neoplasms involving the CTJ with extension into the pulmonary apex, thoracic outlet, and superior mediastinum pose considerable surgical difficulties [2]. Several anterior surgical approaches have been proposed for the cervicothoracic junction. The traditional low anterior cervical approach allows limited tumor resection but is constrained caudally by the cervicothoracic angle (CTA), preventing adequate access below T2 [3]. The anterior cervical transclavicular approach improves exposure to the thoracic outlet, yet clavicular division or sternoclavicular disarticulation often leads to shoulder deformities and functional impairment [4]. While sternotomy enhances anterior visualization and reduces spinal cord manipulation, it introduces risks inherent to transthoracic procedures [2].

To address the inherent anatomical complexity of this region while achieving optimal functional and aesthetic outcomes, the transmanubrial osteomuscular-sparing approach (TMA) was introduced by Grunenwald and Spaggiari in 1997 [5]. This innovative technique provides excellent exposure of the thoracic outlet and cervical structures while preserving clavicular and muscular integrity, thereby avoiding complications including dehiscence, nonunion, and pseudoarthrosis [6]. The combined transmanubrial and posterior approach was subsequently applied to multilevel vertebrectomy involving C7 to T5 in the setting of lung cancer and paravertebral soft tissue tumors with vertebral invasion [7, 8].

In cases of multilevel localized primary spinal bone tumors with anterolateral tumor extension, a solitary anterior or posterior approach often fails to achieve complete resection due to inadequate exposure of either the compromised anterior or posterior spinal column [9]. The present report describes our institutional application of the combined posterior and transmanubrial osteomuscular-sparing anterior approach to three cases of primary tumors at the CTJ and demonstrates that it can be performed safely in specialized centers with appropriate infrastructure and multidisciplinary expertise. This technique, which maintains excellent exposure of the thoracic outlet and cervical structures, facilitates the feasibility of surgical resection and may reduce the incidence of dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Indications and patient selection criteria

Inclusion criteria for combined posterior-transmanubrial approach included: (1) multilevel (≥ 3 levels) tumors at C6-T4 with circumferential involvement; (2) tumors requiring en bloc resection not achievable through isolated anterior or posterior approach; (3) narrowed cervicothoracic angle limiting alternative approaches; (4) patient age < 70 years with ASA score ≤ 3 and ECOG performance status 0–2; and (5) adequate cardiac and pulmonary function for prolonged surgery.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) bilateral vertebral artery involvement; (2) major vascular structures requiring complex reconstruction; (3) severe cardiopulmonary disease; and (4) life expectancy < 6 months.

Patient information

Between September 2020 and Januray 2024, three patients satisfied inclusion criteria. All three patients exhibited tumor invasion between C6 and T3. Among them, two patients presented with primary tumors invading the chest wall or superior mediastinum, while one had a recurrent lesion involved C7-T3. Neurological evaluation utilized manual muscle testing (MRC scale) and the MSTS scoring system. The mean preoperative MSTS score was 23.7. Spinal cord function was evaluated using the Frankel grading system for cord-level deficits; two patients were classified as grade C, and one patient as grade D. For case 3, preoperative neurological examination documented hypoesthesia of the left upper extremity with accentuated deficits in the ulnar nerve territory. Motor examination revealed weakness of left elbow flexion, wrist extensors and flexors, and generalized left hand paresis (MRC grade 2/5) with impaired left thumb opposition.

A comprehensive retrospective review of each patient’s medical records was conducted, including clinical symptoms, histological diagnosis, surgical margin classification, postoperative functional outcomes, and incidence of local recurrence. Surgical procedure outcomes, extent of adjacent tissue involvement, and number of nerve roots sacrificed during surgery were documented (Table 1). The extent of resection was evaluated using the surgical principles dictated by the Enneking classification [10].

Patients were followed postoperatively at 3-month intervals during the first year and every 6 months thereafter for the next 2 years. At each visit, clinical examination and imaging studies were performed and compared with previous results to assess for signs of local recurrence and metastases.

Surgical procedures

The combined approach was selected when: (1) both anterior and posterior column involvement required circumferential visualization, isolated anterior or posterior approach alone would have resulted in gross residual disease or vascular injury risk; (2) tumor extended below T2 or involved mediastinal structures; and (3) alternative approaches offered inferior exposure.

Patient positioning and incision design

Multilevel en bloc spondylectomy entails the release of surrounding neurovascular structures from the tumor mass, en bloc resection of the lesion, posterior instrumentation, and subsequent reconstruction of the anterior column defect [9]. Due to the tumor’s anatomical location and extensive anterior invasion involving the superior mediastinum and chest wall, which significantly impeded clear visualization of the spinal cord, a combined posterior and transmanubrial osteomuscular-sparing anterior approach was adopted in all three cases. The surgical sequences implemented included either a posterior-anterior or an anterior-posterior-anterior strategy, tailored to the individual patient’s anatomical constraints and tumor extent. For the anterior stage, the patient was placed supine with the neck gently extended and the head rotated contralaterally to the operative side. An L-shaped cervicotomy incision was created along the anterior border of the sternocleidomastoid muscle and extended caudally to the manubrial angle (Fig. 1), following the surgical approach originally described by Grunenwald and Spaggiari [5]. The manubrium was then osteotomized in a corresponding L-shaped pattern, with selective resection of the first costal cartilage based on the exposure requirements for elevating the osteomuscular flap. This approach provided a safe and extensive surgical corridor while preserving the integrity of the clavicle and its muscular attachments.

Schematic illustration of the L-shaped cervicotomy based on the surgical approach described by Grunewald et al. a An anterior L-shaped cervicotomy was performed to access the cervicothoracic junction. b Superior mediastinal tumor lesion and adjacent neurovascular structures

Step I: Anterior Transmanubrial Osteomuscular-Sparing Approach

Layered dissection was carried down along the sternocleidomastoid to identify the internal jugular vein, common carotid artery, vagus nerve, and lower portion of the brachiocephalic trunk. The subclavian vessels, brachial plexus, vertebral artery origin, phrenic nerve, and thoracic duct were mobilized under direct vision. By shifting the vascular axis laterally and the tracheoesophageal axis medially, the anterior vertebral plane was exposed. The tumor was then carefully dissected from adjacent soft tissues. The anterior longitudinal ligament and the intervertebral discs immediately above and below the affected vertebrae were transected, as was the posterior longitudinal ligament, to achieve circumferential release. Temporary fixation of the manubrium was performed with braided wires before proceeding. Injury to the left thoracic duct may cause chylothorax; therefore, preemptive ligation is advised when anatomical ambiguity exists.

Step II: Posterior Approach

The patient was turned prone, and a midline posterior incision was made to include the involved vertebra and at least three adjacent segments above and below. Paraspinal muscles were subperiosteally elevated to expose the posterior spinal elements. Pedicle screws were inserted three levels above and below the lesion. Decompressive laminectomies and removal of posterior elements were performed with Kerrison rongeurs and piezosurgery. The posterior longitudinal ligament was transected and residual intervertebral discs were incised. Rib heads attached to the involved vertebrae were cut, and selected thoracic nerve roots were ligated to facilitate mobilization. On the tumor side, the involved roots were sacrificed when necessary. Detailed technical steps are consistent with those described in our previously reported “Changzheng en bloc technique” article [11]. A paramedian fascial incision between the multifidus and longissimus muscles was used to develop a working channel to the lateral tumor margin. Through this corridor, adhesions with pleura or mediastinal structures were dissected sharply under direct vision. The great vessels and their branches were gently retracted anteriorly, demonstrating adequate resistance to tearing. Once circumferential release was achieved, the tumor and involved vertebrae were mobilized, rotated, and removed en bloc. Posterior instrumentation was then completed. Step III: Anterior Reconstruction

The patient was repositioned supine, and through the original anterior incision, anterior column reconstruction was performed. A titanium mesh cage or structural allograft/fibular strut graft was implanted, followed by an anterior plating system to restore stability. This ensured three-column reconstruction with secure fixation. Wounds were closed in layers over deep drains, and a chest tube was placed when necessary.

Results

The combined posterior and transmanubrial osteomuscular-sparing anterior approach was successfully implemented in all three patients with multilevel cervicothoracic localized tumors. Histopathological examination confirmed the following diagnoses: giant cell tumor of bone, osteosarcoma, and recurrent epithelioid hemangioendothelioma. All three cases achieved histologically confirmed marginal en bloc excisions on final pathological assessment. The mean operative time was 21.7 h, accompanied by an average blood loss of 2400 mL. Total vertebrectomy was consistently performed across all cases, involving the resection of three to four vertebral levels. No Clavien–Dindo classification Grade III-V complications, unplanned reoperations, or perioperative deaths were encountered.

Neurologic deficients occurred in case 3, where sacrifice of the left C8/T1 nerve roots was necessitated due to tumor infiltration. Postoperative functional assessment documented severe motor deficits including paralysis of left elbow flexors, absent wrist motion, complete loss of thumb opposition, and near-total paralysis of left hand intrinsic muscles (MRC grade 1/5). Anesthesia was documented in the medial forearm, ring, and little fingers. The other two patients remained free of neurologic deficits and demonstrated functional improvement, with mean postoperative MSTS scores increasing from 23.7 to 25.5. In all cases, the clavicle and sternoclavicular joint were preserved. Clavicular alignment was maintained, and manubrial osteotomy healed without nonunion. Shoulder girdle function was preserved in Cases 1 and 2. All information is summarized in Table 1. Adjuvant therapy was administered based on tumor histology and institutional protocols. No late complications were documented during the follow-up period.

Case 1

A 38-year-old male patient was diagnosed with a giant cell tumor of bone measuring 6.9 × 3.7 cm, involving the vertebral bodies from C7 to T2 with associated paravertebral extension (Fig. 2a). Preoperative imaging studies revealed a suspected mass lesion in the left apical thoracic region. Computed tomography angiography of the vertebral artery revealed involvement of the left common carotid artery and the left subclavian artery (Fig. 2b). Significant tracheal compression with rightward deviation was also observed. The anterior surgical approach was utilized to achieve essential anterior exposure and sufficient release of the subclavian artery, common carotid artery, and scalene muscles (Fig. 2c-d). The remaining surgical procedures are illustrated in Fig. 2 and Figure S1. Immediate postoperative anteroposterior radiograph demonstrated well-positioned and stable anterior and posterior spinal reconstruction following tumor resection (Fig. 2h).

Case 2

A 42-year-old female presented with osteolytic destruction of the C7–T3 vertebrae secondary to recurrent epithelioid hemangioendothelioma. Postoperative radiotherapy for recurrence induced severe fibrotic adhesions. Treatment was subsequently advanced through a two-stage surgical approach. During the anterior stage, a transmanubrial osteomuscular-sparing approach was employed, providing extensive exposure from the cervical region down to the T4 vertebral level. Meticulous sharp dissection was then performed under direct vision to circumferentially isolate and separate the tumor from the surrounding adherent neurovascular structures and mediastinal tissues. No evidence of tumor recurrence was observed during postoperative follow-up (Fig. 3).

Case 3

A male patient presented with a large osteosarcoma located at the left chest wall (C6–T2 level, Fig. 4a and S2). Preoperative evaluation revealed involvement of the left vertebral artery (Fig. 4b-c). Due to limited lateral exposure with a sternotomy-based approach, a transmanubrial anterior approach was utilized to meticulously dissect and separate the vertebral artery from the tumor, followed by ligation of the left C6 to T2 nerve roots. Subsequently, en bloc resection of the C6–T2 vertebrae and the surrounding paravertebral tumor was accomplished via a posterior approach, followed by spinal reconstruction (Fig. 4d-f). The patient died at 18 months of follow-up due to systemic disease progression.

Discussion

The surgical management of cervicothoracic junction tumors is complicated by the deep anatomical location, confinement by surrounding bony structures, and close proximity to major neurovascular vessels including the subclavian arteries, brachial plexus, and thoracic duct. The resultant limited exposure and elevated risk of injury to critical structures collectively hinder both safe and complete tumor resection. Furthermore, in cases where tumors lead to deficiencies in the anterior and middle columns of the spine, the use of a posterior-only approach may exacerbate spinal instability [12]. The traditional low anterior cervical approach, constrained caudally by the cervicothoracic angle, was inadequate for Cases 1–2 which extended to T2-T3 [3]. The transclavicular approach with clavicular division would sacrifice shoulder function, as documented in our institutional experience and published literature [4]. Although sternotomy provides excellent anterior visualization, it introduces significant morbidity from sternal division and lacks adequate lateral exposure required for Case 3’s chest wall involvement [2].

The combined transmanubrial and posterior approach for CTJ tumor resection was pioneered by Grunenwald et al. who published their initial results in 1996 [7] and subsequently reported a series of radical en bloc resections for lung cancer invading the spine across C7 to T5 levels [8]. These seminal contributions established the technical feasibility of the combined approach, demonstrated the oncologic utility of en bloc vertebrectomy at the CTJ, and remain the foundational references for this surgical strategy. The present series implements this established framework specifically for primary spinal bone tumors, with particular attention to patient-tailored surgical strategies and complete visualization afforded by the anterior approach.

Yoshioka et al. [13] and Habibou et al. [14] reported that the lower cervical and upper thoracic vertebrae (C6–T3) can be exposed via a low anterior cervical incision combined with median sternotomy, manubriotomy, or partial clavicular resection (Table 2). However, that the low anterior cervical approach itself is not extensible and offers only limited surgical access from C6 to T2. Furthermore, both median sternotomy and manubriotomy provide constrained caudal exposure beyond the T3 superior aspect with compromised operative working space, further limited by scapular obstruction [15]. Bernstein et al. [16] proposed a muscle-sparing high thoracotomy approach for gross total resection and spinal reconstruction, which provides excellent exposure of the ventral cervicothoracic and upper thoracic spine while avoiding the morbidity associated with transection of shoulder girdle muscles. However, the reported cohort did not involve invasion of the anterior mediastinum or multilevel vertebral segments, thereby limiting this technique broader applicability. Liu et al. [17] introduced a right infraaxillary thoracotomy approach for upper thoracic vertebral pathologies, enabling safe exposure from T2 to T6, though the lack of specifically designed fixation implants remains a limitation.

The combined posterior and transmanubrial osteomuscular-sparing anterior approach provides secure proximal and distal control of the subclavian artery before tumor mobilization, while preserving clavicular and muscular integrity to maintain shoulder girdle function. This technique reduces invasiveness and improves visualization of anterior spinal elements, thereby facilitating safer resection of involved structures and enabling more efficient discectomy, anterior longitudinal ligament resection, and anterior column reconstruction. In this series, three patients had marginal en bloc excisions. This scenario occurred primarily when tumors extensively infiltrated critical anatomical structures such as the vertebral lamina or pedicles, precluding adequate resection margins. In such cases, we employed a sub-marginal resection strategy [11], wherein careful microsurgical dissection meticulously separated the tumor from surrounding neurovascular and skeletal structures prior to vertebral segment removal.

The mean operative time of 21.7 h reflects the substantial technical demands of multilevel en bloc spondylectomy at the CTJ, encompassing staged patient repositioning, comprehensive vascular control and mobilization, multilevel osteotomy, and anterior column reconstruction. Our results are consistent with the original Mazel et al. [1, 18] series of 7 total vertebrectomies (operative time 8.5–20 h, mean 13.2 h; blood loss mean 4071 mL). Our present series achieved comparable operative time but demonstrated substantially reduced blood loss. Although posterior-based specimen retrieval and reconstruction are theoretically possible following complete circumferential release, practical constraints existed in our series. First, anatomical limitations prevented adequate posterior specimen extraction. Second, anterior column reconstruction in close proximity to vital neurovascular structures mandated direct anterior visualization to ensure safe instrumentation placement and prevent vascular injury. Consequently, a distinct anterior surgical stage was necessary.

We strongly recommend that this complex procedure be centralized to high-volume specialized spine oncology centers with dedicated multidisciplinary support including anesthesia, vascular surgery, and thoracic surgery expertise. Literature demonstrates that combined surgical approaches and extensive multilevel spinal segment resection serve as independent predictors of major perioperative complications [19]. Accordingly, reproducibility of this technique outside highly specialized centers with appropriate infrastructure, multidisciplinary coordination, and surgeon experience may be limited.

The brachial plexus demonstrates well-documented inter-individual anatomical variability, including prefixed and postfixed variants, with consequent unpredictability in the functional impact of root sacrifice at the CTJ. In a postfixed plexus, T1 and even T2 contribute meaningfully to intrinsic hand musculature, whereas in a prefixed variant, C8 may predominantly supply these functions. Preoperative electromyography, nerve conduction studies, and brachial plexus MRI are recommended to map root contributions prior to planned nerve root sacrifice. When root sacrifice is unavoidable, the technique of neurotomy with epineural closure and nerve stump embedding into adjacent muscle, as reported by Mazel et al. [20], should be considered to minimize the risk of symptomatic postoperative neuroma formation.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. The relatively small sample size and heterogeneous tumor pathologies restrict the statistical power for detecting differences or rare complications. The short-term follow-up period might not adequately capture long-term outcomes. Additionally, the single-center retrospective design introduces potential selection biases and limits the generalizability of the findings. Therefore, further validation through larger prospective multicenter studies is necessary to establish clinical efficacy, long-term outcomes, and broader applicability of this approach.

In conclusion, the combined posterior and transmanubrial osteomuscular-sparing anterior approach represents a viable surgical option for patients with multilevel cervicothoracic tumors, especially those presenting with a narrow cervicothoracic angle and anterolateral tumor extension requiring en bloc excision. The cases presented demonstrate the technical feasibility of this osteomuscular-sparing, multilevel vertebrectomy technique for carefully selected patients with specific anatomical constraints.
