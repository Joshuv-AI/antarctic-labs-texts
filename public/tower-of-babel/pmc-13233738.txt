Rapidly evolved PD-1 for enhance receptor and checkpoint inhibitor in immunotherapy
Authors: Xiaowei Mei, Xuelong Li, Yasong Wu, Dayan Fu, Fengyu Zhang, Tao Yan, Weiyue Gu, Bin Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPD-1 is a crucial molecule indispensable for maintaining immune homeostasis. Disrupting the interaction between PD-1 and its ligands can significantly enhance the immune system’s ability to combat tumors. By integrating the PD-1 ligand-binding domain with the CD28 signal fragments (switch molecule) on T cells, we can utilize co-stimulatory signals to boost the function of T cells while removing the inhibition of immune checkpoint molecules. However, the extensive application of anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibodies in clinical settings may neutralize the PD-1 switch molecule, thereby restricting its therapeutic potential. In this study, we used sequential mutation and sorting techniques to rapidly evolve a PD-1 molecule that exhibits high affinity for PD-L1 while avoiding the recognition by five of the six clinically approved anti-PD-1 drugs. Experimental results verify that the Fc fusion protein and switch receptor based on such PD-1 mutant enhance the efficacy of therapeutic T cells.Keywords: PD-1 mutation, switch receptor, co-stimulation, checkpoint inhibitor, T cell therapy

Introduction

CD19-specific CAR-T therapies have revolutionized the treatment of B cell cancers, including diffuse large B cell lymphomas and acute lymphoblastic leukemia, now established as standard of care in numerous clinical settings.1 The next frontier of CAR-T therapy is exploring its potential in autoimmune diseases, with systemic lupus erythematosus emerging as a promising target; preliminary clinical data have shown encouraging therapeutic results in this context.2 While CAR-T therapy has achieved remarkable success in hematological malignancies, translation to solid tumors remains challenging due to tumor heterogeneity, immunosuppressive microenvironments, and limited target specificity. A critical area of focus is mitigating the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, a key barrier to solid tumor CAR-T success. Approaches include combinatorial therapies with checkpoint inhibitors and stromal-targeting agents to unleash CAR-T cell activity in complex tumor landscapes.3

PD-1 (programmed cell death-1) is an immune checkpoint protein that regulates T cell activity, maintaining the balance of the immune system by binding to its ligands, PD-L1 (programmed death-ligand 1) or PD-L2.4,5,6,7,8 PD-L1 is overexpressed on the surface of various tumor cells; by binding to PD-1, it inhibits the immune response, enabling tumors to evade immune attacks.9 The intracellular domain of PD-1 contains two independent phosphorylation sites in its C-terminal and N-terminal amino acid residues: the immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motif (ITIM) and the immunoreceptor tyrosine-based switch motif (ITSM).10 Upon binding of PD-1 to PD-L1, the ITIM and ITSM regions are phosphorylated, activating intracellular signaling pathways to achieve immune inhibition. Thus, targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway can restore the immune cell’s anti-tumor activity.11 To overcome immune checkpoint suppression for T cell therapy, combining anti-PD-1 antibodies with T cells has been explored as one strategy. Mac Brenner demonstrated that Keytruda could enhance the efficiency of CAR-T in treating neuroblastoma clinically.12 Researchers at the University of Melbourne found that antibodies could promote CAR-T activation and proliferation, thereby enhancing the tumor-inhibiting effect.13 Addition of Keytruda resulted in more complete response cases than CAR-T alone.14,15 Another strategy involves directly modifying T cells, such as disrupting PD-1 in T cells using CRISPR-Cas9 technology16 or expressing dominant-negative PD-1 extracellular molecules on T cells.17 These strategies can re-establish suppressed T cell function and have shown encouraging effects in early clinical trials.18,19,20

A valuable strategy against the tumor microenvironment is to use an “enhance receptor”, known as “switch molecule” to arm T cells. The enhance receptor is a fusion protein expressed on T cells, including an extracellular segment of PD-1 and a co-stimulatory molecule (e.g., CD28) in the intracellular segment, to reverse the PD-L1 suppressive signal to positive one.21,22,23,24 The strategy has been confirmed effective in hematological tumors clinically.25 For instance, conventional CD19-CAR-T cells armed with PD-1-CD28 switch molecules were used to treat patients with relapsed/refractory B cell lymphomas, achieving significant efficacy.26 Notably, no serious neurotoxic side effect or cytokine release syndrome was observed in 17 adult patients treated. When CD19-CAR-T cells armed with an immune checkpoint switch molecule were used to treat six patients who had failed conventional CD19-CAR-T, half of them achieved complete response efficacy. Qin et al. further explored the mechanism by which the enhance receptor improves CAR-T function: CAR-T cells with enhance receptor improved the antitumor efficacy of anti-mesothelin CAR-T cells both in vitro and in vivo.27 Single-cell RNA sequencing revealed that the enhance receptor promotes the differentiation of CAR-T cells into central memory-like cells, upregulates Th1 gene expression, and reduces Th2 cytokine production through the CD70-CD27 signaling pathway. PD-1 molecules linked to different co-stimulatory signaling molecules have been developed and shown to enhance therapeutic effect of CAR-T based on a similar strategy.28 Wan XS et al. developed a 3-in-1 strategy for T cell therapy that demonstrates effective treatment outcomes for advanced biliary tract cancer.29 However, anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibodies used clinically may neutralize PD-1 switch molecules on CAR-T cells, restricting their application. Furthermore, comparing with anti-PD-L1, an agent targeting both PD-L1 and PD-L2, may offer a significant advantage in terms of overall survival.

In this study, we aim to develop a PD-1 mutant compatible with most current immune checkpoint anti-PD-1 antibodies. This mutated PD-1 must meet two criteria: maintaining or enhancing binding affinity to both PD-L1 and PD-L2 compared to wild-type PD-1 and evading recognition by anti-PD-1 antibody drugs to avoid neutralization. The strategy involves several steps: first, using sequence mutagenesis and sorting techniques to rapidly obtain a PD-1 molecule with enhanced PD-L1 affinity while losing binding to certain anti-PD-1 antibodies, followed by further mutating and screening to identify a PD-1 mutant resistant to neutralization by most clinically approved anti-PD-1 drugs. Studies have demonstrated that this PD-1 mutant potentiates the therapeutic efficacy of T cells when used as a soluble Fc fusion protein or a transmembrane CD28 fusion along with a CAR structure.

Results

Structure-guided evolution of PD-1 for ligand affinity-enhanced variants

Based on the structural information of PD-1/PD-L1, we constructed four sets of lentiviral PD-1 mutant libraries targeting specific loop regions of PD-1 that are critical for binding to PD-L1. These regions include short sequences 70–74, 74–78, 127–131, and 129–133 as illustrated in Figure 1A. We introduced random mutations into these specified regions. The mixed PD-1 mutant library viruses were then used to infect 293T cells. Subsequently, we sorted the cells expressing PD-1 mutants that retained the ability to recognize PD-L1 using PD-L1-immobilized magnetic beads, as shown in Figure 1B. After culturing the 293T cells expressing these PD-1 mutants, we performed flow cytometry analysis on this cell population for their recognitions by anti-PD-1 antibodies. As depicted in Figure 1C, distinct cell populations can be observed in the anti-PD-1 Ab1, Ab2, and Ab4 flow cytometry plots, indicating the presence of some PD-1 mutants that lack binding to corresponding antibodies. To further obtain the sequence information of these PD-1 mutants, the mixed lentivirus-infected 293T cells were sorted by flow cytometry twice to select cells that were positive for PD-L1 binding but did not bind to anti-PD-1 (specific antibodies Ab1, Ab2, and Ab4). Subsequently, the PD-1 mutants in sorted cells were identified by PCR amplification followed by sequencing, as shown in Figure 2A.

Next, 293T cells were directly transfected with the identified PD-1 mutant plasmids, stained with individual antibodies, and subjected to flow cytometry analysis. As depicted in Figure 2B, the clones were verified to retain PD-L1 binding while losing recognition by the specified antibodies (Ab1, Ab2, and Ab4). Further examination through fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis of multiple clones transfected into 293T cells and stained with PD-L1 and various anti-PD-1 antibodies revealed that clones 49 and 56 fully met the criteria of retaining the ability to recognize PD-L1 while not reacting with the specified antibodies Ab1, Ab2, and Ab4, as presented in Figure 2C. Further flow cytometry (FACS) analysis confirmed that mutant 49 and its derivatives exhibited decreased PD-L2 recognition compared to wild-type proteins. The weak binding to PD-L2 by mutant 49 derivatives suggests a potential functional impairment, which could impact their utility in cancer immunotherapy strategies targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway. Consequently, clone 49 was excluded for the subsequent phase of mutation screening (Figure S1).

Identification of PD-1 mutants that are not recognized by more commercial anti-PD-1 therapeutics

In the first round of screening, we obtained clone 56 that retains the ability to bind to PD-L1 molecules while evading recognition by multiple anti-PD-1 antibody drugs. This ensures that the clinical application of this mutant will remain unaffected by widely used immune checkpoint inhibitors. However, it still binds to other three approved antibodies, particularly pembrolizumab (Ab5), a widely used drug internationally for tumor therapy. Given that the structure of the pembrolizumab-PD-1 molecular complex was resolved,30 we decided to initiate a new round of mutations based on the key binding residues derived from the structural analysis. We constructed a new plasmid library, based on clone 56, according to key residues of PD-1 (residues 84–86 and 86–90), which interact with pembrolizumab, using PCR with randomized primers (Figure 3A).

The second round of mutation screening differed from the first. Briefly, we used the initial PD-1 mutation clone 56 as a template to prepare an expression plasmid library. Primers were designed to introduce randomized nucleotides at PD-1/pembrolizumab-binding sites 84–88 and 86–90. The expression plasmid library was directly used for transient transfection of 293T cells. After 48 h, PD-1-positive and Ab5-negative cell populations were isolated via flow cytometry. Plasmids from these cell populations were transformed into competent bacteria, and purified plasmid clones were sequenced to identify new PD-1 mutants (Figure 3B).

Flow cytometry analysis revealed that all the derivatives of clone 56 exhibited similar binding profiles: high-affinity binding to Ab3 and complete evasion of the remaining Ab5 (pembrolizumab) and Ab6 (toripalimab) antibodies (Figure 3C). We did not specifically aim to disrupt the binding between the mutant 56 and its recognition by antibody 6. However, it appears that this antibody binds to epitopes on PD-1 that are similar to those targeted by Ab5 (pembrolizumab). Consequently, the derivatives of the mutant 56 acquire an extra property: they are not recognized by either pembrolizumab (Ab5) or toripalimab (Ab6). Further PD-L1 staining showed that subclones 56-2 and 56-3 lost PD-L2 binding (Table S1), leaving only three candidates, 56-15, 56-16, and 56-108, that met the predefined criteria.

Further characterization of clone 56-108, a PD-1 mutant that binds to PD-L1 with high affinity but not recognized by anti-PD-1 drugs

To further characterize the molecular interactions between PD-1 mutants and their ligands, real-time, label-free Biacore analysis based on surface plasmon resonance (SPR) was conducted. Various PD-1 mutants including wild-type and 108 PD-1were expressed as their corresponding Fc fusion proteins (see Figure 4A). Subsequently, these expressed and purified proteins were immobilized on protein chips, and protein-binding analysis was performed using the Biacore protein interaction instrument, where PD-1 protein ligands PD-L1, PD-L2, and six antibodies function as the mobile phase. It displays on Figures 4B and 4C the real-time binding curves for wild-type PD-1 and 108 PD-1 mutant in interaction with different antibodies and ligands. The affinity constants derived from these curves are presented in Figure 4. Furthermore, subsequent competitive inhibition assays with PD-1-Fc fusion proteins revealed that the 56-16 performed significantly worse (Figure 5B; detailed in subsequent paragraphs). Given the sequence similarity between 56-15 and 56-16, subclone 56-108 (hereafter referred to as “108”) emerged as an optimal candidate.

In Biacore experiments, it was further verified that 108 PD-1 mutant maintains its binding ability to PD-L1, while being no longer recognized by five commercially available antibody drugs. Intriguingly, the affinity constant of the PD-1 mutant 108 for PD-L1 is significantly higher than that of the wild-type PD-1 molecule, increasing from an initial 8.9 μM to an impressive 0.86 nM. This remarkable enhancement establishes a solid foundation for the development of this molecule in various therapeutic contexts.

PD-1 mutant 108 effectively blocks PD-1-mediated immune checkpoint regulation

As a PD-1 mutant with enhanced binding affinity for both PD-L1 and PD-L2, 108 is hypothesized to outperform existing immune checkpoint inhibitors in unleashing anti-tumor immunity. We first expressed the 108-Fc fusion protein using a mammalian cell expression system. Following expression, the protein was purified via sequential Protein A (left panel, lane 2) and Superdex 200 (right panel, lane 3) chromatography, yielding homogenous 108-Fc (Figure 5A).

Next, we investigated the ability of 108-Fc to block PD-1/PD-L1 interaction in vitro. PD-1-CD28-expressing T cells were co-cultured with target cells J82-PD-L1 bladder cancer cells (PD-L1 over-expressing). Engagement of PD-1/CD28 by PD-L1 activates CD28 signaling, inducing IL-2 secretion. Addition of 108-Fc or anti-PD-1 antibodies (e.g., tislelizumab) dose-dependently blocked PD-L1 binding and inhibited IL-2 production (Figure 5B). At optimal concentrations, 108-Fc completely abrogated IL-2 secretion, outperforming the mutant 56#-16 and matching tislelizumab efficacy. Irrelevant IgG4-Fc control does not affect IL-2 levels, confirming specificity. Figure 5C illustrates how PD-L1 expression status influences IL-2 secretion in T cells under different treatment conditions. In the 3M-PDL1-KO (knockout) group, both untreated T cells (T) and 108-T-treated T cells (108-T) maintain very low IL-2 levels, close to baseline. In contrast, in the 3M-PDL1-OE (over-expression) group, while untreated T cells (T) still show minimal IL-2 production, 108-T-treated T cells (108-T) exhibit a pronounced and substantial increase in IL-2 secretion, rising to well over 300 pg/mL. These results indicate that the 108-T treatment robustly induces IL-2 secretion in T cells only when PD-L1 is over-expressed, and that PD-L1 knockout completely eliminates this response, highlighting PD-L1 as a critical mediator of the IL-2 induction triggered by 108-T.

Figure 5E provides definitive evidence that 108-Fc is functionally active in suppressing tumor growth within the MC38-hPD-L1 C57 mouse model, with its activity tied directly to engagement of its intended target (human PD-L1, hPD-L1). In C57/hPD-L1 mice (engineered to express hPD-L1) treated with 108-Fc (administered 3× weekly for 2 weeks), individual tumor-bearing subjects showed rapid regression of baseline tumors (∼100–200 mm3) within ∼20 days, and volumes remained stably suppressed near 0 mm3 over the 50-day observation period. Such sustained anti-tumor effect directly demonstrates 108-Fc’s functional activity in the target-expressing model. Critically, 108-Fc exerted no inhibitory effect in wild-type C57/mPDL1 mice (which express only murine PD-L1, mPDL1): tumors in these subjects grew unchecked, with volumes exceeding 2,000 mm3 in some cases by day 50, confirming 108-Fc’s function is specific to human PD-L1 (rather than non-specific off-target activity). The positive control (atezolizumab, which recognizes both hPD-L1 and mPDL1) inhibited tumor growth in most C57 mice, validating the model’s responsiveness to PD-L1-targeted therapies—thus reinforcing that 108-Fc’s activity in the hPD-L1-expressing cohort reflects its inherent functionality. Collectively, these results confirm that 108-Fc is fully functional in this tumor model, delivering potent, target-dependent suppression of MC38 tumor growth.

Chimeric enhance receptor based on a PD-1 mutant to improve efficacy of therapeutic T cells

The core objective of this research was to engineer a PD-1 mutant that retains ligand-binding capacity while evading recognition by clinically widely used anti-PD-1 antibodies. This mutant was utilized to construct a PD-1-CD28 fusion protein as an enhanced receptor, which converts the inhibitory signal of native T cell PD-1 upon PD-L1 binding into a stimulatory CD28-mediated signal. To assess the effect of this PD1 variant-based enhance receptor on T cell function in vitro, IL-2 secretion experiments were conducted. Malme-3M tumor cells overexpressing human PD-L1 (3M-PDL1-OE) and Malme-3M tumor cells with human PD-L1 knocked out (3M-PDL1-KO) served as target cells. Effector cells included T cells expressing the enhance receptor designated 108-T in Figure 5C and unmodified T cells (T) as controls. As shown in the Figure 5C, when target cells lacked PD-L1 (3M-PDL1-KO), neither control T cells nor 108-T cells (armed with the PD-1 variant receptor) secreted IL-2 (red bars), due to the absence of a second signal stimulus. In contrast, when co-cultured with PD-L1 highly expressing target cells (3M-PDL1-OE), 108-T cells released significantly higher levels of IL-2 upon stimulation. This indicates that the 108-CD28 fusion protein, as an enhance receptor, effectively transmits stimulatory signals to activate T cells. Similarly, 108 fused wiht CD28 as an enhance receptor also potentiates TCR-engineered T cells to eliminate MHC-peptide positive target J82 cells (Figure 5D).

As depicted in Figure 6A, we generated Claudin18.2-targeting CAR-T cells expressing 108-CD28 enhancer receptor to evaluate its ability to augment tumor-suppressing function against SNU-620 (Claudin-18.2-positive) cells. In animal models (Figure 6B), both Claudin18.2 CAR-T cells with and without the enhancer receptor inhibited tumor growth; however, Claudin18.2 CAR-T cells armed with 108-CD28 enhancer receptor sustained tumor growth suppression or induced complete tumor elimination, whereas conventional CAR-T cells showed incomplete inhibition, with tumor recurring after an initial regression; this demonstrates the enhancer receptor’s role in optimizing therapeutic T cell function. In Figure 6C, the tumor growth curves for individual mice within each group are presented. Regarding safety, it is noteworthy that CAR-T cells targeting Claudin18.2 do not induce detectable adverse effects (Figure 6D). The weight curves of mice in the noTD, MZOL-CAR-T, and V4E-MZOL-CAR-T groups remain relatively stable over the observation period after treatment. Meanwhile, the body temperatures of the mice in these groups also show no significant fluctuations and stay within the normal range. These stable weight and temperature profiles suggest that the CAR-T therapies (MZOL-CAR-T and V4E-MZOL-CAR-T) are well tolerated in mice, providing preliminary evidence for their safety at the preclinical stage.

Assessment of off-target binding of 108-Fc in human tissues via immunohistochemical staining

To evaluate the potential off-target binding of 108-Fc in human tissues, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining was performed using human tissue microarrays comprising three distinct tissue sets. The experimental design was divided into three groups: an experimental group treated with 108-Fc (labeled “T1” in the IHC assay), a positive control group using durvalumab (an anti-PD-L1 antibody, labeled “control”), and a negative control group treated with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

IHC results demonstrated that 108-Fc and durvalumab exhibited identical staining patterns across all tissue types (Figure S2). Specifically, both reagents showed no reactivity in critical organs such as the heart, brain, liver, and skin. Conversely, positive staining was observed in the thymus, thyroid, reproductive organs (including breast, placenta, ovaries, and testes), and the gastrointestinal tract (stomach and intestine). These findings indicate that 108-Fc shares a tissue distribution profile with durvalumab, suggesting minimal off-target effects and supporting its potential specificity for intended biological targets.

Discussion

The primary methods for rapidly evolving the affinity of mammalian cell proteins include directed evolution, which simulates natural evolutionary processes by artificially inducing mutations and screening to enhance affinity31; error-prone PCR,32 which introduces random mutations to increase variant diversity; DNA shuffling, which facilitates the recombination and mutation combination of different gene fragments33; and techniques such as phage display and mammalian cell surface display.34 In this paper, affinity-directed mutation guided by protein crystal structure, coupled with mammalian cell display and sorting, are employed to rapidly evolve the PD-1 protein. The generated PD-1 exhibits increased affinity for PD-L1 while simultaneously destroying recognition by commonly used anti-PD-1 antibody drugs.

Maute et al. engineered PD-1’s extracellular domain via yeast surface display to make high-affinity (110 pM) competitive antagonist of PD-L1.35 However, PD-1 and its mutations have primarily been engineered to enhance binding affinity to their ligands. Other properties, such as recognition by anti-PD-1 antibodies, have not been fully considered in these developments. The administration of widely applicable anti-PD-1 antibodies can neutralize PD-1 and its fusion constructs, which limits the application of any products using PD-1 as a building block. The PD-1 mutant developed in this study presents several advantages that could significantly impact the field of cancer immunotherapy. These advantages include its ability to bind or not bind to commercial anti-PD-1 drugs, improved binding affinities with ligands PD-L1 and PD-L2, and the potential for constructing other functional molecules such as enhance receptors. (1) Non-binding to commercial anti-PD-1 drugs: the PD-1 mutant’s ability to avoid binding to five of six commercial anti-PD-1 drugs is a significant advantage. This characteristic allows for the combination therapy approach where the PD-1 mutant can be used alongside these antibodies without competitive inhibition or neutralization, potentially enhancing the overall efficacy of the treatment regimen. (2) Improved binding affinities with PD-L1 and PD-L2: the PD-1 mutant shows equally significant improvements in binding affinities with both PD-L1 and PD-L2 ligands. This enhancement is crucial because it not only relieves the inhibition provided by PD-L1 but also mitigates the inhibitory signals generated by PD-L2 action. Given that PD-L2 is expressed on cells like dendritic cells (DCs) that are directly involved in the immune response, the PD-1 mutant’s ability to enhance immune function more effectively than traditional PD-1 inhibitors could lead to better clinical outcomes. The high-affinity PD-1 variant demonstrated superior tumor penetration and effectiveness in treating both small and large tumors compared to anti-PD-L1 antibodies, highlighting the potential of non-antibody therapeutics for enhanced cancer immunotherapy. (3) Smaller molecular weight and functional versatility: compared to antibody drugs such as anti-PD-L1, the PD-1 mutant contains only one protein functional unit and has a smaller molecular weight. This feature not only facilitates easier delivery and penetration into tumor tissues but also allows for the construction of other functional molecules, such as enhance receptor, to play a role in immune modulation. The smaller size of the PD-1 mutant may also contribute to reduced immune-related adverse events commonly associated with larger monoclonal antibodies. In conclusion, the PD-1 mutant developed in this study offers promising advantages over current therapeutic options for cancer immunotherapy. Its non-binding nature to certain commercial drugs, improved binding affinities with key ligands, and small molecular weight make it a versatile tool in the development of therapeutic products to fight against cancer. Further research and clinical trials are warranted to fully evaluate its safety profile, efficacy, and potential as a combination therapy component.

The initial findings of this research demonstrate that the mutant can be expressed as an Fc fusion protein for use as an immune checkpoint inhibitor. Furthermore, the mutant can be assembled into an advanced receptor that targets PD-L1/2 ligands within the tumor microenvironment, converting the inhibitory signals into activating ones and significantly boosting the activity of T cells. A different type of immune checkpoint inhibitor could be developed drawing from the benefits observed with the PD-1 mutant in this study. This has been validated through animal experiments, demonstrating its efficacy in effectively suppressing tumor growth. The mutant’s robust binding capabilities to both PD-L1 and PD-L2 are notable, especially since PD-L2 is present on professional antigen-presenting cells like DCs and phagocytes. This enables the mutant to exert a more potent immune-enhancing effect. Furthermore, the mutant can specifically act on tumor cells and other related cells without influencing the normal immune regulatory function, making it more advantageous.

The screening process for PD-1 mutants that meet specific criteria has also revealed key amino acids crucial for PD-1’s recognition of various ligands. In the initial mutant library, following magnetic bead sorting and flow cytometry (FACS) sorting, no mutants with amino acid changes between positions 74–78 were identified. This suggests that this region is critical for the binding site where PD-1 interacts with PD-L1, and any alteration in these amino acid residues significantly impacts the recognition ability of the molecule pair. Consistent with the crystal structure of the complex,36 after multiple rounds of screening, no mutants that weakened the binding of tislelizumab to PD-1 were found. The likely reason for this is that the recognition site of tislelizumab and PD-1 is highly consistent with that of PD-L1 and mutations affecting the binding ability of tislelizumab and PD-1 would also affect the binding ability of PD-1 and PD-L1.

In summary, this study developed a method to engineer PD-1 mutants via structure-guided mutation and mammalian cell display, yielding variants with enhanced PD-L1/PD-L2 binding and reduced recognition by most anti-PD-1 antibodies. These mutants show promise as checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., via Fc fusion) or components of enhanced receptors that boost T cell activity. In animal models, the results confirm that the 108-CD28 group exhibits a statistically significant reduction in tumor volume at days 13–15 (p < 0.05), indicating a faster tumor regression process with demonstrated anti-tumor efficacy. The screening also identified critical amino acid regions (e.g., 74–78) for ligand binding and confirmed overlap between PD-L1 and tislelizumab recognition sites, offering insights into PD-1 interactions.

Limitations include the time-consuming initial construction of stable cell line libraries, which may restrict high-throughput screening. While preclinical efficacy is validated, the mutant’s safety, long-term effects, and performance across diverse tumor models or immunocompetent hosts remain uncharacterized. Additionally, the mechanisms behind its selective antibody avoidance and potential immunogenicity require further study.

Future work will focus on optimizing library construction to enable high-throughput screening (e.g., via refined transient transfection protocols). Preclinical investigations should evaluate efficacy in combination with anti-PD-1 antibodies and in tumor models with high PD-L2 expression, given the mutant’s enhanced binding to this ligand. Structural biology approaches could further fine-tune critical ligand-binding regions (e.g., residues 74–78) to improve binding affinity or biostability. Concurrently, translational studies will prioritize pharmacokinetic profiling, immunogenicity assessment, and long-term safety evaluations to facilitate clinical translation of the PD-1 mutant-based therapeutics.

Materials and methods

Antibodies and reagents

Commercially purchased antibodies and reagents included checkpoint inhibitor antibody drugs (Table 1), anti-human CD3 OKT3 (Takara, Japan), human IL-2 (Quanqi, China), X-VIVO 15 serum-free medium (Lonza, USA), CliniMACS Anti-Biotin Microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany), Ficoll-Paque solution (MD Pacific, China), and 7-AAD (BioLegend, USA). Atezolizumab (anti-PD-L1) and durvalumab (anti-PD-L1) were obtained from commercial sources.

Checkpoint inhibitor antibody drugs

Biotinylation of PD-1 monoclonal antibodies: the PD-1 antibody solution was exchanged into 0.1 M PBS buffer. An appropriate amount of antibody was added to a 10 mM NHS-LC-LC-biotin (Sigma, China) solution containing 20-fold molar excess of biotin, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature (RT) with gentle agitation for 2 h. The reaction mixture was then passed through a PD-10 desalting column (Cytiva, China), washed, equilibrated, loaded, and eluted to obtain biotinylated PD-1 monoclonal antibodies.

108-IgG4-Fc (108-Fc) fusion protein was generated by transient co-transfection of the plasmid into ExpiCHO-S cells (Thermo Fisher, China). The supernatant containing the fusion was purified using Protein A affinity chromatography according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The Protein A-purified fraction was desalted into PBS using 50-kDa ultrafiltration disks, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min to remove aggregates. Finally, gel permeation chromatography using Superdex 200 pg (GE Healthcare, China) was performed to eliminate high-molecular-weight species.

Cell lines and culture conditions

Various cell lines were used in this study, including MC38-hPD-L1 generated by lentiviral transduction of hPD-L1 into ATCC-purchased MC38 cells. 293T and SNU-620 from ATCC, were routinely cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C under 5% CO2.

J82-PDL1 tumor cell line: the pLV2-PDL1 vector—a lentiviral construct encoding human PD-L1—was employed to generate this cell line as described below. The human bladder cancer cell line J82 (obtained from the Institute of Basic Medical Sciences, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences; catalog no. 1101HUM-PUMC000346) was transduced with the pLV2-PDL1 lentivirus, resulting in the establishment of J82-PDL1 cells that stably express human PD-L1.

PD1+T cells: frozen whole blood cells from healthy donors were rapidly thawed in a 37°C water bath, added to 5 times the volume of preheated T cell medium (X-VIVO 15 containing 0.5% human serum albumin), mixed well, centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min, and the supernatant was removed. The cells were resuspended in PBS-0.5% HSA to a density of 108 total cells/mL. Biotin-labeled anti-PD1 antibody (Biotin-anti-PD1, BioLegend) was added and incubated at 4°C for 20 min and washed twice with PBS-0.5% HSA. Anti-biotin microbeads (Anti-Biotin Microbeads, Miltenyi) were added and incubated at 4°C for 20 min and washed twice with PBS-0.5% HSA. PD1+T cells were sorted using an LS sorting column (Miltenyi) and cultured in T cell medium.

Claudin18.2 CAR-T cells and 108-Claudin18.2 CAR-T cells: a lentiviral vector pLV2-Claudin 18.2 CAR containing the CAR coding sequence, which specifically recognizes the Claudin 18.2 antigen, and a lentiviral vector containing the PD1 variant enhanced receptor PD1-CD28 (abbreviated as 108-CD28) and the CAR coding sequence were prepared. The lentiviruses were used to infect the PD1+T cells (described above) to make cell lines.

3M-PDL1-OE target cells were constructed by infecting Malme-3M cells with lentivirus expressing human PD-L1 (pLV2-PDL1). The 3M-PDL1-KO cell line was constructed with standard CRISPR knockout technique. Briefly, guide RNA targeting the human PD-L1 gene was designed and cloned into a CRISPR vector. The vector was then transfected into Malme-3M cells, and cells with successful PD-L1 gene knockout were selected using appropriate screening methods.

PD-1 mutant plasmid libraries

The starting plasmid template was pLV2-PD-1-CD28 (from our company’s storage), a lentiviral vector containing the PD-1 signal peptide and extracellular region (155 amino acids; sequence: MQIPQAPWPVVWAVLQLGWRPGWFLDSPDRPWNPPTFSPALLVTEGDNATFTCSFSNTSESFVLNWYRMSPSNQTDKLAAFPEDRINRSQDCRFRVTQLPNGRDFHMSVRARRNDSGTYLCGAISLSTPVWIKESLRAELRVTERRAEVPTAH) fused to the CD28 transmembrane and intracellular signaling regions (80 amino acids; sequence: CPSPLFPGPSKPFWVLVVVGGVLACYSLLVTVAFIIFWVRSKRSRLLHSDYMNMTPRRPGPTRKHYQPYAPPRDFAAYRS).

The PD-1 mutation library was constructed in two rounds. Round 1 targeted amino acids 70–74, 74–78, 127–131, and 129–133; round 2 targeted 84–88 and 86–90. Briefly, fragment 1 (PD-1 signal peptide and upstream sequence) and fragment 2 (PD-1 mutant sequence and CD28) were amplified via PCR using PrimeSTAR Max Premix (2×) (Takara, Japan) with primers containing NNK codons. Cycling conditions were as follows: 98°C for 2 min; 30 cycles of 98°C for 10 s, 55°C for 10 s, and 72°C for 15 s; final extension at 72°C, for 2 min. PCR products were gel-purified using EasyPure Quick Gel Extraction Kit (Transgen, Beijing). Equimolar Fragment ½ mixture underwent fusion PCR under identical conditions as described above. The fusion PCR products and pLV2-PD-1-CD28 plasmid were double digested with Sal I and Xba I (NEB, England) at 37°C for 15 min, purified, and ligated at a 5:1 insert:vector molar ratio with T4 DNA ligase (Transgen, China). Ligation product was transformed into Escherichia coli Trans1-T1 competent cells (Transgen, China), plated on 150-mm ampicillin LB agar plates, and incubated at 37°C for 16–20 h. Random clones were sequenced to confirm mutagenesis. The four regional mutation libraries yielded ∼2 × 106 clones in total. Colonies were scraped and pooled and plasmids extracted using PureYield Plasmid Midiprep System (Promega, England). Library concentration was measured via microspectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher). Primers are listed in Table 2.

PD-1 mutant lentiviral libraries

One day prior to transfection, 2 × 107 293T cells (ATCC, USA) were inoculated into T175 culture flasks in DMEM medium (Viva CELL, China) supplemented with 10% FBS (Gibco, China) and 1× penicillin-streptomycin-gentamycin triple antibiotics (Solarbio, China). For transfection, 5 μg of pCMV-VSVG and 15 μg of dR8.91 plasmids (kind gift from Prof. Liu Changzhen, China Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine, Beijing) and 20 μg of the PD-1 mutant plasmid library were diluted to 1 mL of DMEM. Separately, 120 μL of 1 μg/μL PEI (Polysciences, USA) was diluted in 1 mL DMEM. The plasmids and PEI solution were mixed, added to cells, and incubated for 4 h before replacing the medium. After 48 h, virus-containing supernatant was collected, centrifuged at 2,000 × g for 5 min, filtered through a 0.45-μm membrane, and ultracentrifuged at 100,000 × g for 2 h at 4°C. The lentiviral pellet was resuspended in RPMI 1640.

FACS and magnetic bead sorting for desired PD-1 mutant

One day prior to lentivirus infection, 1 × 107 293T cells were seeded into a T175 flask with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1× penicillin-streptomycin-gentamicin (Solarbio). During infection, concentrated PD-1 mutant library lentivirus was added; after 24 h, complete medium was replaced. At 48 h post-infection, cells were dissociated, 5 × 105 cells were resuspended in 1 mL PBS (Viva Cell), centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min, and resuspended in 100 μL PBS. Next, 2.5 μL anti-PD-1 labeled with PE (BioLegend) was added, incubated at RT in the dark for 30 min, washed three times with PBS, and resuspended in 100 μL PBS for flow cytometry to assess infection efficiency.

For magnetic bead sorting, 3 × 106 infected cells were resuspended in 1 mL PBS, incubated with biotin-labeled PD-L1-Fc (500 ng/tube, ACRObiosystems) at 4°C for 15 min, washed twice with 1 mL PBS containing 2% (w/v) FBS (Gibco), resuspended in 1 mL of PBS, and incubated with Dynabeads M-280 Streptavidin Beads (Thermo Fisher) for 30 min at RT. The cells were magnetically separated, non-adsorbed cells discarded, and beads were washed three times with 1 mL 2% FBS-PBS before transfer to a 6-well plate for culture in DMEM (Viva Cell) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1× penicillin-streptomycin-gentamicin tri-antibiotic (Solarbio).

FACS sorting was performed as follows: 3 × 106 293T cells expressing PD-1 mutant library were incubated with 200 ng PD-L1-Fc (R&D Systems) and DyLight650-anti-IgG Fc (Abcam) at 4°C for 30 min. After washing, cells were co-incubated with 200 ng each of biotin-anti-PD-1 (clones 1#, 2#, and 4#) and 1 μg streptavidin-PE at 4°C for 30 min. Following a final wash, cells were resuspended in 100 μL PBS and flow-sorted to collect 3,000 cells bound to PD-L1 but not the three antibodies. The collected cells were cultured in 24-well plates and expanded before transfer to larger culture vessels.

Molecular cloning and validation were performed as follows: 8 × 105 cells after expansion were used for genomic DNA extraction with Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (TIANGEN). The target fragment was amplified from genomic DNA using P1-For and P1-Rev primers (Table 2); amplified DNA was recovered and digested with Sal I and Xba I and ligated into the similarly digested pLV2-PD-1-CD28 vector. After E. coli transformation, single clones were sequenced. Clones with successful mutations at the target site (without frameshift or premature stop codons) were selected, and plasmids were extracted for lentivirus packaging. Lentiviruses were used to infect 293T cells and T cells. Flow cytometry analysis was performed 48 h (293T) and 10 days (T cells) post-infection to verify binding of PD-1 mutants to PD-L1 and the six antibodies in Table 1.

Biacore SPR binding kinetics analysis

SPR analysis was performed using the Biacore T200 system (Cytiva) to characterize the binding kinetics of PD-1 and its mutant (clone 108) against PD-L1, PD-L2, and six clinically approved anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody drugs. Biotinylated ligands including PD-L1-Fc (Sino Biologicals), PD-L2-Fc (ACRObiosystems), sintilimab (Innovent), nivolumab (BMS), tislelizumab (BeiGene), camrelizumab (Hengrui), pembrolizumab (MSD), or toripalimab (Junshi) were immobilized on streptavidin-coated chips (Cytiva) as the stationary phase. Purified PD-1 or its mutant (clone 108) fusion protein was used as the mobile phase in HBS-EP buffer (Cytiva) at 30 μL/min. Analyte samples were serially diluted from 0.5 to 64 nM (0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, or 64 nM). The association and dissociation phases were monitored for 120 and 600 s, respectively. Sensorgram data were collected and analyzed using Biacore T200 Evaluation Software to derive association (ka) and dissociation (kd) rate constants, with the equilibrium dissociation constant KD = kd/ka.

IL-2 secretion assay

In a 96-well plate, Malme-3M tumor cells overexpressing human PD-L1 (3 M-PDL1-OE) and Malme-3M tumor cells with human PD-L1 knocked out (3 M-PDL1-KO) were used as target cells, and T cells expressing the enhanced receptor composed of PD1 variant (mutant 108) and CD28 (shown as 108-T in Figure 5, also called 108-CD28-T) and unmodified T cells (T) were used as effector cells. They were co-cultured in a medium supplemented with 3 ng/mL PMA (phorbol ester) at a ratio of target cells:108-T = 10:1, i.e., 1 × 105 target cells/well and 1 × 104 108-T cells/well. The supernatant after overnight culture was used as the sample to be tested, and IL-2 ELISA analysis was performed together with the detection standard.

The human IL-2 detection kit (ELISA method) (Dakewei, cat. no. 1110203) was used. The microplate was pre-coated with anti-human IL-2 capture antibody. The standard or sample tested and the detection antibody (biotin-labeled anti-human IL-2 antibody) were added to the microplate at the same time, and then horseradish peroxidase-labeled streptavidin (SA-HRP) was added, which specifically binds to biotin in the immune complex. The enzyme substrate tetramethylbenzidine was added, and a blue color appeared. After 5–10 min of color development, the stop solution was added to terminate the reaction, and the light absorption value (OD) at 450 nm was read by a multifunctional microplate reader. The human IL-2 concentration was proportional to the OD value within a certain range. The standard curve was obtained by ELISA detection using IL-2 standard, and the IL-2 concentration in each co-culture was calculated using the standard curve.

Tissue microarray staining

A human normal tissue microarray derived from normal human tissues was used for the immunohistochemical staining experiment at Shanghai Wei Ao Biotechnology Co. Briefly, the procedure commenced with meticulous preparation of tissue chip section from three distinct human donors. The sections were then carefully mounted onto slides, followed by a baking step to ensure firm adhesion by heating at an optimal temperature. Next, dewaxing step was performed using reagents like xylene to remove paraffin from the tissue sections, succeeded by hydration through a series of gradient ethanol solutions. Subsequent steps included antigen retrieval, inactivation of endogenous peroxidase, and blocking of non-specific binding sites. Test and control samples were diluted under standard laboratory conditions: both 108-Fc (labeled T1 in IHC) and the control (durvalumab, labeled control) were diluted with PBS to a final concentration of 5 μg/mL. The chips were then stained separately by 108-Fc, anti-PD-L1 durvalumab, and PBS control. Post-staining steps included washing, secondary antibody incubation, color development, counterstaining, dehydration, clearing, and cover-slipping. Sealed slices were observed under a microscope and scanned for imaging.

In vivo assessment of tumor growth suppression by 108-Fc

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Chineo Medical Technology and conducted in compliance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Male C57BL/6 were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and male PD-L1 humanized mice on a C57BL/6 background were sourced from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. (SMOC, Shanghai). 5 × 105 MC38-hPD-L1 cells in 100 μL phosphate buffered saline (PBS)were subcutaneously injected into the left dorsal flank of each mouse. Once tumors reached a volume of 70–120 mm3, mice were randomized into treatment groups. Antibodies or 108-Fc fusion protein (200 μg/100 μL) were intravenously administered three times weekly for 2 weeks. Tumor sizes were measured biweekly using a caliper, and volumes were calculated using the formula V= (L × W2)/2, where V = volume, L = length, and W = width.

In vivo antitumor activity of 108-CD28 enhanced CAR-T cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells ([PBMCs], 0.5–2 × 107) were cultured in X-VIVO 15 media (Lonza) supplemented with 10% FBS (Viva CELL) and 1,000 IU/mL interleukin-2 (IL2, SL Pharm). T cells were activated using CD3/CD28 magnetic beads (Thermo Fisher) at a 3:1 bead-to-PBMC ratio. After 24 h, lentiviral particles encoding either Claudin18.2 targeting CAR or Claudin18.2 targeting CAR plus 108-CD28 (V4E) were added for transduction in separate groups. Following 3–5 days culture, magnetic beads were removed, and T cells were further expanded for 8–12 days before harvest and cryopreserved.

NTG mice used in the study were purchased from SPF Biotech. (Beijing, China) and were constructed by CRISPR-Cas9-mediated IL2rg knockout in NOD-SCID mice. The SNU620 cell line was obtained from COBIOER Biosciences (Nanjing, China) and was cultured under the RPMI-1640 + 10% FBS medium. RPMI-1640 and FBS were purchased from VivaCell (Shanghai, China). The Matrigel was from Abwbio (Shanghai, China).

For SNU620 cell-derived xenograft (CDX) models, 2.5 million tumor cells were mixed with equal volume of Matrigel and injected subcutaneously into NTG mice. CAR-T cell intravenous infusion via the tail vein was carried out when the tumor size reaches an average of 100 mm3; 5 million CAR-positive T cells per mice or equivalent number of no-transduced T cells (noTD-T) were used. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula described above. Mouse body weight, temperature and tumor volume were measured twice weekly. Mice were sacrificed when their tumor volume exceeds 1,500 mm3.
