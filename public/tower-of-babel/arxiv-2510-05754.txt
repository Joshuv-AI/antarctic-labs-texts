Gamification of T 0 T_{0} -pseudoweight: cut-and-choose games on topological spaces
Lucas Chiozini, Tamás Csernák, Lajos Soukup

Abstract
. We study transfinite cut-and-choose games on T 0 T_{0} spaces, introducing the point-separating number ps ⁡ ( X ) \operatorname{ps}(X) and the set membership number sm ⁡ ( X ) {\operatorname{sm}}(X) as ordinal-valued invariants measuring the minimal length of a game in which a Seeker can determine a hidden point or subset. A central motivating question is which countable ordinals can occur as the value of ps ⁡ ( X ) \operatorname{ps}(X) , in particular whether any countable ordinal can arise. These invariants are inspired by Scott’s T 0 T_{0} -pseudoweight ψ ​ w 0 \operatorname{\psi w_{0}} . Fundamental inequalities are established relating ps ⁡ ( X ) \operatorname{ps}(X) , sm ⁡ ( X ) {\operatorname{sm}}(X) , ψ ​ w 0 ⁡ ( X ) \operatorname{\psi w_{0}}(X) , and | X | |X| , including the sharp bounds | X | ≤ 2 ps ⁡ ( X ) |X|\leq 2^{\operatorname{ps}(X)} and ψ ​ w 0 ⁡ ( X ) ≤ 2 < ps ⁡ ( X ) \operatorname{\psi w_{0}}(X)\leq 2^{<\operatorname{ps}(X)} . The values of these invariants are computed for familiar spaces such as Cantor cubes, powers of the Alexandroff double arrow space, and certain stationary subsets of cardinals. Finally, we further investigate their behavior under topological sums and products, revealing the striking contrast between ps \operatorname{ps} and sm {\operatorname{sm}} . For metric spaces, we determine that ps ⁡ ( X ) = log ⁡ | X | \operatorname{ps}(X)=\log|X| . However, we do not know such computation for sm ⁡ ( X ) {\operatorname{sm}}(X) ; we can only assert that sm ⁡ ( X ) {\operatorname{sm}}(X) may be arbitrarily large. Finally, we highlight another open problem: whether these games are always determined.

We consider the following transfinite cut-and-choose game played on a

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\left\langle X_{\alpha}:{\alpha}<{\kappa}\right\rangle
be
T_{0}
spaces.
If
\max(\log(\kappa),\sup\{\operatorname{ps}(X_{\alpha}):\alpha<\kappa\})

\operatorname{ps}\!\Big(\sum\nolimits_{\alpha<\kappa}X_{\alpha}\Big)=\max(\log(\kappa),\sup\{\operatorname{ps}(X_{\alpha}):\alpha<\kappa\}).

Proof.

By Theorem
2.1
(a), the inequality “
\geq
” follows immediately.

To show the reverse inequality,
let

X=\sum_{\alpha<\kappa}X_{\alpha}\text{ and }{\delta}=\max(\log(\kappa),\sup\{\operatorname{ps}(X_{\alpha}):\alpha<\kappa\}).

Since
{{\delta}}
is infinite, it has a partition
{{\delta}}=E\cup O

such that
\operatorname{tp}(O)=\log(\kappa)

and
\operatorname{tp}(E)=\sup\{\operatorname{ps}(X_{\alpha}):\alpha<\kappa\}

Fix a family
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{\zeta}:\zeta\in\log({\kappa})\}
such that
for each

there exists
\zeta<{\delta}

with
\alpha\in A_{\zeta}
and
\beta\notin A_{\zeta}
.

For each
\alpha<\kappa
, let
\sigma_{\alpha}
be a winning strategy of
the Seeker in the game
G_{{\delta}}(X_{\alpha})
.

Informally, the Seeker play the following
strategy in the game
G_{{\delta}}(X)
:
on coordinates in
O
, the Seeker plays to determine the unique
\alpha
such that

X_{\alpha}
contains the Hider’s chosen point.
On coordinates in
E
, the Seeker plays a parallel copy of the winning strategy
\sigma_{\alpha}

inside each
X_{\alpha}
.

The formal definition of the Seeker’s strategy
{\sigma}
is as follows.
If
\xi\in O
, then the Seeker’s move is
independent of the Hider’s previous moves:

\sigma(\mathbf{i}\restriction\xi)=\bigcup\{X_{\alpha}:\alpha\in A_{\operatorname{tp}(O\cap{\xi})}\}.

If
\xi\in E
, then

\sigma(\mathbf{i}\restriction\xi)=\bigcup_{\alpha<\kappa}\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathbf{i}\restriction E).

We now verify that
{\sigma}
is really a winning strategy for the Seeker in the game
G_{{\delta}}(X)
.

The family
\mathcal{A}
separates the points of
\kappa
, so the Seeker’s moves on coordinates in
O
guarantee that there exists a unique
\alpha<\kappa
such that

\operatorname{\mathbf{B}}_{X}(\mathbf{U}\restriction O,\mathbf{i}\restriction O)\subseteq X_{\alpha}.

For each
\xi\in E
, we have

\sigma(\mathbf{i}\restriction\xi)\cap X_{\alpha}=\sigma_{\alpha}(\mathbf{i}\restriction E),

so the trace of the game on
X_{\alpha}
using coordinates in
E
is exactly a game in
G_{\delta}(X_{\alpha})
where the Seeker follows the winning strategy
\sigma_{\alpha}
.
Hence,

\bigl|\operatorname{\mathbf{B}}_{X}(\mathbf{U}\restriction E,\mathbf{i}\restriction E)\cap X_{\alpha}\bigr|\leq 1.

It follows that

|\operatorname{\mathbf{B}}_{X}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{i})|\leq 1,

so the Seeker wins the game
G_{\delta}(X)
, as required.
∎

Stepping up in

By Theorem

Theorem 5.1
.

If
\left\langle X,{\tau}\right\rangle
is a
T_{2}
topological space with

{\pi}(X)={\omega}
, then there exists a
T_{2}
topological space

Z
such that
\operatorname{ps}(Z)={\omega}\oplus\operatorname{ps}(X)
and
{\pi}(Z)={\omega}
.
Moreover, if
X
is
0-dimensional, then
Z
can also be chosen to be 0-dimensional.

Proof.

Let
C
be the Cantor set. For each
c\in C
let
\{I^{c}_{n}:n<{\omega}\}

be a sequence of pairwise disjoint clopen intervals in
C
converging to

c
. Let
{\varepsilon}
be the usual Euclidean topology on
C
.
Fix a
{\pi}
-base
\{P_{n}:n<{\omega}\}
of
X
.

The underlying set of
Z
will be
C\times X
.
For
c\in C
,
W\in{\tau}_{X}
, and
c\in E\in{\varepsilon}
define

V(c,W,E)=\Big((\{c\}\times W)\cup\bigcup\{I^{c}_{n}\times X:P_{n}\subset W\}\Big)\cap(E\times X).

For
z=\left\langle c,w\right\rangle\in C\times Z
define

\mathcal{V}_{z}=\{V(c,W,E):w\in W\in{\tau}_{X},c\in E\in\varepsilon,E\text{ clopen}\}.

It is easy to check that
the family

\mathcal{V}=\{\mathcal{V}_{z}:z\in Z\}

is a neighborhood system of a topology
{\tau}_{Z}
on
Z
(see
[
4
]
*Proposition 1.2.3).
Moreover, observe that

(1)

For each
E\in{\varepsilon}
, we have
(E\times X)\in{\tau}_{Z}
.

(2)

If
\mathcal{E}
is a
{\pi}
-base in
C
, then
\{E\times X:E\in\mathcal{E}\}
is a
{\pi}
-base in
Z
.
Hence,
\pi(Z)={\omega}
.

Since

\overline{V(c,W,E)}=V(c,W,E)\cup(\{c\}\times\overline{W}),

the space
Z
is regular or 0-dimensional whenever

X
is regular or 0-dimensional.

Next we show
\operatorname{ps}(Z)\geq{\omega}\oplus\operatorname{ps}(X)
.

Fix
{\alpha}<ps(X)
, and fix a strategy
{\sigma}
for Seeker
in the game

6.
Developable spaces

Recall that a countable collection
\mathbb{U}
of families of open sets in a space
X
is called

quasi-developement
if and only if
\{\operatorname{St}(x,\mathcal{U}):\mathcal{U}\in\mathbb{U}\}

forms a local base at
x
for every
x\in X
, where

\operatorname{St}(x,\mathcal{U})=\bigcup\{U\in\mathcal{U}:x\in U\}.

A quasi-development
\mathbb{U}
is called a
development
if and only if every
\mathcal{U}\in\mathbb{U}
is a cover of
X
.
A space is said to be
quasi-developable
(respectively,
developable
) if it admits a quasi-development (respectively, a development).

In 1986 Scott obtained the following result:

Theorem 6.1

(
[
8
]
*Theorem 1.7)
.

If a space
X
is developable,
then
\operatorname{\psi w_{0}}(X)=\operatorname{\psi w_{1}}(X)=\log\operatorname{w}(X)=\log(|X|)
.

Corollary 6.2
.

If
X
is a metric space, then
\operatorname{ps}(X)=\log(|X|)
.

Proof.

First,
\operatorname{ps}(X)\leq\operatorname{\psi w_{0}}(X)=\log(|X|)
by Theorem
6.1
.
On the other hand,
\log(|X|)\leq\operatorname{ps}(X)
by Theorem
2.1
(a).
∎

Developable spaces are clearly quasi-developable as well, but the converse does not hold:
in
[
3
]
*Example 2.2, Bennett showed that the Michael line (see
[
7
]
) separates these two classes of spaces.
Hence, the following result – although inspired by Theorem
6.1
– does not follow from it.

Theorem 6.3
.

If
X
is a
T_{0}
quasi-developable space, then
\operatorname{ps}(X)=\log|X|
.

Before proving this result, we recall one more definition.
An open cover
\mathcal{U}
of a space
X
is called a

\theta
-base
of
X
if there exist families
\mathcal{U}_{n}\subset\mathcal{U}
for each
n<\omega
such that

\mathcal{U}=\bigcup_{n<\omega}\mathcal{U}_{n},

and for every
x\in X
and every open neighborhood
V
of
x
, there is some
n<\omega
such that
the order of
\mathcal{U}_{n}
at
x
, defined by

\operatorname{ord}(x,\mathcal{U}_{n})=|\{U\in\mathcal{U}_{n}:x\in U\}|,

is finite, and moreover, there exists
U\in\mathcal{U}_{n}
with
U\subset V
.

Bennett and Lutzer
[
2
]
*Theorem 8 proved that a space is quasi-developable if and only if it has a
\theta
-base.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be a base for
X
such that
|\mathcal{B}|=\operatorname{w}(X)
. Now let

\mathcal{U}=\bigcup\{\mathcal{U}_{n}:n<{\omega}\}
be
a
{\theta}
-base for
X
such that:

(1)

\mathcal{U}{\subset}\mathcal{B}
, hence for each
n<{\omega}
,
|\mathcal{U}_{n}|\leq\operatorname{w}(X)
;

(2)

\{\mathcal{U}_{n}:n<{\omega}\}
is a partition of
U
that witnesses that
U
is a
{\theta}
-base, i.e., for each

x{\in}X
and for each
V\in{\tau}(x,X)
, there is some
n<{\omega}
such that
\operatorname{ord}(x,{\mathcal{U}_{n}})<{\omega}
and
there is some
U{\in}U_{n}
such that
x{\in}U{\subset}V.

For every
m,n<{\omega}
, let

\mathcal{V}_{n}^{m}:=\Big\{\bigcap\{U{\in}{\mathcal{U}_{n}}:x{\in}U\}:x{\in}X\text{ and }\operatorname{ord}(x,{\mathcal{U}_{n}})=m\Big\}.

Notice that
|\mathcal{V}_{n}^{m}|\leq|[{\mathcal{U}_{n}}]^{m}|\leq\operatorname{w}(X)
. Thus, for all
x{\in}X
and
V{\in}\tau(x,X)
, there are

m,n<{\omega}
such that
\operatorname{ord}(x,{\mathcal{U}_{n}})=m
and there is some
U{\in}{\mathcal{U}_{n}}
such that
x{\in}U{\subset}V
.
Hence, there is
W=\bigcap\{U{\in}\mathcal{U}_{n}:x{\in}U\}{\in}\mathcal{V}_{n}^{m}
such that
x{\in}W{\subset}U{\subset}V
.
Observe that
\operatorname{ord}(x,\mathcal{V}^{n}_{m})=1
.

For each
m,n<\omega
, let
S_{n}^{m}\subset\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{V}_{n}^{m})
be a separating family on
\mathcal{V}_{n}^{m}
such that

|S_{n}^{m}|\leq\log\operatorname{w}(X).

Define

\mathcal{W}_{n}^{m}=\bigcup\{S:S\in S_{n}^{m}\},

so that
|\mathcal{W}_{n}^{m}|\leq\log\operatorname{w}(X)
.
Let

\mathcal{W}=\bigcup\{\mathcal{W}_{n}^{m}:m,n<\omega\},

and fix an enumeration

\mathcal{W}=\{W_{\alpha}:\alpha<\log\operatorname{w}(X)\}.

Let
\sigma
be the strategy in the game
G_{\log(|X|)}(X)
for the Seeker in which, at inning
\alpha<\log\operatorname{w}(X)
, the Seeker plays
\mathbf{W}({\alpha})=W_{\alpha}
.
Suppose that
\sigma
is not winning. Then there exists a function
\mathbf{i}:\log\operatorname{w}(X)\to 2
such that
\operatorname{\mathbf{B}}_{X}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{i}))
has at least two elements, say
x
and
y
.

Since
X
is
T_{0}
, there exists an open set
U
such that, without loss of generality,
x\in U
but
y\notin U
.
Choose
m,n<\omega
such that

\operatorname{ord}(x,\mathcal{W}_{n}^{m})=1\quad\text{and}\quad\exists\,V_{x}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{m}\;\;(x\in V_{x}\subset U).

Now we distinguish two cases.

If
y\notin\bigcup\mathcal{V}_{n}^{m}
, then choose
S\in S_{n}^{m}
with
V_{x}\in S
, and let

\alpha<\log\operatorname{w}(X)
be such that
W_{\alpha}=\bigcup S
.
Otherwise, there exists
V_{y}\in\mathcal{V}_{n}^{m}
such that

. Since
V_{x}
is the only element of

\mathcal{V}_{n}^{m}
containing
x
, and

, it follows that
V_{x}\neq V_{y}
. Hence, there exists

S\in S_{n}^{m}
such that
V_{x}\in S
but
V_{y}\notin S
, and again we choose

\alpha<\log\operatorname{w}(X)
with

.

Thus, in either case, we have

but
y\notin W_{\alpha}
.
Since

x\in\operatorname{\mathbf{B}}_{X}(\mathbf{W},\mathbf{i})=\bigcap_{\beta<\log\operatorname{w}(X)}\operatorname{B}_{X}(\mathbf{W}({\beta}),\mathbf{i}({{\beta}}))\subset\operatorname{B}_{X}(\mathbf{W}({\alpha}),\mathbf{i}({\alpha})),

it follows that
\operatorname{B}_{X}(\mathbf{W}(\alpha),\mathbf{i}(\alpha))=W_{\alpha}
, and therefore
\mathbf{i}(\alpha)=1
.
On the other hand,
y\in\operatorname{B}_{X}(B_{\alpha},i_{\alpha})
, so

\operatorname{B}_{X}(\mathbf{W}(\alpha),\mathbf{i}(\alpha))=X\setminus W_{\alpha}
and hence
\mathbf{i}(\alpha)=0
.
This contradiction completes the argument.

Hence, the Seeker has a winning strategy in the game
G_{\log(|X|)}(X)
. Thus,

\operatorname{ps}(X)\leq\log(|X|)
. Since
\operatorname{ps}(X)\geq\log(|X|)
by Theorem
2.1
(a),
we finished the proof of the theorem.
∎
