Unraveling disulfidptosis for prognostic modeling and personalized treatment strategies in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Xiangyu Xu, Bingbing Zhang, Jin Zhang, Hongbiao Ma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo construct and identify a prognostic and therapeutic signature based on disulfidptosis-related genes in lung adenocarcinoma.MethodsBioinformatic analysis was performed to assess the differential expression of disulfidptosis-related genes between cancerous and control samples from The Cancer Genome Atlas-Lung Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-LUAD) database. Survival analysis, immune cell infiltration assessment, and examination of oncogenic pathways were performed to uncover potential clinical implications of disulfidptosis gene expression. Differential gene expression analysis between subtypes facilitated the development of a prognostic model using a combination of genes associated with survival. A nomogram was further created using independent clinical and molecular factors.ResultsWe identified the significant upregulation of ten disulfidptosis-related genes and delineated two distinct subtypes, C1 and C2. Subtype C2 was associated with prolonged survival. Then, prognostic modeling utilizing six genes (TXNRD1, CPS1, S100P, SCGB3A1, CYP24A1, NAPSA) demonstrated predictive power in both training and validation datasets. The nomogram, incorporating the risk model with clinical features, provided a reliable tool for predicting one-year (AUC 0.77), three-year (AUC 0.75), and five-year (AUC 0.78) survival rates. Additionally, chemotherapy sensitivity analysis highlighted significant resistance in the high-risk group, primarily associated with subtype C1.ConclusionOur study reveals distinct LUAD subtypes, offers a robust prognostic model, and underscores clinical implications for personalized therapy based on disulfidptosis-related genes expression profiles.

Introduction

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent type of lung cancer and originates from glandular cells in the lungs [1]. It often lacks obvious symptoms in the early stages, presenting noticeable symptoms only when the cancer has progressed to advanced stages [2]. The pathogenesis of LUAD is complex, involving various intracellular signaling pathways and metabolic abnormalities [3–6]. Disturbances in the process of cell death hold significant biomedical value in the study of LUAD [7]. Programmed cell death usually occurs through pathways involving the regulation of apoptosis regulator Bcl-2 family proteins [8] and the activation of caspase family proteins that affect the cellular tumor antigen p53 protein [9], thereby promoting the survival and proliferation of cancer cells. Additionally, the level of cell apoptosis serves as an important indicator for evaluating treatment efficacy and prognosis in LUAD. Chemotherapy drugs exert their effects by inducing cancer cell apoptosis, and an increase in apoptosis level usually indicates treatment effectiveness. The expression levels of apoptosis-related factors, such as Bcl-2 and Bax, are often used as prognostic biomarkers associated with patient survival rates and disease recurrence [10]. The tumor microenvironment of LUAD also plays a significant role in the cell apoptosis pathway. For example, tumor-associated macrophages [11], cancer-associated fibroblasts [12], and other components of the microenvironment can secrete cytokines and growth factors [13], which can inhibit or promote cancer cell apoptosis, thereby affecting tumor progression.

Recently, disulfidptosis, an emerging form of cell death, has attracted widespread attention [14]. Under conditions of glucose deprivation, certain cells experience rapid depletion of NADPH due to the high expression of SLC7A11. This depletion leads to abnormal accumulation of disulfides, such as cystine, ultimately triggering cell death through disulfide stress [15]. Studies have shown that the expression of SLC7A11 is associated with tumor growth and drug resistance [16]. For example, tumor cells may regulate their own survival by modulating the expression of SLC7A11 to adapt to the hypoxic and nutrient-deficient microenvironment [17,18]. Furthermore, inactivation of the WASF3-NCKAP1-CYFIP1 complex, a marker of disulfidptosis, has been shown to significantly affect the proliferation and metastasis of cancer cells in various cancers [19–22]. Multiple studies indicate that disulfidptosis may have important research value in LUAD. Although preliminary research suggests a potential connection between disulfidptosis and LUAD, the specific mechanisms still need further exploration. Future studies not only need to elucidate the specific mechanisms of disulfidptosis in LUAD but also evaluate its potential and strategies as a therapeutic target. This study has identified a novel subtype of LUAD based on characteristic genes associated with disulfidptosis. An in-depth analysis of the distinctions in patient prognosis and sensitivity to drug treatments between these subtypes was conducted. The overarching goal is to offer a novel strategy for personalized therapy in the context of LUAD.

Methods

Data acquisition

The disulfidptosis-related gene set was derived from research conducted by Liu et al. [14]. In addition, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data, along with critical clinical parameters, such as age, gender, tumor stage, survival status, and survival duration for LUAD, were extracted from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). This collection included a cohort of 506 lung cancer samples, each annotated with overall survival (OS) times, and a control group of 59 normal tissue samples. And the TCGA dataset was primarily used as the training set for our study. To validate our analytic outcomes, the GSE13213 dataset, encompassing RNA expression profiles and OS data for 117 LUAD cases, was acquired from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/).

Expression patterns of disulfidptosis-related genes

Within the TCGA-LUAD cohort, a Wilcoxon rank-sum test was conducted to determine the differential expression of disulfidptosis-related genes between cancerous and control samples. In addition, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate the association between the expression levels of disulfidptosis-related genes in the TCGA-LUAD dataset.

Molecular subtyping based on disulfidptosis-related genes

Unsupervised hierarchical clustering was employed to categorize LUAD samples based on the expression levels of significantly differentially expressed disulfidptosis-related genes identified in the training set. This analysis was conducted using the ConsensusClusterPlus package (version 1.54.0), with K values ranging from 2 to 10, to determine the optimal disulfidptosis subtypes (K values). Consequently, the cancer samples in the training set were segregated into distinct molecular subtypes. The prognostic disparities among these disulfidptosis subtypes were then examined using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and evaluated through the survival package (version 2.41-1).

Analysis of the tumor microenvironment among different subtypes

To evaluate the tumor microenvironment, the CIBERSORT algorithm was employed to quantify the proportions of 22 immune cell types within the LUAD training set. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied to discern the variations in immune cell infiltration across different molecular subtype groups. Furthermore, the ESTIMATE algorithm was utilized to approximate the stromal and immune scores of tumor samples based on their expression data. The disparities in these scores among the various disulfidptosis molecular subtypes were assessed using the Wilcoxon test. Additionally, this test was also employed to investigate expression differences of standard immune checkpoint genes and HLA family genes across the different disulfidptosis molecular subtypes.

Differential carcinogenic pathways between subtypes

Using the total gene expression levels from the training set of cancer samples, the identified molecular subtypes were grouped. Gene fold changes between these subtypes were sorted and subjected to Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA), utilizing the hallmark gene set from the MSigDB database (https://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/index.jsp) as the enrichment backdrop. Hallmark signaling pathways significantly linked to the subtype grouping were picked based on their enrichment results, with a statistical significance threshold set at a p-value of <0.05.

Identification of prognostically significant genes

Differential gene expression analysis (DEGs) between the two clusters in the TCGA dataset was performed using the Limma package (version 3.52.4). The defining criteria for differential expression included a p-value threshold of <0.05 and a log2 fold change above 1. To recognize genes with significant survival correlations, a Univariate Cox survival analysis was conducted, ascertaining genes with a p-value of <0.05 for subsequent model refinement and development.

Developing and validating a prognosis risk model

The LASSO Cox analysis was executed using the prognostic DEGs identified earlier to reduce dimensions with the R package glmnet (version 1.2). DEGs retaining a non-zero regression coefficient after 10-fold cross-validation were incorporated to formulate the final prognostic signature. The prognostic risk model was established through step-wise Cox regression using the R package survminer (version 0.4.9) with the detailed formula: Risk score = β1X1 + β2X2 + … + βnXn. The risk score for each TCGA patient was computed, categorizing them into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median value. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis assessed the prognostic predictive ability. Additionally, the risk model was validated in the GSE13213 dataset using the same model construction method.

Constructing a nomogram using the risk score and clinical features

Univariate Cox survival analysis assessed the prognostic impact of clinical features, and multivariate Cox analysis determined the independence of the risk score and selected clinical features in influencing patient prognosis. Independent features were chosen to construct a nomogram using the R package rms (version 5.1-2). The prognostic value of the nomogram was evaluated through ROC curves at 1, 3, and 5 years.

Predicting response to chemotherapy

The R package pRRophetic (version 0.5) estimated chemotherapy agent sensitivity, calculating and quantifying the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50). Differences in drug sensitivity between high- and low-risk groups were compared using the Wilcoxon test. In addition, combining the disulfidptosis subtypes grouping (Cluster) with the high and low-risk groups (RiskGroup), the Sankey diagram was plotted using the ggalluvial package (version 0.12.3) to observe the relationship between disulfidptosis subtypes and high/low-risk groups.

Results

Expression pattern of disulfidptosis-related genes in LUAD samples

We initially examined the expression of ten disulfidptosis-related genes in LUAD and para-tumor normal tissues. We observed significantly higher expression of all the ten genes between LUAD and normal samples (p < 0.05, Supplementary Figure 1(A), Supplementary Table 1). Subsequently, we analyzed the correlation among these genes (Supplementary Figure 1(B)). The highest positive correlation was achieved between NCKAP1 and NDUFS1, with a correlation coefficient of 0.66. The highest negative correlation was obtained between NCKAP1 and NDUFA11, with a correlation coefficient of −0.44. These results suggest potential dysregulation of disulfidptosis pathway in LUAD.

Subtyping LUAD based on expression of disulfidptosis-related genes

To explore the heterogeneity within LUAD related to disulfidptosis, we performed unsupervised clustering analysis on LUAD samples. The optimal number of clusters (K value) was determined to be 2 based on the change in cumulative distribution function (CDF) (Figures 1(A,C)), leading to the identification of two distinct subtypes, C1 and C2 (Figure 1(B), Supplementary Table 2). Each subtype consisted of 253 LUAD samples. We then analyzed the overall survival between C1 and C2 groups, and found significant prolonged survival in C2 group (Figure 1(D), log-rank p = 0.01, HR = 0.68, 95% CI: 0.51–0.91). Of note, no significant differences in clinical features including age, sex, and tumor stage were found between C1 and C2 (Figure 1(E)), which indicated that the prognostic difference between C1 and C2 were mainly caused by expression difference of the ten disulfidptosis-related genes.

The maximum change in the CDF curve and the most stable k value for the decreasing trend of the CDF curve are both 2, indicating that the optimal clustering value is set to 2. The clustering heatmap also shows that when two clusters are set, the population can be clearly classified into different subtypes. (D) Survival curves for the two clusters, with the red curve and blue curve representing C1 and C2, respectively. E. Clinical features in C1 and C2. CDF: cumulative distribution function; AUC: area under the curve.

Differences in tumor microenvironment and oncogenic pathway between C1 and C2

Since previous studies have demonstrated the association between tumor immune microenvironment (TME) and activation level of cell death pathways [23–25], we explored the immune cell infiltration level between C1 and C2. A total of 9 significantly different immune cells were detected (Figure 2(A), Supplementary Table 3). Notably, Dendritic cells (DCs), B cells, and CD4+ T cells were significantly enriched in C1 group, while regulatory T cells (Tregs) were more prevalent in C2 group. Then, immune score, stromal score, microenvironment score, and tumor purity were calculated (Figures 2(B–E), Supplementary Table 4). And we observed significant differences between C1 and C2 in all the above indices. Specifically, immune score, stromal score, and microenvironment score were higher in the C2 subtype, while tumor purity was higher in the C1 subtype.

Tumor microenvironment difference between the C1 and C2. (A) Box plot showing the differences in immune cell types between C1 and C2. (B–D) Violin plot comparing the infiltration of immune score, stromal score, and microenvironment score between C1 and C2. (E) Violin plot comparing tumor purity between C1 and C2. (F,G) Expression of immune checkpoint (F) and HLA molecule (G) in C1 and C2 groups. Blue color represents the C2 group, and red one represents the C1 group.

We then extracted the expression data of immune checkpoint genes (Supplementary Table 5) and compared their expression differences between C1 and C2. All immune checkpoint genes were significantly down-regulated in C1 (Figure 2(F)). Additionally, we compared the expression differences in HLA family gene (Supplementary Table 6) and found all HLA family genes were also significantly down-regulated in C1 (Figure 2(G)).

To explore the activation level of oncogenic pathway difference between C1 and C2, we analyzed the enrichment of hallmark signaling pathways between C1 and C2 (Supplementary Table 7). We identified three significant pathways: ANDROGENRESPONSE, MITOTICSPINDLE, and PROTEINSECRETION (Supplementary Figure 2), all of which were significantly enriched in C1, indicating the higher malignancy degree of C1.

Developing and validating a prognostic model using cluster specific genes

Since C1 and C2 had different clinical features, TME, and oncogenic pathway enrichment, we sought to elucidate the molecular difference between the two clusters. We performed DEGs analysis between the two clusters and obtained 32 DEGs, including 23 upregulated genes and nine downregulated genes (Figure 3(A), Supplementary Table 8).

DEG: differential gene expression analysis; TCGA: The Cancer Genome Atlas; GEO: Gene Expression Omnibus.

We then analyzed the correlation between survival and these DEGs using univariate-Cox survival analysis, which yielded a total of 14 genes whose expression level correlated with survival significantly (Figure 3(B), Supplementary Table 9). Furthermore, using Lasso regression algorithm, we obtained 6 genes, whose combination correlated with overall survival outstanding the rest gene (Figures 3(C,D), Supplementary Table 10). The six genes are TXNRD1, CPS1, S100P, SCGB3A1, CYP24A1, and NAPSA.

Subsequently, a risk score model was constructed based on the regression coefficients of the six genes. The risk score was calculated for each patient, and the cancer samples from the TCGA training set were divided into H and L groups based on the optimal cutoff. As expected, the overall survival exhibited a significant difference between the two risk groups in TCGA dataset (Figure 3(E), Supplementary Table 11, p < 0.001, HR 2.21, 95% CI 1.63–2.98) and GEO validation dataset (Figure 3(F), Supplementary Table 12, p < 0.001, HR 2.64, 95% CI 1.45–4.80). The detailed correlation of risk score, survival time, and gene expression heatmap in TCGA dataset and the validation GEO dataset were also presented (Figures 3(G,H)).

Creating a nomogram using risk score and clinical characteristics

To find the biological significance represented by the risk score, we conducted an analysis of the correlation between risk scores and seven clinical characteristics (Supplementary Table 13). Notably, clinical features including tumor stage, T-, N-, M-stage, and cancer status correlated with overall survival significantly (Figure 4(A)). To identify independent prognostic features, we performed multivariate Cox analysis and found three independent prognostic features, i.e., T-stage, cancer status, and risk score (Figure 4(B), Supplementary Table 14). Among these, the risk score had the smallest p-value.

Creating a nomogram using risk score and clinical characteristics. (A) Features correlated with overall survival significantly. (B) Features correlated with overall survival significantly after multivariate adjusting. (C) Nomogram using risk score, T-stage, and tumor status. (D) Time-dependent ROC curve showed the performance of the nomogram in predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rate. (E–G) Correlation between predicted survival time and actual survival time.

Subsequently, we constructed a nomogram using these three features with a C-index of 0.724 (Figure 4(C), Supplementary Table 15). We then used the nomogram to predict patient one-year, three-year, and five-year survival rates, and the ROC curve showed that area under curve (AUC) values for all the three timepoints were nearly 0.7 (Figure 4(D)). Afterward, we compared the consistency between the predicted one-year, three-year, and five-year survival rates from the nomogram and the actual survival data, yielding a high level of agreement (Figures 4(E–G)).

Chemotherapy drug sensitivity differences between the H and L risk groups

Sensitivity to chemotherapeutic agents including Mitomycin C, Epothilone B, Thapsigargin, Vinblastine, Axitinib, Pyrimethamine, Elesclomol, and Metformin were estimated based on the Cancer Drug Susceptibility Genomics (GDSC) database. Notably, the IC50 values of all the eight chemotherapy drugs in patients in the high-risk group were significantly lower than those in the low-risk group (Figure 5, Supplementary Table 16), indicating the widespread treatment resistance of the high-risk group.

IC50 of the eight chemotherapeutic agents between high- and low-risk groups. IC50: half-maximal inhibitory concentration.

To clarify the relationship between clusters (C1 and C2) and risk group, we performed sample level comparison. As expected, the high-risk group mainly comprised of samples from C1, while the low-risk group contained samples from both C1 and C2, which was consistent with the worse overall survival data in C1 aforementioned (Supplementary Figure 3, Supplementary Table 17).

Discussion

Preliminary studies on disulfidptosis have shown its potential impact on the prognosis of colorectal cancer [26], gastric cancer [27], and breast cancer [28]. LUAD is one of the most common and deadly types of cancer worldwide, and research on LUAD is of great significance in reducing the overall burden of cancer. However, there is currently a lack of research on the pathogenic mechanisms of disulfidptosis in LUAD. Our analysis found disturbances in disulfidptosis genes in LUAD patients and identified two expression clusters based on these genes, i.e., C1 and C2. These two clusters showed differences in patient prognosis, immune microenvironment, and mitotic activity. Furthermore, we identified DEGs between C1 and C2 and constructed a prognostic model based on these genes. Six genes, including TXNRD1, CPS1, S100P, SCGB3A1, CYP24A1, and NAPSA, were primarily responsible for patient prognosis. By integrating clinical features, we found tumor T-stage, cancer status, and risk score were independent prognostic factors. Additionally, there were significant differences in chemotherapy drug sensitivity between high and low-risk groups. Consistently, there was a reasonable match between the clusters and risk groups.

Immune infiltration analysis showed significant differences in the infiltration scores of Tregs, memory B cells, resting CD4 memory T cells, activated CD4 memory T cells, and neutrophils between the two subtypes of LUAD patients. Specifically, the infiltration levels of Tregs and memory B cells were significantly lower in the C1 subtype compared to the C2 subtype, while the two types of memory T cells and neutrophils were significantly higher in the C1 subtype. Under normal conditions, Tregs help suppress excessive immune responses, but their decrease in the tumor microenvironment may contribute to enhanced immune cell attack against tumor cells [29]. Memory B cells play an important role in antibody production and immune memory, and low levels of infiltration may reduce antibody-mediated anti-tumor immune responses [30]. The high infiltration of the two types of memory T cells (including resting CD4 memory T cells and activated CD4 memory T cells) and neutrophils may reflect a strong cellular immune response to the tumor [31–33]. They can recognize and attack tumor cells through different mechanisms, promoting the effectiveness of immune responses. The immune infiltration results indicate that the immune response in C2 patients is mainly T cell-mediated cellular immune response rather than specific humoral immune response, but the decreased infiltration of memory B cells hinders the recognition and activation of T cell receptors [34,35]. This speculation is supported by our analysis results of immune scores, stromal scores, microenvironment scores, and tumor purity, which showed that the microenvironment score of C1 patients was significantly lower than that of C2 patients, while the tumor purity score was significantly higher than that of C1 patients, indicating significant suppression of the immune microenvironment in C1 patients, which may be one of the reasons for their poorer prognosis [36]. The analysis of immune checkpoint and HLA family gene expression was consistent with the immune infiltration analysis results, with significant suppression of immune checkpoint and HLA family gene expression in C1 patients. In summary, the comprehensive analysis of immune features in C1 and C2 patients indicates significant suppression of immune function in C1.

To further explore the potential biological processes that differ between C1 and C2 patients, we conducted GSEA enrichment analysis between the two groups. GSEA revealed differences in the mitoticspindle process between the two groups, with genes related to the mitoticspindle pathway being upregulated and more concentrated in the C1 subtype compared to the C2 subtype. Multiple studies have shown that abnormalities in this process are a direct cause of tumor occurrence and development [37–39]. Krenn PW and Hofbauer SW demonstrated that the expression of immune molecules, such as CD38 can activate the TNFα-NF-κB pathway by inducing integrin-linked kinase (ILK) and cyclin D1 (CCND1), thereby altering the activity of the mitoticspindle process [40]. Additionally, the studies by Genoud V and Espinoza FI indicated the activation of CD40 in the mitoticspindle process. Multiple studies have shown the intricate connection between the tumor immune microenvironment and the activity of mitoticspindle pathway [41–43]. Therefore, we speculate that the differences in the mitoticspindle process between C1 and C2 patients are mainly due to differences in their immune activity.

Based on the differential genes between C1 and C2 patients, we constructed a prognostic model and identified six genes: TXNRD1, CPS1, S100P, SCGB3A1, CYP24A1, and NAPSA. Thioredoxin Reductase 1 (TXNRD1), a member of the thioredoxin (Trx) system [44], is a selenoprotein containing selenocysteine and is involved in regulating cellular redox reactions, growth, and differentiation [45–47]. Carbamoyl-Phosphate Synthase 1 (CPS1) plays an important role in removing excess ammonia from cells [48]. S100 Calcium Binding Protein P (S100P) acts as a calcium sensor and contributes to cellular calcium signaling. It interacts with other proteins (such as EZR and PPP5C) in a calcium-dependent manner and is involved in physiological processes, such as the formation of microvilli in epithelial cells. It may stimulate cell proliferation through the activation of the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE) in an autocrine manner [49]. Secretoglobin Family 3A Member 1 (SCGB3A1) is a cytokine-like protein that inhibits cell growth in vitro [50]. Cytochrome P450 Family 24 Subfamily A Member 1 (CYP24A1) catalyzes many reactions involved in drug metabolism and the synthesis of cholesterol, steroids, and other lipids [51–53]. This mitochondrial protein initiates the degradation of 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D3 (the physiologically active form of vitamin D3) by hydroxylation of its side chain[54] and plays a role in regulating calcium homeostasis and the vitamin D endocrine system [55]. Napsin A Aspartic Peptidase (NAPSA) undergoes proteolytic processing to generate active peptides and mature proteases. The activated peptides of aspartic proteases act as inhibitors of the protease active site [56]. These peptide fragments or propeptide portions are thought to be important for the proper folding, targeting, and control of activation of aspartic protease zymogens. The encoded protease may play a role in the proteolytic processing of pulmonary surfactant protein B [57] and may be involved in protein degradation metabolism in the proximal tubules of the kidney [58]. This gene has been described as a marker for LUAD and renal cell carcinoma [59,60]. Among the prognostic genes we screened, there are genes closely related to tumor cell proliferation, such as S100P [61], SCGB3A1 [62], and NAPSA [63], as well as genes closely related to cellular redox reactions, such as TXNRD1 [64] and CYP24A1 [65].

By matching the high and low-risk groups of the prognostic model with clusters, we found that the proportion of C1 in the high-risk group is significantly higher, which was consistent with the prognosis difference between C1 and C2 clusters. This result indicates that the core genes reflected in the prognostic model are also suitable for explaining the differences in the survival status of C1 and C2 populations.

Conclusion

This study provides a comprehensive investigation into the expression patterns and clinical relevance of disulfidptosis-related genes in LUAD. The observed dysregulation of these genes suggests their potential as biomarkers for LUAD. The identification of distinct subtypes with varying survival outcomes and differences in the tumor microenvironment and oncogenic pathway activation emphasizes the molecular heterogeneity within LUAD. The developed prognostic model, incorporating cluster-specific genes, offers a promising tool for predicting patient outcomes. Overall, these findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the molecular landscape of LUAD and may guide future therapeutic strategies and patient stratification efforts.
