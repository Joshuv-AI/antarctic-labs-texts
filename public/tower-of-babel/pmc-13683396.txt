Noncollinear kagome magnet driven by giant antisymmetric exchange
Authors: David W. Tam, Romain Sibille, Thomas Mazet, Bertrand Roessli, Andrea Piovano, Bernard Malaman, Dariusz Jakub Gawryluk, Loïc Roduit, YouGuo Shi, Xiuliang Yuan, Alexandra A. Turrini, Lukas Keller, Yona Soh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kagome lattices host exotic phenomena such as quantum spin liquids, topological Weyl nodes, and flat bands. The kagome ferromagnet Co3Sn2S2 has been a focus of debate due to conflicting reports on its magnetic character. Here, using weakly perturbative probes—primarily neutron scattering and Mössbauer spectroscopy—we determine its ground state and magnetic Hamiltonian. The spin wave spectrum is reproduced by a simple Hamiltonian dominated by a large in-plane nearest-neighbor Heisenberg interaction J and an antisymmetric Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction that is roughly one third of J. This Hamiltonian yields a canted, noncollinear ferromagnetic ground state with an umbrella-type structure, which our data show to be the true ground state at all temperatures below TC. Additionally, the spin susceptibility above TC exhibits a reduced critical exponent, akin to other spin-anisotropic systems. Our findings explain the unconventional magnetism in Co3Sn2S2, including the giant anomalous Hall effect and the origin of chiral phonons.

Introduction

Topological states of matter have been intensively studied due to the rich physics and robust ground state properties provided by topological protection1–5. While initial interests in kagome systems have been on insulating magnets in pursuit of quantum spin liquids due to the geometrically frustrated kagome lattice6, recently the direction has expanded to metallic magnets in relation to Weyl nodes, a gapless topologically protected state. Many of the identified Weyl semimetals with broken time reversal symmetry happen to be metallic kagome magnets7–9, among them Co3Sn2S210. Since the electronic band structure and associated topologically protected Weyl nodes are directly determined by the magnetic ground state11,12, there is a natural reason to determine the magnetic ground state of Co3Sn2S2 in order to understand its electronic properties. As a result, the magnetic ground state of Co3Sn2S2 has been the subject of numerous studies in recent years.

Generally speaking, the magnetic structure is argued to be close to a pure ferromagnetic (FM) state with moments along the c-axis13,14. However, two antiferromagnetic (AFM) phases have also been proposed, either competing and/or coexisting with the FM order, which are a 120° chiral structure or an all-in-all-out structure, respectively15–18. The 120° structure corresponds to the Shubnikov group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R\bar{3}m$$\end{document}R3¯m, while the FM and all-in-all-out structures belong to the same group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R\bar{3}{m}^{{\prime} }$$\end{document}R3¯m′ and therefore coupling between them is allowed. Such coupling results in an “umbrella” structure, where the relative contribution of each component can be directly measured by the canting angle of the moments away from the c-axis19.

The question of whether any AFM phases exist in Co3Sn2S2 has triggered significant debate. For instance, μSR experiments reported the presence of the 120° phase competing in real space with the FM phase at temperatures above 90 K15,16. However, no other technique apart from μSR has reported the presence of the 120° phase in large volumes. Therefore, it would be interesting to consider whether the perturbing muon may have an effect that has not been elucidated. A phase of unknown type was also observed in spatially resolved ARPES (angle resolved photoelectron emission spectroscopy), and identified as AFM based on comparison to DFT calculations14. However, ARPES is a surface-sensitive technique, and therefore, it is not yet established whether the same phase occurs deep in the bulk.

With regard to the all-in-all-out AFM structure, reports involving neutron diffraction proposed the combined umbrella structure with a large canting angle for In-doped Co3Sn2S217, but argued against it for undoped Co3Sn2S213,17. A nonlinear optics study also claimed to observe the umbrella structure at low temperatures, with an evolution from a dynamical superposition state above 130 K19. On the other hand, a very recent NMR study argued in favor of the umbrella structure at all temperatures, but with an additional breaking of the in-plane threefold symmetry at temperatures above 90 K18. In general, the observation of a shift in the magnetic hysteresis loops in Co3Sn2S2 akin to exchange bias has been speculated to arise from the presence of an AFM phase coexisting with a FM phase20. However, others have pointed out that some behaviors in the applied field may appear as phase transitions but actually arise from changes in the demagnetizing field with temperature21, and are therefore dependent on the sample size and shape. Beyond the magnetic structure, a microscopic understanding of the ground state has also been elusive. A previous report of a large negative orbital magnetic moment in Co3Sn2S222 requires new consideration due to recent x-ray absorption spectroscopy and x-ray magnetic circular dichroism (XAS-XMCD) results14. Based on the sum rule analysis, the orbital contribution to the magnetic moment in Co3Sn2S2 is negligible. Similarly, studies of the exchange interactions and local structure, which could better elucidate how the ground state forms, have produced a number of very puzzling results, such as one claim that the distant-neighbor “cross-hexagon” interaction dominates the Hamiltonian23. These observations clearly suggest the need for more scrutiny on the magnetic structure and dynamics in Co3Sn2S2.

In this work, we undertook a series of experiments with bulk-sensitive probes that are not likely to perturb the magnetic ground state, including inelastic neutron scattering (INS), polarized and unpolarized neutron diffraction, Mössbauer spectroscopy, and diffuse magnetic neutron scattering. We find that all of our experimental observations can be explained within a model containing a simple Hamiltonian, which we derive within linear spin wave theory (LSWT). By including the antisymmetric Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya interaction (DMI), the ground state of this Hamiltonian is the canted umbrella structure with a finite scalar spin chirality, and the spectrum becomes gapped, consistent with previous neutron scattering results17,24. Diffuse magnetic scattering experiments also provide evidence that critical magnetic fluctuations exhibit low spin dimensionality, a situation that places Co3Sn2S2 in a category with other XY-like magnetic systems with strongly anisotropic Hamiltonians. In this case, the spin anisotropy may be generated by the DMI. Finally, polarized and unpolarized neutron diffraction experiments provide additional support for the canted umbrella structure, with a canting angle of about 15°, which is completely consistent with the magnetic ground state predicted by our Hamiltonian. Both polarized neutron diffraction and Mössbauer experiments, which are non-perturbing probes, provide strong evidence that the true microscopic ground state of Co3Sn2S2 remains in a single phase throughout the entire ordered region below TC = 178 K. The crucial role of the DMI in the magnetic ground state invites a new interpretation of magnetism in this kagome magnet, as well as the electronic structure.

Although Co3Sn2S2 is a metal, it is not atypical to fit even highly itinerant metals such as elemental Fe, Ni, and Co with an effective local-moment Hamiltonian. To obtain the values of the different J, we used linear spin wave theory within the SpinW software31 and looped over all three J parameters to build a set of trial models. Since the spin wave energy scales as the product SJ, we label the interaction in this way throughout the work in order to emphasize this dependency. However, it is interesting to note that using the observed ordered moment, S = 0.15, J1 itself results in extremely large and unrealistic exchange interactions, reaching values larger than 370 meV. Therefore, we assume the moment of S = 1 found in metallic Co is more realistic, leading to a natural identification of SJ as J. For every trial model, we started from a ferromagnetic structure and optimized the spin directions to minimize the total energy per spin, and then calculated the spin wave dispersion. Qualitatively, the dispersion exhibits a crossover between elongated ring-like features below E ≲ 30 meV, to stripe-like features for E ≳ 30 meV up to the measurement limit at E = 70 meV. To emphasize this pattern, we also sketch dashed gray lines in the individual scans in Fig. 2, which roughly show the shape and position of the dispersion pattern in Fig. 1f–h. At the highest energies measured, the spin waves are still dispersing outward along the (H00) direction, reaching about halfway to the zone boundary in reciprocal space (Fig. 1h). We note that since our data are collected along the bottom of the spin wave spectrum where the spin wave cones are still steeply dispersing, the J1 interaction represents an effective overall magnetic exchange which, considering the entire magnetic bandwidth, should be distributed among a set of in-plane exchange parameters that we cannot resolve in our experiment. In other words, above 70 meV, our Hamiltonian also predicts the formation of additional spin wave bands, where the specific shapes of these bands may depend on other small next-nearest-neighbor couplings. Nevertheless, we do not expect these small additions to be significant for the ground state and low-energy part of the spectrum.

To further investigate whether a spin-anisotropic Hamiltonian is appropriate for Co3Sn2S2, we carried out diffuse magnetic scattering (DMS) experiments using the cold neutron diffractometer DMC (Paul Scherrer Institut, Switzerland) with the same 450 mg sample as in the IN8 experiments. As shown in Fig. 1i, we find evidence for short-range magnetic correlations which peak at the Q = (003) reflection, and exhibit a maximum at the ordering temperature TC = 178 K. This peak is well-defined and isotropic in reciprocal space with correlation length near 7.4 Å (see Supplementary Material); therefore, it is associated with the critical spin fluctuations related to the ordered phase.

In the critical region near a phase transition, the intensity of elastic diffuse neutron scattering is effectively a measure of the static equal-time structure factor, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S(Q)=\int_{-\infty }^{\infty }S(Q,\omega )d\omega$$\end{document}S(Q)=∫−∞∞S(Q,ω)dω, which also makes it proportional to the static magnetic susceptibility χ0 = S(0)/T due to the Kramers-Kronig relation32,33. This identification allows the DMS technique to measure the susceptibility critical exponent γ associated with the phase transition. Fitting the blue points in Fig. 1i, we find γ = 0.61 ± 0.07, whereas the values are expected to range between 1 and 1.75 for the Ising, Heisenberg, and mean-field models34,35. This suggests that these three model classes do not describe Co3Sn2S2. On the other hand, we find surprisingly good agreement between our experimentally observed γ and a Kosterlitz-Thouless model with XY spin anisotropy, following the procedure detailed in studies of the layered magnetic systems CaRu2O432 and Rb2CrCl433. In this quasi-2D model, the ratio of interaction strengths J1/Jc is a measure of the effective 2D system size36, and we use the values derived from our INS Hamiltonian. Although the isotropic momentum dependence of the diffuse scattering in Co3Sn2S2 suggests spin fluctuations of 3D character, the strong deviation from the expected critical exponent establishes the existence of spin anisotropy. The DMI in our Hamiltonian is a natural origin for such anisotropy, and its giant magnitude in Co3Sn2S2 can also explain why spin anisotropic fluctuations of 2D-like character appear around the high TC = 178 K. This is in contrast with established XY systems like K2CuF4, where the intrinsic XY anisotropy generated by spin-orbit coupling appears only below T = 10 K, while the exchange anisotropy is on the order of 0.00137,38. We are not aware of other works demonstrating a connection between the DMI and low-dimensional critical fluctuations, and it will be interesting to carry out further experiments elucidating its other consequences in Co3Sn2S2. Overall, our DMC experiments provide supportive evidence for the inclusion of a DMI.

The role of the DMI in our Hamiltonian is crucial for two reasons. First, a spin canting angle appears under any strength of the DMI, resulting in the noncollinear umbrella structure. Additionally, previous neutron scattering experiments also reported a low-energy gap developing below TC, and reaching E = 2−3 meV at base temperature17,24. In our model, the DMI alone reproduces this gap with no need to invoke additional forms of anisotropy, such as single-ion anisotropy. Therefore, our simple model is sufficient to capture all the major properties previously known about magnetic interactions in Co3Sn2S2.

Nevertheless, our ZEBRA experiments are consistent with a single canted magnetic structure below TC, with no detectable changes in the structure appearing at any intermediate temperature.

To search for further evidence of a canting angle, we also carried out polarized neutron diffraction, using a specialized scattering geometry to account for the sample's ferromagnetism. As demonstrated in a previous study (ref. 13), standard neutron polarization measurements on Co3Sn2S2 are not very informative, because the neutron beam quickly loses its polarization via Larmor precession when passing through a sequence of FM domains. On the other hand, when the neutron beam polarization can be aligned exactly along the axis of a uniaxial ferromagnet like Co3Sn2S2, the Larmor precession inside the sample will be zero because the neutron spin is parallel to the local field inside every domain, which is along + c or − c. Moreover, even after the scattering event occurs, the neutron spin continues to precess around the same local field direction without depolarization. As a result, measurements of the magnetic structure factor are possible with this geometry (see Methods).

We measured five reflections as a function of temperature (Fig. 3d) with the TASP cold triple-axis spectrometer (Paul Scherrer Institut, Switzerland)41,42, using the 170 mg single crystal previously studied in ref. 13. Neutron polarization analysis was carried out using the MuPad zero field polarimeter43. The beam polarization P along the c-direction decreases smoothly below TC for the three reflections Q1, Q2, and Q3, as shown in Fig. 3d. We omit the two strong nuclear peaks Q4 and Q5 in the analysis, since these are not expected to exhibit large changes in polarization. The absolute values of depolarization (Fig. 3d) are found to be similar at all temperatures for Q1 and Q2, which are indeed expected to be equivalent, while Q3 is inequivalent. This indicates that the measurement technique is not spoiled by another source of depolarization in our experiments, such as fringing fields from the ferromagnetic sample, which would produce strongly different effects even for equivalent reflections. Therefore, the measured polarization of about P = 0.915 (8.5% decrease) and 0.94 (6% decrease) at base temperature are most likely accurate measurements of the magnetic structure factor.

Our model calculations for different canting angles in the umbrella structure are shown in Fig. 3e. To compare this with experimental data, it is important to consider two sources of additional depolarization. One factor is the effect of additional depolarization in domain walls, which in the limit of many domain walls scales as exp( − Nδ2) where N is the number of domain walls and δ is their size44,45. This shows that for small values of Nδ2, the change of P is linear and may be corrected by simply shifting all experimentally measured values by the same amount. Another factor to consider is contamination by the λ/2 beam harmonic, which is expected to have an intensity of a few percent of the main beam, and which can also be corrected by a linear shift (see Methods); the true values of λ/2 and domain wall depolarization are not known independently. Nevertheless, the total linear shift is strongly constrained by the canting angle dependence of the calculated structure factors, leading to only one angle that satisfies the difference 8.5–6 = 2.5% between the two inequivalent reflections. To obtain agreement, as shown in Fig. 3e, the experimental points are shifted by assuming a pure 2.5% λ/2 contribution, and the calculations of the polarization are made using the expected ordered magnetic moment of 0.3 μB and the nuclear structure factors obtained from our ZEBRA refinements. This shows that the best fit canting angle is near 17°, which is precisely consistent with our ZEBRA experiments.

The smoothness of the slope at all three reflections (Fig. 3d) strongly indicates that the magnetic structure of Co3Sn2S2 remains in a single phase throughout the entire ordered phase, since even a change of 5 degrees in canting angle would be detectable in our experiments. Even more importantly, any magnetic structure that introduces additional magnetic domain disorder, such as breaking of the threefold rotational symmetry, can be completely ruled out, since this would become visible as a rapid decrease to zero of the neutron beam polarization. Therefore, our polarized neutron experiments are most consistent with a single magnetic phase in Co3Sn2S2 with a canted umbrella structure. Discussion

We have determined the magnetic ground state and magnetic Hamiltonian of the kagome magnet Co3Sn2S2, which has been the subject of earlier studies by numerous groups and methods. The simple Hamiltonian that we obtained has the nearest-neighbor interaction J between Co atoms in the kagome plane as its most dominant term, followed by an unusually large DMI term that is roughly 1/3 of J. Our model suggests that the low-energy spin wave gap in the magnetically ordered state is formed not from any single ion anisotropy, but directly from the DMI term. The appearance of magnetic critical scattering, with a strongly suppressed critical exponent for the susceptibility, is consistent with spin anisotropy generated by the DMI. The DMI also accounts for a canting of the magnetic ordered moments into the umbrella-type structure in the magnetic ground state. We further add evidence for the large DMI by measuring the canting angle directly using unpolarized neutron diffraction on small samples and polarized neutron diffraction in a special geometry, which indicates that the canting angle is nonzero and constant as a function of temperature. Moreover, we find no signs of any phase mixture or second phase transition in Co3Sn2S2 from polarized neutron diffraction and Mössbauer spectroscopy experiments.

The unusually large DMI term in Co3Sn2S2 is worth noting since in bulk materials it is at most 1% of the exchange term47, except for some systems such as Lu2V2O7 with 18% value48. Even in artificially engineered layers, where an FM layer is grown on top of a material with strong spin-orbit coupling, such as a heavy metal, to maximize interface DMI, the size of the DMI is at most 10% of the exchange interaction47. Our discovery of an unprecedentedly large DMI has significant consequences, including the formation of a non-collinear canted FM structure. We believe that this large DMI term is responsible for the strong magnetic anisotropy exhibited in this compound. If there were no DMI term, but only the Heisenberg terms, there would not be such a large magnetic anisotropy. Our results also invite a re-evaluation of the spin-orbit coupling degree of freedom in Co3Sn2S2, which is the origin of DMI, as well as the position of the Weyl nodes, in order to construct a more accurate electronic structure model that is consistent with our low-energy effective magnetic Hamiltonian.

An important consequence of the DMI term is chirality49,50, which may be the origin of the giant anomalous Hall effect in this compound51 rather than the Weyl nodes that are 60 meV above the Fermi level, which were calculated assuming a collinear c-axis ferromagnet. Our large DMI and associated chirality have direct relevance for understanding the microscopic origin of the appearance of two orbital magnon modes52 and recently observed chiral phonons in Co3Sn2S2 53,54. The chirality coupled to the ferromagnetic order imprints a handedness in the system, tunable by the external magnetic field, which we believe is the origin of the chiral phonons. We additionally speculate that Co3Sn2S2 may be a fertile ground to host skyrmions and that the domain walls are chiral due to the large DMI. Additionally important is the extended parameter regime that our unusually large DMI opens up, which may potentially lead to unexplored emergent physics.

Methods

Sample characteristics

Co3Sn2S2 has the same crystal structure as shandite Ni3Pb2S2 and belongs to the space group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R\bar{3}m$$\end{document}R3¯m. It can be viewed as a layered kagome structure (Fig. 1a), where the transition metal atoms (Ni, Co) form a kagome lattice with post-transition metal atoms (Pb, Sn) sitting at the center of the hexagon forming the kagome. At the center of each Co triangle, S atoms sit either above or below the kagome plane. For adjacent Co triangles, the S alternates between above and below. The distance between the Co atom in the kagome plane and the S atom above or below the kagome plane is the shortest (2.20 Å) in the compound, resulting in strong bonds. The bond length between the Co atoms forming the kagome lattice is 2.864 Å.

Sample growth and characterization

The 8.1 mg Co3Sn2S2 Zebra sample was grown by a chemical vapor transport (CVT) method. Complete details are described in ref. 14.

For the 450 mg sample used at IN8 and DMC, and the 178 mg sample used at TASP, single crystals were grown by a slow-cooling melt method. Co chunks, Sn granules, and S pieces were mixed in the stoichiometric ratio of Co:Sn:S = 3:2:2. The mixture was loaded into a quartz tube, evacuated to a pressure better than 1 × 10−3 Pa, and flame sealed. The sealed ampoule was placed in a furnace and heated according to the following profile. It was first heated to 400 °C at 1.25 °C min−1 and held for 50 h to allow prereaction of sulfur. The temperature was then raised to 1000 °C at 1 °C min−1 and maintained for 72 h, during which the ampoule was thoroughly rocked to ensure complete homogenization of the melt. Subsequently, the melt was slowly cooled to 900 °C at an ultralow rate of 0.017 °C min−1, followed by natural cooling to room temperature over approximately 10 h. Finally, shiny bulk single crystals of Co3Sn2S2 were obtained.

Magnetization measurements

The magnetization measurements were carried out using a vibration sample magnetometer (VSM) with the sample mounted on a straw using GE varnish. The field was oriented parallel to the c-axis. The magnetization vs temperature measurements were carried out at a low field of H = 500 Oe to not smear the phase transition, but large enough such that the low field behavior due to magnetic domain configuration does not play a role in the temperature-dependent magnetization.

IN8 experiments and SpinW model

INS data were collected at the IN8 thermal triple-axis spectrometer at the Institut Laue-Langevin, Grenoble, France29,30. We used a 450 mg single crystal, which was tied to an aluminum plate with aluminum wire and oriented in the (H0L) scattering plane.

For energy transfers up to E = 32 meV, we used kF = 2.66 Å−1 with a Si(111) monochromator and PG(002) analyzer. For E = 32 meV and higher, we used kF = 4.1 Å−1 with a Cu(200) monochromator and PG(002) analyzer. To remove harmonic contamination, a PG filter was put on kF. The error bars in the raw data (Fig. 2) indicate statistical error, while the horizontal error bars on the fitted peak centers are the standard error of the fit parameter.

Since the spin wave spectrum shows upward dispersing cones stemming from the (003) and (006) positions, we started by scanning over these areas at each energy to determine the position of spurious features to avoid. In some of the scans in Fig. 2, we used rocking scans instead of (H00) scans to avoid nearby spurions. However, the differences between L scans and rocking scans are negligible in all cases, since the spin wave dispersion contains features that are highly elongated along the L direction at all energies (see Fig. 1).

ZEBRA experiments

For neutron diffraction experiments on the 170 mg crystal, we used the ZEBRA diffractometer (Paul Scherrer Institut, Switzerland) with neutron wavelengths of λ = 1.383 Å and 1.178 Å to measure the intensity of eight reflections between T = 10 K and 300 K. The use of 1.178 Å completely rules out the possibility of λ/2 contributions, thus proving that the temperature-dependent effects are caused by extinction and/or change in internal positions.

For experiments on the small 8.1 mg sample, the sample was mounted in the four-circle cradle, and we used λ = 1.383 Å from the (220) reflection of a Ge monochromator. Rocking scans were collected at seven reflections as a function of temperature. The appearance of a small second grain in the crystal was noticed (about 10% of the signal), motivating a two-peak Gaussian fit for each scan. The integrated intensity was obtained using the area of the Gaussian fit using the component of the first grain, which was well centered in every scan, and appending the experimentally obtained statistical error of all points in the scan.

Mössbauer experiments

Experiments were carried out in a constant acceleration spectrometer at temperatures between T = 4.2 K and 300 K, using a laboratory Ba119mSnO3 source kept at room temperature. The main phase of Co3Sn2S2 can be fitted using two Mössbauer sites of equal proportion, in agreement with the two crystallographic Sn sites of identical multiplicity (3a and 3b) in Co3Sn2S2. In addition to the main phase, we detected a small ( < 5%) SnIV-based impurity phase near zero velocity that is probably SnO2, and which does not affect our analysis of the main phase.

DMC experiments

We carried out diffuse magnetic scattering (DMS) experiments using the cold neutron diffractometer DMC (Paul Scherrer Institut, Switzerland). We used the long wavelength setting λ = 4.519 Å with the same 450 mg sample used for our IN8 experiments, and collected wide-area maps as a function of temperature between T = 1.5 K and 300 K. The data were analyzed with DMCpy60 and the integration procedure was carried out in Mathematica.
