Morphological and mechanical properties of masticatory muscles in patients with temporomandibular disorders: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Besime Ahu Kaynak, Ümit Yüzbaşıoğlu, Ece Eki̇ci̇, Serkan Taş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTemporomandibular disorders (TMDs) are defined by pain and dysfunction of the masticatory system, often accompanied by alterations in muscle activity and structures. The masticatory muscles play a critical role in jaw function and their morphological and mechanical characteristics may reflect underlying pathophysiological processes. However, alterations reported in muscle thickness, stiffness, in individuals with TMDs remain inconsistent across studies. This study aimed to investigate alterations in masticatory muscle morphology and muscle biomechanical characteristics in individuals with TMDs relative to healthy controls.MethodsWe performed a comprehensive literature search using the PubMed, Web of Science, and Scopus platforms. We included studies that evaluated morphological or biomechanical features of the masticatory muscles in participants with TMDs and healthy groups, provided that the articles were published in English. We evaluated the methodological rigor of each study using a modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale tailored for case-control research. Group comparisons were summarized using standardized mean differences (SMDs) along with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsThirteen studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review, of which 12 were suitable for meta-analysis. Seven studies were rated as high quality and six as moderate quality. The meta-analysis revealed that masseter muscle stiffness was significantly higher in the TMDs group (SMD = 0.78; 95% CI = 0.34–1.22; p < 0.001).ConclusionsThe findings indicate increased masseter muscle stiffness in individuals with TMDs, suggesting possible neuromuscular hyperactivity or altered muscle tone. Further high-quality studies integrating morphological and mechanical parameters are required to clarify the mechanisms underlying these changes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08548-4.

Introduction

Temporomandibular disorders (TMDs) are multifactorial clinical conditions involving the masticatory muscles, temporomandibular joint (TMJ) and associated structures [1, 2]. TMDs, defined as a public health problem affecting approximately 34% of the adult population with growing importance, are explained by the complex interaction of biomechanical, neuromuscular, psychosocial, and biochemical factors [3]. The TMJ has multiple functions, including chewing, swallowing, speaking, and oral cavity articulation [2]. The masticatory muscles, including the masseter, temporalis, medial pterygoid, and lateral pterygoid muscles, play an important role in mandibular movement and functional performance of the TMJ [4]. Current pathophysiological frameworks describe dynamic, bidirectional relationships between muscle dysfunction, pain, and joint alterations, which may differ across TMDs subtypes [4, 5].

In recent years, increased interest in the morphological and mechanical properties of orofacial muscles has suggested that these muscles may play an active role in the pathogenesis of the disorder in addition to showing maladaptive changes related to TMDs [6–8]. Bruxism also may increase mechanical loading on the masticatory muscle–TMJ system and has been suggested as a contributing factor to the development and maintenance of TMDs symptoms [9–11].

When evaluating masticatory muscle morphology, techniques such as ultrasonography (US) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), have been used to examine changes in muscle volume, thickness, fiber orientation, and elasticity. Some studies have identified structural and mechanical differences in these muscles in patients with TMDs, such as atrophy, increased muscle stiffness, and altered elastic modulus [8, 12, 13]. In contrast, some studies have reported an increase in the thickness of the masticatory muscles in individuals with TMDs [7, 14]or no change [15–17] and no significant difference was found in muscle stiffness. The results in the literature are heterogeneous, with some studies reporting distinct muscle morphological changes while others fail to confirm these differences. This may stem from sample differences, diversity in measurement methods, and inadequacies in distinguishing clinical subtypes. Therefore, a systematic review and quantitative synthesis of the current findings is important for understanding the muscle morphology associated with TMDs.

In this context, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to comprehensively evaluate the available evidence by comparing the mechanical and morphological properties of the masticatory muscles in patients with TMDs and healthy individuals. Our study aimed to provide a clearer picture of the role of muscle morphology in the pathophysiology of TMDs by combining data obtained from different measurement methods.

The significance of this study lies in its scientific support for the necessity of considering the structural characteristics of muscle tissue in the clinical assessment and rehabilitation approaches for TMDs. Although a previous meta-analysis has evaluated masseter muscle stiffness in individuals with TMDs, our meta-analysis provides the first comprehensive quantitative synthesis comparing the morphological and mechanical properties of other masticatory muscles between individuals with TMDs and healthy controls [18]. Accordingly, it was hypothesized that masticatory muscles in patients with TMDs would exhibit significantly increased muscle stiffness and decreased muscle thickness and/or mass compared to healthy individuals.

Materials and methods

This review was carried out following the principles outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement for systematic reviews and meta-analyses [19]. The study protocol was prospectively recorded in the PROSPERO database (Registration ID: CRD420251043639).

Eligibility Criteria

Study selection was guided by the PICOS components, including population characteristics, intervention type, comparison conditions, outcome measures, and design features [20]. According to the PICOS framework, the study population consisted of individuals aged 18–65 years with TMDs, while the control group comprised healthy individuals in the same age range. The outcome measures of this study included the thickness and stiffness of the masticatory muscles. In addition to case-control studies, cross-sectional and randomized controlled studies evaluating the mechanical and morphological characteristics of muscles in both sexes were included. Information sources and selection process

Two independent researchers (ÜY, EE) conducted a comprehensive literature search in the Web of Science, PubMed, and Scopus databases. The review included articles published in English between September 1995 and September 2025. Prior to the literature search, keywords were divided into four groups, and searches were performed using the Boolean operators ‘AND’ and ‘OR.’ Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and free-text terms were identified for the literature search. The search strategies employed in the three databases are reported in Appendix 1.

All records identified through the keyword-based literature search were imported into the Rayyan platform. After the database search was completed, two reviewers independently examined the titles and abstracts of the retrieved studies in accordance with predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Articles deemed unsuitable during this initial screening stage were removed from further evaluation. The remaining studies underwent full-text assessment, and any discrepancies between the reviewers were resolved with input from a third researcher (ST). Study data that met the inclusion criteria were independently extracted by two researchers. Data extraction was performed using a standard Excel table categorized according to specific criteria. The criteria included in the standard table were as follows: primary author and year of publication, population characteristics, diagnostic criteria/methods, number and age of participants, muscles evaluated, assessment methods used to determine muscle mechanical and/or morphological characteristics, and outcome measures.

Assessment of risk of bias (methodological quality)

The methodological quality was evaluated using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) adapted for cross-sectional studies by Moskalewicz et al. [22], which has been previously validated and shown to be reliable. In the present study, certain components of the scale were modified: the “non-respondents” item under the “selection” domain and the second item under “comparability” were excluded, as they were not applicable to the included studies. The final adapted version of the NOS comprised three domains and six items—selection (3 items), comparability (1 item), and outcome (2 items)—with a total score ranging from 0 to 6. Based on this scoring, studies were classified as high (6 points), medium (4–5 points), low (2–3 points), or poor quality (0–1 point) [22]. Each item was evaluated independently by two reviewers (ÜY and EE), and any differences in judgments were addressed with input from a third investigator (ST).

Synthesis method and analysis

The meta-analysis procedures were carried out using Review Manager (RevMan) Version 5.4.1 (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, Copenhagen, and The Cochrane Collaboration, London, UK). For each muscle outcome, analyses were initiated only when a minimum of two eligible studies were available. A random-effects approach employing the inverse-variance technique was applied to estimate pooled effects comparing individuals with TMDs to healthy groups. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated in cases where measurement scales differed across studies, whereas mean differences (MDs) were used when outcomes shared identical units. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified using the I² statistic. In accordance with Cochrane recommendations, I² values were interpreted as: 90–100% indicating very high heterogeneity, 60–90% substantial, 30–60% moderate, and 0–30% minimal heterogeneity. When heterogeneity exceeded 75%, potential methodological sources were examined and sensitivity analyses were conducted by omitting lower-quality studies. The studies included in the meta-analysis were evaluated for publication bias and potential bias in results reporting using a funnel plot. A funnel plot is a simple scatter plot where the x-axis shows the estimates of the evaluation results obtained from the studies, and the y-axis shows the estimated standard error, giving an idea about the sample size. The funnel plot shows an asymmetric distribution in the presence of publication bias [23].

Results

Study selection

After duplicate studies were removed, 9.204 unique records proceeded to title/abstract screening, and 9,066 records were excluded because their irrelevance to the research question was clearly evident from the title and abstract. Of the remaining 138 records, one was excluded because the full text could not be accessed. Of the remaining 137 articles, 42 were excluded because they did not examine the mechanical and morphological characteristics of the masticatory muscles, and 53 were excluded because these studies were conducted on different patient populations. In addition, 19 studies were excluded because they did not compare the mechanical and morphological characteristics of the muscles between individuals with TMDs and healthy controls, and 7 studies were excluded due to missing data. A total of 3 articles were excluded due to foreign language suitability, incorrect study design, and retracted articles. Five articles were removed because they did not align with the required study designs (randomized controlled trials or case-control studies). Ultimately, 13 studies were deemed eligible for inclusion. An overview of the screening process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

The characteristics of the included studies are listed in Table 2. All studies were conducted on adult populations. Among the masticatory muscles investigated, five studies examined masseter muscle stiffness, while no measurements of muscle stiffness were found for the other masticatory muscles. In three of these five studies, mechanical properties were assessed using a myotonometer, while the shear wave elastography method was used in two. Regarding the morphological characteristics of the masticatory muscles, masseter muscle thickness was examined in eight studies, temporalis anterior in three studies, and pterygoideus lateralis in two studies. Muscle volume of the masticatory muscles was assessed using MRI in only one study [6].

Meta-analysis

During data entry into the meta-analysis software, several conversions were performed. Specifically, values reported as median (25th–75th percentile) were converted to mean ± standard deviation using formulas recommended in the literature [28]. In addition, muscle thickness measurements reported in cm were converted to mm.

Although 13 studies were included in this compilation, Bilgen et al. [6]could not be included in the quantitative evaluation because it was the only study that assessed masticatory muscle volume. The meta-analysis included 12 studies that evaluated the mechanical and morphological characteristics of the masticatory muscles in individuals with TMDs and compared them with healthy controls.

Masseter muscle stiffness was evaluated in 6 studies involving 147 individuals with TMDs and 176 controls [8, 12, 17, 24–26]. A statistically significant difference in masseter muscle stiffness was observed between the TMDs and control groups. Masseter muscle stiffness in individuals with TMDs was 0.78 times higher than that in controls (SMD, 0.78; 95% CI, 0.34–1.22; p = 0.0005). Substantial heterogeneity between studies was noted (I² = 69%) (Fig. 2-a). Furthermore, although it could not be included in the meta-analysis as it was the only study evaluating anterior temporalis muscle stiffness, Akkoca et al. [17]reported statistically significant higher temporalis muscle stiffness in individuals with TMDs compared to controls (Fig. 2-e).

The analysis results of 8 studies comparing 219 individuals with TMDs and 257 healthy controls in terms of masseter muscle thickness found that masseter muscle thickness was similar between the two groups (MD: -0.12; 95% CI: -1.13, 0.88; p = 0.81) (Fig. 2-b) [7, 12–17, 27].The level of heterogeneity between the studies was considerable (I² = 90%).

Three studies involving 89 individuals with TMDs and 122 healthy individuals were examined for differences in anterior temporalis muscle thickness between the groups [15–17]. No difference was found in anterior temporalis muscle thickness between individuals with TMDs and those in the control group (MD: -0.32; 95% CI: -0.85, 0.21; p = 0.24) (Fig. 2-c). Heterogeneity between the studies was moderate (I² = 41%).

Only two studies compared the morphological characteristics of the lateral pterygoid muscle between groups [16, 27]. The meta-analysis results of these studies indicated that the pterygoideus lateralis muscle thickness did not differ between the groups (MD: 0.28; 95% CI: -0.10, 0.66; p = 0.15; I² = 0%) (Fig. 2-d).

Although Bilgen et al.‘s [6] study could not be included in the quantitative analysis process as it was the only study evaluating muscle volume, the results of this study reported that the masseter and medial pterygoideus muscle volumes were significantly reduced in individuals with TMDs compared to healthy controls (Fig. 2-f and -g). The same study observed that the lateral pterygoideus muscle volume did not differ significantly between the groups (Figure-h).

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

Due to considerable heterogeneity (I²: 90%), a sensitivity analysis was performed for the masseter muscle thickness parameter. Depending on the study design, sensitivity analyses were applied by excluding studies from the meta-analysis. The pooled difference and significant values for the masseter muscle thickness did not change significantly when the relevant studies were excluded. For example, when studies with small sample size and low weight in the meta-analysis were excluded, no significant change in the mean difference or its direction was detected (n = 7, MD= -0.37 [95% CI: -1.55, 0.82], p = 0.54, I²=88%, vs. n = 6, MD= -0.01 [95% CI: -0.98, 0.96], p = 0.99, I²=85%).

A funnel plot was used to detect publication bias. Bias analyses of masseter muscle stiffness and muscle thickness exhibited an asymmetric distribution, indicating a potential risk of publication bias (Fig. 3-a and 3-b). The possible reason for the asymmetric distribution of these plots could be the high heterogeneity obtained and the difference in sample size. However, the Egger Test could not be used to determine the severity of publication bias because it cannot be applied in meta-analyses with fewer than 10 studies. However, the results of the bias analysis performed for anterior temporalis and lateral pterygoid muscle thickness showed that studies evaluating both muscle thicknesses had low-to-medium sample sizes and, due to their symmetrical distributions, did not present a risk of publication bias for either parameter (Fig. 3-c and -d).

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the differences in the morphological and mechanical properties of the masticatory muscles between individuals with TMDs and healthy controls. Accordingly, we included 13 studies comparing masticatory muscle characteristics between the two groups, consisting of 12 case-control studies and one randomized controlled trial. Our results demonstrated that masseter muscle stiffness was significantly higher in individuals with TMDs, whereas no significant differences were observed in the stiffness or thickness of the temporalis, medial pterygoid, and lateral pterygoid muscles. Notably, only a limited number of studies have assessed the properties of muscles other than the masseter and muscle volume was evaluated in just a single study that we could not include in the meta-analysis. In that study, the volume of the masseter muscle were found to be significantly lower in individuals with TMDs [6].

An increase in muscle stiffness reflects reduced tissue elasticity and altered viscoelastic properties, which may negatively affect functional performance by limiting movement compliance and load absorption [29, 30]. This alteration may be associated with factors such as myofascial trigger point activity, chronic nociceptive input, or overuse [17, 31]. Furthermore, the clinical significance of increased muscle stiffness should be evaluated in conjunction with its potential impact on associated anatomical structures, particularly the tendons. It has been suggested that tendons, being relatively weaker components of the masticatory system compared to muscle bellies, are highly susceptible to overload and may represent a primary source of pain during palpation, even before significant muscle alterations become evident. A recent review has highlighted that tendinopathy of the masticatory muscles may constitute an important pathogenetic mechanism in TMDs, whereby persistent mechanical stress leads to localized pain characteristics [32]. Therefore, the increased masseter stiffness identified in our meta-analysis may contribute to, or reflect, an underlying tendinopathic process, potentially explaining the chronic pain patterns observed in these patients. However, stiffness is not always directly correlated with functional impairment, as the findings remain inconsistent. For instance, Taş et al. [8] reported no significant relationship between stiffness and jaw-related limitations, whereas Takashima et al. [33] observed a reduced mandibular range of motion with increased masseter stiffness. Similarly, Hara et al. [34] suggested that masseter stiffness might be one of the parameters that reflect bite force. These discrepancies suggest that the functional impact of stiffness likely depends on individual factors such as sex, age, and clinical characteristics, including the underlying pathogenetic mechanism and symptom severity (e.g., pain duration).

Current findings may be clinically significant, as increased muscle stiffness may reflect changes in muscle function and fatigue of the chewing muscle fibers. Therefore, could partly explain the therapeutic benefit of stabilization splints, which are explained by allowing overloaded muscle fibers to rest by redistributing occlusal forces, and also suggest that manual therapy and taping, which aim to modulate muscle tone, increase circulation, and regulate mechanical loading, may contribute to symptom relief in patients with TMDs by reducing muscle stiffness [35–37].

In our meta-analysis, no significant difference was found in masseter muscle thickness between individuals with TMDs and healthy controls. However, when the findings of the included studies were examined, some inconsistencies were observed in the literature. Three studies reported similar results in terms of masseter muscle thickness between the TMDs and control groups [15–17], while five studies reported differences in masseter thickness in individuals with TMDs [7, 12–14, 27]. The authors attributed these differing results to measurements being taken at different points and in repeated ultrasound measurements taken at the same point.

Although our meta-analysis results did not show a significant difference between the groups in terms of masseter muscle thickness, these heterogeneous findings between individual studies suggest that the subject requires further investigation. Furthermore, funnel plots created for masseter muscle thickness indicate a possible publication bias that may be related to small sample effects. Therefore, the combined effect size obtained should be interpreted with caution, considering that the actual clinical effect may be smaller than estimated.

Recent methodological guidelines for dental research have emphasized the importance of considering expected clinical effect sizes when evaluating the adequacy of study sample sizes. According to the recommendations proposed by Zieliński and Więckiewicz studies on the TMJ and masticatory muscles require approximately 20 participants to detect large clinical effects, around 130 participants to detect moderate effects, and more than 1600 participants to reliably detect small effects with 80% statistical power [38]. When the sample sizes of the studies included in this review were compared with these reference values, it appeared that most studies were adequately powered to detect large effects but were generally underpowered to detect moderate or small clinical effects. Therefore, non-significant findings reported for several morphological outcomes, such as muscle thickness, should be interpreted with caution, as they may reflect limited statistical power rather than the true absence of an effect.

No significant differences were found in the volumes of the medial or lateral pterygoid muscles. Given the masseter’s primary role in mastication, it is plausible that this muscle exhibits earlier or more pronounced changes in response to disuse and pain-related adaptations in TMDs [39, 40]. In addition, a recent study investigating the morphology of the masticatory and digastric muscles showed that perceived stress level was a significant predictor of digastric muscle thickness in individuals with TMDs [41]. This finding suggests that muscle morphology may be influenced not only through atrophy–hypertrophy mechanisms but also by processes such as psychological burden and pain-related motor adaptation.

Additionally, the absence of significant differences in the morphological or mechanical properties of the temporalis and pterygoid muscles between individuals with TMDs and controls can be considered within the context of their functional roles and the limitations of the existing literature. The complex structure and different fibre orientations of all masticatory muscles may lead to measurement variability between anatomical regions, which may make it difficult to obtain consistent results [42]. Furthermore, as functional role of the temporalis and pterygoid muscles in mandibular stabilization and movement control is significant, activation of these muscles is highly dependent on the type of mandibular movement, the phase of the chewing cycle, individual motor patterns and the clinical subtype of TMDs. In contrast, the masseter is considered one of the primary force-generating muscles during mastication. Therefore, alterations related to TMJ symptoms may be more prominently reflected in the masseter muscle than in the temporalis and pterygoid muscles [43, 44]. Finally, these muscles have been evaluated in fewer studies in literature, which may have made it difficult to detect differences between groups.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Firstly, the literature search was conducted using only three databases and only articles published in English were included, which may have introduced a language or selection bias. Secondly, the studies included in our meta-analysis employed a variety of assessment methods and measurement devices, and their study designs also exhibited significant variation. Moreover, the results of elastography measurements are influenced by the applied method (e.g., SWE or strain elastography), mandibular position (rest, light closure, maximum clenching), and the level of muscle activation during evaluation (electromyography-guided or passive relaxation), which should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. Although we standardize the data by extracting baseline measurements, this variability remains a potential limitation. Furthermore, while most of the included studies diagnosed TMDs based on the DC/TMD criteria, a few used alternative diagnostic approaches, which may have contributed to methodological inconsistency. Additionally, due to differences in diagnostic criteria and evaluation methods, we couldn’t perform an isolated analysis of TMDs subtypes, which can be considered another factor increasing heterogeneity. Another limitation is that considerable heterogeneity was observed in some of our analyses, particularly for the masseter muscle thickness parameter. Among the possible reasons for this heterogeneity include the role of differences in measurement. As the cumulative number of studies in literature increases and reaches an adequate threshold for quantitative synthesis, future meta-analyses should consider conducting subgroup analyses that evaluate measurement modalities separately. Finally, there was a limited number of studies evaluating muscle volume, therefore, only one study assessing this parameter could be included. It should also be noted that all studies included in this review were cross-sectional in design; therefore, no definitive causal inferences can be drawn. Despite the methodological heterogeneity across studies, this review provides an important synthesis of the current evidence regarding masticatory muscle characteristics in individuals with TMDs. By bringing together findings derived from different approaches, the present study contributes to a better understanding of the potential role of altered muscle properties in TMDs pathophysiology. Future high-quality studies using standardized methods and subtype-specific approaches are needed to better identify muscular alterations in TMDs. Lasty, this study focused exclusively on the morphological and mechanical properties of the muscles. Future research should also expand its focus to include the tendinous attachments of the masticatory muscles. Given that tendons often manifest pain earlier than the muscles themselves due to their lower tolerance for overload, understanding the relationship between muscle stiffness and tendinopathy could provide a more holistic view of TMD pathophysiology [32].

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated the available evidence comparing the mechanical and morphological properties of the masticatory muscles between individuals with TMDs and healthy controls. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first meta-analysis to provide evidence on variations in the morphology of masticatory muscles other than the M. masseter in individuals with TMDs. Results demonstrated that masseter muscle stiffness was significantly higher in individuals with TMDs, whereas no significant differences were observed in other parameters. These findings might suggest that the most significant changes in TMDs occur in mechanical properties and that the masseter muscle may play a fundamental role in this process. Clinicians should therefore consider therapeutic strategies aimed at improving masticatory muscle elasticity to address potential functional limitations associated with increased stiffness. On the other hand, the absence of significant differences in the temporalis and pterygoid muscles may be related to the limited number of studies evaluating these muscles. Overall, while the existing literature demonstrates the effect of TMDs on muscle properties, the heterogeneity of the methods and diagnostic criteria used in the studies limits the generalisability of the results. In the future, high-quality studies using standardised assessment methods will contribute to a clearer understanding of muscle changes associated with TMDs.
