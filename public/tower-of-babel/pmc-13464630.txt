A transcription factor TOE3 simultaneously promotes growth and antiviral immunity by disrupting ABA core module in tobacco
Authors: Bolei Jiao, Baijun Wu, Haonan Fang, Jiale Jiang, Zhongyue Mai, Ning Jiang, Honghui Lin, Dehui Xi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant immune activation often reduces growth, which is defined as “growth-defense trade-off” (GDT). Uncoupling GDT is promising for breeding of elite cultivars with strong growth and immunity. We previously identified that AP2 transcription factor TARGET OF EARLY3 (TOE3) promotes both growth and antiviral defense. However, the mechanism underlying this GDT uncoupling remains unknown. Here, we find that the amino-terminal domain of TOE3 (T3N) inhibits abscisic acid (ABA) signaling. Mechanistically, T3N binds to an ABA receptor PYL4 to interfere with PYL4-PP2C4 interaction. The PYL4-PP2C4 module regulates tobacco growth and antiviral immunity. Thus, under normal conditions, T3N enhances tobacco growth via down-regulating ABA response. Upon TMV infection, the phosphorylation of T3N is induced. Phosphorylated T3N exhibits stronger binding affinity to PYL4 to further amplify its disruptive effect on PYL4-PP2C4 module and strongly block ABA response, thereby boosting antiviral immunity. These findings reveal how TOE3 uncouples GDT to provide strategies for breeding crops with strong growth and antiviral immunity.

INTRODUCTION

When plants are attacked by pathogens, they often trigger a “moderate” immune defense response with reduced growth or development (1). This adverse relationship between growth and defense is the results of reallocating limited resources, which is defined as “growth-defense trade-off” (GDT) (2).

Development of sustainable agricultural production is vital for ensuring global food security, and breeding of crop cultivars that produce high yields and are resistant to diseases is one of the key agricultural strategies to achieve this milestone (3, 4). However, breeding of such cultivars is often limited by GDT because the integration of disease-resistant genes into existing cultivars with high yield often reduced growth and eventually resulted in low yield (5). Thus, better understanding of the genetic basis and molecular mechanisms underlying GDT can help break this bottleneck.

Uncoupling GDT is considered as a promising strategy for breeding of elite cultivars that produce high yield and are resistant to diseases (6). Mutation of the disease susceptibility (S) genes using CRISPR-Cas9 technology has been used to uncouple GDT. Existing studies showed that mutation of eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4E (eIF4E), Mildew resistance locus O (MLO), and the promoters of SWEET11/13/14 resulted in disease resistance without yield penalties (7–9). Adding a pathogen-inducible motif in the promoters of plant defense factors has also been reported to uncouple GDT. Previous studies found that some immune factors, such as IDEAL PLANT ARCHITECTURE 1 (IPA1) and STOREKEEPER (STK), promoted both yield and disease resistance (10, 11). A recent work reengineered autoactive nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat receptors (NLR) through inserting pathogen protease-cleavable blocking peptides. Plant immune response is strongly activated by viral perception, thus promoting immunity to the virus without reducing plant growth (12). These results indicated that remodeling plant immune receptors provided a previously unexplored approach to uncouple GDT. Despite these advances, the mechanism by which certain host factors uncoupling GDT remains largely unclear.

Phytohormones play pivotal roles in regulating plant growth and defense against various pathogens (13). Abscisic acid (ABA) negatively regulated plant growth and functioned as a dual-function regulator in biotic stress responses (14). The core regulatory mechanism of ABA signaling pathway comprises three essential components: pyrabactin resistance 1 (PYR1)/PYR1-like (PYL)/regulatory components of ABA receptor (RCAR) ABA receptors, clade A phosphatase type-2C (PP2C) co-receptors, and sucrose nonfermenting 1-related kinases subfamily2 (SnRK2) (15–17). In the presence of ABA, PYR1/PYL/RCAR receptor proteins can disrupt the interaction of PP2Cs-SnRK2s, resulting in the release of SnRK2s and inhibition of PP2Cs-mediated dephosphorylation of SnRK2s. Thus, SnRK2s is autophosphorylated and activated, enabling SnRK2 kinases to phosphorylate downstream targets to initiate ABA response (18). It has been reported that ABA enhanced plant resistance against viruses, such as tobacco necrosis virus (TNV), bamboo mosaic virus (BaMV), and wheat yellow mosaic virus (WYMV). The activation of ABA response induced callose deposition, which is critical for enhancing plant immunity against TNV (19). ABA could inhibit BaMV infection through regulating RNA interference pathway (20, 21). ABA signaling pathway was disrupted by viral factor to facilitate WYMV infection (22). ABA could also induce decreased resistance against virus, such as rice black-streaked dwarf virus (RBSDV), rice stripe virus (RSV), potato virus X, and apple necrotic mosaic virus (ApNMV). In rice, ABA treatment increased susceptibility to RBSDV via suppressing the jasmonic acid (JA) pathway and preventing reactive oxygen species accumulation (23). ABA response negatively regulated the expression of Ferredoxin1 (FD1) to suppress plant defense against RSV (24). Overexpression of ABA insensitive 5 (ABI5), an ABA response gene, led to the susceptibility of apple and Nicotiana benthamiana to ApNMV infection (25). Despite ABA pathway is known to play a dual role in plant antiviral defense, little is known about how ABA regulated plant immunity against other viruses and the mechanism of how the ABA signaling is manipulated during viral infection.

Although our previous work found that AP2 transcription factor TARGET OF EARLY3 (TOE3) promotes both plant growth and antiviral defense in tobacco (26, 27), the molecular mechanism by which TOE3 uncouples GDT remains unknown. In this study, we aim to elucidate this mechanism, thereby providing insights for breeding of crop elite cultivars with strong growth and antiviral defense traits.

RESULTS

N-terminal domain of TOE3 interacts with PYL4 and inhibits ABA signaling pathway

Our previous study reported that overexpression of NtTOE3 promoted both growth and antiviral defense in Nicotiana tabacum (26). To investigate the mechanism of uncoupling GDT behind TOE3, we analyzed transcriptome deep sequencing [RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)] data from wild-type (WT) and NtTOE3-knockout plants (CR-TOE3 #29) at 7 days post–TMV infection (dpi) (26). Among these differentially expressed genes (DEGs), 270 DEGs were up-regulated and 233 DEGs were down-regulated in CR-TOE3 #29 lines. Our previous study focused on the up-regulated DEGs that are transcriptionally activated by NtTOE3 (26). In this study, Gene Ontology enrichment analysis revealed that “abscisic acid–activated signaling pathway” and “cellular response to abscisic acid stimulus” showed the most highly enrichment score among the 233 down-regulated DEGs (Fig. 1A and table S1). The results of real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) assay further demonstrated that the relative expression levels of ABA response genes were strongly up-regulated in the leaves of CR-TOE3 #29 and CR-TOE3 #68 tobaccos, compared with those of WT plants (Fig. 1B). To further investigate the relationship between TOE3 and ABA response, we germinated the seeds of WT and NtTOE3-overexpressing tobaccos (TOE3-OE #4 and TOE3-OE #5) on Murashige-Skoog (MS) medium with or without 10 μM ABA. The results showed that there was no significant difference on the germination rates among WT, TOE3-OE #4, and TOE3-OE #5 on MS medium. In the presence of 10 μM ABA, TOE3-OE #4 and TOE3-OE#5 plants were more tolerant to ABA than WT in terms of germination rate (Fig. 1, C and D). When the seeds of WT, CR-TOE3 #29, and CR-TOE3 #68 were germinated on MS, the germination rates of these lines were all 100%. However, when seeds were germinated on MS containing 8 μM ABA, the germination rates of CR-TOE3 #29 and CR-TOE3 #68 were both significantly decreased compared with those of WT (Fig. 1, E and F). Together, these results demonstrate that overexpression of NtTOE3 suppresses ABA signaling.

To explore the molecular mechanism of TOE3-mediated inhibition of ABA response, we analyzed yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screen results of our previous study using NtTOE3 as a bait and a normalized N. tabacum cDNA as a prey library. We identified the ABA receptor NtPYL4 as a potential NtTOE3-interacting protein (27). Then, the interaction between NtPYL4 and NtTOE3 was examined by Y2H assays (Fig. 2A). To further validate the interaction between NtPYL4 and NtTOE3 in planta, bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assays were also performed. As shown in Fig. 2 (B and C), when we coexpressed the full length of NtTOE3 or the N terminus of NtTOE3 fused with the N terminus of yellow fluorescent protein (NtTOE3-nYFP or NtT3N-nYFP) and NtPYL4 fused to the C terminus of YFP (NtPYL4-cYFP) in N. benthamiana leaves, strong YFP fluorescence signals were detected in the nucleus, which indicated that the N terminus of NtTOE3 mediates the physical interaction with NtPYL4 (fig. S1 and Fig. 2, B and C). We also examined this interaction in vivo using split-luciferase complementation (SLC) assays in N. benthamiana leaves. Coexpression of NtTOE3-nLUC or NtT3N-nLUC with cLUC-NtPYL4 resulted in strong luminescent signals, suggesting that NtT3N interacted with NtPYL4 in vivo (Fig. 2D). This interaction was also validated using in vitro glutathione S-transferase (GST) pull-down assays. The Western blotting (WB) results revealed that only GST-NtTOE3 and GST-NtT3N, but not GST, GST-NtT3M, and GST-NtT3C, can pull down maltose binding protein‌ (MBP)-NtPYL4 protein in vitro (Fig. 2E). Together, these data demonstrate that NtTOE3 interacts with NtPYL4 via its N terminus.

Then, we examined the interaction between NbTOE3 and NbPYL4. The results of Y2H, BiFC, and SLC assays confirmed that the N terminus of NbTOE3 (NbT3N) physically bound to NbPYL4 (fig. S2, A to D). RT-qPCR results showed that silencing of NbTOE3 using the tobacco rattle virus (TRV)–induced gene silencing method significantly increased the transcriptional expression levels of ABA response genes (fig. S3, A to C). Moreover, overexpression of NbTOE3 and NbT3N, but not NbT3M and NbT3C, could significantly down-regulate the expression levels of ABA response genes compared to the empty vector (EV) group (fig. S4). These data demonstrate that the N terminus of TOE3 plays a pivotal role in inhibiting ABA response. Collectively, these results suggest that the N terminus of TOE3 interacts with PYL4 and suppressing ABA signaling pathway in both N. tabacum and N. benthamiana.

Overexpression of TOE3 N terminus inhibits ABA response to promote both tobacco growth and antiviral immunity

To further explore whether ABA signaling was involved in regulating tobacco growth and antiviral resistance, 28-day-old N. tabacum and N. benthamiana plants were sprayed with 0.2% ethanol (EtOH; used as control) or 100 μM ABA every 3 days. The results showed that the fresh weight and plant height of N. tabacum and N. benthamiana plants were significantly reduced when treated with 100 μM ABA after 7 days (figs. S5, A to C, and S7, A to C). Furthermore, 28-day-old N. tabacum and N. benthamiana plants were rub inoculated with TMV and sprayed with 0.2% EtOH or 100 μM ABA every 3 days. The disease symptoms were subsequently categorized on the basis of the severity of systematically infected leaves of N. tabacum plants (fig. S6). At 14 dpi, N. tabacum plants treated with 100 μM ABA appeared with obviously interveinal chlorotic symptoms caused by TMV infection, while better growth, milder symptoms and more green recovery tissue were observed in the control plants (fig. S5, A to D). The WB results demonstrated much higher levels of TMV-coat protein (CP) accumulation in N. tabacum plants treated with 100 μM ABA than in control plants at 14 dpi (fig. S5, E and F). At 7 dpi, the systemic leaves of N. benthamiana plants treated with 100 μM ABA exhibited severer disease symptom and had substantially higher viral accumulation level (fig. S7, A to E). These results indicated that ABA treatment negatively regulated the growth and antiviral resistance in both N. tabacum and N. benthamiana.

To further investigate the relationship between ABA suppression and plant growth as well as antiviral defense, fluridone, a specific inhibitor of ABA biosynthesis, was used in this study. Twenty-day-old N. tabacum and N. benthamiana plants were sprayed with 5% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; control) or 100 μM fluridone continuously for 3 days. Seven days later, fluridone-treated plants showed significantly increased fresh weight and plant height compared with control plants (figs. S5, G to I, and S7, F to H). Furthermore, plants were rub inoculated with TMV and then sprayed with 5% DMSO or 100 μM fluridone continuously for 3 days. At 14 dpi in N. tabacum and 7 dpi in N. benthamiana, fluridone-treated plants exhibited more robust growth, milder disease symptoms, and substantially lower TMV-CP accumulation than control plants (figs. S5, H to L, and S7, G to J). These results suggest that inhibition of ABA synthesis enhances both growth and antiviral immunity in both N. tabacum and N. benthamiana.

To validate the relationship between T3N and ABA response, we generated NtT3N-overexpressing N. tabacum lines (T3N-OE) in this study (fig. S8). Then, we germinated the seeds of WT, T3N-OE #6, and T3N-OE #13 on MS medium with or without 10 μM ABA. The results showed that there was no significant difference on the germination rates among WT, T3N-OE #6, and T3N-OE #13 on MS medium. However, when seeds were germinated on MS containing 10 μM ABA, T3N-OE #6 and T3N-OE #13 plants were more tolerant to ABA than WT in terms of germination rate (fig. S9, A and B), suggesting that overexpression of NtT3N suppressed ABA signaling.

To investigate the function of T3N in regulating tobacco growth and antiviral defense, we used T3N-OE #6 and T3N-OE #13 in this study (fig. S8) and TOE3-OE and CR-TOE3 lines (26). At 30 days, the fresh weight and plant height of T3N-OE #6, T3N-OE #13, TOE3-OE #4, and TOE3-OE #5 were significantly higher than that of WT tobaccos, whereas those of CR-TOE3 #29 and CR-TOE3 #68 were significantly reduced (Fig. 3, B and C). Then, all tested plants were rub inoculated with TMV. At 14 dpi, better growth and more green recovery tissues were observed in T3N-OE #6, T3N-OE #13, TOE3-OE #4, and TOE3-OE #5 tobaccos compared with those in WT, whereas CR-TOE3 #29 and CR-TOE3 #68 plants exhibited severer interveinal chlorotic symptoms than WT plants (Fig. 3, A to D). The WB results demonstrated less viral accumulation levels in T3N-OE #6, T3N-OE #13, TOE3-OE #4, and TOE3-OE #5 plants but much higher viral accumulation in CR-TOE3 #29 and CR-TOE3 #68 plants than that in WT (Fig. 3, E and F). To further explore how ABA response was regulated by NtT3N, we performed RT-qPCR assays. The RT-qPCR results indicated that the expression levels of ABA response genes, NtABF4 and NtRAB18, were significantly down-regulated in T3N-OE #6, T3N-OE #13, TOE3-OE #4, and TOE3-OE #5 plants but significantly up-regulated in CR-TOE3 #29 and CR-TOE3 #68 plants compared with those in WT at 14 dpi (Fig. 3, G and H). Together, these results demonstrate that overexpression of TOE3 N terminus inhibits ABA signaling to promote tobacco growth and antiviral immunity.

Overexpression of PYL4 activates ABA response to inhibit tobacco growth but facilitate viral infection

To explore the role of ABA receptor PYL4 in plant response to TMV infection, we further generated NtPYL4-overexpressing N. tabacum lines (PYL4-OE) and two knockout N. tabacum mutants (CR-PYL4) via CRISPR-Cas9 technology (fig. S10, A to C). At 30 days, the fresh weight and plant height of PYL4-OE #3 and PYL4-OE #23 tobaccos were significantly reduced compared with those of WT. In contrast, CR-PYL4 #36 and CR-PYL4 #51 plants exhibited significantly higher levels of fresh weight and plant height (Fig. 4, A to C). Then, WT, PYL4-OE, and CR-PYL4 plants were rub inoculated with TMV. At 14 dpi, PYL4-OE #3 and PYL4-OE #23 showed more chlorotic tissue with more viral accumulation compared with WT (Fig. 4, A to F). In contrast, disease symptoms and viral accumulation were reduced in the systematic leaves of CR-PYL4 #36 and CR-PYL4 #51 (Fig. 4, A to F). RT-qPCR assays were performed to determine the expression of ABA response genes. The results demonstrated that the expression levels of the ABA response genes were substantially higher in PYL4-OE plants but were lower in CR-PYL4 plants (Fig. 4, G and H).

In addition, we silenced NbPYL4 in 25-day-old N. benthamiana plants using virus-induced gene silencing (VIGS) method and detected the expression level of ABA response genes 7 days post–TRV inoculation. RT-qPCR results showed that silencing of NbPYL4 significantly decreased the expression level of ABA response genes, NbABF2 and NbRAB18, compared with that of TRV-GUS control group (fig. S11A). Then, we observed the growth phenotype at 14 days post–TRV inoculation. The fresh weight and plant height of N. benthamiana plants silencing NbPYL4 was significantly increased compared with those of TRV-GUS control plants (fig. S11, B to D). To examine the function of NbPYL4 in response to TMV infection, we inoculated TMV–green fluorescent protein (GFP) in the leaves of TRV-GUS control plants and NbPYL4-silencing plants at 7 days post–TRV inoculation. At 7 dpi, the GFP fluorescence signal was obviously reduced in the leaves of NbPYL4-silencing plants compared with those of TRV-GUS control plants (fig. S11E). The WB results indicated that silencing of NbPYL4 resulted in a greatly reduction in viral accumulation (fig. S11, F and G). In contrast, overexpression of NbPYL4 significantly increased the expression level of ABA response genes and promotes viral infection (fig. S11, H to K). These results demonstrate that overexpression of NbPYL4 inhibits growth and facilitates TMV infection in N. benthamiana.

To investigate the genetic relationship between NtTOE3 and NtPYL4 in promoting tobacco growth and antiviral immunity, we crossed CR-PYL4 #51 with CR-TOE3 #29 N. tabacum mutants. At 30 days, the fresh weight and plant height of the resultant CR-PYL4 CR-TOE3 were significantly increased compared with those of WT, CR-TOE3 #29, and CR-TOE3 #68 plants but were slightly decreased compared with those of CR-PYL4 #36 and CR-PYL4 #51 (fig. S12, A to C). Followed by rub inoculating with TMV, at 14 dpi, better growth, less interveinal chlorotic tissue, and milder disease symptoms were observed in CR-PYL4 CR-TOE3, compared with those in WT, CR-TOE3 #29, and CR-TOE3 #68 plants (fig. S12, A to D). WB analysis demonstrated that the level of viral accumulation in CR-PYL4 CR-TOE3 plants was significantly lower than that in WT and CR-TOE3 plants (fig. S12, E and F). However, the viral accumulation in CR-PYL4 CR-TOE3 plants was slightly higher than that in CR-PYL4 tobaccos (fig. S12, E and F), indicating that knockout of NtPYL4 could not completely rescue the disease phenotype caused by knockout of NtTOE3. Furthermore, the expression level of ABA response genes in CR-PYL4 #36, CR-PYL4 #51, and CR-PYL4 CR-TOE3 plants was significantly lower than that of WT, whereas CR-TOE3 #29 and CR-TOE3 #68 plants had significantly higher expression levels of ABA response genes (fig. S12, G and H). These data suggest that NtTOE3 functions upstream of NtPYL4 in facilitating growth and activating antiviral immunity in N. tabacum. Together, these data suggest that NtPYL4/NbPYL4 act as negative factors in regulating plant growth and antiviral immunity.

PYL4-PP2C4 module regulates both growth and antiviral defense in tobacco

PP2C4, a factor from PP2C group A, acts as an ABA central component that is regulated by PYLs proteins and negatively regulates ABA signaling pathway (28). We examined NtPYL4-NtPP2C4 interaction in vivo using SLC assays in N. benthamiana leaves. Coexpression of NtPP2C4-nLUC and cLUC-NtPYL4 resulted in strong luminescent signals, suggesting that NtPP2C4 interacted with NtPYL4 in vivo (fig. S13).

To investigate the role of NtPP2C4 in regulating tobacco growth and antiviral resistance, we generated two NtPP2C4 knockout N. tabacum mutants (CR-PP2C4) using CRISPR-Cas9 technology (fig. S14, A and B). At 30 days, CR-PP2C4 #11 and CR-PP2C4 #29 exhibited significantly lower levels of fresh weight and plant height than WT (Fig. 5, B and C). Then, WT, CR-PP2C4 #11, and CR-PP2C4 #29 plants were rub inoculated with TMV. At 14 dpi, CR-PP2C4 #11 and CR-PP2C4 #29 tobaccos exhibited more interveinal chlorotic tissue and severer disease symptoms compared with WT (Fig. 5, A to D). Consistently, WB results showed that CR-PP2C4#11 and CR-PP2C4#29 accumulated more TMV-CP compared with WT (Fig. 5, E and F). To examine the level of ABA response in NtPP2C4-knockout mutants, RT-qPCR assays were carried out and the results demonstrated that the expression levels of ABA response genes, NtABF4 and NtRAB18, were significantly down-regulated in CR-PP2C4 plants (fig. S15, A and B). These results suggest that NtPP2C4 plays a positive role in regulating tobacco growth and antiviral immunity.

To investigate whether NtPP2C4 acts downstream of NtPYL4 in promoting tobacco growth and antiviral immunity, we crossed CR-PYL4 #51 with CR-PP2C4 #29 N. tabacum mutants. At 30 days, the fresh weight and plant height of the resultant CR-PYL4 CR-PP2C4 showed no difference with those of CR-PP2C4 #11 and CR-PP2C4 #29 tobaccos but were significantly reduced compared to those of WT plants (Fig. 5, A to C). Followed by rub inoculating with TMV, at 14 dpi, obviously interveinal chlorotic tissue and severer disease symptoms were observed in CR-PYL4 CR-PP2C4, which was similar to CR-PP2C4 #11 and CR-PP2C4 #29 (Fig. 5, A to D). WB analysis proved that the level of viral accumulation in CR-PYL4 CR-PP2C4 plants was slightly lower than that in CR-PP2C4 #11 and CR-PP2C4 #29 plants (Fig. 5, E and F), strongly indicating that knockout of NtPYL4 could not completely rescue the disease phenotype caused by knockout of NtPP2C4. To further explore the role of NbPP2C4 in regulating the growth and antiviral resistance of N. benthamiana, we first confirmed the interaction between NbPYL4 and NbPP2C4 in planta using SLC assays (fig. S16A). Then, two mutant lines that have been reported to activate ABA signaling pathway, Nbpp2c4-5 and Nbpp2c4-10 (28), were used in this study. At 28 days, the fresh weight and plant height of Nbpp2c4-5 and Nbpp2c4-10 were significantly reduced compared with those of WT plants (fig. S16, C and D). Then, 28-day-old Nbpp2c4-5 and Nbpp2c4-10 plants were rub inoculated with TMV. At 7 dpi, these two mutant plants showed stunted growth and much severer symptom and had significantly higher levels of viral accumulation (fig. S16, B to F). These data indicate that knockout of NbPP2C4 resulted in growth retardation and immunity impairment.

To investigate whether NbPYL4 inhibited NbPP2C4-mediated growth promotion and enhanced antiviral resistance, we overexpressed NbPP2C4 in the leaves of N. benthamiana plants and inoculated TMV-GFP infectious clones. Compared with the EV (used as control), leaves overexpressing NbPP2C4 exhibited much lower GFP fluorescence signal (fig. S16G). WB results demonstrated that overexpression of NbPP2C4 resulted in lower viral titers than EV control (fig. S16, H and I). However, when coexpressing NbPYL4 and NbPP2C4 in the leaves of N. benthamiana plants, brighter GFP signal was observed compared with that of the NbPP2C4 + EV group (fig. S16G). Consistently, WB results showed that leaves overexpressing NbPP2C4 and NbPYL4 showed more TMV accumulation compared with the NbPP2C4 + EV group (fig. S16, H and I). These results demonstrate that NbPYL4 suppresses NbPP2C4-mediated positive function in regulating the growth and antiviral resistance in N. benthamiana. Together, these results suggest that PP2C4 positively regulates tobacco growth and antiviral defense, which is inhibited by PYL4.

T3N interferes with the interaction between PYL4 and PP2C4 to uncouple GDT in tobacco

Given that the N terminus of TOE3 blocks ABA signaling and both of T3N and PP2C4 could interact with PYL4, we hypothesis that T3N might interfere with the interaction between PYL4 and PP2C4, releasing PP2C4 to negatively regulating ABA response. To investigate the hypothesis, SLC assays were performed, and the results showed that the fluorescence was obviously reduced in the presence of NtT3N-MYC compared with EV control, which indicated that the binding affinity of NtPYL4 and NtPP2C4 was suppressed by T3N (Fig. 5G). Similarly, SLC assays showed that the fluorescence activated by the interaction between NbPYL4 and NbPP2C4 was strongly reduced in the presence of NbT3N compared with EV control (fig. S17). We further carried out an in vitro competitive binding assay. The results indicated that the protein level of His-NtPP2C4 pulled down by MBP-NtPYL4 gradually decreased as the amount of GST-NtT3N added increased, while GST control did not affect the binding affinity between MBP-NtPYL4 and His-NtPP2C4 (fig. S18). These results suggest that T3N suppresses the ABA central module PYL4-PP2C4 interaction.

To further quantitatively analyze the interfering effect of NtT3N on the NtPYL4-NtPP2C4 interaction in the absence or presence of ABA, we performed microscale thermophoresis (MST) assays (Fig. 5H). The results showed that NtT3N bound to NtPYL4 with high affinity in the absence of ABA [dissociation constant (Kd) = 3.5 ± 0.397 nM] or in the presence of ABA (Kd = 3.6 ± 0.862 nM). As expected, in the absence of ABA, NtPYL4 bound to NtPP2C4 with relatively low affinity (Kd = 1061.7 ± 53.593 nM). ABA significantly enhanced the NtPP2C4-NtPYL4 interaction, as reflected by a markedly decreased Kd value (Kd = 130.48 ± 2.940 nM). In the absence of ABA, the addition of 50 nM NtT3N competed with NtPP2C4 for binding to NtPYL4, resulting in no detectable binding signal. However, in the presence of ABA, 50 nM NtT3N significantly increased the Kd to 505.82 ± 22.797 nM compared with the NtPYL4-NtPP2C4-ABA group (Kd = 130.48 ± 2.940 nM). Moreover, 200 nM NtT3N blocked the interaction between NtPYL4 and NtPP2C4, with no detectable binding signal in the presence and absence of ABA (Fig. 5H). These data strongly indicate that NtT3N competes with NtPP2C4 for binding to NtPYL4 and exhibits a stronger binding affinity to NtPYL4 than NtPP2C4.

To investigate whether NtT3N functions in promoting growth and antiviral immunity by disrupting the function of NtPYL4-NtPP2C4, we overexpressed T3N in the background of CR-PP2C4 #29 N. tabacum tobaccos and generated CR-PP2C4 T3N-OE #6 and CR-PP2C4 T3N-OE #17 lines. At 30 days, the fresh weight and plant height of CR-PP2C4 T3N-OE #6 and CR-PP2C4 T3N-OE #17 exhibited no significant difference compared with those of CR-PP2C4 #11 and CR-PP2C4 #29 tobaccos but were significantly reduced compared to those of WT plants (Fig. 5, A to C). Followed by rub inoculating with TMV, at 14 dpi, obviously interveinal chlorotic tissue and severer disease symptoms were observed in CR-PP2C4 T3N-OE plants, which were slightly milder than those in CR-PP2C4 #11 and CR-PP2C4 #29 but were still much severer than those in WT plants (Fig. 5, A to D). Although WB analysis demonstrated that the level of viral accumulation in CR-PP2C4 T3N-OE plants was slightly lower than that in CR-PP2C4 #11 and CR-PP2C4 #29 plants, TMV titers in CR-PP2C4 T3N-OE plants accumulated much higher than those in WT (Fig. 5, E and F). These data indicated that overexpression of NtT3N could also not completely rescue the disease phenotype caused by knockout of NtPP2C4. In addition, the transcriptional expression levels of ABA response genes were significantly activated in CR-PP2C4 #11, CR-PP2C4 #29, CR-PP2C4 T3N-OE #6, and CR-PP2C4 T3N-OE #17 tobaccos (fig. S15, A and B). Together, these results demonstrate that NtT3N promotes growth and activates antiviral defense through interfering with the interaction of NtPYL4-NtPP2C4 in N. tabacum.

Phosphorylation of T3N amplifies its function in disrupting ABA central module

Our previous study reported that TMV infection induces the expression of casein kinase II α subunit-like protein (CK2αL), thereby activating the phosphorylation of NtTOE3 at Ser58 and Thr128 within the N terminus. This phosphorylation event affects the binding affinity of NtTOE3 to another protein, but not the subcellular localization and the transcriptional regulation ability of NtTOE3 (27). To test whether phosphorylation of NtT3N could alter its binding affinity with NtPYL4 and subsequently affects the interference to NtPYL4-NtPP2C4, we performed a SLC assay and an in vitro His–pull-down assay. The results of SLC showed that brighter fluorescence was observed in the part of leaves overexpressing phosphorylated NtT3N (NtT3NDD)–nLUC/cLUC–NtPYL4 compared with the control NtTOE3-nLUC/cLUC-NtPYL4, whereas coexpression of nonphosphorylated NtT3N (NtT3NAA)–nLUC/cLUC–NtPYL4 exhibited attenuated fluorescence (Fig. 6A). The in vitro His–pull-down assay indicated that the purified MBP-PYL4-His protein had a significantly stronger binding affinity with GST-NtT3NDD than GST-NtT3N and GST-NtT3NAA (Fig. 6B), which demonstrated that NtPYL4 prefers to bind phosphorylated NtT3N rather than nonphosphorylated NtT3N. Because T3N interferes with the interaction between NtPYL4 and NtPP2C4 and phosphorylated T3N strongly enhances its interaction to NtPYL4, we hypothesis that phosphorylation of T3N might intensify T3N-mediated disruptive effect to ABA central module NtPYL4-NtPP2C4. To test this hypothesis, SLC assays were conducted and the results indicated that phosphorylated NtT3N strongly disrupted the interaction between NtPYL4 and NtPP2C4, compared with the native NtT3N. In contrast, in the presence of nonphosphorylated NtT3N, this interaction was decreased compared with that in EV control but was increased compared with that in the native NtT3N (Fig. 6C). To sum up, these data suggest that phosphorylated NtT3N strongly enhances its disruptive effect on the ABA central module NtPYL4-NtPP2C4.

Phosphorylation of T3N blocks ABA signaling and promotes both growth and antiviral immunity in tobacco

To test the antiviral function of the native NtT3N, NtT3NDD, and NtT3NAA, we used T3N-OE #6 and T3N-OE #13 and generated NtT3NDD-overexpressing lines (T3NDD-OE #3 and T3NDD-OE #7) and NtT3NAA-overexpressing tobaccos (T3NAA-OE #13 and T3NAA-OE #19) (fig. S19). At 30 days, the fresh weight and plant height of T3NDD-OE #3 and T3NDD-OE #7 were significantly higher than those of T3N-OE #6 and T3N-OE #13, whereas those of T3NAA-OE #13 and T3NAA-OE #19 were significantly lower than those of T3N-OE #6 and T3N-OE #13 but were still significantly higher than those of WT (Fig. 6, D to F). Then, all tested plants were rub inoculated with TMV. At 14 dpi, T3NDD-OE #3 and T3NDD-OE #7 exhibited a large proportion of green recovery tissue and milder disease symptoms compared with T3N-OE #6 and T3N-OE #13 plants. In addition, T3NAA-OE #13 and T3NAA-OE #19 showed less recovery tissue in the systemic leaves compared with T3N-OE #6 and T3N-OE #13 and demonstrated milder interveinal chlorotic symptoms than WT (Fig. 6, D to G). Consistently, compared with the higher level of viral accumulation in T3N-OE plants, less TMV titers were detected in T3NDD-OE #3 and T3NDD-OE #7 plants (Fig. 6, H and I). RT-qPCR assays indicated that overexpression of phosphorylated NtT3NDD significantly inhibited the expression level of ABA response genes compared with overexpression of NtT3N. Moreover, the expression level of ABA response genes was significantly higher in plants overexpressing NtT3NAA than plants overexpressing NtT3N but was significantly lower than WT (fig. S20, A and B).

Furthermore, we also verified the enhanced inhibition effect of NbT3NDD on the interaction between NbPYL4 and NbPP2C4 using SLC assays (fig. S21, A and B). Overexpression of NbT3NDD significantly down-regulated the transcriptional expression level of ABA response genes compared with overexpression of NbT3N (fig. S21C). As shown in fig. S21D, after inoculation with TMV-GFP infectious clone, while brighter fluorescence signal from TMV-GFP and higher viral accumulation level were observed in the NbT3NAA-overexpressing leaves than NbT3N-overexpressing leaves, overexpression of NbT3NDD decreased the GFP fluorescence signal and viral accumulation compared with overexpression of NbT3N (fig. S21D). Together, these results demonstrate that phosphorylation of T3N strongly enhances its role in promoting tobacco growth and antiviral defense through amplifying its disruptive effect on ABA central module PYL4-PP2C4 in both N. tabacum and N. benthamiana.

DISCUSSION

ABA signaling pathway has been studied for nearly a century. Many studies focused on its role in inhibiting seed germination (29), promoting seed dormancy and leaf senescence (30, 31), and enhancing plant tolerance to abiotic stress (32, 33). Recent studies reported that ABA signaling had both positive and negative roles in regulating plants resistance to biotic stress, which is dependent on the specific plant-microbe interaction (34). It has also surfaced as a crucial factor with dual functions on the interplay between plants and viruses (35, 36). Given the negative effect of ABA signaling on plant growth and its dual roles in regulating plant antiviral defense, it likely serves as a key signaling pathway to decouple GDT in specific plant-virus interaction. In this study, we found that ABA treatment negatively regulates tobacco growth and resistance of tobacco to TMV infection (Fig. 3, A to E). The results of genetic analysis proved that PP2C4 plays a positive role in regulating tobacco growth and antiviral immunity, which is suppressed by PYL4 (Figs. 4 and 5, A to G). This evidence implied that suppression of ABA synthesis pathway or blocking ABA response might be potential strategies to uncouple growth-antiviral defense trade-off in tobacco.

Recent advances have identified many important transcription factors from diverse families, including WRKY (37, 38), MYC (39), AP2/ERF (40, 41), and bZIP (42), as pivotal regulators of plant growth or immunity. Among them, the members of AP2 transcription factor subfamily occupy a central position in regulating plant growth and defense response. Our previous and present studies revealed that an AP2 transcription factor TOE3 plays a positive role in promoting growth and enhancing immunity in tobacco (26, 27). Consistently, in Arabidopsis, TOE1, a member of AP2 transcription factor family, positively regulates not only plant vegetative phase change (43) but also plant innate immunity through binding to the FLAGELLIN-SENSING2 (FLS2) promoter and inhibits its activity (40). Despite these advances, the action mode and molecular mechanism of AP2 transcription factors in uncoupling GDT remain largely unclear. In the present study, transcriptome analysis and seed germination assays showed that TOE3, an AP2 subfamily transcription factor, acts as a “brake” of ABA response and positively regulates tobacco growth and the resistance to TMV infection (Figs. 1 and 3). Y2H screening, SLC, in vitro pull-down assays, and genetic analysis demonstrated that TOE3 disrupted the function of ABA central module PYL4-PP2C4 via binding ABA receptor PYL4, simultaneously promoting tobacco growth and antiviral defense (Figs. 3 and 5). These findings provide an insight into the suppression of ABA signaling pathway with the core protagonist TOE3 to uncouple plant GDT. Considering the dual role of ABA in plant antiviral defense, TOE3-mediated regulation of the PYL4-PP2C4 module likely serves as a flexible switch that uncouples GDT through modulating ABA responses.

Phosphorylation, one important type of the posttranslational modifications, dynamically fine-tunes the protein function to regulate plant defense response. The phosphorylation of IDEAL PLANT ARCHITECTURE 1 (IPA1) at Ser163 alters the DNA binding specificity in response to Magnaporthe oryzae infection, thus promoting both yield and resistance via sustain a balance between growth and immunity (10). However, how phosphorylation uncouples GDT remains largely unknown. Here, we found that phosphorylation of TOE3 N terminus plays as a signal amplifier to strengthen the inhibitory effect of TOE3 on ABA signaling via completely disrupting the function of ABA central module PYL4-PP2C4, thus terminating ABA-mediated inhibition to tobacco growth and antiviral defense to uncouple GDT. Moreover, it has been proved that TMV infection stimulated ABA accumulation (44). Theoretically, ABA response should be substantially increased by ABA. However, the expression level of ABA response genes was slightly up-regulated upon TMV infection in WT N. tabacum plants, while the value of up-regulation rate was only about 3 to 5 (Fig. 3, G and H). The phosphorylation level of TOE3 was significantly increased upon TMV infection (27). Thus, the up-regulation of ABA signaling caused by TMV-induced ABA accumulation might be inhibited because of the inhibitory effect of phosphorylated TOE3 on ABA signaling. In CRISPR-Cas9 technology–mediated TOE3-knockout N. tabacum plants (CR-TOE3 #29 and CR-TOE3 #68), the mutated TOE3 protein was terminated at 123 or 85 amino acids, which resulted in the incorrect expression of TOE3 N terminus (27). This incorrect expression led to the loss of the disrupting effect on the core components of ABA, thus causing a significant up-regulation of the ABA response in CR-TOE3 #29 and CR-TOE3 #68 upon TMV infection (Fig. 3, J and K). This further proved that the N terminus of TOE3 and its phosphorylation are crucial for the inhibition of ABA signaling. To apply this in agricultural production, a feasible strategy is to convert the phosphorylation sites (Ser58 and Thr128) into phosphomimetic residues, such as aspartate or glutamate, using a CRISPR-Cas–mediated base editor (45), thereby mimicking constitutive phosphorylation of NtTOE3. This approach could potentially enhance both growth and defense traits in crops. Unlike IPA1, which alters DNA binding specificity through pathogen-induced phosphorylation to balance yield and immunity (10), and STK, which orchestrates carbon flux and sakuranetin biosynthesis to uncouple GDT in rice (11), NtTOE3 uncouples GDT by directly disrupting the ABA core module PYL4-PP2C4 via its N-terminal domain. The interaction between TOE3 and PYL4 is conserved in both N. tabacum and N. benthamiana. The phosphorylation sites within the N terminus of TOE3 are highly conserved among species of the family Solanaceae, such as potato, tomato, pepper, and other crops (27), suggesting that the TOE3-mediated uncoupling of GDT may be conserved across other Solanaceae crop species and have potential for future applications.

Overexpression of NtT3N could partially rescue the growth inhibition and susceptible phenotype caused by CR-PP2C4, yet the relative expression levels of ABA-responsive genes show no significant difference in CR-PP2C4 or CR-PP2C4 T3N-OE lines. Similarly, knockout of NtPYL4 could also partially rescue the growth stunt and susceptible phenotype of CR-TOE3, while the relative expression levels of ABA-response genes exhibit no significant difference in CR-PYL4 and CR-PYL4 CR-TOE3 lines. These data suggest that T3N might also regulate other pathway to promote growth and antiviral resistance, which requires further investigation.

In summary, we revealed a regulatory mechanism for TOE3 and its phosphorylation interfering with ABA signaling to uncouple growth and antiviral defense in both N. tabacum and N. benthamiana (Fig. 7). In normal condition, tobacco produces little endogenous ABA to maintain homeostasis. Meanwhile, TOE3 inhibits the ABA response via disrupting ABA central module PYL4-PP2C4 to promote tobacco growth. However, when TMV infected, TOE3 protein is highly phosphorylated by increasing casein kinase II α subunit-like protein (CK2αL) (27). Phosphorylated TOE3 strengthens its disruption effect on ABA signaling via enhancing its binding affinity to PYL4, thus activating tobacco antiviral defense. Our findings provide an insight into TOE3-mediated suppression to ABA signaling with uncoupling GDT and offer promising targets for crop breeding with both adjusted growth as well as highly antiviral resistance.

A proposal model indicating the mechanism of TOE3 uncoupling GDT via interfering with ABA response.

In the normal condition, TOE3 inhibits ABA signaling via suppressing the interaction between PYL4 and PP2C4, thus promoting tobacco growth. TMV infection induces CK2αL-mediated phosphorylation of TOE3 (27). Phosphorylated TOE3 enhances the binding affinity to PYL4 and further interferes with ABA core module PYL4-PP2C4 to block ABA response, thus promoting tobacco antiviral defense.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials and growth conditions

N. tabacum ‘NC89’ WT, mutants, and transgenic lines were used in the present study. TOE3-OE #4, TOE3-OE #5, CR-TOE3 #29, and CR-TOE3 #68 N. tabacum were characterized and described in our previous study (26). N. benthamiana plants were also used in this study. Nbpp2c4-5 and Nbpp2c4-10 mutants were described in the previous study and were provided by X. Tao (Nanjing Agricultural University, China) (28). In the seed germination assays, N. tabacum seeds were first surface disinfected with 75% (v/v) EtOH and were then cultured on MS medium (Phyto Technology Laboratories, USA) supplemented with sucrose (30 g/liter) and agar (7 g/liter; pH 5.8) with or without ABA. The medium was autoclaved at 121°C for 15 min before usage. After 10 days of germination, the condition of germination was observed and monitored. All plants were grown in a green-house set at 24 ± 1°C under a 16-hour/8-hour light/dark photoperiod.

Viral inoculation

TMV and TMV-GFP infectious clone were used in this study, and these viral isolates were maintained in 0.02 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; pH 6.8) at −80°C. For the ABA treatment assays and the assays of viral inoculation with transgenic plants, the 30-day-old N. tabacum and N. benthamiana plants were rub inoculated with TMV. Next, the phenotype after TMV infection was observed and monitored. For the transient overexpression assays, Agrobacterium tumefaciens harboring TMV-GFP infectious clones was coinfiltrated with corresponding vectors into the leaves of N. benthamiana plants. Then, the GFP fluorescence from TMV-GFP was observed using a fluorescence microscope at 2 dpi. Leaf samples were collected independently and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for DNA, RNA, or protein extraction.

Vector construction

The full length of TOE3 and PYL4 was amplified using PCR and cloned into PGADT7 or PGBKT7 plasmid to generate AD-TOE3 and BD-PYL4. The full length of TOE3, PYL4, and PP2C4 and the N terminus, M domain, and C terminus of TOE3 from N. tabacum and N. benthamiana were fused with FLAG, or MYC tags and were then cloned into the pCAMBIA1307 plasmid to overexpress corresponding proteins using 35S promoter. The full length of TOE3 and PYL4 and the N terminus, M domain, and C terminus of TOE3 from N. tabacum and N. benthamiana were then cloned into the pXY103/104 plasmids to generated pXY103-TOE3-nYFP, pXY103-T3N-nYFP, pXY103-T3M-nYFP, pXY103-T3C-nYFP, and pXY104-PYL4-cYFP plasmids. The Ser58 and Thr128 of TOE3 N terminus were replaced by aspartic acid or alanine to generate T3NDD or T3NAA. Then, the full length of TOE3, PYL4, and PP2C4 and the N terminus, M domain, and C terminus of TOE3, and T3NDD, and T3NAA from N. tabacum and N. benthamiana were also cloned into pCAMBIA1300-nLUC/cLUC plasmids to generate pCAMBIA1300-TOE3/T3N/T3M/T3C/PP2C4-nLUC and pCAMBIA1300-PYL4-cLUC vectors. The T3NDD and T3NAA were also cloned into pCAMBIA1307 plasmids, respectively. For in vitro pull-down assays, TOE3/T3N/T3M/T3C/T3NDD/T3NAA were cloned and inserted into ‌pGEX-4 T-1 (GST tag) to generate GST-TOE3/T3N/T3M/T3C/T3NDD/T3NAA plasmids. PP2C4 was amplified and fused with FLAG tag and was then cloned into pET32a plasmid to produce FLAG-PP2C4-His protein. PYL4 was cloned into pET28a plasmid (MBP tag) to obtain MBP-PYL4 and was cloned into pET28a plasmid (MBP tag and His tag) to generate MBP-PYL4-His. For VIGS assays, the partial fragment of NbPYL4 was cloned into pTRV2 plasmid. All relative primers used in this study were listed in table S2.

Genetic transformation assays

To generate overexpression N. tabacum lines, these plasmids—pCAMBIA1307-PYL4-FLAG, pCAMBIA1307-T3NDD-FLAG, and pCAMBIA1307-T3NAA-FLAG T3NAA—were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. To generate CRISPR-Cas9 technology–mediated mutants, the CRISPR targets of NtPYL4 and NtPP2C4 were designed in the website (www.crisprscan.org/?page=sequence), and the targets were cloned into pHSE401 binary vector as described previously (46). Then, pHSE401-NtPYL4 and pHSE401-NtPP2C4 were also introduced into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. Then, A. tumefaciens suspension was transformed into N. tabacum plants to generate transgenic tobaccos using the Agrobacterium-mediated leaf disc transformation method as described previously (47). Hygromycin B was used to initially screen the positive transformed tobacco plants. The overexpression of N. tabacum lines was further analyzed by WB. The knockout mutants were further identified using PCR and sequencing. The primers used for the construction of CRISPR vectors and PCR assays were listed in table S2.

Y2H assays

The empty pGADT7 and BD-PYL4, AD-TOE3, and pGBKT7 were used as negative controls. AD-TOE3 was used as prey, and BD-PYL4 was used as bait. Pairs of corresponding constructs were cotransformed into the yeast strain AH109. Then, the strains were cultured on double DO supplement medium (-Leu/-Trp) (Clontech, USA) for 3 days and were transferred onto Quadruple DO supplement (-Leu/Trp/Ade/His) (Clontech, USA) to test possible interactions. In addition, 10 mM 3-amino-1,2,4-triazole was used to inhibit self-activation activity.

BiFC assays

For BiFC assays, the recombinant plasmids—pXY103-TOE3-nYFP, pXY103-T3N-nYFP, pXY103-T3M-nYFP, pXY103-T3C-nYFP, and pXY104-PYL4-cYFP—were introduced into A. tumefaciens strain GV3101. Then, pairs of pXY103-TOE3-nYFP, pXY103-T3N-nYFP, pXY103-T3M-nYFP, pXY103-T3C-nYFP, and pXY104-PYL4-cYFP were coinfiltrated into the leaves of N. benthamiana plants. A. tumefaciens control containing 35S: mCherry was coinfiltrated with the pairs of BiFC constructs and served as a marker for successful transfection. After 3 days, YFP signals were detected with 514-nm excitation laser wavelength using a fluorescence microscope (Leica, Switzerland).

SLC assays

For SLC assays, the recombinant plasmids, pCAMBIA1300-TOE3/T3N/T3M/T3C/PP2C4-nLUC and pCAMBIA1300-PYL4-cLUC, were introduced into A. tumefaciens strains GV3101. Then, Agrobacterium mixtures carrying the nLUC and cLUC constructs were coexpressed in the leaves of N. benthamiana to be examined for protein interaction. The LUC signals were detected using IVIS Lumina III (PerkinElmer, USA) with an exposure time of 2 to 10 min.

In vitro pull-down assays

The recombinant constructs—GST-NtTOE3/T3N/T3M/T3C/T3NDD/T3NAA, FLAG-NtPP2C4-His, MBP-NtPYL4, and MBP-NtPYL4-His—were introduced into Escherichia coli strain BL21 (DE3) and were induced by isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactopyranoside at 16°C. GST-NtTOE3/T3N/T3M/T3C/T3NDD/T3NAA proteins were purified using GST Mag-Beads (BBI Life Sciences Corporation, China), FLAG-NtPP2C4-His was purified using Ni-NTA Agarose (Invitrogen, USA), and MBP-PYL4 and MBP-NtPYL4-His were purified using Amylose Resin (New England Biolabs, USA). For GST pull-down assays, purified MBP-NtPYL4 proteins were incubated with equal amounts of GST, GST-NtT3N, GST-NtT3M, and GST-NtT3C proteins and GST in GST–pull-down binding buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT), 0.5% NP-40, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail] at 4°C for 3 hours, respectively. Then, the beads were collected after washing six times using GST–pull-down washing buffer [50 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM DTT, 1 mM PMSF, and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail]. Last, the collected beads were boiled in 50 μl of 2× SDS loading buffer for 10 min at 100°C. The protein extract was detected by WB analysis using anti-GST (Abcam, UK) or anti-MBP antibodies (Abcam, UK). Similarly, for His–pull-down assays, MBP-NtPYL4-His proteins were incubated with equal amounts of GST, GST-NtT3N, GST-NtT3NDD, and GST-NtT3NAA proteins and Ni-NTA Agarose in His–pull-down binding buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM PMSF, 0.2% Triton X-100, and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail] at 4°C for 3 hours with gentle rotation. The beads were washed six times using His–pull-down washing buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 8.0), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM PMSF, 0.2% Triton X-100, and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail] and then collected and boiled in 50 μl of 2× SDS loading buffer for 10 min at 100°C. Last, the protein extract was detected by WB analysis using anti-His (Abcam, UK) or anti-GST antibodies.

In vitro competitive pull-down assays

The in vitro competitive pull-down assay was performed as described previously (27). In brief, 5 μg of FLAG-NtPP2C4 mixed with 1, 5, or 10 μg of GST-NtT3N was incubated with amylose resin beads containing 10 μg of MBP or MBP-NtPYL4 in 500 μl of pull-down buffer [20 mM tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 200 mM NaCl, 0.2% Triton X-100, 1 mM PMSF, and 1% protease inhibitor cocktail] at 4°C for 3 hours. The beads were then washed six times with the pull-down buffer. Proteins were eluted from beads by boiling in 100°C with 30 μl of 2× SDS loading buffer. Last, WB analysis was conducted using anti-MBP, anti-GST, or anti-FLAG (MBL, China) antibodies.

MST assays

Recombinant proteins, including FLAG-NtPP2C4-His, MBP-NtPYL4, and GST-NtT3N, were expressed in E. coli strain BL21 (DE3) and were purified using Ni-NTA Agarose (Invitrogen, USA), Amylose Resin (New England Biolabs, USA), and GST Mag-Beads (BBI, China), respectively, followed by size exclusion chromatography.

For MST assays, 500 nM purified MBP-NtPYL4 variants were labeled with 30 μM RED-NHS dye (NanoTemper, Germany) and incubated with serial dilutions of FLAG-NtPP2C4-His ligands, with or without GST-NtT3N, in the presence or absence of ABA. Thermophoresis was measured using a Monolith NT.115 instrument (NanoTemper, Germany), and dissociation constants (Kd) were calculated by fitting binding curves to a single-site binding model using MO.Affinity Analysis software (v.2.3, NanoTemper, Germany).

RNA extraction RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from leaf samples of N. tabacum and N. benthamiana plants using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using a HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Vazyme, China). Subsequently, RT-qPCR analysis was performed using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master MiX (Yeasen, China). The CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection system (Bio-Rad, USA) was used for RT-qPCR analysis. Relative expression levels of gene were normalized to the reference gene Actin. The primers used for RT-qPCR were listed in table S2.

VIGS

In the VIGS assays, the pTRV vectors (pTRV1, pTRV2-GUS, and pTRV2-NbPYL4) were introduced into A. tumefaciens strains GV3101. Then, Agrobacterium mixtures carrying pTRV1 and pTRV2-GUS or pTRV2-NbPYL4 were coinfiltrated into the leaves of 25-day-old N. benthamiana plants. After 14 days, the upper leaves were collected to analyze the efficiency of gene silencing. The growth condition was observed and the fresh weight was measured. Then, the tested plants were inoculated with TMV-GFP infectious clone.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated at least three times and statistically analyzed using Student’s t tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA; Tukey’s multiple comparisons test) with GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. The intensity of the protein bands in WB was measured and analyzed using ImageJ software. The significance levels (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and *** P < 0.001), and different lowercase letters (P < 0.05) were used to illustrate statistically significant differences.
