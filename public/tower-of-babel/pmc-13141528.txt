Parity, body mass index, smoking and risk of rheumatoid arthritis: data from the Australian Longitudinal Study on Women’s Health
Authors: Louise Koller-Smith, Ahmed Mehdi, Syeda Farah Zahir, Lyn March, Leigh Tooth, Gita D Mishra, Ranjeny Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSmoking, obesity and lower parity appear to be associated with higher risk of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) in European, American and some Asian studies, but no studies have tested these associations in the Australian context.MethodsWe studied an incident RA cohort of the prospectively collected Australian Longitudinal Study on Women’s Health (ALSWH) dataset. Smoking status, body mass index (BMI) and parity were assessed using survey data. Incident RA status was determined using the government medication dispensing database. We used multivariable logistic regression models to assess the impact of smoking, BMI and parity on RA risk, controlling for age cohort, education and alcohol intake. We performed sensitivity analysis by repeating analyses in the 1946-51 cohort alone.ResultsAmong 40,393 women we identified 332 incident RA cases. Age, ever smoking, BMI and parity were significantly associated with RA risk on univariable analyses. Lower parity was associated with higher odds of RA after adjustment for all covariates (OR 1.12, 95% CI 1.00-1.25, p = 0.05), and became stronger (OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.18–1.40, p < 0.01) when age cohort was excluded. Ever smoking (OR 1.29, 95% CI 1.02–1.64, p = 0.03) was associated with a higher risk of RA controlling for all factors except age. Each unit increase in BMI was significantly associated with higher odds of RA (OR 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.05, p < 0.01) when controlled for smoking, alcohol intake and education, but not once controlled for either age or parity.ConclusionUsing a novel case finding method for RA that relies entirely on pre-collected administrative data, we show an increased risk for RA in Australian women with smoking and lower parity when adjusted for BMI, alcohol intake and education.Keywords: Rheumatoid arthritis, Parity, Obesity, Smoking, Risk, Lifestyle

Background

The inflammatory autoimmune disease rheumatoid arthritis (RA) affects about 1.1% of the Australian population [29]. Over half of RA patients are of working age [5] and more than 70% are women [36]. Anti-citrullinated peptide antibodies (ACPA) or rheumatoid factor (RF) are found in 70% of RA patients, while 30% are seronegative. Studies in Scandinavian, North American, Spanish and Malaysian cohorts demonstrate an increased risk of RA with smoking and obesity, which is stronger in ACPA+ RA [6, 9, 10, 16, 31, 38, 43, 51, 52], although the some studies suggest the interactions with BMI may have as yet understood complexities, such as modification with serostatus, sex and reverse causation in the pre-clinical phase of disease [49, 50]. Further evidence from such studies also favours a protective role for higher parity [23, 37, 39, 46]. However, there are no epidemiological studies of the sociodemographic, reproductive, health and lifestyle risks for RA in Australia or the Oceania [17]. There are several reasons why risk factors identified in an Australian cohort may differ from those reported internationally. Australia has a distinctive population structure, characterized by a highly multicultural society, diverse genetic ancestry, and the presence of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, whose genetic backgrounds and environmental exposures differ from those of non-Indigenous populations. The Australian environment also presents unique exposures, including high levels of solar ultraviolet radiation, region-specific infectious disease patterns, and elevated dust and particulate matter associated with arid landscapes and mining activities, as well as a different urban–rural distribution than many other countries. In addition, lifestyle patterns in Australia—such as smoking behavior, alcohol consumption, diet, and physical activity—differ from those of other populations and may modify susceptibility to chronic disease. Collectively, these factors highlight the importance of examining risk factors for rheumatoid arthritis within an Australian context.

The Australian Longitudinal Study on Women’s Health (ALSWH) is a national, longitudinal, population-based study involving over 57 000 women. The ALSWH began in 1996 and has surveyed women in four age cohorts every 3 years. For the original three age cohorts, this has provided over 20 years of longitudinal data across the adult lifespan on social, behavioural and economic factors, health outcomes and the use of health and related services [12, 30]. Two RA cohorts, agnostic to ACPA or RF status, were developed from the ALSWH population as described [29]; Documented RA (based on hospital/emergency department records) and Treated RA (based on dispensed medications consistent with RA). No information on participant serostatus (RF, ACPA) was available.

We investigated whether obesity, smoking and parity were associated with RA risk in the ALSWH. Based on worldwide data, we hypothesised that obesity and smoking would increase RA risk, and that higher parity would be protective.

Methods

Study population

The ALSWH is a prospective cohort study of 57 404 women living in all states and territories in Australia, initiated in 1996. Participants received surveys approximately every three years from 1996, with the surveys ongoing to date [12, 30]. Participants are surveyed on a broad range of sociodemographic, reproductive, health and psychological variables. Some variables are collected routinely at each survey, while others are not consistent across surveys. Three cohorts of the ALSWH were used in this study, and were from birth years 1921-26, 1946-51 and 1973-78. Women were randomly sampled from the Health Insurance Commission (Australia’s national health insurance system at that time) in 1995, with over-sampling of women from rural and remote areas. The ALSWH survey data were linked to data from the Pharmaceutical Benefits Scheme (PBS), Australia’s national drug subsidy program and with International Classification of Disease 10 Australian modification (ICD-10-AM) codes for hospital admission and discharge.

This study used the Treated RA cohort developed from the ALSWH population as described [29]. In summary, the treated RA cohort encompassed women in the 1921-26, 1946-51 and 1973-78 cohorts of the ALSWH, who had dispensed medications for RA, and who did not have dispensed medications or service codes consistent with other autoimmune conditions. Incident RA was defined as onset of RA according to the above definition at any point subsequent to the first 12 months of PBS dispensing records (2001 onward).

In more detail, PBS data of ATC codes were used to identify RA cases based on PBS-defined medication. Included medications were methotrexate, sulfasalazine, leflunomide, penicillamine, etanercept, infliximab, rituximab, adalimumab, gold preparations, certolizumab, golimumab, abatacept, tofacitinib, upadacatinib, bariticitinib, tocilizumab and hydroxychloroquine. The PBS definitions included patients taking the specified medications at any time point during the PBS data collection period.

To increase specificity by reducing the number of patients taking DMARDs for non-RA conditions, individuals with ATC codes for anti-psoriatic medications (D05) and intestinal anti-inflammatory agents (A07E) in either the PBS or self-reported medication data were excluded. Intestinal anti-inflammatory agents include locally acting corticosteroids (e.g. Rectal foams) and aminosalicylic acid and similar agents (e.g. Mesalazine). Anti-psoriatic agents include topical antipsoriatics (tars, antracen derivatives, psoralens and others), and systemic antipsoriatics (psoralens, retinoids and fumaric acid derivatives). Similarly, individuals with ICD-10 coded episodes corresponding to other autoimmune or inflammatory conditions (inflammatory arthridites, inflammatory bowel disease, myositis, vasculitides and psoriasis), for which the DMARDs used in RA can also be used, were excluded for the same reason. Finally, those patients with MBS codes corresponding to services for inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or psoriasis were excluded.

An incident RA cohort was developed by excluding individuals with a first episode of medication dispensing within 12 months of the first date of PBS records.

Data collection

Risk factor data were obtained from Survey 1 only for this study, as this was the only survey to contain questions about all variables of interest. Survey 1 is the first survey administered to all three birth year cohorts in 1996. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated based on self-reported height and weight and analyzed as a continuous variable. Smoking was divided into ever smoking vs. never smoking. Parity was assessed as a continuous variable based on live births or stillbirths > 20 weeks gestation.

Education level, country of birth and alcohol intake were collected as potential confounders. Education level was grouped into high (tertiary education or higher) and low-moderate (all other). Alcohol intake was graded according to standard drinks per week and categorized as no or low intake (< 2 standard drinks per week), moderate (2–14 standards drinks per week) and high (15 or more standard drinks per week). No/low were compared to moderate for the analysis due to low numbers in the high group. Country of birth data were only available according to pre-specified categories as designated by ALSWH; Australia, other English speaking, Asia, Europe and Other, and Asia/Europe/Other were combined to suppress low counts in the RA group as required by ALSWH ethics approvals.

Age was included as a categorical variable (based on birth year cohort).

Statistical analysis

One-way ANOVA for continuous variables and Chi square tests with contingency tables were conducted for categorical variables. Univariable logistic regression was first performed to examine the relationship between RA and each explanatory variable to obtain odds ratios (OR), P values and 95% confidence intervals. Multivariable analysis used multiple logistic regression with manual variable selection based on clinical judgment and existing literature. Analyses were repeated in the 1946-51-cohort as a study subset, as a sensitivity analysis given there were very small numbers in the other cohorts, which limited the utility of performing sub-analyses in these age cohorts. We added one variable at a time to build several multiple models to test the effect of various combination of BMI and other variables on RA, to explore potential confounding factors influencing this relationship in the cohort. Age was included as a covariate in all models to account for the differences in opportunity to develop incident RA between the age cohorts, with the aim to reduce bias arising from unequal observation periods, while modelling the association between predictors and outcome.

All analyses were conducted in RStudio version 4.2.3 (RStudio Team [42]). Rows with missing values were deleted.

Ethics approval

The study was approved by the Ethics Committees of the Universities of Newcastle (H-076-0795) and Queensland (2004000224). All participants provided informed consent. In addition, linked administrative data have been provided by the following third parties: The Australian Government Department of Health, Disability and Ageing, The Department of Veterans’ Affairs, ACT Health, NSW Ministry of Health, The Northern Territory Department of Health, Queensland Health, SA Health, The Department of Health, Tasmania, The Department of Health, Victoria, The Department of Health, Western Australia and The Population Health Research Network. BMI

Increasing BMI was associated with a significantly higher risk of RA in univariable analyses (3% higher odds for each unit increase in BMI). This association became non-significant after adjusting for age, alcohol, education, parity, and smoking, and remained non-significant when once age cohort was excluded from the model. When the effect of each individual co-variate on the association between BMI and RA was assessed (Table 6), controlling for smoking, alcohol or education individually did not affect the association between BMI and RA, but controlling for either age or parity rendered the association non-significant.

Discussion

There are no epidemiological studies of the sociodemographic, reproductive, health and lifestyle risks for RA in Australia. However, from a public health perspective, it is important to understand whether previously identified risk factors are transferable to an Australian context with its specific genetic admixture and environmental and lifestyle risk profiles. Here we used a prescription-treated RA cohort that includes seropositive and seronegative patients, derived from the ALSWH to explore the association between major identified sociodemographic, reproductive and lifestyle risks and RA in Australia. In women born in 1946-51, we found that ever smoking and lower parity were associated with independent risk of RA, while lower parity remained a significant risk in all age cohorts when also controlled for age. In univariable analysis, BMI was also significantly associated with RA, but the effect became non-significant once corrected for age cohort or lower parity.

The significant association between smoking and RA is consistent with expected results, as smoking is a very well-established risk factor for RA, with most studies suggesting a 20–30% increased risk in those who have ever smoked [9, 10, 29]. Multiple studies have found no association with smoking when seronegative RA patients are examined alone [6, 51, 53]. Thus, we are likely to be observing a diluted effect of the impact of smoking in the ALSWH cohort. We found that the effect of smoking became non-significant when adjusted for age cohort but remained significant when the 1946-51 cohort (which contained most of our cases) was analyzed alone. Although there may be a true confounding effect of age cohort, it would appear more likely that this was due to a loss of power or overadjustment, given the low number of RA cases in the 1973-78 and 1921-26 cohorts. Age was a difficult variable to incorporate effectively as we had three separate age cohorts containing women within a narrow age range, but who were very different in age to the women in each of the other age cohorts.

Our observation that each pregnancy was associated with a lower risk of RA is again in line with most published studies, which have shown an inverse relationship between parity and RA risk [23, 37, 39, 46], although some studies found minimal or no association between parity and RA risk [1, 26]. This was the only studied variable to remain significant after adjustment for age, potentially due to the greater power provided by a continuous variable. Adjustment for the effect of breastfeeding would be an interesting avenue for future research, given that there also appears to be a protective effect of breastfeeding on RA risk [8], and that this could potentially contribute to the protective effect of parity. We were unable to adjust for breastfeeding in the current study as breastfeeding status and duration was not asked in the selected surveys.

We hypothesized that increasing BMI would increase RA risk given the mechanistic probability related to adipocyte release of inflammatory cytokines [20], the relative increase in oestrogen in obesity [53], and the results of a meta-analysis and systematic review identifying 8% and 3% increased risk respectively for every five unit increase in BMI [16, 38]. The magnitude of risk in the current study was 3% per unit increase in BMI, which remained when controlled for smoking, alcohol and education but became non-significant when controlled for age or parity. There are possible multidirectional interactions between these factors, as BMI would be expected to increase with parity, and to increase and then ultimately decrease with age, and this may account for some of this finding. Interestingly, the EPIC-NOAR study found that obesity was only associated with risk of inflammatory arthritis if present within 18 months of the arthritis diagnosis [21], while another study found the cumulative effect of smoking and being overweight provided a 60% risk of RA in one study, compared to a 2% risk in normal weight never smokers, demonstrating a possible synergistic effect with smoking and obesity [11] The limitations related to the timing of risk factor collection and outcome in our dataset may have impacted the strength of association, and there may be possible confounding from underweight BMI related to cachexia in disease states.

While many previous studies have suggested that moderate alcohol intake is protective [6, 28, 33, 40], this was not observed in our cohort, although there was a trend toward higher risk of RA in those with never/rare alcohol intake. Some studies, including the Iowa WHS cohort, also failed to show an association between alcohol intake and RA [7, 20]. Of relevance, a meta-analysis of nine observational studies found no risk reduction in seronegative RA [45], and another recent study found the protective effect of moderate alcohol consumption to exist only in ever smokers [3], so it is possible that stratification by serostatus or smoking status may yield differing results. Overall, the results of this and other studies suggest that the interaction between alcohol intake and RA is complex and not fully understood, with possible confounding due to diet [6] or overall health [45]. Furthermore, compared to the USA, where many previous epidemiological studies were conducted, Australia has less obesity and higher rates of physical activity, while parity and smoking rates are similar [2, 4, 13, 14, 22, 27]. European and Scandinavian risk profiles have high levels of geographic variability, but overall have higher rates of smoking, lower rates of obesity, and healthier diets [25, 44, 47].

There are also limitations in our dataset. First, we acknowledge that age is usually better represented as a continuous variable. Age was included as a categorical variable as we had defined age cohorts. Low sample sizes limited our ability to use flexible methods to also allow use of age as a continuous variable. As the impact of age on RA risk is well-established to be non-linear [48], a linear continuous age term may not capture risk and will thus cause residual confounding. With these small sample sizes, we concluded that categorical age would approximate nonlinearity [41].

Second, we were restricted to using Survey 1, conducted in 1996, to determine risk exposures, as this was the only survey with data collected on all exposure variables of interest. In contrast, the RA diagnosis could occur anytime between 2001 and 2020. This meant that the risk factor profile was in fact analyzed many years distant to RA diagnosis for some participants, so the effects of exposure may have changed in women who changed their lifestyle, or had more children, in the intervening years. For example, RA risk after smoking cessation has been shown to return to baseline within 20 years of cessation, and risk decreases linearly until then [32]. We were unable to use survival analysis to overcome this issue as we did not have the date of first exposure. In addition, risk profiles have changed over the intervening decades, with lower rates of smoking and higher rates of overweight and obesity (approximately 50% of Australians classified as overweight or obese in 1995 compared to 67% in 2022, and 24% of adults smoking daily in 1995 compared to 8% in 2022) [19, 24]. Additionally, the average age of first time mothers in Australia increased from 28.6 years in 1990 to 30.5 years in 2020, and total fertility rate declined from 1.9 in 1990 to 1.58 in 2020 (Australian Bureau of Statistics, 2020; [35]. We were unable to consider age of first pregnancy as this question was not asked in the surveys.

The lack of data on ACPA or RF status also impacted our ability to seek associations with seropositive RA, where the evidence of association of some risk factors is much stronger, than for seronegative disease or total RA. Lack of detail in ICD-10-AM coding for admissions also precluded the use of codes for seropositive (M05) RA, as only 19 women of the RA cohort were coded as M05. Ideally, we would have liked to further validate our RA cases by medical record review or other methods (such as connective tissue disease screening questionnaire, or pathology/radiology results) but ethics restrictions did not allow this, and currently no such large, balanced prospective longitudinal cohort exists in Australia, meaning this type of research can only be conducted in Australia using pragmatic means. However, previously conducted validation studies have shown excellent performance of medication-based definitions for RA when compared to a gold standard, improving our confidence in our method. In the Black Women’s Health Study, the positive predictive value (PPV) of self-reported RA increased to 76% in women who reported taking disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drugs (DMARDs) (compared to 29% in women taking no related medications), and when women using only prednisolone or who reported other rheumatic conditions were excluded from the DMARDs group the PPV increased to 88% [18]. Our methods include only DMARDs and exclude other rheumatic conditions in a similar manner to this study. Similarly, a study by Nguyen et al., [34], combining self-report with checking the medication reimbursement database for any DMARD claims (excluding oral steroid) provided a PPV of 90% [34].

Conclusions

Using a novel case finding method for RA that relies entirely on pre-collected administrative data, we show an increased risk for RA in Australian women with smoking and lower parity when adjusted for BMI, alcohol intake and education. The data suggest that increased risk of RA with higher BMI in Australia may be linked to effects of age and parity. This information provides an important lifestyle message for individuals at risk of RA to abstain from smoking and avoid increasing BMI. This is a public health message for most health conditions but has not been so strongly emphasized to the general population in relation to RA. Our research supports that a prospective clinically-characterized RA risk cohort in Australia would consolidate potentially modifiable factors that contribute to RA burden in Australia. Furthermore, our data suggest that despite very low rates of current smoking, Australian trends towards lower parity and an ageing population structure may negatively impact future RA incidence.
