The improvement of umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells on the ovarian microenvironment: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Kailu Zhao, Yanan Wu, Jinwei Yang, Kehu Yang, Xue Fan, Yanhua Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo systematically evaluate the effects of umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UCMSCs) on the ovarian microenvironment in mouse models of premature ovarian failure (POF).MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted in Wanfang, VIP, CNKI, PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library databases for randomized controlled animal trials on the transplantation of mesenchymal stem cells for treating the ovarian microenvironment. The search period was from the establishment of the databases to September 19, 2025. Two researchers independently screened the literature, extracted data, and evaluated the methodological quality using the SYRCLE risk of bias tool for animal experiments. Quantitative data were analyzed using RevMan 5.4.1 software.ResultsA total of 10 randomized controlled animal studies were included. The meta-analysis results showed that the transplantation of UCMSCs could significantly increase the number of primordial follicles [SMD = 3.71, 95% CI [2.36, 5.07], P < 0.001], primary follicles [SMD = 3.54 , 95% CI [2.66, 4.42]], secondary follicles [SMD = 3.00 , 95% CI [1.36, 4.64], P < 0.001], and antral follicles [SMD = 3.35, 95% CI [2.28, 4.43], P < 0.001] in mice with POF , all with statistical significance. Additionally, it could significantly increase the serum estradiol concentration (E2) [SMD = 3.49 , 95% CI [1.77, 5.20], P < 0.001] and anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) levels [SMD = 2.76, 95% CI [1.21, 4.30], P < 0.001], and significantly decrease the serum follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) level [SMD = -2.70, 95% CI [-4.29, -1.10], P < 0.001], all with statistical significance.ConclusionThese preclinical findings indicate that transplantation of UCMSCs may improve ovarian follicle-related outcomes and hormone profiles in mouse models of POF. These results support the preclinical therapeutic potential of UCMSCs for POF. However, given the high heterogeneity and methodological limitations of included preclinical studies, these findings cannot be directly translated to clinical practice. warranting further investigation to translate these results to clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-026-02148-0.Keywords: Mesenchymal stem cells, Ovarian microenvironment, Premature ovarian failure, Randomized controlled trial, Meta-analysis

Premature Ovarian Failure (POF) is a common gynecological endocrine disorder characterized by complex etiology and an increasing annual incidence rate. It is characterized by elevated gonadotropin levels and decreased estrogen levels, accompanied by primary or secondary amenorrhea, which severely affects patients’ physical and mental health [1–3]. The occurrence of POF may be related to factors such as autoimmunity, metabolism, infection, and iatrogenic causes. The ovarian microenvironment provides nutritional support and signal transportation for the growth and development of follicles, ovulation, and the formation of the corpus luteum. The close interaction between follicles and the ovarian microenvironment determines the fate of follicles and, consequently, the lifespan of the ovary [3]. The interaction between oocytes and the microenvironment is mediated through direct contact with surrounding cells, the extracellular matrix, and signaling molecules, including hormones, growth factors, and metabolites [1, 3]. Therefore, the ovarian microenvironment plays a crucial role in ovarian function. Currently, the primary treatment for POF is hormone replacement therapy (HRT). While HRT can alleviate clinical symptoms, it cannot fundamentally repair the damaged ovary and carries potential long-term risks and cannot reverse ovarian damage [1, 2].

Mesenchymal Stem Cells (MSCs), derived from the mesoderm during embryonic development, are present in tissues such as bone marrow, adipose tissue, umbilical cord blood, and placenta. They are a class of stem cells with self-renewal capacity and multi-directional differentiation potential, serving as precursor cells for various bone marrow stromal cells, including fibroblasts, osteoblasts, and adipocytes. Due to their properties of self-renewal, multipotency, and immunomodulation, MSCs are widely used in cell therapy, tissue engineering, and regenerative medicine [4].

MSCs can effectively treat POF, demonstrating the ability to repair ovarian structure and improve ovarian function. The specific mechanisms may involve the homing capacity of MSCs, direct differentiation into granulosa cells or inhibition of their apoptosis, induction of differentiation into primordial germ cells, promotion of ovarian angiogenesis, immunomodulation, reduction of oxidative stress, and paracrine effects, thereby promoting the recovery of ovarian function and improving fertility in women of reproductive age [5, 6]. Several reviews have summarized the broader role of MSC-based therapies in premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) /POF [5–9]. Currently, human-derived MSCs used in clinical applications for treating POF and other diseases primarily include Bone Marrow Mesenchymal Stem Cells (BMMSCs), Umbilical Cord Mesenchymal Stem Cells (UCMSCs), Adipose-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells (ADMSCs), and Menstrual Blood-Derived Mesenchymal Stem Cells (MDMSCs). The earliest human clinical trials [9–11]were conducted using BMMSCs, while other types of MSCs have so far been limited to animal models or in vitro laboratory studies [9]. However, a focused systematic synthesis of preclinical evidence specifically evaluating UCMSCs and ovarian microenvironment-related outcomes remains limited. UCMSCs, isolated from umbilical cord tissue, possess self-renewal and multi-tissue differentiation potential. They can be isolated from different parts or the entire umbilical cord, offering advantages such as wide availability, low immunogenicity, non-tumorigenicity, and absence of ethical controversies [12, 13]. These characteristics make UCMSCs suitable for allogeneic transplantation therapy, establishing them as a reliable source for therapeutic MSCs and a focus of recent research with broad application prospects [7, 13]. Therefore, this study aims to systematically evaluate the impact of UCMSCs on the ovarian microenvironment in animal models, providing a more targeted evidence summary to support future translational research.

Materials and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

①Study subjects: Female mouse or rat models with chemotherapy-induced premature ovarian insufficiency; ②Interventions: The treatment group received human UCMSC therapy via tail vein injection or local ovarian injection. The control group received a blank control, normal saline, or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS); ③Outcome Measures: Serum levels of estradiol (E2), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH), and counts of primordial follicles, primary follicles, secondary follicles, and antral follicles; ④ Study Design: Randomized controlled animal trials.

Exclusion criteria

①Duplicate publications; ②Studies lacking original data or with incomplete data that could not be analyzed; ③Studies where UCMSCs were co-administered with other therapeutic treatments; ④Conference abstracts; ⑤Non-Chinese or non-English publications.

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in four English databases (PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library) and three Chinese databases (VIP, Wanfang, and CNKI). The search period spanned from the inception of each database to September 19, 2025. The search strategy combined subject headings with free-text terms and was adjusted according to the requirements of each database. References of the included studies were also reviewed to identify additional relevant literature. Chinese search terms included those related to mesenchymal stem cells, mesenchymal, stem cells, premature ovarian failure, ovarian microenvironment, etc. English search terms included: Ovarian Follicles, Graafian Follicles, Atretic Follicles, Mesenchymal Stem Cell, Wharton Jelly Cells, Multipotent Bone Marrow Stromal Cells, etc. All the search terms used in the systematic literature search in Appendix file 1, the full search strategies for each database in Appendix file 2, and the flowchart of study selection in Appendix file 3.

Literature screening and data extraction

There were no restrictions on the language of publication. Two investigators independently performed the database searches and screened the literature. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer. Initially, duplicate records were removed. Subsequently, titles were screened to exclude obviously irrelevant studies. The remaining records were further screened based on their titles, abstracts, and keywords. Finally, the full texts of the remaining articles were thoroughly reviewed, and studies meeting the PICOS (Participants, Interventions, Comparisons, Outcomes, Study design) criteria were included. Data extracted from the included studies encompassed: ① Basic study information: Title, first author, journal of publication, publication year, country, etc.; ② Characteristics of the animal subjects: Species/strain, age, sample size; ③ Intervention details: Method of POF model induction, inducing agent and dosage, group allocation, transplantation protocol (route, cell dose), follow-up duration, outcome measures, etc.; ④ Key elements for risk of bias assessment; ⑤ Relevant outcome indicators and corresponding result data.

Risk of bias assessment of included studies

The risk of bias in the included studies was independently assessed by two investigators, with subsequent cross-verification of the results. The SYRCLE’s (Systematic Review Center for Laboratory Animal Experimentation) risk of bias tool for animal experiments [14] was used for animal experiments. The assessed domains included: ① Was the sequence generation adequate? ② Were the baseline characteristics similar across groups? ③ Was the allocation concealment adequate? ④ Was the allocation of experimental animals randomized? ⑤ Were the animal caregivers and investigators blinded? ⑥ Were animals selected at random for outcome assessment? ⑦ Was the outcome assessor blinded? ⑧ Were incomplete outcome data adequately addressed? ⑨ Are reports of the outcomes free from selective reporting? ⑩ Was the study apparently free of other sources of bias? The assessment results for each domain were categorized as “Yes” (indicating low risk of bias), “No” (indicating high risk of bias), or “Unclear” (indicating uncertain risk of bias). After evaluation, the overall methodological quality of included studies was moderate.

Statistical analysis

Meta-analysis was performed using RevMan software (version 5.4.1). For continuous outcomes, the standardized mean difference (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) was used as the effect measure. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the chi-square test (α = 0.10) and the I2 statistic. A fixed-effect model was planned when P ≥ 0.10 and I2 ≤ 50%; otherwise, a random-effects model was used. Because heterogeneity was substantial for all pooled outcomes in the present review, the random-effects model was ultimately applied throughout. When a study contributed more than one intervention arm, each intervention-control pair was entered as a separate comparison; because shared control groups were not split or otherwise adjusted, these pooled estimates should be interpreted cautiously. a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Where substantial heterogeneity was identified, subgroup or sensitivity analyses were considered to explore potential sources [15, 16].

Results

Literature screening process and results

The initial search identified 216 publications. After preliminary screening, 67 articles remained for full-text review. Through sequential screening, finally, 10 randomized controlled animal studies were included [17–26]. The literature screening flowchart is presented in Appendix file 3.

The final analysis included 10 studies involving a total of 365 experimental animals, with 199 in the intervention groups and 166 in the control groups. The basic characteristics of the included studies are summarized in Table 1.

Risk of bias assessment results

Ten included studies were assessed for risk of bias using the SYRCLE risk of bias tool for animal studies. Overall, reporting quality was limited, and most studies were judged to have high or unclear risk of bias in several domains, particularly allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other sources of bias. These limitations reduce confidence in the pooled estimates and should be considered when interpreting the meta-analysis results. The detailed assessment is presented in Table 2.

Meta-analysis results

Primordial follicle count

Eight studies contributed 11 intervention-control comparisons for primordial follicle count. Because heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 93%), a random-effects model was used. UCMSC transplantation was associated with a higher primordial follicle count [SMD = 3.71, 95% CI [2.36, 5.07], P < 0.001]; see Table 3 and Supplemental Material Figure S3.

Primary follicle count

Eight studies contributed 11 intervention-control comparisons for primary follicle count. Because heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 80%), a random-effects model was used. UCMSC transplantation was associated with a higher primary follicle count [SMD = 3.54, 95% CI [2.66, 4.42], P < 0.001]; see Table 3 and Supplemental Material Figure S4.

Secondary follicle count

Seven studies contributed 10 intervention-control comparisons for secondary follicle count. Because heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 96%), a random-effects model was used. UCMSC transplantation was associated with a higher secondary follicle count [SMD = 3.00, 95% CI [1.36, 4.64], P < 0.001]; see Table 3 and Supplemental Material Figure S5.

Antral follicle count

Five studies contributed 8 intervention-control comparisons for antral follicle count. Because heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 83%), a random-effects model was used. UCMSC transplantation was associated with a higher antral follicle count [SMD = 3.35, 95% CI [2.28, 4.43], P < 0.001]; see Table 3 and Supplemental Material Figure S6.

Estradiol (E2) level

Seven studies contributed 9 intervention-control comparisons for E2. Because heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 95%), a random-effects model was used. UCMSC transplantation was associated with higher E2 levels [SMD = 3.49, 95% CI [1.77, 5.20], P < 0.001]; see Table 3 and Supplemental Material Figure S7.

Follicle-Stimulating Hormone (FSH) level

Five studies contributed 8 intervention-control comparisons for FSH. Because heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 93%), a random-effects model was used. UCMSC transplantation was associated with lower FSH levels [SMD = -2.70, 95% CI [-4.29, -1.10], P < 0.001]; see Table 3 and Supplemental Material Figure S8.

Anti-Müllerian Hormone (AMH) level

Four studies contributed 8 intervention-control comparisons for AMH. Because heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 90%), a random-effects model was used. UCMSC transplantation was associated with higher AMH levels [SMD = 2.76, 95% CI [1.21, 4.30], P < 0.001]; see Table 3 and Supplemental Material Figure S9.

Subgroup analysis results

To explore the sources of between-study heterogeneity, a subgroup analysis was performed according to the administration route (tail vein injection vs. ovarian injection). The results showed that heterogeneity remained high in each subgroup, indicating that this factor could not explain between-study heterogeneity. A sensitivity analysis was further conducted to explore heterogeneity sources. The sensitivity analysis showed that the pooled results were stable, and no single study exerted a significant impact on the overall heterogeneity. Detailed results are presented in Additional file 8: Supplemental Material 4 .

Discussion

In this meta-analysis of 10 preclinical studies, UCMSC transplantation was associated with increased follicle counts across developmental stages, higher E2 and AMH levels, and lower FSH levels in animal models of POF. These findings suggest a potential beneficial effect of UCMSCs on ovarian structure and endocrine-related outcomes; however, the evidence remains preliminary because of substantial between-study heterogeneity and important methodological limitations.

POF is a common and challenging gynecological disorder, with a progressively increasing incidence and a trend toward affecting younger populations. The etiology and pathogenesis of POF are highly complex and not yet fully elucidated. With advancements in regenerative medicine, stem cell transplantation has emerged as a novel clinical approach for treating POF in women [8]. Stem cells are characterized by their self-renewal capacity and multipotent differentiation potential, exhibiting stable biological properties and remarkable abilities in proliferation and differentiation [11]. Under specific conditions, stem cells can differentiate into various tissue cells, facilitating tissue and organ regeneration and repair. Furthermore, their low immunogenicity and potent immunomodulatory effects make them particularly advantageous for allogeneic transplantation therapies [5]. Given the promising therapeutic outcomes demonstrated by mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) in various animal models of disease, along with positive results from preclinical studies, an increasing number of clinical trials are exploring their potential in treating POF.

The observed direction of effect is broadly consistent with previous reviews describing the potential of MSC-based therapies for POI/POF [5–9]. Mechanistically, UCMSCs may act through homing, paracrine signaling, anti-apoptotic effects, immunomodulation, promotion of angiogenesis, and attenuation of oxidative stress [7, 8, 27–33]. At the same time, the present results should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of repair efficacy. Neither subgroup analyses nor sensitivity analyses effectively reduced the heterogeneity, indicating that administration route could not explain the source of heterogeneity in this study. The observed heterogeneity may stem from multiple factors, including differences in baseline characteristics, model establishment methods, cell dosage, intervention duration, experimental animals, and study design across the included studies, as well as the practice of treating multiple intervention arms within single studies as independent comparisons. Future primary studies should adopt unified experimental protocols and standardized outcome evaluation criteria to minimize methodological differences, so as to improve homogeneity and the reliability of pooled results in subsequent meta-analyses.

As a vital female gonad, the ovary undergoes dynamic cyclical morphological and functional changes from puberty to menopause. Centered on follicular development, maturation, ovulation, and corpus luteum formation and regression, these cyclical activities are closely associated with ovarian reserve function at each stage, directly reflecting dynamic changes in the quantity and quality of oocytes. The maintenance of ovarian reserve function and endocrine function, in turn, relies on the coordinated interactions among follicles, granulosa cells, stromal components, blood vessels, extracellular matrix, and local signaling molecules [3, 34]. During reproductive age, ovarian function generally remains relatively stable. When ovarian reserve declines prematurely, manifesting as reduced follicle count, diminished quality, and hormonal fluctuations, it can lead to decreased fertility and accompany a range of perimenopausal symptoms [1, 3]. POF not only affects reproductive health but may also have long-term implications for systemic metabolism, cardiovascular health, and bone health. Its increasing prevalence among younger women underscores the importance of early identification, intervention, and comprehensive management [34]. Therefore, improvements in follicle counts and hormone profiles are biologically relevant indicators of a more favorable ovarian microenvironment. Nonetheless, these surrogate outcomes do not fully establish durable functional recovery, fertility restoration, or long-term safety, and these endpoints should be prioritized in future preclinical work.

Limitations of this study include the following

(1) The literature search was limited to Chinese and English articles, which may lead to the omission of relevant studies in other languages and introduce selection bias [35, 36]; (2) reporting quality of the included studies was suboptimal, and key methods such as allocation concealment and blinding were rarely described, increasing the risk of selection, performance, and detection bias [37]; (3) some variation existed in the baseline characteristics of the included mice, thereby reducing the comparability of some outcome measures. (4) Substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed across pooled analyses, and subgroup analyses did not adequately explain it, which may limit the reliability of the pooled results; (5) Some meta-analyses treated multiple intervention arms from the same study as independent comparisons, which may have underestimated variance; (6) Given that a considerable number of SYRCLE domains were judged to be at high or unclear risk of bias, the pooled effect estimates could be inflated, resulting in limited confidence in the evidence.

In conclusion, UCMSC transplantation shows promise for improving ovarian microenvironment-related outcomes in animal models of POF, but current evidence is not yet robust enough to support strong translational claims. Future studies should use rigorous randomization and allocation procedures, blinded outcome assessment, appropriate handling of multi-arm designs, and standardized reporting of dose, route, timing, and clinically relevant endpoints so that the therapeutic value of UCMSCs can be assessed more reliably [38].
