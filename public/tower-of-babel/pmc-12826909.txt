Left Subclavian Artery Revascularization With In Situ Laser Fenestration During Thoracic Endovascular Aortic Repair for Acute Complicated or High‐Risk Type B Aortic Dissection: Results of the LLTEVAR Trial
Authors: Zhiyou Peng, Siyuan Liang, Rong Zhang, Peng Qiu, Minyi Yin, Guang Liu, Xiaobing Liu, Xinwu Lu, Kaichuang Ye
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCoverage of the left subclavian artery (LSA) during thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) for type B aortic dissection (TBAD) increases the risk of major adverse events (MAEs). In situ laser‐assisted fenestration allows for LSA revascularization in both elective and urgent settings, yet its safety and efficacy lack robust multicenter data.MethodsLLTEVAR (LSA Revascularization With Laser‐Assisted Fenestration During TEVAR) was a prospective, single‐arm, multicenter study that enrolled patients with acute complicated or high‐risk acute TBAD. The primary safety end point was freedom from composite MAEs (mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction, paraplegia, type Ia endoleak, and retrograde aortic dissection/intramural hematoma) within 30 days after the procedure. Univariate and multivariable analyses identified risk factors for composite MAEs.ResultsA total of 100 patients (mean age, 60.5±12.6 years) were enrolled at 5 vascular centers between July 2018 and September 2021. The technical success rate of LSA fenestration was 98.0% (98/100). The 30‐day primary safety end point was 86.0%. Complicated TBAD (OR, 4.04; 95% CI, 1.05–15.59; P=0.04) and type II/III aortic arch (OR, 4.61; 95% CI, 1.18–17.95; P=0.03) were significantly associated with a higher risk of composite MAEs within 30 days.ConclusionIn situ laser‐assisted fenestration for LSA revascularization during TEVAR demonstrates favorable procedural success and acceptable short‐term outcomes in patients with acute TBAD. However, periprocedural MAEs remain a critical consideration, particularly in those with complicated TBAD with type II/III aortic arch anatomy. Further studies with comparative groups and long‐term follow‐up are warranted to validate these outcomes.RegistrationURL: https://clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: NCT03845829.

The procedure of thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) has become the first‐line intervention for patients with acute complicated or high‐risk type B aortic dissection (TBAD). 1 , 2 A healthy proximal landing zone positively affects short‐term and long‐term outcomes. 3 , 4 To achieve an adequate proximal landing zone in patients with a proximal entry tear originating in zones 2 and 3, coverage of the left subclavian artery (LSA) is required and may be associated with a higher incidence of stroke, spinal cord ischemia, vertebrobasilar ischemia, and left arm ischemia. 5 , 6 , 7 Therefore, recent guidelines recommended considering LSA revascularization during TEVAR when LSA coverage is necessary. 8

Currently available methods for LSA revascularization during the TEVAR procedure include surgical bypass or transposition and endovascular techniques. 9 , 10 , 11 While surgical revascularization was previously considered the standard approach, recent evidence has shown comparable periprocedural outcomes for endovascular and surgical techniques. 8 , 12 Furthermore, surgical methods carry significant risk of complications, including lymph leaks, nerve damage, and other procedure‐related complications. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Endovascular techniques are less invasive approaches and are increasingly used to reconstruct the LSA during TEVAR. The parallel stent technique is associated with a high incidence of endoleak, with type I endoleak being the most common (approximately 11%). 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 Branched or fenestrated stent techniques have a lower risk of endoleak and stroke but are limited by anatomical constraints. 22 , 23 While the recent FDA approval of an off‐the‐shelf single retrograde thoracic branch endoprosthesis (TBE, Gore) has expanded the options for elective repair, 24 the availability of such devices for emergency TEVAR, particularly outside the United States, remains limited. Custom‐made branched endografts require manufacturing time and are not suitable for emergency cases, and physician‐modified fenestrated endografts raise concerns regarding late endoleak and endograft instability such as stent fractures. 24 , 25 In situ laser‐assisted fenestration offers a commercially available alternative endovascular technique for LSA revascularization and is applicable in both elective and emergency settings.

The operative feasibility and technical details of in situ laser‐assisted revascularization for arch branches during TEVAR were previously reported in a single‐center study. 26 Owing to the high technical success rate and low procedural risk, this technique is widely used for the revascularization of LSA and other aortic branches in China. 27 , 28 , 29 However, whether the technique increases or reduces the incidence of major adverse events (MAEs) compared with previously reported outcomes without LSA reconstruction was not investigated. Therefore, we conducted a prospective, multicenter single‐arm trial to evaluate the 30‐day safety and 1‐year efficacy outcomes of in situ laser‐assisted fenestration for LSA revascularization during TEVAR in patients with acute complicated or high‐risk TBAD with a proximal entry tear originating in zones 2 and 3.

METHODS

Trial Design and Patients

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. The LLTEVAR (LSA Revascularization with Laser‐assisted Fenestration During TEVAR) study is a prospective, single‐arm, multicenter trial that planned to enroll 100 patients at 5 vascular centers (NCT03845829). Patients presenting with acute (1–14 days) complicated or high‐risk acute TBAD (complicated by malperfusion syndrome or rupture; high‐risk features include refractory pain, refractory hypertension, bloody pleural effusion, aortic diameter >40 mm, imaging evidence of malperfusion, entry tear on lesser curvature, or false lumen >22 mm), 30 with a proximal entry tear originating in zone 2 or zone 3, who were deemed appropriate candidates for TEVAR with LSA revascularization, met the inclusion criteria, and consented to participate were enrolled (Figure 1). The distance from the proximal edge of the LSA orifice to the intended proximal landing location had to be >5 mm. Patients were excluded if they were preprocedurally scheduled to have proximal aortic arch coverage in zone 1 or zone 0. Other main exclusion criteria included patients who were uncomplicated, chronic TBAD, inappropriate for TEVAR due to unfavorable anatomic characteristics (no commercially available aortic stent for the size of the aorta, no available access for TEVAR, both LAS and left vertebral artery originating from the aorta, etc), previous open surgery for the aortic arch, and connective tissue disorders. The LLTEVAR trial was approved by the institutional review board of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Jiaotong University School of Medicine (SH9H‐2018‐T71‐2), and all patients provided written informed consent. Reporting standards for TEVAR were based on the Society of Vascular Surgery guidelines. 31

Patient Management

Upon diagnosis, all patients received optimal medical therapy, consisting of close intensive care unit monitoring and control of blood pressure, heart rate, and pain. TEVAR procedures were performed for patients with complicated and high‐risk TBAD. Patients not responding to medical therapy or presenting with rupture or impending rupture, malperfusion were treated emergently (within 24 hours); otherwise, TEVAR procedures were performed 2 weeks later. The procedure of TEVAR with in situ laser‐assisted fenestration for LSA revascularization has been previously described in detail. 26 , 27 , 28 Briefly, the TEVAR procedures were performed under local or general anesthesia per institutional practice, and aortic stent‐grafts were deployed through femoral access, and LSA fenestration stents were inserted through the brachial artery. The diameter of the aortic stent‐graft was based on the proximal healthy landing zone with approximately 5% oversizing. If the diameter of the stent‐graft mismatched the diameter of the distal attachment site of the aorta (>20% oversizing), a distal restricted stent technique was used. 32 After an aortic stent‐graft was deployed, a 6F steerable sheath combined with a balloon catheter (Mustang, Boston Scientific, 4 mm in diameter and 4 cm in length) allowed the laser tip to be placed in the intended fenestration site outside the aortic stent‐graft. After the fenestration hole was established, the balloon catheter was pushed forward and through the fenestration hole, followed by balloon angioplasty for the established hole, the laser tip was exchanged with a 0.035‐inch guidewire. Then the fenestration hole was subsequently dilated up to the reference diameter of the subclavian artery, and a balloon‐expandable (Lifestream, BD) or self‐expandable (Fluency, BD) covered‐stent (10% oversizing of the LSA in diameter) was deployed in the fenestration. Poststent dilation was always required for the LSA stent. Final arteriography was performed from the ascending aorta to assess the success of the procedure and to identify potential endoleak and residual stenosis of the LSA stent. For a significant type Ia endoleak, coil embolization was performed in the false lumen distal to the aortic endograft landing zone. This procedure was carried out by superselectively accessing the false lumen through the distal entry tear.

All patients were discharged with oral medications to maintain stable blood pressure and heart rate. Aortic CT angiography was routinely performed before discharge, at 1, 6, 12 months, and annually thereafter. Additional CT angiography was conducted for symptom recurrence or suspected aortic pathology.

Trial End Points

The primary safety end point was freedom from a composite of major adverse events (MAEs) within 30 days after the procedure, including mortality, paraplegia, stroke, myocardial infarction, type Ia endoleak, and retrograde aortic dissection/intramural hematoma (IMH). A safety committee adjudicated the end points in this trial. The sample size was determined using a precision‐based approach to ensure a reliable estimate of the primary safety outcome (the 30‐day composite MAE rate). Based on reported 30‐day MAE rates for TEVAR without LSA revascularization (23.9%–30%), 6 , 17 , 19 , 20 , 33 we adopted a conservative expected rate of 25% for calculation purposes. We aimed to estimate the 30‐day MAE rate with a margin of error of ±10% at a 95% confidence level. Applying the formula for estimating a single‐arm study, a minimum of 73 patients was required. Accounting for an anticipated 20% loss to follow‐up, a total of 92 patients needed to be enrolled. To enhance the robustness and precision of our estimates, we ultimately enrolled 100 patients. Univariate and multivariable analyses identified risk factors for MAEs within 30 days after the TEVAR procedure, including general characteristics of patients, anatomic information of dissection, and TEVAR procedure characteristics. Anatomic information of dissection included tear located in inner aortic curve, diameter of proximal tear >10 mm, false lumen diameter >22 mm, distance between LSA and proximal tear <2 cm, proximal entry tear in zone 2, LSA‐Aorta angle <45 (defined as the acute angle formed between the central axis of the LSA and the central axis of the aortic arch at its origin.) and type II/III aortic arch. TEVAR procedure characteristics included time of intervention, type of stent‐graft, type of LSA stent, and distal restrict stent. The secondary efficacy end point was freedom from a composite of all‐cause death; LSA in‐stent restenosis; and reintervention due to dissection progression, growth, and endoleak at 1 year after the procedure.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as means±SDs, and categorical variables are summarized as counts and proportions. Univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to identify the potential risk factors for MAEs within 30 days after the procedure, with statistically significant factors included multivariable analysis. The secondary efficacy outcomes were analyzed for all enrolled patients following the intention‐to‐treat principle. Time‐to‐event analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier method to account for variable follow‐up times. Patients who were event‐free at their last known follow‐up date were censored at that time. SPSS version 26.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used for the statistical analyses. Differences with a P value <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline Characteristics and Procedural Characteristics

Between July 2018 and September 2021, a total of 100 patients were enrolled at 5 vascular centers. Patients’ general characteristics and comorbidities are detailed in Table 1. Seventy‐eight patients were treated under local anesthesia, and 22 under general anesthesia. Aortic stent‐graft implantation was performed through femoral artery puncture, except for 1 patient who underwent an incision. Technical success of the TEVAR procedure was achieved in all patients, with correct deployment of the aortic stent‐grafts and complete exclusion of the proximal entry tear. Intentional coverage of the left carotid artery during the TEVAR procedure in order to achieve an adequate proximal landing zone was required in 4 patients, who were simultaneously treated with parallel stenting, and no major adverse events were observed in these 4 patients. The technical success of in situ laser‐assisted fenestration for LSA revascularization was 98.0% (98/100). Parallel stenting was performed in the remaining 2 patients with failed laser‐assisted fenestration due to inadequate distance between the proximal edge of the aortic graft and the intended fenestration site (Table 2). Three patients presenting with mesenteric ischemia underwent superior mesenteric artery reconstruction using bare metal stents. Six patients required common iliac artery revascularization due to limb ischemia before the TEVAR procedure. Distal restricted stents were used in 12 patients. Coil embolization was simultaneously performed in 4 patients with type Ia endoleaks after TEVAR (Videos S1–S4). Prophylactic cerebrospinal fluid drainage was performed preprocedurally in 1 patient who had prior EVAR for abdominal aorta pathology. The procedural details are presented in Table 2.

Safety Outcomes

The primary 30‐day safety end point was 86.0% (86/100), with 15 MAEs occurring in 14 patients (Table 3). Type Ia endoleak was identified in 5 patients; 4 were treated with simultaneous coil embolization, while the remaining 1 patient underwent close surveillance with daily blood pressure monitoring, monthly telephone follow‐ups for symptoms, and serial CT angiography at 1, 3, and 6 months to evaluate false lumen diameter. Four patients experienced a stroke, and 1 of them died 6 days after the procedure. One patient died 4 days later after the procedure due to persisting mesenteric ischemia, resulting in a 30‐day mortality rate of 2.0% (2/100). One patient developed paraplegia postoperatively but recovered fully within 2 weeks following cerebrospinal fluid drainage. One patient experienced myocardial infarction 2 days after the TEVAR procedure and underwent emergent coronary artery stenting. Retrograde ascending aortic dissection occurred in 1 patient, and the patient was treated with surgical repair. One patient presented with retrograde ascending aortic intramural hematoma, managed with medical therapy; the intramural hematoma partially resolved within 1 month and fully resolved by 6 months (Figure 2).

Risk Factors Associated With MAEs

Univariate logistic regression identified complicated TBAD (OR, 4.63; 95% CI, 1.36–15.69; P=0.01), LSA‐aorta angle <45 (OR, 4.51; 95% CI, 1.12–18.19; P=0.03) and type II/III aortic arch (OR, 5.62; 95% CI, 1.69–18.69; P=0.005) as potential risk factors for composite MAEs within 30 days (Table 4). Multivariable logistic regression analysis confirmed that complicated TBAD (OR, 4.04; 95% CI, 1.05–15.59; P=0.04) and type II/III aortic arch (OR, 4.61; 95% CI, 1.18–17.95; P=0.03) were 2 statistically significant risk factors of MAEs within 30 days after the procedure.

Efficacy Outcomes

The secondary efficacy end point at 1 year after procedure was 90.0% (Figure 3), with 9 events observed. The events comprised 4 deaths, 2 cases of in‐stent restenosis, and 3 reinterventions due to dissection. Four patients who were event‐free at their last follow‐up (6 months) were censored at that time. Eighty‐seven patients completed the full 12‐month follow‐up schedule. Two patients died during the follow‐up: 1 from brain hemorrhage at 5 months and another from acute coronary syndrome at 7 months. In‐stent restenosis of the LSA occurred in 2 patients during follow‐up (at 6 and 7 months after the TEVAR procedure, respectively). The symptomatic patient, who presented with dizziness potentially attributable to subclavian steal syndrome, underwent successful endovascular revision with balloon angioplasty and additional stent placement. Six patients required reintervention due to dissection progression, growth, or endoleak during follow‐up. Two received coil embolization for persistent chest pain due to type Ia endoleak (at 7 months) or type II endoleak (at 11 months). One underwent fenestrated EVAR at 3 months due to the expansion of the abdominal aorta. Of 4 patients with type R endoleak, 3 received distal aortic stent‐graft extension (at 12, 24, and 24 months post‐TEVAR) due to false lumen expansion, while the fourth remained asymptomatic with no false lumen expansion, required only surveillance. No stent‐graft migration and stent fracture were identified in CT angiography during a median of 17 months (range: 1–60 months) follow‐up.

DISCUSSION

This prospective study demonstrates that in situ laser‐assisted fenestration for LSA revascularization during TEVAR for complicated or high‐risk acute TBAD is highly successful with acceptable short‐term outcomes. However, periprocedural MAEs, particularly type Ia endoleak and stroke, remain significant concerns in complicated TBAD patients with type II/III aortic arch. Fortunately, the favorable 1‐year secondary outcomes further support the clinical utility of this technique in appropriately selected patients.

In patients with proximal intimal tears near the LSA (zones 2 and 3), the coverage of the LSA is often necessary to achieve an adequate proximal landing zone. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 However, TVEAR with LSA coverage carries a twofold higher risk of MAEs compared with TVEAR without LSA coverage, 34 with the most frequent adverse events being type II endoleak and persistent false lumen perfusion. 35 Additionally, sacrificing the LSA poses other risks, including spinal cord injury, anterior circulation stroke, arm ischemia, and vertebrobasilar ischemia, rendering it a less favorable approach. 6 , 33 , 36 , 37 Furthermore, some TBAD patients require delayed EVAR in the chronic stage, where the risk of paraplegia is further increased if the LSA is sacrificed. Consequently, current guidelines recommend LSA revascularization when TEVAR obstructs antegrade LSA flow. 8 , 30

Type Ia endoleak represented the most prevalent MAEs in this study. Among the 14 patients who experienced MAEs, 5 had type Ia endoleak, resulting in an overall incidence rate of 5%. The reported incidence of type Ia endoleak following TEVAR with branch artery reconstruction varies substantially by reconstruction techniques: chimney technique (18.8%, 182/964), fenestrated stent grafts (4.8%, 28/575), in situ fenestration (2.2%, 12/528), and branched devices (3.0%, 23/761). 17 A 2012 systematic review reported that parallel stenting was associated with an overall endoleak rate of 21.6%, with 11.8% specifically attributed to type Ia endoleaks. 19 These findings were further supported by a 2018 meta‐analysis, which documented a comparable perioperative endoleak rate of 21%. 20 Our findings indicate that a relatively elevated incidence of this complication might be attributed to a higher proportion of patients with type II/III aortic arch. Piazza M et al reported that a significantly greater proximal sealing length may be necessary to reduce the risk of type Ia endoleak during TEVAR in patients with type II/III aortic arches. 4 Coil embolization proved effective for managing type Ia endoleak in both the concurrent and delayed setting. In our study, all 5 type Ia endoleaks were successfully managed with this technique, 4 during the initial TEVAR, and 1 at the 7‐month follow‐up, with type Ia endoleak still existing.

Stroke represented another significant periprocedural MAE in our study. Although all LSAs were reconstructed in the TEVAR procedure, the incidence of stroke was still as high as 4.0%, which aligns with previous reports, ranging from 2.7% to 5.8% in meta‐analyses and larger registries. 7 , 38 , 39 Besides the coverage of LSA without revascularization, which increases the risk of stroke, patients with complex arch anatomy (type II/III aortic arch) are more susceptible to stroke events after TEVAR. 40 Meanwhile, 1 meta‐analysis revealed that the incidence of stroke after TEVAR procedure in complicated TBAD was higher than in uncomplicated TBAD (5.85% versus 3.92%; P<0.01). 38 Although uncomplicated TBAD patients were not included in the current study, complicated TBAD was identified as a statistically significant risk factor for MAEs (OR, 4.04; 95% CI, 1.05–15.59; P=0.04), which inherently indicates that the incidence of MAEs in this subgroup is significantly higher compared with high‐risk TBAD patients. This elevated incidence of MAE might be partially attributable to the urgent nature of interventions for complicated TBAD, as these procedures were typically performed during superacute or acute phases. Li et al found that patients treated in acute had a significantly higher risk of MAEs within 30 days after TEVAR, with 15.8% in acute compared with patients with 5.2% in the subacute. 41 In 1 meta‐analysis, 42 patients with TBAD treated in hyperacute encounter a higher risk of MAEs within 30 days after the procedure, with an odds ratio of 1.58 compared with late interventions.

Patients with type II/III aortic arch require longer proximal seal length for safe and durable TEVAR in acute TBAD. 4 In the current study, aortic stent graft consistently covered the LSA to achieve adequate proximal sealing. Nevertheless, 15 MAEs still occurred in 14 patients within the initial 30 days post‐procedurally, with a particularly higher incidence among individuals with complicated TBAD and type II/III aortic arch. This phenomenon is multifactorial. Firstly, it is associated with the heightened technical complexity of performing in situ fenestration for LSA revascularization, which inevitably extends the overall duration of the procedure. The iterative maneuvers within the arch during the procedure evidently contribute to an increased risk of stroke. Secondly, these findings underscore the imperative for innovative approaches and modifications in the design of aortic stent grafts tailored to patients with type II/III aortic arch, taking into careful consideration the complexity identified in this subset of individuals.

The secondary efficacy end point was also analyzed in this study. Two patients died from nondissection‐related causes, and 1 patient with LSA in‐stent restenosis required balloon angioplasty. Aortic‐related reinterventions were primarily attributed to endoleaks, which should be vigilantly monitored during postprocedural surveillance. Notably, this study observed no instances of aortic stent‐graft migration, a frequent complication of TEVAR in the mid‐term follow‐up 43 that did not occur in this study. In the procedure of in situ fenestration, the LSA stent acts as an anchor for the aortic stent‐graft and secures the aortic stent‐graft to the aortic wall likely contributed to the absence of aortic stent‐graft migration.

LIMITATIONS

This study has several limitations. First, the single‐arm design precludes direct comparison of safety and efficacy outcomes with traditional surgical approaches, such as carotid‐subclavian bypass and transposition. However, in the current endovascular era, preserving the normal anatomical structure of LSA is crucial for potential access sites in the fenestrated and branched EVAR procedure. Second, the relatively short follow‐up period may underestimate late complications. It has been reported that approximately half of the dissections progress to aneurysmal dilation of the dissected aorta at 5 years after the initial onset of dissection, with one‐third of patients requiring intervention for aneurysmal pathologies of the diseased aorta. 43 , 44 Thus, extended follow‐up is necessary to reveal the long‐term results after the TEVAR procedure. Third, given the limited sample size in this study, the risk factors analysis for MAEs yielded wide CIs in odds ratios. Larger studies are needed to confirm these associations.

CONCLUSIONS

In this prospective, single‐arm, and multicenter trial, the in situ laser‐assisted fenestration of LSA revascularization during TEVAR for complicated or high‐risk acute TBAD with proximal entry tears originating in zones 2 and 3 is a successful procedure with acceptable short‐term outcomes. Attention should be directed towards patients with type II/III aortic arch or those with complicated TBAD, given the higher risk of periprocedural stroke and type Ia endoleak. Further studies with an adequately powered sample size incorporating comparator groups and longer‐term follow‐up are warranted to validate these promising results. Furthermore, next‐generation aortic stent‐grafts should be designed to reduce MAE risk in complicated TBAD with complex arch anatomy.
