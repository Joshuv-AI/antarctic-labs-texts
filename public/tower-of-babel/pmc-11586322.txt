An Exploratory Vector Field Analysis of Ground Reaction Force During Maximum Sprinting Efforts in Male Soccer Players and Sprinters
Authors: Daniel Kadlec, Steffi Colyer, Ryu Nagahara, Sophia Nimphius
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe ability to accelerate and attain high velocities is essential for both individual and team sport athletes. The purpose of this explorative study was to retrospectively analyze the ground reaction force using Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM) vector field analysis, as traditional scalar analyses often fail to acknowledge the interdependence of force vector components. The ground reaction force vector and the scalar components (i.e., vertical and horizontal force) were analyzed for 28 male sprinters and 24 male soccer players at 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1, and between 85%, 90%, 95%, and 100% of their maximum velocity. The ground reaction force vector differed predominately during mid‐stance and late stance between both groups at 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1. Within‐group analysis also revealed that in both groups, the first ~60% of stance tended to increase in relevance when transitioning to higher sprinting velocities as evident by the increasing {T 2 } test statistic between adjacent velocity comparisons. Sprinters and soccer player reached their respective maximal sprinting velocity at ~40 m. This exploratory study discusses the potential limitations of scalar analysis and highlights the benefits of incorporating vector field analysis. Although both analysis methods resulted in similar conclusions in our re‐analysis, vector field analysis may still enhance the understanding of force application in sprint performance by considering the interdependence of force components. We recommend utilizing vector field analyses alongside traditional methods in sports biomechanics research to ultimately enhance the accuracy of interpretations related to vector data.

Introduction

The ability to accelerate effectively and achieve high velocities during sprinting is a critical determinant of success in athletics and a variety of team and field sports. The analysis of ground reaction forces (GRFs) during sprinting is central to understanding the mechanical demands and provides critical insights into the kinetic determinants that differentiate faster from slower athletes [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. Since force is a vector quantity comprising both magnitude and orientation, a detailed understanding of GRF vector dynamics can improve our knowledge of human locomotion and aid in developing effective interventions to enhance sprint performance.

Traditionally, the evaluation of sprint performance has involved decomposing the GRF vector into its vertical and horizontal components for analysis. However, analyzing these components separately reduces the original dimensionality of the vector and assumes independence between the components [6, 7]. This scalar reduction can lead to incomplete or misleading interpretations because it overlooks the interdependence and covariance between the vector components [7, 8].

To address these limitations, advancements in statistical techniques now allow for the analysis of continuous vector data, simultaneously considering both the magnitude and orientation of the vector. One such technique is Statistical Parametric Mapping (SPM), which examines the entire time series and reduces the risk of biases associated with discrete data extraction by focusing solely on peak values [9]. When applied to vector fields, SPM not only preserves the temporal structure of the data but also addresses the covariance between vector components, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the force vectors involved in sprinting [7]. Understanding the temporal structure of the resultant force applied during sprinting, specifically when transitioning from submaximal to maximal velocities in populations with different sprinting abilities, can indicate which factors differentiate performance levels.

The purpose of this study was to retrospectively re‐analyze previously collected sprint data using SPM vector field analysis and to compare the results with traditional analyses of the vertical and horizontal vector components in sprint performance. The original study by Colyer, Nagahara, Takai, and Salo aimed to “understand how specific aspects of ground reaction force waveforms allow some individuals to continue to accelerate beyond the velocity plateau of others” [10]. In this current exploratory study, we first repeated the original analysis by comparing the ground reaction force components (F y and F z ) between male soccer players and sprinters at 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1. Additionally, we extended the analysis by comparing the ground reaction force vector (F yz ) between the same groups and at the same velocities to evaluate whether vector analysis yields differing results compared to separate analyses of the individual scalar components. This aimed to compare the ability to express force at a given velocity between sprinters and soccer players. Further, we expanded upon the original investigation by conducting within‐group analyses at adjacent velocity thresholds of 85%, 90%, 95%, and 100% of maximum velocity to evaluate changes in force expression as athletes increased their velocity. This aimed to determine the change in the magnitude and direction of the force vector as athletes approach their maximum sprinting velocity. Based on this exploratory analysis, we propose testable hypotheses that could inform future research efforts to optimize sprint performance.

Materials and Methods

Experimental Procedures

Twenty‐eight male sprinters (age: 20 ± 1 years, mass: 66.5 ± 3.6 kg, height: 1.73 ± 0.04 m) and 24 male soccer players (age: 20 ± 1 years, mass: 69.1 ± 5.7 kg, height: 1.73 ± 0.06 m) volunteered to participate in this study. Sprinters were sprint specialists with 100‐m personal best times ranging from 10.88 to 11.96 s. Sprinters performed between two and five maximal‐effort 60‐m sprints in spikes from their normal crouched block start position and soccer players performed three maximal‐effort sprints from a standing start in flat running shoes. All trials were performed on an indoor running track with 54 triaxial force plates (1000 Hz; TF‐90100, TF‐3055, TF‐32120; Tec Gihan, Uji, Japan) measured ground reaction forces during sprinting through 52 m from 1.5 m behind the start line to the 50.5 m mark. An extra set of photocells at 0 and 60 m identified each participant's fastest trial for inclusion in subsequent analyses. Ethical approval for this research was granted by a local research ethics committee and all athletes provided written consent before participating.

Data Processing

Ground reaction force data were filtered and the vertical (F z ) and horizontal (F y ) components were extracted as previously described [1]. The vector components were filtered using a fourth‐order low‐pass Butterworth filter with a 70‐Hz cut‐off frequency derived through residual analysis. Touchdown and take‐off thresholds were set at 20 N of vertical force. Horizontal velocity was calculated using the impulse‐momentum relationship, including an aerodynamic drag adjustment. This approach was previously verified against the horizontal step velocity derived from spatiotemporal data (i.e., step length and frequency) [1].

To investigate differences in GRF between sprinters and soccer players across the entire stance phase at matched absolute running velocities, the vertical and horizontal forces during the stance phase with forward velocity at the instant of touchdown of 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1 were extracted and matched across participants. This aimed to replicate the original findings reported by Colyer et al. [10]. Trials were excluded from further analysis if the forward velocity at touchdown was outside of 0.1 m·s−1 from the targeted velocity, resulting in 24 participants in each cohort for 8.0 m·s−1 and 28 sprint and 21 soccer athletes for 8.5 m·s−1, respectively. Next, to evaluate differences in GRF across the entire stance phase between increasing relative sprinting velocities within sprinters and within soccer players, the vertical and horizontal forces during the stance phase with forward velocity at the instant of touchdown at 85%, 90%, 95%, and 100% of maximal velocity were extracted and matched across both cohorts. Additionally, we present the differences between the calculated relative touchdown velocity and the actual velocity observed at the step best matching that relative percentage.

Further, GRF for regions of interest were separately extracted to evaluate between and within‐group differences across three subphases of the stance phase. As such, the regions of interest were (A) “initial braking”, which was defined from initial contact to the local maximum of the horizontal force component between 10% and 30% of stance, (B) “late braking”, defined from the local maximum of the horizontal force component between 10% and 30% of stance to the point before horizontal force becomes positive and (C) “entire propulsion” defined from the point horizontal force becomes positive until the end of stance (Figure 1).

The ground reaction forces for the entire stance phase were linearly registered to 101 nodes (0%–100% stance) and expressed relative to body mass for further analyses.

Statistical Analyses

The open‐source package “spm1d” located at http://www.spm1d.org/ was used to evaluate within and between‐group differences of ground reaction forces across any section of the stance or specific region of interest at 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1 and 85%, 90%, 95%, and 100% of maximal velocity, respectively. A two‐sample Hotelling's T 2 test was used to assess differences in the ground reaction force vector components (i.e., F z and F y ) at 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1 between sprinter and soccer athletes, respectively. A paired Hotelling's T 2 test was used to evaluate only the within‐group differences of the ground reaction force vector between two adjacent velocity thresholds (i.e., differences between 85% and 90%, 90% and 95%, and 95% and 100% were compared). For simplicity of visual representations, a mean resultant ground reaction force trajectory with the standard deviation ellipse was calculated to support the qualitative interpretation of the between and within‐group analyses across the three subphases (Figures 4 and 8). The graphical depiction of the specific regions of interest (Figures 4 and 8) describes the covariance (i.e., the direction of the linear relationship) between the vector components (e.g., between F z and F y ) and demonstrates their non‐independence. The orientation and shape of the confidence ellipse illustrate the covariance and quantify the correlation (i.e., the direction and magnitude of the linear relationship) between F z and F y . If the vector components are correlated and thus dependent, the ellipse forms a circle. This approach is considered the equivalent of the full model involving intra‐trial variability when the residuals are normally distributed [7, 9].

The SPM analysis uses random field theory to objectively identify field regions that co‐vary significantly with the experimental design [7]. Statistical inference was conducted by calculating the scalar output statistics for the multivariate {T 2} and univariate {t} SPM test statistics at each individual data point over the normalized time series. Because of the smoothness of force‐time curves and the interdependence of neighboring points, multiple adjacent points of the {T 2} or {t} curve often exceeded the critical threshold. Temporal smoothness was estimated based on the average temporal gradient. The critical test statistic value was determined, above which only 5% of the data would be expected if the trajectory resulted from an equally smooth random process. Exact p values were computed for each supra‐threshold cluster based on their temporal extent [9, 11].

It has been proposed that multivariate and univariate SPM tests should be implemented hierarchically when testing non‐directed hypotheses of vector fields [7]. The entire vector field should be analyzed first, and single vector components should only be tested in a post hoc manner if statistical significance is reached at the vector‐field level. In this explorative analysis, we only incorporated F z and F y . Following the vector field analysis, post hoc tests were conducted on each vector component. Since current post hoc procedures in spm1d for multivariate data are likely too simple [12], separate SPM one‐dimensional two‐sample t‐tests and paired t‐tests for the vector components were used independently to determine between‐group differences at 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1 and within‐group differences between two adjacent velocity thresholds (same as above), respectively. It has to be acknowledged that this assumes that the post hoc tests are independent and neglect vector component covariance. To retain a family‐wise Type I error rate of α = 5%, we adopted a Bonferroni correction for multiple (n = 2) tests. Statistical significance was accepted at α = 5%. All statistical analyses were completed in the Spyder (version: 5.4.3) distribution of Python (version: 3.11.4 64‐bit).

Discussion

The magnitude and orientation of the ground reaction force during the stance phase dictate an athlete's sprinting performance and provide insights between faster and slower athletes. The purpose of this study was to retrospectively analyze the vector field data of male soccer players and sprinters at 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1 and 85%, 90%, 95%, and 100% of maximum velocity. The ground reaction force vector differed predominantly during late braking and the entire propulsion between both groups at 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1, whereas the first ~60% of stance tended to increase in relevance when transitioning to higher sprinting velocities in both groups. Both groups reached their maximal sprinting velocity at ~40 m. The following discussion explores the benefits and implications of vector field analyses, describes and interprets the current findings, and proposes testable hypotheses relevant to improving sprinting performance.

Vector Field Analysis

Vector field analyses provide more objective approaches to detecting meaningful differences in the resultant vector between groups and are more robust against Type I or Type II errors (i.e., false positives and false negatives) compared to univariate analysis of separate vector components for non‐directed hypothesis testing. Vector field analyses in SPM consider the resulting magnitude and covariance of the resultant vector across the entire measurement domain as the indivisible unit of observation (e.g., 0%–100% of stance) while simultaneously controlling for multiple comparisons [13]. As such, failing to consider the entire vector domain and the covariance amongst vector components can lead to previously defined biases (i.e., Post hoc regional focus bias and Inter‐component covariance bias), potentially resulting in flawed outcomes when reducing a vector to its separate components [7]. Separately analyzing vector components may underestimate group differences. For instance, the magnitude of the resultant vector is, based on Pythagoras' theorem, always larger than the magnitude of each component in isolation—except in the experimentally improbable scenario that the difference of the same vector component between both groups is zero (e.g., in this research project if ∆Fz¯=0 and/or ∆Fy¯=0). Vector field analyses address this issue by testing the entire vector field as one dependent variable, thereby reducing the risk of Type II errors.

Simultaneously analyzing multiple vector components can lead to spurious findings if one fails to adjust for multiple comparisons. When conducting multiple statistical tests, the probability of at least one test reaching a significant threshold increases with the number of tests conducted. For instance, analyzing the X, Y, and Z vector components separately treats each as an independent dataset. However, because these vector components are not independent, an appropriate correction for multiple comparisons is necessary to maintain the family‐wise error rate across all vector components [7, 8]. It has been proposed that statistical significance should first be confirmed at the vector‐field level before proceeding with post hoc testing on separate vector components, adjusted for multiple comparisons. Failing to account for this can alter the results and practical applications of experimental studies, particularly in research on injury risk. For example, knee valgus moments are commonly used as a proxy for anterior cruciate ligament injury risk and isolated reductions in knee valgus moments are usually considered beneficial for minimizing injury risk. However, the knee valgus moment alone may fail to represent the complexity of the multi‐planar loading experienced in dynamic tasks [14, 15]. Similarly, attributing superior relevance to the magnitude of one vector component over another for sprinting performance without acknowledging their interdependence is a misleading approach as it fundamentally neglects basic mechanics [4]. This discussion highlights that analyzing isolated vector components and even reducing them to a discrete value rather than the entire vector can lead to flawed results, questionable interpretations, and ineffective training strategies [16, 17, 18]. A hierarchical approach using vector field analyses may overcome the issue of multiple comparisons and reduce Type I errors in the long run.

This has non‐trivial implications for non‐directed hypothesis testing of vector fields, as separate analyses of isolated vector components assume independence. In the current findings, all covariance ellipses pointed toward the origin, indicating that the mean vertical and horizontal forces in specific regions of interest are dependent (Figures 4 and 8). Ignoring this dependence can increase the risk of Type I errors. Vector field analysis overcomes this issue by assessing the covariance to inform appropriate analytic approaches.

Vector Field Results

The current re‐analysis of the SPM vector field aligns closely with the original findings by Colyer et al. [10], confirming the original results. The additional vector field analysis demonstrated a significantly increased resultant force vector for the sprinters during mid‐stance (31%–76% and 30%–82%) and late stance (86%–100% and 88%–100%) at 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1, respectively. However, the subsequent post hoc analysis of the vertical and horizontal vector components only reduced the significant stance phases due to adjustments for multiple comparisons. For example, the differences in vertical force originally reported over 39%–50%, 55%–62%, and 88%–99% of stance were reduced to 42%–46% and 86%–98% at 8.0 m·s−1. Despite these analytical differences, the current vector field results agree with the original interpretation based on separately analyzing the vector components that “Sprinters were able to generate higher ground reaction forces compared to the soccer players from late braking phase, through mid‐stance and across the majority of the propulsive phase” [10]. In fact, the T 2 statistic clearly exceeds the critical threshold during those stance periods as compared to the t statistic for the separate vector components (Figure 3A,B). This provides more confidence that the between‐group difference in the resultant force magnitude over this part of stance is meaningful and of practical importance, as concluded in the original paper [10]. Additional calculations of multivariate effect sizes would support the interpretation, but they are not yet supported in spm1d. The T 2 statistic values exactly follow effect size value patterns across the 1D domain.

The within‐group SPM vector field analysis between adjacent velocities thresholds revealed an increasing relevance of the first ~60% of stance, especially when transitioning from 95% to 100% of maximum velocity in both groups (Figures 6 and 7). This finding aligns with previous research, indicating that changes in velocity are primarily determined by the average vertical forces applied during the first half of the stance period [19]. This is further confirmed by the significant changes in vertical force during initial and late braking (first 22%–45%) when sprinters and soccer players transition from 95% to 100% of maximum velocity (Figures 6C and 7C). Previously, elite sprinters exhibited an increasingly asymmetrical vertical force pattern while approaching maximum velocity (i.e., vertical force pattern with steeper rising after initial contact and peak force that occurs well before midstance) [19]. This suggested that faster sprinters deviated more from a simple spring‐loaded inverted pendulum (SLIP) model to exploit different force‐augmentation mechanisms, particularly at higher velocities [19].

These increasing asymmetrical ground reaction force patterns across faster velocities may correspond to an athlete's ability to store and release mechanical energy throughout the stance phase. In other words, faster athletes may be better able to “load and release the spring” during different parts of the stance, allowing them to operate beyond the constraints of the intrinsic muscle properties [20, 21]. This can be inferred from the mean force vector across different regions of interest (Figure 8A–C). In detail, no qualitative differences were observed between groups at any specific velocity during the initial braking phase (Figure 8A). However, meaningful variations became evident within and between groups throughout the late braking phase, the phase in which peak vertical force occurs, as sprinting velocities increased (Figure 8B). While considerable between‐group differences were observed during the entire propulsion, the mean vector remained relatively consistent across all velocities within each group (Figure 8C). This may suggest that sprinters are more efficient at storing and subsequently releasing mechanical energy throughout the stance phases. The preceding swing phase characteristics likely contributed to these between‐group variations in resultant ground reaction forces. Particularly, the late‐swing phase position and trajectory of the foot may influence the initial force application and subsequent energy flow dynamics. This highlights the importance of considering the interdependence of kinematic and kinetic factors when evaluating sprinting technique and performance [22, 23].

The results of the between‐group analysis at fixed velocities can be interpreted with the concept of storing and releasing mechanical energy throughout stance. Since no qualitative differences were detected in the mean vector during initial braking (i.e., ~0%–20% of stance), it appears that sprinters have superior abilities during mid‐stance (i.e., ~20%–50% of stance) to utilize force‐augmentation mechanisms. In other words, sprinters can elicit higher resultant forces, especially during late braking, as seen in Figure 5, which may correspond to “loading the spring” harder and faster with increasingly shorter ground contact times and subsequently “releasing the spring” during the entire propulsion [24]. In contrast, soccer players displayed an increasing bimodal shape of the resultant force at faster velocities (Figure 5). This may indicate a limited ability to resist deformation during initial braking and a reduced efficiency in tolerating and coordinating the transition from initial braking to late braking, affecting their ability to store and release mechanical energy effectively during stance. Similarly, the differences for the 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1 during the entire propulsion phase (Figure 4C) can be interpreted as sprinters displaying a superior efficacy to “releasing the spring”, despite trivial differences during the initial braking (Figure 4A). It has to be acknowledged that stance times differed between and within groups and that these have to be considered when interpreting these results (Table 1). Specifically, the variations in stance times mean that when force‐time data are time‐normalized (e.g., interpolated to 101 nodes), the temporal alignment of key events within the stance phase may differ between athletes and could potentially skew the interpretation of the force application patterns.

The commonly used SLIP model to describe legged locomotion [25, 26, 27] fails to explain the asymmetrical horizontal ground reaction force pattern during faster travel velocities (i.e., the large negative spike during early stance compared to the subsequent positive magnitudes) [17, 28, 29]. A more realistic torque‐actuated dissipative spring‐loaded inverted pendulum (TD‐SLIP) model with damping and actuation through a controllable hip torque has demonstrated an increased accuracy in predicting human running across faster velocities [28, 30]. The TD‐SLIP model offers consistent energy regulation across strides and emphasizes the hip extensor's ability to create sufficient torque before and especially during stance to additionally “load the spring” from atop [30], while the ankle and knee to resist deformation (i.e., stiffness) during stance across faster sprinting velocities [31, 32, 33, 34]. Further research is warranted to validate the TD‐SLIP model for elite sprint performance through a segmental energy flow analysis. This could evaluate whether energy absorption during initial braking contributes to energy generation during late braking and the entire propulsion and reveal whether sprinters exhibit differences in the magnitude of energy absorption compared to energy generation, potentially informing joint‐specific determinants of sprinting performance. Understanding these energy dynamics at the ankle, knee, and hip joints could guide targeted interventions to overcome individual limitations and enhance sprinting efficiency. Such complementary insights across disciplines may help researchers and practitioners to understand sprinting performance and facilitate more effective training interventions.

Another interesting and practically relevant finding from both groups was the distance required to attain 95% and 100% of their respective maximal velocity (Table 1). Sprinters and soccer players required ~40 m to reach their maximum velocity, and the transition distances for all relative velocities were almost identical between groups. Given the visual (Figure 5) and analytical (Figures 6 and 7) differences in resultant GRF between 95% and 100% in both groups, especially during early‐to‐mid stance, it appears important to allow for sufficient sprinting distances when exposing athletes to maximum velocities. As such, sprinting up to 40 m may or may not allow one stance phase to be at or close to 100% of their maximum velocity. In other words, distances well above 40 m are needed to expose athletes sufficiently (i.e., enough consecutive steps) to maximal velocities and the associated mechanical demands. Previous research indicates that elite sprint athletes achieve their maximum sprinting velocities at distances between 50 and 80 m [35]. In contrast, field sport athletes reach 97%–98% of their maximum velocity by 18.3 m [36]. These discrepancies to the current findings can be attributed to the distinct characteristics of different sports populations, such as elite sprint athletes versus sub‐elite sprint athletes and American football players versus soccer players. Future research is warranted to evaluate the impact of progressive sprint training beyond 40 m on the maximal sprint velocities of team and field sports athletes. Given the observed differences in resultant GRFs when transitioning from 95% to 100% of maximum velocity, we hypothesize that longer sprint distances provide a crucial mechanical overload while also enabling athletes to learn to coordinate this movement pattern more efficiently, which is likely unattainable at submaximal velocities. Consequently, practitioners should carefully determine the appropriate distances required for maximum velocity testing and training within their specific populations.

Perspective

This retrospective explorative re‐analysis of previously captured data on acceleration and maximal sprinting performance in male soccer players and male sprinters evaluated the ground reaction force vector differences at 8.0 and 8.5 m·s−1 and 85%, 90%, 95%, and 100% of maximum velocity. The current between‐group vector field results further support Colyer et al.'s original findings that sprinters generate higher resultant ground reaction forces from the late braking phase across most of the entire propulsive phase. The vector field analysis further reveals that sprinters generate higher resultant ground reaction forces compared to soccer players from the late braking through to the propulsive phase during all relative velocities. Especially the vector analysis for each region of interest provides new insight into when faster sprinters generate higher ground reaction forces and how this may be associated with different spring‐like behavior models. This suggests that faster athletes display a greater ability to absorb and release energy during stance, which may contribute to superior sprinting performances compared to less fast athletes. Future research examining energy flow dynamics for the hip, knee, and ankle during acceleration and maximum sprinting can provide unique perspectives when testing and training individual athletes.

While our re‐analysis using vector field analysis yielded similar results to traditional scalar methods, we believe this approach offers important methodological advantages. Analyzing the ground reaction force as a vector preserves both its magnitude and direction, providing a more comprehensive understanding of force application. Even though our data interpretations remained consistent across analytical approaches, we support the additional use of vector field analysis as it enhances the robustness and accuracy of the results. This methodological improvement could positively impact other studies and research questions more broadly.
