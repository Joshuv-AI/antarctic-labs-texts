Association between microwave ablation and remission in patients with Graves’ hyperthyroidism receiving continuous antithyroid drug treatment: a single-arm prospective study
Authors: Liu Liu, Ying Chen, Yueting Zhao, Jie Li, Guofang Chen, Chao Liu, Shuhang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGraves’ disease (GD) is the most common cause of hyperthyroidism. Nearly half of patients develop persistent/recurrent GD (prGD) after antithyroid drug (ATD) therapy. Microwave ablation (MWA) is a precise minimally invasive therapeutic modality; however, evidence regarding its efficacy for prGD remains limited. This study therefore evaluated the efficacy of MWA in treating prGD and investigated factors predictive of post-MWA remission.MethodsThis single-arm prospective study enrolled 45 patients with prGD who underwent bilateral thyroid MWA under ultrasound guidance at the Affiliated Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine between January 2023 and April 2024. The primary outcome was the remission rate during follow-up, defined as maintenance of normal thyroid function for at least 6 months after ATD discontinuation. Kaplan-Meier analysis with the log-rank test was used to analyze remission, and logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were used to identify factors associated with remission.ResultsThe cohort included 34 females and 11 males, with a median follow-up of 31.00 (28.00, 33.00) months. Notable findings included a significant reduction in the median thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) receptor antibody level at the last follow-up (P<0.001). Both thyroid volume and the average methimazole (MMI) dose were significantly reduced (both P<0.001). At the last follow-up, remission was achieved in 29 patients, yielding a total remission rate of 64.44%. Patients in the remission group had smaller baseline thyroid volumes than those in the non-remission group. Preoperative thyroid volume was independently associated with remission [odds ratio (OR) =0.829; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.707–0.971; P=0.020], with an optimal cutoff of 29.35 mL [area under the curve (AUC) =74.4%; 95% CI: 0.596–0.892; sensitivity =72.4%; specificity =68.7%]. All nine patients with thyroid volumes less than 20 mL achieved remission. No severe complications were reported during the follow-up period.ConclusionsMWA was associated with decreased thyroid volume and reduced MMI dosage in patients with prGD, and clinical remission was observed in a subset of these patients. A preoperative thyroid volume of less than 29.35 mL is associated with a higher likelihood of remission.

Introduction

Graves’ disease (GD), an autoimmune disorder also known as toxic diffuse goiter, is characterized by the production of antibodies against the thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) receptor [TSH receptor antibody (TRAb)] on the surfaces of thyroid follicular cells. GD is the predominant cause of hyperthyroidism, with an annual incidence of 20-50 cases per 100,000 individuals (1). The principal treatments for GD include antithyroid drugs (ATDs), radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy, and surgery. Both RAI treatment and surgery may cause hypothyroidism by destroying thyroid tissue (2). Meanwhile, surgery may also lead to nerve damage and surgical scars (3), while RAI treatment increases the risk of Graves’ ophthalmopathy (GO) (4). Consequently, ATDs are still regarded as the first-line treatment for GD. However, studies indicate that GD still relapses in nearly half of the patients after discontinuing ATD treatment (5-7), posing a challenge in clinical management of persistent/recurrent GD (prGD).

Thermal ablation, a precision minimally invasive treatment, utilizes the biological effects of heat to induce coagulative necrosis of cells within the target tissue, minimizing damage and blocking its internal blood flow. Current thermal ablation technologies include microwave ablation (MWA), radiofrequency ablation (RFA), and high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU). Recent studies have suggested that thermal ablation may facilitate remission in some prGD patients. Lang et al. (8,9) reported that, of 75 patients undergoing bilateral HIFU treatment and then followed for at least 24 months, 42 showed remission at the last follow-up. Nevertheless, MWA and RFA are the most commonly employed. Fung et al. (10) observed a disease remission (DR) rate of 60.0% at 12 months and 56.7% at 24 months following a single RFA treatment in 30 prGD patients. However, their study is limited by a small sample size, preoperative small thyroid volumes, and low antibody levels; meanwhile, ATD is discontinued immediately post-procedure (11); in addition, the impact of TRAb titers and ablation lesion volume on treatment outcomes is not detailed. Therefore, the efficacy of thermal ablation in treating prGD requires further validation.

In the present single-center study, extensive MWA was performed on bilateral thyroid glands in 45 prGD patients. Data were collected about postoperative thyroid function, TRAb titers, and methimazole (MMI) dosage and remission rates. Our findings further validated the effectiveness and safety of thermal ablation for prGD and provided references for long-term management after thermal ablation. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0634/rc).

Methods

Study subjects

This single-arm prospective study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (No. 2023-LWKYZ-028). Informed consent was obtained from all the patients. Patients were selected from the Department of Endocrinology at the Affiliated Hospital of Integrated Traditional Chinese and Western Medicine, Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine, from January 2023 to April 2024. Ultrasound-guided extensive MWA was performed for bilateral thyroid glands in patients with prGD. Persistent hyperthyroidism despite a MMI therapy for 12–18 months, or recurrent hyperthyroidism after completion of a course of MMI. Relapse was defined as a decrease in TSH the follow-up period after medication discontinuation, with or without an increase in free triiodothyronine (FT3) and/or free thyroxine (FT4) (2,12). By April 2024, a total of 49 patients had completed MWA treatment and were then enrolled into the follow-up group; of them, 45 who had been followed up for more than 24 months were analyzed.

Inclusion criteria: (I) an age ≥18 years; (II) a diagnosis of GD; (III) a definite diagnosis of prGD; (IV) a laryngoscopic examination confirming no vocal cord abnormalities and no damage to the recurrent laryngeal nerve; (V) a follow-up period exceeding 24 months after MWA; (VI) refusal to undergo surgery or RAI for prGD; and (VII) consent to participate in this clinical study and signature on an informed consent form. Exclusion criteria: (I) presence of surgical indications such as compressive goiter or thyroid malignancy; (II) active GO [Clinical Activity Score (CAS) ≥3 points]; (III) concurrent primary diseases of the cardiovascular, liver, kidney, hematopoietic systems, malignancies, and mental illnesses; (IV) severe bleeding tendencies or coagulation disorders; (V) low patient compliance or incomplete medical records; and (VI) pregnancy or lactation (Figure 1).

MWA procedures

Ultrasound-guided MWA was performed by an experienced physician using an MWA system (KY-2000, Canyon Medical, Nanjing, China) consisting of a generator, a power distribution system, and an antenna. A cooled shaft antenna with a 1.6-mm diameter, a 10-cm length, and a 3-mm distance from the electrode to the needle tip was used to puncture into the thyroid gland. The MWA system produced 2,450 MHz and 35 W output power. Comprehensive pre-treatment assessments were conducted for all patients, including tests for thyroid function and related antibodies, thyroid ultrasonography, complete blood count, liver and kidney function, electrocardiogram, and neck computed tomography (CT) scan. Patients were prepared to receive ablation after their symptoms were controlled by MMI treatment, ensuring a heart rate of <90 beats per minute and a basal metabolic rate of <20%.

All MWA procedures were conducted by one endocrinologist who was experienced in thermal ablation, having performed >100 thyroid MWA procedures. The patient was placed in a supine position with the neck fully exposed. The surgical area was disinfected with povidone-iodine and draped appropriately. Based on the location of the lesion, local infiltration anesthesia was administered with 1% lidocaine under ultrasound guidance. Saline was continuously injected between the thyroid capsule and the trachea, esophagus, and recurrent laryngeal nerve to create a safe isolation zone protecting important neck structures. The ablation device was activated, and a safe and proximate route was selected under ultrasound guidance to avoid critical structures, like neck vessels, nerves, trachea, and esophagus. The ablation needle was inserted into the thyroid, and ablation was performed point by point, area by area. Ablation was initiated from the middle third of the thyroid, then extended to the upper and lower thirds, from outer to inner and from deep to shallow, across multiple points and planes. Color Doppler ultrasound was performed to confirm whether the blood flow signal in the ablated area had dissipated. As ultrasound contrast examination showed no blood perfusion, the ablation needle was withdrawn, thus concluding the procedure. MWA should be discontinued when the majority of the thyroid gland on both sides has been ablated, the patient is unable to tolerate the pain during the procedure, or serious adverse reactions occur, such as hoarseness or bleeding, necessitating the termination of the ablation procedure. Vital signs, including heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, and peripheral blood oxygen saturation, were monitored throughout the surgical procedure. Immediately following ablation, an ice pack was pressed onto the puncture and ablation site of the neck. Postoperative thyroid function was routinely monitored, and the MMI dosage was adjusted or discontinued based on thyroid function.

Outcomes and efficacy evaluation

Routine follow-up examinations of thyroid function, TRAb titers, and thyroid ultrasonography shall be conducted at 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months post-ablation. Positive changes in patient symptoms, thyroid function, and thyroid volume changes were documented, with MMI dosage adjusted accordingly. The incidence of adverse reactions during treatment was also recorded. The primary outcome of this study was the remission rate during the follow-up period. The secondary outcomes included changes in postoperative thyroid function and TRAb titers, MMI dosage, thyroid volume, and the incidence of postoperative adverse reactions. DR was defined as either a normal thyroid function maintained (i.e., all TSH, FT3, and FT4 normalized) or a hypothyroid state at 6 months or more after medication discontinuation. Criteria for postoperative medication discontinuation included: euthyroidism or hypothyroidism under a low-dose MMI (≤1.25 mg/d) treatment. The formula for calculating the volume of each thyroid lobe is V=πabc/6 (where a, b, c are the longest diameters perpendicular to each other on ultrasound of the thyroid, in cm). The same formula V=πabc/6 was used for ablation lesion volume. All calculated volume values are presented in mL; the formulation for calculating initial ablation ratio (IAR): IAR (%) = immediate ablation lesion volume/thyroid volume × 100%.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 27.0 software was utilized for statistical analysis. Data normally distributed were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and data not normally distributed as median [25th percentile (P25), 75th percentile (P75)]. The independent samples t-test was employed for comparing normally distributed measurement data between groups, the paired samples t-test for within-group comparisons, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for non-normally distributed measurement data between groups. Kaplan-Meier analysis and the log-rank test were used to analyze the remission rate, and logistic regression analysis to identify factors associated with cessation of medication. P<0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Baseline data

Forty-five patients with prGD were included, comprising 34 females and 11 males (average age 36.89±12.37 years, median disease duration 5 years). Three patients had a history of smoking but had quit it by the time of diagnosis. Fifteen patients experienced GO, with a median CAS of 1.00. All patients had been treated preoperatively with MMI at a median dose of 10.00 (8.75, 15.00) mg/d. All patients were followed for over 24 months, 32 until 30 months (Table 1).

Thyroid function, thyroid volume, and MMI dosage changes after MWA

At the last follow-up, no patients exhibited typical symptoms or signs of hyperthyroidism, and thyroid function had been normalized. No new development or worsening of GO was recorded during the follow-up period. Compared to those before ablation, TSH levels were significantly increased at postoperative 3, 6, 12, 18, 24 months, and MMI dosage was significantly reduced (all P<0.001). Thyroid volume was significantly reduced at postoperative 3, 6, 12, 18, 24 months (all P<0.01). At the last follow-up, the median TRAb level was significantly decreased [10.32 (4.54, 15.23) vs. 2.83 (1.43, 4.30) IU/L, P<0.001], and thyroid volume was significantly reduced [26.63 (20.52, 33.55) vs. 14.52 (9.19, 17.44) mL, P<0.001]. The median MMI dose at the last follow-up was significantly lower than that before MWA [10.00 (8.75, 15.00) vs. 0.00 (0.00, 3.13) mg/d, P<0.001] (Table 2).

Remission after MWA

The remission rates at postoperative 12 and 24 months were 43.18% and 57.14%, respectively. Ultimately, 29 patients achieved drug withdrawal at the last follow-up, resulting in a total remission rate of 64.44%. Compared to the non-drug withdrawal group, patients in the remission group had smaller preoperative thyroid volumes [32.53 (24.06, 36.00) vs. 24.16 (17.63, 31.29) mL, P=0.005], no differences in disease duration, follow-up time, thyroid function, TRAb levels, etc. (all P>0.05) (Table 3). At each follow-up time point after MWA, intergroup differences in TRAb levels between the remission and non-remission groups reached statistical significance solely at 24 months post-MWA, whereas significant differences in thyroid volume were observed only at 3, 6, and 12 months post-MWA (Figure 2).

Factors influencing DR

To identify factors influencing postoperative remission, a logistic regression analysis was carried out, involving age, baseline FT3, FT4, TSH, TRAb, MMI dosage, thyroid volume and IAR. The univariate analysis indicated that a smaller thyroid volume [odds ratio (OR) =0.882; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.800, 0.971; P=0.011] was significantly associated with a larger scale of DR. The multivariate analysis confirmed that postoperative remission was significantly related to preoperative thyroid volume (OR =0.829; 95% CI: 0.707, 0.971; P=0.020) (Table 4).

Exploratory thyroid volume cutoff associated with postoperative remission

A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to identify an exploratory thyroid volume cutoff in relation to postoperative remission. The area under the curve (AUC) for thyroid volume was 74.4% (95% CI: 0.596–0.892), with an optimal cutoff at 29.35 mL (sensitivity =72.4%; specificity =68.7%; P=0.007) (Figure 3). In the Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, postoperative remission rates over time were compared between patients with thyroid volume ≤29.35 mL (n=26) and >29.35 mL (n=19) (Figure 4). Patients with a thyroid volume ≤29.35 mL experienced a higher postoperative remission rate, compared to those with >29.35 mL (P=0.0015). The small thyroid volume group exhibited a higher remission rate (P<0.001) (Figure 5). Patients were categorized into three groups: a small-volume group with thyroid volume ≤20 mL (n=9), a medium-volume group with thyroid volume 20–30 mL (n=19), and a large-volume group with thyroid volume >30 mL (n=17). In 9 patients with thyroid volumes less than 20 mL, the remission rate following MWA was 100% (Figure S1, Table S1).

Postoperative adverse reactions

The major adverse events following MWA included neck swelling, mild pain, and hoarseness. Of them, mild pain and soreness occurred at the ablation site in 4 (8.89%) patients, hoarseness in 3 (6.67%) patients, and a foreign body sensation in the throat in 2 (4.44%) patients. These symptoms resolved within 1 day to 1 week. No severe adverse events were reported, such as thyrotoxic crisis, hoarseness, intraoperative bleeding, or damage to the esophagus or trachea (Table S2).

Discussion

prGD poses a significant challenge in GD management, as it relapses in about 50% of patients after medication cessation. Prior studies have identified thyroid goiter as a critical risk factor for the relapse of prGD after MMI treatment (5-7). Two years follow-up of 121 GD patients revealed that a thyroid volume of ≥28.6 mL was associated with a lower rate of medication cessation, representing a significant independent risk factor for GD relapse (6). Therefore, thermal ablation, which destroys thyroid tissue and blocks its inner blood flow, can be employed to shrink the thyroid goiter, thus promoting GD remission (13). In this study, MWA was performed on 45 prGD patients, resulting in a significant reduction in thyroid volume and MMI dosage within the short-term postoperative period, and a remission rate of 64.44%.

Compared to those treated with RFA in previous studies (8-10), the patients in our study had larger preoperative thyroid volumes and higher TRAb titers, indicating more typical refractory hyperthyroidism and a higher risk of recurrence and persistence. It is therefore of higher clinical value to assess whether such patients can achieve remission through thermal ablation. Unfortunately, the rate of remission exceeded 50% only in those who had a preoperative thyroid volume less than 29.35 mL, but dropped to 50.00% in those with volumes greater than 29.35 mL. In a study by Fung et al. (10), all 15 patients with a thyroid volume less than 20 mL demonstrated remission. Hence, a smaller preoperative thyroid volume is more likely to indicate remission after thermal ablation. The patients were categorized into three groups based on thyroid volume, using 20 and 30 mL as thresholds. All nine patients with thyroid volumes below 20 mL achieved remission. Then, for patients with larger volumes, it is advisable to continue ATDs postoperatively, regularly monitor thyroid function, and adjust medication dosages as needed.

Another advantage in the present study is that DR is defined only when all three indices, including TSH, FT3, and FT4, normalized after ATD discontinuation. No conditions are universally accepted to define a remission. However, a remission can be considered if normal thyroid function can be maintained for at least 1 year after medication discontinued (2,14-17). In the present study, we defined remission as at least 6 months of normal thyroid function maintenance after drug discontinuation, due to the limited follow-ups. This may explain the lower remission rate in the present study, compared to that in the study of Fung et al. (10). Therefore, remission criteria more suitable for clinical practice should be adopted to evaluate the efficacy of thermal ablation treatment on GD.

There lacks a consensus on long-term management after thermal ablation for hyperthyroidism. Fung et al. (10) suggest that ATD treatment should be immediately discontinued after thermal ablation, and thyroid function monitored until reinitiation of ATD treatment in the presence of clinical hyperthyroidism. Tang et al. (17) did not adjust the ATD dose postoperatively, but evaluated thyroid function 2 weeks after ablation and then reduced the medication dosage by about 41%. Cai et al. (18) also continued the use of ATDs post-ablation and monitored thyroid function regularly. If ATDs cannot be ceased due to a persistent disease state, a second ablation should be performed to destroy residual thyroid goiter tissue. Notably, in Cai’s cohort, 60% of patients underwent a second ablation, and 1 year later, 96% of these patients showed normal thyroid function. In the present study, however, we did not immediately stop ATD treatment post-MWA, but rechecked thyroid function 2 weeks later and adjusted the medication dosage accordingly, till the dosage was minimal yet sufficient to maintain normal thyroid function. This approach allows stable control of thyroid function and prevents thyroid-related adverse events, though they are rare and have not been reported in studies of thermal ablation treatment for GD.

ATD dosage reduction is believed as an outcome of MWA. Fung et al. (10) also defined “partial response” as a condition in which a biochemically euthyroid state can be maintained with a lower ATD dose than baseline. However, this definition has been challenged by other studies indicating that the ATD dose can be typically reduced as hyperthyroidism is relieved (19,20), potentially confusing partial response with the natural regression of GD under ATD therapy. Azizi et al. (21) included 258 patients treated with MMI for 18 months, divided them randomly into a conventional short-term group and a long-term treatment group (60–120 months). The results showed that hyperthyroidism recurred in 56% of the former vs. 17% of the latter group. Liu et al. (22) included 128 patients with recurrent GD after first-line standardized treatment. Upon reducing the MMI dose to 2.5 mg/day, the patients were split into two groups: one with this dose maintained for another 5 months before medication cessation; the other with this dose reduced to 2.5 mg every other day for 5 months before medication cessation. The latter group exhibited a significantly higher medication cessation rate (84.62% vs. 66.67%), suggesting that under a long-term ATD treatment, the dosage can be gradually reduced in a significant portion of prGD patients until cessation and remission are achieved.

In our study, 4 patients (8.89%) developed transient hypothyroidism. Following short-term levothyroxine replacement therapy, thyroid function normalized, and no cases of permanent hypothyroidism were observed. In current studies, researchers also consider hypothyroidism as a success of thermal ablation for hyperthyroidism (8-10). Our study suggests that MWA for prGD carries a favorable safety profile. However, four patients experienced recurrence more than 6 months after discontinuing medication (8.89%). These patients resumed ATD therapy, with significantly reduced drug doses compared to those administered prior to thermal ablation. Two of these patients subsequently achieved a second discontinuation of MMI. Taken together, these results highlight the rationality and necessity of maintenance of long-term low-dose ATD therapy after ablation to induce DR. It is undeniable that the continued use of ATDs after thermal ablation may confound the role of thermal ablation in the treatment of prGD. Therefore, future studies are needed to compare it with continued use of ATDs alone to clarify the role of thermal ablation in promoting prGD remission.

To assess the impact of ablation extent on the effectiveness of thermal ablation for prGD, IAR was defined. Previously used to gauge thermal ablation success in benign thyroid nodules, this indicator can quantitative the volume reduction rate of thyroid nodules (23). In the present study, contrast-enhanced ultrasound was used to measure ablation volume, and its ratio to total thyroid volume was calculated. The IAR was 58.69%, indicating that 58.69% of the thyroid goiter tissue was ablated. Tang et al. (17) performed RFA on nine patients with refractory non-nodular hyperthyroidism; at postoperative 1 week, MRI was used to evaluate ablation volumes, showing average ablation rates of 42.7% and 35.8% for the right and left sides, respectively. Although the expert consensus in China advocates for near-total ablation for GD patients (13), but clinical studies still suggest that only hemi-ablation or subtotal ablation is practical due to the requirement to protect surrounding vital organs, such as the trachea, esophagus, and recurrent laryngeal nerves. Lin et al. (24) consecutively enrolled 415 patients with GD undergoing subtotal thyroidectomy. The study demonstrated that persistent hyperthyroidism and recurrence rates significantly increased when residual thyroid gland mass exceeded 6.0 g. Theoretically, similar to that in thyroidectomy, a larger extent of thyroid goiter tissue ablation indicates a quicker recovery of thyroid function and a lower required medication dosage in GD patients. Consequently, Cai et al. (18) performed a second supplementary ablation during follow-up for GD patients who had not demonstrated remission after the initial procedure; as expected, further destroying residual thyroid goiter tissue increased the remission rate significantly. Studies on the benefits of secondary supplementary ablation are scarce, and its clinical value remains to be validated. Although the efficacy of secondary thermal ablation treatment is currently unclear, it is possible that multiple thermal ablations or supplementary thermal ablation treatment may destroy more thyroid gland tissue, and combined with ATD treatment, this may be more beneficial for patients to achieve remission. Future multicenter studies combining multiple ablation sessions with ATD therapy warrant further attention.

TRAb is a participator in the pathogenesis of GD and a significant risk factor for disease persistence or recurrence. Previous studies have shown that the TRAb level decreases after thyroidectomy, following a trend similar to that in the ATD group (25). In the present study, a continuous decline in TRAb titers was observed post-MWA, a finding supported by another study (17). The exact mechanism by which thermal ablation exploits the immune system to reduce TRAb remains unclear; however, three explanations are proposed. First, ATD treatment is not immediately ceased postoperatively, potentially aiding in the further reduction of TRAb titers. Second, thermal ablation destroys thyroid tissue and occludes nourishing blood vessels, thus diminishing antigen exposure. Third, thermal ablation may physically destroy B lymphocytes in the thyroid, which can also be depleted by rituximab (26). Furthermore, no statistically significant difference in TRAb titers was observed between the remission and non-remission groups in this study. Lang et al. (9) treated prGD with HIFU and noted differences in preoperative TRAb titers between the remission and non-remission groups during follow-up. Thus, the relationship between TRAb titer and the efficacy of thermal ablation for GD necessitates further investigations.

This study presents several advantages. For the first time, the therapeutic efficacy of MWA in promoting remission of prGD was documented, and factors influencing MWA efficacy were identified. Contrast-enhanced ultrasound was used to assess the extent of ablation and IAR, making our results more quantitative. Additionally, the study had a relatively large sample size, including patients with larger thyroid volumes and higher TRAb titers, more representative of refractory hyperthyroidism. Remission of hyperthyroidism was induced by combining MWA with continual postoperative ATD treatment, offering a new approach for managing refractory GD after thermal ablation. However, the study has limitations. First, the follow-up period was short, with a median duration of only 31 months, which is insufficient to determine the long-term remission rate after GD thermal ablation. Second, as a prospective single-center cohort study, the skill and experience of the operator could significantly influence the success rate and safety of ablation. Third, the continual postoperative use of ATD, in spite of preventing drastic changes in thyroid function and promoting remission, does not fully reflect the therapeutic efficacy of thermal ablation alone in inducing remission of prGD. Therefore, future research should involve multicenter, larger-scale prospective controlled studies to compare the effects of MWA vs. long-term ATD.

Conclusions

Ultrasound-guided MWA was associated with reduced ATD dosage, decreased TRAb titers, and notable shrinkage of thyroid volume in patients with prGD, and clinical remission was observed in a portion of these patients.
