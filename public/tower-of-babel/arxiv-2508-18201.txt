On Asymptotic Analysis of the Two-Stage Approach: Towards Data-Driven Parameter Estimation
Braghadeesh Lakshminarayanan, Cristian R. Rojas

Abstract
In this paper, we analyze the asymptotic properties of the Two-Stage (TS) estimator—a simulation-based parameter estimation method that constructs estimators offline from synthetic data. While TS offers significant computational advantages compared to standard approaches to estimation, its statistical properties have not been previously analyzed in the literature. Under simple assumptions, we establish that the TS estimator is strongly consistent and asymptotically normal, providing the first theoretical guarantees for this class of estimators.



keywords
Parameter estimation, System identification, Statistical decision theory, Large sample theory.

Introduction

Due to recent advancements in the construction of high-fidelity simulators (or
digital twins
)
[
17
]
, it is possible to estimate unknown parameters by generating synthetic input-output data
[
15
]
under different parameter configurations of such simulators. The resulting datasets, which pair simulated observations with corresponding parameter values, can be used to train a supervised learning model that maps real measurements to parameter estimates. This paved the way to
simulation-driven estimators

[
3
,
7
,
8
]
, which are constructed offline using synthetic data and later applied to real measurements with minimal computational overhead. Related works leveraging synthetic data offline in identification tasks other than parameter estimation are comprehensively covered in
[
15
,
5
]
.

A particularly simple and flexible simulation-based method is the
Two-Stage (TS) estimator

[
6
,
7
]
. The TS approach proceeds in two steps: (i) the simulated data is first compressed via a feature map that retains key information in the data, and (ii) a regression model is trained to predict the parameter values from the compressed features. Once trained, the learned estimator is applied directly to real observations to produce a parameter estimate. This framework avoids the need for an explicit likelihood or prediction error function and is especially attractive when inference speed is critical. It supports a wide range of compression functions and regression models, and has been shown to perform competitively against methods such as Prediction-Error Method (PEM), Indirect Inference (II), Particle filters (PF) and Kalman filtering (KF)
[
7
]
. Extensions include minimax variants
[
11
]
, statistical decision theoretic interpretations
[
10
]
, and applications to controller tuning
[
2
]
. Despite the computational advantages of the TS approach, its statistical properties remain mostly unexplored. In particular, no general results are available regarding its consistency or asymptotic distribution. This paper aims to fill this gap. Subject to specific choices of the first and second stages of the TS approach, our main contributions are:

•

We demonstrate that the TS estimator converges almost surely to a limit functional as the number of training parameter samples and observation length grow large, thus establishing its strong consistency.

•

As a preliminary result, of independent interest, we establish the consistency of sample quantiles as estimators of the quantiles of a distribution.

•

We establish the asymptotic normality of the TS approach, characterizing its asymptotic covariance.

2
Overview of the TS Estimator

In this section, we define the parameter estimation problem and introduce the Bayes TS estimator as a method to address it, leveraging synthetic data from a high-fidelity simulator to estimate the unknown parameters of a system.

2.1
Problem setup

Consider a high-fidelity simulator
\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
of a system, parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 1
, which can produce observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}=(y_{1}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})},\ldots,y_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
for any
N
. The ‘true’ system is described by
\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
, where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\in\Theta
is unknown. Then, the goal of parameter estimation is to determine the parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}
of the system from observed data
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)}=(y_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,y_{N}^{(0)})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. The parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
are random variables defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
. The joint distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
is
\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})=\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
, where
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is a prior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
over
\Theta
, and
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})
is the conditional distribution of
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
given
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. In the case of independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) data, we have that
y_{i}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}{f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
, with
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})=\prod_{i=1}^{N}f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigl(y_{i}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\bigr),

where
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is the probability density function (pdf) of an individual sample.

The
risk
incurred for estimating
\boldsymbol{\theta}
as
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is given by

R(\boldsymbol{\theta},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}}\bigl[L\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})\bigr)\bigr],

(1)

where the expectation is with respect to the distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
of the observation vector
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
. The
loss
function
L\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
is taken as

L\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta},\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})\bigr)=\bigl\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}-\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})\bigr\rVert_{2}^{2},

(2)

which measures the squared error between the true parameter and its estimate.

The posterior distribution of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
given the data is

\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{y})=\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y})\,\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\int_{\Theta}\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{y})\,\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime})\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}^{\prime}}.

The Bayes estimate minimizes the expected risk under the prior, and is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{Bayes}}(\boldsymbol{y})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\pi}\bigl[L(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta})\mid\boldsymbol{y}\bigr]

(3)

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\int_{\Theta}L(\boldsymbol{\theta},\boldsymbol{\eta})\,\mathbb{P}(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{y})\,d\boldsymbol{\theta}.

In this paper, we focus on the Bayes TS estimator
[
6
,
7
,
10
]
, a simulation-driven approach that learns a functional mapping from observations to parameters, approximating the Bayes estimate (
3
).

2.2
TS Estimator and its Asymptotic Properties

In this section, we present the mathematical framework of the two-stage (TS) estimator, introduced in
[
6
,
7
]
, and then formalize the asymptotic properties used in our analysis by stating the definitions of consistency and asymptotic normality.

Remark 1
.

In the sequel, observations for
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}\in\Theta
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
are denoted
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(i)}=(y_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,y_{N}^{(i)})^{\top}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}=(y_{1}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})},\ldots,y_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})^{\top}
respectively, and for the true system,
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)}=(y_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,y_{N}^{(0)})^{\top}
.

The TS estimator operates in two phases:

1.

Training Phase
: Sample parameters
\{\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\pi
. For each
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}
,
\mathcal{M}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i})
generates observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(i)}=(y_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,y_{N}^{(i)})^{\top}
, forming
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}=\{(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(i)},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{m}
. The TS estimator learns a mapping

\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{TS}}(\cdot)=\arg\min_{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot)\in\Delta}\;\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}L\bigl(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(i)}),\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}\bigr),

(4)

where
\Delta=\{\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}\}
. It has a composite structure:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{TS}}=g^{*}\circ h_{N},

(5)

with
\boldsymbol{h}_{N}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\ll N
, a compression function (e.g., sample quantiles), and
g^{*}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
solving

g^{*}=\arg\min_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}L\bigl(g\bigl(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(i)})\bigr),\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}\bigr),

(6)

where
\mathcal{G}
is a class of supervised learning models (e.g., kernel regressors).

2.

Implementation Phase
: Once the mapping
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{TS}}
has been trained, it is applied directly to new, real-world observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)}=(y_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,y_{N}^{(0)})^{\top}
to produce an estimate of the true parameter:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{TS}}\bigl(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)}\bigr).

(7)

No optimization is performed at this stage; the mapping learned offline in the training phase is used to map the compressed observations to a parameter vector.

Remark 2
.

Numerical comparisons of the TS estimator against standard parameter estimation approaches such as PEM, PF, KF and II can be found in
[
7
]
, where the advantages of the TS approach compared to these approaches are demonstrated with several numerical examples, including a practical application.

The training phase of TS typically involves large values of
m
and
N
, and in our asymptotic analysis we consider regimes in which
N
(and subsequently
m
) grows. In the limit, the estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{TS}}
converges to a limit:

\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{TS}}(\cdot)=\bar{g}\circ\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\cdot),

(8)

where

\bar{g}=\arg\min_{g\in\mathcal{G}}\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}L\bigl(g\bigl(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(i)})\bigr),\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}\bigr).

(9)

The limit
N\to\infty
being taken before
m\to\infty
reflects the assumption that the observation length grows faster than the number of parameter samples. Consistency and normality will be proved for
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{TS}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)})
as
N\to\infty
.

Asymptotic Properties
: We establish consistency and asymptotic normality, accounting for three limits.

Definition 2.1

(Consistency)
.

The TS estimator is said to be
consistent
if, when observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)}
are generated from the true system
\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
,

\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{TS}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)})\xrightarrow[\mathrm{a.s.}]{N\to\infty}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0},\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\in\Theta.

Definition 2.2

(Asymptotic Normality)
.

The TS estimator is said to be
asymptotically normal
if there exists a positive definite matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that, when observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)}
are generated from
\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
,

\sqrt{N}\left[\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\mathrm{TS}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)})-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\right]\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),\quad\text{for all }\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\in\Theta.

Remark 3
.

The need for three limits (
m\to\infty
and
N\to\infty
in training, and
N\to\infty
in implementation) to define the asymptotic properties of TS distinguish it from standard point estimation, requiring a tailored analysis.

In this paper, we seek to establish a simplified analysis of the TS estimator’s asymptotic properties, according to Definitions
2.1
and
2.2
. In the following section, we shall state the choice of first and second stages of the TS estimator for which its asymptotic consistency and normality are established under simplified assumptions.

3
Asymptotic Analysis of TS Estimator

In this section, we shall state the choice of the first and second stages of the TS estimator, followed by stating our main assumptions for its asymptotic analysis.

First stage of the TS estimator

The first stage of the TS estimator is a data compression function, denoted by
\boldsymbol{h}_{N}\colon\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n\ll N
is fixed. In this paper, we choose
\boldsymbol{h}_{N}
as the function that computes sample quantiles as in Definition
3.3
.

Definition 3.3

(Sample quantiles)
.

Let
y_{(1)}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\leq\cdots\leq y_{(N)}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
denote the order statistics of a sample
y_{1}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})},\ldots,y_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}
. Fix
n\ll N
and define levels
\gamma_{k}=\frac{k}{n+1}
for
k=1,\dots,n
. The empirical
\gamma_{k}
-quantile is
y_{(\lfloor\gamma_{k}N\rfloor)}
, and the first-stage compression function collects these quantiles into

\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(y_{1}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})},\dots,y_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})=\bigl(y_{(\lfloor\gamma_{1}N\rfloor)}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})},\dots,y_{(\lfloor\gamma_{n}N\rfloor)}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\bigr)^{\top}.

(10)

As
N\to\infty
, the sample quantiles typically converge to the
\gamma
-quantiles (proved later, See Lemma
3.5
), which are defined as follows.

Definition 3.4

(
\gamma
-quantiles)
.

For a parameter value
\theta\in\Theta
and a level
\gamma\in[0,1]
, the
\gamma
-quantile of the distribution
F_{\theta}
is defined by

q^{(\theta)}(\gamma):=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}\colon F_{\theta}(x)\geq\gamma\}.

For the vector of quantile levels
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}=(\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n})^{\top}
, define

\boldsymbol{h}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n})=\bigl(q^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}(\gamma_{1}),\dots,q^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}(\gamma_{n})\bigr)^{\top}.

(11)

The vector
\boldsymbol{h}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n})
collects the
\gamma
-quantiles corresponding to the order statistics extracted in the first stage.

Second stage of the TS estimator

The second stage of the TS estimator will be a supervised learning model that has a suitable functional representation
\boldsymbol{g}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this paper, we choose
g
to be a polynomial regressor, which is a functional of the form:

\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{z})=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{z}),

(12)

where
\boldsymbol{J}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a continuously differentiable feature map (e.g., monomials of fixed degree) and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a
p\times d
coefficient matrix. In the scalar‑parameter case
d=1
,
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
g(z)=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{J}(z)
yields a scalar output.

Thus,
\boldsymbol{g}
receives the data from the first stage, as given by (
10
), and then outputs an estimate of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
as

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{TS}}((y_{1}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})},\ldots,y_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})^{\top})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{g}(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}((y_{1}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})},\ldots,y_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})^{\top}))

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}((y_{1}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})},\ldots,y_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})^{\top})).

(13)

Therefore, combining (
10
) and (
3
), the TS estimator is a functional of the form

\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{TS}}=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{J}\circ\boldsymbol{h}_{N}).

(14)

Remark 4

(Choice of regression model)
.

Although we focus on polynomial feature maps in (
12
), our consistency and normality results extend to any regression function
\boldsymbol{g}
that is continuous and sufficiently expressive to approximate the mapping
\mathbf{z}\mapsto\boldsymbol{\theta}
uniformly over
\Theta
. For example, one may replace
\boldsymbol{J}
by a neural network of enough capacity. The key requirement is that the bias error due to the function approximation vanishes faster than the variance error as
m,N\to\infty
. This is analogous to the universal approximation assumption used in nonparametric regression and prediction-error methods.

3.1
Main Assumptions

In this subsection, we state the main assumptions considered for the asymptotic analysis of the TS estimator.

Assumption 1
.

The system
\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
and true system are autonomous,
i.e.
, do not have input signals.

Assumption 2
.

For each
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
,
\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
generates observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}=(y_{1}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})},\ldots,y_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
, where, given
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
y_{j}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}{f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
with conditional distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})=\prod_{j=1}^{N}f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(y_{j}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})
, whose pdf
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and cumulative distribution function (cdf)
F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
are continuous.

Assumption 3
.

The true system is
\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0})
, with
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{0}\in\Theta
.

Assumption 4
.

There exists a
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
such that
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*\top}\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}))=\boldsymbol{\theta}
for each
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
, where
\boldsymbol{J}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a continuously differentiable function, and
\boldsymbol{h}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(cf. Eq. (
11
)).

Assumption 5
.

\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}[\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}))\boldsymbol{J}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}))]
is invertible.

Assumption 6
.

F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\cdot)
has connected support, and
F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{-1}(\cdot)
is differentiable for each
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\Theta
.

The following remarks on these assumptions are in order.

1.

Autonomous models arise naturally when inputs are constant or unobserved— as is typical in reliability or vibration problems. Section
3.4
shows that, appealing to standard prediction–error arguments, the theory extends to systems with external inputs.

2.

The i.i.d. hypothesis is the classical baseline for large-sample analysis. The same consistency and normality conclusions typically hold for weakly dependent, stationary data by replacing independence with common mixing
[
1
]
or
m
-dependence
[
12
]
. The continuity of
f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and
F_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
ensures that the quantiles are smooth functions of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
.

3.

Assuming that
\mathcal{M}(\theta_{0})
belongs to the model class defines a clear target parameter.

4.

Assumption
4
is the TS analogue of global identifiability, as considered in PEM: it requires that
\boldsymbol{\theta}
be a linear functional of the feature vector. The condition can be relaxed if
\boldsymbol{J}
is replaced by a universal approximator with controlled error.

5.

Assumption
5
prevents degeneracy in the training regression by ensuring the feature covariance matrix is nonsingular, mirroring the requirement of local identifiability in classical estimation.

6.

Assumption
6
, on the connected support and a differentiable quantile map, is a standard regularity condition that justifies the Delta-method step; virtually all continuous distributions used in practice satisfy it.

3.2
Consistency of the TS Estimator

In this section, we derive the consistency and asymptotic normality of the TS approach. To derive the consistency, we first prove that the first stage, compression function
\boldsymbol{h}_{N}
, converges almost surely (a.s.) and consequently, also the second stage, which is a continuous map of the first stage, converges a.s., thereby establishing the consistency of TS. The derivation of asymptotic normality follows similar lines.

Note:
For ease of exposition we present the proofs for the
scalar
parameter case (
d=1
). In this setting the regression coefficient
\boldsymbol{\beta}
(and its finite-sample estimate
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
) is a vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The results easily extend to the multivariate case
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
by treating
\boldsymbol{\beta}
as a
p\times d
matrix and applying the analysis component-wise.

3.2.1
Almost sure convergence of empirical quantiles

First, we prove that the order statistics converge a.s. to
\gamma
-quantiles. The key result is the following:

Lemma 3.5
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
2
, for each
\gamma\in[0,1]
and
\theta\in\Theta
,
y_{(\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor)}^{(\theta)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\rightarrow}}q^{(\theta)}(\gamma)
as
N\rightarrow\infty
.

PROOF.

See Appendix
A.1
.
\blacksquare

Based on this lemma,
y_{(\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor)}^{(\theta)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}q^{(\theta)}(\gamma)
as
N\to\infty
for each fixed
\gamma\in[0,1]
. Then, it immediately follows that the first stage of TS converges a.s. to the true quantiles.

3.2.2
Strong consistency of the TS estimator

We study the TS estimator on a fresh test sample
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)}=(y_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,y_{N}^{(0)})^{\top}
,

y_{j}^{(0)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathbb{P}_{\theta_{0}}
, generated by the true system
\mathcal{M}(\theta_{0})
.
The finite-sample estimate is
\hat{\theta}_{0}=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{J}\bigl(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)})\bigr)
, with
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
obtained by solving

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\underset{\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}{\operatorname{arg\,min}}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\tilde{\theta}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(i)})))^{2}.

(15)

The solution to (
15
) is given by

\begin{split}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}&=\bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(i)}))\boldsymbol{J}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(i)}))\bigg]^{-1}\\
&\quad\quad\cdot\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(i)}))\tilde{\theta}_{i}.\end{split}

(16)

The limiting estimate
\bar{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{\text{TS}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)})
(cf. Eq. (
8
) and (
9
)) is given by

\bar{\theta}_{0}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{J}\!\bigl(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)})\bigr),\,\text{ where }\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}.

Our aim is to prove:

\displaystyle\text{(i) }\bar{\theta}_{0}\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\mathrm{a.s.}}\theta_{0}.

(17)

\displaystyle\text{(ii) }\sqrt{N}\bigl(\bar{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0}\bigr)\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\mathrm{TS}}).

(18)

The next lemma provides the almost-sure convergence of the first-stage (compression) and serves as the starting point of the argument.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\boldsymbol{J}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a continuous function and
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{x}_{N}
be i.i.d. random variables defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
with
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, if
\boldsymbol{x}_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}\boldsymbol{x}
, it holds that
\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{x}_{N})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{x})
.

PROOF.

See
[
16
, page 8]
.
\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\blacksquare

As the result of Lemma
3.6
,

\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\theta)}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(\theta)}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}))\text{ for each }\theta\in\Theta.

(19)

To establish the strong consistency of the TS estimator (c.f. Eq. (
17
)), we begin by showing in Theorem
3.7
together with Eq. (
19
) that, as
N\to\infty
and
m\to\infty
, we have that
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\to\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with probability
1
, where

\begin{split}&\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}=\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(\theta)}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}))\boldsymbol{J}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(\theta)}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}))]^{-1}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[\theta\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(\theta)}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}))].\end{split}

(20)

Theorem 3.7
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
2
and Lemma
3.6
,
\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
.

PROOF.

See Appendix
A.2
.
\blacksquare

Finally, applying Theorem
3.7
, the strong consistency of the TS estimator is established in Theorem
3.8
.

Theorem 3.8

(Strong consistency)
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
5
,
\bar{\theta}_{0}=\bar{\theta}_{\text{TS}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{(0)}_{N})=\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}\theta_{0}
as
N\to\infty
.

PROOF.

See Appendix
A.3
.
\blacksquare

Now, we shall establish the asymptotic normality of the TS estimator.

3.3
Asymptotic Normality of the TS estimator

Having established the consistency of the TS estimator, we now establish its asymptotic normality, in the form of Eq. (
18
). The argument proceeds in three steps:

1.

derive a central-limit theorem (CLT) for the empirical quantiles that form the first stage of TS;

2.

apply the Delta method (Lemma
3.9
) together with Lemma
3.10
to transfer the CLT to the vector
\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\theta)})
;

3.

propagate this limit through the continuous second stage to obtain the asymptotic distribution of the TS estimate.

Throughout this subsection we keep the notation introduced in Section
3.2.1
. For each
x\in\mathbb{R}
define the empirical cdf

F_{N}^{(\theta)}(x)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathds{1}\{y_{i}^{(\theta)}\leq x\}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Z_{i}^{(\theta)},

(21)

where the
Z_{i}^{(\theta)}
’s are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with mean
F_{\theta}(x)
and variance
F_{\theta}(x)(1-F_{\theta}(x))
. Hence, we can apply the central limit theorem to (
21
) to obtain

\sqrt{N}(F_{N}^{(\theta)}(x)-F_{\theta}(x))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,F_{\theta}(x)(1-F_{\theta}(x))).

(22)

Since we want to evaluate the asymptotic distribution of the sample quantiles, we need to analyze the asymptotic distribution of
F_{\theta}^{-1}(F_{N}^{(\theta)}(x))
, as seen later. Under Assumption
6
, the Delta method
[
4
,
16
]
can be applied to (
22
) to obtain the asymptotic distribution of
F^{-1}_{\theta}(F_{N}^{(\theta)}(x))
.
Once we have this asymptotic distribution, we can easily extend it to obtain the asymptotic distribution of
y_{(\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor)}^{(\theta)}
. To this end, we will use the following version of the Delta method.

Lemma 3.9

(Delta method)
.

Let
g\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a differentiable function. Let
x_{N}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
be i.i.d. random variables defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{A},\mathbb{P})
such that
N^{b}(x_{N}-a)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}x
for some constants
b>0
and
a
. Then,

N^{b}(g(x_{N})-g(x))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}g^{\prime}(a)(x).

(23)

PROOF.

See
[
16
, page 26, Theorem 3.1]
.
\blacksquare

As we can notice from Lemma
3.9
, we require to compute the derivative of
F^{-1}_{\theta}
to derive the asymptotic distribution of
F^{-1}_{\theta}(F_{N}^{(\theta)}(x))
. We can compute this derivative as follows:
Let
t=F^{-1}_{\theta}(z)
, and let
f_{\theta}
be the corresponding pdf. Then,
z=F_{\theta}(t)
implies that
dz=f_{\theta}(t)dt
, hence

\frac{dt}{dz}=\frac{1}{f_{\theta}(t)}=\frac{1}{f_{\theta}(F^{-1}_{\theta}(z))}
.
Therefore,

(F^{-1}_{\theta})^{\prime}(z)=\frac{1}{f_{\theta}(F^{-1}_{\theta}(z))}.

(24)

Using (
24
) together with
b=1/2
in Lemma
3.9
and (
22
) yields

\sqrt{N}(F^{-1}_{\theta}(F_{N}^{(\theta)}(x))-F^{-1}_{\theta}(F_{\theta}(x)))\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{F_{\theta}(x)(1-F_{\theta}(x))}{f_{\theta}^{2}(x)}\right).

(25)

In fact, the following result also holds:

Lemma 3.10
.

y_{(\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor)}^{(\theta)}-F^{-1}_{\theta}(F_{N}^{(\theta)}(x))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}0
for
\gamma=F_{\theta}(x)
.

PROOF.

The proof is similar to that of Lemma
3.5
.
\quad\blacksquare

Lemma
3.10
implies that for each
x\in\mathbb{R}
, there is a
\gamma=F_{\theta}(x)
such that
y_{(\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor)}^{(\theta)}-F^{-1}_{\theta}(F_{N}^{(\theta)}(x))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}0
. Furthermore,
y_{(\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor)}^{(\theta)}-F^{-1}_{\theta}(F_{N}^{(\theta)}(F^{-1}_{\theta}(\gamma)))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\to}}0
for each
\gamma\in[0,1]
.

With this observation and upon applying Eq. (
25
) with Slutsky’s theorem
[
4
]
, we finally obtain

\sqrt{N}(y_{(\lfloor\gamma N\rfloor)}^{(\theta)}-F^{-1}_{\theta}(\gamma))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\frac{\gamma(1-\gamma)}{(f_{\theta}(F^{-1}_{\theta}(\gamma)))^{2}}\right).

(26)

Since
y_{1}^{(\theta)},\ldots,y_{N}^{(\theta)}
are i.i.d. with distribution
F_{\theta}
, Eq. (
26
) implies that

\sqrt{N}(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\theta)})-\boldsymbol{h}^{(\theta)}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),

(27)

where
\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n}=(\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n})^{\top}
, and

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}=\text{diag}\left(\frac{\gamma_{1}(1-\gamma_{1})}{(f_{\theta}(F_{\theta}^{-1}(\gamma_{1})))^{2}},\ldots,\frac{\gamma_{n}(1-\gamma_{n})}{(f_{\theta}(F_{\theta}^{-1}(\gamma_{n})))^{2}}\right).

(28)

Therefore, the output of the compression function
\boldsymbol{h}_{N}
, the first stage of TS, is asymptotically normal. Now, it remains to show that the TS estimator is also asymptotically normal. This immediately follows from the definition of TS followed by the Delta method (see Lemma
3.9
), as established in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.11

(Asymptotic Normality)
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
6
,

\sqrt{N}(\hat{\theta}_{0}-\theta_{0})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\to}}\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{\text{TS}})
, where
\Sigma_{\text{TS}}=\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\top}\nabla\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(0)})\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\nabla\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(0)})^{\top}\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, and
\nabla\boldsymbol{J}(\boldsymbol{h}^{(0)})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the Jacobian matrix of
\boldsymbol{J}
evaluated at
\boldsymbol{h}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}_{n})
.

PROOF.

See Appendix
A.4
.

Remark 5
.

The asymptotic normality has been established in Theorem
3.11
for the case when
\theta
is a scalar. However, the analysis can be easily extended to the multivariate case by noting that
\bar{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times d}
if
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In this case,
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\text{TS}}
is a
d\times d
matrix.

3.4
Discussion on the Non i.i.d. Case

In this subsection we briefly outline how the analysis of the TS estimator can be extended when the data–generating mechanism is non i.i.d. Here, Assumptions
1
–
2
are relaxed: the model structure
\mathcal{M}(\theta)
and the true system
\mathcal{M}(\theta_{0})
have external inputs and generate stationary, dependent data. A convenient first stage is given by the least-squares coefficients of an
\operatorname{ARX}(n_{a},n_{b})
model fitted to each input–output record.

Let
n=n_{a}+n_{b}
be the total number of ARX coefficients.
For each training pair, collect the input–output record in

\boldsymbol{z}_{N}^{(i)}=(u_{1}^{(i)},y_{1}^{(i)},\ldots,u_{N}^{(i)},y_{N}^{(i)})^{\top}
and define

\begin{split}h_{N}(\boldsymbol{z}_{N}^{(i)})&=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\sum_{t=n_{a}+1}^{N}\bigl(y_{t}^{(i)}-\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)}\bigr)^{2},\\
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}&=(y_{t-1}^{(i)},\ldots,y_{t-n_{a}}^{(i)},u_{t-1}^{(i)},\ldots,u_{t-n_{b}}^{(i)})^{\top}.\end{split}

(29)

Standard prediction-error theory
[
13
]
, together with a persistently exciting input of order
n
for every
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}\in\Theta
, implies

h_{N}(\boldsymbol{z}_{N}^{(i)})\;\xrightarrow[N\to\infty]{\mathrm{a.s.}}\;(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(i)})^{\ast}.

Combining this limit with Assumption
4
and the continuity of the second-stage map
\boldsymbol{g}
, the proof of strong consistency in Section
3.2
carries over verbatim, with Lemma
3.5
replaced by the ARX limit above. Lemmas 2–3 and Theorems 2–3 in this paper are still required for the remainder of the argument.

Analogously, prediction-error results give the central limit theorem

\sqrt{N}\bigl(\boldsymbol{h}_{N}(\boldsymbol{z}^{(0)})-(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(0)})^{\ast}\bigr)\;\xrightarrow{d}\;\mathcal{N}\!\bigl(\boldsymbol{0},\,\sigma^{2}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{t}^{\top}]\bigr)^{-1}\bigr),

which, via the Delta method, yields the asymptotic normality of the TS estimator under non-i.i.d. assumptions.

Remark 6
.

Alternatively, we can replace independence with weak-dependence assumptions (mixing/
m
-dependence) so that the asymptotic theory for order statistics and our quantile-based first stage still apply
[
1
,
14
,
18
]
.

4
Numerical Validation of Asymptotic Results

In this section, we corroborate the asymptotic results of Section 3 with a simple numerical illustration. Specifically, we consider the problem of estimating the signal-to-noise ration (SNR) of a system
\mathcal{M}({\theta})
, where
\theta=\frac{\mu^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}
is the SNR, with known mean
\mu=5
and true parameter
\theta_{0}=5
, so
\sigma_{0}^{2}=\frac{\mu^{2}}{\theta_{0}}=5
. The observations
\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(0)}=(y_{1}^{(0)},\ldots,y_{N}^{(0)})^{\top}
are generated according to

y_{t}=\mu+e_{t},\quad e_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

(30)

implying that
y_{t}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle i.i.d.}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
, with a uniform prior
\pi(\theta)\sim\mathcal{U}[2,10]
. The TS estimator uses sample quantiles with
n=5
as the compression function
h_{N}
, selecting order statistics at indices
s=\lfloor j\cdot\frac{N}{n+1}\rfloor
,
j=1,\ldots,n
. Training involves
m\in\{100,1000,10000\}
parameter samples and observation lengths
N\in\{100,1000,10000\}
. The mapping
g
is learned via polynomial regression (degree
=2
). In the implementation phase, the trained TS estimator is evaluated on test sequences whose length matches the number of training samples
N
. The mean squared error (MSE), given by,
\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bigl(\hat{\theta}^{(r)}-\theta_{0}\bigr)^{2}
is computed over
R=100
Monte-Carlo runs.

The Fisher Information for
\sigma^{2}
is

I(\sigma^{2})=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{\partial\log\mathbb{P}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\boldsymbol{y}_{N}^{(\boldsymbol{\theta})})}{\partial\sigma^{2}}\right)^{2}\right]=\frac{N}{4(\sigma^{2})^{2}}.

Transforming this result to
\theta
, with
\frac{\partial\sigma^{2}}{\partial\theta}=-\frac{\mu^{2}}{\theta^{2}}
, yields the Fisher Information for
\theta
as

I(\theta)=I(\sigma^{2})\cdot\left(\frac{\partial\sigma^{2}}{\partial\theta}\right)^{-2}=\frac{N}{4(\sigma^{2})^{2}}\cdot\frac{\theta^{4}}{\mu^{4}}=\frac{N\theta^{2}}{4\mu^{4}}.

(31)

Thus, the Cramer-Rao Bound (CRB) for
\theta
is given by
\text{CRB}(\theta)=\frac{1}{F(\theta)}=\frac{4\mu^{4}}{N\theta^{2}}=\frac{4\theta\mu^{2}}{N}.

For
\theta_{0}=5
and
\mu=5
,
\text{CRB}(\theta)=\frac{4\cdot 5\cdot 5^{2}}{N}=\frac{500}{N}
. Figures
2
and
2
corroborate, via Monte Carlo simulations of this system, the consistency and asymptotic normality of the TS estimator respectively.

Consistency of TS across training sizes
m
and lengths
N
. Each marker aggregates 100 runs. MSE decreases with larger
(m,N)
and approaches the CRB (
500/N
, shown for reference), indicating near-efficiency in large samples.

Figure 2:
Asymptotic normality: empirical distribution of
\sqrt{N}\,(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{N}-\theta_{0})
for large

. The standardized errors concentrate and align with a Gaussian law, consistent with Section 3.3.

The consistency plot validates our result on the consistency of TS under i.i.d. assumptions. As
m
and
N
increase to
10^{4}
, the MSE decreases, approaching the CRB, which indicate near-efficiency and consistency as
N\to\infty
,
m\to\infty
. The normality plot confirms that for large
m
and
N
, the distribution of
\sqrt{N}(\hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{N}-\theta_{0})
is approximately Gaussian, corroborating our theoretical results from Section 3.3.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we have established that the TS estimator is
consistent
and
asymptotically normal
in the i.i.d. setting with quantile-based compression and a linear-in-features second stage, and we have derived a closed-form expression for its asymptotic covariance. We have also briefly outlined how our analysis can be extended to stationary, weakly dependent data.

For future work, we aim to provide a rigorous treatment of the non-i.i.d. case, robustness under model misspecification, non-asymptotic (finite-sample) guarantees, and principled design of the compression stage for statistical efficiency.

\theta_{0}\in[-0.9,0.9]

The TS estimator uses an ARX(
5,5
) model for compression, computing coefficients via least squares, followed by a two-layer neural network with ReLU activations. We compare TS against:

•

Extended Kalman Filter (EKF)
: The system is reformulated with an additional state
x^{(3)}_{k}=\theta
:

\displaystyle x^{(1)}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}x^{(1)}_{k}+u_{k}+v^{(11)}_{k},

(36)

\displaystyle x^{(2)}_{k+1}

\displaystyle=(1-(x_{k}^{(3)})^{2})\sin(50(x_{k}^{(3)})^{2})x^{(2)}_{k}-

\displaystyle x_{k}^{(3)}x^{(2)}_{k}+\frac{x_{k}^{(3)}}{1+(x_{k}^{(3)})^{2}}u_{k}+v^{(12)}_{k},

\displaystyle x_{k+1}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=x_{k}^{(3)}+w_{k},

\displaystyle y_{k}

\displaystyle=x^{(2)}_{k}+v^{(2)}_{k},

where
w_{k}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,10^{-6})

P(0)
