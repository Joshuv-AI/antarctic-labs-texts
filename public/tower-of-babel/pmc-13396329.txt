P53 loss and BRAF V600E mutation suggest potentially divergent pathways to aggressiveness in high grade differentiated thyroid carcinoma
Authors: Jiaojiao Cui, Cui Li, Xiaofeng Liu, Jingwen Duan
Published: 2026

Introduction

In the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Endocrine and Neuroendocrine Tumours, thyroid malignancies of follicular cell origin underwent significant nosological revision. Specifically, high-grade differentiated thyroid carcinoma (HGDTC) was formally introduced as a distinct diagnostic entity—separate from both conventional differentiated thyroid carcinomas (DTCs) and poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma (PDTC) [1]. Although HGDTC and PDTC share overlapping clinical behaviors and intermediate prognoses—situated between classic DTCs and anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC)—they are fundamentally distinguished by divergent histopathological criteria [2, 3]. Per the 2022 WHO definition, HGDTC is characterized by a malignant neoplasm arising from thyroid follicular epithelial cells that retains definitive architectural and/or cytological features of differentiated carcinoma (i.e., papillary, follicular, or oncocytic patterns), yet concurrently exhibits unequivocal high-grade morphological features: (i) ≥ 5 mitotic figures per 10 high-power fields (HPFs) (equivalent to a standardized area of 2 mm²), and/or (ii) the presence of tumor necrosis [4, 5]. Notably, aggressive variants of papillary thyroid carcinoma—including the tall cell, columnar cell, and shoelace–nail subtypes—should be classified as HGDTC when they fulfill either of these high-grade criteria [6, 7].

As an emerging diagnostic category, HGDTC remains undercharacterized due to its rarity and recent formal recognition. Consequently, consensus guidelines for its clinical–pathological evaluation, molecular profiling, and evidence-based management are still evolving [8, 9]. In routine practice, diagnostic challenges persist: morphological overlap with conventional DTCs and PDTC frequently leads to underdiagnosis or misclassification, thereby compromising risk stratification and therapeutic planning [10, 11]. Current molecular evidence indicates that BRAF V600E is the predominant driver mutation in HGDTC; however, cases exhibiting concurrent p53 aberrations—either complete loss or strong nuclear overexpression—are exceedingly rare in the literature [3, 12]. Critically, the interrelationships among BRAF status, p53 immunophenotype, distinctive histomorphology (e.g., shoelace–nail architecture), and clinical aggressiveness remain poorly defined.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study reports two rigorously confirmed cases of HGDTC harboring both BRAF V600E mutations and aberrant p53 immunoreactivity. We present a comprehensive analysis of their clinicopathological parameters, immunohistochemical profiles (including TTF-1, Pax-8, CK19, galectin-3, cyclin D1, E-cadherin, β-catenin, and Ki-67), and molecular findings. Integrated with a systematic review of contemporary literature, this report aims to clarify the histomolecular correlates of HGDTC, refine diagnostic criteria, and inform prognostic assessment and individualized clinical decision-making.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 75-year-old woman was admitted to the hospital on September 5, 2025, for evaluation of a one-month history of intermittent cough. Physical examination revealed marked right cervical swelling, tracheal deviation to the left, and a firm, non-tender, mobile thyroid mass measuring approximately 5.0 × 4.0 cm in the right lobe—exhibiting normal excursion with swallowing.

Neck color Doppler ultrasound demonstrated diffuse parenchymal echotexture changes in the thyroid gland, a dominant right lobe nodule with partial coarse calcification, and two additional hypoechoic nodules located in the mid- and lower poles of the right lobe, measuring 3.6 × 3.5 × 3.0 cm and 1.1 × 1.0 × 0.8 cm, respectively. According to the Chinese Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System (C-TIRADS), the dominant nodule was classified as category 4b, indicating a moderate to high suspicion of malignancy and warranting histopathological confirmation. Preoperative fine-needle aspiration biopsy was not performed. Intraoperative frozen section examination of the tumor was reported as ‘follicular neoplasm, malignancy cannot be excluded.’ Based on this indeterminate diagnosis, combined with the patient’s advanced age (75 years), the patient subsequently underwent right hemithyroidectomy with isthmusectomy and central compartment lymph node dissection. Intraoperatively, a firm, adherent tumor was identified in the right thyroid lobe, with dense fibrous adhesion to the overlying strap muscles.

Gross pathological examination revealed an irregular, grayish-red specimen comprising the resected right lobe and isthmus, measuring 6.0 × 5.7 × 4.0 cm. On sectioning, a well-circumscribed, encapsulated, grayish-white nodule measuring 4.2 × 3.8 cm was identified; it exhibited a slightly hard consistency and central calcification (Fig. 1). Four lymph nodes were retrieved from the central compartment, ranging in diameter from 0.1 to 0.5 cm. Histologically, the tumor displayed a mixed growth pattern—predominantly follicular and solid sheet-like architecture (solid component comprising ~ 30% of the tumor volume) (Fig. 2A)—without nuclear features diagnostic of papillary thyroid carcinoma. Capsular invasion (Fig. 2B) and vascular invasion (Fig. 3A) were confirmed; focal tumor necrosis was present within the solid areas (Fig. 2C). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed diffuse, strong positivity for TTF-1, TPO, and CD56 in both solid and non-solid components; p53 expression was completely absent (Fig. 3B, C); Ki-67 labeling index was 10% in hotspot areas; galectin-3 and CK19 were negative; and MC (metallothionein) showed diffuse positivity in solid regions and patchy positivity in non-solid regions. The final pathological diagnosis was high-grade differentiated thyroid carcinoma, with focal necrosis and calcification. The tumor measured 4.2 × 3.8 cm, exhibited a mitotic count of approximately 3 mitoses per 2 mm², and demonstrated multifocal capsular and vascular invasion, including focal extracapsular extension into adjacent soft tissue. No evidence of perineural invasion was identified.

Case 2

An 88-year-old woman was admitted to the hospital on November 18, 2025, for evaluation of a progressively enlarging anterior cervical mass present for over 10 months and acute spontaneous rupture with hemorrhage occurring twice within the same day. The patient reported initial detection of a quail-egg–sized asymptomatic nodule, which was not medically evaluated. Over time, the lesion enlarged to approximately egg size and developed a bluish-purple discoloration. At approximately 03:00, the mass ruptured spontaneously, resulting in superficial bleeding that was temporarily controlled by manual compression. A second episode of rupture and active bleeding occurred at approximately 16:00; the patient was subsequently transferred to our institution for urgent surgical assessment and management. Physical examination revealed a well-demarcated, round, bluish-purple cervical swelling measuring approximately 10 cm in maximum diameter. Multiple punctate hemorrhagic foci were observed over the inferior aspect of the lesion, with persistent oozing from a small area; the site was covered with sterile gauze pending definitive intervention (Fig. 4A).

Clinical features. (A) Circular swelling in the neck, bluish-purple in color, with a small amount of active bleeding. (B) During the operation, it was observed that the tumor base penetrated the strap muscles and platysma muscle, showing an invasive growth pattern. (C) Tumor bleeding, involving the subcutaneous tissue

Neck color Doppler ultrasound demonstrated a solid, hypoechoic nodule in the right thyroid lobe—classified as C-TIRADS 4c—and a separate solid nodule in the right level II cervical lymph node region, suggestive of metastatic involvement. Given the clinical suspicion of aggressive malignancy and the absence of distant metastases on preoperative staging, a diagnostic partial thyroidectomy was performed. Intraoperatively, the tumor exhibited extensive local invasion: it infiltrated through the platysma and sternocleidomastoid muscles at its base, encased the trachea from the cricoid cartilage caudally, and diffusely invaded the anterior and lateral tracheal walls (Fig. 4B). Intraoperative frozen section examination confirmed the diagnosis of malignancy. Due to the patient’s advanced age (88 years), significant comorbidity burden, and the high procedural risk—including anticipated need for permanent tracheostomy to achieve R0 resection—the family was comprehensively counseled regarding goals of care. After shared decision-making, palliative debulking was performed: the exophytic component adherent to the tracheal surface was excised, while gross residual disease within the tracheal wall and deep soft tissues was intentionally left in situ.

Gross pathological examination of the resected specimen—which included portions of the right thyroid lobe and overlying skin and subcutaneous tissue (Fig. 4C)—measured 7.5 × 6.0 × 3.0 cm. The thyroid parenchyma appeared reddish-brown; sectioning revealed a discrete, grayish-white, granular-surfaced nodule measuring 2.7 × 2.7 × 2.0 cm with slightly firm consistency. The associated skin specimen measured 6.7 × 4.0 × 3.0 cm and exhibited a unilocular cystic architecture; within the cyst cavity, a circumscribed, rough, grayish-white area measuring 2.7 × 2.5 cm was identified. On cut surface, this area displayed fine granularity and firm texture. Histopathological analysis demonstrated a biphasic papillary architecture: approximately 50% of the tumor exhibited classic papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) morphology, while the remaining 50% showed hobnail variant features—including apical nuclear positioning, moderate nuclear atypia, micro-papillary formations, and focal tumor necrosis (Fig. 5A and C). No residual benign thyroid follicles or normal architectural elements were identified within the tumor bed. Immunohistochemistry revealed diffuse, strong positivity for TTF-1, Pax-8, CK19, galectin-3, cyclin D1, E-cadherin, and β-catenin in both histologic components (Fig. 6A and B). p53 expression was elevated relative to wild-type but did not reach the mutant threshold, and the Ki-67 proliferation index was 20% in hobnail-dominant areas (Fig. 6C) and 10% in classic PTC–dominant regions (Fig. 6D). Targeted molecular testing confirmed the presence of a BRAF V600E mutation. The final pathological diagnosis was high-grade differentiated thyroid carcinoma, hobnail variant (50%) with classic PTC component (50%), characterized by tumor necrosis, a mitotic count of 5 per 2 mm², a maximal dimension of 2.7 × 2.5 cm, and direct invasion into the dermis and subcutaneous adipose tissue.

The patient from Case 1 underwent postoperative radioactive iodine ablation and remained free of locoregional recurrence or distant metastasis at 5-month follow-up. In contrast, the patient from Case 2 declined adjuvant therapy following surgery and opted for best supportive care with close clinical surveillance. She survived with progressive unresectable disease for 3 months postoperatively. Comparative clinicopathologic characteristics of both cases are summarized in Table 1. Discussion

High-grade differentiated thyroid carcinoma (HGDTC), formally introduced as a distinct diagnostic entity in the 2022 WHO Classification of Thyroid Tumours, has rapidly emerged as a focal point in thyroid pathology research—particularly concerning its diagnostic criteria, differential diagnosis, molecular underpinnings, and clinical behavior. The two cases presented herein—one characterized by aberrant p53 immunoreactivity and the other by a BRAF V600E mutation—exhibit divergent histomorphologic patterns, immunophenotypic profiles, and proliferative indices. Together, they exemplify the intrinsic heterogeneity of HGDTC and provide clinically annotated evidence for integrative molecular–immunohistochemical–morphologic correlations. This discussion synthesizes current understanding across four domains: (i) diagnostic and differential diagnostic challenges; (ii) molecular–immunohistochemical–morphologic relationships; (iii) therapeutic implications and prognostic considerations; and (iv) methodological limitations, contextualized against contemporary literature.

The core diagnostic principle for HGDTC is defined as “retention of unequivocal differentiated thyroid morphology alongside ≥ 1 high-grade histologic feature” [2]. Although Case 1 did not meet the WHO-specified mitotic threshold (≥ 5 mitoses per 2 mm²), it demonstrated unequivocal tumor necrosis and preserved follicular architecture with capsular and vascular invasion—fulfilling the criterion of “differentiated features + high-grade manifestation.” Case 2 exhibited both tumor necrosis and a mitotic count of ≥ 5 per 2 mm², while retaining classic and hobnail-pattern papillary carcinoma morphology—including nuclear apical positioning and micro-papillary structures—thereby satisfying diagnostic requirements. Notably, Case 1 underscores the diagnostic ambiguity between HGDTC and poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma (PDTC). Per the 2022 WHO classification, HGDTC retains recognizable high-grade morphology but may coexist with low-grade components without quantitative stipulation [3]; conversely, the Turin consensus defines PDTC as a follicular epithelial-derived neoplasm requiring: (i) solid, insular, or trabecular growth pattern; (ii) absence of definitive papillary nuclear features; and (iii) ≥ 3 mitoses per 2 mm² and/or tumor necrosis and/or nuclear pleomorphism with folding. Given the lack of standardized quantification for low-grade areas in HGDTC, diagnostic overlap persists. While some studies propose thresholds of ≥ 30% or ≥ 75% low-grade component to classify tumors as low-grade differentiated carcinoma [3, 13]. In Case 1 of this study, the poorly differentiated component constituted 30% of the tumor.

Differential diagnosis of HGDTC encompasses three principal entities: (1) Low-grade differentiated thyroid carcinoma (e.g., conventional PTC or FTC): Typically exhibits predominantly follicular or papillary architecture, lacks tumor necrosis, and demonstrates ≤ 5 mitoses per 2 mm². Ki-67 labeling index generally remains < 10%. (2) Poorly differentiated thyroid carcinoma (PDTC): Defined by solid/insular/trabecular growth, absence of papillary nuclear features, and fulfillment of ≥ 1 high-grade criterion (mitoses ≥ 3/2 mm², necrosis, or nuclear pleomorphism). Ki-67 is commonly elevated (10–30%) [14]. (3) Anaplastic thyroid carcinoma (ATC): Characterized by marked cytologic atypia, highly irregular nuclei, sarcomatoid/giant cell/epithelioid differentiation, and frequent loss of thyroid lineage markers (TTF-1, thyroglobulin). Ki-67 typically exceeds 50% [10].

Emerging evidence indicates that HGDTC arises through acquisition of secondary genetic or epigenetic alterations (“second hits”) superimposed upon foundational driver mutations of well-differentiated thyroid carcinomas [15]. Our two cases illustrate distinct molecular trajectories underlying high-grade transformation: (1) p53-aberrant pattern: Case 1 represents high-grade progression of follicular carcinoma driven by complete loss of p53 protein expression—a well-established surrogate for TP53 gene inactivation. p53 dysfunction is strongly associated with dedifferentiation, increased invasiveness, and adverse prognosis in thyroid malignancies. Its absence in this case, coupled with necrosis and vascular invasion, supports p53 immunophenotype as a robust biomarker for high-grade behavior in follicular-derived tumors. This aligns with prior reports identifying p53 aberration as a late-event molecular alteration in HGDTC evolution [10, 16]. (2) BRAF V600E–driven phenotype: Case 2 exemplifies high-grade transformation within a BRAF V600E–mutant papillary carcinoma. BRAF V600E is the predominant oncogenic driver in PTC and is enriched in aggressive variants—including tall cell, cribriform, and hobnail subtypes [17, 18]. Here, the hobnail morphology (present in ~ 50% of the tumor) was accompanied by apical nuclear positioning, focal necrosis, elevated Ki-67 (20% in hobnail areas), and strong p53 overexpression—suggesting that hobnail differentiation reflects a morphologic correlate of BRAF-mediated phenotypic destabilization. Supporting this, the cribriform variant of PTC is independently associated with extrathyroidal extension, lymph node metastasis, and higher risk of high-grade progression when combined with necrosis or elevated proliferation [19–21], consistent with the locally invasive behavior observed in Case 2.

Given its recent formal recognition, no consensus treatment guidelines exist for HGDTC. Current management is largely extrapolated from protocols for PDTC [22, 23], emphasizing surgical resection as the cornerstone, supplemented by radioactive iodine (RAI), external beam radiotherapy (EBRT), or systemic therapy [24]. Based on the molecular–immunophenotypic stratification illustrated in these cases—and integrated with contemporary evidence—we propose a biologically informed, individualized therapeutic framework:

*BRAF V600E–mutant HGDTC*: In younger patients with localized disease and no distant metastases, total thyroidectomy with comprehensive central/lateral neck dissection is recommended, followed by RAI ablation and adjuvant BRAF/MEK inhibition (e.g., dabrafenib + trametinib). In elderly or frail patients with extensive local invasion (as in Case 2), palliative debulking may be prioritized to alleviate symptoms, with close surveillance and supportive care.

*BRAF V600E–mutant HGDTC*: In younger patients with localized disease and no distant metastases, total thyroidectomy with comprehensive central/lateral neck dissection is recommended, followed by RAI ablation and adjuvant BRAF/MEK inhibition (e.g., dabrafenib + trametinib). In elderly or frail patients with extensive local invasion (as in Case 2), palliative debulking may be prioritized to alleviate symptoms, with close surveillance and supportive care.

*p53-aberrant HGDTC*: As p53 dysfunction confers resistance to conventional chemotherapy [10], cytotoxic regimens are not routinely indicated. For resectable disease, wide surgical excision followed by EBRT is advised. In unresectable cases, best supportive care with serial imaging and biochemical monitoring is appropriate.

*p53-aberrant HGDTC*: As p53 dysfunction confers resistance to conventional chemotherapy [10], cytotoxic regimens are not routinely indicated. For resectable disease, wide surgical excision followed by EBRT is advised. In unresectable cases, best supportive care with serial imaging and biochemical monitoring is appropriate.

Follow-up must reflect the aggressive natural history of HGDTC. We recommend intensified surveillance: thyroid ultrasound, serum thyroglobulin (Tg), anti-Tg antibodies, and TSH every 3 months during the first 2 postoperative years; every 6 months in years 3–5; and annually thereafter. Any evidence of structural recurrence or metastatic progression warrants prompt reassessment and therapeutic adaptation to optimize outcomes.

Cyclin D1, E-cadherin, and β-catenin were assessed to document their immunohistochemical expression patterns in HGDTC. Similar immunohistochemical panels have been used in prior studies of thyroid tumors with aggressive features, including the hobnail variant of papillary thyroid carcinoma, to evaluate cellular polarity, cohesion, and potential EMT-related alterations [25–27].

First, the follow-up duration is limited: 5 months for Case 1 and 3 months for Case 2. This is insufficient to draw definitive conclusions about long-term prognosis, disease behavior, or treatment response. Accordingly, we present these observations as hypothesis-generating, and they require confirmation in larger prospective studies with longer follow-up.This study has several limitations: (1) Its single-center, retrospective design and small sample size (n = 2) preclude robust statistical inference regarding subtype-specific prognosis or survival correlations; (2) TP53 gene sequencing was not performed—only p53 protein expression was assessed immunohistochemically—thus limiting conclusions about underlying mutational mechanisms; (3) Broader molecular profiling (e.g., NRAS, HRAS, PIK3CA, TERT promoter) was not undertaken, potentially overlooking cooperative genomic events influencing phenotype and behavior. Future investigations should prioritize multicenter, prospective cohorts with longitudinal follow-up, comprehensive genomic characterization, and standardized histopathologic review to refine diagnostic criteria, validate prognostic biomarkers, and establish evidence-based management algorithms for HGDTC.

Conclusion

Given the small sample size, these observations are hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger prospective cohorts.This report describes two molecularly and phenotypically distinct cases of high-grade differentiated thyroid carcinoma—defined by p53 protein loss and BRAF V600E mutation, respectively—highlighting substantial intertumoral heterogeneity and reinforcing the critical interplay among driver mutations, immunophenotype, histomorphology, and proliferative activity. Aberrant p53 expression appears to serve as a key immunohistochemical marker of high-grade transformation in follicular-derived carcinomas, whereas hobnail morphology and regional Ki-67 elevation in BRAF-mutant papillary carcinomas signify acquisition of aggressive biological behavior. Continued multidisciplinary investigation is essential to consolidate diagnostic precision, elucidate pathogenic hierarchies, and advance personalized therapeutic strategies for this newly recognized, clinically consequential entity.
