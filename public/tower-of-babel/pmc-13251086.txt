Associations of long-term average and variability of estimated glucose disposal rate with frailty progression: evidence from two prospective cohorts
Authors: Yihong Ding, Zhaoping Wang, Ting Shen, Gulisiya Hailili, Minqing Yan, Xiaoran Liu, Di He, Changzheng Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR), when measured at a single time point, has been linked to frailty. This study aimed to investigate the associations between long-term eGDR patterns and frailty progression.MethodsWe conducted prospective analyses among participants aged ≥ 45 years from two cohorts: the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA). Two metrics were derived to characterize long-term eGDR patterns over an 8-year exposure period: average eGDR and eGDR variability (assessed by variation independent of the mean [VIM]). The frailty index (FI, 0–100 points) was calculated every two years during the follow-up period, with higher scores indicating more severe frailty. Linear mixed-effects models were used to analyze the associations of long-term eGDR patterns with frailty progression.ResultsThis study included 6,829 participants from HRS (mean age: 67.7 years, 60.0% females) and 2,152 participants from ELSA (mean age: 69.7 years, 56.5% females). Compared with individuals in the lowest tertile (T1) of average eGDR, those in the highest tertile (T3) showed a significantly slower increase in FI over time (pooled β: −0.347, 95% CI: −0.536 to −0.157; I2 = 58.6%). For each 1-standard deviation (SD) increase in average eGDR, the pooled β value was −0.139 (95% CI: −0.203 to −0.075; I2 = 40.2%). Conversely, independent of the average eGDR level, higher eGDR variability (T3 vs. T1 of VIM) was associated with accelerated FI progression (pooled β: 0.226, 95% CI: 0.107 to 0.346; I2 = 0.0%). Each 1-SD increase in eGDR variability was associated with an additional annual FI increase of 0.076 (pooled β: 0.076, 95% CI: 0.027 to 0.124; I2 = 0.0%).ConclusionsBoth long-term average and variability of eGDR were associated with frailty progression, highlighting the importance of monitoring eGDR dynamics and implementing interventions to stabilize eGDR in aging populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08223-w.Keywords: Insulin resistance, Estimated glucose disposal rate, Cumulative exposure, Variability, Frailty

Introduction

With the global population aging rapidly, frailty has emerged as a significant public health issue. This geriatric syndrome, marked by a decline in physiological reserves across multiple organ systems, is increasingly recognized for its widespread prevalence among middle-aged and older adults [1–3]. Although definitions of frailty may differ, a substantial body of research consistently links this condition to heightened risks of negative health outcomes, such as disability, cognitive decline, and early mortality [4–9]. Notably, recent studies highlight the dynamic nature of frailty, demonstrating that it can both worsen and improve over time [10–14]. These findings underscore the importance of identifying modifiable risk factors and early indicators to inform effective prevention and intervention strategies.

Among the various contributors to frailty, insulin resistance (IR) has been identified as a key factor influencing both its onset and progression [15–18]. While the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp remains the gold standard for assessing IR, its invasive procedure and high cost restrict its application in large epidemiological studies and routine clinical settings [19]. In response to these limitations, the estimated glucose disposal rate (eGDR) has gained attention as a practical and reliable surrogate for IR. Derived from commonly measured clinical variables, including waist circumference (WC), hypertension status, and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), eGDR offers a cost-effective alternative for evaluating insulin sensitivity in broader populations [20–23].

Recent prospective studies have shown that higher eGDR levels are significantly associated with slower frailty progression [24–27]. However, these studies have focused solely on static, single time-point measurements of eGDR, without considering cumulative exposure or temporal fluctuations over time. As a dynamic biological parameter, eGDR is influenced by modifiable factors such as physical activity, dietary habits, and medication use [28–30]. As a result, a single measurement may capture only temporary fluctuations, potentially underestimating or overestimating the long-term effects and cumulative metabolic risk from midlife onward. Therefore, it is essential to assess long-term patterns of eGDR using high-quality population cohorts and to examine their impacts on frailty progression.

To address these gaps, we analyzed data from two prospective cohorts to investigate the associations of long-term average eGDR and its variability with frailty progression among middle-aged and older adults.

Methods

Study participants

Data were obtained from the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) and the English Longitudinal Study of Ageing (ELSA), two prospective, nationally representative cohorts of community-dwelling adults in the US and UK, respectively. The objectives, design, and methods of these cohorts have been described in detail elsewhere [31, 32]. In HRS, half of the randomly selected sample received blood tests in Wave 8 (2006), Wave 10 (2010), and Wave 12 (2014), while the other half were assessed in Wave 9 (2008), Wave 11 (2012), and Wave 13 (2016). In ELSA, blood samples were collected during nurse visits starting in Wave 2 (2004) and repeated every 4 years. We calculated the average and variability of eGDR values over an 8-year exposure period in HRS (2006–2014 or 2008–2016) and ELSA (2004–2012). Frailty was evaluated during the follow-up periods from 2014/2016 to 2020 in HRS and from 2012 to 2018 in ELSA. The study timelines and follow-up periods for each cohort are illustrated in Fig. 1. The HRS was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Michigan and the National Institute on Aging (HUM00061128). The ELSA was approved by the London Multicenter Research Ethics Committee (MREC/01/2/91). Informed consent was obtained from all participants in both cohorts.

We included participants aged ≥ 45 years who had 3 valid eGDR measurements during the exposure period. Participants without available frailty index (FI) data at baseline or without any subsequent FI assessments during follow-up were excluded. Finally, 8,981 eligible participants (6,829 from HRS and 2,152 from ELSA) were included in the analysis (Fig. 2).

Evaluation of longitudinal eGDR patterns

The eGDR was calculated using the following formula: eGDR (mg/kg/min) = 21.158 – 0.09 × WC (cm) − 3.407 × hypertension status (coded as 1 for yes and 0 for no) − 0.551 × HbA1c (%) [24, 25]. Details of these measurements are provided in Supplementary Appendix. Longitudinal eGDR patterns were evaluated using both the average and variability of eGDR over time. The average eGDR was determined by calculating the mean of eGDR values from 3 health examinations. As recommended in previous studies [33–35], eGDR variability was assessed using the variation independent of the mean (VIM), calculated as 100 × (standard deviation [SD] / meanβ), where β is the regression coefficient of ln (SD) on ln (mean). Participants were stratified into tertiles based on these continuous values.

Assessment of frailty

A 31-item FI was developed using the deficit accumulation approach to assess frailty, as described in previous studies [13, 36, 37]. This index captured a broad range of health deficits from the HRS and ELSA datasets, including comorbidities (excluding hypertension), physical functioning, disability, depression, and cognitive status (Table S1). FI scores were computed biennially during follow-up and standardized to a 0–100 scale, with higher values reflecting greater frailty severity. If up to 10% of FI items were missing, the denominator was adjusted accordingly in the calculation. Further details on FI construction are provided in Supplementary Appendix.

Covariates

To account for potential confounders, we adjusted for a range of covariates [24, 25, 27]. Sociodemographic variables included age, sex (male or female), race and ethnicity (White or non-White), education level (below college or college and above), and household income (< $25,000, $25,000–49,999, or ≥ $50,000). Lifestyle characteristics comprised smoking status (never, former, or current), drinking status (never or ever), and the frequency of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (< 1 time/week, 1–2 times/week, or ≥ 3 times/week [38]). Health-related measures included total cholesterol (TC) and C-reactive protein (CRP), selected based on established protocols [25, 27]. Missing values among covariates were addressed using multiple imputation via chained equations [39]. The proportion of missing data for each covariate is detailed in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as means and SDs, and categorical variables as numbers with percentages. Using linear regression models, we analyzed the associations of longitudinal eGDR patterns with baseline frailty. The regression coefficients (β values) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated with sequential adjustment for the above-mentioned covariates. In addition, these models were mutually adjusted for eGDR variability and average eGDR in the respective analyses. Linear mixed-effects models were applied to assess the relationships between longitudinal eGDR patterns and FI progression, adjusting for the same covariates. All repeated FI measurements (including baseline) served as outcome variables, with time defined as the interval from baseline to each FI assessment wave. Fixed effects included eGDR patterns, time, their interaction, and covariates. The coefficients for eGDR patterns represented average baseline differences (for categorical variables) or per 1-SD increase (for continuous variables). Time coefficients reflected the overall annual change in FI, while interaction terms indicated differences in FI change rates associated with eGDR patterns. Random intercepts and slopes were included to account for individual variability at baseline and over time, with Cholesky decomposition used for the covariance structure. Cohort-specific estimates were then pooled via random-effects meta-analysis [40, 41]. Heterogeneity between studies was assessed using the I2 statistic and the between-study variance (τ2). Notably, these estimates should be interpreted with caution given the limited number of studies included.

Stratified analyses were conducted by age (< 70 years or ≥ 70 years), sex (male or female), education level (below college or college and above), body mass index (BMI) categories (< 30.0 or ≥ 30.0 kg/m2), and type 2 diabetes (T2D) status (yes or no). The statistical significance of interaction terms was evaluated using the likelihood ratio test. Several sensitivity analyses were performed to examine the robustness of our main results. First, we further excluded participants who were identified as frail (FI ≥ 25 [13, 36]) at baseline. Second, to eliminate potential diagnostic overlap between eGDR criteria and diabetes status, we reconstructed FI by removing the diabetes item from the original 31-item scale, creating a modified 30-item FI. Third, we additionally included the quadratic term of age in the primary model to account for the non-linear association between age and frailty progression. Fourth, we additionally adjusted the models for the use of antihypertensive, antidiabetic, and lipid-lowering drugs (not available in HRS). Fifth, we further adjusted for TC and CRP variability during the exposure period. Sixth, we restricted our analyses to participants without any missing data on FI items. Finally, to explore the influence of multiple imputation on covariates, we excluded participants with missing data on covariates and repeated the main analyses.

Statistical analyses were performed using R 4.3.0. We reported two-sided p-values throughout and p < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 6,829 participants from HRS (mean [SD] age, 67.7 [9.9] years; 4,097 females [60.0%]) and 2,152 participants from ELSA (mean [SD] age, 69.7 [8.0] years; 1,216 females [56.5%]) were included at baseline. Overall, participants in the highest tertile (T3) of average eGDR were younger, more likely to be female, had a tertiary education, and reported higher household income and physical activity levels, compared with those in the lowest tertile (T1). They also showed lower proportions of former smokers and never drinkers, and higher TC but lower CRP levels (Table 1). Associations of long-term eGDR patterns with baseline frailty

The association of average eGDR with baseline FI is summarized in Table S4. Individuals in the highest tertile (T3) of average eGDR exhibited the lowest baseline FI (pooled β: −5.045, 95% CI: −8.402 to −1.688; I2 = 96.3%) compared with those in the lowest tertile (T1). Each 1-SD increase in average eGDR was associated with a 2.424-point reduction in baseline FI (pooled β: −2.424, 95% CI: −3.707 to −1.142; I2 = 95.7%).

After adjusting for covariates, higher eGDR variability was associated with higher baseline FI (Table S5), with a pooled β value of 1.285 (95% CI: 0.675 to 1.895; I2 = 8.6%) for those in T3 of variability compared with those in T1. Each 1-SD increment in eGDR variability corresponded to a pooled β value of 0.324 (95% CI: 0.088 to 0.560; I2 = 0.0%).

Associations of long-term eGDR patterns with frailty progression

As shown in Table 2, individuals in the highest tertile (T3) of average eGDR exhibited significantly slower progression of FI compared with those in the lowest tertile (T1), with a pooled β value of −0.347 (95% CI: −0.536 to −0.157; I2 = 58.6%). The pooled β value for each 1-SD increase in average eGDR was −0.139 (95% CI: −0.203 to −0.075; I2 = 40.2%). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

The associations of average eGDR and eGDR variability with FI progression did not substantially differ across major subgroups of participants stratified by age, sex, education level, and T2D status (all P-difference > 0.05; Tables S6 & S7). However, we observed a significant interaction by BMI for average eGDR (P-difference < 0.001; Table S6). Specifically, although the inverse association was statistically significant in both groups, it was more pronounced among non-obese participants (BMI < 30.0 kg/m2) compared with obese participants (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2).

Multiple sensitivity analyses further demonstrated the robustness of our main findings. Additional exclusion of individuals who were frail at baseline did not alter the observed associations (Table S8). In analyses using a 30-item scale to construct FI, the associations were consistent with those using a 31-item FI (Table S9). We observed similar associations when we additionally adjusted the models for quadratic terms of age, medication use, or variability of TC and CRP (Tables S10–S12). The associations were unchanged when we restricted the analyses to participants without missing data on FI items or those with complete covariate data (Tables S13 & S14).

Discussion

Using data from two prospective cohorts with a total of 8,981 middle-aged and older adults, our study explored how both the mean and variability of eGDR over an 8-year period relate to baseline frailty and its progression. We observed that individuals with higher average eGDR tended to have lower baseline FI and experienced slower increase in FI over time. In contrast, greater fluctuations in eGDR were independently linked to higher baseline FI and more rapid progression of frailty, regardless of the average eGDR level. These results indicate that both sustained eGDR levels and their variability over time may help identify individuals at heightened risk of accelerated frailty progression.

Previous studies have revealed a close association between eGDR, a reliable surrogate marker of IR, and frailty progression [24–26]. However, existing studies have primarily focused on static, single time-point measurements, overlooking its temporal variations. Therefore, we expanded prior research by analyzing the long-term average and variability of eGDR using 3 assessments. To date and to our knowledge, this is the first investigation to assess the impact of long-term eGDR patterns on frailty progression. We identified a significant inverse relationship between average eGDR during the exposure period and subsequent frailty progression in middle-aged and older adults. Moreover, increased eGDR variability was associated with a faster rate of frailty progression, independent of mean eGDR. This suggests that even those with relatively high average eGDR may still be at elevated risk if they experience substantial long-term fluctuations. By focusing on the dynamic nature of eGDR, our study adds new insights into the relationship between eGDR and frailty. Notably, our stratified analyses confirmed that these associations persisted with consistent effect directions across subgroups defined by BMI and T2D status, although the association between average eGDR and frailty progression was stronger in non-obese individuals. This indicates that eGDR reflects systemic insulin sensitivity beyond simple adiposity or hyperglycemia.

The adverse impact of lower eGDR, reflecting reduced insulin sensitivity, may be explained by the multifaceted role of IR in promoting frailty through various biological mechanisms. IR can drive frailty progression by inducing metabolic disturbances, oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and persistent inflammation [42, 43]. For instance, IR-related mitochondrial dysfunction and excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) production can intensify oxidative stress, leading to cumulative cellular damage and functional decline across organ systems [44]. Chronic low-grade inflammation, exacerbated by IR, increases the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), fostering a systemic inflammatory environment that accelerates muscle breakdown and impairs mitochondrial function [45–48]. Additionally, skeletal muscle, an insulin-sensitive tissue, experiences reduced glucose uptake and altered protein synthesis under IR, contributing to sarcopenia, a hallmark of frailty [49]. Collectively, these interconnected pathways highlight the pivotal role of IR in initiating and accelerating frailty among aging individuals.

Beyond the direct effects of IR, the variability of eGDR plays a crucial but distinct role in frailty progression. In geriatric medicine, the capacity to maintain homeostatic regulation is a hallmark of physiological resilience [50]. High eGDR variability, independent of the mean level, likely reflects a loss of this resilience and systemic metabolic instability. As eGDR is a composite surrogate reflecting the interplay of glycemic control, adiposity, and vascular tone, its fluctuations signify a broader disruption in the metabolic milieu. Evidence suggests that such large oscillations in insulin sensitivity and metabolic parameters can trigger oxidative stress and endothelial damage, which may be more deleterious than chronically sustained metabolic dysfunction [51]. These fluctuations can induce repetitive physiological stress, exhausting the body’s adaptive reserves and accelerating the cumulative decline across multiple systems, thereby promoting frailty [50].

Our findings have important implications for clinical practice and public health. First, compared with single time-point measurements, long-term average eGDR reflects the cumulative metabolic burden over time. Therefore, incorporating longitudinal monitoring into routine care allows clinicians to identify individuals with persistently low eGDR who are at high risk of accelerated frailty progression, facilitating timely targeted interventions. Second, our findings indicate that eGDR fluctuations are associated with frailty progression independent of the mean level. High eGDR variability should be regarded as a clinical “red flag”, signaling metabolic instability, potential medication non-adherence, or suboptimal disease control. When observing such fluctuations, clinicians should investigate the underlying causes rather than dismissing them as random variations, even among individuals with historically high average eGDR levels. Third, regarding intervention strategies, priority should be given to sustainable and steady management (e.g., consistent medication adherence and gradual, long-term lifestyle modifications) rather than drastic, short-term changes that may induce metabolic oscillations. Future trials are warranted to verify whether stabilizing eGDR trajectories can effectively decelerate the progression of frailty.

Our study has several strengths. First, we evaluated the long-term average and variability of eGDR using data from 3 time-point assessments to better identify trends and patterns of eGDR fluctuations over time while minimizing the impact of random variations. These methodological approaches enabled us to robustly investigate the associations between longitudinal eGDR patterns and frailty progression and to contribute novel evidence that builds upon prior research in this field. Furthermore, the inclusion of two prospective cohorts with diverse ethnic backgrounds, rigorous study designs, and large sample sizes strengthens the validity of our findings. The consistency of results across both cohorts enhances the generalizability of our conclusions, which are further supported by rigorous sensitivity analyses confirming robustness.

However, several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, distinguishing between healthy fluctuations (e.g., lifestyle improvements) and pathological instability remains a challenge in observational studies. However, it is worth noting that steady, linear improvements in metabolic health typically result in low variability (VIM), whereas high variability captures erratic or oscillatory patterns [52, 53]. In older adults, such metabolic instability often reflects compromised homeostatic regulation or suboptimal disease control [50, 54]. Consequently, the high variability observed in our study likely represents detrimental instability rather than steady health improvements. Second, frailty was assessed using a deficit accumulation model relying on self-reported items, which may introduce recall or reporting bias. Notably, the procedure for constructing FI has been proven to possess relatively good reliability in HRS and ELSA [13, 36]. Third, the limited number of studies included in our meta-analysis warrants caution when interpreting heterogeneity estimates, as these metrics may lack precision due to limited statistical power. Fourth, as our cohorts predominantly comprised Western populations, the generalizability of these findings to other ethnic or cultural groups with different lifestyles or genetic backgrounds remains uncertain. Finally, it is possible that preclinical frailty or declining physical function might accelerate the progression of IR (lower eGDR) during the exposure period, potentially contributing to the observed associations. To mitigate this bias, we performed sensitivity analyses excluding participants with prevalent frailty at baseline. The consistency of our findings (Table S8) supports the robustness of the prospective associations and suggests that eGDR patterns may precede frailty progression. However, given the observational nature of this study, definitive causal inferences cannot be drawn.

Conclusions

In conclusion, both long-term average and variability of eGDR were associated with baseline frailty and its progression. These findings underscore the clinical importance of monitoring long-term eGDR trajectories and implementing targeted interventions to maintain eGDR within an optimal range as part of frailty prevention strategies.
