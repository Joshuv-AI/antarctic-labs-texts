Long-term impact of cholecystectomy on myocardial infarction, ischemic cerebrovascular accident, and peripheral vascular disease: insights from a nationwide analysis
Authors: Nir Messer, Adi Rov, Guy Lahat, Eran Nizri, Tzipi Hornik-Lurie, Fahim Kanani, Jonathan B Yuval, Adam Abu Abeid, Yonatan Lessing, Avishai M Tsur, Jacob Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAcute myocardial infarction (MI), ischemic cerebrovascular accident (CVA), and peripheral vascular disease (PVD) are significant global health burdens that have been associated with cholelithiasis by multiple studies. However, the relationship between cholecystectomy and these vascular morbidities remains unclear. This study aims to investigate the long-term impact of cholecystectomy on MI, CVA and PVD.MethodsThis nationwide study analyzed data, which accounted for 59% of the Israeli population. Adult patients with cholelithiasis and no prior vascular morbidity (MI, CVA, and PVD) were followed for over 8 years and categorized into two groups: those who underwent cholecystectomy and those who did not. Incidence rates of MI, ischemic CVA (including transient ischemic attack), and PVD were compared between the groups using Cox regression models adjusted for demographics and comorbidities.ResultsAmong the 142 834 eligible patients with cholelithiasis, 37 173 underwent cholecystectomy. Cholecystectomy cohort was associated with a modest protective effect on MI, ischemic CVA, and PVD, with lower incidence rates of MI (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.96), ischemic CVA (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.89 to 0.99), and PVD (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.80 to 0.95) compared with the No-cholecystectomy cohort. Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated significant separation in cumulative incidence rates favoring the cholecystectomy group for all three outcomes.ConclusionCholecystectomy appears to be associated with reduced cumulative incidences of MI, ischemic CVA, and PVD in patients with cholelithiasis. Nonetheless, the relatively modest risk reduction observed in our study raises questions about the procedure’s overall benefit versus the potential risks in the context of risk reduction measures.Level of evidenceLevel IV.Keywords: myocardial infarction, cholecystectomy, stroke, gallbladder

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (MI), ischemic stroke, and peripheral vascular disease (PVD) are leading causes of morbidity and mortality worldwide.1,3 Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the primary cause of death in the USA, with approximately 1.5 million cases of MI occurring annually.1 Cerebrovascular accident (CVA) ranks as the second leading cause of death globally, with over 600 000 incidents annually in the USA.2 PVD affects over 200 million people, with 20% of the elderly population affected.3 While understanding the associated risk factors is essential for preventing these vascular morbidities, recent data suggest an association between cholelithiasis and these vascular morbidities.4,6

Cholelithiasis is the most prevalent condition in the gastrointestinal field worldwide, with an incidence of 10–15% in the USA.7 Numerous studies have demonstrated an elevated risk of CAD among patients with cholelithiasis, supported by two meta-analyses of high-quality studies confirming a significant association.8,11 Similarly, research focusing on CVA outcomes indicates a notably heightened risk among individuals with cholelithiasis.8 However, the exact relationship between cholelithiasis and these vascular morbidities remains unclear, raising the question of whether cholelithiasis directly contributes to these conditions or if both stem from a shared underlying pathology.

Cholecystectomy is a commonly performed and generally safe surgical intervention. Annually in the USA, approximately 300 000 cholecystectomies are performed, with bile duct injury occurring in 0.15–0.36% of cases.12 13 Prophylactic cholecystectomy in asymptomatic patients is not recommended clinically, given that approximately 80% of individuals with cholelithiasis remain asymptomatic throughout their lifetimes.14 Despite the well-established association between cholelithiasis and vascular pathologies, the impact of cholecystectomy on these vascular morbidities remains uncertain. Existing literature presents conflicting findings regarding the role of cholecystectomy in modifying vascular morbidity risks. While some studies have suggested a decreased risk of CAD after cholecystectomy, others have indicated mixed or potentially increased risks associated with the procedure.415,17 Consequently, this study aimed to assess the long-term effects of cholecystectomy on acute MI, ischemic stroke, and PVD.

Methods

In this retrospective, registry-based cohort study, we followed adult patients who had no history of any heart disease, CVA, or PVD, with documented cholelithiasis for at least 8 years. Patients were identified from the Clalit Health Services (CHS) electronic database in Israel, covering the period from January 2005 to December 2015. The article was written and edited according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.18

The CHS electronic database is an integrated health maintenance organization service in Israel that provides healthcare to more than 5 800 000 insured enrollees (>59% of the Israeli population). The CHS central computerized database was founded in 1998 and since collects medical and administrative data originating from its healthcare facilities. The registry is subjected to a continuous validation process based on repeated appraisals of diagnoses made by CHS physicians, registered prescriptions, pharmacy claims, laboratory tests, and auxiliary tests for each patient. It includes 14 general hospitals and over 1300 primary care clinics. The database includes patient demographics, clinical characteristics, hospital discharge and outpatient clinical diagnoses, laboratory test results, medical treatments, and medication dispensation information. Data were extracted from CHS using the secure Clalit Research Data Sharing Platform powered by MDClone (Beer Sheva, Israel). MDClone is a secure platform granting access to a comprehensive repository of anonymized patient data, using synthetic datasets that accurately replicate real patient information while rigorously protecting patient privacy.

To evaluate the impact of cholecystectomy on vascular morbidities, patients were classified into two groups: ‘Cholecystectomy’ and ‘No-cholecystectomy’. The groups were compared with assessing long-term vascular morbidity, including MI, ischemic CVA, and PVD. The cholecystectomy date was set as the index date for the Cholecystectomy cohort, while the index date for the No-cholecystectomy cohort was set at the time of the initial diagnosis of cholelithiasis. The primary endpoint was the occurrence of MI, including ST-elevation MI and non-ST elevation MI, all ischemic CVA, including transient ischemic attack (TIA), and PVD within 3 months after cholecystectomy for the Cholecystectomy cohort or after the diagnosis of cholelithiasis for the No-cholecystectomy cohort, or until December 31, 2022.

Data collected encompassed age, gender, race, occupation, smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and history of diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), hypertension, atrial fibrillation, hyperlipidemia, cholecystitis, cholangitis, pancreatitis, and biliary colic prior to the diagnosis of cholelithiasis. New incidences of heart failure, COPD, cholecystitis, cholangitis, pancreatitis, biliary colic, MI, ischemic CVA, PVD, and mortality were recorded after cholecystectomy for the Cholecystectomy cohort or the diagnosis of cholelithiasis for the No-cholecystectomy cohort. All clinical diagnoses of MI, ischemic CVA, and PVD were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision codes. The specific codes are listed in online supplemental table 1.

All statistical analyses were conducted using two-tailed tests, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant. Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to evaluate the association between cholecystectomy and each vascular outcome, estimating HRs and 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption was verified via graphical inspection. Unadjusted, partially adjusted (age and sex), and fully adjusted models (adding comorbidity measures) were fitted. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated to illustrate the cumulative incidence of each outcome. Missing data were minimal, and a complete-case approach was applied. All analyses were performed according to recognized methodological standards and institutional guidelines.

Results

From January 2005 to December 2015, we identified 173 735 patients with cholelithiasis. Among these, 142 834 (82.2%) met the inclusion criteria, with 37 173 (26%) undergoing cholecystectomy and 105 661 (74%) not undergoing the procedure (figure 1). In the Cholecystectomy cohort, 72.5% were female, and the median age was 50.8 years, compared with 60.9 years in the No-cholecystectomy cohort. The Cholecystectomy cohort exhibited significantly lower comorbidity rates, including DM (17.8% vs. 24.0%, p<0.001), hypertension (32.7% vs. 45.6%, p<0.001), dyslipidemia (32.9% vs. 41.8%, p<0.001), and COPD (2.7% vs. 5.4%, p<0.001), compared with the No-cholecystectomy cohort (table 1).

Over 1 350 779 person-years of follow-up, 7847 patients experienced MI. The crude incidence of MI was 41 per 10 000 person-years in the Cholecystectomy cohort and 61 per 10 000 person-years in the No-cholecystectomy cohort. Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated increasing separation over time, with the final cumulative incidence of MI being lower in the Cholecystectomy cohort compared with the No-cholecystectomy cohort (7.0% vs. 10.3%, p<0.001) (figure 2). In the unadjusted Cox regression model, cholecystectomy was associated with a reduced hazard for MI (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.60 to 0.67). In the demographically adjusted model for age and gender, cholecystectomy remained associated with a reduced hazard for MI, though the effect was attenuated (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.85 to 0.95). In the fully adjusted model accounting for age, gender, and comorbidities, the results were consistent (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.96) (table 2).

Discussion

This study investigated the association between cholecystectomy and vascular morbidities, including MI, ischemic CVA, and PVD, in patients with cholelithiasis. Consistent with previous studies, our findings suggest that cholecystectomy is associated with modestly lower rates of vascular morbidities. However, these findings represent associations and should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal protective effect. After adjusting for other potential confounders using Cox regression analysis, the impact of cholecystectomy on vascular morbidities became weaker, with HRs of 0.91 for MI, 0.94 for CVA, and 0.87 for PVD. These findings question the efficacy of cholecystectomy as a practical strategy for reducing the risk of vascular morbidities.

This study represents the most extensive investigation to date on the incidence of vascular morbidities after cholecystectomy, encompassing over 5.8 million individuals, which is nearly 60% of the Israeli population. As the gallbladder plays a crucial role in bile storage and concentration, cholecystectomy alters bile metabolism and excretion and potentially impacts cardiovascular health.19 Theoretically, this change can affect lipid profiles, which are closely linked to cardiovascular health. Given the paucity of data on the association between cholecystectomy and vascular morbidities, we aimed to assess the long-term effects of cholecystectomy on acute MI, ischemic CVA, and PVD. A recent study based on the Korean National Health Insurance assessed 84 467 individuals with cholelithiasis and reported a risk reduction rate of 11.4% for MI and 16.1% for CVA after cholecystectomy.6 Another study using data from Taiwan’s National Health Insurance Research Database included 155 356 individuals with cholelithiasis, of whom 11 913 underwent cholecystectomy.4 The findings demonstrated a risk reduction of 40% for overall CVA (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.59 to 0.61), 41% for ischemic CVA (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.61), and 34% for hemorrhagic CVA (HR 0.56, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.59). However, other research indicates an increased risk of MI, particularly in younger individuals without DM.20 A study by Chavez-Tapia et al compared 399 patients who underwent cholecystectomy with 399 patients with cholelithiasis who did not undergo cholecystectomy and found no significant differences in the incidence of cardiovascular disease.17 The study did, however, identify an increased prevalence of metabolic diseases among patients with cholecystectomy, which are known to elevate cardiovascular risk. Specifically, the ORs for type 2 DM (OR 2.2, 95% CI 1.1 to 4.5, p=0.018), hypertension (OR 5.1, 95% CI 2.6 to 10.1, p=0.001), and hypercholesterolemia (OR 2.7, 95% CI 1.3 to 5.5, p=0.004) were notably higher in the cholecystectomy group. Our study revealed that cholecystectomy leads to a risk reduction of 9% for MI (HR 0.91, 95% CI 0.86 to 0.96), 6% for CVA (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.89 to 0.99), and 13% for PVD (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.80 to 0.95).

Cholecystectomy is the most common elective abdominal surgery in the USA, with an estimated 750 000 operations performed annually.21 However, it may be associated with significant morbidity, including bile leak and bile duct injury. A systematic review of 151 studies involving 505 292 patients reported an overall morbidity rate of 1.6–5.3%, a bile duct injury rate of 0.32–0.52%, and a mortality rate of 0.08–0.14%.22 Prophylactic cholecystectomy in asymptomatic patients with cholelithiasis is not clinically indicated, as approximately 80% of individuals with cholelithiasis remain asymptomatic throughout their lives.14 Following our study and others indicating that cholecystectomy reduces vascular morbidity risks, some may argue for re-evaluating the indications for prophylactic cholecystectomy in asymptomatic patients with high risk for MI or CVA. However, many of these studies, including ours, show a relatively modest risk reduction after cholecystectomy. Our calculations of the number needed to treat to prevent one case of MI, CVA, or PVD revealed that 31 patients would need to undergo cholecystectomy to prevent one case of MI, 39 patients to prevent one case of CVA, and 63 patients to prevent one case of PVD. Considering that some MI, CVA, or PVD cases are mild, we conclude that the risk reduction does not justify the potential risks associated with cholecystectomy. Furthermore, our findings and those of other studies should be interpreted cautiously, as there may be other confounders and other factors not accounted for that could diminish the observed risk reduction.

A recent study evaluating the genetic association between cholelithiasis and CAD and CVA using the UK Biobank concluded that an intrinsic link exists between cholelithiasis and both CAD and CVA.5 However, it was found that cholelithiasis does not elevate the risk of CAD and CVA, and treatment of cholelithiasis may not directly confer significant cardiovascular health benefits. This study establishes that there is no direct causal relationship between gallstones and vascular morbidities, suggesting the presence of underlying confounding factors. Consequently, cholecystectomy is likely less associated with risk reduction in vascular morbidity than previously stated by other studies.

Several limitations need to be acknowledged. First, several confounders were not included, such as body mass index, lipid and cholesterol levels, family history of cardiovascular disease, socioeconomic status, lifestyle, smoking status, alcohol consumption, statin use, anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy, and other unknown variables. Each of these factors exerts a well-established influence on vascular morbidity, and their absence limits the ability to fully adjust for confounding. Second, although our fully adjusted Cox regression models accounted for major demographics and comorbidities, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Specifically, the observed younger age and lower comorbidity burden in the cholecystectomy cohort raise the possibility of healthy user bias. Propensity score matching would represent a rigorous method to further minimize this bias, but the size and complexity of our dataset limited its feasibility. Thus, our findings should be interpreted with this limitation in mind. The severity of MI, CVA, and PVD was not evaluated. For instance, our outcome definition grouped TIAs under the broader category of CVA. While this approach is consistent with prior database studies, it disregards differences in severity and prognosis. This methodological choice could dilute or exaggerate the observed effect size, depending on the relative distribution across the two cohorts. Additionally, this study included patients older than 18 years, but vascular morbidities tend to occur in individuals in their sixth decade of life or older, which may have influenced our results. Moreover, the diagnosis of cholelithiasis is usually incidental in asymptomatic patients, limiting the evaluation of time-to-event analysis in the No-cholecystectomy cohort. The characteristics of cholelithiasis vary across races, and our findings may not be applicable to other populations. However, the Israeli population is an immigrant population, with 20–25% originally from Eastern Europe, 15–20% from Western Europe, 20–25% from North Africa, 30–35% from the Middle East, and 10% from North and South America. Although our analysis did not evaluate the distribution of origin populations, we think the large sample size (5.8 million) overcomes this limitation.

Future investigations should focus on identifying high-risk subgroups in whom the association may be most pronounced, such as patients with metabolic syndrome, strong family history of vascular disease, or younger individuals diagnosed with cholelithiasis. Stratified analyses in these populations may clarify whether the modest associations observed are driven by specific patient cohorts and could inform tailored risk prediction.

Conclusion

Cholecystectomy may lead to a lower incidence of CAD, ischemic CVA and PVD in patients with cholelithiasis compared with those with asymptomatic cholelithiasis who did not undergo the procedure. However, the impact of cholecystectomy in asymptomatic patients, as observed in our study, is relatively modest and may not justify the potential risks associated with the surgery. Additional investigations are warranted to validate our findings and better understand the potential association between cholecystectomy and vascular morbidities.
