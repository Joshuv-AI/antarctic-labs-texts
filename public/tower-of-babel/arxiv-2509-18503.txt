Abstract
We present TOI-2155 b, a high-mass transiting brown dwarf discovered using data from NASA’s Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) mission and confirmed with ground-based RV measurements from the Tillinghast Reflector Echelle Spectrograph (TRES). We also analyze ground-based follow-up photometric data from the Wendelstein Observatory (WST), Las Cumbres Observatory Global Telescope (LCOGT), and Wild Boar Remote Observatory (WBR). TOI-2155 b is a short-period brown dwarf with P = 3.7246950 − 0.0000028 + 0.0000029 P=3.7246950^{+0.0000029}_{-0.0000028} days. The radius and mass of TOI-2155 b are found to be R b = 0.975 ± 0.008 ​ R J R_{b}=0.975\pm 0.008\,\mathrm{R_{J}} and M b = 81.1 ± 1.1 ​ M J M_{b}=81.1\pm 1.1\,\mathrm{M_{J}} , respectively, corresponding to a density of ρ b = 110 ± 3.0 \rho_{b}=110\pm 3.0 g cm -3 . The effective temperature of the subgiant host star is estimated at T eff = 6085 ± 78 T_{\rm eff}=6085\pm 78 K, which identifies it as an F-type star with a radius R \thinstar = 1.705 − 0.064 + 0.066 R_{\thinstar}=1.705^{+0.066}_{-0.064} R ⊙ \mathrm{R_{\odot}} and a mass M ⋆ = 1.33 ± 0.008 M_{\star}=1.33\pm 0.008 M ⊙ . With a mass close to the hydrogen-burning limit, TOI-2155 b occupies a high-mass regime in the brown dwarf mass–radius diagram, becoming a valuable benchmark system for testing models of substellar structure and evolution.



I
Introduction

The search for exoplanets in recent decades has also deepened our understanding of brown dwarfs (BDs): substellar objects that occupy the region between planets and stars, with masses
\sim
13-80 M
J
and radii ranging from 0.7 to 1.4 R
J

(
J. Šubjak et al. 2020
;
S. Csizmadia 2016
;
M. Deleuil et al. 2008
;
A. Burrows et al. 2001
)
. However, a simple mass-based definition is not enough to classify BDs. These traditional boundaries of BDs are approximate and can be affected by metallicity and other factors, being within 11–16 M
J

(
D. S. Spiegel et al. 2011
)
and 75–80 M
J

(
T. W. Carmichael et al. 2022
;
I. Baraffe et al. 2002
)
. For example, recent models by
C. V. Morley et al. 2024
suggest that BDs can be as massive as 81.7 M
J
under specific conditions such as low metallicity and the absence of atmospheric clouds. For this reason, the study of BDs and their position in the mass-radius diagram can contribute to a more complete understanding of how extrasolar systems form and evolve.

The interior of BDs is supported predominantly by electron degeneracy pressure. Therefore, BDs do not sustain hydrogen fusion in their cores as they cool and contract over time
(
A. Burrows et al. 2001
)
. BDs are also physically distinguished by their high bulk densities that are directly related to changes in their temperature.

A. Burrows et al. 2001
presents extensive theoretical models of BDs and giant planets, explaining that their mass-density relationship reaches a maximum near the minimum hydrogen burning mass of approximately 60 to 70 M
J
, where the core degeneracy pressure results in peak densities reaching up to
\sim 100
g cm
-3
. This boundary establishes the transition between core nuclear-burning stars and degenerate-core BDs (whereas low-mass stars are less dense because hydrogen fusion inflates their radii). TOI-2155 b with a mass of
M_{b}=81.1\pm 1.1

{M_{J}}
and
\rho_{b}=110\pm 3.0
g cm
-3
consistent with electron degeneracy pressure, falls within this expected brown dwarf regime despite uncertainties in exact mass boundaries. BDs and low-mass stars are believed to form similarly, through the gravitational collapse of dense molecular cloud cores. However, unlike low-mass stars, BDs do not undergo sustained hydrogen fusion and therefore evolve differently over time
(
G. Chabrier et al. 2000a
)
. BDs may undergo brief deuterium or lithium burning phases, but never achieve the core temperatures required for hydrogen burning
(
F. D’Antona & I. Mazzitelli 1997
;
P. Myers & P. Benson 1983
;
C. Beichman et al. 1986
;
R. B. Larson 1981
)
.

Precise measurements of mass and density of BDs test theoretical models of the interiors of substellar objects
(
D. Saumon & M. S. Marley 2008
;
I. Baraffe et al. 2003
;
G. Chabrier & I. Baraffe 1997
, e.g.,)
. Such precise measurements of mass and radius (and hence density) are best achieved when a BD is in a transiting orbit around its host star. These physical parameters are critical factors in the development of evolutionary models of BDs
(
D. Saumon & M. S. Marley 2008
, e.g.,)
, to which the observed population of transiting BDs is compared. The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) mission provides us with precise light curves of such systems, which help us determine critical orbital parameters, such as the period, inclination, and radius of transiting companions
(
T. W. Carmichael et al. 2021
)
. When combined with radial velocity (RV) measurements, they allow us to determine BD masses and, crucially, bulk densities
(
B. A. Henderson et al. 2024
;
T. W. Carmichael et al. 2022
)
.

While large-scale surveys have identified over 1000 solitary BDs in the Solar neighborhood
(
B. Burningham 2018
)
, only
\sim
50 transiting brown dwarf systems have been discovered to date. In recent years, TESS has been a primary tool for detecting new transiting BDs
(
N. Vowell et al. 2025
, e.g.,)
, detecting more than two-thirds of these 50 systems
(e.g.
E. Y. Zhang et al. 2025
, and references therein)
. This number is significantly smaller than the fraction of low-mass stars found in close binary systems, as
\sim
26% of M dwarfs are in multiple systems
(
G. Duchêne & A. Kraus 2013
)
.

In this paper, we introduce TOI-2155 b, a high-density brown dwarf near the hydrogen burning mass limit. We describe the observations collected for TOI-2155 in Section
II
. The properties of the host star as well as the modelling of TOI-2155 b are explained in Section
III
. In Section
IV
, we compare TOI-2155 b to other known transiting BDs, showing its significance within the broader brown-dwarf population. Finally, we conclude in Section
V
with a summary of this work.

II.2
Ground-based follow-up photometry

Bright nearby sources can significantly affect both the radial velocity measurements and the photometry of the target star, potentially leading to false positives or biasing the derived companion mass and radius. To mitigate such contamination, we first confirm the transit signal through ground-based photometric follow-up observations to ensure it originates from the star TOI-2155. Subsequently, we assess the presence of close stellar companions using high-resolution imaging to identify any contaminating sources that could dilute the transit depth and affect the measured radius of the brown-dwarf companion (See
II.2.2
).

II.2.2
High-resolution imaging and contaminating sources

We confirm that the transits originate from the TOI-2155 through ground-based photometric follow-up. However, we also pursue observations at resolutions higher than the seeing limit to detect any close companions and assess whether they contaminate the photometry of the target star.

We observed TOI-2155 on October 24, 2020, at 22:48 UT with the Speckle Polarimeter
(
B. Safonov et al. 2017
)
on the 2.5 m telescope at the Caucasian Mountain Observatory (CMO) of the Sternberg Astronomical Institute (SAI) of Lomonosov Moscow State University. The CMO is located in the northern Caucasus, and the telescope coordinates are
43^{\circ}44^{\prime}10^{\prime\prime}N,42^{\circ}40^{\prime}03^{\prime\prime}

E, 2100 m above sea level. An Electron-Multiplying CCD, the Andor iXon 897, was used as a detector. The atmospheric dispersion compensator allowed observation through the wide-band
I_{c}
filter. The power spectrum was estimated using 4000 frames with a 30-ms exposure. The detector has a pixel scale of
20.6
mas pixel
-1
, and the angular resolution was 89 mas. The long-exposure vision was
1.45^{\farcs}
. We did not detect any stellar companions brighter than
\Delta I_{c}=4.1
and
5.9
at
\rho=0\farcs 25
and
1\farcs 0
, respectively, where
\rho
is the separation between the source and the potential companion. Given TESS’s large pixel scale of 21
\arcsec
, our high-resolution imaging, with an angular resolution of 89 milliarcseconds, effectively excludes nearby companions that could contaminate the photometry.

Using a custom pipeline to process data from the speckle polarimeter
(
A. Tokovinin et al. 2010
)
, we show in Figure
2
the 5
\sigma

Gaia

II.2.3
Radial velocities

To characterize the TOI-2155 system and confirm the nature of its transiting companion, we obtained a total of 27 spectra using the Tillinghast Reflector Echelle Spectrograph (TRES)
(
G. Fűrész 2008
)
on the 1.5-meter telescope at the Fred L. Whipple Observatory on Mount Hopkins, Arizona. These 27 spectra were used to measure the radial velocity (RV) variations induced by the companion, allowing us to determine its mass.
TRES covers a wavelength range of 3850 to 9096 Å and has a resolving power of approximately
R\sim 44{,}000
. The TRES team measured relative RVs at each orbital phase using multiple échelle orders per spectrum
(
L. A. Buchhave et al. 2010
)
. Cosmic rays were identified and removed through visual inspection of each order, and low signal-to-noise orders were excluded, along with those contaminated by telluric absorption. Each spectrum’s radial velocity (RV) was determined by cross-correlating the individual échelle orders against a median co-added template constructed from the out-of-transit spectra, with outlier pixels replaced during the template creation. The relative RV for each observation was computed as the average of the velocities from all usable orders. Finally, the measurements were shifted to correct for the long-term drift in the TRES zero point, which is measured via nightly monitoring of RV standard stars
(
S. N. Quinn et al. 2014
, similar to the process outlined in)
. The RV uncertainties are calculated from the standard deviations of the RVs of the individual échelle orders, and inflated by the instrumental jitter measured in the scatter of the standard star RVs (typically 10 to 15 m s
-1
).
With exposure lengths of 1300 s to 2400 s, respectively, these spectra were obtained on September 23, 2020, and October 11, 2020, in approximately opposite orbital phases (near RV extrema), providing us with an S/N between 27.6 and 31.9.
The RVs derived from TRES are listed in Table
3
.

III.1.2
Photometric modulation and stellar rotation period

During initial analysis of TESS light curves, we find photometric modulation in the light curve. To investigate this, we combine the out-of-transit data from all sectors and use the Generalized Lomb-Scargle (GLS) periodogram
(
M. Zechmeister & M. Kürster 2009
)
. From the GLS periodogram analysis, we detected a marginal signal with a period of 5 days. A similar signal was identified when individual sectors (52, 59, 78, and 86) were analysed, suggesting a potential stellar rotation period. However, the detection is not robust, as it shows marginal significance and may be influenced by TESS spacecraft systematics, such as momentum dumps, that are expected during this period
(
T. Jansen & D. Kipping 2020
)
. We were unable to confirm this signal with All-Sky Automated Survey for SuperNovae (ASAS-SN;
T. W.-S. Holoien et al. 2017
) data due to the longer cadence of the observations.

IV
Discussion

The discovery of TOI-2155 b adds another transiting BD to the growing population of transiting BDs with precisely measured masses and radii, now exceeding 50 known systems
(
N. Vowell et al. 2025
;
B. A. Henderson et al. 2024
;
T. W. Carmichael et al. 2020
;
J. Šubjak et al. 2020
)
. As outlined in Section 1, BDs are generally defined to have masses between approximately 13 and 80
M_{\mathrm{J}}
. Although TOI-2155 b’s mass of
81.1\pm 1.1~M_{\mathrm{J}}
marginally exceeds the classical upper limit, its very high density (
\sim 110\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}
) and inferred age of several gigayears strongly indicate that it is supported predominantly by electron degeneracy pressure and does not sustain hydrogen fusion in its core
(
A. Burrows et al. 2001
;
G. Chabrier et al. 2000a
)
. These characteristics support its classification as a brown dwarf, despite its mass being slightly above the traditional limit. Still, we cannot fully rule out the possibility that TOI-2155 b is a low-mass star.

IV.1
TOI-2155 b and the Brown Dwarf Desert

TOI-2155 b joins the growing number of BDs found in close-in orbits around their host star. With a mass around 81.1
{M_{J}}
and an orbital period of just 3.72 days, it lies in the lower part of the so-called ‘brown dwarf desert’: a reference to the relative scarcity of BDs on short orbital periods (
P\lesssim 10
-100 days) around stars
(
P. Wilson et al. 2016
;
D. Grether & C. H. Lineweaver 2006
;
G. W. Marcy & R. P. Butler 2000
)
. It is not fully understood why such a desert might exist, with some explanations pointing to the severe difficulties that BDs face in forming so close in. In contrast, those that do form might be pushed out or destroyed by interactions in the early stages of the system
(
B. A. Henderson et al. 2024
;
N. Grieves et al. 2021
;
P. J. Armitage & I. A. Bonnell 2002
)
.

Transiting systems like TOI-2155 b are especially valuable because the combination of transit photometry and radial velocity measurements lets us directly measure both the mass and radius of the BD, giving us crucial information about its structure and evolution. Although transit detections typically limit us to explore the parameter space of short-period BDs (usually
P_{\mathrm{orb}}<30
days to detect and characterize multiple transits efficiently), they are still essential to fill out the population of BDs with measured masses
(
T. W. Carmichael et al. 2022
)
. It is worth noting that recent discoveries of BDs from the NASA’s TESS mission
(e.g.
Barkaoui, K. et al. 2025
;
N. Vowell et al. 2025
;
B. A. Henderson et al. 2024
;
T. W. Carmichael et al. 2022
, and references therein)
are steadily populating the brown dwarf desert, challenging its traditional definition. With the growing number of transiting brown dwarfs such as TOI-2155 b being discovered, the existence of a well-defined “brown dwarf desert” is no longer valid.
The characteristics of TOI-2155 b, therefore, provide important clues that will help us understand how BDs form and evolve, especially in close-in, highly irradiated environments.

Although TOI-2155 b’s mass of
81.1\pm 1.1~M_{\mathrm{J}}
marginally exceeds the classical brown dwarf limit, its high density (
\sim 110\,\mathrm{g\,cm^{-3}}

(
A. Burrows et al. 2001

G. Chabrier et al. 2000b

.

IV.3
Orbital Characteristics, Eccentricity, and Tidal Evolution

To further explore the dynamical properties of the population of BDs found in orbit around stars, we present an orbital period-mass diagram color-coded by orbital eccentricity in Figure
9
. This diagram highlights a clear trend: systems with more extended orbital periods tend to exhibit higher and diverse eccentricities, consistent with incomplete tidal circularization. In contrast, short-period systems such as TOI-2155 b generally show low eccentricities, supporting the interpretation that tidal forces have acted efficiently on Gyr timescales to circularize their orbits
(
A. Psaridi et al. 2022
)
.

TOI-2155 b, with an orbital period of 3.72 days and
e_{b}=0.012^{+0.01}_{-0.008}
, lies firmly in the low-eccentricity (nearly circular) regime, consistent with our estimates of the tidal circularization time scale.

This interpretation aligns with the system’s short-period, nearly circular orbit (
e_{b}=0.012^{+0.01}_{-0.008}
) and its age of approximately 3.2  Gyr. We investigated the tidal evolution of the system by estimating the orbital circularization timescale due to tidal dissipation in the host star, using the formalism of
B. Jackson et al. 2008
. If we calculate the circularization timescale from the formalism of
B. Jackson et al. 2008
, we find
\tau=0.33\,
Gyr, which is much shorter than the system’s age (3.2 Gyr). This implies that the host star would have had enough time to circularize the orbit of the BD, thus erasing any primordial eccentricity that may have existed. Even when using the most conservative estimates for the tidal quality factor of the star (
Q_{\star}=10^{7}
) and the BD (
Q_{BD}=10^{5}
)
(
B. A. Henderson et al. 2024
)
, we arrive at this conclusion. However, given the usual difficulty in measuring Q, the circularization timescale could be longer.

IV.4
Age, Formation Pathway and Mass Ratio

TOI-2155 b resides in the BD regime, with a system mass ratio of
0.061_{-0.003}^{+0.003}
, exceeding the typical planet–star boundary of approximately 0.01
(
R. Poleski et al. 2017
;
A. P. Hatzes & H. Rauer 2015
)
.
Its high mass ratio places it closer to the star-star regime than the planet–star regime, suggesting a stellar-like formation pathway, such as gravitational collapse or fragmentation of molecular clouds
(
B. P. Bowler et al. 2020
)
. Most BDs likely form through such processes, with only a few exceptions for companions orbiting very massive host stars
(
G. Duchêne et al. 2023
)
.

Finally, given TOI-2155 b’s mature age ( 3.2 Gyr) and lack of detectable lithium absorption, it is expected to have cooled and contracted significantly. However, its radius shows mild inflation, likely due to moderate but significant stellar irradiation from its close orbit, rather than being young or internally hot.

V
Conclusion

In conclusion, TOI-2155 b stands out as a high-mass transiting brown dwarf that contributes significantly to the known brown dwarf population and lies close to the hydrogen-burning limit. At the same time, the exact boundary between BDs and low-mass stars remains debated—particularly near the hydrogen-burning limit—TOI-2155 b’s high density and lack of evidence for core hydrogen burning make it consistent with a BD rather than a very low-mass star
(
A. Burrows et al. 2001
;
G. Chabrier & I. Baraffe 2000
)
. With a mass of
M_{b}=81.1\pm 1.1,M_{J}
, TOI-2155 b provides a well-characterized example of a high-mass brown dwarf near the hydrogen-burning limit. Moreover, its position near the hydrogen burning limit offers a critical benchmark for testing models of substellar evolution.

TOI-2155 b suggests a mild radius inflation compared to theoretical expectations from evolutionary models such as
M. S. Marley et al. 2021
;
I. Baraffe et al. 2003
for a BD at an age of
\approx 3.2\,\mathrm{Gyr}
. Although we cannot claim robust evidence for inflation in this case, it is worth mentioning that some recent discoveries of high-mass BDs have shown radius inflation (e.g.
S. P. Schmidt et al. 2023
;
N. Grieves et al. 2021
;
S. Casewell et al. 2020
).

Given TOI-2155 b’s close-in, nearly circular orbit (
e=0.012
), tidal forces could be responsible for the observed low eccentricity, but the system’s orbital history remains uncertain. TOI-2155 b’s mass ratio suggests a formation pathway more akin to stellar-like processes.

TOI-2155 b, lying near the hydrogen-burning limit, serves as a key benchmark for testing these models in a regime where small differences in mass and composition can significantly impact the observed radius and density
(
I. Baraffe et al. 2003
)
. This system offers a unique opportunity to study the effects of stellar environment and proximity on BD properties, making it a valuable target for future investigations. Continued observations and comparisons with other transiting BDs will help refine theoretical models and clarify the physical processes regarding their formation and evolution.
