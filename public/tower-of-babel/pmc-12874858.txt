The effect of Hippophae rhamnoides on Hydroxychloroquine-Induced stomatitis in rats
Authors: Muhammet Dilber, Zeynep Suleyman, Nurinisa Yucel, Gulce Naz Yazici, Renad Mammadov, Osman Kagan Cakir, Fatih Celik, Taha Abdulkadir Coban, Ismail Salcan, Huseyin Baykal, Halis Suleyman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStomatitis is the most common mucosal side effect associated with Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ). Hippophae Rhamnoides (HR) is known to contain several phytonutrients, including anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties. This study aimed whether HR can mitigate HCQ-induced oxidative stress and inflammation in rat cheek tissue and to corroborate biochemical findings with macroscopic and histopathological evaluations.MethodsTwenty four rats were used and split into four groups: healthy control group (HG), receiving HR alone, receiving HCQ alone, and receiving both HR and HCQ (HRHQ). The drugs were administered for 10 days, and at the end of the 10th day, the rats were euthanized with high dose of thiopental sodium anesthesia and the inner cheek tissues removed.ResultsHR decreased malondialdehyde, and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, which increased by HCQ, while it increased anti-oxidant levels, which decreased by HCQ. The inner cheek tissue was examined macroscopically for edema, hemorrhagic cracks, hyperemia and gray appearance that developed due to HCQ use, which were alleviated with HR. Histopathologically, epithelial necrosis, muscle degeneration, scar tissue, polymorphonuclear cell (PMNL) infiltration, haemorrhage and edema were observed in the HCQ group. In contrast, in the HRHQ group, epithelial necrosis, and muscle degeneration were normal, haemorrhage was absent, PMNL infiltration persisted in some-areas, and moderate capillary congestion and dilatation were observed. detected.ConclusionThe results indicate that HR may be a potential candidate for preventing and treating stomatitis associated with HCQ use. Translating these findings into clinical practice could support the safe and effective use of HR as a therapeutic agent.Graphical abstract Keywords: Hippophae rhamnoides, Hydroxychloroquine, Cheek, Antioxidants, Pro-inflammatory

Introduction

Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) is a 4-aminoquinoline derivative drug used to treat malaria [1]. In addition to its antimalarial effects, HCQ is effective against bacterial and viral infections [2]. As a metabolite of chloroquine, HCQ is also used to treat rheumatic diseases, including systemic lupus erythematosus and rheumatoid arthritis [3]. HCQ penetrates intracellular structures, such as the lysosomes and food vacuoles of malaria parasites, and increases pH. As a result, this pH increase inhibits the conversion of heme to hemozoin, thereby elevating toxic heme levels and leading to parasite lysis [1]. Its antibacterial and antiviral activity is attributed to the alkalinisation of intracellular acidic organelles damaged by bacteria and the inhibition of viral protein glycosylation [4].

In recent years, with the identification of new mechanisms of action, the therapeutic applications of HCQ have expanded significantly, including its use in treating COVID-19 [5]. However, this broadening therapeutic spectrum has also brought various adverse effects associated with HCQ [6]. These include gastrointestinal disturbances, allergic reactions, cardiomyopathy, neuromyotoxicity, cytopenias, and toxic effects leading to vision loss [3]. Less common cutaneous manifestations include hyperpigmentation, psoriasiform dermatitis, photodermatitis, melanonychia, hair loss, and stomatitis [7].

Stomatitis has been reported in the literature as the most frequently observed mucosal adverse effect associated with HCQ, typically occurring after high cumulative doses [8]. Stomatitis is an inflammatory disease of the oral mucosa, often accompanied by pain [9]. The relationship between inflammation and oxidative stress.One of the most commonly observed mechanisms in development of oral adverse effects-has been well documented in the literature [10]. Currently, HCQ-related toxicity is believed to occur through a multifactorial mechanism involving oxidative stress [11]. As is well known, the dosage recommended for COVID-19 infection is significantly higher than that used for malaria treatment [12].

Despite the rapid advances in modern medicine, alternative methods such as phytochemicals and Chinese herbal remedies remain significant, and research in this area is ongoing. One reason for this may be that alternative medicine methods tend to have fewer side effects, complications and costs than modern medicine [13, 14].

In this study, Hippophae rhamnoides (HR), obtained from the berries of Hippophae rhamnoides L., was investigated for its potential protective effect against oral damage induced by HCQ in rats. HR is widely consumed as a food and health supplement worldwide and contains proteins, minerals, vitamins, flavonoids, lignans, volatile oils, tannins, terpenoids, steroids, and various phytonutrients, including organic acids and alkaloids (Fig. 1) [15]. Polysaccharides, a key bioactive component, exhibit hepatoprotective, immunomodulatory, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antitumor, hypoglycemic, and anti-obesity activity [16]. The antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, antibacterial, antineoplastic, immunomodulatory, and hepatoprotective activities of HR are attributed to its content of vitamins A and C, alpha-tocopherol, high amounts of carotenoids, vitamin E, monosaccharides, amino acids, glycerophospholipids, phytosterols, zeaxanthin esters, and polyphenolic compounds [17, 18]. There are many in vivo and in vitro studies in the literature proving that HR is a powerful antioxidant and anti-inflammatory [19, 20]., It was reported that HR protects heart and liver tissues from oxidative damage [18, 21] Therewithal, it was known that HR is effective on oral mucositis caused by everolimus and 5-fluorouracil [20, 22]. HR has also been reported to significantly prevent methotrexate-induced oxidative and inflammatory damage to cheek, lower lip and tongue tissues and oropharyngeal mucosal damage in rats [23, 24] According to a recent study, HR has been shown to accelerate transdermal wound healing in a rat diabetic wound model by reducing oxidative stress [25]. Based on these findings, HR may have a protective effect against stomatitis induced by HCQ use. Therefore, this study proposed to whether HR can mitigate HCQ-induced oxidative stress and inflammation in rat cheek tissue and to corroborate biochemical findings with macroscopic and histopathological evaluations.

Materials and methods

Animals

Twenty four male albino Wistar rats, weighing between 280 and 290 g, were obtained from Erzincan Binali Yildirim University Medical Experimental Application and Research Center for the study. Prior to the experiment, the animals were housed in groups (n = 6) under standard room temperature (22 °C) with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and fed ad libitum. The Local Ethics Committee of Erzincan Binali Yildirim University approved the experimental protocols (Approval Number: 2024-11/51). The experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals established by the Guide for the Care and use of Laboratory Animals (https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/12910/guide-for-the-care-and-use-of-laboratory-animals-eighth).

Drugs and agents

The thiopental sodium used in the experiment was obtained from IE Ulagay (Türkiye), Hippophae rhamnoides was obtained from Sea buckthorn oil, 50 mL (the excipient is sunflower oil and the product claims carotenoids ≥ 180 mg%), PhytoLab (Russia), and Hydroxychloroquine (Plaquenil 200 mg tablet) from Sanofi (Türkiye).

Animal groups

The rats were split into four groups (6 rats each): healthy control group (HG), receiving 50 mg/kg Hippophae rhamnoides HR alone (), receiving 120 mg/kg Hydroxychloroquine alone (HCQ), and receiving 50 mg/kg + 120 mg/kg HCQ group (HRHQ).

Experimental procedure

In the HR and HRHQ groups, HR was received orally via gavage at a dose of 50 mg/kg [26] three times daily. The HG and HCQG groups received an equivalent volume of solvent (sunflower oil) via the same route. HCQ and HRHQ groups were administered HCQ orally via gavage at 120 mg/kg twice daily one hour after the last dose of HR [27]. Drug applications were repeated for 10 days. At the end of this period, the animals were euthanized with 50 mg/kg thiopental sodium anesthesia (0.5 g/20 mL vial). Oral mucosal tissues (inner cheek) were excised from the euthanized animals and evaluated macroscopically. Subsequently, malondialdehyde (MDA) levels were measured as an oxidative stress parameter, while total glutathione (tGSH), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT) levels were assessed as antioxidant markers. Pro-inflammatory markers tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) were measured. Histopathological examination was performed on the tissue samples.

MDA, tGSH, SOD, and CAT analysis

The tissue samples were weighed for 0.2 g. For the purpose of measuring MDA, samples were diluted to 2 ml with a 1.15% potassium chloride solution, and for the purpose of measuring tGSH, samples were diluted to 2 ml with a phosphate buffer at a pH of 7.5. The samples were then homogenised using liquid nitrogen and the tubes were centrifuged at + 4 °C at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. The resulting supernatant, which collected at the top of the centrifuge tubes after centrifugation, was obtained and used as the sample for further analysis. Tissue MDA, GSH, and SOD levels were measured using rat enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) assay kits according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Cayman Chemical Company, CAT numbers: 706002, 703002, and 10009055, respectively). CAT analysis was performed using the method described by Goth [28].

TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 analysis

Tissue samples were weighed, cut into small pieces, rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen, and homogenized using a mortar and pestle. The homogenized samples were maintained at 2–8 °C after thawing. Phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) was added at a 1:10 (w/v) ratio, vortexed for 10 s, and centrifuged at 10,000×g for 20 min. The supernatants were carefully collected. Tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α:ng/tissue), Interleukin 1 beta (IL-1β:pg/tissue), and Interleukin-6 (IL-6:ng/tissue) levels were quantified using a commercial ELISA kit (Eastbiopharm Co Ltd, China).

Histopathological analysis

Tissue samples were fixed in 10% formaldehyde for 72 h and washed under tap water in cassettes for 24 h. Following fixation, the samples were dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 90%, and 100%) to remove water from the tissues. The tissues were then cleared in xylene and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 4–5 μm thickness were cut from the paraffin blocks and stained with hematoxylin–eosin. The sections were photographed and analyzed using the DP2-SAL firmware program and a light microscope (Olympus Inc., Tokyo, Japan). One central and five peripheral areas were chosen from the serial sections received for semi-quantitative grading. For each subject, degeneration criteria were scored in the selected areas. The degree of tissue damage in the cheek tissue was assessed based on epithelial necrosis, muscle degeneration, polymorphonuclear leukocyte infiltration, and edema. Each sample was scored for each criterion as 0: no damage; 1: mild; 2: moderate; and 3: severe damage. Histopathological assessment and scoring were carried out by a pathologist blinded to the rat groups.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the IBM SPSS Statistical Program for Windows (IBM Corp., Version 27.0, released in 2020, Armonk, NY, USA). The figures created with the GraphPad Prism program (GraphPad Software, Version 8.0.1, released in 2018, San Diego, CA, USA). the Shapiro–Wilk test was used to define whether the distribution was normal. A one-way ANOVA test was used to analyze normally distributed variables. By the Levene’s test, the homogeneity of variances was provided, Tukey’s HSD was used as a post hoc test. Semi-quantitative data were presented as “Mean ± Standard Deviation” and “Median (Minimum-Maximum)” and the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by the Dunn–Bonferroni–test was used in the analysis. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Biochemical findings

MDA, tGSH, SOD, and CAT in cheek tissue

As shown in Table 1, HR alone significantly reduced MDA production in the cheek tissue compared to the healthy control group while increasing tGSH production (p < 0.001). Furthermore, HR significantly inhibited the HCQ-induced increase in MDA levels and the decrease in tGSH levels in cheek tissue (p < 0.001). No statistically significant difference was found in MDA and tGSH levels of the HG and HRHQ groups (p > 0.05). Similarly, HR alone led to an increase in SOD and CAT activities in the cheek tissue compared to the HG (p < 0.001). HR also significantly inhibited the decrease in SOD and CAT activities in the cheek tissue of HCQ-treated animals (p < 0.001).

Pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) in cheek tissue

In animals treated with HR alone, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels in cheek tissue were lower than in the HG (p < 0.001). Moreover, HR significantly (p < 0.001) suppressed the HCQ-induced increase in these pro-inflammatory cytokine levels. No statistically significant difference was found in interleukin-1β and interleukin-6 levels in the cheek tissues of the HG and HRHQ groups (p > 0.05). All biochemical analysis is offered in Table 1.

Macroscopic findings

As shown in Fig. 2A, the cheek tissue of the healthy control group appeared light pink, with no macroscopically noticeable abnormalities. Similarly, in HR group, no macroscopic damage was observed, and the cheek tissue maintained a light pink appearance similar to that of the HG group. (Fig. 2B). However, in HCQ group, edema was observed throughout the cheek tissue. Hemorrhagic cracks were present in the tissue. Additionally, while the edges of the tissues were hyperemic, the central surface appeared grayish (Fig. 2C). In the HRHQ group treated with HR, edema, hyperemia, and grayish discoloration were observed at mild levels. The cracks observed in the HCQ group were not prominent in the HRHQ group (Fig. 2D).

Histopathological findings

As shown in Fig. 3a, in the inner cheek mucosa sections of the healthy control group, the outer layer was lined with stratified squamous epithelium, and the inner portion was covered with connective tissue belonging to the mucosa. The tissue exhibited a normal histological structure with muscle tissue and capillaries. In the cheek mucosa sections of the HR group treated with HR alone, the tissue morphology exhibited a histological appearance similar to that of the HG group (Fig. 3b). In the HCQG group treated with HCQ alone, the integrity of the stratified squamous epithelium was disrupted, and scar tissue formation was observed in areas where epithelial continuity was lost. Hemorrhagic accumulation was present in the underlying connective tissue. Degeneration of muscle tissue, significant capillary dilation, congestion, and intense polymorphonuclear cell infiltration in the capillary surroundings and deep connective tissue areas were observed (Fig. 3c). In the HRHQ group treated with HR and HCQ, the epithelium maintained its continuity like the healthy control group; the muscle tissue generally exhibited a normal structure, no hemorrhage was observed in the connective tissue, and moderate capillary dilation and congestion were present. Polymorphonuclear cell (PNML) infiltration persisted in some areas of the deep connective tissue (Fig. 3d). Histopathological grading analysis is shown in Table 2.

Discussion

Originally developed as an antimalarial drug, HCQ is now used primarily for treating immune-mediated diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus, cutaneous lupus, and rheumatoid arthritis [29, 30]. It is also known to be effective in treating diabetes, human immunodeficiency virus, fungal infections, and various dermatological disorders [31]. However, despite this broad therapeutic profile, there is extensive evidence of HCQ’s systemic toxicity [3, 6, 11]. These toxic effects include severe conditions such as cardiomyopathy and neuromyotoxicity, as well as gastrointestinal disturbances, allergic reactions, dermatological side effects, and stomatitis [6, 8, 32–36]. Clinical and preclinical studies have shown that oxidative stress in tissues is the primary mechanism underlying HCQ’s toxic effects [37, 38]. Furthermore, an in vitro study demonstrated significant genotoxic effects of HCQ, such as oxidative DNA damage and mutation [39]. Oxidative stress is defined as an imbalance between increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and antioxidant defense mechanisms. HCQ has been shown to induce excessive ROS production in the body through oxidative stress, reduce the activities of ROS-scavenging enzymes (SOD, CAT, and glutathione peroxidase), and increase MDA levels, which serve as an indicator of lipid peroxidation [40]. Topak et al. (2023) demonstrated that oral HCQ administration in rats increased MDA levels, reduced SOD and CAT activity, and negatively affected fracture healing by inducing oxidative stress [41]. In the present study, MDA levels were increased, while tGSH, SOD, and CAT levels were decreased in the cheek mucosal tissue of the HCQ-treated groups. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating HCQ-induced oxidative stress in various tissues [34–38]. Contrary to this findings, Zannah et al. (2015) reported that HCQ, when used in combination with oral antidiabetic drugs, protected the pancreas against oxidative stress and exerted hepatoprotective effects [42]. Similarly, a separate study by Ahmed et al. (2022) reported that HCQ treatment increased total antioxidant activity and reduced lipid peroxidation [43]. These experimental results indicate that HCQ causes oxidative stress, while some previous studies HCQ reduces oxidative stress. These contradictions indicate that more research on HCQ is required.

In the groups where HR was administered to investigate its effects against HCQ-induced stomatitis, MDA, tGSH, SOD, and CAT enzyme levels were observed to be maintained at levels similar to those in the healthy control group. In the literature, HR has been reported to exert beneficial effects in various tissues by reducing oxidative stress-induced tissue damage through its antioxidant properties. Erhan et al. (2017) demonstrated that HR prevented methotrexate-induced oropharyngeal oxidative damage in rats [24]. Priyadarshi et al. (2022) reported that HR leaf extract improved bioenergetic activation in burn wound healing by reducing oxidative stress and inflammation [44]. Similarly, another study suggested that HR could be beneficial in acrylamide-induced neurotoxicity by preventing the increase in MDA levels and the decrease in tGSH levels in brain tissue [45, 46]. The findings obtained in the present study are consistent with also the vitro experiment in literature. Lipophilic (carotenoids) and hydrophilic antioxidants (phenolic compounds, ascorbic acid) found in the structure of HR play a fundamental role against oxidative damage. In a vitro study, exposure to UV radiation or H₂O₂ induced oxidative stress in human skin fibroblasts. Incubation with ascorbic acid caused a reduction in the level of oxidative stress [47]. In another study, reported that β-carotene decreases the accumulation of ROS in undifferentiated rat pheochromocytoma (PC12) cells exposed to hydrogen peroxide [48].

A direct link between ROS levels, oxidative stress, and inflammatory conditions has been demonstrated in the literature [49]. This relationship is also considered a potential mechanism for inflammatory diseases of the oral cavity and potential neoplastic lesions [50]. In the present study, increased levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) were detected in the cheek tissue of the HCQ-treated group. This finding suggests that HCQ may contribute to stomatitis development by triggering the inflammatory response. However, some studies have reported that HCQ may exhibit anti-inflammatory properties by inhibiting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in tissues [51]. Hu et al. (2022) demonstrated that HCQ alleviated neuro-inflammation by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in brain injury caused by cortical impact in mice [52]. Similarly, Wakiya et al. (2022) reported that HCQ use in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus decreased serum TNF-α and IL-6 levels [53]. This experimental results indicate that HCQ increases cytokine levels and causes inflammation, which conflicts with the anti-inflammatory response exhibited by HCQ by lowering cytokine levels as shown in other studies. Additionally, in the group where HR was administered alone, TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels were lower than the healthy control group. When HR was co-administered with HCQ, it suppressed the increase in cytokine levels, thereby mitigating HCQ-induced inflammation. Several studies have reported the therapeutic effects of HR on dermatological lesions [54, 55]. In addition to its potent antioxidant properties, its strong anti-inflammatory effects have been demonstrated in various studies [20]. One study showed that peritoneal macrophages isolated from Balb/c mice and treated with HR exhibited significant suppression of LPS-induced increases in pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 [56]. Gu et al. (2022) demonstrated that topical application of total flavonoids of HR reduced cytokine levels such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in mice and improved atopic dermatitis-like lesions [57]. Flavonoids, carotenoids, tocopherols, tocotrienols and sitosterols vs. found in the structure of HR are known to be responsible for anti-inflammatory activity. An in vitro study reported that purified HR flavonoids inhibited the expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 [58]. In addition, it has been reported that isorhamnetin inhibits TNF-α-induced inflammation and proliferation by regulating the flavonoid MAPK and NF-κB signaling pathways [58].

The results obtained in the present study are consistent with the literature and emphasize the anti-inflammatory activity of HR and its therapeutic potential in HCQ-induced stomatitis.

In the present study, biochemical findings in inner cheek tissue were supported by both macroscopic and histopathological evaluations. In previous study, it was observed that HR alleviated 5-fluorouracil-induced edema, hyperemia, and hemorrhage in cheek tissue [22]. The histopathological findings of this study demonstrated that HCQ caused severe damage to the oral mucosa, particularly disrupting epithelial integrity, leading to muscle tissue degeneration, hemorrhage in connective tissue, and dense polymorphonuclear cell infiltration. In contrast, in the HR-treated group, epithelial integrity was largely preserved, and inflammatory damage was minimal. Kuduban et al. (2016) reported that HR reduced elevated MDA, IL-1β, and TNF-α levels caused by methotrexate in the cheek, lower lip, and tongue tissues, reversing histopathological damage [26]. Similarly, in another study by Erhan et al. (2017) investigating the beneficial effects of HR on mucosal damage, rats treated with HR exhibited healing in oropharyngeal tissues similar to healthy tissue [24]. The findings obtained in the present study are consistent with these reports, clearly demonstrating the protective effects of HR on tissue morphology and histological structure.

Safety considerations: H. Rhamnoides extracts are widely used as foods/supplements and have demonstrated favorable tolerability in preclinical and clinical contexts reported in the literature. In our experiment, rats receiving HR alone exhibited no macroscopic oral damage and normal cheek histology, consistent with a benign local tissue profile under the present regimen. While these results are reassuring for short-term use in this model, definitive human safety requires dose-finding and controlled clinical evaluation specific to HCQ-treated populations.

Conclusion

In summary, the results of the current research indicate that the antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of HR may be effective in the care of HCQ-induced stomatitis. Due to its polyphenolic compounds and vitamins, HR may play a protective role in the oral mucosa by reducing oxidative stress and suppressing inflammation. Although various studies have reported these effects of HR, this study provides novel insights by specifically examining its effects on HCQ-induced stomatitis. The results indicate that HR may be a potential candidate for preventing and treating stomatitis associated with HCQ use. Adding HR to the treatment protocol for patients with Rheumatoid Arthritis, Primary Sjögren’s Syndrome, COVID-19, and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus who are currently taking HCQ may be a new way to reduce and/or prevent the side effects of HCQ.HR.
