A real-world study of endoscopic and fluoroscopic management of benign colorectal strictures: clinical and cost-effectiveness outcomes
Authors: Yuyan Liu, Guangshao Cao, Yonghua Bi, Ping Liu, Xinwei Han
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVarious endoscopic and interventional techniques have been employed in the management of benign colorectal strictures (BCS), but the optimal treatment strategy has not been established. Seventeen patients in the Stent group preferred stent placement as the first treatment, while 12 patients in the EI group preferred endoscopic incision, with or without balloon dilation. After dilation, stricture length and stricture rates decreased significant, and stricture diameter increased significant in LB Group (P < 0.0001). Mild bleeding and abdominal pain were the common minor complications occurred in either group. Background

Benign colorectal strictures (BCS) are a common disease, with inflammatory stricture and postoperative anastomotic strictures being the most common [1]. Patients with BCS often complain of obstructive symptoms such as abdominal pain, abdominal distension, and cessation of defecation. Medical treatment is of little benefit in treating such cases, while traditional surgical treatment often implies a traumatic operation. Endoscopic or fluoroscopic dilation offers a safe and efficient alternative treatment to conventional surgery. Various endoscopic and interventional techniques have been employed in the management of BCS, including balloon dilation, stent placement and endoscopic incision, and so on. Among these, balloon dilation is the most common intervention, and its safety and efficacy have been established through numerous studies [2–7]. However, the optimal treatment strategy has not been established and recurrence of stricture remains a big challenge, often requiring repeated dilation procedures in order to achieve clinical success [1, 2, 8], leading to an increased cost of hospitalization and impaired health-related quality of life [9].

Similar problems have been observed in benign esophageal stenosis, and according to previous reports, large balloon dilation can reduce recurrence rates of stricture and improve clinical efficacy [10, 11]. In this real-world study, we divided the patients into four groups based on their preferred treatment regimens: large balloon first, small balloon first, stent first, and endoscopic incision first. The purpose of this study was to compare the difference in the clinical outcomes and cost-effectiveness of each method to explore the possible optimal therapeutic strategy.

Patients and methods

Patients

This retrospective study was approved by Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was waived due to retrospective nature. A retrospective study of all patients of BCS, endoscopically and fluoroscopic treated between October 2012 and February 2025, was conducted. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age between 14 and 82 years; (2) Diagnosed with BCS through colonoscopy and/or colonography, as well as abdominal computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), including stenosis caused by inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and anastomotic stenosis. All patients underwent abdominal CT and/or MRI to rule out malignant stenosis, and endoscopic examination and biopsy were performed when necessary for pathological diagnosis (Fig. 1A, B). Exclusion criteria: (1) Concurrent colorectal leakage or fistula; (2) Recurrent tumor stenosis after colorectal surgery, or malignant stenosis caused by malignant tumors; (3) Pregnant women.

A total of 76 consecutive patients (47 men, 29 women; mean age 55.0 ± 14.9 years, range 14–82 years) were reviewed. In the LB group, 15 patients were treated by means of large balloon (diameter: 20–26 mm) dilation first, while in the SB group 32 patients underwent dilation with small balloon (diameter: 10–18 mm) dilation first. Seventeen patients in the Stent group received stent placement as the first treatment, while 12 patients in the EI group received endoscopic incision, with or without balloon dilation. The technical and clinical success, complications, number of dilation sessions, disease-free time intervals, and the overall cost of each group were evaluated and compared.

Endoscopic techniques

The endoscopic technique was performed under direct vision, with patients under conscious analgesia and sedation. A pediatric endoscopy (11 mm in diameter) can also be used if adult endoscopy fails to pass through the stricture. A stiff steel guidewire was inserted through the endoscopic operator channel to across the stricture. The deflated balloon is introduced and advanced through the stenosis under direct visualization. After the balloon passes through the stricture, it was gradually inflated with water. For patients in the Stent group, a stent was advanced through the stricture along the stiff guidewire and released. For patients in the EI group, endoscopic radial incision was conducted with an electric loop or IT knife, followed by balloon dilation or not.

Fluoroscopic techniques

All fluoroscopic procedures were conducted in the Interventional Radiology Department, with patients under conscious condition. A guidewire and a 5 F multifunctional catheter are inserted through the anus. With the assistance of the guidewire, they pass through the narrowed or obstructed sections of the bowel. A stiff guidewire is then exchanged, and a balloon catheter is delivered for dilation (Fig. 1C, D). For patients in the Stent group, a zebra guidewire is inserted, along which a stent is delivered and released. Vital signs including blood pressure and pulse oximetry were monitored for 6–8 h after treatment.

Definitions and end points

BCS are defined as a narrowing of the bowel lumen that cannot be passed through a colonoscopy of 10 mm in diameter, whether or not there are obstructive symptoms [6, 12]. The stricture rate was calculated using the following formula: 100%-stricture diameter/estimated normal intestinal diameter × 100%. The estimated normal diameter is the mean diameters of the upper and lower segments of the stricture. A mild stricture was defined as an intestinal narrowing through which the adult colonoscope could be passed with no resistance or stricture rate less than 80%. A moderate severe stricture was defined as a narrowing that offered resistance to passage of the colonoscope, or stricture rate of 80% to 90%. A severe stricture was defined as a narrowing or occlusion through which the conoloscope could not be passed, or stricture rate more than 90%.

Technical success was defined as the achievement of a bowel lumen wide enough to allow passage of a standard 13-mm diameter adult colonoscopy after treatment, with no severe complications such as bowel perforation, major bleeding, and stent migration. For patients treated in Interventional Radiology without colonoscopy, technical success was determined based on contrast passage, disappearance of balloon waist, and the decrease of stricture rates by at least 20%. Clinical success, the primary end point, is defined as a significant improved symptoms or symptoms resolution during the initial or subsequent session 2 weeks later. Clinical cure is defined as a successful relief of stricture without symptoms (such as abdominal pain, distention, or astriction) for more than one year. Clinical improvement is defined as successful reliefs of stenosis with mild symptoms that are significantly improved compared to before treatment and persist for at least 6 months. Ineffectiveness is defined as a stricture that fails to be relieved, or symptoms relieved for less than 6 months, or are transferred to surgical resection or stoma. The secondary end points were complications and the recurrence of stricture. Complications are divided into major complications, such as perioperative death, severe pain that makes dilation unbearable, stent migration or restenosis, colorectal perforation, massive bleeding; and minor complications, such as mild abdominal pain and minor bleeding. Treatment duration is defined as the time interval between the first balloon dilation or stenting and the last balloon dilation or stenting treatment.

Follow-up

For patients with incomplete dilations and stent placement, endoscopic and/or abdominal CT follow-up was scheduled every 1–2 months and upon symptom occurrence. Symptom recurrence, bowel status and cause of death were collected by telephone or outpatient follow-up. The final follow-up date was April 25, 2025. Treatment is repeated for the patient with a recurrence of stenosis.

Cost analysis

A cost analysis among four groups was performed, taking the following parameters into account: cost of hospitalization, personnel costs, cost of materials used during each procedure, cost for each CT and colonoscopy examination, the total number of treatment sessions and hospitalizations required for a cure of stricture. Total hospital stay and hospital stay after procedure for each treatment session. Treatment duration was defined as interval from the initial treatment to last treatment until the cure or failure of stricture.

Statistical analysis

Data were shown as median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-parametric distributions or means ± standard deviation for normal distributions. Comparative analysis was performed by Chi-square tests and one-way analysis of variance followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests. The disease-free survival and 1-year patency rate differences among four groups’ patients were demonstrated by Kaplan–Meier survival curves. The log-rank test was used for test the differences among the survival curves. A P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The statistical software of GraphPad (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, Calif) was used in this study.

Results

Patients

There were 62 cases of anastomotic stricture and 14 cases of inflammatory bowel disease. The indications for surgical resection were rectal (n = 37), colon (n = 11), or distal sigmoid colon (n = 2) carcinoma in 50 cases, intestinal perforation or intestinal torsion in 6 cases, and other cancer infiltration in 5 cases, and one case of colitis gravis. For patients with history of surgery for malignancy, anastomotic biopsy was performed before treatment to exclude the recurrence of tumor. A biopsy was also carried out during follow-up after treatment to determine whether the tumor had recurred. A total of 67 patients were diagnosed by colonoscopy with biopsy, 4 patients were diagnosed by biopsy under fluoroscopy or CT, two patients examined by PET-CT to exclude malignancy, and only 3 patients who underwent surgical resection for benign lesions were examined by CT without biopsy.

Fifty-nine (77.6%) patients were symptomatic at presentation, and presented with abdominal distention, abdominal pain, constipation, feeling of incomplete defecation, stringy stools, or signs of obstructive ileus (Table 1). The remaining 17 asymptomatic patients were unintentionally discovered strictures during follow-up colonoscopy and underwent dilation due to the need to observe the proximal colon. Median distance of the stricture from the anal verge was 10.0 cm (IQR 7.0–17.5 cm). The median time interval between stricture and surgery was 10.2 months (IQR 5.9–23.4 months).

Technical outcomes

As shown in Table 2, the technical success rates were 92.3% in the LB group, 78.9% in the SB group, 71.4% in the Stent group, and 84.4% in the EI group, respectively (P = 0.1481). Recurrence of stricture and colorectal perforation were the main reasons for technical failures. In the LB group, 26 dilation procedures were performed, with a median of 1.0 (IQR 1.0–2.0) dilation per patient. Only one session of dilation was conducted in 9 patients, while two sessions were performed in 4 patients and more three sessions were required in 2 patients. In the SB group, a total of 57 dilation procedures were performed, with a median of 1.0 (IQR 1.0–2.0) dilation per patient. Twenty-three patients had one session, 4 patients had two sessions, and 5 patients had 3 to 9 sessions to maintain successful clinical outcome. The average treatment procedures were 3.0 (IQR 2.0–4.0) and 2.0 (2.0–4.8.0.8) per patient in the Stent and the EI groups, respectively. After dilation, stricture length and stricture rates decreased significant, and stricture diameter increased significant in the LB group (P < 0.0001).

Cost analysis

The stent first technique was found to be statistically significantly (P = 0.0072) more expensive (median 2.9 × 104 Chinese Yuan or 4155 USD) compared with endoscopic incision first (median 1.0 × 104 Chinese Yuan or 1418 USD) (Table 2). Longer hospital stay after procedure and total hospital stay were observed in the LB and Stent groups compared with the EI group (P < 0.0001).

Complications

No severe complications, such as massive bleeding and death, were observed among four groups. Self-restricted mild bleeding and abdominal pain were the common minor complications occurred in either group (Table 3). Colorectal perforations occurred in 6 dilations: 2 (7.7%) in the LB group, 3 (5.3%) in the SB group and 1 (2.2%) in the EI group, respectively (P = 0.2629). Two patients with peritonitis underwent surgical resection and stoma 1 and 7 days after perforation, and the remaining 4 patients without peritonitis healed spontaneously after conservative treatment (Fig. 1E, F).

Complications, clinical outcomes and follow-up

Stent-related complications were observed in 8 patients (47.1%) during 13 sessions (44.8%) of stenting in the Stent group, including stent restenosis in 4 patients (5 sessions), stent migration in 2 patients (5 sessions) and stent intolerance due to pain in 2 patients (2 sessions).

Clinical efficacy

A total of 29 patients (38.2%) were clinically cured: 7 (46.7%) in the LB group, 15 (50.0%) in the SB group, 3 (17.6%) in the Stent group and 3 (25.0%) in the EI group (P = 0.1223), respectively. The clinical efficacy rates were 73.3%, 62.5%, 47.1% and 75.0% in the LB, SB, Stent, and EI groups, respectively (P = 0.3492). There is a numerical trend toward the highest clinical efficacy in LB and EI groups, but the difference was not statistically significant (Table 3).

Follow-up

Due to the small number of lost follow-ups (n = 5), we excluded them from the analysis of patency rates and efficacy evaluations. The median interval between treatment and stricture recurrence were 39.5 months in the LB group, 27.9 months in the SB group, 5.2 months in the Stent group, and 9.5 months in the EI groups, respectively. The 1-year patency rates were 53.8%, 58.2%, 30.0% and 36.7% in the LB, SB, Stent, and EI groups, respectively (P = 0.1492). Patients in the LB and SB groups were characterized by extended 1-year patency rate and disease-free survival time intervals, compared with patients in the Stent and EI groups (Fig. 2). Nevertheless, this difference is not statistically significant (P = 0.1492). In addition, patients in the Stent group (76.5%) and EI group (75.0%) presented a higher stricture recurrence after first treatment session than did patients in the LB and SB groups. However, recurrence rate did not differ statistically significantly among four groups.

Discussion

The exact pathophysiology of BCS is not fully understood, and an optimal therapeutic strategy has not been established [1]. A wide range of fluoroscopic and endoscopic techniques have been employed to treat BCS, such as mechanical dilation by fixed-diameter push-type dilators or expanding radial balloon dilators, laser stricturotomy or electroincision with or without mechanical dilation [13], and temporary placement of plastic or self-expandable metal stents [14–16].

One of the first treatments have been employed to treat BCS is Bougienage. Werre et al. [17] treated benign anastomosis stricture by Savary–Gilliard bougies in 15 patients, ten of whom underwent 1 to 3 dilation sessions and showed no symptoms recurrence during follow-up. The remaining 5 patients required at least 4 dilations, and normal defecation was partially restored. Balloon dilation, first described by Brower and Freeman, has been used to treat an adult patient with rectal stricture [18], followed by a large number of studies that render balloon dilation as the most common method [19, 20].

Despite the success rates of balloon dilation for BCS being as high as 86% to 97% [21], most studies show a high recurrence rate of stricture after dilation [1, 8], requiring multiple repeated dilation procedures to achieve and maintain adequate clinical efficacy, which significantly increases the total cost of treatment and impaired health-related quality of life [9]. For example, Johansson et al. reported a re-stricture frequency of 30%, with a repeated dilation rate as high as 88% [22]. Similar problems have been observed in benign esophageal stenosis, and according to previous reports, large balloon dilation can reduce recurrence rates of stricture and improve clinical efficacy [10, 11]. In this real-world study, we divided the patients into four groups based on their preferred treatment regimens and compared the differences in the clinical outcomes and cost-effectiveness in order to explore the possible optimal therapeutic strategy.

The role of large balloon dilation in the management of BCS has rarely been studied [6, 21]. In contrast, it has been applied in benign esophageal strictures and shows a better efficacy without increasing risk of complications [10, 11, 23]. The main concern with its implementation is the fear of putative high risk of complications, particularly the potential increased risk of perforation.

Stent first method proved less effective in patients with BCS, considering the more hospitalization cost, increased treatment sessions required and the longer hospital stay compared with other groups. Patients in LB and SB group presented a trend towards lower rates of disease recurrence after first dilation compared with the Stent and EI groups, as well as more extended stenosis-free time intervals i.e., 39.5 months compared with 5.2 months, respectively. However, these observations were not statistically significantly different. The success rate appears higher in the EI group, which is likely related to the addition of balloon dilation. Among these patients, 8 received both balloon dilation and EI treatment. It seems that most patients in the EI group had severe stricture (66.7%), which may be the reason for higher recurrence rate after first treatment session (75.0%). However, it should be noted that the assessment of stricture severity in the EI group primarily relies on colonoscopy rather than colonography. Since some patients lack data on colonography, using colonoscopy to evaluate stricture may overestimate its severity.

The safety outcomes were equivalent with the four techniques. Mild bleeding and abdominal pain were the common minor complications occurred in either group. However, an indisputable disadvantage of the stent first technique, compared with other three groups, is the high hospitalization cost, more treatment sessions and the high stent-related complications, including stent restenosis in 4 patients (5 sessions), stent migration in 2 patients (5 sessions) and stent intolerance due to pain in 2 patients (2 sessions). For patients with Crohn’s disease-related BCS, the major complications rate for balloon dilation was 2%, and the perforation rate was 3.7% [4]. In this study, colorectal perforations occurred in 6 dilations: 2 (7.7%) in the LB group, 3 (5.3%) in the SB group and 1 (2.2%) in the EI group, respectively. Interestingly, the large balloon dilation did not significantly increase the risk of perforation. However, although not statistically significant, perforations were numerically higher in the LB group than SB and EI groups. Given that perforation is a sentinel adverse event, this numerical trend may be clinically important and should be taken seriously. With a larger sample, it may have reached significance.

This study has some limitations. This is a small-sample, single-center retrospective study, which may have selection bias and confounding factors. The samples in LB and EI Groups are small, which may bring certain biases and require larger studies to confirm the conclusions. There is a inadequate data of colonography in EI Group and no data are presented on stricture angulations or presence of more information in IBD strictures, such as active inflammation, standardized questionnaires or validated PRO measures or medical therapy, which are clinically important. The SB and Stent groups contained proportionally more IBD patients than the LB and EI groups, although not statistically significant. Since IBD strictures are longer, more fibrotic, and more prone to recurrence, this difference likely biased outcomes against the SB and Stent groups and in favor of the LB and EI groups.

Conclusions

Balloon dilation, stenting and endoscopic incision are safe and effective for the treatment of BCS. From the economical point of view, stent placement first with/without dilation, associated with more treatment sessions, increased hospitalization cost and stent-related complications, should not be the preferred treatment for BCS, primarily anastomotic BCS.
