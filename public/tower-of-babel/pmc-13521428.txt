Psychometric Properties of Ecological Momentary Assessment of Psychosocial Functioning in Adults With Complications Related to Type 1 Diabetes
Authors: Hayley Centola, Melissa Plegue, Dana Albright, Kara Mizokami-Stout, Lynn Ang, Jennifer Iyengar, Emily Hirschfeld, Anna Kratz, Evan L. Reynolds, Brian C. Callaghan, Rodica Pop-Busui, Joyce M. Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) is a promising methodology for examining the contextual impact of psychosocial functioning on chronic disease management in individuals with type 1 diabetes (T1D). Little is known regarding psychometric properties of EMA of psychosocial functioning in adults with complications related to T1D. The aim of this study is to explore the feasibility, reliability, and validity of EMA psychosocial measures examining depression, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, and fear of hypoglycemia in a sample of adult individuals with complications related to T1D.Methods:Fifty-one participants with T1D completed a 14-day EMA of symptoms of depression, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, and fear of hypoglycemia. Multilevel linear models were used to evaluate the association of EMA psychosocial questions with standard psychosocial measures.Results:Feasibility was demonstrated via high response rates across the 14-day EMA response period (3 times daily). All 4 EMA measures (depression, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, and fear of hypoglycemia) showed meaningful (i.e. ≥25%) within-person variability (from 28.7% in diabetes-related distress to 46.5% in fear of hypoglycemia). All measures exceeded between-person reliability of 0.8 within 1 to 3 days. None of the measures demonstrated significant diurnal patterns. Convergent validity was established via significant correlations between all aggregate EMA measures with corresponding “gold standard” surveys.Conclusions:Overall, findings support the psychometric properties (feasibility, reliability, and validity) of EMA of psychosocial measures in an adult cohort with complications related to T1D. This is the first study to date to examine psychometric properties of EMA of psychosocial measures examining depression, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, and fear of hypoglycemia simultaneously in an adult population with complications related to T1D.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is a chronic disease requiring considerable daily self-management to successfully regulate blood glucose (BG) levels and mitigate the risk of dangerous health complications. There exists a reciprocal association between psychosocial functioning and daily fluctuations of BG levels [1]. For example, mood, anxiety, and stress levels vary throughout the day and may indirectly impact diabetes management by decreasing attention to glucose testing, altering eating behaviours, and reducing sleep quality, as well as directly impact glycemic control via release of cortisol [2]. Simultaneously, cumulative fluctuations in BG levels may contribute to increased levels of perceived stress highlighting the need to refine interventions targeting daily disease stressors [3].

In addition to diabetes-related distress, adults with T1D may experience elevated levels of depressive symptoms, anxiety symptoms, and fear of hypoglycemia, all of which may reciprocally impact glycemic outcomes [4,5]. These psychosocial factors fluctuate in the context of real-world variables highlighting the significance of the impact of contextual factors on the daily burden of management. Specifically, successful self-management requires navigating ever-changing situational and organizational factors that interact with social factors (e.g. competing social demands) [6]. In-the-moment measurements of psychosocial functioning may therefore elucidate the dynamics of psychosocial factors and self-management behaviours.

Compared with traditional survey-based measures, the use of experience-sampling methods such as Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA; real-time self-report) methodology has demonstrated utility in capturing the daily dynamism of relevant psychosocial factors (e.g. mood, anxiety, stress) [7]. EMA allows for an examination of within-person variability, or individual patterns of symptom fluctuation, which are essential features of psychological constructs such as depression and anxiety [8]. EMA has become an increasingly popular methodology as it provides valuable real-time data that minimizes recall bias through integration into the day-to-day lives of study participants [8]. This style of responding considers dynamic patterns of change and temporal factors that impact daily decision-making, which are embedded in management of chronic illnesses such as T1D. Furthermore, by enhancing our collective understanding of changing diurnal symptom patterns outside of clinic settings, measurement reliability is increased [9,10]. EMA can also serve to personalize intervention strategies [11,12] by integrating targeted interventions for distress, depression, and anxiety symptoms into the daily lives of individuals with T1D [11,13].

Despite the importance of ensuring reliable and valid EMA of psychosocial variables, little research has been done in populations with T1D. Murray and colleagues [14] theorized that this omission may be due to presumed confidence in the psychometric properties of EMA questions adapted from previously validated measures as well as lack of methodologic guidance. Recent research therefore highlights the significance of examining psychometric properties of EMA measures at the within- and between-person levels while simultaneously ensuring brevity in assessment [14]. Ehrmann and colleagues [15] recently conducted an observational study examining the relationship between glycemic outcomes and an EMA measurement of diabetes-related distress as well as the relationship between EMA diabetes-related distress and questionnaire-based diabetes-related distress. They found significant associations between EMA-derived measures and questionnaire-based diabetes-related distress, providing evidence for sound psychometric properties of EMA measurement of diabetes-related distress. In another recent study, Hernandez and colleagues [16] examined within- and between-person variability in the response times to noncognitive (e.g. mood, stress, activity) EMA questions in a sample of adults with T1D. Overall, they provided sound psychometric support for EMA response times as indicators of processing speeds, both momentary and average [16]. Other studies have also used EMA in the T1D population [17–19], although less research exists regarding psychometric properties of these measures.

Greater understanding of the psychometric properties of a comprehensive set of EMA psychosocial measures would provide valuable insight into the daily experience of symptoms and may provide the opportunity for future use of these measures for assessment and development of psychosocial interventions for populations with T1D. Therefore, our objective in this study was to explore the feasibility, reliability, and validity of the EMA psychosocial measures examining depression, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, and fear of hypoglycemia in a sample of adult individuals with complications related to T1D. This includes evaluation of participant response rates, within- and between-person variability to assess the intraclass correlations (ICCs) and between-person reliability of depression, anxiety, distress, and fear of hypoglycemia, and the association of EMA psychosocial measures with standard measures of depression, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, and fear of hypoglycemia (convergent validity).

Methods

Participants

Individuals were eligible for the study if they had a diagnosis of T1D, previously agreed upon to be part of a phenotyping study [20–23], which was comprised of participants with T1D and diabetes-related complications. Eligibility criteria also specified that participants be fluent in spoken and written English and willing to carry their mobile phone during the home monitoring period with daily access to cellular or WiFi connectivity. Participants were deemed ineligible for this study if they experienced any social or medical condition that would prevent complete participation in the study or would pose significant hazard to the subject’s participation.

Study procedures

This study was approved by the medical institutional review board of the University of Michigan. Participation in this study was voluntary and all participants provided written informed consent for the main study procedures, which consisted of 2 in-person visits over a 2-year observational period. Study-visit procedures included medical history and examination, laboratory measures, heart rate variability testing, cognitive testing, and surveys. Participants were given the option to indicate separate consent to additional procedures, including the 14-day home monitoring period with EMA questions immediately following baseline study visit 1. For participants who provided consent for this study component, an additional payment of $3 per day was conferred to those who completed the EMA questions (with maximum $42 provided for the 14-day EMA period). At baseline, written informed consent was obtained and study participants completed demographic, clinical, and psychosocial surveys using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at the University of Michigan [24,25].

Baseline measures

Depressive symptoms were measured via several questionnaires to fully examine the nuance of both state and trait experiences of psychosocial functioning. The 8-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-8) was used to measure clinical symptoms of depression. The PHQ-8 is a widely used measure of depressive symptoms. Respondents rate the frequency of depressive symptoms on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), with higher scores indicating the presence of more symptoms. Scores range from 0 to 24. The PHQ-8 has been validated for use in many populations and settings [26]. Other aspects of low mood and internalizing symptoms were also measured via the Adult Sickle Cell Quality of Life Measurement Information System (ASCQ-Me) Emotional Impact Short Form version 2.0 [27] and the National Institutes of Health Toolbox for Assessment of Neurological and Behavioral Function (NIH Toolbox) Sadness Fixed Form Age 18+ version 2.0 (8 items) [28]. The NIH Toolbox Sadness Fixed Form allows for exploration of nonclinical experience of sadness. Higher scores on the NIH Toolbox Sadness Fixed Form Age 18+ indicate more sadness with T scores ≥60 suggestive of high levels of sadness and more surveillance or concern. Scores range from 8 to 40. The ASCQ-Me Emotional Impact Short Form assesses an individual’s experience of depressive symptoms by capturing the emotional impact of a health condition (e.g. “How lonely did you feel because of your health problems?” or “How much did you worry about getting sick?” [27]), allowing for exploration of health-related experience of depression and anxiety. Higher scores indicate lower levels of emotional distress and lower impact of emotional distress with scores ranging from 5 to 25.

Anxiety symptoms corresponding to the clinical criteria for generalized anxiety disorder were assessed via the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item (GAD-7) scale [29], a 7-item self-report measure validated for use in a variety of settings. Respondents rate the frequency of anxiety-related symptoms on a 4-point Likert scale from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), with higher scores indicating the presence of more symptoms. Scores range from 0 to 21. The impact of anxiety symptoms on quality of life were also assessed via the Quality of Life in Neurological Disorders (Neuro-QoL) Short Form Version 1.0 Anxiety, an 8-item validated measure of quality of life specific to neurologic disorders [30]. Scores on the Neuro-QoL range from 8 to 40. The Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) [31,32] was also used to examine the impact of stress on daily life (nonclinical). The PSS-10 is a 10-item self-report measure of the perception of stress including the appraisal of life circumstances as unpredictable and uncontrollable [31], with higher scores indicating the presence of more symptoms. PSS-10 scores range from 0 to 40. The PSS-10 was selected as a convergent measure.

Diabetes-related distress symptoms were measured with the Diabetes Distress Scale (DDS17) [33], a 17-item questionnaire used to measure diabetes-related distress, including emotional burden, regimen-related distress, physician-related distress, and interpersonal distress, with higher scores indicating the presence of higher levels of diabetes-related distress. The scale yields an overall average distress score that ranges from 1 to 6. Diabetes-related distress symptoms were also measured with the Problem Areas in Diabetes (PAID) scale [34], a 20-item self-report questionnaire that examines negative emotions related to diabetes (e.g. fear, anger, frustration) commonly experienced by patients with diabetes, with higher scores indicating greater levels of diabetes-related distress. Scores range from 0 to 100.

Fear of hypoglycemia was measured using the Hypoglycemia Fear Survey II (HFS-II) [35]. The HFS-II is a 33-item self-report questionnaire validated for use in individuals with type 1 diabetes to assess their fear of low blood sugar, including worries about future hypoglycemic episodes as well as avoidance behaviours. Respondents rate the frequency of occurrence of each item on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (never) to 4 (always), with higher scores indicating the presence of more symptoms. Scores range from 0 to 132. The HFS-II has displayed good reliability and validity in adults with type 1 diabetes [35]. The HFS-II has 2 subscales, the Worry subscale (18-items), comprised of items focusing on worries about the consequences of hypoglycemia, and the Behaviour subscale (15-items), focused on behaviours taken to avoid or minimize hypoglycemia. Scores on the Worry subscale range from 0 to 72 and scores on the Behaviour subscale range from 0 to 60.

EMA questions

After completion of baseline measures, individuals participated in a 14-day EMA reporting period using the LifeData RealLife Exp app during the following 3 intervals: wake to 2:00 PM (morning), 2:00 PM to 6:00 PM (midday), and 6:00 PM to bedtime (evening). There were 3 daily EMA alerts capturing symptoms of depression, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, and fear of hypoglycemia; the third alert was combined with an end-of-day diary capturing daily diabetes-related distress, fear of hypoglycemia, and pain. Specific timing of the prompt was at random via the platform during the session windows with 2 additional reminders automatically sent at 5 and 10 minutes after the initial notification prompt if participants did not respond. Sessions expired within the time window to prevent participants from completing more than 1 session at once. EMA questions were adapted from validated measures described in what follows. The study team opted to use a consistent scale (numeric rating scale), ranging from 0 (no symptoms) to 10 (worst imaginable), across all EMA prompts to maximize the readability of the EMA items and reduce the cognitive load on participants. This decision was made after beta testing with the EMA battery.

Depressive symptoms were assessed “in the moment” via a single-item question from the NIH Toolbox Sadness (ages 18+) [30], “How depressed do you feel, right now?” and assessed via an 11-point Likert scale from 0 (no symptoms) to 10 (worst imaginable).

Anxiety symptoms were determined using a single item from the GAD-7 questionnaire [29], “How anxious do you feel, right now?” and assessed via an 11-point Likert scale from 0 (no symptoms) to 10 (worst imaginable).

Diabetes-related distress was measured with a single item from the DDS17 [33], “Are you feeling overwhelmed with the demands of living with diabetes, right now?” and assessed via a 6-point Likert scale from 0 (not a problem) to 5 (a very serious problem).

Finally, fear of hypoglycemia was assessed via a single item from the HFS-II [35], “How worried are you about a low blood sugar, right now?” and assessed via a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (not worried) to 4 (very worried).

End-of-study measures

The same survey measures just described that were collected at baseline (PHQ-8, ASCQ-Me, NIH Toolbox Sadness Fixed Form, GAD-7, NeuroQoL, PSS-10, DDS17, PAID, and HFS-II) were also collected at remote visit 2 (performed up to 4 days after the 14-day EMA reporting period).

Data analyses

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. Feasibility was assessed by calculating frequency of completion for each EMA prompt, including overall averages across the full 14-day monitoring period as well as average daily and within-day responses.

Within- and between-person variability for depression, anxiety, distress, and fear of hypoglycemia were calculated using null mixed effect models to demonstrate the variability in an individual’s level of psychological symptoms across the EMA period as well as how much the psychological symptoms differ between individuals. ICC values were calculated to evaluate the proportion of total variability in EMA measures that were due to between-individual differences. Between-person reliability of each measure was examined at different lengths of time (1 day, 1 to 2 days, 1 to 3 days, etc) by restricting data to the corresponding number of days of response and using the variance components of null models in the formula: Reliability = Between-person variance / (Between-person variance + [Within-person variance / n]), with n representing the average number of responses per person over the given time. Diurnal patterns of each measure were calculated to examine the amount of change in response over the course of 1 day by including time as a fixed effect in the multilevel model. Overall p values for time of day were assessed through likelihood-ratio tests.

Within-person variability was also assessed by calculating the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD) at the person level to quantify temporal instability in each of the 4 EMA measures [36]. For each participant, the differences in adjacent EMA responses were squared, averaged across all available pairs, and square-root transformed. The RMSSD was summarized across participants using the mean (standard deviation) and median (interquartile range). The reliability of the EMA measures to detect systematic within-person change over time was examined by calculating the reliability of change [37]. Using a linear mixed effects model with a fixed effect for day of EMA, a random intercept for person, and random slope for day of EMA, the reliability of change was defined as the ratio of the estimated variance for the random slope for day to the sum of the random day slope variance and the residual error variance. Finally, diurnal patterns of each measure were calculated to examine the amount of change in response over the course of a day by including time of day as a fixed effect in the multilevel model. Overall effects for time of day were evaluated through likelihood ratio tests.

Convergent validity was evaluated via Pearson correlation coefficients examining the association of EMA psychosocial questions with standard psychosocial measures for depression, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, and fear of hypoglycemia in patients with T1D. EMA measures were aggregated to the person level by averaging and correlating with end-of-study measures of the same constructs. Correlations were assessed based on the magnitude and consistency of correlations rather than rigid cutoffs, with moderate-to-strong associations interpreted as supportive of convergence, particularly given differences in measurement method and time frame [38]. All analyses were completed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, United States).

Results

A total of 51 participants were included in this study. Table 1 shows participants’ demographics.

Feasibility

Across 714 total EMA days (51 participants over 14 days), 697 days (97.6%) had a response to at least 1 of the 3 prompts. At the participant level, 43 individuals (84.3%) responded to at least 1 prompt on all 14 study days, with 12 (23.5%) completing all 42 EMA prompts. The minimum level of engagement observed was 18 completed prompts across 11 study days for a single participant.

Adherence was high throughout the day: the morning prompts had an 88.4% response rate; the midday prompts had an 87.5% response rate; and the evening prompts had an 87.2% response rate. At the participant level, 28 participants (54.9%) responded to all 14 midday prompts, compared with 21 (41.2%) in the morning and 22 (43.1%) in the evening. Most participants responded to at least 7 of 14 prompts within each time period, with 3 or less responding on fewer than 7 days for any given time-of-day prompt. No participants discontinued EMA participation before completion of the 14-day period.

Reliability

Analyses between- and within-person variability showed the measure with the lowest proportion of between-person variability to be the fear of hypoglycemia measure (53.5%), and the measure with the highest proportion of between-person variability to be diabetes-related distress (71.3%) (Table 2). All measures demonstrated high between-person reliability for the full 14 days; between 0.977 and 0.989 (Table 2), exceeding 0.80 after 1 day for depression and diabetes-related distress, after 2 days for anxiety, and 3 days for fear of hypoglycemia (Figure 1). Supplementary Figure 1 provides a visual depiction of participant-level distributions of EMA responses.

Across the 4 EMA measures, average RMSSD values were comparable relative to their respective scale ranges, indicating similar levels of moment-to-moment temporal instability. Reliability of change estimates were uniformly low (0.002 to 0.010), suggesting limited precision in detecting systematic within-person change over time relative to random error using the single-item EMA measurements. In addition, none of the measures demonstrated significant changes across different times of the day (morning, midday, evening), indicating no evidence of diurnal effects (Table 2).

Convergent validity

Convergent validity was supported with significant correlations between all aggregate EMA measures and their corresponding end-of-study measurements. Correlations ranged from 0.69 to 0.86. The ASCQ Emotional Impact score had a significant strong negative correlation due to the directionality of the measure (higher values indicate healthier status). The lowest correlation (0.69) emerged between the fear of hypoglycemia EMA aggregate and the HFS-II total score (Table 3).

It is important to note that there were generally low levels of depression, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, and fear of hypoglycemia in the sample. Only 8 participants (15.7%) had PHQ-8 scores ≥10, and 6 (11.8%) had GAD-7 scores ≥10, consistent with moderate or greater depressive or anxiety symptoms. Only 4 participants (7.8%) met the criteria for elevated diabetes-related distress (DDS ≥3.0), and 7 (13.7%) scored above the PAID cutoff of 40. Although the restricted range of symptom severity may limit generalizability to more distressed populations, the EMA measures captured variability across the lower end of validated scale ranges.

Discussion

Although EMA measurement has seen an increase in popularity as a method of assessing dynamic changes in symptoms, there historically has been a lack of empirical evidence supporting the psychometric properties of EMA measures [14,39]. Without the support of sound psychometric properties, evidence-based assessment of psychological functioning and diabetes-specific constructs (e.g. distress and fear of hypoglycemia) is compromised. Given the importance of ensuring reliable and valid measurement of these constructs to guide appropriate and effective intervention, we have aimed to examine the psychometric properties of single-item EMA psychosocial measures examining depression, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, and fear of hypoglycemia in an adult population with T1D. In their recent study, Ehrmann and colleagues [15] examined the psychometric properties of an EMA measure of diabetes-related distress, but to our knowledge our study is the first to examine psychometric properties of a comprehensive set of psychosocial and diabetes-specific EMA measures in this population. Overall, findings from our study provide evidence for the feasibility, reliability, and convergent validity of these measures for ambulatory assessment of psychosocial functioning and diabetes-specific constructs in an adult population with T1D.

Feasibility

Study findings demonstrate the feasibility of EMA questions assessing psychological functioning (depression, anxiety) and diabetes-specific constructs, including diabetes-related distress and fear of hypoglycemia. Response rates were relatively high throughout the day, with the vast majority (97.6%) of EMA days revealing a response to at least 1 of 3 prompts. Similarly high response rates have been found in other EMA studies [40], and those demonstrated in this study exceed thresholds (>70% compliance) set by other studies [12], which supports the feasibility of EMA measurement for this population. There was little variability in response rate by time of day (88.4% in morning to 87.2% in evening). Previous studies have reported a waning trend of diurnal response patterns, which has been attributed to added response burden over time [41,42]; however, our results demonstrate only a 1% decrease in completed responses throughout the day.

Reliability

Importantly, all the EMA measures demonstrated high reliability over the 14-day assessment period. Specifically, the combination of relatively high levels of between-person variability (53% to 71%) and relatively low within-person variability (29% to 47%) across all measures indicate sound reliability of EMA measurement. Regarding assessment of psychological symptoms (depression and anxiety), patterns did not demonstrate significant changes throughout the day (morning, middle, late afternoon). This is unsurprising given research supporting the stability of within-person measurement for psychological functioning, such as depression and anxiety [43], and is in contrast to other EMA study findings, such as those examining the daily dynamism of pain and fatigue in chronic pain populations (e.g. see Kratz et al [44]). These patterns of relative stability within an individual indicate that daily contextual factors may have a lessened impact on psychological functioning (e.g. depression and anxiety) for adults with T1D with T1D-related complications measured in this study. Furthermore, given the stability in symptom patterns throughout the day for this sample, compared with 3 daily EMA measurements, these findings suggest that a single daily measurement may be sufficient in capturing comparable data quality for mood and anxiety symptoms.

Diabetes-related distress demonstrated the highest reliability among all EMA measures with the highest between-person variability and lowest within-subject variability. The relatively high levels of between-person variability on this dynamic construct are to be expected given the known influence of situational factors that vary between individuals. The high between-person variability findings replicate Ehrmann et al’s [15] results for EMA measurement of participants’ diabetes-related distress. Such findings highlight the importance of person-centred approaches to intervention and the utility of EMA measurement regarding idiosyncratic associations between glycemic management and diabetes-related distress [45]. That is, through better understanding of how symptom profiles of diabetes-related distress relate to glucose excursions, treatment can be better tailored to the individual, as recently suggested by both Hermanns and colleagues [11] and Ehrmann and colleagues [45]. Furthermore, our findings surrounding the stability of diabetes-related distress via EMA measurement, coupled with previous research, also suggest that EMA measurement alone may not be sufficient to capture within-person variability. Diabetes-related distress showed modest moment-to-moment variability (RMSSD) but very low reliability of change, suggesting that, although distress varies across assessments, these single-item fluctuations do not reflect reliable systematic change over time. Future studies may consider use of event-based sampling (e.g. based on BG excursion, persistent highs/lows, or series of technology alerts) as opposed to time-based sampling (e.g. morning, afternoon, evening). In addition, the study’s low within-subject variability coupled with relatively low mean scores on the PAID and DDS17 suggest that further research may aim to examine this same construct in a population with higher average levels of diabetes-related distress. Future research in this area may help to better understand potential triggers for diabetes-related distress and associations in glycemic outcomes.

Interestingly, the other diabetes-related construct measured, fear of hypoglycemia, had both the lowest between-person variability and highest within-person variability, suggesting single-item EMA measurement of fear of hypoglycemia represents either a more dynamic state construct or more error variance compared with the other constructs measured. This finding contrasts with those of other studies such as the Hypo-METRICS study [46], which showed greater between- than within-person variability for questions focused on worries about hypoglycemia. These discrepancies in findings may be partially explained by the different samples (Hypo-METRICS sampled patients with both type 1 and type 2). In addition, other research has indicated that fear of hypoglycemia may not be stable over time, such as after traumatic experiences with hypoglycemia [47] and/or after interventions that reduce the frequency of hypoglycemia [48]; these events may be less likely to have occurred during the limited 2-week measurement period in this study for this sample of adults. Importantly, this measure also exhibited low reliability of change, suggesting that much of the observed within-person variability may reflect transient fluctuations or measurement noise rather than systematic intraindividual change.

Validity

These findings provide support for convergent validity via high levels of shared variance between EMA measures and corresponding existing self-report measures. Significant correlations were found between all aggregate EMA measures and their corresponding end-of-study measurements, suggesting that EMA is a valid form of assessment of psychosocial functioning in this population. The highest correlation emerged between EMA depression and NIH Sadness measures, which is consistent with prior research demonstrating the validity of EMA measurement of depression with existing questionnaires in a population with T1D (e.g. see Ehrmann et al [15]). EMA anxiety was also highly correlated with each of the baseline measures (GAD-7, Neuro-QoL, PSS), also consistent with prior research [14].

Regarding diabetes-specific constructs, EMA diabetes-related distress was highly correlated with both the PAID and DDS17. The high correlation between diabetes-related distress and the PAID replicate those shown by Ehrmann et al [15] and provide additional support for the validity of EMA single-item measurement of diabetes-related distress. This finding also underscores the support for EMA single-item measurement with both the PAID and DDS17. Both the PAID and DDS17 demonstrated sound psychometric properties as measures of diabetes-related distress in prior research [49]. Compared with the content of the PAID, which focuses on emotional and food-related concerns, the content of the DDS17 has a stronger emphasis on diabetes self-management and metabolic outcomes [49]. Our findings suggest that a single-item EMA measurement of diabetes-related distress is supported as a valid measurement of this construct, although more research is needed to better understand its relationship with various outcomes of interest (e.g. coping styles, quality of life, diabetes self-management, and metabolic outcomes).

The lowest correlation was found between the fear of hypoglycemia EMA aggregate and the HFS-II total score. This finding may be partially explained by the lower temporal reliability of the HFS-II (0.74 for entire scale) [34] relative to other measures in this study. Furthermore, the single item used in the current study was taken from the Worry subscale, which demonstrated lower temporal reliability in the initial validation study by Gonder-Frederick and colleagues [35] when compared with the Behaviour subscale. Given that the EMA item in this study assesses only worries about hypoglycemia as compared with behaviours, the authors also examined the correlation of the EMA item with the individual HFS-II subscales (Behaviour and Worry subscales). The findings indicate a lower correlation between the Behaviour subscale with the fear of hypoglycemia EMA aggregate when compared with the Worry subscale, although overall the total score and both subscales demonstrated relatively low correlations with the EMA aggregate. Future research may consider addressing the validity of alternative items to determine whether these may enhance methodologic rigour.

Limitations

Our study findings should be interpreted while considering some limitations. The sample identified primarily as being of Caucasian race and non-Hispanic ethnicity, with local geography limiting overall generalizability. The participants were drawn from a pool of individuals who had participated in a larger study that focused primarily on diabetes-related complications. As such, the study sample is characterized by a relatively older adult population, all of whom experienced complications related to T1D. To generalize these findings to broader populations, future studies should aim to include a more diverse ethnic/racial sample. Furthermore, our population was older, had diabetes-related complications, and was relatively well-controlled in terms of glycemia, so findings could differ in populations with higher levels of distress, lower control of glycemia, and those with fewer diabetes-related complications, namely adolescents and young adults [50].

There are also methodologic limitations regarding the EMA items used in this study, as there are many potential combinations of EMA and baseline questionnaires to assess psychological functioning. Each EMA assessment in our study was comprised of a single item as opposed to multiple items. Including multiple EMA items to evaluate reliability and validity may have allowed for more refined selection of the most appropriate EMA items to assess each domain. In evaluating psychometric properties, future studies may benefit from inclusion of multiple EMA items. Furthermore, scaling of the EMA items differed across domains. In the current study, in the absence of a standardized approach in the literature, methodologic decisions were made with the goal of keeping EMA scales consistent with the validated instruments when reasonable, particularly for the primary outcomes of diabetes-related distress for comparison with the baseline question. Differences in scoring approaches impacted direct comparison across EMA items as well, which may limit the validity and interpretation of our findings. The comparison between baseline and EMA data points was a primary question and we designed the study to enhance that comparison, however, this adds to the complexity of comparing results across EMA items.

Although the limited representation of clinically elevated symptom levels may constrain generalizability and evaluation of EMA performance at the higher end of severity, graphical inspection and correlation analyses indicated that the EMA items were nonetheless sensitive to meaningful variability across the lower end of the validated scale ranges. Last, we did not have the ability to examine discriminant validity, which is an essential element of construct validation as it helps determine whether the constructs measured are distinct from other unrelated constructs. Future studies should address additional types of validity, such as content validity (i.e., via expert panel review and evaluation of adequateness of items measured in each construct) at the within- and between-person levels.

Implications

Effectively measuring the psychological functioning of individuals with diabetes through momentary assessments will have meaningful implications for research and clinical practice. From a research perspective, the stability of diabetes-related distress and fear of hypoglycemia in time-based EMA sampling suggests that other sampling approaches may be needed to capture the dynamic nature of these constructs. Given the intensity of EMA methodology, it is essential to understand the optimal sampling approaches to assess the nuances of diabetes-specific constructs. In addition, the relatively low variability of diabetes-related distress within individuals reflects that individuals who experience high levels of distress may benefit from additional dynamic study. For instance, future research may help identify those who have a stronger association between glycemic management and diabetes-related distress to inform individualized approaches to care [11]. Furthermore, psychometrically sound EMA assessments of diabetes adjustment constructs will allow researchers to better understand the within-person variation to identify patterns that lead to poorer outcomes and tailor interventions to address these concerns. EMA measurement may promote the development of apply “just-in-time” interventions [51] by correlating individuals’ experiences of distress with continuous glucose monitoring data or other environment-based triggers.

Overall, this work supports the feasibility, reliability, and validity of EMA to capture depressive symptoms, distress, anxiety, and fear of hypoglycemia questions for psychosocial domains in an adult T1D population. Our findings provide evidence for the psychometrically sound properties of EMA measures of depression, anxiety, diabetes-related distress, and fear of hypoglycemia, compared with the baseline survey measures from existing validated self-report measures. By establishing the psychometric properties of these EMA single-item questions, our work may promote the integration of diabetes-specific constructs into EMA methodologies that could reduce response burden and recall bias. The findings also provide support for in-the-moment measurement to inform the effectiveness of real-world interventions that better target psychosocial symptom variability and contextual factors that influence diabetes management behaviours in adults with T1D. Further studies are needed to validate these measures in a more diverse sample and to assess the utility of EMA in relation to glycemic outcomes.
