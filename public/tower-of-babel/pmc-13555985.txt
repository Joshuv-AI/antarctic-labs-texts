Three-dimensional spheroid models in breast cancer: tumor microenvironment complexity, cancer stem cell-driven resistance, and translational model integration
Authors: Yasmine K. Douglas, Sirin A. Adham
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe limited clinical success of anticancer therapies highlights the need for preclinical models that more accurately recapitulate tumor complexity, therapeutic response, and patient-specific heterogeneity. Conventional two-dimensional (2D) culture systems fail to capture the structural organization, cellular diversity, and dynamic microenvironmental gradients of in vivo tumors, thereby limiting their translational predictive value.Main bodyThree-dimensional (3D) spheroid models have emerged as widely adopted platforms that better mimic tumor architecture, microenvironmental gradients, and cell–cell interactions. In this review, we provide a comprehensive and critical evaluation of 3D spheroid systems in breast cancer, with particular emphasis on their role in enriching breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs), modeling therapeutic resistance, and improving translational relevance. We comparatively assess scaffold-free and scaffold-based approaches, including hanging drop systems, ultra-low attachment cultures, hydrogels, microfluidics, patient-derived organoids (PDOs), and bioprinting technologies, while clearly distinguishing multicellular tumor spheroids (MCTSs) from organoid-based models according to their structural complexity, self-organization hierarchy, patient-derived fidelity, and translational applicability. While 3D spheroid models recapitulate key features of the tumor microenvironment, including hypoxia, nutrient gradients, extracellular matrix interactions, and stemness-associated signaling, their utility remains constrained by experimental heterogeneity, incomplete vascular and immune integration, and limited cross-platform standardization. We further discuss conflicting evidence regarding predictive drug-response accuracy and examine the persistent translational gap between preclinical findings and patient outcomes. Importantly, we propose a tiered, fit-for-purpose framework for the strategic selection and integration of preclinical cancer models based on the biological question, cancer subtype, and translational objective. Emerging technologies, including patient-derived organoids, vascularized organ-on-chip systems, and AI-assisted analytical platforms, are evaluated as complementary strategies to overcome current biological, technical, and translational limitations.Conclusion3D spheroid models represent a critical intermediate platform for physiologically relevant cancer modeling; however, their translational impact depends on integration with more complex, standardized, and clinically validated systems. A multi-model, context-driven approach incorporating advanced bioengineering, immune-competent platforms, AI-assisted analytics, and patient-specific models will be essential for improving translational predictability and advancing precision oncology in breast cancer.

Introduction

Translational challenges in cancer research: from 2D monolayers to 3D models

Cancer comprises a group of diseases characterized by uncontrolled cell proliferation and the spread of abnormal cells, accounting for millions of new cases and deaths annually [1, 2]. Despite decades of intensive research, the complex mechanisms underlying cancer progression, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance remain incompletely understood [3]. A central contributor to this gap is the persistent translational disconnect between preclinical models and clinical outcomes, a disconnect that has fueled the historically low success rate of anticancer therapies in clinical trials, estimated at less than 5% from phase I to approval [4]. Much of translational cancer research still relies on 2D monolayer cell culture models, in which cancer cells grow on a flat solid surface under controlled conditions [5]. These systems have been foundational for establishing core principles of cancer cell biology; however, their fidelity to in vivo tumor biology is critically limited. 2D models fail to recapitulate three-dimensional architecture, cellular heterogeneity, and the complex signaling landscape of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [6, 7]. Beyond structural simplification, 2D cultures impose unphysiological cytoskeletal organization, forced cell polarization, and altered gene and protein expression patterns [8–10].

Critically, cells in 2D experience uniform nutrient and oxygen exposure, which is mechanistically distinct from the steep gradients of hypoxia, acidity, and metabolic waste that characterize solid tumors in vivo [11]. These conditions profoundly affect drug penetration, cell cycle kinetics, and resistance mechanisms, which likely explains why drug sensitivities measured in monolayer cultures are frequently poor predictors of clinical response. It is important to acknowledge, however, that not all 2D findings are without translational value. For rapidly dividing, genetically homogeneous cancer cell lines, 2D systems remain efficient platforms for high-throughput screening (HTS) and mechanistic hypothesis generation, owing to their scalability, cost-effectiveness, and compatibility with rapid cytotoxicity assays [12–14]. The critical issue is not that 2D models are inherently invalid, but that their systematic over-reliance, particularly in the absence of contextual validation, contributes to the translational attrition observed in oncology drug development. This highlights an urgent need for more physiologically relevant preclinical models that bridge the gap between reductionist cell biology and the complexity of human tumors.

Three-dimensional (3D) culture models have emerged as promising intermediate platforms that better approximate tumor architecture and function. At the outset, it is important to distinguish multicellular tumor spheroids (MCTSs) from patient-derived organoids (PDOs), as these terms are sometimes used broadly in the 3D culture literature but refer to biologically distinct model systems. MCTSs are typically self-assembled aggregates of established cancer cell lines, primary tumor cells, or defined co-cultures grown under scaffold-free or scaffold-assisted conditions [15, 16]. They primarily recapitulate key tumor-like features, including cell–cell interactions, oxygen and nutrient gradients, hypoxic cores, altered proliferation zones, and drug-penetration barriers [16, 17]. In contrast, PDOs are generated directly from patient tumor specimens and cultured under matrix-supported, lineage-permissive conditions that promote self-organization and preservation of tumor-specific cellular hierarchy, genetic heterogeneity, and patient-dependent therapeutic responses [18, 19]. Thus, MCTSs and PDOs should be regarded as related but non-equivalent 3D models: MCTSs offer simplicity, reproducibility, and scalability for mechanistic and drug-screening studies, whereas PDOs provide greater patient-specific and tissue-level fidelity but are associated with higher technical complexity, cost, and challenges in standardization [15, 18, 19]. More broadly, the field of 3D cancer modeling is highly heterogeneous, encompassing a wide range of platforms, including MCTSs, PDOs, scaffold-based systems, and tumor-on-a-chip devices, each with distinct biological fidelity, technical demands, and translational relevance. Critically, no single 3D platform universally recapitulates all hallmarks of in vivo tumors, and the reproducibility and standardization of these models remain significant, underappreciated challenges. There are currently no standardized methods for culturing organoids, leading to confusion in organoid studies and potentially affecting the accuracy of tumor biology assessments [20, 21]. Similarly, widely used reagents such as fetal bovine serum and Matrigel introduce substantial batch-dependent inconsistencies due to their undefined composition, thereby limiting the reproducibility and standardization of 3D culture systems across studies [22–25]. Differences in spheroid formation protocols, organoid culture conditions, and matrix composition can produce inconsistent outcomes across laboratories, limiting cross-study comparability and complicating the development of regulator-ready 3D cancer models [26–29].

This review critically evaluates the landscape of 3D cancer models, emphasizing their comparative strengths and limitations, the methodological barriers that impede their broader adoption, and their current and prospective utility in translational oncology. To address the conceptual and translational fragmentation that currently characterizes the field, we propose a tiered, context-driven framework for the rational selection and integration of preclinical cancer models.

In this framework, experimental systems are not viewed as competing alternatives but as complementary tools positioned along a continuum of biological complexity and translational relevance. Reductionist models, such as 2D monolayers, are best suited for HTS and mechanistic dissection under controlled conditions, whereas intermediate 3D systems, including spheroids and organoids, enable interrogation of cell-cell and cell-microenvironment interactions with increasing physiological fidelity. More advanced platforms, such as microfluidic tumor-on-a-chip systems and patient-derived organoids, offer enhanced potential for modeling dynamic processes and patient-specific responses, albeit with increased technical and standardization challenges.

Critically, we argue that no single model is sufficient in isolation; rather, robust translational insight emerges from the strategic integration of multiple model systems, guided by the specific biological question, cancer subtype, and intended clinical application. 3D culture systems

To overcome the limitations of oversimplified two-dimensional (2D) cultures, three-dimensional (3D) models have emerged as essential platforms that more faithfully recapitulate the complexity of the tumor microenvironment and key features of primary tumors [30]. However, it is important to acknowledge that while 3D systems better approximate tumor architecture, these limitations are discussed in detail in the Critical Limitations section. In contrast to 2D monolayers, spheroids promote cell-cell and cell-ECM interactions, better mimicking tumor characteristics (e.g., hypoxia, altered growth kinetics, phenotypic heterogeneity, cell signaling activity. Importantly, spheroids also mimic characteristics of the extracellular matrix (ECM), thereby providing both mechanical and biochemical cues that enhance their physiological relevance [31].

The outer layer of cells, with the greatest access to nutrients and oxygen, contains rapidly proliferating cells, whereas cells in the intermediate and core layers are quiescent and/or senescent due to diffusion limitations, often resulting in regions of apoptotic or necrotic cell death [32]. While this spatial organization is often cited as a strength of spheroid models, diffusion gradients are highly sensitive to spheroid size and culture conditions, which can lead to inconsistent experimental outcomes if not carefully controlled [33, 34]. Spheroids generate physiologically relevant gradients of oxygen, nutrients, and metabolic waste, closely mimicking in vivo tumor conditions [35]. Despite these advantages, the extent to which such gradients accurately reflect those observed in human tumors remains debated, particularly in the absence of perfusion and dynamic vascular exchange The broader category of 3D cancer culture models encompasses several related but distinct systems, including MCTSs, tumor spheres, PDOs, scaffold-based constructs, and microfluidic tumor-on-chip platforms; however, in this review, the term “spheroids” refers specifically to MCTS-type aggregates unless otherwise stated, whereas “organoids” refers to patient-derived or tissue-derived self-organizing cultures with higher architectural and biological fidelity. Among them, spheroids are the most widely used in cancer research due to their simplicity, reproducibility, and ease of handling [36, 37]. However, this simplicity also represents a limitation, as spheroids generally lack the structural and cellular complexity of more advanced 3D systems such as organoids or microfluidic tumor-on-chip platforms, which may provide greater physiological fidelity in certain applications [38, 39]. Spheroids are multicellular aggregates that self-assemble into three-dimensional structures, either as monocultures or co-cultures with stromal cells [40]. Importantly, differences in co-culture composition and stromal integration strategies further contribute to inter-model differences and highlight the absence of standardized protocols across the field [41, 42]. Spheroids also provide a more accurate representation of cellular responses to drugs and chemotherapeutic agents than 2D cultures, influencing how cells receive treatments and communicate with one another [43]. Nevertheless, conflicting evidence suggests that drug penetration and response in spheroids can be highly model-dependent and may not consistently correlate with in vivo or clinical outcomes, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation [4, 44]. Although spheroids are considered simple in 3D culture systems, they are powerful tools for studying the fundamental behavior of cancer cells and their responses to drugs. Collectively, these findings support the use of spheroids as intermediate models between conventional 2D cultures and more complex in vivo systems, although their suitability depends on the specific experimental objective. A critical challenge moving forward is integrating spheroid data into broader translational modeling frameworks while improving cross-study reproducibility [45, 46].

Breast cancer and cancer stem cells

Breast cancer (BC), a heterogeneous disease, remains one of the most prevalent and lethal malignancies affecting women worldwide, despite advancements in screening and therapy [47]. Studies conducted by epidemiologists refer to breast cancer as the most frequently diagnosed type of cancer in females on a global scale, leading to increased cancer-related deaths [48, 49]. Currently, international datasets are utilized for the prediction of recurrence and risks for metastasis, acting as a guide for management [50]. Breast cancer is known to show intertumoral and intratumoral molecular heterogeneity, along with various microenvironmental interactions [51]. This characteristic nature of breast cancer cells influences metastasis, recurrence, and chemotherapeutic resistance, and is attributed to clonal evolution and the cancer stem cell (CSC) paradigm [52].

The heterogeneous nature of tumor cells reflects variations in metabolic activity and encompasses phenotypic diversity driven by highly malignant cells known as cancer stem cells (CSCs) [53]. These cells possess self-renewal capacity and can differentiate into diverse tumor cell lineages [35]. Studies have attributed tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and chemoresistance to CSCs across various malignancies, including breast cancer [54]. There have been numerous investigations into CSCs within solid tumors, especially in breast cancer, which revealed a small subpopulation of malignant cells termed breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs) [55]. Furthermore, BCSCs actively interact with the tumor microenvironment through extensive cellular crosstalk, complicating targeted therapeutic strategies [56].

Given their vital role in tumor recurrence and persistence, the targeted elimination of BCSCs has become a top focus of cancer drug development [57]. However, because BCSCs are rare, their low abundance in tumors poses a significant challenge. To address this, various techniques have been developed to enrich and isolate BCSCs from breast cancer cell lines and tissues [58]. Breast cancer stem cells can be identified by detecting certain surface markers such as CD44, CD24, CD133, and EPCAM [59]. Additionally, high aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH) activity is another main method for detecting BCSCs [60]. However, these markers should not be interpreted as fixed or universally interchangeable identifiers of BCSCs. Recent evidence indicates that BCSC phenotypes represent dynamic cellular states rather than stable entities. For example, ALDH-positive and CD44^high/CD24^low populations may be biologically distinct, partially overlapping, and influenced by EMT–MET transitions, metabolic state, and microenvironmental stress. Therefore, marker-based enrichment requires functional validation using sphere-forming capacity, serial passaging, tumor-initiation assays, and treatment-response profiling rather than relying on immunophenotyping alone [61].

However, surface markers alone present problems when utilized as the only identifiers for BCSCs because they are often restricted to certain subtypes of breast cancer, such as triple-negative and luminal type subtypes [62]. Therefore, researchers have devised alternative approaches using in vitro culture systems to enrich and identify breast cancer stem cells. A prevalent technique involves producing 3D spheroids under non-adherent, serum-free, and differentiation-inhibiting conditions that support the survival and expansion of stem-like cells, whereas differentiated cells fail to thrive [63]. Nevertheless, mammosphere and spheroid assays are not without limitations. Sphere formation does not exclusively reflect stemness. It may also be influenced by factors such as proliferation rate, survival under anoikis-resistant conditions, cell aggregation, seeding density, culture medium composition, and passage number. These variables reduce inter-laboratory reproducibility and complicate direct comparison between studies unless standardized reporting criteria are used [64]. In this regard, 3D spheroid cultures, especially mammosphere assays, are important tools for studying BCSC biology, as BCSCs can proliferate as stem-like cells/spheres while other cells differentiate. These models enable scientists to analyze cell heterogeneity, nutrient and oxygen gradients, and matrix interactions, thereby facilitating a deeper understanding of signaling pathways, therapeutic resistance mechanisms, and stem cell plasticity.

This review aims to: (1) discuss the advantages and limitations of 3D spheroid models for studying BCSCs, (2) critically summarize key findings on BCSC behavior, plasticity, and regulatory mechanisms within these systems, and (3) evaluate how spheroid-based models can accelerate therapeutic development while acknowledging the translational gap between simplified in vitro systems and patient tumors. In this context, 3D spheroids should be viewed not as complete tumor surrogates but as reductionist platforms that capture selected features of tumor architecture, oxygen and nutrient gradients, drug-penetration barriers, and stem-like enrichment. Their translational value is likely strongest when integrated with patient-derived organoids, stromal/immune co-cultures, extracellular matrix-based systems, and clinically annotated drug-response data [65]. Thus, the central argument of this review is that 3D spheroid models are most valuable when used within a tiered translational framework: conventional mammospheres for BCSC enrichment, multicellular spheroids for tumor-stroma interaction and drug penetration studies, patient-derived organoids for individualized therapeutic testing, and hybrid scaffold/co-culture systems for modeling clinically relevant microenvironmental complexity.

3D spheroid models and cancer stem cell biology

Biological features of BCSCs in 3D models

3D spheroid culturing has become an essential tool for analyzing CSCs, as it more accurately mimics the tumor microenvironment that traditional 2D cultures fail to recapitulate [66]. However, 3D spheroids should not be interpreted as a uniformly superior model for BCSC biology, but rather as a spectrum of platforms with different biological fidelity and experimental trade-offs. Scaffold-free mammospheres are useful for assessing anchorage-independent growth and stem-like enrichment, but they poorly reproduce extracellular matrix stiffness and stromal signaling. Matrix-embedded spheroids better capture cell–ECM crosstalk, invasion, and mechanotransduction, although undefined matrices such as Matrigel may introduce batch-dependent inconsistencies. More complex co-culture, vascularized, organoid, microfluidic, and bioprinted systems improve TME representation, but often reduce scalability, increase cost, and complicate assay standardization. Therefore, evidence generated from 3D models should be interpreted according to the specific biological question being asked, rather than assuming that all spheroid systems model BCSCs equivalently [67].

Importantly, conflicting observations across studies may reflect differences in model architecture rather than true biological disagreement. For example, increased ALDH activity, CD44^high/CD24^low phenotype, mammosphere-forming capacity, EMT activation, and drug tolerance are frequently reported in 3D cultures, but the magnitude and even persistence of these features vary depending on breast cancer subtype, initial seeding density, spheroid size, culture duration, oxygenation, ECM composition, and whether stromal or immune cells are included. These inconsistencies highlight a major methodological limitation, as key experimental parameters are often inconsistently reported across studies. More rigorous reporting criteria and quantitative quality-control metrics will be needed to improve reproducibility and cross-platform comparison [68]. Studies show that 3D spheroid models enrich for BCSCs and provide a more reliable platform for detailed analysis of the molecular mechanisms underlying stemness, therapy resistance, and disease progression [69].

From a translational perspective, the main value of BCSC-enriched 3D models lies not only in reproducing drug resistance but also in identifying mechanisms that may be clinically actionable. Patient-derived and short-term “zero-passage” organoids are particularly valuable in this context, as they better preserve patient-specific heterogeneity than long-established cell lines and can support drug-response testing within clinically relevant timeframes. However, the clinical translation of spheroid findings remains limited. Key challenges include incomplete immune and vascular modeling, the absence of pharmacokinetic and endocrine influences, limited prospective clinical validation, and uncertainty regarding whether in vitro CSC markers reliably predict patient outcomes. Thus, future studies should integrate spheroid assays with omics profiling, spatial imaging, and patient-derived materials, and correlate these with clinical outcomes to determine which 3D-derived BCSC phenotypes are predictive rather than merely descriptive [65, 70, 71]. Together, these considerations support a “fit-for-purpose” conceptual framework, in which distinct 3D platforms should be selected based on the specific biological or translational question rather than assumed to universally represent tumor biology. Phenotypic and molecular enrichment in 3D spheroids

Numerous studies have used various breast cancer cell lines, such as MCF-7, MDA-MB-231, BT-549, SUM159, MDA-MB-468, ZR-75–1, and SKBR3, to show that when propagated as 3D spheroids and mammospheres, there is an enrichment of a certain population of cells that express canonical BCSC markers, such as CD44+/CD24-, ALDH1, and CD133 [88, 89]. In addition to the multiple breast cancer cell models, multiple studies have also reported similar findings with patient-derived breast cancer tissues. For example, a low-density spheroid protocol was employed to selectively culture BCSCs from patient-derived ER+/HER2- breast cancer tissues, thereby validating an increase in the CD44+/CD24- ratio and in vivo tumor-forming ability [90]. Despite consistent reports of BCSC marker enrichment in 3D cultures, the interpretation of these findings remains context-dependent and, in some cases, controversial. The expression of markers such as CD44+/CD24−, ALDH1, and CD133 is not exclusive to stem-like populations and may be dynamically regulated by microenvironmental conditions, including hypoxia, cell density, and nutrient gradients inherent to spheroid systems [91–94]. Moreover, differences in spheroid formation protocols, culture duration, and analytical methods can significantly affect the proportion of marker-positive cells, raising concerns about reproducibility and inter-platform consistency. Importantly, several studies suggest that marker expression alone may not reliably define functional BCSCs, emphasizing the need to integrate phenotypic assays with functional validation, such as tumor initiation and lineage tracing [95, 96]. In highly aggressive breast cancer cell lines such as MDA-MB-231 and BT-549, studies demonstrate that not only are CD44+/CD24− and ALDH+ populations enriched, but they also exhibit marked phenotypic plasticity, whereby non-BCSCs can convert into BCSCs and vice versa, particularly under chemotherapeutic stress [97, 98]. This observation challenges the notion of a rigid stem cell hierarchy and instead supports a dynamic model in which stem-like and non-stem-like cancer cells exist along a continuum [99, 100]. Within this context, 3D culture conditions may actively induce stem-like phenotypes rather than merely enriching pre-existing BCSCs, potentially confounding the interpretation of experimental outcomes [101]. Furthermore, the extent to which such plasticity occurs in vivo, particularly under immune and systemic regulatory constraints, remains incompletely understood [102]. Such results highlight the clinical relevance of 3D models, given the large population of BCSCs and their resistance to standard chemotherapies (e.g., doxorubicin and paclitaxel), which contribute to increased metastatic potential and poor clinical outcomes [103].

At the molecular level, these context-dependent effects are reflected in the activation of key transcription factors and EMT-associated markers, including Nanog, Oct4, Sox2, Bmi1, Vimentin, and Slug, which are frequently upregulated in 3D spheroid cultures [104–106]. Importantly, mechanistic studies further support this dynamic framework. For example, the calcium channels Orai1 and Orai3 regulate distinct signaling pathways in 3D-enriched BCSCs, with Orai1 promoting glycolysis and tumorigenicity, whereas Orai3 operates through glycolysis-independent mechanisms. Similarly, inhibition of fatty acid synthase (FASN), which is frequently upregulated in BCSCs, reduces their expansion in 3D cultures [104, 107–109]. High-throughput approaches, including transcriptomics, miRNA profiling, and CRISPR-based screening, have also revealed context-specific molecular signatures; notably, hypoxia-induced upregulation of miR-210 in 3D spheroids promotes migration, invasion, and self-renewal by suppressing E-cadherin. Collectively, these findings emphasize that the molecular landscape of BCSCs in 3D systems is highly context-dependent and reinforce the value of spheroid models for dissecting functional heterogeneity, while also highlighting the need for careful interpretation in translational settings. Together, these findings suggest that 3D spheroid systems may function not only as enrichment platforms for pre-existing BCSCs but also as dynamic microenvironmental systems capable of actively reshaping stem-like phenotypes through adaptive cellular plasticity.

Functional validation of BCSC properties

Moreover, recent advances in 3D culture platforms, ranging from bio-printed hydrogels and electrospun scaffolds to tumor-on-a-chip microfluidic devices, have enabled continued research into BCSC heterogeneity and the complex relationship between cancer and stromal cells [83, 110–112]. Despite these advances, substantial inter-platform differences in matrix composition, stiffness, and fabrication methods can affect reproducibility and comparability across studies. These differences can substantially affect reproducibility across platforms [113]. Additionally, such advanced models show that ECM stiffness, stromal cell interconnectivity, and exogenous carcinogenic agents further regulate the properties of BCSCs and their capacity for tumor formation [67]. Nevertheless, these observations are not always consistent across different 3D systems, as variations in scaffold composition and cell sourcing can lead to divergent cellular behaviors, highlighting the need for careful interpretation of experimental outcomes [114, 115]. Tumor sphere formation, serial passaging, and in vivo xenograft transplantation are all functional assays that can validate the ability of 3D-enriched BCSCs to self-renew, initiate tumorigenesis, and metastasis, to the point where only 100 spheroid-derived BCSCs are needed to form tumors in immunodeficient mice [116, 117]. While these findings feature the enhanced tumorigenic potential of 3D-enriched BCSCs, xenograft models in immunodeficient mice do not fully recapitulate human immune interactions, thereby limiting their predictive value for clinical outcomes [118, 119]. In addition to paving the way for the development of precise anti-BCSC therapies, 3D cultures provide crucial insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying stemness. Consequently, they have become a widely used, yet not fully standardized, tool for the functional validation of breast cancer stem cell (BCSC) properties, providing a biologically accurate setting for assessing self-renewal, differentiation, and tumorigenic potential. Despite their widespread use, functional validation assays in 3D systems remain highly sensitive to experimental heterogeneity, including spheroid size, passage number, oxygenation state, extracellular matrix composition, and cell seeding density. These variables can substantially alter sphere-forming efficiency, stemness marker expression, and treatment response, thereby limiting direct comparison between studies. Furthermore, many commonly used assays, including mammosphere formation, may enrich for stress-resistant or highly proliferative populations rather than exclusively identifying bona fide stem cells. Consequently, interpretation of functional BCSC assays requires integration with complementary approaches such as lineage tracing, transcriptomic profiling, patient-derived models, and in vivo validation. These considerations reinforce the need for standardized reporting criteria and fit-for-purpose model selection in translational BCSC research [120]. Importantly, these limitations do not diminish the value of 3D functional assays, but rather emphasize that their translational utility depends on rigorous experimental design, standardized reporting, and integration with complementary biological models. Such models promote the propagation and upkeep of BCSCs, enabling scientists to carry out a wide scope of functional assays, confirming their stemness:

Self-renewal assays assess the self-renewal capacity of BCSCs in 3D culture. One of the simplest self-renewal assays includes the mammosphere formation assay. In this protocol, single cells are propagated in non-adherent, serum-free conditions, allowing only stem cells to propagate and form spheroids. However, it should be noted that mammosphere assays may also permit the survival of progenitor cells, potentially overestimating stem cell frequency and introducing variability in interpretation [121]. According to existing literature, studies have shown that MCF-7 and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells derived from spheroids show a considerably greater capacity to form both primary and secondary spheres than those cultured in 2D models, demonstrating enhanced self-renewal [63, 122]. Furthermore, manipulating certain regulators, such as miR-210 or let-7, alters self-renewal. For instance, elevated miR-210 expression in MCF-7 spheroids enhances sphere formation, whereas let-7 overexpression reduces it. This confirms the mechanistic relationship between 3D signaling and stem cell characteristics [122].

Differentiation potential assays assess the ability of BCSCs to differentiate. Spheroid-derived cells can be induced to differentiate into various mammary epithelial cell types. This is evidenced by the expression of lineage-specific markers and their capacity to differentiate into both ductal and myoepithelial cells in vitro [123]. The ability to undergo multilineage differentiation, a hallmark of stemness, is significantly diminished when these cells are cultured in 2D. Despite this, the extent to which in vitro differentiation accurately reflects in vivo lineage hierarchies remains under debate, particularly given the absence of systemic and immune regulatory signals in most 3D models [124, 125]. Moreover, the balance between self-renewal and differentiation within 3D models is often shifted due to the modulation of certain pathways, such as Sonic Hedgehog or Pigment Epithelium-Derived Factor (PEDF), which further confirms the stem-like nature of spheroid-derived BCSCs [126, 127].

Tumor-initiation capacity is the most reliable functional validation of BCSC properties, as it reflects their in vivo tumor-forming potential. Cells exhibiting a CD44+/CD24- phenotype within 3D spheroids have been shown to initiate tumors at high frequency upon injection into immunodeficient mice, with 100–1,000 cells sufficient to form tumors, whereas 2D cultures require a significantly larger cell mass [128, 129]. These tumors recapitulate the heterogeneous nature of the original cancer, further validating the 3D-enriched BCSC stemness. Nonetheless, differences in experimental design, including cell injection site, mouse strain, and matrix support, can significantly influence tumor initiation rates, complicating cross-study comparisons [123]. 3D-enriched BCSC in vivo studies have demonstrated that manipulating self-renewal pathways (e.g., Nrf2, p63, miR-210) can modulate tumor growth and metastatic potential [93, 127, 130].

These functional assays provide robust evidence that BCSCs enriched in 3D culture exhibit authentic stem-like behavior and tumor-initiating capacity. Consequently, they aid in identifying and confirming BCSC characteristics and serve as effective tools for drug screening, resistance assessment, and studies of disease mechanisms. However, bridging the gap between these preclinical findings and clinical application remains challenging because current 3D systems do not fully capture patient-specific variability and systemic influences. Importantly, relatively few studies have prospectively correlated spheroid-derived BCSC phenotypes with patient therapeutic response or long-term clinical outcome. As a result, the predictive value of many currently used 3D assays remains incompletely validated. Future translational studies should therefore prioritize clinically annotated patient-derived models, standardized multi-center protocols, and integration of immune-competent systems to determine which in vitro BCSC features are genuinely predictive of treatment resistance and disease recurrence in patients. Future efforts should focus on integrating patient-derived cells, immune components, and harmonized criteria to enhance translational relevance. Incorporating functional validation and molecular profiling into a 3D environment accelerates the development of precise anti-BCSC therapies, ultimately improving clinical outcomes in breast cancer [131, 132].

Key signaling pathways in BCSCs

3D models have revealed unique mechanistic insights into the roles of key signaling pathways in modulating BCSCs, highlighting differences between conventional 2D and 3D systems. For instance, in 3D spheroids, the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is characterized by nuclear β-catenin accumulation and overexpression of downstream targets such as c-Myc and Cyclin D1, establishing a direct connection between spatial organization and the transcriptional switch that confers stem-like properties and increased malignancy [133, 134]. Within the 3D culture context, augmentation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway is driven by specific microenvironmental signaling, along with factors such as Nectin-4 (a BCSC marker), which has been shown to promote the nuclear translocation of β-catenin and self-renewal through the PI3K/Akt axis, an effect that is notably reduced in 2D cultures [135, 136]. However, some studies report context-dependent variability in pathway activation, suggesting that microenvironmental cues and model selection can differentially influence signaling outcomes [137].

The Notch signaling pathway in 3D cultures is commonly triggered by ligand binding mediated by juxtacrine contact between cells within the spheroid core, amplifying the self-renewal and sustained existence of the BCSC pool [138, 139]. Enhanced Hedgehog ligand diffusion occurs within 3D microenvironments, establishing spatially diverse activation zones that recapitulate in vivo tumor organization, thereby fostering the growth of BCSCs and drug resistance [140–142]. Persistent Akt phosphorylation and mTOR activation within the PI3K/Akt/mTOR axis is sustained by mechanical cues such as extracellular matrix (ECM) stiffness and the tight aggregation of cells within 3D spheroids, bolstering BCSC metabolic plasticity and resistance to harsh conditions [143–146]. Previous studies have shown that the PI3K and Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathways converge on GSK3β in 3D models, where Akt phosphorylation stabilizes β-catenin, promoting the growth of stem-like, drug-resistant populations [136, 147–149]. These signaling pathways constitute a highly integrated signaling axis in which the regulation of one pathway induces the engagement of another’s feedback loop. Collectively, these observations support the notion that BCSC maintenance in 3D systems arises from highly interconnected signaling networks rather than from isolated linear pathways, thereby underscoring the importance of studying stemness within spatially organized microenvironments. This dynamic becomes obvious only in 3D systems. However, the extent to which these signaling interactions are faithfully reproduced varies substantially across scaffold-free spheroids, matrix-based systems, and organoid platforms, underscoring that pathway activation may reflect both biological behavior and model-specific architecture. Integrated translational framework linking 3D breast cancer models, BCSC regulation, and clinical application

The cancer stem cell (CSC) niche plays a pivotal role in maintaining BCSCs by combining ECM composition, oxygen-deficient gradients, and stromal-immune interactions, which together modulate stemness, transformation, and survival [150]. Despite these advantages, most 3D models still lack a full representation of the immune system and vascular perfusion, which are critical components of the in vivo tumor niche [80]. The upregulation of HIF-1 alpha and mesenchymal transcription factors is usually accompanied by elevated invasiveness and motility. Additionally, ECM stiffness further regulates the fate of BCSCs: optimal stiffness enhances tumorigenicity, whereas excessive stiffness can cause reversible inactivity, highlighting the critical role of biochemical signals in the 3D microenvironment [101, 151, 152].

Moreover, interactions between stromal cells within 3D spheroid niches are equally important. CAFs, otherwise known as cancer-associated fibroblasts, remodel the ECM, promoting a fibrotic and immunosuppressive niche. This niche shields BCSCs from radiotherapy and immune responses and is linked to T-cell exclusion in aggressive breast tumor subtypes [78, 153]. Furthermore, CAFs can contribute to paracrine signaling via extracellular vesicles. CAF-secreted vesicles carrying regulatory RNAs (like lncRNA H19) can communicate with breast cancer cells under hypoxic conditions, increasing stemness, resistance, and immune evasion by regulating gene expression and inhibiting tumor-suppressive microRNAs [154].

In addition to CAFs, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) present in 3D spheroids play a role in improving the survival of BCSCs via the IL-6/STAT3 axis, while regulatory T cells (Tregs) reduce cytotoxic responses and intensify cytokine feedback, promoting stemness [155]. The presence of M2-polarized macrophages and Tregs within 3D spheroids creates a hostile immune microenvironment, thereby promoting survival and therapy resistance. These spheres can be cultivated in vitro by co-culturing breast cancer cell lines, such as MDA-MB-231, or patient-derived F2-7 cells alongside monocytes or differentiated macrophages at a 1:1 ratio in (pHEMA)-coated plates. Spheroid formation can be facilitated by adding small amounts of growth factor-reduced Matrigel, resulting in structures that self-assemble after several days of incubation [79]. The ECM, immune, and stromal components collectively form an intricate communication network that is faithfully recapitulated in complex 3D systems such as bio-printed organoids and microfluidic co-cultures [78]. These findings confirm that 3D spheroid niches modulate the microenvironment to favor BCSC survival, plasticity, and chemotherapy resistance by leveraging combined physical forces, metabolic, and immune factors. Moving forward, integration of stromal-rich spheroids with vascularized microfluidic systems and patient-derived immune components may provide more clinically relevant platforms for evaluating anti-BCSC therapies and mechanisms of immune escape.

Tumor microenvironment recapitulation in 3D spheroids

Architectural and microenvironmental features

Compared with 2D cultures, three-dimensional spheroid architecture enables significant changes in the biological state of cancer cells, thereby establishing and mimicking gene/protein expression and microenvironmental gradients, as in vivo tumors. Most importantly, the unique spatial organization of cancer cells within 3D spheroids allows for the overexpression of genes/proteins involved in pluripotency, extracellular matrix (ECM) formation, cell adhesion, and cell survival pathways, including Yamanaka factors, collagen IV, fibronectin, N-cadherin, β-catenin, STAT3, and hypoxia inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF-1α) [156, 157].

Hypoxia and metabolic gradients

Transcriptomic changes are largely driven by physiological gradients established during spheroid formation, particularly oxygen gradients absent in 2D cultures [158]. When spheroids propagate, the distance over which oxygen can diffuse is limited, leading to the development of a hypoxic core that subsequently stabilizes HIF-1α and activates its downstream targets. This transcription factor promotes glycolysis, angiogenesis, cell survival, and aggressive tumor behavior, closely capturing in vivo characteristics [159, 160]. However, because most spheroid systems lack functional perfusion and vascular exchange, hypoxic gradients may vary considerably between platforms and do not always quantitatively reflect those observed in human tumors.

In addition to recapitulating in vivo tumor physiological gradients, 3D spheroids provide an environment for cancer cells to re-establish intercellular connections and junctional systems similar to those found in native tumors [161]. This supports the maintenance of tissue-like architecture, modulates gene expression, signaling pathway activity, and drug response profiles, making 3D spheroids a more physiologically relevant platform [162]. Studies have shown that prominent oncogenic signaling pathways, such as AKT–mTOR–S6K, exhibit spatially confined activity, with decreasing signaling activity from the periphery to the core, thereby mimicking in vivo microregional differences [163].

Tumor-stromal Interactions

3D hybrid spheroid models allow the inclusion of mesenchymal cells, such as stromal and endothelial cells, thereby aiding the analysis of tumor-stroma and tumor-vasculature interactions and consequently enhancing the biological significance of such systems [164, 165]. 3D spheroids support endothelial co-culture and measurement of VEGF secretion, thereby promoting vascular signaling exchange [166, 167]. Moreover, the features of 3D spheroid models allow the evaluation of metastatic and angiogenic inhibitors in a context that mimics in vivo tumor behavior, thereby making them a highly relevant culturing method for therapeutic testing [168, 169].

ECM stiffness and mechano-transduction

Within 3D spheroids, the ECM not only acts as a scaffold but also actively modulates BCSC behavior. Collagen-rich matrices further amplify hypoxic signaling and stemness [170]. These biomechanical and biochemical cues translate into measurable phenotypes. Embedding spheroids in collagen or Matrigel facilitates direct observation of peripheral cells and their ability to invade the surrounding extracellular matrix, providing a reliable experimental framework for analyzing motility characteristics and testing metastatic inhibitors; this is one of many quantitative invasion assays that can be employed [171, 172].

Drug penetration and resistance

Many researchers have adapted existing 3D viability assays to assess drug effects robustly. Researchers employ advanced measurement methods to enable 3D drug testing at scales that support both feasibility and quantitative metrics. Typically, drug effects can be measured in 3D using several techniques, including ATP-based luminescence (a measure of cellular energy), Live/Dead staining, using Calcein AM for live cells and Propidium Iodide for dead cells, combined with high-content imaging, which allows for an accurate assessment of both size and shape of spheroids. These techniques enable high-throughput, quantitative assessment of drug responses, thereby converting an intricate model into a fast, measurable assay [173, 174]. However, spheroid heterogeneity and diffusion gradients can complicate the consistent assessment of drug response across platforms [175]. Overall, these developments emphasize the predictive capacity of 3D models for drug effectiveness and resistance, while also highlighting the technical considerations required for their effective use in preclinical research.

EMT and invasion

In addition to recapitulating the tumor microenvironment and resistance mechanisms observed in vivo, 3D spheroid models show immense potential for dissecting the molecular and cellular mechanisms that promote cancer invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and early metastasis and angiogenesis. Important governors of metastasis, like transcriptional regulators Snail, Twist, and ZEB1, and the proteases MMP2 and MMP9, show differential behaviors in 3D versus 2D cultures, with key changes such as the loss and upregulation of E-cadherin and N-cadherin, respectively; therefore, showing a more enhanced and physiologically relevant picture within a spheroid context [171, 176–178].

Collectively, these observations support a conceptual framework in which 3D spheroid models serve as intermediate systems that bridge conventional 2D cultures and in vivo tumors. While they significantly improve physiological relevance by incorporating spatial architecture, cell-cell interactions, and microenvironmental gradients, their limitations in standardization, immune representation, and clinical predictability must be addressed to realize their translational potential fully. Collectively, the evidence discussed above supports the view that BCSC behavior in 3D systems arises from dynamic interactions among signaling pathways, extracellular matrix architecture, hypoxia, stromal signaling, and immune modulation, rather than from isolated molecular mechanisms. Importantly, these interactions influence not only stemness and therapy resistance but also the translational utility of different 3D platforms.

Technologies for 3D spheroid generation

Scaffold-free approaches

Scaffold-free methods utilize the ability of breast cancer cells to self-aggregate in a non-adherent environment, resulting in spheroids that mimic certain critical aspects of solid tumor architecture [179]. Importantly, the biological behavior observed in scaffold-free systems is strongly shaped by how effectively each platform reproduces nutrient gradients, cell compaction, and spatial heterogeneity, all of which influence BCSC enrichment and therapeutic response. Scaffold-free systems depend solely on cell-cell adhesion as the primary mechanism of structural emergence due to their independence of external matrices. The essence of scaffold-free spheroid cultures is to reduce cell adhesion to culture surfaces while encouraging suspended-cell aggregation. This is accomplished using surface modifications, dynamic culture conditions, and external forces [180]. Emerging microfluidic devices allow for the development of micro-organs-on-chips. These devices are designed to promote spheroid formation in microchambers and provide channels for cell and medium delivery [181]. Many common methods have been developed in recent years, including the pellet culture system, hanging drop method, ultra-low attachment (ULA) plates, dynamic culture systems, magnetic levitation, microfluidics, and polymer surfaces.

Forced aggregation (pellet culture system) involves physically clustering breast cancer cells in conical tubes using techniques such as centrifugation. This system confines breast cancer cells in proximity in highly defined, geometric patterns, thereby prompting them to aggregate, leading to the formation of spheroids [182]. This formation into spheres allows the breast cancer cells to interact closely with one another, recapitulating the in vivo cell-cell and spatial interactions. Although this system simulates in vivo relationships, it establishes a possible risk of the development of a central necrotic core due to varying gradients in oxygen, pH, and nutrients [86, 183]. Furthermore, the utilization of conical tubes requires space and consumes time. In response to such challenges, high-throughput microwell systems have risen, allowing cells to aggregate more efficiently [180].

The hanging drop method is widely regarded as one of the most established scaffold-free culture techniques for developing multicellular breast cancer spheroids. This method does not require specialized equipment and can be adapted to include other biological agents, enabling co-culture of two or more distinct cancer cell populations to study cell-cell or cell-extracellular matrix (ECM) interactions. In this technique, small droplets (10–30 microliters) of a cell suspension are placed on an inverted, non-coated dish lid, allowing gravity to aggregate and concentrate the cells at the bottom of the droplet, forming spheroids. Its simplicity of execution, lack of expensive tools, and ability to promote cell-cell communication with other non-cancerous cells make this scaffold-free culture method advantageous for researchers. Consequently, hanging-drop systems are particularly useful for mechanistic studies of early spheroid assembly and stemness regulation, although their translational scalability remains limited. Since its discovery in 1910 by R. Harrison, who cultured neuronal cells using this method, it has been widely used to culture various cancers and cancer stem cells, including breast cancer and BCSCs, respectively [184, 185].

Even though the hanging drop method can control breast cancer spheroid size, media changing and treatment with drugs can be challenging if the spheroids are not switched to non-adherent culture dishes after assembly [36]. Additionally, increases in breast cancer cell number and culture duration lead to greater necrosis and apoptosis. A high-throughput culture system consisting of an array of hundreds of hanging drops on a plate has been employed to address such challenges. This system was intentionally designed for the spheroid culturing of cancer stem cells, allowing for efficient scalability and pharmaceutical analysis [82].

Ultra-low attachment (ULA) plates, also known as multicellular tumor sphere formation assays, are widely used to recapitulate the intricacies of multicellular tumors and analyze the response of breast cancer cells to different therapies [5]. The main strategy of this method is to minimize cell adherence to culture surfaces while fostering spheroid development through elevated cell-cell interactions. Treatment of ULA plates or standard plastic plates with inert substrates such as poly-2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate (poly-HEMA) and agarose is required to prevent breast cancer cells from attaching to the well surface, thereby forming spheroids [186]. In addition to ULA plates, micro-molded non-adhesive hydrogels, made of agarose or polyacrylamide, enable spontaneous spheroid formation in low-attachment micro molds. Micro molds can be crafted into many different structures for array-based, high-throughput spheroid cultures, and thus represent a flexible and scalable means of creating spheroids of a uniform size [187, 188]. The relative simplicity and scalability of ULA-based systems have therefore made them among the most widely adopted platforms for high-throughput BCSC and drug-resistance studies.

Dynamic culture systems (e.g., spinning flasks, rotating vessels, and rocking culture) use continuous motion to prevent cell adherence, thereby enhancing spheroid formation and improving nutrient and oxygen exchange, better recapitulating in vivo physiological conditions. The spinner flask method uses an impeller to enable cells to collide, thereby facilitating uniform nutrient distribution and preventing cell sedimentation [189]. Initially, integrin binding causes breast cancer cells to aggregate loosely; however, they condense over time via E-cadherin relationships [190]. Similarly, vessels for rotational wall culture minimize cell sedimentation by rotating about the x-axis, thereby generating a microgravity-like environment with reduced shear forces [191]. Additionally, shakers or rockers are used to culture systems, creating a dynamic environment in which breast cancer cells in suspension slowly develop into spheroids over weeks [5]. Overall, these dynamic systems enable the formation of numerous spheroids with uniform size and structure, making them well-suited for macroscale applications. However, constant mixing and shear stress must be monitored carefully, as excessive disturbances can adversely affect cell viability and behavior. These systems provide clear advantages for long-term cultures, stem cell expansion, and experimentation, owing to their tissue-like complexity [127]. From a translational perspective, dynamic systems offer greater physiological relevance by more accurately reproducing fluid-mediated nutrient exchange and gradient formation; however, increased shear-related variability may complicate standardization across laboratories.

Magnetic levitation is a new scaffold-free 3D culture technique in which breast cancer spheroids are formed using magnetic forces to counteract cell sedimentation. The breast cancer cells are incubated with magnetic nanoparticles overnight to promote particle adherence, then returned to suspension in a low-adhesion microwell plate above a magnet [40]. The microwell plate setup allows breast cancer cells to levitate and aggregate to form complex 3D spheroid structures. Magnetic levitation systems enable accurate cell-cell interactions, are highly tunable by varying exposure times and magnetic force strength, and are compatible with co-cultures (e.g., stromal cells), thereby allowing the creation of 3D architectures [181]. Additionally, this method supports rapid spheroid formation by eliminating physical contact with scaffolds, reducing resource usage, unwanted mechanical cues, and potential damage. While this scaffold-free method efficiently forms breast cancer spheroids, specialized tools and equipment are required, such as biocompatible magnetic nanoparticles, which may increase the complexity and cost of the process [192]. Accordingly, magnetic levitation platforms may be most valuable for specialized mechanistic or co-culture applications rather than routine large-scale translational screening.

Scaffold-free microfluidic devices provide a controlled 3D environment for generating on-chip scaffold-free spheroids. They use microfluidic channels and wells to manipulate minuscule volumes of fluid accurately, enabling uniform production of spheroids [193]. This process involves seeding breast cancer cells into hydrodynamically trapped areas, where they aggregate spontaneously due to their proximity and fluid shear. This type of technology outperforms other spheroid-forming methods in handling dynamic flow and gradients of oxygen, nutrients, and growth factors, which are key factors of the tumor microenvironment [194]. Moreover, biosensors can be integrated into microfluidic systems, thereby enhancing high-throughput drug screening and real-time imaging. However, obtaining consistent and reproducible results is challenging, and their complexity may require the addition of specialized equipment, increasing operational costs [195]. Collectively, scaffold-free approaches represent highly accessible and scalable platforms for studying BCSC enrichment, drug resistance, and cell-cell interactions; however, important trade-offs exist between simplicity, reproducibility, and physiological fidelity. Methods such as hanging-drop and ULA systems are cost-effective and amenable to high-throughput screening, but incompletely reproduce extracellular matrix signaling and biomechanical regulation. In contrast, dynamic and microfluidic systems better capture nutrient flow, oxygen gradients, and spatial heterogeneity, although they introduce greater technical complexity and reduced inter-laboratory reproducibility. These differences reinforce the importance of selecting scaffold-free platforms based on the specific biological or translational objective, rather than assuming functional equivalence among 3D systems.

Polymer-modified surfaces are another scaffold-free method to induce spheroid formation by reducing cell-substrate adhesion. One of the many methods involved coating cell culture plates with chitosan (with or without hyaluronan), providing an alternative to the traditional use of low-attachment plates [195]. The purpose of this process is to alter the tissue culture surface, enabling cell adhesion and proliferation, followed by migration away from the surface, ultimately aggregating into breast cancer spheroids [196]. This could lead to a range of spheroid sizes, depending on specific cell parameters, due to enhanced migration or cell adhesion. Advanced polymer-based methods have been developed over the years that use thermoresponsive culture dishes (TRCDs) coated with poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PIPAAm), which respond to temperature. Higher temperatures make the surface hydrophobic, while lower temperatures make it hydrophilic and promote gentle spheroid detachment without enzymatic treatment [197, 198]. Moreover, these systems are used to generate homogeneous spheroids and can contain multiple cancer cell types. Modifications in their wettability, stiffness, and biochemical presentation aid in characterizing drug responses and stem-like behaviors. However, such advanced methods require precise temperature regulation to recover breast cancer spheroids effectively, complicating the process further. Despite the growing diversity of scaffold-free technologies, the absence of harmonized criteria for spheroid size, culture conditions, and endpoint analysis continues to hinder reproducibility and cross-platform benchmarking. Establishing harmonized quality-control criteria will be essential for broader translational adoption and regulatory acceptance of spheroid-based assays.

Scaffold-based approaches

Although the term spheroid often refers to condensed, self-assembled aggregates generated through scaffold-free methods, 3D culture systems with a scaffold component provide a unique and integral approach. Such systems permit breast cancer cells to propagate and self-organize into multicellular masses or sphere-like structures by offering them natural or synthetic biomaterials that mimic an extracellular matrix (ECM) or ECM-like environment [204]. By incorporating biomaterial support structures, these systems attempt to model not only tumor architecture but also the biomechanical and biochemical signals that critically regulate BCSC plasticity, invasion, and therapy resistance.

The appropriate scaffold provides an environment conducive to cell adhesion, proliferation, differentiation, migration, and invasion. Scaffold-based systems offer mechanical and biochemical cues that replicate an in vivo environment by providing structural support, binding sites for biochemicals, and resistance to mechanical deformation [205]. These scaffolds can influence breast cancer cell behavior and, therefore, their stemness.

Importantly, although scaffold-based systems better recapitulate extracellular matrix architecture and mechano-transduction than scaffold-free spheroids, they also introduce additional experimental variability. Matrix composition, stiffness, porosity, and degradation kinetics can independently alter stemness-associated signaling pathways, making it difficult to distinguish biologically relevant effects from scaffold-dependent artifacts. Consequently, interpretation of BCSC behavior in scaffold systems requires careful consideration of biomaterial-specific influences.

The solubility, stability, degradation rate, biocompatibility, pore size, and compliance are all scaffold properties that can be modified and controlled to meet the requirements of any experiment, and various technologies can be applied, such as 3D printing [206, 207]. Tissue culture quality and data interpretation may be influenced by both biomaterial selection and the scaffold’s physical state, in which breast cancer cells grow [76]. The scaffold biomaterials used to develop breast cancer spheroids can be broadly classified into three groups: polymer scaffolds, hydrogels, and hybrid scaffolds incorporating microfluidic devices.

Polymer scaffolds. This is a broad umbrella that encompasses a wide range of biomaterials, and polymer-based scaffolds can be classified as natural (e.g., collagen, gelatin, fibrin, chitosan, Matrigel, silk, alginate) and synthetic polymers (e.g., polylactic acid (PLA), polyglycolic acid (PGA), polycaprolactone (PCL), poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) [PLGA], poly (2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate) [PHEMA]) [208, 209]. They provide a biomimetic environment that imitates the natural ECM and can therefore promote spheroid formation, enhance the expression of genes associated with numerous malignant processes, and enrich the cancer stem cell population [210, 211]. These findings reinforce the concept that extracellular matrix composition is not merely structural, but actively shapes stemness-associated signaling and adaptive tumor behavior within 3D systems.

Natural polymers offer better support for cell adhesion, growth, differentiation, and migration. Moreover, they accurately resemble natural ECM by comprising diverse components, meeting extracellular signaling needs, and supporting breast cancer cell behavior [212]. These polymers, however, have limitations in their inconsistent composition and limited availability, as well as unwanted immune triggers [213, 214]. Synthetic biodegradable polymer scaffolds are easy to create, the materials are readily available, can provide experimental reproducibility, and can integrate additional ECM proteins or polypeptides [215]. Their lack of native ECM proteins, artificial interactions between ECM and cells, and their toxic acidic degradation byproducts pose major drawbacks, therefore, compromising the signaling induction of cellular events [216].

The advent of new fabrication technologies, specifically 3D printing, has improved the fidelity and geometry of polymer-based scaffolds. 3D printing enables precise control over the scaffold structure, porosity, and mechanical properties, yielding a better resemblance to the physical signals of the tumor microenvironment [73]. Thus, the choice between natural and synthetic scaffolds reflects a broader translational trade-off between physiological fidelity and experimental reproducibility, a recurring challenge across 3D cancer modeling platforms.

Hydrogel scaffolds comprise a three-dimensional array of hydrophilic polymer chains (natural, synthetic, or hybrid), resulting in microenvironments with tunable physical characteristics (e.g., stiffness, porosity) and biochemical properties [40]. They can absorb large volumes of biological fluids while retaining their overall structure [217]. Hydrogel scaffolds support breast cancer spheroid formation by providing physical support and coupling that deliver critical signals for cell adhesion, polarization, and communication. They also provide a biocompatible environment similar to native tissues and have porous structures that allow cell function, nutrient and oxygen diffusion, and waste removal [218]. Because hydrogel properties can be chemically and mechanically tuned, these systems also provide an important platform for systematically dissecting how matrix stiffness and biochemical composition influence BCSC behavior and therapeutic response. There are four common methods for culturing with hydrogel scaffolds. The dome method involves placing and mixing breast cancer cells with hydrogels, then seeding them as droplets into cell culture dishes and covering them with media, thereby supporting spheroid formation in a confined dome [40]. Insert wells utilize porous inserts to hold the mixture of hydrogel and cells, allowing cell culture media to be poured into the well surrounding the insert. This system recapitulates a more segregated matrix environment used to study nutrient diffusion and the invasion of heterogeneous breast cancer spheroids that form at the bottom of the inserts due to cell-cell interactions [219]. The gel-bottom support system involves cell seeding a concentrated cell suspension onto a thick hydrogel layer at the bottom of the well. The concentrated cell solution is then trapped in a porous hydrogel structure. The embedding technique involves suspending breast cancer cells in a hydrogel, placing the mixture at the bottom of a culture vessel, and then fully submerging the suspension in cell culture media. This allows breast cancer cells to grow within the hydrogel matrix and can be used to study cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, thereby partially recapitulating the in vivo three-dimensional microenvironment. Compared with conventional scaffold-free spheroids, hydrogel and hybrid scaffold systems generally provide superior modeling of invasion, ECM remodeling, and mechanical signaling; however, these advantages are often offset by reduced scalability, increased cost, and lower experimental standardization. Furthermore, widely used biologically derived matrices such as Matrigel remain poorly defined and exhibit substantial batch-dependent effects, limiting reproducibility and complicating regulatory translation.

These limitations have driven growing interest in synthetic, tunable biomaterials that enable more controlled modeling of tumor biomechanics. Moving forward, the development of chemically defined and mechanically tunable biomaterials may help reduce batch-to-batch variability while enabling more reproducible investigation of ECM-dependent mechanisms underlying BCSC survival and therapeutic resistance.

Hybrid scaffolds combine various scaffold types or technologies to create 3D culture systems that closely resemble the physiological aspects and microenvironment of native living tissues [40]. This type of system integrates natural and synthetic polymers, hydrogels, and bioactive elements such as hydroxyapatite to elevate certain functional and molecular cues that influence malignant processes [40, 220]. Hybrid scaffolds are particularly used to study and enrich breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs) because they critically require an accurate representation of ECM dynamics and cell-cell interactions. One method involved forming spheroids utilizing a biomimetic composite scaffold that integrated magnesium-doped hydroxyapatite and collagen to replicate a CSC environment [221].

Microfluidics offers a multipurpose platform for 3D cell culture by supporting both scaffold-based and scaffold-free spheroid formation. Such platforms are easily adjustable to suit various experimental requirements, from cell-laden scaffolds to spheroid or organoid formation. Microfluidic-based hybrid scaffolds enable dynamic control over the microenvironment and nutrient flow, and introduce various gradients by cultivating breast cancer cells within microscale channels [222]. In addition, they can be adapted for coculture systems that resemble in vivo tumor-stroma interactions. Previous work has reported enhanced spheroid formation, EMT-related signaling, altered proliferation markers, drug-resistance phenotypes, and activation of stromal cells when breast tumor cells were cultured in collagen-filled microchannels [223–225]. Overall, these systems hold great promise for drug screening, BCSC research, and personalized medicine, as they more accurately mimic the in vivo tumor microenvironment. Spheroid characterization

After the formation of 3D spheroids, extensive characterization is needed to validate their structural and functional relevance to tumors found in living organisms. Morphological assessment is usually the first step, in which light or phase-contrast microscopy is utilized to analyze the size, shape, and compactness of spheroids [84]. Such factors are highly important, as they significantly affect the penetration of drugs and nutrients. Image analysis software is usually used to measure the size of spheroids, and in turn, this size is utilized as an indicator of growth kinetics [230]. Apart from measuring size, viability assays are used to measure the metabolic activity of cells; they include MTT, Resazurin reduction, measuring ATP, and acid phosphatase assay [231–233]. Histological analysis and immunohistochemical staining with Hematoxylin and Eosin can be employed to disclose the internal architecture and assess the expression of proliferation (Ki-67) or apoptosis (cleaved caspase-3) markers [234]. Surface markers can also be evaluated by immunofluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry [235]. These characterization tools demonstrate that spheroids maintain tumor-like features, including hypoxic cores and distinct gene expression profiles.

Therapeutic resistance and translational applications of 3D spheroids

Mechanisms of drug resistance

Since 3D spheroid models can more effectively recapitulate the tumor microenvironment and resistance mechanisms, they have revolutionized in vitro drug testing and screening. Unlike 2D monolayer cultures, they drive cell-cell and cell-matrix cross-talks, consequently developing physiological gradients and architectural features that significantly govern drug responses [236, 237]. A defining trait of 3D cancer spheroids is their enhanced capacity to resist chemotherapy, a phenomenon known as enhanced drug resistance.

This resistance is driven by multiple mechanisms. ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, namely ABCG2/BCRP and P-gp/MDR1, function as efflux pumps that push chemotherapeutics out of the cell, rather than allowing sufficient concentrations to build up to kill the tumor [85, 238–240]. Pathways of survival, especially the PI3K/Akt pathway, are a key mechanism underlying therapeutic resistance. The PI3K/Akt (pathway) remains an emerging mechanism of MDR as it drives cell division at a much higher frequency and can override normal genetic signals for cell death (apoptosis). It is a coordinated survival mechanism that leads to a more robust defense force [241, 242].

These resistance mechanisms are pronounced in breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs), identified by high CD44/low CD24/ALDH1 levels, which actively express efflux pumps (ABCG2). This ensures that the few cells responsible for long-term tumor survival are highly resistant to conventional chemotherapy [236, 243]. From the perspective of drug resistance, it has been documented that BCSCs within 3D spheroids are able to sustain a much higher level of ABC transporters and ALDH1, along with needing a greater drug concentration to achieve cell death. This closely mimics clinical scenarios and uncovers mechanisms involved in resistance [243–245].

Therapeutic strategies

In this sophisticated 3D testing environment, researchers deploy a range of powerful agents. The most common major therapeutic classes fall into two main categories: broad-acting cytotoxic agents (like the powerful chemotherapy drugs doxorubicin and paclitaxel) and highly specific targeted therapies (such as inhibitors designed to block cancer’s key communication hubs, like HER2 and PI3K) [85]. Such drug classes have shown lowered potency in 3D models compared to 2D ones due to restricted penetration and active efflux [163, 246]. Importantly, these findings highlight a broader methodological issue in 3D drug testing: reduced therapeutic efficacy in spheroids does not necessarily imply greater physiological relevance across all contexts. While decreased drug penetration and hypoxic gradients more closely resemble in vivo tumors, they may also artificially exaggerate resistance depending on spheroid size, matrix composition, and culture duration. Consequently, direct comparisons between 2D and 3D systems should be interpreted cautiously, particularly when evaluating drug potency or predicting patient response. Moreover, different 3D platforms vary considerably in their ability to reproduce stromal interactions, oxygen diffusion, and extracellular matrix architecture, suggesting that no single spheroid model can currently serve as a universal standard for translational drug screening

Anti-CSC drugs

A study has shown that modified salinomycin (a monocarboxylic polyether antibiotic) analogs, in both 2D monolayers and 3D organoids, exhibited greater efficacy in precisely targeting the CD44+/CD24-low BCSC fraction, with certain analogs proving more effective at suppressing cell migration and self-renewal, especially in the 3D context [247].

Combination therapies

One study examined the combination of paclitaxel, a well-known chemotherapeutic drug, and reparixin, an agent that targets CSCs, in advanced 3D bio-printed tumor-stromal models, demonstrating a synergistic effect against CSC populations, a consequence not observed with either drug alone or in 2D cultures.

Nanoparticle-based therapies

Nanotechnology-based approaches are advancing rapidly, and nanoparticle-based therapies enable precise delivery and enhanced efficacy against BCSCs. Current studies have engineered nanoparticles with the ability to carry both chemotherapeutic drugs and cell lineage inducers, thereby controlling drug release in anoxic CSC habitats and decreasing resistance mediated by stem-like characteristics [248]. A study employed dual-agent nanocarriers that respond to signals within the tumor microenvironment. They found that the combined delivery of all-trans retinoic acid and camptothecin suppresses cell proliferation and impedes recurrence in breast cancer models [248, 249]. Furthermore, the use of 3D organoids has revealed that bispecific aptamer-conjugated nanoparticles, targeting both tumor and stromal cells, improved targeted cell death and specificity, thereby developing new opportunities in precise phototherapeutic approaches [250].

Predictive drug screening

3D models that incorporate stromal components enhance therapeutic predictability and enable assessment of therapies in an environment that mimics the invasive capacity and molecular characteristics of breast cancer subtypes, thereby providing a more robust framework for large-scale analysis [251, 252]. This is achieved by incorporating fibroblasts, immune cells, and patient-sourced scaffolding. Receptor-specific molecules are another feasible strategy for selectively targeting markers that confer stemness. Consequently, 3D systems are essential for accurate screening and separation of compounds exhibiting authentic CSC activity. Moreover, 3D models have more accurately captured the impact of therapeutic drugs on cell viability and resistance, thereby underscoring the need to use complex 3D systems for accurate laboratory testing [253, 254]. Although spheroid-based systems provide substantial advantages over conventional monolayer cultures, important functional differences exist among current 3D platforms. Scaffold-free spheroids are relatively inexpensive, reproducible, and suitable for HTS; however, they often lack extracellular matrix complexity and vascular-like organization. In contrast, scaffold-based systems better mimic matrix-mediated signaling and tumor stiffness but introduce variability related to biomaterial composition and batch-to-batch inconsistency. Patient-derived organoids preserve tumor heterogeneity more effectively and show promise for personalized medicine applications, yet they remain technically demanding and difficult to standardize across laboratories. Microfluidic and organ-on-chip platforms provide dynamic perfusion, immune-cell integration, and spatiotemporal control of the microenvironment, but their high cost and technical complexity currently limit widespread implementation

Critical limitations of current 3D spheroid models

Despite their advantages, 3D spheroid models remain imperfect representations of in vivo tumors. A major limitation is the lack of functional vascularization, which restricts accurate modeling of drug delivery, oxygen transport, and immune cell trafficking [217, 246, 255, 256]. In addition, many spheroid systems fail to fully recapitulate the complexity of the tumor immune microenvironment, particularly adaptive immune responses, which are critical determinants of therapeutic outcomes. This limitation is particularly important because immune-cell activation, persistence, cytotoxicity, and exhaustion dynamics can substantially influence antitumor efficacy, underlining the need for more immune-competent preclinical models when evaluating therapeutic response and translational potential [257, 258]. Furthermore, differences in spheroid size, cellular composition, and culture conditions pose significant challenges to reproducibility and cross-laboratory standardization, limiting the utility of these systems for large-scale drug screening [66]. While scaffold-based and hybrid systems improve physiological relevance, they also introduce additional complexity and cost, hindering scalability and widespread adoption.

Importantly, although 3D models generally outperform 2D cultures in predicting therapeutic responses, substantial discrepancies between preclinical findings and patient outcomes continue to persist. Many spheroid systems still fail to fully replicate systemic physiological factors such as vascular perfusion, immune surveillance, endocrine signaling, and metastatic dissemination, all of which significantly influence treatment response in patients. Furthermore, differences in culture protocols, biomaterial composition, and analytical endpoints complicate cross-study validation and limit regulatory acceptance. Consequently, while 3D spheroids represent an important intermediate platform between 2D cultures and animal models, their predictive value for clinical decision-making remains only partially validated and requires further prospective correlation with patient-derived clinical data [259, 260].

Beyond these fundamental limitations, additional technical challenges further constrain the application of 3D spheroid models. The inherent structural complexity of 3D spheroids (e.g., size differences arising from internal diffusion barriers) can hinder the standardization of drug-response observations. Common issues that can negatively affect experimental outcomes and the reliability of drug screening include differences in spheroid size and morphology, challenges in standardizing protocols, and difficulties in obtaining reproducible results [113] Furthermore, such spheroids can be technically demanding when it comes to imaging due to light dispersion, limited dye penetration, and to achieve high-resolution quantitative analysis, advanced imaging techniques are required [261–264]. Many 3D organoids and spheroids lack crucial components needed to analyze interactions within the tumor microenvironment and assess responses to specific drugs [265]. Challenges are encountered in obtaining consistent and reproducible results; for example, scaffold-free microfluidic complexity may require specialized equipment, increasing operational costs [195].

Today’s challenges in implementing and optimizing 3D models in oncology can be categorized into methodology, biological, and clinical/commercial utility. Challenges in methodology include the lack of standardized protocols for culturing and assessing 3D cultures, leading to differences in size, morphology, cellular composition, and observed outcomes across laboratories. These variations usually diminish reproducibility, complicating comparisons of interlaboratory results and, consequently, making it difficult to develop reliable 3D models. In addition, the intricacies of extracting data and viewing images, such as advanced microscopy for spheroid cell analysis from 3D models, add to the already present technical burdens and can be time-intensive, requiring specialized tools [16, 266, 267]. Another existing issue is the industrialization of 3D models for screening, such as sophisticated systems that often require bioreactors, microfluidic devices, or platforms that use bioprinters, leading to an increase in expenses and demand for skilled technical personnel [268–270].

Biological hurdles usually arise from the challenges of fully recapitulating the native tumor microenvironment. Although 3D systems can better mirror certain aspects of tissue morphology and cell-cell interactions than 2D models, they still struggle to replicate the intricacies of living tumors. Some pressing limitations include inadequate vascularization, limited diversity of immune and stromal cells, and difficulty in maintaining the tissue’s native complexity and cellular heterogeneity over time [77, 271]. Many 3D organoids and spheroids lack crucial components required to analyze interactions within the tumor microenvironment and to assess responses to certain drugs [265].

Obstacles to practical implementation and commercialization pose major challenges, particularly in scalability, quality control, and seamless integration with existing drug screening protocols. Furthermore, scaling up the production of 3D models while maintaining the level of reproducibility required for high-throughput industrial pharmaceutical screening remains difficult. Another significant challenge lies in sourcing consistent raw materials, including matrices such as Matrigel [74, 272]. Adherence to certain regulations and rigorous validation standards further complicates the clinical and commercial application of 3D systems. Moreover, the pharmaceutical sector faces resistance to adopting 3D models because many existing drug development workflows are optimized for 2D cultures and animal models, meaning the shift to 3D models requires expensive retooling of both infrastructure and specialized personnel [269, 273].

Collectively, current evidence suggests that the future of breast cancer 3D modeling lies not in a single platform but rather in the progressive integration of complementary technologies into multi-component systems capable of simultaneously reproducing tumor architecture, stromal interactions, immune dynamics, and patient-specific heterogeneity. In this conceptual framework, scaffold-free spheroids may serve as scalable screening tools, organoids as patient-specific disease models, and microfluidic systems as dynamic platforms for modeling vascular and immune interactions. The convergence of these technologies with genomic profiling, artificial intelligence-assisted image analysis, and precision oncology approaches may ultimately improve the predictive accuracy of preclinical drug testing and facilitate the development of clinically translatable therapeutic strategies.

Overcoming these current challenges is crucial for understanding the full potential of 3D models and their role in cancer research and drug development, enabling more reliable and clinically applicable drug testing.

Translational relevance

Recent advances in sophisticated three-dimensional (3D) tumor models, such as patient-derived organoids (PDOs), bioprinted cancer constructs, and micro-organospheres, are rapidly bridging the gap between laboratory research and clinical practice. These models achieve this by more accurately replicating tumor heterogeneity, the complexity of the native microenvironment, and patient-specific therapeutic responses. In addition to closely mirroring the characteristics of native tumors, they also enhance drug discovery by enabling high-throughput, physiologically relevant screening platforms that offer improved prediction of clinical outcomes and toxicity compared to traditional 2D cultures or animal models. However, despite these advantages, not all 3D platforms exhibit equal predictive performance, as variations in extracellular matrix composition, culture duration, oxygen gradients, and spheroid size can significantly influence therapeutic responses and inter-study reproducibility [274]. For instance, patient biopsies that were used to construct 3D bio-printed, vascularized cancer models have proven to be robust in the identification of novel therapeutic targets and immune checkpoints [275, 276]. Figure 4 summarizes the comparative strengths and limitations of major 3D tumor platforms and highlights the principal biological and technical factors contributing to the persistent translational gap between preclinical modeling and clinical outcomes.

Co-Clinical Models: Strong correlations between PDOs, xenograft-derived organoid cultures, and clinical outcomes have been observed, thereby facilitating their integration into co-clinical trials, where they serve as predictive avatars to personalize treatments and identify markers associated with drug sensitivity or resistance [275, 277, 278]. Nevertheless, several studies have reported inconsistencies between PDO drug sensitivity profiles and actual patient outcomes, particularly in highly heterogeneous or treatment-resistant tumors, highlighting that current models may still incompletely capture systemic immune interactions, stromal remodeling, and metastatic behavior [279]. These models can be further enhanced by the addition of technologies such as microfluidics and bioprinting, providing large-scale platforms to create uniform cancer constructs and aiding the assessment of real-time drug responses within suitable timeframes [280, 281]. Moreover, such models have already been integrated into targeted therapy development frameworks, serving as a guide for treatment selection, elucidating pathways of drug resistance, and facilitating the identification of new biomarkers for early detection [275, 282]. As these sophisticated technologies approach clinical translation, their broader implementation remains constrained by several unresolved issues, including regulatory approval pathways, high production costs, ethical considerations, limited scalability, and the lack of universally uniform protocols for model generation, validation, and quality control. These barriers contribute to the persistent translational gap between promising experimental findings and routine clinical application, particularly across diverse healthcare settings and patient populations [283–285].

Current barriers to clinical translation and standardization

Despite the substantial advances made in 3D spheroid systems, several challenges continue to limit their widespread clinical translation and adoption as standardized preclinical platforms. One of the principal barriers is the lack of harmonized protocols for spheroid generation, culture conditions, and analytical endpoints [30]. Variations in cell seeding density, culture media composition, extracellular matrix components, spheroid size, and assay methodologies can lead to considerable inter-study and inter-laboratory variability, reducing reproducibility and limiting cross-platform comparisons [286]. Another important challenge is the incomplete recapitulation of tumor complexity [287]. Although spheroids reproduce key features of tumor architecture and cell–cell interactions more effectively than conventional 2D cultures, many models still lack functional vascular, immune, neural, and stromal components that influence tumor progression and therapeutic response in vivo [288]. Consequently, the predictive value of spheroid-based findings for clinical outcomes remains variable across different cancer contexts [289]. Clinical implementation is further constrained by the limited availability of standardized validation frameworks that link spheroid-derived results to patient responses [290]. Large-scale multicenter studies and prospective co-clinical investigations are still needed to establish robust performance benchmarks and demonstrate clinical utility [291]. In addition, regulatory pathways for the qualification and acceptance of advanced 3D cancer models remain underdeveloped, creating additional barriers to their integration into drug development pipelines and precision oncology workflows [73, 291]. Addressing these limitations will require coordinated efforts toward protocol harmonization, establishment of reporting standards, multicenter validation initiatives, and integration of advanced technologies such as vascularized co-culture systems, microfluidics, artificial intelligence, and patient-derived platforms. Overcoming these challenges will be essential for realizing the full translational potential of 3D spheroid models in cancer research and personalized medicine [21].

Future directions

Building on the limitations outlined above, several emerging technologies are actively addressing the remaining gaps in 3D cancer modeling. Organ-on-a-chip systems have been introduced, offering a dynamic approach by creating nutrient gradients and mimicking tissue-to-tissue interactions. Such platforms enhance the replication of physiological conditions, enabling in situ tracking and rapid, high-volume drug screening, thereby addressing challenges in scalability and reproducibility [292–294]. Although organ-on-a-chip technologies provide superior control over dynamic physiological parameters compared with conventional spheroid systems, their technical complexity, specialized fabrication requirements, and limited accessibility may restrict widespread adoption in routine laboratory workflows [295, 296].

3D bioprinting represents a paradigm shift because it enables the construction of complex multicellular cancer models with detailed spatial organization and modifiable extracellular matrix properties. This advancement not only improves consistency and enables large-scale deployment but also supports patient-specific model generation and the incorporation of vascular structures, which are crucial for recapitulating native tumor architecture [270, 297]. Despite these advantages, differences in bioink composition, printing resolution, and post-printing cellular viability continue to affect reproducibility and may limit cross-platform standardization between laboratories [298, 299]. Importantly, increasing attention is being directed toward synthetic or chemically defined hydrogel systems, including functionalized polyethylene glycol (PEG)-based matrices, alginate blends, and other tunable biomaterials, as alternatives to poorly defined natural matrices such as Matrigel [300–302]. These engineered hydrogels can be designed with controlled stiffness, degradability, ligand presentation, and batch-consistent biochemical composition, thereby improving reproducibility and enabling systematic investigation of how defined extracellular matrix cues regulate tumor growth, stemness, invasion, and therapeutic response [301, 303, 304]. Although such materials may not fully recapitulate the biochemical complexity of native tumor ECM, their modularity and standardizable composition make them promising platforms to improve the reliability and regulatory translatability of scaffold-based and bioprinted cancer models [299–301]. Furthermore, converging bioprinting, organoids, and microfluidics yield integrative, physio-mimetic platforms that facilitate co-culturing, multi-tissue interconnectivity, and in-depth cellular profiling, thereby addressing the previously mentioned methodological and biological barriers present in 3D models [305, 306].

Standardization and multicenter validation frameworks

A critical but under-addressed priority is establishing harmonized minimum reporting standards for 3D spheroid and organoid studies [21]. As discussed throughout this review, the absence of universally accepted criteria for spheroid diameter, viability thresholds, passage number, matrix composition, seeding density, and analytical endpoints is a primary driver of inter-laboratory irreproducibility and limits the regulatory acceptability of preclinical 3D data. Future efforts should prioritize developing consensus reporting guidelines analogous to the MIAME standards for microarray data or the CONSORT framework for clinical trials, adapted specifically for 3D cancer model research [307]. Concretely, this would require minimum reporting of spheroid size distribution with tolerance ranges, viability gradients confirmed by orthogonal assays, matrix composition with batch identifiers, and parallel benchmarking against a standardized 2D reference and at least one patient-derived comparator [308, 309]. Multicenter validation studies, in which identical cell lines, bioinks, or patient-derived materials are cultured under protocol-defined conditions across independent laboratories, would provide the cross-institutional data needed to identify which experimental variables most critically drive inconsistencies in outcomes [307, 310]. Such studies are feasible within existing academic consortium structures and represent a tractable near-term priority for the field.

Vascularized and immune-competent next-generation platforms

Two biological deficiencies identified consistently across this review, the absence of functional vascularization and the incomplete representation of adaptive immune responses, will require purpose-built engineering solutions rather than incremental refinements of existing spheroid systems. Vascularized organoid-on-chip models, in which perfusable microvascular networks are integrated into tumor constructs using endothelial co-culture or sacrificial templating techniques, represent the most promising current approach to recapitulating oxygen delivery, drug pharmacokinetics, and immune cell trafficking simultaneously [311–313]. Future platforms should move beyond static endothelial co-culture toward dynamically perfused systems capable of sustaining physiological shear stress, barrier function, and angiogenic remodeling over the timescales relevant to drug response assessment [314, 315]. In parallel, immune-competent models incorporating autologous tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, patient-matched macrophage polarization states, and regulatory T cell populations would enable meaningful evaluation of immune checkpoint inhibitors and other immunotherapeutic strategies within a 3D context, an application for which current spheroid systems are largely unsuited [316, 317]. Patient-derived peripheral blood mononuclear cells and tumor-resident immune cells, integrated into bioprinted or microfluidic constructs using standardized co-culture ratios and defined cytokine environments, offer a concrete path toward this goal within the reach of current bioengineering capabilities [318].

AI-assisted multi-omics integration and digital twin modeling

The increasing volumetric and molecular complexity of advanced 3D platforms generates data at a scale that exceeds the interpretive capacity of conventional analytical approaches. Artificial intelligence and machine learning tools offer a concrete solution to several outstanding methodological challenges simultaneously [319]. For spheroid characterization, convolutional neural network-based image analysis pipelines can automate the quantification of size, morphology, viability gradients, and invasion fronts from brightfield and fluorescence images at throughputs incompatible with manual annotation, thereby reducing observer bias [320, 321]. For multi-omics integration, dimensionality reduction and network inference algorithms can identify mechanistically coherent transcriptomic, proteomic, and epigenomic signatures in 3D-derived BCSC populations that would otherwise be obscured by the noise inherent in bulk analyses of heterogeneous spheroid cultures [322–324]. Critically, integrating patient-derived organoid drug-response data with matched clinical outcomes using supervised machine learning models offers a path to validating which in vitro 3D phenotypes are genuinely predictive of treatment resistance or recurrence, the key translational question that remains unanswered for most current platforms [325]. Digital twin approaches, in which a computational model of an individual patient’s tumor is parameterized using organoid drug-response profiles, spatial transcriptomics, and clinical imaging data, represent an ambitious but tractable longer-term framework for connecting 3D preclinical findings to personalized therapeutic decisions [326, 327].

Co-clinical trial integration and regulatory pathway development.

The translational impact of advanced three-dimensional (3D) cancer models will depend not only on continued biological and engineering refinement, but also on their integration into prospective clinical trial frameworks and therapeutic decision-making pipelines [328]. Co-clinical strategies using patient-derived organoids (PDOs) generated in parallel with patient enrollment have demonstrated promising predictive capacity for treatment response and resistance across multiple tumor types [329]. However, broader clinical implementation requires standardized tissue-processing workflows, improved efficiency in organoid establishment across heterogeneous breast cancer subtypes, and clinically actionable drug-response timelines compatible with treatment planning [330, 331]. Regulatory agencies, including the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the European Medicines Agency (EMA), have increasingly recognized the value of organoid and microphysiological systems in drug development, although robust fit-for-purpose validation criteria and clinically relevant concordance standards remain insufficiently defined [332]. Collectively, advances in platform standardization, AI-assisted analytics, and co-clinical validation frameworks may enable 3D spheroid and organoid systems to become clinically actionable tools for precision oncology in breast cancer.

Collectively, these innovations are expected to redefine preclinical cancer modeling and accelerate the transition toward precision oncology. The emerging integration of vascularized organoid-on-chip systems, immune-competent platforms, AI-assisted multi-omics analysis, and co-clinical trial frameworks may collectively improve translational predictive accuracy by addressing the biological, technical, and clinical limitations of current 3D models (Fig. 4). Taken together, these advances suggest a sequential pathway through which 3D spheroid technologies may evolve from experimental research tools into clinically actionable platforms. Conclusion

The evidence synthesized in this review points to a clear and actionable conclusion: the translational value of 3D spheroid models in breast cancer research is not determined by the technology itself, but by how deliberately it is selected, validated, and integrated within a broader experimental framework. Across BCSC biology, signaling pathway analysis, drug resistance modeling, and therapeutic screening, the studies reviewed here consistently show that 3D systems generate mechanistic insights that 2D cultures cannot, but only when the platform is matched to the biological question and its limitations are explicitly acknowledged. As illustrated in Fig. 4, therapeutic resistance and cancer stem cell enrichment form a self-reinforcing feedback loop that is uniquely accessible in spatially organized 3D systems and that remains largely invisible in conventional monolayer cultures.

What emerges from this body of evidence is not a hierarchy of platforms, but a case for strategic integration. Simple spheroids, matrix-embedded systems, co-culture models, patient-derived organoids, and microfluidic devices each capture distinct and complementary aspects of tumor biology. The field’s next critical step is therefore not the development of a single superior model, but the establishment of standardized, context-driven workflows that combine these platforms in a principled way guided by cancer subtype, clinical endpoint, and the specific translational objective at hand. As outlined in Fig. 5, achieving this goal will require coordinated progress in model standardization, biological validation, integration of advanced bioengineering technologies, patient-specific platforms, and prospective clinical validation.

Collectively, the continued integration of advanced bioengineering technologies with clinically relevant 3D models, as outlined in the translational framework proposed in Fig. 5, is expected to improve the predictive accuracy of preclinical research and accelerate the development of more effective, personalized therapeutic strategies for breast cancer management.

Importantly, this review extends beyond descriptive summaries of three-dimensional breast cancer models by proposing a fit-for-purpose, context-driven framework for the strategic selection and integration of preclinical systems based on the biological question, cancer subtype, and translational objective. Unlike prior reviews that primarily focus on the advantages of individual 3D platforms, we critically evaluate the limitations, inconsistent outcomes, and translational gaps associated with spheroids, organoids, scaffold-based systems, and microfluidic technologies, while emphasizing that no single model can fully recapitulate tumor complexity in isolation. By positioning these platforms along a continuum of biological fidelity and clinical relevance, this framework provides a practical foundation for integrating complementary model systems in future translational research, thereby supporting more predictive drug testing, biomarker discovery, and the development of precision oncology strategies in breast cancer.
