Dew formation is associated with higher ecosystem productivity across diverse ecosystems
Authors: Mengyun Sun, Wenzhe Jiao, Cynthia Gerlein-Safdi, Max Berkelhammer, Lixin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Dew is a common yet often overlooked water input in terrestrial ecosystems. Current dew research is constrained to a limited number of leaf-level field measurements, leaving a key knowledge gap in how dew affects vegetation productivity at the ecosystem scale. Using eddy covariance data from the National Ecological Observatory Network from 2017 to 2022, we quantify characteristics of dew formation in the U.S. and examine their impacts on vegetation gross primary productivity across two focal ecosystem types (grasslands and forests) and climatic regions (i.e., drylands and non-drylands). We find that dew formation is significantly associated with higher gross primary productivity across grasslands, forests, drylands, and non-drylands, highlighting the ecological contribution of dew to ecosystem productivity. Although its contribution is smaller than that of major climatic predictors, higher gross primary productivity is more pronounced with longer dew duration, particularly in grasslands and drylands. Our findings demonstrate that dew plays a significant role in shaping ecosystem carbon dynamics, particularly in drylands, and is likely to have broad implications for future carbon-water feedback in a changing climate.

Introduction

Water is a fundamental but often limiting resource for vegetation growth, especially in drylands1. Dew, although only a small component of the water cycle, is a common phenomenon that has been widely observed in many inland deserts2–5, coastal regions6,7, and plateau areas8,9. Although rainfall is undoubtedly the primary source of water for most ecosystems10, dew can, in some cases, provide greater inputs in both quantity and frequency, particularly in certain water-scarce regions or during extreme drought years5,7,11,12. Due to its high frequency or long duration4,13,14, dew can be a critical supplementary source of moisture in drylands, thereby supporting plant growth and sustaining plant survival15,16.

With intensified global warming, global vegetation is facing increasing stress from more pronounced water constraints associated with more severe droughts and an increased evaporative demand, presenting a significant challenge to vegetation survival17–19. Dew, therefore, is expected to become increasingly important not only as a water source but also as a key driver of ecosystem functions2,20–22. Multiple studies have demonstrated that dew has a measurable impact on carbon, water, and energy fluxes across leaf surfaces by suppressing transpiration23, reducing short-term carbon exchange24,25, enhancing leaf water potential20, and influencing local microclimates, including temperature, leaf albedo, and vapor pressure deficit (VPD)24,26,27. Moreover, a survey from an individual flux site has found that dew benefits grassland net ecosystem production in the early morning28.

Dew forms when atmospheric moisture condenses into liquid water on a surface that is passively cooled by radiation29,30. Although dew formation is primarily dependent on clear skies, high relative humidity, and low wind speed, the importance of specific climatic drivers diverges across different environments and seasons31. In dry areas or during dry seasons, vapor condensation is primarily limited by available atmospheric moisture and temperature32, but in wet environments or seasons with sufficient water vapor, radiative cooling is the dominant limiting factor14. However, given that recent warming has led to an increasing atmospheric dryness (VPD)33–35 and higher incoming longwave radiation36, several studies show that dew formation is likely going to decline on a global scale7,37. Reduced dew formation, together with higher temperature38, more frequent drought36,39, and more variable rainfall17,18 may further exacerbate plant water stress and vegetation growth reduction across various ecosystems19,40.

In theory, small changes at the stomatal level could potentially lead to substantial impacts at the landscape scale24,41. However, current research on dew has so far predominantly focused on a limited number of field measurements at the leaf or plant level. At the ecosystem scale, it is unclear how dew varies across ecosystems and climatic conditions, and how changes in dew formation affect vegetation productivity. These knowledge gaps hinder the accurate assessment of dew contribution to ecosystem functions at a broader scale, limiting a better understanding of the water-vegetation relationship and robust predictions of the trajectory of global water and carbon dynamics.

The National Ecological Observatory Network (NEON) provides standardized eddy covariance measurements and meteorological variables across a diverse domain. In particular, canopy temperature (Tc) observations enable estimation of dew formation independently of eddy covariance data. Thus, in this study, we first use NEON measurements from 2017 to 2022 at 36 sites across the U.S. to quantify dew formation. Sites are specifically grouped into forests (mainly evergreen needleleaf forest, deciduous broadleaf forest, and mixed forest) and grasslands (including grassland, open shrubland, and savanna), which represent key biomes at broader spatial scales in the study region42. Gross primary productivity is a widely used proxy for vegetation productivity. We evaluate the effects of dew formation on GPP in forests and grasslands. A comparison between drylands and non-drylands is also conducted, as dew may contribute differently to ecosystem water balance in these contrasting climates12,43. We then analyze the exact contribution of dew duration to GPP using machine learning methods. The relative contribution of dew to GPP is compared with that of other climatic factors to assess its relative importance. Through these analyses, this study not only advances our scientific understanding of the ecological significance of dew but also informs sustainable land management and conservation strategies.

Results

Dew characteristics across different ecosystems and climatic regions

The spatial patterns of dew characteristics (dew frequency and dew duration) were investigated at 36 sites across different canopies and climatic regions in the U.S. from 2017 to 2022 (Fig. 1). Detailed information for each site is provided in Supplementary Table 1. The optimal nocturnal dew window was identified as 20:00 to 7:00 the following day, covering over 85% of dew events across all the sites and more than 80% across different ecosystem types and climate regions (Supplementary Table 2, and Supplementary Fig. 1). The median of dew frequency was 10.8% in forests and 13.1% in grasslands, and 9.5% in drylands and 10.9% in non-drylands (Fig. 1a). The median dew duration was 2.6 h in forests and 3.0 h in grasslands, and 2.8 h in drylands and 2.6 h in non-drylands (Fig. 1b). Using a two-sided Mann-Whitney U test, we found that dew frequency did not differ between forests and grasslands, nor between drylands and non-drylands (All p values > 0.05; Fig. 1a). By comparing the dew characteristics in this study from 2017 to 2022 with the results from Ritter et al.42 during the period of 2015 to 2017 based on the same NEON sites, we found that the dew frequency and dew duration in grasslands were significantly decreased, although the decline was much stronger for dew duration (Supplementary Figs. 2a, b). To quantify site-level changes while minimizing potential sensitivity to uneven record lengths across sites, we applied the Mann–Kendall test and Theil–Sen slopes (hours year−1) at each site (Supplementary Fig. 3). Site-level trends were generally small and not statistically significant, suggesting limited change over the study period (Supplementary Fig. 3).

To ensure robust estimates of dew formation and subsequent dew characteristics, we employed both empirical and Clausius-Clapeyron-based methods to calculate the dew point temperature (Td) (See “Methods”). Specifically, we established the relationship between Td from the two methods, binned by relative humidity (RH) (Supplementary Fig. 4a). The coefficient of determination (R2) between the two methods increased from 0.65 at low RH intervals (0–10%) to 1.00 at higher RH levels (60–100%) (Supplementary Fig. 4a). This trend was further supported by the increase in R2 when considering a subset with RH above 60% versus all data (Supplementary Figs. 4b, c). These findings indicated that the empirical equation provided a reliable estimation of dew formation, especially at high RH levels. Given the simplicity and robustness of the empirical method, we primarily use it to estimate dew formation and assess its impact.

The impacts of dew formation on ecosystem productivity

We quantified the effects of dew on vegetation productivity by comparing next-day GPP (6:00–18:00) after dew nights and non-dew nights across diverse ecosystem types and climatic regions (Fig. 2). A dew night is defined as nights with a minimum cumulative dew duration longer than 0.5 h (See Methods). The results showed that, regardless of whether nighttime or daytime partitioning was applied, GPP following dew nights was significantly higher than GPP after non-dew nights, both in grassland sites and forest sites (p < 0.001; Fig. 2a, c), as well as in drylands and non-drylands (p < 0.001; Fig. 2b, d). Full statistical results using two-sided t-tests are provided in Supplementary Table 3. The finding that next-day GPP was higher after dew nights than after non-dew nights was consistent across all tested minimum dew-duration thresholds (1, 3, and 5 h) (Supplementary Fig. 5). To minimize uncertainty in GPP data from flux partitioning methods, we repeated our analysis using GPP from the daytime partitioning method and compared it with GPP from the nighttime partitioning method (Fig. 2c, d). The influence of dew formation on GPP was found to be consistent between the two methods (Fig. 2). To strengthen counterfactual inference (i.e., comparability between dew and non-dew conditions), we fitted a mixed-effects model with site and year random intercepts and presented the estimated dew effect sizes on GPP (Supplementary Fig. 6). These mixed-effects estimates show the same positive direction and overall pattern as our primary analysis (Fig. 2), supporting the robustness of the inferred positive dew–GPP association.

We further tested the robustness of the results by reclassifying drylands and non-drylands using both Aridity Index (AI) values and different mean annual precipitation thresholds (Supplementary Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 8). The analysis showed that dew formation is significantly associated with higher GPP under all classifications, with only minor variations in significance levels (Supplementary Fig. 8). The AI-based approach was ultimately chosen as the primary classification standard because it incorporates both water supply and atmospheric water demand44.

When examining dew effects on next-day GPP at individual sites, 36.4% and 48.0% of grassland and forest sites, respectively, showed significantly higher GPP after dew nights based on night-time partitioning (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). After controlling for multiple testing across sites using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (BH-FDR), the percentages decreased to 27.3% and 44.0%, respectively (Supplementary Table 4), suggesting the effects of dew were not universal across sites. Nevertheless, the distribution of site-level effect sizes showed consistently positive central tendencies (positive medians) across forests and grasslands, as well as across drylands and non-drylands (Supplementary Table 5). These results indicate that dew is a modest yet systematic predictor of next-day GPP, with effects that vary across sites and biomes.

To explore an exploratory framing of the relationship between dew formation and GPP, we used a structural equation model (SEM) approach45 to quantify the indirect pathways whereby dew influences GPP via morning VPD and leaf temperature, for both forests and grasslands (Supplementary Fig. 11). The SEM results showed that dew formation was significantly associated with daily GPP for both forests and grasslands, with the VPD-mediated pathway being the dominant contributor to the dew-GPP relationship (Supplementary Fig. 11).

The contribution of dew duration to GPP using machine learning methods

We assessed the contribution of dew duration on GPP using Boosted Regression Tree (BRT) models across 100 resampled runs. Separate models were built for each of the vegetation types (forests and grasslands) and climate regions (drylands and non-drylands). In addition to dew duration, key climatic predictors include RH, air temperature (Ta), soil water content (SWC), and net radiation (Rn). Based on the relative contributions from BRT models, the median values of dew duration to GPP consistently range from 5.2% to 6.8% (Fig. 3). In both grasslands and drylands, GPP was largely influenced by Rn, accounting for 33.6% and 29.1%, respectively (Fig. 3b, c). However, in forests and non-drylands, thermal energy variables (Ta and Rn) showed stronger contributions than moisture-related variables (RH and SWC), with median contributions of 23.7% and 26.6% in forests, and 24.3% and 26.7% in non-drylands, respectively (Fig. 3a, c). In addition, permutation-based importance was quantified as a robustness check for variable importance estimates from the BRT models (Supplementary Table 6). Overall, permutation-based importance was consistent with the patterns revealed by the relative contribution analysis (Supplementary Table 6 and Fig. 3). We further evaluated model performance using site-blocked 5-fold cross-validation to remove within-site dependence between training and test data. To further evaluate whether soil water availability might confound the observed relationship between dew and GPP, we examined the interactive effects of dew and SWC using multiple linear regression (MLR) and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The results showed no significant interaction between dew and SWC (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9).

To validate the robustness of the attribution results, we additionally implemented Random Forest (RF) models. The SHAP-based rankings of predictors from RF models were largely consistent with the BRT results, confirming the persistent role of dew duration and other climatic drivers in influencing GPP (Supplementary Fig. 13). This cross-model agreement reinforces the reliability of our findings. Given the superior predictive performance and interpretability of the BRT models, we used them as the primary framework for the attribution analysis throughout this study.

The non-linear relationship between dew duration and GPP fitted by BRT models is shown in partial dependent plots (Fig. 4). We observed a consistent increase in GPP in grasslands as dew duration extended, whereas GPP in forests remained unaffected by dew duration up to 8.5 hours (Fig. 4a). However, when the dew duration exceeded 8.5 h, GPP in both forests and grasslands showed a more dramatic rise (Fig. 4a). Similar to grasslands, GPP in drylands also showed a continuous increasing trend with dew duration (Fig. 4b). In contrast, the effect of dew duration on GPP in non-drylands remained stable across dew duration up to 10.0 h, after which the impact increased slightly (Fig. 4b). Discussion

The similar distribution of dew across diverse canopies and climatic regions

We found that dew formation characteristics, including dew frequency and dew duration, were similar across sites with forests and grasslands, as well as sites in drylands and non-drylands across the entire U.S. (Fig. 1). The similar distribution of dew frequency and duration is largely due to the combined effect of vegetation adaptation strategies and climatic conditions across different regions. In dry areas with scarce atmospheric moisture and high temperatures, grasslands and shrubs often predominate. The simpler canopy structure of these plant functional types creates favorable conditions for dew deposition with effective radiative cooling42, while rapid evaporation limits dew accumulation and duration. In non-drylands with denser canopies, even with abundant moisture, complex vertical stratification can reduce surface radiative cooling and obstruct airflow, inhibiting heat exchange between the surface and ambient air46 and dew formation. Moreover, coastal areas are likely to benefit from increased moisture input from wind circulation over the sea and lake47, which may provide favorable conditions for dew formation. Consistent with this, our results suggest that sites near large water bodies, such as oceans and lake shores, tend to exhibit high dew frequency (Fig. 1a). Thus, although dew is widely distributed across different climates and canopies, dew characteristics may be similar due to competing meteorological and biophysical controls.

Potential declining trend of dew characteristics and its impacts

Our analysis revealed a declining trend in dew characteristics across the U.S. from 2015 to 2022, especially in grasslands (Supplementary Fig. 2a, b). This finding aligns with multiple observational, experimental, and modeling results. For example, a controlled-warming experiment on the Tibetan Plateau of China demonstrated that warming reduced dew duration by 10.3% and dew amount by 41.6%–91.1%37. Similarly, modeling results suggest that dew yield could decline by 27.0% during summer over the Mediterranean Basin by the end of 20807. Collectively, these results suggest a substantial weakening of dew formation over recent years, potentially indicating a broader trend associated with ongoing climate change. However, because our analysis spans only 2015–2022, these results should be interpreted as short-record trends. Longer observational records are needed to reliably assess long-term trajectories.

We identify three plausible explanations for this short-record based declining trend. First, with global warming and the associated rise in VPD34, key climatic drivers of dew deposition, such as Ta and RH, are also expected to change accordingly48,49. Second, changes in land use and land cover, particularly urban expansion, can alter surface albedo50, evapotranspiration rates51, and nocturnal cooling52, thereby affecting the conditions favorable for dew deposition and duration32,51,53. Third, aerosol loading from natural processes (e.g., dust storms and volcanic eruptions) or anthropogenic activities (e.g., fossil fuel combustion, industrial emissions, biomass burning) can affect nocturnal cooling by modifying longwave radiation fluxes54,55, ultimately influencing the microclimatic environment in which dew forms. Although these variables were not directly assessed in this study due to data limitations, they represent plausible and important mechanisms that merit further investigation.

The potential ecological consequences of reduced dew formation are considerable, especially in drylands and during extreme drought years16,43. A decline in dew could diminish its role as a supplementary water source, potentially reducing its capacity to alleviate water stress and support plant physiological processes. Moreover, reduced dew input would no longer deliver the vegetation benefits it currently offers, including enhanced photosynthetic activity8,56,57, improved water-use efficiency58, and offsetting drought stress28. From a carbon cycle perspective, these physiological constraints could lower GPP, particularly in ecosystems where dew substantially contributes to daily water balance, thereby weakening their carbon uptake capacity and resilience to climate extremes.

Implications of the observed association between dew formation and ecosystem productivity

Our results found that daily GPP after nights with dew events was significantly higher than GPP after nights without dew events (Fig. 2), suggesting that vegetation productivity tends to be higher following nights with dew formation. This finding is consistent with previous field studies, which have shown that the presence of dew can mitigate drought stress28, improve plant water status20,59, and promote plant growth8,57. Such an association between dew formation and productivity is likely to contribute to greater carbon uptake at the canopy level when scaled up. Our study demonstrates this link and provides a continent-wide perspective on the broader ecological contribution of dew formation at the canopy scale.

Although some research has reported that dew-induced wetness may have negative effects, such as an increased risk of plant diseases or pests60–62, our results show that dew is significantly linked with higher GPP (Fig. 2). The SEM analysis provides additional exploratory support consistent with the hypothesized pathways linking dew to GPP via reductions in morning atmospheric water stress, as reflected by lower VPD. This VPD-mediated pathway showed a stronger association than the leaf temperature pathway across both forests and grasslands (Supplementary Fig. 11). It should be noted that the results do not rule out alternative causal structures, such as shared meteorological regimes that simultaneously influence both dew formation and GPP. Nevertheless, our results suggest that at the canopy scale, the positive effects of dew on synoptic-scale carbon uptake may outweigh potential suppressive impacts. Thus, overlooking the effect of dew input may lead to inaccurate quantification of plant productivity, particularly in drylands where an inconsistent relationship between precipitation and vegetation productivity is often observed1,63,64. This reinforces the importance of dew as a key, yet often overlooked, contributor to ecosystem productivity and underscores the need to consider its impacts when predicting and modeling the response of historical and future vegetation dynamics to climate.

While the BRT model results indicate that the relative contribution of dew duration to GPP is typically small (median approximately 6%), this contribution is of the same magnitude as other major climatic drivers. More importantly, compared with other climatic predictors across all vegetation types and climatic regions (Fig. 3), this metric reflects the overall proportion of variance explained by each variable across the entire dataset and time span. It does not fully represent the absolute magnitude of dew-induced GPP enhancement under specific environmental conditions. In drylands or during periods of high atmospheric demand (e.g., during drought episodes or early morning hours before stomatal closure), even modest dew input has the potential to support substantial gains in carbon uptake by alleviating water stress. For example, field observations in the arid Negev Desert have shown that dew can provide up to 63% of the water requirements of certain plant2. Additionally, in continental semi-arid grasslands, dew was estimated to contribute between 4.9% and 10.2% of annual precipitation, significantly reducing the water deficit during dry periods43. In such cases, the presence of dew can lead to short-term GPP increments that may be comparable in magnitude to those driven by other major climatic factors.

The varying impacts of dew duration on vegetation productivity across diverse ecosystem types and climatic regions

Despite its similar distribution across diverse canopies and climatic regions, dew has varying effects on vegetation productivity. Specifically, higher daily GPP following dew nights was associated with longer nightly dew duration in both grasslands and drylands (Fig. 4). In contrast, GPP in forests remained unchanged by dew duration up to 8.5 h, after which it increased dramatically (Fig. 4a). These results indicate that dew has a more immediate and direct impact on vegetation growth in grasslands and drylands, while its influence on forests becomes apparent only after exceeding a certain threshold.

This is jointly associated with the long-term differences in water needs, canopy structure, and climate adaptation across different environments. A simple vertical canopy structure for grasslands is more beneficial for leaf wetting and foliar water uptake65. Besides, grassland productivity in the U.S. is far more sensitive to variations in VPD than to variations in precipitation66. This suggests that dew, which can mitigate the effects of high VPD by providing surface moisture, may play a relatively more significant role in supporting grassland productivity compared to ecosystems less sensitive to VPD. In contrast, since forest growth typically relies more on deeper soil moisture and precipitation, it may not respond to dew input immediately upon its formation but rather exhibit a delayed response until dew accumulates to a certain threshold. Furthermore, in non-drylands with relatively abundant water sources, vegetation growth is less dependent on surface water, such as dew. As a result, GPP enhancement in non-drylands remained negligible regardless of dew duration (Fig. 4b).

It is worth noting that this study specifically focuses on grassland and forest ecosystems, and its findings should be cautiously extrapolated to other ecosystem types. For example, in croplands, the widespread use of irrigation may diminish the contribution of dew to productivity. Similarly, in wetlands, where water is rarely limited, the influence of dew is likely smaller.

Distinguishing the influence of soil water on dew impacts

Dew formation, as commonly defined, results from the condensation of atmospheric water vapor on surfaces5,67. However, in some cases, surface wetness may also arise from soil distillation68,69, i.e., condensation derived from soil moisture or vapor in the soil pore spaces, rather than directly from atmospheric moisture. In drylands such as the Negev Desert, for example, soil distillation can occasionally occur following rainfall events when moist soils release vapor that subsequently condenses on plant surfaces70,71.

However, our measurements were made at the canopy level, well above the soil surface, and under low-turbulence night-time conditions, the vertical transport of vapor from the soil to the canopy is likely limited72. This physical separation between the soil and the measurement height substantially limits the potential influence of soil distillation on our dew metrics. Moreover, any vapor released from the soil would still contribute to local atmospheric humidity to some degree, meaning that its contribution to dew formation would be partially captured by our approach.

In addition, we used MLR and ANOVA analysis to evaluate the independent and potential synergistic effects of dew and SWC on GPP (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). We found that both dew duration and SWC significantly contribute to GPP, while their interaction was not statistically significant (Supplementary Tables 8 and 9). This suggests that the contributions of dew and SWC to GPP are largely independent.

While these lines of evidence point to limited influence from soil distillation or SWC on our results, the possibility cannot be entirely ruled out. Future work combining high-resolution micrometeorological profiling, leaf wetness sensors, and isotopic analysis could help to disentangle the contributions of atmospheric dew and soil distillation, particularly in dryland ecosystems where both processes may be significant.

Summary

Our study provides robust evidence of associations between dew and higher vegetation productivity at the canopy scale at a continental extent. These results show that the benefits outweigh potential negative impacts, such as disease and pest risks, at the canopy scale, emphasizing that neglecting dew inputs, particularly in drylands, can lead to underestimating productivity and misrepresenting precipitation-vegetation relationships in models. The research specifically focuses on forest and grassland ecosystems, and drylands and non-drylands, representing key biomes and climate in the study region. Despite dew characteristics being similar across diverse canopies and climatic regions, longer dew duration led to more pronounced enhancements in vegetation productivity in grasslands and drylands compared to forests and non-drylands. These findings offer valuable insights into better understanding the complex relationship between vegetation growth and water availability across diverse canopies and climate conditions.

Nevertheless, certain ecosystem types, such as wetlands and intensively managed agricultural lands, remain underrepresented in our analysis. These systems may have distinct dew characteristics and play unique roles in ecosystem water balance and productivity. Exploring the role of dew across a broader range of ecosystems would be a valuable direction for future research, enhancing the comprehensiveness and generalizability of the findings. Moreover, because this study relies on observational data, the reported patterns reflect statistical associations rather than established causal mechanisms. Future work incorporating mechanistic experiments, controlled manipulations, or process-based modeling will be essential to establish causality and to elucidate the processes underlying dew–vegetation interactions. In addition, the lack of direct dew measurements at NEON sites limits our ability to validate dew-night identification or conduct misclassification checks. In the future, integrating direct dew measurements, such as leaf wetness sensors, into ecosystem monitoring networks would greatly improve the accuracy and interpretability of dew-related research.

With climate warming and associated rising VPD conditions likely reducing both dew occurrence and duration32,37,73, it is essential to investigate and predict the spatiotemporal dynamics of dew across multiple scales and its impacts on vegetation. Future research could benefit from incorporating longer-term observational datasets spanning a wider range of ecosystems, thereby enabling the detection of interdecadal patterns and variability in dew formation. In addition, coupling these extended observations with process-based or land surface models could improve our ability to project long-term trends in dew occurrence and intensity under future climate scenarios. Such modeling efforts would also provide a framework for assessing how changes in dew formation could influence the global carbon cycle, particularly through their effects on the capacity of dryland ecosystems to sustain photosynthesis and sequester carbon under increasing climatic stress.

Methods

Data source and site selection

The National Ecological Observatory Network (NEON, http://data.neonscience.org) provides half-hourly flux and meteorological measurements through eddy covariance flux towers at its terrestrial field sites across the U.S. All the analyses performed in this study are based on the dataset released by NEON74–78 and verified by the AmeriFlux (ameriflux.lbl.gov)79, with a half-hourly resolution from 2017 to 2022. All available NEON sites include biome information based on the International Geosphere-Biosphere Program (IGBP). According to IGBP, we first grouped these biome types into forests, including Evergreen Needleleaf Forest (ENF), Deciduous Broadleaf Forest (DBF), and Mixed Forest (MF); and grasslands, including Grasslands (GRA), Open Shrubland (OSH), and Savannas (SAV). Then all sites across the U.S. were selected based on the following criteria: a) biome classification as forests or grasslands and b) at least one dew event during the growing season (April to October). A total of 36 sites met these conditions and were included in the analysis, which included 25 forest sites and 11 grassland sites (Fig. 1).

To classify the sites according to their climatic region, we used the aridity index (AI), defined as the ratio of annual precipitation to potential evapotranspiration. AI values were sourced from the Version 3 of Global Aridity Index and Potential Evapotranspiration (ET0) Climate Database44. The study region was categorized into drylands (AI ≤ 0.65), which included 17 sites, and non-drylands (AI > 0.65), with 19 sites following into that category (Fig. 1). To assess the robustness of this classification, drylands and non-drylands were classified based on the mean annual precipitation (MAP) threshold, including 500 mm MAP, 800 mm MAP and 1000 mm MAP (Supplementary Fig. 7). Further analyses using both AI-based and MAP-based classifications of drylands and non-drylands can be seen in Supplementary Fig. 8. Details for each site, including name, location, IGBP biome, climate metrics, and elevation, are available at (https://data.neonscience.org/) and Supplementary Table 1.

Meteorological and flux data acquisition and processing

The meteorological variables used in this study include air temperature (Ta), precipitation (Pre), relative humidity (RH), soil water content (SWC), and net radiation (Rn)74–78. Canopy surface temperature (Tc) is a key climatic variable for calculating dew formation and is measured routinely by NEON. Vapor pressure deficit (VPD) was derived from RH and Ta. The SWC was measured using the Frequency Domain Reflectometry sensors installed at a depth of 0.06 m. In addition, sensors for measuring other meteorological variables at each site were selected to be positioned at approximately the same height. The daily values for Ta, Tc, Pre, RH, SWC, and Rn were calculated as the mean of their half-hourly data.

The half-hourly flux data encompass net ecosystem CO2 exchange (NEE), sensible heat (H), latent heat (LE), and friction velocity (Ustar)74. NEE is the half-hourly measurements of ecosystem exchange, representing the balance of the carbon uptake during photosynthesis and the release during respiration; respiration (RECO) is the ecosystem respiration estimated by extrapolation of nighttime measurements (when photosynthesis stopped) to the whole day. GPP is the amount of carbon fixed during photosynthesis, deriving from gap-filled NEE and RECO data using the nighttime partitioning method80, as implemented in the R package REddyProc81. Uncertainty in GPP data mainly arises from flux partitioning methods and NEE processing82,83. To enhance the robustness of the results, we repeated our analysis using GPP from the daytime partitioning method84 and compared it with the night time partitioning method. Concerning NEE processing, gap-filling was performed with the 50th percentile Ustar distribution.

Definition and identification of dew events

The dew point temperature (Td) is the temperature at which air becomes saturated with moisture. A half-hourly dew event was defined as a 30-min interval during which Tc was less than or equal to Td; otherwise, the interval was classified as a non-dew event. Quantification of dew characteristics

In this study, dew characteristics include dew frequency and dew duration, both derived from half-hourly dew events aggregated to the night level by summation. We tested multiple candidate nocturnal dew formation windows using site-normalized counts of half-hourly dew events as the criterion (Supplementary Table 2). Considering both the ecological expectation of dew occurrence and disappearance and the event-count comparison, we selected 20:00 to 7:00 (the following day) as the optimal nocturnal dew formation window, which captured over 80% of half-hourly dew events across forests and grasslands, as well as drylands and non-drylands (Supplementary Table 2, and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Dew duration per night was calculated as the cumulative sum of all half-hourly dew events within a night, multiplied by 0.5 to convert from half-hour units to hours. If a night did not have a dew event, the dew duration was recorded as 0. A dew night was defined as any night with a minimum cumulative dew duration exceeding 0.5 h between 20:00 and 7:00 (the following day). Sensitivity analyses across thresholds of 1, 3, and 5 h confirmed that the dew-night definition is robust (Supplementary Fig. 5). To avoid the misinterpretation of precipitation as dew duration, rainy nights (precipitation > 0 mm) are not included in the dataset. To minimize the influence of observation gaps from the flux tower on the analysis, a night was excluded if the number of missing values when determining a dew event exceeded half of the half-hourly intervals between 20:00 and 7:00.

Dew frequency per site was the percentage of the number of dew nights relative to the total number of non-rainy nights at each site. Dew duration per site was calculated as the mean of the dew duration per night at each site. The dew frequency and dew duration per site across the U.S. were mapped spatially and classified into three categories: low frequency (0–20%), moderate frequency (20–40%), and high frequency (40–70%); short duration (1-2 h), moderate duration (2–4 h), and long duration (4–6 h) (Fig. 1).

To explore the temporal change in dew characteristics, we compared dew frequency and dew duration, together with climatic conditions such as RH and Ta, against the results reported in Ritter & Berkelhammer42 based on same NEON sites from 2015 to 2017 (Supplementary Fig. 2). To mitigate potential bias from uneven record lengths across sites, we quantified site-level trends in annual mean dew duration using the Mann–Kendall test and Theil–Sen slopes (hours year−1) with 95% confidence intervals (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Quantifying the effects of dew formation on GPP

Considering there is no photosynthesis during the evening, GPP was calculated between 6:00 and 18:00. The next-day GPP after dew nights or non-dew nights was calculated by normalizing half-hourly GPP values using a z-score for every month over the growing season (April to October). GPP is then paired with dew formation and dew duration values according to the date. The effects of dew formation on GPP across diverse ecosystem types and climatic regions were evaluated by comparing GPP after dew nights and non-dew nights using two-sided t-tests (Fig. 2; and Supplementary Table. 3). To aid ecological interpretation, we additionally summarized the mean differences in next-day z-scored GPP between dew and non-dew nights (Supplementary Table. 3).

We further tested the robustness of the results of drylands and non-drylands using both AI values and different MAP thresholds (Supplementary Fig. 8). In addition, we conducted the same two-sided t-tests at the site level, comparing next-day GPP following dew versus non-dew nights within each site (Supplementary Figs. 9 and 10). To account for multiple comparisons across sites, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate procedure to adjust the resulting p values (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5).

Predictor selection and machine learning models

Five predictor factors were considered and consistently used in all modeling approaches: dew duration, Ta, Rn, RH, and SWC. Dew duration is a key predictor that reflects not only the occurrence of dew but also the persistence of dewy conditions, which can influence photosynthesis and transpiration. The other variables were selected based on well-established plant physiological and ecohydrological mechanisms: Ta and Rn represent thermal energy conditions of the surrounding environment, while RH and SWC reflect atmospheric and soil moisture availability, respectively. Both are crucial for regulating stomatal conductance and carbon assimilation. Multicollinearity among the predictors was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor, and no significant multicollinearity was detected (Supplementary Table 11). To isolate the synergistic effects of soil water, which is the primary water source in many ecosystems, and dew on GPP, we tested interaction effects between dew and SWC using multiple linear regression (MLR) analyses (Supplementary Table 8) and evaluated their significance using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (Supplementary Table 9). Daily values of Ta, Tc, RH, SWC, and Rn were calculated as the mean of half-hourly measurements between 6:00 and 18:00.

The boosted regression tree (BRT) models were applied to attribute the effects of five predictors on GPP. As a machine-learning method that integrates the strengths of boosting and regression trees, BRT models effectively characterize complex nonlinear relations with good prediction performance, handle different types of predictor variables (both numeric and categorical) and their interaction effects, and accommodate missing data86. Tree complexity and learning rate are key parameters that influence model structure and learning behavior: the former determines the level of interaction among predictors, while the latter controls the contribution of each tree to the growing model86. We set tree complexity to 3, enabling the capture of up to three-way interactions among predictor variables that reflect more nuanced ecosystem responses to dew. A learning rate of 0.01 was chosen to balance training speed and model stability, allowing the model to reveal subtle ecological effects while reducing overfitting. A default bag fraction of 0.5 was used, meaning 50% of the training data were randomly drawn without replacement at each iteration, introducing stochasticity that improves model robustness86. Additionally, 10-fold cross-validation was implemented within the BRT framework to evaluate model predictive performance and to determine the optimal number of trees during model fitting. To quantify the uncertainty and robustness of the estimated effects of the explanatory variables, a Monte Carlo bootstrap procedure was further applied by performing 100 independent model runs, each based on random samplings with replacement from original dataset. All analyses were conducted using the gbm package in R (version 4.3.2)86.

Across these bootstrap replicates, the importance of each predictor to GPP was quantified by the built-in relative contribution of the BRT models, and the resulting distributions were used to assess the stability of importance estimates across resampled datasets (Fig. 3). In addition to the bootstrap-based relative contribution analysis, we further quantified predictor importance using permutation-based importance, defined as the increase in prediction error after randomly permuting each predictor while keeping all other predictors unchanged. Permutation importance was summarized by the mean value and its 95% confidence intervals across bootstrap replicates (Supplementary Table 6). To account for potential information leakage in site–time data, we re-evaluated model performance using site-blocked 5-fold cross-validation (repeated 100 times), in which all observations from a given site were assigned to the same fold (Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Fig. 12). The relationship between dew duration and predicted GPP based on BRT models was explored using partial dependence plots (Fig. 4), which are the graphical visualizations of the marginal effect of a given predictor variable after accounting for the average effects of all other variables in the model86.

Furthermore, we employed another machine learning method, the Random Forest (RF) model, to verify the variable importance rankings from the BRT models. RF builds an ensemble of decision trees using randomly selected subsets of data and predictors and aggregates their results to improve prediction accuracy and reduce overfitting87. Here, we defined each RF using randomForest package in R with 500 binary trees, selecting 5 predictors at each split to ensure diverse decision paths while maintaining ecological interpretability, and set the minimal terminal node size to 1 to allow deep trees capable of capturing subtle, fine-scale variations in GPP responses to environmental drivers. With the RF models, variable importance ranking was quantified by the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method across 100 runs, each based on random sampling with replacement. The resulting SHAP-based rankings were then compared with the relative contributions derived from the BRT models (Supplementary Fig. 13). To minimize potential information leakage, both the BRT and RF analyses adopted a consistent predictor set and resampling framework. All predictors were constructed independently of the response variable (GPP), and model fitting and evaluation were performed within each resampling iteration, thereby preventing any information from the full dataset from leaking into either model.
