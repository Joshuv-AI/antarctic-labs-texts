Protocol for analyzing invadopodia formation and gelatin degradation
Authors: Weijian Meng, Qingwen Huang, Rui Zhang, Jie Shen, Daxing Xie, Cong Xu, Liang Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryInvadopodia are actin-rich protrusions on the tumor cell membrane that degrade the extracellular matrix and play a crucial role in tumor cell invasion and metastasis. Here, we present a protocol to examine invadopodia’s ability to form and degrade the extracellular matrix during tumor invasion and metastasis. We detail the procedure for using immunofluorescence staining to indirectly detect invadopodia formation and assess their extracellular matrix degradation capability via the gelatin degradation assay.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Huang et al.1Subject areas: Cell Membrane, Cancer, Microscopy

Before you begin

Invadopodia are unique actin-rich protrusions formed on the cell membrane of tumor cells.2 Various protein molecules are present in invadopodia, among which cortactin, Tks5, and MMP14 are widely used as markers.3,4,5,6 Their co-localization with F-actin can be used to mark the formation of invadopodia.

In this protocol, we choose to co-localize cortactin with F-actin to mark invadopodia. Cortactin is an F-actin-binding protein and an important multi-domain protein involved in actin polymerization and the formation of actin branches.7,8 Therefore, it is widely used as a marker for invadopodia.

Invadopodia on tumor cell membranes typically exist in two states: an active state with gelatin matrix degradation function and an inactive state without gelatin degradation function. Only invadopodia with gelatin-degrading activity can degrade the extracellular matrix through secreted metalloproteinases, allowing tumor cells to break through the matrix barrier for metastasis and diffusion.9,10,11 The most widely accepted in vitro method to verify invadopodia function is the gelatin degradation assay.

The basic principle of this assay is that cells with functional invadopodia secrete metalloproteinases (MMPs), which can degrade fluorescently labeled gelatin (0.1% Oregon Green 488 Conjugate Gelatin), resulting in black spot areas where degradation has occurred. Co-localizing these areas with cortactin and F-actin-labeled invadopodia allows assessment of invadopodia function: the more black spot areas observed, the more gelatin is degraded, indicating stronger invadopodia function.

Expected outcomes

By employing the aforementioned protocols, we can effectively label Cortactin (green) and F-actin (red) within tumor cells, enabling their co-localization as a reliable indicator of Invadopodia (yellow) (Figure 1). Additionally, coverslips coated with fluorescein conjugate gelatin allows for degradation by functional Invadopodia-secreted metalloproteinases, which can be distinctly visualized as a zone of gelatin degradation (black spots) in confocal microscopy (Figure 2). Percentage of cells with invadopodia was quantified (N = 100 cells per sample). Gelatin degradation was judged as a black area beneath the cell. Quantification of the gelatin degradation area was evaluated using the software ImageJ and statistically normalized. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Limitations

In this protocol, Cortactin and F-actin co-localization is used to mark Invadopodia, but Invadopodia encompass various protein molecules, including Tks5 and MMP14. Other markers for the detection of invadopodia were not addressed in this protocol. Additionally, the aforementioned protocols entail the specific binding of antibodies or reagents to the target molecule, followed by fluorescent labeling for visualization and detection of specific proteins or tissue structures. Due to the light sensitivity and potential degradation or denaturation of fluorescent dyes used in fluorescence staining, prolonged exposure to light may lead to denaturation or degradation, affecting the stability and intensity of their fluorescence signal and ultimately compromising the accuracy and reliability of the experimental results.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Gelatin has not been sterilized, and it can cause contamination during cell culture.

Potential solution

Prepared 0.1% Oregon Green 488 Conjugate Gelatin solution should be filtered through a 0.22 μm filter to remove bacteria, then divided into individual solutions and stored at −20°C in the dark.

Problem 2

The coating of fluorescent gelatin is a key to the success of the experiment.

Potential solution

In order to make the fluorescent gelatin thin and evenly coated, we conducted numerous experiments to determine the most suitable experimental conditions. Simply placing a coverslip upside down on a drop of fluorescent gelatin for 20 min will result in an ideal phosphorescent gelatin coverslip.
