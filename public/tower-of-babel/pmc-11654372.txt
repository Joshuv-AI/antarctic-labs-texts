Computer case-based reasoning simulation versus traditional lectures for medical interns teaching of diagnosis and antibiotic prescribing for acute respiratory infection: a comparative quasi-experimental study
Authors: Fatemeh Rangraz Jeddi, Mansooreh Momen-Heravi, Razieh Farrahi, Ehsan Nabovati, Hossein Akbari, Maryam Edalati khodabandeh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute respiratory infections are a common presentation in clinical practice and medical interns must learn proper diagnosis and antibiotic prescribing. Traditional lecture-based teaching may not provide sufficient opportunities for students to apply their knowledge in realistic scenarios, whereas computer case-based simulations offer an alternative approach that allows active learning and decision-making in simulated patient cases. This study investigated the effectiveness of computer case-based reasoning simulation versus traditional lectures for medical interns teaching of diagnosis and antibiotic prescribing for acute respiratory infections.MethodsThis comparative quasi-experimental study was conducted from 2020 to 2022 in the Department of Infectious Diseases at Shahid Beheshti Hospital, affiliated to Kashan University of Medical Sciences. The samples were selected using a convenience method and assigned to the intervention and control groups using a permuted block randomization approach. Over a period of ten months (Each month, an average of eight medical interns), a total of 40 medical interns received traditional lecture-based teaching, while another 40 medical interns were taught using a Computer Case-based Reasoning simulation. The medical interns’ knowledge in both groups was assessed using pre- and post-tests. The collected data from the pre- and post-tests were then analyzed statistically using paired t-tests, independent t-tests, and ANCOVA.ResultsThe posttest scores of the medical interns in both groups were significantly higher than the pretest scores (P < 0.001). No statistically significant differences were observed between the two teaching methods regarding mean knowledge gains in diagnoses and antibiotic prescribing practices. (P > 0.21). The results of the ANCOVA, after controlling for pre-test scores, showed no statistically significant difference between the two teaching methods in their effect on medical interns’ diagnostic and antibiotic prescribing performance (P > 0.33).ConclusionThe study found that both computer case-based reasoning simulation and traditional lectures were effective in improving medical interns’ knowledge of diagnosis and antibiotic prescribing practices for acute respiratory infections. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two teaching methods. Thus, computer- based simulation could replace face-to-face teaching when this method is impractical or computerized methods are more cost-effective.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06453-4.Keywords: Anti-bacterial agents, Respiratory tract infections, Prescriptions, Education, Medical, Problem-based learning

Introduction

Acute respiratory infections (ARIs) are among the most common reasons for seeking medical care and antibiotic prescriptions [1–3]. However, the over-prescription and inappropriate use of antibiotics for acute respiratory tract infections, which are often caused by viruses, can lead to the emergence of antibiotic resistance, which is currently a major threat to public health worldwide, as well as increasing healthcare costs and adverse drug reactions [4–6]. Therefore, Effective teaching of medical interns in ARI diagnosis and treatment should be a key priority in medical education.

Traditionally, medical education has relied heavily on lecture-based approaches to convey foundational knowledge [7]. Lectures offer a structured framework for information delivery but may inadequately prepare students for the complex decision-making and practical skills required in clinical settings [8, 9].

In response to these challenges, technological advances have allowed the way for new strategies, such as information technology interventions, to complement traditional teaching methods and transform healthcare activities as well as the education of healthcare professionals. These innovations also hold the potential to change physicians’ prescribing behavior. Medical education simulators are one such technology used to teach healthcare professionals knowledge and skills. Medical simulation technology provides a safe, realistic training environment where medical interns can practice clinical skills and decision-making without the risk of harm to patients [10].

Computer-based medical simulators, that simulate the thinking, reasoning, and decision-making of professionals, provide an ideal platform for simulator-assisted training programs for medical interns [11].

Studies have shown that the use of educational simulators can improve the knowledge and skills of medical interns [12–14], junior doctors and specialists [15].

The results of a published study from Australia showed that the average level of knowledge of junior doctors in training who used the Qstream online training platform was significantly higher (69.7–81.5%) than the minimal improvement in knowledge in the group of doctors who used face-to-face training (67.6–67.9%) [15]. To improve knowledge and change prescribing behavior of medical interns in the future, medical education in addition to using traditional methods of education, should be accompanied by various approaches that influence prescribing, such as problem-based learning and the use of information technology tools such as computer simulations, in addition to traditional teaching methods [16, 17].

To enable remote medical education during the COVID-19 pandemic, researchers developed a computer case-based reasoning simulation called HAMTA, which can be used to simulate cases of ARIs for medical intern teaching. HAMTA uses case-based reasoning to create interactive simulations based on evidence-based clinical guidelines. The introduction of this computerized teaching tool allowed for continued teaching in respiratory tract infections in accordance with public health restrictions on face-to-face teaching. After in-person teaching resumed, the researchers continued to use the HAMTA to teach medical interns at Shahid Beheshti Hospital, Kashan University of Medical Sciences. Given the importance of appropriate diagnosis and antibiotic prescribing behavior for ARIs, this study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of computer case-based reasoning simulation versus traditional lectures for teaching these competencies to medical interns. By comparing these two-teaching method, the study sought to determine whether the active learning and decision-making opportunities afforded by case simulations could enhance medical interns’ diagnostic and antibiotic prescribing skills for ARIs.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study used a comparative quasi-experimental design to compare the effectiveness of a computer case-based reasoning simulation with traditional lecture-based teaching for medical interns’ education on the diagnosis and antibiotic prescribing for ARIs.

Study environment

The Infectious Diseases Department with seven Infectious Diseases specialists and an average of eight medical interns per month at Shahid Beheshti Hospital, the largest teaching hospital affiliated with Kashan University of Medical Sciences with 688 approved inpatient beds distributed across 26 wards, constituted the research environment.

Study population

The study population comprised medical interns enrolled in the Infectious Diseases Department at Shahid Beheshti Hospital.

Sample size calculation and randomization

Based on the information from Shalom et al.‘s study [18] and taking into account a 95% confidence level, 95% test power, and a minimum difference of 15 points between the two groups, the minimum required sample size for this study was calculated to be 23 people in each group. However, considering the available facilities, the number was increased to 40 people per group, resulting in a total of 80 medical interns being included in this study. The samples were drawn from medical students in the internship phase, whose list was extracted from the education unit of the medical school. The sample was selected using a convenience method, and every intern who was interested in participating in the study was included. Allocation of participants to intervention and control groups was done by the permuted block randomization method. For randomization, assistance was obtained from the Clinical Trial Randomization Tool (https://ctrandomization.cancer.gov).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Medical interns, including guest students, who were starting their rotation in the Infectious Diseases Department, were included in the study. However, those in the last 3 months of their internship, who had completed the main clinical rotations and achieved the maximum score in the pretest, were excluded. Participants with similar pretest scores were selected for inclusion. Additionally, any medical interns who did not attend either the pre-test or post-test sessions were excluded from the study.

Interventions

Computer case-based reasoning simulation

medical interns in this group received teaching through a computer case-based reasoning simulation developed specifically for this study. The simulation software was designed to present realistic patient cases with varying presentations of ARIs. Medical interns were required to read scenarios, then based on the information, select one of the differential diagnoses and make decisions regarding appropriate antibiotic prescribing. After diagnosis and treatment, the system provided feedback based on clinical guidelines and teacher experience. The cases were designed to cover a range of complexity levels and included common pitfalls in ARI management. Each group underwent four 2-hour sessions over four weeks.

Traditional lecture-based teaching

This group received traditional lecture-based teaching on the diagnosis and treatment of ARIs. The lectures were delivered by experienced infectious disease specialists and covered the same content as the simulation cases, including diagnostic criteria, treatment guidelines, and antibiotic stewardship principles. Each group attended four 2-hour lectures over four weeks.

Outcome measures

the medical interns’ knowledge regarding the correct diagnosis of the disease, the correct prescription of antibiotics, and the medical intern’s overall clinical knowledge were assessed as the primary outcomes of the study. Secondary outcome, for the group that used the simulation, information such as the rate of adherence to clinical practice guidelines, the rate of system usage, and the time spent on each scenario was collected.

Data collection

Preparation of scenario bank

In creating the scenario bank, efforts were made to ensure that all relevant content from the infectious diseases’ curriculum and clinical guidelines regarding the diagnosis and antibiotic prescription for acute respiratory infections was included. At this stage, two infectious disease specialists, based on their experience from the Infectious Disease Department and outpatient examinations at the Infectious Clinic, drafted seven clinical scenarios for each of the seven acute respiratory infections: pneumonia, sinusitis, bronchitis, pharyngitis, otitis media, influenza, and the common cold, resulting in a total of 49 scenarios. For each scenario, in addition to the description of the scenario, they included differential diagnoses, clinical signs & symptoms in favor of the correct diagnosis, paraclinical procedures, appropriate antibiotics for treatment, drug prescriptions, and educational recommendations for the medical interns to give to the patients (Supplementary file 1). Subsequently, the scenarios were reviewed by three infectious disease specialists, who were faculty members in the infectious disease department, to assess their content coverage and level of difficulty. The scenarios were then revised based on their feedback to ensure alignment with the intended educational objectives, which demonstrated the validity of the scenarios. To evaluate the reliability of the scenarios, a test-retest method was used. Seven infectious disease specialists in infection disease department were asked to review the scenarios, providing diagnoses and selecting appropriate antibiotics based on the cases. After a one-month interval, the same experts were asked to complete the same scenarios again. The reliability of their responses over time was assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient, yielding a value of 0.93, indicating a high degree of consistency between the two administrations of the scenarios.

Test design

From the scenario bank, 14 scenarios (two from each disease) were selected. To control for contamination between groups, we implemented two different tests, each with seven distinct scenarios. Both tests were carefully calibrated to be equivalent in difficulty and complexity, as confirmed by two infectious disease specialists from the infectious diseases department. Furthermore, the pre-test for the incoming group was administered only after the previous group had completed their rotation and left the department, ensuring no overlap or interaction between the groups. Each scenario included questions related to disease diagnosis and antibiotic prescription, with the total scores of these two questions considered the clinical knowledge score.

that measures the primary outcomes.

Teaching

Five groups (40 people in the infectious disease department rotation) were given traditional teaching through lectures. This group was taught ARI diagnosis and antibiotic prescription by the professor in the classroom. After that, five other groups (rotation of the infectious disease department) consisting of 40 people were taught through the HAMTA simulation system. After identifying the system requirements, and its design and evaluation by experts and students, the system was designed on a web-based platform. This system was designed and developed to write and manage simulated scenarios of acute respiratory infections, as well as to display scenarios and teach medical interns how to diagnose these diseases and rationally prescribe antibiotics. For teaching through the system, the medical interns were provided with the system’s URL and necessary teaching on how to use it. Then, the medical interns were asked to enter the system using their assigned username and password.

Evaluating the effectiveness of two teaching methods

To evaluate the effectiveness of each teaching method, two types of tests designed in the second stage were used. For the first group, one type of test was randomly selected using a coin flip as a randomization technique. This test was given to the medical interns as a pre-test in the first session of their attendance in the department, and the same test was administered as a post-test in the last session of their attendance in the infectious disease department. In the next group, the same process was followed, but the second type of test was used as the pre-test and post-test. These tests were used to measure primary outcomes. Also, the rate of adherence to clinical practice guidelines, the rate of system usage, and the time spent on each scenario were evaluated as secondary outcomes.

based on the reports extracted from the HAMTA simulation system.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS software. Descriptive statistics indicators (mean and standard deviation) were used to describe the status of each group. The paired t-test was used for intra-group comparisons (lecture and teaching through HAMTA), while the independent t-test was used for inter-group comparisons. Additionally, univariate analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was used for multivariate analysis and to control for confounding factors. For all calculations, statistical significance was determined by a p-value less than 0.05.

Demographic information of participants

In the lecture-based education group, out of 40 medical interns, 19 were men (47.5%) and 21 were women (52.5%). In the simulation-based education group, there were 21 men (52.5%) and 19 women (47.5%). Within the lecture-based education group, 13 medical interns were in their sixth year (32.5%) and 27 were in their seventh year (67.5%) of medical school, while in the simulation-based education group, 16 medical interns were in their sixth year (40%) and 24 in their seventh year (60%).

Discussion

In this study, the effect of two teaching methods on medical interns’ knowledge of diagnosing and prescribing antibiotics for acute respiratory infections was evaluated. The findings showed that although the mean scores of medical interns’ knowledge of diagnosing and prescribing antibiotics increased in both teaching methods; after controlling the pre-test effect, no statistically significant difference was observed between the two teaching methods in medical interns’ knowledge of diagnosis and antibiotic prescription (P > 0.33).

No significant difference was found between the two teaching methods regarding medical interns’ knowledge of diagnosis and antibiotic prescription (P > 0.33). This result differs from the findings of Menon et al. (2020), which showed a significantly higher increase in physicians’ knowledge in the online education group using QStream (from 69.7 to 81.5%) compared to the minimal improvement in the face-to-face education group (from 67.6 to 67.9%) (P = 0.01). Several factors may account for this discrepancy, including the 10-week duration of the online course, the use of gamification features to engage participants, and the involvement of infectious disease specialists, pharmacists, and young physicians in the design of multiple-choice questions [15]. This highlights the importance of involving medical interns in the development of educational content and objectives for courses on antimicrobial prescribing. Additionally, incorporating interactive elements such as gamification could enhance the appeal and effectiveness of online educational programs. These considerations should be taken into account when designing future educational interventions.

Stevens et al. (2019) found a significant improvement in student performance when online content was used alongside traditional methods, with increases of 5.57% (P > 0.001) in multiple-choice and 2.08% (P > 0.002) in short-answer questions [19]. In contrast, the current study used only the HAMTA simulation system to teach acute respiratory infections, leading to different outcomes. Both studies, however, shared similarities in using case-based learning and similar evaluation methods. So that, first-term scores of the second year were examined and analyzed for comparison between the two groups, and in this study, the pre-test scores were controlled using statistical analysis. However, a notable difference between the two studies is the blinding of evaluators when assessing students’ responses. In the present study, the teacher acted as the evaluator of the questions and was a member of the research team, thus not being blind to the study groups. Future research should combine computer-based simulations with traditional lectures to better understand their impact on diagnosing respiratory infections and antibiotic prescribing.

O’Hare et al. (2018) reviewed 56 studies on e-learning in pharmacy education, finding that 20 of the 23 studies comparing e-learning to face-to-face methods showed e-learning to be at least as effective. However, design flaws, such as the lack of baseline knowledge assessments, were identified in 16 of those studies, potentially affecting results [20]. The present study addressed these issues by assessing baseline knowledge and using clinical scenarios to evaluate medical interns, with a larger sample size than previous studies. it recommends using randomized clinical trials to assess the impact of computer-based simulation on medical interns’ knowledge and examining how the extent of HAMTA system use affects their learning outcomes.

The study by Sikkens et al. (2018) demonstrated that an e-learning module significantly improved medical students’ antimicrobial prescribing skills. Key findings include enhanced drug selection (long-term effect, p = 0.034), improved antimicrobial knowledge (short-term effect, p = 0.032; long-term effect, p = 0.31), and overall performance (long-term effect, p = 0.007) compared to a control group [14]. The study employed a robust design, including randomized interventions, blind assessments, and the use of WHO’s Good Prescribing Guide [21]. While the results contrast with the present study, similarities include pre- and post-tests, interactive learning, and problem-solving-based education. Therefore, it suggests integrating the WHO six-step prescribing program into future educational tools like the HAMTA simulation system.

Therefore, can be stated, HAMTA simulation system as e-learning module can be considered as a viable alternative to traditional teaching methods, potentially offering advantages such as flexibility, accessibility, and cost-effectiveness. Based on the conducted analyses, several factors appear to have contributed to the lack of a significant difference between the two teaching methods. One notable factor is the short duration of system usage per medical intern, averaging only two hours. Meaningful improvements in learning outcomes require the careful integration of simulator use into the curriculum, regular monitoring, and structured feedback sessions with interns and faculty. These measures help maintain a consistent evaluation and improvement loop in the educational process. Furthermore, extending the duration of usage to mirror the typical classroom time spent by students is critical. Studies have shown that prolonged use of e-learning platforms significantly enhances learning outcomes by increasing exposure to content, reinforcing knowledge, and improving retention and application of learned material [22, 23].

However, the results of the Spearman’s correlation test revealed no significant relationship between the time spent studying the scenario and the scores achieved. This suggests that individual characteristics, such as students’ motivation and learning styles, as well as environmental factors like access to digital tools (e.g., laptops and tablets) and prior familiarity with these technologies, may significantly influence their responses and engagement.

Another contributing factor is the limited number of scenarios studied by each medical intern, with an average of only eight scenarios. While eight scenarios were designed for each disease, these scenarios may have lacked the diversity or complexity necessary to align with real-world conditions and meet the educational needs of interns. Furthermore, after completing a scenario, students received feedback based on clinical guidelines, which was identical across all eight scenarios for a given disease. Only instructor-specific feedback varied. This uniformity reduced students’ motivation to engage with additional scenarios, as they perceived diminishing returns in studying beyond one scenario per disease. Consequently, the duration of system usage decreased, limiting exposure to the educational content. Additionally, other factors contributed to the study’s limitations, including a relatively small sample size, the absence of robust methodologies such as clinical trials to determine effectiveness, and students’ reliance on direct consultation with infectious disease faculty members instead of utilizing the HAMTA system. Furthermore, the lack of medical intern involvement in designing clinical scenarios to address their specific educational needs further compounded these challenges. Collectively, these factors likely contributed to the observed lack of a significant difference between the two teaching methods.

Strengths and weaknesses

Some strengths of the study include the formulation of clinical scenarios by experienced infectious disease specialists, as well as collaboration between experienced and renowned specialists in confirming the validity and reliability of the educational scenarios. The study also benefited from including all stages of clinical reasoning in each scenario for student education, such as diagnosis, determination of clinical symptoms, determination of paraclinical procedures, and drug prescription. It is also possible to mention the use of the system for teaching the disease of COVID-19 due to the absence of students during the COVID-19 pandemic. As the clinical guidelines for this disease were continuously revised until the final implementation stage of the system, the educational content related to this disease within the system was regularly updated multiple times to align with the latest guidelines.

Some limitations of this study include the existence of multiple clinical guidelines for acute respiratory infections. The most recent guidelines were identified through extensive searching and the use of appropriate keywords in relevant databases. Additionally, infectious disease specialists had limited availability for full attendance in focused group discussion sessions to select guidelines and functional requirements due to their busy schedules during the COVID-19 pandemic. However, this issue was addressed through follow-up communication, negotiations, telephone contacts, and short-term meetings.

Furthermore, medical interns did not fully and accurately complete the pre-test due to the perception that its scores would not impact their final grades. In contrast, they completed the post-test more meticulously as it contributed to their grading. Consequently, efforts were made to control for the effect of pre-test scores using statistical analysis. Additionally, this research was conducted at a single medical center; thus, caution should be exercised when generalizing the results, and efforts should be made to replicate the study at other medical centers.

Implication for practical

Before medical interns enter the clinical care field, such platforms should be used concurrently with traditional classroom teaching to educate clinical guidelines for various diseases, and the impact of guideline-based education on students’ knowledge should be assessed. To use the system at a national level, feedback from associations and national institutions should be sought to identify users’ requirements and needs. Feedback should be considered as a primary feature in teaching systems. A platform for exchanging and sharing information between instructors and students should be activated. The relationship between the extent of use of systems and students’ acquired knowledge should be investigated. In simulations systems, the use of images and videos to illustrate antibiotic forms and paraclinical procedures should be employed. The cost of drugs and procedures should be included in the system because cost considerations are not emphasized in the initial years of education and become a habit that leads to irreparable damage.

Implication for future

The extent of medical interns’ use of this system and the impact of education through this system on their knowledge, actions, and behaviors in the long term and overall professional development should be examined, and the satisfaction of students educated with the system should be evaluated using standard tools. Given the prevalent tendency of medical schools towards problem-based learning and the costliness of this type of education due to expensive educational resources for small-group teaching sessions and patients with specific conditions, these systems have been utilized for teaching knowledge and skills among healthcare providers.

Conclusion

Both lecture-based teaching methods and computer case-based simulation systems enhanced medical interns’ knowledge in diagnosing acute respiratory infections, prescribing antibiotics, and overall clinical knowledge related to these conditions. The impact of the two teaching approaches on medical interns’ knowledge did not demonstrate statistically significant differences. In this study, the short-term effect of the computer-based simulation system on medical interns’ knowledge was evaluated; however, its long-term effect on the knowledge and decision-making behavior of medical interns and early-career physicians in the diagnosis and antibiotic prescription practices remains unknown, warranting further investigation. Additionally, it is recommended to assess the extent of utilization of this computer-based simulation system in medical universities and to evaluate the impact of teaching through this system on the clinical knowledge and competencies of medical interns.
