Potential profiling of social alienation in elderly stroke patients and study of influencing factors
Authors: Kexin Lin, Yuchen Ma, Xinru Guo, Chunyan Hao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo explore the potential profiling of social alienation in elderly stroke patients and to analyze their influencing factors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted. From June 2024 to March 2025, elderly patients with stroke who were hospitalized in the Department of Neurology and the Rehabilitation Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University were selected as the research subjects. A general information questionnaire, social support rating scale, psychological elasticity scale, self-perceived burden scale, and general alienation scale were used to investigate. Latent profile analysis was employed to explore potential subtypes of patients’ social alienation, and multivariate logistic regression was used to analyze the influencing factors of different categories.ResultsElderly stroke patients’ social alienation was categorized into 3 subtypes: the " Low-level social alienation " (25.4%), the “moderate social alienation” (60.1%), and the " High-level social alienation” (14.5%). Patient age, education level, monthly family income, degree of self-care, number of episodes, number of comorbid chronic diseases, social support, psychological resilience, and self-perceived burden were predictors of potential social alienation typology in elderly stroke patients (p < 0.05).ConclusionHeterogeneity exists in the social alienation of elderly stroke patients, and healthcare professionals should take targeted measures according to different categories of patients to improve their social alienation and promote their physical and mental health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06540-1.Keywords: Elderly, Stroke, Social alienation, Potential profile analysis, Psychological care

Background

Stroke, commonly known as stroke, is a class of neurological dysfunctional diseases caused by the obstruction of blood circulation in the brain, including both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke types [1]. As a common chronic disease, the disease is characterized by high morbidity, high mortality, high recurrence, and high disability, and is the main disease leading to disability and death of residents [2]. According to the survey, the number of new stroke cases was about 12.2 million worldwide in 2019, and the number of deaths due to stroke reached 6.55 million in the same period, which makes the disease a major threat to global public health [3]. Data show that about 70% of stroke survivors will be left with varying degrees of motor and speech impairments, and 40% of survivors will have reduced ability to perform activities of daily living (ADLs) and be unable to care for themselves [4]. With the acceleration of aging and changes in residents’ lifestyles, the incidence of stroke increases gradually with age, and the size of the geriatric stroke population continues to expand [5]. Due to the high disability rate of the disease, patients are faced with a long recovery period, which imposes a heavy burden on their lives. The decline in their ability to move freely and communicate has hindered their daily activities and made them reluctant to participate in social activities [6]. The research has found that the social participation level of stroke patients is relatively low. Only 28% of the patients actively engage in social activities [7]. Stroke patients often experience multiple changes, such as redefinition of social and family roles due to sequelae, limited physical activity ability, and reduced participation in social activities. They are prone to negative psychological emotions like self-abandonment, loneliness, anxiety, and depression [8, 9]. In normal social situations, they tend to exhibit behaviors of actively distancing themselves from others and self-isolation, resulting in a significant decrease in their social interaction intentions. This situation further leads to behavioral patterns of social avoidance and withdrawal, making it difficult for the patients to smoothly integrate into social groups. Eventually, they fall into the predicament of negative emotions such as loneliness, meaninglessness, and alienation, which form the phenomenon of “social alienation“ [10, 11]. According to research, stroke patients exhibit varying degrees of social alienation [12, 13]. Social alienation not only has negative impacts on patients’ physical, psychological, and social functions but also poses severe challenges to subsequent treatment and rehabilitation [14].

Social alienation is a subjective psychological state, referring to the situation where an individual, due to various factors during social interaction, becomes distant from others or a group, resulting in a subjective emotional experience including feelings of loneliness and meaninglessness, which in turn hinders the individual’s effective social activities [15]. This concept was first proposed by scholar Berkman [16] in 1979. Subsequently, it was widely applied in the fields of sociology and psychology. American nursing scholar Biordi [17] introduced this concept into the field of nursing and combined it with nursing practice, emphasizing that nursing staff should also pay attention to the social alienation phenomenon when assessing patients’ mental health. Studies have shown that social alienation significantly affects the cardiovascular system, immune function, and nervous system of patients, thereby increasing the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases as well as the recurrence of stroke [18]. About half of the individuals with stroke face social alienation, which is characterized by limited ability to participate in social activities, impaired verbal communication, and isolation and loneliness experienced during interpersonal interactions [19, 20]. Social alienation has a significant negative impact on stroke patients, as it can significantly weaken their social support network, resulting in weakened emotional ties with relatives and friends, which can lead to social adaptation disorders and family dysfunction [21], Secondly, severe social alienation may lead to changes in lifestyle and health habits, impairing cognitive functioning, which can exacerbate the risk of recurrence, further affecting the recovery process, and further affecting patients’ health. This may further affect the recovery process and ultimately the quality of life and well-being of patients [19, 22]. Attention to the social alienation of stroke patients is of great clinical significance to improve their social function recovery, enhance their mental health, and promote the overall recovery process.

Most of the current studies on social alienation in elderly stroke patients have been current investigations, and most of these studies have used scale scores to assess the level of social alienation, ignoring the heterogeneity that exists between individuals. Therefore, in order to deeply explore the complexity and heterogeneity of social alienation among elderly stroke patients, this study employed the latent profile analysis (LPA) method. The aim was to identify different subgroups of social alienation based on core variables and to explore the differences in demographic and clinical characteristics among these subgroups, thereby providing a solid basis for the subsequent formulation of highly targeted intervention management plans and personalized support measures. Latent profile analysis (LPA) is an individual-focused statistical method that looks at how individuals are categorized into latent categories, rather than focusing solely on the relationships between variables. It aims to reveal potential segmentation groups through careful analysis of variable patterns. By using latent profile variables to estimate the interrelationships among the manifest indicators, it is ensured that these manifest indicators maintain a certain degree of independence within a local scope [23]. LPA selects the optimal model by comparing the fitting indices of different category models, thereby improving the accuracy of classification. The present study explores the characteristics of potential categories of social alienation through LPA and clarifies the relationship between each influencing factor and the potential categories of social alienation, to provide a theoretical basis for the development of scientific and effective interventions to improve the sense of social alienation in elderly stroke patients and to promote physical and mental health.

Methods

Research subjects

This study adopted a cross-sectional design. From June 2024 to March 2025, using the convenience sampling method, patients with stroke who were hospitalized in the Neurology Department and Rehabilitation Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Jinzhou Medical University were selected as the research subjects. Sample size inclusion criteria: (1) age ≥ 60 years old; (2) meet the clinical diagnostic criteria about stroke; (3) the disease is in a stable stage, conscious and willing to cooperate; (4) informed consent and willing participants; exclusion criteria: (1) cognitive dysfunction or people with psychiatric diseases; (2) people with other major diseases, such as severe cardiopulmonary, hepatic, and renal dysfunction, as well as patients with malignant tumor. According to Kendall’s [24] method for calculating sample size, the sample size should be 5 to 10 times the number of variables. This study contains 28 variables, and the required sample size is 140 to 280 cases. Considering a 20% sample loss rate, the sample size should be 168 to 336 cases. A total of 330 questionnaires were distributed in this study, and 315 valid questionnaires were ultimately recovered, with an effective questionnaire recovery rate of 95.5%.

Research tools

General information questionnaire

The researcher designed the questionnaire by himself through reviewing the literature, including patients’ gender, age, education level, marital status, place of residence, monthly family income, type of stroke, number of episodes of stroke, and comorbid chronic diseases. (For details, please refer to Supplementary Material 1.)

Social support rating scale (SSRS)

This scale was designed and developed by Chinese scholar Xiao Shuiyuan [25] in 1986. This scale covers 3 dimensions of subjective support, objective support, and support utilization, totaling 10 entries. It was scored using a 5-point Likert scale with a total score ranging from 12 to 66. A higher total score indicates a higher level of social support received. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.827.

Psychological resilience scale (CD-RISC)

This scale was compiled by American psychologists Connor and Davidson [26] et al. and later translated and revised by Chinese scholars, such as Yu Xiaonan [27]. The scale contains 3 dimensions of self-improvement, resilience, and optimism, with 25 entries. A 5-point Likert scale was used, with the options“Never”, “Rarely”, “Sometimes”, “Often”, and “Always” scored from 0 to 4 points, respectively. The total score ranges from 0 to 100, and the higher the score, the higher the level of psychological resilience of the individual. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.783.

Self-perceived burden scale (SPBS)

This scale was developed by scholars such as Cousineau [28] and later adapted into Chinese by Chinese scholars such as Wu Yanyan [29] and others. The scale includes three dimensions of physical burden (entries 1, 2, 5, 7, and 8), emotional burden (entries 4, 6, 9, and 10), and economic burden (entry 3), with a total of 10 entries. A 5-point Likert scale was used, with the options “never”, “occasionally”, “sometimes”, “often”, “always” scored from 1 to 5, respectively. The total score ranges from 10 to 50. The higher the score, the heavier the individual’s self-perceived burden. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.863.

General alienation scale (GAS)

This scale was compiled by Jessor [30] in 1977 and adapted for our scholars Wu Shuang [31] et al. The scale includes 4 dimensions of self-alienation, others’ alienation, meaninglessness, and skepticism, with a total of 15 entries. The scale is scored on a 4-point Likert scale, with scores ranging from 0 to 4 on a scale from “Strongly Disagree” to “Strongly Agree”, and total scores ranging from 15 to 60. The higher the score, the stronger the sense of social alienation. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in this study was 0.796.

Ethical review and data collection

This study was conducted under the authorization of the Ethics Committee of Jinzhou Medical University (JZMULL22025100) and adhered to the principles of the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent revisions. Two well-trained researchers informed the respondents of the purpose of the survey before the investigation and obtained the patients’ informed consent. They ensured that the patients fully understood and voluntarily participated, respecting their privacy and choice rights. All the information of the respondents was protected to guarantee the security of the data. For patients with mild symptoms who could take care of themselves, they were allowed to fill out the questionnaire by themselves. For those who had difficulty filling it out, the researchers would fill it out on their behalf through questioning, or they would complete it together with the assistance of their family members. The distribution and collection of the questionnaires were carried out on-site, and the invalid parts were excluded after verification.

Statistical methods

The data were statistically analyzed using SPSS 27.0. The count data were described by frequency (n) and percentage (%), and the comparison between groups was conducted using the chi-square test. For measurement data that did not conform to a normal distribution, the median (interquartile range) [M (QR)] was used to represent them, and the Kruskal-Wallis H test was employed for the comparison between groups. The indicators with statistical significance in the single-factor analysis were included in the multivariate logistic regression to explore the influencing factors of social alienation in patients of different profiles. The significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Mplus 8.11 was used to model the potential profiles. The four-dimensional scores in GAS were used as exogenous variables, and 1 ~ 5 profiles were selected sequentially for analysis. The following indicators were commonly used to evaluate the model fitting effect: (1) Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC). Akaike information criterion (AIC), Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and adjusted Bayesian information criterion (aBIC), the smaller the value of the three indicators, the better the fit of the model; (2) entropy (entropy) represents the accuracy of the classification, and the closer it is to 1, the more accurate the classification is. (3) Bootstrap’s Likelihood Ratio Test (BLRT) and Lo-Mendell-Rubin Likelihood Ratio Test (LMRT) were used to compare the fitting differences between the models, when P < 0.05, indicating that the model with k categories is significantly better than the model with k-1 categories. Based on the fit index, the best categorization model was determined.

Discussion

Current status and potential characterization of social alienation in elderly stroke patients

The research results show that the overall level of social alienation among elderly stroke patients is at a medium to high level, which is similar to the results of studies on patients with heart failure and breast cancer [32, 33]. Although this study differs slightly from previous studies in terms of the research subjects, the distress caused by related symptoms triggered by the disease, the strong sense of stigma deep within the patients, and the significant changes in their life roles, all these factors, as the core elements leading to social alienation, are widespread among various patient groups and have broad applicability. As people age, their physical functions show a significant decline, including changes in the skeletal system, circulatory system, and other aspects. These physiological changes directly limit the daily activities of the elderly, reducing their frequency of participating in social activities or going out to communicate [34]. Moreover, the degenerative changes in sensory systems (such as decreased vision and hearing) further weaken the elderly’s ability to obtain information from the outside world, making it difficult for them to integrate into group interactions and thus passively fall into a state of social alienation [35]. Stroke patients are left with hemiparesis, aphasia, and other physical disabilities after the disease, which directly affects the patients’ social ability and daily life; elderly patients are often combined with chronic diseases such as hypertension and diabetes [36], which aggravate the physical burden of the patients and limit their social activities. In addition, patients may have negative emotions such as anxiety, depression, and stigma due to their illness [37], which further exacerbate their social alienation. In addition, due to the change of their own image, patients tend to actively adopt coping strategies such as withdrawal and avoidance, which leads to a significant reduction in their social participation. At the same time, the disability caused by the disease greatly reduces the possibility of patients returning to work, which can easily lead to social alienation [38]. Therefore, medical personnel should pay attention to the social alienation problem of elderly stroke patients, provide them with psychological guidance, help them correctly recognize and understand the disease, guide them to actively participate in rehabilitation training, help them rebuild their social will, and enhance their confidence and ability to participate in social activities.

The results of this study show that through LPA, the social alienation of elderly stroke patients can be classified into three types: low-level social alienation, moderate social alienation, and high-level social alienation. This indicates that the social alienation of elderly stroke patients is not a homogeneous entity but rather exhibits significant individual differences. By identifying the characteristics of different categories, when formulating intervention strategies and providing support, we must fully consider the specific circumstances and needs of the patients and then implement personalized and precise intervention strategies. The characteristics of these three types are as follows: ① The proportion of low-level social alienation is 25.4%, and the level of social alienation of stroke patients in this subtype is lower, which may be attributable to the fact that the patients attributed to this subtype have higher monthly family incomes, and this type of patients have a wider social circle, and their channels of participation in social activities are more diversified, and they are more positive and active in social interactions; and the cultural level of patients in this category is higher. Patients in this category are more literate, and older adults with higher levels of literacy usually have stronger cognitive reserves [39], including memory, logical reasoning, and learning abilities. After a stroke, they can adapt more quickly to changes in physical functioning and maintain social interactions through cognitive compensatory mechanisms (e.g., use of aids, adjustments in communication) [40]. ② The percentage of moderate social alienation was 60.1%, which was a large percentage overall, and the mean scores of each dimension of the scale were at a medium level among the 3 categories. “Moderate social alienation” is the main type of social alienation in elderly stroke patients. ③ The percentage of the high-level social alienation was 14.5%, which was relatively small overall, and the mean scores of the dimensions of this category were at a high level. Among them, the degree of self-care is unable to take care of themselves accounted for more patients, when the degree of self-care of the patients declined, they often need more external support and help, this state of dependence may lead to the patients feel that their connection with the community weakened, and then produce a sense of social alienation [41]; stroke patients need to rely on the care of others due to the decline in physical function, and at the same time, the higher cost of treatment makes the patients prone to produce a burden mentality. This emotion will further exacerbate the patient’s sense of social alienation, thus reducing social activities and avoiding interactions with others [42].

Analysis of influencing factors of potential categories of elderly stroke patients

Patients with lower educational levels and lower monthly household income are more likely to experience social alienation

In this study, we found that compared with the " low-level social alienation “, patients with junior high school education or below and a monthly household income of < 3000 were more likely to belong to the " high-level social alienation “, which is consistent with the results of previous studies [33, 43]. Elderly stroke patients with junior high school education or below, due to their limited education, often lack sufficient knowledge and skills in disease awareness, rehabilitation training and psychological adjustment, which makes them easily feel incompetent in social activities, gradually weakening the connection with the society, and eventually experiencing a high level of social alienation [44]; some low-education patients are affected by traditional concepts, thinking that the disease is a “family scandal” and are unwilling to seek help from the outside world. This cultural taboo makes them disconnected from the mainstream social support system, forming a reinforcing mechanism of “disease shame-social alienation“ [37]. Family income is an important influence on patients’ healthcare behavior and active rehabilitation [45]. Lower income levels limit their access to quality rehabilitation resources and make them stretched in their daily social activities. Due to financial constraints, they may not be able to participate in some social gatherings or rehabilitation activities that require payment, which undoubtedly reduces their opportunities for contact with the outside world. Meanwhile, in the face of illness and financial pressure, stroke patients from low-income families may be more prone to negative emotions such as anxiety and depression, which may affect their social willingness and socialization ability [46]. Therefore, healthcare professionals should strengthen health education for patients, pay attention to their mental health status, find and channel their negative emotions on time, and at the same time, they should actively create social opportunities for patients and encourage them to participate in some social activities.

Influence of age, degree of self-care, number of morbidities, and number of comorbid chronic diseases on the sense of social alienation

The results of this study showed that elderly stroke patients aged 70 years or older, with partial or no self-care, two or more episodes, and two or more comorbid chronic diseases were more likely to be classified as " moderate social alienation” and " high-level social alienation”. ①Related studies have confirmed that the severity of social alienation is closely related to age [47], and the older the patient, the stronger the sense of social alienation. Some of the elderly patients have difficulties in operating electronic devices and using network tools, which directly leads to the limitation of their access to information, as well as the problem of miscommunication at the level of social interaction. Secondly, as they grow older, their physical functions gradually deteriorate, their daily actions become slower and slower, and their ability to integrate into various activities in society gradually weakens, leading to an increased sense of social alienation [34]. ② The level of self-care ability also significantly affects the patients’ sense of social alienation, and the poorer the self-care ability, the stronger the sense of social alienation of elderly stroke patients, which is consistent with the results of a previous study [48]. Specifically, such patients have to rely on others to take care of them due to physical limitations that make it difficult for them to perform basic life behaviors independently. When interacting with the outside world for a long period of time, they are affected by mobility limitations, poor rhythm of life, and other differences, and are prone to feelings of being marginalized by society, feeling deeply out of place in their surroundings, which in turn intensifies their sense of social alienation. ③ The greater the number of episodes, the greater the sense of social alienation of elderly stroke patients. Multiple episodes of the disease make it more difficult for patients to grasp the subsequent development of their disease, and the sense of uncertainty becomes stronger. At the same time, the deterioration of physical condition also reduces their contact with society, and the sense of social alienation increases. In addition, as the number of disease episodes increases, resulting in them needing to spend a lot of time and energy on treatment and rehabilitation, the cost of medical care rises, and they are often reluctant to socialize for fear of placing more pressure on their families, which ultimately leads to a higher level of social alienation [49]. ④ The higher the level of social alienation in patients with multiple chronic diseases combined, which is consistent with the results of previous studies [50]. Elderly stroke patients are often associated with other chronic diseases and have complex and fragile health conditions. The intertwining of multiple diseases makes them slow and energetic, making it difficult for them to integrate into normal social life, gradually disconnecting them from society and increasing their sense of social alienation. Therefore, healthcare workers should pay more attention to these patients, communicate with them to understand their inner needs, help them relieve negative emotions such as anxiety and depression, enhance their confidence and courage to face the disease, and improve their sense of social belonging and self-worth.

The stronger the social support and psychological resilience, the lower the sense of social alienation among elderly stroke patients

The results of this study show that social support and psychological resilience are protective factors for social alienation and are negatively correlated with social alienation. Social support significantly influences the level of social alienation in elderly stroke patients [51]. When elderly stroke patients have a high level of social support, they can receive help from multiple sources, such as family members, friends, the community, and social welfare organizations. The care and companionship of family members can significantly reduce the loneliness of elderly stroke patients and provide them with emotional support [52]. Family members’ assistance in daily life, such as diet and living, is crucial for patients’ recovery and reduction of alienation. At the same time, family members provide psychological guidance to patients through listening and understanding to help them better adapt to the changes brought by the disease. Regular follow-up visits and psychological counseling by professional medical teams can help patients better adapt to the disease, and by providing personalized rehabilitation treatment plans, they can help stroke patients recover their physical functions and reduce their sense of social alienation.

Psychological resilience is a kind of positive adaptive ability formed by patients when they face disease and stress [53]. The more psychologically resilient elderly stroke patients are, the more they tend to be able to cope with the physical dysfunction and change of life rhythm brought about by the disease with a more optimistic mindset. They are good at finding hope in difficult situations and actively seek support and help. They are more eager to establish close ties with others and have a stronger willingness to participate in various social activities, which significantly reduces their sense of social alienation [54]. Therefore, healthcare professionals should comprehensively assess the social support status and psychological resilience level of patients, understand their actual needs and difficulties through in-depth communication with patients and their families, and on this basis, formulate personalized interventions according to the different conditions of patients in order to reduce the sense of social alienation.

Elderly stroke patients with heavier self-perceived burdens have higher feelings of social alienation

The results of this study show that patients with higher self-perceived burdens have a higher probability of belonging to the “moderate social alienation” and “high-level social alienation”. Self-perceived burden refers to a negative psychological feeling that patients have, where they believe that due to their illness, they need to rely on others for care during the treatment process and thus become a burden to their family [28]. For elderly stroke patients, the burdens they perceive are often multifaceted. The disability, dependence caused by the disease, and the fear of future uncertainties all contribute to a strong sense of “becoming a burden to others”, which significantly exacerbates their psychological stress [55]. Moreover, the pain, fatigue, and functional impairments caused by the disease place an excessive burden on the patients’ bodies beyond the normal range; coupled with the long-term nature of disease treatment and rehabilitation, patients need to continuously pay high medical expenses, which also imposes a heavy economic pressure on them [56]. Patients with higher self-perceived burdens were not only worried about their health, but also worried about the financial and emotional pressure on their families, and were filled with guilt and self-blame [57]. The accumulation of such negative emotions makes them resistant to social activities and actively reduces contact with others, thus increasing the degree of social alienation. It is suggested that healthcare professionals should not only pay attention to the physical recovery of patients, but also pay more attention to their psychological health. Through psychological counseling, we can help patients to correctly recognize their illness and reduce their self-burden; at the same time, we can actively encourage patients to engage in social activities and create more social opportunities to enhance their social confidence.

Based on the above research, the social alienation of elderly stroke patients is influenced by multiple factors and requires systematic intervention strategies to effectively reduce the degree of social alienation in this group and enhance their social integration level. It is suggested that clinical medical staff should formulate targeted intervention measures based on the actual situation of patients. For patients with negative emotions, cognitive-behavioral therapy can be used to adjust their cognitive patterns and behavioral responses, helping them alleviate anxiety, depression and other emotions, and rebuilding their confidence in social participation [58]; Secondly, a rehabilitation plan oriented towards “group rehabilitation” should be formulated, and mutual support groups should be established to provide a platform for emotional communication, so that patients can not only better perform functional exercises in the group, but also learn social skills and establish peer support, and regain a sense of belonging [59]. The encouragement and support provided by family members can effectively alleviate the social anxiety, loneliness, and uncertainty about the future faced by patients, thereby reducing the negative psychological states caused by the disease [60]. Therefore, family members should actively guide patients to participate in community group activities or interest clubs to maintain their normal social interactions. At the same time, they should assist patients in mastering the skills of electronic devices such as smartphones and tablets, enabling them to maintain close contact with relatives and friends who cannot communicate in person through online channels such as videos and social media platforms. Through the above intervention measures, a complete social support network can be constructed, more effectively promoting the optimization and improvement of patients’ physical and mental states, and effectively reducing their sense of social alienation.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The sample of this study only came from Liaoning Province in China, which affected the representativeness of the sample. It is recommended that a larger sample from other regions be selected in the future to include more factors to further investigate the results. Second, this study was a cross-sectional investigation, which could not establish the causal relationship between variables, establishing a longitudinal research design to further explore the dynamic process of social alienation in elderly stroke patients in future studies. Furthermore, although this study controlled for potential confounding factors caused by individuals with cognitive impairments through strict inclusion and exclusion criteria, there may still be unidentified mild cognitive impairments that could be confounding variables. It is recommended that, in future investigations, professional cognitive assessment tools be used to conduct routine screening for all study subjects, thereby reducing the possibility of measurement bias. Finally, this study adopted a self-report survey method, which inevitably introduced subjective biases. This posed certain challenges to the objectivity of the results and suggested that subsequent research should explore strategies to reduce the influence of subjectivity.

Conclusion

In this study, we categorized the social alienation of elderly stroke patients into “low level social alienation”, “moderate social alienation”, and “high level social alienation " through latent profile analysis, indicating the existence of group heterogeneity. Each category had significant differences in age, literacy, monthly household income, self-care, number of episodes, number of comorbid chronic diseases, social support, psychological resilience, and self-perceived burden. Healthcare professionals should pay attention to the heterogeneity of social alienation in elderly stroke patients and develop individualized interventions according to the characteristics of different categories to effectively improve their social alienation, enhance their quality of life and mental health, and help them better integrate into society.
