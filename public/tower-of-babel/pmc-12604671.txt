Transcriptional pause release at enhancers mediates cell identity
Authors: Thomas L Ekstrom, Steven A Johnsen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis short review highlights recent findings related to transcription factor-mediated RNA polymerase II (Pol II) pause release at enhancers and its role in regulating cell identity. We discuss how the transcriptional state of Pol II, either paused or released, shapes enhancer–promoter interactions, thereby influencing gene expression programs that define cell identity. We further summarize current knowledge of DNA-binding transcription factors that regulate Pol II pause release at enhancers and explore how this process influences cell identity. We propose a novel concept of therapy-induced molecular amnesia, where temporary inhibition of enhancer-proximal Pol II pause release and enhancer RNA transcription affects enhancer–promoter interactions differently in distinct cellular contexts. In some contexts, preservation of enhancer–promoter interactions allows rapid transcriptional reactivation and maintenance of cell identity upon recovery. In other contexts, repeated inhibition of enhancer RNA transcription leads to the permanent dissociation of enhancer–promoter interactions and loss of cell identity. Our model suggests that manipulating Pol II pause release at enhancers could selectively reverse certain aggressive tumor cell identities, limit tumor cell plasticity, and improve therapy responsiveness.

Introduction

Gene expression is a tightly controlled process with multiple layers of regulation. Briefly, transcription can be broken down into distinct steps: pre-initiation complex (PIC) formation, transcriptional initiation, RNA polymerase II (Pol II) pause, subsequent pause release and productive elongation with FACT (FAcilitates Chromatin Transcription) chromatin remodeling, and ultimately transcriptional termination. Here, we will provide a brief overview of PIC formation and Pol II pause release, while more comprehensive reviews can be found elsewhere on these transcriptional processes [1–3]. Initially, TFIID, comprised of TATA-binding protein (TBP) and TBP-associated factors (TAFs), complexes with TFIIA at both TATA-containing and TATA-less promoters to recruit general transcription factors along with other factors to recruit Pol II to the promoter region. The TFIIH subunit cyclin-dependent kinase 7 (CDK7) phosphorylates serine 5 (Ser5) of the YSPTSPS heptapeptide repeat of the C-terminal domain (CTD) of the large Pol II subunit RPB1 (Pol II pSer5) [4]. The CTD consists of 52 heptapeptide repeats whereby modification at nearly any residue can influence its scaffolding function. CDK7-mediated Ser5 phosphorylation allows the recruitment of capping factors, escape from core promoter-bound general transcription factors (GTFs), and initiation of transcription [5–7]. Another TFIIH subunit, xeroderma pigmentosum group B complementing protein (XPB), utilizes its helicase activity to open the DNA to allow Pol II access to the template strand [8, 9]. After initiation, the pausing factors negative elongation factor (NELF; composed of subunits A, B, C/D and E) and DRB sensitivity-inducing factor (DSIF; composed of SPT4 and SPT5) associate with the nascent transcript emerging from Pol II and clamp upstream DNA, thereby inducing a promoter-proximal paused state [10–13]. Transcriptional pausing is often a major rate-limiting step in gene expression [14] and serves as a regulatory checkpoint to ensure proper 5′ capping of nascent RNA before transcriptional elongation commences, and can help to maintain chromatin accessibility near promoters [15].

Initiating or paused Pol II frequently becomes terminated [16]. A fraction of unterminated Pol II can remain paused at many genes until P-TEFb (positive transcription elongation complex-b) is recruited either by bromo- and extraterminal (BET) domain protein BRD4, super elongation complex (SEC), Mediator, and/or directly by a transcription factor. P-TEFb consists of the protein kinase subunit CDK9 and its regulatory subunit Cyclin T1 [17, 18]. Interestingly, studies have shown that the BRD4 bromodomains, which bind acetylated histones, are dispensable for P-TEFb recruitment. Instead, the P-TEFb-interacting C-terminal motif (CTM) of BRD4 is required for CDK9 recruitment and pause release by binding directly to Pol II [19]. CDK9 phosphorylates the subunits of DSIF and NELF complexes, the ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme 2A (UBE2A), and serine 2 (ser2) on the CTD of the large Pol II subunit RPB1 (Pol II pSer2) [20–25]. Phosphorylation of NELF leads to its dissociation from the nascent transcript emerging from Pol II while the DSIF complex remains associated with Pol II and assumes a positive role in elongation upon phosphorylation. Phosphorylation of UBE2A is a pre-requisite for RNF20/40-mediated H2B monoubiquitination, a chromatin modification intimately connected to transcriptional elongation [25].

Typically, P-TEFb exists in an equilibrium between an active and inactive complex with small nuclear RNA (snRNA) 7SK and the HEXIM1/2 proteins [26]. Other factors also influence Pol II release. For example, loss of Pol II-associated factor 1 (PAF1) results in the release of Pol II through the rapid recruitment of SEC to the promoter [27]. SPT6 was reported to maintain the paused state by recruiting PAF1C to Pol II. Loss of SPT6 results in Pol II release and association of NELF in the gene bodies, leading to termination of longer genes [28]. After Pol II release, the CTD recruits additional factors for proper elongation, co-transcriptional splicing, termination, and 3′ end formation.

While the mechanisms and functions of pause release at promoters have been extensively studied, the role of pausing and pause release at enhancers is only beginning to be defined. Importantly, the regulation of Pol II pause release at enhancers not only controls cell identity but also represents a potential therapeutic vulnerability in cancer. Emerging evidence suggests that disrupting enhancer-specific Pol II dynamics could transcriptionally reprogram cancer cells, making them more susceptible to targeted therapies. These findings suggest that CDK9 inhibitors and other substances that influence pause release at both promoters and enhancers, but lead to a more context-specific effect at enhancers, could be explored as novel cancer treatments.

Transcription factors mediate enhancer Pol II pause release

One of the first examples of a transcription factor that functions in large part through Pol II promoter-proximal pause release was c-Myc [29], and since then a number of transcription factors have been reported to regulate this transcriptional regulatory step (Table 1). Master transcription factors (MTFs) and Mediator have been shown to establish super-enhancer networks to control cell identity genes [30]. MTFs are transcription factors that frequently bind their own super-enhancer as well as the other members, creating a core regulatory circuit (CRC), ultimately regulating their own gene expression that of the other members. This phenomenon was initially discovered with OCT4, SOX2, and NANOG for their ability to maintain stem cell pluripotency via establishing the enhancer landscape [30, 31]. To date, examples can be seen with TP63, SOX2, and KLF5 in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma [32], TP63, BHLHE40, HIF1A, and RXR in pancreatic cancer [33], ELF3, GATA6, KLF5, and EHF in esophageal adenocarcinoma [34], PAX5 in chronic lymphocytic leukemia [35], or PRRX1 in cancer-associated fibroblast reprogramming [36]. Broadly, we refer to MTFs as transcription factors that drive cell identity through enhancer reprogramming. Here, we propose that MTFs not only establish enhancer networks but also regulate cell identity by promoting Pol II promoter-proximal pause release, frequently influencing Pol II pausing particularly at enhancers. Although Pol II pausing occurs downstream of all start sites [37–40], different genes show different degrees of pausing. Notably, pausing is thought to facilitate the rapid induction of gene expression in response to stimuli, as in the case with heat shock [41, 42] or hypoxia [43]. Conversely, rapid Pol II pausing occurs in response to stimuli such as oxidative stress [44]. Recent work has shown that Pol II dynamics play a role in chromatin regulatory functions between enhancers and promoters [45–49]. Pol II pausing regulates cell fate [50], development [51], and somatic reprogramming [52] among other cellular processes. Thus, we posit that lineage-specific TFs, largely localizing at enhancers, regulate Pol II pausing at enhancers to activate a subset of genes that define cell identity.

Vertebrate transcription factors regulate Pol II pause release

Previous work has shown that the regulation of Pol II transcription at the transcriptional start site (TSS) of protein-coding or long non-coding RNA genes also happens at enhancers [67, 68]. For example, there is a well-positioned nucleosome downstream (+1 nucleosome) and upstream (–1 nucleosome) of the TSS of both promoters and enhancers, thus a symmetric architecture of initiation regions is consistently observed across promoters and enhancers [68, 69]. Moreover, Pol II at enhancers associates with pausing factors SPT5 and NELF, and Pol II release is dependent upon P-TEFb [67]. However, Pol II at enhancers is generally less stable and may terminate earlier [67, 68]. Early termination is consistent with lower levels of Pol II observed via chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP-seq) at enhancers compared with the promoter regions of cognate genes. Similar to the P-TEFb-mediated phosphorylation of NELF and SPT5 to release paused Pol II at the pause site of mRNA genes, enhancers were sensitive to CDK9 inhibition by flavopiridol, suggesting similar regulation of both regulatory regions [67]. Recent evidence shows that CDK9 localizes to super-enhancers in a BRD4-dependent [70, 71] and independent [72] manner. In our own work, CDK9 inhibition induced pausing at many distal elements [56]. In line with this hypothesis, previous work demonstrated that knockout of PAF1-bound enhancers or PAF1 depletion increased Pol II pausing at the cognate gene and increased enhancer–promoter interactions, respectively [73]. This is consistent with previous results showing CDK9 inhibition with flavopiridol-stabilized enhancer–promoter interactions by stabilizing Pol II at the enhancer and promoter as compared with triptolide treatment, which abolishes PIC formation by targeting the ATPase activity of XPB [45]. Furthermore, rapid degradation of NELF subunit B (NELFB) decreased enhancer–promoter contacts by decreasing Pol II pausing [45], consistent with the previous finding that enhancer–promoter interactions are associated with paused Pol II [74]. In another study, rapid depletion of SPT5 resulted in Pol II instability at both the enhancer and the promoter [75]. Consistently, in mouse B cells, enhancer–gene pairs showed co-regulated transcription that was SPT5-dependent [76]. Depletion of SPT5 resulted in the loss of enhancer transcription via loss of Pol II. However, it did not affect H3K27ac or chromatin accessibility. This suggests that the presence of Pol II at these enhancers, possibly from the associated enhancer transcription, mediates the enhancer–promoter interaction. Spt5 depletion in Drosophila cells resulted in loss of engaged Pol II at both promoters and enhancers, further corroborating the above findings [67].

Consistent with these findings, our recent work uncovered a specific function for GATA6 in regulating the less aggressive “classical” pancreatic cancer molecular subtype identity by promoting Pol II pause release at enhancers and promoters [56]. Notably, GATA6 knockdown in classical pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cells did not lead to a decrease of H3K27ac or chromatin accessibility. Another study showed that enhancer–promoter loops were dissolved upon rapid degradation of Pol II, demonstrating that the presence of Pol II at these enhancers and promoters is required for their interactions [46]. Consistently, another study showed that genes that were not actively transcribed but had paused Pol II maintained their genome architecture [49]. Super-enhancers, however, exhibit low pausing indices and high activity, suggesting that pausing, specifically mediated by NELF, is short lived [67]. The authors additionally observed that genes associated with super-enhancers had increased gene transcription [67]. This suggests that genes associated with super-enhancers are efficiently released into productive elongation. All these studies strengthen the concept that enhancers directly influence transcriptional elongation.

Cell- or subtype-specific transcription factors often interact, directly or indirectly, with the elongation machinery to regulate cell fate through enhancer elements [56]. TFs can act on multiple levels of enhancer regulation—acting as pioneer factors to open the chromatin, recruiting KMT2C/D to add H3K4 monomethylation to epigenetically prime the enhancer, or recruitment of p300/CBP to add histone acetylation for activation [77]. However, in this review, we focus specifically on the recruitment and release of Pol II at the enhancer. Even at the level of pause release, this may, for example, occur through direct interactions with P-TEFb or nucleosomal positioning, providing multiple layers to transcriptional regulation. Krüppel-like factor 5 (KLF5) has previously been shown to recruit BRD4 at multiple enhancers to bring CDK9 to target genes in order to facilitate pause release [60]. Consistently, KLF5 knockout inhibited BRD4 recruitment to enhancers and abrogated Pol II pSer2 and Pol II pSer5 in the gene body, while not affecting Pol II pSer5 at the TSS of cognate genes. We hypothesize that this may result in paused Pol II at KLF5-bound enhancers as well. Additionally, heat shock factor 1 (HSF1) binds to distal enhancers and activates genes by promoting the release of paused Pol II. In contrast, the loading of Pol II on promoters occurs independently of HSF1, suggesting that the release of promoter-proximal pausing is the key regulatory step in all HSF1-mediated transcriptional activation during the heat shock response [42, 57, 58]. In our own work, we show that GATA6 interacts with CDK9, and dislodging GATA6 exclusively from a single binding site at the enhancer, using a catalytically deactivated Cas9 (dCas9), induces Pol II pausing at both the enhancer and the target gene TSS [56]. Moreover, we artificially prevented Pol II elongation in a transcription factor-independent manner at individual enhancer regions by simultaneously recruiting dCas9 left and right of Pol II, thus creating a transcriptional roadblock preventing Pol II from elongating while allowing new Pol II molecules to be recruited to the enhancer. This artificially simulated Pol II pausing at the enhancer. Similar to the effects of dislodging GATA6, preventing Pol II elongation at individual enhancers also induced promoter-proximal Pol II pausing at the cognate promoter. This, for the first time, shows that enhancer-proximal pause release can, indeed, influence pause release at the promoter. These results can be interpreted in multiple ways; one possibility may be that enhancer-associated proteins more effectively release P-TEFb from the inhibitory 7SK/HEXIM complex and the elongating Pol II at the enhancer, which loops to the promoter. This, in turn, may stimulate phase-separated condensates that further stimulate promoter-proximal pause release. Previous work has shown that phosphorylation of the CTD of Pol II drives an exchange from a transcription initiation condensate to an elongating RNA processing condensate [78, 79]. These condensates often originate from clustered enhancer elements; therefore, influencing pause release at these regions may shift the condensate preference of the affected loci. Alternatively, perhaps the enhancer RNA (eRNA) itself can promote pause release, as described for an androgen receptor-regulated eRNA associated with the PSA gene [80]. However, this explanation would require that all eRNAs are able to adopt a similar secondary structure that enables them to activate P-TEFb. Given the large number of eRNA-producing enhancers, this explanation appears unlikely.

TFs can also play a context-dependent role in regulating their target genes. For instance, GATA6 was recently reported to establish the open chromatin landscape and physically interact with CTCF to shape the 3D genome [81]. Additional studies have also shown GATA6 and other GATA factors associate with CDK9 in cardiomyocytes [82, 83] or erythrocytes [84]. Thus, transcription factors that are considered pioneer factors in one system may regulate Pol II pause release in another system. One such example is MYC, which was previously reported to facilitate promoter-proximal pause release, while new evidence shows that it is required for Pol II recruitment at the enhancer, supporting dual roles of transcription factors in regulating Pol II [85].

Several transcription factors have been shown to directly interact with P-TEFb components CDK9 or Cyclin T1, but have not yet been shown to influence Pol II pausing at either promoters or enhancers through perturbation studies. Such examples include aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) binding to distal xenobiotic response elements (XREs) to facilitate elongation at gene promoters [86]. Similar results have been shown for the autoimmune regulator (AIRE) transcription factor interacting with P-TEFb [87]. Other transcription factors include androgen receptor (AR) [88], B-MYB [89], C/EBPb [90, 91], estrogen receptor alpha (ESR1) [92], GATA1 [84], GATA4 [82, 83], HIC2 [93], NCOA2 [94], PURA [95], and STAT3 [96]. Other factors such as FBXL19 [97] have been shown to interact with the CDK8 [43] kinase module (CKM) of Mediator, which has also been implicated in Pol II pause release [98]. A-MYB [99] co-localizes with BRDT at promoters during differentiation, and BRDT has been shown to function similarly to BRD4 [100].

Enhancer RNA

Consistent with Pol II pausing as a crucial mechanism for defining lineage identity, Pol II localizes at enhancer regions and produces short-lived transcripts termed eRNAs [101, 102], which can influence Pol II pausing [103, 104] and transcription factor recruitment [105]. Namely, eRNA synthesis is particularly useful for identifying enhancers that are transcriptionally active [106] and especially associated with cell identity and specific transcription factor activity [107]. We showed that GATA6 depletion decreases elongating Pol II specifically at GATA6-bound enhancers, whereas enhancers devoid of GATA6 were unaffected. In contrast, ΔNp63 depletion led to a loss of nascent transcription at enhancers. We termed these eRNA profiles subtype-specific transcribed enhancer programs (STEPs), which were regulated by their respective MTF. Interestingly, eRNAs are reported to be transcribed simultaneously with their target genes [108]. Thus, detection of nascent RNA synthesis from enhancers can serve as a rapid on-target validation of inhibition of either transcription factor binding or activity given their role in maintaining cell identity [109].

Molecular amnesia, iHUBs, and therapeutic applications

As described above, transcriptional regulation at enhancers differs between distinct cancer molecular subtypes, influencing how enhancer–promoter interactions respond to targeted therapies. Based on this, we propose a novel concept of molecular amnesia to explain how targeting Pol II pause release at enhancers may lead to identity-specific therapeutic responses in cancer.

Our work demonstrated that interactions between identity-specific enhancers and promoters in the classical subtype of pancreatic cancer are stabilized upon CDK9 inhibition [56]. Specifically, in classical pancreatic cancer cells, CDK9 inhibition blocks escape into productive elongation but preserves enhancer–promoter interactions. In our model, upon withdrawal of the inhibitor, transcription would rapidly resume since the chromatin architecture remained intact. In this way, classical pancreatic cancer cells would be primed for transcriptional reactivation after transient CDK9 inhibition (Fig. 1). Previous work shows that phosphorylation of the CTD of promoter-bound Pol II is stimulated by enhancers occupied by Mediator [110–112] or eRNAs [80]. Thus, not only CDK9 recruitment at the promoter, but also TF-mediated recruitment of CDK9 at the enhancer, may result in transcriptional elongation.

Molecular amnesia hypothesis. Intermittent CDK9 inhibition in classical cells transiently decreases transcription but maintains enhancer–promoter interactions. Upon inhibitor removal, gene and enhancer transcription resumes. Conversely, intermittent inhibition of CDK9 in basal-like cells down-regulates transcription and disrupts enhancer–promoter interactions. Over time, the chromatin loops dissolve and are permanently lost, leading to a loss of basal-like subtype identity.

In contrast, basal-like pancreatic cancer cells respond differently to CDK9 inhibition. In this context, CDK9 inhibition disrupts enhancer–promoter interactions, leading to chromatin loop dissolution and permanent loss of enhancer–promoter contact. We anticipate that this effect will be incomplete in the first round of intermittent inhibitor treatment, but that repeated cycles of CDK9 inhibition will specifically disrupt basal-like subtype enhancer–promoter interactions to such a degree that cells are unable to re-establish the basal-specific transcriptional program. In this way, basal-like cells will ultimately have a loss of cell identity—a phenomenon we term molecular amnesia. Given the highly aggressive nature of basal-like pancreatic cancers and their propensity for therapeutic resistance, we anticipate that this approach to transcriptional reprogramming may significantly improve responsiveness to standard of care chemotherapy treatment.

A related mechanism involves interactive enhancer hubs (iHUBs), which mediate transcriptional reprogramming and adaptive resistance in pancreatic cancer. Our previous work identified iHUBs as a distinct class of enhancers that drive paclitaxel resistance by promoting high enhancer–promoter interaction frequencies and increased eRNA transcription [113]. Briefly, enhancer hubs can be classified as promoter hubs (multiple enhancers interact with one promoter), enhancer hubs (one enhancer interacts with many promoters), or multi-connected hubs (multiple enhancers interact with many promoters) [114, 115]. iHUBs resemble promoter hubs, whereby paclitaxel-resistant pancreatic cancer cells gain interactions that promote the gene expression of drivers of paclitaxel resistance. Notably, iHUBs do not display elevated levels of H3K27ac, suggesting that their transcriptional activity is not dependent solely on the extent of classical enhancer acetylation marks, but rather on their architectural stability and Pol II activity. Transcription factor-driven oncogenic 3D chromatin hubs have been shown to promote aggressiveness in many cancers including acute leukemia [116], glioblastoma [117], and Ewing sarcoma [118]. Interestingly, these enhancer elements include, but are not limited to, super-enhancers. Multi-connected hubs have been associated with high transcriptional activity, often regulating genes critical for cell identity [119], also observed in pancreatic cancer [120].

Paclitaxel-resistant pancreatic cancer cells exhibit increased enhancer–promoter interactions at key resistance-associated genes such as LIF (leukemia inhibitory factor) and GPX4 (glutathione peroxidase-4). CDK9 inhibition selectively disrupts iHUB activity by destabilizing enhancer–promoter interactions, reversing resistance and restoring sensitivity to paclitaxel. This suggests that iHUBs function as transcriptional “memory nodes” that reinforce cell plasticity and chemoresistance.

Molecular amnesia provides a framework for understanding this process. CDK9 inhibition in paclitaxel-resistant cells disrupts iHUB-driven enhancer–promoter interactions, thereby impairing the ability of resistant cells to maintain their transcriptional identity. This vulnerability could be exploited therapeutically whereby combining CDK9 inhibitors with paclitaxel or other chemotherapies could transcriptionally reprogram resistant cells by altering chromatin architecture, enhancing drug sensitivity, and reducing tumor cell plasticity.

Future bioinformatic analyses and experiments are needed to dissect how the transcription machinery responds differently to CDK9 inhibition, especially across different malignant and normal cellular contexts and lineages. For example, a time course experiment should be carried out of intermittent CDK9 inhibition and washout over multiple days followed by an extended washout after the last day to observe if chromatin interactions remain dissolved and gene expression resumes. One explanation as to why basal-specific chromatin interactions may be more sensitive to CDK9 inhibition is that paused polymerase on ΔNp63-bound enhancers may be more unstable, displaying a more rapid turnover through termination [121]. This is consistent with previous work suggesting that pause duration influences initiation rates after CDK9 inhibition [122].

These mechanistic differences suggest that cyclic CDK9 inhibition could have selective therapeutic benefits depending on the lability of the enhancer–promoter architecture of different cancer subtypes. In classical subtype pancreatic cancer cells, transcriptional recovery following transient CDK9 inhibition preserves cell identity, thereby maintaining therapeutic responsiveness. In basal-like subtype tumors, repeated CDK9 inhibition could erode malignant transcriptional programs, leading to subtype identity loss, reduced plasticity, and improved chemotherapy responsiveness. In paclitaxel-resistant tumors, CDK9 inhibition disrupts iHUB-driven enhancer–promoter interactions, sensitizing tumors to chemotherapy and overcoming resistance.

Concluding remarks and perspective

Recent studies have begun to decipher the intricacies in the function of transcription factors and transcriptional machinery that enable cell identity-specific transcription programs. In particular, the differential importance of transcriptional initiation and elongation at both TSSs and distal enhancers opens up potential new innovative approaches to target specific transcriptional programs in cancer. Further studies will be necessary to test our molecular amnesia hypothesis and determine the clinical utility of CDK9 inhibitors and optimal stratification approaches for their application.
