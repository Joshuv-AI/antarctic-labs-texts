A system information modelling method in the cross-disciplinary project collaboration context for industrial digitalisation
Authors: Qianhang Lyu, Baifan Zhou, Martin G Skjæveland, Eduard Kamburjan, Andreas Schüller, David B Cameron, Yunqing Rao, Dimitris Kiritsis, Arild Waaler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIndustrial digitalisation for project management (PM) is a promising strategy for organising knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to successfully complete projects. However, system information modelling (SIM), as a key approach that enables the digitalisation of industrial systems, is often applied separately without a PM context. This situation results in fragmentation of project information and hinders collaboration among stakeholders. Limited research has explored on integrating SIM technologies and PM principles. To bridge this gap, this paper proposes IMF-PM, a SIM methodology that integrates the Information Modelling Framework (IMF) with Project Management Body of Knowledge (PMBOK) standards for digitalisation in the context of cross-disciplinary project collaboration. IMF-PM provides an engineer-friendly, multi-aspect language that enables cross-disciplinary modelling within a unified framework, while also supporting Resource Description Framework (RDF) serialisation for digitalisation. We present an industrial case study of a gas cooling system, providing exploratory practical evidence of function-implementation consistency and non-destructive integration. The case study and comparative analysis provide exploratory evidence of the practical utility of IMF-PM for interdisciplinary coordination, traceability, and project information integration in cross-disciplinary industrial projects. This research provides a new paradigm for project information digitalisation in the industry.Keywords: Digital twin, Industrial digitalisation, Information modelling, Multidisciplinary collaboration, Project managementSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In the context of industrial digitalisation, complex projects increasingly depend on structured and shareable system information to support cross-disciplinary collaboration across engineering teams and project stages1,2. Such projects typically involve multiple disciplines, heterogeneous tools, and fragmented information sources, which together make coordination, integration, and traceability difficult throughout the project lifecycle3. Project management (PM) provides processes, methods, skills, knowledge, and experience for achieving project objectives within constraints such as scope, time, and cost4. However, in complex industrial settings, effective collaboration also depends on how system-related information is represented, communicated, and integrated across disciplines.

System information modelling (SIM) provides a structured way to represent, organise, and integrate information about complex industrial systems across disciplines and lifecycle stages5,6. By capturing system elements, relationships, and associated attributes in a formalised and reusable manner, SIM can support communication, consistency, and information sharing in engineering work7. This is particularly important in industrial contexts, where different disciplines such as process, control, and safety engineering often work with partial but overlapping views of the same system. SIM helps bring these views together into a coherent information structure that supports consistency and interoperability across project stages.

The need for such modelling support is particularly evident in industrial projects in domains such as energy, transportation, aerospace, and manufacturing, where system complexity and interdisciplinary collaboration place increasing demands on how project-related system information is structured and exchanged8,9. The main challenges can be summarised as follows:

System complexity. Managing complex industrial systems demands precise control over project scope, including appropriate granularity and clear functional requirements. These systems are typically divided into sub-systems handled by specialised teams, followed by the challenging integration of them into a cohesive whole.

Despite these evolving demands, research into engineer-friendly SIM methods tailored for cross-disciplinary collaboration in complex industrial contexts remains fragmented. Existing approaches typically offer only partial support due to misalignments between technical rigour and practical usability. For instance, Building Information Modelling (BIM) provides a mature framework for the AEC (Architecture, Engineering, and Construction) sector. As demonstrated by Wang et al., BIM excels at representing static spatial geometries11, yet it lacks the functional and multi-aspect flexibility required to represent dynamic industrial assets and process-heavy systems12. The Systems Modelling Language (SysML) provides the necessary depth for systems engineering by enabling the formal specification of system requirements, behaviour, and structure through its defined diagrams13. Hossain et al. investigated the application of SysML in an unmanned aerial vehicle system14, while also noting that the rigid syntax and high abstraction of the SysML meta-model often alienate domain engineers. This creates “modelling silos” where the formal architectural model remains disconnected from the practical, discipline-specific insights of the wider engineering team. Semantic technologies provide a strong foundation for formalisation and interoperability. Arista et al. explored the use of ontologies for manufacturing system design, enabling structured and consistent semantic representation of system knowledge15. However, the heavy maintenance burden and the lack of intuitive interfaces make such methods difficult to sustain in fast-paced, project-oriented environments16.

To address this gap, we propose IMF-PM, a system information modelling method for cross-disciplinary project collaboration in industrial digitalisation. IMF-PM integrates the Information Modelling Framework (IMF) with PMBOK-based (Project Management Body of Knowledge) project management practices to support the structured modelling, coordination, and integration of project information across project stages. In contrast to existing approaches, IMF-PM is designed to support multiple aspects of system representation, providing broader support for process-oriented industrial contexts. It combines formal and reusable digital representations with accessible graphical representation and project-oriented usability. The method provides a more unified and intuitive modelling language that can be used by engineers from different disciplines without extensive specialised training. In this way, IMF-PM is intended to provide a structured, formally grounded, and digitally compatible SIM method that remains practically usable for cross-disciplinary engineering collaboration in response to system complexity, interdisciplinary coordination, and digitalisation demands. Related works

This section reviews prior work relevant to the study from three perspectives: system information modelling, project management in complex industrial settings, and existing modelling approaches related to SIM.

System information modelling

SIM provides a structured digital approach to managing system data across lifecycles, supporting integration, decision-making, and collaboration through formal representations of components and their relations7. The approach can be understood both as data modelling by data experts and conceptual system modelling by designers17. SIM captures, integrates, analyses, and communicates system information5, supporting human-to-human and human-computer interactions, verification, and decision-making18. It is particularly relevant in settings where multiple disciplines need to work with overlapping views of the same system and consistent information must be maintained over time. SIM can support standardisation and interoperability across disciplines and lifecycle stages, and it has been applied in areas such as construction19, manufacturing20, and facility-asset development21.

The relevance of SIM is especially clear for complex industrial domains such as energy systems, where physical equipment, control logic, operational constraints, and documentation must be considered together22. Such systems are characterised by multiple engineering views and distributed responsibilities. These characteristics make SIM a promising foundation for supporting cross-disciplinary collaboration in industrial projects.

PM in complex industrial settings

PM organises the application of knowledge, expertise, workforce, tools, and technologies to schedule resources systematically, ensuring timely and cost-effective achievement of project objectives4. In industrial contexts, project complexity has increased because of global competition, stricter regulatory requirements, and rising demands for efficiency, productivity, and digitalisation1,2. Modern projects often involve many stakeholders from different engineering disciplines, using domain-specific artefacts and representations such as 3D models, single-line diagrams, piping and instrumentation diagrams (P&IDs), and safety analyses3,23. As a result, project success depends not only on planning and resource control, but also on the ability to maintain coherent and usable information across disciplinary and organisational boundaries.

Digital technologies have been increasingly introduced into project settings to support communication, planning, monitoring, and decision-making. In particular, digital representations of systems and assets can improve consistency, traceability, and collaboration across project phases16,24. However, in many industrial settings, project information remains fragmented across documents, tools, and disciplinary views, which limits the practical effectiveness of digitalisation efforts. This creates a need for approaches that can connect project-management concerns with structured representations of system information.

Existing modelling approaches and remaining gap

A range of modelling approaches has been used to support system representation and digitalisation, but they differ significantly in focus, expressiveness, and usability. BIM is applied in construction and building projects11. Its strengths lie in structured digital asset representation in the built-environment domain, but it provides limited support for many industrial systems that require richer modelling of functions, behaviours, and non-building system structures. SysML is used in systems engineering to model requirements, structure, and behaviour across complex systems25,26. However, SysML is often difficult for non-specialist engineers to adopt because of its large number of constructs, multiple diagram types, and dependence on systems-engineering expertise. This can limit its practical suitability for cross-disciplinary project collaboration in industrial environments. Semantic and ontology-based approaches, including those based on OWL and related industrial ontologies, provide strong support for formalisation, machine-readable representation, and interoperability27. They are valuable for data integration, semantic consistency, and reasoning across heterogeneous sources. However, these approaches are often less intuitive for engineers who need visual and workflow-oriented modelling support during project work, and they may require specialised knowledge in ontology engineering or semantic technologies.

Overall, these approaches demonstrate important advances in digital modelling, but they also leave a gap. BIM is domain-specific, SysML is often too complex for broad engineering use, and ontology-based approaches are difficult to apply directly in cross-disciplinary project collaboration. There is a need for a system information modelling method that combines structured representation, formal grounding, and digital compatibility with practical support for industrial collaboration across project stages.

Our methodology: IMF-PM

IMF-PM unifies process management, understood as the core of project activities, with the PM knowledge areas of scope and integration management by embedding the IMF modelling method. The project management perspective is based on the 6th edition of PMBOK Guide4, which provides a detailed and widely used framework of five process groups and knowledge areas. A software architecture is implemented as a supporting tool to operationalise the proposed IMF-PM methodology.

The overarching philosophy of applying IMF modelling in PM is twofold: (1) to use IMF modelling for project planning and structuring, and (2) to leverage these models as inputs to and outputs from project processes, thereby contributing to documentation, communication, and verification. In this sense, the novelty of IMF-PM lies not only in applying IMF within a PM context, but in transforming IMF into a project-management-oriented method through explicit mappings to PM dimensions, project-oriented reinterpretation of modelling constructs, and operational support for integration and coordination.

Scope management with IMF context definition

Scope management defines and controls what is included in a project and what is not. In PMI’s standard process, it includes scope definition, scope validation, and scope control. IMF context definition refers to defining model purpose & target users, planned scale & granularity, and interest & modality. In IMF-PM, the IMF context and the system root node are defined in a way that is tailored to scope management, so as to address the specific complexities of industrial systems in cross-disciplinary collaboration.

Project purpose & participants

In IMF-PM, the purpose of the IMF model aligns with the project purpose (e.g., design, procurement), while the target users correspond to the project participants who will use the system model. Stakeholders are identified early, including their motivations and expectations.

Planned scale & granularity

Defining the model s scale and granularity allows the project team to establish the necessary detail level. Scale refers to the size and time frame of the industrial system, while granularity indicates detail depth.

Interest & modality

Specifying the interest (i.e., specific areas or functionalities of the system) and modality (i.e., the system s actual or intended state) refines the scope further. Interest specifies the project perspective, whether from the client (who requests the building of a large industrial facility), the contractor (who is responsible for building the facility), and the supplier (who delivers the cooling system); Interest also pinpoints the project positioning within the system lifecycle, for example whether the system is considered as a stand-alone product, an in-plant design, or an asset in operation. Based on these, interest informs the model s structure and granularity, guiding the inclusion of essential elements. Modality distinguishes between actual (operational systems) and intended (planned systems), allowing the scope to address current installations and future elements as necessary.

System root node definition

The system root node is the primary or “root” element of the model, from which all other elements are derived. It defines project scope boundaries and serves as the hierarchical focal point in the WBS, encapsulating the model s purpose and objectives.

Process management with IMF modelling

The processes in PMBOK include: initiating, planning, executing, monitoring & controlling, and closing. These processes are supported by IMF steps of context, top-level breakdown, function modelling, product modelling, and attribute modelling (Fig. 4). In essence, IMF modelling can be used as a critical tool for PM planning, and IMF models are important parts of both inputs and outputs across project processes, contributing to documentation and verification.

IMF-PM for executing, monitoring & control processes

The IMF-PM method uses iterative IMF modelling steps to streamline and enhance project implementation and verification as envisioned during planning. For execution, the IMF models created during project planning, including the context definition, top-level breakdown, etc. are incrementally and iteratively (Fig. 3) extended, validated, and refined, via function modelling, product modelling, and attribute modelling. This provides a unified structure to represent the project system (e.g., an industrial design) in a formalised and adaptable manner. For Monitoring & Control processes, IMF modelling serves as dynamic documentation for being validated with inspections, either manual or automated, to assess progress and identify areas requiring adjustment.

IMF-PM for closing processes

The PM Closing Processes involve finalising project activities, ensuring deliverables meet objectives, and documenting lessons learned. This phase confirms all requirements are satisfied, providing closure for stakeholders. IMF-PM supports this phase by providing an integrated system information model that consolidates process-related data and system knowledge from different disciplines. This model serves as a comprehensive documentation format, helping stakeholders review whether all project work is complete and if the project has met its objectives.

Integration management with IMF model integration

Integration management focuses on unifying and consolidating various sub-projects, ensuring their deliverables are consistent with each other. In IMF-PM, this is achieved by establishing interfaces that connect sub-models, while eliminating redundancies via merging identical elements. Each model element corresponds to a project activity, reflecting a dual relationship between information modelling and project implementation. This integration operation aims to combine project activities from discipline-specific project activities/information models into a unified project result/IMF model. A specific contribution of IMF-PM is that this integration is formalised through explicit principles, rules, and merging procedures for discipline-specific project information models.

Three principles and their rationales are identified for integration management:

P1, Clear integration interfaces. To avoid conflicts, integration interfaces should be defined to clarify how different sub-projects/sub-models interact and exchange information. These interfaces specify interactions among discipline-specific project activity/information models, ensuring seamless knowledge transfer across engineering teams.

P2, Complete integration. The integrated model should contain all sub-model information to ensure no data is omitted. This completeness guarantees that the model reflects the entire system s behaviour and structure. Additionally, complete integration considers dependencies and links between sub-models, ensuring that shared elements are consistently represented across disciplines.

Product model integration rules (R2-R4) define how to merge the product modelling elements and relations, with a particular focus on how to eliminate duplications. Attribute integration. It aims to collect the constraints and quantitative requirements across various disciplines into a unified information model. R5 defines the integration rule for attributes and the detailed process of attribute integration (Fig. 5c). An attribute consists of a value, a unit, and a name. The difference from other modelling elements is that it may have a default value and a unit. During integration, attribute comparison is carried out only after the corresponding model elements have been aligned through the preceding integration rules. Attributes attached to the same integrated element are grouped by name. If grouped attributes express the same property with the same unit and value, they are merged directly. If grouped attributes express the same property but with different values, the interpretation depends on context. When the differing values refer to the same system element under the same project scope, modality, and intended design state, they indicate a conflict and should be flagged for resolution through interdisciplinary review. When the differing values instead correspond to different operating states, discipline-specific viewpoints, safety-related constraints, or alternative design solutions, they are retained as parallel attributes in the integrated model. In this way, attribute integration remains non-destructive while also making conflicts explicit, which improves both reproducibility of the integration procedure and traceability of subsequent project decisions.

IMF-PM software architecture

The IMF-PM architecture is a three-layer software framework designed to support IMF-PM digitalisation, as shown in Fig. 7a. This architecture is part of the contribution of IMF-PM because it operationalises the method, supporting the practical application of the method through structured modelling, information integration, and digital project coordination. The data layer includes both unstructured data (e.g., project documents) and structured data, such as relational databases, RDF-serialised IMF models, and supporting ontologies. The Logic Layer defines the core operations: a project management component for status visualisation; an IMF element repository handles information modelling; an RDF module serialises relational and graphical data into RDF format; and integration algorithms that support model integration. The Application Layer encapsulates main functionalities: project management, modelling module, and model integration, that are supported by corresponding logic operations. The frontend offers a project dashboard (Fig. 7c), and a graphical user interface serving as model editor for IMF modelling (Fig. 7b).

IMF-PM software architecture and user interfaces. (a) Three-layer software architecture linking data, logic, and applications; (b) modelling editor interface for creating and editing IMF models; and (c) project dashboard for visualising project scope, progress, and key project information.

RDF serialisation is not only a technical mechanism for formalising model data, but also supports project management in practical ways. In IMF-PM, RDF is used because it represents model information as explicit subject-predicate-object triples. For example, statements such as PumpingFunction- fulfilledBy - PumpSystem, or PumpSystem - hasAttribute - FlowRate, can be stored in a standard and machine-readable form. This allows the same project information to be interpreted both through the graphical model by users and through formal queries by software tools.

For project management, the value of RDF lies in the managerial functions enabled by such explicit representations. In particular, it supports: (1) traceability, by preserving explicit links between requirements, functions, implementations, interfaces, and attributes; (2) consistency across disciplines, by allowing process, automation, and safety teams to refer to the same system elements in a shared digital form; (3) integration of project information, because information from models can be linked through common identifiers and relations; and (4) querying and oversight, since structured project information can be searched to inspect modelling status, dependencies, and integration progress.

A practical example is the handling of a design change in the cooling system case. Suppose that the required flow rate of the pumping system is revised during the planning or execution stage. In a conventional document-based workflow, the project manager and engineers must manually check multiple discipline-specific documents to determine which functions, components, interfaces, and safety constraints are affected, and inconsistencies may easily remain unnoticed. With RDF serialisation, these dependencies are explicitly linked in the model. This makes it possible to identify, for example, which function is fulfilled by the pump system, which attributes record the required flow rate, which automation elements depend on this parameter, and which safety-related elements may also be affected. From a managerial perspective, this supports faster impact assessment, clearer assignment of follow-up tasks across teams, and more reliable monitoring of whether the change has been consistently reflected in the integrated project information.

In the proposed architecture, the model editor provides the graphical interface for creating IMF-PM models, while RDF serialisation makes these models reusable in the integration module and project dashboard. This improves coordination, information reuse, and oversight across project stages, especially when project managers need to follow how technical changes propagate across discipline-specific models and project responsibilities.

Use case: project management for designing a cooling system

We employ the proposed IMF-PM methodology in an industrial gas cooling system design project to illustrate its application in a realistic industrial setting. This case study is simplified from a real-world scenario involving a supplier of a large international company in the energy sector. This project involves a diverse group of stakeholders from six different affiliations across five countries in Europe and Asia, representing both academia and industry. This work is also carried out as a contribution to developing standards for automation in the chemical industries, e.g., NAMUR.

Project and system description

The project aims to design a cooling system, serving as a thermal energy exchange system that uses a liquid medium (e.g., seawater) to cool high temperature gas. As conceptualised in Fig. 8a, b, a large volume of coolant is continuously cooled via heat exchangers and a globe valve, stored in a reservoir tank, and circulated by a pump. The need for improved cooling-system design is driven by industrial demands, since cooling systems may account for approximately one fifth of total electricity consumption in the relevant energy-sector setting29. In addition to this practical motivation, the inherently cross-disciplinary nature of the cooling system makes it a suitable case study involving process engineering, automation and safety engineering.

Process discipline

The process engineering work for designing the cooling system is planned and executed through IMF modelling procedure. For the monitoring of process design, IMF model inspections are carried out by stakeholders, including internal process engineering partners and engineers from automation and safety engineering.

Following modelling steps and process conceptual planning (Fig. 8b), the process engineering team iteratively works on process function aspect model and product aspect model. The function aspect focuses on the decomposition of process engineering functions. Similar to the top-level breakdown, in function modelling, the parent node “Process Cooling” is decomposed into five sub-activities: SteeringFlow, StoringInTank, Cooling gas, PumpingByCentrifugalMotion, and Cooling LiquidMedium. The product aspect represents the resources intended to fulfil these activities and the way in which they are connected. Five connected subsystems are assigned: Globe Valve, Water Tank, Plate Heat Exchanger, Pumping System, and Coolant Chiller. These subsystems can be further decomposed to finer-grained components that are worked by smaller teams or team members. The attributes are specified to model elements when needed.

All model elements are serialised into RDF for digital documentation. For example, the Globe Valve is depicted graphically in the model editor and can also be serialised into RDF/Turtle, as illustrated in Fig. 8c and d. The discipline-specific modelling figures are derived from the corresponding process model.

Automation discipline

The automation design for the cooling system project is planned and executed using IMF modelling. In this case, the pump is controlled by the signals from the water tank and the gas cooler. For the PM monitoring of the automation design, IMF model inspections are conducted by internal automation partners and engineers from process and safety engineering to verify the automation design scheme.

Following modelling steps and process conceptual planning (Fig. 8a), the automation engineering team iteratively works on automation modelling. The Automatic control function is decomposed into two instrumentation activities: MeasuringProcessVariable, and ControllingProcessVariable, handling liquid level and temperature. The previous function block delivers an electronic signal to the second through a Connector. Product modelling specifies the physical implementation of instrumentation: Level Switch and Temperature Element fulfil measurement, while Variable Frequency Drive (VFD) controls motor speed. The VFD receives signals from both the Level Switch in the Tank and the Temperature Transmitter in the Plate Heat Exchanger, and controls the Alternating CurrentMotor through an Electronic Frequency Converter, ensuring appropriate flow speed in response to system conditions.

Safety discipline

The safety engineering work for designing the cooling system is planned and executed using IMF modelling. For the monitoring and control of the safety design, IMF model inspections are implemented by internal safety engineering partners, facilitating verification of the safety design scheme under the unified architecture. Meanwhile, the safety model can be communicated to engineers in automation and process engineering to inform them of safety-related requirements. The primary hazards include dry running due to insufficient coolant and excessive motor speed of the pump, both of which pose operational risks. These risks are mitigated by ensuring sufficient coolant levels and regulated motor operation, driven by stakeholder demands for operational safety.

Following modelling steps and process conceptual planning (Fig. 8a), the safety engineering team iteratively works on safety modelling. In Function Aspect, the block “Operating Safely” is decomposed into subfunctions such as Measuring Process Variable, Instrumentation Activity, and Shutting Off Flow, focusing on monitoring motor speed and coolant levels. The product model includes instrumentation like Level Switch for level measurement, and detailed representations of the Pumping System, including Rotation Speed Element and Variable Frequency Drive, which fulfil monitoring and shutdown functions. This structured modelling supports clear communication and traceability of safety design decisions.

Beyond these structural observations, the case study also provides exploratory practical evidence of how IMF-PM supports project management and interdisciplinary coordination. The cooling system project information model follows the syntactic and compositional rules of IMF-PM. The numerical relationships serve as consistency checks of the resulting model, indicating structural coherence, completeness of integration, and adherence to the IMF-PM modelling and integration rules for future modelling efforts. While the present case study does not yet constitute a controlled quantitative performance evaluation, it provides concrete practical evidence of the applicability and project-management utility of IMF-PM.

Simplicity

Simplicity refers to the ease of adoption, understanding, and execution of a methodology for industrial system projects. This criterion is particularly relevant to the challenge of system complexity, because methods with excessive modelling overhead are difficult to apply consistently in large, cross-disciplinary industrial projects. It ensures that the modelling approach is accessible to practitioners with varying levels of expertise and minimises unnecessary complexity. Simplicity is influenced by two key factors: language elements and procedure.

Language element simplicity. Language elements are the basic building blocks or constructs of a modelling language, such as symbols, graphical elements, and their relationships. A simpler set of language elements reduces cognitive load on users and enhances usability, particularly for interdisciplinary teams.

PM+SysML: SysML provides a robust but complex modelling language with a large set of graphical elements and diagram types. For example, SysML includes nine diagram types, each with unique symbols and semantics. Elements like ports are subdivided into numerous variants (e.g., ProxyPort, FullPort), creating a steep learning curve for users. While SysML offers detailed modelling capabilities, its complexity makes it less accessible to non-experts.

Engineer-friendliness

A SIM method is considered to be engineer-friendly when engineers can readily understand and effectively apply it in projects. Engineer-friendliness relates to both system complexity and interdisciplinary collaboration, since methods that require highly specialised expertise are less likely to be adopted effectively by engineers from different domains. Note that a method should also cover the specific needs of engineering disciplines in order to be suitable. Engineer-friendliness is characterised by three key aspects (Table 5): the required expertise of users, clear guidance for engineers, and tool support tailored to engineering practitioners. Engineer-friendliness appears to be an important distinguishing characteristic of IMF-PM, ensuring accessibility and usability for engineers across different domains. The approach reduces cognitive overhead by aligning with engineers workflows and providing intuitive modelling steps that facilitate project implementation.

PM+SysML: For PM using SysML, users need to learn both data modelling and engineering discipline knowledge, which can be challenging. Furthermore, SysML data is typically encapsulated in an eXtensible Markup Language (XML) format, hiding engineering information from domain expert readers. Taking the cooling system design project case as an example, the professional background knowledge required for users of these three languages includes Process engineering, Automation engineering, Safety engineering, Systems engineering, and SysML specifications. Due to the necessity of interdisciplinary collaboration, engineers need to both engage in iterative communication with the model designer and be familiar with language specifications. In practical applications, incorrect usage often arises from an inaccurate understanding of SysML concepts. For example, activity diagrams, designed for behaviour expression, are often misused in requirement representation. This conceptual confusion can introduce errors into the information model. SysML is oriented towards systems engineers, and its inapplicability to engineering experts in other fields, such as process systems engineering, has been noted in existing publications31.

Interdisciplinarity

Interdisciplinarity refers to the applicability of the method across various engineering fields throughout the project lifecycle. This criterion directly addresses the challenge of interdisciplinary collaboration identified in Section Introduction, as it evaluates how well a method supports communication, coordination, and shared understanding across engineering disciplines. As shown in Table 5, the cooling system design project involves three engineering discipline groups, each playing a specific role in project management processes. Generally, system engineers are responsible for modelling the entire system and must maintain continuous communication with other engineering groups. Process engineers not only develop operational procedures but also participate in the selection, configuration, and procurement of core equipment. Automation engineers are tasked with implementing the automatic control of facility assets based on process engineering and selecting appropriate hardware devices. Flexibility

Flexibility refers to a method’s adaptability in constructing information models for various PM process scenarios. High flexibility may improve implementation efficiency and allow a broader range of modelling expressions. This concept can be examined from two primary perspectives: aspect flexibility and expression flexibility. Aspect flexibility assesses how well the modelling can accommodate different project views, while expression flexibility evaluates the method’s capability to support specific expressions of diverse PM process scenarios. A comparison of flexibility criteria among alternatives is presented in Table 6.

IMF-PM: IMF-PM supports the flexible establishment of function and product models for the intended project, allowing for either sequential or simultaneous development. Specifically, project requirements and related components can be represented as interconnected function aspect blocks and product aspect blocks within a single model. Alternatively, requirements can be defined through a function model followed by a product model, or the system structure can be depicted in a product model with potential activities subsequently analysed and expressed through function modelling. For expression flexibility, IMF-PM accommodates the reality that various industrial companies have different preferred workflows and task breakdowns. Digitalisation support

Digitalisation refers to the extent to which a method supports digital transformation, including the formal system representation and data integration of complex industrial systems. This criterion directly addresses the challenge of digital transition, since industrial digitalisation depends on formal, reusable, and interoperable representations that support information sharing, integration, and digital reuse across project stages. In the following, we discuss methods from the perspectives of formalisation and compatibility with existing software ecosystems, as outlined in Table 7. Formalisation includes both the visual representation and serialisation into data formats such as JSON and XML. Compatibility refers to the capability to integrate with existing tools and workflows. These criteria are essential for automated processing and rapid decision-making, thereby facilitating the resilience and sustainability of the method.

IMF-PM: IMF-PM appears strong in both formalisation and compatibility through the integration of graphical visualisation and semantic ontology. The formalisation of IMF consists of two components: the IMF logical language (IMF-FO), which formalises the semantics in first-order logic, and the IMF ontological language (IMF-OL), which describes a subset of the semantics in ontology form. By using first-order logic, efficient solvers can be utilised to validate and analyse IMF models without the need for triple-based encoding. In particular, it is possible to use such analyses on-the-fly during development to aid the developer. The division into two distinct formal semantics, between which an IMF model can be converted, increases the compatibility of IMF with established tools. Using the OWL-based semantics, IMF models can be serialised in RDF allowing them to be stored in established graph databases, and analysed using off-the-shelf efficient reasoning engines. This compatibility facilitates the connection of IMF tools with other software and allows for the integration of IMF models with other graph-based models, such as those based on different ontologies, through ontology alignment. For example, to interpret an IMF model in terms of another ontology, such as the Industrial Data Ontology, implementing a transformation to that ontology would suffice.

Conclusion and outlook

This paper introduces IMF-PM, a system information modelling method that supports project management digitalisation, particularly in managing complex industrial project information characterised by interdisciplinary collaboration. The complexity challenge is addressed through the top-level breakdown of project purpose and establishing a unified framework that manages scope, processes, and integration activities throughout project stages. The method specifies a structured and engineer-friendly approach that facilitates communication and collaboration for project engineering teams. The integration mechanisms support digitalisation by providing consistent data models and model serialisation based on interface definition. The gas cooling system case study provides descriptive evidence of the applicability of IMF-PM in project planning, execution support, and cross-disciplinary collaboration. In addition, the expert survey and comparative analysis offer an exploratory assessment of IMF-PM against alternative approaches on the selected criteria. The results suggest that IMF-PM provides a promising balance of usability, interdisciplinarity, and digitalisation support in the studied context. The main contribution of this article is methodological. IMF-PM extends IMF into a project-management-oriented system information modelling method by mapping the IMF stages, reinterpreting IMF modelling constructs for project-management use, defining explicit integration mechanism for combining discipline-specific project information models, and operationalising these ideas through a supporting software architecture with RDF-based traceability, project-information integration, and change-impact support. In this sense, the work helps address the limited literature on methods and case studies concerning the use of digital technologies for cross-disciplinary industrial project collaboration.

For future directions, although the case study demonstrates applicability in a realistic setting and the expert survey provides informed comparative judgement, it does not yet establish statistically generalisable improvements in project duration, coordination effort, rework reduction, or inconsistency-resolution time. Future work will therefore include broader industrial evaluations with repeated cases and more explicit measurements of coordination overhead, change-propagation time, inconsistency-resolution effort, and information-reuse benefits. Additional directions include artificial-intelligence-driven modelling automation and broader coverage of PMBOK knowledge areas.
