Latent class analysis of hospitalization, ICU admission, and mortality among COVID-19 patients in Iran
Authors: Maryam Ranjbar, Mohammad Reza Bordbar, Seyed Alireza Mirhosseini, Mohammad Ali Moghimizadeh, Kamran Bagheri Lankarani, Navid Omidifar, Hossein Molavi Vardanjani
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Identifying COVID-19 patients’ clinical phenotypes based on characteristics and comorbidities, as well as their differences, helps in terms of clinical care and potential crises. Our goal is to use Latent Class Analysis (LCA) to identify COVID-19 patient profiles based on demographics, symptoms, and comorbidities, and evaluate their correlation with ICU admission, hospitalization duration, and mortality in a cross-sectional study. We included hospitalized patients with positive SARS-CoV-2 tests in two referral hospitals in the south of Iran between January 2020 and July 2021. Data from 7,968 patients were evaluated in two time-specific intervals, known as “stratum,” based on the point at which the COVID-19 fourth peak and vaccinations were nearly initiated. After applying LCA, three classes identified the most at-risk individuals, including elderly women with hypertension, diabetes, and heart disease, and elderly men with heart and lung conditions, compared to two classes of healthier, younger individuals. Hospital stays were longer in the first stratum compared with the second (P-value = 0.043). ICU admission during the first stratum was significantly higher than in the second stratum (P < 0.001) and lower in classes one and two in the first stratum compared to class three (reference class). Mortality rates were not significantly different between strata (P = 0.054). Hospital stays and mortality rates significantly differed between the classes in each first and second stratum (P < 0.001). Using LCA to investigate COVID-19 patients has provided valuable insights into different patterns and outcomes of the disease. Patient variables, including age, gender, and comorbidities, were associated with greater mortality and ICU admission, which can assist in resource allocation and vaccination planning.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75712-7.

Background

The COVID-19 pandemic has killed approximately seven million people globally, demonstrating the virus’s fatality and global effect1,2. Among those infected, some develop severe respiratory symptoms requiring ICU hospitalization for mechanical ventilation and other critical care procedures3. Predicting patient outcomes accurately through pandemics, including COVID-19, is still a crucial clinical issue4–6.

A more accurate prognostic classification of COVID-19 patients could be accomplished by considering the interplay of demographic information and medical history4–6. Latent Class Analysis (LCA) is a statistical approach that employs observable categorical data to discover hidden subgroups in a population and assigns a probability of class membership to each person7. Unlike traditional approaches, which frequently regard data as homogenous and consider variables individually, LCA reveals patterns and heterogeneity in the data that may not be immediately apparent through traditional or machine learning approaches. Providing brief, explicit probabilistic classifications makes it simpler for physicians to understand and utilize in practical contexts compared to a machine learning approach8–10.

LCA is particularly appropriate for the determining the profiles of individuals who are at a greater risk of severe COVID-19 infection, which may require long-term hospitalization or admission to the ICU. It promotes the development of a risk stratification model by including multiple risk factors such as demographics, symptoms, and comorbidities, which is particularly valuable in resource-limited settings, including the COVID-19 pandemic6,11.

Until now, a variety of studies have been carried out using LCA to categorize hospitalized COVID-19 patients into subgroups based on sociodemographic factors, baseline comorbidities, acute phase symptoms, radiological imaging, and laboratory test findings6,12–14. Despite the potential of LCA, several studies have used variables that are frequently unavailable at the beginning admission, or advanced models that are impractical to implement in the real world. Furthermore, while some studies have employed LCA in Iran, they primarily focused on COVID-19 symptoms rather than comprehensively investigating comorbidities and disease outcomes15–17.

The aim of this study is to use LCA to determine subgroups of COVID-19 patients based on their demographics, symptoms, and comorbidities, and to evaluate the correlation of these subgroups with various peaks and mutations, ICU admission, duration of hospitalization, and mortality. According to our hypothesis, LCA can identify unique patient profiles that are correlated with different levels of disease severity and outcomes, providing the groundwork for more individualized medical treatments and targeted policymaking.

Methods

Study design and population

A retrospective COVID-19 database was set up to align data collection for research with the clinical care of all patients who are tested for COVID-19 in the two referral centers in Southern Iran ( Namazi and Faghihi hospitals) from January 13, 2020, to July 28, 2021. All patients diagnosed with COVID-19 while being treated in a hospital were also included in this study, in addition to those who were hospitalized with the COVID-19 diagnosis. The study was approved by the research committee, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived.

Inclusion criteria

11,545 patients were admitted to two central hospitals (Namazi and Faghihi hospitals) in Southern Iran from January 13, 2020, to July 28, 2021. The number of patients with a positive SARS-CoV-2 PCR test was 8,262, of whom 7,968 were included in the study. The COVID-19 diagnosis was made based on the polymerase chain reaction for SARS-CoV-2.

Exclusion criteria

When a patient has had two or more tests and had experienced several hospitalizations, the research only takes into account the first hospital that identified the patient as having COVID-19; subsequent hospitalizations, even those for which a COVID-19 diagnosis was made, are not included to preserve the consistency of the data and providing comparability. Because the purpose of our study was to investigate the conditions of hospitalized patients and their results, we did not include patients who were not hospitalized in the study [Fig. 1].Figure 1 Study flow diagram.

Study flow diagram.

Missing data

Missing data for each variable were found to be less than 1%, and variable age had the maximum missing data (8 out of 7,968).The study replaced the missing values with the mean (for continuous variables) or the mode (for nominal variables) of the valid data.

Data extraction

Study data were collected and managed using Hospital Information System (HIS) database tools hosted at both hospitals. The most recent edition of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) was used to obtain and code patient diagnoses, comorbidities, presenting symptoms, hospitalization information, and outcomes. The system records patients’ diagnoses via multiple stages, which include an initial impression prior to admission and a conclusive diagnosis. The medical team documents all diagnoses in the patient’s file, utilizing the hospital’s medical records department and specialists in this field to assign appropriate codes. Comorbidities were categorized under the same general concept, including hypertension (HTN), diabetes (DM), cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)/emphysema, asthma, ischemic heart disease (IHD)/congestive heart failure (CHF), chronic kidney disease (CKD)/renal failure (RF), liver cirrhosis/viral hepatitis, and human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) disease. Based on the hospital’s hoteling calculation system, the exact number of days of hospitalization for patients in standard and special care departments was extracted.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

The histogram reveals a distinct shift in the patterns of hospital admissions around March 31, 2021. This specific date provides a logical reference point for splitting the data to compare the two distinct periods, which is also linked to the beginning of the fourth peak of COVID-19 and the implementation of vaccination in Iran. Dividing the data into two strata enables us to conduct a more precise analysis and uncover possible differences and patterns that might go unnoticed if we looked at the whole dataset as one piece18,19.

Consequently, we separated the patients into two strata: the first stratum (n = 5521) through March 31, 2021, and the second stratum (n = 2447) starting on April 1, 2021 [Figure 2].Figure 2Distribution of patients based on hospital admission date.

Distribution of patients based on hospital admission date.

Latent class analysis

Latent Class Analysis (LCA) was used to discover groupings of patients with identical characteristics within a population. This strategy is especially useful in identifying latent (invisible) groups when working with categorical outcome variables16. When deciding on the best model, several criteria should be considered, including parsimony, substantive consideration, and several fit indicators. In terms of parsimony, the fewest number of classes are favored while yet sufficiently covering the population. In terms of substantive considerations, the model and number of courses should be consistent in theoretical and practical practice20.

The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were used to analyze the models’ relative fit and sufficiency. A model with a better fit has a lower AIC and BIC20. The BIC prefers simpler models than the AIC and has been proven to be the most accurate criterion for picking the best latent class model in a Monte Carlo simulation21. After determining the number of latent classes, each participant in the sample was subsequently allocated to the class that they were most likely to belong to based on various variables known as “predicted probabilities”.

Statistical tests, such as Chi-Square, Kruskal-Wallis, and Mann-Whitney U were used to see if there were differences in the number of signs and symptoms, the length of hospitalization, and the rates of ICU admission and death within the two groups. Logistic regression and survival analysis were used to determine the difference in mortality and ICU admission between the defined classes. The default assumptions of logistic regression, such as the variance inflation factor (VIF) and Hosmer and Lemeshow’s goodness-of-fit test, were validated before the analysis was started. In survival analysis, survival time was defined as the time from hospital admission to either ICU admission or death, depending on the outcome being analyzed. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were utilized to illustrate the differences in survival probabilities across patient phenotypes and strata. The primary outcomes under investigation were time from hospitalization to ICU admission and time from hospitalization to death. Censoring was applied to patients who did not experience the respective outcomes within the study duration. Log-rank tests were used to statistically compare survival curves between classes within each stratum. The statistical significance for all tests was indicated by a P-value less than 0.05. R software version 4.2.0 (poLCA, ggplot2,cowplot packages) and SPSS Statistics Version 24 were used for all analyses.

Results

Patient characteristics

48.9% of patients were older than 60 (mean = 57, median = 60), and 54.2% were male. Patient comorbidities included HTN (23.60%), DM (16.90%), IHD/CHF (8.06%), CKD/RF (2.28%), COPD/Emphysema (2.32%), Cancer (1.10%), Liver cirrhosis/ Viral hepatitis (0.28%), Asthma (1.40%), and HIV disease (0.20%). Major symptoms at presentation were dyspnea (45.7%), cough (30.10%), and fever (27.5%). The largest group (Class 3, 50.8%) had a median age of 56 and a relatively balanced gender distribution. The second stratum (Classes 4–5) included younger patients, with a median age of 51–68 years and a lower prevalence of comorbidities. Characteristics of LCA-defined phenotypes for patients

After analyzing AIC and BIC results, we chose models with a better fit and the lowest BIC value for simplicity. The best-fitting model consisted of five classes (first stratum: 3 classes, second stratum 2: classes) with two demographic factors and nine comorbidity variables. The demographic variables were age and sex, and the comorbidity variables included the presence of HTN, DM, IHD/CHF, CKD/RF, COPD/Emphysema, Cancer, Liver cirrhosis/ Viral hepatitis, Asthma, and HIV disease [Tables 1 and 2 in supplementary material].

Discussion

In this research, we used LCA, which is an innovative method to investigate patients’ different phenotypes and outcomes, including ICU admission and mortality rates. Out of 7,968 COVID-19 patients, 5,521 patients during the first stratum and 2,447 patients during the second stratum were admitted to hospitals. Patients who were older and had more comorbid illnesses (class 1 and 2 in the first stratum, and class 4 in the second stratum) had higher mortality rates compared to the patients in the reference class, who were younger and without comorbidities.

Our findings were quite similar to those published by Teng et al. in the United States, which found that the C1 group, which consisted of older patients with a higher proportion of comorbidities, such as cardiovascular diseases (HTN, coronary artery disease, and CHF) and pre-existing respiratory and neurological diseases, had higher death rates. This class of patients shared similarities with classes one and two in the first stratum, and class four in the second stratum, besides having a higher mortality rate. In their study, the C2 group correlated with classes 3 in the first stratum and 5 in the second stratum, which included younger patients with no history of CHF or CAD. Other comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, CKD, or pre-existing neurological, gastrointestinal, or respiratory disease, were much lower. In contrast to the absence of inflammatory indicators in our study, the C2 group had a higher level of C-reactive protein (CRP) and was more obese; neither of them were evaluated in our research11.

Previous studies about coronavirus showed that old male patients with comorbidities such as HTN, DM, renal failure, and cardiovascular disease have a higher risk of ICU admission and mortality22,23. Likewise, we found in our study that older men with underlying cardiovascular and respiratory diseases had higher mortality rates despite lower ICU admissions. Although age is an independent risk factor of disease severity and mortality in COVID-19 patients, the effects of gender on the outcome as an independent risk factor is a matter of debate. Whether comorbidities including HTN, DM, and IHD occur more frequently in men or women is not clear, and varies in different populations. The proper use of these data could help manage and use medical resources more efficiently24.

Inflammatory biomarkers are valuable predictors of disease severity and prognosis14. According to X. Wang et al. study, a sub-class of older COVID patients with elevated CRP, lymphopenia, anemia, hypoalbuminemia, and renal failure are at the highest risk of death or ICU admission in comparison to the other 4 sub-classes. In the second place, a class of patients with elevated CRP, white blood cells (WBC), and platelets had the highest probability of transfer to ICU and mortality. These patients were younger and also had lower comorbidities than the previous sub-class8.

Renal dysfunction plays an important role in the patient’s characteristics and severity of illness. In a multicentered latent class study of COVID-19 patients, they identified low-morbidity, high-morbidity, and renal morbidity phenotypes. Renal morbidity phenotype patients had worse oxygenation with mechanical ventilators, and a greater risk of cardiac arrest, non-infectious shock, and bleeding, while high-morbidity phenotype patients had a greater incidence of bacterial pneumonia, other infections, and septic shock. Mortality in both phenotypes was higher than mortality in the low-morbidity phenotype. The risk of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) and pulmonary embolism in patients with low-morbidity phenotype was higher than other phenotypes. Their findings support the notion that renal function is a significant outcome predictor that should be considered during disease treatment and recovery. Despite these findings, renal impairment, due to its low prevalence in our population, does not appear to play a critical role in predicting patients’ phenotype or disease outcome25.

Lower respiratory tract infections are considered an important cause of morbidity and mortality especially in children younger than 5 years of age and low socioeconomic groups26. We studied adults with COVID-19 disease without information about their socioeconomic level. Interesting future studies should focus on the class analysis of patients accounting for their socioeconomic level.

According to Heidari et al.‘s findings, the vaccination of the general population in Iran began in a restricted manner in February 2021 and continued at a slow pace through March 31, 2021, which linked with the beginning of the fourth peak of Iran. As shown earlier in Fig. 2 on March 31, 2021, the notable change in the admission rate indicates a significant transformation in the primary factors influencing hospital admissions. Therefore, it is essential to investigate the two strata independent in order to fully understand the dynamics prior to and following that increase18,19.

Furthermore, it is evident that during the second stratum, which was simultaneous with the onset of the fourth wave and the implementation of vaccination in Iran, there was a reduction in ICU admissions (17–11%) compared to deaths (19–18%). It might show early substantial effects of vaccination on ICU admission for high-risk individuals with comorbidities, up to the end of our study, which was around the middle of the fifth peak, while only 4% of the population was vaccinated. The minor impact on mortality reduction might be because death lags behind ICU admission as an indicator of illness severity, requiring more time to manifest than ICU admission.

Another explanation for this minor variation in mortality could be the effects of the prevalent COVID-19 mutation, including its aggression and severity moreover the targeted group For instance, it appears that women and young people are more affected by the delta variation than men. This is evident in the absence of class two, which consists of older, more male individuals with IHD/CHF and COPD/emphysema, in the second stratum, where the dominant delta variant appears at the fifth peak, compared to the first stratum, where the alpha variant dominates. Additionally, due to the aggressive and severe nature of variation delta, vaccination did not reduce fatality rates27,28.

Our study was powered by a quite large number of participants and studying the patients in two different strata of the disease, which made the conclusions more robust. Because the phenotypic classifications in earlier research were determined using a large number of factors, they would be difficult to apply in practice without the systematic administration of big data. As a consequence, we created a basic probabilistic prediction model for phenotypic assignment using age, gender, comorbidities, and viral symptoms. Our research exposes patterns that may not be obvious using traditional or machine learning methodologies. Providing quick, unambiguous probabilistic classifications makes it easier for physicians to easily understand and apply in a practical setting than a machine learning method. Additionally, we delved into the patient profiles, disease outcomes, and the various peaks of COVID-19, as well as their dominant variation at the same time, which was less detailed in any previous study.

Despite these strengths, the study was limited by a lack of socioeconomic level, laboratory inflammatory biomarkers, and information about body metrics such as body mass index (BMI) in the hospitalized patients.Moreover, in cases with two or more positive tests and several hospitalizations, the research only takes into account the first hospital that identified the patient as having COVID-19 for preserving the consistency of data and providing comparability; we suggest that future research should look at longitudinal data from multiple hospitalizations and readmission rates of COVID-19 patients to gain a more complete understanding of the COVID-19’s clinical course and long-term effects, especially in severe cases or susceptible ones with multiple admissions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, LCA is a useful tool to predict disease outcomes and undestanding features of various varients in adult COVID-19 patients. It could be helpful in planning the optimum vaccination target and managing patients more efficiently, particularly when medical resources, such as hospital capacity, ICU beds, and medical staff, are limited. Additional studies with more clinical and paraclinical factors are required to validate the findings of such investigations.
