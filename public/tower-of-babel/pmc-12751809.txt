Local translation of national guidance on discontinuing COVID-19 isolation into hospital policies results in considerable differences
Authors: Cynthia P. Haanappel, Juliëtte A. Severin, Karin Ellen Veldkamp, Jean-Luc Murk, Anne J. G. Mutsaers van Oudheusden, Emile Schippers, Matthew B. B. McCall, Carla van Tienen, Jeroen J. ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic underscored the vital role of infection prevention and control (IPC) policies in hospitals to protect patients and healthcare workers. National guidance documents have traditionally provided a framework for local IPC policy development. This study aimed to examine the translation of the Dutch national COVID-19 IPC guidance documents into local Dutch hospital policies, specifically focusing on discontinuing isolation measures for COVID-19 patients.MethodsThis multicentre retrospective study investigated the IPC policies of seven Dutch hospitals, of which three academic hospitals and four non-academic hospitals. IPC practitioners collected data from hospitals’ (archived) IPC policy guidelines implemented between March 2020 and December 2021. A composite index based on the criteria for discontinuation of isolation measures for COVID-19 patients was created to capture variation in policy translation within and across hospitals over time, relative to the national guidance document. Using this index, a descriptive analysis was performed to assess the extent to which hospitals adopted the national guidance for discontinuing isolation measures for specific patient groups.ResultsThe national COVID-19 IPC guidance document for discontinuing isolation measures established targeted criteria for six patient groups, and included criteria based upon days since symptom onset, symptoms, symptom-free period, and RT-PCR results. Hospitals distinguished 16 patient groups. While the national guidance was updated regularly, the criteria for discontinuing isolation measures changed once; the frequency of local hospital updates ranged from four to 12 times. Policy variation was observed both within and across local hospital policies over time. Local hospital policies tended to be less strict for patients on ventilation and/or with a tracheostomy (78% of the time), while for other patient groups, they were more often stricter.ConclusionsCompared to the national guidance, local hospital guidelines showed more variety in the number of patient groups described as well as more policy changes throughout the first two years of the pandemic. Furthermore, variation was observed within and between hospitals over time. These variations show the complexity of striving for a uniform and unambiguous policy on a national level, and indicate the need for awareness of the presence of variation in settings regarding patient groups and infrastructure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13756-025-01664-w.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic significantly impacted healthcare systems worldwide, underscoring the vital role of robust infection prevention and control (IPC) policies to protect both patients and healthcare workers (HCW) [1–3]. In the pandemic’s early stages, with limited information available about the novel virus, IPC hospital guidelines and policies were initially based on insights from previous pandemics, similar viruses and early findings from heavily affected countries like China and Italy. Although initial assessment suggested the need for strict airborne isolation measures, this rapidly became challenging due to the increasing patient numbers and limited isolation capacities. Guidelines and policies needed continuous development and adaptation in response to the evolving scientific literature, rapidly changing insights, and availability of personal protective equipment (PPE).

National and international guidance documents have always played a crucial role in harmonizing IPC measures across healthcare facilities. Typically, they provide a framework for local healthcare facilities to develop and implement tailored IPC policies suited to their specific needs and circumstances, while simultaneously aiming for uniformity across hospitals. However, in this case, the unprecedented situation brought immense pressure to act fast and provided limited time for shared decision-making, elaborate discussion or revisions.

Therefore, this study aims to examine the translation of the Dutch national COVID-19 IPC guidance documents into local hospital policies, specifically focusing on the guidance for discontinuing isolation measures for admitted COVID-19 patients. Hereby, this research evaluates the translation process, identifies areas for improvement, and contributes to the development of IPC guidelines and policies to strengthen pandemic preparedness. Specifically, this study compares the national guidance and the local policies on the number of patient groups included, the frequency of policy updates over time, and by this measures adoption of the national guidance through a composite index of criteria. Studying the guidance translation is particularly important to prepare for future similar situations that may again require rapid decision-making in the absence of extensive consultations or complete insight into local conditions.

Methods

We performed a multicentre retrospective analysis of IPC policies in force between 29 April 2020 and 31 December 2021 in seven hospitals in The Netherlands in comparison to the national guidance document [5].

Setting

The first COVID-19 case in the Netherlands was reported on 27 February 2020, and from then on, a total of 3.1 million cases, 88,700 hospital admissions and 20,900 deaths were registered in 2020 and 2021 [11–13]. There were several fluctuations in the number of cases and hospital admissions nationally, with an overall decrease in severe disease and intensive care unit (ICU) admissions since the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron variant appeared in December 2021 [14]. Similar to other countries, the Dutch government implemented country-wide restrictive measures in an attempt to prevent the spread of SARS-CoV-2, including school and childcare closures, workplace closures, cancellation of public events, and travel restrictions [15].

Hospital policies of three academic and four non-academic Dutch hospitals were evaluated. The academic hospitals in our study included the Radboud University Medical Center, Leiden University Medical Center and Erasmus MC University Medical Center Rotterdam, which are three out of seven academic hospitals in The Netherlands. The participating non-academic hospitals were the Medisch Spectrum Twente, Maasstad Hospital, Haga Teaching Hospital, and Elisabeth-TweeSteden Hospital. Academic hospitals’ size ranged between approximately 610 and 855 beds and 25,000 to 32,000 clinical admissions per year. The non-academic hospitals had a size ranging from 350 to 640 beds and 27,700 to 42,800 clinical admissions per year.

National IPC guidance

IPC measures established in Dutch healthcare facilities were guided by the national COVID-19 guidance document created by the Dutch Federation of Medical Specialists (FMS). At the start of the pandemic in 2020, the FMS formed an infection prevention expert team consisting of medical specialists (including clinical microbiologists highly educated, trained and experienced in IPC, infectious disease specialists, ear, nose, and throat (ENT) specialists, anesthesiologists and surgeons from academic and non-academic hospitals) mandated for participation by their respective national professional (scientific) societies. This expert team met on a weekly basis and formulated guidance documents based on the available scientific literature, international guidance documents and reports, expert knowledge and took into account guidance from long-term care facilities and the National Institute for Public Health and Environment. FMS guidance documents were further developed through feedback given by Dutch scientific associations and medical specialty societies.

Local hospital IPC policies

In the majority of participating hospitals, the IPC unit was responsible for developing local IPC policies in collaboration with a team of medical specialists. Typically, members of each hospital’s IPC unit chaired the respective ad hoc policy committee that oversaw IPC measures. These committees included medical specialists involved in the treatment of COVID-19 patients, such as infectious disease specialists, intensive care specialists, emergency medicine physicians, pulmonologists, virologists and medical microbiologists. Additionally, occupational health physicians, representatives from logistical support departments, and clinical department managers were part of the committee, ensuring a comprehensive and multidisciplinary approach.

Data collection

Archived versions of the national guidance documents were requested and obtained from the FMS for the period of 1 March 2020 and 31 December 2021. For the local hospital policies, IPC practitioners collected data from their hospital’s (archived) IPC policies implemented in our study period. To obtain standardised detailed information on the hospital-specific policies for discontinuing isolation measures, each hospital was asked to specify which patient groups and criteria were covered by each policy version, along with its start and end dates. Since policies were mainly described in large text fields, a schematic summary of policies was made to obtain a chronological overview of the local policy changes by categorizing several aspects of policy criteria (further described below). Additionally, this overview was used to measure the extent of deviation from the national guidance and to analyse patterns of policy changes and their development over time.

Data analysis

The national guidance on discontinuing isolation was categorized into six groups: 1) “COVID-19 patients with mild symptoms”, 2) “COVID-19 patients with moderate to severe symptoms”, 3)“Immunocompromised COVID-19 patients”, 4) “COVID-19 patients on ventilation or with a tracheostomy”, 5) “COVID-19 patients with persistent and/or pre-existing symptoms”, and 6) “asymptomatic COVID-19 patients”. This study focused on patient groups 1–4, as these were the most common hospitalised patient groups and described in local hospital policies. For each patient group four criteria were assessed: (i) days since symptom onset, (ii) symptom-free period or clinical improvement, (iii) required (lack of) symptoms, and (iv) RT-PCR test requirements. Patient groups and according criteria from all policy versions within our study period were analysed.

To capture policy variation within and across hospitals relative to the national guidance, we created a composite index, that assessed hospital adoption guidance for discontinuing COVID-19 isolation measures on an ordinal scale. For each criterion per patient group, local hospital policies were scored weekly as: -1 ( less strict than the national guidance), 0 (equivalent), or + 1 (stricter, e.g., requiring longer symptom-free period of additional RT-PCR testing). Scores of criteria were summed per patient group to generate an overall weekly policy score: < 0 indicated a less strict local hospital policy, 0 indicated equivalence, and > 0 indicated a stricter policy. To visualize variation within and between hospitals, timelines showing hospital policy scores over time were created and used to analyse patterns in policy changes.

To handle cases where local policies differed from national guidance on specific criteria, predefined rules were applied to assign scores. If a criterion specified in the national guidance was absent for a patient group in a local policy, it was assumed this criterion was lacking and thus classified as “less strict (-1)”. For example, if no days since symptom-onset were described or the decision was left to the treating physician, while national guidance specified a fixed number of days. Contrarily, if hospital policies prohibited discontinuation of isolation measures during admission or added optional requirements (e.g., an RT-PCR test not mandated nationally), criteria were scored as “stricter [1]”. However, if the national guidance document suggested “consider performing an RT-PCR test” but a local hospital policy did not mandate it, this was classified as 0, as it was not a strict requirement. If two options were given for a criterion, the scoring was based upon the least strict option (e.g., 48 h symptom-free or 48 h clinical improvement, while the national guidance required 48 h symptom-free, was scored as -1).

To account for discrepancies in patient groups between local policies and the national guidance a set of assumptions was made to enable consistent analysis. If a patient groups described by the national guidance was absent from a local hospital policy (for a certain period of time), the criteria for the most similar patient groups were scored instead. For example, if “immunocompromised COVID-19 patients” or “COVID-19 patients on ventilation or with a tracheostomy” were not described, criteria for “COVID-19 patients with moderate to severe symptoms” were used. It was assumed these criteria were used for this patient group rather than criteria for “COVID-19 patients with mild symptoms”. If local policies made no distinction between mild and moderate to severe cases, the criteria described for “symptomatic non-ICU COVID-19 patients” were scored. Patient groups described in local policies as “COVID-19 patients with a solid organ transplantation or stem cell transplantation” in local policies were categorized under “Immunocompromised COVID-19 patients”. The category of “COVID-19 patients on ventilation or with a tracheostomy” was expanded to include the local policy category “COVID-19 patients admitted to the ICU”, assuming that most ventilated patients in the Netherlands were admitted to the ICU.

This study was approved by the medical ethical research committee of the Erasmus MC (MEC-2022–0270) and was adjudged not to be subject to the Medical Research Involving Human Subjects Act.

Results

The national COVID-19 guidance document for discontinuing isolation measures was first published on 29 April 2020. Between April 2020 and December 2021 eight version updates were published. However, the criteria for discontinuing isolation measures changed only once, in version 6, published on 21 December 2020. Other version updates consisted mainly of justification of the recommended policy based on newly available literature. The number of local hospital policy updates during this period ranged between 4 and 12 in the participating hospitals.

The national guidance document described 6 different patient groups in its policy (Table 1), however, local hospital policies made distinctions between a total of 16 different patient groups (Additional file 1). Within local hospital policies, this ranged from the description of 5 to 10 different patient groups. In contrast to the national guidance document, two out of seven hospitals did not make a distinction between mild and moderate to severe symptoms for symptomatic COVID-19 patients. Furthermore, only four out of seven hospitals described criteria for asymptomatic patients. All local hospital policies described separate criteria for “COVID-19 patient on ventilation or with a tracheostomy”. However, three hospitals rather made the distinction between ICU patients and non-ICU patients, whereby the COVID-19 ICU patients were assumed to be “COVID-19 patients with ventilation or a tracheostomy”. One hospital did not describe “immunocompromised COVID-19 patients”. The adaptation of the national guidance in local policies varied from less strict to stricter and differed both by patient group and by criterion (Fig. 1). Criteria for patients with mild symptoms were more often stricter in local policies, on average 52% of the time during the study period, whereas criteria for patients with moderate to severe symptoms and immunocompromised patients were mostly equivalent to the national guidance, for 55% and 60% of the time, respectively. Criteria for COVID-19 patients on ventilation or with a tracheostomy, were less strict than the national guidance for 78% of the time. The criteria of symptomatic COVID-19 patients with mild symptoms were often stricter; this could mainly be attributed to of the required days since symptom-onset and the symptom-free period.

For patients with moderate to severe symptoms and immunocompromised COVID-19 patients, the variation was mostly related to the requirement of an RT-PCR test and the symptom-free period. For immunocompromised COVID-19 patients, four out of seven hospitals did not mandate RT-PCR testing in 2020 while the national guidance did recommend it if available. For COVID-19 patients on ventilation or with a tracheostomy on the other hand, the criteria for symptoms were mostly described as “clinical improvement” in local hospital policies rather than the national guidance’s “clinical recovery” and the requirement of 2 RT-PCR tests.

A delay between 6 to 45 weeks was observed in the adaptation of the national guidance update from a 48-h symptom-free period to a 24-h symptom-free period for COVID-19 patients with mild, moderate to severe symptoms and those who were immunocompromised.

No apparent differences were observed for the total score of policies between academic hospitals and non-academic hospitals (Additional file 2). Both stricter and less strict hospital policies were found among both hospital types.

Discussion

This study investigated the translation of the Dutch national guidance on discontinuing isolation measures for COVID-19 into local hospital policies. Isolation measures are particularly of interest as they strongly influence the hospital’s patient and personnel capacity. We observed considerable variation in the local hospital policies on how to discontinue isolation both within and between hospitals. Notably, the number and characteristics of patient groups described varied substantially across hospitals. While the Dutch national guidance document only changed once during our study period, local hospital policies changed between 4 and 12 times. We also observed a delay of 6 to 45 weeks in hospitals adopting the change in the national guidance criterion on shortening the required duration of symptom resolution from 48 to 24 h, even though the communication from the national guidance to the hospitals was instant. Furthermore, hospitals tended to apply less strict criteria for COVID-19 patients on ventilation or with a tracheostomy, than the national guidance, whereas for other patient groups, they were more often stricter.

Key factors driving variation in guideline implementation have been extensively studied in non-pandemic settings [16–18]. Insights from these studies highlight several barriers, which can generally be classified into guideline-related factors, personal factors and external factors [19]. Guideline-related factors include lack of clarity, insufficient evidence and limited applicability [20]. Personal factors involve among others, disagreement with the guidelines or hesitancy of implementation due to concerns about potential harm [21]. External factors include heavy workloads, time constraints for implementation, legal or regulatory compliance requirements, and resource capacity (e.g. staff, materials, structure and therefore also laboratory capacity),. However, it remains uncertain whether COVID-19 guidelines, which were made under immense time pressure and without extensive feedback rounds from the field, actually differ more in their translation and implementation to local policies compared to other guidelines or guidance documents.

Research available on policy variation in the COVID-19 response has mainly focused on government responses [15, 22, 23], and very few studies have investigated the translation and implementation of IPC guidelines into practice during a pandemic [24–27]. These studies on IPC adherence highlight both personal and external factors, such as fear and protection of patients and HCW, pandemic phase (e.g., community prevalence and vaccine rollout) and resource limitations (e.g., availability of PPE and testing capacity). These findings are in line with the known fundamental elements for surge capacity: staff, material, structure, and systems, including laboratory testing [28, 29].

For the criteria to discontinue isolation measures for COVID-19 patients in particular, several capacity-related factors likely influenced policy decisions, such as SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR testing, hospital bed occupancy and isolation capacity. This was reflected in the application of RT-PCR criteria for immunocompromised patients: four out of seven hospitals did not mandate an RT-PCR test in 2020, despite the national guidance recommending it when available. By describing the criteria for RT-PCR as “if available”, the national guidance document hereby accounted for potential differences in testing availability, leaving room for deviation. An important distinction between guidance documents and guidelines or policies is the fact that guidance documents provide a general framework and allow for deviations without legal consequences from the medical inspectorate, while adherence to guidelines or policies is more stringent. Guidelines also require a more thorough evidence-based development process, including extensive stakeholder input and adherence to the GRADE approach [30]. This distinction is especially relevant in a pandemic context, where time constraints and evolving evidence make stringent guidelines impractical.

Several factors could explain the observed frequent updates in local hospital policies, compared to the national guidance. Hospitals faced a variety of challenges throughout the pandemic, due to resource limitations which were at times driven by limited PPE availability, while at other times limited staff availability and bed capacity constraints were more pressing. Additionally, practical considerations in the implementation of criteria may have contributed to policy adjustments, for example, difficulties in monitoring the symptom-free period for patients, as well as possible obligations imposed by higher management to adhere to national guidance specifically [21, 31].

To better support local adaptation, the national guidance could have provided a more detailed classification of patient groups, as multiple hospitals independently expanded or modified patient groups (e.g. post-ICU or paediatric patients). Incorporating retrospective feedback from local policymakers at an earlier stage could possibly have helped address hospital-specific needs and contextual factors in the guidance document. Stakeholder involvement is essential for developing effective guidance, as is widely recognized in literature [26, 31, 32]. While medical speciality societies were partially involved in providing feedback prior to the issuance of the guidance, additional input after issuance might have been able to improve the applicability of the national guidance across different hospital settings. On the other hand, while extensive feedback rounds and multidisciplinary input are desired for a better guideline, in pandemic times with a time constraints, lack of resources and a dynamically changing need in care, this is not feasible. Therefore, a guidance document which allows for variability and adaptation to local circumstances appears to be a more pragmatic approach to maintain both flexibility and attainability.

The “adapt-to-adopt” principle advocated by the WHO, which allows and encourages local adaptation of broad guidance frameworks, was particularly relevant in the early dynamic stages of the pandemic. As hospitals operate with different groups and infrastructures, for example, single-occupancy rooms versus multiple-occupancy rooms, local adjustments are necessary. However, the flexibility of local policies also introduces challenges. Studies have shown that frequent changes in guidelines can reduce adherence among HCW, as they struggle to keep up with evolving recommendations and create difficulties and frustrations within the workforce [17, 27]. In our study, we observed a frequent change of local guidelines, potentially also causing adherence challenges for HCW. Importantly, while the national IPC guidance underwent weekly reviews, no urgent need to modify the isolation discontinuation criteria was perceived. This underscores the importance of balancing adaptability with clarity and consistency in guidance documents. Balancing these aspects is, however, a complex challenge. A key starting point for this is maintaining strong connections with the field for whom the guidance or policy is intended. This involves both the national guidance expert team listening to hospitals and hospital actively engaging with their frontline healthcare workers. Establishing a post-issued feedback mechanism would allow for more insights into the on-the-ground application to improve guidance or policy updates, enhancing its applicability and feasibility.

Limitations

Although our findings are descriptive and did not directly assess the adherence to policies, nor the underlying mechanisms for policy variation, they provide a valuable overview of policy translation. This analysis is an important first step in identifying how national guidance is translated locally, thereby providing a basis for future research to explore on guidance completeness, the desired level of detail of guidance and the barriers and facilitators for implementation. Our data was analysed using composite index with an additive factor, by merging scores from different criteria for harmonization, more detailed information and subtleties can be lost. Nonetheless, this method is effective in simplifying complex information for better understanding [33]. When interpretating the results, assumptions underlying the categorization of policy stringency have to be considered. For example, if a treating physician determined isolation policy rather than the infection control department, this was classified as less strict, while the opposite could also be true. However, because we compared policy criteria specifically to the extent to what was mandated compared to the national guidance, having no binding criteria was classified as less strict. Investigating the underlying reasons for deviation from the national guidance more in-depth, using for example semi-structured interviews with policymakers, could provide further direction for national policymakers.

Conclusion

Comparison of the translation of the national guidance on discontinuing COVID-19 isolation measures to local hospital policies, showed more variety in the number of patient groups described in local policies as well as more policy changes throughout the first two years of the pandemic. Furthermore, variation was observed within and across local hospital policies over time. These variations show the complexity of striving for clear and comprehensive guidance on a national level and indicate the need for awareness of variation in hospital settings regarding patient groups and infrastructure. Therefore, future pandemic preparedness efforts should aim for a post-issued feedback mechanism, to identify whether guidance documents met the desired level of detail, completeness, and if their implementation is feasible in diverse hospital settings. Post-issued feedback mechanisms could also be useful for other national IPC guidelines. Furthermore, elucidating the reasons for differences in policy translation can help ensure further that guidance documents are sufficiently comprehensive.
