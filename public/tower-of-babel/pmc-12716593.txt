Abnormal vortex formation by 4D flow MRI as a marker for aortic valve replacement in moderate aortic stenosis: an exploratory study
Authors: Sungho Park, Jeong-Eun Yi, Su Ho Kim, Bae Young Lee, Hojin Ha, Alex J Barker, Hyungkyu Huh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPredicting progression to aortic valve replacement (AVR) in moderate aortic stenosis (AS) is challenging. This study explored whether haemodynamic parameters from four-dimensional flow MRI (4D flow MRI) are associated with the potential progression to AVR.Methods15 patients with moderate AS underwent baseline 4D flow MRI and echocardiography, with repeat echocardiography within 2 years. Patients were categorised into AVR (n=8) or no-AVR (n=7) groups based on whether they underwent AVR during follow-up.ResultsAVR occurred a mean of 396±156 days after baseline. The AVR group had higher follow-up peak velocity (p=0.001), mean pressure gradient (p=0.022) and smaller valve area (p=0.004). Baseline peak vortex volume was greater in the AVR group (p=0.009) and was associated with AVR with an area under the curve of 0.88 (95% CI 0.82 to 1.00). Peak vortex volume moderately correlated with baseline (r=0.69) and follow-up (r=0.63) peak velocity and baseline mean pressure gradient (r=0.59) measured by echocardiography.ConclusionAbnormal vortex formation may reflect haemodynamic alterations associated with AS progression and eventual AVR. These exploratory results should be validated in larger cohorts to define the potential role of 4D flow MRI-based vortex assessments in AS evaluation.

Introduction

Aortic stenosis (AS) is the leading form of valvular heart disease, with a prevalence of up to 12.4% among the elderly.1 In patients who remain untreated, the 4-year all-cause mortality rates are substantial, indicating 33.5% for moderate AS, 45.7% for moderate-to-severe and 44.9% for severe AS.2 Aortic valve replacement (AVR) is recommended for patients with severe AS, particularly when symptoms are present or when there is evidence of left ventricular dysfunction.3 However, no specific guidelines exist for managing patients with moderate AS, despite their high risk of morbidity and mortality.4

Conventional transthoracic echocardiography has shown that, in patients with moderate AS, rapid progression to severe disease, defined by a faster annual decline in aortic valve area (AVA), is independently associated with higher all-cause mortality compared with slower progression.5 However, progression rates vary widely among individuals, making prediction challenging. For example, Palta et al reported a mean AVA decline of 0.10±0.27 cm2/year, highlighting substantial variability.6 In addition, rapid AS progression could be identified with peak velocity increase ≥0.3 m/s/year, but was unpredictable at baseline.7 While recent work suggests that baseline echocardiographic measures such as peak velocity, mean pressure gradient (MPG) and AVA can distinguish patients who progress to severe disease from those who remain stable,8 prior observations underscore the need for complementary imaging biomarkers capable of detecting early haemodynamic changes and improving risk stratification beyond traditional measures.

Three-dimensional, time-resolved phase-contrast MRI (four dimensional (4D) flow MRI) enables comprehensive assessment of complex thoracic aortic haemodynamics using metrics such as vorticity, wall shear stress and energy loss.911 Previous studies have demonstrated its feasibility in evaluating flow alterations before and after AVR. For example, AVR increases blood flow velocity and wall shear stress while modifying flow eccentricity and displacement patterns.12 Kamada et al reported that severe AS is characterised by jet and swirling flow at the aortic root and helical flow in the ascending aorta (AAo), which are attenuated or diminished after intervention.13 More recently, 4D flow-derived peak velocity has been suggested to improve AS severity grading and intervention prediction compared with standard echocardiography.14 Peak viscous energy loss in the AAo better discriminated AS-related events, including AVR, than conventional echocardiographic parameters for patients with asymptomatic severe AS.15

In this study, we hypothesised that baseline 4D flow MRI could provide insight into disease progression in patients with moderate AS who later require AVR. Thus, the goal of this study was to determine whether baseline 4D flow MRI can identify haemodynamic differences between patients who subsequently undergo AVR and those who do not, with validation against baseline and follow-up echocardiographic measures.

Materials and methods

Study population and design

Adults (≥18 years) newly diagnosed with moderate AS at our institution between January 2021 and August 2023 were prospectively recruited. AS severity was assessed by transthoracic echocardiography following the American Society of Echocardiography guidelines,16 using the continuity equation-derived AVA and MPG. Moderate AS was defined as AVA>1.0–≤1.5 cm², peak velocity ≥3.0–<4.0 m/s, and MPG≥20–<40 mm Hg.17 18 Patients were excluded if they had a bicuspid aortic valve (BAV, left ventricular ejection fraction <55%, New York Heart Association class ≥III, uncontrolled hypertension, atrial fibrillation, ≥grade II disease of other valves, cardiomyopathy, significant coronary artery disease (>50% stenosis in two or more major epicardial vessels or in the left main/proximal left anterior descending artery),19 prior myocardial infarction or revascularisation, chronic kidney disease, intracardiac device, previous cardiac surgery, congenital heart disease, prior chemotherapy or radiotherapy, active malignancy or autoimmune disease, severe anaemia (haemoglobin <80 g/L), or any contraindication to cardiovascular MR.

A total of 15 patients with moderate AS (mean age 80.4±3.9 years; range 73–84; four men, 11 women), all with tricuspid aortic valves, met the inclusion criteria. Demographic characteristics, baseline and follow-up echocardiographic findings are summarised in table 1. No patient had undergone AVR at baseline. At the time of visit, each patient underwent 4D flow MRI as part of the research protocol. During 2 years of follow-up, eight patients underwent AVR (AVR group), and seven were managed conservatively (no-AVR group). AVR was performed when guideline-directed indications were met, in accordance with the 2020 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) recommendations,20 based on progression to severe AS haemodynamics (peak velocity, MPG or AVA) and/or the development of symptoms such as dyspnoea, angina or syncope. The study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB No. PC20OCSI0133), and all participants provided written informed consent in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

4D flow MRI acquisition and post-processing

4D flow MRI examinations were performed using a MAGNETOM Vida scanner (Siemens Healthcare). The scans were acquired in addition to standard clinical imaging to support diagnostic evaluation of AS and were not part of the patients’ routine clinical protocol. All 4D flow scans were retrospectively ECG-gated during free breathing using navigator gating, and all included patients had normal sinus rhythm without evidence of arrhythmia. The 4D flow data were preprocessed to correct for eddy current phase offsets, background noise and aliased velocity voxels using in-house MATLAB code (MathWorks, Natick, Massachusetts, USA).21 4D flow data were acquired at a relatively low velocity encoding (venc) compared with peak velocity to assess turbulent kinetic energy (TKE). Due to the low venc setting, significant velocity aliasing was present during systolic phases. To address this, a rigorous antialiasing procedure was semiautomatically applied. Three-dimensional phase-contrast MR angiography images were reconstructed and used to segment fluid regions using 3D Slicer (V.5.4.0). Scan parameters are as follows: Scan time: 4.5–8.9 min; echo time: 2.25–2.48 ms; repetition time: 4.58–4.71 ms; field of view: 400×320×70; spatial resolution: 2.5 mm3; bandwidth 558 Hz/voxel; flip angle: 7° (12° with contrast); venc: 188–300 cm/s.

4D flow-derived flow quantification

The thoracic aorta was divided into three regions: the AAo, aortic arch and descending aorta (DAo). For each region, the following variables were measured: velocity, vorticity integrated over the aortic volume, vortex volume, viscous energy loss rate (EL'), voxel-wise TKE and TKE integrated over the aortic volume (total TKE). Peak values during systole were subsequently obtained, summarized as peak velocity, peak vorticity, peak vortex volume, peak viscous energy loss rate (EL'peak), peak voxel-wise TKE (TKEpeak), and peak total TKE (total TKEpeak).

Peak velocity was assessed in each region. A noise thresholding method based on the 99th percentile of maximum velocity values was applied to the entire velocity field, as previously described.22

Rotational flow indices were evaluated within the aorta to identify abnormal flow patterns, to characterise vorticity (microscale rotational flow) and vortex formation (macroscale coherent rotational flow). Prior to vorticity and vortex quantification, the entire velocity field was smoothed using a 3×3×3 Gaussian filter. Voxel-wise vorticity (1/s) was first computed using the equation, ωx,t= ∇×u(x,t), where u(x,t) represents the time-resolved velocity vector field. Vorticity describes micro-scale rotational flow characteristics within the aorta. Subsequently, vorticity was integrated over the volume of each aortic segment (mL/s) and calculated separately for the AAo, aortic arch, and DAo.23

Statistical analysis

Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data are presented as mean±SD for normally distributed variables or as median with IQR for non-normally distributed variables. Fisher’s exact test was performed for categorical variables. Group comparisons were performed using an unpaired two-sample t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test. Required group size for vortex volume was further estimated using Cohen’s d with α=0.05 and 80% power. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated, and the area under the curve (AUC) with 95% CIs was calculated to evaluate the ability of 4D flow-derived parameters to estimate potential progression to AVR. Pearson’s correlation heatmaps were created to assess linear relationships between 4D flow-derived parameters specifically within the AAo and baseline and follow-up echocardiographic variables for all patients. A two-sided p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R (V.4.4.2, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and RStudio (V.2024.12.1).

Results

No significant differences in baseline demographic and echocardiographic characteristics, including the parameters for AS severity assessment and left ventricular function, were observed between the groups, except for the interventricular septal thickness (table 1). In contrast, follow-up echocardiography in the AVR group showed significantly higher peak velocity and MPG and a smaller AVA.

Discussion

This study demonstrated the feasibility of using 4D flow MRI parameters to identify patients with moderate AS who may require AVR, based on a retrospective analysis of baseline data from the initial clinical visit. The key findings are: among the 4D flow-derived parameters, (1) only vortex volume was significantly higher in the AVR group compared with the no-AVR group; (2) vortex volume showed the highest AUC for the need for AVR and (3) vortex volume exhibited stronger correlations with echocardiographic measures.

Vortex visualisation using the Q-criterion in the present study captures the full spectrum of rotational flow, including both vortical flow and helical flow. Abnormal vortical and helical flow has been well documented in patients with BAV, where complex rotational patterns frequently coexist with eccentric forward flow.9 Such flow abnormalities have been associated with accelerated aortic growth29 and the severity of helical flow has been shown to increase in proportion to AS severity in BAV.30 Moreover, the degree of helical flow is higher in patients with AS, including those with a tricuspid aortic valve, compared with individuals without AS, and helical flow decreases after transcatheter AVR.31 These studies indicate that abnormal flow patterns have been associated with AS disease progression or severity. Our findings further suggest that baseline vortex abnormalities may provide additional discriminatory value, particularly when baseline echocardiography and other 4D flow-derived parameters could not distinguish between patients who will and will not require AVR.

For the assessment of abnormal helical or vortical flow, visual grading methods have been used in which grade 0 indicates mild rotation (<180°), grade 1 indicates moderate rotation (<360°) and grade 2 represents pronounced rotation (≥360°).32 33 However, this approach lacks standardisation and is inherently observer-dependent. To address these limitations, volumetric metrics such as vorticity, helicity and local normalised helicity have been proposed.30 34 35 In the present study, we demonstrated that the vortex quantification method can identify abnormal helical and vortical flow patterns simultaneously, although it does not distinguish between the two flow types. However, it is noteworthy that the selected threshold has a direct influence on the measured vortex volume. We observed that low thresholds (eg, 1000) retain weak vortices, resulting in larger peak vortex volumes and eliminating group differences, whereas higher thresholds suppress these weak structures and reveal clearer distinctions. While the present study provides exploratory feasibility for the proposed vortex quantification method, future investigations in larger and multicentre cohorts, along with methodological standardisation, are warranted.

In addition, caution is warranted when interpreting AVR as an endpoint. In patients with moderate AS, current guidelines recommend clinical and echocardiographic follow-up every 1–2 years to assess progression,20 and, in accordance with these guidelines, patients in our cohort underwent follow-up within this interval. AVR was performed only when guideline-directed indications were met, such as progression to severe AS haemodynamics or the development of symptoms (eg, dyspnoea, angina, syncope).20 Accordingly, the timing of AVR may not fully represent the true biological disease progression, as biomarkers such as N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) and troponin T,36 37 which reflect myocardial stress and injury, may progress independently of haemodynamic features. Because AVR decisions can vary with clinician judgement, institutional practice and patient preference, the use of AVR as an endpoint may introduce variability, and our findings should be interpreted within this limitation. Future studies may benefit from investigating the relationship between vortex formation and biological markers of AS progression, as well as related morphological alterations such as valve calcification.

The advantage of 4D flow MRI further lies in its ability to directly quantify energy dissipation, encompassing both non-turbulent viscous and turbulent energy losses, which cannot be captured by conventional imaging techniques. Prior studies have reported that patients with AS exhibit greater viscous energy loss than healthy individuals.15 24 In severe AS, viscous energy loss has also been shown to independently correlate with transvalvular energy loss estimated by the Doppler-based energy loss index.15 Turbulent energy loss, assessed via TKE, has likewise been proposed as a promising biomarker for identifying high-risk individuals, particularly those with BAV and dilated ascending aortas.38 Previous research demonstrated a strong correlation between TKE in the AAo of patients with AS and irreversible pressure loss.39

These forms of energy loss have been well known to contribute to left ventricular overload and reflect AS severity.40 In addition, echocardiography-derived energy loss index, including viscous and turbulent loss, has been associated with all-cause mortality after transcatheter AVR.41 In the present study, however, neither viscous nor turbulent energy loss differed between the no-AVR and AVR groups, despite moderate correlations with vortex formation and strong correlations with vorticity, which itself showed a marginal but non-significant association with AVR. These results suggest that, within this moderate AS cohort, the decision to proceed with AVR may not be directly influenced by energy loss metrics. This discrepancy may reflect that previous studies primarily evaluated cross-sectional differences between AS and non-AS groups, whereas our study examined longitudinal outcomes. Therefore, 4D flow-derived energy loss parameters warrant further evaluation to determine their feasibility for predicting long-term outcomes in a larger cohort.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The relatively small sample size (n=15), designed to provide preliminary insight given the exploratory nature of this study, may limit the generalisability of our findings. With this small cohort, evaluating multiple parameters may introduce statistical instability due to multiple testing, and the ROC analysis may also overestimate discriminative performance, reflecting a risk of overfitting. Nevertheless, the discriminatory performance of vortex volume may warrant further investigation.

Vortex volume quantification is highly sensitive to the threshold selected for vortex detection, and future work should examine the influence of MRI acquisition parameters, including temporal and spatial resolution, as well as postprocessing procedures. Additionally, because the timing of AVR was determined at the discretion of treating physicians, some variability in outcomes may have been unavoidable. This study also did not include follow-up 4D flow MRI or post-AVR imaging, and therefore cannot determine whether vortex volume reflects baseline severity or true disease progression.

As such, the findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than predictive. While vortex volume quantification may offer insight into flow abnormalities associated with disease progression, validation in larger and multicentre cohorts, incorporating longitudinal 4D flow MRI, will be needed to confirm these observations.

Conclusion

In moderate AS, vortex volume was the only 4D flow MRI-derived parameter that differed between patients who did and did not undergo AVR and showed modestly stronger associations with echocardiographic measures than other haemodynamic metrics. Our exploratory findings suggest that vortex volume may provide additional insight into flow abnormalities related to disease progression, warranting further investigation in larger cohorts.
