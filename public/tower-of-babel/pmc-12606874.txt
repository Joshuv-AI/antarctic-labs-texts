Early lactate kinetics predicts survival and neurological outcomes after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Hung-Hsin Huang, Chin-Han Lin, Yen-Wei Chiu, Shuang-Yu Lu, Shao-Hua Yu, Hong-Mo Shih
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLactate dynamics reflects restoration of tissue perfusion and has prognostic value in critical illness. However, the temporal evolution of lactate and its prognostic implications after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) have not been fully elucidated.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed adult patients with OHCA with sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) at a tertiary medical center in Taiwan between 2016 and 2022. Serial lactate levels were measured at 2, 6, 12, and 24 h after ROSC. The primary outcome was a favorable neurological status at discharge, and a key secondary outcome was survival to discharge. Linear mixed-effects models were applied to evaluate longitudinal lactate trajectories and their associations with outcomes, accounting for repeated measures and adjusting for age, initial rhythm, bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), CPR duration, public location, witnessed status, targeted temperature management, percutaneous coronary intervention, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation.ResultsOf the 496 included patients, 90 (18.1%) achieved a favorable neurological outcome and 241 (48.6%) survived to discharge. Lactate levels declined significantly over time in both groups (p < 0.001), but patients with favorable neurological outcomes and survivors showed a steeper decline between 12 and 24 h. Interaction analyses revealed significant time × outcome effects for neurological outcome at 12 h (β = − 0.33, 95% CI − 0.65 to − 0.001, p = 0.049) and 24 h (β = − 0.61, 95% CI − 0.91 to − 0.30, p < 0.001), and for survival at 12 h (β = − 0.38, 95% CI − 0.64 to − 0.13, p = 0.002) and 24 h (β = − 0.48, 95% CI − 0.72 to − 0.24, p < 0.001).ConclusionSerial lactate trajectories within the first 24 h after ROSC were strongly associated with survival and neurological recovery in OHCA patients. Modeling lactate as a continuous dynamic biomarker using LMM captured prognostic information beyond single time points and may help guide individualized post-resuscitation management.Keywords: Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, Lactate kinetics, Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, Neurological outcome, Return of spontaneous circulation, Linear mixed-effects model

Background

Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) a major global health concern, with annual incidences ranging from 50 to 100 per 100,000 population worldwide [1]. Despite advances in resuscitation, survival to hospital discharge remains below 10% globally and around 4% in Taiwan, where the mean incidence between 2000 and 2012 was 51.1 per 100,000 person-years and the recent ROSC rate approximately 22% [1, 2].

Lactate clearance—the percentage reduction in serum lactate levels over time—is widely recognized as an indicator of tissue perfusion and a predictor of clinical outcomes in critically ill patients. In emergency and intensive care units, higher lactate clearance has demonstrated strong prognostic significance in conditions such as septic shock, severe trauma, and postcardiac arrest states. Numerous studies have revealed that higher lactate clearance is associated with improved survival rates and decreased severity of organ dysfunction. Specifically, lactate clearance of >10% during the initial hours of resuscitation has been linked to favorable clinical outcomes, underscoring its potential utility as an early therapeutic goal [3–5].

Given the promise of lactate clearance as a prognostic marker in various critical care settings, its early application in the context of OHCA warrants further investigation. Although some studies have explored the potential role of lactate clearance in outcome prediction, evidence is sparse regarding its utility during the initial phase of care, particularly in guiding timely clinical decision-making and resource allocation [6–8]. However, prior studies have reported inconsistent results in OHCA populations, partly due to differences in lactate sampling time, analytic methods, and adjustment for baseline lactate levels [6–8]. Clarifying whether dynamic lactate changes—so-called lactate kinetics—provide additional prognostic information beyond the initial value may help refine early risk stratification and treatment strategies. A burgeoning area of interest is whether early trends in lactate clearance can serve as reliable and clinically meaningful predictors of outcomes such as survival or neurological recovery. Identifying such early indicators may improve patient stratification, support more efficient use of intensive care resources, and help avoid futile interventions in patients with poor prognosis [9]. Therefore, we aimed to characterize the temporal trajectory of lactate levels within the first 24 h after ROSC and determine their association with neurological and survival outcomes using a linear mixed-effects modeling approach.

Materials and methods

Study background and setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary medical center in Taichung City, Taiwan, whose emergency department (ED) receives approximately 160,000 patient visits annually. The hospital has nearly 1700 inpatient beds and offers comprehensive 24-hour services, including percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), and post-ROSC care.

As an urban medical center, the hospital serves a densely populated area. According to the Taichung City Fire Bureau’s 2020 Emergency Medical Services Report, the average transport time from the scene to a city hospital is approximately 6.4 min [10]. Approximately 2500 OHCA cases occur annually in Taichung City [11], with shockable rhythms present in 10%–15% [12, 13] and bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) rates reaching 60%–70% in metropolitan areas [14]. The city’s emergency medical technicians provide standardized prehospital care for patients with OHCA, including high-quality chest compressions, airway support, and early defibrillation when indicated. After arrival at the hospital, advanced life support measures, which include defibrillation, airway management, and medication, are promptly initiated. For patients with refractory cardiac arrest who did not achieve sustained ROSC with conventional resuscitation, extracorporeal CPR (ECPR) was available as an adjunctive therapy [15]. Targeted temperature management (TTM) and urgent PCI were routinely available as part of post-ROSC care, and decisions regarding ECMO, PCI, or TTM were made by a multidisciplinary post-cardiac-arrest team based on standardized institutional criteria. These consistent in-hospital capabilities allowed for comparable post-resuscitation management across study participants, supporting the validity of serial lactate measurements used in this analysis.

Participants

All patients with OHCA transported by emergency medical services (EMS) between 2016 and 2022 were considered for inclusion in the study. Patients meeting any of the following criteria were excluded: were aged < 18 years, had OHCA secondary to trauma or specific circumstances (e.g., hanging, intoxication, or drowning), had been transferred from another hospital (including patients who received first aid elsewhere), had do-not-resuscitate orders or no resuscitation attempts in the ED, did not achieve sustained return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) in the emergency department, or had incomplete clinical data. Patients were further excluded if they did not achieve sustained ROSC, which was defined as the absence of chest compressions for at least 20 min with persistent signs of circulation during that period [16]. Patients who left against medical advice or transferred to another hospital from our ED were also excluded. Additionally, patients arriving via self-transport were excluded because key prehospital information, such as CPR duration, initial rhythm, and exact timing of ROSC, was unavailable, precluding accurate alignment of serial lactate measurements. Patients without initial lactate data were excluded because their trajectory could not be defined. This selection ensured that all included cases had standardized timing of lactate sampling and allowed for reliable modeling of serial lactate changes within the first 24 h post-ROSC.

Data collection

The following prehospital data were collected in accordance with the Utstein-style template for OHCA, which includes patient demographics and event characteristics: age, sex, witnessed status, location of collapse, presence of bystander CPR, use of an automated external defibrillator, and the course of EMS attendance. These data were obtained from the EMS database maintained by the National Fire Agency of Taiwan, which includes standardized prehospital records and dispatch information.

In-hospital data were retrospectively retrieved from the hospital’s electronic medical records system. Variables included the initial cardiac rhythm upon ED arrival, time to first electrical defibrillation, total CPR duration, lactate (mg/dL) levels, time of ROSC, and components of postresuscitation care. Relevant postresuscitation interventions, including targeted temperature management (TTM), PCI, and extracorporeal CPR, were also recorded. All lactate measurements in this study were obtained after the patient’s arrival at the emergency department (ED), immediately following confirmation of sustained ROSC.

Lactate levels were measured immediately after ROSC (baseline) and at 2, 6, 12, and 24 h, and all blood samples were analyzed in the hospital’s central laboratory using an enzymatic (fixed-reaction) method on a standard automated chemistry analyzer, following manufacturer protocols and internal quality-control procedures. The exact time of each sampling was recorded relative to the documented ROSC time to ensure temporal accuracy of lactate kinetics. Outcome measurement

The primary outcome was neurological outcome at hospital discharge. Neurological outcomes were evaluated using the Cerebral Performance Category (CPC) scale at hospital discharge, which ranges from 1 (good cerebral performance) to 5 (brain death). A favorable outcome was defined as a CPC score of 1 or 2, and a score of 3–5 indicating a poor outcome [17, 18]. The secondary outcomes were survival to hospital discharge, duration of intensive care unit stay, and total length of hospital stay.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA); two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered significant. Descriptive statistics were used to compare baseline demographic and clinical characteristics between patients with favorable and unfavorable neurological outcomes. Categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-square test. Continuous variables are expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR) as appropriate. The independent t-test was used for normally distributed variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric variables such as lactate clearance.

To evaluate longitudinal lactate dynamics, we applied linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) incorporating all available lactate measurements at 2, 6, 12, and 24 h after ROSC. Each patient was treated as a random effect to account for within-subject correlation, whereas time, outcome group (favorable vs. unfavorable neurological outcome or survival status), and their interaction (time × outcome group) were specified as fixed effects. Covariates entered in all models included age, shockable initial rhythm, public location of arrest, bystander CPR, CPR duration, witnessed arrest, targeted temperature management (TTM), percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Because lactate values were right-skewed, they were log-transformed before model entry. Model coefficients (β) with standard errors and 95% confidence intervals were reported to describe the rate of lactate decline over time.

Results

From January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2022, a total of 3306 patients with OHCA were enrolled. We excluded 56 pediatric patients (age < 18 years), 252 patients whose OHCA was related to trauma or specific circumstances (e.g., hanging, intoxication, or drowning), 681 patients with do-not-resuscitation orders, 299 patients who arrived via self-transport or were transferred from other hospitals, and 1324 patients who did not achieve ROSC. Additionally, we excluded 144 patients who either died in the ED, left against medical advice, or transferred out shortly after admission. Of the remaining 550 patients, we excluded 54 patients because of incomplete data, yielding a final cohort of 496 patients for further analysis (Fig. 1).

Among the 496 patients included in the final cohort, lactate availability at each time point was as follows: baseline 496/496 (100%), 2 h 34/496 (6.9%), 6 h 306/496 (61.7%), 12 h 336/496 (67.7%), and 24 h 357/496 (72.0%). The mean initial lactate level was lower among patients with favorable outcomes (12.05 ± 5.66 mmol/L) than in those with unfavorable outcomes (14.03 ± 6.06 mmol/L, p = 0.004). The 2-hour lactate clearance did not differ significantly between groups [median (IQR): 0.04 (0.00–0.09) vs. 0.03 (0.01–0.06), p = 0.498]. However, lactate clearance at 6 h [0.07 (0.04–0.08) vs. 0.05 (0.02–0.07), p = 0.001], 12 h [0.08 (0.07–0.09) vs. 0.06 (0.03–0.08), p < 0.001], and 24 h [0.09 (0.08–0.09) vs. 0.07 (0.04–0.09), p < 0.001] were significantly higher in the favorable-outcome group (Mann–Whitney U test). Advanced post-arrest interventions were similar between groups: ECMO was performed in 26.7% vs. 20.9% (p = 0.234) and targeted temperature management (TTM) in 35.6% vs. 38.2% (p = 0.642) of favorable and unfavorable groups, respectively.

To further evaluate lactate kinetics, LMM analyses were performed. For neurological outcome, lactate levels declined significantly over time across the entire cohort (p < 0.001 for all time points). Compared with baseline, the estimated decreases were 0.89 mmol/L at 2 h, 1.04 mmol/L at 6 h, 1.09 mmol/L at 12 h, and 1.25 mmol/L at 24 h (all p < 0.001). Interaction analysis (time × neurological outcome) showed that patients with favorable neurological outcomes exhibited a steeper decline at 12 h (β = − 0.33, 95% CI − 0.65 to − 0.001, p = 0.049) and 24 h (β = − 0.61, 95% CI − 0.91 to − 0.30, p < 0.001) than those with poor outcomes. Among model covariates, ECMO use was significantly associated with improved neurological outcome (β = 0.59, 95% CI 0.38–0.81, p < 0.001), whereas other factors including age, shockable rhythm, public location, bystander CPR, and TTM were not statistically significant after adjustment.(Table 2).

Discussion

Our study demonstrates the considerable prognostic value of serial lactate trajectories during the first 24 h after ROSC in patients with OHCA. Using LMM, we found that lactate levels declined significantly over time in all patients, but the rate of decline was steeper among survivors and those with favorable neurological outcomes. Relevant studies have primarily focused on single-point lactate levels or smaller multicenter cohorts, which are shortcomings addressed by our study. To our knowledge, our investigation is one of the largest single-center studies (n = 496) specifically examining longitudinal lactate kinetics and neurological recovery following OHCA. Moreover, the inclusion of a broad set of clinical covariates, such as shockable initial rhythm, comorbidities, and prehospital variables, enabled a comprehensive multivariate analysis. Collectively, these strengths enhance clinical relevance and generalizability of our results within the context of postresuscitation prognostication.

Consistent with our results, earlier investigations in critically ill populations, including patients with septic shock and trauma, have demonstrated that early lactate clearance is associated with improved clinical outcomes [3–5]. This relationship is believed to reflect the balance between ongoing tissue hypoxia and recovery of oxidative metabolism after circulatory failure [3, 19]. Prior studies in OHCA populations have also demonstrated that lactate levels after ROSC are associated with outcome, although the incremental value of clearance beyond the initial level remains controversial [6–8, 20]. Our analysis clarifies previous inconsistencies by demonstrating that the entire lactate trajectory—not a single percentage threshold—better captures the prognostic signal of lactate dynamics. This approach highlights the importance of evaluating lactate as a continuous time-dependent biomarker rather than relying on arbitrary clearance cutoffs. Importantly, our study also confirmed that ECMO was independently associated with both neurological recovery (β = 0.59, p < 0.001) and survival (β = 0.55, p < 0.001), supporting prior evidence that metabolic stabilization under mechanical circulatory support facilitates lactate clearance and may reflect improved tissue perfusion rather than confounding of indication [21].

Several related studies have reported conflicting findings regarding the prognostic role of lactate clearance in patients with OHCA; however, these inconsistencies may reflect differences in study design and methodology. For instance, one study noted only modest associations between prehospital lactate levels and patient outcomes, suggesting limited predictive utility [9]. Another investigation with patients receiving TTM observed that although absolute lactate levels at subsequent time points (12, 24, and 48 h) were associated with neurological outcomes, lactate clearance itself was not an independent predictor [20]. Moreover, a systematic review highlighted substantial variability in the prognostic value of early lactate measurements, attributing the inconsistencies to heterogeneity in lactate measurement timing, outcome definitions, and analytical approaches [7]. By contrast, our study used linear mixed-effects models to capture repeated lactate measurements at 2, 6, 12, and 24 h after ROSC, providing a continuous view of lactate kinetics instead of isolated percentage changes. This approach revealed that survivors and patients with favorable neurological outcomes exhibited significantly steeper declines at 12 and 24 h, supporting the clinical utility of serial lactate trends rather than single-time-point clearance. We observed that differences between outcome groups became statistically significant after 6 h and were most pronounced at 12 and 24 h, suggesting that early lactate values are influenced by post-resuscitation catecholamine surge, tissue washout, and transient hypoperfusion, whereas later stabilization more accurately reflects recovery of systemic perfusion and mitochondrial function. This finding underscores that the prognostic value of lactate derives from its dynamic trajectory, which integrates the interplay between tissue hypoperfusion, hepatic metabolism, and mitochondrial recovery over time. Such interpretation aligns with experimental and clinical data suggesting that persistent hyperlactatemia reflects ongoing anaerobic metabolism, inflammatory stress, and cellular hypoxia, whereas its resolution is an indicator of metabolic recovery and successful resuscitation physiology [19, 22]. In contrast to the recent BOX sub-study by Beske et al. [23], which focused on baseline lactate levels and hemodynamic responses to different mean arterial pressure targets, our analysis emphasized longitudinal lactate kinetics. While Beske et al. found that higher initial lactate was linked to increased vasopressor requirements and poorer outcomes without benefit from a higher mean arterial pressure target, our results demonstrate that the time-dependent decline in lactate over 12–24 h better reflect metabolic recovery and clinical prognosis. Recent studies in post-cardiac arrest models also demonstrate that mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired pyruvate oxidation contribute to early lactate accumulation, whereas normalization of lactate correlates with improved microcirculatory flow and neuronal survival [22]. Therefore, the steeper lactate decline observed in our favorable-outcome group likely mirrors more effective oxygen delivery and utilization, whether spontaneously or aided by advanced therapies such as ECMO or TTM.

Our study also has several limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design limits the generalizability of the findings. Our institution serves a large urban population and provides advanced post-arrest interventions such as PCI, ECMO, and TTM; therefore, results may not be fully applicable to smaller or rural hospitals with different resources or patient characteristics. Second, selection bias is possible, as only patients who achieved sustained ROSC were included, excluding those with refractory arrest or early death. Although ECMO, TTM, and PCI were performed following standardized institutional algorithms, the timing and decision to initiate these treatments ultimately relied on clinical judgment, potentially introducing residual confounding. Third, certain prehospital variables—such as the quality of bystander CPR, exact AED shock timing, and detailed resuscitation quality metrics—were unavailable, which may have influenced both low-flow duration and outcomes. Fourth, missing lactate data were unavoidable, as is common in retrospective studies. Although the linear mixed-effects model accommodates unbalanced repeated measures under a missing-at-random assumption, the possibility of non-random missingness—particularly among patients with early death or treatment withdrawal—cannot be excluded. Such informative missingness may have introduced residual bias despite the robustness of the analytic approach. Fifth, while we adjusted for major interventions (ECMO, PCI, and TTM) in the LMM, other in-hospital variables—such as vasoactive medication use, mechanical ventilation strategy, or renal replacement therapy—were not captured and may have influenced lactate kinetics. Finally, although our LMM demonstrated that lactate trajectories were independently associated with outcomes beyond initial levels, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. Prospective multicenter studies with standardized lactate sampling, comprehensive prehospital and in-hospital data collection, and concurrent microcirculatory or metabolic assessments are warranted to validate the prognostic role of lactate dynamics and confirm the temporal thresholds identified in this study.

Conclusions

Serial lactate trajectories during the first 24 h after ROSC were strongly associated with both survival and neurological outcomes among patients with sustained ROSC after OHCA. Using a linear mixed-effects model, we demonstrated that survivors and patients with favorable neurological recovery exhibited significantly steeper lactate declines over time—particularly at 12 and 24 h—compared with non-survivors and those with poor outcomes. These findings suggest that the prognostic information of lactate lies not only in its initial level but also in its temporal dynamics, emphasizing the importance of continuous monitoring of lactate kinetics during the early post-resuscitation period. Incorporating lactate trajectories into post cardiac arrest prognostic models may enhance early risk stratification, guide the intensity of hemodynamic and metabolic support, and inform decisions regarding advanced therapies such as ECMO or TTM.
