Identification of a component of the tubulin poly-glutamylase complex required for phosphoinositide homeostasis and cilium assembly and maintenance
Authors: Binshad Badarudeen, Loren Collado, Hung-Ju Chiang, Lei Wang, Irma Sanchez, Brian David Dynlacht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The tubulin poly-glutamylase complex (TPGC) is comprised of TTLL1 and at least five associated proteins that promote the addition of glutamate residues to carboxy-terminal tails of tubulin subunits comprising microtubules. Despite its discovery two decades ago, the enzyme has been refractory to characterization owing to its complex multimeric nature and the inability to detect poly-glutamylase activity after assembling the six-subunit complex. We now show that TPGC is the key enzyme driving centriolar and ciliary poly-glutamylation. We identified two additional TPGC subunits, TBC1D19 and KIAA1841, and showed that both components play an essential role in the assembly of the eight-subunit holo-enzyme. We were able to reconstitute the activity of TPGC with all eight subunits. TBC1D19 and KIAA1841 were essential for TPGC activity, and loss of TBC1D19 strongly compromised multiple tubulin modifications, including axonemal poly-glutamylation. TBC1D19 loss also abolished transport of ARL13B and other ciliary membrane proteins, abrogating primary cilium assembly. Structural predictions suggested an essential role for TBC1D19 and KIAA1841 in complex assembly, microtubule binding, and preferential poly-glutamylation of α-tubulin. We found that TBC1D19 loss abrogated the ciliary localization of phosphatidyl inositol phosphatase, INPP5E, which coincided with cilium instability. Ciliogenesis in TBC1D19 null cells could be restored through inhibition of a specific phosphatidyl inositol phosphate (PIP) kinase, PIP5K1C, suggesting that TBC1D19 is required to maintain PIP homeostasis during ciliogenesis. Collectively, our data show that TPGC is a multi-functional enzyme essential for cilium assembly and maintenance.

Introduction

Recent studies have suggested that tubulin poly-glutamylation regulates a panoply of cellular processes. For example, poly-glutamylation modulates microtubule (MT) dynamics by stimulating severing enzymes1–4 or by altering recruitment of kinesins, dynein, or microtubule associated proteins (MAPs), which can promote MT assembly5–7. Interestingly, in vitro studies suggest that poly-glutamylation has a rheostat-like role in modulating microtubule cleavage by spastin, since precise levels of glutamylation are required for optimal enzyme activity3.

Tubulin glutamylation also plays a role in the assembly of flagella and primary cilia. Primary cilia are antenna-like, microtubule-based projections found on the surface of most eukaryotic cells. In the early stages of cilium assembly, a protein (CP110) found at the distal end of centrioles is destroyed at the mother centriole, triggering microtubule growth from the basal body and generating a microtubular axoneme that supports the cilium and promotes intra-flagellar transport (IFT) required for its assembly8,9. The nascent cilium then migrates apically and docks with the plasma membrane. As cilia assemble, axonemal microtubules (MTs) are de-tyrosinated and decorated with several post-translational modifications (PTMs), including acetylation and glutamylation, and these modifications are thought to provide the underlying stability of the axoneme10–12. Conversely, MTs are de-acetylated during disassembly13. However, the functional and mechanistic links between microtubule glutamylation and axoneme assembly and dynamics in mammalian cells are not completely understood, and functional roles in both cilium assembly and disassembly have been ascribed to glutamylation, suggesting complex and, potentially, context-dependent roles for this modification11,14–16.

Microtubule glutamylation is catalyzed by the tubulin tyrosine ligase-like (TTLL) family of enzymes comprised of 13 members. Several family members (TTLL1, TTLL5, TTLL6, and TTLL9) have been shown to promote ciliary axonemal poly-glutamylation17. The TTLL family can be sub-divided into enzymes that catalyze the addition of an initial branch point glutamate (initiases, including TTLLs 4 and 5), those that promote assembly of long poly-glutamate chains (elongases, including TTLL1 and TTLL6), and those that possess both functions (TTLL7)18. Understanding the biological impact of microtubule (MT) poly-glutamylation has been challenging, in part due to redundancy within the TTLL family and species- and tissue-specific roles for glutamylation. For example, depletion of Ttll1 in mice leads to shortened sperm flagella but normal respiratory cilia19–21. Further, depletion of another family member, TTLL5, in human RPE1 cells or mice provoked cilium disassembly and sperm motility defects, respectively12,22.

Initially, TTLL1 was thought to have both initiase and elongase activity, but more recently, mechanistic studies suggest that it functions as an elongase18. Furthermore, in contrast with all other glutamylases, TTLL1 is not active as a monomer and lacks a MT-binding domain universally found in other family members23,24, suggesting that one or more associated proteins modulate its function by directing its sub-cellular localization, by recruiting the holo-enzyme to MTs, or by allosterically activating the enzyme. To that end, biochemical purification of a mammalian tubulin poly-glutamylation complex (TPGC) two decades ago identified TTLL1 and four additional associated proteins (LRRC49/CSTPP2, NICN1, TPGS1, and TPGS2)19. More recent studies revealed a sixth TPGC subunit, C11ORF49/CSTPP1, and ablation experiments have confirmed the importance of TTLL1-associated proteins in processes that extend beyond microtubule poly-glutamylation25. For example, loss of C11ORF49/CSTPP1 or LRRC49/CSTPP2 compromised tubulin poly-glutamylation, resulting in aberrant nuclear envelope assembly and nuclear lobulation, and null cells exhibited abnormal persistence of primary cilia in growing cells25. Moreover, Tpgs1-/- mice exhibited selective loss of α-tubulin poly-glutamylation and defects in transport of synaptic vesicles, which are kinesin cargoes, resulting in impaired neuronal synaptic transmission26. Taken together, studies on TTLL1/TPGC suggest that this enzyme plays a fundamental role in regulating the function of several critical biological processes linked to a precise MT poly-glutamylation “code,” the loss of which leads to tissue-specific abnormalities.

The observation that TTLL1 stably integrates into a large multimeric complex suggests an intricate role for TPGC in precisely regulating and fine-tuning microtubule poly-glutamylation and perhaps other cellular processes, and given the pervasive links between genetic defects associated with aberrant tubulin modifications and human disease27, it is essential to understand the underlying mechanisms. In an effort to comprehensively define all aspects of the TPGC, we affinity-purified this complex and identified two additional subunits, TBC1D19 and KIAA1814, required to assemble an active holo-enzyme. Structural predictions for the TPGC holo-enzyme aligned with our experimental findings and provided entryways into understanding why TTLL1 activity requires both TBC1D19 and KIAA1814 for robust microtubule-binding, positioning of the carboxy-terminal tail of α-tubulin within its catalytic cleft, and poly-glutamylase activity. Interestingly, we observed that TBC1D19, like TTLL123, localizes to the basal body, not the cilium, and that it is required for tubulin poly-glutamylation as well as cilium assembly and maintenance, in part by regulating entry of ARL13B and INPP5E, a phosphatidyl inositol phosphatase, into the primary cilium. The ciliary membrane is uniquely decorated with distinct populations of phosphoinositides: whereas PI(4)P is distributed along the ciliary membrane, PI(4,5)P2 is restricted to the base of the organelle28, and this pattern is enforced by INPP5E ciliary entry. Since INPP5E promotes phosphoinositide homeostasis by enhancing levels of PI(4)P while reducing levels of PI(4,5)P2, a phospholipid that recruits IFT-A and the associated protein, TULP3, into the cilium28,29, the net effect of TBC1D19 loss is aberrant retention of these ciliary proteins as well as alterations in ciliary morphology and stability. Our findings provide important insights into mechanisms regulating the biological activity of TPGC, a major cellular tubulin poly-glutamylase, and the role of this complex in ciliogenesis.

Results and Discussion

Identification of TBC1D19 and KIAA1841 as integral components of the TPGC holo-enzyme

Our previous studies revealed that ablation of two TPGC-associated proteins, C11ORF49/CSTPP1 or LRRC49/CSTPP2, significantly reduced overall levels of microtubule poly-glutamylation, but neither protein proved essential for this activity25. However, when we attempted to assemble the entire complex through simultaneous expression of all six known TPGC subunits (C11ORF49/CSTPP1, LRRC49/CSTPP2, TTLL1, TPGS1, TPGS2, and NICN1) in mammalian cells, we were unable to reconstitute poly-glutamylase activity. These findings prompted us to search for additional TPGC-associated factors required to assemble and/or activate the enzyme. The BioGRID repository revealed two candidates (TBC1D19 and KIAA1841/SANBR), which had been identified in proteome-wide affinity capture screens with multiple TPGC subunits30, although these proteins remain uncharacterized (TBC1D19) or partially characterized (KIAA1841/SANBR)31. TBC1D19 has a Tre2-Bub2-Cdc16 (TBC) domain, a motif found in known RAB-specific GTPase-activating proteins (GAPs)32. We generated a stable human RPE1 cell line expressing GFP-TBC1D19 protein, purified proteins associated with the fusion protein through GFP-trap chromatography, and performed mass spectrometric sequencing, which captured all known subunits of TPGC, except for TPGS2, as well as KIAA1841/SANBR (Fig. 1a). We also confirmed these interactions through co-immunoprecipitation with GFP-TBC1D19 and immuno-blotting and found that KIAA1841/SANBR was indeed a stable member of this complex (Fig. 1b and S1a). KIAA1841/SANBR, a protein implicated in class switch recombination in B cells31, was previously identified in high-throughput affinity-capture studies as an interacting partner of multiple TPGC components, including TTLL1, NICN1, C11ORF49/CSTPP1, LRRC49/CSTPP2, and TPGS130. These studies suggested that TBC1D19 and KIAA1841/SANBR are bona fide components of the TPGC complex. Structural predictions for the TPGC holo-enzyme indicate a core and regulatory “arm”

We used AlphaFold3 to predict high-confidence inter-molecular interactions within holo-TPGC and TPGC sub-complexes33, in an effort to begin mechanistically dissecting the function of additional, uncharacterized subunits within the holoenzyme. Cryo-EM studies have elucidated the structures of several TTLL enzymes, including TTLL7, which docks to the body of α-tubulin through its microtubule-binding domain (MBD), allowing the enzyme to preferentially bind and glutamylate the carboxy-terminal tail of β-tubulin24, and, as anticipated, these interactions were observed using AlphaFold3 (Fig. S1b). Although all resulting structures are predictive in nature, they prompted us to use AlphaFold3 to make structural predictions regarding holo-TPGC, and we were able to predict high-confidence structures able to accommodate all eight subunits of the complex (Fig. S1c). This approach led to another prediction: when we attempted to assemble the holo-enzyme without TBC1D19, nearly all high-confidence inter-protein interactions were nullified (see below), suggesting that the protein is an essential assembly factor (Fig. S1d).

TTLL1 is not active as a monomer and does not have a recognizable microtubule-binding domain23,24. Further, TTLL family members preferentially poly-glutamylate microtubules rather than tubulin monomers23. To explore the structural bases for these observations, we used AlphaFold3 to predict interactions between holo-TPGC or TTLL1 alone and tubulin dimers (Figs. S1e and S2a, b). Structural predictions for TTLL1 alone with tubulin dimers failed to yield high-confidence interactions, and indeed, all such predictions indicated that the α-tubulin carboxy-terminal tail was located far from the active site of TTLL1 (Fig. S2b). In contrast, when we docked holo-TPGC onto tubulin dimers, we predicted that TTLL1 would form extensive contacts with α-tubulin, but not β-tubulin (Figs. S1e and S2a, b), consistent with the observation that this glutamylase preferentially modifies the α-tubulin carboxy-terminal tail34, unlike TTLL7 (Figure S1b). Although tubulin carboxy-terminal tails are thought to be intrinsically disordered35—which would pose a challenge for AlphaFold—we nevertheless obtained structural predictions that the α-tubulin tail can dock within the catalytic cleft of TTLL1 when assembled into holo-TPGC (Fig. S2a, b and supplementary Movie 3). In contrast, AlphaFold predicted that the carboxy-terminal tail of β-tubulin was unable to engage the active site cleft of TTLL1, further suggesting a mechanism whereby the enzyme preferentially modifies α-tubulin. Since TTLL1 alone was unable to dock with the tubulin dimer, our predictions suggest that binding to other TPGC subunits, or assembly of holo-TPGC, is required to position the α-tubulin tail within the active site of TTLL1.

Our structural predictions for TTLL1 after assembly into TPGC recapitulated the crystallographic data for TTLL4 as well as homology-based predictions generated for TTLL5, TTLL6, and TTLL7, with respect to the conserved active site and β-sheet motifs near the catalytic cleft of the enzyme (Fig. S2a)35. Notably, although each of these family members shared homology within their active sites, we were unable to obtain high-confidence structural predictions for holo-TPGC when we substituted TTLL6 for TTLL1, indicating that residues in TTLL1 outside the catalytic cleft were essential for its interactions with other subunits within holo-TPGC. These observations further attest to the ability of AlphaFold3 to predict holo-TPGC structure.

Next, we investigated a role, if any, for subunits within holo-TPGC as regulators of the interaction between TTLL1 and the C-terminal tail of α-tubulin. To gain insights into the role of individual proteins within holo-TPGC when docked to a tubulin protomer, we predicted the impact of removing one subunit at a time using a “leave-one-out” strategy. This approach showed that TBC1D19 formed extensive interactions with TTLL1, and the docking of holo-TPGC and tubulin dimers required TBC1D19, TTLL1, and KIAA1841 (Figs. S1c–e and S2b). Omission of either KIAA1841 or TBC1D19 led to predictions wherein the α-tubulin carboxy-terminal tail was unable to dock with TTLL1, and indeed, the tail was again predicted to be located at a substantial distance from TTLL1 (Fig. S2b). Our structural predictions indicate that both TBC1D19 and KIAA1841 are required for TTLL1 to properly dock to α-tubulin. In contrast, omission of either LRRC49/CSTPP2 or C11ORF49/CSTPP1 was not predicted to abrogate TTLL1 docking to the α-tubulin tail, indicating that these proteins may not be required to promote interactions between TTLL1 and α-tubulin (Fig. S2b).

Thus, structural predictions indicated that omission of TBC1D19 would lead to the collapse of holo-TPGC and abrogate docking of the α-tubulin tail to TTLL1. In contrast, C11ORF49/CSTPP1 and LRRC49/CSTPP2 were predicted to interact with one another to form a module peripheral to a “core” complex composed of the remaining subunits. Importantly, these predictions agree with our analysis of C11ORF49/CSTPP1 and LRRC49/CSTPP2 knock-out cells, wherein ablation of C11ORF49/CSTPP1 or LRRC49/CSTPP2 had a reciprocal and significant impact on the stability of each other but had a muted effect on TPGS1 and TPGS225. Interestingly, loss of C11ORF49/CSTPP1 or LRRC49/CSTPP2 led to decreased stability of TTLL1, potentially explaining the diminution of poly-glutamylated microtubules in these knockout lines25. Further, omission of either LRRC49/CSTPP2 or C11ORF49/CSTPP1 moderately altered the positioning of the catalytic cleft with respect to the α-tubulin tail in our predictive structures (Fig. S2b), but their removal did not predict the displacement of the tail from the active site, in contrast with the loss of TBC1D19, suggesting a plausible explanation for the degree to which poly-glutamylation (poly-E) is affected in the respective knockouts (see below)25.

With the exception of LRRC49/CSTPP2, all other TPGC components, including TBC1D19 and KIAA1841, are well-conserved in vertebrates, including zebrafish (Fig. S2c–e), allowing us to predict the structure of the orthologous complex. Interestingly, despite the lack of a zebrafish LRRC49/CSTPP2 ortholog and 61% amino acid identity overall (when averaged across all seven remaining TPGC subunits), intra- and inter-molecular interaction maps for human and zebrafish holo-TPGC strongly resembled one another, suggesting both structural and functional conservation (compare Figs. S2f and S1c). Thus, our structural predictions regarding human holo-TPGC mirrored those of the zebrafish complex.

Reconstitution of holo-TPGC poly-glutamylase activity

Next, we asked whether we could reconstitute the TPGC complex and catalyze glutamylation in vitro. Prior efforts to assemble an active enzyme with six subunits (C11ORF49/CSTPP1, LRRC49/CSTPP2, TTLL1, TPGS1, TPGS2, and NICN1) were unsuccessful. Here, we took advantage of 293 T cells, since expression of proteins in these cells is used extensively in this field to facilitate the capture of weaker/lower affinity protein-protein interactions and to promote assembly of multi-subunit complexes. We expressed all eight subunits in 293 T cells and analyzed cellular glutamylation. We were able to reconstitute robust TPGC glutamylation activity through simultaneous expression of all eight subunits, including TBC1D19 and KIAA1841 (Fig. 1c). Omission of C11ORF49/CSTPP1 or LRRC49/CSTPP2 had little or no impact on activity in this system. Importantly, however, we observed significant diminution of glutamylation activity when we omitted either TBC1D19 or KIAA1841. Moreover, TTLL1 and two proteins (TPGS2 and NICN1) neighboring TBC1D19 in our predicted structures were destabilized when all subunits except TBC1D19 were expressed (Fig. S1c and S3a), consistent with a role for TBC1D19 in the stabilization of TPGC subunits and/or holo-enzyme assembly and integrity. Further, we observed that TPGS1 was destabilized when C11ORF49/CSTPP1 was omitted (Fig. S3a), consistent with their proximity and interactions within TPGC in predicted structures (Fig. S1c, e). Overall, our TPGC reconstitution experiments support our predictions regarding the importance of TBC1D19 in holo-enzyme assembly and stimulation of glutamylation activity. These reconstitution studies are also consistent with our structural predictions of TPGC that indicate an indispensable role for TBC1D19 and that provide insights into why TTLL1 alone cannot independently bind and glutamylate MTs.

TBC1D19 is required for cilium assembly and stability

Given the pivotal role of TBC1D19 as an essential assembly factor for the TPGC, we focused all subsequent studies on elucidating its function. First, we examined its localization using our stable GFP-TBC1D19 RPE1 cell line and found that the protein was enriched at centriolar satellites, electron-dense structures surrounding the centrosome that contribute to cilium assembly, consistent with studies on TPGS1, C11ORF49/CSTPP1, and LRRC49/CSTPP2 (Fig. 2a and S3b)25. Further, when cells were induced to ciliate, TBC1D19 localized to a region proximal to the basal body, and a portion of this protein co-localized with basal body markers (Fig. 2b). To elucidate the role of TBC1D19 in poly-glutamylation and ciliogenesis, we used CRISPR to knock out the gene in human RPE1 cells. Since we predicted that TBC1D19 would have an impact on TTLL1 function, we also ablated TTLL1 and compared the resulting phenotypes in both knockouts with C11ORF49/CSTPP1 and LRRC49/CSTPP2 null RPE1 cells (Fig. S4a, b)25. Consistent with our predictions and holo-enzyme reconstitution studies, we found that ablation of TBC1D19 resulted in the near-disappearance of multiple TPGC subunits and reductions in tubulin poly-glutamylation in growing and serum-starved cells (Fig. 2c). We were able to restore poly-glutamylation and native levels of expression for each of these proteins upon expression of TBC1D19 in knockout cells, confirming that their loss resulted specifically from ablation of this protein (Fig. 2d). Proper localization of C11ORF49/CSTPP1 was not dependent upon TBC1D19, and likewise, TBC1D19 was not impacted by loss of TTLL1 (Fig. S4c), consistent with predictions in which these TPGC subunits can independently localize to centriolar satellites, perhaps through an association with PCM1, which could serve as a platform to stabilize or promote complex assembly25.

Interestingly, in contrast with LRRC49/CSTPP2 and C11ORF49/CSTPP1 knock-out cells, we did not observe nuclear lobulation in TBC1D19 or TTLL1 null cells. Further, LRRC49/CSTPP2 and C11ORF49/CSTPP1 knock-outs exhibit persistent ciliation. We therefore deprived TBC1D19-/- cells of serum and visualized multiple ciliary markers to examine primary cilium assembly. Analysis of these markers indicated that TBC1D19 loss strongly reduced ciliation, and cilia that assembled were short and stunted (Fig. 3a, b and S4d). We visualized ciliary axonemes using an antibody, GT335, which detects branch-point glutamylation of α-tubulin, the first step needed for subsequent addition of poly-glutamate chains. Ablation of TBC1D19 drastically reduced (by 3-fold) the percentage of GT335-positive cilia, as compared to the control (Fig. 3a), although total levels of this modified tubulin were not affected (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, the percentage of cells with acetylated tubulin (Ac-tubulin)-positive cilia was drastically reduced in the TBC1D19 knockout (Fig. 3a), again consistent with loss of ciliation. Detection of a third, well-established ciliary marker, ARL13B, revealed a more significant reduction in ciliation (~12-fold), and, importantly, localization of ARL13B was altered even in cells that assembled GT335-positive cilia (Fig. 3a, c). Since total ARL13B levels were not affected (Fig. S4e), our results suggest an intrinsic defect in ARL13B ciliary localization. Loss of primary cilia could not be ascribed to defects in cell cycle progression or an inability to enter a quiescent state (Fig. S4f). We expressed GFP-TBC1D19 in knock-out cells and rescued cilium assembly, deduced by staining Ac-tubulin and ARL13B, confirming that ciliation defects stemmed from loss of TBC1D19 directly (Fig. S4g, h). Our data suggest that TBC1D19 is essential for assembly of primary cilia, and those cells able to bypass this requirement assemble aberrant or unstable cilia, consistent with the loss of tubulin acetylation and glutamylation, modifications associated with the stabilization of microtubules10–12,36. Our results also indicate that TBC1D19, TTLL1, and C11ORF49/CSTPP1 play distinct roles, and although each protein is stably associated with TPGC, the loss of each differentially impacts ciliation.

TBC1D19 null cells fail to assemble structurally normal cilia

Since few cilia assemble in the TBC1D19 knock-out, and ARL13B was visualized at the base of cilia rather than axonemes (Fig. 3c), our data suggested that ciliation arrests at an early stage, in accordance with other studies showing that ARL13B is required to promote cilium extension and maturation12,37. In an effort to further pinpoint the mechanistic basis for defective cilium assembly in TBC1D19-/- cells, we examined a cohort of proteins associated with each step in the process (Fig. 3b–f). In one of the earliest steps of ciliogenesis, the mother centriole/basal body recruits a ciliary vesicle to the distal appendages8. We found that distal appendage proteins that we examined (CEP89 and CEP164) were properly recruited (Fig. 3b). Likewise, proteins necessary for assembly of the ciliary vesicle, including Rab8, Rabin 8, and Myo-Va, were properly recruited to basal bodies (Fig. 3b). Another subsequent, signature event, CP110 destruction at mother centrioles, also occurred normally (Fig. 3d)9. Thus, the earliest events known to occur during intracellular ciliogenesis, namely, maturation of the basal body, recruitment of ciliary vesicles, and destruction of CP110, occurred normally in TBC1D19 knock-out cells. Since the majority of TBC1D19 null cells do not assemble a mature cilium, we also investigated recruitment of Rab34, which localizes to the ciliary vesicle and regulates early events in ciliation38–41, and assembly of the transition zone, a regulatory gate needed to transport proteins into the cilium. Interestingly, although Rab34 was faithfully recruited to ciliary vesicles after serum starvation of TBC1D19 knock-out cells, this protein aberrantly accumulated at basal bodies and failed to re-distribute from this location during cilium assembly, unlike control or TTLL1 KO cells, in which Rab34 appears in the nascent cilium and disappears as ciliation progresses (compare Fig. 3e and S6a, b)40,41. We also examined recruitment of proteins (CEP290, TCTN1, TMEM67, and RPGRIP1L) to the transition zone, since this structure assembles after ciliary vesicle docking and is required to transport ARL13B to the cilium and maintain it within this location42–45. We found that each of these transition zone proteins was recruited to basal bodies upon serum deprivation of TBC1D19 knock-out cells (Fig. 3f). Upon inspection of knock-out cells, however, we observed a marked reduction of Tectonic 1 (TCTN1) in TBC1D19 null as well as TTLL1 knockout cells, without changes in another component, TMEM67 (Fig. 3f and S6c). Since ARL13B recruitment is normal in TTLL1 null cells (Fig. S5c), TCTN1 loss may not explain the diminished entry of ARL13B into cilia of TBC1D19 knockout cells.

Altogether, our results are consistent with the conclusion that TBC1D19 null cells fail to complete all of the steps associated with ciliary axoneme extension and/or maturation of the axoneme or ciliary membrane. To further investigate possible structural changes in TBC1D19-/- cilia, we performed transmission electron microscopy (TEM) on knock-out cells after serum deprivation. Consistent with our immunofluorescence studies, considerably fewer cilia were observed in TBC1D19-/- cells as compared to control (sgCTL) and C11ORF49/CSTPP1-/- cells, which assembled mature primary cilia, as expected (Fig. 3g and S6d). We found that TBC1D19-/- cilia exhibited several notable structural abnormalities. First, knock-out cells frequently assembled short, abnormally formed nascent cilia with aberrantly shaped ciliary vesicles or more mature, fully formed cilia with extensive vesiculation or membrane blebbing (Fig. 3g and S6e). Upon examination of contiguous sections, we found that portions of TBC1D19-/- cilia frequently “disappeared” in one section and reappeared in an adjacent one, in contrast with control cilia that were generally visible in a single plane of focus (Fig. 3g). We conclude that TBC1D19 knock-out cilia exhibited significant curvature from the base to distal tip. Most notably, TBC1D19-/- cilia also had membrane abnormalities that were not observed in control cilia, namely, whereas control cilia were enveloped with a uniform, smooth membrane with a symmetrical ciliary pocket, TBC1D19-/- cilia frequently exhibited enlarged, asymmetric ciliary vesicles, pockets, and membranes. The diameters of TBC1D19-/- ciliary axonemes were highly variable, and the distal ends were either abnormally bulbous or tapered (Fig. 3g, S6e, and supplementary Movies 1 and 2). TBC1D19-/- ciliary membranes were associated with vesicular elements at the proximal end and distal tip, and defects at distal ends were reminiscent of cilia derived from cells of ciliopathy patients with INPP5E mutations46,47. Our results are consistent with the conclusion that the majority of TBC1D19 knock-out cells arrest before the growth of a ciliary axoneme, and the population of cells able to bypass this step assemble morphologically abnormal cilia with severe membrane defects.

TPGC subunits fine-tune microtubule poly-glutamylation and acetylation

Previous work has led to seemingly conflicting conclusions regarding the role of tubulin glutamylation in ciliogenesis and cilium maintenance. Indeed, two tubulin de-glutamylating enzymes, CCP1 (also named AGTPBP1) and the related protein, CCP5/AGBL5, were shown to function either as positive or negative regulators of cilium assembly, respectively48,49. Further, forced removal of glutamylation from microtubules specifically within the axoneme (through expression of the de-glutamylase, CCP5) severely compromised elongation of immature cilia and delayed anterograde, but not retrograde, IFT11. Improper levels of glutamylation, provoked by loss of either CCP1 or CCP5, led to destabilization of the distal portion of the axoneme50. These observations suggest that tubulin poly-glutamylation is subject to exquisite fine-tuning, and imbalances can result in diverse ciliation defects. We therefore assessed the ability of TPGC subunits to promote tubulin modifications within the centrosome, basal body, and axoneme in cells before and after induction of ciliation. Using our panel of knock-out cell lines, we examined the impact of ablating four different TPGC subunits (TBC1D19, TTLL1, C11ORF49/CSTPP1, and LRRC49/CSTPP2). Consistent with previous results25, ablation of C11ORF49/CSTPP1 or LRRC49/CSTPP2 triggered a modest decrease in poly-glutamylation at centrioles and ciliary axonemes (Fig. 4a, b). In contrast, TBC1D19 and TTLL1 knock-out cells exhibited near-complete loss of axonemal and centriolar poly-glutamylation, which could be rescued by re-expression of the corresponding cDNA (Fig. 4a, b and S7). Microtubule modifications are thought to be coordinated through a “tubulin code,” although much remains unknown about crosstalk between modifications27. We therefore examined other tubulin modifications, including tubulin de-tyrosination. We found that cellular tubulin de-tyrosination levels were significantly reduced in both TBC1D19-/- and TTLL1-/- but not C11ORF49/CSTPP1-/- cells (Fig. S8a). These findings suggest that residual levels of poly-glutamylation in the latter knock-out may be sufficient to trigger de-tyrosination, whereas a more substantial reduction in poly-glutamylation in TBC1D19-/- and TTLL1-/- cells is required to reveal de-tyrosination defects. Biochemical studies have suggested that microtubule de-tyrosination is stimulated by poly-glutamylation in vitro51, and our findings confirm this link in living cells. Interestingly, we also found that the percentage of cilia (deduced by scoring for IFT88-positivity) marked by Ac-tubulin was significantly reduced in TBC1D19 null cells but not in cells depleted of TTLL1 or C11ORF49/CSTPP1 (Fig. 4c and S8a)25. Expression of TBC1D19 in null cells rescued this defect, confirming that the phenotype resulted from loss of this gene (Fig. S8b). In an effort to link axonemal acetylation with ciliary phenotypes, we treated cells with tubacin to inhibit the tubulin de-acetylase, HDAC6, but this treatment did not rescue axonemal acetylation or ciliation (Fig. S8c, d), suggesting that this defect did not stem from aberrant removal of the modification but rather the inability to instigate acetylation in knock-out cells in the first place. Earlier studies have demonstrated a relationship between acetylated-tubulin and microtubule detyrosination52. It is possible that the reduction in each of these modifications is linked to the loss of glutamylation, although future studies will be required to further investigate underlying mechanisms.

We used three approaches in an effort to rescue glutamylation in the TBC1D19 null cells. First, since axonemal glutamylation and de-glutamylation are in equilibrium at steady-state, we asked whether tubulin modifications and ciliation can be restored in knock-out cells after depletion of CCP1, a tubulin de-glutamylase, in TBC1D19 null cells. Depletion of CCP1 enabled partial restoration of tubulin de-tyrosination and ac-tubulin in TBC1D19 null cells (Fig. S8e), concomitant with increased ciliation (Fig. 4d). Interestingly, however, CCP1 depletion did not rescue ARL13B entry into cilia, suggesting that ARL13B transport requires additional mechanisms associated with TBC1D19/TPGC (Fig. 4e; see below). Our studies suggest that these three modifications may be jointly regulated to help stabilize microtubules, but cross-talk may involve additional regulatory steps. Second, we artificially targeted TTLL1 to microtubules by fusing the enzyme to the MAP4 microtubule-binding domain in an effort to restore glutamylation in TBC1D19 knockout cells. However, expression of this fusion protein was unable to rescue either cellular glutamylation or ciliation in TBC1D19 null cells (Fig. 4f and S8f). This result suggests that targeting TTLL1 to microtubules is not sufficient to restore glutamylation when TBC1D19 has been ablated and TPGC has been compromised. Third, we expressed two monomeric glutamylases, TTLL5 and TTLL6, in TBC1D19 null cells in an effort to rescue glutamylation. TTLL5 is an initiase, adding the branch-point glutamate, whereas TTLL6 is an elongase. Although we succeeded in rescuing cellular poly-glutamylation levels in TBC1D19 knock out cells through enforced expression of TTLL6 (Fig. S8g), neither TTLL5 nor TTLL6 expression rescued ciliation in TBC1D19 null cells (Fig. 4g). This result suggests that TPGC-mediated glutamylation and perhaps other functions associated with this complex, including other α-tubulin tail modifications distinct from those deposited by TTLL5/6 (see discussion below), are critical for assembly and/or maintenance of primary cilia.

Collectively, these experiments make several important points. First, we conclude that TPGC uniquely regulates both ciliation and MT poly-glutamylation and that TTLL family members are unable to compensate for loss of TPGC function. Second, since recruitment of TTLL1 to MT is not, by itself, sufficient to restore either glutamylation or ciliogenesis, TPGC components play additional roles beyond MT recruitment. The latter finding comports well with our structural predictions and subsequent experimental data described below. Third, TBC1D19/TPGC regulates ARL13B localization to the cilium, and this step involves a mechanism that is distinct from those that control glutamylation and cilium assembly.

TBC1D19 is essential for INPP5E ciliary entry, phosphoinositide homeostasis, and cilium stability

Our EM analysis revealed prominent ciliary defects in TBC1D19-/- cells, most notably, severe ciliary membrane perturbations that could be associated with diminished stability, and we sought a mechanistic explanation for these observations. Phosphoinositides have been shown to play a key role in cilium assembly, stability, and function, and they partition uniquely to distinct portions of the basal body, transition zone, and cilium. During cilium assembly, PI(4)P localizes to the cilium, whereas PI(4,5)P2 localizes in a cell type-dependent manner to the transition zone (RPE1 cells) or the proximal cilium (3T3/IMCD3 cells)28,53. INPP5E, a phosphatidylinositol poly-phosphate 5-phosphatase, is a principal mediator of phosphoinositide homeostasis within the cilium, enhancing levels of PI(4)P while maintaining low levels of inositol phosphates, PI(4,5)P2 and PI(3,4,5)P328,46,47,54,55. Loss of INPP5E was shown to affect cilium stability but not its assembly46,47. In other studies, however, INPP5E was shown to be required for axoneme assembly, and displacement of the enzyme during growth induction was associated with cilium decapitation55,56. Interestingly, INPP5E ciliary localization was significantly reduced in ciliated TBC1D19 knock-out cells, and this phenotype can be rescued through re-expression of TBC1D19 (Fig. 5a and S9a). Loss of INPP5E localization could not be explained by changes in total protein levels (Fig. S4e). Concordantly, we found that PI(4)P was depleted from cilia, whereas PI(4,5)P2 accumulated at a region encompassing the basal body or transition zone in TBC1D19-/- cells (Fig. 5b, c and S9b, c). In contrast, ciliary INPP5E localization in TTLL1, LRRC49/CSTPP2, and C11ORF49/CSTPP1 knockout cells were not altered (Fig. 5a and S9d). We asked whether INPP5E localization could be rescued in TBC1D19 knockout cells through CCP1 depletion, but ablation of this protein did not restore its ciliary compartmentalization (Fig. S9e). Since INPP5E plays a demonstrable role in cilium stability, we compared axonemal stability in quiescent control and knock-out cells after short-term serum stimulation. We found that primary cilia in TBC1D19 knock-out cells were substantially less stable, and within 30 minutes of serum addition, ~40% of cilia had disassembled, as compared to <10% of wild-type cilia (Fig. 5d, e). In contrast, cilium disassembly was induced in TTLL1-/- cells with kinetics indistinguishable from controls (Fig. S9f). Thus, loss of axonemal poly-glutamylation per se cannot explain this de-stabilization. To test the hypothesis that loss of INPP5E could account for ciliary defects observed in TBC1D19 knockout cells, we treated cells with inhibitors of kinases that convert phosphatidyl inositol PI(4)P to PI(4,5)P2 and PI(3,4,5)P3, namely, PIP5K1C (phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate 5-kinase γ) and PI3K, respectively57,58. Treatment of cells with an inhibitor of PIP5K1C (UNC3230), but not PI3K (LY294002), markedly increased ciliation in TBC1D19 knockout cells (Fig. 5f). On the other hand, treatment of cells with ISA-2011B59, an inhibitor of the related kinase, PIP5K1A (phosphatidylinositol-4-phosphate 5-kinase α), had no impact (Fig. 5f). Accordingly, treatment of TBC1D19 knockout cells with the PIP5K1C inhibitor rescued the loss of ciliary PI(4)P and accumulation of PI(4,5)P2 at the basal body and/or transition zone (Fig. 5b, c and S9b, c). These findings are consistent with the observation that PIP5K1C, but not PIP5K1A, localizes to basal bodies60. Collectively, our data suggest that loss of INPP5E targeting to cilia abolishes phosphoinositide balance, in part explaining membrane defects in TBC1D19 knockout cells, and the resulting ciliation phenotype can be rescued by counteracting the activity of an opposing enzyme (PIP5K1C) to restore homeostasis.

TBC1D19 and ARL13B transiently localize to basal bodies during ciliogenesis

Gating of proteins by the transition zone was shown to regulate entry of both ARL13B and INPP5E into the cilium28,45. However, we did not detect diminished localization of transition zone proteins, with the exception of TCTN1, in TBC1D19-/- cells (Fig. 3f). Ciliary targeting of INPP5E also requires ARL13B63, and therefore, ARL13B trafficking defects might account for the depletion of INPP5E from cilia in TBC1D19-/- cells. We found that ARL13B and TBC1D19 co-localized at basal bodies (Fig. 7a and see below) and posited that TBC1D19 localization might be linked to transport of ARL13B into cilia. Therefore, we explored potential spatio-temporal changes in TBC1D19 localization as growing cells were induced to ciliate through serum deprivation. We visualized GFP-TBC1D19, γ-tubulin, and ARL13B, which decorates basal bodies during the earliest stages of ciliation prior to its entry into the cilium64, over a time course spanning the early events of ciliogenesis and ciliary maturation (1, 6, 12, and 24 h post-serum deprivation) and compared the pattern with growing cells (Fig. 7a). TBC1D19 localized to centriolar satellites in growing cells, as expected (Fig. 2a), and during a window within 1-6 h after serum depletion, the protein co-localized with ARL13B at the basal body. Concomitant with ciliary membrane extension, TBC1D19-positive granules condensed at the base of the cilium and partially co-localized with ARL13B. However, TBC1D19 disappeared within the next 12 h as cilia fully elongated. To test the possibility that the two proteins could transiently interact, we expressed both proteins in 293 T cells. Interestingly, ARL13B co-immunoprecipitated with GFP-TBC1D19, suggesting that these proteins can interact (Fig. 7b). Since a portion of TBC1D19 co-localizes with ARL13B at basal bodies at a stage preceding, or coinciding with, the earliest stages of cilium assembly, we propose that co-localization with ARL13B could potentiate these interactions. Alternatively, TBC1D19 might assist in recruiting or stabilizing ARL13B at the basal body or transporting the latter protein into the cilium. Although further experiments are required to determine whether TBC1D19 plays a role in modifying ARL13B activity or localization, our data suggest that TBC1D19--by itself or when assembled into TPGC or an unidentified complex--is required for ARL13B recruitment, which promotes INPP5E localization essential for stabilization and maintenance of primary cilia. In total, our findings suggest a model in which TBC1D19 and TPGC play multiple roles, to instigate tubulin poly-glutamylation and other modifications, enable recruitment of ARL13B to basal bodies/cilia, and facilitate ciliary entry of INPP5E, which, in turn, promotes cilium stability and proper membrane morphology (Fig. 7c).

Despite its discovery two decades ago19, a complete understanding of the activity and biological function of TPGC has remained elusive. In addition, although we recently identified a sixth component of this complex, C11ORF49/CSTPP125, we were nevertheless unable to reconstitute robust poly-glutamylase activity with all components, prompting us to identify two additional subunits, TBC1D19 and KIAA1841/SANBR. Here, using gene editing and reconstitution of the enzyme with all eight components, coupled with recently developed protein structure prediction approaches, we have gained insight into the function of this complex and have proposed models to begin understanding how this enzyme is regulated. Although our functional studies of KIAA1841 are ongoing, our data suggest that TBC1D19 and KIAA1841/SANBR are required for the assembly and activation of TPGC. TBC1D19, either independently or as a component of TPGC, promotes microtubule poly-glutamylation and ARL13B and INPP5E transport, and our data suggest a requirement during two stages, first, to promote cilium assembly and secondly, to maintain the structure once formed. Cells that bypass the requirement for proper localization of INPP5E and assemble cilia show severe defects in the transport of ciliary membrane proteins and membrane defects, as well as depletion of multiple tubulin modifications (glutamylation, acetylation, and de-tyrosination). This compendium of defects is likely to account for the observed organelle instability. Control of phosphoinositide homeostasis and INPP5E localization are suggestive of a multi-faceted role for TBC1D19/TPGC beyond tubulin poly-glutamylation (summarized in Fig. 7c, d). Although other poly-glutamylases, including TTLL5 and TTLL6 (and perhaps others), are likely to modify axonemal microtubules, our data conclusively show that TPGC is the primary poly-glutamylase regulating centrosomal and ciliary microtubule modifications and, as a consequence, it may be indispensable for organelle assembly and maintenance in some physiological contexts. Intriguingly, although TTLL5 and 6 have been shown to localize to basal bodies and cilia12, we have not observed transport of TPGC subunits from basal bodies into cilia, prompting the need to mechanistically explore how a complex residing within the basal body can promote axonemal modifications. It is possible that TPGC promotes the localization of ancillary factors, such as CEP41, required for the transport of TTLLs to the ciliary compartment14. Higher resolution experiments, such as proximity ligation analysis, will also more precisely define where TBC1D19 interacts with ARL13B during the early stages of ciliogenesis.

TBC1D19 knockout cells exhibited phenotypes that were not observed in TTLL1 null cells, suggestive of additional roles in ciliation (Fig. 7c, d). It will be interesting to compare the impact of ablating KIAA1841 to determine how its function relates to that of other TPGC subunits. Taken together with our previous observations regarding phenotypes in C11ORF49/CSTPP1 and LRRC49/CSTPP2 knockout cells25, we conclude that TPGC subunits exhibit diversification of function (Fig. 7d). It is attractive to speculate that specific subunits (including TBC1D19 and KIAA1841) are essential for assembly of the complex and catalysis, whereas others (C11ORF49/CSTPP1 and LRRC49/CSTPP2) have a more restricted role in fine-tuning enzymatic function. In addition to defects in poly-glutamylation, we found that other tubulin modifications, including acetylation and de-tyrosination, were also compromised as a result of TBC1D19 loss. These observations are reminiscent of defects associated with patient mutations in two genes, ARMC9 and TOGARAM1, linked to the neurodevelopmental ciliopathy, Joubert Syndrome, namely, shortened cilia and decreased axonemal acetylation and poly-glutamylation without disruption of transition zone function16. Furthermore, mutations in INPP5E have also been linked to Joubert Syndrome46,47. Thus, it will be interesting to explore links between the additional TPGC subunits we have identified and human disease using patient ciliopathy databases. Our co-localization and biochemical studies suggest that TBC1D19 is required for transport of ARL13B, a small GTPase, to cilia. Future experiments will be required to determine whether TBC1D19 functions as a GAP, and if so, this would necessitate discovery of its targets. It will also be essential to elucidate detailed mechanisms linking TBC1D19 to the localization of INPP5E and ARL13B.

There are several limitations associated with our studies. First and foremost, although our predictive structures have guided our study of the architecture, activity, and regulation of the TPGC complex, these predictions will need to be tested rigorously with structure-based studies, such as cryo-EM. This will necessitate purification of TPGC after assembly with all eight recombinant proteins. Currently, we are unable to assess whether the TPGC binds MTs as a monomer or multimer, and while we are able to dock a tubulin dimer onto the holo-TPGC complex, we have not predicted the structure of holo-TPGC with multiple protomers or proto-filaments. Further, given that TPGC is an elongase, verification of our predictive structures will require co-crystals of TTLL enzymes and the carboxy-terminal tail of α-tubulin modified with the branch-point glutamate, which do not currently exist. Currently, it is unknown which residues of the α-tubulin tail are modified by TPGC and whether subunits within this complex acquire their distinct activities by directing the poly-glutamylation of specific tail residues. It will be important to determine if and how these MT modifications differ from those directed by other TTLL family members, including TTLL6. We note that Tpgs1 null cells lack α-tubulin glutamylation, although others have shown that β-tubulin glutamylation may also be affected in mouse tissues lacking Ttll117,19,20,26. It will be interesting to mutate residues within TTLL1 and TPGC based on AlphaFold3 and test how α- and β-tubulin glutamylation is affected in complexes reconstituted with mutant enzymes.

Methods

Cell culture and generation of knock-out cells

hTERT-RPE1 and HEK 293 T cells were obtained from ATCC and were grown in DMEM/F12 or DMEM medium, respectively, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Cells were grown in DMEM/F12 without FBS for 24 h or 48 h for cilia assembly, as indicated. To construct knock-out (KO) cells, RPE1 cells were infected with Flag-Cas9 and sgRNA expressing lentivirus and propagated for 10 days. Next, cells were seeded in 96-well dishes for single cell isolation, and colonies were analyzed for genome editing. sgRNAs included sgCTL (5’-GAGACGTCTAGCACGTCTCT-3’), sgTBC1D19 (5’-GAGTATCCCACTGGCACGAAA-3’), and sgTTLL1 (5’CCTTCCGGGTCAGTTCATAG-3′). C11ORF49/CSTPP1 and LRRC49/CSTPP2 KO cells have been described previously25.

Transfection and lentiviral infection

Polyethylenimine (PEI) was used for transfection of HEK293T cells at a DNA:PEI ratio of 1:3. HEK293T cells were incubated for 48 hours prior to collection for protein extraction and immunoprecipitation. Lentivirus was generated in HEK293T cells by co-transfection of the transfer plasmid, Δ8.2 envelope, and VSV-G packaging plasmid using PEI. Lentiviral supernatant was collected 48-72 hours after transfection. RPE1 cells were infected with virus supernatant and 8 or 16 μg/ml Polybrene at a 1:1 ratio. Infected cells underwent selection using Geneticin (G418) at 500 μg/ml to 1 mg/ml. Lipofectamine RNAiMax (Invitrogen; 13778075) was used for siRNA transfection of RPE1 cells.

Electron microscopy

Cells were plated on 35 mm glass-bottom dishes (P35G-1.5-14-C, MatTek) and fixed with solution containing 2% paraformaldehyde, 2.5% glutaraldehyde, 2 mM CaCl2 in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2) at 37 °C for 5 min, then transferred to fresh fixative and fixed overnight at 4 °C. The cells were washed three times, 10 min each, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide and 1% potassium ferrocyanide for 1.5 h on ice, then en block stained with 0.25% uranyl acetate aqueous solution overnight at 4 °C in the dark. The cells were then washed with ddH2O, dehydrated in serially in 30%, 50%, and 70% ethanol on ice for 10 min each, transferring to room temperature, and incubating 10 min each with 85%, 90%, and 100% ethanol. Finally, cells were infiltrated and en face embedded in Araldilte 502 (Electron Microscopy Sciences). The sample blocks were removed by immersing the dish in liquid nitrogen and trimming under stereoscope. Serial thin (100 nm) sections were cut using a Leica UC6 ultramicrotome, collected on formvar-coated slotted copper grids, and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Stained grids were examined with a JEOL1400 Flash transmission electron microscope (Japan) and photographed with a Gatan Rio 16 camera (Gatan Inc., Pleasanton, CA).

Mass spectrometric analysis

Whole-cell extracts were prepared from stable RPE1 cells expressing GFP (control) or GFP-TBC1D19 using ELB buffer as described above, and approximately 50 mg of protein were immuno-purified using GFP-trap agarose chromatography. Mass spectrometric (MS) analysis was performed after tryptic digestion with MS-grade TPCK trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI), and one-third of the supernatant was analyzed per MS run, as previously described25. LC separation was performed online with an EVOSEP1112 column, and peptides were gradient eluted onto a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) using a 44 min gradient. N = 2 independent experiments.

Flow cytometry and cell cycle analysis

Cells were trypsinized, collected and resuspended in cold 1x PBS. Cells were centrifuged at 110 x g for 5 min at 4 °C. Cells were then resuspended in 1 ml of 70% ethanol and incubated on ice for 30 min, pelleted, and resuspended in 500 μl of 1x PBS/0.1 mg/ml RNAse A (Sigma R5503), and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells were then incubated in 250 μL 1x PBS containing 0.5 mg/ml propidium iodide (Sigma P4170) for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. Cells were then pelleted and resuspended in 2% FBS/1x PBS and filtered through a 0.35 μm cell strainer into a FACS tube (Falcon 352235). Cells were analyzed with a BD Biosciences LSR Fortessa flow cytometer. PI was measured using the PE-Texas Red channel. Data was analyzed using FlowJo_v10 software using the cell cycle analysis tool.

Statistics
