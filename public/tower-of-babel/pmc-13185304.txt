Engaging patients throughout the continuum of implementation science and practice: adapted evidence mapping review and practical guide for clinicians and researchers
Authors: Lauren Steeves, Srinivas Sampalli, Robin Urquhart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe role of patients in healthcare is dramatically changing. Patients have increased autonomy and responsibility for managing care interventions that influence their morbidity, mortality, and quality of life. Engagement of patients to meaningfully participate in health research, care, and decision-making is limited, negatively impacting diverse outcomes. Implementation science (IS) and patient engagement (PE) strive to improve patient care and outcomes. Integration of IS and PE may aid researchers/healthcare teams in engaging patients and improving outcomes. There is a paucity of literature and guidance to support integration of IS and PE. The aims of this research are to (1) present an overview of select IS theories/frameworks, strategies, outcomes, and reporting guidelines that may facilitate PE and (2) present a framework outlining opportunities within the IS research process to integrate PE approaches.Main textThere are common theoretical constructs, identified across IS theories/frameworks, that may facilitate PE. Examples of such constructs include “engagement” and “patient/target audience characteristics and needs”. In addition, 6 of the top 10 most cited IS theories/frameworks include more than one (> 3) of these constructs. Evidence-informed implementation strategies are indexed and categorized into thematic clusters in the published results of the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) project. Examples of implementation strategies that may facilitate PE include “conduct educational meetings” and “prepare patients/consumers to be active participants”. Proctor et al. propose a working taxonomy of implementation outcomes for IS research. Implementation outcomes that may facilitate measurement of PE-relevant outcomes include satisfaction, function, and symptomatology. There are no reporting guidelines that include both IS and PE research reporting recommendations. IS reporting guidelines such as Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies (StaRI) may be extended by combining them with existing PE-reporting guidelines such as the Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public (GRIPP) 2 reporting checklist.ConclusionsThere are IS theories/frameworks, strategies, and outcomes that may facilitate PE. PE-informed selection of IS methodologies is critical to improving patient outcomes by optimizing the patient’s role in research, care, and health system decision-making. This article proposes a framework outlining important stages of IS research at which PE strategies, outcomes, and reporting guidelines may be suitable to integrate.

Background

The scientific areas of implementation science (IS) and patient engagement (PE) are highly aligned: Both strive to improve care and health outcomes, and enhance healthcare systems to best serve their communities [1, 2]. Despite this alignment, there is a paucity of literature elucidating how integration of these two rapidly evolving areas may be achieved to further advance the reach and impact of both.

Implementation science seeks to optimize health resource utilization and patient care outcomes through timely, effective, and sustained integration of evidence-based interventions into practice [2]. IS accomplishes its aims through the use of validated theories, models, frameworks and approaches (so-called IS tools) that support the identification and application of targeted strategies and evaluation through measurement of predefined implementation outcomes [2]. The success of implementation interventions is influenced by the involved individuals’ and teams’ capability, opportunity, motivation and behaviour as they relate to the changes required of them to implement and maintain the intervention [3].

Historically, IS was focused on the level of health providers, organizations, policy-makers and health systems as these were the predominant groups involved in operationalizing patient care interventions when healthcare was, most commonly, delivered in the hospital setting. With advances in diagnosis, treatment, service delivery models and technology, the role of the patient in their own care, and the healthcare ecosystem overall, is dramatically changing. Patients (inclusive of clients, patients and informal caregivers) have increased autonomy and responsibility for the operationalization, fidelity and maintenance of the healthcare interventions they are receiving [4]. These changes are particularly evident in care for patients with noncommunicable diseases, which have surpassed infectious diseases as the primary cause of morbidity and mortality globally [5].

Patient engagement is rooted in the understanding that patients have an essential role as co-producers of their health as they represent the only consistent factor throughout the healthcare pathway [6]. PE has the ability to foster meaningful and active collaboration with patients in areas of governance, priority setting, research and knowledge translation [1]. PE is guided by the principles of inclusiveness, support, mutual respect and co-building [1]. Healthcare providers, researchers, organizations and policy-makers are being called on to ensure that opportunities for PE exist at all levels: direct care at an individual level; organizational governance and systems design; research; and policy development and implementation [7]. Patients from diverse backgrounds and lived experiences must be involved as much and as meaningfully as possible for health research, including in the area of IS, to successfully identify, design, implement, evaluate and maintain change interventions that are responsive to the evolving needs of society [1].

The critical role of PE is not well described in IS literature and is inconsistently incorporated into IS tools, with the reverse also being true. The primary objective of this article is to systematically map PE-relevant constructs integrated within commonly used IS tools using a standardized repository. Specifically, authors mapped PE-relevant constructs extracted from IS tools that include a focus on implementation at the socioeconomic level of the individual, as this level is most likely to include concepts or strategies reflective of, or transferable to, PE. As a secondary objective, authors present a single, coherent framework that integrates both IS and PE tools, strategies, outcomes and reporting guidance across the continuum of an IS intervention life cycle. The systematically mapped PE constructs in IS tools and integrated IS/PE framework presented in this article are intended to support researchers and practitioners as practical resources and decision aids.

Results

Implementation science (IS) tools

This review article used an adapted evidence mapping methodology [8]. We selected this methodology because it facilitated the authors aims to (1) chart the existing IS tools and any associated PE-relevant constructs to identify patterns or gaps across commonly used IS tools, (2) develop practical visual tools (decision aid tables, frameworks) and (3) produce hypothetical case examples to enhance the accessibility of our results, ultimately supporting research teams seeking to conduct IS research that meaningfully engages patient partners. The authors consider this an adaption of evidence mapping methods as we conducted our search strategy in only one database, the Dissemination and Implementation Models in Health Research and Practice website (D&I website) [8, 9]. The D&I website was selected as the source for this article as it is the most comprehensive and up to date indexed electronic database of IS tools.

Our search strategy focused on identifying the top 10 most cited IS tools indexed by the D&I website as including the “Socio-Economic” level of “individual” and having a dominant focus on “implementation”. Selection of IS tools guided by citation frequency was used, as citation frequency is considered a surrogate for academic and research influence and frequency of use in research and practice [10]. The authors decision to exclude IS tools that only focused on “Socio-Economic” levels of the “Organization”, “Community”, “System”, and/or “Policy” was guided by the intended focus to identify IS tools that may contain PE-relevant constructs, which would be expected to be most likely to exist in IS tools designed for application by research seeking to impact the health system at the level of individual members or contributors, i.e. patients. As the focus of this research is on IS, authors excluded tools indexed within the D&I website that had a single or dominant focus on dissemination, having a lesser or no focus on implementation.

An important consideration when selecting an IS tool(s) are the constructs of the given tool. Constructs, in the context of IS, refer to concepts or elements in the implementation model, intervention, strategy or hypothesized outcome(s) [9]. When selecting an IS tool, it is recommended that you assess for best fit of the tool with key constructs related to your project [9]. Using interactive tools, such as the D&I website, is a helpful place to start to guide your selection of IS tools [9]. The D&I website is a comprehensive repository of IS tools, indexed by common constructs for easy searching and selection [9]. Examples of preindexed constructs outlined within the D&I website that may support PE within the implementation process include, but are not limited to, “engagement” (“involving appropriate individuals in the implementation and use of the intervention through a combined strategy of social marketing, education, role modeling, training, and other similar activities”) and “patient/target audience characteristics and needs” (“individuals who will be impacted by the intervention. For healthcare and public health interventions, these are often patients, consumers, or community members. These individuals can be, but are most often not, the user of the intervention”) [9].

We completed a search of the D&I website’s indexed database, conducted on 1 April 2022, using the following filters: implementation (socioecologic levels), individual and the constructs of “engagement” and “patient/target audience characteristics and needs”. This search identified 51 tools of the more than 180 on the D&I website. The 51 tools were sorted by “number of times cited”. In descending order, from most to least cited, the first 10 tools that had an equal or greater focus on implementation (compared with dissemination), and that included the socio-ecologic level of the individual, were selected for review and presentation in this article (Fig. 1). The 17 most cited IS tools were reviewed to achieve the target of identifying the top 10 most cited articles that met the inclusion criteria. Three IS tools were excluded as they were focused on dissemination and did not include the socioeconomic level of the individual. One IS tool was excluded as it focused predominantly on dissemination. Two IS tools were excluded as they did not include the socioeconomic level of the individual. One IS tool was excluded as it was a preliminary version of an IS tool that was already included in the selected IS tools for review in this article (duplication). The 10 included IS tools were reviewed, within the D&I website, and the following data were extracted from each: included socioecologic levels, construct flexibility as rated by the D&I website on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (general, flexible, allows for adaptability for broad application) to 5 (operational, providing step-by-step process), total number of constructs assigned by the D&I website, and a list of the framework-specific constructs (as assigned by the D&I website) that may facilitate PE (Table 1). Of note, the descriptions of the constructs that may facilitate PE (Table 1: Potential client/patient-oriented constructs (elements)) were extracted as written from the D&I website; there is variance in the level of detail in the descriptions of constructs extracted from the D&I website. IS strategies and outcomes

Within IS, implementation strategies are considered so-called interventions. Implementation strategies are approaches or techniques used to enhance the adoption, implementation, sustainability and scale-up of an innovation [11]. Implementation strategies may be discrete or combined interventions [11]. Selection of implementation strategies is guided by potential implementation barriers identified during the formative evaluation phase of the IS process [12]. Some IS tools have provided guidance for researchers to support selection of implementation strategies on the basis of identified barriers [13].

The Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) project outlines an evidence-informed, consensus-based set of 73 implementation strategies, organized in nine thematic clusters [14]. The thematic clusters and implementation strategies outlined in ERIC that describe consideration of, or engagement with, the patient are outlined below (Table 2). Seven implementation strategies, across four thematic clusters, specifically identify a level of engagement with or involvement of the patient. In contrast, there are five of the nine thematic clusters that do not address involvement of patients, an example being “developing stakeholder interrelations”[14]. In these cases, although no formal implementation strategies that address patient involvement are included within the ERIC tool, these thematic clusters still represent areas of opportunity to engage with or support patients in IS research. Using “developing stakeholder interrelations” as an example, patients are active stakeholders in interventions that impact their health, therefore implementation strategies to “develop stakeholder interrelationships” would be relevant to extrapolate to patient partners involved in research, as proxy representatives of patients who would be receiving care related to the intervention being studied. A similar approach to extrapolation of implementation strategies to patient partners would be applicable across other thematic clusters outlined in ERIC. Where research teams extrapolate in this way, a priori identification and description of the approaches used, rationale, outcome measures and balancing measures taken should be clearly outlined, to both increase methodologic rigour and transparency in research but also to establish an evidence base to support identification and validation of novel implementation strategies to strengthen and expand the methods by which patients are engaged in IS research.

Within IS, implementation outcomes reflect the effects of implementation strategies [15]. The functions of implementation outcomes include indicators of implementation success and proximal indicators of implementation processes, and provide insight into systems or clinical outcomes related to treatment effectiveness or quality of care-oriented research [15]. Proctor et al. outline a conceptual framework for implementation outcomes that proposes an eight-item heuristic, working “taxonomy” of distinct implementation outcomes; these implementation outcomes influence the resulting service outcomes [16], both of which ultimately impact patient outcomes [15]. Implementation outcomes outlined include: acceptability, adoption, appropriateness, costs, feasibility, fidelity, penetration and sustainability [15]. Service outcomes outlined include: efficiency, safety, effectiveness, equity, patient-centredness and timeliness [15]. Two of the eight implementation outcomes (acceptability and appropriateness) include the perspective of the client/patient/consumer in the “level of analysis” [15].

As with implementation strategies, PE strategies form the interventions or processes to achieve meaningful PE [17]. In 2018, a systematic review characterized existing literature describing the impact of PE on the quality of healthcare [17]. This review described several PE strategies, barriers, facilitators and outcomes (care related and patient-experience related) associated with varying levels of PE [17]. The PE strategies focus on employing techniques to enhance PE across research aspects related to designing for engagement, diverse and representative participant recruitment, participant involvement in research, creating a receptive context, and leadership actions. When designing PE-focused IS research, PE strategies and outcomes can be incorporated, a priori, into study methods and reassessed throughout the continuum of IS research [17].

Proctor et al. include equity as a service outcome that may be achieved through IS research [15]. PE is guided by principles of inclusiveness, support, mutual respect and co-building [1] Both fields seek to improve health and care outcomes for members of society [1, 2]. When discussing the role that PE may have when incorporated into IS research, it is imperative to speak directly to the realities that (1) it is the voices of those who are engaged in research that shape the interventions and systems in which they are engaged and that (2) the opportunity to be engaged in research is not inherently equal for all members of society [16]. An article published by Dr Snow in 2022 addresses the reality that engagement of patients that represent only so-called dominant populations (white, male, cisgender, heterosexual, without disability, and so on) does not foster equity in healthcare but instead may further marginalize groups that are oppressed by current societal and health systems infrastructures [16]. It is the responsibility of those in positions of power to actively create safe, inclusive, supportive and respectful circumstances in which patient partners with diverse circumstances and experiences may meaningfully engage with their role to improve health systems and care interventions that create democratic and socially just improvements in healthcare [16]. Dr Snow outlines methodologic approaches necessary for healthcare planners and evaluators to democratically engage with patient partners, across the continuum of PE (specifically, before, during and after PE); these approaches equally apply to IS research, which engages patients. Examples of the recommended approaches include: understanding and applying methods that focus on intersectionality to inform identification and empowerment of representative patient partners, demising societal and health system power dynamics and bringing awareness to different biases that are present among different health systems members [16].

IS reporting guidelines

As the areas of IS and PE evolve, consistent reporting is paramount to increase the reproducibility and transparency of methodologies, strategies, evaluation and outcomes. Transparency and standardization in reporting supports enhanced scientific rigour [17]. Reporting guidelines are an essential tool to increase the transparency of reporting, quality of research outputs and methodologic rigour, and help establish common definitions, taxonomy and unified terminology [18]. A lack of clarity of concepts and/or ill-defined/insufficient transparency and low reproducibility have a negative impact on the evolution of scientific research and introduce underappreciated barriers to novice researcher/clinician engagement with evolving scientific areas, such as IS and PE [19].

Reporting guidelines exist for both IS and PE research independently. There are no IS reporting guidelines that incorporate PE such that a single reporting guideline could be used to fulsomely report a research initiative that meaningfully employs both IS and PE. An example of an IS-focused reporting guideline is the Standards for Reporting Implementation Studies (StaRI) statement [20]. An example of a PE-focused reporting guideline is Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public (GRIPP2) reporting checklist [21]. IS and PE reporting guidelines may be applied using a layered approach when reporting research that incorporates both areas. An opportunity for future research could include the design, implementation and evaluation of a PE-focused reporting guideline extension that may be applied to/combined with IS reporting guidelines (e.g. a GRIPP2 extension evaluated in combination with the StaRI reporting guideline) [20, 21].

Limitations

This commentary was undertaken to describe existing tools, strategies and outcomes across two related but, as yet, not integrated scientific areas: IS and PE. The intention is to provide guidance for clinicians and researchers and propose possible future opportunities for research to embed both PE and IS. There are several limitations to this work, including but not limited to:Literature search – this article did not undertake a methodologically comprehensive systematic review of all potential existing implementation models, strategies, outcomes or reporting guidelines. We focused on outlining an approach that may be taken by a novice implementation practitioner, clinician or policy-maker/administrator seeking to engage with IS related to an intervention that would be enhanced by PE.Literature search – the literature search was conducted in April 2022. There may be IS tools that have been published since our search date that would be relevant to consider for PE-focused IS research. If the tools referenced in this review are not suitable for a given research intervention, we would encourage research teams to follow our search and selection strategy applied, described above, to the D&I website to see whether any new IS tools exist that may be applicable for their methodologic needs.Literature search – authors used citation frequency to select the top 10 most cited IS tools indexed within the D&I website, filtered by other selection criteria factors described above. While citation frequency is an acknowledged metric indicating academic or research influence in a given field of study, it is not necessarily a metric reflecting research quality [10]. There may be applicable IS tools of high quality, that would have met the study inclusion criteria but were cited less than those top 10 most cited articles. We would encourage research teams to not dismiss an IS tool that is suitable for their PE-focused research exclusively on the basis of citation frequency, but rather to consider the other methodologic considerations and data extraction processes undertaken by this review article to determine alternative IS tool applicability to their PE research.D&I website limitations [9] – potential limitations associated with using the D&I website for IS tool review include: reliance on the coding and alignment of constructs and elements by D&I website developers and the potential that emerging, relevant tools in IS or PE may not be indexed in this database at the time of this publication. Conclusions

To date, IS tools, strategies, outcomes and reporting guidelines place a dominant focus on the insights, perspectives, barriers, facilitators, priorities and values of healthcare providers, institutions, organizations and policy-makers. This focus is no longer reflective of the ultimate implementers and users of most healthcare interventions – the patients. Patients have increasing autonomy and responsibility for implementing health-related interventions in their daily lives, beyond the time and space of their finite healthcare encounters. This article highlights existing IS tools that focus on the socioeconomic level of the individual and include constructs and elements that, in concert with robust PE strategies and outcomes, would facilitate meaningful engagement of patients as partners in implementation research. Use of the outlined IS tools, concurrently with PE strategies and outcomes, will help healthcare communities and society at large realize the optimized and sustained impact in improved health and care outcomes for patients, which is the ultimate goal of both IS and PE.
