Trends in kidney and renal pelvis cancer mortality and associated risk factors in the United States
Authors: Sizhe Li, Xudan Chen, Ying Wang, Wayne R Lawrence, Yingxi Chen, Xinlei Deng, Yanji Qu, Ziqiang Lin, Yongqing Sun, Man Zhang, Kaili Zhu, Chuifei Zhong, Jie Sun, Zhicheng Du, Yuantao Hao, Wangjian Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKidney and renal pelvis cancer (KRPC) is a leading cause of cancer death in the United States, yet the mortality trends by sociodemographic characteristics are not well understood.MethodsThis serial cross-sectional analysis used National Centre for Health Statistics data (1999–2020) to examine kidney and renal pelvis cancer mortality trends among individuals aged ≥ 20, stratified by sex, age, race/ethnicity, and county-level socioeconomic status (SES) (Yost index) and rurality. County-level SES was categorized into distribution-based quintiles, where higher quintiles represented greater SES. Age-standardized mortality trends and average annual percent change were calculated, and age-adjusted multivariate quasi-Poisson regression assessed mortality relative to county-level factors.ResultsFrom 1999 to 2020, overall KRPC mortality declined (average annual percent change [AAPC]: -0.9%, 95% CI [-1.0%, -0.8%]), with decreases observed among men (-1.0%) and women (-1.2%). Black individuals had the most substantial decline (AAPC: -1.3%, 95% CI [-1.8%, -0.7%]) compared with all other racial groups. American Indian and Alaska Native individuals showed no significant changes over the study period. KRPC mortality was approximately 1.2 times higher in counties in the lowest SES quintile compared with the highest quintile. Counties with the highest degree of rurality tended to have the greatest risk of KRPC (Complete rural, < 2500 vs. Metropolitan, > 1 million, 1.0 vs. 0.6).ConclusionsThe mortality trends varied by demographics. The close association of mortality with rurality highlights the necessity for focused public health interventions to address specific demographic needs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26055-z.Keywords: Ethnic groups, Kidney and renal pelvis cancer, Mortality trends, Socioeconomic status

Introduction

In the U.S., the incidence rate of kidney and renal pelvis cancer (KRPC) continues to increase [1]. Though prior studies have suggested improved survival among patients diagnosed with KRPC [2], understanding of mortality trends by population characteristics and socioeconomic status (SES) remains limited, thereby, hindering the ability to identify vulnerable populations.

Substantial evidence supported the association between the development of KRPC and various risk factors, including obesity, smoking, and chronic renal disease [3–5]. According to a meta-analysis, an increase in BMI of 5 kg/m2 is linked to a 24% higher risk of renal cell carcinoma in men and a 34% higher risk in women [6]. Smoking contributes to reduced survival rates independently across all KRPC stages. In comparison to individuals who have never smoked, both current and former smokers exhibit an approximate 30% and 14% higher risk of mortality associated with chronic kidney disease [7]. Apart from these factors, SES may also be significant predictors. For instance, in a cohort of 20 common cancers from 2011 to 2022, patients living in lower SES regions had greater delay in treatment initiation compared to higher SES regions [8]. However, existing evidence on the contribution of demographic characteristics and area-level socioeconomic factors on disparities in KRPC mortality remains limited.

In this study, we examined KRPC mortality trends among individuals ≥ 20 (1999 to 2020), categorized by sex, race and ethnicity, and age group. We further investigated the association between KRPC mortality and county-level SES and rurality.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

We extracted demographic information and KRPC mortality from death certificate data compiled by the National Center for Health Statistics. Deaths were classified using the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results Cause of Death Recodes based on the Tenth Revision of the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems (ICD-10) codes (specifically, C64-C65). Population estimates were stratified by year, age group, sex, and race/ethnicity. Our study included adults aged 20 years and older who died of KRPC. Race and ethnicity were categorized as non-Hispanic White (White), non-Hispanic Black (Black), American Indian and Alaska Native (AI/AN), Asian or Pacific Islander (A/PI), and Hispanic/Latino, with American Indian/Alaska Native limited to Indian Health Service Purchasing/Referral Health Care Areas (PRCDA). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines [9].

County Attributes

County-level socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed utilizing the Yost index, which comprises seven census indicators. These indicators include median house value, median rent cost, median household earnings, the proportion of individuals living below 150% of the poverty threshold, educational index (percentage of individuals with less than high school education, high school education only and post-high school education among ages ≥ 25 years), percentage of working class, and percentage of individuals unemployed. Counties were categorized into distribution-based quintiles based on the entire U.S [10]. The degree of rurality for each county was determined utilizing the Rural-Urban Continuum Codes established by the U.S. Department of Agriculture in 2013 [11]. Rurality was categorized as completely rural areas of < 2,500 people, urban areas of 2,500 to < 20,000 people, urban areas of ≥ 20,000 people, metropolitan areas of < 250,000 people, metropolitan areas of 250,000 to < 1 million people, and ≥ 1 million people.

Statistical analysis

SEER*Stat (version 8.4.2) was used to calculate the annual age-standardized KRPC mortality trends from 1999 to 2020 by sex, age groups (20–64 years and ≥ 65 years old), and race/ethnicity (White, Black, AI/AN, A/PI, and Hispanic/Latino individuals). Additionally, age-standardized mortality was calculated for the year 2020 alone by sex and race/ethnicity. All rates were age-adjusted using the 2000 U.S. population as the standard and reported as rates per 100,000 people. We utilized the Joinpoint Regression Program to calculate the annual average percentage changes (AAPC) in KRPC mortality from 1999 to 2020[12]. We also computed annual percentage changes (APCs) to identify notable periods of change and quantify the extent of these changes. The parametric method was utilized for calculating 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) to assess trends. P values were computed based on the permutation distribution of the test statistics, and statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 using a two-sided test.

We estimated KRPC mortality rates at the county level by sex, age, and race and ethnicity from 1999 to 2020. We then conducted an age-adjusted multivariate quasi-Poisson regression analysis to examine the correlation between SES and rurality with KRPC mortality rates (rates in 1999–2020). In this analysis, actual death counts were used as the dependent variable, while the Yost index and rurality of each county were treated as independent variables (categorical variables), adjusting for age. Additionally, we established the lowest level of the Yost index and rurality as the reference point. For KRPC mortality, we calculated age-adjusted rate ratio to compare mortality rate differences across various levels. Statistical analyses were performed using R software, with statistical significance defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Trends of KRPC Mortality

From 1999 to 2020, there were 286,051 KRPC deaths. The mortality rates for KRPC showed an increasing trend from 1999 to 2001 (APC, 2.2%; 95% CI, 0.3% to 3.6%), followed by a subsequent decline, with a more pronounced decrease from 2014 to 2020 (APC, −1.8%; 95% CI, −3.0% to −1.4%) (Fig. 1, Supplemental Table 1).

The average death rates were greater among men (7.9/100,000) than women (3.4/100,000). There was a decreasing trend in KRPC mortality for both men (AAPC, −1.0%; 95% CI, −1.2% to −0.8%) and women (AAPC, −1.2%; 95% CI, −1.4% to −0.9%). KRPC death rates decreased rapidly among women from 2004 to 2020 (APC, −1.7%; 95% CI, −2.0% to −1.5%) and among men from 2014 to 2020 (APC, −1.9%; 95% CI, −3.7% to −1.2%) (Fig. 1, Supplemental Table 2).

KRPC mortality was more than ten times higher among individuals aged ≥ 65 years compared to the 20–64 age group. From 1999 to 2020, individuals 20–64 years of age experienced a more pronounced decline (AAPC, −1.8%; 95% CI, −2.1% to −1.6%) than those of ≥ 65 year of age (AAPC, −0.5%; 95% CI, −0.6% to −0.3%). The ≥ 65 years of age group experienced a faster rate of decline between 2013 and 2020 (APC, −1.5%; 95% CI, −2.3% to −1.1%) (Fig. 1, Supplemental Table 3).

KRPC death rates varied by race/ethnicity

In 2020, among men, the highest death rates were observed among AI/AN (12.8/100,000), followed by White (7.4/100,000), Black (7.1/100,000), and Hispanic/Latino individuals (6.5/100,000). Similarly, in women, the highest death rates were among AI/AN in 2020, followed by White (3.0/100,000), Black (3.0/100,000) and Hispanic/Latino (2.6/100,000) individuals. (Table 1). Among Black individuals, KRPC death rates decreased 1.3% annually (95% CI, −1.8% to −0.7%) from 1999 to 2020, and there was a decrease of 2.4% per year (95% CI, −6.7% to −1.2%) from 2014 to 2020. Among White adults, KRPC mortality declined throughout the study period (AAPC, −0.8%; 95% CI, −1.0% to −0.6%), with a more marked decrease of 1.6% per year (95% CI, −3.2% to −1.2%) from 2013 to 2020. There was a decrease in KRPC mortality of 2.3% per year (95% CI, −6.2% to −1.1%) among A/PI individuals from 2009 to 2020 and 2.9% per year (95% CI, −6.8% to −0.9%) among Hispanic/Latino individuals from 2016 to 2020. From 1999 to 2020, AI/AN (AAPC, −0.7%; 95% CI, −1.4% to 0.2%) and A/PI (AAPC, −0.5%; 95% CI, −1.4% to 1.1%) adults did not show significant changes in death rates. Overall, the most significant decline in KRPC mortality was among Black individuals from 1999 to 2020 (AAPC, −1.3%; 95% CI, −1.8% to −0.7%), followed by Hispanic/Latino individuals (AAPC, −0.9%; 95% CI, −1.2%, −0.4%) and White individuals (AAPC, −0.8%; 95% CI, −1.0%, −0.6%) (Fig. 1, Supplemental Table 4).

County-Level Factors

KRPC mortality (1999–2020) varied across county-level SES and rurality quintiles. Counties in lower SES quintiles and those with greater rurality tended to have higher KRPC mortality rates, particularly among males, those aged ≥ 65 years, and AI/AN individuals. For example, in examination by racial groups, compared with counties in the first quintile of the Yost index, KRPC mortality was 0.8 times lower among White individuals and 0.6 times lower among AI/AN individuals who lived in counties in the fifth quintile (Fig. 2, Supplemental Tables 5 and 6). Poisson regression models suggested that risk of KRPC mortality was elevated in counties with higher rurality. For example, compared with complete rural counties, KRPC mortality was observed to be 0.7 times lower in metropolitan areas with a population > 1 million. However, these associations were not apparent among adults aged ≥ 65 years, and no significant association between SES and KRPC mortality was observed (Fig. 3, Supplemental Tables 7 and 8).

Discussion

There were largely substantial declines in KRPC mortality by race and ethnicity, age group, and sex. The decline in death rates were greater among Black people compared with all other racial and ethnic groups. In 2020, the racial and ethnic group with the highest KRPC mortality was AI/AN, followed by White, Black, and A/PI. Poisson analysis showed that lower SES and higher rurality tended to be associated with greater risk of KRPC mortality.

A potential reason for the decline in KRPC mortality is due to advancement in treatment and improved access to quality medical care. Studies have documented that frequent imaging examinations and effective systemic treatments are positively correlated with favorable prognosis for KRPC, while health care expenditures were negatively associated with mortality-to-incidence ratios [13]. In the U.S., treatment strategies for KRPC have gradually evolved, resulting in improved survivorship. Compared with radical nephrectomy, partial nephrectomy has become increasingly common in clinical practice. Studies have suggested that partial nephrectomy may be associated with better overall survival, particularly among patients with tumor stages T1b and T2, who tend to demonstrate more favorable postoperative survival outcomes [14, 15]. A meta-analysis indicated that patients with upper tract urothelial carcinoma (UTUC) who received adjuvant chemotherapy (AC) demonstrated favorable pathological response rates and tumor downstaging [16]. Additionally, there is level 1 evidence supporting that compared with radical nephroureterectomy (RNU) alone, AC provides superior outcomes in terms of overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) [16]. In the field of first-line treatment for advanced KRPC, a combination of targeted therapy and immunotherapy has demonstrated favorable outcomes [17]. In particular, the identification of tyrosine kinase inhibitors as anti-angiogenic therapy for renal cell carcinoma has substantially prolonged the survival of individuals with advanced KRPC [18]. Furthermore, a decrease in smoking rates may also an important contributor to the decline in KRPC mortality [19].

Prior studies have reported that compared with women, men are more likely to be diagnosed with KRPC, present with more aggressive histology, larger tumors, higher grade and stage, as well as have poorer outcomes [20]. These disparities can be attributed to factors such as genetics and greater prevalent risk factors, including hypertension, obesity, lifestyle, and the influence of female sex hormones [21, 22]. For instance, a recent sex-specific genome-wide association study (GWAS) involving over 8000 renal cell cancer patients revealed sex-specific associations for two known genetic loci related to renal cell cancer (DPF3, 14q24.2 associated with females, and EPAS1, 2p21 associated with males), further emphasizing the role of genetic susceptibility in determining sex differences in KRPC [23].

In young adults, KRPC typically presents with smaller tumor sizes, a higher likelihood of being confined to the organ, less aggressive tumor grades, and less progression to advanced stages compared with older adults [24, 25]. As the population ages, there is a rise in the incidence of chronic conditions and diseases, such as obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and vascular diseases [26]. Therefore, early diagnosis becomes particularly crucial, especially for older patients with systemic illnesses.

AI/AN populations had the highest KRPC mortality rates, which remained largely stagnant over the study period, aligning with previous studies, aligning with previous studies [1, 27]. This is likely due to this population experiencing substantial barriers to quality healthcare across the cancer control continuum [28]. Prior studies reported that compared to White individuals, AI/AN individuals are more likely to reside in socioeconomically disadvantaged neighborhoods and have greater difficulties accessing preventive care [29]. Moreover, studies have shown that AI/AN populations have significantly higher uninsured rates compared to other racial/ethnic groups [30]. Among rural residents, AI/AN represent a significant proportion (19.7%) and face heightened vulnerability due to greater poverty rates and reduced healthcare access. These systemic disadvantages substantially increase their cancer risks and lead to poorer treatment outcomes and reduced survival rates compared to other populations [31]. Additionally, AI/AN individuals have higher rates of smoking, obesity, diabetes, hypertension, and kidney disease [28]. Reductions in smoking rates, early detection, and advances in targeted cancer treatment have contributed to a decline in cancer mortality among both Black men and women [32]. KRPC mortality was lowest among A/PI individuals. This is potentially due to a large proportion of the Asian population are foreign-born, where healthier individuals are more likely to immigrate and potential differences in prevalence of health risk factors. Some foreign-born individuals with declining health may also return to their home countries prior to death, and therefore may not be included in U.S. mortality statistics [33]. Although the overall mortality rate is low, KRPC mortality among A/PI populations has not shown a significant downward trend. This may be related to these groups encountering greater race-related healthcare bias due to cultural and language differences that may hinder access to continuous, coordinated, and high-quality healthcare services [34]. The disparities in KRPC mortality among racial groups are primarily a result of long-standing structural inequality, leading to differences in risk factor exposure and unequal opportunities for cancer prevention, early detection, and treatment [35]. Addressing these disparities requires targeted and culturally appropriate public health strategies. For instance, community-based interventions—such as deploying lay health workers, providing education materials in native languages, and offering patient navigation services—have been shown to effectively increase cancer screening uptake among minority populations. Tailoring these interventions to the specific cultural and structural barriers faced by AI/AN and A/PI communities is critical to improving early detection, enhancing treatment access, and ultimately advancing health equity in KRPC outcomes [36].

SES is associated with various factors related to KRPC [22]. A study conducted in Southern California found that even among insured cancer patients, individuals in the lowest SES group faced a higher risk of death following diagnosis. Previous research has shown that SES is strongly associated with disparities in cancer mortality [37]. Similar to prior studies [37, 38], our study observed that KRPC mortality tended to be lower in counties with higher SES and greater degrees of urbanization. These findings emphasize the possibility of directing public health resources to specific counties for primary and secondary prevention of KRPC, accounting for SES and rurality. While KRPC mortality in the U.S. has declined over the past two decades, it is crucial to address the ongoing racial/ethnic and geographic disparities within this trend. These disparities highlight the unequal distribution of healthcare resources and the need for continued advocacy for structurally marginalized communities [28]. Additionally, environmental and lifestyle factors play a significant role. Targeted policies to reduce KRPC risk in disproportionately affected populations are necessary, including measures to reduce poverty, enhance educational opportunities, and improve access to timely and high-quality treatment [39].

Several limitations must be noted. First, there is potential for misclassification for causes of death and racial/ethnic groups on death certificates, as well as the broad grouping of populations and ethnicities which may obscure within-group differences. For example, within the A/PI (Asian or Pacific Islander) category, there are significant variations in cancer mortality rates between Asian and Pacific Islander subgroups [40]. Additionally, we were not able to examine county-level risk factors among A/PI and Hispanic/Latino groups due to the population size. Furthermore, this study is a serial cross-sectional analysis, thus did not allow for causal inferences at the individual-level. Lastly, due to the database combining these two diseases into a single category, we were unable to distinguish between ICD-10 codes C64 (malignant neoplasm of the kidney, excluding the renal pelvis) and C65 (malignant neoplasm of the renal pelvis). Combining them may obscure certtain differences.

Conclusion

From 1999 to 2020, KRPC mortality declined overall, but not uniformly across demographic groups. The decline was evident among women, Black individuals, and those aged 20–64. In contrast, mortality rates remained relatively high and showed less improvement in counties with lower socioeconomic status and in more rural areas. This highlights the need for targeted interventions and equitable access to healthcare to reduce persistent disparities.
