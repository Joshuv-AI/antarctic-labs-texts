Background

In a typical circle of Willis configuration, cerebral perfusion is supplied by two interconnected arterial systems. The vertebrobasilar system ensures blood supply of the posterior circulation via two vertebral arteries merging into a single basilar artery, which ends in two posterior cerebral arteries. Further, the anterior circulation is supported by two internal carotid arteries (ICA) whose terminal branches are the anterior and middle cerebral arteries [1]. The circle of Willis is an anastomotic circle where the anterior and posterior circulation are interconnected via the posterior communicating arteries (PComA) that link the posterior cerebral artery (PCA) and ICA.

Fetal posterior cerebral artery (FPCA) is an anatomical variant occurring in 23–29% of the population unilaterally and 1–9% bilaterally [2]. In FPCA, P2 portion of PCA emerges from PComA, while the P1 portion of PCA is usually hypoplastic or atrophic. In this specific variant, the posterior circulation, especially to the occipital lobe and mesencephalon, is provided by PComA [3].

Whether FPCA constitutes a risk factor for ischemic stroke (IS) is still controversial [4]. Several mechanisms of IS in FPCA have been identified. A recent study observed a more frequent small vessel disease-related IS and a lower frequency of embolic IS in FPCA configuration [5]. Yet, dissection of the ICA is an exceptional mechanism of IS in FPCA, with very few cases described to date.

Herein, we report a rare and misleading case of young-onset IS in the territory of PCA caused by extracranial and intracranial dissection of ICA.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old North African nonsmoker male patient presented to the neurology department of the National Institute Mongi Ben Hmida of Neurology, Tunis-Tunisia, 24 hours from acute onset of slurred speech, weakness, and impaired sensation in the left upper and lower limbs, as well as impaired visual field. His family history was unremarkable and his medical history was significant for hypertension with poor medication adherence. No history of recent trauma was reported in our patient.

General physical examination was unremarkable. Neurological examination noted dysarthria, left hemiplegia with tactile hemihypoesthesia, left homonymous hemianopia, and right Horner’s syndrome. National Institutes of Health Stroke scale (NIHSS) score at admission was 20, with modified Rankin’s scale at 4.

Brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance arteriography (MRA) were performed at 24 hours from symptom onset, showing a cytotoxic edema in the right occipital lobe and the posteromedial portion of the right temporal lobe consistent with an acute stroke in the territory of the posterior cerebral artery (Fig. 1a, b). Axial T1-weighted image with fat suppression at the origin of the ICA showed an enlargement of the vessel with a crescent of hyperintensity, suggesting a mural hematoma (Fig. 2). Three-dimensional (3D) maximum intensity projection (MIP) reconstruction of the magnetic resonance angiography sequence showed the complete absence of flow in the cervical, petrous, and cavernous segments of the right ICA (Fig. 3). Axial view from the 3D maximum intensity projection (MIP) reconstruction of the 3D time of flight (TOF) image of the circle of Willis showed a hypoplastic P1 segment of the right PCA with P2 coming directly from the ipsilateral posterior communicating artery, consistent with FPCA (Fig. 4).

Given this radiological aspect, the diagnosis of acute IS in the territory of PCA was confirmed. Etiological diagnosis was also made since MRI and MRA features were consistent with right intracranial and extracranial ICA dissection associated with FPCA variant.

Transthoracic echocardiogram showed no intracardial thrombus, no permeable foramen ovale, and no diastolic or systolic abnormalities, and left ventricular ejection fraction was normal at 82%. No valvular lesions were detected. Cardial cavities were not dilated.

On laboratory investigations, total cholesterol was 4.23 mmol/l (normal range < 4.8 mmol/l); triglycerides, 3.48 mmol/l (normal range < 1.7 mmol/l); high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, 0.78 mmol/l (normal range > 1.5 mmol/l); and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, 2.88 mmol/l (normal range < 2.6 mmol/l). The level of glycated hemoglobin was 5.59% in our patient. Serum cyanocobalamin level was 326 pg/ml (normal range 175–1000 pg/ml). Antinuclear antibodies and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies were negative. Thrombophilia workup showed free protein S and protein C within normal limits, as was the case of liquid antithrombin.

Treatment with acenocoumarol 3 mg/day and atorvastatin 40 mg/day was implemented, as well as physical therapy.

The patient’s condition improved at 6-month follow-up (modified Rankin’s scale at 2) and no recurrence of IS was noted under anticoagulation to date. However, the patient refused to perform a radiological follow-up.

Discussion and conclusion

Reported cases of IS in the territory of PCA associated with ICA dissection

Cervical artery dissection is the most frequent cause of young-onset IS, accounting for 25% of cases, and the prevalence of right-sided unilateral FPCA is 15% [3, 12]. Nevertheless, the co-occurrence of both is extremely rare.

Whether FPCA may predispose to IS occurrence is still debatable since different studies have presented conflicting results [13, 14]. However, in steno-occlusive disease of ICA, the PCA territory has been demonstrated to be more vulnerable to ischemia and is often the only arterial territory affected by hemodynamic compromise. A possible explanation is that leptomeningeal collaterals may fail to develop in FPCA configuration, leaving large cerebral volumes at risk of hypoperfusion [3]. Unilateral FPCA may also induce major asymmetry in blood-flow supply, possibly explaining why unilateral FPCA is overrepresented in the IS population [4]. Most importantly, no correlation with IS severity or outcome has been identified in the FPCA variant [15].

Anticoagulation was prescribed in line with the latest guidelines due to the large size of the infarction and intradural extension of dissection [16].
