The tolerance-related psychology and dynamic activity in the peripheral nervous system of Internet gaming disorder during playing online games
Authors: Hung-Ming Chi, Tzu-Chien Hsiao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInternet Gaming Disorder (IGD) has become an important emerging research topic with the popularization of Internet technology, but until now, few studies have investigated the dynamic relationship between persistent tolerance symptoms and physiological feedback. From a biomedical signal processing perspective, this gap in research development is due to the difficulty of identifying and examining the relationship between tiny, rapid fluctuations in non-linear, non-stationary physiological signals with limited time-resolved processing techniques. In this study, we propose to use high time-resolved complementary ensemble empirical pattern decomposition and normalized direct quadrature algorithm to explore the interactions between psychological characteristics and peripheral autonomic activity of individuals with IGD during a one-minute play session using the technique of instantaneous pulse rate variability (iPRV).Results26 healthy students and 20 individuals with IGD were recruited to participate in two levels of online games (G1 and G2). The IGD group reported having a fear of missing out and striving for high game standards, both reflecting the inadequacy component of tolerance symptoms. The inadequacy component was significantly positively correlated with IGD risks (r = 0.657). The IGD group also perceived G2 as significantly more challenging than G1. During the G1 stage, emotional intensity was significantly positively correlated with the time component of tolerance (r = 0.293). At the sixth minute of the G1 stage, normalized very high-frequency (r = − 0.330) and normalized low-frequency (nLF) bands iPRV (r = 0.329) were significantly negatively and positively correlated with the IGD risk, respectively. This nLF band was significantly positively correlated with the inadequacy component (r = 0.313).ConclusionThese results suggest that tolerance-related motivational components may trigger heightened emotional intensity and peripheral autonomic activation in individuals with IGD. This study provides novel insights into short-term psychophysiological dynamics underlying IGD, contributing to a deeper understanding of its persistent addictive behavior.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12938-025-01471-9.Keywords: Empirical mode decomposition, Gaming tolerance, Internet gaming disorder, Instantaneous pulse rate variability

Introduction

According to 2024 statistics, the number of online game players worldwide is 3.32 billion (approximately 40.54% of the world population) [1]. However, persistent and compulsive gaming may have negative consequences [2–4], such as conflicts with family members, discontinuation of education, underperformance at work, poor emotional regulation, and reduced decision-making. The aforementioned gaming-related problems were classified as Internet Gaming Disorder (IGD; Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition) [5]. The World Health Organization included the gaming disorder in the International Classification of Diseases 11th Revision and warned of the negative effects of gaming disorder [6], such as impaired control over gaming and continuing to game despite negative consequences. The behaviors lead to significant impairment in persons’ functioning and are associated with other health concerns, including insufficient physical activity and problems with eyesight and hearing. Consequently, the psychological characteristics involved in the progression to IGD have garnered considerable attention.

Tolerance symptoms are an important psychological characteristic of IGD [2]. Tolerance refers to the need for extended gaming time to gain satisfaction [2, 5, 7, 8] and a desire for more exciting games and upgraded gaming equipment [8]. Prior research had conceptualized tolerance as comprising several motivational components, such as pursuing in-game rewards, increased game complexity and difficulty, and reduced fear of missing out (FoMO) [9, 10]. As a result, investigating the tolerance symptoms in IGD may provide insight into how the gaming behavior escalates over time.

Tolerance symptoms in IGD have been linked to psychological processes. For instance, metacognitive beliefs and difficulties in recognizing negative emotions significantly predict tolerance symptoms [11]. FoMO was identified as a mediator in the relationship between negative emotional states and IGD [12]. These findings suggest that individuals with higher tolerance levels may use gaming as a cognitively and behaviorally driven strategy to regulate negative emotional states. Research [13] also observed that gratification partially mediates the link between game engagement and craving in individuals with IGD due to increased tolerance.

In addition to psychological aspects, tolerance symptoms in IGD were also associated with autonomic physiological activity. For instance, the game-related reward was positively correlated with dopamine activity, a neurotransmitter essential for experiencing pleasure, in individuals with IGD [14]. The game-related reward pictures elicited greater brain electrical activity in those with IGD compared to monetary pictures [15]. Using heart rates as an assessment of tolerance levels, regular gamblers exhibited a reduction in heart rate after engaging in fruit machine–based gambling, indicating possible physiological habituation to the gambling stimulus [16]. Similarly, the high-frequency (HF) heart rate variability (HRV; variations in the time intervals between continuous heartbeats) was reduced in individuals with IGD when they focused on gaming compared with the baseline values [17]. In addition, low-challenge gaming sessions did not elicit significant changes in heart rate among individuals with IGD, further suggesting diminished physiological responsiveness in the context of tolerance [18]. However, gamers dynamically regulate both emotional and physiological responses during gaming. While most studies have examined psychological and physiological changes in individuals with IGD over a period, few studies have focused on their emotional reactions and instantaneous physiological responses. This gap highlights the need for further research into the dynamic processes of psychophysiological regulation, particularly as they relate to developing tolerance symptoms in IGD.

HRV is a biomarker of the autonomic nervous system response in individuals with IGD [17, 19–21]. However, HRV analysis in unhealthy populations or during non-resting states typically requires signals of at least five minutes in duration [22]. This constraint may stem from the limited temporal resolution of conventional signal processing methods, which often cannot capture rapid changes in physiological signals. As an alternative, pulse rate variability (PRV; variations in the time intervals between continuous pulse waves) offers more diverse information on peripheral autonomic responses and cardiovascular regulation than HRV [23–25]. PRV was used to observe the autonomic activity of patients with mental disorders [26, 27]. Moreover, the frequency bands of instantaneous PRV (iPRV) were not constrained by the temporal resolution of pulse waves, which was used to observe instantaneous autonomic regulation under varying breathing rates [28, 29].

Our previous study found that individuals with IGD exhibited higher low-frequency (LF) and HF iPRV compared with healthy individuals when they watched positive online gaming videos [30]. However, relatively few studies have investigated the iPRV among individuals with IGD, and no study has explicitly examined the specific relationship between tolerance symptoms, emotional responses, and autonomic activity. Therefore, this study investigated IGD tolerance-associated motivational components and evaluated the effect of these components on emotional responses and instantaneous autonomic activity in individuals with IGD while they played games. The findings may assist psychologists in rapidly assessing the risk of IGD and offer insights into the dynamic psychophysiological changes underlying persistent addictive behaviors in this population. The organization of this study is as follows. Results section describes the results for questionnaire scores and iPRV. Discussion section discusses the relationship between questionnaire scores and iPRV. Materials and Methods section presents the methods used for collecting questionnaires and analyzing pulse wave signals.

Results

To assess IGD symptoms, participants whose scores on the Chen Internet Addiction Scale (CIAS) and IGD Scale (IGDS) were > 63 and > 4, respectively, constituted the IGD group. By contrast, individuals whose scores on the CIAS and IGDS were ≤ 63 and ≤ 4, respectively, constituted the healthy-control (HC) group. The IGD and HC groups comprised 20 (17 males and 3 females; 22.65 ± 3.22 years old) and 26 (23 males and 3 females; 23.38 ± 2.59 years old) individuals, respectively. No significant correlation was found between sex and IGD risk (χ2 = 0.119; p = 0.730). The Internet Gaming Tolerance Survey (IGTS) was used to evaluate motivational components associated with IGD tolerance, including achievement, inadequacy, and wealth. The questionnaire scores of CIAS, IGDS, and IGTS in the HC and IGD groups are summarized in Table 1. Significant between-group differences were observed in the scores on the following CIAS factors: compulsive behavior, withdrawal symptoms, time component of tolerance symptoms, health problems, and time management problems. The IGD group scored significantly higher on the IGTS and inadequacy component than the HC group.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to explore the tolerance-related motivational components in relation to gamers’ emotional responses, immersion, and dynamic peripheral autonomic activity. We found that IGDS and CIAS scores were significantly and positively correlated with both the total IGTS score and the inadequacy component of the IGTS. Moreover, the IGD group exhibited a stronger FoMO on online games (inadequacy component), had higher standards for game completion (inadequacy component), and required more game resources (wealth component) than did the HC group. These findings are consistent with the study, indicating that tolerance-related motivators of individuals with IGD include more complex games, difficulty levels, or higher levels of FoMO [9]. These motivators were positively correlated with the IGD risk [10], especially levels of FoMO [31–33]. Furthermore, individuals with addiction often manage and increase game resources [34, 35] and exhibit a perfectionistic personality [36]. The addiction group also tends to pursue higher-risk rewards [37]. Therefore, we infer that FoMO, higher standards for games, and more game resources are core motivational components of tolerance that contribute to the development of IGD behaviors, leading individuals with IGD to persist in online gaming.

The IGD and HC groups perceived the challenge in the G2 stage to be significantly higher than that in the G1 stage. Compared to during the G1 stage, the emotional valence and flow state of the IGD group were significantly lower during the G2 stage. During the G1 stage, the tolerance-related time component of the CIAS was significantly and positively correlated with emotional arousal. During the G2 stage, the withdrawal symptoms of the CIAS exhibited significantly negative and positive correlations with emotional valence and arousal, respectively. These findings are supported by previous studies. Individuals with IGD require additional gaming time to achieve the same level of satisfaction they achieved previously [2–5, 8], and therefore, they are sensitive to emotional intensity. Individuals with IGD also pursued achievement and high game standards; if these individuals failed to complete difficult games, they exhibited low emotional tolerance, high frustration, and increased depressive feelings [38]. Moreover, those with IGD were unable to complete difficult game levels; this condition may be similar to withdrawal symptoms. IGD group exhibit withdrawal symptoms, such as anger, anxiety, and sadness, when they reduce or stop gaming [5, 8, 20]. Therefore, we infer that both the time component of tolerance and withdrawal symptoms were associated with emotional responses. Challenging games may reduce gaming behavior and elicit negative emotions in individuals with IGD.

This study also investigated the association between iPRV and self-reported symptoms of IGD during baseline, game stimuli (G1 and G2), and recovery stages. During the G1 and G2 stages, most questionnaire scores were negatively correlated with nVHF values. Notably, a significant negative correlation was observed between nVHF value at the sixth minute of the G1 stage and IGDS scores. In contrast, significant positive correlations were observed between nLF and both the IGDS and the inadequacy component of the IGTS at the sixth minute of the G1 stage. At the fifth minute of the G1 stage, nLF was significantly negatively correlated with the wealth component of the IGTS. Moreover, the LF/HF ratio was positively correlated with most questionnaire scores during the G1, G2, and recovery stages. These correlations show convergence with previous reports of autonomic dysregulation in psychiatric disorders, while extending prior findings by demonstrating that similar patterns of autonomic imbalance are associated with IGD symptom severity in a dynamic gaming context. For example, patients with major depressive disorder had a higher LF/HF ratio PRV when executing mental tasks than did healthy individuals [27]. The IGD group exhibited a higher LF/HF ratio HRV during playing an online game than did the HC group [19]. In addition, patients with depression showed more severe peripheral vascular diseases than those without depression [39]. Furthermore, the machine learning model achieved high IGD classification accuracy when the LF feature was included [30, 40, 41], underscoring the predictive value of autonomic indices. The inconsistency between our findings and previous results may be due to the different stimuli used and the dynamic psychophysiological regulatory responses in individuals with IGD [42–44]. Moreover, PRV is a complex physiological index that reflects the effects of breathing, blood pressure and vessels, muscle contraction, and other physiological responses [23, 25, 45]. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that iPRV responses observed at the end of gaming may reflect underlying inadequacy and wealth components of tolerance, while also overlapping with reward-related processes [46, 47] that conceptually intersect with tolerance [9, 10]. The present study demonstrates that tolerance-related motivational components are reflected in emotional responses and instantaneous autonomic activity, offering insight into the psychophysiological mechanisms that sustain persistent gaming. These insights may assist psychologists in identifying at-risk individuals and developing interventions to mitigate addictive behaviors.

This study has some limitations. First, the participant pool for this study was predominantly male (40 males, 6 females), and the uneven sex distribution across groups (HC: 23 males, 3 females; IGD: 17 males, 3 females) may have confounded the results. Although no significant sex differences in the risk of IGD were observed, future studies should recruit more balanced samples and control for sex as a covariate to minimize potential bias. Second, the participants played Crazy Racing Kart Rider games with robots rather than human players; this might have influenced the participants’ psychophysiological responses. Third, the fixed sequence of experimental stages (baseline, G1, G2, and recovery) may have introduced order effects, as psychophysiological responses could persist across consecutive stages. Future studies should incorporate recovery intervals between the G1 and G2 stages to minimize this potential bias. Fourth, the experimental protocol should be tested in multiple ways to validate our results. Fifth, future studies may consider collecting questionnaire data at baseline, during game stimuli, and in the recovery stage, which would allow for a comprehensive mixed-design analysis. Sixth, although a 1-min window size was used for segmenting the pulse wave signal, the robustness of the results across different segment lengths remains untested. Future research should examine the effects of varying window sizes (e.g., 30 s and 2 min) on psychophysiological responses. Finally, although tolerance symptoms were assessed before gaming, the observed psychophysiological responses at the end of gaming may also involve reward processes. Future studies should separately examine reward anticipation and persistence in gaming.

Conclusion

We explored IGD tolerance-related motivational components and the effects of these components on dynamic psychophysiological responses. Individuals with IGD exhibited FoMO and strove to achieve high game standards (inadequacy components). The challenge level of the G1 stage in the IGD group was lower than the G2 stage. During the G1 stage, the time component was positively correlated with emotional intensity. During the sixth minute of the G1 stage, the nVHF iPRV was significantly negatively related to the IGDS score, and the nLF iPRV was significantly positively correlated with the IGDS score and inadequacy component. Inadequacy and time components, emotional intensity, challenge level of the game, and nVHF and nLF iPRV were the dominant features in assessing IGD risk. These findings suggest IGD tolerance-related components caused a higher emotional intensity and dynamic peripheral autonomic activity while playing online games. We provide new information about IGD tolerance-related motivators and dynamic autonomic functions that may help assess the persistent gaming behavior of IGD. Future studies should explore the longitudinal research of IGD tolerance-related motivators on psychophysiological responses.

Materials and methods

Questionnaire

To assess the psychometric variables associated with problematic gaming, we used the CIAS [7], IGDS [8], and IGTS [10]. The 26-item CIAS has been widely used to evaluate Internet addiction and IGD in an East Asian population [20]. The responses are rated on a 4-point Likert scale with endpoints ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 4 (strongly agree). This scale is used to assess five factors of the CIAS: compulsive behavior, withdrawal symptoms, tolerance-related time conditions, health problems, and time management problems. The score for the CIAS was higher than 63, which classified participants as having an Internet addiction [48]. The internal reliability of the CIAS was 0.931 in this study.

The 9-item IGDS is commonly used to assess IGD symptoms [8]. The responses are rated on a dichotomous scale, with the responses being no (0 point) and yes (1 point). The score of the IGDS was higher than 4, categorizing participants as having the IGD. The Cronbach’s α of the IGDS was 0.750 in this study.

The 12-item IGTS is used to evaluate IGD tolerance-related motivators [10]. Scores on the IGTS offer insights into the three factors measured: achievement, inadequacy, and wealth. The responses are rated on a dichotomous scale, with the responses being no (0 point) and yes (1 point). Higher scores on the IGTS indicated greater severity of IGD tolerance symptoms. The internal reliability of the IGTS in this study was 0.591, which falls below the commonly recommended threshold of 0.70 [49]. This reduced reliability may be partly attributable to the dichotomous response format and the limited number of items, both of which can restrict score variability and lower reliability estimates. Although Guttman’s λ6 reached 0.709 for the IGTS, suggesting that the scale may achieve marginally acceptable reliability under optimal conditions, the overall results indicate limited internal consistency. Therefore, findings involving the IGTS should be interpreted with caution, and future research should refine the measurement, for example, by adopting a Likert-type response format and recruiting larger sample sizes.

To evaluate the degree of immersion in online games, we used the Challenge-Skill Scale [50] and the Flow Short Scale [51]. The 2-item Challenge-Skill Scale evaluates a game’s challenge level and a user’s skills. Responses are rated on a 5-point Likert scale with end points ranging from 1 (extremely low) to 5 (extremely high). The 10-item Flow Short Scale is used to evaluate the level of immersion in gaming activities. The responses are rated on a 7-point Likert scale with end points ranging from 1 (not at all) to 7 (very much). Higher scores on the Flow Short Scale indicated a greater flow experienced by gamers.

The emotional responses, gaming experiences, and basic characteristics (age and sex) of the study participants were recorded. A 2-item emotion assessment scale was used to measure emotional valence and arousal. Emotional valence was assessed using a 9-point Likert-type scale with end points ranging from 1 (strongly negative emotion) to 9 (strongly positive emotion), and 5 measured neutral emotion. Emotional arousal was also evaluated using a 9-point Likert-type scale, with end points ranging from 1 (extremely low intensity) to 9 (extremely high intensity).

Participants

Participants were recruited from a university in Hsinchu, Taiwan. To assess IGD symptoms, all participants completed the CIAS and the IGDS. Based on their scores, participants were assigned to one of two groups: those who scored above 63 on the CIAS and above 4 on the IGDS were classified as the IGD group, whereas those who scored 63 or below on the CIAS and 4 or below on the IGDS were categorized as the HC group. Inclusion criteria for both groups included: (1) being between 20 and 40 years of age, and (2) frequent engagement in online multiplayer role-playing games. Exclusion criterion for both groups was having received a diagnosis of a mental disorder within the past 6 months. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Physiological signal

Pulse wave signals were measured using a continuous arterial blood pressure monitor (CNAP Monitor 500; CNSystems Medizintechnik, Graz, Austria). Signals were recorded at a sampling rate of 200 Hz by DAQCard (USB 6255, 16-bit resolution, NI, Austin, TX, USA).

Game with two levels

Crazy Racing Kart Rider is an online multiplayer racing game [52]. Gamers play the speed model with seven other gamers or robots and can select the game difficulty level ranging from 1 (very easy) to 5 (very difficult). To investigate the influence of different challenge levels in games on gamers’ psychophysiological responses, three researchers selected levels 2 and 4 of Crazy Racing Kart Rider as the G1 and G2 game stages to stimulate participants, respectively. These researchers were familiar with Crazy Racing Kart Rider.

Experimental procedure

Before the experiment, the participants were instructed to avoid consuming caffeinated foods and drinks to ensure consistent cardiovascular responses at baseline. They also completed the IGTS, ensuring that tolerance-related motivational components were measured independently of in-game experiences. The experimental procedure then comprised four sequential stages: baseline, G1, G2, and recovery. During the baseline of 6 min, the participants gazed at a gray picture with a black circle in the center. During the G1 and G2 stages (each 7 min), the participants played Crazy Racing Kart Rider of levels 2 and 4, respectively. After each game stage, the participants completed three questionnaires—the emotion assessment scale, the Challenge-Skill Scale, and the Flow Short Scale—to assess their psychological responses. Finally, at the recovery stage of 6 min, the participants again gazed at the gray picture with a black circle in the center. The participants’ pulse wave signals were measured during these four stages to evaluate the peripheral autonomic activity.

Signal processing

Pulse wave signal was analyzed using a framework of nonlinear signal processing [30], which included preprocessing, decomposition, transformation, and classification. Figure 3 shows the signal processing.

Signal processing. CEEMD, complementary ensemble empirical mode decomposition; IMFs, intrinsic mode functions; NDQ, normalized direct quadrature; HF, high-frequency; LF, low-frequency; VHF, very high-frequency

In the preprocessing step, the 6 min signals recorded during the baseline and recovery stages and the 7 min signals recorded during the G1 and G2 stages were segmented into 1-min signals to observe very short-term peripheral autonomic responses. This choice was based on a prior study that observed HRV during mental stress using electrocardiography signals shorter than 1-min [53], providing both temporal resolution and signal stability. Subsequently, all 1-min signals were downsampled from 200 to 50 Hz to reduce the decomposition time.

In the decomposition step, each signal was decomposed into 10 different fluctuations (intrinsic mode functions; from high to low frequencies) by using the complementary ensemble empirical mode decomposition method [54], which is an adaptive filter method. Next, the pulse rate component was extracted as the fourth intrinsic mode function based on relevant studies [30, 55, 56].

In the transformation step, the fourth intrinsic mode function was transformed into the corresponding instantaneous pulse wave by using the normalized direct quadrature method [57]. From the instantaneous pulse rate, iPRV was calculated using the auto power spectrum method, which involved the power calculation of LF (0.04‒015 Hz; sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system activities), HF (0.15‒0.4 Hz; parasympathetic nervous system activity) [58], and very HF (VHF, 0.4‒0.9 Hz; peripheral vascular responses and respiration-linked modulation) [55]. The iPRV was used to observe the sympathovagal balance and peripheral autonomic responses during baseline and rest stages [28–30]. To minimize intergroup differences, the LF, HF, and VHF were normalized by dividing each by the sum of all bands (LF + HF + VHF), resulting in the nLF, nHF, and nVHF variables, respectively. Signal processing, from preprocessing to transformation steps, was conducted using LabVIEW software (version 2021, National Instruments, Austin, TX, USA).

Statistical analysis

A mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with group (HC and IGD) as the between-subjects factor and stage (the sixth minute of the baseline, seven G1 intervals, seven G2 intervals, and six recovery intervals) as the within-subjects factor to examine changes in iPRV over time. Given the small sample size and potential deviations from normality, the Mann–Whitney U (MWU) test was used to analyze the intergroup differences in questionnaire scores and iPRV. The association between IGD risk and tolerance-related motivational components was investigated using the chi-square test. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to conduct intragroup comparisons of questionnaire scores and iPRV at the baseline stage and after exposure to game stimuli; at the baseline and recovery stages. The correlations between the scores on the different questionnaires were investigated through Spearman correlation analysis. In addition, the correlations between the questionnaire scores (CIAS, CIAS factors, IGDS, IGTS, and IGTS components) and iPRV during each 1-min period were also evaluated using Spearman correlation analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 22; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA) and used a two-tailed and alpha level of 0.05.
