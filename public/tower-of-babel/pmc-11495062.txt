Kaempferol promotes osteogenic differentiation in bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells by inhibiting CAV-1
Authors: Yingxue Li, Ying Wang, Qian Liu, Shuiying Lv, Yali Wang, Huanhuan Zhang, Qiuhong Zhao, Lei Shang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOur study focused on the effects and molecular mechanisms of kaempferol, a major active component of Eucommia ulmoides Oliver (EUO), on the osteogenic differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs).MethodsTarget molecules for EUO, osteoarthritis, and osteogenic differentiation were identified through network pharmacology analysis. BMSCs were isolated and treated with various concentrations of kaempferol. Optimal concentration was determined through MTT assays. Osteogenic differentiation was assessed using alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and Alizarin Red S staining, while osteogenic markers (Collagen I, RUNX2, and OPN) and CAV-1 expression were analyzed using RT-qPCR and Western blot. The effects of combined treatment with kaempferol and an overexpression vector for CAV-1 (oe-CAV-1) on osteogenic differentiation were also observed.ResultsNetwork pharmacology analysis identified kaempferol as the primary active component influencing CAV-1 targeted in subsequent experiments. It was found that 10 µM kaempferol was optimal for treating BMSCs. Post-treatment, significant increases in ALP activity and calcium deposition were observed, along with elevated expression of osteogenic markers, and decreased CAV-1. Overexpression of CAV-1 significantly reversed the promotive effects of kaempferol on BMSC osteogenic differentiation, effectively inhibiting the process.ConclusionCollectively, kaempferol promotes osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs by inhibiting CAV-1 expression.Keywords: Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, Kaempferol, CAV-1, Osteogenic differentiation

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) stands as a prevalent arthritis form, notorious for its chronic degenerative impact on synovial joints. It is marked by a range of joint abnormalities including cartilage degradation, subchondral bone loss, synovial tissue proliferation, and tendon and ligament instability [1, 2]. Presently, OA management primarily revolves around pain alleviation and symptom management using analgesics and non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, though these approaches do not address the underlying disease and may only offer temporary relief. Surgical options like knee or hip replacements offer pain relief and can correct deformities but come with the risk of postoperative issues [3]. Recent developments in OA treatment have spotlighted the potential of mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) therapy. MSCs, which can be derived from various adult tissues, are multipotent cells capable of differentiating into several cell types, including fat cells, bone cells, and cartilage cells [4]. Bone marrow-derived MSCs (BMSCs), in particular, are pivotal in bone formation and repair, and thus, are increasingly incorporated into tissue engineering strategies aimed at bone regeneration [5]. However, the detailed molecular pathways through which BMSCs contribute to bone formation are not yet completely defined, highlighting the need for continued research into the regulatory factors and mechanisms guiding their osteogenic differentiation.

Eucommia ulmoides Oliver (EUO), a traditional Chinese medicinal plant, is rich in a spectrum of bioactive compounds such as lignans, iridoids, phenolics, steroids, terpenoids, and flavonoids. These compounds are recognized for their beneficial effects on liver and kidney health and blood pressure regulation [6]. Among these, kaempferol, a flavonoid, is notable for its broad range of health benefits [7] and is one of the major active components of EUO which was reported for its suppression on osteoarthritis [8]. Recent evidence highlights kaempferol’s potential in cancer prevention, where a notable association has been found between its intake and a lowered risk of cancer [9]. Furthermore, kaempferol has been documented to possess anti-inflammatory properties [10]. Research also suggests that kaempferol contributes to bone health in various models of bone loss and fractures by opposing adipogenesis and inflammation, reducing oxidative stress, and modulating the processes of autophagy in osteoclasts and apoptosis in osteoblasts while promoting osteoblastic autophagy [11].

Caveolin-1 (CAV-1) is a trans-membrane protein found widely across various human tissues. It plays key roles in maintaining cell membranes, cellular signaling, differentiation, proliferation, and migration, as well as regulating apoptosis and autophagy [12]. Previous evidence has documented that CAV-1 expression is upregulated in MSCs undergoing osteogenic differentiation and that downregulating CAV-1 via siRNA can boost MSC proliferation and osteogenic activity [13]. Despite these findings, the precise functions and mechanisms of CAV-1 in the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs are not fully understood, drawing significant scientific interest.

This study aimed to explore the impact of kaempferol, a known bioactive compound, on the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, with a specific focus on how it influences CAV-1 expression. Through detailed experimental approaches including cell culture, pharmacological interventions, molecular biology techniques, and proteomic analysis, this study elucidated the interplay between kaempferol and CAV-1 during osteogenic differentiation, aiding in understanding the biological functions of honokiol and the development of new treatments for bone diseases and gene therapy advancements.

Materials and methods

Network pharmacology analysis

Active components of EUO were identified through the TCMSP platform (http://tcmspw.com/tcmsp.php), with parameters set for oral bioavailability (OB) ≥ 30% and drug-likeness (DL) ≥ 0.18. Using their MOL.ID numbers, the individual herb component targets were searched. The target proteins were then annotated using the Uniprot database (https://www.uniprot.org). Relevant targets were further identified by searching the keywords “osteogenic differentiation” and “osteoarthritis” in the GeneCards (https://www.genecards.org/) and OMIM (https://www.omim.org/) databases.

The Venny software (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) was utilized to identify intersecting targets between the active compounds of EUO and disease-related genes, highlighting potential key targets affecting chondrogenesis. Enrichment analyses for biological processes, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways, were conducted using the open-source bioinformatics software Bioconductor (http://www.bioconductor.org/) within the R environment, employing packages such as clusterProfiler, Stringin, and Pathview. Visualization was facilitated via the bioinformatics cloud platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn).

Intersection genes were uploaded to the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org) to analyze protein-protein interactions (PPI), with results saved in TSV format. These interactions were visualized using Cytoscape (3.9.0). Network topology was analyzed to identify potential core target proteins, defined as those with a degree value exceeding the average. A PPI network of potential core target proteins was constructed using Cytoscape (3.9.0).

Cell culture

Human BMSCs (HUM-iCell-s011) were procured from iCell Bioscience Inc. (Shanghai, China). BMSCs were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment.

Identification of BMSCs

The BMSC medium was refreshed every three days, and cells were passaged upon reaching 80% confluence after multiple rounds of enzymatic harvesting. Third-passage cells were collected to observe BMSC morphology and growth. Flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) was utilized to analyze the expression of positive markers CD29, CD90, CD44 and negative markers CD45 and CD34.

Alcian blue staining

After 21 days of chondrogenic induction of BMSCs, the medium was removed, cells were fixed in 10% formalin for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, and stained with Alcian blue solution (BP-DL241, SenBeiJia Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) for 3 min, followed by another wash with PBS and a further 20–30 min stain. The cells were then rinsed with PBS and examined under a microscope (Olympus, IX50, Tokyo, Japan).

Alizarin red S (ARS) staining

Following 21 days of osteogenic induction, BMSCs were washed three times with PBS, fixed with 2 mL 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed twice, and stained with 2 mL 1% ARS solution (GP1055, Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China) for 10 min. After washing twice with distilled water, cells were examined under a microscope.

Oil red O staining

After 14 days of adipogenic induction, BMSCs were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 2 mL of 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. Post-fixation, cells were stained with 1 mL Oil Red O solution (BP-DL101, SenBeiJia Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China) for 30 min, rinsed four times with PBS, and examined under a microscope.

Cell transfection

Transfection of BMSCs was performed utilizing the CAV-1 overexpression vector (oe-CAV-1, 100 nM) and negative control (sh-NC) (all purchased from HanBiotech, Shanghai, China). In short, cells were plated evenly in a six-well plate and transfected at about 70% confluence using Lipofectamine 2000 in a 2:1 ratio. After 24 h-incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, the stably transfected cells were screened using puromycin for verification of transfection efficiency. Then the cells were collected and incubated with kaempferol (10 µM) before further detection.

MTT assay for cell viability

Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate, with three replicates per sample, and treated with varying concentrations of kaempferol (0 µM, 0.1 µM, 1 µM, 10 µM, 20 µM, and 50 µM) for 24 h. Post-treatment, 10 µl of MTT solution (M6494, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added to each well for reaction for 4–6 h. The medium was then removed, and 100 µl of DMSO (Sigma, USA) was added to each well for termination reaction. Absorbance at 490 nm was measured employing a spectrophotometer (Model 680; Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining

Well-grown BMSCs were seeded in a six-well plate and cultured until approximately 80% confluence. The culture medium was replaced with an osteogenic induction medium, refreshed every two days. After 14 days, cells were fixed with 1 mL 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min, washed with PBS 3 times, and stained for ALP activity, and images were collected under a microscope.

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells employing TRIZOL reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA), and cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (RR047A, Takara, Japan) was used to synthesize the complementary DNA. The PCR products were stored at -80 °C to prevent degradation. RT-qPCR was conducted using SYBR GreenMix (Takara) on an ABI7500 Real-Time PCR system (ABI, Foster City, CA, USA), with data analyzed utilizing the 2−ΔΔCt method [14]. GAPDH was utilized as the normalizer, and primer details for PCR are provided in Table 1.

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed on ice utilizing RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), and protein concentrations were determined employing the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk before incubating with primary antibodies: Collagen I (ab138492, 1:2000, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA), RUNX2 (ab236639, 1:1000, Abcam), OPN (ab214050, 1:1000, Abcam), CAV-1 (ab2910, 1:500, Abcam), and GAPDH (ab181602, 1:1000, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C. Membranes were washed three times with TBST, 10 min each. Secondary antibodies (ab205718 or ab205719, 1:2000, Abcam) were added and incubated at ambient temperature for 1 h, followed by three 10-minute TBST washes. Detection was performed with the help of a chemiluminescence imaging system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed employing GraphPad Prism 7 software and are detailed as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were made utilizing the T-test, while one-way ANOVA was employed for comparisons among multiple groups, with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test used for post-hoc analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Network pharmacology analysis results

The top five active components of EUO are detailed in Fig. 1A. Kaempferol, a major active component of EUO, was found to suppress osteoarthritis [8], but its working mechanism remains to be determined. After removing duplicate targets, 97 potential drug targets were identified. Post-deletion of duplicate genes, 4919 targets associated with OA and osteogenic differentiation were retrieved. Venn diagrams identified 39 intersecting targets between EUO, OA, and osteogenic differentiation (Fig. 1B). The network of active components and targets is illustrated in Fig. 1C. Enrichment analysis of the 39 intersecting genes using GO and KEGG highlighted key biological processes such as epithelial cell proliferation, regulation of epithelial cell proliferation, and cellular response to chemical stress (Fig. 1D), with major pathways including AGE-RAGE signaling in diabetic complications, lipid metabolism and atherosclerosis, and fluid shear stress and atherosclerosis (Fig. 1E).

Isolation and identification of BMSCs

MSCs, due to their self-renewal capability, multilineage differentiation potential, low immunogenicity, and minimally invasive harvest, have emerged as promising candidates for treating OA. The extracted BMSCs were first characterized under a microscope, displaying a typical whirlpool-like arrangement, consistent with BMSC morphology (Fig. 2A). Flow cytometry analysis for surface markers CD29, CD90, CD44 (positive expression) and CD45, CD34 (negative expression) confirmed the identity of BMSCs (Fig. 2B). Tri-lineage differentiation assays showed positive results (Fig. 2C-E). Specifically, the osteogenic differentiation indicated deep red calcium nodules with Alizarin Red staining; adipogenic differentiation showed distinct red lipid droplets with Oil Red O staining; and chondrogenic differentiation revealed light blue cartilage cells and matrix with Alcian Blue staining (Fig. 2C-E). The above findings confirmed the high purity and tri-lineage differentiation potential of our BMSCs.

Effect of kaempferol on osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs

Subsequently, the influence of kaempferol on the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs was examined. MTT assays confirmed that kaempferol was non-toxic to BMSCs within the range of 0.1–10 µM. However, at concentrations above 10 µM, a dose-dependent decrease in cell proliferation was observed (Fig. 3A). ALP staining and ARS staining were implemented to assess osteogenic differentiation, showing that both ALP activity and calcium deposition significantly increased with kaempferol concentration, but the number of osteoblasts unexpectedly decreased following the treatment of higher concentrations (20 and 50 µM) of kaempferol (Fig. 3B-C). The optimal concentration for promoting osteogenic differentiation was determined to be 10 µM, consistent with prior reports [15]. Consequently, this concentration was used in subsequent experiments. Expression of osteogenic markers Collagen I, RUNX2, and OPN was significantly enhanced by treatment with 10 µM kaempferol, as determined by RT-qPCR and Western Blot analyses (Fig. 3D-E). Conclusively, kaempferol significantly promoted the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.

Kaempferol suppressed CAV-1 expression

In the above experiments, it was noted that kaempferol promoted osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, though the molecular mechanisms involved were unclear. The 39 intersecting genes from the network pharmacology analysis were input into the STRING database, using the highest confidence level of 0.900. Visualization and network topology attributes analysis were performed using Cytoscape 3.9.0. The PPI network of core target proteins is shown in Fig. 4A, where CAV-1 was identified as a hub gene linked with EUO, OA, and BMSC osteogenic differentiation. One recent study has shown that silencing CAV-1 can alleviate OA progression in mice by reducing oxidative stress and inflammatory responses in chondrocytes [16]. Here, we found that treatment with 10 µM kaempferol significantly suppressed CAV-1 mRNA and protein expression (Fig. 4B-C). The aforementioned findings uncovered that kaempferol may promote BMSC osteogenic differentiation by inhibiting CAV-1 expression.

Overexpression of CAV-1 reversed the promotive effects of kaempferol on osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs

To validate our findings, the effects of combined treatment with kaempferol and oe-CAV-1 on the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs were examined. The results demonstrated that oe-CAV-1 reversed the promotive effects of kaempferol on CAV-1 expression and osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs (Fig. 5). Specifically, compared to the kaempferol + oe-NC group, the kaempferol + oe-CAV-1 group showed a marked increase in CAV-1 expression and significant reductions in ALP activity and calcium deposition as well as the expression of osteogenic differentiation markers, Collagen I, RUNX2, and OPN (Fig. 5A-F). These results verified that overexpression of CAV-1 reversed the stimulatory effects of kaempferol on osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs.

Discussion

Extensive research suggests that BMSCs play a critical role in regenerating cartilage erosion in OA, not only by transforming into chondrocytes but also by influencing the dynamics of subchondral bone [17]. However, the challenging microenvironment of OA, marked by inflammation and oxidative stress, diminishes BMSCs’ viability. Additionally, elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines impede the osteogenic potential of BMSCs [18]. Given these challenges, strategies that enhance the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and bolster their survival under adverse conditions are crucial for effective tissue repair and regeneration. The network pharmacology analysis in this study demonstrated the potential drug targets of EUO, which were analyzed for overlaps with 4919 targets associated with OA and osteogenic differentiation. Furthermore, GO and KEGG enrichment analysis demonstrated key biological functions of the intersecting genes. Based on this information, we delved into one highly studied active component of EUO, kaempferol, and demonstrated that kaempferol could lower CAV-1 expression to facilitate the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs.

In our study, we first assessed the cytotoxic effects of kaempferol on BMSCs using the MTT assay, which indicated a dose-dependent decrease in cell proliferation at concentrations exceeding 10 µM. This finding led us to select 10 µM as the optimal dose for promoting osteoblast differentiation, which was evident from enhanced ALP activity and calcium deposition. Notably, this concentration also significantly boosted the expression of key osteogenic markers such as Collagen I, RUNX2, and OPN. BMSCs are pivotal in bone metabolism due to their potent self-renewal capacity and ability to differentiate into various cell types including osteoblasts, which are essential for bone repair in osteonecrosis [19]. Their utility is further highlighted in bone tissue engineering, where their immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties help modulate the local microenvironment, enhancing tissue repair [20]. Our findings resonate with prior studies that highlight kaempferol’s ability to mediate osteogenic and chondrogenic differentiation. For example, it upregulates genes associated with cartilage and bone formation, including collagen type II alpha1, cartilage oligomeric matrix protein, fibromodulin, SOX9, ALP, core-binding factor alpha 1, OPN, and RUNX2 [21]. Other studies corroborate the osteogenic benefits of kaempferol, emphasizing its role in enhancing mineralization, increasing ALP activity, and upregulating osteogenic markers such as Runx-2 and Osterix, while also influencing the phosphorylation of signaling proteins crucial for cellular growth and differentiation [22]. Similar results have also been confirmed in previous research on kaempferol, indicating that the optimal concentration of kaempferol for promoting osteoblast differentiation is 10 µM [15]. By integrating these insights, we underline the therapeutic potential of kaempferol, not only as a promoter of osteogenic differentiation but also as a modulator of the cellular microenvironment, suggesting broad applications in treating bone-related disorders. A recent study using network pharmacology analysis and experimental validation identified 54 potential therapeutic targets for kaempferol in osteoporosis treatment [23]. However, the focus of this study was to explore the molecular mechanisms of kaempferol on the osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs, therefore more attention of this study was paid to determining the downstream target of kaempferol using in vitro experiments.

Next, our investigation into the molecular mechanisms revealed that kaempferol likely enhanced osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs by downregulating CAV-1 expression. Specifically, reducing CAV-1 expression appears to facilitate this differentiation, while the overexpression of CAV-1 tends to negate the osteogenic effects of kaempferol. This dynamic underscores the role of caveolin-1 in cellular processes related to bone health, as CAV-1 has been previously noted for its involvement in osteoarthritis pathogenesis through its regulatory effects on articular chondrocytes [24]. Additionally, experiments using siRNA to knock down CAV-1 in stromal cells have shown an increase in alkaline phosphatase protein and the expression of osteogenic markers such as osterix and Runx2, aligning with osteoblast differentiation [25]. Furthermore, the silencing of CAV-1 has been associated with mitigated osteoarthritis progression in mice by reducing both oxidative stress and inflammatory responses within chondrocytes [16]. Previous evidence has noted that the functional characteristics of endothelial caveolae, including their role in the uptake of persistent organic pollutants and subsequent inflammatory response, can be influenced by dietary components like lipids and polyphenols. These nutrients can alter the activation of signaling proteins associated with caveolae [26]. Kaempferol, a flavonol, exhibits multiple osteogenic biological activities, including inhibiting osteoclast bone resorption and promoting differentiation and mineralization in preosteoblasts [27]. Considering these findings, we hypothesize that CAV-1 significantly influences the impact of kaempferol on osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs.

In conclusion, kaempferol effectively enhances osteogenic differentiation in BMSCs by inhibiting the expression of CAV-1, presenting a novel potential therapeutic for OA and other bone diseases. Future studies could further explore the effects of kaempferol under dynamic conditions and consider its impact on other cell types, as well as the specific regulatory mechanisms of CAV-1 in these processes. Additionally, broader clinical trials are essential to confirm the safety and efficacy of kaempferol, facilitating its application in medical practice.
