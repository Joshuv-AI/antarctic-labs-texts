Utility and applications of high-resolution anterior segment optical coherence tomography in unraveling the diagnosis of isolated bilateral corneal haze in children
Authors: Sunita Chaurasia, Muralidhar Ramappa
Published: 2026

Anterior segment optical coherence tomography (OCT) is an imaging system that utilizes low-coherence interferometry to delineate the characteristics of the biological tissues.[1] The high-resolution OCT system (HR-OCT) characterizes the individual layers of the cornea and therefore offers the opportunity to identify the abnormalities in various corneal diseases.[2] In a normal cornea, the first thin band of hyperreflectivity is the normal tear film. The HR-OCT imaging in a normal cornea has a typical and consistent pattern. The corneal epithelium is seen as a dark homogeneous, hyporeflective band with a normal thickness ranging from 50 to 60 microns. The Bowman’s layer is seen as a linear structure with reflectivity similar to the underlying stroma which has a variable thickness of 400–500 microns. The Descemet membrane endothelial complex is seen as a thin, hyperreflective line of 10–15 microns thickness.

There are several etiological causes of bilateral corneal haze or opacification at birth or in early childhood such as the developmental anomalies of the anterior segment, congenital glaucoma, birth trauma, systemic storage diseases, and various corneal dystrophies.[3] While the complete list of corneal opacification or haze in pediatric age can be exhaustive having a multitude of differentials, the commonly encountered conditions with isolated bilateral corneal haze in young children include congenital hereditary endothelial dystrophy (CHED), congenital hereditary stromal dystrophy (CHSD), posterior polymorphous corneal dystrophy, mucopolysaccharidosis (MPS), and mucolipidosis. Children afflicted with any of these conditions can present in the clinic at a varied stage and severity of the disease. Several associated clinical features may help in differentiating the varied clinical entities such as the age of onset and course of progression, associated systemic features, family history, symptoms, and coexisting corneal features such as shape and pachymetry. However, the accurate diagnosis of the exact clinical entity in some patients may still be challenging. Often, the correct diagnosis may need genetic testing, which may not be always possible in view of the costs involved in genetic tests and dissent of the patient’s family members to undergo genetic evaluation. Most of the above conditions, corneal dystrophy or metabolic disorder, exhibit a unique optical pattern on OCT imaging reinforcing the crucial role of HR-OCT as an important adjunct in making a sound clinical diagnosis. In this report, we highlight the distinct features noted on HR-anterior segment OCT that additionally assist in making an accurate diagnosis in children with isolated bilateral cornea haze, thereby helping in formulating the exact plan of the management in such eyes. CHED presents at birth or in early infancy as a grayish-white corneal haze. Depending on the age of presentation, severity of the disease, and intraocular pressure (IOP), HR-OCT reveals varied characteristics.[4] In most cases at an early stage of the disease and milder severity, the epithelium, stroma, and posterior membranes are distinctly visualized with increased overall pachymetry [Fig. 1a and b]. The abnormal thickening of the Descemet membrane is seen as a thin area of hyporeflectivity. In chronic stages of the disease, as seen in older age children, the disruption of Bowman’s layer is remarkably seen as hyperreflectivity at the level of subepithelium [Fig. 1c and d]. As the disease progresses and frank endothelial decompensation ensues, epithelial edema becomes apparent with bullae, which is seen as hyperreflective changes at the level of epithelium. Elevated IOP can also cause transient hyperreflectivity at the epithelial level [Fig. 1e and f] and hence should be differentiated from endothelial decompensation which is seen as a persistent hyperreflectivity sometimes associated with secondary changes [Fig. 1g and h].

CHSD presents with early onset and diffuse stromal haze.[5] The condition being primarily a stromal disease, the HR-OCT shows normal epithelium with dense, homogeneous stromal hyperreflectivity merging into the posterior membrane, often obscuring the Descemet membrane complex [Fig. 2a and b]. In contrast to CHED, hyperreflectivity is more homogeneous within the stroma and pachymetry may be often normal or marginally thicker.

PPCD presents with an asymmetrical, patchy, or nonhomogeneous haze associated with vesicular or band-like endothelial changes.[6] The HR-OCT may reveal characteristic posterior stromal hyperreflectivity and irregularities at the posterior membrane level with an altered posterior contour [Fig. 3a and b].

MPS commonly present in the first decade of life with progressive visual decline and uniform dense grayish-white stromal haze.[7] The HR-OCT demonstrates hyperreflectivity predominantly in the midstromal region, with relative sparing of the epithelial and posterior layers of the cornea [Fig. 4a and b]. These findings correlate with glycosaminoglycan deposition and can precede overt systemic diagnoses.

Mucolipidosis presents with a milder haze which is predominantly in the anterior layer. HR-OCT showed marked epithelial hyperreflectivity with a relatively clear stroma [Fig. 5a and b].[8]

A close mimicker of all the above noninflammatory conditions, which should be ruled out in young children with bilateral corneal haze/ cloudiness, is primary congenital glaucoma. Clinical signs and symptoms typically include bluish corneal discoloration, photophobia, tearing, Haab striae, increased corneal diameter, limbal stretching, flat corneal contour, and scleral thinning. These features, along with increased IOP and axial length, help in distinguishing the condition from the other corneal conditions.

Although genetic testing remains the definitive modality for confirming diagnosis of isolated bilateral corneal haze in children, its applications may be limited in many parts of the world due to constraints in accessibility, affordability, and patients’ acceptability to undergo testing. The HR-OCT offers an additional practical surrogate critical insights into diagnosis, especially when interpreted in conjunction with age of onset, family history, ocular symptoms, systemic manifestations, corneal biometry, visual acuity, and IOP.
