Raman Microspectroscopy for Real-Time Structure Indicator in Ultrafast Laser Writing
Xingrui Cheng, Eugenio Picheo, Zhixin Chen, Martin J. Booth, Patrick S. Salter, Álvaro Fernández-Galiana

Abstract
Raman microspectroscopy provides non-destructive, structure-specific contrast suited to in situ monitoring of femtosecond (fs) laser fabrication processes. In this work, we introduce a sample-agnostic Raman workflow that screens candidate spectral indicators using drift-resistant, self-normalized metrics and, where appropriate, label-free hyperspectral unmixing to extract endmembers and validate metric selection. Using diamond as an example, hyperspectral maps and electrical tests show that depletion of the 1332 cm -1 sp3 line is a monotonic, robust predictor of resistance—counterintuitively outperforming sp2-based measures often assumed optimal. This enables a practical implementation: a single narrowband channel at the selected marker provides real-time feedback without full-spectrum acquisition. Because the selection hinges on general band definitions and functional validation rather than diamond-specific physics, the approach translates Raman phase information directly into device specifications and is transferable to other Raman-active hosts for in-line quality control of laser-written structures.



I
Introduction

Femtosecond laser writing enables direct, three-dimensional patterning of embedded features in transparent hosts via nonlinear absorption—multi-phonon ionization (MPI) and Zener breakdown—that initiates localized structural transformation
Jeschke et al. 1999
;
Ashikkalieva et al. 2016
;
Kononenko et al. 2016
. These processes permit buried and surface carbon wires and electrode architectures with arbitrary geometry for electrically conductive devices
Sun et al. 2014
, including radiation detectors
Lagomarsino et al. 2013
. Practical subsurface fabrication benefits from pre-compensating high-index–induced spherical aberration using adaptive optics such as a spatial light modulator (SLM) to restore a tight focal volume
Simmonds et al. 2011
;
Salter and Booth 2019
. While the resulting microstructures have been examined by destructive or ex-situ methods—TEM
Ashikkalieva et al. 2019
;
Salter et al. 2024
, SEM
Ashikkalieva et al. 2016
, and X-ray diffraction microscopy (XRD)
Yin et al. 2024
—their complexity and inability to operate in situ limit process monitoring.

Raman microspectroscopy is a vibrational spectroscopic technique based on the inelastic scattering of monochromatic light. It has been extensively used for non-destructive identification, structural characterization, and monitoring of chemical and physical properties across materials science, chemistry, and biology
Fernández-Galiana et al. 2024
. In carbon systems in particular, Raman can distinguish bonding configurations, crystallinity, and local strain, making it suitable for non-invasive, in situ, spatially resolved monitoring during fabrication
Sotillo et al. 2018
.

To obtain robust, comparable indicators from spectra acquired across fields of view and samples, previous studies have adopted a deconvolution and normalization strategy that favors self-referenced, phase-aware metrics over single-peak intensities
Fernández-Galiana et al. 2024
. Absolute intensities are sensitive to power drift, focus/NA mismatch, baseline fluorescence, and strain; by contrast, combining phase-informative windows and leveraging label-free spectral deconvolution stabilizes the readout and enables cross-instrument comparison. In practice, we explicitly assign bands, normalize to pristine-material pixels, and compute complementary indicators of conversion (ratios and depletion measures defined below), converting hyperspectral maps into quantitative metrics suitable for in situ monitoring
Sotillo et al. 2018
.

Diamond is an instructive case for Raman-guided fs fabrication. As sp3-bonded carbon, it is an electrical insulator with exceptional hardness, high thermal conductivity, a wide bandgap, and chemical robustness, enabling applications in high-power electronics, photonics, and radiation detection. Point defects such as nitrogen vacancy centers further extend functionality to quantum information and nanoscale sensing
Hanson and Awschalom 2008
;
Aharonovich et al. 2016
;
Rondin et al. 2014
;
Maletinsky et al. 2012
. The broader carbon design space—sp2-bonded forms (graphite, graphene, carbon nanotubes) with delocalized
\pi
electrons for high electrical/thermal conductivity
Novoselov et al. 2004
;
Avouris et al. 2007
, allotropic variants such as hexagonal diamond (lonsdaleite)
Hanneman et al. 1967
, and mixed-phase nanocomposites such as ’diaphite’
Salter et al. 2024
;
Németh et al. 2020a
;
Németh et al. 2020b
—underscores the value of a phase-resolved optical readout. Although diamond is transparent at 520 nm (2.38 eV) due to its 5.47 eV bandgap, which prevents linear photon absorption from the writing laser, the fabrication process instead relies on nonlinear absorption involving multi-phonon ionization (MPI) and Zener breakdown, initiating a confined sp3 to sp2 phase transition
Jeschke et al. 1999
;
Ashikkalieva et al. 2016
;
Kononenko et al. 2016
, forming local graphitized regions with device-relevant geometries.

Here, we use Raman microspectroscopy to systematically assess fs-laser-written graphitic electrodes in diamond. Leveraging these vibrational modes, we perform direct mapping of surface graphitization through explicit band assignment and label-free spectral deconvolution, and we correlate the spectral signature of the electrodes, specifically the relative intensities of the sp2 band, sp3 line and sp2 to sp3 ratio, with measured electrical conductivity. In doing so, we demonstrate a practical pathway to in-line process monitoring and optimization for laser-written diamond electrodes.

1
Spectral characterization of Laser-written Electrodes

As shown in Figure
1
, one of the limitations of both brightfield microscopy and broadband PL imaging is that despite being good techniques for qualitative assessment of the fabrication they present limitations when used to provide quantitative information. As discussed in Supplementary Notes 3 and 4, PL could in principle provide quantitative evaluation of sp3 to sp2 conversion but, due to the broad integration, is very sensitive to other, unrelated phenomena, reducing quantitative fidelity. For this reason, we assessed the suitability of Raman spectroscopy for this task.

1.2
Multi-band characterization

Laser-written tracks in diamond contain both unconverted sp3-bonded lattice and newly formed sp2-bonded carbon. Within a confocal voxel, these phases typically coexist as sub-micron domains, so a single Raman spectrum is an average over a heterogeneous local volume. In the previous section we showed that the degree of graphitization can be inferred from the intensity of the sp3 line. However, relying on a single peak can be fragile in practice because the apparent intensity can be influenced by various factors such as laser power fluctuations, defocusing, local topography and debris, baseline fluorescence, or strain-induced shifts/broadening.

To mitigate these effects, ratio metrics that combine several informative bands are usually preferred over single-peak readouts. In our case the most natural choice is to use the sp2/sp3 ratio, containing the information carried by the G/D complex and the 1332 cm
-1
diamond line. Figure
3
illustrates this for a representative wire written at 50
\mu
m s
-1
: a 2D map of the ratio
\rho=I_{1332}/I_{1580}
provides robust, self-normalized contrast across the field, enabling empirical thresholds that segment the scene into non-graphitized, partially graphitized, and fully graphitized classes. Class-averaged spectra make the assignments transparent: pristine regions show a narrow sp3 line accompanied by NV
0
fluorescence, partially graphitized pixels exhibit concurrent sp3 attenuation and the emergence of D/G features, and fully graphitized regions are dominated by broad D/G bands with a strongly depleted 1332 cm
-1
line.

While ratio maps remove much of the acquisition variability, they still require manual selection of specific bands. Hyperspectral unmixing offers a label-free alternative that exploits the entire spectrum and returns per-pixel composition in the form of abundances.
As described in
IV
Materials and Methods
, endmembers are extracted directly from the data using well established unmixing methods, and abundances are then estimated per pixel, which enables a direct fractional interpretation without the need for external references.

Applying this pipeline over a broad region of interest (1000–1800cm
-1
) yields endmembers that, while not identical to the actual reference spectra presented in Figure
2
), preserve the expected relevant features: the diamond-associated endmember is characterised by a sharp 1332 cm
-1
line and the NV
0
contribution in the window, whereas the graphite-associated endmember exhibits a broad D/G complex with a suppressed diamond line. Importantly, this decomposition is entirely computational and label-free. The resulting abundance maps closely track the simpler sp3-window intensity maps, which could be anticipated given that the 1332 cm
-1
line is the most intense and spatially specific feature, but provide a unified, quantitative representation across the whole spectrum.

To test sensitivity to the dominant diamond line, we repeated the analysis after excluding 1332 cm
-1
(spectral ROI = 1345–1800 cm
-1
). In this case, the diamond-associated endmember is defined primarily by the NV
0
contribution within the spectral ROI, while the complementary endmember remains the broad G/D complex. The corresponding abundance maps retain the expected spatial structure but exhibit lower signal-to-noise, as anticipated when the most intense, phase-specific line is removed. This cropped-ROI approach is nevertheless useful in scenarios where the diamond line saturates the detector, where strong local strain or temperature gradients shift or distort the 1332 cm
-1
feature beyond a fixed window, or when one wishes to decouple the analysis from sp3-dominated contrast (e.g. to emphasize changes within the sp2 manifold).

Overall, the unmixing results reinforce the interpretation from ratio maps while removing the need to hand-pick bands: knowing only that two materials are present, the algorithm discovers representative endmembers and delivers per-pixel abundance maps that agree with the PL/Raman contrasts. These abundance maps offer a compact, quantitative metric for automated thresholding and closed-loop control (e.g. stop conditions based on sp3 depletion or sp2 abundance). Yet, in the particular case of monitoring graphitization in diamond, where the bands of interest are few and well known, hyperspectral unmixing does not provide a significant performance advantage over band-specific metrics.

Raman Monitoring of Electrical Performance

Ultimately, the optimal feedback signal for in-situ monitoring of fabrication should be directly tied to the functional performance of the resulting devices. For graphite electrodes on diamond, this performance is best represented by the conductivity of the laser-written features. To this end, the electrical conductivity of the pads and wires was measured using a custom setup (see
2

Figure

presents our findings and consolidates the link between laser scan speed, local bonding state, and device-level conductivity of the laser-written features. Figure
4

\pm 500

\mu

100~\mu

for the wire at the same speed. The lower pad resistance reflects its larger cross-section and additional over-writing during fabrication, which further graphitizes the diamond and increases the graphite fraction. Figure
4

\pm 500

In Figure
4

As the scan speed increases, the resistance rises concurrently with the normalized sp3 intensity, indicating reduced graphitization and weakened conductive pathways. For wires (Figure
4
c), the normalized sp2 intensity increases significantly as the scan speed decreases from high to intermediate values, corresponding to a sharp drop in resistance. However, once the sp3 contribution is strongly diminished (deeply graphitized regime), sp2 intensity alone becomes a less reliable measure of further conductivity enhancement. Since the sp2 intensity rises as the sp3 intensity decreases with scan speed, their ratio mirrors these trends. Pads show the same qualitative behavior (Figure
4

Collectively, these data establish Raman-derived sp2/sp3 metrics as a non-contact, quantitative proxy for electrical performance and a practical handle for in-situ process control during fs-laser fabrication.

3
Discussion

We have evaluated the performance of three characterization methods (optical micrography, PL mapping, and Raman microspectroscopy) for phase transformation from insulating sp3-bonded lattice to conductive sp2-bonded carbon, an ultrafast phase change that has been extensively studied in both surface and bulk geometries
Kononenko et al. 2008
;
Sun et al. 2014
;
Ali et al. 2022
. Among these characterization methods, only Raman provides a direct, quantitative, phase-specific measurement of the bonding state.

As shown in Figure
2
, the sp3 and sp2 Raman features evolve with fabrication conditions and degree of graphitization. Reducing the scan speed, and thus increasing the energy deposited per unit length, leads to a steady decrease in sp3 intensity and a general increase in sp2 intensity, consistent with progressive phase conversion from diamond to graphitic carbon. At the slowest speeds, however, the sp2 intensity reaches a maximum and then declines despite continued sp3 depletion, a behavior also reported in other ultrafast graphitization studies
Ali et al. 2022
;
Khomich et al. 2023
.

This counter-intuitive reduction in sp2 intensity can be interpreted within the ‘amorphization trajectory’ framework of Ferrari et al.
Ferrari and Robertson 2000
;
Ferrari and Robertson 2004
, wherein progressive disorder drives a transition from nanocrystalline graphite to amorphous sp2-rich carbon and, ultimately, to highly disordered or tetrahedral amorphous forms. In this regime, shrinking and fragmenting sp2 clusters suppress well-defined Raman modes, so the apparent sp2 signal diminishes even as the actual sp2 bonding fraction remains high.
At intermediate doses, ordered sp2 clusters grow, strengthening the D and G bands. At extreme doses, however, high defect densities and reduced cluster sizes suppress well-defined vibrational modes, broadening and merging the D and G peaks into a low-contrast background. The resulting spectral degradation reduces the apparent sp2 Raman intensity, even when the actual sp2 bonding fraction remains high. Under such high-dose conditions, sp3 depletion provides a more reliable metric for tracking graphitization progression, as it decreases monotonically with increasing laser dose and is insensitive to spectral contrast loss
Ali et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2000
.

The stronger sp3 depletion observed for pads can be interpreted by stress-assisted graphitization during overwriting. Lattice mismatch between diamond and graphite generates tensile stress in the transformed surface layer and, crucially, in-plane compressive stress in the adjacent diamond
Wang et al. 2000
. Since the graphitization barrier decreases under lattice contraction, compression promotes earlier conversion as the lattice temperature rises. In pad writing, each new pass overlaps previously graphitized regions, introducing a local compressive field in neighboring diamond and enhancing the conversion rate. This local compressive strain field during overwriting explains the greater sp3 depletion and lower resistance of pads relative to single-pass wires at identical scan speeds, with the Raman shift direction matching established stress coefficients for diamond (blue shift in wavenumber)
Grimsditch and Ramdas 1975
;
Zaitsev 2001
;
Orwa et al. 2000
(see Supplementary Note 6).

As presented in Figure
3
, hyperspectral unmixing could also serve as a label-free, instrument-agnostic tool for generation of phase-fraction maps from 2D Raman data. The unmixing technique agrees with simpler ratio metrics while exploiting the full spectrum. By discovering two data-driven endmembers (ascribed to diamond and graphite phases) and enforcing non-negativity/sum-to-one constraints, the linear mixing model yields abundances that are directly interpretable as local composition and are robust to power drift, focus variations, and baseline fluorescence, issues that can affect single-band readouts. The approach is not without limits: severe disorder, stress-induced line shifts, or strong fluorescence can deviate from strict linearity and slightly bias abundance estimates.

Electrical measurements shown in Figure
4
reveal ohmic behavior for both pads and wires, with pads exhibiting systematically lower resistance due to their larger cross-section and cumulative overwriting during fabrication. Notably, the correlation between Raman metrics and resistance highlights a counter-intuitive result: sp3 depletion is a more robust indicator of electrical performance than the absolute sp2 signal. This distinction likely arises because sp3 suppression directly reflects the fraction of diamond lattice converted, irrespective of the ordering of the resulting carbon, whereas sp2 intensity is sensitive to disorder and may be suppressed even in highly conductive, amorphous-rich networks. Similar conclusions have been drawn in buried-wire studies where the attenuation of the diamond peak correlated linearly with conductive cross-section. Moreover, as shown in Figure
1
, the superior SNR of the sp3 line relative to the sp2 band makes sp2-based metrics inherently more susceptible to broadband background from non-graphitic damage (see Supplementary Note 2). This is also true for full-spectrum based metrics, such as hyperspectral unmixing (see Supplementary Note 8).

From a metrology standpoint, this insight opens a streamlined path to in-situ control. In our experiments, one-second integrations of the diamond peak were sufficient to resolve sp3 depletion without sample cleaning. This can be further aided by the incidental removal of surface debris by the 532 nm probe laser (see Supplementary Note 1) which also reduces the masking effect of debris that might affect the fabrication.

III
Conclusion and Outlook

Femtosecond laser writing in diamond produces conductive carbon features whose performance can be anticipated from phase-specific Raman spectral features. We have provided two-dimensional Raman mapping of laser-written surface structures under different fabrication conditions. Overall, our findings show that the sp2/sp3 intensity ratio provides a robust and reliable indicator of graphitization progression. Because it is derived from relative band intensities, this ratio is largely insensitive to variations in laser power and other experimental fluctuations. However, under high-dose irradiation conditions, monitoring sp3 depletion becomes a more consistent metric, as it decreases monotonically with increasing laser dose and remains unaffected by the loss of spectral contrast that accompanies extreme structural disorder
Ali et al. 2022
;
Wang et al. 2000
.

To make the readout robust, we complemented simple intensity ratios with blind hyperspectral unmixing performed without any pre-labelling. The algorithm discovers two endmembers (sp3-like and sp2-like) directly from the data and returns per-pixel abundances that agree with PL/Raman contrast while exploiting the full spectrum. This analysis also shows that comparing the background sp3 (pristine diamond) to the written sp2 provides the highest-SNR indicator of conversion and is therefore ideal for in-situ characterization.
This means that a full spectrometer may not be necessary for feedback: the 1332 cm
-1
line could be monitored using narrowband filtering and a CCD or APD, reducing system complexity and acquisition time. By establishing a depletion threshold corresponding to the target resistance, the fabrication process could be terminated or parameters adjusted in real time. Closed-loop optimization approaches, potentially incorporating machine learning for rapid spectral classification
Wahab et al. 2020
, could then maintain consistent electrical performance across large-area, multi-feature devices.

Beyond diamond and electrical performance, the same characteisation framework can be optimized and implemented in other materials by (a) acquiring (offline) representative sample datasets under the target fabrication conditions and apply the same label-free post-processing (ratio maps and blind unmixing) to discover robust, instrument-agnostic indicators and setpoints (e.g., depletion of a host phonon, bounds on D/G, endmember abundances); and (b) deploy those indicators as real-time monitors during fabrication using narrowband detection or lightweight spectral sampling. This strategy generalises as a local phase/composition sentinel across laser fabrication processes: (i) waveguide manufacturing in glasses and wide-bandgap crystals, where Raman-visible densification/strain fingerprints correlate with refractive-index change and loss
Li et al. 2008
;
Little et al. 2010
; (ii) laser-induced graphene on polymers/resists
Wang et al. 2025
, where D/G evolution guides sheet resistance and uniformity; (iii) embedded heaters/electrodes in SiC, sapphire, silica, and ceramics, where host phonons and carbon signatures define safe process windows
Choi et al. 2016
;
Naseri et al. 2022
; and (iv) phase-specific transformations in oxides and bio-relevant substrates, where a Raman marker provides a chemically selective stop condition
Shaikh et al. 2017
.

In summary, hyperspectral Raman provides a phase‐specific, quantitative readout that links fabrication conditions to device performance during ultrafast laser writing in diamond. Across pads and wires, we find that depletion of the diamond sp3 line at 1332 cm
-1
is a more reliable predictor of conductivity than absolute sp2 intensity, which can diminish at high disorder. A simple, self‐referenced sp3 metric, benchmarked to the local pristine background, therefore offers a robust in situ indicator, while a ratio-based metric, such as sp2-to-sp3 ratio offers better robustness to power, focus, and collection variations. Combined with label‐free unmixing to extract per‐pixel phase abundances in case of post-fabrication mapping, this framework enables calibrated thresholds for stop/adjust decisions and is readily implemented with narrowband detection for real‐time process control. Because the indicators are phase‐agnostic and instrument‐agnostic, the approach generalizes to other hosts and functionalities (e.g., waveguides, embedded heaters/electrodes, laser‐induced graphene), providing a practical route toward specification‐driven fs‐laser microfabrication.

Materials and Methods

1
Electrode fabrication

A single-crystal, optical-grade
\langle 100\rangle
diamond grown by chemical-vapor deposition (Element Six; nominal N concentration
<\!1
ppm) served as the substrate for this study.
Surface graphitic pads and wires were inscribed with a fs laser operating at 520 nm (
\tau_{\text{p}}=400
fs,
f_{\text{rep}}=10

\mathrm{NA}=0.50

Wire electrodes were written in a single pass under continuous stage translation (no overwriting). Successive pulses partially overlapped to form a continuous track; at fixed
f_{\text{rep}}
the pulse-to-pulse separation was set by the scan speed. Pad electrodes were produced by stacking parallel wires with a constant 0.5
\mu
m pitch, maintained for all scan speeds.

2
Electrical conductivity measurement

The resistance of laser-written pads and wires was determined from two-probe current–voltage (I–V) measurements using a low-noise transimpedance amplifier (DLPCA-200, FEMTO) together with a real-time voltage source and data-acquisition controller (ADwin-Gold II, Jäger). Electrical contact was made with two micromanipulated Pt probes. For pad devices, the probes contacted opposite ends of each laser-written pad. For wire devices, both termini were first connected to laser-written square pads (50
\mu
m
\times
50
\mu
m, written at 100
\mu
m s
-1
), and the probes landed on these pads to standardize the contact area. The ADwin generated quasi-static voltage sweeps within the ohmic window (typically
\pm 500
mV) and synchronously digitized the DLPCA-200 output.

3
Brightfield imaging

Optical micrographs were captured with an integrated transmission optical microscope. A red-LED array situated under the sample serves as illumination source and the images are captured on a CCD camera (EC650, Prosilica Inc.).

4
Confocal Imaging

Our fabrication setup has an integrated custom confocal microscope that can be simultaneously utilized for photoluminescence and Raman spectral collection. Using a 532 nm continuous-wave excitation laser (Cobolt Samba 150mW) and a fast steering mirror (FSM-300, Newport) relayed via a 4f into the back aperture of the system’s objective, 2D PL and RS maps can be collected. Notably, in this study, the 2D maps were collected post-fabrication utilising a higher numerical aperture objective (Olympus PlanApo 0.95NA, 60x).

Both photoluminescence and Raman spectra were collected simulataneously employing 30 mW of laser excitation. The samples were scanned with a 500nm pixel-to-pixel lateral displacement and a 1 second exposure time. The collected signal was split 99:1 between the Raman and the photoluminescence collection paths. For the latter, signal was collected via a single photon avalanche detector (SPCM-AQRH-14-FC, Excelitas Inc.). Given that the collection path of our confocal setup features a 550 nm long-pass dichroic, and that the SPAD quantum efficiency drops significantly after 850 nm, the PL collection window can be considered 550-850 nm. For the Raman collection path, scattered light was dispersed by a 1200 lines mm
-1
blazed spectrograph (SpectraPro HRS-1200) and detected on a deep-cooled CCD camera (PIXIS 100, Princeton Instruments).

6
Spectral Unmixing

In this work we adopt the widely used
linear mixing model
(LMM), in which each spectrum
x\in\mathbb{R}^{b}
is expressed as a linear combination of
n
endmember spectra
m_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{b}
:

x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}m_{i}+\varepsilon

(1)

where
\alpha_{i}
denotes the abundance of the
i
-th endmember, and .

Endmember identification.

To identify the endmember spectra from the data, we employed two well-established blind unmixing algorithms (a)
Vertex Component Analysis (VCA)

Nascimento and Dias 2005
, a fast geometrical method that projects the dataset along random directions to iteratively identify extreme points of the spectral simplex, which serve as candidate endmembers; and (2)
N-FINDR

Winter 1999
, an algorithm that searches for the set of endmembers maximising the volume of the simplex formed by candidate spectra, under the assumption that pure pixels are present in the dataset. Both algorithms require specifying the number of endmembers in advance. Here, we constrained the problem to two endmembers (sp2 and sp3), which reflects the known physics of the system and avoids overfitting.

Abundance estimation.

Once endmembers are identified, abundances can computed by solving the linear inverse problem under different constraints: (a)
Fully Constrained Least Squares (FCLS)

Heinz et al. 2001
: enforces both non-negativity (
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
), and the sum-to-one constraint (
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}=1
), ensuring abundances can be interpreted as fractions; and (b)
Non-Negative Least Squares (NNLS)

Lawson and Hanson 1995
: relaxes the sum-to-one condition but maintains non-negativity, which can be useful when baseline variations are present. As shown in Figure
3
, the abundance maps at 50
\mu
m/s, the pristine diamond region does not show an abundance of exactly 0 for the graphite endmember and 1 for the diamond endmember. This is expected since the endmembers are extracted across all wire maps, focus and intensity differences between datasets also shift the estimated simplex (see Supplementary Note 8). The result is a small residual assignment of sp2 abundance even in pristine regions. Importantly, the maps still provide clear contrast: pristine areas are dominated by the sp3 endmember, while graphitized tracks are dominated by sp2, confirming the physical validity of the decomposition.

Limits of linear mixing model.

Raman scattering is, to first order, linear in the number of scatterers, so spatial coexistence of sp3 and sp2 domains within the confocal volume leads to additive spectra, which the LMM captures. Moreover, our maps are recorded on polished surfaces with modest absorption at 532 nm outside the most heavily graphitized regions, reducing multiple scattering and re-absorption that could induce non-linear mixing. That said, three effects can challenge strict linearity: (i) strong broadband fluorescence in highly damaged zones, (ii) self-absorption and re-emission in thick, strongly absorbing tracks, and (iii) spectral distortions from stress/strain gradients that shift or broaden peaks in a way not representable by a fixed endmember. In such cases,
non-linear
or
bilinear
models (e.g., polynomial post–nonlinear mixing, kernel-based unmixing, or autoencoder-based unmixing
Georgiev et al. 2024a
) may yield incremental gains, at the cost of additional parameters and reduced interpretability.

Implementation.

We conducted the hyperspectral unmixing analysis with Python using the RamanSPy package
Georgiev et al. 2024b
.

Supplementary Note 8: Hyperspectral unmixing

We performed hyperspectral unmixing with a linear mixing model using VCA for endmember extraction and FCLS for abundance estimation. As shown in Supplementary Figure
16
, the unmixing results consistently show progressive loss of the sp
3
-like component with decreasing scan speed, while the sp
2
-like component increases and eventually saturates. This reinforces the main conclusion that sp
3
depletion provides a reliable, monotonic indicator of graphitization. Hyperspectral unmixing is a powerful technique that leverages the full spectrum and does not require pre-defined labels. However, it is sensitive to the presence of noise or species not considered in the analysis. As shown in Supplementary Figure
16
a, the presence of the broadband emission damage shell when fabricating at intermediate speeds is mislabeled as being a mixture of diamond and graphite phases.

Supplementary Figure
17
presents the endmembers extracted using different unmixing techniques and spectral regions of interest, exhibiting the variance that these endmembers can have. This behavior is expected: VCA and N-FINDR are geometric algorithms that approximate the data as lying within a convex simplex. They perform best when pixels lie close to the true extremes, but in practice the confocal voxel contains mixtures, variable SNR, NV
0
fluorescence, and stress-induced peak shifts. The algorithms therefore identify different “extreme” representatives of the sp
3
-like and sp
2
-like responses, leading to small method-dependent variations that do not alter the physical interpretation. Indeed, the endmembers preserve the spectral features of their associated spectra, that is, the sp3 peak and NV0 band for the endmember associated to diamond, and the G band and, to lesser extent, the D band for the one associated to graphite.

Notably, for the ROI including the sp3 peak, the large dynamic range of this feature amplifies small fluctuations which can distort the convex geometry and bias endmember selection. Excluding this line (ROI 1345–1800 cm
-1
) reduces leverage and produces noisier but qualitatively similar maps.

It is important to distinguish geometric unmixing from PCA. VCA and N-FINDR seek spectra at the boundaries of the data cloud that can be interpreted as approximate endmembers, whereas PCA decomposes variance into orthogonal components that need not resemble real spectra and can take negative values. PCA is valuable for denoising and diagnostics, but its components cannot be interpreted as abundances.
