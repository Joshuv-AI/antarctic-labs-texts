Garside shadows and biautomatic structures in Coxeter groups
Fabricio Dos Santos

Abstract
. In 2022, Osajda and Przytycki showed that any Coxeter group W W is biautomatic. Key to their proof is the notion of voracious projection of an element g ∈ W g\in W , which is used iteratively to construct a biautomatic structure for W W : the voracious language. In this article, we generalize these two notions by defining them for any Garside shadow B B in a Coxeter system ( W , S ) (W,S) . This leads to the result that any finite Garside shadow in ( W , S ) (W,S) can be used to construct a biautomatic structure for W W . In addition, we show that for the Garside shadow L L of low elements, the biautomatic structure obtained corresponds to the original voracious language of Osajda and Przytycki. These results answer a question of Hohlweg and Parkinson.



1.
Introduction

Coxeter groups are widely studied in mathematics as abstractions of reflection groups. They were introduced by H.S.M. Coxeter in 1934
[
7
]
, and since then many of their important algebraic, geometric and algorithmic properties have been established. One of these properties is biautomaticity, which was introduced in the book
[
14
]
and roughly says that there is a ’’well behaved‘‘ system of paths in the Cayley graph of the group (see Section
4
for a rigorous definition). In particular, biautomaticity is strongly linked to algorithmic properties of a group and provides a good setting for studying its structure.

A slightly weaker version of this property was established for Coxeter groups in the 1990s. Namely, Davis and Shapiro proved, under the assumption of the Parallel Wall Theorem, that Coxeter groups are automatic
[
8
]
. The result was then completed by Brink and Howlett with their proof of the Parallel Wall Theorem
[
4
]
. The problem of proving that Coxeter groups are biautomatic, however, remained open for much longer. Partial results were obtained in multiple subclasses of Coxeter groups, namely Euclidean and Gromov hyperbolic
[
14
]
, right-angled
[
18
,
19
]
, no Euclidean reflection triangles
[
2
,
5
]
, relatively hyperbolic
[
6
]
and 2-dimensional
[
17
]
. Finally, in 2022, Osajda and Przytycki proved the result for a general Coxeter group
W

[
20
]
.

Crucial to the proof of biautomaticity by Osajda and Przytycki is the notion of
voracious projection

p(g)
of an element
g\in W
, which is used iteratively to construct a biautomatic structure for
W
, namely the
voracious language

\mathcal{V}

[
20
]
. In this paper, we study and generalize these two notions, leading to a new class of biautomatic structures for the Coxeter group
W
. In particular, we define a voracious projection
\nu_{B}
and voracious language
\mathcal{V}_{B}
for any Garside shadow
B
in a Coxeter system
(W,S)
, that is, a subset
B\subseteq W
containing
S
which is closed under taking suffixes and joins (in the right weak order), introduced in
[
13
]
. These definitions are motivated by an observation of Hohlweg and Parkinson, who reinterpreted the voracious projection
p(g)
as the
g
-translate of a particular element of the set
L
of
low elements
, which is a finite Garside shadow in
(W,S)
. The main result of this paper is the following, answering in the positive a question of Hohlweg and Parkinson.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
B
be a finite Garside shadow in
(W,S)
. Then
(S,\mathcal{V}_{B})
is a biautomatic structure for
W
.

We also show in Proposition
4.3
that the original voracious projection
p
and voracious language
\mathcal{V}
of Osajda and Przytycki are obtained from
\nu_{B}
and
\mathcal{V}_{B}
as a special case when
B=L
.

Organization.
We start by recalling in Section
2
definitions related to Coxeter groups, walls and the right weak order. In Section
3
, we define Garside shadows and their associated projections, and give examples and properties relevant to our result. Then, in Section
4
, we define biautomatic structures, and introduce the notion of voracious projection
\nu_{B}
and voracious language
\mathcal{V}_{B}
of a Garside shadow
B
. We proceed by defining regular languages in Section
5
and showing that
\mathcal{V}_{B}
is regular when
B
is finite. Finally, we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
in Section
6
by proving the fellow traveller property for
\mathcal{V}_{B}
when
B
is finite.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Coxeter groups and words

Let
S
be a finite set and
W
be a group generated by
S
subject only to relations
s^{2}=\text{id}
for
s\in S
and
(st)^{m_{st}}=\text{id}
for
s\neq t\in S
, where
m_{st}=m_{ts}\in\{2,3,\ldots,\infty\}
, and
m_{st}=\infty
means that we do not impose a relation between
s
and
t
. Such a pair
(W,S)
is called a
Coxeter system
and
W
is called a
Coxeter group
. The
word length
of an element
g\in W
, denoted
\ell(g)
, is the minimal number
n\geq 0
such that we can write
g=s_{1}\ldots s_{n}
where each
s_{i}\in S
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. The
word metric
on
W
is the function
d:W\times W\to\mathbb{N}
defined by
d(g,h)=\ell(g^{-1}h)
. For
g,w\in W
, we say that
w
is a
suffix
of
g
if there exists
u\in W
such that
g=uw
and
\ell(g)=\ell(u)+\ell(w)
.

Denote by
S^{*}
the set of all words over
S
. For
v\in S^{*}
a word of length
n
, write
v(i)
for the prefix of
v
(that is, initial subword of
v
) of length
1\leq i\leq n-1
and
v
itself for
i\geq n
. For
1\leq i\leq j\leq n
let
v(i,j)
denote the subword of
v(j)
obtained by removing the prefix
v(i-1)
. Finally, for a word
v\in S^{*}
let
\overline{v}\in W
be the element of the group
W
represented by
v
.

2.2.
Reflections and walls

Let
X^{1}
be the
Cayley graph
of
W
with respect to
S
, that is the graph with vertex set
X^{0}=W
and edges of length 1 joining each
g\in W
to
gs
for
s\in S
. We consider the action of
W
on
X^{0}
by left-multiplication, which induces an action of
W
on
X^{1}
. A
reflection
of
W
is an element of the set
R=\bigcup_{g\in W}gSg^{-1}
. Given
r\in R
, the
wall

\mathcal{W}_{r}
of
r
is the fixed point set of
r
on
X^{1}
. In particular, for a wall
\mathcal{W}_{r}
in
X^{1}
such
r\in R
is unique. Every wall
\mathcal{W}
separates
X^{1}
into two components, called
half-spaces
. We say that two walls
\mathcal{W}
and
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}

intersect
if
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
is not contained in a half-space of
\mathcal{W}
(this relation is symmetric). Furthermore, we say a wall
\mathcal{W}

separates
an element
g\in W
(or a wall
\mathcal{W}^{\prime}
) from another element
h\in W
(or wall
\mathcal{W}^{\prime\prime}
) if
g
(or
\mathcal{W}
) belongs to a different half-space of
\mathcal{W}
than
h
(or
\mathcal{W}^{\prime\prime}
). It is proven in
[
23
, Lem 2.5]
that a geodesic edge-path in
X^{1}
intersects
\mathcal{W}
at most once, thus, for
g,h\in W
, we have that
d(g,h)
equals the number of walls in
X^{1}
separating
g
and
h
.

2.3.
The right weak order

We will be considering the
right weak order

\preceq
on
W
. This partial order is defined by setting
g\preceq h
for
g,h\in W
if
g
lies on a geodesic in
X^{1}
from
id
to
h
. Equivalently,
g\preceq h
if there is no wall in
X^{1}
separating
g
from both
id
and
h
. It is known that
(W,\preceq)
is a complete meet-semilattice, that is, any subset
A
of
W
has a greatest lower bound
\bigwedge A
called the
meet
of
A
(see
[
3
, Thm 3.2.1]
). This implies that any bounded subset
A
of
W
also has a least upper bound
\bigvee A
called the
join
of
A
.

3.
Garside shadows

3.1.
Garside shadows and the
B
-projection

Garside shadows were introduced by Dyer and Hohlweg in
[
13
]
as analogs to the notion of Garside families in a monoid (see
[
9
,
10
]
). They were used in
[
16
]
to construct automata recognizing the language of reduced words of
(W,S)
.

Definition 3.1
.

A
Garside shadow
in
(W,S)
is a subset
B\subseteq W
containing
S
such that:

(1)

for any
X\subseteq B
, if
\bigvee X
exists then
\bigvee X\in B
;

(2)

for any
b\in B
, if
w
is a suffix of
b
then
w\in B
.

Condition (
1
) means that
B
is closed under taking joins (when they exist), and (
2
) that
B
is closed under taking suffixes.

Important examples of finite Garside shadows are the
low elements

L
and
m
-low elements

L_{m}
, which we describe in §
3.2
, and the set
\Gamma
of
gates
of the
cone type partition
, described in §
3.3
. Furthermore, note that the intersection of Garside shadows is a Garside shadow, so there exists a smallest Garside shadow, which turns out to be
\Gamma
.

Garside shadows come with a natural projection, introduced in
[
16
]
, which can be defined as follows.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
B
be a Garside shadow. The map
\pi_{B}:W\to B
given by

\displaystyle\pi_{B}(g)=\bigvee\{b\in B:b\preceq g\}

is called the
B
-projection
.

Note that this map is well-defined since the set
\{b\in B:b\preceq g\}
is bounded, so its join always exists. Furthermore, this map satisfies
\pi_{B}\circ\pi_{B}=\pi_{B}
, so it is indeed a projection in this sense. It is also clear from the definition that
\pi_{B}(g)\preceq g
for every
g\in W
.

The
B
-projection induces a partition
\mathscr{P}_{B}
of
W
with parts
P_{b}=\{g\in W:\pi_{B}(g)=b\}
for
b\in B
, which is one example of a ’’regular partition‘‘
[
21
, Def 3.10]
. Furthermore, by
[
21
, Lem 4.17]
, the element
b\in B
is a
gate
of the part
P_{b}
, meaning that
b
is the smallest element of
P_{b}
with respect to the right weak order
\preceq
.

We now prove the following simple lemma which will be useful later on.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
B
and
B^{\prime}
be Garside shadows such that
B\subseteq B^{\prime}
, and let
x,y\in W
. If
\pi_{B^{\prime}}(x)=\pi_{B^{\prime}}(y)
, then
\pi_{B}(x)=\pi_{B}(y)
.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{B^{\prime}}(x)=\pi_{B^{\prime}}(y)=b^{\prime}\in B^{\prime}
,
\pi_{B}(x)=b_{1}\in B
and
\pi_{B}(y)=b_{2}\in B
. Since
b_{1},b_{2}\in B^{\prime}
, we have that
b_{1},b_{2}\preceq b^{\prime}
and thus their join
b=b_{1}\vee b_{2}
exists and belongs to
B
. Then,
b_{1}\preceq b\preceq b^{\prime}\preceq x
and
b_{2}\preceq b\preceq b^{\prime}\preceq y
, therefore we must have that
b=b_{1}=b_{2}
.
∎

3.2.
Shi partitions, elementary walls and low elements

Let
\mathcal{W}
be a wall in
X^{1}
,
g\in W
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. We say that
\mathcal{W}
is
m
-close
to
g
if there are at most
m
walls separating
g
from
\mathcal{W}
. The wall
\mathcal{W}
is called
m
-elementary
if it is
m
-close to
id
. We denote by
\Sigma_{m}
the set of
m
-elementary walls, and note that these correspond to the
m
-small roots
in
[
12
]
(also called
m
-elementary roots
in
[
4
,
15
]
). For
m=0
, we simply call such walls
elementary
and denote by
\Sigma
the set
\Sigma_{0}
. The Parallel Wall Theorem of Brink and Howlett, which was crucial to the proof that every Coxeter group is automatic, states that the set
\Sigma
is finite
[
4
]
. More generally, it is also the case that
\Sigma_{m}
is finite for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
, as proven by Fu in
[
15
]
.

The connected components
Y
of
X^{1}\setminus\Sigma_{m}
are called
m
-Shi components
and the
m
-Shi part
associated to
Y
is
Y\cap X^{0}
. The
m
-Shi partition

\mathscr{S}_{m}
is the partition of
W
which has as parts the
m
-Shi parts. For
m=0
, we simply call the parts
Y\cap X^{0}

Shi parts
and denote by
\mathscr{S}
the partition
\mathscr{S}_{0}
, called the
Shi partition
. The partitions
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{S}_{m}
are examples of regular partitions
[
21
, Thm 3.11]
.

It is proven in
[
12
]
in the general case, and independently in
[
22
]
for the case
m=0
, that each
m
-Shi part has a smallest element with respect to the order
\preceq
, called a
gate
in
[
21
]
. Denote by
L_{m}
the set of gates of the
m
-Shi partition, that is the set of smallest elements for each
m
-Shi part. Similarly, denote by
L
the set
L_{0}
. Note that by
[
12
, Thm 1.1]
,
L_{m}
is equal to the set of so called
m
-low elements
, and thus forms a finite Garside shadow for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
. This fact was proven in the case
m=0
in
[
13
]
and independently in
[
22
]
, and the general case was completed in
[
11
]
. This leads to the following result.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
g\in W
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
then
\pi_{L_{m}}(g)
is the gate of the
m
-Shi part containing
g
. Therefore, the partition
\mathscr{P}_{L_{m}}
is equal to
\mathscr{S}_{m}
and the partition
\mathscr{P}_{L}
is equal to
\mathscr{S}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{\mathscr{S}_{m}}:W\to L_{m}
be the map assigning
g\in W
to the gate of the
m
-Shi part containing
g
. Suppose for a contradiction that
\pi_{\mathscr{S}_{m}}(g)\neq\pi_{L_{m}}(g)
, meaning that there is an
m
-elementary wall
\mathcal{W}
separating
\pi_{\mathscr{S}_{m}}(g)
from
\pi_{L_{m}}(g)
. Then, by definition of
\pi_{L_{m}}
, we have that
\pi_{\mathscr{S}_{m}}(g)\preceq\pi_{L_{m}}(g)\preceq g
. This implies that
\mathcal{W}
also separates
\pi_{\mathscr{S}_{m}}(g)
from
g
, contradicting the fact that they belong to the same
m
-Shi part. Hence,
\pi_{\mathscr{S}_{m}}=\pi_{L_{m}}
, and since two elements
x,y\in W
belong to the same
m
-Shi part if and only if
\pi_{\mathscr{S}_{m}}(x)=\pi_{\mathscr{S}_{m}}(y)
, it follows that
\mathscr{S}_{m}=\mathscr{P}_{L_{m}}
and
\mathscr{S}=\mathscr{P}_{L}
.
∎

3.3.
Cone types

The
cone types
in the group
W
give rise to a second important example of a finite Garside shadow in
(W,S)
, which turns out to be the smallest one.

Definition 3.5
.

Let
g,h\in W
. The
cone type of

g

based at

h
is the set

\displaystyle T_{g,h}=\{f\in W:\ell(g^{-1}h)+\ell(h^{-1}f)=\ell(g^{-1}f)\}.

Equivalently,
T_{g,h}
is the set of elements
f\in W
for which there exists a geodesic in
X^{1}
from
g
to
f
passing through
h
. The set
T_{g^{-1},\text{id}}
is called the
cone type of

g
, and corresponds to the set
T(g)
in the notation of
[
21
]
. The
cone type part of

g
is the set of all elements
h\in W
such that
T_{h,\text{id}}=T_{g,\text{id}}
.

It turns out that the set of all cone types in
W
is finite, which is a consequence of the fact that
\Sigma
is finite
[
21
, Cor 2.10]
. Let
\mathscr{T}
be the
cone type partition
, that is the partition of
W
which has as parts the cone type parts. Parkinson and Yau show in
[
21
]
that
\mathscr{T}
is another example of a regular partition, and in particular that
\mathscr{S}
and
\mathscr{S}_{m}
are refinements of
\mathscr{T}
(in the sense that every part of
\mathscr{S}
or
\mathscr{S}_{m}
is contained in some part of
\mathscr{T}
). The partition
\mathscr{T}
also has the property that it is
convex
, meaning that for all pairs of elements
x,y
in a cone type part
Q
any geodesic from
x
to
y
has all vertices in
Q
. This was proven in
[
21
, Prop 4.3]
and alternatively in
[
22
, Rem 3.1]
, and we state a special case of this as a Lemma for later use.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f,g,h\in W
be such that
f\preceq g\preceq h
. If
f
and
h
belong to the same cone type part then
T_{f,\text{id}}=T_{g,\text{id}}=T_{h,\text{id}}
.

It is also the case that every cone type part has a gate. This was proven in
[
21
, Thm 1]
and alternatively in
[
22
]
. Let
\Gamma
be the set of gates of
\mathscr{T}
, which is finite. The set
\Gamma
was used in
[
21
]
to construct the minimal automaton for the language of reduced words of
(W,S)
. Furthermore, Parkinson and Yau showed in that paper that
\Gamma
is closed under taking suffixes, and conjectured that
\Gamma
is the smallest Garside shadow in
(W,S)
. Przytycki and Yau then verified in
[
22
]
that
\Gamma
is also closed under taking joins, thus settling this conjecture and a conjecture of Hohlweg, Nadeau and Williams in
[
16
]
that the automaton constructed from the smallest Garside shadow is the minimal automaton for the language of reduced words of
(W,S)
. This leads to the following result, similar in nature to Lemma
3.4
.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
g\in W
then
\pi_{\Gamma}(g)
is the gate of the cone type part containing
g
. Therefore, the partition
\mathscr{P}_{\Gamma}
is equal to
\mathscr{T}
.

Proof.

Let
\pi_{\mathscr{T}}:W\to\Gamma
be the map assigning
g\in W
to the gate of the cone type part containing
g
. Suppose for a contradiction that
\pi_{\mathscr{T}}(g)\neq\pi_{\Gamma}(g)
, meaning that
T_{\pi_{\mathscr{T}}(g),\text{id}}\neq T_{\pi_{\Gamma}(g),\text{id}}
. Then, by definition of
\pi_{\Gamma}
, we have that
\pi_{\mathscr{T}}(g)\preceq\pi_{\Gamma}(g)\preceq g
. But this implies by Lemma
3.6
that
T_{\pi_{\mathscr{T}}(g),\text{id}}=T_{\pi_{\Gamma}(g),\text{id}}=T_{g,\text{id}}
, which is a contradiction. Hence,
\pi_{\mathscr{T}}=\pi_{\Gamma}
, and since two elements
x,y\in W
belong to the same cone type part if and only if
\pi_{\mathscr{T}}(x)=\pi_{\mathscr{T}}(y)
, it follows that
\mathscr{T}=\mathscr{P}_{\Gamma}
.
∎

Another important result about the Garside shadow
\Gamma
which we will use later on is the following Proposition, verified in
[
21
, Prop 4.29]
.

Proposition 3.8
.

If
B
is a Garside shadow then
\Gamma\subseteq B
.

4.
Biautomatic structures

4.1.
Biautomaticity

Biautomatic structures were introduced in the book
[
14
]
. We provide the following definition, which is equivalent to the characterisation of biautomaticity in
[
14
, Lem 2.5.5]
, and refer the reader to §
2.1
for a reminder on the notations used below.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
G
be a group. A
biautomatic structure for

G
is a pair
(S,\mathcal{L})
where
S
is a finite generating set for
G
closed under inversion and
\mathcal{L}
is a regular language (see Section
5
for the definition of a regular language) for which there exists constants
C
and
C^{\prime}
, satisfying the following conditions:

(1)

for every
g\in G
, there is a word in
\mathcal{L}
representing
g
;

(2)

for every
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}
representing elements
g,g^{\prime}\in G
with
g^{\prime}=gs
for some
s\in S
, we have
d(\overline{v(i)},\overline{v^{\prime}(i)})\leq C
for all
i\geq 1
;

(3)

for every
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}
representing elements
g,g^{\prime}\in G
with
g^{\prime}=sg
for some
s\in S
, we have
d(s\overline{v(i)},\overline{v^{\prime}(i)})\leq C^{\prime}
for all
i\geq 1
.

Conditions (
2
) and (
3
) are called the
fellow traveller property
. In particular, we will refer to condition (
2
) as the first fellow traveller property, and condition (
3
) as the second fellow traveller property. If
\mathcal{L}
satisfies conditions (
1
) and (
2
) but not necessarily (
3
), then
(S,\mathcal{L})
is called an
automatic structure
.
G
is said to be
automatic
if it admits an automatic structure, and
biautomatic
if it admits a biautomatic structure.

We note that this definition is equivalent to the original definition of biautomaticity if in condition (
1
) the set of words in
\mathcal{L}
representing each element of
G
is finite
[
1
, Thm 6]
.

4.2.
The voracious projection and voracious language

Crucial to the proof of the biautomaticity of Coxeter groups by Osajda and Przytycki
[
20
]
is the notion of the
voracious projection
of an element
g\in W
, which is introduced in that same paper. In order to define it, they consider the set
\mathcal{W}(g)
of walls in
X^{1}
that separate
g
from
id
and which are closest to
g
(that is
0
-close to
g
). Then, they define the set
P(g)
of elements
p\in W
such that
p\preceq g
and
p
is not separated from
id
by any wall in
\mathcal{W}(g)
. Finally, they show that
P(g)
has a largest element
p(g)
with respect to
\preceq
and set the voracious projection of
g
to be that element.

Hohlweg and Parkinson proposed the following generalization (as we will see in Proposition
4.3
) of the notion of voracious projection, which we study here.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
B
be a Garside shadow. The
voracious projection with respect to

B
is the map
\nu_{B}:W\to W
given by
\nu_{B}(g)=g\pi_{B}(g^{-1})
.

Let
g\in W
and denote by
p(g)
the voracious projection defined as above. We verify in the following proposition that the voracious projection
\nu_{L}(g)
of
g
with respect to the set
L

Proposition 4.3

Proof.

by any wall in

and

separates

from
id
. But note that

since for any wall

separating

from

5.
Regularity

We are now ready to define the notion of a regular language and prove that

Definition 5.1
.

A
finite state automaton over S
(FSA for short) is a finite directed graph
\Gamma
together with the following data:

(1)

a vertex set
A

a_{1}
to

and at most one directed edge from
a_{2}
to

;

(2)

a map

(the power set of
S^{*}
) assigning to each edge in

a finite set of labels;

(3)

a vertex
a_{0}\in A
called the
start state
of
\Gamma
;

(4)

a subset
A_{\infty}\subseteq A
called the
accept states
of
\Gamma
.

The language
\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)
of the FSA
\Gamma
is the set of words
v\in S^{*}
which can be decomposed into subwords
v=v_{0}\ldots v_{m}
labelling a directed edge-path
(e_{0},\ldots,e_{m})
in
\Gamma
from
a_{0}
to a vertex in
A_{\infty}
, that is
v_{i}\in\phi(e_{i})
for

. A subset
\mathcal{L}\subseteq S^{*}
is a

if it is the language of some FSA
\Gamma
over

.

Now, we define a FSA

for the language
\mathcal{V}_{B}
. Since

will be based on

and needs to have a finite number of states, we will require the Garside shadow

Definition 5.2
.

We define the FSA
\mathcal{A}_{B}
for

as follows. The vertex set of

is

, with start state

and accept states

. The edges of
\mathcal{A}_{B}
are defined as follows. Let

be such that
\pi_{B}(wb)=w
. Then, we put an edge

in

from

to

6.
Fellow traveller property

In this section, we prove the first and second fellow traveller property for
\mathcal{V}_{B}
when
B
is a finite Garside shadow.

Proposition 6.1
.

If
B
is finite then
\mathcal{V}_{B}
satisfies the first fellow traveller property with
C=2M
, where
M
is the constant from Lemma
4.5
.

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as to that of
[
20
, Cor 5.2]
. Let
g\in W
,
s\in S
and
g^{\prime}=gs
. Assume without loss of generality that
\ell(g^{\prime})<\ell(g)
. Then,
s\preceq g^{-1}
and so
s\preceq\pi_{B}(g^{-1})\preceq g^{-1}
by definition of
\pi_{B}
since
s\in B
. Thus, we find that
\nu_{B}(g)\preceq g^{\prime}\preceq g
and we can iteratively apply Lemma
4.4
to obtain

\displaystyle\text{id}=\nu_{B}^{n}(g^{\prime})\preceq\nu_{B}^{n}(g)\preceq\ldots\preceq\nu_{B}^{2}(g^{\prime})\preceq\nu_{B}^{2}(g)\preceq\nu_{B}(g^{\prime})\preceq\nu_{B}(g)\preceq g^{\prime}\preceq g

for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\nu_{B}^{k}
denotes the
k
-fold composition of
\nu_{B}
(for convenience also define
\nu_{B}^{0}(g)=g
). Let
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}_{B}
be words representing
g
and
g^{\prime}
respectively. If
\ell(\nu_{B}^{k}(g^{\prime}))\leq i\leq\ell(\nu_{B}^{k}(g))
for some
k\geq 0
, then

\displaystyle d(\overline{v(i)},\overline{v^{\prime}(i)})\leq d(\overline{v(i)},\nu_{B}^{k+1}(g))+d(\nu_{B}^{k+1}(g),\overline{v^{\prime}(i)})\leq 2M

by Lemma
4.5
. Otherwise, we have
\ell(\nu_{B}^{k+1}(g))\leq i\leq\ell(\nu_{B}^{k}(g^{\prime}))
for some
k\geq 0
and thus

\displaystyle d(\overline{v(i)},\overline{v^{\prime}(i)})\leq d(\overline{v(i)},\nu_{B}^{k+1}(g^{\prime}))+d(\nu_{B}^{k+1}(g^{\prime}),\overline{v^{\prime}(i)})\leq 2M

by Lemma
4.5
again.
∎

To prove the second fellow traveller property we will need the following result, which is a generalization of the Parallel Wall Theorem proven by Brink and Howlett
[
4
, Thm 2.8]
, and is a direct consequence of the fact that
\Sigma_{m}
is finite for every

[

]
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
W
be a Coxeter group and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. There exists a constant
Q_{m}=Q(W,m)
such that for each
g\in W
and wall
\mathcal{W}
at distance greater than
Q_{m}
from
g
in
X^{1}
, there are
m
walls separating
g
from
\mathcal{W}
.

We can now finish the proof of Theorem
1.1
with the following.

Proposition 6.3
.

If
B
is finite then
\mathcal{V}_{B}
satisfies the second fellow traveller property with

, where
M
is the constant from Lemma
4.5
and

is the constant from Theorem

.

Proof.

Let
g\in W
and
s\in S
. We will prove this by induction on
\ell(g)
. Assume without loss of generality that
\ell(sg)>\ell(g)
. For

there is nothing to prove, so suppose

. Let

be words representing

and

respectively.

Suppose first that
\pi_{B}(g^{-1})=\pi_{B}((sg)^{-1})
. Then,

\displaystyle s\nu_{B}(g)=sg\pi_{B}(g^{-1})=sg\pi_{B}((sg)^{-1})=\nu_{B}(sg)

so

and

represent the same element of

. Hence, the second fellow traveller property is satisfied by induction for

Now, assume that

separating

we have that

, so

since we can reflect through

the part of the geodesic from

to
\nu_{B}(sg)
in

lying on the same side of
\mathcal{W}_{s}
as

. We thus have a geodesic

from

to

passing through

and
\nu_{B}(sg)
, but then translating

, which implies that

by definition of

, giving a contradiction. Hence,

lies on the same side of
\mathcal{W}_{s}
as

. See Figure

for an illustration.

Finally, we claim that the wall
\mathcal{W}_{s}
is

. Assume for a contradiction that it is not. Then,

is not

\displaystyle d(sg,\nu_{B}(g))\leq d(sg,s\nu_{B}(g))+d(s\nu_{B}(g),\nu_{B}(g))\leq M+2Q_{M}
