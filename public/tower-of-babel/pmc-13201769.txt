Integrating AI-blockchain framework with Spider Monkey Federated Extreme Learning for enhanced healthcare data protection
Authors: V S Nishok, S Dhanasekaran, G Suresh, A Peter Soosai Anandaraj

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) has largely revolutionized the healthcare sector because of its rapid growth that allows continuous monitoring and data-driven services that are based on intelligence. Nonetheless, such a rising connectivity also heightens the susceptibility of sensitive medical data such that, strong security and privacy-driving solutions are required. In order to overcome these issues, this paper presents SMOFEL (Spider Monkey Optimized Federated Extreme Learning) which is an integrated system that incorporates Federated Learning (FL), Extreme Learning Machine (ELM), Spider Monkey Optimization (SMO), and AI-Based Blockchain Technology. FL enables decentralized training of models by making sure that raw patient data are stored on local IoMT devices, which improve privacy and regulation. SMO enhances the convergence and optimization of parameters in the learning process, which is why the framework can be used in resource-constrained IoMT settings. Data integrity is also enhanced with the help of blockchain technology as it offers an immutable and transparent list of model updates and safe transactions. Smart contracts provide the capability to enter into automated and immutable data-sharing contracts across involved nodes. Simulated healthcare data experimental assessment proves that SMOFEL supports an accuracy of 98.08, which indicates its potential to increase the security, efficiency, and predictive power. Altogether, the suggested framework presents a holistic way to achieve secure, scalable, and privacy-saving healthcare analytics, and SMOFEL can be a great solution to next-generation IoMT ecosystems.Keywords: Internet of medical things (IoMT), Blockchain technology, Spider Monkey Optimization, Federated extreme learning machineSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

PhysioBank dataset and preprocessing

In this study, we will rely on the MIT-BIH Normal Sinu Rhythm Database of PhysioBank23, which is a repository of long-term ECG records of healthy subjects. All records have been computerized at a frequency of 128 Hz and 12 bits deep and have been labeled as being a normal heart activity. The dataset was selected due to the fact that it is also widely utilized in clinical ECG studies and can be used to test the predictive systems that rely on the IoMT. It was 18 long-term ECG recordings that produced over 400,000 ECG segments as a result of preprocessing and segmentation. The uniform preprocessing protocol preprocessed all the raw ECG signals by trying to remove noise, and also uniformly standardize segment quality prior to training. A bandpass filter (0.5–40 Hz) was employed to eliminate the baseline drift, muscle noise and electrical interference. Independent Component Analysis (ICA) was used to reduce the movement induced artifacts. ICA was then used to repress the artifacts caused by motion and the noise of electrodes. The signal that had been filtered was divided into 2-second windows with an overlap of 50% in order to capture short-term cardiac dynamics. All of these segments were retained when meeting predefined quality criteria (i) no flat-line regions (those with zero variance were removed), (ii) no absent or corrupted samples, (iii) the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) was above 15 dB, (iv) detectability of R-peaks was physiologically valid, (v) the range of the amplitude was within 3 standard deviations (after Z-score normalization); and (vi) no excessive motion of the baseline beyond threshold limits. Segments that did not meet either of these requirements were discounted. This filtering step removed noisy, incomplete or clinically invalid samples and the final set of 95,488 high-quality ECG segments was used to do stable federated training and achieve reliable classification performance.

ELM is a distinctive paradigm in the realm of machine learning, recognized for its simplicity, efficiency, and rapid learning capabilities. At its core, ELM is a single-layer feedforward neural network, comprising an input layer, a hidden layer, and an output layer. Federated learning (FL)

FL is a distributed machine learning method that relies on local devices to train models and relies on central exchanges for model updates only. This ensures that raw data remains on individual devices, addressing privacy concerns associated with centralized approaches. It represents a transformative paradigm in machine learning, introducing a decentralized approach to model training that aligns with privacy concerns and the distributed nature of modern data sources. At its essence, FL enables collaborative model development across multiple devices without the necessity of centralizing sensitive data. This comprehensive overview delves into the core principles, mechanisms, applications, and challenges that define FL. The key principle of FL is collaborative learning, harnessing the collective intelligence of a multitude of devices. Each device trains its local model on its unique dataset, contributing only the model updates to a central server. This decentralized collaboration is orchestrated through mechanisms like federated averaging, where the aggregated insights from diverse devices shape a global model. Differential privacy techniques further fortify FL, ensuring that individual contributions are confidential, even in the presence of a shared global model.

Mechanisms like federated averaging and secure aggregation are the backbone of FL, ensuring the efficient exchange of model updates while preserving privacy. The ability to personalize models on local devices enhances FL’s versatility, catering to diverse user needs and preferences. The applications of FL span various domains, with notable impacts in healthcare, edge computing, and financial services. In healthcare, FL enables the creation of personalized treatment models without compromising patient privacy, leveraging insights from wearable devices and health monitors. At the edge, FL thrives in environments with limited bandwidth, where devices collaboratively contribute to model development. In financial services, FL aids in fraud detection, customer segmentation, and risk assessment, tapping into the collective knowledge of local branches or individual devices.

Decentralized model training with ELM

At the core of Federated ELM lies the principle of decentralized model training, synergizing the efficiency of ELM with the decentralized model training principles of FL. This unique approach represents a departure from traditional centralized model training methodologies. In Federated ELM, local devices, exemplified by healthcare monitoring tools, play a pivotal role in the training process. Each device independently trains its ELM model on its individual dataset, embodying the decentralized nature of the framework. This decentralized model training strategy holds significant advantages, particularly in the healthcare domain, where sensitive patient data is involved. By conducting model training locally, raw healthcare data remains securely stored on the individual device, mitigating privacy concerns and ensuring compliance with stringent data protection regulations.

The efficiency and simplicity inherent in the ELM architecture contribute to the effectiveness of decentralized model training. ELM’s randomized input weights and one-shot learning approach make it well-suited for scenarios where quick and efficient model deployment is essential. Federated ELM, by building upon these decentralized model training principles, establishes a foundation for robust, privacy-preserving analytics in healthcare, allowing for the extraction of meaningful insights without compromising individual data security.

Blockchain integration

The proposed SMOFEL uses blockchain as a validation and audit layer of federated learning. The local IoMT clients can calculate ELM model updates during every communication round, create cryptographic hash of the updates, and only send the hashes and metadata to the blockchain. Smart contracts authenticate the updates and store verified ones on the ledger that can never be changed. If the valid updates are based on blockchains, only then they are regarded as possible to be aggregated on the global level to avoid the tampered or unjustified model input. The suggested framework utilizes Hyperledger Fabric (version 2.5) a permissioned blockchain platform, chosen due to low latency, its modularity, access control at a fine level, and the ability to comply with regulatory aspects in healthcare settings. The Raft-based consensus implemented in Fabric does not rely on the mining process, which is an energy-consuming operation and allows scalable and secure verification of transactions that can be implemented in IoMT deployment.

Blockchain data storage

To provide safe, non-repudiable and verifiable aggregation of federated model updates, SMOFEL uses a permissioned blockchain architecture based on Hyperledger Fabric, which was chosen due to its modularity, low-latency transaction execution, endorsement policies and fine-grained access control. The permissioned design contrasts with public blockchains since it is more compatible with regulatory needs in healthcare, in which only authenticated institutions, including hospitals, clinics, and edge gateways, are allowed to participate in the network. Scalability experiment shows that block confirmation time varies between 1.8 s (5,000 devices) and 2.7 s (10,000 devices) which is acceptable in periodic FL rounds. No or very limited computational resources are required to endorse and sign chaincode, even at the scale of a large network, because the updates submitted are light (hashes instead of raw data). The consensus algorithm is carried out with the Raft protocol of Fabric, which does not imply the intensive cryptographic challenge of Proof-of-Work blockchains. SMOFEL only stores hashes and metadata on-chain in order to avoid ledger bloat. The real ELM model updates are stored in off-chain with distributed repositories on institutional servers. This is a hybrid design, which maintains auditability and scales to long-term storage. The average rate of ledger growth that was witnessed in the experimentation process did not exceed 350 MB per 10,000 rounds, which is not too much to handle considering the current healthcare IT infrastructure. Though blockchain creates the moderate latency and metadata storage costs, the overheads are compensated by the security of update validation, non-repudiation, and transparent auditability. The approved, resource-efficient solution guarantees that SMOFEL can be used successfully within large-scale IoMT systems without exerting restrictive computation and storage loads.

Economic and energy model

In order to complete the technical analysis of SMOFEL, a comprehensive economic analysis model was designed to investigate the possible financial and operational consequences of the implementation of the system in the real-life IoMT environment. This model assumes five significant categories of costs. The cost of infrastructure refers to the purchase of IoMT sensors, gateways, servers, and blockchain nodes, which are usually covered by hospitals or healthcare providers. Transaction cost is an overhead payment to write model updates and audit records to the blockchain; and in permissioned networks, this cost tends to be distributed among the members of the consortium. Storage cost represents the long-term cost of storing encrypted patient data off-chain and model versions and ledger metadata on-chain. Operational cost includes system maintenance including security patches, network maintenance, staffing of IT and software maintenance. Energy cost is the amount of energy used by the devices of an IoMT system, gateways, and blockchain validators during model training and synchronization. A complementary analysis was carried out to find out who incurs such costs. The infrastructure, storage, and operating related expenses are also normally distributed between hospitals and clinics, with the blockchain-related transaction and consensus costs spread across the participating healthcare organizations. Cloud vendors must pay the price of operating validator nodes in hosted systems, and the hospitals handle the power needs of their edge systems and gateways. Such cost structure explicitly points to the trade-offs of security benefits and extra financial burden of blockchain integration. It also explains the value to stakeholders, especially, the higher level of privacy and auditability that patients and health providers receive.

Novelty in proposed work

Even though the idea of combinations of Federated Learning (FL), blockchain and metaheuristic optimization has already been investigated in previous research, the proposed SMOFEL (Spider Monkey Optimized Federated Extreme Learning) framework has quite a number of innovations compared to the other research where none of the studies have implemented such innovations. In currently used literature, such components are typically united in a decoupled or procedural way, e.g. blockchain is used only to log data, metaheuristics is used only to hyperparameter tune a deep network, a conventional deep network is used instead of lightweight alternatives. However, SMOFEL proposes a more restrictive, mathematically-based integration where each element acts as a particular problem in a single optimization pipeline that interacts with the rest. One of the major novelties is the implementation of Federated Extreme Learning Machine (F-ELM) as opposed to conventional FL-based models, including CNNs, LSTMs, or autoencoders. The closed-form solution and randomized hidden layer structure of ELM makes calculations per client drastically smaller, which ultimately allows it to scale to IoMT devices. ELM established in prior work does not complement FL and blockchain in a manner that enables verifiable and secure distributed learning that can be performed with communication-efficiency.

The second significant technology is the in-loop integration of Spider Monkey Optimization (SMO) to optimize global ELM parameters. In contrast to the previous case that the metaheuristic algorithms are used outside of the system to optimize hyperparameters or model characteristics, SMO in SMOFEL uses a system in which the weights of the federated outputs are optimized during each communication round. Such hierarchical leader-follower interaction speeds up the convergence and makes it resistant to model poisoning and non-IID data distributions. The third innovation is that blockchain is used as a dynamic layer of validation as a consensus mechanism and not as a passive storage. All client updates are hashed, broadcast and verified prior to aggregation such that malicious or inconsistent gradients are flaked out before optimization. The studies that have been performed so far usually use blockchain to trace but fail to integrate it into the learning feedback system to sift adversarial updates. Fourth novelty is full mathematical formalization as found in SMOFEL. As opposed to past conceptualizations, SMOFEL has clear objective functions on Federated ELM, leader-based SMO update rules, blockchain validation logic, and convergence criteria. This brings the methodology to the level of descriptive and completely reproducible and mathematically consistent. Lastly, SMOFEL modifies the new assessment dimensions, such as data transaction efficiency, convergence speed modeling, privacy-preservation scores, energy consumption, and cost analysis, which were not systematically brought up in previous FL-blockchain-metaheuristic research.

Results and discussions

Implementation and evaluation of the SMOFEL framework was done thoughtfully and by taking into account the dynamic character of the modeled healthcare environment, the datasets that were acquired in the extensive PhysioBank repository. All the applications were carried out using Python 3.9 and PyTorch 2.0 to calculate local models, the Flower 1.2 simulated federated learning system, and Hyperledger Fabric 2.5 to validate and achieve consensus in blockchain. SMO implementation as the described methodology was subjected to the rules of updating hierarchical leader-followers. The experiments were conducted on an Intel Xeon processor and 32 GB of RAM and Nvidia RTX 3080 graphics card and 3.1 GHz CPU. Global training was defaulted by default to a 50 round of communication with 50 simulated clients of IoMT. The model hyperparameters such as learning rate and batch size were also maintained constant to make a comparison of the models. The federated learning experiments were developed based on the Flower (version 1.2) federated learning in Python 3.9, which used PyTorch 2.0 to perform local Extreme Learning Machine (ELM) calculations. The aggregation strategy adopted was Federated Averaging (FedAvg) on 50 simulated IoMT clients throughout 50 communication rounds. The Flower structure allowed the simulation of decentralized devices under control, facilitating the transmission of model updates safely, and configurable client involvement, which guaranteed the realistic imitation of the setting of the distributed learning based on IoMT. The blockchain validation and benchmarking layer was developed based on Hyperledger Fabric version 2.5, which is a permissioned blockchain platform due to its modular architecture and low-latency implementation. The Raft consensus algorithm has been used to prevent the use of energy-intensive mining, retaining crash fault tolerance and scalable transaction validation. Go was used to develop smart contracts (chaincode) to validate, endorse, and record hashed model updates. Performance indicators of blockchain were considered as block confirmation latency, transaction throughput, ledger growth rate, and consensus overhead with large-scale parameters of 5,000 and 10,000 simulated devices to confirm the scale and stability.

The hardware and software synergy achieved in this configuration ensured not only the computational prowess demanded by the multifaceted SMOFEL framework but also the agility required for steering through the intricacies of comprehensive evaluations and analyses within the simulated healthcare ecosystem. This computational robustness became the linchpin for delving into the intricate interplay of FL, ELMs, SMO, and blockchain technologies encapsulated within SMOFEL. The utilization of PhysioBank datasets further injected a layer of realism, allowing for the emulation of authentic physiological signals commonly encountered in IoMT scenarios. The holistic approach to the implementation and evaluation underscored the commitment to rigorously test SMOFEL under diverse conditions, simulating the dynamic landscape of IoMT. The controlled yet expansive simulated environment, coupled with the formidable computing setup, positioned the evaluation as a crucial benchmark in gauging SMOFEL’s capabilities and its potential transformative impact on fortifying healthcare data security within the ever-evolving IoMT paradigm. Experimental evidence shows that 5,000 devices and 10,000 devices take average block confirmation time of 1.8s and 2.7s respectively and can sustain a throughput over 210 transactions per second. Storage of hashes on-chain in a lightweight manner meant that the ledger could grow in a controlled manner; this showed that blockchain incorporation has added manageable overhead but with high integrity and auditability.

Security analysis and threat modeling

In order to deliver a formal evaluation of the security assurances by SMOFEL, a threat model analysis was performed based on the framework of the STRIDE, considering adversaries who may seek to manipulate local model updates, guess private information or even seek to recreate inputs in an unauthorized way. The analysis incorporated three typical and the most common attack vectors in the environment of federated learning: model poisoning, membership inference attacks (MIA), and model inversion attacks (MIA-2). These were simulated scenarios and tested SMOFEL resilience in realistic adversarial conditions.

Model poisoning attack (20% malicious clients)

In order to determine the resistance of SMOFEL to the poisoning attacks, an adversarial attack was modeled where 20% of the involved clients in the IoMT acted maliciously by injecting poisoned gradients when undergoing the federated aggregation process. The findings revealed that even at this hostile condition SMOFEL was very stable. It has been noted that this resilience can be largely due to a combination of the effects of SMO-driven gradient optimization and blockchain-verified model updates and weighted federated aggregation, each of which minimizes the role of anomalous or extreme updates. Although a regular FL model lost a performance of 18–22% as those malicious clients were introduced to it, SMOFEL showed less than 4% variance compared to its clean training benchmark. This indicates that the framework can retain persistent convergence trend and prevent the poisoning attacks that are integrated.

Membership inference attack (MIA)

Membership inference attacks were tested to find out whether an attacker would be able to successfully guess whether a given sample of data belonged to a specific client local training. SMOFEL architecture was very resistant to these attacks. This security is a result of a number of design decisions such as the fact that ELM calculations are done on the local machine entirely avoiding the leakage of backpropagation gradients used in MIAs. Moreover, every updated shared model is being sent encrypted and the federated architecture does not imply sharing raw patient data. Consequently, the success rate of the attack was very close to 51, which is a level just above random guessing (50%). In comparison, the traditional FL models are normally characterized by 60–72% attack success rates considering the same settings. These results demonstrate that SMOFEL does not cause any serious membership-based privacy violation and effectively hides the existence of specific samples in the training set.

Model inversion attack

In model inversion attacks, the sensitivity of input features, including biomedical signals, is reconstructed using information stored in the model parameters in a shared part of the model. In comparison to such adversarial efforts, SMOFEL had good reconstruction resistance. This has much to do with the fact that it relies on randomized ELM hidden layer weights that derail the mathematical stability needed of gradient-based reconstruction methods. Also, SMO encourages enhanced model generalization and prevents overfitting and it is unlikely that the model will recall patient-level details. Inversion is also impossible as the integrity controls based on blockchain can ensure that global model versions are not tampered with by attackers. Practically, SMOFEL attempted reconstructions had very low fidelity, and structural similarity index (SSIM) scores below 0.12, which suggests that no information that would be meaningful or clinically relevant to a patient, was recoverable about the attack. This validates the strength of SMOFEL to withstand privacy breach, which is inverted.

Comparative cost–benefit analysis of blockchain integration

Although SMOFEL is based on blockchain to ensure the safety of updating models and transferring data, one should explain why this option is selected over the other integrity-assuring systems widely implemented in distributed computing. In this respect, we are looking at three minimum strategies that include: (i) conventional secure centralized or cluster-based databases (e.g., relational or NoSQL databases with TLS and role-based access control), (ii) distributed hash tables (DHTs), and (iii) content-addressable data storage like IPFS. The two dimensions of concern include computational and communication cost (including latency and processing overhead) and security value (including tamper resistance, auditability and failure resilience).

As shown in Table 10, blockchain-based SMOFEL is the most appropriate to regulated, multi-party internet of medical things health care settings as it provides better tamper resistance and auditing at acceptable overheads. To conclude, the comparative analysis has revealed that blockchain creates a computational cost but offers significant security and governance advantages. In the case of highly-regulated environments in healthcare, such as non-repudiation, forensic traceability, and resistance to insider manipulation are essential, these properties make blockchain integration in the SMOFEL framework warranted. Other mechanisms can be suitable to less critical environments, however, they lack that mix of decentralized trust, tamper-proof logging, and verifiable auditability of trusted IoMT healthcare data management.

Scalability analysis

To determine the scalability of SMOFEL in large-scale IoMT settings, further testing of the simulated networks with 5,000 and 10,000 involved devices were carried out. Individual devices did the training with ELM locally, and then periodically uploaded encrypted model updates to the blockchain-based aggregation layer. The permission blockchain had steady performance in the 5,000-device setup with an average block confirmation time of about 1.8 s and an effective throughput of nearly 220 transactions per second. The overhead of communication was somewhat higher since the updates traffic was higher and therefore it is estimated that the uplink and downlink message exchange increased by 14% over the small deployments. This growth did not lead to any loss in the behavior of smooth convergence, and no loss in global model performance was experienced. The system was scaled to 10,000 devices, after which the consensus and communication load were again increased, although it remained reliable. The mean block confirmation time increased to about 2.7 s and the throughput had stabilized at approximately 210 transactions per second, even when the update burst was at its peak. The overhead of communication of each training round increased by around 31% compared to the baseline, which was mostly attributed to the extra metadata and hash pointers stored on-chain. However, blockchain layer was always used to maintain the integrity of updates, and the federated learning process was steady, which indicates that SMOFEL could be used in the high-density networks of IoMT with tolerable computational and communication expenses. These findings substantiate the conclusion that blockchain integration does come with quantifiable overhead, but still the system can be scaled and still offers both significant security and auditability advantage as the network grows.

Conclusion and future work

The proposed SMOFEL framework introduces an all-encompassing and safe solution to healthcare data management in the IoMT ecosystems through combining Federated Learning, Extreme Learning Machine, the Spider Monkey Optimization, and AI-Based Blockchain Technology. The decentralized model training by SMOFEL provides an effective way of reducing raw patient data exposure and enhancing the effectiveness and flexibility of the learning process. SMO greatly improves convergence of the model and optimization of the parameters, and blockchain guarantees transparent and tamper resistant data transactions. Experimental analysis validates that SMOFEL has high predictive accuracy of 98.08%, and significantly better privacy preservation, convergence speed and data integrity as compared to the current models. Although these benefits are true, some limitations have to be mentioned to present both the sides of the story. Federated Learning creates a communication overhead particularly as the number of IoMT devices participating in it grows. Training at the local level can also increase power use of edge nodes resource-constrained nodes. Moreover, blockchain implementation may also result in latency and scalability issues, especially when there is a large amount of model updates or high frequency of transactions. All these limitations underscore the fact that more optimization is required on a system level to allow a broad real-world implementation.

The SMOFEL framework can be further developed by considering a variety of directions in future studies. To begin with, FL communication overhead can be lowered by lightweight communication-compression schemes, like update sparsification, quantization, or adaptive communication scheduling. Second, the long-term participation of the battery-powered IoMT equipment may be facilitated by energy-aware learning strategies and hardware-efficient optimization. Third, blockchain scalability like sharding, layer-2, or even ledgers through the use of a DAG, would alleviate the drawbacks of latency and throughput. The implementation of patient-centric privacy controls, such as customizable consent management and fine-grained access policy, is another viable option. Finally, it can be proposed that future research can be conducted by putting SMOFEL into practice in real-life hospital or remote monitoring settings to confirm its work in different network conditions and heterogeneous devices.
