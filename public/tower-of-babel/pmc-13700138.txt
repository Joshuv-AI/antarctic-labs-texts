Biomechanical study on the interaction between the extent of lumbar vertebral lamina and facet joint removal and segmental stability: a finite element analysis
Authors: Guoqiang Zhang, Xiangyao Sun, Aixing Pan, Yong Hai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to establish a finite element model of the lumbar spine, evaluating the impact of lamina and facet joint removal on lumbar stability and stress distribution in posterior structures.MethodsA healthy volunteer was randomly selected for developing a preoperative model (M0) through finite element analysis. Depending on the extent of the lateral articular facet joint resection, five intraoperative models M1(A)-M1(E) were built. as five subgroups, and all subgroups underwent the same degree of unilateral laminectomy (with a resection ratio of 50%). Under six different conditions, the models were evaluated for lumbar range of motion and maximum von Mises stress in the intervertebral disc, in the facet joints and in the isthmus.ResultsAs the extent of surgical resection increased, the maximum stress within the L4-5 intervertebral disc exhibited a progressive upward trend. Compared with M0, M1(A)-M1(E) showed only modest increases in maximum disc stress; in contrast, the extensive resection model demonstrated a more pronounced elevation. In the L4-5 segment, M1(A), M1(B), and M1(C) exhibited slight increases in maximum stress at the left facet joint relative to M0; M1(D) and M1(E) showed progressively greater increases. Under all six loading conditions, the maximum stress at the right L4-5 facet joint increased in M1(A), M1(B), and M1(C) compared with M0. Specifically, during right lateral bending, left rotation, and right rotation, the maximum stress at the right facet joint rose by 12.46–63.27%, 25.67-181.57%, and 18.86-185.09%, respectively. Stress distribution analyses indicated that progressive resection of posterior bony and ligamentous structures elevates mechanical loading on the facet joints and the isthmus—particularly during rotational motions. Extensive posterior resection may induce abnormal stress redistribution and thereby exacerbate segmental instability.ConclusionsThrough finite element analysis, this study confirmed that when the partial resection of the unilateral lamina and facet joint was ≤ 50%, the relative biomechanical stability of the L4-5 segment can be maintained; however, when the resection exceeds 50%, it will lead to excessive increase in segmental mobility, abnormal redistribution of stress in the intervertebral disc, facet joint and pedicle, and may cause iatrogenic instability. These findings provide direct biomechanical evidence for defining the safe bone resection threshold in minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12891-026-10266-5.

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation is a degenerative condition characterized by clinical manifestations such as lumbago and leg pain. Along with the prolongation of life expectancy and the attendant aging of the global population, the prevalence of degenerative lumbar disorders has witnessed an exponential surge [1, 2]. Minimally invasive lumbar spine surgery has emerged as a widely adopted minimally invasive approach for the management of lumbar spine conditions [3]. With the rapid advancement of fully visualized spinal endoscopy technology, the surgical safety and postoperative therapeutic efficacy of this technique have been remarkably enhanced [4, 5]. The intervertebral plate approach requires the removal of the posterior structures of the lumbar spine to establish the working channel [6]. By expanding the bony space of the intervertebral plate gap to create a surgical channel, the interference with the nerves can be minimized, the distance between the protruded intervertebral disc and the surgical site can be shortened, and the surgical indications of lumbar endoscopy can also be expanded [7]. However, some patients with lumbar disc herniation experienced lumbar instability after undergoing spinal endoscopy surgery [8–10]. Therefore, in spinal endoscopy surgery, it is very important to ensure good surgical efficacy while avoiding lumbar instability caused by excessive resection of the intervertebral plate or facet joints.

Finite element analysis (FEA) serves as a pivotal method for exploring the biomechanical stability of the lumbar spine. This technique allows for the simulation of the mechanical microenvironment surrounding the lumbar spine, accurately reproduces spinal motion patterns, and facilitates in-depth biomechanical simulation analysis of the lumbar region, thereby scientifically reflecting the physiological, anatomical, and biomechanical changes occurring in the spine [11, 12]. FEA-based simulations have been extensively applied in spinal surgery-related research, demonstrating exceptional performance in spinal biomechanical analysis and providing valuable theoretical support for clinical practice [13]. Recent studies have further enriched the theoretical basis of spinal biomechanics by exploring different surgical instruments and reconstruction devices [14]). Biswas et al. conducted FEA and mathematical modeling to evaluate lumbar pedicle screws with varying design parameters, thereby establishing quantitative relationships among screw pitch, diameter, thread profile and corresponding stress-strain distributions [15]. Their findings confirmed that trapezoidal-threaded screws exhibited superior mechanical safety and induced lower bone stress compared with triangular-thread screws. Manickam et al. performed FEA of cervical interbody fusion devices featuring distinct morphological characteristics in the context of anterior cervical discectomy and fusion. They demonstrated that the device geometry significantly influences graft subsidence, stress distribution and biomechanics of adjacent segments; moreover, adjunctive anterior plating and screw fixation can effectively reduce the displacement and subsidence of the fusion device [16]. Meanwhile, a comprehensive review of bionic artificial intervertebral disc development has been published, summarizing advances in simulation-driven design, commercial motion-preserving prostheses, lattice-structured architectures, and additive manufacturing techniques. The review underscored the critical importance of accurately replicating the native intervertebral disc’s biomechanical properties— particularly its load-bearing capacity and kinematic fidelity— to preserve physiological spinal motion and enhance long-term clinical outcomes. Complementary clinical and biomechanical evidence further corroborates that the extent of bone resection during surgery and the mechanical properties of the implant are key factors affecting the stability of the spinal segment after surgery [17]. Therefore, on the basis of the above studies, further clarifying the biomechanical changes caused by different degrees of laminectomy and facet joint resection in minimally invasive lumbar surgery has important clinical value, thereby providing a more scientific basis for surgical decision-making.

The primary objective of this study was to employ FEA to investigate the impacts of varying degrees of lumbar bony structure resection during spinal endoscopic surgery on the postoperative biomechanical properties of the lumbar spine. Specifically, this study examined the effects of spinal endoscopic surgery on the stress distribution of the intervertebral disc and facet joints in the target segment, its influence on the lumbar range of motion (ROM), as well as its regulatory effect on the von Mises stress in the isthmic region of the lumbar vertebrae.

Materials

Study subject

This study was conducted in November 2023 at the Department of Spinal Surgery, Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University. We collected and analyzed imaging data from a healthy male volunteer who had no prior history of spinal diseases, spinal trauma, or structural abnormalities of the lumbar spine. The study protocol was reviewed and ethically approved by the Clinical Research Management Office of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (Ethics Approval No.: BFHHZS20230218), and written informed consent was signed by the participant prior to the initiation of the study to confirm their voluntary participation. The primary observational parameters of this study included the ROM of the L4-5 segment, the maximum stress distribution in the posterior structures of the L4-5 segment, as well as the maximum von Mises stress in the intervertebral disc of L4-5, the left and right facet joints of L4-5, and the left and right isthmic regions of the L4 vertebra.

Materials

For CT data acquisition, a Philips Brilliance iCT 128-slice (equivalent to 256-layer) scanner, manufactured in the United States, was utilized. The scanning parameter was specifically set with a slice thickness of 0.6 mm, which enabled the generation of high-resolution cross-sectional images of the lumbar spine to ensure the accuracy of subsequent three-dimensional (3D) model reconstruction. The hardware configuration supporting data processing and model establishment included an Intel (R) Core (TM) i7-9750 H central processing unit operating at a clock speed of 2.90 GHz, combined with 32.0 GB of random-access memory to guarantee efficient operation of data analysis and model computation tasks. The computer was equipped with a 64-bit operating system based on an x64 processor architecture, providing stable and compatible system support for the installation and operation of professional software involved in the study. A series of specialized software packages from renowned manufacturers worldwide were employed for different stages of the research: Mimics 20.0 (developed by Materialise, Belgium) for 3D reconstruction of lumbar vertebral structures from CT images, Geomagic 2020 (produced by Geomagic, USA) for optimization and smoothing of the reconstructed 3D models, SolidWorks 2018 (manufactured by Dassault Systemes, USA) for integrating cortical bone, cancellous bone, intervertebral discs, and other spinal components to construct a complete preoperative and postoperative finite element model, and ANSYS Workbench 20.0 (provided by ANSYS, USA) for implementing contact settings, ligament simulation via spring stiffness, meshing of the model, and final biomechanical simulation analysis. To verify the anatomical representativeness of the finite element model constructed, we measured the key size parameters of the L4-L5 vertebral bodies of healthy volunteers and compared them with the classic large-sample morphometric study conducted by Wolf et al. (which included 55 normal adult subjects), confirming that this model can accurately reflect the anatomical characteristics of the adult human lumbar spine [18]. In this study, the material properties of all spinal components were set as linear elasticity, which was consistent with the modeling method commonly used in lumbar finite element studies [19–21]. The ligaments were simplified as spring elements that only bear tension, and their stiffness values were taken from published anatomical and biomechanical literature [19, 22]. This simplified treatment can reliably simulate the constraining effect of ligaments in segmental movement, and has been verified in previous spinal stability analyses. Similar to most comparative studies, this model did not include muscle force because the influence of muscle force on all models is consistent and does not change the relative differences between groups.

Creation of initial lumbar vertebrae (L4-5) model and model optimization

The DICOM-format CT images were first imported into Mimics 20.0 software. Through a series of standardized image processing steps including threshold segmentation, region growing, and anatomical structure segmentation, an initial three-dimensional anatomical model of the L4-L5 vertebral segment was successfully reconstructed. This initial model accurately depicted the gross morphological features of the L4 and L5 vertebrae, including the anatomical contours of cortical bone, cancellous bone, and articular surfaces, as presented in Fig. 1. Subsequently, the reconstructed 3D models of the L4 and L5 vertebrae were individually exported in STL format, which is widely compatible with 3D model optimization software. These STL-format models were then separately imported into Geomagic 2020 software to perform targeted model optimization. The optimization process primarily involved smoothing the surface of the models to eliminate noise and irregularities generated during the initial reconstruction, repairing minor surface defects such as tiny gaps or overlapping meshes, and refining the geometric structure while preserving key anatomical landmarks—all aimed at enhancing the geometric accuracy and quality of the models to meet the requirements of subsequent finite element modeling. Following the completion of the optimization process for both L4 and L5 models, the cortical bone and cancellous bone components of each vertebra were carefully separated and extracted within the software. Finally, these two bone components (cortical bone and cancellous bone) of the L4 and L5 vertebrae were exported in STEP format, a widely recognized neutral file format that ensures lossless data transmission and compatibility with subsequent finite element modeling software, thereby laying a solid foundation for the integration of spinal soft tissue components and the establishment of complete preoperative and postoperative models.

Establishment of preoperative model M0

In SolidWorks software, the STEP-format models of cortical bone and cancellous bone of the L4 and L5 vertebrae (exported after optimization in Geomagic 2020) were imported first, and the precise integration of these two bone tissue components was gradually completed through the software’s assembly and geometric constraint functions, ensuring that their relative anatomical positions and structural connections were consistent with the physiological state of the human lumbar spine. On the basis of the integrated bone tissue model, the key soft tissue components of the lumbar motion segment were sequentially established, including the intervertebral disc of the L4-5 segment, as well as its internal structural components (the annulus fibrosus and nucleus pulposus), the upper and lower vertebral endplates, and the articular cartilage of the facet joints. Among these structural settings, the thickness of the vertebral endplates (both the upper endplate of L5 and the lower endplate of L4) was uniformly set to 2 mm, a parameter determined with reference to the average anatomical data of the human lumbar spine and relevant published literature. As the elastic core part of the intervertebral disc, the nucleus pulposus was designed to occupy 50% of the total volume of the intervertebral disc, which is consistent with the structural characteristics of the normal lumbar intervertebral disc in the human body. After all soft tissue components were constructed in accordance with anatomical standards and accurately assembled with the bone tissue model, the final and complete preoperative finite element model of the L4-5 lumbar segment was successfully established. This intact model, which fully simulates the normal anatomical structure and biomechanical environment of the lumbar spine, was denoted as M0 and served as the baseline control for subsequent biomechanical comparisons with the intraoperative simulated surgical models [23].

Contact settings, additional ligaments and FEA

The complete finite element models established in SolidWorks were imported into ANSYS Workbench 20.0 software for subsequent biomechanical simulation setup and analysis. In terms of contact relationship definition, the articular cartilage of the L4-5 facet joints was set to form frictional contact with the cortical bone of the L5 upper articular process, and the friction coefficient between these two contact surfaces was strictly defined as 0.2 to simulate the physiological frictional characteristics of the lumbar facet joint interface; all other adjacent anatomical structures were defined as bonded contact to replicate the integral mechanical transmission characteristics of the normal lumbar spine. For the material mechanical parameters of each spinal component, the Poisson’s ratios and Young’s Modulus corresponding to different anatomical structures as well as the stiffness values of the ligaments were determined based on the physiological characteristics of human lumbar anatomical structures and the reliable data reported in published biomechanical literature (Tables 1 and 2) [19]. To accurately simulate the mechanical function of the lumbar ligaments, different types of spinal ligamentswere represented by springs with corresponding stiffness values, and the establishment of the ligament system in the finite element model was completed by setting specific spring stiffness parameters in the software.

Subsequently, the finite element meshing process was carried out for all models to discretize the geometric models into calculable finite element units. To ensure the accuracy and reliability of the subsequent biomechanical analysis results, while balancing the computational efficiency and result precision (avoiding excessive computational load caused by overly small elements or result deviation due to overly large elements), the element sizes of different anatomical components in the models were adjusted and optimized according to their structural characteristics and mechanical requirements: the element size of cortical bone was set to 2 mm, cancellous bone to 2 mm, articular cartilage to 2 mm, upper and lower vertebral endplates to 2 mm, annulus fibrosus to 1 mm, and nucleus pulposus to 2 mm In this finite element study, all anatomical structures were modeled using 10-node tetrahedral elements. Different mesh sizes were set according to the biomechanical analysis requirements and the published standards for lumbar spine modeling: To ensure accurate calculation of stress distribution, the element size of the annulus fibrosus was refined to 1.0 mm; for cortical bone, cancellous bone, articular cartilage, vertebral endplates, and nucleus pulposus, a mesh size of 2.0 millimeters was used to balance computational efficiency and numerical accuracy, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. Using the meshing module of ANSYS Workbench 20.0 software, the surface finite element meshing technique was first applied to process the geometric models of all spinal components, generating a high-quality triangular surface mesh that accurately fits the anatomical contours of each structure. On the basis of the triangular surface mesh, a free partitioning method was further employed to convert the surface mesh into a three-dimensional tetrahedral mesh, which is suitable for biomechanical calculation and can effectively capture the local stress and strain characteristics of the model. Through this systematic meshing process, the final tetrahedral finite element mesh models of M0 and M1A-M1E were successfully obtained, laying a solid foundation for the subsequent static structural analysis and biomechanical parameter calculation.

Additionally, a mesh convergence analysis was conducted to verify the rationality of the above mesh settings. A grid sensitivity analysis was performed to verify the reliability of the finite element model and determine the optimal grid density. Three different grid resolutions were established for the models: a coarse grid model (fibrous ring 2.0 mm, cortical bone, cancellous bone, articular cartilage, endplate, and nucleus pulposus all 3.0 mm), a medium grid model (fibrous ring 1.0 mm, other structures 2.0 mm), and a fine grid model (fibrous ring 0.5 mm, other structures 1.0 mm). The ROM of the L4-L5 segment under various conditions (bending, extension, left lateral bending, right lateral bending, left axial rotation, and right axial rotation) and the maximum von Mises stress of the intervertebral disc, bilateral facet joints, and L4 pedicle were calculated and compared for each model. The results showed that the maximum relative difference in all biomechanical parameters between the medium grid model and the fine grid model was 4.2%, which met the convergence criteria commonly used in spinal biomechanical research (relative error < 5%). Considering both calculation accuracy and efficiency, all subsequent finite element analyses used the medium grid model.

Validation of finite element model

In the static structural analysis module of ANSYS Workbench 20.0 software, the lower surface of the L5 vertebral body was configured as a fully fixed support constraint, which restricted all degrees of freedom in three translational directions and three rotational directions in space, so as to simulate the mechanical environment of the lower end of the lumbar spine under physiological conditions in the human body. Correspondingly, the upper surface of the L4 vertebral body was specifically designated as a uniform force-bearing surface, serving as the loading interface for applying the preset vertical axial force and multi-directional pure moment, aiming to reproduce the mechanical load conditions that the lumbar spine may bear during daily activities such as standing, bending, and rotating. For this static structural analysis, a vertical axial load of 400 N was applied to the upper surface of the L4 vertebral body to simulate the average body weight load that the lumbar spine bears when the human body is in an upright posture, and a pure moment of 8 Nm was applied in six different directions to simulate the mechanical effect of the lumbar spine during various directional movement behaviors.

Subsequently, the ROM data of the L4-5 segment of the preoperative intact finite element model M0 under the above six typical movement conditions was systematically extracted and statistically collated. These data were then comprehensively compared and analyzed with the published lumbar spine biomechanical experimental results from SHIM et al. and Jiang et al. [20, 21], which are widely recognized in the field. The comparative analysis showed that the ROM values of the L4-5 segment of model M0 in each corresponding movement condition not only had a high degree of numerical consistency with the data from the two aforementioned studies but also showed an identical variation trend in response to different movement modes. This strong concordance fully indicated that the finite element model constructed in this study could accurately simulate the physiological movement characteristics and biomechanical response rules of the human lumbar spine. Therefore, the simulation system established in this research had good reliability and validity, laying a solid foundation for the subsequent biomechanical analysis of various simulated surgical models (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Results

ROM for different models at L4-5

As the posterior structure was gradually resected, the ROM of L4-5 segment gradually increased, especially during axial rotation and lateral flexion. Compared with M0, the ROM of M1(A), M1(B) and M1(C) only showed a mild increase (< 15%), still within the physiological stability range, consistent with the results of published literature. In contrast, the ROM of M1(C) and M1(D) increased obviously (38.2%-217.4%), with axial rotation being the most severely affected. These results confirmed that the facet joint was crucial for maintaining rotational stability and clearly indicated that an instability threshold occurred when more than 75% of the posterior structure was resected (Fig. 4).

The maximum stress in the L4-L5 intervertebral discs

As the extent of surgical resection expands, the maximum stress within the L4-5 intervertebral disc showed a gradually increasing trend. The stress distribution was concentrated in the posterior annulus fibrosus and facet joint regions, which was consistent with the biomechanical patterns reported in previous spinal stability studies. Compared with M0, M1(A)- M1(E) showed a relatively small increase in maximum stress, while the extensive resection model showed an increase (Figs. 5 and 6). These research results supported the consensus that excessive resection of the facet joints would damage the load distribution function of the intervertebral disc and may accelerate intervertebral disc degeneration.

The maximum stress in the left facet joint of the L4-5 segment

In the L4-5 segment, M1(A), M1(B) and M1(C) demonstrated a slight increase in the maximum stress of the left facet joint when compared to M0; M1(D) and M1(E) exhibited varying degrees of increased maximum stress in the left facet joint (Fig. 7A). The stress distribution was concentrated on the articular surface and the inter-facet region, which was consistent with the typical biomechanical pattern reported in previous studies of spinal instability. These changes confirmed that posterior structure resection directly altered the load transfer path. The results further clarified the threshold for stress increase, providing a basis for the quantitative assessment of the biomechanical impact of the decompression range.

The maximum stress in the right facet joint of L4-5 segment

Under six conditions, compared with the complete model M0, the maximum stress of the right L4-5 facet joint in the M1(A), M1(B), and M1(C) all increased. Specifically, for models M1(A), M1(B) and M1(C), the maximum stress in the right facet joint increased by 12.46–63.27% during right lateral bending, 25.67-181.57% during left rotation, and 18.86-185.09% during right rotation (Fig. 7B).

The maximum stress in the isthmic region of L4

In the left isthmic region of L4, the stress of the M1(D) and M1(E) models during flexion and extension was higher than that of M0, while the stress decreased during axial rotation. The stress distribution patterns of M1(D) and M1(E) were almost identical under all loading conditions (Fig. 8A). In the right isthmic region of L4, the stress during flexion-extension and lateral flexion movements was similar to that of M0; in contrast, the stress in the M1(A)-M1(C) significantly increased during left-right rotation, which was consistent with the decreased stability observed after more extensive resection (Fig. 8B). The above stress distribution characteristics implied that the progressive resection of the posterior structure would increase the load on the articular facet joints and the isthmus, especially in rotational movements.

L4-5 vertebral stress distribution diagram

During flexion movement, the overall stress distribution patterns of the posterior structures of each model were basically the same, showing no significant difference compared to the complete model (M0). During extension movement, the maximum stress of M0, M1(A), M1(B), and M1(C) models was concentrated on the left pedicle of L5, while M1(D) and M1(E) models showed increased stress in the left interarticular part of L4 and the lateral part of L5. During lateral flexion movement, the peak stress was mainly located on the ipsilateral pedicle of L4-L5. In axial rotation movement, the stress concentration of M0-M1(C) models was in the pars lateralis of L4 and the left isthmus of L5, while M1(D) and M1(E) models presented multifocal increased stress (Fig. 9). These findings showed that extensive posterior structure resection led to abnormal stress redistribution and aggravates segmental instability.

Discussion

In recent years, minimally invasive lumbar spine surgery has gained growing recognition and widespread clinical application due to its distinct advantages over traditional open lumbar spine surgery, such as smaller surgical incisions, faster postoperative recovery, and shorter hospital stays [24, 25]. To establish a sufficient surgical working channel for effective decompression and precise operation, minimally invasive lumbar spine surgery inevitably requires the selective resection of partial posterior lumbar bony structures, including the laminae and/or facet joints [6]. By expanding the bony space between the adjacent laminae to form a surgical pathway, this approach can minimize iatrogenic interference with the surrounding neural structures, shorten the surgical distance to the herniated intervertebral disc, and further expand the surgical indications for lumbar endoscopic procedures [7]. However, clinical practice has shown that a subset of patients with lumbar disc herniation may develop postoperative lumbar instability following spinal endoscopic surgery [8]. Numerous previous biomechanical studies have confirmed that posterior spinal structures, particularly the laminae and facet joints, play a crucial role in maintaining lumbar segmental stability, and their structural integrity is closely associated with the biomechanical balance of the lumbar spine [6, 26–29]. Therefore, in the clinical practice of spinal endoscopic surgery, achieving optimal surgical outcomes while avoiding excessive resection of the laminae or facet joints (which may induce iatrogenic lumbar instability) has become a critical issue that requires careful consideration and resolution.

Regarding the impact of surgical resection on lumbar segmental stability, Nicholas T et al. conducted a dedicated FEA to investigate the biomechanical effects of laminectomy with different resection proportions on the L4-5 segment [6]. Their study designed scenarios ranging from 0% (intact) to 75% bony resection, with the resection extent gradually increasing from the medial to the lateral side of the lamina. The results of their research concluded that laminectomy within a certain range had minimal impact on the basic ROM of the lumbar segment. These findings are highly consistent with the core results of our current study: our data demonstrated that when unilateral laminectomy combined with facet joint resection did not exceed 50%, the ROM of the L4-5 segment under all six motion conditions (flexion, extension, left bending, right bending, left rotation, and right rotation) did not undergo statistically significant changes. In contrast, when the extent of unilateral laminectomy and facet joint resection exceeded 50%, the ROM of the L4-5 segment increased significantly across all six motion conditions, indicating a notable decline in lumbar segmental stability.

In the present study, unilateral posterior laminectomy combined with facet joint resection of less than 50% led to a moderate increase in intervertebral disc stress, but the magnitude of this increase was not statistically significant. However, when the extent of facet joint resection exceeded 50%, especially under left and right axial rotation conditions, the intervertebral disc stress showed a statistically significant elevation, with the stress concentration predominantly localized to the lateral and posterior regions of the intervertebral disc on the operated side. Relevant literature has indicated that laminectomy may potentially compromise spinal stability, and thus preserving partial laminae, spinous processes, supraspinous ligaments, and interspinous ligaments during laminectomy is crucial for mitigating stress concentration within the intervertebral disc, particularly during flexion movements [30, 31]. Additionally, existing research has shown that the intensity of stress and the location of stress concentration on the annulus fibrosus may vary among different individuals during axial rotation and flexion [30, 31], which is consistent with the stress distribution characteristics observed in our study.

With respect to facet joint stress, the results of our study revealed that when the extent of lumbar lamina and facet joint resection during minimally invasive lumbar spine surgery exceeded 50%, the maximum stress on the left facet joint increased noticeably. Among all motion conditions, axial rotation induced the most significant stress increase, followed by lateral bending. Similarly, the maximum stress on the right facet joint also showed a statistically significant elevation, with axial rotation being the most affected motion pattern, followed by lateral bending. A previous biomechanical study using an L4-5 finite element model reported that when proportional right laminectomy was performed, the facet joint forces on the operated side decreased as the bony resection rate increased from 0% (complete preservation) to 75%; specifically, during axial rotation, the facet joint forces decreased significantly at 25%, 50%, and 75% resection levels, while during right bending, a significant decrease in facet joint forces was observed at the 75% resection level [6]. Furthermore, Shih et al. demonstrated in their research that facet joint forces exhibited the most pronounced changes during axial rotation, followed by lateral bending [22], which is highly consistent with the findings of our current study.

Concerning the maximum stress in the isthmic region, Nicholas T et al. found in their study that after posterior laminectomy, the stress between the facet joints increased with the increase in bony resection rate (from 0% to 75%) [6]. In line with this research direction, our study showed that when laminectomy combined with facet joint resection exceeded 50%, the maximum stress in the right isthmic region increased significantly during right bending, left rotation, and right rotation. In contrast, the maximum stress in the left isthmic region decreased during right bending, left rotation, and right rotation. Notably, when partial lumbar lamina and the entire facet joint on one side of the lumbar spine were resected (i.e., the 100% facet joint resection group, M1(E)), this phenomenon of increased maximum stress concentration in the right isthmic region became more prominent, suggesting a potential risk of isthmic fatigue fracture or structural damage under such resection conditions.

This study provides clear and practical guidance for clinical decision-making in minimally invasive lumbar surgery. Firstly, when performing unilateral decompression through the transforaminal approach, the surgeon should limit the resection of the lamina and facet joints to 50% or less to maintain segmental stability. Excessive resection beyond 50% will significantly increase segmental mobility and lead to asymmetric stress distribution, which may increase the risk of postoperative back pain, accelerated intervertebral disc degeneration, and even iatrogenic instability. In clinical scenarios requiring extensive decompression, the surgeon should be vigilant about the need for additional internal fixation or fusion to prevent late instability. Secondly, the phenomenon of significant increase in stress in the interfacetal part and facet joint of the model after resection beyond 50% alerts clinicians to avoid excessive resection of posterior structures, especially in revision surgeries or patients with existing degeneration, where caution is even more necessary.

Compared with our previous study, the main difference is in the simulated spinal endoscopic surgery approach and the bone resection plan [23]. The this article focused on the inter-vertebral plate approach endoscopy, fixing the resection of 50% of the unilateral vertebral plate, only adjusting the range of facet joint resection by gradient increase or decrease, and exploring the mechanical effect of combined injury of vertebral plate and facet joint; Our previous article was for the PETD surgery, preserving the entire vertebral plate throughout the process, only separately grinding the superior facet joint at four levels: lateral, medial, ventral, and full layer, without involving bone destruction of the vertebral plate. The grouping logic is completely distinct. The variable of the former is the degree of combined resection of facet joint and vertebral plate, while the latter only uses the position and area of the grinding of the superior facet joint as a single variable, respectively adapting to two different clinical surgical procedures: unilateral biportal endoscopy or inter-vertebral plate endoscopy and transforaminal endoscopic spine system.

It is important to acknowledge that this study has several limitations that need to be addressed in future research. First, the current study focused exclusively on the biomechanical effects of unilateral lamina and facet joint resection on the L4-5 segment via the posterior interlaminar approach, and did not account for the potential biomechanical impacts associated with the resection of posterolateral (transforaminal) structures. Second, FEA was performed only on a single-segment (L4-5) model, neglecting the biomechanical interactions and cascading effects on other single or multiple lumbar segments, which may limit the comprehensiveness of the study results. Third, the research exclusively constructed and analyzed a finite element model based on the anatomical data of a single healthy volunteer without evident lumbar degenerative changes (such as disc degeneration, osteophyte formation, or ligament laxity), and did not explore the biomechanical characteristics of models with such degenerative alterations, which may affect the applicability of the results to clinical populations with lumbar degenerative diseases. Fourth, the study lacked statistical validation due to the single-sample nature of the finite element model, and the use of anatomical data from a single volunteer may lead to individual-specific biases, reducing the generalizability of the findings. Fifth, although the finite element model included key soft tissue components (such as ligaments, intervertebral discs, and articular cartilage), the dynamic mechanical properties of soft tissues (e.g., muscle contraction tension, ligament creep, and viscoelasticity of the intervertebral disc) were not fully considered, which may have introduced certain deviations into the biomechanical simulation results. In future research, we aim to address these limitations by expanding the sample size, incorporating multi-segment models, including degenerative lumbar spine models, performing statistical validation, and optimizing the simulation of soft tissue biomechanics.

Conclusions

In summary, the results of this finite element study indicate that for the L4-5 segment undergoing full-endoscopic lumbar spine surgery via the posterior interlaminar approach, gradual unilateral laminectomy combined with up to 50% facet joint resection could maintain relative lumbar segmental stability without causing significant biomechanical disorders. In contrast, when the resection exceeds 50%, there would be a substantial risk of lumbar segmental instability, as evidenced by significantly increased ROM and abnormal stress distribution in the intervertebral disc, facet joints, and isthmic region. These findings will provide biomechanical evidence for defining the safe bone resection threshold in minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar surgery. Surgeons may consider preserving at least 50% of the facet joint to avoid postoperative instability. When more extensive resection is inevitable, internal fixation or fusion surgery should be considered.

Future research should incorporate patient-specific degeneration models, multi-segmental interactions, and dynamic muscle force effects to enhance physiological realism. At the same time, longitudinal clinical studies should be conducted to validate these biomechanical findings and develop evidence-based surgical guidelines that strike a balance between adequate decompression and maintaining spinal stability.
