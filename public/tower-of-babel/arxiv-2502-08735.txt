CV4Quantum: Reducing the Sampling Overhead in Probabilistic Error Cancellation Using Control Variates
Prasanth Shyamsundar, Wern Yeen Yeong

Abstract
. Quasiprobabilistic decompositions (QPDs) play a key role in maximizing the utility of near-term quantum hardware. For example, Probabilistic Error Cancellation (PEC) (an error mitigation technique) and circuit cutting (which enables large quantum computations to be performed on quantum hardware with a limited number of qubits) both involve QPDs. Computations based on QPDs typically incur large sampling overheads that grow exponentially, e.g., with the number of error-terms mitigated or the number of circuit-cuts employed, limiting their practical feasibility. In this work, we adapt the control variates variance reduction technique from the statistics literature in order to reduce the sampling overhead in QPD-based computations. We demonstrate our method, dubbed CV4Quantum, using simulation experiments that mimic a realistic PEC scenario. In more than 50% of the PEC-based estimations performed in the study, we observed a more than 50% reduction in the number of samples needed to achieve a given precision when using our technique. We discuss how future research on constructing good control variates can lead to even stronger sampling overhead reduction.

1.2.
Sampling Overhead

The variance of
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{basic}}
is given by

(16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{basic}}}}\right]=\frac{\mathrm{Var}[WX]}{N}\,.

From (
16
), one can see that the number of datapoints needed (i.e., the number of operations
\mathcal{E}_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})}
required to be sampled and performed) in order to reach a given precision in the Monte Carlo estimate is proportional to the variance of
WX
. A difficulty with the Monte Carlo estimation technique is that this variance can be quite large, which results in a significant sampling overhead in the estimation of
T

(
Xiong et al., 2020
;
Piveteau et al., 2022
;
Xiong et al., 2022
;
Chen et al., 2024
)
. This paper is focused on reducing this sampling overhead, by constructing more efficient estimators of
T
than
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{basic}}
, using the control variates variance reduction technique
(
Lemieux, 2017
)
, discussed
Section
2
onwards.

Using the law of total variance
(
Weiss, 2005
)
, the variance of
WX
can be written as

(17)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[WX]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Big.\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[WX\,\big|\,k_{1},\dots,k_{M}}}\right]}}\right]}_{\text{inter-operation variance term}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{~~\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Big.\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[WX\,\big|\,k_{1},\dots,k_{M}}}\right]}}\right]~~}_{\text{intra-operation variance term}}

(18)

\displaystyle=\overbrace{\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Big.W\,\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X\,\big|\,k_{1},\dots,k_{M}}}\right]}}\right]}

\displaystyle+\overbrace{\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Big.W^{2}\,\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X\,\big|\,k_{1},\dots,k_{M}}}\right]}}\right]}\,.

Here the first term captures the effect of
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[WX\,\big|\,k_{1},\dots,k_{M}}}\right]
not being the same for different choices of
(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})
. On the other hand, the second term captures the effect of the measurement
X
, and consequently
WX
, not being fully determined by the choice of
(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})
. We will refer to the first and second terms as the inter- and intra-operation variance terms, respectively. The intra-operation variance term can be reduced simply by increasing the number of shots used to compute
X
after sampling
(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})
. On the other hand, the inter-operation variance term cannot be reduced this way; its contribution to the variance of
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{basic}}
can only be reduced by increasing
N
, the number of times the quantum operations
\mathcal{E}_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})}
are sampled and performed. In current and near-term hardware, the resource-overhead for loading and performing different quantum circuits is much higher than the resource requirements for taking more shots of a given quantum circuit. Consequently, in typical PEC applications, e.g. as in Ref.
(
van den Berg et al., 2023
)
, the inter-operation variance is the dominant source of the QPD sampling overhead. As will be seen later, the CV4Quantum technique only ameliorates the effect of this inter-operation variance (a generalization to ameliorate intra-operation variance is discussed briefly in
Section
5
).

There are situations where the dominant source of sampling overhead is the intra-operation variance term. For example, if the total number of terms
N_{\Pi K}
in the linear combination in (
1
) is sufficiently small (e.g., in a circuit cutting application with only a few gate-cuts), then one can perform every operation operation
\mathcal{E}_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})}
in the summation, avoiding the MC estimation estimation technique discussed in this section entirely. In this case the only source of uncertainty is the intra-operation variance term. This is the case, for example, in the analysis of sampling overhead in Ref.
(
Mitarai and Fujii, 2021
)
; the techniques of this paper cannot be directly used to ameliorate the sampling overhead in such situations.

For completeness, next we will discuss the sampling overhead in terms of the
\gamma

(19)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[W]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}^{2}[W]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[W^{2}}}\right]=-\mu_{W}^{2}+\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[~\sum_{k_{m}=1}^{K_{m}}\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|q_{m}(k_{m})}}\right|^{2}}{p_{m}(k_{m})}~}}\right]\,.

This variance is minimized if the sampling distributions

are chosen to be proportional to

as follows:

(20)

\displaystyle p^{\text{(special-case)}}_{m}(k_{m})

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|q_{m}(k_{m})}}\right|}{\gamma_{m}}\,,\qquad\qquad\text{where}\quad\gamma_{m}\equiv\sum_{k_{m}=1}^{K_{m}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|q_{m}(k_{m})}}\right|\,.

This is a popular choice for the sampling distribution; in this case, the weights

and

are given by

(21)

\displaystyle w^{\text{(special-case)}}_{m}(k_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{sgn}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Big.q_{m}(k_{m})}}\right)\,\gamma_{m}\,,

(22)

\displaystyle W^{\text{(special-case)}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathrm{sgn}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\Big.q_{m}(k_{m})}}\right)}}\right]\,\gamma\,,\qquad\qquad\text{where}\quad\gamma\equiv\prod_{m=1}^{M}\gamma_{m}\,.

Here
\mathrm{sgn}
is the sign function. The corresponding weight-variance is given by

(23)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}^{\text{(special-case)}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[W^{\text{(special-case)}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}-\mu_{W}^{2}\,.

In this way, it can be seen that a larger value of
\gamma
corresponds to a larger variance of
W
. The connection between
\gamma
and the variance of
WX
can be seen easily for the special case when

is a random variable with a constant magnitude, say

. In this case, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}^{\text{(special-case)}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[W^{\text{(special-case)}}X_{\text{(constant-mod)}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\,a^{2}-T^{2}\,.

This demonstrates that a larger value of
\gamma
corresponds to a larger variance of
WX
, and thus a larger sampling overhead. This feature generally holds true a) for both the inter- and intra-operation variance terms, and b) even when
X
does not have a constant magnitude. Note that similar to
N_{\Pi K}
,
\gamma
also scales exponentially in
M
.

N_{\Pi K}
, if
\gamma

As a result, the sampling overhead grows exponentially, e.g., with circuit-depth in PEC, or with the number of wire- and gate-cuts in circuit cutting.

The value of

2.
Methodology

2.1.
Introduction to the Control Variates Technique

Control Variates techniques
(
Lemieux, 2017
)
is a popular Monte Carlo variance reduction technique. The basic idea is as follows: Let
U
be a random variable whose expected value
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U}}\right]
we are interested in estimating, and let
V
be a random variable whose expected value
\mu
is known a priori. Now, one can create a one-parameter family of random variables, say
U_{\lambda}
, given by

(25)

\displaystyle U_{\lambda}\equiv U-\lambda(V-\mu)\,,\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\,,

such that
\mathbb{E}[U_{\lambda}]=\mathbb{E}[U]
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Here
V
is referred to as a control variate. Although
U_{\lambda}
has the same expectation as
U
, it could have a different variance. Concretely, using Bienaymé’s identity
(
Klenke, 2013
)
, the variance of
U_{\lambda}
can be written as

(26)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[U_{\lambda}]=\mathrm{Var}[U]+\lambda^{2}\mathrm{Var}[V]-2\,\lambda\,\mathrm{Cov}[U,V]\,,

where
\mathrm{Cov}[U,V]
represents the covariance of
U
and
V
.
\mathrm{Var}[U_{\lambda}]
is minimized by setting the coefficient
\lambda
to
\lambda^{\ast}
given by

(27)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\ast}\equiv\begin{cases}\frac{\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}[U,V]}{\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[V]}\,,&\qquad\qquad\text{if }\mathrm{Var}[V]\neq 0\,,\\
\quad 0\,,&\qquad\qquad\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

The corresponding minimum variance is given by

(28)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[U_{\lambda^{\ast}}]

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}~~\mathrm{Var}[U]-\frac{\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}^{2}[U,V]}{\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}[V]}=\mathrm{Var}[U]\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\rho_{(U,V)}^{2}}}\right)\,,&\qquad\quad\text{if }\mathrm{Var}[V]\neq 0\,,\\[5.0pt]
~~\mathrm{Var}[U]\,,&\qquad\quad\text{otherwise}\,.\end{cases}

where
\rho_{(U,V)}
is the Pearson correlation coefficient of
U
and
V
. Thus, if
V
is correlated to
U
, (
28
) provides a way to construct a random variable with the same expectation as
U
but a lower variance, thereby ameliorating the sampling overhead—the stronger the correlation between
U
and
V
, the greater the variance reduction. In the context of this paper,
U
will be identified with
WX
, the random variable whose mean is to be estimated. We will postpone the discussion of suitable control variates to
Section
2.2
.

The control variates technique can also be generalized to include multiple control variates, say
V_{1},\dots,V_{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}
, with means
\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}
, respectively. In this case, one can create an
N_{\mathrm{cv}}
-parameter family of random variables
U_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N_{\mathrm{cv}}})}
given by

(29)

\displaystyle U_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N_{\mathrm{cv}}})}\equiv U-\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}\lambda_{a}\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V_{a}-\mu_{a}}}\right)\,,\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}\in\mathbb{R}\,,

with the same expectation as
U
. Again using Bienaymé’s identity, the variance of
U_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N_{\mathrm{cv}}})}
can be written as

(30)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N_{\mathrm{cv}}})}}}\right]=\mathrm{Var}[U]+\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}\lambda_{a}\,\lambda_{b}\,K_{ab}-2\,\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}\lambda_{a}\,\mathrm{Cov}[U,V_{a}]\,,

where
K_{ab}
is the the covariance of
V_{a}
and
V_{b}
or, equivalently, the
(a,b)
-th element of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{K}
of the control variates.
\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{N_{\mathrm{cv}}})}}}\right]
is minimized by setting
\lambda_{a}
-s to
\lambda^{\ast}_{a}
-s given by

(31)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\ast}_{a}=\sum_{b=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}K^{+}_{ab}\,\mathrm{Cov}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U,V_{b}}}\right]\,,

where
K^{+}_{ab}
is the
(a,b)
-th element of the Moore–Penrose inverse (or the pseudoinverse)
\mathbf{K}^{+}
of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{K}
. The corresponding minimum variance is given by

(32)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{(\lambda^{\ast}_{1},\dots,\lambda^{\ast}_{N_{\mathrm{cv}}})}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}[U]-\sum_{a,b=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}K^{+}_{ab}\,\mathrm{Cov}[U,V_{a}]\,\mathrm{Cov}[U,V_{b}]\,.

The variance of

unexplained
by
(

Note that the values of

2.2.
Constructing Control Variates

In order to use the control variates technique for QPD sampling overhead reduction, we need random variables with a priori known (i.e., pre-computable) expectations to use as controls. Note that the weight
W
itself can be used as a control, with its mean
\mu_{W}
pre-computed using (
15
). Likewise, the constituent weights
w_{m}
can also be used as controls, with their means pre-computed using (
14
). More generically, one can create a random variable
V_{\mathrm{fac}}
, which is a factorizable function of
(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})
, as follows:

(33)

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{fac}}(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})

\displaystyle\equiv\prod_{m=1}^{M}v_{m}(k_{m})\,.

Here each
v_{m}
is an arbitrary real-valued function of
k_{m}\in\{1,\dots,K_{m}\}
.
V_{\mathrm{fac}}
can be used as a control, with its expected value pre-computed as follows:

(34)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V_{\mathrm{fac}}}}\right]

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=1}^{M}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[v_{m}}}\right]\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[v_{m}}}\right]=\sum_{k_{m}=1}^{K_{m}}p_{m}(k_{m})\,v_{m}(k_{m})\,.

The full weight
W
as well as the constituent weights
w_{m}
are all special cases of the generic factorizable control variate
V_{\mathrm{fac}}
. In addition to factorizable functions like
V_{\mathrm{fac}}
, their linear combinations, e.g.,
a\,V_{\mathrm{fac}}+b\,V^{\prime}_{\mathrm{fac}}
, can also be used as control variates. The mean of the linear combination will simply be the linear combination of the means, which can be pre-computed easily as long as the number of terms in the linear combination is practicable. Note that by constructing control variates as functions of
k_{m}
-s, we are facilitating correlations between the controls and
WX
(which itself is influenced by the
k_{m}
-s). Such correlations are crucial for variance reduction using the control variates technique. Also, because these control variates are deterministic functions of
(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})
, they can only reduce the effect of the inter-operation variance term in (
18
), and not the effect of the intra-operation variance term.

We end this subsection by noting that in addition to the expectations, other statistical properties like covariance can also be pre-computed for control variates of the forms discussed in this subsection. As an illustration, for pairs of factorizable control variates of the form in (
33
), say
V_{\mathrm{fac}}
and
V^{\prime}_{\mathrm{fac}}
, the covariance can be pre-computed using the formula

(35)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Cov}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V_{\mathrm{fac}}\,,\,V^{\prime}_{\mathrm{fac}}}}\right]\equiv\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V_{\mathrm{fac}}\,V^{\prime}_{\mathrm{fac}}}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V_{\mathrm{fac}}}}\right]\,\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[V^{\prime}_{\mathrm{fac}}}}\right]\,,

by noting that the product
V_{\mathrm{fac}}\,V^{\prime}_{\mathrm{fac}}
is also a factorizable function of the
k_{m}
-s. For a pair of control variates, each of which is a linear combination of factorizable control variates, one can compute the covariance using the distributive property of covariance over addition, as long as the numbers of terms in the linear combinations are practicable.

2.3.
Existence of a “Golden-Ticket” Control Variate

As a special case of the

2.4.
The Effectiveness of Weight as a Control

As noted above, the dimensionality of the space of deterministic functions of
(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})
could be very large. As such, if one picks a control variate randomly from this high-dimensional function space (using some arbitrary sampling distribution), one would expect the correlation between the control and

Let us consider the performance of the weight

2.5.
Adapting the Standard Control Variates Technique

Having described a method to construct control variates for QPD-estimations, next we will turn to the task of calculating good values for the coefficients
\lambda_{a}
in (
29
). From (
31
), the optimal coefficients
\lambda^{\ast}_{a}
for

is given by

(42)

\displaystyle\lambda^{\ast}_{a}

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}K^{+}_{ab}\,\mathrm{Cov}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[W\,X,V_{b}}}\right]

(43)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}K^{+}_{ab}\,\mathrm{Cov}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Big.W\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\big.X-\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X}}\right]}}\right),V_{b}}}\right]+\sum_{b=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}K^{+}_{ab}\,\mathrm{Cov}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Big.W\,\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X}}\right],V_{b}}}\right]

(44)

\displaystyle=\sum_{b=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}K^{+}_{ab}\,\mathrm{Cov}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Big.X,W\,\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\big.V_{b}-\mu_{b}}}\right)}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[X}}\right]\,\sum_{b=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}K^{+}_{ab}\,C_{b}\,,

where
C_{a}
is the covariance of
W
and
V_{a}
:

(45)

\displaystyle C_{a}\equiv\mathrm{Cov}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[W,V_{a}}}\right]\,.

In general the value of
\lambda_{a}^{\ast}
is not known a priori. In such situations, the general strategy is to use a data-based-estimate for the
\lambda^{\ast}_{a}
-s as the coefficients for the control variates. Explicitly, the corresponding control-variate-based estimator for
T
would be given by

(46)

\displaystyle\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{standard-cv}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[W^{(i)}\,X^{(i)}-\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}\widehat{\lambda}^{\ast}_{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V_{a}^{(i)}-\mu_{a}}}\right)}}\right]

Here,
V_{a}^{(i)}
is the value of the
a
-th control variate for the
i
-th datapoint.
\widehat{\lambda}^{\ast}_{a}
-s are data-based-estimates for
\lambda^{\ast}_{a}
—they are functions of the full dataset, which includes the values of
X^{(i)}
,
W^{(i)}
, and
V_{a}^{(i)}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
and
a\in\{1,\dots,N_{\mathrm{cv}}\}
. Here we will modify this standard approach in the following ways.

2.5.1.
Eliminating Bias

Since the coefficients
\widehat{\lambda}^{\ast}_{a}
are functions of the random data, they are themselves random variables that could be correlated with the values of
V_{a}^{(i)}
. As a result,
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{standard-cv}}
is not an unbiased estimator for
T
. As the number of datapoints used to estimate
\widehat{\lambda}^{\ast}_{a}
increases, the bias in
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{standard-cv}}
will reduce in magnitude, since the correlation between
\widehat{\lambda}^{\ast}_{a}
and
V_{a}^{(i)}
for any given
i
reduces in magnitude. In many applications of the control variates technique, the number of datapoints is large enough for the bias to be negligible. However, this may not be the case in quantum computing applications, so we modify

\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{standard-cv}}

as follows to eliminate the bias:

(47)

\displaystyle\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{unbiased-cv}}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[W^{(i)}\,X^{(i)}-\sum_{a=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}\lambda^{(\neg i)}_{a}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(V_{a}^{(i)}-\mu_{a}}}\right)}}\right]\,,

where
\lambda^{(\neg i)}_{a}
is an estimate for
\lambda^{\ast}_{a}
that uses all the datapoints except the
i
-th one. Each datapoint
i
will have a different value for
\lambda^{(\neg i)}_{a}
. Since
\lambda^{(\neg i)}_{a}
is independent of
V_{a}^{(i)}
,
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{unbiased-cv}}
will be unbiased, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{unbiased-cv}}}}\right]=T
. There are other ways to eliminate the bias, e.g., a) splitting the dataset into two halves, and for each half of the dataset, using the value of
\widehat{\lambda}^{\ast}_{a}
estimated from the other half, b) using a small fraction of datapoints
only
for estimating
\widehat{\lambda}^{\ast}_{a}
. However, we avoid such approaches here because they a) lead to estimators that are not invariant under permutations of the dataset, and b) only use a small fraction of the dataset (which is likely to be small to begin with in near-term quantum applications) to estimate the coefficients.

2.5.2.
Enforcing Shift Invariance

Under the transformation
X\mapsto X+\delta
, it is easy to see that
T
transforms as
T\mapsto T+\mu_{W}\,\delta
. This motivates the definition of a shift invariance property for estimators of
T
. Let us define an estimator
\widehat{T}
for
T
as being shift invariant if, under the transformation
X^{(i)}\mapsto X^{(i)}+\delta
, the estimator transforms as
\widehat{T}\mapsto\widehat{T}+\mu_{W}\,\delta
. Somewhat surprisingly, the basic Monte Carlo estimator
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{basic}}
is not a shift invariant estimator
8
8

8

This lack of shift-invariance was the original clue which pointed us towards the use of control variates for this problem.
, since it transforms as

(48)

\displaystyle\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{basic}}\mapsto\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{basic}}+\frac{\delta}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}W^{(i)}\not\equiv\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{basic}}+\mu_{W}\,\delta\,.

If one uses an estimator that is not shift invariant, the estimated value of

would be different if one simply shifts

by
\delta\neq 0
before performing the QPD estimation (and applies the appropriate inverse-shift of

after the estimation). This is arguably inelegant, so shift-invariance is a desirable property for estimators of

; we will design our control-variate-based estimators to satisfy this property.
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{unbiased-cv}}
will be shift invariant if the following two conditions are both satisfied (proved as

A.3
in

A

(1)

(W-\mu_{W})
lies in the span of the random variables
(V_{a}-\mu_{a})

(2)

\displaystyle\lambda^{(\neg i)}_{a}\mapsto\lambda^{(\neg i)}_{a}+\delta\sum_{b=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}K^{+}_{ab}\,C_{b}

The first condition can be enforced simply by including

(or a variable proportional to

) as one of the controls. In order to enforce the second condition, we will use the expression for
\lambda^{\ast}

(49)

\displaystyle\lambda^{(\neg i)}_{a}

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{b=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}K^{+}_{ab}\,S_{b}^{(\neg i)}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{1}{N-1}\sum_{j\neq i}X^{(j)}}}\right)\sum_{b=1}^{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}K^{+}_{ab}\,C_{b}\,,

where

is the sample covariance of

computed using all the datapoints except the
i
-th one. Note that the values of

and

can be pre-computed for the control variates constructed and used in this paper, as discussed in
Section
2.2
.
The Moore–Penrose inverse of

2.5.3.
Error Estimation

The basic Monte Carlo estimator
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{basic}}
is the sample mean of
N
independent and identically distributed random variables
W^{(i)}\,X^{(i)}
. In this case, one can estimate the error in
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{basic}}
, e.g., as
(1/\sqrt{N})
times the sample standard deviation of
W^{(i)}\,X^{(i)}
. On the other hand, because of the data dependence of the coefficients
\lambda^{(\neg i)}_{a}
, the control-variate-based estimators are sample means of identically distributed and
dependent
(possibly correlated) random variables. In such situations, the aforementioned error-estimation-technique could underestimate the uncertainty of the estimator. The degree of this underestimation decreases as the number of datapoints
N
increases—if
N
is sufficiently large this problem can be ignored. However, to cover the case where
N
is not very large, in
Section
3
, we provide some error estimation formulas pertaining to our Cv4Quantum estimators. These formulas, which are derived in
Appendix
B
, include higher order correction terms to provide a better uncertainty estimate.

2.6.
Choosing the Number of Controls

Adding more controls to a given set of control variates cannot increase the minimum variance
\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{\lambda_{1}^{\ast},\dots,\lambda_{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}^{\ast}}}}\right]
in (
32
). In fact, under some weak conditions, adding more controls will strictly decrease
\mathrm{Var}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[U_{\lambda_{1}^{\ast},\dots,\lambda_{N_{\mathrm{cv}}}^{\ast}}}}\right]
. This suggests that one should use as many control variates as is computationally feasible in order to improve the variance reduction achieved. However, note that in practice one does not have access to the optimal coefficients
\lambda_{a}^{\ast}
, and instead one uses coefficients estimated from data; using poorly estimated coefficients can lead to a poor estimation of
T
. Since one only has access to a finite-sized dataset, increasing the number of controls indefinitely will naturally lead to poorly estimated coefficients and, consequently, worsening of the errors in the estimate of
T
(this is reflected in the error estimation formula in
Section
3
,
Algorithm
3
).

The prudent approach is to find a good compromise between the potential improvement from incorporating more controls and the deterioration resulting from the poorer estimation of the corresponding coefficients. The number of controls
N_{\mathrm{cv}}
one uses should depend on the number of data points
N
used in the estimation—a larger value of
N
should allow for a larger value of
N_{\mathrm{cv}}
. Providing a more concrete prescription for how to choose the number of controls is beyond the scope of this paper.

We use the Pauli–Lindblad noise model studied in Ref.
(

(66)

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{Paulis}}

\displaystyle=(4\times 3)+(3\times 9)=39\,,\quad

\displaystyle\text{for 4-qubit-circuits}\,,

(67)

\displaystyle N_{\mathrm{Paulis}}

\displaystyle=(10\times 3)+(9\times 9)=111\,,\quad

\displaystyle\text{for 10-qubit-circuits}\,.

4.1.3.
PEC Implementation

Our PEC implementation, described here for completeness, also mirrors Ref.
(
van den Berg et al., 2023
)
. The inverse of
\Lambda_{\text{\sc t}}
is given by

(70)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{\sc t}}^{-1}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Lambda_{\text{\sc t},i}^{-1}\circ\dots\circ\Lambda_{\text{\sc t},N_{\mathrm{paulis}}}^{-1}}}\right](\rho)\,,\qquad\qquad\text{where}\,,

(71)

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{\sc t},i}^{-1}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-2\,\epsilon_{\text{\sc t},i}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\Big.\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(1-\epsilon_{\text{\sc t},i}}}\right)\,\rho-\epsilon_{\text{\sc t},i}\,P_{i}\,\rho\,P_{i}^{\dagger}}}\right]\,.

Note that the operations
\Lambda_{\text{\sc t},i}^{-1}
and
\Lambda_{\text{\sc t},j}^{-1}
commute with each other. The effect of the noise on the computation-results can be cancelled by quasi-probabilistically performing these inverse channels immediately
before
performing the corresponding noisy two-qubit-gate-layers. Operationally, for every local Pauli noise-term
\Lambda_{\text{\sc t},i}
(of every noisy layer in every Trotterization step in the circuit),
one quasi-probabilistically performs
\Lambda_{\text{\sc t},i}^{-1}
by either
inserting or not inserting (with a certain probability) the corresponding Pauli-gate
P_{i}
, immediately before the layer.
The resulting random PEC “mitigation circuit instance” corresponds to the operation
\mathcal{E}_{(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})}
in (
1
).

Under the notation used in this paper,
M
is the total number of such binary decisions to be made to generate a single mitigation circuit instance.

(72)

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle=4\times N_{\mathrm{Paulis}}\times n_{\mathrm{trot}}\,,

(73)

\displaystyle K_{m}

\displaystyle=2\,,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\forall m\in\{1,\dots,M\}\,.

For each value of
m
, let
\epsilon(m)
represent the error rate
\epsilon_{\text{\sc t},i}
of the corresponding noise-term, and let
k_{m}
being
1
and
2
correspond to the choice of not inserting and inserting the corresponding Pauli, respectively. Under our notation

(74)

\displaystyle q_{m}(1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\epsilon(m)}{1-2\,\epsilon(m)}\,,\qquad\qquad q_{m}(2)=\frac{-\,\epsilon(m)}{1-2\,\epsilon(m)}\,.

Following the standard practice, we chose the PEC sampling probabilities
p_{m}
to be proportional to the absolute value of
q_{m}
:

(75)

\displaystyle p_{m}(1)

\displaystyle=1-\epsilon(m)\,,\qquad

\displaystyle p_{m}(2)=\epsilon(m)\,,

(76)

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow w_{m}(1)

\displaystyle=+\gamma_{m}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle w_{m}(2)=-\gamma_{m}\,,\qquad\qquad\text{where}\quad\gamma_{m}=\frac{1}{1-2\,\epsilon(m)}\,.

For the 4-qubit circuits, the value of

All the quantum circuit simulations in this study were performed using Qiskit
(
Qiskit contributors, 2023
)

In addition to these, for each target circuit, we also performed a) noisy simulations without PEC (

7\times(2+(1+200)\times 2\times 1024)=2{,}881{,}550

12

We will refer to the estimation of a given observable for a given target circuit as an “estimation task” in the rest of this paper; a total of
(15\times 2\times 5)+(7\times 2\times 11)=304

4.4.
Note on numerical stability

The value of
M
gets sufficiently large in our experiments that one can run into underflow or overflow issues with finite precision arithmetic, when multiplying
M
numbers to compute the controls as in
(
81
).
16
16

16

If
Y\equiv y_{1}\times\dots\times y_{M}
, with the
y_{m}
-s being independent random variables, the dynamic range (DR) (i.e., ratio between the largest and smallest values) of
|Y|
equals the product of DRs of
|y_{m}|
-s. In this way, the DR of
|Y|
grows exponentially in
M
.
Similarly, the expected value of
Y^{2}
(or
|Y|
) is the product of the expected values of
y_{m}^{2}
-s (or
|y_{m}|
-s); this can also grow exponentially if left unchecked. The scaling factors in (
81
) solve the latter issue by ensuring that
\mathbb{E}[V_{a}^{2}]=1
, but this does not solve the DR issue. Note that the DR issue is not encountered when multiplying
w_{m}
-s of the form
\pm\gamma_{m}
to compute
W
, since the DR of each
|w_{m}|
is 1 in this case.
To mitigate this problem, we use log-domain computations where necessary; this involves representing each real-valued quantity
r
with the pair
(\mathrm{sgn}(r),\log(|r|))
, and performing a) multiplications/divisions using additions/subtractions of the logarithms and b) summations using a numerically stable implementation of the log-sum-exp operation
(
Virtanen et al., 2020
)
. No underflow/overflow issues were encountered with this measure in place.

Summary, Conclusions, and Outlook

In this work, we have proposed using the control variates variance reduction technique to decrease the sampling overhead in quantum computations based on quasi-probabilistic decompositions. We adapted the standard control variates technique in a few ways, in order to make the technique more appropriate for QPD-based estimations. We also described a generic way to construct control variates for use with our technique. Collectively, the techniques introduced in this paper have been dubbed “CV4Quantum”. We applied our method to probabilistic error cancellation in a realistic simulations-based study, and found significant reductions in sampling overheads in many of the PEC estimations considered. In the rest of this section, we will discuss some directions for future research.

As discussed in this paper, the performance gain achieved by our technique depends on the effectiveness of the control variates used for the particular estimation task at hand. The construction of effective controls to use with our technique could be an impactful line of research. To motivate this, one can consider the ceiling on the variance reduction achievable using the control variates technique. As discussed in
Section
2.3
, the a priori unknown “golden-ticket” control variate can completely eliminate the inter-operation variance term in (
18
). The corresponding performance improvement can be translated into a ‘‘ceiling DAF’’ and a ‘‘ceiling SORP’’ value.
21
21

21

This was done by estimating the intra- and inter- operation variances from the experimental data.
In our study, the
25^{\mathrm{th}}
,
50^{\mathrm{th}}
,
75^{\mathrm{th}}
, and
90^{\mathrm{th}}
percentiles (over the 304 estimation tasks) of the ceiling DAF (ceiling SORP) values are 4.55 (78%), 35.03 (97%), 145.43 (99.3%), 405.54 (99.7%), respectively. Comparing this to
Table
4
, we see that there is room to improve the variance reduction by refining the choice of control variates. This is understandable because a) the inter-operation variance is the dominant source of uncertainty in our study and b) refining the choice of controls for each estimation task was not a goal of this paper.

In this paper, we restricted ourselves to controls whose expectations may be exactly precomputed. However, the control variates technique may also be used with controls whose expectations are only approximately known (e.g., estimated using Monte Carlo sampling), as long as the uncertainties on the means of the controls are comparatively small, as explained in Section 3.2 of Ref.
(
Botev and Ridder, 2017
)
. This opens up the possibility of using techniques like machine learning to construct/train functions of the indices
(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})
that are highly correlated with the observable whose expectation is to be estimated.

A seemingly simple strategy for finding effective controls is the following: Perform the estimation using multiple sets of control variates, and use the estimate
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{cv}}
with the smallest associated uncertainty
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{\sigma}}}_{\text{cv}}
. However, since
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{\sigma}}}_{\text{cv}}
is only an
estimate
(with random fluctuations) for the standard deviation of
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{T}}}_{\!\text{cv}}
, picking the minimum
\lx@scalerel@obj{\hat{\lx@scalerel@obj{\sigma}}}_{\text{cv}}
over several sets of control variates could lead to underestimated uncertainties due to the look-elsewhere effect. One should be wary of this when using any approach to construct or distill good controls using data, including the ML-based approach and the try-multiple-sets-of-controls approach described here. A foolproof procedure would be to first use a subset of the available experimental data to construct good controls, and then use these controls with the rest of the experimental data to perform the actual estimation, without a look-elsewhere effect.

The CV4Quantum as described in this paper works only for reducing the inter-operation variance in (
18
). However, it can be extended to tackle the intra-operation variance as well, by creating controls that are not deterministic functions of the indices
(k_{1},\dots,k_{M})
. More specifically, in addition to the observable of interest
X
, one can have auxiliary observables, say
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots
, whose noiseless expectations are exactly known. These auxiliary observables can be measurements on some auxiliary qubits whose final state, at the end of the noiseless computation, is exactly known. They can also be some special combinations (with known noiseless expectations) of the measurements of the primary qubits. Using such
Y_{i}
-s as controls, one can potentially reduce the intra-operation variance. Exploring this idea is left for future work.
