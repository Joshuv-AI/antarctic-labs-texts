The association between maternal multivitamin intake and premature birth
Authors: Jia Sun, Yiming Zhang, Zhengmin Qian, Pamela K. Xaverius, Hong Xian, Morgan LeBaige
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In urban settings, unsupervised maternal multivitamin use is common, but its impact on premature birth (PTB) remains unclear. We conducted a secondary analysis of a nested case–control study within a population-based prospective cohort in Wuhan, China (2011–2013), including 6656 births. Maternal multivitamin intake was assessed during the preconceptional period (three months before pregnancy) and the prenatal period (during pregnancy). Multivariable logistic regression models were used, adjusting for socio-demographic, lifestyle, and clinical factors. Multivitamin use during both the preconceptional and prenatal periods was associated with higher odds of medically-indicated PTB (adjusted OR: 1.54, 95% CI 1.18, 2.03). Causality cannot be confirmed due to limited exposure assessment, residual confounding, and potential reverse causation. Maternal intake behaviors and whether multivitamins provide adequate levels of key nutrients may also influence outcomes, highlighting the need for further investigation. While multivitamins are important, they cannot replace medical treatments for preventing PTB. Monitoring maternal vitamin levels and providing clear guidance from healthcare providers are essential to ensure safe and effective use and to protect maternal and neonatal health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56290-2.

Introduction

Multivitamins are widely available as over-the-counter dietary supplements, meaning that many pregnant women take them without direct medical supervision. However, their effects can be influenced by underlying health status, dietary patterns, nutrient interactions, maternal behaviors, and environmental exposures1–7. Maternal multivitamin intake has been studied in controlled experimental settings for premature birth (PTB)8,9, but evidence from real-world clinical practice shows inconsistent associations10,11.

Fetal development increases vitamin demands beyond maternal nutrient stores, particularly in underweight women12,13. Multivitamins are therefore important for supporting placental development and fetal growth10–12. Although women can obtain vitamins from diet, some cannot meet pregnancy dietary recommendations10,14–17. In nutritionally insecure populations, vitamin deficiencies have been linked to PTB13–16,18,19, and supplementation is recommended10,11,17. In developed countries, evidence is less consistent, potentially due to differences in study design, inconsistencies in covariate adjustment, and residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as maternal health, diet, lifestyle, and insurance coverage10–12.

Few studies in China have examined maternal multivitamin intake and PTB. In our previous analysis of women with a family history of hypertension in China, multivitamin use was associated with lower PTB risk20; however, whether this association applies to the general pregnant population, including those without a family history, remains unclear. To address this gap, we conducted a study using data from a population-based prospective cohort nested case–control study21 to examine maternal multivitamin intake during preconception and/or prenatal periods and PTB risk. According to our literature review, this study provides the first evidence of the impact of unsupervised multivitamin use on both spontaneous and medically-indicated PTB in an urban Chinese population.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study is a secondary data analysis of a nested case–control study within a prospective cohort of 95,911 pregnant women in the core urban areas of Wuhan21. The cohort included 95,911 live births that met their inclusion criteria, representing 68.8% of all births in the core district of Wuhan21. In this setting, all inpatient and outpatient prenatal care and delivery services provided within the Wuhan Medical and Health Center for Women and Children (WMHCWC) system were captured in its electronic medical record system, as all deliveries and related maternal care in the core district were managed and recorded by the WMHCWC21. PTBs were identified within 10 days of delivery, and controls were randomly selected from the same cohort and matched to PTBs by newborn’s birth date and residential community (based on maternal addresses)21. WMHCWC conducted home-visit interviews for all PTBs and controls within 42 days postpartum, achieving a 63.8% response rate21. The final sample included 2,010 spontaneous PTBs, 383 medically-indicated PTBs, and 4263 controls. The mean maternal age was 27.46 years. No missing data were identified for the variables included in the analysis.

Premature birth data

According to the World Health Organization, PTB is a live birth of less than 37 complete weeks of pregnancy22. Controls were defined in the original nested case–control study as full-term deliveries (> 37 completed weeks of pregnancy) with a normal weight of 2,500–4,500 g21. All PTBs and controls identified in the original study21 were included in the present analysis. Medically-indicated PTB was defined as PTB resulting from maternal medical conditions; otherwise, PTB was classified as spontaneous PTB20.

Maternal multivitamin intake and potential confounders

WMHCWC trained nurses and medical staff collected information on maternal multivitamin intake and potential confounders from structured questionnaires21. Potential confounders included demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status, lifestyle behaviors, sleeping duration, physical activity, diet, psychosocial stress, and environmental exposures.

Maternal multivitamin intake was defined based on responses to two questionnaire items regarding use during the three months prior to pregnancy and during pregnancy. Participants were asked whether they took multivitamins during these two periods. “Yes” to both indicated use in both the preconception and prenatal periods; “yes” during pregnancy only indicated prenatal use; “yes” during the three months prior to pregnancy only indicated preconception use; and “no” to both indicated nonuse.

Confounders were selected based on two criteria: first, literature indicating they are potential confounders of the association under study; and second, they collectively altered the effect estimates by more than 5%20,21,23. They were defined and measured as follows: maternal age at delivery was classified into < 21, 21–30, and > 30 years; separate grouping for > 35 was not appropriate due to limited statistical power (few participants in this group). Household income was defined as the maternal monthly household income and categorized based on our literature review (< 7000 RMB, ≥ 7000 RMB)24. Maternal health problems were defined based on self-reported history of medical conditions before and during pregnancy, including diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disease, anemia, hepatitis, epilepsy, heart disease, asthma, renal disease, depression, severe chest tightness, anxiety, threat of abortion, vomiting, dehydration, cerclage/suture for incompetent cervix, preeclampsia, toxemia, placental abruption, premature rupture of membranes, vaginal bleeding, blood transfusion, accident, cold/fever, diarrhea, and pneumonia. Maternal self-rated health was defined as maternal self-perceived overall health during pregnancy (poor, fair, good, very good, and excellent). Family history of premature birth was defined as maternal immediate mother or sisters having PTB (yes/no). Prenatal care utilization was assessed using the Chinese Ministry of Health guidelines, based on the timing of prenatal care initiation and completion of recommended visits (inadequate, intermediate, adequate, and adequate plus care)25. Maternal vegetable and fruit intake was measured via maternal self-reported daily consumption of vegetables and fruits in servings (pre-pregnancy, first trimester, and after the first trimester). Maternal folic acid intake during pregnancy (yes/no), pre-pregnancy BMI (calculated from weight and height), parity (0, ≥ 1), and second-hand smoking exposure (yes/no) were self-reported via questionnaire.

More than 99% of participants in the original study were non-drinkers, non-smokers, and married21; therefore, these variables were not considered as confounders in our analysis.

Statistical methods

We summarized participant characteristics according to PTB status and multivitamin use. We then built a series of regression models to evaluate the association between maternal multivitamin intake and PTB, using binary logistic regression for overall PTB and multinomial logistic regression for PTB subtypes. First, we fitted crude models including multivitamin intake only. We then added a priori covariates consistently adjusted for in previous studies of this association. Finally, additional covariates identified in our confounder analysis were included, resulting in the final models. Crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to quantify the associations (see Supplementary Table S1 online).

Sensitivity analysis

Although education was excluded from the main models due to collinearity with maternal age and household income, we remained concerned that its omission might introduce residual confounding. Therefore, we conducted a sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for education21,23.

Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P value below 0.05. We used SAS University Edition (SAS Institute Inc. 2018) for statistical analyses and R (R Core Team, 2024) for data visualization.

Ethical approval

Our study protocol was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of Saint Louis University, MO, USA. Patients’ informed consents were obtained prior to their participation in our study. We do not disclose any information that could be used to identify our study participants (IRB#: 16,744, approval date 1 August 2008).

Results

Descriptive results

Spontaneous PTB was more likely to occur among women who were multiparous, having self-rated health as good, having family history of PTB, exposed to second-hand smoke, and having intermediate prenatal care. Model analyses

Figure 1 shows the results of the final models examining the associations between maternal multivitamin intake and PTB. Discussion

This study was based on data from a prospective cohort nested case–control study in Wuhan, China (2011–2013). Maternal multivitamin intake during both the preconceptional and prenatal periods was significantly associated with higher odds of medically-indicated PTB. After adjusting for potential confounders, including maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, household income, parity, health problems, self-rated health, family history of PTB, second-hand smoking, prenatal care utilization, folic acid intake, vegetable intake, and fruit intake, the estimates did not change significantly. We did not observe evidence of a direct relationship between maternal multivitamin intake and reduced risk of PTB. However, post-hoc power analysis suggests that the non-significant findings may be due to limited statistical power and should be interpreted with caution, whereas this limitation does not apply to the significant result (see Supplementary Table S1 online).

Our results are consistent with previous findings among young nulliparous women in developed countries, where maternal multivitamin intake was not significantly associated with PTB11,27–31, suggesting that multivitamins alone are not effective in preventing PTB20,32–34. We aimed to investigate whether multivitamin intake during specific preconceptional and prenatal periods is associated with PTB, as these windows may correspond to critical stages of fetal development35, however, no clear window was identified. Only combined preconceptional and prenatal multivitamin use was associated with higher odds of medically-indicated PTB. One possible explanation is that women who take multivitamins throughout both periods may differ in underlying risk profiles or clinical management. To account for this, we adjusted for major maternal health conditions that, based on our clinical experience, might lead physicians to recommend multivitamin use, but the results did not change significantly. Nevertheless, residual confounding by unmeasured or inadequately measured factors may still contribute to the observed association, such as air pollution36. Another possibility is that some women may have relied on multivitamins rather than seeking medical advice for specific health conditions. Additionally, certain maternal conditions may require higher amounts of specific vitamins, minerals, or nutrients than most multivitamin products can provide16,37–39. For example, pregnant women with anemia may need iron doses higher than those contained in most multivitamin products33,40. Two studies in China reported that supplementation with certain nutrients, such as vitamin E, fat, and iron-folic acid, rather than multivitamins, decreased PTB risk37,41. Therefore, the amount of multivitamin consumed in our study population might not have been sufficient to meet the levels of these nutrients needed to reduce PTB risk.

Inconsistent findings have been reported in previous studies. A Danish cohort study found a relationship between periconceptional multivitamin intake and a reduced risk of early PTB28,29. A similar protective effect of maternal multivitamin use during the first and second trimesters was reported in a U.S. study42. Although these studies suggested that the timing of intake might influence PTB risk, the underlying mechanism remains unclear28,29,42. In contrast, our study did not observe evidence of a distinct critical window for this association. In our study, women generally reported higher vegetable and fruit intake during these periods compared with U.S. dietary recommendations43 (Table 2). As these foods are natural sources of vitamins, our study population may have been less prone to vitamin deficiencies than populations in those studies10,17. Mean vegetable and fruit intake among women with PTB was similar to or slightly higher than that among women with full-term births, and multivitamin use was also higher among PTB cases (Table 1). Previous studies have suggested that excessive intake of certain vitamins, such as vitamin B6 and calcium, may be associated with increased PTB risk44. Taken together, these findings suggest that excessive vitamin intake may be a potential concern in this population. However, given potential biases and limitations of the present study, no definitive conclusions can be drawn, and further research is needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms driving these observed associations.

Far as we know, our study is the largest prospective cohort nested case–control study to evaluate the relationship of maternal multivitamin intake and PTB in China10,11,17,37,41. We collected comprehensive information on maternal exposures during the preconceptional and prenatal periods, enabling more important confounders to be adjusted. Additionally, our dataset had no missing data for the variables included in this study; therefore, all models were fitted with the same sample size, and differences in effect estimates reflect covariate adjustment rather than differences in sample size.

Despite these strengths, our study has several limitations. First, multivitamin intake and several covariates were collected retrospectively through postpartum questionnaires, which may introduce recall bias and potential misclassification of exposure and covariates. Importantly, this misclassification may be differential between preterm birth cases and controls, as women experiencing adverse pregnancy outcomes may recall or report supplement use differently compared with those with term deliveries. This could bias the observed associations in either direction and should be considered when interpreting the findings. The response rate was 63.8%, which may introduce selection bias. As this is a secondary data analysis, we were unable to assess differences between responders and non-responders, and the direction of any resulting bias is unknown. In addition, the study results may not be generalizable to populations with different nutritional profiles or healthcare systems. Secondly, we lacked information on the exact timing of multivitamin initiation. Previous studies have reported that multivitamin use in the third trimester is associated with an increased risk of PTB27,29–31,42. Some studies have also assessed the dose and frequency of individual vitamin intake11,27,37,44; however, such detailed information, including exact days of use, was not available in our study, which may have led to exposure misclassification. In addition, we did not measure serum vitamin concentrations and therefore could not assess the absorbed amounts of individual vitamins. Differences in vitamin absorption among pregnant women, as well as variations in the composition and bioavailability of multivitamin products may have led to variability in actual exposure33,45–49. Also, given the lack of strict regulatory oversight for multivitamin supplements, variability in product quality may exist50. Lack of such detailed exposure information limits the interpretability and clinical relevance of the findings.

Our study findings should be interpreted in the context of existing evidence. Multivitamins are important for pregnant women, particularly those who are underweight, obese, or malnourished17,37,44. In settings with a high prevalence of malnutrition, multivitamin intake may play a role in preventing PTB10,17,37,51,52. However, multivitamin supplementation should not be considered a substitute for a healthy diet50. Individual differences in nutrient absorption, as well as variations in product bioavailability, should also be taken into account by healthcare providers33,45–49. For example, a randomized controlled trial in Burkina Faso found no improvement in iron status among pregnant women receiving iron supplements45. If necessary, assessment of serum vitamin levels may help guide clinical decision-making. These findings warrant further investigation using more detailed exposure assessments, including information on dosage, frequency, timing of initiation and cessation, and supplement formulation or brand. In addition, residual confounding, particularly confounding by indication, should be carefully considered. Future studies incorporating more detailed baseline maternal health assessments, stratified analyses, and advanced causal inference approaches such as propensity score methods53 may help better address residual confounding and clarify the nature of this association.

Conclusions

We observed an association between maternal multivitamin intake during both preconceptional and prenatal periods and higher odds of medically-indicated PTB; however, causality cannot be inferred. This finding highlights the need for further investigations to clarify this association and its underlying mechanisms.
