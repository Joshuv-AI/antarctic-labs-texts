Perception of Rubella and readiness to accept the vaccine among medical students in Enugu and Ebonyi States, Nigeria
Authors: Awoere T. Chinawa, Ikenna C. Nwokoye, Francis N. Ogbuka, Jude T. Onyia, Chidimma S. Nwike, Josephat M. Chinawa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRubella and Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS) affect children with several complications and high mortality.ObjectiveThis sis to document the knowledge of rubella disease and readiness to receive Rubella vaccine among clinical medical students.MethodsThis study was carried out in three medical schools in Enugu and Abakaliki metropolis from May 2025 to June 2025. Two hundred and forty-one clinical medical students from three medical schools in Southeast Nigeria were enlisted in the study using a two-stage random sampling. The study was done with the use of interviewer-administered questionnaire.ResultsA minority of the respondents 16 (6.5%) have heard of rubella disease while a small proportion of respondents 32 (13%) knew of Rubella disease. Only 34 (13.8%) of respondents are ready to receive rubella vaccination. The proportion of respondents with good knowledge was highest among those aged 19–29 years 32(13.4%). Seventy-one (42.8%) of males indicated readiness to be vaccinated, a figure slightly higher than 32 (40.0%) of females that also showed readiness to be vaccinated. The association was not statistically significant (χ² = 0.17, p = 0.680), and the odds ratio of 1.1 (95% CI: 0.6–1.9) was noted.ConclusionA good number of clinical medical students had poor knowledge of Rubella disease. A small number of them were ready to receive the vaccine especially the female folk. This study will inform the urgent need for curriculum changes and the WHO-recommended RCV introduction in Nigeria. A multi-faceted and well organized policy for rubella vaccination especially among the female folk is prime. This will include the involvement of global health initiatives, collaboration between the state and federal government aimed to eliminate the disease through global immunization.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12302-6.

Introduction

Rubella is a viral infection which is usually acquired during pregnancy [1, 2]. Rubella may affect newborn and may occur as Congenital rubella syndrome (CRS). The symptoms of CRS include microcephaly, sensorineural deafness, pulmonary artery branch stenosis and patent ductus arteriosus. Other features of CRS are intellectual impairment, jaundice, and low birth weight [3, 4].

Fetal infection occurs trans-placentally during the mothers viremic phase in congenital rubella syndrome (CRS) [4–6].

Over 3 decades, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended all countries on routine immunization against rubella especially among children and women of childbearing age [7]. in the early 60s, it was documented that 78 out of 214 countries had a national rubella vaccination program but this has increased to 105 in 1999 [8–10].

Rubella is a viral illness which presents with a febrile illness, presence of significant lymph nodes with generalized erythematous, maculopapular rash [1]. The transmission of this virus occurs as an airborne droplets. Rubella may also go unrecognized with features of subclinical illness [11–13]. Its endemicity remains in many parts of the world especially in sub-Saharan Africa [11]. In the pre-vaccine era, the peak incidence occurs in children aged 5 to 9 years, with sporadic cases occurring year-round in temperate region [4]. Rubella affects both gender with a female preponderance in the adolescent age [13].

Rubella is contagious infectious disease with occasional sub-clinical infection.

The incubation period for rubella ranges between 14 and 21 days. Childhood infection could be mild and resolves without complication. Before the introduction of vaccination, out-breaks of rubella occur every 5 to 6 years [13].

Some studies have documented various findings on the knowledge of Rubella disease. For instance, Aldali et al. in a cross-sectional study involving 448 female medical students in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. They noted that 59.8% of the participants were unaware of complications of Rubella disease [14]. They also noted that 36.2%if the medical students are unaware of the availability of Rubella vaccine [14].

Furthermore, Xia et al. [15] in China, among 1286 young females, noted that though about 76% were willing to receive rubella vaccine, however their level of awareness of the rubella was very low. Similarly, Gupta et al. [16] in their study among 202 women health professionals from a tertiary institutions and teaching hospital in India, noted that despite a high level of awareness (94.1%) about rubella disease, the vaccine coverage was as low as 42.1%. they noted a significant gap in the knowledge of rubella and readiness to vaccinate against Rubella disease in health professionals. It is important to highlight that awareness alone cannot predict the attitude of the people towards vaccination.

The implementation of the Rubella-containing Vaccine (RCV) strategies with routine childhood immunization and vaccination of rubella-susceptible women and adolescents of reproductive age may confer life-long protection. Commencement of RCV for adolescents 11–14 years of age [17] and adoption of three-dose RCV vaccination strategy have all been implemented to curb rubella infection in Italy, USA and China respectively [18–21]. China has also introduced additional RCV dose for 6-year-old children in 2006, with a significant increase in rubella IgG antibody positivity [18–20].

The remedy for the reduction of the menace of rubella and CRS is always a welcome development.

It is important to note that vaccination is the best method that can be used to reduce the trend of Rubella disease. Poor knowledge and lack of awareness of the etiology, pattern of presentation of Rubella among clinical medical students could worsen the spread of the disease especially among the female folk. This study is unique in that it focused on the perception of Rubella disease and its vaccine among clinical students in Nigeria. Besides, this the first study on rubella vaccine perception among Nigerian medical students in this locale. An in-depth analysis of the level of Knowledge of Rubella among clinical students is important in reducing the menace caused by this disease. This article was aimed to highlight the perception of the clinical students on Rubella and to document associated factors. Furthermore, the study investigated medical students’ knowledge and perspectives on readiness to accept the Rubella vaccine and factors associated with this.

Materials & methods

Design and setting(s)

This was a cross-sectional study conducted in three medical schools in Southeast Nigeria. The study was done between 21st May 2025 and Jun 2025. The study involved clinical medical students in their fourth to sixth year of study drawn from three South-eastern Nigerian medical schools: University of Nigeria Enugu campus, Enugu State University of Science and Technology, Enugu and Ebonyi State University, Abakaliki.

Participants and sampling

Two hundred and forty-six (246) clinical medical students from three medical schools in Southeast Nigeria were recruited in the study using a two-stage random sampling. The medical students in clinical class who gave a written informed consent were enrolled in the study while the pre-clinical class medical students and cases without consent were excluded from the study.

The clinical medical students for this study in the six accredited medical schools were selected by a two-stage multi sampling method. A simple random sampling technique of balloting was used to select three medical students from the six accredited medical schools.

20% attrition rate was considered, this brought the final value to 243 but this was rounded off to 246.

Tools/Instruments

A modified and validated interviewer-administered questionnaire was used for the study [15–17, 23–25]. The questionnaire is made of three models which include the health belief model: this model assesses domains, such as perceived susceptibility, perceived benefits, and perceived barriers while the domain on behaviour assessed knowledge, attitude and practice of clinical medical students to Rubella infection and readiness to accept vaccine.

The socio-demographic status of the medical students was ascertained with a model which included the gender, age, marital and employment status and parents’ occupation and level of education.

The contents and purpose of the study was described with the medical students in detail. Cronbach’s α was used to evaluate the internal validity of the questionnaires by Ray et al. [17].

Using the formula: α = N *C/v+(N-1) * C. A score of 0.8 was obtained which shows a high level of validity.

Scores for assessment of knowledge of rubella

Ten variables were used to assess knowledge of rubella. Symptoms of Rubella disease were used to assess five of the ten variables. A score of 1 was awarded to a correct answer and zero for an incorrect answer I for each of those five variables. The prevalence of the disease, the age group commonly affected by the disease, the impact of the disease, the treatment techniques and the prevention of the disease made up the other six variables. A score of four points was awarded to any of the correct answer by any of the reach of those six variable while a score of zero was awarded to an incorrect answer. A score of 29 was awarded as a maximum score for each respondent. Eight variables were used to assess the Knowledge of rubella vaccination. A score of one was awarded to any correct answer to the respondents and a score of zero for an incorrect response. A score of 8 was awarded as the maximum score each respondent could obtain.

Data collection methods

The questionnaires were distributed to the medical students in their normal class session.

Data analysis

Data analysis was done with the SPSS v25 Software (IBM, USA). Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize the demographic characteristics, sources of information, awareness, perceptions, and readiness to receive the rubella vaccine among the study population. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe categorical variables such as age groups, gender, educational level, and responses to knowledge and perception items. Measures of central tendency and dispersion, including mean and standard deviation, were employed where appropriate, such as for age distribution. To assess associations between demographic variables and knowledge or readiness to receive the rubella vaccine, Chi-square tests of independence were conducted. In cases where the expected frequencies in any cell of a contingency table were less than five, Fisher’s Exact Test was applied to ensure statistical validity. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05. Furthermore, to evaluate the strength and direction of association between independent variables (such as age, gender, and knowledge level) and the outcome variable (readiness to receive the rubella vaccine), odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated.

When assessing knowledge level, only 37.5% of those with good knowledge of rubella were ready to receive the vaccine, compared to 42.5% of those with poor knowledge. This counterintuitive finding was not statistically significant (χ² = 0.2, p = 0.591), with an odds ratio of 0.8 (95% CI: 0.3–1.7), indicating that better knowledge did not significantly predict a greater likelihood of vaccination readiness in this sample.

Discussion

The study documented the awareness of Rubella and readiness to accept the vaccine among clinical medical students. The study showed a low level of knowledge of Rubella disease among medical students (13%).This may be attributed to the fact that most information on Rubella was not taught to the medical students. The level of awareness of Rubella among medical students was lower than that obtained by Poornima et al. [26] where all the students knew about the existence of Rubella disease. In addition, Prasad et al. [12] in India noted that in a total of 200 subjects, 87.2% of medical and nursing students were aware of Rubella. The reason for the variation in awareness of Rubella disease could be due to the differences in medical curriculum by the various medical schools of study, the level of students in the medical school and differences in methodology.

The study also showed that a low number of medical students know that they could be infected with Rubella disease and a much lower number were aware they are in the priority group for Rubella Vaccination. This differs to the study of Prasad et al. who noted that 37% of their subjects were aware of the age of vaccination involving 1 to 15 years old children. Similarly, Poornima et al. [26] also documented low values of students who showed low knowledge on age of infection and vaccination at 1–15 years of the age group.

The study noted no association with gender in the perception of rubella disease. Rubella and CRS had more deleterious effects on females than males especially during the early first semester. There is therefore an urgent need to intensify the spread of information on Rubella disease and CRS among the female folk.

The study noted that fewer medical students knew the symptoms of Rubella and CRS. Anna et al. [27] in a comparative analysis of Knowledge of Rubella between medical and non-medical students noted that 100% of medical students knew the symptoms of Rubella disease while 21% of non-medical students were aware of the disease. These poor awareness of symptoms of CRS and Rubella disease is a clarion call for an urgent medical curriculum review. This shows a serious and urgent need to intensify the dissemination of information on the spread of the diseases especially among clinical medical students. This message should also pass down to medical students especially their female counterpart so as to reduce the impact of this menace.

The current study showed no link between the class of study and knowledge of Rubella disease. Introduction of lecture and reviewing curriculum is key to dissemination information on this disease. It is necessary and crucial to introduce lecture on the awareness and prevention of Rubella and CRS in the first year of study.

The clinical medical students at younger age (19–29 years) had more perception and knowledge of Rubella and CRS than medical students who were older. Prasad et al. [12] also noted that younger medical students have about 9 times knowledge more than the older once on Rubella and CRS. They also noted that among the 138 subjects, participants from the upper and middle class were about 2 times more aware than those from the lower class. This was also in keeping with the current study. The study showed that a minor proportion of clinical medical students had good knowledge of the rising prevalence of Rubella disease and a minor population believe that Rubella can cause death. A rising prevalence of Rubella has been documented in some studies [28–31]. In Hossawa, Southern Ethiopia, the trend has increased to 2.1% and 86.3% for IgM and IgG respectively [28, 29]. In addition. Algeria, had documented an increase in sero-prevalence of rubella IgG antibodies as 68.6%. Rubella vaccine uptake is a crucial issue especially for women of child bearing age. The study in focus showed that very few number of medical students were aware of Rubella vaccine and 13.8% believe that there has been no vaccine developed for the prevention of rubella. This poor knowledge may arise as a result of the few number of the medical students who accepted to be included for vaccination. More so, a large number of them also believed that vaccination against Rubella is unsafe. The low prevalence obtained from this study may also be explained by the poor knowledge of Rubella disease among the medical students. The poor vaccination uptake or readiness to accept vaccine was similar to the work of Prasad et al. [12]. who noted vaccine uptake against rubella disease as 54% among medical students. The small sample size and study location may have also affected this finding.

This study showed that a low number of medical students were ready to accept the Rubella vaccine. The low values obtained in this study may be attributed to the low percentage of the medical students who noted the possibility of adverse reaction from Rubella vaccination, more so a high number of them also noted that Rubella vaccination is not of any value with most of them noting that majority of the adverse reaction are severe. Although Rubella vaccine is proven to be safe, there is a possible risk of an adverse reaction. The Rubella vaccine may cause redness and mild pain at site of injection coupled with rash, muscle aches, and low grade fever, which is self-limiting. Rarely severe adverse effects have been reported [32, 33].

There are no significant odds between clinical medical students who had good knowledge of Rubella vaccination and likelihood to receive the vaccine compared to those with poor knowledge. Good knowledge of Rubella vaccination alone does not predict readiness to vaccinate against rubella. There are several confounding variables that may affect the decision-making process. This includes confidence, convenience, complacency, context and communication.

The current study showed that medical students with younger age were more ready to receive Rubella vaccine than those who were older. The vaccine hesitancy among older clinical students is corroborated in the work of Ishimaru et al. [34] who noted that the older the respondents, the higher the odds of rejection vaccination. Awareness of Rubella vaccine uptake and the benefits thereof should be addressed in all cadre of medical schools with more emphasis to those in final year class where older students abound. Male medical students are slightly more ready to receive Rubella vaccine. An intensive information dissemination and education is very crucial among the females on the menace of this disease especially in child bearing age. This finding is not in tandem with the aphorism of Ishimaru et al. who opined that females who tend to be more proactive in preventive behaviors, are less ready to accept vaccination than the males [35]. Other studies have confirmed readiness to receive vaccine among females than males [36–39], although Lazarus et al. [38] were not in the affirmative. Sociocultural differences and the proportion of sample size used could explain the varying findings.

What the study adds

This study has demonstrated the importance structured curriculum of the rubella disease especially at the earlier years of medical school. Improving public awareness of rubella disease and vigorous information dissemination on its severe consequences could be strong remedies to vaccine hesitancy among medical students. It is crucial to balance the effects of knowledge of rubella disease and vaccine acceptance by using knowledge-based interventions as well as applying mechanism like the WHO’s BeSD tools, and strengthening health education for women of reproductive age, integrating rubella vaccination information into preconception care programs.

Limitation

This study is limited by self –report and recall bias, the use of only 3 schools sampled, and the exclusion of pre-clinical students.

Conclusion

A good number of clinical medical students had poor knowledge of Rubella disease. A small number of them were ready to receive the vaccine especially the female folk. This study will inform the urgent need for curriculum changes and the WHO-recommended RCV introduction in Nigeria. A multi-faceted and well organized policy for rubella vaccination especially among the female folk is prime. This will include the involvement of global health initiatives, collaboration between the state and federal government aimed to eliminate the disease through global immunization.
