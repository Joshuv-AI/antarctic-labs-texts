Trends of congenital rubella syndrome among infants (0–11 months old) in India: a multicentre hospital-based sentinel surveillance (2016–2025)
Authors: Devika Shanmugasundaram, Sanjay Verma, Shalini Tripathi, Bhagirathi Dwibedi, Kuldeep Singh, Sangappa Mahantesh, Shikha Malik, Himabindu Singh, Sridhar Santhanam, Nivedita Mondal, Malay Kumar Sinha, Manish Jain, Bhabesh Kant Chowdhary, Rajlakshmi Vishwanathan, Shailesh D Pawar, Gajanan Sapkal, Tapas kumar Som, Amber Kumar, Sananda Pati, Supratim Datta, Sabarinathan Ramasamy, Padma Priya Rajendran, Mini P Singh, Radha Kanta Ratho, Suruchi Shukla, Baijayantimala Mishra, Ravishekhar Gadepalli, Vijay Lakshmi Nag, Ashok Munivenkatappa, Debasis Biswas, Sudha Madhuri Devara, Sudha Rani Vannavada, Asha Mary Abraham, Rahul Dhodapkar, Raja Ray, Binod Kumar Pati, Priya Sreenivasan, Saradakutty Geetha, Ashish Bavdekar, Jayant Prakash

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundIndia aims to eliminate measles and rubella by 2026. In this study we analysed surveillance data from 2016 to 2025 from a facility-based surveillance of 16 sentinel sites across India to understand the burden and trend of congenital rubella syndrome (CRS).MethodsCRS surveillance included eligible infants between 0 and 11 months of age with suspected CRS, defined as the presence of congenital heart defects, hearing impairment, ocular abnormalities or maternal history of rash during pregnancy. All enrolled infants underwent comprehensive clinical evaluation, including cardiac, ophthalmologic, and hearing assessments. Serum samples were tested for rubella-specific IgM and IgG antibodies. The final analysis summarised clinical characteristics and trend of laboratory-confirmed CRS infants.FindingsBetween November 2016 and May 2025, 8953 infants with suspected CRS were enrolled across 16 sentinel sites, among them 583 (6.5%) patients were classified as having laboratory-confirmed CRS. The proportion of laboratory-confirmed patients declined from 24.9% in 2017 to 2.5% in 2025. Among laboratory-confirmed CRS patients, 81.3% had structural heart defects, 26.8% had hearing impairment, and 24.2% had any of the group A ocular sign (cataract, glaucoma or pigmentary retinopathy). Of the 583 infants with laboratory-confirmed CRS, 541 were tested for rubella virus by RT-PCR, of whom 56 (10.4%) were positive, with no positive detections after 2021. Follow-up information was available for 452 laboratory-confirmed CRS patients, of whom 126 died, with a cumulative probability of death by one year of 32.2% (95% CI 26.7–38.4).InterpretationThe observed decline in the proportion of laboratory-confirmed CRS among suspected CRS infants in this facility-based surveillance is consistent with declining rubella virus transmission in India, although changes in surveillance sensitivity over time could not be excluded. Sustained high-quality surveillance is essential to monitor progress towards CRS elimination in the country.FundingCRS surveillance was funded by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Govt of India, through Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance (Grant number: 00101970).Keywords: Rubella, Congenital rubella syndrome, Sentinel surveillance, India

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed for estimates of laboratory-confirmed congenital rubella syndrome (CRS) patients in India on April 2, 2026, using the terms (“Congenital Rubella Syndrome” [MeSH] OR “congenital rubella syndrome” [tiab] OR (CRS [tiab] AND rubella [tiab])) AND (India [tiab] OR “India” [MeSH]). We identified 54 publications, of which 40 were not directly relevant to estimating CRS burden. Among the remaining 14 studies, two indirectly estimated CRS burden using serosurveys among pregnant women with mathematical modelling approaches. Seven publications originated from the same CRS sentinel surveillance network, reporting data up to 2021, with the proportion of laboratory-confirmed CRS patients ranging from 26% in 2017 to 8.7% in 2021. Two studies assessed in-utero rubella infection and CRS, while two others described the prevalence of congenital heart defects and ocular manifestations among CRS patients. Additionally, a hospital-based study from south India (2008) reported 20% laboratory confirmation among clinically suspected postnatal rubella patients. Overall, there is limited evidence on recent CRS burden in India, particularly in the context of high measles–rubella vaccination coverage (>90% since 2019).

Added value of this study

Our study findings provide updated evidence on recent trend of CRS in India, leveraging a multi-site sentinel surveillance. Among infants with suspected CRS, 6.5% were laboratory confirmed, with the proportion declining from 24.9% in 2017 to 2.5% in 2025. Majority of laboratory-confirmed CRS patients had structural heart defects (81.3%) followed by hearing impairment (26.8%) and ocular manifestations (24.2%). The RT-PCR positivity among laboratory confirmed CRS was 10.4% and there were no positive detections since 2021. The cumulative probability of death was high, reaching 32.2% at 1 year and 57.3% at 2 years after diagnosis.

Implications of all the available evidence

The decline in rubella seropositivity among suspected CRS patients suggests declining trend in rubella transmission in India. Early detection of infants with CRS remains critical to enable timely management of various birth defects thereby reducing morbidity and mortality. Maintaining high coverage of rubella-containing vaccination is essential to further interrupt transmission and sustain progress towards CRS elimination.

Introduction

Rubella is a contagious viral illness typically presenting with maculopapular rash and post-auricular or sub-occipital lymphadenopathy. Among children, rubella infection usually presents as a mild, self-limiting illness and is rarely associated with complications. However, one of its most serious complications is the teratogenic effect of infection during pregnancy.1 Acquiring maternal rubella infection shortly before conception or during the first trimester can result in spontaneous abortion, intrauterine foetal death, stillbirth or birth of an infant with multiple congenital anomalies collectively known as Congenital Rubella Syndrome (CRS).2,3

CRS is characterised by multiple congenital defects, including ophthalmic manifestations (cataracts, congenital glaucoma, pigmentary retinopathy, microphthalmia and chorioretinitis), auditory (sensorineural hearing loss) and cardiac defects (peripheral pulmonary artery stenosis, patent ductus arteriosus and ventricular septal defects). Infants affected with CRS may shed rubella virus for prolonged periods, posing a potential risk of infection to nonimmune pregnant women.4,5 CRS is an important cause of birth defects in rubella endemic countries. Between 1996 and 2010, an estimated 105,000 CRS-affected infants were born annually worldwide, with India accounting for 38% of these births.6

Rubella vaccines are safe and highly immunogenic, with an efficacy exceeding 90% when administered to children older than 12 months.7 All member states of the WHO South-East Asia Region are committed to elimination of measles and rubella by 2026.8 India is implementing the National Strategic Plan for Measles and Rubella elimination to achieve this goal.9,10 The plan included a countrywide supplementary immunisation activity (SIA) with measles-rubella containing vaccine (MRCV) for children between 9 months and 14 years of age and introduction of MRCV in the routine childhood immunisation program. The Indian Council of Medical Research (ICMR), with the support from Ministry of Health and Family welfare, Government of India initiated a case-based sentinel surveillance in 2016 to generate estimates of disease burden and to monitor progress made towards CRS elimination. In this study we analysed surveillance data from 2016 to 2025 from a facility-based surveillance of 16 sentinel sites across India to understand the burden, clinical characteristics and trend of CRS.

Methods

The CRS surveillance was implemented in two phases, with phase I launched in November 2016 in five sentinel sites. The surveillance was expanded in 2019 (Phase II) with the addition of 11 sites, all of which were tertiary care hospitals (Appendix p.2 Supplementary Fig. S1). These sentinel sites serve a wider catchment population across the city, nearby districts and neighbouring states. We adapted WHO recommended case definitions for CRS surveillance.11,12 Surveillance targeted infants (0–11 months) who were suspected of having CRS, defined as having any of the following: (a) structural heart defect confirmed by echocardiography, (b) hearing impairment confirmed by audiometry, (c) ocular abnormalities (any of the eye signs: congenital cataract, glaucoma, pigmentary retinopathy, microphthalmos or microcornea), (d) maternal history of rash during pregnancy, or (e) strong clinical suspicion. Suspected infants were enrolled either during routine examination of newborns delivered at the sentinel hospitals or during outpatient consultation in the departments of Paediatrics, Cardiology, ENT or Ophthalmology. The identified infants were referred to the surveillance coordinator (paediatrician) for a complete clinical assessment. In addition to the paediatrician, the surveillance team included cardiologist, ophthalmologist, ENT surgeon, obstetrician, and microbiologist.

The demographic, epidemiological and clinical characteristics of suspected CRS infants were recorded on the standard case report form. All suspected CRS infants underwent complete clinical evaluation, including cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, neurological, ophthalmological and ENT examination. Cardiac assessment was performed using 2D echocardiography and the cardiac defects were grouped into “simple” and “complex” categories based on the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute (NHLBI) classification system.13 Confirmation of hearing impairment was based on brainstem evoked response audiometry (BERA) or auditory steady-state response (ASSR) or automated auditory brainstem response (aABR) screening. At a few sentinel sites where these confirmatory tests were unavailable, two consecutive ‘refer’ results on otoacoustic emission (OAE) screening were used as the operational criterion for hearing impairment.

Two millilitres of blood was collected in a plain blood collection tube from the suspected CRS infants for serological investigations. An additional blood specimen was collected four weeks following the collection of the initial sample from (i) infants aged less than 1 month at the time of first sample collection whose initial serum sample tested negative for rubella-specific IgM antibodies, and (ii) infants aged 6–11 months whose initial serum sample was positive for rubella-specific IgG antibodies. Serum sample from suspected infants younger than 6 months were tested for presence of IgM antibody against rubella, whereas samples from children older than or equal to 6 months were tested for both IgM and IgG antibodies using a commercially available Anti-Rubella Virus Glycoprotein IgM and IgG Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (Euroimmun, Germany) at the sentinel sites.

Suspected CRS infants were classified using a combination of clinical signs and rubella serology. The group A (major) signs included cataract, congenital glaucoma, pigmentary retinopathy, congenital heart defect, or hearing impairment. The signs in group B (minor) included microcephaly, developmental delay, meningoencephalitis, splenomegaly, purpura, radiolucent bone disease, or jaundice with onset within 24 h after birth (Appendix p.3 Supplementary Box 1). Serological evidence of rubella infection was defined as either the presence of rubella-specific IgM antibodies among infants younger than 6 months, or sustained level of rubella-specific IgG antibodies on at least two occasions among infants aged 6–11 months, without history of recent rubella vaccination. Infants with serological evidence of rubella infection and at least one Group A sign were classified as laboratory-confirmed CRS. Those with serological evidence of rubella infection but without any Group A sign were classified as congenital rubella infection (CRI).

Clinically compatible CRS was defined as infants presenting with two major (Group A) or one major (Group A) and one minor (Group B) signs, in whom blood samples were unavailable for laboratory testing. Infants meeting criteria for laboratory-confirmed CRS or CRI who had previously received MRCV were classified as vaccine-associated rubella seropositive infants. Suspected infants with negative serological findings, irrespective of clinical signs, were classified as discarded cases (Appendix p.3 Supplementary Box 2).

Oropharyngeal swabs were collected from all suspected CRS infants in a viral transport medium. Swabs were transported to ICMR National Institute of Virology, Mumbai Unit, Mumbai. Infants with serological evidence of rubella underwent rubella virus RNA detection using reverse-transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). Representative samples positive by RT-PCR across all regions were genotyped as per WHO guidelines.14

As per the surveillance protocol, laboratory-confirmed CRS patients were followed up periodically to assess viral excretion and clinical outcomes (alive/deceased). Laboratory-confirmed patients identified at each sentinel site were contacted and requested to visit the sentinel hospital for clinical examination and collection of swabs. If parents or guardians were unwilling to visit the hospital, information about child's status was collected telephonically. For RT-PCR positive children, follow-up swabs were collected every three months and continued for up to two years or until the patient became RT-PCR negative. An additional oropharyngeal swab was collected from RT-PCR negative children after a minimum interval of one month to confirm viral clearance.

As part of quality control, serum samples from all laboratory-confirmed patients and a random 10% of negative patients were re-tested for rubella-specific IgM and IgG antibodies at ICMR National Institute of Virology (NIV), Pune. In addition, quality assurance was maintained across participating laboratories by distributing panels containing confirmed positive and negative samples and comparing reported results with expected outcomes to assess testing accuracy.

WHO-recommended indicators were used to assess the quality of CRS surveillance. These indicators included adequacy of case investigation, sample collection and laboratory testing, sample collection for viral detection, specimen collection for monitoring viral excretion, timeliness of case detection, sample transport to the laboratory, and reporting laboratory results.15,16

Statistical analysis

The distribution of laboratory-confirmed CRS patients was described by place (district of residence), person (age, sex, and clinical features), and time (rubella positivity by year). Analyses were conducted using available data for each variable, and denominators are reported separately where data were missing. Crude facility-based reporting rate of suspected and laboratory-confirmed CRS was estimated by using the number of suspected and laboratory-confirmed CRS patients delivered at the sentinel hospitals as the numerator and the total number of deliveries at these sites as the denominator over an 8-year period. Cumulative probability of death after diagnosis was estimated using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis. Infants alive at last contact were censored on the date of last follow-up. All data were entered in the web-based data entry portal. We used Stata (StatCorp LLC, TX 77845, version 17) for data analysis.

Ethics statement

Ethics approval was obtained from ICMR National Institute of Epidemiology, Chennai (NIE/IHEC/201604-01) and from the ethics committees of all sentinel sites. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians prior to enrolling infants into the surveillance.

Role of the funding source

The funders had no role in the study design, data collection and analysis, decision to publish, or preparation of the manuscript.

Results

Between November 2016 and May 2025, a total of 8953 infants with suspected CRS were enrolled across 16 sentinel sites (Appendix p.4 Supplementary Table S1). About one-fifth (n = 1683, 18.8%) were enrolled through routine newborn examination, while the remaining were detected from among patients attending various outpatient departments. Overall, 6518 (72.8%) suspected CRS infants were younger than or equal to 5 months, with a median age of 2 months (IQR: 1, 6) at the time of diagnosis (Table 1). Among the suspected CRS infants, 72.4% (n = 6481) were enrolled based on the presence of structural heart defect. Ocular signs and hearing impairment were present in 11.2% each, while 7.6% had a maternal history of febrile illness with rash during pregnancy. Of the 8953 suspected CRS infants, 583 (6.5%) were classified as laboratory-confirmed CRS, 1539 (17.2%) as clinically compatible cases and 76 (0.8%) as CRI. An additional 310 (3.5%) infants met serological criteria for rubella positivity (249 with CRS compatible clinical manifestations and 61 with CRI) but had a recent history of MRCV vaccination. Their seropositivity was considered attributable to recent vaccination. The remaining 6445 (72.0%) were classified as discarded cases.

The mean maternal age of suspected CRS infants was 26.2 (SD: 4.4) years. A total of 1980 (22.1%) suspected CRS infants were delivered in the sentinel hospitals including 99 laboratory-confirmed CRS infants. Facility-based reporting rates were estimated for 13 sentinel hospitals with available delivery data. Among the 583 laboratory-confirmed CRS patients, 474 (81.3%) had structural heart defects, 126 (26.8%) of the 470 had hearing impairment and 141 (24.2%) had group A ocular signs. The group A ocular signs included congenital cataract (n = 114, 19.6%), glaucoma (n = 11, 1.9%) and pigmentary retinopathy (n = 26, 4.5%). Ten patients had more than one ocular sign. Among the 474 laboratory-confirmed CRS patients with structural heart defects, details of the type of defect were available for 467 of the patients, of whom 305 (65.3%) had simple and 162 (34.7%) had complex defects. The most common simple heart defects were ventricular septal defect (n = 138, 45.2%), atrial septal defect (n = 81, 26.6%), and patent ductus arteriosus (n = 74, 24.3%) (Appendix p.8 Supplementary Table S3). Among the 583 laboratory-confirmed CRS infants, oropharyngeal swabs were collected from 563 (96.6%). Of the 541 samples tested, 56 (10.4%) were positive for rubella virus RNA by RT-PCR. Follow-up oropharyngeal swabs were collected and tested from 17 (30.4%) of the 56 baseline RT-PCR-positive participants, with a median follow-up duration of 15.3 months (IQR: 13.4, 18.6). Among these, 5 remained RT-PCR positive at follow-up. Among participants who were RT-PCR negative at baseline, follow-up samples were collected and tested from 101 (20.8%) participants at a median follow-up duration of 9.7 months (IQR: 5.4, 15.7). The 20 representative samples from different geographic regions selected for genotyping belonged to genotype 2B. No RT-PCR positive samples were detected after 2021.

Follow-up information was available for 452 (77.5%) of the 583 laboratory-confirmed CRS patients and 126 died during the follow-up period. The cumulative probability of death was 32.2% (95% CI: 26.7, 38.4) by one year and 57.3% (95% CI: 48.2, 66.6) by two years after diagnosis (Appendix p.9 Supplementary Fig. S3).

Overall, 8865 (99.0%) suspected CRS infants underwent adequate clinical investigation, and laboratory samples were collected and tested for 7129 (79.6%) infants. As the laboratories were located within the hospital premises, samples were transported immediately after collection. About 41% (n = 2930) samples were tested within 4 days of collection, and 60.5% (n = 4315) were tested within 7 days. Baseline oropharyngeal swabs were collected and tested from 541 (92.8%) laboratory-confirmed patients for viral detection. However, for monitoring viral excretion, follow-up oropharyngeal swabs were collected from 204 (46.6%) of the 438 laboratory-confirmed infants who were alive. 120 (58.8%) of the follow-up oropharyngeal swabs were tested for RT-PCR. Overall, 256 (43.9%) laboratory-confirmed infants were detected within 3 months of birth (Table 3).

Discussion

Over the study period 8953 suspected CRS infants were enrolled across 16 sentinel sites, of whom 6.5% were classified as laboratory-confirmed CRS. The proportion of laboratory-confirmed CRS patients among suspected CRS patients declined from 24.9% in 2017 to 2.5% in 2025. Similarly, we observed a decline in crude facility-based reporting rate of laboratory-confirmed CRS from 5.2 per 10,000 live births in 2017 to 0.78 per 10,000 live births in 2025. This decline coincided with rapid scale-up of the measles–rubella (MR) immunisation program in India. Between 2017 and 2021, coverage with the first dose of MRCV increased from approximately 6% in 2017 to 89% in 2021, following the nationwide MR catch-up campaigns and expansion of routine immunisation. Coverage peaked at 95% in 2019, declined modestly to 89% during 2020–2021 due to pandemic-related disruptions, and subsequently stabilised at around 92–93%.17 The observed decline in the proportion of laboratory-confirmed CRS patients is consistent with reduced rubella virus transmission in the country. The data from measles-rubella surveillance in India showed a 48% decline in reported rubella incidence during 2017–21, from 2.3 to 1.2 per million population.17

The CRS surveillance achieved high levels of clinical investigation and sample collection for viral detection among laboratory-confirmed patients, but timeliness of detection, laboratory reporting, and monitoring of viral excretion remained suboptimal. In the present CRS surveillance, only 44% of the patients were detected within first 3 months of life. This was likely due to the predominance of outpatient–based enrolment, while only about one-fifth of the suspected CRS infants were identified through newborn screening activities. The gap in monitoring virus excretion was primarily due to the challenges in follow-up of laboratory-confirmed CRS patients. Despite repeated telephonic reminders, many families did not return to the sentinel site for scheduled follow-up visits and specimen collection, resulting in incomplete follow-up and limited monitoring for viral excretion. The delays in laboratory reporting were mainly due to the surveillance protocol for sentinel sites to perform testing once a week. In addition, occasional shortages of testing kits affected specimen testing during certain surveillance periods.

About three fourth of suspected CRS patients were identified based on congenital heart defects, while ocular manifestations and hearing impairment each accounted for approximately 11% of suspected CRS patients. Among the laboratory confirmed CRS patients, 81.3% had structural heart defects, 26.8% had confirmed hearing loss, and 19.6% had cataract. Confirmatory hearing assessment was achieved in 80.6% of the laboratory-confirmed CRS patients; the remaining 19.4% were not assessed due to incorrect contact details or unsuccessful follow-up attempts. Infants with structural heart defects are more likely to present with symptoms and therefore seek care earlier, increasing the likelihood of detection. In addition, routine newborn screening for hearing and ophthalmic abnormalities was not universally available across all sentinel sites, which may have contributed to the lower proportion of infants identified with these conditions.

In our study, less than 20% of the patients were identified during newborn examinations, while majority were identified later during routine clinical consultations. The median age of laboratory-confirmed CRS infants was 4 months (IQR: 1–8 months). These findings highlight the need to strengthen newborn screening for congenital anomalies to facilitate early detection of CRS infants. Early diagnosis is critical for timely clinical management of cardiac, ophthalmic, and hearing defects, with the potential to reduce morbidity and mortality as well as transmission in the community. In addition, systematic newborn screening could also lead to more complete estimates of disease burden by detecting CRS patients earlier and reducing missed or delayed diagnoses.

Our study documented a high cumulative probability of death (32.2%) by one year after diagnosis among laboratory-confirmed patients. We also observed a substantially higher one-year mortality among infants with complex cardiac defects compared with those with simple defects (44.8% [95% CI: 33.9–57.4] vs. 23.4% [95% CI: 17.4–31.2]). However, we could not ascertain the specific causes of death, as most deaths occurred at home. High mortality among infants with CRS has been documented in several countries. Sentinel surveillance in South Africa reported case fatality rates ranging from 7% to 15% among laboratory-confirmed CRS infants, mainly among those with severe cardiac defects.18 In Vietnam, following the large rubella epidemic during 2010–2011, surveillance data indicated a mortality rate of 34% among CRS infants.19

An important finding of this study was the absence of RT-PCR positivity among serologically confirmed CRS patients since 2021. During the entire surveillance period, the laboratory protocol for RT-PCR testing remained unchanged. The median age of laboratory-confirmed CRS patients diagnosed before 2021 was 4 (IQR: 1, 7) months and 6 (IQR: 1, 9) months among the infants diagnosed after 2021. One possible explanation for the absence of RT-PCR positivity after 2021 could be delayed identification of laboratory-confirmed infants, as rubella virus is most readily detected by RT-PCR during early infancy, particularly within the first 3 months of life, after which viral shedding declines substantially.4,5 Alternatively, declining rubella virus transmission following the introduction and scale-up of rubella-containing vaccines may also have reduced opportunities for virus detection. Further studies are needed to better understand the factors contributing to absence of RT-PCR positivity in recent years.

Our CRS surveillance had certain limitations. We did not assess the sensitivity of CRS surveillance system. As surveillance relied on sentinel site–based reporting with passive case detection across multiple clinical departments, differences in case ascertainment across sites and the possibility of missed cases over time cannot be excluded. Therefore, the observed decline in CRS positivity should be interpreted with caution, as it assumes that surveillance sensitivity remained reasonably stable throughout the study period. We were unable to estimate population-based incidence of CRS as our surveillance did not capture all births within the catchment areas of sentinel hospitals and was limited to facility-based enrolment. In our surveillance a higher proportion of suspected patients were identified based on cardiac abnormalities compared with ocular or hearing defects, which may reflect differences in access to specialised diagnostic services and could have resulted in under-detection of non-cardiac manifestations.

In conclusion, CRS surveillance conducted in India during 2016–2025 suggest a declining trend in rubella seropositivity among suspected CRS infants and in the crude facility-based reporting rate of laboratory-confirmed CRS. The existing network of sentinel surveillance sites provides a platform for monitoring progress toward rubella elimination and could be further strengthened by incorporating systematic newborn screening for major congenital anomalies. Such measures would facilitate earlier detection of CRS infants and more accurate estimation of CRS incidence.
