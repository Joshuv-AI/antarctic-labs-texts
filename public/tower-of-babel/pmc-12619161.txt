An overview of hepatitis B virus in gastric carcinogenesis: from clinical association to molecular mechanisms
Authors: Yu Jiang, Yan Lu, Zhiyuan Li, Wenchun Jiang, He Han, Xin Fan, Jixiang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have established that Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection contributes to the development of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). In recent years, growing attention has been directed towards the association between HBV and extrahepatic malignant tumors, particularly gastric cancer (GC), with increasing evidence suggesting that HBV infection may elevate the risk of GC. Several meta-analyses have examined the link between HBV and GC. However, the underlying pathogenic mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study conducts a systematic review of existing research to investigate the association between HBV infection and GC risk. Additionally, it offers a comprehensive analysis of the epidemiological data, virological characteristics, and molecular mechanisms. The available evidence suggests that HBV may contribute to GC development and progression through mechanisms such as HBV DNA integration, regulation of HBx protein, chronic inflammation, and immune dysregulation. However, limitations in the current literature—such as reliance on single-source samples, insufficient control of confounding factors, and limited mechanistic studies—should be acknowledged. Large-scale, multicenter, prospective studies with clearly defined mechanisms are needed to further clarify the causal relationship between HBV and GC and to identify potential biomarkers. In conclusion, addressing HBV-related GC is a critical clinical and public health priority.

Introduction

As of 2019, approximately 296 million individuals worldwide were infected with chronic HBV, with the majority residing in Asia and sub-Saharan Africa, according to the World Health Organization (WHO) [1]. Chronic HBV infection is the leading cause of liver decompensation, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [2]. Furthermore, data from GLOBOCAN 2022 indicate that gastric cancer (GC) ranks as the fifth most prevalent cancer globally and is the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths [3]. This database includes 185 countries or regions, with incidence rates estimated based on gender, age, and the most reliable local data sources. The highest incidence rates of GC are predominantly observed in East Asia, a region also characterized by a high prevalence of HBV infection [4]. Individuals who are HBsAg-positive face a significantly increased risk of developing HCC, along with a notable association with several other tumors, including esophageal cancer, GC, intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, extrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma, pancreatic cancer, non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, and leukemia [5]. Additionally, previous studies have confirmed a substantial association between HBV infection and GC [5, 6]. Thus, investigating the potential link between HBV and GC, particularly its pathogenic mechanisms and clinical significance, holds considerable scientific value. This comprehensive approach not only aids in optimizing cancer prevention strategies but also contributes to the development of more effective interventions aimed at reducing GC incidence and mortality, ultimately improving patient prognosis and survival rates. This review systematically synthesizes existing evidence on the relationship between HBV and GC, emphasizing its potential mechanisms and offering insights for future research on HBV-related GC.

Hepatitis B

HBV life cycle: entry, replication, and egress

HBV is an enveloped DNA virus classified as a hepatotropic virus [7]. Its genome consists of relaxed-circular DNA (rcDNA). The viral life cycle involves several stages of hepatocyte entry (Fig. 1). Initially, HBV interacts with the low-affinity receptor heparan sulfate proteoglycan (HSPG) on the hepatocyte surface. This is followed by binding of the Pre-S1 region of HBV to the sodium taurocholate co-transporting polypeptide (NTCP), facilitating viral entry via endocytosis with the help of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), thereby promoting further infection [8]. Inside the nucleus, the HBV nucleocapsid disassembles, and the rcDNA enters the nucleus, where it is converted into covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) with the assistance of host proteins. The cccDNA then integrates host histones to form small chromosomal structures, establishing a stable presence in the cell nucleus [9]. The HBV genome contains four overlapping open reading frames (ORFs): C, P, S, and X, each encoding a distinct viral protein. The C region encodes core proteins, including HBeAg and the 22-kDa precore protein (p22cr), and the Po protein. The S region encodes three surface antigens: large (L-HBs), middle (M-HBs), and small (S-HBs). The X region encodes the HBV X protein (HBx). These proteins play pivotal roles in the viral life cycle. Specifically, cccDNA serves as a template for the transcription of HBV RNA of varying lengths, including 3.5 kb, 2.4 kb, 2.1 kb, and 0.7 kb. These RNA molecules are transcribed from specific promoters within the HBV genome, with each fragment encoding particular proteins. For example, the 3.5-kb RNA encodes C and P proteins, the 2.4-kb RNA encodes L-HBs, the 2.1-kb RNA encodes M-HBs and S-HBs, and the 0.7-kb RNA encodes HBx. HBV utilizes these proteins, in conjunction with host factors, to complete viral replication and assembly, leading to viral proliferation within host cells [10, 11].

Epidemiology

HBV infection represents a significant public health challenge. Global estimates indicate that approximately 2 billion individuals have experienced current or past HBV infection based on serological markers, with 1.5 million new infections reported annually [12]. High prevalence rates are particularly noted in the Asia-Pacific region and sub-Saharan Africa (Fig. 2), with China accounting for one-third of the world’s chronic HBV cases [13, 14]. HBV infection has been linked to a spectrum of hepatic diseases, including acute hepatitis, fulminant hepatic failure, chronic hepatitis, cirrhosis, and HCC [15]. Acute HBV infection is typically characterized by non-specific symptoms and is generally self-limiting; however, 5% of affected individuals may develop persistent infection, which can evolve into chronic HBV infection. Chronic HBV infection is often asymptomatic, but a significant proportion of chronic carriers—ranging from 15% to 40%—may progress to cirrhosis and/or HCC [16]. Beyond liver-related diseases, HBV infection has been associated with various extrahepatic complications, including polyarteritis nodosa, non-rheumatoid arthritis, polyarthritis, non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma, cryoglobulinemia, serum sickness syndrome, glomerulonephritis, neurological disorders, and dermatological conditions [16–18]. Current therapeutic strategies focus on maximizing inhibition of HBV replication to reduce liver necrosis, inflammation, and fibrosis, ultimately delaying and mitigating the onset of liver failure, decompensated cirrhosis, HCC, and other associated complications. This approach improves patients’ quality of life and prolongs life expectancy [19, 20].

The oncogenic roles of HBV

HBx is a regulatory protein that plays a pivotal role in viral replication and carcinogenesis. It has been shown to affect the stability of DNA in host hepatocytes through transactivation, facilitating HBV infection and enabling extensive viral replication [21]. In HCC development, HBx also plays a central role by promoting viral replication [22, 23]. Errors in HBV replication can lead to the integration of double-stranded linear DNA (dslDNA) into the hepatocyte genome. This integration results in the sustained expression of the dslDNA, thereby stabilizing the transcription of HBx and HBsAg [24–26]. Additionally, the integration of HBV DNA into the host genome can cause chromosomal translocations, which contribute to genomic instability and ultimately promote malignant transformation. Integration typically occurs within the early days of infection, indicating that carcinogenesis may begin early and is a critical mechanism in both intrahepatic and extrahepatic cancers [27–29]. Extensive research has shown elevated levels of HBx expression in cancer cells from patients with GC and pancreatic cancer, suggesting that HBV infection may significantly contribute to GC development [30]. In a landmark study, Li et al. first identified HBV integration in HBV-associated GC, revealing that HBV can induce genomic instability and structural variations in GC. Consequently, the integration of HBV genes may be a key factor in GC development [31].

Gastric cancer

Epidemiology

GC is a major global health issue, particularly in East Asian countries [32]. According to the Global Cancer Statistics Report, GC ranks as the fifth most prevalent cancer worldwide and is the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths. Annually, over 968,000 new cases of GC are reported, resulting in approximately 660,000 deaths [3, 33]. Like most cancers, GC is rare in individuals under 50 years of age, with incidence rates increasing with age [34]. However, recent studies show a rising incidence of non-cardia GC among younger individuals (< 50 years old), especially in countries such as the United Kingdom and the United States [35–37]. In China, GC is one of the leading malignancies, ranking third in both incidence and mortality among all cancer types. Chinese patients account for nearly 44.0% of new global GC cases and 48.6% of GC-related deaths [38].

Molecular classification of gastric cancer

GC is typically classified based on the Lauren classification, which categorizes the cancer into intestinal, diffuse, and mixed types. While widely used, this histopathological classification has limitations, as it does not provide detailed insights into the tumor’s developmental process and is insufficient for selecting optimal treatment strategies [39]. The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) further refines the classification of GC into four molecular subtypes: Epstein-Barr virus-positive (EBV+), tumors with microsatellite instability (MSI), tumors with chromosomal instability (CIN), and genomically stable (GS) tumors. This molecular classification significantly contributes to advancing research aimed at improving the diagnosis and treatment of patients with GC [40]. As the understanding of GC continues to progress, a subgroup of EBV-positive patients with GC has been identified, and their characteristics, mechanisms, and treatment approaches have been thoroughly reviewed and analyzed [41]. Further investigation into HBV-associated GC is also warranted.

Multiple causative factors in gastric cancer development

GC develops through a multifactorial and multistep process, with key risk factors including genetic predisposition, dietary habits, alcohol consumption, tobacco use, and infections with Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) and EBV [42]. H. pylori is recognized as the primary risk factor for GC, with extensive evidence supporting its role in the multistep progression of gastric tumorigenesis [43–45]. Infection with H. pylori leads to chronic gastritis, which can progress to gastric mucosal atrophy, intestinal metaplasia, dysplasia, and eventually gastric adenocarcinoma [46]. H. pylori is globally widespread, with regional variations in its prevalence. It is estimated that approximately 50% of the global population is infected with this bacterium [47]. The epidemiology of H. pylori infection is shaped by various factors, including age, geographic location, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, living conditions, and hygiene practices [48]. Infection rates in young children range from 36% in developed countries to as high as 50% in developing countries [49–51]. To reduce the global burden of GC associated with H. pylori, it is crucial to collect and utilize these data in the development of effective public health strategies [52]. In addition, EBV infection accounts for approximately 8.77% of GC cases. It is hypothesized that EBV may contribute to GC development by expressing latent genes (e.g., EBNA1, BART miRNAs) and inducing abnormal host DNA methylation [53]. Recent studies have also revealed a significant association between HBV infection and GC [5, 6], prompting further investigation into the potential causal relationship between HBV and GC. Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis is considered a robust method for inferring causal links between exposures and outcomes, using genetic variants as instrumental variables to strengthen causal inference [54]. Cai et al. conducted the first comprehensive study combining MR and bioinformatics analyses to explore the mechanisms linking chronic HBV infection to GC. Their findings provided evidence that chronic HBV infection increases the risk of GC [55], suggesting a potential causal relationship between the two.

HBV in gastric cancer risk

In recent years, numerous meta-analyses have explored the potential link between HBV infection and GC (Table 1). Cui et al. conducted a meta-analysis that combined eight studies (three case-control and five cohort studies) and found a 23% higher risk of developing GC in individuals infected with HBV compared to those without HBV infection (OR = 1.23, 95% CI: 1.10–1.37). However, the authors’ own case-control study did not reveal a significant association between HBV serological markers and GC in cancer patients, suggesting that HBV infection-induced systemic immune dysfunction may contribute to these results [56]. Consistent with this, Wang et al. performed a meta-analysis including seven studies with 53,396 GC patients and 325,458 controls. This study found a positive correlation between HBV infection and GC risk, with an odds ratio of 1.56 (95% CI: 1.18–2.07). The association remained significant even after excluding individuals positive for H. pylori (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.11–1.75) [57]. Similarly, Yang et al. reviewed 10 studies and concluded that HBV infection significantly elevated GC risk (pooled hazard ratio [HR] = 1.26, 95% CI: 1.08–1.47) [58]. In a more recent meta-analysis, Yu et al. analyzed 16 studies and found a significant association between HBV infection and increased GC risk (pooled OR = 1.29, 95% CI: 1.16–1.44). Subgroup analyses showed this association was particularly pronounced in the Chinese population (OR = 1.35), further supporting the link between HBV infection and GC risk [59].

Despite these findings, some contradictory results have been reported. Baghbanian et al. did not find a direct association between HBV infection and GC incidence in a cohort study of the Iranian population. However, they observed a significantly higher incidence of GC in patients co-infected with HBV and H. pylori compared to those infected with H. pylori alone, suggesting that HBV infection may promote the progression of precancerous lesions and carcinogenesis [60]. Liu et al. conducted a prospective cohort study involving 93,402 participants and found that HBV infection significantly increased the risk of gastrointestinal cancers (e.g., liver, gallbladder, and pancreatic cancer), although the study did not support a significant association with GC [61]. Nevertheless, a number of studies continue to indicate a positive correlation between HBV and GC risk [62]. Notably, Wei et al. were the first to report a significant association between HBV infection and GC in a case-control study (OR = 1.49, 95% CI: 1.06–2.10), suggesting that latent HBV infection, along with the synergistic effect of HBV infection and blood type A, may further elevate GC risk [63]. In a large-scale case-control study, Wang et al. used propensity score matching analysis to determine that HBsAg positivity was significantly associated with an increased risk of GC (adjusted OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.05–1.85). Conversely, anti-HBs positivity was found to be protective. In univariate analysis, both HBsAg and anti-HBe were significantly associated with a higher risk of GC, and multivariate analysis further confirmed the significant relationship between HBsAg positivity and GC [64]. Li et al. compiled 12 cohort and case-control studies and found that HBV infection raised the risk of GC by approximately 29% (sOR = 1.29; 95% CI: 1.22–1.37), with a notably higher risk in regions with moderate to high HBV prevalence compared to those with low to moderate prevalence. This further reinforces the established correlation between HBV infection and GC, positioning HBV as an independent adverse prognostic factor. Additionally, potential carcinogenic mutation genes associated with HBV were identified, offering valuable insights for future mechanistic research in this area [65]. In a recent case-control study in Fujian Province, Chen et al. found that the risk of GC in individuals with HBsAg positivity was 3.30 times higher than in uninfected individuals (95% CI: 1.84–5.91). A meta-analysis combining existing studies yielded an odds ratio of 1.34 (95% CI: 1.19–1.50). The study also identified a significant interaction between HBV infection and smoking, with the combined presence increasing the risk of GC by 5.45 times [66]. Although confounding factors cannot be completely excluded, the results of most meta-analyses align with epidemiological studies, supporting the conclusion that HBV infection substantially increases the risk of GC [64].

Moreover, a study utilizing the Japanese Biobank (Biobank Japan, BBJ) with a two-sample MR approach identified a potential causal relationship between chronic HBV infection and extrahepatic cancers. This analysis confirmed the association between HBV infection and cervical cancer (OR = 1.57, 95% CI = 1.29–1.91, P = 0.0001) and GC (OR = 1.12, 95% CI = 1.05–1.19, P = 0.0001), yielding statistically robust results [67]. A similar MR study using the BBJ database employed five independent genetic variants strongly associated with chronic HBV infection as instrumental variables to investigate the mechanisms underlying the link between chronic HBV infection and GC. The study further supported the existence of a potential causal link between HBV infection and GC, with sensitivity analyses confirming the robustness of the results. The association was found to potentially be mediated by the TLR signaling pathway, involving genes such as chemokine (CXCL9) and collagen (COL6A2), thus providing a foundation for further mechanistic investigations [55].

Pathogenesis of HBV-associated gastric cancer

HBV DNA integration

HBV infection has been shown to mediate the integration of viral DNA into the host genome [68]. HBV integration refers to the insertion of HBV-derived DNA fragments into the human genome [69] and has been detected in approximately 85% of HBV-associated HCC cases [70]. This integration promotes tumorigenesis through three primary mechanisms: [1] regulation of cancer-related genes; [2] induction of CIN; and [3] expression of HBV proteins resulting from integration-related mutations [71]. Previous research has demonstrated the expression of HBV DNA and HBcAg in GC tissues, with HBV DNA or HBV antigens detected in extrahepatic organs, including the bile duct epithelium [72]. Recent evidence has provided direct confirmation of HBV integration into the host genome of GC tissues. HBV cccDNA was detected in 85.7% of GC samples and 87.5% of adjacent cancerous tissues. HBV integration primarily occurs in the 3’ terminal regions of chromosomes 7, 11, 21, and the C and X genes. The genomic instability and structural variations induced by this integration likely contribute to GC development. Notably, the bud-like RTK signal antagonist 3 (SPRY3) was identified with up to 102 instances of HBV reintegration. The present study shows that suppression of SPRY3 significantly inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of GC cells. In summary, this study delineated a more comprehensive carcinogenic mechanism: the presence and integration of HBV interfere with the expression and structure of key host genes (e.g., SPRY3), thereby promoting the malignant transformation of gastric epithelial cells and subsequent tumor progression [31] (Fig. 3).

HBx protein

HBx, the sole regulatory protein encoded by the HBV genome, plays a central role in viral replication, interferon-mediated HBV-associated liver carcinogenesis, and modulates cccDNA transcription through various mechanisms [73–75]. Biologically, HBx induces liver cell cycle arrest at the G1 phase and promotes HCC development via the JNK pathway [76]. Additionally, in the context of tumorigenesis and metastasis, HBx regulates the ten hallmarks of cancer through multiple pathways, playing a pivotal role in the development and metastasis of HCC [73]. Beyond HCC, growing evidence highlights a strong association between HBx and the development of nephritis, diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, and GC [77–79]. Guo et al. reported that HBx expression was significantly higher in gastric ulcer tissues compared to normal gastric tissues. The upregulation of HBx resulted in a notable increase in the expression of endoplasmic reticulum stress molecules (BiP and CHOP) and activation of the JNK pathway, promoting apoptosis and G1 phase arrest in gastric mucosal cell lines (GES-1), which may exacerbate gastric mucosal damage and induce gastric ulcers [80]. Further studies revealed that HBx activates the β-catenin/TCF signaling pathway by increasing the expression of its downstream target gene URG11, while also promoting cyclin D1 and MT1-MMP expression, thereby contributing to the development of GC. These findings suggest that URG11 may serve as a key oncogenic factor in HBV-associated GC [77].

Cancer-associated gene mutations

In a targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) study conducted in Anhui Province, China, focusing on GC patients with and without HBV infection, elevated mutation frequencies in five genes — KMT2B, KMT2D, SOX1, FGF12, and TUBB2B — were observed in HBV-positive patients. This suggests that HBV infection may contribute to the molecular mechanisms underlying GC development [65]. KMT2B (also known as MLL4), a member of the histone H3K4 methyltransferase family, plays a pivotal role in regulating spontaneous movement and modulating epigenetic modifications. These actions promote tumorigenic effects in cancerous cells [81, 82]. HBV often integrates into the third to sixth exons of the KMT2B gene, creating virus-host fusion transcripts that disrupt normal epigenetic regulation and contribute to HCC development [69]. Recent studies employing gene editing to induce induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) models, free from interference from other tumor-related mutations, demonstrated that HBV-KMT2B integration leads to abnormal cell proliferation, elevated levels of the cell cycle-related marker histone 3 lysine 4 tri-methylation (H3K4me3), replication stress, and DNA damage, all of which ultimately promote HCC formation [83]. These findings suggest that HBV integration may induce abnormal H3K4me3 modifications, which in turn promote cell proliferation and DNA damage, contributing to HCC development. NGS analysis has also revealed that KMT2B is frequently mutated in uterine corpus endometrial carcinoma (UCEC), esophageal sarcomatoid carcinoma (ESC), and GC [84–86]. A study investigating the association between tumor mutation burden (TMB) and prognosis in GC patients identified KMT2B as one of the top five most mutated genes, suggesting its potential direct involvement in GC development and its contribution to poor patient outcomes [87]. Lysine methyltransferase 2D (KMT2D), a widely expressed H3K4 methyltransferase, is found throughout human tissues [88]. Mutations in KMT2D have been associated with developmental disorders and various cancers [89]. KMT2D exhibits a dual role in tumors, acting as a tumor suppressor in bladder cancer by inhibiting cell growth and metastasis [88, 90]. In GC, however, high-frequency KMT2D mutations promote cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, while KMT2D knockdown induces apoptosis, suggesting its function as a pro-tumor factor [91, 92]. SOX1, a member of the SOX transcription factor family, plays a critical role in embryonic development and stem cell maintenance, and is closely involved in tumor initiation and progression [93, 94]. In cancer, SOX1 shows functional heterogeneity. While serving as a tumor suppressor in various cancers, including hepatocellular, cervical, breast, lung, and laryngeal cancers, its expression in GC is regulated by miR-155, promoting tumor progression [95, 96]. Fibroblast growth factor 12 (FGF12), a member of the fibroblast growth factor family, is overexpressed in GC and co-activates cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) alongside Ectodysplasin-A, thus contributing to GC development [97, 98]. The exact role of TUBB2B mutations in GC remains unclear, but their high frequency suggests a potential involvement in the disease. It is important to note that although these mutations are more prevalent in HBV-infected patients, current evidence indicates that they may act as coexisting factors in the development of HBV-associated gastric cancer rather than as direct causative agents. Therefore, HBV infection may promote gastric cancer initiation and progression through synergistic interactions with these mutations.

Cirrhosis

Cirrhosis is a major complication and leading cause of mortality in chronic liver disease worldwide, with HBV being one of its primary etiological factors [99]. HBsAg has been identified as an independent risk factor for cirrhosis [100]. Cirrhosis can lead to HCC and liver dysfunction, presenting with clinical symptoms such as ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and variceal bleeding [101]. Additionally, it has been shown to serve as an independent risk factor for GC [102]. The incidence of GC and esophageal cancer is significantly higher in patients with cirrhosis [103, 104]. A prospective study in Greece demonstrated that peptic ulcers are observed in the asymptomatic stage of cirrhosis, with peptic ulcers being positively associated with GC risk [105, 106]. Furthermore, most cirrhosis patients exhibit portal hypertension-related gastric disorders, including congestive gastropathy (CG), gastric erosion, and ulcers, which may increase the risk of GC due to chronic inflammation of the gastric mucosa [107]. H. pylori infection is common among cirrhosis patients, and HBV infection may synergistically elevate the risk of GC in H. pylori-positive individuals, indicating a potential carcinogenic synergy between HBV and H. pylori [108–110]. Therefore, this study hypothesizes that H. pylori and HBV may work synergistically in the development of GC. Cirrhosis can also impair gut-liver axis function, leading to gut microbiota dysbiosis and exacerbating carcinogenic risks [111]. Consequently, due to the synergistic effects of multiple factors, patients with cirrhosis are a high-risk population for GC [107].

Metabolic reprogramming

Metabolic reprogramming represents a hallmark of cancer-related physiological alterations, with cancer cells preferentially utilizing glycolysis to meet their bioenergetic and biosynthetic demands [112]. HBV reprograms several core metabolic pathways—including glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, oxidative phosphorylation, and lipid homeostasis—to support viral replication and evade immune surveillance [113]. Owing to its profound impact on host metabolic networks, HBV has been characterized as a “metabolic virus” [114]. Persistent HBV infection results in marked alterations in glucose metabolism [115]. In particular, HBx has been shown to activate NF-κB p65 in hepatocytes, thereby enhancing aerobic glycolysis through the NF-κB p65/HK2 signaling axis. This leads to excessive lactate accumulation and accelerates oncogenic transformation [116]. In addition to glucose metabolism, HBV infection substantially remodels lipid metabolism. Activation of the MAPK/mTOR signaling pathway by HBV reprograms lipid metabolism in HCC cells, while the resulting metabolic intermediates further stimulate the MAPK/mTOR cascade, forming a self-perpetuating loop that promotes tumor progression [117]. Moreover, HBV-related factors may also contribute to carcinogenesis in gastric tissues through metabolic mechanisms. Recent evidence indicates that hepatitis B X-interacting protein (HBXIP) plays a pivotal role in this process. Elevated HBXIP expression reprograms glucose metabolism in GC cells by activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and suppressing p53 activity, thereby promoting aerobic glycolysis, increasing lactate production, and ultimately facilitating GC cell proliferation, migration, and invasion [118].

Chronic inflammation

Chronic inflammation is widely recognized as a significant pathogenic mechanism in various digestive system tumors, including GC, HCC, and colorectal cancer. Inflammatory cells and the molecular mediators they release have been shown to promote gene mutations by inducing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and DNA damage, thus increasing the risk of malignant transformation [119, 120]. The presence of HBV in gastric tissue generates a persistent inflammatory environment, characterized by substantial epithelial atypia and lymphocytic infiltration within the lamina propria. This suggests that inflammation may act as a catalyst for gastric epithelial cell carcinogenesis [56]. Moreover, it has been demonstrated that chronic inflammation induced by HBV can trigger cellular genetic instability and exacerbate epigenetic alterations [58]. Further evidence suggests that HBV may induce permanent DNA damage, initiating a “subthreshold tumor state” that in turn stimulates local gastric inflammation, creating conditions conducive to the development of GC [121, 122].

Tumor microenvironment (TME)

The TME is a complex and dynamic system that serves as the foundation for tumor initiation and progression. It consists of tumor cells, stromal cells, and the extracellular matrix (ECM), with their interactions driving malignant tumor formation [123, 124]. Chronic HBV infection has been shown to alter TME characteristics by depleting immune cells and modulating the host immune system, promoting tumor immune escape, invasion, and metastasis [125, 126]. The TME facilitates tumor cell survival and proliferation, enhancing their adaptability and resistance to therapeutic interventions [126]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the interactions between HBV infection and the TME is essential for developing targeted therapeutic strategies for HBV-associated GC.

Treatment and prognosis

As highlighted in the study conducted in Anhui Province, China, patients with HBV-associated GC generally have a poorer prognosis. Compared to those negative for HBV, HBV-positive patients exhibited significantly shorter disease-free survival (HR = 1.98; 95% CI: 1.39–2.82) and overall survival (HR = 1.84; 95% CI: 1.19–2.85). Cox proportional hazards regression analysis further confirmed that HBV infection is an independent adverse prognostic factor for GC [65]. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) primarily target programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligand PD-L1 [127]. Over the past decade, substantial progress has been made in oncology, with novel treatments demonstrating remarkable efficacy in managing advanced cancers by blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction [128]. A study from Tongji Hospital in China showed that PD-L1 expression is increased in chronic HBV infection and HCC, potentially leading to immune suppression, viral persistence, and tumor development, suggesting that PD-L1 may be a promising target for immunotherapy [129]. Notably, approximately 5.3% of HBsAg-positive patients experienced HBV reactivation during treatment with anti-PD-1 or anti-PD-L1 therapy, a serious complication that may arise in cancer patients receiving ICIs or chemotherapy. However, HBsAg-positive status should not be considered a contraindication for immune checkpoint inhibitor use [130]. Conversely, studies have found that PD-L1 expression levels are reduced in HBV-associated GC, potentially diminishing the clinical benefits of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy [56]. Recent studies have reported that anti–PD-1 therapy has achieved favorable therapeutic outcomes in GC patients with concomitant chronic HBV infection [131]. Among the emerging therapeutic approaches, aberrant regulation of the ubiquitin–proteasome system (UPS) plays a crucial role in the initiation and progression of numerous malignancies. Notably, ubiquitination in HBV-induced HCC exhibits a characteristic “double-edged sword” effect: on one hand, it facilitates viral degradation by the host defense system, while on the other, HBV exploits this process to evade immune surveillance and promote its replication. Therefore, targeting dysregulated UPS activity is considered a promising next-generation strategy for combating HBV-associated HCC [132]. Protein degradation–based therapies, such as proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs), have recently emerged as a revolutionary approach to cancer treatment [133]. Leveraging the regulatory characteristics of the UPS, Chen et al. developed PROTAC molecules using crizotinib as a ligand to recruit the Cereblon (CRBN) E3 ligase and induce MET protein degradation. These PROTACs demonstrated significant antitumor efficacy in both GC cell lines and animal models [134], providing a novel conceptual framework for applying targeted protein degradation therapy to HBV-associated gastric cancer. In addition, covalently cccDNA of HBV remains a critical therapeutic target for the eradication of HBV infection [9]. The CRISPR/Cas gene-editing system can efficiently cleave conserved regions of the HBV genome through multi-target guide RNA (gRNA)–directed CRISPR/Cas9 activity, thereby disrupting both cccDNA and integrated viral DNA to suppress viral replication and antigen expression [135]. Furthermore, CRISPR/Cas9-mediated base editors (BEs) enable precise, permanent inactivation of cccDNA and integrated HBV DNA without inducing double-strand DNA breaks (DSBs), offering a potential curative strategy for HBV infection [136]. In the context of gastric cancer, CRISPR/Cas9 technology has also been employed to systematically identify essential genes for tumor cell survival and to uncover novel therapeutic targets [137]. Thus, CRISPR/Cas9-based genome editing holds great promise not only for direct viral genome disruption but also for the discovery and development of innovative therapeutic strategies for HBV-associated gastric cancer.

Conclusion and prospects

Despite being classified as a hepatotropic virus, HBV DNA expression has been detected in various human organs and tissues, including the stomach, spleen, kidneys, and bile duct epithelium. The association between HBV and GC has gained increasing attention in recent years. Substantial clinical and epidemiological evidence supports the notion that chronic HBV infection significantly elevates the risk of GC, offering new insights into its pathogenesis and highlighting HBV’s potential role in GC development. While numerous studies have clearly established distinct carcinogenic mechanisms in HBV-mediated HCC, the role of HBV in GC development remains poorly understood. Most research has focused on the carcinogenic roles of HBV DNA integration, HBx protein, cirrhosis, metabolic reprogramming, chronic inflammation, and the TME in GC. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the included studies varied in design, population characteristics, and diagnostic criteria for both HBV and gastric cancer, which may limit the comparability of findings. Most available evidence is retrospective, making it challenging to establish a causal relationship between HBV infection and gastric cancer. Moreover, potential confounding factors (such as dietary habits, genetic susceptibility, and regional bias) may influence the generalizability of the conclusions. Nevertheless, acknowledging these limitations helps identify important directions for future research. Future studies will conduct more systematic, prospective, and mechanistic investigations. First, gastric mucosa and cancer tissue samples will be collected from GC patients with well-defined HBV infection status. Second, multi-omics sequencing technologies will be employed to analyze viral integration sites, host gene mutation profiles, and features of the immune microenvironment. Third, in vitro cell culture and animal co-infection models will be established to simulate the interactions between HBV and H. pylori, thereby elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which HBV contributes to malignant transformation and inflammatory responses. Collectively, these studies are expected to uncover critical aspects of HBV-associated gastric carcinogenesis and provide a theoretical foundation for improving clinical treatment strategies and patient outcomes.
