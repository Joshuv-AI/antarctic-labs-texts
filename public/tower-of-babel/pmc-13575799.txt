ABSTRACT
SummaryRheumatic heart disease (RHD) remains a significant public health challenge in Latin America, disproportionately affecting vulnerable populations despite global declines in disease prevalence and mortality. It is known that RHD affects over 55 million individuals worldwide, accounting for more than 390,000 deaths annually. However, region-specific RHD epidemiologic data are scarce, and most clinical studies on RHD epidemiology date back to the 1990s. On the other hand, the emergence of echocardiographic screening initiatives in the last decade reinforced the burden of the disease, revealing alarming rates of subclinical RHD in Latin American schoolchildren and young adults. This comprehensive review focused on epidemiologic data on RHD from Latin America and highlighted subgroups at highest risk for the disease.

Introduction

In the 17th century, Thomas Sydenham, known as the “English Hippocrates”, first accurately described an acute, febrile polyarthritis that later became known as acute rheumatic fever (ARF). At that time, Sydenham attributed this acute rheumatism to a “morbific matter in the system”.1 In 1788, David Pitcairn and Edward Jenner were the first to associate rheumatic fever and heart disease definitively, and the pathologist Lasègue wrote in 1884 the famous phrase “rheumatic fever licks the joints but bites the heart”.1 Over the years, researchers have continued to study this acute rheumatism and described various manifestations associated with it, including thickening of the heart valves as well as the presence of valve vegetations.2 At the end of the 19th century and beginning of the 20th century, the central piece of the rheumatic fever puzzle arose with the recognition that group A streptococcus is responsible for rheumatic fever.3 A few years later, Fleming's discovery of penicillin in 1928 enabled a primary prophylaxis of ARF through the adequate treatment of group A streptococcus pharyngotonsillitis.4 After centuries of morbidity and mortality, improved access to primary prophylaxis and living conditions led to a substantial decline in ARF and rheumatic heart disease (RHD) by the late 1900s in middle- and high-income countries. However, low- and middle-income countries continued to bear a disproportionate burden, leaving RHD a neglected and inequitable disease.

RHD remains the most common acquired valvular condition in children and adolescents and is the leading indication for mitral valve surgery in low- and middle-income countries.5,6 Globally, around 54 million individuals are estimated to live with RHD, and over 380,000 deaths occur annually.7,8 Its burden is highly concentrated in low- and middle-income countries, reflecting disparities in income, healthcare access, and preventive measures.9 However, it has recently been recognized, at least in the pediatric population, that even in Western countries, RHD is “forgotten but not gone,” with under-recognition in patients without significant stenosis or regurgitation despite morphologic features of RHD.10

RHD develops as a consequence of ARF, an immune-mediated complication of untreated group A streptococcus pharyngotonsillitis (Fig. 1).9 Group A streptococcus throat colonization is high among RHD patients.11 Recurrent clinical or subclinical group A streptococcus infections perpetuate an exacerbated inflammatory response that culminates in disease progression and advanced valvular heart disease. Diagnosis of ARF relies on a combination of clinical, serological, and echocardiographic criteria, whereas the diagnosis of RHD is based predominantly on characteristic echocardiographic findings.9 Notably, a significant proportion of children with echocardiographic evidence of RHD report no prior history of ARF.12 Despite this, approximately 7.5% of subclinical RHD cases progress to clinically significant disease, and at least 60% remain with echo-detected alterations.12Fig. 1Rheumatic Fever: Progression and Interventions. The significant burden imposed by the disease during the 20th century led to the establishment of numerous global efforts to reduce its consequences, as many programs were discontinued. Even the Global Rheumatic Heart Disease Control Program, coordinated by the WHO since 1984, was terminated in the early 2000s.13 For years thereafter, RHD became a neglected disease, with limited high-quality research and scarce progress in management. More recently, however, new initiatives spearheaded by institutions such as the World Heart Federation (WHF) and the WHO have renewed attention to RHD and stimulated further research.14 The U.S. National Institutes of Health issued a special funding call to advance research on rheumatic heart valve disease. In parallel, the Leducq Foundation continues to support international collaborative networks focused on biomarker discovery, group A streptococcus vaccine development, and the creation of polymeric heart valves for advanced rheumatic heart valve disease.

Importantly, RHD is preventable. Adequate antibiotic treatment of streptococcal pharyngitis prevents ARF, while penicillin prophylaxis after ARF or following the detection of subclinical RHD can halt progression to advanced disease (Fig. 1).9,15 Understanding the epidemiology of ARF and RHD in endemic regions is therefore crucial for strengthening primary and secondary prevention. This review focuses on Latin America, where RHD remains endemic, with the aim of identifying populations at highest risk and informing strategies to reduce its burden. The review is structured in two parts: first, the overall epidemiology of RHD in Latin America, and second, the special population and high-risk groups living in the region.

We searched PubMed, EMBASE, and LILACS in September 2025 from inception using terms related to ‘Latin America’, ‘Caribbean’, ‘South America’, ‘rheumatic heart disease’, and ‘acute rheumatic fever’, including MeSH terms and abbreviations. We also screened reference lists and performed backward snowballing to capture additional studies. We prioritized systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and Global Burden of Disease estimates, and systematically screened prior global reviews and meta-analyses to identify studies conducted in Latin America. To contextualize Latin American findings, we additionally reviewed screening and epidemiological studies from Oceania, Africa, Asia, Europe, and the United States.

Epidemiology of RHD in Latin America

Incidence, prevalence, and outcomes of RHD in Latin America

RHD poses a significant burden in Latin America. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) gathers the most robust data in RHD epidemiology and provides significant insights into the disease.8,16 Importantly, GBD has inherent limitations as there is a paucity of quality data, especially in low- and middle-income countries, including those in Latin America other limitations include data availability and data gaps, with high heterogeneity across regions.17 On a global scale, RHD was responsible for 14.5 million DALYs (disability-adjusted life year) in 2023.16

A study delved specifically into the burden of the RHD during 1990–2019 using the 2019 GBD.18 Regarding age-standardized trends, in Latin America and the Caribbean, RHD had an incidence of 39 per 100,000 and a prevalence of 655 per 100,000 in 2019, both of which were not significantly different from 1990 levels.18 Conversely, RHD caused 1.1 deaths per 100,000 in 2019, which represented a 65% decrease compared to 1990, probably attributed to optimized and more accessible surgical and non-surgical treatments for RHD, besides secondary prophylaxis.18

In Latin America and the Caribbean, RHD had a prevalence of 738.8 per 100,000 women and 566.5 per 100,000 men in 2019 (more than 5 times the prevalence in the U.S.).7,19 In the same year, RHD caused 1.3 deaths per 100,000 in women and 0.8 per 100,000 in men in Latin America.7 Comparing 1990 and 2019 data, there were non-significant changes in the prevalence and incidence of RHD among both sexes, while the mortality decreased significantly in both (65.6% in women and 64.8% in men). Interestingly, these data contrasts with most RHD published studies that report a risk ratio of at least 1.6–2.0 for females compared with males.20 For mitral stenosis, the female predominance is even more pronounced, and a large Brazilian study reported a 4:1 female: male ratio.20

Furthermore, in age-standardized data per 100,000, RHD also accounted for 64.7 DALYs, 33.6 YLLs (years of life lost), and 31.2 YLDs (years lived with disability) in 2019, concerning Latin America and the Caribbean.7 Worldwide, RHD was associated with 132.9 DALYs, 107.7 YLLs, and 25.2 YLDs in the same year. Importantly, compared to 1990 data, YLLs and DALYs of RHD dramatically decreased, but YLDs increased, probably due to treatment of RHD and its cardiac consequences.

However, region-specific RHD epidemiologic data are scarce. A high-quality systematic review performed in 2022 exposed the current situation of ARF and RHD in Latin America and the Caribbean (Fig. 2).17 Two Brazilian studies using national mortality databases evaluated deaths from RHD among women of reproductive age. The first study, published in 1991, reported a mortality rate of 2.6 deaths per 100,000 women in 1986, the second study, published in 2000, reported a mortality rate of 1.58 deaths per 100,000 women between 1991 and 1995.22,23 Additionally, the Informatics Department of the Brazilian National Health System (DATASUS) provides data on hospitalizations and mortality rates for various diseases. Importantly, these data are dependent on the ICD code provided by the physician at the discharge or death; therefore, these numbers are likely underestimated. According to DATASUS, between 2013 and 2023, RHD accounted for 82,080 hospitalizations and 6751 deaths in Brazil, with 7812 hospitalizations and 597 deaths in 2023. Although crucial, GBD and the national administrative registries that provide most of the epidemiological data on RHD in Latin America have inherent limitations that are accentuated in low- and middle-income regions. Importantly, these databases rely on proper ICD coding by the health professional. A combination of health system overload due to high demand and infrastructure and software limitations could lead to misclassification and significant variability in ICD coding. In Latin America, these obstacles may lead to an underestimation of the regional incidence, prevalence, and mortality of RHD. Furthermore, as an echocardiogram is a cornerstone of RHD diagnosis, a shortage of proper equipment and personnel in underserved areas also contributes to the underestimation of these parameters. Nevertheless, even with these limitations, epidemiological estimates derived from GBD, and national administrative databases are indispensable for highlighting RHD as a neglected disease that warrants further public health strategies to ease this burden.

Complications and procedures related to RHD

RHD is associated with several complications that pose significant morbidity and mortality. Atrial fibrillation is the most common arrhythmia, affecting approximately 30% of patients in a large Brazilian cohort with rheumatic mitral valve disease.25 In the same study, the incidence of stroke was 1.47 strokes per 100 patient-years.25 In a Brazilian randomized controlled trial comparing warfarin and aspirin in RHD patients reported an incidence of embolic events of 3.7% patient-year and a better outcome in the warfarin group.26

The hemodynamic burden of rheumatic mitral stenosis ultimately leads to pulmonary hypertension. A Peruvian hospital-based study from 1994 reported a prevalence of pulmonary hypertension of 16.7%, based on a retrospective analysis of 66 patients with rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease admitted to a single tertiary cardiology center.27 In Cuba, a preventive study, the incidence of heart failure in patients with RHD decreased from 11.2% in 1986–1990 to 1.5% in 1989–1993.28

Two Brazilian studies found a need for intervention (surgical or percutaneous) of 21.5%–34.4% in patients with RHD, while a study conducted in Cuba reported a decreased rate of 4.5% in 1986–1990 to 0.5% in 1991–1996, and another study conducted in Peru reported a 12.1% rate from 1989 to 1993.25,27,29 These discrepancies are likely related to the severity of the disease of the patients included.

A study conducted in the Dominican Republic from 2014 to 2018 collected clinical and procedural data and assessed yearly RHD trends across seven surgical centers, revealing that among 1626 valvular heart surgeries, 45% were attributable to RHD.30 Similarly, in Salvador, Brazil was performed a retrospective analysis from 2002 to 2005 of open heart valve surgeries. They found that 60% of surgeries were due to RHD, with a median age of 37 years and a mean annual incidence of 4.86 open-heart surgeries secondary to RHD per 100,000 population.31 In another study, RHD accounted for 20% of mitral valve replacements and was independently associated with prolonged hospitalization after adjustment for established postoperative risk factors.32

According to DATASUS, from 2013 to 2023, there were 2658 balloon valvuloplasty in Brazil, with a highly uneven distribution: 1532 in the Southeastern states and 18 in the Northern region, with the remaining cases spread across the other regions.33 Higher-income areas, with only about 1.5 times the population, have more than 85 times the number of valvuloplasty.

A multicenter, hospital-based and prospective study of 13,696 patients with RHD from 24 countries, including some from Latin America, found that annual mortality reached 4.7% and was linked to valvular disease severity. Both surgical valve replacement and percutaneous valvuloplasty, as expected, were associated with reduced mortality.34

Acute rheumatic fever: historical and contemporary trends

ARF is still underdiagnosed, and poor data exist concerning the epidemiology of this disease worldwide, especially regarding incidence and prevalence. For instance, several published studies reference that Brazil has approximately 30,000 new cases of ARF annually.35 However, this estimate has been used since the early 2000s, based on a census reporting ∼10 million cases of pharyngotonsillitis every year and applying a 0.3% rate of ARF based on literature findings.36

In 1992, a study conducted in Belo Horizonte, Brazil with schoolchildren revealed a prevalence of 3.6 ARF cases per 1000 by echocardiogram screening (Fig. 2).37 In Pinar del Río Province, Cuba, a study based on cross-sectional school surveys—a less specific method—reported prevalences of 1.75 and 5.78 per 1000 schoolchildren in 1985 and 1996, respectively.28 Furthermore, a study searching national data and published records in PubMed reported that ARF incidence in Chile decreased from 2.2 per 100,000 in 1978 to 0 in 1998, and a retrospective study of yearly hospital admission conducted in Barbados found that ARF incidence decreased from 13 per 100,000 in 1971 to 2 per 100,000 in 1990 (Fig. 2).38,39

A recent study using DATASUS analyzed temporal trends of ARF hospitalization and mortality in Brazil from 2008 to 2022, also considering the regional distribution of these trends over the years. Overall, ARF hospitalization decreased from 1104 in 2008 to 243 in 2022.40 These studies have intrinsic limitations when using national databases based on ICD codes and cause of death; however, they all point toward a decrease in ARF incidence. Which is not as clear for RHD and will be discussed in the next section.

Prevalence of RHD by echocardiography

Historically, cardiac auscultation played a central and, for many years, exclusive role in detecting rheumatic carditis, particularly in asymptomatic patients with normal electrocardiograms. In the context of fever, arthritis, or other signs of ARF, an apical holosystolic murmur louder than grade II/VI, with a blowing quality and radiation to the axilla, was considered diagnostic of rheumatic carditis. In 1989, Taranta and Markowitz even suggested that screening schoolchildren for heart murmurs in regions with a high prevalence of RHD could be worthwhile.41 Soon after, echocardiography emerged as a more sensitive tool for detecting and evaluating rheumatic carditis.42 Beyond monitoring established RHD, echocardiography enabled the detection of subclinical carditis in patients with suspected ARF and equivocal auscultatory findings. Over time, it became the cornerstone for RHD assessment, and the 2015 Jones Criteria revision formally incorporated echocardiographic evidence of subclinical carditis as a major criterion for ARF.43

A significant proportion of patients with RHD do not recall any episode suggestive of ARF, and many asymptomatic individuals remain undiagnosed until later stages, when hemodynamic compromise is already present.44 As Taranta and Markowitz proposed in 1989, screening during the silent phase between unrecognized ARF and clinically significant RHD may be critical for detecting early cardiac lesions and preventing severe outcomes.41 In this context, echocardiography began to be used as a screening tool. Since then, thousands of asymptomatic children and young adults with no history of ARF have been diagnosed with RHD and started on secondary prophylaxis. The largest screening study in terms of sample size conducted in Latin America was the PROVAR study in Belo Horizonte, Brazil.48 Between 2014 and 2017, 12,048 schoolchildren in Belo Horizonte, a state capital, were screened, revealing a 4% prevalence of borderline RHD (478 cases) and 0.5% prevalence of definite RHD (63 cases) by the 2012 WHF criteria (Table 1).48,50 The PROVAR study also performed echocardiographic screening in 1112 pregnant women.49 After performing a standard echocardiogram in 56 cases suspected of heart abnormality, 12 (about 1% of the total) had findings suggestive of RHD, of whom only 2 had a prior history of RHD (Table 1).49 The hemodynamic changes associated with pregnancy to match the demands of the fetus often decompensate previously asymptomatic RHD, leading to adverse maternal and fetal outcomes, such as preterm delivery and intrauterine growth restriction.51

A study conducted from 2006 to 2009 using echocardiographic screening of children and young adults in Nicaragua for RHD found that among the 3150 included children, 150 had RHD by the WHO criteria.45 Furthermore, using a specific criterion designed by the authors, among the 489 adult subjects, 11 (2.2%) had definite RHD (Table 1).45

A study in Arequipa in Peru screened 1023 schoolchildren from 20 schools and found that 20 children (1.9%) had echocardiographic findings consistent with RHD according to the WHO criteria, yielding a prevalence of 19.7 per 1000 children within this sample population.47 However, applying the 2012 WHF criteria to the same population, the number decreases to 4 children with RHD, resulting in a prevalence of 3.9 per 1000 (Table 1).47

A recent meta-analysis of the global prevalence of RHD was based solely on echocardiographic screening studies in individuals aged 5–20 years in endemic regions.21 In Latin America, the meta-analysis included three studies, and the pooled prevalence of RHD was 22 per 1000 (Table 2).21 A meta-regression of prevalence by screening year found no change, indicating no clear decrease in subclinical RHD from 2006 to 2024.21 Compared with ARF studies, the RHD studies we described are more specific, as echocardiography can be used as a diagnostic tool and screening provides a better representation of the true burden. Furthermore, a specific Latin America systematic review synthesized various echocardiographic screening strategies in Latin America and reinforced not only their cost-effectiveness and importance for timely diagnosis and implementation of secondary prevention, but also highlighted approaches to scale up screening in endemic areas.52 Among them, training non-experts to acquire imaging was shown to be feasible, and non-experts’ acquisition of a single parasternal long-axis view with a sweep of the heart reviewed by an off-site expert provided reasonable sensitivity and specificity for subclinical RHD.52,53

Given discrepancies between WHO and WHF criteria across studies worldwide and the limitations of the criteria used, a new classification has emerged. In 2023, the WHF criteria for diagnosing RHD were redefined.54 The experts elaborated four stages of the disease, creating a disease spectrum that is essential, given the heterogeneity in presentation and progression.54 Since the redefinition of the WHF criteria, no echocardiographic screening studies have been published in Latin America; however, the PROVAR study is still ongoing in Brazil.

Population at higher risk for ARF and RHD in Latin America

As previously described, RHD presents several stages where an intervention is possible, from the treatment of streptococcal pharyngitis to the management of ARF and the early detection of subclinical RHD by echocardiography.9,15 Because of this, certain groups face a higher risk of developing the disease, particularly those with limited access to timely healthcare.55,56 Barriers in diagnosis, treatment, and prevention create vulnerable populations that carry a disproportionate burden of RHD. In the following section, we describe these special populations in Latin America and the main factors that contribute to their increased susceptibility.

Indigenous and Quilombola communities: historical and contemporary vulnerabilities

The history of Latin America is deeply marked by the experiences of its Indigenous peoples, whose numbers were drastically reduced after European colonization through slavery, violence, and the spread of disease.57 Today, UNESCO estimates that around 58 million Indigenous individuals live in the region (about 10% of the population).58 In Brazil, more than 250 distinct groups speak over 160 languages, totaling nearly 1.7 million people.59 Quilombola communities—formed by descendants of enslaved Africans who resisted slavery and established settlements known as quilombo—remain culturally significant, with approximately 1.3 million individuals registered.60 Both populations share geographic isolation and limited access to healthcare, making them particularly vulnerable to diseases such as RHD.

Evidence from Australia illustrates how these inequities affect outcomes. A retrospective cohort study from 2013 to 2017 showed that mortality from RHD among Indigenous Australians was 14 times higher than in non-Indigenous populations.61 Another Australian study, reviewing 1425 ARF episodes between 2015 and 2017, found that nearly 89% of cases occurred in Indigenous children and adolescents.62 While hypotheses about genetic susceptibility or bacterial strain differences exist, such striking disparities are more convincingly explained by barriers to healthcare and differences in living conditions.

In Latin America, research is scarcer. A scoping review that included both Indigenous and Quilombola populations highlighted consistent patterns of health inequities, with greater risks of cardiovascular disease, malnutrition, and poor outcomes compared to Caucasian populations.63 These findings indicate that the higher prevalence of cardiovascular diseases in Indigenous and Quilombola groups is driven less by biology and more by structural inequities, minimal healthcare access, and adverse socioeconomic conditions.63,64

In 2020, a study was conducted in Brazilian Amazon using echocardiographic screening, where more than 4% of individuals auto-declare as indigenous, recruiting a random sample of 488 adults with a mean age of 40 years.65 In this study, two patients (0.4%) had borderline RHD findings, and 17 had definite RHD (3.5%); none of these patients had a prior diagnosis of RHD.65

Refugees and migrant populations in Latin America and the risk of RHD

The global refugee population has continued to rise, driven by armed conflicts, political persecution, climate change, lack of healthcare, and other crises. Displacement also occurs within national borders, as people move in search of better socioeconomic opportunities. In the Americas, UNHCR (United Nations High Commissioner for Refugees) reported 21.9 million forcibly displaced individuals by the end of 2024. In Brazil alone, 454,165 people applied for asylum between 2015 and 2024, with Venezuelans, Cubans, Haitians, and Angolans accounting for 82.6% of all requests.66

Refugees often face limited access to healthcare, poor registration systems, and precarious living conditions, all of which increase their vulnerability to preventable conditions, including malnutrition, tuberculosis, and cardiovascular disease.67, 68, 69 Illustrating this vulnerability, an echocardiographic screenings study among 653 unaccompanied foreign minors from various African countries residing in Italy, identified 17 cases of definite RHD (26 per 1000) and 122 borderline cases (187 per 1000), highlighting the disproportionate burden of RHD among refugee and migrant populations.70 In the recent Global systematic review and meta-analysis of the topic, this was the highest prevalence among studies.21

Socioeconomic disparities: rural and low-income communities

Large segments of the Latin American population live in rural areas or urban settlements such as favelas, where poor living conditions are common. In Brazil alone, over 25 million people reside in rural areas and more than 15 million in favelas, facing limited access to sanitation, overcrowding, and inadequate healthcare.60 These factors increase the risk of multiple diseases, including ARF and RHD. Evidence from the UK National Child Development Study (1958–1969), which followed 17,416 children, showed that environmental and social factors (such as large family size, recurrent infections, overcrowding, and close-contact settings) were strongly associated with childhood ARF.71 A review compiled studies that similarly identified risk factors such as damp housing, low maternal education, and restricted access to healthcare.56

RHD exerts its greatest impact in low- and middle-income countries, where inadequate diagnosis and treatment of streptococcal infections, limited secondary prophylaxis, and overcrowded living conditions persist. By contrast, high-income countries benefit from stronger health systems and improved socioeconomic conditions, leading to a marked decline in prevalence. For example, In Latin America, the overall prevalence, according to GBD estimates, was 738.8 per 100,000 women and 566.5 per 100,000 men in 2019, in sharp contrast to the 123.4 per 100,000 people reported in the United States in 2021.7,19 Nonetheless, disparities remain evident within countries: studies consistently show higher RHD rates among children in rural areas and low-income families in the same region.21,45

A retrospective multicenter study assessing ARF and RHD in the U.S. reveals that these conditions persist endemically despite the country's high-income status.10 By reviewing 947 pediatric cases across 22 institutions, the authors found substantial heterogeneity in disease burden in the U.S., with clusters of more severe disease occurring in communities with greater socioeconomic deprivation.10 Children living in these disadvantaged regions were significantly more likely to present with advanced valvular damage and to experience gaps in guideline-recommended care.10 Although 96% of patients were prescribed secondary prophylaxis, only 58% received benzathine penicillin G.10 This finding underscores the persistence of regional inequities even in developed countries.

Familial risk and genetic susceptibility

Genetic predisposition has long been recognized as a factor in ARF and RHD. In 2011, a systematic review and meta-analysis of twin studies, including 435 twin pairs found a pooled concordance risk of 44% in monozygotic twins compared with 12% in dizygotic twins (odds ratio 6.39), with an estimated heritability of 60%.72

Studies investigating HLA genes and susceptibility to RHD date back to the 1980s.73 While early candidate-gene approaches had methodological limitations, more recent genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have provided more substantial evidence for the genetic predisposition to RHD.74 One GWAS demonstrated that specific HLA haplotypes predispose individuals to RHD by binding streptococcal epitopes that trigger cross-reactive immune responses, findings that also suggest potential vaccine strategies based on non-cross-reactive epitopes.75

Beyond genetics, family members share environmental and socioeconomic conditions that further increase their risk as shown in a systematic review and meta-analysis of four echocardiographic screening studies (n = 776 relatives) conducted in Africa, South America, and Oceania.76 They reported a 7% prevalence of definite RHD among first-degree relatives, nearly double that of controls without a family history of RHD or ARF (relative risk 0.44).76

Female susceptibility and pregnancy-related challenges

Women are disproportionately affected by RHD. While ARF shows no clear sex predominance, chronic RHD consistently displays a higher prevalence among females, with at least a 2:1 ratio, and in advanced stages such as rheumatic mitral stenosis, studies report over 80% of cases occurring in women.29,77 Echocardiographic screening studies suggest a progressive gradient: an equal sex distribution in ARF, a modest female predominance in subclinical RHD, and a marked predominance in chronic disease.21,29,40

Several mechanisms may explain this pattern. Environmental exposure, such as women's closer involvement in child-rearing in many settings and thus higher risk of streptococcal infection, may contribute.78 Biological predisposition is also relevant, given the greater susceptibility of females to autoimmune conditions.79 On a molecular level, a 2022 proteomic study identified increased prothymosin-alpha in RHD-affected valves, a protein involved in antigen presentation, CD8 activation, and estrogen receptor alpha signaling, supporting a role for sex-related immune modulation in RHD progression.80

RHD also represents the most common acquired valvular disease during pregnancy, especially in low- and middle-income countries where prevalence remains high.51 Pregnancy poses particular challenges due to increased hemodynamic demands, particularly in women with rheumatic mitral stenosis. In this setting of RHD is associated with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes, including heart failure, preterm delivery, and intrauterine growth restriction.51 Management of advanced RHD in pregnancy is challenging and often requires multidisciplinary care and, in selected cases, valvular intervention during pregnancy.

Practical actions on preventing and controlling RHD

In high-risk communities for ARF and RHD, prevention must rely on structured, proactive strategies rather than symptom-based care. Primary prevention should prioritize early diagnosis and treatment of all group A streptococcus infections, including pharyngitis and, in tropical settings, pyoderma (Fig. 2).81, 82, 83 Public health systems need to ensure same-day assessment, strep testing, and access to antibiotics while integrating school-based and community programs to reach the most vulnerable populations.81,84 These measures are strongly supported by historical penicillin-era trials demonstrating that prompt antibiotic therapy substantially reduces first-episode ARF, and by subsequent studies showing that nearly one-third of cases occur after subclinical infections.15,85,86 Sustained prevention depends on community-embedded programs with verified allergy testing, adherence reinforcement, and reliable antibiotic supply chains.14,81 It is also important to emphasize that the indication for patients with RHD and atrial fibrillation is the usage of a Vitamin K antagonist instead of rivaroxaban, which led to fewer cardiovascular events and deaths.87

For individuals with prior ARF or established RHD, continuous antibiotic prophylaxis remains the cornerstone of disease control. Long-acting intramuscular benzathine penicillin G administered every 28 days is the preferred regimen, supported by decades of observational data, randomized trials, and recent studies showing reduced recurrence and stabilization of early, subclinical RHD (echo-detected).15,88,89 In very high-incidence settings or when breakthrough infections occur, a 21-day dosing interval may be indicated.90 Oral penicillin V is acceptable when injections are impractical, but require strict adherence monitoring due to a higher recurrence risk.89 The GOALIE trial is comparing the efficacy of intramuscular to oral penicillin to prevent progression of mild RHD at 2 years.91 The duration of prophylaxis should be individualized by age, recurrence risk, and severity, typically extending through young adulthood for those with carditis, within national, register-based prevention programs.

Progression of subclinical RHD and management

The natural history of subclinical RHD is heterogeneous. Longitudinal studies have shown that echocardiographic findings may regress, remain stable, or progress over time.12,92 The GOAL study demonstrated that secondary antibiotic prophylaxis in early-stage RHD reduced disease progression at 2 years.15 However, it also highlighted the dynamic nature of early disease, with spontaneous regression observed in approximately 50% of borderline and 30% of mild cases, even in the absence of treatment.15

This heterogeneity underscores the diagnostic and management challenges, particularly in distinguishing early pathological changes from normal variants in high-risk populations. Individuals may transition between these states over time, contributing to uncertainty regarding progression and treatment thresholds.

In accordance with the 2023 WHF recommendations, management of subclinical RHD is stage-specific.54 Individuals with minimal echocardiographic findings at potential risk of progression should be enrolled in registries to ensure structured longitudinal follow-up, with periodic reassessment and consideration of secondary antibiotic prophylaxis based on clinical and echocardiographic evolution.54 In patients with mild RHD, secondary antibiotic prophylaxis is generally recommended. However, the optimal duration of therapy remains uncertain.

Conclusion

Rheumatic fever and rheumatic heart disease remain important and unevenly distributed health conditions across Latin America, reflecting persistent socioeconomic inequities and healthcare access disparities. Although advances in echocardiographic screening have improved detection, particularly of subclinical disease, significant gaps remain in epidemiologic data, accurate estimation of the true burden of acute rheumatic fever, prevention strategies, and access to care. Addressing these challenges will require better-quality regional data and targeted public health approaches focused on vulnerable populations.
