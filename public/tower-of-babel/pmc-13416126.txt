Characterization of resistance to rice root-knot nematode Meloidogyne graminicola in an indica rice land race Aganni: insights on pre and post infection defence responses
Authors: Nethi Somasekhar, Satish Namdeo Chavan, D Sanjeeva Rao, P Senguttuvel, S V Sai Prasad, Raman Meenakshi Sundaram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rice root-knot nematode Meloidogyne graminicola is one of the important biotic constraints limiting rice production in many rice growing areas of the world, especially in aerobic and direct seeded conditions. Controlling M. graminicola using traditional management methods is challenging. Identification of rice genotypes resistant to M. graminicola is crucial for developing effective and sustainable nematode management strategies. In the present study, twenty-five indica rice genotypes were evaluated for their resistance against M. graminicola. Among them, one genotype was found resistant, two as moderately resistant, while the remaining were either susceptible or highly susceptible based on relative root-galls index. The rice genotype Aganni exhibited the highest level of resistance, with a relative root gall index ranging from 0.80 to 0.85. The consistency of resistance in Aganni was further validated across multiple seasons and under different experimental setups. Root attraction assays with the resistant genotype Aganni and the susceptible cultivar TN1 revealed reduced nematode attraction to Aganni roots, indicating a pre-infection resistance response. In addition, the comparative biological studies revealed that the nematode penetration, development, and reproduction were significantly suppressed in Aganni compared to the susceptible cultivar TN1, demonstrating the operation of both pre- and post-infection resistance mechanisms. Significantly higher activities of antioxidant defence enzymes such as ascorbate peroxidase, catalase, and superoxide dismutase were observed in the roots of Aganni plants upon M. graminicola infection, indicating their role in plant defence. The resistant rice genotype Aganni identified in this study therefore represents a valuable genetic resource for breeding rice cultivars resistant to M. graminicola.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53100-7.Keywords: Rice, Root-knot nematode, Meloidogyne graminicola, Resistance, Defence mechanismsSubject terms: Biotechnology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Rice is a major cereal crop and serves as the primary staple food for more than half of the global population, especially across Asia and Africa. In Asia alone, it meets the energy requirements of nearly 2 billion people. Beyond being a staple food, rice is also a vital grain contributing essential dietary components such as fibre, energy, minerals, vitamins, and other biomolecules1. India is the largest producer of rice in the world, with a production of 146.6 million tonnes in 2024–25, surpassing China’s 142.2 million tonnes2. It contributes approximately one-fourth of the total global rice production. Rice is the backbone of the economy for many nations. It is primarily cultivated under flooded conditions using the traditional transplanting method3. However, due to climate change, as well as increasing water and labour shortages, it is becoming increasingly unsustainable to continue rice cultivation in the conventional manner. This situation has given impetus to the adoption of water-saving rice production systems such as direct seeded rice (DSR), aerobic rice, Alternate Wetting and drying, and the System of Rice Intensification4. However, such changes in rice cultivation practices are leading to the emergence of several biotic stresses, including weeds and plant-parasitic nematodes.

The rice root-knot nematode, Meloidogyne graminicola, is one of the most important plant-parasitic nematodes (PPNs) occurring across all types of rice ecosystems, such as upland, irrigated, and deep-water rice5–8. Its significance is increasing with the expansion of direct seeded rice and aerobic rice cultivation8–10. Although both these water-saving rice production systems are environmentally friendly and require less water, they are more susceptible to infestation by M. graminicola11–13. Although M. graminicola has a broad host range, it predominantly infects rice5. Several economically important crops commonly grown in rice-based cropping systems, such as wheat, finger millet, and tomato, can also support its reproduction. This nematode is a sedentary endoparasite that primarily invades the root tips of rice plants. Its infection triggers the formation of characteristic hook-shaped galls, particularly at the root tips, which severely disrupt root development and function. Infected rice plants exhibit various above-ground symptoms, including stunted growth, reduced vigour, and chlorotic or curled leaves, both in nurseries and in the main field. On upland rice, it is known to reduce grain yield by 16–32% due to incomplete grain filling5. In severe cases, M. graminicola infestation can lead to yield losses of up to 87%.

Farmers largely depend on conventional methods for managing nematodes, including clean cultivation, crop rotation with non-host crops, organic amendments, and the use of chemical nematicides5,6,14. Integrated nematode management using biocontrol agents is also deployed to manage nematode problems in many crops15–19. However, managing M. graminicola in rice remains a significant challenge due to its short life cycle, wide host range, adaptability to diverse environmental conditions, and the limited availability of effective nematicides. Traditional cultural practices are often insufficient, acting slowly and failing to provide consistent control. The lack of reliable chemical options further exacerbates the difficulty in managing this nematode effectively.

The use of resistant rice cultivars is widely regarded as one of the most cost-effective and eco-friendly approaches for managing M. graminicola14,20,21. Nevertheless, the limited availability of strong resistance sources, particularly within indica rice varieties, poses a significant challenge. Extensive screenings carried out in India have revealed that most rice cultivars are highly susceptible to the nematode, with only a small number demonstrating moderate resistance or tolerance22–25. In a recent breakthrough, a major resistance gene Mg1 against M. graminicola was identified and characterised in a japonica rice cultivar in China, highlighting the promise of resistance-based breeding strategies26. Studying resistance mechanisms allows efficient use of resistant genotypes in integrated nematode management programs and for breeding nematode resistant rice cultivars. Reports of strong and reliable nematode resistance in rice cultivars are scarce, particularly for indica rice, which is widely grown in India and other Asian countries.

Hence, the present investigation was undertaken to screen indica rice germplasm against the rice root-knot nematode M. graminicola to identify potential sources of resistance. Among the screened genotypes, the highly resistant rice genotype ‘Aganni’ was selected for further studies to investigate the mechanisms of nematode suppression against M. graminicola.

Material and method

Screening for nematode resistance

Plant material and growth conditions

Seeds of the rice genotypes were obtained from the Crop Improvement Section of the ICAR-Indian Institute of Rice Research (ICAR-IIRR), Hyderabad. Twenty-five rice genotypes were screened using the Sap-tube assembly platform27–29 (Supplementary material Fig. S1) during the Kharif season (July–October 2023). Rice seeds were pre-soaked in water in Petri dishes and incubated for three days at room temperature on moist germination paper maintained at 27 °C in a BOD incubator. After germination, the sprouted seeds were transferred to SAP tubes (polyvinyl chloride [PVC] tubes, 3 cm in diameter and 20 cm in length) containing a SAP mixture substrate (sand mixed with absorbent polymer), as described by Reversat et al.30, with five replications for each rice genotype. Plants were irrigated three times a week with 10 mL of Hoagland’s solution31 each time. To confirm the results, the screening experiment in the Sap-tube assembly was repeated during the Rabi season (January–April 2024).

Nematode culture and inoculation to the plants

The M. graminicola culture used in this study was obtained from a pure culture of M. graminicola (isolate Drr-Mg1; GenBank: JF949754.1) maintained on the susceptible rice cultivar TN1 in the glasshouse of the Nematology Section, ICAR-IIRR, Hyderabad. The roots of infected TN1 plants were washed free of soil, and galled roots were gently teased apart using needles under a stereo-zoom microscope, then placed on a modified Baermann funnel setup for the extraction of Second stage juveniles (J2s)32. The J2s are the only infective stage of this nematode. Freshly hatched J2s were used for inoculation.

Rice plants grown in the sap-tube assembly platform were inoculated with 100 J2s per plant, one week after planting the sprouted seeds into the Sap-tubes. Observations on galls were recorded two weeks post-inoculation under a stereo microscope using the acid fuchsin staining technique33.

Pluronic plate assay

The rice genotype Aganni which was found resistant to the nematode in sap-tube assembly experiment was additionally evaluated in the Pluronic plate assay (Pluronic F-127; Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. P2443)35 in the laboratory to reconfirm the resistance (Supplementary material Fig. S2). Rice genotype TN1 was used as susceptible check. Rice seeds were surface-sterilized by treating them with 70% ethanol for 3 min, followed by immersion in 4% NaOCl solution for 20 min. The sterilized seeds were then kept in the dark at 27 °C in Petri plates containing moist germination paper for two days to germinate. Sprouted seeds were transferred onto Pluronic plates, with each 90 mm × 15 mm cell culture dish (Torsons, Cat No. 460092) containing 30 ml of 23% Pluronic gel and six sprouted seeds. Plants were inoculated two days after placing the sprouted seeds in the plates and were inoculated with M. graminicola at a rate of 100 J2s per plant. The nematodes were applied near the root tips using small droplets of water delivered with a micropipette. Observations on galls were recorded two-week post nematode inoculation under stereo microscope using the acid fuchsin staining technique33.

Large-pot experiments

The resistant rice genotype Aganni was further evaluated in large plastic pots (3 kg volume) during July–October 2024 for assessing the reaction of the genotype when exposed to the multiple generations of nematode over a period of three months (Supplementary material Fig. S3). Three parts of red soil were mixed with one part of well-decomposed compost. The pots and soil-compost mixture used for the experiment were autoclaved before use. Sprouted seeds were sown into these pots at three seeds per pot. Three pots per genotype were used, with a total of nine plants per genotype for the pot study. Plants were inoculated with the M. graminicola at a rate 6,000 J2s per pot one-week post transplanting. Observations on galls were recorded three months after inoculation under a stereo-zoom microscope using the acid fuchsin staining technique33.

Comparative biology of M. graminicola in resistant and susceptible rice cultivars

The comparative biology of M. graminicola in the resistant rice genotype Aganni and the susceptible cultivar TN1 was studied during January to April 2025.

Root attraction assay

The pluronic gel plates were prepared as described above. Two-day-old germinated seeds were placed on Pluronic plates, which were oriented vertically to promote uniform, straight, downward root growth suitable for nematode attraction studies (Supplementary material Fig. S4). Seedlings possessing a single root of 4–5 cm length were selected for the experiment. Each Petri plate contained two seedlings, and three replicates per cultivar were maintained for each time point. Plates were inoculated with M. graminicola at a rate of 100 J2s per plant, applied at a distance of 2 cm from the root tip.

The number of nematodes in contact with the root tips was recorded under a stereo microscope at 4, 8, 12, and 24 h post inoculation (hpi) to evaluate the degree of nematode attraction or repulsion. This approach enabled a comparative assessment of the behavioural response of M. graminicola towards the roots of susceptible rice cultivar TN1 and the resistant genotype Aganni.

Nematode penetration, development and reproduction

To further assess the resistance to M. graminicola, rice plants were grown in sap tubes as described above. One-week-old seedlings were inoculated with 100 J2s per plant. Observations on gall formation and nematode developmental stages were recorded at 3, 7, 12, 14, and 18 days post-inoculation (dpi) using the acid fuchsin staining technique33 under a stereo microscope. Eight replications were maintained for each treatment at each time interval.

Biochemical assays

Plants of the selected resistant (Aganni) and susceptible (TN1) rice cultivars were grown in SAP tubes under glasshouse conditions as described above. One-week-old seedlings were inoculated with 1 mL of nematode suspension containing 100 J2s. Two treatments i.e. nematode inoculated and uninoculated (control), were maintained for both resistant and susceptible rice cultivars (total four treatments) and each treatment was replicated five times. Plant roots were collected at 1 and 3 days post-inoculation (dpi), snap freezed and stored at -80 till further analysis. For all enzyme assays, 0.1 g of plant tissue was homogenized using a tissue lyser (QIAGEN, Germany) in 1 mL of extraction buffer consisting of 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.1 mM EDTA, 5 mM β-mercaptoethanol, 2% PVP-40, and 1 mM PMSF. The homogenate was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected and used for catalase activity estimation. The extraction buffer for SOD and APX assays was supplemented with 5 mM ascorbic acid.

Ascorbate peroxidase (APX) activity was estimated spectrophotometrically by measuring the decrease in absorbance at 290 nm as described by Nakano and Asada36. A 3 mL reaction mixture containing 50 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.5 mM ascorbic acid, 0.1 mM EDTA, and 0.1 mL enzyme extract was prepared. The reaction was initiated by adding 30 µL of 200 mM H2O2, and absorbance was recorded immediately (30 s reading) and after 5 min (5 min 30 s reading). H2O2 decomposition was calculated from the absorbance drop over 1 min, and APX activity was expressed as µmol ascorbate oxidized per gram fresh weight per minute.

Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined spectrophotometrically by measuring the inhibition of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) reduction at 560 nm, as described by Prochazkova et al.37. The 3 mL reaction mixture included 13 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, 0.1 mM EDTA, 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 0.1 mL enzyme extract, and 0.1 mL of 1 mM riboflavin. Samples were exposed to light for 15 min, and absorbance was recorded. A reaction without enzyme served as the control, and a non-irradiated mixture as the blank. Absorbance was recorded at 560 nm. One unit of SOD activity was defined as the amount of enzyme causing 50% inhibition of NBT reduction.

Catalase activity was determined spectrophotometrically by monitoring the decrease in absorbance of H2O2 at 240 nm, as per the method described by Velikova et al.38. A 3 mL reaction mixture containing 10 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 0.1 mL enzyme extract was prepared. The reaction was started by adding 40 µL of 3% H2O2, and absorbance was recorded immediately (30 s reading) and after 5 min (5 min 30 s reading). H2O2 decomposition was calculated from the absorbance drop over 1 min, and catalase activity was expressed as µmol H2O2 reduced per gram fresh weight per minute.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out using SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data were analysed by Student’s t-test and Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) wherever applicable, as specified in the respective figure and table legends. Prior to analysis, the data were examined for compliance with the assumptions of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, which were found to be satisfactory. Differences between two treatments (resistant and susceptible cultivars) at individual time points were assessed using Student’s t-test, and significance levels are denoted by asterisks (p < 0.05, *p < 0.01, **p < 0.001). For multiple comparisons, DMRT was applied at P = 0.05, and treatments sharing different letters are considered significantly different.

Results

Screening of rice genotypes against M. graminicola in sap tubes

Screening of twenty-five rice genotypes conducted during Kharif (July–November 2023) against M. graminicola using the SAP tube assay revealed that, out of the twenty-five genotypes, only one was resistant, three were moderately resistant, and the remaining genotypes ranged from susceptible to highly susceptible (Table 2). The experiment repeated during Rabi (January–April 2024) also yielded similar results (Table 2). Among all the evaluated genotypes, the rice genotype Aganni was observed to be the most resistant with gall index values of 0.80 and 0.83 in the two experiments (Table 2, Fig. 1).

Evaluation of the resistance in rice genotype Aganni in pluronic gel and in pot culture assay

The resistance reaction of the rice genotype Aganni was further evaluated using the Pluronic plate assay (Fig. 2) and large pot experiments (Fig. 3). In the Pluronic plate assay, plants of the susceptible check TN1 showed an average of 10 galls per plant, whereas Aganni exhibited an average of 2.1 galls per plant with a gall index of 0.86 (R). Similarly, in the pot experiment, where plants were allowed to grow for three months post nematode inoculation, TN1 showed an average of 103 galls per plant, while Aganni recorded an average of only 9 galls per plant, exhibiting a consistent resistant (R) reaction with a gall index of 0.36. These results demonstrate that resistance in the rice genotype Aganni is consistent and robust, and is in line with the findings from the initial screening assay (Table 2).

Comparative biology of M. graminicola in resistant and susceptible rice cultivars

Root attraction assay

The rice root-knot nematode M. graminicola preferentially infects rice root tips5,7. At early observation times (4 and 8 hpi), significantly fewer J2s accumulated near the root tips of the resistant cultivar Aganni, whereas greater accumulation of J2s was observed around the root tips of the susceptible cultivar TN1 (Fig. 4, 5). These results indicate delayed movement of J2s towards roots of Aganni at earlier time points of 4 and 8 h. By 12 hpi, the number of J2s in proximity to TN1 roots declined sharply, most likely due to rapid penetration of the nematodes into the root tissues. In contrast, J2 numbers around Aganni roots remained relatively stable at this stage, suggesting delayed entry and/or the activation of early defense mechanisms that restrict nematode ingress (Fig. 4, 5). At 24 hpi, J2 abundance near TN1 roots decreased further, indicating continued invasion over time. Conversely, Aganni still have stable nematode count near the root surface, implying that nematodes were either unable to penetrate effectively or remained attached without successful infection. Notably, a small gall was visible at the root tip of TN1 at 24 hpi (Fig. 5), confirming early feeding site establishment following nematode entry. Such gall formation or root tip swelling was not observed in Aganni at the same time point (Fig. 5). The hook-shaped terminal gall, a characteristic feature of M. graminicola infection in rice, was clearly visible at 36 h post-nematode inoculation, whereas complete gall formation was still not observed in the roots of Aganni at this time point (Fig. 5).

Nematode penetration, development and reproduction

The total number of nematodes within the roots was significantly lower in the resistant genotype Aganni compared to the susceptible cultivar TN1 at all observation time points (Table 3, Fig. 6). At 3 and 7 dpi, the number of nematodes that entered the roots was 24.50 and 18.00 in Aganni, respectively, whereas substantially higher numbers (56.00 and 54.83) were recorded in TN1 at the corresponding time points. The consistently lower nematode population in Aganni indicates that resistance in this genotype operates starting from the entry stage.

In the susceptible cultivar TN1, the majority of J2s progressed to J3 and J4 stages by 7 dpi and further developed into females by 12 and 14 dpi. In contrast, in the resistant genotype Aganni, most nematodes remained at the J2 stage at 7 dpi, reached the J3/J4 stages at 14 dpi, and persisted at these stages even at 18 dpi (Table 3, Fig. 6). The presence of egg-laying females was first detected in TN1 at 12 dpi, whereas no egg-laying females were observed in Aganni at this stage. Egg-laying females appeared in Aganni only at 14 dpi, and by 18 dpi, only a small proportion of nematodes (12.66%) developed into egg-laying females, compared with 75.54% in TN1 at the same time point. Collectively, these findings suggest that Aganni negatively affects nematode development and reproduction and delays completion of the nematode life cycle.

Biochemical assays

No significant differences in the constitutive levels of these antioxidants were observed between the susceptible (TN1) and resistant (Aganni) rice genotypes under non-infected conditions at both the time points of 1 and 3 dpi. However, upon nematode infection, antioxidant activities increased in both genotypes compared to the non-inoculated controls (Fig. 7). Notably, the nematode inoculated resistant genotype Aganni showed strong and robust APX and SOD activities compared to the nematode inoculated TN1 plants at 1 dpi (Fig. 7). Similarly, a significantly higher catalase and SOD activities were observed in the roots of nematode inoculated Aganni plants compared to the nematode inoculated TN1 plants. These results indicate the role of APX, catalase and SOD in the plant defence in rice against M. graminicola.

Discussion

Identification of robust resistance genotypes and understanding the mechanisms of resistance are crucial for developing nematode-resistant rice cultivars. In this study, twenty-five rice genotypes were screened against the rice root-knot nematode M. graminicola. The resistance of the selected rice genotype was confirmed after rigorous evaluation across four seasons (2023–2024 to 2024–2025) and using three different screening methods: SAP tube assay, Pluronic plate assay, and large pot experiments. A comparative study on the biology of M. graminicola was conducted using the resistant genotype Aganni and the susceptible genotype TN1, focusing on the pre- and post-penetration responses of these genotypes to M. graminicola.

Getting true and robust resistance in cultivated rice is challenging and relatively rare22–25,39–42. In our screening experiments, only one genotype (Aganni) was found to be resistant among the twenty-five rice genotypes evaluated (Table 2). Srivastava et al.22 reported that only 2 out of 87 rice cultivars were resistant based on gall numbers. Mishra et al.43 evaluated 101 rice cultivars but did not identify any resistant genotypes. Similarly, Berliner et al.39, who screened 414 rice cultivars using the root gall index with TN1 as the susceptible control, identified only two resistant lines showing less than 10% galling. Similar observations were reported by Berliner et al.25 and Subudhi et al.42, further demonstrating the rarity of resistance in cultivated rice. Some genotypes can exhibit resistance to multiple insect pests and diseases. In addition to M. graminicola, rice genotype Aganni is also resistant to the rice insect pest gall midge Orseolia oryzae44.

Consistency of resistance in a genotype is crucial for its effective utilization in nematode resistance breeding programmes. To assess the stability and robustness of resistance in the selected genotype Aganni was evaluated across four different seasons through four independent experiments, using TN1 as the susceptible check. Aganni was also tested under three different evaluation setups to further validate its consistency of response to M. graminicola. Across all experiments, genotype Aganni consistently exhibited a resistant (R) reaction (Tables 2, 3; Figs. 1, 2, 3). Dimkpa et al.45 identified Khao Pahk Maw and LD 24 as robust rice genotypes resistant to M. graminicola. These genotypes were initially screened using small plug trays, and their resistance was subsequently confirmed in large pot experiments. The resistance observed in both genotypes was consistent and reproducible across different experimental conditions, including variations in substrate, pot size, plant age, and laboratory settings.

Resistant plants counteract infecting nematodes through multiple mechanisms including the pre and post infection defence responses46,47. In our study, we investigated for such responses in the selected resistant genotype Aganni against M. graminicola. A significantly less number of nematodes were attracted towards the roots of Aganni compared to the susceptible TN1 (Fig. 4, 5) indicating that the roots of Aganni are less attractive or repulsive to the M. graminicola. The reduced nematode attraction observed in Aganni is might be due to the release of inhibitory or repellent compounds from the root system. It is well established that rice roots exude a diverse array of chemical compounds capable of influencing nematode behavior, including orientation, movement, host recognition, and survival48–50. Recent studies have demonstrated that ethylene and diterpenoid phytoalexins in rice contribute to defence against plant-parasitic nematodes and can influence nematode infection dynamics in rice51–53. Similarly, Dash et al.54 showed that resistance to M. graminicola in a mutant rice line was associated with elevated expression of genes involved in diterpenoid phytoalexin biosynthesis. These findings underscore the importance of root-derived metabolites in mediating nematode deterrence in Aganni and warrant further investigation in future studies.

Even when parasitic nematodes successfully locate and reach the host, resistant plants can still restrict them at the stages of penetration, migration, establishment, and/or development. In our study, significantly fewer nematodes were recorded in the resistant genotype Aganni than in the susceptible cultivar TN1 at all observation time points (Table 3). The significantly lower nematode numbers in Aganni roots at 3 dpi indicate that resistance is already effective at the penetration stage. In addition, Aganni showed an adverse effect on nematode development and reproduction when compared with TN1 (Table 3). Taken together, these findings suggest that the resistant genotype Aganni aslo exhibits a post-infection resistance mechanism that limit M. graminicola penetration into the roots, suppresse the nematode development and reproduction, and delay the completion of the nematode life cycle relative to the susceptible cultivar TN1 (Table 3). While evaluating resistance mechanisms in the resistant rice cultivar Zhonghua 11 (ZH11), Phan et al.55 reported high levels of J2 penetration in both ZH11 and the susceptible check IR64 during the early stages of infection. However, the number of penetrated J2s declined rapidly in ZH11 over subsequent days, indicating that resistance in ZH11 is expressed at the post-penetration stage. In a comparative study on the biology of M. graminicola in the resistant rice genotype Huaidao 5 and the susceptible genotype Nanjing 9108, Feng et al.56 observed no significant differences in J2 attraction to the root tips of NJ9108 and Huaidao 5 at 1, 2, 4, 6, and 8 hpi using a Pluronic gel assay. However, although J2s successfully entered the roots by 12 hpi, their subsequent movement toward the root tips was restricted in Huaidao 5, leading to significantly fewer nematodes within the roots compared with Nanjing 9108. These findings suggest that resistance in Huaidao 5 operates primarily at the post-infection stage. In contrast to this, resistance in genotype Aganni is found to be operative at both pre- and post-infection stages. The observed differences in the timing and nature of defense responses among these rice genotypes may be attributed to their inherent genetic and physiological characteristics.

Biochemical responses in plants play a vital role against plant parasitic nematodes27,46,57,58. Nematode infection in plants triggers an oxidative burst, leading to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) such as superoxide and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). To counter this, plants activate antioxidant enzymes like peroxidases, catalases and superoxide dismutase, which help detoxify ROS. Production of these enzymes activates the overall defence machinery in the plants58. Our results showed significantly higher activity of antioxidant enzymes such as APX, catalase, and SOD in the roots of nematode infected resistant genotype Aganni compared to the roots of nematode infected susceptible TN1 plants (Fig. 7), suggesting their role in plant defence. Increases in antioxidant activities in resistant rice genotypes upon M. graminicola infection have been previously reported by several authors59–62. To determine the precise roles of these biochemical responses, in-depth whole-genome RNA-seq and metabolomic analyses of infected and non-infected root samples from both resistant and susceptible cultivars will be required. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in diverse rice genotypes, including wild rice, have led to the identification of significant SNPs associated with resistance to M. graminicola45,63. Wang et al.26 identified and cloned Meloidogyne graminicola-resistance gene 1 (MG1), a major R gene conferring resistance to the nematode in the rice variety ZH11, and further reported a putative protease inhibitor that directly interacts with MG1 during MG1-mediated resistance. Similarly, the identification of resistance genes/QTLs in Aganni against M. graminicola in the future may help unravel the genetic basis of resistance and facilitate the development of resistant cultivars.

Conclusion

The rice root-knot nematode M. graminicola is an important plant-parasitic nematode causing significant yield losses across diverse rice-growing ecologies in many rice-producing countries. Breeding nematode-resistant rice cultivars is an ecologically sound and economically viable approach for the effective management of this hidden enemy to rice production. In the present study, we identified Aganni as a strong and robust rice genotype resistant to M. graminicola. The consistency of resistance in Aganni was evaluated across multiple seasons and under different experimental setups. Through multiple experiments, we demonstrated that resistance in Aganni operates at both pre- and post-infection stages, confirming Aganni as a reliable source of resistance against M. graminicola. We also demonstrate the enhanced antioxidant defence enzymes in the roots of Aganni upon nematode infection. The identified genotype Aganni holds substantial potential for use in breeding programs aimed at developing nematode-resistant rice cultivars. Furthermore, the identification of resistance-associated genes or quantitative trait loci (QTLs) and elucidation of the molecular mechanisms underlying resistance in Aganni will be crucial for its effective deployment in resistance breeding programs.
