Recovery from work and physical activity among Finnish higher education personnel – an online survey study
Authors: Sari Soutukorva, Outi Kähkönen, Ari Voutilainen, Sara Suikkanen, Terhi Saaranen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecovery from work is precious for higher education personnel due to workload pressures, psychosocial distress, and burnout risk. Research is needed on how they recover from their work and whether they utilize physical activity as a recovery activity. This study aimed to explore recovery from work, physical activity, and active breaks among higher education personnel.MethodsThe participants were Finnish higher education personnel members who belonged to a professional association (N = 1578). Data was collected between 1–2/2025 with an online survey, comprising questions of background information, recovery from work (the Need for Recovery, NfR), and physical activity (Global Physical Activity Questionnaire, GPAQ). Descriptive statistics, crosstabulation, nonparametric tests, and linear regression were used.ResultsThe response rate was 15% (n = 229). The respondents recovered moderately, with a median NfR of 3.1 on a scale of 1–5. On average, they had vigorous leisure-time physical activity (LTPA) 3 days per week and moderate LTPA 4 days per week, while 88% reported no occupational physical activity (OPA). Persons who were physically active during breaks had a lower NfR (median 2.9 vs. 3.2), as well as those with 3–4 vigorous LTPA days per week (median NfR 2.7), than persons not engaging in vigorous LTPA (3.2). However, in the linear regression model adjusted for age, gender, sedentary time, active commuting, and physical activity and sports as a hobby, the associations of vigorous LTPA and active breaks with NfR were not statistically significant. Only age explained NfR.ConclusionsThe findings of this study confirm differences in NfR by physical activity during breaks and LTPA. Adjusted models did not verify direct associations of NfR with active breaks and LTPA. Only age explained NfR in the adjusted linear regression model.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-26104-7.Keywords: Occupational well-being, Physical activity, Recovery from work, Higher education, Survey study

Introduction

Occupational well-being is a wide-ranging phenomenon that should consider all aspects of working life [1]. Promoting occupational well-being requires identifying and balancing work-related resources and workload factors. Occupational well-being promotion should happen in collaboration with the employer, employees, and occupational health care [2, 3].

Concerning higher education personnel’s occupational well-being, work-related resources and workload factors have been identified [4–6]. For example, higher education personnel’s job resources are associated with work engagement [4, 6] and job satisfaction, whereas job demands are related to experienced stress [6] and burnout [4]. Higher education personnel have work-related resources, such as collegial and organizational support and opportunities for creativity and growth, whereas workload pressures, a lack of belonging [5], psychosocial distress, and burnout risk have been identified as workload factors [7].

Recovery from work is important in an intensified working life [8]. Due to workload factors [5, 7], recovery from work is precious for higher education personnel [9, 10]. By definition, recovery from work involves unwinding and restoration processes during which an employee’s increased strain level returns to the level before a reaction to a stressor [11, 12]. The return of strain levels depends on specific recovery activities (such as physical activity) and recovery experiences (such as psychological detachment from work) [11]. Recovery from work can be measured objectively utilizing physiological measures, such as heart rate variability (HRV) [11, 13, 14], and subjectively utilizing questionnaires, such as the need for recovery (NfR) [15, 16]. Research evidence is lacking on how higher education personnel recover from their work.

Leisure-time physical activity (LTPA) can be one way to promote occupational well-being [17, 18], mental health [19–24], and recovery from work [11, 13, 25–27]. Physical activity is any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that results in energy expenditure [28]. High LTPA is associated with lowered stress [13], decreased need for recovery [26], and enhanced physical fitness [13]. In addition to LTPA, activities during work, such as stair climbing, active breaks [26], and park walks during the lunch break [29], can promote recovery from work. Physical activity as a break during the working day has many positive effects, such as reducing sedentary behavior [18] and fatigue in the afternoon [30], as well as increasing decision-making ability [18] and concentration [18, 30].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), adults aged 18‒64 should have regular physical activity on several days per week. They need at least 150–300 min of moderate aerobic physical activity, at least 75–150 min of vigorous aerobic physical activity, or a combination of moderate and vigorous aerobic physical activity in a week. In addition, strength training on at least two days a week is recommended [31]. Achieving physical activity recommendations can be measured objectively, such as by activity monitors and pedometers, accelerometers, and combined sensors [32, 33], and subjectively, such as using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire IPAQ [34] and the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire GPAQ [35].

Contradictory estimates of physical activity levels among higher education personnel have been presented [36–38]. Only half of the Californian university personnel met the weekly physical activity recommendations [37], and most of the Indonesian university lecturers had a low level of physical activity [36]. In contrast, university personnel have also been found to achieve the physical activity recommendations in the United Kingdom [38]. More research is needed on the physical activity level of higher education personnel and whether they utilize physical activity as a recovery activity. This study aims to investigate: (1) How do the Finnish higher education personnel recover from work?, (2) How physically active are the Finnish higher education personnel?, and (3) Are physical activity and active breaks associated with recovery from work?

Methods

The aim, participants, and procedure

This study aimed to explore recovery from work, physical activity, and active breaks among Finnish higher education personnel. The data was collected with a web-based survey, using Webropol Survey and Reporting Platform [39]. The survey was conducted between January 16 and February 16, 2025, among Finnish higher education (specifically universities of applied sciences, UAS) personnel members who belonged to the professional association [40]. The professional association granted permission for the survey (November 3, 2024) and sent the survey instructions and a link to its members (N = 1578). In the sample size calculation, an open population was expected. Then, the target sample size was calculated with Survey Monkey [41], with a confidence level of 95% and a margin of error of 5%, resulting in 310 respondents. The professional association sent two reminder messages at the end of the second and third survey weeks. Responding to the survey was voluntary. The survey was anonymous and in Finnish.

Before answering the survey, respondents were able to read information about the purpose of the survey and how the data would be handled. At the beginning of the survey, the respondents provided written informed consent to participate in the study. Because the study did not involve intervening in the physical integrity of participants, ethics approval was not required according to Finnish legislation [42] and the University of Eastern Finland (UEF) Research Ethics Committee. Based on the committee’s statement (Ref. no 21/2024, August 16, 2024), the study does not require formal ethics approval as there are no interventions interfering with physical integrity. This study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki [43].

Measures

The survey comprised four sections: background information, recovery from work, physical activity, and recovery from work through physical activity. This study included the first three sections. Background information included gender, age, work experience in total and higher education, professional title, the highest degree and its discipline, the location of the main place of work [44], main working unit, work tasks (i.e., teaching, RDI - research, development, and innovation work, and/or other work tasks), place of work (i.e., in the workplace, in the field, and/or remotely), and hobbies (i.e., recreational games, animals, languages, literature and literary art, fine arts and media, handicrafts, physical activity and sports, nature, music, food and beverages, theatre, dance, and circus, science and technology, as well as communality and helping) [45].

The recovery from work section included the main outcome measure, the Finnish version [46] of the Need for Recovery (NfR) [16, 47]. It consists of eleven statements related to recovery, including challenges in relaxing, feelings of exhaustion, tiredness, and being worn out, reduced concentration and interest in social interactions, as well as the time required to feel fully recuperated. NfR can be used on a dichotomous scale [15], a 5-point scale [48], or a 4-point scale [49]. In this study, all eleven questions were asked on a scale of 1–5, i.e., applying the same scale as in the previous teachers’ recovery study [48]. NfR has good test-retest reliability in stable work environments and is sensitive to detecting change; therefore, it is a valuable tool for monitoring employees’ recovery from work [15]. Overall, a higher NfR indicates a greater need for recovery [16, 46, 47]. In addition, the recovery from work section comprised dichotomous questions (yes vs. no) concerning engaging in specific recovery activity inside and outside the working day. Recovery activities included the same activities as hobbies [45].

The physical activity section included the Finnish version [50, 51] of the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [35, 52]. WHO has developed the GPAQ to produce estimates of physical activity across different populations [52]. GPAQ has moderate-to-good reliability and validity for assessing physical activity on a group level; however, it also has limitations in accurately capturing physical activity and sedentary behavior compared to objective measures [50]. This study considered the number of days per week for vigorous and moderate occupational physical activity (OPA) and LTPA and sedentary time (hours and minutes) per day. In addition, the survey inquired about active breaks (yes vs. no) and active commuting (yes vs. no).

Statistical analysis

Background information was described as frequencies, percentages, and mean with standard deviation (SD). Concerning recovery from work, Cronbach’s alpha of the NfR scale was 0.84. NfR was calculated as the median with interquartile range (IQR) for different background information groups. The Cross-tabulation with the Chi-Squared Test was conducted for age and gender groups with NfR. Engaging in recovery activities inside and outside a workday was reported as frequencies and percentages. The normality of the NfR was tested with the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Because the NfR was not normally distributed (p = 0.002), nonparametric tests, i.e., the Mann-Whitney U Test and the Kruskal-Wallis Test with the Dunn’s post hoc test [53], were used to test differences in NfR across different background information groups and engaging (yes vs. no) in single recovery activity inside and outside a working day.

Vigorous and moderate OPA days per week (yes vs. no) were combined into a single OPA variable. Vigorous and moderate LTPA were divided into groups: 0, 1–2, 3–4, and ≥ 5 days per week. OPA and vigorous and moderate LTPA were described as frequencies and percentages. Cross-tabulation with the Chi-Squared Test was conducted for age and gender groups with OPA, vigorous LTPA days per week, and moderate LTPA days per week. In addition, mean active commuting time and sedentary time (minutes per day) with SD were calculated. Further, sedentary time was divided into groups of ≤ 480 min and > 480 min according to the recommendation [54]. Cross-tabulation with the Chi-Squared Test was conducted for age and gender groups with sedentary time groups. Median NfR with IQR were calculated for active breaks, active commuting, OPA, vigorous LTPA days per week, moderate LTPA days per week, and sedentary time groups. The Mann-Whitney U Test and the Kruskal-Wallis test with the Dunn’s post hoc test [53] were used to test the difference in NfR across the active breaks, active commuting, OPA, vigorous LTPA, moderate LTPA, and sedentary time groups.

A hierarchical linear regression was used to model NfR (dependent variable). Model 1 included age, gender, and vigorous LTPA days per week as independent variables. Vigorous LTPA was chosen to represent physical activity based on preliminary analyses in which it was more strongly associated with NfR compared to OPA and moderate LTPA (Additional file 1). Model 2 included the following additional independent variables: sedentary behavior [55], active breaks [55], active commuting [56], and physical activity and sports [26] as a leisure-time hobby. To assess model fit, standardized residuals were inspected, and to measure multicollinearity, variance inflation factors were computed (Additional file 2). IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0.2.0 [57] was applied as a statistical platform in every analysis. This study was reported according to Strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) Checklist for cross-sectional studies [58, 59].

The mean overall work experience was 28 years (SD 9.8), and the mean work experience in higher education was 13 years (SD 9.0). Recovery from work

Respondents’ median NfR was 3.1 (IQR 2.2–3.6). The NfR according to gender, age, work experience, and workplace location is presented in Table 1, and hobbies in Table 2. There was no statistically significant difference in NfR between genders (p = 0.19) or age groups (p = 0.31). The three most common recovery activities inside a working day were food and beverages (37%), physical activity and sports (29%), and animals (23%). In turn, the three most typical recovery activities outside a working day were physical activity and sports (89%), nature (79%), and food and beverages (72%). There was no statistically significant difference in NfR according to engaging in any specific recovery activity inside (p-values ranged from 0.08 to 0.90) or outside a working day (p-values ranged from 0.06 to 0.98) (Table 2).

Physical activity

Over half (59%) were physically active during breaks, and 33% commuted actively, with the mean time of 48 min (SD 35) per day. Mostly, respondents (88%) had no OPA (Table 3). On average, they had vigorous LTPA on 3 (SD 1.5) days per week and moderate LTPA on 4 (SD 1.9) days per week. The distribution of vigorous and moderate LTPA days per week is presented in Table 3. There was no statistically significant difference in OPA (p = 0.50), vigorous LTPA (p = 0.79), or moderate LTPA (p = 0.25) according to gender groups. In addition, there was no statistically significant difference in OPA (p = 0.75), vigorous LTPA (p = 0.41), or moderate LTPA (p = 0.77) according to age groups. The mean sedentary time per day was 507 min (SD 189). Overall, 57% had ≤ 480 min of sedentary time, as recommended [54] (Table 3). There was no statistically significant difference in sedentary time groups according to gender (p = 0.37) or age groups (p = 0.43).

Recovery from work and physical activity

Concerning recovery from work and physical activity, there was a statistically significant difference in NfR according to active breaks (p = 0.042): persons who were physically active during breaks had a lower NfR (median NfR 2.9 with IQR 2.2–3.5) than those who did not (median NfR 3.2 with IQR 2.5–3.8). There was also a statistically significant difference in NfR according to vigorous LTPA days per week (p = 0.049). Post hoc test showed (p = 0.007) that persons with 3–4 vigorous LTPA days per week had lower NfR (median NfR 2.7 with IQR 2.1–3.4) than persons not engaging in vigorous LTPA (median NfR 3.2 with IQR 2.5–3.8). There was no statistically significant difference in NfR according to active commuting (p = 0.52), moderate LTPA days per week (p = 0.09), OPA (p = 0.86), or sedentary time (p = 0.16) (Table 3).

In Model 1, age (p = 0.020) and vigorous LTPA days per week explained NfR (p = 0.003), but gender did not (p = 0.18). The unstandardized coefficient for age was − 0.02, indicating that for each additional year of age, the NfR decreases by 0.02 units. The unstandardized coefficient for vigorous LTPA days per week was − 0.17, indicating that each change between vigorous LTPA categories of 0, 1–2, 3–4, and ≥ 5 days per week is associated with a 0.17 unit decrease in the NfR (Table 4).

Discussion

This study indicated that higher education personnel recovered moderately from work. Mostly, they were physically active in their leisure time, but they hardly had OPA. Persons who were physically active during breaks had a lower NfR, as well as those with 3–4 vigorous LTPA days per week, than persons not engaging in vigorous LTPA. However, in the linear regression model adjusted for age, gender, sedentary time, active commuting, and physical activity and sports as a hobby, the associations of vigorous LTPA and active breaks with NfR were not statistically significant. Only age explained NfR. Overall, the respondents mainly represented middle-aged, female senior lecturers with extensive work experience and employed in social work, health, sports, and beauty units. The majority had physical activity and sports as a hobby. Therefore, the results cannot be generalized to all those working in Finnish higher education.

In this study, the respondents’ NfR was slightly lower than in a previous study in which Finnish class teachers’ or subject teachers’ NfR varied between 3.2 and 3.4, depending on age [48]. However, it is important to note that all 11 items were used in this study, whereas Kinnunen et al. (2019) used only six items [48]. Although the response scale of (1–5) was the same in both studies, differences in the content of the measures limit comparability. Furthermore, higher education personnel may have different work-related resources and workload factors compared to class and subject teachers. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution. More research is needed on recovery from work among higher education personnel, measured by NfR.

The respondents were mostly physically active in their leisure time. Only 35% reported having no vigorous LTPA, and 11% had no moderate LTPA. Due to the self-reported physical activity survey, there may be social desirability, i.e., the tendency to give socially desirable responses instead of true behaviors [60], and recall bias, i.e., responding erroneously due to problems in recalling past events [61]. In this study, social desirability and recall bias may have led respondents to overestimate their LTPA.

The findings of this study confirm differences in NfR by physical activity during breaks and vigorous LTPA. Persons with vigorous LTPA 3–4 days per week had a lower NfR than persons with no vigorous LTPA. Lower NfR may be due to LTPA regulating hormones [64] and enhancing recovery experiences, such as psychological detachment [64, 65]. In addition, persons who were physically active during breaks had a lower NfR than those who were not, which is also supported by previous research [55]. However, the differences in NfR were small in absolute terms (0.5 and 0.3), and these changes are unlikely to represent clinically meaningful variation in NfR. Nevertheless, the differences in NfR are consistent with previous research indicating that physical activity is linked to recovery from work [26]. The clinical interpretation of these small differences is uncertain, particularly given the cross-sectional design, which cannot determine whether physical activity improves recovery or whether better recovery promotes physical activity.

In this study, in the linear regression model adjusted for age, gender, sedentary time, active commuting, and physical activity and sports as a hobby, the associations of vigorous LTPA and active breaks with NfR were not statistically significant. Earlier research has shown an association between OPA and NfR, but no relationship between LTPA and NfR [63]. Previous research has also shown that employees with high OPA tend to engage in less LTPA compared to those working in sedentary jobs [62].

Previous studies have adjusted for other factors such as personal preferences [11] and sleep [66, 67], which may confound or affect pathways between physical activity and recovery. For example, individual recovery experiences [11] can lead to some people experiencing physical activity as restorative, while others find it more burdensome. In the adjusted Model 2, only age explained NfR. Each additional year of age decreased NfR slightly. Previous research has also shown the association between NfR and age: NfR increases with age until the age of 55, after which older employees have decreased NfR due to, for example, work environment, personal life, and compensation strategies [68].

Strengths of this study

Firstly, this study was reported according to the STROBE Checklist [58, 59] (Additional file 3). Secondly, this study provides information on physical activity and recovery from work based on validated questionnaires. Thirdly, this study shows differences in NfR by physical activity during breaks and vigorous LTPA, which is consistent with previous research. Fourthly, the study also shows that age explains NfR. However, this is only one of the explanatory factors for NfR.

Limitations of this study

Firstly, the response rate was low despite a four-week response period and two reminder messages. The target sample size was not reached, which introduces the risk of non-response bias. Secondly, the respondents were mostly middle-aged female senior lecturers having extensive work experience, working in social work, health, sports, and beauty units, and having physical activity and sports as a hobby. Due to the overrepresentation of that group in the data, the results of this study cannot be generalized to all individuals working in higher education. The external validity of this study is limited. Thirdly, this study was based on self-reported physical activity and recovery from work. There may be a risk of social desirability [60, 61] and recall bias [61]: for example, respondents may overestimate their physical activity and recovery from work. In addition, physically active persons may be overrepresented because they are more likely to respond, whereas inactive people are a so-called “hard-to-reach group” [69].

Conclusions

The higher education personnel participating in this study recovered moderately from work, and most of them were physically active in their leisure time. The findings of this study confirm differences in NfR by physical activity during breaks and LTPA. Adjusted models did not verify direct associations of NfR with active breaks and LTPA. Only age explained NfR in the adjusted linear regression model. The results of this study identify a need for further research to determine whether vigorous LTPA is more efficient in promoting recovery from work than moderate LTPA. In addition, research is needed on why employees do or do not experience and believe in recovering from work through physical activity. These experiences and beliefs are valuable and are to be considered in promoting recovery from work through physical activity in a motivating way at the individual, community, and organizational levels [70, 71] nationally and internationally.
