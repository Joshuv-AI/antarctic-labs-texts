Prevalence and awareness of tobacco-related habits among the students of Gannavaram: A cross-sectional questionnaire-based study
Authors: A. Usha Kiranmai, Ananya Tirumani, G Pravallika, P. Ooha Sai, Meka Purna Venkata Prabhat, Gummadapu Sarat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Tobacco use is the leading cause of oral cancer worldwide. In India, it was reported in 2021 that 1 million deaths were tobacco-related; of those, 79.8% deaths were due to smoking and 21.0% were due to second-hand smoke. It is available in various forms and has become a venerable practice in India. Recently, the increased initiation of usage has been noted among adolescents, which can cause severe adverse effects in the future. Thus, it is necessary to create awareness regarding the adverse effects of tobacco among the youth and encourage its disuse. The purpose of this study is to assess the knowledge of the use of tobacco and its products among students in Gannavaram and to evaluate their awareness regarding the catastrophic effects of tobacco consumption.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A descriptive epidemiological study was conducted among the students in the province of Gannavaram, Krishna district, Andhra Pradesh. A questionnaire was prepared to estimate the frequency of usage, which will be used to assess and create awareness among the students. Statistical analysis was done by using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.RESULTS:This study underscores the need to enhance anti-smoking awareness and implement targeted tobacco cessation initiatives among school and college students in Gannavaram, particularly given the significant gender disparity in usage (12.1% in males vs. 1% in females) and limited knowledge of anti-tobacco programs (78%).CONCLUSION:The findings emphasize the importance of robust research methodologies and educational campaigns to guide public health strategies, evaluate the effectiveness of tobacco bans, and promote informed decision-making among youth.

Introduction

Tobacco, either in smoke or smokeless form, is an exclusively leading colossal avertable cause of illness and premature deaths in the world today.[1] According to the World Health Organization (WHO), in 2022, the tobacco epidemic was responsible for over 8 million global deaths annually.[2] A new report jointly launched by WHO and STOP (Stopping Tobacco Organizations and Products), a global tobacco industry watchdog, shows that globally, 37 million children aged 13–15 years are estimated to use tobacco, and in many countries, usage of e-cigarettes exceeds that of adults.[3] Tobacco use in India is more than what it is in most other countries. Only 20% of total tobacco consumption is in the form of cigarettes. In India, an estimated 65% of all men and 33% of all women use some form of tobacco, snuff (inhaled powdered tobacco), Khaini (mixture of tobacco and slaked lime), Creamy snuff, and Mishri (burnt tobacco for cleaning teeth).[4]

The prevention of tobacco use in young people appears to be the single greatest opportunity for preventing non-communicable diseases in the world today. If current trends continue, tobacco will kill nearly 250 million of today’s children.[5] There are 25 tobacco-related diseases known today. These include cancers of the lung, oral cavity, pharynx, esophagus, pancreas, bladder, cervix, and leukemia.[6] Nicotine dependence indicates a compulsive use of nicotine-containing tobacco; physiologic tolerance, which means yearning to use increased amounts of nicotine to attain desired effects; nicotine withdrawal, which causes symptoms like anxiety, craving, impatience, increased hunger, anger, desperation, and continued use despite many problems due to its usage.[7] The Fagerstrom Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND) is widely used to determine the nicotine dependence among smokers in different population groups globally.[8] Although studies were conducted to determine the nicotine dependency using the FTND among the Indian population, previous studies focused on polydrug users and psychiatric patients.[9]

Limited studies focus on the prevalence of tobacco use and awareness among students in semi-urban areas like Gannavaram, India, which remain underrepresented compared to urban or rural settings. This study provides localized data from Gannavaram to guide targeted interventions by policymakers and public health decision-makers. It assesses the prevalence of tobacco use and students’ awareness of its health risks, aligning usage patterns with educational needs. By focusing on students, the research highlights behaviors and awareness during formative years, offering valuable insights for early intervention strategies. A cross-sectional, questionnaire-based approach integrates quantitative and qualitative insights to comprehensively address the issue. Hence, this study was conducted, using a questionnaire, to determine the awareness about the risk associated with the usage of tobacco and its products among students in Gannavaram.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional observational study was designed to assess tobacco-related habits and awareness among adolescents in Gannavaram, Andhra Pradesh, India, over a three-week period in October 2021. The study setting included schools where data collection was permitted by institutional authorities.

Study participants and sampling

A total of 500 study subjects aged 13–19 years were selected based on a non-probabilistic, convenience sampling method. Participants were recruited from schools after obtaining permission from principals. Both male and female students were included to minimize gender bias. Students unwilling to participate were excluded from the study.

Data collection tool and technique

To ensure the validity of the questionnaire, a comprehensive review of existing literature was conducted to identify relevant tobacco-related habits and awareness topics. Content validity was ensured through expert review by healthcare professionals and faculty members. A pilot test was first conducted with a small group of students before the main study to assess the questionnaire’s validity and reliability.

To evaluate construct validity, the Kaiser-Meyer-Oklin (KMO) test and Bartlett’s test of Sphericity were performed. The KMO value was 0.71, indicating that the sample was adequate for factor analysis. Additionally, Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ² (21) = 76.878, P < 0.001), confirming that the correlations among the items were strong enough to justify factor analysis. These findings suggest that the questionnaire has a meaningful structure and provides support for its construct validity.

The internal consistency of the questionnaire was measured using Cronbach’s alpha; the analysis yielded values ranging from 0.605 to 0.622, indicating moderate reliability. This suggests that the final questionnaire has an acceptable level of internal consistency for measuring tobacco-related habits and awareness among students in Gannavaram. Students were asked to fill out the questionnaire after they were given a brief explanation about it.

Ethical consideration

Prior approval for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Committee. The procedure and purpose of the study were explained to the school principals, and written informed consent was obtained. Students were informed about the study’s objectives and assured of the confidentiality of their responses. Any student unwilling to participate was excluded.

Statistical analysis method

Statistical analysis was performed by using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 Armonk, NY: IBM Corp. Statistical tests included the KMO measure, Bartlett’s test, and Cronbach’s alpha to evaluate construct validity and reliability.

Discussion

There are increased calls for anti-smoking awareness by healthcare professionals, with a greater need for their involvement in tobacco cessation activities.[10] The lower tobacco prevalence in the present study could be due to greater awareness of the harmful effects of tobacco among the children. In the present study, a total of 500 subjects were included. The age of the respondents ranged from 13 to 19 years. Overall, among the 500 respondents, there were 404 (80.8%) males and 96 (19.2%) females [Table 1, Figure 1], which is similar to the study conducted by Raina et al. and Yue et al. This study was conducted as part of a prevalence and awareness program, and it is possible that many children did not report the habit correctly. Explaining the harmful effects of tobacco use before giving out the questionnaire can lead to an underexpression of tobacco use.[11] An attempt was made to find out the awareness of school students about the anti-tobacco program or law. The majority (78%) of the students reported that they do not know about any program or law on tobacco. Only a few (22%) students talked about the knowledge of the ban on selling tobacco to minors.

The study clearly reveals that there is an urgent need to disseminate information about legal programs among school children. In this study, only 50 (10%) students had a habit of smoking, and 450 students (90%) did not have a habit of smoking.[12] With respect to gender, our finding that tobacco usage was higher among males (49 (12.1%)) when compared to females (1 (1%)), multiple studies have consistently demonstrated that male adolescents have higher tobacco consumption rates compared to females.[13] Recent studies reveal gender disparities in tobacco consumption and motivational differences among males and females in tobacco usage. Factors initiating females in tobacco use are weight control desires, social image of smoking, emotional vulnerability, lower positive effect, and higher negative effect.[1415] In males, they are more likely initiated by demonstration of independence, peer social influences, and social acceptability at parties.[1316] However, the estimates of the prevalence of tobacco use among males and females in our study are lower than those reported by these studies.[17]

According to various prevalence surveys, teens are starting to smoke more frequently, which may continue into adulthood. Smokeless tobacco usage represents a complex public health challenge, particularly among adults. Males demonstrate significantly higher smokeless tobacco consumption rates compared to females.[18] Studies reveal the following sociocultural and psychological reasons that trigger tobacco usage: individual and public beliefs, family tobacco usage, insufficient supervision,[19] unemployment, educational level, and wealth index is inversely correlated with tobacco use. Our study shows a lack of effective communication, since 78% of students had little knowledge of anti-tobacco activities. More inclusive awareness efforts could be made possible by grounded theory’s ability to shed light on the obstacles adults have when trying to understand hazards or obtain tools for quitting.

Another interesting observation was that young adults (aged 15–19 years) were more aware of the harmful effects of tobacco, and it was reflected in the low prevalence in that age group [Figure 2]. However, the prevalence increases with age due to the addictive nature of the habit. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study of its kind in assessing the prevalence of tobacco-related habits among school and college students of Gannavaram. In this study, an attempt was made to assess the prevalence of tobacco habits among school and college students. The results of this work will be instrumental in assessing the prevalence of tobacco-related habits in Gannavaram. This will help in formulating strategies to achieve a tobacco-free society. This will also act as a surrogate measure to assess the effectiveness of the ban imposed by the government on the sale of tobacco.

Limitations and recommendations

Firstly, limited geographic representation with a relatively small sample size, which is not specified. Secondly, a significant gender imbalance is seen (80.8% males, 19.2% females), leading to potential selection bias and self-reporting bias due to limited objective verification of reported data, in which participants might provide socially desirable responses. Finally, the study was designed to be representative of a narrow age range, i.e., 13–19 years, which may not reflect adult tobacco behaviors. Future research could explore developing age-specific intervention models, targeting disadvantaged and institutional youth as they are more prone to engage in tobacco use, including a more geographically diverse sample and balanced gender representation. While internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, the specific values were not reported. This omission limits the ability to fully evaluate the reliability of the tool.

Conclusion

This study underscores the need to enhance anti-smoking awareness and implement targeted tobacco cessation initiatives among school and college students in Gannavaram, particularly given the significant gender disparity in usage (12.1% in males vs. 1% in females) and limited knowledge of anti-tobacco programs (78%). These findings emphasize the importance of robust research methodologies and educational campaigns to guide public health strategies, to evaluate the effectiveness of tobacco bans, and promote informed decision-making among youth. Further, educational institutions are recommended to implement comprehensive multi-dimensional school-based tobacco prevention programs to sensitize students about the short- and long-term physiological effects of tobacco by addressing specific risk factors for male and female adolescents.
