Fluid Reservoir Characteristics in Established Scleral Lens Wear, Part I—Evaluation of Fluid Reservoir Depth and Midday Fogging
Authors: Jennifer Swingle Fogt, Muriel Schornack, Ellen Shorter, Jennifer S. Harthan, Amy Nau, Cherie Nau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives:Fluid reservoir (FR) depth is an important parameter in the evaluation of scleral lens fitting, yet the range of depths compatible with successful wear, clinicians' ability to estimate depth, and implications of FR depth on midday fogging have yet to be fully elucidated. This study quantified FR depth of habitual scleral lens wearers, compared clinician estimates and image-based measurements of depth, and evaluated optical density of the FR to assess the effect of FR depth on midday fogging.Methods:Habitual scleral lens wearers consented to participate at three sites. FR depth was estimated using slitlamp evaluation by experienced examiners. Measurements of the depth and optical density of the FR were collected using Scheimpflug images.Results:The mean estimated (265±97; range: 0–500 µm) and image-derived (264±111; range: 40–555 µm) FR depths were not statistically different (P=0.9). No correlation was found between optical density and depth of the FR.Conclusion:Successful scleral lens wear can occur with a wide range of FR depths. Experienced practitioners can accurately estimate FR depth using slitlamp examination. Midday fogging due to debris behind a scleral lens was not related to FR depth in this group of habitual wearers.

The presence of a fluid reservoir (FR) between the anterior surface of the eye and the posterior lens surface is one of the defining characteristics of a scleral lens fit. Because a scleral lens rests on the conjunctival tissue overlying the sclera and vaults the cornea, the FR both maintains continuous hydration of the corneal epithelium and creates a tear lens that contributes to optical correction.1 Much attention has been given to the depth of the FR in both scleral lens research and clinical practice. It has been suggested that minimizing the depth of the FR may minimize corneal hypoxia.2 Indeed, several studies have shown that increased FR depth has a greater impact on corneal edema than SL center thickness.3–5 Increased FR thickness has also been linked to scleral lens decentration.6 In addition, increased FR thickness has been implicated as a potential causative factor in midday fogging. Formal investigations into this association have produced conflicting results.7–9 On the other hand, inadequate FR thickness may lead to mechanical interaction and damage to the anterior cornea or the Palisades of Vogt at the corneo-scleral junction, which houses the corneal epithelial stem cells.10

Most studies of the effects of FR thickness on corneal response to scleral lens wear have been conducted on nondiseased participants who do not typically wear scleral lenses. The limited studies that have reported substantial variations in FR thickness in habitual scleral lens wear,9,11 report patients successfully wearing scleral lenses with considerably greater FR thickness than the low values (less than 200 µm) recommended by theoretical modeling of oxygen transmissibility through the lens and FR.2

Given the emphasis placed on managing FR depth over the past decade, surprisingly few studies have assessed clinicians' ability to accurately estimate this value. In 2016, Fuller et al.12 published a study on the accuracy of FR depth estimation by fourth year optometry students, finding that neophyte fitters tended to significantly overestimate the amount of FR depth compared with anterior segment OCT measurements, with accuracy diminishing as the tear reservoir depth decreased. Yeung and Sorbara13 conducted a study in which participants estimated FR depth when viewing static slitlamp images of four eyes wearing scleral lenses, finding that participants overestimated FR depth by a mean of 103.3 µm, although estimates were highly correlated with anterior segment OCT measurements. In addition, increased experience with scleral lens fitting did not significantly improve the correlation between estimates and measurements; however, intermediate and experienced practitioners exhibited less interobserver variability than novices. A study by Macedo-de-Araujo found agreement between FR thickness estimated by slitlamp evaluation compared with IOLMaster and digital image analysis and suggested that clinical estimation accuracy may improve as practitioners gain experience.14

Although two studies have reported greater FR depth in scleral lens wearers with reported midday fogging,7,8 other studies have not identified a correlation between midday fogging and FR depth.9,15,16 Given these conflicting results, the potential relationship between FR depth and midday fogging in actual scleral lens wearers warrants further investigation.

Existing literature on FR depth provides some insights on this unique scleral lens fitting parameter, but questions remain. Defining the range of FR depth observed in successful scleral lens wearers for different indications could provide guidance for clinicians who prescribe these devices. Determining the accuracy of practitioner estimates of FR depth could help to determine the importance of anterior segment imaging technology in scleral lens practice. Evaluating optical clarity of the FR in patients with a range of depths could increase understanding of factors that may contribute to fogging of scleral lenses.

The primary purposes of this multicenter prospective observational study were to report the range of FR depths in established scleral lens wearers and compare clinical estimations of FR depth by experienced observers with image-based measurements. Additional outcome measures investigated the association between FR depth and optical density of the FR and the effects of lens haptic design and FR filling solution on FR density.

METHODS

A multicenter, prospective, observational study was approved by The Ohio State University Institutional Review Board. Data collection occurred at three sites: Illinois College of Optometry, Chicago, IL; University of Illinois at Chicago, Chicago, IL; Mayo Clinic, Rochester, MN. Informed consent was obtained before collection of study data. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the requirements of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Established scleral lens wearers were recruited for this study. Patients who wore lenses in one or both eyes for any indication were eligible for participation if they had been using the same lens design(s) for at least 6 months, with habitual wearing time of at least 5 hours per day on at least 6 days per week. None of the participants enrolled were determined to require alteration in lens design, wearing schedule, or care regimen after the study visit. Participants were required to wear their lenses for a minimum of 2 hours before the study visit. Four practitioners performed clinical evaluations for this study: two at the Mayo Clinic and one each at Illinois College of Optometry and University of Illinois in Chicago. These examiners have been fitting scleral lenses as a large part of their clinical practices over a span of 14 to 19 years.

Demographic data were collected, including participant age, gender, and the indication for scleral lens wear. Lens haptic design and filling solution information was also collected. Maintaining a 45° angle between illumination and viewing components of the slitlamp and using a narrow optic section in which the light beam created a 90° angle with the surface of the lens, examiners dynamically assessed FR depth at the slitlamp. The known central thickness of the scleral lens worn was used as a reference to estimate the FR depth. Scheimpflug images of the lenses were obtained using Pentacam (Oculus, Wetzlar, Germany). The central FR depth was measured using the caliper program in the instrument to draw a line from the anterior central cornea to the posterior surface of the scleral lens, as described by Nau et al.17 Optical density of the central FR was assessed using Pentacam analysis software to capture the densitometry index value at a point in the mid-FR above the central cornea, following the method described by Schornack et al.18 All data were recorded in REDCap.19,20

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented, and data analysis was completed with Minitab Version 21.3.1 (Minitab, LLC, State College, PA). Because of the known asymmetry of scleral contour between eyes in a single individual,21 asymmetric disease presentation in patients with primary or secondary corneal ectasia, and resultant differences in lens designs required for each eye, it was believed that all eyes/lenses could be included in analysis of FR depth, even in patients wearing lenses in both eyes. To statistically determine the independence of the lens characteristics in those participants wearing two lenses, a paired t test and Pearson correlation test were used to determine if any relationships existed between the FR depth of right and left lenses. The same comparison tests were used to compare the density of the FR of wearers of two lenses.

Descriptive statistics were calculated to report the range of FR depth found in this population of habitual scleral lens wearers, both by slitlamp examination and by instrument measurement. After assessing data for normal distribution, paired t testing was used to compare the clinician estimated FR depth using slitlamp with Scheimpflug measurements. Comparisons of the estimated and measured FR depths were also compared based on fitting indication.

The difference between the clinician-estimated FR depth and the image-based measurement was calculated. Positive values indicated an overestimation of FR depth with slitlamp estimation compared with the instrument image–measured FR depth, whereas negative values indicated an underestimation. Linear regression of the differences was completed to determine if trends existed in these differences relative to the measured tear reservoir depth. Pearson correlation was used to evaluate the correlation of the differences to the measured FR depth.

Linear regression of optical density of the FR was analyzed to assess if greater FR depth was associated with midday fogging. One-way ANOVA was used to assess if haptic design and filling solution had an effect on FR optical density. One eye per participant was used for the statistical analyses assessing optical density.

Sixty-two eyes of 37 participants (23 female) wearing scleral lenses were assessed. The participants had a mean age of 49±16 years (range 21–77 years). Thirty-two eyes wore scleral lenses for corneal irregularity, 27 wore lenses for ocular surface disease, and three for other indications. Comparison of the measured FR depth found a statistically significant difference when comparing the right and left lenses of participants wearing two lenses (n=25, P=0.02; mean difference=−57; 95% CI: −104 to −11). Pearson correlation found that no meaningful relationship between the FR depth of the two eyes (r=0.3, P=0.1; 95% CI: −0.09 to 0.64) and demonstrated independence between the FR depths of the right and left lenses in patients who wore SL in both eyes, thus enabling the use of all lenses for data analysis.

The mean ± SD central FR depth estimated by slitlamp evaluation was 265±97 µm with a range of 0 to 500 µm. Forty eyes had an estimated clearance greater than 200 µm, and nine had an estimated clearance greater than 400 µm. The mean ± SD FR depth measured at the center of the lens using Scheimpflug imaging and calipers was 264±111 µm, with a range of 40 to 555 µm. There was no statistically significant difference between the slitlamp-estimated and image-based measurements (P=0.9; mean paired difference=−2 ± 126 µm; 95% CI: −34 to 31). Similarly, there was no statistically significant difference in the mean clinician-estimated FR depth and image-based measurements in eyes with corneal irregularity (P=0.7; mean paired difference −7 ± 139 µm; 95% CI: −57 to 43) or in eyes without corneal irregularity (P=0.8; mean paired difference 4.5±113 µm; 95% CI: −38 to 47). DISCUSSION

In this prospective study of stable, established scleral lens wearers, the mean FR depth was 265 µm with a range up to 500 µm. Although theoretical modeling suggests that a FR depth less than 200 µm is necessary to prevent corneal edema,2 73% of the lenses exceeded this threshold in these habitual lens wearers, none of whom required lens refitting because of ocular surface compromise or other scleral lens complications. Indeed, central FR depth was greater than 400 µm in 10% of eyes with no clinically significant adverse effects. This observation supports fitting preferences reported by experienced practitioners in a survey-based study by Harthan et al.22 Not only were experienced practitioners generally more tolerant of greater FR depth than their less experienced colleagues, but over 90% of experienced practitioners considered FR depths of ≥400 μm to be acceptable in scleral lens fits. It also suggests that higher-than-recommended levels of clearance as reported by Sonsino (mean±SD value of 380±110; range 200–600 μm) and Skidmore (306±159 μm; range: 50–690 μm) can be tolerated by some scleral lens patients.9,11

The overall similarity of the FR depth values estimated through slitlamp by experienced practitioners compared with image-based measured values is both impressive and reassuring, especially considering the significant overestimation of FR depth values reported by Fuller and overestimation of FR depth depicted in slitlamp photographs compared with values obtained through anterior segment optical coherence tomography as reported by Yeung.12,13 Unlike the neophyte clinicians who evaluated the images in Fuller's study, practitioners who estimated FR depth at the slitlamp in the current study were all experienced in scleral lens evaluation, which likely contributed to the accuracy of their assessments. Live slitlamp evaluation, as opposed to evaluation of digital images obtained through slitlamp photography, may have also increased the accuracy of observer estimations of FR depth reported here.

When fitting scleral lenses over highly irregular corneas, wide variations in FR depth from one region of the cornea to another are commonplace, with minimal FR depth between cornea and lens over the most elevated areas of the cornea and excessive FR depth over areas of less elevation. Based on this clinical observation, the authors hypothesized that slitlamp-based estimation of FR depth may be less accurate in patients with corneal irregularity compared with those with ocular surface disease (who, presumably, would have more regular corneal contour). Clinically, one must ensure that adequate corneal FR depth exists over the corneal apex, whether or not the apex corresponds to the anatomical center of the cornea; given this, one might expect that a clinician's attention could be drawn to the area of least FR depth on slitlamp examination. This could lead to an underestimation of FR depth elsewhere. Comparison of the estimates by indication, however, did not reveal any meaningful differences between estimated and measured values. This suggests that slitlamp estimation of FR depth by experienced observers can be accurate regardless of indication for scleral lens wear.

In addition, the relationship of FR depth and midday fogging was studied using optical density measurements of the eyes of these habitual scleral lens wearers. Although some previous studies have concluded that greater FR depth corresponds to more midday fogging,7,8 the results of this study of habitual scleral lens wearers failed to find any such correlation and are supported by a previous study by Skidmore et al.9 In the current study, optical density assessments were used to quantify the clarity of the FR as a surrogate assessment for midday fogging. It should be noted that other potential causes of midday fogging symptoms, including front surface nonwetting, were not assessed by Scheimpflug imaging.

One limitation of this study is that advanced testing for subtle signs of hypoxic stress was not performed. Assessing potential hypoxic changes in ocular parameters would have required not only additional imaging (such as endothelial cell density) but also comparison between corneal parameters at the study visit compared with prelens values. Baseline images were not available for most of these patients. Although it is possible that some patients may have experienced subclinical, long-term hypoxic changes over time, none of the patients enrolled exhibited any clinically apparent signs of hypoxia and all were asymptomatic. Longitudinal studies of habitual scleral lens wearers could provide more information on the impact of FR depth on corneal health. Although not a limitation, this manuscript does not cover all aspects of midday fogging but objectively quantified the optical density of the FR of habitual scleral lens wearers over the range of FR depths found in this study population.

No previous studies of habitual scleral lens wearers existed on which to base a sample size for this study. To determine if the sample size was adequate to find significance, a post-hoc analysis using the data from this study (mean difference=1.5 μm, SD=126) in a sample size calculator (G*Power) found that 91,692 eyes would be needed to be examined to find a statistical difference when comparing examiner and instrument-measured FR depth. The necessity to evaluate this large number eyes to find any kind of difference further highlights that the FR depth values assessed with these two methods are equivalent and the differences are not clinically significant.

In conclusion, this study confirms the results of prior studies, which show that successful scleral lens wear can be achieved even when lens fit features fairly generous FR depths. This study also confirms that estimation of central FR depth using slitlamp evaluation by experienced observers is accurate. Accuracy of estimation of FR depth along with a relatively wide range of tolerable values for this parameter suggests that, although image-based measurements of FR depth may provide additional confidence in the appropriateness of a given scleral lens fit (particularly in less experienced providers or complex corneal topographies), clinical scleral lens fitting can be successfully accomplished without advanced anterior segment imaging.
