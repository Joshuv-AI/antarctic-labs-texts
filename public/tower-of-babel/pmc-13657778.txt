Channel attention of the image for distinguishing ductal carcinoma in situ from benign mimics based on ultrasound images
Authors: Yu Yan, Yifei Qian, Yichen Xu, Lizhi Cao, Jingwu Ma, Runqiu Cai, Xiaowei Cai, Yi Zhang, Linjing Shi, Zhennan Lu, Yiyun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuctal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is widely recognized as a precursor to invasive breast cancer, with an estimated 20–50% of cases progressing to invasive disease. The early detection of DCIS could save doctors and patients more time to deal with the breast cancer, which has posed a great threat to the life safety of women worldwide. This study aimed to develop a deep learning algorithm based on the image-level attention to distinguish DCIS from the benign mimics on ultrasound images, assisting doctors in screening for benign breast lesions.MethodsBy screening the biopsy results of 3,327 patients, the ultrasound images of 612 patients were retrospectively collected, including DCIS and benign mimics. The dataset was randomly split at the patient level into training (70%, n=430) and test (30%, n=182) sets. Using ResNet18 as the backbone architecture, we compared and developed models with image-level attention mechanisms. Model performance was evaluated on the test set using the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision, and F1-score. To assess the statistical significance of performance differences between models, the Holm-Bonferroni correction was employed to account for multiple comparisons.ResultsThrough quantitative comparison with 6 shallow benchmark algorithms such as AlexNet, VGG16, GoogleNet, ResNet18, CoAtNet and Swin Transformer, the effectiveness of our method was demonstrated, which achieved the best performance with AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, precision and F1-score of 0.868±0.009, 0.835±0.016, 0.767±0.056, 0.916±0.057, 0.916±0.059, 0.835±0.04, respectively. After Holm-Bonferroni correction, these improvements were statistically significant (corrected P<0.05) compared to all other models. The effect size of AUC, specificity, precision, F1-score were 0.69, 0.68, 0.89, 0.58, respectively, revealing large practical improvement. Furthermore, Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (GradCAM) visualization confirmed that our model focused more accurately on lesion regions, providing interpretability for its superior feature extraction.ConclusionsIn this paper, we proposed the ResNet18-CA for the classification of DCIS and confused cases, which was good at learning the ultrasound image features. The proposed network achieved higher accuracy than the other methods being compared.

Highlight box

Key findings

• We proposed a channel attention (CA)-based deep learning model (ResNet18-CA) for distinguishing ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) from benign mimics on ultrasound images.

• The model achieved an area under the curve of 0.868, specificity of 0.916 and precision of 0.916, significantly outperforming 8 benchmark algorithms.

• Ablation experiments demonstrated that CA alone outperforms spatial attention and hybrid attention (Convolutional Block Attention Module) for ultrasound-based DCIS classification.

• A clinically curated dataset of 612 ultrasound cases was constructed, deliberately including benign lesions frequently misdiagnosed as DCIS (adenosis, papilloma, fibroadenoma, phyllodes tumor).

What is known and what is new?

• Existing deep learning studies on DCIS rely heavily on mammography, while ultrasound-based DCIS diagnosis remains underexplored. Previous methods treat all features equally, and benign samples are often randomly selected.

• Our CA mechanism adaptively recalibrates feature responses to emphasize diagnostically relevant patterns (e.g., microcalcifications, hypoechoic lesions) while suppressing noise, offering a practical solution for small-sample ultrasound datasets.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The high specificity (0.916) and precision (0.916) suggest that our model could help reduce unnecessary biopsies and follow-up examinations in ultrasound screening.

• The comprehensive benchmarking against 8 algorithms provides a reproducible baseline for future research on ultrasound-based DCIS classification.

• Future work should focus on external validation with larger, multi-center cohorts and integration of multimodal data.

Introduction

Breast cancer is one of the cancers with the highest mortality among women in the world. Ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) is considered as the precursor of invasive breast cancer, which refers to a precancerous lesion in which cancer cells are confined to the interior of the breast duct (1). DCIS accounts for 15% of newly diagnosed breast cancer (2,3) and about 20–50% of cases progress to invasive disease (4), posing a serious threat to women’s health. Since DCIS is highly curable through surgery such as breast‑conserving surgery and mastectomy, improving its detection rate allows timely intervention, prevents progression, and ultimately improves patient survival.

At present, the imaging diagnostic methods for DCIS mainly rely on ultrasound, mammography, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) (5,6). As the primary screening modality for breast cancer worldwide, mammography effectively detects microcalcifications but exposes patients to ionizing radiation (7). MRI offers high sensitivity for high‑risk populations but is expensive and time‑consuming, which limits its routine screening use (8). Ultrasound is non‑invasive, radiation‑free, rapid, and low‑cost (6,9-11), yet its diagnostic rate for DCIS remains only 50–60% (12), mainly due to poor signal‑to‑noise ratio and operator-dependent (6,9-11). In most cases, ultrasound usually plays the role of preliminary screening. There is important clinical significance to improve the diagnosis rate of DCIS by ultrasound images, which not only allows for early diagnosis, but also reduces the financial, psychological, and physical burden on patients.

Most simple hyperplastic lesions have a low risk of malignant transformation (13), while DCIS is far more likely to progress into invasive breast cancer as shown in Figure 1. In ultrasound examinations, low-risk benign lesions such as breast adenosis, papilloma, fibroadenoma and mastitis often mimic the sonographic features of DCIS, introducing substantial interference for DCIS diagnosis. Therefore, reliably distinguishing DCIS from these confounding benign lesions remains a critical challenge for both clinicians and researchers.

Researchers have increasingly applied computer vision algorithms to improve DCIS diagnosis (14-18). Most existing studies employ mammography-based deep learning or machine learning models for classification, risk stratification, or invasion prediction, achieving promising results of area under the curve (AUC) up to 0.84 (17). However, these works heavily rely on mammography, which has limited sensitivity in dense breasts and involves radiation exposure, leaving ultrasound‑based DCIS diagnosis underexplored (18). Additionally, current methods tend to treat all features equally, overlooking differential feature importance, and their benign control samples are mostly randomly selected, rather than focusing on the specific benign lesions that mimic DCIS on ultrasound, such as fibroadenoma and adenosis.

To address these challenges, this study proposes a channel attention (CA)-based deep learning framework for DCIS ultrasound datasets. The choice of CA is motivated by the unique characteristics of ultrasound images. Unlike natural images, where spatial patterns dominate, DCIS diagnosis on ultrasound relies on fine-grained textural and echogenicity cues that are distributed across different feature channels. The CA mechanism can adaptively emphasize channels capturing diagnostically relevant patterns such as microcalcifications or hypoechoic lesions, while suppressing channels dominated by background noise and speckle artifacts. Methods

Dataset

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine (Approval No. 2023NL-071-01). Due to the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized data that posed no more than minimal risk to participants, the requirement for written informed consent was waived. Patient records and imaging data were de‑identified prior to analysis to ensure confidentiality. The data used in this study were breast ultrasound examination images taken at Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine from 2018 to 2025, with a total of 3,327 female patients. After applying inclusion criteria, 612 patients were finally included in the study. The patient inclusion flowchart is depicted in Figure 2. The dataset comprises two categories: DCIS cases and challenging benign cases. The challenging benign cases were defined as benign breast lesions that mimic DCIS on ultrasound. Specifically, these included histopathologically confirmed diagnoses of breast adenosis, papilloma, fibroadenoma, or mastitis—benign entities that frequently present with overlapping ultrasound features with DCIS, such as hypoechoic appearance, indistinct margins, and acoustic shadowing. All ultrasound examinations were retrospectively collected from routine clinical practice at Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine. Each examination was performed following the institutional standardized breast ultrasound acquisition protocol. All ultrasound images and diagnostic reports were reviewed and confirmed by two senior radiologists, each with 10 years of experience in breast imaging. The dataset was randomly partitioned into training and test sets at a ratio of 7:3 using stratified random sampling, which ensured that the proportion of DCIS and benign cases remained consistent between the two subsets. Five-fold cross-validation was applied to the training data to ensure unbiased model evaluation.

Model development and implementation

Convolutional neural networks (CNN)

Since the rectified linear unit (ReLU) was introduced into convolutional network by Alex and won first place in the ImageNet classification competition in 2012 (19), the convolutional neural networks have been applied to various fields and achieved good results. CNN are deep learning frameworks composed of convolutional layers, activation layers, pooling layers and fully connected layers. CNN have the characteristics of local connections, weight sharing and automatic feature extraction, which reduce the complexity of the model and improve the computational efficiency. The shallow convolutional networks such as AlexNet (19), VGG16 (20), ResNet18 (21) and GoogleNet (22) were selected for the comparison because of the limited number of the dataset. All models were implemented using PyTorch (version 1.10.1) with Python (version 3.9.15).

Spatial attention mechanism (SAM)

Decomposing an image generally yields two parts of information. One is the spatial information of the image, which refers to the relationship between pixels. The other is channel information, which is 1-dimensional for grayscale images and 3-dimensional for red-green-blue (RGB) images. In convolutional neural networks, different channel and spatiality have different learning focuses.

The main purpose of SAM is to increase the weight of the spatial regions that leave a deep impression on it and to suppress uninteresting regions. The main approach is to use pooling operations on the channel dimension to find the maximum value or average value on the channel as shown in Figure 4A. In this paper, we use average values of the whole feature maps. After cascading the pooling results, convolution operation is used to obtain a 1-dimensional spatial feature map with weighted channels. Then map the feature map back to the input, which suppresses the uninterested parts and enhances the interested parts. The spatial attention guides the model to concentrate its computational resources on salient anatomical regions in ultrasound images.

CA mechanism

CA obtains the importance weights of each channel through pooling and sigmoid (23,24). Then the effect of feature maps is enhanced based on the importance weights and the effect of subtle feature map weight is weakened. Firstly, the spatial dimension is compressed through pooling; each feature map is transformed into a real number that has a global receptive field to some extent. At this stage, the number of the output channel is the same as the input. Then the weights of each channel are generated through the sigmoid operation, explicitly modeling the correlation of the channels. Finally, the weights output by the sigmoid are sequentially weighted onto the original feature maps to complete the recalibration of the original features in the channel dimension, achieving the goal of learning the different feature maps discriminately. In ultrasound images, the CA allows the network to adaptively emphasize diagnostically critical feature maps, enhancing its sensitivity to specific tissue textures and echogenic patterns. The schematic diagram of CA is shown as Figure 4B.

Integration of spatial and CA

A hybrid attention model is obtained after parallel or sequential integration of the CA and spatial attention. The Convolutional Block Attention Module (CBAM) (25) proposes a sequential combination of channel and spatial attention module as shown in Figure 4C, which take into account the importance of where and which. In theory, the performance is better than the method that only utilizes a single attention and the performance has been validated on publicly available large datasets. The hybrid attention, which coalesces these dual dimensions, enables a comprehension of the context, leading to superior performance in the features that are defined by both their intrinsic characteristics and precise spatial location.

ResNet18-CA

Benchmarking against four classic shallow networks such as AlexNet, VGG16, ResNet18 and GoogleNet, ResNet18 achieved the highest AUC of 0.855 shown as Table 2, prompting its selection as the baseline for subsequent investigations. To systematically validate the contribution of the attention mechanisms to the performance of proposed model, we conducted a series of ablation experiments using ResNet18 as the baseline backbone. Trough qualitative and quantitative comparison, the ResNet18-CA is more suitable for DCIS ultrasound image classification task. The CA module is added in each residual module in ResNet18 as shown in Figure 5. Unlike conventional neural networks that treat all features uniformly, the module adaptively recalibrates channel-wise feature responses to emphasize diagnostically salient patterns while suppressing redundant information and assigns different learning weights to different channels. The residual skip connections preserve shallow image features while mitigating the risk of gradient explosion. Finally, a fully connected layer is applied to perform binary classification between DCIS and challenging benign lesions.

Implementation details

The ultrasound images of DCIS and benign mimics were resized to 256×256 to ensure the consistency of the experiments. The resized images were normalized in order to accelerate the convergence of the model. Then the normalized images were sent into the models for training. During the model training process, the learning rate was set to 0.0001 and the batch size was 16. Model weights were initialized using a normal distribution with zero mean and unit variance. The optimizer employed stochastic gradient descent and the objective function was cross-entropy loss. The 5-fold cross-validation was employed to evaluate model generalization and guard against overfitting. The consistency of performance metrics across the five folds serves as an indicator of model stability; low variance suggests that the model is not overfitting to any particular subset of the data. To ensure reproducibility, a fixed random seed of 42 was used for all randomized operations. All experiments were conducted on Nvidia RTX 2080Ti with 11G of memory.

To comprehensively evaluate the classification performance, we employed a set of complementary metrics. Accuracy provides an overall assessment of correctness. Given the clinical context where misclassifying DCIS as benign and misclassifying benign as DCIS have different consequences, we adopted sensitivity to quantify the ability of the model to detect true DCIS cases and specificity to measure its ability to correctly identify benign lesions. Precision was used to assess the reliability of positive predictions, while the F1-score offered a balanced harmonic mean of precision and sensitivity. Finally, the AUC was calculated to evaluate the inherent capability of the model to distinguish between DCIS and benign lesions across all classification thresholds, providing a threshold-independent measure of diagnostic power. Used true positive (TP) to represent the positive samples (DCIS) that predicted correctly, true negative (TN) to represent negative samples (benign) that predicted correctly, false positive (FP) to represent positive samples that were predicted to negative, and false negative (FN) to represent negative samples that were predicted positive. To assess the statistical significance of performance differences between models, we conducted pairwise comparisons based on the 5-fold cross-validation results. For each performance metric, paired t-tests were conducted on the per-fold results from the five cross-validation folds. The resulting P values were then adjusted using the Holm-Bonferroni correction method (29) to account for multiple comparisons. To quantify the practical significance of observed differences, we calculated effect sizes using Cohen’s d statistic (30). Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted using 5-fold cross-validation to ensure unbiased model evaluation, with performance reported as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) across the five folds. To assess whether performance differences between the proposed model and each benchmark were statistically reliable, paired t-tests were performed on the per-fold results, as the models were evaluated on identical data splits, making a paired design appropriate. To control for the inflation of Type I errors arising from multiple comparisons, the Holm-Bonferroni sequential correction method was applied. For the comparison with the six benchmark algorithms (Table 2), the correction was applied across six pairwise comparisons per metric; for the ablation study (Table 3), the correction was applied across three pairwise comparisons per metric against the baseline. A corrected P value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To complement hypothesis testing and quantify the practical significance of observed differences, effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d. Values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 were interpreted as small, medium, and large effects, respectively.

Discussion

In this paper, we introduced a CA-based deep learning model for classifying DCIS from challenging benign lesions on ultrasound images. By systematically comparing our method with spatial attention, CBAM, two transformer-based architectures and four classic convolutional neural networks, we demonstrated the superiority of the proposed approach. To our knowledge, this is the first study to benchmark such a comprehensive set of algorithms on the same DCIS ultrasound dataset, providing a valuable experimental foundation for future research in this domain.

As shown in Table 2, our ResNet18-CA achieved the highest performance across most evaluation metrics, with an AUC of 0.868±0.009, accuracy of 0.835±0.016, specificity of 0.916±0.057, precision of 0.916±0.059, and F1-score of 0.835±0.040. Statistical analysis confirmed that these improvements were significant compared to all other algorithms after Holm-Bonferroni correction (P<0.05), as indicated by the asterisks in Table 2.

From a clinical perspective, the high specificity of 0.916 and precision of 0.916 are particularly meaningful. High specificity indicates a low FP rate, meaning that when our model classifies a lesion as benign, clinicians can have high confidence in avoiding unnecessary biopsies. This addresses a critical clinical need, as current ultrasound-based diagnosis by radiologists alone achieves only 50–60% accuracy in distinguishing DCIS from benign lesions. The large effect sizes for specificity, which was 0.68 compared to the second-best model in Table 2 and was 1.86 compared to baseline in Table 3, confirm that these improvements are substantial enough to be clinically perceptible.

It is important to address the sensitivity of our model, which was 0.767±0.056. It was moderate and not the highest among the compared methods. While this indicates that our model can assist doctors in detecting DCIS to some extent, approximately 23% of true DCIS cases may still be missed. It is a limitation that requires careful consideration.

The CA mechanism, while dramatically improving specificity and precision, came at a cost of reduced sensitivity compared to the baseline as shown in Table 3. This trade-off suggests that channel-wise feature recalibration may inadvertently suppress some subtle cues that are important for detecting all DCIS cases. We argue that the trade-off is acceptable in the intended clinical scenario. Our model is designed as an assistive tool rather than a standalone diagnostic system. In practice, cases classified as benign by the model would still undergo routine clinical follow-up, while cases classified as DCIS would be prioritized for biopsy. The high specificity ensures that most benign lesions are correctly identified, reducing unnecessary procedures and the moderate sensitivity still captures the majority of DCIS cases.

ResNet18-CBAM showed marginal improvements over baseline, while ResNet18-CA achieved significant gains in specificity, precision, F1-score and AUC. We attribute the underperformance of ResNet18-CBAM to two factors. First, with only 612 ultrasound images, the additional spatial attention parameters in CBAM increase model complexity and overfitting risk, whereas simpler channel‑wise recalibration generalizes better. Second, spatial attention alone caused a notable sensitivity drop (0.825–0.749, Table 3), suggesting it over‑emphasizes prominent features while missing subtle diagnostic cues—an effect partially mitigated but not eliminated when combined with CA. This suggests that channel‑wise recalibration alone is more suitable, and adding spatial attention introduces unnecessary complexity without complementary benefit. Notably, the accuracy improvement of ResNet18-CA over Swin Transformer was not statistically significant after correction (corrected P>0.05, Table 2); we therefore focus our claims primarily on specificity and precision.

Deep learning models are often criticized as “black boxes”, limiting clinical trust and adoption. To address this, we employed Grad-CAM to visualize the regions of interest learned by each model. Figure 7 shows that ResNet18-CA produced heatmaps more tightly concentrated on lesion areas compared to other models, indicating better alignment with clinically relevant regions—confirming that our superior performance is achieved through meaningful feature extraction rather than spurious correlations.

However, there are some limitations in this paper. First, our dataset comprises only 612 ultrasound images from a single institution in China, limiting sample size and ethnic diversity. Although Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (GradCAM) has demonstrated that the proposed method has strong feature extraction capabilities, diversifying dataset in future work would yield more robust results and enhance generalizability to other ethnic groups. Second, the lack of an external validation cohort means that our cross‑validation results may overestimate real‑world performance; multi‑center prospective studies are needed to confirm robustness. Third, this study relied solely on unimodal and single-frame images, which provide limited information compared to multimodal data or video sequences. Fourth, the clinical information of patients such as age, menstrual history, family history of cancer, was not incorporated into our model. Future work should explore multimodal fusion strategies that combine imaging features with clinical variables to achieve more reliable and personalized predictions. These are the goals we will strive for further in the future. Fifth, the moderate sensitivity of our model requires further optimization. Potential solutions include implementing cost-sensitive learning and developing ensemble methods that balance the strengths of different attention mechanisms. Additionally, the retrospective design inherits the operator-dependent variability inherent to ultrasound acquisition, which may limit the generalizability of our model to images acquired with different operators and equipment. Prospective multi‑center validation is needed to confirm model robustness across clinical settings.

Conclusions

We have proposed a CA algorithm based on ResNet18 for distinguishing DCIS from challenging benign lesions through comparing two different dimensions of the ultrasound images. Quantitative and qualitative comparative experiments were conducted with four classic convolutional neural networks, CoAtNet and Swin Transformer to demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed algorithm on small ultrasound datasets. In addition to continuing to expand the DCIS ultrasound dataset, our next work will focus on processing multimodal and video data.
