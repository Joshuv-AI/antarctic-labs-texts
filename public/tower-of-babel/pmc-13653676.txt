EPLIN stabilizes cell–cell adhesion complexes to facilitate TGF-β1-induced fibroblastic differentiation of human periodontal ligament stem cells
Authors: A-Hyeong Choi, Seon-Yle Ko, Young-Joo Jang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPeriodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) are essential for periodontal regeneration, yet the molecular mechanisms governing their differentiation into functional fibroblasts remain poorly understood. This study aimed to identify novel regulatory molecules of PDL fibroblastic differentiation and elucidate the role of epithelial protein lost in neoplasm (EPLIN) in the fibroblastic differentiation.MethodshPDLSCs were differentiated into PDL fibroblasts using TGF-β1. A novel antibody (anti-LG45) identified EPLIN as a target antigen. Functional roles of EPLIN were investigated through siRNA-mediated depletion and ectopic overexpression. Phalloidin staining for F-actin and co-immunoprecipitation were performed to analyze adherens junction integrity.ResultsTGF-β1 treatment successfully induced fibroblastic differentiation, as evidenced by significant increases in fibroblastic marker expressions, which were abolished by the TGF-β receptor inhibitor SB-431542. A novel anti-LG45 antibody was found to specifically recognize EPLIN in differentiated PDL fibroblasts. EPLIN depletion significantly reduced the fibroblastic differentiation and disrupted the typical elongated morphology of PDL fibroblasts. Mechanistically, EPLIN depletion led to filament fragmentation and cytoskeletal collapse, decoupled N-cadherin from the cytoskeleton, and accelerated cell proliferation. Conversely, ectopic overexpression of EPLIN-α or β was sufficient to inhibit proliferation and induce fibroblastic markers even without TGF-β1 stimulation.ConclusionEPLIN is a key regulatory factor that stabilizes the linkage between the actin cytoskeleton and adherens junctions, maintaining the fibroblastic phenotype and preventing unscheduled proliferation in hPDLSCs. This study indicates the importance of cytoskeletal structural integrity in PDL differentiation. Under this circumstance, EPLIN serves as a regulator that maintains the fibroblastic differentiation state of hPDLSCs and prevents unscheduled proliferation.Keywords: human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs), TGF-β1, epithelial protein lost in neoplasm (EPLIN), PDL fibroblastic differentiation, adherens junction

The differentiation of human periodontal ligament stem cells (hPDLSCs) into mature fibroblasts is essential for periodontal regeneration. This study identifies epithelial protein lost in neoplasm (EPLIN) as a critical factor of this process. Induced by TGF-β1, EPLIN acts as a vital molecular bridge, linking adherens junctions to the actin cytoskeleton to stabilize stress fibers. This Adherens Junction-EPLIN-Actin complex is strictly required to maintain the elongated fibroblast architecture and enforce lineage-specific gene expression. When EPLIN is depleted, this cytoskeletal network collapses, halting differentiation and triggering unwanted cell proliferation. Ultimately, our findings highlight that EPLIN-mediated mechanical stabilization is fundamentally required for periodontal development.

Introduction

The periodontal ligament (PDL) is a specialized connective tissue that anchors the tooth root to the alveolar bone, enduring substantial mechanical stress during mastication. The remarkable ability of the PDL to undergo continuous remodeling, maintain homeostasis, and repair after damage is due to the presence of PDL stem cells (hPDLSCs).1–3 As a type of adult mesenchymal stem cell (MSC), hPDLSCs possess robust multipotent differentiation potential, capable of generating various cell lineages critical for periodontal structure, including cementoblasts, osteoblasts, and most notably, PDL fibroblasts.4–7 Among these progenitors, PDL fibroblasts are responsible for synthesizing, organizing, and maintaining the extensive fibrous extracellular matrix (ECM) of the PDL.8–11 Therefore, the ability to precisely induce hPDLSCs into a functional fibroblast lineage is crucial for regenerative medicine, ensuring the formation of organized primary collagen fibers. Fibroblastic differentiation of MSCs is tightly controlled by both soluble growth factors and mechanical and biochemical cues from the surrounding microenvironment, including the ECM and adjacent cells.12,13 Among the soluble factors, transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) is recognized as a master regulator of connective tissue development, repair, and fibrogenesis.14–16 TGF-β1 signaling, typically mediated through the Smad pathway, promotes the conversion of various progenitor cells into a fibroblastic phenotype across multiple tissues.17–19 TGF-β1 drives the expression of ECM components (eg, fibronectin and collagen) and key regulatory transcription factors, facilitating the transition from a progenitor to an activated, matrix-producing fibroblast.20,21 However, maintaining a stable fibroblast phenotype requires more than just TGF-β1 signaling, and significant changes in cell structure are essential. While much attention is focused on cell–ECM interactions, cell adhesion is receiving attention as an essential yet often underestimated regulator in stem cell differentiation and tissue formation.22–24 Adherens junctions (AJs) provide mechanical connections between adjacent cells and serve as signaling hubs that regulate cell shape, polarity, and fate.25,26 The core structure of AJs involves transmembrane cadherins such as N-cadherin in mesenchymal tissues that link across the intercellular space. Transmembrane cadherins link to the actin cytoskeleton via intracellular catenins, which mediate intercellular signaling, maintain tissue structure, and integrate external signals with the cell’s internal machinery.27–32 In many cell types, including MSCs, the remodeling of these cell-cell junction complexes and the associated cytoskeleton is a prerequisite for highly organized tissues like the PDL. This junctional assembly and stability dictate the level of intercellular tension and regulate downstream signaling pathways, including those involved in MSC differentiation.33–36 Disruption of cell–cell interaction, often seen during epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), is a key marker of cellular plasticity and migration, while their formation is crucial for stabilizing the differentiated state.37,38 In addition, disruptions to these junctional proteins, or their associated regulators, have been shown to destabilize tissue architecture and interfere with differentiation outcomes.39,40 Therefore, identifying novel accessory proteins that bridge the cadherin/catenin complex to the cytoskeleton is critical for fully understanding fibroblastic commitment. In the search for specific molecular regulators that integrate TGF-β1 signaling with the necessary cytoskeletal changes for stable PDL fibroblast differentiation, attention has been drawn to key proteins associated with adherens junctions.41,42

Epithelial protein lost in neoplasm (EPLIN), also known as LIMA, is an actin-binding protein that exists in two primary isoforms EPLIN-α and EPLIN-β. EPLIN is a critical regulator of the actin cytoskeleton, particularly in stabilizing actin filaments and mediating the linkage between the actin network and components of the cell-cell adhesion machinery, such as alpha-catenin.43–45 While initially studied in the context of cancer and epithelial integrity, where its loss is often associated with increased motility and malignancy, emerging evidence suggests a crucial role for EPLIN in regulating the ECM environment and differentiation of stromal cells.44,46–48 The precise function of EPLIN in MSC fibroblastic differentiation, particularly in the unique context of hPDLSCs which must maintain a delicate balance between ligament, bone, and cementum phenotypes, remains largely unexplored.48

Previously, to elucidate the mechanism underlying PDL fibroblastic differentiation, we centered on identifying PDL fibroblast-specific molecules during TGF-β1-induced fibroblastic differentiation of hPDLSCs. For this, we developed a set of monoclonal antibodies against PDL fibroblastic molecules through decoy immunization. We used the anti-LG45 antibody among 20 new IgG antibodies to identify a specific antigen in hPDL fibroblasts, and this antigen was EPLIN. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the essential functional role of EPLIN during TGF-β1-induced fibroblastic differentiation of hPDLSCs. The central hypothesis of this study was that EPLIN is an essential regulatory factor for TGF-β1-induced PDL fibroblastic differentiation by maintaining the integrity of the cell junction complex and coordinating cytoskeletal organization. In this study, we ultimately demonstrate that EPLIN is a crucial component for maintaining the cell junction complex necessary for the differentiated fibroblastic phenotype in hPDLSCs, positioning it as a key regulatory factor in this critical regenerative process.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

For the culture of human PDL stem cells (hPDLSCs), third molars were obtained from patients (19-23 years of age) under guidelines approved by the IRB of Dankook University (DKU NON 2020-008) with approval of patients visiting DKU Dental Hospital. As a result of the review, the discarded teeth used in this study were determined to be exempt from IRB review for the use of human-derived materials. PDL tissues were enzymatically digested in α-MEM (Gibco) containing 4 mg/mL dispase (Sigma-Aldrich) and 3 mg/mL collagenase (Millipore) for 1 hour at 37°C. hPDLSCs growing out of the dissociated tissues were incubated in α-MEM supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Hyclone) and antibiotics (Cytiva) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. To induce the fibroblastic differentiation, hPDLSCs were treated with 10 ng/mL of TGF-β1 (Sino Biological) in α-MEM containing 5% FBS for 9 days. HeLa cells were maintained in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (Hyclone) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the AccuPrep Universal RNA Extraction Kit (Bioneer) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized by reverse transcription using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Kit (Toyobo). qRT-PCR was performed using the iTaq Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad). Primer sequences for the target genes are listed in Table 1. qRT-PCR was carried out using the StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95°C for 30 seconds, followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 60 seconds. Melting curve analysis was performed from 65 to 95°C with 0.5°C incremental increases. Glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as an internal control for normalization. Relative gene expression levels were calculated as following: ΔCt was calculated as [Ct (target gene) − Ct (GAPDH)]. ΔΔCt was then calculated as [ΔCt (experimental group) − ΔCt (control group)]. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2(−ΔΔCt). All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) of three independent biological replicates. Statistical significance was analyzed using Student’s t-test and the statistical significance were indicated as follows: ***P < .001; **P < .01; *P < .05.

Flow cytometry

Cells were removed from a culture dish with Cell Dissociation Buffer (Gibco), fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 5 minutes, washed, and then treated with 0.2% Tween 20 in PBS containing 1% bovine serum albumin (PBAT solution) at room temperature for 5 minutes to provide permeability. Cells were incubated with primary antibody in PBAT solution on ice for 1 hour. After washing, cells were incubated with either FITC-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:100, BD Biosciences) or Cy3-conjugated anti-mouse IgG (1:100, Jackson ImmunoResearch) as secondary antibodies. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a FACSCalibur system (BD Biosciences). Data were analyzed using Cell Quest Pro (version 6.0) and Flowing Software (version 2.5.1).

Immunoprecipitation and western blot analysis

Cells were lysed using Lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM EGTA, 2 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, with phosphatase and protease inhibitors). For immunoprecipitation, lysates were incubated with primary antibody at 4°C for 4-16 hours, followed by the additional incubation with Protein G-agarose (Santa Cruz) for 3 hours at 4°C. The immunoprecipitates were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked in TBST (1% Tween 20 in Tris buffered saline) containing 5% skim milk for 1 hour, then incubated with primary antibody. Membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Bio-Rad) for 1 hour. Protein bands were visualized using ECL Western Blotting Detection Reagents (Cytiva) and detected by X-ray film exposure. Information on antibodies used in this experiment is shown in Table 2. The anti-LG45 mouse monoclonal antibody used in the flow cytometry and immunoprecipitation analysis was produced in-house in our laboratory.

Transfection

Full-length cDNAs of human EPLIN-α and EPLIN-β subcloned in pCMV5 were purchased from Addgene (#40928 and #40929). The ORF clone was tagged by DYKDDDDK in N-terminal region. For cDNA expression, cells were transfected with cDNA using Lipofectamine 2000 (ThermoFisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Transfection was repeated at 2-day intervals during the 9 days of the fibroblastic differentiation. To overexpress EPLIN in HeLa cells, transfection was performed using Calcium Phosphate Transfection Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Gene silencing using small interfering RNA (siRNA)

For gene silencing experiments, 20 000 cells per well were seeded in a 6-well plate and transfected with 60 pmol of siRNA using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instruction. siRNA transfection was repeated at 2-day intervals during the 9 days of the fibroblastic differentiation. Commercial siRNA reagents provided by BIONEER Corp. (product number LIMA1-573) were used, which are designed as the company’s proprietary system for the 3'UTR region of the EPLIN gene. AccuTarget Negative Control siRNA (product number SN-1001, BIONEER Corp.) was used as the scrambled control RNA.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells were cultured on coverslips pre-coated with 10 μg/mL fibronectin (Sigma) for at least 24 hours. After cell fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde at 4°C for 30 minutes, cells were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 minutes. Coverslips were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted 1:200 in 5% horse serum. After washing, cells were incubated with FITC-conjugated anti-rabbit or anti-mouse secondary antibodies (Jackson ImmunoResearch). For cytoskeletal and nuclear staining, samples were treated with Phalloidin-FITC (Abcam) and DAPI (Sigma), respectively. Images were captured using a confocal microscope (LSM700, Carl Zeiss).

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was measured using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) according to the manufacturer’s protocol (MedChemExpress). Briefly, the water-soluble tetrazolium salt [2-(2-methoxy-4-nitrophenyl)-3-(4-nitrophenyl)-5-(2,4-disulfophenyl)-2H-tetrazolium], which is reduced by cellular dehydrogenases in viable cells to produce an orange dye. The amount of dye produced is directly proportional to the number of living, metabolically active cells. Since the synthesized tetrazolium salt strongly absorbs light at 450 nm, the absorbance serves as a reliable indicator of relative cell viability and proliferation. For this assay, cells were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for the indicated times. 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 4 hours. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (TECAN).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data are presented as the mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments (n = 3). Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (v 9.0). Statistical significance of changes in gene expression levels before and after differentiation was evaluated using a paired, two-tailed Student’s t-test, pairing samples derived from the same biological three batch. Pairwise comparisons of cell proliferation between individual differentiation time points were performed using multiple paired Student’s t-tests with Bonferroni correction to adjust for family-wise error rates. Samples from the same biological batch were paired for statistical testing. A P-value of less than .05 was considered statistically significant (*P < .05; **P < .01; ***P < .001).

Results

A novel monoclonal antibody, anti-LG45 antibody, specifically recognizes EPLIN

hPDLSCs were cultured from PDL tissue extracted from three adult third molars and differentiated into PDL fibroblasts by treatment with low concentrations of TGF-β1. After 9 days of treatment with 10 ng/mL of TGF-β1, the fibroblastic differentiation was confirmed by the transcriptional expression of the representative fibroblastic markers using qRT-PCR. In all three stem cell populations cultured from three different teeth, PDL-associated protein-1 (PLAP-1) and scleraxis (SCX) significantly increased after TGF-β1 treatment (Supplementary Figure S1, T in PLAP-1 and SCX). Treatment with SB-431542, a selective inhibitor of the TGF-β superfamily type I receptor kinase decreased the expression of these PDL fibroblast markers (Supplementary Figure S1, TS in PLAP-1 and SCX). These results indicate that low concentrations of TGF-β1 induce PDL fibroblastic differentiation in hPDLSCs. The differentiation results of #335 out of the three batches of hPDLSCs are shown in Figure 1A. This batch of hPDLSCs was used in subsequent experiments and the three batches were not mixed. The results shown in this article were obtained using #335, and the results obtained from two other independent hPDLSC batches are shown in Supplementary Figure S2.

To confirm the lineage specificity of TGF-β1-induced differentiation, we also evaluated the expression of key osteogenic transcription factors, RUNX2 and Osterix (OSX) (Figure 1A and Supplementary Figure S1). Interestingly, treatment with TGF-β1 significantly downregulated both RUNX2 and OSX expression compared to the control group. Conversely, inhibition of TGF-β1 signaling using SB431542 completely abrogated the TGF-β1-induced upregulation of fibroblastic markers (PLAP-1 and SCX) and markedly rescued/upregulated RUNX2 and OSX expression levels. These reciprocal expression patterns indicate that TGF-β1 specifically promotes a fibroblastic phenotype while simultaneously suppressing osteogenic lineage commitment.

Previously, we generated a set of monoclonal antibodies against TGF-β1-induced PDL fibroblasts by decoy immunization using hPDLSCs treated with SB431542 as controls.49 Among about 20 IgG antibodies, anti-LG45 antibody was used to identify the antigenic molecules in PDL fibroblasts in this study. Flow cytometric analysis indicated that the cell-binding affinity of anti-LG45 antibody was highly increased in hPDLSCs treated with TGF-β1 (Figure 1B, T). The cell-binding affinity of this antibody decreased after SB431542 treatment (Figure 1B, TS). cDNA sequencing and IMGT/V-QUEST database-based similarity analysis defined the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) of anti-LG45 antibody.50,51 Amino acid sequences around the variable chains of CDRs were conserved (Figure 1C, shadow box in a). The V and J segments of the heavy chain (VH) were found to share 92.01% and 92.50% similarity with Musmus IGHV9-2 and Musmus IGHJ4, respectively. The V and J segments of the light chain (VL) were found to share 98.21% and 66.67% similarity with Musmus IGKV5-48 and Musmus IGKJ2, respectively (Figure 1C, b). These data indicate that anti-LG45 antibody is a novel antibody belonging to the IgG1 and IGκ subgroups of the light chain.

To detect the antigenic molecule recognized by the anti-LG45 antibody, we performed immunoprecipitation using cell extracts prepared from PDL fibroblasts following biotinylation. A specific band between the 75 and 100 kDa protein markers was strongly detected in the anti-LG45 immunoprecipitates by a streptavidin-HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Supplementary Figure S3A, LG45 IP). The protein band was then excised from the gel and analyzed by mass spectrometry, and from this, a hit with significant score identified it as EPLIN alpha (other name LIMA1) (Supplementary Figure S3B).

The human EPLIN gene, located on chromosome 12q13.12, produces two major protein isoforms, EPLIN-α and EPLIN-β, through the use of alternative promoters.44,52 The larger β isoform encompasses 760 amino acids and is translated beginning at exon 1. In contrast, the shorter α isoform, which consists of 600 amino acids, is transcribed starting from exon 4. Consequently, the translation of EPLIN-α initiates at the methionine residue that corresponds to position 161 of the β isoform (Figure 2A).

To validate antibody specificity, immunoprecipitation was performed using the anti-LG45 antibody. In HeLa cells exogenously expressing either isoform, the LG45 precipitates were clearly detected by both commercial anti-EPLIN and anti-FLAG antibodies (Figure 2B). Furthermore, when applied to TGF-β1-stimulated PDL fibroblasts, the anti-LG45 antibody successfully pulled down endogenous EPLIN-α and -β, which were subsequently confirmed by commercial EPLIN antibodies (Figure 2C). These findings verify that the antigen targeted by the anti-LG45 antibody is indeed EPLIN. Notably, during the fibroblastic differentiation of PDL cells, EPLIN-α was predominantly expressed, whereas EPLIN-β levels were barely detectable (Figure 2C, open triangle).

Loss of EPLIN impairs fibroblastic differentiation and structural stability of hPDLSCs

To elucidate the specific role of EPLIN during differentiation, we utilized siRNA to knock down the upregulated endogenous EPLIN in PDL fibroblasts. Immunoblot analysis confirmed the successful silencing of both isoforms, reinforcing our previous observation that baseline EPLIN-α expression is significantly higher than that of EPLIN-β in these cells (Figure 3A, lane 3 in upper panel). When hPDLSCs were treated with 10 ng/mL of TGF-β1 for 9 days, the cells were observed to have a typical fibroblastic morphology, thin and elongated, and were arranged in a very regular manner with directionality. However, upon EPLIN depletion, this dense and regular arrangement of cells became disordered (Supplementary Figure S4A). Interestingly, the increase in the transcriptional levels of representative fibroblast markers PLAP-1 and SCX by TGF-β1 in hPDLSCs was significantly reduced by 80.7% and 48.7%, respectively, after EPLIN depletion (Figure 3B, PLAP-1 and SCX). In addition, EPLIN knockdown impaired key fibroblastic ECM synthesis such as Type I Collagen and fibronectin in PDL fibroblasts (Supplementary Figure S4B).

EPLIN binds actin filaments into bundles, creating stress fibers essential for maintaining fibroblast shape and enhancing the stability of transient cell–cell contacts.43,48 The effect of EPLIN deficiency on fibroblast morphology maintenance was investigated using phalloidin staining to visualize F-actin. In undifferentiated hPDLSCs, phalloidin signals were concentrated in irregular aggregates (Figure 3C, −T in Cy3-Phalloidin). While PDL fibroblasts displayed long, continuous stress fibers and a well-defined elongated shape, the EPLIN-depleted cells showed actin remodeling characterized by filament fragmentation and a collapsed elongated cytoskeleton (Figure 3C, +T and + T/siRNA in Cy3-Phalloidin). The transition from an elongated to a clustered actin phenotype upon EPLIN knockdown demonstrates that EPLIN is essential for stabilizing F-actin bundles and maintaining cellular structural integrity in TGF-β1-induced PDL fibroblasts.

EPLIN depletion disrupts the junctional–cytoskeletal integrity in fibroblastic differentiation of hPDLSCs

Adherens junctions in fibroblasts play a crucial role in mediating intercellular communication, coordinating contractile forces in connective tissue, and facilitating mechanotransduction.53,54 The junction proteins are multiprotein complexes, including transmembrane N-cadherins and catenins, and are connected to actin filaments, which allow for structural support and tension regulation. Based on these previous studies, we investigated the effect of the presence or absence of EPLIN on the interaction between junction proteins and actin filament and the cytoskeletal structure during TGF-β1-induced fibroblastic differentiation. During the induction of fibroblast differentiation by treatment with TGF-β1 for 9 days, the expression levels of N-cadherin and catenin, including EPLIN, gradually increased (Figure 4A). Then, the interactions between EPLIN, junction proteins, and actin were analyzed using immunoprecipitation. Indeed, EPLIN was detected in the immunoprecipitates of actin (Figure 4B, lane 2 in EPLIN IB in a) and vice versa (Figure 4B, lane 2 in actin IB in b). As is already known,45 α-catenin and EPLIN were strongly bound to each other (Figure 4B, lane 3 in EPLIN IB in a and lane 2 in α-cat IB in b). In contrast to the strong binding of EPLIN, actin protein did not bind to junction proteins (Figure 4B, lanes 3 and 4 in actin IB and lane 2 in α-cat IB and N-cdh IB in a). While EPLIN bound to α-catenin, no significant binding was observed to N-cadherin (Figure 4B, lane 4 in EPLIN IB in a and lane 2 in N-cdh IB in b).

To determine whether inhibition of endogenous EPLIN expression in PDL fibroblasts disrupts the interconnections between junctional proteins and the cytoskeleton, we examined the subcellular localization of N-cadherin and F-actin. Despite EPLIN depletion, expressions of other junction proteins did not change with EPLIN expression in PDL fibroblasts (Figure 5A). EPLIN depletion in PDL fibroblasts was also confirmed through a decrease in the Cy3 signal of EPLIN in immunofluorescence images (Supplementary Figure S5). In PDL fibroblasts, the fluorescence signals of N-cadherin in membrane and F-actin of cytoskeleton were overlapped, meaning that these factors were colocalized in cells. However, N-cadherin and F-actin were not colocalized in cells depleted EPLIN (Figure 5B, a). When the microscopic image with the fluorescence signals merged is enlarged, it was observed that colocalization of F-actin and N-cadherin does not occur in fibroblastic cells with EPLIN depletion (Figure 5B, b). Indeed, when the distribution of intracellular fluorescence was plotted as a histogram along the line marked in the magnified area, it was evident that the separation of F-actin and N-cadherin was clear in cells with EPLIN depletion (Figure 5B, c).

EPLIN depletion promotes proliferation via ERK/ARK activation while overexpression induces fibroblastic differentiation in hPDLSCs

To evaluate the biological impact of EPLIN gene silencing on cellular growth, a cell proliferation assay was performed over a course of 7 days (Figure 6A). Both control and siRNA treated cells exhibited a time-dependent increase in cell number. Notably, a statistically significant acceleration in proliferation in the + siRNA group was revealed compared to the control starting from day 5 (2.22 ± 0.24 vs 1.54 ± 0.15 × 104 cells), respectively. This discrepancy became more pronounced by day 7, with the + siRNA group showing a significant, approximately 1.3-fold higher cell count than the control group (3.01 ± 0.26 vs 2.30 ± 0.16 × 104 cells), respectively. While the control cells gradually proliferated from day 2 to day 5 and day 5 to day 7, the EPLIN-knockdown cells displayed a sharper exponential growth trajectory, particularly between day 2 and day 5 as well as between day 5 and day 7. Taken together, these results demonstrate that the knockdown of the target gene significantly promotes cell proliferation over time. The accelerated cell proliferation in EPLIN-depleted cells appeared to be due to the activation of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) and protein kinase B (AKT). According to EPLIN depletion, the active phosphorylation of ERK and AKT was significantly increased (Figure 6B, lane 2 in pERK and pAKT), and based on the non-phosphorylated ERK and AKT and actin, it was analyzed that phosphorylated ERK increased 4.5-fold and phosphorylated AKT increased 2.8-fold in the case of EPLIN depletion compared to the case without siRNA (Figure 6B, graph). This data indicated that disruption of interaction between adherens junction and F-actin under EPLIN depletion results in activation of the growth signaling, promoting a shift from a differentiated to a proliferative state in PDL fibroblasts. EPLIN is known to contribute as a potent tumor suppressor by inhibiting cancer cell proliferation, adhesion, and invasion.46,55 Since depletion of EPLIN increased cell proliferation and decreased TGF-β1-induced fibroblastic differentiation, we investigated whether its overexpression affected the differentiation of hPDLSCs rather than cancer cells. When EPLIN-α or EPLIN-β was ectopically overexpressed in hPDLSCs (Figure 7A), cell proliferation was inhibited compared to the control group (Figure 7B, open and closed squares). Interestingly, even in the absence of TGF-β1 induction, the expression of fibroblast markers PLAP-1 and SCX was significantly increased. In the case of EPLIN-α overexpression, the expression of PLAP-1 and SCX increased 4.3- and 3.7-fold, respectively, compared to the control, and in the case of EPLIN-β overexpression, the expression of PLAP-1 and SCX increased 4.9- and 3.6-fold, respectively (Figure 7C, PLAP-1 and SCX). EPLIN overexpression inhibited the growth of hPDLSCs (Figure 7), but did not induce apoptosis (Supplementary Figure S6A). These results suggest that stable association between membrane junctions and the actin cytoskeleton mediated by EPLINs may be important for fibroblastic differentiation of hPDLSCs.

Discussion

The differentiation of hPDLSCs into mature PDL fibroblasts is an essential process for maintaining periodontal homeostasis and tissue regeneration. In this study, we identified EPLIN as a key protein upregulated during PDL differentiation through a novel anti-LG45 antibody obtained during the development of PDL fibroblast-specific antibodies. While EPLIN is well-characterized in epithelial cells as a stabilizer of the circumferential actin bundle, its role in the PDL, which is a mesenchymal tissue, has remained largely unexplored.

The PDL is a unique connective tissue that must withstand significant mechanical loads during mastication. A defining characteristic of a mature fibroblast is its elongated, spindle-shaped morphology, which is maintained by a dense network of F-actin stress fibers. Our findings reveal that EPLIN is the primary organizer of this architecture in PDL fibroblasts. Consequently, PDL fibroblasts require a robust structural framework that integrates intercellular communication with mechanical resilience. Our results demonstrate that EPLIN serves as the essential molecular bridge in this framework.

EPLIN exists in α and β isoforms. Although both EPLIN-α and EPLIN-β could induce PDL fibroblastic differentiation upon ectopic overexpression (Figure 7), the rare occurrence of β-isoform suggests that α-isoform is the primary effector in fibroblastic lineage of hPDLSCs (Figures 2C and 3A). This suggests that the specific structural requirements of the PDL are best met by shorter α-isoform. EPLIN-α is known to cross-link actin filaments into bundles, increasing their stability.47,56

During TGF-β1-induced differentiation, the expression of junction proteins such as N-cadherin and α-catenin increases (Figure 4A). Our immunoprecipitation data reveals that these junctional proteins do not bind directly to the actin cytoskeleton. Instead, EPLIN facilitates this connection by binding simultaneously to α-catenin and actin filaments (Figure 4B). This adherens junction–EPLIN–actin complex is what allows PDL fibroblasts to transition from the irregular and aggregated actin phenotype of stem cells to the long and continuous stress fibers characteristic of mature fibroblasts. When EPLIN is depleted, this link is severed, leading to a loss of colocalization between N-cadherin and F-actin and the subsequent collapse of the elongated cellular architecture (Figure 5B).

One of the notable results of this study is that EPLIN is not merely a sub-marker of differentiation but a functional driver leading the fibroblast differentiation program. EPLIN deficiency drastically reduced the transcriptional levels of PLAP-1 and SCX (Figure 6), whereas ectopic overexpression of EPLIN was sufficient to induce these markers even in the absence of TGF-β1 (Figure 7). In addition to ligament fibroblast differentiation, we performed qPCR to analyze the effects of EPLIN overexpression on osteocyte and adipocyte differentiation. Interestingly, EPLIN overexpression appears to confer the potential for PDL stem cells to differentiate into various lineages from an undifferentiated state (Supplementary Figure S6B). Based on these data, we tentatively propose a working model in which EPLIN-mediated assembly of the cytoskeleton and adherens junctions may participate in downstream gene regulation and cellular proliferation. While our findings demonstrate a strong correlation between EPLIN loss, cytoskeletal disassembly, marker suppression, and ERK/AKT activation, we acknowledge that direct evidence for a mechanotransduction pathway—such as physical force transmission or nuclear translocation of mechanosensitive transcription factors57—remains to be established. It is also possible that biochemical signaling cascades secondary to junctional disruption independently drive these transcriptomic and proliferative shifts. We also propose that the formation of stable actin bundles and mature adhesion junctions mediated by EPLIN generates the mechanical tension necessary to maintain fibroblast gene expression patterns within the cell. Without the cytoskeletal structure provided by EPLIN, the cell cannot maintain its identity as a differentiated fibroblast, regardless of the presence of biochemical signals such as TGF-β1.

Our findings also provide insights into the regulatory balance between cell proliferation and differentiation. EPLIN has historically been known as a tumor suppressor in epithelial cells, and the loss of EPLIN promotes EMT and increased cell motility.58,59 We observed similar phenomena in hPDLSCs. EPLIN deficiency induced a 1.3-fold increase in cell growth and the activation of ERK and AKT signaling pathways (Figure 6). This suggests that the formation of the EPLIN–actin junction complex plays a controlling role in cell proliferation. As hPDLSCs differentiate into PDL fibroblasts, the cell resources shift from cell division to maintaining structure and generating ECM. Disrupting this structural complex through the inhibition of EPLIN expression causes the cells to return to a proliferative state. This indicates that for successful periodontal regeneration, simply inducing growth is insufficient; structural maturation must be stabilized to prevent excessive and disordered proliferation.

In summary, we demonstrate that the fibroblastic differentiation of hPDLSCs is a process defined by biochemical induction and mechanical stabilization. EPLIN is a key factor of this process, acting as an essential link between the cell membrane and cytoskeleton. By connecting the adhesion junctions with the actin cytoskeleton, EPLIN facilitates the mechanical signaling required for the expression of PDL-specific markers and provides the structural stability necessary to the conversion of proliferative stem cells to functional PDL fibroblasts.

Conclusion

EPLIN is a central regulator of hPDLSC fate. It facilitates TGF-β1-induced fibroblastic differentiation by stabilizing the F-actin cytoskeleton and anchoring it to N-cadherin-based adherens junctions. This structural stability not only defines the fibroblast morphology but also serves as a signaling scaffold that promotes fibroblastic gene expression while simultaneously suppressing proliferation and divergent lineages. These findings provide a new molecular target for periodontal regenerative therapies, emphasizing the importance of mechanical and structural integrity in stem cell differentiation.
