Ethnoveterinary practices and traditional medicinal plant use in North Wollo, Ethiopia
Authors: Mulugeta Alemu, Ermias Lulekal, Zemede Asfaw, Bikila Warkineh, Asfaw Debella, Eyob Debebe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEthiopia’s diverse ecosystems and substantial livestock population have sustained a long tradition of ethnoveterinary practices that predate and coexist with formal veterinary services. Despite the importance of this knowledge, systematic documentation in many districts remains limited, and associated plant resources face growing threats from environmental change and cultural erosion. This study aims to document and analyze traditional knowledge related to the use of medicinal plants in treating livestock ailments in Habru District, North Wollo Zone, Ethiopia.MethodologyEthnoveterinary data were collected through semi-structured interviews, focus group discussions, guided field walk and direct observations from 388 informants across 13 kebeles in Habru District. The data were analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics, including Fidelity Level (FL), Informant Consensus Factor (ICF), and Preference Ranking (PR) to evaluate the relative importance and consensus on the use of ethnoveterinary medicinal plants.ResultsA total of 42 medicinal plant taxataxa belonging to 39 genera and 27 families were documented with associated indigenous and local knowledge working closely with knowledgeable informants. The highest FL values (100%) were recorded for Kalanchoe petitiana A. Rich., Ocimum lamiifolium Hochst. ex. Benth., and Ruta chalepensis L. for treating swelling, bloating, and blackleg in all livestock types, respectively. The highest ICF values were found for gastrointestinal (0.85) and dermatological (0.81) conditions, indicating strong informant consensus. Preference ranking identified Allium sativum L. and Withania somnifera (L.) Dunal as the most reported plants for treating anthrax in cattle. Cattle received disproportionately more treatments than expected from their population size (χ²=143.0, p < 0.001). The documentation of Solanum somalense and distinctive preparation methods (heated compresses, smoke inhalation) reveals locally unique knowledge.ConclusionThe study reveals the richness of ethnoveterinary knowledge in Habru District and emphasizes the need to integrate this traditional knowledge with modern veterinary practices. The high FL values for key taxa (Kalanchoe petitiana, Ocimum lamiifolium, and Ruta chalepensis) warrant their prioritization for further pharmacological investigation, while the high ICF values underscore the common disease categories (gastrointestinal and dermatological) managed by these plants. The documentation of this knowledge is crucial for its preservation, for guiding the sustainable use of plant resources, and for informing future pharmacological research aimed at supporting animal healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13002-026-00907-3.Keywords: Ethiopia, Ethnoveterinary, Herbal medicine, Habru district, Livestock

Background

Ethiopia, with its diverse ecosystems and substantial livestock population, has a long history of traditional ethnoveterinary practices that predate and coexist with modern veterinary services [1]. The country’s agricultural sector is highly dependent on animal husbandry, with an estimated livestock population comprising 65 million cattle, 40 million sheep, 51 million goats, 8 million camels, and 49 million chickens [2, 3]. Livestock production in Ethiopia is not only a critical source of income for many households but also plays a pivotal role in the socio-economic fabric of the country, contributing significantly to the livelihoods of the rural population [4].

Despite the importance of livestock, the health care system for the domestic animals in Ethiopia faces numerous challenges [5]. These include limited access to modern veterinary services, a scarcity of veterinary drugs, and a lack of infrastructure [6, 7]. Consequently, many Ethiopian farmers and herders turn to traditional practices and medicinal plants to treat their domestic animals [8].

Medicinal plants are an important source of ethnoveterinary remedies, as they are locally available, affordable, and effective for many diseases [9, 10]. Ethiopia has a rich flora of medicinal plants, with about 5,757 vascular plant taxa, of which about 10% are considered endemic to the country [11, 12]. Traditional healers are the custodians of the indigenous and local knowledge and skills of using natural remedies incuding medicinal plants for prevention and treatment of veterinary ailments. In Ethiopia, the use of ethnoveterinary medicine, which largely involves the use of plant-based remedies and traditional healing practices, is a common approach to animal healthcare. This traditional system plays a vital role in managing and treating various livestock diseases, especially in remote and rural areas where modern veterinary services are either limited or non-existent [13, 14].

Despite the importance of medicinal plants and traditional healers for ethnoveterinary practices, there is a lack of documentation and validation of their knowledge and use [7]. Moreover, the medicinal plant resources and the associated indigenous knowledge are threatened by various factors, such as deforestation, land degradation, urbanization, cultural erosion, and biopiracy [9, 15–19]. Therefore, there is a need to conduct ethnoveterinary studies to document, conserve, and promote the traditional ethnoveterinary knowledge and practices in Ethiopia.

Habru District is one of the districts in the North Wollo Zone of the Amhara Region of Ethiopia [20]. The district is characterized by diverse agro-ecological zones, ranging from lowland to highland, with different types of vegetation and climatic conditions [20]. The district is also known for its rich ethnoveterinary knowledge and practice, as it has a large number of traditional healers and medicinal plants used for treating livestock diseases [21]. Therefore, the primary aim of this study is to investigate and analyze the ethnobotanical knowledge related to the traditional use of medicinal plants in treating livestock ailments in the Habru District. The findings are intended to inform strategies for bridging veterinary healthcare gaps in remote areas by evaluating locally available resources. Specifically, the study seeks to: (1) critically assess the ethnoveterinary practices by identifying the most commonly used medicinal plants and their therapeutic roles; (2) evaluate the efficacy, preparation, and administration methods of these remedies, using ethnobotanical and statistical tools to gauge the consensus among practitioners; (3) contribute to the conservation of this indigenous knowledge, highlighting the sustainable use of medicinal plant resources, and (4) provide a scientific foundation for future pharmacological research that could facilitate the development of new, evidence-based, and sustainable veterinary medicines, which can be integrated into modern veterinary practices. A key focus is to identify and highlight plant species with distinctive or inadequately recorded ethnoveterinary applications within the Ethiopian context.

Materials and methods

Description of the study area

Habru District is situated in the North Wollo Zone, Amhara Region, Ethiopia, covering a total area of 1,350.43 km². The district encompasses 36 rural kebeles and three urban administrations. Geographically, it lies within coordinates 11o35’0”-11o55’0"N and 39o30’10”- 40o10’0"E, with an altitudinal range of 1,430–2,800 m.a.s.l. Three distinct agroecological zones are recognized: lowland (Kolla), midland (Woina Dega), and highland (Dega). Mean annual temperature is 20.1 °C, with monthly minimum and maximum of 10.6 °C and 30.8 °C, respectively, based on records from the Ethiopian National Meteorology Agency Sirinka station (1986–2019 [20, 22]. The district has a total population of 192,742, predominantly rural and engaged in mixed crop–livestock farming [20] [22]. Habru District is characterized by a deep and historically rooted culture of medicinal plant use. Our prior ethnobotanical study in the district documented 134 medicinal plant taxa used by local communities to treat human ailments, representing a substantial body of indigenous knowledge transmitted across generations [21]. The district is home to numerous traditional healers and community elders who serve as custodians of this knowledge, and the community’s oral traditions including the rich Amharic oral poetry which preserves historical events, agricultural practices, and communal values [23], facilitate the intergenerational transmission of plant-based knowledge. According to the 2007 Ethiopian national census, the district’s population is predominantly Amhara (99.73%), with 95% Muslim and 5% Ethiopian Orthodox Christian [2, 20], reflecting a religiously diverse society. The long-standing tradition of using medicinal plants for human healthcare, documented in our previous study [21], combined with the central role of livestock in local livelihoods (cattle, goats, and chickens together comprise over 80% of the district’s livestock population; Table 1), has fostered a parallel body of ethnoveterinary knowledge. This cultural context where plant-based healing, livestock husbandry, and oral knowledge transmission are deeply intertwined makes Habru District an ideal setting for investigating traditional ethnoveterinary practices. Geologically, the district is situated at a transition zone between the Ethiopian Plateau and the Afar Depression, positioned along the western escarpment of the Ethiopian Rift Valley a tectonically active region characterized by dramatic contrasts between rugged highlands and lowland plains. In contrast, the adjacent Afar Region to the east consists predominantly of uniform lowland plains with elevations mostly below 1,000 m, supporting a less diverse agroecological landscape [24] This geographic diversity has direct implications for biodiversity. A recent study in North Wollo Zone recorded 55 woody taxa across four distinct plant communities, with altitude, slope, and tree cutting identified as significant factors influencing community composition (p ≤ 0.001). In our current research, this diversity translates into a rich ethnoveterinary pharmacopoeia: informants reported collecting medicinal plants from all three agroecological zones within Habru District, with lowland taxa such as Calotropis procera and Opuntia ficus-indica used alongside highland taxa such as Juniperus procera and Eucalyptus globulus. The plant communities identified across elevation gradients in North Wollo correspond to the 42 medicinal plant taxa documented in this study, confirming the direct link between the district‘s geographic diversity and its ethnoveterinary plant resources [24] For a detailed description of the study area, including its geographical, climatic, and sociocultural attributes, readers are referred to our earlier work titled “Ethnobotanical Study on Medicinal Plants Used by the Local People in Habru District, North Wollo Zone, Ethiopia” [21]. The present study builds upon this established foundation, extending our ethnobotanical inquiry to veterinary applications. Reconnaissance survey and site selection

A reconnaissance survey was conducted in Habru District from 14 June to 2 July 2021, prior to the main data collection phase. The purpose of this initial survey was to: (1) gain firsthand familiarity with the district’s agroecological variation, settlement patterns, and accessibility of kebeles; (2) consult with community leaders, local health personnel, and district administrators to obtain preliminary information on the distribution of traditional healers and medicinal plant use; and (3) identify and confirm the 13 study kebeles based on the selection criteria described below [25]. The survey design was informed by prior knowledge from: (1) the urgent need to document indigenous plant knowledge before its erosion due to deforestation, land-use change, and cultural shifts [20, 21, 24]; (2) established ethnobotanical survey methods recommended by Martin [25] and Cotton [26]; and (3) the demonstrated applicability of quantitative indices in previous Ethiopian ethnoveterinary studies [18, 21, 27, 28].

For site (kebele) selection, purposive sampling was employed to identify 13 study kebeles from the district’s 36 rural kebeles, ensuring representation across all three recognized agroecological zones: lowland (Kolla), midland (Woina Dega), and highland (Dega). This agroecological stratification was the primary criterion, as distinct vegetation communities and plant availability vary markedly across elevation gradients in Habru District [21]. Within each agroecological zone, kebeles with reported richness of medicinal plant knowledge as indicated by community leaders, elders, and district healthcare personnel were prioritized. A secondary criterion was the presence of active home gardening, which is directly linked to the conservation of ethnoveterinary plant resources. The selected kebeles are spatially distributed across the geographic extent of the district, encompassing both densely populated agricultural areas and more remote pastoral zones. Although the 13 kebeles represent 36.1% of total rural kebeles, their collective coverage of the full agroecological spectrum and spatial distribution across the district provides a sound basis for characterizing the district’s ethnoveterinary pharmacopeia. Purposive sampling was used only for kebele selection; informant sampling is described separately in the following Sects. [25–27]. Before full implementation, the interview checklist was validated through a pilot study in two kebeles (Wergesa and Merto) with 20 informants (10 per kebele), assessing question clarity, cultural appropriateness, and interview duration, leading to minor refinements in the wording of questions related to plant preparation methods. The pilot testing assessed question clarity, cultural appropriateness, and the time required for each interview, leading to minor refinements in the wording of questions related to plant preparation methods. The final interview checklist (see Supplementary File 2) covered: informant demographics; livestock owned; common ailments observed; medicinal plants used for each ailment; detailed preparation methods; administration route; dosage; perceived efficacy; and conservation practices. All interviews were conducted in Amharic by the first author, who is a native speaker. The survey, authorized by the Department of Plant Biology and Biodiversity Management (DPBBM) at Addis Ababa University, aimed to understand the region’s agro-ecological characteristics, indigenous knowledge on plant use, and environmental conditions.

Sample size determination and selection of informants

The sample size for general household informants was calculated using the formula described by Cochran [29] as cited in Bartlett et al. [30]. Two sampling strategies were employed: (1) Systematic random sampling for general informants: From each kebele, households were listed using records from the Kebele Administration Office. A sampling interval (k) was calculated by dividing the total number of households in the kebele by the proportional number of informants allocated to that kebele. The first household was selected randomly, and every kth household thereafter was included. This method ensured representative coverage across each kebele. (2) Purposive sampling for key informants: key informants were purposively selected based on recommendations from community leaders, elders, and local health personnel. Key informants included traditional healers, renowned ethnoveterinary practitioners, and elderly individuals with recognized knowledge of medicinal plants and livestock treatment. This purposive approach was necessary to capture specialized knowledge not evenly distributed across the general population [25–27]. Therefore, the total number of informants was 388. From these 388 general informants, a subset of 42 individuals (34 males, 8 females) was purposively selected as key informants based on their specialized ethnoveterinary knowledge (traditional healers, renowned practitioners, and elders aged 40–98 years). Thus, the 42 key informants are not an additional sample but rather a subgroup of the 388. Quantitative analyses (ICF, FL, chi‑square tests) were conducted using data from all 388 informants. Qualitative analyses (preference ranking and in‑depth descriptions of preparation methods) drew specifically on the 42 key informants to capture specialized knowledge not evenly distributed across the general population. For preference ranking of anthrax treatments, a further subset of 10 key informants (selected from the 42 based on their detailed experience with anthrax cases) participated. Data collection

Ethnoveterinary data were gathered across three field trips spanning from 14 June 2021 to 14 December 2022: Phase 1 (reconnaissance survey): 14 June–2 July 2021; Phase 2 (main data collection, first round): July–September 2021; Phase 3 (main data collection, second round): October 2021–December 2022. All primary data collection activities described in this study were conducted within this timeframe. Subsequent mentions of fieldwork dates in this section refer to specific phases within this overarching period. We employed a combination of qualitative and quantitative methods to gather data from informants, primarily through semi-structured interviews prepared in accordance with guidelines described by [25–27]. Detailed documentation of these interviews and other field notes was essential to capture the nuanced knowledge of the local community. We followed Bennett’s Golden Rules [31] during fieldwork. Our fieldwork methodology, including group discussions, guided field walks, market surveys, preference ranking, and direct matrix ranking were employed, as recommended by [25]. Voucher specimens for all cited medicinal plants were collected, assigned unique codes (e.g., MA01), and deposited at the National Herbarium (ETH) of Addis Ababa University for taxonomic verification. Identification was performed by comparing specimens with authenticated herbarium materials and using the published volumes of the Flora of Ethiopia and Eritrea (Volumes 1–8) [11, 12] as the primary taxonomic reference. Nomenclature follows the International Plant Names Index (https://www.ipni.org/).

In practice, semi-structured interviews were conducted with each informant at their home or a place of their choice. The interviews, conducted in Amharic, followed a checklist covering: informant demographics; livestock owned; common ailments observed; medicinal plants used for each ailment; detailed preparation methods (part used, condition, processing); administration route; dosage; and perceived efficacy. Focus Group Discussions (FGDs) (5–8 participants each) were held in selected kebeles to gather communal knowledge and resolve discrepancies. Guided field walks with key informants enabled plant identification and collection of voucher specimens. Direct observations were made during remedy preparation and administration when possible. All information was recorded in field notebooks and later digitized.

A total of eight focus group discussioms were conducted across the 13 study kebeles. Seven FGDs were carried out, one in each of seven purposively selected kebeles (Wergesa, Sirinka, Girana, Abuarie, Merto, Mehal Amba, and Were Lalo) chosen to represent the three agroecological zones (lowland, midland, highland). An additional FGD was held in the kebele with the highest reported density of traditional healers (Merto) to capture specialized knowledge. Each FGD comprised 5–8 participants, including both general informants and key informants. Participants were selected with the assistance of kebele administrators and local elders to ensure representation across age, gender, and livestock-keeping experience.

Focus group discussions were held in community meeting halls or under large shade trees (as preferred by participants) at times convenient for the community (typically late afternoon after livestock returned from grazing). Each session lasted 90–120 min and was facilitated by the first author (a native Amharic speaker) with a note-taker recording key points. A semi-structured guide covered: (1) common livestock ailments and their seasonal patterns, (2) medicinal plants used for each ailment, (3) preparation and administration methods, (4) sources of medicinal plants (wild, home garden, market), (5) perceived changes in plant availability, and (6) conservation practices. All FGDs were audio-recorded with participants’ oral consent, and recordings were transcribed verbatim in Amharic, then translated into English.

All data collection activities described in this section were conducted after obtaining prior oral informed consent [32] from all participants, in accordance with the ethical procedures approved by the Department of Plant Biology and Biodiversity Management, Addis Ababa University, and as fully detailed in the Ethical Consideration on Approval and Consent to Participate section at the end of this manuscript. The decision to use oral consent was based on the high proportion of non-literate informants and the cultural primacy of oral communication in the study context.

Data analysis

For this study, a combination of qualitative and quantitative ethnobotanical analytical tools was employed, in line with methodologies proposed by [25, 26]. Data were organized in Excel spreadsheet and analyzed using descriptive and inferential statistics, aiding in identifying key multipurpose plants and understanding their usage patterns. We employed preference ranking (PR) and direct matrix ranking (DMR) techniques for evaluating plant preferences and multipurpose uses, respectively, as recommended by [25, 26]. Preference ranking was used to assess the relative perceived effectiveness of different medicinal plants used for the same livestock ailment (specifically anthrax, the most frequently reported bacterial disease). Ten key informants were purposively selected from the 42 key informants for this exercise based on the following criteria: (1) personal experience using at least three of the seven ranked plant species for anthrax treatment; (2) recognized specialist status in anthrax treatment, as confirmed by at least three independent nominations from other community members or informants; and (3) a minimum of ten years of active experience treating livestock ailments. Participants included both dedicated traditional healers and experienced farmer-herders; all met the defined selection criteria. These 10 informants were asked to rank a set of seven medicinal plants (identified from earlier interviews as commonly used for anthrax) on a scale of 1 to 5, where 5 = most effective and 1 = least effective. The scores from all informants were summed for each plant species, and the total scores were compared to determine the rank order of preference. This method follows the procedure described by Martin [25] and Cotton [26]. Direct matrix ranking was employed to evaluate multipurpose medicinal plants. For selected plant taxa, key informants were asked to rank the taxon against multiple use categories using a predefined scale (e.g., 1–5, where 5 = highest utility). The scores were summed across informants and use categories to generate a comparative ranking of the plants’ overall utility. Direct matrix ranking was applied to plants cited for at least three different uses. This method follows the guidelines in [25, 26]. Additionally, the Informants’ Consensus Factor (ICF) and Fidelity Level (FL) calculations provided insights into consensus among healers and the relative importance of plant species for treating specific ailments [27, 33, 34, 34]. For analysis, each distinct therapeutic application mentioned by an informant was treated as an independent use report. A use report is defined as a single record linking a medicinal plant species to its use for treating a specific ailment in a specified livestock type, as reported by one informant. In Habru District, common livestock ailments were identified through a triangulated approach: (1) Direct reports from local informants during interviews and FGDs, where they described disease names, symptoms, and affected animals in their own terms; (2) Validation and clarification of these disease terms through discussions with community animal health workers (CAHWs) present in some kebeles; and (3) Reference to annual disease reports from the district veterinary office to correlate locally reported conditions with standard veterinary diagnoses. This approach ensured that the ailments documented reflect genuine local concerns and align with recognized veterinary conditions. Data from all use reports were compiled and organized in a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, with columns representing: Informant ID, Plant Species, Family, Local Name, Plant Part Used, Condition, Ailment Treated, Livestock Type, Preparation Method, Administration Route, and Use Report.

Considerations and limitations of quantitative indices

This study documents reported use and local consensus on efficacy; it does not constitute clinical or pharmacological validation of the remedies described. This represents a key direction for future work: the pharmacological screening of high-priority species (e.g., those with high FL and ICF values) is essential to validate their safety and efficacy. Furthermore, while threats to medicinal plants are recognized, this study did not quantitatively assess population status or harvesting pressure. Future ecological studies should evaluate the sustainability of wild populations for the most frequently cited species to inform conservation planning.

Results

Demographic profile of informants

Among the 388 general informants, the majority were male (250, 64.4%) and female (138, 35.6%). Among the 42 key informants, 34 (81.0%) were male and 8 (19.0%) were female. The age of participants ranged from 19 to 98 years, with 116 informants (29.9%) classified as young to middle-aged (19–39 years) and 272 (70.1%) as elderly (40–98 years). Literacy levels showed that 119 informants (30.7%) were literate (completed at least primary education), while 269 (69.3%) were non-literate. Regarding marital status, 331 informants (85.3%) were married, with the remainder being divorced, widowed, or single. This demographic diversity is summarized by kebele in Table 2.

Livestock population in Habru District

Livestock production is a cornerstone of the agricultural economy in Habru District, with most rural households practicing mixed crop livestock farming systems. According to the Habru District Agriculture and Rural Development Office (HDARDO) 2021 report, the district has a diverse livestock population totaling 272,284 animals (excluding poultry and bee colonies when counted as “hives”) [20]. Cattle and goats are predominant, comprising approximately 24% each of the total livestock, followed by a significant number of chickens (34%) and sheep (9%). Donkeys, camels, mules, and honey bee colonies are also present but in smaller quantities (Table 1). These district level figures are consistent with national statistics reported by the Central Statistical Agency (CSA) of Ethiopia, which estimated Ethiopia‘s total livestock population at 65 million cattle, 40 million sheep, 51 million goats, 8 million camels, 7 million equines, and 49 million chickens (CSA, 2020 Agricultural Sample Survey, Volume II, Statistical Bulletin 589) [36]. More recent World Bank data confirm that Ethiopia has the largest livestock population in Africa, with approximately 70.3 million cattle, 95.4 million sheep and goats combined, and 8.1 million camels [37].

Within Habru District, livestock contribute to household livelihoods through multiple pathways: draught power for crop cultivation, milk and meat for household consumption and sale, manure for soil fertility, and as liquid assets that can be converted to cash during emergencies. However, livestock diseases remain a major constraint. A study on seasonal disease patterns in Habru District reported that livestock disease outbreaks often peak between January and April, coinciding with dry season nutritional stress [20]. The district’s mixed crop livestock system means that crop residues (e.g., teff straw, maize stover) serve as important dry season feed sources, but feed scarcity and water shortage during drought periods increase livestock vulnerability to disease.

While a precise breakdown per kebele was not available from official records, the relative proportions of livestock taxa were consistent across the study area, with cattle, goats, and chickens being the most common taxa in all kebeles. The substantial and varied animal population underscores the critical importance of accessible and effective veterinary services both modern and traditional for sustaining agricultural productivity and household food security in Habru District.taxa.

Use report distribution by livestock taxa

To examine the variance in ethnoveterinary treatments across different livestock taxa, each use report (UR) was assigned to all taxa listed in the “Animals treated” column. Across the 314 UR (total use report), the cumulative taxa-specific UR totalled 578 (because a single UR may apply to multiple taxa). Cattle received the highest number of UR (302, 52.3% of the taxa-specific total), followed by sheep (131, 22.7%), goats (121, 20.9%), donkeys (14, 2.4%), and camels (10, 1.7%).

A chi-square goodness-of-fit test was conducted to compare the observed UR distribution with the expected distribution based on the livestock population proportions from Table 1 (cattle 36.9%, sheep 13.6%, goats 37.8%, donkeys 6.4%, camels 5.2%). The observed distribution differed significantly from the expected (χ² = 143.0, df = 4, p < 0.001), indicating that cattle receive a disproportionately high number of ethnoveterinary treatments relative to their population size, while goats and donkeys receive fewer than expected (Supplimentary File 1).

Indigenous and local ethnoveterinary knowledge on livestock ailments in Habru District

Focus Group Discussions and key informant interviews revealed that the local people of Habru District employ two primary conservation practices for medicinal plant resources. The first is home gardening, in which households cultivate medicinal plants within their home compounds. Among the 42 medicinal plant taxa documented (Table 3), at least 12 taxa (including Ruta chalepensis, Ocimum lamiifolium, Allium sativum, Withania somnifera, and Zingiber officinale) were reported to be actively cultivated in home gardens by multiple informants. The second practice is the protection of remnant forest patches, which serve as in-situ reservoirs for wild-harvested taxa such as Juniperus procera, Olea europaea subsp. cuspidata, and Euclea racemosa. These practices were reported across all three agroecological zones of the district.

Disease types, modes of remedy preparation and application

A total of 23 livestock health problems were reported in Habru District. The most commonly reported individual ailments were dermatological conditions, with wound (24 UR, 7.6% of total UR), swelling (29 UR, 9.2%), and eye infection (22 UR, 7%) being the most cited. Within bacterial infections, blackleg accounted for 44 UR (14%) and anthrax for 35 UR (11.1%). Among gastrointestinal conditions, bloating (33 UR, 10.5%), diarrhea (27 UR, 8.6%), and jaundice (12 UR, 3.8%) were the most frequently reported. Snake bite accounted for 19 UR (6.0%), parasitic leech for 16 UR (5%), and ticks for 11 UR (3.5%). All remaining ailments each contributed less than 3% of total UR. Various methods of preparation and application were recorded in the study, highlighting the traditional knowledge and practices of the local people. Aqueous crushed solutions were the most frequently used method, with 43 UR, making up 53.1% of the total. Liquid coating was the next most common method, with 16 UR (19.8%). Paste applications were used in 11 UR (13.6%), followed by topical powder applications in 4 UR (4.9%). The traditional methods of preparation and application are described in Table 4.

Plant parts and conditions used

Various plant parts were utilized for medicinal purposes, reflecting the broad application of different plant components in ethnoveterinary practices. Leaves were the most commonly used part, with 38 UR, constituting 46.9% of the total plant parts used. Roots were the second most used part, with 19 UR (23.5%). The condition of plant parts used for medicinal purposes was categorized into three main groups: fresh, dry and fresh, and dry. The majority of the plant parts used were fresh, with 57 UR, representing 70.4% of the total. This was followed by plant parts that were both dry and fresh, which accounted for 14 UR (17.3%), and exclusively dry parts, which were used in 10 UR, making up 12.3%.

Routes of administration

The routes of administration for the medicinal plant preparations against livestock ailments in Habru District were predominantly oral, with 51 UR, accounting for 63.0% of the total. Dermal applications were the second most common route, with 23 UR (28.4%). Nasal administration was noted in 4 UR (4.9%), and optical applications were used in 3 UR, representing 3.7% of the total routes of administration. The manufacturing process for each ethnoveterinary remedy involved a sequence of steps transforming raw plant materials into a finished product. Across the 314 use reports, nine distinct manufacturing methods were described (Table 4). The most common process was the preparation of aqueous crushed solutions (43 UR, 53.1% of total UR), which involves crushing fresh plant parts (predominantly leaves or roots), mixing with cold water (typically 200–500 mL per treatment), and administering the unfiltered suspension either orally or dermally. For liquid coatings (16 UR, 19.8%), the manufacturing process involved expressing juice or latex directly from fresh plant parts, sometimes mixed with fresh butter, and applied without dilution. Paste preparations (11 UR, 13.6%) required crushing plant parts into a thick consistency, often with minimal added water, and were used for wound dressing.

From the entirety of 23 distinct livestock ailments reported within Habru District, eight disease categories were identified (Table 5). The Informant Consensus Factor (ICF) values provide valuable insights into the consensus among informants regarding the use of medicinal plants for treating various disease categories in Habru District. The highest ICF values were observed for gastrointestinal diseases (0.85) and dermatological conditions (0.81). Parasitic infections also showed an ICF value of 0.80. Respiratory diseases and nutritional/metabolic issues had ICF values of 0.50.

Indigenous and local knowledge on conservation practices

The focus group discussions (FGD) and key informant (KI) interviews revealed that the local people of Habru District possess substantial indigenous and local knowledge regarding the conservation of plant resources. Participants highlighted various conservation practices employed by the community to preserve medicinal plants used for treating livestock ailments. One significant method is home gardening, where households cultivate medicinal plants within their home compounds, ensuring a readily available supply while reducing pressure on wild populations. Additionally, the community actively engages in protecting forest patches, which serve as crucial habitats for wild medicinal plants. These forest areas are safeguarded through collective community-based conservation practices, reflecting a strong sense of stewardship and communal responsibility. Such practices not only preserve biodiversity but also sustain the traditional ethnoveterinary knowledge that is vital for the health and well-being of their livestock. This blend of home gardening and community-driven forest protection underscores the effectiveness of indigenous conservation strategies and highlights the community’s proactive role in managing and preserving their natural resources.

Discussion

The ethnoveterinary practices documented in Habru District reveal a rich diversity of medicinal plant taxa and deep-rooted traditional knowledge, consistent with findings from other Ethiopian regions [55, 58, 67]. The diversity of livestock types reflects a complex agricultural system in which different species fulfil distinct yet complementary roles in food security and economic stability; this diversity simultaneously increases the district’s vulnerability to various diseases, underscoring the need for comprehensive veterinary services.The prevalence of ailments such as blackleg, anthrax, and bloat highlights exposure to both bacterial and environmental threats: blackleg, for instance, poses a serious concern owing to its rapid transmission and high mortality rate among cattle [47, 60]. Research in comparable agro-pastoralist regions of Ethiopia and Africa has emphasised the importance of integrating traditional livestock management with modern veterinary practices to mitigate such outbreaks [73–75], suggesting that local reliance on traditional remedies, while valuable, may not alone be sufficient to control the spread of severe infectious diseases.

Ethnoveterinary medicinal plant diversity: the role of local knowledge

The diversity of medicinal plant taxa identified in Habru District reflects deep-rooted ethnobotanical knowledge among local communities. The dominance of Solanaceae, represented by seven taxa used for treating livestock ailments, is consistent with reports from other parts of Ethiopia [47] and is attributable to the family’s pharmacologically active alkaloids, which confer antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic properties [76]. The broader prevalence of Solanaceae and Lamiaceae across East and Southern African ethnoveterinary pharmacopeias underscores the shared recognition of their bioactive potential [77, 78]; nevertheless, the specific taxa employed (e.g., Ocimum lamiifolium in Ethiopia versus Ocimum gratissimum subsp. gratissimum in West Africa) and unique preparations such as smoke inhalation for cough highlight local ecological adaptation and cultural innovation within this shared framework [79]. Similarly, the prominence of Euphorbiaceae, valued for anti-inflammatory and wound-healing properties [8, 9, 38], and Fabaceae, recognised for its efficacy against parasitic infections and digestive disorders, reflects cross-cultural patterns observed across Ethiopia in which the same families are utilised for both human and animal ailments [38].

The effectiveness of these traditional treatments raises important evaluative questions regarding their comparative efficacy relative to modern interventions and the degree to which they are being integrated into formal veterinary practice. Studies indicate that while ethnoveterinary remedies can be effective for certain ailments, their potency and safety vary considerably depending on the taxa and method of preparation [80]. The widespread use of medicinal plants in Habru District therefore points to both the potential and the necessity for further scientific validation, which would bridge the gap between traditional knowledge and modern veterinary science.

Comparative analysis with other regions of Ethiopia

To contextualize the ethnoveterinary knowledge of Habru District, the 16 most frequently cited medicinal plant taxa were compared with previous reports from other Ethiopian regions. These taxa account for 314 total use reports (78.4% of all UR), each cited by at least eight informants, and represent the most culturally significant plants in the study area. The comparison (Table 8) reveals both overlapping and unique therapeutic applications. Plants such as Allium sativum, Calpurnia aurea, and Croton macrostachyus are widely used across multiple regions to treat blackleg, anthrax, and scabies [46, 55, 58, 67, 78], and this cross-regional consensus identifies them as strong candidates for pharmacological validation. Conversely, Euclea racemosa (19 UR for snakebite) and Jasminum abyssinicum (10 UR for jaundice) have fewer mentions in other regions [10, 63], potentially indicating regional specificity in disease prevalence or traditional knowledge. Most notably, Solanum somalense is uniquely employed in Habru District for diarrhoea and swelling, with no records of ethnoveterinary use documented in other Ethiopian regions, underscoring the value of site-specific studies for revealing under-documented taxa with novel therapeutic potential.

Growth form

The predominance of shrubs and herbs as the primary growth forms utilised for ethnoveterinary purposes in Habru District reflects the practical considerations of plant selection. Shrubs and herbs tend to grow closer to human settlements and are easier to harvest and process than trees, and their abundance in the local environment facilitates widespread use for treating livestock ailments. This observation aligns with findings from other ethnobotanical studies in which shrubs and herbs consistently emerge as primary sources of medicinal plants in both human and veterinary medicine [81]. Their faster growth cycles, ease of propagation, and often higher concentrations of bioactive compounds enhance both their sustainability as resources and their therapeutic utility across a wide range of conditions [60]. The comparatively lower use of trees may additionally reflect ecological considerations: harvesting trees carries a more significant environmental impact than harvesting shrubs or herbs, which regenerate more rapidly [38].

Methods of preparation, application, and routes of administration

The diversity of preparation and application methods documented in Habru District reflects the adaptability and depth of traditional ethnoveterinary knowledge. Aqueous crushed solutions were the most common preparation method (53.1% of UR), favoured for the simplicity of water extraction in isolating bioactive compounds and for the widespread availability of water in rural contexts [82]. Liquid coatings (19.8%), pastes (13.6%), and topical powders (4.9%) address conditions requiring direct external contact, such as swelling, skin disease, wounds, and persistent parasitic infestations, respectively. Following the methodological frameworks proposed by Disler et al. [83] and Munengwa et al. [84], the full sequence from raw material to finished product was recorded: the prevalence of aqueous solutions reflects the importance of water-extractable compounds, while the use of adjuvants such as butter and milk suggests that lipid-soluble constituents may also be relevant for certain ailments. These documented manufacturing processes provide a foundation for pharmacological replication studies, as the efficacy of a remedy may depend critically on specific processing steps.

The predominant use of oral administration reflects its practicality and the efficient absorption of bioactive compounds, consistent with findings from other ethnoveterinary studies [9, 14, 38, 74, 85]. Dermal applications, accounting for 28.4% of the total, primarily address skin conditions, wounds, and external infections, with the high proportion of dermal use reports reflecting a sound understanding of the benefits of localised topical treatment in environments where animals face harsh conditions and parasite exposure [47]. Together, the varied preparation and administration methods point to a sophisticated, culturally refined pharmacopoeia that merits further scientific investigation.

Plant parts and condition of parts used

The extensive use of diverse plant parts in Habru District reflects both the depth of traditional ethnoveterinary knowledge and the capacity to harness distinct plant components for their therapeutic potential. Leaves (46.9%) are favoured for their accessibility and their high concentrations of secondary metabolites such as alkaloids, flavonoids, and tannins, which confer antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and healing properties [54], their primacy in traditional medicine is consistent with findings from across Ethiopia [13, 54]. Roots (23.5%) are valued for their concentrated bioactive compounds, though their harvest requires ecological sensitivity to avoid threatening plant sustainability. Other components, including fruits, seeds, and latex, further illustrate the diversity of plant morphology considered in local practice [85].

Fresh plant parts are preferred because they contain higher concentrations of bioactive compounds that are thought to diminish during drying [9, 14, 38, 85], making them more effective for treating acute conditions such as infections, wounds, and gastrointestinal disorders. Nonetheless, the use of dry plant parts, whether exclusively or in combination with fresh material, ensures that treatments remain available during dry seasons or periods when certain plants are not in bloom. This dual use of fresh and dry material reflects a practical understanding of seasonal availability and an adaptive strategy for preserving the therapeutic qualities of medicinal plants throughout the year [13].

Quantitative ethnobotanical indices

The quantitative indices calculated in this study include Informant Consensus Factor (ICF), Fidelity Level (FL) and Preference Ranking (PR), provids complementary insights into the reliability, specificity, and perceived effectiveness of medicinal plant use in Habru District.

The high ICF values for gastrointestinal (0.85) and dermatological (0.81) conditions indicate strong informant consensus, consistent with patterns across sub-Saharan Africa where parasites and skin infections represent universal challenges in extensive livestock systems [79]. The high consensus for parasitic infections (ICF 0.80) similarly underscores the effectiveness of traditional treatments in controlling common parasitic diseases [86], while the consistent use of specific plants for these ailments reflects a deeply ingrained cultural confidence in their efficacy [34, 38, 39]. In contrast, lower ICF values for respiratory diseases (0.50) and nutritional/metabolic issues (0.50) suggest either lower incidence or greater treatment variability, likely reflecting the diagnostic complexity of these conditions and the diversity of taxa employed [38]. The significant overrepresentation of cattle in ethnoveterinary use reports reflects their central economic and cultural role, providing draught power, milk, meat, manure, and social capital [4]. The lower-than-expected UR for goats (20.9% observed vs. 37.8% expected) and donkeys (2.4% vs. 6.4%) may indicate lower treatment-seeking behaviour driven by species-specific economic thresholds, suggesting that livestock taxa should be treated as a key variable in ethnoveterinary research [34].

Fidelity Level values complement this picture by indicating the degree of community consensus around specific plant–ailment pairings. The highest FL values (100%) were observed for Kalanchoe petitiana A. Rich. (swelling), Ocimum lamiifolium Hochst. ex Benth. (bloating), and Ruta chalepensis L. (blackleg), reflecting unanimous community confidence in these remedies and signalling their potential as priority candidates for pharmacological validation [10, 21, 38, 46]. High FL values are widely regarded as indicators of high healing potential and targets for conservation [34, 39]. Plants with lower FL values, such as Dodonaea viscosa subsp. angustifolia (L.f.) J.G. West (57.1%) and Eucalyptus globulus Labill. (52.9%) for bloating and blackleg respectively, remain significant within local practice but exhibit greater variation in perceived effectiveness, possibly reflecting broader therapeutic use across multiple conditions or ongoing situational adaptation.

The preference ranking results for plants used to treat anthrax provide insights into the relative importance and perceived effectiveness of key taxa. Allium sativum emerged as the most preferred plant, with the highest overall score, reflecting both its widespread availability and its well-documented antibacterial properties [87]. Withania somnifera Dunal ranked second with a score of 35, consistent with its recognised immunomodulatory and anti-inflammatory properties [88]. Verbascum sinaiticum is also valued for its use against anthrax, while Justicia schimperiana and Lepidium sativum received lower preference scores, suggesting their efficacy is less well established or that they are used more commonly in combination with other treatments rather than as primary remedies. Most urgently, Solanum somalense, documented here for the first time in an ethnoveterinary context for diarrhea and swelling in cattle, represents a priority target for preliminary phytochemical screening, as no relevant pharmacological data currently exist for this taxon.

Indigenous and local knowledge on conservation practices and novel contributions

The local people of Habru District employ two main conservation strategies: home gardening and community-based forest protection. Home gardening involves cultivating medicinal plants within household compounds, reducing harvesting pressure on wild populations while ensuring ready access, an ex-situ practice documented elsewhere in Ethiopia that links traditional healthcare with sustainable resource management [21]. Complementing this, the community actively protects remnant forest patches serving as in-situ reservoirs for wild taxa, including Juniperus procera and Olea europaea subsp. cuspidata, through collective agreements reflecting communal stewardship [66]. This integrated approach conserves biodiversity and ensures continued transmission of traditional ethnoveterinary knowledge [89], aligning with international frameworks such as the Convention on Biological Diversity [18, 51]. The explicit linkage between health-centred motivations and community conservation represents an integrated resource management model suggesting that ethnoveterinary objectives can be powerful drivers of community-based stewardship.

While confirming the widespread use of many plants reported across Ethiopia (e.g., Allium sativum for anthrax), this study documents knowledge specific to Habru District. Most notably, Solanum somalense is used for diarrhoea and swelling in cattle a taxon absent from major Ethiopian ethnoveterinary compilations [38, 46, 77, 78], highlighting the value of site-specific studies for revealing under-reported taxa with pharmacological interest. Other taxa showing localised use include Jasminum abyssinicum (Jaundice) and Euclea racemosa (snakebite) [8, 21, 77, 78]. Specialised preparation methods such as heated plant compresses (Opuntia ficus-indica for anthrax) and smoke inhalation (Solanum marginatum for cough in sheep), though representing 2.4% of use reports combined, demonstrate local innovation in adapting treatments to specific clinical presentations. It is important to acknowledge that ritual and magical dimensions of such practices were not systematically captured; nonetheless, it is well established that ritual-magical practices constitute an integral dimension of traditional healing systems in Ethiopia [90]. Together, these contributions underscore the importance of in-depth, site-specific research and identify priority taxa Solanum somalense, Kalanchoe petitiana, Ocimum lamiifolium, and Ruta chalepensis—for future pharmacological investigation.

Implications for veterinary practice, public health, and sustainability

The ethnoveterinary practices documented in Habru District carry significant implications for veterinary practice, public health, and environmental sustainability. The documented pharmacopoeia offers a reservoir of potential adjunct therapies and first-aid treatments particularly for wounds, bloat, and parasitic infections where conventional products may be inaccessible and the prudent use of scientifically validated plant-based remedies could help mitigate antibiotic overuse in livestock, thereby addressing the growing concern of antimicrobial resistance as a One Health issue. From a public health perspective, healthier livestock supports food security and safer animal-source foods, contributing to stable meat, milk, and by-product supplies essential for household nutrition and economic stability in rural settings with limited access to modern veterinary services [56, 68, 86]. Environmentally, reliance on locally sourced medicinal plants, when managed sustainably as observed in Habru, promotes biodiversity conservation and reduces the ecological footprint associated with synthetic drug production and waste [86, 91, 92]. For these practices to be fully integrated into modern systems, the safety and efficacy of the most promising remedies must be scientifically validated. This study therefore underscores the need to harmonise traditional knowledge with modern veterinary practice to promote sustainable, resilient livestock production systems that safeguard both the environment and public health.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Habru District harbors rich and extensive ethnoveterinary knowledge, with 42 medicinal plant taxa documented across 27 families. The high fidelity level for Kalanchoe petitiana, Ocimum lamiifolium, and Ruta chalepensis reveals unanimous community consensus on their specific uses for swelling, bloating, and blackleg, respectively, identifying these taxa as priority candidates for pharmacological validation. The strong informant consensus for gastrointestinal and dermatological conditions indicates well-established local treatment protocols for these common ailment categories. Preference ranking further identified Allium sativum and Withania somnifera as the most preferred anthrax treatments. Notably, the study documents distinctive local knowledge, including the first ethnoveterinary report of Solanum somalense for diarrhea and swelling in cattle, and innovative preparation methods such as heated plant compresses and smoke inhalation.

Based on these findings, we recommend the following actions to preserve, validate, and sustainably utilize this ethnoveterinary knowledge:

Pharmacological validation. High-FL taxa (Kalanchoe petitiana, Ocimum lamiifolium, Ruta chalepensis) and high-preference taxa (Allium sativum, Withania somnifera) should be prioritized for phytochemical and pharmacological screening to validate their safety and efficacy.

Conservation of medicinal plant resources. Sustainable harvesting methods and community-managed home gardens should be promoted to reduce pressure on wild populations. Ecological sustainability assessments are needed for the most heavily utilized wild taxa (e.g., Euclea racemosa, Jasminum abyssinicum).

Ethical consideration on approval and consent to participate

This study was approved by the Department of Plant Biology and Biodiversity Management, Addis Ababa University. Additional permissions were obtained from the Armauer Hansen Research Institute and the Habru District Administration. Prior to any data collection, oral informed consent was obtained from all participants. The decision to use oral consent was based on the local context, where a significant proportion of informants were non-literate and oral traditions are the primary mode of information exchange. Written consent would have created barriers to participation and was deemed culturally inappropriate. The consent process was conducted in the local language (Amharic) as follows: each potential informant was informed about (1) the purpose of the research, (2) the voluntary nature of their participation, (3) their right to withdraw at any time without consequence, (4) how the information would be used (academic publication and archiving at the National Herbarium), and (5) measures taken to ensure confidentiality (anonymization of personal identifiers in all records). After this explanation, informants were asked whether they agreed to participate. Verbal agreement was documented in field notebooks, with each informant’s consent recorded by date and the interviewer’s initials. For key informants and focus group discussion participants, additional verbal consent was obtained before each session. All informants were assured that their participation was voluntary and that declining to participate would not affect their standing in the community or their access to any services. The research adhered to the ethical guidelines for ethnobiological research, including the International Society of Ethnobiology Code of Ethics (2010) and the principles outlined by the American Anthropological Association.
