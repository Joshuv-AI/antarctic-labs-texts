Cerebrovascular Pressure Restoration Rates during Recanalisation with Aspiration and Stent-Retriever Thrombectomy for Acute Ischaemic Stroke: An in Vitro Study
Authors: Ezekiel Dinama, Gilbert Gravino, Sanjeev Nayak, Ying Yang, Christine Roffe
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveMechanical thrombectomy alters cerebrovascular hemodynamics in ways that remain poorly characterized. This in vitro study investigated the pressure restoration rate (PRR), a quantitative measure of how quickly pressure returns to baseline in an occluded vessel, during thrombectomy using aspiration and stent-retriever devices in different cerebrovascular anatomical configurations.MethodsA cerebrovascular glass model with pulsatile flow was constructed to simulate good (symmetrically connected) and poor (unilateral) collateral anatomy. Pressure sensors were integrated into the system to record hemodynamic responses during thrombectomy procedures using SOFIA 5F and 6F aspiration catheters (Terumo Neuro, Aliso Viejo, CA, USA) and a Solitaire 6 × 30-mm stent retriever (Medtronic, Irvine, CA, USA). PRR was calculated in the middle cerebral artery at a 5-ms resolution and compared across devices and anatomical variants using Kruskal–Wallis tests.ResultsAspiration thrombectomy exhibited significantly higher PRRs than the stent-retriever thrombectomy across both good and poor collateral models (p <0.05). Median PRRs were 17.9 μmmHg/5 ms (SOFIA 5F), 13.5–14.3 μmmHg/5 ms (SOFIA 6F), and 0.8–1.3 μmmHg/5 ms (stent retriever). Aspiration was associated with a rapid, near-instantaneous return to baseline pressure, whereas stent-retriever thrombectomy produced a more gradual restoration with a plateau phase followed by a lower magnitude instantaneous return. Momentary reciprocal pressure fluctuations were also observed in neighboring vessels during clot removal.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that thrombectomy induces different hemodynamic flow return patterns in the targeted vessel, with technique-specific PRR values. High PRRs, particularly with aspiration, may subject compromised cerebral vessels to potentially injurious pressure surges during reperfusion. These findings suggest a possible mechanistic link between device-technique modality and complications such as hemorrhagic transformation, warranting further investigation in clinical settings.

Introduction

Mechanical thrombectomy is an effective treatment for acute ischemic stroke. However, the procedure is not without complications, with brain hemorrhage and distal embolization as potential concerns.1) There is a wide range of techniques and devices currently in use, and more are being developed.2) Ideal techniques should demonstrate a high rate of successful recanalization with the least number of attempts and be associated with a low incidence of distal embolization and hemorrhagic transformation.3)

The conventional techniques used are aspiration or stent retrieval of the thrombus, or a combination of both. The choice of device and technique is determined by availability, operator preference, vessel geometry, and collateral flow. Literature comparing the risk of adverse events from aspiration versus stent retrieval thrombectomy remains controversial, with some studies describing clear differences and others not.4–7) We hypothesize that the procedure, which involves rapid flow restoration, exposes the cerebral blood vessels to rapid changes in endovascular pressure, which could potentially have an impact on hemorrhagic risk, reperfusion injury, and distal embolization. Although validation of such a hypothesis would require rigorous evaluation of patient outcomes in relation to different pressure changes, it is sensible and prudent to first understand how hemodynamics are affected by different thrombectomy approaches in a laboratory setting.

Currently, there are no studies reporting hemodynamic pressure changes during mechanical thrombectomy. Such assessments are difficult to conduct in vivo. Therefore, the aim of this in vitro study was to assess the rate and pattern of pressure changes in an occluded vessel (the direct target for thrombectomy) and in adjacent collateral vessels (COLs).

Materials and Methods

Vessel model of the cerebral circulation

A physical pulsatile flow model adapted from Saleh et al.8) and Jakobek et al.9) was designed in glass to mimic the anterior cerebral circulation (Figs. 1 and 2). The model included the internal carotid artery (ICA), middle cerebral artery (MCA), anterior cerebral artery (ACA), and another COL.

Two different glass models were used to represent cerebrovascular variants, one with good collaterals and another with poor collaterals. The former refers to a bilaterally symmetrically connected circle of Willis with supply from both bilateral ICAs and communicating vessels connecting either side (Fig. 2A), whereas the latter refers to a unilateral system with supply from a single ICA (Fig. 2B).

The internal diameter of the vessels was 3.0 mm, and a roughened pinch point of 2.0 mm internal diameter at the M1 segment of the MCA was used to facilitate reproducible thrombus placement and occlusion. A 0.9% saline solution with osmolality similar to human blood was made to circulate within the system. This solution was heated to 37 ± 2.5°C using a hot water bath (Nickel-Electro, Weston-Super-Mare, UK). The system was pressurized using a fluid-filled reservoir placed 1220 mm above the vessel model to provide hydrostatic pressure. A peristaltic pump (Watson Marlow, Falmouth, Cornwall, UK) set at a rate of 70 rotations per minute was used to provide simulations of systole–diastole rhythms and related pressure variations. The density of 0.9% normal saline at 37°C is approximately 1.006 g/cm3, and its viscosity is approximately 1.15 centipoise (cP).9)

Clot analogue

Porcine blood was collected immediately after slaughter at an abattoir and allowed to coagulate naturally at room temperature to form red blood cell-rich clots. The thrombus was then cut into smaller pieces and frozen. At the time of the experiment, the pieces were defrosted in saline at room temperature and cut to weigh 0.20 ± 0.01g. The prepared clot was assessed for structural integrity and then introduced into the model (Supplementary Fig. 1 and Supplementary Video demonstrate the clot in vitro when occluding the vessel and after extraction).

Ethics declaration

This study did not require institutional ethics review as it involved in vitro experiments using a glass model of cerebral vasculature, with no human or live animal subjects involved.

Pressure monitoring

Four absolute pressure sensors (Akozon, Shenzhen, China) were incorporated into the cerebral circulation models to record pressure changes with different thrombectomy techniques (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2). The 1st sensor (S1) was connected to the section of the model simulating the origin of the ICA. The 2nd sensor (S2) was connected to the MCA distal to the occlusion site. Sensor 3 (S3) was connected to the ACA, and sensor 4 (S4) was connected to a COL, as shown in Fig. 2. The baseline pressures were set at physiological levels as determined by Coogan et al.10) approximately 80–115 mmHg (diastole–systole) at the MCA and 80–120 mmHg (diastole–systole) at the ICA.

Raw pressure datasets were sampled at a rate of 200 Hz and processed using MATLAB software (Simulink, Natick, MA, USA) with a moving average filter (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Pressure restoration rate calculation

Pressure restoration rate (PRR) was calculated using the following formula [1]:

[1] PRR=Pbaseline−PocclΔt(mmHg⋅5ms−1)

where Pbaseline is the restored pre-occlusion pressure, Poccl is the pressure immediately prior to recanalization (i.e., at no flow), and Δt is the duration of the recanalization phase. This interval—illustrated between points A and B in Figs. 3 and 4—corresponds to the time between the initial pressure rise (marking clot aspiration or stent deployment) and the full restoration of flow.

PRR was computed as the average pressure recovery gradient over this interval using finite differences at a temporal resolution of 5 ms, which matched the sampling rate of the pressure sensors. All PRR values are therefore expressed per 5 ms interval.

In vitro thrombectomy procedure

All devices were introduced into the system through a rotating hemostatic valve. An 8-Fr Neuron Max guide catheter (Penumbra, Alameda, CA, USA) was used in all procedures and advanced to the mid-ICA. Thrombectomies were performed with 3 different retrieval devices, including a SOFIA 5F (Terumo Neuro, Aliso Viejo, CA, USA), SOFIA 6F (Terumo Neuro), and a 6 × 30-mm Solitaire 2 FR stent retriever (Medtronic, Irvine, CA, USA). A total of 34 thrombectomy procedures were performed, evenly split between the good collateral and poor collateral models: 10 with 5F SOFIA aspiration, 12 with 6FR SOFIA aspiration, and 12 with a stent retriever. All thrombectomies included in this study involved complete recanalization.

For aspiration, the SOFIA aspiration catheters were navigated to the clot surface over a guidewire (Terumo Medical Corporation, Somerset, NJ, USA). After withdrawing the guidewire, aspiration was applied using a 20-mL Luer lock syringe. The aspiration catheter was pulled out of the system as soon as the clot was aspirated into the syringe or removed under continuous aspiration after a maximum of 90 s if the clot did not come back into the syringe.

For stent-retriever thrombectomy, an Avigo Hydrophilic microwire (ev3; Medtronic) was used to guide a Rebar-18-ev3 (Medtronic) microcatheter across the occlusion. After withdrawing the microwire, the stent retriever was advanced and deployed with its proximal segment at the occlusion site. This was left in situ for 4 minutes to maximize its integration with the clot, and then it was pulled out while applying aspiration with a 60-mL VacLok syringe (Merit Medical, South Jordan, UT, USA) through the guide catheter.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (v2024 12.1; Posit, Boston, MA, USA). Descriptive statistics, including median, interquartile range (IQR), and counts, were computed for PRR values within each device and collateral subgroup. Median PRR values were visualized using bar plots with IQR-based error bars. To assess statistical differences in PRR between devices, a Kruskal–Wallis test was conducted due to the nonparametric nature of the data. When significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Bonferroni correction applied to account for multiple testing. All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Interestingly, as demonstrated in Figs. 3 and 4, retrieval with both distal aspiration catheters and with stent retrievers resulted in short-lived reciprocal pressure changes in the adjacent vessels neighboring the MCA while the thrombus was being retrieved. A short-lived drop in pressure in all monitored vessels was also noted when removing the guide catheter.

Discussion

The results of this in vitro study quantify rapid pressure changes in the target vessel during mechanical thrombectomy. These pressure changes are significantly greater with aspiration thrombectomy when compared to stent-retriever thrombectomy (p <0.0005). The higher PRRs with aspiration versus stent retrieval are at least in part due to Δt (refer to mathematical formula [1]) being smaller for aspiration. With aspiration (Fig. 3) recanalization tends to manifest much more suddenly with a sharp instantaneous return of pressure, either as soon as the clot gets engulfed into the aspiration catheter or as soon as the corked clot (stuck at the aspiration catheter tip) is pulled back with the catheter. With stent retrieval (Fig. 4) recanalization tends to start during stent deployment (sustained for 4 minutes) when the stent displaces the clot and allows a flow channel (with a gradual and partial return of flow that plateaus), and then culminates when the clot–stent complex is retrieved with an instantaneous return of pressure to baseline. The protocols and the manner in which these devices were employed in these in vitro experiments are identical to widely accepted clinical application methods, and therefore these pressure observations should be reflective of the true nature of these techniques.

Systemic blood pressure and its variability have been shown to have an impact on complications and patient outcomes, with endothelial damage, cerebral edema, and intracranial hemorrhage being implicated.11–13) In addition, it is also established that cerebral vessels involved in stroke lose the ability to autoregulate.14) Therefore, it follows that high PRR during reperfusion may exacerbate vessel injury, especially because compromised arterioles distal to the occluded segment must accommodate a high tempo of reperfusion and a rapid return of systolic pressures. This appears to subject the vessels to sudden instantaneous radial forces, potentially damaging the endothelium and predisposing to hemorrhagic injury, which may in part explain why some patients experience hemorrhagic transformation while others do not. This would warrant the application of a technique with a more gradual and controlled return to baseline pressure, which may translate into improved patient outcomes.

Some studies have recorded more hemorrhagic transformation events in the aspiration thrombectomy group,15) while others reported more in the stent-retriever group.7,16) A meta-analysis showed that the differences noted were not significant.17,18) However, the choice of device is only one aspect of thrombectomy, and any difference between devices in the clinical setting could be confounded by many other aspects, such as thrombolysis, extent of ischemia at presentation, and treatments provided after the thrombectomy. The advantage offered by in vitro experiments is that individual parameters can be examined in isolation while maintaining other variables constant.

An aspiration catheter with a smaller lumen demonstrated a higher PRR than aspiration catheters with a larger lumen. An in vitro study by Jakobek et al. demonstrated that the placement of a larger bore aspiration catheter significantly reduced flow into the target vessel and the COLs.9) Therefore, the differences in PRR between a smaller and larger bore aspiration catheter may be explained by the latter only allowing a narrow passage of fluid on its exterior, with low pressures acting on the vessel walls and thrombus (Fig. 6). In contrast, an aspiration catheter with a smaller bore allows a larger stream of fluid around the catheter. Therefore, upon recanalization, pressure distal to the aspiration catheter builds at a faster rate with a higher PRR.

Observational analysis of pressure dynamics during mechanical thrombectomy showed a universal pattern of a momentary “dip” in pressure in the neighboring branches during thrombus removal from the MCA segment for both aspiration and stent-retriever thrombectomy (Figs. 3 and 4). This is thought to be due to a drop in resistance as soon as the clot is removed, which causes rapid decongestion of the system. This likely results in a steal phenomenon to supply the previously occluded vessel, with a decrease in flow in adjacent vessels before a return to baseline. These reciprocal pressure changes were only transient, and therefore their clinical impact is questionable, though they may still affect patient outcomes.

Limitations

The glass model is rigid compared to deformable blood vessels in vivo. Glass lacks the elastic properties and the Windkessel effect of arteries. However, the alternative option of silicone models currently available still do not accurately represent a Young’s modulus comparable to that of the cerebral arteries. The saline solution has lower viscosity than blood. Under physiological conditions, the viscosity of human blood is typically around 3–5 cP19) compared to the normal saline viscosity of 1.15 cP.20) While these limitations may have affected absolute numbers, the principle and relative pressure differences noted with the application of different devices remain relevant. Our study was limited to conventional guide catheters. Further work should also investigate the effect of balloon guide catheters (BGC) on PRR, as balloon inflation provides additional flow arrest. The resulting hemodynamics may help explain a recent trial that reported worse outcomes associated with the use of BGC.21)

Conclusion

This study investigated PRR during mechanical thrombectomy in vitro using aspiration and stent-retriever techniques. Statistically higher PRRs were observed with aspiration than with stent retrieval thrombectomy. Thrombectomy with larger bore aspiration devices showed lower PRRs compared to that with smaller bore aspiration devices, although this was not statistically significant. The pressure changes during thrombectomy and their clinical implications may be important considerations when evaluating poor patient outcomes despite successful radiological outcomes. Further studies are needed to understand the implications of pressure changes on complications and patient outcomes.
