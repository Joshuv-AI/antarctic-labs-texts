Homological stability for automorphisms of symmetric bilinear forms
Vikram Nadig

Abstract
We establish homological stability for automorphisms of symmetric bilinear forms over a class of principal ideal domains that includes all fields, the integers, the Gaussian integers, and the Eisenstein integers. In conjunction with Grothendieck-Witt theoretic calculations, this determines a large part of the stable cohomology of the odd orthogonal groups O ⟨ g , g ⟩ ​ ( ℤ ) O_{\langle g,g\rangle}(\mathbb{Z}) in low degrees.

Introduction

The cohomology of groups, and specifically of arithmetic groups, is a unifying theme within mathematics that has far-reaching applications to diverse areas like number theory, algebraic geometry, and
K
-theory. In recent times, homological stability has emerged as a nearly ubiquitous feature that these groups enjoy. From a calculational perspective, this has the consequence that knowledge of the stable (co)homology alone leads to the determination of many (co)homology groups. In practice, the stable (co)homology can be calculated by methods that are independent from those used to calculate any specific (co)homology groups. For instance, the stable (co)homology of general linear groups can be accessed through algebraic

An important class of arithmetic groups are automorphisms of quadratic and symmetric bilinear forms (i.e. orthogonal groups) on finitely generated projective modules over a number ring. The stable cohomology thereof can be accessed through Grothendieck-Witt theory. To make this more precise, let

Let us say that an element

To put this into context, let us contrast this with the case

The former establishes homological stability for orthogonal groups of hyperbolic forms over these rings, and the latter improves upon the methods of the former to allow for an arbitrary non-zero offset form in the stability results.

This is in sharp contrast to the case of symmetric bilinear forms. To the best of our knowledge, there are no homological stability results for the automorphism groups thereof in the existing literature, outside the case that the element

3
Symmetric bilinear forms and associated posets

In this section, we gather all the algebraic facts that we shall use in the sequel.

We begin with a quick review of some standard results. Much of this is basic and will be used subsequently without comment.

Recall that a symmetric bilinear form over a commutative ring
R
is a pair
(M,\lambda)
of an
R
-module
M
and a function
\lambda:M\times M\to R
that is
R
-linear in each argument and is invariant under the flip
C_{2}
-action on
M\times M
. The form
(M,\lambda)
is non-degenerate if the map
M\to M^{*}
given by
v\mapsto\lambda(v,-)
is an isomorphism. If
N\subseteq M
is a submodule such that
(N,\lambda|_{N})
is non-degenerate, then the form
(M,\lambda)
decomposes as
(M,\lambda)\cong(N,\lambda|_{N})\oplus(N^{\perp},\lambda|_{N^{\perp}})
. In particular, in this case,
(M,\lambda)
is non-degenerate if and only if
(N^{\perp},\lambda|_{N^{\perp}})
is.

Suppose
N
is a finitely generated free module and
\{e_{1},...,e_{n}\}
is a basis, then a symmetric bilinear form
\lambda^{\prime}
on
N
is completely determined by the
(n\times n)
-matrix
\lambda^{\prime}(e_{i},e_{j}),1\leq i,j\leq n
. If
(M,\lambda)
is a symmetric bilinear form and
(x_{1},...,x_{n})
is a unimodular sequence (defined towards the end of Section
2.1
) such that
\lambda(x_{i},x_{j})=\lambda^{\prime}(e_{i},e_{j}),1\leq i,j\leq n
, then
e_{i}\mapsto x_{i}
determines an embedding
(N,\lambda^{\prime})\hookrightarrow(M,\lambda)
. In this situation, if
(N,\lambda^{\prime})
and
(M,\lambda)
are non-degenerate, and
M
is finitely generated and free of the same rank as
N
, then the embedding
(N,\lambda^{\prime})\hookrightarrow(M,\lambda)
is an isomorphism because its orthogonal complement must be
0
.

Unimodular sequences in modules equipped with a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form enjoy a special property:

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
(M,\lambda)
be a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form, and
(v_{1},...,v_{n})
an element of

. Then
(v_{1},...,v_{n})
is unimodular if and only if there exist

in

such that
\lambda(v_{i},w_{j})=\delta_{i,j}
.

Proof.

The proof of
[
8
, Lem. 3.3]
, which deals with quadratic forms, goes through verbatim.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let

be a principal ideal domain. A vector

is unimodular if and only if

, where

is the

This follows from applying Proposition
3.1
in the case

to any non-degenerate form on

(for example, the standard diagonal form

).
∎

Let
(M,\lambda)
be a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form, and

The inclusion

When no confusion can arise, we will simply write

We will restrict our study to rings

or

Examples of rings satisfying this assumption include all fields and several number rings of interest like

, and

. A general class of examples and non-examples is furnished by the following result:

Proposition 3.5

Suppose

is the ring of integers of

for some square-free integer

be a non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form. Consider the subset
I(M)\coloneq\{\lambda(x,x)|x\in M\}\subseteq R
. We define the parity of
M
,
P(M)
, as the image of
I(M)
in
R/(2)
.

In particular, the parity of the zero form is
\{0\}
.

Let
U(R)
be the set of squares in
R/(2)
.

Proposition 3.10
.

Suppose
2
is not a unit in
R
. Then
U(R)
is a subfield of the ring
R/(2)
, and for any non-degenerate symmetric bilinear form
M
,
P(M)
acquires the structure of a vector space over
U(R)
. Furthermore,
P(M)
is finite dimensional over
U(R)
and its dimension is at most
\textup{rank}(M)
.

Proof.

The assertion that
U(R)
is a field is immediate from Assumption
3.4
. The vector space structure also follows from Assumption
3.4
and the observations that
\lambda(x,x)+\lambda(y,y)\equiv\lambda(x+y,x+y)(\text{mod }2)
and
\lambda(ux,ux)=u^{2}\lambda(x,x)
for
x,y\in M
and a unit
u\in R
. The second statement follows from the observation that for any basis
\{e_{1},...,e_{\text{rank}(M)}\}
of
M
, the images of the
\lambda(e_{i},e_{i})
in
R/(2)
generate
P(M)
as a
U(R)
-vector space. Indeed, for
x=\Sigma_{i=1}^{\text{rank}(M)}x_{i}e_{i}
, we notice that
\lambda(x,x)\equiv\Sigma_{i=1}^{\text{rank}(M)}x_{i}^{2}\lambda(e_{i},e_{i})(\text{mod }2)
and the assertion then follows from Assumption
3.4
.
∎

Lemma 3.11
.

For forms
M
and
M^{\prime}
, we have
P(M\oplus M^{\prime})=P(M)+P(M^{\prime})
. In particular,
P(M^{n})=P(M)
for all
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

Obvious.
∎

In what follows, we will sometimes view
\theta
as a function valued in forms, and not in isomorphism classes thereof.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let

. Then
\theta(r)\oplus\theta(r+s)\cong\theta(r)\oplus\theta(s)
.

Proof.

By checking on the standard basis, it is evident that the map
\psi:\theta(r)\oplus\theta(r+s)\to\theta(r)\oplus\theta(s)
given in standard coordinates by
((x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2}))\mapsto((x_{1},x_{2}+y_{2}),(y_{1}-x_{1}-rx_{2},y_{2}))
is an isomorphism of forms.
∎

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
r_{1},...,r_{n}\in R
. The image of the set
\{\Sigma_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}^{2}r_{i}|u_{i}\in R^{\times}\cup\{0\}\}
under the quotient map

is
P(\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\theta(r_{i}))
.

Proof.

By definition,
I(\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\theta(r_{i}))=\{2x_{1}x_{2}+r_{1}x_{2}^{2}+...+2x_{2n-1}x_{2n}+r_{n}x_{2n}^{2}|(x_{1},...,x_{2n})\in R^{2n}\}
. The assertion immediately follows from Assumption
3.4
.
∎

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
M
and
M^{\prime}
be metabolic forms. Then
M\cong M^{\prime}
if and only if
\textup{rank}(M)=\textup{rank}(M^{\prime})
and
P(M)=P(M^{\prime})
.

Proof.

The “only if” direction is obvious. Let us turn to the converse.

By Propositions
3.7
and
3.8
, we can write
M\cong\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\theta(r_{i})
and
M^{\prime}\cong\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\theta(r^{\prime}_{i})
, for
r_{i},r^{\prime}_{i}\in R
. Assume without loss of generality that
r_{i}=r^{\prime}_{i}
for
i\leq k
and
r_{i}\neq r^{\prime}_{i}
for
i>k
, for some
k\in\{0,...,n-1\}
. We will show that the sequences
(r_{1},...,r_{n})
and
(r^{\prime}_{1},...,r^{\prime}_{n})
can then be modified to sequences
(s_{1},...,s_{n})
and
(s_{1}^{\prime},...,s_{n}^{\prime})
, respectively, such that
M\cong\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\theta(s_{i})
,
M^{\prime}\cong\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\theta(s^{\prime}_{i})
, and
s_{i}=s^{\prime}_{i}
if

.

Let us delineate two cases.

•

Suppose
P(\oplus_{i=1}^{k}\theta(r_{i}))=P(M)=P(M^{\prime})
. By Lemma

, there exists a subset
J\subset\{1,...,k\}
and for each

, a unit

such that
\Sigma_{j\in J}u_{j}^{2}r_{j}\equiv r_{k+1}(\text{mod }2)
. If

is not empty, using
\theta(r_{j})=\theta(u_{j}^{2}r_{j})
, Proposition
3.8
and Lemma

(repeatedly), we see that
\oplus_{i=1}^{k+1}\theta(r_{i})\cong\oplus_{i=1}^{k}\theta(r_{i})\oplus\theta(0)
. This decomposition also clearly holds when

is empty. Similarly,
\oplus_{i=1}^{k+1}\theta(r^{\prime}_{i})\cong\oplus_{i=1}^{k}\theta(r^{\prime}_{i})\oplus\theta(0)
, and we are done.

•

Else, let us assume without loss of generality that the image of
r^{\prime}_{k+1}
in
R/(2)
does not belong to
P(\oplus_{i=1}^{k}\theta(r_{i}))
. Since
P(M)=P(M^{\prime})
, it follows from Lemma

that there exists a subset
J\subset\{1,...,n\}
such that
J^{\prime}\coloneq J\cap\{k+1,...,n\}
is non-empty, and units
u_{j}\in R
for each

such that
\Sigma_{j\in J}u_{j}^{2}r_{j}\equiv r^{\prime}_{k+1}(\text{mod }2)
. Assume without loss of generality that

. As in the previous case, using
\theta(r_{j})=\theta(u_{j}^{2}r_{j})
, Proposition
3.8
and Lemma
3.12
(repeatedly), we can replace

by
\Sigma_{j\in J^{\prime}}u_{j}^{2}r_{j}
. By the same reasoning together with our assumption that

, we can also replace
r_{k+1}
by
\Sigma_{j\in J^{\prime}}u_{j}^{2}r_{j}
. Therefore we have arranged for

if

∎

Example 3.15
.

Let us illustrate that the above rank-parity classification of metabolic forms does not hold in general for rings, and even principal ideal domains, that do not satisfy Assumption

. We note that the definition of parity (Definition
3.9
) makes sense for forms defined over any ring. Let
S
be the ring of integers of
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{37})
. It is known that
S
is a PID, and that its fundamental unit is
6+\sqrt{37}
. We see from Proposition
3.5
that
S
does not satisfy Assumption
3.4
. Moreover, from the proof of Proposition
3.5
, we learn that
S/(2)\cong\mathbb{F}_{4}
and that the set of squares in
S
surjects onto
S/(2)
, while the square units are all congruent to
1
modulo
2
. Let
\alpha=\frac{1+\sqrt{37}}{2}\in S
. These observations show that the metabolic planes over
S
given by

\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\text{ and }\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&\alpha\end{pmatrix}

have the same parity. We claim that these are not isomorphic. Indeed, if they were, there would exist
x_{1},x_{2}\in S
with
2x_{1}x_{2}+\alpha x_{2}^{2}=1
. This relation forces
x_{2}
to be a unit, so
x_{2}^{2}\equiv 1(\text{mod }2)
. Reducing the previous relation modulo

however yields that
\alpha x_{2}^{2}\equiv 1(\text{mod }2)
, and we have arrived at a contradiction because
\alpha\not\equiv 1(\text{mod }2)
.

We will crucially use the following notion to associate a meaningful notion of parity to isotropic unimodular sequences.

Definition 3.16

Let

. A sequence

\lambda(v_{i},w_{j})=\delta_{ij}

when

, for

Note, by Proposition
3.1

A witnessing sequence

to

determines an embedding

e_{v,w}:T_{1}\oplus...\oplus T_{k}\to N

where

is isomorphic to

for both the embedding and its image when the context is clear. As a preliminary observation, we note that the isomorphism type of the orthogonal complement

does not depend on the choice of witnessing sequence

Lemma 3.17
.

Let

and
(w_{1}^{\prime},...,w_{k}^{\prime})
are witnessing sequences to it. Then
e_{v,w}^{\perp}\cong e_{v,w^{\prime}}^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Choose a basis
\{a_{1},...,a_{n}\}
of

. The elements
a^{\prime}_{i}\coloneqq a_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\lambda(a_{i},w^{\prime}_{j})v_{j}

and satisfy

, thereby furnishing a map

, which is an isomorphism because
\text{rank}(e_{v,w}^{\perp})=\text{rank}(e_{v,w^{\prime}}^{\perp})
.

Lemma 3.34

Let

. Suppose

. There exists a witnessing sequence

-vector space

Proof.

We start with any witnessing sequence

5
Applications

Let
R
be a ring satisfying Assumption
3.4
. We recall from the introduction that the symmetric Grothendieck-Witt theory of
R
can be defined as the group completion

\mathcal{GW}^{s}(R)\coloneq(\text{Unimod}^{s}(R)^{\simeq},\oplus)^{\text{grp}}

We also recall that a form
M\in\text{Unimod}^{s}(R)^{\simeq}
is said to be
cofinal
if for any
N\in\text{Unimod}^{s}(R)^{\simeq}
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
and
N^{\prime}\in\text{Unimod}^{s}(R)^{\simeq}
such that
N\oplus N^{\prime}\cong M^{n}
. As explained in the introduction, the stable (co)homology of orthogonal groups of cofinal forms can be accessed through Grothendieck-Witt theory by means of the group completion theorem.

We begin this section by establishing the criterion for the existence of cofinal forms stated in the introduction (Theorem
1.2
).

Theorem 5.1
.

The following statements are equivalent:

1.

There exists a cofinal form in
\textup{Unimod}^{s}(R)^{\simeq}
that is metabolic.

2.

There exists a cofinal form in
\textup{Unimod}^{s}(R)^{\simeq}
.

3.

There exists a form
M
in
\textup{Unimod}^{s}(R)^{\simeq}
such that
P(M)=R/(2)
.

4.

If
2
is not invertible in
R
,
R/(2)
is finite dimensional over
U(R)
.

In this case, a metabolic form is cofinal if and only if its parity equals
R/(2)
.

Proof.

\underline{(1)\implies(2)}
: Obvious.

\underline{(2)\implies(3)}
: If
M
is cofinal, then for each
r\in R/(2)
, there exists
n
such that
\theta(r)\hookrightarrow M^{n}
. Thus,
r\in P(\theta(r))\subset P(M^{n})=P(M)
by Lemma
3.11
, and
P(M)=R/(2)
.

\underline{(3)\implies(4)}
: This follows from Proposition
3.10
.

\underline{(4)\implies(1)}
: As noted in the introduction, metabolic forms are cofinal in all forms: given
N\in\text{Unimod}^{s}(R)^{\simeq}
, we let
-N
be the form with the same underlying
R
-module as
N
, and
\lambda_{-N}(x,y)=-\lambda_{N}(x,y)
for all
x,y
; then
N\oplus(-N)
is metabolic with the diagonal
N\to N\oplus(-N)
serving a Lagrangian. Therefore, it suffices to exhibit a metabolic form that is cofinal among all metabolic forms. Since metabolic forms are closed under direct sums and classified by their rank and parity (Theorem
3.14
), it follows that if a metabolic form
M
has parity
R/(2)
, then it is cofinal. We can build such an
M
as follows - if
2
is invertible, we can take
M\coloneq H
; else we choose a
U(R)
-spanning set
r_{1},...,r_{n}
of
R/(2)
and put
M\coloneq\oplus_{i=1}^{n}\theta(r_{i})
(Lemma
3.13
).

The remaining assertion in the statement of the theorem follows immediately from a direct examination of the arguments above.
∎

An immediate application of this result is that a field of characteristic
2
admits a cofinal form if and only if it is finite over its Frobenius. We proceed to discuss more examples below.

Example 5.2
.

1.

The form
\theta(1)
is cofinal over
\mathbb{Z}
as
U(\mathbb{Z})=\mathbb{Z}/(2)
. This may also be deduced from the classical fact that non-degenerate symmetric bilinear forms over the integers are classified by their rank, signature, and parity (see
[
26
, Chp. 5, Sec. 2.2, Thm. 6]
).

2.

The rank one form
[1]
is cofinal over any perfect field
F
of characteristic
2
. Indeed, because every element is a square, the set
S(F)
has two elements and thus the form
\theta(1)
is cofinal. Now,
\theta(1)\cong\text{diag}(1,-1)\cong\text{diag}(1,1)
, whence the statement follows.

3.

The form
\text{diag}(1,i)
is cofinal over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
. To see this, observe that
U(\mathbb{Z}[i])=\mathbb{F}_{2}
and
\mathbb{Z}[i]/(2)\cong\mathbb{F}_{2}[x]/(x^{2})
, so
\theta(1)\oplus\theta(i)
is cofinal. Further,
\theta(1)\cong\text{diag}(1,1)
and
\theta(i)\cong\text{diag}(i,i)
, and the statement follows.

4.

The form
\theta(1)\cong\text{diag}(1,-1)
is cofinal over
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
. This is because
U(\mathbb{Z}[\omega])=\mathbb{Z}[\omega]/(2)
, as we explained in the course of proving Proposition
3.5
.

As before, let us restrict ourselves to constant coefficients in our homological stability statements for the sake of brevity.

For the remainder of this section, we will assume that
R
satisfies the conditions of Theorem

. In this case, let

be

or

depending on whether

.

Suppose

and the minimal rank assumption. Moreover,

∎

As indicated by Example

. Then for any non-degenerate rank one subform
E\subseteq D
, the form
M\oplus E
is isomorphic to a diagonal form.

Proof.

We make a preliminary observation: every non-degenerate diagonal form over
\mathbb{Z}[i]
is of the form
[1]^{n}\oplus[i]^{m}
, and
z([1]^{n}\oplus[i]^{m})=\lfloor n/2\rfloor+\lfloor m/2\rfloor
, as can be checked using the isomorphisms
\theta(1)\cong\text{diag}(1,1)
and
\theta(i)\cong\text{diag}(i,i)
, together with Corollary
3.21
and the fact that the isotropic rank of the diagonal form
\text{diag}(1,i)
equals
0
. In particular, every diagonal form of rank at least
3
has non-zero isotropic rank.

Choose a decomposition
M\cong M^{\prime}\oplus M^{\prime\prime}
such that
z(M^{\prime})=0
and
M^{\prime\prime}
is metabolic (using Corollary
3.21
). An iterative application of Lemma
5.5
shows that the
M^{\prime\prime}\oplus[r]
is isomorphic to a diagonal form for
r\in\{1,i\}
. Therefore, we reduce to the case that
z(M)=0
.

We may assume without loss of generality that
D
is metabolic. Corollary
3.21
gives
z(M)=z(D^{\prime})-\text{rank}(D)/2\geq\frac{\text{rank}(D^{\prime})-\text{rank}(D)}{2}-1=\frac{\text{rank}(M)-2}{2}
, with the inequality following from the first paragraph. Thus, we only have to deal with the case
\text{rank}(M)=2
.

Let
E
be a non-degenerate rank one subform of
D
, and consider the form
N\coloneq M\oplus E
. There exists a diagonal form
E^{\prime}
(for instance, the orthogonal complement of
E
in
D
) such that
N\oplus E^{\prime}\cong D^{\prime}
. Since
\text{rank}(N)=3
, the previous paragraph informs us that
z(N)=1
. Hence,
N
has a summand in
\text{Met}_{2}(R)
and it follows that
N
is isomorphic to a diagonal form by Lemma
5.5
.
∎

Theorem 5.7
.

Let
R=\mathbb{Z}[i]
and
F=\theta(r)
for
r\in\{1,i\}
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{F}
be the full subgroupoid of
\textup{Unimod}^{s}{(\mathbb{Z}[i])}^{\simeq}
consisting of
0
and all diagonal forms
M
with
g_{F}(M)\geq 1
. Then
\mathcal{D}_{F}
is
F
-cancellative.

Proof.

Conditions 1, 2 and 3 of Definition
3.26
are immediate (recall
F\cong\text{diag}(r,r)
).

To verify Condition 4, suppose
M\oplus F\in\mathcal{D}_{F}
and
g_{F}(M)\geq 1
. Writing
M=N\oplus F\cong N\oplus\text{diag}(r,r)
, an application of Lemma
5.6
shows that
N\oplus[r]
is isomorphic to a diagonal form, from which it follows that
M\in\mathcal{D}_{F}
.

It remains to check Condition 5. Suppose
M,N\in\mathcal{D}_{F}
satisfy
M\oplus F\cong N\oplus F
. Clearly
M=0
if and only if
N=0
. Otherwise,
\text{rank}(M)=\text{rank}(N)
, and since
g_{F}(M)
and
g_{F}(N)
are at least
1
, we also have
P(M)=P(M\oplus F)=P(N\oplus F)=P(N)
. The fact that
M\oplus F\cong N\oplus F
also implies that both
M
and
N
have the same discriminant. The maps in the proof of Lemma
5.5
furnish an isomorphism
\text{diag}(1,1,1,i)\cong\theta(0)\oplus[i]\oplus[1]\cong\text{diag}(i,i,i,1)
, whence it follows that diagonal forms of the same parity, rank, and discriminant are isomorphic. Thus
M\cong N
.
∎

Corollary 5.8
.

Let
R=\mathbb{Z}[i]
. For constant coefficients
{A}
, the map

H_{i}(O(\textup{diag}(1,i)^{n}),{A})\to H_{i}(O(\textup{diag}(1,i)^{n+1}),{A})

is an isomorphism if
i\leq\frac{n-12}{2}
.

Proof.

Let
M
be the form
\text{diag}(1,i)^{2}
; it is the cofinal metabolic form of minimal rank (see Example
5.2
). Theorem
5.3
tells us that the map
H_{i}(O(M^{n}),A)\to H_{i}(O(M^{n+1}),A)
is an isomorphism if
i\leq n-6
. The key observation is that both
M^{n}
and
M^{n}\oplus\text{diag}(1,i)
are objects of the
F
-cancellative groupoid
\mathcal{D}_{F}
(Theorem
5.7
), where
F=\theta(1)
or
\theta(i)
. Thus, the stabilisation-by-
M
map, which can be written as a composite of stabilisations by
\theta(1)
and
\theta(i)
,

H_{i}(O(M^{n}\oplus\text{ diag}(1,i)),A)\to H_{i}(O(M^{n}\oplus\text{ diag}(1,i)\oplus\theta(1)),A)\to H_{i}(O(M^{n+1}\oplus\text{ diag}(1,i)),A)

is an isomorphism if
i\leq n-6
. Consequently, in the sequence

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H_{i}(O(\text{diag}(1,i)^{2n}),A)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H_{i}(O(\text{diag}(1,i)^{2n+1}),A)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H_{i}(O(\text{diag}(1,i)^{2n+2}),A)}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces{H_{i}(O(\text{diag}(1,i)^{2n+3}),A)}}

where each arrow is the stabilisation-by-
\text{diag}(1,i)
map, we gather that the composites of the first two and the last two arrows are isomorphisms if
i\leq n-6
. Therefore, each arrow is an isomorphism by the
2
-out-of-
6
property, proving the corollary.
∎

One can similarly deduce, using Proposition
5.4
in place of Theorem
5.7
, homological stability with respect to stabilisation by the form
[1]
in the case that
R
is a finite field of characteristic
2
.

We conclude with calculations of the low-dimensional cohomology of the cofinal form
\theta(1)
for
R=\mathbb{Z}
, as stated in the introduction (Corollary
1.5
). Let
\textup{O}_{\langle n,n\rangle}(\mathbb{Z})
denote the group of isometries of the form
\theta(1)^{n}
.

Corollary 5.9
.

For an odd regular prime
p
, there are maps

\mathbb{F}_{2}[v_{i},w_{i},a_{i}|i\geq 1]\to H^{*}(\textup{O}_{\langle n,n\rangle}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{F}_{2})

\mathbb{F}_{p}[p_{i}^{+},p_{i}|i\geq 1]\otimes_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}\Lambda_{\mathbb{F}_{p}}[y_{i}|i\geq 1]\to H^{*}(\textup{O}_{\langle n,n\rangle}(\mathbb{Z}),\mathbb{F}_{p})

where the
v_{i},w_{i}
have degree
i
, the
p_{i}^{+}
have degree
4i
, the
p_{i}
have degree
2(p-1)i
, the
y_{i}
have degree
(p-1)(2i-1)
, and the
a_{i}
have degree
2i-1
, that are isomorphisms in degree
\leq\frac{n-9}{2}
.

Proof.

Both assertions follow from Theorem
5.3
coupled with calculations in other work - the first from
[
13
, Theorem 8.1.9]
and the second from upcoming work of Hebestreit, Land, and Nikolaus (
[
12
]
).
∎
