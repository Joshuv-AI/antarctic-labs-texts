How I do it: preoperative simulation and augmented reality assisted surgical resection of Glioblastoma in Broca’s area
Authors: Jiahe Guo, Peihai Zhang, Kai Zhang, Xuejun Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe emergence of virtual reality and augmented reality makes preoperative simulation and intraoperative real-time guidance possible, especially for lesions in functional areas.MethodPreoperatively, the virtual reality technique allowed the simulation of tumor resection while visualizing the fiber tracts. During resection, we used augmented reality to localize the terminations of dorsal language pathways and strictly followed the preoperative plan to perform the surgery. Gross total resection and favorable functional outcome were finally achieved.ConclusionThis case demonstrates that preoperative simulation and augmented reality can truly assist in glioma surgery in functional areas.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00701-024-06368-5.Keywords: Preoperative simulation, Augmented reality, Glioblastoma, Broca’s area

Introduction and relevant surgical anatomy

Groundbreaking observations published by Pierre Broca defined the region of word production, currently known as Broca’s area, an essential part of the classic language model [9]. Severe motor aphasia can develop after injury to Broca’s area or frontal operculum surgery. To achieve “maximal safe resection”, intraoperative stimulation mapping (ISM) should be universally applied in glioma surgery in Broca’s area as standard of care [2]. However, general anesthesia is possible if the patient is not able to tolerate awake craniotomy or has poor preoperative speech function. Without ISM guidance, surgery in functional areas is highly dependent on the understanding of functional anatomy and meticulous surgical planning by the neurosurgeon. Augmented reality (AR) has been incorporated into neurosurgical practice, offering visualization and surgical planning solutions that have been proven to enhance surgical outcomes [4]. Luzzi and colleagues conducted a series of studies employing AR tractography to evaluate glioma resection and prognosis, particularly within the motor area [6, 7]. However, there are no studies that specifically address the preservation of functional integrity in the language area during the resection of gliomas with the aid of augmented reality (AR) guidance. In this report, we detail a case of successful resection of glioblastoma in Broca’s area, achieved through meticulous preoperative planning and the use of intraoperative three-dimensional (3D) AR guidance.

A 68-year-old man was admitted to this hospital because of speech difficulty with memory loss and personality change for six months. The preoperative magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed enhancing margin in T1-weighted image with contrast enhancement (T1CE) (Fig. 1A), hyperintensive cystic area in fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) image (Fig. 1B) and no restriction for the cystic component in diffusion weighted image (Fig. 1C). The cystic glioblastoma in Broca’s area was highly suspected. Unfortunately, the patient was unable to complete a functional MRI or cooperate with awake surgery. The patient underwent diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) test, and tumor-associated fiber tracts were reconstructed based on the DTI (Fig. 2). Well-registered volumetric imaging revealed that the tumor grows anteromedial to the ventral precentral gyrus (vPCG), which perfectly matched the anterior terminations of the arcuate fasciculus (AF) and third branch of the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF-III). The tumor failed to invade the uncinate fasciculus (UF) and frontal aslant tract (FAT).

Radiological findings. Preoperative MRI revealed a large cavitating tumor with a enhancing nodule and thin wall measuring about 5.8 × 4.5 cm (A, T1CE), associated with surrounding vasogenic edema (B, FLAIR), significant mass effect, and restricted diffusion (C, DWI) in the left frontal lobe

The preoperative surgical plan integrated an enhanced component of the tumor (yellow), cystic component (purple), cerebral arteries (red), and language-associated fiber tracts, including frontal aslant tract (light blue), uncinate fasciculus (dark blue), arcuate fasciculus (light green), and superior longitudinal fasciculus III (dark green). In recent years, mounting evidence has demonstrated the lesion’s resectability within or near Broca’s area [1]. Several studies have already proved substantially convergence of speech arrest area in the anterior vPCG with the anterior terminations of the AF and the SLF-III [5, 10]. Therefore, it is possible to remove deep glioma in Broca’s area without causing neurological deficit, while ensuring the integrity of the dorsal language fiber tracts and the vPCG.

Thus, we conducted a preoperative simulation using virtual reality. With the aid of the 3D-AR guidance, vPCG with the anterior terminations of the AF and the SLF-III was located intraoperatively. Consequently, the tumor was safely removed. Three days after the surgery, the patient’s speech function was improved.

Description of the technique

Preoperative simulation

The resulting DTI images were preprocessed in standard workflows (including eddy-current and motion correction, phase distortion correction, generalized Q-sampling imaging reconstruction) using the DSI Studio (http://dsi-studio.labsolver.org). Deterministic fiber-tracking algorithm was used to reconstruct AF, SLF-III, FAT and UF as following parameters (FA threshold: 0.2, angular threshold: 45 degrees, step size = 1 mm, track length > 100 mm). All fiber tracts were saved in anatomical imaging (T1CE/FLAIR) space.

Preoperative reconstruction of tumor and relevant fiber tracts (Fig. 2) showed that lateral part of the tumor invaded Broca’s area and adhered closely to the vPCG and the anterior terminations of AF/SLF-III. Due to the anterior terminations of AF overlapping with the anterior terminations of SLF-III in fiber tracts reconstruction, only the AF was reserved in order to simplify following surgical simulation. The tract file of the AF and the patient’s anatomical imaging (T1CE) was imported into the Medivis SurgicalAR system (Medivis, New York, NY).

The left frontotemporal craniotomy was planned. After rendering, the patient head model was rotated to right side with the pterion area upwards in the virtual space. The HoloLens2 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA) was then linked to the Medivis SurgicalAR system. The surgeon used the HoloLens2 to recognized the localizer. The brush type: erase was selected in the menu interface. The incision line is outlined based on tumor projections. The scalp, temporal muscle, and skull flap were all erased. To better illustrate the transcortical approach to remove the lesion, we striped the skull and then exposed the brain surface. The anterior terminations of the AF were clearly revealed on the vPCG. A small corticotomy was made, and the tumor was entered. During tumor resection, en passage arteries, may supplying vPCG, should be preserved to prevent postoperative ischemia. The cystic fluid would be drained from a puncture in the real surgery. The cyst wall was removed at last. The matched images between preoperative simulation and real surgical procedures have been demonstrated (Fig. 3).

Intraoperative augmented reality

Under general anaesthesia, the patient was placed supine on an operating table with the head fixed in a Mayfield 3-point holder and rotated to the contralateral side. Anatomical registration to the Medivis SurgicalAR system was performed based on preoperative plan (Fig. 4A&B). With the head-mounted AR system, the surgeon outlined the tumor boundary on the patient’s scalp, and designed a personalized incision of frontotemporal approach. The craniotomy was made in the regular steps, and then the inferior frontal gyrus and vPCG were exposed. Once the vPCG and the anterior terminations of the AF were identified by applying 3D-AR system (Fig. 4C), following a small corticotomy on the pars opercularis, the tumor was entered and debulked. The en passage arteries between tumor and vPCG were preserved well. After removing the lateral solid part of the tumor, we punctured and drain the cystic fluid under 3D-AR navigation. Following the cyst decompression, the surgeon finally stripped the inner cyst wall (Fig. 4D). Scenarios depicting the use of 3D-AR system in surgery were presented (Fig. 5A-D).

Indications

Once feasible, awake craniotomy is recommended for surgical resection of gliomas in the eloquent regions [8]. However, sometimes, asleep craniotomy is also an option. Surgical resection of gliomas in the Broca’s area under asleep craniotomy is subject to the following criteria: (1) patients who are aged or have multiple comorbidities cannot tolerate awake surgery; (2) patients with poor preoperative speech function fail to cooperate with intraoperative language tests; (3) cortical projections of AF/SLF-III perfectly match vPCG, while tumor doesn’t infiltrate AF/SLF-III and vPCG (Fig. 6A). Because Zhao et al. [10] showcased the concordance between anterior terminations of AF/SLF-III and speech output area, which indicated cortices without AF/SLF-III projection can be considered silent areas without the voice output function. However, when AF/SLF-III displace or mismatch with vPCG (Fig. 6B&C), awake craniotomy remains the first choice if feasible. Furthermore, the anatomical landmarks on the brain’s surface are often indistinct due to cerebral edema during brain tumor surgery. Augmented reality can aid us in directly grasping the 3D perspective of fiber tract projections, even with minimal prior knowledge, thus facilitating a more seamless surgical procedure.

Limitations

In this case, the virtual head model was still a rigid-body in the preoperative simulation, which failed to imitate the deformations during the surgical resection. During surgery, it remains essential to alternate between head-mounted displays and microscopes, a practice that may not be familiar to some neurosurgeons. It is essential to highlight that fiber tractography is not always dependable [3]. Three dimensional AR fiber tractography should be cautiously applied in clinical practice.

How to avoid complications

Firstly, awake craniotomy should be chosen as much as possible for patients with eloquent glioma to achieve “maximal safe resection”. Secondly, a surgeon should carefully review the anatomical relationship between lesions and functional structures before a surgery, if awake craniotomy is unavailable. Thirdly, preoperative simulation is beneficial for creating a surgical plan and assisting junior surgeons in getting acquainted with the surgical pathway during complex cases with deep lesions (i.e., lateral ventricles) and narrow approaches (i.e., trancortical approach), while 3D-AR system can guide our surgeons to localize predefined areas in the surgery.

Specific information for the patient

Patients are required to fully understand the potential risks of permanent speech function impairment. It is also available for patients to learn about the surgical procedure virtually to relieve preoperative anxiety.

Ten key points summary

1. Broca’s area is crucial for language production, and damage to this area can lead to severe motor aphasia.

2. Awake craniotomy remains the first choice for patients with eloquent glioma if feasible.

3. Gliomas involving Broca’s area can be removed under general anaesthesia for patients with preoperative severe language deficits or poor surgical tolerance.

4. Concordance between anterior terminations of AF/SLF-III and vPCG is the key to perform an successful asleep craniotomy to avoid complications.

5. Surgeons should carefully review the anatomical relationship between tumors, fiber tracts, and functional cortical areas to fully understand the risk of asleep craniotomy.

6. The surgery in Broca’s area should be proceeded meticulously, with special attention to preserving en passage arteries and ensuring the integrity of the surrounding language fiber tracts.

7. Preoperative assessments included DTI, which helped reconstructing tumor-associated fiber tracts, providing critical insights into the relationship between the glioma and relevant brain structures.

8. Preoperative simulation is useful to develop a surgical plan and to help junior surgeons familiarize with the surgical pathway in complicated cases.

9. Utilizing 3D-AR system allows for a detailed intraoperative identification of key anatomical structures.

10. 3D-AR system can be used for educational purposes by allowing medical students and residents to visualize complex neuroanatomy in a more interactive and immersive way.
