A new hierarchy for complex plane curves
Takuro Abe, Alexandru Dimca, Piotr Pokora

Abstract
. We define the type of a plane curve as the initial degree of the corresponding Bourbaki ideal. Then we show that this invariant behaves well with respect to the union of curves. Curves of type 0 0 are precisely the free curves, while curves of type 1 1 are the plus-one generated curves. In this paper, we first show that line arrangements and conic-line arrangements can exhibit all the theoretically possible types. In the second part, we study the properties of the curves of type 2 2 and construct families of line arrangements and conic-line arrangements of this type.

1.
Introduction

Let
S=\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]
be the polynomial ring in three variables
x,y,z
with complex coefficients, and let
C:f=0
be a reduced curve of degree
d\geq 3
in the complex projective plane
\mathbb{P}^{2}
. The ring
S
and all the graded
S
-modules considered below are graded with respect to the natural grading.
We denote by
J_{f}
the Jacobian ideal of
f
, i.e. the homogeneous ideal in
S
spanned by the partial derivatives
f_{x},f_{y},f_{z}
of
f
, and by
M(f)=S/J_{f}
we denote the corresponding graded quotient ring, called the Jacobian (or Milnor) algebra of
f
.
Consider the graded
S
-module of Jacobian syzygies of
f
, namely

D_{0}(f)=\{(a,b,c)\in S^{3}\ :\ af_{x}+bf_{y}+cf_{z}=0\}.

According to Hilbert’s Syzygy Theorem, the graded Jacobian algebra
M(f)
has a minimal resolution of the form

(1.1)

0\to F_{3}\to F_{2}\to F_{1}\to F_{0},

where clearly
F_{0}=S
,
F_{1}=S^{3}(1-d)
and the morphism
F_{1}\to F_{0}
is given by

(a,b,c)\mapsto af_{x}+bf_{y}+cf_{z}.

With this notation, the graded
S
-module of Jacobian syzygies
D_{0}(f)
has the following minimal resolution

0\to F_{3}(d-1)\to F_{2}(d-1).

We say that
C:f=0
is an
m
-syzygy curve
if the module
F_{2}
has rank
m
. Then the module
D_{0}(f)
is generated by
m
homogeneous syzygies, say
r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{m}
, of degrees
d_{j}=\deg r_{j}
ordered such that

1\leq d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq\ldots\leq d_{m}.

We call these degrees as the
exponents
of the curve
C
, and
r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}
as a
minimal set of generators
for the module
D_{0}(f)
. Note that
d_{1}=mdr(f)
is the minimal degree of a non-trivial Jacobian relation in
D_{0}(f)
. It is known that

(1.2)

d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq d_{3}\leq d-1,

see for instance
[
17
, Theorem 2.4]
.
On the other hand, for line arrangements we have a better bound, namely

(1.3)

d_{m}\leq d-2,

see
[
26
, Corollary 3.5]
, and for conic-line arrangement we have

(1.4)

d_{m}\leq d-1,

see
[
9
, Corollary 1.5]
.

Let
I_{f}
denote the saturation of the Jacobian ideal
J_{f}
with respect to the maximal ideal
{\bf m}=(x,y,z)
in
S
. Consider the following local cohomology group, usually called the Jacobian module of
f
,

N(f)=I_{f}/J_{f}=H^{0}_{\bf m}(M(f)).

We set
n(f)_{k}=\dim N(f)_{k}
for any integer
k
and we introduce the
freeness defect of the curve

C
by the formula

\nu(C)=\max_{j}\{n(f)_{j}\}

as in
[
17
]
.
Note that
C
is free if and only if
N(f)=0
and hence
\nu(C)=0
, and
C
is nearly free if and only if
\nu(C)=1
, see for instance
[
6
,
15
]
for the definitions of free and nearly free curves.
Recall that the total Tjurina number of a given curve
C\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
is defined to be

\tau(C)=\sum_{p\in{\rm Sing}(C)}\tau_{p},

where
{\rm Sing}(C)
denotes the set of all singular points of
C
, and
\tau_{p}
is the Tjurina number of the singularity
(C,p)
.

Consider the general form of the minimal resolution for the Milnor algebra
M(f)
of a curve
C:f=0
that is assumed to be not free, namely

(1.5)

0\to\oplus_{i=1}^{m-2}S(-c_{i})\to\oplus_{j=1}^{m}S(1-d-d_{j})\to S^{3}(1-d)\to S,

with
c_{1}\leq\ldots\leq c_{m-2}
and
d_{1}\leq\ldots\leq d_{m}
.
It follows from
[
20
, Lemma 1.1]
that one has

(1.6)

c_{j}=d+d_{j+2}-1+\varepsilon_{j},

for
j=1,\ldots,m-2
and some integers
\varepsilon_{j}\geq 1
. Using
[
20
, Formula (13)]
, it follows that one has

(1.7)

d_{1}+d_{2}=d-1+\sum_{j=1}^{m-2}\varepsilon_{j}.

This formula implies the following result, see
[
17
, Theorem 2.3]
for details.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
C:f=0
be a reduced plane curve of degree
d
and let
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
be the minimal degrees of a minimal system of generators for the module of Jacobian syzygies
D_{0}(f)
as above.
Then the following conditions hold:

(1)

The curve
C
is free if and only if
d_{1}+d_{2}=d-1
.

(2)

The curve
C
is plus-one generated if and only if
d_{1}+d_{2}=d
.

(3)

The curve
C
is neither free nor plus-one generated if and only if
d_{1}+d_{2}>d
.

For the definition of plus-one generated curves, see
[
1
,
17
]
.
In view of this result, it seems natural to introduce the following invariant, which leads to an interesting hierarchy among all plane curves.

Definition 1.2
.

Let
C:f=0
be a reduced plane curve of degree
d
and let
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
be the two smallest degrees of a minimal system of generators for the module of Jacobian syzygies
D_{0}(f)
as above.
We then say that
C
has type
t(C)
, where
t(C)
is a positive integer defined by the formula

t(C)=d_{1}+d_{2}+1-d.

The fact that this invariant plays a key role in the study of plane curves
follows from the following alternative definition. Let
C:f=0
be a reduced curve in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
and
r_{1}
be a minimal degree generator of the graded
S
-module
D_{0}(f)
. Then the corresponding Bourbaki ideal
B(C,r_{1})\subset S

of
D_{0}(f)
was introduced in

[
18
, Section 5]
, and
[
17
, Section 3]
, after being considered
in a non-explicit way by du Plessis and Wall in
[
19
]
, and the first author in
[
6
]
. Its properties were further explored in the recent paper
[
22
]
. It follows from the definition of the Bourbaki ideal
B(C,r_{1})
that the type of the curve
C
is just the initial degree of the
homogeneous ideal
B(C,r_{1})
, namely

(1.8)

t(C)=\indeg B(C,r_{1})=\min\{s\in\mathbb{Z}\ :\ B(C,r_{1})_{s}\neq 0\}.

Recall that the Bourbaki degree
\Bour(C)
of the curve
C
is defined by

(1.9)

\Bour(C)=\deg B(C,r_{1})

and it satisfies the equality

(1.10)

\Bour(C)=(d-1)^{2}-d_{1}(d-d_{1}-1)-\tau(C),

see
[
18
, Theorem 5.1 (1)]
and
[
22
, Theorem 2.1]
.
It follows that, if
d_{1}\leq(d-1)/2
, then

(1.11)

\Bour(C)=\nu(C),

see Theorem
2.1
below.

Note that a curve
C
is free exactly when
t(C)=0
and also if and only if
\Bour(C)=0
.
On the other hand, a curve
C
is plus-one generated exactly when
t(C)=1
. A plus-one generated curve is a 3-syzygy curve with exponents
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
satisfying
d_{1}+d_{2}=d
, see
[
17
, Theorem 2.3 (2)]
. For such a curve one has

(1.12)

\tau(C)=(d-1)^{2}-d_{1}(d-d_{1}-1)+d_{2}-d_{3}-1,

see
[
17
, Proposition 2.1 (4)]
. It follows that, for a plus-one generated curve
C
with exponents
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
one has

(1.13)

\Bour(C)=d_{3}-d_{2}+1,

and hence
\Bour(C)=1
if and only if
d_{2}=d_{3}
. This occurs when
C
is a nearly free curve, which is a special type of plus-one generated curve.

The type of a curve is a positive integer bounded by
2d-2
in view of (
1.2
), and this upper bound is attained, for instance, by a smooth curve. For line arrangements the corresponding upper bound is

2d-4
in view of (
1.3
), see Corollaries
1.8
and
1.10
below for more on this subject.

Our first result says that the type behaves well with respect to the union of two curves. Consider two reduced curves
C_{1}:f_{1}=0
and
C_{2}:f_{2}=0
, with no common irreducible component. Then one has the following easy result, concerning the union curve
C=C_{1}\cup C_{2}:f=f_{1}\cdot f_{2}=0
, see
[
14
, Theorem 5.1]
.

Proposition 1.3
.

With the above notation, one has

\max\{mdr(f_{1}),mdr(f_{2})\}\leq mdr(f)\leq\min\{mdr(f_{1})+\deg C_{2},mdr(f_{2})+\deg(C_{1})\}.

The type of curves behaves similarly with respect to the union of curves,
namely we have the following result.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
C_{1}:f_{1}=0
and
C_{2}:f_{2}=0
be two curves in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
with no irreducible component in common. Then the union curve
C=C_{1}\cup C_{2}:f=f_{1}\cdot f_{2}=0
satisfies

t(C)\leq\min\{t(C_{1})+\deg(C_{2}),t(C_{2})+\deg(C_{1})\}.

Remark 1.5
.

If
C_{1}
is free and
C_{2}
is a line, then this result is already known – see
[
8
,
23
]
.

Corollary 1.6
.

If
C
is a free curve and
Q
is a conic intersecting
C
in a finite number of points, then the union
C^{\prime}=C\cup Q
is a curve of type at most
2
.
Similarly, if
C
is a plus-one generated curve and
L
is a line, which is not an irreducible component of
C
, then the union
C^{\prime}=C\cup L
is a curve of type at most
2
.

The next result tells us what happens for the type
t(C)
, in most cases, when the curve
C_{2}
is generic,
namely smooth and transversal to
C_{1}
.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
C_{1}:f_{1}=0
be a curve and let
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
be the two smallest degrees of a minimal system of generators for the module of Jacobian syzygies
D_{0}(f_{1})
.
Let
C_{2}:f_{2}=0
be a smooth curve such that the intersection
C_{1}\cap C_{2}
consists of
\deg C_{1}\cdot\deg C_{2}
points. If

d_{2}\leq\deg C_{1}-2,

then the union curve
C=C_{1}\cup C_{2}:f=f_{1}\cdot f_{2}=0
satisfies

t(C)=t(C_{1})+\deg C_{2}.

More precisely, if
d_{1}^{\prime}\leq d_{2}^{\prime}
denote the first two smallest degrees of a minimal system of generators for the module of Jacobian syzygies
D_{0}(f)
, then

d_{1}^{\prime}=d_{1}+\deg C_{2}\text{ and }d_{2}^{\prime}=d_{2}+\deg(C_{2})\leq\deg(C)-2.

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be a line arrangement in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
containing at least 3 lines. Then one has

0\leq t({\mathcal{A}})\leq\deg({\mathcal{A}})-3.

Conversely, for any integers
t
and
d
such that
0\leq t\leq d-3
,
there are line arrangements in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
such that
t({\mathcal{A}})=t
and
\deg({\mathcal{A}})=d
.

Proving a result similar to Corollary
1.8
for conic-line arrangements is more complicated. The smooth conic
C_{1}
in Example
3.4
satisfies
t(C_{1})=d-1
, while the conic-line arrangement
C
from the same example satisfies
t(C)=d-2
. It is easy to check that 2 smooth conics meeting in 4 points give rise to conic-line arrangement
{\mathcal{C}}
with
t({\mathcal{C}})=2=d-2
, since
d=\deg({\mathcal{C}})=4
.
We believe that such examples are exceptional, and ask the following question.

Question 1.9
.

Is it possible to list all the conic-line arrangements
{\mathcal{C}}
satisfying

t({\mathcal{C}})\geq deg({\mathcal{C}})-2\ ?

On the positive side, we have the following analog of Corollary
1.8
for conic-line satisfying an additional condition.

Corollary 1.10
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}:f=0
be a conic-line arrangement and let
d_{1}\leq d_{2}
be the two smallest degrees of a minimal system of generators for the module of Jacobian syzygies
D_{0}(f_{1})
.
If
d_{2}\leq deg({\mathcal{C}})-2
, then

0\leq t({\mathcal{C}})\leq\deg({\mathcal{C}})-3.

Conversely, for any integers
t
and
d
such that
0\leq t\leq d-3
,
there are conic-line arrangements
{\mathcal{C}}
containing at least one conic in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
such that
t({\mathcal{C}})=t
and
\deg({\mathcal{C}})=d
.

In particular, for any degree
d\geq 3
, there are free conic-line arrangements
{\mathcal{C}}
containing at least one conic such that
\deg({\mathcal{C}})=d
.

Before presenting further results, we would like to make the following observation regarding the type.

Remark 1.11
.

The type in the class of line arrangements is not determined by the combinatorics.
Indeed, the pair of arrangements

{\mathcal{A}}:f=xy(x-y-z)(x-y+z)(2x+y-2z)(x+3y-3z)(3x+2y+3z)

(x+5y+5z)(7x-4y-z)=0

and

{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=xy(4x-5y-5z)(x-y+z)(16x+13y-20z)(x+3y-3z)(3x+2y+3z)

(x+5y+5z)(7x-4y-z)=0

constructed by Ziegler
[
32
]
and Yuzvinski
[
31
]
have the same combinatorics, but the exponents are

(5,6,6,6)
and
(6,6,6,6,6,6)
, respectively. Therefore, the type of
{\mathcal{A}}
is
3
, while the type of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
is
4
.

One can make a loose analogy of the type of curves introduced in Definition
1.2
with Arnold’s notion of modality of function germs. In the case of function germs, the
0
-modal (alias the simple singularities), the
1
-modal (alias the unimodal singularities) and the
2
-modal singularities are classified, see for instance
[
2
]
, and the complexity of the corresponding geometry, e.g. reflected in their Milnor lattices, is increasing as the modality increases.

Following this path, from now on in this note on we focus on curves
C
having type
2
. The exponents and the corresponding Tjurina numbers of the curves of type
2
are described in the following result, where the notation from (
1.7
) is used.

Proposition 1.12
.

Let
C
be a curve of type
2
.
Then one of the following two cases occurs.

(1)

C
is a 3-syzygy curve and
\varepsilon_{1}=2
. If
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
are the exponents of
C
, then

\tau(C)=d_{1}^{2}+d_{1}d_{2}+d_{2}^{2}-2d_{1}-2d_{2}-2d_{3}.

(2)

C
is a 4-syzygy curve and
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=1
. If
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4})
are the exponents of
C
, then

\tau(C)=d_{1}^{2}+d_{1}d_{2}+d_{2}^{2}-2d_{1}-2d_{2}-d_{3}-d_{4}+1.

Conversely, if
m=3
and
\varepsilon_{1}=2
or
m=4
and
\varepsilon_{1}=\varepsilon_{2}=1
, then
t(C)=2
.

In case
(1)
(resp.
(2)
),
we say that
C
is a curve of type

2A
(resp.
2B
).
For the curves
C
of type 2, we also have simple formulas for the freeness defect
\nu(C)
, as shown below.

Proposition 1.13
.

Suppose that
C
has type
2A
and exponents
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
.
Then the following hold.

(1)

If
d_{1}<d_{2}-2
, then

\nu(C)=2(d_{3}-d_{2})+4\geq 4.

(2)

If
d_{1}=d_{2}-2
, then

\nu(C)=2(d_{3}-d_{1})\geq 4.

(3)

If
d_{1}=d_{2}-1
, then

\nu(C)=2(d_{3}-d_{1})+2\geq 4.

(4)

If
d_{1}=d_{2}
, then

\nu(C)=2(d_{3}-d_{2})+3\geq 3.

Proposition 1.14
.

Suppose that
C
has type
2B
and exponents
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3},d_{4})
.
Then the following hold.

(1)

If
d_{1}<d_{2}-2
, then

\nu(C)=d_{3}+d_{4}-2d_{2}+3\geq 3.

(2)

If
d_{1}=d_{2}-2
, then

\nu(C)=d_{3}+d_{4}-2d_{1}-1\geq 3.

(3)

If
d_{1}=d_{2}-1
, then

\nu(C)=d_{3}+d_{4}-2d_{1}+1\geq 3.

(4)

If
d_{1}=d_{2}
, then

\nu(C)=d_{3}+d_{4}-2d_{2}+2\geq 2.

The equality in Proposition
1.14
(4) holds if and only if

d_{1}=d_{2}=d_{3}=d_{4}
, see also
[
17
, Theorem 3.11 and Example 3.12]
in relation with such curves.

It is natural to ask the question whether in Corollary
1.6
, in both cases, the two types
2A
and
2B
are possible for the curve
C^{\prime}
.
Our next result gives a rather general construction to start with a plus-one generated line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
and get arrangements
{\mathcal{A}}
of type
2B
, by adding a generic line.

Theorem 1.15
.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
be a line arrangement of type 1 with exponents
(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})
with
m_{1}\geq 3
. Let
L
be a line such that the intersection
L\cap{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
consists of
\deg{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}=m_{1}+m_{2}
points.
Then the line arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}\cup L
has type
2B
and the exponents of
{\mathcal{A}}
are

(m_{1}+1,m_{2}+1,m_{3}+1,m_{1}+m_{2}-1).

In the next result, we consider a line arrangement as a special type of conic-line arrangement, i.e. a conic-line arrangement where the number of conics is zero. The example of conic-line arrangement constructed in Theorem
1.16
is very different from the conic-line arrangements
{\mathcal{C}}
constructed in the proof of Corollary
1.10
, since here all the singularities situated on the conic are quasi-homogeneous, which is not the case for the singularity
({\mathcal{C}},(0:0:1))
in general, see Remark
3.5
.

Theorem 1.16
.

Let
{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
be a free conic-line arrangement with exponents
(m_{1},m_{2})
with
m_{1}\geq 2
. Let
Q
be a smooth conic such that the intersection
Q\cap{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}
consists of
2\deg{\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}=2(m_{1}+m_{2}+1)
points.
Then the arrangement
{\mathcal{C}}={\mathcal{C}}^{\prime}\cup Q
has type
2A
with exponents
(m_{1}+2,m_{2}+2,m_{1}+m_{2}+1)
.

Remark 1.17
.

It is interesting to note that the formula for
\tau(C)
in Proposition
1.12
(1) can be obtained by setting
d_{4}=d_{3}+1
in formula for
\tau(C)
in Proposition
1.12
(2). Similarly, all the formulas for

in Proposition

can be obtained by setting

in the corresponding formulas for

in Proposition

.
In particular, the sets of exponents

. For this reason the Tjurina number and the freeness defect cannot be used to show that the type

To get families of line arrangements of type

of type

Proposition 1.18
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}_{1}
(resp.

{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{B}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{B}}_{2}

and exponents

, this directly implies the following.

If we add a generic line to the line arrangement

(n_{1}+1,n_{2}+1,n_{1}+n_{2}-1,n_{1}+n_{2}-1).

to the arrangement
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
in Proposition

, passing through exactly one double point of
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
.

.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}
be the line arrangement from Proposition

. Let

.

Finally, we present an enumerative description of line arrangements of type
2
. Let us first fix the notation. Let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
be an arrangement of
d
lines. We denote by
t_{k}:=t_{k}(\mathcal{L})
the number of
k
-fold intersection points of
\mathcal{L}
, and we define the maximal multiplicity of
\mathcal{L}
as follows:

m(\mathcal{L})={\rm max}\{k:\text{arrangement }\mathcal{L}\text{ admits }k\text{-fold points}\}.

Theorem 1.21
.

Let
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
be an arrangement of
d
lines of type
2
. Then one has

m(\mathcal{L})\geq\bigg\lceil\dfrac{4d}{d+5}\bigg\rceil.

Moreover, the following hold.

(1)

If
\mathcal{L}
has type
2A
and exponents
(d_{1},d_{2},d_{3})
, then

(2)

The content of this paper is outlined below. Section 2 provides a recap of some necessary preliminaries. The subsequent sections then proceed to prove these results and provide a number of useful examples and related additional remarks.

2.
Some preliminaries

With the notation from Introduction, we have the following result.

Theorem 2.1

(

)

Let

2.4.
Exact sequences for line arrangements

Consider the triple of line arrangements
({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime})
, where

{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}={\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{L\}
with

being a line in
{\mathcal{A}}

{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime}

corresponding to the intersection set

By a change of coordinates we can take

{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}:f^{\prime}=0,\ {\mathcal{A}}:f=0\text{ and }{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime}:f^{\prime\prime}=0,

for some polynomial

S
-modules, sometimes called the Euler exact sequence of the triple

(2.8)

0\to D(f^{\prime})(-1)\to D(f)\to D(f^{\prime\prime}),

where
D(f^{\prime})
,
D(f)
and
D(f^{\prime\prime})
are as in Remark
2.3
and in addition

is regarded as an
S
-module using the restriction of scalars given by the ring morphism
\psi:S\to R
, which replaces
x
by
0
in any polynomial

. Here the first morphism

D(f^{\prime})(-1)\to D(f)
is the multiplication by
x
, and the second morphism is given by

\rho:D(f)\to D(f^{\prime\prime}),\ \rho(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=(\psi(a_{2}),\psi(a_{3})),

see
[
25
, Proposition 4.45]
and
[

, Lemma 2.6]
that covers a more general case when

is a conic-line arrangement, maybe under some quasi-homogeneity conditions.
If we replace the modules

,

and

by the modules

,
\overline{D}(f)
and
\overline{D}(f^{\prime\prime})
, the sequence
obtained from (

, we get the following short exact sequence

0\to E_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(-1)\to E_{{\mathcal{A}}}\to i_{*}E_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime}}\to 0,

where we followed the notation from (

), see
[

. Using this exact sequence we can give the following useful
sufficient condition for the sequence (

to be surjective.

Theorem 2.5
.

With the above notation, let

0\rightarrow\oplus_{i=1}^{m-2}S[-c^{\prime}_{i}]\rightarrow\oplus_{i=1}^{m}S[-d_{i}]\rightarrow D_{0}({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})\rightarrow 0

be a minimal free resolution of
D_{0}({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})
. If
|{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime}|>c_{i}^{\prime}-1
for all
i
, then
the Euler exact sequence

0\rightarrow D({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})[-1]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\cdot x}}{{\rightarrow}}D({\mathcal{A}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho}}{{\rightarrow}}D({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime})

is right exact.

Proof.

Let
|{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime}|=n
, and note that this implies
|{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}|\geq n
.
Then the
S
-module
D({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime})
has two generators, one of degree
1
and another of degree
n-1
. The first element is the Euler derivation, which is always in the image of
\rho
.
It follows that

D({\mathcal{A}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\rho}}{{\rightarrow}}D({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime})

is surjective if and only if the induced morphism

\overline{D}({\mathcal{A}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\overline{\rho}}}{{\rightarrow}}\overline{D}({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime\prime})

is surjective.
Moreover, it suffices to show that
\overline{\rho}
is surjective in degree
n-1
. Using the exact sequence (
2.9
), it suffices to show that

H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2},E_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(n-2))=0
.
By the minimal free resolution of
D_{0}({\mathcal{A}}^{\prime})
, we have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2},\oplus\mathcal{O}(-d_{i}+n-2))\rightarrow H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2},E_{{\mathcal{A}}^{\prime}}(n-2))

\displaystyle\rightarrow

\displaystyle H^{2}(\oplus\mathcal{O}(-c^{\prime}_{i}+n-2))\simeq H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{2},\oplus\mathcal{O}(c^{\prime}_{i}-n+2-3))=0.

The last vanishing follows by our assumption, and the first one from the fact that
H^{1}(\mathbb{P}^{2},\mathcal{O}(k))=0
for any integer

. This completes the proof.

∎

{\alpha}\in D(f)
such that
\rho({\alpha})
is the generator of
D(f^{\prime\prime})
of degree
n-1
. The element
xE
is discarded from the union since we have
E
in this union.

6.
The proof of Theorem
1.21

Let us recall that by
[
13
, Theorem 2.1]
if
C
is a reduced curve of degree
d
in
\mathbb{P}^{2}
having only quasi-homogeneous singularities, then

d_{1}\geq\alpha_{C}\cdot d-2,

where
\alpha_{C}
denotes the Arnold exponent of
C
. Let us recall that the Arnold exponent of a given curve
C
is the minimum over all log canonical thresholds of singular points of
C
. Coming back to our setting, since for line arrangements all singular points are quasi-homogeneous, we can use the aforementioned result, but first we need to find
\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}
. Using
[
3
, Theorem 1.3]
, we see that

\alpha_{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{2}{m(\mathcal{L})},

and we get

d_{1}\geq\frac{2d}{m(\mathcal{L})}-2.

Since
d_{1}\leq(d+1)/2
, we finally obtain

m(\mathcal{L})\geq\bigg\lceil\dfrac{4d}{d+5}\bigg\rceil.

To prove the claim in (1), we use Proposition
1.12
(1).
Since
d^{2}=d_{1}^{2}+d_{2}^{2}+2d_{1}d_{2}-2d_{1}-2d_{2}+1
, we get

\tau(\mathcal{L})=d^{2}-d_{1}d_{2}-2d_{3}-1.

Recall that

\tau(\mathcal{L})=\sum_{r\geq 2}(r-1)^{2}t_{r}=\sum_{r\geq 2}(r^{2}-2r+1)t_{r}.

Since
d^{2}-d=\sum_{r\geq 2}(r^{2}-r)t_{r}
, we have

\tau(\mathcal{L})=d^{2}-d+\sum_{r\geq 2}(-r+1)t_{r}.

This gives us

d^{2}-d+\sum_{r\geq 2}(-r+1)t_{r}=d^{2}-d_{1}d_{2}-2d_{3}-1,

and finally

\sum_{r\geq 2}(r-1)t_{r}=d_{1}d_{2}-d+2d_{3}+1=d_{1}d_{2}-d_{1}-d_{2}+2d_{3}+2.

The claim follows now from the fact that
d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq d_{3}
.

To prove the claim in (2), we use Proposition
1.12
(2). As above we get

\tau(\mathcal{L})=d^{2}-d_{1}d_{2}-d_{3}-d_{4}.

This yields

d^{2}-d+\sum_{r\geq 2}(-r+1)t_{r}=d^{2}-d_{1}d_{2}-d_{3}-d_{4},

and hence

\sum_{r\geq 2}(r-1)t_{r}=d_{1}d_{2}+d_{3}+d_{4}-d_{1}-d_{2}+1.

The claim (2) follows, since
d_{1}\leq d_{2}\leq d_{3}\leq d_{4}
.

.

We start by recalling
[

. Let us consider the line arrangement
\mathcal{L}\subset\mathbb{P}^{2}
given by

This arrangement has

with

has

we have equality in Theorem
1.21
(2).
