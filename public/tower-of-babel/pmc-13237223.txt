The value of multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging in the prognostic judgment of prostate cancer
Authors: Yanyan Yu, Xiao Feng, Yuchen Wei, Shengchen Jiang, XingQing Qin, Yi Li, QiuYing Wei, Yuanfang Tao, Jin-yuan Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBoth the Gleason grading system, which assesses prostate cancer aggressiveness, and the D’Amico risk classification, which predicts the likelihood of tumor recurrence, require post-surgical data to provide results. This highlights the significant research need for non-invasive methods of preoperative prediction of tumor aggressiveness and recurrence risk. To help fill this need, we investigated the correlation between various multiparametric magnetic resonance imaging (mpMRI) parameters and the ISUP 2014 Gleason grade group and D’Amico risk classification for prostate cancer. We prospectively collected pelvic mpMRI data from 561 patients who presented with elevated prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels at our hospital. After we semi-quantitatively scored primary prostate lesions on a five-point scale according to the PI-RADS v2.1 standard, we post-processed diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) images to quantitatively measure the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values of the primary lesions. We then calculated Kendall’s tau-b correlation coefficients to assess correlations between the above MR parameters and the ISUP 2014 Gleason grade group and D’Amico risk classification. Our analysis indicates that several mpMRI parameters are correlated with the International Society of Urologic Pathologists (ISUP) 2014 Gleason grade group and the D’Amico risk classification in prostate cancer. In particular, ADC values and PI-RADS scores show strong correlations with the ISUP 2014 Gleason grade group. mpMRI parameters may serve as non-invasive surrogate markers, aiding in preoperative risk stratification for prostate cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48419-0.Keywords: Prostate cancer, Multiparametric MRI, PI-RADS v2.1, Prognostic, Risk stratificationSubject terms: Urology, Prostate

Introduction

According to the data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer, in 2022, prostate cancer ranked fifth globally in terms of incidence, second in terms of incidence and mortality among male patients, and first in terms of new cases1. Prostate cancer incidence increased by 2–3% per year in the U.S. from 2015 to 20192. The use of PSA levels, which is common in screening for prostate cancer, has a high rate in detecting prostate cancer, especially in men over the age of 70; however, it remains controversial due to its association with overdiagnosis3. mpMRI has been proven to be a valuable diagnostic tool before biopsy in men who are clinically suspected of having prostate cancer4.

The AdMeTech Foundation, the European Society of Uroreproductive Radiology (ESUR), and the American College of Radiology (ACR) have recommended the use of the Prostate Imaging-Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) v2.1 in order to simplify and standardize the reporting of mpMRI prostate cancer imaging. PI-RADS v2.1 has become a universal criterion for the reporting and interpretation of prostate mpMRI5,6. Moreover, the 2016 World Health Organization Blue Book and the International Society of Urological Pathology recommend the use of the Gleason grading system7. The Gleason score is the strongest related predictor of prostate cancer recurrence; the higher the grouping grade, the lower the differentiation of the tumor; the more aggressive the tumor, the worse the prognosis8.

The EAU-EANM-ESTRO-ESUR-SIOG guidelines continue to use the European Association of Urology risk group classification and the 2017 tumor-node-metastasis prostate cancer staging classification, also known clinically as the D’Amico risk classification, to categorize the biochemical recurrence risk after localized prostate treatment9. The D’Amico risk classification10 is determined by clinical indicators such as the Gleason score, PSA level, and tumor T stage. If one or more of these three indicators are met, patients are classified into an ISUP 2014 Gleason grade group, which is the most important indicator in determining the treatment plan according to the treatment guidelines. This classification is built on clinical indicators for predicting the biochemical recurrence risk after localized prostate treatment. Although this classification relies solely on clinical indicators, it holds great research value regarding whether mpMRI can be used to non-invasively predict the risk of tumor invasiveness and biochemical recurrence. To investigate this possibility, we explored the correlation between mpMRI parameters and ISUP 2014 Gleason grade, as well as the D’Amico risk grade of prostate cancer.

Results

Study patients

Among the 634 potential patients included in this research, 51 were excluded due to incomplete clinical or imaging data, while another four were excluded for having non-prostate adenocarcinoma of one of the following types: prostate sarcoma, stromal tumor of the prostate, small cell carcinoma of the prostate, or neuroendocrine tumor of the prostate. Additionally, 19 patients were excluded for being on oral medication, leaving 561 eligible patients included in this study (Fig. 1; Table 1).

Further analysis

Kendall’s tau-b correlation coefficient was used to assess the correlation between the variables. A strongly negative correlation was found between the ADC values and Gleason grade group of prostate cancer (t = − 0.730, P < 0.001), and a negative correlation was found between the ADC values and D’Amico risk grade of prostate cancer (t = − 0.394, P < 0.001). The prostate-specific antigen density (PSAD), T2WI score, and PI-RADS score were moderately and strongly positively correlated with the Gleason grade group of prostate cancer (t = 0.404, 0.593, and 0.620, respectively, P < 0.001) and with the D’Amico risk grade of prostate cancer (t = 0.584, 0.453, and 0.548, respectively, P < 0.001), as shown in Table 5. The results of the univariable analysis and ROC analysis for each variable are summarized in Table 6. The inter-radiologist agreement for PI-RADS v2.1 scoring was substantial, with a Cohen’s kappa of 0.630 (95% CI 0.583–0.677; P < 0.001).

Discussion

Both clinicians and patients are highly concerned about the recurrence risk after treatment for localized prostate cancer, which influences treatment methods and surgical approaches, as well as patients’ confidence and expectations regarding their treatment. Currently, clinicians must determine the tumor’s aggressiveness and clinical staging through pathological results. The 2014 ISUP Gleason score is the strongest predictive factor for prostate cancer recurrence, with higher scores and grade groups indicating lower tumor differentiation, greater aggressiveness, and poorer prognosis8. Clinically, the D’Amico classification method, which predicts biochemical recurrence based on PSA levels, tumor staging, and pathological Gleason score, stratifies patients into low, intermediate, and high-risk categories. Radical prostatectomy (RP) or radiation treatment is considered the gold standard for treating medium- and high-risk prostate cancer, whereas active surveillance is deemed a reasonable choice for low-risk lesions. However, the desired outcomes of both types of treatment can only be confirmed postoperatively through pathological analysis. Therefore, non-invasive preoperative prediction of tumor aggressiveness and recurrence risk is particularly important.

The PSA level is a recognized and widely used biological marker for prostate cancer prognosis. With advancements in PSA testing technology, its sensitivity has significantly improved. The current major controversy surrounding the use of PSA level is that it leads to overtreatment3. In this study, we found a strongly positive correlation between PSAD and the D’Amico risk classification (Kendall’s tau-b = 0.584, P < 0.001), likely due to the significant relationship between PSAD and the PSA basis of the D’Amico classification. In contrast, we found a moderate correlation between PSAD and Gleason grading (Kendall’s tau-b = 0.404), indicating that PSAD cannot distinguish between tumors that are of high and low aggressiveness. These findings align with those of Lamy et al11.

The 2020 EAU-EANM-ESTRO-ESUR-SIOG guidelines indicate that mpMRI is a key tool for optimizing biopsies and that combining it with PSAD and PI-RADS scores can help identify patients who require a biopsy9,12. In recent years, researchers have increasingly attempted to use mpMRI to non-invasively predict the aggressiveness and recurrence risk of prostate cancer13–16. One study that examined the differentiation between category 4 lesions and category 5 lesions based on PI-RADS v2 found that category 5 lesions were correlated with extraprostatic extension (EPE) and higher Gleason scores17. Based on their findings, Kim et al. suggested that PI-RADS v2 can be used to predict long-term outcomes after radical prostatectomy (RP) for prostate cancer18. Another retrospective study indicated that tumor localization with PI-RADS v2.1 can also predict prognosis after RP19. To our knowledge, no study has examined the value of semi-quantitative and quantitative parameters of PI-RADS v2.1 in predicting aggressiveness and recurrence risk of prostate cancer. To fill this study gap, we used semi-quantitative PI-RADS v2.1 scores based on mpMRI to predict Gleason grading and biochemical recurrence risk and to guide clinical treatment and prognostic assessment with promising results. We found a strong positive correlation between PI-RADS v2.1 scores and T2WI scores obtained from T2WI and fat-suppressed sequences with Gleason grading (Kendall’s tau-b = 0.630, 0.593, P < 0.001) and a moderately positive correlation with D’Amico risk classification (Kendall’s tau-b = 0.453, 0.480, P < 0.001). These results indicate that MRI-based imaging information has predictive value for the differentiation and recurrence risk of prostate cancer, consistent with the findings of Anwar et al.20. We also found that lower ADC levels indicate poorly differentiated prostate cancer, whereas higher ADC levels suggest well-differentiated prostate cancer, demonstrating an inverse relationship between ADC levels and tumor differentiation (t = − 0.730, P < 0.001). These findings are similar to those of Shaish21. Additionally, we found that ADC levels were moderately negatively correlated with the D’Amico risk classification for prostate cancer, indicating that lower ADC levels were related with a higher biochemical recurrence risk (t = − 0.394, P < 0.001).

However, our study has several limitations. First, we did not have postoperative follow-up data; therefore, we used the clinical D’Amico risk classification as a surrogate endpoint, which limits the ability to directly evaluate the prognostic value of the investigated parameters for true recurrence outcomes. Second, this was a retrospective single-center study, and selection bias may be present; thus, the generalizability of our findings warrants external validation in multicenter prospective cohorts with standardized imaging acquisition and long-term follow-up. Third, ADC measurements were performed by a single senior radiologist and repeated three times with the mean value used for analysis; we did not conduct a formal intra- or inter-observer reproducibility analysis for ADC measurements. Therefore, measurement variability across readers could not be fully quantified.

Despite these limitations, our analysis revealed that multiple mpMRI parameters were associated with pathological aggressiveness and clinical risk stratification in prostate cancer. These findings support the potential role of mpMRI–derived metrics as practical, non-invasive tools to aid preoperative risk stratification. In future work, we plan to conduct multicenter prospective studies incorporating postoperative biochemical recurrence outcomes to further validate the prognostic utility and clinical applicability of these parameters. It is also noteworthy that, as the application of artificial intelligence and radiomics in prostate MRI continues to expand, the quantitative or semi-quantitative MRI parameters used in this study can serve as readily available and clinically interpretable baseline features and be integrated with high-dimensional imaging features extracted by radiomics or deep learning models. Such integration may further improve the robustness and accuracy of prognostic prediction while preserving interpretability, and warrants systematic evaluation in future studies.

Methods

Participants

Prospective data were collected from 634 patients with elevated PSA who underwent prostate mpMRI scans at our hospital between June 2015 and December 2020. These cases were confirmed by pathology and met the inclusion criteria, with imaging and clinical indicators such as serum total PSA documented. This study is a bidirectional study and has been approved by the Ethics Review Committee of our hospital. All methods were carried out in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations and with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) without endocrine, radiotherapy, or other non-surgical treatment before prostate MRI examination and (2) prostate lesions confirmed by pathology within 1 month after prostate MRI. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) PSA levels lacking specific values (e.g., > 100 ng/ml), (2) poor-quality MRI images affected by motion artifacts or metal interference, (3) prior prostate biopsy (repeat biopsy cases).

Examination methods

The pelvic mpMRI was performed using Siemens MAGNETOM Verio and Prisma 3.0 T scanners with body phased-array coils in accordance with the PI-RADS v2.1 guidelines. Scanning sequences included T1WI, T2WI, DWI, and dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI). Patients were instructed to retain a small volume of urine. The scan was centered 1 cm above the pubic symphysis, covering at least the prostate and seminal vesicles. Scan parameters are provided in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

Analysis of images

Image analysis was performed according to the PI-RADS v2.1 standard6. The scoring and identification of the primary lesion (either the largest lesion or one with extracapsular extension) were conducted on a PACS workstation. Two radiologists with three and twelve years’ experience in prostate MRI, respectively, assessed each case on a five-point scale according to the PI-RADS v2.1 scoring criteria, without knowledge of the pathological findings. For subsequent analyses, we used the PI-RADS v2.1 score assigned by the senior radiologist (12 years of experience).

Measurement of prostate volume and calculation of PSAD

According to the PI-RADS v2.1 standard, prostate volume (PV) was measured according to the following formula, assessing the maximum anteroposterior and craniocaudal diameters on the mid-sagittal T2WI images and the maximum transverse diameter on the axial T2WI images: PV = maximum transverse diameter × maximum anteroposterior diameter × maximum craniocaudal diameter × 0.52; PSAD = PSA/PV, with units in ng/ml2 ref.6.

Principles for placing the ROI in DWI

The following procedure was followed for placement: (1) Place the ROI at the center of the primary lesion (the largest lesion or the lesion with extracapsular extension)6. For non-lesional areas, place the ROI at the lowest signal intensity within the bilateral transition zones and peripheral zones and calculate the average value. (2) Place the ROI in the area with the lowest ADC levels and the most pronounced early enhancement within the suspected primary lesion. (3) The ROI should be circular, with an area of approximately 3 × 3 mm2. (4) Avoid regions of hemorrhage, calcification, blood vessels, and the urethra. Record the size and parameter values of each ROI, and repeat the measurements three times to obtain the average value by a senior radiologist.

Scoring criteria

T2WI was the primary sequence for evaluating the transition zone (TZ), while DWI was a secondary sequence. The TZ score was primarily based on the T2WI score. A combined score of 3 was assigned only when the DWI score was 4 or 5 and the T2WI score was 2; when the DWI score was 5 and T2WI score was 3, a combined score of 4 was assigned. DWI was the primary sequence for evaluating the peripheral zone (PZ), while DCE was a secondary sequence. The PZ score was primarily based on the DWI score. A combined score of 4 was only assigned when the DWI score was 3 and DCE was positive. The lesion with the highest PI-RADS score was considered the primary focus, and its score was used as the final prostate score. If multiple lesions had similarly high PI-RADS scores, the primary focus was the lesion with evident extracapsular extension. A higher PI-RADS score indicated a greater likelihood of csPCa. A score of 1 indicated a very low likelihood, 2 indicated a low likelihood, 3 indicated uncertainty, 4 indicated a high likelihood, and 5 indicated a very high likelihood.

Reference standards

Pathological sections of lesions were obtained through transrectal or transperineal ultrasound (US)-guided systematic prostate biopsy, MRI-US fusion targeted biopsy, or RP. A pathologist specializing in the urogenital system performed the pathological diagnosis of each region based on gross pathology, microscopic pathology, and immunohistochemistry results from the biopsy or surgical specimens. The biopsy physician recorded the location of the biopsy samples. For patients undergoing surgery, step-sectioning of RP specimens served as the diagnostic gold standard, with the pathologist recording the tumor location. Using the latest ISUP Gleason grading group system for histological grading22, the lesion with the highest Gleason score was considered the primary lesion in cases of multifocal prostate cancer.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 and MedCalc. Inter-reader agreement for PI-RADS v2.1 scoring was assessed using Cohen’s kappa statistic. Each variable was subjected to univariable binary logistic regression to estimate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for discriminating csPCa from non-csPCa. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed for variables included in ROC analysis, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) with 95% CI was calculated. Kendall’s tau-b correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the correlation between ADC, PSAD, PI-RADS score, T2WI score, and prostate cancer ISUP 2014 Gleason grading group and D’Amico risk classification. |τb|≥ 0.30 was considered indicative of at least a moderate (non-trivial) correlation according to traditional effect-size guidelines for correlation coefficients (small ≈ 0.1, moderate ≈ 0.3, large ≈ 0.5)23. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
