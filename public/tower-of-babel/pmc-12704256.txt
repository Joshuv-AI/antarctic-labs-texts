Comparison of community firearm violence news reports across four local television stations in Philadelphia, PA, USA: A quantitative media content analysis
Authors: Siena Wanders, Jennifer Midberry, Anita Wamakima, Tyrone Muns, Kallie Palm, Jim MacMillan, Christopher N. Morrison, Jessica H. Beard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveOn television news in Philadelphia, PA, USA, community firearm violence (CFV) is rarely covered as a public health issue, and harmful content elements are pervasive. It is unknown whether and how coverage differs across networks. To fill this gap, we compared CFV stories across all local English-language Philadelphia stations: 6ABC, CBS3, NBC10, and FOX29.MethodsWe performed a quantitative media content analysis using a stratified sample of CFV news clips. On each day of 2021, one network and broadcast time were chosen randomly and all clips featuring a firearm-injured person in Philadelphia were collected. We coded the clips to identify victim characteristics and the frequency of 11 harmful content elements. We calculated harmful content scores and compared content frequencies.ResultsMost clips contained harmful content (99.7 %) and most harmful content scores were similar. Differences included: NBC10 less frequently reported clinical condition(p = 0.01) and number of gunshot wounds(p = 0.02) with a significantly lower individual-level harm score(p = 0.04). Differences in the frequency of reported age and child victims were noted.ConclusionsCFV reports were similar across television news outlets in Philadelphia, suggesting that networks are approaching coverage in the same manner: placing high news value on crime framing and frequently including harmful content.

Introduction

Community firearm violence (CFV), defined as fatal and non-fatal shootings from interpersonal violence, peaked in 2021 in the United States with an estimated 20,966 firearm homicides (Simon, 2022; Betz et al., 2021). For decades, but especially surrounding this unprecedented increase, crime stories of CFV have been a key feature of American television news with sensationalized reports of murders and mass shootings (Lowry et al., 2003).

News reports on violence often emphasize individual blame over structural causes and have been criticized for perpetuating stereotypical narratives about Black communities (McKeever et al., 2022; Dorfman et al., 1997). American news outlets rarely contextualize the complexity of CFV with public health framing, often failing to include epidemiologic context (data or trends), root causes, public health narrators (e.g. community representatives, public health professionals), and solutions (Dorfman et al., 1997; Thorson and Rodgers, 2001; Beard et al., 2024). Television, radio, print, and online news shape public understanding of CFV, especially for those with limited personal experience themselves (McKeever et al., 2022; Dorfman et al., 1997; Thorson and Rodgers, 2001; Beard et al., 2024; Iyengar, 1994). Current media narratives of CFV can thus lead audiences to blame victims and undermine effective public health responses, such as community violence intervention, by not exploring evidence-based solutions (Lowry et al., 2003; McKeever et al., 2022; Dorfman et al., 1997; Thorson and Rodgers, 2001; Beard et al., 2024; Iyengar, 1994; Beard et al., 2023).

Messaging about CFV is reflected in the framing of the news story, or how the news story is told. Framing occurs when journalists make choices about who to interview, which content to include or exclude, and which attributes of an event to emphasize over others (Dorfman et al., 1997; Thorson and Rodgers, 2001). Episodic and thematic framing are two ways communication scholars classify news stories about violence and crime. Previous research has further defined episodic crime framing of CFV (Beard et al., 2024; Beard et al., 2023; Marvel et al., 2018). This is framing that is episodic, defines violence as a crime issue, privileges police narrators, and is the dominant frame in CFV stories.

In our prior qualitative interview study, firearm-injured people expressed that episodic crime stories of their own shootings were dehumanizing and traumatizing (Beard et al., 2023). Participants noted news elements, such as graphic content and naming the treating hospital, resulted in trauma flashbacks and threats to personal safety (Beard et al., 2023). Our previous quantitative media content analysis of local Philadelphia CFV news stories found that most stories utilized episodic framing with law enforcement as the primary information source, while stories featuring a public health perspective were rare (Beard et al., 2024). Additionally, our modified Delphi consensus study identified 12 news content elements that are potentially harmful to firearm-injured people, impacted communities, and society (Beard et al., 2024a). The Delphi panel included experts with lived experience of CFV, journalists, and scholars resulting in the development of a tool to measure harmful reporting, the Harmful Reporting Codebook (Beard et al., 2024a).

While harmful CFV reporting is common, it is unknown whether and how CFV coverage differs across local television networks. In this study, we aimed to compare the content of CFV news stories across all four local English-language Philadelphia television news stations. We examined if and how networks report on CFV differently with attention to specific news content elements that are potentially harmful to individuals, communities, and society. It is likely that CFV content differs due to variations in news formatting and broadcast templates. The goal of this research is to explore these variations, inform our understanding of television news production of CFV stories, and identify opportunities for collaboration with networks to minimize harmful reporting as a public health intervention.

Methods

Study design and population

We conducted a quantitative media content analysis to identify trends in television news content and to compare content between news stations. Television news was chosen because it is the modality with the largest reach that also creates novel content (Jeon and Nasr, 2016; Forman-Katz and Matsa, 2023). For this study, we sampled clips, segments extracted from a news broadcast, from an existing dataset of 7497 individual stories about CFV aired during three newscasts (6 AM, 6 PM, 11 PM) on four local English-language Philadelphia television news stations (6ABC, CBS3, NBC10, and FOX29) in 2021. These stations all produce original local news broadcast at similar time periods throughout the day, enabling comparison. To create this database, we used a comprehensive list of 37 terms to perform daily keyword searches in TVEyes, a subscription television news monitoring platform (Beard et al., 2024; Beard et al., 2024b). Because of the availability of this existing dataset prospectively compiled in 2021, the study period for this research is 2021. Following best practices for quantitative media content analysis, we used a random number generator to select a broadcast time and station for each day in 2021 (Riffe et al., 2019). To answer our specific research question, we compiled all clips that featured details about a specific firearm-injured person in Philadelphia aired on the randomly selected day and time, creating a representative sample of clips for analysis (Riffe et al., 2019).

Measures

The clip length variable was collected from each video's time stamps. For the individual-level analysis, clips were coded to collect demographic information of the firearm-injured people and shooting events. The variables included: total number of victims reported, fatality, age, race/ethnicity, and gender.

For the clip-level analysis, clips were coded to identify harmful content using the Harmful Reporting Codebook (Beard et al., 2024a) (A.1). The 12 harmful content elements defined in our prior Delphi consensus study include: not a follow-up, only law enforcement narrators, missing perspective of firearm-injured person, missing community perspective, graphic/explicit content (e.g. video of shooting, still photo of a body, audio of screaming, etc.), the victim's clinical condition, name of treating hospital, number of gunshot wounds, the relationship between the firearm-injured person and the perpetrator, the perpetrator's mugshot (police-released headshot), and episodic framing (Beard et al., 2024a). In this study, we did not include not a follow up story because it was difficult to operationalize and achieve adequate intercoder reliability (ICR), resulting in 11 harmful content elements.

Only law enforcement narrators was calculated by tallying the instances in the narrators variable where 1) a journalist was the only narrator and attributed police sources and 2) a law enforcement representative was the only person interviewed. Missing perspective of firearm-injured person and missing community perspective were calculated if the clip lacked an interview, quotation, or paraphrased comment from a firearm-injured person/their loved ones and people from the impacted community, respectively. The presence or absence of additional harmful content was coded using binary yes/no answers. These variables included: graphic/explicit content, clinical condition, name of treating hospital, number of gunshot wounds, relationship between firearm-injured person and perpetrator, and mugshot. Clips were coded as episodic framing if most of the time focused on a specific shooting event(s), and they were coded as thematic framing if most of the clip discussed CFV with social context, epidemiological trends, root causes, and/or solutions.

Four coders worked with practice material until they reached ICR scores of at least 0.7 using Krippendorf's alpha and Gwet's AC1 (Lacy et al., 2015). They then coded the same 10 % of the sample until acceptable ICR levels were confirmed. They coded the remaining clips individually.

Our prior Delphi study assessed the degree to which each of the news content elements could cause harm (i.e. mild, moderate, severe) at each level of harm (i.e. individual, community, society) (Beard et al., 2024a). To operationalize our assessment of harmful content, we assigned each grade of severity a numerical score (mild = 1, moderate = 2, severe = 3). For each clip, a harmful content score was calculated at each level of harm. The numerical value of severity for each element present was summed. The maximum scores for the individual, community, and society harm levels were 29, 25, and 24, respectively.

Statistical analysis

In this study, harmful content scores and frequencies, victim characteristics, and clip length were compared between each television news station with one-way ANOVA and chi-squared tests for continuous and categorical variables, respectively. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Temple University Institutional Review Board determined that this analysis of publicly available television news clips was not human subjects research and did not require review. Microsoft Excel was used for the quantitative analysis.

Discussion

In this study, we utilized a database of television news clips covering CFV in Philadelphia in 2021, the deadliest year in recent history. We found that local coverage of CFV in Philadelphia was generally similar across the four stations and frequently included harmful news content. In fact, harmful news content elements were present in all but two clips and the harmful content scores were similar across stations. These findings suggest Philadelphia television networks are approaching CFV coverage in a similar manner: placing high news value on crime framing rather than public health framing and prioritizing sensationalized portrayals of CFV. Across the stations, we found no significant differences between the reporting frequencies of specific characteristics of firearm-injured people such as race/ethnicity and gender.

While reporting was generally similar across the stations, some differences were uncovered in the individual-level and clip-level analysis. NBC10 less frequently reported clinical condition and number of gunshot wounds. NBC10 also had a lower individual-level harmful content score. These differences support the notion that while a framework for reporting CFV content exists, there are small variations across news networks. This finding indicates a potential avenue for news formatting and reporting changes that could inform journalist training and broadcast templates in Philadelphia.

Additionally, we noted differences between the reporting of shootings involving child victims. CBS3 reported more frequently on firearm-injured children. Our previous work found that children are over-represented as shooting victims in news reports about CFV (Beard et al., 2024b). During the pandemic-related surge in CFV, Philadelphia saw a significant increase in the number and proportion of children shot in the city (Afif et al., 2022). Our finding suggests that CBS3 is making narrative decisions to report more shootings involving child victims. This finding further suggests that news stations' narrative choices can impact the epidemiological picture of CFV presented to news audiences.

The Philadelphia Center for Gun Violence Reporting (PCGVR), a community-based organization that supports ethical reporting on CFV as public health intervention, has been a key collaborator ("The Philadelphia Center for Gun Violence Reporting", 2025). Informed by our previous studies, PCGVR created and implemented a novel course, Gun Violence Prevention Reporter Certification, that aims to teach journalists to produce CFV stories that are trauma-informed, contain public health framing, and explore solutions ("The Philadelphia Center for Gun Violence Reporting", 2025). Scaling this training to local television newsrooms in Philadelphia and encouraging networks to shift the typical CFV reporting framework could contribute to minimizing harmful news content. With PCGVR, we plan to present this study's results to Philadelphia television networks, emphasizing opportunities to minimize harm.

This study is limited by our clip sampling technique and descriptive design. We included television news broadcast in a single city experiencing a CFV surge. Therefore, our findings are not generalizable to other American cities, to national television news, or to print, radio, or social media content. Future research should examine news reports about CFV in other locations.

In conclusion, this study provides novel insight into harmful reporting on local television news in Philadelphia during the CFV surge of 2021. It identifies differences between some news stations' reporting frameworks, which could inform journalist training and broadcast templates. Our findings open the door to narrative change and encourage the implementation of journalist education and news network collaboration with PCGVR.
