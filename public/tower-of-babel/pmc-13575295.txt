Effects of Statistical Learning on Target Detection Speed Can Reflect Prediction
Authors: Laura J. Batterink, Daniela Herrera‐Chaves, Stefan Köhler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn a recent study in Cognitive Science, Sheinenson, Kleiman, and Siegelman found that statistical learning of visual pairs produces response time facilitation during target detection that is driven exclusively by the predictive value of the preceding shape rather than by the predictability of the target itself. These intriguing results suggest that facilitation effects in the target detection task may reflect attentional modulation by statistical structure, whereby greater attention is allocated to temporal segments of greater certainty rather than the prediction of specific upcoming items. Here, we highlight an important divergent finding that suggests that Sheinenson and colleagues’ framework may not generalize across all statistical learning studies using the target detection task. Using a speech‐based statistical learning paradigm with a conceptually similar design, we recently showed that facilitation during target detection depends entirely on whether the target itself is specifically predicted by the preceding syllable and not on whether the preceding syllable is predictive, as suggested by Sheinenson and colleagues. We suggest that the relative stability of the statistical environment and the domain of statistical learning may be important factors that critically shape whether prediction is invoked during target detection. The target detection task can index learning of specific regularities, at least in the speech domain when violations of learned structure are rare. Identifying the precise boundary conditions under which prediction does and does not operate in statistical learning will be an important goal of future research.

Statistical learning, the ability to extract patterns in the environment simply through exposure, plays a fundamental role across diverse perceptual and cognitive domains, from vision (Fiser & Aslin, 2001) to language (Saffran, Aslin, & Newport, 1996; Saffran, Newport, & Aslin, 1996) to motor processing (Theeuwes, Huang, Frings, & van Moorselaar, 2024). Understanding this ability has emerged as a central goal for cognitive science. A signature outcome of statistical learning is the finding that later‐occurring items within a unit or pattern are processed more quickly than initial items (e.g., Batterink, Reber, Neville, & Paller, 2015; Bertels, Franco, & Destrebecqz, 2012; Franco, Eberlen, Destrebecqz, Cleeremans, & Bertels, 2015; Kim, Seitz, Feenstra, & Shams, 2009; Turk‐Browne, Jungé, & Scholl, 2005), a finding that is commonly interpreted as reflecting prediction (e.g., Turk‐Browne, 2012). This type of facilitation effect is typically demonstrated using a target detection task, in which participants are asked to detect target elements (e.g., syllables or visual shapes) embedded within continuous stimulus streams, most often following an initial learning phase.

Recently, using this type of design, Sheinenson, Kleiman, and Siegelman (2026; hereafter SKS) demonstrated that statistical learning of visual pairs leads to facilitation effects that depend solely on whether the preceding element had previously served as a predictive cue for other elements. In other words, if visual pair A–B were presented as part of the initial learning stream, participants showed faster responses to any shape immediately following A (a previously predictive shape), compared to shapes immediately following B (a previously non‐predictive shape), in the subsequent target detection task. Moreover, no benefit whatsoever was observed when the original pairings were preserved; that is, shape B presented after A did not elicit faster reaction times (RTs) than any other shape presented after A. This is a striking finding, as it upends the general idea that facilitation to later‐occurring elements within learned patterns reflects prediction of specific upcoming elements. This observed facilitation following predictive elements is interpreted as reflecting modulations in attention to the stream, whereby attentional resources are guided to “low uncertainty regions” of the continuous stream. These results led SKS to suggest that “performance in the target detection task may not reflect learning of specific regularities but rather how attentional resources are allocated to different parts of the input.”

While we find SKS's findings important and intriguing, recent work suggests that these conclusions may not generalize across all forms of statistical learning and that there are important boundary conditions for this proposed account of attentional modulation by statistical structure. This divergent evidence comes from a recent auditory‐linguistic statistical learning study (Batterink, Hsiung, Herrera‐Chaves, & Köhler, 2025), in which we presented target syllables in both “match” and “mismatch” contexts, a design that is conceptually highly similar to that of SKS. Specifically, in this study, a target syllable could occur within its expected position in its usual “word” (“match” target), or in an unexpected context, replacing either the first, second, or third position of another word where it normally did not occur (“mismatch” target). If facilitation effects in this paradigm are driven by the predictability of the preceding element in the stream, as demonstrated by SKS, one would expect faster responses to later‐occurring syllables (second and third positions) in both “match” words and “mismatch” words alike. This is because the preceding syllables are identical in each condition; only the congruency of the target differs. However, our results show a markedly different pattern.

Unlike SKS's results, mismatch targets in our prior study elicited significantly slower RTs than match targets, and critically this “mismatch cost” was not modulated by the preceding syllable position, such that target response times were similarly delayed across the first, second, and third syllable positions. In other words, there was no evidence that the predictive value of the immediately preceding syllable influences target response times. Rather, in this study, it appears to be the specific associations between the preceding syllable(s) and target that influence participants’ RTs. Further, the mismatch “cost” (the relative RT delay to mismatch syllables, controlling for individual baseline RTs) strongly correlated with the facilitation effect to predictable syllables (the relative RT speedup), both across participants and at the individual syllable level. This tradeoff represents a behavioral hallmark of genuine prediction (Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016; Van Petten & Luka, 2012), supporting that RT facilitation in the target detection task represents true prediction of specific syllables. In summary, whereas the facilitation effects in SKS's study were driven entirely by the predictive value of the preceding stimulus, facilitation in our previous study (Batterink et al., 2025)—in the context of speech‐based statistical learning— was primarily determined by the strength of the association between the preceding syllable(s) and the current target.

Understanding why these two seemingly similar paradigms produce such disparate patterns of findings will be an important target for future work. Here, we suggest several potential factors that may explain the discrepancies in results, which must await confirmation by further research.

First, the overall predictability of targets within the streams is a crucial consideration, a suggestion also raised by SKS. The target detection tasks in both Experiments 1 and 2 of SKS's study, as well as in the original study by Barakat, Seitz, and Shams (2013) that SKS's Experiment 1 sought to replicate, included a relatively high proportion of violation trials. Specifically, in Experiment 1 (and in Barakat and colleagues’ study), 75% of targets did not follow the shape from their original pair, while in Experiment 2, targets never appeared after the shape from their original pair, making them never locally predictable. Thus, predicting a specific element based on the preceding shape (e.g., predicting B from A) would have provided only limited benefit for target detection in Experiment 1 and no benefit in Experiment 2. Given evidence that learners can rapidly adapt to novel statistical regularities in the environment (e.g., Llanos, Wu, Abel, & Holt, 2025; Vernaillen & Bogaerts, 2026), learners in SKS's experiments may have quickly become sensitive to the fact that targets were not locally predictable; they may thus have shifted away from a specific pairwise prediction strategy, as it would not benefit performance.

In contrast, in our prior study (Batterink et al., 2025), approximately 85% of the target syllables occurred in their expected contexts, and thus engaging in prediction based on specific learned associations could be expected to benefit detection overall. Target detection paradigms that maintain a high proportion of the original structures presented during initial learning may be much more likely to engage prediction mechanisms and, conversely, may not necessarily invoke the same modulation of attention by statistical structure described by SKS. Broadly speaking, prediction incurs benefits when confirmed but costs when disconfirmed (Kuperberg & Jaeger, 2016; Van Petten & Luka, 2012; see Posner, 1980). Prediction has been proposed to follow a utility function that weighs both its advantages and disadvantages (Kuperberg & Jaegar,2016), and thus may be deployed in the context of statistical learning only when the statistical environment is relatively stable and predictable. As the statistical uncertainty of an environment increases, prediction effects may decrease until they are no longer observed.

A second notable factor that differs between the two studies is the repetition of targets within individual streams. In our prior study (Batterink et al., 2025), the same target syllable repeated up to 15 times within individual streams, whereas SKS (and Barakat and colleagues) used only a single target shape per stream. Interestingly, a reanalysis of the prior data from Batterink et al. (2025) showed that the prediction effect for standard words, while present from the very first target presentation, significantly increased as the targets repeated across a given stream (triplet position × target repetition: p < .001).1 Recent and repeated encounters of a target may transiently prime representations of the corresponding statistical structure, thereby facilitating prediction. This result indicates that recent experience has a measurable impact on prediction above and beyond longer‐term learning effects, highlighting the rapid, dynamic and adaptive nature of statistical learning (Frost et al., 2026).

A final, third factor worth considering is the learning domain. The numerous design differences between SKS's and our previous (Batterink et al., 2025) paradigms preclude a direct comparison between learning modalities based on currently available data. However, based on the findings between these two studies, a hypothesis to be tested by future research is that stimulus‐specific prediction may arise more readily from statistical learning of speech, compared to statistical learning of nonlinguistic stimuli, possibly including both visual shapes and non‐linguistic auditory sounds (Barakat et al., 2013). Testing this hypothesis could be accomplished by parametrically manipulating the proportion of violation trials in target detection tasks using different types of stimuli. If our hypothesis is supported, prediction effects in speech‐based target detection paradigms should occur more strongly, relative to visual and nonlinguistic auditory paradigms, as the proportion of violations increases.

In conclusion, we suggest caution in generalizing SKS's findings and proposed mechanisms to all forms of statistical learning or when “re‐evaluat[ing] claims from other SL studies using target detection paradigms” (SKS, p. 13): performance in the target detection task can reflect the learning of specific regularities, although—as shown by SKS—it does not do so ubiquitously. While we consider SKS’ findings valuable and of considerable interest, the conclusions drawn from these results may not apply equally across all paradigms and forms of statistical learning. At the same time, item‐specific prediction arising from statistical learning is also unlikely to be ubiquitous; it may emerge only when patterns in the environment are relatively stable, reflecting a weighing of both expected benefits and costs. When the costs exceed the potential benefits—as in the case of a relatively unpredictable environment—learners may no longer attempt to predict what will happen next. These costs and benefits can be directly quantified in the target detection task by RT slowing to unexpected targets and facilitation to expected targets, respectively (Batterink et al., 2015). Understanding the dynamic interactions among statistical learning, attention, and prediction and how these mechanisms are crucially shaped by the learning environment will be a critical next step in refining theoretical models of statistical learning.
