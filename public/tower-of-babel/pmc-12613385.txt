COVID-19 re-infection: a large cross-sectional population-based study in the northeast of Iran
Authors: Mina AkbariRad, Soheila Ehsani Roodi, Mohammadtaghi Shakeri, Maryam Salari, Amir Mohamad Hashem Asnaashari, Abdollah Firoozi, AmirAli Moodi Ghalibaf
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundToday, COVID-19 reinfection has raised further public health concerns than its primary infection; which make major global health concern that going to the possibility of COVID-19 re-infection, and how strong the body’s immunity against this disease in individuals with the previous infection. The present study aimed to investigate the probability of COVID-19 re-infection leading to hospitalization in the population referred to hospitals affiliated with Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, Mashhad, Iran.MethodThe present cross-sectional epidemiological study was conducted on patients referred to hospitals affiliated to the Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, Mashhad, Iran supervision with re-infection of COVID-19 from January 21, 2020, to August 23, 2022. The patients had a definite diagnosis through RT-PCR testing in their first and re-infections, with a time interval of at least one month between the two episodes of infection.ResultsOut of 125,857 patients evaluated, 9,710 (7.7%) were readmitted with COVID-19 infection more than once, with a time interval of more than one month from their previous hospitalization. The average age of hospitalized patients was 54.8 ± 22.97 years, and more than half were men (51.4%). The most common underlying diseases were hypertension (19.4%) and diabetes (14.8%). cardiovascular diseases, asthma, and cancer were the strongest independent risk factors of COVID-19 re-infection (OR = 2.53, 2.47, and 2.36, respectively, p < 0.001 for all cases).ConclusionsIn conclusion, our study demonstrates the importance of understanding the risk factors associated with COVID-19 re-infection and re-hospitalization, particularly in vulnerable populations with comorbidities and substance use disorders.Keywords: COVID-19, Hospitalization, Re-infection

Introduction

The outbreak of COVID-19, which originated in Wuhan, China, in 2019, has led to a global pandemic, with millions of confirmed cases and hundreds of thousands of deaths worldwide [1]. Following the World Health Organization’s (WHO) declaration of COVID-19 as a global pandemic, the virus rapidly spread globally, putting a strain on numerous nations’ healthcare systems [2]. As the virus continues to spread, researchers are racing to better understand the characteristics of the virus and the disease it causes. Previous studies revealed that the COVID-19 number of new cases approximately 120 per 100,000 people while its fatality rate calculated as 1% to 3% in different areas of the world [3]. One area of concern is the risk of re-infection and hospitalization after recovery from COVID-19. Recent studies have shown that many patients are readmitted to hospitals after discharge, indicating the need for further investigation into the risk factors associated with re-infection [4–6]. However, due to the limited evidence available, COVID-19 re-infection is rare and occurs in less than 0.3% of the individuals with previous history of COVID-19 infection [7].

Today, COVID-19 reinfection has raised further public health concerns than its primary infection; which make major global health concern that going to the possibility of COVID-19 re-infection, and how strong the body’s immunity against this disease in individuals with the previous infection [8–10]. Immunity against COVID-19 reinfection can affect the disease’s severity and spread; in other words, herd immunity, vaccination, and general epidemic simulations depend on the effectiveness and duration of immunity against COVID-19 [11, 12]. Due to previous studies, it could be stated that lack of immunity due to the lack of the previous infection is the main cause of the rapid and global spread of the disease and epidemic continuation [13]. Therefore, considering great information and knowledge about COVID-19 re-infection epidemiology, its clinical manifestations, and the immunity against COVID-19 reinfection is not only essential for better management of the disease but also for its prevention.

It seems that the first case of reinfection with a phylogenetically distinct strain of SARS-CoV-2 was reported in the literature on August 25, 2020 [14]. Despite several other studies have been reported COVID-19 re-infected cases up to now; Additionally, although the evidence shows a high level of immunity against COVID-19 reinfection in healthcare providers, the risk of reinfection in the general population is still unknown; however, there is still a lack of understanding about the clinical effects of re-hospitalization on patients’ prognosis and the risk factors related to readmission and re-infection. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the COVID-19 re-infection risk factors.

Methods

Study design

The present cross-sectional study was conducted in hospitals under the supervision of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, Mashhad, Iran from January 21, 2020, to August 23, 2022. All patients with a history of COVID-19 infection confirmed by positive RT-PCR test who were admitted to hospitals that supervised by Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, Mashhad, Iran, were included in the study; All data were extracted from Medical Care Monitoring Center (MCMC); therefore, only inpatients whose data were electronically recorded to the MCMC were included; however, the reinfection criteria were considered as recurrent positive SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR in more than 30 days which was defined by Zare et al. [15]. The exclusion criteria were considered as the uncertainty of re-infection status and lack of complete registration information in each of the stages of initial or re-infection with COVID-19. The protocol of the current study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, Mashhad, Iran (Ref: IR.MUMS.REC.1400.171).

Data collection

Patients’ data, including demographic information (including gender, age, COVID-19 vaccination history), social history (including social history of smoking and opium addiction), past medical histories (underlying diseases), vital signs (i.e. blood oxygen saturation), and outcomes in both first and second hospitalization episodes, were acquired from the electronic records. In detail, all electronic recorded data were gathered from Medical Care Monitoring Center (MCMC) of the Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, Mashhad, Iran.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 22 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA). Quantitative and qualitative data were reported using mean ± standard deviation (SD) and frequency (percentage). Quantitative variables were compared using an independent sample t-test, and the chi-Square test was used for qualitative variables. Finally, a logistic regression model was used to determine independent risk factors of COVID-19 re-infection in the study population. In all statistical analyses, statistical significance was considered as p < 0.05.

Results

A total of 125,857 COVID-19 patients (51.4% males) with a mean age of 54.80 ± 22.97 years were admitted to the hospitals of Mashhad University of Medical Sciences during the study period (Fig. 1). 2858 patients (2.3%) were current smokers, and 5022 (4%) were opium addicts. The most frequent underlying diseases were hypertension (19.4%) and diabetes (14.8%). 28,358 individuals (22.5%) had received one vaccine against COVID-19, while 37,163 patients (29.5%) had received two or more doses. Blood O2 saturation levels were less than 93% upon admission in 70,829 patients (56.3%). O2 saturation levels were recorded during the first hospitalization episode for all patients, as this data was more uniformly documented across the study. The mean hospital stay was 4.60 ± 76.33 days, and the overall case fatality rate among the study population was 14.6%. 9710 patients (7.7%) had a recurrent COVID-19 infection > 30 days after the first hospitalization. These data are shown in Table 1.

Older age and female gender were associated with higher risks of COVID-19 re-infection (p < 0.001 in both cases). Underlying diseases, smoking, and opium addiction were also associated with increased risks of re-hospitalization due to COVID-19 (p < 0.001 in all cases). Individuals who had received at least one dose of the COVID-19 vaccine were more likely to experience re-infection. On the other hand, mortality rates were lower among patients with re-infection (p < 0.001 in both cases, Table 1).

Finally, a logistic regression model was used to identify variables independently associated with a higher risk of COVID-19 re-infection. As seen in Table 2, older age and female gender were associated with a slight increase in the risk of re-infection (OR = 1.01 for age and OR = 1.08 for female gender, p < 0.001 in both cases). Such was the case for opium addiction (OR = 1.28, p < 0.001) and blood oxygen saturation of less than 93% (OR = 1.26, p < 0.001). Among underlying diseases, cardiovascular diseases, asthma, and cancer were the strongest independent risk factors of COVID-19 re-infection (OR = 2.53, 2.47, and 2.36, respectively, p < 0.001 for all cases) (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to investigate the probability of COVID-19 re-infection leading to hospitalization in patients referred to the hospitals affiliated with the Mashhad University of Medical Sciences, Mashhad, Iran. Overall, 7.7% of the studied population represented the approved COVID-19 re-infection which required re-hospitalization. Previous studies indicated different rates of the COVID-19 re-infection. For example, a study by Zare et al. in Shahroud, Iran reported 2.5 cases of re-infection per 1000 patients and Tavakoli et al. reported this value as 0.97% in Shiraz, Iran [15, 16]. While other studies in the United Kingdom reported various re-infection incidence rates such as 0.0%, 0.2%%, 0.057%, and < 0.05% [17, 18]. Additionally, Abu-Raddad et al. found a re-infection incidence of 0.66% per 10,000 individuals in Qatar [19]. However, Pilz et al. reported this rate of less than 0.3% in Austria [7]. These differences could be due to several differences in the definition of reinfection, the duration between the initial infection and reinfection, the specificity and sensitivity of the diagnostic tests, and the history of vaccination.

Our higher reinfection rate (7.7%) may be due to the inclusion of a hospitalized population only, longer follow-up (32 months), and the broader testing protocol in our region. Additionally, our definition required a positive RT-PCR > 30 days apart, which may capture more recurrent infections than stricter definitions. We found significant differences between the re-infected and non-re-infected studied populations in all measured parameters. Additionally, our results show that older age (OR = 1.01), female gender (OR = 1.08), opium addiction (OR = 1.28), hypertension (OR = 1.20), diabetes (OR = 1.11), cancer (OR = 2.36), cardiovascular disease (OR = 2.53), asthma (OR = 2.47), chronic liver diseases (OR = 1.35), chronic hematologic diseases (OR = 1.58), and blood O2 saturation level below 93% (OR = 1.26) were significant and independent risk factors for COVID-19 re-infection and re-hospitalization. In line with our study, Tavakoli et al. reported that malignancy could increase the risk of re-infection about 3.5-folds; this could be due to the immunocompromised situation of the cancer patients. Additionally, elderly (OR-1.403) and chronic kidney disease (OR = 2.239) increased the risk of re-infection; in contrast, they reported that being male was reported as the risk factor (OR = 1.283) [16]. These findings are consistent with previous literature highlighting the susceptibility of individuals with comorbidities to severe COVID-19 outcomes [20–22]. The association of female gender with increased risk of re-infection and re-hospitalization contradicts some previous studies that found males to be more susceptible to severe COVID-19 outcomes [23, 24]. While some studies report higher susceptibility in males, our finding of female predominance in reinfection may relate to behavioral, occupational, or hormonal factors and warrants further investigation. Furthermore, the identification of opium addiction as a risk factor for COVID-19 re-infection and re-hospitalization is particularly noteworthy, as it highlights the need for targeted public health interventions in populations with substance use disorders. These individuals may require additional support to prevent re-infection, such as access to COVID-19 vaccination, education on preventive measures, and appropriate medical care for their underlying conditions. Previous studies have also suggested a link between substance use and increased risk of severe COVID-19 outcomes [25, 26]. This discrepancy between our findings and prior studies may be attributed to differences in study design, population characteristics, or data collection methods may be attributed to factors such as differences in study populations, healthcare settings, or potential confounding variables that were not accounted for in our analysis. Further studies are needed to clarify the role of gender in COVID-19 re-infection and re-hospitalization risk.

In examining the implications of COVID-19 re-infection on the applicability of study results to broader populations, it is crucial to consider the variability in immune responses among different demographic groups. Interestingly, individuals who had received at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine showed a higher rate of re-infection. This may be explained by behavioral factors such as a false sense of security leading to reduced caution or by the waning immunity over time. Furthermore, differences in variant prevalence post-vaccination might also contribute to this trend. Lower blood oxygen saturation levels (< 93%) during the initial hospitalization were also independently associated with re-infection. This likely reflects a more severe initial disease, potentially resulting in chronic pulmonary damage or immune dysregulation. Hypoxia can impair immune cell function, which may reduce the ability to mount an effective response to subsequent viral exposures. The re-infection rates can differ significantly based on factors such as age, comorbidities, and vaccination status. This heterogeneity may influence the generalizability of findings, as populations with varying immune profiles may exhibit distinct susceptibility to re-infection and differing clinical outcomes. Due to database limitations, we could not assess reinfection risk by vaccine type. Future studies should explore vaccine-specific effectiveness against reinfection. Furthermore, the evolving nature of SARS-CoV-2 variants poses additional challenges, as mutations may alter the virus’s behavior and the immune system’s response. Therefore, while specific studies provide valuable insights, their applicability must be critically assessed in the context of diverse population characteristics and the dynamic landscape of viral evolution, highlighting the need for ongoing research that encompasses a wide range of demographic variables to ensure comprehensive understanding and effective public health strategies. Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the results. First, the retrospective design may have led to biases in data collection and patient selection. Second, the generalizability of our findings may be limited by the fact that the study was conducted in a single city with a specific demographic profile. Another limitation is the lack of data regarding SARS-CoV-2 variants, which may have affected reinfection risks. Genotyping information was not available in our electronic records. Future multi-center, prospective studies should be conducted to validate our findings and explore potential risk factors for COVID-19 re-infection and re-hospitalization in more diverse populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates the importance of understanding the risk factors associated with COVID-19 re-infection and re-hospitalization, particularly in vulnerable populations with comorbidities and substance use disorders. These findings can inform public health strategies and clinical management guidelines to minimize the risk of re-infection and reduce the burden on healthcare systems.
