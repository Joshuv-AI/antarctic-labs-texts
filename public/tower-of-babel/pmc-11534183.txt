Successful endoscopic resection of a rare extension of peripheral giant cell granuloma into the infratemporal fossa via the maxillary sinus: A case report study
Authors: Waddah Al-Saadie, Nour Raffoul, Karim Chahine
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction and significancePeripheral giant cell granuloma (PGCG) is a benign reactive lesion commonly found in the mandible but rarely in the maxillary sinus and extending to the infratemporal fossa, which poses a major challenge in excision in this case. It can occasionally lead to bone erosion. Complete surgical excision is the primary treatment.Case presentationWe present a rare case of PGCG in the maxillary sinus of a male patient following the extraction of the maxillary second molar, resulting in destruction of the bony margins. The patient underwent complete endoscopic resection with a flap to repair the bone loss in the maxilla.Clinical discussionClinicians should be aware of this uncommon location of PGCG to ensure accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment with minimal patient trauma and excellent outcomes.ConclusionEndoscopy is a highly effective method for removing PGCG located in the maxillary sinus and extending to the infratemporal fossa, even in cases of large size. It offers excellent results and provides good visualization of the maxillary sinus.

Introduction

Peripheral giant cell granuloma (PGCG) is a benign lesion that is often asymptomatic in the gums or associated mucosa. It is considered a hypertrophic lesion, not a neoplasm [1]. The majority of detected cases are approximately 1–1.5 cm in diameter, and the tumor rarely exceeds this size. The exact mechanism of occurrence is unclear, but it is believed to arise from the periosteum or gingival membrane of the alveolar ridge due to injury or trauma [2]. Several factors have been reported to cause PGCG, including chronic infections, periodontal surgery, or tooth extraction [3]. This tumor typically affects the mandible more frequently than the maxilla [4]. The majority of these lesions are found anywhere in the gums or at the level of the alveolar mucosa, primarily concentrated in front of the molars [5,6].

Radiological evaluation is crucial to assess the extent of the tumor. The primary treatment is surgical complete excision.

Our case represents the first reported treatment of a rare PGCG localization in the maxillary sinus and extending to the infratemporal fossa, using a fully endoscopic approach without any external surgical incision.

This case is described in accordance with the criteria of SCARE [7].

Presentation of case

Patient information

We present the case of a 45-year-old male patient who underwent excision of his right maxillary molar by a dentist approximately one month after the appearance of a hard, gradually increasing lesion at the site of the previous intervention with oozing and pain during swallowing. Two weeks ago, the patient began to complain of severe pain in the upper jaw, right cheek, and behind the eyeball, accompanied by swelling of the right cheek and fever. The patient had no prior history of tumors and did not suffer from any allergic diseases.

Upon arrival, the patient was seen in the emergency department where the general condition was good, with a noticeable swelling of the right cheek (Fig. 1-A) and difficulty breathing. Therapeutic intervention

Based on the previous clinical and radiological findings, it was decided to perform endoscopic surgical intervention. Using endoscopy, the nasal cavity was entered (Fig. 3-A), and the mass in the right nasal part was removed. The middle meatus was well exposed, revealing destruction of the adjacent bony process. Subsequently, the maxillary sinus orifice was widened, and most of the medial wall of the maxillary sinus was resected to expose the sinus-filling lesion. The maxillary sinus was then entered through the nose. A fragile, bleeding lesion was found, which was dissected from the edges of the maxillary sinus, where there was damage to the maxillary sinus wall. The lesion was removed with difficulty completely using endoscopy from the maxillary sinus (Fig. 3-B).Fig. 3-AIntraoperative image showing the beginning of the endoscopy where the lesion was found to be red with oozing edges. After removing the mass from the entire maxillary sinus, we entered through the bony loss in the lateral wall of the maxillary sinus, reaching the lateral extension of the mass in the infratemporal fossa. The extended part of the mass was completely removed and pushed through the lower wall of the maxillary sinus into the oral cavity, with excision of the adjacent bony edges to reduce recurrence. Discussion

To understand the causes and nature of PGCG, several theories have been proposed in the past. These theories suggest that this tumor is an osteoclast expressing the remnants of physiological tooth resorption or may result from a lesion injury. Previously, these tumors were called giant cell regenerative granulomas, but the regenerative effect has not yet been proven, making the activity of osteoclasts questionable [8,9]. PGCG is considered a common lesion occurring in the mouth, constituting less than 10 % of lesions found in the periodontium [10]. These tumors appear as hard or soft nodules, often serrated and of varying sizes. The majority of these tumors are less than 1.5 cm in diameter and may be sessile or pedunculated. They often have an ulcerated surface and tend to spread by penetrating the periodontal membrane [11]. In our case, this lesion was large in size and completely filled the maxillary sinus and extending to the infratemporal fossa. The lesion does not always manifest itself with pain, and growth and enlargement may be the apparent feature of the lesion. These tumors can be seen in all age groups but are most often observed between 40 and 60 years old and frequently occur in the lower jaw, rarely in the upper jaw [12].

The preferred location for these tumors is the incisors and molars area. Some reports have indicated that they always occur in the canine or incisor area [13]. The exact cause of the formation of these lesions remains unclear, and several factors may play a role in their development, such as tooth extraction, poor restorations, chronic infection, nutritional deficiency and its effects, inappropriate prosthetic devices, or trauma [14].

The differential diagnosis of PGCG includes pyogenic granuloma, brown tumor, peripheral osteofibroma, and metastatic carcinomas [15]. There are no clinical features that can distinguish PGCG from other forms of gingival hyperplasia. The most similar differential diagnosis is central GCG, where these lesions are located within the bone itself and exhibit more aggressive behavior. The distinction between them can only be made by radiography. The final diagnosis is made by histopathological examination.

PGCG contains numerous foci of large, multinucleated cells and hemosiderin molecules, which are located in the connective tissue stroma. Chronic inflammation is seen in all areas of the lesion. The overlying epithelium is usually enlarged and ulcerated. Surgical treatment is essential for the treatment of these lesions, with removal of any possible causative factors. It includes complete surgical excision to the underlying bone, as superficial excision may cause tumor recurrence. The recurrence rate after surgery is approximately 11–50 % [14].

Conclusion

It is uncommon for peripheral giant cell granulomas (PGCGs) to extend into the maxillary sinus and cause such extensive bone destruction. Our case presents a unique instance of a PGCG being completely removed from the maxillary sinus and extending to the infratemporal fossa using an endoscopic approach, with no recurrence observed in the postoperative follow-up.
