On the Sample Complexity of Learning for Blind Inverse Problems
Nathan Buskulic, Luca Calatroni, Lorenzo Rosasco, Silvia Villa

Abstract
Blind inverse problems arise in many experimental settings where the forward operator is partially or entirely unknown. In this context, methods developed for the non-blind case cannot be adapted in a straightforward manner. Recently, data-driven approaches have been proposed to address blind inverse problems, demonstrating strong empirical performance and adaptability. However, these methods often lack interpretability and are not supported by rigorous theoretical guarantees, limiting their reliability in applied domains such as imaging inverse problems. In this work, we shed light on learning in blind inverse problems within the simplified yet insightful framework of Linear Minimum Mean Square Estimators (LMMSEs). We provide an in-depth theoretical analysis, deriving closed-form expressions for optimal estimators and extending classical results. In particular, we establish equivalences with suitably chosen Tikhonov-regularized formulations, where the regularization depends explicitly on the distributions of the unknown signal, the noise, and the random forward operators. We also prove convergence results under appropriate source condition assumptions. Furthermore, we derive rigorous finite-sample error bounds that characterize the performance of learned estimators as a function of the noise level, problem conditioning, and number of available samples. These bounds explicitly quantify the impact of operator randomness and reveal the associated convergence rates as this randomness vanishes. Finally, we validate our theoretical findings through illustrative numerical experiments that confirm the predicted convergence behavior.

Blind inverse problems; Linear minimum mean square estimation; Tikhonov regularization; Random forward operators; Error bounds

1
Introduction

Blind inverse problems arise in many scientific and engineering applications, where the forward operator is unknown or only partially known. This lack of operator knowledge fundamentally limits reconstruction quality and necessitates the development of principled and computationally efficient solution methods. Among the various instances of blind inverse problems, blind image deconvolution is arguably one of the most extensively studied, across both image processing and inverse problems communities; see, for example,
Kundur and Hatzinakos 1996
;
Bertero et al. 1998
;
Jiang et al. 2003
;
Levin et al. 2009
for surveys and representative contributions.

Over the past decade, learning-based methods have achieved state-of-the-art performance in several applied inverse problems contexts upon suitable supervised/unsupervised training and availability of training data. However, such data-driven approaches are often difficult to interpret and typically lack rigorous theoretical guarantees, which are essential in safety-critical applications such as biomedical imaging
Ongie et al. 2020
;
Knoll et al. 2020
. Most learning-based approaches to inverse problems are built upon highly nonlinear models, which often hinder interpretability and theoretical analysis. In this work, we adopt a complementary viewpoint and focus on linear estimators, including, for instance, linear neural networks
Saxe et al. 2013
;
Gunasekar et al. 2018
;
Woodworth
et al. 2020
. This restriction enables a complete theoretical treatment while retaining sufficient modeling power to investigate fundamental aspects of blind inverse problems.

In this paper, we study blind linear inverse problems, in which the goal is to recover an unknown signal
\mathbf{x}\in X
, assumed to be drawn from a distribution
\pi(\mathbf{x})
, from indirect and noisy observations
\mathbf{y}\in Y
, when the forward operator
\mathbf{A}
is unknown or only partially known. The observation model reads

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x})+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}.

(1)

We consider a linear forward model with an unknown operator
\mathbf{A}:X\to Y
and additive noise
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in Y
.
As is standard in the inverse problems literature, the noise is modeled as a centered Gaussian random vector,

\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\beta\mathbf{I})
, where
\beta>0
denotes the noise level.
To account for the blind setting, the forward operator
\mathbf{A}
is treated as a random matrix drawn from a distribution

\pi(\mathbf{A})
and is not directly observed; this contrasts with the classical non-blind setting, where the operator is assumed
to be fixed and known. Throughout this work, we restrict attention to the finite-dimensional case, with

X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
Y=\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.

Classical variational approaches computing a the stable solution of ill-posed inverse problems of retrieving
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathbf{y}
are based on the minimization of suitable energy functionals.
In the non-blind case, this amounts to solving

\displaystyle\Argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\;D(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+J(\mathbf{x}),

(2)

where
D:Y\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
is a data-fidelity term measuring consistency with the observations, and

J:X\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a regularization functional that penalizes undesirable structures in
\mathbf{x}
and mitigates the ill-posedness of the problem. Such variational formulations admit a Bayesian interpretation: for appropriate choices of
D
and
J
, solving (
2
) is equivalent to computing a maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimate, namely

\Argmin_{\mathbf{x}}\;-\log\pi(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{x})-\log\pi(\mathbf{x}).

In the blind setting, it has been shown that MAP solutions can be very bad
Fergus et al. 2006
;
Rameshan et al. 2012
;
Perrone and Favaro 2014
, even when using advanced diffusion-based prior
Nguyen et al. 2025
.
In the context of blind deconvolution of natural images, for instance, the no-blur solution, where the optimal convolution kernel is a Dirac and where the optimal
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}
in the noiseless case, turns out ineed to be the global MAP solution. Similar data-driven methods
Chihaoui
et al. 2024
;
Chung
et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2024
;
Sanghvi et al. 2024
;
Laroche et al. 2024
need to either add new regularization terms to work in practice, without any kind of theoretical motivation, or optimize a marginalized posterior rather than the joint posterior.

Given such poor adaptation of MAP-type reconstruction procedures to blind inverse problems, it is natural to explore alternative estimators that might be more robust in this setting. A good candidate is the Minimum Mean Square Estimator (MMSE), that is given by
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y}\right]
, which is known to be more stable
Freeman and
Brainard 1995
, usually at the cost of being more computationally demanding as one has to integrate over the posterior distribution
p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{y})
or, when sample approximations are used, draw many samples to compute sufficiently precise approximations. However, in the simplified case where a Linear MMSE (LMMSE) estimator
\mathbf{L}:Y\rightarrow X
is considered, a closed form formula can be found, see, e.g.
Kay 1998
for a survey. This has been extensively studied for decades in the non-blind case, being such estimators at the very basis of the Wiener-Kolmogorov
Wiener 1964
and Kalman filters
Kalman
(see
Kailath and Sayed 2001
for a review over linear estimator theory).

Note that LMMSE estimators can equivalently be characterized as solutions of variational problems of the form (
2
), where the data-fidelity term
D
corresponds to the mean squared error and the regularization term
J
is of Tikhonov type; see
Kay 1998
;
Alberti et al. 2021
for the finite- and infinite-dimensional settings, respectively. In particular, the regularization takes the form

J(\mathbf{x})=\lambda\|\mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}\|^{2},

for an optimal parameter
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{*}
and a suitable linear operator
\mathbf{M}:X\to X
.
From this perspective, constructing or learning an LMMSE estimator amounts to identifying an optimal Tikhonov regularization for the inverse problem under consideration. In
Alberti et al. 2021
and
Chirinos-Rodríguez
et al. 2024
this problem has been studied in the non-blind setting using datasets of signal–observation pairs and empirical risk minimization to compute optimal operators
\mathbf{M}
and
\lambda
, respectively.

Although a substantial body of work exists for the non-blind case, the extension of these results to the blind setting, including the computation of LMMSE estimators, has not yet been systematically addressed.

Contributions.

In this paper, we provide a systematic study of linear minimum mean square error (LMMSE) estimation in the context of blind inverse problems. Our main contributions are twofold:

(i)

We derive explicit expressions for the LMMSE estimator in the blind setting and specialize them to two complementary estimation tasks: recovering the unknown signal from the observations and recovering the unknown forward operator from the same data, a feature specific to blind inverse problems. In both cases, we identify the associated Tikhonov-regularized variational formulations, which serve as a basis for discussing joint estimation strategies for the signal and the forward operator.

(ii)

We address the problem of learning the LMMSE estimator from data in the blind setting. Under a Hölder-type source condition, we show that the reconstruction error of a regularized empirical LMMSE estimator can be bounded, in expectation, by the sum of an approximation error—depending on the noise level and the regularity of the problem—and a sampling error—depending on the conditioning of the problem and the number of available samples. For each error term, we provide theoretical results that characterize their behavior across different regimes.

We complement our theoretical analysis with exemplar one-dimensional numerical experiments that validate the proposed estimators and illustrate the predicted error regimes.

1.1
Notations

We denote the expected value of a random vector
\mathbf{z}
as
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{z}\right]=\theta_{\mathbf{z}}
and the expected value of a random matrix
\mathbf{M}
as
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{M}\right]=\Theta_{\mathbf{M}}
. If we do not precise the variable with which we take the expectation, it is assumed that we take the expectation with respect to every random variable. We also write the cross-covariance matrix of two random vectors
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
by
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}\right]\right)\left(\mathbf{y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}\right]\right)^{\top}\right]
, and thus the notation
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
denote the covariance matrix of the random vector
\mathbf{x}
. The squared coefficent of variation of a scalar random variable
z
is written as
\CV^{2}\left(z\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(z-\theta_{z}\right)^{2}\right]\theta_{z}^{-2}
. We denote by
\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{z})
the vector (or matrix) containing the variances of each entry of the random vector
\mathbf{z}
. We say that
a\lesssim b
if
a\leq cb
with
c>0
a fixed constant.

2
LMMSE for blind inverse problems

The notion of optimal (in the MSE sense) linear estimator is very useful as it can be understood in a very general sense. Consider two random vectors
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{w}
drawn from distributions
\pi(\mathbf{z})
and
\pi(\mathbf{w})
which have finite first and second moments. Then, if one wishes to estimate
\mathbf{z}
from
\mathbf{w}
, the LMMSE is given by a closed form formula
(
Kay 1998
, Equation 12.20)
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{z}}=\mathbf{L}^{*}\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{b}^{*}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{z}\mathbf{w}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{w}\mathbf{w}}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{w}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{w}\right]\right)+\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{z}\right]

(LMMSE)

\displaystyle\text{where }\left(\mathbf{L}^{*},\mathbf{b}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\Argmin_{\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{w}}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{z}-\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{w}+\mathbf{b}\right)\right\rVert^{2}\right].

The fact that this hold for any kind of relationship between
\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{w}
is the reason why the LMMSE or its derivatives have been so important over many fields.

In the case of linear non-blind inverse problems as described in (
1
) where the forward operator
\mathbf{A}
is fixed, we have a clear relationship between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
which allows us to write down
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
as we wish to recover
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathbf{y}
. In that context, if the noise is centered and independent from the signal, it is known
(
Kay 1998
, Theorem 12.1)
that the LMMSE is given by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{LLMSE}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}\right]+\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}+\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}\right]\right).

(LMMSE-D)

Note here, that the invertibility of
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
is either not an issue as
\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
can often be full-rank, or if it is not, one can use the pseudo-inverse thanks to the orthogonality principle. Moreover, we will consider in the rest of this work a regularized version of the LMMSE, which ensures de-facto the invertibility.

While this result for non-blind inverse problems has been known for a long time, there is no such result, to the best of our knowledge, for the blind case where we consider that
\mathbf{A}
is random. Importantly, while in the blind setting the true goal is still to recover the signal
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathbf{y}
, it can also be very informative to recover the forward operator
\mathbf{A}
. In the following we will see how one can extend the LMMSE framework to the blind inverse problem setting to recover
\mathbf{x}
or
\mathbf{A}
or even perform a joint estimation.

General assumptions

For the remaining of this work, we will consider the following general assumptions:

(A-1)

\pi(\mathbf{A})
,
\pi(\mathbf{x})
and
\pi(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})
are mutually independent.

(A-2)

\pi(\mathbf{A})
,
\pi(\mathbf{x})
and
\pi(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})
have finite first and second moments.

(A-3)

\pi(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})
is a centered distribution with
\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=\beta\mathbf{I}
for
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
.

(A-4)

Without loss of generality,
\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert
.

Assumption
(A-1)
is standard and general, as for many applications, the uncertainty over the operator is independent from the observed signal. For example, the uncertainty over the convolution kernel of a telescope (imagine unknown meteorological conditions) is independent from the part of the sky it observes. The independence of the noise distribution is also very standard and correspond to widely appearing Gaussian noise for example. Additionaly, the noise follow
(A-3)
which is made for simplicity of presentation but does not impede the generality of our results. Moreover,
(A-2)
is necessary to the existence of our estimators and is once again very standard in inverse problems. Finally,
(A-4)
is also made for simplicity in the computation of some constant in our theorem but can be removed easily at the cost of slightly less clear constants.

2.1
LMMSE to recover
\mathbf{x}

Let us now go to the blind setting where
\mathbf{A}
is random and derive an LMMSE estimator to recover
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathbf{y}
. Looking back at the general (
LMMSE
), we need to derive the expression of
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
in our blind setting. Let us start by finding that
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{x}-\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\right)\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}-\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\right)^{\top}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\right]-\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}+\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}-\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}

where we used that
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{x}
are independent, and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
is zero-mean.

The derivation of
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
requires more attention. We first see by the fact that the mean of
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
is centered that

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{y}-\theta_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\left(\mathbf{y}-\theta_{\mathbf{y}}\right)^{\top}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\right]-\theta_{\mathbf{y}}\theta_{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}+\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}

One can see that if
\mathbf{A}
is not random, this formulation converge back to
LMMSE-D
. To understand how it changes for the blind case, let us look at one element of
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\right]
:

\displaystyle\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\right]\right)_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{q=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}_{ik}\mathbf{A}_{jq}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{q}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{q=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{A}_{j}}-\theta_{\mathbf{A}_{i}}\theta_{\mathbf{A}_{j}}^{\top}\right)_{kq}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}-\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)_{kq}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{q=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{A}_{j}}\odot\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right)_{kq}+\theta_{\mathbf{A}_{i}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\theta_{\mathbf{A}_{j}}+\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{A}_{j}}\theta_{\mathbf{x}}+\theta_{\mathbf{y}_{i}}\theta_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}.

Let us define the matrix
\mathbf{D}
such that
\mathbf{D}_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{q=1}^{n}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{A}_{j}}\odot\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right)_{kq}
, and let
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}}
be the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{a}=\mathrm{Vec}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{mn}
. With these element defined, we can express
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
in the blind case as

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}=\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}+\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}+\mathbf{D}+\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}

(3)

A few remarks are in order. First, we see that now
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
has four main components. The first one that does not change is the noise, which we need to take into account the same way as in the non-blind case. The three other components represent the interactions between the randomness of
\mathbf{x}
and the one of
\mathbf{A}
. One term represent how the covariance of
\mathbf{x}
interacts with the mean of
\mathbf{A}
, this is the term that appear in (
LMMSE-D
) when
\mathbf{A}
is not random and where
\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{A}
. The second term represent the interaction between the covariance of
\mathbf{A}
with the mean of
\mathbf{x}
, which disappear if
\mathbf{A}
is deterministic or if
\mathbf{x}
is centered. Finally, the component given by the matrix
\mathbf{D}
represent the interaction between the two covariances of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{A}
. There are two special cases of interest that simplify
\mathbf{D}
:

Independent lines
: If
\mathbf{A}
has independent lines, then, when
i\neq j
,
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{A}_{j}}=0
which means that
\mathbf{D}
is diagonal and the element of the diagonal are a linear combination of the elements of
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{A}_{i}\mathbf{A}_{i}}
.

Independent columns/entries
: The case where
\mathbf{A}
has independent columns is equivalent to the matrix having independent entries, and it simplifies
\mathbf{D}
even more such that
\mathbf{D}=\mathrm{diag}\left(\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{A})\mathrm{Var}(\mathbf{x})\right)
. In that case, only the variances of the elements of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{x}
matters.

Now that we have defined correctly
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
for our blind case, we can give the the LMMSE for blind inverse problems when we want to recover
\mathbf{x}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\text{LMMSE}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}\right]+\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\left(\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}+\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}+\mathbf{D}+\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-\theta_{\mathbf{y}}\right)

(LMMSE-B)

An important result in the non-blind case is that there is a direct link between the LMMSE and the solution of a Tikhonov regularized problem
(
Kay 1998
, Theorem 12.1)
. This connection is still valid in the blind case as we state in the following lemma:

Lemma 2.1

((
LMMSE-B
) as Tikhonov solution)
.

Assume that
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
are invertibles. Then, the estimate given in (
LMMSE-B
) is the solution of the generalized Tikhonov problem:

\displaystyle\Argmin_{\mathbf{z}}\left\lVert\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{C}_{p}^{-1}}+\left\lVert\mathbf{z}-\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}}

(Tikh-sig)

where
\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbf{z}^{\top}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{z}
and
\mathbf{C}_{p}=\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}+\mathbf{D}+\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}
.

Proof.

See Section
5.1

∎

A special case of this result would be when
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{I})
,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}\mathbf{I})
and when
\mathbf{A}
has independent columns and that the sum of variances of each line
i
are equal to a constant, i.e.,
\sum_{j}^{m}\sigma_{\mathbf{A}_{ij}}^{2}=c_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}
. In that case we have that
\mathbf{C}_{p}=(c_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}\sigma_{x}^{2}+\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2})\mathbf{I}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}=\sigma_{x}^{2}\mathbf{I}
, which means that the LMMSE correspond to the solution of a classical Tikhonov problem with regularization parameter
\lambda=c_{\mathbf{A}}^{2}+\frac{\sigma_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}^{2}}{\sigma_{x}^{2}}
.

2.2
LMMSE to recover
\mathbf{A}

Since we are considering the particular setting of blind inverse problems, we can ask not only the classical and important question of estimating
\mathbf{x}
from
\mathbf{y}
, but also, more uniquely, the question of estimating
\mathbf{A}
from
\mathbf{y}
. As for the estimation of
\mathbf{x}
, we can ask ourselve what would be the optimal linear estimator
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{mn\times m}
such that:

\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{A}}=\Argmin_{\mathbf{L}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\mathbf{y}-\mathrm{Vec}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\right\rVert^{2}\right].

Let us recall
\mathbf{a}=\mathrm{Vec}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)
and the associated covariance matrix
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}}
that gives the covariance of each entries of
\mathbf{A}
. By (
LMMSE
), we know that
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{A}}
will take the form
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}
. As we already have calculated
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
in (
3
), the question is about the form of
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{y}}
. To understand this, we will first provide an observation that will be very useful for the rest of this section, which is that:

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\mathbf{x}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{a}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}

(4)

where
\mathbf{I}_{m}
is the identity matrix of size
m
and
\otimes
is the Kronecker product. We can thus now easily express
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{y}}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\right]-\theta_{\mathbf{a}}\theta_{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^{\top}\right]\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}-\theta_{\mathbf{a}}\theta_{\mathbf{y}}^{\top}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}}-\theta_{\mathbf{a}}\theta_{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}-\theta_{\mathbf{a}}\theta_{\mathbf{a}}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}

where we used the independence between
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
and that
\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right]=0
. Thus we can express the optimal linear estimator of
\mathbf{a}
by using (
3
) to get that:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{a}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}\left(\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}+\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}}\left(\mathbf{I}_{m}\otimes\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\right)^{\top}+\mathbf{D}+\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{y}-\theta_{\mathbf{y}}\right)+\theta_{\mathbf{a}}.

(LMMSE-op)

As for the LMMSE for estimating
\mathbf{x}
, we can express (
LMMSE-op
) as the solution of a Tikhonov regularized problem akin to the result of Lemma
2.1
:

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
are invertibles. Then the estimate given in (
LMMSE-op
) is the solution of the generalized Tikhonov problem:

\displaystyle\Argmin_{\widehat{\mathbf{a}}=\mathrm{Vec}\left(\widehat{\mathbf{A}}\right)}\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{A}}\theta_{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{C}_{p}^{-1}}+\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{a}}-\theta_{\mathbf{a}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}}^{-1}}.

where
\mathbf{C}_{p}=\mathbf{D}+\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}+\theta_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\theta_{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}
.

Proof.

See Section
5.1

∎

It makes sense that weight of the norm that represent the data-fidelity term is the same as in Theorem
2.1
but the weight of the Tikhonov regularization is adapted to the fact that we are regularizing on the operator and not the signal. It is worthwhile to note that here, we are considering the LMMSE that minimizes the
\ell_{2}
norm of the difference of vectorized matrices, which explain why we obtain something so similar to the LMMSE to recover the signal. However, one could wonder if it is possible to consider the LMMSE under adapted matrix norms, such as an operator norm, which might in turn not hold the same link to classical Tikhonov regularization. We leave this question for a future work.

2.3
Remarks on joint LMMSE estimation

We combine the individual LMMSE estimates for the signal
\mathbf{x}
and the forward operator
\mathbf{A}
into a joint optimization framework of the form

\Argmin_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{A}}\left\lVert\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{C}_{p_{1}}^{-1}}+\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\theta_{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{C}_{p_{2}}^{-1}}+\left\lVert\mathbf{x}-\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}}+\left\lVert\mathbf{a}-\theta_{\mathbf{a}}\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}}^{-1}},

(5)

where
\mathbf{C}_{p_{1}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{p_{1}}
are chosen as the covariance matrices
\mathbf{C}_{p}
appearing in Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
, respectively. In contrast to classical MAP-based formulations, which involve a joint data-fidelity term of the form
\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}
and thus lead to intrinsically non-convex optimization problems and potential issues (see, e.g.,
Rameshan et al. 2012
;
Perrone and Favaro 2014
;
Nguyen et al. 2025
), the objective (
5
) relies on a different structure of the data-fidelity term. Specifically, the contribution of each unknown is marginalized by taking expectations with respect to the other variable, resulting in two decoupled quadratic data terms. This decoupling renders the joint optimization convex and allows the minimization to be carried out in a single step, since the estimate of
\mathbf{x}
(resp.
\mathbf{A}
) does not depend on the current estimate of
\mathbf{A}
(resp.
\mathbf{x}
). Proceeding as in Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
, one can show that solving (
5
) is equivalent to computing the joint linear MMSE estimator

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}},\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{a}}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{y}}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{a}}\mathbf{y}-\mathrm{Vec}\left(\mathbf{A}\right)\right\rVert^{2}\right],

that is, minimizing the sum of the mean squared errors associated with the signal and operator estimates. By linearity, this joint problem decomposes into two independent LMMSE problems with respect to
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{A}
, which can be solved separately. By the tower property, the joint risk can equivalently be expressed in terms of conditional risks, e.g.,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{y}}\left[\|\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{A}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{A}}\left[\|\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}\mid\mathbf{A}\right]\right]

This reformulation becomes substantively different only if the inner estimator is allowed to depend on the conditioning variable, or on an estimate thereof. In that case, the design of the estimator naturally leads to a nested (bilevel) optimization problem, in which the signal is estimated in a lower-level problem for a fixed forward operator, while the operator itself is identified at the upper level based on the resulting reconstruction, see, e.g.,
Hintermüller and
Wu 2015
.

In the present work, we do not pursue this bilevel formulation further and focus instead on the estimation of the signal for a fixed joint LMMSE structure; exploring fully bilevel formulations for blind LMMSE estimation constitutes a natural direction for future work.

3
Empirical reconstruction of
\mathbf{x}

Now that we established the formulation of the LMMSE for our blind setting, it is natural to wonder what kind of theoretical performance guarantees one should except from such estimators. In order to do this, we will consider a setting where the information from the different probability distributions necessary to build the LMMSE can only be approximated by using a finite number of paired signal-observation samples. That is, we consider that we have access to
N
i.i.d. samples
(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{y}_{k})_{k=1}^{N}
where
\mathbf{x}_{k}\sim\pi(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{y}_{k}=\mathbf{A}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k}
with
\mathbf{A}_{k}\sim\pi(\mathbf{A})
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{k}\sim\pi(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})
. From these samples, we build a regularized empirical LMMSE operator
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}
such that

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}.

(6)

We regularize the empirical approximation of
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
by adding
\lambda\mathbf{I}
where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{*}
parametrize the strength of the regularization. This regularization ensures the invertibility of the matrix and will allow us to balance the classical bias-variance tradeoff that appear when building such empirical estimators.

Indeed, the error that our empirical LMMSE will make is split between an estimation error, that quantifies the distance between the empirical estimator and the ideal one (variance), and an approximation error that is due to the inherent randomness of the problem, from the noise and the forward operator, alongside the limitation of using a linear estimator (bias). These two error sources will be studied and discussed on their own in Section
3.1
and Section
3.2
.

The approximation error is considered under a Hölder source condition, which we discuss in more depth in Section
3.1
, to ensure proper convergence rates of the problem.

Definition 3.1

(Hölder source condition with random singular values)
.

We say that a distribution
\pi(\mathbf{x})
obey a source condition if

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{A}}\left[\left(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\right)\right]^{\alpha},

(7)

with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
.

We present in the next theorem our main theoretical error bound that balances this bias-variance trade-of and give performance guarantees when using an empirical LMMSE in a blind inverse problem setting.

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume that
\pi(\mathbf{x})
obeys Definition
3.1
with
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{+}_{*}
and that all
\mathbf{A}\sim\pi(\mathbf{A})
share the same singular vectors where the
i
th singular value
\sigma_{i}
is sampled (i.i.d.) from
p_{\sigma_{i}}
. Let

K=\log\left(n+m\right)\frac{4\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert}{3\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert^{2}}
be a constant that depends on the problem at hand and
\gamma=\sigma_{\min}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right)
. Then if
N>K
and
\lambda+\gamma\geq 4\sqrt{\frac{K}{N}}
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{\lambda}_{\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\lesssim m\left(\beta+\lambda\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}+\sum_{i}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}\right]^{\alpha}\frac{\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}{1+\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}+m\frac{C}{\left(\gamma+\lambda\right)N}\left(1+\frac{1}{\left(\gamma+\lambda\right)^{2}}+\frac{1}{\gamma+\lambda}\right)

with probability
1-2\left(n+m\right)^{-1}
where
C
depends on
\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert
and
K
.

Proof.

This result is obtained by combining the results from Theorem
3.3
and Corollary
3.5
.
∎

A few remarks are in order. The first one is that we can clearly separate the error from the uncertainties inherent to the problem from the error that comes from using a finite sample approximation of the LMMSE. The first term that depends on
\beta
is very classical in non-blind inverse problem literature and is known as the “convergence rate” which indicates how the expected error bound evolve when the noise level decreases. However, in our blind case we have a new term that is added to the convergence rate which encodes the error that one can expect given the level of randomness over the forward operator. More precisely, we see that our rate depends on the coefficient of variation of each singular value, scaled by its magnitude, which is natural as high level of randomness on an important singular value will be much more impactful on the forward operator than the same randomness on a small magnitude singular value.

The last term represents the sampling error due to having only a finite number of sample from
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
to approximate the moments of their distributions. As is classical for such sampling bounds in inverse problems, it decreases with a rate of
O(1/N)
and is scaled by some power of
(\gamma+\lambda)^{-1}
. Notably, for ill-conditioned problems,
\gamma
will be very small and the regularization term allows to avoid the explosion of
1/\gamma
that would in turn needs for
N
to be extremely large to compensate the ill-posedness. Thus,
\lambda
here controls the scaling that ensures that even with a limited number of samples, this sampling bound stays meaningful.

However,
\lambda
has a negative impact on the noise convergence rate which means that
\lambda
acts a trade-off to balance the noise level and the number of available samples given the ill-posedness of the problem.. While there exists an optimal choice of
\lambda
, this choice is dependent on exact information of the source condition,
\gamma
, and
\beta
which are typically not available with enough precision. We also note that while providing a theoretical guarantee in expectation, the best
\lambda
that optimizes this bound can be different from what is the best
\lambda
for a practical problem. Let us now examine how each error behave in more details.

3.1
Approximation error under spectral regularization

In this section we will study in more detail the approximation error of
\mathbf{L}_{\mathbf{x}}^{\lambda}
, that is, the regularized version of the estimator (
LMMSE-B
). As mentioned before, we consider this error under the source condition (i.e., spectral regularity), given in definition
3.1
, which is very common when deriving convergence rates for inverse problems, and which is a necessary condition to achieve fast convergence rates. Furthermore, as a simplification in order to understand what is at play when we are studying random operators, we will consider that all our potential operators share the same singular vectors and that only the singular values are drawn from some probability distribution.

The Hölder source condition for the blind case is a natural extension of the source condition in the non-blind case which often reads as
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}=\left(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\right)^{\alpha}
. In both these context,
\alpha
denotes a notion of regularity of the solution, where the higher
\alpha
is, the smoother the solution. For the non blind case, it directly translate to better convergence rates with respect to the noise, but we will see that this changes in the blind setting.

The condition that all our forward operators share the same singular values, while limiting ourselves, does not render our results meaningless. Indeed, many real-world problems are based on specific type of operator which share the same basis. For example, if you consider a set of blurring operators, they are all diagonalizable on a Fourrier basis. This means that our theorem still apply to many practical problems.

We now write the approximation error bound that can be obtained for the blind case under spectral regularity:

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider a distribution of forward operator
\mathbf{A}\sim\pi(\mathbf{A})
that shares the same fixed singular vectors. Assume that the distribution of signals
\mathbf{x}\sim\pi(\mathbf{x})
obeys
3.1
. Then, the expected reconstruction error is bounded such that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq m\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\right)\left(\beta+\lambda\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}+C_{3}\sum_{i}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}\right]^{\alpha}\frac{\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}{1+\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}

(8)

with
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3}\in[0,1[
and
\sigma_{i}
the
i
-th singular value of
\mathbf{A}
.

Proof.

For the sake of readability, as the proof is a bit lengthy, we delegate it to Section
5.2.1

∎

As already discussed after Theorem
3.2
, the first term in the bound, known as the convergence rate, is very classical for the non-blind setting and represents the effect that should be expected from the noise given the regularity of the problem. In this bound, the regularization term
\lambda
has a detrimental effect as it acts as a perturbation to the true LMMSE described in (
LMMSE-B
). The parameter
\alpha
of the source condition dictates the rate of convergence with the power
\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}
, which gets better (when
\beta+\lambda\leq 1
) the bigger
\alpha
is.

The second term, which is novel due to the blind setting, encodes the impact of the randomness of the forward operator. The first thing to note is that in the non-blind setting,
\CV(\sigma_{i})=0
, which cancels this second term and gives us the classical non-blind inequality, making this blind bound a generalization of the non-blind one. The second thing to note is that the role of
\alpha
for this second term is different, as
\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}\right]^{\alpha}
grows exponentially with
\alpha
. This means that for such source condition in the blind case, there is a trade-off between the two terms in the bound given by the
\alpha
term, where in the non blind case, the higher the
\alpha
, the better.

The convergence rate of the second term is given by
\frac{\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}{1+\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}
which is independent of
\alpha
and converge faster than the noise term, which is limited to a linear convergence rate at best. Of course, this is limited by the exponential effect of
\alpha
on this second term, which very quickly make it much bigger than the noise term, even for singular values with values close to 1. This indicates clearly that the main source of error under this blind setting is due to the randomness of the operator and not anymore by the noise. This is natural as one can intuit how the randomness of the operator will propagate on the measurement at a bigger scale than the noise for the same initial randomness level.

3.2
Sampling bounds

Let us now turn to the estimation error that affect Theorem
3.2
to study it in more detail. This error is due to the fact that we have only a finite number of samples to estimate
\mathbf{L}^{\lambda}
using (
6
). We assume in this work that our signals and observations are bounded (i.e.,
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert\leq\rho_{\mathbf{x}}
and
\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert\leq\rho_{\mathbf{y}}
with
\rho_{\mathbf{x}}<\infty
and
\rho_{\mathbf{y}}<\infty
) almost surely. Let us start by giving our main sampling theorem that allow to obtain with high probability an operator arbitrarly close to the LMMSE given enough samples:

Theorem 3.4

(Sampling bound of LMMSE)
.

Consider the empirical operator
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{\lambda}
described in (
6
) for some
\lambda>0
. Then, for any positive
\xi<\lambda+\gamma
, where
\gamma=\sigma_{\min}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right)>0
, and
d>0
, if the number of samples
N
is such that

\displaystyle N>\log\left(\frac{n+m}{d}\right)\frac{2\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\left(3+2\xi\right)}{3\xi^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert^{2}},

(9)

the error of
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{\lambda}
with respect to the true LMMSE operator
\mathbf{L}^{\lambda}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}^{\lambda}\mathbf{y}-\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{\lambda}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq m\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert^{2}\frac{\xi^{2}}{\gamma+\lambda}\left(1+\frac{\left(1+\xi\right)^{2}}{\left(\gamma+\lambda-\xi\right)^{2}}+\frac{2\left(1+\xi\right)}{\left(\gamma+\lambda-\xi\right)}\right)

(10)

with probability at least
1-2d
.

Proof.

See Section
5.3.1

∎

This theorem is very general and it gives different ways of interpreting the bound for a given number of sample
N
. The theorem is dependent on three parameters,
\xi
,
\lambda
and
d
, which can be balanced in various ways. The main choice lie in the value of
\xi
(and therefore an associated
\lambda
) as for the error bound in (
10
), low values of
\xi
are better, but we still need
N
to reach condition (
9
), where the lower
\xi
, the harder the condition is. One can also somewhat play on the variable
d
to make this condition slightly easier at the cost of rapidly losing probability for the theorem to hold.

We also note the impact of
\lambda
, for which the value should be chosen depending on the conditioning given by
\gamma
. Indeed,
\lambda
is crucial for ill-posed problem with
\gamma
close to zero, as it ensures that
\frac{1}{\gamma+\lambda}
does not explode, which is necessary for the bound to be meaningful. However,
\lambda
should be chosen with caution as it has a negative impact on the approximation bound previously discussed, and the optimization of this sampling bound should be done by tuning appropriately
\xi
.

As this theorem is quite general, it renders the dependence of our sampling bound to our number of samples somewhat obscure. We thus provide a corollary to Theorem
3.4
where we specialized the values of
\xi
and
d
to make this dependency on
N
appear more clearly.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
K=\log\left(n+m\right)\frac{4\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert}{3\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert^{2}}
be a constant that depends on the problem at hand, then if
N>K
and
\lambda+\gamma\geq 4\sqrt{\frac{K}{N}}
, we have that the error of
\widehat{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda}
with respect to the LMMSE operator
\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}
is bounded by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}^{\lambda}\mathbf{y}-\widehat{\mathbf{L}}^{\lambda}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq m\frac{9\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert^{2}K}{\left(\gamma+\lambda\right)N}\left(1+\frac{16}{\left(\gamma+\lambda\right)^{2}}+\frac{8}{\gamma+\lambda}\right)

with probability at least
1-2\left(n+m\right)^{-1}
.

Proof.

See Section
5.3.2

∎

Remark.
We want to point out that for the sake of readability of the corollary, we chose to use the bound
\xi=c\sqrt{\frac{K}{N}}
with
c=2
which in turn necessitate that
N>K
. However, in cases where
K
is large with respect to
N
, one can choose to use bigger values of
c
which constrain more
\lambda
but in turn exponentially reduce the condition on
N
with respect to
K
. For example, taking
c=4
, we only need that
N>\frac{K}{26^{2}}
. This means that even for relativaly low number of samples with respect to
K
, similar sampling bound can be achieved.

4
Numerical Experiments

In this section we present a set of numerical experiments illustrating the
behaviour of the linear LMMSE estimators introduced in
Sections
2
and
3
under uncertainty on both the data
distribution and the measurement operator.
All simulations are performed on synthetic one-dimensional signals of
length
n
. For this, we first
provide in Section
4.1
several numerical details clarifying the statistics of the
synthetic signals and measurements used throughout the numerical study.

As a first numerical test, we focus on the approximation error bound under spectral regularization derived in Theorem
3.3
, and verify numerically that the reconstruction error behaves in accordance with the source-condition–dependent constants.

As a second numerical verification, we consider the results reported in Section
3.2
.
In particular, we focus on the sampling-induced variance component of the LLMSE estimator and show empirically that the empirical operator
\hat{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda}
converges to its population counterpart
\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}
at the predicted rate.

4.1
Data Generation: Sinusoidal Signals with Uncertain Measurements

We begin by describing the synthetic data model used throughout the
numerical section.
Each ground-truth signal
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is generated as a
non-zero-mean sinusoid corrupted by additive Gaussian perturbations,
namely, for
i=1,\ldots,n

x_{i}=b\sin\left(\frac{2\pi\,i}{n}\right)+z_{i},

(11)

where
b>0
, and
\mathbf{z}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,b\mathbf{I}_{n})
represents random variability across the signal family.
This construction yields a class of signals whose mean function exhibits
a clear non-zero oscillatory profile, while its standard deviation
captures the spread induced by noise.
In
Figure
1(a)
, we report the exact and empirical signal estimated by a collection of
N=1000
i.i.d. samples

\{\mathbf{x}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
. The shaded areas indicate
\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\pm\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}
, where

\hat{\sigma}_{\mathbf{x}}
denotes the square root of the empirical diagonal of

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
with
b=2.
The corresponding measurements
\{\mathbf{y}^{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
are generated by convolving each sample
\mathbf{x}^{j}
with an
uncertain smoothing kernel and additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN). These kernels are generated as follows: let
\bar{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
denote a fixed
reference kernel, chosen here as a normalized Gaussian one. For each
realization
j=1,\ldots,N
and each
i=1,\dots,n
, we draw an independent perturbation of the kernel mean,
modeled as

\mathbf{k}^{j}_{i}=\bar{\mathbf{k}}_{i-\theta^{j}},\qquad\theta^{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\theta}^{2}),

so that the effective kernel is shifted randomly along the signal
domain. By incorporating also AWGN
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_{n}^{2}\mathbf{I}_{n})
, the resulting modelling process thus takes the form:

\mathbf{y}^{j}=\mathbf{k}^{j}\ast\mathbf{x}^{j}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}

and it can be employed to model uncertainty in the alignment or calibration of
measurement devices, depending on the parameters
\theta
and
\sigma_{n}
controlling the level of
operator uncertainty and measurement noise, respectively.

Remark 4.1

(Particular case: convolution matrices)
.

In the setting of our experiments, the sampled random operators
\mathbf{A}^{j}
are
convolution matrices. It is therefore useful to make explicit how the
general formulas in Sections
2
and

3
specialize in this structured case and how
one can exploit this structure in numerical experiments.

Assume for simplicity that
m=n
and that
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is
the matrix associated with a (vectorised) convolution kernel

\mathbf{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
(for instance, the vectorised form of a

d\times d
kernel with
d^{2}=n
). Under periodic boundary
conditions, such matrices are block-circulant with circulant blocks
(BCCB) and can be represented as linear combinations of “shift”
matrices. More precisely, let
\{\mathbf{B}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
denote the
family of BCCB matrices corresponding to a unit impulse located at
position
i
, so that applying
\mathbf{B}_{i}
amounts to circularly
shifting the signal by
i
pixels. Then,
\mathbf{A}
can
be written as

\mathbf{A}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}k_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i}=:T(\mathbf{k}),

where
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a linear map taking kernels as input and outputting
convolution matrices.

Since
T
is linear, it admits a matrix representation: by stacking
the coefficients of
\mathbf{A}
into a vector, we obtain

\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{A})\;=\;\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{k},\qquad\mathbf{P}=\bigl[\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{B}_{1}),\ldots,\mathrm{vec}(\mathbf{B}_{n})\bigr]\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{2}\times n},

that is, each column of
\mathbf{P}
contains the vectorised version
of one shift matrix. This representation is particularly convenient
when
\mathbf{k}
is random as it allows to transfer knowledge on the distribution on
\mathbf{k}
directly into the distribution of
\mathbf{A}
. Precisely, if
\mathbf{k}
has mean

\theta_{\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and covariance

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, then by
linearity of expectation and covariance we obtain

\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{A}}\left[\mathbf{A}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{k}}\left[T(\mathbf{k})\right]=T\!\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{k}}\left[\mathbf{k}\right]\bigr)=T(\theta_{\mathbf{k}}),

so the mean operator is simply the convolution matrix associated with
the average kernel.

Furthermore, the covariance of
\mathbf{A}
is obtained by expanding

\mathbf{A}-\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}(k_{i}-(\theta_{\mathbf{k}})_{i})\,\mathbf{B}_{i}.

Using bilinearity of the covariance and the fact that the

\mathbf{B}_{i}
are deterministic, we obtain

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbf{C}_{k_{i},k_{j}}\,(\mathbf{B}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{B}_{j}),

where each covariance entry
\mathbf{C}_{k_{i},k_{j}}
multiplies the
appropriate shift-matrix pair. Thus all second-order statistics of

\mathbf{A}
are fully determined by the statistics of the kernel

\mathbf{k}
and the fixed matrices
\mathbf{B}_{i}
. From a computational perspective, this representation is extremely
advantageous: once the shift matrices
\mathbf{B}_{i}
are
precomputed, updating
\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{A},\mathbf{A}}
for different
kernel distributions reduces to updating the much lower-dimensional
kernel statistics
\theta_{\mathbf{k}}
and

\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{k},\mathbf{k}}
.

Figures
1(b)
and
1(c)

illustrate exemplar realizations of
\mathbf{y}
along with samples of the
perturbed kernels. In the following experiments, we use a Gaussian kernel
\bar{\mathbf{k}}
of size
d=21

Lemma 5.1

(Operator norm of
\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}
)
.

Assume that any operator

share a same diagonalization basis. Then we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\right\rVert\leq C\left(\beta+\lambda\right)^{-\frac{1/2}{\alpha+1}}

Proof.

We start by using the fact that our random operators share fixed singular vectors with
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
due to our source condition (
3.1
). Before diving in, let us denote that by a singular value decomposition we define
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}\right]=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}_{\mu}\mathbf{V}

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\right]=\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}^{2}}\mathbf{V}
where
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
are unitary matrices and
\mathbf{D}_{\mu}
and
\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}^{2}}
are diagonal matrices. We can now get that :

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}^{\top}\right]\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\right]+\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}^{2}}^{\alpha}\mathbf{D}_{\mu}\mathbf{V}\left(\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}^{2}}^{\alpha+1}\mathbf{V}+\left(\beta+\lambda\right)\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}^{2}}^{\alpha}\mathbf{D}_{\mu}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}^{2}}^{\alpha+1}+\left(\beta+\lambda\right)\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{V}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\max_{i\in\{1\dots m\}}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}^{2}}^{\alpha}\mathbf{D}_{\mu}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}^{2}}^{\alpha+1}+\left(\beta+\lambda\right)\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right)_{i,i}

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\sigma)=\frac{\sigma^{\alpha}}{\left(\sigma^{\alpha+1}+\beta\right)^{2}}\left(\left(\alpha+1/2\right)\beta-\sigma^{\alpha+1}\right),

which means that we need to maximize

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\right\rVert\leq f(\sigma^{*})=\frac{\left(\alpha+1/2\right)^{\frac{\alpha+1/2}{\alpha+1}}}{\alpha+1}\left(\beta+\lambda\right)^{-\frac{1/2}{\alpha+1}}

∎

14
), we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq mC_{1}\beta\left(\beta+\lambda\right)^{-\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}\leq mC_{1}\left(\beta+\lambda\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}

where
C_{1}\in]0,1[
.

Step 3: Bound the approximation error.
Let us look at the approximation error when dealing with random operators. We start by observing that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr\left(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\right]\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{A}}\left[\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\right]\right)

where we used the trace trick, the cyclical property of the trace, the linearity of the expectation and the law of total expectation. Now we can use that
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\right]=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}+\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}
and see by trace properties that:

\displaystyle\Tr\left(\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{A}}\left[\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\Tr\left(\theta_{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\theta_{\mathbf{x}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert^{2}\right],

which means that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert^{2}\right]=\Tr\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\right]\right).

(15)

We first note that under our assumptions, by Lemma
5.2
we have that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\right]\prec\mathbf{V}^{\top}\left(\beta_{\lambda}^{2}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}^{2}}^{\alpha+1}+\beta_{\lambda}\mathbf{I}\right)^{-2}+\mathbf{D}_{\CV^{2}}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{D}_{\CV^{2}}\right)^{-1}\right)\mathbf{V}.

Going back to (
15
), we can now see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle<\Tr\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}^{2}}^{\alpha}\left(\beta_{\lambda}^{2}\left(\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{A}^{2}}^{\alpha+1}+\beta_{\lambda}\mathbf{I}\right)^{-2}+\mathbf{D}_{\CV^{2}}\left(\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{D}_{\CV^{2}}\right)^{-1}\right)\right)

(16)

\displaystyle<\sum_{i=1}^{m}\frac{\beta_{\lambda}^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}\right]^{\alpha}}{\left(\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}\right]^{\alpha+1}+\beta_{\lambda}\right)^{2}}+\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}\right]^{\alpha}\frac{\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}{1+\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}.

Our last step is to bound the first term in the sum. For this, we can do the same analysis as in the error noise to find an upper bound on this last expression. To that end, we define the function

\displaystyle f(\sigma)=\frac{\beta_{\lambda}^{2}\sigma^{\alpha}}{\left(\sigma^{\alpha+1}+\beta_{\lambda}\right)^{2}}

and obtain by standard calculus that the derivative of
f
is given

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\sigma)=\beta_{\lambda}^{2}\frac{\sigma^{\alpha-1}\left(\beta\alpha-\left(\alpha+2\right)\sigma^{\alpha+1}\right)}{\left(\sigma^{\alpha+1}+\beta_{\lambda}\right)^{3}},

where when we equate to zero the derivative we obtain
\sigma^{*}=\left(\frac{\alpha\beta_{\lambda}}{\alpha+2}\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}
. We can now input this into
f
to have that

\displaystyle f(\sigma^{*})=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\left(1+\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+2}\right)^{-2}\beta^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\leq C_{2}\beta^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}},

where
C_{2}\in]0,1[
.
This means that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{b}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle<\sum_{i=1}^{m}C_{2}\beta^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}+\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}\right]^{\alpha}\frac{\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}{1+\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}.

Let us finally put everything together to obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{b}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq m\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\right)\beta_{\lambda}^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}+\sum_{i}^{m}\mathbb{E}\left[\sigma_{i}^{2}\right]^{\alpha}\frac{\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}{1+\CV^{2}\left(\sigma_{i}\right)}

which gives the claim.

5.3
Proofs for the sampling theorem

We start by giving some useful lemmas that will be of use for the proof of the theorem. We start with a lemma that bound the Lipschitz constant of operators of the LMMSE type.

Lemma 5.3

(MSE of LMMSE operators)
.

Consider the regularized LMMSE operator
\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}
and the approximate one
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda}=\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}
with
\lambda\geq 0
where both
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
are symmetric positive definite matrices. We define
\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}=\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}-\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}
,
\delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}=\left\lVert\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\right\rVert
and
\delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}=\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert
. Then we have:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\Tr\left((\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}^{2}+\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\right\rVert\delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}+2\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}\delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}.

Proof.

We start by observing that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{y}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\mathbf{y}^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})\mathbf{y}\right].

From there we use the so-called trace trick and the linearity of expectation to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\mathbf{y}^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})\mathbf{y}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\Tr\left(\mathbf{y}^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})\mathbf{y}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\Tr\left((\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left((\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right).

Let us define the quantities
\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}=\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}-\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}
and
\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}=\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
. By using a mixed-product in
\left(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda}\right)
we can see that:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr\left((\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right)&=\Tr\Big(\left(\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}+\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\right)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}+\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\right)\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\Big)\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}&=\Tr\Big(\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\\
&\qquad+\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\Big)\end{split}

We now use the linearity of the trace and its invariance to circular shift to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr\left((\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right)&=\Tr\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\right)+2\Tr\left(\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right)\\
&+\Tr\left(\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}^{\top}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right).\end{split}

(17)

We can use the fact that
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
is symmetric to observe that
\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\preceq\mathbf{I}
.

Knowing this, let us apply to (
17
) the Von Neumann trace inequality alongside the fact that
\sigma_{i}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{B}\right)\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{A}\right\rVert\left\lVert\mathbf{B}\right\rVert
to get

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\Tr\left((\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})^{\top}(\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda})\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}^{2}+\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\right\rVert\delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}+2\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}\delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}

which is the claim of the theorem.
∎

Lemma 5.4

.

. Assume that

when
N\geq\log\left(\frac{2m}{d}\right)\frac{\rho_{y}\left(6+4\xi\right)}{3\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi^{2}}
.

Lemma 5.5

(Sampling bound of empirical cross-covariance)
.

Consider two finite sequences
\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\mathbf{y}_{k}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
of independent random vectors with dimension
n
and

\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\rho_{x}
and

Finally, we provide a lemma to bound the norm of the difference of two inverses of regularized operators.

Lemma 5.6

(Difference of regularized inverses)
.

Let
\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\in\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
be symmetric matrices such that
\left\lVert\widehat{\mathbf{M}}-\mathbf{M}\right\rVert\leq\xi
with
\xi>0
. Let us also define
\gamma=\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{M})
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}}
. If
\xi<\gamma+\lambda
, then:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}-\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\frac{\xi}{\left(\gamma+\lambda\right)-\xi}

Proof.

Using the fact that for invertible matrices
\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\mathbf{B}^{-1}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\right)\mathbf{B}^{-1}
, we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}-\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\left(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{M}-\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\right)\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\left(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\xi.

(18)

The next step is to bound
\left\lVert\left(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert
where we first reformulate it as

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{I}-\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{M}-\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\right)\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert.

Now we can use the fact that
\xi\leq\gamma+\lambda
which means that
\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{M}-\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\right)\right\rVert<1
and thus by Neumann series, we obtain that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{I}-\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\left(\mathbf{M}-\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\right)\right)^{-1}\right\rVert=\frac{1}{1-\frac{\xi}{\gamma+\lambda}}.

This means that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\frac{1}{1-\frac{\xi}{\gamma+\lambda}}=\frac{1}{\left(\gamma+\lambda\right)-\xi}.

(19)

We can now combine this result with (
5.3
) to get:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}-\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{M}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\frac{\xi}{\left(\gamma+\lambda\right)-\xi}.

(20)

which concludes the proof.
∎

We can now state the proof of our sampling Theorem:

5.3.1
Proof of Theorem
3.4

The starting point of our proof is Lemma
5.3
that tells us that if we have a linear operator of the form of
\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda}
, and if we can bound
\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\right\rVert
,
\left\lVert\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\right\rVert
and
\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert
where
\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}=\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}-\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}
, then we can bound the error of such operator with respect to the LMMSE.

In our case we want to consider as our operator the “empirical” LMMSE, that uses supervised data to estimate
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
and
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}
. More precisely, we will use
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)
where we added the regularization
\lambda\mathbf{I}
to ensure invertibility. In order to obtain our required intermediate bounds, we will rely on Lemma
5.5
and Lemma
5.4
which provide sampling bounds for such empirical (cross-)covariance matrices.

What we can observe is that since
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}+\mathbf{M})
with
\mathbf{M}
positive semi-definite, we have
\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert
. Thus if we consider a number of samples
N
such that
N>\log\left(\frac{n+m}{d}\right)\frac{2\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\left(3+2\xi\right)}{3\xi^{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert^{2}}
, we have by Lemma
5.5
and by Lemma
5.4
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}-\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi

hold with probability
1-2d
by using a union bound. We thus have our first intermediate bound.

In order to have a bound over
\left\lVert\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\right\rVert
, we use Lemma
5.6
with our previous result which gives us that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}-\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\frac{\xi}{\left(\gamma+\lambda\right)-\xi}.

For our last intermediate bound, by combining that
\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert\prec\mathbf{I}
and that for two invertibles matrices
\mathbf{A}^{-1}-\mathbf{B}^{-1}=\mathbf{A}^{-1}\left(\mathbf{B}-\mathbf{A}\right)\mathbf{B}^{-1}
, we obtain that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right)\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\xi\left\lVert\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}+\lambda\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert.

Now we can use the result of (
19
) to obtain our final intermediate bound:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\Delta_{\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{-1}}\right\rVert\leq\frac{\xi}{\gamma+\lambda-\xi}

Equipped with our three required bounds we can go back to Lemma
5.3
and use that
\left\lVert\widetilde{\mathbf{C}}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert(1+\xi)
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\frac{1}{m}\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{y}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert^{2}\frac{\xi^{2}}{\gamma+\lambda}\left(1+\frac{\left(1+\xi\right)^{2}}{\left(\gamma+\lambda-\xi\right)^{2}}+\frac{2\left(1+\xi\right)}{\left(\gamma+\lambda-\xi\right)}\right)

which is the claim of the theorem.

5.3.2
Proof of Corollary
3.5

Let us start from the results of Theorem
3.4
where the goal is to choose an
\xi
that gives us the desired rate. We will choose in our case
\xi=2\sqrt{\frac{K}{N}}
which means that by our assumption on
\lambda
our original bound
\gamma+\lambda>\xi
trivially hold. The next question is when does (
9
) hold. If we input our
\xi
into (
9
) we get:

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle>K\frac{3+2\xi}{\xi^{2}}

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle>\frac{N}{4}\left(3+\frac{\sqrt{K}}{\sqrt{N}}\right)

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle>N\left(3/4+\frac{\sqrt{K}}{4\sqrt{N}}\right)

which holds when
N>K
.

We can now input this choice of
\xi
in (
10
). We first observe that under our choice of
\lambda
and
\xi
, we have that
\frac{\xi}{\gamma+\lambda}\leq 1/2
, which allows us to have the following chain of inequalities:

\displaystyle\frac{\xi+1}{\gamma+\lambda-\xi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma+\lambda}\frac{1+\xi}{1-\frac{\xi}{\gamma+\lambda}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\gamma+\lambda}\left(2+2\xi\right)\leq\frac{4}{\gamma+\lambda}

where we used that when
N>K
then
\xi\leq 1
. With this result in mind, we can replace directly
\xi
in (
10
) to obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}_{\lambda}\mathbf{y}-\widetilde{\mathbf{L}}_{\lambda}\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq m\frac{9\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert^{2}K}{\left(\gamma+\lambda\right)N}\left(1+\frac{16}{\left(\gamma+\lambda\right)^{2}}+\frac{8}{\gamma+\lambda}\right)

which proves the claim.

6
Conclusions and Outlook

In this work, we provided the first theoretical characterization of Linear Minimum Mean Square Estimators (LMMSEs) for blind inverse problems and showed how uncertainty in the forward operator modifies the structure of the covariance matrix of the measurements compared to the non-blind setting. We derived finite-sample complexity bounds under an appropriate source condition, by decomposing the expected error of the empirical LMMSE into the sum of an approximation error and a sampling error. Notably, the approximation error generalizes classical (i.e., non-blind) results by explicitly separating the contributions of measurement noise and operator uncertainty. In contrast, the sampling error bound does not show dependence on the source condition and achieves the same

O(1/N)
convergence rate as in the non-blind case, with
N
denoting the sample size. All theoretical results are validated through a range of numerical experiments.

Future work will focus on extending the presented theoretical analysis to nonlinear estimators tailored to the blind setting, including sparsity-promoting regularization schemes, in the spirit of recent work on learning optimal synthesis operators
Alberti
et al. 2024
. Beyond the linear regime, we aim to further explore the role of (L)MMSE-type estimators in applied inverse problems, where they may provide a principled and computationally efficient alternative to commonly used MAP estimators, which are often suboptimal in practice
Nguyen et al. 2025
. To this end, we plan to investigate learning-based approaches—particularly neural network–based methods—to estimate the components of the LMMSE more efficiently. Building on recent theoretical advances in the non-blind setting, such approaches may improve sample efficiency in high-dimensional regimes while preserving the approximation properties of LMMSE estimators and enabling tighter finite-sample guarantees.

A.1
Proof of Lemma
5.4

Lemma

(
5.4
Sampling bound of empirical covariance)
.

Consider a finite sequence
\{\mathbf{y}_{k}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
of independent random vectors with dimension
m
. Assume that
\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\rho_{\mathbf{y}}
almost surely. Then, with probability at least
1-d
, we have for
\xi>0
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi,

when
N\geq\log\left(\frac{2m}{d}\right)\frac{\rho_{y}\left(6+4\xi\right)}{3\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi^{2}}
.

Proof.

This is a very classical proof but we put it here for the sake of completness. The goal is to use the matrix Bernstein inequality
(
Tropp et al. 2015
, Theorem 6.1.1)
in order to concentrate around its expectation the sum of independent bounded matrices. We will use as our summand
\mathbf{S}_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\left(\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right)
. We can directly see that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{k}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\left(\mathbf{y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}\right]\right)\left(\mathbf{y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}\right]\right)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{y}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}+\left\lVert\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{y}}\left[\left(\mathbf{y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}\right]\right)\left(\mathbf{y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}\right]\right)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\rho_{\mathbf{y}}}{N}.

The other quantity of interest in the matrix Bernstein inequality is the matrix variance statistic of the sum, which for symmetric summands is given by
\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}_{k}\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert
. We can bound this quantity by using the following inequalities:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}_{k}\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right)\left(\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}\right]-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{2}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{y}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}\right]-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{2}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho_{\mathbf{y}}}{N}\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{2}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho_{\mathbf{y}}}{N}\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert

where we used the fact that
-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}^{2}
is a negative semi-definite matrix which we can thus drop in the norm. We can now use these two bounds to calculate our concentration inequality which thus read in our case

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\geq t\right)\leq 2m\exp\left(-\frac{3t^{2}N}{2\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\left(3\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert+2t\right)}\right).

Let us choose
t=\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi
for some
\xi>0
which now gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi\right)\leq 2m\exp\left(-\frac{3\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi^{2}N}{2\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\left(3+2\xi\right)}\right).

Our last step is to find some
N
such that the right-hand side of this equation is equal to
d>0
. We have in a straightforward fashion that we need:

\displaystyle 2m\exp\left(-\frac{3\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi^{2}N}{2\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\left(3+2\xi\right)}\right)\leq d

\displaystyle\frac{3\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi^{2}N}{2\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\left(3+2\xi\right)}\geq\log\left(\frac{2m}{d}\right)

\displaystyle N\geq\log\left(\frac{2m}{d}\right)\frac{2\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\left(3+2\xi\right)}{3\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi^{2}}

which gives the claim of the lemma.
∎

A.2
Proof of Lemma
5.5

Lemma

(
5.5
Sampling bound of empirical cross-covariance)
.

Consider two finite sequences
\{\mathbf{x}_{k}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{\mathbf{y}_{k}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
of independent random vectors with dimension
n
and
m
respectively. Assume that
\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\rho_{x}
and
\left\lVert\mathbf{y}\right\rVert^{2}\leq\rho_{y}
almost surely. Then, with probability at least
1-d
, we have for
\xi>0
that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi,

when
N\geq\log\left(\frac{n+m}{d}\right)\frac{2\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\max\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)\left(3+2\xi\right)}{3\min\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)^{2}\xi^{2}}
.

Proof.

The proof will be similar as the one of Lemma
5.4
with some added subtilities due to the fact that our matrices are not symmetric anymore. Our goal is once again to use the matrix Bernstein inequality
(
Tropp et al. 2015
, Theorem 6.1.1)
where we use now as summands
\mathbf{S}_{k}=\frac{1}{N}\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right)
. To use the matrix Bernstein inequality we want a bound on the norm of each summand and the variance statistic of the sum of summand.

Let us start by bounding the norm of each summand in the following way:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{S}_{k}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}\right]\right)\left(\mathbf{y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}\right]\right)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rVert\left\lVert\mathbf{y}_{k}\right\rVert+\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}\right]\right)\left(\mathbf{y}-\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}\right]\right)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\sqrt{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}\rho_{\mathbf{y}}}}{N}\leq\frac{2\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)}{N}.

We now turn our attention to the variance statistic of the sum of summands. As the summands are not symmetric we have to verify both
\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}_{k}\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert
and
\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{k}\right]\right\rVert
. We start with the bound on
\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}_{k}\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}_{k}\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right)\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right)^{\top}\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right]-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{y}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\right]-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho_{\mathbf{y}}}{N}\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert

where we used that
-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}^{\top}
is negative semi-definite by definition and we could thus drop it in the norm. We now go to the bounding
\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{k}\right]\right\rVert
:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{k}\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{N^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right)\right]\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}\right]-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{N}\left\lVert\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{x}_{k}\right\rVert^{2}\mathbf{y}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}\right]-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\rho_{\mathbf{x}}}{N}\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert

Where the last inequality hold because
-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}^{\top}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
is negative semi-definite. We can now combine the last to bound to see that the variance statistic of our sum of summands is given by:

\displaystyle\max\left(\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}_{k}\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\top}\right]\right\rVert,\left\lVert\sum_{k=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{S}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{S}_{k}\right]\right\rVert\right)\leq\frac{\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\max\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)}{N}.

We are now in the position to calculate our matrix Bernstein concentration inequality which reads:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\geq t\right)\leq\left(n+m\right)\exp\left(-\frac{3t^{2}N}{2\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\left(3\max\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)+2t\right)}\right).

Let us choose
t=\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi
for some
\xi>0
. We first use that:

\displaystyle\min\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\leq\max\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)

to obtain that our inequality is now:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\lVert\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=0}^{N}\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{y}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\geq\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\xi\right)\leq\left(m+n\right)\exp\left(-\frac{3\min\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)^{2}\xi^{2}N}{2\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\max\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)\left(3+2\xi\right)}\right).

The last step is to find some
N
such that the right-hand side of this equation is equal to
d>0
. We obtain our result by the following chain of inequalities:

\displaystyle\left(m+n\right)\exp\left(-\frac{3\min\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)^{2}\xi^{2}N}{2\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\max\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)\left(3+2\xi\right)}\right)\leq d

\displaystyle\frac{3\min\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)^{2}\xi^{2}N}{2\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\max\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)\left(3+2\xi\right)}\geq\log\left(\frac{n+m}{d}\right)

\displaystyle N\geq\log\left(\frac{n+m}{d}\right)\frac{2\max\left(\rho_{\mathbf{x}},\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\right)\max\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)\left(3+2\xi\right)}{3\min\left(\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\right\rVert,\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}\right\rVert\right)^{2}\xi^{2}}

which gives the claim of the lemma.

\mu_{\mathbf{x}}=0

\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\nu}\right]=0

\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\nu}\hat{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}_{\text{LLMSE}}\right]=0

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}^{-1}+\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{D}+\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\right)^{-1}\Theta_{\mathbf{A}}\right)^{-1},

where we used the Woodbury identity for the last line. Note that in the case of linear inverse problems, the diagonal element of

Appendix C
Results in the non-blind setting

Theorem C.1
.

For a fixed operator
\mathbf{A}
, if
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}=\left(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\right)^{\alpha}
, and the noise
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
comes from a centered distribution with covariance
\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=\beta I
independent of
\mathbf{x}
, then the expected reconstruction error is bounded such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq m\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\right)\beta^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}

with
C_{1},C_{2}\in[0,1[
.

Proof.

The proof is very classical as it resembles a lot what is done in Tikhonov minimization with a source condition. The idea is to split the error between a noise error and an approximation error and analyze both independently thanks to the independence between
\mathbf{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
. We start be splitting our problem by seeing that:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}+\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right]++\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right],

(21)

where we used that
\mathbf{x}
and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
are independent and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
is a centered variable. Let us focus first on the noise error term. We start by noting that by using the so called trace trick alongside the fact that
\mu_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=0
and
\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}=\beta\mathbf{I}
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\right\rVert^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}\right]=\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\right\rVert^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right]=\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\right\rVert^{2}\Tr\left(\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\right)=m\beta\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\right\rVert^{2}.

(22)

The next step is thus to bound the operator norm of
\mathbf{L}
. By the definition of the LMMSE and the source condition on
\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}
we have that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{A}^{\top}+\mathbf{C}_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\right)^{\alpha}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{A}\left(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}\right)^{\alpha}\mathbf{A}^{\top}+\beta\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\right\rVert.

Since the operator norm corresponds to the largest singular value of a linear operator, we can use the spectral decomposition
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{U}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{V}
where
\mathbf{U}
and
\mathbf{V}
are unitary matrices and
\mathbf{D}
is a diagonal matrix which holds the singular values of
\mathbf{A}
. If we associate this to the fact that by definition of unitary matrices,
\mathbf{I}=\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{I}\mathbf{V}
, we can obtain that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert\mathbf{V}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{2\alpha+1}\left(\mathbf{D}^{2\alpha+2}+\beta\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{U}^{\top}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=\max_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{2\alpha+1}}{\sigma_{i}^{2\alpha+2}+\beta}

with
\sigma_{i}
is the
i
-th singular value of
\mathbf{D}
. Let us consider the following function

\displaystyle f(\sigma)=\frac{\sigma^{2\alpha+1}}{\sigma^{2\alpha+2}+\beta},

(23)

where by construction we have
\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\right\rVert\leq\max_{\sigma\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}f(\sigma)
. In order to find the maximum of
f
, we rely on the optimality condition and try to equate
f^{\prime}(\sigma)
to zero. By standard calculus we have that

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\sigma)=\frac{\sigma^{2\alpha}}{\left(\sigma^{2\alpha+2}+\beta\right)^{2}}\left(\left(2\alpha+1\right)\beta-\sigma^{2\alpha+2}\right),

which means that we need to maximize
f
we need to take
\sigma^{*}=\left(\beta\left(2\alpha+1\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+2}}
. Inserting this value into
f
we get

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\right\rVert\leq f(\sigma^{*})=\frac{\left(2\alpha+1\right)^{\frac{2\alpha+1}{2\alpha+2}}}{2\alpha+2}\beta^{-\frac{1}{2\alpha+2}}

and thus combining with (
22
) we obtain finally that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\left(\frac{\left(2\alpha+1\right)^{\frac{2\alpha+1}{2\alpha+2}}}{2\alpha+2}\right)^{2}\beta^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}<\beta^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}.

We can now turn to the approximation term in our decomposition. We note that by the trace trick and the fact that
\theta_{\mathbf{x}}=0
, the following hold:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right)

\displaystyle=\Tr\left(\mathbf{C}_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\right).

(24)

By leveraging our source condition, we can use an SVD decomposition to obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{D}^{2\alpha+1}\left(\mathbf{D}^{2\alpha+2}+\beta\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{D}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{D}^{2\alpha+2}-\mathbf{D}^{2\alpha+2}-\beta\mathbf{I}\right)\left(\mathbf{D}^{2\alpha+2}+\beta\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{V}

\displaystyle=\beta\mathbf{V}^{\top}\left(\mathbf{D}^{2\alpha+2}+\beta\mathbf{I}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{V}.

(25)

Combining our last two results we thus have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\beta^{2}\Tr\left(\mathbf{D}^{2\alpha}\left(\mathbf{D}^{2\alpha+2}+\beta\mathbf{I}\right)^{-2}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma_{i}^{2\alpha}}{\left(\sigma_{i}^{2\alpha+2}+\beta\right)^{2}}

with
\sigma_{i}
the singular values of
\mathbf{A}
. We now do the same analysis as in the error noise to find an upper bound on this last expression. To that end, we define the function

\displaystyle f(\sigma)=\frac{\beta^{2}\sigma^{2\alpha}}{\left(\sigma^{2\alpha+2}+\beta\right)^{2}}

and obtain by standard calculus that the derivative of
f
is given by

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\sigma)=2\beta^{2}\frac{\sigma^{2\alpha-1}\left(\beta\alpha-\left(\alpha+2\right)\sigma^{2\alpha+2}\right)}{\left(\sigma^{2\alpha+2}+\beta\right)^{3}},

where when we equate to zero the derivative we obtain
\sigma^{*}=\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{\alpha+2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\alpha+2}}
. We can now input this into
f
to have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\left(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{I}\right)\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq\sum_{i\in\{1,\dots,m\}}f(\sigma^{*})\leq m\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+2}\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\left(\frac{\alpha+2}{2\alpha+2}\right)\beta^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}\leq m\beta^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}.

(26)

We combine our two bounds concerning the noise error and the approximation error to obtain the final claim that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left\lVert\mathbf{L}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{x}\right\rVert^{2}\right]\leq m\left(C_{1}+C_{2}\right)\beta^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}

with
C_{1}<1
and
C_{2}<1
.
∎
