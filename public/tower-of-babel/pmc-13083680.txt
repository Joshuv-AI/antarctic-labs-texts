Radiation-induced remodeling of the T cell landscape in head and neck cancer
Authors: Abdullah A Memon, Imaad Said, Ean Norenberg, Anne Frei, Jamie Foeckler, Fumou Sun, Michael B Dwinell, Robert B Lochhead, Jennifer Bruening, Kenneth Akakpo, Becky Massey, Stuart J Wong, Peiman Hematti, Tyce J Kearl, Musaddiq J Awan, Heather A Himburg, Joseph Zenga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHead and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) is an aggressive malignancy associated with high recurrence rates and substantial treatment-related morbidity. Radiation therapy remains a central component of the management of locoregional disease. Although radiation directly damages malignant cells through DNA breaks and associated stress pathways, radioresistance remains common, and a proportion of patients continue to develop locoregional recurrence or distant metastasis despite adequate locoregional control. Recent advances in single-cell and spatiotemporal profiling of HNSCC have clarified how radiation alters systemic and intra-tumoral T cell populations and produces a post-radiation tumor microenvironment that provides insufficient support for the robust priming of new tumor-reactive responses. These insights help explain why clinical trials combining radiotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors have not reproduced the synergy predicted by preclinical models. At the same time, they point to opportunities to design radiation as a programmable immune adjuvant. In this review, we summarize emerging mechanistic data on radiation-induced remodeling of the T cell landscape, outline the barriers that limit productive immune priming after treatment, and discuss strategies aimed at restoring effective anti-tumor immunity in the post-radiation setting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04389-w.Keyword: Head and neck cancer, Radiation therapy, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), encompassing cancers of the oral cavity, pharynx, and larynx, is an aggressive malignancy associated with high recurrence rates and substantial treatment-related morbidity. Radiation therapy remains a central component of management for locoregionally advanced disease, both as definitive therapy and in the postoperative setting. Although radiation directly damages malignant cells through DNA breaks and associated stress pathways, radioresistance remains common: 15–20% of patients develop locoregional recurrence, and a similar proportion experience distant metastasis despite adequate locoregional control [1, 2]. These outcomes indicate that the radiation-induced immune response is inadequate to eliminate residual malignant cells within the treatment field and fails to generate a systemic response capable of preventing metastatic progression.

At the same time, HNSCC exhibits features of immunogenicity. Many tumors harbor a moderate-to-high mutational burden, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) achieve durable responses in a subset of patients with recurrent or metastatic disease [3–6]. This contrast between measurable tumor immunogenicity and the limited durability of immune responses after standard radiation-based therapy points to a central problem: radiation not only eliminates malignant cells but may also remodel the tumor immune microenvironment in ways that can limit effective anti-tumor immunity. This concern is reinforced by three recent randomized trials that tested the addition of ICIs to radiotherapy in HNSCC. Although supported by preclinical evidence that radiation might augment immune activation [7–12], these studies failed to demonstrate clinical benefit, indicating that current radiation regimens may induce tumor microenvironment (TME) changes that counteract immunological synergy [1, 13, 14] .

Until recently, the immunological effects induced by radiotherapy were understood largely through preclinical models and bulk profiling, which suggested both immunostimulatory and immunosuppressive consequences (Supplementary Fig. 1) [15–17]. Bulk RNA sequencing was foundational for defining dominant radiation-induced transcriptional programs and building clinically testable signatures predicting treatment response [18–20]. However, because these profiles are generated from heterogeneous tumor specimens, they inherently reflect population-level averages rather than cell-resolved biology, limiting mechanistic interpretation and obscuring critical cellular subsets. As such, many details remained unresolved, particularly in human disease. Advances in single-cell and spatial profiling technologies now allow unprecedented resolution of immune dynamics in HNSCC, including temporal analysis of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), myeloid subsets, and stromal interactions across the course of treatment. These data show that radiation profoundly remodels the TME through direct tissue injury, potentiated tumor hypoxia, and alterations in both innate and adaptive immune cell recruitment. Critically, radiation profoundly alters the T cell compartment, such that the post-radiation TME provides insufficient support for the robust priming of new tumor-reactive responses, retaining persistent suppressive influences. Targeted interventions are needed to restore or replace effective anti-tumor immunity. This review is therefore organized around three themes: (1) dynamic alterations in the T cell landscape during radiotherapy, (2) barriers that limit productive immune priming after treatment (Fig. 1), and (3) strategies to re-establish an anti-tumor immune response in the post-radiation setting (Fig. 2). We discuss these in the context of HNSCC as a representative solid tumor, and touch on parallel results in other solid tumors.

Radiation-induced dynamic alterations in the systemic and tumor T cell landscape influence locoregional immunogenicity and distant metastatic control

Profound lymphopenia is a consistent clinical feature of radiation for HNSCC and may undermine both existing and newly generated anti-tumor immune responses. Activated tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) are lost during treatment, regional nodal irradiation may be deleterious to T cell priming, and the systemic pool of circulating effector and naïve T cells is diminished. Together these effects reduce the diversity and durability of anti-tumor immunity during and after radiation.

Radiation-associated systemic lymphocyte depletion

Profound lymphopenia is a consistent clinical feature of radiation for HNSCC, especially with treatment regimens that include concurrent chemotherapy in cases of advanced disease or extracapsular extension [21]. Serial analyses during definitive chemoradiation show circulating lymphocytes can fall by over 70% within two months, and more than 70% of patients experience severe radiation-induced lymphopenia (grade ≥ 3 RIL, < 0.5 × 103/µL) [22, 23]. In multivariate analyses, these patients experience earlier disease progression and reduced survival. Several modeling approaches have been developed to better quantify radiation exposure to circulating immune cells, including effective dose to immune cells (EDIC), hematologic dose (HEDOS), and the circulating lymphocyte dose (CLD). These methods either incorporate computational approaches to simulate blood flow through irradiated volumes over time, or utilize static proportions of cardiac output attributed to major organs and vessels. [24, 25] Across disease sites, higher EDIC and HEDOS values have been associated with more severe radiation-induced lymphopenia and inferior oncologic outcomes, including worse overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) [24, 26–28]. Although no prospective trials specifically testing CLD-limiting strategies have been published, these retrospective and modeling studies indicate that reducing blood pool irradiation may mitigate lymphopenia and potentially improve immune-mediated tumor control. Because circulating lymphocytes are essential for sustaining immune surveillance and trafficking newly primed clones, systemic lymphopenia likely undermines both the maintenance of pre-existing responses and the generation of new ones.

Additionally, specific to the head and neck region, radiation is often unavoidably delivered at high doses to the cervical spine, despite sophisticated radiation planning. This iatrogenic injury to active bone marrow may impair hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell differentiation, leading to quantitative and functional defects in downstream lymphocyte populations, including reduced CD11a (LFA-1) expression, which is critical for effective T cell activation and proliferation [29]. Higher mean cervical spine doses have been independently associated with severe lymphopenia and worse oncologic outcomes [30]. Taken together, reducing unnecessary exposure of circulating blood and active marrow may represent a modifiable strategy to preserve systemic immune competence during radiotherapy.

Radiation-induced rapid and profound TIL depletion

The systemic radiation-induced loss of lymphocytes is mirrored within the tumor, where radiotherapy rapidly depletes resident TILs, including tumor-reactive subsets critical for ongoing immune control. Serial single-cell profiling with paired T cell receptor (TCR) analysis across treatment timepoints, including biopsies obtained immediately after the final fraction of radiation, has demonstrated near-complete ablation of pre-existing tumor-reactive clones [31]. In the post-radiation window, effective anti-tumor immunity therefore depends on the quality of T cell repopulation. Current evidence indicates that this process is immunologically unfavorable: clonal tracking studies show that T cells repopulating the TME are dominated by non-specific populations, including suppressive Tregs and high-frequency circulating bystander clones with limited anti-tumor potential [31, 32].

Reconstitution of tumor-reactive TIL subsets after radiation must rely on either residual intra-tumoral priming or renewed priming in TDLN. Mechanistically, type I conventional dendritic cell (cDC1) cross-presentation has been shown to be crucial for the activation of CD8 T cells and acts largely as the engine of the anti-tumor response [33]. Recent single-cell and spatial analyses in HNSCC and other solid tumors show that intra-tumoral priming can occur within tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) [34, 35]. However, clinical-dose radiotherapy is potently lymphotoxic to both T and B cells, leading to TLS ablation and subsequent impairment of the cDC1-CD8 T cell cross-priming [36]. Likewise, elective nodal irradiation (ENI), routinely prescribed in HNSCC, may compromise the ability of TDLNs to support post-radiation priming. Available clinical data tracking T cell clonotype repopulation come from settings where TDLN function was impaired, either by ENI in HNSCC or by the anatomical constraints of the blood–brain barrier in brain metastases, which may limit the emergence of new tumor-reactive clones [31, 32].

Impaired T cell priming with elective nodal irradiation of tumor-draining lymphatics

The vulnerability of TDLNs in this context is particularly important, as ENI was historically adopted to sterilize occult micrometastases based on consensus patterns of nodal spread, forming the basis of current radiation target-volume guidelines. Emerging data, however, indicate that this practice can come at a significant immunologic cost. TDLNs are the principal sites of DC-T cell cross-priming through the fibroblastic reticular cell network and may be a key potential source of newly generated tumor-reactive clones. Their irradiation, however, may disrupt both priming and subsequent trafficking.

In pre-clinical HNSCC models, ENI diminishes local and systemic anti-tumor immunity, reduces intra-tumoral accumulation of effector T cells and weakens abscopal responses. Identified mechanisms are multifactorial and include both disrupted trafficking and direct radiation injury to immune cells. ENI can interrupt CCR7-dependent DC homing to TDLNs, limiting antigen delivery and T cell priming [37, 38]. When priming does occur, ENI can induce apoptosis of activated DCs and newly generated tumor-reactive T cells within the nodes [39]. By reducing the pool of newly primed effector T cells that enter the tumor and sustain an immunogenic microenvironment, ENI diminishes CXCL9 and CXCL10 induction, limiting CXCR3-mediated recruitment of additional T cells [40]. Across models, the adverse impact of ENI on disease control is dependent on both CD4 and CD8 T cells, as depletion of either subset abolishes the therapeutic benefit of sparing the nodes. Collectively, these findings indicate that ENI disrupts the priming phase of anti-tumor immunity within TDLNs, when newly generated T cells are not yet exhausted and remain less dependent on PD-1 signaling. Consistent with this biology, ENI omission synergizes most powerfully with Treg-targeted therapies such as anti-CTLA-4 or anti-CD25, which relieve suppressive CD4 influences and support Th1-mediated DC licensing. This, in turn, facilitates priming, expansion, and trafficking of tumor antigen-specific CD8 T cells [37, 39–41].

Correlative human data support these pre-clinical observations. In HNSCC, omission of ENI has been associated with greater intra-tumoral CD8 T cell infiltration and increased activation within TDLNs [39, 42]. Single-cell transcriptional analysis of TDLNs further demonstrates that nodal basins contain stem-like, tumor-reactive T cells, suggesting that ENI may deplete reservoirs essential for durable systemic immunity [43]. Consistent with a priming-dependent mechanism, early single-arm studies in metastatic disease reported responses above historical expectations when anti-CTLA-4 was added to tumor-only radiotherapy, while without radiotherapy, anti-CTLA-4 therapies have not demonstrated benefit in recurrent and metastatic HNSCC, suggesting that preservation of TDLN priming allows for synergy with Treg-depleting strategies [4, 5, 44–46]. By contrast, radiation combined with anti-PD-1 has largely failed to improve outcomes in randomized trials in both the metastatic and previously untreated settings [1, 13, 14, 47]. Taken together, these data suggest that preserving TDLNs facilitates de novo T cell priming that is improved primarily with Treg blockade rather than PD-1 inhibition. Anti-PD-1 therapy may augment responses when given sequentially after radiotherapy [38, 48, 49], but its benefit is likely dependent on the local tumor-immune context. In particular, a favorable intra-tumoral effector-to-Treg ratio may be more predictive of efficacy, whereas Treg dominance can blunt outcomes even when PD-1+ effector T cells are disinhibited. Thus, while ENI reduces regional relapse risk by sterilizing occult disease, it may simultaneously constrain systemic anti-tumor immunity.

In contrast with the negative trials combining radiotherapy with anti-PD-1 therapy, the recent KEYNOTE-689 and NIVOPOSTOP studies in HNSCC have demonstrated signals of benefit when PD-1 blockade is administered in the neoadjuvant or adjuvant setting [2, 50]. However, patients in these trials underwent complete tumor resection and neck dissection prior to or shortly after immunotherapy. Although TDLNs were potentially ablated, the post-surgical environment in these patients is characterized by markedly reduced tumor burden and tumor-associated hypoxia, representing a fundamentally different immune TME than that of patients receiving definitive chemoradiation, where intact TDLNs are irradiated. Thus, the effectiveness of PD-1 blockade is determined not solely by relief of inhibitory signaling, but by the immune ecosystem into which it is introduced, including tumor burden, metabolic stress, and the capacity for sustained T cell priming and expansion.

Effects of radiation dose and fractionation on T cell depletion and priming

Balancing total dose and fractionation determines whether radiotherapy functions chiefly as a lymphodepleting insult or as a primer of T cell-mediated anti-tumor immunity. Very high single fractions excel at killing tumor and inducing antigen release, but they can also perpetuate a tolerogenic microenvironment. Mechanistically, doses above 12 Gy in one fraction induce the exonuclease TREX1 in irradiated cancer cells, degrading cytosolic double-stranded DNA and silencing cGAS-STING and subsequent type-I IFN signaling, the same pathway DCs rely on to sense danger and license cross-priming. In multiple tumor models and cell lines, TREX1 induction correlated with loss of type-I IFN, muted DC activation, and diminished systemic T cell responses, despite greater local cytotoxicity [18]. These data argue that more ablative is not automatically more immunogenic, and they motivate radiation regimens that generate antigen release while preserving the DNA-sensing signals that instruct DCs toward immunogenic rather than tolerogenic outcomes.

Pre-clinical work points to moderate hypofractionation to avoid this problem, where repeated sub-ablative hits provide antigen and damage-associated signals without triggering TREX1. The canonical demonstration is that 8 Gy × 3 or 6 Gy × 5 combined with anti-CTLA-4, produced robust abscopal responses and systemic CD8 expansion across solid tumor models, an effect which did not occur using 20 Gy × 1 (Table 1) [51]. Importantly, each radiotherapy regimen alone induced similar growth delay at the primary tumor without abscopal effect and anti-CTLA-4 alone had no impact. It was only the combination of fractionated radiotherapy and anti-CTLA-4 that induce major response at untreated lesions. The interpretation is that fractionation sustains cGAS-STING/type-I IFN and serial DC licensing, thereby improving priming within secondary lymphoid organs. However, these results again emphasize the critical balance between tumor antigen-specific effector T cells and Tregs: effective priming alone and Treg depletion alone were each insufficient and only the combination could induce deep response in metastatic solid tumors. These data suggest a core principle that dose per fraction and treatment schedule are immunologic design variables, not just radiobiologic ones, and regimens that act in a priming window pair best with agents that promote immunogenic DC-T cell interaction.

Clinically, this signal is emerging but mixed, reflecting heterogeneity in fractionation, fields, adjuvant partners, and sequencing. The randomized PEMBRO-RT trial in metastatic non-small cell lung cancer, which included 8 Gy × 3 to one lesion followed by pembrolizumab, showed higher response rates versus pembrolizumab alone but did not reach pre-specified statistical endpoints [52]. A pooled analysis of PEMBRO-RT with an institutional MD Anderson trial strengthened the case that this fractionation can modestly augment PD-1 therapy systemically, even though each study alone was underpowered for key survival endpoints [53]. Conversely, in the more recent multi-tumor randomized CHEERS phase 2 study, no significant PFS benefit was seen when adding multi-site (≤ 3 metastatic lesions) hypofractionated radiation at 8 Gy × 3 to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade, emphasizing that fractionation is necessary but not sufficient and that regimen details, timing, and disease context matter [54]. While moderate hypofractionation without ENI may lead to T cell priming and systemic release of tumor-reactive CD8 T cells, which may be supported by post-radiation anti-PD-1 therapy, the immunosuppressive context of the TME is crucial and may limit the efficacy of these newly-primed T cells. Taken together, available human data neither invalidate the pre-clinical paradigm nor prove a one-size-fits-all recipe. Instead, they suggest that moderate multi-fraction schedules are plausible partners for checkpoint blockade, particularly when the goal is priming, but careful regimen engineering remains essential and multi-pronged approaches will be necessary to both increase tumor-reactive T cells systemically, while addressing T cell infiltration and intra-tumoral immunosuppression.

Radiation-driven remodeling of tumor antigen presentation and neo-antigen landscapes

Extensive pre-clinical work shows that ionizing radiation enhances antigen processing and presentation and, in some settings, alters which peptides are displayed. Early studies reported that radiotherapy increases tumor visibility by upregulating MHC class I on cancer cells, enhancing tumor-cell phagocytosis, and improving DC cross-presentation. Identified mechanisms include cGAS-STING-driven type-I IFN, NLRC5-dependent induction of the antigen-processing machinery, immunogenic cell-death signals (ecto-calreticulin, ATP, HMGB1), attenuation of CD47-mediated anti-phagocytic signaling, and chemokine programs that recruit cross-presenting cDC1s [55–60]. Recent proteogenomic work has validated that radiation can upregulate key components of antigen-processing machinery (APM) and expands the class I immunopeptidome, including increased presentation of tumor-specific neo-antigens [61]. Complementary data indicate that radiation can shift proteasome usage toward the immunoproteasome, further reshaping the peptide repertoire loaded onto MHC I [62]. Beyond quantitative effects, radiation has been shown to remodel the neo-antigen landscape. In low-mutation tumor models, sub-curative radiation can generate de novo mutations that yield functional CD8 targets [63]. Collectively, these pre-clinical findings support a system in which radiation remodels antigen presentation both by boosting APM activity and by qualitatively shifting the presented peptide repertoire.

Clinically, however, direct biopsy-paired human datasets remain limited. Recent analyses suggest that the overall neo-antigen repertoire is largely conserved from pre- to post-radiation, and may even contract, with only a small subset of new candidate neo-antigens emerging [31, 64]. In other tumor types, including renal cell carcinoma and breast cancer, biopsies obtained weeks after radiation show increased IFN signaling, higher expression of MHC I and antigen-processing genes, and greater effector T cell infiltration, consistent with improved presentation and accessibility of existing peptide-MHC (pMHC) complexes [65–67]. Together, these data suggest that clinical radiation in patients primarily affects the quantity and accessibility of existing pMHC display rather than rather than wholesale rewriting of the peptidome. Such findings have important implications for therapy, suggesting that in the immediate post-radiation window, the same immunogenic targets are often present but may be more visible. This supports pairing radiation with TIL or TCR-engineered therapies and with personalized vaccines that boost recognition of pre-existing, clinically relevant tumor antigens.

Radiation-induced tissue injury, hypoxia, and wound-healing programs constrain anti-tumor immunity after priming

The same radiation-induced tissue injury that generates immunogenic stimuli simultaneously activates a wound-healing cascade that reshapes the tumor microenvironment in ways that can ultimately restrain sustained anti-tumor immunity. A central mediator of this remodeling is vascular injury. Endothelial cells within irradiated tumors experience dose-dependent damage. At moderate doses, transient upregulation of adhesion molecules such as ICAM-1 and VCAM-1 may temporarily facilitate immune cell adhesion and extravasation [68]. In contrast, higher single fractions can cause durable microvascular disruption, reduced perfusion, and architectural collapse of tumor blood vessels [69]. This vascular compromise limits the physical delivery of circulating lymphocytes and newly primed tumor-reactive clones into the tumor bed. Thus, even if systemic lymphopenia is mitigated and tumor-draining lymph node priming is preserved, effective immune control remains contingent on intact vascular access to the tumor.

Persistent vascular damage also contributes to tissue hypoxia, which further transforms the immune landscape. HNSCC is frequently hypoxic at baseline, and radiation-induced microvascular loss can exacerbate oxygen deprivation [45, 70–72]. Stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factors (HIFs) reprograms tumor and stromal metabolism, drives aberrant angiogenesis, and alters chemokine gradients. Hypoxia directly impairs cytotoxic T cell function by constraining metabolic flexibility, reducing cytokine production, and favoring sustained expression of inhibitory receptors. Simultaneously, hypoxic signaling interferes with dendritic cell maturation and CCR7-dependent migration to tumor-draining lymph nodes, limiting effective priming and replenishment of tumor-reactive clones [73]. Fractionation strategy may also determine whether transient reoxygenation can partially restore immune function or whether permanent vascular injury entrenches a chronically hypoxic, suppressive state [74–76].

Hypoxia and tissue stress then promote recruitment and expansion of suppressive myeloid populations. Radiation-induced inflammatory signaling and metabolic disruption attract myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and support polarization of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) toward wound-healing, pro-angiogenic phenotypes [77]. These cells can secrete IL-10, TGF-β, arginase, and reactive nitrogen species, which blunt T cell proliferation and cytotoxicity while reinforcing matrix remodeling and aberrant neovascularization. The cumulative effect is a shift in the tumor microenvironment toward myeloid dominance and regulatory signaling, even in the presence of tumor antigen and previously primed effector T cells.

Taken together, these sequential processes illustrate a central paradox of radiotherapy. While radiation can enhance antigen visibility and support T cell priming, it simultaneously initiates vascular injury, hypoxia, and myeloid-driven wound healing that constrain effector function and limit immune infiltration. Thus, even after optimizing systemic lymphocyte preservation, sparing tumor-draining lymph nodes, and delivering tumor-reactive T cells into the tumor bed, those cells must operate within a metabolically restricted and suppressive ecosystem. Effective combination strategies must therefore address not only T cell priming and trafficking, but also vascular integrity, hypoxia mitigation, and myeloid remodeling to achieve durable anti-tumor control.

Future directions to leverage the dynamic effects of radiotherapy to improve anti-tumor immunogenicity

Future work should treat radiotherapy as a programmable immune adjuvant rather than only a cytotoxic modality. Dose, fractionation, and field design may be engineered to favor priming over depletion and limit the adverse effect of post-radiation wound-healing suppressive programs. Conventional fractionation (2 Gy/fx), when delivered over large fields and many fractions, can exacerbate cumulative irradiation of the circulating blood pool and lymphoid tissues, increasing the risk of radiation-induced lymphopenia. In contrast, very high single-fraction doses may better preserve lymphoid populations, but also risk attenuating immunogenicity through dose-dependent TREX1 induction and suppression of IFN signaling. Moderate hypofractionated regimens (for example 6–8 Gy in 3–5 fractions) can elicit favorable immunologic activity in multiple preclinical and translational settings, while the risk of treatment-related lymphopenia is driven primarily by field size, integral dose, and estimated dose to circulating immune cells. Very large treated volumes and higher integral dose may also promote vascular dysfunction and chemokine programs that recruit suppressive myeloid populations, which can limit effective T cell infiltration and function [78]. FLASH radiotherapy is an emerging approach with promising normal-tissue sparing in early clinical translation, but its capacity to preserve systemic immunity and lymphocyte recovery in patients remains to be established [79, 80].

To preserve anti-tumor immunity beyond the primary field, prospective studies should evaluate whether omitting ENI is safe in the era of modern metabolic staging and three-dimensional planning [37]. Avoidance of ENI may protect TDLN function and improve the quality and specificity of post-radiation T cell repopulation. Although there is a trade-off between nodal prophylaxis and preservation of systemic immunity, close regional nodal surveillance can permit early salvage when nodal recurrence occurs [39]. Contemporary trials already support selective fields and reduced elective doses in appropriate patients, creating an opportunity to test protocols that prioritize tumor-only priming, with prespecified immunological endpoints [81].

In addition to immuno-protective radiation dose and delivery, the immediate post-radiation window remains a key opportunity to convert antigen release into productive priming. Strategies that activate and license antigen-presenting cells in situ should be scheduled within hours to days of each fraction, as early repopulation can occur in clinical tumors with non-specific and regulatory subsets, overwhelming tumor-reactive populations. Intra-tumoral or systemic delivery of antigen-presenting cell activators, such as CD40 agonism, CD47-SIRPα blockade, or DC vaccines, may synchronize with radiation-induced antigen availability while reprogramming suppressive myeloid populations toward more permissive immunogenic phenotypes [71, 82–85]. Parallel approaches using chemotactic mechanisms may increase endogenous cross-presentation capacity by recruiting cDC1s, such as with peri-tumoral XCL1 delivery [86, 87]. Additionally, because clinical radiation has demonstrated tumor T cell repopulation skewed toward Treg subsets, combinations that limit post-radiation Treg dominance, are likely to be essential. Chemotactic blockade of returning Tregs, such as administration of CCR4, CCR6, and CCR8 antagonists may indeed help reprogram the radiation-induced remodeling of the TME from being immunosuppressive to immunostimulatory in nature. Multiple preclinical studies have demonstrated that blockade of these axes either prevents Treg recruitment or depletes tumor-infiltrating Tregs, leading to favorable responses. Specifically, radiation may induce CCL20 secretion by tumor cells and suppressive myeloid populations, recruiting CCR6 + Tregs to the TME. Inhibition of this axis has resulted in tumor growth suppression that was enhanced when combined with radiotherapy, rather than either treatment alone [88]. Additionally, a large component of the immunosuppressive TME is the recruitment of M2 tumor-associated macrophages, which have been shown to largely interact with CCR4 + Tregs to facilitate metastatic potentiation [89]. Targeting CCR4 + Tregs may therefore represent a therapeutic strategy to limit metastatic spread, although this remains yet to be tested in the clinical setting. The blockade of these axes, when paired with preservation of cytotoxic T cell infiltration, is likely to improve the effector-to-Treg ratio and ultimately lead to durable tumor control [90]. While seeking the potential oncological benefits to Treg chemotaxis blockade, this approach requires caution given the critical role Tregs play in wound healing and repair following tissue injury from radiotherapy. Taken together, these data illustrate how radiation-induced chemokine remodeling may be intentionally reprogrammed, reinforcing the concept of radiotherapy as a programmable immune adjuvant rather than a fixed immunosuppressive insult.

Adoptive cell therapies also fit naturally into this post-radiation framework. For TIL or TCR-engineered products, collection and product development should occur before radiotherapy to avoid attrition of the tumor-reactive repertoire. Further, in settings where conventional systemic lymphodepletion is undesirable, tumor-only radiotherapy may serve to both clear the microenvironment of regulatory and exhausted subsets and simultaneously boost antigen presentation, facilitating both infiltration and antigen targeting of adoptive cell products [31, 64–67]. Personalized vaccines that amplify pre-existing targets are also well-aligned with this radiobiology and vaccination post-radiation based on pre-radiation neo-antigens may amplify the same specificities that radiation exposes [91, 92].

Ongoing work tests many of these ideas, and Table 2 presents Phase I-III trials that seek to improve radioimmunotherapy approaches. Perhaps most importantly, given the absence of validated, actionable biomarkers in HNSCC, future trials should incorporate immune selection and response metrics grounded in mechanistic science. These may include the intra-tumoral effector-to-Treg ratio, cDC1 abundance and activation state, type-I IFN transcriptional signatures, and chemokine axis activation such as CCL20-CCR6 and CCR4 pathways. Translationally, these studies should also incorporate paired tumor and blood sampling at baseline, during therapy, and after treatment whenever possible, together with single-cell TCR tracking, antigen-presentation signatures, and spatial mapping of tumor-reactive clonotypes in both tumor and TDLNs. Although resource-intensive, these approaches will clarify the immediate effects of clinical therapies and guide our next iterations. Additionally, continued improvement in single-cell technologies, such as long-read sequencing platforms, may enable higher-resolution capture of immune cells while reducing technical dropout associated with stochastic mRNA capture. As cost and scalability improve, these technologies are more likely to be to be incorporated into prospective trials, enabling timely patient-specific TME characterization and more precisely tailored therapeutic strategies. The aim is to build time-resolved decision rules that link dose, fields, and sequencing to immunological outcomes, turning radiotherapy into a reliable initiator of anti-tumor immunity that can be amplified with rational therapeutic combinations.
