Direct sampling for recovering a clamped cavity from the biharmonic far-field data Thanks: The research of I. Harris and H. Lee is partially supported by the NSF DMS Grants 2107891 and 2208256.
Isaac Harris, Heejin Lee, Peijun Li

Abstract
. This paper concerns the inverse shape problem of recovering an unknown clamped cavity embedded in a thin infinite plate. The model problem is assumed to be governed by the two-dimensional biharmonic wave equation in the frequency domain. Based on the far-field data, a resolution analysis is conducted for cavity recovery via the direct sampling method. The Funk–Hecke integral identity is employed to analyze the performance of two imaging functions. Our analysis demonstrates that the same imaging functions commonly used for acoustic inverse shape problems are applicable to the biharmonic wave context. This work presents the first extension of direct sampling methods to biharmonic waves using far-field data. Numerical examples are provided to illustrate the effectiveness of these imaging functions in recovering a clamped cavity.

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we address an inverse shape problem arising from the study of wave propagation in a thin infinite elastic plate. For such a plate, the displacement is governed by the Kirchhoff–Love model, which corresponds to the biharmonic scattering problem in the frequency domain for the total displacement. We assume that the far-field data are measured and propose a numerical method to recover the unknown clamped cavity. The clamped boundary conditions imply that the total displacement has zero Cauchy data on the boundary of the cavity. This problem is analogous to the sound soft inverse shape problem in acoustic scattering, as discussed in
[
4
,
15
,
22
]
. Such inverse problems have applications in non-destructive testing, where data collected from the exterior of an object are used to reconstruct its interior structure. These problems are relevant to various fields, including engineering, medical imaging, geophysics, and remote sensing. A similar inverse shape problem for biharmonic scattering has been explored in
[
6
]
, using near-field data and an optimization strategy.

We develop a qualitative (i.e., non-iterative) method for recovering the cavity. For an in-depth discussion on qualitative methods applied to various inverse scattering problems, see
[
4
,
8
,
17
]
. Although extensive literature exists on applying qualitative methods to inverse acoustic, electromagnetic, and elastic problems, there is less theoretical and computational development specifically for biharmonic scattering.
This makes the investigation mathematically interesting and challenging. This is due to the fact that well–known identities and theorems that are vital to the investigation may no longer be valid. Also, as we will see, by using the far-field data one inherently loss information about the solution outside the scatterer which is not the case for acoustic scattering.
Qualitative methods can be advantageous compared to nonlinear optimization approaches, which often require a prior information that may not be easily accessible, such as the number of regions to be identified, and can be computationally expensive. Therefore, we propose using the direct sampling method, which is computationally simple to implement and numerically stable. The proposed imaging functions involve computing the inner product or norm of the known data operator with a specific test function to obtain an analytic formula that can be exploited. However, it is worth noting that most qualitative methods rely on full aperture data for their theoretical justification.

There has been recent interest in studying inverse problems for biharmonic waves. In
[
7
,
27
]
, the authors investigate the inverse parameter problem for the biharmonic operator in both bounded and unbounded domains. Additionally,
[
12
]
addresses the biharmonic wave equation in the time domain and demonstrates that it is possible to uniquely determine the density and sources from the measured displacement and its Laplacian on the boundary. Other relevant works include
[
20
,
21
]
, where the authors examine the inverse random source and potential problems for the biharmonic wave equation in the frequency domain. This paper contributes to the limited literature on numerical methods for shape reconstruction involving biharmonic waves.
Indeed, there are so few papers that provide the analytical framework for extending qualitative methods for biharmonic scattering problems. Also, the results obtained here are interesting and surprising that the same imaging functions for acoustic scattering works without alteration for biharmonic scattering. In many mathematical investigations, adding a single lower order term can dramatically alter the analysis as well as the results. The fact that at the moment this manuscript and
[
14
]
are the only investigations for extending qualitative methods makes the results invaluable to researches in this field.

The paper is outlined as follows. Section
2
presents the problem formulations, introducing both the direct and inverse problems. Section
3
derives a factorization of the far-field operator, which is utilized in the resolution analysis of our imaging functions. Numerical examples demonstrating the applicability of the direct sampling method for cavity recovery are presented in Section
4
. Finally, Section
5
offers a brief discussion and conclusion of our results.

2.
Formulation of the Problem

We consider the Kirchhoff–Love infinite plate problem involving a clamped cavity, where the cavity is represented by a bounded region
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
with an analytic boundary
\partial D
. The cavity is exposed to a time-harmonic incident plane wave, denoted as
u^{\text{inc}}(x)=\text{e}^{\text{i}kx\cdot d}
, where
d
is the incident direction on the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. The total displacement field
u
consists of the scattered field
u^{\text{scat}}
and the incident field
u^{\text{inc}}
, i.e.,
u\coloneqq u^{\text{scat}}+u^{\text{inc}}
. Moreover, the total field satisfies the biharmonic scattering problem in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D}
for a fixed wave number
k>0
, described by

(1)

\displaystyle\Delta^{2}u-k^{4}u=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D},

with the boundary conditions

(2)

\displaystyle u\big|_{\partial D}=0\quad\text{ and }\quad\partial_{\nu}{u}\big|_{\partial D}=0,

where
\nu
denotes the outward unit normal vector on the boundary
\partial D
.

Since the incident field
u^{\text{inc}}
satisfies

\Delta^{2}u^{\text{inc}}-k^{4}u^{\text{inc}}=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2},

the scattered field satisfies

\Delta^{2}u^{\text{scat}}-k^{4}u^{\text{scat}}=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D}.

To complete the system, we require that the scattered field
u^{\text{scat}}
satisfies the Sommerfeld radiation condition at infinity (cf.
[
19
]
):

(3)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\sqrt{r}(\partial_{r}{u^{\text{scat}}}-\text{i}ku^{\text{scat}})=0,\quad r=|x|,

which holds uniformly in
\hat{x}=x/|x|
.

It has been proven that the scattering problem given by (
1
)–(
3
) is well-posed. For a detailed analysis, we refer to
[
9
]
for the boundary integral equation approach and
[
2
]
for the variational approach. It has also been shown in
[
2
,
9
]
that the scattered field
u^{\text{scat}}
can be expressed in terms of two auxiliary functions,
v_{H}
and
v_{M}
, defined as

(4)

\displaystyle v_{H}=-\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\big(\Delta u^{\text{scat}}-k^{2}u^{\text{scat}}\big),\quad v_{M}=\frac{1}{2k^{2}}\big(\Delta u^{\text{scat}}+k^{2}u^{\text{scat}}\big)

which implies that
u^{\text{scat}}=v_{H}+v_{M}
.

It can be verified that
v_{H}
satisfies the Helmholtz equation in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D}
, while
v_{M}
satisfies the modified Helmholtz equation in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D}
. Therefore, the biharmonic wave scattering problem (
1
)–(
3
) is equivalent to the following problem:

(5)

\displaystyle\Delta v_{H}+k^{2}v_{H}=0,\quad\Delta v_{M}-k^{2}v_{M}=0\quad

\displaystyle\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D},

(6)

\displaystyle v_{H}+v_{M}=-u^{\text{inc}},\quad\partial_{\nu}(v_{H}+v_{M})=-\partial_{\nu}u^{\text{inc}}\quad

\displaystyle\text{on }\partial D,

(7)

\displaystyle\lim_{r\to\infty}\sqrt{r}(\partial_{r}{v_{H}}-\text{i}kv_{H})=0,\quad\lim_{r\to\infty}\sqrt{r}(\partial_{r}{v_{M}}-\text{i}kv_{M})=0.

In our analysis, it is useful to note that the Sommerfeld radiation condition given in (
7
) can also be expressed as

(8)

\displaystyle\partial_{r}v_{H}-\text{i}kv_{H}=\mathcal{O}\big(r^{-3/2}\big),\quad\partial_{r}v_{M}-\text{i}kv_{M}=\mathcal{O}\big(r^{-3/2}\big),\quad r\to\infty.

According to the analysis in
[
9
]
, both
v_{H}
and
v_{M}
belong to
H^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D})
. It follows from the separation of variables that
v_{H}
and
v_{M}
admit the Fourier series expansions in

\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{B_{R}}
:

\displaystyle v_{H}(r,\theta)=\sum\limits_{|n|=0}^{\infty}\frac{H^{(1)}_{n}(kr)}{H^{(1)}_{n}(kR)}v^{(n)}_{H}\text{e}^{\text{i}n\theta},\quad v_{M}(r,\theta)=\sum\limits_{|n|=0}^{\infty}\frac{H^{(1)}_{n}(\text{i}kr)}{H^{(1)}_{n}(\text{i}kR)}v^{(n)}_{M}\text{e}^{\text{i}n\theta},

where
H^{(1)}_{n}
denote the Hankel function of first kind of order
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,
v^{(n)}_{H}
and
v^{(n)}_{M}
are the Fourier coefficients of
v_{H}(R,\theta)
and
v_{M}(R,\theta)
, respectively. Given this fact, the solution
v_{M}
to the modified Helmholtz equation decays exponentially. Specifically, for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

|H^{(1)}_{n}(\text{i}kr)|=\mathcal{O}\big(r^{-1/2}\text{e}^{-kr}\big),\quad r\to\infty.

Additionally, using the recursive relationship for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}H^{(1)}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\big(H^{(1)}_{n-1}(t)+H^{(1)}_{n+1}(t)\big),

it follows that
\partial_{r}v_{M}
decays exponentially as
r\to\infty
(cf.
[
25
]
).

By Green’s representation theorem, for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D}
, we have

(9)

\displaystyle v_{H}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial D}\left(v_{H}(y)\partial_{\nu_{y}}\Phi_{k}(x,y)-\partial_{\nu_{y}}v_{H}(y)\Phi_{k}(x,y)\right)\mathrm{d}s(y),

(10)

\displaystyle v_{M}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial D}\left(v_{M}(y)\partial_{\nu_{y}}\Phi_{\text{i}k}(x,y)-\partial_{\nu_{y}}v_{M}(y)\Phi_{\text{i}k}(x,y)\right)\mathrm{d}s(y),

where
\Phi_{k}(\cdot,\cdot)
and
\Phi_{\text{i}k}(\cdot,\cdot)
are the fundamental solutions of the Helmholtz equation and the modified Helmholtz equation in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, respectively. Explicitly, the fundamental solution is given as

\Phi_{k}(x,y)=\frac{\text{i}}{4}H^{(1)}_{0}(k|x-y|).

Since
v_{M}
decays exponentially and
v_{H}
is a radiating solution to the Helmholtz equation, the scattered field
u^{\text{scat}}
exhibits the asymptotic behavior

(11)

\displaystyle u^{\text{scat}}(x,d)=\frac{\text{e}^{\text{i}\pi/4}}{\sqrt{8\pi k}}\frac{\text{e}^{\text{i}k|x|}}{\sqrt{|x|}}\left(u^{\infty}(\hat{x},d)+\mathcal{O}(|x|^{-1})\right),\quad|x|\to\infty.

Here,
u^{\infty}(\hat{x},d)
denotes the far-field pattern of
u^{\text{scat}}
. Combining (
9
) and (
11
) yields
u^{\infty}(\hat{x},d)=v^{\infty}_{H}(\hat{x},d)
and

(12)

\displaystyle u^{\infty}(\hat{x},d)=\int_{\partial B_{R}}\Big(v_{H}(y)\partial_{\nu_{y}}\text{e}^{-\text{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}-\partial_{\nu_{y}}v_{H}(y)\text{e}^{-\text{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}\Big)\mathrm{d}s(y),

where
\text{e}^{-\text{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}
represents the far-field pattern of the fundamental solution
\Phi_{k}(x,y)
to the Helmholtz equation.

Notice that the far-field data does not contain any information about the exponentially decaying component
v_{M}
of the scattered field. Although some information is lost in the far field,
u^{\infty}
still uniquely determines the cavity
D
, as proven in
[
10
]
. We define the far-field operator
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
as follows:

(13)

\displaystyle(Fg)(\hat{x})=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}u^{\infty}(\hat{x},d)g(d)\,\mathrm{d}s(d).

The goal is to develop a direct sampling method using the far-field operator to numerically recover the cavity
D
. To achieve this, the following section focuses on the necessary analysis to derive the proposed reconstruction method.

3.
Direct Sampling Method

In this section, we address the inverse shape problem of recovering the cavity
D
from known biharmonic far-field data
u^{\infty}
for all
\hat{x}
and
d
in
\mathbb{S}^{1}
. We aim to extend the applicability of direct sampling methods to biharmonic scattering data, marking the first exploration in this direction, to the best of our knowledge. Direct sampling methods have been studied for the analogous acoustic scattering problem, using far-field data in
[
22
]
and near-field data in
[
15
]
. For acoustic scattering problems (i.e., the Helmholtz equation), these methods are closely related to the factorization of the far-field operator; see
[
17
]
for details on this factorization. We introduce the framework for the direct sampling method and define imaging functions for reconstructing
D
, which will be applied in subsequent numerical experiments.

As previously mentioned, the direct sampling method relies on the factorization of the far-field operator. We now proceed to derive a factorization of the far-field operator using boundary integral operators. Following the approach in
[
9
]
, we assume that
v_{H}
and
v_{M}
can be represented as the double and single layer potentials with densities
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
, respectively given as follows:

\displaystyle v_{H}(x)=\int_{\partial D}\partial_{\nu_{y}}\Phi_{k}(x,y)\varphi_{1}(y)\,\text{d}s(y),\quad v_{M}(x)=\int_{\partial D}\Phi_{\text{i}k}(x,y)\varphi_{2}(y)\,\text{d}s(y).

Therefore, the scattered field is give by

(14)

\displaystyle u^{\text{scat}}(x)=\int_{\partial D}\partial_{\nu_{y}}\Phi_{k}(x,y)\varphi_{1}(y)\,\text{d}s(y)+\int_{\partial D}\Phi_{\text{i}k}(x,y)\varphi_{2}(y)\,\text{d}s(y),

which satisfies the biharmonic wave equation

\Delta^{2}u^{\text{scat}}-k^{4}u^{\text{scat}}=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{D},

along with the radiation conditions (
3
).

The densities
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
can be determined by utilizing the boundary conditions in (
6
). Applying the boundary conditions and jump relations, we obtain the system of boundary integral equations

\displaystyle\big({K}_{k}+{I}\big)\varphi_{1}+{S}_{\text{i}k}\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle=-2u^{\text{inc}}

\displaystyle{T}_{k}\varphi_{1}+\big({K}^{\prime}_{\text{i}k}-{I}\big)\varphi_{2}

\displaystyle=-2\partial_{\nu}u^{\text{inc}}.

For simplicity, the above linear system can be written as

(15)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}{K}_{k}+{I}&{S}_{\text{i}k}\\
{T}_{k}&{K}^{\prime}_{\text{i}k}-{I}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{1}\\
\varphi_{2}\end{pmatrix}=-2\begin{pmatrix}u^{\text{inc}}\\
\partial_{\nu}{u^{\text{inc}}}\end{pmatrix},

where the bounded integral operators are defined as follows (cf.
[
18
,
24
]
):

\displaystyle({S}_{\tau}\phi)(x)=2\int_{\partial D}\Phi_{\tau}(x,y)\phi(y)\,\text{d}s(y),\quad

\displaystyle\quad({K}_{\tau}\phi)(x)=2\int_{\partial D}\partial_{\nu_{y}}{\Phi_{\tau}(x,y)}\phi(y)\,\text{d}s(y),

\displaystyle({K}^{\prime}_{\tau})\phi(x)=2\int_{\partial D}\partial_{\nu_{x}}{\Phi_{\tau}(x,y)}\phi(y)\,\text{d}s(y),

\displaystyle\quad({T}_{\tau})\phi(x)=2\int_{\partial D}\partial_{\nu_{x}}\partial_{\nu_{y}}{\Phi_{\tau}(x,y)}\phi(y)\,\text{d}s(y),

for
x\in\partial D
, with the parameter
\tau
equal to either
k
or i
k
.

The matrix operator in (
15
) can be denoted as
\mathcal{Q}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\big(H^{p}(\partial D)\big)^{2}\to\big(H^{p+2}(\partial D)\big)^{2}
for any
p\geq 0
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}{K}_{k}+{I}&{S}_{\text{i}k}\\
{T}_{k}&{K}^{\prime}_{\text{i}k}-{I}\end{pmatrix}.

It is shown in
[
9
]
that, provided that the boundary
\partial D
is analytic, the operator
\mathcal{Q}
is a continuous bijection for any
p\geq 0
. In our analysis, we consider the case when
p=0
. Therefore, by the bounded inverse theorem, we have that

\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\big(H^{2}(\partial D)\big)^{2}\to\big(L^{2}(\partial D)\big)^{2}

is a bounded linear operator. Hence, the system of boundary integral equations in (
15
) can be solved as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{1}\\
\varphi_{2}\end{pmatrix}=-2\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}u^{\text{inc}}\\
\partial_{\nu}{u^{\text{inc}}}\end{pmatrix}.

Now, we consider the injection
I_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\big(L^{2}(\partial D)\big)^{2}\to L^{2}(\partial D)
given by

\displaystyle I_{1}\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{1}\\
\varphi_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\varphi_{1}.

This operator
I_{1}
is linear and bounded, as follows from its definition. From (
14
), we have

u^{\infty}(\hat{x},d)=\int_{\partial D}\partial_{\nu_{y}}\text{e}^{-\text{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}\varphi_{1}(y;d)\,\text{d}s(y),

where the dependence on the incident direction
d
is made explicit. To proceed, notice that the above expression represents the far-field pattern of the double layer potential. With this in mind, we define

\displaystyle(DL_{k})^{\infty}\varphi_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial D}\partial_{\nu_{y}}\text{e}^{-\text{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}\varphi_{1}(y;d)\,\text{d}s(y)=-\text{i}k\int_{\partial D}\nu(y)\cdot\hat{x}\text{e}^{-\text{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}\varphi_{1}(y;d)\,\text{d}s(y),

which is a bounded linear operator mapping
L^{2}(\partial D)
to
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
.

Therefore, the far-field pattern for a clamped cavity
D
can be expressed as

(16)

\displaystyle u^{\infty}(\hat{x},d)=-2(DL_{k})^{\infty}I_{1}\mathcal{Q}^{-1}(u^{\text{inc}},\partial_{\nu}{u^{\text{inc}}})^{\top}.

Since the kernel of
(DL_{k})^{\infty}
and
(u^{\text{inc}},\partial_{\nu}{u^{\text{inc}}})^{\top}
depend analytically on
\hat{x}
and
d
, respectively, the far-field pattern
u^{\infty}
depends analytically on
(\hat{x},d)\in\mathbb{S}^{1}\times\mathbb{S}^{1}
. Consequently, we obtain the following result.

Theorem 3.1
.

The far-field operator
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, as defined by (
13
), corresponding to the biharmonic clamped scattering problem (
1
)–(
3
), is compact.

We now focus on deriving a factorization of the far-field operator
F
. This factorization is crucial for analyzing the direct sampling methods studied in this section. Once an appropriate factorization of
F
is obtained, we can assess its applicability in reconstructing
D
from the given data.

We define the operator
H\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to\big(H^{2}(\partial D)\big)^{2}
as follows:

\displaystyle Hg=(v_{g},\partial_{\nu}{v_{g}})^{\top},

where the Herglotz wave function
v_{g}
is given by

(17)

\displaystyle v_{g}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\text{e}^{\text{i}kx\cdot d}g(d)\,\text{d}s(d).

Notice that, similar to the case of acoustic scattering, the far-field operator can be factorized using (
16
) and the superposition principle. This follows directly from the linearity of our scattering problem (
1
) with respect to the incident field and its normal derivative on
\partial D
.

Theorem 3.2
.

The far-field operator
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, defined by (
13
) for the biharmonic clamped scattering problem (
1
)–(
3
), has the following factorization

\displaystyle Fg=-2(DL_{k})^{\infty}I_{1}\mathcal{Q}^{-1}Hg.

To complete the derivation of our direct sampling method, we require one additional key ingredient. Specifically, we utilize the Funk–Hecke integral identity (cf.
[
22
]
):

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\text{e}^{\text{i}k(x-z)\cdot d}\,\text{d}s(d)=2\pi J_{0}(k|x-z|),

where
J_{0}
denotes the first kind Bessel function of order zero. This result can be readily derived from the Jacobi–Anger expansion for an incident plane wave (cf.
[
8
]
). If we let
\phi_{z}=\text{e}^{-\text{i}kz\cdot d}
, which is the far-field pattern of
\Phi_{k}(z,y)
, then
v_{\phi_{z}}=2\pi J_{0}(k|x-z|)
. It is clear to note that the adjoint of
(DL_{k})^{\infty}
is given by

\left[(DL_{k})^{\infty}\right]^{*}\phi=\text{i}k\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\nu(y)\cdot d\text{e}^{\text{i}ky\cdot d}\phi(d)\,\text{d}s(d)=\partial_{\nu_{y}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}\text{e}^{\text{i}ky\cdot d}\phi(d)\,\text{d}s(d),

which implies that the
\left[(DL_{k})^{\infty}\right]^{*}\phi=\partial_{\nu_{y}}v_{\phi}
, i.e., the normal derivative of the Herglotz wave function.

Combining the factorization and the above integral identities, we study two direct sampling imaging functions. We demonstrate that the following two imaging functions

(18)

\displaystyle z\longmapsto\left|(\phi_{z},F\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right|^{\rho},\quad z\longmapsto\|F{\phi_{z}}\|^{\rho}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

can effectively recover the cavity because they exhibit ‘rapid’ decay as
\text{dist}(z,D)\rightarrow\infty
. The parameter
\rho>0
can be selected to enhance the resolution of the imaging functions.

Lemma 3.1
.

The far-field operator
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, defined by (
13
) corresponding to the biharmonic clamped scattering problem (
1
)–(
3
), satisfies

\left|(\phi_{z},F\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right|\leq C\|J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|^{2}_{H^{4}(D)},

where
\phi_{z}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kz\cdot d}
is the far-field pattern of the fundamental solution
\Phi_{k}(z,{y})
.

Proof.

Recalling the factorization
F=-2(DL_{k})^{\infty}I_{1}\mathcal{Q}^{-1}H
given in Theorem
3.2
and using the boundedness of the operators

I_{1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\big(L^{2}(\partial D)\big)^{2}\to L^{2}(\partial D),\quad\mathcal{Q}^{-1}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\big(H^{2}(\partial D)\big)^{2}\to\big(L^{2}(\partial D)\big)^{2},

we have

\displaystyle\left|(\phi_{z},F\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right|

\displaystyle=2\left|\left([(DL_{k})^{\infty}]^{*}\phi_{z},I_{1}\mathcal{Q}^{-1}H\phi_{z}\right)_{L^{2}(\partial D)}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left\|[(DL_{k})^{\infty}]^{*}\phi_{z}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial D)}\left\|H\phi_{z}\right\|_{H^{2}(\partial D)\times H^{2}(\partial D)}.

It follows from the Funk–Hecke integral identity that

H\phi_{z}=2\pi\big(J_{0}(k|x-z|),\partial_{\nu_{x}}{J_{0}(k|x-z|)}\big)^{\top},\quad[(DL_{k})^{\infty}]^{*}\phi_{z}=2\pi\partial_{\nu_{x}}{J_{0}(k|x-z|)}.

It is clear to note that

\displaystyle\left\|[(DL_{k})^{\infty}]^{*}\phi_{z}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial D)}=2\pi\|\partial_{\nu}J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|_{L^{2}(\partial D)}\leq C\|\partial_{\nu}J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial D)}.

By applying the trace theorem and utilizing the continuous embedding of
H^{4}(D)
into
H^{2}(D)
, we obtain

\left\|[(DL_{k})^{\infty}]^{*}\phi_{z}\right\|_{L^{2}(\partial D)}\leq C\|J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|_{H^{4}(D)}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\left\|H\phi_{z}\right\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\partial D)\times H^{2}(\partial D)}

\displaystyle=2\pi\|J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\partial D)}+2\pi\|\partial_{\nu}J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|^{2}_{H^{2}(\partial D)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|^{2}_{H^{7/2}(\partial D)}+\|\partial_{\nu}J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|^{2}_{H^{5/2}(\partial D)}\right).

By applying the trace theorem again, we have

\displaystyle\left\|H\phi_{z}\right\|_{H^{2}(\partial D)\times H^{2}(\partial D)}\leq C\|J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|_{H^{4}(D)},

which completes the proof.
∎

We now aim to use the estimate from Theorem
3.1
to show that the imaging functions in (
18
) decay as the sampling point
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
moves away from the cavity. In previous works on acoustic scattering problems (see, for example,
[
13
,
15
,
16
,
28
]
), it was sufficient to estimate
\|J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|^{2}_{H^{1}(D)}
. Using the asymptotic estimates

J_{0}(t)=\frac{\cos t+\sin t}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\left\{1+\mathcal{O}(t^{-1})\right\},\quad J_{1}(t)=\frac{\cos t-\sin t}{\sqrt{\pi t}}\left\{-1+\mathcal{O}(t^{-1})\right\}

as
t\to\infty
, it follows that

\|J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|_{H^{1}(D)}=\mathcal{O}\left(\mathrm{dist}(z,D)^{-1}\right),\quad\mathrm{dist}(z,D)\to\infty.

In our case, we need to establish this estimate for
\|J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|^{2}_{H^{4}(D)}
. This estimate can be extended to hold for any
H^{q}(D)
-norm, where
q\in\mathbb{N}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

For a fixed bounded domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\|J_{0}(k|\cdot-z|)\|^{2}_{H^{4}(D)}=\mathcal{O}\big(\mathrm{dist}(z,D)^{-1}\big),\quad\mathrm{dist}(z,D)\to\infty.

Proof.

It follows from the Funk–Hecke integral identity that

v_{\phi_{z}}=2\pi J_{0}(k|x-z|).

Therefore, it suffices to prove the result for the Herglotz wave function. For any
s,t\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
such that
0\leq s+t\leq 4
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{1}}^{s}\partial_{x_{2}}^{t}v_{\phi_{z}}=(\text{i}k)^{s+t}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}d_{1}^{s}d_{2}^{t}\text{e}^{\text{i}k(x-z)\cdot d}\,\text{d}s(d).

Using
d=(\cos\phi,\sin\phi)
and the Jacobi–Anger expansion

\text{e}^{\text{i}k(x-z)\cdot d}=\sum\limits_{|n|=0}^{\infty}\text{i}^{n}J_{n}(k|x-z|)\text{e}^{\text{i}n(\theta-\phi)},

where
\theta
is the polar angle for
x-z
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{1}}^{s}\partial_{x_{2}}^{t}v_{\phi_{z}}=(\text{i}k)^{s+t}\sum\limits_{|n|=0}^{\infty}\text{i}^{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}J_{n}(k|x-z|)\text{e}^{\text{i}n(\theta-\phi)}(\cos{\phi})^{s}(\sin{\phi})^{t}\,\text{d}\phi,

which implies

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{1}}^{s}\partial_{x_{2}}^{t}v_{\phi_{z}}=(\text{i}k)^{s+t}\sum\limits_{|n|=0}^{\infty}\text{i}^{n}J_{n}(k|x-z|)\text{e}^{\text{i}n\theta}\omega_{n},

where

\displaystyle\omega_{n}=\frac{1}{\text{i}^{t}2^{s+t}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\big(\text{e}^{\text{i}\phi}+\text{e}^{-\text{i}\phi}\big)^{s}\big(\text{e}^{\text{i}\phi}-\text{e}^{-\text{i}\phi}\big)^{t}\text{e}^{-\text{i}n\phi}\,\text{d}\phi.

Note that, due to the orthogonality of
\text{e}^{\text{i}n\phi}
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
over the interval
[0,2\pi]
, it follows that
\omega_{n}=0
for all
|n|>s+t
.
This implies that

\displaystyle\partial_{x_{1}}^{s}\partial_{x_{2}}^{t}v_{\phi_{z}}=\sum_{|n|=0}^{s+t}c_{n}J_{n}(k|x-z|)\text{e}^{\text{i}n\theta},

where
c_{n}=(\text{i}k)^{s+t}\text{i}^{n}\omega_{n}
.

Given the asymptotic behavior of the Bessel function (cf.
[
8
,
28
]
),

\displaystyle J_{n}(t)=\mathcal{O}(t^{-1/2})\quad\text{as }\quad t\to\infty,

and the fact that the partial derivatives
\partial_{x_{1}}^{s}\partial_{x_{2}}^{t}v_{\phi_{z}}
form a finite sum of Bessel functions, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\partial_{x_{1}}^{s}\partial_{x_{2}}^{t}v_{\phi_{z}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}=\mathcal{O}\big(\mathrm{dist}(z,D)^{-1}\big),\quad\mathrm{dist}(z,D)\to\infty,

which proves the claim.
∎

With this result, along with Lemma
3.1
, we state our first resolution analysis result for the imaging function

z\longmapsto\left|(\phi_{z},F\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right|^{\rho},\quad\rho>0.

The following result shows that the this imaging function decays rapidly as the sampling point
z
moves away from the cavity
D
.

Theorem 3.3
.

The far-field operator
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, defined by (
13
) corresponding to the biharmonic clamped scattering problem (
1
)–(
3
), satisfies

\left|(\phi_{z},F\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right|=\mathcal{O}\big(\mathrm{dist}(z,D)^{-1}\big),\quad\mathrm{dist}(z,D)\to\infty,

where
\phi_{z}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kz\cdot d}
is the far-field pattern of the fundamental solution
\Phi_{k}(z,{y})
.

Next, we turn our attention to the other imaging function under consideration, given by

z\longmapsto\|F{\phi_{z}}\|^{\rho}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})},\quad\rho>0.

We demonstrate that this imaging function also decays rapidly as the sampling point
z
moves away from the cavity
D
. To achieve this, we extend a well-known result from the case of acoustic sound-soft scatterers to our case of a biharmonic clamped cavity. This result is commonly used in the study of the factorization method for recovering sound soft scatterers. One major advantage of direct sampling methods over the more analytically rigorous factorization method is their stability with respect to noisy data. In fact, these methods can be used to recover scatterers even when the data contains a significant amount of noise. The factorization method for recovering a biharmonic clamped cavity has not yet been studied.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
be the far-field operator defined by (
13
), corresponding to the biharmonic clamped scattering problem (
1
)–(
3
). Then, there holds

\displaystyle F-F^{*}=\frac{\textup{i}}{4\pi}F^{*}F,

where
F^{*}
is the adjoint operator of
F
.

Proof.

To prove the claim, let
g,h\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, and consider the Herglotz wave functions with kernels
g
and
h
, respectively. Let
P,Q
represent the total fields associated with the biharmonic clamped scattering problem (
1
)–(
3
), corresponding to the incident waves
v_{g}
and
v_{h}
, respectively. As in the previous section, the corresponding scattered fields have the decomposition
P^{\text{scat}}=P_{H}+P_{M}
and
Q^{\text{scat}}=Q_{H}+Q_{M}
, as defined by (
4
), and satisfy (
5
)–(
7
) with the respective incident waves.
By the superposition principle,
Fg
and
Fh
correspond to the far-field patterns for the scattered fields
P^{\text{scat}}
and
Q^{\text{scat}}
, respectively. Then, by appealing to the exponential decay of the solutions to the modified Helmholtz equation, we obtain

Fg=P_{H}^{\infty},\quad Fh=Q_{H}^{\infty}.

From Green’s identifies in
B_{R}\setminus\overline{D}
, we have

(19)

\displaystyle 0=\int_{B_{R}\setminus\overline{D}}\left(\Delta^{2}P\overline{Q}-P\Delta^{2}\overline{Q}\right)\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\partial_{\nu}P\Delta\overline{Q}-P\partial_{\nu}\Delta\overline{Q}+\partial_{\nu}\Delta P\overline{Q}-\Delta P\partial_{\nu}\overline{Q}\right)\mathrm{d}s,

where the boundary integral over
\partial D
vanishes due to the clamped boundary conditions.

Notice that since the Herglotz wave function solves the Helmholtz equation in the entire plane, along with (
5
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\Delta P=k^{2}(P_{M}-P_{H}-v_{g})\quad\text{and}\quad\Delta Q=k^{2}(Q_{M}-Q_{H}-v_{h}).

With this, we see that the first two terms in (
19
) can be written as

\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\partial_{\nu}P\Delta\overline{Q}-P\partial_{\nu}\Delta\overline{Q}\right)\mathrm{d}s\\
=k^{2}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left[\partial_{\nu}\left(P_{H}+P_{M}+v_{g}\right)\overline{\left(Q_{M}-Q_{H}-v_{h}\right)}-\left(P_{H}+P_{M}+v_{g}\right)\partial_{\nu}\overline{\left(Q_{M}-Q_{H}-v_{h}\right)}\right]\mathrm{d}s.

Since the terms
P_{M},\partial_{\nu}P_{M},Q_{M}
, and
\partial_{\nu}Q_{M}
decay exponentially as
R\to\infty
, we can simplify the previous equation as follows

\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\partial_{\nu}P\Delta\overline{Q}-P\partial_{\nu}\Delta\overline{Q}\right)\mathrm{d}s\\
=k^{2}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left[\partial_{\nu}\left(P_{H}+v_{g}\right)\overline{\left(-Q_{H}-v_{h}\right)}-\left(P_{H}+v_{g}\right)\partial_{\nu}\overline{\left(-Q_{H}-v_{h}\right)}\right]\mathrm{d}s+o(1)

as
R\to\infty
. It can be verified from the Sommerfeld radiation condition (
8
) and the asymptotic behavior (
11
) that

\displaystyle k^{2}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(P_{H}\partial_{\nu}\overline{Q}_{H}-\partial_{\nu}P_{H}\overline{Q}_{H}\right)\mathrm{d}s\to-\frac{\text{i}k^{2}}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}P_{H}^{\infty}\overline{Q}_{H}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}s=-\frac{\text{i}k^{2}}{4\pi}\left(Fg,Fh\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

as
R\to\infty
. By appealing to (
17
) we have that

\displaystyle k^{2}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(v_{g}\partial_{\nu}\overline{Q}_{H}-\partial_{\nu}v_{g}\overline{Q}_{H}\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=k^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}g(\hat{x})\left[\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\text{e}^{\text{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}\partial_{\nu}\overline{Q}_{H}(y)-\partial_{\nu}\text{e}^{\text{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}\overline{Q}_{H}(y)\right)\mathrm{d}s(y)\right]\mathrm{d}s(\hat{x})

\displaystyle=-k^{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{1}}g(\hat{x})\overline{Q}_{H}^{\infty}(\hat{x})\mathrm{d}s(\hat{x})

\displaystyle=-k^{2}\left(g,Fh\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})},

where the conjugate of the far-field pattern of
Q_{H}
from (
12
) is used to obtain

\displaystyle k^{2}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(P_{H}\partial_{\nu}\overline{v}_{h}-\partial_{\nu}P_{H}\overline{v}_{h}\right)\mathrm{d}s=k^{2}\left(Fg,h\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}.

Notice that the term

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(v_{g}\partial_{\nu}\overline{v}_{h}-\partial_{\nu}v_{g}\overline{v}_{h}\right)\mathrm{d}s=0

due to the fact that, both
v_{g}
and
v_{h}
satisfy the Helmholtz equation in the entire plane. Therefore, we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\partial_{\nu}P\Delta\overline{Q}-P\partial_{\nu}\Delta\overline{Q}\right)\mathrm{d}s\to-\frac{\text{i}k^{2}}{4\pi}\left(Fg,Fh\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}-k^{2}\left(g,Fh\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}+k^{2}\left(Fg,h\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

as
R\to\infty
.

Now, we consider the other in (
19
). Arguing is a similar manner, we see that

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\partial_{\nu}\Delta P\overline{Q}-\Delta P\partial_{\nu}\overline{Q}\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=k^{2}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left[\partial_{\nu}\left(-P_{H}+P_{M}-v_{g}\right)\overline{\left(Q_{M}+Q_{H}+v_{h}\right)}-\left(-P_{H}+P_{M}-v_{g}\right)\partial_{\nu}\overline{\left(Q_{M}+Q_{H}+v_{h}\right)}\right]\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=k^{2}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left[\partial_{\nu}\left(-P_{H}-v_{g}\right)\overline{\left(Q_{H}+v_{h}\right)}-\left(-P_{H}-v_{g}\right)\partial_{\nu}\overline{\left(Q_{H}+v_{h}\right)}\right]\mathrm{d}s+o(1)

as
R\to\infty
. Note that we have used the exponential decay of the solutions to the modified Helmholtz equation as in the analysis of the first two terms. With this we see that

\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\partial_{\nu}\Delta P\overline{Q}-\Delta P\partial_{\nu}\overline{Q}\right)\mathrm{d}s\\
=k^{2}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left[\partial_{\nu}\left(P_{H}+v_{g}\right)\overline{\left(-Q_{H}-v_{h}\right)}-\left(P_{H}+v_{g}\right)\partial_{\nu}\overline{\left(-Q_{H}-v_{h}\right)}\right]\mathrm{d}s+o(1)

as
R\to\infty
. This implies letting
R\to\infty
we have

\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\partial_{\nu}\Delta P\overline{Q}-\Delta P\partial_{\nu}\overline{Q}\right)\mathrm{d}s\to-\frac{\text{i}k^{2}}{4\pi}\left(Fg,Fh\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}-k^{2}\left(g,Fh\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}+k^{2}\left(Fg,h\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

i.e. the first two terms and the last two terms have the same limiting value. Combining the two limiting results we have that

\displaystyle 0=\int_{\partial B_{R}}\left(\partial_{\nu}P\Delta\overline{Q}-P\partial_{\nu}\Delta\overline{Q}+\partial_{\nu}\Delta P\overline{Q}-\Delta P\partial_{\nu}\overline{Q}\right)\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 108.405pt\to-\frac{\text{i}k^{2}}{2\pi}\left(Fg,Fh\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}-2k^{2}\left(g,Fh\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}+2k^{2}\left(Fg,h\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})},

as
R\to\infty
. Therefore, we have obtained that

\displaystyle\frac{\text{i}}{4\pi}\left(Fg,Fh\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}=\left(Fg,h\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}-\left(g,Fh\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}

which completes the proof.
∎

We are now prepared to derive the resolution analysis for the second imaging function. As we will demonstrate, both of the aforementioned functions can be used to recover the cavity
D
. These imaging functions are numerically straightforward to compute and stable with respect to noisy data. This provides two practical methods for numerically recovering the unknown region
D
from the measured far-field data.

Theorem 3.5
.

The far-field operator
F\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})
, defined by (
13
) corresponding to the biharmonic clamped scattering problem (
1
)–(
3
), satisfies

\|F\phi_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}=\mathcal{O}\big(\mathrm{dist}(z,D)^{-1/2}\big),\quad\mathrm{dist}(z,D)\to\infty,

where
\phi_{z}=\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}kz\cdot d}
is the far-field pattern of the fundamental solution
\Phi_{k}(z,{y})
.

Proof.

First, recall that the imaginary part of the far-field operator is defined as

\Im\{F\}=\frac{1}{2\text{i}}(F-F^{*}).

Using the result from Theorem
3.4
, we show that the two imaging functions bound each other from above and below. Specifically, we establish that

\|F\phi_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S})}
and
\big|(\phi_{z},F\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\big|
are related in a sense of equivalence.

Indeed, it is clear that

\displaystyle\big|(\phi_{z},F\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\big|\leq\|\phi_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\|F\phi_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}=\sqrt{2\pi}\|F\phi_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}.

Conversely, we have

\displaystyle\left|(\phi_{z},F\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right|\geq\left|\Im\left\{{(\phi_{z},F\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}}\right\}\right|=\left|(\phi_{z},\Im\left\{{F}\right\}\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right|=\left|\frac{1}{2\text{i}}\left(\phi_{z},(F-F^{*})\phi_{z}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right|.

From Theorem
3.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\big|(\phi_{z},F\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\big|\geq\frac{1}{8\pi}\left(\phi_{z},F^{*}F\phi_{z}\right)_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}=\frac{1}{8\pi}\|F\phi_{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}.

This establishes the claim by utilizing the decay result in Theorem
3.3
.
∎

From the proof of Theorem
3.5
, it is clear that the two imaging functions under consideration are equivalent in the sense that there exist constants constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

c_{1}\|F\phi_{z}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\leq\left|(\phi_{z},F\phi_{z})_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}\right|\leq c_{2}\|F\phi_{z}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{1})}.

Therefore, we expect both functions to yield similar reconstructions of the cavity
D
. The primary difference is that the imaging function defined via the inner product has a faster decay rate compared to the imaging functional defined via the norm. As we will demonstrate in our numerical experiments, both functions provide good reconstructions, and the difference is not detectable in practice, provided that
\rho
is chosen such that both functions exhibit the same decay rate as
\mathrm{dist}(z,D)\to\infty
.

Numerical Validation

In this section, we provide numerical examples to illustrate the effectiveness of the two imaging functions discussed in the previous section. We use synthetically generated far-field data for these examples. To obtain this data, we employ the numerical method described in
[
9
]
to solve the boundary integral equation (BIE) (
15
) for the densities
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
where the integral equations are discretized using the standard method as described in
[
9
]
and
[
18
]

In our examples, we define the matrix

\textbf{F}=\Big[u^{\infty}(\hat{x}_{i},d_{j})\Big]^{64}_{i,j=1},

where

\displaystyle u^{\infty}(\hat{x},d)=-\text{i}k\int_{\partial D}\nu(y)\cdot\hat{x}\text{e}^{-\text{i}k\hat{x}\cdot y}\varphi_{1}(y;d)\,\text{d}s(y).

Here, we discretize
\mathbb{S}^{1}
by setting

\hat{x}_{i}=d_{i}=\big(\cos(\theta_{i}),\sin(\theta_{i})\big)^{\top},\quad\theta_{i}=2\pi(i-1)/64,\quad i=1,\dots,64.

Thus, the multi-static far-field matrix
\textbf{F}\in\mathbb{C}^{64\times 64}
corresponds to
64
incident and observation directions.

To simulate measurement error in the data, random noise is introduced into the multi-static far-field matrix
\mathbf{F}

\mathbf{F}^{\delta}=\Big[\mathbf{F}(i,j)\big(1+\delta\mathbf{R}(i,j)\big)\Big]^{64}_{i,j=1},

\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{C}^{64\times 64}
consists of random values within the interval

representing the relative noise level added to the computed data. Consequently, the discretized imaging functions are given by

W_{\text{ip}}(z)=\big|(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{z},\textbf{F}^{\delta}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{z})_{\ell^{2}}\big|^{\rho},\quad W_{\text{norm}}(z)=\big\|\textbf{F}^{\delta}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{z}\big\|^{\rho}_{\ell^{2}},

where the vector
\boldsymbol{\phi}_{z}
is computed as

\boldsymbol{\phi}_{z}=\big(\text{e}^{-\text{i}kz\cdot d_{1}},\dots,\text{e}^{-\text{i}kz\cdot d_{64}}\big)^{\top},\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}.

Recall that

is a parameter that can be adjusted to refine the resolution of the reconstructions. According to Theorems

\mathrm{dist}(z,D)\to\infty

D

5.
Conclusion

We have conducted a resolution analysis for two straightforward direct sampling imaging functions aimed at recovering an unknown clamped cavity from biharmonic far-field data. To date, there has been limited investigation into extending qualitative reconstruction methods to biharmonic scattering problems. For instance,
[
3
]
studied the linear sampling method using near-field data, which appears to be the only other work addressing an inverse shape problem for biharmonic waves using a qualitative approach. Additionally, we have provided numerical validation of our method. Our experiments demonstrate that the direct sampling imaging functions are stable and effective for handling non-convex cavities.

There are several potential directions for expanding this research. For instance, it would be valuable to investigate the applicability of the direct sampling method to cases involving penetrable cavities. Additionally, exploring the associated transmission eigenvalue problems that arise in the study of the far-field operator could offer further insights and contribute to the advancement of the field.
