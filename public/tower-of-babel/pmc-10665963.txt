Effects of ultrasonic-enzymatic-assisted ethanol precipitation method on the physicochemical characteristics, antioxidant and hypoglycemic activities of Tremella fuciformis polysaccharides
Authors: Yixuan Li, Yan He, Hua Zhang, Xia Ma
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Tremella fuciformis polysaccharides, Ultrasonic-enzymatic-assisted ethanol precipitation extraction, Structural characterization, Antioxidant activity, Hypoglycemic activity

Introduction

Edible mushrooms are rich sources of essential fatty acids [1], proteins [2], dietary fiber [3], vitamins [4], polysaccharides [5] and polyphenols [6]. Among these constituents, edible mushroom polysaccharides as a natural biological macromolecule, are extracted from various parts of edible fungi, including the fruiting bodies, mycelium, spores, and fermentation broth [7], [8]. These polysaccharides are formed through glycosidic bonds, creating chain polymers. Recent research has unveiled the diverse effects of edible mushroom polysaccharides, such as anti-inflammatory effects [9], immunomodulation [10], anti-diabetes [11], [12] and anti-obesity [13]. TFPs were the main active substances of the edible and medicinal fungus T. fuciformis. Additionally, TFPs not only had various health-promoting effects but also protected the skin and had good moisturizing functions [14]. At present, research on TFPs mainly focuses on fruiting bodies and mycelium, with less research on spores and fermentation broth. According to studies conducted by Sun et al. [15], it has been shown that spore fermentation is an effective strategy to produce Tremella aurantialba polysaccharides, as a substitute for fruiting body extraction method. In our previous work [16], the Tremella fuciformis polysaccharides fermented from GCMCC5.39 showed excellent water solubility and good moisturizing properties. Therefore, T. fuciformis spores can serve as a valuable material for TFP production.

In general, the biological activity of polysaccharides was intricately linked to their structure, which encompasses factors such as monosaccharide composition, molecular weight and surface morphology [17], [18]. Research has consistently demonstrated that the choice of polysaccharide extraction method can significantly alter the structural and physicochemical properties of these compounds [19], [20]. Currently, the extraction of fermented polysaccharides was often relies on hot water extraction and ethanol precipitation [21], in which the yield of polysaccharides was low, so it is crucial to explore more effective and convenient extraction methods. Besides, acid and alkali extraction methods are commonly employed for polysaccharide extraction [22], [23]. However, these methods often suffer from drawbacks such as low yield and prolonged processing times. In pursuit of higher extraction rate and improved efficiency for polysaccharides, researchers have turned to innovative extraction technologies. These technologies encompass microwave-assisted extraction [24], ultrasonic-assisted extraction [25] and enzyme-assisted extraction [26], among others. The combination of multiple methods can increase the yield of fermented polysaccharides. Particularly noteworthy is ultrasonic-assisted enzymatic extraction (UAEE), which has proven effective in increasing polysaccharide extraction yields. In UAEE process, acoustic cavitation disrupts cell walls, facilitating improved interaction between solvents and target compounds [27]. Enzymes could destroy the cell wall by hydrolyzing cellulose and protein to release more cellular contents [28]. Do et al. [29] utilized a method involving ultrasonic-assisted enzymatic extraction to achieve elevated yields of Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharides, which exhibited exceptional anticancer properties. Therefore, it is imperative to investigate the structure, physicochemical properties and biological activities of TFPs using diverse extraction methods.

Based on the aforementioned, this study employed three extraction methods: ultrasonic-assisted ethanol precipitation extraction, enzymatic-assisted ethanol precipitation extraction and ultrasonic-enzymatic-assisted ethanol precipitation extraction. To determine the optimal conditions for ultrasonic-enzymatic-assisted ethanol precipitation extraction of polysaccharide, five single-factor conditions, including compound enzyme ratio, enzyme addition, enzymolysis temperature, ultrasonic power and ultrasonic time, as well as Box-Behnken experimental design, were used to optimize the process. Afterward, three extraction methods were compared to evaluate their effects on the physical and chemical properties, structural characterization and in vitro activity of TFPs. The research findings have the potential to provide valuable insights for the systematic development and utilization of TFPs.

Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The spores of T. fuciformis were isolated from the laboratory of Shanghai Institute of Technology (Shanghai, China). Papain (800 U/mg), Cellulase (50 U/mg) and α-amylase (2000 U/g) were provided by Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other chemical regents used were of analytical grade.

Ultrasonic-enzymatic-assisted ethanol precipitation extraction of TFP

Extraction of the crude polysaccharides

An appropriate amount of fermentation broth was taken in a beaker. Composite enzymes (cellulase/ papain) were added for enzymatic hydrolysis. Then, the mixture was used ultrasound treatment under specified power and time conditions (ultrasonic cell crusher JY92-IIDN, Ningbo Xinzhi Biological Technology Co., Ltd., China). Following ultrasound treatment, the extract was cooled and then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15 min to isolate the supernatant. The supernatant was concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator set at 50 °C and subjected to protein removal utilizing the Sevag method. After another round of centrifugation, the supernatant was blended with 95 % ethanol to achieve an 80 % concentration and subsequently stored at 4 °C. Finally, the precipitate was centrifuged, followed by dissolution at 60 °C. Then, it was dialyzed at 4 °C for a period of two days and freeze-dried to acquire the crude polysaccharide EUP. Single factor experiments

A series of univariate experiments (compound enzyme ratio, compound enzyme addition, enzymolysis temperature, ultrasonic power and ultrasonic time) were performed on the T. fuciformis fermentation broth. Each experiment focused on changing one factor while keeping the others constant. For the single-factor experiments, the ratio of composite enzymes (cellulase/ papain) was varied as 3:1, 2:1, 1:1, 1:2 and 1:3. The amounts of composite enzymes added were 1000U/100 mL, 2000U/100 mL, 3000U/100 mL, 4000U/100 mL and 5000U/100 mL. The enzymolysis temperature was set at 36 °C, 42 °C, 48 °C, 54 °C and 60 °C. The ultrasonic power levels used were 1 W/mL, 2 W/mL, 3 W/mL, 4 W/mL and 5 W/mL. 10min, 15 min, 20 min, 25 min and 30 min for ultrasonic time.

Ultrasonic-assisted ethanol precipitation extraction of TFP

Took an appropriate amount of T. fuciformis fermentation liquid and perform ultrasonic fragmentation in an ice water bath. The extraction flowchart of different extraction methods.

Enzymatic-assisted ethanol precipitation extraction of TFP

Took an appropriate amount of T. fuciformis fermentation broth and add 4000U/100 mL of compound enzyme (cellulase/papain) with an enzyme ratio of 2:1, which consists of cellulase and papain. The enzymatic hydrolysis process would be carried out at 50 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the mixture was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min, following the steps outlined in Section 2.2.1 (Fig. 1). After dialyzed, the solution would be freeze-dried to obtain crude polysaccharide EAP.

Deproteinization treatment of extraction solution

One-fourth of the volume of Sevag reagent (chloroform: butanol = 4:1) was added to the fermented liquid after concentration. Vigorously agitated for a duration of 20 min and allowed it to rest for 15 min. Subsequently, it was centrifuged at a speed of 8000 rpm for 10 min to remove precipitates and organic layers. The supernatant was collected and dialyzed with distilled water for 36 h (molecular weight cutoff: 14,000 Da, temperature: 4 °C), changing the water every 6 h.

Physicochemical characterization of TFPs

The total polysaccharide content was determined using phenol–sulfuric acid method [31]. Protein concentration was measured following the Bradford method [32]. Viscosity was determined using an apparent viscometer at steady-state rheology at shear rates from 0.01 to 100 s−1.

Structure characterization of TFPs

FT-IR analysis

The KBr pellet method was implemented for the analysis of infrared spectra. TFPs samples (1 mg) were mixed with 150 mg dried KBr. The mixture was thoroughly ground until it adhered to the walls. The infrared spectroscopy was conducted within the range of 4000 ∼ 500 cm−1.

SEM analysis

Referred to the method of Ma et al. [33] and modified it. TFPs were observed and analyzed using a scanning electron microscope (TM4000PLUS, Japan) under 5 kV accelerating voltage.

Glucose dialysis retardation index (GDRI)

Following the method proposed by Qi et al. [36], GDRI was measured. Firstly, 10 mg sample was weighed and added it to the glucose solution (5 mL, 0.05 mol/ L). The mixture was thoroughly mixed until complete dissolution. Subsequently, the solution was transferred into a dialysis bag (20 cm; Mw cutoff = 14000 Da) and securely fastened. The dialysis bag, containing the sample solution, was immersed in a beaker containing 80 mL of deionized water and placed in a 37 °C water bath. Statistical analysis

All data were represented as means ± standard deviation and analyzed by Origin 2021 software (Origin Lab Corporation, Northampton, Mass., USA). Significant analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed using SPSS (version 26, IBM Corporation, USA) to assess intergroup differences.

Results and discussion

Influence of different factors on extraction of TFPs

Effects of compound enzyme ratio on polysaccharide extraction

The effect of compound enzyme ratio on TFPs yield and the inhibition rate on α-amylase is shown in Fig. 2A. The results showed that the TFPs yield and the inhibition rate on α-amylase demonstrated an increase, reaching their peak values at a cellulase- to- papain ratio of 2:1. However, when the proportion of papain exceeded that of cellulase, the TFPs yield and the inhibition rate of polysaccharides on α-amylase showed a decreasing trend. This may be due to the different active sites of different types of enzymes, resulting in differences in the structure and properties of the extracted polysaccharides, leading to variations in their biological activity and yield. Cellulase could degrade cellulose and non-specifically hydrolyzed chitin in the cell wall. Papain could also non-specifically hydrolyze chitin in the cell wall. And the dissolution of TFPs was promoted by degrading proteins and separating them from proteins [37], [38]. Therefore, the optimal enzyme ratio is cellulase: papain = 2:1.

Effect of different compound enzyme ratios (A), compound enzyme addition (B), enzymolysis temperature (C), ultrasonic power (D) and ultrasonic time (E) on the yield and α-amylase inhibition rate of EUP.

Effects of compound enzyme addition on polysaccharide extraction

Fig. 2B shows that the yield and the α-amylase inhibition rate of polysaccharides continued to increase with the addition of enzymes ranging from 1000 to 4000U/100 mL. This indicated that increasing the amount of cellulase and papain protease within a certain range could better disrupt the cell wall structure of T. fuciformis and hydrolyze the glycoproteins and polysaccharides [39]. However, when the enzyme addition increased further, the inhibition rate on α-amylase began to decrease. Therefore, the optimal amount of compound enzyme addition is determined to be 4000U/100 mL.

Effects of enzymolysis temperature on polysaccharide extraction

The results are shown in Fig. 2C, the α-amylase inhibition rate of TFPs increased with increasing enzymolysis temperature. When the temperature reached to 48 °C, the inhibition rate was the highest, at 61.11 %. This may be due to the enzyme ratio of cellulase to papain being 2:1 in the experiment, with the optimal temperature for cellulase being 50 °C and the optimal temperature for papain being 60 °C [40]. As the temperature continued to rise, both the inhibition rate and the polysaccharide yield decreased. Consequently, the optimal temperature for enzymolysis was determined to be 48 °C and subsequent experiments will be conducted at this temperature. Enzyme catalytic reactions, like most chemical reactions, are influenced by temperature.

Effects of ultrasonic power on polysaccharide extraction

As the Fig. 2D showed, with the amplification of ultrasonic power, the phenomenon of cavitation induced by ultrasound intensifies, thereby engendering a heightened fragmentation of cavitation bubbles and higher content of TFPs. The maximum value is reached at 3 W/mL. Nevertheless, within the realm of 3 to 5 W/mL, the content gradually decreased with the increase in power. This occurrence may be explicated as an outcome arising from structural modifications or hydrolysis of TFPs owing to excessive power, which arises from the impacts of cavitation and mechanical waves [41].

Effects of ultrasonic time on polysaccharide extraction

From Fig. 2E, it can be observed that when the ultrasonic power was constant, both the yield and α-amylase inhibition rate initially increased, followed by a decrease as the ultrasound time was prolonged. The yield and α-amylase inhibition rate reached their maximum value at an ultrasonic time of 20 min. This phenomenon may be attributed to the shear force and instantaneous high pressure generated by the cavitation effect of ultrasonic waves, which may destroy the polysaccharide structure and increase the number of enzyme binding sites. As a result, the inhibition rate of the product with inhibitory activity increased [42]. Therefore, the optimal ultrasonic time for preparing TFPs was 20 min.

Validation of optimal conditions for response surfaces

By analyzing the response surface results, the optimal conditions for high polysaccharide retention rate and α-amylase inhibition rate could be obtained: compound enzyme ratio (cellulase: papain) was 2:1, enzyme addition was 4000U/100mL, enzymolysis temperature was 49.12 °C, ultrasonic power was 3.12 W/mL and ultrasonic time was 19.99 min. The predicted polysaccharide yield and α-amylase inhibition rate were 23.40 % and 59.72 % respectively. Based on practical operations, the enzymolysis temperature, ultrasonic power and ultrasonic time were adjusted to 49 °C, 3 W/mL and 20 min respectively. Under these conditions, the polysaccharide yield and α-amylase inhibition rate were 23.94 % and 61.44 % respectively, which closely near the predicted values derived from the regression equation. This further demonstrated the good fit between the response surface model and the actual results. And it could indicate the reliability of the model.

Effects of different extraction methods on TFPs

The polysaccharide yield serves as an important indicator for evaluating the efficacy of various extraction methods. Table 4 presented the impact of three different extraction methods on sugar content, protein content and yield of TFPs. The yield of TFPs decreased in the following order when different methods were used for extraction: EUP > EAP > UAP. The ultrasonic-enzymatic-assisted ethanol precipitation method could improve the yield of TFPs. TFPs were mainly composed of extracellular polysaccharides in the fermentation broth and intracellular polysaccharides obtained by destroying T. fuciformis spore cell walls. According to the research by Li et al. [8], the fungal spore cell wall was mainly composed of chitin, lignin and cellulose. Different extraction methods had a significant impact on cell wall degradation and polysaccharide production. Ultrasonication effectively ruptured cell walls, facilitating solvent penetration into cells and thereby maximizing the dissolution of polysaccharides. Furthermore, ultrasonic vibrations promoted the uniform release of polysaccharides from cells, resulting in heightened polysaccharide yield [27]. Enzymatic-assisted extraction weakened or disrupted the cell wall structure and facilitated the breakdown of complex internal storage compounds, releasing intracellular substances such as polysaccharides [43]. To achieve higher yields of fermented polysaccharides, various methods such as ultrasound, microwave, enzymatic hydrolysis or a combination of methods can be used to disrupt the cell wall. Mai et al. [44] conducted a comparative study of polysaccharide yield using various extraction methods, including boiling water, enzymatic method, ultrasound, microwave and a combination method. The results indicated that the polysaccharide yield extracted by the composite extraction method was high. By determining the sugar content of the extracted polysaccharides, it was found that the enzymatic-assisted ethanol precipitation method had the highest polysaccharide content, at 67.32 ± 1.94 %. The ultrasonic-enzymatic-assisted ethanol precipitation method and the ultrasonic-assisted ethanol precipitation method had lower polysaccharide contents, at 54.10 ± 0.15 % and 31.88 ± 0.09 % respectively. This indicated that carbohydrates are the main components of polysaccharides.

Three types of polysaccharides all contained proteins. Currently, the commonly used method for removing proteins in the literature was the Sevag method, which was characterized by mild reaction conditions that did not easily destroy the structure of the polysaccharides. The results indicated a positive correlation between concentration and viscosity, with all three polysaccharides exhibiting increased viscosity at higher concentrations. Notably, EUP exhibited the lowest viscosity among the three. Wang et al. This may be due to an increase in the number of molecules per unit volume at lower concentrations, as well as the presence of branching structures, which may affect the entanglement and interaction within and between chains to accommodate a dense state, reducing intermolecular forces and apparent viscosity [47].

FT-IR analysis of TFPs

FT-IR was carried out to identify the primary structure of TFPs using different extraction methods. The spectra of TFPs in the wavenumber range of 4000 ∼ 500 cm−1 are shown in Fig. 6 [37]. All three samples exhibited a typical wide and strong signal peak near 3420 cm−1, primarily attributed to the stretching vibration of –OH, signifying the presence of hydrogen bonds in the polysaccharides extracted by the three methods [48]. Furthermore, the UAP and EUP samples showed stronger absorption after ultrasound treatment. The weak absorption peak at 2950 cm−1 corresponded to the bending vibration of C-H [49]. Additionally, two robust absorption peaks at approximately 1640 cm−1 and 1405 cm−1 were usually caused by C-O vibrations, indicating the presence of sugar acids in all three polysaccharides [50]. The absorption band of polysaccharides was observed between 1400 ∼ 1000 cm−1 [51], indicating a pyranose configuration for glucose [52]. The absorption peaks at 1290 cm−1 and 1230 cm−1 belong to C-O-C vibrations [53]. And the absorption peak at 865 cm−1 confirmed the presence of α-D-glycosidic bonds in all three polysaccharides [54].

FT-IR spectra of different TFPs.

The TFPs functional groups extracted from the three methods were identical. However, the primary distinctions emerged within the fingerprint region spanning 1000 to 500 cm−1. In this spectral range, the absorption peaks primarily corresponded to the types of sugar rings and glycosidic bonds.

Scanning electron microscope observation of TFPs

Fig. 7 illustrated the surface morphology of TFPs recorded through scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images. The appearance of all observed TFP fragments varies in size and shape. UAP exhibited large voids, a non-smooth and protruding surface and irregular geometric shapes. EAP appeared as relatively smooth thin sheets with some areas showing a sponge-like surface. EUP has relatively uniform and densely distributed surface voids, with the addition of some reticular structures compared to EAP, indicating a tighter connection. The polysaccharides exhibited similar characteristics as observed in the study by Xu et al. [55]. Enzyme treatment was observed to reduce the surface roughness of the polysaccharides, possibly attributed to the deposit/aggregation of biopolymers like polysaccharides or proteins and their complexes. These observations confirmed that different extraction methods result in different surface morphologies and appearances of TFPs.

In vitro antioxidant activities of TFPs

Antioxidants played a significant role in the fields of medicine and food. Researchers have discovered that polysaccharides with remarkable antioxidant properties also have potential effects on lowering blood sugar levels in the body. This can be achieved by improving the dysfunction of β cells through antioxidant action [56]. The in vitro antioxidant activities of TFPs obtained through different extraction methods were investigated by assessing their capacity to scavenge DPPH radical, ABTS+ radical and hydroxyl radical scavenging activity (Fig. 8). Within the concentration range of 0.1–2.0 mg/mL, the DPPH scavenging activity of TFPs exhibited a dose-dependent relationship with the concentration of polysaccharides. All TFPs samples exhibited similar trends in DPPH scavenging activity (Fig. 8A). At a polysaccharide concentration of 0.5 mg/mL, EUP exhibited the highest DPPH free radical scavenging ability at 37.31 %, followed by UAP and EAP.

In vitro antioxidant activity of TFPs. (A) DPPH• free radical scavenging activity; (B) ABTS• free radical scavenging capacity; (C) • OH radical scavenging activity.

As shown in Fig. 8B, the removal rate of ABTS+ by TFPs was significantly increase at polysaccharide concentrations ranging from 0.1 to 2.0 mg/mL. When the concentration of EUP was 3.0 mg/mL, the removal rate of ABTS+ by EUP reached 88.34 %, which was close to 96.17 % of Vc. Overall, EUP demonstrated a significantly better clearance effect on ABTS+ compared to UAP and EAP.

Hydroxyl radicals were the most harmful and toxic free radicals generated during the biological metabolic process. They could easily cause oxidative damage to DNA and cell membranes [57]. As shown in Fig. 8C, the results indicated that polysaccharides extracted by three different methods and Vc have strong •OH scavenging activities within the concentration range of 0.1–2.0 mg/mL. Among them, EUP and UAP had similar clearing abilities within the concentration range of 0.5–2.0 mg/mL, which were superior to the clearing ability of EAP for •OH.

In general, compared to UAP and EAP, EUP has a better antioxidant capacity in vitro. This may be due to the lower molecular weight of EUP. There have been reports suggesting that antioxidant activities increase with decreasing molecular weight [48]. Therefore, EUP not only improved the yield of polysaccharides but also has advantages in terms of antioxidant activities in vitro.

In vitro hypoglycemic activities of TFPs

α-Amylase could break down long-chain carbohydrates. By inhibiting the activity of α-amylase, it is possible to slow down the increase in postprandial blood glucose levels, thus controlling blood sugar levels [58], [59]. The inhibitory effect of TFPs on α-amylase activity is shown in Fig. 9A. The inhibitory rate of TFPs on α-amylase activity was dose-dependent. As the concentration of TFPs increased, the inhibition rate of polysaccharides on α-amylase activity also increased. At a concentration of 5 mg/mL, the inhibition rates of UAP, EAP and EUP were 77.78 %, 71.08 % and 77.99 % respectively, which were lower than that of acarbose (86.76 %). EUP has a higher inhibition rate on α-amylase activity compared to UAP and EAP, indicating that TFPs have the potential to inhibit α-amylase activity.

In vitro hypoglycemic activities of TFPs. (A) α-amylase inhibitory activity; (B) glucose dialysis retardation index.

The glucose dialysis retardation index (GDRI) of polysaccharides was an intuitive indicator of their ability to lower blood glucose levels in vitro and was used to predict their ability to slow down intestinal glucose absorption. The delayed glucose dialysis ability of TFPs is shown in Fig. 9B, from which it can be seen that the GDRI values of the three polysaccharides decrease with increasing dialysis time, eventually reaching equilibrium. Among them, the GDRI value of EUP was consistently lower than that of EAP and UAP after 60 min, possibly due to differences in viscosity. Relevant studies have found that the higher the viscosity of the sample, the tighter the binding with glucose, resulting in a larger GDRI value [60].

Conclusion

In this study, we used the fermentation broth of T. fuciformis spores as the raw material. Based on a single-factor experiment, we developed an ultrasonic- enzymatic- assisted ethanol precipitation method to extract fermented TFP using the Box-Behnken response surface optimization scheme. Combining the model with practical experiments, we determined the optimal experimental conditions to be: compound enzyme ratio (cellulase: papain) of 2:1, compound enzyme addition of 4000U/100 mL, enzymolysis temperature of 49 °C, ultrasonic power of 3 W/mL and ultrasonic time of 20 min. Under these conditions, the polysaccharide yield and α-amylase inhibition rate were found to be 23.94 % and 61.44 %, respectively. In addition, the effects of different extraction methods on the fermentation of TFPs were investigated. The results indicated that ultrasonic, enzymatic hydrolysis and ultrasonic-enzymatic combined treatment all caused changes in the yield, structural characteristics and biological activities of TFPs. Among them, EUP had the highest yield and lowest viscosity, which also exhibited good antioxidant and hypoglycemic activities. In terms of structural characterization, the three polysaccharides had similar FT-IR spectra but different morphologies. Therefore, ultrasonic- enzymatic- assisted ethanol precipitation was a good method for extracting TFPs, which was environmentally friendly, safe and had a higher yield. Further research is needed to explore the purification, structural characterization and bioactivity of TFPs.
