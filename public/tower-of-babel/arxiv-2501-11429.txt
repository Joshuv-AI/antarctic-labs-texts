The Explanation Game – Rekindled (Extended Version)
Joao Marques-Silva, Xuanxiang Huang, Olivier Létoffé

Abstract
. Recent work demonstrated the existence of critical flaws in the current use of Shapley values in explainable AI (XAI), i.e. the so-called SHAP scores. These flaws are significant in that the scores provided to a human decision-maker can be misleading. Although these negative results might appear to indicate that Shapley values ought not be used in XAI, this paper argues otherwise. Concretely, this paper proposes a novel definition of SHAP scores that overcomes existing flaws. Furthermore, the paper outlines a practically efficient solution for the rigorous estimation of the novel SHAP scores. Preliminary experimental results confirm our claims, and further underscore the flaws of the current SHAP scores.

The importance of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) cannot be
overstated, being widely accepted as a fundamental pillar of
trustworthy AI.
Both in the European Union (EU) and the United States (US), there has
been recent legislation aiming at regulating the use of systems of AI,
especially in domains that directly impact humans.

As a result,
XAI is expected to provide critical support in attaining

XAI methods can be broadly categorized into those based on feature
attribution and those based on feature selection. Feature attribution
aims at assigning degrees of importance to features, thereby enabling
the ranking of the relative importance of features. In contrast,
feature selection aims at selecting subsets of features, each encoding
a rule for some prediction. For example, Anchors
(
Ribeiro et al., 2018
)

exemplifies XAI by feature selection, while LIME
(
Ribeiro et al., 2016
)

exemplifies XAI by feature attribution. Moreover, whereas some works
are model-agnostic
(
Ribeiro et al., 2016
;
Ribeiro et al., 2018
)
, others
require access to the machine learning (ML)
models
(
Bach et al., 2015
)
. Despite the importance of XAI, most
past efforts are characterized by lack of
rigor
(
Ignatiev, 2020
;
Marques-Silva, 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2024
)
.
As a consequence, most methods of XAI in current use are unworthy of
trust
(
Weber et al., 2024
)
.

The tool SHAP
(
Lundberg and Lee, 2017
)
is arguably the most popular
method of XAI by feature attribution.
As its core, the tool SHAP approximates Shapley
values
(
Shapley, 1953
)
based on a definition that is standard in
XAI
(
Strumbelj and Kononenko, 2010
;
Strumbelj and Kononenko, 2014
)
.
Shapley values are defined on a game, which consists of a set of
elements (e.g. voters) and a given characteristic function that maps
subsets of a set of elements (e.g. players or voters in game theory,
or features in an ML model) to the reals.
Depending on the characteristic function used, different Shapley
values are obtained. However, for all chosen characteristic functions,
the obtained values respect the key axioms of Shapley
values
(
Shapley, 1953
)
.
In the case of game theory, concretely when measuring a priori voting
power, Shapley values are instantiated starting from a given
characteristic function, which was first proposed in 1954 by Shapley
& Shubik
(
Shapley and Shubik, 1954
)
.
The same characteristic function has been used, explicitly or
implicitly, in most other proposals of power indices, i.e. measures of
relative voting power. This is the case with the well-known Banzhaf,
Deegan-Packel and Holler-Packel
indices
(
Felsenthal and Machover, 1998
;
Letoffe et al., 2024
)
, among many others.

In the case of XAI, the tool SHAP is based on a concrete instantiation
of Shapley values, one that uses a specific characteristic function.
However, this characteristic function bears no relationship with those
that have been used in a priori voting power since the
1950s
(
Felsenthal and Machover, 1998
)
.
The theoretical SHAP scores denote the values that the tool
SHAP only approximates, given the chosen characteristic function.
Since its publication in 2017, the tool SHAP, and the corresponding
estimated SHAP scores, have become ubiquitous in a growing number of
uses of XAI.
Furthermore, the complexity of computing the theoretical SHAP scores has been studied in a number of
works
(
den Broeck et al., 2022
;
Arenas et al., 2023
)
.

Our recent work
(
Marques-Silva and Huang, 2024
)
demonstrated
that theoretical SHAP scores can produce misleading
information,
5
5

5

The theoretical SHAP scores are the values that the tool SHAP
would produce if it were capable of computing SHAP scores with the
utmost rigor.
Our results apply to the theory of SHAP scores, and as a consequence
to the values that the tool SHAP approximates. The corollary is that
the tool SHAP approximates Shapley values that can be misleading for a
human-decision maker.

assigning the most importance to absolutely unimportant features,
and not assigning any importance to critically important features.
These negative results are further corroborated by several additional cases
studies
(
Huang and Marques-Silva, 2023
;
Huang and Marques-Silva, 2024
)
.
It should be plain that the results in
(
Marques-Silva and Huang, 2024
)
also serve to question the conclusions from
thousands of papers that build on SHAP, but also from the massive uses of SHAP in companies and in academic and research institutions.
Motivated by the flaws in SHAP’s theoretical foundations, some
researchers have proposed explanations by feature attribution
based on exploiting alternatives to Shapley
values
(
Biradar et al., 2024
;
Yu et al., 2024
)
. The downside of such
approaches is that these are
not
based on Shapley values, and
compute measures of importance that can also yield unsatisfactory
results
(
Letoffe et al., 2024
)
.
Furthermore, as pointed out in recent
work
(
Letoffe et al., 2025
;
Letoffe et al., 2024
)
, the limitations of SHAP scores are not the result of Shapley values
per se, but are instead the result of the characteristic function that
has become ubiquitous in XAI. Finally, as also arguedin

(
Letoffe et al., 2025
;
Letoffe et al., 2024
)
, other characteristic functions in use, e.g. baselines
(
Sundararajan and Najmi, 2020
)
, are also inadequate.

Given the above, a natural question is whether the use of SHAP scores
should be relinquished, at least in application domains where the rigor
of explanations is paramount. Although the answer must be affirmative
for existing implementations (and supporting theory) of SHAP
scores
(
Lundberg and Lee, 2017
)
, this paper proposes a rigorous solution
for the computation of Shapley values for XAI (we will continue to use
the name
SHAP scores
) such that all the limitations reported in
earlier work
(
Marques-Silva and Huang, 2024
)
are overcome.
Moreover, the proposed solution further highlights the connections
between explanations using feature attribution and explanations using
feature selection, which are revealed by adopting a logic-based
approach to explainability.
Finally, the experiments provide conclusive practical evidence to the
flaws of both of earlier definitions of SHAP scores and the tool SHAP.

Definitions

This section adapts and extends the most recent notation used in the
field of logic-based explainability
(
Marques-Silva, 2024
)
.

Classification & regression problems.

Let
{\mathcal{F}}=\{1,\ldots,m\}
denote a set of features.
Each feature
i\in{\mathcal{F}}
takes values from a domain
\mathbb{D}_{i}
.
Domains can be categorical or ordinal. If ordinal, domains can be
discrete or real-valued.
Feature space is defined by

\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{D}_{1}\times\mathbb{D}_{2}\times\ldots\times\mathbb{D}_{m}
.
Throughout the paper domains are assumed to be discrete-valued.
Thus, for real-valued features, some sort of finite discretization is
assumed.
6
6

6

Discretization of real-valued domains is fairly
standard, e.g.
(
Ribeiro et al., 2018
)
.

The notation
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
denotes an arbitrary point in
feature space, where each
x_{i}
is a variable taking values from

\mathbb{D}_{i}
. Moreover, the notation
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})

represents a specific point in feature space, where each
v_{i}
is a
constant representing one concrete value from
\mathbb{D}_{i}
.
A classifier maps each point in feature space to a class taken from

{\mathcal{K}}=\{c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{K}\}
. Classes can also be categorical or
ordinal. However, and unless otherwise stated, classes are assumed to
be ordinal.
In the case of regression, each point in feature space is mapped to an
ordinal value taken from a set of values
\mathbb{V}
, e.g.
\mathbb{V}

could denote
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{R}
.
Therefore, a classifier
{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
is characterized by a
non-constant
classification function

\kappa
that maps feature
space
\mathbb{F}
into the set of classes
{\mathcal{K}}
,
i.e.
\kappa:\mathbb{F}\to{\mathcal{K}}
.
A regression model
{\mathcal{M}}_{R}
is characterized by a non-constant

regression function

\rho
that maps feature space
\mathbb{F}

into the set elements from
\mathbb{V}
, i.e.
\rho:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{V}
.
A classifier model
{\mathcal{M}}_{C}
is represented by a tuple

({\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{F},{\mathcal{K}},\kappa)
, whereas a regression model

{\mathcal{M}}_{R}
is represented by a tuple

({\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{V},\rho)
.
When viable, we will represent an ML model
{\mathcal{M}}
by a tuple

({\mathcal{F}},\mathbb{F},\mathbb{T},\pi)
, with prediction
\pi:\mathbb{F}\to\mathbb{T}
,
without specifying whether
{\mathcal{M}}
denotes a classification
or a regression model, and where
\mathbb{T}
can either be
{\mathcal{K}}
or

\mathbb{V}
. An instance denotes a pair
(\mathbf{v},q)
, where

\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}
,

q\in\mathbb{T}
, with
q=\pi(\mathbf{v})
.
An explanation problem is a tuple
{\mathcal{E}}=({\mathcal{M}},(\mathbf{v},q))
,
where
{\mathcal{M}}
is some ML model
and
(\mathbf{v},q)
is a target instance.

Similarity relational operators.

For each feature
i\in{\mathcal{F}}
, we will assume a user-specified
similarity relational operator (a predicate)

\approx\,\,:\mathbb{D}_{i}\times\mathbb{D}_{i}\to\{\top,\bot\}
, such that

x_{i}\approx{v_{i}}
holds true (
\top
) if the values
x_{i}
and
v_{i}

are deemed sufficiently close to each other.
For example, for categorical features we might require that similarity
should mean that
x_{i}
and
v_{i}
represent the same value. In contrast,
for real-valued features, we might accept a small difference in value,
with different measures of absolute or relative change envisaged.
7
7

7

https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_change
.

Moreover, the similarity relational operator can be generalized as
follows.
Given
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{F}
, we define similarity between
the features in some set
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
:

\mathbf{x}_{{\mathcal{S}}}\approx\mathbf{v}_{{\mathcal{S}}}\quad{\coloneq}\quad\bigwedge\nolimits_{i\in{\mathcal{S}}}x_{i}\approx{v_{i}}

when
{\mathcal{S}}={\mathcal{F}}
, we write
\mathbf{x}\approx\mathbf{v}
, for
simplicity.
Finally, a similarity relational operator is also assumed for the
prediction, such that
\pi(\mathbf{x})\approx\pi(\mathbf{v})
holds when

\pi(\mathbf{x})
is sufficiently close to
\pi(\mathbf{v})
.

Logic-based explainability.

Abductive and contrastive explanations (resp. AXps/CXps) are examples
of formal explanations for classification
problems
(
Marques-Silva, 2022
;
Darwiche, 2023
)
. As argued
in recent work
(
Marques-Silva, 2024
)
, the same concepts can be generalized
to the case of regression problems. The presentation below just
requires a well-defined similarity operator.

A weak (i.e. non-subset minimal) abductive explanation (WAXp) denotes
a set of features
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, such that for every point
in feature space the ML model output is similar to the given instance:

(\mathbf{v},q)
.
The condition for a set of features
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
to
represent a WAXp (which also defines a corresponding predicate

\mathsf{WAXp}
) is as follows:

8

{\mathcal{M}}

, and by

(\mathbf{v},q)

(1)

\mathsf{WAXp}({\mathcal{S}};{\mathcal{E}})~~\coloneq~~\forall(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}).(\mathbf{x}_{{\mathcal{S}}}\approx\mathbf{v}_{{\mathcal{S}}})\limply(\pi(\mathbf{x})\approx\pi(\mathbf{v}))

Furthermore, an AXp is a subset-minimal WAXp.

A weak contrastive explanation (WCXp) denotes a set of features

{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, such that there exists some point in
feature space, where only the features in
{\mathcal{S}}
are allowed to
change, that makes the ML model output distinguishable from the given

(\mathbf{v},q)
.
The condition for a set of features
{\mathcal{S}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
to

Observe that for ML models that compute some function, (W)CXps
can be viewed as implementing actionable
recourse
(
Ustun et al., 2019
)
. Furthermore, (W)CXps formalize the

elsewhere
(
Miller, 2019
)
.
Finally, feature (ir)relevancy is a key concept, that served to
demonstrate that SHAP scores can yield misleading
information. A feature is
relevant
if it is included in at least one AXp;
otherwise it is
irrelevant

Throughout the paper, it is assumed a

3.
The Flaws of
\mathsf{SHAP}_{\mathrm{T}}
Scores

The fact that theoretical SHAP (i.e.
\mathsf{SHAP}_{\mathrm{T}}
) scores use the
expected value of an ML model raises a number of critical issues.
First, in the case of classification, where classes are categorical,
the expected value is ill-defined.
Unfortunately, the use of expected values is more problematic. This
section overviews the known flaws of
\mathsf{SHAP}_{\mathrm{T}}
scores, and briefly
discusses generalizations of those flaws to the case of regression
problems.

3.1.
Known Flaws of
\mathsf{SHAP}_{\mathrm{T}}
Scores

It is reasonably simple to show that
\mathsf{SHAP}_{\mathrm{T}}
scores can be
misleading.
The key insight is that one can create examples of ML models where
feature influence for a prediction is self-evident, and then force the
computation of the
\mathsf{SHAP}_{\mathrm{T}}
scores to produce misleading values by
assigning no importance to influent features, and by assigning some
importance to non-influent features. This basic insight can then be

thereby destroying any correlation that
\mathsf{SHAP}_{\mathrm{T}}

with respect to real feature influence.

3.2.

A possible criticism of our earlier work

4.1.
Selecting the Characteristic Function

Recent work dissected the causes for the flaws of the theoretical
SHAP scores (i.e.
\mathsf{SHAP}_{\mathrm{T}}
)
(
Letoffe et al., 2025
;
Letoffe et al., 2024
)
. Concretely, these flaws result solely from
the choice of characteristic function. This characteristic function is used for example in the SHAP
tool
(
Lundberg and Lee, 2017
)
, but was studied in earlier
works
(
Strumbelj and Kononenko, 2010
;
Strumbelj and Kononenko, 2014
)
. More importantly, this
recent work
(
Letoffe et al., 2025
;
Letoffe et al., 2024
)
proposed alternative characteristic functions that eliminate all of the issues
reported in earlier works
(
Marques-Silva and Huang, 2024
;
Huang and Marques-Silva, 2024
)
.
Accordingly,
in this paper we consider the characteristic function

\upsilon_{a}:2^{{\mathcal{F}}}\to\mathbb{R}
, defined as follows:

(9)

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\upsilon_{a}({\mathcal{S}})&\coloneq&\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcl}1&&\textnormal{if $\mathsf{WAXp}({\mathcal{S}})$}\\[3.0pt]
0&&\textnormal{otherwise}\\
\end{array}\right.\end{array}

The novel characteristic function is inspired by those commonly used
in game theory
(
Shapley and Shubik, 1954
)
. The motivation then and now is
to assign importance to the elements (features or voters) which are

critical
for changing the value of a decision of interest.
In the case of voting power, one assigns importance to voters that
cause coalitions to become winning when the voter is included, and
that are losing when the voter is not included.
In the case of explainability, one assigns importance to a feature
that causes fixed sets of features to be sufficient for the prediction
when the feature is also fixed, and that causes fixed sets of features
not to be sufficient for the prediction when the feature is not fixed.

It is clear that, given the novel characteristic function
\upsilon_{a}
,
we can use the definition of Shapley values to obtain SHAP scores
different from those obtained with
\upsilon_{e}
.
As noted earlier, the new SHAP scores will be referred to by the
acronym
\nu\mathsf{SHAP}_{\mathrm{T}}
;
their properties
are the topic of recent work
(
Letoffe et al., 2025
;
Letoffe et al., 2024
)
.

It is also apparent that we impose few constraints on the
ML models for which the novel SHAP scores can be computed. We

just
need to be able to decide whether a set of selected (and
so fixed) features is sufficient for predicting the value we are
interested in.
It should also be
underscored that the similarity predicate allows us to consider both
classification and regression models.
Finally, as underscored in recent work
(
Letoffe et al., 2025
)
, the
proposed characteristic function reveals a fundamental relationship
between explanations based on feature selection, and those based on
feature attribution.

corollary of

4.2.
Rigorous Estimation of Shapley Values

The complexity of rigorously computing Shapley
values is well-known to be
unwieldy
(
den Broeck et al., 2022
;
Arenas et al., 2023
)
. As a result,
except for small examples or special tractable cases, the most common
solution is to approximate the Shapley value.
In contrast with earlier works on applying Shapley values in XAI, we
opt to estimate the Shapley value using an algorithm known for its
strong theoretical guarantees
(
Castro et al., 2009
)
. (This algorithm
will be referred to as CGT, and is shown as
Algorithm

1

The inputs to the algorithm consist of a game
(
Chalkiadakis et al., 2012
)
,
characterized by a number of elements and a characteristic function
\upsilon:2^{N}\to\mathbb{R}
, such that

\upsilon(\emptyset)=0
, and also the values of
\epsilon
and
\alpha
.

\epsilon
denotes the error of Shapley value estimation, that one
wants to guarantee with probability
1-\alpha
. The algorithm
executes a number
r
of runs of a basic estimation procedure, such
that
\mathbf{Pr}(|\mathsf{\hat{Sc}}_{i}-\mathsf{Sc}_{i}|\leq\epsilon)\geq 1-\alpha
, where
\mathsf{Sc}_{i}

denotes the exact Shapley value for feature
i
, and
\mathsf{\hat{Sc}}_{i}
denotes
the estimate of the Shapley value.
r
denotes the number of times the
main loop of the CGT algorithm is executed; its estimation is detailed
in
(
Castro et al., 2009
)
.
The algorithm iteratively picks a permutation of
N

from the set of permutations
\psi(N)

then used for defining a total of
n
sets of features, each with a
different size, for each of which the value of
\Delta_{i}
is computed
and used for updating
\mathsf{\hat{Sc}}

4.3.
Deciding Weak Abductive Explanations

A key component of estimating rigorous SHAP scores is the ability to
efficiently decide whether a set of features is a weak AXp.
Clearly,
logic-based computation of explanations can be used in this
context
(
Marques-Silva, 2022
;
Marques-Silva, 2023
;
Marques-Silva, 2024
)
.
However, for large-scale datasets and respective ML models,
this approach is unlikely to scale.
First, there is the complexity of reasoning, which is still a
challenge for highly complex ML models despite recently
observed progress
(
Izza et al., 2024
)
.
Second, even if deciding whether a set of features is a weak AXp could
be done reasonably efficiently, the very large number of tests that
must be considered for estimating Shapley values would likely make the running times prohibitively large.
The consequence is that deciding whether a set of features is a weak
AXp should be attained with a negligible running time, ideally
with polynomial-time guarantees.

Therefore, instead of exploiting a rigorous model-based approach for
explaining an ML model, we consider instead a rigorous

data-based
approach.
Concretely, we propose instead to exploit
sample-based
explanations

(
Cooper and Amgoud, 2023
)
(sbXps). sbXps are computed with
respect to a
sample
of the feature space, i.e. a set of
instances, and are rigorous with respect to that sample. For example,
the sample might be the original dataset, or consist of the sampling
carried out by tools such as LIME
(
Ribeiro et al., 2016
)
,
SHAP
(
Lundberg and Lee, 2017
)
or Anchor
(
Ribeiro et al., 2018
)
, or an
aggregation of both. However, sbXps are
rigorous
given the
sample. Thus, in cases where the sample is the feature space,
sbXps match AXps/CXps.

A
sample space

\mathbb{S}
is a subset of feature space,
i.e.
\mathbb{S}\subseteq\mathbb{F}
. (We refer to a sample space as a

dataset
when the prediction is known for each point in the
sample space.)
A set of features
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
is a weak sbAXp if,

(11)

\forall(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}).\left(\mathbf{x}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\approx\mathbf{v}_{{\mathcal{X}}}\right)\limply(\pi(\mathbf{x})\approx\pi(\mathbf{v}))

If no proper subset of

is also a weak sbAXp, then
{\mathcal{X}}
is
declared an sbAXp.
Similarly, a set of features
{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
is a weak sbCXp
if,

(12)

\exists(\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}).\left(\mathbf{x}_{{\mathcal{F}}\setminus{\mathcal{Y}}}\approx\mathbf{v}_{{\mathcal{F}}\setminus{\mathcal{Y}}}\right)\land(\pi(\mathbf{x})\not\approx\pi(\mathbf{v}))

SHAP and nuSHAP were compared using two metrics: i) the ranking of
feature importance imposed by different scores;
and ii) the runtime for computing these scores.
Specifically, for each tested instance, we first compute its nuSHAP
and SHAP scores.
We then determined the order of feature importance based on these
scores.
For SHAP, we considered two orders: one based on the original SHAP
scores and the other based on the absolute values of the SHAP scores.
Next, we compare the order imposed by nuSHAP scores with the order
imposed by original SHAP scores, and separately compare the order
imposed by nuSHAP scores with the order imposed by the absolute values
of SHAP scores.

To compare the rankings of feature importance,
we used the metric rank-biased overlap
(RBO)
(
Webber et al., 2010
)
for each pair of scores.
RBO is a metric used to measure the similarity between two ranked
lists, and it ranges between 0 and 1.
A higher RBO value indicates a greater degree of similarity between
the two rankings, with 1 denoting a perfect match for the top-ranked
elements considered.
A publicly available
implementation
17
17

17

https://github.com/changyaochen/rbo
.
of
RBO was used in our experiments.
Given the fact that most people focus on top-ranked
features
(
Miller, 1956
)
, we set
persistence
to
0.5
and

depth
to
5
in our setting. This means that we focused on the
top-5 features while placing greater emphasis on the top-3 features.

Table

4
summarizes the RBO values for all the tested
instances, including the minimum, maximum, and mean RBO values.
As can be observed, there is essentially
no
correlation between
the
\nu\mathsf{SHAP}_{\mathrm{E}}
scores obtained with the nuSHAP tool, and the

\mathsf{SHAP}_{\mathrm{E}}
scores obtained with the SHAP tool.

Figure

4
shows the distribution of RBO values for the different
instances for the datasets corral and mux6, both of which are purely
binary.
(The blue (resp. green) histograms depict the RBO values comparing
nuSHAP and SHAP (resp. the absolute value of SHAP).
Surprisingly, the RBO measure is never 1, and most often it is
not even close to 1.
For values of the RBO metric close to 0, this means that the important
features reported by the tool SHAP are not the important features
reported by nuSHAP. And as argued in this paper, nuSHAP is

guaranteed
not to mislead.
For larger values of the RBO metric (e.g. around
0.8{\sim}0.9
),
most of the important features may be the same, but either their
relative order of importance is computed incorrectly by the tool SHAP,
or one of the features is assigned undue importance.

6.
Discussion

Recent work
(
Marques-Silva and Huang, 2024
;
Huang and Marques-Silva, 2024
)
revealed
critical flaws with the definition of SHAP scores
(
Lundberg and Lee, 2017
)
,
i.e. the proposed use of Shapley values
(
Shapley, 1953
)
in XAI.
Concretely, this work constructed classifiers for which
the theoretical SHAP scores
convey misleading information.
This implies that the tool SHAP
(
Lundberg and Lee, 2017
)
approximates
scores that can be misleading.
As a consequence, the identified flaws question the conclusions from
thousands of papers that build on the tool SHAP.

Motivated by these negative results, a number of more recent
works proposed alternatives to the use of SHAP
scores
(
Biradar et al., 2024
;
Yu et al., 2024
)
. Unfortunately, these
alternatives are not based on Shapley values, and so they can
also produce unsatisfactory results
(
Letoffe et al., 2024
)
.

In contrast, this paper proposes a novel solution, one that targets
the efficient computation of a novel definition of SHAP scores,
thereby addressing the known flaws of the SHAP scores in current
use
(
Marques-Silva and Huang, 2024
;
Huang and Marques-Silva, 2024
;
Letoffe et al., 2025
)
.
The experiments confirm the scalability of the proposed solution,
and
provide further evidence of SHAP’s flaws, not only in theory, but also
in practice.
Concretely, the experiments demonstrate that the measures of feature
importance obtained with the tool SHAP are misleading in almost all
of the tests considered. The lack of quality in the rankings of the
scores obtained with the tool SHAP is always observable, and most
often very significant, confirming that these scores can be
fairly unrelated with those obtained with the novel non-misleading
definition of SHAP scores.
