Background

Low back pain (LBP) is a pervasive and debilitating condition affecting millions of individuals globally [1, 2]. It represents a significant burden on healthcare systems and the economy due to its high prevalence and the complexity of its diagnosis and treatment. The imaging techniques, particularly magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), have become indispensable tools in the assessment and management of spinal pathologies [3, 4]. MRI provides detailed visualization of spinal structures, enabling clinicians to diagnose a variety of conditions ranging from degenerative disc disease to spinal metastases. Despite the advantages of MRI, interpreting these images can be challenging due to the complexity and variability of spinal diseases, necessitating the integration of sophisticated computational methods to enhance diagnostic accuracy [4, 5].

Machine learning (ML) and, more specifically, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have shown considerable promise in the field of medical imaging [5–7]. CNNs excel in extracting hierarchical features from imaging data, thereby improving the segmentation and classification of pathological structures within MRI scans. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of CNNs in enhancing MRI image clarity and facilitating the accurate diagnosis of various spinal conditions [8, 9]. However, traditional CNNs may not always provide the high level of detail required for distinguishing subtle pathological changes, leading to the exploration of more advanced techniques, such as self-attention mechanisms integrated into CNN frameworks [10, 11].

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence, particularly the development of large language models (LLMs), have shown great promise in addressing these challenges by leveraging their advanced pattern recognition capabilities [12–14]. Our research focuses on the development of domain-specific LLMs designed to improve the precision classification of lumbar spine diseases. This study aims to harness the power of LLMs to analyze MRI images and accurately identify a range of lumbar spine conditions, including degenerative disc disease (DDD), foraminal stenosis (FS), hyperlordosis, hypolordosis, osteoporosis, spinal stenosis, spondylolisthesis, and VCF (vertebral compression fractures). By training these models on a comprehensive dataset of mid-sagittal MRI views, we aim to enhance the diagnostic accuracy and reliability of MRI interpretations.

The central research question guiding this study is: “Can domain-specific large language models significantly improve the accuracy of classifying lumbar spine diseases from MRI images?” Our hypothesis is that the implementation of LLMs will result in higher diagnostic accuracy compared to traditional methods, due to their ability to recognize complex patterns and subtle differences in MRI scans. This research aims to address the critical need for more accurate diagnostic tools in the evaluation of lumbar spine disorders. By developing and applying domain-specific LLMs, we seek to improve the precision and consistency of MRI interpretations, ultimately leading to better patient outcomes and more effective treatment planning for individuals suffering from low back pain.

Research question

Our research seeks to determine whether a multimodal deep learning approach, integrating CNN-extracted MRI features with numerical spinal measurements (e.g., lumbar lordotic angle (LLA), lumbosacral angle (LSA), and disc heights), can significantly improve the accuracy and precision of classifying complex lumbar spine disorders such as spondylolisthesis, spinal stenosis, and disc degeneration. Specifically, we aim to evaluate if combining these data types through early fusion in a BERT-based LLM enhances the model’s ability to diagnose multiple spine disorders concurrently and generalizes effectively in real-world clinical settings.

Hypothesis

We hypothesize that integrating MRI texture features and numerical spinal parameters via early fusion layers in the BERT-based spinal LLM will lead to more accurate classification of lumbar spine disorders compared to traditional single-modality approaches [15–19]. Furthermore, we expect that the iterative refinement of diagnostic standards using CNN-based MRI texture analysis will increase the model’s sensitivity to subtle anatomical variations, allowing for better diagnostic performance, which will be confirmed by external validation on an independent dataset of expert-validated MRI cases.

Methods

Data source

This composite dataset, comprising mid-sagittal views of the lumbar spine, includes images annotated with ground truth labels and spinal measurements [20]. The dataset was created to establish a strong correlation between these images and clinically relevant spinal measurements. Currently, these measurements are obtained either manually or using computer-assisted tools. For instance, in spinal fusion surgery, relevant spinal measurements—such as the lumbar lordotic curve angle (both segmental and whole lumbar spine), lumbosacral angle, spinal heights, and vertebral body dimensions.

Segmentation of lumbar spine in MRI images

We developed a domain-specific LLM tailored for the segmentation of lumbar spine structures in MRI images. This LLM was trained on a comprehensive dataset to enhance its accuracy and reliability in identifying and segmenting spinal regions. MRI scans were acquired using high-resolution imaging protocols, optimized to capture the mid-sagittal and axial views of the lumbar spine. These protocols ensured the clarity and detail necessary for precise segmentation, providing comprehensive coverage of the anatomical structures.

The segmentation protocol involved multiple stages to ensure accuracy and consistency. An iterative annotation process was employed where initial segmentations were reviewed and corrected by expert radiologists and spinal surgeons, refining the segmentation boundaries and improving the model’s performance. Semi-automatic segmentation tools were used to provide an initial segmentation, which was then manually corrected by experts, combining the efficiency of automatic tools with the precision of manual adjustments. Manual corrections by expert radiologists were essential to address any inaccuracies and to validate the segmentation results, ensuring they met clinical standards.

Data preprocessing involved several steps to prepare the MRI images for segmentation. Intensity normalization was applied to reduce variations in image brightness and contrast, and Gaussian smoothing techniques were used to reduce noise and enhance image quality. Additionally, images were aligned to a common anatomical reference to ensure consistency across the dataset. The baseline algorithm for segmentation included a CNN architecture specifically designed for medical image analysis. The network was trained using annotated MRI images, and performance metrics such as dice coefficient (cutoff: 0.88) and intersection over union (IoU, cutoff: 0.92) were used to evaluate its accuracy.

The segmented images were further classified into various disease categories, including DDD, FS, HD, hyperlordosis, hypolordosis, normal, osteoporosis, spinal stenosis, spondylolisthesis, and VCF. Each disease category was assigned a specific label for classification purposes, aiding in the detailed analysis and interpretation of the segmentation results. This classification provided valuable insights for clinical decision-making, facilitating the diagnosis and treatment of various spinal conditions.

CNN-transformation of MRI data

The CNN extracts critical anatomical features from MRI data, including LLA: 40.5o, LSA: 30.2o, anterior/posterior disc heights (e.g., L1L2_Ant: 9.5 mm, L5S1_Post: 8.3 mm), and superior/inferior vertebral heights (e.g., L1_Sup: 29.4 mm, L2_Inf: 31.5 mm). These features are derived from individual MRI slices and are essential for understanding the structure and condition of the vertebrae and discs. The CNN architecture employs 5–10 convolutional layers with 3 × 3 filter sizes and max pooling to reduce dimensionality while retaining critical features, transforming the MRI data into structured feature vectors. These vectors capture positional relationships and structural characteristics related to conditions like disc degeneration and spondylolisthesis, enabling precise analysis without high-dimensional embeddings.

CNN output as input to BERT

The BERT-based model processes the CNN-extracted features as direct scalar values, representing key lumbar spine measurements such as LLA, LSA, and disc heights. These features are treated as tokens, retaining their scalar values for input into the BERT model. For example, LLA (40.5o) is treated as a scalar value. The model applies positional encodings to capture the positional relationships between various parameters, such as the relative height between L1L2_Ant (9.5 mm) and L5S1_Ant (8.3 mm). This allows BERT to understand the anatomical relationships between different lumbar spine features.

Once tokenized, the features pass through BERT’s multi-head self-attention mechanism, enabling the model to focus on relevant relationships between these anatomical features (e.g., how LLA affects disc height changes). This mechanism captures interdependencies across the lumbar spine features, ensuring that the model considers positional relationships effectively. The model’s classification layer then processes these tokens to output probabilities for various lumbar spine disorders, such as herniated disc (0.85 probability), spondylolisthesis (0.75 probability), or spinal stenosis (0.90 probability).

Multimodal data integration

The dataset used for training the BERT-based model consists of 28,065 patients drawn from 290 referenced studies, providing a robust foundation for diagnosing a wide range of lumbar spine disorders, including disc degeneration, spinal stenosis, spondylolisthesis, and lumbar fractures. The dataset includes detailed parameter values, such as LLA, LSA, anterior/posterior disc heights (e.g., L1L2_Ant, L2L3_Post), and superior/inferior vertebral heights (e.g., L1_Sup, L5_Inf), which serve as key indicators of spinal health. These parameters help establish initial diagnostic standards, which are further refined through CNN-based MRI texture analysis to enhance accuracy. Important anatomical measurements—such as the lumbar lordosis angle, lumbosacral angle, vertebral body heights, and disc heights—are used to define disease-specific value ranges, allowing for more precise classification of conditions like spondylolisthesis or herniated discs. The CNN-extracted MRI features are integrated with numerical values using early fusion layers, allowing the model to learn complex interrelationships between anatomical parameters and MRI-derived features. This multimodal integration improves diagnostic accuracy across various conditions, refining diagnostic standards based on observed MRI textures. The fusion of numerical measurements and MRI data enhances the model’s ability to classify complex spine disorders with high precision across multiple spine disorders (Supporting Table S1).

Training and testing

To ensure robust model performance, the data is split into training (70%), validation (15%), and test (15%) sets, with balanced representation across diagnostic labels to mitigate potential class imbalances. A stratified k-fold cross-validation approach is applied to the training and validation sets, where the data is split into 5 folds, and each fold serves as the validation set once while the remaining folds are used for training. This method ensures that performance metrics are computed consistently across different subsets of the data. The input pipeline integrates three data modalities: textual reports processed by the BERT-based LLM, MRI images handled by the CNN, and numerical measurements analyzed by a feed-forward neural network. To evaluate the model’s performance, metrics such as accuracy, precision, recall, and the F1-score are computed for each cross-validation fold. Additionally, early stopping and model checkpoints are employed to prevent overfitting by halting training when validation performance plateaus and saving the best-performing model. This strategy ensures both improved generalization and reliable diagnostic performance across multiple lumbar spine disorders.

External validation

To address the issue of external validation and real-world testing, the model’s performance was thoroughly evaluated using an independent dataset consisting of 514 MRI cases, which were rigorously validated by domain experts. This external dataset served as a robust benchmark to assess the model’s clinical applicability. By testing the model on MRI cases from a different source, this external validation ensured that the model generalizes well across populations and clinical environments, reducing the risk of overfitting to the original dataset. The inclusion of this independent dataset significantly enhances the model’s clinical relevance and robustness, providing confidence in its ability to diagnose lumbar spine disorders in diverse real-world settings.

Comparative analysis of spinal structural variations

To perform the comparative analysis of spinal measurements across different lumbar disorders, we utilized a comprehensive dataset of mid-sagittal MRI views of the lumbar spine, which included conditions such as DDD, FS, HD, hyperlordosis, hypolordosis, normal, osteoporosis, spinal stenosis, spondylolisthesis, and VCF. Using PairGrid visualizations, we generated scatter plots and density plots for pairs of measurements, allowing for a detailed comparison of the distributions and relationships between different parameters. The visualizations were color-coded to represent different disorders, facilitating the identification of unique structural patterns associated with each condition. Statistical analysis was conducted to calculate the mean, median, and standard deviation for each parameter within each disorder group, ensuring a robust comparison. The data was preprocessed for normalization and outlier detection, and the visualizations were reviewed by clinical experts to validate the findings and ensure their relevance for diagnostic and treatment planning purposes.

Precision classification of spinal lumbar disorders using BERT-based spinal LLM model

Using a domain-specific LLM trained on spinal MRI images, we segmented the lumbar spine structures. These initial segmentations were refined through an iterative process involving expert radiologists and spinal surgeons to ensure accuracy. We extracted critical spinal measurements, including the lumbar lordotic angle, lumbosacral angle, intervertebral distances (e.g., L1L2_Ant, L2L3_Post), and vertebral dimensions (e.g., L1_Sup, L2_Inf, S1_Ant). Preprocessing steps included intensity normalization to reduce variations in image brightness and contrast, and Gaussian smoothing to reduce noise and enhance image quality. Additionally, images were aligned to a common anatomical reference to ensure consistency across the dataset. For statistical analysis, we calculated the correlation coefficients for all pairs of spinal measurements to determine the degree of linear relationship between each pair. We used data analysis and visualization software, specifically Python with libraries such as Seaborn and Matplotlib, to create the correlation matrix. Each cell in the matrix represents the correlation coefficient between two measurements. Positive correlations were depicted in shades of red, negative correlations in shades of blue, and zero or no correlation in white or neutral colors. The resulting heatmap provided a visual representation of these relationships, highlighting significant structural patterns and variations within the lumbar spine.

To achieve precision classification of spinal lumbar disorders, we employed a deep machine learning LLM to analyze a comprehensive dataset of spinal measurements. The LLM model was trained on this dataset to recognize patterns and differentiate among 61 distinct lumbar spine disorder categories, including normal (0), DDD (1), FS (2), HD (3), hyperlordosis (4), hypolordosis (5), osteoporosis (6), spinal stenosis (7), spondylolisthesis (8), and VCF (9). The model utilized scatter plots, density plots, and statistical analysis, including ANOVA p-values, to validate significant differences across categories and ensure high classification accuracy. Measurements such as LLA and LSA were key differentiators, with distinct values across categories. This structured approach enabled the LLM model to provide a robust and precise classification system, aiding in accurate diagnosis and effective treatment planning for lumbar spine disorders.

Results

Quality assurance

The dataset comprised mid-sagittal views of the lumbar spine, annotated with ground truth labels and spinal measurements. The MRI dataset was obtained from the study by Masood et al. [20], which included axial views. These images were converted to mid-sagittal views for evaluating spinal deformities and analyzing spinal balance. A total of 514 MRI scans were used, covering a range of conditions such as DDD, FS, HD, hyperlordosis, hypolordosis, normal, osteoporosis, spinal stenosis, spondylolisthesis, and VCF.

Segmentation quality was evaluated using dice coefficients and IoU metrics. The domain-specific LLM for segmentation achieved an average Dice coefficient of 0.92 and an average IoU of 0.88 across all segmented regions, indicating a high degree of overlap between the model’s predictions and the manually annotated ground truth labels. Quality assurance involved multiple rounds of manual correction and validation by expert radiologists and spinal surgeons. The iterative annotation process ensured that the segmented regions accurately represented the anatomical structures. The manual correction process led to a 5% improvement in segmentation accuracy, as verified by the experts.

The annotation accuracy was measured by comparing the initial semi-automatic segmentations with the final manually corrected annotations. The iterative process of manual corrections and expert validation resulted in an annotation accuracy of 95%. This high level of accuracy underscores the reliability of the annotated dataset for training and evaluating the segmentation algorithm. The algorithm evaluation focused on its ability to accurately identify and segment lumbar spine regions in MRI images. The evaluation metrics included the dice coefficient, IoU, precision, recall, and F1 score. The algorithm achieved a precision of 91%, recall of 90%, and F1 score of 90.5%, indicating its high accuracy and reliability in segmentation tasks.

Discussion

The correlation matrix heatmap reveals significant relationships between various spinal measurements (Fig. 2), emphasizing the structural coherence within the lumbar spine and highlighting critical parameters that can inform clinical assessments and interventions. Recent research supports and adds nuance to these findings: a study by Caprariu et al. [21] demonstrated weak to moderate correlations between certain preoperative MRI parameters and Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) scores in patients with lumbar spinal stenosis. Specifically, the severity of lumbar canal stenosis had a weak positive correlation with initial ODI scores, while the cross-sectional area of the psoas muscle showed a moderate negative correlation with ODI scores both preoperatively and at a 1-year follow-up [21] and Mirzashahi et al. (2020) evaluated the impact of surgical correction of adult spine deformity on radiological parameters and their correlation with clinical outcomes. Restoration of sagittal alignment was particularly important for enhancing patient function and quality of life [22]. Certainly, there is some contrast evidence: Costa et al. (2020) found that preoperative spinopelvic parameters did not significantly predict clinical outcomes after lumbar decompressive surgery for non-deformity patients. Their study highlighted that while imaging provides essential structural information, it must be combined with clinical and functional assessments for comprehensive patient evaluation [23]. The study by Liu et al. (2020) emphasized that while postoperative PROMIS scores reflect patient outcomes reasonably well, preoperative PROMIS scores alone do not adequately capture the disease-specific impact of spinal stenosis. This suggests that a holistic approach integrating various assessment tools is necessary to fully understand and predict patient outcomes [24]. Understanding these correlations is vital for diagnosing spinal conditions and optimizing surgical outcomes.

The density distributions of spinal measurements reveal significant structural differences between individuals with lumbar disorders and healthy controls (Fig. 3). These variations in parameters such as Lumbar Lordotic Angle, Lumbosacral Angle, intervertebral distances, anterior and posterior measurements, vertebral heights, and vertebral body dimensions underscore the impact of lumbar spine conditions on spinal anatomy. A comprehensive radiological analysis of individuals with developmental spinal stenosis found significant differences in axial anteroposterior vertebral canal diameter, interpedicular distance, and lamina angle between stenosis patients and controls. These structural variations emphasize the developmental nature of spinal stenosis and its implications for clinical diagnosis and management [25]. Understanding these differences is crucial for diagnosing and treating lumbar spine disorders effectively.

The density distributions in Fig. 4 provide a detailed visualization of how various spinal measurements differ across lumbar disorders. These insights are crucial for understanding the structural implications of each condition and can inform more targeted diagnostic and therapeutic strategies. Duculan et al. (2022) highlighted the utility of quantitative computed tomography (qCT) in differentiating BMD at various vertebral levels in patients with lumbar degenerative spondylolisthesis (LDS). The study found that BMD varied significantly at different lumbar levels, with lower BMD associated with LDS at L4-L5 [26]. This emphasizes the importance of level-specific BMD measurements for accurate diagnosis and treatment planning. Kim et al. (2023) explored the relationship between BMD and the texture of the multifidus muscle using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and spinal CT. The study found strong correlations between muscle texture features and BMD, suggesting that muscle health could be an important factor in understanding and managing bone density and spinal disorders [27]. Noh et al. (2023) developed a machine learning model incorporating global alignment and proportion scoring, body mass index, and bone mineral density to predict mechanical complications in adult spinal deformity surgery. The model showed high predictive accuracy, underscoring the potential of integrating multiple parameters for improved surgical outcomes [28]. The distinct patterns and parameter values identified in the distributions underscore the importance of precise measurement in the assessment and management of lumbar spine disorders.

The PairGrid visualizations provide a comprehensive comparison of spinal measurements across lumbar disorders (Fig. 5). The detailed analysis of anterior and posterior measurements highlights the unique structural characteristics associated with each condition, offering valuable insights for clinical diagnosis and treatment planning.

In conclusion, the LLM model provided a precise and detailed classification of 58 spinal lumbar disorder cases, each defined by unique spinal measurement profiles (Table S1). This precision classification aids in the accurate diagnosis and effective treatment planning for various lumbar spine conditions. PairGrid visualizations provide a multi-dimensional view of spinal measurements, which is essential for understanding the unique structural characteristics of various lumbar disorders. A study by Hong et al. (2022) highlighted the importance of detailed imaging in diagnosing spinal diseases, demonstrating how GANs can generate realistic synthetic MRI images from CT scans, thereby enhancing diagnostic accuracy [29]. Deep learning algorithms have shown promising results in improving the diagnostic accuracy of lumbar MRI scans. Lewandrowski et al. (2020) reported that their deep learning system achieved high sensitivity and specificity in detecting neural element compression, indicating its potential in clinical practice [30]. This aligns with the findings of our study, where the LLM model demonstrated robust performance in classifying lumbar disorders. The precision classification provided by the LLM model has significant implications for clinical diagnosis and treatment planning. As highlighted in a systematic review by Huang et al. (2023), MRI remains a gold standard for diagnosing lumbar disc herniation due to its high diagnostic value (Huang et al., 2023) [31]. The integration of models like ours can further enhance the accuracy and reliability of these diagnostic tools.

The precise classification of lumbar spine disorders using LLMs represents a significant advancement in medical imaging and diagnostics. This study’s comprehensive analysis of mid-sagittal MRI views, processed through a deep machine learning LLM, has resulted in the precision classification of 58 distinct lumbar spine disorder cases. By examining unique measurement profiles, such as the Lumbar Lordotic Angle and Lumbosacral Angle, this model ensures high accuracy in disease classification. Recent studies support the efficacy of deep learning models in improving diagnostic accuracy for lumbar spine conditions. For instance, a study published in the European Spine Journal demonstrated the successful application of a CNN for detecting and classifying lumbar spinal stenosis, achieving high sensitivity and specificity across different stenosis types (central, lateral recess, and foraminal) [32]. This underscores the potential of neural network architectures in accurately identifying and grading spinal conditions. Additionally, research in the journal Diagnostics highlighted the utility of CNNs in the automatic detection and grading of intervertebral disc degeneration (IDD) using MRI images. The study achieved an accuracy of over 95% in classifying lumbar IDD, emphasizing the robustness of deep learning models in handling complex medical imaging tasks [33]. These findings align with our results, where the LLM model achieved high accuracy metrics, including a Dice coefficient of 0.92 and an IoU of 0.88.

Study limitations

First, while the model demonstrates high performance across validation and test datasets, its ability to generalize to more diverse and heterogeneous patient populations remains uncertain. The dataset, though extensive, may not fully capture the wide range of anatomical and pathological variations present in global populations. As such, the model’s performance may vary in real-world clinical settings that include patients with rare or complex spine conditions that were underrepresented in the training data. Future studies should focus on expanding the dataset to include more diverse populations and rare lumbar spine disorders to enhance the model’s generalizability. Another limitation concerns the reliance on expert-confirmed MRI cases as the benchmark for assessing the model’s predictions. While this is an important strength, it also introduces potential bias, as the model’s performance is contingent on the accuracy of expert diagnoses. Human experts, despite their proficiency, can be subject to diagnostic variability. The lack of a universally standardized protocol for MRI interpretation across different clinical settings could impact the consistency of these expert confirmations. Future work should aim to incorporate a multi-center validation approach, where the model is tested across various institutions with different diagnostic protocols, to ensure the robustness and transferability of its performance.

Additionally, while the BERT-based spinal LLM integrates multimodal data inputs, including textual reports, numerical measurements, and MRI images, the complexity of integrating these inputs introduces challenges. Variability in the quality and structure of clinical reports, differences in MRI imaging protocols, and inconsistencies in numerical data capture could all affect model performance. Moreover, the model’s dependence on pre-processing steps like tokenization and normalization requires manual intervention and standardization, which may introduce variability and limit its scalability. To address these concerns, future efforts should focus on automating and standardizing the data pre-processing pipeline, ensuring that the model can handle varying data formats and quality across different clinical environments without compromising its diagnostic accuracy.

Future potential application.

The results of the BERT-based spinal LLM model indicate the potential for future clinical validation and application. With accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-scores all near 0.9, the model has demonstrated the ability in classifying complex lumbar spine disorders, such as disc degeneration, spinal stenosis, and VCF. The inclusion of multimodal inputs—MRI images, textual clinical reports, and numerical measurements—adds robustness to the model, allowing it to generalize effectively across various cases. The successful application of the model to unseen data, including 514 expert-confirmed MRI images, further strengthens the argument for its use in clinical environments, where consistent validation from domain experts is critical for trust and reliability in real-world settings.

The diagnostic framework established by the model, which relies on key spinal measurements such as LLA, LSA, and vertebral heights, is another factor that supports its future potential. By developing disease-specific value ranges based on these measurements, the model can classify 61 different combinations of lumbar spine disorders, as shown by the results. This ability to handle a wide range of disorders, from more localized issues like vertebral compression fractures to broader conditions like spinal stenosis and spondylolisthesis, suggests that the BERT-based spinal LLM could become a key tool in diagnosing and tracking the progression of lumbar spine conditions. Further clinical trials and validation studies involving large patient cohorts would help confirm these findings and enhance the model’s reliability and utility in clinical practice.

The heatmap analysis of the model’s performance also highlights its future validation potential. The clear clustering patterns observed in the correlations between anatomical measurements and disease categories demonstrate that the model is capable of distinguishing between a wide array of spine disorders. For instance, the highly positive correlations between LLA and LSA in cases of hyperlordosis (e.g., Category 61, hyperlordosis, spondylolisthesis, VCF, scoliosis, osteoporosis, spinal stenosis) and the strong negative correlations in spondylolisthesis and spinal stenosis (e.g., Category 9, hypolordosis, spondylolisthesis, VCF, osteoporosis, spinal stenosis, scheuermann’s disease) show that the model effectively captures the biomechanical changes associated with these conditions. Additionally, its ability to reflect complex interrelationships between vertebral measurements, as seen in hypolordosis and flatback syndrome (e.g., Category 4, hypolordosis, flatback syndrome, DDD, osteoporosis, ankylosing spondylitis), highlights the model’s diagnostic precision. These insights suggest that the BERT-based spinal LLM is well-positioned for future validation through real-world clinical trials, where it could significantly enhance diagnostic accuracy and improve patient outcomes in the management of lumbar spine disorders.

Conclusions

The BERT-based spinal LLM model represents an advancement in the classification and diagnosis of lumbar spine disorders, demonstrating high performance across key metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1-score. By integrating multimodal inputs—textual reports, numerical measurements, and MRI images—the model provides a comprehensive diagnostic tool capable of generalizing across a wide range of lumbar spine conditions. Its ability to classify 61 different combinations of disorders, coupled with expert-confirmed validation, underscores its potential as a valuable asset in clinical practice for diagnosing complex conditions such as disc degeneration, spondylolisthesis, and spinal stenosis. However, despite these promising results, certain limitations must be addressed to maximize its clinical applicability. The need for broader population-based datasets and multi-center validation is paramount to ensure the model’s generalizability. Additionally, standardizing data input and pre-processing methods will be essential to maintain diagnostic accuracy across diverse clinical environments. As the model evolves through future validation and refinements, it holds the potential to become a transformative tool in the field of spinal healthcare, enhancing diagnostic precision, streamlining clinical workflows, and ultimately improving patient outcomes of lumbar spine disorders.
