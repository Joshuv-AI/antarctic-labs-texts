Fundamental Properties and Embedding Results in a Novel ( Φ x , ψ ) \displaystyle\left(\Phi_{x},\psi\right) -Fractional Musielak Space with an Application to Nonlocal BVP
Ayoub KASMI 1 , El Houssine AZROUL 2 , and Mohammed SHIMI 3

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce and study a novel class of generalized ( Φ x , ψ ) \displaystyle(\Phi_{x},\psi) -fractional Musielak spaces 𝒦 Φ x α , β , ψ \displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}^{\alpha,\beta,\psi} , which extends classical fractional spaces and offers the flexibility to model heterogeneous and nonlinear phenomena with memory and nonlocal effects. A detailed and rigorous analysis of their functional structure is carried out. Several new properties and embedding results are established, highlighting the originality of the proposed framework and its relevance to nonlocal BVPs. To illustrate the significance of this functional setting, we prove the existence of nontrivial solutions to a nonlinear fractional differential problem under an Ambrosetti–Rabinowitz type condition, using the mountain pass theorem. Our results provide new perspectives for the analysis of nonlocal and nonhomogeneous equations in variable-exponent and Musielak–Orlicz settings.

This naturally raises the question of why new function spaces are developed when classical frameworks already exist.

The answer lies in the ongoing generalization of functional spaces to meet increasingly complex and heterogeneous problems. Classical Lebesgue spaces

Fractional calculus has profoundly reshaped the modeling of nonlocal interactions in physical systems. In contrast to classical derivatives, which are local and describe the instantaneous rate of change at a point, fractional derivatives are integral operators whose evaluation inherently depends on the function over an extended domain. This built-in nonlocality enables the modeling of processes in which the present state is influenced by the entire past evolution of the system.

From a mathematical perspective, the kernels of fractional derivative operators serve as weighting functions that systematically account for past contributions. Such a property makes fractional calculus particularly effective for capturing hereditary behaviors and spatially distributed effects that are often neglected in classical models.

Overall, the adoption of fractional derivatives—including Riemann–Liouville, Caputo, and Hilfer formulations—provides a rigorous and unified framework for incorporating nonlocality and historical dependence into complex systems. This approach continues to expand the theoretical and applied analysis of nonlinear, heterogeneous, and memory-influenced media

Remark 2.1

K_{\Phi_{x}}(\Omega)=\Big\{u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}\ \text{measurable}\ :\ \int_{\Omega}\Phi_{x}(|u(x)|)\,dx<\infty\Big\},

\overline{\Phi}_{x}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\overline{\varphi}_{x}(s)\,ds,\quad\forall x\in\overline{\Omega},\ t\geq 0,

\overline{\varphi}_{x}(t)=\sup\{s\in\mathbb{R}:\varphi(x,s)\leq t\}.

(2.2)

(2.3)

1<\varphi^{-}:=\inf_{t\geqslant 0}\frac{t\varphi_{x}(t)}{\Phi_{x}(t)}\leqslant\varphi^{+}:=\sup_{t\geqslant 0}\frac{t\varphi_{x}(t)}{\Phi_{x}(t)}<+\infty\quad\text{ for all }x\in\overline{\Omega}\text{ and }t\geq 0.

\displaystyle\Phi

\text{ for each }x\in\overline{\Omega}\text{, the function }[0,\infty)\ni t\rightarrow\Phi_{x}(\sqrt{t})\text{ is convex . }

\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}:=\mathcal{K}^{\alpha,\beta,\psi}_{\Phi_{x}}=\left\{u\in L_{\Phi_{x}}(\Lambda,\mathbb{R});\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u\in L_{\Phi_{x}}(\Lambda,\mathbb{R})\right\}.

This space is endowed with the norm given by

(3.2)

\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}}=\|u\|_{\Phi_{x}}+[u]_{\Phi_{x}},

where
\displaystyle[\cdot]_{\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}}
being the Luxemburg norm defined as follows

[u]_{\Phi_{x}}=\inf\left\{\delta>0:\int_{\Lambda}\Phi_{x}\left(\frac{\left|\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u\right|}{\delta}\right)dx\leqslant 1\right\}.

For any
\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}^{\psi}
, we associate the modular

\displaystyle\rho_{\Phi_{x}(\cdot)}^{\psi}:\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}^{\psi}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\rho_{\Phi_{x}(\cdot)}^{\psi}(u)=\int_{\Lambda}\Big(\Phi_{x}(|u|)+\Phi_{x}(|\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u|)\Big)\,dx.

As a result, the norm
\displaystyle\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}^{\psi}}
turns out to be equivalent to the modular norm

(3.3)

\|u\|_{\rho_{\Phi_{x}(\cdot)}^{\psi}}=\inf\left\{\delta>0:\rho_{\Phi_{x}(\cdot)}^{\psi}\!\left(\tfrac{u}{\delta}\right)\leq 1\right\}.

The space
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}
is defined as the completion of

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{0}^{\infty}(\Lambda,\mathbb{R})
under the norm

\displaystyle\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}}
introduced in (
3.2
),
that is,

\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}=\{\,u\in\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}:u(0)=u(T)=0\,\}.

On the other hand, for any
\displaystyle v\in\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}
, we introduce the convex modular associated with the space
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}
, which is defined by

{}^{0}\rho_{\Phi_{x}}^{\psi}(u)=\int_{\Lambda}\Phi_{x}(|\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u|)\,dx.

The norm associated with this modular, often referred to as the Luxemburg norm, is then given by

\|u\|=[u]_{\Phi_{x}}=\inf\left\{\delta>0:\rho_{\Phi_{x}}^{\psi}\!\left(\frac{u}{\delta}\right)\leqslant 1\right\}.

Proposition 3.1
.

Assume that condition (

Proof.

Let us recall that

\overline{\varphi}_{x}(t)=\sup\{s:\varphi_{x}(s)\leq t\},\qquad\overline{\Phi}_{x}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\overline{\varphi}_{x}(s)\,ds,\quad\forall x\in\Lambda,\ t\geq 0.

Furthermore, for every
\displaystyle x\in\Lambda

\displaystyle\varphi_{x}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is an increasing homeomorphism,
in particular from
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{+}
onto
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{+}
.
Hence, for each
\displaystyle x
, the map
\displaystyle t\mapsto\varphi_{x}(t)
has an inverse
function
\displaystyle t\mapsto\varphi_{x}^{-1}(t)
.
It follows that
\displaystyle\varphi_{x}(s)\leq t
if and only if

\displaystyle s\leq\varphi_{x}^{-1}(t)
, which implies

\overline{\varphi}_{x}(t)=\varphi_{x}^{-1}(t).

Consequently,

\overline{\Phi}_{x}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\varphi_{x}^{-1}(s)\,ds,\quad\forall x\in\Lambda,\ t\geq 0.

Next, using a change of variables in the integral, we have

\overline{\Phi}_{x}\bigl(\varphi_{x}^{-1}(s)\bigr)=\int_{0}^{\varphi_{x}^{-1}(s)}\varphi_{x}(\theta)\,d\theta=\int_{0}^{s}r\,\frac{d}{dr}\varphi_{x}^{-1}(r)\,dr=s\,\varphi_{x}^{-1}(s)-\overline{\Phi}_{x}(s),\quad\forall x\in\Lambda,\ s\geq 0.

This identity yields

\overline{\Phi}_{x}(s)\leq s\,\varphi_{x}^{-1}(s),\quad\forall x\in\Lambda,\ s\geq 0.

Choosing
\displaystyle s=\varphi_{x}(t)
gives

\overline{\Phi}_{x}\bigl(\varphi_{x}(t)\bigr)\leq t\,\varphi_{x}(t),\quad\forall x\in\Lambda,\ t\geq 0.

Finally, by assumption
(
2.3
)
,

t\,\varphi_{x}(t)\leq\varphi^{+}\,\Phi_{x}(t).

By combining the above inequalities, we deduce that

\overline{\Phi}_{x}\bigl(\varphi_{x}(t)\bigr)\leq\varphi^{+}\,\Phi_{x}(t),\quad\text{for all }x\in\Lambda\text{ and }t\geq 0,

which concludes the proof.

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\left\|u\right\|_{\Phi_{x}}\leqslant\left[\frac{\left(\psi(T)-\psi(0)\right)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{\varphi^{-}}}\left[u\right]_{\Phi_{x}}^{\frac{\varphi^{+}}{\varphi^{-}}},~~\text{ if }\|\cdot\|>1,\\

(3.16)

\|u\|_{\infty}\leqslant\frac{M\left(\overline{\Phi}_{x}(1)\right)^{\frac{1}{\varphi+}}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\left(\psi(x)-\psi(0)\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\varphi^{+}}}\left[u\right]_{\Phi_{x}}.

Proof.
Since
\displaystyle\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u\in L_{\Phi_{x}}
it follows from Proposition

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\left\|\mathbf{I}_{0+}^{\alpha;\psi}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u\right\|_{\Phi_{x}}\leqslant\left[\frac{\left(\psi(T)-\psi(0)\right)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{\varphi^{-}}}\left[u\right]_{\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}}^{\frac{\varphi^{+}}{\varphi^{-}}},~~\text{ if }\|\cdot\|>1,\\
\left\|\mathbf{I}_{0+}^{\alpha;\psi}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u\right\|_{\Phi_{x}}\leqslant\left[\frac{\left(\psi(T)-\psi(0)\right)^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\right]^{\frac{1}{\varphi^{+}}}\left[u\right]_{\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}}^{\frac{\varphi^{-}}{\varphi^{+}}},~~\text{ if }\|\cdot\|<1.\\
\end{array}\right.

Using Proposition
3.5
, we obtain the first result
\displaystyle(\ref{315.})
.

By
\displaystyle(\ref{HOLDER})
,
we have for all

\displaystyle\left|\mathbf{I}_{0+}^{\alpha;\psi}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{M\left(\overline{\Phi}_{x}(1)\right)^{\frac{1}{\varphi^{+}}}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\left(\psi(x)-\psi(0)\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\varphi^{+}}}\left[u\right]_{\Phi_{x}}.

4.
Mountain Pass Solutions for
\displaystyle\left(\Phi_{x},\psi\right)
-Fractional Musielak Equations

In this section, we investigate problem
(
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
)
within the new
\displaystyle\left(\Phi_{x},\psi\right)
-fractional Musielak space
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{\Phi_{x}}
, together with the embedding results established in Section
3
. The analysis is developed through the application of the Mountain Pass Theorem, which ensures the existence of a nontrivial critical point of the corresponding energy functional under appropriate structural assumptions.

In that context, we say that
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}
is a weak solution of problem
(
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
)
, if, for all
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}
, we have

(4.1)

\int_{\Lambda}a_{x}\left(\left|\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}(u)\right|\right)\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}(u)\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}(\phi)dt=\int_{\Lambda}h(t,u)\,\phi\,dt.

We consider the energy functional

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}:\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}\to\mathbb{R}

associated with (
4.1
), defined as follows:

\mathfrak{J}(u)=\int_{\Lambda}\Phi_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u\right)\,dt-\int_{\Lambda}\mathcal{H}(t,u)\,dt,

where
\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(t,x)=\int_{0}^{x}h(t,s)\,ds.

By a standard argument similar to that used in
[
18
, Lemma 4.1]
, it follows that

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}_{\lambda}\in\mathscr{C}^{1}\bigl(\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}},\mathbb{R}\bigr)
, and
for all
\displaystyle u,\phi\in\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}
we infer

\left\langle\mathfrak{J}^{\prime}(u),\phi\right\rangle=\int_{\Lambda}a_{x}\left(\left|\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u\right|\right)\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}\phi dt-\int_{\Lambda}h(t,u)\phi dt

Therefore, the weak solutions of
(
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
)
correspond to the critical points of the functional
\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}
.

We are now in a position to state our main existence result as follows:

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\displaystyle 0<\alpha<1
and
\displaystyle 0\leq\beta\leq 1
.
Assume that the function
\displaystyle h
satisfies the conditions
\displaystyle(
h
{}_{1})
and
\displaystyle(
h
{}_{2})
.
Then, the problem
(
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
)
admits at least one nontrivial weak solution

\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}
.

To prove Theorem
4.1
, we make use of the Mountain Pass Theorem.
Before proceeding, we recall and establish several auxiliary results that play a fundamental role
in the proof of the main existence result presented in this section.

Let

\displaystyle\ell=\inf\{\,\mathcal{H}(t,u)\;|\;t\in\Lambda,\ |u|=1\,\}

Then, for any
\displaystyle s\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}

The following result, commonly known as the Palais-Smale compactness condition (PS), provides the compactness criterion essential for the application of the Mountain Pass Theorem.

Lemma 4.3

Let
\displaystyle\Phi_{x}

Proof.
Let
\displaystyle\left\{u_{k}\right\}

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}
on
\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}

(4.5)

\left|\mathfrak{J}\left(u_{k}\right)\right|\leq M\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\mathfrak{J}^{\prime}\left(u_{k}\right)=0.

We begin by showing that the sequence
\displaystyle\{u_{k}\}
is bounded, recall that

\mathfrak{J}(u_{k})=\int_{\Lambda}\Phi_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u_{n}\right)\,dt-\int_{\Lambda}\mathcal{H}(t,u_{k})\,dt,

\left\langle\mathfrak{J}^{\prime}(u_{k}),u_{k}\right\rangle=\int_{\Lambda}a_{x}\left(\left|\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u_{k}\right|\right)\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u_{k}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u_{k}dt-\int_{\Lambda}h(t,u_{k})u_{k}dt.

Then by
\displaystyle(\ref{45})

(4.6)

\displaystyle\left|\mathfrak{J}\left(u_{k}\right)-\tfrac{1}{\mu}\left\langle\mathfrak{J}^{\prime}\left(u_{k}\right),u_{k}\right\rangle\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(1+\left\|u_{k}\right\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}\right).

3.3
and
\displaystyle(\ref{S})

(4.7)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}(u_{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\Phi_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u_{k}\right)\,dt-\int_{\Lambda}\mathcal{H}(t,u_{k})\,dt

\displaystyle\geqslant\left\|u_{k}\right\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}^{\varphi^{\pm}}-\int_{\Lambda}\mathcal{H}(t,u_{k}(t))dt.

In addition

(4.8)

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathfrak{J}^{\prime}(u_{k}),u_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}a_{x}\left(\left|\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u_{k}\right|\right)\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u_{k}\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u_{k}dt-\int_{\Lambda}h(t,u_{k})u_{k}dt

\displaystyle\leqslant k\left\|u_{k}\right\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}^{\varphi^{\pm}}-\int_{\Lambda}h(t,u_{k}(t))u_{k}(t)dt.

\displaystyle(\ref{S6})

\displaystyle\left(1-\tfrac{k}{\mu}\right)\left\|u_{k}\right\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}^{\varphi^{\pm}}-\int_{\Lambda}\mathcal{H}\left(t,u_{k}(t)\right)dt+\tfrac{1}{\mu}\int_{\Lambda}h\left(t,u_{k}(t)\right)u_{k}(t)dt

We are now ready to establish the proof of the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem

4.1
. Evidently,
\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}(0)=0
. It thus remains to establish that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}
meets the geometric criteria stipulated by the mountain pass theorem.

From
\displaystyle(\ref{IN})
, we obtain

\max_{t\in\Lambda}|u(t)|\leq R\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}},\quad\forall u\in\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}},

where

R=\frac{M\left(\overline{\Phi}_{x}(1)\right)^{\frac{1}{\varphi+}}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\left(\psi(x)-\psi(0)\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\varphi^{+}}}.

Next, let
\displaystyle C_{1}=\frac{1}{R}
. Then, by the above inequality together with
\displaystyle(\ref{43})
, we deduce that if
\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}\leq C_{1}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Lambda}\mathcal{H}(t,u(t))\,dt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Lambda}\mathcal{H}\Big(t,\frac{u(t)}{|v(t)|}\Big)|u(t)|^{\mu}\,dt

\displaystyle\leq R^{\mu}T\tilde{C}\,\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}^{\mu}.

Then

\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Lambda}\Phi_{x}\left(\mathfrak{D}_{0^{+}}u\right)\,dt-\int_{\Lambda}\mathcal{H}(t,u)dt\,

\displaystyle\geq\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}^{\varphi^{\pm}}-R^{\mu}T\tilde{C}\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}^{\mu},\quad\text{ if }\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}\leq C_{1},

Therefore

\mathfrak{J}(v)\geq C_{1}^{\varphi^{\pm}}-R^{\mu}TC_{1}^{\mu}\tilde{C},\text{ if }|v\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}=C_{1}.

Let us consider
\displaystyle L<\min\left\{C_{1},\left(\tfrac{1}{R^{\mu}T\tilde{C}}\right)^{\frac{1}{\mu-\varphi^{\pm}}}\right\}
and
\displaystyle\theta=L^{\varphi^{\pm}}-L^{\mu}T\tilde{C}R^{\mu}
, then

\mathfrak{J}(u)\geq\theta~~\text{ with }\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}=L.

Therefore,
\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}
satisfies the first geometric condition required by the mountain pass theorem.

Let
\displaystyle s\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
and
\displaystyle u\in\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}
. From Lemma
4.2
, we drive

\mathfrak{J}(su)\leq s\,\|u\|_{\mathcal{K}^{0}_{\Phi_{x}}}^{\varphi^{\pm}}-\ell|s|^{\mu}\int_{\Lambda}|u(t)|^{\mu}\,dt+T\ell.

Since
\displaystyle\mu>k
, letting
\displaystyle s\to+\infty
gives
\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}(su)\to-\infty
.
Hence, the second geometric condition of the mountain pass theorem is satisfied by taking
\displaystyle e=sv
with
\displaystyle s
sufficiently large, so that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}(e)\leq 0
.

Therefore, the functional
\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}
satisfies the mountain pass condition. Consequently,
\displaystyle\mathfrak{J}
admits a nontrivial critical point, which corresponds to a nontrivial weak solution of problem
(
\displaystyle\mathcal{P}
)
.
