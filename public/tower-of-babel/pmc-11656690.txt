Elucidating the Dynamics of Biodegradation and Biosynthesis of Magnetic Nanoparticles in Human Stem Cells
Authors: Alberto Curcio, Guilhem Curé, Ana Espinosa, Nicolas Menguy, Itziar Galarreta‐Rodriguez, Ali Abou‐Hassan, Bérénice Piquet, Laurence Motte, Yoann Lalatonne, Claire Wilhelm, Aurore Van de Walle
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIron oxide nanoparticles, due to their magnetic properties, are versatile tools for biomedical applications serving both diagnostic and therapeutic roles. Their performance is intricately intertwined with their fate in the demanding biological environment. Once inside cells, these nanoparticles can be degraded, implying a loss of magnetic efficacy, but also transformed into neo‐synthesized magnetic nanoparticles, potentially restoring functionality. This study aims to delineate biological features governing these processes. Magnetic nanoparticles are internalized in human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs), and their biotransformations are investigated from nano‐ to micro‐scale using electron microscopy (STEM‐HAADF, HRTEM, SAED), a benchtop magnetic sensor, and fine structural characterizations (synchrotron XRD, VSM). Results evidence a delicate equilibrium between the biodegradation and biosynthesis of magnetic nanoparticles, with biotransformation kinetics depending on cell density at magnetic labeling and on spatial cell configuration (monolayers vs spheroids). The biotransformed nanoparticles, composed of magnetite or maghemite, are localized within endosomal/lysosomal compartments and associated with the recruitment of ferritin proteins.

Introduction

The introduction of iron oxide nanoparticles into biomedical research has catalyzed innovative approaches in therapeutic strategies.[ 1 ] These nanoparticles possess unique and versatile physicochemical features, including superparamagnetic properties.[ 2 ] It is these magnetic characteristics that enable their use as contrast agent for imaging,[ 3 , 4 ] carrier for drug delivery,[ 5 , 6 ] guiding tool for magnetic bioprinting,[ 7 ] and anti‐cancer therapeutic through magnetic hyperthermia.[ 8 ] A prerequisite for these applications is the safe injection of the nanoparticles into the body, which raises concerns about their biocompatibility. Extensive studies and reviews have confirmed that magnetic nanoparticles exhibit high biocompatibility when the iron oxide core is associated with a biocompatible coating and administered within specific dosage limits that will vary depending on nanoparticle specifications.[ 9 , 10 ] Their clinical approval as contrast agent for magnetic resonance imaging, hyperthermal agent for the treatment of brain glioblastoma, and treatment for anemia further comforts in their safety.[ 11 ] This biocompatibility is largely attributed to their iron‐based composition, as iron is naturally present in the human body and essential for life.[ 12 ]

Another large body of work concerning iron oxide (magnetic) nanoparticles is the optimization of their design according to the targeted application.[ 13 , 14 ] However, unless being protected by a non‐degradable coating (e.g., polymeric,[ 15 ] gold,[ 16 ] or silica[ 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 ]) or by encapsulation (e.g., with poly(d,l‐lactide‐co‐glycolide) (PLGA)[ 21 ]), these nanoparticles do not remain intact in the biological environment. Along their in vivo journey, they can be internalized in cells where they can be assimilated, transformed.[ 22 ] Understanding the nanoparticles transformations in the biological environment thus becomes essential, particularly due to the direct link between nanoparticle integrity and diagnostic or therapeutic applicability. One goal can be to develop nanomaterials that degrade rapidly and integrate into the organism's natural metabolism upon achievement of purpose. Alternatively, long‐term stability may be sought.

Initial assessments of nanoparticle integrity in the biological environment were qualitative only, due to the lack of quantitative methods. In vivo studies have showed that magnetic nanoparticles can undergo degradation, releasing iron ions that may integrate the biological iron pool of the cells. This has notably been demonstrated since 1989 using nanoparticles containing iron isotopes (e.g., 59Fe) that are absent from the organism and can be differentiated from endogenous iron (56Fe), when assimilated into hemoglobin.[ 23 ] Precise quantification of biotransformations has become possible in the past ten years, with the adaptation of magnetometry approaches to biological samples.[ 24 ] This has been the case with conventional magnetometry measurements such as VSM (Vibrating Sample Magnetometer)[ 16 , 25 , 26 , 27 ] or SQUID (Superconducting Quantum Interference Device) magnetometer,[ 25 , 28 , 29 ] magnetophoresis,[ 26 , 30 ] and also a magnetic sensor allowing real‐time rapid measurements,[ 31 ] even on living samples. In all cases, the magnetism of cells that have internalized magnetic nanoparticles is measured, allowing for quantification of nanoparticle integrity, with a decrease in cell magnetism indicating the degradation of the magnetic nanoparticles.

As a result, magnetometry studies confirmed that magnetic nanoparticles can undergo significant degradation in human mesenchymal stem cells (hMSC) when these cells differentiate into chondrocytes (cartilage cell type). Up to 90% of internalized nanoparticles were degraded in less than one month only.[ 26 ] More surprisingly, it was recently demonstrated that hMSCs can also neo‐synthetize magnetic nanoparticles from the products of degradation: Iron ions released over the degradation of initially internalized magnetic nanoparticles are used as iron precursors for the biosynthesis of new magnetic nanoparticles, within the cells.[ 30 , 32 ] This phenomenon has been observed in hMSCs differentiating into adipocytes, osteocytes, or maintained undifferentiated.[ 30 ] This capacity for the bioproduction of magnetic nanoparticles by human cells has been corroborated by a number of papers showing, since the 1990s, the presence of magnetic nanoparticles in human tissues, including the brain,[ 33 , 34 , 35 ] heart,[ 36 ] and tumor tissues.[ 37 ] In vitro, the appearance of a magnetic signal following the culture of human and mouse MSC cells in medium supplemented with iron salts (being non‐magnetic) was also recently evidenced.[ 38 ] The biosynthesis of other types of nanoparticles has also been reported, such as gold ones in endothelial and muscle cells.[ 39 ] Taken together, these studies underscore the importance of nanoparticle biosynthesis and biomagnetism in the organism as well as for use in biomedicine. However, our understanding of its origin possibly stemming from the biotransformations of magnetic nanoparticles in human cells remains limited.

In light of these considerations, this study comprehensively explores the biotransformations of magnetic nanoparticles within hMSCs, taking into consideration cell–cell interactions by varying their spatial geometry (monolayer vs spheroid). A multi‐tool approach is employed, including magnetometry, imaging, and advanced structural characterization techniques. First, the internalization and integrity of magnetic nanoparticles within cells are tracked using a benchtop magnetic sensor. Results reveal that nanoparticle internalization varies between experiments achieved under the same incubation conditions, in part due to cell confluency at labeling. It underscores the importance of confirming the internalized nanoparticle dose rather than relying on incubation concentrations. The biotransformations of magnetic nanoparticles is then assessed depending on spatial cell configuration: 2D (cell monolayer) or 3D (cell spheroids). Magnetic sensor signals reveal a bioactive system that exhibits variability but demonstrates strong reproducibility within individual experiments. Signals for 2D and 3D models evolve as waves in the first days of culture (day 0 to 4), with signal decrease due to nanoparticle dissolution being followed by an increase back indicative of nanoparticle re‐synthesis. These magnetic signals are completed with electron microscopy analyses (STEM‐HAADF, HRTEM, SAED) as well as structural characterizations (synchrotron XRD). Results validates previous observations of the biodegradation of magnetic nanoparticles and, remarkably, of the de novo synthesis of magnetic nanoparticles directly within the cells. These biosynthesized nanoparticles appear composed of magnetite or maghemite and localized within the cellular endosomes/lysosomes. Moreover, spatial cell configuration induces different biotransformation trends on the longer term. In 2D, a progressive decrease in magnetic signal is obtained between day 4 and 21, indicative of nanoparticle biodegradation within time. In 3D, magnetic signal remains constant between day 4 and 21, indicative of nanoparticles remaining intact or of a reached equilibrium between biodegradation and biosynthesis rate. Altogether, this study delineates some of the intricate molecular dynamics underlying the transformations of magnetic nanoparticles within human cells.

Results

Real‐Time Measurement of Live Cells' Magnetic Properties with a Multi‐Frequency Magnetic Sensor Reveals Significant Environmental Variability

Maghemite nanoparticles, with a core diameter of ≈8 nm and coated with citrate, were produced in a large scale by the Massart procedure.[ 40 ] These nanoparticles (at concentrations of 0.5, 1, or 2 mm) were incubated with hMSCs for 30 min in RMPI medium. After nanoparticle incubation, cells were rinsed with RPMI medium, and a chase was performed in complete DMEM, to allow for the nanoparticles to be internalized in cells (3 h chase time in average). This incubation results in the internalization of the magnetic nanoparticles in intracellular compartments, as seen in Figure 1A where iron has been stained in blue, and condensates of nanoparticles can be visualized as dots in the cytoplasm of the cells. Scanning transmission electron microscopy images acquired in the high‐angle annular dark‐field (STEM‐HAADF) imaging mode, reveal that internalized nanoparticles (appearing in white) are confined in endosomes (Figure 1B). D‐spacings calculated from corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) confirms the spinel structure such as maghemite or magnetite (Figure 1C). This result is further confirmed by high‐resolution TEM observations and fast Fourier transform (FFT) analysis (Figure 1D). The amount of nanoparticle internalized per cell was quantified using a benchtop magnetometer, here named “magnetic sensor” (Figure 1E). Measurements are based on the nonlinear magnetization of superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles. The magnetic sensor emits two alternating magnetic fields, one at a frequence of 100 kHz and an amplitude of 10 Oe, and one at 100 Hz and 200 Oe. The sample is submitted to these two fields, and the signal obtained is proportional to the quantity of nanoparticles inside the sample. It is expressed in arbitrary units and here referred to as magnetic sensor signal. This signal can be correlated to more common magnetometry measurements, such as the sample magnetic moment at saturation obtained by vibrating sample magnetometry (VSM), via a correlation curve (correlation coefficient of 0.97, see Figure S1, Supporting Information).

Quantification of the magnetic load per cell following incubation with magnetic nanoparticles can be achieved in this manner. Briefly, cells labeled with magnetic nanoparticles were detached from their culture dish, placed in a 500 µL Eppendorf tube, centrifuged, and inserted in the magnetic sensor. Measurement of magnetic nanoparticle load internalized by cells was accurately and rapidly determined (30 s per measurement in average, repeated three times, meaning 1.5 min total). It confirmed an increase in amount of internalized magnetic nanoparticles with increasing magnetic nanoparticle concentration (Figure 1F). And this, on live cells.

Moreover, systematic measurements of nanoparticle load per cell upon incubation with a same concentration of magnetic nanoparticles, under the same incubation conditions, indicated that the amount of internalized nanoparticle varies from one experiment to another as shown in Figure S2A (Supporting Information) where results for seven independent experiments with incubations at 1 mm of nanoparticles are plotted. Interestingly, adding 5 mm citrate to the incubation medium with the aim of increasing the nanoparticle stability leads to similar internalization values as those of the incubations without citrate (Figure S2B, Supporting Information). The difference in nanoparticle load between experiments can in part be explained by varying confluency at labeling (Figure 2A,B), which were not fully controlled over the experiments as not initially perceived as meaningful. This might be linked to the fact that, when cells are less confluent, they are more spread out thus have a higher surface available to nanoparticle internalization (Figure 2C,D). Additionally, upon nanoparticle labeling, a chase time is performed to allow for full endosomal internalization of the nanoparticles. Over this chase, non‐confluent cells can migrate and, in the process, internalize nanoparticles remaining adherent to the bottom of the cell culture dish (Figure 2E). This is particularly true if cells are at low confluency, thus providing more surface area where nanoparticles can adhere, and cells can migrate. It must indeed be noted that differences in nanoparticle internalization are mostly observed for important disparities in confluence levels. Other mechanisms governing differential internalization might also be at play. These first results underline the importance of quantifying nanoparticle internalization and not only relying on the incubation concentration to have a strong understanding of the dose of nanoparticles that cells are exposed to, in particular for cytotoxicity studies. The use of the multi‐frequency magnetic sensor for real‐time measurement of the magnetic properties of live cells has revealed significant environmental variability. It underscores the complexity of the cellular and extracellular environment and the necessity for real‐time and rapid measurement tools to accurately monitor the internalization amount of magnetic nanoparticles.

Biotransformations of Magnetic Nanoparticles in Conventional 2D Cell Culture (Monolayers)

Upon magnetic labeling, cells were cultured in 2D and harvested at given times for magnetic signal measurement. Results from three independent experiments were normalized to an initial signal value at 100 and averaged such as each datapoint has a n > 20 (Figure 3A). The obtained graph indicates that the magnetic signal first decreases (Day 0 to 1), implying a degradation of the nanoparticles. Subsequently, a signal resurgence can be witnessed (Day 1 to 2), indicative of the bioproduction of magnetic nanoparticles. It is followed by a second biodegradation (magnetic signal decreases from Day 2 to Day 21). It is further confirmed by inductively coupled plasma allowing measurement of total iron content in the sample (magnetic + non‐magnetic iron) that there was no iron escape over the experimental time, as total iron content does not decrease (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The timing of the initial biodegradation followed by a bioproduction may vary between experiments, with a magnetic signal rebound sometimes observed as early as Day 2 or 3 and sometimes on Day 9 (see Figure S4A,B, Supporting Information). Nonetheless, this three‐step trend has consistently emerged across all conducted experiments and was obtained on cells cultured in non‐differentiating medium (complete DMEM) as well as in medium driving cell differentiation down the adipogenic pathway (Figure S5A, Supporting Information). It recalls data obtained on primary hMSCs (derived from patients) cultured in 2D where the bioproduced nanoparticles had a rock‐like structure 8 nm in diameter in average.[ 30 , 32 ] Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of the iron oxide nanostructures present at day 3 and 9 in the cells corroborate these previous observations (Figure 3B,C; Figures S6–S9, Supporting Information). The observed magnetic nanoparticles (made of maghemite/magnetite as confirmed by XEDS, high resolution imaging, and FFT) remain confined within endosomes/lysosomes and, for most, retain architectures similar to the initial nanoparticles and previously described biosynthesized ones (≈8 nm in diameter and rock‐like).[ 30 , 32 ] Smaller crystalline nanostructures were also occasionally observed (Figure 3B,C). These could either be nanoparticles undergoing dissolution or in the process of synthesis. Immunofluorescence of both ferritin subunits (L and H) show a diffuse staining in the control and Day 0 cells, indicating ferritin presence throughout the cytoplasm (Figure 3D,E). Interestingly, dots can be observed at Day 3 for both subunits, indicating the confinement of multiple ferritins at a same site and suggesting ferritin recruitment in endosomes/lysosomes, where the nanoparticles are located.

Biotransformations of Magnetic Nanoparticles is Modified by 3D Cell Culture (Spheroids)

Upon labeling with magnetic nanoparticles, cells were detached, resuspended in 500 µL Eppendorf tubes, centrifuged, and kept in culture in these tubes over extended time periods (up to 33 days achieved, data not shown). Cells form cohesive spheroids within 24 h. The Eppendorf tubes perfectly fit within the coil of the magnetic sensor; they can be regularly taken out from the incubator and quickly measured (1.5 min per sample in average), without compromising cell viability. It allowed repeated measurements and tracking of magnetic signal evolution on each independent spheroid over the culture time without impacting cell viability, as previously confirmed.[ 31 ] These measurements on 3D cell spheroids indicate two possible magnetic signal trends (Figure S10, Supporting Information; Figure 4A). A first one where the signal remains constant over time (Figure S10, Supporting Information), and a second one, most often occurring in our experiments and encompassing three events (Figure 4A). First, the magnetic signal increases (between Day 0 and Day 1). In a second step, it decreases (Day 1 to Day 4). Then, a steady state is reached, and the magnetic signal remains constant (Day > 4). The temporality of this three steps process (increase → decrease → steady state) varies between experiments (Figure S4C,D, Supporting Information), but reproducibility is surprisingly robust within a same experiment as shown by the small error bars on each graph and by curves of independent spheroids tracked in parallel (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Moreover, total iron content did not decrease over the experimental time indicating that the iron released over nanoparticle degradation remained in the cells (Figure S3, Supporting Information). These results indicate a bioproduction of magnetic nanoparticles, followed by a biodegradation, to finally reach an equilibrium representative of nanoparticles either remaining intact or undergoing dissolution/biomineralization cycles at a balanced rate. With this reached steady state, the magnetic signal remains at 93% at Day 21. Interestingly, this equilibrium is achieved in cells cultured in a non‐differentiating medium, whereas in a medium promoting differentiation along the chondrogenic pathway, the nanoparticles are progressively degraded and the magnetic signal decreases down to 63% at Day 21 (Figure S5B, Supporting Information). This further attests that nanoparticle fate is highly dependent on the intracellular microenvironment. TEM analyses of these nanostructures confirm here also their spinel structure and their size in the 8–9 nm range (Figure 4B,C; Figure S12, Supporting Information), making it difficult to differentiate internalized from bioproduced nanoparticles. Alongside these biotransformations, the production of the two subunits of the ferritin protein is observed, as demonstrated by a brighter staining at Day 21 than at Day 3 as well as the appearance of dots suggesting ferritin recruitment at nanoparticle site (within endosomes/lysosomes) (Figure 4D,E).

Dissecting Biotransformation Scenarios

These two types of trends observed in spheroids (either a magnetic signal remaining constant or the three‐step process increase → decrease → steady state) can in part be explained by cell confluency at labeling (Figure S13, Supporting Information). When cells have been split very recently and confluency is low at labeling (≤10% corresponding to 7000 cells cm− 2 or less), there is an increased signal between Day 0 and Day 1–5, which does not arise at higher confluences. It might be due to higher metabolic activity at labeling and increased nanoparticle processing for low confluences.

It remains to explain the magnetic signal that increases above initial values in the first days of culture. To do so, exploring deeper magnetism evolution prior to measurement of Day 0 is needed. Indeed, upon incubation with magnetic nanoparticles, a chase time of approximately three hours is performed. All cells are then seeded, and magnetism measurements are finally achieved (about one hour is needed for seeding of all cells and centrifugation of all Eppendorfs). A time delay of approximately four hours between initial nanoparticle incubation and measurement of Day 0 thus exists. Impact of this delay on magnetic signal was assessed by subjecting the cells to different chase times (0, 2, 3, or 4 h). At the end of this chase, cells were detached and pelleted in Eppendorf tubes, the magnetic signal of the cell pellet was then measured right at the end of the chase as well as 1, 2, 24, 48, and 72 h later (Figure S14, Supporting Information). First off, cells subjected to either 0 or 2 h of chase presented a decreased magnetic signal within the 2 h following cell pellet formation. No significant decrease after cell pellet formation was observed for the 3 or 4 h chase, on the contrary, magnetic signal significantly increased between the 1 h and the 72 h measurement. Interestingly, this increase led to values at 72 h above the initial ones (at 0 h). This indicates that a rapid degradation of the nanoparticles can occur over the chase and seeding time, meaning that the magnetic signal measured at Day 0 is below the very initial one right after magnetic labeling. The increase of cell magnetism observed within the first days upon spheroid formation thus corresponds to a bioproduction of magnetic nanoparticles from the iron ions released over this initial, very rapid, nanoparticle biodegradation.

The increase in magnetic values between Day 0 and Day 1–3 and its association with the bioproduction of magnetic nanoparticles was additionally confirmed by a standard magnetometry technique (VSM) and by high brilliance synchrotron X‐ray diffraction (XRD) performed at Spline BM25 beamline at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facilities (ESRF, Grenoble). Curves obtained by VSM at low magnetic field (between – 1500 and 1500 Oe) are typical of superparamagnetic materials thus fitting the description of magnetic nanoparticles (Figure 5A). An increase in magnetism from Day 0 to Day 3 was confirmed by this VSM approach as magnetization at saturation obtained between 15 000 and 30 000 Oe increased (Figure 5B). Figure 5C shows the diffraction results (XRD) for this series at 15 keV (λ = 0.8265 Å). The diffraction peaks of the cell samples at Day 0 and Day 1 fit with the peaks that correspond to magnetite/maghemite (Figure 5C,D) (see 220, 311, 400, 511 and 440 reflection maxima) along with crystals of fcc‐like physiological salts formed within the cells (marked with asterisks). It confirms that the maghemite crystallographic structure of the particles remains conserved at Day 1. Additionally, the diffraction pattern was analyzed by peak‐fitting Gaussian functions to study the difference in the degree of crystallinity between the two days. This process allowed the evaluation of the crystallinity index (CI), CI = Ac/Aa , where Ac​ represents the integrated area under the selected crystalline peaks, and Aa represents the integrated area of all diffraction peaks. Obtained values for 18.64 and 32.08 reflexion peaks (331 and 440 planes) shows a CI increasement of the 15% from Day 0 to Day 1, which confirms the bioproduction of magnetic nanoparticles made of maghemite/magnetite. Moreover, the crystalline size evolves from 8.78 nm (Day 0) to 8.85 nm (Day 1).

Final Investigation on the Role of Cell Density and Nanoparticles Concentration

To investigate possible factors influencing the biodegradation/bioproduction, we conducted experiments varying cell seeding numbers and magnetic nanoparticle labeling concentrations. In 2D cell cultures, a lower cell number per surface area means more space to colonize for the cells, ultimately leading to higher cell proliferation. Cells were seeded at two different densities: either 50 000 or 200 000 cells per well of a 6‐well plate. Cell number was determined at days 0, 3, 9, and 14 and shows that cells proliferate overtime for both seeding densities (Figure S15, Supporting Information). The number of cells per well remains significantly lower up to day 3 for the seeding at 50 000 cells/well. At Day 9, the number of cells becomes similar for both seedings and remains similar at Day 14. This coincides with a higher proliferation for the lower seeding (at 50 000 cells/well) than the higher one (200 000 cells/well). It results in a more diluted load of nanoparticle overtime for the seeding at 50 000 cells/well than for the 200 000 cells/well, which can be visualized by Perls staining (Figure S16, Supporting Information). This nanoparticle load is not homogeneously shared between daughter cells as, at day 0 all cells appear to have internalized iron (in the form of nanoparticles), and, later on, at days 3 and 21, some cells appear not having iron while others having a high iron load. Despite the differences in cell proliferation, thus in metabolic activity, evolution of the magnetic signal follows a similar trend for all conditions, independently of the nanoparticle labeling concentration in addition to the cell seeding density (Figure 6A–F). First, a decrease of the magnetic signal is detected between Day 0 and Day 1 (8.0% ± 2.9 decrease in average for all conditions) followed by an increase back from Day 1 to Day 3 or 4 (9.6% ± 2.6 increase) and again a decrease on the longer term (20.2% ± 1.1 decrease between Day 3 or 4 and Day 14). The amplitude of this decrease‐increase‐decrease varies independently of cell number and nanoparticle labeling concentration (Figure S17, Supporting Information).

For cell spheroids, a lower cell number means better access to nutrients (including oxygen) as diffusion of many molecules is limited when cells are located above 150–200 µm from a nutrient source.[ 41 ] Spheroids were formed from 50 000 or 200 000 hMSCs labeled with magnetic nanoparticles and pelleted together by centrifugation. At day 2, once cell‐cell adhesions have been formed, spheroids with 50 000 cells have a radius of 240 µm and those with 200 000 cells a radius of 420 µm. This means a reduced access to nutrients and oxygen at the spheroid core, more important for the 200 000‐cell spheroids. Cell numbers increase for both seeding densities and have reached a plateau after 7 days of culture (Figure S18, Supporting Information). The 50 000 cell seeding results in a fourfold increase in cell number, while the 200 000 cell seeding results in a twofold increase when plateauing. Under this 3D conformation, cell seeding number appears to influence nanoparticle biotransformation rates (Figure 7 ). Signal increase between Day 0 and Day 1–3 is highest for the 50 000‐cell spheroids when comparing between same incubation concentrations. Moreover, the bioproduction of magnetic nanoparticles tends to be higher when nanoparticle labeling concentration was lowest for the 50 000‐cell seeding (Figure 7D), a trend that is significant for the 200 000‐cell seeding (Figure 7H).

Discussion and conclusion

The superparamagnetic features of iron oxide nanoparticles enabled tracking of their integrity, where a loss of this magnetic signal indicates nanoparticle degradation. In the present study, we used a magnetic sensor initially developed by Nikitin et al.[ 42 ] for accurate monitoring of magnetic signal in live cells. This device exposes the superparamagnetic nanoparticles to a two‐frequency magnetic field, and the resulting nonlinear magnetization of the nanoparticles generates a response signal strictly proportional to the quantity of magnetic nanoparticles. This sensor has been exploited for applications ranging from electrical engineering to life science.[ 43 ] Initially developed for magnetic immunoassays involving antibodies or antigens conjugated to magnetic nanoparticles,[ 44 , 45 ] it has been commercialized under the registered trademark MIAtek, enabling monitoring of the synthesis and functionalization of magnetic nanoparticles.[ 46 , 47 ] More recently it has been employed for assessing magnetic nanoparticle internalization[ 48 , 49 , 50 ] and integrity[ 31 , 51 ] in the intracellular environment.

In this context, the magnetic sensor was first employed herein to reveal variability at initial nanoparticle labeling. It has already been demonstrated that the quantity of internalized nanoparticles varies depending on nanoparticle design[ 52 , 53 ] or cell type.[ 54 , 55 ] In the present study, using the same cell model as well as same nanoparticle design, concentration, and incubation time, nanoparticle uptake can significantly vary, with differences up to four‐fold in internalization value between experiments. One of the factors at play appears to be cell confluency at the time of labeling. These results emphasize the need of confirming the internalized dose of nanoparticle upon labeling, and not only the incubation concentration. This could be achievable on live cells, within minutes, prior to launching experiments, thanks to the magnetic sensor.

The biotransformations of magnetic nanoparticles were subsequently tracked in human stem cells cultured either in 2D (monolayer) or in 3D (spheroids) as spatial cell organization is known to influence cell metabolism. Deposition of ECM, secretion of growth factors, and gene expression profiles can indeed be modified by cell spatial configuration.[ 56 , 57 , 58 ] As nanoparticle biotransformations depend on the complex dynamics of the biological environment, it is anticipated that magnetic signal trends vary with spatial configuration. Remarkably, for both the 2D and 3D models, magnetism signal waves arise in the first days of cell culture. It recalls initial real‐time data obtained with the magnetic sensor that evidenced signal waves on the very short term (within hours) indicating nanoparticle handling by cells and a permanent balancing of the biological system between nanoparticle biodegradation and bioproduction.[ 31 ] As magnetic signal was here assessed daily for initial data points, to allow measurement of more than one sample at a time, waves at the scale of hours were not measured. However, at the scale of days, a same phenomenon could be observed. In cell monolayers (2D), a diminution of the magnetic signal (generally between Day 0 and Day 1) is first observed, suggesting a biodegradation of the nanoparticles. Subsequently, a signal resurgence occurs (between Day 1 and Day 4), indicative of nanoparticle bioproduction. In cell spheroids (3D), either the magnetic signal remains stable or an increase in magnetic signal is observed in the first days of culture (Day 0 to 1–4). This increase can here be explained by a previous, very rapid, dissolution of incubated magnetic nanoparticles during the labeling process.

On the longer‐term (Day 4 to 21), the increased spacing between data points may have obscured the detection of magnetic signal oscillations. However, what arises is that magnetic nanoparticles are progressively degraded when cells are cultured as monolayers. Interestingly, in cell spheroids this biodegradation is not observed. Rather, a steady magnetic signal is obtained either implying that nanoparticles remain intact or that a stable equilibrium between biodegradation and biosynthesis has been reached. These results emphasize that, by modifying cell‐cell interactions, and without the addition of any external chemical factor, the intracellular fate of magnetic nanoparticles is modulated. If combined with the supplementation of chemical factors, effect varies again. A strong degradation indeed occurs when cell spheroids are cultured in medium containing chemical factors known to drive their chondrogenic differentiation.[ 26 ] Additionally, the initial dose of nanoparticles internalized per cell seems to influence the extent of biotransformations observed in 3D. The ratio of biotransformations is more important when the load of nanoparticle per cell is lower. This has already been observed over the biodegradation of magnetic nanoparticles under chondrogenic differentiation of hMSC[ 30 ] and might be due to the cell's capacity to allocate more resources at the nanoparticle level.

Fine characterization techniques including STEM‐HAADF, HRTEM, SAED, XEDS, and synchrotron XRD confirmed that the biosynthesized nanoparticles consist of magnetite or maghemite, are located in the lysosomes, and have a size neighboring 8–9 nm in diameter, features in agreement with previous observations.[ 30 , 32 , 38 ] Moreover, immunostainings evidenced the presence of ferritin proteins that appear recruited in lysosomal compartments, where the nanoparticles are located. Ferritins are proteins dedicated to iron storage, via the incorporation of iron ions into its core, typically in the form of ferrihydrite (non‐magnetic).[ 59 ] However, ferritin cores can also be loaded with magnetic iron, in the form of magnetite/maghemite, forming magnetoferritin.[ 59 , 60 ] Interestingly, the ferritin core itself is ≈8 nm in diameter, which may explain the controlled size of the neo‐formed nanostructures, likely confined within. The synthesis of magnetoferritin has been successfully achieved in vitro,[ 60 ] and parameters leading to optimal loading with magnetite have been explored.[ 61 ] While its in vivo occurrence has long been suggested, precise mechanism behind its formation is not fully understood yet.[ 62 ] One possible explanation may involve the lysosomal environment, where the magnetic nanoparticles have been initially internalized in our study and are subjected to acidic pH (4.5–5.5) that facilitate enzymatic hydrolysis. Without protective measures such as specific coatings or strategies to promote lysosomal escape, the magnetic nanoparticles are degraded in this environment.[ 63 ] This degradation releases iron ions that are then concentrated and confined in these acidic lysosomes, providing singular ferritin loading conditions.

As previously mentioned, magnetic nanoparticles can be modified using ligands with high chemical affinity for the iron oxide surface or through polymer coatings. These added molecules can be reversibly linked to, or used to entrap, specific drugs or nucleic acids, enabling their use as controlled drug delivery systems.[ 64 ] However, once internalized, cells process the organic and inorganic components separately, particularly due to the specialized machinery dedicated to iron metabolism and regulation of its homeostasis.[ 65 ] As a result, the modified function of the nanoparticles may be lost over their biotransformation. Interestingly, if the nanostructures bioproduced in human cells are indeed magnetoferritins, those are already being explored for biomedical applications.[ 60 ] While magnetoferritins are currently produced chemically, the ability to bioproduce such nanostructures directly within human cells could offer significant advantages for diagnostics and therapy. Furthermore, molecular modification of biosynthesized magnetic nanoparticles has been achieved in magnetotactic bacteria, which naturally produce magnetic nanoparticles named magnetosomes.[ 66 ] These nanoparticles are enveloped by a phospholipid bilayer containing transmembrane proteins anchored to the iron surface, extending externally. These proteins can be genetically modified to express functional moieties, such as peptides or protein subunits, which can bind drugs or serve as targeting molecules. Increasing our understanding of magnetic nanoparticle biosynthesis in human cells could open to the in cellulo engineering of modified magnetic nanoparticles.

Overall, these results underscore that biotransformations depend on the complex dynamics of the biological environment. This bioactive system shows variability but demonstrates strong reproducibility within a given experiment, with consistent overall trends remaining across independent experiments.

Experimental Section

Citrate‐Coated Iron Oxide Nanoparticles

Iron oxide nanoparticles were synthesized by coprecipitation following the Massart procedure.[ 40 ] Briefly, a magnetic core (composed of magnetite) was obtained by coprecipitation of two iron ions, ferrous and ferric chlorides, in alkaline medium. The oxide obtained (Fe3O4) was oxidized to form maghemite (γ‐Fe2O3). The maghemite was diluted in water and citrate was added to stabilized it. Citrate bound to the iron oxide and created a complex, which thanks to the carboxylate groups (COO−), charged the surface negatively. Electrostatic repulsion obtained with the negative charges was enough to stabilize the iron oxide nanoparticles in water. Nanoparticles were analyzed and the characteristic mass magnetization obtained was 65 emu g−1 of iron with a superparamagnetic feature. The size follows a log‐normal law with a mean value of 8.4 nm and a polydispersity of 0.35. The magnetic moment of a single nanoparticle is ≈6.8 × 10−17 emu and its mass ≈10−18 g.

Cell Culture

Human immortalized mesenchymal stem cells (hTERT) (T0523, Applied Biological Materials (abm), Canada) were cultured in Prigrow II medium (abm) supplemented with 10% MSC qualified FBS (Fisher Scientific, France), 10−6 m hydrocortisone (H0888, Sigma‐Aldrich, France), and 1% penicillin streptomycin (Gibco, 15140‐122) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Cells were grown until passage 8 to 17 and at ≈80% confluence prior to seeding.

Cell Labeling and Culture after Labeling

Cells were labeled by incubation with iron nanoparticles at concentrations [Fe] = 0,5, or 1 or 2 mm in RPMI medium (Thermo Fischer Scientific) for 30 min. When specified, this medium was supplemented with trisodium citrate at a concentration of 5 mm for nanoparticle stabilization. At the end of the 30 min incubation with nanoparticles, cells were rinsed with RPMI and placed in DMEM with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin‐streptomycin for 3 h.

After nanoparticle labeling, cells were detached using Trypsin‐Ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (0.05%) phenol red (Gibco, 25300‐054) and cultured either as monolayers (2D) or as spheroids (3D). For 2D culture, cells were seeded in 6‐well plates or flasks. For spheroids, cells were resuspended in 400 µL of culture medium, placed in a 500 µL Eppendorf (Eppendorf Safe‐Lock Tubes, 500 µL, 00 301 21023), and centrifuged at 270 g for 5 min to obtain a cell pellet. After 24 h of incubation, spheroids become cohesive. For 2D and 3D culture, medium was replenished twice a week.

Cells were further cultured in DMEM with 10% of heat inactivated FBS (Gibco, 61965‐026) and 1% penicillin streptomycin (Gibco, 15140‐122) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. For the cell spheroids, Eppendorf were kept with the lid open in the incubator and placed in a box to preserve sterility.

Quantification of the Internalized Magnetic Iron by Vibrating Sample Magnetometry

Magnetic iron in fixed cell samples was measured by magnetometry using a PPMS device equipped with a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) option (Quantum Design). Field‐dependent magnetization curves were measured at 300 K as a function of the external field. A low range of −150–+150 mT was performed to obtain low field results. Then, another measure from 0 to 3000 mT allows to determine magnetization at saturation.

Quantification of the Internalized Magnetic Iron with the Magnetic Sensor

Magnetic iron in living cells samples was measured by magnetometry using bench magnetometer. This magnetometer emits two alternating magnetic fields, one at f1 = 100 kHz frequency and 10 Oe amplitude, and a second one at f2 = 100 Hz and 200 Oe. By performing the Fourier transform of the voltage of the sensing coil (modulated by both frequencies), the response of the sample at the combinatorial frequencies f = f1 ± 2 x f2 is measured. This response is proportional to the third derivative of the sample magnetization around zero field and room temperature and is directly proportional to the quantity of nanoparticles inside the sample. The magnetic signal was measured just after cells were detached with trypsin at each given day for 2D samples. For spheroids, culture was achieved in 500 µL Eppendorf tubes exactly fitting the holder of the magnetic sensor. Spheroids were thus inserted in the magnetometer for measurement at each given day then placed back in the culture incubator.

Alamar Blue and Cell Metabolism

Cell metabolic activity was quantified with the Alamar Blue assay. Alamar reagent was diluted to DMEM (10%) and 200 µL were added to cells incubated in 96‐wells plate. At the end, 50 µL of the medium of each well is transferred in a 96‐well plate and read by fluorescence with an Enspire Multimode Plate Reader (Perkin Elmer, Villebon‐sur‐Yvette, France) at 570 nm excitation wavelength and 585 nm emission wavelength. Results obtained are normalized by the metabolic activity of control cells.

Cell Count

2D cell number was measured using an automated cell counter (Countess 3FL, Invitrogen). Cells were detached then resuspended in DMEM growth medium and 10 µL were added in the automated cell counter cell. 3D cell number was measured using dsDNA quantification with QubitTM reagent (Q32853, Thermo Fischer Scientific). Cells were lysed using 400 µL of a lysis buffer (0,1 mm EDTA (pH 8,0), 0,5%(w/v) SDS, 10 mm Tris‐Cl (pH 8,0)) and then 10 µL of the solution was mixed with 190 µL of QubitTM dsDNA working solution. The fluorescent signal of samples that represents the DNA quantity was measured using Enspire Multimode Plate Reader (Perkin Elmer, Villebon‐sur‐Yvette, France) at 470 nm excitation wavelength and 520 nm emission wavelength. The quantity of dsDNA is proportional to the number of cells.

Prussian Blue Staining

Intracellular ferric ions were stained in blue thanks to the Prussian blue chemical reaction. Cells were first fixed in paraformaldehyde (PFA 4%, 30 min, room temperature). Iron was then stained with potassium hexacyanoferrate (II) trihydrate (Sigma) in acidic condition for 10 min. The nucleus was stained using Nuclear Fast Red solution for 15 min.

Immunofluorescence

The samples were fixed in 4% PFA (Sigma) in PBS for one night at 4 °C and washed three times with PBS, then the samples were permeabilized in 0.15% Triton X‐100 (Thermo‐Fisher) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature, before the blocking in 1% BSA(Sigma), 22.52 mg mL−1 glycine (Merck) and 0.15% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 1 h for the 2D samples and 2% BSA and 0.15% Triton X‐100 in PBS for 2 h for the spheroids. Without additional washing step, the samples were incubated with the primary antibodies (anti‐FerritinH #ab65080 and anti‐FerritinL #ab69090, Abcam) at the concentration of 1 µg mL−1 diluted in their blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C for the 2D and at room temperature for the spheroid. The samples were then washed three times in PBS for 5 min for the 2D and for 30 min for the spheroids. The secondary antibody anti‐Rabbit AlexaFluor594 (ab150080, Abcam) was incubated at the concentration of 3 µg mL−1 diluted in 2% BSA in PBS at room temperature in the dark for 1 h for the 2D samples and overnight for the spheroids. Then the samples were washed again in the same way and counterstained with DAPI at 500 nm in PBS for 5 min for 2D samples and 2 h for the spheroids followed by 3 washing in PBS. The samples were imaged with a Leica DMi8 inverted confocal microscope using a 40X water‐immersion objective or a 63X oil‐immersion.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

For TEM microscopy, samples fixed in 4% PFA for 1 h and rinsed in PBS were resuspended in 0,2 mm cacodylate buffer. Samples were fixed for a second time in 5% glutaraldehyde for 1 h and stored at 4 °C in 0,2 mm cacodylate buffer until use. Samples were post‐fixed using 1% osmium tetroxide and dehydrated progressively in 35% to 100% ethanol baths. Samples were then included in Spurr resin. Ultrathin slices (60 nm) were cut with an ultramicrotome (Powertome XL, RMC) and deposited on 150 mesh copper carbon grids (EMS, USA). Samples were observed with a JEOL 2100F operating at 200 kV, equipped with a Schottky emission gun, an ultra‐high‐resolution pole piece, and a Gatan US 4000 CCD camera. This microscope is also equipped with a JEOL XEDS spectrometer and a STEM device, which allows Z‐contrast imaging in high‐angle annular dark field (HAADF) mode.

Synchrotron X‐Ray Diffraction

For high brilliance XRD, cells were labeled with 2 mm magnetic nanoparticles. Upon the 3 h chase, spheroids of 700 000 cells were formed. Samples were fixed at Day 0 and Day 1 in 4% PFA, rinsed in PBS, and preserved at 4 °C in PBS. High brilliance XRD analyses were performed on spheroid for Day 0 and for Day 1 at 15 keV (λ = 0.8265 Å). Measurements were achieved at the Spanish CRG SpLine BM25 beamline of the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility ESRF) in Grenoble (France).

Statistical Analysis

All values are reported as means and standard error of the mean. Significant differences between two conditions were determined via a t‐test. Significant differences between more than two conditions were determined using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and a Tukey post hoc. * denotes a p‐value <0.05 (significant result), ** a p‐value <0.01 (very significant), and *** a p‐value <0.001 (highly significant).
