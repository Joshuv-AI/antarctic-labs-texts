Hybrid carbon matrices enable the suppression of polysulfide shuttle effect in Li–S batteries
Authors: Aizhan Kazymbetova, Akbota Kelgenbayeva, Ayaulym Belgibayeva, Arailym Nurpeissova, Aliya Mukanova, Zhumabay Bakenov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries offer high theoretical energy density but face critical challenges due to sulfur’s poor conductivity and the polysulfide shuttle effect. Here we report a novel cathode design utilizing a hybrid carbon matrix derived from buckwheat biomass and single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNT) to overcome these issues. The buckwheat-derived hard carbon (HC), obtained at 1000 °C, provides hierarchical porosity to anchor polysulfides and buffer sulfur expansion, while SWCNT (optimized at 6%) creates a conductive network. As a result, S@SP/HC/SWCNT cathode delivers an initial discharge capacity of ~ 1250 mAh g− 1 at 0.1 C and retains ~ 60% of this capacity after 100 cycles with ~ 100% Coulombic efficiency. Overall, this sustainable, low-cost, and scalable carbon–sulfur cathode architecture effectively addresses key Li–S challenges and demonstrates strong promise for practical high-energy, long-life Li–S batteries.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56662-8.Keywords: Lithium–sulfur batteries, Hard-carbon, Sulfur host, SWCNT, Electrochemical performanceSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Materials science

Introduction

Lithium–sulfur (Li–S) batteries have attracted intense interest as next-generation energy storage systems for electric vehicles and grid applications due to their exceptionally high theoretical energy density (≈ 2600 Wh kg− 1) - nearly three times higher than that of Li-ion batteries—and a sulfur cathode that is both low-cost and environmentally benign1–3. Sulfur is naturally abundant and inexpensive (~$0.02 g− 1), and sulfur-based systems emit roughly 31% fewer greenhouse gases than conventional alternatives4. Recent studies indicate that Li–S batteries are a focal technology, with recent literature establishing design goals of approximately ~ 450Whkg− 1 energy density and > 1000 stable cycles (in full cells) for automotive applications5. However, the application of Li–S batteries is plagued by a few challenges: (i) the shuttle effect caused by the dissolution and migration of lithium polysulfides (LiPS) (Li₂Sn, 4 ≤ n ≤ 8)6–8, (ii) the intrinsically poor conductivity of sulfur and its discharge products9, and (iii) the substantial ~ 80% volume expansion during cycling, which leads to electrode degradation and capacity fading10. Addressing these intertwined electrochemical and mechanical instabilities demands cathode architectures that combine electronic conductivity, chemical confinement, and structural resilience. The critical role of ordered pore channels and surface chemical functionality in polysulfide confinement has been compellingly demonstrated in recent studies: Zr-TCPP(Ni) MOF nanosheets with a BET surface area of 427.9 m2 g− 1 and pore size of ~ 14.1 Å exhibit selective Li⁺ sieving while blocking polysulfides11, similarly, HOF-PTPS-based separators leverage hierarchical pore structures and multiple hydrogen-bonding networks to achieve both rapid Li⁺ transport and strong polysulfide anchoring via Li–N coordination12. These findings reinforce that maximizing accessible internal surface area and chemical affinity for polysulfides are the key parameters governing confinement effectiveness.

Carbon materials have shown great promise in addressing the key challenges of Li–S batteries, as porous matrices can physically confine LiPS while providing electron-conducting frameworks13–15. In this regard, carbonaceous materials derived from biomass are gaining interest as hosts given their renewability, intrinsic micro- and mesoporosity, as well as tunable chemistry16,17. Here we introduce buckwheat (Fagopyrum esculentum) seeds as a new and previously unexplored precursor for sulfur cathodes. The lignocellulosic composition of buckwheat seeds and their decomposition behavior during pyrolysis yield a distinctive hard-carbon framework with hierarchical porosity and abundant defect sites—features favorable for anchoring polysulfides and accommodating sulfur volume expansion.

To fully use the capabilities of this unique carbon scaffold, we create a triphasic hybrid carbon matrix that combines buckwheat-derived HC, single-walled carbon nanotubes (SWCNTs), and Super P (SP). We systematically investigate how SWCNT concentration and HC synthesis temperature influence the architecture, sulfur distribution, polysulfide adsorption capability, and resulting electrochemical behavior. Each component of the composite plays a distinct yet synergetic role. The hard carbon framework serves as a porous and chemically active backbone, providing abundant confinement sites for polysulfide anchoring and to accommodate the substantial volumetric sulfur expansion during cycling. Interwoven within the matrix, SWCNTs establish highly conductive one-dimensional pathways that speed up electron transport and strengthen the electrode structure to reduce polarization and keep it stable during cycling18–21. In parallel, Super P connects particles, makes the current distribution more even, and improves electronic percolation within the composite22,23.

This study establishes buckwheat biomass as a sustainable and high-performance carbon precursor for Li–S cathodes and demonstrates a scalable strategy to engineer conductive and chemically interactive carbon matrices that address the intrinsic limitations of sulfur electrochemistry. The insights gained here provide a generalizable framework for the rational design of biomass-derived hybrid carbons for next-generation Li–S batteries.

Experimental section

Preparation of bio-waste-derived hard carbon and sulfur composites

The synthesis of HC from buckwheat seeds, a bio-waste source, follows the procedure outlined in our previous work (Mukanova et al.24. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the process involves annealing the buckwheat seeds at various temperatures. Importantly, the synthesis protocol incorporates a pre-oxidation step prior to carbonization, a procedure established in our previous work on buckwheat-derived hard carbon24. As demonstrated therein, the pre-oxidation step promotes the redistribution and interconnection of internal pores, breaking down compact lignocellulosic structures and generating an accessible pore network, which is advantageous for sulfur host applications, and maximizes internal surface area.

Schematic illustration of the synthesis procedure for hybrid sulfur cathode composites based on buckwheat-derived hard carbon (HC), Super P (SP), and SWCNTs.

Buckwheat seeds (Almaty region, Kazakhstan) were first purified by immersion in 2 M HCl solution overnight (~ 12 h) at room temperature to remove mineral impurities and ash, then washed repeatedly with deionized water until neutral pH was confirmed by pH paper, and subsequently dried at 80 °C under vacuum for 24 h. The pre-oxidized seeds were then carbonized at 600–1200 °C for 2 h under continuous N2 flow with a heating rate of 4 °C min− 1, followed by natural cooling to room temperature under N2 protection, to yield hard carbon samples denoted HC-600, HC-800, HC-1000, and HC-1200, respectively.

The sulfur-carbon composite was prepared via a two-stage melt diffusion method. First SWCNT (Tuball, Luxembourg) were pre-dispersed by adding the required ratio of isopropanol (IPA) and sonicated with ultrasonic processor for 10–15 min to achieve a homogeneous nanotube suspension and minimize agglomeration prior to composite preparation (morphology before and after ultrasonic confirmed by SEM, Figure S2). In parallel, sulfur powder (99%, Sigma-Aldrich), with a fixed mass ratio corresponding to 60 wt% of composite, was stirred with carbon disulfide and isopropanol (CS2:IPA = 7:3, v: v) solution for 10 min at room temperature. Subsequently, Super P carbon-black (SP, morphology confirmed by SEM, Figure S1), bio-derived HC (buckwheat), and SWCNTs were mixed in various weight ratios ranging from 60:19:20:1% to 60:11:20:9% (S: SP: HC: SWCNT, wt%) using sonication for one hour at 80 °C. The resulting solution was then cast into a ceramic crucible and the solvent was evaporated at 60 °C for 12 h in a dry oven under gentle agitation to yield a dry powder precursor. The resulting composite was annealed at 155 °C for 12 h (5 °C min− 1) in Ar atmosphere, followed by a secondary annealing at 200 °C for 2 h to eliminate non-encapsulated sulfur residues and ensure effective sulfur confinement within the carbon matrix25. The sulfur-carbon composites were denoted as S@SP/HC/SWCNT-x%, where x corresponds to the content of SWCNTs and equals 1%, 3%, 6%, or 9%.

Materials characterization

The morphology and microstructure of the obtained composites were examined by field-emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM, Crossbeam540, Zeiss, Germany) in high-vacuum mode with an acceleration voltage of 5 kV. The elemental distribution of the materials was analyzed by an energy dispersive spectrometer (Ultim Extreme EDS detector (Oxford’s instrument)).

The structural properties of the samples were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD, Miniflex, Rigaku powder diffractometer, Japan) with a monochromatic Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 0.15406 nm). XRD patterns were collected in the range of 10 < 2θ < 90° with a step size of 2θ = 0.05° and a counting time of 12 s per step.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was performed using a Thermal Analyzer (STA) 6000 under nitrogen flow at a heating rate of 10 °C min− 1 from room temperature to 600 °C to determine the sulfur content in the composite materials.

Polysulfide adsorption capability was evaluated using UV-visible spectroscopy, using UV-1900i UV-vis Spectrophotometer. A 0.002 M Li2S6 solution was prepared in DOL: DME (v/v = 1:1), and 3 mg of each host composite (HC-600/SP/SWCNT-6%, HC-800/SP/SWCNT-6%, HC-1000/SP/SWCNT-6%, and HC-1200/SP/SWCNT-6%) was immersed in 5mL of solution for 12 h at room temperature.

The specific surface area and pore structure of the buckwheat derived hard carbon samples (HC-600 to HC-1200) were characterized by N2 adsorption-desorption measurements (BET analysis). Full BET characterization data, including surface area values, pore volume for all HC samples are reported in our prior publication Mukanova et al.24, which forms the structural foundation of the present study.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were carried out on a Thermo Scientific Nexsa system equipped with a monochromated Al Kα source (1486.6 eV), a 180° double-focusing hemispherical analyzer with a 128‑channel detector, and a flood gun for charge compensation. High-resolution spectra were acquired using selectable spot sizes (10–400 μm) under standard operating conditions.

Preparation of sulfur cathode

Prepared sulfur composites were combined with acetylene black (AB) as the conductive agent and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) as the binder in a mass ratio of 8:1:1 to prepare the cathode. The mixture, along with an appropriate amount of N-methylpyrrolidone (NMP) as a dispersant, was placed in an agate tank. Homogenization was performed using a Thinky centrifugal mixer (Planetary Centrifugal Mixer ARV-310) at 1200 rpm for 15 min, repeated three times, to achieve a uniform and stable slurry with well-dispersed active materials and additives. The resultant slurry was evenly coated onto a carbon-coated aluminum foil current collector using a doctor blade and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h. The prepared electrode was subsequently compressed using a roll press machine, achieving a reduction to 60% of the initial thickness. The dried cathode material was then cut into circular discs with a diameter of 14 mm using a precision disc cutting machine. The sulfur loading was controlled at ~ 2.5 ± 0.1 mg cm− 2 to produce usable electrodes.

Cell assembly and electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical measurements were conducted using a coin-type cell (CR2032) assembled in an Ar-filled glove box (MBraun, O2 < 0.1 ppm, H2O < 0.1 ppm). The assembly included lithium metal foil (99.95% purity, 0.6 mm thick, 16 mm diameter), a polypropylene separator (Celgard 2400), and the complete cathode, all stacked sequentially. The electrolyte used was 1 M lithium bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide (LiTFSI) dissolved in a mixture of 1,3-dioxolane (DOL) and dimethoxyethane (DME) in a volume ratio of 1:1 with 1 wt% LiNO3 additive. Each cell contained 30 µL of electrolyte (E/S ≈ 20 µL mg− 1). The cycling performance, charge-discharge profile, and rate capacity of the Li–S batteries were tested on the Neware battery testing system (BTS4000) within the potential range of 1.6–2.8 V vs. Li/Li+. The current density was varied between 0.1 and 2 C (1 C = 1675 mA g− 1), and the test temperature was controlled at 25 °C. Differential capacity (dQ/dV) profiles were derived from the galvanostatic charge-discharge data at selected cycle numbers (1st, 10th, 25th, 50th, and 100th) to monitor the evolution of sulfur redox reaction kinetics and peak positions as a function of cycling, providing direct insight into polarization growth and capacity fade mechanisms across the different HC synthesis temperatures.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed using the Biologic potentiostat/galvanostat after the 3rd galvanostatic cycle at open circuit potential (OCP). The impedance response was fitted to an equivalent circuit model of the form Rb(RSEICPESEI)(RctCPEelectrode)Zw (Figure S6), where Rs represents a bulk ohmic resistance, a high-frequency parallel element representing the SEI resistance (RSEI) and its associated non-ideal capacitance (CPESEI), a mid-frequency parallel element representing the charge transfer resistance (Rct) and double-layer capacitance at the electrode–electrolyte interface (CPEelectrode), and Zw describes the solid-state diffusion of Li-ion and polysulfide species within the electrode at low frequencies. All EIS measurements were conducted at room temperature.

Results and discussion

Structural evolution of the S@SP/HC/SWCNT composites

SEM analysis (Fig. 2a) reveals a clear evolution of cathode architecture as the SWCNT content varies from 1% to 9%. At low loading (1–3%) the nanotubes are sparsely distributed and fail to form effective interparticle bridges, leaving the carbon domains largely disconnected. In contrast, the composite with 6% SWCNT produces a uniformly interconnected architecture in which nanotubes weave through the Super P/HC framework, reinforcing mechanical cohesion while preserving pore accessibility. However, at 9% loading, the excessive nanotube content induces pronounced agglomeration, producing dense clusters that partially obstruct the pore network. This obstruction is detrimental, as it reduces sulfur-accessible surface area and dilutes the active material fraction.

Morphology and elemental distribution of S@SP/HC-600/SWCNT composites with varying SWCNT contents: (a) high-magnification SEM images; (b) low-magnification SEM images; (c) EDS mapping of S@SP/HC-600 composites.

In the low-magnification SEM pictures (Fig. 2b) further highlight the importance of maintaining an optimal SWCNT content. The 6% composite displays a coherent carbon matrix, whereas 1–3% SWCNT shows an open but weakly connected framework. Nanotube bundling breaks up the porosity and continuity of the host matrix at 9%. EDS elemental mapping (Fig. 2c) supports structural patterns further. The 6% composite has the most even distribution, with carbon (magenta) and sulfur (green) signals well mixed throughout the matrix. The 1% and 3% samples, on the other hand, show patchy sulfur dispersion, while 9% composite localized sulfur accumulation due to nanotube agglomeration.

The influence of carbonization temperature on porosity and sulfur accommodation is further illustrated in the SEM/EDS maps of S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% composites (Fig. 3). The SEM pictures of the pristine hard carbons (Fig. 3a) indicate a clear evolution of pore structure with carbonization temperature. HC-600 has a shape that is uneven and partially collapsed, while HC-800 grows a more open and connected porous network. At higher temperatures, the framework gets coarser, with enlarged voids and surface roughness, reflecting progressive graphitization and pore coalescence. As reported by Mukanova et al.24, where HC-1000 exhibits the largest surface area (69.46 m² g⁻¹), directly correlate with its sulfur infiltration and distribution.

Morphology and elemental distribution of buckwheat-derived hard carbon and S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% composites: (a) SEM images of derived HC; (b) SEM and (c) EDS mapping of S@SP/HC composites synthesized at different temperatures with 6% SWCNT.

Indeed, the SEM images of the sulfur composites (Fig. 3b) highlight how these structural variations govern sulfur embedding. Across the HC-600 - HC-1200 series, sulfur distribution transitions clearly from surface-dominated deposition to deep pore-level infiltration as carbon microstructure evolves. In HC-600, the severely collapsed and poorly connected pores prevent melt-diffused sulfur from entering the internal framework, resulting in sulfur accumulating almost exclusively on the external surface - prone to polysulfide dissolution. HC-800 and HC-1000 display progressively more accessible and interconnected porosity, enabling sulfur to penetrate the internal pore network and distribute uniformly throughout the carbon matrix, as evidenced by consistent C–S overlap in EDS maps. In contrast, HC-1200, though graphitized and structurally smooth, exhibits reduced internal porosity; sulfur forms a thin, uniform coating along external surfaces rather than permeating the diminished pore volume. Overall, sulfur resides primarily on the surface in HC-600 and HC-1200, while it is internally confined within the pore network of HC-800 and HC-1000.

The XRD patterns of the sulfur composite (S@SP/HC/SWCNT), pure sulfur, SWCNTs, and HC are shown in Fig. 4a. The XRD pattern of hard carbon exhibits a broad peak around 20°–30°, indicative of its amorphous nature, while the SWCNTs display a broad peak near 25° corresponding to the graphitic structure. The XRD pattern of pure sulfur shows intense, sharp peaks characteristic of orthorhombic sulfur in the 2θ range of 15°–60°26. In contrast, the XRD pattern of the S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% composite reveals significantly reduced peak intensities associated with crystalline sulfur, along with broadening of the remaining peaks. This reduction and broadening of sulfur peaks confirm the successful encapsulation of sulfur within the carbon matrix during the melt diffusion process, leading to a reduction in sulfur crystallinity and a more uniform sulfur distribution within the composite. This encapsulation behavior is corroborated by TGA results (Fig. 4b), which quantify sulfur loading and reflect the influence of carbonization temperature on composite architecture. All S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% composites exhibited a pronounced mass loss between ~ 200–300 °C, corresponding to sulfur evaporation. Notably, composites derived from HC synthesized at 1000 and 1200 °C retained the sulfur content ~ 60wt%, in contrast to ~ 55wt% for HC-600 and HC-800 °C counterparts. These findings advocate previously that higher carbonization temperature promotes pore development and graphitic ordering, enabling greater sulfur infiltration.

(a) XRD of the prepared S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% composite; (b) TGA curves of S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% composites synthesized at different carbonization temperatures (600 °C, 800 °C, 1000 °C, 1200 °C); (c) UV-vis absorption spectra of Li2S6 solution before and after adsorption by S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% composites synthesized at different carbonization temperatures (600–1200 °C); (d) photographs of Li2S6 solutions after 12 h adsorption.

To further investigate chemical interactions between the host materials and soluble LiPS species Li2S6 adsorption tests were conducted (Fig. 4c, d). Pure Li2S6 solution exhibits a strong absorption band at ~ 410 nm, characteristic of species27. After 12 h of exposure to the hybrid carbon hosts, the solutions treated with HC-1000- and HC-1200-based composites show dramatic attenuation of this peak, indicating highly effective polysulfide adsorption. HC-600 and HC-800 composites retain slightly higher absorbance intensities, reflecting weaker chemical affinity for LiPS. These results directly correlate with visual inspection (Fig. 4d): Li2S6 solutions treated with HC-1000 composite are almost colorless, whereas others retain a pronounced yellow-brown color. Together, these findings establish that HC carbonized at 1000 °C provides the optimal balance of pore structure and chemical functionality required for dual physical–chemical regulation of polysulfides in Li–S batteries.

Polysulfide adsorption and surface chemistry

To further investigate the origin of the different LiPS adsorption capabilities of HC-T samples, XPS analyses were performed on HC-800 and HC-1000 after Li2S6 adsorption and subsequent drying treatment. HC-800 and HC-1000 were specifically selected for in-depth XPS comparison because the UV-vis adsorption data (Fig. 4c, d) revealed that the transition from weak to strong LiPS uptake occurs precisely between these two carbonization temperatures, while HC-600 (also weak) and HC-1200 (also strong) cluster with HC-800 and HC-1000 respectively. Thus, the HC-800/HC-1000 pair provides the most informative spectroscopic contrast for elucidating the chemical origin of the LiPS-affinity transition across the HC-T series; high-resolution C 1s, Li 1s, O 1s, and S 2p spectra for HC-800 are provided in Figure S3, while the corresponding spectra for HC-1000 are presented in Fig. 5b–e. The survey spectra (Fig. 5a) confirmed the presence of C, O, S, and Li species on both samples, indicating the retention of LiPS-derived species after adsorption. Quantitative analysis derived from XPS survey spectra (Table S1) showed that HC-1000 retained significantly higher amounts of Li- and S-containing species than HC-800. Specifically, the Li content increased from 0.11 at% for HC-800 to 0.51 at% for HC-1000, while the sulfur content increased from 0.06 to 0.16 at%, confirming the stronger LiPS adsorption capability of HC-1000.

XPS characterization of HC-1000 after Li2S6 adsorption and drying treatment: (a) survey spectra of HC-800 and HC-1000; (b) C 1s spectrum; (c) S 2p spectrum; (d) Li 1s spectrum; and (e) O 1s spectrum.

The high-resolution C 1s spectrum of HC-1000 (Fig. 5b) was deconvoluted into four components centered at 284.74, 285.95, 287.04, and 290.95 eV. The dominant component at 284.74 eV corresponds to sp2-hybridized graphitic carbon (C = C/C–C). The peak at 285.95 eV is assigned to carbon singly bonded to oxygen (C–O/C–OH) and to sp3 defective carbon, both of which are common in biomass-derived hard carbons. The component at 287.04 eV is attributed to ether/epoxide-type C–O–C and carbonyl C = O environments, while the high-binding-energy feature at 290.95 eV corresponds to the π–π* shake-up satellite of the aromatic sp2 network28.

The S 2p spectrum (Fig. 5c) consisted of two sulfur doublets. The low-binding-energy doublet at 164.47/165.57 eV (spin-orbit splitting ≈ 1.1 eV, typical of S 2p3/2–S 2p1/2) is assigned to host-bound polysulfide species. The positive shift relative to free Li2Sₓ (terminal S− 1 at ~ 161.6 eV and bridging S0 at ~ 163.1 eV) is characteristic of LiPSs chemically anchored to a polar host surface. The high-binding-energy doublet at 168.72/170.02 eV corresponds to oxidized sulfur species (thiosulfate-/polythionate-like S–O at ~ 167–168 eV convoluted with sulfate-like SO₄2− at ~ 169–170 eV). Such oxidized species are widely interpreted as fingerprints of chemical conversion of LiPSs at the host surface, providing direct spectroscopic evidence that HC-1000 not only physically retains but also chemically activates adsorbed polysulfides29.

The Li 1s spectrum (Fig. 5d) exhibited a characteristic peak centered at approximately 56.5 eV, confirming the presence of retained Li-containing species after adsorption and drying treatment. In addition, HC-1000 showed a significantly stronger Li signal than HC-800, further demonstrating its superior LiPS adsorption capability. The O 1s spectrum (Fig. 5e) was deconvoluted into two components at 532.77 and 534.22 eV, assigned to C–O/C–OH functionalities and to adsorbed oxygen-containing species (chemisorbed water/hydroxyls and oxidized S–O environments overlapping in this region), respectively. It should be noted that, because the XPS measurement was performed after Li2S6 adsorption and drying, the elevated total oxygen content of HC-1000 (2.62 at%) compared with HC-800 (0.78 at%) reflects a convolution of two contributions: (i) intrinsic oxygen-containing surface groups of the carbon framework, and (ii) oxygen brought in by the chemisorbed LiPS-derived species themselves (S–O fingerprints observed in S 2p above, together with residual solvent-derived oxygen). Within this combined picture, the parallel enrichment in Li (0.51 vs. 0.11 at%), S (0.16 vs. 0.06 at%) and O on HC-1000 is internally consistent with stronger overall host–LiPS interaction at the HC-1000 surface, rather than indicating a purely electrolytic adsorbate layer30.

Overall, the XPS results demonstrate that HC-1000 possesses stronger LiPS affinity than HC-800. The enhanced adsorption behavior is attributed to the synergistic effects of oxygen-containing surface functionalities, improved carbon framework, and optimized porous structure, which collectively promote sulfur confinement.

Electrochemical performance of S@SP/HC/SWCNT composite

SWCNTs play a crucial role in improving electrical conductivity and relieving polarization of the sulfur cathode. This effect is clearly demonstrated by the electrochemical performance of the S@SP/HC/SWCNT cathodes, as shown in Fig. 6. The galvanostatic charge/discharge profiles of the S@SP/HC/SWCNT cells (Fig. 6a) reveal that the S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% cathode achieved an initial capacity of 1144 mAh g− 1 at 0.1 C, which is significantly higher compared to the S@SP/HC/SWCNT-1% cathode (759.5 mAh g− 1). This significant increase demonstrates the effect of SWCNT in raising the cathode’s conductivity, which promotes the utilization of sulfur and raises the initial capacity. Excessive SWCNT loading (9%) lowers capacity by promoting nanotube agglomeration and diluting the active sulfur content, whereas 6% provides the optimal balance of conductivity and porosity. The reduction in polarization, as shown in the Fig. 6b demonstrates that the 6% SWCNT cell exhibits the lowest polarization (ΔV = 0.1367), compared to 0.1683 V, 0.1573 and 0.1451 V for 1%, 3% and 9% cells, respectively. The observed decrease in polarization implies that the addition of SWCNTs enhances the electrical conductivity of the cathode, thereby facilitating the sulfur redox processes. In particular, the final transformation of Li2S2 into Li2S, typically hindered by poor electron transport and a high interfacial barrier31, proceeds more efficiently when a continuous conductive network is established. As demonstrated by the cycling performance data (Fig. 6c), the S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% cell performs better than the other samples, retaining a discharge capacity of about 600 mAh g⁻¹ after 100 cycles at 0.1 C. This is significantly more than the capacities that the S@SP/HC/SWCNT-1%, S@SP/HC/SWCNT-3%, and S@SP/HC/SWCNT-9% cells were able to retain. Furthermore, during the cycling period, all of the S@SP/HC/SWCNT cells show an average Coulombic efficiency of almost 100%, showing exceptional electrochemical performance.

(a) The potential profiles of the cathodes with different content of SWCNT; (b) charge-discharge profiles at the 100th cycle with calculated polarization; (c) cycling performance of the cells at 0.1 C; (d) the potential profiles of the cathode’s samples initial capacity cathode of cells with differently synthesized HCs (synthesized at various temperatures); (e) voltage profile during 100th cycle, including calculated polarization; (f) cycling performance of the cells at 0.1 C.

SWCNT insertion into the sulfur cathode composite has shown to be very advantageous; the best concentration for balancing conductivity, sulfur usage, and long-term stability is 6% SWCNT content. This design enables decreased polarization, increased sulfur reactivity, and increased capacity retention — all of which are essential for development of Li–S batteries toward practical applications.

After optimization of SWCNT content comparing different mass ratios of SWCNT, we proceeded to investigate the effect of the HC synthesis temperature (600–1200 °C) on the performance of the S-based cathode various bio-derived HC syntheses at different temperatures, all combined with optimized SWCNT content of 6%. This subsequent comparison aimed to identify the optimal synthesis conditions for the bio-derived hard carbons to further enhance the electrochemical performance of the composites. The highest initial capacity (1259.89 mAh g− 1) is obtained for S@SP/HC-1000/SWCNT, consistent with the initial discharge profiles in Fig. 6d. Under identical testing conditions, the initial capacities decrease to 1161.6 mAh g⁻¹ for S@SP/HC-600/SWCNT and 1134.49 mAh g⁻¹ for S@SP/HC-1200/SWCNT, while S@SP/HC-800/SWCNT delivers the lowest value describing 941.2 mAh g− 1, suggesting comparatively less efficient sulfur utilization in the HC-800-based electrode. The difference in sulfur utilization was further supported by XPS analyses after Li2S6 adsorption (Fig. 5 and Table S1). HC-1000 retained significantly higher amounts of Li- and S-containing species than HC-800, with Li and S contents increasing from 0.11 to 0.51 at% and from 0.06 to 0.16 at%, respectively. Figure 6e provides additional information about sulfur utilization efficiency, notably, the S@SP/HC-800/SWCNT electrode displayed the largest polarization (ΔV = 0.1413), indicating slow kinetics and high resistance. Meanwhile, the S@SP/HC-1000/SWCNT and the S@SP/HC-1200/SWCNT demonstrated reduced polarization values of 0.1387 and 0.1386, respectively, suggesting better charge transfer dynamics and more effective sulfur redox reactions. The enhanced redox kinetics of HC-1000 are consistent with its stronger LiPS adsorption behavior revealed by XPS. The higher Li and sulfur retention, together with the presence of oxygen-containing surface functionalities, suggest more efficient suppression of polysulfide migration and improved interfacial sulfur conversion. In terms of cycling stability (Fig. 6f), the electrodes show distinct capacity-retention trends as a function of HC synthesis temperature. Among the series, S@SP/HC-800/SWCNT delivers the highest retention, maintaining 61.73% of its capacity with a remaining capacity of 581.1 mAh g− 1 after 100 cycles. In contrast, the S@SP/HC-1000/SWCNT electrode, despite the highest initial capacity, retains ~ 60% of its capacity (746.8 mAh g− 1) after 100 cycles. Yet, because of its exceptionally high starting capacity and strong sulfur accommodation, the electrode achieves the highest absolute retained capacity of all samples, making it the most effective host. S@SP/HC-1200/SWCNT also exhibits high retention (61.0%, 696.8 mAh g⁻¹ after 100 cycles), indicating stable cycling performance despite a lower initial capacity (1134.49 mAh g− 1) than HC-1000. At first sight, the apparently lower percentage retention of HC-1000 (59%) compared with HC-800 (62%) and HC-1200 (61%) may seem inconsistent with the strongest LiPS affinity revealed by UV-vis and XPS for HC-1000. However, percent retention is normalized against the initial capacity and is therefore biased toward samples that activate less sulfur initially: HC-800 starts at only 941 mAh g⁻¹ and thus has less active sulfur available for irreversible loss in absolute terms. When evaluated on the energetically relevant basis — absolute retained capacity — HC-1000 outperforms all other samples (746.8 mAh g⁻¹ after 100 cycles vs. 581.1, 696.8 and 524.9 mAh g⁻¹ for HC-800, HC-1200 and HC-600, respectively). The combination of the highest absolute residual capacity and the strongest LiPS-binding signatures (Sect. 3.2) therefore identifies HC-1000 as the optimal sulfur host of the series. In contrast, S@SP/HC-600/SWCNT shows the fastest capacity decay, retaining only 45.18% (524.9 mAh g⁻¹); this trend is consistent with its lower sulfur content (~ 55 wt%) and weaker polysulfide adsorption/host development, as evidenced by the corresponding characterization results.

To establish a meaningful baseline for evaluating the contribution of each component in the hybrid matrix, the electrochemical performance of two control cathodes — S@SP (Super P only, no HC, no SWCNT) and S@SP/HC (Super P + HC, no SWCNT) — was first evaluated and is presented in Figures S4 and S5, respectively. The S@SP cathode (Super P only) delivered ~ 650 mAh g⁻¹ with < 50% capacity retention after 100 cycles — reflecting the absence of polysulfide confinement and a structured conductive network. Adding HC (S@SP/HC) raised the initial capacity to ~ 850 mAh g− 1 through improved porosity and adsorption, yet retention remained ~ 55% with elevated polarization, confirming HC alone cannot provide continuous long-range electron transport. The optimized S@SP/HC-1000/SWCNT-6% cathode surpassed both controls, delivering 1259.89 mAh g− 1 initial capacity and retaining 746.8 mAh g− 1 after 100 cycles with ~ 100% Coulombic efficiency — demonstrating that SP, HC, and SWCNT each play an indispensable and synergistic role in the hybrid architecture.

The dQ/dV profiles (Fig. 7a-d) of the cathodes show characteristic multi-step sulfur redox reaction and its dependence on carbonization temperature. In all cases, discharge features in the ~ 2.2–2.3 V are attributed to the reduction of S8 to soluble long-chain polysulfides, whereas the lower-voltage reduction ~ 2.0–2.15 V corresponds to further conversion of polysulfides to insoluble Li2S2/Li2S; the charge peaks between ~ 2.2 and 2.45 V reflect the reverse oxidation pathway32. HC-1000 delivers the most consistent peak positions and shapes from the 10th to the 100th cycle, implying comparatively stable kinetics and limited polarization growth. This behavior also agrees well with the XPS results obtained after Li2S6 adsorption. The enhanced retention of Li- and S-containing species on HC-1000 suggests improved polysulfide confinement, which helps maintain stable sulfur conversion pathways and suppresses polarization growth during prolonged cycling.

The Nyquist plots of S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% with different HC-carbonization temperatures after 3rd cycle (Fig. 8a) reveal that the Rb values are low for HC-800, HC-1000, and HC-1200 (~ 8–13 Ω), while HC-600 shows a substantially elevated Rb (~ 18 Ω) reflecting incomplete carbonization and imposing a fixed ohmic penalty that directly drives its rapid capacity decay. The mid-frequency Rct follows the order HC-800 (~ 15 Ω) > HC-1000 (~ 13 Ω) > HC-1200 (~ 11 Ω) > HC-600 (~ 5 Ω); for HC-800 and HC-1000, the two RC elements produce overlapping semicircles that appear as a single elongated arc in the Nyquist plot, consistent with their closely similar time constants at the applied frequency range. Despite its lowest Rct, HC-1200 is limited by a near-vertical Warburg tail indicating severe mass transport limitation from pore collapse at 1200 °C The HC-1000 cathode simultaneously achieves the optimal balance of all three impedance component: low Rb (~ 8 Ω), moderate Rct (~ 13 Ω), and a gradual diffusion, reflecting the optimal combination of bulk conductivity, surface charge transfer, and open hierarchical porosity (BET: 69.46 m2 g− 1)24— a triple balance that explains its highest initial capacity (1259.89 mAh g− 1) and largest absolute retained capacity (746.8 mAh g− 1 after 100 cycles). In addition, XPS analyses demonstrated stronger LiPS adsorption capability for HC-1000, evidenced by the higher Li and S contents after adsorption treatment (Table S1). The improved sulfur confinement likely contributes to the reduced interfacial resistance and enhanced electrochemical kinetics.

The S@SP/HC-1000/SWCNT-6% cathode displays strong recovery and stability at lower C-rates, as shown by the rate capability data (Fig. 8b), suggesting that the material can tolerate quicker charge/discharge cycles without suffering appreciable deterioration. This is crucial for practical applications where both energy density and rate capability are essential.

Figure 9 shows SEM images of the S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% cathode after 100 galvanostatic cycles. Compared to its pristine state, the cycled electrode exhibits visible but not critical morphological changes. These features are characteristic of polysulfide migration and re-precipitation, a well-known degradation pathway in Li–S systems. Despite these changes, the electrode retains a generally cohesive and porous structure, suggesting that the hybrid carbon matrix partially mitigates structural failure over prolonged cycling.

SEM images of S@SP/HC-1000/SWCNT-6% cathode after 100 cycles: (a) electrode surface before and after and (b) cross-section before and after.

While conventional S-based cathodes tend to undergo significant and cumulative volume expansion during cycling, our porous S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% cathode demonstrates exceptional structural stability, with only about 20% expansion observed after 100 cycles according to cross-sectional SEM analysis. Our cathode avoids catastrophic fracture, which is a strong indicator that the hierarchical, flexible hybrid matrix plays a buffering role. The presence of well-dispersed, nanoscale SWCNT bundles also facilitates the redistribution of mechanical stress, reducing the formation of microcracks that would otherwise accelerate capacity fading. Together, the hierarchically porous HC and the percolating SWCNT scaffold provide dual mechanical and electrochemical stabilization, effectively suppressing typical degradation pathways in Li–S cathodes. Thus, the post-cycling observations align with the electrochemical behavior reported earlier: the S@SP/HC/SWCNT-6% composite not only enhances sulfur utilization and redox kinetics but also maintains structural robustness throughout repeated cycling. These results highlight the importance of integrated mechanical–electrochemical design for achieving durable high-capacity Li–S cathodes.

The obtained experimental results, together with the comparative literature data, confirm the strong advantage of utilizing buckwheat seeds as a precursor for hard carbon synthesis and demonstrate its superior electrochemical behavior as a sulfur host in Li–S batteries.

Within this context, buckwheat-derived hard carbon stands out by delivering consistently high specific capacities over a broad temperature range. This performance is indicative of a unique carbon microstructure that promotes efficient sulfur confinement, enhanced redox kinetics, and improved sulfur utilization, thereby confirming the suitability of buckwheat seeds as an advantageous precursor for high-performance sulfur host materials in Li–S batteries.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduce a sustainable hybrid carbon matrix composed of buckwheat-derived HC, Super P, and a SWCNT network as an effective sulfur host for Li–S batteries. Through systematic variation of SWCNT loading and HC synthesis temperature, we identify the structural and chemical parameters that govern sulfur utilization, polysulfide confinement, and long-term electrochemical stability. An optimal SWCNT content of 6% demonstrated superior electrochemical performance, achieving an initial capacity of 1144 mAh g− 1 at 0.1 C, with reduced polarization. The initial increase of SWCNT content was promising, however the agglomeration effects led to diminishing effects, suggesting the controlled SWCNT loading. Further investigations into bio-derived HC synthesized at various temperatures highlighted the critical role of synthesis conditions in determining the electrochemical properties of the cathode materials. XPS analysis after Li2S6 adsorption further confirmed the enhanced polysulfide affinity of HC-1000, revealing higher retention of Li- and S-containing species compared to HC-800, which supports its improved sulfur confinement capability. Among the tested synthesis temperatures, HC synthesized at 1000 °C exhibited the highest initial capacity (1259.89 mAh g− 1) and maintained a competitive final capacity after 100 cycles (746.8 mAh g− 1) at 0.1 C, with nearly 100% Coulombic efficiency. Post-cycling analysis confirms that the hierarchical HC scaffold and SWCNT reinforcement together suppress structural collapse and limit volume expansion to ~ 20%, contributing to the stability of the composite.

Importantly, the use of buckwheat-derived HC and a modest 6% SWCNT content provides not only electrochemical benefits but also a commercially viable solution. The HC/SWCNT composite delivers low material cost, and is compatible with scalable, low-cost synthesis methods, which renders the S@SP/HC-1000/SWCNT cathode a promising candidate for practical Li–S battery applications.
