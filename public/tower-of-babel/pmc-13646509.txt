A20 Inhibits IFN‐γ Signaling in Hepatocellular Carcinoma Cells by Binding to STAT1, Compromising Anti‐Tumor Immunity
Authors: Yanlei Dong, Jing Yi, Ruoxuan Yang, Yingyue Wang, Zelong Ma, Hong Yuan, Meichen Song, Ying Xiao, Chun Guo, Jianing Wang, Xiaoyan Wang, Faliang Zhu, Lining Zhang, Yuchen Fan, Yongyu Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImmune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has demonstrated efficacy in clinical cancer therapy; however, a significant proportion of cancer patients do not respond to ICB. One contributing factor to this lack of response is the impairment of IFN‐γ signaling within cancer cells. A20 acts as a negative regulator of IFN‐γ signaling. Our study aimed to investigate the mechanism by which A20 exerts its negative regulatory effect on IFN‐γ signaling in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) cells. Bioinformatic analysis revealed that A20 expression in HCC cell lines is inversely associated with the activation of IFN‐γ signaling. Functional loss and gain experiments demonstrated that A20 inhibits IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells. The interaction between A20 and STAT1 was assessed using Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) assays and bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BioFC) assays. Our results also indicated that A20 suppresses the expression of downstream molecules involved in IFN‐γ signaling, such as CXCL9, CXCL10, and HLA‐ABC, in HCC cells. Additionally, enforced expression of A20 in HCC cells inhibited anti‐tumor immunity in a mouse model of HCC. Bioinformatic analysis suggests that A20 may negatively regulate IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells from human HCC cohorts. Collectively, our study uncovers a novel mechanism whereby A20 inhibits IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells through direct binding with STAT1. These findings enhance our understanding of the regulatory mechanisms of IFN‐γ signaling in cancer cells and provide insights for designing strategies to enhance the effectiveness of ICB therapy.Keywords: A20, hepatocellular carcinomas, IFN‐γ signaling, TNFAIP3

Introduction

ICB therapy is designed to block inhibitory signals of T cell activation to promote anti‐tumor immune responses. Over the past decade, the success of ICB that targets CTLA‐4, PD‐1, or its ligand PD‐L1 has transformed cancer care and substantially improved the survival outcomes of patients with cancer. However, therapeutic responses to ICB at the individual level are highly variable, and durable long‐term disease control is only achieved in a fraction of patients [1]. For example, ICB monotherapy in HCC showed an objective response rate (ORR) of 15%–20%, and the most patients showed resistance to the therapy [2]. To broaden the clinical utility of ICB, it is important to understand the key factors limiting their action. Therefore, understanding mechanisms of resistance is an area of considerable research interest.

Resistance to ICB therapy in tumors can be attributed to the dysregulation of IFN‐γ signaling within tumor cells.

IFN‐γ signaling is a cascade of tyrosine phosphorylation events triggered by the binding of IFN‐γ to the IFN‐γ receptor (IFNGR), which results in the initiation of interferon‐stimulated gene (ISG) transcription. Upon binding of IFN‐γ to IFNGR1 and IFNGR2, JAK1/2 activation occurs at the cytoplasmic domain of the receptors. Activated JAK1/2 subsequently triggers the phosphorylation of STAT1, resulting in the formation of a dimer composed of two phosphorylated STAT1 molecules. This phosphorylated dimer translocates into the nucleus, where it binds to the interferon gamma‐activated sequence (GAS) motifs within the promoters of specific genes, thereby facilitating their transcription [3, 4].

Gene mutations in the IFN‐γ signaling pathway have been linked to resistance to ICB therapy. Loss of essential genes of this pathway, like IFNGR1, IRF1, JAK2, and IFNGR2, as well as the amplification of important inhibitors of this pathway such as SOCS1 and PIAS4, contributes to around 75% of melanoma patients who do not respond to CTLA‐4 blockade [5]. Additionally, JAK1 and JAK2 gene mutations correlate to both primary and acquired resistance to PD‐1 blockade in melanoma [6, 7]. These findings suggest that tumors in non‐responder patients often carry genomic defects that compromise IFN‐γ signaling, potentially leading to primary resistance to ICB therapy.

Experimental evidence further supports the impact of disrupted IFN‐γ signaling on tumor cell resistance to ICB therapy. For example, knockdown of the IFNGR1 gene in B16 melanoma cells results in resistance to CTLA‐4 blockade therapy [5]. Similarly, melanoma cells with deficiencies in STAT1, JAK1, or IFNGR1 exhibit resistance to PD1 blockade therapy [8]. Therefore, the effectiveness of immunotherapy is intricately linked to the intact IFN‐γ signaling within tumor cells. Understanding the regulatory mechanisms governing IFN‐γ signaling is crucial for overcoming resistance to ICB therapy.

A20, also known as TNFAIP3, is a regulator of the IFN‐γ signaling pathway. It functions as a ubiquitin‐editing enzyme that plays a significant role in modulating various signaling pathways in different cell types [9]. Structurally, the A20 molecule consists of an ovarian tumor (OTU) domain followed by seven ZnF (zinc finger) domains. The OTU domain is responsible for the deubiquitinating activity of A20, while the ZnF4 domain is involved in the ubiquitin ligase activity of A20. The ZnF7 domain contributes to the non‐enzymatic functions of A20, enabling it to bind to proteins modified with M1 ubiquitin chains. A20 utilizes these domains to modulate key molecules in various signaling pathways [10]. A20 inhibits IFN‐γ signaling in various cell types, including dendritic cells (DC) [11], astrocytes [12], macrophages [13], endothelial cells [14], smooth muscle cells [14], hematopoietic stem cells (HSC) [15], as well as tumor cells such as lung cancer cells [16]. However, the mechanism behind this inhibition remains unclear.

In the present study, we ask how A20 suppresses IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells. Our findings reveal that A20 binds to STAT1, leading to the inhibition of IFN‐γ signaling by STAT1 in HCC cells. The inhibitory effect of A20 on IFN‐γ signaling compromises anti‐tumor immunity in a mouse model of HCC. Understanding how A20 modulates IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells may provide insights for improvement of ICB therapy.

Material and Methods

Cell Culture

The HCC cell line Hep3B was cultured in Minimum Essential Medium, while Huh7 and SNU387, and the human embryonic kidney cell line HEK293T, along with the murine hepatocellular carcinoma cell line Hepa1‐6, were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle's Medium. All cell cultures were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and maintained at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere.

Lentivirus Infection

Huh7 cells with dual knockout of A20 and STAT1 genes were prepared in the study. Huh7 cells were initially transfected with an A20 knockout lentivirus prepared in our previous research [17]. Following the successful selection of A20 knockout Huh7 cells, these clones were further transfected with a STAT1 gene knockout lentivirus (Lv‐STAT1‐sgRNA) obtained from Genechem, Shanghai, China. After obtaining the double‐knockout clones, we validated them using Western blotting to confirm the successful knockout of both genes. In parallel, we engineered Hepa1‐6 cells to overexpress A20. This was achieved by transfecting the cells with a lentivirus (lv‐A20) designed for A20 overexpression, also obtained from Genechem, Shanghai, China. After the selection process using puromycin, a cell line with stable A20 overexpression was established. For comparison, we also generated control cell lines using a control lentivirus, following the same procedure as the experimental groups.

Plasmids and siRNAs

The plasmid pRK5‐A20 was created by inserting human A20 cDNA into plasmid pRK5. The plasmid pRK5‐Flag‐A20 was generated by incorporating the human A20 gene and Flag tag sequence into plasmid pRK5. Additionally, the plasmid pMyc‐STAT1 was generously provided by Dr. Hui Zheng from Soochow University, Suzhou, China. Plasmid pBiFC‐VN173‐A20 was crafted by inserting the human A20 gene into plasmid pBiFC‐VN173, while plasmid pBiFC‐VC155‐STAT1 was created by inserting the human STAT1 gene into pBiFC‐VC155.

In our previous study, we engineered the plasmid PRK5‐OTU mutant, where a cysteine at position C103 in the OTU domain was replaced with an arginine. This mutation disrupts the deubiquitinase (DUB) activity of the OTU domain within the A20 protein [18]. The plasmid pCMV‐ZnF4 mutant (Y614A/F615A) and pCMV‐ZnF7 mutant (F770A/G771A) were sourced from the Miaoling Biology company. The Y614A/F615A mutation impairs the function of the ZnF4 domain [19], while the F770A/G771A mutation has been shown to impair polyubiquitin binding capabilities [20].

The siRNA targeting the A20 gene was purchased from the BioSune Biotechnology company. For transfection, the plasmids were introduced into HCC cells using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher, USA), while the siRNAs were delivered into HCC cells using Interferin (Polyplus‐transfection, France) following the manufacturer's instructions. Subsequently, the cells were collected for quantitative RT‐PCR assay, Western blot assay, or flow cytometry Analysis 6 or 24 h after transfection. Details of the siRNAs used can be found in Table S1.

Bioinformatic Analysis

RNA‐seq data from 25 HCC cell lines was downloaded from the CCLE website (https://sites.broadinstitute.org/ccle/). The cell lines were divided into A20 high‐expression and A20 low‐expression groups based on the median A20 gene expression value. The gene expression matrix of the HCC cell lines was then subjected to Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) using R software (v4.4.1) with the GSVA package. IFN‐γ signaling gene sets were obtained from the GSEA website (https://www.gsea‐msigdb.org) to serve as reference gene sets. The results of the analysis were visualized as a heatmap using the pheatmap package in R.

We conducted differential gene expression analysis comparing the A20 high‐expression group to the A20 low‐expression group using the DESeq2 package in R. This analysis provided the log fold change (logFC) values for each gene, reflecting the differences in expression between the two groups. We then utilized these logFC values for Pre‐Rank analysis in the Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) software.

Single‐cell RNA‐sequencing data from HCC patients were obtained from GEO (GSE149614) (www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo). After quality control and normalization, cells were clustered using PCA‐based dimensional reduction and Louvain clustering, followed by UMAP visualization. Cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes and original dataset annotations, identifying seven major populations, including hepatocytes, malignant HCC cells, T/NK cells, B cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and myeloid cells.

Malignant HCC cells were extracted for A20‐associated analysis. A20‐expressing malignant cells were subjected to scTenifoldKnk‐based in silico perturbation analysis to identify potential downstream targets. Functional enrichment analysis of perturbed genes was performed using enrichR against Gene Ontology Biological Process, KEGG Human, MSigDB Hallmark, WikiPathway Human, and Reactome databases. Spearman correlation analysis and GSEA were subsequently performed to evaluate the association between A20 expression and interferon‐γ signaling activity.

Co‐Immunoprecipitation (Co‐IP)

Samples were collected in IP lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors, and the lysates were clarified by centrifugation. The target protein was immunoprecipitated overnight at 4°C with the respective antibody and Protein A/G PLUS Agarose beads. Following centrifugation to collect the beads, the bound fractions were eluted using SDS‐PAGE sample buffer and subjected to analysis on an SDS‐PAGE gel. Subsequently, an immunoblot was conducted using the relevant antibodies.

Bimolecular Fluorescence Complementation (BioFC) Assays

For BiFC analysis, plasmids pBiFC‐A20‐VN173 and pBiFC‐STAT1‐VC155 were constructed by subcloning the A20 cDNAs and STAT1 cDNA into pBiFC‐VN173 and pBiFC‐VC155, respectively. HEK293T cells were co‐transfected with these two plasmids for 24 h, followed by fixation in 4% PFA for 15 min. Nuclei were then stained with DAPI for 10 min. Subsequently, images were captured under oil immersion using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Andor & Leica, Dragonfly 200, UK) and analyzed utilizing the Imaris‐Viewer software package.

Western Blot Analysis

To prepare total cell lysates, samples were collected and lysed in ice‐cold RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors for 30 min. Protein lysates were quantified using BCA methods, followed by separation on an SDS‐PAGE gel and transfer to a PVDF membrane. Subsequently, the membrane was blocked with 5% BSA for 90 min at room temperature, and the proteins of interest were probed with their respective primary and secondary antibodies. Details of the antibodies used can be found in Table S2.

Quantitative RT‐PCR

In brief, total RNA from cell lines was extracted using the RN07‐EASYspin RNA isolation kit (Aidlab Biotechnologies) and subsequently converted into cDNAs using HiScript II Q RT Super Mix (Vazyme Biotech, China). Quantitative PCR was then conducted using 2 × Phanta Flash Master Mix (Dye Plus) (Vazyme Biotech, China) along with gene‐specific primers. The β‐actin gene was employed as an internal control. The relative expression of the target genes to the internal control gene was determined using the formula: Relative expression = 2−ΔCT, where ΔCT = CTtarget gene − CTinternal control [21, 22]. The primers of the genes were presented in Table S3.

Subcutaneous Mouse Tumor Model

Subcutaneous tumor models were established by inoculating 4 × 106 control Hepa1‐6 cells or A20‐overexpressing Hepa1‐6 cells into the inguinal region of WT mice (n = 7 / group) or NOD‐SCID mice (n = 5 / group). Tumor size was measured every two days, and tumor tissues were harvested on day 18 or day 22 for tumor weight determination. The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of shandong university.

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissue sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, followed by antigen retrieval in citrate buffer for 3 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide, and nonspecific binding was blocked with normal goat serum. For the assessment of A20 expression and CD8+ T cell infiltration, consecutive sections were incubated with primary antibodies against A20 and CD8A, respectively, overnight at 4°C. After washing, the sections were incubated with horseradish peroxidase–conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 30 min. Signals were visualized using 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine. The intensity of A20 staining was quantified using ImageJ software, which allowed for the objective assessment of A20 expression levels.

Flow Cytometry

Cells were harvested and stained with anti‐HLA‐ABC antibody coupled to fluorescein (Table S2) and then analyzed by flow cytometry. The data were analyzed using the FlowJo Software.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism software. The data are presented as mean ± S.D. Variances between groups were assessed for statistical significance using a two‐tailed Student's t‐test. p‐values equal to or less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Results

A20 Expression Is Inversely Associated With IFN‐ γ Signaling in HCC Cells

It remains unclear whether the regulation of IFN‐γ signaling by A20 is a widespread phenomenon in HCC cells, despite reports indicating that A20 regulates this signaling pathway in immune and certain epithelial cells. To investigate this relationship, we analyzed the enrichment of IFN‐γ signaling gene sets in both the A20 low‐expression and A20 high‐expression groups of 25 HCC cell lines using RNA‐seq data from the CCLE website. Both GSVA and GSEA revealed that gene sets Brown_interferon_responsive_genes, Reactome_interferon_gamma_signaling, Zhang_interferon_response, Einav_interferon_signature_in_cancer, and Sana_response_to IFNG_up were enriched in the A20 low‐expression group while the gene set Chiang_liver_cancer_subclass_interferon_dn was enriched in the A20 high‐expression group (Figure 1a,b). These findings suggest that A20 may play a role in regulating IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells.

GSEA and GSVA analysis of IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cell lines based on A20 expression. GSVA (a) and GSEA (b) were performed on 25 hepatocellular carcinoma cell lines from the CCLE database, revealing differential enrichment of IFN‐γ signaling–related gene sets between A20 high and A20 low expression cell lines.

A20 Inhibits IFN‐ γ Signaling Depending on Its ZnF7 Domain in HCC Cells

Next, we conducted loss‐ and gain‐of‐function experiments to assess the regulation of A20 on IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells. We found that transient silencing of A20 expression using siRNA facilitated STAT1 activation in Hep3B cells stimulated by IFN‐γ (Figure 2a). Conversely, overexpression of A20 through plasmid transduction suppressed STAT1 activation in Huh‐7 cells stimulated by IFN‐γ (Figure 2b). This result indicated that A20 inhibited IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells.

Representative blots are shown on the left, and densitometric quantification of p‐STAT1 (Tyr701) normalized to β‐actin is shown on the right. Previous studies have shown that A20 inhibits STAT1 expression, thereby inhibiting IFN‐γ signaling [12]. To eliminate the confounding effects of endogenous A20 and STAT1, we generated Huh‐7 cells with knockout mutations in both the A20 and STAT1 genes using the lentiviral CRISPR system (Figure 2c). Subsequently, we introduced recombinant plasmids for A20 and STAT1 overexpression into these knockout cells. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that, following IFN‐γ stimulation, exogenous A20 inhibited the nuclear translocation of exogenous STAT1 (Figure 2d). This result indicated that A20 inhibited IFN‐γ signaling independent of inhibition of STAT1 expression.

We further investigated the domain responsible for A20's inhibition of IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells. Co‐transduction of plasmids containing wild‐type A20, OTU mutant, ZnF4 mutant, or ZnF7 mutant along with the STAT1 overexpression plasmid into Huh‐7 cells lacking endogenous A20 and STAT1 genes revealed that only the ZnF7 mutant failed to inhibit STAT1 phosphorylation induced by IFN‐γ (Figure 2e). This result indicated that A20 inhibited IFN‐γ signaling through its ZnF7 domain. To further confirm this conclusion, we performed co‐IP assays. In the IFN‐γ signaling pathway, upon engagement of the IFN‐γ receptor, STAT1 binds to the receptor. We introduced exogenous wild‐type A20 or the ZnF7 mutant into Huh‐7 cells lacking both A20 and STAT1 genes via transfection with the corresponding recombinant plasmids. The co‐IP result showed that exogenous wild‐type A20 disrupted the binding of STAT1 to IFNGR2, while the A20 mutant with the ZnF7 mutation did not (Figure 2f). These findings further support the conclusion that A20 inhibits IFN‐γ signaling through its ZnF7 domain.

A20 Binds to STAT1 Through Its ZnF7 Domain in HCC Cells

ZnF7 in A20 usually mediates the non‐enzymatic function of A20 when A20 regulates its substrates. It mediated binding of A20 to the M1‐ub chain conjugated to its substrates. A previous study showed that STAT1 has an M1‐ub chain, and we also confirmed that in HCC cells (Supplemental Figure S1). Therefore, we asked whether A20 binds to STAT1 through its ZnF7 domain. First, we performed a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) assay to assess whether A20 and STAT1 proteins interact with each other. HEK293T cells were transfected with both pBiFC‐A20‐VN173 and pBiFC‐STAT1‐VC155 plasmids, and the results demonstrated a physical association between A20 and STAT1 (Figure 3a). To further support the interaction between A20 and STAT1, we performed co‐immunoprecipitation assays. In the exogenous co‐IP experiment, HEK293T cells were transfected with pRK5‐Flag‐A20 and pMyc‐STAT1 plasmids. Following precipitation of the fusion protein Myc‐STAT1 with the anti‐Myc antibody, the fusion protein Flag‐A20 was detected in the Myc‐STAT1 and anti‐Myc complex, indicating an interaction between A20 and STAT1 (Figure 3b). Subsequent endogenous co‐IP experiments in Huh7 and SNU387 cells further validated this interaction. Here, the STAT1 protein was found in the A20 protein complex precipitated by the anti‐A20 antibody, confirming the binding between A20 and STAT1 (Figure 3c and Figure 3d). To investigate whether the ZnF7 domain is involved in the interaction between A20 and STAT1, we performed additional co‐immunoprecipitation assays in Huh‐7 cells with dual knockout of A20 and STAT1. The results showed that the A20 mutant harboring a ZnF7 domain mutation failed to interact with STAT1 (Figure 3e).

The positive control is the HEK293T cells transfected with pBiFC‐bFosVC155 and pBiFC‐bJunVN173 for 24 h. Yellow represents colocalization, and blue represents nuclei. Scale bars, 20 μm. (b) The exogenous co‐IP assay showed that the fusion protein Flag‐A20 bound to the fusion protein Myc‐STAT1 in NEK293T cells transfected with plasmid pMyc‐STAT1 and/or pRK5‐Flag‐A20. The endogenous co‐IP assay showed that A20 bound to STAT1 in Huh7 cells (c) or SNU387 cells (d). (e) The co‐IP assays were performed in the Huh7 cells with double knockout of A20 and STAT1 genes. The mutant A20 with the ZnF7 mutation did not co‐precipitate with fusion protein Myc‐STAT1, while the wild‐type A20 did.

Taken together, both A20's binding to STAT1 and its inhibition of IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells depend on the ZnF7 domain. Therefore, A20 inhibits IFN‐γ signaling by directly binding to STAT1 through its ZnF7 domain in HCC cells.

A20 Suppression of IFN‐γ Signaling in HCC Cells Undermines Anti‐Tumor Immunity in Mouse Models of HCC

Given that CXCL9, CXCL10, and HLA‐ABC are IFN‐γ–inducible genes and associated with anti‐tumor immunity mediated by CD8 T cells [1, 3]. We examined their expression in hepatocellular carcinoma cells with A20 overexpression or knockdown to evaluate the impact of A20 on IFN‐γ signaling. A20 was overexpressed in Huh7 cells through transfection with a recombinant lentivirus vector containing the A20 gene, while it was silenced via siRNA transduction in Hep3B cells. We found that A20 overexpression suppressed the IFN‐γ‐induced expression of CXCL9 (Figure 4a), CXCL10 (Figure 4b), and HLA‐ABC (Figure 4c). Conversely, silencing A20 expression facilitated the IFN‐γ‐induced expression of CXCL9 (Figure 4d), CXCL10 (Figure 4e) and HLA‐ABC (Figure 4f). These results demonstrated that A20 suppressed the expression of chemokines and MHC I molecules associated with anti‐tumor immunity in HCC cells.

A20 suppresses the expression of CXCL9, CXCL10, and MHC I molecules in HCC cells. (a–c) A20 overexpression inhibited the expression of CXCL9 (a), CXCL10 (b), and HLA‐ABC (c) in Huh7 cells stimulated with IFN‐γ (50 ng/mL, 6 h or 24 h). (d–f). The silenced expression of A20 enhanced the expression of CXCL9 (d), CXCL10 (e), and HLA‐ABC (f) in Hep3B cells stimulated with IFN‐γ (50 ng/mL, 6 h or 24 h). *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

To further explore the implications of A20 inhibition of IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells, we conducted experiments using mouse models (Figure 5a). First, control Hepa1‐6 cells and Hepa1‐6 cells overexpressing A20 were generated. A20 overexpression was confirmed by Western blot (Figure 5b). Subsequently, we injected Hepa1‐6 cells, both control and A20‐overexpressing, subcutaneously into SCID mice and wild‐type (WT) mice. We found that tumors derived from A20‐overexpressing Hepa1‐6 cells grew faster than those from the control group in WT mice (Figure 5c and Figure 5d), while this phenomenon was abrogated in SCID mice (Figure 5f and Figure 5g). Similar results were observed regarding tumor weights at the endpoint of the experiment (Figure 5e and Figure 5h), indicating that A20 overexpression in Hepa1‐6 cells inhibits anti‐tumor immunity in WT mice.

Furthermore, CD3 and CD8 T cell infiltration was significantly reduced in the A20‐overexpressing group compared to the control group in WT mice (Figure 5i), and there was no T cell infiltration in NOD‐SCID mice (Figure 5j). Correspondingly, the chemokines CXCL9 and CXCL10, which are crucial for CD8 T cell recruitment, were downregulated in the tumor tissues of the A20‐overexpressing group (Figure 5k–n). These findings suggest that A20 overexpression in HCC cells compromises CD8 T cell‐mediated anti‐tumor immunity.

Inverse Correlation of A20 Expression With Interferon‐γ Signaling and CD8+ T Cell Infiltration in Human HCC

To enhance the clinical relevance of our study, we conducted an additional bioinformatic analysis using single‐cell RNA‐sequencing data (GSE149614) from HCC patients. Post quality control, we identified 7169 malignant HCC cells from a pool of 69,471 cells. These malignant cells were distinguished based on HCC‐associated markers and were selected for further examination (Figure 6a). Out of these malignant cells, 2659 exhibited detectable A20 expression and were subjected to an in silico A20 gene knockout analysis using scTenifoldKnk. This analysis identified 69 significantly perturbed genes (Figure 6b, Table S4), with enrichment analysis indicating a significant activation of the MSigDB Hallmark interferon‐γ response pathway (Figure 6c, Table S5). This finding suggests a notable association between A20 expression and interferon‐γ signaling. Further analysis revealed four interferon‐γ response‐related genes—TAP1, PSME2, SOD1, and CDKN1A—among the perturbed genes. A negative correlation was observed between A20 expression and these genes (Figure 6d). This observation was further corroborated by preranked GSEA analysis, which demonstrated a preferential enrichment of interferon‐γ signaling‐related gene sets in malignant HCC cells with low A20 expression (Figure 6e). Collectively, our results suggest an inverse correlation between A20 expression and interferon‐γ signaling in human HCC cells.

We also investigated the association between A20 expression and IFN‐γ signaling in the TCGA‐LIHC cohort by employing a methylation‐based method (Supplemental Figure S2). The signals of A20 expression also encompass those emanating from immune cells, as determined by timer analysis [23] (Supplemental Figure S2a). The cases with key gene deletions on interferon‐γ signaling were excluded (Supplementary Figure S2b). Considering that A20 methylation is generally observed in cancer cells rather than in immune or stromal cells, and that it exhibits a correlation with A20 expression (Supplementary Figure S2d), we categorized the HCC cases based on the methylation status of the A20 gene. The fully methylated A20 group exhibited significant enrichment of interferon‐γ signaling gene sets (Table S6), increased CD3E expression (Supplementary Figure S2e), and a trend towards higher infiltration of CD8+ T cells as deconvoluted by CIBERSORT [24] (Table S7), compared to the unmethylated A20 group (Supplementary Figure S2f). These results imply that A20 expression in HCC cells may inversely correlate with interferon‐γ signaling and CD8+ T cell infiltration in human HCC tissues.

Additionally, we corroborated the correlation between A20 expression and the infiltration of CD8+ T cells in human HCC specimens using IHC assays on serial sections of tumor tissues. Immunohistochemical analysis of 50 HCC specimens revealed that the regions from 26 cases exhibited high A20 expression, while the regions from 24 cases showed low A20 expression (Figures 6f,g). Moreover, regions of HCC tissues with high A20 expression displayed reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration compared to regions with low A20 expression (Figures 6f,h).

Taken together, these findings from single‐cell analysis, public cohorts, and clinical specimens suggested that A20 may negatively regulate interferon‐γ signaling in human HCC cells and the CD8+ T‐cell infiltration in human HCC tissues.

Discussion

IFN‐γ plays a crucial role in anti‐tumor immunity and ICB therapy. Understanding the regulatory mechanisms of IFN‐γ signaling in tumor cells is essential. In this study, we investigate how A20 inhibits IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells. Our findings reveal that A20 can directly interact with STAT1 through its ZnF7 domain, resulting in the suppression of STAT1 activation.

We have identified STAT1 as a novel substrate protein for A20. A20, a crucial signaling regulatory protein, interacts with various substrates to modulate multiple cellular signaling pathways and biological processes. For instance, A20 binds to NEMO to regulate the NF‐κB signaling pathway [25]. Moreover, A20 binds to RIPK1 to protect cells from necroptosis [26]. In hepatocytes, A20 directly interacts with and deubiquitinates ASK1 to ameliorate nonalcoholic steatohepatitis [27]. Additionally, A20 inhibits glycolysis in HCC by interacting with PFKL and promoting its degradation [28]. In endometrial cancer cells, A20 forms a complex with ERα to enhance estrogen sensitivity [29]. It also interacts with ATG16L1 to regulate the intestinal epithelial barrier stability [30]. Furthermore, A20 binds to mTORC1 or mTORC2 to restrict their activity [17, 31]. Our discovery of this new substrate adds to the diverse range of A20's substrates.

A20's direct inhibition of STAT1 activation represents a novel mechanism for its inhibitory role in IFN‐γ signaling. Previous research has shown that A20 inhibits STAT1 expression, thereby suppressing IFN‐γ signaling. In astrocytes, A20 suppresses the transcriptional expression of STAT1 in an NF‐κB‐dependent manner, leading to the inhibition of IFN‐γ signaling by reducing STAT1 expression [12]. In smooth muscle cells, A20 inhibits TBK1 activation, which suppresses basal IFN‐β signaling critical for STAT1 expression. Consequently, A20 attenuates IFN‐γ signaling by inhibiting STAT1 expression in a non‐NF‐κB‐dependent manner [14]. In our current study, we have for the first time revealed that the direct interaction between A20 and STAT1 suppresses IFN‐γ signaling independently of inhibiting STAT1 expression. Therefore, A20 utilizes both STAT1 expression‐dependent and STAT1 expression‐independent modes to inhibit IFN‐γ signaling.

Our discovery of the novel mechanism by which A20 inhibits IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells offers valuable insights for developing therapeutic strategies to address resistance to ICB therapy. For instance, the development of small molecule inhibitors could target the interaction between A20 and STAT1, thereby releasing A20's inhibitory effect and enhancing IFN‐γ signaling activity. Given the critical role of IFN‐γ signaling in cancer cells for ICB therapy [32, 33], combining ICB therapy with these small molecule inhibitors may have the potential to enhance the efficacy of ICB therapy.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the in vivo findings were not validated in a humanized mouse model, which could better replicate the immune microenvironment of human HCC. The use of the Hepa1‐6 cell model, known for its “hot” immunophenotype, may not accurately represent the typically “cold” nature of human HCCs. While A20 overexpression in Hepa1‐6 cells led to less “hot” tumors, this model may not fully mirror human HCCs. Therefore, the interpretation of the results should be approached with caution. Secondly, the current clinical data were insufficient to establish an independent association between A20 expression and patient prognosis. Moreover, we failed to systematically compare A20 levels with other molecular alterations within the IFN‐γ pathway in clinical samples. Further studies with larger human HCC cohorts are required to validate these clinical associations.

In summary, our study uncovered a novel mechanism through which A20 inhibits IFN‐γ signaling in HCC cells. A20 binds directly to STAT1 through the ZnF7 domain of A20, leading to the inhibition of STAT1 activation and suppression of anti‐tumor immunity (Figure 7). This discovery offers valuable insights into the regulation of IFN‐γ signaling in cancer cells and provides a potential avenue for developing drugs to boost IFN‐γ signaling in cancer cells, thereby overcoming resistance to ICB therapy in cancer treatment.
