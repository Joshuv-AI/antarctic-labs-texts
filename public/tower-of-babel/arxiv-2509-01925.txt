Time-like Janus Solution
– holographic global quantum quench –

Abstract
We construct a time-like Janus solution, which is mediated by a time-dependent dilaton field in asymptotic AdS spacetime. This solution breaks the null energy condition, but we argue that it is nevertheless useful as a toy model of holographic global quantum quench. The dual CFT is given by conformal perturbation theory, where the primary scalar operator that is dual to the bulk dilaton field is coupled with a global-quench-type time-dependent source. We compute one-point functions of the scalar operator and the stress-energy tensor, and confirm that the results are consistent with the proposed CFT picture. We also evaluate the holographic entanglement entropy for late time after the global quench, and show that the result agrees with the CFT computation. The stability of the time-like Janus solution against a scalar perturbation is also discussed.

Introduction

The AdS/CFT correspondence
[

It provides an explicit and promising approach to quantum gravity by rewriting it in terms of a microscopic and non-gravitational theory.
It can also provide a novel approach for studying the strong-coupling limit of a large class of quantum field theories, thanks to its strong-weak duality nature.

The Janus solution

which has a geometry of AdS

The asymptotic value of the dilaton field approaches a constant in one half of the boundary space and a different value in the other half.
Hence, this solution provides one of the simplest examples of the AdS/ICFT correspondence.
This solution is used to compute the holographic boundary/interface entropy
[

,

,
holographic one-point functions in the AdS/BCFT correspondence
[

,
and to construct a traversable wormhole
[

.
With its vast application of the Janus solution, it is interesting to consider another type of Janus deformation.

The previously studied Janus solution is a space-like solution, in the sense that the dilaton field has a spatial dependence.
In this paper, we construct a time-like Janus solution whose dilaton field has a time-dependence and no spatial dependence.
A time-like Janus solution was previously considered in
[
16
]
, but their solution contains a naked singularity.
In order to avoid this naked singularity, we perform an analytical continuation of the Janus deformation parameter to a pure imaginary value.
This leads to a breaking of the null energy condition.
We nevertheless argue that our solution is useful as a toy model of holographic global quantum quench in the context of the AdS/ICFT correspondence.

2
Time-like Janus Solutions

In this section, we study the three-dimensional solution and postpone our discussion for higher dimension in section
7
.
As in the original Janus solution
[
8
]
, we study the Einstein-dilaton theory in three-dimensional AdS spacetime:

\displaystyle I\,=\,\frac{1}{16\pi G_{N}}\int d^{3}x\sqrt{-g}\Big[R\,-\,g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi\,+\,2\Big]\,.

(2.1)

The equations of motion derived from (
2.1
) are the Einstein and Klein-Gordon equations

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}\,+\,2g_{\mu\nu}\,=\,\partial_{\mu}\phi\partial_{\nu}\phi\,,

(2.2)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\partial_{\mu}\big(\sqrt{-g}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\phi\big)\,=\,0\,.

(2.3)

In order to search for a time-like Janus solution with the dS
2
slicing, we employ the following ansatz

\displaystyle ds_{3}^{2}\,=\,d\rho^{2}+f(\rho)ds_{\textrm{dS}_{2}}^{2}\,,\qquad ds_{\textrm{dS}_{2}}^{2}\,=\,\frac{-d\eta^{2}+dx^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\,,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\phi\,=\,\phi(\rho)\,,

(2.5)

with unknown functions
f(\rho)
and
\phi(\rho)
.
Upon this ansatz, the Einstein and Klein-Gordon equations are reduced to

\displaystyle 2ff^{\prime\prime}\,-\,f^{\prime 2}\,-\,4f^{2}\,+\,2f^{2}\phi^{\prime 2}\,=\,0\,,

(2.6)

\displaystyle f^{\prime 2}\,-\,4f\,-\,4f^{2}\,-\,2f^{2}\phi^{\prime 2}\,=\,0\,,

(2.7)

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}\big(f\phi^{\prime}\big)\,=\,0\,,

(2.8)

where the prime denotes a derivative with respect to
\rho
.
The Klein-Gordon equation leads to

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}\,=\,\frac{\hat{\gamma}}{f}\,,

(2.9)

with an integration constant
\hat{\gamma}
.
Provided with this result of the Klein-Gordon equation, the remaining two Einstein equations are not independent of each other, and they are also solved by

\displaystyle f(\rho)\,=\,\frac{-1+\sqrt{1-2\hat{\gamma}^{2}}\cosh(2\rho)}{2}\,.

(2.10)

When
\hat{\gamma}=0
, this agrees with the empty AdS
3
as
f(\rho)=\sinh^{2}\rho
. This time-like Janus solution was previously considered in
[
16
]
.
Taking a trace of the Einstein equation (
2.2
), the Ricci scalar is given by

\displaystyle R\,=\,\big(\phi^{\prime}\big)^{2}-\,6\,=\,\left(\frac{\hat{\gamma}}{f}\right)^{2}-\,6\,.

(2.11)

From this expression, one can see that when
\hat{\gamma}^{2}>0
the solution develops a naked singularity.
In order to avoid a naked singularity, in the following we focus on the
\hat{\gamma}^{2}<0
case, where there is no naked singularity.
Therefore, it is more convenient to introduce
\hat{\gamma}=i\gamma
with
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, such that

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}\,=\,\frac{i\gamma}{f}\,,\qquad f(\rho)\,=\,\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+2\gamma^{2}}\cosh(2\rho)}{2}\,,\qquad(\gamma\in\mathbb{R})

(2.12)

\displaystyle R\,=\,\big(\phi\big)^{2}-\,6\,=\,-\left(\frac{\gamma}{f}\right)^{2}-\,6\,.\qquad

(2.13)

In the following discussion, we consider the
\gamma>0
case. The
\gamma<0
case is related by a complex conjugation.
We note that
f(\rho)>0
for any value of
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
. Finally, the dilaton solution is given by

\displaystyle\phi(\rho)\,=\,\phi_{0}\,+\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\log\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{1+2\gamma^{2}}-i\sqrt{2}\gamma\coth\rho}{1+\sqrt{1+2\gamma^{2}}+i\sqrt{2}\gamma\coth\rho}\right)\,.

(2.14)

In summary, our time-like Janus solution is given by

\displaystyle ds_{3}^{2}\,=\,d\rho^{2}+f(\rho)ds_{\textrm{dS}_{2}}^{2}\,,\qquad ds_{\textrm{dS}_{2}}^{2}\,=\,\frac{-d\eta^{2}+dx^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\,,

(2.15)

\displaystyle f(\rho)\,=\,\frac{-1+\sqrt{1+2\gamma^{2}}\cosh(2\rho)}{2}\,,\qquad(\gamma>0)

(2.16)

\displaystyle\phi(\rho)\,=\,\phi_{0}\,+\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\log\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{1+2\gamma^{2}}-i\sqrt{2}\gamma\coth\rho}{1+\sqrt{1+2\gamma^{2}}+i\sqrt{2}\gamma\coth\rho}\right)\,.

(2.17)

We note that this solution is not just a simple Wick rotation from the Euclidean version of the usual space-like Janus solution, without a Wick rotation of the defomation parameter.
In fact, these solutions correspond to two different branch of solutions for the Einstein equations.
We will discuss more in detail the Euclidean solutions in Appendix
A
.

This time-like Janus solution breaks the null-energy condition.
A null vector is given by

\displaystyle N^{\mu}\,=\,\left\{1,\,\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{f}},\,0\right\}\,,

(2.18)

and the null-energy condition is

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu}N^{\mu}N^{\nu}\,=\,\frac{f^{\prime 2}-2f-ff^{\prime\prime}}{2f^{2}}\,=\,\phi^{\prime 2}\,=\,-\left(\frac{\gamma}{f}\right)^{2}\,.

(2.19)

With
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
, the solution (
2.12
) gives
R_{\mu\nu}N^{\mu}N^{\nu}\leq 0
, where the equality is saturated by the empty AdS
3
(
\gamma=0
).
Therefore, this time-like Janus solution is energetically unstable, and one might wonder what the usefulness of this solution is.
Here let us point out a similarity with traversable wormholes.
This construction of the solution is similar to the construction of traversable wormholes
[
17
]
in the sense that we modify the geometries by tuning on the negative energy.
Furthermore, the construction of wormholes by tuning imaginary sources for marginal operators was recently discussed in
[
18
,
15
]
.
Even though this time-like Janus solution breaks the null-energy condition, we will argue that it is nevertheless useful as a toy model of holographic global quantum quench from the next section.

Solution for the other patch

Before discussing an application for the AdS/CFT correspondence, let us also consider the other patch.
As we mentioned, the solution presented above only covers the blue patch depicted in the left panel in figure
2
.
In this patch,
\eta
is a time-like coordinate and
\rho
is a space-like coordinate.
The other patch in the right panel in figure
2
can be thought of a doubly Wick rotation
\eta
and
\rho
.
Therefore, we introduce
\rho=i\hat{\rho}
and
\eta=i\hat{\eta}
.

From the Poincare coordinates of AdS
3
, if we use coordinate transformation

\displaystyle t\,=\,\hat{\eta}\,\cot\hat{\rho}\,,\qquad z\,=\,\frac{\hat{\eta}}{\sin\hat{\rho}}\,,

(2.20)

we arrive at

\displaystyle ds_{3}^{2}\,=\,-d\hat{\rho}^{2}+\sin^{2}\hat{\rho}\left(\frac{d\hat{\eta}^{2}+dx^{2}}{\hat{\eta}^{2}}\right)\,.

(2.21)

Therefore, now
\hat{\rho}
is a time-like coordinate and
\hat{\eta}
is a space-like coordinate.

Now we consider an ansazt

\displaystyle ds_{3}^{2}\,=\,-d\hat{\rho}^{2}+f(\hat{\rho})ds_{\textrm{EAdS}_{2}}^{2}\,,\qquad ds_{\textrm{EAdS}_{2}}^{2}\,=\,\frac{d\hat{\eta}^{2}+dx^{2}}{\hat{\eta}^{2}}\,,

(2.22)

\displaystyle\phi\,=\,\phi(\hat{\rho})\,,

(2.23)

then the solution is given by

\displaystyle f(\hat{\rho})\,=\,\frac{1-\sqrt{1-2\gamma^{2}}\cos(2\hat{\rho})}{2}\,,\qquad(\gamma\in\mathbb{R})

(2.24)

\displaystyle\phi(\hat{\rho})\,=\,\phi_{0}\,+\,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\log\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{1-2\gamma^{2}}-i\sqrt{2}\gamma\cot\hat{\rho}}{1+\sqrt{1-2\gamma^{2}}+i\sqrt{2}\gamma\cot\hat{\rho}}\right)\,.

(2.25)

This solution covers the pink patch in the right panel of figure
2
.
Nevertheless, for an application for AdS/CFT correspondence, we mostly need the previous solution (
2.15
), since the blue patch covers almost all asymptotic AdS boundary, except
t=0
line.

3
One-point Functions

Now we consider the AdS/CFT correspondence with the bulk solution given by the ones discussed in the previous section.
We note that the asymptotic value of the solution (
2.14
) is given by

\displaystyle\phi_{+}\,

\displaystyle=\,\phi_{0}\,+\,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\log\left(\frac{1-i\sqrt{2}\gamma}{1+i\sqrt{2}\gamma}\right)\,,\qquad(\textrm{for}\ \ t>0)

(3.1)

\displaystyle\phi_{-}\,

\displaystyle=\,\phi_{0}\,-\,\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}}\log\left(\frac{1-i\sqrt{2}\gamma}{1+i\sqrt{2}\gamma}\right)\,,\qquad(\textrm{for}\ \ t<0)

(3.2)

Therefore, the dual CFT can be interpreted as an interface conformal field theory (ICFT) with the interface being localized in the time direction at
t=0
and extended to all spatial directions.
(see the left panel of figure.
3
for a schematic picture of the dual ICFT.)
This interface can also be interpreted as a global quantum quench
[
19
,
20
]
as we discuss below.

In order to investigate this holographic ICFT, let us now compute one-point functions.
First, we consider the dual ICFT operator of the bulk dilaton field
\phi
. In the asymptotic limit
\rho\to\infty
, the dilaton solution behaves as

\displaystyle\phi(\rho)\,=\,\phi_{+}\,-\,\frac{2i\gamma}{\sqrt{1+2\gamma^{2}}}\,e^{-2\rho}\,+\,\cdots\,,

(3.3)

for
t>0
.
In the asymptotic limit, we have

\displaystyle e^{-\rho}\,\sim\,\frac{(1+2\gamma^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}}{2}\ \frac{z}{t}\,,

(3.4)

where the factor
(1+2\gamma^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}
will be explained later.
Therefore, the one-point function of the dual ICFT operator is

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t,x)\big\rangle\,=\,-\,\frac{i\gamma}{2}\,\frac{1}{t^{2}}\,,\qquad(\textrm{for}\ \ t>0)

(3.5)

The
t<0
result can be obtained by complex conjugation as

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t,x)\big\rangle\,=\,\frac{i\gamma}{2}\,\frac{1}{t^{2}}\,,\qquad(\textrm{for}\ \ t<0)

(3.6)

Next, we also study the one-point function of the holographic ICFT stress-energy tensor. This is computed from the bulk metric by

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}\big\rangle\,=\,\frac{1}{8\pi G_{N}}\lim_{z\to 0}g_{\mu\nu}\,.

(3.7)

We note that the coordinates of the dual ICFT are given by
(t,x)
as in the Poincare coordinates (
1.1
).
Therefore, to compute the holographic stress-energy tensor, it is useful to move back to these coordinates.
One naively thinks that this is archived by the inverse coordinate transformation of (
1.4
)
which is explicitly written as
\rho=\textrm{arccosh}(t/z)
and
\eta=\sqrt{t^{2}-z^{2}\,}
for
t>z
.
This definition of
(t,z)
transforms the background geometry (
2.15
) into

\displaystyle ds_{3}^{2}\,

\displaystyle=\,-\,\frac{(f-1)t^{2}+z^{2}}{(t^{2}-z^{2})^{2}}\,dt^{2}\,+\,\frac{2t((1+f)z^{2}-t^{2})}{z(t^{2}-z^{2})^{2}}\,dtdz

\displaystyle\qquad+\,\frac{t^{4}-t^{2}z^{2}-fz^{4}}{z^{2}(t^{2}-z^{2})^{2}}\,dz^{2}\,+\,\frac{f}{t^{2}-z^{2}}\,dx^{2}\,,

(3.8)

where
f=f\big(\rho=\textrm{arccosh}(t/z)\big)
.
However, this is not the Gaussian normal coordinates like the Fefferman-Graham coordinates, as
g_{tz}=g_{zt}\neq 0
.
In order to archive the Gaussian normal coordinates at least asymptotically, we define the
(t,z)
coordinates by

\displaystyle\rho\,=\,\textrm{arccosh}\left(\frac{t}{z}\right)\,-\,\frac{1}{4}\log(1+2\gamma^{2})\,,\qquad\eta\,=\,\sqrt{t^{2}-z^{2}\,}\,.

(3.9)

The second term in
\rho
explains the factor
(1+2\gamma^{2})^{\frac{1}{4}}
in (
3.4
).
Since this is simply a shift of the
\rho
coordinate compared to the previous definition of
(t,z)
, the metric is still given by the form of (
3.8
), but now
f
is defined by

\displaystyle f\,

\displaystyle=\,f\Big(\rho=\textrm{arccosh}(t/z)\,-\,\tfrac{1}{4}\log(1+2\gamma^{2})\Big)

\displaystyle=\,\frac{2(t^{2}-z^{2})+\left(2t^{2}-z^{2}-2t^{2}\sqrt{1-\tfrac{z^{2}}{t^{2}}}\right)}{2z^{2}}\,.

(3.10)

With this definition, we have
g_{tz}=g_{zt}=\mathcal{O}(z^{3})
.
This leads to

\displaystyle g_{tt}\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{(1-f)t^{2}-z^{2}}{(t^{2}-z^{2})^{2}}\,=\,-\frac{1}{z^{2}}\,-\,\frac{\gamma^{2}z^{2}}{8t^{4}}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(z^{4})\,,

(3.11)

\displaystyle g_{xx}\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{f}{t^{2}-z^{2}}\,=\,\frac{1}{z^{2}}\,+\,\frac{\gamma^{2}z^{2}}{8t^{4}}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(z^{4})\,,

(3.12)

and
g_{tx}=g_{xt}=0
.
Therefore, the holographic CFT stress-energy tensor is

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}\big\rangle\,=\,0\,.

(3.13)

This result shows that energy and momentum are still conserved when
\gamma>0
.

As in the usual space-like Janus case discussed in
[
21
,
8
]
, the dual ICFT can be understood as a conformal perturbation theory

\displaystyle I_{\lambda}\,=\,I_{0}\,+\,\gamma\int d^{2}x\Big(\,\theta(t)\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t)\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\mathcal{O}(t,x)\,,

(3.14)

where
I_{0}
is the holographic CFT of empty AdS
3
and
\phi_{\pm}^{(1)}
are the linear order coefficient of
\phi_{\pm}
(
3.1
) for small
\gamma
.
In this theory, the one-point function of
\mathcal{O}
is computed as

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t,x)\big\rangle_{\gamma}\,

\displaystyle=\,i\gamma\int d^{2}x^{\prime}\Big(\,\theta(t^{\prime})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t^{\prime})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t,x)\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\big\rangle_{0}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})

\displaystyle=\,\frac{a\pi\gamma}{2}\bigg(\frac{\theta(t)\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t)\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}}{t^{2}}\bigg)\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})\,,

(3.15)

where we used the two-point function in the non-perturbed theory

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t,x)\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\big\rangle_{0}\,=\,\frac{a}{\big((x-x^{\prime})^{2}-(t-t^{\prime})^{2}\big)^{2}}\,,

(3.16)

with some numerical coefficient
a\neq 0
.
This agrees with the bulk computation up to the overall numerical coefficient.
The next-order contribution is given by the three-point function

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t_{1},x_{1})\mathcal{O}(t_{2},x_{2})\mathcal{O}(t_{3},x_{3})\big\rangle_{0}\,=\,\frac{C_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}}{(x_{12}^{2}-t_{12}^{2})(x_{23}^{2}-t_{23}^{2})(x_{31}^{2}-t_{31}^{2})}\,,

(3.17)

where
t_{ij}=t_{i}-t_{j}
and
x_{ij}=x_{i}-x_{j}
, as

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})\,

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\int d^{2}x^{\prime}\int d^{2}x^{\prime\prime}\Big(\,\theta(t^{\prime})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t^{\prime})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\Big(\,\theta(t^{\prime\prime})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t^{\prime\prime})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt\times\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t,x)\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\big\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\,\frac{\pi^{2}C_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}(\phi_{+}^{(1)})^{2}}{2}\,\frac{\gamma^{2}}{t^{2}}\,.

(3.18)

The detail of this computation is presented in Appendix
B
.
On the other hand, the bulk computation (
3.5
), (
3.6
) tells us that
there is no
\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})
contribution to the one-point function
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\gamma}
.
This implies that
C_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}=0
is one of the required CFT data for the holographic dual of the time-like Janus solution.
In fact,
C_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}=0
is also the case for the five dimensional space-like Janus solution
[
21
]
.

Let us also study the one-point function of the stress-energy tensor in this conformal perturbation theory.
At the first order in the deformation parameter, it is given by

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}(t,x)\big\rangle_{\gamma}\,

\displaystyle=\,i\gamma\int d^{2}x^{\prime}\Big(\,\theta(t^{\prime})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t^{\prime})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}(t,x)\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\big\rangle_{0}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})

\displaystyle=\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})\,,

(3.19)

where we used the vanishing two-point function
\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}(t,x)\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\big\rangle_{0}=0
in the non-perturbed theory
[
22
]
.
The above result is consistent with the bulk computation in (
3.13
).
We also look at the second order correction which is given by the three-point function as

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}(t,x)\big\rangle_{\gamma}\,

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\int d^{2}x^{\prime}\int d^{2}x^{\prime\prime}\Big(\,\theta(t^{\prime})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t^{\prime})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\Big(\,\theta(t^{\prime\prime})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t^{\prime\prime})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt\times\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}(t,x)\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},x^{\prime})\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\big\rangle_{0}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{3})\,.

(3.20)

The three-point function is explicitly given by
[
22
]

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}(t_{1},x_{1})\mathcal{O}(t_{2},x_{2})\mathcal{O}(t_{3},x_{3})\big\rangle_{0}\,=\,\frac{C_{T\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}\,h_{\mu\nu}(X)}{(x_{12}^{2}-t_{12}^{2})(x_{23}^{2}-t_{23}^{2})(x_{31}^{2}-t_{31}^{2})}\,,

(3.21)

where

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}(X)\,=\,\frac{X_{\mu}X_{\nu}}{X^{2}}\,-\,\frac{1}{2}\,\eta_{\mu\nu}\,,\qquad X^{\mu}\,=\,\frac{x_{21}^{\mu}}{x_{21}^{2}}\,-\,\frac{x_{31}^{\mu}}{x_{31}^{2}}\,.

(3.22)

Without performing the integrals, we can see several facts.
First, the trace must vanish
\big\langle T^{\mu}{}_{\mu}(t,x)\big\rangle_{\gamma}=0
, since
h_{\mu\nu}
is traceless.
This is consistent with the bulk computation.
Second, the off-diagonal component vanishes
\big\langle T_{tx}(t,x)\big\rangle_{\gamma}=\big\langle T_{xt}(t,x)\big\rangle_{\gamma}=0
, since its integrand is an odd function of
x^{\prime}
or
x^{\prime\prime}
.
This is also consistent with the bulk computation.
Finally, this one-point function is symmetric under time reversal
\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}(-t,x)\big\rangle_{\lambda}=\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}(t,x)\big\rangle_{\lambda}
, since the source term is anti-symmetric under time reversal as
\phi_{-}^{(1)}=-\phi_{+}^{(1)}
.
To find the explicit results for the diagonal components
\big\langle T_{tt}(t,x)\big\rangle_{\gamma}
and
\big\langle T_{xx}(t,x)\big\rangle_{\gamma}

We have not yet obtained vanishing results for these diagonal components, but some our attempts are presented in Appendix
B

In order to be consistent with the bulk computation, this might imply that
C_{T\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}=0
in addition to
C_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}=0
.
It is important to resolve this question, but we will not investigate this point further in the present paper and leave it to future work.

5
Stability

Even though our time-like Janus solution breaks the null energy condition,
we can show that the time-like Janus solution is stable under the scalar perturbation.
Therefore, in this section, we discuss the stability of the background (
2.15
) against dilaton perturbation following the discussion of
[
6
]
.
Even though the dilaton is massless, let us start from a general massive scalar perturbation

\displaystyle 0\,

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{1}{\sqrt{-g}}\Big(\partial_{\mu}\sqrt{-g}g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\phi\Big)\,+\,m^{2}\phi\,.

(5.1)

By assuming a separable form of solution

\displaystyle\phi\,=\,e^{ikx}F(\rho)G(\eta)\,,

(5.2)

The equation can be decomposed into

\displaystyle\Big(f^{-1}\partial_{\rho}f\partial_{\rho}\,-\,m^{2}\Big)F(\rho)\,=\,-M^{2}F(\rho)\,,

(5.3)

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{\eta}^{2}\,+\,k^{2}\,+\,\frac{M^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\right)G(\eta)\,=\,0\,,

(5.4)

where
M^{2}
is the separation constant.
The equation for
G(\eta)
is solved as

\displaystyle G(\eta)\,=\,\sqrt{\eta}\,Z_{\nu}(k\eta)\,,\qquad\left(\nu\,=\,\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{4}-M^{2}}\right)

(5.5)

where
Z_{\nu}
can be any Bessel function.
Since (
5.4
) is the Klein-Gordon equation for dS
2
, one might wish to impose the Bunch–Davies vacuum condition,
which leads to
G(\eta)=\sqrt{\eta}\,H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k\eta)
, where
H_{\nu}^{(1)}
is the Hankel function of the first kind.
However, as we will show, this solution leads to divergent energy and instability.
The correct solution is given by

\displaystyle G(\eta)\,=\,\sqrt{\eta}\,J_{\nu}(k\eta)\,,

(5.6)

which is a unique solution that leads to finite energy.
The boundary condition that leads to the Bessel
J_{\nu}
function is the usual conformal Dirichlet boundary condition for AdS, and this is natural since the original three dimensional spacetime we started from is AdS
3
.

The energy of the perturbation is given by

\displaystyle E\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\sqrt{-g}\Big[-g^{\eta\eta}(\partial_{\eta}\phi)^{2}+g^{xx}(\partial_{x}\phi)^{2}+g^{\rho\rho}(\partial_{\rho}\phi)^{2}+m^{2}\phi^{2}\Big]\,.

(5.7)

Using the equation for
F(\rho)
(
5.3
), this can be rewritten as

\displaystyle E\,=\,\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}x\left[(\partial_{\eta}\phi)^{2}+(\partial_{x}\phi)^{2}+\frac{M^{2}f}{\eta^{2}}\,\phi^{2}\right]\,.

(5.8)

If we focus on the near boundary limit (
\eta\to 0
), the second term is subleading, and the first and third terms behave as

\displaystyle\frac{\phi^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\,\sim\,\eta^{\nu-1}

(5.9)

Therefore, when
\nu>0
(i.e.
M^{2}<1/4
), the
\eta
-integral leads to a finite result. We note that if we had the Hankel function
G(\eta)=\sqrt{\eta}\,H_{\nu}^{(1)}(k\eta)
, the asymptotic behavior is

\displaystyle\frac{\phi^{2}}{\eta^{2}}\,\sim\,\eta^{-1}(\,\#\,\eta^{2\nu}\,+\,\#\,\eta^{-2\nu})\,.

(5.10)

This leads to a divergent result for the
\eta
-integral for any value of
\nu
regardless of whether
\nu
is real or imaginary.

Next, we consider the
\rho
-integral in the last term of (
5.8
).

\displaystyle I\,=\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\rho\,M^{2}f\phi^{2}\,\propto\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\rho\,f\Big[(\partial_{\rho}F)^{2}+m^{2}F^{2}\Big]\,.

(5.11)

Now let us consider the
\rho\to+\infty
limit. Since
f\sim e^{2\rho}
in this limit, assuming
F\sim e^{\gamma\rho}
, the equation of
F(\rho)
in (
5.3
) gives
\gamma(\gamma+2)=m^{2}-M^{2}
. Therefore, the solutions are

\displaystyle\gamma_{\pm}\,=\,-1\pm\sqrt{1+m^{2}-M^{2}}\,.

(5.12)

Only
\gamma_{-}
gives a finite result for the
\rho
-integral when
m^{2}>M^{2}-1
. The case with
M^{2}=0
is the BF bound for AdS
3

[
29
]
.
In the above discussion of
G(\eta)
, we saw that
M^{2}
can be 1/4 at most. Therefore, when
m^{2}>-3/4
, the
\rho
-integral leads to a finite result.
Hence, we conclude that the time-like Janus solution is stable against the massless perturbation
m^{2}=0
.

8
Conclusions and Discussions

In this paper, we have constructed a time-like Janus solution with a time-dependent dilaton field.
In order to avoid a naked singularity, we performed an analytical continuation of the Janus deformation parameter
\gamma
to a pure imaginary value.
This led to a breaking of the null energy condition.
We nevertheless argued that our solution is useful as a toy model of holographic global quantum quench in the context of the AdS/ICFT correspondence.
The asymptotic value of the dilaton field approaches a constant value when
t>0
of the ICFT and a different value when
t<0
.
Hence, the
t=0
discontinuity can be understood as a global quench or a spacelike interface.
We computed holographic one-point functions and holographic entanglement entropy.
For the dual ICFT, we proposed a conformal perturbation theory interpretation with a global-quench-type time-dependent source.
We computed one-point functions in this conformal perturbation theory and confirmed agreement with the bulk computation.
We also discussed its stability against a scalar perturbation and generalization to finite temperature and to higher dimensions.

We note that our time-like Janus solution has two patches depicted in figure
2
.
In fact, the dilaton value coincides at the boundary of the two patches as
\phi(\rho=0)=\phi(\hat{\rho}=0)=\phi_{0}\pm i\pi/\sqrt{2}
, but its derivative does not.
Similarly, the metric solution is finite at the boundary of the two patches, but it does not connect smoothly.
This type of discontinuity might be understood as a shock wave propagating at the speed of light on the boundary of the two patches at
t=\pm z
.
Since a local quench
[
30
]
or a small perturbation
[
31
]
in a holographic CFT produces a shock wave in the bulk AdS spacetime,
it seems natural that our global quench also produces a shock wave in the bulk AdS spacetime.
It would be interesting to investigate this possibility further, but we leave this investigation for future work.

In this paper, we computed the holographic entanglement entropy for a later time
t_{0}\gg\ell
, but not for an early time.
The CFT computation
[
27
]
tells us that in the early time
t_{0}<\ell/2
, the entanglement entropy grows linearly with time.
It is an important question how to derive this linear growth of the entanglement entropy from the bulk computation.
For an sufficiently early time, it seems like that the extremal surface crosses the boundary of the patch
t=\pm z
.
Therefore, it seems important to incorporate the shock wave contribution to the holographic entanglement entropy computation, if our shock wave interpretation is correct.

Euclidean Solutions

In this Appendix, we study Euclidean version of the Janus solutions, and clarify the difference between the usual space-like solution and the time-like solution we are using in this note.
We start from the Euclidean version of the Einstein-dilaton theory, and use the following ansatz

\displaystyle ds_{3}^{2}\,=\,d\rho^{2}+f(\rho)ds_{H_{2}}^{2}\,,\qquad ds_{H_{2}}^{2}\,=\,\frac{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}{y^{2}}\,,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\phi\,=\,\phi(\rho)\,.

(A.2)

This ansatz must contain the Euclidean version of the usual space-like Janus solution (which is used for example in
[
32
]
), as well as the Euclidean version of our time-like Janus solution.
With this ansatz, the Einstein and Klein-Gordon equations are reduced to

\displaystyle 2ff^{\prime\prime}\,-\,f^{\prime 2}\,-\,4f^{2}\,+\,2f^{2}\phi^{\prime 2}\,=\,0\,,

(A.3)

\displaystyle f^{\prime 2}\,+\,4f\,-\,4f^{2}\,-\,2f^{2}\phi^{\prime 2}\,=\,0\,,

(A.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{\rho}\big(f\phi^{\prime}\big)\,=\,0\,.

(A.5)

Only the second term in the second equation is changed from the Lorentzian version of the equations.
As in the Lorentzian case, the Klein-Gordon equation leads to

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}\,=\,\frac{\hat{\gamma}}{f}\,,

(A.6)

with an integration constant
\hat{\gamma}
.
Then the remaining Einstein equations are written as

\displaystyle 2ff^{\prime\prime}\,-\,f^{\prime 2}\,-\,4f^{2}\,+\,2\hat{\gamma}^{2}\,=\,0\,,

(A.7)

\displaystyle f^{\prime 2}\,+\,4f\,-\,4f^{2}\,-\,2\hat{\gamma}^{2}\,=\,0\,.

(A.8)

These equations have two branch of solutions and the generic solutions are given by

\displaystyle f_{\pm}(\rho)\,=\,c_{1}\,e^{2\rho}\,+\,c_{2}\,e^{-2\rho}\,\pm\,\sqrt{4c_{1}c_{2}+\frac{\hat{\gamma}^{2}}{2}}\,,

(A.9)

where
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
are integration constants.

The Euclidean version of the usual space-like Janus solution corresponds to the
f_{+}
branch with

\displaystyle c_{+}\,=\,c_{-}\,=\,\frac{\sqrt{1-2\hat{\gamma}^{2}}}{4}\,.

(A.10)

This leads to

\displaystyle f_{+}(\rho)\,=\,\frac{1+\sqrt{1-2\hat{\gamma}^{2}}\,\cosh(2\rho)}{2}\,.

(A.11)

On the other hand, the Euclidean version of our time-like Janus solution corresponds to the
f_{-}
branch with

\displaystyle c_{+}\,=\,c_{-}\,=\,\frac{\sqrt{1-2\hat{\gamma}^{2}}}{4}\,.

(A.12)

This leads to

\displaystyle f_{-}(\rho)\,=\,\frac{-1+\sqrt{1-2\hat{\gamma}^{2}}\,\cosh(2\rho)}{2}\,.

(A.13)

Appendix B
Integrals in the Conformal Perturbation Theory

In this appendix we summarize some details of the integrals appeared in the conformal perturbation theory discussed in section
3
.
In order to avoid some confusion, in this appendix, we use
y
to denote the spacial coordinate in the CFT
2
, and use
x=x^{\mu}=\{t,y\}
to denote the Lorenzian two-dimensional vector.

Let us start from the
\gamma
order computation for the one-point function
\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t,y)\big\rangle_{\gamma}
.
This computation is basically presented in
[
21
]
, but for later use, we summarize it here. It is explicitly given by

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t,y)\big\rangle_{\gamma}\,

\displaystyle=\,i\gamma\int d^{2}x^{\prime}\Big(\,\theta(t^{\prime})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t^{\prime})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t,y)\mathcal{O}(t^{\prime},y^{\prime})\big\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=\,ia\gamma\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt^{\prime}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dy^{\prime}\,\frac{\big(\,\theta(t^{\prime})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t^{\prime})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\big)}{\big((y-y^{\prime})^{2}-(t-t^{\prime})^{2}\big)^{2}}\,.

(B.1)

The
y
-dependence can be absorbed in the
y^{\prime}
-integral. We first perform the
y^{\prime}
-integral, and then perform the
t^{\prime}
-integral.
In order to make the
y^{\prime}
-integral convergent, we make a Wick rotation to the Euclidean time by
t=-i\tau
during the
y^{\prime}
-integral.
After the
y^{\prime}
-integral, we move back to the Lorentzian time, and perform the
t^{\prime}
-integral as a Lorentzian time.
Therefore, the
y^{\prime}
-integral is evaluated as

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dy^{\prime}}{\big(y^{\prime 2}-(t-t^{\prime})^{2}\big)^{2}}\,\to\,\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dy^{\prime}}{\big(y^{\prime 2}+(\tau-\tau^{\prime})^{2}\big)^{2}}\,=\,\frac{\pi}{2|\tau-\tau^{\prime}|^{3}}\,\to\,\frac{i\pi}{2|t-t^{\prime}|^{3}}\,.

(B.2)

There is an overall sign ambiguity in this
i\epsilon
prescription, but we absorb this ambiguity in the coefficient
a
.

Now, the
t^{\prime}
-integral is written as

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{|t-t^{\prime}|^{3}}\,\Big(\,\theta(t^{\prime})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t^{\prime})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\,=\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{|t-t^{\prime}|^{3}}\,+\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{|t-t^{\prime}|^{3}}\,.

(B.3)

When
t>0
, the first integral has a divergence, while when
t<0
the second integral has a divergence.
We manage these divergences by the splitting regularization as in
[
21
]
.

(i)
t>0
.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{|t-t^{\prime}|^{3}}\,\to\,\int_{t+\delta}^{\infty}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{(t^{\prime}-t)^{3}}\,+\,\int_{0}^{t-\delta}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{(t-t^{\prime})^{3}}\,=\,\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\,-\,\frac{1}{2t^{2}}\,.

(B.4)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{|t-t^{\prime}|^{3}}\,=\,\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{(t-t^{\prime})^{3}}\,=\,\frac{1}{2t^{2}}\,.

(B.5)

(ii)
t<0
.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{|t-t^{\prime}|^{3}}\,=\,\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{(t^{\prime}-t)^{3}}\,=\,\frac{1}{2t^{2}}\,.

(B.6)

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{0}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{|t-t^{\prime}|^{3}}\,\to\,\int_{t+\delta}^{0}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{(t^{\prime}-t)^{3}}\,+\,\int_{-\infty}^{t-\delta}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{(t-t^{\prime})^{3}}\,=\,\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}\,-\,\frac{1}{2t^{2}}\,.

(B.7)

We neglect the cutoff
\delta
-dependent terms. Then the result is summarized as

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt^{\prime}}{|t-t^{\prime}|^{3}}\,\Big(\,\theta(t^{\prime})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t^{\prime})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{\theta(t)}{2t^{2}}\,\big(-\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\phi_{-}^{(1)}\big)\,+\,\frac{\theta(-t)}{2t^{2}}\,\big(\phi_{+}^{(1)}-\phi_{-}^{(1)}\big)

\displaystyle=\,-\,\frac{\big(\,\theta(t^{\prime})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t^{\prime})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\big)}{t^{2}}\,,

(B.8)

where we used the fact that
\phi_{-}^{(1)}=-\phi_{+}^{(1)}
.
Therefore, the one-point function is given by

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t,y)\big\rangle_{\gamma}\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{a\pi\gamma}{2}\bigg(\frac{\theta(t)\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t)\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}}{t^{2}}\bigg)\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})\,.

(B.9)

Next, we proceed to the
\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})
computation for this one-point function. This is explicitly given by

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t_{1},y_{1})\big\rangle_{\gamma}\,

\displaystyle\supset\,-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\int d^{2}x_{2}d^{2}x_{3}\Big(\,\theta(t_{2})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{2})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\Big(\,\theta(t_{3})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{3})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt\times\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t_{1},y_{1})\mathcal{O}(t_{2},y_{2})\mathcal{O}(t_{3},y_{3})\big\rangle_{0}

(B.10)

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{C_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}\gamma^{2}}{2}\int d^{2}x_{2}d^{2}x_{3}\frac{\big(\,\theta(t_{2})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{2})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\big)\big(\,\theta(t_{3})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{3})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\big)}{(y_{12}^{2}-t_{12}^{2})(y_{23}^{2}-t_{23}^{2})(y_{31}^{2}-t_{31}^{2})}\,.

The
y_{2}
and
y_{3}
integrals are given by the following form of integral as

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dy_{c}}{(y_{ac}^{2}-t_{a}^{2})(y_{bc}^{2}-t_{b}^{2})}\,=\,\frac{i\pi(|t_{a}|+|t_{b}|)}{|t_{a}||t_{b}|\big((|t_{a}|+|t_{b}|)^{2}-y_{ab}^{2}\big)}\,,

(B.11)

where we used the Wick rotation as before.
Then, the remaining
t
-integrals are given by

\displaystyle\qquad\big\langle\mathcal{O}(t_{1},y_{1})\big\rangle_{\gamma}

\displaystyle\supset\,-\frac{\pi^{2}C_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}\gamma^{2}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt_{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt_{3}\frac{\big(\,\theta(t_{2})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{2})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\big)\big(\,\theta(t_{3})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{3})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\big)}{|t_{12}||t_{23}||t_{31}|(|t_{12}|+|t_{23}|+|t_{31}|)}

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{\pi^{2}C_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}\gamma^{2}}{2}\left[-\frac{(\phi_{+}^{(1)})^{2}}{4t_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\phi_{+}^{(1)}\phi_{-}^{(1)}}{4t_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\phi_{-}^{(1)}\phi_{+}^{(1)}}{4t_{1}^{2}}-\frac{(\phi_{-}^{(1)})^{2}}{4t_{1}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\,\frac{\pi^{2}C_{\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}(\phi_{+}^{(1)})^{2}}{2}\frac{\gamma^{2}}{t_{1}^{2}}\,,

(B.12)

where we used the splitting regularization as before and
\phi_{-}^{(1)}=-\phi_{+}^{(1)}
.

Finally, we study the stress-energy tensor one-point function at
\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})
order. This is given by

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}(t_{1},y_{1})\big\rangle_{\gamma}\,

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}\int d^{2}x_{2}\int d^{2}x_{3}\Big(\,\theta(t_{2})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{2})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\Big(\,\theta(t_{3})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{3})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt\times\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}(t_{1},y_{1})\mathcal{O}(t_{2},y_{2})\mathcal{O}(t_{3},y_{3})\big\rangle_{0}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{3})\,.

(B.13)

The three-point function is explicitly given by
[
22
]

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{\mu\nu}(t_{1},y_{1})\mathcal{O}(t_{2},y_{2})\mathcal{O}(t_{3},y_{3})\big\rangle_{0}\,=\,\frac{C_{T\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}\,h_{\mu\nu}(X)}{(y_{12}^{2}-t_{12}^{2})(y_{23}^{2}-t_{23}^{2})(y_{31}^{2}-t_{31}^{2})}\,,

(B.14)

where

\displaystyle h_{\mu\nu}(X)\,=\,\frac{X_{\mu}X_{\nu}}{X^{2}}\,-\,\frac{1}{2}\,\eta_{\mu\nu}\,,\qquad X^{\mu}\,=\,\frac{x_{21}^{\mu}}{x_{21}^{2}}\,-\,\frac{x_{31}^{\mu}}{x_{31}^{2}}\,.

(B.15)

We first note that the
y_{1}
-dependence only appears through
y_{21}
and
y_{31}
, so by shifting the integration variables, the result is independent of
y_{1}
.
Furthermore, since we can express

\displaystyle X^{2}\,=\,\frac{x_{23}^{2}}{x_{21}^{2}x_{31}^{2}}\,,

(B.16)

h_{ty}(X)=h_{yt}(X)
is an odd function of
y_{2}
or
y_{3}
. This leads to the vanishing off-diagonal components
\langle T_{ty}\rangle_{\gamma}=\langle T_{yt}\rangle_{\gamma}=0
.
We also note that by construction
[
22
]
the three-point function is traceless
h^{\mu}{}_{\mu}=0
.
This leads to
\langle T^{\mu}{}_{\mu}\rangle_{\gamma}=0
, so for the diagonal components, we only need to compute one of them.
The integrations for
T_{yy}
are simpler, so we compute
\langle T_{yy}\rangle_{\gamma}
here.
There are two contributions for this one-point function; the first comes from the first term in
h_{yy}(X)
and the second comes from the second term in
h_{yy}(X)
.
We denote these contributions by
\langle T_{yy}\rangle_{\gamma}^{(1)}
and
\langle T_{yy}\rangle_{\gamma}^{(2)}
, respectively.
The integrations for
\langle T_{yy}\rangle_{\gamma}^{(2)}
are equivalent to those evaluated for the
\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{2})
order of
\langle\mathcal{O}\rangle_{\gamma}
above.
Therefore, we only need to evaluate
\langle T_{yy}\rangle_{\gamma}^{(1)}
here.
Since an odd function of
y_{2}
or
y_{3}
vanishes, this contribution is written as

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{yy}(t_{1},y_{1})\big\rangle_{\gamma}^{(1)}\,

\displaystyle=\,-\gamma^{2}C_{T\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}\int d^{2}x_{2}\int d^{2}x_{3}\Big(\,\theta(t_{2})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{2})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)

(B.17)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt\times\Big(\,\theta(t_{3})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{3})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\frac{y_{2}^{2}}{x_{12}^{4}x_{23}^{4}}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{3})\,.

The
y_{2}
and
y_{3}
integrals are evaluated by using the Wick rotation as before. This leads to

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{yy}(t_{1},y_{1})\big\rangle_{\gamma}^{(1)}\,

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{\pi^{2}C_{T\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}\gamma^{2}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt_{2}}{|t_{12}|}\Big(\,\theta(t_{2})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{2})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)

(B.18)

\displaystyle\hskip 100.0pt\times\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt_{3}}{|t_{23}|^{3}}\Big(\,\theta(t_{3})\,\phi_{+}^{(1)}+\,\theta(-t_{3})\,\phi_{-}^{(1)}\,\Big)\,.

The
t_{3}
-integral is evaluated in (
B.8
) and we find

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{yy}(t_{1},y_{1})\big\rangle_{\gamma}^{(1)}\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{\pi^{2}C_{T\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}\gamma^{2}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt_{2}}{|t_{12}|t_{2}^{2}}\Big(\,\theta(t_{2})\,(\phi_{+}^{(1)})^{2}+\,\theta(-t_{2})\,(\phi_{-}^{(1)})^{2}\,\Big)

\displaystyle=\,\frac{\pi^{2}C_{T\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}(\phi_{+}^{(1)})^{2}\gamma^{2}}{2}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dt_{2}}{|t_{12}|t_{2}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\,-\frac{\pi^{2}C_{T\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}(\phi_{+}^{(1)})^{2}}{2}\,\frac{\gamma^{2}}{t_{1}^{2}}\,.

(B.19)

Combining the
\langle T_{yy}\rangle_{\gamma}^{(2)}
contribution, we finally find

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{yy}(t_{1},y_{1})\big\rangle_{\gamma}\,=\,-\frac{3\pi^{2}C_{T\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}(\phi_{+}^{(1)})^{2}}{4}\,\frac{\gamma^{2}}{t_{1}^{2}}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{3})\,.

(B.20)

Then the traceless condition
\langle T^{\mu}{}_{\mu}\rangle_{\gamma}=-\langle T_{tt}\rangle_{\gamma}+\langle T_{yy}\rangle_{\gamma}=0
gives

\displaystyle\big\langle T_{tt}(t_{1},y_{1})\big\rangle_{\gamma}\,=\,-\frac{3\pi^{2}C_{T\mathcal{O}\mathcal{O}}(\phi_{+}^{(1)})^{2}}{4}\,\frac{\gamma^{2}}{t_{1}^{2}}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{3})\,.

(B.21)

Appendix C
Extremal Surface

In this appendix we discuss the co-dimenion two extremal surface in the time-dependent spacetime (
3.8
).
As discussed in section
4
, we consider a geodesic anchored at
(t,x,z)=(t_{0},\pm\ell/2,0)
.
The geodesic distance is given by

\displaystyle L\,=\,\int ds_{3}\,=\,2\int_{0}^{z_{*}}dz\sqrt{-g_{tt}\,t^{\prime 2}+2g_{tz}\,t^{\prime}+g_{zz}+g_{xx}\,x^{\prime 2}}\,,

(C.1)

where the metric is given in (
3.8
) and the prime denotes a derivative with respect to
z
.
Therefore, we characterize the geodesic by functions

\displaystyle t\,=\,t(z)\,,\qquad x\,=\,x(z)\,.

(C.2)

The solution for these functions are determined by the Euler-Lagrange equations

\displaystyle 0\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial t}\,-\,\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial t^{\prime}}\right)\,,

(C.3)

\displaystyle 0\,

\displaystyle=\,\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x}\,-\,\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x^{\prime}}\right)\,,

(C.4)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the corresponding Lagrangian read off from (
C.1
).

It is difficult to find the exact expression of the geodesic, so let us consider small deformation parameter
\gamma
, and we expand

\displaystyle L\,=\,L_{0}\,+\,\gamma^{2}L_{2}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{4})\,.

(C.5)

Then, the solutions are also expanded as

\displaystyle t(z)\,

\displaystyle=\,t_{0}\,+\,\gamma^{2}t_{2}(z)\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{4})\,,

(C.6)

\displaystyle x(z)\,

\displaystyle=\,\pm\sqrt{\left(\tfrac{\ell}{2}\right)^{2}-z^{2}}\,+\,\gamma^{2}x_{2}(z)\,+\,\mathcal{O}(\gamma^{4})\,.

(C.7)

Since we have reflection symmetry in the
x
-direction, we only study
x>0
region in the following discussion.
The
x<0
region can be obtained by this reflection symmetry.
The unknown functions
t_{2}(z)
and
x_{2}(z)
are the ones we want to determine, which satisfy the boundary conditions

\displaystyle t_{2}(z=0)\,=\,0\,,\qquad x_{2}(z=0)\,=\,0\,.

(C.8)

Finding the exact solutions for
t2
and
x_{2}
is difficult, but we can easily find the solutions by power series of
z/t_{0}
as

\displaystyle t_{2}(z)\,

\displaystyle=\,t_{0}\left(\frac{z^{6}}{48t_{0}^{6}}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(z^{8})\right)\,,

(C.9)

\displaystyle x_{2}(z)\,

\displaystyle=\,\ell\left(\frac{z^{6}}{24\ell^{2}t_{0}^{4}}\,+\,\mathcal{O}(z^{8})\right)\,.

(C.10)

This agrees with the maximal area surface we used in (
4.1
).
