Leather waste collagen/purple cabbage composite film: a pH indicator for real-time meat freshness assessment
Authors: Xiao-yi Wang, Mi-zhao Song, Hong-bin Li, Wen-rui Chi, Yong-jie Zheng, Yan-liang Gao, Jun-kai Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Real-time monitoring of meat freshness is essential for ensuring food safety and reducing waste; however, conventional methods are often complex and environmentally unsustainable. In this study, a fully biodegradable smart indicator film was developed by incorporating purple cabbage powder (PCP) into a collagen matrix extracted from leather waste. The film was covalently crosslinked with microbial transglutaminase (TG) and physically reinforced with κ-carrageenan (κC) to enable rapid pH-responsive color changes. Increasing the PCP content reduced the tensile strength and elongation at break, but enhanced surface hydrophilicity, thereby facilitating the rapid pH response of the film. The film exhibited a visible color transition from red to green in the pH range of 2–8, and when applied to pork stored at 25 °C, it underwent a distinct color change as the total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) content—a key indicator of protein decomposition—reached the spoilage threshold of 15.21 mg/100 g. This work provides a sustainable strategy for the valorization of leather solid waste while offering an effective and eco-friendly solution for real-time monitoring of meat.

Introduction

Protein-rich foods, such as meat and seafood,are highly susceptible to microbial spoilage during storage and distribution, leading to the accumulation of total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) and the generation of off-odors.1 This progressive deterioration not only causes significant economic losses but also poses potential health risks to consumers of these products. Consequently, there is an urgent need for rapid, low-cost, and intuitive methods to monitor food freshness in real time without relying on sophisticated laboratory instrumentation.2

Traditional freshness monitoring methods typically rely on instrumental analyses, such as chromatography3 and spectroscopic techniques.4 Although precise, these methods involve complex operations and expensive equipment, making them impractical for daily use. To address this issue, researchers have proposed incorporating pH indicators into packaging. Early approaches often involved adding synthetic dyes like bromocresol green,5 methyl red,6 bromothymol blue,7 and other synthetic dyes. However, these approaches still present significant drawbacks. First, synthetic dyes may possess toxicity and carry the risk of migration into food,8–10 compromising food safety. Second, the plastic matrix they rely on is non-biodegradable, increasing the environmental burden upon disposal and contradicting sustainable development principles.

Consequently, research focus has gradually shifted toward developing edible, biodegradable smart packaging films using natural biopolymers.11,12 Among the numerous natural polymers, collagen stands out as an ideal candidate material because of its excellent biocompatibility, abundant raw material sources, and rich active functional groups.13 Compared to materials such as gelatin and chitosan, collagen possesses a natural triple helix structure that enables the formation of a more stable fiber network, which is beneficial for enhancing the mechanical properties and structural stability of the films. The amino and carboxyl groups on collagen side chains not only form strong bonds with natural pigments such as anthocyanins through hydrogen bonding or electrostatic interactions, but the good hydrophilicity and water vapor permeability of collagen also facilitate the absorption of volatile ammonia compounds, thereby improving the film's response rate to TVB-N and enhancing its intelligent recognition capability. However, traditional methods of extracting collagen from mammalian tissues face limitations in large-scale packaging applications because of their high costs14 and competition with food resources.15 Consequently, identifying more economical and sustainable collagen sources has become a critical need.

An innovative and sustainable strategy involves the high-value utilization of collagen-rich waste from the leather industry. Processes such as dehairing, shaving, fleshing, and splitting generate substantial byproducts,16 with these collagen-rich scraps remaining largely underutilized. Extracting collagen from such waste for high-value applications—including multifunctional packaging17 and biomass adsorbents18—offers a dual advantage by avoiding competition with food resources while enabling the resource utilization of tannery solid waste. This approach fully aligns with circular bioeconomy and clean production principles, effectively transforming environmental burdens into valuable resources.19

For active indicator components, anthocyanin-rich plant extracts offer a safe, edible, and effective alternative to synthetic dyes.20 Many reported methods rely on solvent extraction to obtain anthocyanins, which increases operational complexity,21 costs, and environmental impact.22 The direct incorporation of anthocyanin-rich plant powders, such as purple carrot peel,23 purple sweet potato powder,24 blueberry powder,25 and blueberry pomace,26 into film matrices simplifies the process and offers environmental advantages. Purple cabbage powder, which is rich in stable anthocyanins27 and exhibits pronounced color-changing responses,28 is particularly well-suited for this approach.

Despite these advances, the development of a fully biodegradable smart film-using collagen derived from leather industry waste as the matrix, employing cleaner solvent-free extraction, and directly incorporating purple cabbage powder (PCP) as a natural pH-sensitive indicator specifically for meat freshness monitoring remains unexplored. To address these gaps, this study aimed to develop a fully biodegradable smart film for meat freshness monitoring by employing the enzymatic extraction of collagen from sheepskin trimmings generated by the leather industry. To enhance its functional properties for packaging, collagen was covalently crosslinked using microbial transglutaminase (TG), which enhances the toughness of the collagen base film via ε-(γ-glutamyl)lysine bonds,29 and physically blended with κ-carrageenan (κC), a polysaccharide extracted from red algae with excellent film-forming properties that forms a physically interpenetrating network through hydrogen bonding with collagen.30 Purple cabbage powder (PCP) was incorporated directly into the composite film as a natural pH indicator through a clean, extraction-free process. The developed composite films were analyzed via scanning electron microscopy (SEM), Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and thermogravimetric analysis (TG). Subsequently, they were evaluated using light transmittance measurements, mechanical testing, release testing, and pH-sensing performance. Additionally, the relationship between film color changes and pork freshness was investigated to assess the potential applications (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Materials

Sheepskin trimmings were obtained from Henan Prosper & Colomer Moda Co., Ltd (Mengzhou, Henan, China). They were commercially purchased as by-products of the leather industry; no animals were sacrificed specifically for this study. κ-Carrageenan was purchased from Sichuan Xinyuan Biotechnology Co. Ltd Microbial transglutaminase was obtained from Henan Shengfa Biotechnology Co., Ltd Fresh purple cabbage and pork were procured from local markets (Qiqihar, China). Other major chemicals used in the experiments were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Preparation of collagen and composite films

Preparation of collagen and PCP

Collagen was extracted from waste sheepskin trimmings (generated during leather processing) via pepsin digestion in 0.5 M acetic acid at 4 °C for 48 h, followed by dialysis and freeze-drying to obtain a spongy lyophilized solid.31 Purple cabbage powder (PCP) was prepared by freeze-drying, grinding, and sieving fresh purple cabbage through a 200-mesh screen.32

Preparation of composite films

Collagen was dissolved in 0.5 M acetic acid to obtain a homogeneous solution of collagen. TG (4%, w/w relative to collagen) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 45 °C for 60 min to allow enzymatic crosslinking, followed by enzyme inactivation at 90 °C for 10 min. κC (10%, w/w relative to collagen) and glycerol (25%, w/w relative to collagen) were introduced and mixed at 60 °C for 30 min. PCP was then incorporated at final concentrations of 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% (w/w relative to collagen) under continuous stirring for 30 min to ensure a uniform dispersion. The resulting film-forming solutions were cast onto molds, dried at 50 °C for 24 h, and the obtained films were peeled off and equilibrated at 25 °C and 45% relative humidity for 72 h prior to characterization. Pure collagen film (Col) and TG-crosslinked collagen film without κC or PCP (CT) were used as controls. The specific process flow is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Film characterization

Structural and thermal characterization

The surface and cross-sectional morphologies were observed using a scanning electron microscope (S-3400N, Hitachi, Japan) after gold coating. Cross-sections were obtained by cryo-fracturing the samples in liquid nitrogen. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded on a Spectrum Two spectrometer (PerkinElmer, USA) in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1. X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were obtained using a D8 Advance diffractometer (Bruker, Germany) with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) over a scanning range of 5–60° at a rate of 5° min−1. Thermogravimetric analysis (TG) was performed using a STA449F3A thermal analyzer (Netzsch, Germany). Approximately 5 mg of each film was heated from 30 to 600 °C at 10 °C min−1 in a nitrogen atmosphere.

Mechanical properties

The film thickness was measured at ten random points using a digital thickness gauge (GBCY01, Zhejiang Zhengtai Electric Co., Ltd), and the average value was used for calculations. The tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EB) were determined using a universal testing machine (Jinan Languang Electromechanical Technology Co., Ltd) according to ASTM D882. The film specimens (10 mm × 70 mm) were conditioned at 25 °C and 45% relative humidity for 12 h before testing.

Barrier and surface properties

The water vapor permeability (WVP) was measured according to ASTM E96. A moisture-permeable cup containing anhydrous calcium chloride was sealed with the film and placed in a desiccator at 25 °C and 45% RH. Weight gain was recorded to calculate the WVP using the formula WVP = (Δm × d)/(Δt × S × ΔP), where ΔP is the water vapor pressure difference (1426 Pa). Static water contact angle (WCA) was measured using a fully automatic contact angle meter (HS-150B, Tianjin Yunfan Technology Co., Ltd) to evaluate the surface hydrophilicity. UV-Vis transmittance spectra were recorded using a Lambda 35 spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, USA) over the wavelength range of 200–800 nm.33

Biodegradability test

Biodegradability was evaluated via a soil burial test according to a previously reported method with slight modifications.34 Film specimens (10 mm × 10 mm) were dried in a desiccator for 24 h and weighed to record the initial mass (W0). The specimens were then buried in natural soil at a depth of 5 cm, with the soil moisture maintained at approximately 65%. At predetermined intervals (3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, and 21 days), and dried at 105 °C to constant weight to obtain the final mass (W1). The degradation rate was calculated using the following equation: degradation rate (%) = (W0 − W1)/W0 × 100%.

pH-responsive properties

For the release test, the ethanol release test, while not a standardized method, has been widely employed in intelligent packaging literature to evaluate the immobilization stability of natural dyes within polymer matrices.35 Film samples were immersed in ethanol with gentle stirring for 12 h, and the color difference (ΔE) before and after immersion was measured using a colorimeter (LS175, Shenzhen Linshang Technology Co., Ltd.).35 Ammonia sensitivity was assessed by placing the film over the opening of a conical flask containing 25% ammonia solution, sealing it, and incubating at 25 °C for 30 min, followed by ΔE measurement.36 pH responsiveness was evaluated by immersing film strips (2 mm × 10 mm) in buffer solutions at pH 2–8 and measuring the color parameters; ΔE > 3.5 was considered visually detectable.37

Application for meat freshness monitoring

For pork freshness monitoring, 20 g of pork was sealed in a glass dish with the test film and stored at 25 °C.38 When the film turned green, the total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) content was determined according to the Chinese national standard GB 5009.288-2016.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicates. Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey's multiple comparison test, was performed. Graphs were generated using Origin 9.8.

Results and discussion

Structure and physicochemical properties

Prior to film fabrication, the molecular integrity of collagen extracted from sheepskin trimmings was verified using SDS-PAGE. As shown in Fig. 3c, the pattern exhibited characteristic type I collagen bands: α1 (∼115 kDa) and α2 (∼115 kDa) at an intensity ratio of approximately 2 : 1, along with β (dimer, ∼200 kDa) and faint γ (trimer, >200 kDa) bands. The absence of low-molecular-weight fragments below α2 confirmed no hydrolytic degradation occurred during enzymatic extraction, confirming retention of the native triple helical structure and molecular integrity.39

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to examine the microstructural homogeneity and interfacial compatibility among collagen, κ-carrageenan, and purple cabbage powder (PCP), as the presence of phase separation, voids, or particle agglomeration directly influences the mechanical integrity and barrier properties of the final packaging material. The surface SEM images (Fig. 3a) show that the Col film exhibits a rough fibrous texture with distinct grooves. After crosslinking with TG (CT), the surface became smoother and more uniform, attributed to the formation of ε-(γ-glutamyl)lysine covalent crosslinks catalyzed by TG,40 which enhanced interfacial compatibility. With the addition of κ-carrageenan (CP0), the surface remained smooth, indicating good interfacial compatibility between collagen and κC,41 which was further corroborated by FTIR analysis. As the PCP content increased from 5% to 25% for CP5 to CP25, granular structures gradually appeared and became denser at higher PCP loadings (CP20 and CP25), indicating the physical filling of PCP particles within the matrix. The cross-sectional SEM images (Fig. 3b) show that all the composite films (CP0, CP5 and CP25) exhibit continuous, dense morphologies without observable voids, cracks, or phase separation interfaces, confirming the tight encapsulation of PCP within the matrix.

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was utilized to identify the molecular interactions and chemical bonding patterns within the matrix, thereby confirming the successful incorporation of the active components and elucidating the crosslinking mechanism responsible for the film's stability. FTIR spectra of the films are shown in Fig. 3d. With 15% PCP (CP15), the amide A absorption significantly broadened (FWHM ≈ 245 cm−1), and multi-peak Gaussian fitting further resolved two components that were otherwise masked by the intense amide I envelope: a shoulder at 1727.9 cm−1 (FWHM ≈ 118 cm−1) attributable to the ester CO stretching of pectin, and a statistically significant component at 1585.9 cm−1 (FWHM ≈ 54.8 cm−1, p < 0.05) assignable to the aromatic CC stretching of anthocyanins.44 The red-shift of this band from its typical position (∼1603 cm−1) indicates strong hydrogen-bonding interactions within the matrix, as hydrogen bonding alters the electron density distribution around functional groups and modifies their vibrational force constants.45 Collectively, these spectral changes, in conjunction with the XRD results and release test, confirm the successful integration of PCP components into the collagen/κC network.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed to assess the crystalline or amorphous nature of the films, since the degree of crystallinity governs both the mechanical rigidity and the permeability of the polymer network. The XRD patterns (Fig. 3e) of Col show diffraction peaks at 7.21° and 20.02°, corresponding to the regular molecular lateral stacking of collagen chains and the intact triple-helix axial repeating structure, respectively.46 For CT, the peaks shifted to 7.17° and 20.28° with significant broadening and weakening, indicating that enzymatic crosslinking loosens the regular lateral stacking of collagen molecules. In CP0, the peak near 7° disappeared, leaving only a broad diffuse peak at 20.86°, indicating that κ-carrageenan synergistically interacted with the TG crosslinking network, transforming the matrix into a highly homogeneous amorphous structure. With PCP incorporation (CP15 and CP25), the characteristic peak intensity near 20° further diminished. The relative crystallinity (CrI) values derived from the XRD patterns are summarized in Table 1, showing a progressive decrease from 35% (Col) to 15% (CP25) with increasing PCP content, further confirming the gradual disruption of ordered collagen packing upon crosslinking and filler incorporation.47 This indicates that PCP particles, acting as physical fillers, do not introduce new crystalline phases but dilute the segment density of the matrix, leading to a sustained decrease in crystallinity.

Physicochemical and mechanical properties of collagen-based films with varying PCP content

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) was carried out to evaluate the thermal stability of the films under conditions that simulate potential thermal processing or elevated storage temperatures encountered in food supply chains. The thermal stability is shown in Fig. 3f and g, respectively. The TG curves (Fig. 3f) show that all films underwent three stages of degradation: volatilization of low-molecular-weight compounds (including absorbed moisture and residual acetic acid from the casting solution) at 30–150 °C, polymer decomposition at 150–400 °C, and carbonization of residues above 400 °C. The addition of PCP had little effect on the overall degradation process. The DTG curves (Fig. 3g) indicate that the maximum thermal degradation rate (Tmax) of Col occurred at 336.2 °C. The slight decrease in Tmax with increasing PCP content is attributed to the incorporation of PCP constituents (e.g., pectin, cellulose, and anthocyanins), which have lower intrinsic thermal stability than collagen and accelerate the overall thermal degradation of the composite.48 Nevertheless, the maximum degradation temperatures of all films remained above 300 °C. Although this temperature range far exceeds typical food storage conditions (4–25 °C), the TGA data confirm adequate thermal stability for potential processing steps such as heat-sealing during packaging manufacture. The integrated molecular structure and interaction mechanism are illustrated schematically in Fig. 3h. Collagen triple helices are covalently crosslinked by TG through ε-(γ-glutamyl)lysine bonds, forming a robust protein network. κ-Carrageenan chains interpenetrate this network and associate with collagen via multiple hydrogen bonds between the sulfate ester groups and the amide/hydroxyl groups of collagen. PCP particles, which contain anthocyanin glycosides and pectin polysaccharides, are physically entrapped within this hybrid matrix and stabilized by extensive hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. Anthocyanin molecules predominantly exist in the red flavylium cationic form under the slightly acidic conditions of the film.

Mechanical and barrier properties

The evaluation of mechanical properties, including tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EB), is of paramount importance for any packaging material intended for practical use. During handling, transportation, and storage, the film must withstand external stresses such as stretching, folding, and puncturing without undergoing catastrophic failure. A film with insufficient tensile strength is prone to tearing, while inadequate elongation at break may result in brittleness and cracking, thereby compromising the protective function of the packaging and potentially exposing the food product to external contaminants. The tensile strength (TS) and elongation at break (EB) of the films are shown in Fig. 4a and b, respectively. The pure collagen film (Col) exhibited a TS of 6.28 MPa and EB of 14.33%. After crosslinking with TG (CT), both TS and EB significantly increased to 11.97 MPa and 67.01%, respectively, to the formation of covalent crosslinks. With the further addition of κ-carrageenan (CP0), the TS reached its highest value of 13.11 MPa, while the EB decreased to 41.30%. This improvement was attributed to the physical reinforcement provided by κ-carrageenan. As the PCP content increased from 0% to 25% (CP0 to CP25), TS decreased from 13.11 MPa to 2.37 MPa, and EB decreased from 41.30% to 17.83%. This reduction is attributed to the dilution of the load-bearing polymer network by the PCP fillers, which are inert and rigid relative to the soft collagen/κC matrix, thereby reducing the effective chain density available for stress transfer, as well as localized stress concentration around these rigid particles that promotes premature crack initiation under tensile load, even in the absence of macroscopic defects. As confirmed by FTIR analysis, κC formed abundant hydrogen bonds with collagen (via sulfate ester groups of κC and amide/hydroxyl groups of collagen), and XRD analysis revealed that the composite matrix transformed into a highly homogeneous amorphous structure. These physical interactions increased the network density and stiffness, resulting in the highest tensile strength (13.11 MPa). In contrast to the covalent ε-(γ-glutamyl)lysine bonds introduced by TG, the κC-mediated physical reinforcement simultaneously reduced the elongation at break (from 67.01% to 41.30%), consistent with the behavior of a more rigid but less extensible network. The representative stress–strain curves of all film formulations are provided in Fig. 4d. The curves show that Col exhibited typical brittle behavior with a sharp drop after yielding, while CT and CP0 displayed more ductile deformation with extended elongation regions, consistent with the formation of a crosslinked and reinforced network. With increasing PCP content, the curves gradually shifted toward lower stress and reduced strain, reflecting the progressive embrittlement and weakening of the matrix due to filler dilution and localized stress concentration effects. The film thickness as a function of the PCP content is shown in Fig. 4c. As the PCP concentration increased from 0% to 25%, the average film thickness increased progressively from approximately 180 µm to 207 µm. This monotonic increase was attributed to the physical addition of the solid PCP filler, which increased the total solid content of the film-forming solution and consequently produced thicker films upon drying. The thickness values are consistent with the dense and continuous cross-sectional morphologies observed by SEM (Fig. 3b), confirming that PCP incorporation does not introduce porosity or voids within the matrix. Conversely, the water vapor permeability (WVP) is a critical determinant of the film's ability to regulate moisture exchange between the packaged food and the surrounding environment. An excessively high WVP may lead to undesirable dehydration of fresh meat or, alternatively, allow excessive moisture ingress that promotes microbial growth. The water contact angle (WCA) measurement serves as a direct indicator of surface hydrophilicity, which in turn governs the film's affinity for water vapor and volatile amine compounds. A more hydrophilic surface facilitates the rapid adsorption and diffusion of basic spoilage metabolites, thereby enhancing the sensitivity and response speed of the pH-indicating function. The optical transparency and UV–vis light barrier properties are relevant to consumer acceptance and food preservation, as excessive light transmission, particularly in the ultraviolet region, can accelerate lipid oxidation and nutrient degradation in packaged meat products. The water vapor permeability (WVP) and water contact angle (WCA) are shown in Fig. 4f and g, respectively. Col had the highest WVP of 4.71 × 10−10 g (m−1 s−1 Pa−1). After crosslinking and κC blending (CP0), the WVP decreased to 4.22 × 10−10, owing to the formation of a denser structure through crosslinking and hydrogen bonding interactions. With the addition of PCP, the WVP first decreased to 3.85 × 10−10 at 10% PCP (CP10) and then increased to 4.31 × 10−10 at 25% PCP (CP25). This trend suggests that moderate amounts of PCP acted as a physical filler, enhancing the film density, whereas excessive amounts of PCP disrupted the matrix continuity and created more hydrophilic pathways. The water contact angle of the Col was 89.81°. After crosslinking and κC blending, CP0 exhibited a higher contact angle of 97.37°, indicating enhanced hydrophobicity and a compact structure. As the PCP content increased from 5% to 25%, the contact angle gradually decreased from 87.34° to 51.14°, reflecting increased surface hydrophilicity due to the polar groups in PCP. This heightened hydrophilicity facilitates the absorption of environmental water vapor and volatile basic amines, thereby enabling a rapid pH response.49 Representative water droplet images of the film surfaces are shown in Fig. 4h. The progressively flattened droplet profiles with increasing PCP content visually confirmed the enhanced surface hydrophilicity, consistent with the decreasing water contact angle values presented in Fig. 4g. The UV-Vis transmittance spectra of the films are shown in Fig. 4e. Col exhibited the highest transparency. Crosslinking with TG and blending with κC (CT and CP0) reduced the transmittance owing to the tighter matrix structure. With increasing PCP content, the transmittance further decreased, especially in the UV region (200–400 nm), indicating an excellent UV-shielding performance. This property is beneficial for food packaging as it prevents light-induced lipid oxidation.

The biodegradability of the CP15 film was evaluated via a soil burial test over 21 days, and the degradation rate is shown in Fig. 4i. The degradation rate increased progressively with burial time, reaching 25.7% after 3 days and 63.8% after 21 days. Notably, the film fragmented into small pieces after 6 days of burial. This early-stage fragmentation is attributed to three synergistic factors: the leaching of hydrophilic glycerol (25 wt%) from the matrix, which creates microchannels that facilitate water penetration and loss of soluble components; the stress concentration points generated by PCP particles, which promote crack propagation; and the susceptibility of collagen to enzymatic attack by proteases ubiquitously present in soil, leading to rapid enzymatic hydrolysis even at the initial stage of burial.50 The degradation rate accelerated markedly between 12 and 21 days (from 43.0% to 63.8%), suggesting progressive disruption of the crosslinked network and increased bioavailability of matrix components to soil microorganisms. These results confirm the biodegradable nature of the developed film, and support its potential as an environmentally sustainable packaging alternative.

Smart response and application

The ultimate functionality of the developed composite film hinges upon its ability to undergo a visually discernible color change in response to the volatile basic nitrogen compounds generated during meat spoilage.

The release test in ethanol was conducted to assess the immobilization stability of the purple cabbage powder components within the crosslinked collagen–κ-carrageenan matrix, as any substantial leaching of the natural dye into the food product would represent both a loss of sensor functionality and a potential quality concern. The release test indicated that anthocyanins were adequately retained within the matrix at PCP loadings up to 15%, as evidenced by the relatively low ΔE values (1.07–2.34 for CP5–CP15). However, at the highest PCP loading (CP25), a more pronounced color difference (ΔE = 5.56) was observed, suggesting that excessive PCP incorporation led to partial leaching of anthocyanins from the outer film surface. The ammonia sensitivity test was designed to simulate the alkaline headspace environment that develops within sealed meat packaging, providing a rapid and standardized method to evaluate the responsiveness of the anthocyanin-based indicator. The dynamic response of the films to volatile ammonia vapor is shown in Fig. 5c. Exposure to ammonia caused a progressive color transition from red to green, which is attributed to the deprotonation of anthocyanins under the alkaline microenvironment generated by the absorbed vapor. The color change intensity increased significantly with increasing PCP content, and a high-contrast color shift was observed at 15% PCP. The sensitivity over time (Fig. 5c) increased continuously and then became stable. Based on these results and the release test, CP15 was selected for further application. pH responsiveness. Prior to the pH-response and pork spoilage tests, the initial color of the selected CP15 film was recorded under ambient conditions (25 °C, 45% RH) to establish a visual baseline. The as-prepared film exhibited a light reddish-pink appearance, with CIELAB color parameters of L* = 72.85 ± 0.23, a* = 5.94 ± 0.09, and b* = −0.38 ± 0.08 (ΔE = 0). This coloration is attributed to the flavylium cation form of anthocyanins, which is predominantly stabilized under the acidic microenvironment derived from the 0.5 M acetic acid used in the film-forming solution. This initial state was used as the reference (ΔE = 0) for monitoring subsequent color alterations during the pH-dependent response and meat freshness assessment. The pH-dependent colorimetric response across the range of pH 2–7 was characterized to establish a calibration curve for the film's performance, since the pH of fresh meat (typically 5.5–6.5) gradually increases toward neutrality and slightly alkaline values upon microbial decomposition. The color changes of the CP15 film at different pH values are shown in Fig. 5d. The film exhibited a stable red color under acidic conditions (pH 2–4), turned purple at pH 5–6, and became blue at pH 7, which is consistent with the typical pH-dependent behavior of anthocyanins.51 The pH range of 2–8 was selected to encompass the full spectrum of conditions relevant to meat freshness monitoring, covering the slightly acidic pH of fresh meat (typically 5.5–6.5) to the alkaline pH values (>7.0) reached during advanced spoilage. The CIELAB color parameters (Table 2) showed that the a* value decreased from 15.87 at pH 2 to −0.46 at pH 8, whereas the b* value increased from −2.35 to 2.46 over the same range. At pH 8, the film turned green with ΔE = 7.89 relative to pH 2, indicating a distinct visual transition at the spoilage-relevant alkaline condition. The total color difference ΔE relative to pH 2 (Fig. 5f) exceeded 5 in the pH range of 2–4, indicating a clear visual distinction. At pH 5–7, ΔE remained from 2.67 to 3.21, which was still distinguishable. This pH range corresponds to the initial spoilage phase of high-protein foods,52 where the pH increases owing to the release of amine compounds. The application trial with pork stored at 25 °C was performed to validate the correlation between the film's color change and the actual accumulation of total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N), the standard chemical index of meat freshness. By establishing a quantitative linear relationship between the color difference (ΔE) and TVB-N content, the study aims to demonstrate that the film can serve as a reliable, real-time visual indicator that alerts consumers to the onset of spoilage without the need for destructive sampling or laboratory analysis. The practical application of CP15 for monitoring pork freshness at 25 °C is shown in Fig. 5g–i. The TVB-N value of pork increased from 8.31 mg per 100 g (fresh) to 15.21 mg per 100 g after 18 h of storage (Fig. 5g), exceeding the Chinese national standard threshold of 15 mg per 100 g (GB 5009.288-2016). The photographs in Fig. 5g illustrate the corresponding visual changes in pork from fresh to spoiled. Fig. 5h confirms that the TVB-N increase was driven by microbial activity, as the sterile group showed no significant changes over time. Most importantly, the ΔE of the CP15 film exhibited a strong linear correlation with the TVB-N value (R2 = 0.977, Fig. 5i), with the fitting equation y = 0.33138x + 7.92574. Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a highly sensitive pH-responsive film by directly incorporating purple cabbage powder (PCP) into a composite matrix of leather-waste-derived collagen covalently crosslinked by transglutaminase (TG) and physically reinforced by κ-carrageenan (κC) via hydrogen bonding. The film exhibited a distinct and visually discernible color transition from red to green in response to pH changes induced by volatile basic nitrogen compounds, enabling the effective and real-time detection of freshness deterioration in pork. The systematic characterization confirmed that the synergistic crosslinking network imparted adequate mechanical integrity and thermal stability, while the incorporation of PCP conferred rapid and sensitive colorimetric responsiveness.

This work offers a dual-benefit strategy with significant implications for both environmental sustainability and food safety. On one hand, it provides a high-value utilization pathway for collagen-rich solid waste from the leather industry, transforming an environmental burden into a functional biomaterial and thereby advancing the principles of the circular bioeconomy and cleaner production. On the other hand, it delivers an effective, eco-friendly, and fully biodegradable solution for real-time meat freshness monitoring, reducing the reliance on synthetic indicators and non-degradable plastics in intelligent packaging. From a chemical perspective, this study demonstrates that TGase-catalyzed crosslinking of leather waste collagen, combined with κ-carrageenan, generates a stable network capable of immobilizing anthocyanins without covalent modification, while preserving their pH-sensitive chromic behavior.

Future studies should focus on validating the universality and reliability of the developed film across a broader range of perishable protein-rich foods, including various meat types (e.g., poultry, lamb) and aquatic products (e.g., fish, shrimp). While the fully biodegradable nature of the film is anticipated based on its composition, future studies should include systematic biodegradation tests under simulated environmental conditions to quantitatively assess the degradation rate and kinetics. Additionally, systematic investigations are warranted to evaluate the film's long-term stability, colorimetric response accuracy, and mechanical durability under actual cold chain conditions, including fluctuating temperatures and high-humidity environments typical of commercial storage and transportation. Such validations are essential to advance this laboratory-scale innovation toward practical industrial applications in smart food packaging.
