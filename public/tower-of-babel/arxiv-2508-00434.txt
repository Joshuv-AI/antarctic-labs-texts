Accurate Latent Inversion for Generative Image Steganography via Rectified Flow
Yuqi Qian, Yun Cao, Meiyang Lv, Haocheng Fu

Abstract
Steganography based on diffusion models has attracted increasing attention due to its ability to generate high-quality images and exhibit strong robustness. In such approaches, the secret message is first embedded into the initial latent variable, and then the stego image is generated through the forward process. To extract the message, an inversion process is required to reconstruct the latent variables from the received image. However, inaccurate latent inversion leads to significant discrepancies between the reconstructed and original latent variables, rendering message extraction infeasible. To address this issue, we propose RF-Stego , a novel generative image steganography method that enables accurate latent inversion and significantly improves extraction accuracy. First, we develop the P ath C onsistency L inear I nversion ( PCLI ), which imposes formal constraints on the inversion process. By explicitly aligning it with the forward generation path and modeling both directions along a shared linear path, PCLI eliminates path mismatch and ensures path consistency throughout the steganographic process. Second, through rigorous theoretical proof, we demonstrate that R ectified F low (RF) offers both theoretical reversibility and numerical stability in the inversion process. Based on this, we replace traditional unstable samplers with RF sampler which effectively improves the numerical precision of the inversion process. Experimental results show RF-Stego outperforms state-of-the-art methods in terms of extraction accuracy, image quality, robustness, security and generation efficiency.

Introduction

With the rapid advancement of generative artificial intelligence techniques, AI-generated content (AIGC) has proliferated across social networks, creating new opportunities for steganographic applications. Image steganography is the technology that conceals secret data within an image without arousing notice
(
Fridrich 2009
)
. It can be classified into embedding-based steganography and generative steganography, depending on whether the cover image is modified
(
Peng et al. 2023
)
. Embedding-based approaches suffer from the risk of leaving modification traces on the cover image, particularly in high-capacity scenarios. These residual traces can be effectively exploited by advanced steganalysis techniques thereby reducing the security
(
Xu, Wu, and Shi 2016
;
Ye, Ni, and Yi 2017
;
You, Zhang, and Zhao 2020
)
. Generative steganography directly generates the stego image from secret message, eliminating the need for the cover image. This makes it less susceptible to detection by steganalysis tools and greatly enhances security
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
. Diffusion models
(
Ho, Jain, and Abbeel 2020
;
Song, Meng, and Ermon 2020
;
Dhariwal and Nichol 2021
;
Rombach et al. 2022
)
have emerged as a promising choice for generative steganography due to their impressive image generation capabilities. In diffusion-based steganography, secret message is embedded into the initial latent variables, and then generates the stego image through the forward process. During extraction, a latent inversion process is applied to reconstruct the latent variables. When the latent inversion becomes inaccurate, the reconstructed latent variables diverges markedly from the original, ultimately resulting in message extraction failure. Existing methods
(
Peng et al. 2023
;
Peng et al. 2024
;
Zhou et al. 2025
;
Hu et al. 2024
;
Kim et al. 2025
)
focus on designing sophisticated embedding mechanisms to compensate for inversion error. Nevertheless, as shown in Fig.
1
, although advanced embedding mechanisms can tolerate a certain level of error, their error correction capability is bounded. Let
x_{0}
denote the original latent variable and
\hat{x}_{0}
its reconstructed counterpart obtained via inversion. Message can only be extracted when the inversion error
\|\hat{{x}}_{0}-{x}_{0}\|
remains below a predefined tolerance threshold
R_{\text{tol}}
. Once the inversion error exceeds
R_{\text{tol}}
, the embedding mechanism becomes incapable to ensure successful message extraction.

Specifically, inaccurate inversion arises from two fundamental issues. First, the inversion frameworks in these methods suffer from a mismatch between the forward and the inversion paths. This mismatch leads to non-uniqueness and accumulated deviations in the inversion path. Second, their samplers such as DDPM and DDIM are inherently non-reversible and numerically unstable in inversion settings. These limitations result in uncontrolled numerical errors.

To address these issues, we propose
RF-Stego
, a novel generative image steganography method that enables accurate latent inversion and improves extraction accuracy. Specifically,
we propose
P
ath
C
onsistency
L
inear
I
nversion (
PCLI
), which imposes formal constraints on the inversion process. It guarantees the path consistency and resolve the mismatch problem. Moreover, through rigorous theoretical analysis, we adopt
R
ectified
F
low (
RF
) sampler to replace traditional unstable samplers thereby addressing the issues of irreversibility and numerical instability.
Our main contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We propose
RF-Stego
, a novel generative image steganography method that achieves high-accuracy message extraction by explicitly reducing inversion error.

•

We design
PCLI
, an ODE-based framework that ensures path consistency by explicitly aligning the inversion process with the forward generation. By modeling both directions along a shared linear path, PCLI effectively eliminates trajectory mismatch.

•

We conduct rigorous theoretical analysis to demonstrate that
RF
offers both theoretical reversibility and numerical stability in the inversion process. Accordingly, we propose replacing traditional unstable samplers with RF results in a pronounced reduction in inversion error and improves the accuracy of message extraction.

•

Experimental results show RF-Stego achieves better performance than the state-of-the-art diffusion-based steganography methods in terms of extraction accuracy, image quality, robustness, security, and generation efficiency.

Related Work and Preliminary

Diffusion-based Generative Image Steganography

Diffusion-based generative image steganography utilizes diffusion models to hide secret data during image generation. Researchers focus on designing effective embedding mechanisms.
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
embed secret bits into the DCT coefficients of the initial latent noise vector. This frequency-domain encoding preserves the noise distribution and enables nearly lossless message extraction through an invertible diffusion path.
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
propose a dual-key mapping scheme that transforms the message into a Gaussian-distributed latent vector. The design ensures statistical indistinguishability from standard noise and enhances extraction robustness under lossy channels.
(
Kim et al. 2025
)
introduce a distribution-consistent embedding mechanism that maps message into the latent space while maintaining Gaussian characteristics. By offering adjustable embedding modes, they balance extraction accuracy and payload capacity. Despite their effectiveness, these approaches depend on precise inversion for accurate message extraction, yet they lack dedicated method to ensure inversion accuracy, resulting in suboptimal extraction performance.

Rectified Flow

Rectified Flow
(
Liu, Gong, and Liu 2022
)
is a generative modeling method based on ordinary differential equations (ODEs), designed to efficiently transform a simple prior distribution
\pi_{0}
into a complex data distribution
\pi_{1}
. It aims to construct a nearly straight trajectory in the data space, thereby reducing both generation and inversion errors. Given an initial latent variable
Z_{0}\sim\pi_{0}
, Rectified Flow defines a continuous-time ODE:

dZ_{t}=v(Z_{t},t)dt,t\in[0,1]

(1)

where
v(\cdot,t)
is a learnable velocity field parameterized by a neural network. By solving this ODE from
t=0
to
t=1
, the distribution of
Z_{t}
is gradually transformed, and ideally
Z_{1}
matches the target distribution
\pi_{1}
.
To guide the learning of
v
, Rectified Flow introduces a linear interpolation path between samples
X_{0}\sim\pi_{0}
and
X_{1}\sim\pi_{1}
, defined as

X_{t}=(1-t)X_{0}+tX_{1}

(2)

The derivative of this path is constant:
\frac{\mathrm{d}X_{t}}{\mathrm{d}t}=X_{1}-X_{0}
. Although this path cannot be directly simulated causally as an ODE trajectory, it serves as an ideal reference for
v
.
Rectified Flow optimizes the velocity field
v
by minimizing the squared deviation from the linear velocity:

\min_{v}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|(X_{1}-X_{0})-v(X_{t},t)\|^{2}\bigr]dt

(3)

where the expectation is over
(X_{0},X_{1})
pairs. This formulation essentially turns the problem into a regression task, requiring
v(Z_{t},t)
to approximate the linear vector
X_{1}-X_{0}
as closely as possible.

Methodology

Overview Architecture

The architecture of our RF-Stego is shown in Fig.
2
. RF-Stego is composed of PCLI jointly deployed with RF sampler, which together ensure accurate message extraction.

PCLI contains four main components: a message mapping module
F
using advanced embedding mechanism, a diffusion model
\varphi
equipped with a pretrained VAE, a noise sampler
S_{\theta}
for latent trajectory during the diffusion process and a noise simulation module
\mathcal{N}
. To ensure successful message embedding and extraction, the sender and receiver must share a set of side information, including the key
K
, the generation prompt
P
, and sampling parameters such as the number of steps
N
and the guidance scale
w
.

Message Embedding

Given a secret binary message
M\in\{0,1\}^{k}
, the sender first uses the mapping module
F
to obtain an initial latent variable:

x_{0}=F(M,K),\quad x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(4)

This latent variable
x_{0}
then serves as the initial condition for a continuous-time generative process governed by an ordinary differential equation (ODE):

\frac{dx_{t}}{dt}=f(\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(x_{t},t,P,w),t),\quad t\in[0,T]

(5)

Here,
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
is a pretrained noise sampler conditioned on
x_{t}
,
t
,
P
, and
w
. The transition function
f
converts the predicted noise into the latent velocity (e.g., via scaled residual denoising). To ensure a unique and deterministic forward trajectory, we assume
f
is Lipschitz continuous with respect to
x
; by the Picard–Lindelöf theorem, this guarantees a unique solution to the ODE and induces a smooth diffeomorphic mapping
\Phi_{T}:x_{0}\mapsto x_{T}
.

In practice, the ODE is discretized using Euler integration with
N
uniform steps
\Delta t=T/N
:

x_{t_{n+1}}=x_{t_{n}}+\Delta t\cdot f(\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(x_{t_{n}},t_{n},P,w),t_{n})

(6)

After the final step
t_{N}=T
, the latent variable
x_{T}
is decoded into a stego image via the VAE decoder:

X_{\text{stego}}=D_{\text{VAE}}(x_{T})

(7)

Corruption Simulation

To simulate real-world transmission noise, an optional noise simulation module
\mathcal{N}
may degrade the image:

\hat{X}_{\text{stego}}=\mathcal{N}(X_{\text{stego}})

(8)

Specifically, we introduce a variety of noise types, including JPEG compression, median filtering, and Gaussian noise, to simulate typical lossy operations encountered in practical
communication channels.

Message Extraction

The receiver encodes the received image
\hat{X}_{\text{stego}}
into a latent variable:

\hat{x}_{T}=E_{\text{VAE}}(\hat{X}_{\text{stego}})

(9)

The inversion process proceeds using a time-reversed Euler scheme:

\hat{x}_{t_{n}}=\hat{x}_{t_{n+1}}-\Delta t\cdot f(\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(x_{t_{n+1}},t_{n+1},P,w),t_{n+1})

(10)

After
N
steps, the reconstructed latent variable
\hat{x}_{0}
is obtained.
To ensure that the inversion process retraces the forward trajectory, PCLI enforces a
linear-consistency constraint
between adjacent steps:

f(\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(x_{t_{n}},t_{n},P,w),t_{n})=f(\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(x_{t_{n+1}},t_{n+1},P,w),t_{n+1})

(11)

This condition implies that the estimated directional field remains locally invariant, allowing the discretized trajectory to approximate a straight line in latent space. Under this constraint, the forward and reverse Euler updates become symmetric, thus ensuring that the numerical inversion remains accurate even in the presence of discretization. Combined with the Lipschitz continuity of
f
, this design guarantees bounded reconstruction error and promotes reliable message extraction.

Finally, the embedded message is extracted by applying the inverse of the mapping module:

\hat{M}=F^{-1}(\hat{x}_{0},K)

(12)

However, despite the path-wise reversibility guaranteed by the integration scheme, practical implementations may still suffer from numerical deviation due to unstable noise estimation by the sampler
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
. Specifically, at each time step
t_{n}
, the predicted noise direction
\hat{\epsilon}_{t_{n}}=\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(x_{t_{n}},t_{n})
deviates from the ideal residual
\epsilon_{t_{n}}^{*}
, leading to a local approximation error:

\delta_{t_{n}}=\|\hat{\epsilon}_{t_{n}}-\epsilon^{*}_{t_{n}}\|.

(13)

Over
N
steps of integration, these local errors accumulate and result in a total inversion deviation:

\|\hat{x}_{0}-x_{0}\|\leq C\cdot\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\delta_{t_{n}},

(14)

where
C
is a constant dependent on the Lipschitz bound of the transition function
f
.

Crucially, such errors often arise because traditional samplers (e.g., DDPM, DDIM) fail to generate noise estimates that are consistent with the linear-consistency constraint imposed by the PCLI. These samplers are typically trained with stochastic or heuristic objectives, which do not enforce temporal symmetry or path regularity. As a result, the forward and inversion trajectories become mismatched, breaking the path consistency condition and ultimately degrading the accuracy of message extraction particularly in scenarios with lossy distortions or limited sampling steps.

Rectified Flow Sampler

In this section, we introduce Rectified Flow (RF) as a principled alternative to traditional samplers, aiming to address their limitations in path consistency. We demonstrate that RF offers two key theoretical advantages over traditional samplers: (i) controllable integration error under discrete steps, and (ii) realizability of linear latent trajectories required by the PCLI framework.

Error Controllability.

Let the latent trajectory be generated via the first-order discretization:

x_{t_{n}}=x_{t_{n+1}}+\Delta t\cdot f(\hat{\epsilon}_{t_{n+1}},t_{n+1}),\quad\hat{\epsilon}_{t_{n+1}}=\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(x_{t_{n+1}},t_{n+1})

(15)

where
f
is a fixed transition function and
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
predicts the noise direction at each time step. The total deviation from the ideal trajectory accumulates from two sources: the sampling error in
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
and the discretization error of the ODE solver. Under the assumption that
f
is Lipschitz continuous, the error satisfies:

\|x_{0}-\hat{x}_{0}\|\leq C\cdot\Delta t+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)

(16)

where
\varepsilon
denotes the mean squared error of the learned sampler and
C
is a constant depending on the Lipschitz bound of
f
. Unlike traditional samplers whose accumulated errors grow with the number of steps, RF minimizes
\varepsilon
via direct residual regression and ensures bounded deviation along the trajectory.

Linear Trajectory Realizability.

Rectified Flow is trained to predict time-weighted residuals toward the initial latent state
x_{0}
. Its training objective is formulated as:

\min_{\theta}\,\mathbb{E}_{x_{0},x_{t},t}\left[\left\|\frac{x_{t}-x_{0}}{t}-\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\right\|^{2}\right]

(17)

This objective encourages
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
to approximate the mean velocity vector along the path from
x_{0}
to
x_{t}
, thereby inducing near-linear trajectories:

x_{t}\approx x_{0}+t\cdot\bar{v},\quad\text{where}\quad\bar{v}=\frac{x_{T}-x_{0}}{T}

(18)

Consequently, the predicted noise directions across all timesteps are nearly constant, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\approx\mathcal{S}_{\theta}(x_{t^{\prime}},t^{\prime}),\quad\forall t,t^{\prime}\in[0,T]

(19)

This aligns with the linear trajectory constraint required by PCLI, where the latent states evolve along a straight line with consistent directional updates. Unlike traditional samplers that generate nonlinear and time-varying transitions, Rectified Flow enforces directionally uniform noise predictions, which enables precise reconstruction of each previous state during inversion.

Within the RF-Stego, the Rectified Flow sampler is used at every time step in both the forward and inversion processes. Its core contributions lie in enabling smooth latent transitions and ensuring time-symmetric gradient consistency.

In the forward process, Rectified Flow predicts the residual noise direction
\hat{\epsilon}_{t_{n}}
for each latent state
x_{t_{n}}
, which is transformed into a state update via a transition function
f
. The learned vector field is globally regularized and directionally consistent, allowing smooth trajectory construction even under a small number of integration steps
N
. Compared to stochastic samplers or heuristically trained denoisers, RF produces stable updates that reduce latent drift and path curvature.

In the inversion process, RF’s learned time-symmetric field ensures that the predicted residuals at time
t_{n}
and its counterpart
T-t_{n}
are aligned, satisfying the PCLI consistency constraint without additional constraints. As a result, the recovered latent
\hat{x}_{0}
remains numerically close to the original
x_{0}
, even when the input image is corrupted by transmission noise. In practice, RF contributes to the RF-Stego in the following three aspects:

•

Stable Trajectory Construction:
Regular and consistent noise predictions lead to smooth and controllable latent paths.

•

High Accuracy with Low Step Counts:
Efficient sampling with fewer steps reduces computational cost while maintaining inversion precision.

•

Symmetric Inversion Support:
Time-aligned residuals facilitate deterministic recovery under the PCLI reversibility constraint.

In summary, beyond its theoretical reversibility, Rectified Flow provides concrete benefits that enhance accuracy, robustness and efficiency in generative steganography. It serves as a foundational component enabling the effectiveness of the proposed RF-Stego.

In this study, we adopt Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the base diffusion model which is trained on the LAION-5B dataset
(
Schuhmann et al. 2022
)
, and employ 2-Rectified Flow
(
Liu, Gong, and Liu 2022
)
as the noise sampler. Without any fine-tuning, we directly use the pretrained base model to ensure the generality and reproducibility of the experimental results. The main hyperparameters are set as follows: the latent space dimension is
L
=4×64×64, the latent channel number
C
=64, and the image height and width are
H
=
W
=512, with guidance scale
w
=1.25 and sampling steps
N
=20. The final image resolution=3×512×512. All experiments are conducted on NVIDIA RTX L40 GPU using PyTorch 2.3.1 and CUDA 11.8.

Evaluation Datasets

We conduct a systematic evaluation of the proposed method on several public datasets. Considering that the two baseline methods used in subsequent experiments target different image resolutions, we select datasets of varying resolutions for comprehensive comparison, including

Lin et al. 2014

)

Evaluation Metrics

Visual Quality
: Assessed using the Fréchet Inception Distance (FID) score, which measures the similarity between the distributions of generated images and real images in feature space. A lower FID score indicates better visual quality.

Extraction Accuracy
: Assuming that the message
M
and the extraction
\hat{M}
are equally long and follow a random bit distribution, we use the Hamming distance
dist(.,.)
to measure the extraction accuracy as follows:

acc=1-\frac{dist(M,\hat{M})}{L}

(20)

Robustness
: Assessed by measuring the variation in extraction accuracy under different distortion attacks, including:

•

Resize: Images are resized to 0.5×, 0.75×, 1.25×, and 1.5× their original sizes using bilinear interpolation to simulate resolution changes.

•

JPEG Compression: Evaluated at quality factors of 90, 70, and 50 to represent various degrees of compression degradation.

•

Median Blur: Median filtering with kernel sizes of 3×3, 5×5, and 7×7 to simulate mild, moderate, and severe smoothing distortions.

•

Gaussian Blur: Gaussian filtering with kernel sizes of 3×3, 5×5, and 7×7 to model different levels of blur attacks.

•

Gaussian Noise: Additive Gaussian noise with standard deviations of 0.01, 0.05, and 0.1 to simulate different noise intensities.

Security
: Refers to the ability of an adversary to distinguish between cover images and stego images. In our method, images generated from random noise are defined as cover images, while those generated from secret messages are defined as stego images. We utilize three steganalyzers, namely XuNet
(
Xu, Wu, and Shi 2016
)
, YeNet
(
Ye, Ni, and Yi 2017
)
, and SiaStegNet
(
You, Zhang, and Zhao 2020
)
, and use the detection error
P_{E}
to evaluate the security of steganographic methods, which is defined as follows,

P_{E}=\frac{P_{FA}+P_{MD}}{2}

(21)

where
P_{FA}
is the false alarm rate and
P_{MD}
is the missed detection rate.

Generation Efficiency
: We adopt per-image latency as a metric to evaluate generation efficiency, which is defined as the average time required to generate a single image throughput on a single GPU. This metric provides a straightforward and fair comparison of generation speed across different methods.

Ablation Study

To evaluate the effectiveness of RF sampler and the influence of different sampling parameters settings on extraction accuracy, we conducted a two-stage ablation study. First, we replaced the RF sampler with two widely used samplers (DDIM and DDPM) to investigate the effect of sampler choice on inversion performance. Then, under each of the three samplers, we independently examined how sampling steps and the classifier-free guidance (CFG) scale affect extraction accuracy. For consistency, all experiments adopt the embedding mechanism proposed in Baseline A.

Sampling Steps

In this experiment, the guidance scale is fixed at
w=1.25
for all samplers to ensure comparability. Table 4 illustrates the variations in message extraction accuracy across different sampling steps. The result shows that RF consistently outperforms both DDIM and DDPM across all sampling step settings. Notably, with RF, fewer sampling steps lead to higher extraction accuracy.
This counterintuitive result is attributed to the globally consistent vector field learned through straight-line supervision. While increasing the sampling steps theoretically reduces discretization error at the rate of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-p})

E(N)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}e_{i}+\mathcal{O}(N^{-p})
does not necessarily decrease monotonically and may even grow. Consequently, using a moderate or smaller sampling steps allows RF to better exploit its global consistency and suppress cumulative errors, resulting in more accurate inversion.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed RF-Stego, a novel generative image steganography method that significantly improves message extraction accuracy by performing accurate latent inversion. First, we designed PCLI to enforce path consistency by explicitly aligning the inversion process with the forward generation and constraining both directions to follow a shared linear trajectory, thereby effectively eliminating mismatch between the two paths. Second, we conducted rigorous theoretical analysis in theoretical reversibility and numerical stability. Then we introduce RF sampler addressing the issues of irreversibility and numerical instability. Extensive experiments show that RF-Stego consistently outperforms state-of-the-art methods across multiple metrics, including extraction accuracy, image quality, robustness, security, and generation efficiency.
