A multi-gene urinary extracellular vesicle mRNA panel as a non-invasive biomarkers for prostate cancer detection
Authors: Sreekanth Patnam, Aditya Koppula, Sarwareddy Kartik Kumar, Supriya Thalakanti, Rajesh V. Bendre, Sanjai Addla, Sasidhar Venkata Manda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prostate cancer (PCa) remains one of the most common malignancies in men older than 50 globally and in India. Although prostate-specific antigen (PSA) remains the standard diagnostic tool, its low specificity leads to overdiagnosis and unnecessary biopsies, highlighting the need for improved non-invasive biomarkers. Urinary extracellular vesicles (UEVs) have emerged as a promising, non-invasive source of molecular biomarkers, particularly RNA, for cancer detection. This study evaluated the diagnostic potential of six PCa associated genes (AGR2, ERG, PTEN, PCA3, AMACR, SPDEF) in UEVs.UEVs were isolated from 15 healthy individuals and 25 PCa patients via polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation and characterized by NTA, TEM and western blotting. Gene expression was quantified using qRT-PCR, and diagnostic performance was assessed using logistic regression models and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. Our results showed that all six genes (AGR2, ERG, PTEN, PCA3, AMACR and SPDEF) were significantly upregulated in PCa patients (p < 0.05) compared to healthy individuals. ROC analyses of these genes yielded area under the curve (AUC) of 0.821, 0.754, 0.746,0.746, 0.730, 0.722 respectively. A combined six-gene logistic model achieved an AUC of 0.96, while a simplified three-gene model1 (AGR2, SPDEF, PCA3) emerged as the most parsimonious model with an AUC of 0.954.Gene expression did not correlate with Gleason score or tumor stage, whereas PSA correlated with higher Gleason score (p = 0.01). These findings highlight the potential of UEV-derived multigene mRNA panels to serve as non-invasive biomarkers for PCa detection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47999-1

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) is the most diagnosed malignancy and remains a second leading cause of cancer-related mortality in men worldwide with an estimated 1.4 million new cases and over 396,792 deaths in 20221. In India, the incidence of PCa has been rising steadily, particularly in urban centers, due to aging demographics and lifestyle transitions. Unlike in Western countries where structured screening programs exist, diagnosis in India is often delayed, and patients frequently present at advanced stages, contributing to poor outcomes2,3. Early detection is critical, as curative treatment options are most effective for localized disease, whereas prognosis worsens significantly in advanced stages. Although, serum prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing remains the primary screening tool for PCa. However, its limited specificity and sensitivity, often lead to unnecessary biopsies, overdiagnosis and increased patient anxiety, as elevated PSA levels may also arise from benign conditions such as benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), prostatitis, or other non-malignant disorders4. These limitations have driven growing interest in non-invasive liquid biopsy markers, which can improve early diagnosis, guide treatment decisions without the discomfort and risks of traditional biopsies and offer advanced risk stratification tools to better inform biopsy decisions5. Urinary biomarkers have gained significant attention because prostate-derived cargo is naturally secreted and drained into urine, making it a reliable source for molecular profiling6. A review by M Salagierski et, al (2010) reported that urinary biomarkers such as prostate cancer antigen 3(PCA3) and transmembrane serine protease 2–v-ets erythroblastosis virus E26 oncogene homolog(TMPRSS2-ERG) along with serum PSA have demonstrated improved specificity. However, these markers have not replaced PSA in routine clinical practice7. Furthermore, T Borbiev et, al (2023) reviewed the commercial urine-based multiplexed biomarker tests, including SelectMDx and MyProstateScore (MPS), and reported that they have demonstrated moderate diagnostic performance with an AUC of 0.70–0.86, offer an advanced risk stratification tool to guide biopsy decisions, but concerns about reproducibility, validation across populations, and cost-effectiveness limit their widespread clinical adoption6,8–10. This underscores the need for robust, non-invasive, and multiplexed biomarker strategies that can enhance diagnostic accuracy and support risk stratification.

Urinary extracellular vesicles (UEVs) have emerged as a highly promising platform for biomarker discovery. UEVs are lipid bilayer nano vesicles, with the size range of 30–200 nm diameter, secreted by prostate and urogenital tract cells, and they carry molecular cargo RNA, DNA, and proteins that reflects the biological status of their tissue of origin11. The RNA within UEVs is protected by the lipid bilayer, remains stable in urine, and can be detected without the need for prostate massage. Moreover, the prostate directly drains into the urinary tract and the RNA content present in the UEVs tends to be prostate specific, which improves their utility for PCa detection12.

In line with these observation, Donovan MJ (2015) and P Motamedinia (2016) demonstrated that the urinary exosomal mRNAs including PCA3, T2: ERG, ERG and baculoviral IAP repeat-containing 5 (BIRC5) can aid in the detection of PCa.However, the molecular heterogeneity of PCa limits their reliability of single-gene assays, underscoring the need for multi-marker approaches13,14. The commercial multigene panel test such as ExoDx Prostate IntelliScore (EPI) uses a three-gene panel (PCA3, ERG, and SAM pointed domain containing Ets transcription factor (SPDEF) to stratify PCa aggressiveness and help avoid unnecessary biopsies15. Similarly, Gan et al. (2022) reported that the utility of a six-gene urinary exosomal mRNA panel (ERG, PCA3, ARV7(Androgen receptor splice variant 7), PSMA(Prostate-specific membrane antigen), CK19(cytokeratin 19), and EpCAM (Epithelial cell adhesion molecule) as non-invasive biomarkers and able to distinguish PCa patients from healthy individuals(HI)16. These findings support the use of multi-marker RNA panels to improve diagnostic precision and address inter-patient variability6. However, comprehensive evaluation of such panels in urinary exosomes remains limited, and the optimal transcript combination for maximal diagnostic performance has yet to be determined.

In this study, we systematically evaluated the diagnostic performance of a six-gene UEVs mRNA panel PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homolog), AMACR (Alpha-methylacyl-CoA racemase), ERG, AGR2 (Anterior gradient 2), SPDEF, and PCA3 selected based on their established roles in PCa biology and prior detection in tumor tissue or exosomes. PTEN, a tumor suppressor frequently lost in advanced PCa, is associated with tumor progression, treatment resistance, and poor prognosis, and therefore represents an important prognostic biomarker17. This genomic loss often leads to metabolic reprogramming, contributing to increased tumor aggressiveness18. In line with this, AMACR, an enzyme involved in β-oxidation of branched-chain fatty acids, is consistently overexpressed in PCa and serves as a robust diagnostic marker in tissue and urine19. Meanwhile, ERG, a transcription factor activated through the TMPRSS2–ERG fusion, is one of the most common genomic alterations in PCa and influences cell invasion and proliferation9,20,21. Additionally AGR2, a protein disulfide isomerase, is linked to epithelial differentiation but paradoxically promotes tumor growth, metastasis, and therapy resistance22. SPDEF, is an another ETS family transcription factor, plays a role in epithelial cell differentiation and has been associated with aggressive disease phenotypes23. Finally, PCA3 gene, a prostate-specific long noncoding RNA, is highly overexpressed in PCa compared to benign tissue and forms the basis of an FDA-approved urine test24.

Therefore, based on the previous reports, in our study, our objective is to assess the individual and combined diagnostic utility of these mRNAs (PTEN, AMACR, ERG, AGR2, SPDEF, and PCA3) and to develop logistic regression models capable of delivering a clinically applicable, non-invasive multigene panel. This approach has the potential to support early PCa detection while reducing unnecessary biopsies and improving clinical decisions.

Materials and methods

Study population

A total of 40 male participants were recruited in this study, comprising 25 patients with histologically confirmed PCa and 15 healthy individuals (HI) as controls. All participants were recruited from a single-center, Apollo Cancer Institute, Apollo Hospitals, Jubilee Hills, between May 2022 and September 2024. The study protocol was reviewed approved by the Apollo Hospitals Ethics Committee (AHJ-ACD-071/08–21), and informed consent was obtained before sample collection from each participant. The Urine samples were collected from the patients undergoing clinical evaluation and biopsy for PCa diagnosis. The patients who were unwilling to provide informed consent, those with biopsy negative PCa, and individuals diagnosed with other malignancies were excluded from the study. Diagnosis was confirmed through histopathological analysis, and clinical staging was performed according to the Tumor-Node-Metastasis (TNM) classification by clinical pathologist. The healthy control group consisted of men aged above 55 years with no known history of prostate disease, cancer, or lower urinary tract symptoms, and with normal serum PSA levels within the normal reference range at the time of recruitment. All participants were clearly informed about the purpose of the study and their right to withdraw at any stage without any repercussions. Urine samples were labelled to maintain anonymity, and no personally identifiable information was included in the data. All experimental procedures were conducted in compliance with applicable ethical guidelines and regulations. Clinical and demographic data, including age, PSA levels, prostate volume, Gleason score, and TNM stage (for PCa patients), were collected from patient records and used for descriptive and comparative analysis.

Urinary extracellular vesicles (UEV) isolation using polyethylene glycol (PEG) precipitation

Urine derived EVs were isolated using the optimized PEG based protocol developed by AD Singh et al., 202325. Briefly, the urine samples were collected from all participants in aseptic urine collection containers and processed within 2 h of collection to preserve EVs integrity. Samples were centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to remove cells and debris. The supernatant was transferred to fresh tubes for downstream processing.

To recover vesicles bound to the urinary protein matrix, the pellet was treated with 100 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. The DTT-treated solution was then combined with the cleared urine supernatant. For Precipitation, PEG 6000 was added to the urine-DTT mixture to a final concentration of 12% (w/v). The solution was mixed gently and incubated overnight at 4 °C to allow vesicle aggregation. Following incubation, samples were centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C to pellet the UEVs. The UEV pellet was dissolved in 1×PBS and stored at -80 °C for further downstream analysis.

Characterization of UEVs

The isolated UEVs were characterized for their size, morphology, and the presence of EV markers to confirm their integrity.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

The UEVs were evaluated for their size and concentration using the ZetaView PMX 110 (Particle Metrix, Germany) with ZetaView software. The instrument was calibrated using 100 nm polystyrene beads (Applied Microspheres) in distilled water, as per manufacturer instructions. One mL of UEVs sample was diluted in 1× PBS to get an optimal detection range of 50–200 particles per frame and injected into the analysis chamber. Measurements were conducted at 11 pre-defined positions, with 30 frames captured per position across three technical replicates. The shutter speed was set to 100, and sensitivity settings were adjusted to 80 for small particles and 65 for larger particles. All measurements were performed at a constant temperature of 25°C, and the cell was thoroughly rinsed with HPLC grade water between each sample to prevent cross-contamination.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Following NTA analysis for size and concentration, UEVs were further characterized to assess their morphology and size using TEM.Briefly, resuspended UEVs were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde, loaded onto carbon-coated copper grids, negatively stained with 1% uranyl acetate, and imaged using a transmission electron microscope (JEM-2100 TEM)) operating at 80 kV.

Total UEV protein lysate preparation and quantification

The isolated UEVs were lysed using 1X RIPA buffer with a protease inhibitor (Roche, Switzerland) and the lysate passed 10–15 times through a 25G needle using a 1 ml syringe and incubated 15 min on ice. Followed incubation the sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 12 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant containing the protein lysate was collected. Protein concentration was estimated using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a TECAN GENios Pro plate reader (TECAN Group Ltd, Männedorf, Switzerland).

Western blotting

Protein lysates (40 µg) obtained from C-UEVs and PC-UEVs were resolved on 12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad, USA) was blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk in 1× TBST and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against CD63, CD81, and HSP70 (Abcam, UK; 1:2000). After washing with TBST, incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Abcam, UK; 1: 5000) for 1 h at RT. The membranes were visualized using a chemiluminescent detection reagent (Bio-Rad, USA), and the resulting signals were imaged with the ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad, USA).

RNA isolation

Total RNA was extracted from UEVs using TRIzol™ LS Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The extracted RNA was subsequently quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

cDNA conversion

First strand Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesised using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 500 ng of total RNA as per manufacturers protocol. The resulting cDNA was stored at − 20 °C until quantitative PCR analysis.

Quantitative real-time PCR

One microliter (1 µL) of cDNA was used to pre-amplify the target genes, including both prostate cancer-specific genes (PCA3, AMACR, SPDEF, AGR2, ERG, PTEN) and the housekeeping gene (B2M), using SapphireAmp Fast PCR Master Mix (Takara Bio, USA). All primer sequences, except for SPDEF, were designed using the NCBI primer designing tool (Primer3) and their specificity was verified by NCBI Primer-BLAST. The SPDEF primer sequences were obtained from the PrimerBank database (PrimerBank ID: 356582512c1)26. The details of the primer sequences and PCR cycling parameters are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Subsequently, 1 µL of the 1:10 diluted pre-amplified PCR product was used as the template for RT-qPCR using the above-mentioned primers and cycling conditions on the Applied Biosystems 7500 Real-Time PCR.Each reaction contained 5 µL of SYBR® Premix Ex Taq II (ROX plus; Takara Bio USA, Inc.), 0.15 µL of each 10 µM primer (BioServe), and 1 µL of the diluted cDNA template. All reactions were performed in triplicate, along with non-template controls (NTCs). Gene expression levels were normalized to Beta-2-Microglobulin (B2M) using the ΔCt method [ΔCt = Ct (gene of interest) – Ct (B2M)], where lower ΔCt values indicate higher gene expression16,27. ΔCt values were compared between control and prostate cancer groups to assess differential gene expression.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) and RStudio (Posit Software, Boston, MA, USA). Quantitative variables are summarised as means ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Gene expression levels were compared between PCa patients and HI using unpaired two-tailed t-tests or the Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the normality. To assess the diagnostic performance of individual and combined mRNA biomarkers, binary logistic models were constructed using combinations of the six candidate genes (ERG, PTEN, PCA3, AGR2, SPDEF, AMACR). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to estimate the area under the curve (AUC), Optimal cutoff (based on Youden’s index), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV).Associations between mRNA expression levels and clinical features Gleason score (≤ 7 vs. > 7) and tumour stage (T2 vsT3/T4) were evaluated using the Mann–Whitney U test or independent-sample t-test depending on data distribution. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed using the ‘dcurves’ R package to estimate the clinical net benefit and the reduction in unnecessary interventions across a range of decision thresholds. All The plots were generated using ‘ggplot2’ R package and GraphPad Prism 10.0 (violin plots). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all tests.

Results

Demographic and clinical characteristics

The study cohort included 40 male participants, of which 25 patients were histologically confirmed prostate cancer (PCa) and 15 were healthy individuals (controls). The clinicopathological details of the PCa patients and HI are provided in Table 1. The median age of PCa patients was 72 years and is higher than that of the control group, which had a median age of 59 years (p = 0.001). This age difference reflects the well-established age-associated risk in prostate cancer. The serum PSA levels were significantly higher in PCa patients with a median of 16.50 ng/mL, compared with the control group, which had a median PSA of 0.78 ng/mL (p = 0.0001). These findings are consistent with the clinical utility of PSA as a biomarker, despite its known limitations in specificity.

Within the PCa group, Gleason scores ranged from 6 to 9. The majority of the patients (n = 15) had a Gleason score of 7, while 3 patients had a score of 6, 5 had a score of 8, and 2 patients had a score of 9. This distribution suggests that most participants had intermediate-grade tumors, with a subset presenting with high-grade disease. Tumor staging, based on the TNM classification, revealed that 10 patients were at stage T2, 12 at stage T3, and 3 at stage T4. This range includes both organ-confined and locally advanced disease, making the cohort suitable for evaluating biomarkers across clinically relevant PCa stages (Table 1).

Characterization of isolated UEVs

The purity of the isolated UEVs was validated using a combination of biophysical, biochemical, and surface marker analyses. NTA revealed that the UEVs had a diameter ranging from 30 to 200 nm (Fig. 1A,C). TEM further validated their morphology, revealing intact, round, double-membraned vesicles measuring between 30 and 150 nm (Fig. 1B,D). Western blotting demonstrated the presence of EV associated markers CD81, CD63, and HSP70, confirming the identity and purity of the isolated vesicles (Fig. 1E). These findings confirm the purity of our isolated UEV preparation based on size, structural integrity, and protein marker expression.

Differential expression of PTEN, PCA3, ERG, AMACR, AGR2, and SPDEF in HI and PCa patients

To evaluate the diagnostic relevance of UEV RNA, the expression levels of six candidate mRNAs such as PTEN, PCA3, ERG, AMACR, AGR2, and SPDEF were quantified in urinary UEVs from HI and PCa patients. Our analysis revealed that all six mRNAs were significantly upregulated in the PCa group compared to healthy controls (Fig. 2). We observed significant upregulation of PTEN (p = 0.0047), ERG (p = 0.0069), PCA3 (p = 0.0088), AMACR (p = 0.0150), SPDEF (p = 0.0191), and AGR2 (p = 0.0005) in PCa patients relative to HI. Among these, AGR2 showed the most pronounced upregulation (p = 0.0005; Fig. 2E), indicating strong discriminatory potential.

Diagnostic performance of UEVs derived mRNAs

The differential expression of PTEN, AMACR, ERG, AGR2, SPDEF, and PCA3 in UEVs from PCa patients versus HI underscores their potential as biomarkers for PCa diagnosis. We further conducted ROC curve analysis to determine their utility as predictive biomarkers for PCa diagnosis. The ROC analysis in Fig. 3; Table 2 reveals that all six mRNAs (PTEN, AMACR, ERG, AGR2, SPDEF, and PCA3) demonstrated AUC values of 0.746, 0.730, 0.754, 0.821,0.722 and 0.746 respectively, indicating their potential as reliable predictors of PCa. Among them, AGR2 demonstrated the highest diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.8213 (95% CI: 0.6749–0.9678). At its optimal cut-off value of −2.1313, AGR2 achieved 80.0% accuracy, 80.0% sensitivity, 80.0% specificity, a positive predictive value (PPV) of 88%, and a negative predictive value (NPV) of 70.6%, indicating a strong predictive capacity (82.1%) to differentiate PCa patients from HI. These findings (Fig. 3; Table 2) highlight AGR2 as a strong candidate for PCa detection. The AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV values of the other markers are shown in Table 2.

Predictive model performance of combined gene panels

Building on the diagnostic utility of individual markers, we next evaluated the combined predictive power of these UEV derived mRNAs using logistic regression modelling to enhance diagnostic precision. ROC curve analysis of the full six-gene model including PTEN, AMACR, ERG, AGR2, SPDEF, and PCA3 revealed superior diagnostic performance over individual genes, with an AUC of 0.96 (95% CI: 0.9075 −1.00). At its optimal cut-off value > 1.0413, the full model achieved 92.5% accuracy, 88.0% sensitivity, 100% specificity, a PPV of 100%, and a NPV of 83.3%, this highlights the model robustness in identifying both true positives and true negatives (Fig. 4; Table 3).

To further simplify the gene panel for potential clinical application, we developed two partial models using a parsimonious method that reduced the number of transcripts while preserving predictive power. Partial model 1, A three-gene panel comprising AGR2, SPDEF, and PCA3 yielded an AUC of 0.954 (95% CI: 0.8992–1.8992). At its optimal cut-off value of > 0.600, the performance metrics were as follows: sensitivity (88%), specificity (93.3%), PPV (95.7%), NPV (82.4%%), and accuracy (90%). similarly, another three-gene partial model 2 using ERG, SPDEF, and PCA3 achieved an AUC of 0.9093 (95% CI: (0.8169–1.8169). At an optimal cut of value of > 0.610, this model demonstrated 87.5% accuracy, 88.0% sensitivity, 87% specificity, a PPV of 91.7% %, and a NPV of 81.3%%. Among 3 models the full model exhibited the highest overall predictive performance compared with both partial models. The partial model 1 (AGR2-SPDEF-PCA3) may offer a practical balance between diagnostic efficiency and clinical feasibility. These results demonstrate that multigene models able to distinguish PCa from HI and significantly enhance diagnostic potential compared with individual transcripts, laying the foundation for developing a urinary non-invasive multigene molecular test for PCa detection.

Association between tumor stage and biomarker expression

To assess whether these UEV RNA biomarkers correlate with PCa clinical stage, PCa patients were categorised into two groups based on tumor stage- early-stage T2 and advanced-stage (T3/T4). Across all six biomarkers, none exhibited statistically significant differences between the two groups (Supplementary Table 2). However, PSA displayed an increasing trend in median values from early to advanced stage (p = 0.05528), consistent with its known association with tumor burden. Age did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.9556), indicating minimal confounding. Overall, these results suggest that no UEV RNA biomarkers demonstrated statistically significant associations with tumor stage in this cohort.

Association between gleason score and biomarker expression

Following the tumor stage analysis, we further assessed the association between biomarker expression levels and Gleason score by comparing patients with low-grade PCa (Gleason score ≤ 7) and high-grade PCa (Gleason score > 7) (SupplementaryTable-3). No statistically significant differences were observed in the expression of PTEN, AMACR, ERG, AGR2, SPDEF, or PCA3 between the two groups (p > 0.4) for all the genes. In contrast, serum PSA levels were significantly higher in the high-grade group compared to the low-grade group (p = 0.010). Patient age did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.6487).

Discussion

Serum PSA remains the most widely used biomarker for prostate cancer (PCa) detection; however, its low specificity due to elevated levels in both malignant and benign conditions limits its diagnostic reliability4. These shortcomings highlight the urgent need for more accurate and disease-specific biomarkers to improve early detection. Previous studies have demonstrated that differential mRNA expression patterns in prostate tissues can effectively distinguish cancerous from non-cancerous states28,29. In parallel, urinary biomarkers have emerged as a non-invasive and informative alternative to serum PSA.The urinary mRNA-based tests such as Progenase PCA3 test (PCA3 and PSA), My prostate score (Serum PSA and urinary PCA3, TMPRSS2: ERG) and Select MDx (DCX1, HOXC6 along with serum PSA, DRE, Age, and family history) offering improved specificity and specificity over PSA alone to reduce the unnecessary biopsies for PCa diagnosis8,9,30. Despite this progress, the diagnostic potential of urinary mRNA markers remain variable due to differences in analytical methods, pre-analytical handling factors, inherent instability of RNA, and limited validation across diverse populations, which can alter the measured RNA profile6,31,32.

UEVs overcome many of these limitations by protecting their RNA cargo within their lipid bilayer, which prevents degradation, maintains molecular stability and offer more accurate representation of the disease state11. UEVs derived mRNA expression has been explored as non-invasive biomarker for various diseases including PCa16,33–35. In support of this, a commercially available UEVs based multigene test (ExoDx Prostate) comprising of (ERG, PCA3,and SPDEF) has demonstrated the ability to determine the risk of high grade prostate cancer at initial biopsy to avoid unnecessary biopsies15. However, these biomarker performance can vary across different populations due to genetic, demographic, and environmental differences6. Additionally, high costs and limited availability in different regions pose significant barriers in low-resource regions, highlighting the need for population-specific, affordable diagnostics.

To address this gap, we investigated a panel of six UEV-derived mRNAs such as PTEN, AMACR, ERG, AGR2, SPDEF, and PCA3 in an Indian cohort of PCa patients and healthy individuals, with the aim of developing a simple qRT-PCR–based assay that could improve PCa diagnosis and prediction. UEVs were isolated using a precipitation-based method. Our data revealed that the isolated EVs were below 200 nm in diameter and exhibited the typical spherical morphology with a bilayer lipid membrane. western blot analysis further validated for the detection of EV-specific markers, including CD63, HSP70, and TSG101.These findings confirm the integrity of the UEVs and suitability for downstream applications and are consistent with previous reports25,36.

In our study, we observed significant upregulation of PTEN, AMACR, ERG, AGR2, SPDEF, and PCA3 in the UEVs of PCa group, which is in line with previous studies reported increased expression of these genes in UEVs and prostate tissue from PCa patients compared with healthy individuals16,17,19,23,24,37. The diagnostic potential to identify the predictive power of these genes (PTEN, AMACR, ERG, AGR2, SPDEF, and PCA3) was assessed using the area under ROC curve analysis. Among AGR2 found to be highly predictive biomarker of PCa detection. Remaining markers demonstrated moderate diagnostic performance with AUC of 0.7–0.8. To further improve predictive power of the six genes, we combined as a panel to distinguish PCa patients from healthy individuals. Overall, the combined six gene panel yielded a high predictive power (AUC 0.96) with a sensitivity of (88%) and a specificity of (100%), thus suggesting the utility of multi-gene panel as non-invasive diagnostic biomarkers for PCa detection. Although all the six biomarkers independently showed robust positive PPV but limited NPV in predicting PCa diagnosis, but their integration enhances overall precision, effectively distinguishing PCa patients from healthy individuals. Furthermore, although these gene expression levels were expected to be associated with Gleason score and tumor stage based on previous studies23,38–41, no such association was observed in our cohort, likely due to the limited sample size.

To further enhance real world clinical applicability of the gene panel by reducing the number of genes, we developed two partial gene models. Among two partial models, Model 1 (AGR2, SPDEF, PCA3) achieved high predictive power with an AUC of 0.95 sensitivity of (88%) and a specificity of (93.3%), outperforming over previously reported urinary single and multigene biomarker panels15,16. Whereas Model 2 (PCA3, ERG, SPDEF) showed lower predictive power with an AUC of 0.90, sensitivity of (88%) and a specificity of (87%), Although the combined gene panel demonstrated high predictive power, The partial Model 1 offers a more practical and cost-efficient diagnostic option with improved accuracy.

In addition, we have evaluated the net clinical benefit of these models to minimize the unnecessary biopsies by decision curve analysis. DCA shows that both the combined six gene panel and partial Model 1 yielded higher net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities compared to PSA alone, as well as the “treat-all” and “treat-none” strategies. Notably, partial Model 1 showed the greatest clinical utility, further supporting its potential to reduce unnecessary biopsies by maintaining high detection rates for clinically significant PCa.

The strong predictive power of partial Model 1 underscores the combined diagnostic potential of AGR2, SPDEF, and PCA3,consistent with their established roles in prostate cancer progression and metastasis38,42,43. These findings suggest that integrating multiple urinary EV-derived transcripts as a panel may overcome the limitations of individual markers and offer a more robust, non-invasive approach for prostate cancer detection.

Although our study reveals promising findings on the potential use of UEVs derived mRNA gene panels as biomarkers for PCa diagnosis, it bears several limitations that need to be considered. Our study was conducted on limited patient’s samples for the establishment of their diagnostic potential, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Expanding the sample size in future studies would improve the applicability of these findings in diverse patient populations. The single-centre design and absence of longitudinal follow-up also prevented us from assessing biomarker stability over time or determining whether the gene panel can differentiate between aggressive and indolent disease. Additionally, while the study included HI as controls, the absence of BPH samples for comparative analysis represents a limitation, as it restricts the evaluation of the panel’s specificity in distinguishing prostate cancer from clinically relevant non-malignant conditions. Future large-scale, multi-institutional studies with longitudinal monitoring are required to validate these gene models further and explore their prognostic potential. Inclusion of BPH cohorts will be important to assess the specificity of the panel in distinguishing PCa from benign conditions. Additionally, integrating these markers into rapid, point-of-care molecular platforms may also offer a cost-effective screening strategy, particularly in resource-limited settings. Overall, our study provides a proof-of-concept that a multi-gene urinary EV mRNA panel offers superior diagnostic accuracy compared with individual markers which may aid in reducing unnecessary biopsies for prostate cancer detection.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that urinary extracellular vesicles represent a reliable and minimally invasive source of molecular biomarkers for prostate cancer detection. Evaluation of six prostate-associated mRNA transcripts showed that both individual and combined markers can discriminate prostate cancer patients from healthy individuals. Notably, a simplified three-gene panel comprising AGR2, SPDEF, and PCA3 achieved superior diagnostic accuracy compared with other models, supporting its clinical applicability. Although the study is limited by its modest sample size and single-centre design, these findings provide proof-of-concept evidence for the utility of UEV-derived mRNA panels as non-invasive diagnostic tools that may enhance early detection and reduce unnecessary biopsies. Validation in larger, multi-institutional cohorts is warranted.
