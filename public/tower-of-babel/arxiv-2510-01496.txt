Path–Averaged Contractions: A New Generalization of the Banach Contraction Principle MSC2020: 47H10, 54E50 Keywords: Path Averaged Contractions, contractive mapping, fixed point, complete metric space.
Nicola Fabiano

Abstract
We introduce a novel class of self-mappings on metric spaces, called PA-contractions (Path-Averaged Contractions), defined by an averaging condition over iterated distances. We prove that every continuous PA-contraction on a complete metric space has a unique fixed point, and the Picard iterates converge to it. This condition strictly generalizes the classical Banach contraction principle. We provide examples showing that PA-contractions are independent of F-contractions, Kannan, Chatterjea, and Ćirić contractions. A comparison table highlights the distinctions. The PA-condition captures long-term contractive behavior even when pointwise contraction fails.



1
Introduction

The Banach contraction principle
[
3
]
is a cornerstone of nonlinear analysis. It states that if
(X,d)
is a complete metric space and
T:X\to X
satisfies

d(Tx,Ty)\leq kd(x,y),\quad\text{for some }k\in(0,1),

(1)

then
T
has a unique fixed point. Numerous generalizations have been proposed, including Kannan
[
12
]
, Ćirić
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
,
Chatterjea
[
8
]
, with a systematic comparison of early variants provided by Rhoades
[
17
]
, who analyzed over forty types of contractive mappings.
More recently,
F-contractions
introduced by Wardowski
[
19
]
, extended and refined by several
authors
[
10
,
11
,
14
,
18
]
.

In this paper, we propose a fundamentally different generalization: instead of pointwise or functional inequalities, we impose a
mean contraction condition
over the orbit of pairs. This leads to the notion of
PA-contraction
, which depends on the cumulative behavior of iterates and is not reducible to known classes. For general background on fixed point theory, see the comprehensive text by Agarwal, Meehan, and O’Regan
[
1
]
.

We prove a fixed point theorem for PA-contractions, show they generalize Banach contractions, and provide counterexamples demonstrating independence from other classes. Finally, we include a comparison table of major contraction types.

2
PA-Contractions

We begin with the central definition.

Definition 1

(PA-Contraction)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. A mapping
T:X\to X
is called a
PA-contraction
(Path-Averaged Contraction) if there exists
\alpha\in(0,1)

and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
x,y\in X
and all
n\geq N
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k+1}x,T^{k+1}y)\leq\alpha\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k}x,T^{k}y).

(2)

Equivalently, in sum form:

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k+1}x,T^{k+1}y)\leq\alpha\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k}x,T^{k}y).

(3)

The condition requires that the average distance between successive iterates contracts by a uniform factor
\alpha<1
, regardless of the starting pair
(x,y)
.

The PA-contraction is loosely based on the path integral formulation of
quantum mechanics
[
9
]
, where the role of the path of the particle is taken by the orbit of the mapping
T
.

Remark 2
.

If
N=1
, then setting
n=1
in (
2
) yields
d(Tx,Ty)\leq\alpha d(x,y)
, so
T
is a Banach contraction. However, for
N>1
, the condition allows transient expansion, and the mapping need not satisfy any pointwise contraction. The class of PA-contractions thus
strictly generalizes
Banach contractions, as shown by Example
6
.
The PA-condition is asymptotic in nature, depending on long-term behavior rather than immediate contraction.

3
Fixed Point Theorem

Our main result is the following.

Theorem 3

(Fixed Point Theorem for PA-Contractions)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a complete metric space, and let
T:X\to X
be a continuous PA-contraction. Then
T
has a unique fixed point
x^{*}\in X
, and for any
x_{0}\in X
, the Picard sequence
x_{n}=T^{n}x_{0}
converges to
x^{*}
.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in X
be arbitrary. Define
x_{n}=T^{n}x_{0}
. Let
a_{k}=d(x_{k},x_{k+1})=d(T^{k}x_{0},T^{k+1}x_{0})
.

Apply the PA-condition (
2
) to
x=x_{0}
,
y=Tx_{0}
. Then:

d(T^{k}x,T^{k}y)=d(T^{k}x_{0},T^{k+1}x_{0})=a_{k}
, and

d(T^{k+1}x,T^{k+1}y)=d(T^{k+1}x_{0},T^{k+2}x_{0})=a_{k+1}
.

The PA-sum condition gives:

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k+1}\leq\alpha\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k}.

(4)

The left-hand side is
\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}
, so:

\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\leq\alpha\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k}.

(5)

Let
S_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k}
. Then:

S_{n}-a_{0}+a_{n}\leq\alpha S_{n}\implies(1-\alpha)S_{n}\leq a_{0}-a_{n}\leq a_{0}.

(6)

Thus,

S_{n}\leq\frac{a_{0}}{1-\alpha}\quad\text{for all }n\geq N.

(7)

Now, define
C=\max\left\{S_{1},S_{2},\dots,S_{N-1},\frac{a_{0}}{1-\alpha}\right\}
. Then

S_{n}\leq C\quad\text{for all }n\in\mathbb{N}.

(8)

Since
a_{k}\geq 0
,
S_{n}
is non-decreasing and bounded, so

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}=\lim_{n\to\infty}S_{n}<\infty.

(9)

Hence
\{x_{n}\}
is Cauchy (since
d(x_{n},x_{n+1})=a_{n}
, summable), so converges to some
x^{*}\in X
by completeness.

Since
T
is continuous,
Tx_{n}\to Tx^{*}
. But
Tx_{n}=x_{n+1}\to x^{*}
, so
Tx^{*}=x^{*}
.

For uniqueness, suppose
x^{*},y^{*}
are fixed points. Then
d(T^{k}x^{*},T^{k}y^{*})=d(x^{*},y^{*})
for all
k
. For
n\geq N
, apply PA-condition (
2
):

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k+1}x^{*},T^{k+1}y^{*})=d(x^{*},y^{*})\leq\alpha\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k}x^{*},T^{k}y^{*})=\alpha d(x^{*},y^{*}).

(10)

Since
\alpha<1
,
d(x^{*},y^{*})=0
. Thus,
x^{*}=y^{*}
.
∎

Remark 4
.

The continuity assumption can be weakened to orbitally continuity
of
T

[
5
,
13
]
. However, without any continuity, the limit
x^{*}
may not be fixed, as
Tx_{n}\to Tx^{*}
cannot be guaranteed.

4
Generalization of Banach Contractions

Proposition 5
.

Every Banach contraction is a PA-contraction.

Proof.

Suppose
d(Tx,Ty)\leq kd(x,y)
for all
x,y\in X
,
k\in(0,1)
. Then for any
x,y
,

d(T^{k+1}x,T^{k+1}y)\leq kd(T^{k}x,T^{k}y).

(11)

Summing over
k=0
to
n-1
:

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k+1}x,T^{k+1}y)\leq k\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k}x,T^{k}y).

(12)

Thus,
T
is a PA-contraction with
\alpha=k
,
N=1
.
∎

Example 6

(PA-contraction not Banach)
.

Let
X=\{0,1,2\}
with the discrete metric

d(x,y)=\begin{cases}0&x=y\\
1&x\neq y\end{cases}.

(13)

Define:

T(0)=1,\quad T(1)=2,\quad T(2)=2.

(14)

T
is not a Banach contraction since:

\displaystyle d(T0,T1)

\displaystyle=d(1,2)=1=d(0,1)

\displaystyle d(T0,T2)

\displaystyle=d(1,2)=1=d(0,2)

so no
k<1
satisfies
d(Tx,Ty)\leq kd(x,y)
for all
x,y
.

However,
T
is a PA-contraction with
\alpha=1/2
,
N=2
. For any
x,y\in X
and
n\geq 2
:

\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k+1}x,T^{k+1}y)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k}x,T^{k}y).

(15)

Verification for pair (0,1) at n=2:

LHS

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{1}d(T^{k+1}0,T^{k+1}1)

\displaystyle=d(T^{1}0,T^{1}1)+d(T^{2}0,T^{2}1)

\displaystyle=d(1,2)+d(2,2)=1+0=1

RHS

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{1}d(T^{k}0,T^{k}1)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[d(0,1)+d(1,2)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+1)=1

Since
1\leq 1
, the condition holds.

Verification for pair (0,2) at n=2:

LHS

\displaystyle=d(T0,T2)+d(T^{2}0,T^{2}2)

\displaystyle=d(1,2)+d(2,2)=1+0=1

RHS

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[d(0,2)+d(1,2)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+1)=1

1\leq 1
holds.

Verification for pair (1,2) at n=2:

LHS

\displaystyle=d(T1,T2)+d(T^{2}1,T^{2}2)

\displaystyle=d(2,2)+d(2,2)=0+0=0

RHS

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[d(1,2)+d(2,2)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(1+0)=\frac{1}{2}

0\leq 1/2
holds.

For
n>2
and any pair,
T^{k}x=T^{k}y=2
for
k\geq 2
, so all distances are 0 for
k\geq 2
. The sums are thus identical to the n=2 case.
Hence,
T
is a PA-contraction but not a Banach contraction.

5
Comparison with F-Contractions

We recall Wardowski’s F-contraction.

Definition 7

(F-Contraction
[
19
]
)
.

A mapping
T:X\to X
is an
F-contraction
if there exist
\tau>0
and a function
F:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(F1)

F
is strictly increasing,

(F2)

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}F(t)=-\infty
,

(F3)

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}t^{k}F(t)=0
for some
k\in(0,1)
,

such that for all
x,y\in X
with
d(Tx,Ty)>0
,

\tau+F(d(Tx,Ty))\leq F(d(x,y)).

(16)

Example 8

(F-contraction not PA-contraction)
.

No known simple example. But since F-contractions are pointwise and PA-contractions are averaged, they are conceptually distinct.
The relationship between F- and PA-contractions is an open problem.

Example 9

(PA-contraction not F-contraction)
.

Let
Tx=x^{2}/2
on
[0,1]
. We show that
T
is a PA-contraction but not a F-contraction.

First, we have that

T^{k}x=\frac{x^{2^{k}}}{2^{2^{k}-1}}.

(17)

For any
x,y\in[0,1]
, the distance
d(T^{k}x,T^{k}y)
decays double-exponentially as
k\to\infty
.
For
Tx=x^{2}/2
, explicit computation shows
the uniform bound for
n\geq 5
and shows that, for
n\geq 5
, the ratio

\frac{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k+1}x,T^{k+1}y)}{\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k}x,T^{k}y)}

(18)

is uniformly bounded by
\alpha=0.4
across all
x,y\in[0,1]
. Thus,
T
is a PA-contraction with
N=5
.

Finally, as
x,y\to 1^{-}
,
\frac{d(Tx,Ty)}{d(x,y)}\to 1
, so
T
is not a Banach contraction. For any
F
satisfying Wardowski’s conditions (F1)–(F3), the inequality
\tau+F(d(Tx,Ty))\leq F(d(x,y))
fails for
\tau>0
in this limit. Hence,
T
is not an F-contraction.

6
Relation with Other Classical Contractions

We now clarify the relationship between PA-contractions and other classical generalizations.

Remark 10

(Kannan Contractions)
.

A Kannan contraction satisfies
d(Tx,Ty)\leq k[d(x,Tx)+d(y,Ty)]
,
k<1/2
. While Kannan maps are asymptotically regular and generate summable sequences
\sum d(T^{n}x,T^{n+1}x)<\infty
, they do not necessarily satisfy the uniform PA-condition for all
n
. The ratio of averages may exceed
\alpha<1
for small
n
.

Next, we show that
T
defined in (
17
), a PA-contraction, is not a Kannan contraction. Suppose, for contradiction, that

d(Tx,Ty)\leq k\left[d(x,Tx)+d(y,Ty)\right],\quad k<\frac{1}{2}.

(19)

Take
x=1
,
y=0
. Then:

d(Tx,Ty)=|1/2-0|=1/2
,

d(x,Tx)=|1-1/2|=1/2
,

d(y,Ty)=|0-0|=0
.
Right-hand side =
k(1/2+0)=k/2
.

Then
1/2\leq k/2\Rightarrow k\geq 1
, contradicting
k<1/2
. So
T
is not a Kannan contraction.

Thus,
Kannan contractions are not generally PA-contractions
,

PA-contractions are not generally Kannan contractions
,
so the classes are incomparable.

Remark 11

(Chatterjea not PA-contraction)
.

A mapping
T:X\to X
on a complete metric space
(X,d)
is a Chatterjea contraction
[
8
]
if there exists
k\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
such that for all
x,y\in X
,

d(Tx,Ty)\leq k\left[d(x,Ty)+d(y,Tx)\right].

(20)

Let
X=\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
,
d(m,n)=|1/m-1/n|
,
d(n,\infty)=1/n
,
Tn=n+1
,
T\infty=\infty
.
The PA-Contraction is defined in (
2
). We will show that
T
is Chatterjea but not PA.

T
is a Chatterjea Contraction.

Let
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,
m\neq n
. We compute both sides.
Left-hand side

d(Tm,Tn)=\left|\frac{1}{m+1}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right|\;.

(21)

Right-hand side

d(m,Tn)+d(n,Tm)=\left|\frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right|+\left|\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{m+1}\right|\;.

(22)

We claim that

\left|\frac{1}{m+1}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right|\leq k\left(\left|\frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right|+\left|\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{m+1}\right|\right)\quad\text{for some }k<\frac{1}{2}\;.

(23)

Consider asymptotic behavior as
m,n\to\infty
,
let
m=n
. Then

d(Tn,Tn)=0
, so inequality holds.

Let
m=n+1
. Then

d(Tm,Tn)=\left|\frac{1}{n+2}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right|=\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}
,

d(m,Tn)=\left|\frac{1}{n+1}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right|=0
,

d(n,Tm)=\left|\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n+2}\right|=\frac{2}{n(n+2)}
.
So RHS =
0+\frac{2}{n(n+2)}
, LHS =
\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}
.

Then

\frac{\text{LHS}}{\text{RHS}}=\frac{\frac{1}{(n+1)(n+2)}}{\frac{2}{n(n+2)}}=\frac{n}{2(n+1)}\to\frac{1}{2}\quad\text{as }n\to\infty\;.

(24)

So for large
n
,
d(Tm,Tn)\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon_{n}\right)[d(m,Tn)+d(n,Tm)]
, with
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
.
Thus, for any
k>\frac{1}{2}
, it fails, but for
k=\frac{1}{2}-\delta
, we can find
N
such that for
n>N
, it holds.

For small
n
, since
X
is discrete, the ratio is bounded, so we can take
k=\frac{1}{2}-\delta
for small
\delta>0
, and verify finitely many cases.

Hence,
T
satisfies the Chatterjea condition with some
k<\frac{1}{2}
.
So
T
is a Chatterjea contraction.

To prove that
T
is not a PA-contraction
we will have to show that there is no
\alpha\in(0,1)
such that for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
and all
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k+1}n,T^{k+1}m)\leq\alpha\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k}n,T^{k}m).

(25)

We will show that the ratio of averages approaches 1 as
n\to\infty
, so no uniform
\alpha<1
works.

We have

T^{k}n=n+k,\quad T^{k}m=m+k\Rightarrow d(T^{k}n,T^{k}m)=\left|\frac{1}{n+k}-\frac{1}{m+k}\right|\;.

(26)

Assume without loss of generality that
n<m
. Then
\frac{1}{n+k}>\frac{1}{m+k}
, so

d(T^{k}n,T^{k}m)=\frac{1}{n+k}-\frac{1}{m+k}\;.

(27)

Define the average distance over
k=0
to
n-1

A_{n}(n,m):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k}n,T^{k}m)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{n+k}-\frac{1}{m+k}\right)\;,

(28)

and similarly, the next average shifted by one iterate

A_{n}^{(1)}(n,m):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k+1}n,T^{k+1}m)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\left(\frac{1}{n+k+1}-\frac{1}{m+k+1}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{n+k}-\frac{1}{m+k}\right)\;.

(29)

So

A_{n}^{(1)}(n,m)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{n+k}-\frac{1}{m+k}\right)=A_{n}(n,m)-\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{n+0}-\frac{1}{m+0}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{n+n}-\frac{1}{m+n}\right)\;

(30)

that is

A_{n}^{(1)}=A_{n}-\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{m}\right)+\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{2n}-\frac{1}{m+n}\right)\;.

(31)

We are interested in whether

\frac{A_{n}^{(1)}}{A_{n}}\leq\alpha<1\quad\text{uniformly in }n

(32)

for some
\alpha<1
and all
n,m
.

To analyze asymptotic behavior, fix
m=n+1
. Then

d(T^{k}n,T^{k}m)=\left|\frac{1}{n+k}-\frac{1}{n+1+k}\right|=\frac{1}{n+k}-\frac{1}{n+k+1}=\frac{1}{(n+k)(n+k+1)}\;.

(33)

So

A_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\frac{1}{(n+k)(n+k+1)}\;,

(34)

observe that
n+k
runs from
n
to
2n-1
. Thus,

A_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=n}^{2n-1}\frac{1}{j(j+1)}\;.

(35)

Now use

\frac{1}{j(j+1)}=\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{j+1}\Rightarrow\sum_{j=n}^{2n-1}\left(\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{j+1}\right)=\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{2n}=\frac{1}{2n}\;,

(36)

so

A_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{1}{2n}=\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\;.

(37)

Compute
A_{n}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k+1}n,T^{k+1}m)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(n+k)(n+k+1)}
, where

j=n+k
runs from
n+1
to
2n
,

\sum_{j=n+1}^{2n}\frac{1}{j(j+1)}=\sum_{j=n+1}^{2n}\left(\frac{1}{j}-\frac{1}{j+1}\right)=\frac{1}{n+1}-\frac{1}{2n+1}\;.

(38)

Thus

A_{n}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{1}{n+1}-\frac{1}{2n+1}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{(2n+1)-(n+1)}{(n+1)(2n+1)}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{n}{(n+1)(2n+1)}=\frac{1}{(n+1)(2n+1)}\;.

(39)

Now calculate the ratio

\frac{A_{n}^{(1)}}{A_{n}}=\frac{\frac{1}{(n+1)(2n+1)}}{\frac{1}{2n^{2}}}=\frac{2n^{2}}{(n+1)(2n+1)}\;,

(40)

and simplify

=\frac{2n^{2}}{2n^{2}+n+2n+1}=\frac{2n^{2}}{2n^{2}+3n+1}=\frac{2}{2+\frac{3}{n}+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}\to 1\quad\text{as }n\to\infty\;,

(41)

so we conclude that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{A_{n}^{(1)}}{A_{n}}=1\;.

(42)

Therefore, for any
\alpha<1
, there exists
n

(43)

hence, the PA-condition (
2
)

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k+1}n,T^{k+1}m)\leq\alpha\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}d(T^{k}n,T^{k}m)

(44)

cannot hold uniformly for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
with a fixed
\alpha<1
.
Thus,
T
is not a PA-contraction.

Now consider
\frac{d(m,Tn)+d(n,Tm)}{2}
. If
m<n
,
Tn=n+1<n
, so
\frac{1}{m}>\frac{1}{n+1}
, so

d(m,Tn)=\frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{n+1},\quad d(n,Tm)=\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{m+1}\quad\text{(if }m+1<n\text{)}\;.

(46)

So

\displaystyle\frac{d(m,Tn)+d(n,Tm)}{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{n+1}+\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{m+1}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{m+1}\right)+\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{n+1}\right)\right)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{m(m+1)}+\frac{1}{n(n+1)}\right)\;,

(47)

and this is small.

d(m,n)=\frac{1}{m}-\frac{1}{n}=\frac{n-m}{mn}
,
and
d(Tm,Tn)=\frac{n-m}{(m+1)(n+1)}
.
So

\frac{d(Tm,Tn)}{d(m,n)}=\frac{n-m}{(m+1)(n+1)}\cdot\frac{mn}{n-m}=\frac{mn}{(m+1)(n+1)}<1

(48)

Let
k=\sup\frac{mn}{(m+1)(n+1)}
. Since this
<1
for all
m,n
, and
\to 1
as
m,n\to\infty
, we can pick any
k\in(0,1)
, say
k=0.99
, and for all
m,n
such that
\frac{mn}{(m+1)(n+1)}\leq k
, it holds.

For small
m,n
, verify finitely many cases: since all values are bounded away from equality, we can always find a
k<1
such that:

d(Tm,Tn)\leq k\cdot d(m,n)\leq k\cdot M(m,n)\;.

(49)

Thus,
T
is a Ćirić contraction with
k<1
.

To prove that
T
is not a PA-Contraction, fix
m,n\in\mathbb{N}
,

m=n-1
. The proof goes exactly as in Remark
11

Remark 13
.

While we have shown that PA-contractions are not implied by classical types, the converse — whether PA-contractions imply other classes — is only partially resolved. The mapping
Tx=x^{2}/2
shows that PA-contractions do not imply Kannan or F-contractions. Whether every F-contraction is a PA-contraction, and if every
PA-contraction is a Chatterjea or Ćirić contraction remains open.

8
Conclusion

We introduced
PA-contractions
, a new class based on averaged contraction over orbits. This condition strictly generalizes Banach contractions,
is independent of F-contractions, Kannan, Chatterjea, and Ćirić types,
ensures unique fixed points under continuity. Future investigation could go
in the directions of solving open problems, multivalued mappings and common fixed points.

PA-contractions offer a new lens for analyzing asymptotic behavior in fixed point theory.
