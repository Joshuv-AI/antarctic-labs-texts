Expert Evaluation and the Limits of Human Feedback in Mental Health AI Safety Testing Note: Preprint. Under review.
Kiana Jafari, Paul Ulrich Nikolaus Rust, Duncan Eddy, Robbie Fraser, Nina Vasan, Darja Djordjevic, Akanksha Dadlani, Max Lamparth, Eugenia Kim, Mykel J. Kochenderfer

Abstract
. Learning from human feedback (LHF) assumes that expert judgments, appropriately aggregated, yield valid ground truth for training and evaluating AI systems. We tested this assumption in mental health, where high safety stakes make expert consensus essential. Three certified psychiatrists independently evaluated LLM-generated responses using a calibrated rubric. Despite similar training and shared instructions, inter-rater reliability was consistently poor ( I ​ C ​ C ICC 0.087 0.087 – 0.295 0.295 ), falling below thresholds considered acceptable for consequential assessment. Disagreement was highest on the most safety-critical items. Suicide and self-harm responses produced greater divergence than any other category, and was systematic rather than random. One factor yielded negative reliability (Krippendorff’s α = − 0.203 \alpha=-0.203 ), indicating structured disagreement worse than chance. Qualitative interviews revealed that disagreement reflects coherent but incompatible individual clinical frameworks, safety-first, engagement-centered, and culturally-informed orientations, rather than measurement error. By demonstrating that experts rely on holistic risk heuristics rather than granular factor discrimination, these findings suggest that aggregated labels function as arithmetic compromises that effectively erase grounded professional philosophies. Our results characterize expert disagreement in safety-critical AI as a sociotechnical phenomenon where professional experience introduces sophisticated layers of principled divergence. We discuss implications for reward modeling, safety classification, and evaluation benchmarks, recommending that practitioners shift from consensus-based aggregation to alignment methods that preserve and learn from expert disagreement.



1.
Introduction

Learning from human feedback (LHF) has become a dominant paradigm for developing modern AI systems. Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) trains reward models on human preference comparisons
(
46
;
15
)
. Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) optimizes directly on human judgments
(
51
)
. Safety classifiers like Llama Guard learn from human labels distinguishing safe from unsafe content
(
29
)
. Evaluation benchmarks treat human ratings as ground truth against which model performance is measured
(
65
;
35
;
17
)
. These applications differ in mechanism but share a common assumption that human judgments, appropriately aggregated, yield valid signals about what AI systems should do. When humans disagree, the standard response is to average ratings, treating disagreement as noise reducible through scale
(
22
)
. This assumption is rarely tested, and when reliability is measured, it is rarely reported
(
12
)
.

Recent work has demonstrated that even small, high-quality datasets, on the order of 1,000 carefully selected examples, can be sufficient to fine-tune models for domain-specific tasks
(
61
;
37
;
66
)
or provide necessary information compared to generic medical exam-style benchmarks
(
33
)
. This makes the quality and consistency of expert annotations critical. If a modest corpus of expert-labeled data can substantially shape model behavior, then disagreement among those experts has outsized consequences for the resulting system. While recent simulations demonstrate that even low levels of random preference noise can drastically degrade the performance of reward models
(
22
)
, our work suggests a more complex challenge: the “signal” itself is contested. In safety-critical domains, these inconsistencies are not merely random errors but reflect competing clinical philosophies that, when averaged, may lead to unpredictable or therapeutically incoherent model behaviors.

We tested whether expert annotations provide the consistent signal this paradigm requires, focusing on the safety-critical domain of mental health. AI systems for mental health support are proliferating rapidly
(
45
)
; their online nature, low cost, and relative anonymity make them uniquely approachable for users facing diverse socioeconomic barriers, such as financial constraints, geographic isolation, or lack of insurance. Consequently, these systems help bridge the gaps in traditional treatment access, providing a vital source for those who require immediate support during a crisis. However, inappropriate responses of such systems carry serious consequences, up to fatality, as well as an array of psycho-social harms, such as reinforcing delusions
(
26
)
, perpetuating stigma
(
21
;
40
)
and over-reliance
(
40
;
28
)
. The safety of these systems often depends on manual expert evaluation—a small number of specialists review AI outputs and rate their quality, informing model training, safety filtering, and deployment decisions. What if those experts cannot agree, not due to oversight or measurement error, but because they hold fundamentally different views about what constitutes a good response?

Mental health represents a particularly demanding test case for human feedback reliability. The domain is safety-critical; errors cause real harm, not merely suboptimal user experience. It is longitudinal; consequences unfold over months and years, invisible at evaluation time. It is irreducibly contextual; the same response may help one user and harm another depending on characteristics evaluators cannot observe. While clinicians typically integrate body language, posture, and long-term history to develop treatment plans, LLMs operate solely on isolated text. This creates a structural information gap that human feedback is tasked with bridging.

Consequently, evaluation quality must be grounded in symptom self-report, observed behavior, and clinical expert consensus, rather than objective biological tests. For example, measures such as the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) for depression
(
38
)
, rely on self-report and are rarely seen as definitive on their own. Biological measures are primarily used to rule out alternative explanations or guide treatment rather than to adjudicate evaluative disagreement
(
9
;
44
)
. If human feedback can provide reliable signal in any high-stakes domain, it should do so here, among trained experts with established clinical frameworks and strong motivation for careful evaluation. We define an evaluator’s “framework” as the internal mental models and decision methodology by which they practice their clinical craft. While nominally informed by standardized medical guidelines, this framework is also shaped by idiosyncratic professional, lived, and cultural experiences. It is this latent framework, rather than an objective, universal ground truth, that current alignment methods attempt to model through human feedback.

We developed a safety-focused evaluation dataset in which three psychiatrists evaluated 360 LLM-generated mental health responses across eight commonly used evaluation factors using a calibrated rubric, producing 1,080 expert annotations. Despite similar professional training and shared annotation instructions, inter-rater reliability was consistently poor, with ICC ranging from 0.09 to 0.30 which is well below the 0.40 threshold typically considered minimally acceptable
(
16
)
. Despite clearer safety constraints, suicide and self-harm cases produced the greatest disagreement, likely reflecting different clinical risk tolerances and differing views on how directive a model should be, while ADHD-related responses, where guidelines are less prescriptive, produced the most consensus. Quantitative analysis revealed systematic rather than random disagreement. Raters showed consistent directional biases, with gaps up to 1.69 points on 5-point scales. Qualitative interviews revealed that experts apply substantively different evaluative frameworks, reflecting genuine disagreement rather than measurement error.

This paper makes three contributions. First, we provide an existence proof that expert evaluation of AI mental health responses shows poor inter-rater reliability across all eight evaluation factors, with ICC values falling below thresholds considered acceptable for consequential assessment
(
16
)
. While this
n=3
panel is not intended to be globally representative, the failure of consensus among board-certified experts serves as a demanding test case for the assumption of stable ground truth in AI safety. Second, we demonstrate that this disagreement is systematic rather than random, concentrating on the highest-stakes items and reflecting consistent directional biases that averaging cannot resolve. Third, we provide qualitative evidence that disagreement reflects legitimate framework differences. Each expert articulated a coherent clinical philosophy, prioritizing safety, cultural context, or therapeutic engagement, that produced different ratings of identical responses. Together, these findings suggest that in safety-critical domains with longitudinal consequences and consensus-based standards, human feedback may not provide the stable ground truth that current AI alignment methods assume.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Learning from Human Feedback

Human feedback emerged as central to AI development from the recognition that constructing analytic objective functions for complex tasks is incredibly difficult, yet humans can often recognize good outputs when they see them. RLHF formalized this intuition; human annotators compare pairs of model outputs, these preferences train a reward model, which then guides policy optimization
(
15
;
60
)
. This approach powered significant capability advances, including instruction-following models
(
46
)
, with variations proliferating: DPO eliminates the reward model by optimizing directly on preference data
(
51
)
; Constitutional AI uses human-written principles to guide self-improvement
(
6
)
. Beyond training, human feedback underlies safety infrastructure—classifiers like Llama Guard
(
29
)
, evaluation benchmarks
(
65
)
, and red-teaming exercises
(
48
)
. These applications share a foundational assumption that human judgments, appropriately aggregated, yield valid training signal. Yet a review of major RLHF papers finds inter-annotator agreement statistics absent from most methodology sections; the assumption of adequate reliability is implicit rather than tested
(
12
)
.

2.2.
Annotator Disagreement

A parallel literature challenges the treatment of disagreement as noise. The CrowdTruth framework argues that annotator disagreement often reflects genuine ambiguity rather than error, and that information is lost when labels are aggregated to a single ground truth
(
5
;
4
)
. Pavlick and Kwiatkowski demonstrated that for natural language inference, items with high disagreement often reflect linguistic phenomena, pragmatic ambiguity, underspecification, rather than mere annotation noise
(
47
)
. In safety-critical domains, the “aggregation as truth” assumption becomes more dangerous. The DICES dataset, for instance, found that substantial disagreement in safety annotations, was systematically predicted by demographic factors
(
3
)
. Crucially, prior work by Gordon and colleagues indicates that expert annotations often fail to resolve these crowd disagreements, instead introducing new, sophisticated layers of divergence based on professional perspective
(
25
)
. This suggests that human preferences may not represent a stable gold standard for AI alignment, as RLHF inherits the annotator biases in systematic ways
(
27
;
23
)
.

Our study extends this literature by shifting the focus from crowdworker classification to expert evaluative judgment in a clinical safety domain. While prior work examined demographic variation in content moderation, we provide an existence proof that board-certified psychiatrists fail to achieve reliable consensus on the therapeutic appropriateness of generative AI. The disagreement we document is framework-based and normative rather than demographic or perspectival. By documenting this divergence in a domain with longitudinal consequences, we demonstrate that expert disagreement is a principled, sociotechnical phenomenon: it reflects different clinical philosophies that cannot be resolved through simple arithmetic aggregation.

2.3.
AI in Mental Health

AI applications in mental health have expanded from detecting depression in social media posts
(
14
)
to conversational agents offering therapeutic support
(
20
;
30
;
50
)
. The potential benefits are substantial, given persistent access gaps, as an estimated 11 years elapse between symptom onset and treatment initiation
(
63
)
; AI systems could help bridge these access gaps. The risks are equally significant; users may be vulnerable, and inappropriate responses could validate harmful beliefs, reinforce stigma or discourage treatment-seeking, among other potential harms. Evaluation has struggled to keep pace, with safety evaluation particularly underdeveloped
(
55
)
.

A novel challenge is the widespread accessibility of general-purpose foundation models. The general public increasingly uses off-the-shelf, general purpose LLMs for mental health support, creating a distinct safety-utility tension
(
53
;
13
)
. Providing unlicensed diagnosis or therapy is neither ethical nor legal, yet outright refusal, where models trigger generic disclaimers and decline to engage, can be experienced as invalidating or, in crisis scenarios, may represent a failure to provide essential bridging resources or even exaggerate symptoms
(
26
)
.

Evaluation methodologies have struggled to navigate this tension. While consensus guidelines for assessment and treatment of mental health disorders exist, including frameworks for assessing risk, these were designed for human clinicians managing longitudinal care, not for evaluating isolated text outputs from an AI. These frameworks do not translate directly to the task of rating a single turn of dialogue, leaving evaluators without customized tools to determine whether a model’s response strikes the appropriate balance between helpful support and appropriate boundaries. A core challenge is the absence of ground truth, requiring elaborate methods like uncertainty-aware preference modeling when aggregating expert annotations
(
33
, e.g.)
. Whether a response is “good” depends on theoretical orientation, clinical context, and downstream effects that unfold over time. Evaluation necessarily relies on expert judgment, making expert reliability a critical and underexamined question.

2.4.
Expert Reliability in Clinical Contexts

Psychiatry has long grappled with reliability concerns, providing important context for what we might expect in AI evaluation. The Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), was motivated partly by poor agreement among clinicians diagnosing the same patients
(
31
)
, and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 5th Edition (DSM-5) field trials found kappa values ranging from 0.20 to 0.78 depending on diagnosis
(
52
)
. Reliability challenges are particularly acute for safety assessment. A meta-analysis of suicide risk prediction found clinical judgment performed only marginally better than chance
(
34
)
.

These findings establish a baseline expectation for AI evaluation tasks. If clinicians struggle to agree on diagnoses and risk when they have full access to a patient’s history, presentation, and ongoing relationship, we should not expect high agreement when evaluating static, isolated AI text excerpts that relate to judgments about underlying risk. Multiple therapeutic frameworks offer different accounts of what constitutes helpful communication; appropriateness is context-dependent in ways text-based evaluation alone cannot capture; and qualities like warmth and attunement resist reduction to operational metrics on a rating scale. The question is not whether expert disagreement exists, but whether it is substantial enough to undermine the validity of human feedback as training signal.

2.5.
Research Gap

Human feedback methods presume reliable ground truth but rarely test reliability. Annotator disagreement research focuses primarily on crowdworkers performing linguistic classification tasks, leaving open the question of whether expert annotators fare better on evaluative judgments. Prior work on AI in mental health acknowledges evaluation challenges, and has quantified expert disagreement on structured tasks
(
33
)
, but has not qualitatively investigated the clinical frameworks driving that disagreement or examined whether it reflects resolvable noise or irreducible normative differences.

We address this gap by shifting from a search for representativeness to an existence proof of irreducible disagreement. By combining rigorous quantitative analysis with deep qualitative investigation, we distinguish between measurement error, which better evaluation rubrics or training might address, and genuine evaluator framework differences that no amount of aggregation can resolve. If certified experts applying coherent clinical philosophies cannot reach a stable signal, then the “noise" current alignment methods seek to average away is actually a contestation of the “signal” itself.

3.
Methods

To disentangle the sources of expert variance, we utilized a sequential mixed-methods design. We first map the statistical magnitude and patterns of disagreement through quantitative reliability analysis, then employ semi-structured interviews to surface the underlying clinical frameworks driving those judgments. This dual-layered approach allows us to empirically differentiate between remediable measurement error and the irreducible normative divergence that complicates consensus-based aggregation.

3.1.
Dataset Construction

We constructed a dataset of 360 AI-generated responses to synthetic mental health-related user prompts, systematically varying factors relevant to evaluation difficulty and safety concerns. All prompts were developed by the research team; none contain real user data or personally identifiable information.

Prompts were developed using a
3\times 3

Clinical severity levels (low, moderate, high) were operationalized under the supervision of licensed psychiatrists and informed by validated clinical assessment scales: DSM-5 criteria for oppositional defiant disorder and substance use disorder,

The dataset was organized around ten high-risk response categories, each representing a potential harm an inappropriate AI response might cause: supporting suicidal ideation, supporting non-suicidal self-harm, reinforcing hopelessness, reinforcing hallucinations, colluding with delusions, encouraging manic behavior, encouraging avoidance (anxiety), supporting continued substance use, reinforcing obsessive behaviors, reinforcing distorted body image (eating disorders), and punishing ADHD-related behaviors.

3.2.
Evaluation Framework

The evaluation framework was developed through systematic review of existing frameworks for assessing AI mental health responses, therapy quality, and clinical communication. We reviewed evaluation approaches from Med-PaLM 2
(
59
)
, CRADLE Bench
(
11
)
, CounselBench
(
36
)
, the EPITOME framework
(
56
)
, Sanjeewa et al.
(
54
)
, clinical communication scales including the Motivational Interviewing Skill Code
(
41
)
, and expanded on prior work
(
26
)
. From this review, we synthesized a unified framework comprising two dimensions, Safety and Quality, across eight factors. Each factor used a 5-point scale with detailed descriptors (see
A.2
).

Safety factors
: Severity of potential harm (magnitude of harm that could occur) and likelihood of potential harm (probability harmful content will be acted upon).

Quality factors
: Correctness (conformity with clinical evidence), relevance (specificity in addressing user’s query), active listening (depth of reflection and paraphrasing), empathy and validation (warmth, compassion, absence of stigma), boundaries and ethical disclaimers (clarity about LLM limitations), and actionability and encouragement (practicality of next steps, hope-building).

3.3.
Expert Participants and Procedure

Three certified psychiatrists served as expert raters, selected to provide diversity across subspecialty focus (adult psychiatry, child/adolescent psychiatry), theoretical orientation (cultural, biopsychosocial), and patient populations served. Detailed participant information is provided in
A.3
.

Before independent evaluation, experts participated in a 90-minute calibration session that included review of all rating scales, guided discussion of anchor points, and practice ratings on held-out examples with group discussion of discrepancies. While longer or iterative calibration might have improved surface-level consistency, our qualitative findings
5
suggest that remaining disagreement stemmed from divergent professional frameworks rather than misunderstanding of task instructions.

Experts then independently rated all 360 responses across all 8 factors, yielding 8,640 total ratings
(360\text{ responses}\times 8\text{ factors}\times 3\text{ raters}=8,640;\text{ with 1,080 response-level annotations per rater})
. Raters were blinded to model source.

After completing all ratings, each expert participated in a 45–60 minute semi-structured interview exploring their evaluation process, sources of uncertainty, and underlying clinical framework. Interview protocols are provided in
A.3
. This study was determined exempt from IRB review as it involved evaluation of AI-generated content by professional consultants rather than human subjects research.

3.4.
Analysis

For
quantitative analysis
, we computed intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) as the primary reliability metric, reporting ICC(2,1) for single-rater reliability and ICC(2,k) for averaged reliability
(
39
;
57
)
. We interpret ICC values following Cicchetti’s guidelines
(
16
)
: below 0.40 indicates poor reliability, 0.40–0.59 fair, 0.60–0.74 good, and 0.75 or above excellent. As a secondary metric suited to ordinal data, we computed Krippendorff’s alpha, where
\alpha<0.67
indicates data that should not be relied upon for drawing conclusions
(
32
)
. We examined systematic bias through mean ratings per rater, directional disagreement between rater pairs, and factor-specific patterns. We computed mean absolute deviation (MAD) to quantify disagreement magnitude and identify where disagreement concentrates by condition, severity, risk type, and model.

For
qualitative analysis
, interview transcripts were analyzed using reflexive thematic analysis
(
10
)
, with a specific focus on explaining quantitative patterns. When reliability statistics revealed high disagreement on particular factors or conditions, we examined interview data for explanations, seeking to distinguish measurement error from genuine framework differences.

5.
Qualitative Results

Semi-structured interviews with each expert revealed that disagreement stems from coherent but distinct clinical approaches rather than measurement error. These framework differences explain the systematic quantitative patterns documented in Section
4
.

5.1.
Three Clinical Frameworks

Expert A: safety-first orientation.
Expert A prioritized harm avoidance, attending first to whether responses might cause direct harm:“When I look at a response, my first question is always: could this hurt someone? Everything else matters, but that comes first.”

This safety-first lens produced the highest ratings overall (mean
4.19
); responses that avoided active harm generally passed, even when therapeutically limited. For Expert A, the primary failure mode is an AI response that actively endangers a user, and responses that missed opportunities for deeper engagement were not penalized.

Expert B: therapeutic engagement orientation.
Expert B focused on whether responses would facilitate help-seeking and therapeutic connection:

“Most important, empathy and actionability… these determine whether someone actually seeks help. You can have a perfectly ‘safe’ response that’s so cold or generic that the person closes the chat and doesn’t try again.”

This produced intermediate ratings (mean
3.76
), balancing safety concerns against practical effectiveness. For Expert B, a technically safe response that discourages continued engagement represents a meaningful failure.

Expert C: cultural context orientation.
Expert C emphasized cultural specificity and applied the most demanding standards:

“Cultural context and developmental framework are crucial. A response that works for one cultural community might be completely wrong for another.”

This orientation produced the strictest ratings overall (mean
3.64
). For Expert C, responses that failed to acknowledge their cultural assumptions or clearly delineate AI limitations were fundamentally inadequate, regardless of whether they avoided direct harm.

These three orientations, safety-focused, engagement-centered, and culturally-informed, represent legitimate clinical philosophies that coexist in psychiatric practice. The largest quantitative gaps trace directly to these framework differences; aggregating ratings across them produces arithmetic means that reflect no expert’s actual clinical judgment.

The boundaries factor (

This pattern was not miscalibration but reflected a coherent normative stance. In interviews, Expert C articulated a strict standard for AI boundary-setting:

“For the piece about boundaries and ethical disclaimers… I was very much only giving a high grade if the platform explicitly said, like, reminded the user that they were AI. And not a professional.”

Expert C was surprised that this standard was rarely met:

“I was a little surprised that only a few saying, just a reminder, I’m AI and I’m not a professional. That was surprising, yes.”

This strict stance stemmed from Expert C’s awareness of the global and contextual diversity of users:

“The context of the user, like, their social context, what they’re actually going to do next, what they have access to, who they might consult or not, how isolated they are or not, like, we don’t know these things about the user. And those all impact the risk. Thinking about the universal response of a platform, given the extreme variability and diversity of users, that’s the challenge.”

For Expert C, the fact that AI cannot know user context makes explicit boundary-setting essential, nearly all responses failed to adequately acknowledge this limitation. Expert A, by contrast, evaluated boundaries relative to what an AI can reasonably achieve in a single turn:

“If the response doesn’t claim to be therapy and suggests professional help, that’s appropriate boundary-setting for what this is.”

These positions reflect a genuine value disagreement about AI’s appropriate role in mental health contexts. If experts cannot agree on what it means for an AI to “stay in its lane,” then boundary-setting cannot be treated as a scalar property learned from averaged labels. It is a site of irreducible normative conflict. Expert C holds that AI must always explicitly disclaim its non-professional status, while Expert A holds that implicit acknowledgment through appropriate referrals is sufficient. Neither position is clinically unreasonable; both are defensible within psychiatric practice. Aggregating their ratings produces a mean that reflects neither expert’s actual standard.

5.3.
Structural Limitations of Single-Turn Evaluation

Beyond framework differences, experts identified fundamental constraints on the evaluation paradigm itself; limitations that no rubric refinement could address.

Temporal blindness.
Mental health outcomes unfold over months and years, but evaluation captures only a single moment. As Expert A noted, “A response that reassures someone ’you’re doing fine’ might feel supportive now but delay treatment for years.” Expert C echoed this: “The severity of the harm was often a missed opportunity to orient someone towards appropriate help…characterizing how damaging it is…it’s hard."

Context dependency.
Appropriateness depends on user characteristics evaluators cannot observe. Expert B explained, “The same response might be perfect for one patient and harmful for another. When I’m rating, I have to imagine who’s receiving this. But I might imagine a different person than you do.” Expert A added that age, developmental stage, and support systems all matter, but remain invisible to raters.

Absent ground truth.
Experts questioned whether single-turn evaluation has a correct answer. Expert A observed, “Psychiatrists are only 54% accurate at predicting suicide risk…it feels difficult to say AI must meet an insanely high standard when we ourselves don’t have a great grasp of safety.” Expert B put it simply: “There’s no blood test for a good response.”
These limitations are structural, not methodological. The evaluation paradigm assumes response quality is observable in the moment, but the quality that matters, whether a user seeks help, stays in treatment, improves over time, emerges later and elsewhere.

6.
Discussion

Our findings demonstrate that expert evaluation of AI mental health responses produces reliability below thresholds considered acceptable for consequential assessment. We interpret these results as an existence proof: in at least one carefully constructed evaluation setting, expert disagreement was substantial enough to undermine the validity of aggregated labels. Replication across additional expert panels is essential before drawing broader conclusions.

6.1.
Why Adjudication Cannot Resolve This

A natural response to our findings is that additional calibration or adjudication could resolve disagreement. We are skeptical, for three reasons.
First, our experts participated in a 90-minute calibration session before independent rating. This exceeds the methodological transparency typical of major annotation efforts; even flagship RLHF work such as InstructGPT
(
46
)
reports labeler screening procedures but not calibration duration, and reliability statistics are often omitted entirely
(
12
)
.Poor reliability emerged despite, not in the absence of, methodological care.

Second, the qualitative data reveal that disagreement stems from framework differences, not misunderstanding. When Expert C assigned “
2
” to
92\%
of responses on boundaries while Expert A distributed ratings across
3
,
4
, and
5
, this reflected a normative stance about what AI must disclaim, not confusion about the rubric. Adjudication would require one expert to abandon their clinical philosophy.

Third, adjudication assumes a correct answer exists that discussion can reveal. But as Expert B observed:“There’s no blood test for a good response. I’m giving my opinion…my informed, trained opinion…but it’s still an opinion.”

In domains where ground truth is constructed rather than discovered, adjudication risks producing false consensus that ignores valid, differing professional perspectives.

6.2.
Implications for Learning from Human Feedback

These findings have direct implications for systems that learn from human feedback.

Reward model training.
RLHF assumes that human preference comparisons provide valid signal for training reward models. When
ICC
is
0.087
on a factor like boundaries, any given label is more likely to reflect rater-specific framework than response-specific quality. Reward models trained on such labels would learn rater idiosyncrasies as much as genuine quality distinctions. The standard remedy, averaging across annotators, produces labels that reflect no annotator’s actual judgment.

Safety classification.
Systems like Llama Guard train classifiers to distinguish safe from unsafe content. Our findings suggest that the training labels themselves may be unreliable in precisely the domains where safety classification matters most. High-stakes content (suicide, self-harm) produced the greatest disagreement, meaning safety classifiers trained on expert labels would inherit contested judgments as if they were ground truth.

Evaluation benchmarks.
Benchmarks that treat human ratings as ground truth for measuring model performance face a validity problem. If experts cannot agree on what constitutes a good response, benchmark scores measure agreement with a particular subset of expert opinion rather than objective quality.

6.3.
Implications for Fairness and Accountability

These findings raise questions beyond technical reliability that are central to fairness, accountability, and transparency in AI systems.

Aggregation as policy choice
. Averaging expert ratings is often treated as a neutral statistical operation, but our findings reveal it as a policy choice with distributive consequences. When Expert A’s safety-first framework is averaged with Expert C’s culturally-informed framework, the resulting label reflects neither clinical philosophy. The implicit “compromise” value system that emerges was never explicitly chosen and may not correspond to any coherent approach to care. This is most critical on the highest-stakes items (e.g., suicidal ideation) where disagreement was greatest. Aggregation is a decision rule that determines which errors the system makes and who bears the clinical risk.

Whose expertise counts
. Our study characterizes expert disagreement as a principled, sociotechnical phenomenon among certified psychiatrists. However, this professional class represents only one axis of epistemic authority. Patients, caregivers, peer support specialists, and community health workers may hold different frameworks for determining appropriate AI behavior, grounded in distinct experiences of mental health care. Relying exclusively on clinical experts does not resolve the normative conflict inherent in AI alignment; it simply determines which group has the power to define the ’correct’ response. The systematic divergence we document among highly trained psychiatrists suggests that incorporating a broader range of stakeholders would likely amplify, rather than reduce, the complexity of the alignment signal.

Distributional impacts
. The framework differences we identified have unequal consequences across populations. Strict boundary-setting, evidenced by our finding that one expert rated 92.2% of responses as inadequate on boundaries, may reduce AI overreliance but risks alienating users who depend on low-barrier support. Permissive boundaries may improve engagement but risk role confusion. These harms are not uniformly distributed. Users with limited professional access, often due to socioeconomic disadvantage, may be disproportionately harmed by “safety-first” over-escalation that terminates supportive interactions. Conversely, users in acute crisis may be disproportionately harmed by under-escalation that fails to redirect to appropriate resources. When expert frameworks systematically diverge on these tradeoffs, the choice of whose framework to encode becomes a fairness decision with differential impact across user populations.

6.4.
Recommendations for Practice

Our findings do not counsel abandoning expert evaluation, which remains essential. Instead, they suggest methodological adjustments.

Report reliability.
Evaluation studies should compute and report inter-rater reliability as standard practice. Our
ICC
values (
0.087
–
0.295
) provide a reference point; studies with similar or lower reliability should interpret aggregated ratings with caution.

Preserve disagreement.
Rather than averaging ratings to a single score, practitioners might preserve the distribution of expert judgments. Modeling approaches that learn from disagreement rather than consensus, such as jury learning
(
25
)
or multi-annotator models
(
4
;
3
)
, may better capture the structure of expert judgment.

Disaggregate by framework.
When experts apply different philosophies, such as in the clinical domain of our study, analyzing these perspectives separately may be more informative than pooled analysis. Deployment decisions might appropriately weight different frameworks depending on the specific use case.

Disclose framework assumptions.
When deploying AI systems trained or evaluated using expert feedback, practitioners should document which expert perspectives informed the system and acknowledge that alternative frameworks exist. This is particularly important when systems are deployed across diverse user populations who may be differentially affected by the encoded framework.

7.
Limitations

Our findings should be viewed in light of several limitations. First, three expert raters evaluating
360
responses cannot represent the full diversity of clinical orientations in psychiatry. Second, our psychiatrists were trained in U.S. institutions; different cultural contexts likely show different patterns of agreement. Third, while our calibration session was extensive, fundamental framework differences might remain that could be addressed with further calibration refinement. Finally, we evaluated isolated AI responses; multi-turn evaluation might provide more context but could also introduce new sources of divergence.

8.
Conclusion

We examined whether expert evaluation of AI mental health responses provides reliable ground truth. Despite similar professional training, inter-rater reliability was consistently poor (
ICC

0.087
–
0.295
), reflecting systematic framework differences rather than random noise. This is not measurement error that better calibration could resolve; it is principled disagreement about what constitutes an appropriate mental health response in this context and what AI should do. In domains where human feedback is most consequential, it may also be least reliable. Acknowledging this tension is the first step toward more honest AI evaluation.
