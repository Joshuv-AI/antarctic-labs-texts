Effects of double-low rapeseed meal on growth performance, meat quality, gut microbiota, and nitrogen excretion in Cherry Valley ducks based on low-protein dietary strategies
Authors: Yidan Zhang, Zhaobin Wang, Tao Zeng, Li Chen, Yong Tian, Wenwu Xu, Lizhi Lu, Tiantian Gu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to investigate the effects of low-protein (LP) diet with double-low rapeseed meal (DLR) partially replacing soybean meal (SBM) on the growth performance, meat quality, and nitrogen excretion of Cherry Valley ducks. In a randomized complete block design, a total of 504 one-d-old Cherry Valley ducks with consistent health, weight, and genetic background (body weight = 51.80 ± 1.49 g) were randomly assigned to seven groups (six replicates/group, 12 ducks/replicate). The dietary crude protein (CP) level was reduced in 0.5 percentage point increments, with the amino acid and energy balance maintained. The dietary CP levels were 20.01% (CON), 19.51%, 19.01%, 18.51%, 18.01%, 17.51%, and 17.01% (LP1, LP2, LP3, LP4, LP5, and LP6, respectively) for 1 to 14 d of age, 17.50% (CON), 17.00%, 16.50%, 16.00%, 15.50%, 15.00%, and 14.50% (LP1, LP2, LP3, LP4, LP5, and LP6, respectively) for 15 to 42 d of age. The results showed that dietary treatment significantly affected feed conversion ratio (FCR) (P < 0.05), but no linear or quadratic trends were observed (P > 0.05); blood urea nitrogen (BUN) content in the grower phase changed significantly (P < 0.05); abdominal fat yield (AFY) increased linearly (P = 0.002); meat color parameters (lightness [L∗], redness [a∗], and yellowness [b∗]) were significantly altered, showing both linear and quadratic trends (P < 0.05); and among meat quality traits, only shear force was significantly affected, also displaying linear and quadratic trends (P < 0.05). The LP diet improved the structure of the intestinal microbiota and significantly reduced nitrogen excretion (P < 0.05). In conclusion, reducing the dietary CP level by 2 percentage points was the optimal strategy, as it maintained growth performance while reducing nitrogen pollution, provided theoretical basis and practical guidance for the design of LP diet in the poultry industry, and contributed to the goal of cost reduction and efficiency improvement in China's livestock and poultry production.Keywords: Low-protein diet, Double-low rapeseed meal, Cherry Valley duck, Meat quality, Gut microbiota, Nitrogen emission

Introduction

China's livestock and poultry industry has long relied primarily on a corn-soybean meal (SBM) type diet. Soybean meal, second only to corn in inclusion level, is a core protein source for ensuring animal growth performance and muscle deposition due to its high crude protein (CP) content, balanced amino acid profile, low levels of anti-nutritional factors, and high digestibility (Gaffield et al., 2024; Trabue et al., 2021), yet its conventional inclusion rate of 23.00%–30.00% aggravates the depletion of protein resources (Aldis et al., 2024). The traditional high-protein diet model results in severe protein waste, as livestock cannot fully digest and utilize the ingested protein. The undigested portion is excreted as fecal nitrogen, constituting a significant factor in environmental pollution (Cappelaere et al., 2021; Heo et al., 2023). Therefore, strategies such as reducing SBM usage, broadening protein feedstock sources, and establishing a diversified protein supply system can help conserve protein resources and promote cost-effective and sustainable development in animal husbandry.

Low-protein (LP) diet technology allows for a reduction of 2 to 4 percentage points in dietary CP level compared to the NRC (1994) recommendations, representing a feeding strategy aligned with sustainable development. Aderibigbe et al. (2024) showed that reducing dietary CP levels by 2 to 3 percentage points had little effect on the growth performance of Ross × Ross 708 broiler chicks, provided that essential amino acids (EAAs) were sufficient and supplemented in a limiting order. Teng et al. (2021) demonstrated that reducing the dietary CP levels from 19.00% to 16.00% in broilers, along with the addition of 0.75% arginine, glutamic acid, or threonine, had no adverse effects on performance and mildly improved intestinal health in coccidia-infected chickens. Reducing dietary CP level also improves nitrogen utilization efficiency and lessens environmental pollution (Woyengo et al., 2023). In 2020, China proposed requirements such as “promoting the reduction and substitution of corn and SBM”. Current research indicates that adding unconventional feed ingredients to LP diets can further reduce SBM inclusion and enhance the production performance of livestock and poultry (Spring et al., 2020). For instance, Xia et al. (2022a) found that partially replacing SBM with peanut meal in the diet of 21-week-old Longyan laying ducks did not adversely affect egg production or egg quality when up to 75.00% of SBM was replaced; other studies have shown that feeding laying hens a mixture of sunflower seed meal and rapeseed meal did not negatively impact body weight, egg production, or egg quality (Pirgozliev et al., 2023). Under the premise of balanced EAAs, optimizing dietary structure with LP diets can not only reduce SBM usage and lower feed costs in poultry production but also improve nutrient utilization efficiency, thereby reducing nitrogen emissions, without compromising growth performance. It may even enhance the health status of poultry (Adhikari et al., 2025). Double-low rapeseed meal (DLR) low in erucic acid and glucosinolates, an important plant protein resource in China, is derived from varieties developed in Canada. It is characterized by high protein content, low toxicity, low levels of anti-nutritional factors, and high digestibility, allowing its direct use in swine and poultry diets without detoxification treatment. It is a protein source with significant potential for future development and has already been widely adopted by producers.

Therefore, this experiment was conducted to investigate the effects of LP diets with DLR as a partial replacement for SBM on growth performance, serum biochemistry, meat quality, flavor characteristics, and nitrogen emissions in Cherry Valley ducks. This study aims to provide a theoretical basis and practical guidance for the application of DLR in LP diets, while supporting the cost reduction and efficiency improvement of China's livestock and poultry industry.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

Animal experiment was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of the Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences (permit number 25ZALAS33; June 9, 2025).

Experimental design, animals, and diet composition

This trial was carried out at Huzhou Haiguan Agricultural Development Co., Ltd., Huzhou, Zhejiang, China, from October to November 2024. The experiment lasted 42 d, consisting of starter (1–14 d) and finisher (15–42 d) phases. Using a randomized complete block design, 504 one-d-old Cherry Valley ducks of similar body weight (body weight = 51.80 ± 1.49 g), uniform health status and genetic background were allocated randomly to seven dietary treatments. The control group (CON) received diets containing 20.01% CP (d 1–14) and 17.50% CP (d 15–42) without DLR inclusion. For the six LP treatments (LP1-LP6), dietary CP was gradually reduced in both phases, with SBM partially substituted by DLR: 1) LP1, 19.51% CP (1–14 d, 1.58% SBM replaced by DLR); 17.00% CP (15–42 d, 1.77% SBM replaced by DLR); 2) LP2, 19.01% CP (1–14 d, 3.30% SBM replaced by DLR); 16.50% CP (15–42 d, 2.76% SBM replaced by DLR); 3) LP3, 18.51% CP (1–14 d, 4.93% SBM replaced by DLR); 16.00% CP (15–42 d, 4.03% SBM replaced by DLR); 4) LP4, 18.01% CP (1–14 d, 6.02% SBM replaced by DLR); 15.50% CP (15–42 d, 5.04% SBM replaced by DLR); 5) LP5, 17.51% CP (1–14 d, 7.22% SBM replaced by DLR); 15.00% CP (15–42 d, 6.08% SBM replaced by DLR); 6) LP6, 17.01% CP (1–14 d, 8.23% SBM replaced by DLR); 14.50% CP (15–42 d, 7.09% SBM replaced by DLR). The DLR used in this study was obtained from Jiaxing Shunfeng Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd. (Jiaxing, Zhejiang, China), and chemical compositions of the DLR are presented in Table 1. All experimental diets were formulated in accordance with nutrient requirements of meat-type ducks (Ministry of Agriculture of the People’s Republic of China, 2012) (Table 2, Table 3). Crude protein levels were gradually lowered by 0.50 percentage points across treatments, while dietary metabolizable energy (ME) was kept identical. All EAAs including Lys, Met, Met + Cys, Thr, Trp, Ile and Arg were balanced to meet standard requirements. The diets were produced and supplied by Jiaxing Shunfeng Agriculture and Animal Husbandry Co., Ltd. (Jiaxing, Zhejiang, China). Throughout the entire experimental period, ducks had ad libitum access to feed and water. The duck house was regularly disinfected, and ventilation and lighting conditions remained consistent. The initial room temperature was maintained at 32 to 33 °C. After 3 d of age, the house temperature was reduced by approximately 1.5 °C every 3 d until it reached around 20 °C by 21 d of age. Dietary transitions between the two phases were carried out using a gradual transition method.

A total of 450.00 g of each of the 14 diet samples was collected, the CP (method 991.20), dry matter (DM; method 930.15), ether extract (EE; method 920.39), crude fiber (CF; method 962.09) and acid insoluble ash (AIA; method 942.05) in diets were analyzed following AOAC (2019). Dry matter content was determined by drying samples at 103 ± 2 °C for 4 ± 0.1 h in an electric thermostatic drying oven (Model DGG-9240A, Shanghai Senxin Experimental Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Ether extract content was measured using a Soxhlet extraction apparatus (BÜCHI Extraction System B-811, BÜCHI Labortechnik AG, Flawil, Switzerland). Crude fiber content was determined by boiling defatted samples successively in 1.25% sulfuric acid and 1.25% potassium hydroxide solutions for 30 min each. The residue was then filtered, washed, dried at 130 °C, and ashed at 500 °C; CF was calculated based on mass loss. For ash analysis, samples were ignited at 550 °C to obtain crude ash, which was then treated with dilute hydrochloric acid, boiled, and filtered. The acid-insoluble residue was washed, dried, and reignited at 550 °C to constant weight to calculate the AIA content. Amino acid contents in diets according to the method in GB/T 18246-2019 (China National Standard, 2019), hydrolysis with 6 mol/L HCl at 110 °C for 22 to 24 h. The ME was calculated based on standard feed composition values from the Feed Composition and Nutritional Value Table (China Feed Database, 2021).

Sample collection

At 14 and 42 d of age, 1 duck per replicate was randomly selected, fasted for 12 h, and weighed. Blood was collected from the jugular vein into clot-promoting tubes. The ducks were then euthanized and defeathered to measure carcass weight. Abdominal fat yield (AFY) was calculated based on carcass weight. The left breast muscle and thigh muscles were excised and weighed, and muscle yield was determined relative to eviscerated weight, immediately used to measure meat color, pH, shear force, and drip loss. Samples of the right breast muscle (30.50 g) were collected and stored at −20 °C for amino acid and fatty acid analysis. Cecal content was collected into sterile cryotubes, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for 16S rRNA sequencing. Feed samples (450.00 g) from both phases were stored in a dry and ventilated environment for subsequent analysis. During the last three days of each phase, excreta (30–40 g, free of feathers, feed, and other contaminants) were collected in sealed bags and stored at −20 °C for further determination.

Measurement of meat quality

The measurement of meat quality traits was performed according to GB/T 40469-2021 (China National Standard, 2021). Meat color was measured 45 min post–slaughter using a NR110 precision colorimeter (Guangdong Sanenshi Technology Co., Ltd., Guangzhou, Guangdong, China) at different locations on the breast muscle. Results were recorded as lightness (L∗), redness (a∗), and yellowness (b∗) values, with three measurements taken per spot and the average calculated. Muscle pH was measured directly on the breast muscle using a PH-STAR pH meter (German MATTHAUS, Beijing Bulade Technology Development Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Three incisions were made into the muscle, and the pH probe electrode was fully embedded in the muscle tissue. The reading was recorded after stabilization, with three measurements per duck averaged. Shear force was measured using an GR-150 tenderness meter (American G&R Technology Inc., Thousand Oaks, CA, USA). Three muscle strips (approximately 1.00 cm thick) were cut from different locations of the breast muscle, with two shear force measurements per strip averaged. A breast muscle sample of approximately 1.00 cm3 was placed in a drip loss tube and weighed to obtain the initial weight. The tube was then stored vertically in a cool place (3–9 °C). After 24 h, the sample was weighed again to record the final weight.

Medium-long chain fatty acids detection

Based on previous studies with modifications, determined the medium-long chain fatty acids in the breast muscle (Gao et al., 2021). Weigh 0.50 g of muscle sample into a 2-mL centrifuge tube, add 1.00 mL of chloroform-methanol (2:1) solution and 100.00 mg of glass beads. The mixture was homogenized using a MB-96 High-throughput tissue grinde (Zhejiang Meibi Instrument Co., Ltd., Jiaxing, Zhejiang, China) at 55 Hz for 1 min, with this homogenization step repeated twice. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to KW-100TDV Ultrasonic cleaner (Kunshan Shumei Ultrasonic Instrument Co., Ltd., Kunshan, Jiangsu, China) at room temperature for 30 min, followed by centrifugation in an H2050R centrifuge (Hunan Xiangyi Experiment Equipment Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China) at 12,000 × g and 4 °C for 10 min. The entire supernatant was transferred to a 10.00-mL glass centrifuge tube, and 2.00 mL of 1% sulfuric acid-methanol solution was precisely added. After thorough mixing and vortexing, the mixture was esterified in an 80 °C water bath for 30 min. Following cooling, 1.00 mL of n-hexane was added for extraction, and the mixture was vortexed and allowed to stand for 2 min. Then, 5.00 mL of H2O was added for washing, and the mixture was centrifuged in an H2050R centrifuge (H2050R, Hunan Xiangyi Experiment Equipment Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China) at 3500 × g and 4 °C for 10 min. A 700.00 μL aliquot of the supernatant was transferred to a 2.00-mL centrifuge tube, and 100.00 mg of anhydrous sodium sulfate powder was added to remove residual moisture. After vortex mixing, the solution was diluted two-fold. A 300.00 μL portion was transferred to another 2.00-mL centrifuge tube, and 15.00 μL of 500 μg/mL methyl salicylate was added as an internal standard. The mixture was vortexed thoroughly, and an appropriate volume of the supernatant was transferred to a detection vial for analysis. Chromatographic and mass spectrometric conditions were implemented according to the methods described by Hoving et al. (2018) and Beccaria et al. (2018).

Measurement of hydrolyzed amino acid composition

The determination of hydrolyzed amino acids in breast muscle was performed with reference to an established protocol (Bai et al., 2019). Fresh breast muscle samples from slaughtered ducks were collected for the determination of amino acid composition, according to the GB/T 5009.124-2003 (China National Standard, 2003). A 0.5 g breast muscle sample was placed in a hydrolysis tube, and 10.00 mL of 6 mol/L HCl was added. The tube was sealed using an alcohol burner and hydrolyzed in a constant temperature oven at 110 °C for 22 h. After removal and cooling to room temperature, the hydrolysate was transferred to a 50 mL volumetric flask. The hydrolysis tube was rinsed multiple times with deionized water, and the combined rinses were used to bring the volume in the flask to 50 mL, followed by thorough mixing. One milliliter of the filtrate was pipetted into a 40 mm × 25 mm glass weighing dish and evaporated to dryness in a 65 °C water bath. The residue was then completely dissolved by adding 1 mL of 0.02 mol/L HCl with the aid of ultrasonication. The solution was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane filter, diluted fourfold with 0.02 mol/L HCl, and transferred into a vial for instrumental analysis. Analysis was performed using an LA8080 high-speed amino acid analyzer (Hitachi Scientific Instruments (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The chromatographic conditions were as follows: Na+-type cation exchange resin column; analytical column temperature maintained at 57 °C; mobile phase flow rate of 0.45 mL/min; reaction column temperature set at 135 °C; reaction solution flow rate of 0.30 mL/min; and detection wavelength of 570 nm.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry

Fifty milligrams of tissue were homogenized in methanol–water extract, centrifuged in an H2050R centrifuge (Hunan Xiangyi Experiment Equipment Co., Ltd., Changsha, Hunan, China) at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was subjected to LC-MS analysis. Separation was performed on a C18 column with a gradient of water (0.10% formic acid) and acetonitrile. Mass spectrometry was conducted in both positive and negative ion modes (m/z 50–1500) with a resolution ≥ 30,000. The analysis was conducted by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China.

16S rRNA sequencing

This work was conducted in accordance with a previous study (Li et al., 2021). Total genomic DNA was extracted from the samples, and the quality of the extracted DNA was verified by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. The hypervariable V3–V4 region (forward: 5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′, reverse: 5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified, and the PCR products were purified. Following quantification and normalization, a sequencing library was constructed and subjected to high-throughput sequencing. Bioinformatic analysis was performed using QIIME2 (version 2021.2), R language. Alpha diversity and beta diversity analyzes were conducted to characterize the cecal microbial community structure. The analysis was conducted by Shanghai Majorbio Bio-pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China.

Apparent metabolizability of nutrient

Fecal samples from both phases were dried at 65 °C for 72 h. The apparent nutrient metabolizability was determined using the AIA method. Analyzes were conducted according to the procedures described by Van Keulen and Young (1977). Discussion

Effects of LP diets on growth performance of Cherry Valley ducks

Poultry production performance is closely linked to the dietary protein level. Numerous studies have evaluated the effects of LP diets on poultry growth performance. A reduction of 1 to 2 percentage points in the dietary CP level had no significant effect on overall body weight gain, feed intake, or FCR in broilers (De Cesare et al., 2019). However, when the dietary protein level was reduced by more than 2 percentage points, negative effects on broiler growth performance were detected (Chrystal et al., 2020). Reducing the dietary CP level by 3 percentage points in broiler starter and grower diets (under amino acid-balanced conditions) had no significant effect on body weight, feed intake, FCR, or breast meat quality, and instead increased breast muscle yield (Benahmed et al., 2023). Research by Kop-Bozbay et al. (2020) demonstrated that a LP (CP 20.00%) diet under summer conditions had no significant effects on body weight gain, FCR, or carcass weight of broilers at 14 or 42 d of age. Nie et al. (2025) reported that moderate replacement of SBM with high-protein cottonseed meal or dephenolized cottonseed meal had no negative effects on the growth performance of yellow-feathered broilers; however, excessive replacement ratios impaired animal growth performance. The results of this trial indicate that reducing the dietary CP level by up to 2.5 percentage points below the minimum standard did not affect ADFI or ADG in Cherry Valley ducks during 1 to 14 d. However, a 3 percentage point reduction significantly decreased both ADFI and ADG, though no significant difference in FCR was observed compared to the CON. During 15 to 42 d, a 2.5 percentage point reduction in CP significantly reduced ADFI but did not affect ADG or FCR, while a 3 percentage point reduction significantly improved FCR. This phenomenon may be attributed to reduced appetite caused by inadequate protein intake, although amino acid supplementation ensured sufficient nutrient supply to support growth without restriction. Despite formulating LP diets based on the principle of amino acid balance, the CP level should not be reduced by more than 3.00% below the standard. This study also demonstrated that a 2.00% reduction in dietary CP level had no significant effect on FCR in Cherry Valley ducks. Appropriate partial substitution of SBM with DLR reduced SBM inclusion by 12.17% in 1 to 14 d and 11.36% in 15 to 42 d, significantly decreasing SBM usage. These findings confirm the feasibility of using DLR as a partial substitute for SBM in meat duck diets, thereby reducing feeding costs.

Effects of LP diets on serum biochemical parameters of Cherry Valley ducks

Serum biochemical parameters reflect metabolic status, energy transfer, growth and development, as well as the health status of animals (Elsohaby et al., 2019). The levels of TP and ALB in serum are important indicators of liver function, immune capacity, and nutritional status in livestock and poultry. They provide insight into protein synthesis, absorption, and utilization, with albumin specifically involved in the repair of damaged tissues. A low-protein SBM diet significantly decreased serum ALB levels in Ross 308 broilers during the starter phase (Amer et al., 2021). Zhou et al. (2021) found that dietary serine supplementation had no significant effect on serum TP or ALB levels in laying hens. Similarly, adding cystine to a LP diet did not significantly affect serum TP or ALB levels in broilers (Wang et al., 2021). Consistent with the results of the present study, reducing the dietary protein level decreased TP and ALB concentrations in meat ducks during the 1 to 14 d, but had no significant effect on TP or ALB during the 15 to 42 d. The reason for this phenomenon may be that 1 to 14 d ducks have very limited protein reserves and rely almost entirely on dietary supply. When the dietary protein level is reduced, the supply of amino acids becomes insufficient, and the availability of precursors for hepatic synthesis of TP and ALB is restricted, leading to a significant decrease in serum TP and ALB. In contrast, during 15 to 42 d, the digestive system of meat ducks becomes well-developed, and the efficiency of amino acid utilization increases. Even when the CP level is reduced, the liver can still utilize the supplemented synthetic amino acids to prioritize the synthesis of essential proteins such as albumin, thereby maintaining serum TP and ALB within the normal range. Uric acid is the end product of protein metabolism in poultry and reflects the utilization of dietary protein and the balance of amino acids (Donsbough et al., 2010). Urea nitrogen is the end product of protein catabolism in the body and reflects the utilization efficiency of dietary protein in animals (Kohn et al., 2005). Its concentration is primarily influenced by the balance of amino acids in the diet. Elevated BUN levels occur when dietary CP is excessive or amino acids are unbalanced and not utilized efficiently by animals (Zhao et al., 2021). Hejdysz et al. (2022) found that reducing the CP content resulted in a clear decreasing trend in BUN content. Li et al. (2026) reported that reducing dietary protein led to a decrease in serum BUN concentration in WOD188 broilers. Similarly, Camp et al. (2022) found that BUN levels were significantly lower in groups fed LP and low- SBM diets compared to the CON group, which aligns with the results of this trial. These findings suggest improved utilization of dietary protein, and a reduction in protein metabolites, leading to decreased BUN levels.

Effects of LP diets on slaughter performance of Cherry Valley ducks

Protein is a crucial component of muscle cells. Efforts are often directed towards increasing protein deposition and the proportion of protein deposited in meat by improving protein utilization efficiency, thereby enhancing slaughter performance and improving meat quality. Consequently, slaughter performance in meat-producing livestock and poultry serves as a vital indicator reflecting meat quality traits and holds significant importance. Aderibigbe et al. (2024) found that reducing the dietary CP level by approximately 3 percentage points had no effect on the weight or proportion of carcass, breast muscle, and leg muscle in broilers. Liu et al. (2024) reported that appropriate supplementation of glycine in LP diets did not affect the slaughter performance of broilers aged 29 to 42 d, whereas excessive or insufficient glycine supplementation could negatively impact slaughter traits. That reductions in dietary CP concentrations generate increases in abdominal fat deposition in broiler chickens is evident in a Meta-analysis (De Rauglaudre et al., 2023). Hejdysz et al. (2022) confirmed that reducing the CP content leads to an increase in the proportion of abdominal fat. This aligns with the findings of the present study. The LP diet with DLR showed an overall decreasing trend in dressing percentage, eviscerated yield, semi-eviscerated yield, breast muscle yield, and thigh muscle yield in meat ducks, although the differences were not statistically significant. However, a significant increase in abdominal fat yield was observed. This suggests that LP diets may promote fat deposition through a “protein-energy imbalance”. When dietary protein is insufficient, excess carbohydrates can promote lipogenesis via the acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) pathway while simultaneously inhibiting AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-mediated lipolysis (Hu et al., 2024). This is consistent with Yang (2017) view that a higher energy-to-protein ratio in LP diets leads to dietary energy substantially exceeding the requirements for protein deposition, thereby increasing fat deposition efficiency in broilers. Therefore, LP diets can significantly increase abdominal fat yield in livestock and poultry.

Effects of LP diets on meat quality of Cherry Valley ducks

Parameters such as shear force, meat color, and pH value are important indicators for evaluating muscle quality. Meat color serves as an intuitive representation of internal physiological and biochemical processes as well as microbial metabolic activities in muscle, and is a core concern for consumers when assessing meat quality (Sosnówka-Czajka et al., 2023). Shear force quantifies tenderness; lower shear force indicates higher tenderness. Water-holding capacity directly affects the water retention and juiciness of muscle. Lower drip loss signifies better water-holding capacity, resulting in juicier and more tender meat. The L∗ value of breast muscle represents lightness, related to color saturation and surface exudate. The a∗ value represents redness, primarily regulated by myoglobin content in the tissue, showing a positive correlation. The b∗ value represents yellowness, significantly correlated with pigment content in the feed. pH is a core evaluation index for meat quality, and its rate of decline can directly affect color, tenderness, flavor, water-holding capacity, and shelf life (Marchewka et al., 2023; Islam et al., 2022; Weng et al., 2022). Hejdysz et al. (2022) demonstrated that dietary protein reduction led to statistically significant increases in the L∗ value of breast muscle and the b∗ value of leg muscle. According to Banaszak et al. (2020), increases in muscle L∗ and b∗ values are associated with muscle fat content. Xia et al. (2022b) found that adding lysine, methionine, threonine, and other amino acids to LP diet significantly reduced the shear force of breast muscle in broilers. Ge et al. (2024) reported that LP diets could improve muscle color without significantly affecting shear force, pH, or drip loss. These findings are consistent with the results of this study. In the present study, reducing the dietary CP level by up to 2% resulted in a decreasing trend in L∗, a∗, and b∗ values and shear force. Further reduction in protein level led to these index values being significantly higher than those in the CON group, while pH and drip loss were not significantly affected.

Effects of LP diets on medium and long-chain fatty acids of Cherry Valley ducks

Fatty acid composition significantly influences flavor characteristics. Fats, composed of triglycerides, phospholipids, and sterols, contribute to aroma and mouthfeel. Medium- and long-chain fatty acid profiles are important indicators of meat quality. Zhou et al. (2024) found that low protein affected the fatty acid content of breast and thigh muscles. Jiang et al. (2025) showed that dietary protein had no significant effect on the fatty acid content of breast muscle, which is consistent with the results of the present study. The contents of SFA, MUFA, and PUFA were not significantly affected among the groups. This suggests that the specific composition and metabolic influences of the LP diet with DLR may play a role, but overall, reducing the dietary CP level does not impair the meat flavor quality of Cherry Valley ducks. The presence of anti-nutritional factors and the unique nutritional profile of DLR may influence fatty acid synthesis and metabolism in meat ducks. C18:1n12t, C18:2n6t, and C22:1n9t are total fatty acids (TFA), whose formation is associated with the isomerization of unsaturated fatty acids during high-temperature feed processing. Double-low rapeseed meal contains low levels of glucosinolates, which may reduce the degradation of heat-sensitive fatty acids during processing. Additionally, rapeseed meal contains natural antioxidant compounds that inhibit lipoxygenase activity, thereby reducing TFA generation. The reduction in C22:1n9 and its trans isomer, C22:1n9t, may be related to the activity and expression of enzymes involved in fatty acid metabolism in meat ducks. Erucic acid requires specific enzymes and metabolic pathways for digestion, absorption, and transformation. Incremental inclusion of DLR in the diet may further enhance the activity of enzymes that metabolize erucic acid into more utilizable or excretable substances, accelerating its catabolism. Simultaneously, improved liver metabolic function may convert erucic acid into other forms of fatty acids or metabolites, which are then transported via blood circulation to other tissues for utilization or excretion, thereby reducing its accumulation in breast muscle.

Effects of LP diets on amino acids of Cherry Valley ducks

Duck meat is rich in various amino acids, including eight EAAs and nine non-essential amino acids (NEAAs), which form the fundamental basis of its flavor profile. Notably, glutamic acid and aspartic acid, the most abundant amino acids in duck meat, contribute umami notes and enhance its overall flavor. Glycine, alanine, serine, and threonine impart sweetness, while valine, methionine, isoleucine, leucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, and histidine contribute bitterness (Lee et al., 2016), while arginine and lysine exhibit alkaline tastes. Wang et al. (2020) analyzed taste compounds in Beijing Youji chicken broth and found that the mass fraction of umami- and sweet-tasting amino acids (54.52%) was much higher than that of bitter amino acids (34.47%). Among them, alanine and glutamic acid had taste activity values greater than 1, indicating significant contributions to the broth's umami. Shi et al. (2024) demonstrated that most EAAs, NEAAs, and total hydrolyzed amino acids were lower in a LP group compared to the CON group. Benahmed et al. (2023) reported that under the premise of meeting EAA requirements, reducing the dietary CP level during the grower phase by 1.5 or 3 percentage points had no significant negative effect on the amino acid composition of breast muscle in Ross 308 broilers, which is largely consistent with the findings of this study. The present study observed a decreasing trend in the contents of EAAs and NEAAs in duck breast muscle as dietary protein levels declined, though the differences were not statistically significant. This suggests that reducing the dietary protein level within a certain range does not adversely affect the flavor quality of Cherry Valley duck meat, while simultaneously reducing feeding costs in livestock production.

Effects of LP diets on pectoral muscle metabolomics of Cherry Valley ducks

Based on PCA and OPLS-DA score plots, clear separation between groups indicated significant differences in metabolites compared to the CON group. The number and direction of change (up- or down-regulation) of differential metabolites varied. LP4 and LP6 groups exhibited more up-regulated metabolites, particularly amino acids and flavonoids, while LP2 group showed more down-regulated metabolites. These variations may be related to the composition of rapeseed meal in the LP diets, such as the influence of glucosinolates and tannins. Enrichment analysis revealed that the melanogenesis pathway was commonly enriched, which may affect pigment deposition in skin or feathers and be associated with reprogramming of energy metabolism. Compared to existing studies on LP diets in poultry, the common enrichment of the aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathway in LP2, LP4, and LP6 groups - which is related to protein synthesis - aligns with trends observed in mammalian studies, suggesting an adaptive decrease in protein translation efficiency. In Cherry Valley ducks fed LP diet with DLR, compounds such as amino acids, flavonoids, benzene and substituted derivatives, and organic oxygen compounds were significantly up-regulated, while antibiotics, acid compounds, and hydroxyindoles were significantly down-regulated. One study reported that LP diets significantly up-regulated amino acid metabolism-related pathways in chicken liver (Feng, 2021), consistent with the marked up-regulation of amino acids observed here. This indicates that LP diets may maintain nitrogen balance through compensatory metabolism. Another study found that flavonoids were suppressed by dietary protein levels in broilers. The discrepancy may be related to unique glucosinolate derivatives in rapeseed meal inducing antioxidant metabolism via the nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) pathway. Flavonoids, secondary metabolites produced by plants, have been widely shown to possess significant antibacterial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory effects (Xie et al., 2015). The up-regulation of secondary metabolites may reflect an adaptive response in meat ducks through activation of antioxidant and immunomodulatory mechanisms under LP conditions.

Effects of LP diets on intestinal function of Cherry Valley ducks

The gut microbiota serves as an indispensable “digestive organ” for animals, playing crucial roles in nutrition, immunity, and metabolism (Gong et al., 2021). Previous studies have shown a close relationship between animal performance and changes in dietary composition, gut microbiota, and host metabolism (Yin et al., 2023). The gut microbiota is a key driver of innate immune programming, influencing the digestion and absorption of protein and amino acids, and consequently affecting nitrogen utilization efficiency (Lee et al., 2023). Therefore, modulating the intestinal microbiota to improve gut health and reduce ammonia emission is of critical importance for meat-type poultry production. Most exogenous nitrogenous compounds and some endogenous nitrogenous substances are degraded by the gut microbiota and subsequently absorbed and reutilized by the host (Yang et al., 2020), with some microorganisms even possessing nitrogen-fixing capabilities. In the present study, reducing the dietary protein level had little effect on the α-diversity of the intestinal microbiota in meat ducks and did not affect host health. Analysis of the duck fecal bacterial community structure revealed that, at the phylum level, the LP diet with DLR significantly reduced the relative abundances of Bacillota and Pseudomonadota while significantly increasing the relative abundance of Bacteroidota. Bacillota are primarily Gram-positive bacteria that can utilize short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) as a carbon source and couple nitrate reduction to enhance denitrification efficiency under low carbon/nitrogen (C/N) conditions (Jordan et al., 2023) playing a crucial role in host nutrition and metabolism (Sun et al., 2023; Shini and Bryden, 2022). Bacteroidota are mainly associated with carbohydrate breakdown. Guo (2023) showed that Ganoderma lucidum residue supplementation reduced the Bacillota/Bacteroidota (F/B) ratio in mice; a higher F/B ratio can lead to fat accumulation, suggesting a potential role in reducing fat deposition. The decreased F/B ratio in this study suggests that feeding Cherry Valley ducks an LP diet with DLR may reduce fat deposition to some extent. Pseudomonadota are associated with intestinal barrier dysfunction, infections, and the onset of inflammatory bowel disease; their involvement can disrupt the balance of other microbial groups and influence disease progression, while they also play a positive role in energy production in vivo (Chen et al., 2023). At the genus level, unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae, belonging to the phylum Bacillota, is a potentially beneficial bacterium involved in the metabolism of various carbohydrates. It can decompose long-chain fatty acids into SCFAs via the β-oxidation pathway. SCFAs possess antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and immunomodulatory activities (Shang and Qiao, 2022). One study showed that Prevotellaceae_Ga6A1_group was significantly positively correlated with leg and breast muscle weight in geese, indicating a key role in promoting the growth and development of leg and breast muscles (Xu et al., 2024). The gut microbiota may enhance the metabolic pathways of intestinal probiotics or promote the production of SCFAs, thereby exerting beneficial effects (Dai et al., 2021; Yue et al., 2024). Linking these findings with the existing results, it can be inferred that reducing the dietary protein level may affect protein digestion, absorption, and fermentation processes, alter the abundance and metabolic products of the gut microbiota, and subsequently influence intestinal barrier function, immune status, and host metabolism (Tobón-Cornejo et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2022). This, in turn, affects the balance of inflammation and energy homeostasis, leading to an energy deficit that impairs the growth performance of meat ducks and redirects energy toward abnormal abdominal fat deposition, ultimately resulting in an increased abdominal fat percentage.

Effects of LP diets on NER of Cherry Valley ducks

Odors generated from livestock excreta represent a major source of environmental pollution. These odors are primarily produced through microbial fermentation of undigested carbohydrates or proteins (Heo et al., 2023; Park et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2021). Numerous early studies have demonstrated that the application of LP diets in livestock and poultry production can effectively reduce nitrogen excretion (Dao et al., 2021). A low apparent metabolic rate of protein leads to protein waste, increased nitrogen emission, and consequently environmental pollution. Proteins in feces are decomposed into nitrates and ammonia. Nitrates can contaminate water sources, while ammonia, characterized by its pungent odor, is detrimental to the health of plants, animals, and humans. Incomplete digestion of protein provides a nitrogen source for gut microbiota. An imbalance between protein levels and fermentable carbohydrate content can result in intestinal damage and loss of function (Bedford and Apajalahti, 2022). Zhu et al. (2023) found that reducing the dietary CP level by 10.00 g/kg in broilers significantly decreased nitrogen intake and nitrogen excretion, indicating that LP diets can markedly reduce nitrogen emission. Alfonso-Avila et al. (2022) reported that reducing the CP level from 19.00% to 17.00% in broilers aged 1 to 21 d could decrease nitrogen excretion by 29.00%. Recent studies have shown that amino-acid-balanced LP diets can reduce nitrogen excretion without compromising growth performance in broilers (Nanto-Hara et al., 2026). The results of this trial indicate that reducing the dietary protein level can significantly decrease nitrogen excretion, with the LP4 and LP5 groups showing the most favorable effects. By precisely supplementing limiting EAAs and maintaining appropriate ratios, the excessive catabolism caused by amino acid imbalance is significantly reduced, thereby decreasing nitrogen excreted in the form of uric acid and urea (Nanto-Hara et al., 2026). A LP diet can downregulate the mRNA expression of genes (muscle atrophy F-box protein-1 [atrogin-1], muscle-specific RING finger protein-1 [MuRF-1], and myogenic differentiation 1 [MyoD1]) related to protein degradation in skeletal muscle as well as the autophagy marker microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3B (LC3B), reducing endogenous protein breakdown and consequently lowering nitrogen loss (El-Far et al., 2024). It is noteworthy that different formulations using various miscellaneous meals to replace SBM can lead to variations in carbon and nitrogen footprints (Hu et al., 2023).

While these findings demonstrate that LP diet beneficially modulates the composition and metabolic activity of the gut microbiota, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The trial lasted only 42 d, and the long-term effects of the LP diet on the performance, health, and meat quality of Cherry Valley ducks remain unknown. The experiment was conducted under controlled environmental conditions using a single genotype (Cherry Valley ducks), which may not fully reflect the variability of commercial production systems with different genetic backgrounds and management practices. Future research should address these limitations as much as possible. Graded inclusion levels of DLR should be evaluated under commercial farm conditions to validate the practical applicability of these findings. Furthermore, long-term environmental and economic assessments are necessary to support the sustainable adoption of LP feeding strategies in the poultry industry.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results of this study demonstrate that the LP diet with DLR partially replacing SBM supported the growth of Cherry Valley ducks, improved their intestinal microbiota, and effectively reduced their NER. Reducing the dietary CP level by 2 percentage points below the minimum requirement in both the starter and grower phases, with SBM replacement ratios of 6.02% and 5.04%, respectively, achieved the most favorable outcomes. This strategy provides an effective approach to transform duck production towards resource-saving and environmentally friendly practices, and offers practical value for the design and production of LP diets in the poultry industry.
