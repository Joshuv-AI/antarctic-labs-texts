Violence against women and its interface with reproductive characteristics: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Franciele Marabotti Costa Leite, Luiza Albina Ribeiro, Ranielle de Paula Silva
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundViolence against women is a social issue that impacts the lives of thousands of women and characterizes a human rights violation, causing a dramatic public health problem.AimTo verify the prevalence and factors associated with violence against women according to reproductive characteristics.MethodologyA cross-sectional, population-based study conducted in Vitória, the capital of Espírito Santo, from January to May 2022, with 1,086 women aged 18 years or older. Bivariate and multivariate analyses were performed, using the Poisson regression model with robust variance estimation.ResultsPsychological, physical, and sexual violence were associated with the number of children, history of sexually transmitted infections, refusal to use condoms, and number of sexual partners in the previous year. Forced first sexual intercourse and age at coitarche were associated with psychological and sexual violence.ConclusionResults reveal the association between intimate partner violence against women and reproductive characteristics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25350-z.

Introduction

Violence against women is a social issue that impacts the lives of thousands of women and characterizes a human rights violation, causing a dramatic public health problem [1]. Assault against women is strongly marked by intimate partner violence (IPV) when an intimate partner seeks to exert control over the victim through acts of physical, sexual, or psychological abuse such as physical aggression, sexual coercion, and intimidation [1, 2].

A report released by the World Health Organization [3] stated that one in three women worldwide experiences violence, with 736 million enduring physical or sexual violence from intimate partners. Additionally, in countries in Oceania, South Asia, and sub-Saharan Africa, IPV prevalence is estimated to range from 33% to 51% among women aged 15 to 49 years [3]. In Brazil, the 2019 National Health Survey estimated total IPV prevalence in the 12 months before the survey at 7.60%, with psychological violence coming first (7.07%), followed by physical (2.75%) and sexual (0.68%) violence [4].

IPV dynamics involve different factors that interact with each other increasing exposure to situations of violence [3]. Some examples are sociodemographic factors such as age, education, and income, and those related to life experience, such as previous or family history of violence [4, 5]. Moreover, experiencing violence has numerous consequences that impact the lives and well-being of women at different levels, whether social, economic, interpersonal, or mental and physical health [6–8].

In this context, we highlight the National Policy for Comprehensive Attention to Women’s Health (PNAISM), aimed at comprehensive care and health promotion for Brazilian women to strengthen sexual and reproductive rights, including the fight against domestic and sexual violence [9]. PNAISM also establishes objectives for the prevention and care of women with sexually transmitted infections, chronic non-communicable diseases, and cervical cancer, which are interconnected conditions as a consequence of violence [7–9].

Considering the repercussions of violence against women, the health system and its professionals stand out as a strategic and privileged sector to identify and embrace IPV victims. However, studies indicate that health professionals are often unaware of the epidemiological characteristics of violence against women, exhibiting inadequate practices, and facing difficulties in managing and monitoring cases [10]. Effective and supportive health actions require knowledge about the epidemiological aspects of intimate partner violence. Thus, this study analyzes the prevalence and associated factors of intimate partner violence against women according to reproductive characteristics in Vitória, Espírito Santo, Brazil.

Methods

Study design

This is a cross-sectional population-based study conducted in Vitória, the capital of Espírito Santo. It has an estimated population of 369,534 people in 2021. According to the 2010 census, 49.98% (163,853) of the total population are women [11]. This study is a subset of broader research previously published [12]. Accordingly, it employed a similar methodology and sample characterization as previously published; however, it address distinct objectives and incorporates novel data that do not overlap with those previously reported.

Study population

This study was conducted with women aged 18 years or older, who had or have had an intimate partner in the 24 months prior to the interview. Women with intellectual or sensory deficits were excluded due to limitations in answering the research data collection instruments.

Data collection procedures

Data were collected from January to May 2022, at the participants’ homes and conducted by a previously trained female team of interviewers and field supervisors. Fieldwork began after data analysis of the pilot study in December 2021. After clarification concerning the research objectives, those women who agreed to participate signed an informed consent form. A questionnaire has been applied to participants by the interviewers. The interviews took place individually and in a private environment. (Appendix 1 - supplementary file).

Variables

The dependent variables were psychological, sexual, and physical violence against women practiced by an intimate partner in their lifetime. Outcomes were assessed by the World Health Organization Violence Against Women (WHO VAW STUDY) instrument, translated and validated in Brazil [12]. The validation study presented Cronbach’s alpha coefficients of 0.88 for São Paulo and 0.89 for Zona da Mata in Pernambuco, demonstrating excellent internal consistency. When checking the internal consistency of each factor, Cronbach’s alpha values for the psychological, physical, and sexual violence domains were, respectively, 0.78, 0.83, and 0.78 for São Paulo, and 0.79, 0.83, and 0.77 for the Zona da Mata of Pernambuco [13].

The independent variables comprised demographic and socioeconomic characteristics: age group (18 to 29; 30 to 39; 40 to 49; 50 to 59; 60 or older); self-declared skin color (white; black; mixed-race); years of education (0 to 8; 9 to 11; 12 or more); per capita family income in tertiles (1st – poorest; 2nd; 3rd – richest). The reproductive variables included the number of children (none; 1; 2–3; or 4 or more), age at coitarche (≤ 14 years; 15–19 years; or ≥ 20 years), refusal to use condoms during sexual intercourse (no; yes), forced first sexual intercourse (no; yes), number of sexual partners in the previous year (1; 2 or more), and history of sexually transmitted infection (STI) (no; yes).

Data processing and analysis

We adopted a multi-stage sampling process. A total of 100 census tracts were randomly selected using the list provided by the Brazilian Institute of Geography and Statistics (IBGE) in the 2010 Demographic Census [11]. Subsequently, we randomly selected 11 households per census tract, totaling 1100 households. In each household, a list of eligible women who met the study’s inclusion criteria was created, and one was then selected to participate in the interview. The sample size was calculated by estimating the prevalence of intimate partner violence in the population studied of 50% to maximize the sample, with a confidence level of 95% and an acceptable error of 5%. We considered a 95% level, 80% power, and 1:1 exposed/unexposed ratio to study the association of risk factors. To this value, 10% were added for losses, and 30% for confounding factors, reaching a sample size of 1100 women. A refusal occurred when the selected participant in the household did not agree to participate, and thus was not replaced. Additionally, if the woman was not found at her household after three attempts by the interviewer, the participant would be considered a non-response. At the end of the study, 1086 women participated.

Descriptive analysis was performed presenting crude and relative frequency. Fisher’s chi-square heterogeneity and exact tests were used in bivariate analysis according to assumptions. Poisson regression with robust variance was used to estimate the crude and adjusted prevalence ratios (PR) and their respective 95% confidence intervals (95%CI). Multivariate analysis adopted the hierarchical model with four levels.

Initially, the variables associated with the outcomes under study (p < 0.20) were included in the model to consider possible confounding factors. In the modeling stage, the independent variables were included from the most distal level of demographic and socioeconomic variables to the proximal level of women’s reproductive characteristics. Backward selection was used to exclude variables. The final model adopted a 5% significance level (p < 0.05). Statistical analyses were performed using the statistical program Stata® version 15.1.

Results

In the study sample (N = 1,086), 24.1% were aged 60 years or older, 41% were white and 40.6% were mixed race, 48.9% had 12 or more years of education, and 34,0% had per capita household income of 1 st tertile (poorest). Regarding reproductive characteristics, most women had 2 to 3 children (45.3%) and had their first intercourse between 15 and 19 years old (63.7%). About 17% of the women reported that their partner had already refused to use a condom during sexual intercourse, 6.3% had a forced first sexual intercourse, 9.2% had 2 or more sexual partners in the previous year, and almost 9% had a history of sexually transmitted infection in their lifetime (Table 1).

Lifetime psychological violence was 76% more prevalent among women who had their first sexual intercourse under the age of 14 when compared with those who had sexual intercourse at the age of 20 or older (PR: 1.76 IC95% 1.38–2.23). Women with four children or more had a 31% higher prevalence of psychological violence compared with those with no children (Table 3).

Women whose partners had already refused to use condoms had a 1.37 times higher prevalence of lifetime psychological violence than women in the reference group. The same result was found in women whose first sexual intercourse was forced (PR:1.37; CI 95%: 1.15–1.64). Those who reported two or more sexual partners in the previous year had a 41% higher prevalence of psychological violence when compared with women who had only one partner (Table 3).

Table 4 shows that physical violence remained associated with per capita family income, age at first sexual intercourse, number of children, refusal to use condoms, having had two or more sexual partners in the previous year, and history of sexually transmitted infection after adjusting for confounding variables (p < 0.05). Women who had their first sexual intercourse under the age of 14 had 109% more physical violence in their lives when compared with women aged 20 years or older. Women with four or more children had 62% more physical violence in their lives when compared with women without children (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Discussion

The results provide evidence of an association between lifetime physical, psychological, and sexual violence and demographic, socioeconomic, and reproductive characteristics. Physical, psychological, and sexual violence were associated with the number of children, history of sexually transmitted infections, refusal to wear condoms by the partner, and number of partners in the previous year.

From 2011 to 2016, Brazil recorded 211,991 occurrences of intimate partner violence against women [14]. Rosa et al. [2] highlight that IPV manifests itself in close relationships, involving harmful behaviors that result in physical, sexual, or psychological harm. Society perpetuates and normalizes unequal power dynamics between genders, to the point of creating hierarchical relationships of control between men and women, which in turn helps to perpetuate violence against women, particularly IPV [15].

Women aged 30 to 49 years showed a higher prevalence of experiencing psychological and sexual violence in their lifetime than women aged 60 years or older. Data from the 2019 National Health Survey showed a 1.42 times higher prevalence of IPV among women aged 25 to 39 compared with those aged 40 to 59 years [4]. A cross-sectional study conducted in the same municipality found a higher prevalence of psychological violence among women aged 50 to 59 years [16].

These differences in prevalence between younger and older women could be explained by the fact that younger women may be more dependent on their partners for child-rearing and household support. On the other hand, older women may have greater economic and social independence, which gives them an advantage in breaking free from abusive relationships [17, 18]. Another point to consider is that young women may find it easier to identify and report situations of violence.

Psychological and sexual harm were associated with years of education, whereas physical violence was associated with income. This finding is similar to another study, which found a 34% and 41% higher prevalence of physical violence among women with up to 9 years of study and family income in the 2nd tertile, respectively [5]. The National Health Survey revealed a 55% higher prevalence of intimate partner violence among women with an income of up to one minimum wage but found no association with education level [4].

These findings may reveal the relationship between violence and the lower educational attainment of victims, which can subsequently lead to lower income and lower-prestige occupations [19]. The study demonstrates that women who are victims of violence have higher rates of absenteeism, leading to job instability, reduced income, and access to resources, which contributes to greater exposure to violence [20]. Additionally, family and housing contexts can directly impact lower household income, such as the number of children [19].

We observed a higher prevalence of psychological and physical intimate partner violence among women who had their first sexual intercourse up to 14 years of age. A similar finding was observed in a cross-sectional study with Primary Health Care users, in which physical (47%) and psychological (27%) violence were more prevalent among women who had their coitarche at or before the age of 14 [5]. In addition to socioeconomic factors, a history of violence is highlighted as a factor associated with early sexual initiation in adolescents. The impacts of early sexual initiation can be observed, such as a greater risk of unprotected sexual intercourse, inadequate use of contraceptive methods, unplanned pregnancies, and sexually transmitted infections [21].

In countries with higher levels of education, where sexual education is introduced in schools, sexual initiation occurs later [21]. In this context, efforts are needed to develop sexual and reproductive education initiatives, especially for girls under 14 years of age, to raise awareness of their sexual and reproductive rights and to encourage positive, safe, and healthy behaviors [22]. Furthermore, these initiatives should be complemented by the implementation of strategies to prevent violence, promote a culture of peace, and combat gender discrimination [21].

Women with four or more children showed a higher prevalence of psychological, physical, and sexual intimate partner violence. A study conducted with women from a Women’s Care Center in Londrina, Paraná, found a higher frequency of domestic violence among women with three or more children [23].]. The dysfunctional societal idea of marital relationships is highlighted, as it imposes that women live for their spouses, neglecting their own needs and desires. Thus, the prevailing notion is that they should prioritize the care of their partner and children above themselves, enduring various situations, including violence, to maintain the family [24]. Thus, in a family context, the situation can be aggravated with the arrival of children due to the need for support to raise and educate them and the responsibility to provide for basic needs [25].

Importantly, sexual and reproductive planning is a conquered right that must be ensured for women [26]. Data from the home-based National Survey on Access, Use, and Promotion of the Rational Use of Medicines showed a prevalence of 32.7% of oral or injectable contraceptive use among women aged 15 to 49 years [27]. PNAISM aims to improve women’s lives and health conditions by, for example, expanding and ensuring access to contraceptive methods and family planning services [9]. Strengthening sexual and reproductive planning actions is thus necessary to favor sexual freedom and the free choice of motherhood, with autonomy and control of one’s own body, especially when identifying women in situations of violence [26].

We found an association of psychological, physical, and sexual violence among women who have had an STI in their lifetime. A quantitative cross-sectional study conducted in Paraná, in 2016, showed that women who were victims of sexual violence were 7.4 times more likely to contract an STI [28]. Violent relationships are marked by inequality and abuse, limiting women’s control of their bodies and sexuality, resulting in less sexual autonomy to decide in favor of protected sex, making them susceptible to contracting sexually transmitted infections [29].

Women whose partners refused to use condoms at some point in their relationships showed a higher prevalence of psychological, physical, and sexual violence. In a cross-sectional study conducted in the countryside of Sergipe, northeastern Brazil, Oliveira et al. [22] evidenced the non-use of condoms related to partner refusal. In a patriarchal society, in which the man’s decision prevails over the woman’s, the refusal to use condoms points to unequal gender relations, making this act a form of exercising power and control over women’s bodies, negatively affecting their physical and mental well-being [30, 31]. Refusal to use condoms during sexual intercourse is also a risk behavior that enables sexually transmitted infections and unwanted pregnancies [22].

Psychological and sexual violence were higher in women who experienced forced first sexual intercourse. A cross-sectional study conducted in Brazil found that 10% of women from São Paulo and 14% from Pernambuco responded positively to their first sexual intercourse being forced [32]. Sexual violence and its related aspects are sometimes normalized, with men’s will and desires taking precedence over those of women. This creates a ‘rape culture’ that permeates society, legitimizing and encouraging such aggression, making the identification of violent situations increasingly complex [33].

According to the Brazilian Penal Code, forced sexual intercourse and sexual harassment are sexual crimes defined by law [34]. Both acts violate women’s rights and the female body, echoing gender-based violence in the social context [35]. Moreover, experiencing forced first sexual intercourse affects the victims’ mental health, increasing the risk of developing mental and eating disorders and suicidal behaviors [6].

Psychological, physical, and sexual violence had a higher prevalence among women who had two or more sexual partners in the previous year. A study by Schraiber, D’Oliveira, and Junior [32], conducted in 1998 and 2005 with the Brazilian urban population, pointed to a higher prevalence of violence among women who had two or more sexual partners (p < 0.005). A high number of intimate partners may indicate greater exposure to risky situations with violent partners, whereas, within stable relationships, violence may be difficult to recognize [36].

Intimate partner violence is therefore a complex phenomenon that is present throughout women’s lives and requires attention from different sectors of society, particularly the healthcare sector. Experiencing violence has serious repercussions on women’s mental, physical, sexual, and reproductive health. Low self-esteem, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder, self-injury, suicidal behavior, and psychotic disorder are common mental health consequences [6]. Physical repercussions include an increase in metabolic disorders, such as cardiovascular diseases and diabetes, which may be more prevalent in victims of violence as a result of changes in habits after the traumatic experience [7]. Additionally, a higher prevalence of refusal to undergo pap smears and unwanted pregnancies was observed [6, 8].

Women who are victims of violence often seek care and counseling through this system, making it crucial not only for early identification of IPV but also for implementing appropriate preventive measures and interventions [37]. Primary Health Care plays a crucial role in addressing violence against women, as it serves as an entry point into Brazil’s Unified Health System (SUS). This level of care significantly focuses on health promotion and prevention and is structured around providing continuous and legitimate access to women throughout their lives. Additionally, it establishes a closer relationship with the community and addresses common health issues frequently associated with domestic and sexual violence against women [38].

Regarding the limitations of the study, we highlight the selection bias due to the exclusion of women with intellectual disabilities, a group that is more vulnerable to violence. However, it is understood that there are specific studies with this population. Another limitation to consider is the data collection through face-to-face interviews, which may lead to information bias due to the difficulty of reporting and the shame associated with victimization. However, many other studies use this data collection methodology. These factors may have influenced the accuracy of the information reported and led to an underestimated prevalence. To minimize bias, however, we conducted all interviews in a private space, with only the participant and the interviewer present.

Furthermore, the cross-sectional design of the study is another limitation to be highlighted, given the possibility of reverse causality, since temporality may not be possible to determine for some variables. In the context of violence, however, they are important associations for public health and should be analyzed.

Conclusion

Our findings show an association between a higher prevalence of intimate partner violence and women’s reproductive characteristics. Given the broad scale and impact of this offense, healthcare professionals must receive awareness-raising training on the topic to implement prevention and coping strategies, ensuring that these individuals are included in the healthcare and protection network, helping to break the cycle of violence.

A strategically structured and coordinated health service, along with trained professionals knowledgeable on the topic, can enhance the quality of care by employing welcoming tools, establishing bonds, monitoring, and referring these women to specialized services and support networks.
