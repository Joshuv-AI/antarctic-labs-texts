Infinite dimensional symmetric cones and gauge-reversing maps
Mark Roelands, Marten Wortel

Abstract
The famous Koecher–Vinberg theorem characterises the finite dimensional formally real Jordan algebras among the finite dimensional order unit spaces as the ones that have a symmetric cone. An alternative characterisation of symmetric cones was obtained by Walsh who showed that the symmetric cones correspond exactly to the finite dimensional order unit spaces with cone C C for which there exists a bijective map Ψ : C ∘ → C ∘ \Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ} with the property that Ψ \Psi is an order-antimorphism and homogeneous of degree − 1 -1 . In this paper we prove an infinite dimensional version of this characterisation of symmetric cones.



Keywords:
Jordan algebras, symmetric cones, order unit spaces, order-antimorphisms

Subject Classification: 58B20, 46B40, 17C36

1
Introduction

A
real Jordan algebra
is a real vector space
A
equipped with a commutative bilinear product
x\bullet y
satisfying the Jordan identity
x^{2}\bullet(x\bullet y)=x\bullet(x^{2}\bullet y)
. It is said to be
formally real
if
x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{k}^{2}=0
implies
x_{1}=\ldots=x_{k}=0
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. The concept of a formally real Jordan algebra has a rich history in mathematics. It was originally introduced by P. Jordan, J. von Neumann and E. Wigner
[
14
]
as an algebraic model for quantum mechanics, but soon after unexpected connections with Lie algebras, differential geometry and analysis were discovered (cf.
[
3
,
7
,
12
,
13
,
23
,
24
,
32
]
).

A fundamental connection between formally real Jordan algebras and the geometry of cones was discovered independently by Koecher
[
15
]
and Vinberg
[
33
]
. They showed that any real finite dimensional Hilbert space
A
with a
symmetric cone

A^{\circ}_{+}
, i.e., the closed cone
A_{+}
is self-dual and the group of linear automorphisms of
A_{+}
acts transitively on
A_{+}^{\circ}
, is a formally real Jordan algebra where
A_{+}
corresponds to the cone of squares. Conversely, a finite dimensional formally real Jordan algebra
A
can be endowed with an inner product so that the interior of the cone of squares is a symmetric cone.

The Koecher-Vinberg Theorem provides a striking link between real Jordan algebras and differential geometry. More specifically, symmetric cones are prime examples of Riemannian symmetric spaces of non-compact type. In fact, the (Riemannian) symmetry at
x\in A_{+}^{\circ}
is given by

S_{x}(y):=Q_{x}(y^{-1})
for
y\in A_{+}^{\circ}
, where
Q_{x}
is the
quadratic representation of
x
given by
Q_{x}(z):=2x\bullet(x\bullet z)-x^{2}\bullet z
. The symmetry
S_{e}
at the unit
e
is the inverse map
\iota\colon x\mapsto x^{-1}
on
A_{+}^{\circ}
, which has a special order theoretic property. Indeed, the cone
A_{+}
induces a partial ordering
\leq
on
A
by
x\leq y

if
y-x\in A_{+}
. With respect to this partial ordering the inverse map
\iota
is an order-antimorphism, i.e.,
x\leq y
if and only if
\iota(y)\leq\iota(x)
. Moreover,
\iota
is homogeneous of degree
-1
in the sense that
\iota(\lambda x)=\lambda^{-1}\iota(x)
for all
\lambda>0
and
x\in A^{\circ}_{+}
.

In finite dimensions there is a remarkable result due to Walsh
[
34
]
, which says that given a closed cone
C
(with non-empty interior) in a real vector space,
C^{\circ}
is a symmetric cone (with respect to some inner product) if and only if there exists a homogeneous degree
-1
bijective map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
that is an order-antimorphism. This result provides a completely order theoretic characterisation of finite dimensional symmetric cones. Note that in possibly infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces, the definition of a symmetric cone given above makes perfect sense, and so the main goal of this paper is to establish an infinite dimensional version of Walsh’ result, see Theorem
9.1
.

The natural infinite dimensional generalisation of formally real Jordan algebras are so-called JB-algebras, which were introduced by Alfsen, Schultz and Størmer
[
4
]
. A
JB-algebra

A
is a real Banach space with a Jordan algebra product
x\bullet y
and unit
e
(note that we define a JB-algebra to be unital, and some authors do not make this assumption), where the norm satisfies:

\|x\bullet y\|\leq\|x\|\|y\|,\quad\|x^{2}\|=\|x\|^{2},\mbox{\quad and\quad}\|x^{2}\|\leq\|x^{2}+y^{2}\|.

Important examples include the self-adjoint parts of
C^{*}
-algebras and the self-adjoint matrices
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})_{sa}
,
M_{n}(\mathbb{H})_{sa}
or
M_{3}(\mathbb{O})_{sa}
all equipped with the Jordan product
x\bullet y:=(xy+yx)/2
.
JB-algebras are very special examples of so-called complete order unit spaces, see
[
2
]
.
(Definitions will be recalled in the next section.) However, not all infinite dimensional JB-algebras
A
can be equipped with an inner-product that turns it into a Hilbert space and with respect to which the interior of the cone of squares
A_{+}
becomes a symmetric cone. It was shown by Chu
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
that Hilbert spaces with a symmetric cone are precisely the so-called unital JH-algebras. A JH-algebra is a real Jordan algebra
A
that can be endowed with an inner-product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
, making it a Hilbert space, such that
\langle x\bullet y,z\rangle=\langle y,x\bullet z\rangle
for all
x,y,z\in A
. As we shall see in the next section these unital JH-algebras are JB-algebras for the equivalent order unit norm.

Thus, to characterise the symmetric cones among the possibly infinite dimensional complete order unit spaces in a similar way as Walsh’s finite dimensional one, additional assumptions need to be made on the order unit space or on its cone. In this paper we establish such an infinite dimensional characterisation if one assumes either reflexivity or a condition on the cone closely related to reflexivity, cf.
Theorem

9.1
. We mention that in an earlier work by the first two authors and H. van Imhoff
[
21
]
the case where the cone in the complete order unit space is strictly convex was considered. In that case it was shown that there exists a homogeneous degree
-1
bijective map that is an order-antimorphism if and only if the order unit space is a spin factor, which form a particular class of JH-algebras.

The proof contains two important steps. Firstly, we show in Section
7
that the cone is homogeneous, i.e., the group
\mathrm{Aut}(C)
of linear automorphisms of
C
acts transitively on
C^{\circ}
. In fact, to establish that result we only need to assume that the order unit space is spanned by the extreme vectors of the cone. Subsequently we will use ideas from metric geometry, involving horofunctions, in Section
8
to construct an inner-product on the order unit space, and show that the cone is self-dual. Basically, the horofunctions allow one to study the action of the order-antimorphism on the boundary at infinity, which can be connected with the dual space of the order unit space. The relevant results about horofunctions will be recalled in Sections
4
and
5
, many of which were established by Walsh in
[
34
,
35
]
. But we begin by collecting the basic concepts concerning order unit spaces.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Order unit spaces

Firstly, we fix the terminology and recall the basic concepts concerning order unit spaces.

Let
V
be a real vector space. A subset
C
of
V
is called a
cone
if
C
is convex,
\lambda C\subseteq C
for all
\lambda\geq 0
, and
C\cap-C=\{0\}
. The cone
C
induces a partial ordering on
V
by,
v\leq w
if
w-v\in C
, which turns it into a partially ordered vector space. The cone is said to be
Archimedean
if for each
v\in V
and
w\in C
we have that
nv\leq w
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
implies that
v\leq 0
. It is said to be
almost Archimedean
if
-x/n\leq y\leq x/n
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
x,y\in V
implies that
y=0
. A straightforward verification shows that every Archimedean cone is almost Archimedean.

An element
u\in C
is said to be an
order unit
if for each
v\in V
there exists
\lambda\geq 0
such that
-\lambda u\leq v\leq\lambda u
.
The triple
(V,C,u)
is said to be an
order unit space
if
C
is an Archimedean cone in
V
with order unit
u
. In that case
V
can be equipped with the so-called
order unit norm
,

\|v\|_{u}:=\inf\{\lambda\geq 0\colon-\lambda u\leq v\leq\lambda u\}\mbox{\quad for }v\in V.

With respect to the order unit norm the cone
C
is closed and has a non-empty interior, denoted
C^{\circ}
, and
u\in C^{\circ}
. Moreover, each
w\in C^{\circ}
is an order unit for
(V,C)
. Furthermore, the boundary of
C
is denoted by
\partial C
.
Throughout the paper we almost always work with order unit spaces.

The dual space
V^{*}
of an order unit space
V
is an ordered vector space with
dual cone

C^{*}:=\{\varphi\in V^{*}\colon\varphi(v)\geq 0\mbox{ for all }v\in C\}
. The elements of
C^{*}
are called
positive
linear functionals. It is known
[
2
, Lemma 1.16]
that a linear functional
\varphi
on an order unit space
V
is positive if and only if it is bounded with
\|\varphi\|=\varphi(u)
. A positive linear functional
\varphi
on an order unit space
V
is called a
state
if
\varphi(u)=1
. The set of all states is called the
state space
of
V
and will be denoted by
S=S(V)
. The state space
S
is a
w^{*}
-compact convex set, and hence it is the
w^{*}
-closure of the convex hull of its extreme points by the Krein-Milman theorem. The extreme points of
S
are called
pure
states. A useful fact that will be used several times in the paper is that the states determine the norm and ordering on
V
, see
[
1
, Lemma 1.18]
.

Given
v\leq w
in
V
we write
[v,w]:=\{z\in V\colon v\leq z\leq w\}
to denote the
order interval
. A subset
A
of
V
is said to be
full
if
[v,w]\subseteq A
for all
v,w\in A
with
v\leq w
. We call a non-zero vector
r\in C
an
extreme vector of

C
if
0\leq v\leq r
implies that
v=\lambda r
for some
\lambda\geq 0
.

We will also need the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
(V,C)
be a partially ordered vector space and
x\leq y
be distinct points in
V
. Consider the following statements.

(i)

[x,y]
is totally ordered;

(ii)

[x,y]=\{(1-t)x+ty\colon 0\leq t\leq 1\}
;

(iii)

y-x
is an extreme vector of
C
.

Then
(iii)\Leftrightarrow(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
, and
(i)\Rightarrow(iii)
if
(V,C)
is almost Archimedean.

Proof.

Since translation by
-x
is an order-isomorphism, we may assume that
x=0
. If
y
is an extreme vector of
C
, then
[0,y]=\{ty\colon 0\leq t\leq 1\}
, which yields
(ii)
. If
[0,y]=\{ty\colon 0\leq t\leq 1\}
, then
[0,y]
is totally ordered, as comparison of elements now only depends on the parameter
0\leq t\leq 1
, and so
y
is an extreme vector of
C
. This shows
(iii)\Leftrightarrow(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
.

For the last implication, suppose that
(V,C)
is almost Archimedean and
[0,y]
is totally ordered. Let
z\in[0,y]
. For each
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
it follows that
z
and
\lambda y
are comparable and define
\mu:=\sup\{\lambda\geq 0\colon\lambda y\leq z\}=\inf\{\lambda\leq 1\colon\lambda y\geq z\}
. Then
(\mu-\frac{1}{n})y\leq z\leq(\mu+\frac{1}{n})y
and so
-\frac{1}{n}y\leq z-\mu y\leq\frac{1}{n}y
. Since
(V,C)
is almost Archimedean,
z-\mu y=0
. So
z=\mu y
showing that
y
is an extreme vector of
C
.
∎

Definition 2.2
.

Given
x\in C^{\circ}
and
w\in V
we define
\ell_{x}^{w}:=\{x+tw\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}\cap C^{\circ}
. A subset
L
of
C^{\circ}
is called an
extreme half-line
if there exists an
x\in C^{\circ}
and and extreme vector
r
of
C
such that
L=\ell_{x}^{r}
.

Note that, given
x\in C^{\circ}
and
w\in V
, we have
\ell_{x}^{w}=\ell_{x}^{-w}
. The following lemma gives an order-theoretic characterisation of extreme half-lines and is similar to
[
17
, Proposition 3.1]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be an order unit space and
L\subseteq C^{\circ}
. Then
L
is an extreme half-line if and only if
L
contains at least two points and is maximal among the subsets of
C^{\circ}
that are totally ordered and full.

Proof.

Suppose that
L\subseteq C^{\circ}
contains distinct points
x
and
y
and is maximal among the sets in
C^{\circ}
that are totally ordered and full. We first show that
L\subseteq\ell_{x}^{y-x}
. If
z\in L\setminus\{x,y\}
, then there are six options describing how
x,y
, and
z
are ordered. Since it is analogous to show that
z\in\ell_{x}^{y-x}
for all these cases, we shall only cover the situation where
z\leq y\leq x
. As
[y,x]\subseteq L
is totally ordered, it follows from
Lemma

2.1
that
x-y
is an extreme vector of
C
, so
\ell_{x}^{y-x}
is an extreme half-line. Furthermore, as
[z,x]\subseteq L
is totally ordered as well, we have that
y=sz+(1-s)x
for some
0<s\leq 1
by Lemma
2.1
. Hence
z=x+\frac{1}{s}(y-x)\in\ell_{x}^{y-x}
. As any extreme half-line in
C^{\circ}
is totally ordered and full by Lemma
2.1
, it follows from the maximality of
L
that
L=\ell_{x}^{y-x}
.

For the converse, suppose
L
is an extreme half-line. Then there exists
x\in C^{\circ}
and an extreme vector
r
of
C
such that
L=\ell_{x}^{r}
, and so
L
contains two distinct points. Let
S\subseteq C^{\circ}
be a full and totally ordered subset that contains
\ell_{x}^{r}
. If
y\in S
is different from
x
, then
y\leq x
,
x\leq y\leq x+r
, or
x+r\leq y
. If
y\leq x
, then
x\in[y,x+r]
and
[y,x+r]
is a subset of
S
, as
S
is full, so it is totally ordered. So, by Lemma
2.1
,
x=\lambda y+(1-\lambda)(x+r)
for some
0<\lambda\leq 1
, and hence
y=\lambda^{-1}x+(1-\lambda^{-1})(x+r)=x+(1-\lambda^{-1})r
. On the other hand, if
x\leq y\leq x+r
, then
y=\sigma x+(1-\sigma)(x+r)=x+(1-\sigma)r
for some
0\leq\sigma\leq 1
by Lemma
2.1
, as
[x,x+r]\subseteq S
is totally ordered. Likewise, if
x+r\leq y
, then
x+r\in[x,y]
and we have
x+r=\tau x+(1-\tau)y
for some
0\leq\tau<1
, which implies that
y=x+(1-\tau)^{-1}r
. We conclude that
S=\ell_{x}^{r}=L
and so
L
is maximal among the sets in
C^{\circ}
that are full and totally ordered.
∎

We conclude this subsection with the following useful fact.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be an order unit space with state space
S
. Then
T\colon V\times C^{\circ}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
given by
T(x,y)=M(x/y)
is continuous.

Proof.

If we equip
S
with the
w
*-topology, the map
\psi\colon(V,\|\cdot\|_{u})\to(C(S),\|\cdot\|_{\infty})
defined by
\psi(x)(\varphi)=\varphi(x)
is a
bi-positive isometric embedding by
[
1
, Lemma 1.18]
. Note that the composition of continuous functions

\displaystyle(x,y)

\displaystyle\mapsto(\psi(x),\psi(y))\mapsto(\psi(x),\psi(y)^{-1})\mapsto\psi(x)\psi(y)^{-1}

\displaystyle\mapsto\|\psi(x)\psi(y)^{-1}\|_{\infty}=\sup_{S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{\varphi(y)}=M(x/y)

implies that
T
is continuous as required.
∎

2.2
Gauge-reversing maps on cones in order unit spaces

Let
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
be order unit spaces. A map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is said to be
antitone
if
v,w\in C^{\circ}
with
v\leq w
implies
\Psi(w)\leq\Psi(v)
. It is said to be an
order-antimorphism
if
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a bijective antitone map whose inverse is antitone. The map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is said to be
homogeneous of degree
-1
if
\Psi(\lambda v)=\lambda^{-1}\Psi(v)
for all
v\in C^{\circ}
and
\lambda>0
. Order-antimorphisms
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
which are homogeneous of degree
-1
correspond to so-called gauge-reversing maps.

Given
v\in V
and
w\in C^{\circ}
we define

M(v/w):=\inf\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}\colon v\leq\mu w\}.

(2.1)

Note that
M(v/w)<\infty
, as
w
is an order unit. The
M
-function is called a
gauge
. A bijective map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is said to be
gauge-reversing
if
M(v/w)=M(\Psi(w)/\Psi(v))
for all
v,w\in C^{\circ}
.
The following result is well-known
[
26
]
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
be order unit spaces and
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
a bijection. Then
\Psi
is an order-antimorphism and homogeneous of degree
-1
if and only if
\Psi
is gauge-reversing.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a bijective order-antimorphism and homogeneous of degree
-1
. If
v\leq\lambda w
in
C^{\circ}
, then
\lambda^{-1}\Psi(w)=\Psi(\lambda w)\leq\Psi(v)
, so that
\Psi(w)\leq\lambda\Psi(v)
. This implies that
M(\Psi(w)/\Psi(v))\leq M(v/w)
. On the other hand,
\Psi^{-1}
is also an order-antimorphism and homogeneous of degree
-1
, so
M(w/v)\leq M(\Psi(v)/\Psi(w))
, and hence
\Psi
is gauge-reversing.

Conversely, suppose
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing bijection. If
v\leq w
in
C^{\circ}
, then
M(\Psi(w)/\Psi(v))=M(v/w)\leq 1
, so that
\Psi(w)\leq\Psi(v)
. Likewise
\Psi(w)\leq\Psi(v)
implies
M(v/w)=M(\Psi(w)/\Psi(v))\leq 1
,
so that
v\leq w
, which shows that
\Psi
and
\Psi^{-1}
are antitone. To see that
\Psi
is homogeneous of degree
-1
,
let
v\in C^{\circ}
and
\lambda>0
and consider
w=\lambda v
. It follows that
M(\Psi(w)/\Psi(v))=M(v/w)=\lambda^{-1}
and

M(\Psi(v)/\Psi(w))=M(w/v)=\lambda
. Hence
\lambda\Psi(w)\leq\Psi(v)\leq\lambda\Psi(w)
, so
\Psi(\lambda v)=\Psi(w)=\lambda^{-1}\Psi(v)
.
∎

We point out that in the same vein it can be shown that a bijective map
f\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is an order-isomorphism and homogeneous of degree
1
if and only if
f
is gauge-preserving, see
[
26
]
. In fact, it was shown by Schäffer
[
30
, Theorem B]
that if
V
and
W
are order unit spaces, every bijective map
f\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
which is an order-isomorphism and is homogeneous of degree
1
must be linear. We will use this result later in the paper.

Lemma
2.3
has the following direct corollary for gauge-reversing maps.

Corollary 2.6
.

If
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are order unit spaces and
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map, then
\Psi
maps extreme half-lines onto extreme half-lines.

2.3
JBW-algebras

A
JBW-algebra
can be defined as a JB-algebra that is a dual space; it is the Jordan analogue of a von Neumann algebra. A finite type
I
JBW-factor is a JBW-algebra isomorphic to the self-adjoint matrices
M_{n}(\mathbb{R})_{sa}
,
M_{n}(\mathbb{C})_{sa}
,
M_{n}(\mathbb{H})_{sa}
,
M_{3}(\mathbb{O})_{sa}
, or a spin factor
\mathbb{R}\oplus H
where
H
is a real Hilbert space and
(\lambda,x)\bullet(\mu,y):=(\lambda\mu+\left\langle x,y\right\rangle_{H},\lambda y+\mu x)
; all these algebras are also JH-algebras, where the matrix algebras are equipped with the inner product
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle:=\mathrm{Tr}(A\bullet B)
. For more details about these concepts and unexplained terminology in the next proof we refer to
[
3
, Chapters 2 and 3]
.

Recall that a JH-algebra
A
is a real Jordan algebra with an inner-product, for which it is a Hilbert space, such that
\left\langle x\bullet y,z\right\rangle=\left\langle y,x\bullet z\right\rangle
for all
x,y,z\in A
. It turns out that unital JH-algebras are JB-algebras for the equivalent order unit norm. This relies on the spectral decomposition for elements in JH-algebras, and on page 41 in
[
27
]
a sketch of the proof for this result is given. For sake of clarity, we will work this out in more detail.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
A
be an associative JH-algebra such that the map
L\colon A\to B(A)
given by
L(x)y:=x\bullet y
is injective. Then there exists an orthogonal basis of idempotents
(p_{k})_{k}
such that
A=\mathbb{R}p_{k}\oplus A_{0}
as an orthogonal algebra direct sum for all
k
and
A=\ell_{2}-\bigoplus_{k}\mathbb{R}p_{k}
.

Proof.

If
A
is an associative JH-algebra and
L
is injective, then
A
is a flat JH-triple such that
L(A,A)\neq 0
, which meets the requirements of the starred remark in
[
25
, Theorem IV.2.4]
. Here
L(x,y)=x{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.2pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt,height=5.0pt\kern 5.0pt\vrule width=0.2pt}\hrule height=0.2pt}}\,}\,y:=L(x\bullet y)+[L(x),L(y)]
, where
[T,S]:=TS-ST
, and the triple product is given by
\{x,y,z\}:=(x{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.2pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.2pt,height=5.0pt\kern 5.0pt\vrule width=0.2pt}\hrule height=0.2pt}}\,}\,y)(z)
. Hence there exists a nonzero generalised tripotent
c
in
A
, that is
c^{3}=\pm c
, such that
A=\mathbb{R}c\oplus A^{\prime}
as an orthogonal algebra direct sum. Note that
0<\left\langle c,c\right\rangle=\pm\left\langle c^{3},c\right\rangle=\pm\left\langle c^{2},c^{2}\right\rangle
forces
c^{3}=c
, and
p:=c^{2}
is a nonzero idempotent for which we also have
A=\mathbb{R}p\oplus A^{\prime}
as an orthogonal algebra direct sum. Now consider a maximal set of orthogonal idempotents
(p_{k})_{k}
such that
A=\mathbb{R}p_{k}\oplus A_{k}
for all
k
. It follows that
A_{0}:=\cap_{k}A_{k}
is an associative JH-algebra, and if
A_{0}\neq\{0\}
, then it would contain a nonzero idempotent by the argument above as
L
is also injective when restricted to
A_{0}
, which contradicts the maximality of
(p_{k})_{k}
. We conclude that
A_{0}=\{0\}
, and if
x\in A
is such that
\left\langle x,p_{k}\right\rangle=0
for all
k
, then
x\in A_{0}
so
x=0
. Hence
(p_{k})_{k}
is an orthogonal basis for
A
and we have that
A=\ell_{2}-\bigoplus_{k}\mathbb{R}p_{k}
.
∎

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
A
be a JH-algebra with unit
e
. Then
e
is an order unit and the order unit norm is an equivalent norm that turns
A
into a JB-algebra.

Proof.

Since
A
is unital, the map that sends
x\in A
to the left multiplication operator
L(x)y:=x\bullet y
is injective, since if
L(x)=0
, then
0=L(x)e=x
. Hence the map
L\colon A\to B(A)
defined by
x\mapsto L(x)
is nonzero and bounded by
[
27
, Lemma 1.1]
. Let
x\in A
and consider the JH-algebra generated by
x
and
e
denoted by JH
(x,e)
. Since
L
is bounded, it follows that JH
(x,e)
is an associative JH-algebra. Furthermore, the restriction of
L
to JH
(x,e)
is injective, so by
Lemma

2.7
there is an orthogonal basis of idempotents
(p_{k})_{k}
such that
\mathrm{JH}(x,e)=\ell_{2}-\bigoplus_{k}\mathbb{R}p_{k}
. For each
p_{k}
it follows that

\|p_{k}\|=\|L(p_{k})p_{k}\|\leq\|L(p_{k})\|\|p_{k}\|\leq\|L\|\|p_{k}\|^{2},

so that
\|p_{k}\|\geq\|L\|^{-1}
. If
(p_{k})_{k}
would contain a countably infinite set
p_{1},p_{2},\dots
, then for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we would have for
P_{n}:=p_{1}+\dots+p_{n}
that

\left\langle P_{n},P_{n}\right\rangle=\left\langle P_{n},P_{n}\right\rangle+\left\langle P_{n},e-P_{n}\right\rangle=\left\langle P_{n},e\right\rangle\leq\left\langle e,e\right\rangle,

so that
n\|L\|^{-2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|p_{k}\|^{2}=\|P_{n}\|^{2}\leq\|e\|^{2}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, a contradiction. Hence there is an
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(p_{k})_{k}=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}
,
p_{1}+\dots+p_{n}=e
, and
x=\lambda_{1}p_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{n}p_{n}
for some
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. By
[
27
, Lemma 1.4]
we have that
p\bullet q=0
for any two orthogonal idempotents and the identity in the proof of this statement also shows that
\left\langle p,q\right\rangle\geq 0
for any two idempotents
p
and
q
in
A
. This implies that
x\in A
is a square if and only if
\left\langle x,y^{2}\right\rangle\geq 0
for all
y\in A
. Hence the set of squares is an Archimedean cone in
A
and
e
is an order unit, as

-\Bigl(\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\lambda_{k}\Bigr)e\leq x\leq\Bigl(\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\lambda_{k}\Bigr)e.

If we denote the order unit norm by
\|\cdot\|_{e}
, observe that

\|x\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}^{2}\|p_{k}\|^{2}\leq\left(\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\lambda_{k}^{2}\right)\sum_{k=1}^{N}\|p_{k}\|^{2}=\|e\|^{2}\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\lambda_{k}^{2}=\|e\|^{2}\left\|x\right\|_{e}^{2},

so that
\|x\|\leq\|e\|\|x\|_{e}
. On the other hand, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality we have that

\|x\|_{e}=\max_{1\leq k\leq N}|\lambda_{k}|=\max_{1\leq k\leq N}|\left\langle x,p_{k}\right\rangle|\leq\|x\|\max_{1\leq k\leq N}\|p_{k}\|\leq\|e\|\|x\|.

Hence the norms are equivalent. Moreover, if
-e\leq x\leq e
, then
-1\leq\lambda_{k}\leq 1
for all
k
, and so
\lambda_{k}^{2}\leq 1
for all
k
, thus
0\leq x^{2}\leq e
. By
[
3
, Theorem 1.11]

A
is a JB-algebra for the order unit norm.
∎

Theorem

2.8
is used in the following characterisation of unital JH-algebras.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
A
be a unital real Jordan algebra with unit
e
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

A
can be equipped with an inner product under which it is a JH-algebra.

(ii)

(A,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{e})
is a reflexive JB-algebra.

(iii)

(A,\left\|\cdot\right\|_{e})
is a JBW-algebra not containing an infinite sequence
(p_{n})_{n}
of orthogonal projections.

(iv)

A
is a finite direct sum of finite type
I
JBW-factors.

Proof.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
: By
Theorem

2.8
, the order unit norm is a JB-algebra norm equivalent with the JH-norm.

(ii)\Rightarrow(iii)
: Since
A
has a predual, it is a JBW-algebra. Suppose that
A
contains an infinite sequence
(p_{n})_{n}
of orthogonal projections. Then

(\lambda_{n})_{n}\mapsto\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{n}p_{n}

is an isometric embedding of
c_{0}
into
A
, contradicting the reflexivity of
A
.

(iii)\Rightarrow(iv)
: If the central projection onto the nonatomic part of
A
is nonzero, then we can keep splitting it up as a sum of two orthogonal projections, yielding an infinite sequence of orthogonal projections. Hence
A
is atomic, so it is a direct sum of type
I
factors. The restriction on the projections yields that there can only be finitely many direct summands, and that each direct summand has to be a finite type
I
JBW-factor.

(iv)\Rightarrow(i)
: Take
\left\langle x,y\right\rangle
to be the sum of the inner products on each component.
∎

3
Finsler metric and symmetries

One can use the gauge function
M(x/y)
to define a metric on
C^{\circ}
in an order unit space
V
as follows:
For
x,y\in C^{\circ}
let

d_{T}(x,y):=\max\{\log M(x/y),\log M(y/x)\}.

This metric is called the
Thompson metric

[
31
]
, and has a Finsler structure. More precisely, if one defines on the tangent space
T_{w}C^{\circ}\cong V
at
w\in C^{\circ}
the Finsler metric by
F(w,x):=\|x\|_{w}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{w}
is the order unit norm with respect to
w
, then
d_{T}(x,y)
is the infimum of lengths,

L(\gamma)=\int_{0}^{1}F(\gamma(t),\gamma^{\prime}(t))\,\mathrm{d}t,

over all piecewise
C^{1}
-smooth paths
\gamma\colon[0,1]\to C^{\circ}
with
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(1)=y
, see
[
28
]
.
It should be pointed out that in this setting
F
does not satisfy the usual smoothness and strong convexity conditions used in the theory of Riemann-Finsler manifolds
[
6
]
.

It is well-known that the metric balls of
d_{T}
are convex and the topology of
d_{T}
coincides with the order unit norm topology on
C^{\circ}
if
V
is an order unit space, see
[
19
, Chapter 2]
. Clearly all gauge-preserving maps and gauge-reversing maps
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
are
d_{T}
-isometries.

One of the main results of this paper is that the existence of a single gauge-reversing map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
turns
(C^{\circ},d_{T})
(and hence also
(K^{\circ},d_{T})
) into a symmetric Finsler space, see Theorem
7.4
.

Definition 3.1
.

Given
(C^{\circ},d_{T})
and
x\in C^{\circ}
we call a surjective
d_{T}
-isometry
\sigma_{x}\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
a
d_{T}
-symmetry at
x
if
\sigma_{x}
has
x
as a unique fixed point and satisfies
\sigma^{2}_{x}(y)=y
for all
y\in C^{\circ}
. Moreover,
(C^{\circ},d_{T})
is said to be a
symmetric Finsler space
, if there exists a
d_{T}
-symmetry at each
x\in C^{\circ}
.

The interior of the cone of squares in a JB-algebra
(A_{+}^{\circ},d_{T})
is a symmetric Finsler space where the symmetry at
x\in A_{+}^{\circ}
is given by
y\mapsto Q_{x}(y^{-1})
.

It turns out that
d_{T}
-symmetries
\sigma_{x}\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
are gauge-reversing maps. To show this it is convenient to first recall some facts about the geodesics in
(C^{\circ},d_{T})
. Recall that a map
\gamma\colon I\to C^{\circ}
, where
I
is a possibly unbounded interval in
\mathbb{R}
, is called a
geodesic path
if

d_{T}(\gamma(t),\gamma(s))=|t-s|\qquad\mbox{ for all }t,s\in I.

The image
\gamma(I)
will be called a
geodesic
in
(C^{\circ},d_{T})
.

Of particular interest in this work are unique geodesics of
d_{T}
, which were studied in
[
20
]
. To analyse them, it is useful to note that if
x,y\in C^{\circ}
are linearly independent, then the distance
d_{T}(x,y)
is completely determined by their distance in the
2
-dimensional subcone
C(x,y):=\mathrm{Span}\{x,y\}\cap C
. Unique geodesics in
2
-dimensional cones can be characterised as follows, see
[
20
, Lemma 3.3]
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
C
be a
2
-dimensional cone and let
x,y\in C^{\circ}
. Then there is a unique geodesic in
(C^{\circ},d_{T})
connecting
x
and
y
if and only if either

(i)

M(x/y)=M(y/x)
, or,

(ii)

M(x/y)=M(y/x)^{-1}
, in which case
x=\lambda y
for some
\lambda>0
.

Given
x
and
y
linearly independent vectors in
C^{\circ}
with
M(x/y)=M(y/x)
, we call the unique geodesic passing through
x
and
y
in
(C(x,y)^{\circ},d_{T})
a
type I

x
is called a
type II
geodesic in
(C(x,y)^{\circ},d_{T})
. These geodesics also define geodesics in
(C^{\circ},d_{T})
and the type II geodesic is of the form
t\mapsto e^{-t}x
, and the type I geodesic is of the form
t\mapsto\alpha(e^{t}v+e^{-t}w)
for some linearly independent vectors
v,w\in\partial C
and
\alpha>0
by

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be an order unit space and let
x,y\in C^{\circ}
be linearly independent. Suppose that
M(x/y)=M(y/x)
and define

x

x

y

if and only there exist no
z\in V\setminus\{0\}
and
\varepsilon>0
such that

x^{\prime}+tz\in\partial C(x,y,z)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad y^{\prime}+tz\in\partial C(x,y,z)

for all
|t|<\varepsilon
. Here
C(x,y,z):=\mathrm{Span}\{x,y,z\}\cap C
.

In particular, we have the following consequence, which will be useful in the sequel.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be an order unit space and
x\in C^{\circ}
. If
r,s\in\partial C
are such that
r+s=x
, then the type I geodesic
\gamma(t)=e^{t}r+e^{-t}s
passing through

is unique in
(C^{\circ},d_{T})
if either
r
or
s
is an extreme vector.

Proof.

Suppose without loss of generality that
r
is an extreme vector and in view of Theorem

, assume for sake of a contradiction, that there is a
z\in V\setminus\{0\}
and an
\varepsilon>0
such that
r+tz,s+tz\in\partial C(z,r,s)
whenever
|t|<\varepsilon
. But then

r=\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}(r+\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon z)+\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}(r-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon z),

and so by the extremality of
r
, it follows that
r+\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon z
and
r-\textstyle{\frac{1}{2}}\varepsilon z
are positive multiples of
r
. Hence
z
is a multiple of
r
and by our assumption
s-\lambda r\in\partial C(r,s)
for some
\lambda>0
which is impossible.

Using the knowledge of the unique geodesics we now show that each

Theorem 3.5

Let

\gamma_{x}(t)=e^{-t}x

.

\displaystyle\qquad=\log M(\sigma_{x}(z)/\lambda^{-1}\sigma_{x}(y))=\log\lambda+\log M(\sigma_{x}(z)/\sigma_{x}(y)),

Remark 3.6
.

It should be noted that not every gauge-reversing map is a
d_{T}
-symmetry as is illustrated in the example below. On the
2\times 2
symmetric real matrices, consider the gauge-reversing map given by

A\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\
2&0\\
\end{pmatrix}A^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\
-1&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Expressing this in terms of the coefficients yields

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&c\\
\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\frac{1}{ac-b^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}a&2b\\
2b&4c\\
\end{pmatrix}.

This map has no positive-definite fixed points, since its square is the map

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
b&c\\
\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{4}a&b\\
b&4c\\
\end{pmatrix}

which does not have positive-definite fixed points as
a
and
c
must be nonzero for the matrix to be positive-definite.

4
Horofunctions and the detour distance

In our analysis horofunctions will play an important role. In particular, we will use the so-called Funk and reverse-Funk horofunctions, see
[
34
]
. Recall that on
C^{\circ}
in an order unit space
V
the
Funk metric
is defined by

F(x,y):=\log M(x/y),

and the
reverse-Funk metric
is given by

RF(x,y):=\log M(y/x)\mbox{\qquad(so $RF(x,y)=F(y,x)$)}.

It should be noted that
d_{T}(x,y)=\max\{F(x,y),RF(x,y)\}
for
x,y\in C^{\circ}
.

The functions
F
and
RF
are not metrics on
C^{\circ}
, but are quasi-metrics. Recall that a
quasi-metric
on a space
X
is a function
\rho\colon X\times X\to\mathbb{R}
satisfying:

(1)

\rho(x,x)=0
for all
x\in X
.

(2)

\rho(x,z)\leq\rho(x,y)+\rho(y,z)
for all
x,y,z\in X
(triangle inequality).

(3)

\rho(x,y)=0=\rho(y,x)
for
x,y\in X
implies
x=y
.

So, a quasi-metric
\rho
need not be symmetric nor nonnegative. It is straightforward to check that if
\rho
is a quasi-metric on
X
, then the reverse function,
\rho_{R}(x,y)=\rho(y,x)
, is also a quasi-metric on
X
. Moreover,

\rho_{\infty}(x,y):=\max\{\rho(x,y),\rho_{R}(x,y)\}
is a metric on
X
, and for each
y\in X
the functions
x\mapsto\rho(x,y)
and
x\mapsto\rho_{R}(x,y)
are
1
-Lipschitz on
(X,\rho_{\infty})
.

This allows one to construct the so-called horofunction boundary of
(X,\rho)
as follows. Fix a basepoint
b\in X
and consider the space
C(X)
of continuous functions on
(X,\rho_{\infty})
equipped with the pointwise convergence topology. Let
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
be the set of
1
-Lipschitz functions
f
on
(X,\rho_{\infty})
with
f(b)=0
. Then it follows from Tychonov’s compactness theorem that
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
is a compact subset of
C(X)
, as
f(x)\in[-\rho_{\infty}(x,b),\rho_{\infty}(x,b)]
for all
x\in X
, and
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
is a closed subset.

We like to point out that on
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
the topology of compact convergence coincides with the topology of pointwise convergence. Indeed, the sets
B_{C}(h,\varepsilon):=\{g\in\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)\colon|g(x)-h(x)|<\varepsilon\mbox{ for all }x\in C\}
, where
C\subseteq X
is compact and
\varepsilon>0
, generate the compact convergence topology. As
C
is compact, we can find
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in C
such that
C\subseteq B(x_{1})\cup\ldots\cup B(x_{n})
, where
B(x_{i})=\{y\in S\colon\rho_{\infty}(x_{i},y)<\varepsilon/3\}
. Then
U:=\{g\in\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)\colon|g(x_{i})-h(x_{i})|<\varepsilon/3\mbox{ for }i=1,\ldots,n\}
is a neighbourhood of
h
in the pointwise convergence topology. But for
g\in U
and
x\in C
, there exists a
k
with
x\in B(x_{k})
, and

|g(x)-h(x)|\leq|g(x)-g(x_{k})|+|g(x_{k})-h(x_{k})|+|h(x)-h(x_{k})|<\varepsilon.

It follows that
U\subseteq B_{C}(g,\varepsilon)
. As the compact convergence topology is finer than the pointwise convergence topology, we conclude the topologies are the same on
\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
.

Define
i_{\rho}\colon X\to C(X)
by

i_{\rho}(y)(x):=\rho(x,y)-\rho(b,y)\mbox{\qquad for $x\in X$}.

(4.1)

Then
i_{\rho}(y)\in\mathrm{Lip}_{b}(X)
, and hence
i_{\rho}(X)
has a compact closure in
C(X)
, which is denoted
\overline{i_{\rho}(X)}
and called the
horofunction compactification
of
(X,\rho)
. The boundary
\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(X):=\overline{i_{\rho}(X)}\setminus i_{\rho}(X)
is called the
horofunction boundary
of
(X,\rho)
, and its elements are called
horofunctions
. It should be noted that in this generality the horofunction compactification need not be a compactification in the usual topological sense, which will not be required here.

Furthermore, if
f\in\overline{i_{\rho}(X)}
, then there exists a net
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in
X
such that
(i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
converges to
f
. Indeed, if
V_{f}
denotes the collection of all neighbourhoods of
f
in
\overline{i_{\rho}(X)}
, then
A=\{(V,y)\in V_{f}\times X\colon i_{\rho}(y)\in V\}
can be preordered by setting
(V_{1},y_{1})\leq(V_{2},y_{2})
if
V_{2}\subseteq V_{1}
, which forms a directed set. By letting
y_{\alpha}
be the second coordinate of
\alpha\in A
, we obtain a net
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in
X
such that
(i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
converges to
f
.

In particular, we get horofunction boundaries of the Funk metric and reverse-Funk metric:

\mathcal{H}_{F}=\mathcal{H}_{F}(C^{\circ})=\overline{i_{F}(C^{\circ})}\setminus i_{F}(C^{\circ})\mbox{\qquad and \qquad}\mathcal{H}_{RF}=\mathcal{H}_{RF}(C^{\circ})=\overline{i_{RF}(C^{\circ})}\setminus i_{RF}(C^{\circ}),

which are respectively called the
Funk horofunction boundary
and
reverse-Funk horofunction boundary
.

In general it is complicated to compute the horofunctions explicitly. In case
C^{\circ}
is a finite dimensional symmetric cone, however, there exists the following characteristation, see
[
18
,
16
]
:

\mathcal{H}_{RF}=\{x\in C^{\circ}\mapsto\log M(y/x)\colon y\in\partial C\mbox{ with }\|y\|_{e}=1\}

and

\mathcal{H}_{F}=\{x\in C^{\circ}\mapsto\log M(z/x^{-1})\colon z\in\partial C\mbox{ with }\|z\|_{e}=1\}.

For our purposes we need to consider the so called Busemann points in the horofunction boundary. Let us recall the definition of these points.
A net
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in a quasi-metric space
(X,\rho)
is called an
almost geodesic for
s\in X
if for all
\varepsilon>0
there is a
\beta
such that

\rho(s,y_{\alpha^{\prime}})\geq\rho(s,y_{\alpha})+\rho(y_{\alpha},y_{\alpha^{\prime}})-\varepsilon

for all
\beta\leq\alpha\leq\alpha^{\prime}
. A net
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in
(X,\rho)
is called an
almost geodesic
if it is an almost geodesic for some
s\in X
.

The next lemma was proved in
[
35
, Proposition 2.3]
in the context of metric spaces. It provides a characterisation of almost geodesics and shows that an almost geodesic net in a quasi-metric space
(X,\rho)
yields a function in the horofunction compactification. To formulate it we need the following notion. A net of real-valued functions
(f_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
on
S
is said to be
almost non-increasing
if for each
\varepsilon>0
there is a
\beta
such that
f_{\alpha}\geq f_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\varepsilon
for all
\beta\leq\alpha\leq\alpha^{\prime}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

A net
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in a quasi-metric space
(X,\rho)
is an almost geodesic for
s
if and only if the net of functions
f_{\alpha}(x):=\rho(x,y_{\alpha})-\rho(s,y_{\alpha})
is an almost non-increasing net. Moreover, the net of functions
(i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
given by (
4.1
) converges to a function in the horofunction compactification
\overline{i_{\rho}(X)}
with basepoint
b
.

Proof.

Suppose
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is an almost geodesic for
s
and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there exists a
\beta
such that

\rho(s,y_{\alpha^{\prime}})\geq\rho(s,y_{\alpha})+\rho(y_{\alpha},y_{\alpha^{\prime}})-\varepsilon

for all
\beta\leq\alpha\leq\alpha^{\prime}
. It follows that for each
x\in X
,

f_{\alpha}(x)+\varepsilon=\rho(x,y_{\alpha})-\rho(s,y_{\alpha})+\varepsilon\geq\rho(x,y_{\alpha})+\rho(y_{\alpha},y_{\alpha^{\prime}})-\rho(s,y_{\alpha^{\prime}})\geq\rho(x,y_{\alpha^{\prime}})-\rho(s,y_{\alpha^{\prime}})=f_{\alpha^{\prime}}(x),

hence the net
(f_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is an almost non-increasing net.

Conversely, suppose that
(f_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is an almost non-increasing net,
\varepsilon>0
, and
\beta
is such that
f_{\alpha}\geq f_{\alpha}^{\prime}-\varepsilon
for all
\beta\leq\alpha\leq\alpha^{\prime}
. Then for
x=y_{\alpha}
we have that

-\rho(s,y_{\alpha})=\rho(y_{\alpha},y_{\alpha})-\rho(s,y_{\alpha})=f_{\alpha}(y_{\alpha})\geq f_{\alpha^{\prime}}(y_{\alpha})-\varepsilon=\rho(y_{\alpha},y_{\alpha^{\prime}})-\rho(s,y_{\alpha^{\prime}})-\varepsilon,

showing that
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is an almost geodesic for
s
.

To show the second assertion, we need to show that if
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is an almost geodesic for
s
, then
i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha})
converges pointwise to a function
h
on
X
. We first note that if
x\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
, then there exists a
\beta
such that
f_{\alpha}(x)\geq f_{\alpha^{\prime}}(x)-\varepsilon
for all
\alpha^{\prime}\geq\alpha\geq\beta
.
Thus,

f_{\alpha}(x)\geq\sup_{\alpha^{\prime}\geq\alpha}f_{\alpha^{\prime}}(x)-\varepsilon
for all
\alpha\geq\beta
, hence

\liminf_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(x)\geq\inf_{\beta\leq\alpha}f_{\alpha}(x)\geq\limsup_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(x)-\varepsilon.

We conclude that
\liminf_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(x)=\limsup_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(x)
, and therefore
f_{\alpha}(x)
converges to
h_{s}(x)
, as
f_{\alpha}(x)\geq-\rho(s,x)>-\infty
for all
\alpha
.

Now using the identity

i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha})(x)=f_{\alpha}(x)-f_{\alpha}(b),

we see that
i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha})(x)
converges to
h(x)=h_{s}(x)-h_{s}(b)
.
∎

A horofunction
h\in\overline{i_{\rho}(X)}
is called a
Busemann point
if there exists an almost geodesic
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
such that
i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
converges to
h
. The set of all Busemann points is denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(X)
.

It is known, see e.g.,
[
22
]
, that on
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(X)
one can define a metric known as the detour metric, which we will exploit. Let us recall the necessary definitions.
Given horofunctions
g
and
h
in
\overline{i_{\rho}(X)}
, let
V_{g}
denote the collection of all neighbourhoods of
g
in
\overline{i_{\rho}(X)}
and define the
detour cost
of
g
and
h
by

\displaystyle\delta(g,h):=\sup_{V\in V_{g}}\left(\inf_{i_{\rho}(x)\in V}\rho(b,x)+h(x)\right).

(4.2)

We note that
\delta(g,h)\in[0,\infty]
. Indeed, if
(w_{\beta})_{\beta}
is a net in
X
with
i_{\rho}(w_{\beta})\to h
, then for each
x\in X
and each
\beta
we have that

\rho(b,x)+i_{\rho}(w_{\beta})(x)=\rho(b,x)+\rho(x,w_{\beta})-\rho(b,w_{\beta})\geq 0,

so
\rho(b,x)+h(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in X
.

There is an alternative definition of
\delta(g,h)
using nets, which is useful in practice. To see this we need the following observation from
[
35
, Lemma 2.6]
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(X,\rho)
be a quasi-metric spaces. If
g
and
h
are horofunctions in
\overline{i_{\rho}(X)}
, then there is a net
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in
X
such that
(i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
converges to
g
and
\delta(g,h)=\lim_{\alpha}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})
.

Proof.

Let
V_{g}
denote the set of neighbourhoods of
g
in
\overline{i_{\rho}(X)}
. Let

A:=\{(V,y)\in V_{g}\times X\colon i_{\rho}(y)\in V\}

be preordered by the relation
(V_{1},y_{1})\leq(V_{2},y_{2})
if
V_{2}\subseteq V_{1}
, and note that this preorder defines a directed set. Now given
\alpha=(V,y)\in A
, we set
y_{\alpha}:=y
, so
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is a net in
X
such that
i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha})\to g
.

Suppose that
W
is an open neighbourhood of
\delta(g,h)
in
[0,\infty]
and
\alpha=(V_{0},z)\in A
. Then there is a neighbourhood
V\in V_{g}
such that

\inf_{i_{\rho}(y)\in V}\rho(b,y)+h(y)\in W.

Note that
V\cap V_{0}\in V_{g}
and

\inf_{i_{\rho}(y)\in V}\rho(b,y)+h(y)\leq\inf_{i_{\rho}(y)\in V\cap V_{0}}\rho(b,y)+h(y)\leq\delta(g,h),

hence

\inf_{i_{\rho}(y)\in V\cap V_{0}}\rho(b,y)+h(y)\in W.

Thus, we may choose
\beta=(V\cap V_{0},w)\in A
such that
\rho(b,w)+h(w)\in W
. As
\beta\geq\alpha
, we conclude that
\delta(g,h)
is a cluster point of the net
(\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
, hence there exists a subnet such that
\delta(g,h)=\lim_{\alpha}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})
.
∎

The following lemma gives an alternative way to define the detour cost.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
g
and
h
be horofunctions and
N_{g}
be the collection of all nets
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in a quasi-metric space
(X,\rho)
such that
(i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
converges to
g
. Then

\delta(g,h)=\inf_{(y_{\alpha})\in N_{g}}(\liminf_{\alpha}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})).

Proof.

Let
V
be an open neighbourhood of
g
and
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be a net in
N_{g}
. There exists a
\beta
such that
i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha})\in V
for all
\alpha\geq\beta
. Hence
\inf_{i_{\rho}(x)\in V}\rho(b,x)+h(x)\leq\inf_{\alpha\geq\beta}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})
and so
\inf_{i_{\rho}(x)\in V}\rho(b,x)+h(x)\leq\liminf_{\alpha}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})
. Since
V
and
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
were arbitrary, it follows that
\delta(g,h)\leq\inf_{N_{g}}(\liminf_{\alpha}\rho(u,y_{\alpha})+g(y_{\alpha}))
.

On the other hand, there is a net
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in
N_{g}
such that
\lim_{\alpha}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})=\delta(g,h)
by Lemma
4.2
, from which we conclude that other inequality
\inf_{N_{g}}(\liminf_{\alpha}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha}))\leq\delta(g,h)
also holds.
∎

To compute the detour cost between Busemann points the following observations are useful, see
[
22
, Lemma 3.1]
or
[
35
, Lemma 2.6]
.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is an almost geodesic in a quasi-metric space
(X,\rho)
such that
(i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
converges to a Busemann point
g
, then

\lim_{\alpha}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+g(y_{\alpha})=0.

Moreover, for any horofunction
h
, we have

\delta(g,h)=\lim_{\alpha}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha}).

Proof.

Suppose that
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is an almost geodesic for
s\in X
and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there is a
\beta
such that

0\leq\rho(s,y_{\alpha})+\rho(y_{\alpha},y_{\alpha^{\prime}})-\rho(s,y_{\alpha^{\prime}})\leq\varepsilon

for all
\beta\leq\alpha\leq\alpha^{\prime}
. So,

0\leq\rho(s,y_{\alpha})+\rho(y_{\alpha},y_{\alpha^{\prime}})-\rho(b,y_{\alpha^{\prime}})-(\rho(s,y_{\alpha^{\prime}})-\rho(b,y_{\alpha^{\prime}}))\leq\varepsilon

for all
\beta\leq\alpha\leq\alpha^{\prime}
.
Now fixing
\alpha^{\prime}\geq\alpha
and taking the limit over the subnet indexed by
\gamma\geq\alpha^{\prime}
, we find that
0\leq\rho(s,y_{\alpha})+g(y_{\alpha})-g(s)\leq\varepsilon
for all
\alpha\geq\beta
. Thus,
\lim_{\alpha}\rho(s,y_{\alpha})+g(y_{\alpha})=g(s)
.
The first statement now follows from

\lim_{\alpha}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+g(y_{\alpha})=\lim_{\alpha}(\rho(b,y_{\alpha})-\rho(s,y_{\alpha}))+\lim_{\alpha}(\rho(s,y_{\alpha})+g(y_{\alpha}))=-g(s)+g(s)=0.

For the second part of the statement, we first note that for each
x,z\in X
we have that

h(x)\leq(\rho(x,z)-\rho(b,z))+(\rho(b,z)+h(z)).

(4.3)

Indeed, if
(w_{\beta})_{\beta}
is a net in
X
such that
i_{\rho}(w_{\beta})\to h
, then for each
\beta
,

\rho(x,w_{\beta})-\rho(b,w_{\beta})\leq\rho(x,z)+\rho(z,w_{\beta})-\rho(b,w_{\beta})=(\rho(x,z)-\rho(b,z))+(\rho(b,z)+\rho(z,w_{\beta})-\rho(b,w_{\beta})),

hence (
4.3
) holds.

It follows that for each
x\in X
we have that

h(x)\leq\inf_{(z_{\gamma})\in N_{g}}\left(\liminf_{\gamma}(\rho(x,z_{\gamma})-\rho(b,z_{\gamma}))+(\rho(b,z_{\gamma})+h(z_{\gamma}))\right)=g(x)+\delta(g,h),

(4.4)

where
N_{g}
is the set of all nets
(z_{\gamma})_{\gamma}
such that
i_{\rho}(z_{\gamma})\to g
by Lemma
4.3
.

This implies that

\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})\leq\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+g(y_{\alpha})+\delta(g,h)

for all
\alpha
. Now using the first part of the lemma we find that

\limsup_{\alpha}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})\leq\delta(g,h).

On the other hand,

\delta(g,h)\leq\liminf_{\alpha}\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha}),

which completes the proof.
∎

Note that the identities in Lemma
4.4
do not depend on the almost geodesic converging to the Busemann point.

On
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(X)
the
detour metric
,
\Delta\colon\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(X)\times\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(X)\to[0,\infty]
, is defined by

\Delta(g,h):=\delta(g,h)+\delta(h,g).

It is known
[
22
, Proposition 3.1]
that
\Delta
satisfies all the usual properties of a metric where the distance can attain the value
\infty
.

Theorem 4.5
.

The function
\Delta\colon\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(X)\times\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(X)\to[0,\infty]
is a (possibly
\infty
-valued) metric.

Proof.

The fact that
\Delta
is symmetric is clear from the definition, and
\Delta(g,h)\geq 0
, as
\delta(g,h)\geq 0
.
Suppose
g
and
h
are Busemann points with
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
and
(z_{\beta})_{\beta}
almost geodesics such that
i_{\rho}(y_{\alpha})\to g
and
i_{\rho}(z_{\beta})\to h
. It follows from Lemma
4.4
that
\Delta(g,g)=0
. Now suppose
\Delta(g,h)=0
. Let
x\in X
, and note that the triangle inequality together with the inequality
\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})\geq 0
yield

\displaystyle\rho(x,y_{\alpha})-\rho(b,y_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\leq\rho(x,z_{\beta})+\rho(z_{\beta},y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\rho(x,z_{\beta})+\left(\rho(z_{\beta},y_{\alpha})-\rho(b,y_{\alpha})\right)+\left(\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})\right).

By taking the limit for
\alpha
, it follows from Lemma
4.4
that
g(x)\leq\rho(x,z_{\beta})+g(z_{\beta})+\delta(g,h)
. Hence

g(x)\leq\left(\rho(x,z_{\beta})-\rho(b,z_{\beta})\right)+\left(\rho(b,z_{\beta})+g(z_{\beta})\right)+\delta(g,h).

Subsequently taking the limit for
\beta
we deduce that

g(x)\leq h(x)+\delta(h,g)+\delta(g,h)=h(x)+\Delta(h,g)=h(x).

Interchanging the roles of
g
and
h
now shows that
g=h
.

To show the triangle inequality, let
f
be a Busemann point with almost geodesic
(x_{\gamma})_{\gamma}
, so
i_{\rho}(x_{\gamma})\to f
. From the triangle inequality for
\rho
, we have

\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+\rho(y_{\alpha},z_{\beta})-\rho(b,z_{\beta})\leq\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+\rho(y_{\alpha},x_{\gamma})-\rho(b,x_{\gamma})+\rho(b,x_{\gamma})+\rho(x_{\gamma},z_{\beta})-\rho(b,z_{\beta}).

Taking the limit for
\beta
first yields

\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})\leq\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+(\rho(y_{\alpha},x_{\gamma})-\rho(b,x_{\gamma}))+(\rho(b,x_{\gamma})+h(x_{\gamma})).

Subsequently taking the limit for
\gamma
gives

\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})\leq\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+f(y_{\alpha})+\delta(f,h)

by Lemma
4.4
. Finally, taking the limit for
\alpha
gives
\delta(g,h)\leq\delta(g,f)+\delta(f,h)
. By swapping the roles of
g
and
h
, we see that
\delta(h,g)\leq\delta(h,f)+\delta(f,g)
, so

\Delta(g,h)\leq\Delta(g,f)+\Delta(f,h)
.
∎

The relation
g\sim h
if
\Delta(g,h)<\infty
is an equivalence relation on
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(X)
and its equivalence classes are called
parts
. So, the set of Busemann points is partitioned into parts, each of which is a metric space.

Remark 4.6
.

It should be noted that on the horofunction boundary

\mathcal{H}_{\rho}(X)
there is a natural equivalence relation where horofunctions
g
and
h
are equivalent if

\sup_{x\in X}|h(x)-g(x)|<\infty.

On
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(X)
this equivalence relation coincides with the
\sim
relation. Indeed, it follows from (
4.4
) that

\sup_{x\in X}h(x)-g(x)\leq\delta(g,h).

On the other hand, if
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is almost geodesic for
g
, then by Lemma
4.4
,

h(y_{\alpha})-g(y_{\alpha})=\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})-(\rho(b,y_{\alpha})+g(y_{\alpha}))\to\delta(g,h),

hence
\sup_{x\in X}h(x)-g(x)\geq\delta(g,h)
. This implies that
\sup_{x\in X}h(x)-g(x)=\delta(g,h)
, which shows that
\sup_{x\in X}|h(x)-g(x)|<\infty
for
g,h\in\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(X)
if and only if
g
and
h
are in the same part.

In the sequel the parts of
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}(X)
consisting of a single Busemann point will play a key role. We will refer to these parts as the
singleton parts
.

Remark 4.7
.

We note that the detour metric is independent of the basepoint
b\in X
. Indeed, if we change the basepoint from
b
to say
s
in
X
, then the horofunctions with respect to the basepoint
s
are of the form
h_{s}(x)=h(x)-h(s)
, where
h
is a horofunction with respect to basepoint
b
. So, the detour cost
\delta(g_{s},h_{s})
between two Busemann points
g_{s}
and
h_{s}
with respect to
s
satisfies

\delta(g_{s},h_{s})=\inf_{(y_{\alpha})}\left(\liminf_{\alpha}\rho(s,y_{\alpha})+h_{s}(y_{\alpha})\right)=\delta(g,h)+g(s)-h(s).

Here the infimum is taken over all nets
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
such that the function
x\in X\mapsto\rho(x,y_{\alpha})-\rho(s,y_{\alpha})
converges to
g_{s}
. This shows that
\Delta
is independent of the basepoint.

It turns out that any surjective isometry between quasi-metric spaces induces a detour metric isometry between the Busemann points. Indeed, if
(X_{1},\rho_{1})
and
(X_{2},\rho_{2})
are quasi-metric spaces and

\varphi\colon X_{1}\to X_{2}
is a surjective isometry, then we can consider the map
\Phi\colon\mathcal{H}_{\rho_{1}}(X_{1})\to\mathcal{H}_{\rho_{2}}(X_{2})
defined by

\Phi(h)(x):=h(\varphi^{-1}(x))-h(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2})),

(4.5)

for
x\in X_{1}
, where
b_{2}
is the basepoint in
X_{2}
.
We note that
\Phi(h)
is in
\mathcal{H}_{\rho_{2}}(X_{2})
. Indeed, if
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is a net in
X_{1}
such that
(i_{\rho_{1}}(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
converges to
h
, then for each
x\in X_{2}
we have that

\displaystyle i_{\rho_{2}}(\varphi(y_{\alpha}))(x)

\displaystyle=\rho_{2}(x,\varphi(y_{\alpha}))-\rho_{2}(b_{2},\varphi(y_{\alpha}))=\rho_{1}(\varphi^{-1}(x),y_{\alpha})-\rho_{1}(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2}),y_{\alpha})

\displaystyle\qquad=(\rho_{1}(\varphi^{-1}(x),y_{\alpha})-\rho_{1}(b_{1},y_{\alpha}))-(\rho_{1}(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2}),y_{\alpha})-\rho_{1}(b_{1},y_{\alpha}))

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\to h(\varphi^{-1}(x))-h(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2}))=\Phi(h)(x).

It is straightforward to check that
\Phi
has an inverse given by
\Phi^{-1}(g)(x)=g(\varphi(x))-g(\varphi(b_{1}))
, hence
\Phi
is a bijection. Furthermore, if
(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is a net that converges (pointwise) to
h
in
\mathcal{H}_{\rho_{1}}(X_{1})
, then for any
x\in X_{2}
,

\Phi(h_{\alpha})(x)=h_{\alpha}(\varphi^{-1}(x))-h_{\alpha}(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2}))\to h(\varphi^{-1}(x))-h(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2}))=\Phi(h)(x),

so
\Phi
is continuous. Similarly, we find that
\Phi^{-1}
is continuous, so
\Phi
is a homeomorphism.

Clearly, a net
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is an almost geodesic at
s_{1}\in X_{1}
if and only if
(\varphi(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
is an almost geodesic at
\varphi(s_{1})\in X_{2}
, thus
\Phi
also induces a bijection from
\mathcal{B}_{\rho_{1}}(X_{1})
onto
\mathcal{B}_{\rho_{2}}(X_{2})
. The next theorem shows that
\Phi
is actually a detour metric isometry, see
[
22
, Lemma 3.2]
.

Theorem 4.8
.

If
\varphi\colon(X_{1},\rho_{1})\to(X_{2},\rho_{2})
is a surjective isometry, then for all
g,h\in\mathcal{B}_{\rho_{1}}(X_{1})
we have
\Delta(\Phi(g),\Phi(h))=\Delta(g,h)
.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
there exists a net
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in
X_{1}
such that
(i_{\rho_{1}}(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
converges to
g
and

\lim_{\alpha}\rho_{1}(b_{1},y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha})=\delta(g,h).

It follows that

\rho_{2}(b_{2},\varphi(y_{\alpha}))+\Phi(h)(\varphi(y_{\alpha}))=(\rho_{1}(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2}),y_{\alpha})-\rho_{1}(b_{1},y_{\alpha}))+(\rho_{1}(b_{1},y_{\alpha})+h(y_{\alpha}))-h(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2})).

Taking the limit over
\alpha
yields
\lim_{\alpha}\rho_{2}(b_{2},\varphi(y_{\alpha}))+\Phi(h)(\varphi(y_{\alpha}))=g(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2}))-h(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2}))+\delta(g,h)
.
Now from Lemma
4.3
it follows that
\delta(\Phi(g),\Phi(h))\leq g(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2}))-h(\varphi^{-1}(b_{2}))+\delta(g,h)
, hence

\Delta(\Phi(g),\Phi(h))\leq\Delta(g,h).

Applying the same argument to
\Phi^{-1}
gives
\Delta(\Phi(g),\Phi(h))=\Delta(g,h)
.
∎

We see that
\Phi
maps parts of
\mathcal{B}_{\rho_{1}}(X_{1})
onto parts of
\mathcal{B}_{\rho_{2}}(X_{2})
. In particular singleton parts are mapped to singleton parts. This fact will be exploited in the proof of the main result in this paper.

5
Funk and reverse-Funk Busemann points of the cone

The singletons for the Funk and reverse-Funk horofunction boundary of the cone in an order unit space were analysed by Walsh in
[
35
]
. We recall, and slightly refine, the results in that paper here. In particular, we shall characterise the singletons in the reverse-Funk horofunction boundary in case
(V,C,u)
is reflexive. We will need the representation of an order unit space and its bi-dual in terms of affine functions on the state space, which is discussed in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
. In what follows, we will choose the base point for the reverse-Funk and the Funk geometry to be the order unit
u
.

The following theorem is attributed to Kadison, see
[
2
, Theorem 1.20]
,

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space with state space
S
. For
x\in V
let
\hat{x}
be the function on
S
given by
\hat{x}(\varphi):=\varphi(x)
. The map
x\mapsto\hat{x}
is an order- and norm-preserving isomorphism from
V
onto
A_{w*}(S)
, the space of
w^{*}
-continuous affine functions on
S
.

The bi-dual of a complete order unit space has the following characterisation, see
[
2
, Proposition 1.11]
.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space with state space
S
. The restriction of the functionals in
V^{**}
to
S
is an order- and norm-preserving isomorphism from
V^{**}
onto
A_{b}(S)
, the space of all bounded affine functions on
S
, equipped with the pointwise ordering and the supremum norm.

In
Theorems

5.1
and
5.2
the ordering on the spaces of affine functions is pointwise, so that
C
corresponds to the cone
A_{w*}(S)^{+}
of nonnegative functions in
A_{w*}(S)
, and similarly,
C^{**}
corresponds to
A_{b}(S)^{+}
. We will also need an intermediate cone between
C
and
C^{**}
, which we will discuss next.

Affine semi-continuous functions

We denote by
A_{usc}(S)
the bounded affine upper semi-continuous functions on
S
, and the cone
C_{sc}
denotes
(A_{usc}(S)^{+}-A_{usc}(S)^{+})^{+}
, i.e., the cone of nonnegative bounded affine functions that can be written as a difference of two nonnegative affine upper semi-continuous functions. Note that
C\subseteq C_{sc}\subseteq C^{**}
, and also note that if
V
is reflexive, the cones are all equal. The equality
C_{sc}=C
plays an important role in the sequel. There is, however, an order theoretical characterisation for order unit spaces where
C=C_{sc}
, which we will state below. For this characterisation we need the following two notions. An order unit space
V
is said to be
monotone complete
if every monotone decreasing net
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
that is bounded from below has an infimum in
C
. A state
\varphi
is called
normal
if for every monotone decreasing net
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
with infimum
x
we have
\varphi(x_{\alpha})\to\varphi(x)
. We will also need the following results, which can be found in
[
1
]
, cf.
[
1
, Proposition 1.1.2]
and the proof of
[
1
, Corollary 1.1.4]
. Here, we write
f<g
for two real functions
f
and
g
if
f(x)<g(x)
for all
x
in the domain.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space with state space
S
. If
f\colon S\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a
w^{*}
-lower semi-continuous affine function on the state space, then for each
\varphi\in S
we have

f(\varphi)=\sup\bigl\{g(\varphi)\colon f>g|_{S},\ g\colon V^{*}\to\mathbb{R}\ \mbox{affine and $w^{*}$-continuous}\bigr\}.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space with state space
S
. If
f\colon S\to(-\infty,\infty]
is a
w^{*}
-lower semi-continuous affine function on the state space, then the set of affine
w^{*}
-continuous functions
g\colon V^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
such that
g|_{S}<f
is directed upwards, hence is a net indexed by itself converging pointwise to
f
when restricted to
S
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space. Then
C=C_{sc}
if and only if
V
is monotone complete and all states are normal.

Proof.

We start by identifying
V
with
A_{w^{*}}(S)
where
S
is the state space of
V
under the isometric order isomorphism
x\mapsto\hat{x}
, where
\hat{x}(\varphi):=\varphi(x)
. Suppose that
C=C_{sc}
. Let
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be a nonnegative decreasing net in
V
. Then
f(\varphi):=\inf_{\alpha}\hat{x}_{\alpha}(\varphi)
defines an upper semi-continuous affine function on
S
and by assumption
f\in C
. Furthermore, if
y\in V
is such that
y\leq x_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha
, then
y\leq f
, so
f
is the infimum of
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
. This shows that
V
is monotone complete. Also note that for any state
\varphi
we have
\varphi(x_{\alpha})\to\varphi(f)
, so
\varphi
is normal.

Conversely, suppose
V
is monotone complete and that every state is normal. Let
f
be a nonnegative upper semi-continuous affine function on
S
. Then there exists a decreasing net of
w
*-continuous affine functions
(\hat{x}_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
such that
f(\varphi)=\inf_{\alpha}\hat{x}_{\alpha}(\varphi)
for all states
\varphi
by
Corollary

5.4
. Since
V
is monotone complete, this net has an infimum
x_{0}
in
V
, and since all states are assumed to be normal,
x_{0}
and
f
are equal as they are the same pointwise limit. Hence
C=C_{sc}
.
∎

Remark 5.6
.

For a JBW-algebra
M
the unit ball is
\sigma
-weakly compact by
[
3
, Corollary 2.56]
, so the cone
M_{+}
equals
(M_{+})_{sc}
if and only if all states are normal, which is equivalent to the unit ball being weakly compact. By Kakutani’s theorem
[
11
, Theorem V.4.2]
this is equivalent to
M
being reflexive. Since the reflexive JBW-algebras are precisely the JH-algebras by
Theorem

2.9
, a JBW-algebra
M
is a JH-algebra if and only if
M_{+}=(M_{+})_{sc}
.

Below, we give two examples of non-reflexive order unit spaces where both equality and strict inclusion occur between
C
and
C_{sc}
. In particular, the fact that
C=C_{sc}
does not imply that
V
is reflexive.

Example 5.7
.

Let
X
be a Banach space and equip
V:=\mathbb{R}\oplus X
with the cone generated by
B_{X}
, the unit ball of
X
, at level
1
, i.e.,
C:=\{\lambda(1,x)\colon\lambda\geq 0,\ x\in B_{X}\}
. We choose
(1,0)\in V
as the order unit. Then
V^{*}=\mathbb{R}\oplus X^{*}
,
S=\{(1,x^{*})\colon x^{*}\in B_{X^{*}}\}
, and
V^{**}=\mathbb{R}\oplus X^{**}
. Let
v^{**}:=\lambda(1,x^{**})\in V^{**}
and denote the corresponding element of
A_{b}(S)
from
Theorem

5.2
by
\hat{v}^{**}
. Let
\varphi=(1,x^{*})\in S
. Then
\hat{v}^{**}(\varphi)=\lambda+x^{**}(x^{*})
, so when determining whether
\hat{v}^{**}
is upper semi-continuous, we may assume that
\lambda=0
and only consider the upper semi-continuity of
x^{**}
viewed as a function on
(B_{X^{*}},w^{*})
.

Suppose
x^{**}
is upper semi-continuous. Let
(x^{*}_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be a net in
B_{X^{*}}
converging to
x^{*}\in B_{X^{*}}
. Then
-x^{*}_{\alpha}\to-x^{*}
and so
\limsup_{\alpha}x^{**}(-x^{*}_{\alpha})\leq x^{**}(-x^{*})
, hence
-\limsup_{\alpha}x^{**}(-x^{*}_{\alpha})\geq-x^{**}(-x^{*})=x^{**}(x^{*})
. It follows that

\liminf_{\alpha}x^{**}(x^{*}_{\alpha})=\liminf_{\alpha}-x^{**}(-x^{*}_{\alpha})=-\limsup_{\alpha}x^{**}(-x^{*}_{\alpha})\geq x^{**}(x^{*}),

showing that
x^{**}
is also lower semi-continuous, thus
x^{**}
is continuous, so
x^{**}\in X
and therefore
v^{**}\in V
. Hence
A_{usc}(S)=A_{w^{*}}(S)
and we conclude that
C_{sc}=C
.

In the next class of examples
C_{sc}
is almost always different from
C
.

Example 5.8
.

Let
X
be a compact Hausdorff space and let
V:=C(X)
with the pointwise ordering. We denote by
C_{usc}(X)
the bounded upper semi-continuous functions on
X
. The pure states of
V
equipped with the
w
*-topology are homeomorphic to
X
, and under this identification, restricting
f\in A_{usc}(S)
to

yields a map from
A_{usc}(S)
to
C_{usc}(X)
. Conversely, if

,
[

shows that there exists a unique

such that

. Hence

can be identified with
C_{usc}(X)
.

If

is finite, then

, and so

. If, however,

is not finite, then there exists an

is closed but not open. So,
\mathbf{1}_{\{x\}}\in C_{usc}(X)\setminus C(X)
and

in that case.

Reverse-Funk Busemann point

First, observe that for any
z\in C^{\circ}
we have

i_{RF}(z)(x)=\log M(z/x)-\log M(z/u)\qquad(x\in C^{\circ}),

from which it follows that we may chose
z
such that
\|z\|_{u}=1
. By
Theorem

5.1
, we can view
z
as a strictly positive
g\in A_{w*}(S)^{+}
with supremum
1
, so that

i_{RF}(z)(x)=\log M(z/x)=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(z)}{\varphi(x)}=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g(\varphi)}{\varphi(x)}\qquad(x\in C^{\circ}).

We recall the description of the reverse-Funk Busemann points (see
[
35
, Theorem 6.3]
).

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space with state space
S
. The Busemann points of the reverse-Funk metric on
C^{\circ}
are precisely the functions of the form

h(x):=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g(\varphi)}{\varphi(x)}\qquad(x\in C^{\circ}),

(5.1)

where
g
is a
w^{*}
-upper semi-continuous nonnegative affine function on
S
with supremum 1, that does not correspond to an element of
C^{\circ}
.

The proof of this result relies on the following facts together with
Proposition

5.3
and
Corollary

5.4
.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space and let
g
and
g^{\prime}
be
w^{*}
-upper semi-continuous nonnegative affine functions on
S
taking values in
[0,\infty)
. If

\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g(\varphi)}{\varphi(x)}\leq\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}{\varphi(x)}\mbox{\hskip 20.44434ptfor all $x\in C^{\circ}$,}

then
g\leq g^{\prime}
.

Proof.

We know from Lemma
10.3
that
g^{\prime}
attains its maximum on
S
. From Corollary
5.4
we know there exists a decreasing net
(g^{\prime}_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
in
A_{w*}(S)
with
g^{\prime}_{\alpha}>g^{\prime}\geq 0
for all
\alpha
and
(g^{\prime}_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
converges pointwise to
g^{\prime}
. By Theorem
5.1
we know that
g^{\prime}_{\alpha}
corresponds to an
x^{\prime}_{\alpha}\in C^{\circ}
. So for each
\alpha
and each
\psi\in S
we have that

\frac{g(\psi)}{g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\psi)}=\frac{g(\psi)}{\psi(x^{\prime}_{\alpha})}\leq\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g(\varphi)}{\varphi(x^{\prime}_{\alpha})}\leq\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}{\varphi(x^{\prime}_{\alpha})}=\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}{g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\varphi)}\leq 1,

hence
g(\psi)\leq g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\psi)
for all
\psi
and all
\alpha
. Thus,
g(\psi)\leq\lim_{\alpha}g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\psi)=g^{\prime}(\psi)
for all
\psi\in S
.
∎

The proof of Theorem
5.9
also uses some facts about upper semi-continuous functions, which are collected in the Appendix.

Proof of Theorem
5.9
.

Suppose that
(y_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is an almost geodesic in
C^{\circ}
for the reverse-Funk geometry such that
(i_{RF}(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
converges to a Busemann point
h
. We may scale each
y_{\alpha}
such that
M(y_{\alpha}/u)=1
, as
i_{RF}(\lambda x)=i_{RF}(x)
for all
\lambda>0
. Now let
g_{\alpha}:=J(y_{\alpha})|_{S}
for all
\alpha
, where
J(y_{\alpha})\in V^{**}
is given by
\varphi\in V^{*}\mapsto\varphi(y_{\alpha})
. Recall that by Lemma
4.1

(i_{RF}(y_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
is an almost non-increasing net of functions.

It follows that for any
\varepsilon>0
there is an index
\alpha_{0}
such that

e^{\varepsilon}\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g_{\alpha}(\varphi)}{\varphi(x)}\geq\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g_{\beta}(\varphi)}{\varphi(x)}\qquad(\mbox{for all }x\in C^{\circ})

for all
\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha\leq\beta
. By evaluating in
y_{\alpha}
, it follows that
e^{\varepsilon}g_{\alpha}(\varphi)\geq g_{\beta}(\varphi)
for all
\varphi\in S
for all
\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha\leq\beta
and we conclude that
(\log g_{\alpha}|_{S})_{\alpha}
is an almost non-increasing net. By Lemma
10.5
(i) the net
(\log g_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
converges on
S
to a pointwise limit, and therefore
(g_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
also converges pointwise on
S
to a
w^{*}
-upper semi-continuous limit, say
g
, by Lemma
10.5
(ii).

Note that
g
is affine and nonnegative on
S
, since
g_{\alpha}
is positive and affine for all
\alpha
. Using that
(\log g_{\alpha}|_{S})_{\alpha}
is almost non-increasing again, for
\varepsilon>0
there is an index
\alpha^{\prime}
such that
\log g_{\beta}(\varphi)\leq\log g_{\alpha}(\varphi)+\varepsilon
for all
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha\leq\beta
and all
\varphi\in S
. Note that as
M(y_{\alpha}/u)=1
, there exists a
\varphi_{\alpha}\in S
such that
g_{\alpha}(\varphi_{\alpha})=\varphi_{\alpha}(y_{\alpha})=1
. It follows that
0\leq\log g_{\alpha}(\varphi_{\beta})+\varepsilon
for all
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha\leq\beta
. By possibly passing to a convergent subnet, we may assume that
(\varphi_{\beta})_{\beta}
converges
w^{*}
to a
\psi\in S
, hence

for all
\alpha^{\prime}\leq\alpha
. Taking the limit for
\alpha
yields
0\leq\log g(\psi)+\varepsilon\leq\sup_{\varphi\in S}\log g(\varphi)+\varepsilon
. As
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we conclude that
\sup_{\varphi\in S}g\geq 1
. But
g
is the pointwise limit of functions with supremum 1 on
S
, so
\sup_{\varphi\in S}g(\varphi)=1
.

By Lemma
10.5
we find that
\sup_{\varphi\in S}g_{\alpha}(\varphi)/\varphi(x)
converges to
\sup_{\varphi\in S}g(\varphi)/\varphi(x)
, which means that
i_{RF}(y_{\alpha})(x)=RF(x,y_{\alpha})-RF(u,y_{\alpha})
converges to
\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}g(\varphi)/\varphi(x)
. Thus,

h(x)=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g(\varphi)}{\varphi(x)}\qquad(x\in C^{\circ}).

As
h
is a Busemann point, it must be a horofunction, hence
g
does not correspond to a point in
C^{\circ}
.

To complete the proof we need to show that each function of the form (
5.1
) can be realised as a reverse-Funk Busemann point. For
g
as in (
5.1
) we know by Corollary
5.4
that there is a decreasing net
(g_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
of affine
w^{*}
-continuous functions
g_{\alpha}\colon V^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
, with

, such that
(g_{\alpha}|_{S})_{\alpha}
converges pointwise to
g
. By Theorem

we know that
g_{\alpha}|_{S}=J(y_{\alpha})|_{S}
for some
y_{\alpha}\in C^{\circ}
for each
\alpha
, as
g_{\alpha}(\varphi)>g(\varphi)\geq 0
for all
\varphi\in S
.

Let

. Note that the functions

are

-continuous, decreasing in

, and converge pointwise to the function

on

. By Lemma

we have that
\sup_{\varphi\in S}G_{\alpha}(\varphi)
converges to
\sup_{\varphi\in S}G(\varphi)
. Hence
\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}G_{\alpha}(\varphi_{\alpha})
converges to

.
Moreover,
\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}g_{\alpha}(\varphi)
converges to

has supremum 1 on

.

We find that

\log M(y_{\alpha}/x)-\log M(y_{\alpha}/u)=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(y_{\alpha})}{\varphi(x)}-\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\varphi(y_{\alpha})=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g_{\alpha}(\varphi)}{\varphi(x)}-\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}g_{\alpha}(\varphi),

hence

converges pointwise to

(\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}G_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is decreasing and

is a Busemann point provided that

. To that end, suppose

. Then

\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}{\varphi(x)}=h(x)=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g(\varphi)}{\varphi(x)}\qquad(x\in C^{\circ})

for some

. Applying

does not correspond to an element of

.
∎

Combining Theorem
5.9
with Remark

gives the following corollary, cf.

.

Corollary 5.11
.

If
h
and

are reverse-Funk Busemann points with

-upper semi-continuous nonnegative affine functions

and
g^{\prime}
, respectively, then
h
and
h^{\prime}
are in the same part if and only if there exists
\lambda>0
such that
g^{\prime}/\lambda\leq g\leq\lambda g^{\prime}
.

Reverse-Funk singletons

The following proposition gives a sufficient condition for a reverse-Funk metric Busemann point to be a singleton.

Proposition 5.12
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space. If
h
in (
5.1
) is a Busemann point of the reverse-Funk metric on
C^{\circ}
and
g
is on an extreme ray of
C_{sc}
, then
h
is a singleton Busemann point.

Proof.

Take a reverse-Funk Busemann point
h^{\prime}
in the same part as
h
. Then by
Theorem

5.9
it is induced by some function
g^{\prime}\in C_{sc}
. By Corollary
5.11
there exists some
\lambda>0
with
g^{\prime}/\lambda\leq g\leq\lambda g^{\prime}
. Since
g
is on an extreme ray, there is some
\mu\geq 0
with
g^{\prime}/\lambda=\mu g
and so
g^{\prime}=\lambda\mu g
. Since
g
and
g^{\prime}
both have supremum 1 on
S
, we have that
\lambda\mu=1
, hence
g^{\prime}=g
. We conclude that
h^{\prime}=h
.
∎

Proposition 5.13
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space with
C=C_{sc}
. If
h
in (
5.1
) is a singleton Busemann point of the reverse-Funk metric on
C^{\circ}
, then
g
lies on an extreme ray of
C
.

Proof.

As
C=C_{sc}
,
g
is a
w^{*}
-continuous function on
S
. Now suppose that
g=g_{1}+g_{2}
for some
g_{1},g_{2}\in C
. Let
\mu=\sup_{\varphi\in S}(g_{1}+2g_{2})>0
and consider
g^{\prime}=\mu^{-1}(g_{1}+2g_{2})
. We note that
g^{\prime}
does not correspond to an element of
C^{\circ}
. Indeed, if
g^{\prime}
is positive on
S
, then
g
is also positive on
S
, since
g^{\prime}\leq 2\mu^{-1}g
. This would imply that
g
corresponds to an element of
C^{\circ}
, which is not possible, as
h
is a Busemann point. Thus, by Theorem

the function

. Since

Funk Busemann points

First, observe that for any

Note that a lower semi-continuous function can be infinite-valued, and we will use the convention that

Lemma 5.16
.

Let

be a complete order unit space and let

-lower semi-continuous nonnegative affine functions on

then
g^{\prime}\leq g
.

Proof.

We know from Lemma
10.3
that
g^{\prime}
attains its minimum
\varepsilon
on
S
. From Corollary
5.4
there exists an increasing net
(g^{\prime}_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
of functions in

with
\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon\leq g^{\prime}_{\alpha}<g^{\prime}
for all

and
(g^{\prime}_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
converges pointwise to

. By Theorem
5.1
we know that

corresponds to an

. So for each
\alpha

\frac{g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\psi)}{g(\psi)}=\frac{\psi(x^{\prime}_{\alpha})}{g(\psi)}\leq\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x^{\prime}_{\alpha})}{g(\varphi)}\leq\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x^{\prime}_{\alpha})}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}=\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\varphi)}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}\leq 1,

Theorem 5.17

(g_{\alpha})_{\alpha}

\displaystyle i_{F}(x_{\alpha})(x)

\displaystyle=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{\varphi(x_{\alpha})}-\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\varphi(x_{\alpha})^{-1}=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g_{\alpha}(\varphi)}-\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}g_{\alpha}(\varphi)^{-1},

4.1

\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}=h(x)=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g(\varphi)}\qquad(x\in C^{\circ})

for some strictly positive

5.16

, which contradicts the fact that

does not correspond to an element of
C^{\circ}
.

Conversely, suppose that
(x_{\alpha})_{\alpha}

for the Funk metric such that
(i_{F}(x_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}

e^{\varepsilon}\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g_{\alpha}(\varphi)}\geq\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g_{\beta}(\varphi)}\qquad(\mbox{for all }x\in C^{\circ})

for

e^{\varepsilon}g_{\beta}(\varphi)\geq g_{\alpha}(\varphi)

(5.2)

for all

We conclude that

Using that

Singleton Funk Busemann points

The singleton Funk Busemann points correspond to pure states of
S
. Indeed, Walsh proved the following results
[
35
, Corollary 7.4]
.

Proposition 5.18
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space with state space
S
. If
h
is a Busemann point for the Funk metric on
C^{\circ}
, then
h
is a singleton if and only if it can be written as
h(x)=\log\psi(x)
for some pure state
\psi\in S
.

Proof.

Let
\psi\in S
be a pure state, and suppose that
h(x)=\log\psi(x)
for all
x\in C^{\circ}
. Then the function
g
on
S
defined by

g(\varphi):=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $\varphi=\psi$};\\
\infty&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

is affine and
w^{*}
-lower semi-continuous with infimum 1 that does not correspond to an element of
C^{\circ}
. Furthermore, we have that

\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g(\varphi)}=\log\psi(x)=h(x)\qquad(x\in C^{\circ})

by construction of
g
, hence
h
is a Busemann point by Theorem
5.17
.

Suppose
h^{\prime}
is a Busemann point that lies in the same part as
h
. By Theorem
5.17
there is a
w^{*}
-lower semi-continuous positive affine function
g^{\prime}
on
S
which has infimum 1 such that

h^{\prime}(x)=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}\qquad(x\in C^{\circ}).

Using Remark
4.6
we have
\sup_{x\in C^{\circ}}|h(x)-h^{\prime}(x)|=\Delta(h^{\prime},h)
, so that

\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}-\Delta(h,h^{\prime})\leq\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g(\varphi)}\leq\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}+\Delta(h,h^{\prime}),

hence

e^{-\Delta(h,h^{\prime})}\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}\leq\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g(\varphi)}\leq e^{\Delta(h,h^{\prime})}\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}

for all
x\in C^{\circ}
. By Corollary
5.4
there exists an increasing net
(g^{\prime}_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
of positive affine
w^{*}
-continuous functions
g^{\prime}_{\alpha}\colon V^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
, with
g^{\prime}_{\alpha}|_{S}<g^{\prime}
for all
\alpha
, such that
(g^{\prime}_{\alpha}|_{S})_{\alpha}
converges pointwise to
g^{\prime}
. So, for
\alpha
we have that
g^{\prime}_{\alpha}|_{S}=J(x_{\alpha})|_{S}
for some
x_{\alpha}\in C^{\circ}
by Theorem
5.1
. We find that

\frac{g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\varphi)}{g(\varphi)}\leq\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\varphi)}{g(\varphi)}\leq e^{\Delta(h,h^{\prime})}\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\varphi)}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}\leq e^{\Delta(h,h^{\prime})},

hence
g^{\prime}(\varphi)\leq e^{\Delta(h,h^{\prime})}g(\varphi)
for all
\varphi\in S
. Using the other inequality, a similar argument shows that
g(\varphi)\leq e^{\Delta(h,h^{\prime})}g^{\prime}(\varphi)
for all
\varphi\in S
. It follows that
g^{\prime}(\varphi)=\infty
for all
\varphi\neq\psi
and
g^{\prime}(\psi)
is finite. As
g^{\prime}
must have infimum 1, we conclude that
g=g^{\prime}
, so
h=h^{\prime}
and
h
is a singleton Busemann point.

Conversely, suppose that
h
is a singleton Busemann point, hence
h
is of the form

h(x)=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g(\varphi)}\qquad(x\in C^{\circ})

for some
w^{*}
-lower semi-continuous positive affine function
g
on
S
which has infimum 1 that does not correspond to an element of
C^{\circ}
by Theorem
5.17
. By Lemma
10.3

g
attains its infimum at say
\psi
, and suppose that
g
has a finite value at another state
\psi^{\prime}
. There is an element
x\in C^{\circ}
of norm 1 such that
\psi(x)\neq\psi^{\prime}(x)
. Define
\lambda:=\inf_{S}(g(\varphi)+\varphi(x))
, and
g^{\prime}:=\lambda^{-1}(g+J(x)|_{S})
. Note that
g^{\prime}
is a
w^{*}
-lower semi-continuous positive affine function on
S
with infimum 1 that does not correspond to an element of
C^{\circ}
. Thus, by Theorem
5.17
, the function

h^{\prime}(x):=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}\qquad(x\in C^{\circ})

is a Busemann point. Note that

\lambda^{-1}g(\varphi)\leq g^{\prime}(\varphi)\leq\lambda^{-1}g(\varphi)+\lambda^{-1}\leq 2\lambda^{-1}g(\varphi)

for all
\varphi\in S
. Thus
h(x)+\log\frac{1}{2}\lambda\leq h^{\prime}(x)\leq h(x)+\log\lambda
for all
x\in C^{\circ}
, hence
h
and
h^{\prime}
must lie in the same part by Remark
4.6
. Thus, we must have that
h=h^{\prime}
, so

\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g(\varphi)}=\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(x)}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}.

As before, by Corollary
5.4
there exists an increasing net
(g^{\prime}_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
of positive affine
w^{*}
-continuous functions
g^{\prime}_{\alpha}\colon V^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
, with
g^{\prime}_{\alpha}|_{S}<g^{\prime}
for all
\alpha
, such that
(g^{\prime}_{\alpha}|_{S})_{\alpha}
converges pointwise to
g^{\prime}
. So, for
\alpha
we have that
g^{\prime}_{\alpha}|_{S}=J(x_{\alpha})|_{S}
for some
x_{\alpha}\in C^{\circ}
by Theorem

. It now follows that

\frac{g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\varphi)}{g(\varphi)}\leq\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\varphi)}{g(\varphi)}=\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{g^{\prime}_{\alpha}(\varphi)}{g^{\prime}(\varphi)}\leq 1

hence
g^{\prime}(\varphi)\leq g(\varphi)
for all
\varphi\in S
. Interchanging the roles between

and

shows that

.

This implies that
\lambda g=g+J(x)|_{S}
, which is absurd, as

and

does not correspond to an element of

. We conclude that there is no other state that is mapped to a finite value by

, hence

g(\varphi)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $\varphi=\psi$};\\
\infty&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Since

for all

6
Atoms and gauge-reversing maps

The main goal of this section is to show how gauge-reversing maps
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
give rise to a correspondence between extreme vectors of the cones
C
and
K
, and the pure states of the order unit spaces
V
and
W
. This will further be used to prove that a reflexive order unit space
V
is spanned by the extreme vectors of the cone in case a gauge-reversing map exists.

Recall that a gauge-reversing map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
maps extreme half-lines to extreme half-lines, see Corollary
2.6
. In fact, more can be said.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
be a order unit spaces and
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
be a gauge-reversing map. Let
x\in C^{\circ}
and suppose that
p\in C
is an extreme vector with
M(p/x)=1
. Then the following hold:

(i)

For the extreme half-line
\ell_{x}^{p}
we have that
\ell_{x}^{p}=\{x+tp\colon t\in(-1,\infty)\}
.

(ii)

There exists an extreme vector
q
of
K
with
M(q/\Psi(x))=1
such that

\Psi(x+tp)=\Psi(x)-\textstyle{\frac{t}{t+1}}q\mbox{\hskip 20.44434ptfor all $t\in(-1,\infty)$}.

In particular,
\Psi
maps the extreme half-line
\ell_{x}^{p}
bijectively onto the extreme half-line
\ell_{\Psi(x)}^{q}
.

Proof.

Since
M(p/x)=1
it follows that
x-p\in\partial C
, which yields
\ell_{x}^{p}=\{x+tp\colon t\in(-1,\infty)\}
.

We know that
\Psi(x+tp)=\Psi(x)+s(t)q
for some extreme vector
q\in K
and scalar function
s
by Corollary
2.6
. We can normalise
q
so that
M(q/\Psi(x))=1
. As
\Psi
is antitone, we see that if
t>0
, then
s(t)<0
. Likewise, if
t<0
, then
s(t)>0
.

We claim that
d_{T}(x+tp,x)=|\log(t+1)|
for all
t\in(-1,\infty)
. Indeed, for
t>0
we have
M(x/x+tp)\leq 1
, so
d_{T}(x+tp,x)=\log M(x+tp/x)
. Suppose
\lambda>0
is such that
x+tp\leq\lambda x
. Then
p\leq\frac{\lambda-1}{t}x
, so
\frac{\lambda-1}{t}\geq M(p/x)=1
. Thus,
1+t\leq\lambda
, which yields
1+t\leq M(x+tp/x)
. As
x+tp\leq(t+1)x
, we see that
M(x+tp/x)\leq t+1
, hence
d_{T}(x+tp,x)=\log(t+1)
. Now, if
-1<t<0
, then similarly, we have
M(x+tp/x)\leq 1
, so
d_{T}(x+tp,x)=\log M(x/x+tp)
. Furthermore, if
\lambda>0
is such that
x\leq\lambda(x+tp)
, then
p\leq\frac{\lambda-1}{-t\lambda}x
, so
\frac{\lambda-1}{-t\lambda}\geq M(p/x)=1
. Thus,
\frac{1}{t+1}\leq\lambda
, which yields
\frac{1}{t+1}\leq M(x/x+tp)
. Since we also have
x\leq\frac{1}{t+1}(x+tp)
, it follows that
M(x/x+tp)=\frac{1}{t+1}
, hence
d_{T}(x+tp,x)=-\log(t+1)
.

In the same way it can be shown that
d_{T}(\Psi(x)+s(t)q,\Psi(x))=|\log(s(t)+1)|
, hence

|\log(s(t)+1)|=d_{T}(\Psi(x)+s(t)q,\Psi(x))=d_{T}(\Psi(x+tp),\Psi(x))=d_{T}(x+tp,x)=|\log(t+1)|.

This implies that either
s(t)=t
or
s(t)=-\frac{t}{t+1}
. Since
s(t)
and
t
have opposite signs, we conclude that
s(t)=-\frac{t}{t+1}
and
\Psi(x+tp)=\Psi(x)-\frac{t}{t+1}q
as required.
∎

We call an extreme vector
p
of the cone
C
in an order unit space
(V,C,u)
an

if
M(p/u)=1
. Furthermore, we say that atoms

of

are

if
p_{1}+\dots+p_{n}\leq u
.

Let
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
be complete order unit spaces with
C=C_{sc}
. Suppose there exists a gauge-reversing map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
with
\Psi(u)=e
. Then we can consider the extension of
\Psi
to the reverse-Funk Busemann points given in (

), i.e.,

\Psi(h)(x)=h(\Psi^{-1}(x))\qquad(x\in K^{\circ}).

This extension maps parts onto parts by
Theorem

. In particular, it maps singleton reverse-Funk Busemann points bijectively onto singleton Funk Busemann points. As
C=C_{sc}
, we know that the singleton reverse-Funk Busemann points are precisely the functions of the form

h_{p}(x):=\log\sup_{\varphi\in S}\frac{\varphi(p)}{\varphi(x)}=\log M(p/x)\qquad(x\in C^{\circ})

where

is an atom of

. So,

\Psi(h_{p})(x)=h_{p}(\Psi^{-1}(x))\qquad(x\in K^{\circ})

is a singleton Funk Busemann point. By

there exists a pure state
\psi_{p}\in S(K)
such that
\Psi(h_{p})(x)=\log\psi_{p}(x)
for

. We will denote the induced bijection between the atoms of
C
and the pure states of

by

, so

\Psi^{*}(p)=\psi_{p}\qquad(\mbox{$p$ atom of $C$).}

Using these observations and the previous lemma we get the following result.

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space such that

. If there is a complete order unit space
(W,K,e)
and a gauge-reversing map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}

\Psi(u)=e
, then

(i)

There is a bijection between the atoms of
C
and the atoms of
K
.

(ii)

The map
\Psi^{*}
is a bijection between the atoms of

Taking

The following theorem precisely describes how linear combinations of orthogonal atoms relate to the interior of the cone, the order unit norm, and how they correspond to orthogonality via a gauge-reversing map.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be a complete order unit space such that
C=C_{sc}
, and suppose that there is a complete order unit space
(W,K,e)
with
K=K_{sc}
and a gauge-reversing map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
with
\Psi(u)=e
. Let
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
be orthogonal atoms of
C
and let
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
be the corresponding atoms of
K
according to
Lemma

6.1

. Then the following hold:

(i)

For all
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
we have

u+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}p_{k}\in C^{\circ}\ \mbox{ if and only if }\ \lambda_{k}>-1\ \mbox{ for all }k=1,\dots,n.

(ii)

For all
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}>-1

\Psi\Bigl(u+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}p_{k}\Bigr)=e-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k}+1}q_{k}.

(iii)

The atoms
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
are orthogonal.

(iv)

The atoms
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
are linearly independent.

(v)

For all
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
we have

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}p_{k}\right\|_{u}=\max_{1\leq k\leq n}|\lambda_{k}|.

(vi)

The following are equivalent:

(a)

p_{1}+\dots+p_{n}=u
;

(b)

p_{1}+\dots+p_{n}\in C^{\circ}
;

(c)

q_{1}+\dots+q_{n}=e
;

(d)

q_{1}+\dots+q_{n}\in K^{\circ}
.

Proof.

(i)
: It follows from the fact that
M(p_{1}/u)=1
that if

, then
\lambda_{1}>-1
. Suppose that for some

we have
u+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}p_{k}\in C^{\circ}
and, without loss of generality, assume that
\lambda_{n}\leq-1
. Then there exists
\varepsilon>0
such that
u+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{k}p_{k}\geq(1+\varepsilon)p_{n}
. Define
\lambda:=\max\{0,\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n-1}\}
and
P:=p_{1}+\dots+p_{n-1}
. It follows that

and

(1+\lambda)u-\lambda p_{n}\geq u+\lambda P\geq u+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{k}p_{k}\geq(1+\varepsilon)p_{n}.

Hence
(1+\lambda)u\geq(1+\lambda+\varepsilon)p_{n}

(ii)

6.1

We say that in an order unit space
(V,C,u)
two vectors
x,y\in C^{\circ}
are
connected by a path of finite piecewise extreme directions
if there are atoms
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
of
C
and
x=x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}=y
for some
n\geq 1
such that
x_{k}\in C^{\circ}\cap\{x_{k-1}+tp_{k}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}\setminus\{x_{k-1}\}
for all
k=1,\dots,n
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
(V,C,u)
be an order unit space. Then
V
is the span of the atoms of
C
if and only if for every two
x,y\in C^{\circ}
there is a path of finite piecewise extreme directions connecting
x
and
y
.

Proof.

If
V
is the span of the atoms of
C
, then for any two distinct
x,y\in C^{\circ}
we can write
y-x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}p_{i}
for some
n\geq 1
, where
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
are linearly independent atoms of
C
and none of the
\lambda_{k}
are zero and in decreasing order.
Let
I:=\{i\colon\lambda_{i}>0\}
and
J:=\{i\colon\lambda_{i}<0\}
. Define
x_{0}:=x
and
x_{k}:=x+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}p_{i}
for
k=1,\dots,n
. It follows that
x_{k}\in C^{\circ}
for all
k
. Indeed, if
k\in I
, then
x_{k}\geq x
, and if
k\in J
, then
x_{k}\geq y
. Hence
x_{k}\in C^{\circ}\cap\{x_{k-1}+tp_{k}\colon t\in\mathbb{R}\}\setminus\{x_{k-1}\}
for all
k=1,\dots,n
, so
x
and
y
are connected by a path of finite piecewise extreme directions.

Conversely, let
x\in V\setminus\{0\}
. Then there is an
\varepsilon>0
such that
u\neq u+\varepsilon x\in C^{\circ}
. Since
u
and

are connected by a path of finite extreme directions, there are atoms

such that
\varepsilon x=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}p_{k}
, showing that

is spanned by the atoms of

.
∎

It follows from

and

is spanned by the atoms of

.
∎

Theorem 6.6

\psi

The main goal of this section is to show that if there exists a gauge-reversing map

is homogeneous.
Lemma

6.1
allows us to analyse directional derivatives of gauge-reversing maps.

Proposition 7.1
.

Let
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
be a gauge-reversing map,
x\in C^{\circ}
, and
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}
be linearly independent extreme vectors of
C
with
M(r_{k}/x)=1
for
k=1,\ldots,n
. If
q_{1},\ldots,q_{n}
are extreme vectors of
K
such that
\Psi
maps the extreme half-line
\ell_{x}^{r_{k}}
onto the extreme half-line
\ell_{\Psi(x)}^{q_{k}}
and
M(q_{k}/\Psi(x))=1
for
k=1,\ldots,n
, then

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\left(\Psi\Bigl(x+t\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}r_{k}\Bigr)-\Psi(x)\right)=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}q_{k}.

Proof.

We argue by induction on
n
. For
\theta\neq 0
define the function
F_{\theta}\colon t\mapsto\frac{\theta t}{\theta t+1}
for
t\in\mathbb{R}
with
|t|
sufficiently small. It follows that
\Psi(x+t\lambda_{1}r_{1})=\Psi(x)-F_{\lambda_{1}}(t)q_{1}
by Lemma

\lim_{t\to 0}{\textstyle\frac{1}{t}}\left(\Psi(x+t\lambda_{1}r_{i})-\Psi(x)\right)=\lim_{t\to 0}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{t}}F_{\lambda_{1}}(t)q_{1}=-\lambda_{1}q_{1},

proving the base case.

Suppose that for some
m\geq 1
we have

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\left(\Psi\Bigl(x+t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}r_{k}\Bigr)-\Psi(x)\right)=-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}q_{k}.

If
|t|
is sufficiently small, then by Lemma
6.1

\displaystyle\Psi\Bigl(x+t\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\lambda_{k}r_{k}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi\Bigl(\Bigl(x+t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}r_{k}\Bigr)+t\lambda_{m+1}r_{m+1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi\Bigl(x+t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}r_{k}\Bigr)-F_{M_{m+1}(t)}(t\lambda_{m+1})q_{m+1}(t),

where
M_{m+1}(t)=M(r_{m+1}/x+t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}r_{k})
and
q_{m+1}(t)
is the extreme vector of
K
given by

-q_{m+1}(t)=F_{M_{m+1}(t)}(1)^{-1}\left(\Psi\Bigl(x+t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}r_{k}+r_{m+1}\Bigr)-\Psi\Bigl(x+t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}r_{k}\Bigr)\right).

By Lemma
2.4
,
M(r_{m+1}/x+t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}r_{k})\to M(r_{m+1}/x)=1
as
t\to 0
, hence
F_{M_{m+1}(t)}(1)^{-1}\to 2
as
t\to 0
. As the order unit norm topology coincides with the
d_{T}
-metric topology on
C^{\circ}
and
K^{\circ}
(see e.g.,
[
19
]
), we find that
-q_{m+1}(t)\to-q_{m+1}
as
t\to 0
. Again by Lemma
2.4
, we have
\frac{1}{t}F_{M_{m+1}(t)}(t\lambda_{m+1})\to\lambda_{m+1}
as
t\to 0
, hence by applying the induction hypothesis we conclude that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}

\displaystyle\left(\Psi\Bigl(x+t\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\lambda_{k}r_{k}\Bigr)-\Psi(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{1}{t}\left(\Psi\Bigl(x+t\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}r_{k}\Bigr)-\Psi(x)\right)-\lim_{t\to 0}{\textstyle\frac{1}{t}}F_{M_{m+1}(t)}(t\lambda_{m+1})q_{m+1}(t)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}q_{k}-\lambda_{m+1}q_{m+1},

which completes the induction step.
∎

From Proposition
7.1
we see that if
(V,C,u)
is a complete order unit space that is spanned by the atoms of
C
, then
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is Gateaux differentiable at every
x\in C^{\circ}
. In this case we will denote the Gateaux derivative at
x
by
D\Psi(x)\colon V\to W
. Using that
W
is spanned by the atoms of
K
by
Corollary

6.5
, one can show in the same way that
\Psi^{-1}
is Gateaux differentiable at any
x\in K^{\circ}
.

Lemma 7.2
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map, then for
x\in C^{\circ}
we have that
-D\Psi(x)\colon V\to W
is a homogeneous of degree 1 order-isomorphism with inverse
-D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))
.

Proof.

We start by proving that
-D\Psi(x)
is homogeneous of degree 1 and order-preserving. Let
y,z\in V
and
\lambda\neq 0
. Then

\displaystyle-D\Psi(x)(\lambda y)

\displaystyle=-\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\Psi(x+\lambda ty)-\Psi(x)}{t}=-\lambda\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\Psi(x+\lambda ty)-\Psi(x)}{\lambda t}=-\lambda D\Psi(x)(y).

If
y\leq z
, then we have for any
t\neq 0
sufficiently small that

\frac{\Psi(x+tz)-\Psi(x)}{t}\leq\frac{\Psi(x+ty)-\Psi(x)}{t},

as
\Psi
is antitone. Thus,
D\Psi(x)(z)\leq D\Psi(x)(y)
, hence
-D\Psi(x)(y)\leq-D\Psi(x)(z)
.

By interchanging the roles of
\Psi
and
\Psi^{-1}
, one can show in the same way that
\Psi^{-1}
is Gateaux differentiable at any
x\in K^{\circ}
so that
-D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))
is also homogeneous of degree 1 and order-preserving.

Next, we prove that
-D\Psi(x)
is bijective with inverse
-D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))
. Let
y=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}r_{k}
where
r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}
are linearly independent extreme vectors of
C
such that
M(r_{k}/x)=1
for all
k=1,\ldots,n
. By Corollary
2.6
and Lemma
6.1
there are extreme vectors
q_{k}
of
K
such that
M(q_{k}/\Psi(x))=1
and

\Psi(x+tr_{k})=\Psi(x)-\frac{t}{t+1}q_{k}\qquad\mbox{for all $t\in(-1,\infty)$ and $k=1,\ldots,n$}.

So,

D\Psi(x)(r_{k})=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\Psi(x+tr_{k})-\Psi(x)}{t}=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{-1}{t+1}q_{k}=-q_{k}

and

\displaystyle D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))(-D\Psi(x)(r_{k}))=D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))(q_{k})=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x)+tq_{k})-x}{t}=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{-1}{t+1}r_{k}=-r_{k}.

Using the identity for the Gateaux derivative for gauge-reversing maps in Proposition
7.1
now yields

-D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))(-D\Psi(x)(y))=-D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}q_{k}\right)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}r_{k}=y.

By interchanging the roles of
\Psi
and
\Psi^{-1}
, we also have that
-D\Psi(x)(-D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))(y))=y
, hence
-D\Psi(x)\colon V\to W
is a bijection with inverse
-D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))
. Thus,
-D\Psi(x)\colon V\to W
is an order-isomorphism.
∎

Given a gauge-reversing map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
, we define the map
S_{x}\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
by

S_{x}(y)=-D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))(\Psi(y))\mbox{\qquad for $y\in C^{\circ}$}.

(7.1)

We shall prove that
S_{x}
is a
d_{T}
-symmetry for each
x\in C^{\circ}
, for which we need the following lemma.

Lemma 7.3
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map, then for each
x\in C^{\circ}
the map
S_{x}
satisfies:

(i)

S_{x}(x+\lambda r)=x-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+1}r
for any extreme vector
r\in C
with
M(r/x)=1
and
\lambda>-1
.

(ii)

S_{x}
is a gauge-reversing map.

(iii)

DS_{x}(x)=-\mathrm{Id}_{V}
.

Proof.

Let
r\in C
be an extreme vector such that
M(r/x)=1
. By Lemma
6.1
we have that

\Psi(x+\lambda r)=\Psi(x)-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+1}q

for some extreme vector
q\in K
with
M(q/\Psi(x))=1
and all
\lambda>-1
. Note this also implies that we have the identity

\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x)+\mu q)=\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x-\textstyle{\frac{\mu}{\mu+1}}r))=x-\textstyle{\frac{\mu}{\mu+1}}r

for all
\mu>-1
. Hence for
\lambda>-1
and sufficiently small
|t|
, we have

\displaystyle\Psi^{-1}\bigl(\Psi(x)+t\Psi(x+\lambda r)\bigr)-x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Psi^{-1}\bigl(\Psi(x)+t(\Psi(x)-\textstyle{\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+1}}q)\bigr)-x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle{\frac{1}{t+1}}\Psi^{-1}\bigl(\Psi(x)-\textstyle{\frac{t\lambda}{(t+1)(\lambda+1)}}q\bigr)-x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle{\frac{1}{t+1}}\bigl(x+\textstyle{\frac{t\lambda}{(t+1)(\lambda+1)-t\lambda}}r\bigr)-x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\textstyle{-\frac{t}{t+1}}x+\textstyle{\frac{t\lambda}{(t+1)^{2}(\lambda+1)-t(t+1)\lambda}}r,

and so, it follows that

S_{x}(x+\lambda r)=-\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\Psi^{-1}\bigl(\Psi(x)+t\Psi(x+\lambda r)\bigr)-x}{t}=\lim_{t\to 0}\left(\textstyle{\frac{1}{t+1}}x-\textstyle{\frac{\lambda}{(t+1)^{2}(\lambda+1)-t(t+1)\lambda}}r\right)=x-\textstyle{\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+1}}r.

This proves statement
(i)
.

For statement
(ii)
, note that
-D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))\colon K^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
is a homogeneous degree 1 order-isomorphism by Lemma
7.2
, hence
-D\Psi^{-1}(\Psi(x))
is gauge-preserving. This implies that
S_{x}\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
is gauge-reversing.

To prove the final assertion let
y\in V
. We can write
y=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}r_{k}
where the
r_{k}
’s are extreme vectors of
C
such that
M(r_{k}/x)=1
for all
k
and
\lambda_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
S_{x}
is a gauge-reversing map, Proposition
7.1
together with statement
(i)
imply that

DS_{x}(x)(y)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{S_{x}(x+ty)-S_{x}(x)}{t}=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}r_{k}=-y,

which proves statement
(iii)
.
∎

We can now show that
S_{x}
is a
d_{T}
-symmetry.

Theorem 7.4
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map, then for each
x\in C^{\circ}
the map
S_{x}\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
is a
d_{T}
-symmetry with unique fixed point
x
. Moreover,
(C^{\circ},d_{T})
and
(K^{\circ},d_{T})
are Finsler symmetric spaces.

Proof.

Taking
\lambda=0
in Lemma
7.3
(i)
yields that
x
is a fixed point of
S_{x}
. Moreover, we also know from Lemma
7.3
that
S_{x}
is gauge-reversing, and hence a
d_{T}
-isometry.

To show that
S^{2}_{x}
is the identity map on
V
, we first note that
S^{2}_{x}
is a gauge-preserving map. It follows from
[
30
, Theorem B]
that there exists
T_{x}\in\mathrm{Aut}(C)=\{T\in\mathrm{GL}(V)\colon T(C)=C\}
such that
T_{x}=S^{2}_{x}
on
C^{\circ}
. Note that
T_{x}x=x
and by Lemma
7.3
(i)
we have
T_{x}(x+r)=S_{x}^{2}(x+r)=x+r
for
r\in C
an extreme vector with
M(r/x)=1
, hence, since
T_{x}
is affine, it leaves the line through
x
and
x+r
invariant. So
T_{x}(x+\lambda r)=x+\lambda r
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Let
y\in C^{\circ}
and write
y=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}r_{k}
where
r_{k}
is an extreme vector of
C
with
M(r_{k}/x)=1
for all
k
. Then

nx+y=\sum_{k=1}^{n}(x+\lambda_{k}r_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{x}(x+\lambda_{k}r_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}T_{x}x+T_{x}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}r_{k}\right)=nx+S^{2}_{x}(y),

hence
S^{2}_{x}(y)=y
.

To show that
x
is the unique fixed point of
S_{x}
assume by way of contradiction that
y\neq x
is a fixed point of
S_{x}
. Then
y
must be linearly independent from
x
, as
S_{x}
is homogeneous of degree
-1
. Consider the open
d_{T}
-ball with centre
y
,

B=\{z\in C^{\circ}\colon d_{T}(z,y)<d_{T}(x,y)\}.

It is known that
B
is open for the order unit norm and convex, see
[
19
, Corollary 2.5.6 and Lemma 2.6.2]
. Note that
S_{x}(B)\subseteq B
, since
y
is a fixed point of
S_{x}
, and that
x
is in the boundary of
B
. The Hahn-Banach separation theorem implies the existence of a continuous linear functional
\psi
on
V
and
\alpha\geq 0
such that
\psi(x)=\alpha
and
\psi(z)>\alpha
for all
z\in B
. The continuity of
\psi
implies that
\psi\circ S_{x}
is Gateaux differentiable. Now, if we compute the derivative of
\psi\circ S_{x}
at
x
in the direction of
y-x
, we find that

D(\psi\circ S_{x})(x)(y-x)=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\psi(S_{x}(x+t(y-x)))-\psi(S_{x}(x))}{t}=\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{\psi(S_{x}(x+t(y-x)))-\alpha}{t}\geq 0.

However, if we use Lemma
7.3
(iii)
and the continuity of
\psi
again, we also have

D(\psi\circ S_{x})(x)(y-x)=\psi(DS_{x}(x)(y-x))=\psi(x-y)<0,

which is absurd. We conclude that
S_{x}
is a
d_{T}
-symmetry with unique fixed point
x
.

The final assertion is a direct consequence of the first one.
∎

Remark 7.5
.

We like to point out that in
[
9
]
Chu studied Finsler symmetric cones that are linearly homogeneous and in which the symmetries are bi-analytic maps on
C^{\circ}
. He showed that such cones correspond to cones in JB-algebras. The smoothness assumptions on the symmetries and the fact that the cone is linearly homogeneous allow one to use methods from Lie theory that cannot be used here, as no smoothness assumptions are made on the gauge-reversing map.

In the remainder of this section we show that the
d_{T}
-symmetries,
S_{x}\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
for
x\in C^{\circ}
, can be used to show that the cone
C
is homogeneous. To begin we note that if
x,y\in C^{\circ}
, then
S_{y}\circ S_{x}\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
is a homogeneous degree 1 order-isomorphism, so by
[
30
, Theorem B]
there exists a linear map
T\in\mathrm{Aut}(C)
such that
T=S_{y}\circ S_{x}
on
C^{\circ}
. The idea is to use compositions of these linear automorphisms of
C
to show that
C^{\circ}
is homogeneous.

Lemma 7.6
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
, and let
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
be a gauge-reversing map. For each
x\in C^{\circ}
, each extreme vector
r\in C
with
M(r/x)=1
, and each
\lambda>-1
, there exists a linear map
T\in\mathrm{Aut}(C)
such that
Tx=x+\lambda r
.

Proof.

We will use Lemma
7.3
(i) and find a
\mu>-1
such that
S_{x+\mu r}(x)=x+\lambda r
. So,
S_{x+\lambda r}\circ S_{x+\mu r}
maps
x
to
x+\lambda r
. It then follows from
[
30
, Theorem B]
that there exists a linear map
T\in\mathrm{Aut}(C)
such that
T=S_{x+\lambda r}\circ S_{x+\mu r}
on
C^{\circ}
, since the composition is a homogeneous degree 1 order-isomorphism.

Let
\mu=\sqrt{\lambda+1}-1
. It is easy to check that
M(r/x+\mu r)=\frac{1}{1+\mu}
, as
M(r/x)=1
.
Set
r^{\prime}=(1+\mu)r
, so
M(r^{\prime}/x+\mu r)=1
. Now note that by Lemma
7.3
(i) we have

S_{x+\mu r}(x)=S_{x+\mu r}\Bigl(x+\mu r-\frac{\mu}{1+\mu}r^{\prime}\Bigr)=x+\mu r+\mu r^{\prime}=x+(2\mu+\mu^{2})r=x+\lambda r,

which completes the proof.
∎

The previous lemma has the following consequence.

Corollary 7.7
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
, and let
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
be a gauge-reversing map. If
x,y\in C^{\circ}
are such that
y-x=\lambda r
for some
r\in\mathrm{ext}(C)
, then there exists a linear map
T\in\mathrm{Aut}(C)
such that
Tx=y
.

Proof.

If we write
\lambda r=\lambda M(r/x)M(r/x)^{-1}r:=\lambda M(r/x)p
, then
M(p/x)=1
. Therefore, we must have
\lambda M(r/x)>-1
, as
x-p\in\partial C
and
x+\lambda M(r/x)p=y\in C^{\circ}
. Applying Lemma
7.6
to
y=x+\lambda M(r/x)p\in C^{\circ}
shows that there is a linear map
T\in\mathrm{Aut}(C)
such that
Tx=y
.
∎

It now follows immediately from
Corollary

7.7
and
Lemma

6.4
that
C^{\circ}
is homogeneous.

Theorem 7.8
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
. If there exists a gauge-reversing map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
, then
C^{\circ}
is homogeneous.

8
Self-duality of the cone

In this section we show that if there exists a gauge-reversing map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
, then
C
is self-dual with respect to an inner-product on
V
. Here the assumption that
C=C_{sc}
will play a crucial role. The key idea is to show that the atoms of
C
correspond exactly to the pure states of
S
in that case.

Proposition 8.1
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
and
C=C_{sc}
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map, then for every atom
p
of
C
there is a unique pure state
\psi_{p}
such that
\psi_{p}(p)=1
. Moreover,

\psi_{p}(x)=M(p/S_{u}(x))\qquad(x\in C^{\circ}),

(8.1)

where
S_{u}\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
is the symmetry at
u
given by (
7.1
). In particular,
u-p
is a point of smoothness of
C
. Furthermore, for each pure state
\xi
of
(V,C,u)
there exists an atom
p
of
C
such that
\xi(p)=1
, i.e.,
\xi=\psi_{p}
.

Proof.

Applying
Theorem

6.2
taking
S_{u}
for
\Psi
and
Lemma

7.3
, we get a pure state
\psi_{p}
of
(V,C,u)
such that
\psi_{p}(p)=1
. Moreover,

\psi_{p}(x)=M(p/S_{u}(x))
for
x\in C^{\circ}
.

To show uniqueness, suppose that
\xi
is a pure state of
(V,C,u)
with
\xi(p)=1
. Then
g(x):=\log\xi(x)
is a singleton Funk Busemann point by Proposition
5.18
. It follows that
S_{u}(g)
is a singleton reverse-Funk Busemann point by
Lemma

7.3
(ii)
and
Theorem

4.8
, which must be of the form
S_{u}(g)(x)=\log M(q/x)
for some atom
q
of
C
by Proposition
5.13
.
It follows from (
4.5
) that

\log M(q/x)=S_{u}(g)(x)=g(S_{u}(x))=\log\xi(S_{u}(x))\mbox{\qquad for all $x\in C^{\circ}$}.

In particular, for
p_{n}=\frac{1}{n}u+(1-\frac{1}{n})p
we find that
M(q/p_{n})=\xi(S_{u}(p_{n}))=\xi(nu+(1-n)p)=1
by
Lemma

7.3
(i)
. Thus,
q\leq p_{n}
for all
n
, which implies
q=p
, as
p_{n}\to p
. So,
\xi(x)=M(p/S_{u}(x))=\psi_{p}(x)
for all
x\in C^{\circ}
, hence
\xi=\psi_{p}
and
\psi_{p}
is the unique pure state such that
\psi_{p}(p)=1
. This implies that
u-p
is a point of smoothness, since
\psi_{p}
is the unique supporting state at
u-p
.

Finally, if
\rho
is a pure state, then
f(x):=\log\rho(x)
is a singleton Funk Busemann point by Proposition
5.18
. Reasoning as before,
h(x):=S_{u}(f)(x)=f(S_{u}(x))
is a singleton reverse-Funk Busemann point by
Lemma

7.3
(ii)
and
Theorem

4.8
, which is of the form
h(x)=\log M(p/x)
for some atom
p
of
C
by Proposition
5.13
. As
M(p/x)=\psi_{p}(S_{u}(x))
and
h(x)=\log\rho(S_{u}(x))
, we see that
\rho
and
\psi_{p}
coincide on
C^{\circ}
, hence
\rho=\psi_{p}
, which completes the proof.
∎

The
d_{T}
-symmetry
S_{u}
at
u
satisfies the following property for orthogonal atoms of
C
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
and
C=C_{sc}
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map, then for orthogonal atoms
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
of
C
the map
S_{u}
given by (
7.1
) satisfies

S_{u}\left(u+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}p_{k}\right)=u-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k}+1}p_{k}

for
u+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}p_{k}\in C^{\circ}
.

Proof.

We know from
Theorem

6.3
(i)
and
(ii)
that

S_{u}\left(u+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}p_{k}\right)=u-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{k}+1}q_{k}

for
u+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}p_{k}\in C^{\circ}
, where the atoms
q_{k}
of
C
are the ones from
Lemma

6.1
with
\Psi=S_{u}
and
x=u
. It now follows from
Lemma

7.3
that
q_{k}=p_{k}
for all
k
.
∎

Proposition 8.3
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
and
C=C_{sc}
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map, and if
p,q
are atoms of
C
with corresponding unique pure states
\psi_{p},\psi_{q}
such that
\psi_{p}(p)=1
and
\psi_{q}(q)=1
, then
\psi_{p}(q)=\psi_{q}(p)
.

Proof.

Let
\psi_{p}
and
\psi_{q}
be the unique pure states such that
\psi_{p}(p)=1
and
\psi_{q}(q)=1
from
Proposition

8.1
. Define
p_{n}=\frac{1}{n}u+(1-\frac{1}{n})p
and and
q_{m}=\frac{1}{m}u+(1-\frac{1}{m})q
for
m,n\geq 1
. Then
M(p_{n}/x)\to M(p/x)
as
n\to\infty
for all
x\in C^{\circ}
by Lemma
2.4
. Analogously, we have
M(q_{m}/x)\to M(q/x)
as
m\to\infty
for all
x\in C^{\circ}
. Furthermore, using (
8.1
), we obtain

\lim_{m\to\infty}\lim_{n\to\infty}M(p_{n}/S_{u}(q_{m}))=\lim_{m\to\infty}M(p/S_{u}(q_{m}))=\lim_{m\to\infty}\psi_{p}(q_{m})=\psi_{p}(q)

and similarly

\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}M(p_{n}/S_{u}(q_{m}))=\lim_{n\to\infty}\lim_{m\to\infty}M(q_{m}/S_{u}(p_{n}))=\lim_{n\to\infty}\psi_{q}(p_{n})=\psi_{q}(p).

Let
g_{n}(m)=M(p_{n}/S_{u}(q_{m}))
and
\lim_{n}g_{n}=g
, where

g(m)=M(p/S_{u}(q_{m}))
.

We claim that
\lim_{n}g_{n}=g
is uniform in
m
. To see this, let
\varepsilon>0
. As
p_{n}\geq p
, we know that
M(p_{n}/S_{u}(q_{m}))\geq M(p/S_{u}(q_{m}))=g(m)
. Now take
\frac{1}{n}<\varepsilon
and using that
(\varepsilon-\frac{1}{n})p\leq(\varepsilon-\frac{1}{n})u
,

\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{n}u+(1-\frac{1}{n}})p

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon u+(1-\varepsilon)p

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon u+(1-\varepsilon)g(m)S_{u}(q_{m})

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon S_{u}(q_{m})+(1-\varepsilon)g(m)S_{u}(q_{m})

\displaystyle=[\varepsilon(1-g(m))+g(m)]S_{u}(q_{m})

\displaystyle\leq[\varepsilon(1-\psi_{p}(q))+g(m)]S_{u}(q_{m}),

as
g(m)=M(p/S_{u}(q_{m}))=\psi_{p}(q_{m})\geq\psi_{p}(q)
.

Thus,
M(p_{n}/S_{u}(q_{m}))-g(m)\leq\varepsilon(1-\psi_{p}(q))
for all
m
.

It follows that the limits over
n
and
m
for
M(p_{n}/S_{u}(q_{m}))
are interchangeable and converge to the same value by
[
5
, Theorem 9.16]
, hence
\psi_{p}(q)=\psi_{q}(p)
.
∎

For an atom
p
of
C
, we define the linear form on
V
by

B(p,y):=\psi_{p}(y)\qquad(y\in V).

(8.2)

We obtain a bilinear form on
V
after further extending
B
to all of
V
in the first argument, that is, for
x,y\in V
and
x=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}p_{k}
where
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
are atoms of
C
, we put
B(x,y):=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}B(p_{k},y)
. Suppose that
x=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}p_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}q_{i}
where
q_{1},\dots,q_{m}
are atoms of
C
. Now,
by also writing
y=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\eta_{j}r_{j}
as a linear combination of atoms of
C
, it follows from Proposition
8.3
that

B(x,y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}B(p_{k},y)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\eta_{j}B(p_{k},r_{j})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{l}\eta_{j}B(r_{j},p_{k})=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\eta_{j}B(r_{j},x),

and similarly,

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mu_{i}B(q_{i},y)=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\eta_{j}B(r_{j},x).

Thus,
B
is a well-defined symmetric bilinear form on
V
. It should be clear that for any
x\in C
the map
B(x,\cdot)
is positive, and for such bilinear forms we can prove the following more general identities, which hold for positive bilinear maps on partially ordered vector spaces.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
X,Y,Z
be partially ordered vector spaces, and
B\colon X\times Y\to Z
be a positive bilinear map. If
-v\leq x\leq v
and
-w\leq y\leq w
, then
-B(v,w)\leq B(x,y)\leq B(v,w)
. Moreover, if
(X,X_{+},v)
and
(Y,Y_{+},w)
are order unit spaces, and
Z
is a normed vector lattice (e.g.
\mathbb{R}
), then
B
is bounded with
\left\|B\right\|=\left\|B(v,w)\right\|_{Z}
.

Proof.

The inequalities follow from the fact that
v\pm x,w\pm y\geq 0
,

0\leq B(v-x,w+y)+B(v+x,w-y)=2B(v,w)-2B(x,y)

together with

0\leq B(v+x,w+y)+B(v-x,w-y)=2B(v,w)+2B(x,y).

Now suppose that
v
is an order unit of
X
,
w
is an order unit of
Y
, and
Z
is a normed vector lattice. If
-v\leq x\leq v
and
-w\leq y\leq w
, then by the above,
|B(x,y)|\leq B(v,w)=|B(v,w)|
and so
\left\|B(x,y)\right\|_{Z}\leq\left\|B(v,w)\right\|_{Z}
. Taking the supremum over all such
x
and
y
yields
\left\|B\right\|\leq\left\|B(v,w)\right\|_{Z}
; combined with
\left\|B\right\|\geq\left\|B(v,w)\right\|_{Z}
we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎

Theorem 8.5
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
and
C=C_{sc}
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map with
\Psi(u)=e
, and
B
is the symmetric bilinear form on
V
as defined above, then for atoms
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
of
C
the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

B(p_{k},p_{l})=0
whenever
k\neq l
;

(ii)

p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
are orthogonal.

Moreover, a set of orthogonal atoms
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
sums to
u
if and only if it has cardinality
N:=B(u,u)
. Furthermore, every collection of orthogonal atoms can be extended to such a maximal set.

Proof.

(i)\Rightarrow(ii)
: Let
\emptyset\neq I\subsetneq\{1,\dots,n\}
be such that
\sum_{I}p_{i}\leq u
. Let
j\in\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus I
. Note that
\sum_{I}p_{i}\neq u
as otherwise the fact that
B(p_{j},p_{i})=0
for all
i\in I
would imply

1=\psi_{p_{j}}(u)=B(p_{j},u)=\sum_{I}B(p_{j},p_{i})=0.

Define
P:=\sum_{I}p_{i}\neq u
, so
u-P>0
, and further, for
m\geq 1
define

P_{m}:={\textstyle\frac{1}{m}u+(1-\frac{1}{m})(u-P)=u-(1-\frac{1}{m})P}.

It follows from (
8.1
),
Lemma

8.2
and
Proposition

8.3
that

\displaystyle M(p_{j}/P_{m})

\displaystyle=\psi_{p_{j}}(S_{u}(P_{m}))=\psi_{p_{j}}\left(u+\frac{m-1}{2m-1}P\right)=1+\frac{m-1}{2m-1}\psi_{p_{j}}(P)

\displaystyle=1+\frac{m-1}{2m-1}\sum_{i\in I}\psi_{p_{j}}(p_{i})=1+\frac{m-1}{2m-1}\sum_{i\in I}\psi_{p_{i}}(p_{j})=1,

so
p_{j}\leq P_{m}
for all
m
, hence
p_{j}\leq u-P
. It follows that
p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
are orthogonal.

(ii)\Rightarrow(i)
: If
p_{1}+\dots+p_{n}\leq u
, then for
k\neq l
it follows from
p_{k}\leq u-\sum_{i\neq k}p_{i}\leq u-p_{l}
, that
p_{k}\in\ker\psi_{p_{l}}
. Hence
B(p_{k},p_{l})=0
.

For the second part of the theorem, suppose
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
are orthogonal atoms. If these atoms sum to
u
, then
N=B(u,u)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}B(u,p_{k})=n
. If
p_{1}+\dots+p_{n}<u
, then let
P^{\prime}:=\sum_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}
, and let
q_{1},\dots,q_{n}
the corresponding atoms of
K
as in
Theorem

6.3
. By
Theorem

6.3
(vi)
it follows that
Q:=q_{1}+\dots+q_{n}\in\partial K
, hence the
w
*-closed face
\{\varphi\in S(W)\colon\varphi(Q)=0\}
of
S(W)
is non-empty. So, the Krein-Milman theorem implies that there exists a pure state
\psi
of
W
such that
\psi(Q)=0
. Let
p
be an atom of
C
such that
M(p/x)=\psi(\Psi(x))
for all
x\in C^{\circ}
by
Theorem

6.2
. Then for any
\lambda>-1
it follows from
Theorem

6.3
that

M\Bigl(p/u+\lambda P^{\prime}\Bigr)=\psi(\Psi(u+\lambda P^{\prime}))=\psi\Bigl(e-\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+1}Q\Bigr)=1,

so
p\leq u+\lambda P^{\prime}
and letting
\lambda\downarrow-1
we find that
p+p_{1}+\dots+p_{n}\leq u
and so the atoms
p,p_{1},\dots,p_{n}
are orthogonal. Therefore

N=B(u,u)\geq\sum_{k=1}^{n}B(u,p_{k})+B(u,p)=n+1>n,

which shows the second part of the theorem.
∎

Given an order unit space
(V,C,u)
as in
Theorem

8.5
, the integer
N=B(u,u)
will be called the
rank
of
V
, and a set of orthogonal atoms
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{N}\}
will be called a
frame
, so
u=p_{1}+\cdots+p_{N}
.

Note that the linear map
\hat{u}\colon V\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
\hat{u}(x):=B(x,u)
is a continuous linear functional by
Lemma

8.4
. If
x\in C
is such that
\hat{u}(x)=0
, then for any pure state
\varphi
of
V
it follows from
Proposition

8.1
that
\varphi=\psi_{p}
for some atom
p
of
C
. By
Theorem

8.5
we can extend
p
to a frame
\{p,p_{1},\dots,p_{N-1}\}
, and hence

0\leq\psi_{p}(x)=B(x,p)\leq B(x,p)+\sum_{k=1}^{N-1}B(x,p_{k})=B(x,u)=\hat{u}(x)=0,

so
x=0
by the Krein-Milman theorem and the fact that the states determine the ordering on
V
by
[
1
, Lemma 1.18]
. Hence
\hat{u}
is a strictly positive functional. Let

\Sigma:=\{x\in C\colon\hat{u}(x)=1\}

be the corresponding
base
of
C
.

Lemma 8.6
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
and
C=C_{sc}
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map and
B
is a bilinear form on
V
as above and the rank of
V
is
N
, then for all
x\in\Sigma
we have that
B(x,x)\geq\frac{1}{N}
.

Proof.

If
x\in\Sigma\cap C^{\circ}
, then by
Theorem

7.8
there is a linear map
T\in\mathrm{Aut}(C)
such that
Tu=x
. Let
\{q_{1},\dots,q_{N}\}
be a frame. Then
x=Tu=\sum_{k=1}^{N}Tq_{k}=:\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}p_{k}
, where each
\lambda_{k}>0
and
p_{1},\dots,p_{N}
are atoms of
C
. Furthermore,
1=B(x,u)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}\psi_{p_{k}}(u)=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lambda_{k}
.

It follows that

B(x,x)=\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}\lambda_{j}B(p_{i},p_{j})\geq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}^{2}B(p_{k},p_{k})=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}^{2}\geq\frac{1}{N},

(8.3)

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, as

1=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}\right)^{2}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda^{2}_{k}\sum_{k=1}^{N}1=N\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}^{2}.

As
B
is continuous by
Lemma

8.4
, this inequality also holds for general
x\in\Sigma
.
∎

We will use the strictly positive functional
\hat{u}
to show that
B
is positive semi-definite on
V
by first proving that
B
is positive semi-definite on
\ker\hat{u}
and then representing any element of
V
as a linear combination of an element in
\ker\hat{u}
and
u
.

Lemma 8.7
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
and
C=C_{sc}
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map and
B
is a bilinear form on
V
as above, then
B
is positive semi-definite on
\ker\hat{u}
.

Proof.

Let
N
be the rank of
V
and let
x\in\ker\hat{u}
. There is an
M>0
such that
x+Mu\in C^{\circ}
, and so there also is a
\mu>0
so that
\mu(x+Mu)\in\Sigma
. Hence, it follows that
\mu(x+Mu)-\frac{1}{N}u\in\ker\hat{u}
and so
\mu M=\frac{1}{N}
, and we can write

x=\mu^{-1}(\mu(x+Mu)-{\textstyle\frac{1}{N}}u).

(8.4)

That is, every element of
\ker\hat{u}
is a scalar multiple of the difference of an element in
\Sigma
and
\frac{1}{N}u
. Now, it follows from
Lemma

8.6
that

\displaystyle\mu^{2}B(x,x)

\displaystyle=B(\mu(x+Mu)-{\textstyle\frac{1}{N}}u,\mu(x+Mu)-{\textstyle\frac{1}{N}}u)

\displaystyle=B(\mu(x+Mu),\mu(x+Mu))-2\frac{1}{N}B(\mu(x+Mu),u)+\frac{1}{N^{2}}B(u,u)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N}-2\mu M+\frac{1}{N}=0.\qed

We can use the fact that
B
is positive semi-definite on
\ker\hat{u}
to show that
B
defines an inner product on
V
.

Proposition 8.8
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
and
C=C_{sc}
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map and
B
is the bilinear form on
V
as above, then
B
is an inner product on
V
.

Proof.

Let
N
be the rank of
V
, let
x\in V
, and write
x=y+\lambda u
where
y\in\ker\hat{u}
. Then it follows from
Lemma

8.7
that

\displaystyle B(x,x)

\displaystyle=B(y,y)+2\lambda B(y,u)+\lambda^{2}B(u,u)=B(y,y)+\lambda^{2}B(u,u)\geq 0,

and
B
is positive semi-definite on
V
. Suppose that
x\in V
is such that
B(x,x)=0
. Then we see that
\lambda=0
and
x\in\ker\hat{u}
. As in (
8.4
), write
x=\mu^{-1}(\mu(x+Mu)-\frac{1}{N}u)
, so
\mu(x+Mu)\in\Sigma\cap C^{\circ}
and
\mu M=1/N
. From
Lemma

8.6
we now have that

0=B(x,x)\geq\mu^{-2}\left(B(\mu(x+Mu),\mu(x+Mu))-\frac{1}{N}\right)\geq 0,

so
B(\mu(x+Mu),\mu(x+Mu))=\frac{1}{N}
.
Reasoning as in the proof of
Lemma

8.6
there exists a linear map
T\in\mathrm{Aut}(C)
such that
Tu=\mu(x+Mu)
, so we can write
\mu(x+Mu)=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}p_{k}
, where
\lambda_{k}>0
with
\sum_{k=1}^{N}\lambda_{k}=1
and
p_{1},\dots,p_{N}
are atoms of
C
. Now each inequality in (
8.3
) is an equality, and so
\lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{N}
for all
1\leq k\leq N
, hence
B(p_{k},p_{l})=0
for all
k\neq l
. It now follows from
Theorem

8.5
that
\{p_{1},\dots,p_{N}\}
is a frame. Therefore
\frac{1}{N}u=\mu(x+Mu)=\mu x+\frac{1}{N}u
, so
x=0
and
B
is an inner product on
V
, which completes the proof.
∎

Before we show that
C
is self-dual, we prove that the order unit norm topology and the inner product norm topology on
V
are the same.

Lemma 8.9
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
and
C=C_{sc}
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map and
B
is a bilinear form on
V
as above, then the order unit norm and the inner product norm are equivalent.

Proof.

Let
N
be the rank of
V
. For
x\in V
we have
-\|x\|_{u}u\leq x\leq\|x\|_{u}u
, so
-N\|x\|_{u}^{2}\leq B(x,x)\leq N\|x\|_{u}^{2}
by
Lemma

8.4
. Hence
\sqrt{B(x,x)}\leq\sqrt{N}\|x\|_{u}
. On the other hand, since the pure states are norming on
V
by the Krein-Milman theorem and
[
1
, Lemma 1.18]
, and all pure states are of the form
\psi_{p}=B(\cdot,p)
for some atom
p
of
C
by
Proposition

8.1
, it follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality that

|B(x,p)|\leq\sqrt{B(x,x)}\sqrt{B(p,p)}=\sqrt{B(x,x)}.

Hence,
\|x\|_{u}=\sup\{|B(x,p)|\colon\mbox{$p$ is an atom of $C$}\}\leq\sqrt{B(x,x)}
.
∎

Theorem 8.10
.

Suppose that
(V,C,u)
and
(W,K,e)
are complete order unit spaces such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
and
C=C_{sc}
. If
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map and
B
is a bilinear form on
V
as above, then
C
is self-dual with respect to
B
.

Proof.

If
x\in C
, then
B(\cdot,x)
is a positive linear functional that is continuous by
Lemma

8.4
. On the other hand, if
\psi
is a positive continuous linear functional, then there is an
x\in V
such that
\psi=B(\cdot,x)
, and since every pure state on
V
is of the form
\psi_{p}
for an atom
p
of
C
by
Proposition

8.1
, it follows that
0\leq\psi(p)=B(p,x)=\psi_{p}(x)
. By the Krein-Milman theorem and
[
1
, Lemma 1.18]
, the pure states also determine the ordering on
V
, so that
x\in C
, showing that
C
is self-dual with respect to
B
.
∎

9
Main result

Collecting the results we can now prove the main theorem.

Theorem 9.1
.

If
(V,C,u)
is a complete order unit space such that
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
and
C=C_{sc}
, then the following statements are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a complete order unit space
(W,K,e)
and a gauge-reversing map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
.

(ii)

There exists a
d_{T}
-symmetry
S_{x}\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
at some
x\in C^{\circ}
.

(iii)

(C^{\circ},d_{T})
is a symmetric Finsler space.

(iv)

There exists an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
V
making
V
a Hilbert space and
C^{\circ}
a symmetric cone.

(v)

There exists an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
V
and a Jordan product
\bullet
on
V
such that
(V,\bullet,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
is a JH-algebra with unit
u
and
C
equals the set of squares.

In particular, the equivalences hold if
(V,C,u)
is a reflexive order unit space.

Proof.

We first show that all statements imply
(i)
if
(V,C,u)
is a complete order unit space. We know that if
S_{x}\colon C^{\circ}\to C^{\circ}
is a
d_{T}
-symmetry at
x\in C^{\circ}
, then it is gauge-reversing by
Theorem

3.5
, hence
(ii)
, and also
(iii)
, imply
(i)
. On the other hand, if there exists an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on
V
for which is it is a Hilbert space such that
C^{\circ}
is a symmetric cone, then it follows from
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
that
V
is a JH-algebra with cone of squares
C
, unit
u
, and the inner product satisfies
\left\langle x\bullet y,z\right\rangle=\left\langle y,x\bullet z\right\rangle
for all
x,y,z\in V
. By
Theorem

2.8
, it follows that
V
must be a JB-algebra. But if
V
is a JB-algebra with unit
u
, then the map
x\mapsto x^{-1}
is clearly homogeneous of degree
-1
and is antitone by
[
3
, Lemma 1.31]
and the functional calculus
[
3
, Proposition 1.21]
. Hence, taking the inverse defines a gauge-reversing map on
C^{\circ}
, showing that
(iv)
, and hence also
(v)
, imply
(i)
.

To show that all statements are equivalent in the case where
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
and
C=C_{sc}
, we note first that it follows from
Theorem

7.4
that
(i)
implies
(ii)
and
(iii)
. Moreover, if
(W,K,e)
is a complete order unit space and
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
is a gauge-reversing map, then the cone
C
is homogeneous by
Theorem

7.8
and self-dual by
Theorem

8.10
. As
V
is complete for the order unit norm, it is a Hilbert space for the inner product as well by
Lemma

8.9
. Thus,
(i)
implies
(iv)
. We already mentioned that
(iv)
implies
(v)
by
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
.

It remains to show that
(i)
implies each of the other statements when
(V,C,u)
is a reflexive order unit space. In that case
(V,C,u)
complete, as it is reflexive. Moreover, if there exists a complete order unit space
(W,K,e)
and a gauge-reversing map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
, then
V
is spanned by the atoms of
C
by
Theorem

6.6
, and
C=C_{sc}
, as
(V,C,u)
is reflexive. So, the result follows from the previous case.
∎

We believe that the JB-algebras are precisely the complete order unit spaces
(V,C,u)
for which there exists an order unit space
(W,K,e)
and a gauge-reversing map
\Psi\colon C^{\circ}\to K^{\circ}
. This would provide an elegant characterisation of JB-algebras in purely order theoretic terms.

Appendix: Semi-continuous functions

Recall that for a topological space
X
a function
f\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty]
is said to be
lower semi-continuous at
x
if for every
\lambda<f(x)
there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
x
such that
\lambda<f(y)
for all
y\in U
. The function
f
is said to be
lower semi-continuous
if it is lower semi-continuous at every
x\in X
. Note that
f
is lower semi-continuous if and only if
\{x\in X\colon f(x)>\lambda\}
is open for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Similarly, a function
f\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty]
is said to be
upper semi-continuous at
x
if for every
\lambda>f(x)
there exists a neighbourhood
U
of
x
such that
\lambda>f(y)
for all
y\in U
. The function
f
is called
upper semi-continuous
if it is upper semi-continuous at all
x\in X
, and
f
is upper semi-continuous if and only if
\{x\in X\colon f(x)<\lambda\}
is open for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Furthermore, it follows that
f
is upper semi-continuous if and only if
-f
is lower semi-continuous.

Note that the definition of lower semi-continuity for a function
f\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty]
is equivalent to the following statement. For any
x\in X
and any
\varepsilon>0
there is an open neighbourhood
O
of
x
such that
f(y)\geq f(x)-\varepsilon
for all
y\in O
. For an upper semi-continuous function the statement is similar with the inequality reversed.

Lemma 10.1
.

Let
X
be a topological space. Then
f\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty]
is lower semi-continuous if and only if for all
x\in X
and
x_{i}\to x
we have
\liminf_{i}f(x_{i})\geq f(x)
.

Proof.

Suppose
f
is lower semi-continuous and let
x\in X
. If
(x_{i})_{i}
is a net in
X
such that
x_{i}\to x
, then there are two cases to check;
f(x)=-\infty
and
f(x)\in(-\infty,\infty]
. In the case where
f(x)=-\infty
, we clearly have that
\liminf_{i}f(x_{i})\geq f(x)
. If
f(x)>-\infty
, let
\lambda<f(x)
. Since
O:=\{y\in X\colon f(y)>\lambda\}
is open and contains
x
, there is an index
j
such that
x_{i}\in O
whenever
i\geq j
. Hence
\inf_{i\geq j}f(x_{i})\geq\lambda
, so
\liminf_{i}f(x_{i})\geq\lambda
. As
\lambda<f(x)
was arbitrary, it follows that
\liminf_{i}f(x_{i})\geq f(x)
.

Conversely, suppose that
f
is not lower semi-continuous, then there is a
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
such that
O_{\lambda}:=\{y\in X\colon f(y)>\lambda\}
is not open. Hence there is
x\in O_{\lambda}
and a net
(x_{i})_{i}
in
X
satisfying
x_{i}\to x
and
x_{i}\notin O_{\lambda}
. But then
\liminf_{i}f(x_{i})\leq\lambda<f(x)
.
∎

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
X
be a topological space. For
Y\subseteq X
the characteristic function
\chi_{Y}
is lower semi-continuous if and only if
Y
is open.

Proof.

If
\chi_{Y}
is lower semi-continuous, then
Y=\{x\in X\colon\chi_{Y}(x)>0\}
is open. Conversely, if
Y
is open, then

\{x\in X\colon\chi_{Y}(x)>\lambda\}=\begin{cases}X&\mbox{if $\lambda<0$};\\
Y&\mbox{if $0\leq\lambda<1$};\\
\emptyset&\mbox{if $\lambda\geq 1$}.\end{cases}

Hence
\chi_{Y}
is lower semi-continuous.
∎

Lemma 10.3
.

Let
X
be a compact topological space and let
f\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty]
be a lower semi-continuous function. Then
f
attains its minimum.

Proof.

If there is a
x\in X
such that
f(x)=-\infty
, then clearly
f
attains its minimum. Suppose that
f(x)\in(-\infty,\infty]
for all
x\in X
. Define for every
n\geq 1
, the open sets

O_{n}:=\{y\in X\colon f(y)>-n\}.

Since
O_{n}\subseteq O_{n+1}
for all
n\geq 1
and
\bigcup_{n\geq 1}O_{n}
is an open cover of
X
, the compactness of
X
implies that
X=O_{m}
for some
m\geq 1
. Let
\alpha:=\inf_{X}f(x)
, and let
(x_{i})_{i}
be a net such that
f(x_{i})\to\alpha
. Let
(x_{j})_{j}
be a subnet converging to some
x
, and note that Lemma
10.1
implies
f(x)\leq\liminf_{j}f(x_{j})=\alpha
. Hence
f(x)=\alpha
and
f
attains its minimum.
∎

Lemma 10.4
.

Let
X
be a topological space. If
f,g\colon X\to[0,\infty)
are upper semi-continuous, then so is the product
fg
.

Proof.

Let
x\in X
and
\varepsilon>0
. Define
\delta>0
such that
\delta(f(x)+g(x))+\delta^{2}<\varepsilon
. Then there are open neighbourhoods
O
and
V
of
x
such that
f(y)<f(x)+\delta
whenever
y\in O
and
g(y)<g(x)+\delta
whenever
y\in V
. Hence
f(y)g(y)<f(x)g(x)+\varepsilon
whenever
y\in O\cap V
.
∎

Lemma 10.5
.

Let
X
be topological space and let
(f_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
be an almost non-increasing net of real-valued functions on
X
.

(i)

Then
f_{\alpha}
converges pointwise to a function
f\colon X\to[-\infty,\infty)
.

(ii)

If
f_{\alpha}
is upper semi-continuous for all
\alpha
, then so is the pointwise limit
f
.

(iii)

If
X
is compact and
f_{\alpha}
is upper semi-continuous for all
\alpha
, then
\sup_{X}f_{\alpha}
converges to
\sup_{X}f
.

Proof.

Let
x\in X
and let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there is an index
\beta
such that
f_{\alpha}\geq f_{\alpha^{\prime}}-\varepsilon
whenever
\beta\leq\alpha\leq\alpha^{\prime}
. It follows that for
\alpha\geq\beta
we have
f_{\alpha}(x)\geq\sup_{\alpha\leq\alpha^{\prime}}f_{\alpha^{\prime}}(x)-\varepsilon
. Hence

\liminf_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(x)\geq\inf_{\beta\leq\alpha}f_{\alpha}(x)\geq\limsup_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(x)-\varepsilon,

so
\liminf_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(x)=\limsup_{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(x)
and therefore the net
(f_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
converges pointwise to a function
f
.

Suppose
f_{\alpha}
is upper semi-continuous for all
\alpha
. Let
(x_{\beta})_{\beta}
be a net in
X
that converges to
x
in
X
. Then by Lemma
10.1
we have that
\limsup_{\beta}f_{\alpha}(x_{\beta})\leq f_{\alpha}(x)
for each
\alpha
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Since
(f_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is almost non-increasing and
f_{\alpha}
converges pointwise to
f
, there is an index
\alpha_{0}
such that
f\leq f_{\alpha}+\varepsilon
and
f_{\alpha}(x)\leq f(x)+\varepsilon
whenever
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}
. Now for all
\alpha\geq\alpha_{0}
,

\limsup_{\beta}f(x_{\beta})\leq\limsup_{\beta}f_{\alpha}(x_{\beta})+\varepsilon\leq f_{\alpha^{\prime}}(x)+\varepsilon\leq f(x)+2\varepsilon,

which shows that
f
is upper semi-continuous, since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary.

If
X
is compact, then for each
\alpha
the function
f_{\alpha}
attains its maximum at say
x_{\alpha}
by Lemma
10.3
. Let
(f_{\beta}(x_{\beta}))_{\beta}
be a subnet of
(f_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
. Since
X
is compact
(x_{\beta})_{\beta}
has a convergent subnet that converges to say
x
in
X
, and since
(f_{\alpha})_{\alpha}
is almost non-increasing, there is an index
\beta_{0}
such that

f_{\beta}(x)\leq f_{\beta}(x_{\beta})\leq f_{\beta_{0}}(x_{\beta_{0}})+1

whenever
\beta_{0}\leq\beta
. Hence we may pass to a subnet
(f_{\gamma}(x_{\gamma}))_{\gamma}
such that
(x_{\gamma})_{\gamma}
converges to
x
and
(f_{\gamma}(x_{\gamma}))_{\gamma}
converges to say
\lambda
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. Then there is an index
\gamma_{0}
such that
f_{\gamma^{\prime}}(x_{\gamma^{\prime}})\leq f_{\gamma}(x_{\gamma^{\prime}})+\varepsilon
whenever
\gamma_{0}\leq\gamma\leq\gamma^{\prime}
. Taking the upper limit for
\gamma\leq\gamma^{\prime}
yields
\lambda\leq f_{\gamma}(x)+\varepsilon
by Lemma
10.1
and subsequently taking the limit for
\gamma
yields
\lambda\leq f(x)+\varepsilon\leq\sup_{X}f+\varepsilon
. As
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we have
\lambda\leq\sup_{X}f
. Conversely, for any
y\in X
, it follows that

f(y)=\lim_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}(y)\leq\lim_{\gamma}f_{\gamma}(x_{\gamma})=\lambda,

so
\sup_{X}f\leq\lambda
. We have shown that every subnet of
(f_{\alpha}(x_{\alpha}))_{\alpha}
has a subnet that converges to
\sup_{X}f
, hence
\sup_{X}f_{\alpha}
converges to
\sup_{X}f
.
∎
