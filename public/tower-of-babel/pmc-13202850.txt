Evaluating the effect of an audiovisual intervention on routine immunization knowledge among urban frontline healthcare workers in Eastern India
Authors: Hem Nandani Pathak, G Jahnavi, Bijit Biswas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRoutine immunization (RI) plays a pivotal role in preventing vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs). This study assessed an audiovisual educational intervention aimed at strengthening RI knowledge among urban frontline healthcare workers (FHWs) in Deoghar, Jharkhand.MethodsA quasi-experimental interventional study conducted in April 2024 at the Urban Primary Health Centre (UPHC), Kalyanpur, involved 45 FHWs. A validated 55-item questionnaire (Cronbach’s alpha: 0.802–0.852) assessed pre- and post-intervention knowledge on RI topics, including the National Immunization Schedule, cold chain management, biomedical waste disposal, and immunization myths.ResultsThe mean knowledge scores increased significantly from 29.0 ± 9.8 (corresponding to 52.7% correct responses) pre-intervention to 44.8 ± 6.8 (corresponding to 81.4% correct responses) post-intervention, with a mean difference of 15.8 ± 5.2 (28.7%, p < 0.001) and a large effect size (Cohen’s d = 3.0, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 2.3–3.7). Knowledge improved significantly across domains, including vaccine efficacy, minor adverse effect management, and key messages for beneficiaries. In the multiple linear regression analysis, higher educational levels (unstandardized beta [B] = 3.59, standard error [SE] = 1.27, p = 0.008) and prior training on RI (B = 6.61, SE = 1.85, p = 0.001) emerged as independent predictors of knowledge gain, collectively accounting for 53.2% of the variability in outcomes.ConclusionThe audiovisual intervention significantly improved RI knowledge among FHWs, with higher education levels and prior training as independent predictors of knowledge gain. Further research is needed to assess scalability and the impact of knowledge improvement on practice and immunization service delivery.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14580-5.Keywords: Health personnel, Primary health acre, Immunization, Knowledge, Early intervention, Educational

Introduction

Immunization is one of the most impactful public health interventions, preventing 3.5–5 million deaths annually by averting vaccine-preventable diseases (VPDs) such as measles, diphtheria, and tetanus [1–3]. It is a cornerstone of efforts to reduce child mortality and achieve universal health coverage, valued for its cost-effectiveness and significant health benefits [2, 4]. Despite progress, global immunization coverage remains insufficient, with 14.3 million children missing essential vaccines in 2022 [2, 3, 5]. In India, the Universal Immunization Programme (UIP), launched in 1985, provides free vaccines against 12 VPDs—tuberculosis, polio, diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, hepatitis B, measles, rubella, mumps, Japanese encephalitis, Haemophilus influenzae type B, and rotavirus diarrhoea. The program has significantly contributed to reducing child mortality and combating infectious diseases [6].

While the UIP has expanded immunization services, achieving and sustaining high coverage remains challenging, particularly due to gaps in knowledge and practices among frontline healthcare workers (FHWs) [7–11]. A meta-analysis of 95 studies shows that targeted interventions for healthcare providers can increase vaccination uptake by 1.2 times and improve coverage by 13% [12]. India’s immunization program depends heavily on FHWs, such as Accredited Social Health Activists (ASHAs) and Auxiliary Nurse Midwives (ANMs). ANMs organize routine immunization (RI) sessions, administer vaccines, manage cold chains, and dispose of biomedical waste, while ASHAs mobilize communities, assist in session activities, and report adverse events following immunization (AEFI) [13, 14]. However, studies highlight deficiencies in cold chain management, biomedical waste handling, and countering vaccine myths, which hinder effective service delivery [7, 8, 15].

Despite their crucial role, FHWs often receive inadequate training to meet the demands of immunization service delivery. Equipping these workers with up-to-date knowledge and skills is essential to address vaccine hesitancy, ensure safe practices, and deliver effective post-immunization care [16–18]. There is a lack of studies evaluating the knowledge of FHWs on RI [19–23], and the few available are predominantly non-interventional [19–21, 23], especially in the Indian context [20]. We hypothesized that the audiovisual intervention would significantly improve immunization-related knowledge among urban FHWs (ASHAs and ANMs). The intervention, implemented in Deoghar, Jharkhand, was designed to strengthen their understanding of immunization by addressing misconceptions and bridging critical knowledge gaps. By evaluating the effectiveness of this educational tool, the study aims to inform targeted strategies for building the capacity of FHWs, ultimately enhancing immunization practices and addressing persistent gaps in vaccine coverage.

Methods

This quasi-experimental interventional study was conducted during World Immunization Week in April 2024 at the Urban Primary Health Centre (UPHC), Kalyanpur, Deoghar, Jharkhand. With support from urban district health authorities, 50 FHWs were invited, and 47 (25 ASHAs and 22 ANMs) attended. Data from two ASHAs were excluded due to incomplete responses, resulting in a final sample of 45 urban FHWs, surpassing the minimum required sample size of 34. The sample size calculation, done using Statulator [24], was based on an anticipated effect size of at least 0.5, a precision within 5%, and statistical power of 80%.

A structured questionnaire was developed for the study, drawing from existing literature [13, 14, 20, 22, 25–29] and refined through expert consultation to ensure construct and criterion validity. To validate its clarity and comprehensibility, the questionnaire underwent pilot testing among five FHWs from a neighbouring block with similar educational and professional backgrounds. The final questionnaire consisted of 55 knowledge-based items, systematically organized into distinct domains. These domains encompassed the number and specific types of diseases preventable through vaccines listed in the National Immunization Schedule (NIS); accurate knowledge of the timing, dosage, and intervals for vaccinations, particularly focusing on tetanus vaccination protocols during pregnancy; identification and rectification of prevalent myths regarding immunization; detailed responsibilities of ASHAs before, during, and after immunization sessions; procedures for the appropriate management and timely reporting of adverse events following immunization; maintenance and monitoring of the cold chain, including correct vaccine storage practices; adherence to biomedical waste management protocols at immunization sites; recommended waiting periods for beneficiaries after vaccination; management strategies for minor vaccine-related side effects; and critical communication messages that ANMs must convey to mothers post-vaccination. Additionally, the questionnaire captured sociodemographic details such as age, occupation, length of service, religion, caste, education level, family size, and monthly family income. Questions were designed with varied directions of inquiry to ensure reliable assessment. The questionnaire’s internal consistency and reliability were confirmed by Cronbach’s alpha values ranging from 0.802 to 0.852, measured both pre- and post-intervention. Scoring involved assigning one point for each correct response, and total knowledge scores were calculated by aggregating individual item scores across both assessment periods.

The training was conducted in the UPHC conference hall and lasted for two hours. It began with a 15-minute pre-test questionnaire in Hindi to assess baseline knowledge, followed by a 90-minute interactive audiovisual session delivered via PowerPoint presentation. The session concluded with a 15-minute post-test to evaluate knowledge improvement. The 55-item questionnaire was administered in Hindi, incorporating local expressions and terminology to enhance cultural relevance and ensure comprehensibility for ASHAs and ANMs in Jharkhand. On average, participants took approximately 15 min to complete the questionnaire. The session focused on essential aspects of RI, starting with an overview of vaccines under the NIS and the diseases they prevent. It emphasized the roles of primary FHWs in identifying and mobilizing beneficiaries, supporting immunization activities, and reporting adverse events. Other key topics included cold chain maintenance during session for vaccine efficacy, biomedical waste management, addressing common immunization myths, and managing minor adverse effects. The session also stressed conveying four key messages to mothers: vaccine names and purposes, next vaccination details, managing side effects, and preserving the immunization card. Textual and pictorial content from the NIS and national operational guidelines ensured clarity and relevance. The facilitator addressed doubts immediately and encouraged active discussion to maintain participant engagement and comprehension. The session ended with a question-and-answer segment, enabling participants to clarify remaining doubts and reinforce understanding.

Statistical analysis plan

Data collected during the study were digitized using Google Forms and analysed using JAMOVI (version 2.3.28). Descriptive statistics, including means and standard deviations (SD), were used to summarize quantitative data, while frequencies and percentages were used for qualitative variables. Differences in individual knowledge items before and after the intervention were assessed using McNemar’s chi-square test, and overall changes in knowledge scores were evaluated with a paired t-test. The intervention’s effect size was quantified using Cohen’s d with a 95% confidence interval (CI). Univariate analysis was performed using Spearman’s rho correlation to identify factors associated with the intervention’s effectiveness. This was followed by multiple linear regression analysis using the forced entry method to determine independent predictors of effectiveness. The strength of associations in the regression model was expressed as unstandardized beta coefficients (B). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The study participants had a mean age of 33.5 years (SD: 6.5; range: 24–48 years). Most were ASHAs (51.1%), with 51.1% having less than five years of service experience (mean: 1.9 years; SD: 1.2; range: 0–18 years). Regarding education, 60.0% had attained at least a secondary level qualification. The mean per capita monthly family income was 93.7 United States Dollar (USD) (SD: 51.4; range: 23.7–256.5 USD). Additionally, two-thirds (66.7%) had received prior training in RI (Table 1).

Pre-intervention knowledge of various aspects of RI ranged from 15.6% to 82.2%, while post-intervention knowledge improved to a range of 51.1% to 91.1%. A significant increase in knowledge was observed across most items related to RI, except for a few areas: the correct number of tetanus shots required for a pregnant woman with a four-year-old child, the understanding that the MR vaccine prevents both measles and rubella, the role of ASHAs in assisting ANMs with line listing, and the misconception that pentavalent vaccines should be placed directly on the ice pack’s surface. Overall, the mean knowledge scores increased significantly from 29.0 ± 9.8 pre-intervention to 44.8 ± 6.8 post-intervention, with a mean difference of 15.8 ± 5.2 and a large effect size of 3.0 (95% CI: 2.3–3.7) (Table 2 and Fig. 1).

Discussion

This study assessed the effectiveness of an audiovisual educational intervention in enhancing RI knowledge among FHWs in an urban setting. The interactive session comprehensively addressed key topics, including vaccines under the NIS, diseases they prevent, debunking immunization myths, roles of FHWs during immunization sessions, management of minor adverse effects, cold chain maintenance, biomedical waste disposal, and essential communication to mothers. Significant knowledge improvements were observed, with higher education levels and prior training identified as key factors influencing better learning outcomes.

In the present study, two-thirds (66.7%) of participants had received prior training in RI, aligning with findings from a study in Rajasthan by Swarnkar et al. [20], where a similar proportion of participants had attended at least one training session. However, myths about vaccination persist despite prior training, as 64.4% of participants in our study believed that vaccination should be deferred for a child with mild illness or fever. This prevalence was comparable to findings from a study in Qatar by Abdulla et al. [21], where 66.7% and 62.5% of participants in two cohorts shared this misconception. The persistence of such myths across diverse cultural and healthcare settings suggests that current training programs may inadequately address this issue. Future training modules should explicitly include myth-busting exercises, possibly incorporating scenario-based learning or storytelling techniques to enhance retention and practical relevance. These findings emphasize the need for targeted interventions tailored to debunk vaccination myths, ensuring that training programs universally equip FHWs to address these misconceptions effectively.

In our study, baseline knowledge about the diseases prevented by immunization ranged from 15.6% to 82.2%, significantly lower than the 87.0% reported by Swarnkar et al. [20], where participants had correct knowledge regarding diseases prevented by vaccination. Notably, 82.2% of participants in our study correctly identified that the MR vaccine prevents measles and rubella, likely reflecting sensitization efforts during the MR campaign in the preceding year. However, this was lower than findings from Mukhtar et al. [30] in Malaysia, where 94.0% of FHWs knew that the MMR vaccine prevents measles, mumps, and rubella. This disparity may be attributed to differences in the study populations, as Mukhtar et al. [30] included doctors, paramedics, and medical support staff, who likely had more formal training and greater educational exposure compared to the ASHAs and ANMs in our study. These findings underscore the need for periodic, targeted training programs tailored to address persistent knowledge gaps and enhance the capacity of FHWs to deliver effective immunization services.

In our study, 48.9% of participants were aware that any AEFI should be reported immediately, a figure comparable to findings by Sebastian et al. [27] in Mysuru city, where 45.6% of healthcare professionals recognized the need to report all post-vaccination events. This similarity highlights the persistent gaps in awareness of AEFI reporting, emphasizing the need for more focused training to improve vaccine safety monitoring and ensure prompt action. Pre-intervention knowledge of various aspects of RI in our study ranged from 15.6% to 82.2%, reflecting variability in participant understanding. Following the intervention, knowledge improved significantly, with post-intervention scores ranging from 51.1% to 91.1%, demonstrating the effectiveness of the audiovisual intervention in addressing most knowledge gaps. However, some gaps persisted post-intervention, such as understanding the correct number of tetanus shots required for a pregnant woman with a four-year-old child, recognizing that the MR vaccine prevents both measles and rubella, the role of ASHAs in assisting ANMs with line listing, and misconceptions about vaccine storage, such as placing pentavalent vaccines directly on ice packs. These persistent gaps suggest the need for targeted reinforcement in future training sessions to ensure comprehensive knowledge among frontline health workers, which is critical for effective immunization service delivery.

In our study, the mean knowledge scores improved significantly from 29.0 (corresponding to 52.7% correct responses) pre-intervention to 44.8 (corresponding to 81.4% correct responses) post-intervention, with a mean difference of 15.8 (corresponding to 28.7%). Baseline knowledge in our study (52.7%) was comparable to the Turkish study by Uskun et al. [22], which assessed immunization knowledge and reported pre-test scores of 21.5 (corresponding to 53.7%). However, the Turkish study achieved higher post-test scores of 36.7 (corresponding to 91.7%) with an overall improvement of 38.0%, exceeding the improvement observed in our study. This greater improvement in Turkey could be due to the inclusion of 38.9% general practitioners and a rural-to-urban participant ratio of 2:1, compared to our study’s exclusive focus on ASHAs and ANMs in an urban setting. In comparison, the urban Indian study by Sebastian et al. [27] assessed knowledge on best immunization practices and reported a higher baseline knowledge of 60.5%, with pre-education scores of 24.2, rising to 37.25 (corresponding to 93.12%) post-education. The overall improvement of 32.62% was moderately higher than the improvement in our study. The higher baseline knowledge and greater improvement observed in Sebastian et al.‘s [27] study can be attributed to their inclusion of doctors, pharmacists, and nurses, whose professional expertise and educational background provided a stronger foundation for understanding and adopting knowledge of best immunization practices compared to the ASHAs and ANMs in our study.

We observed that higher educational levels were associated with greater knowledge gains post-intervention, aligning with findings by Swarnkar et al. [20], who reported increased knowledge with advancing qualifications. Similarly, Mukhtar et al. [30] found that participants with higher education levels had significantly better knowledge of childhood vaccination than those with lower education levels. This underscores the critical role of education in enabling effective knowledge acquisition and retention during training interventions, particularly in complex areas like immunization. In our study, prior receipt of training was positively associated with knowledge gains post-intervention, contrasting with Swarnkar et al. [20], who reported a negative association. This discrepancy may stem from differences in the quality, content, or frequency of training sessions in the respective contexts. Nicol et al. [28] supported the necessity of both pre-service and in-service training, emphasizing that while pre-service training provides foundational skills, ongoing in-service training combined with supportive supervision is essential for maintaining and enhancing competency, particularly in managing immunization-related responsibilities. Further evidence highlights the value of structured, well-designed training programs. For instance, a case study from Nigeria by Obi-Jeff et al. [29] demonstrated significant improvements in knowledge and competence among FHWs, with 85% reporting high satisfaction with training based on adult learning principles. In India, Panda et al. [31] showed that the Extension for Community Healthcare Outcomes (ECHO) platform effectively enhanced ASHAs’ knowledge and skills across diverse regions. These findings collectively emphasize the importance of tailored, continuous training initiatives to address knowledge gaps and improve the overall effectiveness of FHWs in immunization programs.

The significant improvement in immunization knowledge among urban FHWs following the audiovisual intervention demonstrates both statistical significance and meaningful practical relevance. The considerable effect size highlights the effectiveness of structured audiovisual training in addressing critical knowledge gaps in vaccine scheduling, cold chain management, biomedical waste disposal, handling minor adverse events, and dispelling common immunization myths. Given its brief duration, minimal cost, and compatibility with existing training infrastructure, the audiovisual module is practically feasible for integration into standard training curricula for ASHAs and ANMs. These findings indicate that such targeted training can potentially enhance FHWs competencies, leading to improved immunization coverage, safer practices, and more effective health service delivery. Policymakers and healthcare administrators should integrate audiovisual training modules systematically within routine FHWs education programs, particularly prioritizing individuals with lower educational attainment or limited prior immunization training. However, given the study’s urban-specific context, generalizing these findings to other geographical or rural settings must be approached cautiously. Moreover, the absence of long-term follow-up restricts understanding regarding the sustainability of knowledge improvements and their translation into enduring practice changes. Further research incorporating broader and diverse populations along with longitudinal follow-up is necessary to substantiate these findings and enhance their applicability for wider policy and practice implications. A follow-up assessment after 6 to 12 months could help evaluate the sustainability of knowledge gains. Future studies should also examine behavioral and practice changes among FHWs, such as correct AEFI reporting, cold chain management, and adherence to biomedical waste protocols, to assess the translation of knowledge into improved service delivery.

The study, while valuable, has a few limitations that merit consideration. Firstly, the relatively small sample size and focus on a single urban setting may limit the generalizability of findings to other populations, particularly in rural contexts. To improve generalizability, future studies could adopt a multi-site design involving both urban and rural health facilities across districts, enabling comparative analysis and better representation of varied frontline healthcare contexts. Secondly, the study did not evaluate long-term knowledge retention, leaving uncertainty about the sustainability of the observed gains over time. Nonetheless, the significant immediate improvements underscore the potential of audiovisual interventions for short-term knowledge enhancement. Lastly, while the study emphasized knowledge improvement rather than practical skill application or behavioural changes, it effectively addressed critical gaps, providing a strong foundation for future efforts to strengthen FHWs immunization practices and service delivery.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that an audiovisual educational intervention significantly improved RI knowledge among frontline health workers, addressing gaps in vaccine schedules, cold chain management, biomedical waste disposal, and immunization myths. Higher education levels and prior training were identified as independent predictors of knowledge gain. While the intervention proved effective, further research is needed to explore its scalability and the impact of improved knowledge on practical application and overall immunization service delivery. Periodic training needs assessments and refresher sessions might be necessary to sustain knowledge gains and tailor interventions to address emerging gaps effectively.

The study, designated as reference number 2024-355-IND-04, received prior approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) of the All India Institute of Medical Sciences (AIIMS), Deoghar, Jharkhand, India. Data collection was integrated into the training for FHWs on RI during the World Immunisation Week 2024 event. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment. Confidentiality and anonymity of participants were rigorously maintained throughout data collection, entry, analysis, and reporting.
