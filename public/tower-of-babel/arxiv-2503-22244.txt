Analysis of On-Policy Policy Gradient Methods Under Distribution Mismatch
Weizhen Wang, Xinzhe Zhou, Jianping He, Xiaoming Duan corresponding

Abstract
Policy gradient methods are among the most successful approaches for solving challenging reinforcement learning problems. Despite their empirical success, many state-of-the-art policy gradient algorithms for discounted problems estimate update directions using a sampling distribution that differs from the discounted state distribution required by the policy gradient theorem. In this work, we analyze the impact of this distribution mismatch on policy gradient methods. Specifically, for tabular direct and softmax parameterizations, we establish global convergence of objective values under biased updates. For tabular softmax policies, we further derive the exact leading-order coefficients of the asymptotic objective errors under biased and normalized exact updates. Their ratio provides an explicit criterion for determining when distribution mismatch accelerates or slows asymptotic convergence. We then extend the analysis to more general policy parameterizations by deriving explicit bounds on both the state distribution mismatch and the induced normalized policy-gradient mismatch in episodic and continuing MDPs. These mismatches vanish at least linearly as the discount factor approaches one. Under an additional smoothness assumption, we further show that the limit inferior of the squared norm of the normalized exact policy-gradient direction along the biased iterates is bounded by O ⁡ ( ( 1 − γ ) 2 ) O((1-\gamma)^{2}) in both settings. Our results provide theoretical insights into the robustness of policy gradient methods to distribution mismatch and shed light on the gap between their theoretical formulations and practical implementations.

Convergence analysis, distribution mismatch, policy gradient methods, reinforcement learning.

Introduction

1.1
Problem Description and Motivation

Policy gradient algorithms aim to identify a parameterized policy that optimizes a performance metric by iteratively updating the policy parameters along a gradient direction. In reinforcement learning (RL), popular policy gradient methods, such as Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)
(
Schulman et al., 2015
)
, Asynchronous Advantage Actor-Critic (A3C)
(
Mnih et al., 2016
)
, and Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)
(
Schulman et al., 2017
)
are widely used to maximize the
expected
discounted cumulative reward and have demonstrated strong empirical performance. Since the policy gradient can be expressed as an expectation with respect to a state distribution, it is commonly
estimated from trajectory samples in model-free implementations. However, as noted in
(
Thomas, 2014
)
, there exists a discrepancy between the practical sampling distribution and the
discounted state distribution required by the policy gradient theorem
. Specifically,
many on-policy implementations

collect state-action pairs by executing
the current policy,
store them in a rollout buffer, and estimate the update direction using minibatches sampled uniformly from the buffer
.
Since the theoretically required state distribution
weights state visits according to their temporal discount factors
, uniform sampling from the rollout buffer
without corresponding weighting or correction
leads to a distribution mismatch.

This distribution mismatch introduces bias into gradient estimates, resulting in biased update directions in many widely used policy gradient algorithms.

Consequently, standard guarantees for exact gradient ascent do not directly apply, and such biased updates can lead to suboptimal performance in certain settings

(
Nota and Thomas, 2020
)
.
Nevertheless, due to their ease of implementation and strong empirical performance, biased policy gradient algorithms remain widely adopted,
often without explicitly correcting the distribution mismatch
.

Understanding the effect of this mismatch and why such algorithms can remain effective therefore calls for further theoretical analysis.
.

1.2
Related work

The state distribution mismatch in policy gradient methods has been highlighted in numerous studies
(
Thomas, 2014
;
Nota and Thomas, 2020
;
Zhang et al., 2022
;
Wu et al., 2022
;
Tosatto et al., 2022
;
Che et al., 2023
;
Pan et al., 2023
)
.

Existing studies have investigated both its effects on policy optimization and methods for correcting the resulting bias.

Zhang et al.
(
2022
)
consider this mismatch in the
episodic
setting.

Using the temporal policy-gradient formulation in
(
Nota and Thomas, 2020
)
, they implement DisPPO by restoring the factor
\gamma^{t}
in the actor update for each transition observed at time
t
within an episode.

This approach requires
tracking the within-episode time index and assigns exponentially smaller weights to transitions occurring later in an episode
.

Their experiments show that the uncorrected PPO can outperform DisPPO in some tasks, and they interpret this advantage through the role of later transitions in representation learning using an auxiliary-task formulation.

Since the bias originates from a distribution shift, another common approach is to correct the mismatch through importance sampling. Che et al.
(
2023
)
focus on the
continuing
setting and introduce an averaging correction factor that theoretically corresponds to the importance ratio.
To approximate this ratio in practice, they collect
timestamped transitions in a rollout buffer and train an auxiliary neural network by regression
.

This state-dependent correction provides a lower-variance alternative to directly weighting samples by
\gamma^{t}
, but requires learning an additional correction model.

Instead of correcting the distribution, Tosatto et al.
(
2022
)
derive an alternative formulation of policy gradient that avoids the discounted distribution by connecting it with the gradient of the action value function
\nabla_{\theta}Q^{\pi}
, which they refer to as the gradient critic. If both the action value function
Q^{\pi}
and the gradient critic
\nabla_{\theta}Q^{\pi}
are known, then an exact policy gradient can be computed by averaging over the initial state distribution, thereby avoiding reliance on the discounted state distribution. They further show that the gradient critic satisfies a Bellman equation, allowing it to be estimated through temporal-difference methods.
This approach avoids explicit distribution correction but requires estimating the gradient critic in addition to the action-value function.
Pan et al.
(
2023
)
investigate several heuristic bias rectification approaches,
including learning rates adjustment, adaptive optimizers, and KL regularization
, through extensive experiments, but their study
is primarily empirical.

These studies provide methods for correcting the mismatch and insights into the empirical effectiveness of biased updates.
Complementing these efforts, we focus on the optimization guarantees that remain available without distribution correction.
Specifically, we study whether biased updates preserve global convergence under tabular parameterizations, compare the exact asymptotic convergence rates of biased and exact updates under tabular softmax parameterization, and how the mismatch affects stationarity guarantees under more general parameterizations.

1.3
Contributions

Our main contributions are summarized as follows:

•

Convergence and exact asymptotic rates under tabular parameterizations.
We establish global objective-value convergence under biased updates for both direct and tabular softmax parameterizations. For tabular softmax policies under a unique optimal action assumption, we further derive the exact leading-order coefficients of the asymptotic objective errors for the biased and exact updates. Their ratio provides an explicit comparison of their asymptotic convergence speeds.

•

Distribution and gradient mismatch bounds for general parameterizations.

For episodic and continuing MDPs, we derive explicit bounds on the mismatch between the discounted and undiscounted state
distributions. Under general policy parameterizations with bounded policy derivatives, we further bound the induced normalized policy-gradient mismatch and show that it vanishes at least linearly as
\gamma\to 1
.

•

Optimization guarantees under objective smoothness.
Under an additional smoothness assumption on the objective, we bound the limit inferior of the squared norm of the normalized exact policy-gradient direction along the biased iterates by
O((1-\gamma)^{2})
in both episodic and continuing MDPs.

A preliminary conference version of this work has been accepted for publication in
(
Wang et al., 2026
)
. This paper extends that version with exact asymptotic objective-error coefficients, stronger tabular convergence results allowing arbitrary positive constant stepsizes, and sharper mismatch and approximate-stationarity bounds, improving the continuing-MDP guarantee from
O(1-\gamma)
to
O((1-\gamma)^{2})
. It also provides complete proofs and expanded experiments validating the asymptotic coefficients and comparing neural-network-based actor–critic methods.

Background

2.1
Policy gradient methods

Markov decision processes:
We consider a finite Markov decision process (MDP)
M=\langle\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},R,\mathbb{P},\gamma,d_{0}\rangle
, where
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathcal{A}
are the
finite
state and action spaces, respectively,
R:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}
is the reward function,
\mathbb{P}\left(s^{\prime}\,|\,s,a\right)
is the of transitioning to state
s^{\prime}
from state
s
after taking action
a
,
\gamma\in[0,1)
is the discount factor, and
d_{0}
is the initial state distribution. We assume that rewards are uniformly bounded:
|R(s,a)|\leq R_{\max}
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
.

Policy and performance metric:
A
stationary
policy
\pi:\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
satisfies
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a\,|\,s)=1
for all
s\in\mathcal{S}
, where
\pi(a\,|\,s)
denotes the probability of selecting action
a
at state
s
.

We seek to maximize the expected discounted return

J(\pi)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t}\right]
, where
R_{t}=R(s_{t},a_{t})
and the expectation is taken over trajectories starting from
s_{0}\sim d_{0}
and following policy
\pi
.
The state value function and the state-action value function are defined,
respectively, as
V^{\pi}(s)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t}\,|\,s_{0}=s\right]
and
Q^{\pi}(s,a)\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R_{t}\,|\,s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a\right]
.
The objective can be written as
J(\pi)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}d_{0}(s)V^{\pi}(s)

(
Zhao, 2025
)
. For a fixed stationary policy
\pi
, the induced Markov chain has transition matrix
P^{\pi}
given by
P^{\pi}(s,s^{\prime})=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a\,|\,s)\mathbb{P}(s^{\prime}\,|\,s,a)
.

Continuing and episodic MDPs:
We consider both continuing tasks, in which the process evolves indefinitely, and episodic tasks, in which each episode terminates upon reaching a terminal state.
Without loss of generality, we represent an episodic MDP using a single absorbing state
z
, since multiple terminal states can be merged without changing the returns. At
z
,
\mathbb{P}(z\,|\,z,\cdot)=1
and
R(z,\cdot)=0
, and hence
V^{\pi}(z)=0
.
Since actions at the absorbing state do not affect the return, we define the set of states relevant to policy
optimization as
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
, which satisfies
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{S}

in continuing MDPs and

\tilde{\mathcal{S}}=\mathcal{S}\setminus\{z\}

in episodic MDPs.
We assume that
d_{0}(s)>0
for every

s\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
, following similar assumptions in
(
Mei et al., 2020
;
Agarwal et al., 2021
)
.
For episodic MDPs, we additionally assume
d_{0}(z)=0
. This entails no loss of generality provided
d_{0}(z)<1
: removing the initial probability mass at
z
and renormalizing the remaining distribution only rescales

J(\pi)
by a positive constant since
V^{\pi}(z)=0
, and therefore leaves the optimal policies unchanged.

Policy gradient:
We represent the policy by a parameterized function
\pi_{\theta}
and write
J(\theta)
as shorthand for
J(\pi_{\theta})
. Gradient-based methods iteratively update the parameters according to
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\eta_{t}\nabla_{\theta}J\left(\theta_{t}\right)
, where
\eta_{t}
is the step size.
For brevity, we write
\pi
for
\pi_{\theta}
. The policy gradient theorem

(
Sutton et al., 1999
)
gives

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta)=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\mu_{\pi,\gamma}(s)\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)Q^{\pi}(s,a),

(1)

where
\mu_{\pi,\gamma}
is the discounted state occupancy measure of
\pi
:

\mu_{\pi,\gamma}(s)=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\mathbb{E}_{s_{0}\sim d_{0}}\left[\mathbb{P}(s_{t}=s\,|\,s_{0},\pi)\right].

(2)

2.2
Distribution mismatch

Although (
1
) provides an exact characterization of the policy gradient, it is generally not directly computable when the MDP model is unknown. In particular, the discounted occupancy measure
\mu_{\pi,\gamma}
is typically unavailable, and explicit summation over all states becomes impractical in large state spaces. This motivates an expectation-based representation. Specifically, let

\kappa_{\pi}\triangleq\sum_{x\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}}\mu_{\pi,\gamma}(x),\qquad d_{\pi,\gamma}(s)=\frac{\mu_{\pi,\gamma}(s)}{\kappa_{\pi}},\quad s\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}},

(3)

with
d_{\pi,\gamma}(z)=0
in episodic MDPs.
Thus,
d_{\pi,\gamma}
is a probability distribution on
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
obtained
by normalizing the discounted occupancy measure
\mu_{\pi,\gamma}
.
In continuing MDPs, normalization is performed over the entire state
space and
\kappa_{\pi}=1/(1-\gamma)
. In episodic MDPs, we instead normalize the discounted occupancy over
the nonterminal states. This is consistent with episodic
data collection, in which the trajectory terminates upon reaching

z
, and is also theoretically valid because
the absorbing state
z
with
Q^{\pi}(z,\cdot)=0
does not contribute to the policy gradient direction. We define the
normalized exact policy-gradient direction
by

\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\pi,\gamma}}\left[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\mid s)Q^{\pi}(s,a)\right].

(4)

By (
1
)–(
3
),
\nabla_{\theta}J=\kappa_{\pi}\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J
. Since
\kappa_{\pi}>0
, the normalization preserves the ascent
direction. In continuing MDPs,
\kappa_{\pi}=1/(1-\gamma)
, whereas
in episodic MDPs it may depend on the policy. Although this positive
factor can in principle be absorbed into the stepsize

(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
)
, we explicitly account for it in the subsequent
analysis.

Practical implementations, however, do not generally sample states
according to
d_{\pi,\gamma}
.
In particular, many on-policy algorithms, including PPO
(
Schulman et al., 2017
)
, collect trajectories
under the current policy and form minibatches by sampling uniformly
from a rollout buffer.
To characterize the resulting state distribution, consider data collected under a fixed policy
\pi
, starting from
s_{0}\sim d_{0}
.

In episodic tasks, a new episode starts from
d_{0}
whenever the absorbing state is reached, whereas in continuing tasks the trajectory proceeds without restarting.
As the amount of collected data grows, the empirical state frequencies within the buffer converge to an undiscounted state distribution
d_{\pi}
.
In continuing tasks,
d_{\pi}
is the stationary distribution of
P^{\pi}
.
In episodic tasks,
d_{\pi}(z)\triangleq 0
, while for
s\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
,
d_{\pi}(s)
is obtained by normalizing the undiscounted cumulative occupancy measure
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}_{s_{0}\sim d_{0}}\left[\mathbb{P}(s_{t}=s\,|\,s_{0},\pi)\right]
over
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
, as detailed in (
16
).

Consequently, uniform sampling from the rollout buffer approximates an expectation under
d_{\pi}
rather than
d_{\pi,\gamma}
. Replacing
d_{\pi,\gamma}
by
d_{\pi}
in (
4
) gives the biased policy-gradient direction:

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{s\sim d_{\pi}}\left[\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)Q^{\pi}(s,a)\right].}

(5)

To isolate the effect of the distribution mismatch from finite-sample
estimation noise, we study the idealized biased update

\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\eta_{t}\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t}),

(6)

where
\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})
is the expected biased direction rather
than a finite-sample estimate.

3
Main results

In this section, we analyze biased policy-gradient methods under distribution mismatch. We first consider tabular parameterizations,
where global convergence results can be established via off-policy convergence theory, and then derive exact asymptotic
objective-error rates for tabular softmax policies. We subsequently
move beyond the tabular setting and quantify the distribution and
gradient mismatches under more general policy parameterizations.

Throughout the analysis, we use the following standard assumptions for episodic and continuing MDPs, respectively.
For episodic MDPs, we adopt the following absorption condition, as in
(
Wu et al., 2022
)
.

Assumption 1

(Almost-sure absorption)
.

For episodic MDPs, the absorbing state
z
is reached almost surely from every initial state under any stationary policy
\pi
.

For continuing MDPs, we impose the following standard ergodicity condition, as in Che et al.
(
2023
)
and Wu et al.
(
2022
)
.

Assumption 2

(Ergodicity)
.

For continuing MDPs, the Markov chain
P^{\pi}
is irreducible and aperiodic under every stationary policy
\pi
.

3.1
Tabular policy parameterization

We begin with two widely studied tabular policy parameterizations: the direct and softmax parameterizations. For each state-action pair
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
, the parameter
\theta_{s,a}

represents an action probability in the direct parameterization and a logit in the softmax parameterization. Both formulations have been widely used to obtain theoretical insights into policy-gradient methods
(
Mei et al., 2020
;
Agarwal et al., 2021
;
Li et al., 2021
;
Lan, 2023
)
.

Direct parameterization:

Under the direct parameterization,

\pi_{\theta}(a\,|\,s)=\theta_{s,a}
.
The biased update direction in (
5
) has components

(\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J)_{s,a}=d_{\pi}(s)Q^{\pi}(s,a).

(7)

To maintain valid action probabilities, each update is projected
onto the policy simplex

\Pi=\prod_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\Delta(\mathcal{A})
:

\theta_{t+1}=\operatorname{Proj}_{\Pi}\left(\theta_{t}+\eta\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})\right),

(8)

where
\operatorname{Proj}_{\Pi}
is Euclidean projection onto
\Pi
, and
\theta_{0}\in\Pi
.

Tabular softmax parameterization:

Under the tabular softmax parameterization,

\pi_{\theta}(a\,|\,s)=\frac{e^{\theta_{s,a}}}{\sum_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}e^{\theta_{s,a^{\prime}}}}
.
The corresponding biased update direction has components

(\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J)_{s,a}=d_{\pi}(s)\pi(a\,|\,s)A^{\pi}(s,a),

(9)

where
A^{\pi}(s,a)=Q^{\pi}(s,a)-V^{\pi}(s)
is the advantage
function. The parameters are updated according
to (
6
) without projection.

satisfy the coverage condition required in the work of Laroche and Tachet des Combes

3.2
Bounds for distribution mismatch

We now move beyond the tabular setting to general policy parameterizations, for which the preceding global
convergence and exact asymptotic rate
characterizations need not hold. As a first
step, we quantify the underlying state distribution mismatch between
the normalized discounted distribution
d_{\pi,\gamma}
and the
undiscounted distribution
d_{\pi}
. These bounds will serve as the
basis for analyzing the induced policy gradient mismatch in the next
subsection.

Wu et al.
(
2022
)
also quantify errors arising from the distribution mismatch. However, in episodic MDPs, their analysis concerns unnormalized discounted and undiscounted state occupancy measures. We instead compare the normalized distributions
d_{\pi,\gamma}
and
d_{\pi}
, which are the distributions directly
associated with the normalized exact and biased policy gradient
directions considered in this paper. In particular,
d_{\pi}

describes the limiting state frequencies induced by uniform sampling
from the rollout buffer.
For continuing MDPs, we directly apply the
distribution mismatch bound of Wu et al.
(
2022
, Theorem 4.4)
.

Episodic MDPs
:
We first bound the distribution mismatch for episodic MDPs under
Assumption

1
.

Theorem 3

(Mismatch bound for episodic MDPs)
.

For episodic MDPs under
Assumption

1
, there exist constants
m>0
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
, independent of
\pi
and
\gamma
, such that for every stationary policy
\pi
and every
\gamma\in[0,1)
,

\|d_{\pi,\gamma}-d_{\pi}\|_{1}\leq\frac{2m}{1-\alpha}(1-\gamma).

: We next consider continuing MDPs, where the undiscounted distribution

coincides with the stationary distribution of the transition matrix

.

We apply
(

.
As

.

For continuing MDPs under

, there exist constants

and

, independent of

3.3
Gradient mismatch induced by distribution mismatch

Building on the distribution mismatch bounds in
Section

3.2
, we now quantify the discrepancy between the normalized exact policy-gradient direction
\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J
and the biased direction
\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J
.
We impose the following boundedness condition on the policy derivatives.

Assumption 4

(Bounded policy derivatives)
.

There exists a constant
G>0
such that, for every
(s,a)\in\mathcal{S}\times\mathcal{A}
and every
\theta
,

Global convergence under tabular parameterizations.

5
Conclusion

We study the impact of state distribution mismatch on policy gradient methods for discounted MDPs. Under direct and tabular softmax parameterizations, we establish objective-value convergence to the global optimum despite biased updates. For tabular softmax policies, we derive the exact leading-order coefficients of
the asymptotic objective errors for biased and normalized exact
updates. Their ratio provides an explicit comparison of the
asymptotic convergence speeds of the two updates under the same
constant stepsize.
For general parameterizations, we derive explicit bounds on the
mismatch between discounted and undiscounted state distributions and
on the resulting normalized policy-gradient mismatch in both
episodic and continuing MDPs. Under an additional smoothness assumption, we further establish an approximate-stationarity guarantee for the biased iterates, with an error bound that vanishes as the discount factor approaches one in both settings.
Numerical experiments illustrate
the theoretical results and the effects of distribution mismatch
under both tabular and neural-network policy parameterizations.

for all

\eta>0

For episodic MDPs,

similarly gives

The framework in

Thus, in both episodic and continuing MDPs, the conditions required by

(

.

\lim_{t\to\infty}J(\pi_{t})=\lim_{t\to\infty}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}d_{0}(s)V^{\pi_{t}}(s)=J^{*}

B.3
An example where the biased gradient achieves faster convergence under general parametrization

We consider the gridworld example from
(
Sutton and Barto, 2018
, Example 4.1)
, which is also employed in
Section

4
. The gridworld is a
4\times 4
grid, where each cell represents a state, with
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\dots,16\}
, and states
1
and
16
are terminal states. In each state, the agent can take one of four possible actions:
\mathcal{A}=\{\text{up},\text{down},\text{left},\text{right}\}
. In the environment, a northwest wind is present, such that regardless of the agent’s current state and chosen action, there is an additional
\epsilon
probability of transitioning to the right and
\epsilon
probability to the downward state, and a probability of
1-2\epsilon
to move according to the intended action. The reward for all transitions is
-1
until the terminal state is reached. The objective is to find the shortest path to one of the terminal states. We take
\epsilon=0.1
and for all nonterminal states
s
, the policy is
\pi(\textup{up}\,|\,s)=\pi(\textup{right}\,|\,s)=\frac{e^{\theta}}{2(1+e^{\theta})}
and
\pi(\textup{down}\,|\,s)=\pi(\textup{left}\,|\,s)=\frac{1}{2(1+e^{\theta})}
.

Figure 6:
The
4\times 4
gridworld with terminal states 1 and 16 and stochastic wind toward the right and down.

We employ the unbiased policy gradient update
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\eta\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})
and the biased policy gradient update
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\eta\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})
for varying discount factor
\gamma
. The results are shown in
Fig.

7
,
where the reference optimum within the restricted policy class is computed numerically by evaluating
J(\theta)
through the Bellman equations and maximizing it over the shared scalar parameter
\theta
.

Figure 7:
Objective values under biased and unbiased policy-gradient updates with a shared scalar policy parameter in the gridworld task.

From the experimental results, we observe that under this restricted parameterization (the policy class is restricted such that not all optimal or possible policies can be represented), the biased algorithm not only converges to the same
optimal value
as the unbiased method but also exhibits faster convergence. As
\gamma
approaches
1
, the distribution mismatch diminishes, and the performance of the two algorithms becomes more similar.

This simple example demonstrates that despite the existence of distribution shift and the resulting theoretical gap, the biased policy gradient method can still perform well, and even outperform the unbiased method in practice.
Experiments reported in
(
Zhang et al., 2022
;
Che et al., 2023
;
Pan et al., 2023

-norm on both sides of (
34
) yields

\displaystyle\|\tilde{d}_{\pi,\gamma}-\tilde{d}_{\pi}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq 2(1-\gamma)\frac{q_{\gamma}^{\top}\text{1}}{Z_{\gamma}}.

Since
Y
and
Y_{\gamma}
are convergent power series in
\tilde{P}^{\pi}
, these three matrices commute, i.e.,
Y\tilde{P}^{\pi}Y_{\gamma}=Y_{\gamma}\tilde{P}^{\pi}Y
. Hence,
q_{\gamma}^{\top}=\tilde{d}_{0}^{\top}Y_{\gamma}\tilde{P}^{\pi}Y
.
By (
29
) and the row-substochasticity of
\tilde{P}^{\pi}
,

\tilde{P}^{\pi}Y\text{1}\leq\frac{m}{1-\alpha}\tilde{P}^{\pi}\text{1}\leq\frac{m}{1-\alpha}\text{1}.

Multiplying by the nonnegative row vector
\tilde{d}_{0}^{\top}Y_{\gamma}
yields

q_{\gamma}^{\top}\text{1}=\tilde{d}_{0}^{\top}Y_{\gamma}\tilde{P}^{\pi}Y\text{1}\leq\frac{m}{1-\alpha}Z_{\gamma}.

Consequently,

\|d_{\pi,\gamma}-d_{\pi}\|_{1}=\|\tilde{d}_{\pi,\gamma}-\tilde{d}_{\pi}\|_{1}\leq\frac{2m}{1-\alpha}(1-\gamma).

This completes the proof of
Theorem

3
.
∎

D.1
Proof of
Theorem

5

Proof.

We first establish a uniform bound on the state-action value function
Q^{\pi}
. Fix
s\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}
and
a\in\mathcal{A}
. Let
\pi^{\prime}
be the stationary policy that selects
a
with probability one at
s
and agrees with
\pi
at every other state. Define
\sigma:=\inf\left\{t\geq 1:s_{t}\in\{s,z\}\right\}
, i.e., the first time after leaving the initial state that the process either returns to
s
or reaches the absorbing state
z
. Let
p:=\mathbb{P}_{\pi^{\prime}}(s_{\sigma}=s\,|\,s_{0}=s)
, so that
p
is the probability of returning to
s
before absorption, while
1-p
is the probability of reaching
z
before returning to
s
. Consider two processes starting from
s
. The first selects action
a
at time
0
and follows
\pi
thereafter, while the second follows
\pi^{\prime}
throughout. By construction,
\pi^{\prime}
selects
a
at
s
and agrees with
\pi
at every state other than
s
. Hence, before time
\sigma
, the two processes evolve according to the same transition law. In particular, they have the same distribution of
\sigma
and the same probability
p
of returning to
s
before reaching
z
. We now decompose the absorption time at
\sigma
. For the first process, if
s_{\sigma}=z
, the process is already absorbed and the remaining time is zero. If
s_{\sigma}=s
, which occurs with probability
p
, then from time
\sigma
onward the process follows
\pi
starting again from state
s
. By the strong Markov property, the conditional expected remaining absorption time is therefore
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s]
. Hence,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a]&=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[\sigma\,|\,s_{0}=s]\\
&\quad+p\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s]+(1-p)\cdot 0\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[\sigma\,|\,s_{0}=s]+p\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s].\end{split}

(35)

Similarly, for the second process, if
s_{\sigma}=z
, the remaining absorption time is zero; if
s_{\sigma}=s
, then the process continues under the same stationary policy
\pi^{\prime}
from state
s
. Therefore, again by the strong Markov property,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s]&=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[\sigma\,|\,s_{0}=s]\\
&\quad+p\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s]+(1-p)\cdot 0\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[\sigma\,|\,s_{0}=s]+p\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s].\end{split}

(36)

Eliminating
\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[\sigma\,|\,s_{0}=s]
in (
35
) using (
36
) gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a]

\displaystyle=(1-p)\mathbb{E}_{\pi^{\prime}}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s]+p\,\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{m}{1-\alpha},

where we used the uniform expected hitting-time bound (
14
).
For
s=z
, the same bound holds trivially. Hence
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a]\leq\frac{m}{1-\alpha}
holds for every state-action pair
(s,a)
and every stationary policy
\pi
. Since
|R(s,a)|\leq R_{\max}
and rewards vanish after absorption,

\displaystyle|Q^{\pi}(s,a)|

\displaystyle=\left|\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\tau-1}\gamma^{t}R_{t}\,|\,s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a\right]\right|

(37)

\displaystyle\leq R_{\max}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\tau-1}\gamma^{t}\,|\,s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a\right]

\displaystyle\leq R_{\max}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\tau\,|\,s_{0}=s,a_{0}=a]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{mR_{\max}}{1-\alpha}.

We now bound the gradient mismatch. In the episodic case,

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J-\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J=\sum_{s}\bigl(d_{\pi,\gamma}(s)-d_{\pi}(s)\bigr)\sum_{a}\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)Q^{\pi}(s,a).

Therefore, by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J-\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s}|d_{\pi,\gamma}(s)-d_{\pi}(s)|\sum_{a}\|\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)\|_{2}|Q^{\pi}(s,a)|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{mR_{\max}G|\mathcal{A}|}{1-\alpha}\left\|\tilde{d}_{\pi,\gamma}-\tilde{d}_{\pi}\right\|_{1},

where we used
\|\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)\|_{2}\leq G
from
Assumption

4
and the uniform bound on
Q^{\pi}
in (
37
). Finally, by
Theorem

3
,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J-\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{G|\mathcal{A}|mR_{\max}}{1-\alpha}\frac{2m}{1-\alpha}(1-\gamma)

\displaystyle=\frac{2G|\mathcal{A}|m^{2}R_{\max}}{(1-\alpha)^{2}}(1-\gamma)\triangleq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}(\gamma).

∎

D.2
Proof of
Theorem

6

Proof.

In continuing MDPs, the state-action value function
Q^{\pi}
may grow as
(1-\gamma)^{-1}
when
\gamma\to 1
. We therefore exploit the fact that an action-independent baseline does not affect the policy gradient and rewrite the statewise gradient contribution in terms of the advantage function
A^{\pi}
.
For a fixed policy
\pi
, define
g^{\pi}(s):=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)Q^{\pi}(s,a)
. Since
\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)=\nabla_{\theta}\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a\,|\,s)=\nabla_{\theta}1=0
,
we may subtract the value function
V^{\pi}(s)
inside the summation and obtain

\displaystyle g^{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)\left(Q^{\pi}(s,a)-V^{\pi}(s)\right)

(38)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)A^{\pi}(s,a).

We next derive a uniform bound on
A^{\pi}
. Define the expected one-step reward under
\pi
by
{r}_{\pi}(s)=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a\,|\,s)R(s,a)
and its stationary mean by
\eta_{\pi}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}d_{\pi}(s){r}_{\pi}(s).
Let
\bar{r}_{\pi}(s):=r_{\pi}(s)-\eta_{\pi}.
Then
\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}d_{\pi}(s)\bar{r}_{\pi}(s)=0.
Using
V^{\pi}=\left(I-\gamma P^{\pi}\right)^{-1}r_{\pi}
,
r_{\pi}=\eta_{\pi}\text{1}+\bar{r}_{\pi}
and
P^{\pi}\text{1}=\text{1}
, we obtain

V^{\pi}=\frac{\eta_{\pi}}{1-\gamma}\text{1}+H_{\pi,\gamma},

(39)

where
H_{\pi,\gamma}:=(I-\gamma P^{\pi})^{-1}\bar{r}_{\pi}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}(P^{\pi})^{t}\bar{r}_{\pi}
.
Since
d_{\pi}^{\top}\bar{r}_{\pi}=0
,
\left(P^{\pi}\right)^{t}\bar{r}_{\pi}=\left(\left(P^{\pi}\right)^{t}-\text{1}d_{\pi}^{\top}\right)\bar{r}_{\pi}.
Therefore,

H_{\pi,\gamma}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left(\left(P^{\pi}\right)^{t}-\text{1}d_{\pi}^{\top}\right)\bar{r}_{\pi}.

By the geometric convergence result in (
13
), for every state
s
,
\|(P^{\pi})^{t}(s,\cdot)-d_{\pi}\|_{1}\leq 2D\beta^{t}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\|H_{\pi,\gamma}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\max_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\|(P^{\pi})^{t}(s,\cdot)-d_{\pi}\|_{1}\|\bar{r}_{\pi}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2D\|\bar{r}_{\pi}\|_{\infty}\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(\gamma\beta)^{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{2D}{1-\gamma\beta}\|\bar{r}_{\pi}\|_{\infty}.

Since
|R(s,a)|\leq R_{\max}
,
|r_{\pi}(s)|\leq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(a\,|\,s)R_{\max}=R_{\max}
and
\left|\eta_{\pi}\right|=\left|\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}d_{\pi}(s)r_{\pi}(s)\right|\leq R_{\max}
, and hence

\left\|\bar{r}_{\pi}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\|r_{\pi}\|_{\infty}+|\eta_{\pi}|\leq 2R_{\max}.

Thus,

\left\|H_{\pi,\gamma}\right\|_{\infty}\leq\frac{4DR_{\max}}{1-\gamma\beta}.

(40)

We now bound the advantage function. From the Bellman equations
Q^{\pi}(s,a)=R(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}\mathbb{P}\left(s^{\prime}\,|\,s,a\right)V^{\pi}\left(s^{\prime}\right)
and
V^{\pi}(s)=r_{\pi}(s)+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}P^{\pi}\left(s,s^{\prime}\right)V^{\pi}\left(s^{\prime}\right)
, we obtain

\displaystyle A^{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle=R(s,a)-r_{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(s^{\prime}\,|\,s,a\right)-P^{\pi}\left(s,s^{\prime}\right)\right)V^{\pi}\left(s^{\prime}\right).

Substituting (
39
), the constant component
\eta_{\pi}/(1-\gamma)
vanishes because
\sum_{s^{\prime}}\bigl(\mathbb{P}(s^{\prime}\,|\,s,a)-P^{\pi}(s,s^{\prime})\bigr)=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle A^{\pi}(s,a)

\displaystyle=R(s,a)-r_{\pi}(s)

\displaystyle+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left(\mathbb{P}\left(s^{\prime}\,|\,s,a\right)-P^{\pi}\left(s,s^{\prime}\right)\right)H_{\pi,\gamma}\left(s^{\prime}\right),

and using (
40
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left|A^{\pi}(s,a)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|R(s,a)-r_{\pi}(s)\right|

(41)

\displaystyle+\gamma\sum_{s^{\prime}}\left|\mathbb{P}\left(s^{\prime}\,|\,s,a\right)-P^{\pi}\left(s,s^{\prime}\right)\right|\left|H_{\pi,\gamma}\left(s^{\prime}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 2R_{\max}+2\gamma\left\|H_{\pi,\gamma}\right\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2R_{\max}+\frac{8\gamma DR_{\max}}{1-\gamma\beta}.

Combining (
38
), (
41
), and
\|\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)\|_{2}\leq G
, we have

\displaystyle\|g^{\pi}(s)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}\|\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)\|_{2}\cdot\left|A^{\pi}(s,a)\right|

\displaystyle\leq G|\mathcal{A}|\left(2R_{\max}+\frac{8\gamma DR_{\max}}{1-\gamma\beta}\right).

Finally,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J-\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{s}\left(d_{\pi,\gamma}(s)-d_{\pi}(s)\right)\sum_{a}\nabla_{\theta}\pi(a\,|\,s)Q^{\pi}(s,a)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{s}\left(d_{\pi,\gamma}(s)-d_{\pi}(s)\right)g^{\pi}(s)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}}\left|d_{\pi,\gamma}(s)-d_{\pi}(s)\right|\cdot\|g^{\pi}(s)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq G|\mathcal{A}|\left(2R_{\max}+\frac{8\gamma DR_{\max}}{1-\gamma\beta}\right)\|d_{\pi,\gamma}-d_{\pi}\|_{1}.

Invoking
Theorem

4
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \left\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J-\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J\right\|\\
\leq G|\mathcal{A}|\left(2R_{\max}+\frac{8\gamma DR_{\max}}{1-\gamma\beta}\right)\frac{2D}{1-\beta\gamma}(1-\gamma)\triangleq\varepsilon_{\mathrm{c}}(\gamma).\end{aligned}

∎

Appendix E
Proof of

Section

3.4

Proof.

We give a unified proof for episodic and continuing MDPs. Define
\Delta_{t}:=\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})-\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})
. Then the biased policy-gradient update in (
6
) can be written as
\theta_{t+1}=\theta_{t}+\eta_{t}\bigl(\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})+\Delta_{t}\bigr)
. By
Theorems

5
and
6
, we have
\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\gamma}
for all
t
, where
\varepsilon_{\gamma}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}(\gamma)
in episodic MDPs and
\varepsilon_{\gamma}=\varepsilon_{\mathrm{c}}(\gamma)
in continuing MDPs.

Recall from (
4
) and (
3
) that

\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})=\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t}).

(42)

Moreover, the time-zero contribution to the
discounted occupancy measure gives

\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}=\sum_{s\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}}\mu_{\pi_{\theta_{t}},\gamma}(s)\geq\sum_{s\in\tilde{\mathcal{S}}}d_{0}(s)=1.

By
Assumption

5
, for any vector
u
,

J(\theta_{t}+u)\geq J(\theta_{t})+\langle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{t}),u\rangle-\frac{L}{2}\|u\|_{2}^{2}
.
Taking
u=\eta_{t}\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})
and using (
42
), we obtain

\displaystyle J(\theta_{t+1})-J(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\eta_{t}\langle\nabla_{\theta}J(\theta_{t}),\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})\rangle-\frac{L\eta_{t}^{2}}{2}\|\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\eta_{t}\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}\langle\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t}),\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})\rangle-\frac{L\eta_{t}^{2}}{2}\|\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
\Delta_{t}=\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})-\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})
,

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 2\langle\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t}),\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})\rangle&=\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\|\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}.\end{aligned}

Substituting this identity gives

\displaystyle J(\theta_{t+1})-J(\theta_{t})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\eta_{t}\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}}{2}\bigl(\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr)

\displaystyle+\frac{\eta_{t}}{2}\bigl(\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}-L\eta_{t}\bigr)\|\hat{\nabla}^{\textup{e}}_{\theta}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}.

Since
\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}\geq 1
and

0<\eta_{t}\leq 1/L
,
\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}-L\eta_{t}\geq 0
.
Dropping the resulting nonnegative term and using
\|\Delta_{t}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\gamma}
, we obtain

\displaystyle J(\theta_{t+1})-J(\theta_{t})\geq\frac{\eta_{t}\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}}{2}\bigl(\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\varepsilon_{\gamma}^{2}\bigr).

(43)

Summing (
43
) from
t=N
to
T
gives

J(\theta_{T+1})-J(\theta_{N})\\
\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=N}^{T}\eta_{t}\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}-\frac{\varepsilon_{\gamma}^{2}}{2}\sum_{t=N}^{T}\eta_{t}\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}.

Since
J(\theta_{T+1})\leq J^{*}
, it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{t=N}^{T}\eta_{t}\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{t=N}^{T}\eta_{t}\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}}\leq\frac{2\bigl(J^{*}-J(\theta_{N})\bigr)}{\sum_{t=N}^{T}\eta_{t}\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}}+\varepsilon_{\gamma}^{2}.

Because all weights
\eta_{t}\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}
are positive,

\displaystyle\min_{N\leq t\leq T}\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{2\bigl(J^{*}-J(\theta_{N})\bigr)}{\sum_{t=N}^{T}\eta_{t}\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}}+\varepsilon_{\gamma}^{2}.

Moreover, by

\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}\geq 1
and

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\eta_{t}=\infty
,
\sum_{t=N}^{T}\eta_{t}\kappa_{\pi_{\theta_{t}}}\geq\sum_{t=N}^{T}\eta_{t}\longrightarrow\infty

as
T\to\infty
for every fixed
N
.
Since bounded rewards imply

J^{*}-J(\theta_{N})<\infty
, letting
T\to\infty
yields

\inf_{t\geq N}\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\gamma}^{2}
.
Finally, letting
N\to\infty
and using
\liminf_{t\to\infty}x_{t}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\inf_{t\geq N}x_{t}
, we conclude that
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\gamma}^{2}
. By
Theorems

5
and
6
,

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{e}}(\gamma)=O(1-\gamma)
and

\varepsilon_{\mathrm{c}}(\gamma)=O(1-\gamma)
. Therefore, in both episodic and continuing MDPs,
\liminf_{t\to\infty}\|\nabla_{\theta}^{\textup{e}}J(\theta_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq O\bigl((1-\gamma)^{2}\bigr)
.
