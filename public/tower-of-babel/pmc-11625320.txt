Advancing inclusive and democratic medical pluralism in Nepal
Authors: Bamdev Subedi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Medical pluralism is a global norm rather than an exception. However, the kind of medical pluralism that exists in many settings is exclusionary and undemocratic. In many nations, medical pluralism has official acceptance, allowing both biomedicine and traditional systems of medicine a legitimate space into the formal healthcare system. However, traditional systems of medicine fall far behind biomedicine in terms of structural superiority and institutional strengths. Moreover, various forms of traditional medicine, particularly of popular variants, remain excluded from formal healthcare system, and a variety of traditional healers lack official legitimacy. Though conceptually medical pluralism sounds more or less equal standing of co-existing systems of medicine, the reality is that biomedicine enjoys a dominant status over heterodox medical systems. Upon examining the amount of budgetary allocation, number of health facilities, size of health human resources, educational institutions and research output, this paper reveals an overemphasis on biomedicine, overshadowing both scholarly and popular traditional medicine. This arrangement underscores the undemocratic and exclusionary nature of medical pluralism in the country. In light of the published data sources this paper examines the structure of medical pluralism and proposes measures that can contribute to advancing inclusive and democratic medical pluralism in Nepal.

Introduction

Medical pluralism is the rule around the world, not the exception [1]. Plurality in medicine and medicinal practices has been acknowledged as a global phenomenon. The concept of medical pluralism basically indicates the existence and use of different forms of medicine [2,3]. Defined as the “co-existence of diverse medical traditions in a single setting” [4], medical pluralism enables patients to choose from different systems of medicine based on their specific needs, cultural backgrounds, and the nature of their health conditions [5]. Most commonly, medical pluralism entails the use of biomedicine and traditional medicine [6].

The scholarships on medical pluralism in the early 1970s created a background for global attention toward the traditional systems of medicine. By the time of Alma-Ata Conference on Primary Health Care in 1978 the role of traditional medicine in health and wellbeing got global appreciation. Nation states were encouraged to give traditional medicine a legitimate space in their national health care systems by creating a favorable policy environment for the growth and development of traditional medicine and what is now known as complementary and alternative medicine (CAM) in many developed countries. Medical pluralism got an official acceptance when traditional systems of medicine were integrated into the national health care system [7]. The role of global health leaders and global organization such as WHO contributed to the wider acceptance of medical pluralism in many parts of the world.

Traditional systems of medicine have a history of suppression, marginalization and exclusion [[8], [9], [10]]. They have transitioned from de facto to de jure, or from a de-legitimized to a re-legitimized states, from pre-colonial to postcolonial times [11]. In recent decades, there has been a resurgence of interest in traditional, complementary and alternative medicine (TCAM) [12]. The world today sees an increasing popularity of TCAM as the demand for TCAM services and products are on the rise [[13], [14], [15], [16]]. WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy 2014–2023 states, “More countries have gradually come to accept the contribution of TCAM to health and well-being of the people and to the comprehensiveness of their health care system” [16]. People have been using TCAM increasingly across the world and the patients with chronic conditions often take recourse to TCAM practitioners [16].

The growing use of TCAM can be taken as an evidence that no single medical system can claim solutions to every form of physical and mental suffering [17]. One of the reasons for the greater acceptance of medical pluralism is the growing recognition of the limitations of biomedicine [18,19]. People use traditional medicine for various reasons, not only because of proven efficacy in some of the disease conditions but also because of cultural beliefs, accessibility, perceived effectiveness, and personal preferences. Many people turn to traditional medicine to address the root cause of their health problems (healing) rather than just to treat the symptoms (curing). They prefer traditional medicine not only because of the cost but also because of its cultural closeness and holistic nature [20]. An increasing number of people, tired of biomedical products and services, have started looking back to traditional medicine, as a source of natural health, drugless treatment, and herbal products and services [21].

Today, even the popular traditions including “ethnic community and ecosystem specific traditions”, are getting attention and the concept of “fourth tier”, which refers to the “non-institutional, community and household-based tier of health care services” are being valued [22,23]. However, the phrases such as ‘revitalization of local health tradition’, ‘revival of folk or indigenous medicines’, ‘integration of traditional healers’ allude that these traditions are withered or experienced in the immediate past a great loss and have not recovered from the damage that had been caused to them, and traditional healers remain unintegrated or excluded. According to WHO Global Report on Traditional and Complementary Medicine, only 36 member states out of 194 reportedly regulated the indigenous traditional medicine providers. The terms “Indigenous traditional medicine providers” has been used to “include those who practice indigenous traditional medicine, such as traditional healers, bone setters, herbalists, traditional birth attendants, etc.” [24].

A large body of literature on medical pluralism recognize it as normal, natural and ubiquitous phenomena. However, the kind of medical pluralism that exists in many settings is undemocratic and exclusionary [[25], [26], [27]]. Though conceptually, medical pluralism sounds “more or less on an equal footing” of the co-existing systems of medicine, biomedicine dominates other forms of medicine [28]. Charles Leslie, who is known for the conceptual development of medical pluralism, asserts that biomedicine progressively subordinates other forms of medicine [29]. There appears a hierarchical nature of medical pluralism, with biomedicine occupying the top position, scholarly traditional and complementary medicine in the middle and popular traditions at the bottom [[30], [31], [32]].

Although medical pluralism is officially recognized in Nepal, where scholarly traditional medicine along with biomedicine secure a legitimate space in the formal healthcare system, a variety of local, folk or indigenous traditions lack such a space [33]. This paper examines the structure of the medical pluralism in Nepal and suggests some measures to make medical pluralism more democratic and inclusive. Despite its primary focus on Nepal, this paper can offer valuable insights into the hierarchical structure of medical pluralism in several contexts.

Classification of medical systems

Based on geographical and cultural settings, Dunn [34] classifies medical systems into three groups: cosmopolitan, regional, and local medical systems. The cosmopolitan medical system refers to biomedicine. Regional medical systems include scholarly traditional systems of medicine which are distributed over a relatively large area, such as Ayurveda, Unani and traditional Chinese medicine. Local medical systems include the popular traditions of small-scale societies. Both regional and local medical systems are indigenous traditions, but the former represents scholarly or textual traditions, and the latter represents oral and non-scholarly traditions. Diverse forms of medicine co-exist in Nepal and, following Dunn, can be divided into the following three groups.iBiomedicine which is invariably known as allopathy, modern and scientific medicine.iiRegional medical systems include Ayurveda, Homeopathy, Unani, Yoga and Naturopathy, and Sowa-Rigpa medicine. These are legitimized and institutionalized systems. Other traditional and complementary medicines, such as traditional Chinese medicine and acupuncture, can be included in this group.iiiLocal medical systems include diverse forms of oral, non-scholarly and popular traditions. Developed and used by local and ethnic communities over time, non-scholarly and popular traditions include traditional healing, herbal healing, bone setting, spiritual healing, and midwifery and massage practices. These forms of medicine lack official legitimacy, and the practitioners or traditional healers practice informally at the household or community level. These forms of medicine are culturally acceptable, physically accessible and economically affordable, and provide a sort of health security to the poor section

A big tree and small herbs

I would prefer to use the analogy of a big tree and small herbs to describe the hierarchical structure of Nepali medical pluralism. If we look into the amount of budgetary allocation, number of health facilities, size of health human resources, education and research, biomedicine looks like a giant tree under which scholarly and popular traditional medicine look like shrubs and herbs (See Fig. 1). We can imagine a picture of a large tree (biomedicine) under which some shrubs and some more herbs are struggling to grow, often deprived of sunlight. Budgetary allocations

The government of Nepal allocates around five percent of its budget to the health sector, but less than five percent of the health budget goes to the traditional medicine sector [35]. For example, in 2017/18 Rs. 32,954,405,000 was allocated to the Ministry of Health and Population, of which Rs. 643,173,000 (1.95%) was allocated to the Department of Ayurveda and Alternative Medicine (DOAAM) [36], the department responsible for the management of traditional and alternative medicine (which include Yoga, Naturopathy, Sowa Rigpa, Homeopathy, Unani, Traditional Chinese Medicine, and other traditional medicine and healing practices). Among the traditional systems of medicine, Ayurveda looks like a big tree under which other traditional and alternative medicines appear as shrubs and herbs. Sometimes, it seems that Ayurveda is the only traditional medicine practiced in Nepal. Other traditional systems of medicine do not receive even five percent of the budget that the DOAAM receives. A nominal amount is allocated to the popular traditional medicine sector and traditional healers’ training and research activities.

Health facilities

A total of 4184 public health facilities (201 hospitals, 189 primary health care centers, and 3794 health posts) deliver biomedicine-based health care services, whereas 382 Ayurveda facilities (two hospitals, 14 Zonal Ayurveda Dispensaries, 61 District Ayurveda Health Centers, and 305 Ayurveda Dispensaries) deliver Ayurveda services [37]. Only one public hospital provides Homeopathy, and one dispensary provides Unani services. Even in the private sector, biomedicine has by far the highest number of hospitals, medical schools, nursing homes, clinics, and pharmacies. Comparing all of the traditional systems of medicine that are being practiced, Ayurveda has by far the highest number of facilities.

Human resources

There are a total of 267891 registered human resources for health in Nepal, of which only 5544 are registered traditional medicine practitioners (5071 Ayurveda, 71 Naturopathy and Yoga, 174 Acupuncture, and 228 Homeopathy and Unani) [37]. Practitioners of Sowa-Rigpa (Aamchi) have not yet registered. This shows that traditional medicine practitioners constitute only around two percent of the total human resources. The traditional healers may outnumber the registered traditional medicine practitioners, but to date, only 19 traditional healers are registered with the Nepal Ayurveda Medical Council (See https://namc.org.np/). The National Strategy for Human Resources for Health 2021–2030, however, does not recognize traditional healers as a category of human resource for health.

The total sanctioned health human resources under the Ministry of Health and Population is 31592 [22], of which only around 1500 (or less than five percent or the total health work force) are with DOAAM. The number of government employees for Homeopathy and Unani is far less, not more than two percent of the total staff of DOAAM.

Educational institutions

There are a total of 22 medical colleges that offer graduate courses with a total intake of around 2000 (MBBS) seats, whereas only three Ayurveda medical colleges offer graduate courses with an intake of around 120 (BAMS) seats. Only one college affiliated with Lumbini Buddhist University has started to offer an academic course on Bachelor of Sowa-Rigpa Medicine, and Kathmandu University has started to offer Bachelor in Yogic Sciences and Wellbeing with limited seats, but none exists for Naturopathy, Homeopathy and Unani medicine. Similar is the situation with the number of colleges and enrollment capacity for undergraduate courses. According to the annual report 2022/23 of CTEVT (Council for Technical Education and Vocational Training), of the 168 health institutions that offer certificate courses on health sciences (general medicine, nursing, lab technology, physiotherapy and Ayurveda) only five of them are related to Ayurveda. Similarly, the total enrolment capacity in certificate or diploma programs in health sciences was 18,172, whereas the total intake capacity for traditional systems of medicine was just 640 (Ayurveda 520 seats, Homeopathy 40, Yoga and Naturopathy 40, and Acupuncture, Acupressure and Moxibustion 40 seats). This is a 3-year course to produce middle level health service providers and the graduates are licensed from the respective professional councils to work as auxiliary health workers in respective hospitals, dispensaries and clinical settings.

Research output

Research is important to advance traditional medicine by improving safety, efficacy and quality. However, traditional systems of medicine fall far behind biomedicine in terms of research. Though the National Ayurveda Research and Training Center has been established to advance research and training activities on Ayurveda and other alternative systems, it is still in its early stage. A stark disparity can be observed when comparing the number of journals, research articles published, and sources of funding for research between biomedicine and traditional medicine. For instance, as of December 2023, the NepMed database, which compiles bibliographic information from academic biomedical journals and allied health sciences, includes 46 journals. However, a closer examination of these journals and research articles reveals a noticeable scarcity of content related to traditional systems of medicine.

Undemocratic and exclusionary medical pluralism

The above indicators show that the kind of medical pluralism that exists in Nepal is undemocratic and exclusionary. It is undemocratic because only one medical system (i.e. biomedicine) controls resources and exerts dominance over other traditional systems of medicine, and traditional systems of medicine have very limited roles to play in the formal structure of the healthcare system. It is exclusionary because local, folk and “indigenous traditional medicines” are not included into the fold of formal health system, and traditional healers remain outside of statutory regulations. What is notable is that poor sections have poor access to the state favored medicine while the traditional medicine, specially of popular variants, to which they have access, have no access to the state resources [38]. When only one system receives state favor and the lion's share of the health budget, while others, often referred to as national medical systems in policy documents, receive nominal state resources, yet some others on which poor sections from rural areas heavily rely, are not counted as forms of medicine, the situation can be described as undemocratic, exclusionary, unjust medical pluralism.

Medical pluralism is criticized because it tends to obscure hierarchies and perpetuate health disparities, thereby justifying unequal access to healthcare. It is crucial to ensure that people have access to safe, effective, and high-quality healthcare options. The utilization of traditional medicine, particularly popular variants, is associated with the exploitation of vulnerable populations, compelling them to rely on medical practices that lack sound scientific evidence. Those who rely on traditional healers may have limited access to official health care, either because healthcare services are unaffordable or because health facilities are not available in the areas where they live, or what is available to them as official healthcare services may be of poor quality. Despite the historical use and potential benefits of popular traditional medicine, ensuring its safety, efficacy, and quality is paramount. Recognition of traditional medicine, particularly the popular one, establishing regulatory mechanisms, and allocating resources are essential steps toward achieving inclusive and democratic medical pluralism.

Practitioners of scholarly traditional medicine are officially registered with either the Nepal Ayurveda Medical Council or the Nepal Health Professional Council. However, traditional healers typically practice informally and are not registered with any government or professional bodies. The Department of Ayurveda and Alternative Medicine (DOAAM) has recently drafted a Registration Standard for Traditional Healers as per Article 22(3) of the Public Health Service Act 2018. This standard makes it mandatory to be registered to practice traditional healing. According to the Draft Standard, traditional healers must be registered with local governments to provide traditional treatment services to the communities.

The Draft Standard defines traditional healers as paramparaagat upachaarak for the individuals engaged in treating specific diseases using certain herbs or sources, having gained healing knowledge through a minimum of 15 years of learning from ancestors or gurus, and who have adopted traditional healing as their main occupation [39]. The standard attempts to bring traditional healers into a regulatory framework by defining their role and legitimizing their practices. Though the registration standard was drafted three years ago, none of the 753 local level governments have yet initiated the registration process.

A large section of the rural population in Nepal depends on traditional healers for their primary healthcare needs. For rural populations in remote areas, what is available and accessible as traditional medicine is the healing services provided by these healers. Traditional healers' services are readily available at the community level, and frequently offered either for free or at a minimal cost [33].

Moving toward inclusive and democratic medical pluralism

The following measures are important to move toward inclusive and democratic medical pluralism.

Addressing the historical injustice

The potential value and historical and cultural importance of traditional medicine must be recognized. Great damage has been caused to traditional systems of medicine due to the state's skepticism and unfair and unjust treatment [40]. This injustice and damage must be corrected by increased funding for the traditional systems of medicine and expanding traditional medicine services across the country. In the public sector, there are only three Ayurveda hospitals outside Kathmandu Valley, and none exist for Homeopathy, Naturopathy and Unani. The number of traditional medicine facilities has not increased significantly during the entire planned development period. For example, before the beginning of the first five-year plan in 1956, there were 343 Ayurveda facilities, and now, even after the end of the 14th plan in 2019/20, there are just 382 Ayurveda facilities [41]. Similar is the situation with Homeopathy, Unani and Nauropathy. The state policy, as stipulated in The Constitution of Nepal is “to protect and promote Ayurveda, natural therapy, and homeopathy system” [42]. However, only one public hospital and one dispensary are available for Homeopathy and Unani medicine, and no public facilities are available for Naturopathy medicine. Traditional medicine facilities must be expanded in a way that allows people to access them easily.

Legitimizing traditional healers

The kind of traditional medicine service to which rural population have access is not what the institutionally trained Ayurveda, Homeopathy and Unani practitioners provide but the healing services which traditional healers, herbalists, bone setters, traditional midwives provide. People in rural and hilly areas of Nepal are more likely to contact these healers first than other healthcare providers [43]. These traditional healers are often described as the “first point of contact”, the “primary source of care”, and sometimes the “only source of care” for the people from rural and hilly regions. Even today, less than one-third of scholarly traditional medicine practitioners serve in rural areas where more than two-third of the population resides. Despite their robust presence in rural areas, traditional healers lack official legitimacy and serve as informal providers. Traditional healers should be encouraged to get registered with the local governments as per the registration standard drafted by the DOAAM. They should be brought under the regulatory framework by establishing standards for registration, certifying their practices, delimiting their role, and building mechanisms for training and capacity-building activities. Another point of concern is that the indigenous health knowledge is eroding, experienced healers' number is dwindling and young generation is demotivated to learn and practice traditional healings [44]. Initiatives are needed to document the traditional healing practices, to encourage the traditional healers to share their knowledge and to collect and publish healers’ manuscripts.

Respecting people's right to choose

People should have the right to choose the healthcare options that best meet their needs, and this choice should be respected and supported by the national healthcare system. People use diverse forms of medicine for their health problems. Even for a single episode of an illness, they may use different medicines and therapies. For example, a diabetic patient may use herbal remedies or Ayurveda medicine in conjunction with biomedicine. Though medical pluralism offers more choice and option for everyone, it does not serve all equally; some benefit but others do not. Many people find it difficult to choose the right care for their problems and delay in getting appropriate care, which might result in treatment failure, complications, increased cost, and suffering. People should be provided with the information they need to decide about the different options available to them. Pluralism has not only advantages but also disadvantages and the challenge is to increase the advantages of pluralism while correcting its disadvantages.

Allocating resources and ensuring quality

The type of medicine people use, whether it be biomedicine or traditional systems of medicine, must be safe, effective, and of sufficient quality. The concern regarding quality is specifically directed at traditional medicine, especially to its popular variants. It is ridiculous to water one plant and complain about the undergrowth of another. Traditional medicine, both scholarly and popular, need increased funding for quality-enhancing activities such as training, education, research and validation. We need to make significant investments if we envisage traditional systems of medicine moving forward and playing an important role in the healthcare delivery system.

Promoting open dialogue, mutual trust and collaboration

Undeniable is the fact that there is an unequal distribution of medical power among practitioners from different streams of medical knowledge. Practitioners vary in their training and expertise across different medical fields. Despite this diversity, a hierarchical structure exists, with biomedical practitioners holding the highest position, scholarly traditional medicine practitioners in the middle, and traditional healers at the lowest tier. However, in the eyes of ordinary people, all practitioners and their services are equally important. From the patient's perspective, they all are healers, regardless of the stream in which they are trained and specialized. When the common goal of all practitioners is to help patients, there is no point in pitting one against another. It is important to promote open and respectful dialogue, nurture mutual trust, and encourage collaboration between practitioners of different medical traditions. By establishing avenues for dialogue and communication, nurturing mutual understanding and respect, sharing knowledge and expertise, and working together, it may be possible to find common ground and improve the overall quality of patient care.

Conclusion

Medical pluralism has gained official recognition in Nepal where both biomedicine and traditional systems of medicine have space in the formal healthcare system. However, the form of medical pluralism that exists in Nepal is unjust, exclusionary, and undemocratic. Though official documents are full of commitments to develop and expand traditional systems of medicine [45,46], a harsh reality is that these systems fall far behind biomedicine in terms of budgetary allocation, health human resources, service delivery, education and research. Moreover, the value of popular traditions is often overlooked, and traditional healers face challenges in securing a legitimate space. The provision of an adequate budget for the traditional medicine sector, promotion of research, teaching and training activities, expansion of scholarly traditional medicine facilities across the country, recognition of popular traditions and integration of traditional healers into the formal healthcare system can contribute to Nepal's effort toward inclusive and democratic medical pluralism.
