Nonlinear Hall effect driven by spin-charge-coupled motive force
Kohei Hattori, Hikaru Watanabe, Ryotaro Arita

Abstract
Parity-time-reversal symmetric ( 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric) magnets have garnered much attention due to their spin-charge coupled dynamics enriched by the parity-symmetry breaking. By real-time simulations, we study how localized spin dynamics can affect the nonlinear Hall effect in 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} -symmetric magnets. To identify the leading-order term, we derive analytical expressions for the second-order optical response and classify the contributions by considering their transformation properties under 𝒫 ​ 𝒯 \mathcal{PT} symmetry. Notably, our results reveal that the sizable contribution is attributed to the mixed dipole effect, which is analogous to the Berry curvature dipole term.



I
introduction

The nonlinear Hall effect (NHE) is a nonlinear transverse current response induced by an electric field
[
1
]
.
Several mechanisms underlying the NHE have been proposed, exhibiting distinct behaviors depending on system parameters, particularly the relaxation time.
In time-reversal symmetric (
\mathcal{T}
-symmetric) systems, the Berry curvature dipole term is well known to play a crucial role, which scales linearly with the relaxation time
[
2
,
3
]
.
The Berry curvature dipole reflects the intrinsic properties of the electronic band structure.
In fact, the topology of the electronic structure of nonmagnetic systems has been explored by measuring the nonlinear Hall conductivity experimentally
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
.
In addition to the Berry curvature dipole term, impurity effects also play a significant role in the NHE
[
8
,
9
,
10
]
.
On the other hand, in
\mathcal{T}
-breaking systems, the NHE can arise from the Drude term, which scales quadratically with the relaxation time
[
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.
Another contribution is the positional shift effect, which is independent of the relaxation time and can dominate in dirty systems where the electron relaxation time is short
[
16
,
12
,
17
,
14
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
.
The NHE associated with this term has also been experimentally observed
[
21
,
22
]
.
In these studies, the mechanisms of the NHE are primarily limited to the effects of an electric field on the electronic system.

In recent years, the nonlinear response generated by the dynamics of order parameters has garnered significant attention.
The photocurrents arising from collective excitations have been theoretically investigated through perturbative analysis
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
.
Several studies have also examined the optical responses originating from the coupled dynamics of the charge and order parameter by using real-time simulations for excitonic insulators
[
27
,
28
]
and magnetic insulators
[
29
,
30
]
.
Moreover, photocurrent responses driven by order parameter dynamics have been experimentally observed in ferroelectric materials
[
31
]
, excitonic insulators
[
32
,
33
]
, and magnetic insulators
[
34
]
.
The dynamics of order parameters enriches the nonlinear optical response, offering new insights into the mechanism underlying the NHE.

In this paper, we study the effect of localized spin dynamics on the NHE in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric collinear antiferromagnetic metals. This phenomenon is particularly intriguing due to recent developments in antiferromagnetic spintronics
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
. In parity-breaking antiferromagnets, the Néel vector can be manipulated by an electric field or current via the Edelstein effect
[
38
,
39
,
40
,
41
]
.
Experimentally, electrical control of antiferromagnetic domains has been demonstrated in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric collinear antiferromagnetic metals such as CuMnAs
[
42
,
43
]
and
\text{Mn}_{2}\text{Au}

[
44
]
.
Since the Edelstein effect can arise from the Fermi surface effect, spin-charge coupled dynamics can be induced by an external electric field in these magnets, particularly at low frequencies.
Given these considerations, collinear antiferromagnetic metals represent a promising platform for uncovering new mechanisms underlying the NHE driven by localized spin dynamics.

To clarify the role of the Edelstein effect in the NHE, we adopt a simple model representing a two-sublattice collinear antiferromagnet.
We explore the impact of the localized spin dynamics on the NHE by calculating the time evolution of the charge and spin degrees of freedom simultaneously.
We employ the real-time simulation scheme for spin-charge coupled systems developed in the previous works
[
45
,
46
,
29
,
30
]
.
This approach allows us to examine the effects of localized spin dynamics on current responses, providing a clear understanding of the NHE from the perspective of spin dynamics.

Using symmetry analysis, we identify an optically active mode in the localized spin system and investigate its role in both the linear response function and the NHE. In the NHE spectrum, we observe an enhancement of the peak in the low-frequency regime and the emergence of a new resonant peak associated with collective spin excitations.
To further elucidate the mechanism of the NHE, we analytically decompose the NHE into several components.
Through symmetry analysis and this decomposition, we identify a significant contribution, which we call the mixed dipole term analogous to the Berry curvature dipole term.

The Berry curvature dipole term is described as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{BCD;L}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}=\frac{1/\tau}{\omega^{2}+1/\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}(\partial_{\lambda}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\nu}]+\partial_{\nu}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}])f_{a},

(1)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{BCD;C}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}=\frac{i\omega}{\omega^{2}+1/\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle\times\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}(\partial_{\lambda}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\nu}]-\partial_{\nu}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}])f_{a},

(2)

where
\omega
denotes the frequency of the external light field,
\xi_{ab}
is the Berry connection,
\partial_{\nu}
represents the partial derivative with respect to the
\nu
-axis in
\bm{k}
-space, and

denotes the Fermi-Dirac distribution for the eigenenergy
\epsilon_{\bm{k}a}

\bm{k}

Here,
\sigma_{\mathrm{BCD;L}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
(
\sigma_{\mathrm{BCD;C}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

This term is characterized by the Berry curvature dipole expressed as

\displaystyle D_{\text{BCD}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}=\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a}\partial_{\lambda}\Omega_{a}^{\mu\nu}f_{a},

(3)

where
\Omega_{a}^{\mu\nu}=-2\sum_{b\neq a}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\nu}]
is the Berry curvature for the band

The Berry curvature dipole represents the dipole moment of the Berry curvature in momentum space and determines the amplitude of the NHE.

On the other hand, the mixed dipole term is expressed as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{MD}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}=\frac{J(1/\tau+i\omega)}{\omega^{2}+1/\tau^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\partial_{\lambda}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}B_{ba}^{\nu}]f_{a}.

(4)

Here,
J
represents the exchange coupling between the spin moment of the itinerant electrons and the localized spin moment, and
B_{ab}
is the spin operator.
This term is obtained by replacing the Berry connection
\xi_{ba}^{\nu}
with the spin operator
B_{ba}^{\nu}
in the Berry curvature dipole term.
This term is proportional to the dipole moment of the imaginary component of the product of the Berry connection and the spin operator over the occupied states as

\displaystyle D^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{\text{M}}=\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\partial_{\lambda}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}B_{ba}^{\nu}]f_{a}.

(5)

We define this physical quantity as the mixed dipole.
The mechanism of the NHE driven by the mixed dipole closely resembles that of the NHE induced by the Berry curvature dipole.
A schematic illustration of the NHE originating from the mixed dipole is presented in FIG.
1
.
The electron trajectory is bent by the localized spin dynamics through the mixed dipole.
This mixed dipole mechanism is important in the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric AFM metals, where the localized spin dynamics can be induced by an electric field via the Edelstein effect.
These findings highlight the importance of localized spin dynamics in understanding and engineering nonlinear current responses in complex magnetic systems.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}=\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{ele}}+\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{exc}}+\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{spin}}+\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{E}.

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{ele}}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\bm{\hat{c}^{{\dagger}}}(\bm{k})\bm{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{ele}}(\bm{k})\bm{\hat{c}}(\bm{k}),

(8)

where

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle=-t_{2}(\mathrm{cos}k_{x}+\mathrm{cos}k_{y}),

(9)

\displaystyle=-2t_{1}\mathrm{cos}\frac{k_{x}}{2}\mathrm{cos}\frac{k_{y}}{2},

(10)

-X-M-Y-

The last term

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{E}=-\bm{E}(t)\sum_{\bm{k}\bm{k}^{\prime}}\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{\sigma}\quantity[i\pdv{\bm{k}}\delta(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime})]\hat{c}_{\bm{k}\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}\alpha\sigma}

II.2
Calculation scheme

Following Ref.
[
29
]
, here we introduce the calculation scheme of real-time simulation. To investigate the spin-charge coupled system, we solve the von Neumann equation and Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation simultaneously.
Firstly, the time evolution of the itinerant electrons can be described by the single-particle density matrix (SPDM)
\rho_{\alpha\beta}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{k})=\left\langle{\hat{c}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(\bm{k})\hat{c}_{\alpha\sigma}(\bm{k})}\right\rangle
.
SPDM satisfies the von Neumann equation
[
52
]
expressed as,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\partialderivative{\vb*{\rho}(\bm{k}, t)}{t}=-i\quantity[\vb*{H}(\bm{k},t),\, \vb*{\rho}(\bm{k}, t)]-\bm{E}(t)\cdot\frac{\partial{\bf\it\rho}(\bm{k},t)}{\partial\bm{k}}\\
-\frac{1}{\tau}({\bf\it\rho}(\bm{k},t)-{\bf\it\rho}_{\text{eq}}(\bm{k})).\end{split}

(15)

Secondly, the time evolution of the localized spin system is governed by the LLG equation described as

\displaystyle\dfrac{d\bm{\mathrm{S}}_{\alpha}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{1+\alpha_{G}^{2}}\left(\bm{\mathrm{h}}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eff}}\times\bm{\mathrm{S}}_{\alpha}+\alpha_{G}\bm{\mathrm{S}}_{\alpha}\times\left(\bm{\mathrm{S}}_{\alpha}\times\bm{\mathrm{h}}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eff}}\right)\right),

(16)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{h}}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{eff}}

\displaystyle=-J\langle\bm{\sigma}_{\alpha}\rangle+\dfrac{\delta\mathcal{H}_{\text{spin}}}{\delta\bm{\mathrm{S}}_{\alpha}}.

(17)

In Eq. (
15
)
,{\bf\it H}(\bm{k},t)
represents the time-dependent electronic Hamiltonian at each
\bm{k}
point defined as follows

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{ele}}+\hat{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathrm{exc}}=\sum_{\bm{k}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\quantity[\vb*{H}(\bm{k},t)]_{\alpha\beta}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}(\bm{k})\hat{c}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{k}).

(18)

The
k
-derivative of the delta function in Eq. (
14
) is handled as the
k
-derivative of the SPDM, making it computationally feasible.
In the LLG equation Eq. (
17
),
\left\langle{{\bf\it\sigma}_{\alpha}}\right\rangle
represents the sublattice-resolved spin density of itinerant electrons, which can be calculated from SPDM.
This method enables us to capture the dynamics of both itinerant electrons and localized spins.
We set the chemical potential
\mu=-0.6
, placing it below the bandgap, as indicated by the horizontal dashed line in FIG.
2
.
For the parameters
t_{1}=1.0
,
t_{2}=0.08
,
\lambda=0.8
,

, and
K_{x}=0.05
, the collinear antiferromagnetic order characterized by
\bm{\mathrm{S}}_{A}=(1,0,0)
and
\bm{\mathrm{S}}_{B}=(-1,0,0)
is stable.

In real materials, excited carriers undergo relaxation processes due to electron-electron correlations, electron-phonon interactions, and impurity scattering. To model a physically realistic response to light, we incorporate these effects phenomenologically. Specifically, we apply the relaxation time approximation in the von Neumann equation as
1/\tau({\bf\it\rho}(\bm{k},t)-{\bf\it\rho}_{\text{eq}}(\bm{k}))
in Eq. (
15
), and introduce the Gilbert damping term
\alpha_{G}
in Eq. (
16
). Here,
{\bf\it\rho}_{\text{eq}}(\bm{k})
denotes the SPDM at equilibrium for a temperature of
T=0
, as shown below.

The SPDM in equilibrium
{\bf\it\rho}_{\text{eq}}(\bm{k})
denotes the SPDM in the initial state at zero temperature (
T=0
). SPDM in the band basis
\tilde{{\bf\it{\rho}}}_{\text{eq}}(\bm{k})
is given by

\displaystyle\quantity[\tilde{\vb*{{\rho}}}_{\text{eq}}(\bm{k})]_{nn^{\prime}}=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\Theta(\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{k}n}),

(19)

where
\Theta(\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{k}n})
represents the occupation number, where
\epsilon_{\bm{k}n}
being the eigenvalue of the Hamiltonian
{\bf\it H}(\bm{k})
.
By using this expression, we can calculate the SPDM in the original basis
{\bf\it\rho}_{\text{eq}}(\bm{k})
as

\displaystyle{\bf\it\rho}_{\text{eq}}(\bm{k})={\bf\it U}(\bm{k})\tilde{{\bf\it\rho}}_{\text{eq}}(\bm{k}){\bf\it U}^{\dagger}(\bm{k})

(20)

with
{\bf\it U}
is a unitary matrix which diagonalizes the Hamiltonian as,

\displaystyle{\bf\it U}^{\dagger}(\bm{k}){\bf\it H}(\bm{k}){\bf\it U}(\bm{k})={\bf\it\mathcal{E}}(\bm{k}),

(21)

\displaystyle\quantity(\vb*{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{k}))_{nn^{\prime}}=\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\epsilon_{\bm{k}n}.

(22)

We can evaluate the current density from SPDM in each time step, as follows.
The current operator in the length gauge is described as

\displaystyle\bm{\hat{J}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{k}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\partialderivative{\qty[\vb*{H}(\bm{k},t)]_{\alpha\beta}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}}{\bm{k}}\hat{c}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}(\bm{k})\hat{c}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{k})

\displaystyle\equiv\sum_{\bm{k}}\sum_{\alpha\beta}\sum_{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\quantity[\vb*{J}(\bm{k})]_{\alpha\beta}^{\sigma\sigma^{\prime}}\hat{c}_{\alpha\sigma}^{\dagger}(\bm{k})\hat{c}_{\beta\sigma^{\prime}}(\bm{k}).

(24)

It is noted that since the

\bm{k}

We solve the coupled equations of motion Eq. (
15

16

In each time step, we evaluate the sublattice-resolved spin density
\langle\bm{\sigma}_{\alpha}\rangle

\displaystyle\bm{J}(t)=\sum_{\bm{k}}\operatorname{Tr}\quantity[\vb*{J}(\bm{k})\vb*{\rho}(\bm{k}, t)].

(25)

To obtain the

15

\displaystyle\partialderivative{\vb*{\rho}(\bm{k}, t)}{\bm{k}}=\dfrac{{\bf\it\rho}(\bm{k}+d\bm{k},t)-{\bf\it\rho}(\bm{k}-d\bm{k},t)}{2|d\bm{k}|},

where

In this subsection, we analyze the symmetry-adapted basis of the localized spins that is linearly coupled to the external light field.
Owing to the stringent constraints on the photocurrent response by the symmetry of the system and the external field, the symmetry analysis of localized spin dynamics plays a crucial role in this study.

The system has the following symmetries and belongs to the magnetic point group
G
, explicitly given by

\displaystyle\bm{G}=\bm{H}\oplus\mathcal{PT}\bm{H},

(27)

\displaystyle\bm{H}=\left\{\mathcal{I},\mathcal{G}_{x},\mathcal{G}_{z},\mathcal{S}_{y}\right\}.

(28)

\mathcal{PT}\bm{H}

IV.1
Analytical formula for photocurrent conductivity

We elucidate mechanisms for the photocurrent conductivity under the external electric field and localized spin dynamics.
Firstly, we focus on the photocurrent generated by the optical excitation, discussed in the previous studies
[
58
,
59
]
.
Photocurrent response arising from the external field can be induced by the Fermi surface effect and the interband effect.
The Fermi-surface effect is a photocurrent generation from the light-induced shift of the Fermi surface, and it can occur only in the metallic system.
On the other hand, the interband effect is governed by the electronic transitions between the conduction and valence bands.
The photocurrent response associated with the interband effect exhibits a resonant peak structure at the optical gap energy separating the conduction and valence bands.

In the previous paragraph, we discuss the photocurret response arising only from the electric field effect.
In this study, we discuss the dynamics of both the electronic system and the localized spin system in the spin-charge-coupled system by a real-time simulation, and therefore the calculated responses also include that coming from the spin-mediated force.
Thus, we consider the photocurrent generation from the external light field and the localized spin dynamics in the spin-charge-coupled system.
Localized spin dynamics can induce both Fermi-surface and interband mechanisms.
In particular, for the Fermi-surface contribution, the interference between the electric field and the localized spin dynamics can give rise to the mixed dipole effect.
The detailed derivation of the photocurrent conductivity is summarized in Appendix
A
and Appendix
B
.

It is important to note that the phase degrees of freedom of the driving fields impose constraints on the types of photocurrents that can be generated.
As a general case, let us consider the photocurrent induced by two external fields
X(\omega_{p})
and
Y(\omega_{p})

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{XY}(\omega_{p})&=\sigma_{XY}(0;-\omega_{p},\omega_{p})X(-\omega_{p})Y(\omega_{p})\\
&+\sigma_{XY}(0;\omega_{p},-\omega_{p})X(\omega_{p})Y(-\omega_{p}).\end{split}

(71)

Owing to the fact that the time-domain external field is real, the external field in the frequency domain satisfies the relation

\displaystyle X(\omega)=X^{\ast}(-\omega).

(72)

Therefore, the product of the two external fields in Eq. (
71

\displaystyle X(-\omega_{p})Y(\omega_{p})

\displaystyle=X^{\ast}(\omega_{p})Y(\omega_{p}),

\displaystyle=R^{XY}(\omega_{p})+iI^{XY}(\omega_{p}).

(73)

Here, we decomposed the product of the external field into real and imaginary parts defined by

\displaystyle R^{XY}(\omega_{p})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Re}\{[X(\omega_{p})]^{\ast}Y(\omega_{p})\},

(74)

\displaystyle I^{XY}(\omega_{p})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Im}\{[X(\omega_{p})]^{\ast}Y(\omega_{p})\},

(75)

which are related to the Stokes parameters
[

]
in the case of

\displaystyle\begin{split}J_{XY}(\omega_{p})=2\operatorname{Re}[\sigma_{XY}(0;-\omega_{p},\omega_{p})]R^{XY}(\omega_{p})\\
-2\operatorname{Im}[\sigma_{XY}(0;-\omega_{p},\omega_{p})]I^{XY}(\omega_{p}).\end{split}

(76)

\sigma_{\text{SS;IFS}}
when combined with a linear electromagnetic susceptibility proportional to
\tau^{1}
.
Finally, we note that the leading term from
\sigma_{\text{SS}}
includes a factor of
1/(\omega-\epsilon_{g})
, which produces a resonant structure near the optical gap
\epsilon_{g}
. In systems with a large optical gap, these terms can be significantly suppressed by this resonant factor at the low-frequency regime.

.
This inset demonstrates that
\sigma_{\text{col-}E}^{\text{inter}}

Next, we discuss the NHE arising from
\sigma_{\text{col-}E}^{\text{inter}}
, which contains the mixed dipole term. The
\tau
-dependence of
\sigma_{\text{col-}E}^{\text{inter}}
is shown in FIG.

, where it is evident that
\sigma_{\text{col-}E}^{\text{inter}}

is proportional to
\tau^{2}
.
As detailed in Appendix
B
,
\sigma_{\text{col-}E}^{\text{inter}}
contains a mixed dipole term
\sigma_{\text{MD}}
expressed as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{MD,C}}^{y;\nu x}

\displaystyle=\frac{Ji\omega}{\omega^{2}+(1/\tau)^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\partial_{x}\operatorname{Im}[\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{y}\mathcal{S}_{ba}^{\nu}]f_{a},

(80)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{MD,L}}^{y;\nu x}

\displaystyle=\frac{J/\tau}{\omega^{2}+(1/\tau)^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\partial_{x}\operatorname{Im}[\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{y}\mathcal{S}_{ba}^{\nu}]f_{a}.

(81)

Here, the
\bm{\mathcal{A}}_{ab}
is the U(2) gauge Berry connection and the
\bm{\mathcal{S}}_{ab}
is the interband spin operator in the U(2) gauge.
Considering the double degeneracy, we introduce the U(2) gauge description of the photocurrent response (see Appendix
B
).
This term originates from the second-order response perturbed by one photon and one localized spin dynamics,
\text{L}^{y}
, as summarized in TABLE
4
.
The former term,
\sigma_{\text{MD,C}}
, is induced by the out-of-phase component of
E^{x}
and
\text{L}^{y}
and vanishes at
\omega=0
.
In contrast, the latter term,
\sigma_{\text{MD,L}}
, arises from the in-phase component of
E^{x}
and
\text{L}^{y}
and shows
\tau^{1}
-dependence in the low-frequency regime.
As discussed earlier, the linear electromagnetic susceptibility of the collective spin mode,
\operatorname{Re}\chi_{\text{L}^{y}E^{x}}
, is proportional to
\tau
.
Consequently, the mixed dipole term
\sigma_{\text{MD,L}}
is proportional to
\tau^{2}
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{col-}E}^{\text{MD}}(0;\omega)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\text{MD;L}}(0;\omega)\operatorname{Re}\chi_{\text{L}^{y}E^{x}}(-\omega),

\displaystyle\propto\tau^{2}.

(82)

In comparison, other terms in
\sigma_{\text{S}E}
and
\sigma_{E\text{S}}
are at most proportional to
\tau^{0}
, leading to an NHE with a linear dependence on
\tau
in the highest order. These contributions are, therefore, negligible compared to the mixed dipole term.
Unlike the NHE contributions from
\sigma_{\text{SS}}
, the mixed dipole term
\sigma_{\text{MD;L}}
does not include a suppression factor that reduces its amplitude
\propto 1/(\omega-\epsilon_{g})
. As a result, even in systems with a large optical gap, the contribution from the mixed dipole term can be significant.

V
Summary

In this study, we investigated the effect of localized spin dynamics on NHE in a
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric collinear antiferromagnet by using real-time simulations. First, we identified the optically active mode of the localized spin system and identified the significant role of the Edelstein effect in the low-frequency regime. Our findings reveal that collective spin dynamics significantly enhance the NHE near
\omega=0
.
To clarify the role of localized spin dynamics in the NHE, we decomposed the NHE into three components and found that
\sigma_{\text{col-}E}
and
\sigma_{\text{col-col}}
contribute significantly.
Furthermore, to determine the dominant terms in the interference contributions
\sigma_{\text{col-}E}
, we decomposed the NHE into intraband and interband components.
Through symmetry analysis, we classified the photocurrent response originating from the interplay of the external light field and localized spin dynamics.
Our results show that the mixed dipole term is dominant for the interference contributions and is proportional to
\tau^{1}
.
This is consistent with the analytical formulas.
This term is further enhanced by the Edelstein effect, making its contribution to the NHE substantial as large as
\tau^{2}
in the clean limit.
These insights highlight the pivotal role of the mixed-dipole mechanism in the NHE of
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric antiferromagnetic metals, where the localized spin dynamics can be driven electrically via the Edelstein effect.
Experimental identification of the NHE induced by the Edelstein effect is an important challenge.
The NHE from localized spin dynamics could be significant in electrically switchable antiferromagnets such as CuMnAs and
\text{Mn}_{2}\text{Au}
.
Our findings reveal another avenue for understanding the mechanisms of the NHE and suggest a promising direction for future research in this area.

Appendix A
Nonlinear optical response functions based on density matrix formalism

In this section, we formulate the perturbative expansion of the von Neumann equation Eq. (
15
) incorporating both the light field and light-induced spin dynamics.
The electronic Hamiltonian is expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}(t)=\mathcal{H}_{0}+\Delta\mathcal{H}(t).

(83)

\Delta\mathcal{H}(t)
represents the perturbative Hamiltonian, comprising an external light field and the light-induced spin dynamics, defined as

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{H}(t)

\displaystyle=\Delta\mathcal{H}_{E}(t)+\Delta\mathcal{H}_{\text{spin}}(t),

(84)

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{H}_{E}

\displaystyle=-r^{\mu}E^{\mu},

(85)

\displaystyle\Delta\mathcal{H}_{\text{spin}}(t)

\displaystyle=-JB^{\mu}\Delta\mathrm{S}^{\mu}(t),

(86)

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbf{S}(t)

\displaystyle=\quantity(\Delta\mathrm{L}^{y}(t),\Delta\mathrm{M}^{z}(t))^{T},

(87)

\displaystyle{\bf\it B}

\displaystyle=\quantity(\sigma_{y}\tau_{z},\sigma_{z}\tau_{0})^{T}.

(88)

Here,
\sigma^{\mu}
and
\tau^{\mu}
denote the Pauli matrices representing the spin and sublattice degrees of freedom, respectively.

\mathcal{H}_{0}
is the nonperturbative Hamiltonian expressed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{0}=\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a}\epsilon_{{\bf\it k}a}c_{{\bf\it k}a}^{\dagger}c_{{\bf\it k}a}.

(89)

Here,
c_{{\bf\it k}a}^{\dagger}
(
c_{{\bf\it k}a}
) represents the creation (annihilation) operator for the Bloch state
\ket{\psi_{\vb*{k}a}}=\exp(i\vb*{k} \cdot\vb{r})\ket{u_{\vb*{k}a}}
, where
\ket{u_{\vb*{k}a}}
is the cell-periodic part of the Bloch state and satisfies the equation

\displaystyle H_{0}({\bf\it k})\ket{u_{\vb*{k}a}}=\epsilon_{{\bf\it k}a}\ket{u_{\vb*{k}a}}.

(90)

According to
[
70
]
, in the infinite volume limit, the position operator in Eq. (
85
) is expressed in the Bloch basis as

\displaystyle\quantity[\vb*{r_{k}}]_{ab}=i\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}\delta_{ab}+{\bf\it\xi}_{ab}.

(91)

Here,
{\bf\it\xi}_{ab}=i\matrixelement{u_{\vb*{k}a}}{\nabla_{\vb*{k}}}{u_{\vb*{k}b}}
is the Berry connection. In the following calculation,
k
-dependence is omitted unless explicitly mentioned.

As we explained in the main text, we can write the von Neumann equation of SPDM
\rho_{ab}=\langle c_{{\bf\it k}b}^{\dagger}c_{{\bf\it k}a}\rangle
as

\displaystyle i\partialderivative{\rho_{ab}(t)}{t}

\displaystyle=\quantity[\mathcal{H}, \rho]_{ab}=\quantity[\mathcal{H}_{0}, \rho]_{ab}+\quantity[\Delta\mathcal{H}(t), \rho]_{ab}.

(92)

We define the Fourier transformation as

\displaystyle f(t)=\int\dfrac{d\omega}{2\pi}f(\omega)e^{-i(\omega+i\eta)t},

(93)

and we obtain the frequency representation of the von Neumann equation,

\displaystyle(\omega+i\eta-\epsilon_{ab})\rho_{ab}(\omega)=\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}}{2\pi}\quantity[\Delta\mathcal{H}(\omega_{1}), \rho(\omega- \omega_{1})]_{ab}.

(94)

Here, the infinitesimal parameter
\eta
is introduced to describe the adiabatic application of the external field and
\epsilon_{ab}=\epsilon_{\bm{k}a}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}b}
.
We can perform a perturbative expansion of this equation by introducing
\rho^{(n)}
, which represents the
n
-th order term with respect to the external field.

\displaystyle(\omega+i\eta-\epsilon_{ab})\rho_{ab}^{(n+1)}(\omega)=\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}}{2\pi}\quantity[\Delta\mathcal{H}(\omega_{1}), \rho^{(n)}(\omega- \omega_{1})]_{ab}.

(95)

We introduce the matrix
d(\omega)
as

\displaystyle d_{ab}(\omega)=\dfrac{1}{\omega+i\eta-\epsilon_{ab}}=\mathcal{P}\dfrac{1}{\omega-\epsilon_{ab}}-i\pi\delta(\omega-\epsilon_{ab}),

(96)

and by using the Hadamard product
(A\odot B)_{ab}=A_{ab}B_{ab}

[
71
]
, we can rewrite the
von Neumann equation Eq. (
95

\displaystyle\rho_{ab}^{(n+1)}(\omega)=\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}}{2\pi}\quantity(d(\omega) \odot[\Delta\mathcal{H}(\omega_{1}), \rho^{(n)}(\omega- \omega_{1})])_{ab}.

(97)

In the following calculation, we solve this equation with the condition of
\rho_{ab}^{(0)}(\omega)=2\pi\delta(\omega)f_{ab}\delta_{ab}
.

Since we focus on the second-order current response to the perturbation, we solve the equation

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{ab}^{(2)}(\omega)&=\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}}{2\pi}\quantity(d(\omega) \odot[\Delta\mathcal{H}(\omega_{1}), \rho^{(1)}(\omega- \omega_{1})])_{ab}\\
&=\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\quantity(d(\omega) \odot[\Delta\mathcal{H}(\omega_{1}), d(\omega- \omega_{1})\odot[\Delta\mathcal{H}(\omega_{2}), \rho^{(0)}(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})]])_{ab}.\end{split}

(98)

Since we consider the perturbation to involve both the light field and the spin dynamics, we can devide
\rho_{ab}^{(2)}(\omega)
into three components as,

\displaystyle\rho_{ab}^{(2)}(\omega)=\rho_{EE,ab}(\omega)+\rho_{E\mathrm{S},ab}(\omega)+\rho_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S},ab}(\omega),

(99)

where

\displaystyle\rho_{EE,ab}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}E^{\nu}(\omega_{1})E^{\lambda}(\omega_{2})\quantity(d(\omega) \odot[r^{\nu}, d(\omega- \omega_{1})\odot[r^{\lambda}, \rho^{(0)}(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})]])_{ab}+[(\nu,\omega_{1})\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\omega_{2})],

(100)

\displaystyle\rho_{E\mathrm{S},ab}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{J}{2}\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}E^{\nu}(\omega_{1})\mathrm{S}^{\lambda}(\omega_{2})\quantity(d(\omega) \odot[r^{\nu}, d(\omega- \omega_{1})\odot[B^{\lambda}, \rho^{(0)}(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})]])_{ab},

(101)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{S}E,ab}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{J}{2}\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\mathrm{S}^{\lambda}(\omega_{1})E^{\nu}(\omega_{2})\quantity(d(\omega) \odot[B^{\lambda}, d(\omega- \omega_{1})\odot[r^{\nu}, \rho^{(0)}(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})]])_{ab},

(102)

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S},ab}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{J^{2}}{2}\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\mathrm{S}^{\nu}(\omega_{1})\mathrm{S}^{\lambda}(\omega_{2})\quantity(d(\omega) \odot[B^{\nu}, d(\omega- \omega_{1})\odot[B^{\lambda}, \rho^{(0)}(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})]])_{ab}+[(\nu,\omega_{1})\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\omega_{2})].

(103)

Based on this decomposition, we formulate the photocurrent formula in the presence of spin dynamics.

Based on this decomposition, we can classify

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{EE,ab}^{(ii)}&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}E^{\nu}(\omega_{1})E^{\lambda}(\omega_{2})\quantity(d(\omega) \odot[r_{i}^{\nu}, d(\omega- \omega_{1})\odot[r_{i}^{\lambda}, \rho^{(0)}(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})]])_{ab}+[(\nu,\omega_{1})\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\omega_{2})]\\

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{EE,ab}^{(ei)}&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}E^{\nu}(\omega_{1})E^{\lambda}(\omega_{2})\quantity(d(\omega) \odot[r_{e}^{\nu}, d(\omega- \omega_{1})\odot[r_{i}^{\lambda}, \rho^{(0)}(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})]])_{ab}+[(\nu,\omega_{1})\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\omega_{2})]\\

(108)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho_{EE,ab}^{(ee)}&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}E^{\nu}(\omega_{1})E^{\lambda}(\omega_{2})\quantity(d(\omega) \odot[r_{e}^{\nu}, d(\omega- \omega_{1})\odot[r_{e}^{\lambda}, \rho^{(0)}(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})]])_{ab}+[(\nu,\omega_{1})\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\omega_{2})]\\

&\hskip 341.43306pt+[(\nu,\omega_{1})\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\omega_{2})].\end{split}

\displaystyle\begin{split}J^{\mu}_{EE}(\omega)&=\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{abc}J_{ab}^{\mu}\rho_{EE,ba}(\omega)\\
&\eqqcolon\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}{\sigma}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{EE}(\omega,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})E^{\nu}(\omega_{1})E^{\lambda}(\omega_{2})2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}).\end{split}

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}=\sigma_{EE,(ii)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}+\sigma_{EE,(ie)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}+\sigma_{EE,(ei)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}+\sigma_{EE,(ee)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}.

We can obtain the photocurrent conductivity by setting

\displaystyle\omega=0,\omega_{1}=-\Omega,\omega_{2}=\Omega.

(112)

In the following calculation, we will derive the Drude term, Berry curvature dipole term, injection current term, injection current term, and intrinsic Fermi surface term.

A.2
Fermi surface effect I: Drude term

Firstly, we focus on
\sigma_{EE,(ii)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,(ii)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a}-v_{aa}^{\mu}d_{aa}^{0}d_{aa}^{\Omega}\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\lambda}f(\epsilon_{\bm{k}a})+\left[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)\right],

(113)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2i\eta}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a}v_{aa}^{\mu}\left(\frac{1}{\Omega+i\eta}+\frac{1}{-\Omega+i\eta}\right)\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\lambda}f(\epsilon_{\bm{k}a}),

(114)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}+\eta^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a}v_{aa}^{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\lambda}f(\epsilon_{\bm{k}a}).

(115)

By replacing
\eta
with
1/\tau
phenomenologically, we can obtain the Drude term formula as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{\mathrm{D}}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)=\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}+(1/\tau)^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\lambda}\epsilon_{\bm{k}a}f(\epsilon_{\bm{k}a}).

(116)

This term shows the
\tau^{2}
-dependence at the peak
\omega=0
. In
\mathcal{T}
-symmetric system,
\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{\mathrm{D}}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)
vanishes because
\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\lambda}\epsilon_{\bm{k}a}f(\epsilon_{\bm{k}a})
is odd function of
\bm{k}

On the other hand, the breaking of the
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{T}
-symmetries allows the band dispersion to be antisymmetric between
\bm{k}
, therefore
\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{\mathrm{D}}
can be finite.
The Drude term comes from the diagonal part of the current operator in the output vertex.

A.3
Fermi surface effect II: Berry curvature dipole

Secondly, we derive the Berry curvature dipole term from
\sigma_{EE,(ei)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
.

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,(ei)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(\Omega+i\eta)}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\nu}\partial_{\lambda}f_{ba}+\left[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)\right],

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(\Omega+i\eta)}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}(\xi_{ba}^{\mu}\xi_{ab}^{\nu}-\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\nu})\partial_{\lambda}f_{a}+\left[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)\right],

\displaystyle=\frac{i\Omega}{\Omega^{2}+\eta^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}(\partial_{\lambda}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\nu}]-\partial_{\nu}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}])f_{a}

\displaystyle+\frac{\eta}{\Omega^{2}+\eta^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}(\partial_{\lambda}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\nu}]+\partial_{\nu}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}])f_{a}.

(117)

In the
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric system, the Berry curvature dipole term vanishes because the Berry curvature vanishes at every wave vector.
By replacing the
\eta
with
1/\tau
, we can obtain the formula for the Berry curvature dipole term as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{BCD;C}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i\Omega}{\Omega^{2}+1/\tau^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}(\partial_{\lambda}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\nu}]-\partial_{\nu}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}])f_{a},

(118)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{BCD;L}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1/\tau}{\Omega^{2}+1/\tau^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}(\partial_{\lambda}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\nu}]+\partial_{\nu}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}])f_{a}.

(119)

The former term can be induced by circularly polarized light, while the latter term can be induced by non-circularly polarized light and exhibits
\tau
-dependence when
\Omega=0
. The Berry curvature dipole term originates from the off-diagonal component of the current operator
J^{\mu}
in the output vertex.

A.4
Interband effect I: Injection current

Here, we focus on
\sigma_{EE,(ee)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
with the diagonal component of the current operator in the band basis denoted as
\sigma_{EE,(ee;d)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
;

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{EE,(ee;d)}(\omega,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq c}J_{aa}^{\mu}d_{aa}(\omega)\quantity[d_{ca}(\omega-\omega_{1})\xi^{\nu}_{ac}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}f_{ac} - d_{ac}(\omega-\omega_{1})\xi_{ca}^{\nu}\xi_{ac}^{\lambda}f_{cb}]+[(\nu,\omega_{1})\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\omega_{2})]\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{1}{\omega+i\eta}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq c}(J_{aa}^{\mu}-J_{cc}^{\mu})\quantity[d_{ca}(\omega-\omega_{1})\xi^{\nu}_{ac}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}f_{ac}]+[(\nu,\omega_{1})\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\omega_{2})]\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{1}{\omega+i\eta}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq c}(J_{aa}^{\mu}-J_{cc}^{\mu})\xi^{\nu}_{ac}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}f_{ac}\quantity[d_{ca}(\omega-\omega_{1}) + d_{ac}(\omega-\omega_{2})]\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{1}{\omega+i\eta}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq c}\Delta_{ac}^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}_{ac}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}f_{ac}\quantity[d_{ca}(\omega-\omega_{1}) + d_{ac}(\omega-\omega_{2})].\end{split}

(120)

Here,
\Delta_{ac}^{\mu}=J_{aa}^{\mu}-J_{cc}^{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}\epsilon_{\bm{k}a}-\partial_{\mu}\epsilon_{\bm{k}b}
is the velocity difference matrix.
In the
\omega\to 0
limit, the expression diverges because of the factor of
1/(\omega+i\eta)
. To eliminate this unphysical divergence, we introduce the finite sum frequency
\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}=\delta
and will take the limit
\delta\rightarrow 0
.

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{EE,(ee;d)}(0,-\Omega,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{1}{\delta+i\eta}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\Delta_{ab}^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}_{ab}\xi^{\lambda}_{ba}f_{ab}\quantity[d_{ba}(\Omega) + d_{ab}(-\Omega)],

(121)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{1}{2}\dfrac{1}{\delta+i\eta}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\Delta_{ab}^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}_{ab}\xi^{\lambda}_{ba}f_{ab}\quantity[\frac{1}{\Omega+\frac{\delta}{2}+i\eta-\epsilon_{ba}} + \frac{1}{-\Omega+\frac{\delta}{2}+i\eta+\epsilon_{ba}}].

Here, we perform the Taylor expansion,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Omega+\frac{\delta}{2}+i\eta-\epsilon_{ba}}+\frac{1}{-\Omega+\frac{\delta}{2}+i\eta+\epsilon_{ba}}=2\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\Omega+i\eta-\epsilon_{ba}}-2\operatorname{Re}\frac{\delta/2}{(\Omega+i\eta-\epsilon_{ba})^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\delta^{2}).

(122)

By using this method and replacing the
\eta
with
1/\tau
, we can obtain the formulation of the injection current and intrinsic Fermi surface effect as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{EE,(ee;d)}(0,-\Omega,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{EE,\mathrm{inj}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(0,-\Omega,\Omega)+\sigma_{EE,\mathrm{IFSI}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(0,-\Omega,\Omega),

(123)

here,

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\text{inj}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(0,-\Omega,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\pi\tau\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq c}\Delta_{ac}^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}_{ac}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}f_{ac}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ca}),

(124)

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\mathrm{IFSI}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(0,-\Omega,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq c}\xi^{\nu}_{ac}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}f_{ac}\partial_{\mu}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ca}}.

(125)

Here, we decompose the
\sigma_{EE,\text{inj}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
into real and imaginary parts as

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\text{Inj;M}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\pi\tau\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq c}\Delta_{ac}^{\mu}\operatorname{Re}\quantity[\xi^{\nu}_{ac}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}]f_{ac}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ca}),

(126)

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\text{Inj;E}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=i\pi\tau\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq c}\Delta_{ac}^{\mu}\operatorname{Im}\quantity[\xi^{\nu}_{ac}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}]f_{ac}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ca}).

(127)

\sigma_{\text{Inj;M}}
(
\sigma_{\text{Inj;E}}
) is called magnetic (electric) injection current, which is induced by linearly (circularly) polarized light.
We can also decompose the
\sigma_{EE,\text{IFSI}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
into real and imaginary parts as

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\mathrm{IFSI;M}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(0,-\Omega,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq c}\operatorname{Re}[\xi^{\nu}_{ac}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}]f_{ac}\partial_{\mu}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ca}},

(128)

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\mathrm{IFSI;E}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(0,-\Omega,\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq c}\operatorname{Im}[\xi^{\nu}_{ac}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}]f_{ac}\partial_{\mu}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ca}}.

(129)

\sigma_{\mathrm{IFSI;M}}
(
\sigma_{\mathrm{IFSI;E}}
) is called magnetic (electric) intrinsic Fermi surface term I, which is induced by linearly (circularly) polarized light.
The photocurrent responses that we derived in this section come from the diagonal part of the current operator
J^{\mu}
in the output vertex.

A.5
Interband effect II: Shift current and intrinsic Fermi surface effect

Here, we derive the shift current term and intrinsic Fermi surface term based from
\sigma_{EE,(ee)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
and
\sigma_{EE,(ie)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
.
The photocurrent responses that we derive in this section come from the off-diagonal part of the current operator
J^{\mu}
in the output vertex.
First, we focus on
\sigma_{EE,(ee)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
with the off-diagonal component of the velocity operator defined as
\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{EE,(ee;o)}
;

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{EE,(ee;o)}(0,-\Omega,\Omega)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b\neq c}J_{ab}^{\mu}d_{ba}(0)\quantity[d_{ca}(\Omega)\xi^{\nu}_{bc}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}f_{ac} - d_{bc}(\Omega)\xi_{ca}^{\nu}\xi_{bc}^{\lambda}f_{cb}]+[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)]\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b\neq c}i\epsilon_{ab}\xi_{ab}^{\mu}d_{ba}(0)\quantity[d_{ca}(\Omega)\xi^{\nu}_{bc}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}f_{ac} - d_{bc}(\Omega)\xi_{ca}^{\nu}\xi_{bc}^{\lambda}f_{cb}]+[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)]\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b\neq c}i\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\quantity[d_{ca}(\Omega)\xi^{\nu}_{bc}\xi^{\lambda}_{ca}f_{ac} - d_{bc}(\Omega)\xi_{ca}^{\nu}\xi_{bc}^{\lambda}f_{cb}]+[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)]\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b\neq c}i(\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{bc}^{\nu}-\xi_{bc}^{\mu}\xi_{ab}^{\nu})\xi_{ca}^{\lambda}f_{ac}d_{ac}(\Omega)+[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)]\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq c}\quantity(\qty[D^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}]_{ac}- \qty[D^{\nu}\xi^{\mu}]_{ac})\xi_{ca}^{\lambda}f_{ac}d_{ac}(\Omega)+[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)].\end{split}

(130)

In the last line, we introduced U(1)-covariant derivative

\displaystyle\quantity[D^{\mu}O]_{ab}=\partialderivative{O_{ab}}{k^{\mu}}-i\quantity(\xi_{aa}^{\mu} - \xi_{bb}^{\mu})O_{ab},

(131)

and used the following formula under the condition of
a\neq c
;

\displaystyle\quantity[D^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}]_{ac}-\quantity[D^{\nu}\xi^{\mu}]_{ac}=\sum_{b}i(\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\xi_{bc}^{\nu}-\xi_{bc}^{\mu}\xi_{ab}^{\nu}).

(132)

Next, we focus on the
\sigma_{EE,(ie)}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
component;

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{EE,(ie)}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)\\
&=-\dfrac{i}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}J_{ab}^{\mu}d_{ba}(\omega)\quantity[\pdv{k^{\nu}}\qty(d_{ba}(\Omega)f_{ba}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda} - i \qty(\xi_{aa}^{\nu} - \xi_{bb}^{\nu})d_{ba}(\Omega)f_{ba}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda})]+[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)]\\
&=-\dfrac{i}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}i\epsilon_{ab}\xi_{ab}^{\mu}d_{ba}(\omega)\quantity[\pdv{k^{\nu}}\qty(d_{ba}(\Omega)f_{ba}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda} - i \qty(\xi_{aa}^{\nu} - \xi_{bb}^{\nu})d_{ba}(\Omega)f_{ba}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda})]+[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)]\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\xi_{ab}^{\mu}\quantity[\pdv{k^{\nu}}\qty(d_{ba}(\Omega)f_{ba}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}) - i \qty(\xi_{aa}^{\nu} - \xi_{bb}^{\nu})d_{ba}(\Omega)f_{ba}\xi^{\lambda}_{ba}]+[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)]\\
&=-\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\quantity[D^{\nu}\xi^{\mu}]_{ab}d_{ba}(\Omega)f_{ba}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}+[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)].\end{split}

(133)

By summing up these contributions, we get

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{EE,(ee;o)+(ie)}&=\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{EE,(ee;o)}(0,-\Omega,\Omega)+\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{EE,(ie)}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\quantity[D^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}]_{ab}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}f_{ab}d_{ba}(\Omega)+[(\nu,-\Omega)\leftrightarrow(\lambda,\Omega)]\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\quantity[D^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}]_{ab}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}f_{ab}d_{ba}(\Omega)+\quantity[D^{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}]_{ab}\xi_{ba}^{\nu}f_{ab}d_{ba}(-\Omega)\\
&=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\quantity[\qty[D^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}]_{ab}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda} + \qty[D^{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}]_{ba}\xi_{ab}^{\nu}]f_{ab}\mathcal{P}\dfrac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}\\
&-\dfrac{i\pi}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\quantity[\qty[D^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}]_{ab}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda} - \qty[D^{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}]_{ab}\xi_{ba}^{\nu}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}).\end{split}

(134)

The absorptive part with
\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba})
corresponds to the shift current conductivity, and we divide it into the real and imaginary parts as

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\text{shift}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}=\dfrac{\pi}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Im}\quantity[\qty[D^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}]_{ab}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda} - \qty[D^{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}]_{ba}\xi_{ab}^{\nu}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}),

(135)

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\text{gyro}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}=-\dfrac{i\pi}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Re}\quantity[\qty[D^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}]_{ab}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda} - \qty[D^{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}]_{ba}\xi_{ab}^{\nu}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}).

(136)

\sigma_{\text{shift}}
(
\sigma_{\text{gyro}}
) is called shift (gyration) currents, which is induced by linearly (circularly) polarized light.
On the other hand, the non-absorptive part
\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}
corresponds to the intrinsic Fermi surface term II, and we divide it into the real and imaginary parts as

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\text{IFSI\hskip-1.2ptI;M}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\partial_{\mu}\operatorname{Re}[\xi^{\nu}_{ab}\xi^{\lambda}_{ba}]f_{ab}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}},

(137)

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\text{IFSI\hskip-1.2ptI;E}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\partial_{\mu}\operatorname{Im}[\xi^{\nu}_{ab}\xi^{\lambda}_{ba}]f_{ab}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}.

(138)

Here, we used the relation,

\displaystyle\quantity[D^{\mu}\xi^{\nu}]_{ab}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}+\quantity[D^{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}]_{ba}\xi_{ab}^{\nu}=\partial_{\mu}[\xi_{ab}^{\nu}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda}].

(139)

We can get the intrinsic Fermi Surface term
\sigma_{\mathrm{IFS}}
by combining the
\sigma_{\mathrm{IFSI}}
and
\sigma_{\mathrm{IFSI\hskip-1.2ptI}}
as

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\mathrm{IFS}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{\mathrm{IFSI}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}+\sigma_{\mathrm{IFSI\hskip-1.2ptI}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda},

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\xi^{\nu}_{ab}\xi^{\lambda}_{ba}\partial_{\mu}f_{ab}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}.

(140)

We can decompose the intrinsic Fermi surface term into the real and imaginary parts as

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\mathrm{IFS;M}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Re}[\xi^{\nu}_{ab}\xi^{\lambda}_{ba}]\partial_{\mu}f_{ab}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}},

(141)

\displaystyle\sigma_{EE,\mathrm{IFS;E}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Im}[\xi^{\nu}_{ab}\xi^{\lambda}_{ba}]\partial_{\mu}f_{ab}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}.

(142)

A.6

\rho_{\mathrm{SS}}
, we can express photocurrent response to the spin field as

\displaystyle J^{\mu}_{\mathrm{SS}}

\displaystyle=\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{abc}J_{ab}^{\mu}\rho_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S},ba}^{(2)}(\omega)

(143)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{\mathrm{SS}}(\omega,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\Delta\mathrm{S}^{\nu}(\omega_{1})\Delta\mathrm{S}^{\lambda}(\omega_{2})2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2})

\xi
, associated with the external light field in

(145)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S},\text{gyro}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=-J^{2}\dfrac{i\pi}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Re}\quantity[\qty[D^{\mu}B^{\nu}]_{ab}B_{ba}^{\lambda} - \qty[D^{\mu}B^{\lambda}]_{ba}B_{ab}^{\nu}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}),

(146)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S},\text{Inj;M}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\pi J^{2}\tau\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\Delta_{ab}^{\mu}\operatorname{Re}\quantity[B^{\nu}_{ab}B^{\lambda}_{ba}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}),

(147)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{S}\mathrm{S},\text{Inj;E}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=i\pi J^{2}\tau\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\Delta_{ab}^{\mu}\operatorname{Im}\quantity[B^{\nu}_{ab}B^{\lambda}_{ba}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}),

(148)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{SS},\mathrm{IFSI;M}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{J^{2}}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Re}[B^{\nu}_{ab}B^{\lambda}_{ba}]f_{ab}\partial_{\mu}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}},

(149)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{SS},\mathrm{IFSI;E}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

(150)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{SS},\text{IFSI\hskip-1.2ptI;M}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{J^{2}}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\partial_{\mu}\operatorname{Re}[B^{\nu}_{ab}B^{\lambda}_{ba}]f_{ab}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}},

(151)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{SS},\text{IFSI\hskip-1.2ptI;E}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{iJ^{2}}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\partial_{\mu}\operatorname{Im}[B^{\nu}_{ab}B^{\lambda}_{ba}]f_{ab}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}.

(152)

A.7
Interference of light field and spin dynamics

Following the previous subsection, we consider the photocurrent response coming from the interference of the light field and spin dynamics. Using
\rho_{E\mathrm{S}}
and
\rho_{\mathrm{S}E}
, we can write photocurrent formula as

\displaystyle J_{E\mathrm{S}}^{\mu}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{abc}J_{ab}^{\mu}(\rho_{E\mathrm{S},ba}^{(2)}(\omega)+\rho_{\mathrm{S}E,ba}^{(2)}(\omega))

(153)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\int\dfrac{d\omega_{1}d\omega_{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left[\sigma_{\text{MD}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(\omega,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})+\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{S}E}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(\omega,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\right]\Delta\mathrm{S}^{\nu}(\omega_{1})E^{\lambda}(\omega_{2})2\pi\delta(\omega-\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}).

(154)

Here, the term
\sigma_{\text{MD}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}(\omega,\omega_{1},\omega_{2})
can be expressed as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{MD}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{2(\Omega+i/\tau)}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}(\xi_{ba}^{\mu}B_{ab}^{\nu}-\xi_{ab}^{\mu}B_{ba}^{\nu})\partial_{\lambda}f_{a},

\displaystyle=\frac{J(1/\tau+i\Omega)}{\Omega^{2}+1/\tau^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\partial_{\lambda}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}B_{ba}^{\nu}]f_{a}.

(155)

\rho_{\mathrm{S}E}^{(2)}(\omega)
.

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{MD,C}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{Ji\Omega}{\Omega^{2}+1/\tau^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\partial_{\lambda}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}B_{ba}^{\nu}]f_{a},

(156)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{MD,L}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{J/\tau}{\Omega^{2}+1/\tau^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\partial_{\lambda}\operatorname{Im}[\xi_{ab}^{\mu}B_{ba}^{\nu}]f_{a}.

(157)

The former term can be induced by circularly polarized light and the latter term can be induced by non-circularly polarized light.
The latter term exhibits a
\tau
-dependent peak at
\Omega=0
.
On the other hand, the
\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{S}E}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}
can be classified into the following eight component.

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{S}E,\text{shift}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=J\dfrac{\pi}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Im}\quantity[\qty[D^{\mu}B^{\nu}]_{ab}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda} - \qty[D^{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}]_{ba}B_{ab}^{\nu}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}),

(158)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{S}E,\text{gyro}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=-J\dfrac{i\pi}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Re}\quantity[\qty[D^{\mu}B^{\nu}]_{ab}\xi_{ba}^{\lambda} - \qty[D^{\mu}\xi^{\lambda}]_{ba}B_{ab}^{\nu}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}),

(159)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{S}E,\text{Inj;M}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\pi J\tau\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\Delta_{ab}^{\mu}\operatorname{Re}\quantity[B^{\nu}_{ab}\xi^{\lambda}_{ba}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}),

(160)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{S}E,\text{Inj;E}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=i\pi J\tau\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\Delta_{ab}^{\mu}\operatorname{Im}\quantity[B^{\nu}_{ab}\xi^{\lambda}_{ba}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}),

(161)

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{S}E,\mathrm{IFSI;M}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\frac{J}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Re}[B^{\nu}_{ab}\xi^{\lambda}_{ba}]f_{ab}\partial_{\mu}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}},

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{S}E,\mathrm{IFSI;E}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

Appendix B
U(2) gauge description of photocurrent response in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric systems

The Bloch states have at least U(2)-gauge degree of freedom due to the PT-symmetry-endowed double degeneracy.
In the previous section, we formulated the photocurrent responses in spinless systems or
\mathcal{PT}
-violated spinful systems.
The formulas for the photocurrent responses in the previous section are not invariant under the U(2)-gauge transformation. Thus, we rewrite the formulas for the photocurrent responses in the U(2) invariant form
[
58
]
.
More general treatment can be found in Ref.
[
72
]
where the projection operator is utilized in order to retain the gauge invariance.
First, we can decompose the Berry connection as

\displaystyle\xi_{ab}^{\mu}=\alpha_{ab}^{\mu}+\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{\mu}

(166)

where the
\alpha_{ab}^{\mu}
is the intraband component and the
\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{\mu}
is the interband component of the Berry connection. With this decomposition of the Berry connection, the intraband component of the position operator
r^{\mu}_{i}
is modified as

\displaystyle(r^{\mu}_{i})_{ab}=i\partial_{\mu}\delta_{ab}+\alpha_{ab}^{\mu},

(167)

and the interband component of the position operator is given by
(r_{e}^{\mu})_{ab}=\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{\mu}
.
Using the intraband component of the position operator, the U(2)-gauge-covariant derivative is defined by
\mathfrak{D}_{\mu}=-ir_{i}^{\mu}
.
The derivative acts on the physical quantities in the Bloch representation
O_{ab}
as

\displaystyle[\mathfrak{D}_{\mu}O]_{ab}=\partial_{\mu}O_{ab}-i\left(\sum_{c}\alpha_{ac}^{\mu}O_{cb}-\sum_{c}O_{ac}\alpha_{cb}^{\mu}\right).

(168)

We can check that
[\mathfrak{D}_{\mu}O]_{ab}
is U(2) covariant by taking the U(2)-gauge transformation
\ket{u_a(\bm{k})}\rightarrow\ket{u_b(\bm{k})}U_{ba}
, where the summation of the band index is taken over the Kramers pair.
Using this U(2)-gauge covariant derivative, we show the formulas for the photocurrent response in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric spinful systems. In the same manner as the Berry connection, we can decompose the spin operator as

\displaystyle B_{ab}^{\mu}=s_{ab}^{\mu}+\mathcal{S}_{ab}^{\mu},

(169)

where the
s_{ab}^{\mu}
is the intraband component and the
\mathcal{S}_{ab}^{\mu}
is the interband component of the spin operator.
By using these methods, we show the formulas for the photocurrent responses in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric spinfull systems in the following subsections.

B.1
Light field induced photocurrent in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric spinful systems

Here, we show the formulas for the photocurrent responses from the light field in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric systems.
In the previous section, we can derive the formulas for the photocurrent responses in spinless systems.
In the same manner, we can get the formulas for the photocurrent responses in
\mathcal{PT}
-symmetric spinful systems by replacing the Berry connection
\xi
with
\mathcal{A}
and the U(1)-covariant derivative
D_{\mu}
with the U(2)-covariant derivative
\mathfrak{D}_{\mu}
.
The formulas are expressed as

\displaystyle\sigma^{\mu;\nu\lambda}_{EE,\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}+1/\tau^{2}}\int\frac{d\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a}\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\lambda}\epsilon_{\bm{k}a}f(\epsilon_{\bm{k}a}),

\displaystyle\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Im}[\mathcal{S}_{ab}^{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{ba}^{x}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba})=\frac{1-\sigma_{\mathcal{S}^{\nu}}}{4i}\sum_{a\neq b}[\mathcal{S}_{ab}^{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{ba}^{x}-\mathcal{S}_{ba}^{x}\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{x}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}),

(274)

\displaystyle\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Re}[\mathcal{S}_{ab}^{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{ba}^{x}]f_{ab}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}=\frac{1+\sigma_{\mathcal{S}^{\nu}}}{4}\sum_{a\neq b}[\mathcal{S}_{ab}^{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{ba}^{x}+\mathcal{S}_{ba}^{x}\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{x}]f_{ab}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}},

(275)

\displaystyle\sum_{a\neq b}\operatorname{Im}[\mathcal{S}_{ab}^{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{ba}^{x}]f_{ab}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}=\frac{1-\sigma_{\mathcal{S}^{\nu}}}{4i}\sum_{a\neq b}[\mathcal{S}_{ab}^{\nu}\mathcal{A}_{ba}^{x}-\mathcal{S}_{ba}^{x}\mathcal{A}_{ab}^{x}]f_{ab}\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}.

(276)

In addition to the
\mathcal{PT}
symmetry restriction, phase degrees of freedom between the light field and fictitious spin field play an important role in the photocurrent response.
Rewriting the Eq. (

\chi_{\mathrm{S}E^{x}}

\displaystyle J_{ES}^{y}

\displaystyle=\int\dfrac{d\Omega}{2\pi}[\sigma_{\mathrm{MD}}^{y;\nu x}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)+\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{S}E}^{y;\nu x}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)]\chi_{\mathrm{S}^{\nu}E^{x}}(-\Omega)E^{x}(-\Omega)E^{x}(\Omega),

\displaystyle=\int\dfrac{d\Omega}{2\pi}[\sigma_{\mathrm{MD}}^{y;\nu x}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)+\tilde{\sigma}_{\mathrm{S}E}^{y;\nu x}(0;-\Omega,\Omega)]\quantity[\operatorname{Re}\chi_{\mathrm{S}^{\nu}E^{x}}(-\Omega) + i\operatorname{Im}\chi_{\mathrm{S}^{\nu}E^{x}}(-\Omega)]\quantity[E^{x}(\Omega)]^{\ast}E^{x}(\Omega),

Appendix D
Relaxation time dependence of photocurrent response

In this section, we discuss the relaxation time dependence of the photocurrent response, especially the injection current, shift current, and intrinsic Fermi surface term from the interband transition of the electrons.

D.1
Injection current

First, we discuss the relaxation time dependence of the injection current. We can express the injection current in general as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{Inj}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\pi\tau\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\Delta_{ab}^{\mu}X^{\nu}_{ab}X^{\lambda}_{ba}f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}),

(278)

where the matrix
X_{ab}
is defined as the operator, such as the Berry connection or spin operator. Considering the effect of the phenomenological relaxation, we can replace the delta function
\delta(\omega)
with the Lorentz function, which is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\omega)=\frac{\tau^{-1}}{\pi}\frac{1}{\Omega^{2}+(1/\tau)^{2}}.

(279)

By using this method, we can rewrite the formula for the injection current as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{Inj}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=\pi\tau\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\Delta_{ab}^{\mu}X^{\nu}_{ab}X^{\lambda}_{ba}f_{ab}\frac{\tau^{-1}}{\pi}\frac{1}{(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba})^{2}+(1/\tau)^{2}}.

(280)

In the low-frequency regime, in which the frequency of the light satisfies the relation
\left\lvert\omega-\epsilon_{ba}\right\rvert\gg\tau^{-1}
, we can approximate the formula for the injection current as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{Inj}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle\simeq\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\Delta_{ab}^{\mu}X^{\nu}_{ab}X^{\lambda}_{ba}f_{ab}\frac{1}{(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba})^{2}}.

(281)

Considering this formula for the injection current, we find the injection current does not show the relaxation time dependence and shows frequency dependence as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{Inj}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}\propto\frac{1}{(\Omega-\epsilon_{g})^{2}},

(282)

where the
\epsilon_{g}
reflects the property of the optical gap of the electronic system.

D.2
Shift current

Second, we focus on the relaxation time dependence of the shift current. We can discuss the behavior of the shift current in the same manner as the injection current case. In general, the shift current term can be expressed as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{shift}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}=-\dfrac{i\pi}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\quantity[\qty[D^{\mu}X^{\nu}]_{ab}X_{ba}^{\lambda} - \qty[D^{\mu}X^{\lambda}]_{ab}X_{ba}^{\nu}]f_{ab}\delta(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}).

(283)

By replacing the delta function with the Lorentz function, we can rewrite the formula for the shift current as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{shift}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}=-\dfrac{i\pi}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\quantity[\qty[D^{\mu}X^{\nu}]_{ab}X_{ba}^{\lambda} - \qty[D^{\mu}X^{\lambda}]_{ab}X_{ba}^{\nu}]f_{ab}\frac{\tau^{-1}}{\pi}\frac{1}{(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba})^{2}+(1/\tau)^{2}}.

(284)

In the low-frequency regime, we can approximate the formula for the shift current as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{shift}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}\simeq-\dfrac{i\tau^{-1}}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}\quantity[\qty[D^{\mu}X^{\nu}]_{ab}X_{ba}^{\lambda} - \qty[D^{\mu}X^{\lambda}]_{ab}X_{ba}^{\nu}]f_{ab}\frac{1}{(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba})^{2}}.

(285)

By considering this formula, we can find the relaxation time dependence of the shift current below

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{shift}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}\propto\frac{\tau^{-1}}{(\Omega-\epsilon_{g})^{2}}.

(286)

D.3
Intrinsic Fermi surface term

Finally, we discuss the relaxation time dependence of the intrinsic Fermi surface term. In general, we can express the formula for the intrinsic Fermi surface term as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{IFS}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}X^{\nu}_{ab}X^{\lambda}_{ba}\partial_{\mu}f_{ab}\mathcal{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}.

(287)

Considering the effect of the relaxation time, we can replace the principle value as

\displaystyle\mathrm{P}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}\to\frac{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}{(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba})^{2}+(1/\tau)^{2}}.

(288)

In the low-frequency regime, we can approximate the formula for the intrinsic Fermi surface effect as

\displaystyle\sigma_{\text{IFS}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}X^{\nu}_{ab}X^{\lambda}_{ba}\partial_{\mu}f_{ab}\frac{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}{(\Omega-\epsilon_{ba})^{2}+(1/\tau)^{2}},

\displaystyle\simeq-\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d{\bf\it k}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{a\neq b}X^{\nu}_{ab}X^{\lambda}_{ba}\partial_{\mu}f_{ab}\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{ba}}.

(289)

Considering this formula, we can find the frequency dependence of the intrinsic Fermi surface effect below

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathrm{IFS}}^{\mu;\nu\lambda}\propto\frac{1}{\Omega-\epsilon_{g}}.

(290)

Therefore, the intrinsic Fermi surface term shows no
\tau
-dependence in the low-frequency regime.
