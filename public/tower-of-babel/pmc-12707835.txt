Fatty Acid Profile and Hazard Index of Ready-to-Eat Foods Available in the Market: A Risk Survey
Authors: Masoumeh Moslemi, Abdol-Samad Abedi, Narges Shahbazpour, Ghazaleh Bahramian, Roholla Ferdousi, Seyed Ehsan Beladian Behbahan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Dietary fat is of necessary macronutrients for body health. According to the guidelines, up to 10% of calories can be supplied by saturated fat. Moreover, intake of trans fats should be reduced up to zero, if possible. Owing to the increasing share of ready-to-eat foods in the daily basket, we aimed to analyze these types of foods to determine their fatty acid profile, and the hazard indices of atherogenicity and thrombogenicity.Methods:Four types of industrial and seven types of retail ready-to-eat foods were purchased from local markets. The fatty acid profile was determined using a gas chromatograph–flame ionization detector.Results:According to our results, trans fats were found at very low levels in the products. The amount of saturated and trans fats increased by increasing the concentration of meat. In comparison, the most concentration of unsaturated fatty acids was observed in the products prepared with vegetable oils and/or nonmeat ingredients, including sausage, salami, falafel, and samosa. Among the fatty acids, palmitic, oleic, and linoleic acids were predominant in both industrial and retail foods. Evaluation of hazard indices in the foods revealed that both atherogenicity (0.2–0.85) and thrombogenicity (0.46–1.51) indexes were within the normal ranges, except for industrially packed hamburger with a thrombogenicity index of 3.7 due to its high stearic acid.Conclusions:Considering the low atherogenicity and thrombogenicity indexes, we concluded that the ready-to-eat foods did not compromise the health of the consumers. It was approved by the low ratio of SFA/USFA in almost all the products.

Introduction

Dietary pattern and food composition have great importance for healthcare systems. The effect of food components on the health of individuals has been addressed by several studies.[1234] Other than supplying the body requirements, food ingredients may be harmful to humans, especially when ingested in high amounts. According to the dietary recommendations, up to 25–30% of the body needs should be supplied by fat.[5] Fat composition in the diet has a significant role in growth and/or development of associated diseases.[6] Dietary fat is mainly composed of triglycerides, diglycerides, monoglycerides, and cholesterol. Fatty acids, as the constituting agent of tri-, di-, and monoglycerides, are composed of a hydrocarbon backbone linking the carbon atoms by single or double bounds in structure. Saturated fatty acids are those containing single bound in the chain and have the potential to precipitate in the body vessels due to their high melting point. Based on the position of side chains around the double bounds, two isomers of cis and trans fatty acids with different metabolism pathways in the body are formed. On the other hand, the side chains in trans fatty acids are located on the opposite sides around the double bond in the hydrocarbon backbone, while the side chains of the cis isomer are located on the same side.[7]

There has been an increasing concern about trans fatty acids following the notice of the United States Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) on removal of partially hydrogenated oil from the list of “generally recognized as safe” in 2013. The US FDA stated that distribution of partially hydrogenated oil in the market should be subjected to FDA approval because there was no consensus about the safety of trans fatty acids.[8] In comparison, despite the overall view on association of saturated fatty acids with cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and other noncommunicable diseases, Astrup et al.[9] stated that type of saturated fatty acid and/or its metabolites and food composition are important critically. The authors believed that the food matrix and its composition affect the absorption and postprandial metabolism of saturated fats. For example, heptadecanoic acid (17:0) may reduce the risk of coronary heart disease, and a large size of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) has no association with CVDs. In their opinion, there is no doubt about the adverse effect of trans fatty acids on CVDs, but the decision on saturated fatty acids needs evaluation of the fatty acid profile and composition of the vehicle food. In fact, the ingested fat is absorbed in the intestine and transferred to the liver for further metabolism in the form of chylomicron. It has been stated that the activity of the cholesterol ester transfer protein (an enzyme responsible for the transfer of cholesterol) increases when trans fats are ingested, by which more cholesterol is transferred from HDL to LDL. Therefore, the ratio of LDL/HDL increases in the blood that is associated with the development of coronary heart diseases.[10]

Industrial trans-fat is formed through hydrogenation of vegetable oil and under high temperature of oil processing. Ruminant trans-fat is formed by biohydrogenation of oil in the rumen of animals.[11] Elaidic acid is the most prevalent industrial trans fatty acid, and vaccenic acid and its derivative (conjugated linoleic acid) are the most predominant ruminant trans fatty acids in the diet.[12] Ruminant and industrial trans fats are different in position of double bounds. Therefore, their pathogenicity might be different.[13] Nonetheless, it is assumed that both ruminant and industrial trans fatty acids would have negative effects on the body.[14] Prada et al.[15] investigated the possible effect of industrial and ruminant trans fats on the development of type 2 diabetes in 1159 German individuals during 6.5 years. The authors found no association of industrial trans fats with type 2 diabetes, but ruminant trans fats showed different associations, so that 18:1n-7t and t10c12-CLA had an inverse association, and c9t11-CLA had a positive association with type 2 diabetes. On the other hand, Lechner et al.[16] found positive association of industrial trans fat with cardiovascular risk factors. Undoubtedly, food composition affects metabolism of trans fatty acids. Indeed, despite the inconsistencies about the effects of natural trans fats in the body, food composition may attenuate the negative effect of trans fatty acids on blood lipid profile and other associated diseases.[12131617] In general, without distinguishing the source of trans fatty acids, 2% increase in dietary intake of trans fats will result in 23% increase in risk of CVDs.[18] In this regard, a maximum level of 1% energy has been determined for intake of both industrial and ruminant trans fatty acids by the World Health Organization.[19]

With respect to the significant role of daily foods in the incidence of the body disorders, the fatty acid profile of the oil extracted from ready-to-eat (RTE) foods available in Tehran market (capital city of Iran) has been determined in the laboratory. In detail, we analyzed 11 types of industrial and retail RTE foods, and the concentration of saturated, unsaturated, and trans fatty acids in the products was determined. Moreover, the hazard indices associated with the development of heart diseases were assessed.

Materials and Methods

Sample collection

In 2018, a total number of 212 RTE foods including sausage, salami, retail hamburger, industrially packed hamburger, retail stew, canned stew, kebab, Turkish kebab, pizza, falafel, and samosa were purchased from local markets in the north, south, east, west, and center of Tehran (capital city of Iran). The products were selected randomly. Then, they were transferred to the laboratory of Faculty of Nutrition Sciences and Food Technology of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences for analysis.

Fatty acid profile

Fatty acids were methylated according to the method of Fallon et al.[20] and Trigueros and Sender[21] with some modifications. At first, 1 g of the sample was mixed with 6 ml nonadecanoic acid methanolic solution (0.3 mg/ml), followed by addition of 700 μl KOH 10 N. The mixture was heated in a water bath at 55 ºC for 90 min. Meanwhile, the tube was shaken every 20 min for 10 sec. After 90 min, the tube was cooled, and 570 μl sulfuric acid 20 N was added to the content. Then, it was heated again at 55 ºC for 90 min in a water bath by shaking every 20 min. In the next step, the tube was cooled again, and 2 ml hexane was added to the mixture. After shaking for 5 min, the tube was centrifuged at 4000 rpm. The supernatant was removed and injected to a gas chromatograph–flame ionization detector (Varian, CP3800) equipped with an HP88 capillary column (100 m × 25 mm × 0.2 μm). For analysis, 2 μl of the supernatant was injected to the instrument at a split ratio of 1:100. Helium was used as a carrier gas at a rate of 1 ml/min. The temperature of the injector and detector was set at 280 ºC and 250 ºC, respectively. The initial temperature was set at 40 ºC and reached 140 ºC at a rate of 5 ºC/min. The temperature was held at 140 ºC for 5 min. Then, it increased to 240 ºC at a rate of 10 ºC/min. The analysis was done within 45 min. The fatty acid profile is reported as percent of the foods.

Results

As shown in Tables 1 and 2, the trans-fat content was lower than 1% in all products. The amount of saturated and trans fats increased in the products by increasing the concentration of meat [Figure 1 and Figure 2]. As seen in the figures, kebab, Turkish kebab, pizza, and retail hamburger had the highest concentration of saturated fatty acids, while kebab, Turkish kebab, and industrially packed hamburger had the highest concentration of trans fat. In comparison, the most concentration of unsaturated fatty acids was observed in the products prepared with vegetable oils and/or nonmeat ingredients, including sausage, salami, falafel, and samosa [Figure 3]. Approximately, palmitic, oleic, and linoleic acids were the predominant fatty acids in both industrial and retail RTE products. Concentration of stearic and linoleic acids was correlated together so that the lower concentration of linoleic acid in retail products was compensated for the concentration of stearic acid compared to industrial RTE foods [Table 1 and Table 2]. The correlation of stearic and linoleic acids is more significant for kebab [Table 2]. Our results showed that both AI and TI were within the normal ranges (lower than 1.32 for AI and lower than 1.7 for TI, approximately). There was an exception for industrially packed hamburger with TI of 3.7. Some foods had an h/H ratio higher than the common range (1.3–2.8 approximately). Comparison of saturated and trans fats in RTE products from our survey with those reported in other studies is shown in Figure 4. Accordingly, our samples contained similar and/or higher levels of saturated and trans fats compared to the RTE foods investigated in other Asian countries.

Discussion

Intake of high fat by individuals is a significant risk factor in the development of fatness and overweight, and noncommunicable diseases (NCDs) such as cardiovascular diseases, cancers, and diabetes.[30] Therefore, the World Health Organization designed an action plan in 2013 to decrease the rate of NCDs in the world. One of its strategies was reduction of trans fat intake up to 1% of calories by the individuals. To adhere to this goal, countries have developed several policies in their territories. Iranian policy-makers mandated the food industries to reformulate their products toward lower saturated and trans fats composition.[2] On the other hand, educational programs have been developed among healthcare systems to increase the public knowledge about the adverse effects of non-nutritional ingredients. Since 2016, all frying and cooking oils in the market have been reformulated so that they have contained saturated and trans fats up to 30% and 2%, respectively.[2] To evaluate the effectiveness of these policies, fat composition of RTE foods was studied in current work. Despite the increasing consumption of RTE products in the population, there was no concern about their saturated and trans fatty acids, and their adverse impact on health [Figures 1 and 2]. Similar results were observed in a study by Albuquerque et al.,[23] in which most RTE and fast foods in Portugal contained low levels of trans fat, and a low number of foods containing ruminant fat were considered possibly hazardous to the consumers. The authors believed that the restrictive strategies developed in the country were effective in decreasing the adverse effects of these fats in the populations. The significant effect of meat content on the concentration of saturated and trans fats in the RTE products studied in the current work was in agreement with the work of Musaiger et al.,[30] who studied fatty acid profiles of RTE foods in Bahrain. Moreover, the correlation of stearic and linoleic acids [Tables 1 and 2] was of great importance because stearic acid has beneficial metabolism in the body and can decrease the risk of cardiovascular disease after metabolism.[3132] Therefore, other than its nature as a saturated fatty acid, it has a positive role in the body. Although some other studies revealed that stearic acid has a neutral effect on blood cholesterol and does not increase the rate of heart diseases,[3334] the concentration of stearic acid is used in the calculation of TI formula as a saturated fatty acid.

In terms of hazard indices of our samples, atherogenicity (formation of atheromatous plaques in the arteries) and thrombogenicity (promotion of blood clot in the arteries) were assessed. Indeed, these factors are associated with the development of coronary heart diseases and inflammation,[3536] and their evaluation in fat-containing foods is useful for early prevention of the associated diseases. As perceived, there is no defined level for these factors. Therefore, the foods with the lower hazard indices are considered safer compared to the others. In this regard, Chen and Liu reported the common AI and TI for different foods. AI was in the range of 0.165–1.32 for different types of meats and meat products.[37] A normal range of 0.5–1 was also reported for AI of meat by Carneiro et al.[22] Furthermore, the common range of TI was 0.288–1.694 for meat and meat products.[37] Our RTE products were meat-based, and the above normal ranges were used for comparison in our study. Accordingly, both AI and TI in our samples were within the normal ranges introduced by other researchers. Exceptionally, the high TI of industrially packed hamburger (3.70) was due to the high level of stearic acid with no approved adverse effect on the lipid profile of the body. Interestingly, some RTE foods had an h/H ratio higher than the common range of 1.27–2.786 reported by Chen and Liu.[37] It was due to the high USFA content in the products rather than SFA, which provides a margin of safety for the consumers. Nonetheless, the position of fatty acids in triacylglycerol has a pivotal role in their positive or negative effect on the body. In fact, the fatty acid in sn-2 position of triacylglycerol is absorbed dominantly in the small intestine. Specifically, it has been reported that the adverse effect of palmitic acid from plant sources is relatively lower than those originating from animal sources because palmitic acid is positioned dominantly at sn-1 and sn-3 in plants, through which its absorption is reduced in the small intestine.[38] The RTE products investigated in the current work were formulated by vegetable oil, and the fatty acids from animal sources were provided from meat in the matrix. Considering the low AI and TI of RTE products and the low fat content of pure meat as an animal source (approximately 2.5%), we believe that the RTE foods do not compromise the health of the consumers. The low ratio of SFA/USFA in almost all the products confirms this hypothesis.

Consumption of RTE foods has increased fast in recent years due to modernization of societies. Moreover, higher involvement of parents in outworking jobs has resulted in more tendency to RTE foods. Owing to their palatability, these foods are more popular among teenagers and young adults. On the other hand, it provides an opportunity to eat out and spend time with friends in the restaurants. Social factors have significant roles in the tendency of people to eat fast and RTE foods. Majabadi et al.[39] reported that the tendency of people to participate in public places with relatives and friends is a motivating factor, while the negative advertisements, evidence, and experiences about these foods are an inhibitor. However, the authors found that the motivating factors are stronger than the inhibiting parameters in the population. It is of great concern because frequent consumption of these products at early ages may become a habit in adulthood. Usually, these foods contain high fats and oils, and a low concentration of micronutrients and dietary fibers that are essential for body health. In this regard, Gautam et al.[40] studied the fatty acid composition and nutrients of industrial RTE and homemade foods. In their study, some industrial products had higher saturated and trans fats than homemade foods, while some others had equal concentrations of both fatty acids. In addition, lower nutrients and dietary fibers were determined in industrial foods. However, the nutritional composition and health status of RTE foods are determined by their ingredients and processing. For example, Hungarian traditional and innovative meals had no significant difference in concentration of saturated fats, despite the higher amount of total fat in traditional meals in the study by Aloudat et al.[41] To find out the status of the Iranian population in consumption of RTE foods, the content of saturated and trans fats in the RTE foods of Iran has been compared to those of other countries in Figure 4. As seen in the figure, RTE foods available in Asian countries contained lower levels of both saturated and trans fats than the foods in European, African, and American countries. It might contribute to the common dietary pattern in the region so that most Asian meals are prepared with vegetable oils and starchy materials, while meat-based foods are more popular in the western countries. However, concerning the increasing rate of obesity and cardiovascular diseases in the population, RTE foods should be reformulated toward the use of dietary fibers to decrease the adverse effect of high fat levels in the products.

Conclusions

Concentration of trans fats was low in both industrial and retail RTE products. In detail, the highest trans fats were observed in kebab, Turkish kebab, and industrially packed hamburger. In comparison, the highest saturated fats were determined in kebab, Turkish kebab, pizza, and retail hamburger. Concentration of saturated and trans fats increased by increasing the concentration of meat in the mixture. The highest concentration of unsaturated fatty acids was observed in products prepared with vegetable oils and containing nonmeat ingredients. The calculated AI and TI were within the ranges of 0.2–0.85 and 0.46–1.51, respectively. Accordingly, all the samples were within the normal range except for the industrially packed hamburger with TI of 3.7 due to its high stearic acid. As reported, stearic acid has no significant effect on lipid profile in the body, and its high concentration would not be of serious concern to the consumers. Moreover, comparison of saturated and trans fats in our RTE foods with those analyzed by other studies showed that our samples contained lower levels of saturated and trans fats in comparison to RTE foods available in European, American, and African countries, likely due to the fact that Asian foods are mainly based on carbohydrate and dietary fibers rather than meat as a main ingredient in western foods.
