Primary cilia gene defects in BMSCs induce idiopathic scoliosis
Authors: Li Zhang, Wenhui Yang, Zhi Zhao, Yingsong Wang, Haonan Wang, Ninghui Zhao, Jin Zhou, Zhibo Song, Zhaoquan Zhang, Tao Li, Shangjia Zhe, Genghao Qian, Yingliang Liu, Jingming Xie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This paper investigated the role of cilia gene defects in bone marrow stem cells (BMSCs) in idiopathic scoliosis (IS). IS, a spinal deformity commonly occurring in adolescence, impacts patients’ quality of life. Cilia, axial filaments covered by the cell membrane and composed of microtubules on the eukaryotic cell surface, include motile cilia and primary cilia. Primary cilia on the surface of most cells mediate intracellular signaling pathways. Cilia dysfunction correlates with skeletal diseases. Defects in cilia genes disrupt osteogenic signaling pathways in BMSCs, promoting IS progression. By sample analysis, lentiviral transfection of BMSCs, and in vivo experiments in zebrafish, we explored the impact of cilia gene defects on BMSCs’ osteogenic signaling pathways and IS, revealing that defects impaired ciliogenesis, cellular signaling, and mechanical sensing, and affected vertebral alignment and skeletal patterning. These findings highlight the role of cilia gene defects in IS pathology, providing potential targets for clinical treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-06004-0.

Introduction

Idiopathic scoliosis (IS) is by far the most common kind of scoliosis, which develops most frequently in preteens or teenagers during rapid skeletal growth. “Idiopathic” means unknown cause. Recent research increasingly emphasizes genetic, environmental, and developmental factors in IS. Environmental factors may play a crucial role, particularly through mechanisms that may affect postnatal spinal development [1]. Some gene mutations are an important reason for the increased probability of IS occurrence and the worsening of the IS condition. Several genetic markers associated with IS have been identified, suggesting a polygenic influence on its pathogenesis [1, 2]. The interaction between these genetic predispositions and environmental exposures, such as differences in nutrition, physical activity, and overall lifestyle, contributes to the complexity of IS occurrence.

The role of mutations in genes like POC5, HSPG2, AKAP2, and MAPK7, along with changes in osteogenic signaling pathways, has been recognized as significant factors in the pathology of IS[3–6]. Bone marrow stem cells (BMSCs) can differentiate into multiple cell types, including osteoblasts responsible for bone formation. BMSCs are essential for bone health but can be influenced by cilia gene defects [7]. Cilia are important cellular structures that exist on the surface of most mammalian cells, including two types: motile cilia and primary cilia. Among them, motile cilia, primarily found in bronchi, sinuses, and ventricles [8–11], typically occur in clusters and influence cellular movement and the fluid environment surrounding cells [12]. Primary cilia are independent signal receptors that exist on the cell surface and can act as downstream signals for intercellular communication and various signaling pathways, playing an essential role in organ development and tissue generation [13, 14]. Dysfunctional cilia can cause multisystemic disease defects in the body, causing adverse effects on various tissues and organs, including the eyes, brain, heart, and bones [13]. Abnormal differentiation of BMSCs may contribute to the development of IS[15]. Therefore, elucidating whether the abnormal differentiation and function of BMSCs impact the occurrence of IS is of utmost significance.

A lack of understanding of how cilia gene defects affect osteogenic pathways in BMSCs has existed as a scientific challenge. These gene defects disrupt ciliogenesis, cellular signaling, and mechanical sensing, affecting vertebral alignment and skeletal patterning [16–18]. It has been shown that mutations in cilia genes or misregulation impact osteogenic differentiation and alter the expression of signaling pathways, contributing to the development of IS[19]. It is still unclear how cilia gene defects directly contribute to IS development by affecting BMSCs’ osteogenic pathways. Exploring this link is essential to gain a deeper understanding of the underlying causes of spinal deformities.

Therefore, this paper proposes the following hypothesis: defects in ciliary genes affecting the osteogenic signaling pathway of BMSCs may be a crucial factor in the onset of IS. This project elucidated the relationship between ciliary gene defects in BMSCs and their osteogenic signaling pathways and the occurrence of IS by analyzing clinical samples, utilizing lentiviral transfection of BMSCs, and conducting in vivo experiments in zebrafish. This research aimed to provide new potential targets for the clinical treatment strategies of IS patients.

Materials and methods

Clinical patient samples

15 IS bone marrow tissue samples and 15 normal samples were collected and divided into the control (C) group and the IS group. The IS tissue samples were obtained from IS patients requiring surgical correction, while normal tissue samples were obtained from patients with exogenous bone injury. The procedure for collecting bone marrow from human donors can be found in Supplementary Material 1. All procedures were approved by the internal review and the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical University (PJ-2021-100). Relative informed consent, following all applicable ethical regulations, was obtained from all patients from our institution or healthy donors. All methods were carried out under the Declaration of Helsinki principles [20].

Based on steps in a previous report [21], BMSCs were isolated from bone marrow and resuspended in Eagle medium (GIBCO, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) using gradient centrifugation. The cells were cultured at 37 ℃, 5% CO2, and 100% relative humidity in a cell culture incubator (Thermo Fisher, USA). The medium was changed to a fresh α-MEM after 48 h, with the subsequent medium changed every three days.

Flow cytometry

Initially, bone marrow samples were collected and processed using a sterile technique in a biosafety cabinet. The cells were isolated using a Ficoll density gradient centrifugation at 400 × g for 25 min, followed by washing with PBS and resuspension in α-MEM culture medium. Cell counting was performed using a hemocytometer to ensure accurate seeding density for subsequent culturing in a 6-well plate. After reaching 80–90% confluence, cells were detached using trypsin and passaged. For flow cytometry, cells were incubated with antibodies against CD34, CD45, CD90, and CD105, alongside respective isotype controls, and analyzed by using a BD FACS AccuriC6 flow cytometer. The purpose of employing these specific markers was to confirm the mesenchymal stem cell phenotype by demonstrating negative expression for hematopoietic markers (CD34, CD45) and positive expression for stromal markers (CD90, CD105).

For the detection of cell apoptosis, after the cells are digested and washed, 100 µl of binding buffer was added to resuspend the cells. Then, 5 µl of PI and 5 µl of FITC-Annexin V were added and mixed evenly. The mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Finally, 400 µl of binding buffer was added and mixed well, and then the cells were detected using a flow cytometer.

MTT assay

The 3-(4,5-Dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay was utilized to evaluate the proliferative ability of cells. MTT is a yellow water-soluble tetrazolium salt that can be reduced to purple insoluble formazan crystals by dehydrogenases in live cell mitochondria, and dead cells do not have this ability. After the dissolution of formazan, the absorbance (OD value) is measured by a spectrophotometer, indirectly reflecting the number and activity of cells. BMSCs, adherent in nature, were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin in a 37 ℃ incubator with 5% CO2. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, cells were washed with PBS, treated with trypsin for 1–3 min, and neutralized with a complete medium. The cell suspension was then centrifuged at 100 × g for 5 min, and the pellet was resuspended in a fresh medium for passaging at a 1:3 ratio. For the assay, cells were seeded at 5000 cells per well in a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h before MTT solution (5 mg/mL) was added. After approximately 3 h of further incubation, the medium was removed, and 100 µL of DMSO was added to each well to dissolve the formazan crystals. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Statistical analysis was conducted using a t-test, where a P < 0.01 was considered significant.

Cell scratch assay

The cells were cultured under standard conditions in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin at 37 ℃ in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, cells were passaged using trypsin and seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 500,000 cells per well. After 24 h, a uniform scratch was made using a pipette tip, and the cells were washed with PBS and subsequently cultured in a serum-free medium to inhibit proliferation and focus on migration. Cell migration into the scratch was photographed after 0, 12, and 24 h to assess the closure of the scratch, which served as an indicator of cell migratory speed and efficiency.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qPCR)

The qPCR was used to quantify mRNA levels of specific genes, including Nanog, Sox2, Pou5f1, Runx2, BSP, ALPL, LPL, and PPARγ. This method was chosen due to its sensitivity and specificity in detecting and quantifying minute amounts of nucleic acids, which is crucial for accurate gene expression analysis in stem cell studies. RNA was extracted from the cells using the Trizol method, followed by chloroform separation and isopropanol precipitation. The purity and concentration of RNA were assessed by using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. After RNA extraction, genomic DNA was eliminated, and reverse transcription was performed by using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (Takara). The cDNA obtained was then used for qPCR with SYBR Green I dye, employing specific primers for each gene of interest. The qPCR reactions were set up on ice to prevent degradation and nonspecific reactions, and the mixture was gently mixed before being centrifuged briefly to collect all contents at the bottom of the tubes. The qPCR amplification involved an initial denaturation step, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation, annealing, and extension, with fluorescence measurements taken at the end of each cycle to quantify gene expression levels. All genes were subjected to three independent biological replicates. The normalization of the mRNA levels of specific genes was fulfilled by using GAPDH and β - actin as internal reference genes (Supplementary Material 2 for the results using β - actin as an internal reference). The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences used can be found in Supplementary Table 1. Statistical analysis of the qPCR data was performed using independent sample t-tests to determine the significance of differences in gene expression between the control and test groups. The criteria for selecting the samples included cells that were harvested at similar confluence levels and treated under identical conditions to minimize variability.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) staining

ALP is a key enzyme involved in phosphate metabolism and serves as an enzyme marker for early osteoblast differentiation. It plays a role in bone mineralization by hydrolyzing phosphate groups [22]. This study used ALP staining to evaluate the potential of BMSCs to differentiate into osteoblasts and reflected early differentiation efficiency through changes in their activity. Initially, BMSCs were cultured in ɑ-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin in a humidified incubator at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, cells were passaged using a trypsinization process and subsequently seeded at a density of 2 × 106 cells per well in a 6-well plate. To induce osteogenic differentiation, the medium was replaced with an osteogenic induction medium (HUXMA-90021, Cyagen) after the cells adhered to the wells. The medium was refreshed every three days. After 7 and 14 days of induction, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde and stained using a BCIP/NBT alkaline phosphatase color development kit. The staining process was conducted at room temperature for 2 h, followed by thorough washing with deionized water. The stained cells were then observed under a Nikon Ts2R microscope to assess the intensity and uniformity of the ALP staining, which serves as an indicator of osteogenic differentiation.

Von Kossa staining

Von Kossa technique utilizes silver nitrate to form calcium salt deposits at sites containing calcium phosphate or calcium carbonate, resulting in the formation of black deposits of calcium salts in tissues [22]. This study used Von Kossa staining to observe the mineralization degree of osteoblasts. BMSCs were cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin in a 37 ℃ incubator with 5% CO2. Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, cells were passaged using a trypsinization process and subsequently seeded at a density of 2 × 106 cells per well in a 6-well plate. Following cell adherence, the medium was replaced with an osteogenic differentiation medium, which was refreshed every three days. After 7 and 14 days of induction, cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde, stained using the Von Kossa method to visualize mineral deposits, and analyzed under a Nikon Ts2R microscope.

Primary culture of BMSCs

We collected bone marrow samples from clinical biopsies and patients with idiopathic scoliosis. The samples were placed in a 15 ml centrifuge tube and transferred to an ultraclean workstation on ice. The cells were resuspended in 2 ml α-MEM medium and centrifuged at 220 × g for 5 min to remove the supernatant. We added Ficoll monocyte isolation solution, centrifuged cells at 400 × g for 25 min, then gently removed the monocytes using a pipette and resuspended the cells in PBS. After the centrifugation at 220 × g for 5 min to remove the supernatant, 2 ml of α-MEM medium was added to resuspend the cells. We counted them using a hemocytometer, and inoculated 1 × 106 cells into a 6-well plate. We placed the cells in the incubator for cultivation. After the third day of cultivation, we observed the cell adhesion and replaced the medium with a new culture medium to continue cultivation. Afterward, we observed the growth status of cells daily and changed the culture medium every 3 days.

Cell transfection and lentivirus infection

The siRNA transfection was conducted. Specifically, cells were inoculated on a 6-well plate and cultivated overnight to adhere to the wall (with a cell fusion rate reaching 50–70%). We transfected primary BMSCs with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX and diluted 100 nM siRNA (si-zmynd10, si-IFT88, si-KIF3B) with RNAiMAX in a 1:1 ratio in Opti-MEM. After incubating at room temperature for 20 min to form complexes, they were added to cell culture plates. We collected cells 48 h after transfection for subsequent experiments. All siRNAs were synthesized by Shanghai GenePharma, and three pairs of siRNAs were designed for each target gene to screen for the sequence with the best interference efficiency for subsequent experiments. The detailed information of the siRNA sequence can be found in Supplementary Table 2.

The steps for infecting cells with shRNA are as follows: We seeded the cells into a 6-well plate and cultured them overnight to adhere to the wall. We constructed shRNA lentivirus (packaged by Shanghai GenePharma) based on pLKO.1 vector, and selected the optimal siRNA sequence validated above as the target sequence. We added shRNA lentivirus (at a ratio of 1:100) and added 5 µg/ml polybrene. After 24 h of lentiviral infection, we removed the virus-containing culture medium and added it to the normal culture medium. After 48 h of cell culture, puromycin was added for screening positive cells, followed by amplification culture for subsequent experiments.

Western blotting

Protein extraction was conducted using RIPA buffer (50 mmol/L Tris·HCl pH 8.0, 150 mmol/L NaCl, 1% TritonX-100, 100 µg/ml PMSF, and a protease inhibitor cocktail) for 30 min on ice. Lysates were centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 5 min at 4 ℃, and the supernatants were collected and stored at −20 ℃. Protein concentrations were measured with a BCA assay kit, using a BSA standard curve. For SDS-PAGE electrophoresis, the Mini-PROTEAN system from Bio-Rad was used, with gels ranging from 4% to 16% acrylamide to accommodate different target protein sizes. Following electrophoresis, proteins were transferred onto methanol-activated PVDF membranes (Millipore) using the Trans-Blot SD system (Bio-Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST and incubated with primary antibodies at optimized dilutions. Secondary HRP-conjugated antibodies (anti-mouse HRP, Beyotime A0216; anti-rabbit HRP, Beyotime A0208) were used to detect primary antibodies. Detection was performed via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL, Tanon), and images were captured with the Tanon 5200 imaging system. Protein levels were normalized to GAPDH and quantified using ImageJ software to ensure accurate expression analysis. Antibody information can be found in Supplementary Table 3.

Immunofluorescence (IF) staining

After transfection with sh-zmynd10, sh-IFT88, and sh-KIF3B lentivirus, BMSCs were cultured in a CO2 incubator for 48 h. The culture medium was changed to 0.5% FBS to induce ciliogenesis for 24 h. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized and blocked with cell-blocking buffer (3% BSA/0.3% NP-40/PBS) for 30 min. Primary antibody against Ac-Tubulin (1:1000) was then incubated with cells overnight at 4 ℃, followed by secondary antibody incubation. Samples were counterstained with DAPI nuclear stain. After that, samples were observed under a fluorescence microscope. Image acquisition and analysis were performed using Zen software (ZEISS, version 3.8) through the maximum intensity projection method.

RNA seq and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIZOL Reagent following the manufacturer’s protocol, and its integrity was assessed via an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Only samples with a RIN above 7 were used. RNA was purified using the RNAClean XP Kit and DNase treatment to eliminate DNA contamination. mRNA was enriched and synthesized into cDNA using the VAHTS Stranded mRNA-seq Library Prep Kit. After cleaning the libraries with AMPure XP Beads, quality was verified using the Qubit™ dsDNA HS Assay Kit. Cluster generation was done on an Illumina cBot, and sequencing proceeded on an Illumina platform. Quality-controlled reads were aligned to the hg38 genome with Hisat2. Gene expression was quantified with Stringtie, and differential expression was analyzed using edgeR, followed by GO and KEGG enrichment to identify significant pathways. Based on the STRING database (https://string-db.org/), protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis of cilia genes and signaling pathway-related genes was carried out.

Animal experiments

Zebrafish groups (6 groups) included WT- (Vehicle, Wnt/β− catenin agonist (HLY78)), Wnt/β− catenin inhibitor (JW67)), zmynd10−/− (Vehicle, Wnt/β− catenin agonist (HLY78)), Wnt/β-catenin inhibitor (JW67)).

Zebrafish model detection entailed the following steps. Zmynd10−/− zebrafish gene mutation model was used to observe the phenotypic changes of zebrafish by adding the above-selected signal pathway agonists/inhibitors during the feeding process. The specific method was as follows: The drug mother liquor was diluted with fish water and added to a 6-well plate (10 µM HLY78; 10 µM JW67). 30 zebrafish were introduced into each well. 3 ml of fish water or drug solution was added. After 48 days of feeding in each group, the zebrafish were fixed with molds and scanned using a microCT (Hiscan XM) instrument. The instrument parameters were scanning voltage of 60 kV, current of 200 µA, and scanning resolution of 50 μm. After scanning, Hiscan Analyzer (v3.0) software was used for 3D reconstruction to analyze the skeletal structure of each zebrafish.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8 software was employed for statistical analyses. All experiments were conducted in three independent biological replicates. Student’s t-tests were utilized to assess statistical significance between the two groups, and one-way ANOVA analysis was used for the comparison among multiple groups. Thresholds of statistical significance were set at P < 0.05 and 0.01. Mean ± SEM represented the displayed data.

Results

Biological functional changes of BMSCs in IS

BMSCs were obtained from both healthy individuals and IS patients and validated through cellular characteristics. As depicted in Fig. S1a-b, the isolated and cultured BMSCs showed negative expression for CD34 and CD45, and positive expression for CD90 and CD105, confirming their identity as bone marrow stromal cells. Analysis from Fig. 1a revealed a reduction in the proliferative capacity of IS-BMSCs (BMSCs of IS patients) compared to C-BMSCs (BMSCs of healthy control samples) as detected by MTT assay. Flow cytometry showed (Fig. 1b) an increase in apoptosis in IS-BMSCs compared to C-BMSCs. Moreover, cell migration capability was decreased in IS-BMSCs compared to C-BMSCs, as demonstrated by scratch assays (Fig. 1c). The mRNA expression levels of cell self-renewal-related genes (Nanog, Sox2, Pou5f1) [23] and osteogenic-related genes (Runx2, BSP, ALPL, LPL, and PPAR γ)[24, 25] were evaluated by qPCR. Compared with BMSCs from the control group, the expression of these genes in BMSCs from IS patients was significantly reduced, indicating that BMSCs from IS patients may exhibit a compromised differentiation capacity (Fig. 1d). Finally, the osteogenic differentiation potential of BMSCs derived from IS patients was assessed. Using an osteogenic induction medium, ALP staining, and Von Kossa staining (Fig. 1e-f) on days 7 and 14 revealed reduced ALP activity and bone mineral density in IS-BMSCs compared to control BMSCs, indicating impaired osteogenic differentiation. Furthermore, we analyzed the ciliary length and number of IS patients through IF. Defects in IS-associated primary cilia genes lead to decreased osteogenic differentiation capability of BMSCs

Zmynd10 is involved in the development of cilia, and mutations in zmynd10 are associated with the pathogenesis of IS[26, 27]. The IFT88 is involved in cilia assembly and function and is crucial in promoting cilia development by regulating the stability and transport of cilia proteins [28]. KIF3B is crucial for IFT and cilia assembly, participating in cilia transport and signal transduction, and playing a key role in cilia development [29]. First, we examined the changes in the protein expression of Zmynd10, IFT88, and KIF3B in C-BMSCs and IS-BMSCs. The results showed that compared with C-BMSCs, the protein expression levels of Zmynd10, IFT88 and KIF3B in IS-BMSCs were significantly lower (Fig. 2a). Then, to verify the relationship between primary cilia defects and abnormal differentiation of BMSCs, we established a cell line with primary cilia-related genes (zmynd10, IFT88, and KIF3B) knocked down. We detected the effects of these genes on cilia structure and function. siRNAs targeting primary cilia-related genes, zmynd10, IFT88, and KIF3B were synthesized and transfected into C-BMSCs via liposomes for 48 h, followed by qPCR analysis. Among three siRNAs, the knockout efficiencies of si-zmynd1-2, si-IFT88-1 and si-KIF3B-1 were the best. (Fig. S2a). Screening and verification for primary cilia-mediated osteogenic signaling pathways associated with IS occurrence

Initially, we employed transcriptome sequencing, along with KEGG analysis, to identify primary cilia-mediated osteogenic signaling pathways associated with IS occurrence (Fig. 3a-d). According to the results of KEGG analysis, it was found that several proteins of Wnt signaling, Hippo signaling, and HIF-1 signaling were downregulated (Fig. 3b) (Supplementary Table 4), while multiple proteins of Wnt and Hippo were involved in osteogenesis [30]. Based on the results of transcriptome and pathway analysis (Wnt/Hippo signaling pathway is associated with abnormal osteogenesis), we further focused on osteogenic signaling molecules related to primary cilia regulation and screened out the following 10 candidate genes: BMP 2, fzd 6, WNT 1, WNT 3 A, WNT 3, lrp 5, lrp 6, dkk 1, dkk 4, and axin 2. These 10 proteins were used as subsequent candidates for further study. Subsequently, qPCR was used to examine the expression of 10 molecules related to primary cilia-mediated osteogenic signaling pathways (Fig. 4a). We then evaluated the expression of these six signaling molecules (BMP2, fzd6, WNT1, WNT3A, dkk1, and axin2) in clinical samples. Bone marrow tissue from 15 IS patients was collected, and BMSCs were isolated for qPCR analysis of six molecules related to primary cilia-mediated osteogenic signaling pathways. The results (Fig. 4b) showed significant downregulation of BMP2, fzd6, WNT1and WNT3AmRNA expression, and significant upregulation of dkk1 and axin2 mRNA expression in IS-BMSCs compared to healthy controls. WB of the six molecules (Fig. 4c) revealed significant decreases in BMP2, fzd6, WNT1, and WNT3A protein expression, while dkk1 and axin2 protein expression levels were significantly elevated. Next, the zebrafish model was employed to validate the primary cilia-osteogenic signaling pathway in vivo.

Using the zmynd10-/- IS zebrafish model, we collected zebrafish larvae and extracted bone tissue for total RNA and protein analysis. The results demonstrated that the expression patterns of the six signaling pathway molecules were consistent with those observed in clinical samples. qPCR analysis of zebrafish specimens (Fig. 4d) showed significant reductions in BMP2, fzd6, WNT1and WNT3A mRNA expression, along with a significant increase in dkk1 and axin2 mRNA expression. WB (Fig. 4e) revealed significant decreases in BMP2, fzd6, WNT1, and WNT3A protein expression, whereas dkk1 and axin2 protein expression levels were notably elevated. The analysis provided a detailed examination of how cilia gene defects impact BMSCs and their potential role in the development of IS. Our findings revealed significant changes in gene expression, particularly the decrease in BMP2 protein levels in zebrafish with ZMYND10 knocked down, which were critical for cilia function and osteogenic signaling. In contrast, genes related to compensatory metabolic processes were upregulated. Firstly, ZMYND10 plays a crucial role in cilia formation and cell signaling [31]. Its downregulation may directly impair the BMSCs’ response to biomechanical signals, affecting proper bone formation and potentially leading to skeletal abnormalities observed in IS. On the other hand, BMP2, essential for bone development, shows decreased expression, which may reduce BMSCs’ osteogenic potential, resulting in inhibited bone growth and the progression of scoliosis. Furthermore, the Wnt and Hippo signaling pathways, which are pivotal for tissue growth and contact inhibition, also exhibit downregulation. Inhibiting these pathways could alter the proliferation and differentiation of BMSCs, exacerbating the effects of impaired ciliogenesis and further promoting the pathological process of IS. Simultaneously, we observed an upregulation of genes involved in lysine degradation and glycerolipid metabolism (Fig. 3c) (Supplementary Table 5), suggesting that BMSCs may shift to an alternative metabolic state in the context of impaired ciliary signaling. This metabolic alteration might represent an adaptive response to changes in the cellular environment caused by cilia dysfunction. However, the specific implications of these changes within the IS context remain unclear and warrant further investigation. These findings suggest that the disruptions in the Wnt and BMP pathways, which are crucial for osteogenic differentiation, may be an important factor contributing to the development of IS due to cilia defects.

Validation of the selected signaling pathways’ relevance to IS occurrence in an in vivo model

Finally, we utilized a zebrafish transgenic model to validate the relevance of the selected signaling pathways to IS occurrence. Zebrafish were treated with agonists (HLY78, an activator of the Wnt signaling pathway) and inhibitors (JW67, a specific inhibitor of β-catenin, which can reduce active β-catenin, and subsequently downregulate Wnt target genes) targeting the above-mentioned genes, and their phenotypic changes were observed via CT imaging. We conducted a quantitative analysis of the spinal curvature angles of each zebrafish. The results (Fig. 5a-b) showed that zmynd10-/-zebrafish exhibited pronounced body curvature at 30 h post-treatment. By 48 days post-treatment, zmynd10-/-zebrafish displayed spinal curvature, while the WT group had normal spines. Agonist treatment reduced the degree of spinal curvature, while inhibitor treatment increased spinal curvature. Discussion

During adolescence, the bones are in a stage of rapid growth, and the growing spine is continuously stimulated to respond to the applied mechanical forces to promote growth and bone remodeling [32]. The primary cilia are an outward protruding organelle-like structure that plays an important role in bone mechanical transduction. Ciliary defects in IS bone cells are related to heterozygous gene variations associated with cell mechanical transduction [19]. The primary cilia coordinate the central mechanisms of HH, G protein-coupled receptors, WNT, receptor tyrosine kinases, and TGFβ/BMP signal transduction to control the developmental process, tissue plasticity, and organ function [14]. Additionally, mutations in ciliary movement genes can produce the IS phenotype, while mutations that disrupt ciliary movement genes can produce similar adolescent spinal curvature phenotypes, demonstrating that ciliary movement dysfunction is the cellular biological cause of idiopathic scoliosis [33]. In this project, we first used BMSCs from patients with typical IS to explore whether the biological function of BMSCs in IS diseases has changed. Firstly, we verified the nature of the specimen. Prior studies have underscored the significance of phenotypic markers in identifying the biological functions of BMSCs, which aligns with our detection of CD90 and CD105 expression [34]. Regarding our research question, it was evident that the isolated BMSCs predominantly expressed CD90 and CD105 while showing negative expression for CD34 and CD45, suggesting a robust mesenchymal lineage. The high expression of CD90 and CD105, at 95.3% and 96.2% respectively, might indicate a preserved or enhanced mesenchymal characteristic in the context of IS.

By analyzing the activity of BMSCs, we found that the cell activity of BMSCs in IS patients was significantly reduced, including decreased cell proliferation in vitro, increased apoptosis, decreased migration ability, and decreased expression of bone differentiation genes. These findings suggest that IS may impair the regenerative capabilities of BMSCs, potentially affecting their therapeutic efficacy. Consistent with our project, many studies suggest that alterations in the biological functions of BMSCs may play a crucial role in the development and progression of IS. For instance, decreased osteogenic differentiation capacity of BMSCs has been observed in IS patients, suggesting that disruptions in the function of BMSCs could contribute to the structural change characteristic of scoliosis [15]. The genetic and molecular pathways involved in this process are still under investigation, but early evidence points to the disruption of signaling pathways that are crucial for bone and spine health [35]. Furthermore, the role of cellular signaling in regulating BMSCs’ activity, including pathways like Wnt and BMP (bone morphogenetic protein), which are integral to bone metabolism and vertebral development, is a focal point of the study [36, 37]. Abnormalities in these signaling pathways can directly affect the behavior and function of BMSCs, thereby influencing spine morphology and the risk of scoliosis [38, 39].

The activity of BMSCs is regulated by various cell signaling pathways, including Wnt, Notch, and so on[38, 40]. These signaling pathways may be abnormal in IS patients and result in a decrease in the activity of BMSCs. However, several problems persist in IS research: What specific genetic or molecular pathways are altered in IS patient-derived BMSCs? How do these alterations affect the overall prognosis or treatment outcomes in IS? Addressing these questions is crucial in developing targeted therapies and improving patient management strategies in IS.

Therefore, we used transcriptome sequencing and GO/KEGG analysis to identify the primary cilium-mediated osteogenic signaling pathways associated with the occurrence of IS. The results of GO/KEGG analysis uncovered that multiple proteins of Wnt, Hippo, and HIF-1 signaling pathways were down-regulated, while multiple proteins of Wnt and Hippo were involved in osteogenesis. Based on the primary cilia regulation associated with the IS of the experimental purpose, we selected BMP 2, fzd 6, WNT 1, WNT 3 A, WNT 3, lrp 5, lrp 6, dkk 1, dkk 4, and axin 2. In the detection of relevant cilia-silenced BMSCs, changes in the expression of these genes compared to controls, and interestingly, alterations in these genes were consistent with findings in clinical specimens of IS disease. This finding also confirmed our scientific hypothesis that defects in IS-related ciliary genes can cause a decrease in the osteogenic differentiation ability of BMSCs by affecting the primary cilia structure and function.

Primary cilia are microtubule-based organelles protruding from the cell surface with crucial roles in cellular signaling, motility, and sensory perception. To confirm the effect of IS-related cilia gene defects on the structure and function of fibrils and the relationship with the decrease of osteogenic differentiation ability of BMSCs, we selected three related cilia genes, zmynd10, OVL, and KIF3B. First, we constructed zmynd10-siRNA, IFT88-siRNA, and KIF3B-siRNA, respectively. Three plasmid RNAs were constructed for each type and transfected into cells by liposome to verify the effectiveness of the plasmid, and the plasmid with the best expression effect was selected for subsequent experiments. After detecting the activity of BMSCs transfected with the plasmid, the defects of these three cilia-related genes significantly reduced the activity and cilia development of BMSCs.

Zmynd10 is a conserved nuclear protein encoded by the zmynd10 gene located on chromosome 3p25. Zmynd10 has garnered attention due to its involvement in diverse cellular processes, including transcriptional regulation, chromatin remodeling, and ciliogenesis [26]. The recent study has implicated zmynd10 mutations in the pathogenesis of familial and IS, suggesting its potential as a key player in spine development and homeostasis [27]. Zmynd10 has been implicated in promoting ciliogenesis by regulating the expression of ciliary genes and coordinating intraflagellar transport [26]. Additionally, zmynd10 interacts with various ciliary proteins, such as LRRC6, further highlighting its essential role in cilium assembly and function [41]. Association of zmynd10 mutations with scoliosis: genetic studies have identified zmynd10 mutations in individuals with familial and IS, implicating its role in spine curvature abnormalities [27]. These mutations are predicted to disrupt zmynd10 function, leading to aberrant ciliary signaling and impaired spine development. Furthermore, zmynd10 expression is dysregulated in the spinal tissues of scoliotic patients, underscoring its potential as a diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target [27].

The OVL (Ovarian Cancer-Associated Protein) gene has recently emerged as a novel player in ciliogenesis and its dysregulation, being capable of affecting the normal function of IFT88 and participating in various developmental disorders [28]. Initially identified as a gene associated with ovarian cancer, OVL has garnered attention due to its conserved expression in ciliated tissues and its role in ciliogenesis [42]. OVL is implicated in various developmental processes, including neural tube formation, left-right patterning, and organogenesis, possessing great significance in embryonic development and tissue homeostasis [43–45]. ovltz288b encodes IFT protein Ift88 in zebrafish, which is instrumental for cilia formation [28, 46]. OVL has been shown to localize to the basal body and ciliary axoneme, implicating its involvement in cilia assembly and function [47]. OVL is crucial in promoting ciliogenesis by regulating the stability and trafficking of ciliary proteins [48]. It interacts with intraflagellar transport (IFT) complexes and motor proteins, facilitating the bidirectional movement of cargo along the ciliary axoneme [49].

KIF3B, a member of the kinesin superfamily, serves as a key motor protein involved in ciliary transport and maintenance. As a subunit of the kinesin-II motor complex, KIF3B is essential for IFT and ciliary assembly, playing a pivotal role in ciliogenesis by mediating the anterograde transport of ciliary cargo, including structural components and signaling molecules [29]. KIF3B interacts with various ciliary proteins, including IFT complex components and ciliary receptors, regulating ciliary trafficking and signaling pathways [50]. Genetic studies have linked KIF3B mutations or dysregulation to a spectrum of developmental disorders, including ciliopathies, neural tube defects, and skeletal dysplasias [51]. Dysfunctional KIF3B leads to impaired ciliary transport and signaling, disrupting embryonic patterning and organogenesis, with aberrant KIF3B expression observed in patient-derived tissues affected by developmental disorders, which highlights its potential as a diagnostic marker and therapeutic target [52, 53].

After silencing related cilia genes using siRNA, defects in the IS-related cilia genes were found to affect the assembly of primary cilia and osteogenic differentiation ability. The qPCR assay showed that the mRNA expression of cell self-renewal, Nanog, Sox 2, Pou5f1, and Runx 2, BSP, ALPL, LPL, and PPAR was significantly decreased, which suggests impaired stem cell characteristics and osteogenic potential in these cells.

Finally, we performed in vivo experiments using zebrafish with specific gene knockout. The zmynd10-/-zebrafish gene mutation model was used to observe the phenotypic changes of zebrafish by adding the above-selected signaling pathway agonists/inhibitors during the feeding process. It was found that the agonists could effectively reduce the degree of spinal curvature of zebrafish. After inhibiting these target genes, the degree of spinal curvature of zebrafish was aggravated. The results of in vivo experiments also further demonstrated that the related ciliary genes screened by us can affect the structure and function of fibrils, which leads to the decrease of osteogenic differentiation ability of BMSCs and aggravates the symptoms of IS. This is also the first report on the important role of cilia genes in IS. This study has high clinical value, proffering a new target for treating IS patients. Nevertheless, there are certain limitations in this study. Firstly, the protein interaction network analysis did not show a direct interaction between ZMYND10 and the screened Wnt/BMP pathway proteins. However, changes in Wnt/BMP pathway protein expression were observed after interfering with ZMYND10 expression. Therefore, we speculated that the absence of ZMYND10 may indirectly regulate pathway activity through other cilia-related proteins, or other signaling pathway abnormalities caused by cilia dysfunction may have a secondary impact on the Wnt/BMP pathway. Therefore, in future research, we will focus on ZMYND10 and verify its specific mechanism through Co-IP or dual fluorescence reporter genes.

In clinical applications, more studies should be conducted to further elucidate the role of these genes in BMSCs and their potential value, and to assess the risk of targeting these genes in treatment regimens. Future studies should focus on replicating these findings under broader conditions and exploring the underlying mechanisms that lead to observed changes in gene expression, which will aid in determining whether the observed changes are a direct result of IS or are affected by other confounding factors.

Conclusion

The decrease in BMSCs’ activity is one of the causes of IS in patients. Among them, IS-related cilia gene defects are an essential factor in the reduction of the osteogenic differentiation ability of BMSCs. Ciliary gene defects represent a novel pathway for IS research, revealing the genetic basis of spinal deformity. Elucidation of the molecular mechanisms underlying ciliary dysfunction can provide broad insights into the pathogenesis of IS and may provide potential intervention targets for future personalized therapies.
