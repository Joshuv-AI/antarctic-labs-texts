Deep learning based local feature classification to automatically identify single molecule fluorescence events
Authors: Shuqi Zhou, Yu Miao, Haoren Qiu, Yuan Yao, Wenjuan Wang, Chunlai Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong-term single-molecule fluorescence measurements are widely used powerful tools to study the conformational dynamics of biomolecules in real time to further elucidate their conformational dynamics. Typically, thousands or even more single-molecule traces are analyzed to provide statistically meaningful information, which is labor-intensive and can introduce user bias. Recently, several deep-learning models have been developed to automatically classify single-molecule traces. In this study, we introduce DEBRIS (Deep lEarning Based fRagmentatIon approach for Single-molecule fluorescence event identification), a deep-learning model focusing on classifying local features and capable of automatically identifying steady fluorescence signals and dynamically emerging signals of different patterns. DEBRIS efficiently and accurately identifies both one-color and two-color single-molecule events, including their start and end points. By adjusting user-defined criteria, DEBRIS becomes the pioneer in using a deep learning model to accurately classify four different types of single-molecule fluorescence events using the same trained model, demonstrating its universality and ability to enrich the current toolbox.Subject terms: Single-molecule biophysics, Biophysical methods

Introduction

Single-molecule fluorescence techniques, particularly single-molecule fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET), are advanced biophysical tools for investigating the dynamics and interactions of biomolecules at the nanoscale1. Single-molecule FRET relies on the principle of energy transfer from a donor fluorophore to an acceptor fluorophore when they are within a few nanometers of each other. The FRET efficiency is a useful tool for inferring relative distances between labeling sites and the conformational dynamics of molecules, offering insights into the dynamic processes that were once difficult to access through ensemble methods2,3. Thus, single-molecule FRET and fluorescence tools have been widely used to examine the real-time conformational dynamics of proteins and nucleic acids, including the ribosome4–7, Cas proteins8–10, membrane proteins11–14, chromatin15, RNA riboswitches16,17 and so on. They have revealed inherent heterogeneity or transient intermediates hidden under other ensemble measurements, providing insights into their molecular mechanisms.

Long-term single-molecule FRET measurements are usually conducted with a total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscope, tracking changes in the brightness of individual fluorophores over time to reveal the dynamic information of the corresponding molecules. Although high-throughput TIRF-based single-molecule FRET experiments allow for massive data collection, they also pose significant challenges in data analysis and interpretation due to potential artifacts or ambiguities18. The analysis process involves classifying single-molecule trajectories and identifying potential conformational states and transitions among them within each trace. Typically, single-molecule FRET events are identified through manual or semi-automatic methods, such as by setting thresholds19–22 or grouping signals23. However, these methods often require user-defined parameters and may introduce bias. When dealing with multiple states, rapid transitions, and complex fluorescence signals24,25, manual analysis can be labor-intensive and heavily reliant on the analyst’s expertise. This increases the risk of errors and reduces the reproducibility of results26–28.

Recently, deep learning models have made remarkable progress in the field of single-molecule fluorescence29–31. Deep learning models have improved the analysis process by reducing human error and bias and speeding up the process27,32. Leading models in this field include DeepFRET33, AutoSIM34, and Deep-LASI35. DeepFRET rapidly categorizes single-molecule FRET traces, offering an easy-to-use tool for non-experts. AutoSIM innovatively converts one-dimensional fluorescence intensity data into two-dimensional images to facilitate trace classification. Deep-LASI is a pioneer model in sorting three-color single-molecule traces, determining correction factors and transitions among different FRET states. These deep learning models were trained using user-classified or simulated traces and provided as compiled software for non-experts. However, it is difficult to fully comprehend which features in single-molecule traces were learned by these models. Optimizing these models further, such as modifying or adding classification criteria, for their own unique dataset is inconvenient for users. Thus, models trained on limited datasets are unlikely to fit all user’s needs, due to their unique experimental conditions and designs.

Here, we have developed a Deep lEarning Based fRagmentatIon approach for Single-molecule fluorescence event identification (DEBRIS). DEBRIS is the pioneering method to achieve automatic classification of dynamically emerging events by classifying short-fragment traces instead of long-duration ones, setting it apart from other methods. Similar to identifying a large object from the local features of its individual parts, DEBRIS first classifies fragments of long single-molecule traces into defined categories. Each trace is then further classified, and desired events are identified based on user-defined criteria. The user-defined criteria allow users to cope with a wide range of different experimental designs and signal patterns, thereby enhancing the applicability of the DEBRIS. This approach improves the performance and universality of the DEBRIS, which can analyze traces collected by the TIRF microscopy under both equilibrium and non-equilibrium conditions and achieve 90% agreement with manual classification.

Results

Training and workflow of DEBRIS

The fundamental logic of DEBRIS is that all long single-molecule traces are constructed from short fragments that belong to finite categories of local features. Thus, intensity-time traces can be converted to pattern-time traces with a finite number of categories. From these pattern-time traces, desired single-molecule events can be identified using user-defined criteria. The DEBRIS model’s deep neural networks were trained using simulated traces, which contained seven categories of two-channel short-segment intensity-time traces to mimic local features that are commonly observed in single-molecule two-color fluorescence experiments (Fig. 1a, details in methods). DEBRIS’s neural network architecture comprises three layers of one-dimensional convolution units, which are adept at extracting features at different levels from the data, and two layers of Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) units to capture the long-term dependencies in sequential data (see Supplementary Fig. S1 in supplementary information file for the structure of the network). The trained neural network was tested using newly simulated fragments as the validation dataset, whose prediction accuracy was ~98% (Supplementary Fig. S2).

Training and use of DEBRIS.

a Examples of simulated normalized short fragments belonging to seven categories of local features, including ‘FRET’, ‘Donor disappear (Ddis)’, ‘Donor appear (Dapp)’, ‘Acceptor disappear (Adis)’, ‘Acceptor appear (Aapp)’, ‘Non-FRET’ and ‘Junk’. b The DEBRIS model employs a sliding window classification method in its application. Initially, the long-term intensity-time trace is segmented into 10-frame sliding windows of the same length as the training dataset. Subsequently, each 10-frame segment is predicted by the trained network, and the pattern classification is output. Finally, all patterns are merged to generate the long-term pattern-time trace.

The DEBRIS model employs a sliding time-window classification approach during its application, which facilitates the expeditious prediction of segment-based neural networks and applies to traces of varying lengths for the precise classification of local features in a sequential manner. Firstly, it analyzes single-molecule traces in 10-frame sliding windows, which are the same length as the training dataset. This process converts the intensity-time traces to pattern-time traces frame by frame (Fig. 1b). Consequently, the resulting pattern traces are 9 frames shorter than the original traces. User-defined criteria were then applied to further classify them into different groups and to identify single-molecule events.

Based on our own empirical criteria, steady single-molecule FRET traces exhibit fluorescence and FRET signals at the moment of data collection and were classified into three groups named: ‘donor photobleaching’, ‘acceptor photobleaching’, and ‘discarded’ (Fig. 2d). Traces classified as ‘donor photobleaching’ and ‘acceptor photobleaching’ were genuine FRET traces that exhibited fluorescence and FRET signals at the time of data collection, and whose FRET signals were terminated by donor photobleaching and acceptor photobleaching, respectively. The following strict criteria were applied to ensure the accuracy of the classification process. 1) Before the donor photobleaching, the donor and acceptor have to exhibit either steady intensities or variable intensities with anti-correlation behaviors. 2) Photobleaching occurs in one step and reduces the intensity of the corresponding channel to the background level. 3) Donor photobleaching causes the simultaneous disappearance of both donor and acceptor intensities. 4) Acceptor photobleaching causes the disappearance of acceptor intensity accompanied by an increase in donor intensity, which remains close to constant before photobleaching. A one-step donor photobleaching must occur after the acceptor photobleaching to confirm the presence of only one donor fluorophore. 5) There should be no continuous ‘Junk’ pattern (> 4 frames) before photobleaching. 6) There should be no continuous ‘Non-FRET’ pattern (> 1 frame) before the disappearance of FRET caused by photobleaching. Traces that violated any of these criteria were discarded (Supplementary Fig. S3). It is important to note that users have the ability to adjust these criteria to better suit their specific needs.

Benchmark of DEBRIS against manual selection

To evaluate the performance of DEBRIS, we collected five single-molecule two-color FRET datasets using three different biological samples (Fig. 2a–c), the ribosome36, the β2 adrenergic receptor37 and DNA Holliday Junction38. ~1500 traces were classified by two experts manually in each dataset. When we used the manually classified results as the benchmark, the accuracy of our DEBRIS model was approximately 90% for all five datasets (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. S4a). Manual classified results performed by two independent experts also showed only about 90% agreement (Supplementary Figs. S4d–S4f), indicating that the DEBRIS model performs similarly to a well-trained expert in analyzing all five datasets. The time points of photobleaching, which serve as the boundary between the FRET segment and the no FRET segment, were determined by the DEBRIS model and agreed well with those picked manually by experts (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. S4b). The one-frame uncertainty in defining the photobleaching time points may be caused by the one-frame midpoints in the one-step photobleaching traces (Supplementary Fig. S5) as the actual photobleaching occurs within these frames. Finally, the FRET distributions calculated by the DEBRIS model exhibited 99% or greater similarity to those of manually selected benchmarks across all five datasets (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. S4c). This highlights the performance of DEBRIS, which is comparable to expert users in terms of reproducibility, but with greater efficiency.

Application of DEBRIS in single-molecule fluorescent assays

Next, we showcase the versatility and power of DEBRIS in classifying single-molecule fluorescence traces, enabling multi-scenario applications by adjusting the trace classification criteria with the same original training network. DEBRIS can adapt to both single-color and two-color single-molecule traces of varying lengths. It can handle steady fluorescence signals at equilibrium, as well as dynamically emerging signals under equilibrium or non-equilibrium conditions.

Analysis of two-color traces of steady fluorescence signals

Steady fluorescence signals are defined as single-molecule traces that exhibit fluorescence and FRET signals at the moment of data collection, regardless of whether they have single or multiple FRET states (see example traces in Fig. 2d). It is important to note that during the measurement, signals may disappear due to photobleaching, but fluorescence signals should not appear in the middle of the measurement. This is the most common scenario for single-molecule FRET measurements. Here, we used β2 adrenergic receptor (β2AR 148C–378C) prepared in our recent work as an example. This receptor mainly stays in its high-FRET state in the presence of 200 μM isoproterenol and shifts to its low-FRET state with increasing GsGDP (Supplementary Fig. S6). FRET distributions extracted from DEBRIS-classified traces fully restored such trends (Fig. 3a). We determined the apparent EC50 value of 88 ± 1 nM (Fig. 3b), compared to the reported value of 100 ± 3 nM39. Therefore, DEBRIS is highly robust in analyzing two-color traces of steady fluorescence signals.

Analysis of two-color traces of dynamically emerging signals

Dynamically emerging signals are identified from the single-molecule traces that exhibit no fluorescence signals at the moment of data collection. Subsequently, the fluorescence signals may appear during the measurement, followed by steady or variable FRET efficiency until the disappearance of fluorescence (see example traces in Fig. 4b). Interactions between diffusing molecules and immobilized molecules cause these behaviors under equilibrium or non-equilibrium conditions. Examples include the initiation and elongation stages of the ribosome5,39, β2-adrenergic receptor signaling40, and target recognition and cleavage of Cas proteins38,41. These emerging signals provide important dynamic information about molecules in action. Following an evaluation of DEBRIS in comparison to existing deep learning models, it was found that DEBRIS demonstrated superior performance in the analysis of dynamically emerging signals.

Our test dataset was collected using our previously published design41. This design employs Cas12a effector proteins, including Acidaminococcus sp. Cas12a (AsCas12a)42,43, Lachnospiraceae bacterium ND2006 Cas12a (LbCas12a)44 and Francisella novicida U112 Cas12a (FnCas12a)42,45, which can be guided by crRNA to recognize DNA target sites adjacent to PAM sites to initiate R-loop formation and double-stranded DNA cleavage. Based on previous assay41, single-molecule FRET between Cy3 labeled Cas12a/crRNA complex and immobilized Cy5 labeled DNA can capture the Cas12a/crRNA/DNA complex formation, non-target strand cleavage, target strand cleavage, and DNA fragment release sequentially (Fig. 4a). Usually, we need to collect movies with a length of 8000 frames under these experimental conditions to observe the whole dynamic process. Long traces extracted from these movies pose a challenge to current deep-learning models. To apply the DEBRIS model, as described above, the sliding window prediction approach was used to convert the intensity-time traces to pattern-time traces frame by frame (Fig. 4b). Further event identification was performed using user-defined criteria. The following criteria were applied: 1) A dynamically emerging event is identified from the sudden appearance of donor intensity to the sudden disappearance of donor intensity, with no acceptor intensities present right before or after the event. 2) At the first time point of the emerging event, the acceptor intensity may appear simultaneously with the donor. After the last time point of the event, the acceptor intensity, if present, must disappear simultaneously with the donor. 3) During the event, the donor and acceptor intensities must exhibit either steady intensities or variable intensities with anti-correlation behavior before donor photobleaching. 4) The disappearance of acceptor intensity must be accompanied by an increase in donor intensity. 5) There should be no continuous ‘Junk’ pattern (>4 frames). 6) There should be no continuous ‘Non-FRET’ pattern (>1 frame) before the disappearance of FRET caused by photobleaching.

Using DEBRIS with these criteria, we automatically identified the emerging events of Cas12a, with which time-dependent FRET probability density plots were generated by synchronizing all identified emerging events at the initiation or termination time points of the FRET signals (Fig. 4c). Consistent with the previous report, after the simultaneous appearance of fluorescence and FRET signals, all FRET density plots showed a gradual transition from a short-lived low-FRET state (S1) to medium-FRET (S2) and high-FRET states (S3 and S4). These states correspond to conformational transitions of Cas12a complexes from initial R-loop formation to R-loop extension, non-target strand cleavage, and target strand cleavage, respectively. As anticipated, introducing mismatches between the crRNA and target DNA (3 MM, 6 MM, and 7 MM) did not affect the appearance time (Supplementary Fig. S7), but impeded the transition toward the high-FRET cleavage state (Fig. 4c). Again, this is consistent with the previous report, which found that LbCas12a spent less time in the high-FRET state than AsCas12a and that the FRET value of the FnCas12a medium-FRET state was higher than that of AsCas12a. In addition, the FRET dwell times of LbCas12a and FnCas12a were shorter than those of AsCas12a, probably due to their fast cleavage product release rate46, resulting in the zero FRET population appearing ~5 s after binding of LbCas12a and FnCas12a (Fig. 4c). In addition, DEBRIS can accurately identify short events with a minimum of 5 frames. Thus, we revealed two types of single-molecule traces in one experiment (Supplementary Fig. S8). Short events (<10 s) with no FRET changes were likely caused by non-productive test binding of AsCas12a, whereas long events (≥10 s) exhibiting increasing FRET and disappearance of acceptor signal at the high-FRET state represented productive of AsCas12a leading to DNA cleavage.

Analysis of one-color traces of steady fluorescence signals

One-color single-molecule fluorescence experiments capture signals from a single fluorophore, which are typically used to characterize the photoproperties of fluorophores38 and simple binding-dissociation events47. In this study, we applied DEBRIS to select Cy3 traces that exhibit single-step photobleaching as a demonstration (Fig. 5a). The DEBRIS model was trained using two-color traces. However, we discovered that it can also handle one-color traces successfully with one additional step, which was to duplicate the single-channel traces into two-channel traces. Thus, ‘artificial-generated’ two-channel traces formed by two identical single-channel traces were predicted by the DEBRIS to generate corresponding pattern traces. Due to these unique properties, only five of the seven categories of local features shown in Fig. 1a remain. Specifically, ‘Acceptor disappearance (Adis)’ and ‘Acceptor appearance (Aapp)’ have been eliminated, while ‘FRET’, ‘Donor disappear (Ddis)’, ‘Donor appear (Dapp)’, and ‘Non-FRET’ features now correspond to ‘Signal (Sig)’, ‘Signal disappear (Sdis)’, ‘Signal appear (Sapp)’ and ‘Non-signal (Non-Sig)’ features of one-color traces, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S9). The steady one-color traces were categorized into two groups: ‘selected’ and ‘discarded’ based on the following criteria (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. S10): 1) Photobleaching occurs in one step and reduces the intensity to the background level. 2) There should be no continuous ‘Junk’ pattern (>4 frames) before the signal disappears. 3) There should be no continuous ‘Non-Sig’ pattern (>1 frame) before the signal disappears.

To evaluate the performance of DEBRIS on one-color traces, we tested three samples: Cy3-PCB, Cy3-POD, and Cy3-FBP labeled DNA. An expert user manually classified approximately 1500 traces in each dataset to serve as the benchmark. The accuracy of our DEBRIS model was approximately 94% for all three datasets (Fig. 5c). The time points of photobleaching determined by the DEBRIS model agreed well with those picked manually (Fig. 5d). The total photon number, bleaching time, intensity, and signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) were quantified from selected single-molecule traces. The values determined by the DEBRIS model were found to be very close to those determined by the expert (Supplementary Fig. S11). Although the DEBRIS is trained by two-color traces, it is fully compatible with single-molecule one-color traces.

Analysis of one-color traces of dynamically emerging signals

As previously mentioned, traces that contain dynamically emerging signals do not exhibit any fluorescence signals at the moment of data collection and, instead, fluorescent signals appear during data acquisition. To identify one-color emerging events, artificial two-color traces were generated by duplicating one-color traces, processed by the DEBRIS, and screened using the following criteria (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. S12): 1) A dynamically emerging event is identified from the sudden appearance of fluorescence intensity to the sudden disappearance of fluorescence intensity. 2) There should be no continuous ‘Junk’ pattern (>4 frames).

In our previous study, we used one-color single-molecule assays to examine the one-dimensional diffusion-driven target search of AsCas12a on double-stranded (dsDNA)41. The target dsDNA was immobilized on the microscope slide. When a labeled free-diffusing AsCas12a recognizes and binds to the target, it generates a transient binding event identified as an emerging event (Fig. 6a). The DEBRIS method can accurately identify multiple emerging events within the same trace (Fig. 6c). We quantified the effective target search rates by extracting the intervals between sample injection and signal appearance, as well as the intervals between two adjacent emerging events, which we termed as the appearance time (Supplementary Fig. S13). We determined the one-dimensional diffusion lengths of AsCas12a on PAM upstream and PAM downstream to be 11 ± 3 bp and 33 ± 7 bp, respectively, by quantifying the target search rates under different extension lengths in dsDNA. These values are almost identical to our previously reported values (11 ± 3 bp and 33 ± 3 bp), confirming the robustness of the DEBRIS (Fig. 6d).

Discussion

In summary, DEBRIS is a pioneering deep learning-based approach that identifies single-molecule events through local feature classification and user-defined criteria. As in the well-known game Tetris, seven distinct types of tetrominoes can form various structures. Similarly, complex long single-molecule fluorescence traces are assembled from short fragments belonging to a limited number of local features. In this study, we classified the local features of two-color single-molecule traces into seven types. DEBRIS is a deep learning-based model that can classify local features and identify steady events and dynamically emerging events within two-color single-molecule traces of varying lengths based on user-defined criteria. It should be noted that the current version of DEBRIS is trained to analyze two-color fluorescence traces under single laser excitation, which is also fully compatible with one-color traces. However, the deep-learning model needs to be retrained to analyze three or more colors traces and single-molecule traces under Alternating Laser Excitation (ALEX) mode using two or more lasers. Also, the current DEBRIS does not account for quenching and protein-induced fluorescence enhancement (PIFE) phenomena. The traces displaying these phenomena were discarded by our stringent criteria and can be selected if the criteria are adjusted. To maximize the accessibility of the DEBRIS model, we offer MATLAB source codes for fragment simulation, deep learning network training, and single-molecule fluorescence event identification for four different scenarios presented in our study. Thus, users can optimize the DEBRIS deep learning model or adjust user-defined criteria for their unique experimental conditions and designs. In summary, DEBRIS offers users a wider range of options to choose from for their desired and designed single-molecule signaling patterns. This will significantly aid and expedite the application of single-molecule methods under various conditions.

Methods

Simulation of short fragments for training

Seven categories of two-channel ten-frame-length intensity-time traces were simulated to mimic local features commonly observed in single-molecule two-color fluorescence experiments. These categories include ‘FRET’, ‘Donor disappear (Ddis)’, ‘Donor appear (Dapp)’, ‘Acceptor disappear (Adis)’, ‘Acceptor appear (Aapp)’, ‘Non-FRET’ and ‘Junk’ (Fig. 1a). Except for the ‘Junk’ category, intensity is set to 0 with Gaussian noise (standard deviation = 0.1) when there is no signal, and total intensities of the donor and acceptor channels are set to 1 with Gaussian noise (the standard deviation = 0.125 in each channel) when there is fluorescence signal. ‘Junk’ traces were simulated by introducing drifting and sudden signal changes.

Network training

The neural network was constructed and trained using the DeepNetworkDesigner toolbox in MATLAB (2022b). The network, illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1, consisted of three one-dimensional convolutional layers with 32, 64, and 128 filters of size 3, respectively. Each convolutional layer was followed by a Leaky ReLU layer with a leak rate of 0.01. The sequences were processed by two bidirectional long short-term memory (biLSTM) layers with 128 hidden units, which were complemented by a dropout layer with a factor of 0.5 to prevent overfitting. The network also consisted of a fully connected layer with 8 outputs, a softmax layer, and a classification layer for multi-class classification. The training dataset comprised 4 million simulated fragments. The training parameters included the Adam optimizer (initial learning rate 0.001, factor for dropping the learning rate 0.7 every 5 epochs) with a total of 100 epochs. The mini-batch size for each training iteration was 500, and the training fragments were shuffled before each training epoch.

Varying the standard deviation of background Gaussian noises within the range of 0.08–0.125, varying the standard deviation of signal Gaussian noises within the range of 0.1–0.167, or removing all three one-dimensional convolutional layers all only have minor effects on the performance of our DEBRIS model, confirming its robustness.

Application of DEBRIS to identify events

DEBRIS utilizes a sliding window approach to classify short fragments of every 10 consecutive frames (1–10, 2–11, 3–12, ……, n–n + 9, etc.) into one of the pre-defined categories. This converts single-molecule intensity-time traces into pattern-time traces. For two-color traces, the corresponding pattern-time traces contain seven categories defined in simulation, including ‘FRET’, ‘Donor disappear (Ddis)’, ‘Donor appear (Dapp)’, ‘Acceptor disappear (Adis)’, ‘Acceptor appear (Aapp)’, ‘Non-FRET’ and ‘Junk’. For one-color traces, the signals were duplicated to generate artificial two-color traces containing two identical intensity channels. The fragments of these traces were classified by DEBRIS. Due to these unique properties, their corresponding pattern-time traces contain five categories, which are ‘Signal (Sig)’, ‘Signal disappear (Sdis)’, ‘Signal appear (Sapp)’, ‘Non-signal (Non-Sig)’, and ‘Junk’. User-defined criteria were used to identify desired single-molecule events from pattern-time traces. Traces that did not meet the user-defined criteria were labeled as ‘discarded’.

User-defined criteria for classification

The gamma factor18 value has been used to compensate for variations in sensitivity between different detection channels. Our standard datasets (Supplementary Fig. S14) demonstrate that DEBRIS can tolerate the variation of the input gamma factor value to a certain extent. In addition, DEBRIS calculates the gamma factor from the ‘acceptor photobleaching’ traces.

To analyze steady two-color traces, a threshold of 3 frames is established for the ‘Ddis’ feature, indicating that only ‘Ddis’ feature lasting 3 frames or longer will be classified as reliable donor photobleaching events. Similarly, the ‘Adis’ feature also has a threshold of 3 frames, while the ‘Junk’ feature is set at 5 frames, and the ‘Non-FRET’ feature threshold is 2 frames. For the analysis of steady one-color traces, the ‘Sdis’ category retains a threshold of 3 frames, and the ‘Junk’ feature is set at 5 frames.

In the assessment of dynamically emerging events in two-color traces, the threshold for the ‘Dapp/Aapp’ feature is established at 5 frames, and ‘Ddis’ is also set to 5 frames. The ‘Adis’ feature maintains a threshold of 3 frames, with the ‘Junk’ feature set at 5 frames, and the ‘Non-FRET’ feature at 2 frames. For dynamically emerging events in one-color traces, the thresholds are set as follows: ‘Sapp’ at 5 frames, ‘Sdis’ at 5 frames, ‘Junk’ at 5 frames, and ‘Non-Sig’ at 2 frames.

Holliday Junction sample preparation: for Cy3 labeling of the e-strand and Cy5 labeling of the w-strand, an overnight incubation process using ~500 µM of amino-modified DNA and 5 mM of NHS-active dye (Lumiprobe) in 0.1 M NaHCO3 was used. Excess dye was subsequently removed by ethanol precipitation. Labeling efficiency was quantified by Nanodrop measurements of A550, A650, and A260 to calculate the dye to DNA concentration ratio. DNA Holliday junctions were formed by mixing an unlabeled s-strand, a Cy3 labeled e-strand, a Cy5 labeled w-strand, and a biotinylated n-strand, heated at 80 °C for 10 min, and then gradually cooled to room temperature at a rate of −1 °C/min. Excess unlabeled s-strand (2 folds) was used.

Labeled DNA for one-color single molecule measurements: for dye conjugation, maleimide or aryl-modified dyes (in excess of 10 folds) were incubated with thiol-containing DNA (w-SH-strand) and 1 mM TCEP at room temperature (~23 °C) overnight. Excess dye was subsequently removed by ethanol precipitation. Concentrations of DNA and dye were determined by A260 and A550 via a Nanodrop, respectively, from which labeling efficiency was calculated. Labeled dsDNAs were formed by mixing Cy3 labeled w-strand with biotinylated complementary ssDNA (n-strand-biotin) in a 1:2 ratio, heated at 80 °C for 10 min, and then gradually cooled to room temperature at a rate of −1 °C/min.

Slide preparation for single-molecule experiments

The microscope slides and coverslips underwent sequential sonication in ethanol (10 min), 0.2 M KOH (20 min), and ethanol again (10 min) at 40 °C. After washing, the slides were treated with an aminosilane reagent (1 ml 3-aminopropyltriethoxysilane, 5 ml acetic acid, and 94 ml methanol) overnight at room temperature. After washing, the slides and coverslips were further treated with PEG (from Laysan Bio, Inc., containing 20% w/w mPEG-Succinimidyl Valerate, MW 2000, and 1% w/w Biotin-PEG-SC, MW 2,000) in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate (pH 8.3) at room temperature for 3 h. After drying with clean N2, slides, and coverslips were placed in 50 mL falcon tubes, vacuum-sealed in plastic bags, and stored at −20 °C.

Single-molecule measurements

Single-molecule fluorescence and FRET measurements were performed using a custom-built objective-type TIRF microscope with a Nikon Eclipse Ti-E, an EMCCD camera, and 488 nm, 532 nm, and 640 nm excitation lasers at room temperature. PEG-passivated glass slides were first incubated with streptavidin for specific attachment of biotinylated biomolecules. Labeled DNA and proteins samples were excited, and fluorescence was collected and spectrally separated using a Dual-Viewer spectral splitter (Photometrics, Inc., Tucson, AZ) with a specific dichroic mirror (T635lpxr, Chroma) and bandpass filters (ET585/65 m, ET700/75 m, Chroma). Oxygen scavengers and triplet-state quenchers, including 3 mg/mL glucose, 10 units/mL glucose oxidase (Sigma-Aldrich), 1200 units /mL catalase (Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mM cyclooctatetraene (COT, Sigma-Aldrich), 1 mM 4-nitrobenzylalcohol (NBA, Sigma-Aldrich), 1.5 mM 6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethyl-chromane-2-carboxylic acid (Trolox, Sigma-Aldrich), were included in the buffers during fluorescence signals recording. For steady single-molecule two-color FRET measurements, the Holliday junction DNA samples were prepared in a low-salt buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2) or a high-salt buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 500 mM MgCl2) and recorded under 10 mW 532 nm laser power at 10 frame s-1 with 80 s acquisition time.

For steady single-molecule one-color fluorescence measurements (dye characterization), the Cy3-labeled double-stranded DNA samples were prepared in a buffer containing 50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.5 and 100 mM NaCl and recorded under 10 mW 532 nm laser power at 10 frame s-1with 80 s acquisition time.

The ribosome pre-translocation (PRE) complex containing Cy3 labeled L11 protein and Cy5 labeled Val-tRNAVal in the A-site was prepared according to the previous procedure36 and used for steady single-molecule two-color FRET measurements. Data was recorded under 10 mW 532 nm laser power at 10 frame s−1 with 42.5 s acquisition time.

Following our published procedure37, two sites, 148 C, and 378 C, of β2AR were labeled with Alexa 555 and Cy5. Then, steady single-molecule two-color FRET measurements captured dynamic structural changes of the β2AR carboxyl terminus (CT) in the apo state or the presence of saturating concentrations of formoterol. Conformational dynamics of β2AR-CT under a series of GsGDP concentrations with 200 μM isoproterenol were also captured. Data was recorded under 10 mW 532 nm laser power at 10 frame s−1 with 60 s acquisition time.

For Cas12a-related experiments, Cy3 was labeled at the 3′ end of crRNA, and Cy5 was labeled at the target strand located 28 nt downstream of the PAM41,48. To capture two-color dynamically emerging signals, Cy5-labeled dsDNA was immobilized at the slide surface and Cas12a/Cy3-crRNA was flowed into the channel while recording the single-molecule signals and recorded under 2 mW 532 nm laser power at 2 frame s−1, acquisition time was 750 s. To capture one-color dynamically emerging signals, unlabeled dsDNA was immobilized and Cas12a/Cy3-crRNA was flowed into the channel while recording the single-molecule signals and recorded under 100 mW 532 nm laser power at 100 frame s−1, acquisition time was 80 s. Details of sample preparation and sequences of DNA and RNA were shown in our previous report41.

Single-molecule FRET data analysis

Movies were analyzed by a custom-made ImageJ plugin. Fluorescence spots were fitted by a 2D Gaussian function, and the background subtracted total volume of the 2D Gaussian peak was used as raw fluorescence intensity I. The donor and acceptor spots were matched using a variant of the Hough transform. All single-molecule fluorescence traces were classified by users or by the DEBRIS model with user-defined criteria. Apparent FRET efficiency (E) was calculated as IA/(IA + ID), in which IA and ID are intensities of acceptor and donor spots, respectively.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were conducted using the Origin 2021 (OriginLab Corporation) software. Three repeats were performed to ensure the consistency and reliability of the data. No data were excluded from the analyses, and the sample sizes were not predetermined using statistical methods.
