Predictive gene expression signature diagnoses neonatal sepsis before clinical presentation

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundNeonatal sepsis is a deadly disease with non-specific clinical signs, delaying diagnosis and treatment. There remains a need for early biomarkers to facilitate timely intervention. Our objective was to identify neonatal sepsis gene expression biomarkers that could predict sepsis at birth, prior to clinical presentation.MethodsAmong 720 initially healthy full-term neonates in two hospitals (The Gambia, West Africa), we identified 21 newborns who were later hospitalized for sepsis in the first 28 days of life, split into early-onset sepsis (EOS, onset ≤7 days of life) and late-onset sepsis (LOS, onset 8–28 days of life), 12 neonates later hospitalized for localized infection without evidence of systemic involvement, and 33 matched control neonates who remained healthy. RNA-seq was performed on peripheral blood collected at birth when all neonates were healthy and also within the first week of life to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Machine learning methods (sPLS-DA, LASSO) identified genes expressed at birth that predicted onset of neonatal sepsis at a later time.FindingsNeonates who later developed EOS already had ∼1000 DEGs at birth when compared to control neonates or those who later developed a localized infection or LOS. Based on these DEGs, a 4-gene signature (HSPH1, BORA, NCAPG2, PRIM1) for predicting EOS at birth was developed (training AUC = 0.94, sensitivity = 0.93, specificity = 0.92) and validated in an external cohort (validation AUC = 0.72, sensitivity = 0.83, and specificity = 0.83). Additionally, during the first week of life, EOS disrupted expression of >1800 genes including those influencing immune and metabolic transitions observed in healthy controls.InterpretationDespite appearing healthy at birth, neonates who later developed EOS already had distinct whole blood gene expression changes at birth, which enabled the development of a 4-gene predictive signature for EOS. This could facilitate early recognition and treatment of neonatal sepsis, potentially mitigating its long-term sequelae.FundingCIHR and NIH/NIAID.Keywords: Neonatal sepsis, Transcriptomics, Predictive biomarkers, Ontogeny, Machine learning

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Neonatal sepsis is a disease with high mortality and long-term developmental consequences but is difficult to diagnose due to non-specific clinical signs. There is a need for better diagnostic biomarkers that are effective as early as possible, ideally predictive biomarkers right at birth. Gene expression biomarkers are an exciting new field of diagnostics with potentially increased sensitivity and specificity. We searched PubMed for research articles published in English between Jan 1, 2010, and Mar 30, 2024, with the terms: “neonate”, “sepsis OR septic shock”, AND “transcriptomics OR multiarray OR RNAseq”. While there are several studies that investigate gene expression biomarkers in neonatal sepsis, no studies analyzed gene expression in term neonates at birth prior to neonates showing clinical signs of sepsis; instead, these existing studies analyzed neonates after they presented to hospital with signs of sepsis.

Added value of this study

In this multicentre, prospective study of term neonates born in The Gambia with a validation cohort from an external dataset in neonates from the United States, we derived and validated a four-gene expression signatures from peripheral blood for neonatal sepsis. This signature could predict, at birth when neonates were still healthy, which neonates would later develop early onset sepsis within 7 days of life. The signature had an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.94, sensitivity of 0.93, and specificity of 0.92 and was validated in an external cohort (validation AUROC = 0.72, sensitivity = 0.83, and specificity = 0.83).

Implications of all the available evidence

This predictive gene expression signature for neonatal sepsis can identify which neonate, at birth, would later develop early-onset sepsis, while the neonate still appeared clinically well. Our findings provide an early diagnostic tool of neonatal sepsis that could possibly be used in combination with other screening tools at birth. This would be essential in facilitating timely therapeutic interventions in order to decrease mortality and the risk of developing long-term sequelae due to neonatal sepsis.

Introduction

Sepsis causes life-threatening organ failure due to dysregulated host responses to infection1 and manifests distinctly by age.2 Neonatal sepsis affects neonates in the first 28 days of life and occurs in 2–3 per 100 live births globally, with preterm and low-birth-weight neonates disproportionately affected.3 Mortality from neonatal sepsis is as high as 17.6%3 and even higher in low/middle-income countries.4 For example, in the low-income country The Gambia, the incidence of neonatal sepsis ranges from 2 to 7%,5,6 and sepsis was the third leading cause of death in neonates ≤28 days old (22%), behind prematurity (25%) and birth asphyxia/trauma (29%) in 2016.7 Furthermore, 39% of survivors either develop a major disability or die by two years of age.8 Thus, early identification of neonatal sepsis is critical for timely treatment, while understanding how sepsis disrupts immune development may inform interventions to prevent long-term sequelae.

The clinical signs of neonatal sepsis are often non-specific (e.g., irritability, poor feeding, respiratory distress)9 and can also be associated with non-infectious causes.10 Although the gold standard for neonatal sepsis diagnosis is microbiological confirmation in blood cultures, results require several days, necessitating empiric antibiotics pending results,11 and 72% of clinical cases of neonatal sepsis lack a microbiological identification of a pathogen.12 This puts physicians in a difficult dilemma between stopping antibiotics with respect to antibiotic stewardship, thus risking under-treatment, or continuing empiric antibiotics with the presumptive diagnosis of “clinical sepsis”.13 However, prolonged empiric antibiotic overuse can disrupt gut microbiota,14 expose neonates to ototoxic and nephrotoxic drugs,10 and contribute to antibiotic resistance.10 Thus, specific biomarkers for neonatal sepsis independent of blood cultures are critically needed for early and accurate diagnosis. Commonly investigated neonatal sepsis biomarkers largely represent repurposed adult sepsis biomarkers, including cell counts,15 plasma proteins,16, 17, 18, 19, 20 cytokines,21 and gene expression of leukocytes,22 with varying sensitivity and specificity.9 However, findings in adult sepsis are often not applicable to neonatal sepsis, given the markedly distinct neonatal immune system.23 Furthermore, none of these are predictive biomarkers (i.e., able to identify sepsis before clinical signs/symptoms appear), which would represent a game-changer for early diagnosis and treatment.

In addition, the first week of life is highly dynamic24 as neonates adapt to the extrauterine environment, including metabolic changes,25 a rapid cardiorespiratory transition,25 and gradual immune maturation.26,27 We showed previously that these physiological changes are reflected in ∼3000 genes changing expression over time in the first week of life.24 However, it is unclear how sepsis might affect these developmental trajectories (“ontogeny”).

Developing predictive sepsis biomarkers and analyzing ontogeny effects requires samples collected pre- and post-sepsis onset; however, this is challenging since hundreds of samples must be collected prospectively to capture enough neonates who later develop sepsis (2–3% of neonates develop sepsis).2 Thus, almost all neonatal sepsis studies to date collected samples after sepsis onset, when cause and effect are no longer distinguishable. In contrast, we describe here a unique cohort, embedded within a larger prospective study, where samples were available pre- and post-sepsis onset for transcriptomic analysis, providing a rare opportunity to identify gene expression biomarkers that can predict neonatal sepsis before clinical presentation. The objectives of this study were to: 1) determine gene expression changes in neonates who developed sepsis compared to those who remained healthy or developed only a localized infection; 2) identify predictive biomarkers for neonatal sepsis at birth; and 3) characterize how sepsis disrupts gene expression trajectories in the first week of life.

Methods

Neonatal sample collection

The Systems Biology to Identify Biomarkers of Neonatal Vaccine Immunogenicity study (NCT03246230)28,29 investigated early immune signatures of Hepatitis B vaccination (Supplemental Methods). In total, 720 full-term neonates (≥37 weeks gestational age), who were healthy at birth (determined by medical history, physical examination, and judgment of the study physician), were enrolled from The Gambia, West Africa, from 2017 to 2019. Whole blood was collected at two time points: all neonates had day-of-life (DOL) 0 samples taken (on average at 10.3 ± 7.2 h of life) and infants were then randomized to collect a second sample either at DOL 1, 3, or 728 (Figure S1). Fractions from the same blood sample were used for RNA-Seq and flow cytometry, with further details in the published clinical protocol.28

Within this large cohort, 33 neonates were eventually hospitalized within 28 days of life for clinical signs/symptoms potentially consistent with sepsis (Table S1). Of these neonates, 21 were diagnosed with sepsis according to the following criteria: (a) blood culture-proven bacterial sepsis with a clinically significant pathogen (n = 6); or (b) clinically-diagnosed sepsis with negative blood culture but syndrome of fever, excessive crying, poor feeding, vomiting, mottled skin, high-pitched cry, convulsions, and/or laboratory parameters such as neutrophilia (n = 15). The other hospitalised neonates were classified as “sepsis unlikely, Localized Infection Only” neonates (LIO, n = 12), who had alternative diagnoses (e.g., bronchiolitis, gastroenteritis, skin infection), negative blood cultures, and no systemic signs/symptoms of illness, representing a comparison group of sick infants who required hospitalization but were not septic (Figure S1). Septic neonates were further separated into early onset sepsis (EOS, n = 15, onset ≤7 days after birth) and late onset sepsis (LOS, n = 6, onset 8–28 days after birth), consistent with published nomenclature for term neonates.3,30, 31, 32 A clear separation of disease onset time was also observed at DOL7 among the septic neonates (Figure S1C). Clinical classifications were undertaken by a senior pediatrician, blinded to molecular/omics data. In addition, 33 healthy controls were selected from the larger cohort within the same study by carefully matching for biological sex (reported by clinician), DOL sub-group, and blood collection time relative to birth (Figure S2) to the hospitalized neonates (Table 1, Table S2).

Differential gene expression analysis

Blood was processed for RNA-seq as described previously.24 All bioinformatic analyses were performed in R (v4.2.2). Batch correction using ComBat-Seq from the package sva (v3.42.0)33 was performed for all collected samples (Figure S3); the samples of interest were then extracted from the batch-corrected count matrix for analysis. Hemoglobin-related genes and low count genes (counts >10 in fewer than 10 samples) were removed,24 resulting in 12,166 ENSEMBL gene IDs for analysis.

The package DESeq2 (v1.36.0)34(p2) was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs, absolute fold change ≥1.5 and an adjusted p-value <0.05 via Wald test using Benjamini-Hochberg correction) between healthy, LIO, and sepsis neonates at birth and over time. For longitudinal analyses, paired samples (DOL0 and DOL7) were analysed pairwise using DESeq2 to index against underlying individual factors. Enrichment of Reactome pathways35 was performed on DEGs using SIGORA (v3.1.1),36 which is an over-representation analysis that uses gene pairs rather than single genes to increase specificity of pathway enrichment. Significantly enriched pathways had an adjusted p-value <0.001 (via distribution function of hypergeometric probabilities with Bonferroni correction, more stringent cutoff recommended in SIGORA due to its usage of gene pairs). Pathways were “upregulated” if their DEGs were overrepresented in upregulated DEGs, and vice versa for “downregulated”.

Sepsis gene biomarker identification

Predictive gene biomarkers for all sepsis or specifically EOS were developed using two machine learning approaches to identify the most discriminative genes for classification. Sparse Partial Least-Squares Discriminant Analysis (sPLS-DA)37 from mixOmics (v6.3.2)38 was performed starting with the top 3000 most variable genes in all the DOL0 samples, and the model was then tuned with 5-fold cross-validation to identify the optimal number of components and discriminatory variables to distinguish sepsis or EOS DOL0 samples from the other samples, while minimizing balanced error rate.

Monte Carlo cross-validation (balanced 80:20 train:test split) repeated 50 times determined that using the top 500 upregulated DEGs ranked by adjusted p-value in EOS compared to other groups at DOL0 resulted in the best performance in the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression model from glmnet (v4.1.6).36 Feature selection was performed to shrink the coefficients of less discriminatory genes using LASSO regression (alpha = 1) and tuning of the lambda hyperparameter with leave-one-out cross-validation to minimize mean-squared error. DOL0 samples were first split into balanced 80:20 train:test samples, followed by DE analysis of EOS vs non-EOS in the training samples to identify the top 500 upregulated genes ranked by smallest p-value. Coefficients of non-discriminatory genes were shrunken using LASSO regression creating a condensed gene signature, and a training and testing AUC were calculated. This process was iterated 50 times to perform Monte Carlo cross-validation. To create the final model and signature, all DOL0 samples were used and the intersect of upregulated DEGs found in all three comparisons (EOS vs LOS, EOS vs LIO, and EOS vs healthy) were further condensed into a 4-gene signature using LASSO regression. AUC, sensitivity, and specificity of this final LASSO model was calculated using the cohort DOL0 samples, as well as an external validation microarray dataset39 (GSE26440, 6 EOS and 6 healthy, Table S3), the cohort DOL1 samples, and varying healthy controls via bootstrapping. Lastly, an elastic net model was also performed by setting alpha to 0.5 in glmnet to balance L1 and L2 penalties from ridge regression and LASSO. Signature enrichment was assessed using gene set variation analysis (GSVA v1.20.0).40 See Supplemental Methods for further details.

Statistics

p-values in DESeq2 were calculated using the Wald test and adjusted using Benjamini-Hochberg correction. For demographic data, categorical variables used the Chi-squared test with Yates's correction, or Fisher's exact test if any expected value was <5; continuous variables used the Wilcoxon Rank–Sum test. A power analysis was performed using the package ssizeRNA (v1.3.2)41 and determined that the study was adequately powered for finding DEGs (Figure S4). Additional inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined in Supplemental Methods. Clinical classifications were undertaken by a senior pediatrician, blinded to molecular/omics data.

Results

Neonatal demographics

All neonates in this cohort were born at term (37–42 weeks, median 40 weeks) and considered healthy with Apgar scores ranging from 8 to 10 (median 10). Healthy and hospitalized neonates had similar demographics and clinical parameters at birth, without any statistically significant differences, although this may be influenced by the limited size of the cohort (Table 1, Table S2). At the time of hospitalization, septic neonates had significantly higher neutrophil count, received antibiotics more frequently, and had longer hospitalization compared to LIO neonates (Table S1).

Transcriptional differences occurred in septic neonates prior to clinical presentation

Even though all neonates were healthy at birth, neonates with DOL0 samples who later developed sepsis (n = 20) had 469 DEGs compared to healthy neonates (n = 32) and 476 DEGs compared to LIO neonates (n = 12) at birth (DOL0, Figure S5A). After separating septic neonates into EOS (n = 14) and LOS (n = 6), neonates with EOS had 1067 DEGs vs LOS, 984 DEGs vs LIO, and 1086 DEGs vs healthy neonates (Fig. 1a). “Regulation of HSF1-mediated heat shock response”, “Heme biosynthesis”, “Antigen processing”, “Transcriptional regulation”, “Recognition of DNA damage”, and multiple “Cell Cycle” pathways were enriched by DEGs in EOS neonates (Fig. 1b), indicating that multiple host processes were dysregulated before onset of clinical signs/symptoms.

Enrichment of cell cycle pathways might reflect changes in blood leukocyte populations. However, at DOL0, cellular proportions did not significantly differ between sepsis, LIO, and healthy neonates (Figure S6), even after splitting into EOS and LOS (Figure S7). Furthermore, cellular ratios previously documented to be elevated during neonatal sepsis (neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio42 and immature-to-total neutrophil ratio43) were not elevated at DOL0 in neonates who later developed sepsis (Figure S8). Adjusting for cellular proportions during DEG analysis still showed similar enriched pathways (Figure S9). This suggested that changes in leukocyte populations were not substantially contributing to changes in gene expression and not responsible for cell cycle pathway upregulation.

At birth, heat shock proteins predicted future development of neonatal sepsis

Since DEGs at DOL0 were seen in neonates who later developed EOS when compared to all other neonates (LOS, LIO, and healthy), we investigated whether certain genes could serve as predictive biomarkers at birth for EOS and distinguish these neonates from LIO and healthy neonates. First, the machine learning algorithm sPLS-DA (Fig. 2a) was used since it accounts for datasets having vastly more features than samples (e.g., large RNA-Seq datasets) by selecting the genes that are strong discriminators of each classification.37 The two highest contributors in component 1 for classifying EOS were two heat shock proteins, HSPH1 (heat shock protein family H member 1) and DNAJB1 (DnaJ heat shock protein family member B1) (Fig. 2b, Figure S10). HSPH1 and DNAJB1 were upregulated in EOS (and all sepsis) neonates when compared to all other neonates at DOL0 (Table S4). These two genes are part of the “Regulation of HSF1-mediated heat shock response” pathway, which was also significantly enriched by DEGs in EOS (and all sepsis) neonates at DOL0 (Fig. 1b, Figure S5B).

Another machine learning strategy, LASSO regularization, which had been successfully used to develop adult sepsis signatures,44 was employed to identify discriminative signatures. Four genes were identified that could distinguish EOS from healthy, LIO, and LOS at birth. HSPH1 was again selected (Fig. 3c), together with PRIM1 (DNA primase subunit 1), BORA (BORA aurora kinase A activator), and NCAPG2 (non-SMC condensin II complex subunit G2). This 4-gene signature had the following AUC = 0.94, sensitivity = 0.93, and specificity = 0.92 when assessed on the DOL0 samples (Fig. 3a) and was significantly and markedly enriched in EOS compared to non-EOS neonates (Fig. 3b). PRIM1, BORA, and NCAPG2 are all involved in cell cycle pathways, consistent with the upregulation of these pathways in EOS neonates (Fig. 1B, Figure S5B). All four genes remained top candidates when a gene signature was created using an alternative regression technique, an elastic net model that balances penalizations, further confirming their importance in identifying EOS (Figure S11). The stability of the gene signature was also assessed using Monte Carlo cross-validation, with HPSH1, NCAPG2, and PRIM1 appearing the most frequently across each signature iteration (Figure S12, Supplemental Methods). Overall, these results suggested that at birth, heat shock proteins and cell cycle processes were already perturbed in those neonates who later developed EOS, and thus could be used to predict neonatal EOS before clinical signs/symptoms of sepsis. Similar sets of genes were also observed that could discriminate all septic neonates (combined EOS and LOS) from those without sepsis, with HSPH1 again selected as a key feature by both methods (Figure S13).

To independently validate the EOS gene expression signature, we assessed sensitivity, specificity, and AUC in an external microarray dataset (GSE26440)39 (Figure S14A). Wynn et al. (2011) studied full-term neonates who developed septic shock45 ≤28 days after birth and were admitted to the pediatric ICU in 11 institutions in the United States (between 2003 and 2010), with blood collected close to birth (demographic data in Table S3).39 In total, 6 EOS and 6 age-matched healthy neonates were used for validation (AUC = 0.72, sensitivity = 0.83, and specificity = 0.83). An ideal validation cohort would include term neonates who were sampled prior to symptom onset; however, neonatal sepsis studies are rare to begin with and such a dataset does not exist in the public domain. From an ethics perspective, it is extremely difficult to get ethical approval to obtain blood draws from healthy-appearing neonates as was done in this study, especially since this is not an interventional study, since a blood draw is an invasive procedure for a neonate, making a prospective study with septic neonates sampled when healthy even rarer. Considering all these factors, the current validation cohort is a reasonable approximation with limitations. This signature was also assessed using 26 RNA-Seq samples collected at DOL1 in the original cohort: 5 EOS (of which two were symptomatic 5 and 18 h before sampling), 1 LOS, 7 LIO, and 13 matched healthy neonates (Figure S1). It had AUC = 0.90, sensitivity = 1, and specificity = 0.67 in this DOL1 cohort (Figure S14B), although again this cohort was limited by not having all septic neonates sampled prior to symptom onset. Lastly, a bootstrapping methodology was used by substituting the matched healthy controls with subsets of the 583 other healthy controls from the larger cohort, iterated 100 times, and assessing the 4-gene signature with this new group of samples (Supplemental Methods). The signature had an average AUC of 0.90 through this bootstrapping method (Figure S15).

Sepsis disrupts immune and metabolic aspects of normal ontogeny

In addition to DOL0 samples, and as part of the overall protocol, subsequent samples were collected from neonates within the first week of life, enabling assessment of how neonatal sepsis might impact developmental trajectories. Our previous work identified thousands of DEGs during the first week of life,24 representing key developmental changes/ontogeny. We limited this analysis to a subset of five EOS neonates who had a second blood sample collected at DOL7 while still hospitalized for sepsis. These samples were analyzed together with five matched healthy controls to determine the effects of sepsis on ontogeny.

Between DOL7 and DOL0, there were 2456 DEGs in healthy neonates and 3931 DEGs in EOS neonates, with 2063 DEGs shared between the two groups. The substantial overlap suggests that underlying ontogeny is a key driver of gene expression changes over time, even during sepsis24 (Figure S16A). However, ongoing sepsis resulted in the perturbation of expression of 2261 genes over time (1868 DEGs unique to sepsis and 393 DEGs unique to healthy ontogeny). Indexing of transcriptomic data to each patient's DOL0 sample was essential to enable the discovery of ontogeny differences in these patients despite small sample size (as direct comparison of DOL7 samples yielded no DEGs, Figure S17), by accounting for individual heterogeneity.

Pathway enrichment was performed on the unique and shared DEGs over time of EOS and healthy neonates (Fig. 4, Figure S16B). Uniquely enriched pathways in healthy neonates included upregulated mitochondrial pathways (“Complex I biogenesis”, “Citric Acid Cycle”, “Mitochondrial protein import”) and immune pathways (“Interferon alpha/beta signaling”, “ISG15 antiviral mechanism”). Uniquely enriched pathways in EOS neonates included downregulated “Regulation of HSF1-mediated heat shock response” and “Class I MHC mediated antigen processing and presentation”, and various upregulated RNA processing and nucleotide metabolism pathways (Fig. 4). Despite these metabolic and immune pathways being disrupted in sepsis, many pathways were still shared between the EOS and healthy neonates over time, suggesting conservation of certain ontogeny processes, including upregulated adaptive immunity pathways (“Butyrophilin family interactions”, “Immunoregulatory interactions between a lymphoid and non-lymphoid cell”, “Generation of second messenger molecules”) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Development of neonatal sepsis predictors has been challenging because the vast majority of neonatal sepsis studies collect blood after signs/symptoms of sepsis are evident or after hospitalization. Therefore, current biomarkers are not predictive. Critically, our prospective study provided the unique opportunity to identify gene expression biomarkers that could predict, at birth, which healthy-appearing neonates will develop sepsis, and to follow these neonates during sepsis.

HSPH1 consistently appeared as a top predictor for EOS (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Figs. S10, S13), and the 4-gene signature could discriminate EOS from LOS, LIO, and healthy neonates at birth when all neonates appeared healthy. This was also validated in an external validation dataset (Figure S14A). Heat shock proteins (HSPs) are highly expressed during times of cellular stress as molecular chaperones that prevent stress-induced protein misfolding and aggregation,46 play a role in infection responses,47 and can be cytoprotective during sepsis in animal models.48 In EOS neonates, the concerted upregulation of HSPH1 and other HSPs at birth (Table S4) as part of the “Regulation of HSF1-mediated heat shock response” pathway (Fig. 1b) potentially reflected perinatal stresses associated with developing sepsis, an early sepsis response that is not yet clinically apparent, or a predisposition to develop sepsis. Interestingly, elevated heat shock proteins represented a transient response, since this pathway was downregulated over time in EOS neonates (Fig. 4). HSPH1 has not been investigated in neonatal sepsis, although it has been studied in older children and adult sepsis studies. In pediatric sepsis, HSPH1 was upregulated in non-survivors of septic shock compared to survivors.49 Conversely, in adults, HSPH1 was downregulated in severe sepsis patients with immunosuppression and a higher mortality rate,50 in hypothermic sepsis patients,51 and in surgical patients with sepsis,52,53 further emphasizing the importance of age-specific sepsis biomarkers. In addition to HSPH1, the other EOS signature genes were cell-cycle genes (Fig. 3c). HSPs can also regulate cell cycle processes during stress,54 suggesting an important link between these two processes during sepsis. Overall, our highly sensitive and specific gene expression signatures should be further evaluated in larger cohorts and could be used in conjunction with current screening methods at birth55, 56, 57 to identify neonates with high risk of developing EOS. This would address a critical need for better diagnostics for neonatal sepsis, particularly in LMICs where the burden of neonatal sepsis is high. New point-of-care tools that perform transcript quantification from blood are currently under development and could soon be accessible broadly, even in LMICs. Furthermore, despite this signature being generated from neonates in an LMIC, it also performed well in the validation cohort from the United States, suggesting applicability in HICs as well.

Gene expression trajectories in the first week of life were analyzed to determine how sepsis affects developmental processes during the first week of life (Fig. 4). These trajectories were highly dynamic with thousands of DEGs changing over time, consistent with our previous ontogeny study,24 and were relatively conserved, even with sepsis. A key shared process was the upregulation of adaptive immune pathways (Fig. 4), likely reflecting the slow maturation of adaptive immunity. Disruption to metabolism post-sepsis was reflected by the upregulation of mitochondrial pathways seen only in healthy neonates (Fig. 4). Mitochondrial impairment in neonatal sepsis58 may contribute to organ failure59 and long-term cognitive impairment,60 and could reflect an infection-induced shift from oxidative phosphorylation to glycolysis (“Warburg Effect”) in leukocytes61 with immunoregulatory consequences.62 Further disruptions to immune-related developmental processes were reflected in the absence of the “Interferon alpha/beta signaling” pathway in EOS neonates (Fig. 4), which we previously identified as a key upregulated pathway during the first week of life in an ontogeny study of healthy neonates.24 The long-term consequences of this immune perturbation in the first week of life are unknown, but one possibility may be a predisposition to recurrent infections.63,64

A limitation in this study was the small sample size, including the validation cohort, due to the difficulty of prospectively enrolling neonates prior to sepsis onset. While this validation cohort did not sample neonates before onset of sickness, limiting its value as a validation cohort, it was the only cohort that closely approximated the original cohort available currently. This may limit the generalizability of our results and further validation is needed to fully assess the signature's predictive value. Importantly, despite the small sample size, substantial gene expression differences were still observed at birth and over time, and the derived biomarkers were discriminatory in most patients. Our results can now guide larger targeted prospective studies to validate these highly promising data. To further validate our findings and broaden applicability, future studies should also include pre-term neonates, neonates born via C-section, and neonates from other countries from both LMICs and HICs, to ensure broad applicability.

Conclusions

In this unique prospective longitudinal study, we found that neonates who later developed sepsis, particularly EOS, already showed differences in gene expression profiles at birth. This was leveraged to develop a highly sensitive, specific, and robust 4-gene signature (HSPH1, PRIM1, BORA, NCAPG2) that could predict, at birth, future EOS, distinguished from healthy, LIO, and LOS neonates. We also found that neonatal sepsis disrupted immune and metabolic processes during the first week of life, with potential influence on future health status, but most of ontogeny remained conserved. These results have important implications regarding screening, prevention, and timely management of neonatal sepsis.
