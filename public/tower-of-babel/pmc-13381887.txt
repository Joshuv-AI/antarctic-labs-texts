The neurocomputational mechanisms underlying the impact of social comparison on effort investment
Authors: Jiarui Dong, Yachao Rong, Shengjie Ma, Yang Xu, Ping Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Previous research has shown that favorable monetary outcomes relative to others enhance neural responses; however, these paradigms often confound the subjective utility of social comparison with absolute reward magnitude. It remains unclear how performance-based social comparisons modulate an individual’s self-efficacy and the subsequent effort mobilization. To address this gap, we conducted behavioral (N = 32) and electrophysiological (N = 34) experiments using a self-paced effortful task with downward/upward social comparison feedback. Computational modeling and multilevel mediation analyses revealed that downward (vs. upward) feedback dynamically updated self-efficacy, which fully mediated subsequent effort mobilization. Electrophysiological results showed stronger neural responses to downward versus upward feedback. Critically, downward comparison enhanced preparatory markers for the next round, including the contingent negative variation (CNV) and cue-beta power. Moreover, higher self-efficacy predicted more negative-going CNV potentials. These findings demonstrate that performance-based comparisons dynamically regulate self-efficacy, shaping both neural preparation and effort allocation in goal-directed behavior.

Introduction

Social comparison is a pervasive psychological phenomenon that shapes individual’s judgments, emotions, and goal-directed behaviors1. Rooted in social comparison theory, people inherently evaluate their abilities and self-worth against others, particularly when objective assessment criteria are absent2. Among its various effects, social comparison may exert the greatest influence on individual’s self-perception of ability, providing an immediate and efficient mechanism for assessing their abilities relative to others3,4. These self-perceptions of ability, in turn, underpin individual’s willingness to invest effort in behaviors needed to achieve desired outcomes5–7. Theoretically, social comparison influences goal-directed behavior via a dynamic, multi-stage process: evaluating comparison outcomes, updating self-evaluations of abilities, and guiding subsequent effort allocation.

Social comparison initiates a process in which individuals first evaluate discrepancies between their own performance and that of others, perceiving either superiority (downward comparison) or inferiority (upward comparison). To date, extant neurocognitive studies have primarily used gambling paradigms with monetary payoffs to investigate these evaluations8–13. In contrast, fewer studies have used performance-based social comparison, such as knowledge quizzes14 or stopwatch tasks15,16. Research in this domain consistently reported stronger neural responses following downward as opposed to upward comparison feedback17–19. These include enhanced amplitudes of the reward-related positivity (RewP), feedback-related potentials (fb-P3), and late positive potential (LPP) in electrophysiological studies11,18,19, as well as increased activation of the ventral striatum in neuroimaging research14. Such findings suggest that individuals may perceive downward comparisons as intrinsically favorable, eliciting positive affect by affirming social superiority. However, the reliance on monetary payoffs as the comparison dimension introduces a critical confound: it remains unclear whether these neural signatures reflect the social utility of the comparison itself or the valuation of absolute monetary gains. This ambiguity leaves a significant gap in our understanding of how social comparison shapes self-perceptions of ability in purely performance-driven contexts, where the “gain” is the affirmation of competence rather than external compensation.

Following comparison outcomes, individuals may dynamically update their self-efficacy—the internal belief in one’s capacity to execute the actions required for goal attainment5. In social contexts, downward and upward comparisons serve as proxies for success and failure, respectively, directly driving efficacy updates5,20. Neurocomputational evidence likewise suggests that self-related belief updating is sensitive to social feedback7,21,22, and that favorable social feedback can strengthen self-related beliefs23,24, in line with Bandura’s self-efficacy framework.

Despite the importance of these updates, how they translate into sustained physical or cognitive effort during goal-directed behavior remains a subject of theoretical debate. Bandura’s self-efficacy framework posits that social comparison feedback and updated self-efficacy jointly regulate effort5, but empirical evidence for this relationship remains limited. While some studies have reported more risk-taking behaviors following upward comparisons12,25–27, recent research using ecological momentary assessment has reported findings contradict Bandura’s predictions3,28. Specifically, Diel and colleagues reported that upward, rather than downward, social comparisons increased self-improvement motivation and strengthened effort intentions. By contrast, downward social comparisons appeared to foster complacency, prompting individuals to disregard improvement strategies and enter a “coasting” state marked by reduced effort investment. These effects are particularly pronounced in contexts of moderate social comparison3,28,29. However, a critical limitation of these studies is their reliance on self-report measures of motivation and effort, which may be susceptible to social desirability bias or distorted by inaccurate self-perceptions.

The resolution of this theoretical conflict—whether downward social comparison facilitates effort through efficacy-driven enhancement or inhibits it through complacency—is critical for unpacking the mechanisms of human motivation and self-regulation. Beyond basic theory, this investigation holds significant clinical and practical importance. In the domain of mental health, disorders, such as depression are marked by a “reduced value of control,” wherein upward social comparisons may trigger a catastrophic collapse in self-efficacy and effort mobilization30,31. Identifying the neural markers of this breakdown—such as altered feedback evaluation (e.g., RewP, fb-P3) or disrupted preparatory activity—could provide precise targets for neuromodulatory interventions. Similarly, in educational and organizational settings, understanding the conditions under which competitive feedback promotes invigoration versus burnout can inform the design of “nudge” strategies to optimize human productivity.

To elucidate these mechanisms, we conducted two experiments—one behavioral (Experiment 1) and one electrophysiological (Experiment 2)—to dissect the neurocognitive links between performance-based comparison, self-efficacy updates, and subsequent effort allocation. Crucially, this paradigm isolates social evaluation from the confounding influence of monetary magnitudes. Both experiments utilized a consistent procedural framework. Participants performed a self-paced effort-based task in which they autonomously determined the number of Stroop trials to complete within a randomized time window of each round32 (Experiment 1: 8 s, 10 s, 12 s; Experiment 2: 6 s, 8 s, 10 s). Effort investment was operationalized as the total number of correct responses per round, capturing sustained cognitive engagement. Following each round, participants received performance feedback signaling better (downward comparison) or worse (upward comparison) performance relative to a purported competitor. These outcomes were entirely irrelevant from monetary incentives. Every 3–5 round, participants rated their perceived likelihood of winning the subsequent round as an index of self-efficacy. This design ensured that participants incurred a genuine cognitive effort cost to obtain social feedback, enabling a mechanistic assessment of how comparative information drives the dynamic recalibration of efficacy and subsequent effort. We maintained a moderate comparison context by delivering a balanced ratio of downward and upward feedback in pseudorandom order. This approach served three primary purposes. First, it aligns with previous research using ecological momentary assessment3,28, facilitating a direct comparison between self-reported effort intentions and objective behavioral output measured in a controlled laboratory setting. Second, an unbalanced feedback distribution might have led participants to perceive a significant skill disparity between themselves and the competitor. By ensuring the competitor remained a credible and relevant social referent, we avoided potential “ceiling” or “floor” effects on motivation and maintained the psychological integrity of the social comparison context. Third, maintaining a comparable frequency of downward and upward feedback is essential for the electroencephalography (EEG) analyses in Experiment 2. Feedback-locked event-related potential (ERP) components, such as the RewP and fb-P3, are highly sensitive to stimulus probability and expectancy33–36. By balancing the occurrence of these outcomes, we ensured that the observed neural differences reflected the valence of the social comparison rather than confounding effects of frequency-dependent surprise or differing signal-to-noise ratios between conditions.

Behaviorally, we contrasted Bandura’s theory5—predicting that downward social comparison bolsters self-efficacy and effort—against the “coasting” hypothesis, which instead suggests upward comparison facilitates improvement3,28. We employed reinforcement learning (RL) and linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) to quantify round-by-round self-efficacy updates and determine how feedback and self-efficacy jointly regulate subsequent effort allocation. Furthermore, we utilized multilevel mediation models to evaluate whether self-efficacy serves as the latent mechanism through which social comparison influences behavior.

Electrophysiologically (Experiment 2), we investigated neural correlates of feedback evaluation and task preparation. During evaluation, we expected enhanced RewP, fb-P3, and LPP amplitudes following downward compared to upward feedback, reflecting the intrinsic utility of social superiority. For the preparatory phase, we hypothesized that both social comparison feedback and updated self-efficacy would modulate task-preparatory responses in the subsequent round, specifically the contingent negative variation (CNV) amplitude and cue-beta band power.

Our experimental design served a tripartite purpose: to assess the neural signatures of performance-based social comparison evaluation, to track the dynamic fluctuations in self-efficacy, and to determine how these factors converge to shape subsequent effort expenditure. Our findings revealed two primary insights: first, relative to upward comparison, downward social comparison elicited more robust neural responses and higher efficacy levels; second, both downward feedback and elevated self-efficacy positively contributed to subsequent effort investment. These results suggest that in performance-driven contexts, social superiority functions as a motivational catalyst rather than a trigger for complacency.

Next, to characterize the dynamic updating of efficacy expectations by social comparison feedback, we compared three candidate reinforcement learning models: an intercept-only (null) model, a single-learning-rate (α) model, and a dual-learning-rate (αpos, αneg) model, with sampler diagnostics for candidate models shown in Supplementary Tables 4 and 5. Model comparison based on Leave-One-Out Information Criterion (LOOIC, see Reinforcement Learning Model comparison and validation, in Method) identified the dual-learning-rate model as the most parsimonious fit (see Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 6). Consequently, we utilized the parameters from this winning model to generate round-by-round latent efficacy estimates for all subsequent analyses. As a model-validation analysis, we then examined how the model-based efficacy estimates related to participants’ actual ratings. The results revealed that model-based efficacy estimates significantly predicted rating scores, F (1, 31.52) = 79.42, b = 0.530, p < .001, 95% CI [0.414, 0.647], power = 100.0% [99.63. 100.0] (Fig. 2b, e), confirming that the model-based efficacy estimates accurately reflect participants’ subjective experiences.

Finally, we analyzed the impact of social comparison feedback on model-based efficacy estimates. The LMM results revealed that social comparison feedback significantly predicted the model-based efficacy estimates, F (1, 31.99) = 11, b = 0.320, p < .001, 95% CI [0.260, 0.379], power = 100.0% [99.63. 100.0], with higher model-based efficacy estimates following downward social comparison compared to upward comparison (0.61 vs. 0.48, Fig. 2c).

These results provide evidence that participants adjusted their efficacy expectations based on different social comparison feedback, with downward feedback leading to increased efficacy estimates. Our findings not only demonstrate that the dual-learning-rate model successfully captures round-by-round changes in efficacy expectations, but also confirm that downward social comparison feedback functions as a positive performance-related experience, thereby effectively enhancing efficacy expectations.

Subsequently, we fitted the Hierarchical Drift Diffusion Model (HDDM) to trial-level data to investigate the latent computational processes underlying these performance patterns. We first performed HDDM to explore how social comparison feedback influence drift rates and response threshold. The results revealed that the best-fitting model was the one included Stroop congruency as a control variable (see Hierarchical Drift Diffusion Model in Method). The main effect of social comparison feedback on drift rates was significant, with faster drift rates observed following downward compared to upward social comparison feedback (b = 0.05, 95% CI [0.01, 0.08], Pb>0 = 0.97, Fig. 3c). However, there was no significant main effect of social comparison on response threshold (b = −0.00, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.01], Pb<0 = 0.67). Next, we performed HDDM to explore the effect of model-based efficacy estimates on drift rates and response threshold. The best-fitting model was also the one controlled for Stroop congruency. However, the results revealed neither the significant main effect of model-based efficacy estimates on drift rates, (b = 0.08, 95% CI [−0.09, 0.25], Pb>0 = 0.79, Fig. 3c), nor the effect of model-based efficacy on response threshold (b = −0.08, 95% CI [−0.25, 0.09], Pb<0 = 0.79).

These results indicate that both downward social comparison feedback and higher efficacy estimates are associated with improved performance, as evidenced by higher CRPS and/or faster drift rates. Importantly, this improved performance after downward feedback reflects greater cognitive control investment, with increased attentional allocation to targets rather than a shift in the speed-accuracy tradeoff.

Experiment 1: efficacy estimates mediated the relationship between social comparison and effort investment

The findings so far demonstrate that participants adjusted their efficacy estimates in response to different social comparison feedback, which subsequently influenced their effort investment in the upcoming round. To further test this relationship and elucidate the mechanism of social comparison and effort investment, we conducted multilevel mediation analyses to examine whether efficacy estimates mediate the effect of social comparison feedback on effort, as indexed by CRPS and mean RTs, respectively. The results revealed that model-based efficacy estimates fully mediated this relationship (Fig. 3d). The indirect effect on CRPS was trend-level (ab = 0.022, p = .087, 95% CI [−0.003, 0.048]), whereas the indirect effect on mean RTs was significant (ab = −0.026, p = .018, 95% CI [−0.047, −0.005]). Specifically, downward social comparison feedback enhanced efficacy estimates, which then led to greater effort investment in the next round.

Experiment 2: behavioral results

The results of Experiment 1 demonstrated that participants updated their efficacy estimates based on social comparison feedback, which subsequently guided their effort investment at the behavioral level. Building on these findings, Experiment 2 aimed to identify the neural signatures associated with the evaluation of social comparison feedback and to reveal how social comparison feedback and efficacy estimates modulate subsequent effort investment at the neural level.

Descriptive statistics for feedback-condition frequencies (downward, upward, lateral), participants’ self-reported ratings, and model-based efficacy estimates are provided in Supplementary Table 1. Task performance (accuracy, mean RTs, CRPS) is reported in Supplementary Table 2. Post-task pleasantness ratings for each feedback type are reported in Supplementary Table 3 as a manipulation check of the social feedback symbols.

Consistent with Experiment 1, we used dual-learning-rate model (the best fitting, Supplementary Table 7) to compute round-by-round model-based efficacy estimates. We observed a significant effect of social comparison feedback on participants’ self-reported ratings, with higher scores following downward versus upward comparison (63.89 vs. 58.54), F(1, 34.21) = 18.03, b = 0.140, p < .001, 95% CI [0.075, 0.204], power = 99.5% [98.84, 99.84]. As a model-validation analysis, model-based efficacy estimates significantly predicted self-reported ratings, F(1, 31.66) = 154.57, b = 0.469, p < .001, 95% [0.395, 0.543], power = 100.0% [99.63, 100.0]. Social comparison feedback also significantly influenced efficacy estimates, F(1, 34.00) = 37.57, b = 0.248, p < .001, 95% [0.168, 0.327], power = 100.0% [99.63, 100.0], with higher efficacy estimates following downward versus upward feedback (0.58 vs. 0.47). These findings replicate those of Experiment 1 and support the influence of social comparison feedback on both self-reported ratings and model-based efficacy estimates.

LMM analysis of CRPS revealed neither the significant main effect of social comparison feedback (1.083 vs. 1.078), F(1, 106.68) = 0.89, b = 0.013, p = .348, 95% [−0.013, 0.038], power = 14.3% [12.19, 16.62], nor the effect of model-based efficacy estimates, F(1, 24.38) = 2.20, b = 0.045, p = .150, 95% [−0.019, 0.106], power = 33.70% [30.77, 36.72]. Although these results did not reach statistical significance, the observed trends were directionally consistent with those found in Experiment 1. Similarly, LMM analysis of mean RTs revealed neither the significant effect of social comparison feedback (597 vs. 600 ms), F(1, 62.46) = 2.00, b = −0.017, p = .161, 95% [−0.040, 0.006], power = 25.9% [19.85, 25.11], nor the effect of model-based efficacy estimates, F(1, 27.44) = 2.21, b = −0.056, p = .149, 95% [−0.129, 0.018], power = 32.30% [29.31, 35.20].

The best-fitting HDDM models were again those controlled for the effect of Stroop congruency (see Supplementary Tables 12 and 13). The results showed that social comparison feedback had neither significant main effect on drift rate (b = 0.01, 95% CI [−0.03, 0.06], Pb>0 = 0.68) nor on threshold (b = −0.00, 95% CI [−0.02, 0.01], Pb<0 = 0.60). However, model-based efficacy estimates exerted a significant main effect on drift rate, indicating that higher efficacy was associated with faster drift rates (b = 0.23, 95% CI [0.02, 0.44], Pb>0 = 0.96). Model-based efficacy had no effect on the threshold (b = 0.05, 95% CI [−0.11, 0.23], Pb<0 = 0.28).

Multilevel mediation analyses further showed model-based efficacy estimates fully mediated the influence of social comparison on effort investment, as indicated by significant indirect effects on both CRPS (ab = 0.039, p < .001, 95% CI [0.020, 0.058]) and mean RTs (ab = −0.054, p < .001, 95% CI [−0.071, −0.037]).

Overall, multilevel mediation findings in Experiment 2 replicated the indirect efficacy-based pathway observed in Experiment 1, whereby social comparison updates model-based efficacy expectations to drive behavior, which in turn shaped subsequent effort investment. However, the direct facilitating effects of social comparison and efficacy estimates on performance (CRPS) were weaker in Experiment 2 than that in Experiment 1.

The CNV was significantly affected by model-based efficacy estimates, F (1, 43.94) = 9.05, b = −0.064, p = .004, 95% [−0.107, −0.020], power = 83.00% [80.53, 85.28], with more negative-going waveforms associated with higher model-based efficacy estimates (Fig. 5c). However, model-based efficacy estimates had no significant effect on Cue-beta band power (Fig. 6c), F (1, 30.51) = 0.02, b = −0.003, p = .884, 95% [−0.044, 0.038], power = 2.90% [1.95, 4.14].

These results suggest that participants engaged in enhanced task preparation for upcoming targets following both downward social comparison feedback and higher model-based efficacy estimates. Collectively, these findings underscore the role of social comparison dynamics and self-efficacy in shaping the neural mechanisms associated with preparatory engagement and cognitive readiness for subsequent tasks.

Discussion

By manipulating a social comparison context, we investigated how evaluating one’s performance relative to others modulates effortful behavior. Participants reported more pleasantness when receiving downward versus upward social feedback, and exhibited enhanced neural responses to the former than the latter feedback, indexed by larger RewP, fb-P3, and LPP components. Following these evaluations, participants dynamically updated their self-efficacy based on the direction of social comparison feedback. Critically, both downward social comparison and elevated self-efficacy improved the preparatory engagement of cognitive control, as indicated by enhanced CNV amplitude and/or cue-beta power, and facilitated the subsequent effort investment, leading to improved performance in the following round.

We observed more positive RewP, fb-P3, and LPP amplitudes in the downward versus upward feedback condition, aligning with previous studies using gambling tasks and monetary payoffs to manipulate social comparison13,19,40,41. The RewP and fb-P3 are canonical components for outcome evaluation, with larger amplitudes elicited by favorable monetary or social outcomes39. Additionally, the LPP component is associated with later-stage of affective evaluation and the motivational relevance of the feedback9,11,19. Together with the post-experiment ratings that participants reported greater pleasantness following downward rather than upward social comparisons, these stronger neural signatures suggest that downward social comparison is experienced as inherently favorable, associated with greater evaluative salience and/or motivational significance of superior social-comparison information14,40,42. Crucially, because our task explicitly decoupled task performance from monetary compensation, these heightened electrophysiological responses to downward versus upward feedback clearly demonstrate that these components are sensitive to social utility of the comparison itself, independent of external financial gains.

Participants dynamically updated their self-perceptions of ability based on different types of social comparison feedback. Previous studies have indicated that positive social feedback (e.g., approving feedback from others or better performance feedback in competitive contexts) enhances participants’ self-esteem and improves self-evaluations of ability4,20,23,24. Our results confirm that downward social comparison can be conceptualized as a form of performance accomplishment that significantly improves participants’ efficacy perceptions, whereas upward comparisons undermines them5.

Based on social comparison direction and updated efficacy estimates, participants adjusted their effort investment. More negative-going CNV waveforms and stronger cue-beta power following downward comparison and/or higher efficacy estimates indicate enhanced control preparation and motor readiness for the upcoming round38,39,43,44,45. This translated into superior behavioral performance, evidenced by higher CRPS following downward social comparison. HDDM analyses further revealed that participants actively adjusted their attention in response to downward feedback and elevated self-efficacy, characterized by accelerated evidence accumulation without compromising the speed-accuracy trade-off in the next round. Downward social comparison functions as rewarding feedback: it fosters positive emotions, boosts self-efficacy, and increases the perceived likelihood of goal attainment. These factors collectively motivated participants to invest greater effort to maintain this favorable state. Our multilevel mediation model further supported this process, confirming that self-efficacy fully mediated the relationship between social comparison and effort investment. Moreover, while the positive learning rate was numerically larger than the negative learning rate, there were no significant differences between them as evidenced by the bias score (see Supplementary Table 9), indicating that the observed effort effects in our study are unlikely to be driven by a general tendency to prioritize or learn more effectively from positive feedback.

Although downward social comparison facilitated both neural markers of preparation (CNV amplitude, cue-beta power) and behavioral output (CRPS), and estimated efficacy correlated with CNV amplitudes, we observed no direct correlation between these neural signatures and behavioral performance. This dissociation likely stems from the nature of the cognitive task employed. Unlike simple physical tasks, the continuous Stroop paradigm necessitates a complex optimization of both speed and accuracy to maintain a competitive advantage32,46–48. While downward feedback enhanced motivational vigor, participants may have strategically calibrated their responses—a process evidenced by our HDDM results revealing faster evidence accumulation (drift rate) without shifts in decision thresholds. Unlike physical force-grip tasks, where neural preparatory signals may translate linearly into motor output, cognitive effort in the Stroop task is constrained by strategic bottlenecks. Therefore, the absence of a direct brain-behavior correlation in our study suggests that neural preparation reflects the intent to mobilize effort, whereas the final behavioral output is filtered through the strategic demands of conflict resolution and accuracy maintenance49–51. This aligns with recent findings reporting heightened physiological arousal (e.g., cardiovascular responses) during social comparison in the absence of behavioral facilitation during complex video game tasks52,53. Future studies should investigate how social comparison influences other types of effort investment, such as physical effort.

Furthermore, as noted in the Introduction, recent studies using ecological momentary assessment have found that upward comparisons promote greater self-reported effort investment intentions3,28, an observation inconsistent with our results. This discrepancy may stem from methodological differences in effort assessment. Retrospective self-reports are susceptible to social desirability and memory biases54, and even accurately-reported intentions may not translate into actual behavior due to the well-known intention–behavior gap55. While upward comparison might increase the desire for self-improvement52,53, our real-time behavioral data suggest that downward comparison more effectively triggers the actual allocation of cognitive resources.

Prior research has manipulated perceived control efficacy in non-social context, where different cues indicated whether performance feedback (i.e., monetary reward) was contingent on participants’ performance (high efficacy) or not (low efficacy)47,48. These studies reported better behavioral performance under high-efficacy conditions. In contrast, our study employed a fixed, non-meaningful cue for task preparation46. The enhanced brain responses and improved behavioral performance following downward social comparison suggest effort adjustments are driven by past comparison outcomes and internal self-efficacy updates, highlighting the critical role of self-efficacy in shaping goal-directed behavior5. These findings converge with animal studies showing that “winning” mice exhibit heightened initiative and sustained effortful behaviors in competitive contexts compared to “losing” mice56, reflecting the “winner effect”, where a history of success boosts the likelihood of future achievement in both humans and animals56–58.

Nonetheless, our findings should be interpreted with caution. We observed a stronger motivational effect of downward versus upward social comparison in moderate competitive contexts (50% downward/50% upward comparisons), but this relationship may vary with the ratio of downward to upward feedback. Furthermore, while the core findings—specifically the impact of downward social comparison on self-efficacy, the overall facilitation of effort, and the mediation effect of efficacy between social comparison and task performance—were robust across both experiments, certain behavioral and computational effects varied between experiments. For instance, estimated efficacy had a significant effect on CRPS but not drift rate in Experiment 1, whereas it had a significant effect on drift rate but not CRPS in Experiment 2. These discrepancies may be attributed to several factors. First, participants in Experiment 2 had to adhere to EEG environment constraints (e.g., minimizing head and body movement, reducing eye-movements or blinks during critical periods), which may have imposed greater cognitive demand than in Experiment 1. Second, we shortened the task time window from 8–12 to 6–8 s in Experiment 2, which may have curtailed the time available for participants to translate motivational vigor into observable behavioral output, potentially narrowing the effect sizes. Finally, research on the effort-discounting of rewards suggests that the subjective value of an outcome is often diminished by the cost of the effort required to obtain it51,59,60. It remains possible that a similar discounting mechanism applies to social outcomes. While our post-hoc comparisons suggest that task duration and environment influenced engagement, future studies should explicitly manipulate effort requirements to examine the interaction between effort costs and the psychological utility of social comparison outcomes. Importantly, our results do not discount the adaptive effects of upward comparison; rather, both comparison directions may foster adaptive motivational responses3,52,53. In particular, the impact of social comparison feedback may be modulated by individual characteristics (e.g., social-comparison orientation61, attributional style62) and the broader comparison environment (e.g., the overall probability of upward vs. downward feedback12, the perceived attainability of upward comparison targets63). These factors may shape how individuals interpret upward social-comparison feedback. Future research should explore performance dynamics in more complex competitive environments.

In conclusion, our study delineates the cognitive and neural mechanisms through which downward and upward social comparisons influence effort investment, tracing the process from initial feedback evaluation to self-efficacy updates and subsequent effort allocation. Our findings reveal that, compared to upward comparison, downward comparison feedback elicits enhanced neural activity and stronger self-efficacy expectations, both of which positively correlate with improvements in behavioral performance. Critically, self-efficacy fully mediates the relationship between social comparison and effort investment, underscoring its pivotal role as a motivational driver of goal-directed effort. These findings provide empirical support for a dynamic, multi-stage framework in which social comparison shapes efficacy expectations and guides effort allocation over time.

Methods

Experiment 1: participants

We recruited 32 healthy participants (10 male; mean age: 22.75 ± 2.03 years) with non-psychology backgrounds. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal color perception. We conducted a post hoc power analysis using G*Power (Faul et al., 2007) for this sample size (n = 32), using a paired-sample t-test (to test the difference between two dependent means). With an α level of 0.05, the analysis yielded a statistical power of 0.85. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Psychology at Capital Normal University, and all participants gave written informed consent prior to the experiment. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

Experiment 1: experimental design

We used a within-participant design with two social comparison conditions: downward social comparison and upward social comparison. PsychoPy3 was used to present experimental stimuli and record participant response data (https://www.psychopy.org).

Before the formal experiment, participants completed a practice session to familiarize themselves with the procedure. The practice consisted of two parts. The first part included 80 trials in which Stroop stimulus was presented at the center of the screen. Participants were required to discriminate the color of the Stroop target written in Chinese by pressing the corresponding keys (red-D, green-F, yellow-J, blue-K) with their left and right index and middle fingers. Feedback was presented after each response, with a checkmark (“√”) indicating a correct response and a cross (“×”) indicating an incorrect one. Participants needed to achieve at least 80% accuracy in this part before progressing to the next. The second part of the practice mirrored the formal experiment. A triangle cue was displayed for 1500 ms, followed by a series of Stroop targets. Participants responded to the color of each Stroop target one after another within a randomly selected time window (8 s, 10 s, or 12 s). At the end of this time window, a diamond feedback symbol was presented for 2000 ms to signal the end of that round. After completing 12 practice rounds, participants rated the difficulty of the practice on a scale from 0 to 100, with a time limit of 10 s to submit their response. Participants were then informed of the social comparison feedback rules (see below) and that they would receive a final-pay of 55 RMB, regardless of their task performance.

The formal experiment contained 144 rounds. Each round began with a variable interval of 1400–1600 ms. A triangle cue was then displayed for 1500 ms, followed by a variable interval of 1000–1200 ms, during which participants were required to concentrate and prepare. The Stroop targets was then presented consecutively. Participants were instructed to respond to the color of each Stroop target by pressing the D, F, J, or K (red-D, green-F, yellow-J, blue-K) with their left and right index and middle fingers. There was no fixed presentation time for each Stroop target stimulus. Instead, a 200 ms fixation cross appeared after each response, and the next Stroop target stimulus was presented immediately after the fixation cross disappeared. Each round consisted of a randomly selected time window (8, 10, or 12 s), during which participants were free to respond to as many Stroop targets as they chose. The use of variable time windows served to prevent participants from employing counting strategies to track their progress32. Effort investment was quantified as CRPS, defined as the total number of correct responses divided by the duration of the time window in each round. Upon expiration of the time window, the round terminated immediately; any response initiated after this cutoff was not recorded. To isolate intrinsic motivation and voluntary effort allocation, we explicitly informed participants that there were no minimum accuracy or response requirements, and that their performance would not influence their final monetary compensation. To ensure a consistently high level of cognitive conflict and prevent task habituation, the ratio of incongruent to congruent targets was maintained at 3:1. Subsequently, a fixation cross displayed for 500 ms, followed by a “calculating” screen displayed for 1500 ms to enhance the realism of the social comparison context. Finally, after a variable interval of 1400–1600 ms, feedback was presented for 2000 ms, indicating the social comparison feedback for that round (Fig. 1a).

To effectively induce social comparison, participants were explicitly informed before the experiment that they would engage in a real-time comparison with a genuine competitor. They were informed about how social comparison feedback presented. CRPS, serving as an indicator of participants’ performance, was calculated at the end of each round. Social comparison feedback was then presented based on the CRPS values of the participant and the competitor. An upward arrow indicated better performance (i.e., higher CRPS than the competitor in that round, downward social comparison). In contrast, a downward arrow indicated worse performance, (i.e., lower CRPS than the competitor in that round, upward social comparison). A bidirectional arrow indicated equal performance between the participant and the competitor (lateral social comparison, Fig. 1a). To prevent semantic interference, technical terms, such as “upward/downward comparison” were never used during the instruction; participants only encountered the directional performance labels (Better/Worse/Equal). The lateral rounds (indicating a “tie”) served as fillers to enhance the ecological validity and credibility of the social comparison manipulation. Given the nature of the CRPS calculation, it was mathematically probable that performance would occasionally be equivalent between the participant and the competitor; thus, including these rounds prevented the feedback from appearing artificial. We intentionally used abstract arrow symbols as a minimal indicator of social relative standing. This choice enabled us to manipulate only the direction of social comparison while avoiding additional confounding information, such as exact magnitude of the performance gap or absolute performance levels.

Moreover, participants were systematically provided with downward arrow in rounds where performance was exceptionally low (CRPS ≤ 0.4). This constraint was implemented to maintain the credibility of the manipulation by preventing participants from receiving “better” feedback during periods of poor performance. Conversely, when performance exceeded this threshold, the upward or downward feedback was predefined for each participant and presented in a pseudo-randomized sequence. This design was based on the premise that both participant and competitor performance would naturally fluctuate under the high cognitive demands of the continuous Stroop task, thereby preserving the ecological validity of the social comparison context.

The task consisted of 144 rounds, divided into six blocks of 24 rounds with a mandatory two-minute rest period between blocks. Ideally, the paradigm aimed to deliver 66 rounds of upward comparison, 66 rounds of downward comparison, and 12 lateral feedback rounds. However, as mentioned above, upward comparison was presented when CRPS was exceptionally low (CRPS ≤ 0.4). In practice, participants received an average of 65.69 ± 0.59 downward comparison feedback rounds and 66.34 ± 0.83 upward comparison feedback rounds in Experiment 1, and an average of 65.82 ± 0.38 downward-feedback rounds and 66.15 ± 0.36 upward-feedback rounds in Experiment 2 (see Supplementary Table 1 for detailed distribution). To prevent the development of response biases or suspicion, the same feedback type was restricted to appearing no more than three times consecutively, except when the CRPS was below 0.4.

After every 3–5 rounds, participants were required to complete a rating based on their past performance to estimate the probability of winning in the subsequent round, with scores ranging from 0 to 100 (see Fig. 1b). Participants had 10 s to submit their responses. A total of 36 ratings were gathered throughout the experiment.

Upon completion of the experiment, participants assessed the affective valence of the social comparison feedback by rating how pleasant they felt when receiving each symbol (upward, downward, and bidirectional arrows). These ratings were provided on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (“very unpleasant”) to 7 (“very pleasant”).

Experiment 1: behavioral analyses

Descriptive statistics: For each feedback condition, we report the mean and standard deviation of the round frequency across participants, alongside subjective ratings, model-based efficacy estimates, and behavioral performance metrics (accuracy, CRPS, and mean RTs for correct responses) for the next round. For both CRPS and mean RTs analyses, outliers were identified and excluded within each condition and each participant using a Mean ± 3 SD criterion. We also report the mean and standard deviation of post-task valence ratings as a manipulation check. Condition differences in post-task pleasant ratings were tested using a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA implemented in RStudio (version 2025.09.0 + 387; Posit) with the afex package (https://github.com/singmann/afex). Follow-up pairwise comparisons were performed using emmeans package (https://rvlenth.github.io/emmeans/) with Bonferroni adjustment (see Supplementary Table 3).

Linear Mixed Model framework: We fitted linear LMMs using the lme4 package and evaluated fixed effects using the lmerTest package (https://github.com/runehaubo/lmerTestR). Participant age and gender were included as covariates in all models (see Supplementary Table 16 for robustness checks with respect to the inclusion of demographic covariates). Following the “maximal random effects structure” recommendation (Barr et al., 2013), our models included random intercepts for participants and random slopes for the primary predictors to account for inter-individual variability in baseline responses and sensitivity to the experimental manipulations.

Detailed model specifications are provided in the respective results sections. Standardized coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were derived using the model_parameters function from the parameters package (https://easystats.github.io/parameters/). Simulation-based power was estimated using the powerSim function (1,000 simulations per model) from the simr package (https://github.com/pitakakariki/simr). All analyses were conducted in RStudio (version 2025.09.0 + 387; Posit).

Experiment 1: the effects of social comparison feedback and efficacy estimates on effort investment

To examine the impact of social comparison feedback and model-based efficacy estimates (derived from the RL model) on effort investment, we employed LMMs to test their effects on round-level effort investment (indices by mean RTs and CRPS).

For the mean RTs analysis, incorrect Stroop trials (7.91% of total trials) were excluded prior to calculating the round-level means. Data cleaning was performed within each participant and social comparison condition to ensure robust estimation; specifically, rounds with mean RTs exceeding ±3 standard deviations (1.91% of total rounds) or CRPS values beyond ±3 standard deviations (1.28% of total rounds) were identified as outliers and removed.

To decompose task performance into latent cognitive subprocesses, we applied hierarchical drift diffusion models (HDDMs) to trial-level Stroop response times and accuracy data using the HDDM (version 1.0.1) package in Python (https://secure.travis-ci.org/hddm-devs/hddm.png?branch=master). We tested how social comparison (downward vs. upward) and model-based efficacy estimates were associated with core decision parameters, specifically the drift rate and the decision threshold (see below). Due to the relatively low frequency of lateral comparison rounds, these computational analyses focused exclusively on the contrast between upward and downward social comparisons.

Linear Mixed Models: We first conducted LMM to examine the effects of social comparison feedback at round (t) and model-based efficacy estimates for the next round (t + 1) on effort investment at round (t + 1), measured by mean RTs and CRPS. Mean Stroop congruency (proportion of congruent vs. incongruent trials per round) was included as a level-1 control variable to account for task-related variance in performance. Hierarchical Drift Diffusion Models: We then fit HDDMs to trial-level data, including RTs and response accuracy (incorrect = 0 and correct = 1) for each Stroop target, across different social comparison conditions. HDDM estimates two key parameters linked to effort and decision-making: drift rate (v, representing the speed of evidence accumulation) and response threshold (a, indicating the degree of response caution) under different social comparison conditions (with upward social comparison set as the intercept). Additionally, we investigated how drift rate and threshold were influenced by model-based efficacy estimates.

For each experiment, we fitted two models focused on social comparison feedback: one examined the effects of social comparison feedback at round (t) on drift rate and response threshold at round (t + 1), while the other additionally controlled for Stroop trial congruency (congruent vs. incongruent) in both parameters. Model comparison revealed that the model accounting for the effect of Stroop congruency was the best-fitting one for social comparison feedback (see Supplementary Table 12). Similarly, we fitted two models for efficacy estimates: one assessed the effects of efficacy estimates for the next round (t + 1) on drift rate and response threshold in round (t + 1), and the other likewise incorporated Stroop congruency as a control predictor for both parameters. Again, model comparison confirmed that the best-fitting model for efficacy estimates was the one that included the effect of Stroop congruency (see Supplementary Table 13). Finally, we selected these best-fitting models for further analyses on how social comparison and efficacy estimates influence participants’ effort allocation. Specifically, for the social comparison effect, with upward feedback as the reference level, we examined the posterior probability that the drift-rate parameter was higher following downward than upward feedback, P(b > 0), and that the response-threshold parameter was lower following downward than upward feedback, P(b < 0). For efficacy estimates, we examined the posterior probability that higher efficacy estimates were associated with higher drift rates, P(b > 0), and lower response thresholds, P(b < 0). Here, P denotes the posterior probability and b denotes the estimated regression coefficient.

All models utilized 5 Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) chains, drawing 7,000 samples and discarding the first 4,000 samples as burn-in for each chain. Model convergence was assessed using the Gelman-Rubin statistic \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left(\hat{{{{\rm{R}}}}}\right)$$\end{document}R^; all model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\hat{{{{\rm{R}}}}}$$\end{document}R^ values close to 1 and below 1.1 indicated good convergence and minimal variation between chains66. Model comparison was performed using the deviance information criterion (DIC), where lower values indicate a better fit, to select the optimal model67. Posterior predictive checks (PPCs) were performed to evaluate the generative capacity of the optimal model. Specifically, we drew 500 parameter samples from the joint posterior distribution to simulate synthetic datasets, which were then compared against the observed empirical data. For the best-fitting model in each experiment, the observed summary statistics fell within the 95% posterior predictive intervals of the simulated distributions. These results indicate that the models successfully recovered the underlying distributional structure of the observed data and demonstrated a high level of predictive accuracy (see Supplementary Tables 14 and 15).

Experiment 1: efficacy expectations mediated the relationship between social comparison and effort investment

Multilevel Mediation Model: To test whether model-based efficacy estimates for the next round (t + 1) mediate the relationship between social comparison feedback at round (t) and effort investment at round t + 1 (indexed by mean RTs and CRPS, respectively), we conducted two 1-1-1 multilevel mediation models (level-1: rounds; level-2: participants) using round-level data in SPSS 25.00 with the MLmed macro (https://njrockwood.com/mlmed). These models included independent variable (X, social comparison feedback, coded as 0 = upward, 1= downward) at level-1, mediator (M, model-based efficacy estimates at level-1), and two outcome variables (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Y}_{1}$$\end{document}Y1, mean RTs, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Y}_{2}$$\end{document}Y2, CRPS all at level-1), which allowed us to estimate the mediation effect at the round level. Lateral comparison rounds were excluded from these analyses.

Model estimation was based on Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML). Indirect effects were assessed via Monte Carlo confidence intervals with 10,000 simulated samples and considered statistically significant at p < .05 when the 95% Monte Carlo confidence interval (CI) did not include zero.

Experiment 2: participants and experimental design

We recruited another 42 healthy participants with non-psychology backgrounds. All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal color perception. Two participants were excluded due to crash of computer program, and four participants were excluded for not completing the formal experiment. Additionally, two participants were excluded due to insufficient data per condition (< 30 rounds) after removing the eyeblinks or muscle artifacts (see below EEG acquisition and processing). Consequently, the final sample included 34 participants (9 males, 22.12 ± 1.91 years). We conducted a post hoc power analysis using G*Power68 for this sample size (n = 34), employing a paired-samples t-test (to test the difference between two dependent means). With an α level of 0.05, the achieved statistical power was 0.88. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the School of Psychology at Capital Normal University, and all participants gave written informed consent prior to the experiment. All ethical regulations relevant to human research participants were followed.

The experimental procedure in Experiment 2 was identical to that of Experiment 1, with one modification: the duration of target presentation was shortened to a randomly selected duration of 6 s, 7 s, and 8 s. This adjustment was made to reduce participant fatigue during the EEG experiment. Additionally, participants received a fixed payment of 95 RMB, independent of their task performance.

Experiment 2: EEG acquisition and preprocessing

Participants were seated 60 cm from the computer monitor in a dimly lit and sound-attenuated room. EEG data were recorded by 64-channel Neuroscan equipment (NeuroScan 4.3.1, USA), following the international 10–20 system of EEG electrode placement. The left mastoid served as the reference electrode, and the center of the forehead served as the ground electrode. Vertical electrooculograms were placed above and below the right eye to record eye blinks, while horizontal electrooculograms were placed at the outer canthi of the eyes to record eye movements. All electrode impedance were kept below 5 kΩ during recording, and the sampling rate was set at 500 Hz.

Offline EEG data were preprocessed using the EEGLAB toolbox (http://sccn.ucsd.edu/eeglab) in MATLAB2018b. The EEG data were re-referenced to the average of the left and right mastoid electrodes and filtered with a band-pass of 0.1–40 Hz. Independent components analysis (ICA) was performed to detect and remove eye blinks and movement artifacts. For ERP analysis, the cleaned EEG data were then divided into epochs for the cue phase (−200 to 1400 ms) and the feedback phase (−200 to 1500 ms). Baseline correction was applied using a pre-stimulus interval of 200 ms for each epoch. Any epoch with amplitudes exceeding ± 100 μV was excluded from further analysis, resulting in the exclusion of 10.24% of epochs from the cue phase and 11.27% from the feedback phase.

Time-frequency analysis was performed on the preprocessed EEG data using MATLAB2018b. The ICA-cleaned EEG data were segmented into epochs from −600 to 1400 ms for the cue phases. Epochs with amplitudes exceeding ± 100 μV were removed, resulting in the exclusion of 10.96% of epochs from the cue phase and 12.15% from the feedback phase. The EEG data were then analyzed using short-time Fourier transform (STFT) with a fixed 400 ms Hanning-tapered window. A pre-stimulus time interval from −400 to −200 ms was used for baseline correction, and power values were converted to a decibel scale (dB, 10 × log10).

As in Experiment 1, incorrect trials (7.96% of total trials) were first excluded from each round for each participant. Following this, we calculated the mean RTs for each round. Rounds with mean RTs exceeding three standard deviations (1.39% of total rounds) and CRPS values beyond three standard deviations (1.88% of total rounds) were subsequently removed from each social comparison condition for each participant.

We used the same analytical approach as in Experiment 1, including the RL model, LMMs, HDDMs, and multilevel mediation models. Experiment 2: EEG analyses

To explore how participant evaluate social comparison feedback, we analyzed three feedback-locked ERP components: the RewP component (300–350 ms, across C1, Cz, and C2 electrodes), the fb-P3 component (350–450 ms, across CP1, CPz, and CP2 electrodes), and the LPP component (600–1000 ms, across CP1, CPz and CP2 electrodes) during the social comparison feedback phase. Time windows and electrode Regions Of Interest (ROIs) were defined using a collapsed localizers approach69 and based on previous studies9,10,39,70. The RewP component is a fronto-central positive deflection peaking at 250–350 ms post-feedback onset, which is sensitive to reward feedback valence9,39,44. The fb-P3 component is a centro-parietal positive deflection that peaks at 300–600 ms post-feedback onset, reflecting the evaluation of feedback valence and magnitudes9,19,39. Statistics and reproducibility

Across both experiments (Experiment 1: n = 32 participants; Experiment 2: n = 34 participants), statistical analyses and computational modeling were integrated across several platforms. RStudio (v2025.09.0 + 387; Posit) served as the primary environment for analyzing post-task ratings (ANOVAs), fitting and validating the reinforcement learning models (Hierarchical Bayesian Modeling, parameter recovery, and PPCs), performing all linear mixed-effects (LMM) analyses—including standardized coefficient estimation (95% CIs) and simulation-based power calculations, and posterior-based hypothesis testing for the HDDM parameters.

HDDM fitting, model comparison, and posterior predictive checks were implemented in Python (v3.8) using the HDDM library. Electrophysiological data, including EEG preprocessing and round-level EEG measures extraction, were conducted in MATLAB via the EEGLAB toolboxes. Finally, multilevel mediation analyses were executed using the MLmed macro in SPSS.
