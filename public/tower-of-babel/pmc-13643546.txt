Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Lifetime Management of Aortic Valve Disease

The concept of lifetime management has become increasingly relevant as transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) expands to lower-risk patients with longer life expectancy, and for aortic valve reinterventions—so-called valve-in-valve procedures, including TAV (transcatheter aortic valve)-in-SAV (surgical aortic valve) procedure for failed surgical bioprosthesis and TAV-in-TAV procedure for failed transcatheter valves—are being performed with increasing frequency.1,2 Consequently, clinicians are now encountering patients who require a third aortic valve intervention after one or more prior transcatheter interventions.

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Bianchini et al3 describe a compelling example of such a scenario: a TAV-in-TAV-in-SAV procedure (Evolut FX-in-Lotus Edge-in-Epic) performed in a high-surgical-risk patient. The patient initially underwent a surgical aortic valve replacement with an Epic #27 in 2008, followed by a TAV-in-SAV procedure using Lotus Edge #25 in 2019. Several years later, progressive valve dysfunction necessitated a third intervention.

Prior to implanting the Evolut FX #29, the operators performed aggressive predilatation of the Lotus-Epic complex, resulting in improved Lotus valve expansion and fracture of the Epic valve frame. This strategy enabled optimal expansion of the third valve and excellent postprocedural hemodynamics. This case highlights 2 important considerations for lifetime management of aortic valve disease.

Importance of Addressing TAV Underexpansion and Optimizing Valve Expansion

TAVs, unlike most surgical bioprostheses, are frequently underexpanded in vivo relative to their nominal size. This has also been reported after TAV-in-SAV procedures,4 in which valve sizing is based on the true internal diameter of the prior SAV and often involves intentional oversizing to ensure anchoring and fixation.

As illustrated in the present case, underexpanded or deformed TAVs may lead to asymmetric leaflet opening, suboptimal hemodynamics, and potentially reduced durability.5 Without aggressive balloon expansion of the Lotus-Epic complex, the third valve would have remained significantly underexpanded, resulting in suboptimal valve performance and raising concerns regarding long-term valve durability. This case underscores the importance of optimizing valve expansion at each step of the lifetime management pathway, particularly when multiple transcatheter valves are anticipated.

Evaluating the Risk of Coronary Obstruction and Sinus Sequestration

A second critical consideration in a third aortic valve intervention is the complex assessment of the risk of coronary obstruction or sinus sequestration. In a TAV-in-TAV-in-SAV scenario, 2 layers of pinned open leaflets will be present, making it essential to determine which leaflet or neoskirt plane will be taller: the top level of the pinned leaflets of the first SAV or the neoskirt of the second and third TAV-in-TAV combination. In the Evolut-in-Lotus combination, the neoskirt plane corresponds to the top of the stent frame of the Lotus valve.6 Of note, because of the unique design of the Lotus valve, the neoskirt plane for this combination is considered higher than the pinned open leaflets. Careful CT evaluation is required to determine the relationship between the tallest neoskirt plane, coronary risk plane, and the sinotubular junction. In the reported case, the authors identified that the neoskirt for Evolut-in-Lotus would be above the Epic leaflets but still below the coronary risk plane, allowing the procedure to be performed with minimal risk of coronary obstruction.

However, when the neoskirt plane is above the coronary risk plane or above sinotubular junction, more cautious assessment is required. Further, if use of a balloon expandable TAV or balloon expansion (pre or postdilatation) is planned, the valve-to-aorta distances may decrease during the TAV-in-TAV procedure by index TAV expansion.7 Operators should therefore estimate the potential valve-to-aorta decrease and estimate the risk based on the maximal expected expansion.

Conclusions

The case reported by Bianchini et al3 exemplifies key considerations in the lifetime management of aortic valve disease. As patients increasingly undergo multiple transcatheter interventions, procedural optimization at each step becomes a crucial determinant of long-term success. The lessons from this case will continue to inform strategies for managing the growing population of patients who will live through multiple generations of aortic valve therapy.
