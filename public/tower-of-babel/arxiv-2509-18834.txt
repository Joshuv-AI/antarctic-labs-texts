Quantum-memory-assisted on-demand microwave-optical transduction
Hai-Tao Tu 1,2,3 , Kai-Yu Liao 1,2,3 , Si-Yuan Qiu 1,2 , Xiao-Hong Liu 1,2 , Yi-Qi Guo 1,2 , Zheng-Qi Du 1,2 , Yang Xu 1,2 , Xin-Ding Zhang 1,2 , Hui Yan 1,2,4,5 and Shi-Liang Zhu 1,2,3,5

Abstract
Microwave-optical transducers and quantum memories are fundamental components of quantum repeaters, essential for developing a quantum internet in which solid-state quantum computers serve as nodes interconnected by optical fibers for data transmission. Although both technologies have made significant advancements, the integration of microwave-optical conversion and quantum memory functionalities remains a challenge. Here, we theoretically propose and experimentally demonstrate a memory-enhanced quantum microwave-optical transduction using a Rydberg ensemble. By utilizing a cascaded electromagnetically induced transparency process, we store microwave photons in a highly excited collective state and subsequently convert them into optical photons during the retrieval process. Taking advantage of the optical depth with order of millions for microwave photons in Rydberg ensemble, combined with a minimal storage dephasing rate at the single-photon level, the transducer achieves an area-normalized storage efficiency greater than 90%, a bandwidth of 2.1 MHz, and a noise equivalent temperature as low as 26 K, even in cavity-free conditions. Our findings pave the way for the practical implementation of quantum repeaters based on atomic ensembles in quantum information processing.



The fundamental goal of quantum science and technology is to build a quantum internet where quantum computers serve as nodes, connected by optical fibers as data transmission lines
KimbleNature2008
;
WehnerSci2018
. The leading candidates for quantum computers are solid-state qubits, including those based on superconducting circuits
ClarkNature2008
;
DevoretScience2013
, nitrogen-vacancy centers
PompiliScience2021
, and semiconductor quantum dots
ArquerScience2021
. In addition to quantum computers and optical fibers, two other essential components are required to establish such a quantum internet: microwave-optical (MO) transducers and quantum memories. The solid-state quantum computers typically operate within the microwave (MW) frequency range; however, the loss of MW signals during transmission is significant. This necessitates the use of MO transducers to convert quantum information carriers, represented by MW photons, into optical photons
LambertReview2019
;
LaukReview2020
;
XiangRMP2013
;
XHan2021
. Since optical photons experience less loss in optical fibers, long-distance quantum information transmission can be achieved through optical fibers. The establishment of long-distance entanglement between nodes relies on the Bell-state measurement of two optical photons arriving simultaneously at the beam splitter of a middle station
Duan2001
;
Sangouard2011
. Synchronicity is crucial for the successful realization of entanglement in the protocol, and it is generally ensured by directing the photons into a quantum memory and retrieving them at the required time to guarantee simultaneity
LvovskyNP2009
.

Extensive research has been conducted on the aforementioned transducers and quantum memories, yielding a series of significant advances. Quantum transducers have been experimentally realized or theoretical proposed in a variety of systems, ranging from optomechanical systems

;

By utilizing an intermediate system that coherently couples to both MW and optical modes in steady states, all of these demonstrations must adhere to direct transduction protocols, where heralded MW photons are instantly converted into optical signals without delays.
However, direct transduction schemes face issues such as atomic heating and additional optical noise introduced by continuous-wave optical pumping during the conversion process
Kurokawa2022
.
On the other hand, quantum memory is regarded as a vital element in quantum information processing, serving as a synchronization tool that aligns various processes in quantum computing, a device for converting heralded photons into on-demand photons, and a buffer for temporally isolating intensive optical pumping. Quantum memories have also been experimentally implemented in atomic ensembles and doped rare-earth solid-state systems

;
Julsgaard2013
;
Ranjan2020
;
Flurin2015
;
Reagor2016
;
Pfaff2017
;
Bao2021
;
Palomaki2013
;
Liu2023
;
Hsiao2018
;
Wang2019
. Currently, there is a pressing need to develop quantum memories that can achieve both high efficiency and long-duration storage. Of course, integrating these components to perform various functions remains a highly challenging task. The development of a single device capable of multiple functions is an important direction for advancement.

In this article, we theoretically propose and experimentally realize an on-demand microwave-optical quantum transducer (OMQT) with integrated conversion and quantum memory capabilities based on a Rydberg ensemble. By employing an electromagnetically induced transparency (EIT) method, we store MW photons in a highly excited collective state and subsequently convert them into optical photons during the retrieval process. Benefiting from the optical depth (OD) with order of millions for MW photons in the Rydberg ensemble, combined with a minimal storage dephasing rate at the single-photon level, we establish optimal control strategies for the storage and retrieval of photon wave packets. This results in an area-normalized storage efficiency (ASE), defined as the ratio of recalled optical photons to input MW photons, exceeding 90%, along with a storage time of 0.56
\mu s
at the threshold efficiency of the no-cloning quantum regime, a bandwidth of 2.1 MHz, and a noise-equivalent temperature as low as 26 K under cavity-free conditions. Our findings pave the way for implementing quantum repeaters based on atomic ensembles. In the near term, they also provide an additional degree of freedom for detection applications, such as radio astronomy
Komatsu2022
and next-generation MW sensors
Schlossberger2024
in the single-photon domain.

Results

Theoretical scheme and experimental setup

The OMQT we proposed is a device that combines the functionalities of a quantum memory and a MO transducer. Specifically, this device allows a MW photon to be stored for a predetermined duration before being up-converted into an optical photon on demand. The OMQT has numerous potential applications, one of which is depicted in Fig.
1
. This application illustrates the generation of entanglement between a solid-state qubit Q
a
located in node
A
and a qubit Q
b
in node
B
. Each node comprises solid-state qubits and its respective OMQT. Initially, a Bell state is established between the solid-state qubits Q
x
(where x = a, b) and the corresponding MW photon M
x
. The MW photon M
x
is then directed into the OMQT
x
for on-demand storage. Subsequently, M
x
is up-converted into an optical photon Ph
x
, which becomes entangled with qubit Q
x
. The optical photon Ph
x
is subsequently transmitted to a central station. At this station, a Bell-state measurement involving the photons Ph
a
and Ph
b
serves as a herald for the successful generation of entanglement between qubits Q
a
and Q
b
. The on-demand capability of the OMQT is crucial, as it ensures that both optical photons reach the central station simultaneously.

In a proof-of-principle demonstration, we utilize a cold
87
Rb ensemble in a two-dimensional magneto-optical trap (MOT) to implement OMQT. Figure 1b presents a simplified diagram of the experimental setup, which is described in greater detail in the Methods. The quantization axis is defined by a 6.4 G bias magnetic field aligned along the
z
-direction. Our OMQT scheme can be regarded as the photon memory process consisting of two sets of EIT mediated by a Rydberg ensemble. The relevant atomic levels and control-field waveforms are illustrated in Fig.1c and 1d, respectively. During the storage process, atomic population is initially coherently trapped in a superposition state encompassing the ground level
|1\rangle
and the Rydberg level
|3\rangle
. With the aid of dressed states, MW photons that resonate with the Rydberg transition
|3\rangle\rightarrow|4\rangle
are introduced into the ensemble under cascade-type EIT conditions while the write field is activated. Subsequently, the MW photon is stored as a collective excitation in the Rydberg state
|5\rangle
after the write field is turned off. Once the designated storage period concludes, the excitation is retrieved in reverse from the
|6\rangle\rightarrow|1\rangle
transition and converted into an optical photon by applying the read field to the ensemble
Wang2019
. Under conditions of weak excitation, the transduction dynamics for the MW and optical field envelopes
\Omega_{\rm{M}}(z,t)
and
\Omega_{\rm{L}}(z,t)
are described by the first-order Maxwell-Bloch equations:

\left(\partial_{z}+\frac{1}{c}\partial_{t}\right)\binom{\Omega_{\rm{M}}(z,t)}{\Omega_{\rm{L}}(z,t)}=-\frac{i}{2}\binom{n_{3}\sigma_{\rm{M}}\Gamma_{4}P_{43}(z,t)}{n_{1}\sigma_{\rm{L}}\Gamma_{6}P_{61}(z,t)},

(1)

where
P_{ij}
denotes the first-order atomic coherence associated with the transition
|i\rangle\leftrightarrow|j\rangle
,
\Gamma_{j}
represents the total decay rate of the atomic coherence at level
|j\rangle
,
n_{j}
is the number density of atoms in state
|j\rangle
, and
\sigma_{M}
(
\sigma_{L}
) is the atomic absorption cross section for the MW (optical) transition. The parameter
d_{M}=n_{3}\sigma_{M}L
(
d_{L}=n_{1}\sigma_{L}L
) denotes the OD of the MW (optical) transition for an ensemble of length
L
.

It is noteworthy that the maximum efficiency of EIT-based memory is solely dependent on the OD of the medium when optimal control strategies are employed for the storage and retrieval of photon wave packets
Gorshkov2007a
. Furthermore, the OD for MW frequencies can readily reach the order of millions, owing to the large electric dipole moment
\mu
and long lifetime (i.e., small decay rate
\Gamma
) of Rydberg states since the absorption cross section
\sigma_{M}=\frac{4\pi|\mu|^{2}}{\lambda\varepsilon_{0}\hbar\Gamma}
, where
\lambda
is the wavelength and
\varepsilon_{0}
is the permittivity of free space. In contrast, the OD observed for optical photon is typically several hundreds. This raises the question of whether this characteristic can be extended to our EIT-memory-based transduction method. If so, we could achieve high ASE for MO transducer, benefiting from the optimal control strategies utilized in quantum memory for photons
Wang2019
;
Hsiao2018
. To investigate this possibility, we solve Eq. (
1
) to calculate the ASE. In the context of EIT-memory-based transduction, the ASE is influenced by three key factors: transmission efficiency
\eta_{t}
, spin-wave survival efficiency
\eta_{s}
, and the fraction
\eta_{c}
of Gaussian input pulse that is compressed into the cigar-shaped ensemble during the writing process. Thus, the ASE can be expressed as
\eta=\eta_{t}\eta_{s}\eta_{c}
. For short storage time and large OD, both
\eta_{s}
and
\eta_{c}
approach unity, indicating that the ASE is primarily determined by
\eta_{t}
. By solving Eq. (
1
) under the conditions of forward propagation and backward retrieval of a Gaussian wave packet, we derive the ASE (
\eta
) for short storage times as follows:

\eta\simeq\eta_{t}=\eta_{0}e^{-2\gamma_{51}t_{d}},

(2)

where
\eta_{0}=1/\sqrt{(1+\alpha_{M}/{d_{M}})(1+\alpha_{L}/d_{L})}
,
\gamma_{51}
denotes the decay rate of Rydberg coherence between the
|1\rangle
and
|5\rangle
states,
t_{d}
represents the total delay time, and
\alpha_{M}
(
\alpha_{L}
) is a dimensionless coefficient linked to the MW (light) delay time, the decoherence rates, and the pulse full width at half maximum (FWHM) duration. Using the relevant experimental parameters
\gamma_{51}/2\pi=10.8
kHz,
t_{d}=623
ns,
d_{M}=(7.5\pm 0.3)\times 10^{5}
,
d_{L}=122\pm 4
,
\alpha_{M}=61.61
, and
\alpha_{L}=0.44
, we obtain an ASE
\eta\simeq 0.92
for the single-photon-level transduction.

We conduct the experiment according to the outlined scheme. Following the loading of the MOT, the atoms are optically pumped into the ground state
|1\rangle=|5S_{1/2},F=2,m_{F}=2\rangle
. The maximum OD for the transition
|1\rangle\leftrightarrow|2\rangle=|5P_{3/2},F=3,m_{F}=3\rangle
is measured to be
140\pm 4
. Two auxiliary beams,
\Omega_{A}
and
\Omega_{R}
, sourced from different 480 nm lasers, propagate collinearly along the
z
axis and counterpropagate with additional auxiliary fields
\Omega_{P}
and
\Omega_{W}
. For demonstration, the signal MW is launched into free space via a helical antenna and directed toward the atomic cloud at a
3^{\circ}
angle relative to the
z
-axis.
By adjusting the power of the probe laser, we can significantly manipulate the population in the Rydberg state
|3\rangle=|47S_{1/2},m_{J}=1/2\rangle
in the presence of the auxiliary fields, thereby regulating the OD of the MW transition
|3\rangle\leftrightarrow|4\rangle=|47P_{3/2},m_{J}=3/2\rangle
. The other relevant energy levels include
|5\rangle=|46D_{5/2},m_{J}=1/2\rangle
and
|6\rangle=|5P_{3/2},F=2,m_{F}=1\rangle
. The experiment is conducted in an ambient environment at approximately
300
K, where only the paraxial background radiation propagating within a tiny solid angle can be efficiently stored in our needle-shaped atomic gas and converted into optical photons (see Supplementary Section IV). Following the backward recovery process, the optical photons
\Omega_{L}
propagate along the probe laser beam, after which they are collected using a single-mode fiber and a series of optical filters. Signal photons are detected using single-photon counting modules (Perkin Elmer, SPCM-AQRH-16), and coincidence counts are recorded by a time-to-digital converter (Fast Comtec P7888) with a time bin of
1
ns. The probe beam is detected by an avalanche photodiode (APD) for OD measurement, and a
50/50
fiber beamsplitter is employed to conduct the Hanbury Brown-Twiss (HBT) experiment.

Experimental results

We first search the phase-matching direction through realizing a direct MO transduction via a six-wave mixing process, which is driven by the constant and all-resonant auxiliary fields, and then achieve the OMQT to benchmark the noises. The Gaussian input pulse has a FWHM of
T_{p}
= 300 ns. Without the temporal isolation of the read and write process, the probe laser beam increases the stray photon noise compared with OMQT, and hence the input pulse is set with a higher mean photon number per input MW pulse
\bar{N}
= 130 for a sufficient signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). The direct transduction has a relatively low efficiency (
\eta\approx
32%) mainly because
\eta

|D\rangle\propto\left(\Omega_{\mathrm{W}}^{*}\Omega_{\mathrm{A}}^{*}|1\rangle-\Omega_{\mathrm{W}}^{*}\Omega_{\mathrm{P}}|3\rangle+\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}^{*}\Omega_{\mathrm{P}}|5\rangle\right)
. Then we carry out the on-demand transducer following the time sequences described in Fig.
1
d. Representative signal counts for
\bar{N}
= 0.1 are illustrated in Fig. 2a (middle) for the storage periods of 50 ns and 550 ns. The total noise count after a 50 ns storage time is 0.119 per pulse, where the noise sources contain thermal photon noise (
\bar{n}_{th}

\sim
0.109 per pulse), Rydberg fluorescence, stray photon noise (
\bar{n}_{st}

\sim
0.01 per pulse), and SPCM dark counts. That is to say, among them the 92% counts are derived from the stored thermal MW photons (Fig. 2b). In the absence of input photons, the ratio of thermal counts to other intrinsic noise is 10.9, and this proportion gives rise to a noise-equivalent temperature of
T_{NE}
= 26 K. Without the additional cavity-assisted filter, our system that benefits from the temporal isolation of storage process features a low noise-equivalent temperature, which is only half of that reported in the direct transduction scheme

We observe that the retrieved pulse shapes, unlike the input waveform, exhibits a slowly falling tail at the rear probably due to the transverse inhomogeneity of recovery process. During the storage stage, the input MW is homogeneously mapped in the cross section of medium under the broadband EIT condition. After turning on the focused read beam, the spin wave is recovered but the rim light backward propagates with a lower velocity than the centered light, leading to an asymmetric waveform of retrieved signal. The same phenomenon is also observed for the recovered waveform of the thermal MW photons, as shown in Fig.2a (bottom).

Discussions and conclusions

The direct MO transduction method
Han2018
;
Tu2022
;
Kumar2023
;
Borowka2024
relies on a steady-state process that requires impedance matching conditions to achieve complete conversion. In contrast, our OMQT employs a distinct mechanism: through adiabatic storage operations with time-dependent modulation and non-steady-state dynamics, we suppress the influence of dark states
Han2018
;
Tu2022
, thereby eliminating their detrimental effects on transduction efficiency.
Furthermore, the OMQT incorporates quantum memory-optimized control strategies
Gorshkov2007a
;
Wang2019
and exhibits a parameter-dependent efficiency profile fundamentally different from direct MO transduction.
The exceptionally high OD for MW photons enables near-unitary ASE, which raises the theoretical upper limit of conversion efficiency significantly beyond that achievable with conventional approaches.

Beyond these, our OMQT offers additional advantages for practical application. First, it integrates quantum storage and MO conversion functions, creating a unique device for synchronization and storage essential for generating entanglement between distant nodes in a quantum network.
Second, the OMQT requires only a simple optical filter to achieve MO conversion for the incident single-photon energy, whereas conventional ambient MO transduction methods require an average photon number of

\bar{N}>50
to observe detectable conversion
Tu2022
due to their limited signal-to-noise ratio. Finally, the OMQT exhibits a broader bandwidth, as the system does not necessitate reaching a steady state.

Several strategies can be adopted to enhance the OMQT’s performance further. First, the Rydberg dephasing inherently limits the storage time in OMQT. By employing a two-photon transition to transfer collective excitations from the Rydberg state
|5\rangle
to a hyperfine clock state, we can eliminate residual spin wave momentum and suppress decoherence, thereby extending the storage time to the second level. Second, unlike optical quantum memory, our OMQT utilizes different EIT channels to store and recall photons, effectively avoiding noise photons generated from undesirable multi-wave mixing associated with auxiliary fields
Hsiao2018
. Specifically, we can significantly reduce the noise-equivalent temperature by inserting a narrowband etalon to minimize stray photon noise, thereby improving signal-to-noise ratio for transduction.

In summary, we have theoretically proposed and experimentally demonstrated a Rydberg atom-based on-demand MO transducer characterized by a high ASE for single MW photons. Our methods can be extended to various types of MO transducer systems

;

;
XHan2021
. A more challenging extension involves realizing our OMQT using Rydberg atoms trapped in superconducting circuits

;

, especially inside the millikelvin cryogenic environment that is suitable for superconducting qubits integration
LandraPRA2019
. This quantum-memory-enhanced transducer serves as an ideal quantum interface for establishing a quantum network between solid-state quantum nodes, thereby expanding the entanglement resources available for distributed quantum computing. Additionally, the high-performance transducer lays the groundwork for next-generation radiofrequency sensing.

SI. Transduction mechanism

As shown in Figure 1c of main text, we utilize a cascade-type five-level system to store the input MW photons, and then on-demand retrieve the optical photons in another cascade-type EIT system. In the writing scheme, the atomic-level system involves three Rydberg states, and in generally sustains the dipole-mediated interactions between atoms excited into Rydberg manifolds. Because the atomic density is not high (i.e., less than one atom within a blockade sphere) in a medium driven by the auxiliary fields
|\Omega_{P}|\ll|\Omega_{A}|
, we can ignore the photon blockade and utilize the effective dephasing terms to phenomenologically quantify the transmission property in the storage model. Assuming that the input MW is sufficiently weak, the writing process is regarded as a perturbation of the three-level atomic system
|1\rangle
,
|2\rangle
, and
|3\rangle

Hsiao2018

is small compared with the auxiliary fields
\Omega_{P}
and
\Omega_{A}

\rho_{11}=\frac{\left|\Omega_{A}\right|{}^{2}}{\left|\Omega_{P}\right|{}^{2}+|\Omega_{A}|^{2}},

SIV. Thermal background radiation

The OMQT can store the ambient thermal MW photons and converts them into the optical photons. Unlike the input MW signal, blackbody radiation is unpolarized and isotropic. For a given mode, the mean photon number is given by

\bar{n}(\nu,T)=\frac{1}{e^{h\nu/kT}-1},

(S31)

yielding approximately 170 photons at temperature
T\simeq
300 K and frequency
\nu

\Phi(\nu,T)=\int d\nu\int_{\Omega_{0}}d\Omega\frac{2\nu^{2}\bar{n}(\nu,T)}{c^{2}}\cos\theta\cdot A,

(S32)

where
d\Omega=\sin\theta d\theta d\varphi
is the unit solid angle, and
A=2\pi r^{2}+2\pi rL
is the surface area of the cylindric interaction medium (radius
r=66\mu
m and length
L
= 20 mm). Integrating over the 2.1 MHz storage bandwidth, the thermal photon flux is
\Phi\approx 295
MHz. Since only
\sigma^{+}
circularly polarized MW photons are converted in our scheme, the number of thermal photons participating in the storage process within the interaction time

550 ns is:

N=\frac{\Phi\times\tau}{2}\approx 81.2.

SV. Experimental parameter calibration.

Since the MOT lies in the far-field region of the antenna, the MW fields can be treated as plane waves relative to the small atomic medium. We first measure a moderate MW amplitude
|E|=\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm{M}}/|\bm{d}_{34}|
in the Autler-Townes splitting regime
Liao2020
, corresponding to a MW intensity
I_{\mathrm{M}}=\frac{|E|^{2}}{2}\sqrt{\frac{\epsilon_{0}}{\mu_{0}}}
, where
\mu_{0}
is vacuum permeability. Using these field amplitudes, we calibrate the MW generator power and extrapolate the low intensity setting for few-photon storage. The Rabi frequencies of the auxiliary optical fields (
\Omega_{\mathrm{P}}
,
\Omega_{\mathrm{A}}
, and
\Omega_{\mathrm{R}}
) are determined from their measured intensities.

As in prior works
Tu2022
;
JHan2018
;
Vogt2019
;
Borowka2024
, all auxiliary beams are focused onto the same end of the atomic ensemble. Due to the significantly larger beam waist of the blue light compared to the red light, photon storage is confined to the red-light volume where all beams overlap. The MW incident angle with respect to the
z
axis is small, so the MW receiving area corresponds to the transverse region relative to the
z
-axis. The ensemble length is comparable to the Rayleigh range of the red-light beam. Accounting to the Gaussian atomic density distribution and the red beam’s expansion along
z
, we derive the averaged MW storage cross-section below.

The longitudinal atomic density distribution is modeled as:

\tilde{n}(z)=n_{\mathrm{max}}e^{-2\left[(z-L/2)/\textit{w}_{\text{A}}\right]^{2}}\theta\left(z\right)\theta\left(L-z\right),

(S36)

where

is the Heaviside step function, and
\textit{w}_{\text{A}}
=

is the
1/e^{2}
half width of the atomic density distribution. For a given optical depth
d_{\text{L}}
and medium length
L

n_{\mathrm{max}}=\frac{{d_{\text{L}}}\Gamma_{2}}{\bar{\beta}}\left(\int_{0}^{L}e^{-2\left[(z-L/2)/\textit{w}_{\text{A}}\right]^{2}}\mathrm{~d}z\right)^{-1},

(S37)

where
\bar{\beta}=2\omega_{\mathrm{P}}\left|\bm{d}_{21}\right|^{2}/\left(\hbar\epsilon_{0}c\right)
and
\Gamma_{2}/2\pi
= 6 MHz (natural linewidth of rubidium). The normalized density distribut is

=

(S38)

(S39)

where the red beam radius at

(S40)

Here,

SVI. Remarks on the definition of the ASE

We here demonstrate that the ASE defined in Eq.(5) of the main text is equivalent to the flux-density method under the condition
S_{I}=S_{M}
. This condition is inherently satisfied in the collinear configuration employed in our study. Here, the average cross-sectional area
S_{I}
represents the effective beam area for photon retrieval and conversion. For a pulse duration
t_{0}
, the intensity of the converted field is:

{I_{L}}=\frac{N_{L}{\hbar\omega_{L}}}{S_{I}t_{0}},

(S43)

where
N_{L}
is the number of converted photons. The mean MW intensity is:

\bar{I}_{M}=\frac{\int I_{M}(t)\,dt}{t_{0}}.

(S44)

Therefore, we find an intensity-to-intensity efficiency
\eta_{ii}

\eta_{ii}=\frac{I_{L}/\hbar\omega_{L}}{\bar{I}_{M}/\hbar\omega_{M}}=\frac{N_{L}\hbar\omega_{M}}{S_{I}\int I_{M}(t)\,dt}=\eta,

(S45)

under the condition
S_{I}=S_{M}
. In this case, we establish the linear relationship between MW intensity (
I_{M}
) and source power, along with the averaged cross-section (
S_{I}
). By substituting
S_{M}=S_{I}
into Eq.(S26), we precisely tune the average MW photon number
\bar{N}
from 0.1 to 130 by adjusting the MW source power.

It demonstrated that the ASE remains a practical physical metric for characterizing the conversion efficiency in free-space configurations, although the interaction region is substantially subwavelength for the MW fields. As experimentally demonstrated in Ref.
Borowka2024
, the measured ASE in their system agrees with theoretical expectations. Similarly, in our experimental setup, the measured ASE aligns closely with the theoretical derivation detailed in Supplementary Section I.
The possible reasons are as follows. The ASE defined here is equivalent to the flux-density method. To precisely determine the flux-density in the experiment, the atomic ensemble resides in the far-field region, where the MW can be treated as a plane wave with a highly uniform and stable electric field.
