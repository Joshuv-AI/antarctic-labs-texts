Socioeconomic status, depressive symptoms and cardiometabolic kidney syndrome: an analysis based on national representative data
Authors: Yunzhe Li, Zixin Luo, Zhiyuan Zhang, Nan Huang, Li Xiao, Kang Zou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCardiometabolic Kidney Syndrome (CKM) is a global chronic disease challenge. While links between socioeconomic status (SES) and individual CKM components exist, research often overlooks CKM’s multi-system interactions and the role of psychosocial factors. Depression, prevalent in low SES groups and linked to CKM risk, may mediate the SES-CKM relationship.MethodsUsing 2005–2018 NHANES data with 11,367 participants, this study quantified SES via the family poverty-income ratio (PIR), assessed depressive symptoms with the PHQ-9 scale, and defined CKM per AHA criteria. Statistical analyses included multivariable logistic regression and structural equation modeling to examine associations and mediating effects.ResultsHigh SES was associated with 81% lower odds of advanced CKM (OR = 0.19, 95% CI: 0.10–0.36). Each unit increase in PIR was associated with 33% lower odds of depression (OR = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.63–0.72). Depression mediated 27.64% of the SES-advanced CKM link. A nonlinear threshold effect between SES and depression was found at PIR = 3.28, with subgroup analyses highlighting stronger SES-CKM associations in non-Hispanic Blacks and Whites.ConclusionSocioeconomic disadvantage is significantly associated with depressive symptoms and advanced CKM in this cross-sectional study. Depressive symptoms may play a substantial mediating role based on structural equation modeling of observational data. These findings suggest that incorporating mental health interventions into CKM prevention strategies and implementing targeted policies to address health inequities could potentially enhance health equity. Future prospective studies are needed to validate these findings before refining the CKM prevention framework.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25228-0.

Introduction

Cardiometabolic Kidney Syndrome (CKM), as a complex disease with multi-system interactions, has become a central challenge in the prevention and control of chronic diseases worldwide in recent years [1–3]. The staging criteria for CKM, published by the American Heart Association (AHA) in 2023, clearly state that its essence is a systemic imbalance driven by multi-organ interactions. The syndrome is characterized by a synergistic pathological process of cardiovascular dysfunction, metabolic abnormalities (e.g., insulin resistance, obesity), and chronic kidney disease, forming a vicious pathological network [2, 4]. Epidemiological data show that CKM has a very high prevalence in middle-aged and older populations and a significantly increased risk of death, highlighting its urgency as a central challenge in global chronic disease prevention and control [5, 6].

The association between Socioeconomic Status (SES) and single components of CKM (e.g., coronary heart disease, diabetes mellitus) has been clearly demonstrated [7, 8]. A number of studies have shown that people with low SES have a significantly increased risk of cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, and other diseases [9, 10]. However, there are two major limitations in these studies: first, single organ or metabolic indicators have been analyzed in isolation, ignoring the core features of CKM as a multi-system interaction network; and second, the role of psychosocial factors in bridging the association between SES and multi-organ co-morbidities has not been systematically assessed. This ‘fragmented’ research model makes it difficult for public health intervention strategies to precisely target the complex etiological network of CKM. In contrast, depression, a highly prevalent co-morbidity in low SES populations, has been shown to be independently associated with the risk of CKM [11, 12]. This dual association suggests that depression may be an important mediating variable in the transformation of SES disadvantage to CKM pathology [13]. Specifically, low SES induces psychopathological changes (e.g., negative cognitive biases, impaired emotion regulation) through resource constraints and chronic stress, whereas depressive states further drive the progression of CKM through multi-system interactions (e.g., autonomic imbalance, metabolic-inflammatory axis dysregulation) [14–16].

By systematically analyzing the complex association between SES, depressive symptoms, and cardiometabolic kidney syndrome (CKM), this study reveals for the first time the mediating role of depression in the development of advanced CKM in low SES populations and provides a scientific basis for breaking the vicious circle of ‘poverty-psychological stress-metabolic disorders,’ suggesting that future public health policy needs to incorporate mental health intervention into the CKM prevention and control system, thereby reducing the disease burden caused by health inequality.

Materials and methods

Study participant data sources and presentations

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), led by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) of the United States of America, is a biennial national survey designed to assess the nutritional and health status of the United States population and to provide a scientific basis for the development of public health policies. Each of its survey cycles covers a different population, and participants undergo a series of assessments (including physical exams, interviews, and laboratory tests) during a single cycle and are usually not re-contacted or followed up in subsequent cycles. Therefore, the data between 2005 and 2018 are not multiple measurements (follow-ups) of the same population but rather a collection of cross-sectional data from different groups at different times. We screened a total of 70,190 participants between 2005 and 2018. First, we excluded participants younger than 20 years of age (n = 30,441), then we excluded participants with a lack of data related to CKM (n = 21,413), a lack of data related to poverty-income ratio (PIR) as well as depression (n = 3,290), and lastly, participants with missing covariates (n = 3679) were also excluded. 3679) were also excluded by us. The final number of participants in this study was 11,367 (Fig. 1). This study utilized de-identified NHANES data with prior ethical approval by the National Center for Health Statistics Ethics Review Board. Definition of SES

This study adopts the PIR as the core indicator of SES. This choice prioritizes PIR’s superior objectivity and quantifiable nature compared to traditional metrics like education and occupation [10, 17, 18]. PIR more directly reflects actual capabilities for acquiring health-affecting resources, while avoiding complexities inherent in defining and comparing education and occupation indicators across groups [19, 20]. These attributes establish PIR as a widely recognized and reliable core SES measure [21, 22].

PIR was strictly calculated using the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) official poverty guidelines. Median values were applied for income ranges, and values ≥ 5.0 were uniformly set to 5.0 for privacy protection. Grouping emphasized practical resource accessibility and policy relevance: The Low SES group (PIR ≤ 1.3) corresponds to eligibility thresholds for federal assistance programs (e.g., SNAP), signifying significant economic hardship and constrained resources [23]. The Medium SES group (1.3 < PIR < 3.5) covers a substantial segment of the population with incomes between 130% and 350% of the poverty line, representing a critical spectrum from near-poverty to relative economic stability (https://www.britannica.com/topic/Patient-Protection-and-Affordable-Care-Act). The High SES group (PIR ≥ 3.5) indicates sufficient financial capacity to meet essential living needs and healthcare costs, substantially mitigating economic barriers to health resource access. This approach aims to capture meaningful gradients in resource availability influencing health (https://aspe.hhs.gov/poverty-guidelines).

Definition of depression

In this study, the PHQ-9 scale was used as a depression assessment tool to assess symptom severity. The total score was calculated by summing responses to nine binary questions, with values ranging from 0 to 27–higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms [24]. According to the International Diagnostic Criteria, a PHQ-9 total score ≥ 10 is defined as the threshold for clinically significant depressive symptoms [25].

Definition of CKM

The staging criteria for CKM are as follows: stage 0 (no risk factors, e.g., normal blood pressure); stage 1 (obesity or metabolic abnormalities); stage 2 (combined metabolic abnormalities or moderate-to-severe nephropathy); stage 3 (end-stage renal disease or 10-year high risk of CVD); and stage 4 (confirmed cardiovascular disease, e.g., coronary artery disease). In this article, stage 0 CKM is used as a reference, early CKM is defined as 1–2, and advanced CKM is defined as stage 3–4, as it suggests an existing or very high-risk state of cardiovascular disease. Specific indicators were defined according to the NHANES data standards (Supplement 1) [26].

Assessment of covariates

The core demographic factor dimensions (sex, age, race, educational level, and poverty-to-income ratio) were derived from the National Health Survey structured database. Where race was categorised as Mexican American, Other Hispanic, non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, or other. Educational level was categorised according to whether or not they had graduated from high school. With regard to behaviour and lifestyle, smoking history was classified according to the CDC questionnaire criteria, with lifetime smoking < 100 cigarettes defined as non-smokers and ≥ 100 cigarettes (including current and previous smoking) uniformly classified as smokers [27]; alcohol intake strictly followed the NIAAA high-risk drinking guidelines, with ≥ 4 drinks/day classified as excessive drinking [28], and the metabolic equivalence (MET) score was calculated according to activity, with a threshold of ≥ 500 MET/wk to classify the low/high-intensity group, and the participants were classified into two categories: low physical activity and high physical activity [29]. For physiological and metabolic indicators, body mass index (BMI) was calculated by certified clinical personnel using calibrated equipment to measure height and weight; criteria for determining hypertension included: (1) whether a doctor or other healthcare provider had told you that you had hypertension; (2) hypertensive status required meeting either threshold: systolic ≥ 140 mmHg or diastolic ≥ 90 mmHg on triplicate measurements; and criteria for the diagnosis of diabetes included: (1) whether a doctor or other healthcare provider had told you that you had diabetes mellitus; (2) fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; (3) 2-h postprandial glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL during 75 g OGTT; (4) glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.0%; (5) active hypoglycemic therapy (insulin/oral agents); Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD) is determined by (1) eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (CKD-EPI 2021 equation) and (2) UACR ≥ 30 mg/g. The selection of covariates for inclusion in the analyses was made on the basis that they exhibited an impact size effect of more than 10% for x, as well as a one-way regression p-value of < 0.5, ultimately combined with the clinical significance of previous studies (Table S1).

Statistical analysis

We analyzed the data statistically according to the guidelines set by the CDC. Appropriate weights were applied to account for oversampling of minorities, which ultimately provided unbiased and accurate effect estimates. We grouped the SES and used this to describe the baseline characteristics of the study population. Data collation and statistical analyses were based on the Storm Statistical Platform (www.medsta.cn/software) and R version 4.4.0, where normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean (SE), and comparisons between multiple groups were analyzed by ANOVA; categorical data were expressed as n (%) using chi-square tests or Fisher exact probability methods. To determine the association between SES, depression, and advanced CKM, weighted multivariate logistic regression models were applied multiple times. Model 1 was unadjusted, whereas Model 2 adjusted for sex, age, and race. Model 3 was further adjusted for education level, BMI, smoking history, alcohol intake, and metabolic equivalent scores. Immediately following this, we performed complex weighted RCS curves to test for potential nonlinear relationships and to identify inflection points via two-stage logistic regression. Subgroup analyses were performed to assess the effects of variables in different subgroups on the association between SES and advanced CKM. Finally, SEM combined with bootstrap sampling was used to test the mediating role of depressive symptoms in the association between SES and advanced CKM and to quantify the indirect effect of SES on CKM via depressive symptoms.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the participant population

A total of 11,367 participants were included in this study and analyzed based on SES divided into three groups (low, medium, and high). In terms of demographic characteristics, there was a significant difference in age between the groups, with the mean age of the high SES population being significantly higher than that of the low SES population. The high SES population had the highest percentage of males (51.46%), with the highest percentage of non-Hispanic whites (82.14%). The high SES population was generally more educated. In terms of lifestyle, the high SES population tended to have a better body mass index (BMI: 28.51), had the lowest percentage of smokers, but higher alcohol intake. In terms of health status, we found that the prevalence of depression in the population decreased substantially with increasing SES (15.3%−6.7%−3.4%). Significant differences in the proportion of CKM stages were observed across different SES groups within this population (p < 0.001). In the high SES group, 14.16% had advanced CKM, while the highest proportion (17.28%) was found in the low SES group. Most participants in all SES groups were at stages 1–2. Notably, the high SES group contained the largest proportion (10.68%) of stage 0 (no risk factors) participants (Table 1).

Association between SES and CKM

In model 3, each unit increase in PIR was associated with 14% lower odds of early CKM (OR: 0.86, 95% CI: 0.80–0.93), while showing a stronger inverse association with advanced CKM (35% lower odds; OR: 0.65, 95% CI: 0.55–0.76). When using low SES as the reference group, high SES individuals had significantly lower odds of early CKM (OR: 0.56, 95% CI: 0.41–0.76) and 81% lower odds of advanced CKM (OR: 0.19, 95% CI: 0.10–0.36). Middle SES showed no significant association with early CKM but had significantly lower odds of advanced CKM (OR: 0.48, 95% CI: 0.29–0.79) (Table 2).

Association between SES and depression

In model 3, each additional unit of PIR was associated with 33% lower odds of depression (OR: 0.67, 95% CI: 0.63–0.72). Using the low SES group as reference, middle SES was associated with 58% lower odds of depression (OR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.34–0.51), and high SES was associated with 77% lower odds of depression (OR: 0.23, 95% CI: 0.18–0.28) (Table 3).

Association between depression and CKM

In model 3, each additional point in depressive symptom scores was associated with 4% higher odds of early CKM (OR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.08) and 10% higher odds of advanced CKM (OR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.06–1.15). Subgroup analyses

The subgroup analysis revealed a significant negative association between PIR and both early and advanced CKM. In the advanced CKM analysis, race-level effects demonstrated marked interaction modification (P for interaction = 0.002). Specifically, PIR showed a statistically significant negative correlation with advanced CKM in non-Hispanic white and Black populations, while no statistically significant associations were observed in Mexican, other Hispanic, or other race groups. Conversely, factors such as sex, smoking history, alcohol intake, metabolic equivalent scores, and age exhibited no significant interactions in the advanced CKM analysis (all P for interaction > 0.05). The negative correlation between PIR and early CKM showed high consistency across subgroups, with only age demonstrating marginal interaction modification (P for interaction = 0.045). Other examined stratified factors—including sex, race, smoking history, alcohol intake, and metabolic equivalent scores—also failed to reveal significant interaction effects (all P for interaction > 0.05) (Table 6) (Table 7).

Discussion

This study systematically explored the association between SES, depression, and CKM and its underlying mechanisms based on nationally representative data. The core results can be summarized in the following four points: first, a strong graded negative association was observed between SES disadvantage and advanced CKM, with high SES demonstrating 81% lower odds of advanced CKM compared to low SES; second, depressive symptoms significantly mediated the association between SES and advanced CKM, with a mediating effect as high as 27.64%; and third, there was a non-linear threshold effect for the association between SES and depression, with an inflection point of PIR = 3.28; fourth, there was a strong and comparable association between SES and advanced CKM in the non-Hispanic Black and non-Hispanic White groups, and the strength of this association was significantly greater than that observed in other racial/ethnic groups.

The low SES group had 4.26 times higher odds of advanced CKM compared to the reference group. Additionally, each additional unit of PIR was associated with 35% lower odds of advanced CKM. This result is consistent with the social determinants of health theory that economic deprivation drives synergistic impairments of the metabolic-cardiovascular-renal system through multiple pathways (e.g., lack of healthcare resources, low health literacy, chronic stress accumulation) [30, 31]. Notably, the intensity of the effect of SES on advanced CKM was significantly higher than that of early CKM, suggesting that low SES may accelerate multiorgan dysfunction through prolonged exposure to adverse environments (e.g., air pollution, low access to healthy foods) and psychological stress [32–34]. This finding highlights the need to incorporate SES into risk prediction models for CKM and provides a target population for precision interventions.

Based on the observed mediating effect of depressive symptoms in the association between SES and advanced CKM (27.64%), we might speculate that mental health interventions could represent a potential target for mitigating the poverty-disease cycle. From a mechanistic perspective, it is plausible that depression in low SES populations may contribute to CKM progression through multiple pathways: at the neuroendocrine level, chronic stress-induced dysregulation of the HPA axis might lead to disturbed glucocorticoid secretion, which has been implicated in promoting vascular calcification and glomerulosclerosis [35, 36]. Behaviorally, depression-associated reduction in treatment adherence could potentially increase the rate of glycemic and blood pressure control failure [37]. At the molecular level, inflammatory factors might simultaneously exacerbate arterial plaque instability and renal interstitial fibrosis through activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [38]. However, it is important to note that depression did not significantly mediate the association in early CKM, a finding that could potentially be related to early lesions being primarily characterized by reversible metabolic disturbances, while late lesions appear more dependent on chronic inflammation and autonomic remodeling1. Furthermore, we should emphasize that depression is unlikely to be the sole explanatory factor for the SES-CKM association, even in disadvantaged groups.

Our results indicated that the significant negative association between SES and depression was no longer present when PIR exceeded 3.28. This threshold might reflect a phenomenon of diminishing psychological benefit among more economically advantaged groups, where beyond meeting basic needs, additional economic resources could potentially become less effective for mental health, while non-economic factors like work-related stress and social role conflicts might become more prominent contributors to depression [16, 39, 40]. Conversely, for populations with PIR below this threshold, we might speculate that economic assistance programs could potentially improve quality of life and physical health, with these improvements possibly translating into mental health benefits [41, 42]. Therefore, when considering intervention strategies for different SES groups, approaches might need to combine appropriate financial support for vulnerable populations with efforts to address relevant psychosocial factors [43]. However, it is crucial to emphasize that inferring mediation or causal pathways from our cross-sectional data remains speculative, and these potential implications for graded interventions would require validation through longitudinal or interventional studies.

This study reveals a significant and comparable negative correlation between SES and advanced CKM among non-Hispanic Black and White populations, while this association is relatively weaker in other ethnic groups. Notably, sample size differences significantly affect statistical power, with non-Hispanic White and Black populations constituting larger proportions compared to other ethnic groups. This suggests potential uncertainties in current data’s estimation of associations among minority populations. The association results between non-Hispanic White populations and early CKM shown in Table 6 may similarly reflect such limitations. Future research should expand minority population samples to validate trend generalizability and avoid over-interpreting biases caused by insufficient statistical power. Additionally, weaker correlations in other groups may stem from health migration effects, cultural protective factors (e.g., family support networks, traditional dietary habits), and buffering effects of intra-group heterogeneity. However, it should be noted that this does not equate to risk immunity—the health disadvantages of vulnerable immigrant populations may manifest with extended settlement duration [44].

The present study has some limitations. First, the cross-sectional design could not completely exclude reverse causation (e.g., increased depression due to CKM); second, the SES assessment was based only on income indicators and did not include multidimensional data such as education and occupation; and finally, variables such as depression were assessed relatively subjectively based on questionnaires, which may be biased. Future studies are needed to validate the causal time series through prospective cohorts and to conduct studies in other countries and regions.

Conclusions

This study suggests that depressive symptoms may play a partial mediating role in the association between socioeconomic disadvantage and the severity of cardiorenal metabolic syndrome. This finding suggests that mental health factors are important pathways for understanding health inequalities. Future studies should explore and validate the robustness of this study’s findings through a prospective design and a larger sample of multiethnic cohorts to provide an evidence-based foundation for the development of multilevel public health policies to reduce health inequalities in CKM.
