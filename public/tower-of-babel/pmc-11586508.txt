Non‐alkylating agents‐induced gonadotoxicity in pre‐pubertal males: Insights on the clinical and pre‐clinical front
Authors: Sruthi Sriram, Tiago Macedo, Annelies Mavinkurve‐Groothuis, Marianne van de Wetering, Leendert H J Looijenga
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhilst chemotherapy regimens have proven to be more successful for pediatric cancer patients over the years, their influence on long‐term side effects is relatively poorly understood. One of the possible targets is the gonads, with gonadotoxic agents representing those that threaten the patient's ability to have children post surviving the primary disease treatment. Many risk stratification guidelines have categorized these agents based on the severity of their effect on the pre‐pubertal testis. While the consensus is that those agents factored with a cyclophosphamide equivalent dosage pose the highest threat to fertility (e.g. alkylating agents), other agents might still contribute to a reduced testis function; especially in the case of combination therapies. Besides, it is important to note that studies deciphering the effect of other non‐alkylating agents on the pre‐pubertal testis lack standardized conclusions for clinically relevant outcomes. This makes it imperative to ensure the knowledge gap is addressed between the clinic and pre‐clinic to understand potential gonadotoxic effects, ultimately leading to improved patient care. Therefore, this review will summarize the key findings in understanding the gonadotoxic effects of the most commonly researched non‐alkylating agents: vincristine, etoposide, doxorubicin, and imatinib on the pre‐pubertal testis.

INTRODUCTION

Improved cancer survival rates over recent decades have led to a growing population of survivors, bringing increased attention to the long‐term side effects of treatments. One such critical aspect is infertility, as therapies can impair the reproductive function in both post‐ and pre‐pubertal patients. As such, oncofertility gained additional relevance as a key component of the survivors' quality of life. 1

For postpubertal males, freezing sperm before the start of cancer treatment is a well‐established fertility preservation method. However, this approach is not feasible for pre‐pubertal boys. Although there is no approved clinical method to restore fertility in these patients, some medical institutions provide the experimental option of cryopreserving a small sample of testicular tissue, presumed to contain the stem cells of the germ‐cell lineage, that is, Spermatogonial Stem Cells (SSCs). Current research on the subsequent steps to restore fertility has focused on two main approaches, 2 provided that the tissue contains indeed SSCs: (i) expanding those SSCs and transplanting them back on the testis to restore physiological spermatogenesis or (ii) differentiating SSCs in vitro to the stage of spermatid/spermatozoon, which can be used in assisted reproductive technology techniques to generate offspring.

The impact of chemotherapy regimens on the pre‐pubertal testis is hard to estimate, as it depends on several variables such as the drugs' cumulative doses, treatment length, patient age, and individual sensitivity. Given this, there have been growing efforts to categorize treatments based on their estimated gonadotoxicity risk 3 and establish guidelines with adequate recommendations for patients. 4

A consensus in the literature exists that alkylating and platin agents carry the highest risk of infertility, especially those factored in the cyclophosphamide equivalent dose. 5 In a recent review, we compiled the most relevant clinical and pre‐clinical studies that assess the individual gonadotoxicity of those agents for pre‐pubertal boys. 6 As little is known about the impact of other classes of agents on the pre‐pubertal testis, we hereby have broadened our search to include those that also have substantiated evidence of gonadotoxicity on either the clinical or pre‐clinical front.

Understanding the individual effect of drugs and drug classes on the pre‐pubertal testis is key to effectively advise young cancer patients about their long‐term fertility prospects after successful treatment and the potential need for fertility preservation procedures upfront of the start of treatment.

The growing interest in the impact of chemotherapy on fertility has recently led the PanCareLIFE Consortium to define fertility preservation recommendations for male patients with childhood, adolescence, and young adult (CAYA) cancer. 4 One of the criteria for suggesting the harvesting of testicular tissue for cryopreservation is precisely the cumulative dose of alkylating agents administered throughout the cancer treatment. Still, chemotherapeutic agents from alternative classes can also pose a moderate risk of infertility in pre‐pubertal boys. Our literature review revealed Topoisomerase II Inhibitors, Vinca Alkaloids, and Tyrosine Kinase Inhibitors, to be the most substantiated ones. These agents are frequently used in multi‐drug chemotherapeutic regimens alongside alkylating agents. Since Vinca Alkaloids and TKIs only have a single, relatively well‐explored drug concerning pre‐pubertal male gonadotoxicity ‐ Vincristine and Imatinib, respectively ‐ we have chosen to expand upon our findings on an individual agent basis. Furthermore, the intrinsic variability in findings across the different studies also supports an individual drug analysis.

Ultimately, our goal is to provide a general overview of the concordance as well as discrepancies between pre‐clinical and clinical studies. Bridging the insights from both fronts is crucial to support translational recommendations to be considered in clinical practice and thus facilitating a better understanding of their risk of inducing infertility while used at a pre‐pubertal age in males.

Search methodology

Our search focused on the most used non‐alkylating chemotherapy agents in childhood cancer. 7 The search strategy on PubMed involved combining each drug name with the keywords “Infertility,” “Testis damage,” “Testis toxicity,” “Testis” and “Spermatogenesis.” Additionally, to ensure the pre‐pubertal character of the models used, the following terms were included one at a time: “pre‐pubertal,” “developing,” “undifferentiated,” “immature,” “juvenile” and “young.” Among the commonly used agents, Etoposide, Doxorubicin, Vincristine, and Imatinib had the strongest evidence of gonadotoxicity in pre‐pubertal males, supported by either pre‐clinical and/or clinical studies.

PRE‐CLINICAL EVIDENCE OF TESTICULAR DAMAGE

In our literature search for pre‐clinical studies assessing the gonadotoxicity of chemotherapy treatments, we included only those that explicitly specified the pre‐pubertal/immature character of the models used. All studies were conducted using murines (mouse or rat), either in vivo or in vitro. Those conducted with mature/adult models are referred to in the “Adult models” section for each of the selected chemotherapy agents.

In vivo studies assessing germ cells

Etoposide has a clear gonadotoxic effect when administered in vivo in pre‐pubertal rats. Assessment of their testis immediately after 2 daily injections of 5 mg/kg revealed an increase in apoptotic cells, which were mainly differentiating spermatogonia and primary spermatocytes. The subsequent germ‐cell quantification showed a decrease in both intermediary and type B spermatogonia. A longer treatment regimen of 8 daily 5 mg/kg injections also increased the number of apoptotic cells, but these were mainly primary spermatocytes and round spermatids. As expected, the loss of germ cells was more significant than the prior regimen, with some tubules even exhibiting a “Sertoli‐only” phenotype. 8 Germ‐cell loss appears to be a result of increased apoptotic rates, as evidenced by the rise in the activity of caspases 3, 8, and 9 and the increased expression of the p53 protein (allows pro‐apoptotic gene expression). 9

Somatic cells assessment

There is evidence showing that etoposide can impair Sertoli cells. Eight injections of 5 mg/kg per day in pre‐pubertal rats led to the persistent loss of their capability to secrete transferrin, used by germ cells as their source of iron ions. While it could be hypothesized that the depletion of germ cells reduced their paracrine stimulus on Sertoli cells, there was no rebound increase in transferrin secretion once spermatogenesis recovered. This led the authors to theorize that etoposide could have a direct effect on Sertoli cell function. 10 In another study, the same regimen led to the progressive detachment of Sertoli cells from the seminiferous epithelium and migration to the tubular lumen, even after 148 days of recovery. Other reported characteristics include the presence of lipid inclusions and vacuoles in the cytoplasm and indentations in the nuclear membrane, which further evidence a persistent toxic effect on Sertoli cells. 11

Long‐term impact

In order to evaluate the long‐term impact of Etoposide, rats exposed to the 8 daily injections were also assessed after 32 and 95 recovery days. Interestingly, apoptosis decreased after a 32‐day recovery period relative to controls but exhibited an increase after 95 days of recovery, primarily affecting round spermatids. After the 32‐day period, there was an accentuated decline in the number of germ cells and significant tubular vacuolation, with evidence of “Sertoli‐only” tubular sections. However, these parameters exhibited substantial recovery in the 95‐day rats. 8 Later studies performed by the same authors with the same drug regimen further elucidated that the histological recovery of the seminiferous epithelium happened 95 days after drug administration, 10 , 12 with evident signs of reestablished germ‐cell numbers at 148 days. 10

Another interesting finding relates to the testis capacity to repopulate germ cells after etoposide treatment. Undifferentiated spermatogonia were evaluated with the labeling of UTF1, SOX2, and PLZF, as these markers were shown in prior studies to be relevant for identification across different species. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 While 12 h after treatment ended there was only a reduction in SOX2+ spermatogonia, later evaluations noted an increase in UTF1+, SOX2+, and PLZF+ 32 days after treatment and in SOX2+ and UTF1+ 95 days after. The increase in the numerical density of these spermatogonia was suggested by the authors to constitute a compensatory mechanism to re‐establish spermatogenesis after SSC damage. 19

Adult models

Regarding the effects on the adult testis, there is only a single in vivo study that analyzed the histological effects of etoposide on mice after 10–20 mg/kg dosages. Among them are aberrant spermatogonial metaphases, higher incidence of chromosomal aberrations, and distorted spermatocytes and sperm cells. In all experiments, the authors used a positive control treated cyclophosphamide, which induced more pronounced effects than Etoposide. 20 Moreover, Etoposide has been found to exhibit mutagenic effects, particularly in spermatocytes, while no such effects have been observed in SSC or differentiating spermatogonia. 21 , 22

Doxorubicin (studies summarized in Table 1)

In vitro studies assessing germ cells

In vitro studies also support the notion that doxorubicin is a gonadotoxic agent. Treating early pre‐pubertal rat testis fragments with 0.04 and 0.10 μg/mL led to germ‐cell loss at both 24 and 48 h, which was preceded by a reduction in proliferation and an increase in apoptosis. 23 Interestingly, another study that treated fragments from pre‐pubertal mice with 0.05 μg/mL did not find any evidence of increased apoptosis at multiple time points. Nonetheless, it was clear that doxorubicin was as gonadotoxic as cyclophosphamide and cisplatin. More specifically, there was a similar decline in both the density and proliferative capacity of germ cells 48 h after exposure to the three drugs, with doxorubicin causing a greater increase in DNA damage. 24

In vivo studies assessing germ cells

Younger pre‐pubertal rats (pnd 6) in the initiation phase of spermatogenesis were found to be more sensitive to doxorubicin than older (pnd 16–24), but still pre‐pubertal rats. Indeed, a 3 mg/kg dose in pnd 6 rats resulted in an evident apoptotic phenotype in spermatogonia, which exhibited nuclear fragmentation and condensation 24 and 48 h after treatment, accompanied by an increase in p53 expression. In contrast, pnd 16 and pnd 24 rats, there were no evident signs of apoptotic cells, and spermatogenesis progression was not impaired. 25 Nevertheless, subsequent studies have shown that older pre‐pubertal rats are still prone to doxorubicin‐induced gonadotoxicity, as underlined in the long‐term section.

Somatic cells assessment

The higher susceptibility at an early pre‐pubertal age also appears to extend to the somatic cell population, with treatment causing a reduction in density, atrophy, and fragmentation of the nucleus of Sertoli cells. Such abnormalities were not noted when treatment was administered to late pre‐pubertal rats. 25 , 26 There was also a tendency for a hyperplastic phenotype in Leydig cells from rats treated during early prepuberty, which was reported to persist several weeks after treatment. 26 , 27

Nonetheless, subsequent studies with rats treated at later stages showed induction of abnormal nuclear features 28 and increased apoptosis 29 in Sertoli cells.

In in vitro studies, there was a tendency for a reduction in Sertoli cell density 48 h after culturing rat testis fragments with 100 ng/mL, which was preceded by a reduction in proliferation. 23 However, another study revealed no impact on Sertoli cells, even when a 5× higher concentration (0.5 μg/mL) was administered. 24 Exposing a pre‐pubertal rat Sertoli cell line to 0.01–10 μM showed a dose‐dependent reduction in cell viability that was explained by an increase in ROS and oxidative damage to the DNA. 30

Long‐term impact

Studies performing long‐term evaluations of doxorubicin's gonadotoxic potential reveal conflicting results regarding the supposed lower sensitivity of older pre‐pubertal rats.

While it was found that younger pre‐pubertal rats (pnd 6–16) had persistent germ‐cell loss for up to 3 months after treatment, 26 , 27 pnd 24 rats were still affected, albeit with a recovery observed at the last time point. 26

Furthermore, even older pnd 30 rats were still significantly impacted by a 5 mg/kg doxorubicin treatment. Signs of recovery were ambiguous, with one study showing only occasional germ‐cell loss after 60 days, 31 and others denoting partial depletion of germ cells still present after 8–10 weeks. 29 , 32

Intermediary pre‐pubertal rats receiving 3 mg/kg at pnd 15 + 2 mg/kg at pnd 22 had a partial reestablishment of spermatogenesis 105 days after treatment at an intermediary age. 28

The only in vitro study analyzing the long‐term effects of doxorubicin noted that they seem to be less persistent than those of cisplatin. Using testicular tissue from pre‐pubertal mice (pnd 5) that had undergone an 8‐week recovery period, cisplatin was found to cause a reduction in the number of spermatocytes and total germ cells. In contrast, doxorubicin‐treated tissues (0.01, 0.03, and 0.05 μg/mL) all exhibited signs of recovery. 33

Adult models

The literature on the effects of doxorubicin on the adult testis is fairly extensive, with recent studies focused on finding cytoprotective agents to offset its gonadotoxicity. 34 , 35 , 36 In addition to studies that report a generalized reduction in germ cells and an increase in apoptosis and DNA damage, Gungor‐Ordueri and colleagues evaluated whether the toxic effects were related to PARP‐1 signaling molecules. PARP enzymes are involved in detecting and repairing DNA damage. 37 However, they become damaging to cells in the presence of extensive DNA damage. 38 , 39 After injecting mice with doxorubicin, there was an increase in PARP‐1 expression. When treatment included both doxorubicin and a PARP‐1 inhibitor, PJ34, there was a slight reduction in morphological testicular damage, an increase in live birth rates after mating, and complete prevention of the reduction in sperm motility. 40

In vitro studies assessing germ cells

The only in vitro study used a germline stem cell culture derived from mice. While imatinib did not hinder the self‐renewal of SSCs, as the cells could be maintained for more than a month after multiple passages at 1 μM, it reduced the number of differentiated spermatogonia. This was again hypothesized to be due to c‐KIT inhibition. 41 This receptor, besides being crucial for the migration of germ cells, is also required for the survival of differentiated spermatogonia. 42 , 43 It remains to be proven that this model is valid because of the absence of the interaction between the germ cells and somatic cell population.

In vivo studies assessing germ cells

When early pre‐pubertal murine models were treated with a cumulative dose of 400–2000 mg/kg of imatinib, there was a consistent blocking of the migration of gonocytes to the periphery of the seminiferous tubules, all observed within 5 days after the end of treatment. This was accompanied by a reduction in germ‐cell density due to increased apoptosis 44 and decreased proliferation. 45 Given this, the authors argue imatinib may severely compromise their ability to form the stem cell pool. 44 , 45 , 46 This was likely due to c‐KIT inhibition, as blocking this receptor was shown to impair germ‐cell migration. 47

Somatic cells assessment

Despite similar findings on germ cells, these studies diverge when it comes to the effect on Sertoli cells and the levels of hypothalamic–pituitary‐testicular axis hormones. A cumulative dose of 450 mg/kg in pre‐pubertal rats did not impact Sertoli cells, but led to an increase in inhibin B levels 80 days after treatment. 44 In another study, a similar cumulative dose of 400 mg/kg also did not impact Sertoli or Leydig cells. 45 Still, higher doses of 2000 mg/kg caused the vacuolization of Sertoli cells and their detachment from the basement membrane. 46

Long‐term impact

In vivo studies in murine models report that there is a there is a complete recovery in the seminiferous epithelium integrity within 70–115 days of 400–450 mg/kg treatment, 44 , 45 , 48 although a higher 2000 mg/kg treatment induced more persistent damage. 46 Furthermore, the expression of receptor tyrosine kinase signaling genes was also not affected by pre‐pubertal treatment when assessed in adulthood. 48

Akin to conventional clinical regimens, in which imatinib is taken daily, Tauer and colleagues assessed the impact of TKIs in pre‐pubertal rats by treating their drinking water with 1–2 mM of imatinib and 50–100 μM of dasatinib or bosutinib over a period of 10 weeks. Dasatinib and bosutinib are both second‐generation TKIs that share some of the targets with imatinib, such as TKs like stem cell factor/c‐kit. In line with the previous in vivo studies, there was no prolonged effect on gonadal function (assessed by the levels of testosterone and inhibin B). 49

Adult models

The adult testis also shows the same pattern of recovery. Treatment with 50–100 mg/kg twice daily for 5 days led to an increase in general cytotoxicity (assessed by the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release) and a decrease in testosterone levels up to 5 weeks after treatment. After that, there was a recovery in weeks 7 and 10, with LDH levels returning to those of control mice at the latter time point for all treatment doses. Histological damage to the testes, although reported, was not evaluated across all the established time frames. It included sloughing of the seminiferous epithelium, an increase in abnormal sperm, and lower sperm counts. 50 Using much higher total doses of 60–120 mg/kg once daily for 8 weeks on mice, damage features included a reduction and sloughing of the seminiferous epithelium, malformations in sperm, and vacuolization of Sertoli cells. Interestingly, the higher dosage even compromised the blood‐testis barrier in Sertoli cells, which buckled and loosened. Adding to the dissimilarity across the verified changes in hormonal levels, the authors described no changes in FSH, testosterone progesterone, and prolactin levels, while LH and estradiol had the same level of increase after all treatment doses. 51

In vitro studies assessing germ cells

The only completely in vitro study demonstrated a dose‐dependent decrease in viability using rat spermatogonial cell line treated with vincristine ranging from 1 nM to 10 μM, alongside an elevation in apoptosis. 52 Again, the validity of this model system still remains to be proven.

In vivo studies assessing germ cells

Three days after a 200 μg/kg vincristine injection, pre‐pubertal mice exhibited disrupted seminiferous epithelium features, including the appearance of vacuoles and cells with pyknotic nuclei. 53 A more in‐depth evaluation by the same authors after the same regimen further revealed a reduction in the percentage of seminiferous tubules containing proliferating cells, but no increase in apoptosis or DNA double‐strand breaks in spermatogonia was apparent. 54

Somatic cells assessment

Most studies did not evaluate somatic cells after vincristine treatment, but there is an indication that Sertoli cells might be impaired after a 50 μg/kg injection in rats. 55

Long‐term impact

More than 3 months after vincristine treatment in murine models the integrity of the seminiferous epithelium was still compromised. 53 , 55 Furthermore, vincristine significantly reduced the fertility index of treated mice, but did not impact offspring development. 53

When testicular fragments from mice treated with 100 μg/kg vincristine were cultured in vitro there was no impairment of spermatogenesis progression. 54

Adult models

Research utilizing adult models further substantiates the gonadotoxic effects of vincristine. Administration of both 1 and 2 mg/kg treatment regimens resulted in decreased sperm counts and elevated abnormalities within 1 month. However, it was observed that only the 2 mg/kg dosage led to a reduction in fertility index. 56 Additionally, a study conducted on mice revealed a noticeable decrease in germinal epithelium thickness 35 days following a dose of 1.5 mg/kg, attributed to the loss of spermatogonial cells. 57

CLINICAL EVIDENCE OF INFERTILITY

Topoisomerase II inhibitors

Type II topoisomerases are a group of enzymes that allow the untangling of DNA by making double‐stranded breaks. This step is crucial not only for the steps of DNA replication and transcription but also for chromosome segregation. 58 As such, drugs that inhibit these enzymes are commonly used in chemotherapy for their anti‐proliferative effect. Etoposide and Doxorubicin will be specifically referred to here as the representative examples that have the most evidence of gonadotoxicity.

Etoposide

Etoposide is one of the most active agents for pediatric neoplasms, testicular, and lung cancers. 59 To the extent of our knowledge, no study has shown that etoposide is particularly toxic to the male gonads.

In patients treated during childhood or adolescence, a cumulative dose of 1000 mg/m2 was shown to have no impact on FSH and LH levels, 60 the main hormones of the so‐called hypothalamic hypothalamic–pituitary‐testicular axis that regulates the testis development. Furthermore, no association was found between etoposide treatment and pre‐pubertal infertility, even when patients receiving cumulative doses ≥5000 mg/m2 were separately analyzed. 61

Similarly, in adults, intravenous and oral doses of 2400 and 4800 mg/m2, respectively, did not impair sperm counts in adult patients. 62

The only reports of impaired gonadal function stem from chemotherapy regimens that include etoposide in addition to established gonadotoxic agents. Still, experimental results in rodent models concluded that etoposide increased sperm aneuploidy, 22 , 63 which could help to explain the same findings after BEP (bleomycin, etoposide, and cisplatin) treatment for malignant germcell tumors in adults. 64 , 65

Doxorubicin

Doxorubicin is a crucial treatment component for the majority of childhood cancer patients. 66 Besides inhibiting topoisomerase II, doxorubicin is also able to intercalate into DNA and form reactive metabolites. 67 This additional cytotoxic profile contributes to higher rates of long‐term side effects, with cardiotoxicity being the most prominent one. 68

Literature evaluating the specific gonadotoxic effects of doxorubicin is very scarce. No clinical study on patients treated during childhood or adolescence was identified in this context. Besides, there is only a single reference including adult patients, 69 which received highly heterogeneous multi‐drug regimens. After doxorubicin doses that varied between 145 and 625 mg/m2, there was a significant portion of patients with reduced sperm numbers. 69

Moreover, Pryzant et al. 70 have hypothesized that higher doses of doxorubicin could cause a longer impairment of spermatogenesis. The authors verified in their study that 47% of patients treated with the CHOP‐Bleo (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, prednisone, and bleomycin) regimen recovered sperm counts to normospermic levels within 5 years. This regimen included cyclophosphamide doses above 9.5 g/m2 and a median doxorubicin dose of 450 mg/m2. However, in another study with patients who were treated with cyclophosphamide doses exceeding 7.5 g/m2 and higher doxorubicin doses ‐ median of 880 mg/m2 – the percentage of patients who recovered was only 11%. 71 So far, no further studies have built upon this notion. As these two publications are from the early 1990s and refer to adult patients, current treatment regimens may advise updated doses, especially when targeting pre‐pubertal males.

Tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs)

TKIs are a group of small molecules that inhibit the corresponding kinases from phosphorylating their substrates. These kinases coordinate the way the cells behave, regulating events like mitosis, differentiation, and apoptosis. 72 As such, abnormalities in their function caused by mutations, translocations, and amplifications are commonly at the base of tumorigenesis. 73 Given this, the advent of TKIs has significantly increased the survival rate of patients with such abnormalities on these receptors, for example in the treatment of chronic myeloid leukemia. 74

Imatinib

Imatinib was the first TKI approved by the United States Federal Drug Administration (FDA), and being the most extensively studied and described drug of this class. It has a broad spectrum of action against many tyrosine kinases such as CSF1R, ABL, c‐KIT, FLT3, and PDGFR‐β, which potentiates side effects like fatigue, musculoskeletal pain, and diarrhea. 75

In patients treated during childhood or adolescence, doses of 260–300 mg/m2 once daily for 2 years did not alter the levels of testosterone or inhibin B nor did it affect the timing of puberty onset. 49 These two hormones, produced by Leydig and Sertoli cells, respectively, are key indicators of testicular function. 76 The authors have further stated that some adolescents entered puberty during the treatment and exhibited the characteristic spike in testosterone. Although imatinib does not seem to be particularly gonadotoxic, there are two case reports of lowered reduced sperm counts in adolescents treated daily with a dose of 400–800 mg. 77 , 78

In a follow‐up study with adults, there was a correlation between higher doses of imatinib (600–800 mg) and reduced testosterone levels. 79 A later study further showed that 400 mg/day did not impact testosterone, FSH, and LH levels. Nonetheless, it reduced sperm count, density, survival rates, and activity. 80

Vinca alkaloids

Vinca alkaloids disrupt (cancer) cell division by binding to tubulin and preventing the microtubule polymerization needed for mitotic spindle formation. 81 This halts cell division and induces apoptosis. Additionally, they exhibit anti‐angiogenic effects by inhibiting new blood vessel formation crucial for tumor growth and metastasis. 82 Thus, vinca alkaloids are vital in cancer treatment and are generally administered as part of multidrug regimens. Vincristine will be discussed hereunder.

Vincristine

In pediatric oncology, vincristine is used as part of the treatment regimen for acute lymphoblastic leukemia, lymphomas, solid tumors, and brain tumors. 83 A recent systematic review concluded that there is no link between vincristine treatment in childhood and the likelihood of fathering a pregnancy, sperm count, or hormonal disruptions in adulthood. 84

While traditionally perceived as having minimal risk for gonadotoxicity, there is a rising need for more comprehensive investigations prompted by recent discoveries from pre‐clinical research.

FERTILITY PRESERVATION STRATEGIES

In addition to testicular tissue preservation, there are two other main approaches that may be offered to pre‐pubertal patients in the fertility preservation landscape: hormone therapy and treatment with cytoprotective agents. While both have substantial research ongoing, so far positive results have only been observed in murine models and do not seem to translate to the clinical setting.

Pre‐clinical hormone therapy research is concentrated on the administration of gonadotropin‐releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists and antagonists, and Testosterone. The initial line of thinking, with the multiple observations that rapidly proliferating cells are more sensitive to chemotherapy toxicity, was that halting the HPT axis would leave germ cells in a more “dormant”, and thus less susceptible, state. 85 Still, the finding that suppression of gonadotropin secretion does not impact the proliferative capability of germ cells and that SSCs do not appear to be affected by hormonal suppressions led to the disbandment of that hypothesis. 86 Nevertheless, although not consensual, some studies with adult animals reveal that treatment with GnRH‐modulating molecules can be beneficial in preventing part of chemotherapy‐induced gonadotoxicity. 87 , 88 Of the seven clinical trials that assessed the protective effects of hormone suppression techniques, only one had favorable results. 86 Based on this disparity and lack of trials with pre‐pubertal patients, the PanCareLIFE Consortium does not recommend offering hormone therapies, even for patients at higher risk of infertility. 4

The addition of cytoprotective agents to chemotherapy regimens has also been explored as an option to protect the testis against the harmful effects of treatments. As oxidative stress was identified as one of the core underlying causes of gonadotoxicity, research about this topic is concentrated on molecules with antioxidant properties, such as ascorbic acid, astaxanthin, and melatonin. 89 The in vivo pre‐clinical landscape has an interesting number of studies that were done with pre‐pubertal models. These concentrated on exploring the effects of Carnitine 12 , 19 , 29 , 55 , 90 and Amifostine, 27 , 31 , 91 , 92 both with partially good results. Considering these findings, we feel that such agents could be tested in clinical practice. However, it is crucial to emphasize that rigorous evaluation must be conducted to ensure that these cytoprotective agents do not interfere with the overall efficacy of the chemotherapy regimen.

DISCUSSION

In the pre‐clinical domain, a certain degree of variability in study findings is generally anticipated. This is partly explained by the different models used to assess gonadotoxicity, study length, and dose regimen. Although rodents generally provide a reliable way to predict treatment‐derived side effects when the adequate correction factor (K m) 93 and schedule of administration are taken into account, 94 assessing the impact on more intricate processes such as spermatogenesis will always remain challenging. Furthermore, because of the relatively rapid sexual maturation in murine models, most in vivo studies have evaluated the animals already at a pubertal stage/adulthood following drug administration at the pre‐pubertal stage. This approach may not fully replicate the developmental processes that occur in humans, potentially limiting the translational relevance of the findings.

As we have noted in our prior review, 6 there is an ongoing shift in toxicological studies to develop novel techniques to model human tissues and organs as faithfully as possible, most notably with the development of three‐dimensional organoids. 95 While there are efforts to recreate inter‐organ interactions, namely regarding the assessment of cardiotoxicity after drug metabolism in the liver, 96 reconstituting the feedback loop in the hypothalamic–pituitary–testicular (HPT) hormonal axis will likely remain unfeasible.

As such, standardizing and adopting the best practices for in vivo pre‐clinical studies remains the most sensible strategy, at least for the time being. Among the most important factors defined by the European Medicines Agency in their ICH S5 guideline for the detection of reproductive toxicity for human pharmaceuticals is the understanding of the potential for damage reversibility. 97 Therefore, studies should be designed to allow sufficient time for some spermatogenic cycles to complete following the drug regimen to assess testes’ recovery. In addition, selecting suitable doses up to a 10‐fold exposure multiple over the maximum exposure to be achieved in the clinic is crucial. Lastly, using 2 or more species, especially combining murine with non‐murine, favors cross‐species concordance and aids in controlling for the differences in the spermatogenesis differences between mammals.

Throughout our review, we observed an inherent level of discrepancy between pre‐clinical and clinical findings. In the pre‐clinical front, the major consideration regarding the usage of murine models stems from their different spermatogenesis processes compared to humans. In addition to their much quicker sexual maturation, there is a higher number of identified clones of germ cells, at different developmental stages, from SSCs to spermatozoa. 98

Moreover, there is a high disparity in the doses and regimens used, as well as the age of animals when treatment started. In addition, some studies have no mention of the basis for their chosen dosages or indicate whether any adjustments for human‐to‐mouse conversions were considered. Among those that use non‐body weight‐based dosages (mg/kg), the values of reference generally stem from the reported plasma concentrations in patients undergoing chemotherapy. On one hand, the adult testis is a rather confined organ where pharmaceutical agents are known not to reach as high concentration levels as in the blood. 99 On the other hand, this effect is mainly thought to be due to the existence of a blood‐testis barrier, which in humans appears to form from the age of 9 onwards, 100 thus leaving a big portion of pre‐pubertal patients at a higher theoretical risk. At any rate, the clinical concentration of chemotherapy drugs that the testis is exposed to during treatment is generally unknown, and by basing pre‐clinical research on the reported plasma levels the gonadotoxic effects might be broadly overestimated.

Taking Etoposide as an example, while Stumpp and colleagues administered two and eight daily 5 mg/kg intraperitoneal injections to rats of different ages and assessed them up to 95 days after, 8 Ortiz et al. opted instead for single injections of 1–200 μM directly in the testis, with an assessment up to 72 h after. 9 Both studies are insightful, albeit with different focuses – a histological and a molecular evaluation of Etoposide's gonadotoxicity, respectively. In the former, there was no mention of why the authors chose the used dosages, while in the latter the concentration used was based on germ‐cell death evaluations. With such variabilities in study designs it is not possible to draw sensible evidence‐based recommendations that should be explored in a clinical setting.

One theoretical approach to address this limitation would be to investigate whether drugs can penetrate the testes during Phase 0 trials, which are primarily designed for pharmacokinetic evaluation rather than safety assessment. Still, there are ethical concerns related to the invasiveness of testicular biopsies, as semen analyses cannot be performed in pre‐pubertal children. As such, we further highlight the importance of standardizing pre‐clinical experiments as defined by the European Medicines Agency. 97

The few clinical studies on both etoposide and doxorubicin are likely indicative of their relatively low individual risk, especially considering they are administered in conjunction with other, more cytotoxic, chemotherapy drugs like alkylating agents. Despite this, the pre‐clinical studies hint that they might have a noteworthy contribution to the overall toxicity of the treatment regimen (as highlighted in Table 1).

Between the two, doxorubicin warrants attention, which is consistent with its broader mechanism of action. To the best of our knowledge, it is also the only agent from the ones we have selected that has in vivo studies assessing its gonadotoxic impact after administration at different pre‐pubertal ages. Interestingly, and in contrast with the notion that a more underdeveloped testis would be less susceptible to damage, two studies have found that treatment at earlier pre‐pubertal ages in rats was more gonadotoxic. 25 , 26 The age at which damage was significantly reduced coincided with the establishment of the blood‐testis barrier at ~pnd 16, leading the authors to hypothesize that this structure can reduce drug permeation throughout the germinative epithelium. Although theoretically applicable to any toxic agent exposure in the testes, we have not encountered additional studies comparing the impacts of administering other chemotherapy agents at varying pre‐pubertal stages.

When compared to cyclophosphamide and cisplatin at concentrations that cover the range reported in the serum of patients, doxorubicin was equally as damaging to germ cells in mice in the short term. 24 Besides this, it induced the highest increase in DNA damage and was the only drug that reduced the proliferation of interstitial cells. Nevertheless, a subsequent long‐term evaluation also in mice found that doxorubicin induced a less prolonged impairment of spermatogenesis than cisplatin. 33

Doxorubicin may have a peculiar effect on Sertoli cells, as it acts as an iron chelator and decreases the expression of the transferrin receptor. 101 In the testes, Sertoli cells express transferrin receptors to deliver iron ions to germ cells, which are crucial for their proliferation and differentiation. 102

Etoposide, despite the milder mechanism of action and the less abundant literature, was still shown to affect the pre‐pubertal testis. It induces a persistent reduction in germ‐cell density for more up to 3 months. 8 , 10 , 12 In addition, it was also found to disrupt Sertoli cells 11 and impair their ability to synthesize transferrin. 10

Interestingly, etoposide has a stage‐specific effect on spermatogenic DNA synthesis. As it was shown to cause a higher reduction of pre‐mitotic rather than premeiotic DNA synthesis, it was hypothesized that topoisomerase II has a more significant role in intermediate and type‐B spermatogonia. 103 In humans, type B spermatogonia appears by 3–4 years of age. 104 As such, it would be interesting to assess whether younger boys are less vulnerable to the gonadotoxic effects of etoposide and, presumably, doxorubicin.

Topoisomerase II inhibitors, like alkylating agents, are considered potentially genotoxic, 105 but the risk of genetic defects impacting the offspring after chemotherapy is perceived to be minimal. 106 Moreover, due to their age, childhood cancer patients are unlikely to conceive during or shortly after chemotherapy, creating a hiatus that allows for the recovery of genetic aberrations in germ cells. However, if there is cryopreservation of germ cells at those moments, the genotoxicity of the treatment regimen must be thoroughly considered.

Overall, the gonadotoxicity profile of Topoisomerase II inhibitors that we can infer from pre‐clinical studies supports the idea that they should be more carefully assessed in clinical practice. Although doxorubicin and etoposide had the most research regarding their potential gonadotoxicity in the pre‐pubertal testis, two additional studies have assessed Irinotecan, a topoisomerase I inhibitor. Treatment in pre‐pubertal mice was found to reduce spermatogonia numbers, with a subsequent reduction in sperm levels when these were assessed in adulthood. 107 This is consistent with the previous finding that Irinotecan reduced the proliferation of germ cells and led to the appearance of Sertoli cell‐only tubules when culturing pre‐pubertal mice gonads. 108

Just like these agents, Vincristine is also considered to pose a low risk for gonadotoxicity when administered to children. 84 However, recent pre‐clinical research reveals conflicting results (as shown in Table 3). Administering Vincristine to pre‐pubertal mice at low clinical doses resulted in a notable decrease in fertility rates, even surpassing those observed in mice treated with cyclophosphamide. 53 As such, larger clinical studies assessing the contribution of vincristine in infertility are warranted. Vinblastine, which is a part of the traditional ABVD (doxorubicin, bleomycin, vinblastine, and dacarbazine) regimen for pediatric Hodgkin Lymphoma and was recently tested in low‐grade gliomas, 109 , 110 acts by the same mechanism to that of vincristine. Still, we have not found any study assessing it as a potential gonadotoxic agent to the pre‐pubertal testis.

A comparable discrepancy was also observed for Imatinib (outlined in Table 2). Specifically, we express a concern with the finding that it blocks the migration of gonocytes to form the SSC pool. 44 , 45 , 46 In mammals, this event happens shortly after birth. 111 This underscores a significant consideration for newborn babies undergoing chemotherapy with imatinib or women receiving this agent at a later stage in their pregnancy, as it may compromise the establishment of the SSC pool in the babies' testes. Tauer and colleagues further compared the effects of Imatinib to those of Dasatinib and Bosutinib, both newer‐generation TKIs, in pre‐pubertal rats. Similarly to Imatinib, neither led to changes in the serum levels of testosterone or inhibin B. 49

Among other classes of agents not included in our review, we have only found potential concern for Cytarabine, an antimetabolite, and Asparaginase, an asparagine hydrolyzing enzyme. Cytarabine administration in pre‐pubertal mice led to an increase in apoptosis and reduced the number of the most developed stages of germ cells while increasing the number of spermatogonial cells. 112 Asparaginase treatment was assessed at a clinical level by analyzing the histology of testicular samples from pre‐pubertal patients. In a statistical analysis that assessed germ and Sertoli cell marker expression, as well as proliferation markers, Asparaginase was found to have reduced germ‐cell numbers and even created “severe” phenotypes in some patients, which were mostly observed when treatment included alkylating agents. 113

In conclusion, our review has identified relatively unexplored classes of chemotherapy agents that warrant greater research regarding their gonadotoxicity risk to the pre‐pubertal testis. This is highlighted by the important differences we have noted across the pre‐clinical and clinical fronts.

There are very few pre‐clinical studies with pre‐pubertal models that fully reconstitute a multi‐drug chemotherapy regimen. Still, Delessard and colleagues have recently found that treating tissue cultures of mice with a polychemotherapy regimen that included Vincristine and Cyclophosphamide, one of the most substantiated gonadotoxic alkylating agents, induced a worse histological outcome when compared to the individual treatment with each agent. 54 While treatment did not impair the differentiation capability of germ cells, it gives insights that non‐alkylating agents may have a meaningful contribution to the overall gonadotoxicity of alkylating agent‐containing chemotherapy regimens. Therefore, it would be important to determine whether such findings would extend to an in vivo setting.

Such knowledge is instrumental in developing more accurate risk assessment tools, tailoring treatment protocols to minimize gonadotoxicity where possible, and guiding decisions about fertility preservation measures. Ultimately, this research will contribute to better‐informed treatment strategies and improved quality of life for childhood cancer survivors.
