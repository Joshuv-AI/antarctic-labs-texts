Cognitive Impact of Neurotropic Pathogens: Investigating Molecular Mimicry through Computational Methods
Authors: Pascal Büttiker, Amira Boukherissa, Simon Weissenberger, Radek Ptacek, Martin Anders, Jiri Raboch, George B. Stefano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Neurotropic pathogens, notably, herpesviruses, have been associated with significant neuropsychiatric effects. As a group, these pathogens can exploit molecular mimicry mechanisms to manipulate the host central nervous system to their advantage. Here, we present a systematic computational approach that may ultimately be used to unravel protein–protein interactions and molecular mimicry processes that have not yet been solved experimentally. Toward this end, we validate this approach by replicating a set of pre-existing experimental findings that document the structural and functional similarities shared by the human cytomegalovirus-encoded UL144 glycoprotein and human tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member 14 (TNFRSF14). We began with a thorough exploration of the Homo sapiens protein database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLASTx) to identify proteins sharing sequence homology with UL144. Subsequently, we used AlphaFold2 to predict the independent three-dimensional structures of UL144 and TNFRSF14. This was followed by a comprehensive structural comparison facilitated by Distance-Matrix Alignment and Foldseek. Finally, we used AlphaFold-multimer and PPIscreenML to elucidate potential protein complexes and confirm the predicted binding activities of both UL144 and TNFRSF14. We then used our in silico approach to replicate the experimental finding that revealed TNFRSF14 binding to both B- and T-lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA) and glycoprotein domain and UL144 binding to BTLA alone. This computational framework offers promise in identifying structural similarities and interactions between pathogen-encoded proteins and their host counterparts. This information will provide valuable insights into the cognitive mechanisms underlying the neuropsychiatric effects of viral infections.Graphical Abstract

Introduction

Biological complexity, mutuality, and flexibility can be best understood by appreciating specific mechanisms and relationships that extend across taxa. Although this concept is well supported by examples of biological energy and communication pathways that are shared by different and distantly related lifeforms, our understanding of the pathways and structures underlying this apparent universality remains incomplete. A further appreciation of these functional similarities may lead to improvements in human health and psychological well-being.

Infections with neurotropic pathogens, including viruses, bacteria, fungi, and parasites, can result in significant alterations to the nervous system (John et al. 2015). Viruses have been associated with neuropsychiatric disorders for many decades and are believed to affect millions of people worldwide (Zheng and Savitz 2023). Pathogens apply numerous mechanisms to communicate with their hosts, including several that are based on molecular exchange and immunological targeting (Pfeifer et al. 2022; Jaswal et al. 2023). One biological mechanism that has recently become the subject of significant interest is molecular mimicry, i.e., the production of proteins and peptides with similar structural features and functions by distantly related organisms (Stefano 1986; Stefano and Kream 2017). Many dependent life forms employ molecular mimicry to permit them to overtake host cells and induce changes in structure and function that suit their needs and promote their survival. For example, while viruses cannot replicate independently, they develop proteins and peptides that permit them to infect host cells and hijack the cellular reproductive machinery. Virally encoded proteins can alter mitochondrial function, interfere with critical signaling pathways, and alter the life cycle of neurons, among other cells (Wang et al. 2020; Büttiker et al. 2023). Given the dynamic nature of mitochondria, functional changes in these cellular power plants can have pivotal effects on energy availability and host biology (Stefano et al. 2023). Hence, viral infection can generate molecular imbalances and disrupt inter- and intra-neuronal communication pathways, which can lead to neuroinflammation, neurodegeneration, and disturbed cognition (Yadav et al. 2022).

Among the best-characterized examples of this phenomenon are the virus-encoded proteins that promote their own replication by mimicking and altering immunological processes used by their target cells to maintain homeostasis under stressful conditions. For example, viruses of the Herpesviridae family that infect the central nervous system (CNS), including several that have been linked to mental illness (e.g., schizophrenia and bipolar disorder), encode proteins that mimic the function of mammalian cytokines, chemokines, and their receptors (Andreou et al. 2022; Soldan and Lieberman 2023; Lanz et al. 2022; Light et al. 2021). These proteins mimic the actions of target cell proteins and allow viruses to control cell function and evade mammalian immune responses. For example, viral homologs of cytokine receptors (e.g., interleukin-1β receptor [vIL-1βR] and the interferon-γ receptor [vIFN-γR]) can bind to their cognate cytokines with high affinity. However, because they lack both transmembrane and signaling domains, these soluble receptors trap these cytokines and neutralize their activity. The disturbed cytokine balance associated with herpes simplex virus 1 seropositivity may play a central role in the pathogenesis of schizophrenia (Reale et al. 2021).

Human cytomegalovirus (CMV) alters the functional and structural landscape of the CNS via a variety of mechanisms and can lead to neuropathology and neuropsychiatric symptoms, including depression (D'Aiuto et al. 2012; Hogestyn et al. 2018; Zheng et al. 2021; Sankar et al. 2023). Virus-encoded UL144 can bind to B- and T-lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA, also known as CD272) which is a transmembrane glycoprotein expressed by both lymphoid and myeloid cells that regulates both inhibitory and stimulatory signals associated with the immune response (Ning et al. 2021). UL144-mediated interactions with endogenous BTLA may alter the immune response to TNFRSF14 and mimic immuno-inhibitory functions that support the conditions required for viral latency. Likewise, TNFRSF17-BTLA and UL144-BTLA interactions may also have an impact on neuronal communication and cognition via their contributions to dysregulated inflammatory responses and impaired immunity to bacterial infection (Shui et al. 2011).

Structural and functional similarities shared by HCMV UL144 and human TNFRSF14 have been described in the scientific literature (Montgomery et al. 1996; Benedict et al. 1999; Bitra et al. 2019), specifically, that HCMV-encoded UL144 mimicked some of the actions of human TNFRSF14, potentially impairing lymphocyte responses and promoting processes that support life-long latent infection (Poole et al. 2013). Specifically, by mimicking the surface structure of TNFRSF14, UL144 binds selectively to the coinhibitory receptor B and T-lymphocyte attenuator (BTLA) and cross-competes with the pro-inflammatory binding of TNFRSF14 to CD160, thereby inhibiting the activation of natural killer T cells (Šedý et al. 2017).

Cheung et al. (2005) identified a highly conserved cysteine-rich domain 1 (CRD1) in the sequence of TNFRSF14 that has significant homology to a similar domain in UL144; this domain mediates the binding interactions of TNFRSF14 and BTLA and may selectively mimic its inhibitory co-signaling function. Thus, a similar interaction between UL144 and BTLA may inhibit host immune responses and lead to altered neuronal energy metabolism and diminished cognitive function. The results of recent structural and functional studies revealed that the CRD1 region of TNFRSF14 is responsible for binding to BTLA, CD160, and gD, whereas the CRD2/CRD3 regions contain a lymphotoxin-alpha binding site. In contrast to the more promiscuous binding of TNFRSF14, and for reasons as yet unknown, UL144 appears to have evolved to bind exclusively to BTLA (Bitra et al. 2019).

In this paper, and as discussed further below, our goal was to introduce and validate a novel in silico method by recapitulating published experimental findings that describe the biological actions of the membrane-anchored UL144 glycoprotein. We are particularly interested in using this as a baseline so that we can ultimately develop methods that will permit us to explore mechanisms underlying virus-induced and immune-mediated cognitive alterations and neuropsychiatric symptoms (Hogestyn et al. 2018; Marogianni et al. 2020; Rodriguez Murúa et al. 2022; Sankar et al. 2023).

Genome-Wide Detection of Homologous Protein Sequences Using BLAST

The Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST) is an open-source computational tool accessible through the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) that can identify regions of local similarity between DNA, RNA, and protein primary sequences (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). BLAST can be used to compare primary nucleotide or protein sequences to information deposited in various databases and calculate the statistical significance of the resulting matches. BLAST can be used to infer functional and evolutionary relationships between homologous sequences and identify members of gene families (Altschul et al. 1990). BLAST offers a variety of approaches that can be used to conduct these analyses and identify both nucleotide and amino acid sequence similarities. For example, BLASTn permits the user to search nucleotide databases using a nucleotide sequence query, while BLASTp performs the same type of analysis to search protein databases using an amino acid sequence query. Similarly, tBLASTn can be used to search translated nucleotide databases using a protein query, while BLASTx can be used to perform searches of protein databases using a translated nucleotide query.

The first step of this method features the BLASTx tool, which will translate primary nucleotide sequences and use them to search for similar proteins deposited in one or more relevant databases. As one example, this tool can be used to determine whether translated nucleotide sequences from a viral genome encode proteins that are similar to those encoded by another organism.

Previous work revealed that the ectodomain of UL144 exhibits relatively high sequence variability, with five major types: 1A, 1B, 1C, 2, and 3 (Cheung et al. 2005). Although each of these five types of UL144 bound to human BTLA (Cheung et al. 2005), we selected the UL144 type 3 sequence Fiala (AF135184.1) for our analysis because of its enhanced BTLA binding affinity compared to the others, as described in the recently reported the crystal structure of the BTLA-UL144 complex (Bitra et al. 2019). To begin this analysis, we used BLASTx to search for amino acid sequences that are homologous to that encoded by the human herpesvirus UL144 gene. As expected, the BLASTp search yielded a list of UL144 protein sequences. This list includes the protein sequence of the UL144 type 3 Fiala (AF135184.1) identified by BLASTx analysis which is among those with the highest similarity (e-value, identity percentage, length, and other parameters). The UL144 protein identified as immune evasion viroceptor-1 [Human betaherpesvirus 5] (175 aa, ID: AAD43177.1) with an e-value of 2e−45 and an identity percentage of 100% was selected for ongoing analysis.

BLAST alignments with an e-value of < 1e−5 (0.00001) are generally considered significant (i.e., the query and test sequences are considered homologous). However, the results that emerge will depend largely on the parameters used to conduct the search (e.g., sequence length). The search parameters should be scrutinized individually for every search to be performed (Kerfeld and Scott 2011).

The algorithms used to generate these data focused on aligning the 79 amino acids found in the most highly homologous region, as shown. However, using this approach alone may overlook essential homologies in other regions. For a more in-depth analysis of sequence similarity, the complete amino acid sequences of both proteins were evaluated rather than just the segments shown in the alignment in Fig. 1. Hence, the full-length proteins were examined to determine whether other sequence characteristics may also be conserved. These BLAST analyses confirmed that TNFRSF14 and UL144 share high sequence homology specifically within the CRD1 region and moderate sequence homology in the CRD2 region, but not in other domains.

While BLAST and sequence-based tools can be used to detect primary sequence homology, they may not be relevant for detecting structural (and potentially functional) mimicry given that they do not consider secondary or tertiary structures. For example, two proteins with similar amino acid sequences may have different functions, and two proteins with different amino acid sequences may share the same function via mutually shared higher-order structures and protein-binding behavior. This can occur when two proteins have evolved from the same ancestor. While their primary sequences may have diverged, their secondary and tertiary structures may remain largely intact. Common examples of this phenomenon include the functional conservation of infectious mechanisms of viruses and bacteria despite the high rates of mutation observed during genomic replication (Joshi and Xu 2007; Duffy 2018). Thus, a combination of analyses should be used to detect structural mimicry, including sequence- and structure-based analyses as well as those designed to predict protein–protein interactions.

Prediction of Secondary and Tertiary Protein Structure from Primary Amino Acid Sequence Data Using Alphafold2

AlphaFold2 is a deep learning system developed by a team of researchers at DeepMind, a subsidiary of Alphabet (Jumper et al. 2021; reviewed by Borkakoti and Thornton 2023) that uses a neural network to predict three-dimensional (3D) protein structures from primary amino acid sequence data. The AlphaFold2 program algorithms were trained using information obtained from large protein structure databases (e.g., the Protein Data Bank [PDB]) and incorporate other sources of information (e.g., genetic sequences) to improve their predictions. AlphaFold2 can be used as a web-based version via the Google Colab notebook platform (https://colab.research.google.com/github/deepmind/alphafold/blob/main/notebooks/AlphaFold.ipynb) or as a command-line tool (https://github.com/google-deepmind/alphafold). The full open-source command-line tool was used for this analysis, which is slightly more accurate than the web-based tool (Jumper et al. 2021).

AlphaFold2 provides highly reliable predictions of tertiary protein structure and outperformed other methods in the blinded 14th Critical Assessment of Protein Structure Prediction (CASP14) evaluation (Jumper et al. 2021). The learning algorithm’s capacity to perform accurate AlphaFold2 predictions is typically measured using Global Distance Test (GDT) scores, which compare the predicted structure to the actual structure of a known protein. A GDT score of 100 indicates a perfect match, while a score of 0 indicates no match between the predicted and experimentally determined structures. In the aforementioned CASP14 experiment, AlphaFold2 achieved an average GDT score of 92.4 for all targets, with some predictions achieving scores as high as 99.9. This level of accuracy was previously thought to be achievable only through experimental methods (e.g., X-ray crystallography or cryo-electron microscopy). This is especially important when one needs to determine the structure of a protein that has not yet been characterized experimentally.

The confidence measure for an AlphaFold2 prediction (i.e., the confidence value of a single input) is based on per-residue estimates determined on a scale from 0 to 100 using a predicted local distance difference test (pLDDT) (Jumper et al. 2021; Varadi et al. 2021). A pLDDT score above 90 (> 90) indicates that the region has been modeled with high reliability and accuracy and that the results are suitable for any application. Regions with pLDDT scores between 70 and 90 are considered generally confident (good) predictions, while regions with pLDDT scores between 50 and 70 should be interpreted with caution. Structural data from regions with pLDDT scores below 50 (< 50) cannot be interpreted independently; these regions may be intrinsically unstructured under physiological conditions or may achieve structure when included as part of a complex (Varadi et al. 2021). However, disordered (intrinsically unstructured) proteins with complex conformational dynamics are frequently involved in biomolecular regulation and can become critical elements supporting human health and disease (Abyzov et al. 2022; Pietrek et al. 2023). Additional information may be needed to determine whether low-confidence models or regions may be of importance. Additionally, given that these systems rely entirely on input data, it is crucial to recognize and introduce high-quality data when performing predictions.

The AlphaFold2 monomer model was used to predict the 3D structures of the complete amino acid sequences UL144 and TNFRSF14. We decided to use the monomer model because both proteins are relatively small (175 and 283 amino acids, respectively) with no evidence of multiple polypeptide chains or protein complexes (Johansson-Åkhe and Wallner 2022). Both AlphaFold2 results presented structures with regions of very high (pLDDT > 90), confident (90 > pLDDT > 70), low (70 > pLDDT > 50), and very low (pLDDT < 50) scores (Fig. 2). The global pLDDT scores of the best Alphafold2 structures generated for TNFRSF14 and UL144 were 79.15 and 76.76, respectively; while not of highest rank (pLDDT > 90), these findings suggest that these structures can be used “with confidence” for further analysis and interpretation. Further analysis of both structures based on the predicted alignment of their respective sequences revealed that the aligned 79 amino acid segments initially identified by BLASTx (i.e., CRD1 and CRD2) are configured as beta-sheets. Beta-sheets, which consist of extended polypeptide strands that are connected to one another by hydrogen bonds, have been implicated in many fundamental biological processes, notably in critical protein–protein interactions. Thus, these structures may be strong potential targets for disease intervention (Nowick 2008). Thus, we focused our attention on the shared beta-sheet structure, as this may prove to be the locus of their shared biological functionality (Fig. 2). High-Sensitivity Protein Structure Comparisons Using DALI

As described above, individual evaluation of the sequences and structures of UL144 and TNFRSF14 suggested that they shared significant structural similarities. However, the structures need to be compared directly to one another to confirm the full significance of this preliminary information. To do so, the web-based version of the Distance-Matrix Alignment (DALI) program was used, which can be found on their server at http://ekhidna2.biocenter.helsinki.fi/dali/.

Distance-Matrix Alignment can be used to compare and classify different protein structures. Because of its high sensitivity and moderate calculation speed, it is widely used to identify structural similarities based on 3D protein conformation (Holm 2022). DALI is particularly useful for identifying remote homologs, i.e., proteins with a common evolutionary origin that have diverged significantly over time. This application is also helpful in identifying structural motifs, which are recurring structural elements that may be important for protein function.

Distance-Matrix Alignment is based on an algorithm that compares pairwise distances between atoms in different protein structures and then aligns the structures based on these distances. Specifically, DALI uses 3D atomic coordinates of two or more protein structures presented in PDB format to calculate a distance matrix for each protein structure. Protein structures are then aligned based on their pairwise distance matrices. This is achieved using an iterative alignment algorithm to maximize the number of aligned residue pairs while minimizing the root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) between the two structures. The program uses a scoring system to quantify the degree of similarity determined between the structures and generates a structural similarity score for each pairwise comparison. DALI Z-scores above 2 usually correspond to similar folds, and structures with a sequence identity of more than 20% are considered strong matches (Holm et al. 2008). DALI offers a variety of approaches, including pairwise comparisons between two structures, PDB searches that compare a query structure against the entire PDB database, AF-DB searches that permit the user to search a query structure against the entire Alphafold2 database, and all-against-all comparisons that compare query structures against both databases and generate a heatmap of Z-scores. Although DALI is a powerful tool for comparing protein structures and identifying similarities, it is somewhat complicated for general use and interpretation, and its calculations are based on structural information only and do not take into account primary amino acid sequence data.

For this analysis, a pairwise alignment was conducted with UL144 as the query structure and TNFRSF14 as the target. The analyses were performed two times: one analysis was performed using the full protein sequences and one using only the CRD1 and CRD2 domains. The results of the DALI comparison of full structures revealed a Z-score of 9.9 with a sequence identity of 32%, while the comparison between the CRDs yielded a Z-score of 10.6 with a sequence identity of 42% (Fig. 3a, c), suggesting the possibility of significant higher-order structural similarities despite the low sequence identity. For additional comparison and visualization, the Template Modeling (TM)-align algorithm was utilized to align the two structures using US-align (Zhang et al. 2022). TM-align, an algorithm developed by Zhang and Skolnick (2005) is among the key tools used for comparing protein structures and elucidating their functions and relationships. TM-align employs a TM-score rotation matrix and dynamic programming to identify optimal structural alignments between protein pairs and is widely utilized for its accuracy in aligning protein structures. US-align was developed in 2022 by the same researchers as a platform designed to align monomer and complex structures of various macromolecules, including proteins, RNAs, and DNAs (Zhang et al. 2022). This innovation marks a significant advance towards achieving a universal solution for structural alignments across different macromolecules. In our case, the alignment of complete amino acid sequences of UL144 and TNFRSF14 yielded a TM-score of 0.45, indicating moderate similarity. By contrast, the structural alignment of CRD1 and CRD2 regions yielded a TM-score of 0.77 (Fig. 3b, d), suggesting a high level of structural similarity between the CRD regions of the two proteins. Protein Structure Comparison Using Foldseek

Rather than focusing on the results of DALI alone, a two-step comparison was used to integrate the results of these two analytical tools. DALI was selected as an initial test; the information provided was then evaluated further using the bioinformatics tool Foldseek to determine whether the similarities detected using DALI were sufficiently significant to support further analysis.

Foldseek is similar to DALI but facilitates the rapid comparison of extensive data sets with a degree of accuracy and sensitivity close to that exhibited by experimental methods (van Kempen et al. 2023). Foldseek uses a structural alphabet to describe the tertiary interactions between constituent amino acids. The 20 states included in the 3D interaction alphabet (3Di) describe the geometric conformation of each residue with respect to its spatially nearest neighbor. Specifically, Foldseek converts the query structures into 3Di sequences and then uses a pre-trained substitution matrix to search through the 3Di sequences of the target structures. This approach reduces the application’s dependency on consecutive letters and thus results in a more even distribution of state frequencies, thereby increasing the information density and reducing the number of false positive results (van Kempen et al. 2023). Regions of highest and lowest information densities will typically be found in the conserved protein cores and the nonconserved coil/loop regions, respectively. This approach also yields more reliable results than those generally achieved using other structure prediction applications. Importantly, this model allows Foldseek to function at 86% of the sensitivity of DALI and operate at greater speed (van Kempen et al. 2023).

In addition to the 3Di sequence identity, Foldseek also uses a template modeling (TM)-scoring system to rank the various hits and assess topological similarity. Because the TM system is designed to score larger distance errors as weaker than smaller ones, the values obtained are more sensitive to global fold similarity than local structural variations. A TM-score of 1.0 indicates a perfect match between two structures, while scores of ≤ 0.17 represent random (unrelated) protein pairs. Protein pairs that score > 0.5 are considered significantly similar and expected to exhibit the same folding. A TM-score of 0.5 corresponds to a P-value of approximately 5.5 × 10−7 (Xu and Zhang 2010).

Foldseek is available as a web-based version (https://search.foldseek.com/) and as a command-line tool (https://github.com/steineggerlab/foldseek). The web-based version can only perform searches by comparing structures and amino acid sequences to information currently contained in its database. The command-line tool permits the user to perform a more in-depth structural analysis and comparisons.

For our analysis, we used the easy-search module of the command-line tool, which provided us with the opportunity to search a single or multiple query structure presented in PDB/mmCIF format against a target database, folder, or a single protein structure.

Using a combination approach, in which protein structures were computed (DALI) and the global structural similarity of predicted protein folds was compared (Foldseek), it was possible to identify and confirm local structural similarities with very high sensitivity. This combination of these two approaches represents a highly sensitive method that can be used to identify structurally homologous protein domains and improve the quality of homology inferences needed for further structural and functional analyses.

Advanced Structural and Functional Predictions (Protein–Protein Interactions and Molecular Mimicry)

The structures were examined further to identify functionally homologous sites and explain how UL144 might mimic the actions of TNFRSF14. The results from previous experimental studies revealed that UL144 can mimic the actions of inhibitory cosignaling factors via specific binding to BTLA via interactions involving the CRD1 domain. Our results also highlighted the high structural similarity between UL144 and TNFRSF14 at CRD1. Moreover, it has been shown experimentally that, despite the structural similarity of their CRD1 regions, TNFRSF14 binds to both BTLA and gD, proteins via this domain, while UL144 binds only to the BTLA (Bitra et al. 2019).

To validate this finding in silico, the AlphaFold2 multimer model was applied to predict the nature and properties of the BTLA-TNFRSF14, gD-TNFRSF14, BTLA-UL144, and gD-UL144 protein complexes. To perform this advanced functional analysis, we needed to identify database entries representing the amino acid sequences of both BTLA and gD. We selected the entry identified as Chain C, B- and T-lymphocyte attenuator (107aa, ID: 6NYP_C) that represents the Ig-like V-type domain of the BTLA protein and the Chain A, Glycoprotein D (290 aa, ID: 1JMA_A) from the NCBI protein database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/protein/). Although not the full-length protein, the entry for BTLA includes the target interaction domain and, most importantly, represents a structure that has been experimentally determined (Compaan et al. 2005; Bitra et al. 2019). While we certainly recognize the potential influence of the other regions of this protein, we cannot incorporate this information into our algorithm at this time. We then used the Alphafold2 multimer model to predict protein–protein interactions of the four aforementioned complexes using both full sequences of TNFRSF14 and UL144 as well as the CRD1 and CRD2 regions alone. To evaluate protein–protein interactions using AlphaFold2 multimer, we considered both the predicted TM (pTM) and interface predicted TM (ipTM) scores provided by the model which are crucial for determining the accuracy and reliability of the predicted interactions. These scores range from 0 to 1, with values greater than 0.8 indicating high accuracy and confidence in the predicted interactions (Jeppesen and André 2023). The AlphaFold multimer scoring system is based on the ipTM score, which specifically accounts for protein–protein interactions within the targeted structures (Gomes et al. 2022). The algorithm utilizes a weighted combination of the pTM and iPTM scores (0.8*iPTM + 0.2*pTM) to rank models (Bret et al. 2024; Evans et al. 2021; Jeppesen and André 2023; Wallner 2023).

As expected, the AlphaFold2 multimer predictions revealed that both TNFRSF14 and UL144 bind to BTLA via their CRD1 domains with high ipTM scores (greater than 0.83) (Fig. 5). Visual analysis of the results performed with PyMol confirmed that both the TNFRSF14-BTLA and UL144-BTLA interaction sites could be found within the homologous 79 amino acid beta-sheet structure identified by AlphaFold2 analysis. These interaction sites included both CRD1 and CRD2 and were detected within the first 34 amino acids of the CRD1 region. Furthermore, both UL144 and TNFRSF14 interactions targeted the same region of BTLA (Fig. 5), corresponding to the sites previously identified by Cheung and colleagues (Cheung et al. 2005). To assess the reliability of the predicted AlphaFold2 complexes, we used DockQ to compare our predicted complexes to the crystal structures that are available for three of these complexes: TNFRSF14-BTLA (2AW2), TNFRSF14-gD (1JMA), and UL144-BTLA (6NYP). The Swissmodel Expasy webserver was used to compute the DockQ scores (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/assess/). While this analysis allowed us to confirm that our predicted AF2 complexes, additional validation with additional methods, including RMSD or Molecular Mechanics/Generalized Born Surface Area) would also be helpful in this instance. Of note, all three of the complexes exhibited high quaternary structure quality estimate (QSQE) scores (values between 0 and 1 that indicate the anticipated accuracy of the interchain contacts) and medium-to-high DockQ scores.

Additional analytic methods may be used to reveal protein function. Deep learning models, including graph convolutional networks (GCNs), are specifically designed and continuously trained to analyze graph-structured data and may be able to predict the function of a protein based on its sequence and putative 3D structure. One of these applications, known as DeepFRI can be used to predict protein function from this type of structural information (Gligorijevic et al. 2021). DeepFRI is a web-based tool that relies on information available in Gene Ontology (GO) databases (currently, the most comprehensive of these resources) to identify and describe protein functions (https://beta.deepfri.flatironinstitute.org/). Various methods are used to provide specific GO terms to proteins based on a computational analysis that features similarity-based, probabilistic, and machine-learning techniques.

The field of protein function prediction is a novel and highly active area of research. New methods and improvements are continuously under development. While structure-based predictions using GCNs have shown promising results, their reliability should be considered within the context of the specific implementation, dataset, and evaluation metrics used to perform the analysis.

Conclusion

Many biological mechanisms found in different life forms (e.g., cellular energetics and molecular communication) are intrinsically related to one another and share distinct similarities. Because of these similarities, one set of distantly related organisms may have the capacity to influence the cellular machinery of another. For example, pathogens that encode proteins that are structurally similar to those of their host can affect the latter’s metabolism and function by molecular mimicry. The genomes of viral pathogens that infect the human CNS encode cytokines and cytokine receptors that can disrupt cellular homeostasis and facilitate evasion of the mammalian immune response.

The emergence of new sequencing technologies and cutting-edge advances in structural bioinformatics provide us with new opportunities to study molecular mimicry through the prediction and comparison of 3D protein structures. Here, the combination of sequence-based and structural bioinformatics allowed us to replicate experimental findings, notably, UL144 binding to BTLA that mimics the function of human TNFRSF14 (Bitra et al. 2019).

In this manuscript, we reviewed several of the current methods that can be used to determine protein structure and function in silico and presented a systematic bioinformatics approach that might be used to identify structural and functional similarities between proteins encoded by distinct organisms. Our structured in silico analysis replicated earlier experimental findings on the relationship between the HCMV gene product, UL144, and human TNFRSF14, thereby validating the overall approach. The results from earlier studies suggested that TNFRSF14 facilitated the activation and deactivation of critical inflammatory pathways during latent HCMV infection, thereby controlling the cellular immune response. For example, UL144 can activate TNFR-associated factor 6 and upregulate the expression of the chemokine CCL22 (Miller-Kittrell and Sparer 2009); a longitudinal study conducted by Laurikainen and colleagues (Laurikainen et al. 2020) revealed a significant association between elevated serum CCL22 levels and symptoms of psychosis Disturbances in the TNF-mediated signaling pathway have also been linked to the development of psychiatric disorders via inflammatory activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis (Berthold-Losleben and Himmerich 2008).

The similarities observed here provide insight into the potential for UL144 mimicry via interactions with BTLA that may contribute to cognitive disruption and the altered neuronal landscape observed during HCMV infection.

Using a step-by-step approach, we began with a BLASTx search for sequences in the Homo sapiens genome that were similar to UL144. The 3D structures of UL144 and the protein that resulted from this search (i.e., TNFRSF14) were then predicted with AlphaFold2. Full structural comparisons were then performed using DALI, US-align, and Foldseek. Although the original BLASTx search revealed only limited primary sequence homology (79 amino acids), these algorithms identified higher-order structural similarities shared by UL144 and TNFRSF14. The AlphaFold2 multimer model was used as a final step to predict the 3D structures of the predicted TNFRSF14-BTLA and UL144-BTLA, TNFRSF14-gD protein complexes. The recently described machine-learning classifier PPIscreenML (Mischley et al. 2024) was then applied to predict the interacting pairs of proteins. Our results confirmed the experimental findings (Bitra et al. 2019) that TNFRSF14 binds to both BTLA and gD while UL144 binds only to BTLA, and identified CRD1 motifs on TNFRSF14 and UL144 as potential binding sites. Future experimental and in silico approaches might be used to explore the unique roles of each CRD domain and explain the molecular basis of the differential binding observed. Among the possibilities, the binding interfaces of the CRD domains of these two proteins may have one or more unique (and as yet undetected) features or may undergo small conformational changes. Nonetheless, our results suggest that UL144 may be able to recapitulate BTLA-associated inhibitory mechanisms and thus may be involved in HCMV-related neuropsychiatric symptoms.

Using this approach, we demonstrated that primary sequence identity may not always reflect functional homology. As shown, sequences that exhibit comparatively low primary sequence identity can fold into proteins with similar structures. Our results confirm that in silico methods can be used to detect functional conservation and potentially comparable biological actions of proteins across taxa. By replicating the findings of UL144 mimicry and interactions suggesting how it might contribute to HCMV immune-evasive strategies, our findings highlight how our method might be used to predict similar mechanisms that may be involved in neuropsychiatric changes observed in the CNS in response to chronic, persistent infection, notably those that may not yet have been solved experimentally.

Finally, our findings suggest that appropriate use of these technologies may lead to an improved understanding of the specific contributions of one or more proteins in a given disease state. First and foremost, it is critical to understand that structural similarity (primary, secondary, or tertiary) does not necessarily predict functional homology. New methods will be needed to address this critical gap, and will certainly be critical if we are to use in silico approaches to develop new methods for diagnosis and treatment of psychiatric and other disorders (Kim and Kim 2023). By offering a step-by-step manual, we hope that scientists from various areas of expertise will be able to apply these methods and find ways to use these biological data to improve human health and psychological well-being.
