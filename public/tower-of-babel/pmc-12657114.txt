Early‐Onset Hypopigmented Mycosis Fungoides in a Young Female: Navigating Diagnostic Delays and Treatment Complexities—A Case Report
Authors: Ravi Das, FNU Venjhraj, Shilpa Dahara, Naren Kumar Matlani, Meva Ram, Tularam Yadav
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHypopigmented mycosis fungoides (HMF) is a variant of mycosis fungoides (MF). It is classified as a low‐grade lymphoproliferative disorder and is often challenging to diagnose, especially in young, darker‐skinned patients, due to its resemblance to benign conditions such as pityriasis alba, idiopathic macular hypomelanosis (IMH), parapsoriasis, postinflammatory hypopigmentation, vitiligo, and leprosy. We report a case of HMF in a 22‐year‐old Pakistani female presenting with progressive hypopigmented patches and plaques primarily on the back, groin, and trunk. The importance of early assessment in atypical HMF presentations is underscored by the delayed diagnosis despite multiple consultations. The diagnosis was confirmed through skin biopsy findings, which demonstrated CD4+ T‐cell dominance. The first treatment, which included topical steroids and narrow‐band UVB phototherapy, resulted in a partial remission. However, the patient relapsed within a few months and needed to be treated with methotrexate. This example highlights the importance of careful clinical, histological, and immunohistochemical testing for timely HMF detection as well as the need for vigilant monitoring to effectively manage recurrences.

Introduction

Hypopigmented mycosis fungoides (HMF) is the most prevalent form of cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma (CTCL) and is classified as a low‐grade lymphoproliferative disease. It originates from peripheral epidermotropic T‐cells, primarily memory T‐cells (CD45RO+), which express the CD4+ immunophenotype and T‐cell receptor (TCR) [1], [2]. It initially exhibits erythematous patches and/or plaques, which are indicative of the early stages of HMF. It can develop gradually over a number of phases, albeit not all patients experience every stage. Skin disorders occur when malignant T cells infiltrate the skin from the bloodstream, while the skin cells themselves remain noncancerous. Although the exact cause is still unknown, it seems to be caused by long‐term antigenic stimulation that causes T‐lymphocyte clonal growth and epidermal infiltration. However, it is still unclear what mechanism or components are involved in this antigenic activation. Clinically, patches most frequently appear on sun‐protected areas such as the breasts, buttocks, upper thighs, and lower abdomen, and may remain stable over time or disappear and reappear. Some afflicted people develop plaques, the subsequent stage of mycosis fungoides (MF), from patches [3], [4], [5]. The inflammatory interplay in HMF involves cytokines like IL‐17, IL‐22, and TNF‐α, which drive immune dysregulation, angiogenesis, and epidermal changes. Early‐stage lesions have a small number of malignant T cells within an immune infiltrate that limits tumor expansion, with CD8+ lymphocytes linked to a better prognosis. As the disease progresses, a shift from Th1 to Th2 dominance occurs, leading to immune suppression and reduced anti‐tumor activity. Late‐stage CTCL is marked by a strong Th2 response, diminished Th1 cells, and impaired cellular immunity, contributing to disease progression [6], [7]. MF clinical expression varies, with adults showing more common variants than pediatric patients. The classic variant at the plaque stage is more common in adults than in children, with the hypopigmented variant being the most common. In contrast, 44% of the population had the classic variant at the plaque stage, while 17% had the hypopigmented variant. The incidence is higher in men, with a mean age of 50 years, with a 2:1 ratio compared to women. In the study, women were most affected (57.9%), with a 1.3:1 ratio compared to men [8].

This case highlights a diagnostic issue since HMF can resemble other disorders like pityriasis alba, idiopathic macular hypomelanosis (IMH), parapsoriasis, postinflammatory hypopigmentation, vitiligo, and leprosy [9]. It manifests uniquely in a young female with darker skin. Since it can mimic benign hypopigmented lesions, HMF is relatively uncommon and frequently misdiagnosed, especially in patients with darker skin. This delay in identification highlights how crucial it is to take MF into account in atypical presentations and how immunohistochemical staining and histological investigation are necessary to confirm the diagnosis in these situations. This case has been reported according to CARE CRITERIA [10].

Case Presentation

A 22‐year‐old female patient, had been in good health 7 years back when she noticed a small hypopigmented lesion on her lower back, which gradually and progressively increased in size and number over the period of 1 year. These lesions involved the back and sides of the trunk, buttocks, groin, and anterior aspect of the thighs, sparing the face, chest, center of the abdomen, arms, lower legs, palms, and soles, and the largest patch was present on the left flank area.

Few patches were covered with fine scaling, whitish in color, loosely adherent, with no history of oozing of blood upon scratching. Lesions were associated with mild itching, graded as 2/10, with no special time of itching or family history of itching. There was no history of hair loss, decreased sweating, or loss of sensations in these lesions. Lesions were persistent with no aggravating or relieving factors. After 1 year of presentation, she developed scaly, red, raised lesions in a similar distribution. These lesions were persistent with more aggravation in the summer.

She has a history of allergies to dust, perfumes, and bleaching agents, and there is no history of fever, cough, night sweats, weight loss, lumps and bumps on the body, burning sensation in the eyes, joint pain, oral ulcers, or proximal myopathy. She was diagnosed with polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) 3 years ago, is compliant with her medication, and has a history of C‐section 2.5 years ago. She has gained 5 kg in the past 3 years. Her mother has a history of thyroid illness, and her father died due to brain cancer. With these complaints, she had multiple visits to local doctors and which delayed her diagnosis.

On examination, a young female of average height and build is seated comfortably and appears well‐oriented to time, place, and person. Her vital signs are stable, with a blood pressure of 120/70 mmHg, a regular pulse rate of 75 beats per minute, a respiratory rate of 17 breaths per minute, and an afebrile temperature. Her body mass index (BMI) is calculated at 27.2 kg/m2. The sensory system was intact, the superficial and deep tendon reflexes were normal, the cranial nerves (I–XII) were all intact, there were no indications of meningeal irritation, and the tone and reflexes of the lower and upper limbs had a power of 5/5. The coordination, walking, and balance were all normal. Cardiovascular system: On auscultation, S1 and S2 are heard without any additional sounds. Respiratory system: no crackles or wheezes are audible, and the chest cavity is normal with excellent air intake. Examining the abdomen reveals a normal shape, no hepatosplenomegaly, no palpable masses, and regular bowel sounds.

Moreover, the patient had many irregular, distinct, and poorly defined hypopigmented patches when they first appeared. On the side of the trunk, the biggest patch, which measures about 6 × 3 cm, is one of several that show exquisite scaling. The groin area revealed many erythematous, scaly plaques of different sizes, the biggest of which is around 5 × 3 cm. With a negative Auspitz sign, the overlaying scales are thin, white, and loosely adhering with a halo of hypopigmentation that surrounds a few plaques (Figure 1).

On the basis of clinical examination and presenting complaints, we made the differential diagnosis: HMF, leprosy, parapsoriasis, pityriasis alba, pityriasis versicolor, and vitiligo.

Investigations and imaging: Her initial complete blood count report shows normocytic normochromic with raised ESR 38 mm/h (N: 0–25 mm/h) and CRP 4.0 mg/dL (N:< 5.0 mg/dL). Her liver function and renal function tests were within normal range. Serum electrolytes were within normal range. Thyroid function test shows TSH 1.13microU/L (N: 0.5–5 microU/ml). The anti‐nuclear antibody profile is negative. Serum IgE level is mildly elevated at 296 IU/mL (N:0–100 IU/mL). HIV, HCV antibody, and HBsAg serology tests were negative. Serum Beta‐2‐Microglobulin shows a value of 1026 ng/mL (N: 607–2454 ng/mL). TCR gene rearrangement was not done. Wood lamp examination was done to exclude other dermatological conditions like fungal infections, bacterial infections, vitiligo, psoriasis, pityriasis versicolor, and other hypopigmented skin conditions (Figure 3).

Wood lamp examination shows hypopigmented patches that appear lighter than the surrounding normal skin.

Radiological imaging: Ultrasound of the whole abdomen shows a normal scan. Contrast‐enhanced CT scan (CECT scan) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis shows subcentimeter‐sized bilateral inguinal lymph nodes, the largest one on the right side, approximately. 1.9 cm, left side 1.7 cm. Positron emission tomography (PET‐CT scan) findings indicate faint FGD (fluorodeoxyglucose) positivity in bilateral inguinal lymph nodes, which suggests that there is some metabolic activity in these lymph nodes. However, the report notes that this is most likely due to an inflammatory etiology rather than malignancy. Chest X‐ray demonstrates no abnormal findings; midline trachea, lung fields are symmetrical and clear with no abnormal opacities, normal heart size, diaphragms are well defined with sharp costophrenic angles, and no pathological findings in bones and soft tissues (Figure 4).

Chest X‐ray.

Histopathological findings: Histopathological examination of two punch biopsy samples, one from the hypopigmented patch and the other from the plaque, revealed distinctive findings. In the hypopigmented patch, the epidermis showed focal spongiosis, which suggests an inflammatory process, along with the presence of atypical cells with convoluted nuclei, indicating potential malignant transformation or dysplasia. Importantly, there was no significant dermal infiltration, pointing to a less aggressive process in this lesion. In contrast, the biopsy from the plaque demonstrated psoriasiform hyperplasia of the epidermis, a characteristic finding in various dermatologic conditions, including MF. The dermo‐epidermal infiltrate was dense and composed of atypical cells with convoluted nuclei, strongly suggesting cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma. Focal epidermotropism, where atypical cells migrated from the dermis into the epidermis, was noted, which is a key feature in MF. Additionally, dermal fibrosis was observed, indicative of a chronic inflammatory or neoplastic process. Immunohistochemical staining showed that the atypical cells were positive for CD‐4+ and CD‐3+, confirming a T‐cell lineage, while the absence of CD‐8 further supports a diagnosis of MF or other T‐cell lymphomas with a predominant CD4+ phenotype.

Skin biopsy revealed a diagnosis of HMF. According to the Evaluation of the International Society for Cutaneous Lymphoma Algorithm for the Diagnosis of Early MF [11] after completing this assessment, the patient got five points: (2 points for Clinical: Persistent and progressive patches/thin plaques plus non‐sun‐exposed location and size/shape variation; 2 points for histopathologic: superficial lymphoid infiltrate plus lymphoid atypical and epidermotropism without spongiosis; 1 point for immunopathological: CD3+ and CD4 +).

The absence of certain diagnostic tests, such as TCR gene rearrangement analysis, was due to resource limitations.

The International Society for Cutaneous Lymphoma (ISCL) and European Organization of Research and Treatment of Cancer (EORTC) [12] criteria were used to classify her MF as stage 2 (T2N1M0B0) at the time of diagnosis. At this stage, she had notable skin involvement (T2), some lymph node involvement (N1), no organ metastases (M0), and no abnormal blood results associated with the illness (B0).

Initial treatment after being admitted at Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre (JPMC) started with Skin Directed Therapy: Narrow Band UVB (NBUVB) 3 times weekly (total of 40 sessions done) along with topical steroids (Figure 5A).

A shows before treatment; Figure 4B shows good improvements after NBUVB phototherapy and topical corticosteroids treatment.

After completion of 40 sessions of Phototherapy Narrow Band UVB (NBUVB) and topical steroids, with a discernible decrease in plaques and patches, along with alleviation of discomfort and itching, the patient has demonstrated notable progress (Figure 5B).

After 3 months of initial treatment, she again presented with lesions, this time on acral distribution; for that, she was prescribed methotrexate 10 mg orally once a week and advised to follow up closely. On her recent visit, the patient's condition significantly improved.

Discussion

MF accounts for less than 1% of all non‐Hodgkin lymphoma cases, making it the most prevalent primary cutaneous T‐cell lymphoma. Individuals who have a classical form of MF, also called Alibert‐Bazin, experience a lengthy clinical course spanning years or even decades, progressing from patch stage to plaque stage and ultimately to tumor stage illness [13]. MF aetiopathogenesis is still a mystery. A genetic predisposition (e.g., HLA type), chemical exposure (e.g., medicines), or persistent antigen stimulation are some of the theories. Environmental triggers are suggested by the geographic clustering of cases and MF impacting family members. Currently, there is no indication of a viral etiology, in contrast to certain instances of CTCL [14].

In our case report, we describe a case of HMF in a 22‐year‐old female patient. The Literature Review shows very scanty cases of HMF in young female patients. Most cases are found with a median age of 50–60 years and a 2:1 predominance of the male to female ratio [15], [16].

It may be challenging to make an early diagnosis in young patients: Clinically and histologically, HMF can mimic benign inflammatory conditions, especially vitiligo, atopic dermatitis, leprosy, and parapsoriasis [17]. However, our patient has a history of PCOS, allergy to dust and perfumes, and also has a family history of thyroid illness that delays diagnosis. Some literature shows that there is an association of PCOS with dermatological conditions like psoriasis and seborrheic dermatitis [18].

In contrast to the hypopigmentation found in pityriasis alba, lesions in pityriasis versicolor can be hypopigmented or hyperpigmented. This frequent, recurring, superficial fungal infection appears as many macules, patches, or thin plaques, primarily on the upper trunk and upper extremities. Physical examination alone can often raise a strong clinical suspicion, and Wood's lamp examination may reveal yellow to yellow‐green fluorescence. A conclusive diagnosis of pityriasis versicolor, however, necessitates a potassium hydroxide preparation that will reveal the typical hyphal and yeast forms of Malassezia furfur under a microscope [19]. That is why we performed a Wood's lamp examination to help distinguish HMF from pityriasis alba and pityriasis versicolor.

Due to the presence of morphological features characteristic of fully developed MF, the histological diagnosis is typically straightforward. It is tough and tricky to diagnose early MF histopathologically. This is due to the fact that tiny alterations in early lesions might be mistaken for inflammatory dermatoses, including atypical manifestations of common ailments like psoriasis and eczema [1]. The following parameters on light microscopy are highly suggestive of the diagnosis of MF: hyperconvoluted intraepidermal lymphocytes aligned within the basal layer, disproportionate epidermotropism, Pautrier's microabscesses, atypical intraepidermal lymphocytes surrounded by halos, and papillary dermal fibrosis within a lichenoid infiltrate [20]. Immunohistochemical analysis can identify characteristics that help distinguish HMF from classical MF. While CD8+ T cells make up a small portion of reactive T lymphocytes and are mostly found in the dermal papillae, neoplastic CD4+ T cells are the hallmark of epidermotropism in classical MF. Rarely do neoplastic CD8+ T cells invade. Some publications have, however, reported a preponderance of CD8+ cell epidermotropism in HMF [9]. Certain studies have verified that HMF lesions can exhibit typical characteristics of classical MF, including a preponderance of CD4+ T cells and the maintenance of a normal ratio of CD4+ to CD8+ lymphocytes [21]. In our case we took two samples of punch biopsy for histopathological examination: one from a patch lesion that shows epidermis focal spongiosis with few atypical cells with convoluted nuclei; the other sample from a plaque lesion shows skin epidermis with psoriasiform hyperplasia, dense dermo‐epidermal infiltrate is seen composed of enlarged atypical cells with convoluted nuclei, focal epidermotropism is identified, there is dermal fibrosis and on staining atypical cells show positive staining for CD‐4+ and CD‐3+, however, CD‐8 is negative.

Our patient showed no signs of systemic involvement, but even with a good prognosis, it is important to treat HMF because of its potential lethality and relapses. To rule out visceral involvement, a complete clinical assessment, peripheral blood examination, flow cytometry‐based quantification of Sézary cells and T‐lymphocytes, and imaging studies should be conducted.

Progressive macular hypomelanosis (PMH) is a skin illness that causes ill‐defined hypopigmented macules over the trunk, face, neck, and extremities. Despite its prevalence, it remains under‐recognized. It is most typically seen in young women with darker skin who come from tropical climes, but it can occur in any ethnicity [22]. HMF typically presents with an asymmetrical distribution of lesions.

According to a recent study, four patients with HMF experienced partial remission, whereas seven out of 11 patients experienced total remission with NBUVB twice weekly (a mean of 40 sessions). Three individuals experienced relapse after an average of 10 months. Relapses are more common in darker skin tones after receiving NBUVB phototherapy [23]. In our case, the patient received a total of 40 sessions of NBUVB three times weekly, along with topical steroids; our patient experienced relapse after 3 months of treatment. Subsequently, methotrexate 10 mg orally once a week was commenced. The literature also suggests retinoids, vorinostat, brentuximab, mogamulizumab, and low‐dose methotrexate for HMF relapses. Methotrexate (10–25 mg) orally or subcutaneously once a week is prescribed in relapses as maintenance therapy [24].

Our patient was advised to closely follow up if she experiences any signs of other relapses or deterioration in her condition. Early detection and close monitoring are critical in managing HMF, as misdiagnosis can delay adequate therapy. Given its similarities to benign hypopigmented illnesses, doctors should maintain a high level of suspicion, particularly in individuals with chronic or unusual lesions. Regular monitoring and early histological examination can boost diagnostic accuracy and patient outcomes.

TCR gene clonal rearrangement analysis is a useful tool in diagnosing early MF. The TCR‐γ gene is recommended for routine analysis, whereas the TCR‐β gene has potential in combination for intractable cases [25]. In our case, TCR gene clonal rearrangement analysis was not done due to resource limitations.

Future research should look into how genetic and environmental factors influence HMF, particularly in young, dark‐skinned people. Investigating molecular markers, immunological responses, and regional epidemiological patterns may improve early detection and lead to more focused therapy.

Conclusion

We describe a rare case of HMF in a young female, which contrasts with the usual clinical and demographic characteristics of MF. HMF may be difficult to diagnose, especially when trying to differentiate it from benign inflammatory disorders. This was highlighted by the patient's unusual presentation, which included hypopigmented patches and a very early beginning. Immunohistochemical results further reinforced the precise diagnosis made possible by a thorough clinical and histological assessment. Narrow‐band UVB phototherapy and topical steroids produced a partial remission during the initial course of treatment; nevertheless, recurrence required methotrexate therapy, indicating that HMF is relapsing. This example highlights how crucial early identification and ongoing monitoring are to the successful management of HMF, as well as the need to take patient‐specific variables like age, clinical presentation, and response to first treatment into account.
