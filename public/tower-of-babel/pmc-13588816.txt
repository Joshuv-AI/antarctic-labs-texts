Chromosomal instability in cancer: sources, consequences and new therapeutic opportunities
Authors: Angela Flavia Serpico, Domenico Grieco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chromosomal instability (CIN) refers to a high rate of chromosome segregation errors during consecutive cell divisions and is a hallmark of cancers with aggressive behavior and poor prognosis. Cancer cells exhibiting CIN may acquire both numerical (N-CIN) and structural (S-CIN) chromosome alterations, namely by chromosome mis-segregation and DNA replication/repair defects, respectively. Importantly, these processes are tightly interconnected: mis-segregating chromosomes are prone to DNA damage, while DNA replication or repair defects can, in turn, promote chromosome mis-segregation. Consequently, CIN emerges as a consequence of these intertwined occurrences. CIN can confer survival advantages to cancer cells by enhancing their adaptation abilities, promoting intratumor heterogeneity, therapy resistance and acquisition of metastatic potential. In contrast, induction of CIN in healthy cells typically compromises cellular fitness and viability, triggering senescence and apoptosis. Therefore, cancer cells develop specific adaptation capabilities that allow them to tolerate CIN, often becoming dependent on specific CIN tolerance pathways. Elucidating the molecular circuits underlying CIN and clarifying the basis of its multifaceted roles in cancer will deepen our understanding of tumorigenesis and reveal therapeutically exploitable vulnerabilities in chromosomally unstable cancers. Here, we provide an integrated perspective on the principal sources of CIN, newly identified CIN-specific dependencies and vulnerabilities and how CIN impacts genome integrity. We discuss emerging evidence on the potential influence of CIN on tumor evolution or regression, including the CIN paradox. We further highlight how integrating this novel information with emerging diagnostic technologies that allow quantitative, qualitative and spatiotemporal characterization of CIN within tumors is reshaping our ability to define CIN at the single-tumor level, opening new avenues for targeted therapeutic strategies.

Introduction

The maintenance of genome integrity and ploidy during cell division relies on accurate duplication of the parental genome and its equal segregation into daughter cells. These processes are safeguarded by multiple surveillance mechanisms that ensure error-free DNA replication and faithful chromosome segregation.1 When these mechanisms fail, mitotic fidelity is compromised and cells may accumulate numerical and structural chromosomal alterations that create a fertile soil for carcinogenesis.2,3

Chromosomal instability (CIN) represents a major driver of these alterations and of cancer evolution.4 CIN is defined as a dynamic condition characterized by ongoing chromosome segregation errors across successive cell divisions, generating genomic diversity within tumor cell populations. This variability fuels intratumor heterogeneity (ITH) enhancing cancer cell adaptability, proliferation, metastasizing potential, immune evasion and resistance to therapy.4

Despite its central role in tumor biology, CIN remains challenging to be accurately measured because it reflects a dynamic process rather than a static genomic state. However, indirect indicators, such as widespread gene copy-number alterations and complex karyotypes, strongly suggest that CIN is prevalent in many tumors, including serous ovarian, triple-negative breast (TNBC), colorectal (CRC), lung and pancreas cancers as well as hematological malignancies such as acute myeloid leukemia (AML), chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), cancers of unknown primary origin (CUP) and metastatic lesions.5,6

In contrast, some tumors exhibit relatively stable chromosomal aberrations (steady aneuploidy) with low chromosome mis-segregation rates, as observed in gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), thyroid carcinoma, kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) and chronic myeloid leukemia (CML).7 In these cases, the aneuploid state appears to be maintained by selective pressure. Notably, some of these cancers could evolve toward a CIN phenotype, as suggested by studies indicating that aneuploidy itself may, in certain contexts, predispose to genome instability.8–11

While CIN promotes tumor evolution, it is detrimental to normal cells. Increasing CIN levels in healthy cells impairs viability, leading to senescence, cell cycle arrest, cell death and immune clearance.12,13 How CIN cancer cells withstand high CIN rates, in contrast to normal cells, remains an open question. Nevertheless, further increasing CIN beyond a tolerable threshold is also harmful to CIN cancer cells, representing a therapeutic strategy.14

Here, we review current and emerging knowledge on the sources and consequences of CIN, recently identified CIN-associated dependencies and vulnerabilities, the mechanisms that enable CIN tolerance in cancer cells and the impact of CIN on immune surveillance and the tumor microenvironment (TME). By integrating these biological processes with emerging single-tumor diagnostic methodologies capable of capturing the quantitative, qualitative and spatiotemporal features of CIN, we provide an updated and comprehensive framework for understanding CIN-driven cancer biology.

Classification Of CIN

CIN can be subdivided into two main categories: numerical, N-CIN, and structural, S-CIN.15 N-CIN refers to alterations in chromosome number, including gain or loss of whole chromosomes (aneuploidy) or gain of entire chromosome sets (polyploidy) (Fig. 2; N-CIN).16 S-CIN refers to structural alterations of chromosomes, such as amplifications, deletions, inversions, translocations, chromothripsis or extrachromosomal circular DNA (eccDNA; Fig. 2; S-CIN).17 Although these categories arise from distinct mechanisms and occur at different rates, they are nonetheless interconnected, establishing self-reinforcing cycles that exacerbate genomic derangements and fuel tumor evolution (Fig. 2; S and N CIN).18–20Fig. 2Classification of CIN. Mechanisms driving CIN

CIN can develop from a variety of molecular deficiencies. Defects in chromosome segregation can lead to quantitative genomic modifications, mainly resulting in N-CIN, while errors in DNA replication/repair can promote qualitative genetic rearrangements, primarily leading to S-CIN.15 N-CIN preferentially originates from kinetochore-microtubule (K-MT) attachment abnormalities, failure of the safeguard mechanism - spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC) - that delays anaphase onset until spindle assembly, centrosome alterations or chromatid cohesion defects.21 S-CIN usually derives from deregulation of mechanisms controlling DNA replication and integrity, such as replication stress, collapse of replication forks, defects in DNA damage response (DDR) pathways and dysfunction of telomeres.4,22 Additionally, epigenetic perturbations may also promote both N-CIN and S-CIN and as previously mentioned the two forms of instability are mechanistically linked to each other: N-CIN can promote DNA damage and structural alterations, while S-CIN can affect mitotic processes (Fig. 3).4,18–20Fig. 3Mechanisms driving CIN. a Improper K-MT attachments/centromere defects, weakened SAC or hyperactive SAC, centrosome defects, causing monopolar or multipolar spindle formation, and faulty sister chromatid cohesion are all conditions that may lead to chromosome mis-segregation resulting in N-CIN. Lagging chromosomes, in addition to potentially causing N-CIN, may also undergo direct damage by mechanical forces during cytokinesis or end up into micronuclei (MN) and potentially undergo consequent chromothripsis; thus, chromosome mis-segregation may also promote S-CIN. c DNA replication stress, by nucleotide depletion, oncogene activation or hairpin structure formation, DNA damage and telomere dysfunction, leading to telomere shortening or end-to-end fusion events, are all conditions potentially leading to S-CIN. d Nevertheless, replication stress, DNA damage and telomere dysfunction, which initially induce double strand breaks (DSBs) and structural chromosomal rearrangements, may induce chromosome bridging during later mitotic events, thus promoting N-CIN. Improper kinetochore-microtubule attachments

MT attachment to chromosomes in mitosis is a complex, dynamic and reversible process regulated by error correction mechanisms.23 Spindle MTs deputed to connect sister chromatids to opposite spindle poles bind chromosomes at the centromere. The centromere is the primary chromosome constriction where specialized regions of centromeric chromatin allow assembly of the kinetochore (K), a proteinaceous structure ultimately deputed to interact with spindle MTs.24 In mammalian cells, each sister chromatid kinetochore is captured by a number of MTs connected with opposite spindle poles.25 The turnover of K-MT attachments is very fast in early mitosis but slows down upon end-on attachments, when chromosomes finally establish bipolar orientation and align at the metaphase plate.23 K-MT attachment dynamism is required for chromosomes to reach the correct bioriented configuration by allowing correction of faulty attachments via tension-sensing mechanisms.23,26,27 Aberrant K-MT attachments that can occur are monotelic attachments - when only a single kinetochore attaches to MTs -, syntelic attachments - when both sister kinetochores couple to MTs connected to one spindle pole - or merotelic attachments - when a single kinetochore is attached to MTs connected to both spindle poles.28,29 Once these faulty attachments are corrected, sister kinetochores finally establish amphitelic attachments to MTs connected to opposite spindle poles and the spindle is correctly assembled.28,29

Thus, erroneous chromosome configurations are corrected before the onset of anaphase and Aurora B kinase (AURK B), embedded in the multiprotein Chromosome Passenger Complex (CPC), is a crucial player in this process.30 AURK B, along with the CPC, localizes at the inner centromeres and is essential for K-MT attachment dynamics by controlling the MT depolymerase capability of MCAK and Kif2a kinesins as well as regulating components of the outer kinetochore KMN network (KNL1/Mis12 complex/Ndc80 complex), thus destabilizing faulty K-MT attachments and preventing anaphase onset until all chromosomes reach an amphitelic configuration.31–34 Conversely, tumors with CIN often display more stable K-MT attachments than chromosomally stable diploid cells, suggesting that reduced K-MT dynamicity may stabilize erroneous K-MT attachments and affect error correction mechanisms.35,36 Furthermore, reducing K-MT attachment stability in those cancer cells, via MCAK and Kif2b overexpression, restores faithful chromosome segregation.35

In general terms, defects in the function of proteins such as AURK B, MCAK, Kif2b, KMN complex, monopolar spindle protein 1 (Mps1), BUBR1, Mad2, CENP-E, CLASPs and APC may antagonize, directly or indirectly, correction of K-MT misattachments and promote chromosome segregation errors in anaphase and CIN.35–46 Although mutations in these genes are uncommonly observed in human tumors, except for the frequent loss-of-function mutations of APC in colon cancer, their expression is often altered.47–49 This evidence leads to a plausible scenario where imbalances in kinetochore protein levels, generated by an initial chromosome mis-segregation event or epigenetic mechanisms, impair error correction.39 Importantly, as centromeres are the docking sites for the kinetochores, their alterations can lead to errors in K-MT attachments and cause CIN.50 In humans, centromere identity is primarily defined by the restricted deposition of the histone H3 variant CENP-A.24 Overexpression-induced mislocalization of CENP-A to noncentromeric regions has been demonstrated to promote aneuploidy and CIN.51 Indeed, mislocalized CENP-A compromises kinetochore integrity and destabilzes K-MT attachments, leading to lagging chromosomes and micronuclei (MN) formation.51 Consistently, CENP-A is frequently overexpressed in a wide range of cancers and correlates with tumor aggressiveness and poor prognosis, favoring invasion and metastasis and promoting neocentromere formation.52,53 Notably, CENP-A overexpression in p53 defective cells promotes epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT), suggesting a potential contribution to tumor evolution.54

In addition, CENP-B contributes to centromere identity and integrity and plays a key role in ensuring faithful chromosome segregation, as its absence increases mis-segregation rates and leads to CIN in both mouse and human cells.55,56

Moreover, as described below, defective K-MT attachments can also arise from altered epigenetic regulation (see subsection “Epigenetic perturbations”).

Impaired spindle assembly checkpoint

Alterations in K-MT attachment stability directly influence SAC activity. The SAC is a prominent safeguard mechanism against chromosome mis-segregation.57 During mitosis, the SAC delays progression into anaphase in the presence of even a single improperly attached kinetochore by inhibiting the anaphase-promoting complex or cyclosome (APC/C) that, in association with its coactivator Cdc20 (APC/CCdc20), is the crucial ubiquitin ligase that promotes sister chromatid separation and inactivation of the master mitotic kinase cyclin B-dependent kinase 1 (Cdk1).58 This mechanism ensures sufficient time for error correction and promotes correct spindle assembly.58 Indeed, unstable K-MT attachments such as monotelic and syntelic orientations maintain SAC activation, prolong mitotic progression and are generally corrected into proper amphitelic configurations.59 Conversely, hyperstable yet erroneous attachments, such as merotelic attachments, are more prone to evade full SAC activation increasing the risk of chromosome mis-segregation and CIN.60

SAC effector factors associate at mis- or unattached kinetochores to constitute the mitotic checkpoint complex (MCC), comprising MAD2, BUB3, BubR1 and Cdc20, in which Cdc20 is inhibited in activating the APC/C.61 SAC function is controlled by major kinases including AURK A/B, Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1), Mps1 and Cdk1.61–68 Once all sister kinetochores are properly, bipolarly, captured by spindle MTs, the SAC is silenced and the MCC is disassembled, liberating the active APC/CCdc20 complex.57,58 Thus, SAC signaling malfunctions may alter the timing of sister chromatid separation, affect the K-MT attachment error correction mechanisms and feed into chromosome mis-segregation and CIN phenotypes.42,69

A number of SAC components have been observed to be over- or underexpressed in a subgroup of human cancers, including leukemias and breast, gastric, hepatic, colorectal, bladder, ovarian, kidney and lung tumors.70 However, mutations in SAC genes rarely occur in human cancers (The Cancer Genomic Atlas – TCGA - https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov; cBio Cancer Genomics Portal (http://cbioportal.org)), except for Bub1 mutations in some colon tumors, Mad2 mutations in breast tumors and BubR1 germline mutations causative of mosaic variegated aneuploidy (MVA) syndrome type 1, an extremely rare disease entailing dysmorphic features and childhood tumors.41,71,72 Nevertheless, it is plausible that posttranslational and indirect modifications of SAC players contribute to CIN in tumors.73,74 Interestingly, both weakening and hyperactivating SAC signaling have been described to cause CIN.75,76 For instance, some tumors with CIN display decreased levels of Mad2 protein while Mad2 overexpression induces CIN in mice.42,77,78 Cdc20 overexpression has been indicated as a predictor of poor prognosis in breast, colorectal, lung, gastric, oral, prostate, urothelial bladder cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma, likely due to its SAC overriding ability.79 Nevertheless, CIN cancer cells also rely on SAC to survive by preventing precocious anaphase and the consequent increase in CIN to unbearable levels.80,81 Indeed, Cdc20 overexpression has been shown to increase sensitivity to SAC-overriding drugs such as Mps1 inhibitors.82

Centrosome defects

Centrosomes are the main MT-organizing centers (MTOCs) in animal cells and consist of two orthogonal centrioles surrounded by pericentriolar material, a proteinaceous specialized structure.83 They trigger MT de novo assembly and contribute to the arrangement of the mitotic spindle poles.83 Each daughter cell receives one centrosome that has to undergo duplication by disengaging the two centrioles that will be templates for their duplication in order to form two centrosomes, which are required to form the spindle poles in the following mitosis.84 Failure in centrosome duplication may arise from scant centriole disengagement during the telophase of the previous cell cycle, a process that requires the activity of PLK1 to phosphorylate the pericentriolar protein pericentrin, which is subsequently recognized and cleaved by the protease separase.85

Premature centriole disengagement, in contrast, can result in multipolar spindles, which are commonly described in many cancers.86,87 Polyploid cells produced by failed cytokinesis can form multipolar spindles, increasing the likelihood of chromosome mis-segregation during mitosis.88 Although cancer cells can convert multipolar spindles into bipolar spindles through mechanisms of centrosome clustering, transient abnormal spindles can favor erroneous K-MT attachments such as merotelic attachments.28,89,90 Merotelic attachments can easily evade correction because they generate sufficient kinetochore tension and MT occupancy, thereby escaping full SAC activation and leading to lagging chromosomes in anaphase. Lagging chromosomes can either ultimately segregate correctly, not affecting daughter cell ploidy, or mis-segregate and drive N-CIN.91 Importantly, mis-segregating lagging chromosomes can also promote S-CIN as they can undergo mechanical damage, if trapped in the cytokinesis furrow, or end up into MN where they are prone to extensive fragmentation and rearrangement events such as chromothripsis (see subsection “Micronuclei formation and chromothripsis”).18,92

Monopolar spindles may also derive from improper centrosome separation prior to cell division.93 Loss of ubiquitin-specific peptidase 44 (USP44), a centrosome-localized deubiquitinase, impedes full centrosome separation and induces chromosome segregation errors.94 Accordingly, USP44 knockout mice show increased aneuploidy rates and are highly prone to spontaneous tumors.94 Defective centrosome separation can also occur in cells overexpressing kinesin family member 11 (Kif11 or Eg5), which display abnormal spindles as well as changes in DNA ploidy and are capable of tumor formation in mice.95

Proper centrosome number is also controlled by USP33, a deubiquitylating enzyme regulating levels of CP110, a centriolar protein that positively controls centriole duplication.96 During G2/M phase of the cell cycle, CP110 is ubiquitylated by the SCFCyclin F ubiquitin ligase complex and addressed for degradation.97 Simultaneously, USP33 activity antagonizes CP110 SCFCyclin F-mediated ubiquitylation and timely regulates centrosome duplication.96,97 Indeed, depletion of USP33 inhibits centrosome duplication, while its upregulation promotes supernumerary centriolar foci formation and is often detected in pancreatic ductal carcinomas (PDAC).96,98

Centrosome overduplication can occur as a result of PLK4 overexpression and entails multipolar spindle formation.99 PLK4 phosphorylates the centriole protein Stil/Ana2 allowing it to recruit Sas6, the core of the cartwheel assembly that scaffolds centrioles.100 Overexpression of PLK4 or absence of the ubiquitin ligase SCF/Slimb, which is required for its degradation, prompts the formation of multiple centrosomes which are responsible for errors in chromosome segregation and the emergence of aneuploid cells.99,101 Moreover, multiple centrosomes can be found in tetraploid cells, originating from Kif11/Eg5 overexpression or viral-induced cell fusion.96,102

Collectively, these findings indicate that aberrations in centrosome number affect proper spindle formation and promote CIN.

Faulty sister chromatid cohesion

In mitosis, centromeric sister chromatid cohesion is guaranteed by the cohesin multisubunit complex and is required for bipolar chromosome configuration and stabilization at metaphase.103 Under normal conditions, timely cleavage of centromeric cohesins occurs when all chromosomes achieve biorientation (amphitelic attachments), leading to sister chromatid separation.104 Prolonged or shortened chromatid cohesion during anaphase can cause chromosome mis-segregation and CIN.105 Loss of cohesion severely compromises cell fitness: homozygous mutations in cohesin genes display embryonic lethality.106

The cohesin complex is composed of a tripartite ring containing structural maintenance of chromosomes 1 A (SMC1A), structural maintenance of chromosomes 3 (SMC3), RAD21 cohesin complex component (RAD21) and a peripheral subunit of which two isoforms exist, stromal antigen 1 (STAG1/SA1) and stromal antigen 2 (STAG2/SA2).107,108 In particular, RAD21 is a substrate for the endopeptidase separase (extra spindle pole bodies like 1, ESPL1), which performs its proteolytic cleavage in order to separate sister chromatids at the metaphase-to-anaphase transition.109 Separase is kept inactive until anaphase onset primarily by binding to securin, its inhibitory chaperone, and additionally through inhibitory phosphorylation by Cdk1.110 Indeed, separase overexpression can induce premature sister chromatid disjunction promoting chromosome mis-segregation and tetraploidy and is observed in many human cancers, including brain, prostate, breast and blood tumors with high incidence of metastasis and relapse.111,112 Furthermore, separase mRNA overexpression is positively associated with tumor grade and negatively correlated with survival of breast cancer patients.113 Similarly, securin overexpression prevents proper sister chromatid separation and leads to aneuploidy and CIN.114 Securin is abundantly expressed in aggressive human tumors and is associated with the invasive and metastatic potential of cancer cells.115,116

Somatic mutations in genes encoding cohesin subunits and regulators have been found in several human tumors such as Ewing’s sarcoma, bladder cancer and myeloid leukemias, and STAG2 mutations figure prominently.117–119 Notably, some groups of Ewing sarcomas and myeloid neoplasms show reduced cohesin gene expression in the absence of mutations.120,121 Broadly, reduced expression of cohesion controlling genes adversely affects chromatid cohesion and chromosome segregation eventually prompting CIN.122

In addition, cohesins also play a crucial role in establishing DNA topology and cohesin mutations contribute to carcinogenesis by inducing DNA replication stress.123 Indeed, cohesin mutations that impair their recruitment onto chromatin can induce both DNA replication fork collapse, triggering double strand breaks (DSBs) and translocations and can interfere with RNA polymerase II localization to promoters and elongation sites, thus, severely deregulating transcription.124–126

Thus, cohesion defects adversely affect chromosome segregation and generate CIN.

Replication stress, DNA damage and defective DNA repair

Replication stress is a broad term that comprises a variety of abnormal DNA replication conditions, often following oncogene activation, that include increased origin firing, depletion of nucleotides and hairpin structures that may ultimately lead to stalling of replication forks.127 Stalled DNA replication forks convene repair proteins activating the DDR but, if repair fails, may fatally collapse and cause DNA DSBs.128 The primary response to replication stress is controlled by the replication stress sensor ATR kinase which responds to single stranded DNA (ssDNA) and triggers the DNA replication checkpoint by activating the CHK1 and WEE1 kinases.129 This protects stalled forks and delays entry into mitosis by inhibiting full Cdk1 activation.129 Thus, ATR stabilizes stalled replication forks, halts their detachment from the replisome and prevents origin firing.130 When stress is over, the ATR pathway grants the replication forks to restart by phosphorylating FANCI or by loading homologous recombination (HR) factors onto ssDNA distributed near the stalled forks.131,132 ATR or CHK1 deficiency or inhibition can lead to fork collapse, promoting DSBs formation and cell death.133 In the event that the ATR/CHK1 pathway fails and DSBs are generated, ATM and DNA-Pk and other factors can promote repair by HR, nonhomologous end-joining (NHEJ) and microhomology-mediated end-joining (MMEJ).134–136 While ATM-mediated DSB repair preserves genome stability, the other mechanisms are more prone to errors.137

The Fanconi anemia (FA) pathway also participates in cellular responses to replication stress, playing a central role in DNA interstrand crosslink (ICLs) repair and promoting HR-mediated repair of ICL intermediate DSBs.138

Complete processing of DSB by replication-coupled HR repair mechanisms ensures genomic stability, while incomplete processing or DSBs resolution by error prone pathways, such as NHEJ and MMEJ, leads to genomic rearrangements including deletions or translocations, dicentric chromosome formation and leaving chromosomal segments unreplicated or intertwined in mitosis, thus contributing to CIN.136,139 Indeed, these chromosomal alterations can induce deleterious consequences in the subsequent mitosis, leading to DNA bridges in anaphase, breakage-fusion-breakage cycles (BFB) as well as MN formation.86,140 Accordingly, mutations in HR main factors, such as Rad51, BRCA1 and BRCA2, associate to both hereditary and sporadic neoplasia, particularly breast and ovarian tumors, while FA patients are prone to develop various types of cancer.141,142 Somatic mutations in ATM and ATR frequently occur in human tumors and germline mutations causes ataxia telangiectasia and Seckel syndrome, respectively, rare inherited disorders marked by elevated cancer risk.143–145 Similarly, CHK1/CHK2 alterations are associated with hereditary and sporadic malignancies.146

Moreover, telomeres are particularly susceptible to replication stress because of their terminal location, repetitive sequence content and highly condensed conformation.147 Consistently, stalled replication forks and defective repair mechanisms can generate dysfunctional telomeres which, in turn, trigger extensive DNA fragmentation and BFB cycles and contribute to CIN.148

Furthermore, due to the highly repetitive nature of α-satellite sequences and compact chromatin organization, centromeres are also difficult to replicate and particularly susceptible to replication stress.149 In addition, centromeric regions are actively transcribed, which helps kinetochore assembly; nevertheless, transcription-replication conflicts can easily ensue at centromeres when replication forks encounter RNA polymerase complexes.150 These conflicts can create R-loops, RNA-DNA hybrid structures that further hinder DNA replication and that can either directly lead to DSBs or generate unresolved DNA topological intermediates, such as catenanes between sister chromatids.151,152 These factors may ultimately lead to the formation of ultrafine DNA bridges in anaphase and DNA damage.153 Importantly, while centromere transcription helps CENP-A incorporation into centromeric chromatin, CENP-A appears to repress R-loop formation and preserve centromeric DNA integrity.154,155

In addition, polyploidy may also fuel genomic instability by affecting DNA replication. Indeed, polyploid cells are often found in human tumors and a recent study showed that polyploid cells are prone to DNA replication stress even in a single interphase, creating a condition for the acquisition of highly abnormal karyotypes.156,157

Furthermore, nuclear envelope dysfunction is increasingly recognized as a key source of DNA damage and genome instability. Altered lamin expression, a common feature of cancer cells, compromises nuclear integrity, rendering the nuclear membrane vulnerable to deformation and rupture, thereby promoting DNA damage.158,159 In addition, genomic instability can also arise in the absence of overt nuclear envelope defects; indeed, it has been shown that mechanical deformation of the nucleus during confined migration or external compression can induce replication stress and DNA damage.160 Thus, the nuclear envelope has a crucial role in integrating mechanical and structural cues that influence genome stability.

Collectively, this evidence proves that replication stress and dysfunction of DNA repair mechanisms can promote CIN.

Epigenetic perturbations

Epigenetics encompasses a range of molecular mechanisms that lead to heritable changes in gene expression independently of DNA sequence alterations, contributing to genome stability maintenance. Epigenetic dysregulation has emerged as a driver of CIN particularly by impairing centromere integrity and centrosome homeostasis.161,162 Human centromere identity is epigenetically defined by chromatin enrichment in the histone H3 variant CENP-A.163 CENP-A positioning at centromeres is shaped by the methylation patterns of centromeric chromatin, as CENP-A associates with hypomethylated regions flanked by hypermethylated domains.164 This DNA methylation landscape serves as a boundary to constrain CENP-A domains, thereby maintaining centromere stability and function and preventing erroneous K-MT attachments and aneuploidy.161 DNA methylation loss can lead to the expansion of CENP-A subdomains that, in turn, may promote erroneous K-MT attachments or affect centromere-kinetochore mechanics via CENP-B or condensin misregulation.161 Interestingly, DNA hypomethylation is a prominent feature of cancer cells and is often associated with cancers exhibiting CIN, including hepatocellular carcinomas and Wilms tumors, as well as with the immunodeficiency with centromeric instability and facial anomalies (ICF) syndrome.165–168

In addition to DNA methylation, posttranslational modifications of CENP-A also contribute to CIN. Hyperphosphorylation of CENP-A at Ser18 by cyclin E1/Cdk2, upon loss of FBW7, leads to CIN as evidenced by an increased frequency of lagging chromosomes, chromosomal bridges and MN formation.169 Importantly, FBW7 is frequently lost by epigenetic mechanisms in aggressive human cancers, including CRC, endometrial cancer, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and hematopoietic neoplasm, and this is associated with poor prognosis and therapeutic resistance.170

Another major epigenetic regulator implicated in CIN is the histone methyltransferase enhancer of zeste homolog 2 (EZH2), which is frequently overactivated in advanced cancers.171 EZH2 catalyzes the trimethylation of histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27me3), leading to silencing of target gene expression.172 Recent studies indicate that EZH2 is upregulated in metastatic cells with high CIN levels, particularly TNBC, where it actively plays a role in driving metastasis.162 Mechanistically, EZH2 represses tankyrase (TNKS), a member of the PARP family, leading to the disruption of centrosomal P4.1-associated protein (CPAP) function. This action results in centrosome overduplication, multipolar mitosis and chromosome mis-segregation, thereby actively promoting CIN and facilitating metastatic progression.162 In addition, EZH2 also appears to repress the DNA double-strand break repair gene RAD51, further contributing to genomic instability.173

Collectively, these findings provide a strong rationale for targeting epigenetic regulators as a potential CIN-cancer therapeutic approach. In particular, EZH2 inhibitors represent a promising antimetastatic strategy, as these first-in-class agents have been shown to reduce CIN and metastasis and are currently under clinical evaluation in solid tumors.171

Emerging evidence indicates that epitranscriptomic deregulation may also contribute to CIN. RNA modifications fine-tune key pathways governing DNA replication, DNA damage repair, telomere maintenance, R-loop homeostasis, mitotic progression and cancer immunity, thereby influencing genome integrity.174–178 Perturbations in the activity of RNA modifying enzymes and their associated reader proteins can exacerbate replication stress, affect mitotic fidelity and influence immune responses.174,177,178 Thus, modifications of these RNA-based regulatory networks may contribute to plasticity, tumor evolution and therapeutic resistance of CIN cancers.178

Crosstalk between N-CIN and S-CIN

N-CIN and S-CIN rarely occur in isolation. Numerical chromosomal alterations often arise along with structural aberrations that rearrange genomic loci, such as amplifications, deletions, inversions and translocations. Several mechanisms can explain these intertwining conditions, for instance mis-segregating chromosomes can be trapped in the cleavage furrow during cytokinesis and undergo damage and DSBs, eventually badly repaired in the following cell cycle.179 In addition, lagging chromosomes segregating separately from the main chromosome mass can be enwrapped into MN, where defective DNA replication and repair mechanisms can lead to DNA damage, chromosome pulverization and chromothripsis in the following cell cycle (see subsection “Micronuclei formation and chromothripsis”).18,180 Persistent anaphase DNA bridges, by transcription/replication conflicts and/or delayed DNA synthesis in mitosis or telomere dysfunction, may also induce cytokinesis furrow regression leading to tetraploid cell formation.19,181 Beyond the direct consequences of segregation errors on genome stability, chromosome mis-segregation and aneuploidy also perturb chromosome stoichiometry and gene dosage, leading to DNA replication stress and upregulation of DNA replication/repair pathway players.182,183 In addition, aneuploidy significantly alters the transcriptome and the proteome, creating an imbalance in the production and assembly of multiprotein complexes.184 This imbalance can burden protein homeostasis systems, triggering proteotoxic stress and impairing the function of multiprotein complexes; these include the DNA replication machinery, leading to replication fork slowing and genomic instability.11,185,186 These types of alterations due to an abnormal chromosome set may also be at the basis of the evolution of relatively stable aneuploid cancers toward CIN.8–11 Indeed, although direct evidence of transition from stable aneuploidy to CIN in human tumors is hard to prove because of limited longitudinal sampling, in vitro studies in cancer-derived cell lines indicate that aneuploid cell progeny shows progressive chromosome instability during advanced stages of clonal evolution.187,188

On the other hand, structural chromosomal damage and replication stress can cause defective chromosome segregation. For example, even in fully replicated centrosomes, centromeric lesions can compromise kinetochore assembly and MT attachments.189 Similarly, structural aberration may lead to the formation of both acentric or dicentric chromosomes that fail to achieve proper bioriented attachments to the mitotic spindle.19,190,191 Furthermore, activation of DDR in mitosis interferes with K-MT attachment correction and drives whole-chromosome segregation errors and CIN.19,20

Double-edged CIN outcomes

CIN exerts variable influences on tumor development, progression and severity and is typically associated with high grade cancers, metastasis and poor prognoses.192–195 However, a small number of patients with CIN cancers exhibit better survival.196 These apparently incongruous outcomes suggest that the influence of CIN on tumor biology is multifaceted and provide evidence for both advantageous and deleterious effects on cell viability. Deleterious effects of CIN on cancer cells include cell cycle arrest, senescence and immune clearance.197,198 Conversely, CIN beneficial outcomes for cancer cells include immune evasion, metastatic ability and drug resistance.194,199–201 In addition, CIN-induced proteostasis imbalance and metabolic alterations can also potentially translate into beneficial or harmful effects for cancer cells (Fig. 4).185,202,203Fig. 4CIN outcomes for cancer cells. Proteostasis imbalance

Protein homeostasis is essential for cell viability and is achieved through coordination of protein synthesis, folding and degradation. Cells employ a proteostasis network (PN), including chaperones and proteolytic machinery, that manages proper protein folding or conveys unfolded proteins to ubiquitin-dependent degradation by the proteasome to ensure balanced functional protein stoichiometry.204 CIN cells generally display imbalanced levels of protein synthesis due to altered gene dosage or expression as well as by structural alterations in genes and proteins that overwhelm the PN control. These conditions induce the persistence of misfolded proteins and the loss of regular stoichiometry leading to proteotoxic stress.205,206 Conversely, polyploid cells have multiple sets of chromosomes and this redundancy helps them to balance protein stoichiometry and suffer proteotoxic stress less than aneuploid cells.207

Proteotoxic stress can have both detrimental and beneficial effects on aneuploid cells.202,208 For instance, protein misfolding can lead to the accumulation of proteotoxic aggregates, eventually resulting in senescence and cell death.202,209 Misfolded proteins also sustain endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, which initiates the unfolded protein response (UPR).210 Prolonged UPR activation produces deleterious effects for cancer cells: loss of mitochondrial integrity, elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, cytochrome C release and cell death induction.211 At the same time, CIN cancer cells can take advantage of retailored protein stoichiometry, since it can promote aberrant protein interactions that confer proliferative benefits.212 For example, amplification of the MET gene in aneuploid tumors confers resistance to epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors, because of its interaction with EGFR downstream effectors and signaling pathway activation.213

Interestingly, CIN cells can overcome proteotoxic stress injuries by modulating lysosomal activity, translation, RNA processing and availability of chaperones.203,214,215 The heat shock response (HSR) is a fundamental mechanism for proteotoxic stress counteraction and proteostasis facilitation.216 Indeed, when cells experience adverse conditions such as nutrient deprivation, hypoxia, heat and oxidative stress, a cohort of HSP chaperones are upregulated to aid proper protein folding or direct misfolded proteins for degradation.215 Accordingly, CIN correlates with lysosomal activity increase, suggesting that the degradation of superabundant proteins serves as the primary mechanism for proteostasis reestablishment.203 Similarly, heat-shock factor 1 (HSF1), the master regulator of chaperone expression and ER stress, is highly expressed in malignant human CIN cancers.217 In addition, cancer cells can manage proteotoxic stress by immunoproteasome formation, as described in RAS, PTEN and mTORC1-mutated tumors.218

Thus, proteotoxic stress adaptation allows aneuploid cells to enhance their proliferation, escape senescence and cell death and favors their resistance to therapeutic regimens.219,220

Energy metabolism rewiring

CIN can generate several metabolic stresses, such as enhanced glycolytic flux, increased mitochondrial activity and boosted ROS production.185 CIN cancer cells, however, can overcome these potentially injuring conditions through metabolic reprogramming, a hallmark of cancer cells, ultimately earning viability benefits from them.221 Primarily, CIN produces alterations in nutrient-related metabolism rewiring glucose, amino acid and lipid utilization.222 For instance, CIN can induce the Warburg effect, a widespread feature of cancer cells consisting of a shift from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis for energy generation.223 Given the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of cancers, this glycolytic rewiring appears instrumental to allow survival of CIN cancer cells in the more hypoxic areas of the tumor mass.224 In addition, the increased glycolytic activity and the high levels of lactate secreted by these cells create a strong immune-evasive microenvironment.225,226

Moreover, CIN modifies the cellular redox state altering mitochondrial number and activity and enhancing ROS production and oxidative stress.227 High ROS levels can trigger harmful feedback loops: ROS promote protein aggregate formation which alters the ER that, in turn, stimulates ROS production.228 In addition, a vicious cycle links ROS to DNA damage: ROS can induce DSBs that promote CIN, which further elevates ROS.229 Both eventualities negatively impact on cell viability, exacerbating CIN and inducing cell death.230 High ROS also impair Cdc25 phosphatase activity, thus affecting mitosis progression and reducing proliferation rates.231 Nevertheless, a moderate increase in ROS production has been reported to favor tumor progression by promoting metastasis dissemination and CIN cells can adapt to excessive oxidative stress by upregulating antioxidant gene expression and by increasing pentose-pathway-dependent NADPH production for reductive biosynthesis.232–234 In addition, glutamate is exported outside the cell in exchange with cysteine to increase glutathione synthesis, making CIN cancer cells highly dependent on glutamine for other metabolic requirements, from Krebs cycle replenishment to amino acid biosynthesis.15

Different CIN states may be associated with distinct metabolic adaptations.235 Cancer cells with high levels of CIN experience increased oxidative, proteotoxic and replication stress, creating a greater demand for metabolic pathways that support redox homeostasis and stress tolerance. Consequently, high CIN cells may exhibit rather enhanced aerobic glycolysis, increased dependence on cysteine uptake and glutathione synthesis, and context-dependent reliance on glutamine metabolism to sustain tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle activity and NADPH production. Low CIN cancer cells, instead, generally experience less metabolic stress and may rely more on mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation for energy production and biosynthesis.235

Stressed CIN cancer cells can release ATP in the TME, which may favor cancer cell motility and invasiveness but has strong immunostimulant effects, increasing recruitment and activation in the TME of macrophages, dendritic cells and cytolytic T lymphocytes and helping cancer immune clearance.236 Nevertheless, ectonucleotidases such as CD39 and CD73, which are expressed by cancer and TME cells, can convert ATP into the immunosuppressive adenosine favoring immunosuppression and immune evasion.237,238

Thus, CIN can advantage tumor cells by providing them with metabolic reprogramming but can also create actionable metabolic vulnerabilities (see also subsection "Metabolic rewiring").

Cell cycle arrest and apoptosis

CIN could translate into events that ultimately antagonize cell division.239 Mis-segregating chromosomes have been reported to induce cell cycle arrest and apoptosis via p53-dependent mechanisms.239,240 Following chromosome segregation errors, DDR activation, ROS generation, histone H3.3 phosphorylation at Ser31, centrosome defects and p38 MAPK pathway initiation can activate p53 thereby restraining cell proliferation.20,241–244 Once activated, p53 relocates to the nucleus and promotes cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1A (CDKN1A) transcription.245 CDKN1A encodes protein p21, which binds both cyclin E/cyclin-dependent kinase (Cdk) 2 and cyclin D/Cdk4, thus stalling cells at the G1/S transition.246 Ultimately, aneuploid cells that undergo cell cycle arrest can be recognized and eliminated by immune surveillance mechanisms.247

Although G1 arrest strictly requires p53-dependent activation of p21, apoptosis can still be triggered in cells deficient in p53 or p21 via p14ARF-mediated mechanisms.248,249 Accordingly, loss of p14ARF worsens the aneuploid phenotype, allowing cells to evade apoptosis and further promoting CIN.250 From this perspective, it is unsurprising that p14ARF is frequently inactivated in human cancers.251

Moreover, recent studies identified a mitotic stopwatch consisting of USP28-53BP-p53-p21 pathway activation that blocks cells in the following G1 phase if they experience prolonged mitosis.252 The stopwatch complexes assemble in a PLK1-dependent manner and persist in daughter cells serving as a memory signal for extended mitosis.253

Notably, a recent study proposed a mechanosensitive nuclear envelope checkpoint that drives a p53-mediated cell cycle arrest.254 Chromosome mis-segregation induces deformation and softening of the nuclear envelope, along with abnormal heterochromatin organization and aberrant lamin assembly. These changes are sensed by mTORC2 and ATR, which activate the p53/p21 pathway, leading to G1 cell cycle arrest. This arrest prevents normal cells with aneuploid genomes from proliferating.254 Nevertheless, aneuploid cells can bypass this checkpoint, for example via p53 loss, and gain invasive traits and enhanced migratory potential due to nuclear softening, thereby further exacerbating CIN.255

Collectively, p53 pathway activation supports euploid karyotype maintenance by inducing cell cycle arrest or apoptosis in aneuploid cells. Thus, p53 inactivation is an emergent candidate to foster CIN tolerance. Indeed, p53-mutant tumors exhibit heterogeneous and erratic chromosome configurations compared with p53 wild type tumors and highly CIN cancers, including colorectal, endometrial and gastric subtypes, frequently display p53 mutations.156,256

On the other hand, recent studies have proposed an unexpected role for the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway in apoptosis induction: upon mitotic aberrations, cGAS activation induces accumulation of phosphorylated interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), which leads to mitochondrial outer-membrane permeabilization and apoptotic cell death.257,258

Micronuclei formation and chromothripsis

In CIN cancer cells, lagging chromosomes or chromosome fragments may segregate separated from the main chromatin mass during anaphase and be individually enwrapped into a nuclear membrane separated from the main nucleus forming MN in daughter cells.258 DNA replication within MN is rather inefficient and micronuclear membranes often display structural irregularities, such as a lack of lamin B and nucleoporin proteins, and are prone to rupture, exposing their DNA content to the cytoplasm.259 These conditions can trigger chromothripsis, inappropriate chromosome replication resulting in DNA fragmentation, shattering or pulverization and inflammatory response.180,260,261 Interestingly, recent studies have shown that micronuclear envelope integrity is highly susceptible to ROS.262,263 In particular, the close proximity of MN to mitochondria can trigger ROS-dependent micronuclear envelope collapse by causing aberrant accumulation of charged multivesicular body protein (CHMP7), a key scaffold of the membrane repair complex called the endosomal sorting complex required for transport III (ESCR III). Oxidative stress impairs CHMP7’s interaction with its canonical ESCR III partners while favoring its abnormal association with the inner nuclear membrane, thereby promoting MN membrane deformation and eventual collapse.262 This effect is further exacerbated by ROS-dependent recruitment and activation of the autophagy-related protein p62 at MN, where it promotes degradation of canonical ESCR III members, ultimately limiting micronuclear envelope repair and favoring micronuclear collapse.263 MN rupture further drives chromothripsis and enhances cGAS-mediated inflammation.262,263 In addition, defective micronuclear envelopes impair the recruitment of some DNA replication, transcription and repair factors, thus contributing to irregular epigenetic modifications, altered DNA propagation and damage accumulation.18,180,264,265 Moreover, chromatids trapped in MN exhibit kinetochore defects, with decreased levels of assembler factors, such as CENP -A, -C and -T, and impaired ability to activate the SAC, promoting further mis-segregating chromosomes in the following mitosis.266

MN can incur different fates with harmful consequences.258 The largest part of MN persists as free cytoplasmic structures in which DNA replicates independently of the main nucleus.259 Micronuclear DNA can reassociate with that of the main nucleus, across subsequent mitosis, form small chromosome fragments or be freed in the cytoplasm as eccDNA that are asymmetrically distributed between daughter cells.267,268 In some instances, MN can be degraded via apoptosis-like or autophagy mechanisms or extruded into the extracellular space.269–271 Moreover, inaccurate replication/repair mechanisms and membrane abnormalities can lead to amplification of oncogenes or loss of tumor suppressor genes as well as altered epigenetic control of shattered chromosomal fragments within MN.265,272 In addition, chromosomal fragments leaking from altered MN membranes, are directly exposed to the cytoplasm and activate the cGAS-STING pathway along with a downstream type I interferon (IFN-I)-dependent response and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP).261 These circuitries activate the innate immune system, stimulating an inflammatory response that can have complex consequences on tumor evolution.273 Activation of the cGAS-STING pathway stimulates TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1), IRF3 and nuclear factor kB (NFkB), thus promoting a strong IFN-I and cytokine-dependent antitumor response.274 Accordingly, aneuploid cells exhibit high secretion of SASP-related cytokines (IFN-I, IL-6/8) that promote both senescence and immune cell recruitment and activation.275 On the other hand, the chronic activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, occurring in high CIN cancer cells with rupture-prone MN, suppresses the interferon response by tachyphylaxis and upregulates a noncanonical NF-kB pathway.276 These events lead to resistance to apoptosis, immune suppression, high expression of EMT genes and metastasis.276 Thus, MN can have an antitumor effect by promoting senescence and by stimulating immunosurveillance in a non-cell-autonomous manner.199,277 However, at the same time, by promoting chronic cGAS-STING pathway activity and noncanonical NFkB pathway activation, MN can stimulate EMT and metastasis.199,276

Senescence

Mis-segregating chromosomes and aneuploidy can induce cellular senescence via pro-inflammatory SASP initiation, either as a consequence of DNA damage-induced p53 activation or through MN-dependent activation of the cGAS-STING pathway.12,261 DDR activation, after CIN-induced DNA damage, mostly contributes to cellular senescence induction by upregulating the p53-p21cip1 axis, which either temporarily prevents cell cycle progression to allow DNA repair or permanently arrests the cell cycle inducing senescence.278 Although senescent cells are irreversibly excluded from mitosis, they remain viable and develop metabolic alterations responsible for both favorable and dangerous outcomes.279 Indeed, the SASP secretome includes proinflammatory soluble factors, such as cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, angiogenic factors, ROS and matrix metalloproteinases.280 The persistence of these signals may promote senescent cell clearance via NK-mediated cytotoxicity in the short term, while contributing to chronic inflammation and cancer progression in the long term.199,275

Moreover, because of lamin B1 loss and nuclear membrane integrity impairment, the release of damaged DNA into the cytoplasm and cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs) are often found in senescent cells.281 CCFs lead to cGAS-STING-NFkB pathway activation, which induces SASP expression.277 SASP-induced inflammation may trigger reactive oxygen and nitrogen species (RONS) generation in nearby cells, further causing DNA damage, mitotic spindle alterations and CIN.282 In addition, CIN-induced senescence can enhance the migration and invasion capabilities of peripheral tumor cells, per se or via the SASP secretome.283 Moreover, the SASP influences immune and mesenchymal cells within the TME, thereby supporting the maintenance of persistent senescence.284,285

Thus, cellular senescence resulting from CIN may have both suppressive and promoting effects on tumor evolution.

Inflammation and immune modulation

CIN can promote inflammation and modulate antitumor immune responses.261,286 The high frequency of CIN in human cancers suggests that tumor cells not only develop tolerance toward chromosome segregation errors but also achieve the capability to evade immune recognition.287 Nevertheless, the immunological consequences of CIN are highly context dependent.288

At low to moderate levels, CIN can generate tumor cell diversity and neoantigen heterogeneity that may enhance immune recognition and support immune-mediated tumor clearance.289,290 Consistent with this, injection of highly aneuploid cancer cells results in efficient tumor formation in immunodeficient mice but delayed or reduced tumor growth in immunocompetent hosts, suggesting CIN-dependent immunoselection.291

Mechanistically, CIN-associated cGAS-STING signaling can lead to NF-kB activation and IFN-I-dependent inflammatory programs that can promote senescence and immune responses.12,261,276,292 In parallel, cGAMP can function as an extracellular immunotransmitter, propagating STING activation in TME-residing cells and amplifying IFN-I-dependent antitumor immunity.292,293

However, sustained interferon signaling can also induce adaptive immune resistance programs, including PD-L1 upregulation, thereby limiting T-cell function within the TME.276 In addition, senescence-associated signaling can contribute to the establishment of an immunosuppressive milieu characterized by the recruitment of regulatory immune programs, including Tregs and myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), thereby facilitating immune evasion and tumor progression.294

At high levels of CIN, chronic activation of the cGAS-STING axis promotes a functional rewiring of downstream signaling outputs. This includes attenuation of IFN-I responses and a shift toward protumorigenic inflammatory signals such as non-canonical NF-kB- and IL6-dependent pathways, resistance to apoptosis, EMT, metastatic capacity and immune evasion programs.199,276,295 Although high CIN initially promotes macrophage-mediated antitumor immunity, including a prophagocytic shift and antibody-mediated immune response, chronic IL-6/STAT3-driven tumor microenvironmental remodeling ultimately sustains a heterogeneous tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) population and promotes macrophage plasticity toward a M2-like immunosuppressive state.4,199,296–298 Therefore, high CIN tumors frequently display features of immune escape that also include PD-L1 upregulation and impaired antigen presentation through downregulation of antigen-presenting MHC class 1 molecules.276,299–301 Additionally, the overexpression of CD47 and/or CSF-1 observed in high CIN tumors may promote macrophage M2-like polarization, thereby favoring immune evasion.302–305

Finally, extracellular cGAMP can be metabolized by ectonucleotide pyrophosphatase/phosphodiesterase 1 (ENPP1) into AMP, which can be further converted into adenosine by CD73, thereby contributing to an immunosuppressive and prometastatic microenvironment.306

Collectively, these findings indicate that CIN can elicit disparate immune outcomes, from immune activation and tumor clearance to immune evasion and tumor progression, which are highly context dependent.

Metastatic capability

Metastasis is a hallmark of advanced cancer and a negative predictor of clinical outcomes, strongly affecting morbidity and mortality.221,307 Metastatic lesions commonly harbor the primary tumor pathogenic driver signatures but develop additional genetic modifications responsible for the acquisition of growth advantages, expansion of heterogeneity and enhanced adaptability.308,309 By promoting cellular plasticity, CIN can contribute to metastasis initiation in a context-dependent manner.310–312 In this framework, CIN has also been associated with loss of the zinc finger E-box-binding homeobox 1 (ZEB1) gene, which promotes MET and extravasation, thereby fostering distant metastasis.194 Moreover, CIN has been recently found to trigger metastasis in murine models by inducing a cytosolic DNA response as a consequence of micronuclear DNA exposure to the cytoplasm, which leads to the noncanonical NF-kB signaling activation downstream of cGAS-STING pathway stimulation.276 cGAS activation can also promote metastatic progression by leading to the extracellular accumulation of prometastatic metabolites such as adenosine, which also exert immunosuppressive effects (see subsection “Inflammation and immune modulation”).306 Importantly, CIN suppression significantly restrains metastasis occurrence, thus indicating a causal relationship between CIN and the metastatic process.276

In addition, as previously mentioned in subsection “Epigenetic perturbations”, overactivated EZH2 histone methyltransferase appears to be a crucial prometastatic driver especially for chromosomally unstable TNBC.162 Moreover, CIN can also indirectly impact on metastasis progression by disrupting metabolic pathways and modulating small GTPase-associated signaling networks, thereby promoting invasiveness.313,314 Nevertheless, CIN may also produce antimetastatic effects, since the presence of an extra copy number of chromosomes 13 or 18 has been reported to halt metastasis emergence in colon cancer models.315

Thus, the potential impact of CIN on the metastatic processes needs further clarification.

Resistance to therapy

CIN equips tumor cells with extensive and constantly increasing genomic heterogeneity that has a key role in dampening therapeutic efficacy.310 CIN-induced ITH serves as a survival strategy, known as cellular bet-hedging, a phenotypic plasticity useful to overcome hostile environments, including those imposed by antitumor therapies.316 Interestingly, CIN tumors mostly exhibit chemotherapy resistance and high aggressiveness.317,318 Indeed, genomic modifications originating from CIN can affect drug uptake and metabolism or alter molecular pathways required for drug efficacy and also drive multidrug resistance, as in the case of upregulation of ATP binding cassette subfamily G member 2 (ABCG2/BCRP) drug efflux pump in topotecan-resistant NSCLC cells.319 Because of its inherent plasticity and its large-scale genomic remodeling and epigenetic adaptability, CIN also mainly contributes resistance to oncogene-targeted therapy by upregulating alternative oncogenic pathways, for instance in the case of MET in EGFR-targeted therapy in lung and colon cancers, MAPK in BRAF inhibitor-resistant melanoma and AKT or PTEN loss in breast cancers resistant to HER2-targeted therapy.320 By upregulating antioxidant mechanisms (see also subsection “Energy metabolism rewiring”), CIN confers resistance to oxidative stress-targeted therapy (including radiation, some DNA-targeting agents or mitotic spindle-targeting agents such as Taxol), while by upregulating DDR genes and antiapoptotic pathways, it confers resistance to DNA damage-targeted therapies.185,310,316,319 CIN can also promote proteotoxic stress-based therapy resistance by activating the UPR, increasing autophagy, upregulating or mutating proteasome subunits.321

Most chemotherapeutic strategies exert their anti-neoplastic effects by promoting high CIN levels, exploiting the limited extent of further chromosomal alterations bearable by cancer cells.322 Importantly, even CIN tumor cells must limit excessive chromosomal alterations to preserve their fitness and viability.323–326 Nevertheless, ITH triggered by CIN can complicate treatment responses because of cell-to-cell genotypic varieties and lead to inconsistencies in drug efficacy.318 Indeed, tumors harboring cell subclonal populations with a diverse range of CIN may exhibit a significant compliance with a specific treatment and simultaneously show residual expansion of resistant cells, accountable for tumor recurrence.317 In particular, CIN-inducing drugs can strongly compromise the viability of cells with high CIN levels, by rapidly promoting cell death, while only marginally impairing that of low CIN tumor cells.15,322 Accordingly, the inherent CIN size of human tumors correlates with poor responses to anticancer therapies.327 In this context, it is worth mentioning that cGAS-STING pathway activation in CIN cancer cells has been shown to confer resistance to genotoxic treatments in a cancer cell autonomous manner, perhaps by increasing DNA damage tolerance and apoptosis resistance, although how this is achieved is unclear.328 In addition, cGAS has also been shown to promote autophagy of MN, therefore limiting, somehow, excessive CIN.326,329 Moreover, because of ITH and plasticity and the immunoediting capabilities, as described in subsection “Inflammation and immune modulation”, high CIN cancers show poor or inconsistent responses to immunotherapy. Indeed, high CIN/aneuploidy is considered a predictive biomarker for immune checkpoint blockade-based therapies.301,330

Thus, CIN-positive tumors may rapidly evolve and develop resistance to previously effective drugs by generating genetically divergent cells from the primary neoplasm.316,317,327 Nevertheless, it is increasingly evident that additional mechanisms contribute to therapy resistance in CIN cancers, many of which remain incompletely understood.

Mechanisms of cancer cell adaptation to CIN

The mechanisms embraced by cancer cells to mitigate the CIN-dependent detrimental effects of karyotypic alterations and to preserve viability are still poorly understood. Nevertheless, the inherent genomic plasticity of CIN cancer cells appears to provide mechanisms to fine-tune genetic instability to levels sufficient to fuel adaptation yet restrained to preserve viability. SAC modulation

The SAC is a leading mitotic mechanism in attenuating CIN because of its inhibitory control of the APC/C complex until all K-MT interactions have been properly fixed.35,57 Although SAC components are documented to be essential for cell viability, it has been recently unveiled that cancer cells can overcome MAD2 homozygous deletion or Mps1 chemical inhibition when the APC/C activity is simultaneously reduced.334,335 Furthermore, previous studies have shown that a decline in APC/C activity elongates mitosis in order to allow appropriate chromosome segregation.336 Thus, tumor cells can limit excessive CIN by downregulating APC/C activity and recent findings showed that truncating mutations in the CDC27 (ANAPC3) APC/C subunit delayed mitotic progression and decreased CIN, allowing cancer cells to optimize fitness.337

In addition, cancer cells with supernumerary centrosomes often cluster the extra centrosomes to form pseudobipolar spindles.28,89 Although these transient abnormal spindles can favor erroneous K-MT attachments such as merotelic attachments and lead to lagging chromosomes in anaphase, this mechanism mitigates the catastrophic consequences of a multipolar anaphase onset.28,89,90 Interestingly, the acquisition of whole genome doubling (WGD) in CRC cells has been shown to increase tolerance to CIN, albeit the molecular mechanisms underlying this phenomenon remain to be clarified.338 Nevertheless, the mitotic kinesin Kif18A has recently emerged as a necessary element for WGD+ cell viability, since its loss severely affects the growth of tetraploid but not diploid cells.339 Consistently, WGD+ tumors commonly exhibit Kif18A overexpression, a signature that correlates with worse prognosis.339 Similarly, also CIN cancer cells also display a profound dependence on Kif18A to proliferate.340 Thus, Kif18A represents a specific therapeutic target for WGD+ and CIN cancers.339–341

DNA damage and replication stress tolerance

Adaptation to DNA damage/replication stress is certainly helped in CIN cancer cells by inactivation or bypassing of the p53 pathway, a barrier for genomic instability.331,342 Nevertheless, while p53 loss-of-function is not sufficient to induce CIN, some aneuploidies propagate in p53-proficient cells and p53 inactivation is not always observed in CIN tumors, suggesting that alternative pathways might be involved in this adaptation.240,247,343,344 Indeed, aneuploid cells can overcome increased DNA damage via the RAF/MEK/ERK pathway regardless of the p53 status, while the survival of aneuploid CRC cells is promoted by loss-of-function mutations in BCL9L that suppress caspase-2-mediated apoptosis in response to chromosome segregation errors.345,346 An alternative mechanism enabling wild-type p53 tumor cells to tolerate CIN is the overexpression of the p53 degrader Mdm2.347,348 Upstream of Mdm2, p14ARF serves a critical checkpoint regulator: it binds and inhibits Mdm2, stabilizing p53 in response to oncogenic stress.349 Consequently, inactivating alterations of p14ARF are common in tumors with intact p53 and drive malignant progression.350

Furthermore, proteogenomic analyses have recently revealed that adapted aneuploid human cells can reduce replication stress and limit genomic instability through transcriptional reprogramming of DNA replication and repair factors, highlighting the central role of DDR modulation in CIN tolerance.215 Specifically, aneuploid cells exhibited increased expression of replication and repair factors, notably FOXM1 and E2F4, alongside downregulation of lysosomal degradation pathways, suggesting that modulation of protein turnover is a key component of the adaptive program.351 These adaptations mirror patterns observed in aneuploid tumors and are associated with recurrent copy number alterations linked to poor clinical outcomes.351 Moreover, cancer cells can endure intermediate lesions by enhancing and rewiring DDR pathways. Specifically, modulation of the ATR/CHK1 axis allows MYC-driven tumors to tolerate replication stress, limiting DNA damage accumulation while preserving proliferation.352

Furthermore, CIN cancer cells may tolerate replication stress by increasing usage of dormant origin firing and mitotic DNA synthesis (MiDAS), limiting further genome instability.183 Notably, upregulation of translesion synthesis (TLS) and TLS polymerases, a low-fidelity mechanism that facilitates DNA polymerization across DNA lesions or stalled polymerases, is a common way that cancer cells adopt to resist DNA damage/replication stress.353,354 Given their low fidelity, TLS polymerases further increase genomic instability. Nevertheless, they represent dependencies and vulnerabilities of CIN cancer cells, therefore TLS polymerases, such as POL η, POL ι, POL κ, REV1, POL ζ and REV1, are potential drug targets about which there is considerable ongoing pharmacologic research.353,354

Proteotoxic burden compensation

Cancer cells can constrain CIN below a critical threshold by leveraging proteostasis control. To survive the deleterious accumulation of misfolded, unassembled and aggregation-prone proteins cancer cells often increase chaperone production, enhance ubiquitin-proteasome activity as well as autophagic and lysosomal degradation pathways and activate the UPR.203,210,214–216 Across diverse tumor types, especially TNBC, multiple myeloma (MM), NSCLC and CRC, there is accumulating evidence that high CIN correlates with activation of proteostasis pathways (HSR, proteasome activity, UPR), reflecting a scalable dependence on quality control systems to mitigate proteotoxic stress and adapt to CIN.185,333,355,356

Increased RNA turnover, in addition to protein turnover, has also been shown to mitigate proteostasis imbalance in aneuploid cells.184,214 Moreover, CIN tumor cells exploit chronic UPR signaling to resist apoptosis and adapt to other stressors such as hypoxia and nutrient deprivation, in addition to tolerating ongoing proteome imbalance.357 Notably, prolonged PERK signaling can promote redox balance and metabolic rewiring, while IRE1α-XBP1 activity has been linked to protumor immune modulation and therapy resistance.358–360

These dependencies are both biological hallmarks of aggressive cancers and therapeutic vulnerabilities that are amenable to exploitation with proteasome inhibitors, HSP inhibitors, UPR modulators and combination strategies targeting multiple arms of the proteostatis network.356

Metabolic rewiring

Metabolic rewiring appears necessary to adapt to the high energy requirements and oxidative stress conditions imposed by CIN. Indeed, cancer cells can adapt to intrinsic and therapy-induced metabolic stress through dynamic reprogramming of signaling networks and transcriptional programs that sustain energy production, biosynthesis and redox balance and affect the TME.361 Central mediators of this adaptive response include AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), the mTOR/PI3K pathway, HIF-1α, MYC and stress-responsive transcription factors such as ATF4, XBP1/IRE1α and NRF2, which collectively rewire metabolic circuitry to promote glycolysis, glutamine dependency, antioxidant capacity and mitochondrial function.362 Therapeutically, targeting these signaling hubs has emerged as a promising strategy to exploit metabolic vulnerabilities.362

Importantly, these metabolic adaptations extend beyond tumor cell-intrinsic survival mechanisms and exert profound effects on the TME architecture and function. Enhanced glycolytic activity and lactate production can promote local immunosuppression, while altered amino acid and lipid metabolism may impair antitumor immune responses and support the recruitment or maintenance of suppressive immune populations225,226,363

Immune signaling modulation

Adaptation to CIN frequently involves the modulation of innate immune signaling pathways. Rather than sustaining tumor-suppressive immune surveillance, chronic CIN-induced activation of the cGAS-STING axis and downstream programs is co-opted to support tumor cell survival, immune evasion and metastatic progression (see also subsection “Inflammation and immune modulation”).199,276,295 In addition, cGAS can exert homeostatic functions by limiting excessive CIN through MN autophagy.329 Consequently, CIN-positive tumors can tolerate ongoing genomic instability while gaining evolutionary fitness (see also section “Exploring the CIN paradox”).

Thus, CIN tumors also rely on non-cell-autonomous processes including immunoediting and modulation of the TME to foster a permissive niche for tumor persistence and genomic instability tolerance.301

Collectively, integrated CIN-inflammation-TME signatures define a spectrum of features that may be exploited for therapeutic stratification of CIN tumors. These features include cGAS-STING signaling states, NF-κB/IFN-I and STAT1/STAT3 activity, chemokine profiles (including CXCL10 and CCL5), alongside alterations in antigen presentation, immune-suppressive cell infiltration, TAM signature and checkpoint molecule expression.199,261,276,295,299–301,303

The dynamic role of CIN in tumor evolution

CIN is a pervasive feature of human cancers and a major driver of tumor evolutionary dynamics. By continuously generating new karyotypic configurations, CIN fuels ITH and expands the pool of genetic variants upon which somatic selection can act.4,193 Evidence from multiregion sequencing studies has revealed widespread subclonal somatic copy-number alterations across diverse tumor types, demonstrating that chromosomal changes remain dynamic throughout tumor evolution, from early clonal diversification to metastatic dissemination.193,364

It is possible that comparable evolutionary dynamics occur in other biological systems. In bacteria, stressful conditions such as antibiotic exposure can select for strains with elevated mutation rates, increasing the likelihood of generating advantageous variants and larger genome rearrangements provide additional adaptive flexibility.365,366 Viruses similarly exploit high mutation rates to generate genetic diversity, enabling rapid adaptation to antiviral treatments or host immune pressures, yet excessive mutation can lead to population collapse.367 Even normal tissues display evolutionary behavior: in the human epidermis, somatic mutations in genes such as TP53 and NOTCH1 can provide selective advantages under environmental stresses, including UV radiation and oxidative damage, leading to clonal expansion without necessarily progressing to malignancy.368

Nevertheless, cancers characterized by CIN exhibit more complex evolutionary dynamics due to the presence of large-scale genomic rearrangements that generate extensive, simultaneous and rapid genetic variation. Indeed, tumors have traditionally been considered Darwinian systems as originally proposed in Nowell’s model of clonal evolution and evolve through the gradual accumulation of mutations, with natural selection favoring clones that have a growth advantage.369,370 Nevertheless, the Darwinian view of tumor evolution, which emphasizes clonal expansion as a linear and stepwise process, fails to fully capture the diversity of tumor evolution dynamics, especially for CIN cancers.371 For instance, tumors often exhibit a branched growth pattern, comprising genetically distinct subclones that expand simultaneously under selective pressure, with variable fitness.372 Furthermore, in some tumors, particularly CRC cancers, multiple subclones with distinct chromosomal alterations coexist and expand through a random drift rather than strong selection, consistent with a neutral evolutionary model.373 Interestingly, emerging evidence indicates that tumor genomes often evolve through a “punctuated equilibrium” pattern, where short bursts of CIN generating extensive copy-number variations or rearrangements are interspersed with periods of low CIN or relative clonal stability.374 Such CIN-related bursts can create extensive copy-number alterations or chromosomal rearrangements even in a single step, rapidly reshaping the tumor genome.375 Across diverse cancer types, genomic analyses indicate that many somatic copy-number alterations and structural variants emerge simultaneously in bursts, rather than accumulating progressively over time.376

These findings suggest that tumor evolution involves a combination of gradual selection, branched evolution, neutral accumulation of genomic alterations and episodic genomic upheaval. CIN contributes to this process by generating continuous karyotypic diversity which fuels both clonal selection and stochastic diversification within tumor cell populations. In this way, CIN acts as an evolutionary toolkit enabling tumors to explore adaptive landscapes and respond to diverse pressures including microenvironmental constraints, immune surveillance and therapy, thereby enhancing their overall adaptive potential.199,327

Exploring The CIN paradox

Deviations from the ordinary genotypes typically antagonize cellular fitness. Indeed, as early as at the beginning of the last century, T. Boveri documented that normal cells are overwhelmed by chromosomal abnormalities and, as a consequence, undergo cell death.377 Nevertheless, CIN is not invariably detrimental and may confer adaptive advantages under specific circumstances.378

It has been observed that CIN grants growth benefits to aneuploid cells compared with euploid cells under stressful culture conditions but hinders proliferation under standard conditions.379 In particular, it has been shown that aneuploid CRC cells had growth benefits over euploid cells under certain stressful conditions such as serum starvation, hypoxia and drug treatment but not standard conditions.380

Importantly, the quantitative impact of CIN on tumorigenesis appears to be nonlinear. Intermediate levels of CIN seem to optimally balance genetic diversification with cellular viability, thereby facilitating clonal adaptation and tumor progression. In contrast, excessive rates of CIN impose severe fitness costs by triggering mitotic catastrophe, proteotoxic stress and metabolic burden, ultimately leading to cell death or senescence.381 Accordingly, it has been suggested that CIN might participate in cancer development only if it remains below a tolerable threshold (Fig. 6).378 For instance, elevated extent of chromosome mis-segregations (>or = 5 chromosomes per division) dramatically increases the rate of tumor cell death and reduces tumorigenic potential.14,324Fig. 6The CIN paradox in tumor biology. Nevertheless, cellular tolerance and tissue type critically modulate CIN outcomes. Inactivation of key tumor suppressors, particularly TP53, removes checkpoint barriers that would normally eliminate cells with catastrophic genome imbalance, thereby allowing tolerance of higher levels of CIN and facilitating more aggressive clonal expansion.342 Furthermore, recent evidence from mouse models indicates that specific levels of CIN may produce contrasting effects across different tissues. Notably, high levels of CIN do not universally suppress tumor formation and can further enhance tumorigenesis in certain tissues such as colon.382

The evolutionary consequences of CIN also depend on the nature of chromosomal alterations and their persistence over time. N-CIN produces broad gene-dosage changes that gradually reshape cellular fitness landscapes, whereas S-CIN can generate strong oncogenic driver changes in a single evolutionary event.375,383 Temporal dynamics further diversify these effects: transient bursts of chromosome mis-segregation may initiate tumorigenesis by producing advantageous karyotypes, while chronic CIN maintains ITH and enables ongoing adaptation to selective pressures.276,327,384

In addition to its cell-intrinsic effects, CIN exerts profound non-cell-autonomous influences through the TME (as also subsection “Inflammation and immune modulation”).301 Broadly, low-to-moderate CIN may enhance tumor visibility to the immune system by increasing neoantigen generation and activating the cGAS-STING pathway downstream of cytosolic DNA exposure, inducing IFN-I responses.276,289 On the contrary, high CIN, although more deleterious from a cell autonomous point of view, can promote tumor immune evasion by the chronic activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, which can also foster a prometastatic TME.199,276 Moreover, high CIN can facilitate immune escape through loss of heterozygosity (LOH) at HLA class I loci, impairing antigen presentation to CD8+ T cells, and through the emergence of tumor clones with elevated PD-L1 expression.276,299–301

Collectively, these observations outline the CIN paradox and support the “just-right” hypothesis, which posits that tumor cells derive maximal evolutionary benefit from intermediate levels of genomic instability.385 Low CIN promotes tumor initiation and progression by generating genetic diversity, facilitating the acquisition of oncogenic traits and enabling adaptation to adverse conditions, although it can stimulate immune surveillance. In contrast, excessive genomic instability, while creating a cancer permissive microenvironment, can lead to genetic catastrophe, thus limiting cancer cell viability (Fig. 6).4,196,324 Thus, the “just-right” level of CIN in tumors may not represent a fixed state but rather a dynamic population-level equilibrium. This equilibrium emerges from ongoing fluctuations in genomic instability across heterogeneous CIN cancer cell populations and is shaped by the interplay of branched evolution, neutral dynamics and punctuated equilibrium mechanisms (see also section “The dynamic role of CIN in tumor evolution”).4,372–374 Together, these processes continuously generate and filter genomic diversity, constraining CIN within a range that maximizes tumor adaptability while avoiding the detrimental effects of excessive instability. Clinical studies support this model, showing that tumors harboring intermediate levels of CIN exhibit more aggressive behavior and poorer prognosis than those with either low or excessive CIN rates.196,386,387 Nevertheless, in accordance with Muller’s ratchet principle, the accumulation of chromosomal alterations during tumor progression might gradually increase the burden of genomic instability and once this burden exceeds tolerable thresholds cellular fitness collapses (Fig. 6).388 This vulnerability is increasingly being explored therapeutically: strategies aimed at exacerbating CIN may push tumor cells beyond their tolerable instability threshold, selectively inducing cancer cell death.14,389

Challenges in CIN Assessment, from basic research to the clinical translation

Increasing our CIN detection capabilities holds considerable promise for both research and clinical practice purposes. However, as a dynamic process rather than a static state, CIN is difficult to diagnose with precision and its assessment is constrained by technical and interpretative limitations that must be accounted for. Conventional methodologies rely on indirect, population-level proxies rather than measures of ongoing CIN. These include fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH)-based chromosome enumeration, histological scoring of mitotic defects and gene expression-based CIN signatures. FISH interrogates only a limited subset of chromosomes and may be biased by chromosome-specific alterations, whereas mitotic analyses are restricted to highly proliferative tumors with sufficient mitotic figures.390,391 Similarly, gene expression-based CIN scores lack specificity and are strongly influenced by proliferation and steady genomic alterations.392 In parallel, bulk methodologies such as comparative genomic hybridization (CGH), single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) arrays, PCR-based assays, flow cytometry and bulk NGS rely on population-level measurements that average signals across large numbers of cells. While well suited for the detection of copy number alterations, aneuploidy and ploidy shifts, they do not capture the dynamic, single-cell nature of CIN.393–395 Consequently, they may conflate stable genomic states with ongoing CIN and likely underestimate its true extent.393 Collectively, these methods provide indirect, nonstandardized proxies of CIN, highlighting the need for robust quantitative frameworks based on single-cell, spatial and longitudinal analyses.

Emerging technologies for probing chromosome dynamics are reshaping the quantification of CIN, enabling direct, real-time measurements at single-cell resolution.393,394

For research purposes several new approaches are emerging to better characterize CIN. These include live-cell imaging platforms combined with molecular tracers (e.g., fluorescent histone tags or kinetochore markers) and tools such as CRISPR-based locus labeling, that allow direct and dynamic visualization of mitotic errors such as lagging chromosomes, anaphase bridges, multipolar divisions and MN formation.396–398 These approaches enable the quantitative definition of CIN as a rate-based process, measured as the frequency of chromosome mis-segregation events per cell division, thereby capturing its inherently dynamic nature. In addition, extending beyond linear genomic information, 3D genome mapping technologies such as Hi-C can map genome-wide chromatin interactions and reveal how CIN is associated with higher-order genome organization, including altered chromatin compartments, looping interactions, structural genome reorganization and aberrant nuclear architecture.399 Importantly, single-cell Hi-C further refines this approach by resolving chromatin architecture at the level of individual cells, exposing cell-to-cell variability in 3D genome folding and linking structural genome heterogeneity directly to CIN-driven karyotypic diversity.400

In parallel, in a clinically applicable context, single-cell omics have emerged as a central pillar for quantitative CIN analysis. Single-cell DNA sequencing (ssDNA-seq) enables genome-wide profiling of copy number alterations and aneuploidy at individual cell resolution, revealing ITH and reconstructing clonal evolutionary trajectories driven by ongoing CIN.401 Complementary single-cell RNA sequencing (ssRNA-seq) and multiomics approaches further connect genomic alterations to transcriptional and epigenetic states, providing insights into the functional consequences of CIN at the cellular level.402–404

Micronucleus assays provide a complementary functional readout of CIN capturing chromosome mis-segregation events and structural chromosome abnormalities at single-cell resolution, thereby linking genomic instability to cellular phenotypes and downstream signaling responses such as DNA damage and inflammatory pathways.405

In addition, spatial multiomics approaches combine spatial information with genomic inference to map the spatial organization of tumor clones and reveal how distinct cell populations are distributed and shaped by the TME and its features. These methodologies also enable the characterization of abundance and functional states of tumor-associated immune populations, including lymphocytes, macrophages and dendritic cells.406,407 When integrated with longitudinal multiomics capturing temporal changes in genomic, transcriptomic, epigenomic and proteomic states across disease progression or treatment, these approaches enable spatiotemporal reconstruction of tumor evolution providing a deeper insight into how clonal dynamics, cellular plasticity and niche interactions coordinate tumor adaptation and therapeutic response.402

AI-driven integration of multiomics data also holds strong promise for translating complex cancer molecular profiles into clinically actionable insights, thereby improving patient stratification and enabling more accurate prediction of therapeutic response.408

Therefore, a more precise characterization of CIN will enable the accurate identification of targetable and cotargetable vulnerabilities and dependencies on a tumor-specific basis. This will also support the development of strategies to selectively target CIN cancer cells while minimizing toxicity for normal cells. By optimizing dosing schedules and combinations, it will be possible to modulate pathway activities to levels that are insufficient to harm normal cells but sufficient to induce crisis in CIN cancer cells, which are particularly dependent on these pathways for their survival.

Collectively, these methodologies offer a framework for capturing CIN across a large spectrum. Despite this progress, clinical translation remains limited by methodological complexity, interpretability challenges, and the absence of standardized, clinically actionable CIN thresholds. Nevertheless, these emerging CIN profiling technologies hold strong potential for near-future clinical implementation by enabling assessment of CIN across quantitative, qualitative and spatiotemporal dimensions to ultimately refine patient stratification and guide personalized strategies in precision oncology.

CIN-targeted therapeutic strategies in cancer

Enhancing the rate of CIN over a critical threshold is a feasible strategy to kill CIN tumor cells, as discussed in section “Exploring the CIN paradox”.14,389 From this perspective, traditional anticancer drugs, both single-agent and combination therapies, also induce genome destabilization to promote cancer cell death or senescence and stimulate immune clearance.409–411 These treatments target cellular structures such as MTs and DNA as well as functional pathways such as the DNA damage response, exacerbating CIN and exerting therapeutic effects even in CIN cancer patients.409–411 Their efficacy is, however, limited by general toxicity issues (gastrointestinal, hematological and neurological) and by mechanisms of resistance that CIN cancer cells can implement by virtue of their genomic plasticity.412,413

Nevertheless, while promoting tumor evolution, CIN also creates dependencies and vulnerabilities that are increasingly being identified and may be therapeutically exploitable (see also section “Double-edged CIN outcomes”).389 Therefore, numerous targets such as controllers of genomic stability and requirements for CIN tolerance as well as modulators of the TME have been successfully identified to amplify CIN-induced toxic effects to unbearable levels. Among these, crucial targets are cell cycle checkpoint regulators, epigenetic remodelers, proteostasis controllers, metabolism rewiring mechanisms, hypoxic, oxidative and DNA replication/damage stress players and immune signaling modulators.295,356,414–417

Agents in clinical trials targeting CIN cancers

The proteins involved in SAC coordination represent novel and valuable therapeutic targets, given their role in ensuring accurate chromosome segregation. SAC inhibitors (SACi) enhance APC/CCdc20 activation and induce premature exit from mitosis, exacerbating CIN and driving the accumulation of highly abnormal karyotypes.422 Notably, SAC inhibition has been recently shown to be more detrimental to aneuploid than to diploid cells.423 Mps1, a key SAC kinase, has emerged as a crucial target, with several inhibitors currently in clinical development showing promising results, particularly in combination with low-dose docetaxel (Fig. 7; see Table 1).424 However, bone marrow toxicity remains a major limitation.425 Importantly, recent evidence indicates that sensitivity to Mps1 inhibition relies on APC/CCdc20 status: both APC/C dysfunction and Cdc20 loss confer resistance to Mps1 inhibitors, while high Cdc20 expression enhances their responsiveness, supporting its potential as a predictive biomarker.82,335,337,426

Spindle-associated proteins have emerged as a novel class of potential therapeutic targets, given their role in chromosome alignment at the metaphase plate and correct bipolar spindle assembly.414 Small molecules inhibiting kinesin spindle proteins (KSPi), such as kinesin motor protein Eg5 (also known as KIF11 or kinesin-5) and centromere-associated protein E (CENP-E or kinesin-7), have been developed but they have demonstrated limited effectiveness in clinical applications because of drug toxicity and resistance (Fig. 7; see Table 1).427,428 Further investigation is needed to address these challenges and optimize the clinical use of these inhibitors. It is worth mentioning the recent advances in research on Kif18A (or kinesin-8), a K-MT plus end-directed kinesin that suppresses MT dynamics to dampen chromosome oscillations and promote their proper alignment at the spindle equator during metaphase.429,430 Furthermore, Kif18A activity is also involved in SAC silencing, therefore, targeting Kif18A also represents an indirect strategy to interfere with SAC and lead to mitotic catastrophe.431,432 Importantly, Kif18A appears to be dispensable for healthy cell division but essential for cancer cell survival, serving as a vulnerability target in cells with aberrant karyotypes, including those exhibiting CIN and WGD.339–341,433 A range of small molecules targeting Kif18A have been developed and tested in preclinical studies, exhibiting scant toxicity and significant tumor-suppressive activity.434–436 In addition, clinical trials are ongoing and viewed with great optimism as they may provide selective approaches for the treatment of CIN tumors with significant interest in their outcomes (Fig. 7; see Table 1).

Cell cycle regulation

A substantial class of therapeutic targets comprises kinases that regulate cell cycle progression and checkpoints, including Wee1, CHK1/2, AURK A/B and PLK1.410

Wee1 is a key G2/M checkpoint kinase that prevents mitotic entry in response to DNA damage, allowing time for repair.437 Wee1 inhibition may lead to Cdk2 hyperactivation, impairing proper DNA replication and inducing the accumulation of DSBs.438 Likewise, Wee1 inhibition may induce Cdk1 overactivation, which forces cells to enter mitosis, bypassing the G2/M checkpoint and ultimately culminating in mitotic catastrophe and cell death.439,440 Furthermore, Wee1 inhibition has been proposed to perturb mitotic progression, thus delaying mitotic exit.441,442 Wee1 inhibitors (Wee1i) have shown synthetic lethality in the presence of TP53 mutations and can enhance sensitivity to DNA-damaging drugs (Fig. 7; see Table 1).443–445 Unfortunately, the occurrence of hematologic toxicity and emerging resistance mechanisms, including adaptive activation of compensatory checkpoint signaling, restoration of Cdk control and enhanced tolerance to replication stress and DNA damage remain the primary challenge in clinical practice.446

CHK1 and 2 are DNA damage checkpoint proteins regulating p53, Wee1 and Cdc25 activity to halt cell cycle progression and allow DNA repair.447 CHK1/2 inhibitors (CHKi) aim to exacerbate replication stress, DSBs and chromosomal fragmentation, ultimately inducing cell death.448 CHKi have been assessed in combination with other anticancer agents, notably Wee1i and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) (Fig. 7; see Table 1).449,450 Despite strong preclinical rationale, clinical outcomes have been disappointing, largely due to dose-limiting toxicity and resistance mechanisms, including compensatory activation of alternative checkpoint pathways, loss or downregulation of the CHK1 protein and enhanced DNA damage repair capacity.451

AURK A/B have a crucial role in regulating K-MT attachments and their inhibition disrupts mitotic spindle function and error correction, prompting asymmetrical chromosome segregation and leading to gross CIN and cell death.452 AURK A/B inhibitors (AURKi) have shown robust CIN induction in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 7; see Table 1).453 Nevertheless, their clinical efficacy remains limited by dose-limiting toxicities, namely neutropenia.454 In parallel, AURKi efficacy is limited by acquired resistance mechanisms such as kinase domain mutations, activation of compensatory mitotic regulators and upregulation of prosurvival signaling pathways.455

PLK1 has a pleiotropic role in numerous cellular pathways with well-established roles in cancer development and a range of PLK1 inhibitors (PLK1i) have been produced and extensively studied.456,457 However, despite the promising preclinical results, PLK1i have shown limited success in clinical trials because of reduced efficacy and emergence of resistance (Fig. 7; see Table 1).457 Beyond tumor-intrinsic mechanisms, emerging evidence suggests that resistance to PLK1i involves adaptive immune modulation.458 Although acute PLK1 inhibition enhances tumor immunogenicity via cGAS-STING-mediated IFN-I signaling, prolonged exposure may promote immune evasion through upregulation of immune checkpoints, impaired antigen presentation and reduced cytotoxic T-cell activity. Collectively, these adaptive immune changes may support combination strategies with ICIs.458

DNA transcription and damage control

Histone deacetylases (HDACs) play a key role in gene expression regulation by functioning as transcriptional repressors and are often dysregulated in cancers.415 HDAC inhibitors (HDACi) induce genomic instability via multiple mechanisms: histone hyperacetylation may heavily modify chromatin structure affecting DNA repair gene expression, leading to improper DNA damage responses and to chromosome mis-segregation by disrupting the pericentric heterochromatin conformation.459 Although a variety of HDACi have been approved by the FDA for the treatment of hematological cancers such as T-cell lymphomas and MM, their efficacy as a single agent against solid tumors has proven to be limited (Fig. 7; see Table 1).460

In the context of epigenetic modulation, it is worth mentioning the therapeutic rationale of suppressing CIN to inhibit its metastatic potential through specific EZH2 inhibitors (HMTi), which are currently under clinical evaluation (see Table 1).162,171

ATM kinase is a key mediator of DNA damage repair, modulating the response to DSBs.461 Since mutations in DDR genes frequently occur in many tumors, a synthetic lethal approach with ATM inhibitors (ATMi) holds broad therapeutic potential.462 Numerous ATM inhibitors have been screened and explored in clinical trials; however, monotherapy outcomes have been disappointing, with the greatest promise residing in combination strategies (Fig. 7; see Table 1).463 Rational combination strategies can help maximize ATMi efficacy while overcoming multifactorial resistance mechanisms, including compensatory activation of alternative DDR kinases, upregulation of HR repair proteins, enhanced G2/M checkpoint activity and replication stress tolerance.464 In particular, ATMi enhance the impact of conventional DSB-inducing agents and PARPi.464

Given the dependency of CIN cancer cells on TLS polymerases to overcome DNA damage/replication stress, much effort is involved in identifying appropriate inhibitors, however no clinical trials are yet ongoing about this new class of drugs.354

CDK7 is a CDK family member that plays a key role in transcriptional regulation rather than directly in cell cycle progression.465 Indeed, CDK7 in association with cyclin H is a component of the transcription factor TFIIH and regulates the initiation-to-elongation switch of the RNA polymerase II, contributing to the assembly of proper chromatin configuration.466 Several compounds inhibiting CDK7 (CDK7i) have been developed and shown to increase CIN.467,468 Research on CDK7 is rapidly expanding and many clinical trials have been conducted, although further investigations remain necessary to address the challenge of adverse drug effects (Fig. 7; see Table 1).469

Proteostasis regulation

Given the persistent aneuploidy-driven gene dosage imbalance, CIN tumors exhibit a strong dependence on PN. This imposes chronic proteotoxic stress through the accumulation of excess, misfolded and unassembled proteins, placing a continuous burden on cellular protein quality control systems (see subsection “Proteostasis imbalance”).205 This establishes a sort of nononcogene addiction, wherein cancer cells become critically dependent on stress-buffering pathways to survive, even if these pathways are not per se oncogenic.215 As a consequence, partial disruption of proteostasis can tip the balance, leading to catastrophic protein aggregation, activation of stress signaling pathways such as the UPR and ultimately cell death.356 Therapeutically, this vulnerability can be exploited in several ways by using proteasome inhibitors (Pi), HSR inhibitors (HSRi), autophagy inhibitors (Ai) and UPR pathway inhibitors (UPRi) (Fig. 7; see Table 1).356 Importantly, a higher CIN burden correlates with increased activation of heat shock and proteasome gene signatures suggesting that proteostasis dependence scales with genomic instability and providing a compelling rationale for exploiting this vulnerability in precision oncology.185,333,356 Leveraging these insights, targeting proteostasis offers a promising avenue to selectively eradicate tumors with elevated CIN while sparing normal tissues, highlighting an emerging paradigm in cancer therapy.356,470 Nonetheless, drugs targeting proteostasis regulators have shown only modest therapeutic efficacy and off-target effects pose significant challenges. These limitations reflect the pleiotropic roles of these pathways, as chaperones and stress regulators orchestrate a wide array of cellular functions beyond protein homeostasis.470

Importantly, recent studies have demonstrated that inhibition of IRE1α, a central regulator of the PN, can reprogram the TME and enhance antitumor immunity, converting immunologically “cold” tumors, such as prostate, ovarian, breast and colon cancers into ICI-responsive tumors, highlighting its potential as a combination strategy to improve the efficacy of existing immunotherapies (Fig. 7; see Table 1).471,472

Metabolic rewiring

CIN-driven aneuploidy imposes profound metabolic stress on tumor cells, creating a network of metabolic dependencies that can be therapeutically exploited.222 Gene dosage imbalances elevate the demand for glycolysis, nucleotide biosynthesis and glutamine-driven anaplerosis, whereas mitochondrial dysfunction enforces reliance on NADPH-generating and glutathione-based redox buffering systems to mitigate oxidative stress (see also subsection “Energy metabolism rewiring”).222 These adaptations create exploitable vulnerabilities, as perturbation of energy production, biosynthesis or redox homeostasis triggers energetic crisis, oxidative collapse and selective tumor cell death.416 Clinically, these vulnerabilities are leveraged through agents targeting key metabolic nodes: glutaminase inhibitors (GLSi) block glutamine-driven anaplerosis, nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase inhibitors (NAMPTi) disrupt NAD+ biosynthesis and redox balance and glycolysis modulators and mitochondrion-targeted compounds perturb energy flux and mitochondrial metabolism.236,473–476 Many of these agents are in phase I/II trials either as monotherapy or in combination with chemotherapy, targeted therapies or ICIs, highlighting the translational potential of exploiting CIN-driven metabolic fragility (Fig. 7; see Table 1). By selectively targeting the metabolic stress imposed by aneuploidy, these approaches offer a precision oncology strategy to attack tumors that are otherwise resistant to conventional therapies.416

Notably, agents targeting central mediators of the metabolic adaptive response are emerging as a promising strategy to exploit metabolic vulnerabilities: AMPK activators (such as metformin) sensitize tumors to energetic collapse, mTOR inhibitors (mTORi) suppress anabolic metabolism and nucleotide synthesis, HIF-1α inhibitors (HIF-1αi) disrupt hypoxia-driven glycolytic flux and MYC inhibitors (MYCi) destabilize metabolic gene networks essential for biomass accumulation.362 Notably, NRF2-mediated antioxidant networks buffer proteotoxic and oxidative stress in high-CIN or hypoxic tumors and modulators of NRF2 signaling (dimethyl fumarate and NRF2i) have demonstrated preclinical efficacy and immunomodulatory activity, enhancing the response to ICIs in otherwise resistant “cold” tumors (Fig. 7; see Table 1).417 These approaches illustrate a paradigm in which signaling and transcriptional inhibition converges with metabolic targeting, sensitizing cancers to energetic stress, redox imbalance and immunotherapy providing translationally actionable paths to overcome metabolic plasticity and therapeutic resistance.477,478 Despite strong preclinical rationale, targeting metabolic stress in cancer has achieved limited clinical success due to metabolic plasticity, lack of tumor specificity leading to toxicity and ITH, all of which enable adaptive resistance.416,479,480 Additionally, microenvironmental influences and the absence of robust predictive biomarkers further constrain efficacy, underscoring the need for biomarker-guided and combination-based therapeutic strategies.416,479,480

Immune modulation

CIN-induced inflammation is a field of growing scientific interest but remains largely unexplored in terms of therapeutic applications.286 A recent study has shown that chromosomally unstable cancer cells rely on cGAS-STING-mediated inflammatory modulation for their survival and targeting the IL-6/STAT3 pathway might offer a promising treatment strategy for CIN tumors.295,481,482 Treatment with STING agonists (STINGa) or IL-6 inhibitors (IL-6i) induces tumor regression in preclinical studies and in clinical trials both in monotherapy and in combination with conventional chemotherapy or immunotherapy (Fig. 7; see Table 1).483,484 However, many limitations hamper their clinical applicability, including limited bioavailability, the need for intratumoral administration and interference with the immune system.485,486

ENPP1 has emerged as a key extracellular suppressor of cGAS-STING signaling by degrading cGAMP, thereby limiting IFN-I responses and antitumor immunity in CIN tumors also by favoring adenosine accumulation in the TME (see also subsection “Inflammation and immune modulation”).487 Its inhibition stabilizes extracellular cGAMP, enhances paracrine STING activation and promotes immune priming, with early phase clinical trials positioning ENPP1 inhibitors (ENPP1i) as a novel form of innate immune checkpoint and a promising partner for immune checkpoint blockade (Fig. 7; Table 1).488

In addition, macrophage-directed immunotherapies are emerging as a key strategy to overcome immune evasion in high CIN tumors. CIN cancers showing a signature of high CD47 or CSF-1 overexpression could be amenable to macrophage-targeted therapy. In this context, therapeutic approaches targeting the CD47-SIRPα signaling (CD47i) are being actively pursued to restore macrophage-mediated phagocytosis and are currently under clinical investigation (Table 1).489 However, clinical translation has been limited by on-target hematologic toxicity, particularly anemia and thrombocytopenia due to CD47 expression on red blood cells and platelets as well as by adaptive resistance mechanisms, including compensatory upregulation of alternative antiphagocytic pathways.490 CSF-1R inhibitors (CSF-1Ri) are designed to deplete or limit the recruitment of TAMs and are currently being evaluated in clinical trials (Table 1).305 However, their clinical use has been limited by dose-limiting toxicities, including hepatotoxicity and cytopenias.305 In addition, therapeutic resistance frequently emerges via redundancy in myeloid recruitment pathways as well as rapid reprogramming of residual macrophages toward a compensatory immunosuppressive phenotype.305,491

Conclusions and future perspectives

CIN as a dynamic driver of tumor evolution

Over the past two decades, remarkable advances in cancer biology have stemmed from deeper understanding of the mechanisms that preserve genome integrity, particularly those governing DNA replication, DNA damage repair and chromosome segregation fidelity and of the role of CIN.

Once viewed primarily as a consequence of malignant transformation, CIN is now recognized as a fundamental driver of tumor evolution that actively shapes cancer progression and therapeutic response.4 CIN emerges from the convergence of multiple, frequently coexisting abnormalities, including erroneous K-MT attachments, spindle assembly defects, centrosome aberrations, impaired sister chromatid cohesion, replication stress, defective DNA damage repair, telomere dysfunction and epigenetic alterations.18–20,161,161 Together, these processes collectively shape a dynamic landscape of genomic diversity whose biological consequences depend not only on the extent of CIN but also on its temporal dynamics, tissue-specific context and evolutionary selection during tumor progression.

By continuously generating genomic variation, CIN provides the substrate for clonal selection, accelerating tumor evolution and fostering ITH.193 This evolutionary plasticity enables the emergence of genetically distinct subclones capable of adapting to changing microenvironmental conditions and therapeutic pressure, thereby promoting immune evasion, metabolic adaptations, resistance to treatments and metastatic dissemination.4

Paradoxically, the same processes that fuel tumor evolution also impose considerable fitness costs. CIN perturbs proteostasis, rewires cellular metabolism, promotes chronic replication and DNA damage stress and modulates immune pathways.182,205,235,301 The cumulative burden of these stresses threatens cellular viability once it exceeds the buffering capacity of the cell. Consequently, successful cancer cells must maintain CIN within an optimal range that maximizes evolutionary potential while avoiding catastrophic genomic instability and cell death. This evolutionary trade-off positions CIN as a major therapeutic challenge and a unique therapeutic opportunity by exposing targetable vulnerabilities in cancer cells largely absent from normal cells.389

Beyond its cell-intrinsic effects, CIN actively influences the TME by modulating inflammatory and immune signaling pathways, including cytokine secretion, and by affecting immune cell recruitment.301 The functional outcome of these signals is highly context-dependent. Transient or moderate CIN may potentially enhance antitumor immune surveillance, whereas persistent or high CIN often drives chronic inflammation and microenvironment remodeling that ultimately favors tumor progression.288 This duality likely contributes to the heterogeneous relationship between CIN burden and clinical response to immunotherapy. Further understanding of the interplay among CIN, immune surveillance and the evolving TME represents a critical area for future investigation and may inform the development of more effective immunotherapeutic and combination treatment strategies.9,389

Methodological challenges

Despite substantial progress in elucidating the molecular basis of CIN, translating these insights into clinically actionable frameworks remains a major unmet challenge. A key issue is the accurate quantification of CIN in vivo. Current approaches–including genomic, transcriptomic and cytogenetic approaches–lack standardization and frequently capture only static snapshots of a highly dynamic process.393–395 Bulk copy-number profiling, single-cell sequencing, gene-expression profiling and imaging-based assays predominantly capture historical genomic footprints rather than the ongoing CIN. Consequently, distinguishing active CIN from stable genomic alterations remains a critical limitation.393–395 Moreover, the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of CIN complicates patient stratification and raises fundamental questions regarding the identification of driver events, the definition of clinically relevant CIN thresholds and its evolution under therapeutic pressure. Although parameters such as aneuploidy score and tumor mutational burden (TMB) show promise as predictive biomarkers, particularly in the setting of immunotherapy, their ability to capture ongoing CIN require further validation.287,492

Addressing these limitations will require the development of sensitive, robust and clinically translatable biomarkers that directly measure ongoing chromosome mis-segregation and monitor CIN dynamics over time.

Confidently, recent technological advantages are beginning to change this scene. Single-cell multi-omics, spatial transcriptomics, long-read sequencing, live-cell imaging and computational modeling are permitting integrated, high-resolution, analyses of CIN across genomic, transcriptional, spatial and temporal dimensions with unprecedented resolution.401–405 When combined with patient-derived organoids, lineage tracing and longitudinal sampling during treatment, these technologies will enable reconstruction of tumor evolutionary trajectories and real-time monitoring of CIN dynamics.493 Furthermore, the integration of artificial intelligence and machine-learning also promise to identify complex relationships among CIN states, tumor evolution, therapeutic vulnerabilities and clinical outcomes, thereby enabling more precise patient stratification and treatment selection.408

Unlocking the clinical and therapeutic potential of CIN

From a therapeutic perspective, CIN is increasingly recognized not only as a driver of malignancy but also as a context-dependent vulnerability that can be selectively exploited.389 Current therapeutic strategies converge on two complementary approaches. The first seeks to exacerbate CIN beyond the threshold of cellular tolerance, thereby driving cancer cells toward mitotic catastrophe, senescence and immune-mediated elimination.14 Nevertheless, this approach remains constrained by limited tumor specificity, systemic toxicity and the emergence of resistance.412,413

The second strategy targets the adaptive mechanisms that enable CIN-tolerant cells to survive persistent genomic imbalance. This represents a compelling avenue, with several candidates under preclinical and early clinical investigation.205,356,494 Being CIN cancer cell survival reliant on dependencies like mitotic kinase modulation, SAC components, centrosome clustering mechanisms, replication stress regulators, DDR pathways, proteostasis networks, metabolic rewiring, autophagy and immune signaling, these become targets for synthetic lethal therapeutic intervention. In addition, targeting these dependencies offer the possibility to selectively compromise tumor viability while minimizing toxicity to normal proliferating tissues.322,389,495

However, translating these strategies into clinical success will require biomarker-driven patient stratification, together with a deeper understanding of how different CIN states influence treatment sensitivity, resistance evolution and immune responses. Rational combination therapies, including CIN-modulating agents with immunotherapy, DNA-damaging agents or radiotherapy are likely to yield greater therapeutic benefit than single-agent approaches by simultaneously exploiting complementary vulnerabilities while limiting the likelihood of resistance.

Given the extensive spatial and temporal heterogeneity of CIN, longitudinal monitoring throughout therapy will also be essential to capture its evolving biological and clinical consequences. Therefore, the integration of multidimensional measures of CIN with genomic, transcriptomic, metabolic and immune profiling, together with emerging diagnostic technologies that capture real-time tumor evolution, is expected to refine patient stratification and support the rational development of tailored combination therapies.

In conclusion, continued efforts to dissect the mechanisms, consequences and context-specific vulnerabilities associated with CIN, together with advances in technological and clinical innovation, will be essential to transform CIN from a marker of poor prognosis into a tractable, therapeutically actionable, vulnerability. Harnessing this knowledge could expand the current precision oncology paradigm, enabling interventions that target not only tumor-associated genetic alterations but also exploit the evolutionary processes that drive malignant progression, ultimately improving outcomes for patients with chromosomally unstable tumors.
