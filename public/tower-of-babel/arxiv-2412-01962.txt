Pramod N. Achar, Andrea Bourque

Abstract
. We give a “lattice-theoretic” description of the global Schubert variety for GL n \mathrm{GL}_{n} associated to any dominant coweight.



1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a complex reductive group. One can associate to
G
its
affine flag variety

\mathcal{F}l_{G}
and its
affine Grassmannian

\mathcal{G}r_{G}
, both of which play important roles in geometric representation theory. The orbits of
G(\mathbb{C}[[t]])
on
\mathcal{G}r_{G}
are labelled by the set of dominant coweights
\mathbb{X}^{+}
for
G
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
, let
\mathcal{G}r_{G,\lambda}
be the corresponding orbit. Its closure
\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{G,\lambda}}
is called a
spherical Schubert variety
.

The
global affine Grassmannian
, denoted
\mathbf{Gr}_{G}
, is a space that exhibits
\mathcal{F}l_{G}
as a degeneration of
\mathcal{G}r_{G}
. In more detail,
\mathbf{Gr}_{G}
is equipped with a structure map
\rho:\mathbf{Gr}_{G}\to\mathbb{C}
, and we define its
generic part
and
special fiber
by

\mathbf{Gr}_{G,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}=\rho^{-1}(\mathbb{C}^{\times})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{Gr}_{G,{\mathbf{s}}}=\rho^{-1}(0),

respectively. A key fact about
\mathbf{Gr}_{G}
is that we have identifications

\mathbf{Gr}_{G,{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}=\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathcal{G}r_{G}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{Gr}_{G,{\mathbf{s}}}=\mathcal{F}l_{G}.

One version of
\mathbf{Gr}_{G}
was introduced in
[
3
]
; we will work a variant developed in
[
10
,
13
]
.

Now let
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
. The variety
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathcal{G}r_{G,\lambda}
can be identified with a locally closed subvariety of (the generic part of)
\mathbf{Gr}_{G}
. Its closure in
\mathbf{Gr}_{G}
, denoted by
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{G,\lambda}}
, is called a
global Schubert variety
.

For the remainder of the paper, we focus on the case
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, and we omit the subscript
G
from
\mathcal{F}l
,
\mathcal{G}r
, and
\mathbf{Gr}
. It is well known that
\mathcal{F}l
and
\mathcal{G}r
admit descriptions in terms of
lattices
. These descriptions will be reviewed in Section
2
. We will also review the lattice-theoretic description of the spherical Schubert varieties
\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}
.

The goal of this paper is to give a lattice-theoretic description of the global Schubert varieties
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}
. (A statement along these lines has been known to researchers working on Shimura varieties for some time: see the remarks after Theorem
1.1
.) To formulate the main result, we need a lattice-theoretic description of
\mathbf{Gr}
for
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
. This description (to be explained in Section
3
) realizes
\mathbf{Gr}
as a subset

\mathbf{Gr}\subset\mathbb{C}\times\underbrace{\mathcal{G}r\times\cdots\times\mathcal{G}r}_{\text{$n$ copies}},

where the structure map
\rho:\mathbf{Gr}\to\mathbb{C}
is projection onto the first factor. For a dominant coweight
\lambda
, we define a closed subset
X_{\lambda}\subset\mathbf{Gr}
by

X_{\lambda}=\mathbf{Gr}\cap(\mathbb{C}\times\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}\times\cdots\times\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}).

See Remark
3.5
for a very concrete description of
X_{\lambda}
. The main result of this paper is the following characterization of global Schubert varieties for
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any dominant coweight
\lambda
, we have
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}=X_{\lambda}
.

Readers who are familiar with the theory of Shimura varieties will recognize this statement as asserting the “topological flatness of (certain) local models of Shimura varieties.” The “local models” in question are certain schemes over
\mathrm{Spec}\ \mathbb{Z}_{p}
, but when the same constructions are carried out with
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
replaced by
\mathbb{C}[[t]]
, the resulting schemes are essentially the same as global Schubert varieties. The idea of describing local models of Shimura models in terms of lattices was first developed systematically by Rapoport–Zink
[
11
]
. In this setting, for
\lambda
minuscule, Theorem
1.1
was proved by Görtz
[
4
]
. For general
\lambda
, one can deduce Theorem
1.1
from a result of Haines–Ngô
[
7
]
on nearby cycles for local models; see
[
6
, §7]
for a sketch of this argument. A similar short proof of Theorem
1.1
can be obtained by combining results of Kottwitz–Rapoport
[
9
]
, Haines–Ngô
[
8
]
, and Zhu
[
13
]
. (We are grateful to T. Haines for these pointers to the literature.)

The present paper aims to give a self-contained proof of Theorem
1.1
using only basic topology and linear algebra. (In particular, there is no mention of either Shimura varieties or nearby cycles later in the paper.) We hope that this may make the statement more accessible to readers who are unfamiliar with the theory of local models.

Here is an outline of the proof. Once the preliminary definitions are in place, it is relative easy to see that
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}
is contained in
X_{\lambda}
, and that they agree on the generic part:

\mathbf{Gr}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\cap\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}=\mathbf{Gr}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\cap X_{\lambda}=\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}.

Thus, to prove Theorem
1.1
, the main task to is to show that the special fiber of
X_{\lambda}
is not too big. We do this as follows:

(1)

When
\lambda
is minuscule, we write down explicit formulas exhibiting each point of
X_{\lambda}
as a limit of a
1
-parameter family in
\mathbf{Gr}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\cap\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}
.

(2)

For the general case, we use the geometry of (global)
convolution spaces
to obtain the result by induction on
\lambda
.

Step
1
is equivalent to
[
4
, Proposition 4.16]
, which is also proved by an explicit calculation (but not the same as ours). It may be possible to treat general
\lambda
by a similar method (using
[
5
, Lemma 2]
), but we have not pursued this approach here.

Outline of the paper

Section
2
contains background and notation related to lattices,
\mathcal{F}l
, and
\mathcal{G}r
. Section
3
contains background related to
\mathbf{Gr}
and global Schubert varieties. The minuscule case of Theorem
1.1
is proved in Section
4
. Next, Section
5
establishes a technical combinatorial statement that is related to the geometry of convolution spaces. Finally, in Section
6
, we review the construction of convolution spaces, and we complete the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Background on lattices

2.1.
Group-theoretic preliminaries

Let
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}((t))
be the field of formal Laurent series over
\mathbb{C}
with indeterminate
t
, and let
\mathbb{O}=\mathbb{C}[[t]]
be the ring of formal power series. Fix an integer
n\geq 1
. We will consider the groups
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
,
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
, and
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{K})
. For
1\leq i\leq n
, let

e_{i}=(0,\ldots,0,1,0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}

be the vector whose
i
-th coordinate is
1
, with all other coordinates
0
. In some contexts, we may also regard
e_{i}
as a vector in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
or
\mathbb{K}^{n}
.

Let
T\subset\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be the maximal torus consisting of diagonal matrices, and let
\mathbb{X}
be its coweight lattice. Identify
\mathbb{X}
with
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
as follows: if
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, the corresponding cocharacter
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\to T
is

t\mapsto\left[\begin{smallmatrix}t^{\lambda_{1}}\\
&\ddots\\
&&t^{\lambda_{n}}\end{smallmatrix}\right].

As a shorthand, we write
\mathbf{t}^{\lambda}
for the matrix above. Then
\mathbf{t}^{\lambda}
can be regarded as an element of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{K})
. We define the
size
of a coweight
\lambda
to be

|\lambda|=\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}.

For
0\leq i\leq n
, let

\varpi_{i}=e_{1}+\ldots+e_{i}=(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{\text{$i$ terms}},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{\text{$n-i$ terms}})\in\mathbb{X}.

Let
B^{-}\subset\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be the subgroup consisting of lower-triangular matrices. We define
dominant
coweights with respect to the opposite Borel subgroup to
B^{-}
: thus, a coweight
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
is dominant if

\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}.

The set of dominant coweights is denoted by
\mathbb{X}^{+}\subset\mathbb{X}
. Let
\preceq
be the usual partial order on
\mathbb{X}
: we set
\mu\preceq\lambda
if
\lambda-\mu
is a nonnegative linear combination of the simple coroots
(0,\ldots,0,1,-1,0,\ldots,0)
. In concrete terms, for
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
and
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
, we have

\mu\preceq\lambda\qquad\text{if}\qquad\text{$|\mu|=|\lambda|$\quad and\quad$\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}$\quad for $1\leq k\leq n$}.

Let
W=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the Weyl group of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, identified with the symmetric group on
n
letters. This group acts on
\mathbb{X}
in the obvious way. For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}
, there is a unique dominant coweight in its
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-orbit. We denote it by

\mathsf{dom}(\lambda)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\cdot\lambda\cap\mathbb{X}^{+}.

Let

W_{\mathrm{ext}}=\mathfrak{S}_{n}\ltimes\mathbb{X}

be the extended affine Weyl group.

For
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, let
\dot{\sigma}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
be the corresponding permutation matrix. Now suppose
w=(\sigma,\lambda)\in W_{\mathrm{ext}}
. We set

\dot{w}=\dot{\sigma}\mathbf{t}^{\lambda}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{K}).

There is a homomorphism
\mathrm{ev}_{0}:\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})\to\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{C})
given by
t\mapsto 0
. We set

I=(\mathrm{ev}_{0})^{-1}(B^{-})\subset\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O}).

This is an
Iwahori subgroup
of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
.

2.2.
Lattices

A
lattice
in the vector space
\mathbb{K}^{n}
is defined to be a free
\mathbb{O}
-submodule
L\subset\mathbb{K}^{n}
of rank
n
. In particular,
\mathbb{O}^{n}
is called the
standard lattice
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}
, let

\mathbf{L}^{\lambda}=\mathbf{t}^{\lambda}\cdot\mathbb{O}^{n}=\text{the lattice spanned by the columns of $\mathbf{t}^{\lambda}$}.

For example, we have

\mathbf{L}^{\varpi_{i}}=(t\mathbb{O})^{i}\times\mathbb{O}^{n-i}.

Given a lattice
L
, let
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in\mathbb{K}^{n}
be an
\mathbb{O}
-basis for
L
. Then one can form the matrix whose columns are
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
. Then there is an integer
\nu(L)
such that

\det\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}&\cdots&f_{n}\end{bmatrix}=t^{\nu(L)}\cdot(\text{a unit in $\mathbb{O}$}).

Equivalently,
\nu(L)
is the smallest integer such that
t^{\nu(L)}
occurs with nonzero coefficient in
\det\begin{bmatrix}f_{1}&\cdots&f_{n}\end{bmatrix}
. This integer is independent of the choice of basis. It is called the
valuation
of
L
. We clearly have

\nu(\mathbf{L}^{\lambda})=|\lambda|.

If
L^{\prime}\subset L
are two lattices, then
L/L^{\prime}
is a finite-dimensional
\mathbb{C}
-vector space with dimension
\nu(L^{\prime})-\nu(L)
. A
lattice chain
is a sequence of lattices

\underline{L}=(L_{1}\supset L_{2}\supset\cdots\supset L_{n}\supset tL_{1})

such that
\dim L_{i}/L_{i+1}=1
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. (This condition forces
\dim L_{n}/tL_{1}=1
as well.) The
standard lattice chain
, denoted by
\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{e}
, is the lattice chain

\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{e}=(\mathbf{L}^{0}\supset\mathbf{L}^{\varpi_{1}}\supset\mathbf{L}^{\varpi_{2}}\supset\cdots\supset\mathbf{L}^{\varpi_{n-1}}\supset t\mathbf{L}^{0}=\mathbf{L}^{\varpi_{n}}).

For
w=(\sigma,\lambda)\in W_{\mathrm{ext}}
, we set

\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{w}=\dot{w}\cdot\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{e}=(\mathbf{L}^{\sigma(\lambda)}\supset\mathbf{L}^{\sigma(\lambda+\varpi_{1})}\supset\cdots\supset\mathbf{L}^{\sigma(\lambda+\varpi_{n-1})}\supset t\mathbf{L}^{\sigma(\lambda)}).

2.3.
The affine Grassmannian and the affine flag variety

The
affine Grassmannian

\mathcal{G}r
and the
affine flag variety

\mathcal{F}l
are defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}r

\displaystyle=\{\text{lattices in $\mathbb{K}^{n}$}\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{K})/\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}l

\displaystyle=\{\text{lattice chains in $\mathbb{K}^{n}$}\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{K})/I.

The latter identifications above come from the following observation:
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{K})
acts transitively on the set of all lattices and the set of all lattice chains, and the stabilizer of the standard lattice (resp. standard lattice chain) is
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
(resp.
I
).

Both
\mathcal{G}r
and
\mathcal{F}l
can be made into ind-projective ind-varieties: that is, each can be written as a union
\bigcup_{i\geq 1}X_{i}
, where each
X_{i}
is a projective complex variety, and the inclusion maps
X_{i}\to X_{i+1}
are closed immersions. See, for instance,
[
12
]
.

It is well known that the set of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
-orbits on
\mathcal{G}r
is in bijection with
\mathbb{X}^{+}
. Explicitly, for
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
, the corresponding orbit is given by

\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}=\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})\cdot\mathbf{L}^{\lambda}\subset\mathcal{G}r.

On the other hand, the
I
-orbits on
\mathcal{G}r
are in bijection with all of
\mathbb{X}
. We have

(2.1)

\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}=\bigcup_{\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\cdot\lambda}I\cdot\mathbf{L}^{\lambda^{\prime}}.

The closure
\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}
is called a
spherical Schubert variety
. For
\mu,\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
, we have

\mathcal{G}r_{\mu}\subset\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\mu\preceq\lambda.

Combining this with (
2.1
), we find that

(2.2)

I\cdot\mathbf{L}^{\mu}\subset\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\mathsf{dom}(\mu)\preceq\lambda.

Similarly, the set of
I
-orbits on
\mathcal{F}l
is in bijection with
W_{\mathrm{ext}}
: we have

\mathcal{F}l=\bigcup_{w\in W_{\mathrm{ext}}}I\cdot\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{w}.

Remark 2.1
.

For a dominant coweight
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
, one can show that

\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}=\left\{L\in\mathcal{G}r\,\Big|\,\begin{array}[]{c}\text{$\nu(L)=|\lambda|$, $t^{\lambda_{1}}\mathbb{O}^{n}\subset L\subset t^{\lambda_{n}}\mathbb{O}^{n}$, and for $\lambda_{1}\geq i\geq\lambda_{n}$,}\\
\text{$\dim(L\cap t^{i}\mathbb{O}^{n})/t^{\lambda_{1}+1}\mathbb{O}^{n}=\sum_{j=i}^{\lambda_{1}}|\{k\mid\lambda_{k}\leq i\}|$}\end{array}\right\},

and

\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}=\left\{L\in\mathcal{G}r\,\Big|\,\begin{array}[]{c}\text{$\nu(L)=|\lambda|$, $t^{\lambda_{1}}\mathbb{O}^{n}\subset L\subset t^{\lambda_{n}}\mathbb{O}^{n}$, and for $\lambda_{1}\geq i\geq\lambda_{n}$,}\\
\text{$\dim(L\cap t^{i}\mathbb{O}^{n})/t^{\lambda_{1}+1}\mathbb{O}^{n}\geq\sum_{j=i}^{\lambda_{1}}|\{k\mid\lambda_{k}\leq i\}|$}\end{array}\right\}.

See, for instance,
[
1
, Exercise 9.1.5]
(which has a minor typo in it).

Example 2.2
.

For
\lambda=\varpi_{k}
with
1\leq k\leq n-1
, Remark
2.1
reduces to

\mathcal{G}r_{\varpi_{k}}=\overline{\varpi_{k}}=\{L\in\mathcal{G}r\mid\text{$t\mathbb{O}^{n}\subset L\subset\mathbb{O}^{n}$ and $\dim L/t\mathbb{O}^{n}=n-k$}\}.

Identify
\mathbb{O}^{n}/t\mathbb{O}^{n}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and let
G(n,n-k)
be the usual Grassmannian of
(n-k)
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Then we have an isomorphism

\mathcal{G}r_{\varpi_{k}}\cong G(n,n-k)

given by
L\mapsto L/t\mathbb{O}^{n}\in G(n,n-k)
.

2.4.
Kottwitz–Rapoport alcoves

Define a new partial order
\leq_{\mathrm{co}}
(here, “
{\mathrm{co}}
” stands for “coordinatewise”) on
\mathbb{X}
as follows: for coweights
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
and
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n})
, we set

\lambda\leq_{\mathrm{co}}\mu\qquad\text{if}\qquad\text{$\lambda_{i}\leq\mu_{i}$ for $1\leq i\leq n$.}

Now let
x=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})\in\mathbb{X}^{n}
be an
n
-tuple of coweights. Following Kottwitz–Rapoport
[
9
]
, we call
x
an
alcove
if the following conditions hold:

\displaystyle x^{(1)}\leq_{\mathrm{co}}x^{(2)}\leq_{\mathrm{co}}\cdots\leq_{\mathrm{co}}x^{(n)}\leq_{\mathrm{co}}x^{(1)}+\varpi_{n},

\displaystyle|x^{(2)}|=|x^{(1)}|+1,\quad|x^{(3)}|=|x^{(2)}|+1,\quad\ldots\quad|x^{(n)}|=|x^{(n-1)}|+1.

When working with alcoves, it is sometimes convenient to adopt the convention that
x^{(n+1)}=x^{(1)}+\varpi_{n}
.

The definition implies that
x^{(i+1)}
differs from
x^{(i)}
in exactly one coordinate. For an alcove
x
, let
\mathsf{P}(x)\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the permutation characterized by the property that

x^{(i+1)}_{j}=\begin{cases}x^{(i)}_{j}+1&\text{if $j=\mathsf{P}(x)(i)$,}\\
x^{(i)}_{j}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Alternatively,
x^{(i+1)}=x^{(i)}+e_{\mathsf{P}(x)(i)}=x^{(i)}+\mathsf{P}(x)e_{i}
. By induction on
i
, we find

x^{(i)}=x^{(1)}+\mathsf{P}(x)\varpi_{i-1}.

The
base alcove
is the alcove

\omega=(\varpi_{0},\varpi_{1},\ldots,\varpi_{n-1}).

It has the property that
\mathsf{P}(\omega)
is the identity permutation.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{X}^{n}
be the set of alcoves. There is an action of
W_{\mathrm{ext}}
on
\mathcal{A}
, given by

(\sigma,\lambda)\cdot(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})=(\sigma(\lambda+x^{(1)}),\ldots,\sigma(\lambda+x^{(n)})).

As explained in
[
9
, §3.2]
, this action is simply transitive, so there is a bijection

W_{\mathrm{ext}}\overset{\sim}{\leftrightarrow}\mathcal{A}\qquad\text{given by}\qquad w\mapsto w\cdot\omega.

The inverse map is given by
x\mapsto(\mathsf{P}(x),\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}\cdot x^{(1)})
.

This bijection gives us an alternative way to label the
I
-orbits on
\mathcal{F}l
. In particular, if
x=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})
is an alcove, let

\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{x}=(\mathbf{L}^{x^{(1)}}\supset\mathbf{L}^{x^{(2)}}\supset\cdots\supset\mathbf{L}^{x^{(n)}}\supset t\mathbf{L}^{x^{(1)}}).

If
x=w\cdot\omega
for
w\in W_{\mathrm{ext}}
, then
\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{x}=\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{w}
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
be a dominant coweight. An alcove
x=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})
is said to be
\lambda
-permissible
if we have

\mathsf{dom}(x^{(i)}-\varpi_{i-1})\preceq\lambda\qquad\text{for $1\leq i\leq n$.}

For
x,y\in\mathcal{A}
, we say that
x
is in
relative position
\lambda
with respect to
y
if we have

\mathsf{dom}(x^{(i)}-y^{(i)})=\lambda\qquad\text{for $1\leq i\leq n$.}

3.
The global affine Grassmannian

For
1\leq i\leq n
and
y\in\mathbb{C}
, let
\theta_{i}(y):\mathbb{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{K}^{n}
be the linear map defined by

\theta_{i}(y)(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})=((t-y)v_{1},(t-y)v_{2},\ldots,(t-y)v_{i},v_{i+1},\ldots,v_{n}).

In other words,
\theta_{i}(y)
is defined by substituting
t-y
for
t
in the matrix
\mathbf{t}^{\varpi_{i}}
. Since
t-y\neq 0
, the map
\theta_{i}(y)
is always an isomorphism of
\mathbb{K}
-vector spaces. It restricts to a map
\theta_{i}(y):\mathbb{O}^{n}\to\mathbb{O}^{n}
. The latter map is an isomorphism if and only if
y\neq 0
(equivalently, if and only if
t-y
is invertible in
\mathbb{O}
).

We define a
lattice family

F
to be a tuple

F=(y,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})

where
y\in\mathbb{C}
and
L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}\in\mathcal{G}r
, and where the following two conditions hold:

(3.1)

\displaystyle\nu(L_{1})=\cdots=\nu(L_{n}),

(3.2)

\displaystyle L_{1}\supset\theta_{1}(y)(L_{2})\supset\theta_{2}(y)(L_{3})\supset\cdots\supset\theta_{n-1}(y)(L_{n})\supset(t-y)L_{1}.

We define the
global affine Grassmannian
to be the set

(3.3)

\mathbf{Gr}=\{\text{lattice families}\}\subset\mathbb{C}\times\underbrace{\mathcal{G}r\times\cdots\times\mathcal{G}r}_{\text{$n$ copies}}.

This set inherits the structure of an ind-variety from
\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{G}r\times\cdots\times\mathcal{G}r
. There is an obvious map
\rho:\mathbf{Gr}\to\mathbb{C}
given by
\rho(y,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})=y
. Let

\mathbf{Gr}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=\rho^{-1}(\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\{0\})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}=\rho^{-1}(0).

We call
\mathbf{Gr}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
the
generic part
of
\mathbf{Gr}
, and we call
\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}
the
special fiber
.

Lemma 3.1
.

There are isomorphisms of ind-varieties

\mathbf{Gr}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\cong\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathcal{G}r,\qquad\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}\cong\mathcal{F}l.

Proof.

When
y\neq 0
, the maps
\theta_{i}(y)
all lie in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
; in particular, the action of
\theta_{i}(y)
on
\mathcal{G}r
preserves valuation. Since all lattices in the sequence (
3.2
) have the same valuation, the containments are equalities. Thus, the map
\mathbf{Gr}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathcal{G}r
given by
(y,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})\mapsto(y,L_{1})
is an isomorphism, with inverse given by

(y,L)\mapsto(y,L,\theta_{1}(y)^{-1}(L),\theta_{2}(y)^{-1}(L),\ldots,\theta_{n-1}(y)^{-1}(L)).

When
y=0
, the action of
\theta_{i}(y)
on a lattice increases its valuation by
1
, so the sequence in (
3.2
) is a lattice chain in the sense of
2.2
. Thus, the isomorphism
\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}\to\mathcal{F}l
is given by

(0,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})\mapsto(L_{1}\supset\theta_{1}(0)L_{2}\supset\cdots\supset\theta_{n-1}(0)(L_{n})\supset tL_{1}).\qed

Remark 3.2
.

For a complex reductive group
G
, a “fancy” definition of the associated global affine Grassmannian
\mathbf{Gr}^{\mathrm{fancy}}_{G}
can be found in
[
10
, §6.2.3]
or
[
13
, §3.1]
. In this definition,
\mathbf{Gr}^{\mathrm{fancy}}_{G}
is the ind-scheme that classifies principal bundles for a certain “parahoric-style” group scheme related to
G
. An expository account can be found in
[
2
, §2.2.3.2]
, and the case where
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}
is worked out in
[
2
, Example 2.2.16]
. That example shows that
\mathbf{Gr}^{\mathrm{fancy}}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}
agrees with (
3.3
) at the level of
\mathbb{C}
-points.

However, they don’t quite agree algebro-geometrically: it turns out that the ind-scheme
\mathbf{Gr}^{\mathrm{fancy}}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}
is not reduced, and that the variety defined in (
3.3
) is its reduced subscheme. Of course, this distinction is irrelevant for the study of global Schubert varieties, which are reduced by definition.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
, consider the orbit
\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}\subset\mathcal{G}r
. Via Lemma
3.1
, we may regard
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}
as a locally closed subset of
\mathbf{Gr}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
, and we can take its closure
\overline{\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}\subset\mathbf{Gr}
. We call this the
global Schubert variety
labelled by
\lambda
, and we denote it by

\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}=\overline{\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}.

(Note that the notation “
\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}
” is not defined.) The main result of this paper (Theorem
1.1
) states that
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}
coincides with the variety

X_{\lambda}=\{(y,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})\in\mathbf{Gr}\mid L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}\in\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}\}.

It is clear that
X_{\lambda}
is a closed subset of
\mathbf{Gr}
, and that

X_{\lambda}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}.

It follows immediately that
X_{\lambda}\supset\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}
. Thus, Theorem
1.1
comes down to proving the opposite containment. Moreover, since we have already shown that this is an equality over
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, what remains is to show that

X_{\lambda}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}\subset\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}.

Via Lemma
3.1
, we identify
X_{\lambda}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}
with a subset of
\mathcal{F}l
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\lambda
be a dominant coweight.

(1)

The varieties
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}},X_{\lambda}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}\subset\mathcal{F}l
are both stable under
I
.

(2)

For
x\in\mathcal{A}
, we have
I\cdot\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{x}\subset X_{\lambda}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}
if and only if
x
is
\lambda
-permissible.

Proof.

(
1
) Define an action of
I
on
\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{G}r\times\cdots\times\mathcal{G}r
as follows: for
g\in I
,

g\cdot(y,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})=(y,gL_{1},\theta_{1}(y)^{-1}g\theta_{1}(y)L_{2},\ldots,\theta_{n-1}(y)^{-1}g\theta_{n-1}(y)L_{n}).

This “global” action preserves conditions (
3.1
) and (
3.2
), so it restricts to an action on
\mathbf{Gr}
. When
y=0
, the global action coincides with the natural action on
I
on
\mathcal{F}l
via the isomorphism of Lemma
3.1
.

A short calculation shows that for any
g\in I
and any
y\in\mathbb{C}
, the matrix
\theta_{i-1}(y)^{-1}g\theta_{i-1}(y)
lies in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
. It is immediate that
X_{\lambda}
and
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}
are stable under the global
I
-action; the latter case implies that
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}
is as well.

(
2
) Let
x=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})
be an alcove. The lattice chain
\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{x}=(\mathbf{L}^{x^{(1)}}\supset\mathbf{L}^{x^{(2)}}\supset\cdots\supset\mathbf{L}^{x^{(n)}})
lies in
X_{\lambda}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}
if and only if

\theta_{i-1}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{L}^{x^{(i)}}\in\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}

for
1\leq i\leq n
. (When
i=1
,
\theta_{0}(0)
is the identity map.) From the definition, we see that
\theta_{i-1}(0)^{-1}\mathbf{L}^{x^{(i)}}=\mathbf{L}^{x^{(i)}-\varpi_{i-1}}
. By (
2.2
), we conclude that
I\cdot\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{x}\subset X_{\lambda}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}
if and only if
\mathsf{dom}(x^{(i)}-\varpi_{i-1})\preceq\lambda
,
i.e., if and only if
x
is
\lambda
-permissible.
∎

In view of Lemma
3.3
, Theorem
1.1
comes down to the following claim:

(3.4)

For each
\lambda
-permissible alcove
x
, we have
\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{x}\in\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}
.

The following lemma describes one easy case where this condition holds.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
\lambda=(a,a,\ldots,a)
is a constant coweight, then
X_{\lambda}=\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}
.

Proof.

In this case,
\lambda
is minimal with respect to
\preceq
, and
\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}=\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}
is a single point. It follows that
X_{\lambda}
and
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}
are both isomorphic to
\mathbb{C}
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Using Remark
2.1
, we can write out the definition of
X_{\lambda}
as

\left\{(y,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})\,\left|\begin{array}[]{c}\text{$y\in\mathbb{C}$, and $L_{1},\ldots,L_{n}$ are lattices in $\mathbb{K}^{n}$ such that}\\
\text{$L_{1}\supset\theta_{1}(y)(L_{2})\supset\cdots\supset\theta_{n-1}(y)(L_{n})\supset(t-y)L_{n}$, and such}\\
\text{that for $1\leq i\leq n$ and $\lambda_{1}\geq j\geq\lambda_{n}$, we have}\\
\text{ $t^{\lambda_{1}}\mathbb{O}^{n}\subset L_{i}\subset t^{\lambda_{n}}\mathbb{O}^{n}$, $\nu(L_{i})=|\lambda|$, and}\\
\text{$\dim(L_{i}\cap t^{j}\mathbb{O}^{n})/t^{\lambda_{1}+1}\mathbb{O}^{n}\geq\sum_{k=j}^{\lambda_{1}}|\{h\mid\lambda_{h}\leq k\}|$}\end{array}\right.\right\}.

Theorem
1.1
says that this is also a description of
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}
.

Remark 3.6
.

The notion of
\lambda
-permissibility
was introduced in
[
9
]
, along with a related notion called
\lambda
-admissibility
, which is defined in terms of the Bruhat order on
W_{\mathrm{ext}}
. (Neither
\lambda
-admissibility nor the Bruhat order play any role in the present paper.) In
[
8
, Theorem 1]
, Haines–Ngô showed that
\lambda
-permissibility and
\lambda
-admissibility coincide for
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}
(although they do not coincide in general). In
[
13
, Theorem 3.8]
, Zhu showed (for any
G
) that the special fiber
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda}}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}
is the union of
I
-orbits labelled by
\lambda
-admissible alcoves. In view of Lemma
3.3
, we see that among the three statements

[this paper, Theorem
1.1
]

[
8
, Theorem 1]

[
13
, Theorem 3.8 for
G=\mathrm{GL}_{n}
]

any two imply the third.

4.
The case of fundamental coweights

Fix an integer
r
with
1\leq r\leq n-1
. The goal of this section is prove (
3.4
), and hence Theorem
1.1
, for the case of a fundamental coweight
\lambda=\varpi_{n-r}
. Recall from Example
2.2
that
\mathcal{G}r_{\varpi_{n-r}}\cong G(n,r)
. Let us describe
X_{\varpi_{n-r}}
in these terms. For
1\leq i\leq n
and
y\in\mathbb{C}
, let

\bar{\theta}_{i}(y):\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}

be the map induced by
\theta_{i}(y):\mathbb{O}^{n}\to\mathbb{O}^{n}
under the identification
\mathbb{O}^{n}/t\mathbb{O}^{n}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Explicitly,
\bar{\theta}_{i}(y)
is given on basis vectors by

(4.1)

\bar{\theta}_{i}(y)e_{j}=\begin{cases}-ye_{j}&\text{if $j\leq i$,}\\
e_{j}&\text{if $j>i$.}\end{cases}

We have

(4.2)

X_{\varpi_{n-r}}=\{(y,H_{1},\ldots,H_{n})\in\mathbb{C}\times G(n,r)\times\cdots\times G(n,r)\mid{}\\
H_{1}\supset\bar{\theta}_{1}(y)(H_{2})\supset\cdots\supset\bar{\theta}_{n-1}(y)(H_{n})\supset yH_{1}\}.

We will prove the result by giving an explicit formula for a
1
-parameter family in
X_{\varpi_{n-r}}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}=\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times G(n,r)
whose limit at
y=0
is a given point of
X_{\varpi_{n-r}}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\mathbf{s}}
.

4.1.
Notation related to alcoves

Let
x=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})
be a
\varpi_{n-r}
-permissible alcove. In particular, in the coweight
x^{(1)}
, there are
n-r
coordinates equal to
1
, and
r
coordinates equal to
0
. Let
J^{(1)}=(j^{(1)}_{1},j^{(1)}_{2},\ldots,j^{(1)}_{r})
be the set of indices (in some order) of the
0
’s in
x^{(1)}
, i.e.,

x^{(1)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n\\
j\notin J^{(1)}\end{subarray}}e_{j}.

For
1\leq i\leq n
, define a sequence
J^{(i+1)}=(j^{(i+1)}_{1},\ldots,j^{(i+1)}_{r})
by the inductive rule

(4.3)

j^{(i+1)}_{q}=\begin{cases}i&\text{if $j^{(i)}_{q}=\mathsf{P}(x)(i)$,}\\
j^{(i)}_{q}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Using the fact that
(x^{(i+1)}-\varpi_{i})=(x^{(i)}-\varpi_{i-1})+e_{\mathsf{P}(x)(i)}-e_{i}
, we see that

(4.4)

x^{(i)}-\varpi_{i-1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq n\\
j\notin J^{(i)}\end{subarray}}e_{j}.

In particular, since
x^{(n+1)}-\varpi_{n}=x^{(1)}
, we see that
J^{(1)}
and
J^{(n+1)}
are equal as (unordered) sets. As ordered sets, they are permutations of one another. Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{r}
be the permutation such that

j^{(1)}_{q}=j^{(n+1)}_{\sigma(q)}\qquad\text{for $1\leq q\leq r$.}

It will be useful to have an alternative notation for the sequence of integers

j^{(1)}_{q},j^{(2)}_{q},\ldots,j^{(n+1)}_{q}.

Let
f_{q}
be
1+{}
(the number of “changes” in this sequence), i.e.,

f_{q}=1+|\{i\mid\text{$2\leq i\leq n+1$ and $j^{(i)}_{q}\neq j^{(i-1)}_{q}$}\}|.

We record the indices where changes occur as follows: let

1=c(q,1)<c(q,2)<\cdots<c(q,f_{q})<c(q,f_{q}+1)=n+2

be the sequence of integers characterized by

j_{q}^{(c(q,f))}=j_{q}^{(c(q,f)+1)}=\cdots=j_{q}^{(c(q,f+1)-1)}\neq j_{q}^{(c(q,f+1))}\qquad\text{for $1\leq f\leq f_{q}$.}

For
1\leq i\leq n+1
, let
\phi(q,i)=\max\{f\mid\text{$1\leq f\leq f_{q}$ and $c(q,f)\leq i$}\}
. Thus,

c(q,\phi(q,i))\leq i<c(q,\phi(q,i)+1).

Finally, for
1\leq f\leq f_{q}
, let

a(q,f)=j_{q}^{(c(q,f))}=j_{q}^{(c(q,f)+1)}=\cdots=j_{q}^{(c(q,f+1)-1)}.

In particular,
a(q,f_{q})=j_{q}^{(n+1)}
, so

(4.5)

a(q,1)=a(\sigma(q),f_{\sigma(q)}).

Suppose
1\leq q\leq r
, and consider the finite set

\cyc(q)=\{q,\sigma(q),\sigma^{2}(q),\ldots\}\subset\{1,\ldots,r\}.

Let
M(q)\in\cyc(q)
be the element whose corresponding term in
J^{(1)}
is maximal:

j^{(1)}_{M(q)}\geq j^{(1)}_{s}\qquad\text{for all $s\in\cyc(q)$.}

Equivalently,
a(M(q),1)\geq a(s,1)
for all
s\in\cyc(q)
. We also set

\displaystyle m(q)=\sigma(M(q)),\quad C(q)=|\cyc(q)|,\quad\delta(q)=\min\{h\geq 0\mid\sigma^{h}(q)=M(q)\}.

The following observations are immediate from the definitions:

\displaystyle 0=\delta(M(q))\leq\delta(q)\leq\delta(m(q))=C(q)-1<r\quad\text{for all $q$,}

\displaystyle\text{If $q\neq M(q)$, then }\delta(\sigma(q))=\delta(q)-1,

If
f_{q}=1
, then
\phi(q,i)=1
for all
i
,

If
f_{q}>1
, then
f_{s}>1
for all
s\in\cyc(q)
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
1\leq q\leq r
and and
1\leq i\leq n+1
.

(1)

If
2\leq f\leq f_{q}
, then
a(q,f)=c(q,f)-1
.

(2)

Suppose
2\leq f\leq f_{q}
. We have
a(q,f)<i
if and only if
f\leq\phi(q,i)
.

(3)

We have
a(q,1)>a(q,2)
and
a(q,2)<a(q,3)<\cdots<a(q,f_{q})
.

(4)

For
1\leq f\leq f_{q}
, we have
a(q,f)\leq a(M(q),1)
.

(5)

If
i>a(M(q),1)
, then
\phi(q,i)=f_{q}
.

(6)

If
f_{q}=n+1
, then
\sigma(q)=q
.

Proof.

(
1
) Since
f\geq 2
, we have
j^{(c(q,f))}_{q}\neq j^{(c(q,f)-1)}_{q}
. From the definition (
4.3
), we have
j^{(c(q,f))}_{q}=c(q,f)-1
.

(
2
) In view of part (
1
), we have
a(q,f)<i
if and only if
c(q,f)\leq i
, and the latter holds if and only if
f\leq\phi(q,i)
.

(
3
) If
f_{q}=1
, then this is vacuous, as
a(q,f)
is undefined for
f>f_{q}=1
. Otherwise, let
j=a(q,1)
. Then
j\in J^{(1)}
and
j\in J^{(n+1)}
but
j\notin J^{(c(q,2))}
. Let
c
be the smallest integer with
c(q,2)<c\leq n+1
such that
j\in J^{(c)}
. From (
4.3
), we must have
c=j+1
. We deduce that
c(q,2)<a(q,1)+1
, and then by part (
1
),
a(q,2)<a(q,1)
. The remaining inequalities in part (
3
) follow from part (
1
).

(
4
) If
f=1
, this holds by definition. If
f\geq 2
, then
a(q,f)\leq a(q,f_{q})=a(\sigma^{-1}(q),1)\leq a(M(\sigma^{-1}(q)),1)=a(M(q),1)
.

(
5
) If
f_{q}=1
, then
\phi(q,1)=1=f_{q}
automatically. Otherwise, this follows from parts (
2
) and (
4
).

(
6
) For
1\leq i\leq n
, we must have
j_{q}^{(i)}=\mathsf{P}(x)(i)\neq i=j_{q}^{(i+1)}
. In particular,
i=\mathsf{P}(x)(i+1)
for
1\leq i\leq n-1
. It follows that
j_{q}^{(1)}=\mathsf{P}(x)(1)=n=j^{(n+1)}_{q}
. Thus
\sigma(q)=q
.
∎

4.2.
Families of vectors

In this subsection, for
1\leq q\leq r
, we will define vectors
v_{q}(u),v_{q}^{\prime}(u),v^{\prime\prime}_{q}(u)
whose entries are polynomials in an indeterminate
u
. To reduce clutter, throughout this subsection, we write

(4.6)

m=m(q),\qquad M=M(q),\qquad\delta=\delta(q),\qquad C=C(q).

For
0\leq h\leq\delta
, we introduce the notation

\beta(h)=n^{r-h}\qquad\text{and}\qquad B(h)=\beta(0)+\beta(1)+\cdots+\beta(h-1),

where we take
B(0)=0
. Let

v_{q}(u)=e_{a(q,1)}+\sum_{f=2}^{f_{q}}u^{\beta(\delta)(f_{q}+1-f)}e_{a(q,f)}.

If
f_{q}>1
, we can rewrite this using (
4.5
) as

(4.7)

v_{q}(u)=e_{a(q,1)}+u^{\beta(\delta)}e_{a(\sigma^{-1}(q),1)}+\sum_{f=2}^{f_{q}-1}u^{\beta(\delta)(f_{q}+1-f)}e_{a(q,f)}.

Next, assuming
f_{q}>1
, let

(4.8)

v^{\prime}_{q}(u)=\sum_{h=0}^{\delta}(-1)^{h}u^{B(\delta-h)}v_{\sigma^{h}(q)}(u).

Note that
\delta(\sigma^{h}(q))=\delta-h
, so (
4.7
) yields

(4.9)

v_{\sigma^{h}(q)}(u)=e_{a(\sigma^{h}(q),1)}+u^{\beta(\delta-h)}e_{a(\sigma^{h-1}(q),1)}\\
{}+\sum_{f=2}^{f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}-1}u^{\beta(\delta-h)(f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}+1-f)}e_{a(\sigma^{h}(q),f)}.

Expand (
4.8
) using (
4.9
) to get

(4.10)

v^{\prime}_{q}(u)=\sum_{h=0}^{\delta}(-1)^{h}u^{B(\delta-h)}\Big(e_{a(\sigma^{h}(q),1)}+u^{\beta(\delta-h)}e_{a(\sigma^{h-1}(q),1)}\Big)\\
+\sum_{h=0}^{\delta}\sum_{f=2}^{f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}-1}(-1)^{h}u^{B(\delta-h)}u^{\beta(\delta-h)(f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}+1-f)}e_{a(\sigma^{h}(q),f)}.

Since
B(\delta-h)+\beta(\delta-h)=B(\delta-h+1)
, the first summation above is equal to

\sum_{h=0}^{\delta}(-1)^{h}\Big(u^{B(\delta-h)}e_{a(\sigma^{h}(q),1)}+u^{B(\delta-(h-1))}e_{a(\sigma^{h-1}(q),1)}\Big).

Most terms in this summation cancel, and we can rewrite (
4.10
) as

(4.11)

v^{\prime}_{q}(u)=(-1)^{\delta}e_{a(M,1)}+u^{B(\delta+1)}e_{a(\sigma^{-1}(q),1)}\\
+\sum_{h=0}^{\delta}\sum_{f=2}^{f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}-1}(-1)^{h}u^{B(\delta-h)}u^{\beta(\delta-h)(f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}+1-f)}e_{a(\sigma^{h}(q),f)}.

In the special case where
q=m
, so that
\sigma^{-1}(q)=M
, (
4.11
) simplifies to

(4.12)

v^{\prime}_{m}(u)=\Big((-1)^{C-1}+u^{B(C)}\Big)e_{a(M,1)}\\
+\sum_{h=0}^{C-1}\sum_{f=2}^{f_{\sigma^{h}(m)}-1}u^{B(C-1-h)}u^{\beta(C-1-h)(f_{\sigma^{h}(m)}+1-f)}e_{a(\sigma^{h}(m),f)}.

For another description of
v^{\prime}_{q}(u)
, separate the
h=0
terms in (
4.11
) and combine with
u^{B(\delta+1)}e_{a(\sigma^{-1}(q),1)}=u^{B(\delta)}u^{\beta(\delta)}e_{a(q,f_{q})}
to obtain

(4.13)

v^{\prime}_{q}(u)=(-1)^{\delta}e_{a(M,1)}+u^{B(\delta)}\Big(v_{q}(u)-e_{a(q,1)}\Big)\\
+\sum_{h=1}^{\delta}\sum_{f=2}^{f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}-1}(-1)^{h}u^{B(\delta-h)}u^{\beta(\delta-h)(f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}+1-f)}e_{a(\sigma^{h}(q),f)}.

Finally, if
f_{q}>1
and
q\neq m
, let

v^{\prime\prime}_{q}(u)=(1+(-1)^{C-1}u^{B(C)})v^{\prime}_{q}(u)+(-1)^{C+\delta}v^{\prime}_{m}(u).

It will be important to rewrite this as

(4.14)

v^{\prime\prime}_{q}(u)=(1+(-1)^{C-1}u^{B(C)})\big(v^{\prime}_{q}(u)-(-1)^{\delta}e_{a(M,1)}\big)\\
+(-1)^{C+\delta}\Big(v^{\prime}_{m}(u)-\big((-1)^{C-1}+u^{B(C)}\big)e_{a(M,1)}\Big).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
1\leq j\leq n
. Suppose
f_{q}>1
.

(1)

If

occurs with nonzero coefficient in

, then

(2)

If

or

, then

Immediate from the definitions and Lemma
4.1
.

4.3.
Evaluation at
u=0

In this subsection, we retain the convention from (
4.6
). For a positive integer
N
, form the
n\times n
matrix of Laurent polynomials

\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})=\left[\begin{smallmatrix}u^{-N}\\
&\ddots\\
&&u^{-N}\\
&&&1\\
&&&&\ddots\\
&&&&&1\end{smallmatrix}\right]

defined as in (
4.1
). We will consider the vector
\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})v_{q}(u)
and related expressions. Because these vectors involve Laurent polynomials, it may not be possible to evaluate them at
u=0
. We will prove a large number of lemmas describing cases in which evaluation at
u=0
is possible.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose
\phi(q,i)=1
. If
f_{q}=1
, suppose
i\leq a(q,1)
. For
N>0
, we have

\big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot v_{q}(u)\big)\big|_{u=0}=e_{a(q,1)}.

Proof.

If
f_{q}=1
, then
v_{q}(u)=e_{a(q,1)}
, so if
i\leq a(q,1)
, we have
\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})v_{q}(u)=e_{a(q,1)}
, whence the claim follows. Now suppose
f_{q}>1
. Since
2>\phi(q,i)
, we have
i\leq a(q,2)
by Lemma
4.1
(
2
), and then Lemma
4.2
implies that
\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})v_{q}(u)=v_{q}(u)
. The fact that
v_{q}(u)|_{u=0}=e_{a(q,1)}
is clear from the definition.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

If
f_{q}=1
and
i>a(q,1)
, then
\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})v_{q}(u)=u^{-N}e_{a(q,1)}
cannot be evaluated at

. In this case, the relevant statement we use later is

\big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot u^{N}v_{q}(u)\big)\big|_{u=0}=e_{a(q,1)}.

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose
2\leq\phi(q,i)\leq f_{q}
. For
N\gg 0
, we have

\Big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot u^{N-\beta(\delta)(f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i))}(v_{q}(u)-e_{a(q,1)})\Big)\Big|_{u=0}=e_{a(q,\phi(q,i))}.

Proof.

On the left-hand side,
e_{a(q,1)}
does not appear. For
f\geq 2
, the coefficient of
e_{a(q,f)}
is as follows (using Lemma
4.1
(
2
)):

\begin{cases}u^{\beta(\delta)(\phi(q,i)-f)}&\text{if $a(q,f)<i$, or equivalently, if $f\leq\phi(q,i)$,}\\
u^{N+\beta(\delta)(\phi(q,i)-f)}&\text{if $a(q,f)\geq i$, or equivalently, if $f>\phi(q,i)$.}\end{cases}

If
N>-\beta(\delta)(\phi(q,i)-f)
for all
f
, then these coefficients are always of the form
u^{\text{nonnegative integer}}
, with
u^{0}
occurring only when
f=\phi(q,i)
. The lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 4.6
.

Suppose
2\leq\phi(q,i)\leq f_{q}
. For
N\gg 0
, we have

\Big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot u^{N-B(\delta)-\beta(\delta)(f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i))}(v^{\prime}_{q}(u)-(-1)^{\delta}e_{a(M,1)})\Big)\big|_{u=0}=e_{a(q,\phi(q,i))}.

Proof.

First, if
\delta=0
, then
q=M
and
v^{\prime}_{q}(u)=v_{q}(u)
, so the claim reduces to Lemma
4.5
. Now suppose
1\leq h\leq\delta
and
2\leq f\leq f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}-1
. We will prove below that

(4.15)

B(\delta-h)+\beta(\delta-h)(f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}+1-f)>B(\delta)+\beta(\delta)(f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i)).

This inequality implies

\Big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot u^{N-B(\delta)-\beta(\delta)(f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i))}\\
{}\cdot u^{B(\delta-h)}u^{\beta(\delta-h)(f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}+1-f)}e_{a(\sigma^{h}(q),f)}\Big)\Big|_{u=0}=0.

The lemma then follows by combining this observation with (
4.13
) and Lemma
4.5
.

Let us now prove (
4.15
). First, consider the case
h=1
. Using
B(\delta)=\beta(\delta-1)+B(\delta-1)
, the inequality becomes

n^{r-\delta+1}(f_{\sigma(q)}+1-f)>n^{r-\delta+1}+n^{r-\delta}(f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i)).

This can be rewritten as

n(f_{M}-1-f)+n>f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i).

Under our assumptions,
f_{M}-1-f\geq 0
and
f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i)\leq f_{q}-1\leq n
, so we at least have

n(f_{M}-1-f)+n\geq f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i).

It remains to show that equality is not possible. For equality to hold, it is necessary that
f_{q}=n+1
. By Lemma
4.1
(
6
), we then have
\sigma(q)=q
. This contradicts
\delta>0
, so (
4.15
) holds in this case.

Now assume
h>1
. Under our assumptions,
f_{\sigma^{h}(q)}+1-f\geq 2
and
f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i)\leq f_{q}-1\leq n
. It is therefore enough to prove that

B(\delta-h)+2\beta(\delta-h)>B(\delta)+n\beta(\delta).

If
h=\delta=2
, this becomes

2n^{r}>n^{r}+2n^{r-1},

which follows from
2=\delta<r<n
. If
h=\delta>2
, the inequality becomes

2n^{r}>n^{r}+n^{r-1}+\cdots+2n^{r-\delta+1},

which is clear by interpreting both sides in base
n
and using
n>r>\delta>2
. If
h=\delta-1
, then the inequality becomes

n^{r}+2n^{r-1}>n^{r}+n^{r-1}+\cdots+2n^{r-\delta+1},

which is again clear in base
n
.

Now assume
1<h<\delta-1
. A straightforward calculation shows that the inequality is equivalent to

(4.16)

n^{r-\delta+h}>n^{r-\delta+h-1}+n^{r-\delta+h-2}+\cdots+n^{r-\delta+2}+2n^{r-\delta+1}.

As
n>r>\delta>1+h>2
, the above inequality is an obvious statement in base
n
. Therefore, (
4.15
) holds.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

Suppose
2\leq\phi(q,i)\leq f_{q}
and
i\leq a(M,1)
. For
N\gg 0
, we have

\Big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot u^{N-B(\delta)-\beta(\delta)(f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i))}v^{\prime}_{q}(u)\Big)\Big|_{u=0}=e_{a(q,\phi(q,i))}.

Proof.

Since
i\leq a(M,1)
, we have

\big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot u^{N-B(\delta)-\beta(\delta)(f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i))}e_{a(M,1)}\big)\big|_{u=0}\\
=\big(u^{N-B(\delta)-\beta(\delta)(f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i))}e_{a(M,1)}\big)\big|_{u=0}=0.

The result then follows from Lemma
4.6
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Suppose
f_{q}>1
and
i>a(M,1)
. For
N>0
, we have

\big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot(-1)^{C-1}u^{N}v^{\prime}_{m}(u)\big)\big|_{u=0}=e_{a(m,\phi(m,i))}.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.2
,
\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot(-1)^{C-1}u^{N}v^{\prime}_{m}(u)=(-1)^{C-1}v^{\prime}_{m}(u)
. It is clear from (
4.12
) that
(-1)^{C-1}v^{\prime}_{m}(u)|_{u=0}=e_{a(M,1)}
. Finally,
a(M,1)=a(m,f_{m})=a(m,\phi(m,i))
by (
4.5
) and Lemma
4.1
(
5
).
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

Suppose
f_{q}>1
. For
N>0
, we have

\Big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot u^{N-B(\delta+1)}\big(v^{\prime}_{m}(u)-\big((-1)^{C-1}+u^{B(C)}\big)e_{a(M,1)}\big)\Big)\Big|_{u=0}=0.

Proof.

We will show below that if
0\leq h\leq C-1
and
2\leq f\leq f_{\sigma^{h}(m)}-1
, then

(4.17)

B(C-1-h)+\beta(C-1-h)(f_{\sigma^{h}(m)}+1-f)>B(\delta+1).

This claim implies that

\Big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot u^{N-B(\delta+1)}\cdot u^{B(C-1-h)}u^{\beta(C-1-h)(f_{\sigma^{h}(m)}+1-f)}e_{a(\sigma^{h}(m),f)}\Big)\Big|_{u=0}=0.

The lemma follows by combining this with (
4.12
).

Let us prove (
4.17
). Under our assumptions,
f_{\sigma^{h}(m)}+1-f\geq 2
, so it is enough to prove that
B(C-1-h)+2\beta(C-1-h)>B(\delta+1)
, or

\beta(0)+\cdots+\beta(C-h-2)+2\beta(C-h-1)>\beta(0)+\cdots+\beta(\delta).

If
\delta\leq C-h-1
, this is obvious. If
\delta>C-h-1
, our claim reduces to

n^{r-C+h+1}>n^{r-C+h}+n^{r-C+h-1}+\cdots+n^{r-\delta},

and this holds for any
n\geq 2
. Thus, (
4.17
) is proved.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Suppose
f_{q}>1
and
q\neq m
. For
N\gg 0
, we have

\Big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot u^{N-B(\delta+1)}v^{\prime\prime}_{q}(u)\Big)\Big|_{u=0}=e_{a(q,\phi(q,i))}.

Proof.

Since
q\neq m
, we have
\delta<C-1
, so
B(\delta+1)<B(C)
. Thus

(4.18)

\big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})\cdot u^{N-B(\delta+1)}u^{B(C)}(v^{\prime}_{q}(u)-(-1)^{\delta}e_{a(M,1)})\big)\big|_{u=0}=0.

Now expand
v^{\prime\prime}_{q}(u)
using (
4.14
). The lemma follows from (the
\phi(q,i)=f_{q}
case of) Lemma
4.6
, Lemma
4.9
, and (
4.18
).
∎

Theorem 4.11
.

If
1\leq t\leq n-1
, then
X_{\varpi_{t}}=\mathbf{Gr}_{\varpi_{t}}
.

Proof.

Let
r=n-t
, and let
x
be a
\varpi_{n-r}
-permissible alcove. Carry out the constructions of §§
4.1
–
4.2
with respect to this alcove to obtain a sequence of vectors
v_{1}(u),\ldots,v_{r}(u)
.

For each pair
(q,i)
with
1\leq q\leq r
and
1\leq i\leq n+1
, one may invoke the lemmas in §
4.3
. Some of these involve a condition on a positive integer
N
. Choose
N
to sufficiently large for all of these lemmas for all pairs
(q,i)
. Then, define a vector
\hat{v}^{(i)}_{q}(u)
as follows:

\hat{v}_{q}^{(i)}(u)=\hbox{\small$\begin{cases}u^{N}v_{q}(u)&\text{if $f_{q}=1$ and $i>a(q,1)$,}\\
v_{q}(u)&\text{if $\phi(q,i)=1$, and $f_{q}>1$ or $i\leq a(q,1)$,}\\
u^{N-B(\delta)-\beta(\delta)(f_{q}+1-\phi(q,i))}v^{\prime}_{q}(u)&\text{if $2\leq\phi(q,i)\leq f_{q}$ and $i\leq a(M,1)$,}\\
(-1)^{C-1}u^{N}v^{\prime}_{q}(u)&\text{if $f_{q}>1$ and $i>a(M,1)$ and $q=m$,}\\
u^{N-B(\delta+1)}v^{\prime\prime}_{q}(u)&\text{if $f_{q}>1$ and $i>a(M,1)$ and $q\neq m$.}\end{cases}$}

(By Lemma
4.1
(
5
), the five cases above are mutually exclusive.) Let
A^{(i)}(u)
be the
n\times r
matrix whose columns are
\hat{v}^{(i)}_{1}(u),\ldots,\hat{v}^{(i)}_{r}(u)
. In particular, the columns of
A^{(1)}(u)
are
v_{1}(u),\ldots,v_{r}(u)
.

From the definitions of
v^{\prime}_{q}(u)
and
v^{\prime\prime}_{q}(u)
, we see that in all cases,
\hat{v}^{(i)}_{q}
is a linear combination (with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}[u]
) of
v_{1}(u),\ldots,v_{r}(u)
. In other words, there exists an
r\times r
matrix
B^{(i)}(u)
such that

(4.19)

A^{(i)}(u)=A^{(1)}(u)B^{(i)}(u)\qquad\text{for $2\leq i\leq n+1$.}

By Remark
4.4
and Lemmas
4.3
,
4.7
,
4.8
, and
4.10
, we have

\big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})A^{(i)}(u)\big)\big|_{u=0}=\begin{array}[]{c}\text{the matrix with columns}\\
e_{a(1,\phi(1,i))},e_{a(2,\phi(2,i))},\ldots,e_{a(r,\phi(r,i))}\end{array}.

From the definitions, we have
a(q,\phi(q,i))=j^{(i)}_{q}
, so

\big(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})A^{(i)}(u)\big)\big|_{u=0}=\text{the matrix with columns $\{e_{j}\mid j\in J^{(i)}\}$.}

In particular, the columns of
(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})A^{(i)}(u))|_{u=0}
are linearly independent. Their span determines a point of
G(n,r)
, and then a point of
\mathcal{G}r_{\varpi_{n-r}}
via the isomorphism of Example
2.2
. Indeed, from (
4.4
), we see that

(4.20)

\spn(\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-u^{-N})A^{(i)}(u))|_{u=0}\in G(n,r)\quad\text{corresponds to}\quad\mathbf{L}^{x^{(i)}-\varpi_{i-1}}\in\mathcal{G}r_{\varpi_{n-r}}.

(Here, and below, the “span” of a matrix means its column span.)

Let us now specialize
u
to other elements in
\mathbb{C}
. There is a Zariski open subset
U\subset\mathbb{C}
containing
0
such that for
z\in U
, the matrices

(A^{(1)}(u)\big)\big|_{u=z},(\bar{\theta}_{1}(-u^{-N})A^{(1)}(u)\big)\big|_{u=z},\ldots,(\bar{\theta}_{n-1}(-u^{-N})A^{(n)}(u)\big)\big|_{u=z}

all have rank
r
. Define a map
\mathbf{A}:U\to\mathbb{C}\times G(n,r)\times\cdots\times G(n,r)
by

\mathbf{A}(z)=\Big(-z^{N},\spn(A^{(1)}(u)\big)\big|_{u=z},\spn(\bar{\theta}_{1}(-u^{-N})A^{(1)}(u)\big)\big|_{u=z},\ldots,\\
\spn(\bar{\theta}_{n-1}(-u^{-N})A^{(n)}(u)\big)\big|_{u=z}\Big).

Identifying
G(n,r)
with
\mathcal{G}r_{\varpi_{n-r}}\subset\mathcal{G}r
, we may regard
\mathbf{A}
as a map
U\to\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{G}r\times\cdots\times\mathcal{G}r
.
When
z\in U\cap\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, it follows from (
4.19
) that

\spn A^{(1)}(z)=\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-z^{N})\cdot\spn\bar{\theta}_{i-1}(-z^{-N})A^{(i)}(z)\qquad\text{for $1\leq i\leq n+1$.}

In other words, for
z\in U\cap\mathbb{C}^{\times}
,
\mathbf{A}(z)
is lattice family in
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\varpi_{n-r}}}\cap\mathbf{Gr}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
. Passing to closures, we deduce that
\mathbf{A}(0)
also lies in
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\varpi_{n-r}}}
. By (
4.20
),

\mathbf{A}(0)=(0,\mathbf{L}^{x^{(1)}},\mathbf{L}^{x^{(2)}-\varpi_{1}},\ldots,\mathbf{L}^{x^{(n)}-\varpi_{n-1}}),

and this corresponds under the isomorphism of Lemma
3.1
to the lattice chain
\underline{\mathbf{L}}^{x}\in\mathcal{F}l
. We have established (
3.4
), and the theorem follows.
∎

5.
Alcoves and dominant coweights

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
, and let
x=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})
be a
\lambda
-permissible alcove. By definition, this means that
\mathsf{dom}(x^{(i)}-\varpi_{i-1})\preceq\lambda
for each
i
. That is, there are permutations
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that

\delta_{i}(x^{(i)}-\varpi_{i-1})\preceq\lambda.

Most of the work in this section is devoted to showing that each
\delta_{i}
can be chosen in such a way that it differs “minimally” from its neighbors
\delta_{i-1}
and
\delta_{i+1}
. For a precise statement, see Lemma
5.8
. As an application, we will prove a result about alcoves (Theorem
5.10
) that is a combinatorial precursor to the convolution geometry that will be studied in Section
6
.

5.1.
A lemma on dominant coweights

The following fact is undoubtedly well known, but we include a proof as we were unable to find a reference for it.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mu,\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
be dominant coweights such that
\lambda=\mu+\varpi_{t}
, where
1\leq t\leq n-1
. If
\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
is another dominant coweight satisfying
\lambda^{\prime}\preceq\lambda
, then
\mathsf{dom}(\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t})\preceq\mu
.

Proof.

It is immediate from the assumptions that

(5.1)

\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t}\preceq\lambda-\varpi_{t}=\mu.

If
\lambda^{\prime}_{t}>\lambda^{\prime}_{t+1}
, then
\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t}
is already dominant, so there is nothing to prove.

For the remainder of the proof, we assume that
\lambda^{\prime}_{t}=\lambda^{\prime}_{t+1}
. Let
m=\lambda^{\prime}_{t}
. Let
a
be the smallest integer such that
\lambda^{\prime}_{a}=m
, and let
b
be the largest integer such that
\lambda^{\prime}_{b}=m
. Thus,
a\leq t<t+1\leq b
, and

\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\lambda^{\prime}_{2},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{a-1},m,m,\ldots,m,\lambda^{\prime}_{b+1},\lambda^{\prime}_{b+2},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n})

with

\lambda^{\prime}_{1}\geq\lambda^{\prime}_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda^{\prime}_{a-1}>m>\lambda^{\prime}_{b+1}\geq\lambda^{\prime}_{b+2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda^{\prime}_{n}.

We have

\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t}=(\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-1,\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{a-1}-1,\underbrace{m-1,\ldots,m-1}_{\text{$t-a+1$ terms}},\underbrace{m,\ldots,m}_{\text{$b-t$ terms}},\lambda^{\prime}_{b+1},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n}),

and hence

\mathsf{dom}(\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t})=(\lambda^{\prime}_{1}-1,\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{a-1}-1,\underbrace{m,\ldots,m}_{\text{$b-t$ terms}},\underbrace{m-1,\ldots,m-1}_{\text{$t-a+1$ terms}},\lambda^{\prime}_{b+1},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{n}).

Our goal is to prove that
\mathsf{dom}(\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t})\preceq\mu
, or

(5.2)

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{dom}(\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t})_{i}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}\qquad\text{for $1\leq k\leq n$,}

with equality when
k=n
. The equality case follows from
|\mathsf{dom}(\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t})|=|\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t}|=|\lambda-\varpi_{t}|=|\mu|
.
Before proving the inequalities, we set up some additional notation. Since
\lambda=\mu+\varpi_{t}
, we have

\lambda_{i}=\begin{cases}\mu_{i}+1&\text{if $1\leq i\leq t$,}\\
\mu_{i}&\text{if $t+1\leq i\leq n$.}\end{cases}

Since
\mu
is dominant, this implies that

(5.3)

\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{t}>\lambda_{t+1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}.

For
1\leq i\leq n
, let
f_{i}=\lambda_{i}-\lambda^{\prime}_{i}
. Since
\lambda^{\prime}\preceq\lambda
, we have

(5.4)

\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}\geq 0\qquad\text{for $1\leq k\leq n$.}

Since
\lambda^{\prime}_{a}=\lambda^{\prime}_{a+1}=\cdots=\lambda^{\prime}_{b}
, it follows from (
5.3
) that

(5.5)

f_{a}\geq f_{a+1}\geq\cdots\geq f_{t}>f_{t+1}\geq f_{t+2}\geq\cdots\geq f_{b}.

We are now ready to prove (
5.2
). We break up the problem into cases as follows.

Case 1.
1\leq k\leq a-1
.
In this case, (
5.2
) simplifies to

\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-1)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda_{i}-1)\qquad\text{or, equivalently,}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-k\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}-k,

and this holds because
\lambda^{\prime}\preceq\lambda
.

Case 2.
a\leq k\leq b
.

Assuming
1\leq i\leq b
, we have

\mathsf{dom}(\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t})_{i}=\begin{cases}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-1&\text{if $1\leq i\leq a-1$ or $b-t+a\leq i\leq b$,}\\
\lambda^{\prime}_{i}&\text{if $a\leq i\leq b-t+a-1$,}\end{cases}

so the left-hand side of (
5.2
) simplifies to

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{dom}(\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t})_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-1)+|\{i\mid\text{$1\leq i\leq k$ and $a\leq i\leq b-t+a-1$}\}|\\
=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}-k+\min\{k,b-t+a-1\}-a+1=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}+\min\{-a+1,b-t-k\}.

Therefore, (
5.2
) simplifies to

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}+\min\{-a+1,b-t-k\}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mu_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}-\min\{t,k\}.

Since

\min\{t,k\}+\min\{-a+1,b-t-k\}=\min\{t-a+1,k-a+1,b-t,b-k\},

we see that (
5.2
) is equivalent to

(5.6)

\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}\geq\min\{t-a+1,k-a+1,b-t,b-k\}.

To prove this, we consider various subcases.

Case 2a.
k\leq t
and
f_{k}\geq 1
.

Using (
5.4
) and (
5.5
), we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{a-1}f_{i}+f_{a}+\cdots+f_{k}\geq\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{a-1}f_{i}}_{{}\geq 0}{}+(k-a+1)\underbrace{f_{k}}_{{}\geq 1}{}\geq k-a+1.

Case 2b.
k\leq t
and
f_{k}\leq 0
.

By (
5.5
), we have
0\geq f_{k+1}\geq\cdots\geq f_{t}
, and
0>f_{t+1}
. Since
\sum_{i=1}^{b}f_{i}\geq 0
by (
5.4
), we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}\geq-\sum_{i=k+1}^{b}f_{i}=-\sum_{i=k+1}^{t}f_{i}-f_{t+1}-f_{t+2}-\cdots-f_{b}\\
\geq\underbrace{-\sum_{i=k+1}^{t}f_{i}}_{{}\geq 0}{}+(b-t)\underbrace{(-f_{t+1})}_{{}\geq 1}\geq b-t.

Case 2c.
k>t
and
f_{k}\geq 0
.

By (
5.5
), we have
f_{a}\geq\cdots\geq f_{t}>0
and
f_{t+1}\geq\cdots\geq f_{k}\geq 0
, so

\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}=\underbrace{\sum_{i=1}^{a-1}f_{i}}_{{}\geq 0}{}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=a}^{t}\underbrace{f_{i}}_{{}\geq 1}}_{{}\geq t-a+1}{}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=t+1}^{k}f_{i}}_{{}\geq 0}{}\geq t-a+1.

Case 2d.
k>t
and
f_{k}\leq-1
.

We have
\sum_{i=1}^{b}f_{i}\geq 0
, and hence

\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_{i}\geq\sum_{i=k+1}^{b}\underbrace{(-f_{i})}_{{}\geq 1}\geq b-k.

Case 3.
b+1\leq k\leq n
.

In this case, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathsf{dom}(\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t})_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}(\lambda^{\prime}-\varpi_{t})_{i},

so (
5.2
) holds as a consequence of (
5.1
).
∎

5.2.
Rotation of alcoves

Let
\mathsf{c}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the
n
-cycle
(1\,2\,\cdots\,n)
, i.e.,

\mathsf{c}(i)=\begin{cases}i+1&\text{if $1\leq i\leq n-1$,}\\
1&\text{if $i=n$.}\end{cases}

Next, let
x=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})
be an alcove. We define a new sequence of coweights
\mathrm{rot}(x)=(\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)},\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(2)},\ldots,\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(n)})
by the formula

\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(i)}=\begin{cases}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(x^{(i+1)}-\varpi_{1})&\text{if $1\leq i\leq n-1$,}\\
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(x^{(1)})+\varpi_{n-1}&\text{if $i=n$.}\end{cases}

The next two lemmas follow from the definitions; we omit their proofs.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
x
be an alcove.

(1)

The sequence
\mathrm{rot}(x)
is an alcove.

(2)

If
x
is
\lambda
-permissible for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
, then
\mathrm{rot}(x)
is also
\lambda
-permissible.

(3)

For
1\leq j\leq n
, we have

\mathrm{rot}^{j}(x)^{(i)}=\begin{cases}\mathsf{c}^{-j}(x^{(i+j)}-\varpi_{j})&\text{if $1\leq i\leq n-j$,}\\
\mathsf{c}^{-j}(x^{(i-n+j)}-\varpi_{i-n+j-1})+\varpi_{i-1}&\text{if $n-j<i\leq n$.}\end{cases}

(4)

We have
\mathrm{rot}^{n}(x)=x
.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any alcove
x
, we have
\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{rot}(x))=\mathsf{c}^{-1}\mathsf{P}(x)\mathsf{c}
.

5.3.
Partial orders on indices

Let
x
be an alcove. We define a partial order
\trianglelefteq_{x}
on the set
\{1,\ldots,n\}
by the rule shown in Table
1
.

For
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we declare that
i\trianglelefteq_{x}j
if one of the following holds:

\begin{array}[]{lll@{\text{and }}ll@{\text{and }}ll@{\text{and }}l}\hline\cr(1)&x^{(1)}_{i}>x^{(1)}_{j}\\
(2)&x^{(1)}_{i}=x^{(1)}_{j}&&\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(i)<i&&\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(j)<j&&i\geq j\\
(3)&x^{(1)}_{i}=x^{(1)}_{j}&&\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(i)<i&&\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(j)\geq j\\
(4)&x^{(1)}_{i}=x^{(1)}_{j}&&\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(i)=i&&\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(j)=j&&i=j\\
(5)&x^{(1)}_{i}=x^{(1)}_{j}&&\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(i)=i&&\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(j)>j\\
(6)&x^{(1)}_{i}=x^{(1)}_{j}&&\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(i)>i&&\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(j)>j&&i\geq j\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

Table 1.
Definition of
\trianglelefteq_{x}

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
x
be an alcove, and let
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

(1)

If
\mathsf{P}(x)(1)=1
, then
i\trianglelefteq_{x}j
if and only if
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
.

(2)

If
i,j\neq\mathsf{P}(x)(1)
, then
i\trianglelefteq_{x}j
if and only if
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
.

(3)

If
\mathsf{P}(x)(1)\trianglelefteq_{x}j
, then
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(\mathsf{P}(x)(1))\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
.

Proof.

(
1
) Assume first that
\mathsf{P}(x)(1)=1
. In this case,
x^{(2)}=x^{(1)}+\varpi_{1}
, so it follows from the definition that
\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}=\mathsf{c}^{-1}(x^{(1)})
, so for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have

(5.7)

\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)}=x^{(1)}_{i}.

Next, we claim that for all
i
, we have

(5.8)

\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{rot}(x))^{-1}(\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i))<\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(i)<i,

and likewise with “
<
” replaced by “
=
” or “
>
.” Since
\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{rot}(x))^{-1}(\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i))=\mathsf{c}^{-1}(\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(i))
, our claim is equivalent to the assertion that

\mathsf{c}^{-1}(\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(i))<\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(i)<i.

If
2\leq i\leq n
, this assertion is obvious. If
i=1
, then the assumption that
\mathsf{P}(x)(1)=1
means that both inequalities above are false, so the “if and only if” assertion is true. Similar reasoning applies to the “
=
” and “
>
” cases.

Combining (
5.7
), (
5.8
), and the definition of
\trianglelefteq_{x}
, we see that conditions (1), (3), (4), or (5) of Table
1
apply to
i\trianglelefteq_{x}j
if and only if the same condition applies to
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
. Conditions (2) and (6) are trickier because they involve the condition
i\geq j
. However, these conditions also require
\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(i)\neq i
and
\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(j)\neq j
, which means that
i,j\neq 1
. For
i,j\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
, we have
i\geq j
if and only if
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\geq\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
. Thus, in all cases,
i\trianglelefteq_{x}j
if and only if
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
.

(
2
)
Let
r=\mathsf{P}(x)(1)
. If
r=1
, then this part of the proposition is subsumed by part (
1
). Assume for the remainder of this part of the proof that
r>1
. Then

(5.9)

\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)}=\begin{cases}x^{(1)}_{i}&\text{if $i\neq 1,r$,}\\
x^{(1)}_{1}-1&\text{if $i=1$,}\\
x^{(1)}_{r}+1&\text{if $i=r$.}\end{cases}

We consider various cases as follows.

Case 1.
i,j\neq 1,r
.
Under this assumption, we have
\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)}=x^{(1)}_{i}
and
\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)}=x^{(1)}_{j}
. The reasoning in the proof of part (
1
) applies verbatim to show that
i\trianglelefteq_{x}j
if and only if
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
.

Case 2.
i=1
,
j\neq 1,r
, and
x^{(1)}_{1}=x^{(1)}_{j}
.
Our assumptions imply that
\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(1)\neq 1
, and hence
\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(1)>1
, and also that
1<j
. So
i=1
and
j
cannot satisfy any of the conditions in Table
1
; we necessarily have
1\ntrianglelefteq_{x}j
. On the other hand, we have

\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{n}=x^{(1)}_{1}-1<x^{(1)}_{j}=\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)},

so we also necessarily have
n\ntrianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
, as desired.

Case 3.
i=1
,
j\neq 1,r
, and
x^{(1)}_{1}<x^{(1)}_{j}
.

In this case, we again have
1\ntrianglelefteq_{x}j
and
n\ntrianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
.

Case 4.
i=1
,
j\neq 1,r
, and
x^{(1)}_{1}>x^{(1)}_{j}
.

In this case, we necessarily have
1\trianglelefteq_{x}j
, so we must prove that
n\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
. If
x^{(1)}_{1}-x^{(1)}_{j}\geq 2
, then

\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{n}=x^{(1)}_{1}-1>x^{(1)}_{j}=\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)},

and we are done. On the other hand, suppose
x^{(1)}_{1}-x^{(1)}_{j}=1
, so that

\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{n}=\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)}.

We have
\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{rot}(x))^{-1}(n)\neq n
and hence
\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{rot}(x))^{-1}(n)<n
. We obviously have
n>\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
, so
n\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
holds by either condition (2) or (3) in Table
1
.

Case 5.
i\neq 1,r
,
j=1
, and
x^{(1)}_{i}=x^{(1)}_{1}
.

Our assumptions imply that
\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(1)>1
and
i>1
. Depending on how
\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(i)
compares with
i
, we see that one of conditions (3), (5), or (6) in Table
1
must hold, and we necessarily have
i\trianglelefteq_{x}j
. On the other hand, we have

\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)}=x^{(1)}_{i}>x^{(1)}_{1}-1=\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{n},

so by condition (1) of Table
1
, we necessarily have
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}n
.

Case 6.
i\neq 1,r
,
j=1
, and
x^{(1)}_{i}>x^{(1)}_{1}
.

In this case, we again have
i\trianglelefteq_{x}j
and
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}n
.

Case 7.
i\neq 1,r
,
j=1
, and
x^{(1)}_{i}<x^{(1)}_{1}
.

In this case, we have
i\ntrianglelefteq_{x}1
, so we must prove that
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\ntrianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}n
. If
x^{(1)}_{1}-x^{(1)}_{i}\geq 2
, then we have

\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)}=x^{(1)}_{i}<x^{(1)}_{1}-1=\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{n},

so
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\ntrianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}n
as desired. On the other hand, suppose
x^{(1)}_{1}-x^{(1)}_{i}=1
, so that
\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)}=\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{n}
. Since
\mathsf{P}(x)^{-1}(1)\neq 1
, we have
\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{rot}(x))^{-1}(n)\neq n
, and hence
\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{rot}(x))^{-1}(n)<n
. We also have
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)<n
, so none of conditions (2)–(6) in Table
1
apply, and we conclude that
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\ntrianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}n
.

Case 8.
i=j
.

This case is trivial.

Conclusion.
Cases 1–8 cover all possible situations in part (
2
), so this part of the proposition is now proved.

(
3
) We again let
r=\mathsf{P}(x)(1)
, and we assume that
r>1
. The formula in (
5.9
) remains valid. This time, we are only proving one implication, not an “if and only if” statement. Assume
j
is such that
r\trianglelefteq_{x}j
. We may further assume without loss of generality that
j\neq r
. From Table
1
, we must have
x^{(1)}_{r}\geq x^{(1)}_{j}
. Since

\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{r-1}=x^{(1)}_{r}+1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)}=\begin{cases}x^{(1)}_{j}&\text{if $j\neq 1$,}\\
x^{(1)}_{j}-1&\text{if $j=1$,}\end{cases}

we have
\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{r-1}>\mathrm{rot}(x)^{(1)}_{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)}
in all cases, and hence
r-1\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
.
∎

5.4.
Total orders on indices

Let
x
be an alcove. For an integer
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, let

\mathsf{Fix}(x,m)=\{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\mid\text{$x^{(1)}_{i}=m$ and $\mathsf{P}(x)(i)=i$}\}.

In other words,
\mathsf{Fix}(x,m)
is the set of
\mathsf{P}(x)
-fixed points whose corresponding coordinate in
x^{(1)}
has value
m
. The proof of the following lemma is a straightforward consequence of this definition.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
x
be an alcove.

(1)

Two distinct elements
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
are incomparable under
\trianglelefteq_{x}
if and only if there is an
m\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
i,j\in\mathsf{Fix}(x,m)
.

(2)

Let
i,j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Suppose that
i,j\in\mathsf{Fix}(x,m)
but
k\notin\mathsf{Fix}(x,m)
, so that
i
and
j
are each comparable to
k
. We have
i\trianglelefteq_{x}k
(resp.
i\trianglerighteq_{x}k
) if and only if
j\trianglelefteq_{x}k
(resp.
j\trianglerighteq_{x}k
).

(3)

We have

\mathsf{Fix}(\mathrm{rot}(x),m)=\mathsf{c}^{-1}(\mathsf{Fix}(x,m)).

Lemma
5.5
has the following consequence:

(5.10)

Refining
\trianglelefteq_{x}
to a total order on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
is equivalent to choosing a total order on
\mathsf{Fix}(x,m)
for each
m\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Suppose now that we have a refinement of
\trianglelefteq_{x}
to a total order
\blacktriangleleft_{x}
, and a refinement of
\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}
to a total order
\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}
. We say that the pair

\blacktriangleleft_{x},\quad\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}

is
compatible
if for each
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, the total order on
\mathsf{Fix}(x,m)
induced by
\blacktriangleleft_{x}
agrees with that on
\mathsf{Fix}(\mathrm{rot}(x),m)
induced by
\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}
under the bijection of Lemma
5.5
(
3
). Obviously, given a total order
\blacktriangleleft_{x}
refining
\trianglelefteq_{x}
, there is a unique compatible total order
\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}
refining
\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
x
be an alcove, and let

\blacktriangleleft_{x},\quad\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}

be a compatible pair of total orders that refine
\trianglelefteq_{x}
and
\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}
, respectively. Let
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and assume that
i,j\neq\mathsf{P}(x)(1)
. Then

i\blacktriangleleft_{x}j\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j).

Furthermore, if
\mathsf{P}(x)(1)\blacktriangleleft_{x}j
, then
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(\mathsf{P}(x)(1))\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
5.4
and the definition of “compatible.”
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Suppose we have a sequence of total orders

\blacktriangleleft_{x},\quad\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}(x)},\quad\ldots,\quad\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{n-1}(x)}

on
\{1,\ldots,n\}
, where
\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{j}(x)}
refines
\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}^{j}(x)}
, and where consecutive terms are compatible pairs. Then the pair

\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{n-1}(x)},\quad\blacktriangleleft_{x}

is also compatible.

Proof.

For
1\leq k\leq n-1
, it follows from the definition of “compatible pair” that for
i,j\in\mathsf{Fix}(\mathrm{rot}^{k}(x),m)
, we have

i\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k}(x)}j\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\mathsf{c}^{k}(i)\blacktriangleleft_{x}\mathsf{c}^{k}(j).

Let
\blacktriangleleft^{\prime}
be the unique total order refining
\trianglelefteq_{x}=\trianglelefteq_{\mathrm{rot}^{n}(x)}
such that

\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{n-1}(x)},\quad\blacktriangleleft^{\prime}

is a compatible pair. Then, for
i,j,\in\mathsf{Fix}(x,m)=\mathsf{Fix}(\mathrm{rot}^{n}(x),m)
, we have

\displaystyle i\blacktriangleleft^{\prime}j\quad

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathsf{c}(i)\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{n-1}(x)}\mathsf{c}(j)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad\mathsf{c}^{n-1}(\mathsf{c}(i))\blacktriangleleft_{x}\mathsf{c}^{n-1}(\mathsf{c}(j))

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\quad i\blacktriangleleft_{x}j.

In other words,
\blacktriangleleft^{\prime}
coincides with
\blacktriangleleft_{x}
.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
x
be an alcove. Choose a total order
\blacktriangleleft_{x}
refining
\trianglelefteq_{x}
, and then extend it to a sequence

\blacktriangleleft_{x},\quad\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}(x)},\quad\ldots,\quad\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{n-1}(x)}

as in Lemma
5.7
, For
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, let
\delta^{(k)}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be the permutation such that

\delta^{(k)}(i)\leq\delta^{(k)}(j)\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad\mathsf{c}^{1-k}(i)\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{1-k}(j).

Then
\delta^{(k)}(x^{(k)}-\varpi_{k-1})
is a dominant coweight.

Proof.

We begin by proving the lemma in the special case where
k=1
. We must show that
\delta^{(1)}(x^{(1)})
is dominant, i.e., that

x^{(1)}_{(\delta^{(1)})^{-1}(1)}\geq x^{(1)}_{(\delta^{(1)})^{-1}(2)}\geq\cdots\geq x^{(1)}_{(\delta^{(1)})^{-1}(n)}.

Suppose one of these is false, say
x^{(1)}_{(\delta^{(1)})^{-1}(k)}<x^{(1)}_{(\delta^{(1)})^{-1}(k+1)}
. Then
(\delta^{(1)})^{-1}(k+1)\trianglelefteq_{x}(\delta^{(1)})^{-1}(k)
, and hence
(\delta^{(1)})^{-1}(k+1)\blacktriangleleft_{x}(\delta^{(1)})^{-1}(k)
. But this contradicts the definition of
\delta^{(1)}
, which implies that
(\delta^{(1)})^{-1}(k)\blacktriangleleft_{x}(\delta^{(1)})^{-1}(k+1)
.

Next, observe that

\delta^{(k)}(\mathsf{c}^{k-1}(i))\leq\delta^{(k)}(\mathsf{c}^{k-1}(j))\qquad\text{if and only if}\qquad i\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}j.

Thus, the element
\delta^{(k)}\mathsf{c}^{k-1}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
defined in terms of
x
coincides with the permutation
\delta^{(1)}
defined in terms of
\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)
. By the previous paragraph, the coweight

\delta^{(k)}(\mathsf{c}^{k-1}(\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)^{(1)}))

is dominant. Recall from Lemma
5.2
that
\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)^{(1)}=\mathsf{c}^{1-k}(x^{(k)}-\varpi_{k-1})
. We conclude that
\delta^{(k)}(x^{(k)}-\varpi_{k-1})
is dominant.
∎

Lemma 5.9
.

Let
x
be an alcove. Choose a compatible sequence of total orders as in Lemma
5.7
, and let
\delta^{(1)},\ldots,\delta^{(n)}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be as in Lemma
5.8
. Let
t\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\}
. For each
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, there is an integer
b(t,k)
such that

(\delta^{(k+1)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t})=(\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t})-e_{b(t,k)}+e_{\mathsf{P}(x)(k)},

where in case
k=n
, we set
\delta^{(n+1)}=\delta^{(1)}
.

Proof.

List the integers
1,\ldots,n
in order according to
\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}
and
\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k}(x)}
, as follows:

(5.11)

\displaystyle a_{1}\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}a_{2}\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}\cdots\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}a_{n},

(5.12)

\displaystyle b_{1}\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k}(x)}b_{2}\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k}(x)}\cdots\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k}(x)}b_{n}.

Denote the smallest
t
elements with respect to each of these orders as follows:

S=\{a_{1},a_{2},\ldots,a_{t}\},\qquad S^{\prime}=\{b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{t}\}.

Recall that

\mathsf{P}(\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x))(1)=\mathsf{c}^{1-k}(\mathsf{P}(x)(\mathsf{c}^{k-1}(1)))=\mathsf{c}^{1-k}(\mathsf{P}(x)(k)).

Applying to Lemma
5.6
to the alcove
\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)
, we see that if
i,j\neq\mathsf{c}^{1-k}(\mathsf{P}(x)(k))
, we have
i\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}j
if and only if
\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
. We also have that
\mathsf{c}^{1-k}(\mathsf{P}(x)(k))\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}j
implies
\mathsf{c}^{-k}(\mathsf{P}(x)(k))\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)
. Now let
m
and
m^{\prime}
be such that

a_{m}=\mathsf{c}^{1-k}(\mathsf{P}(x)(k)),\qquad b_{m^{\prime}}=\mathsf{c}^{-1}(a_{m})=\mathsf{c}^{-k}(\mathsf{P}(x)(k)).

With this notation, we rephrase the conclusion of Lemma
5.6
as follows:

\displaystyle i\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}j

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow\mathsf{c}^{-1}(i)\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j)

if
i,j\neq a_{m}
,

(5.13)

\displaystyle a_{m}\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}j

\displaystyle\Longrightarrow b_{m^{\prime}}\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(j).

In other words, if we omit
a_{m}
from the sequence (
5.11
) and then apply
\mathsf{c}^{-1}
, we obtain the sequence in (
5.12
) with
b_{m^{\prime}}
omitted. Moreover, (
5.13
) implies that

m^{\prime}\leq m.

Applying these observations to the smallest
t
elements in (
5.11
) and (
5.12
), we find

(5.14)

S^{\prime}=\begin{cases}\mathsf{c}^{-1}(S)&\text{if $t<m^{\prime}$ or $m\leq t$,}\\
(\mathsf{c}^{-1}(S)\smallsetminus\{\mathsf{c}^{-1}(a_{t})\})\cup\{b_{m^{\prime}}\}&\text{if $m^{\prime}\leq t<m$.}\end{cases}

Now, from the definition of
\delta^{(k)}
, we have

\mathsf{c}^{1-k}((\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(1))\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{1-k}((\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(2))\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}\\
\cdots\blacktriangleleft_{\mathrm{rot}^{k-1}(x)}\mathsf{c}^{1-k}((\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(n)).

Comparing this to (
5.11
), we obtain

a_{i}=\mathsf{c}^{1-k}((\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(i)\qquad\text{for $1\leq i\leq n$.}

Similar considerations shows that

b_{i}=\mathsf{c}^{-k}((\delta^{(k+1)})^{-1}(i)\qquad\text{for $1\leq i\leq n$.}

We therefore have

(\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t})=\sum_{i=1}^{t}e_{(\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(i)}=\sum_{j\in\mathsf{c}^{k-1}(S)}e_{j},\qquad(\delta^{(k+1)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t})=\sum_{j\in\mathsf{c}^{k}(S^{\prime})}e_{j}.

Combining these formulas with (
5.14
), we find that

(\delta^{(k+1)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t})=\begin{cases}(\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t})&\text{if $t<m^{\prime}$, or if $m\leq t$},\\
(\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t})-e_{\mathsf{c}^{k-1}(a_{t})}+e_{\mathsf{c}^{k}(b_{m^{\prime}})}&\text{if $m^{\prime}\leq t<m$.}\end{cases}

Recall that
\mathsf{c}^{k}(b_{m^{\prime}})=\mathsf{P}(x)(k)
. Let

b(t,k)=\begin{cases}\mathsf{P}(x)(k)&\text{if $t<m^{\prime}$ or $m\leq t$,}\\
\mathsf{c}^{k-1}(a_{t})&\text{if $m^{\prime}\leq t<m$.}\end{cases}

We thus have
(\delta^{(k+1)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t})=(\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t})-e_{b(t,k)}+e_{\mathsf{P}(x)(k)}
in all cases.
∎

Theorem 5.10
.

Let
\mu,\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
be dominant coweights such that
\lambda=\mu+\varpi_{t}
for some
t\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}
. For any
\lambda
-permissible alcove
x
, there exists a
\mu
-permissible alcove
y
such that
x
is in relative position
\varpi_{t}
with respect to
y
.

Proof.

Let
\delta^{(1)},\ldots,\delta^{(n)}\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
be as in Lemma
5.8
. Define a sequence of coweights
y=(y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(n)})
by

y^{(k)}=x^{(k)}-(\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t}).

We have
|y^{(k)}|=|x^{(k)}|-t
, and since
x
is an alcove, it follows that

|y^{(2)}|=|y^{(1)}|+1,\quad|y^{(3)}|=|y^{(2)}|+1,\quad\ldots\quad|y^{(n)}|=|y^{(n-1)}|+1.

Next, we will show that

(5.15)

y^{(1)}\leq_{\mathrm{co}}y^{(2)}\leq_{\mathrm{co}}\cdots\leq_{\mathrm{co}}y^{(n)}\leq_{\mathrm{co}}y^{(1)}+\varpi_{n}.

For
1\leq k\leq n-1
, by Lemma
5.9
, we have

y^{(k+1)}=x^{(k+1)}-(\delta^{(k+1)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t})\\
=(x^{(k)}+e_{\mathsf{P}(x)(k)})-((\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t})-e_{b(t,k)}+e_{\mathsf{P}(x)(k)})=y^{(k)}+e_{b(t,k)},

and this establishes the first
n-1
inequalities in (
5.15
). But in fact the same calculation is also valid when
k=n
, if we set
x^{(n+1)}=x^{(1)}+\varpi_{n}
and
y^{(n+1)}=y^{(1)}+\varpi_{n}
. We conclude that
y
is an alcove.

For
1\leq k\leq n
, we have
\mathsf{dom}(x^{(k)}-y^{(k)})=\mathsf{dom}((\delta^{(k)})^{-1}(\varpi_{t}))=\varpi_{t}
, so
x
is in relative position
\varpi_{t}
with respect to
y
. Finally, observe that

\delta^{(k)}(y^{(k)}-\varpi_{k-1})+\varpi_{t}=\delta^{(k)}(x^{(k)}-\varpi_{k-1})=\mathsf{dom}(x^{(k)}-\varpi_{k-1})\preceq\lambda.

In particular,
\delta^{(k)}(y^{(k)}-\varpi_{k-1})+\varpi_{t}
is dominant. Lemma
5.1
then tells us that

\mathsf{dom}(y^{(k)}-\varpi_{k-1})=\mathsf{dom}(\delta^{(k)}(y^{(k)}-\varpi_{k-1}))\preceq\mu.

Thus,
y
is
\mu
-permissible.
∎

6.
Convolution

Let
X
and
Y
be two subsets of
\mathcal{G}r
, and assume that
Y
is stable under
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
. Their
twisted product
is the set

X\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}Y=\left\{(L,L^{\prime})\in\mathcal{G}r\times\mathcal{G}r\,\Big|\,\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}\text{$L\in X$, and for some (or any) $g\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{K})$}\\
\text{such that $g\cdot L=\mathbb{O}^{n}$, we have $g\cdot L^{\prime}\in Y$}\end{array}\right\}.

Let us check that this is well-defined: if
g,g^{\prime}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{K})
both satisfy
g\cdot L=g^{\prime}\cdot L=\mathbf{L}^{0}
, then
g^{\prime}g^{-1}
preserves
\mathbb{O}^{n}
, which means that
g^{\prime}g^{-1}\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
. Since
Y
is
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
-stable, we see that
g\cdot L^{\prime}\in Y
if and only if
g^{\prime}\cdot L^{\prime}\in Y
. Let

\mathsf{m}:X\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}Y\to\mathcal{G}r

be the projection map onto the second factor:
\mathsf{m}(L,L^{\prime})=L^{\prime}
.

As an example, for
1\leq k\leq n-1
, we have (cf. Example
2.2
)

(6.1)

X\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}\mathcal{G}r_{\varpi_{k}}=\left\{(L,L^{\prime})\in\mathcal{G}r\times\mathcal{G}r\,\Big|\,\begin{array}[]{@{}c@{}}\text{$L\in X$, $tL\subset L^{\prime}\subset L$,}\\
\text{and $\dim L^{\prime}/tL=n-k$}\end{array}\right\}.

There is an alternative description of
X\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}Y
as a quotient of a certain space with a free
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
-action. This description shows that the
first
projection map
X\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}Y\to X
makes
X\times Y
into a locally trivial fibration over
X
with fibers isomorphic to
Y
. In particular, In particular, if
X
and
Y
are irreducible subvarieties of
\mathcal{G}r
, it follows that
X\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}Y
is also irreducible.

Lemma 6.1
.

The image of
\mathsf{m}:\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\mu}}\to\mathcal{G}r
is
\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda+\mu}}
.

Proof Sketch.

Using (
2.2
), one can check that the largest dominant
\nu
(with respect to
\preceq
) such that
\mathbf{L}^{\nu}
lies in this image is
\nu=\lambda+\mu
. On the other hand, because
\mathsf{m}:\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\mu}}\to\mathcal{G}r
is proper and
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
-equivariant, and because its domain is irreducible, its image must be a closed, irreducible,
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
-invariant subvariety, and hence the closure of some
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
-orbit. The lemma follows.
∎

There is also a “global” version of the twisted product construction. Defining it in full generality requires the introduction of a “global group scheme” that exhibits
I
as a degeneration of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(\mathbb{O})
; see
[
13
, §3.2]
. In this paper, we will make do with an
ad hoc
definition that is sufficient for our purposes. For
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
, let

X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\mu}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{r}(y,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n},L^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,L^{\prime}_{n})\\
\quad\in\mathbf{Gr}\times_{\mathbb{C}}\mathbf{Gr}\end{array}\,\Big|\,\begin{array}[]{c}\text{for $1\leq i\leq n$, we have}\\
(L_{i},L_{i}^{\prime})\in\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\mu}}\end{array}\right\}.

Let

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{m}}:X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\mu}\to\mathbf{Gr}

be the second projection map:
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{m}}(y,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n},L^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,L^{\prime}_{n})=(y,L^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,L^{\prime}_{n})
.

Let
(X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\mu})_{\mathbf{s}}
, resp.
(X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\mu})_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}
, denote the preimage of
0
, resp.
\mathbb{C}^{\times}
, under the projection map
X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\mu}\to\mathbb{C}
. By Lemma
3.1
, we have
(X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\mu})_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\cong\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times(\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\mu}})
, and we can make the following identification:

(6.2)

(X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\mu})_{\mathbf{s}}\subset\mathcal{F}l\times\mathcal{F}l.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
, and let
1\leq k\leq n-1
. If
X_{\lambda}
is irreducible, then so is
X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\varpi_{k}}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be the variety

\mathcal{V}=\{(L,v)\mid L\in\mathcal{G}r,\ v\in L/tL\}.

This is a vector bundle over
\mathcal{G}r
of rank
n
. Next, let

\mathbf{V}=\{(y,(L_{1},v_{1}),(L_{2},v_{2}),\ldots,(L_{n},v_{n}))\in\mathbb{C}\times\mathcal{V}\times\cdots\times\mathcal{V}\mid(y,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})\in\mathbf{Gr}\}.

This is a vector bundle over
\mathbf{Gr}
of rank
n^{2}
. Now, fix a point

(6.3)

(y,(L_{1},v_{1}),(L_{2},v_{2}),\ldots,(L_{n},v_{n}))\in\mathbf{V}.

Recall that
\theta_{i-1}(y)(L_{i})\subset L_{1}
, so
\theta_{i-1}(y):\mathbb{K}^{n}\to\mathbb{K}^{n}
determines a map
\theta_{i-1}(y):L_{i}/tL_{i}\to L_{1}/tL_{1}
. We may therefore consider the sequence of vectors

(6.4)

v_{1},\quad\theta_{1}(y)(v_{2}),\quad\theta_{2}(y)(v_{3}),\quad\ldots,\quad\theta_{n-1}(y)(v_{n})\qquad\in L_{1}/tL_{1}.

Let

\widetilde{\mathbf{Gr}}=\{(y,(L_{1},v_{1}),(L_{2},v_{2}),\ldots,(L_{n},v_{n}))\in\mathbf{V}\mid\\
\text{$v_{1},\ \theta_{1}(y)(v_{2}),\ \ldots,\ \theta_{n-1}(y)(v_{n})$ are linearly independent}\}.

This is an open subset of
\mathbf{V}
. Moreover, its intersection with each fiber of
\mathbf{V}\to\mathbf{Gr}
is nonempty: if
y\neq 0
, then the maps
\theta_{i-1}(y):L_{i}/tL_{i}\overset{\sim}{\to}L_{1}/tL_{1}
are isomorphisms, and any basis for
L_{1}/tL_{1}
determines a linearly independent sequence as in (
6.4
). If
y=0
, then
L_{1}\supset\theta_{1}(0)L_{2}\supset\cdots\supset\theta_{n-1}(0)(L_{n})\supset tL_{1}
is a lattice chain, and we can choose each
v_{i}
so that
\theta_{i-1}(0)(v_{i})\notin\theta_{i}(0)L_{i+1}
to get a linearly independent sequence as in (
6.4
).

Finally, let

\widetilde{X_{\lambda}}=X_{\lambda}\times_{\mathbf{Gr}}\widetilde{\mathbf{Gr}}.

This is a nonempty open subset of the vector bundle
X_{\lambda}\times_{\mathbf{Gr}}\mathbf{V}
over
X_{\lambda}
.

Given a point as in (
6.3
), we have more generally that

\begin{cases}\theta_{i-1}(y)^{-1}\theta_{j-1}(y)(L_{j})\subset L_{i}&\text{if $j\geq i$,}\\
\theta_{i-1}(y)^{-1}\theta_{j-1}(y)\theta_{n}(y)(L_{j})\subset L_{i}&\text{if $j<i$.}\end{cases}

We can generalize (
6.4
) and obtain a sequence of vectors

(6.5)

\hbox{\small$\theta_{i-1}(y)^{-1}\theta_{n}(y)(v_{1}),\ \theta_{i-1}(y)^{-1}\theta_{1}(y)\theta_{n}(y)(v_{2}),\ \ldots,\ \theta_{i-1}(y)^{-1}\theta_{i-2}(y)\theta_{n}(y)(v_{i-1}),$}\\
\hbox{\small$v_{i},\ \theta_{i-1}(y)^{-1}\theta_{i}(y)(v_{i+1}),\ \ldots,\ \theta_{i-1}(y)^{-1}\theta_{n-1}(y)(v_{n})$}\qquad\in L_{i}/tL_{i}

for each
i
,
1\leq i\leq n
. If our point (
6.3
) actually lies in
\widetilde{\mathbf{Gr}}
, then the set in (
6.5
) is again linearly independent for each
i
. In this case, we denote by

g_{i}:\mathbb{C}^{n}\to L_{i}/tL_{i}

the linear isomorphism that sends
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
to the basis vectors listed in (
6.5
). If
H\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a linear subspace, say of codimension
k
, then the subspace
g_{i}(H)\subset L_{i}/tL_{i}
corresponds to some lattice sandwiched between
L_{i}
and
tL_{i}
that has valuation
\nu(L_{i})+k
. In a minor abuse of notation, we denote this lattice by

g_{i}(H)+tL_{i}.

We may also write
g_{i}(L)
for a lattice
L
satisfying
t^{n}\mathbb{O}^{n}\subset L\subset\mathbb{O}^{n}
. In that case,
g_{i}(L)=g_{i}(L/t^{n}\mathbb{O}^{n})+tL_{i}
.

We are now ready to define a morphism of varieties

q:\widetilde{X_{\lambda}}\times_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\varpi_{k}}\to X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\varpi_{k}},

using the description from (
4.2
) for
X_{\varpi_{k}}
. We set

(6.6)

q(y,(L_{1},v_{1}),(L_{2},v_{2}),\ldots,(L_{n},v_{n}),H_{1},\ldots,H_{n})=\\
(y,L_{1},L_{2},\ldots,L_{n},g_{1}(H_{1})+tL_{1},g_{2}(H_{2})+tL_{2},\ldots,g_{n}(L_{n})+tL_{n}).

It is immediate from (
6.1
) that each pair
(L_{i},g_{i}(H_{i})+tL_{i})
lies in
\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda}}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}\mathcal{G}r_{\varpi_{k}}
, so
q
does indeed take values in
X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\varpi_{k}}
. We also claim that
q
is surjective. Indeed, given a point

(6.7)

(y,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n},L^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,L^{\prime}_{n})\in X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\varpi_{k}},

choose any point
(y,(L_{1},v_{1}),\ldots,(L_{n},v_{n}))\in\widetilde{X_{\lambda}}
lying over
(y,L_{1},\ldots,L_{n})\in X_{\lambda}
. Then (
6.7
) is the image under
q
of the point

(y,(L_{1},v_{1}),\ldots,(L_{n},v_{n}),g_{1}^{-1}(L^{\prime}_{1}/tL_{1}),g_{2}^{-1}(L^{\prime}_{2}/tL_{2}),\ldots,g_{n}^{-1}(L^{\prime}_{n}/tL_{n})).

We are now ready to complete the proof of the lemma. Since
X_{\lambda}
is assumed to be irreducible, so is the vector bundle
X_{\lambda}\times_{\mathbf{Gr}}\mathbf{V}
and its open subset
\widetilde{X_{\lambda}}
. The variety
X_{\varpi_{k}}
is also irreducible, by Theorem
4.11
. Since
q
is surjective, we conclude that
X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\varpi_{k}}
is irreducible.
∎

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{X}^{+}
, and let
1\leq k\leq n-1
. If
X_{\lambda}
is irreducible, then the image of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{m}}:X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\varpi_{k}}\to\mathbf{Gr}
is
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda+\varpi_{k}}}
.

Proof.

By (
6.2
) and Lemma
6.1
, the generic part of this image is
\mathbb{C}^{\times}\times\overline{\mathcal{G}r_{\lambda+\varpi_{k}}}
. On the other hand,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{m}}:X_{\lambda}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\varpi_{k}}\to\mathbf{Gr}
is proper, and its domain is irreducible by Lemma
6.2
, so its image is closed and irreducible, and hence equal to
\overline{\mathbf{Gr}_{\lambda+\varpi_{k}}}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

In the special case where

4.11
.

For general

, then

. Suppose now that
\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n}>0
. Then there is some

such that

. Let
\mu=\lambda-\varpi_{k}

, so by induction,

. In particular,
X_{\mu}
is irreducible, so by Lemma
6.2
, so is
X_{\mu}\mathbin{\widetilde{\mathord{\times}}}_{\mathbb{C}}X_{\varpi_{k}}
.

Let
x
be a
\lambda
-permissible alcove. By Theorem
5.10
, there exists a
\mu
-permissible alcove
y
such that
x
is in relative position
\varpi_{k}
with respect to
y
. By Lemma
6.5
,

lies in

, its image

. We have established (

), so

.
