Unveiling the role of sex as a predictor for KPC-Klebsiella pneumoniae and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii bacteremia in critically Ill colonized patients
Authors: Maria Cristina Gagliardi, Maria Teresa Pagano, Elena Ortona, Claudio Maria Mastroianni, Sara Covino, Giancarlo Ceccarelli, Giusy Magni, Francesco Alessandri, Alessandra Oliva, Luca Busani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNosocomial infections caused by multidrug-resistant bacteria represent a major global public health threat. They occur most frequently in Intensive Care Units (ICUs), particularly due to Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase–producing K. pneumoniae (KPC-KP) and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB). Only a limited number of studies have examined sex as a predictor of KPC-KP and CRAB infections. In this study, we investigated whether sex influences patterns of KPC-KP and CRAB colonization in ICU patients, as well as the risk and timing of bloodstream infection (BSI) development among colonized individuals.MethodsWe included critically ill patients who developed rectal colonization by CRAB or KPC-Kp during the ICU stay. The two-sided Mann-Whitney U test, the Student’s t test, the χ2 test or the Fisher’s exact test were applied to analyse the differences in age, colonising pathogens, comorbidities, rate of BSI and mortality between male and female patients. Two age categories were created: 0-54 and over 55 years. Cox regression analysis was conducted to identify independent risk factors for colonization and BSI outcomes. For mortality outcomes backward stepwise logistic regression models were applied. For survival analysis, the Kaplan–Meier method was used. ResultsA total of 273 patients, 185 men and 88 women, were included in the study. The prevalence of KPC-KP and CRAB BSI was 31.8%. Our results show that sex and age affected both the risk and timing of BSI onset after admission to ICU. In particular, we found that the overall risk of BSI irrespective of sex and age was higher in patients with KPC-KP colonization than in those with CRAB colonization (IRR=1.56; 95%CI 1.00-2.45). After stratifying for sex, this risk was statistically higher in men than in women (IRR=2.06; 95%CI 1.22-3.49). Women presented a lower rate (19% vs 31%) and later onset [median 35 days (15-56) days vs 16.5 days (10-24.5), p<0.05] of CRAB BSI in comparison to men. After stratifying for age in women over 55 years, the proportion of BSI was lower and the onset occurred later for both pathogens, while, for KPC-KP BSI, women in 0-54 years group had the highest proportion and earliest onset compared to men irrespective of age group and older women.ConclusionsOur data show that both the risk of developing systemic infections due to KPC-KP and CRAB, as well as the timing from colonization to BSI in critically ill patients colonized with KPC-KP and CRAB, are influenced by sex and age. We therefore propose that sex and age should be incorporated into risk stratification analyses aimed at identifying predictors of systemic infections in colonized critically ill patients. Background

Nosocomial infections with multidrug resistant (MDR) bacteria pose a threat to global public health worldwide. They are frequent mainly in Intensive Care Units (ICU), due to the multitude of invasive procedures and medical devices used, the prolonged antibiotic therapy, the critical conditions of these patients and the long hospitalization [1].

Infections by Klebsiella pneumoniae carbapenemase (KPC)-producing K. pneumoniae (KPC-KP) and carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) in ICU are mainly associated with ventilator-associated pneumonia, sepsis, septic shock and death [2–4].

Colonization with MDR bacteria can persist for long periods and frequently precedes infection, although with a different rate according to the type of patients and microorganisms, ranging from 7.8% for carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae in both ICU and non-ICU settings to 44% for CRAB in the ICU setting [5, 6].

In this challenging scenario, knowing the specific risk factors for infection development in critically ill patients colonized by either KPC-Kp or CRAB is of outmost importance, in order to start an early appropriate therapy aimed at reducing associated morbidity and mortality.

Recent studies have highlighted that sex differences may play a critical role in the susceptibility to and outcomes of bacterial infections [7]. Indeed, male sex has been associated with a higher incidence of sepsis than female, probably as a consequence of differences in the source of infection and of sex-related dysregulation of the immune responses, while conflicting data exist regarding the influence of sex as a prognostic factor for mortality in critically ill adults with sepsis [8–14].

Even though several risk factors for MDR bacteria colonization, such as age, comorbidities, prior hospitalization and extensive antibiotic use, have been well characterized, the influence of sex as a predictor of MDR infection in colonized patients is still largely unexplored, with two recent papers showing that male are associated with higher risk of colonization and infection with multi-drug-resistant organisms than females, including CRAB [15–17].

Understanding how sex, as a biological and physiological factor, influences the colonization/infection by antibiotic-resistant bacteria is essential for developing targeted prevention and treatment strategies, especially in the ICU setting.

In this study, we investigated whether sex influences (i) the features of colonization by CRAB and KPC-KP of patients admitted in ICU, (ii) the risk of and the time to developing BSI in colonized patients and (iii) the mortality from KPC-KP and CRAB colonization.

Furthermore, to further investigate the impact of hormonal changes associated with menopause (typically occurring between 45 and 55 years), we divided the study population according to two different age categories (0–54 years and over 55 years).

Materials and methods

Study population and definitions

We performed a retrospective, observational cohort study including consecutive adult patients admitted to ICU in a 1200-bed tertiary academic hospital in Rome between 2018 and 2023 developing rectal colonization by CRAB or KPC-KP during ICU stay.

Exclusion criteria were (i) CRAB or KPC-KP colonization or infection before ICU admission and (ii) patients who developed CRAB or KPC-KP BSI without previous rectal colonization.

The patients were followed from the admission to ICU to the discharge or death, and the occurrence of KPC-KP or CRAB BSI was recorded.

Demographic, clinical and outcome (development of BSI, in-hospital mortality and 7 days, 14 days and 28-day mortality from colonization and BSI onset) information were anonymously recorded in an electronic database.

According to local infection control guidelines, rectal/stool swabs, respiratory and urine cultures were performed at ICU admission and routinely re-evaluated once a week for detection of CRAB and KPC-KP strains, as previously described [6, 17].

KPC-KP or CRAB rectal colonization was considered as a positive culture without concomitant signs and symptoms of infection, while infected patients were those who developed a KPC-KP or CRAB BSI during hospitalization.

Multisite colonization was defined as the presence of at least 1 site of colonization in addition to the rectal one.

The date of infection onset was defined as the date of the index specimen collection.

Microbiological analyses

Blood cultures or other biological samples were used for bacterial identification by the MALDI-TOF MS system (Bruker Daltonik GmbH, Bremen, Germany). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed with the Vitek2 automated system (bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France) and Microscan Walkaway (Beckman and Coulter, Brea, California, USA) system.

Statistical analysis

To analyse the differences in age, colonising pathogens, comorbidities, rate of BSI and mortality between male and female patients, the two-sided Mann-Whitney U test, the Student’s t test, the χ2 test or the Fisher’s exact test, were applied as appropriate. Two different age categories were created: 0–54 years and over 55 years. Age grouping before and after 54 years of age was used to analyze health differences between men and women because it helps to isolate the impact of hormonal changes associated with menopause and andropause, that typically occur between 45 and 55 years and significantly alters hormonal balance, affecting various aspects of health.

Once a univariate statistic was generated, variables with a p-value of < 0.10 were included in multivariable models according to the outcome.

Cox regression analysis was conducted to identify independent risk factors for colonization and BSI outcomes for which event dates were available. For mortality outcomes, the precise date was not available, thus backward stepwise logistic regression models were applied.

Mortality was recorded at 7, 14 and 28 days after colonization and BSI onset, when occurred, but for the analysis, the overall mortality at 28 days was considered cumulating all the events at the end of the observation (28 days).

For the Cox and Logistic models, we analysed the risk factors by KPC-KP and CRAB. Another set of models was carried out to analyse the same risk factors separately by males and females. For statistical significance a p value < 0.05 was considered.

For survival analysis, the Kaplan–Meier method was used. All statistics were performed with Stata 16 for Windows (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

The number of men colonized after admission in ICU was higher than women, irrespective of age and pathogen (Table 1). The proportion of patients colonized by CRAB and KPC-KP was 52.75% and 47.25%, respectively, with no statistical difference between men and women.

No significant differences have been observed between males and females in the distribution of comorbidities, simplified acute physiology score (SAPS), previous surgery, onset of BSI and overall mortality after colonization. The median length of stay in ICU was 34 days (IQR 19–54), with no differences considering sex and age.

While no differences were observed for multisite colonization, we found that in the age group over 55 years (n = 193, 127 male, 66 female; Table 1), multisite colonization by KPC-KP was more frequent in men compared to women [27 out of 53 men over 55 years colonized by KPC-KP (51%) vs. 9 out of 31 women over 55 years colonized by KPC-KP (29%), p = 0.07]. Differently, in the age group 0–54 years (n = 80, 58 male, 22 female; Table 1) multisite colonization by CRAB was more frequent in women than men [6 out of 8 women 0–54 years colonized by CRAB (75%) vs. 11 out of 29 men 0–54 years colonized by CRAB (38%), p = 0.1]. However, these differences were not statistically significant.

Analysis of BSI occurrence

Overall, BSI occurred in 87/273 (31.8%) patients, with no statistically significant difference between men and women (64, 34.6% vs. 23, 26.1%, respectively, p > 0.1). After stratifying by age, we found that in the age group over 55 years (n = 193, 127 male, 66 female; Table 1), CRAB BSI tended to be more frequent in men than in women (26/72 patients, 36% vs. 6/33 patients, 18%, p = 0.07).

The overall risk of BSI irrespective of sex and age was higher in patients with KPC-KP colonization (IRR = 1.56; 95%CI 1.00-2.45). After stratifying for sex, this risk was statistically higher in men than in women (IRR = 2.06; 95%CI 1.22–3.49).

The hazard (Cox multivariable regressions) of colonization in men was related to previous antibiotic treatment, with longer period from admission to colonization for patients treated 4 or more days with antibiotics (HR = 0.63, 95%CI 0.41–0.95). No specific hazard was associated with colonization in women of the same age group.

Women presented a lower rate (19% vs. 31%) and later onset [median 35 days (15–56) days vs. 16.5 days (10-24.5), p < 0.05] of CRAB BSI in comparison to men, while no difference was observed for KPC-KP BSI (Fig. 1C).

Stratifying by age, the results differed; for both pathogens, in women over 55 years of age, the proportion of BSI was lower and the onset occurred later (Fig. 1D), while women in 0–54 years age group with KPC-KP infection had the highest proportion of BSI and the earliest onset (Fig. 1D).

Logistic regression model to analyse risk factors associated with mortality within 28 days from colonization

Logistic regressions analyzed the risk factors associated with mortality within 28 days from colonization (Table 2). The risk of mortality was reduced in men with colonization with KPC-KP than CRAB (OR = 0.249; 95%CI 0.071–0.881), antibiotic treatment for 4 days or more (OR = 0.302; 95%CI 0.109–0.832), and having had surgery before admission to ICU (OR = 0.24; 95%CI 0.08–0.72). The risk increased in men with hypertension (OR = 3.98; 95%CI 1.279–7.390).

The only factor associated with mortality within 28 days from colonization in women was colonization in multiple sites, which lowered the risk of death (OR = 0.158, 95%CI 0.051–0.567).

Discussion

Our results demonstrate sex and age differences in the overall risk and timing of BSI onset in critically ill patients with CRAB and KPC-KP rectal colonization, suggesting to consider sex and age during ICU stay for a better patient management.

In particular, we found that (i) the overall risk of BSI irrespective of sex and age was higher in patients with KPC-KP colonization than CRAB colonization; (ii) after stratifying for sex, this risk was higher in men than in women; (iii) women presented a later onset of CRAB BSI in comparison to men.

Appling the survival analysis approach, to include also time at the onset in the risk estimates, and taking into account the age stratification, we showed that in women over 55 years of age, the proportion of BSI was lower and the onset occurred later for both pathogens, while, for KPC-KP BSI, women in 0–54 years age group had the highest proportion and earliest onset compared to men irrespective of age group and older women.

MDR colonization is a known risk factor for subsequent systemic infections, which may develop in up to 44% of critically ill patients colonized by CRAB [6]. In our study, we confirmed that the prevalence of subsequent BSI in KPC-KP and CRAB critically ill colonized patients is high, being 31.8%, meaning that approximately one out of three colonized patients develop BSI during hospitalization.

This finding highlights the need of deeply investigating the risk factors associated with BSI development in critically ill patients in order to promptly start an appropriate therapy and reduce mortality driven by inappropriate early therapy.

In our study, being colonized by KPC-KP was associated with a higher risk of BSI than CRAB (IRR = 1.56). This represents an important finding, since it highlights a possible role for the pathogen itself to determine the risk of systemic infection. This finding is in line with the recent report by Karakosta et al., who showed that rectal CRE colonization was significantly associated with more than 5-fold higher risk of subsequent CRE-BSI compared to a 2-fold increase in the risk of subsequent BSI in CRAB colonized patients. Moreover, after stratifying for the type of carbapenemases, authors found that the risk was highest for MBL and KPC [18]. Whether this difference is due to pathogens’ virulence or pathogen-host interaction is still unknown and surely deserves further investigation.

Apart from the well-recognized clinical factors, we concentrated on the possible influence of sex on BSI development, given that sex may influence the risk of infection due to difference in hormones and immune system and given that recent reports suggest that being male may represent a risk factor for infection with MDR bacteria, including CRAB [17].

With this regard, we were able to demonstrate that men had a significantly higher risk of BSI onset than women (IRR = 2.06).

Furthermore, including the time from admission to ICU to BSI, we explored if there were differences by sex and age in the temporal dynamic of such outcomes.

We highlighted that male and female differ also in terms of the time to develop infection, with women presenting a later onset of BSI in comparison to men, in particular for CRAB and if older than 55 years of age. At the same time, we also observed that women under 55 infected by KPC-KP developed BSI more quickly than men.

The role of time from colonization to infection is a matter of active investigation. Studies on CRAB BSI following CRAB colonization showed that the majority of BSIs occurred within 7 days from colonization [6]; likewise, Karakosta and colleagues demonstrated that CRAB and CRE BSI occurred after a median of 8 days from colonization [18]. Taken together, these findings highlight that there is a temporal phase (early after colonization onset) when the patient is at highest risk of developing BSI, probably as a consequence of alteration in the host-pathogen equilibrium. In this scenario, sex and age, and their mutual relation, may possess a crucial role, by delaying the risk of CRAB BSI in women older than 55 years as well as by shortening the time to KPC-KP BSI in women younger than 55 years. The exact pathogenetic mechanism underlying this finding is still largely unexplored.

Anatomical, genetic, hormonal and immunological features altogether contribute to sex differences in susceptibility and outcome of bacterial infection/colonization [14, 19, 20].

Focusing on nosocomial colonization/infections with MDR bacteria, male sex has been postulated as a risk factor for methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus [21], vancomycin-resistant enterococci [22] and resistant gram-negative bacilli carriages at specific body sites.

There could be several reasons for higher MDR bacteria colonization in male patients which range from biological factors, as sexual dimorphism in the immune response or sex-specific microbiome, to sex-specific behavioral features such as hands hygiene.

We also analysed the risk of mortality in men and women after colonization and BSI, and we observed that this risk was higher in men infected by CRAB, in those who received less than 4 days of previous antibiotic treatment and in those not submitted to surgery before ICU admission.

While KPC-Kp and CRAB share healthcare-related risk factors, such as prior carbapenem exposure and prolonged ICU stay, CRAB is more strongly associated with critical illness, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and ICU environmental persistence [4]. The higher mortality observed in men colonized with CRAB compared to those colonized with KPC-Kp may therefore reflect that CRAB more commonly infects patients who are already severely ill.

The only comorbidity significantly associated with mortality was hypertension in men, but the presence of any tumor was also of concern, suggesting the burden of frailty as a predictor of worse outcome.

The only condition associated with the risk of 28-d mortality in women was being colonized in one site only compared to having multisite colonization. Even though this result might seem counterintuitive, we may hypothesize that having multisite colonization prompted physicians to early start antimicrobial therapy aimed at targeting the colonizing pathogen in case of suspected or certain infection. However, the fact that this finding was only observed in women highlights the need of additional investigations targeting at exploring the relation between host (sex) and pathogen (colonization at different sites).

A significant sex difference was observed considering the mortality within 14 days from BSI, irrespective of pathogens, with a higher percentage of women than men (39.3% vs. 14.6%). Although the multivariable analysis was not performed given the paucity of patients, we believe this finding still deserves attention.

Indeed, it appears to be in line with the findings by Sakr et al., who showed that, despite prevalence of severe sepsis was lower in women than in men, female gender was independently associated with a higher risk of death in the ICU for patients with severe sepsis [8]. Likewise, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis showed that females presented higher 28-day all-cause mortality but lower 1-year all-cause mortality than men. However, the quality of the evidence was very low and low, respectively [13].

One proposal to partly explain this apparently contradictory result is that there may be a gender-related bias in the provision of care, with men possibly receiving an increased level of care while women being less likely than men to receive life-supporting treatments. However, the differences in provision of care are probably not alone responsible for the observed worse outcome in women [8].

Our study presents some limitations. First, it is a retrospective single center study, thus not leading to a generalization of the results. Second, we did not have data regarding the cause for ICU admission and the clinical conditions for all the patients, but we used the SAPS score to account for the overall status of the patients, and it was included in the analyses without any evidence of interaction with the considered outcomes. Another limitation is related to the mortality, that was collected as cumulative and not day-by-day. We weren’t able to analyse this information precisely, but only at certain points in time. In addition, we were not able to measure possible changes in infection control practice that may have occurred over the study period (which included also the COVID-19 pandemic), possibly influencing the rate of colonization. The last limitation is related to the overall admissions to ICU in the study period that is not known, so we cannot calculate any rates for the main outcomes we considered.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings indicate that both the risk of developing systemic infections due to KPC-KP and CRAB, as well as the timing from colonization to bloodstream infection (BSI) in critically ill patients colonized with KPC-KP and CRAB, are influenced by sex and age. This suggests that hormonal and immunological factors, in addition to traditional risk factors, may underlie the observed differences in risk. We therefore propose that sex and age—and their potential interaction—should be specifically incorporated into risk stratification analyses aimed at identifying predictors of systemic infections in colonized critically ill patients. Further studies exploring the interplay between host factors (sex, age) and pathogen dynamics are warranted.
