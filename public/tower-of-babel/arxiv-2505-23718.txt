Fast Compressed-Domain N N -Point Discrete Fourier Transform: The “Twiddless” FFT Algorithm
Saulo Queiroz

Abstract
In this work, we present the twiddless fast Fourier transform (TFFT) , a novel algorithm for computing the N N -point discrete Fourier transform (DFT). The TFFT’s divide strategy builds on recent results that decimate an N N -point signal (by a factor of p p ) into an N / p N/p -point compressed signal whose DFT readily yields N / p N/p coefficients of the original signal. However, existing compression-domain DFT analyses have been limited to computing only the even-indexed DFT coefficients. With TFFT, we overcome this limitation by efficiently computing both even- and odd-indexed DFT coefficients in the compressed domain with O ⁡ ( N ​ log ⁡ N ) O(N\log N) complexity. TFFT introduces a new recursive decomposition of the DFT problem, wherein N / 2 i N/2^{i} coefficients of the original input are computed at recursion level i i , with no need for twiddle factor multiplications or butterfly structures. Additionally, TFFT generalizes the input length to N = c ⋅ 2 k N=c\cdot 2^{k} (for k ≥ 0 k\geq 0 and non-power-of-two c > 0 c>0 ), reducing the need for zero-padding and potentially improving efficiency and stability over classical FFTs. We believe TFFT represents a novel paradigm for DFT computation, opening new directions for research in optimized implementations, hardware design, parallel computation, and sparse transforms.

Introduction

The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) is a fundamental computational tool in digital
signal processing, with widespread applications in scientific and engineering domains such as
telecommunications, earthquake forecasting, health monitoring, and sound, image, and video
processing
[
1
]
.
The practical feasibility of these applications relies on fast Fourier transform (FFT) algorithms,
whose origins trace back to the radix-2 Cooley-Tukey algorithm
[
2
]
,
widely regarded as one of the most influential algorithms in the history of computing.

A common characteristic of
N
-point FFT algorithms is their divide-and-conquer approach,
in which the combination step is performed using the so-called
twiddle factors
–
complex numbers that multiply the outputs of smaller DFTs to produce the result of a larger DFT.
This distinctive feature ensures that the
N
-point DFT problem can be solved with
O(N\log N)

time complexity, representing a significant improvement over the
O(N^{2})
complexity of naive DFT computation.

For example, in the radix-2 Cooley-Tukey FFT, the twiddle factors combine two
N/2
-point FFTs into
an
N
-point FFT, requiring
5N\log_{2}N
real arithmetic operations.
Beyond complexity reduction, twiddle factors also facilitate improvements in other key performance metrics
for DFT computations, including numerical stability, parallelization
[
3
,
4
]
,
and hardware efficiency in terms of chip area and power consumption
[
5
]
.
For a comprehensive treatment of various FFT algorithms and their properties, we refer the reader
to the extensive literature in the field
[
6
]
.

Recent works have demonstrated how to decimate a signal, in either the time or frequency domain,
while proportionally preserving the relationship between the DFT coefficients
of the original and the decimated signals.
In particular, leveraging the approach in
[
7
]
, the rectangular index coefficient
(RIC) method
[
8
]
compresses an
N
-point signal
\mathbf{x}
(for even
N=CL
)
into a
C
-point signal
\mathbf{\hat{x}}
using only
N-1
complex additions and no complex multiplications.
Letting
\mathbf{X}=\{X_{0},X_{1},\dots,X_{N-1}\}
and
\mathbf{\hat{X}}=\{\hat{X}_{0},\hat{X}_{1},\dots,\hat{X}_{C-1}\}

denote the DFTs of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{\hat{x}}
, respectively,
the correspondence is given by
X[cL]=\hat{X}[c]
for
c=0,1,\dots,C-1
.
We refer to this as
compressed-domain DFT analysis
.
For example, when
C=N/2
, the compressed DFT
\mathbf{\hat{X}}
contains the even-indexed coefficients of
\mathbf{X}
.
The authors of
[
8
]
exploit this property for the spectral analysis of sparse and harmonic signals
by applying classical FFT algorithms to the compressed signal.

In this work, we present the
twiddless
fast Fourier transform (TFFT), a new algorithm that
leverages the RIC decimation properties introduced in
[
8
,
7
]

to solve the
N
-point DFT problem. TFFT exploits the RIC property recursively to
compute all even-indexed DFT coefficients of the input signal
\mathbf{x}
.
To obtain the odd-indexed DFT coefficients, TFFT modulates the compressed-domain version of

\mathbf{x}
to shift its frequency bins by
r=1
position, ensuring that
X[2c+1]=\hat{X}[c]

for
c=0,1,\dots,C-1
. The number of real arithmetic operations required by TFFT is given by
the recurrence
T(N)=2T(N/2)+4(N-1)+3N=O(N\log_{2}N)
. Solving this recurrence yields

T(N)\approx 7N\log_{2}N-3N+4
, confirming that TFFT belongs to the category of
fast
DFT algorithms.
Although the leading constant of TFFT is higher than that of some other FFT algorithms,
TFFT introduces novel and unique structural characteristics:

•

Parallelization and very simple combination step
:
By noting that the samples in the compressed-domain signal are computed independently,
one can design parallel implementations to accelerate the proposed algorithm in practical scenarios.
Moreover, the smaller compressed sub-DFTs are also independent of one another and can be fully processed
in parallel, as their results require only index reordering rather than butterfly operations.

•

Better cost-effectiveness to implement generic input-length pattern
:
Given the base case for a DFT of length
N=c
, where
c>0
is a non-power-of-two constant,
our algorithm can process signals of length
N=c\cdot 2^{k}
for
k\geq 0
.
Although mixed-radix FFTs can also accommodate such length patterns,
TFFT requires no structural modifications to handle different values of
c
,
necessitating only the definition of the base case for
N=c
.
Thus, the same divide-and-conquer structure employed by TFFT for power-of-two lengths
can be reused for any
c
. Also,
TFFT offers greater flexibility to avoid the performance degradation of radix-
2^{k}
FFT algorithms
in scenarios where significant zero-padding would otherwise be required.
For example, if
N=5120
, radix-2 and radix-4 FFTs would zero-pad the signal to

N^{\prime}=8192
and
N^{\prime\prime}=16384
, respectively, thereby increasing computational complexity and
potentially stability.
In contrast, our algorithm can directly process
N=5120
without zero-padding,
since
5120=5\cdot 2^{10}
.

•

Simple structure hardware Implementations
: The uniform structure and reliance
on global shifts may simplify implementations in FPGA or ASIC architectures.
Indeed, it is well-known that the modulation
x[n]e^{-j2\pi kn}
is very simple
to implement in circuit for
k=1
, which correspond to the way TFFT perform complex
multiplications. Aside of this, only complex additions are necessary, which is also simple
to implement in hardware. Therefore, TFFT is promising for practical circuit implementation
and further research in this sense is needed.

, we present the TFFT algorithm

2.1
Complexity Analysis

The TFFT algorithm is designed as a divide-and-conquer method. At each stage of recursion for an
N
-point input signal, the problem is divided into two
N/2
-point subproblems. The pre-processing steps to generate the inputs for these subproblems, which involve RIC-based compressions and a frequency shift modulation, constitute the linear work performed at each stage.

The number of real arithmetic operations required by TFFT is given by the recurrence relation:

T(N)=2T(N/2)+4(N-1)+3N

This can be simplified by combining the linear terms:
4(N-1)+3N=4N-4+3N=7N-4
. Thus, the recurrence becomes:

T(N)=2T(N/2)+7N-4

This recurrence relation is of a standard form for divide-and-conquer algorithms, and its solution shows an asymptotic complexity of
O(N\log_{2}N)
. More precisely, assuming
T(1)=1
as a base condition, solving this recurrence yields:

T(N)\approx 7N\log_{2}N-4N+4+N\cdot T(1)\implies T(N)\approx 7N\log_{2}N-3N+4

This complexity confirms that TFFT belongs to the category of ”fast” DFT algorithms.

The leading constant in the
N\log_{2}N
term for TFFT is 7. This is higher than some classic FFT algorithms, such as the radix-2 Cooley-Tukey FFT, which typically requires approximately
5N\log_{2}N
real arithmetic operations. While a higher leading constant may suggest a greater number of operations for very large
N
compared to these specific algorithms, the TFFT algorithm introduces several novel structural characteristics that offer other potential advantages,
as previously discussed.

3
Conclusion and Future Directions

In this work, we have introduced the Twiddless Fast Fourier Transform (TFFT), a novel algorithm for computing the
N
-point Discrete Fourier Transform with
O(N\log_{2}N)
complexity. The TFFT builds upon recent advancements in compressed-domain signal processing
[
8
]
[
7
]
, and significantly extends prior work by enabling the efficient computation of both even- and odd-indexed DFT coefficients directly in the compressed domain. A key innovation of TFFT is its recursive decomposition strategy that computes
N/2^{i}
coefficients of the original input at recursion level
i
without relying on traditional twiddle factor multiplications or butterfly structures at the coefficient recombination stage.

One of the significant practical aspects of TFFT is its inherent support
for input lengths of the form
N=c\cdot 2^{k}
(for
k\geq 0
and non-power-of-two
c>0
)
without requiring structural modifications beyond defining the base case for
N=c
.
This flexibility can reduce the need for extensive zero-padding often encountered with
purely radix-2 algorithms, potentially improving computational efficiency and numerical
stability in scenarios involving such input lengths. While the leading constant of TFFT’s
arithmetic complexity (
7N\log_{2}N
) is higher than some highly optimized FFTs, the algorithm presents unique structural benefits. These include straightforward parallelization due to the independence of compressed sub-DFTs and a very simple index reordering step for combining results, instead of complex butterfly operations. Furthermore, its reliance on global shifts and additions suggests potential for simple and efficient hardware implementations

The TFFT algorithm represents a new paradigm for DFT computation. Future work will focus on several promising directions. These include the development of optimized implementations tailored for various hardware architectures (CPUs, GPUs, FPGAs, ASICs), a more exhaustive formal analysis of its numerical stability across different input types and lengths, and exploring its application and potential advantages in sparse signal processing and for related discrete transforms. The generalization to other compression factors (
p>2
) within the TFFT framework also warrants further investigation.
