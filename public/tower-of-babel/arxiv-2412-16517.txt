1.
Introduction

For any prime
p
we denote by
\nu_{p}(n)
the
p
-adic
valuation
of the integer
n\geq 1
.
Thus, if
n=p^{s}m
and
\gcd(p,m)=1
, then
\nu_{p}(n)=s
.
The
p
-adic valuation is essential in expressing any Kalmar elementary function as an arithmetic term.
As proven by Mazzanti
[
11
]
for
p=2
(also see Marchenkov
[
10
]
),
and recently generalized in
[
16
]
for all larger primes
(also see
[
17
]
), the value of
\nu_{p}(n)
can be written as

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(n)=\left\lfloor\frac{\big(\gcd(n,p^{n})\big)^{n+1}\left(\mathrm{mod\;}(p^{n+1}-1)^{2}\right)}{p^{n+1}-1}\right\rfloor.

According to Mazzanti
[
11
, Lemma 3.3 and Lemma 4.2]
, the functions
\gcd(a,b)
and
\binom{2n}{n}
, can also be expressed through certain such arithmetic formulas.
It is then a consequence of Kummer’s theorem that the
Hamming weight

\wt(n)
, which is defined as the number of digits equal with
1
in the binary representation of
n\geq 1
,
is expressible by the arithmetic term

\displaystyle\wt(n)=\nu_{2}\binom{2n}{n}.

We note that the Hamming weight function is essential in expressing the number of solutions of an exponential Diophantine equation with parameters as an arithmetic term depending on these parameters.
Following that, it is conceivable that one could optimize the construction of arithmetic terms for Kalmar elementary functions
if one could find simpler arithmetic terms to express the functions
\nu_{2}(n)
and

\binom{2n}{n}
.
Along these lines, our object in this article is to study the
p
-adic valuation
\nu_{p}(n)

from the point of view of the generating function of the sequence

\{\nu_{p}(n)\}_{n\geq 1}
for any prime
p
.

For any prime
p
, the
generating function

V_{p}(X)
of
the sequence
\{\nu_{p}(n)\}_{n\geq 1}
is the following formal power series in
X
:

V_{p}(X)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\nu_{p}(n)X^{n}\,.

(1)

The radius of convergence of
V_{p}(X)
at
0
is
R=1
.
Then, since
1/2<R
, and

\{\nu_{p}(n)\}_{n\geq 1}

is a sequence of integers satisfying

\nu_{p}(n)\leq\log_{p}n

for all
n\geq 1
,
according
to
[
15
, Theorem 2]
, we have the following uniform representation of the function
\nu_{p}(n)
for all primes
p\geq 2
:

\displaystyle\nu_{p}(n)=\left\lfloor 2^{n^{2}}V_{p}(2^{-n})\right\rfloor\left(\mathrm{mod\;}2^{n}\right)\ \text{ for all $n\geq 1$.}

However, the results of the present article seriously diminish the hope that one could transform the expression above into some usable tool to compute the
p
-adic valuation. Thus, in Theorem
1
we prove that the function
V_{p}(X)
is non-holonomic. Stated differently, not only that the series is not rational or algebraic, but it is far from any combination of familiar analytic functions. Also, we show in Theorem
2
that if
X
is taken from a certain large class of rational numbers then the value
V_{p}(X)
is transcendental.

Our results entail a broader potential and can be applied to the following objects.
Let
q\geq 2
be an integer that is not necessarily prime.
Using the same notation as for the valuation function, for every integer
n\geq 1
we let

\displaystyle\nu_{q}(n):=\max\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\,:\,q^{k}\,|\,n\}.

Given this more general function
\nu_{q}(n)
,
let
V_{q}(X)
be defined as before by formula (
1
).
Then, for any integer
k\geq 2
, let
V_{q,k}(X)
be the reduced modulo
k
version of
V_{q}(X)
, which is formally obtained by reducing all coefficients modulo
k
, that is,

\displaystyle V_{q,k}(X):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\big(\nu_{q}(n)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)\big)X^{n}\,,

(2)

where the coefficients are understood to be the unique integers in the interval
[0,k-1]
that are congruent to
\nu_{q}(n)
modulo
k
.
We are now ready to state our main results.

Theorem 1
.

The series
V_{q}(X)
and
V_{q,k}(X)
defined by (
1
) and (
2
)
are both non-holonomic for every integers
q,k\geq 2
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
a
and
b
be relatively prime positive integers with ratio
a/b<1
.
Then, the real numbers
V_{q}\big(a/b\big)
and
V_{q,k}\big(a/b\big)
are transcendental for all
integers
q,k\geq 2
satisfying condition

q>\frac{2\log b}{\log b-\log a}\,.

(3)

Moreover, while
q
still satisfies condition (
3
), the complex numbers

V_{q}\big(\omega(q,\ell)\cdot a/b\big)
and
V_{q,k}\big(\omega(q,\ell)\cdot a/b\big)
are also transcendental
for all roots of unity
\omega(q,\ell)=\exp\big(\frac{2\pi h}{q^{\ell}}\big)
,
where
\ell\geq 1
and
0\leq h\leq q^{\ell}-1
.

Theorem 3
.

If
\mathbb{A}
is a field of characteristic
0

consisting of algebraic numbers only, then the power series

V_{q}(X)
and
V_{q,k}(X)
viewed as elements of
\mathbb{A}[[X]]

are not algebraic over
\mathbb{A}(X)
.

We point out that Theorems
2

and
3
are part of certain
more general Theorems
5
and
6

that we prove in Sections
4.2

and
4.4
, respectively.

2.
Non-holonomic series

2.1.
The function
V_{q,k}(X)

A formal power series
f=f(X)\in\mathbb{C}[[X]]
is called
holonomic
if there are polynomials

a_{0}(X),a_{1}(X),\dots,a_{r}(X)

\in\mathbb{C}[X]
, with the leading polynomial
a_{r}(X)\neq 0
, such that the following identity holds

\displaystyle a_{r}(X)\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{r}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{r}}$}f(X)+a_{r-1}(X)\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}^{r-1}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X^{r-1}}$}f(X)+\dots+a_{1}(X)\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}X}$}f(X)+a_{0}(X)f(X)=0.

Holonomic series are the generating functions of the holonomic sequences.
Thus, a sequence
\{c(n)\}_{n\geq 0}
of complex numbers is called
holonomic
if there are
r\geq 0
polynomials

a_{r}(X),a_{r-1}(X),\dots,a_{0}(X)\in\mathbb{C}[X]
, with the leading polynomial
a_{r}(X)
not identically zero, such that for all
n\geq 0
,

\displaystyle a_{r}(n)c(n+r)+a_{r-1}(n)c(n+r-1)+\cdots+a_{0}(n)c(n)=0.

In case that all polynomials
a_{r}(X),\dots,a_{0}(X)

are actually constant complex numbers, which means that they all have degree zero,
the sequence
\{c(n)\}_{n\geq 0}
is called
\mathrm{C}
-
recursive
or
constant-recursive
.
A sequence is
\mathrm{C}
-recursive if and only if its generating function is a rational function
A(X)/B(X)
with
\deg(A)<\deg(B)
(see Kauers and Paule
[
9
]
, and Stanley
[
19
]
).
Examples of representations of
\mathrm{C}
-recursive sequences can be found in articles of Prunescu
[
14
]
and with Sauras-Altuzarra
[
15
]
.

When viewed as a power series in
\mathbb{C}[[X]]
,

V_{q}(X)
is absolutely convergent for
|X|<1
.
The fact that the radius of convergence is
1
can be seen by applying the Cauchy-Hadamard theorem,
or the root test, by observing that the
q
-adic valuation of
n
is bounded by
\log n/\log q
and the equality
holds for all powers of
q
. Therefore

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big(\log n\big)^{1/n}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\exp\Big(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\log\log n}{n}$}\Big)=1,

and, likewise,
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}q^{1/n}=1
.

In addition, the series
V_{q,k}(X)
is absolutely convergent for
|X|<1
and the radius of convergence is
1
. Indeed,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\big(\nu_{q}(n)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)\big)^{1/n}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}\,(k-1)^{1/n}=1,

for all integers
k\geq 2
.

Next we prove the non-holonomy of both the series
V_{q}(X)
and the sequence
\{\nu_{q}(n)\}_{n\geq 1}
for all integers
q\geq 2
.
The result is based on a theorem of Pólya and Carlson
(see Pólya
[
13
]
, Carlson
[
3
]
, or Bieberbach
[
2
]
)
according to which
a series with integer coefficients, which is analytic at the origin and converges in the open unit disc,
is either a rational function or has the open unit circle as natural boundary.
For other implications of the theorem of Pólya and Carlson to non-holonomic functions see Flajolet, Gerhold and Salvy
[
7
,
6
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
q\geq 2
, then the series
V_{q}(X)
is not holonomic.
Also, the sequence
\{\nu_{q}(n)\}_{n\geq 1}
is not holonomic.

Proof.

Case 1
:
V_{q}(X)
is rational. A rational function
A(X)/B(X)
can be always written as
P(X)+C(X)/B(X)
, where
A(X),B(X),C(X),P(X)\in\mathbb{C}[X]
are polynomials, and
\deg(C(X))<\deg(B(X))
.
It follows that
\nu_{q}(n)=x(n)+y(n)
for all integers
n\geq 0
,
where
x(n)
is a sequence with finite support
and
y(n)
is a
\mathrm{C}
-recursive sequence.
Therefore there is some integer
N\geq 0
such that for all

n\geq N
,
\nu_{q}(n)=y(n)
. Let
d\geq 1
be the order of
y
as a C-recursive sequence. It follows that for all
n\geq N
, the sequence
\nu_{q}(n)
satisfies the recurrence with constant coefficients

\displaystyle a_{d}\nu_{q}(n+d)+a_{d-1}\nu_{q}(n+d-1)+\cdots+a_{0}\nu_{q}(n)=0,

which is satisfied by the sequence
y
. Here
a_{d},\dots,a_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
are some constants.
We observe that for every
k\geq 1
the minimal distance between two different natural numbers
n_{1}\neq n_{2}
, with
\nu_{q}(n_{1})\geq k
and
\nu_{q}(n_{2})\geq k
, is
q^{k}
. Take
k
such that
q^{k}>d
. Take
m_{1}>N
and
m_{2}>N
such that
\nu_{q}(m_{1})=k
and
\nu_{q}(m_{2})=k+1
. We see that
\nu_{q}(m_{1}-i)=\nu_{q}(m_{2}-i)

for
i=1,\dots,d
, and this would imply
\nu_{q}(m_{1})=\nu_{q}(m_{2})
, which is a contradiction.

Case 2
:
V_{q}(X)
is not rational.
Since the function
V_{q}(X)
has radius of convergence
R=1
at
0
, but is not a rational function, it follows from the theorem of Pólya and Carlson that
V_{q}(X)
has infinitely many singularities on the circle
C(0,1)
. A dense set of singularities will be described in the next section. But a function which is analytic at
0
and holonomic can be continued analytically along any path that avoids the finite set
\mathcal{S}
that consists of the roots of the polynomial
a_{r}(X)

(see Henrici
[
8
, Theorem 9.1]
) for the existence of analytic solutions for differential equations. But this does not happen with the function
V_{q}(X)
, because it cannot be analytically continued outside the disc
D(0,1)
.
∎

Proposition 2.2
.

If
q,k\geq 2
are integers, then the series
V_{q,k}(X)
is not holonomic. Also, the sequence

\{\nu_{p}(n)\bmod k\}_{n\geq 1}

is not holonomic.

Proof.

The proof follows the same steps as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
. Again, by
the theorem of Pólya-Carlson, the series is either a rational function or has the unit circle as a natural boundary.

Case 1
: If the series was a rational function, then the sequence
\nu_{q}(n)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)
would be ultimately a
\mathrm{C}
-recursive sequence of some degree
d\geq 1
. There are infinitely many integers
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
such that

\nu_{q}(m_{1}-j)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)=\nu_{q}(m_{2}-j)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)

for
j=1,\dots,d
,
since
\nu_{q}(m_{1}-j)=\nu_{q}(m_{2}-j)
,
but
\nu_{q}(m_{1})\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)\not=\nu_{q}(m_{2})\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)
.

Case 2
:
If the series is not a rational function, the same argument from Proposition
2.1
applies.
∎

3.
Alternative expressions, identities and singular points

We write the formal power series
V_{q}(X)
with terms separated in such a way that all coefficients are equal to
1

and group them according to the similar types of powers they have, as follows:

V_{q}(X)=\big(X^{q}+X^{2q}+X^{3q}+\cdots\big)+\big(X^{q^{2}}+X^{2q^{2}}+X^{3q^{2}}+\cdots\big)+\big(X^{q^{3}}+X^{2q^{3}}+X^{3q^{3}}+\cdots\big)+\cdots,

so that

\begin{split}V_{q}(X)&=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}X^{mq}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}X^{mq^{2}}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}X^{mq^{3}}+\cdots\\
&=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{X^{q}}{1-X^{q}}$}+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{X^{q^{2}}}{1-X^{q^{2}}}$}+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{X^{q^{3}}}{1-X^{q^{3}}}$}+\cdots.\end{split}

(4)

The domain where
V_{q}(X)
is analytic cannot be extended outside
|X|<1

as can
still be seen from the expression of
V_{q}(X)
in (
4
),
where the fractions
\frac{X^{q^{k}}}{1-X^{q^{k}}}
tend very fast to zero as
k
increases if
|X|<1
, while all of them explode at any root of unity of order a power of
q
.
Geometrically, the poles of the fractions
\frac{X^{q^{k}}}{1-X^{q^{k}}}
are distributed on the unit circle
\mathcal{C}\big((0,0),1\big)

as the vertices of regular polygons, and their union becomes dense on
\mathcal{C}\big((0,0),1\big)
as
k
tends to infinity.

Now let
h,\ell
be fixed positive integers and let
\omega
be the root of unity

\omega:=\exp\Big(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{2\pi ih}{q^{\ell}}$}\Big).

Then

\begin{split}V_{q}(\omega X)=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{q}X^{q}}{1-\omega^{q}X^{q}}$}+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{q^{2}}X^{q^{2}}}{1-\omega^{q^{2}}X^{q^{2}}}$}+\cdots+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{q^{\ell}}X^{q^{\ell}}}{1-\omega^{q^{\ell}}X^{q^{\ell}}}$}+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{q^{\ell+1}}X^{q^{\ell+1}}}{1-\omega^{q^{\ell+1}}X^{q^{\ell+1}}}$}+\cdots.\end{split}

(5)

Here, in the last two fractions shown and in all the others that follow,
the factors with
\omega
at different powers become equal to
1
,
so that the fractions in the expansion are exactly identical to those of
V_{q}(X)
in (
4
).
As a consequence, the difference
V_{q}(X)-V_{q}(\omega X)
is a rational function.
We summarize the result in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
q\geq 2
and let
h
and
\ell
be positive integers.
Suppose
h
is relatively prime to
q
and let

\omega=\exp\Big(\frac{2\pi ih}{q^{\ell}}\Big)
.
Then

\begin{split}V_{q}(\omega X)-V_{q}(X)&=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{q}X^{q}}{1-\omega^{q}X^{q}}$}+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{q^{2}}X^{q^{2}}}{1-\omega^{q^{2}}X^{q^{2}}}$}+\cdots+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\omega^{q^{\ell-1}}X^{q^{\ell-1}}}{1-\omega^{q^{\ell-1}}X^{q^{\ell-1}}}$}\\
&\quad-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{X^{q}}{1-X^{q}}$}-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{X^{q^{2}}}{1-X^{q^{2}}}$}-\cdots-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{X^{q^{\ell-1}}}{1-X^{q^{\ell-1}}}$}\,.\end{split}

(6)

Remark 3.1
.

Two examples:

(i)

If
\ell=1
, then
\omega=\exp\big(\frac{2\pi i}{q}\big)
, so
\omega^{q}=1
,
which implies
V_{q}(\omega X)=V_{q}(X)
,
equality that is included by convention in (
6
) as well.

(ii)

If
p=2
, we get a nontrivial result with roots of
-1
instead of roots of unity. Indeed,
with
\omega=i
, we have

V_{2}(iX)-V_{2}(X)=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{-X^{2}}{1+X^{2}}$}-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{X^{2}}{1-X^{2}}$}=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{-2X^{2}}{1-X^{4}}$}\,.

Taking
\omega
as a root of unity of order greater than
4
,
a similar outcome is also achieved with the difference
V_{q}(\omega X)-V_{q}(X)

being a rational function over
\mathbb{Q}(\omega)
.

In order to treat the series
V_{q,k}(X)
,
we need to introduce
the
bitty series

f_{r}(X)=f_{q,r}(X)

defined by

\displaystyle f_{r}(X):=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}X^{mq^{r}}=\frac{X^{q^{r}}}{1-X^{q^{r}}}\ \text{ for any $r\geq 1$. }

(7)

Proposition 3.2
.

For every integers
q,k\geq 2
, we let

V_{q,k}(X)
be defined by (
2
) and for every integers

r\geq 1
we let
f_{r}(X)
be defined by (
7
).
Then we have:

\begin{split}V_{q,k}(X)&=f_{1}(X)+\dots+f_{k-1}(X)+(1-k)f_{k}(X)+\\
&\quad+f_{k+1}(X)+\dots+f_{2k-1}(X)+(1-k)f_{2k}(X)+\\
&\quad+f_{2k+1}(X)+\dots+f_{3k-1}(X)+(1-k)f_{3k}(X)+\dots\\
&=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}f_{j}(X),\end{split}

(8)

where

\displaystyle a_{j}=\begin{cases}1,&\textrm{ if }\ k\nmid j;\\[2.0pt]
1-k,&\textrm{ if }\ k\mid j.\end{cases}

(9)

Also, if
h
and
\ell
are positive integers,
h
is relatively prime to
q
and
\omega=\exp\Big(\frac{2\pi ih}{q^{\ell}}\Big)
, then
V_{q,k}(\omega X)-V_{q,k}(X)
is a rational function.

Proof.

For every complex number
x
, with
|x|<1
, since the series

\sum_{n\geq 1}\big(\nu_{q}(n)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)\big)x^{n}
is absolutely convergent, we can rearrange and regroup its terms as follows:

\displaystyle V_{q,k}(x)

\displaystyle=f_{1}(x)+\dots+f_{k-1}(x)+(1-k)f_{k}(x)+

\displaystyle\quad+f_{k+1}(x)+\dots+f_{2k-1}(x)+(1-k)f_{2k}(x)+

\displaystyle\quad+f_{2k+1}(x)+\dots+f_{3k-1}(x)+(1-k)f_{3k}(x)+\cdots

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}f_{j}(x),

where the coefficients
a_{j}
are defined as in (
9
).

Let us examine the expansion in the middle of
the right-hand side of formula (
8
) and
compute the coefficient of
X^{mq^{r}}
,
where
q\nmid m
.
Let
r=dk+v
, where
0\leq v=r\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)<k
. We observe that
X^{mq^{r}}
arises in all
f_{j}(X)
with
0<j\leq r
. The sum of its coefficients equals:

\displaystyle\overbrace{\big(\underbrace{1+\cdots+1}_{k-1\text{ $1$'s}}+(1-k)\big)+\dots+\big(\underbrace{1+\cdots+1}_{k-1\text{ $1$'s}}+(1-k)\big)}^{d\text{ terms}}+\underbrace{1+\cdots+1}_{v\text{ $1$'s}}=v.

Thus, the coefficient of
X^{mq^{r}}
on the right-hand side
of formula (
8
) is

\displaystyle v=r\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)=\nu_{q}(mq^{r})\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right).

The fact that
V_{q,k}(\omega X)-V_{q,k}(X)

is a rational function follows from the above
in a similar way as the rationality of
V_{q}(\omega X)-V_{q}(X)
followed in the proof of Proposition

.
∎

Let
k\geq 2
be a fixed integer.
For every integer
j\geq 1
, we let
A_{k,j}(X)
denote the sum

\displaystyle A_{k,j}(X):=f_{kj+1}(X)+\cdots+f_{kj+k-1}(X)+(1-k)f_{k(j+1)}(X),

where
f_{r}(X)
is defined by (
7
) for

.

We observe that
f_{n}(x)>f_{n+t}(x)
for all real numbers
x\in(0,1)
and all integers
t\geq 1
.

We also observe that for any integer
j\geq 0
and for any
x\in(0,1)
we have:

(i)

A_{k,j}(x)>0
; and

(ii)

A_{k,j}(x)>A_{k,j+1}(x)
.

We say that a sequence

\displaystyle W(X):=\sum_{i\geq 1}a_{i}f_{i}(x)

has positive segments
if there exists a strictly increasing sequence
\{n_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
, such that

\displaystyle B_{j}(x)

\displaystyle:=a_{n_{j}}f_{n_{j}}(x)+a_{n_{j}+1}f_{n_{j}+1}(x)+\dots+a_{n_{j+1}-1}f_{n_{j+1}-1}(x)>0,

and

\displaystyle B_{j}(x)

\displaystyle<B_{j+1}(x)

for all

and for all

.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
q\geq 2
and

be integers. Then the series

and

have positive segments.

Proof.

For

the sequence of indices is

, and

for all

. For

the sequence of indices is

. In this case,

for all

.
∎

4.
Proofs of Theorems
2
and
3

Before proving Theorem
2
, we first outline it
with a particular example.

4.1.
A not so random transcendental encounter

Let
q=3
and let
b
be some ordinary integer, for instance let
b=2024
. Our aim is to show that
\alpha=V_{q}(1/b)
is transcendental.
For this, we will make use of a result by Roth from 1955, which we write in the following form.

Theorem 4

(Roth
[
18
]
)
.

Let
\alpha
be a real number and let
\delta>0
.
Suppose there exists a sequence of distinct rational numbers

\{A_{n}/B_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
and an integer
n_{0}

such that

\begin{split}\Big|\alpha-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{A_{n}}{B_{n}}$}\Big|<\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{B_{n}^{2+\delta}}$}\ \ \text{ for $n\geq n_{0}$.}\end{split}

Then
\alpha
is transcendental.

Since the series
\alpha=V_{3}\big(1/b\big)
is absolutely convergent, we can regroup conveniently and add the terms so that it can be expressed as a sum with the generic terms:

\displaystyle\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{{b}^{-3^{k}}}{1-{b}^{-3^{k}}}$}=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{3^{k}}-1}$}\ \text{ for $k\geq 1$.}

Then, as
n
tends to infinity, we have:

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{{b^{3}-1}}$}+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{3^{2}}-1}$}+\cdots+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{3^{n}}-1}$}+O\bigg(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{3^{n+1}}-1}$}\bigg)\,,

(10)

so that

\begin{split}\alpha&=\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{A_{n}}{B_{n}}$}+O\bigg(\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{b^{3^{n+1}}-1}$}\bigg)\,,\end{split}

where
B_{n}=b^{3^{n}}-1
and
A_{n}

is the corresponding numerator of the ratio that adds up all fractions in
the main term of the approximation of
\alpha
in (
10
).
Taking into account the size of the
constant involved in the big
O(\cdot)

estimate, we find that the following
explicit inequality holds

\begin{split}\Big|\alpha-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{A_{n}}{B_{n}}$}\Big|\leq\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{B_{n}^{5/2}}$}\ \ \text{ if $n\geq n_{0}$}\,,\end{split}

for some positive integer
n_{0}
.
This means that the hypotheses of Theorem
4
are
satisfied with
\delta=1/2
, and consequently it follows that

\alpha
is transcendental.

4.2.
A larger class of transcendental numbers

The arguments from the previous section can be adapted to obtain a large class of transcendental numbers, which we will do in the following.

Theorem 5
.

Let
q,M\geq 2
be integers and let

a
and
b
be relatively prime positive integers with ratio
a/b<1
.
We let
W(X)
be defined by

\displaystyle W(X):=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}f_{j}(X)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{X^{q^{j}}}{1-X^{q^{j}}}$},

where all
a_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
satisfy
|a_{j}|<M
.
If
W(X)
is not a rational function and has positive segments, then
W\big(a/b\big)
is transcendental for all
q\geq 2
for which

\displaystyle q>\frac{2\log b}{\log b-\log a}\,.

Proof.

Let
q\geq 2
and let
a<b
be relatively prime positive integers.
Then

W(a/b)=a_{1}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\big(a/b\big)^{q}}{1-\big(a/b\big)^{q}}$}+a_{2}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\big(a/b\big)^{q^{2}}}{1-\big(a/b\big)^{q^{2}}}$}+\cdots+a_{n}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n}}}{1-\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n}}}$}+E_{p,n}(a,b),

(11)

where
E_{q,n}(a,b)
collects the sum of all fractions of higher order from the corresponding analogue of (
4
).
In order for
E_{q,n}(a,b)
to be small, we take
n
sufficiently large such that

\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n}}<1/2,

(12)

and the corresponding queue is positive. (All the indices considered in the proof will belong to the sub-sequence of positive segments.) The inequality
(a/b)^{q^{n}}<1/2
implies that for
k>n
, the general term of the sum
E_{q,n}(a,b)
is

\left|a_{k}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\big(a/b\big)^{q^{k}}}{1-\big(a/b\big)^{q^{k}}}$}\right|\leq 2M\big(a/b\big)^{q^{k}}.

Using this inequality in the definition of
E_{q,n}(a,b)
,
we can further bound it with the sum of a geometric series as follows

\begin{split}\big|E_{q,n}(a,b)\big|&\leq 2M\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n+1}}+2M\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n+2}}+2M\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n+3}}+\cdots\\
&=2M\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n+1}}\bigg(1+\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n+2}-q^{n+1}}+\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n+3}-q^{n+1}}+\cdots\bigg)\,.\end{split}

Here, the general term is bounded using

x^{q^{n+k}-q^{n+1}}\leq\big(x^{q^{n}}\big)^{k-1}\leq 2^{-k+1},

for
k\geq 1
, provided that
0<x^{q^{n}}<1/2
.
Then, with
x=a/b
, as the assumption (
12
) holds,
it follows

\begin{split}\big|E_{q,n}(a,b)\big|\leq 2M\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n+1}}\big(1+2^{-1}+2^{-2}+\cdots\big)\leq 4M\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n+1}}.\end{split}

(13)

Note that all denominators of the fractions in the main term of

W(a/b)
in (
11
), viewed as polynomials in
a/b
, divide

1-\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n}}
.
Next, we define
A_{n}
and
B_{n}
as the numerator and the denominator
of the sum of fractions of the principal term, that is,

A_{n}:=B_{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{a^{q^{k}}}{b^{q^{k}}-a^{q^{k}}}$},\ \ \text{ and }\ \ B_{n}:=b^{q^{n}}-a^{q^{n}}.

(14)

On combining (
11
), (
14
) and (
13
), we obtain

\Big|W(a/b)-\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{A_{n}}{B_{n}}$}\Big|\leq 4M\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n+1}}.

(15)

Since we want to make use of Roth’s theorem
4
,
we must ensure that the hypotheses are met,
of which, taking the inequality (
15
) into account, it remains to see that
there exists
\delta>0
such that

4M\big(a/b\big)^{q^{n+1}}<\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{1}{B_{n}^{2+\delta}}$}.

(16)

Replacing
B_{n}
by its definition in (
14
),
we find that for (
16
) to hold,
it is enough to show that there exists
\delta>0
and an integer
n_{0}

such that

4M\big(b^{q^{n}}\big)^{2+\delta}a^{q^{n+1}}<b^{q^{n+1}}\ \ \text{ for $n\geq n_{0}$}.

(17)

Taking logarithms and dividing both sides by
q^{n}
, (
17
) is equivalent with

\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\log(4M)}{q^{n}}$}+(2+\delta)\log b+q\log a<q\log b\ \ \text{ for $n\geq n_{0}$}.

(18)

As the inequality in (
18
) is strict,
it is fully achieved when
n
is sufficiently large
if it holds without the single term that depends on
n
, that is, if
there exists
\delta>0
such that

(2+\delta)\log b+q\log a<q\log b.

(19)

Turning back to the exponential form, we see that (
19
)
is equivalent with

b^{2+\delta}a^{q}<b^{q}.

(20)

Once again, due to the strict inequality (
20
),
the existence of a sufficiently small

\delta>0
is ensured if the inequality holds even without the factor
b^{\delta}

on the left side, that is if

b^{2}a^{q}<b^{q}.

(21)

But this is exactly the initial requirement (
3
) first imposed on
a
and
b
in the hypothesis of Theorem
2
.

As we have seen following
now backwards transformations (
20
)–(
16
),
this ensures that the last requirement (
16
)
needed for the application of Roth’s theorem
4

is now fulfilled. As a consequence, we have proved that if integers
a,b

satisfy the hypotheses of Theorem
5
, then

W\big(a/b\big)
is transcendental
except for at most a finite number of integers
q
.
To be more specific, equation (
21
) indicates that
the potential exceptions are bounded in size by

2\log b/(\log b-\log a)
.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
5
.
∎

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
3

If we take
W(X)=V_{q}(X)
, respectively
W(X)=V_{q,k}(X)
, we obtain the results stated in Theorem
2
for the transcendence of their values computed in
a/b
.
The last part of the proof of Theorem
2
,
that regarding the transcendence of
V_{q}\big(\omega a/b\big)
, respectively
V_{q,k}\big(\omega a/b\big)
, where

\omega
is a certain root of unity,
follows from Proposition
3.1
and from Proposition
3.2
.
Indeed, by contradiction, if
V_{q}\big(\omega a/b\big)
were algebraic,
then since we already know that
V_{q}\big(a/b\big)
is transcendental,
it would follow that the difference

V_{q}\big(\omega a/b\big)-V_{q}\big(a/b\big)

is also transcendental.
Yet, this directly contradicts the conclusion of Proposition
3.1
,
which implies that the difference

V_{q}\big(\omega a/b\big)-V_{q}\big(a/b\big)
is an algebraic number. The same for
V_{q,k}\big(\omega a/b\big)
.
This concludes the proof of Theorem
2
.
∎

4.4.
A generalized version of Theorem
3

The following is a more general result that includes the
statement that
V_{q}(X)
and
V_{q,k}(X)
are not algebraic over
\mathbb{A}(X)
.

Theorem 6
.

Let
q\geq 2
and let
M\geq 2
be a constant integer.
Let

\displaystyle W(X)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}f_{j}(X)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}a_{j}\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{X^{q^{j}}}{1-X^{q^{j}}}$},

where all
a_{j}
are integers satisfying
|a_{j}|<M

such that
W(X)
is not a rational function
and has positive segments.
Suppose that
\mathbb{A}
is a field of characteristic
0

consisting of algebraic numbers only.
Then
W(X)\in\mathbb{A}[[X]]
is not algebraic over
the field of rational functions
\mathbb{A}(X)
.

Proof.

Suppose that
W(X)
satisfies an equation

\displaystyle g_{r}(X)W^{r}(X)+g_{r-1}(X)W^{r-1}(X)+\dots+g_{0}(X)=0,

where
g_{r}(X)\neq 0
and
g_{j}(X)\in\mathbb{A}(X)

for
0\leq j\leq r
.
The rational functions
g_{j}(X)
are not defined in a finite set of algebraic numbers, which are poles for at least one of them. Then there will always exist a rational number
a/b\in(0,1)
that satisfies the hypotheses of Theorem
5
for
W(X)
, and which is different from all the finitely many exceptional algebraic numbers in which at least one rational function
g_{j}(X)
is not defined.
Consequently

\displaystyle g_{r}(a/b)W(a/b)^{r}+g_{r-1}(a/b)W(a/b)^{r-1}+\cdots+g_{0}(a/b)=0.

But this would mean that
W(a/b)
is algebraic, which contradicts Theorem
5
.
This contradiction invalidates the assumption made, thus concluding the proof of Theorem
6
.
∎

5.
Application to some automatic sequences

Let
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{T}
be finite sets and let
w\geq 2
be integer.
A
finite deterministic automaton with output
is a structure
\Auto=(\mathcal{S},w,\mathcal{T},s_{0},\delta,\pi)
defined as follows: the
state set
is
\mathcal{S}
(also called the
input alphabet
) is the set
\{0,1,\dots,w-1\}
, the
output alphabet
is
\mathcal{T}
, the
start state

is
s_{0}
, the
transition function
is
\delta:\mathcal{S}\times\{0,1,\dots,w-1\}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
, and the
output function
is
\pi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}
.

Let
\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}^{*}
be the set of all finite words built up with
0,1,\dots,k-1
.

We let
\tilde{\delta}:\mathcal{S}\times\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}^{*}\rightarrow\mathcal{S}
be the
transitive closure

of the transition function
\delta
be defined as follows.
If the word
c\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}^{*}
has length
|c|=1
then
\tilde{\delta}(s_{0},c)=\delta(s_{0},c)
. If
c\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}^{*}
is any word and
\tilde{\delta}(s_{0},c)\in\mathcal{S}
is known, then

\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}(s_{0},ac)=\delta\big(\tilde{\delta}(s_{0},c),a\big)\ \text{ for every letter
$a\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}$.}

The sequence
a:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}
is called
w
-
automatic
if there is a finite deterministic automaton
\Auto
such that
a(n)=\pi\big(\tilde{\delta}(s_{0},n_{w})\big)

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, where the word
n_{w}\in\{0,1,\dots,w-1\}^{*}
denotes the representation of
n
in base
w
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
w,k\geq 2
be integers.
Consider the sequence

a:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
given by

\displaystyle a(n)=\nu_{w}(n)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right).

(Here, the representative of the residue class modulo
k
is taken from the interval
[0,k-1]
.)
Then the sequence
\{a(n)\}_{n\geq 1}

is
w
-automatic.

Proof.

We consider the automaton

\Auto=(\mathcal{S},w,\{0,1,\dots,k-1\},s_{0},\delta,\pi)

defined as follows.

Let the state set
\mathcal{S}
be given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\big\{\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}(j,b):\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}(j,b)=j\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)\text{ for }j\in\{0,\dots,k-1\}\text{ and }b\in\{0,1\}\big\}.

The start state is
s_{0}=\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}(0,0)
.
Next, the transition function
\delta
works based on the set of rules:

\displaystyle\delta\big(\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}(j,0),0\big)

\displaystyle=\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}(j+1,0),

\displaystyle\delta\big(\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}(j,0),x\big)

\displaystyle=\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}(j,1),

\displaystyle\delta\big(\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}(j,1),a\big)

\displaystyle=\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}(j,1),

for all
x=1,2,\dots,k-1
and for all
a=0,1,\dots,k-1
,
and
the output function
\pi
is defined by

\displaystyle\pi\big(\bm{{\alpha}}_{k}(j,b)\big)=j.

Then, let us observe that

\displaystyle\pi\big(\tilde{\delta}(s_{0},n_{w})\big)=\nu_{w}(n)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)\ \text{ for every $n\geq 1$.}

This concludes the proof of Theorem
7
.
∎

A result of Christol et al.
[
4
, Theorem 1]
(see also Allouche and
Shallit
[
1
, Theorem 12.2.5]
)
provides an algebraic characterisation of the

q
-automatic sequences, where
q=p^{s}
is a prime-power.
Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be a finite field that is large enough
such that there exists a one-to-one function

\beta:\mathcal{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
Consider the following formal power series associated with the sequence
\{a(n)\}_{n\geq 0}
:

\displaystyle a(X)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta\big(a(j)\big)X^{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[[X]].

Then the sequence
\{a(n)\}_{n\geq 0}
is
q
-automatic if and only if
a(X)
is algebraic over the field of rational functions
\mathbb{F}_{q}(X)
. It is also known that if
p
is a prime number, a sequence is
p
-automatic if and only if it is
p^{s}
-automatic for each prime-power
p^{s}
. (For the last result, see Allouche and Shallit
[
1
, Theorem 6.6.4]
.)
Also, according to
Denef and
Lipshitz
[
5
, Theorem 4.1]
,
if
p^{s}
is a prime-power, a sequence
a:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}\mathbb{Z}

is
p
-automatic if and only if there is a sequence
b:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, where
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is the ring of
p
-adic integers, such that
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
a(n)\equiv b(n)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p^{s}\right)
and the
power series
\sum b(j)X^{j}
is algebraic over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[X]]
.

Let now
p
be a prime number and
let
s\geq 1
and
k\geq 2
be integers. By
\nu_{p^{s}}(n)
we understand the largest integer

v
such that
p^{sv}
divides
n
.
(If
s=1
, this is
\nu_{p^{s}}=\nu_{p}
, the classical
p
-adic valuation.)
We consider the formal power series

\displaystyle V_{p^{s},k}(X)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\big(\nu_{p^{s}}(j)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)\big)X^{j}

over various rings, which remain to be specified.
This object has a series of properties that we list
next.

Theorem 8
.

Let
p
be a prime number
and let
s\geq 1
, and
k\geq 2
be integers.
The following statements hold true:

(i)

The sequence
\{a(n)\}_{n\geq 1}
given by
a(n)=\nu_{p^{s}}(n)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)
is both
p^{s}
-automatic and
p
-automatic
.

(ii)

For every
q=p^{s}\geq k
and for every one-to-one function
\beta:\{0,\dots,k\}\rightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q}
, the series

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\beta\big(\nu_{p^{s}}(j)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)\big)X^{j}\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[[X]]

is algebraic over
\mathbb{F}_{q}(X)
.

(iii)

There is a sequence of
p
-adic integers

b:\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}_{p}
such that the formal power series
\sum b(j)X^{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[X]]
is algebraic over

\mathbb{Z}_{p}(X)

(where
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is the ring of
p
-adic integers) and such that

\displaystyle\big(\nu_{p^{s}}(j)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}k\right)\big)\equiv b(j)\left(\mathrm{mod\;}p^{s}\right)\ \text{for all $j\geq 1$.}

(iv)

For any field
\mathbb{A}
of characteristic
0
consisting of algebraic numbers only, the power series
V_{p^{s},k}(X)\in\mathbb{A}[[X]]
is not algebraic over
\mathbb{A}(X)
.

We observe that the last item is also related to Theorem
3.2
.

Proof.

To prove part (
i
), we put
w=p^{s}
in Theorem
7
and recall that for every prime
p
, a sequence is

p^{s}
-automatic for some
s\geq 2
if and only if it is

p
-automatic.

Part (
ii
) follows from the theorem of Christol.

Part (
iii
) follows from the theorem of Denef and Lipshitz.

Part (
iv
) follows from Theorem
3
.
∎
