First palliation for the fragile: Short-term outcomes of ductal stenting versus surgical shunt in small infants with restricted pulmonary blood flow
Authors: Olfi Lelya, Alesia Prillya Mauna, Michael Sugiyanto, Sisca Natalia Siagian, Yovi Kurniawati, Oktavia Lilyasari, Aditya Agita Sembiring, Damba Dwisepto Aulia Sakti, Indriwanto Sakidjan Atmosudigdo, Rissa Ummy Setiani, Radityo Prakoso
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:In congenital heart disease (CHD) with restricted pulmonary blood flow, infants require urgent palliation, either with a modified Blalock–Taussig–Thomas (m-BTT) shunt or ductal stenting. However, evidence in infants weighing under 5 kg remains limited. This study aims to compare short-term outcomes after the two approaches in infants weighing <5 kg.Methods:This retrospective cohort study included infants weighing under 5 kg diagnosed with CHD and restricted pulmonary blood flow who underwent either ductal stenting or m-BTT shunt between 2018 and 2023 at the National Cardiovascular Centre Harapan Kita. Outcome assessment included mortality, reintervention, rehospitalization, complications, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, and total hospital duration within 30 days.Results:After the 1:1 propensity score matching, we analyzed 106 infants, comprising 53 infants (mean weight: 3525.7 ± 682.0 g) who underwent ductal stenting and 53 infants (mean: weight 3700.9 ± 649.0 g) who received m-BTT shunt surgery. Mortality (18.9% vs. 22.6%; P = 0.63), reintervention (1.9% vs. 7.5%; P = 0.36), and complication (54.7% vs. 71.7%; P = 0.07) rates were not significantly different between the two groups within 30 days. However, rehospitalization rates (3.8% vs. 15.1%; P = 0.046) were significantly smaller in the ductal stenting group. Furthermore, ductal stenting was associated with significantly shorter durations of both in total hospitalization (8 [1–42] vs. 12 [0–75] days; P < 0.001) and in intensive care stay (2 [0–19] vs. 6 [1–37] days; P < 0.001).Conclusions:Both ductal stenting and m-BTT shunt demonstrated comparable short-term efficacy in palliating cyanosis among infants weighing <5 kg, with reasonable periprocedural safety in both the groups. Ductal stenting was associated with shorter total and ICU length of stay as well as lower rehospitalization rates, suggesting potential recovery advantages, though prospective studies are needed to guide individualized decision-making.

INTRODUCTION

Congenital heart disease (CHD) is the most common birth defect in newborns, affecting approximately 1.2% of live births globally.[12] CHD patients with reduced pulmonary blood flow often require palliative procedure to increase pulmonary blood flow before a definitive surgical procedure can be performed. Currently there are two palliative options, which are the modified Blalock-Thomas-Taussig (m-BTT) shunt, a surgical shunt, and ductal stenting.[34] m-BTT shunt has been widely used as the preferred palliation strategy for a long time since its introduction in 1944. However, it results in notably high mortality and morbidity, especially in infants with lower weight.

Ductal stenting is a relatively novel and minimally invasive treatment option for duct-dependent CHD. It can act as a bridge to the definitive surgical treatment.[5] Ductal stenting has become an increasingly attractive and ideal treatment option due to its percutaneous approach, which is associated with minimal procedural incisions, reduced anesthetic exposures, and potentially faster recovery.[456] However, studies comparing ductal stenting with m-BTT shunt have yielded varying outcomes, and neither approach has demonstrated consistent superiority across all patient populations.[78910111213] Among the most recent comparative evidence, a study by Ganta et al. showed comparable outcomes between ductal stenting and aortopulmonary shunts in single ventricle patients.[11] Meanwhile, a study by Ghaderian et al. demonstrated favorable periprocedural safety of ductal stenting with only one recorded mortality in their ductal stenting cohort. Notably, their study population consisted of younger infants, making it particularly relevant to the low-weight subgroup of clinical interest.[12]

Despite the growing yet varying evidence regarding ductal stenting as a safe and effective palliative option for duct-dependent CHD, periprocedural and short-term outcome data remain scarce in the specific stratum of infants weighing <5 kg. This is a critical gap, as low body weight is a well-recognized determinant of procedural risk and postprocedural outcomes in both catheter-based and surgical interventions. While a case series has illustrated the technical feasibility of ductal stenting in infants weighing <2 kg,[14] direct comparisons of periprocedural safety and short-term outcomes between ductal stenting and m-BTT shunt in this weight stratum remain limited to small, single-center studies with modest sample sizes. Therefore, this study aims to address the gap by comparing periprocedural and short-term outcomes between ductal stenting and m-BTT shunt in infants with restricted pulmonary blood flow weighing <5 kg, treated at a single high-volume cardiac referral center. By offering weight-specific insights, we seek to guide decision-making processes and optimize outcomes for this group of vulnerable patients.

METHODS

Study design and settings

A single-center retrospective cohort study based on medical record review was conducted from January 2018 to December 2023. Patient data were extracted from the institutional medical record system. Ethical approval was obtained from the relevant Institutional Review Board prior to study commencement.

Study population

The study population comprised of pediatric patients, weighing <5 kg, with restricted pulmonary blood flow CHD who underwent either ductal stenting procedure or m-BTT shunt surgery. Inclusion criteria were patients with CHD requiring palliative intervention, either by ductal stenting or m-BTT shunt procedure, bodyweight under 5 kg, and patients with pulmonary blood flow restrictions. Meanwhile, patients who had any prior intervention and patients with other sources of pulmonary blood flow than the pulmonary arteries or the ductus arteriosus were excluded. Procedures were further classified by urgency as elective, urgent, which is done within a week, and emergent.

Procedural selection was determined through a structured multidisciplinary case-by-case discussion between the institution’s interventional pediatric cardiologists and cardiothoracic surgeons. Prior to each discussion, patients underwent preprocedural assessments including transthoracic echocardiography as the primary imaging modality, with cardiac computed tomography angiography performed selectively in cases where echocardiographic findings remained inconclusive and the patient’s hemodynamic status permitted. The decision to proceed with ductal stenting or m-BTT shunt was made collectively based on a combination of clinical and anatomical factors, including ductal morphology, pulmonary artery anatomy, hemodynamic stability, body weight, age, and operator experience and preference. No formal written institutional protocol existed for procedural allocation; instead, each case was individualized through multidisciplinary consensus.

Study outcome

The outcome measures included mortality, reintervention, rehospitalization, and complication rates within 30 days after the procedure. Complications in this study were defined as adverse events occurring during the postprocedural period following ductal stenting or m-BTT shunt placement, including bleeding, stent or shunt failure, arrhythmia, thrombosis-related events (such as acute limb ischemia, transient limb ischemia, or femoral thrombosis), and infection. Furthermore, overall hospital or total length of stay (LOS) as well as length of intensive care unit (ICU) stay were also assessed, which were measured after the procedure was performed.

Statistical analyses

To minimize the effects of confounding variables and the potential selection bias in this study, propensity score matching (PSM) was performed. Propensity scores were estimated using binary logistic regression, with the type of procedure as the dependent variable, and the following covariate: age, preprocedure weight, preprocedural oxygen saturation, and procedure urgency status. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching algorithm without replacement was applied, with a caliper width of 0.2 standard deviations of the logit of the propensity score. Matching adequacy was assessed by comparing standardized mean differences of covariates before and after matching. The PSM was conducted using RStudio version 2026.04.0 + 526 (Posit Software, PBC).

Data in the form of numerical variables are presented as means with their standard deviations, or medians with their minimum and maximum value depending on the data normality, while data in the form of categorical variables are presented as proportions. To assess the normality of data distribution, Kolmogorov–Smirnov was performed. P >0.05 was considered indicative of a normal distribution. Numerical data were analyzed using an independent t-test if the data are normally distributed or the Mann–Whitney U test if not. Meanwhile, categorical data were analyzed with a Chi-square test. Statistical significance was indicated with P ≤ 0.05. SPSS version 29.0 was utilized to conduct statistical analysis (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

PSM yielded 53 matched pairs, resulting in a final matched cohort of 106 patients (53 per group). DISCUSSION

The m-BTT shunt has been the traditionally preferred palliation strategy for patients with pulmonary blood flow restriction. However, the Society of Thoracic Surgeons Congenital Heart Surgery Database reported that the early postoperative mortality of m-BTT shunt is approximately 7%, with the morbidity rate of 11%–13%.[15] Since its introduction, the ductal stenting has increasingly become the preferred approach for maintaining pulmonary blood flow in younger, smaller, and more vulnerable patients. The present study compared the short-term outcomes between ductal stenting and m-BTT shunt in pediatric patients weighing <5 kg with restricted pulmonary blood. This population is important to study, as weight plays a critical role in the success of m-BTT shunt. According to a study by Amelia et al., weight <4.25 kg increased the risk of mortality after m-BTT shunt to 20.8 times in an Indonesian center.[16] The role of preoperative weight may be related to the nutritional status and graft availability. With the higher mortality and complication rates, the decision on performing the m-BTT shunt should be thoroughly tailored to the clinical and anatomical characteristics of the patients.

Short-term outcomes of ductal stenting versus modified Blalock–Thomas–Taussig shunt

Mortality, reintervention, complications, rehospitalization, and LOS (total and intensive care) were selected as outcomes because they comprehensively reflect the safety, effectiveness, and early clinical impact of ductal stenting compared with m-BTT shunt. This study found that ductal stenting was not significantly different from m-BTT shunt in terms of all-cause mortality. The finding is consistent with several previously reported studies. A study by Amoozgar et al. in 2012 showed that there is no significant difference between the ductal stenting and m-BTT shunt groups in terms of outcome and mortality.[7] A study by Glatz et al., which found that the mortality rate between the two groups was not significantly different, also confirmed the finding.[10] Our finding demonstrates that ductal stenting offers immediate safety and feasibility comparable to the m-BTT shunt.

Although comparable between the two groups, our study appeared to have higher mortality rates compared to most published international cohorts.[101517] Several factors specific to our patient population and healthcare setting may explain this discrepancy. Our cohort was intentionally restricted for high-risk infants, which were infants weighing under 5 kg. A study by Amelia et al. demonstrated that weight below 4.25 kg was associated with a 20.8-fold increase in mortality following m-BTT shunt in an Indonesian cohort.[16] Furthermore, as a national referral center in lower-middle-income countries, our patients likely present late with established cyanosis, active sepsis during intervention, or concurrent heart failure. With these factors in mind, it is important to interpret our mortality data within its appropriate clinical and institutional context, rather than as a direct comparison with international benchmarks derived from fundamentally different patient populations and healthcare systems.

This study also found that the reintervention rate was not significantly different between the two groups. Reintervention is an important variable to assess because unplanned reintervention is often the predictor of life-threatening conditions such as stent occlusion or thrombotic shunt. A study from McMullan et al. in a Seattle Center showed that there is no difference in freedom from reintervention between the ductal stenting and m-BTT shunt groups.[8] We exclude the previously planned additional interventions as they may not be related to worsening cyanosis. Despite the statistical insignificance, the number of reinterventions in this study is higher in the m-BTT shunt group. This may be related to the higher complication rate in the group, which is higher than the ductal stenting group. Previous studies also reported a similar outcome, which exhibited higher complication rates in m-BTT shunt compared to ductal stenting.[89] Acute blockage of the shunt is among the reasons for reintervention, which was the most common reason in the current study along with reopened ductus after ligation for the m-BTT group. Meanwhile, the patent ductus arteriosus stenting group only reported one case of reintervention due to pulmonary valve restenosis.

Overall complication rates were lower in the ductal stenting group (54.7%) compared to the m-BTT group (71.7%), despite being statistically insignificant (P = 0.07). However, this trend is consistent with the broader literature, particularly a systematic review and meta-analysis by Alsagheir et al.[18] involving four studies, which demonstrated that ductal stenting was associated with a significantly lower risk of complications compared with m-BTT shunt. The aforementioned meta-analysis[18] further supports the directional trend observed in our cohort. However, there are two individual complications that differ significantly between the two groups. Thrombosis-related events were significantly more frequent in the ductal stenting group (15.1% vs. 0%; P = 0.006), consistent with prior studies, which reflects the known risk of thrombosis in small vessels. Conversely, pneumonia occurred more commonly in the m-BTT group (30.2% vs. 11.3%; P = 0.03), which is likely reflecting prolonged mechanical ventilation and the pulmonary vulnerability associated with open surgical intervention in low-weight neonates. Several other complications, including cardiac arrest (20.8% vs. 13.2%; P = 0.44) and arrhythmia (17.0% vs. 7.5%; P = 0.24), trended higher in the m-BTT shunt group without reaching significance, possibly reflecting the hemodynamic burden of surgical palliation in low-weight neonates. Notably, although these complications were more frequent in the m-BTT group, their incidence in the ductal stenting cohort remained considerable, indicating that both strategies are associated with a substantial risk of morbidity for these small infants.

Despite the comparable results in mortality, reintervention, and complication rate with the m-BTT shunt group, the ductal stenting group had significantly lower rehospitalization rate as well as shorter length of overall hospital and ICU stay. These findings are similar to previous studies. Glatz et al. and Lemley et al. reported that the ICU and hospital LOS is significantly shorter in the ductal stenting group compared to the m-BTT shunt group, confirming the present study.[1017] Mallula et al. also showed that the m-BTT shunt group had longer median length of hospital stay compared to ductal stenting.[9] Shorter rehospitalization rates and reduced LOS with ductal stenting not only reflect faster recovery and procedural efficiency but also translate into lower healthcare utilization and reduced burden for families. The longer ICU and hospital stay in the m-BTT shunt group is also related to its higher complication rate. In our study, pneumonia, sepsis, and surgical site infection were not uncommon in the m-BTT shunt group, complicating the postoperative recovery. Therefore, the observed reductions in hospital LOS and ICU duration in our study support the clinical benefits of ductal stenting in this high-risk population.

Study limitation

This study is the first to compare short-term outcomes between ductal stenting and m-BTT shunt in low-weight infants using weight as a specific population marker. While these findings offer valuable insight into the short-term outcomes of palliative procedures in this vulnerable population, several limitations should be acknowledged. The nonprotocolized, clinician-directed selection process is acknowledged as a potential source of selection bias, which may have influenced the comparability of the two treatment groups. Although baseline characteristics such as sex and procedural status were similar between the groups, significant differences were observed in age at intervention, weight, and preintervention oxygen saturation as patients with better clinical characteristics and more favorable anatomy tend to be assigned to undergo the ductal stenting. These baseline imbalances may have affected postintervention outcomes and should be carefully considered when interpreting the comparative findings of this study.

Beyond the selection procedure, the retrospective design based on medical record review poses inherent risks of documentation bias and the inability to control of unmeasured confounders, precluding any causal inference between procedural choice and clinical outcomes. Another drawback of the retrospective design is potential temporal bias. The study spanned 6 years, during which institutional experience with ductal stenting may have evolved. Although PSM was performed to minimize the risk of selection bias, the increasing proportion of stenting cases in the later years of the study period may reflect a learning curve effect that could confound direct comparisons between the groups. As a single-center study conducted at a high-volume national referral center, institutional expertise, operator experience, and resource availability may not be representative of other settings, limiting the broader generalizability of these findings. Moreover, the 30-day follow-up window of this study precludes assessment of several clinically important longer-term endpoints, including interstage mortality, longevity of palliation, loss to follow-up, and progression to definitive surgical repair. The interval between initial palliation and subsequent corrective surgery remains unanswered in this weight stratum. Finally, more subjects are required to better reflect the difference in clinical outcomes between the two groups, which can be achieved by conducting multicenter studies.

CONCLUSION

This study found that in low-weight infants under 5 kg, mortality, reintervention, and complication rates were not significantly different between the ductal stenting group and the m-BTT shunt group suggesting that both the groups are effective in palliating cyanosis with reasonable safety in short-term follow-up. However, the rehospitalization rate was significantly lower in the ductal stent group, and both total hospital stay and ICU stay were significantly shorter in the ductal stenting group compared to the m-BTT shunt group. The findings of this study suggest that ductal stenting may offer logistical and recovery advantages without compromising short-term outcomes and support its consideration as a viable palliative alternative in this high-risk weight category. However, the nonrandomized retrospective design of this study and the inherent selection bias in procedural allocation should be taken into consideration in interpreting the study results. Prospective studies with defined selection criteria with longer follow-up duration are needed to better guide individualized decision-making between the two approaches for the vulnerable group.
