Impact of cardiovascular magnetic resonance-derived right ventricular ejection fraction on adverse outcomes: A robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis
Authors: Tetsuji Kitano, František Bartoš, Yosuke Nabeshima, Alex Ali Sayour, Attila Kovács, Masaaki Takeuchi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere are few meta-analyses examining the prognostic value of right ventricular ejection fraction (RVEF) for a specific type of cardiovascular disease (CVD). The aim of this study was to compare the association of cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR)-derived RVEF with adverse outcomes for several specific types of CVD, using a robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis.MethodsThree databases were searched for CMR articles reporting hazard ratios (HRs) of RVEF restricted to a specific type of CVD. For each specific type of CVD, Bayesian model-averaged meta-analyses with and without publication bias adjustments were conducted to evaluate the strength of evidence for RVEF according to the Bayes factor (BF).ResultsAmong 108 articles (21,166 patients) analyzing 11 CVD types, pooled HR for 5% reduction in RVEF assessed by publication bias-unadjusted, Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis offered moderate or strong evidence of an association with outcomes for all types of CVD (HR: 1.07–1.37, BF10: 4.3–9.6 * 107). In contrast, a robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis, adjusted for publication bias, found moderate or strong evidence in favor of an association of RVEF with outcomes only in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HR: 1.19, 95% credible interval (CrI): 0.98–1.42, BF10: 5.0), dilated cardiomyopathy (HR: 1.16, 95% CrI: 1–1.22, BF10: 23.3), pulmonary hypertension (HR: 1.05, 95% CrI: 1–1.12, BF10: 3.0), and aortic stenosis (HR: 1.15, 95% CrI: 0.97–1.34, BF10: 4.2). There was weak evidence for an association of RVEF with adverse outcomes in seven other CVDs.ConclusionIn a Bayesian meta-analysis adjusted for publication bias, there was moderate or strong evidence for an association of RVEF with outcomes for only four CVDs. Additional data may strengthen evidence regarding other CVDs.

Introduction

Right ventricular (RV) ejection fraction (EF) has recently received increased attention since it offers incremental value over left ventricular (LV) systolic and diastolic parameters in predicting major clinical outcomes [1]. RVEF improves risk stratification in predicting heart failure (HF) hospitalization and all-cause mortality [2], [3]. In addition, with the advent of transcatheter tricuspid valve therapy for severe tricuspid regurgitation (TR), assessment of RV remodeling parameters, such as RV size and global function, has become necessary for decision-making in patients with TR [4]. Although RV functional echocardiographic parameters, such as tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE), fractional area change (FAC), and RV free-wall longitudinal strain (fwLS), are easy to obtain and have independent prognostic ability, there is a controversy regarding whether RVEF has greater utility than traditional two-dimensional echocardiography parameters [5], [6].

Cardiovascular magnetic resonance (CMR) is considered the gold standard for noninvasive estimation of RVEF with high accuracy and reproducibility [7]. Several meta-analyses investigating the prognostic value of RVEF using CMR have been reported. RVEF was the strongest prognostic factor among RV systolic and remodeling parameters in pulmonary hypertension (PH), strongly associated with adverse outcomes in HF, regardless of LVEF, and significantly related to increased mortality in cardiac amyloidosis [8], [9], [10], [11]. However, no meta-analysis studies have compared the prognostic value of CMR-derived RVEF among specific types of cardiovascular disease (CVD).

Accordingly, the purpose of this study was to systematically review CMR articles restricted to a specific CVD group to investigate the association between RVEF and outcomes and to compare the prognostic value of RVEF among several specific CVDs using a robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines and was prospectively registered with the International prospective registry of systematic reviews database (CRD42023394158) [12]. Institutional Ethical Review Board approval was not required because this study concerned published literature.

Search strategy

Literature reporting the association of CMR-derived RVEF with outcomes was searched in three electronic databases (PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library) through July 30, 2022, using the keywords “RVEF,” “CMR,” “Prognosis,” and related phrases (Supplementary Table 1). In addition, reference lists of all relevant articles identified in the full-text screening were reviewed to search for relevant studies.

Literature selection

Titles and abstracts of collected publications (Identification) were reviewed, after removing duplicate articles (Screening), and ineligible articles. Full-text assessments were conducted (Eligibility), and eligible articles were selected (Included). Inclusion and exclusion criteria applied to screening and eligibility were as follows. Inclusion criteria: (1) articles that included adult patients (≥18 years of age) who underwent CMR, (2) articles that reported univariable or multivariate hazard ratios (HRs) of RVEF for death, cardiovascular-, or pulmonary-related events (lung transplantation) using Cox proportional hazards models, and (3) peer-reviewed full-text articles. Exclusion criteria: (1) articles that included children, (2) articles that included non-cardiac disease patients, (3) articles not restricted to a specific disease group, (4) articles that included non-human subjects, (5) articles with missing HRs for RVEF, and (6) case reports, conference abstracts, reviews, editorials, or comments. Two authors (T.K., Y.N.) independently reviewed the searched literature.

Data extraction

From all eligible articles, the following parameters were extracted: (1) first author name, (2) publication year, (3) publication journal, (4) number of patients, (5) characteristics of patients (age, gender, background disease), (6) CMR vendor, (7) numbers of events, (8) types of events, (9) follow-up duration, (10) outcome, (11) mean and standard deviation (SD) of RVEF, (12) HR of RVEF, and (13) mean, SD, and HR of RVFAC, TAPSE, or RVfwLS, if available.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as means ± SDs, and categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers or percentages. HRs and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for RVEF in univariable and multivariable analysis were transformed to logarithmic HRs and standard errors and were rescaled by a 5% reduction in RVEF. Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis with and without publication bias adjustment was conducted to evaluate the strength of evidence for RVEF in each disease according to Bayes factor (BF), using the package “RoBMA” [13], [14], [15]. HR for CMR-derived RVEF was also compared with echocardiography-derived RV parameters (TAPSE, FAC, RVfwLS) (since they are different modalities, this is a complementary evaluation). To compare the HR of RVEF and that of other RV parameters (TAPSE, FAC, and RVfwLS) in the same subjects, the ratio of HRs, which was defined as the HR for a 1-SD reduction in RVEF divided by the HR for a 1-SD reduction in RV parameters, was calculated. A ratio greater than one indicates that a 1-SD reduction in RVEF is associated with a greater increase in hazard compared to a 1-SD reduction in RV parameters. BF10 is the ratio of the probability of data under the alternative hypothesis to the probability of data under the null hypothesis. An alternative hypothesis was defined as “pooled HR is higher than 1.” The interpretation of BF10 was based on previously reported evidence categories (Supplementary Table 2) [16]. Estimated effect size and its 95% credible interval (CrI) were calculated using a prior effect with Student-t distribution with a location of 0 and a scale of 0.13. An inverse gamma with a shape of 2.42 and a scale of 0.30 was used to evaluate heterogeneity [16]. Publication bias-unadjusted, Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis was conducted with the “NoBMA” function, as was publication bias-adjusted analysis (Robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis) was also conducted by “RoBMA” function [15]. BFrf is the ratio of the probability of data under the random-effects model divided by the probability of data under the fixed-effects model. BFrf was also interpreted using the same evidence categories (Supplementary Table 2). We also performed frequentist meta-analysis in the same population. Statistical analytical methods and results can be found in the Supplementary Material (before Supplementary Fig. 2). Sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the impact of differences in outcomes on the results. We also evaluated the impact of pre-capillary PH on the results, and the impact of degree of mean pulmonary arterial pressure (PAP) on the results in HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF). Methodological quality assessment of included studies was performed using the Quality In Prognosis Studies tool [17]. Statistical analysis was conducted using R version 4.3.1 (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and R Studio (version 2023.12.0+369, Posit Software, PBC, Boston, Massachusetts, ).

Results

Of 3065 articles extracted from electronic databases, 928 were excluded due to duplication. Titles or abstracts of 2137 were reviewed and 2014 articles not relevant to this study were excluded, e.g., not prognostic studies, case reports, reviews, editorials, animal studies. We read the full text of the remaining 123 articles, and we excluded 15 of 123 articles because they did not fulfill the inclusion criteria or met the exclusion criteria for this study (reasons for exclusion are provided in Supplementary Table 3). Finally, 108 articles with 21,166 patients were included in this meta-analysis. A PRISMA flow diagram is described in Fig. 1. Of those 108 articles, two presented only multivariable HR analysis results, not univariable HR analysis results. Consequently, results of the univariable HR analysis included 106 articles. Results of the multivariable HR analysis included 43 articles and 67 separate analyses. Among the 106 articles, there were 16 articles reporting univariable HRs for TAPSE, seven articles for FAC, and six articles for RVfwLS. Multivariable analysis

Fig. 2B shows forest plots of multivariable analysis for each type of CVD. The pooled HR from a 5% decrease in RVEF provided strong evidence for associations with adverse outcomes in MI.ICM, DCM, PH, AS, and TR (BF10 ranged from 15.2 in DCM to 3662 in MI.ICM). rTOF, HCM, and HFrEF showed moderate evidence of association between RVEF and adverse outcomes (BF10 ranged from 4.1 in HFrEF to 9.0 in rTOF). There was no evidence that RVEF is associated with adverse outcomes in HFpEF (BF10: 1.0), and weak evidence that RVEF is not associated with adverse outcomes in cardiac sarcoidosis (BF10: 0.45).

As for heterogeneity, there was strong evidence of heterogeneity in rTOF (BF10: 35,105), moderate evidence in HFrEF (BF10: 4.8), and weak evidence in HCM and cardiac amyloidosis. Multivariable analysis

As for effect sizes, forest plots of multivariate analysis for each type of CVD are shown in Fig. 3B. HCM (BF10: 3.2), DCM (BF10: 6.7), AS (BF10: 9.4), and PH (BF10: 4.4) had moderate evidence for an association of RVEF with adverse outcomes, while rTOF, HFrEF, MI.ICM, and TR showed weak evidence (BF10 ranged from 1.1 in MI.ICM to 1.8 in rTOF). Frequentist meta-analysis

A standard frequentist meta-analysis was also performed on the same population, and the results are shown in Supplementary Fig. 2 and 3. In the meta-analysis of univariable analysis results (Supplementary Fig. 2), pooled HRs were significantly (p < 0.05) greater than one for all subgroups except HFrEF and TR. In HFrEF and TR, the 95% confidence intervals of HRs crossed one and were not significant, p = 0.050 and p = 0.057, respectively.

In a meta-analysis of multivariable analysis results, pooled HRs for HCM, MI.ICM, DCM, PH, AS, and TR were significantly (p < 0.05) greater than 1, while for rTOF, HFpEF, HFrEF, and cardiac sarcoidosis, pooled HRs were not significant (p > 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the impact of differences in outcomes on results, sensitivity analyses were performed excluding two articles for rTOF and one article for MI.ICM with arrhythmic events as the outcome. Analyses yielded similar results to the those including all outcomes in rTOF and in MI.ICM (Supplementary Table 6). PH was categorized as pre-capillary PH, post-capillary PH, and mixed type. Twenty-eight articles were classified as pre-capillary PH, four articles as mixed type, and no articles as post-capillary PH. When confined to studies of pre-capillary PH (n = 28), similar results were observed compared with those in all PH studies (Supplementary Table 7).

Finally, we performed meta-regression analysis using mean PAP as a covariate. Invasive hemodynamic evaluation was conducted in five of the nine articles in HFpEF. Bayesian model-averaged meta-regression revealed strong evidence that RVEF is associated with outcome (BF10 = 89) after adjusting mean PAP. Robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-regression provided BF10 of 5.1, reflecting moderate evidence for an association of RVEF with outcomes (Supplementary Table 8).

Discussion

The main findings of this study are as follows: (1) In univariable analysis using Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis, pooled HR per 5% reduction for RVEF showed strong evidence in favor of association with adverse outcomes in rTOF, HCM, HFpEF, MI.ICM, DCM, cardiac sarcoidosis, cardiac amyloidosis, and PH, and moderate evidence of association in HFrEF, AS, and TR; (2) In multivariable analysis using Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis, pooled HR per 5% reduction for RVEF indicated strong evidence in favor of an association with outcomes in MI.ICM, DCM, PH, AS, and TR, and moderate evidence in rTOF, HCM, and HFrEF; (3) There was no evidence of association with adverse outcomes in HFpEF; (4) In univariable analysis using Robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis, only DCM showed strong evidence of an association between a 5% reduction in RVEF and adverse outcomes, and HCM, PH, and AS showed moderate evidence; (5) In multivariable analysis using Robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis, moderate evidence was observed in HCM, DCM, PH, and AS, while cardiac sarcoidosis showed moderate evidence against the association with adverse outcomes.

Previous studies

Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have reported an association between CMR-derived RVEF and adverse outcomes [8], [9], [10], [11]. Dong et al. assessed prognostic values of RV parameters by CMR in eight studies involving 1120 patients with PH for all-cause death and in ten studies involving 604 patients for a composite end point [8]. Among RV parameters, RVEF was the only parameter predicting both all-cause death (HR: 0.95 per 1% increase, 95% CI: 0.92–0.99) and a composite end point (HR: 0.95 per 1% increase, 95% CI: 0.93–0.97). Alabed et al. also investigated prognostic values of CMR parameters in 22 studies with 1938 patients with PH [9]. A 1% reduction in RVEF was associated with a 2.1% increased risk of death and 4.9% clinical worsening. Papanastasiou et al. conducted a meta-analysis of the prognostic value of RVEF evaluated by CMR in patients with HF [10]. Forty-six studies that collectively enrolled 14,344 patients were reviewed, showing pooled HRs of 1.26 (95% CI: 1.18–1.33) for death and 1.31 (95% CI: 1.2–1.42) for death or HF hospitalization for a 10% reduction in RVEF. Those authors also showed that RVEF is significantly associated with mortality or hospitalization in HFrEF as well as HFpEF with the same values of HR (HR: 1.24). Boretto et al. performed a meta-analysis of prognostic prediction in cardiac amyloidosis by CMR [11]. Six studies including 671 patients with cardiac amyloidosis were identified for inclusion. Pooled HR for all-cause mortality according to a 5% reduction in RVEF was 1.13 (95% CI: 1.05–1.22). Thus, previous meta-analyses have demonstrated prognostic usefulness of RVEF for patients with PH, HF, and cardiac amyloidosis. However, prognostic relevance of RVEF in CVDs other than these etiologies has not been investigated. Furthermore, conventional meta-analyses have adopted a frequentist approach, in which p-values were used to make a binary decision regarding the presence or absence of an effect. In a frequentist meta-analysis, a non-significant p-value provides little information, and one cannot determine whether there is absence of evidence or evidence of absence. On the other hand, Bayesian meta-analysis indicates the direction of the evidence (which hypothesis made the better prediction) and to quantify the strength of that evidence using BF [19]. Another reason for preferring the Bayesian approach is that simulation studies show that it performs across a wide range of conditions, even when publication bias is present [20]. This is not true for the standard frequentist meta-analysis approaches that overestimate effect size when publication bias is present [21], [22], [23]. Since publication bias seems to be a very real threat [24], the robust Bayesian meta-analytic approach was chosen for this study.

Current study

The strength of this study is a very large sample size (108 articles with 21,166 patients). The overall population was divided into 11 CVD categories, and analysis was performed for each CVD. Publication bias-unadjusted Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis revealed moderate or strong evidence that RVEF is associated with adverse outcomes in all 11 CVDs (BF10 ranged from 4.3 in TR to 9.6 * 107 in PH). This is consistent with a previous meta-analysis investigating the association between RVEF and prognosis in patients with PH, HF, and cardiac amyloidosis [9], [10], [11], [12].

In addition, we performed a robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis, which provides BF after adjusting for publication bias. In the robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis, there was moderate or strong evidence in favor of associations with outcomes in HCM, DCM, PH, and AS (BF10 ranged from 3.0 in PH to 23.3 in DCM), which means that results after publication bias adjustment are quite different from those without adjustments. To evaluate the presence of publication bias, several previous studies employed funnel plot asymmetry analysis with Egger’s or Begg’s test, but this method is not sensitive [16]. Although PET-PEESE or a selection model can be used to estimate publication bias, these tests provide only a possibility of publication bias, and none provide estimates after adjusting publication bias. Robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis is the most reliable method for estimating whether RVEF is associated with adverse outcomes, considering publication bias adjustments. Evidence in favor of an RVEF association with outcomes in other CVDs was weak; thus, further accumulation of data will be required to strengthen the evidence.

Unlike univariable results, multivariable analysis with Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis showed no evidence that RVEF is associated with outcomes in HFpEF (BF10: 1.0). Robust Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis revealed moderate evidence of an association between RVEF and outcomes in HCM, DCM, PH, and AS, in agreement with results of the univariable analysis. However, results of multivariable analysis should be interpreted with caution because adjustment variables varied among studies and included multiple results from the same populations.

As for the comparison of prognostic ability with other RV parameters, HRs for these RV parameters were only described in 5–15% of all articles. Since these parameters have different units from RVEF, we rescaled HR per 1-SD decrease in each parameter and the ratio of HRs was calculated. Pooled HR ratios for TAPSE, FAC, and RVfwLS were 0.97, 1.04, and 1.06, respectively, and BF10 were 0.69, 0.99, and 1.1, respectively. BF10 were all near 1, suggesting weak evidence regardless of whether there is a difference in prognostic value between RVEF and other RV parameters. This implies an absence of evidence. Consequently, additional data must be accumulated to determine whether there is a difference in prognostic ability between RVEF and other RV parameters, especially in specific CVDs.

Frequentist meta-analysis of results of univariable analysis showed that pooled HRs were not statistically significant in HFrEF and TR, so it was not clear whether a decrease in RVEF significantly increased adverse outcomes. It cannot be determined whether this is evidence of absence or absence of evidence. In contrast, the univariable Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis showed BF10 of 5.0 and 4.3 for HFrEF and TR, respectively, meaning moderate evidence in favor of an RVEF association with adverse outcomes. In frequentist meta-analysis of results of multivariable analysis, a 95% confidence interval of pooled HRs crossed one for rTOF, HFpEF, HFrEF, and cardiac sarcoidosis (p > 0.05). It is impossible to conclude whether this is evidence of absence or absence of evidence. A Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis showed that BF10 of rTOF, HFpEF, HFrEF, and cardiac sarcoidosis were 9.0, 1.0, 4.1, and 0.45, respectively. This means that rTOF and HFrEF showed moderate evidence in favor of an RVEF association with adverse outcomes, HFpEF showed no evidence, and cardiac sarcoidosis showed weak evidence of no RVEF association with adverse outcomes.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as with all meta-analyses, this study was affected by variability of the original publications. Second, because this was a study-level meta-analysis, detailed diagnostic criteria, severity, and stage of disease may differ among articles. Although more detailed results could be obtained if a meta-analysis were conducted using individual participant data, it is unlikely that all authors would provide individual patient data, which could cause selection bias. This should be considered when interpreting these results. Third, outcomes and follow-up duration differed from study to study, which may have affected these results. Fourth, the type and number of adjustment variables in multivariable analysis varied among studies. This means that interpretation of multivariable analysis results should be treated with caution. Fifth, TAPSE, FAC, and RVfwLS are echocardiography-derived parameters, whereas RVEF is a CMR-derived parameter. Hence, in comparing these parameters, results must be interpreted in view of biases related to whether to perform the test and differences in imaging methods, since this is not a systematic approach and head-to-head comparison. Sixth, the HR of RVEF was analyzed as a continuous variable rather than a dichotomous variable using cutoff values, because in many articles the HR of RVEF was reported as a continuous variable. However, since RVEF does not necessarily decrease linearly, evaluation using a dichotomous variable with a cutoff value should also be considered. This is a limitation of study-level meta-analysis. Seventh, reproducibility of RVEF measurements could not be evaluated because very few reports described reliability data, such as intraclass correlation. Finally, RV-pulmonary artery coupling (RVEF/PAP) has not been evaluated. Because the RV is more sensitive to afterload than the LV [25], incorporating PAP, which is one measure of RV afterload, could be an accurate assessment of RV function. This should be kept in mind when interpreting this study’s findings, and is our challenge for the future.

Conclusions

Bayesian model-averaged meta-analysis revealed that RVEF measured by CMR had moderate or strong evidence for associations with adverse outcomes in all types of CVD. However, after adjustment for publication bias, it showed moderate or strong evidence of an association between RVEF and outcomes only for HCM, DCM, PH, and AS. Further Bayesian meta-analysis will be required when additional data have accumulated to re-evaluate the evidence for other CVDs.
