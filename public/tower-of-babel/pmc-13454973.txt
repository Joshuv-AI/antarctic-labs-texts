Early‐Onset Parkinson's Disease with 22q11.2 Microdeletion and Pathogenic GBA1 Variant
Authors: Nikolai Gil D Reyes, Daniel G Di Luca, Ivan Martinez‐Valbuena, Erik Boot, Maria Corral, Ryan KC Yuen, Connie Marras, Anthony E Lang, Anne S Bassett
Published: 2026

Genetic factors are known to underlie Early‐onset Parkinson's disease (EOPD), although data are mostly limited to monogenic cases. Multi‐genic (“oligogenic”) forms are rarely described and focus on coexisting single‐nucleotide (SNV) or exonic copy number variants (CNV) in PD‐associated genes typically detected by targeted multi‐gene panels. 1

GBA1 variants and the 22q11.2 microdeletion are known genetic risk factors for EOPD. Pathogenic GBA1 changes convey a >10‐fold increased PD risk (mean age at onset (AAO): 50.7, SD 11.8 years). Certain variants confer an early AAO, and nearly half of pathogenic GBA1 carriers present as EOPD. 2 The recurrent 22q11.2 microdeletion is the most common pathogenic CNV in humans (~1 in 2148 live births) and accounts for ~0.4–0.5% of EOPD cases, 3 , 4 conferring a ~20‐70‐fold increased PD risk (mean AAO: 39.5, SD 8.5 years). Approximately >70% of affected individuals have EOPD, often with other neuropsychiatric features. 5 We report a case of EOPD associated with both 22q11.2 microdeletion and severe pathogenic GBA1 variant, and discuss potential implications.

The patient is a 27‐year‐old, non‐Ashkenazi Jewish male diagnosed at age 14 with a de novo 22q11.2 microdeletion, later confirmed as a typical 2.5 Mb deletion. Lifetime history reflected neuropsychiatric and systemic features of 22q11.2 deletion syndrome (22q11.2DS; see Table S1). At age 27, six years after limited antipsychotic exposure, he developed new‐onset right‐sided rest tremor, and examination revealed parkinsonism (Video 1). Brain MRI and laboratory investigations were unremarkable. Dermal α‐synuclein seed amplification (SAA) assay was unrevealing (Table S1). Levodopa/carbidopa (100/25 mg tid) resulted in symptom improvement (MDS‐UPDRS III score: 26 ➔17; Video 1). However, subsequent development of motor and non‐motor fluctuation necessitated a switch to levodopa/carbidopa/entacapone therapy (Table S1).

In the first part of the video (recorded at age 27 years), examination of the patient before levodopa therapy shows mild hypomimia, tremulous movements of the tongue, moderate rest tremor on the right hand (with re‐emergent component; not shown), and mild to moderate bradykinesia predominantly on the right upper and lower extremities. Postural reflexes were intact (MDS UPDRS Part III: 27). In the second part of the video (recorded at age 28 years), patient is on low‐dose levodopa/carbidopa 100/25 mg thrice daily. Examination shows improvement of right‐sided rest tremor, modest improvement of tremulous movements of the tongue, but with persistence of some right‐sided bradykinesia on foot tapping and re‐emergence of mild tremor during gait examination (MDS UPDRS Part III: 16).

The early AAO and family history of paternal grandfather with EOPD (AAO: 45 y), prompted targeted multi‐gene panel testing that identified a pathogenic heterozygous GBA1 variant, c.1448T>C (p.Leu483Pro; L483P) (GRCh37, NM_001005741.2). Parental testing revealed inheritance of this variant from the patient's asymptomatic father (Fig. S1).

This case illustrates the potential impact of combined high‐risk genetic variants in PD. Evidence suggests that additional genetic burden, from common and rare variants within and outside PD‐associated loci, modifies disease expression. 6 One could postulate that in our case, the coexistence of two incompletely penetrant variants exerted additive or synergistic effects, each acting as a permissive factor for the other, resulting in an overall increased PD penetrance at an AAO substantially earlier than expected for either variant alone. Given that most GBA1‐associated PD cases demonstrate α‐synuclein pathology, 7 the negative dermal SAA in our patient may suggest a less dominant effect of the GBA1 variant on PD expression relative to the 22q11.2 microdeletion. However, this finding on a single case must be interpreted with caution, especially as data regarding SAA accuracy in genetic and non‐genetic EOPD remain overall limited. Although SAA patterns and exact mechanisms for 22q11.2DS‐associated PD are yet to be elucidated, candidate genes within the deletion region may be relevant to PD pathophysiology (ie, SEPT5, COMT, 6 mitochondrial genes), 3 and may interact with GBA1‐mediated lysosomal dysfunction. 2 These possibilities, however, remain speculative and will require further studies, including validation of different SAA methods and examination of cumulative genetic burden in EOPD cohorts.

Beyond mechanistic possibilities, this case underscores the importance of genome‐wide molecular genetic testing in EOPD (eg, multi‐gene/exome + chromosomal microarray analysis, or genome sequencing), especially as currently available targeted genetic testing may miss high‐impact variants (eg, 22q11.2 microdeletion) and thus underestimate the true prevalence of multi‐genic forms of PD.

Figure S1 Pedigree demonstrating family history of early‐onset Parkinson's disease (PD) and consanguinity. On clinical microarray, the proband (arrowhead) had a de novo typical 22q11.2 microdeletion spanning LCR22A‐LCR22D. There was also an approximately 10‐Mb region of loss of heterozygosity on the long arm of chromosome 20 consistent with consanguinity and/or genetic isolate/founder effect. Targeted testing for PD revealed a paternally inherited, heterozygous pathogenic GBA1 missense variant c.1448T>C (p.Leu483Pro; “L483P”).
