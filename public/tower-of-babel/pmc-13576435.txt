Hypertrophic olivary degeneration (HOD) is a rare form of transsynaptic degeneration resulting from disruption of the dentato-rubro-olivary pathway, also known as the Guillain-Mollaret triangle (GMT).1,2 HOD is characterized by delayed onset degeneration and hypertrophy of the inferior olivary nucleus and is typically associated with symptoms such as palatal myoclonus, tremor, and ataxia after injury to the GMT pathway (Fig. 1).3–7 HOD has been described most commonly following brainstem hemorrhage or surgical intervention in patients with brainstem cerebral cavernous malformations (CCMs), particularly when lesions involve the pons or critical components of the GMT.5–7 However, longitudinal descriptions of the natural clinical and radiographic evolution of HOD, especially progression from unilateral to bilateral involvement in the absence of surgical intervention or recurrent hemorrhage, remain limited. Illustrative Case

In 2021, a 66-year-old right-handed female without clinical symptoms underwent a research brain MRI study that demonstrated two right pontine CCMs clustered around a developmental venous anomaly (Fig. 2A). The MRI study also showed right-sided HOD. Later in 2021, she developed bilateral pulmonary emboli (PE) and was initiated on rivaroxaban. The PE were initially considered provoked by menopausal hormone therapy, and anticoagulation was subsequently discontinued after 3 months. In September 2022, she experienced recurrent PE and was restarted on anticoagulation, which was considered lifelong. Over the subsequent years, the patient noticed a slow, progressive decline in balance. She denied vertigo, oscillopsia, diplopia, nystagmus, or auditory clicking. She reported several falls but no acute neurological events. By 2025, neurological examination revealed palatal myoclonus and mild left-sided limb ataxia. Cranial nerve examination was otherwise unremarkable. There was no nystagmus, no cervical spine pain, and no signs of vestibular dysfunction. No alternative neurological findings were identified to account for the patient’s gait imbalance.

Serial brain MRI demonstrated a clear evolution over time. In 2021, imaging revealed right ventral pontine CCMs with associated ipsilateral HOD (Fig. 2A). By 2025, the central pontine lesion had enlarged and extended across midline to the left side of the pons (Fig. 2B). In addition, imaging demonstrated bilateral HOD on the T2 FLAIR image. The imaging evolution occurred naturally over time and was temporally associated with the patient’s onset of progressive neurological symptoms. Cervical and thoracic spine imaging was performed to rule out spinal stenosis as an alternative cause of gait dysfunction and was unrevealing.

The patient was counseled regarding the natural history of HOD. Given that resection of the CCM would not alter the symptoms or progression of HOD, conservative management was recommended. The patient was advised to continue physical therapy for fall prevention.

Discussion

Observations

The principal observation of this case is the stepwise, longitudinal evolution from unilateral to bilateral HOD occurring in parallel with progressive pontine CCM extension across the midline without symptomatic hemorrhage or surgical intervention and delayed symptoms until the HOD was bilateral. Serial imaging over 4 years demonstrated that bilateral involvement of the inferior olivary nuclei can develop gradually, providing patient-level imaging evidence that HOD may reflect progressive, tract-level disruption of the GMT rather than a single acute symptomatic hemorrhagic or iatrogenic event.

Prior large series demonstrate bilateral HOD at diagnosis, not sequentially after unilateral HOD is initially diagnosed.3 Cases of unilateral HOD in the setting of a hemorrhagic cavernous malformation followed sequentially by bilateral HOD after surgical intervention have been reported.7 Our patient developed sequential HOD due to asymptomatic CCM growth in the absence of surgery or symptomatic hemorrhage. Asymptomatic growth of CCM occurs in less than 3.8%–14.6% of CCM patients.8 This evolution suggests that HOD represents a marker of cumulative disruption of the GMT, rather than the consequence of a single acute hemorrhagic or iatrogenic event.

A second key observation is the delay between radiographic evidence of HOD and clinical symptom onset. Despite clear unilateral olivary changes on MRI in 2021, the patient remained asymptomatic for several years, with palatal myoclonus and gait imbalance emerging only later as bilateral HOD became apparent. This clinical-­radiological dissociation has been reported in prior series demonstrating that HOD is frequently asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic and its emergence can lag behind imaging abnormalities, particularly in unilateral disease.3,9 These observations suggest that radiographic HOD precedes clinical expression and that symptom development reflects a later stage of pathway degeneration. The present case supports this interpretation by demonstrating that initially asymptomatic HOD can represent an early phase of a progressive process rather than a benign or static finding.

Finally, the temporal overlap between lesion evolution and long-term anticoagulation is noteworthy but must be interpreted cautiously. Contemporary observational cohort data and meta-analyses in CCM populations have generally not shown an increased hemorrhage risk or growth associated with antithrombotic therapy.10 In the absence of imaging features such as T1 hyperintensity or clinical symptoms suggestive of hemorrhage, no mechanistic inference can be drawn, although surveillance imaging is potentially indicated in such patients.

Lessons

This illustrative case reinforces that HOD in patients with pontine cavernous malformations can arise and progress independently of symptomatic hemorrhage or surgical intervention, reflecting cumulative disruption of the GMT rather than an acute event alone. Longitudinal imaging may reveal evolution from unilateral to bilateral HOD over time, and clinical manifestations may emerge only after HOD becomes bilateral, emphasizing the need for long-term follow-up and anticipatory counseling. It is important for clinicians to recognize this condition when patients present with new neurological symptoms in the absence of subacute CCM hemorrhage.

Management decisions should therefore be guided by standard CCM surgical indications and individualized risk-benefit assessment, with emphasis on symptom management, fall prevention, and expectation setting once HOD has developed.
