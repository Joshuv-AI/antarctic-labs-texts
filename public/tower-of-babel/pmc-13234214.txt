Delayed traumatic hemipelvectomy after crush injury in a pediatric patient: a case report
Authors: Matthew S Linz, Theodore Wang, Julia Friberg, Arunachalam Thenappan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTraumatic hemipelvectomy is a rare operation, often performed for injuries caused by a high force mechanism such as a traffic or industrial accident, and often fatal. Most often performed emergently, here we report a traumatic hemipelvectomy performed on a pediatric patient one month from initial injury.Case presentationA 7-year-old healthy male was crushed between two motor vehicles while riding his bicycle. He was taken emergently to the nearest trauma center and underwent external fixation of his pelvis, exploratory laparotomy, abdominal packing, diverting colostomy, vesicostomy, and placement of an intra-arterial left iliac to femoral shunt. He was then transferred to our facility for further management. Following stabilization for severe hemorrhagic shock, his shunt was converted to an iliofemoral bypass. Despite good vascular in-flow and aggressive debridement of his left lower extremity, he continued to have ongoing muscle and pelvic necrosis and a persistent SIRS response. He subsequently underwent left hemipelvectomy approximately one month from time of initial injury. He then steadily recovered and was ultimately discharged to acute inpatient rehabilitation.ConclusionWe describe an unusual case of delayed pediatric traumatic hemipelvectomy. The patient not only survived the initial devastating injury but also received timely revascularization. The patient ultimately required hemipelvectomy despite a stepwise, multidisciplinary approach to limb salvage.Keywords: Case report, Hemipelvectomy, Pediatric trauma, Crush injury

Introduction

Traumatic hemipelvectomy is a rare operation, usually necessary in the setting of severe injury to the ligamentous or osseous pelvis with neurovascular rupture [1], [2]. Most patients who sustain such a severe traumatic injury do not survive transport to a hospital [3]. The first known survival following traumatic hemipelvectomy occurred in 1915 [4], and the first documented survival of a pediatric patient was described in 1985 [5]. While traumatic hemipelvectomy is now more likely to result in patient survival [2], [6], [7], [8], it remains a very rare operation, necessary in 0.6 to 2.4% of all traumatic pelvic injuries [2]. Here we present the rare case of a 7-year-old male with severe pelvic crush injury who underwent a series of orthopedic, colorectal, vascular, and plastic surgeries over 3 months, ultimately requiring delayed hemipelvectomy secondary to pelvic necrosis and sepsis. Case report

The patient is a healthy 7-year-old male who was pinned between 2 motor vehicles while crossing the road on his bicycle. He sustained a severe crush injury to the lower abdomen and pelvis. He was transported to the nearest trauma center by Emergency Medical Services (EMS) and was in severe class IV hemorrhagic shock. He was immediately taken to the operating room for hemorrhage control. His catalog of injuries in the operating room included an open book pelvic fracture (Fig. 1A), an intraperitoneal and extraperitoneal rectal injury, a 10 cm long disruption to the left external iliac artery down to the femoral artery (Fig. 1B), and a urethral neck injury. During his initial operation, his abdomen was packed with laparotomy pads for hemostasis, he underwent diverting sigmoidostomy, a suprapubic catheter was placed, an intra-arterial left external iliac to femoral shunt was created, a four-quadrant left lower extremity fasciotomy was performed, and an external fixator was placed to stabilize his pelvis. He was then transferred to our facility, a dedicated pediatric trauma center. On arrival he continued to be in severe class IV hemorrhagic shock and was therefore taken back to the operating room immediately. He returned to the operating room three more times within the first 48 h after injury until adequate hemorrhage control could be obtained. Upon achieving adequate hemorrhage control, he returned to the operating room for conversion of his intra-arterial shunt to an ileo-femoral bypass with PTFE. His left lower extremity fasciotomy sites were also revised to ensure adequate perfusion to the lower leg. Throughout the first 48 h after injury, his intra-arterial shunt remained patent, and he had dopplerable dorsalis pedis and posterior tibial signals. Shortly thereafter, he underwent completion proctectomy secondary to severe devascularization and devastation to the distal colon and rectum with closure of his abdomen. Orthopedic surgery then performed surgical pinning of his pelvis and removed the external fixator.

A) Coronal cut from initial CT pelvis demonstrating multiple acute comminuted pelvic fractures and B) axial cuts (middle and bottom image) showing iliac disruption.

Despite hemodynamic stability and good in-line blood flow to his left lower extremity, the patient continued to exhibit symptoms consistent with a SIRS response secondary to necrosis of muscle in his left lower extremity fasciotomy sites. He was taken to the operating room serially for debridement and washout (Fig. 2) without significant improvement in his inflammatory markers. A CT angiogram of the pelvis and left lower extremity was then performed demonstrating distal blood flow but poor perfusion of the pelvic bones and surrounding pelvic musculature. This was thought to be secondary to shearing and disruption of the small vessels around the hip and gluteal muscles. Additionally, a bone scan was done that demonstrated left hip necrosis. At the time of his initial operation, the left femoral vein was ligated for hemorrhage control. No dedicated cross-sectional venogram was performed during his hospitalization, so it was unclear if venous outflow obstruction also played a role in necrosis of the bone and muscle. Although repeated venous duplex scans of the left lower extremity failed to demonstrate any deep venous thrombosis, the studies were limited secondary to fasciotomy dressings. After multidisciplinary discussion, the decision was made to proceed with left hemipelvectomy and flap closure (Fig. 3A). He subsequently recovered well and was ultimately discharged to rehab after 3 months of total hospitalization. On long-term follow up over 1.5 years with multiple specialties, he has recovered well with excellent wound healing (Fig. 3B) and is now ambulatory with crutches.

The patient's fasciotomy wounds were extended superiorly on the calf and inferiorly on the thigh to release bulging muscle.

Discussion

Hemipelvectomy is a rare operation. Hemipelvectomy is traditionally recommended for malignant or aggressive bone and soft tissue tumors of the pelvis and surrounding muscles. Traumatic hemipelvectomy is an even rarer operation, usually only necessary secondary to trauma caused by a high force mechanism, resulting in significant disruption and instability of the pelvic ring including damage to the pubic symphysis and sacroiliac joint as well as the local nerves and vasculature [4]. Traffic and industrial accidents are the most common mechanism of injury [9], [10]. Lasurt et al. report motor vehicle collisions as the most common etiology, with other described mechanisms including shiploading accidents, mining accidents, farm machinery accidents, winch injuries, and propeller injuries from a boat [10]. As Patch et al. highlight the majority of these traumatic mechanisms lead to direct avulsion of the lower extremity and crush injuries of the pelvis [11]. Patients with these injuries often also sustain additional genitourinary (85%) and colorectal injuries (60%) [4]. Few patients survive their injuries, with only a few case reports described in the literature [9]. It is especially rare in the pediatric population.

Management requires aggressive resuscitation and prompt surgical control for hemorrhage. Hemostasis is of paramount importance to achieve survival. The majority of traumatic hemipelvectomies are done to achieve adequate control of hemorrhage. In the setting where adequate hemorrhage control is achieved early, however, hemipelvectomy may still be necessary in the setting of ongoing tissue necrosis and sepsis [9].

To our knowledge, the largest study describing traumatic hemipelvectomy management was published by He et al. and includes 21 cases [12]. Their management recommendations include rapid bleeding control, debridement of the injured pelvis, and cautious attempts at limb salvage when the injured limb had <6 h warm ischemia [12]. In another case series, Timmers et al. compare their management of two patients requiring traumatic hemipelvectomy ten years apart, and they similarly describe prioritizing survival with aggressive resuscitation, frequent takebacks to the operating room, and involvement of plastic surgery for reconstruction as critical components of management [13]. Patch et al., however, argue that early amputation suppresses a severe inflammatory response worsened by surgery and transfusion of blood products, which increases the risk for multi-organ dysfunction and reperfusion injury. They also highlight that revascularization is a common cause of compartment syndrome and rhabdomyolysis with renal failure [11]. Furthermore, in two published case reports, despite adequate early revascularization but one patient developed skin infection and necrosis requiring delayed hemipelvectomy and amputation, while another patient still eventually required a below-the-knee amputation [2], [14]. Similarly, Haim et al. describe delayed hemipelvectomy on hospital day 12, which was closed with a gluteal flap prior to creation of a split thickness skin graft two weeks later [2] Osti, Li, Klasen, and Cho all describe serial debridement of necrotic wounds with eventual need for hemipelvectomy [6], [7], [14], [15].

Our case is unique in that it involves a pediatric patient. He sustained a severe crush injury, with disruption of the left external iliac artery, but prompt revascularization with a temporary intra-arterial shunt followed by a left ilio-femoral bypass was achieved. The patient had good in-line flow to his left lower extremity as demonstrated by strong doppler signals in the foot and a patent bypass on CTA. Despite this good in-line flow, his traumatic injury still ultimately resulted in necrosis of his pelvic bones and muscles. Decisions were made by a multidisciplinary team including specialists from pediatric trauma surgery, orthopedic surgery, plastic surgery, and pediatric critical care. Operative resections were performed in a stepwise fashion, with a goal of limb-preservation if possible, all while trying to ensure clearance of infection. Established management guidelines are lacking given the scarcity of patients surviving this type of injury. Overall, our case is distinct in that hemipelvectomy was delayed for weeks after the inciting trauma but still resulted in survival and successful discharge from the hospital and return to home life.

Conclusion

Traumatic hemipelvectomy is necessary in the setting of rare traumatic injuries associated with extremely high mortality. In this patient's case, he survived because of aggressive resuscitation, achievement of hemostasis, early revascularization, aggressive wound care with serial debridement, and prioritization of survival over limb salvage. His case is unique in that hemipelvectomy was delayed but still ultimately necessary secondary to acute trauma despite good inline flow, and that resection decisions were made in a stepwise fashion by a multidisciplinary team rather than immediately and ad hoc on arrival to the hospital.
