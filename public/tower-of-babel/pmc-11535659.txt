Impact of a hub-and-spoke approach to hospital antimicrobial stewardship programmes on antibiotic use in Zambia
Authors: Aubrey Chichonyi Kalungia, Martin Kampamba, David Banda, Andrew Munkuli Bambala, Sarah Marshall, Melanie Newport, Anja St Clair-Jones, Luke Alutuli, Elias Chambula, Lucky Munsaka, Audrey Hamachila, Chiluba Mwila, Duncan Chanda, Joseph Chizimu, Roma Chilengi, Michael Okorie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial stewardship programmes (ASPs) aim to optimize antibiotic use and prevent antimicrobial resistance.ObjectiveThis study assessed the impact of ASPs, initiated using a hub-and-spoke approach, on antibiotic use in Zambian public hospitals.MethodsA pre–post study was conducted in 10 ASP-naive hospitals across Zambia using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)’s hospital-based ASP core elements (i.e. leadership, accountability, pharmacy expertise, stewardship actions, tracking progress, reporting and education) checklist and the global point prevalence survey methodology. The intervention involved technical staff from a national ‘hub’ hospital with an established ASP providing on-site orientation and mentorship to multidisciplinary teams of healthcare workers at 10 ‘spoke’ hospitals to build capacity in antimicrobial stewardship. ASP core elements and inpatient antibiotic use prevalence (AUP) were assessed before and 12 months after ASP implementation. Data were statistically analysed.ResultsThe adoption of ASP core elements improved significantly (P = 0.001, 95% CI: −17.8 to −5.42). AUP decreased from 50.1% (±5.8, n = 1477) to 44.3% (±4.6, n = 1400) after 12 months, though the reduction was not statistically significant (P = 0.442; 95% CI: −9.8 to 21.6), with ‘Watch’ list antibiotics remaining the most commonly prescribed across the hospitals.ConclusionsThe hub-and-spoke approach successfully catalysed ASPs in public hospitals in Zambia, demonstrating the potential for improving antibiotic use practices over time, provided structural challenges are addressed. This approach and insights can guide stakeholders in Zambia and similar settings in enhancing hospital ASPs.

Introduction

One of the five objectives of the global action plan (GAP) on antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is to optimize antibiotic use.1 In the past decade, there have been considerable concerns about high AMR rates to commonly prescribed antibiotics, with multidrug-resistant organisms identified in countries like Zambia2–6—a lower middle-income country (LMIC) in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA)7 causing morbidity, mortality and increased healthcare costs.8 There have also been growing concerns regarding current levels of knowledge and attitudes among healthcare workers (HCWs) regarding AMR and antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) in Zambia.9,10 Alongside this, high rates of inappropriate prescribing of antibiotics, including those from the World Health Organization (WHO) ‘Watch’ list, have been reported in Zambia’s hospitals.11–14 High rates of antibiotic use in hospitals are largely driven by high empiric prescribing,11,13 poor access to antibiograms and poor adherence to national guidelines.15 Encouragingly, Zambia demonstrates considerable capacity for addressing AMR.16 This builds on the launch of a multisectoral National Action Plan (NAP) for AMR in 2017.17 The five key objectives of Zambia’s NAP are aligned with the GAP.1,17 Specifically, Objective 4 of the Zambia NAP seeks to optimize antimicrobial use through AMS as a key strategy.17 This is important as hospital-based antimicrobial stewardship programmes (ASPs) have been shown to improve antibiotic use and reduce costs and AMR.18–21 There have been, however, considerable concerns that in many LMICs in SSA including Zambia, resource constraints, including limited diagnostic infrastructure, and personnel shortages with the majority not trained in AMS have hindered the introduction of ASPs in recent years.22–24 Though several initiatives to improve antibiotic use were ongoing in Zambia building on the NAP and the WHO toolkits,25 including the introduction of an antimicrobial surveillance network in 2020,26 the use of a ‘hub-and-spoke’ approach to develop hospital ASPs has not been tried.

In India, a hub-and-spoke model was successful in implementing ASPs in secondary care hospitals with positive gains towards judicious use of antibiotics.27 Similarly, using the hub-and-spoke approach, we selected Zambia’s leading tertiary hospital (herein designated the ‘hub’) with an established ASP to strengthen capacity in other hospitals (herein designated as ‘spoke’ hospitals) across the country. The authors were aware that a multidisciplinary AMS committee comprising infectious disease physicians, pharmacists, nurses and microbiologists was established at the national ‘hub’ hospital to lead AMS activities.28 The lower-level hospitals were, therefore, key targets for ASPs since these are particularly susceptible to harbouring multidrug-resistant organisms and, therefore, pose a major threat to promulgating AMR.29 This is especially the case post-COVID-19 where in LMICs, there was appreciable empirical prescribing of antibiotics in hospitals despite limited evidence of bacterial co-infections,30–33 which might consequently drive up AMR rates if not addressed.34–37 The objective of this study was to evaluate the impact and influence of hospital-based ASPs instigated using a hub-and-spoke approach on antibiotic use in public hospitals across Zambia. We believe the findings of this study are important not only to key stakeholder groups in Zambia but also beyond as countries in SSA strive to operationalize and scale up ASPs to reduce AMR, which is widely seen as the next pandemic.38,39

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This was a before-and-after (pre–post) study covering all of Zambia involving 10 public ‘spoke’ hospitals providing tertiary- and secondary-level healthcare services across nine provinces (i.e. Central, Copperbelt, Northern, Muchinga, Luapula, Southern, Western, Northwestern and Eastern provinces). A specialized tertiary-level public teaching hospital with an established ASP with leadership and tools40 was designated as the ‘hub’ situated in Lusaka Province—the capital of Zambia. With a total population of 19.3 million inhabitants, Zambia has over 2500 health facilities out of which 19 are public second-level (secondary, provincial or district) hospitals and 8 are public third-level (tertiary, central or specialist) hospitals, respectively.41 The targeted secondary- and tertiary-level hospitals purposively selected were those that did not have an established ASP at the time of the study and were earmarked by the Zambian Ministry of Health for capacity building.

Study approach

A three-phased implementation science approach was utilized to facilitate ASP capacity building of the ‘spoke’ hospitals as follows: baseline assessment phase, intervention phase and post-intervention monitoring phase, respectively.

Baseline assessment

Pharmacists from the national ‘hub’ hospital conducted a site visit to each ‘spoke’ hospital to conduct a baseline assessment of the hospital ASP core elements (i.e. leadership, accountability, pharmacy expertise, stewardship actions, tracking progress, reporting and education) and antibiotic use prevalence (AUP). To measure AUP, a point prevalence survey (PPS) was conducted enumerating all medical records of patients admitted to the following hospital wards of interest: adult medical wards (AMWs), adult surgical wards (ASWs), adult intensive care units (AICUs) and paediatric medical wards (PMWs), respectively. The baseline findings informed the starting points for ASP intervention using the hub-and-spoke approach.

Intervention

A multidisciplinary team of health professionals from the national ‘hub’ hospital facilitated on-site mentorship and orientation on AMS concepts to fellow HCWs including prescribers, pharmacists, nurses, laboratory scientists, public health officers and hospital administrators at the ‘spoke’ hospitals using a standardized module developed by the Brighton-Lusaka Health Link (Supplement 1, available as Supplementary data at JAC-AMR Online).42 This was delivered using on-the-job training techniques for up to 5 days. The AMS module content included topics on general principles of AMS, roles of health workers in AMS, rational use of antibiotics, infection prevention and control techniques, diagnostic stewardship and measuring antibiotic use in the hospital. Thereafter, the ‘hub’ mentors helped the multidisciplinary HCWs at each ‘spoke’ hospital to constitute their local AMS team to implement the ASP. The ‘spoke’ AMS teams developed their contextual AMS action plans using Plan-Do-Study-Act cycles for implementation and follow-up. The local ASPs and action plans were then monitored by the designated ‘hub’ mentors. The ASP intervention was implemented in April 2022 with a follow-up period of 12 months.

Post-intervention

The ASP developed at each ‘spoke’ hospital was monitored by tracking the core elements and action plans of the respective AMS teams. Activities were evaluated remotely through online reporting tools administered at the mid-point (6 months). Teams of ‘hub’ mentors then conducted on-site monitoring visits at the endpoint (Month 12) to assess ASP core elements and supervised each ‘spoke’ hospital to conduct their second PPS to measure AUP using the same criteria, methods and tools as previously described.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To select the participating hospitals, the WHO criteria43 for hospital inpatient bed capacity were utilized. Tertiary- and secondary-level hospitals providing both outpatient and inpatient healthcare services in each province of Zambia meeting the WHO criteria and without an active hospital-based ASP were selected to participate. The PPS only included medical records of inpatients admitted to the hospital wards of interest as per the standard PPS methodology previously described.44

Data collection and analysis

Data on ASP core elements were collected using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) checklist45,46 that was interviewer administered to the hospital administrator or head of clinical care at each ‘spoke’ hospital, complemented with a desk review of hospital documents. AUP data were collected using the PPS methodology and tools.44,47,48 The PPS was conducted in April 2022 and repeated in April 2023. At each ‘spoke’ hospital, the multidisciplinary AMS teams that were trained by the ‘hub’ mentors were supervised to collect PPS data. Teams extracted the required PPS data from paper-based medical records according to the Global-PPS methodology as previously described.49 All eligible medical records were surveyed on the same day at each hospital with the survey date recorded for analysis. Antibiotics used were recorded by their International Non-proprietary Names, Anatomical Therapeutic Classification and the WHO AWaRe classification.

Statistical analysis

Statistical computations and visualization of data were done using GraphPad Prism version 5 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA). Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard error. AUP was measured as a proportion with the numerator being the total number of inpatient records with at least one active antibiotic prescribed and the denominator being the total number of inpatients surveyed. An unpaired t-test was used to detect the mean difference between the baseline AUP and post-ASP intervention AUP. In addition, a two-way ANOVA with the Bonferroni multiple comparisons test was used to compare the AUP across different hospitals. To assess the ASP core elements, each ‘spoke’ hospital was scored in the 7 ASP core element categories comprising 37 items described in the CDC checklist tool46 as whether fully implemented (full score), partially implemented (>1 score) or not implemented (0 score). The scores were summed for each of the core element categories and compared at baseline and 12 months after the intervention. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was accepted as a statistically significant difference at 95% confidence limits.

Ethical considerations

This project was part of an ongoing national programme by the Zambian Ministry of Health (MoH) to improve rational medicine use. Official approval and permission were granted by the MoH headquarters in Lusaka, Zambia (reference no. MH/101/22/17), and the project was monitored by the National AMR Coordinating Committee. All data were collected after informed consent from the respective hospital administrators and officers in charge. All data collected were confidentially kept and secured throughout. No identifiers of respective hospitals, HCWs or patients’ particulars were used at any time during this study.

AUP in the hospital wards

Figure 4 shows the overall tracked comparison of AUP before and 12 months after the ASP intervention in AMWs, PMWs, ASWs and AICUs, respectively, across the 10 ‘spoke’ hospitals.

Comparison of AUP pre- and post-ASP intervention across different wards of interest: (a) AMWs, (b) ASWs, (c) AICU and (d) PMW in the respective ‘spoke’ hospitals (n = 10). Across all wards of interest, there was no statistically significant change in AUP before and after the intervention.

In AMWs, the overall AUP was 48.9% ± 5.9 before the ASP and decreased to 44.1% ± 5.4 after the intervention (Figure 4a). However, this reduction was not statistically significant (P = 0.554; 95% CI: −11.91 to 21.51). Only half of the hospitals [(5/10) ID# 3, 5, 7, 9 and 10] demonstrated reductions in AUP in their AMWs. Similarly, AUP decreased from 51.0% ± 6.3 to 45.3% ± 5.95 after the ASP in ASWs (Figure 4b), though this change was not statistically significant (P = 0.522; 95% CI: −12.5 to 23.8). Six of the 10 hospitals (ID# 3, 4, 5, 7, 9 and 10) showed modest reductions in their ASWs following the intervention.

Figure 4(c) presents the average AUP in the AICU across the 10 hospitals, which dropped from 65% ± 15.0 to 46.7% ± 15.9 before and 12 months after the intervention. Again, this change lacked statistical significance (P = 0.412; 95% CI: −27.6 to 64.2).

In PMWs, AUP slightly increased from 54.1% ± 6.3 to 55.7% ± 7.1 post-intervention (Figure 4d), though the difference remained statistically insignificant (P = 0.866; 95% CI: −21.6 to 18.3).

Across the 10 hospitals, the overall mean AUP for ‘Access’ antibiotics was 50.1% ± 4.9 before and 50.9% ± 2.9 at 12 months after the ASP intervention (Table 4). Less than half (4/10) of the hospitals (ID# 1, 3, 8 and 9) recorded a marginal increase in the use of WHO ‘Access’ antibiotics at the endpoint. Regarding the WHO ‘Reserve’ antibiotic use, none utilized them before but at the endpoint, 2/10 hospitals were found prescribing ‘Reserve’ list antibiotics, with hospital ID #2 and #7 recording 2% and 3% AUP for ‘Reserve’ antibiotics, respectively.

Discussion

This study evaluated the impact and influence of hospital ASPs instigated using a hub-and-spoke approach on antibiotic use across 10 ASP-naive public hospitals in Zambia. The sample included eight secondary-level and two tertiary-level public hospitals selected across nine provinces of Zambia. We believe this is the first nationwide study in Zambia to evaluate the influence of instigating hospital ASPs on antibiotic use over a medium-term (12 months) follow-up period. Our findings demonstrate what progress hospitals in a resource-limited setting can make to operationalize ASPs when local capacity is built using evidence-based methods. Hospital-based ASPs often require a multidisciplinary team of HCWs to lead, implement actions and instil sustained change in multiple behaviours at multiple timepoints along the continuum of healthcare and keep monitoring the impact of such actions.50 The necessity for continuous monitoring of hospital ASPs is vital given, as mentioned, the need to mitigate AMR in line with the country’s NAP goals.

Our findings showed a statistically significant improvement in the adoption and implementation of the hospital ASP core elements after the ‘hub’ intervention across the 10 ‘spoke’ hospitals (Figure 1). This signified that the hub-and-spoke approach effectively created momentum to implement ASPs. At 12 months post-intervention, the majority of the ASP core elements were being adopted with accountability and education core elements fully adopted across all 10 hospitals, respectively (Table 2). Accountability as a core element is a key to the success of any ASP. It requires appointing leadership that will be accountable for the ASP process and outcomes. Importantly, the top hospital management’s support for ASPs and protected time for ASP activities are also critical for success.51 In addition, the AMS team cascading the education and training about AMR and AMS to fellow HCWs is an effective local strategy for sustaining the ASP.

While our hub-and-spoke approach had a modest impact on reducing AUP, it significantly strengthened fundamental aspects of ASP across hospitals as adjudged from the significant improvement in the adoption and implementation of key ASP core elements across hospitals (Figure 1). Our findings were similar to Zacchaeus et al.27 in India who used a hub-and-spoke model to implement ASPs in secondary-level hospitals and showed a significant decrease in usage of key antibiotics 8 months after their ASP intervention. In our study, the modest reduction in AUP may suggest that either longer or more intensive interventions are necessary to achieve more significant reductions. Encouragingly, the significant improvement in the adoption of ASP core elements across spoke hospitals demonstrated the hub-and-spoke intervention’s effectiveness in establishing a solid foundation for ongoing and future stewardship efforts. Our findings further consolidate evidence that in low-resource settings in Africa, a hub-and-spoke model is a potentially effective approach to strengthening ASP core elements. We further demonstrate that a hub-and-spoke approach as utilized in our study shifts paradigms from traditional high-cost AMS implementation approaches using ‘unadaptable’ external interventions often from high-income countries to a more contextually relevant and locally driven model. This is important in Africa because the traditional AMS models face implementation challenges due to high costs and health system constraints. Cohn et al.52 advocated a hub-and-spoke model to support appropriate antibiotic use as this enables optimal access and maximal utilization of scarce resources. Our findings corroborate this, further demonstrating that the localized mentorship, training and utilization of multidisciplinary HCWs to implement AMS appear to be critical factors in improving ASPs. Moreover, continued efforts should focus on continuing professional education, monitoring and adjustment of ASP strategies, particularly targeting the observed high usage of ‘Watch’ antibiotics through up-to-date prescribing guidelines informed by local antibiogram data. Going forward, we hope to see more adaptation and utilization of the hub-and-spoke model of implementing ASPs, especially in Africa.

The results indicate mixed outcomes in the use of WHO AWaRE list antibiotics across the hospitals (Table 4). Despite efforts, a larger proportion of inpatients were prescribed WHO ‘Watch’ antibiotics, in particular ceftriaxone (J01DD04) was the most commonly prescribed. This finding agrees with previous point prevalence studies that reported high empirical prescribing of ‘Watch’ antibiotics in Zambian hospitals11–14 and many other African countries.53–57 This is contrary to South Africa58,59 and Tanzania,60 where ‘Access’ antibiotics were the most prescribed in hospitals according to PPS reports. In our study, the overall mean AUP for ‘Access’ antibiotics remained largely unchanged over 12 months, suggesting that the short-term ASP interventions commenced across the hospitals did not significantly alter the antibiotic prescribing patterns. The use of ‘Watch’ antibiotics remained a critical issue, with half of the hospitals increasing their usage. This was concerning given that the WHO recommends keeping ‘Watch’ antibiotic use below 30%,61 a target not met by any of the hospitals in our study. Arguably, the persistence of high ‘Watch’ antibiotic use and the inability to meet WHO recommended thresholds highlight significant structural challenges in changing antibiotic prescribing practices within a short period, pointing to deeper issues in stewardship effectiveness or adherence, including antibiotic selection pressure and supply chain dynamics. We were convinced that this finding underscores the need for the hub-and-spoke ASP approach to be supported with more robust, targeted, harmonized and sustained AMS actions. To achieve more effective management of antibiotic use in local hospitals, the Zambian Ministry of Health should enhance the focus on reducing ‘Watch’ antibiotic use through more intensive training of health workers, stricter monitoring of adherence to treatment guidelines and targeted prescribing interventions, while also implementing stricter controls to prevent the premature use of ‘Reserve’ antibiotics where they are neither warranted nor supported by bacteriological evidence.

Our findings have practical implications for ASP leaders, hospital administrators and policy-makers in Zambia and beyond. First, they underscore the need to mainstream the implementation of ASPs mandating the adoption and adherence to AMS principles to enhance the prudent and optimal use of antibiotics in hospitals across Zambia. This can translate to reducing AMR as part of the NAP goals. Consequently, active support and monitoring of hospital ASPs by the national Ministry of Health through its sub-national structures tasked to implement the NAP are crucial in this respect. This includes the monitoring and reporting of ASP outcomes as quality indicators across hospitals.

We are aware of some limitations of this study. First, apart from AUP and ASP core elements, we did not report other exposures such as patient demographics, infection type or antibiotic availability and other outcomes of ASPs such as prescribing quality, impact on length of hospital stay, costs, duration of therapy and antibiotic consumption using daily defined dose limits, including qualitative metrics to supplement the quantitative findings. Second, our follow-up period was 12 months with PPS measurements at two points (at baseline and endpoint, respectively). The assumption was that the inpatients surveyed at baseline and the endpoint shared similar characteristics since the PPS was conducted in the same hospitals and wards of interest. Despite these limitations, we believe our findings were robust providing direction to all key stakeholders in Zambia and beyond.

Conclusion

The hub-and-spoke approach successfully instigated ASPs in 10 public hospitals in Zambia, leading to significant improvements in the adoption of ASP core elements and modest reductions in AUP. Multidisciplinary AMS teams were effectively established and trained in AMS across the hospitals. With functional ASPs can gradually shift antibiotic use practices with time, addressing broader structural challenges remains critical. To optimize antibiotic use in LMICs like Zambia, it is essential to intensify, sustain and support local AMS teams, with a focus on reducing the overuse of high-priority ‘Watch’ antibiotics in hospitals.
