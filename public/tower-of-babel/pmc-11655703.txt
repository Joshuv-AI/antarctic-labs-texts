Enantio‐Recognition and Charge Transfer Complex Formation Involving Tetrathiafulvalene‐Appended Chiral 1,2‐Cyclohexane‐Diamine: An Integrated Experimental and Theoretical Study
Authors: Camilla Ferrari, Alexandra Bogdan, Flavia Pop, Cosimo Curto, Alberta Carella, Francesco Rossella, Narcis Avarvari, Claudio Fontanesi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn this work, we exploit the electronic features of tetrathiafulvalene (TTF) as a backbone in synthesizing chiral derivatives. The aim is to make use of TTF's well‐known and unique redox and semiconducting properties in the fields of enantio‐selective recognition and chiral charge transfer (CT) complex preparation, with the ultimate objective of obtaining devices with various potential applications, ranging from plasmonics to quantum computing. In particular, both cyclohexane‐bis (TTF‐amide)–based enantiomers 1‐(S,S) and 1‐(R,R), stable under an oxidation regime, have been selected, and under these conditions, the electrochemical enantiospecific response of the four possible systems, coming from the combination with L‐ and D‐tartaric acid, respectively, was tested. The 1:tartaric acid adducts show lower oxidation potentials than the pristine 1, together with clear enantio‐discrimination demonstrated by sizeable potential differences in the range of 29–46 mV between the diastereomeric adducts. Because the oxidation potential of 1 suggests the possibility of the formation of CT complexes, impedance and FT‐IR spectra were recorded to confirm this hypothesis in the case of the CT complex 1:I2. The experimental results obtained through the FT‐IR analysis were also compared with the theoretical results deriving from the DFT‐based calculations.Keywords: charge transfer complexes, chirality, DFT, enantio‐discrimination, tetrathiafulvalene

Introduction

To date, it is already well known that tetrathiafulvalene (TTF) and its derivatives represent one of the most coveted candidates for the development of molecular conductors and superconductors, thanks to the unique behavior of their CT complexes ranging from conducting and semiconducting properties to superconducting and paramagnetic features [1]. Because of the strong interest the scientific community has continuously shown for TTF and its derivatives, starting with the charge transfer (CT) complex TTF‐TCNQ [2] and the first superconducting radical cation salts of the Bechgaard family [3], both its structure [4] and its electrical properties [5] were explored over the years. The introduction of chirality in TTF derivatives is comparatively more recent, having started more as a synthetic challenge, possibly leading to chiral molecular metals [6], which subsequently triggered an intensive endeavor toward the preparation of several families of chiral TTFs [7, 8]. Indeed, TTF derivatives appear as appealing platforms to develop chiral derivatives, in search of combining their redox‐active features with handedness [9]. From a more general perspective, chirality is deeply rooted in determining possible applications in a multitude of phenomena spanning optics, plasmonics, condensed matter physics, and the field of quantum computing as possible logic spin‐valves [10, 11, 12, 13]. In particular, TTF‐based chiral conductors have allowed the observation of the electrical magnetochiral anisotropy (eMChA) effect [14] and of the chirality‐induced spin selectivity (CISS) effect [15]. Among the various families of chiral TTFs, those containing amide groups were envisaged for their propensity to afford original solid‐state structures [16, 17, 18] and supramolecular aggregates thanks to the establishment of hydrogen bonds [19, 20, 21]. In particular, the bis (tetrathiafulvalene)‐1,2‐cyclohexane‐diamine derivative 1 is an original chiral TTF‐based dimer recently reported by Pop and Avarvari [22], which holds the promise of a stable and semiconducting behavior under an oxidation regime (the molecular structure is shown in Scheme 1). In this paper, we focus on the electronic properties of 1 with particular attention to its oxidized electronic states, which can be set by both electrochemical oxidation and via chemical doping using iodine, in view of its possible use in chiral electrochemical recognition and in organic electronic devices.

Materials and Methods

Reagents

The synthesis of the 1‐(S,S) and 1‐(R,R) enantiomers has been described in detail previously [22]. Ethanol ≥ 99.5%, sodium nitrate NaNO3 ≥ 99.0%, KCl ≥ 99.0%, L‐ (D‐) tartaric acid (TA), tetrabutylammonium tetrafluoborate (TBATFB), and acetonitrile (ACN) are from Merck/Sigma‐Aldrich and were used without further purification.

Electrochemical Setup

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were performed by using a PGSTAT 128N (Autolab/Metrohm) and CHI660A (CH‐Instruments) potentiostats. A typical three‐electrode electrochemical cell was employed where a Pt wire and a silver/silver chloride (Ag/AgCl/KClsat) electrodes served as the counter (ce) and reference (RE) electrodes, respectively. A 0.1 M TBATFB solution in ACN solution was used as base electrolyte. A disk of glassy carbon (GC) embedded in a Teflon cylinder (Metrohm, 3.0‐mm diameter) was used as a working electrode (WE). All the potentials reported in this work were referred to the Ag/AgCl/KClsat reference electrode.

FT‐IR Spectra

Grazing angle, GA‐FT‐IR, spectra were recorded using a Bruker VERTEX‐70 FTIR spectrophotometer equipped with a Globar IR source and a mercury–cadmium–telluride (MCT) midband detector, in the 4000–600 cm−1 wavenumber range. Thirty‐two scans averaging allowed a reasonable signal‐to‐noise ratio to be obtained. In screening measurements, the angle of the GA accessory was varied from 80° to 45°, and the spectra shown in this paper are recorded at a 45° angle. Pristine enantiopure 1‐(S,S) and 1‐(R,R) thick film spectra were measured from a thick film obtained by dropcasting a 5‐mM solution of enantiopure 1 in DCM on a Pt sheet used both as the mirror for background recording and as the substrate for the dropcasting. Then, three drops of a 5‐mM iodine solution in DCM were dropcasted on the enantiopure 1 thick film, allowing the CT complex 1:I 2 to be obtained (the reaction time took less than 10 min), and the final GA‐FT‐IR spectrum of the 1:I 2 complex was eventually recorded.

Calculation Details

GAMESS and Firefly Rev 8.2026 (Firefly is partially based on the GAMESS (US)8 source code) suite of programs were used [23] for the theoretical results here reported, by using C1 symmetry and unrestricted wave function. Molecular geometries are obtained by full optimization carried out at the UB3LYP/6‐31G(d) level of the theory. To account for solute−solvent interaction in theoretical calculations we used Barone and Cossi's polarizable conductor model (CPCM), which is based on Tomasi's polarized continuum model (PCM). Plane wave calculations have been performed by using Quantum Espresso [24]. Calculations were run within the general gradient approximation (GGA) of the density functional theory with the application of periodic boundary conditions (pbc). We used PBE functional, Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (GGA) and Rappe–Rabe–Kaxiras–Joannopoulos (Ultrasoft) pseudopotentials, and D3 dispersion energy corrections by Grimme et al. [25, 26], with a 50 Ry cut‐off energy. In solid‐state quantum mechanical calculations, ions' coordinates were not relaxed but rather obtained from the experimental crystal structure [22]. Final band‐structure data have been calculated with a 70‐point K space sampling. The Multiwfn program has been used for visualization and partial elaboration of theoretical results [27].

Results and Discussion

Enantio‐Specific Interactions

Figure 1 shows CV curves of 5‐mM enantiopure solutions of 1‐(S,S) (Figure 1a) and 1‐(R,R) (Figure 1b), in the presence of enantiopure tartaric acid (L‐ or D‐TA) (0.5 mM), using 0.1 M TBATFB in ACN as base electrolyte. The purpose is to probe the effect of enantio‐specific intermolecular interaction, as mediated via the four possible symmetric versus dissymmetric handedness combinations: where symmetric are meant the 1‐(S,S):L‐TA and 1‐(R,R):D‐TA combinations and dissymmetric are meant the 1‐(S,S):D‐TA and 1‐(R,R):L‐TA combinations. The present approach is based on results already reported in the literature concerning bulk‐mediated enantio‐recognition, where CVs show chiral‐discrimination ability in systems where chiral ionic liquids serve as solvents for chiral redox couples [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. It must be noted that for the 1‐(S,S) enantiopure solution, the combination 1‐(S,S):L‐TA (blue line in Figure 1a) presents the oxidation and reduction peaks at higher potential with respect to the 1‐(S,S):D‐TA (red line in Figure 1a) system. The opposite is found to occur for the 1‐(R,R)–based enantiopure solution: 1‐(R,R):L‐TA (blue line in Figure 1b) presents the oxidation and reduction peaks at lower potential with respect to the 1‐(R,R):D‐TA (red line in Figure 1b) system. In the first case, the separation between the peaks of EOX1 of these two systems is 29 mV, whereas for EOX2, it is 32 mV; in the latter, the separation between the peaks of EOX1 of these two systems is 44 mV, whereas for EOX2, it is 46 mV. Thus, the comparison of the CVs shown in Figure 1 allows us to clearly discriminate symmetric versus dissymmetric intermolecular enantio‐interactions. Moreover, major differences occur with respect to both oxidation potentials of the enantiopure 1‐(S,S) and 1‐(R,R) donors, shown in Figure 2. Thus, these results suggest a significant interaction occurring between 1 and TA, inducing an easier oxidation of the adducts compared to 1. Table 1 summarizes the potentials corresponding to the first and second redox processes of pristine 1 (that is, without the presence of the tartaric acid in solution). A related feature is the distinctive pattern of the CVs of compound 1, with the systematic presence of two shoulders (marked S1 and S2 in Figure 2) in the first oxidation and reduction processes (electrochemical reaction (1)), in the forward and backward curves respectively. This result is quite peculiar and can be explained by the occurrence of a dimer radical cation species, intramolecular or intermolecular, following the first oxidation, through π‐π orbital overlap interactions, mainly involving the TTF moieties. This would allow for the formation of a stable dimer with a total charge +1 and a doublet electronic state, with the positive charge being equally shared between the two TTF units. Further molecular insight is provided in the theoretical section vide infra. Eventually, a molecular‐mechanistic interpretation of such a strong enantio‐recognition discrimination observed for enantio‐recognition stemming from bulk solution interactions, as inferred by tight comparison of Figure 1a,b CVs, is yet to come, and it is currently under investigation. An elementary consideration is that, because both the chiral redox couples (1‐(S,S) and 1‐(R,R)) and the “auxiliary‐partner” tartaric acid are in bulk solution, the enantio‐specific interactions able to affect the electrochemical behavior seem to stem from interactions between the two species, that is, formation of an adduct in solution.

Figure 2 sets out the CVs of 1 ((R,R)‐ or (S,S)‐ enantiomers) in solution, where two oxidation (EOX1 and EOX2) and two reduction (ER1 and ER2) peaks of reversible nature are evident [33, 34]. For a potential larger than 1.0 V, a third broad and shallow peak, with also the hint of a backward peak, is present, suggesting a hardly reversible third redox process. The latter, occurring at a relatively high anodic potential, can be attributed to the oxidation of the bis (amide) framework [35]. In particular, the electrochemical process underlying the presence of EOX1 and EOX2 is two successive two‐electron oxidation steps, corresponding to the generation of the dication (1)2+ and then tetracation (1)4+.

CT Complex

As can be inferred from its electrochemical behavior, 1 has the tendency to be readily oxidized, and this feature can be exploited to produce CT complexes, which allow for an easy synthesis of promising organic‐based (semi)conductors. CT complexes have been prepared by simple subsequent dropcasting of iodine (I2 5‐mM solution in DCM) on top of a thick film (obtained by simple dropcast of a 5‐mM solution of 1 in DCM). The effective 1:I2 CT complex formation has been characterized by both impedance measurements and IR spectroscopy. Figure 3 shows the relevant 2D impedance spectroscopy results, in the form of a Bode plot: Figure 3a reports the logZvslogf data, where Z is the impedance modulus, whereas Figure 3b reports the ϕvslogf data. Spectra have been recorded in the 1‐Hz to 100‐kHz frequency range with a sinusoidal perturbing signal of 25‐mV peak‐to‐peak amplitude. The device used in 2D impedance measurements is described in detail in Figure S1 and is a simplification of our electrochemical transistor recently proposed in the literature [36, 37]. The impedance spectrum of pure 1 (red line) is quite scattered, and the magnitude, Z, for f < 200 Hz is larger than 0.11 GΩ, making the measurements basically meaningless (at least in our experimental set‐up). The phase of the impedance shows very large oscillations in the −60° to 100°, for frequencies larger than 1000 Hz. Thus, on the whole, the neutral pristine 1 is behaving like an insulator, an almost pure dielectric. On the contrary, the addition of iodine to the 1 thick film leads to a completely different outcome. The relevant logZvslogf pattern is shown in Figure 3a, blue line. Z features an almost constant value of about 300 kΩ, with a phase quite close to 0, that is, an almost purely resistive behavior. The striking difference between the impedance spectra of the pristine 1 and of 1:I2 after “chemical doping” with iodine strongly suggests the effective formation of a CT complex, with significant semiconducting ability from 1 by chemical doping.

Theoretical Results

Because in the previous study [22] it was shown that in the optimized conformation of neutral 1 the two TTF units are not arranged face‐to‐face to facilitate the occurrence of an intramolecular radical cation dimer, DFT‐based calculations have been exploited here to investigate in primis the ionized states of 1 and of possible strong intermolecular interactions, possibly causing the formation of stable dimers in bulk solution. Figure 5 shows different views of a +1 molecular cluster formed by two 1‐(S,S) monomers (of course the same results hold for the 1‐(R,R) enantiomer). Geometry optimization of a cationic doublet dimer, carried out at the B3LYP/6‐31G(d) level of the theory, allowed to find a stable dimer cation geometry configuration; the relevant molecular structure is depicted in Figure 5 (the effective stability, a minimum in the potential energy multidimensional surface, is confirmed by the calculation of the vibrational spectrum with the absence of any imaginary frequency, i.e., the calculated vibrational frequencies are all real). Remarkably, the charge is shared between the two molecules, most notably between the two TTF units that face each other. Figure 5c shows the HOMO of the dimer and how it extends over the two adjacent TTF units with almost the same weight; moreover, it has a (weak) bonding nature, rather than antibonding (the facing MOs on the two 1 molecules have the same phase). The simple formation reaction of [1:1] +• is shown in Equation (1). Table 2 sets out the relevant energy of the dimer formation. The energy of formation is negative, that is, a spontaneous process; thus, from a quantitative point of view, the cation dimer adduct formation has a small, but significant, energy variation: −13kcalmol−1 in the vacuum and a slightly larger value of −17 kcalmol−1 calculated accounting for both solvent effects and van der Waals dispersion interaction (ACN parameters and cpcm theoretical solvent model and wB97X‐D functional).

Then, the IR spectra have been theoretically calculated as well for the sake of comparison with the experimental outcome, aiming to investigate the doping of 1 by reaction with iodine, with the formation of a CT complex.

Figure 7 shows plane wave results obtained by DFT calculations starting from the X‐ray structure of 1‐(rac), the only form that provided suitable single crystals that could be analyzed by single crystal X‐ray diffraction analysis. The direct band gap in the racemic crystal 1 [22] is about 0.07 eV at Γ, suggesting a promising behavior of 1 as a semiconductor for possible applications in the field of organic electronics; moreover, the band gap, of any, of the chemically doped species is expected to be definitely smaller (as the impedance spectra of the 1:I2 CT complex is virtually purely resistive (please compare Figure 3, Section 3.2). The valence band (the highest electronic occupied states of the crystals) is rather dispersive (ranging from 0.00 to −0.15 eV) along directions such as Γ‐X, Γ‐V or Γ‐U, corresponding to the shortest and most directional intermolecular S···S distances in the crystal [22]. The energy value of the iodine lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) is relatively lower [36], theoretically supporting the CT from 1 to iodine, as suggested by the impedance spectroscopy results.

Conclusions

The electrochemical‐based measurements on the chiral bis (TTF‐amide) 1 allow for an effective characterization of enantio‐recognition also involving bulk interactions, as already pointed out in some seminal papers by Benincori and Mussini [28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. The CV pattern of enantiopure solutions of 1 is significantly altered by the presence of enantiopure tartaric acid. Considering that spin‐driven interactions could be involved in the process of intermolecular chiral recognition, as suggested in recent publications [32, 41, 42, 43], these results can represent supporting experimental evidence to be theoretically rationalized.

Moreover, because of the effective conductive ability in the oxidized state and its chiral potency (as witnessed by the rather large optical rotation ability [22]), 1 holds promise to act as an effective spin‐filter compound with possible applications also in the field of water splitting [44, 45, 46].

Electrochemical measurements, CVs in particular, confirm as an effective experimental tool to monitor the chiral recognition process, that is, symmetric versus dissymmetric enantiomeric intermolecular interactions. At the present stage, the nature of the mechanism that leads to the effective discrimination in the electrochemical results is still unclear. Some concerns are still open and are currently under investigation in our labs, in order to address questions like: Is it a surface‐related formation of an adduct, as a purely bulk phenomenon, able to alter the redox properties in such a dramatic way?

Remarkably, the quite marked and evident enantio‐recognition ability of the selected TTF‐based enantiomers found in bulk solution suggest their possible applications in addressing chiral recognition processes in biological systems and exploit chiral induction in electrosynthesis and electroanalytical essays.
