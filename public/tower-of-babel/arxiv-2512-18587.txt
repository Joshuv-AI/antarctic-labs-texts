Graphon–Level Bayesian Predictive Synthesis for Random Networks
Marios Papamichalis, Regina Ruane

Abstract
Bayesian predictive synthesis provides a coherent Bayesian framework for combining multiple predictive distributions, or agents, into a single updated prediction, extending Bayesian model averaging to allow general pooling of full predictive densities. This paper develops a static, graphon level version of Bayesian predictive synthesis for random networks. At the graphon level we show that Bayesian predictive synthesis corresponds to the integrated squared error projection of the true graphon onto the linear span of the agent graphons. We derive nonasymptotic oracle inequalities and prove that least-squares graphon–BPS, based on a finite number of edge observations, achieves the minimax L 2 L^{2} rate over this agent span. Moreover, we show that any estimator that selects a single agent graphon is uniformly inconsistent on a nontrivial subset of the convex hull of the agents, whereas graphon-level Bayesian predictive synthesis remains minimax-rate optimal-formalizing a combination beats components phenomenon. Structural properties of the underlying random graphs are controlled through explicit Lipschitz bounds that transfer graphon error into error for edge density, degree distributions, subgraph densities, clustering coefficients, and giant component phase transitions. Finally, we develop a heavy tail theory for Bayesian predictive synthesis, showing how mixtures and entropic tilts preserve regularly varying degree distributions and how exponential random graph model agents remain within their family under log linear tilting with Kullback–Leibler optimal moment calibration.

Introduction

Bayesian ensemble methods have long been used to improve predictive performance by combining information from multiple models. In foundational work,
8
;
15
formalized Bayesian procedures for the reconciliation and aggregation of differing probabilistic forecasts, sometimes termed agent opinion analysis. These early methods provided coherent rules for updating and weighting expert provided distributions. More recently, Bayesian predictive synthesis has emerged as a general framework that extends Bayesian model averaging through the allowance of flexible pooling of predictive distributions in entirety rather than only model probabilities or point estimates. Dynamic versions treat each predictive model as an expert density with combination weights that are dependent on covariates or evolve over time. Decision-guided extensions incorporate task specific utility functions into the synthesis so that model combinations are tuned for a given loss criterion.

Despite this progress, applications of Bayesian predictive synthesis to network data remain largely unexplored. Real world graphs typically display several structural mechanisms at once, such as homogeneous connectivity, community structure, latent geometry, triangle closure, and heavy tailed degree behavior, which are only partially captured by standard parametric models. This suggests combining heterogeneous network models rather than choosing a single one. For example, an Erdős–Rényi model captures global edge density but not communities, a stochastic block model captures block structure but not hubs, and a latent space or random dot product graph captures geometry. A graphon level Bayesian predictive synthesis perspective treats each such model as an agent, which provides a predictive law over adjacency matrices and seeks a single synthesized graphon that optimally aggregates their structural information.

Bayesian predictive synthesis (BPS) builds on a long line of Bayesian model combination work that treats each model’s predictive distribution as an expert opinion, from early reconciliation and aggregation procedures for probabilistic forecasts

Recent examples include a growing stochastic block model that superposes block structure with preferential attachment to generate networks with both clear modular communities and heavy-tailed degree distributions

These hybrid models demonstrate that combining structural assumptions can improve generative realism but do so by hard-wiring specific mixtures of mechanisms rather than by a general-purpose Bayesian synthesis. A complementary line of work studies Bayesian model selection and averaging across network model classes: using encompassing priors and marginal likelihoods,

Ensemble methods have also proved powerful in predictive tasks on networks:

This paper develops a static, graphon level theory of Bayesian predictive synthesis (BPS) for random networks. We formalise random graph agents as probability laws on adjacency matrices, show that entropic tilting preserves exponential random graph model (ERGM) families, and obtain closed–form tilts for Erdős–Rényi, stochastic block, and logistic random dot product agents. At the graphon level we prove that BPS coincides with the integrated squared error projection of the true graphon onto the linear span and convex hull of the agent graphons, and we establish a sharp minimax theory for least–squares graphon–BPS estimators based on edge samples, including nonasymptotic oracle inequalities and a “combination beats components” result ruling out consistent single–agent selection on a nontrivial subset of the hull. We derive explicit Lipschitz bounds transferring graphon error into error for edge density, degree functions, subgraph densities, clustering, and sparse giant-component phase transitions, and we show that mixtures and entropic tilts preserve heavy-tailed degree behaviour, with the BPS mixture inheriting the most heavy–tailed exponent among the agents. Together, these results yield a coherent graphon level and decision theoretic framework for aggregating network models with exact optimality guarantees.

2
Problem setup: random graph agents and mixture predictive synthesis

We work with simple undirected graphs on a fixed vertex set
[n]
.

2.1
Graph space and agent forecasts

Definition 2.1

(Random graph space)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathcal{A}_{n}
denote the set of
all simple undirected graphs on vertex set
[n]=\{1,\dots,n\}
,
represented by symmetric adjacency matrices

A=(A_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}
with
A_{ii}=0
and
A_{ij}\in\{0,1\}
.

Definition 2.2

(Random graph agents)
.

A
random graph agent

M_{j}
is a probability mass function
(pmf)
p_{j}(A)
on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
,
j=1,\dots,J
, optionally
including a baseline agent
M_{0}
with pmf
p_{0}(A)
.

2.2
Mixture Bayesian predictive synthesis (mixture–BPS)

Definition 2.3

(Mixture predictive synthesis)
.

Given nonnegative weight functions
\alpha_{j}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\to[0,\infty)

and prior model weights
\pi_{j}\geq 0
with
\sum_{j=0}^{J}\pi_{j}=1
, the

mixture Bayesian predictive synthesis
(mixture–BPS) predictive
distribution for
A
is

f(A)~\propto~\sum_{j=0}^{J}\pi_{j}\,\alpha_{j}(A)\,p_{j}(A),\qquad A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}.

If we define

a_{j}=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\alpha_{j}(A)p_{j}(A),\qquad f_{j}(A)=\frac{\alpha_{j}(A)p_{j}(A)}{a_{j}},\qquad\tilde{\pi}_{j}=\frac{\pi_{j}a_{j}}{\sum_{\ell=0}^{J}\pi_{\ell}a_{\ell}},

then
f_{j}
are proper pmfs and

f(A)=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\tilde{\pi}_{j}f_{j}(A),

i.e. mixture–BPS is a finite mixture of agent–specific
synthesised

graph distributions
f_{j}
.

It is useful to view mixture–BPS as: (i)
synthesise
each agent
distribution
p_{j}
into
f_{j}
using the weight function
\alpha_{j}(A)
,
then (ii)
mix
the synthesised distributions using the updated weights

\tilde{\pi}_{j}
.

Section
3
focuses on a principled and convenient choice of synthesis weights
(entropic tilting) and its exponential-family interpretation; Section
4

derives closed-form tilts for common network models; Sections
5
–
6

connect mixture–BPS to graphon limits and to an
L^{2}
projection viewpoint.

3
Entropic tilting for ERGM agents

3.1
ERGM agents

Definition 3.1

(ERGM agents)
.

Let
T:\mathcal{A}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a vector of graph statistics
(e.g. edge count, triangle count, block edge counts).
An agent
M_{j}
is an
exponential random graph model
(ERGM) with
respect to
T
if

p_{j}(A)~=~\exp\Big\{\theta_{j}^{\top}T(A)-\psi_{j}(\theta_{j})\Big\}\,\mu(A),\qquad A\in\mathcal{A}_{n},

for some parameter
\theta_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, normalising function

\psi_{j}(\cdot)
, and base measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{A}_{n}

(e.g. counting measure or a combinatorial weighting).

3.2
Entropic tilting weights and KL calibration

Definition 3.2

(Entropic tilting weights on graphs)
.

Let
T:\mathcal{A}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be as above.
For each agent
j
and tilt vector
\tau_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
define

\alpha_{j}(A)=\exp\{\tau_{j}^{\top}T(A)\},\qquad A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}.

We refer to this as an
entropic
or
exponential tilting

of
M_{j}
.

The logarithmic score
S(f,A)=-\log f(A)
is a strictly proper scoring
rule: for any two pmfs
f,g
on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
,

\mathbb{E}_{g}[S(f,A)]-\mathbb{E}_{g}[S(g,A)]=KL(g\|f)\geq 0,

with equality if and only if
f=g
.
This makes entropic tilting especially natural: it yields distributions
that satisfy desired moment constraints while remaining as close as possible
to an agent’s baseline forecast under the log score.

Theorem 3.3

(ERGM closure and KL–optimal moment calibration)
.

Suppose agent
M_{j}
is an ERGM with respect to
T
, i.e.

p_{j}(A)=\exp\{\theta_{j}^{\top}T(A)-\psi_{j}(\theta_{j})\}\,\mu(A),\qquad A\in\mathcal{A}_{n},

for some base measure
\mu
on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
and log-partition function

\psi_{j}(\theta)=\log\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\exp\{\theta^{\top}T(A)\}\,\mu(A).

Let
\alpha_{j}(A)=\exp\{\tau_{j}^{\top}T(A)\}
with
\tau_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
and define the synthesised agent distribution

f_{j}(A)\propto\alpha_{j}(A)p_{j}(A)
as in mixture–BPS.
Then:

(i)

f_{j}
is again an ERGM with the same sufficient statistic
T

and updated natural parameter
\theta_{j}+\tau_{j}
, i.e.

f_{j}(A)=\exp\big\{(\theta_{j}+\tau_{j})^{\top}T(A)-\psi_{j}(\theta_{j}+\tau_{j})\big\}\,\mu(A).

(ii)

Fix a target moment vector
m_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
in the
mean–parameter space of
T(A)
and consider the class

\mathcal{F}_{j}(m_{j})~=~\big\{f:\;f\text{ pmf on }\mathcal{A}_{n},\;\mathbb{E}_{f}[T(A)]=m_{j}\big\}.

Among all
f\in\mathcal{F}_{j}(m_{j})
, the tilted ERGM
f_{j}
with
\tau_{j}

chosen so that
\mathbb{E}_{f_{j}}[T(A)]=m_{j}
is the unique minimiser of
the Kullback–Leibler divergence
KL(f\|p_{j})
.

Part (i) says entropic tilting keeps you
inside
the ERGM family: it simply shifts the natural parameter by
\tau_{j}
, so synthesis is algebraically clean and easy to interpret.
Part (ii) gives the main justification: if you want your synthesised distribution to match specific network moments (edges, triangles, block counts, etc.), the tilted ERGM is the
unique
way to do that while changing the original agent forecast as little as possible under KL (equivalently, while minimising log-score regret).

4
Closed–form tilts for canonical network agents

We now specialise to a few canonical agents: Erdős–Rényi,
stochastic block models, and logistic random dot product graphs.

4.1
Erdős–Rényi (ER) agent

Definition 4.1

(Erdős–Rényi agent)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
let
M_{n}:=\binom{n}{2}
be the number of potential
edges.
For
p_{j}\in(0,1)
the Erdős–Rényi model
G(n,p_{j})
has pmf

p_{j}(A)=p_{j}^{E(A)}(1-p_{j})^{M_{n}-E(A)},\qquad E(A):=\sum_{1\leq i<k\leq n}A_{ik}.

This is an ERGM with statistic
T(A)=E(A)
, base measure proportional
to counting measure, and natural parameter

\theta_{j}=\log\{p_{j}/(1-p_{j})\}
.

Proposition 4.2

(Entropic tilting of Erdős–Rényi is Erdős–Rényi)
.

Let
M_{j}
be
G(n,p_{j})
and choose
\alpha_{j}(A)=\exp\{\lambda_{j}E(A)\}
with

\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then the synthesised agent distribution

f_{j}(A)\propto\alpha_{j}(A)p_{j}(A)
is
G(n,p_{j}^{\prime})
with

p_{j}^{\prime}~=~\frac{e^{\lambda_{j}}p_{j}}{e^{\lambda_{j}}p_{j}+(1-p_{j})}~=~\operatorname{logit}^{-1}\big(\operatorname{logit}(p_{j})+\lambda_{j}\big).

\lambda_{j}
acts as a
global shift in edge log-odds
:
\lambda_{j}>0
increases overall density,
\lambda_{j}<0
decreases it, while preserving i.i.d. edges.

Corollary 4.3

(Effect on degrees and sparsity)
.

Under the setting of Proposition
4.2
, the degree
D_{i}

of each vertex satisfies
D_{i}\sim\operatorname{Bin}(n-1,p_{j}^{\prime})
, so

\mathbb{E}_{f_{j}}[D_{i}]=(n-1)p_{j}^{\prime}
and

\operatorname{Var}_{f_{j}}(D_{i})=(n-1)p_{j}^{\prime}(1-p_{j}^{\prime})
.
Entropic tilting by
E(A)
corresponds to a global logit shift of the
edge density, preserving the Binomial degree structure.

4.2
Stochastic block model (SBM) agent

Definition 4.4

(Stochastic block model agent)
.

Fix a community assignment
c:[n]\to\{1,\dots,K\}
.
For a symmetric matrix
B_{j}=(B_{ab}^{(j)})_{1\leq a,b\leq K}
with
entries in
(0,1)
, the stochastic block model (SBM) agent
M_{j}
has pmf

p_{j}(A)=\prod_{1\leq i<k\leq n}\Big(B^{(j)}_{c(i)c(k)}\Big)^{A_{ik}}\Big(1-B^{(j)}_{c(i)c(k)}\Big)^{1-A_{ik}}.

Equivalently, writing

M_{ab}(A):=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<k\leq n}\mathbf{1}\{c(i)=a,\ c(k)=a\}\,A_{ik},&a=b,\\[10.0pt]
\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i<k\leq n}\mathbf{1}\{(c(i)=a,\ c(k)=b)\ \text{or}\ (c(i)=b,\ c(k)=a)\}\,A_{ik},&a<b.\end{cases}

the number of edges between communities
a
and
b
, this is an ERGM with
statistic
T(A)=(M_{ab}(A))_{1\leq a\leq b\leq K}
.

Proposition 4.5

(Entropic tilting of SBM is SBM)
.

Let
M_{j}
be an SBM agent as above and define

\alpha_{j}(A)=\exp\Big\{\sum_{1\leq a\leq b\leq K}\lambda_{j,ab}M_{ab}(A)\Big\},\qquad\lambda_{j,ab}\in\mathbb{R},\qquad\lambda_{j,ab}=\lambda_{j,ba}\ \text{for }a\neq b.

Then the synthesised agent
f_{j}(A)\propto\alpha_{j}(A)p_{j}(A)
is again an SBM
with the same community assignment
c(\cdot)
and updated block
probabilities

B_{ab}^{(j)\prime}=\operatorname{logit}^{-1}\big(\operatorname{logit}(B_{ab}^{(j)})+\lambda_{j,ab}\big),\qquad 1\leq a\leq b\leq K.

Each
\lambda_{j,ab}
is a
blockwise log-odds shift
, so tilting can independently calibrate within- and between-community densities while preserving conditional independence given
c(\cdot)
.

4.3
Logistic random dot product graph (RDPG) agent

Definition 4.6

(Logistic random dot product graph agent)
.

Let
z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be latent positions.
For an agent
M_{j}
define edge probabilities

p_{ik}^{(j)}=\sigma\big(z_{i}^{\top}z_{k}\big),\qquad\sigma(x):=\frac{1}{1+e^{-x}},

and let
p_{j}(A)
be the pmf

p_{j}(A)=\prod_{1\leq i<k\leq n}\big(p_{ik}^{(j)}\big)^{A_{ik}}\big(1-p_{ik}^{(j)}\big)^{1-A_{ik}}.

We call this a logistic random dot product graph (RDPG) agent.

Proposition 4.7

(Global edge–density tilt of logistic RDPG)
.

Let
M_{j}
be a logistic RDPG agent and define

\alpha_{j}(A)=\exp\{\lambda_{j}E(A)\}
with
\lambda_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
and

E(A)=\sum_{i<k}A_{ik}
.
Then the synthesised agent
f_{j}(A)\propto\alpha_{j}(A)p_{j}(A)
is a logistic RDPG with the same latent positions

z_{1},\dots,z_{n}
but with updated edge probabilities

p_{ik}^{(j)\prime}=\sigma\big(z_{i}^{\top}z_{k}+\lambda_{j}\big).

The tilt adds a
global intercept
to all latent dot products, changing overall density without changing the latent geometry encoded by
(z_{i})
.

Remark 4.8
.

Propositions
4.2
,
4.5
and

4.7
instantiate Theorem
3.3
for
three canonical model classes.
Under global (or blockwise) entropic tilting, ER, SBM and logistic
RDPG agents remain in their respective families, with simple logit
shifts in edge probabilities.

5
Graphon limits of BPS mixtures

We now move to asymptotics in
n
, using graphon theory for dense
exchangeable networks.

Assumption 5.1

(Exchangeable agent sequences and graphons)
.

For each agent
j
, let
(G_{n}^{(j)})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of random
graphs with
G_{n}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{A}_{n}
.
Assume:

(a)

For each
j
, the sequence
(G_{n}^{(j)})_{n\geq 1}
is jointly
exchangeable in the sense of the Aldous–Hoover representation.

(b)

There exists a graphon
w_{j}:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
such that

G_{n}^{(j)}
converges in cut distance to
w_{j}
as
n\to\infty
.

Let
f_{j}^{(n)}
denote the pmf of
G_{n}^{(j)}
, playing the role of
p_{j}

at size
n
.

Definition 5.2

(Mixture–BPS synthesis across agents at size
n
)
.

For each
n
, let
f_{j}^{(n),\star}
denote the agent–specific pmf after
entropic tilting, as in Theorem
3.3
, and let

\tilde{\pi}_{j}^{(n)}\in(0,1)
with
\sum_{j}\tilde{\pi}_{j}^{(n)}=1
.
Define the BPS mixture at size
n
by

\Pr(G_{n}=A)=f^{(n)}(A)=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\tilde{\pi}_{j}^{(n)}f_{j}^{(n),\star}(A),\quad A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}.

Theorem 5.3

(Exchangeability and limiting graphon law of BPS mixtures)
.

Assume:

(i)

For each
j\in\{0,\dots,J\}
, the tilted sequence

(G_{n}^{(j),\star})_{n\geq 1}
with pmfs
f_{j}^{(n),\star}
is jointly
exchangeable and converges in cut distance to a graphon
w_{j}^{\star}
.

(ii)

The mixture weights satisfy
\tilde{\pi}_{j}^{(n)}\to\tilde{\pi}_{j}\in(0,1)
as
n\to\infty
for each
j
, with
\sum_{j=0}^{J}\tilde{\pi}_{j}=1
.

For each
n
, define the BPS mixture law

\Pr(G_{n}=A)~=~f^{(n)}(A)~=~\sum_{j=0}^{J}\tilde{\pi}_{j}^{(n)}\,f_{j}^{(n),\star}(A),\qquad A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}.

Then:

(a)

For every
n
, the random graph
G_{n}
is exchangeable, i.e.
its distribution is invariant under permutations of the vertex set
[n]
.

(b)

There exists a random graphon
W^{\star}
taking values in

\{w_{0}^{\star},\dots,w_{J}^{\star}\}
such that

\Pr(W^{\star}=w_{j}^{\star})=\tilde{\pi}_{j},\qquad j=0,\dots,J,

and
G_{n}
converges in distribution to
W^{\star}
with respect to the
cut metric.
Equivalently, for every bounded continuous functional

\Phi
on the space of graphons equipped with the cut metric,

\mathbb{E}\big[\Phi(G_{n})\big]~\longrightarrow~\sum_{j=0}^{J}\tilde{\pi}_{j}\,\Phi(w_{j}^{\star}).

In this sense, the limiting graphon law of the BPS mixture is the
convex combination
\sum_{j=0}^{J}\tilde{\pi}_{j}\delta_{w_{j}^{\star}}
of agent
graphon limits.

At any fixed
n
, mixture–BPS is “sample an agent index, then sample a graph from that agent”—so exchangeability is preserved by mixing.
Asymptotically, each (tilted) agent has a graphon limit
w_{j}^{\star}
, and the BPS mixture converges to a
random
limiting graphon that equals
w_{j}^{\star}
with probability
\tilde{\pi}_{j}
.
This gives a clean large-
n
interpretation: BPS behaves like a mixture over finitely many network regimes, each represented by an agent graphon.

Remark 5.4
.

A short practical summary is: (i) for each
n
, the finite-graph BPS mixture is exchangeable; and
(ii) the limiting graphon law is the discrete mixture
\sum_{j=0}^{J}\tilde{\pi}_{j}\,\delta_{w_{j}^{\star}}
.

Remark 5.5
.

Theorem
5.3
implies, for example, that if the
agents include ER, SBM or logistic RDPG models (tilted as in
Propositions
4.2
–
4.7
), then the BPS
mixture induces a limiting
graphon law
that is a convex
combination of the corresponding piecewise constant or smooth graphon
limits.
In particular, the BPS mixture lives in the convex hull of agent graphons
in the space of probability measures on graphons.
When one wishes to work with a single deterministic graphon summary of
the BPS mixture, a natural choice is the mean graphon

\bar{w}(x,y)=\sum_{j}\tilde{\pi}_{j}w_{j}^{\star}(x,y)
.

6
Graphon–level BPS as an
L^{2}
projection

We now move entirely to the graphon level, viewing candidate agent
graphons as basis elements in
L^{2}([0,1]^{2})
.

Definition 6.1

(Data–generating and agent graphons)
.

Let
(U_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be i.i.d.
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
, and let

w_{\star}:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
be the (unknown) data–generating
graphon.
Conditional on
(U_{i})
, edges are independent with

\Pr(Y_{ij}=1\mid U_{i},U_{j})=w_{\star}(U_{i},U_{j}),\qquad i<j.

Agents provide candidate graphons
w_{1},\dots,w_{J}:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
.
We consider linear synthesis graphons of the form

w_{\beta}(u,v)=\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{j}w_{j}(u,v),\qquad(u,v)\in[0,1]^{2},

with
\beta=(\beta_{0},\dots,\beta_{J})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J+1}
.
We write

\mathcal{H}:=\mathrm{span}\{1,w_{1},\dots,w_{J}\}\subset L^{2}([0,1]^{2})

for the finite–dimensional subspace generated by the agents and the
constant function.

Theorem 6.2

(Graphon BPS as
L^{2}
projection)
.

Let
F(u,v)=(1,w_{1}(u,v),\dots,w_{J}(u,v))^{\top}
and assume that

1,w_{1},\dots,w_{J}
are linearly independent in

L^{2}([0,1]^{2})
.
Define the
(J+1)\times(J+1)
Gram matrix

G=\mathbb{E}\bigl\{F(U_{1},U_{2})F(U_{1},U_{2})^{\top}\bigr\},

and vector

c=\mathbb{E}\bigl\{w_{\star}(U_{1},U_{2})\,F(U_{1},U_{2})\bigr\}.

Then
G
is positive definite and the
L^{2}
risk

R(\beta)=\mathbb{E}\bigl\{(w_{\star}(U_{1},U_{2})-w_{\beta}(U_{1},U_{2}))^{2}\bigr\}

is uniquely minimised at

\beta^{\star}=G^{-1}c.

Moreover
w_{\mathrm{BPS}}:=w_{\beta^{\star}}
is the
L^{2}
projection of

w_{\star}
onto the finite-dimensional subspace
\mathcal{H}
.

This theorem shows that, at the population level, the best linear synthesis of agent graphons is exactly a least-squares projection in the Hilbert space
L^{2}([0,1]^{2})
.
The matrix
G
plays the role of a design Gram matrix, and
c
is the vector of inner products between the true graphon and each basis function.
This is important because it reduces “optimal BPS weights” to a clean linear-algebra target
\beta^{\star}=G^{-1}c
, which directly guides estimation and interpretation.

Remark 6.3
.

Theorem
6.2
identifies the population BPS graphon

w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
as the
L^{2}
–optimal linear combination of agent
graphons and the constant function.
If we interpret
w_{j}(u,v)
as agent
j
’s predictive mean for edge

(u,v)
, then the coefficients
\beta^{\star}
are the best linear
synthesis weights under squared error loss on edge probabilities.

Corollary 6.4

(Finite–graph interpretation)
.

For
n\geq 2
and latent positions
(U_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
, define

q_{ij}^{\star}=w_{\star}(U_{i},U_{j})
and
q_{ij}(\beta)=w_{\beta}(U_{i},U_{j})

for
i<j
.
Let

L_{n}(\beta)=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\bigl\{q_{ij}^{\star}-q_{ij}(\beta)\bigr\}^{2}.

Then, under i.i.d.
U_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl\{L_{n}(\beta)\bigr\}=R(\beta),\qquad L_{n}(\beta)\xrightarrow{a.s.}R(\beta)\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

In particular,
\beta^{\star}
minimises
\mathbb{E}\{L_{n}(\beta)\}
and
the almost–sure limit of
L_{n}(\beta)
over all
\beta
.

7
Least–squares graphon–BPS estimation and minimax optimality

This section studies finite-sample estimation of the graphon-level BPS
combination from i.i.d. edge samples. We show: (i) least squares (LS)
in the agent span is
L^{2}
-consistent with parametric
1/m
risk,
(ii) it is minimax-rate optimal over that span, and (iii) it enjoys clean
oracle inequalities and a formal “combination beats components” result.

7.1
Data model, regression view, and estimator

Assumption 7.1

(Edge sampling model)
.

Let
X=(X_{1},X_{2})
denote a pair of latent variables distributed as

(U_{1},U_{2})
, with
U_{1},U_{2}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
independent.
Given
X
, we observe

Y\mid X\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\bigl(w_{\star}(X_{1},X_{2})\bigr),

independently across samples. We observe an i.i.d. sample

(X_{s},Y_{s})_{s=1}^{m}
from the joint law of
(X,Y)
.

When
w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}:=\mathrm{span}\{1,w_{1},\dots,w_{J}\}
, there is a unique

\beta^{\star}\in\mathbb{R}^{J+1}
such that

w_{\star}(x,y)=w_{\beta^{\star}}(x,y)=\beta^{\star\top}F(x,y),\qquad F(x,y)=(1,w_{1}(x,y),\dots,w_{J}(x,y))^{\top}.

Define population and empirical normal equations

G=\mathbb{E}[F(X)F(X)^{\top}],\qquad h=\mathbb{E}[F(X)Y],\qquad\hat{G}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F(X_{s})F(X_{s})^{\top},\qquad\hat{h}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F(X_{s})Y_{s}.

Definition 7.2

(Least–squares graphon–BPS estimator)
.

Assume
G
is positive definite and that
\hat{G}_{m}
is invertible for
all sufficiently large
m
with probability
1
.
Define the least–squares estimator of
\beta^{\star}
by

\hat{\beta}_{m}=\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\hat{h}_{m},

and the LS graphon estimator by

\hat{w}_{m}(x,y)=\hat{\beta}_{m}^{\top}F(x,y),\qquad(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}.

We measure error using the graphon
L^{2}
norm

\|w\|_{2}^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\!\int_{0}^{1}w(x,y)^{2}\,dx\,dy=\mathbb{E}\big[w(U_{1},U_{2})^{2}\big].

Assumption 7.3

(Regularity of features)
.

We assume:

(a)

G
is positive definite with smallest eigenvalue

\lambda_{\min}(G)>0
and largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}(G)<\infty
.

(b)

There exists
B<\infty
such that
\|F(X)\|_{2}\leq B
almost surely.

Assumption
7.3
holds, for example, when each
w_{j}
is
bounded in
[0,1]
, since then each component of
F
lies in
[0,1]
and

\|F(X)\|_{2}^{2}\leq J+1
.

7.2
Consistency and parametric risk rate

Theorem 7.4

(Consistency and rate of LS graphon–BPS)
.

Under Assumptions
7.1
and
7.3
,
let
\hat{\beta}_{m}
and
\hat{w}_{m}
be as in Definition
7.2
.
Then:

(i)

\hat{\beta}_{m}\xrightarrow{a.s.}\beta^{\star}
and

\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0
as
m\to\infty
.

(ii)

There exists
C<\infty
, depending only on
J,B,\lambda_{\min}(G)
,
such that for all
m
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{C}{m}.

Inside the
d=J+1
dimensional agent span, estimating the optimal synthesis is a
finite-dimensional regression problem, so the error decays at the parametric
1/m
rate.
This result is the basic “statistical stability” guarantee: with enough sampled edges,
LS graphon–BPS recovers the best agent combination in
L^{2}
.

7.3
Minimax optimality over the agent span

Let
d=J+1
and define

\mathcal{B}(R)=\{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|\beta\|_{2}\leq R\},\qquad\mathcal{H}(R)=\{w_{\beta}:\beta\in\mathcal{B}(R)\}.

The minimax
L^{2}
risk is

\mathcal{R}_{m}^{\star}=\inf_{\tilde{w}_{m}}\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\tilde{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big].

Theorem 7.5

(Minimax
L^{2}
risk over the agent span)
.

Under Assumptions
7.1
and
7.3
,
let
d=J+1
denote the dimension of
\mathcal{H}
.
Assume
R\geq 1
, so the constant graphon
w\equiv 1/2
lies in
\mathcal{H}(R)
.
Then there exist constants
0<c\leq C<\infty
(depending only on
J,B,\lambda_{\min}(G)
)
such that for all sufficiently large
m
:

(i)

(Upper bound) The LS estimator satisfies

\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\bigl[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]\leq\frac{Cd}{m}.

(ii)

(Lower bound) The minimax risk obeys

\mathcal{R}_{m}^{\star}\geq\frac{cd}{m}.

This pins down the best possible worst-case rate in the agent span: no estimator can beat order
d/m
,
and LS achieves it.
So, within the information content of the agents (a
d
-dimensional space), LS graphon–BPS is essentially
as good as it gets.

Remark 7.6
.

Theorem
7.5
implies there is no estimator that can uniformly improve the worst–case
L^{2}

risk over
\mathcal{H}(R)
beyond constant factors:
d/m
is the correct complexity.

7.4
Oracle inequalities and “combination beats components”

7.4.1
Projection (misspecification) risk bound

When
w_{\star}\notin\mathcal{H}
, define the approximation error

\inf_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}(R)}\|w_{\star}-w_{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}
.

Theorem 7.7

(LS graphon–BPS: projection risk bound)
.

Suppose Assumptions
7.3
and the sampling model in
Assumption
7.1
hold, but we do
not
assume
w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}
.
Let

G=\mathbb{E}[F(X)F(X)^{\top}],\qquad h_{\star}=\mathbb{E}[F(X)w_{\star}(X)].

Define the (unique)
L^{2}
projection of
w_{\star}
onto
\mathcal{H}
by

\beta^{\circ}:=G^{-1}h_{\star},\qquad w^{\circ}:=w_{\beta^{\circ}}.

Then there exists
C<\infty
(depending only on
J,B,\lambda_{\min}(G)
) such that for all
m
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]\leq\frac{Cd}{m}.

Even if the truth lies outside the agent span, LS graphon–BPS learns the
best approximation inside the span

at the same parametric rate.
This is exactly the “variance cost” of fitting
d
coefficients; the remaining error is purely approximation (bias).

Theorem 7.8

(Oracle inequality for LS graphon–BPS)
.

Under the conditions above, there exists
C<\infty
(depending only on
J,B,\lambda_{\min}(G)
) such that
for all
m
and all graphons
w_{\star}
,

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq 2\,\inf_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}(R)}\|w_{\star}-w_{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{Cd}{m}.

This bound cleanly separates error into (i) approximation: how well the agent span can represent the truth,
and (ii) estimation: the
d/m
price to learn the best combination from data.
It formalizes the idea that LS graphon–BPS “adapts” to whatever combination is best without knowing it in advance.

7.4.2
Combination beats any single agent

Let the convex hull of agents be

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{conv}}=\Big\{w_{\alpha}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{j}w_{j}:\alpha_{j}\geq 0,\ \sum_{j=1}^{J}\alpha_{j}=1\Big\}.

Definition 7.9

(Model selection estimators)
.

A
model selection estimator based on the agents
is a sequence of
measurable mappings

\hat{J}_{m}:\big(([0,1]^{2}\times\{0,1\})^{m},\mathcal{B}\big)\to\{1,\dots,J\},

and the associated graphon estimator is

\hat{w}_{m}^{\mathrm{sel}}=w_{\hat{J}_{m}}.

Theorem 7.10

(Combination beats any single agent)
.

Assume
J\geq 2
and
w_{1},\dots,w_{J}
are distinct in
L^{2}([0,1]^{2})
.
Then there exists
w_{\star}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{conv}}
such that:

(i)

For every model selection estimator
\hat{w}_{m}^{\mathrm{sel}}
,
there exists
\delta>0
(depending on
w_{\star}
and the agents but not on
m
) such that

\sup_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}^{\mathrm{sel}}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\geq\delta^{2}.

(ii)

For the LS graphon–BPS estimator
\hat{w}_{m}
,

\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{Cd}{m}\quad\text{for all }m,

and hence
\hat{w}_{m}
is
L^{2}
–consistent at
w_{\star}
.

If the true graphon genuinely mixes structures, any rule that must pick
one
agent can stay a fixed distance away forever.
LS graphon–BPS avoids this by estimating a
combination
, so it can converge and achieve the optimal rate when mixtures are needed.

Corollary 7.11

(Agent–only minimax optimality)
.

Let
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{H}}
be the class of estimators that always output an element of
\mathcal{H}
.
Under the assumptions of Theorem
7.5
, there exist constants
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}<\infty
such that,
for all sufficiently large
m
,

c_{1}\,\frac{d}{m}\leq\inf_{\tilde{w}_{m}\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{H}}}\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\tilde{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq c_{2}\,\frac{d}{m}.

8
Error bounds for graphon–BPS and network functionals

8.1
Graphon Lipschitz bounds

For any graphon
w:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
define

e(w):=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}w(x,y)\,dx\,dy,\qquad d_{w}(x):=\int_{0}^{1}w(x,y)\,dy,

t(w)=\int_{[0,1]^{3}}w(x,y)w(y,z)w(x,z)\,dx\,dy\,dz,\qquad s(w)=\int_{0}^{1}d_{w}(x)^{2}\,dx,

and the clustering coefficient

C(w)=\begin{cases}t(w)/s(w),&s(w)>0,\\
0,&s(w)=0.\end{cases}

Theorem 8.1

(Lipschitz bounds for graphon functionals)
.

Let
w,w^{\prime}:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
be graphons. Then:

1.

Edge density and degree function are
L^{2}
–Lipschitz:

|e(w)-e(w^{\prime})|\leq\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2},\qquad\|d_{w}-d_{w^{\prime}}\|_{2}\leq\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2}.

2.

Triangle and wedge densities are
L^{2}
–Lipschitz:

|t(w)-t(w^{\prime})|\leq 3\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2},\qquad|s(w)-s(w^{\prime})|\leq 2\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2}.

3.

If
s(w),s(w^{\prime})\geq s_{0}>0
, then

|C(w)-C(w^{\prime})|\leq\frac{3}{s_{0}}\,\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2}+\frac{2}{s_{0}^{2}}\,\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2}.

This theorem turns an
L^{2}
graphon approximation guarantee into guarantees for interpretable network summaries:
edge density, degrees, triangles, wedges, and clustering.
It’s the bridge between “we learned the right graphon” and “we learned the right network properties.”

Corollary 8.2

(Graphon-level BPS error bounds)
.

Let
w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
be the BPS graphon and
w_{\star}
the true graphon, and set

\Delta=\|w_{\star}-w_{\mathrm{BPS}}\|_{2}
. If
s(w_{\mathrm{BPS}}),s(w_{\star})\geq s_{0}>0
, then

|e(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})-e(w_{\star})|\leq\Delta,\qquad|C(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})-C(w_{\star})|\leq\Bigl(\frac{3}{s_{0}}+\frac{2}{s_{0}^{2}}\Bigr)\Delta.

Remark 8.3
.

The constants arise from (i)
0\leq w,w^{\prime}\leq 1
and (ii) the unit measure of
[0,1]^{2}
.
The clustering bound becomes ill-conditioned when wedge density is small, reflected by
1/s_{0}
and
1/s_{0}^{2}
.

8.2
Transfer to finite graphs

We consider graphs
G_{n}(w)
generated from a graphon
w
via i.i.d. latent

U_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
and independent edges

A^{(w)}_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(w(U_{i},U_{j}))
,
i<j
.

Theorem 8.4

(Asymptotic network properties under graphon–BPS)
.

Let
w^{\star}
and
w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
be graphons on
[0,1]^{2}
, and let

(U_{i})_{i\geq 1}
be i.i.d.
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
.
For any
w
, define
G_{n}(w)
on vertex set
[n]
by

\Pr\bigl(A^{(w)}_{ij}=1\mid(U_{k})_{k\geq 1}\bigr)=w(U_{i},U_{j}),\qquad 1\leq i<j\leq n,

with edges conditionally independent given
(U_{i})_{i\geq 1}
.
Write
G_{n}^{\star}:=G_{n}(w^{\star})
and
G_{n}^{\mathrm{BPS}}:=G_{n}(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})
.

Define the degree of vertex
i
in
G_{n}(w)
as

D_{i}^{(w)}:=\sum_{j\neq i}A^{(w)}_{ij},

and the average degree as

D_{n}(w):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}^{(w)}.

Define the triangle and wedge densities as

T_{n}(w):=\binom{n}{3}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq n}A^{(w)}_{ij}A^{(w)}_{ik}A^{(w)}_{jk},\qquad S_{n}(w):=\frac{1}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D_{i}^{(w)}\bigl(D_{i}^{(w)}-1\bigr),

and the clustering coefficient as

C_{n}(w):=\begin{cases}T_{n}(w)/S_{n}(w),&S_{n}(w)>0,\\
0,&S_{n}(w)=0.\end{cases}

1.

LLNs.
For fixed
w
,

\frac{D_{n}(w)}{n-1}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}e(w),\qquad T_{n}(w)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}t(w),\qquad S_{n}(w)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}s(w).

and if
s(w)>0
, then
C_{n}(w)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}C(w)
.

2.

Degree distribution limit.
For fixed
w
,

\frac{D^{(w)}_{I_{n}}}{n-1}\ \Longrightarrow\ d_{w}(U),\quad U\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1].

3.

Stability under BPS.

Assume
s(w^{\star}),s(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})\geq s_{0}>0
and set

\Delta:=\|w^{\star}-w_{\mathrm{BPS}}\|_{2}
.
Then, as
n\to\infty
,

\Biggl|\frac{D_{n}(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})}{n-1}-\frac{D_{n}(w^{\star})}{n-1}\Biggr|=O_{\mathbb{P}}(\Delta)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1),

and

\bigl|C_{n}(G_{n}^{\mathrm{BPS}})-C_{n}(G_{n}^{\star})\bigr|=O_{\mathbb{P}}(\Delta)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

Part (i) says the usual graph statistics concentrate around their graphon limits.
Part (iii) is the key “transfer” message: if graphon–BPS is close to the truth in
L^{2}
, then
finite-graph summaries (average degree, clustering) are close as well—up to the same scale.
This is what lets you turn estimation bounds into guarantees about observable network behavior.

8.3
Dense regime: distances and centrality in
G_{n}(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})

This subsection records simple but useful limiting forms of standard
centrality measures in graphs sampled from the BPS graphon. The key
observation is that when
w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
is bounded away from
0
and

1
, graph distances concentrate on
\{1,2\}
, so closeness becomes a
function of the graphon degree and betweenness vanishes.

Theorem 8.5

(Closeness and betweenness centralities)
.

Assume the BPS graphon
w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
satisfies

\epsilon\;\leq\;w_{\mathrm{BPS}}(x,y)\;\leq\;1-\epsilon,\qquad\text{for some }\epsilon>0,

for almost every
(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2}
.
Let
G_{n}^{\mathrm{BPS}}=G_{n}(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})
be the random graph
generated from
w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
via latent variables

U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
and,
conditionally on
(U_{i})
, independent edges

A^{(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})}_{ij}\mid(U_{i},U_{j})\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}\bigl(w_{\mathrm{BPS}}(U_{i},U_{j})\bigr),\qquad i<j.

Denote graph distance in
G_{n}^{\mathrm{BPS}}
by
\mathrm{dist}_{n}(\cdot,\cdot)
.
For a vertex
i
define its closeness and (normalised) betweenness by

\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(i)=\frac{n-1}{\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{dist}_{n}(i,j)},\qquad\mathrm{Bt}_{n}(i)=\frac{1}{(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\neq k\\
j,k\neq i\end{subarray}}\sigma_{jk}(i),

where
\sigma_{jk}(i)
is the fraction of shortest
j\to k
paths that
pass through
i
. Let
d_{w}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}w_{\mathrm{BPS}}(x,y)\,dy
denote
the graphon degree function. Then:

(i)

For each fixed
i
, with
U_{i}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
,

\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(i)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\frac{1}{2-d_{w}(U_{i})}.

(ii)

For every fixed
i
,
\mathrm{Bt}_{n}(i)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0
,
and moreover
\max_{i}\mathrm{Bt}_{n}(i)=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1/n)
.

When
w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
is uniformly bounded away from
0
and
1
, every
pair of vertices is either directly connected or has many length–2
paths with overwhelming probability. Distances therefore concentrate on

\{1,2\}
, so closeness reduces to a simple transformation of the
(normalised) degree, and no vertex can carry a nontrivial fraction of
shortest paths (betweenness
\to 0
). This gives an interpretable
“centrality limit theory” for BPS graphons: the only asymptotically
meaningful heterogeneity in these dense graphs is degree heterogeneity.

8.4
Sparse regime: phase transition under a
1/n
scaling

We now study a sparse rescaling of the BPS graphon. The result is a
standard kernel-random-graph phase transition, with the critical value
controlled by the spectral radius of the graphon integral operator.
Part (iii) gives a clean way to upper/lower bound the BPS threshold from
agent-level thresholds.

Theorem 8.6

(Phase transition for sparse graphon BPS)
.

Let
w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
be the BPS graphon, and let
T_{w_{\mathrm{BPS}}}
be
the integral operator on
L^{2}[0,1]
,

(T_{w_{\mathrm{BPS}}}f)(x):=\int_{0}^{1}w_{\mathrm{BPS}}(x,y)\,f(y)\,dy.

Let
\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}
denote its spectral radius. For
\lambda>0
and
each
n
, generate the sparse inhomogeneous random graph
G_{n}^{(\lambda)}

as follows: Draw i.i.d. latent variables

U_{1},\dots,U_{n}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
, and for
i<j
set

\Pr\big(A^{(\lambda)}_{ij}=1\mid U_{i},U_{j}\big)=\frac{\lambda}{n}\,w_{\mathrm{BPS}}(U_{i},U_{j}),

with the edges conditionally independent given
(U_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
.
(For each fixed
\lambda
this defines a valid model for all sufficiently
large
n
, which is enough for the asymptotic results below.)
Assume
w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
is irreducible in the usual kernel sense.

(i)

If
\lambda\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}\leq 1
, then for every

\varepsilon>0
,

\Pr\Big(\frac{1}{n}\,\big|\mathcal{C}_{\max}(G_{n}^{(\lambda)})\big|>\varepsilon\Big)\to 0,

where
\mathcal{C}_{\max}
denotes the largest connected component.

(ii)

If
\lambda\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}>1
, then there exists

\zeta(\lambda)\in(0,1)
such that

\frac{1}{n}\,\big|\mathcal{C}_{\max}(G_{n}^{(\lambda)})\big|\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\zeta(\lambda),

and all other components have size
o_{\mathbb{P}}(n)
.

(iii)

Suppose

w_{\mathrm{BPS}}=\beta_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{j}w_{j},

with
\beta_{j}\geq 0
for
j=0,\dots,J
and each agent graphon
w_{j}

having associated operator
T_{w_{j}}
with spectral radius
\rho_{j}
.
Then

\max_{0\leq j\leq J}\beta_{j}\rho_{j}\;\leq\;\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}\;\leq\;\sum_{j=0}^{J}\beta_{j}\rho_{j},

and the critical parameter
\lambda_{c}:=1/\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}
satisfies

\frac{1}{\sum_{j=0}^{J}\beta_{j}\rho_{j}}\;\leq\;\lambda_{c}\;\leq\;\frac{1}{\max_{0\leq j\leq J}\beta_{j}\rho_{j}}.

After scaling by
1/n
, local neighborhoods converge to a branching
process whose mean “offspring operator” is
\lambda T_{w_{\mathrm{BPS}}}
.
A giant component appears exactly when that branching process becomes
supercritical, i.e. when
\lambda\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}>1
. Part (iii) is
practically useful: it shows that if
w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
is a nonnegative
combination of agent graphons, then the BPS phase transition is squeezed
between simple, agent-level spectral-radius bounds.

8.5
ER, SBM and RDPG aggregation

We close the section with a fully explicit three-agent illustration.
The statement packages together (i) parametric
L^{2}
estimation over a
fixed agent span, (ii) Lipschitz transfer from
L^{2}
error to structural
functionals, and (iii) a clean “aggregation beats selection” message.

Corollary 8.7

(ER, SBM and RDPG aggregation for degrees, triangles and clustering)
.

Fix three agents: an Erdős–Rényi agent with graphon
w_{\mathrm{ER}}
,
a stochastic block model agent with graphon
w_{\mathrm{SBM}}
, and a
logistic RDPG agent with graphon
w_{\mathrm{RDPG}}
, all taking values
in
[0,1]
.
Let

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{mix}}=\Big\{w_{\alpha}=\alpha_{1}w_{\mathrm{ER}}+\alpha_{2}w_{\mathrm{SBM}}+\alpha_{3}w_{\mathrm{RDPG}}:\ \alpha_{j}\geq 0,\ \sum_{j=1}^{3}\alpha_{j}=1\Big\}

be their convex hull, and let

.

9
ERGMs under log–linear BPS and subgraph calibration

This section returns to finite-graph ERGM agents. The main point is that

log–linear pooling
(optionally plus entropic tilting) preserves
ERGM structure once we stack sufficient statistics across agents. This
gives a simple route to multi-feature calibration (e.g. combining
different subgraph and block statistics) while remaining in a single
ERGM family.

9.1
Log–linear BPS for ERGM agents

Suppose our agent set includes ERGMs with sufficient statistics

T^{(j)}:\mathcal{A}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d_{j}}
, and we form a stacked
statistic

T_{\mathrm{stack}}(A)=\begin{pmatrix}T^{(1)}(A)\\
\vdots\\
T^{(J)}(A)\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\qquad d=\sum_{j}d_{j}.

A
log–linear BPS
(or log–linear opinion pool) at the graph level
takes the form

f(A)\propto\prod_{j=1}^{J}p_{j}(A)^{\omega_{j}},\qquad\omega_{j}\geq 0,\ \sum_{j}\omega_{j}=1,

possibly combined with entropic tilting weights

\exp\{\tau^{\top}T_{\mathrm{stack}}(A)\}
.
This yields a synthesised distribution

f(A)\propto\exp\Big\{\sum_{j}\omega_{j}\theta_{j}^{\top}T^{(j)}(A)+\tau^{\top}T_{\mathrm{stack}}(A)\Big\}\mu(A),

which is again an ERGM on the stacked statistic.

Corollary 9.1

(ERGMs closed under log–linear BPS)
.

Let
M_{j}
be ERGM agents with statistics
T^{(j)}
and natural parameters

\theta_{j}
.
Let

f(A)\propto\left(\prod_{j}p_{j}(A)^{\omega_{j}}\right)\exp\{\tau^{\top}T_{\mathrm{stack}}(A)\}

with weights
\omega_{j}
and tilt

\tau
.
Then
f
is an ERGM with sufficient statistic
T_{\mathrm{stack}}
and
natural parameter

\theta_{\mathrm{BPS}}=\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}\theta_{1}+\tau^{(1)}\\
\vdots\\
\omega_{J}\theta_{J}+\tau^{(J)}\end{pmatrix},

where
\tau^{(j)}
is the component of
\tau
corresponding to
T^{(j)}
.

9.2
Subgraph frequency calibration

Let
T(A)
collect subgraph counts (edges, stars, wedges, triangles,
and any finite family of small motifs), suitably rescaled as
frequencies. Under Theorem
3.3
(ii), given target moments

m=\mathbb{E}_{f}[T(A)]
we can choose
\tau
so that the tilted ERGM
matches these targets and is KL–closest to the baseline
p_{j}
.

Corollary 9.2

(Subgraph frequency calibration via entropic BPS)
.

Let
T:\mathcal{A}_{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
collect (scaled) counts of a finite
set of subgraphs
H_{1},\dots,H_{d}
.
Let
p_{j}
be an ERGM agent with statistic
T
and parameter
\theta_{j}
.
For any target vector
m
in the mean parameter space of
T(A)
there
exists a unique tilted ERGM

f(A)\propto\exp\{\tau^{\top}T(A)\}p_{j}(A)

such that
\mathbb{E}_{f}[T(A)]=m
, and
f
is the unique minimiser of

KL(f\|p_{j})
among all distributions with those subgraph means.

10
Heavy–tail preservation: mixtures and tilting

This section studies when BPS preserves heavy–tailed degree behavior.
We separate two mechanisms:
(i)
mixtures
(where a heavy-tailed component can dominate the tail),
and (ii)
tilting
(where synthesis weights modify probabilities of
tail events). The main messages are: the smallest tail exponent among
nonnegligible agents dominates under mixtures, and exponent preservation
under tilting is tightly linked to slow variation (or boundedness) of
the tilt on degree-tail events.

10.1
Mixtures: the smallest tail exponent dominates

Assumption 10.1

(Degree tails of agents)
.

For each agent
j\in\{0,\dots,J\}
and each
n\geq 1
, let
D_{i}^{(j,n)}
denote the degree
of vertex
i
in the graph
G_{n}^{(j)}
produced by agent
j
at size
n
. We partition
the agents into a finite heavy–tailed set
H
and a light–tailed set
L
as follows.

[(a)]

(a)

Heavy–tailed agents.

For each
j\in H
there exist constants
c_{j}>0
and
\gamma_{j}>1
, and a number

\alpha_{j}\in(0,1)
, such that for every
\varepsilon>0
there are integers

K_{j}(\varepsilon)\geq 1
and
N_{j}(\varepsilon)\geq 1
with

\sup_{n\geq N_{j}(\varepsilon)}\ \sup_{K_{j}(\varepsilon)\leq k\leq\alpha_{j}n}\left|\frac{\Pr\!\big(D_{i}^{(j,n)}\geq k\big)}{c_{j}k^{-\gamma_{j}}}-1\right|\leq\varepsilon.

(b)

Light–tailed agents.

For each
j\in L
there exist constants
C_{j}>0
and
\eta_{j}>0
such that

\Pr\!\big(D_{i}^{(j,n)}\geq k\big)\leq C_{j}e^{-\eta_{j}k}\qquad\text{for all }n\geq 1\text{ and all integers }k\geq 1.

(c)

Nonvanishing minimal heavy component.

Define

\gamma_{\min}:=\min\{\gamma_{j}:j\in H\}.

There exists at least one heavy–tailed agent
j_{\star}\in H
with exponent

\gamma_{j_{\star}}=\gamma_{\min}
and limiting BPS mixture weight

\tilde{\pi}_{j_{\star}}>0
.

Theorem 10.2

(Heavy–tailed degrees under BPS mixture)
.

Let
G_{n}
be the mixture–BPS graph at size
n
as in
Theorem
5.3
, with mixture weights
\tilde{\pi}_{j}^{(n)}

satisfying
\tilde{\pi}_{j}^{(n)}\to\tilde{\pi}_{j}\in[0,1]
and

\sum_{j=0}^{J}\tilde{\pi}_{j}=1
. Assume
10.1
.
Fix a vertex
i
and let
D_{i}^{(n)}
be its degree in
G_{n}
.
Then there exist a constant
C_{\mathrm{mix}}>0
and a number
\alpha\in(0,1)
such
that for every
\varepsilon>0
there are integers
K(\varepsilon)\geq 1
and

N(\varepsilon)\geq 1
with

(1-\varepsilon)\,C_{\mathrm{mix}}\,k^{-\gamma_{\min}}\ \leq\ \Pr\!\big(D_{i}^{(n)}\geq k\big)\ \leq\ (1+\varepsilon)\,C_{\mathrm{mix}}\,k^{-\gamma_{\min}}

(1)

for all
n\geq N(\varepsilon)
and all integers
k
satisfying

K(\varepsilon)\leq k\leq\alpha n
. Equivalently,

\sup_{K(\varepsilon)\leq k\leq\alpha n}\left|\frac{\Pr\!\big(D_{i}^{(n)}\geq k\big)}{C_{\mathrm{mix}}k^{-\gamma_{\min}}}-1\right|\longrightarrow 0\qquad\text{as }n\to\infty.

In particular, in the pre–truncation regime
1\ll k\ll n
the degree tail of the
BPS mixture is regularly varying with index
\gamma_{\min}
.

A finite mixture cannot “average away” the far tail: once
k
is large,
the exponential tails from light-tailed agents are negligible compared
to any power law, and among heavy-tailed agents the slowest-decaying tail
(i.e. the
smallest exponent
) dominates. The theorem formalizes
that BPS mixtures preserve heavy-tail behavior and identify exactly
which component controls it—critical for modeling hubs and extreme
degrees.

10.2
Degree–based tilting: when does the exponent change?

Assumption 10.3

(Regular variation of baseline degrees)
.

Let
D
be a nonnegative integer–valued random variable (a vertex
degree under
P_{0}
) such that

\Pr_{P_{0}}(D\geq k)\sim C_{0}k^{-\gamma},\qquad k\to\infty,

for some constants
C_{0}>0
and
\gamma>1
.
Assume that the degree distribution is regularly varying, i.e. the
tail is regularly varying with index
-\gamma
in the sense of
Karamata.

We consider degree-dependent tilts

\frac{dP_{\ell}}{dP_{0}}(G)\propto\ell(D(G)),

for some positive function
\ell:\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty)
.

Theorem 10.4

(Tail exponent under degree–based tilting)
.

Under Assumption
10.3
, let
\ell:\mathbb{N}\to(0,\infty)
be a function such that

(a)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\ell(k)\Pr_{P_{0}}(D=k)<\infty
(normalisation);

(b)

\ell
is regularly varying with index
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
, i.e.
\ell(\lfloor tk\rfloor)/\ell(k)\to t^{\rho}
as
k\to\infty
for each fixed
t>0
.

Then necessarily
\rho<\gamma
, and the tilted distribution
P_{\ell}
has regularly varying tail

\Pr_{P_{\ell}}(D\geq k)\sim C_{\ell}k^{-(\gamma-\rho)},\qquad k\to\infty,

for some constant
C_{\ell}>0
. In particular, the power–law exponent is preserved (i.e. remains
\gamma
) if and only if
\rho=0
, i.e. if and only if
\ell
is slowly varying.

A degree-based tilt multiplies probabilities by
\ell(k)
. If
\ell
grows
(or decays) polynomially like
k^{\rho}
, it effectively shifts the power-law
exponent from
\gamma
to
\gamma-\rho
. The only way to preserve the exponent
is to weight degrees by a function that changes
slower than any power

(slow variation), such as
\log(1+k)
or bounded perturbations. This gives a
sharp “exponent preservation iff slow variation” criterion.

10.3
Graph–level entropic tilting: bounded and polynomial control

We now return to graph-level entropic tilting. The bounded-statistic case
shows exponent preservation by a simple sandwich argument; the
polynomially controlled case yields explicit upper/lower power-law
brackets.

Theorem 10.5

(Heavy tails under bounded entropic tilting)
.

Let
P_{0}
be the law of a random graph whose degree distribution is regularly varying with exponent

\gamma>1
as in Assumption
10.3
, i.e. there exists
C_{0}>0
such that

\Pr_{P_{0}}(D_{v}\geq k)\sim C_{0}k^{-\gamma},\qquad k\to\infty.

Let
s(G)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite-dimensional network statistic vector such that

\|s(G)\|\leq B
almost surely for some
B<\infty
. For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
define the
entropically tilted law

P_{\lambda}(g)\;=\;\frac{1}{M(\lambda)}\,P_{0}(g)\exp\!\bigl(\lambda^{\top}s(g)\bigr),\qquad M(\lambda)\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\!\bigl[e^{\lambda^{\top}s(G)}\bigr].

Then
M(\lambda)
is finite and strictly positive for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and for any
fixed
\lambda
the degree distribution under
P_{\lambda}
has the same power–law exponent as under

P_{0}
:

\Pr_{P_{\lambda}}(D_{v}\geq k)\asymp k^{-\gamma},\qquad k\to\infty.

Equivalently, there exist constants
0<c_{1}(\lambda,B)\leq c_{2}(\lambda,B)<\infty
such that for all
sufficiently large
k
,

c_{1}k^{-\gamma}\;\leq\;\Pr_{P_{\lambda}}(D_{v}\geq k)\;\leq\;c_{2}k^{-\gamma}.

If
s(G)
is bounded, then the tilt factor
\exp(\lambda^{\top}s(G))
is
bounded above and below by constants, so it can only rescale tail
probabilities by constant factors. Constants cannot change power-law
exponents, so the heavy-tail exponent is preserved. This formalizes the
robustness of heavy tails to “bounded” synthesis objectives.

Assumption 10.6

(Polynomial control of the tilt on degree tails)
.

Let
P_{0}
be as in Assumption
10.3
and let
s(G)
be a (scalar) network statistic.
We say that the entropic tilt
e^{\lambda s(G)}
is polynomially controlled on degree
tails if there exist constants
c_{-},c_{+}>0
,
\beta_{-},\beta_{+}\geq 0
and
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}

such that for all
k\geq k_{0}
,

c_{-}(1+k)^{-\beta_{-}}\;\leq\;\inf\{e^{\lambda s(G)}:D_{v}(G)\geq k\}\;\leq\;\sup\{e^{\lambda s(G)}:D_{v}(G)\geq k\}\;\leq\;c_{+}(1+k)^{\beta_{+}},

for the value of
\lambda
under consideration.

Theorem 10.7

(Heavy tails under polynomially controlled tilting)
.

Let
P_{0}
be a random-graph law such that the degree
D_{v}
at a fixed vertex
v
has a
regularly varying tail

\Pr_{P_{0}}(D_{v}\geq k)\sim C_{0}k^{-\gamma},\qquad k\to\infty,

for some
C_{0}>0
and
\gamma>1
(Assumption
10.3
).
Let
s(G)
and
\lambda
satisfy Assumption
10.6
with
parameters
c_{\pm},\beta_{\pm},k_{0}
, and set

M(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}_{P_{0}}\!\big[e^{\lambda s(G)}\big].

Assume
0<M(\lambda)<\infty
, so that the tilted law

\frac{\mathrm{d}P_{\lambda}}{\mathrm{d}P_{0}}(G)=\frac{1}{M(\lambda)}e^{\lambda s(G)}

is well defined. Then there exist constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
and
k_{1}\geq k_{0}
such that
for all
k\geq k_{1}
,

C_{1}\,k^{-(\gamma+\beta_{-})}\;\leq\;\Pr_{P_{\lambda}}(D_{v}\geq k)\;\leq\;C_{2}\,k^{-(\gamma-\beta_{+})}.

(2)

Equivalently, if we define the effective power-law exponent

\alpha(k):=-\frac{\log\Pr_{P_{\lambda}}(D_{v}\geq k)}{\log k},

then

\gamma-\beta_{+}\;\leq\;\liminf_{k\to\infty}\alpha(k)\;\leq\;\limsup_{k\to\infty}\alpha(k)\;\leq\;\gamma+\beta_{-}.

(3)

In particular:

1.

If
\beta_{+}<\gamma
, then the tilted degree distribution remains
polynomially heavy–tailed: its tail decays between two power laws
with positive exponents, as in (
2
).

2.

In the bounded–tilt case
\beta_{-}=\beta_{+}=0
we obtain

C_{1}k^{-\gamma}\;\leq\;\Pr_{P_{\lambda}}(D_{v}\geq k)\;\leq\;C_{2}k^{-\gamma},\qquad k\geq k_{1},

i.e. tail equivalence

\Pr_{P_{\lambda}}(D_{v}\geq k)\asymp k^{-\gamma}
with the same
power–law exponent
\gamma
, recovering Theorem
10.5
.

If the tilt on tail events grows (or decays) at most polynomially in the
degree, then the original
k^{-\gamma}
tail gets multiplied by at most

k^{\beta_{+}}
(upper) and at least
k^{-\beta_{-}}
(lower). This produces
explicit bracketing exponents and a transparent “exponent interval”
interpretation. In particular, bounded tilts (
\beta_{\pm}=0
) cannot
change the exponent, while polynomial tilts can shift it in a controlled
way.

11
Experiments

11.1
Design principle: theorem-shaped evidence, not only leaderboard wins

At the graphon level, Bayesian predictive synthesis (BPS) corresponds to an

L_{2}
-optimal linear projection
of the unknown edge-probability kernel onto the
linear span
of an agent library (Theorem
6.2
), and that
least-squares (LS) graphon–BPS enjoys nonasymptotic oracle inequalities separating
approximation
and
estimation
error (Theorem
7.8
).
Moreover, the theory highlights that
selection among misspecified models is not uniformly consistent
(Theorem
7.10
), providing a principled
motivation for synthesis rather than “pick-the-best-agent” strategies.

Accordingly, the experiments are designed to test
mechanism-level signatures
that are sharp consequences of the theorems, under evaluation
protocols that are difficult to misread and easy to audit. We emphasize proper scoring rules (Brier/log-loss) because the projection characterization
and oracle inequalities are statements about probability risk, not primarily about ranking.

11.2
Theory-shaped empirical targets

We pre-register the following theorem-aligned empirical targets.

1.

Projection signature: span vs. simplex/selection.

Under heterogeneous structure (e.g., communities + geometry/low-rank + degree heterogeneity), the optimal
L_{2}
projection onto the agent

span
need not lie in the convex hull of agent predictions. Therefore,
BPS_LS
should strictly improve
proper-score risk

beyond (i) any single agent and (ii) simplex-constrained convex mixing (
BPS_Simplex
), which collapses to averaging/selection when the
convex hull is too restrictive. This is the central falsifiable signature of the projection theorem.

2.

Nonasymptotic learning-curve signature: approximation vs. estimation.

Oracle inequalities predict a decomposition

\text{Risk}(\widehat{p}^{\mathrm{BPS}})\;\lesssim\;\underbrace{\inf_{p\in\mathrm{span}(\mathcal{A})}\text{Risk}(p)}_{\text{approximation}}\;+\;\underbrace{C\cdot d/m}_{\text{estimation}},

so as information increases (larger
n
and/or more observed dyads
m
), uncertainty should shrink while the BPS gap can persist as an

approximation advantage
(reduced misspecification bias). Empirically we therefore expect decreasing variance with
m
but potentially stable
mean gaps in proper scores.

3.

Proper-score mechanism signature: calibration, not “ranking luck.”

Large log-loss gains must be explainable by reduced overconfident probability errors rather than leakage. Reliability diagrams and Murphy’s Brier
decomposition provide a mechanistic audit: improvements should be attributable to improved
reliability
and/or increased
resolution

without catastrophic reliability loss. By contrast, AUC/AP depend mainly on ranking and need not track proper-score gains.

4.

Sparse-regime structural signature (optional / appendix).

In the sparse regime, theory predicts a phase transition for the emergence of a giant component at a critical sparsity level
(Theorem
8.6
). A targeted synthetic experiment should show the onset of macroscopic connectivity near the predicted threshold.

5.

Heavy-tail mixture signature (optional / appendix).

For mixtures of heavy-tailed mechanisms, tail behavior is governed by the smallest tail exponent (Theorem
10.2
). A targeted synthetic experiment
should show estimated tail exponents decreasing toward the heavier-tailed component as the mixture weight increases.

We emphasize
proper scoring rules
: Brier score (empirical analogue of
L_{2}
risk) and log-loss (strictly proper; strongly penalizes
overconfidence). Ranking metrics (AUC/AP) are reported as robustness checks but are not direct theorem targets.

11.3
Methods compared

We use a modular library of complementary structural mechanisms:
(i) Chung–Lu (degree heterogeneity),
(ii) DegHist (
k=10
),
(iii) SBM (
K=5
),
(iv) LogRDPG (
d=3
).
Hyperparameters are fixed across splits to avoid tuning-on-test.

Given training dyads
\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}
with labels

and agent predictions
\hat{p}^{(j)}_{ij}

Least squares (projection onto the span).

(4)

\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{Ridge}}\in\arg\min_{\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{J+1}}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}}\left(A_{ij}-\beta_{0}-\sum_{k=1}^{J}\beta_{k}\hat{p}^{(k)}_{ij}\right)^{2}\;+\;\lambda\|\beta_{1:J}\|_{2}^{2}.

(5)

Simplex-constrained mixing (theorem-critical ablation).

\hat{\beta}_{\mathrm{Simplex}}\in\arg\min_{\beta}\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{D}_{\mathrm{tr}}}\left(A_{ij}-\beta_{0}-\sum_{k=1}^{J}\beta_{k}\hat{p}^{(k)}_{ij}\right)^{2}\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\beta_{j}\geq 0,\;\sum_{j=1}^{J}\beta_{j}=1.

(6)

\hat{p}^{\mathrm{BPS}}_{ij}=\left(\hat{\beta}_{0}+\sum_{j=1}^{J}\hat{\beta}_{j}\hat{p}^{(j)}_{ij}\right)\vee 0\wedge 1.

(7)

We report these as
BPS_LS
,
BPS_Ridge
, and
BPS_Simplex
.

Actionable baselines (no test oracle).

To avoid reliance on a test oracle, we include:

•

CV_BestAgent
: best single agent chosen using a validation split (feasible selection baseline).

•

Stack_Logistic
: logistic stacking trained to optimize log-loss (strong practical competitor).

We include
BestAgent
(test-oracle) only as a diagnostic reference (not actionable).

11.4
Evaluation protocol and regimes (auditable, paired, anti-leakage)

Each replicate uses a fixed seed and a single shared train/val/test split across all methods, enabling paired comparisons. We evaluate two
practically relevant regimes (main paper), and one diagnostic regime (used to isolate the projection signature in proper scores):

1.

Edge holdout (neg:pos fixed):
hold out observed edges; sample non-edges at a fixed negative:positive ratio.

2.

Node holdout (10% nodes):
hold out 10% of nodes; remove
all
incident edges from training; test on dyads crossing held-out
and training nodes (distribution shift / hard generalization).

3.

Uniform dyads (diagnostic):
sample dyads uniformly to stress-test global calibration and better align Brier with an
L_{2}
-type risk
interpretation; used for oracle/simplex diagnostics.

For every split we log the realized test positive rate and negative:positive ratio, verify test-edge removal from training, enforce symmetry (
i<j
),
and remove duplicate dyads.

Discussion

We have developed a static, graphon–level theory of Bayesian predictive synthesis (BPS) for random networks and a finite–sample analysis of graphon–BPS estimators. At the modelling level, we formalised random graph agents as probability laws on adjacency matrices, showed that log–linear (entropic) BPS tilting preserves exponential random graph model (ERGM) families, and derived closed–form tilts for Erdős–Rényi, stochastic block, and logistic random dot product agents. At the graphon level, we identified graphon–BPS as the
L^{2}
projection of the true graphon onto the linear span of the agent graphons, and established sharp nonasymptotic oracle inequalities together with minimax–rate optimality for least–squares graphon–BPS over this agent span. Our “combination beats components” result shows that any estimator that ultimately selects a single agent graphon is uniformly
L^{2}
–inconsistent on a nontrivial subset of the hull, whereas graphon–BPS achieves optimal risk up to constants, providing a precise formalisation of the intuition that appropriately constructed combinations can outperform all individual components.

Beyond these risk bounds, we analysed how graphon–level error translates into structural properties of random graphs. Lipschitz continuity of key functionals in the
L^{2}
metric yields control of edge density, degree functions, triangle and wedge densities, clustering coefficients and, in sparse regimes, phase transitions for the emergence of a giant component in terms of the spectral radius of the BPS kernel. Our heavy–tail results show that mixtures of heavy– and light–tailed agents inherit regularly varying degree distributions with tail index given by the most heavy–tailed agent, and that degree–based and more general bounded or polynomially controlled entropic tilts preserve power–law exponents in the sense of tail equivalence. Together, these findings demonstrate that graphon–BPS provides a principled mechanism for combining heterogeneous network models without leaving familiar classes (ER, SBM, RDPG, ERGM), while retaining crucial structural features such as exchangeability, heavy tails, and critical behaviour.

Several directions for future work are natural. From a modelling perspective, one can stack BPS layers to construct explicit hybrid network models that superpose community structure, geometry, and hub formation, implemented as ERGMs with stacked sufficient statistics, and combine graphon–BPS with sieve or Bayesian nonparametric priors to interpolate between the agent span and richer graphon classes. On the decision–theoretic side, extending our static
L^{2}
and minimax analysis to dynamic BPS schemes for evolving networks, with regret bounds and online reweighting of agents, is of clear interest. Incorporating decision objectives and multiple information sources more fully will require carefully designed loss or utility functions on graph spaces—to encode, for example, the relative importance of specific critical links versus global accuracy in link prediction—and computational methods capable of optimising such criteria in high–dimensional network spaces, such as variational or simulation–based approximations. We expect that graphon–level BPS, augmented with these dynamic and decision–guided components, will serve as a flexible foundation for principled ensemble methods in network statistics.

Remark A.1

(Decision-theoretic interpretation of Theorem
3.3
)
.

Theorem
3.3
shows that once an agent is specified as
an ERGM, BPS with exponential score weights preserves the ERGM form and
simply shifts the natural parameter by
\tau_{j}
.
Part (ii) identifies the tilted ERGM as the Kullback–Leibler projection
of
p_{j}
onto a moment–constrained set of network distributions; if
T

includes subgraph counts (edges, wedges, triangles, small motifs),
entropic BPS implements moment calibration for those subgraph
frequencies in a way that is decision–theoretically optimal under the
logarithmic score, in the sense that it uniquely minimises the
logarithmic score regret

\mathbb{E}_{f}[-\log p_{j}(A)]-\mathbb{E}_{f}[-\log f(A)]=KL(f\|p_{j})

among all
f
satisfying the moment constraint.

Remark A.2

(Further interpretation of SBM tilting)
.

Proposition
4.5
shows that entropic tilting by block–level
edge counts induces a blockwise logit shift in the connection probabilities,
while preserving the SBM form. In exponential–family terms, the block edge
counts
M_{ab}(A)
are sufficient statistics with natural parameters

\theta_{ab}=\operatorname{logit}(B_{ab}^{(j)})
, and the tilt replaces each

\theta_{ab}
by
\theta_{ab}+\lambda_{j,ab}
. Thus moment constraints on
block–level edge densities can be enforced by choosing the tilt vector

(\lambda_{j,ab})_{a\leq b}
so that the resulting SBM matches the desired
block edge moments, i.e. via linear shifts in the blockwise natural
parameters.

Remark A.3

(Consistency across
n
when mixture weights vary)
.

When the weights
\tilde{\pi}_{j}^{(n)}
vary with
n
, the family
of finite‑dimensional marginals
\{f^{(n)}\}_{n\geq 1}
need not be
consistent with a single infinite exchangeable array, so one cannot
expect the sequence
(G_{n})_{n\geq 1}
itself to be jointly exchangeable in
the Aldous–Hoover sense.
If, in addition, the weights are constant in

,

\tilde{\pi}_{j}^{(n)}\equiv\tilde{\pi}_{j}

is consistent, and there does exist a jointly exchangeable random graph
sequence with these marginals, obtained by first sampling a global

exchangeable graph sequence from agent
j
.

Proof of Theorem
3.3

Proof.

(i) By definition of
\alpha_{j}
and the ERGM form of
p_{j}
,

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}(A)p_{j}(A)

\displaystyle=\exp\big\{\tau_{j}^{\top}T(A)\big\}\exp\big\{\theta_{j}^{\top}T(A)-\psi_{j}(\theta_{j})\big\}\mu(A)

\displaystyle=\exp\big\{(\theta_{j}+\tau_{j})^{\top}T(A)-\psi_{j}(\theta_{j})\big\}\mu(A).

Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\alpha_{j}(A)p_{j}(A)

\displaystyle=e^{-\psi_{j}(\theta_{j})}\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}\exp\{(\theta_{j}+\tau_{j})^{\top}T(A)\}\,\mu(A)

\displaystyle=\exp\big\{\psi_{j}(\theta_{j}+\tau_{j})-\psi_{j}(\theta_{j})\big\},

where we used the definition of
\psi_{j}
at
\theta_{j}+\tau_{j}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle f_{j}(A)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha_{j}(A)p_{j}(A)}{\sum_{A^{\prime}}\alpha_{j}(A^{\prime})p_{j}(A^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\exp\big\{(\theta_{j}+\tau_{j})^{\top}T(A)-\psi_{j}(\theta_{j})\big\}\mu(A)\,\exp\big\{-\psi_{j}(\theta_{j}+\tau_{j})+\psi_{j}(\theta_{j})\big\}

\displaystyle=\exp\big\{(\theta_{j}+\tau_{j})^{\top}T(A)-\psi_{j}(\theta_{j}+\tau_{j})\big\}\mu(A),

so
f_{j}
is an ERGM with natural parameter
\theta_{j}+\tau_{j}
.

(ii) Let
f
be any pmf on
\mathcal{A}_{n}
satisfying

\mathbb{E}_{f}[T(A)]=m_{j}
. The Kullback–Leibler divergence is

KL(f\|p_{j})=\sum_{A\in\mathcal{A}_{n}}f(A)\log\frac{f(A)}{p_{j}(A)}.

We minimise
KL(f\|p_{j})
over
\mathcal{F}_{j}(m_{j})
subject to the
normalisation constraint
\sum_{A}f(A)=1
.

Introduce Lagrange multipliers
\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the moment
constraints and
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
for normalisation, and consider
the Lagrangian

\mathcal{L}(f,\tau,\lambda)=\sum_{A}f(A)\log\frac{f(A)}{p_{j}(A)}-\tau^{\top}\Big(\sum_{A}f(A)T(A)-m_{j}\Big)+\lambda\Big(\sum_{A}f(A)-1\Big).

For any
A
with
f(A)>0
, the derivative of
\mathcal{L}
with respect
to
f(A)
is

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial f(A)}=\log\frac{f(A)}{p_{j}(A)}+1-\tau^{\top}T(A)+\lambda.

At a constrained optimum this derivative must vanish, so

\log\frac{f(A)}{p_{j}(A)}=\tau^{\top}T(A)-1-\lambda.

Equivalently,

\log f(A)=\tau^{\top}T(A)+\log p_{j}(A)-\tilde{\lambda},

where
\tilde{\lambda}:=1+\lambda
is a scalar.
Exponentiating and absorbing
e^{-\tilde{\lambda}}
into the normalising
constant gives

f(A)\propto\exp\{\tau^{\top}T(A)\}\,p_{j}(A).

Thus any critical point of the constrained optimisation has the tilted
form

f_{\tau}(A)=\frac{\exp\{\tau^{\top}T(A)\}\,p_{j}(A)}{\sum_{A^{\prime}}\exp\{\tau^{\top}T(A^{\prime})\}\,p_{j}(A^{\prime})}.

Now observe that
KL(f\|p_{j})
is strictly convex as a functional of

f
on the simplex (its Hessian in the interior has diagonal entries

1/f(A)>0
), and the feasible set

\{f:\ \sum_{A}f(A)=1,\ \mathbb{E}_{f}[T(A)]=m_{j}\}

is convex (linear equality constraints on the simplex). Hence any
critical point is the unique global minimiser of
KL(f\|p_{j})
over

\mathcal{F}_{j}(m_{j})
.

By part (i), each
f_{\tau}
is an ERGM with natural parameter

\theta_{j}+\tau
. In an exponential family, the mean parameter

\mathbb{E}_{\theta}[T(A)]
is given by
\nabla\psi_{j}(\theta)
, and
strict convexity of
\psi_{j}
implies that the mapping

\theta\mapsto\nabla\psi_{j}(\theta)
is one-to-one from the natural
parameter space onto the mean–parameter space.
Since
m_{j}
was assumed to lie in the mean–parameter space, there is a
unique
\theta^{\star}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{\theta^{\star}}[T(A)]=m_{j}
. Writing
\theta^{\star}=\theta_{j}+\tau_{j}

defines a unique
\tau_{j}
such that the tilted ERGM
f_{j}=f_{\tau_{j}}

satisfies
\mathbb{E}_{f_{j}}[T(A)]=m_{j}
and is the unique minimiser of

KL(f\|p_{j})
over
\mathcal{F}_{j}(m_{j})
.

A technical inverse–Gram bound

Lemma A.4

(Inverse–Gram bound)
.

Under Assumption
7.3
, there exists
K<\infty
, depending only on

J
,
B
and
\lambda_{\min}(G)
, such that

\sup_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\bigr]\leq K,

where
\hat{G}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F(X_{s})F(X_{s})^{\top}
.

Proof.

We give a standard argument based on matrix concentration and an inverse-eigenvalue
integration bound (see, e.g., Vershynin,
High-Dimensional Probability
,
Theorem 5.39 and Lemma 5.41).

By Assumption
7.3
(b),
\|F(X)\|_{2}\leq B
a.s., hence each
summand
F(X_{s})F(X_{s})^{\top}
has operator norm at most
B^{2}
. Standard matrix
Bernstein/Hoeffding bounds for sample covariance matrices of bounded random
vectors imply: for any
t\in(0,\lambda_{\min}(G))
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\|\hat{G}_{m}-G\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq t\big)\leq C_{1}\exp\Big(-C_{2}\,m\,\frac{t^{2}}{B^{4}}\Big),

for constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
depending only on the dimension
d=J+1
.

Taking
t=\lambda_{\min}(G)/2
yields

\mathbb{P}\Big(\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})\leq\frac{\lambda_{\min}(G)}{2}\Big)\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\|\hat{G}_{m}-G\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq\frac{\lambda_{\min}(G)}{2}\Big)\leq C_{1}\exp(-cm),

for some
c>0
depending only on
J,B,\lambda_{\min}(G)
.

On the event
\{\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})\geq\lambda_{\min}(G)/2\}
we have

\|\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}=\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})^{-2}\leq\Big(\frac{2}{\lambda_{\min}(G)}\Big)^{2}.

On the complement,
\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})^{-2}
may be large but the event
probability decays exponentially in
m
. A standard inverse-moment integration
argument (e.g. Vershynin, Lemma 5.41) yields that
\sup_{m}\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})^{-2}]<\infty
,
hence

\sup_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\bigr]=\sup_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})^{-2}\bigr]\leq K

for some finite
K
depending only on
J,B,\lambda_{\min}(G)
.
∎

Proof.

\varepsilon_{s}=Y_{s}-w_{\star}(X_{s})

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{s}\mid X_{s}]=0
and
|\varepsilon_{s}|\leq 1

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{s}^{2}\mid X_{s}]\leq 1

\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{s}^{2}]\leq 1

G=\mathbb{E}[F(X)F(X)^{\top}],\qquad h=\mathbb{E}[F(X)Y],

w_{\star}(x,y)=\beta^{\star\top}F(x,y)
for a unique

\beta^{\star}

hence
\beta^{\star}=G^{-1}h

Define the empirical quantities

\hat{G}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F(X_{s})F(X_{s})^{\top},\qquad\hat{h}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F(X_{s})Y_{s}.

where

The LS estimator is

\hat{\beta}_{m}=\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\hat{h}_{m}=\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\hat{G}_{m}\beta^{\star}+\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\Delta_{m}=\beta^{\star}+\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\Delta_{m},

\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\star}=\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\Delta_{m}.

(b),

\hat{G}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F(X_{s})F(X_{s})^{\top}\xrightarrow{a.s.}G,\qquad\Delta_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F(X_{s})\varepsilon_{s}\xrightarrow{a.s.}0.

risk can be written as

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\big]=\mathbb{E}\big[(\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\star})^{\top}G(\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\star})\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\big]=\mathrm{tr}\bigl(G\,\mathrm{Cov}(\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\star}\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{m})\bigr),

because the conditional mean of
\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\star}
is
0
.
Using the matrix inequality above,

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\mid X_{1},\dots,X_{m}\big]\leq\frac{1}{m}\,\mathrm{tr}\bigl(G\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\bigr).

Taking expectations over
(X_{s})
gives

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{1}{m}\,\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{tr}(G\hat{G}_{m}^{-1})\big].

It remains to bound
\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{tr}(G\hat{G}_{m}^{-1})]
by a
finite constant that depends only on
J
,
B
and

\lambda_{\min}(G)
(and
\lambda_{\max}(G)
, which is itself bounded
in terms of
B
and
J
). Note that

\mathrm{tr}(G\hat{G}_{m}^{-1})\leq\lambda_{\max}(G)\,\mathrm{tr}(\hat{G}_{m}^{-1})\leq\lambda_{\max}(G)\,(J+1)\,\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})^{-1}.

By Assumption
7.3
(b), the vectors
F(X_{s})
are
almost surely bounded, so the matrices
F(X_{s})F(X_{s})^{\top}
have
spectral norm at most
B^{2}
. Standard matrix Bernstein/Hoeffding
inequalities for sample covariance matrices of bounded random vectors
(see, e.g., Vershynin,
High-Dimensional Probability
, Theorem 5.39)
imply that, for any
t\in(0,\lambda_{\min}(G))
,

\mathbb{P}\big(\|\hat{G}_{m}-G\|_{\mathrm{op}}\geq t\big)\leq C_{1}\exp\Big(-C_{2}\,m\,\frac{t^{2}}{B^{4}}\Big),

for constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
depending only on
J
.
In particular, taking
t=\lambda_{\min}(G)/2
gives

\mathbb{P}\Big(\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})\leq\frac{\lambda_{\min}(G)}{2}\Big)\leq C_{1}\exp(-cm),

for some
c>0
. On the complementary event we have

\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})\geq\lambda_{\min}(G)/2
, hence

\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})^{-1}\leq 2/\lambda_{\min}(G)
.

A standard integration argument using this exponential tail bound
(see, e.g., Lemma 5.41 in Vershynin,
op. cit.
) yields that
for any fixed
q<\infty
there is a finite constant
K_{q}
, depending only
on
J
,
B
and
\lambda_{\min}(G)
, such that

\sup_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\big[\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})^{-q}\big]\leq K_{q}.

In particular,

\sup_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\big[\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})^{-1}\big]\leq K_{1}<\infty.

Combining the displays, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathrm{tr}(G\hat{G}_{m}^{-1})\big]\leq\lambda_{\max}(G)\,(J+1)\,\mathbb{E}\big[\lambda_{\min}(\hat{G}_{m})^{-1}\big]\leq\lambda_{\max}(G)\,(J+1)\,K_{1}=:C_{0},

with
C_{0}<\infty
depending only on
J
,
B
and
\lambda_{\min}(G)
.
Therefore

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{C_{0}}{m}.

Finally, enlarging
C_{0}
if necessary to handle finitely many small
values of
m
(for which
\hat{G}_{m}
is still invertible by
Definition
7.2
), we obtain a constant
C<\infty

such that

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{C}{m}\quad\text{for all }m\geq 1,

which proves part (ii).
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.5

Proof.

By Theorem
7.4
(ii) there exists
C_{0}<\infty
,
depending only on
J
,
B
and
\lambda_{\min}(G)
, such that for every

w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}
(and hence in particular for every

w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)
),

\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\bigl[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]\leq\frac{C_{0}}{m}.

Since
d=J+1
, we can rewrite this as

\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\bigl[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]\leq\frac{Cd}{m}

for a possibly larger constant
C<\infty
depending only on
J
,
B

and
\lambda_{\min}(G)
. This yields the desired upper bound.

We use Fano’s method, with a packing centred at the constant
1/2

graphon and a sample–size dependent step size
\delta_{m}
.

Step 1: Packing around
w\equiv 1/2
.

Let
d=J+1
and let
e_{0},\dots,e_{J}
denote the standard basis of

\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Set

\beta^{(0)}:=\tfrac{1}{2}e_{0}=(1/2,0,\dots,0)^{\top},\qquad w^{(0)}:=w_{\beta^{(0)}}\equiv\tfrac{1}{2}.

For each
m\geq 1
define

\delta_{m}^{2}:=\frac{c_{0}}{m},

where
c_{0}>0
is a constant (to be specified shortly) that depends only
on
J
and
B
.
For any
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{J})\in\{0,1\}^{J}
set

\beta_{m}^{(v)}:=\beta^{(0)}+\delta_{m}\sum_{k=1}^{J}v_{k}e_{k},\qquad w_{m}^{(v)}:=w_{\beta_{m}^{(v)}}.

By definition of
F
,

w_{m}^{(v)}(x,y)=\beta_{m}^{(v)\top}F(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}+\delta_{m}\sum_{k=1}^{J}v_{k}w_{k}(x,y).

By Assumption
7.3
(b) we have
\|F(X)\|_{2}\leq B
almost
surely, so each component of
F
is bounded in absolute value by
B
,
and hence

|w_{k}(x,y)|\leq B\quad\text{for all }(x,y)\in[0,1]^{2},\;k=1,\dots,J

(up to a null set, which is irrelevant for
L^{2}
considerations).

Thus, for every
(x,y)
and every
v
,

\bigl|w_{m}^{(v)}(x,y)-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigr|\leq\delta_{m}\sum_{k=1}^{J}v_{k}|w_{k}(x,y)|\leq\delta_{m}JB.

The largest value of
\delta_{m}
occurs at
m=1
, so

\delta_{m}JB\leq\delta_{1}JB=\sqrt{c_{0}}\,JB\qquad\text{for all }m\geq 1.

Similarly,

\|\beta_{m}^{(v)}\|_{2}\leq\|\beta^{(0)}\|_{2}+\delta_{m}\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{J}v_{k}^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{2}+\sqrt{c_{0}}\sqrt{J}.

Now choose

c_{1}:=\frac{1}{16J^{2}B^{2}},\qquad c_{2}:=\frac{\log 2}{256B^{2}},\qquad c_{3}:=\frac{1}{4J},

and set

c_{0}:=\min\{c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}\}.

Then:

1.

From
c_{0}\leq c_{1}
we get

\sqrt{c_{0}}\,JB\leq 1/4
, hence

\frac{1}{4}\leq w_{m}^{(v)}(x,y)\leq\frac{3}{4}\quad\text{for all }m,v,(x,y).

So every
w_{m}^{(v)}
is a valid graphon with edge probabilities
uniformly bounded away from
0
and
1
.

2.

From
c_{0}\leq c_{3}
we get

\sqrt{c_{0}}\sqrt{J}\leq 1/2
, hence

\|\beta_{m}^{(v)}\|_{2}\leq 1
for all
m,v
.
Since we assume
R\geq 1
, this implies

\beta_{m}^{(v)}\in\mathcal{B}(R)
and

w_{m}^{(v)}\in\mathcal{H}(R)
for all
m,v
.

By the Varshamov–Gilbert bound, there exists a subset

\mathcal{V}\subset\{0,1\}^{J}
with cardinality

M:=|\mathcal{V}|\geq 2^{J/8},

such that for any distinct
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
the Hamming distance
satisfies

\mathrm{Ham}(v,v^{\prime})\geq J/8.

Step 2: Separation in
L^{2}
norm.

For
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{V}
,

\beta_{m}^{(v)}-\beta_{m}^{(v^{\prime})}=\delta_{m}\sum_{k=1}^{J}(v_{k}-v_{k}^{\prime})e_{k},

so

\|\beta_{m}^{(v)}-\beta_{m}^{(v^{\prime})}\|_{2}^{2}=\delta_{m}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{J}(v_{k}-v_{k}^{\prime})^{2}=\delta_{m}^{2}\,\mathrm{Ham}(v,v^{\prime})\geq\delta_{m}^{2}\,\frac{J}{8}.

Since

\|w_{m}^{(v)}-w_{m}^{(v^{\prime})}\|_{2}^{2}=(\beta_{m}^{(v)}-\beta_{m}^{(v^{\prime})})^{\top}G(\beta_{m}^{(v)}-\beta_{m}^{(v^{\prime})}),

Assumption
7.3
(a) gives

\|w_{m}^{(v)}-w_{m}^{(v^{\prime})}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\lambda_{\min}(G)\,\|\beta_{m}^{(v)}-\beta_{m}^{(v^{\prime})}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\lambda_{\min}(G)\,\frac{J}{8}\,\delta_{m}^{2}.

Define

\alpha_{m}^{2}:=\frac{\lambda_{\min}(G)}{8}\,J\,\delta_{m}^{2}=\frac{\lambda_{\min}(G)}{8}\,J\,\frac{c_{0}}{m}.

Then the
M
graphons
\{w_{m}^{(v)}:v\in\mathcal{V}\}
form an

L^{2}
–packing of radius at least
\alpha_{m}
.

For each
v\in\mathcal{V}
let
\mathbb{P}_{v}
denote the joint law of

(X_{s},Y_{s})_{s=1}^{m}
under the graphon
w_{\star}=w_{m}^{(v)}
.

Because the samples are i.i.d.,

\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{v}\|\mathbb{P}_{0})=m\,\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\mathcal{L}(X,Y\mid w_{m}^{(v)})\,\|\,\mathcal{L}(X,Y\mid w_{m}^{(0)})\bigr).

Conditioning on
X
, under
w_{m}^{(v)}
we have

Y\mid X\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(w_{m}^{(v)}(X))
and under
w_{m}^{(0)}
,

Y\mid X\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)
.

Thus

\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{v}\|\mathbb{P}_{0})=m\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\mathrm{Bern}(w_{m}^{(v)}(X))\,\|\,\mathrm{Bern}(\tfrac{1}{2})\bigr)\Big].

For Bernoulli distributions it is standard that for any
p\in[0,1]
,

\mathrm{KL}\bigl(\mathrm{Bern}(p)\,\|\,\mathrm{Bern}(\tfrac{1}{2})\bigr)\leq 4\,(p-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}.

Applying this with
p=w_{m}^{(v)}(X)
yields

\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{v}\|\mathbb{P}_{0})\leq 4m\,\mathbb{E}\big[(w_{m}^{(v)}(X)-\tfrac{1}{2})^{2}\big]=4m\,\|w_{m}^{(v)}-w_{m}^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}.

Using again Assumption
7.3
,

\lambda_{\max}(G)\leq\operatorname{tr}(G)=\mathbb{E}[\|F(X)\|_{2}^{2}]\leq B^{2},

so

\|w_{m}^{(v)}-w_{m}^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(G)\,\|\beta_{m}^{(v)}-\beta^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}\leq B^{2}\,\|\beta_{m}^{(v)}-\beta^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}.

Moreover,

\|\beta_{m}^{(v)}-\beta^{(0)}\|_{2}^{2}=\delta_{m}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{J}v_{k}^{2}\leq\delta_{m}^{2}J=\frac{c_{0}J}{m}.

Combining the last three displays,

\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{v}\|\mathbb{P}_{0})\leq 4mB^{2}\frac{c_{0}J}{m}=4B^{2}Jc_{0},

independently of
m
and
v
.

By our choice
c_{0}\leq c_{2}=\log 2/(256B^{2})
and the bound

M\geq 2^{J/8}
(Varshamov–Gilbert), we have

\frac{1}{M}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{v}\|\mathbb{P}_{0})\leq 4B^{2}Jc_{0}\leq\frac{1}{8}\log M.

Step 4: Fano’s inequality and conclusion.

Let
V
be uniform on
\mathcal{V}
and let
\widehat{V}
be any estimator
of
V
based on the data. Fano’s inequality (e.g. Tsybakov, 2009,
Thm. 2.5) gives

\mathbb{P}(\widehat{V}\neq V)\geq 1-\frac{\frac{1}{M}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{v}\|\mathbb{P}_{0})+\log 2}{\log M}.

Since
\frac{1}{M}\sum_{v}\mathrm{KL}(\mathbb{P}_{v}\|\mathbb{P}_{0})\leq\frac{1}{8}\log M
and
\log M\to\infty
as
J
grows, there exists a
numerical constant
p_{0}\in(0,1)
(for instance
p_{0}=1/4
for all
sufficiently large
J
, and a slightly smaller
p_{0}
for the finitely
many small
J
) such that, after possibly decreasing
c_{0}
further,

\mathbb{P}(\widehat{V}\neq V)\;\geq\;p_{0}

for all
m
sufficiently large. (Any further decrease of
c_{0}
only
shrinks the packing radius by a fixed constant factor and does not
affect the
1/m
rate.)

For any estimator
\tilde{w}_{m}
taking values in

\{w_{m}^{(v)}:v\in\mathcal{V}\}
we have

\tilde{w}_{m}=w_{m}^{(\widehat{V})}
for some estimator
\widehat{V}
, and
whenever
\widehat{V}\neq V
we have

\|w_{m}^{(\widehat{V})}-w_{m}^{(V)}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\alpha_{m}^{2}
by Step 2. Hence

\mathbb{E}\big[\|\tilde{w}_{m}-w_{m}^{(V)}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\geq\alpha_{m}^{2}\,\mathbb{P}(\widehat{V}\neq V)\geq p_{0}\,\alpha_{m}^{2}=p_{0}\,\frac{\lambda_{\min}(G)}{8}J\frac{c_{0}}{m},

where the expectation is over the randomness of
V
and the data.

Since

\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\tilde{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\;\geq\;\frac{1}{M}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{V}}\mathbb{E}_{w_{m}^{(v)}}\big[\|\tilde{w}_{m}-w_{m}^{(v)}\|_{2}^{2}\big],

we conclude that for every estimator
\tilde{w}_{m}
,

\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\tilde{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\geq p_{0}\,\frac{\lambda_{\min}(G)}{8}J\frac{c_{0}}{m}=:\frac{c^{\prime}J}{m}.

Recalling that
d=J+1
and
J\asymp d
, we may rewrite this as

\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\tilde{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\geq\frac{cd}{m}

for some
c>0
depending only on
J
,
B
and
\lambda_{\min}(G)
.
Taking the infimum over
\tilde{w}_{m}
yields

\mathcal{R}_{m}^{\star}\geq cd/m
, which proves part (ii).
∎

Remark A.5

(Full remark for Theorem
7.5
)
.

Theorem
7.5
shows that, within the convex hull (and linear
span) of the agent graphons
\mathcal{H}
(viewed as a
d
–dimensional
linear space), the LS graphon BPS estimator is optimally efficient in

L^{2}
risk up to a constant factor.
In particular, there is no other estimator that can uniformly improve
the order
d/m
of the worst–case
L^{2}
risk over
\mathcal{H}(R)
.

Proof of Theorem
7.7

Proof.

Write
F_{s}:=F(X_{s})
and
Y_{s}
for the responses. As in
Definition
7.2
, let

\hat{G}_{m}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F_{s}F_{s}^{\top},\qquad\hat{h}_{m}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F_{s}Y_{s},

so that
\hat{\beta}_{m}=\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\hat{h}_{m}
.

By definition of
w^{\circ}
,

w^{\circ}(x,y)=\beta^{\circ\top}F(x,y),\qquad\beta^{\circ}=G^{-1}\,h_{\star},\quad h_{\star}=\mathbb{E}[F(X)w_{\star}(X)].

Define the residuals relative to
w^{\circ}
by

r_{s}:=Y_{s}-w^{\circ}(X_{s})=Y_{s}-\beta^{\circ\top}F_{s},\qquad Z_{s}:=F_{s}r_{s},

and set

\Delta_{m}:=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}Z_{s}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F_{s}\bigl(Y_{s}-\beta^{\circ\top}F_{s}\bigr).

Then

\hat{h}_{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F_{s}Y_{s}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}F_{s}\beta^{\circ\top}F_{s}+\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}Z_{s}=\hat{G}_{m}\beta^{\circ}+\Delta_{m},

and hence

\hat{\beta}_{m}=\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\hat{h}_{m}=\beta^{\circ}+\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\Delta_{m},\qquad\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\circ}=\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\Delta_{m}.

We now bound
\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2}
.
First, by Assumption
7.3
(b),

\|F_{s}\|_{2}\leq B
almost surely. Moreover,

\|h_{\star}\|_{2}=\|\mathbb{E}[F(X)w_{\star}(X)]\|_{2}\leq\mathbb{E}[\|F(X)\||w_{\star}(X)|]\leq B\,\|w_{\star}\|_{2}\leq B,

since
0\leq w_{\star}\leq 1
implies
\|w_{\star}\|_{2}\leq 1
.
Thus

\|\beta^{\circ}\|_{2}\leq\|G^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\|h_{\star}\|_{2}\leq\lambda_{\min}(G)^{-1}B,

and for all
(x,y)
,

|w^{\circ}(x,y)|=|\beta^{\circ\top}F(x,y)|\leq\|\beta^{\circ}\|_{2}\,\|F(x,y)\|_{2}\leq\lambda_{\min}(G)^{-1}B^{2}.

Since
Y_{s}\in[0,1]
, this yields the uniform bound

|r_{s}|=|Y_{s}-w^{\circ}(X_{s})|\leq 1+\lambda_{\min}(G)^{-1}B^{2}=:L<\infty.

Consequently
\|Z_{s}\|_{2}\leq BL
and

\|Z_{s}\|_{2}^{2}\leq B^{2}L^{2}
almost surely.

By independence of the samples,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\Delta_{m}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]=\mathbb{E}\Bigl\|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{s=1}^{m}Z_{s}\Bigr\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{s=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}\|Z_{s}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{B^{2}L^{2}}{m}.

Next, by the inverse–Gram bound (Lemma
A.4
), there
exists
K<\infty
, depending only on
J
,
B
and
\lambda_{\min}(G)
,
such that

\sup_{m\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\bigr]\leq K.

Combining this with the previous display yields

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\Delta_{m}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\hat{G}_{m}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\|\Delta_{m}\|_{2}^{2}\bigr]\leq\frac{C^{\prime}}{m},

for some
C^{\prime}<\infty
depending only on
J
,
B
and

\lambda_{\min}(G)
.

Finally,

\|\hat{w}_{m}-w^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\bigl[(\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\circ})^{\top}F(X)F(X)^{\top}(\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\circ})\bigr]=(\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\circ})^{\top}G(\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\circ}),

where the expectation is over an independent copy of
X
.
By the eigenvalue bound for positive definite matrices,

\|\hat{w}_{m}-w^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(G)\,\|\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2},

so

\mathbb{E}\|\hat{w}_{m}-w^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\lambda_{\max}(G)\,\mathbb{E}\|\hat{\beta}_{m}-\beta^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\frac{Cd}{m},

after adjusting constants and recalling that
d=J+1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.8

Proof.

Fix any
\beta^{\circ}\in\mathcal{B}(R)
and write
w^{\circ}=w_{\beta^{\circ}}
.
By the inequality
\|a+b\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|a\|_{2}^{2}+2\|b\|_{2}^{2}
applied with

a=\hat{w}_{m}-w^{\circ}
and
b=w^{\circ}-w_{\star}
, we have

\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\leq 2\|\hat{w}_{m}-w^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2}+2\|w^{\circ}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}.

Taking expectations with respect to the joint law induced by
w_{\star}

gives

\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq 2\,\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2}\big]+2\|w^{\circ}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}.

Now choose
\beta^{\circ}
to be the
L^{2}
projection of
w_{\star}
onto

\mathcal{H}(R)
, so that
w^{\circ}
is the closest element of

\mathcal{H}(R)
to
w_{\star}
in
L^{2}
. By
Theorem
7.7
, applied with this
w_{\star}
and

w^{\circ}
, there exists
C_{0}<\infty
depending only on

J,B,\lambda_{\min}(G)
such that

\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w^{\circ}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{C_{0}d}{m}\qquad\text{for all }m.

Hence

\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{2C_{0}d}{m}+2\|w^{\circ}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}.

By definition of
w^{\circ}
as the
L^{2}
projection,

\|w^{\circ}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}=\inf_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}(R)}\|w_{\star}-w_{\beta}\|_{2}^{2},

so

\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq 2\inf_{\beta\in\mathcal{B}(R)}\|w_{\star}-w_{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{2C_{0}d}{m}.

Renaming
2C_{0}
as
C
yields the claimed oracle inequality.
∎

Remark A.6

(Full remark for Theorem
7.8
)
.

Theorem
7.8
exhibits LS graphon–BPS as an efficient
aggregator: it pays the optimal
d/m
variance cost for estimating the
best combination
\beta^{\circ}
and then automatically adapts to the
unknown bias
\inf_{\beta}\|w_{\star}-w_{\beta}\|_{2}^{2}
.
If the convex hull of the agents approximates the true graphon well,
LS graphon–BPS inherits this approximation advantage without knowing

a priori
which combination of agents is best.

Proof of Theorem
7.10

Proof.

Because
J\geq 2
and the
w_{j}
are distinct, the finite set

\{w_{1},\dots,w_{J}\}
cannot be dense in the convex hull

\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{conv}}
.
Thus there exists
w_{\star}\in\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{conv}}
such that

\delta:=\min_{1\leq j\leq J}\|w_{\star}-w_{j}\|_{2}>0.

Let this
w_{\star}
be fixed and consider any model selection estimator

\hat{w}_{m}^{\mathrm{sel}}=w_{\hat{J}_{m}}
.
Under
w_{\star}
, the random index
\hat{J}_{m}
takes values in

\{1,\dots,J\}
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}^{\mathrm{sel}}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|w_{\hat{J}_{m}}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\Pr_{w_{\star}}(\hat{J}_{m}=j)\,\|w_{j}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{1\leq j\leq J}\|w_{j}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}=\delta^{2}.

This bound holds for each
m
, so

\sup_{m}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\|\hat{w}_{m}^{\mathrm{sel}}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\geq\delta^{2}
,
proving (i).

For (ii), note that by definition
w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)
for some
finite radius
R
(since it is a convex combination of

\{w_{j}\}
and hence lies in their span).
Thus Theorem
7.5
(i) applies at
w_{\star}
and yields

\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\frac{Cd}{m}

for all
m
.
This tends to
0
as
m\to\infty
, establishing consistency and
minimax–rate optimality at
w_{\star}
.
∎

Remark A.7

(Full remark for Theorem
7.10
)
.

Theorem
7.10
provides a formal
“combination beats all components” phenomenon:
for some
w_{\star}
in the convex hull of the agents, every estimator
that selects a single agent graphon—even in a data–dependent way—has a
nonvanishing
L^{2}
error, while LS graphon–BPS is essentially optimal.
In particular, this rules out the possibility that clever
data–dependent model selection among the agents can replace graphon–BPS
aggregation when the true graphon genuinely mixes distinct structures
(e.g. community structure and hubs).

Proof of Corollary
7.11

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{R}_{m}^{\star}:=\inf_{\tilde{w}_{m}}\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\tilde{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]

be the global minimax risk over all (measurable) estimators. Since the
infimum on the left is taken over a larger class of estimators than

\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{H}}
, we have

\mathcal{R}_{m}^{\star}\leq\inf_{\tilde{w}_{m}\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{H}}}\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\tilde{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big].

On the other hand, because
\hat{w}_{m}\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{H}}
,

\inf_{\tilde{w}_{m}\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{H}}}\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\tilde{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\leq\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big].

By Theorem
7.5
, there exist constants
0<c\leq C<\infty

such that, for all sufficiently large
m
,

c\,\frac{d}{m}\;\leq\;\mathcal{R}_{m}^{\star}\quad\text{and}\quad\sup_{w_{\star}\in\mathcal{H}(R)}\mathbb{E}_{w_{\star}}\big[\|\hat{w}_{m}-w_{\star}\|_{2}^{2}\big]\;\leq\;C\,\frac{d}{m}.

Combining these inequalities and relabelling the constants as

c_{1}=c
and
c_{2}=C
(up to harmless multiplicative adjustments)
yields the claimed bounds.
∎

Proof of Theorem
8.1

Proof.

(a) For the edge density,

|e(w)-e(w^{\prime})|=\left|\int_{[0,1]^{2}}(w-w^{\prime})\right|\leq\int_{[0,1]^{2}}|w-w^{\prime}|\leq\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2},

by Cauchy–Schwarz on
[0,1]^{2}
.

For the degree functions,

d_{w}(x)-d_{w^{\prime}}(x)=\int_{0}^{1}(w(x,y)-w^{\prime}(x,y))\,dy.

By Jensen’s inequality,

\bigl(d_{w}(x)-d_{w^{\prime}}(x)\bigr)^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{1}(w(x,y)-w^{\prime}(x,y))^{2}\,dy.

Integrating in
x
gives

\|d_{w}-d_{w^{\prime}}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}(w-w^{\prime})^{2}(x,y)\,dy\,dx=\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2}^{2}.

(b) For triangles, let
\Delta=w-w^{\prime}
. Using the algebraic identity

a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}-b_{1}b_{2}b_{3}=(a_{1}-b_{1})a_{2}a_{3}+b_{1}(a_{2}-b_{2})a_{3}+b_{1}b_{2}(a_{3}-b_{3}),

with

a_{1}=w(x,y),\ a_{2}=w(y,z),\ a_{3}=w(x,z),\quad b_{1}=w^{\prime}(x,y),\ b_{2}=w^{\prime}(y,z),\ b_{3}=w^{\prime}(x,z),

we obtain

\displaystyle t(w)-t(w^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{3}}\Bigl((w-w^{\prime})(x,y)w(y,z)w(x,z)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+w^{\prime}(x,y)(w-w^{\prime})(y,z)w(x,z)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+w^{\prime}(x,y)w^{\prime}(y,z)(w-w^{\prime})(x,z)\Bigr)\,dx\,dy\,dz.

Since
0\leq w,w^{\prime}\leq 1
, each multiplicative factor other than
\Delta
has absolute
value at most
1
, so

\displaystyle|t(w)-t(w^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{[0,1]^{3}}|\Delta(x,y)|\,dx\,dy\,dz+\int_{[0,1]^{3}}|\Delta(y,z)|\,dx\,dy\,dz

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{[0,1]^{3}}|\Delta(x,z)|\,dx\,dy\,dz.

Integrating out the extra coordinate in each term shows that each triple integral
equals
\|\Delta\|_{1}
, and
\|\Delta\|_{1}\leq\|\Delta\|_{2}
on
[0,1]^{2}
. Hence

|t(w)-t(w^{\prime})|\leq 3\|\Delta\|_{2}=3\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2}.

For wedges,

s(w)=\int_{0}^{1}d_{w}(x)^{2}\,dx,\qquad s(w^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{1}d_{w^{\prime}}(x)^{2}\,dx,

so

s(w)-s(w^{\prime})=\int_{0}^{1}(d_{w}-d_{w^{\prime}})(d_{w}+d_{w^{\prime}})(x)\,dx.

Because
0\leq w,w^{\prime}\leq 1
, we have
0\leq d_{w},d_{w^{\prime}}\leq 1
and thus

|d_{w}(x)+d_{w^{\prime}}(x)|\leq 2
for all
x
. Therefore

|s(w)-s(w^{\prime})|\leq 2\int_{0}^{1}|d_{w}-d_{w^{\prime}}|(x)\,dx\leq 2\|d_{w}-d_{w^{\prime}}\|_{2}\leq 2\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2},

using part (a).

(c) Assume
s(w),s(w^{\prime})\geq s_{0}>0
. Then

C(w)-C(w^{\prime})=\frac{t(w)-t(w^{\prime})}{s(w)}+t(w^{\prime})\left(\frac{1}{s(w)}-\frac{1}{s(w^{\prime})}\right),

whenever
s(w),s(w^{\prime})>0
. Since
0\leq t(\cdot)\leq 1
,

\left|\frac{t(w)-t(w^{\prime})}{s(w)}\right|\leq\frac{|t(w)-t(w^{\prime})|}{s_{0}}\leq\frac{3}{s_{0}}\,\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2},

by part (b). Moreover,

\left|\frac{1}{s(w)}-\frac{1}{s(w^{\prime})}\right|=\frac{|s(w)-s(w^{\prime})|}{s(w)s(w^{\prime})}\leq\frac{|s(w)-s(w^{\prime})|}{s_{0}^{2}}\leq\frac{2}{s_{0}^{2}}\,\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2},

again by part (b). Combining these bounds gives

|C(w)-C(w^{\prime})|\leq\frac{3}{s_{0}}\,\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2}+\frac{2}{s_{0}^{2}}\,\|w-w^{\prime}\|_{2},

as claimed.
∎

Proof of Theorem
8.4

Proof.

Throughout, we work on a single probability space on which the i.i.d. latent
variables
(U_{i})_{i\geq 1}
are fixed once and for all; all graphs
G_{n}(w)
are
induced subgraphs on the first
n
vertices of the infinite array

(A^{(w)}_{ij})_{i<j}
.

We repeatedly use the following general pattern: for a given statistic
Z_{n}(w)

built from the adjacency variables,

Z_{n}(w)=\widetilde{Z}_{n}(w)+R_{n}(w),

where

•

\widetilde{Z}_{n}(w)
is a (symmetrised) U–statistic in the latent
variables
(U_{i})
only, whose kernel is the corresponding graphon expression
(e.g.
w
,
w\cdot w\cdot w
); and

•

R_{n}(w)
is an average of bounded, conditionally mean zero Bernoulli
noises.

The U–statistic part converges almost surely to the graphon functional by the
strong law for U–statistics; the noise part is shown to go to zero almost
surely by a simple second–moment/Borel–Cantelli argument.

(i) LLNs.

Edge density and average degree.

For a fixed graphon
w
define the empirical edge density

\widehat{e}_{n}(w):=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}A^{(w)}_{ij}=\frac{\overline{D}_{n}(w)}{n-1}.

Decompose

A^{(w)}_{ij}=w(U_{i},U_{j})+\xi^{(w)}_{ij},\qquad\xi^{(w)}_{ij}:=A^{(w)}_{ij}-w(U_{i},U_{j}).

Then

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\xi^{(w)}_{ij}\mid(U_{k})\bigr]=0,\qquad|\xi^{(w)}_{ij}|\leq 1,

and the
\xi^{(w)}_{ij}
are conditionally independent given
(U_{k})
.
Hence

\widehat{e}_{n}(w)=\widetilde{e}_{n}(w)+R^{(e)}_{n}(w),

where

\widetilde{e}_{n}(w):=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}w(U_{i},U_{j}),\qquad R^{(e)}_{n}(w):=\frac{2}{n(n-1)}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\xi^{(w)}_{ij}.

Step 1: U–statistic part.

\widetilde{e}_{n}(w)
is a symmetric U–statistic of order
2
with kernel

h(u,v)=w(u,v)
.
By the strong law of large numbers for U–statistics,

\widetilde{e}_{n}(w)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mathbb{E}[h(U_{1},U_{2})]=\mathbb{E}[w(U_{1},U_{2})]=e(w).

Step 2: noise part.

Conditionally on
(U_{k})
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[R^{(e)}_{n}(w)\mid(U_{k})\bigr]=0,\qquad\mathrm{Var}\bigl(R^{(e)}_{n}(w)\mid(U_{k})\bigr)=\frac{4}{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}\mathrm{Var}(\xi^{(w)}_{ij}\mid(U_{k})).

Since
\mathrm{Var}(\xi^{(w)}_{ij}\mid(U_{k}))\leq 1
,

\mathrm{Var}\bigl(R^{(e)}_{n}(w)\mid(U_{k})\bigr)\leq\frac{4}{n^{2}(n-1)^{2}}{n\choose 2}\leq\frac{C}{n^{2}}

for some universal constant
C
.
Thus

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(R^{(e)}_{n}(w))^{2}\bigr]\leq\frac{C}{n^{2}}.

By Chebyshev and Borel–Cantelli,

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\Pr\bigl(|R^{(e)}_{n}(w)|>\varepsilon\bigr)\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{C}{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}<\infty

for each fixed
\varepsilon>0
, so
R^{(e)}_{n}(w)\to 0
almost surely.

Combining the U–statistic and noise parts yields

\widehat{e}_{n}(w)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}e(w),

that is,

\overline{D}_{n}(w)/(n-1)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}e(w)
.

Triangle density.

Let

T_{n}(w):={n\choose 3}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq n}A^{(w)}_{ij}A^{(w)}_{jk}A^{(w)}_{ik}.

For
x,y,z\in[0,1]
define the kernel

h_{\triangle}(x,y,z)=w(x,y)w(y,z)w(x,z),

and write

A^{(w)}_{ij}A^{(w)}_{jk}A^{(w)}_{ik}=h_{\triangle}(U_{i},U_{j},U_{k})+\eta^{(w)}_{ijk},

where

\eta^{(w)}_{ijk}:=A^{(w)}_{ij}A^{(w)}_{jk}A^{(w)}_{ik}-h_{\triangle}(U_{i},U_{j},U_{k}).

Then

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\eta^{(w)}_{ijk}\mid(U_{\ell})\bigr]=0,\qquad|\eta^{(w)}_{ijk}|\leq 1,

and, given
(U_{\ell})
, the variables
\eta^{(w)}_{ijk}
are independent across
triples
(i,j,k)
.

Decompose

T_{n}(w)=\widetilde{T}_{n}(w)+R^{(\triangle)}_{n}(w),

where

\widetilde{T}_{n}(w):={n\choose 3}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq n}h_{\triangle}(U_{i},U_{j},U_{k}),\qquad R^{(\triangle)}_{n}(w):={n\choose 3}^{-1}\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq n}\eta^{(w)}_{ijk}.

U–statistic part.

\widetilde{T}_{n}(w)
is a symmetric U–statistic of order
3
with kernel

h_{\triangle}
.
Hence, by the strong law for U–statistics,

\widetilde{T}_{n}(w)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\mathbb{E}[h_{\triangle}(U_{1},U_{2},U_{3})]=t(w).

Noise part.

Conditionally on
(U_{\ell})
,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[R^{(\triangle)}_{n}(w)\mid(U_{\ell})\bigr]=0,\qquad\mathrm{Var}\bigl(R^{(\triangle)}_{n}(w)\mid(U_{\ell})\bigr)={n\choose 3}^{-2}\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq n}\mathrm{Var}(\eta^{(w)}_{ijk}\mid(U_{\ell}))\leq{n\choose 3}^{-1}.

Thus

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(R^{(\triangle)}_{n}(w))^{2}\bigr]\leq{n\choose 3}^{-1}.

Since
\sum_{n}{n\choose 3}^{-1}<\infty
, Borel–Cantelli gives

R^{(\triangle)}_{n}(w)\to 0
almost surely.
Therefore
T_{n}(w)\to t(w)
almost surely.

Wedge density.

Recall that for a graph
G_{n}(w)
,

S_{n}(w)=\frac{1}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}D^{(w)}_{i}\bigl(D^{(w)}_{i}-1\bigr)

counts connected triples (wedges) per ordered triple of distinct vertices.

For a triple
1\leq i<j<k\leq n
define

W^{(w)}_{ijk}:=A^{(w)}_{ij}A^{(w)}_{ik}+A^{(w)}_{ji}A^{(w)}_{jk}+A^{(w)}_{ki}A^{(w)}_{kj}.

In an undirected graph
A^{(w)}_{ij}=A^{(w)}_{ji}
, so

W^{(w)}_{ijk}
is the number of wedges whose three endpoints are

\{i,j,k\}
(one for each possible centre).
Then

S_{n}(w)=\frac{1}{n(n-1)(n-2)}\sum_{i\neq j,k}A^{(w)}_{ij}A^{(w)}_{ik}=\frac{1}{3{n\choose 3}}\sum_{1\leq i<j<k\leq n}W^{(w)}_{ijk}.

Define the symmetric kernel

h_{\wedge}(x,y,z):=w(x,y)w(x,z)+w(y,x)w(y,z)+w(z,x)w(z,y),

and decompose

W^{(w)}_{ijk}=h_{\wedge}(U_{i},U_{j},U_{k})+\zeta^{(w)}_{ijk},

where

\zeta^{(w)}_{ijk}:=W^{(w)}_{ijk}-h_{\wedge}(U_{i},U_{j},U_{k}).

Again,

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\zeta^{(w)}_{ijk}\mid(U_{\ell})\bigr]=0,\qquad|\zeta^{(w)}_{ijk}|\leq 3,

and, conditional on
(U_{\ell})
, the
\zeta^{(w)}_{ijk}
are independent across
triples.

Thus

S_{n}(w)=\widetilde{S}_{n}(w)+R^{(\wedge)}_{n}(w),

where

\widetilde{S}_{n}(w):=\frac{1}{3{n\choose 3}}\sum_{i<j<k}h_{\wedge}(U_{i},U_{j},U_{k}),\quad R^{(\wedge)}_{n}(w):=\frac{1}{3{n\choose 3}}\sum_{i<j<k}\zeta^{(w)}_{ijk}.

U–statistic part.

\widetilde{S}_{n}(w)
is a (scaled) order‑3 U–statistic with kernel

h_{\wedge}/3
, so by the strong law for U–statistics,

\widetilde{S}_{n}(w)\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\frac{1}{3}\,\mathbb{E}\bigl[h_{\wedge}(U_{1},U_{2},U_{3})\bigr].

A direct calculation shows

\mathbb{E}\bigl[h^{\wedge}(U_{1},U_{2},U_{3})\bigr]=3\int_{0}^{1}d_{w}(x)^{2}\,dx=3s(w),

so
S_{n}(w)\to s(w)
almost surely.

Noise part.

Conditionally on
(U_{\ell})
,

\mathrm{Var}\bigl(R^{(\wedge)}_{n}(w)\mid(U_{\ell})\bigr)=\frac{1}{9{n\choose 3}^{2}}\sum_{i<j<k}\mathrm{Var}(\zeta^{(w)}_{ijk}\mid(U_{\ell}))\leq\frac{C}{{n\choose 3}}

for some absolute constant
C
(since
|\zeta^{(w)}_{ijk}|\leq 3
).
Hence

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(R^{(\wedge)}_{n}(w))^{2}\bigr]\leq\frac{C}{{n\choose 3}}.

Again
\sum_{n}{n\choose 3}^{-1}<\infty
, so Borel–Cantelli implies

R^{(\wedge)}_{n}(w)\to 0
almost surely.
Therefore
S_{n}(w)\to s(w)
almost surely.

Clustering.

If
s(w)>0
, then we have shown that

S_{n}(w)\to s(w)
almost surely and
T_{n}(w)\to t(w)
almost surely.
Thus
S_{n}(w)
is eventually bounded away from
0
almost surely, and

C_{n}(w)=\frac{T_{n}(w)}{S_{n}(w)}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\frac{t(w)}{s(w)}=C(w),

by Slutsky’s theorem and continuity of
(a,b)\mapsto a/b
on
\{b>0\}
.

This completes the proof of (i).

(ii) Degree distribution limit.

Fix a graphon
w
and a vertex index
i\geq 1
.
Write

D^{(w)}_{i}=\sum_{j\neq i}A^{(w)}_{ij}=\sum_{j\neq i}w(U_{i},U_{j})+\sum_{j\neq i}\bigl(A^{(w)}_{ij}-w(U_{i},U_{j})\bigr)=:S^{(w)}_{i,n}+R^{(d)}_{i,n}.

Deterministic part.

Given
U_{i}
, the variables
(U_{j})_{j\neq i}
are i.i.d.
\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
,
so by the usual law of large numbers,

\frac{S^{(w)}_{i,n}}{n-1}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}w(U_{i},U_{j})\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}d_{w}(U_{i}).

Noise part.

As before, the summands
\xi^{(w)}_{ij}:=A^{(w)}_{ij}-w(U_{i},U_{j})
are
conditionally mean zero, bounded by
1
in absolute value, and independent
over
j\neq i
given
(U_{k})
.
Thus

\frac{R^{(d)}_{i,n}}{n-1}=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\xi^{(w)}_{ij}

has conditional variance at most
1/(n-1)
, so

\mathbb{E}[(R^{(d)}_{i,n}/(n-1))^{2}]\leq 1/(n-1)
.
Chebyshev plus Borel–Cantelli again yields

R^{(d)}_{i,n}/(n-1)\to 0
almost surely.

Combining,

\frac{D^{(w)}_{i}}{n-1}=\frac{S^{(w)}_{i,n}}{n-1}+\frac{R^{(d)}_{i,n}}{n-1}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}d_{w}(U_{i}).

Now let
I_{n}
be uniform on
\{1,\dots,n\}
and independent of everything else.
By exchangeability of the vertices,

\frac{D^{(w)}_{I_{n}}}{n-1}\ \overset{d}{=}\ \frac{D^{(w)}_{1}}{n-1}\ \Longrightarrow\ d_{w}(U_{1}),

where
U_{1}\sim\mathrm{Unif}[0,1]
.
Renaming
U_{1}
as
U
gives the claim.

(iii) Stability under BPS.

Let
\Delta:=\|w^{\star}-w_{\mathrm{BPS}}\|_{2}
.
By part (i),

\frac{\overline{D}_{n}(G_{n}(w))}{n-1}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}e(w)

for each fixed graphon
w
.
Therefore

\displaystyle\Biggl|\frac{\overline{D}_{n}(G_{n}^{\mathrm{BPS}})}{n-1}-\frac{\overline{D}_{n}(G_{n}^{\star})}{n-1}\Biggr|

\displaystyle\leq\Biggl|\frac{\overline{D}_{n}(G_{n}^{\mathrm{BPS}})}{n-1}-e(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})\Biggr|

\displaystyle+|e(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})-e(w^{\star})|+\Biggl|e(w^{\star})-\frac{\overline{D}_{n}(G_{n}^{\star})}{n-1}\Biggr|.

The first and third terms converge to
0
in probability by (i).
By Theorem
8.1
(a),

|e(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})-e(w^{\star})|\leq\|w_{\mathrm{BPS}}-w^{\star}\|_{2}=\Delta,

so the whole expression is
O_{\mathbb{P}}(\Delta)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
, proving
the degree statement.

For clustering, note that by (i) and the assumption
s(w^{\star}),s(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})\geq s_{0}>0
,

C_{n}(G_{n}(w))\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}C(w)\quad\text{for }w\in\{w^{\star},w_{\mathrm{BPS}}\},

and
C(w)
is well defined and finite by
s(w)\geq s_{0}>0
.

By Theorem
8.1
(c),

|C(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})-C(w^{\star})|\leq L_{C}\,\|w_{\mathrm{BPS}}-w^{\star}\|_{2}=O(\Delta),

for an explicit constant
L_{C}
depending only on
s_{0}
.
Hence

\displaystyle|C_{n}(G_{n}^{\mathrm{BPS}})-C_{n}(G_{n}^{\star})|

\displaystyle\leq|C_{n}(G_{n}^{\mathrm{BPS}})-C(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})|

\displaystyle+|C(w_{\mathrm{BPS}})-C(w^{\star})|+|C(w^{\star})-C_{n}(G_{n}^{\star})|.

The first and third terms converge to
0
in probability by (i); the middle
term is
O(\Delta)
deterministically.
Thus

|C_{n}(G_{n}^{\mathrm{BPS}})-C_{n}(G_{n}^{\star})|=O_{\mathbb{P}}(\Delta)+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1).

This completes the proof of (iii) and of the theorem.
∎

Proof of Theorem
8.5

Proof.

Write
w=w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
for brevity, and recall that

\epsilon\leq w\leq 1-\epsilon
almost everywhere.

Step 1: Distances are almost always at most
2
.

Fix a vertex
i
and consider another vertex
j\neq i
.
Condition on the latent variables
(U_{i},U_{j},U_{k})_{k\neq i,j}
.
Given these, the edges are independent and

\Pr\big(A_{ij}=1\mid U_{i}=x,U_{j}=y\big)=w(x,y)\geq\epsilon.

For each
k\notin\{i,j\}
, conditional on
(U_{i},U_{j},U_{k})
,
the edges
A_{ik}
and
A_{kj}
are independent with

\Pr(A_{ik}=1,A_{kj}=1\mid U_{i},U_{j},U_{k})=w(U_{i},U_{k})w(U_{k},U_{j})\geq\epsilon^{2}.

The event
\{\mathrm{dist}_{n}(i,j)>2\}
is contained in the event that
(i)
A_{ij}=0
and (ii) for every
k\notin\{i,j\}
at least one of

A_{ik},A_{kj}
is
0
.
Using conditional independence of edges given the latents, this yields

\Pr\big(\mathrm{dist}_{n}(i,j)>2\mid U_{i},U_{j},U_{k}\,(k\neq i,j)\big)\leq(1-\epsilon)\,\prod_{k\neq i,j}(1-\epsilon^{2})=(1-\epsilon)\,(1-\epsilon^{2})^{n-2}.

Taking expectations over the latents preserves the bound, so

\Pr\big(\mathrm{dist}_{n}(i,j)>2\big)\leq(1-\epsilon)\,(1-\epsilon^{2})^{n-2}.

By a union bound over
j\neq i
,

\Pr\big(\exists j\neq i:\mathrm{dist}_{n}(i,j)>2\big)\leq(n-1)(1-\epsilon)\,(1-\epsilon^{2})^{n-2}.

Since
(1-\epsilon^{2})^{n-2}
decays exponentially in
n
, the right–hand
side tends to
0
as
n\to\infty
. Thus

\Pr\big(\forall j\neq i:\mathrm{dist}_{n}(i,j)\leq 2\big)\to 1.

(8)

Step 2: Closeness centrality.

On the event in (
8
), we have

\mathrm{dist}_{n}(i,j)\in\{1,2\}
for all
j\neq i
, and hence

\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{dist}_{n}(i,j)=D_{i}^{(w)}\cdot 1+(n-1-D_{i}^{(w)})\cdot 2=2(n-1)-D_{i}^{(w)},

where
D_{i}^{(w)}=\sum_{j\neq i}A_{ij}^{(w)}
is the degree of vertex
i
.
Thus, on this event,

\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(i)=\frac{n-1}{2(n-1)-D_{i}^{(w)}}.

We now show that
D_{i}^{(w)}/(n-1)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}d_{w}(U_{i})
.
Condition on
(U_{i},U_{j})_{j\neq i}
.
Then
D_{i}^{(w)}=\sum_{j\neq i}X_{j}
where, given the latents, the

X_{j}=A_{ij}^{(w)}
are independent with

\mathbb{E}[X_{j}\mid U_{i},U_{j}]=w(U_{i},U_{j}),\qquad\mathrm{Var}(X_{j}\mid U_{i},U_{j})\leq\tfrac{1}{4}.

Hence

\frac{1}{n-1}\mathbb{E}[D_{i}^{(w)}\mid(U_{i},U_{j})_{j\neq i}]=\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}w(U_{i},U_{j}).

By the law of large numbers applied to the i.i.d. latents

(U_{j})_{j\neq i}
, this conditional mean converges almost surely to

d_{w}(U_{i})=\int_{0}^{1}w(U_{i},y)\,dy
.

Moreover,

\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{D_{i}^{(w)}}{n-1}\,\Big|\,(U_{i},U_{j})_{j\neq i}\right)=\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathrm{Var}(X_{j}\mid U_{i},U_{j})\leq\frac{1}{4(n-1)}.

By conditional Chebyshev,

\Pr\left(\left|\frac{D_{i}^{(w)}}{n-1}-\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}w(U_{i},U_{j})\right|>\eta\,\Big|\,(U_{i},U_{j})_{j\neq i}\right)\leq\frac{1}{4\eta^{2}(n-1)},

so the unconditional probability tends to
0
.
Thus

\frac{D_{i}^{(w)}}{n-1}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}d_{w}(U_{i}).

Therefore, on (
8
),

\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(i)=\frac{1}{2-\frac{D_{i}^{(w)}}{n-1}}.

Since the probability of (
8
) tends to
1
and

D_{i}^{(w)}/(n-1)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}d_{w}(U_{i})
, the continuous
mapping theorem implies

\mathrm{Cl}_{n}(i)\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}\frac{1}{2-d_{w}(U_{i})}.

Step 3: Betweenness centrality.

Fix
i
and consider an unordered pair
\{j,k\}
with
j,k\neq i
.

Case 1:
A_{jk}=1
.

Then
\sigma_{jk}(i)=0
.

Case 2:
A_{jk}=0
.

Define the number of common neighbours of
j
and
k
by

N_{jk}=\sum_{\ell\notin\{j,k\}}\mathbf{1}\{A_{j\ell}=1,A_{\ell k}=1\}.

Conditionally on
(U_{j},U_{k},U_{\ell})_{\ell\neq j,k}
, the indicators are
independent across
\ell
, with

\mathbb{E}\big[\mathbf{1}\{A_{j\ell}=1,A_{\ell k}=1\}\,\big|\,U_{j},U_{k},U_{\ell}\big]=w(U_{j},U_{\ell})w(U_{\ell},U_{k})\geq\epsilon^{2}.

Thus
\mathbb{E}[N_{jk}]\geq(n-2)\epsilon^{2}
.

By Hoeffding’s inequality, for
\delta=\epsilon^{2}/2
there exists

c>0
such that

\Pr\big(N_{jk}\leq\tfrac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}n\big)\leq\exp(-cn).

A union bound over at most
n^{2}
pairs implies

Thus with probability tending to
1
,

N_{jk}\geq\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{2}n\quad\text{for all distinct }j,k.

(9)

On (
9
), if
A_{jk}=0
then there are at least

(\epsilon^{2}/2)n
shortest paths of length
2
; at most one goes through a
fixed

\sigma_{jk}(i)\leq\frac{2}{\epsilon^{2}n}.

Summing and normalising gives
\mathrm{Bt}_{n}(i)\leq 2/(\epsilon^{2}n)
on
(
9

1-o(1)

\mathrm{Bt}_{n}(i)=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1/n)
for fixed
i
and

\max_{i}\mathrm{Bt}_{n}(i)=O_{\mathbb{P}}(1/n)
as well.

Proof of Theorem
8.6

Proof.

Parts (i)–(ii) follow from the standard theory of kernel random graphs
for inhomogeneous graphs with kernel

\kappa(x,y):=\lambda w_{\mathrm{BPS}}(x,y)
. In that framework, the
local neighbourhood of a uniformly chosen vertex converges in
distribution to a (possibly multitype) Galton–Watson branching process
with offspring kernel
\kappa
and associated integral operator

T_{\kappa}f(x)=\int_{0}^{1}\kappa(x,y)f(y)\,dy=\lambda T_{w_{\mathrm{BPS}}}f(x)
.
The survival probability of this process is positive if and only if the
spectral radius
\rho(T_{\kappa})
exceeds
1
. Since

\rho(T_{\kappa})=\lambda\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}
, the threshold for the
emergence of a giant component is
\lambda\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}=1
.
Irreducibility of
w_{\mathrm{BPS}}
ensures that any giant component is
unique.

For (iii), write
T_{\mathrm{BPS}}:=T_{w_{\mathrm{BPS}}}
and

T_{j}:=T_{w_{j}}
for
j=0,\dots,J
, where
w_{0}\equiv 1
denotes the
constant graphon. By linearity of the integral operator,

T_{\mathrm{BPS}}=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\beta_{j}T_{j}.

Each
T_{j}
is a bounded self–adjoint operator on
L^{2}[0,1]
with a
nonnegative symmetric kernel, hence positivity–preserving. For any

f\geq 0
,

(T_{j}f)(x)=\int_{0}^{1}w_{j}(x,y)f(y)\,dy\geq 0.

Moreover, since
\beta_{j}\geq 0
and
w_{j}\geq 0
for all
j
,

(T_{\mathrm{BPS}}f)(x)\geq(\beta_{j}T_{j}f)(x)\qquad\text{for every }f\geq 0\text{ and each }j.

Thus
0\leq\beta_{j}T_{j}\leq T_{\mathrm{BPS}}
in the usual order on positive operators.
If
0\leq A\leq B
then spectral radii satisfy
r(A)\leq r(B)
, hence

\beta_{j}\rho_{j}\leq\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}
for all
j
, giving

\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}\geq\max_{j}\beta_{j}\rho_{j}
.

For the upper bound, self–adjointness implies
\rho(T)=\|T\|
, and
subadditivity of the operator norm yields

\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}=\Big\|\sum_{j=0}^{J}\beta_{j}T_{j}\Big\|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{J}\beta_{j}\|T_{j}\|=\sum_{j=0}^{J}\beta_{j}\rho_{j}.

The bounds on
\lambda_{c}=1/\rho_{\mathrm{BPS}}
follow by reciprocals.
