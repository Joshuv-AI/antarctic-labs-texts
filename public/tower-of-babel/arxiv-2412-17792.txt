Distributed Estimation and Gap-Free Analysis of Canonical Correlations
Canyi Chen canyic@umich.edu, Liping Zhu zhu.liping@ruc.edu.cn

Abstract
Massive data analysis calls for distributed algorithms and theories. We design a multi-round distributed algorithm for canonical correlation analysis. We construct principal directions through convex formulation of canonical correlation analysis, and use the shift-and-invert preconditioning iteration to expedite the convergence rate. This distributed algorithm is communication-efficient. The resultant estimate achieves the same convergence rate as if all observations were pooled together, but does not impose stringent restrictions on the number of machines. We take a gap-free analysis to bypass the widely used yet unrealistic assumption of an explicit gap between the successive canonical correlations in the canonical correlation analysis. Extensive simulations and applications to three benchmark image data are conducted to demonstrate the empirical performance of our proposed algorithms and theories.

Introduction

Rapid advances in information technology allow people to collect data of unprecedented size in various scientific areas such as genomics, neuroscience and physics. These datasets are quite often simultaneously characterized by high dimensions and large sample sizes. Analyzing massive datasets in a single machine through conventional in-memory statistical algorithms is perhaps prohibitive due to constraints such as privacy concerns, limited memory and storage space
(
Fan et al., 2014
)
. Easing these constraints calls for distributed algorithms and theories with low communication costs.

We are concerned with canonical correlation analysis
(
Hotelling, 1936
)
that has already grown into a powerful arsenal for revealing mutual variability in multiple views of two sets of random vectors. It has seen many successful applications in regression, clustering and word embedding
(
Dhillon et al., 2011
)
, among many others. In the past two decades many efforts have been made to establish theoretical properties of canonical correlation analysis in the regimes of finite-
(
Anderson, 1999
)
and infinite-dimensions
(
Hardoon et al., 2004
;
Bao et al., 2019
;
Gao et al., 2015
;
Gao et al., 2017
;
Ma and Li, 2020
)
. Existing works implicitly assume that the observations are stored and processed in a single machine.

Canonical correlation analysis of massive data calls for distributed algorithms and theories. Towards this goal, many efforts have been made in a star-networked distributed setting
(
Zaharia et al., 2016
)
. These algorithms can be classified into two classes: single- or multiple-round. In particular, in the first class,
Lv et al. (2020)
developed a one-shot divide-and-conquer algorithm for canonical correlation analysis, which proceeds as follows. Each local machine conducts canonical correlation analysis and transmits the local results to the central machine over which all local estimates are aggregated to produce a final solution. This algorithm indeed generalizes the procedure proposed by
Fan et al. (2019)
to recover the eigenspace of principal component analysis. This generalization, however, suffers from at least two essential issues. First, the local estimates of canonical correlations are no longer unbiased. Therefore, for the divide-and-conquer algorithm to achieve an optimal convergence rate, a few undesirable and even unrealistic restrictions on the number of local machines have to be imposed to eliminate the biases of the local estimates
(
Fan et al., 2019
;
Chen et al., 2022
)
. In a sensor network, the number of local machines usually violates the restrictions. Second, for the canonical correlation analysis to fulfill the orthogonal constraints, the divide-and-conquer algorithm requires each local machine to transmit the full covariance matrices to the central one in order to perform matrix decomposition. This additional procedure introduces an undesirable and even unbearable communication cost, particularly when the random vectors are high or even ultrahigh dimensional. Many multiple-round distributed algorithms, which fall into the second class, are also designed to relax the restrictions on the number of machines that are usually required by distributed principal component analysis. To be precise,
Garber et al. (2017)
presented two algorithms to estimate the leading principal component, which were later extended to a general number of principal components by
Chen et al. (2022)
.

The above methodologies designed for distributed principal component analysis, however, cannot be adapted directly to distributed canonical correlation analysis because the latter involves inversions of two large covariance matrices. Using the pooled inversion would result in significant communication costs, while using the local one would result in non-negligible estimation biases. In addition, calculating inversions of large covariance matrices usually require very expensive computation costs.

In the present article, we design a distributed algorithm for canonical correlation analysis that greatly mitigates the restrictions on the number of local machines and simultaneously eases the communication cost by avoiding communicating the full covariance matrices. The resultant distributed estimate attains an optimal convergence rate after a finite number of communications and fully respects the orthogonal constraints in the canonical correlation analysis. To be precise, we construct the principal directions through convex formulation of canonical correlation analysis, and use the shift-and-invert preconditioning
(
Golub and Van Loan, 1983
)
, together with quadratic programming, to expedite the convergence rate of our proposed distributed algorithm. Solving canonical correlation analysis through shift-and-invert preconditioning dates back to
Golub and Van Loan (1983)
and
Golub and Zha (1995)
. It is an iterative method that transforms the canonical correlation analysis into a series of least squares problems in order to produce a sequence of increasingly accurate estimates.

Ma et al. (2015)
,
Wang et al. (2016)
,
Allen-Zhu and Li (2017)
and
Gao et al. (2019)
use this concept to solve the pooled canonical component analysis, where the least squares problems are solved by stochastic techniques. However, when this concept is implemented in the distributed canonical component analysis, numerous iterations may be involved and consequently, unbearable communication costs are to be introduced. We shall show that, the distributed estimate of our proposed algorithm achieves the same convergence rate as if all observations are pooled together after a finite number of communications. This merely requires to transmit vectors and thus reduces the communication cost substantially. By contrast, the divide-and-conquer method proposed by
Lv et al. (2020)
requires to transmit large dimensional matrices, which results in non-negligible communication cost.

We further make an important theoretical contribution to the literature by deriving a gap-free error bound for the distributed canonical correlation analysis. Most existing convergence analyses depend upon the assumption of an explicit canonical correlation gap,
\rho_{L}-\rho_{L+1}>B_{L}
, where
\rho_{\ell}
is the
\ell
-th largest canonical correlation,
L
is a user-specified integer and
B_{L}
is a theoretical lower bound. Examples of requiring this assumption include, but not limited to,
Wang et al. (2016)
and
Gao et al. (2019)
. This assumption is widely used though, it is likely unrealistic. We demonstrate this phenomenon through three benchmark image data sets, MEDIAMILL
(
Snoek et al., 2006
)
, MNIST
(
Lecun et al., 1998
)
, and MFEAT
(
Dua and Graff, 2017
)
. The Wilks’ lambda test
(
Anderson, 2003
)
indicates that the top
50
canonical correlations are significant with p-values all less than
0.01
. In
Figure

1
, we plot the histogram of the first-order differences,
\rho_{\ell+1}-\rho_{\ell}
, of the top
50
canonical correlations. We also mark the theoretical lower bound of
B_{L}
derived by
Cheng et al. (2021)
with a small diamond on the horizontal axis. It can be clearly seen that, all the first-order differences of the top
50
canonical correlations are substantially smaller than the lower bounds, violating the theoretical requirement apparently. We take a gap-free analysis to bypass this widely used and yet unrealistic requirement in the distributed canonical correlation analysis.

2
Methodology Development

2.1
A brief review

Canonical correlation analysis aims to identify the co-variations between two sets of random vectors. To be precise, let
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf x}}
and
{\bf y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf y}}
have zero mean. We denote the joint distribution function of
({\bf x},{\bf y})
by
\mathcal{D}({\bf x},{\bf y})
, which is sub-Gaussian. Define
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\textrm{cov}({\bf x},{\bf x}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}})
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf y}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\textrm{cov}({\bf y},{\bf y}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}})
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\textrm{cov}({\bf x},{\bf y}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}})
to be the auto- and cross-covariance matrices. Canonical correlation analysis is invariant to linear transformations of the two random vectors
(
Gao et al., 2019
)
, which allows us to assume without loss of generality that
\max\{\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x}}),\sigma_{\max}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf y}})\}\leq 1
and
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\min\{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x}}),\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf y}})\}>0
. Let
L
be a user-specified integer such that
1\leq L\leq\min(d_{\bf x},d_{\bf y})
. Define
{\cal O}_{\mathbf{U}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\{\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf x}\times L}:\langle\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x}}}={\bf I}_{L\times L}\}
and
{\cal O}_{\mathbf{V}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\{\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf y}\times L}:\langle\mathbf{V},\mathbf{V}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf y}}}={\bf I}_{L\times L}\}
. Canonical correlation analysis solves the non-convex optimization of

\displaystyle(\mathbf{U}_{1:L}^{\ast},\mathbf{V}_{1:L}^{\ast})\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\ \mathop{\rm arg\max}_{\mathbf{U}\in{\cal O}_{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{V}\in{\cal O}_{\mathbf{V}}}\textrm{tr}\{\langle\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}}\},

(2.1)

where
\textrm{tr}(\cdot)
stands for the trace operator,
\mathbf{U}_{1:L}^{\ast}=(\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\mathbf{u}_{L}^{\ast})
and
\mathbf{V}_{1:L}^{\ast}=(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\mathbf{v}_{L}^{\ast})
,
(\mathbf{u}_{\ell}^{\ast},\mathbf{v}_{\ell}^{\ast})
stand for the
\ell
-th pair of canonical directions and
\rho_{\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\langle\mathbf{u}_{\ell},\mathbf{v}_{\ell}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}}
is
\ell
-th canonical correlation satisfying
1\geq\rho_{1}\geq\ldots\geq\rho_{L}\geq 0
. Let
\mathbf{T}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x}}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf y}}^{-1/2}
. The
L
-truncated singular value decomposition of
\mathbf{T}
is
\mathbf{\Phi}_{1:L}^{\ast}\mathbf{D}_{1:L}^{\ast}\bm{\Psi}^{*\mbox{\tiny{T}}}_{1:L}
.
Hotelling (1936)
showed that
(\mathbf{U}_{1:L}^{\ast},\mathbf{V}_{1:L}^{\ast})=(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x}}^{-1/2}\mathbf{\Phi}_{1:L}^{\ast},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf y}}^{-1/2}\bm{\Psi}_{1:L}^{\ast})
, and
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{L}
are the singular values of
\mathbf{T}
, which correspond to the respective diagonal elements of
\mathbf{D}_{1:L}^{\ast}
.

Suppose a random sample of size
N
, denoted by
\{({\bf x}_{i},{\bf y}_{i}),i=1,\ldots,N\}
, is available. Let
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}
be the respective estimates of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x}}
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf y}}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}
. Define
\widehat{\cal O}_{\mathbf{U}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\{\mathbf{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf x}\times L}:\langle\mathbf{U},\mathbf{U}\rangle_{{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\bf x}}={\bf I}_{L\times L}\}
and
\widehat{\cal O}_{\mathbf{V}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\{\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf y}\times L}:\langle\mathbf{V},\mathbf{V}\rangle_{{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}_{\bf y}}={\bf I}_{L\times L}\}
. At the sample level, canonical correlation analysis solves the non-convex optimization of

\displaystyle(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{pool}})\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\ \mathop{\rm arg\max}_{\mathbf{U}\in\widehat{\cal O}_{\mathbf{U}},\mathbf{V}\in\widehat{\cal O}_{\mathbf{V}}}\textrm{tr}\{\langle\mathbf{U},\mathbf{V}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}}\},

(2.2)

where
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{pool}}=(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\ldots,\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{L}^{\mathrm{pool}})
and
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{pool}}=(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\ldots,\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{L}^{\mathrm{pool}})
. The pooled estimate of the
\ell
-th canonical correlation
\rho_{\ell}
is given by
\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}}
. We define the empirical version of
\mathbf{T}
by
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}^{-1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}^{-1/2}
. The
L
-truncated singular value decomposition of
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}
is
\widehat{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1:L}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_{1:L}\widehat{\bm{\Psi}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}_{1:L}
, where
\widehat{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1:L}=(\widehat{\bm{\phi}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{\phi}}_{L})
and
\widehat{\bm{\Psi}}_{1:L}=(\widehat{\bm{\psi}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{\psi}}_{L})
. It follows that
(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{pool}})=(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}^{-1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1:L},\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}^{-1/2}\widehat{\bm{\Psi}}_{1:L})
and
\widehat{\rho}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\rho}_{L}
are the corresponding singular values of
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}
, which correspond to the respective diagonal elements of
\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_{1:L}
. It is important to remark here that, directly evaluating
(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{pool}})
through its explicit form involves singular value decomposition, which is computationally expensive and even prohibitive for massive data analysis.

To ease the computational complexity of singular value decomposition,
Garber and Hazan (2015)
and
Wang et al. (2016)
developed a variant shift-and-invert preconditioning iteration method to recast the non-convex optimization problem (
2.2
) as a sequence of least squares problems, each of which corresponds to a linear system and can be solved efficiently. To illustrate the rationale of this iteration method, we first consider approximating the top pair of canonical directions
(\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\ast},\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\ast})
.
Let
\overline{\rho}_{1}
be a crude estimate of an upper bound of the top canonical correlation
\rho_{1}
. Let
d\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}d_{\bf x}+d_{\bf y}
.
Define

\widehat{\mathbf{C}}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\ \begin{pmatrix}{\bf 0}_{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf x}}&\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf y}}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}_{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf y}}&{\bf 0}_{d_{\bf y}\times d_{\bf y}}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}.

It is straightforward to verify that the
L
largest eigenvalues of
(\overline{\rho}_{1}{\bf I}-\widehat{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}
are
1/(\overline{\rho}_{1}-\widehat{\rho}_{1}),\cdots 1/(\overline{\rho}_{1}-\widehat{\rho}_{L})
, and the
L
smallest eigenvalues are
1/(\overline{\rho}_{1}+\widehat{\rho}_{L}),\cdots,1/(\overline{\rho}_{1}+\widehat{\rho}_{1})
. These eigenvalues correspond to the eigenvectors

\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\bm{\phi}}_{1}\\
\widehat{\bm{\psi}}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg/2^{1/2},\cdots,\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\bm{\phi}}_{L}\\
\widehat{\bm{\psi}}_{L}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg/2^{1/2},\cdots,\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\bm{\phi}}_{L}\\
-\widehat{\bm{\psi}}_{L}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg/2^{1/2},\cdots,\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\bm{\phi}}_{1}\\
-\widehat{\bm{\psi}}_{1}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg/2^{1/2}.

To approximate the top pair of canonical directions
(\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\ast},\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\ast})
, we run power iterations on
(\overline{\rho}_{1}{\bf I}-\widehat{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}
through the shift-and-invert preconditioning iteration method
(
Golub and Van Loan, 1983
)
. To be precise, we let
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(0)})
be an initial estimate of the top pair of canonical directions
(\mathbf{u}_{1}^{\ast},\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\ast})
. During the
t
-th iteration,
t=0,1,\ldots,
the variant shift-and-invert preconditioning iteration method takes the form of

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}^{1/2}&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf 0}&\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}(\overline{\rho}_{1}{\bf I}-\widehat{\mathbf{C}})^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}^{1/2}&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf 0}&\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix},

which, after some straightforward algebraic calculations, reduces to

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{H}}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf 0}&\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix},\textrm{ where }\widehat{\mathbf{H}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\rho}_{1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}&-\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}\\
-\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}&\overline{\rho}_{1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(2.3)

Iterating (
2.3
) can be recast as a one-step Newton’s iteration. In particular,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix}-\widehat{\mathbf{H}}^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{g}},\textrm{ where }\widehat{\mathbf{g}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\left\{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}-\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf 0}&\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix}.

(2.4)

Iterating (
2.3
) is also equivalent to solving the following quadratic optimization,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)}\end{pmatrix}=\mathop{\rm arg\min}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}\left\{Q(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\
\mathbf{v}\end{pmatrix}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{H}}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\
\mathbf{v}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}\\
\mathbf{v}\end{pmatrix}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf 0}&\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix}\right\}.

(2.5)

Finally, we perform an additional normalization,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)}\end{pmatrix}=2^{1/2}\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)}\\
\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg/\Bigg(\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)},\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}+\langle\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)},\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}\Bigg)^{1/2},

to ensure that
\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}+\langle\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}=2
, which updates
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t)})
to
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)})
, for
t=0,1,\ldots
. We iterate this procedure until convergence.

Performing direct iterations using (
2.3
) requires to invert a high-dimensional Hessian matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}
. This would result in a prohibitive complexity in both communication and computation. The non-convex canonical correlation analysis in (
2.2
) has been formulated as a sequence of least squares problems. Taking this advantage, we design a computation- and communication-efficient distributed algorithm.

2.2
A distributed estimation

Next we develop a distributed algorithm for canonical correlation analysis when the observations,
\{({\bf x}_{i},{\bf y}_{i}),i=1,\ldots,N\}
, are scattered uniformly at
K
machines, each of size
n
. In other words,
N=nK
. Let
\mathbf{X}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{\bf x}}
and
\mathbf{Y}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{\bf y}}
represent the data matrices in the
k
-th machine,
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},k}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y},k}
,
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y},k}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},k}^{-1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y},k}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y},k}^{-1/2}
be the respective estimates of
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x}}
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf y}}
,
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}
and
\mathbf{T}
, which are obtained using the data matrices
(\mathbf{X}_{k},\mathbf{Y}_{k})
in the
k
-th machine, for
k=1,\ldots,K.

Our proposed distributed algorithm is iterative. To start, we construct an initial estimate of the top pair of canonical directions,
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(0)})
, and a crude estimate of an upper bound,
\overline{\rho}_{1}
, of the top canonical correlation. We merely use the observations in the first machine to avoid communication overhead. Let
\widehat{\bm{\phi}}^{(0)}
and
\widehat{\bm{\psi}}^{(0)}
be the respective left and right singular vectors of
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{1}
that correspond to the largest singular value. We take
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(0)})=(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},1}^{-1/2}\widehat{\bm{\phi}}^{(0)},\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y},1}^{-1/2}\widehat{\bm{\psi}}^{(0)})
, and
\overline{\rho}_{1}=\sigma_{\max}(\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{1})+1.5\ \omega
, where
\omega\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}c_{0}\ (d\log^{2}d/n)^{1/2}
, for some
c_{0}>0
. Alternatively, we can use some stochastic algorithms that completely avoid matrix decomposition to construct a crude upper bound
\overline{\rho}_{1}
. Examples include
Wang et al. (2016)
,
Allen-Zhu and Li (2017)
and
Gao et al. (2019)
.

We propose to estimate the spaces spanned by

\mathbf{U}_{1:L}^{\ast}
and
\mathbf{V}_{1:L}^{\ast}
in a sequential manner. To illustrate the concept of our proposed algorithm, we start from
L=1
. Define

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\rho}_{1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},k}&-\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y},k}\\
-\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y},k}&\overline{\rho}_{1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y},k}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

(2.6)

In the one-step Newton’s iteration (
2.4
), calculating the full Hessian matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}
requires each local machine to transmit the
d\times d
local Hessian matrices,
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{k}
s, to the central machine, which incurs an expensive and even prohibitive communication cost in high dimensions. To address this issue, a natural idea is to approximate the full Hessian matrix
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}
in (
2.4
) with a local one, say,
\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{1}

(
Shamir et al., 2014
;
Jordan et al., 2019
;
Fan et al., 2021
)
. We further approximate
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}
with an average of all
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}
s, which leads to an approximation of the following one-step Newton’s iteration,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{(t+1)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{(t+1)}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix}-(\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{1}K)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{k},\textrm{ where }\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\left\{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{k}-\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},k}&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf 0}&\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y},k}\end{pmatrix}\right\}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix}.

The updated estimate
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{(t+1)},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{(t+1)})
is however sub-optimal in contrast to
(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)},\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)})
in the exact one-step Newton’s iteration (
2.4
). In particular,

(\widetilde{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)},\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)})
is exactly the minimizer of (
2.5
), by contrast,
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{(t+1)},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{(t+1)})
is not. It is important to remark here that, by
Theorem

1
in
Section

3
, such one-step approximation in the inner loop may lead to a sub-optimal convergence rate. For each given
(t+1)
, to further refine the estimate
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{(t+1)},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{(t+1)})
for
j=1,2,\ldots
, we suggest to update with

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j+1}^{(t+1)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{j+1}^{(t+1)}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{(t+1)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{(t+1)}\end{pmatrix}-(\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{1}K)^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,j}^{(t+1)},\textrm{ where }

(2.7)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,j}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\left\{\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{k}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t+1)}_{j}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t+1)}_{j}\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},k}&{\bf 0}\\
{\bf 0}&\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y},k}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(t)}\\
\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(t)}\end{pmatrix}\right\}

(2.8)

is the local gradient of (
2.5
) evaluated at
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{j}^{(t+1)},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{j}^{(t+1)})
. For each given
(t+1)
, the inner iteration procedure proceeds in a distributed fashion. In particular, for each machine, we calculate the local gradients,
\widehat{\mathbf{g}}_{k,j}^{(t+1)}
s, which are transmitted to the central machine to form a full gradient. Therefore, for each given
(t+1)
, the total communication cost is
O(dK)
. This distributed algorithm is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

Let us compare the computation cost of direct and indirect methods in calculating the inversions of large dimensional matrices. The update (
2.3

d\times d

O(d^{3})
, which can be prohibitive for extremely large
d

d=d_{{\bf x}}+d_{\bf y}

\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{1}
, with the diagonal blocks being
\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},1}=\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\mathbf{X}_{1}/n

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y},1}=\mathbf{X}_{1}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\mathbf{Y}_{1}/n

\widehat{\mathbf{H}}_{1}^{-1}

3
Theoretical Analysis

We are in the position to analyze the theoretical properties for the resultant distributed estimates, and relegate all technical proofs to the appendices. We study the distributed estimates of the top and the top-L-dim canonical directions separately, which are derived through
Algorithms

1
and
2
. We apply the full singular value decomposition to obtain that
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}=\widehat{\mathbf{\Phi}}\widehat{\mathbf{D}}\widehat{\bm{\Psi}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}
, where
\widehat{\mathbf{\Phi}}=(\widehat{\bm{\phi}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{\phi}}_{d_{\bf x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf x}}
and
\widehat{\bm{\Psi}}=(\widehat{\bm{\psi}}_{1},\ldots,\widehat{\bm{\psi}}_{d_{\bf y}})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf y}\times d_{\bf y}}
are orthonormal matrices,
\widehat{\mathbf{D}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf y}}
is a diagonal matrix with its
(\ell,\ell)
-th element
\widehat{\mathbf{D}}_{\ell\ell}
being
\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}
, for
\ell=1,\ldots,\min(d_{\bf x},d_{\bf y})
, and
0
for
\ell=\min(d_{\bf x},d_{\bf y})+1,\ldots,\max(d_{\bf x},d_{\bf y})
.

3.1
Distributed estimation of the top canonical directions

We denote the distributed estimates of the top pair of canonical directions by
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}})
. It is important to measure its distance from the pooled estimate
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}})
, which serves as a benchmark in the present context. When there is no confusion, we write

\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq d_{\bf x}\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|^{2}\textrm{ and }\sum_{1\leq\ell\leq d_{\bf y}\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}|^{2}

as

\sum_{\ell\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|^{2}\textrm{ and }\sum_{\ell\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}|^{2},

respectively, where
\delta
is reserved to denote a relative gap threshold. We shall show that the distance between
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}})
and
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}})
satisfies

\displaystyle\max\left(\sum_{\ell\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|^{2},\sum_{\ell\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}|^{2}\right)\leq\varepsilon^{2}/\delta^{2},

(3.1)

for some
\varepsilon>0
and an arbitrary constant
0<\delta<1
. The left-hand side of (
3.1
) stands for the distance we shall use in the present context which, at the conceptual level, it is quite different from and more general than the sine distance
\max\big(\sin^{2}\widehat{\theta},\sin^{2}\widetilde{\theta}\big)
, where
\widehat{\theta}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\ \arccos|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|
and
\widetilde{\theta}\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\ \arccos|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}|
. To see this, we simply consider evaluating the accuracy of
\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}
. Assuming
(\widehat{\rho}_{1}-\widehat{\rho}_{2})>0
,
Wang et al. (2016)
and
Gao et al. (2019)
showed that
\sin\widehat{\theta}\leq C\varepsilon\widehat{\rho}_{1}/(\widehat{\rho}_{1}-\widehat{\rho}_{2})
,
where
C>0
is a generic constant.
This upper bound involves a positive gap between the first two singular values of
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}
, which is undesirable in the present context. We connect this sine distance with the left-hand side of (
3.1
). To be precise, we set
\delta=(\widehat{\rho}_{1}-\widehat{\rho}_{2})/\widehat{\rho}_{1}
to obtain

\sin^{2}\widehat{\theta}=1-|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|^{2}=\sum_{\ell\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|^{2},

which, by (
3.1
), is smaller than or equal to
(\varepsilon^{2}/\delta^{2}),
or equivalently,

\sin\widehat{\theta}\leq(\varepsilon/\delta)=\varepsilon\widehat{\rho}_{1}/(\widehat{\rho}_{1}-\widehat{\rho}_{2})
. By the definition of the sine distance, the first equality holds. The second is a direct consequence of the fact that

\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{\bf x}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|^{2}=(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}})^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\big\{(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{pool}})^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}^{\mathrm{pool}}\big\}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}=(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}})^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}=1.

In other words, by choosing an appropriate
\delta
in (
3.1
), we can obtain the same result as that in
Wang et al. (2016)
and
Gao et al. (2019)
, indicating that the distance we shall use in this context is more general than the sine distance. However, we do not require an explicit gap between the successive singular values of
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}
. We consider instead the enlarged spaces
\{\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}}\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}>(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}\}
and
\{\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}}\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}>(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}\}
to bypass this widely used and yet unrealistic requirement.

(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}^{(0)},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}^{(0)})
be the initial estimate and
\overline{\rho}_{1}
be an upper bound of
\widehat{\rho}_{1}
. Let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be the respective number of outer and inner iterations. Define
\kappa\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\|\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{T}}\|
and
\gamma\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\min\{\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x}}),\sigma_{\min}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf y}})\}
. By using matrix concentration inequalities, we can show that
\kappa=O_{p}\{(d\log^{2}d/n)^{1/2}\}
. One can refer to Lemma A.1 for details in Appendix A. We first provide convergence guarantee for
Algorithm

1

\widehat{\mathbf{T}}
.

. In other words, the requirements of Theorem
1
are fulfilled with an overwhelming probability.

The error bound in Theorem
1
can be further simplified. In particular, if
\omega=(\kappa\delta\widehat{\rho}_{1}/\gamma)^{1/2}/3
, then
\omega=O_{p}(\kappa^{1/2})=O_{p}(\|\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{T}}\|^{1/2})
because both
\delta
and
\widehat{\rho}_{1}
fall within
(0,1)
and
\gamma
is a positive constant. By Lemma A.1 of Appendix A,
\omega=O_{p}\big\{\left(d\log^{2}d/n\right)^{1/4}\big\}
. We further assume
\left(d\log^{2}d/n\right)^{1/4}\ll(\delta\widehat{\rho}_{1})/16
to ensure that
1-128\omega^{2}/(\delta\widehat{\rho}_{1})^{2}\geq 1/2
, which simplifies the error bound as follows.

Corollary 1
.

If
T^{\prime}=T
,
\omega=(\kappa\delta\widehat{\rho}_{1}/\gamma)^{1/2}/3
, and
\left(d\log^{2}d/n\right)^{1/4}\ll(\delta\widehat{\rho}_{1})/16
, then

\displaystyle\max\left(\sum_{\ell\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|^{2},\sum_{\ell\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}|^{2}\right)

(3.3)

\displaystyle=O_{p}\left\{\left(\frac{576\kappa}{\gamma\delta\widehat{\rho}_{1}}\right)^{T}\right\}.\

This corollary indicates that, if
(576\kappa)/(\gamma\delta\widehat{\rho}_{1})\ll 1
,
Algorithm

1
converges at a linear convergence rate, which implicitly requires
\kappa=O_{p}\{(d\log^{2}d/n)^{1/2}\}
needs to be smaller, i.e.,
\kappa=o_{p}(\delta\widehat{\rho}_{1}\gamma)
. By definition,
\kappa=\|\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{1}-\widehat{\mathbf{T}}\|
. Therefore, if the number of observations in each local machine becomes relatively larger,
\kappa
would be smaller, consequently, the condition
\kappa=o_{p}(\delta\widehat{\rho}_{1}\gamma)
can be met more easily.

Next, we compare the communication cost of
Algorithm

1
with that of the one-shot divide-and-conquer method. The latter is generally regarded as communication-efficient because its communication cost is as small as
O(dK)
. By contrast, the communication cost of
Algorithm

1
is
O(TT^{\prime}dK)
. Theorem
1
indicates that, for the resultant estimates to attain the error rate
\varepsilon
, the number of iterations is required to satisfy
T=O\{\log(1/\varepsilon)\}
if
T^{\prime}=T
, which is a logarithm order of
\varepsilon
. The total number of iterations,
(TT^{\prime})
, can thus be regarded as a small number.
Jordan et al. (2019)
stated that the algorithms that merely transmit an
O(d)
vector (instead of
O(d^{2})
matrices) during each iteration are generally regarded as communication-efficient. In this regard, our proposed distributed algorithm is communication-efficient, although its communication cost is slightly higher than that of the divide-and-conquer method.

Corollary 2
.

If the distributed estimate of
Algorithm

1
satisfies that

\displaystyle\max\left(\sum_{\ell\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|^{2},\sum_{\ell\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}|^{2}\right)\leq\varepsilon/2,

it follows that

(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}})^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}})\geq(1-\delta)(1-\varepsilon)\widehat{\rho}_{1}
, where
\widehat{\rho}_{1}=(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}})^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}})
.

Identifying the top pair of canonical directions is theoretically difficult if the gap of successive canonical correlations is extremely small. To the most extreme, if the gap is zero, the first two pairs of canonical directions are not statistically identifiable. To bypass this gap requirement, we turn to capture the co-variability between two sets of random vectors, which is indeed the ultimate goal of canonical correlation analysis. This corollary states that the distributed estimate
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}})
captures almost the same amount of co-variability as the pooled estimate
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}})
up to a
(1-\delta)(1-\varepsilon)
multiplicative factor. This phenomenon is referred to as the gap-free bound in the optimization literature
(
Allen-Zhu and Li, 2016
;
Allen-Zhu and Li, 2017
)
. The parameter
\delta
is pre-specified to measure the proportion of the co-variability captured by the distributed estimate
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}})
. In particular, if we choose
\delta=\varepsilon
,
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}})
captures at least
(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)
of the co-variability captured by the top pair of canonical directions. If we choose
\delta=c_{0}/(\gamma\widehat{\rho}_{1})
such that
(\delta\widehat{\rho}_{1}\gamma)=c_{0}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
, for
Algorithm

1
to converge at a linear convergence rate, it implicitly requires
\kappa=o_{p}(1)
. This is a mild condition.

Next, we show that the distributed estimates approximate the underlying true canonical directions pretty well.

Corollary 3
.

Assume that the distributed estimate obtained from
Algorithm

2
satisfies

\max\left(\sum_{\ell\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|^{2},\sum_{\ell\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{1}}|\langle\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}|^{2}\right)\leq\varepsilon/2,

for some error term
\varepsilon>0
. It follows that

\displaystyle\max\left(\sum_{\ell\colon\rho_{\ell}\leq(1-2\delta)\rho_{1}}|\langle\mathbf{u}_{\ell}^{\ast},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|^{2},\sum_{\ell\colon\rho_{\ell}\leq(1-2\delta)\rho_{1}}|\langle\mathbf{v}_{\ell}^{\ast},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{dist}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\|\widehat{\mathbf{T}}-\mathbf{T}\|^{2}}{\{(1-\delta)(\widehat{\rho}_{1}-\rho_{1})+\delta\rho_{1}\}^{2}}+\varepsilon.

3.2

Distributed estimation of the top-L-dim canonical directions

Next, we study the theoretical properties of
Algorithm

2
, which provides distributed estimates for the top
L
pairs of canonical directions. Define
L_{\delta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\mathop{\rm arg\max}\{1\leq\ell\leq\min(d_{\bf x},d_{\bf y})\colon\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}>(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}\}
, which can be larger than or equal to
L
. We further define
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L_{\delta}}^{\mathrm{pool}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\ldots\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{L_{\delta}}^{\mathrm{pool}})
and
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L_{\delta}}^{\mathrm{pool}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\ldots\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{L_{\delta}}^{\mathrm{pool}})
to be the pooled estimates of canonical directions associated with the largest
L_{\delta}
canonical correlations
\widehat{\rho}_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\widehat{\rho}_{L_{\delta}}
. Let
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{(L_{\delta}+1):d_{\bf x}}^{\mathrm{pool}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{L_{\delta}+1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\ldots\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{d_{\bf x}}^{\mathrm{pool}})
and
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{(L_{\delta}+1):d_{\bf y}}^{\mathrm{pool}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}(\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{L_{\delta}+1}^{\mathrm{pool}},\ldots\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{d_{\bf y}}^{\mathrm{pool}})
, which form the orthogonal complements of
\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L_{\delta}}^{\mathrm{pool}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L_{\delta}}^{\mathrm{pool}}
, respectively. In addition, we introduce some notations for distributed estimates. To be precise,
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}^{(\ell)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}^{-1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}^{(\ell)}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}^{-1/2}
, where

\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}^{(\ell)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y},k}^{(\ell)}\textrm{ and }\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y},k}^{(\ell)}=\mathbf{Q}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:\ell}^{\mathrm{dist}}}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}})\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y},k}\mathbf{Q}_{\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:\ell}^{\mathrm{dist}}}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}),

for
\ell=1,\ldots,L
and
k=1,\ldots,K
.

We first discuss the selection of initial estimates. For
\omega
defined in
Theorem

1
which corresponds to the special case of
\ell=1
, we take
\overline{\rho}_{\ell}=\sigma_{\max}(\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{1}^{(\ell)})+1.5\ \omega
where
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{1}^{(\ell)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},1}^{-1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y},1}^{(\ell)}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y},1}^{-1/2}
is the whitened cross-covariance. It can be verified that
\|\mathbf{Q}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:\ell}^{\mathrm{dist}}}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}})\|\leq 1
and
\|\mathbf{Q}_{\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:\ell}^{\mathrm{dist}}}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}})\|\leq 1
. By definition,

\left\|\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{1}^{(\ell)}-\widehat{\mathbf{T}}^{(\ell)}\right\|\leq\|\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},1}^{-1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y},1}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y},1}^{-1/2}-\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}^{-1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}^{-1/2}\|\leq 0.5\ \omega,

which holds uniformly for
\ell=1,\ldots,L
. This, together with Weyl’s inequality, ensures that
\omega\leq\overline{\rho}_{\ell}-\sigma_{1}(\widehat{\mathbf{T}}^{(\ell)})\leq 2\omega
. We summarize our main result as follows.

Theorem 2
.

Assume that
2\kappa\leq\omega\leq\overline{\rho}_{\ell}-\sigma_{\max}(\widehat{\mathbf{T}}^{(\ell)})\leq 2\omega
for
\ell=1,\ldots,L
.
Let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be the respective number of outer and inner iterations in
Algorithm

2
. Then for an arbitrary constant
\delta\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\max\left(\left\|\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{(L_{\delta}+1):d_{\bf x}}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}\right\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}\right\|^{2},\left\|\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{(L_{\delta}+1):d_{\bf y}}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}\right\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=O_{p}\Bigg[\left(\frac{\widehat{\rho}_{1}L^{2}}{\widehat{\rho}_{L}\delta}\right)^{2}\Bigg\{\left(\frac{128\omega^{2}}{\delta^{2}\widehat{\rho}_{L}^{2}}\right)^{T}+\Bigg.\Bigg.\Bigg.\Bigg.\frac{512}{1-128\omega^{2}/(\delta\widehat{\rho}_{L})^{2}}\left(\frac{64\kappa^{2}}{\gamma^{2}\omega^{2}}\right)^{T^{\prime}}\Bigg\}\Bigg].

Theorem

2
can be further simplified as follows.

Corollary 4
.

If
T^{\prime}=T
,
\omega=(\kappa\delta\widehat{\rho}_{L}/\gamma)^{1/2}/3
, and
(d\log^{2}d/n)^{1/4}\ll\delta\widehat{\rho}_{L}/16
, then

\displaystyle\max\left(\left\|\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{(L_{\delta}+1):d_{\bf x}}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}\right\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}\right\|^{2},\left\|\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{(L_{\delta}+1):d_{\bf y}}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}\right\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}\right\|^{2}\right)=O_{p}\Bigg\{\left(\frac{\widehat{\rho}_{1}L^{2}}{\widehat{\rho}_{L}\delta}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{576\kappa}{\gamma\delta\widehat{\rho}_{L}}\right)^{T}\Bigg\}.

This is an extension of the classical
\sin\Theta
theorem
(
Yu et al., 2015
, Theorem 4)
in the sense that we do not require there be an explicit gap between the successive singular values, which differ from most existing studies such as
Wang et al. (2016)
,
Gao et al. (2019)
and references therein. We bypass this gap assumption by considering the enlarged spaces
(\mathbf{U}^{\ast}_{1:L_{\delta}},\mathbf{V}^{\ast}_{1:L_{\delta}})
.

The following corollary indicates that the distributed estimate
(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}})
can capture almost the same amount of co-variability as the pooled estimate
(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{pool}})
.

Corollary 5
.

Assume that our estimate from
Algorithm

2
satisfies

\max\left(\left\|\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{(L_{\delta}+1):d_{\bf x}}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}\right\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}\right\|,\left\|\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{(L_{\delta}+1):d_{\bf y}}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}\right\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}\right\|\right)\leq C\delta/(\widehat{\rho}_{1}/\widehat{\rho}_{L+1}),

for some
C>0
. Then we have

\displaystyle\widehat{\rho}_{L+1}\leq\left\|\mathbf{Q}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}_{\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}})\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}\mathbf{Q}_{\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}}(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}})\right\|\leq\widehat{\rho}_{L+1}/(1-\delta),\ \text{and}

\displaystyle(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}\leq(\widehat{\mathbf{u}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{dist}})^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}\widehat{\mathbf{v}}_{\ell}^{\mathrm{dist}}\leq\widehat{\rho}_{\ell}/(1-\delta),\ \text{for}\ \ell=1,\ldots,L.

Next we quantify the distance between our proposed distributed estimate
\big(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}\big)
and the orthogonal complements of the first
L_{2\delta}^{\ast}
underlying true canonical directions
\big(\mathbf{U}^{\ast}_{(L_{2\delta}^{\ast}+1):d_{\bf x}},\mathbf{V}^{\ast}_{(L_{2\delta}^{\ast}+1):d_{\bf y}}\big)
, where
L_{2\delta}^{\ast}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{{\tiny def}}}}{{=}}\mathop{\rm arg\max}\{1\leq\ell\leq\min(d_{\bf x},d_{\bf y})\colon\rho_{\ell}>(1-2\delta)\rho_{L}\}
.

Corollary 6
.

Assume that the distributed estimate obtained from
Algorithm

2
satisfies

\max\left(\left\|\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{(L_{\delta}+1):d_{\bf x}}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}\right\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}\right\|,\left\|\left\langle\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{(L_{\delta}+1):d_{\bf y}}^{\mathrm{pool}},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}\right\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}\right\|\right)\leq\varepsilon,\textrm{ for some }\varepsilon>0.

It follows that

\displaystyle\max\left(\left\|\left\langle\mathbf{U}_{(L_{2\delta}^{\ast}+1):d_{\bf x}}^{\ast},\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}\right\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}\right\|,\left\|\left\langle\mathbf{V}_{(L_{2\delta}^{\ast}+1):d_{\bf y}}^{\ast},\widehat{\mathbf{V}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{dist}}\right\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}\right\|\right)\leq\frac{\|\widehat{\mathbf{T}}-\mathbf{T}\|}{(1-\delta)(\widehat{\rho}_{L}-\rho_{L})+\delta\rho_{L}}+\varepsilon.

Though we assume the observations are evenly scattered at different machines, we remark here that this assumption is merely assumed to ease the illustration of our distributed algorithms, and all theoretical properties derived in
Section

3
merely require the number of observations in the first or central machine is sufficiently large.

4

Simulation Studies

We use synthetic examples to demonstrate the performance of our distributed algorithms. We generate the observations in the same way as
Cai and Zhang (2018)
. To be precise, we generate
\{({\bf x}_{i},{\bf y}_{i}),i=1,\ldots,N\}
independently from multivariate normal distribution with mean zero and covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
. The diagonal blocks of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x}}={\bf I}_{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf x}}+({\bf Z}_{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf x}}+{\bf Z}_{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf x}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}})/\|2({\bf Z}_{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf x}}+{\bf Z}_{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf x}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}})\|
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf y}}={\bf I}_{d_{\bf y}\times d_{\bf y}}+({\bf Z}_{d_{\bf y}\times d_{\bf y}}+{\bf Z}_{d_{\bf y}\times d_{\bf y}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}})/\|2({\bf Z}_{d_{\bf y}\times d_{\bf y}}+{\bf Z}_{d_{\bf y}\times d_{\bf y}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}})\|
, where
{\bf Z}_{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf x}\times d_{\bf x}}
and
{\bf Z}_{d_{\bf y}\times d_{\bf y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf y}\times d_{\bf y}}
are independent and standard normal matrices. The off-diagonal blocks of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
are
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}=\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x}}^{1/2}\mathbf{\Phi}\mathbf{D}\bm{\Psi}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf y}}^{1/2}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}
, where
\mathbf{\Phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf x}\times r}
and
\bm{\Psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf y}\times r}
are column-wise orthonormal matrices, and
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is a diagonal matrix of the form

\mathbf{D}=0.1\times{\bf I}_{r\times r}+\mathrm{diag}(3\delta,2\delta,\delta,0,\ldots,0)
,

\delta
is used to control for the gap of canonical correlations. The column-wise orthonormal matrices
\mathbf{\Phi}
and
\bm{\Psi}
are obtained as follows.
Let
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Phi}}=(\widetilde{\phi}_{\ell k})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{\bf x}\times r}
. We first sample
\widetilde{\phi}_{\ell k}
independently from standard normal, for
\ell=1,\ldots,d_{\bf x}
and
k=1,\ldots,r
, then use the Gram-Schmidt process to orthonormalize the matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{\Phi}}
to obtain
\mathbf{\Phi}
. A similar procedure is applied to generate
\bm{\Psi}
.

We compare our proposed distributed estimate with the following competitors.

(1)

The pooled estimate. It is the classic implementation of the canonical correlation analysis, which pools all observations together to produce a single estimate.

(2)

The naive divide-and-conquer estimate. We merely use the observations in the
k
-th machine to calculate
\widehat{\mathbf{T}}_{k}
, and apply the singular value decomposition to obtain
\widehat{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1:L,k}
and
\widehat{\bm{\Psi}}_{1:L,k}
, which are transmitted to the central machine to form
\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{{\bf x}}=K^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1:L,k}\widehat{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1:L,k}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{{\bf y}}=K^{-1}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\widehat{\bm{\Psi}}_{1:L,k}\widehat{\bm{\Psi}}_{1:L,k}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}
.
The final estimates, denoted by
(\widehat{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{DC}},\widehat{\bm{\Psi}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{DC}})
, are formed by the top-L-dim eigenspaces of
\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{{\bf x}}
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{T}}_{{\bf y}}
.

(3)

The whitened divide-and-conquer estimate. It has the form of
(\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x},1}^{-1/2}\widehat{\mathbf{\Phi}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{DC}},\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y},1}^{-1/2}\widehat{\bm{\Psi}}_{1:L}^{\mathrm{DC}})
.

We use the following errors, which are motivated from the error bounds (
3.3
) and (
4
) with the pooled estimate replaced by the population canonical directions. Define
\rho_{\ell}=0
for
\ell=\min(d_{\bf x},d_{\bf y})+1,\ldots,\max(d_{\bf x},d_{\bf y})
.
Specifically, for the case of top pair of canonical directions, we evaluate the accuracy of
(\widehat{\mathbf{u}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}})

\displaystyle\mathrm{error}(\widehat{\mathbf{u}},\widehat{\mathbf{v}})=\max\left(\sum_{\ell\colon\rho_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\rho_{1}}|\langle\mathbf{u}_{\ell}^{\ast},\widehat{\mathbf{u}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf x}}}|^{2},\sum_{\ell\colon\rho_{\ell}\leq(1-\delta)\rho_{1}}|\langle\mathbf{v}_{\ell}^{\ast},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}\rangle_{\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{{\bf y}}}|^{2}\right).

For the case of top-L-dim pair of canonical directions, we evaluate the accuracy of

Concluding Remarks

We propose distributed algorithms for canonical correlation analysis, which adapts the convex formulation to a distributed setting, and allows us to use stochastic algorithms to solve linear systems. This algorithm uses a multi-round scheme to achieve a fast convergence rate and to relieve the constraint on the number of machines. We provide theoretical guarantees for the convergence rate of the resultant estimates. These distributed algorithms can be generalized from different perspectives. For example, one can introduce sparsity to consider ultrahigh dimensional canonical correlation analysis
(
Gao et al., 2017
)
, or adapt our theoretical analyses to many other relevant problems, such as partial least squares regression
(
Chen et al., 2019
)
and linear discriminant analysis
(
Bach and Jordan, 2005
)
. Investigations along these lines are under way.

\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{>(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{>(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}+\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}=\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\bf x}^{-1}
. We have

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\bf x}\mathbf{U}_{\leq(1-2\delta)\rho_{L}}\right\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\bf x}(\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{>(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{>(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}+\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}})\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\bf x}\mathbf{U}_{\leq(1-2\delta)\rho_{L}}\right\|

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\bf x}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{>(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{>(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\bf x}\mathbf{U}_{\leq(1-2\delta)\rho_{L}}\right\|

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\left\|\widetilde{\mathbf{U}}_{L}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\bf x}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}\widehat{\mathbf{U}}_{\leq(1-\delta)\widehat{\rho}_{L}}^{\mbox{\tiny{T}}}\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{\bf x}\mathbf{U}_{\leq(1-2\delta)\rho_{L}}\right\|

. This completes the proof of
