Immune landscape characterization of neurofibromas with atypical features in Neurofibromatosis1 reveals PD-1 and the Tim-3/Galectin-9 pathway as potential therapeutic targets
Authors: Anaïs Brunet, Fanny Coulpier, Audrey Onfroy, Nouhoum Sako, Katarzyna J Radomska, Valérie Aftimos, Laetitia Lacroix, Audrey Briand-Suleau, Pascale Maille, Oana Hermeziu, Ingrid Laurendeau, Eric Pasmant, Pierre Wolkenstein, Piotr Topilko, Nicolas Ortonne
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is a genetic disorder that predisposes individuals to the development of plexiform neurofibromas (pNF), benign tumors of the nerve sheath that can progress to malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNST). Some pNFs exhibit atypical characteristics, particularly atypical neurofibromatous neoplasms of uncertain biological potential (ANNUBP), which meet recently defined criteria. These are pNFs with concerning histological and/or molecular features and uncertain behavior, leading to their consideration as potential precursors of MPNSTs. In this study, we characterized the immune landscape of a series of atypical neurofibromas (exhibiting increased cellularity or atypical cells) and ANNUBPs (ANF/ANNUBP) compared to cutaneous neurofibromas (cNF), pNFs, and MPNSTs, all from patients with NF1, using immunohistochemistry and single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq). Our findings revealed that ANF/ANNUBPs are heavily infiltrated with CD3+ T-lymphocytes and CD163+ macrophages. Transcriptomic profiling using the NanoString technology demonstrated that ANF/ANNUBPs exhibit a distinct immune signature compared to both pNFs and MPNSTs. This includes overexpression of two inhibitory immune checkpoints (ICs): HAVCR2 (encoding the IC receptor Tim-3) and its primary ligand Galectin-9 (Gal9). Subsequent analyses of scRNA datasets corroborated these findings with Prss56Cre/+, Nf1fl/fl, R26tdTom/+ mouse model-derived tumors. Using multiplex immunofluorescence, we showed that CD3+ T-cells express the inhibitory IC PD-1, while CD163+ macrophages express both Tim-3 and Gal9. Furthermore, tumor cells within ANF/ANNUBPs were positive for activated caspase-3, suggesting a possible role of the immune microenvironment in controlling tumor growth or inducing cell death. Through deep-targeted DNA sequencing, we detected loss of cell cycle regulators (especially CDKN2A) in ANF/ANNUBPs but no somatic alteration of polycomb repressor complex components EED and SUZ12, the latter typically associated with MPNSTs, even at low frequencies. Additionally, loss of H3K27me3 was associated with even lower densities of CD3+ T-cells and CD163+ macrophages in MPNSTs, and the loss of p16 seemed to act synergistically. Taken together, these findings suggest that an immune response involving both T-lymphocytes and macrophages may play a role in controlling or delaying the malignant transformation of ANF/ANNUBPs into MPNSTs and point to IC molecules as novel immunopreventive strategies in NF1 patients with ANF/ANNUBPs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40478-026-02310-1.Keywords: Neurofibromatosis 1, Malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumor, Atypical neurofibroma, ANNUBP, PD-1, Tim-3, Galectin 9

Introduction

Neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1) is one of the most common autosomal dominant genetic disorders, affecting approximately 1 in 3000 individuals worldwide [1]. It is caused by the inactivation of the NF1 gene, a tumor suppressor located at chromosome 17q11.2, which encodes neurofibromin, a negative regulator of the RAS signaling pathway [2]. Individuals with NF1 develop a spectrum of tumors, most notably cutaneous neurofibromas (cNFs), which are present in virtually all patients, and plexiform neurofibromas (pNFs), which occur in a subset of cases. These tumors arise following the somatic loss of the second NF1 allele, commonly referred to as loss of heterozygosity (LOH), within cells of the Schwann cell lineage [3]. Whereas cNFs typically originate at nerve endings, pNFs tend to develop along large nerve trunks [4, 5]. Importantly, pNFs carry the potential to transform into malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors (MPNSTs) [6], which represent the primary cause of NF1-associated morbidity and mortality. The lifetime risk of developing MPNSTs in NF1 patients is estimated at 8–13% [7]. These aggressive tumors predominantly affect young adults, with a median age of diagnosis around 34 years, and are associated with a poor prognosis (5-year survival rate 49.7%) [8]. Precursor lesions to MPNSTs were first described as “dysplastic neurofibromas” by our group [9], and then more widely cited in the literature as “atypical neurofibromas”. The classification of these tumors has later been redefined by Miettinen et al. who introduced several subtypes of tumors according to histological features [10]: atypical neurofibroma, cellular neurofibroma and ANNUBP. More recently, more accurate histological and molecular criteria were coined for ANNUBP, and the former low-grade MPNSTs were renamed as ANNUBP with increased proliferation (ANNUBP-wip) to emphasize that these tumors are more related to ANNUBP than to MPNSTs in terms of oncogenic stage of development and malignant potential [11]. It remains unclear whether these “atypical” neurofibromas and ANNUBPs (ANF/ANNUBP) represent distinct entities with varying malignant potential or reflect progressive stage in the transformation to MPNST. Early detection and surgical resection of these lesions could prevent malignant progression and improve clinical outcomes [12, 13]. Interestingly, long-term follow-up of resected ANF/ANNUBP with positive margins suggests that not all lesions inevitably progress to MPNST [12]. Genetic analyses have shown that, in addition to NF1 inactivation, homozygous deletions of CDKN2A/B, encoding a negative regulator of the cell cycle, represent an early and recurrent event in ANNUBPs, without other genomic alteration [14–16]. On the contrary, other alterations commonly observed in MPNSTs, such as mutations in EED and SUZ12, components of the polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) are generally absent in ANNUBPs [17–19]. To date, few studies have explored the immune and inflammatory landscape of NF1-associated ANNUBPs and MPNSTs [20, 21]. Histologically, neurofibromas consist of NF1-mutant Schwann cells embedded within a tumor microenvironment composed of fibroblasts, blood vessels, nerve fibers, extracellular matrix, and a diverse population of immune cells. Emerging evidence underscores the role of NF1 haploinsufficiency in immune cells [22, 23], including mast cells [24] and macrophages [25]. A recent study, mostly focusing on immune cells in the MPNST microenvironment, has shown that macrophages may play a role in the immune evasion of MPNST tumor cells and that their presence has a negative prognostic value [26]. However, the involvement of T-lymphocytes remains largely unexplored.

In this study, we comprehensively analyzed the immune landscape of cNFs, pNFs, ANF/ANNUBPs, and MPNSTs derived from individuals with NF1, with a particular focus on macrophages and T-cell infiltration and activation. This highlighted a different immune signature in ANNUBPs, as compared to NFs and MPNSTs, with overexpression of various immune checkpoints ICs.

Materials and methods

Patients and samples

Patients’ characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The cohort of patients with NF1 comprised 79 patients (46 women; mean age 36.1 [range 6–74] years), for which a tumor was removed between January 2015 to January 2021. The study of this cohort was approved by an institutional review board (Comité de protection des personnes—CPP Ile de France [CPP 2015-11-03]). All patients were followed in the neurofibromatosis referral center of the Henri Mondor Hospital (CERENEF, https://cerenef.org/). The diagnosis of NF1 was assessed clinically or with genotyping, using the recently revised consensus criteria [27]. As shown in Suppl. Fig. 1, a total of 127 formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) available tumor samples from the patients were retrospectively included in the study. Because of tumor heterogeneity in some cases, 131 different tumor areas were identified on Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE)-stained slides after review by two of the authors (AB and NO) and analyzed separately. Briefly, tumors were categorized using the 2022 WHO classification of Tumor of Soft Tissue and Bone criteria [28], and the classification proposed by Miettinen et al. and Lucas et al. [10, 11]. Neurofibromas with hypercellularity or cytonuclear atypia only, but without other morphological and molecular abnormality (i.e. lacks histological or molecular features for the diagnosis of ANNUBP) were referred to as “atypical” neurofibromas (ANF). In total, the cohort includes: 81 NFs (32 cNFs, 49 pNFs), 33 ANF/ANNUBPs (ANNUBP-wip, n = 2; ANNUBP, n = 26; atypical NF, n = 4; cellular NF, n = 1) and 17 MPNSTs. A small series of sporadic tumors from 6 non-NF1 patients was also analyzed: 5 ANNUBPs and 1 MPNST (Suppl Fig. 1).

Immunophenotypic characterization

Single and multiplex immunostainings were done on 4 μm-thick Superfrost+slides from FFPE tissue blocks. Details of primary antibodies and staining procedures are provided in Suppl. Table 1. CD3, CD163, PD-L1, pan-HLA ABC, cleaved caspase-3, Cyclin D1/Bcl-1, p16, H3K27me3, CD4, CD8, CD20, CD56 stainings were performed using an automated immunostainer (Leica Bond-Max; Leica Biosystems) or manually (granulysin). The details of the number of cases are provided in the table in Suppl. Fig. 1. Multiplex immunofluorescence was used to analyze PD-1, Galectin-9 (Gal9) and Tim-3 expression in T-lymphocytes (Tim-3/CD3/CD8 and PD-1/CD8/CD3) and macrophages (Gal9/CD3/CD163 and Tim-3/CD163/CD3) in a subset of samples, as detailed in Suppl. Fig. 1. We used the Opal protocol on dewaxed slides after antigen retrieval by heating in a bain marie. Primary antibodies were incubated for 30 min in a humidified chamber at room temperature, followed by application of the appropriate secondary antibody coupled with HRP kit for 30 min. Then, the appropriate fluorophore Opal was applied (PerkinElmer, Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) for 10 min. Multiplex staining was achieved by removing the antibody complex (stripping) between each staining round. At each staining cycle, protein blocking was achieved. At the end of the last staining, DAPI was used as a nuclear counterstain and the section was cover-slipped using Vectashield mounting media. Using this Opal method, three primary antibodies were applied sequentially (Suppl. Table 1). Slides were visualized by confocal microscopy using the LSM 900 Airyscan 2 (Zeiss) microscope with Zen software.

In one ANNUBP sample and peripheral blood mononuclear cells from a healthy donor used as a control, we performed flow cytometry analysis of lymphocytes after cell dissociation using a LSR device (LSR Fortessa X20), at baseline and after T-cell activation using PHA or IL2. We used antibodies to CD3, CD4, CD8, PD-1, and Tim-3 (see Suppl. Fig. 2 for details).

In situ quantification of mononuclear cells and immune cells markers

Densities of mononuclear immune cells infiltrates were first assessed morphologically on HE slides. Briefly, the count was first done manually on a region of interest (ROI) corresponding to 5 high power fields (0.24 mm2), and expressed as a ratio (number of cells per mm2), on 115 samples/tumor areas of the study cohort (32 cNFs, 47 pNFs, 23 ANF/ANNUBPs, 13 MPNSTs). Quantification of T-cells (CD3) and M2 macrophages (CD163) was then performed on slides digitalized using the NanoZoomer HT scanning device (Hamamatsu Photonics) at 20 × or 40 × magnification. Qupath digital software (version 0.2.0-m10) was used to visualize, analyze, and quantify the immune cells subsets. Tumoral areas were reported according to annotations done during slide reviewing. Optical density (OD) sum and Hematoxylin OD mean were applied as parameters to detect positive cells in tumoral areas. To detect positive cells (i.e. marked cells), the Cytoplasm DAB OD mean parameters was applied. Cell densities of immune cells were expressed as a ratio (number of cells per mm2) and evaluated in all available samples/tumor areas. The levels of HLA-ABC and PD-L1 expression were evaluated semi-quantitatively. HLA-ABC staining was scored using a Histoscore (H-score): intensity × proportion of positive cells. The staining intensity was graded as 0 (no staining), 1 (weak), 2 (medium), or 3 (strong) and the percent of positive cells was assessed using 10% increments so that scores could range from 0 to 300. PD-L1 was scored using the Tumor Proportion Score (TPS) system (number of positive tumor cells / total number of viable tumor cells × 100).

Transcriptomic analysis using Nanostring technology

The Nanostring Immuno-Oncology 360 panel, designed to characterize signatures of tumor immune microenvironment and anti-tumor immune response, was used. We analyzed a subgroup of 42 samples, including 5 cNFs, 12 pNFs, 13 ANNUBPs, 2 ANNUBPs-wip, and 10 MPNSTs. Unstained FFPE slides 4–10 μm thick were used for RNA extraction. Direct quantification of the mRNA was achieved according to a Nanostring Research Use only procedure. Briefly, 300 ng of total RNA extracted from FFPE samples was used as a template to detect 770 targets corresponding to 750 mRNAs of interest and 20 housekeeping genes. A human Universal Reference RNA composed of 10 cell lines and a no template control (water) were also hybridized in parallel with samples of interest. All total RNAs were monitored to ensure their quality, purity, and integrity by performing electrophoresis on Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with Pico assays.

Before hybridization, Nanostring positive and negative controls were added to all samples as spike in controls. Probes and mRNA were hybridized overnight at 65 °C. Hybridized samples were further processed on the NanoString nCounter preparation station to remove excess probes and immobilize biotinylated hybrids on a cartridge coated with streptavidin. Cartridges were scanned at maximum scan resolution (555 fields of view [FOV]) on the nCounter Digital Analyzer (NanoString Technologies) to count individual fluorescent barcodes and quantify RNA molecules. Nanostring nSolver (v4.0.7) software was used to control and normalize raw data and the nCounter advanced analysis module (v2.0.134) was used to analyze the data and generate the figures.

Next-generation sequencing

A total of 36 macrodissected tumor sample areas from 28 NF1 patients (2 pNFs, 20 ANF/ANNUBPs, 2 ANNUBPs-wip and 14 MPNSTs) were studied using next-generation deep sequencing of a targeted panel of genes. Experiments were performed at the next-generation sequencing (NGS) facility of Cochin Hospital, Paris (Assistance Publique, Hôpitaux de Paris, France), as described previously [29]. Briefly, all exons and flanking intronic regions of the NF1, CDKN2A, CDKN2B, TP53, SUZ12, EZH2, EED, and SMARCA2 genes were captured with a custom-made panel (KAPA HyperPETE panel, Roche, USA) and sequenced at 500 × depth on NextSeq 500 (Illumina, San Diego, USA). Sequence alignment, variant calling, and variant annotation were performed using the NAVIFY® Mutation Caller pipeline (Roche) and Polyweb pipeline (Paris Cité University). Variants were named at the coding DNA, RNA, and protein levels according to the Human Genome Variation Society recommendations. An assessment of the pathogenicity of variants was performed according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and the Association for Molecular Pathology guidelines. Assessment of variants implication was mainly performed based on population databases (gnomAD), variant databases (ClinVar), and prediction software and knowledge bases, including CADD, SPiP, dbscSNV, Human Splice Finder, and OncoKB (Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center).

Single-cell RNA sequencing

The results presented here are issued from two datasets described in another study [30]. The new analysis consisted of loading the R Data Set files storing the Seurat object within R Studio. The NF1 patient dataset includes 78,518 cells from 18 tumors from patients (9 pNFs, 4 ANNUBPs, 5 MPNSTs). The mouse dataset includes 60,126 cells from 16 tumors from the Prss56Cre/+, Nf1fl/fl, R26tdTom/+ mouse model (2 pNFs, 10 ANNUBPs, 4 MPNSTs). Cell type annotation and two-dimensional projections were already present. No additional processing steps were performed. The figures were built using the Seurat V3 R package.

Data and code availability

The raw sequencing files and count matrices for the single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq) data are available in the ArrayExpress repository, under the accession number E-MTAB-14222. Additional count matrices were downloaded from the NCBI GEO database: GSE165826 for ANNUBP sample GSM5050721 [31]; GSE179033 for 9 pNF and 4 MPNST samples, corresponding to GSM404032 to GSM404044 [32]. Their computational processing is accessible at https://github.com/audrey-onfroy/Brunet_et_al_scRNASeq_NF1_2026. The specific version of the analysis presented here is stored on Zenodo under record ID 19495087. The Singularity container to render the notebooks is accessible on Zenodo, with record ID 15512051 (https://zenodo.org/records/15512051). The record also contains a table summarizing the version of all the R packages involved in the study.

Statistical analysis

All graphics and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 Software. Non-parametric Mann Whitney student t-test was used. The threshold for statistical significance was set to p-values < 0.05.

Results

ANF/ANNUBPs are characterized by a marked infiltration of mononuclear cells

Histologic exploration reveals that all analyzed ANF/ANNUBP samples (n = 23) were infiltrated by small mononuclear cells with round monomorphic nuclei (median 976.9 [80.52–1575]) which were more abundant than in cNFs (median 53.68 [13.42–187.9] p < 0.0001), pNFs (median 89.46 [8.946–255.0], p < 0.0001), and MPNSTs (median 22.37 [8.946–250.5], p < 0.0001). These cells infiltrated the tumors diffusely (Fig. 1).

To characterize the mononuclear cell infiltrates, we performed immunohistochemistry analysis, which revealed that the majority of mononuclear cells were T-cells (CD3+), predominantly CD8+ effector T-cells, along with M2 macrophages (CD163+) (Fig. 2A and B). In 34% of samples, CD3+cells formed small aggregates, notably found in ANF/ANNUBPs (15%). In contrast, B-cells (CD20+) and NK cells (CD56+) were sparse (data not shown). Quantification of the infiltrates demonstrated significantly higher densities of both CD3+T-cells and CD163+ macrophages in ANF/ANNUBPs compared to cNFs (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.0001, respectively) and pNFs (p < 0.0001 and p < 0.0001, respectively) (Fig. 2C and D). Compared with MPNSTs, we observed similar results for CD163+macrophages (p < 0.0085) and CD3+T-cells (p = 0.0036). Similar results were observed in sporadic tumors (data not shown), although the small sample size precluded statistical analysis.

Dysplastic neurofibromas exhibit a distinct inflammatory and immune transcriptomic profile compared to cNFs, pNFs and MPNSTs

Using NanoString technology and the IO360 PanCancer panel, we performed an unsupervised transcriptomic analysis of cNFs, pNFs, ANF/ANNUBPs, ANNUBPs-wip and MPNSTs. This identified three clusters (c1–3) of tumors (Fig. 3A): (i) c1 comprises all cNFs and most pNFs, (ii) c2 comprises ANNUBPs and ANNUBPs-wip, along with 2 pNFs suggesting transcriptomic commonalities, and (iii) c3 comprises MPNSTs, which further separated into two subclusters (c3a and c3b, green frame, Fig. 3A).

Within the ANF/ANNUBP group, ANFs are identified using a star. MPNSTs cluster in two subgroups, as shown in the green frames, with two tumors in the lower part of the heatmap showing mostly an overexpression of genes involved in tumor progression (remodeling and metastasis, cell proliferation, metabolic stress, angiogenesis, DNA damage repair). Pathway overexpression analysis of c2 (ANNUBPs and ANNUBPs-wip) revealed higher scores in myeloid compartment, consistent with increased macrophage infiltration of those tumors (Fig. 3B, lower right panel). No major differences in lymphoid compartment were observed among tumor types analyzed globally (data not shown). Tumors from c2 showed global upregulation of immune and inflammatory signatures including: interferon signaling, cytokines/chemokines and costimulatory signaling, immune cell adhesion and migration, cytotoxicity and antigen presentation, autophagy and NF-κB, MAPK, PI3K-Akt, and JAK-STAT signaling (Fig. 3A, red frame and Fig. 3B upper and lower left panel). Notably, MAPK pathway activation was particularly enriched in ANNUBPs (Fig. 3A, blue frame). The c3 signature differed from c2 and exhibited higher proliferation scores (potentially in both tumor and immune cells), accompanied by upregulation of signatures associated with metabolic stress, angiogenesis and DNA damage repair, all hallmarks of malignancy (Fig. 3A).

Analysis of differentially expressed genes, using ANF/ANNUBPs as the reference, identified variations in key factors related to immune cells, cytotoxicity, and apoptosis. First, we identified variations in the expression of genes involved in IC regulation (Table 2, Suppl. Table 2): regarding inhibitory IC, HAVCR2, encoding for the co-inhibitory Tim-3 receptor, was one of the most differentially overexpressed genes in ANF/ANNUBPs. Both HAVCR2 and LGALS9, which encodes for Gal9, the main ligand of Tim-3, were found to be overexpressed in ANF/ANNUBPs as compared to MPNSTs. SIRPA, encodes for SIRP-alpha, a receptor expressed by macrophages and involved in the “don’t eat me signal”. It was also among the most differentially expressed genes, and was overexpressed in ANF/ANNUBPs. Contrasting with the Tim-3-Gal9 pathway, CD47, which encodes for the ligand of SIRP-alpha, was found to be underexpressed. Among other inhibitory ICs, VSIR was overexpressed as compared to MPNSTs, while PDCD1 encoding for PD-1 and CTLA4, both of which are targetable ICs, were overexpressed as compared to pNFs and cNFs. Next, we identified variations in cytotoxic gene expression (Table 2). We mostly found an overexpression of GLNY, which encodes for the cytotoxic effector Granulysin, in ANF/ANNUBPs. Although the global cytotoxicity score was higher in ANF/ANNUBPs as compared to the other tumor subtypes, no other individual gene involved in tumor cell cytotoxicity was found to be differentially overexpressed at a significant level. Because apoptosis scores appeared to be higher in NFs and ANF/ANNUBPs and ANNUBPs-wip, we also focused on caspase gene expression. A higher expression of caspase-3 mRNA (CASP3) was found in ANF/ANNUBPs as compared to pNFs and cNFs but the difference was not statistically significant, whereas in MPNSTs, CASP3 appeared to be overexpressed with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.00922) (Table 2). Overall, these data show a distinct transcriptomic signature in ANF/ANNUBPs compared to pNFS and cNFs and MPNSTs, with particular overexpression of genes involved in immunity and inflammation, and expression of IC.

In ANF/ANNUBPs, Tim-3 and Gal9 are co-expressed by CD163+ macrophages, while PD-1 is expressed by both CD8+ and CD8- T-lymphocytes

We next performed proteomic validation in ANF/ANNUBPs for the following candidate markers: Tim-3/Gal9/CD163, Tim-3/Gal9/CD3, and CD3/CD8/PD-1 using multiplex and single-staining immunohistochemistry. Although PDCD1 was not overexpressed at the transcriptomic level, we included it in the analysis due to its immunological and clinical relevance. First, we assessed Tim-3 and Gal9 expression in T- cells and macrophages across cNFs, pNFs, ANF/ANNUBPs, and MPNSTs from our cohort. In all cases, Tim-3 expression was detected in CD163+ macrophages but not in CD3+ T- cells (Fig. 4A). Gal9 was also expressed in CD163+macrophages, predominantly displaying nuclear localization (Fig. 4B). In parallel, we found that PD-1 was expressed by the majority of CD3+ T-lymphocytes, including both CD8+ and CD8- subsets (Fig. 4C and D). Flow cytometry further confirmed PD-1 expression in CD3+ T- cells, within both CD4+and CD8+subsets (Suppl. Fig. 2A). Regarding PD-L1, the TPS was generally low in both MPNSTs (mean: 6.7%, median: 0%, range: 0–75%) and ANF/ANNUBP (mean: 2.8%, median: 0%, range: 0–10%), indicating minimal expression in tumor cells. An exception was a single MPNST sample with a TPS of 75% (Suppl. Fig. 2B, right panel). Among pNFs, only one sample exhibited PD-L1 expression with a TPS of 10%. Given the elevated expression of MAPK pathway genes in ANF/ANNUBPs identified via the Nanostring panel, we further examined the expression of Cyclin D1/Bcl-1, a known downstream target of the MAPK pathway. Cyclin D1 was not only localized to the nuclei of atypical spindle (tumor) cells but was also present in a subset of mononuclear cells within the tumor microenvironment (Suppl. Fig. 2C), suggesting active RAS/MAPK signaling.

HLA class I expression increases with tumor malignancy, whereas cleaved caspase-3 is highly expressed in ANF/ANNUBPs and MPNSTs

Next, we investigated the expression of HLA class I molecules across all tumor samples using immunohistochemistry. HLA class I expression was detected across nearly all tumor types, with a tendency to higher levels observed in MPNSTs and ANF/ANNUBPs compared to pNFs and cNFs with no statistically significant difference (Fig. 5A–C). Granulysin expression was detected in a few scattered immune cells within the infiltrate from all tumor subtypes (data not shown). We also assessed the expression of cleaved caspase-3, a key marker of apoptosis, which was elevated in both ANF/ANNUBPs and MPNSTs compared to NFs (p = 0.0045 and p = 0.0235, respectively), although no significant difference was observed between ANF/ANNUBPs and MPNSTs (Fig. 5D–F). Apoptotic scoring using the Nanostring platform suggested higher apoptosis levels in ANF/ANNUBPs relative to all other tumor subtypes. Notably, this score was markedly reduced in MPNSTs, consistent with enhanced cell survival and resistance to cell death in malignant cells, potentially reflecting mechanisms of immune escape.

scRNAseq reveals overexpression of immune checkpoint program in ANNUBPs

To further characterize the immune landscape of ANNUBPs, we performed scRNAseq on 4 tumor samples (3 ANNUBPs and 1 MPNST) and integrated our dataset with publicly available scRNAseq data [31, 32]. This analysis revealed that all analyzed tumors, including ANNUBPs and MPNSTs, were infiltrated by immune cells, particularly macrophages (Fig. 6). In ANNUBPs, macrophages exhibited high expression of various IC genes (Fig. 6, red frame). Notably, we observed specific overexpression of HAVCR2 and LGALS9 in macrophages, but not in tumor cells. In addition, SIRPA and VSIR transcripts were upregulated in macrophages, while CD47, a key “don’t eat me” signal, was broadly expressed across all cell types, including tumor cells. Although we detected PD-1 protein in T cells from ANF/ANNUBP samples, the corresponding transcripts and those of its ligands (PD-L1, PD-L2), as well as other ICs such as LAG3 and TIGIT, were only weakly expressed in our scRNAseq dataset. This discrepancy suggests potential post-transcriptional regulation or protein persistence. Finally, we also found high expression of class I HLA genes in tumor cells of ANF/ANNUBPs (Fig. 6, blue frame), indicating potential antigen presentation capacity. Furthermore, consistent with enhanced MAPK signaling and cell cycle activity, we observed strong expression of CCND1 (encoding Cyclin D1) in both tumor and macrophages in ANF/ANNUBPs (Fig. 6, green frame). scRNAseq thus confirmed the expression of various ICs together with Cyclin D1 by macrophages in ANF/ANNUBPs.

The Prss56Cre/+, Nf1fl/fl, R26tdTom/+ mouse model also features immune cell peak and IC expression at the ANNUBP stage

We next investigated the expression levels of the same set of genes in the tumors from the Prss56Cre/+, Nf1fl/fl, R26tdTom/+ mouse model [33]. In this model, biallelic inactivation of the Nf1 gene and activation of fluorescent reporter Tomato (Tom) occurs specifically in Prss56-expressing boundary cap cells and their derivatives. Notably, unlike in NF1 patients, the tumor microenvironment in this model remains wild type. To contextualize our findings, we explored the published scRNAseq data from Radomska et al. study [30], comprising 2 pNFs, 10 ANF/ANNUBPs (termed as “dysplastic NF” [dyNF] in the published manuscript), and 4 MPNSTs mouse samples (Suppl. Fig. 3A–B). In this model, the immune infiltration by macrophages and T-cells has been described in ANF/ANNUBPs [30]. The model recapitulates the overexpression of HAVCR2 in a subset of macrophages within ANF/ANNUBPs and further reveals its expression in dendritic cells, distinguishing these tumors from both pNFs and MPNSTs (Suppl. Fig. 3C). The ICs VSIR, LGALS9, SIRPA, and CD47 are overexpressed in macrophages from ANF/ANNUBPs compared to pNFs. However, contrasting with scRNAseq data for patients, high expression of these genes is maintained in MPNSTs (Suppl. Fig. 3C, red frame). Similarly, CD47 was expressed more variably by different cell types and tumor stages. Unlike PDCD1 and LAG3, which were not detected in human scRNAseq data, we observed over-expression of PDCD1 and LAG3 genes in the T cells of ANF/ANNUBPs from the mouse model. Finally, the mouse model recapitulates the overexpression of CCND1 in tumor cells and macrophages from ANF/ANNUBPs. Altogether, despite some discrepancies, our observations show that the mouse model recapitulates most findings identified in NF1 patients, highlighting its potential for future preclinical trials involving IC inhibitors.

Deep targeted gene mutational analysis reveals an absence of MPNST-associated alterations in ANF/ANNUBPs, even at low frequency

To investigate the genetic alterations underlying ANF/ANNUBPs and MPNSTs in this cohort, we performed deep targeted NGS on a subgroup of macrodissected tumor areas (n = 36), which included regions of pNF, ANF/ANNUBP, or ANNUBP-wip contiguous with, or transitioning into, MPNST. Multiple tumor subtypes were sampled from 6 patients, with 2 to 4 samples per patient, and, in 4 cases, different histologic areas from the same tumor were collected and analyzed (Fig. 7A). This approach aimed to detect minor malignant subclones potentially present within ANF/ANNUBPs regions and to explore potential associations between gene alterations and the degree of T-cell infiltration, particularly genetic aberrations known to influence anti-tumor immunity in other types of cancers, such as inactivation of the PRC2 complex and CDKN2A. In addition to interrogating the NF1 locus, we focused on genes implicated in the malignant progression of pNFs to MPNSTs: SUZ12, EED, and EZH2 (components of the PRC2 complex), as well as CDKN2A, SMARCA2, and TP53. CDKN2A deletions were identified in 6 of 19 ANF/ANNUBPs (31%) and 9 of 14 MPNSTs (64%). TP53 pathogenic variants were found in 2 MPNSTs. Notably, in one case (patient 25), the same TP53 mutation was detected at a high allele frequency (56%) in the adjacent ANF/ANNUBPs region, suggesting clonal evolution. Except for 2 ANF/ANNUBPs with heterozygous SUZ12 deletions, no mutations in SUZ12, EED, or EZH2 were detected in ANF/ANNUBPs, even at low allele frequencies. Among MPNSTs, 3 harbored SUZ12 losses (one with a homozygous deletion), and two showed homozygous deletions of EED. These were associated with loss of nuclear H3K27me3 expression, as confirmed by immunohistochemistry. Additionally, 1 MPNST exhibited a SMARCA2 missense mutation alongside homozygous EED deletion. Importantly, we observed that tumors with PRC2 loss of function (loss of H3K27me3 expression) display lower densities of CD163 + macrophages and CD3 + T-cells (although this trend was not statistically significant for the latter) than those with preserved H3K27me3 expression (Fig. 7B and C). Interestingly, the loss of p16 appeared to accentuate this effect synergistically, as illustrated in Suppl. Fig. 4.

Discussion

MPNSTs are a major cause of morbidity in NF1, yet there is currently no effective treatment. Immunotherapy, particularly through PD-1 blockade to stimulate T cell-mediated anti-tumor responses, has revolutionized the treatment of many cancers. However, the response to IC inhibitors has been variable and the cohorts included few or no MPNSTs [34–36]. To date, few studies have reported cases of MPNST treated successfully by PD1-blockade [37–40].

In this study, we demonstrate that ANF/ANNUBPs possess a unique immune cell composition and inflammatory signature compared to cNFs, pNFs, and MPNSTs. ANF/ANNUBPs exhibit upregulated immune and inflammatory pathways, increased infiltration by CD3+ tumor-infiltrating T lymphocytes (TILs), and CD163+ tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Clinically, this observation raises the question of whether it could account for the higher 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose uptake seen in ANF/ANNUBPs compared to NFs, albeit still lower than in MPNSTs [41], potentially reflecting the metabolic activity of activated immune cells. These observations are in agreement with the study by Suppiah et al. which showed that “pre-malignant NF”, as well as subsets of MPNST, disclose higher levels of immune cell infiltration, mostly corresponding to T-cells and macrophages [19]. Zhang et al. also demonstrated T cell and macrophage infiltration in MPNSTs, but unlike our findings, they did not observe a peak in infiltration in ANF/ANNUBPs; the highest densities of TAMs and TILs were found in MPNSTs without PRC2 inactivation [26]. Importantly, these tumors retain and upregulate HLA class I expression during malignant progression, as also demonstrated in the Zhang study. We also observed higher levels of apoptosis in ANF/ANNUBPs and MPNSTs compared with cNFs and pNFs, as evidenced by cleaved caspase-3 staining. These features are consistent with active tumor cell killing and apoptosis, possibly mediated by CD8 + T cells. Granulysin, a cytotoxic effector from NK and CD8 + T cells, was upregulated in transcriptomics but not confirmed at the protein level due to antibody limitations. The antibody we used only recognizes one of the two isoforms of granulysin described to date [42].

Altogether these findings suggest that NF1-associated tumors initiate a specific immune response at the pre-malignant stage, potentially contributing to the containment of malignant transformation. This immune control may be compromised in certain patients during tumor progression, resulting in immune escape and development of MPNSTs. Notably, ANF/ANNUBPs and ANNUBPs-wip show comparable T-cell and macrophage infiltration and cluster together in unsupervised analyses, supporting their biological similarity. Furthermore, Röhrich et al. also reported common methylation profiles between these tumor types, supporting their molecular commonalities [43].

Beyond TILs, TAMs are gaining attention in immunotherapy research. Our data show that macrophages dominate tumor infiltrates and peak in ANF/ANNUBPs. Prada et al. identified Iba1 + macrophages in both human and mouse NFs, with the proportion increasing during progression to MPNSTs [25]. Other mouse models suggest that macrophages contribute to NF initiation and growth via inflammatory gene induction [44, 45]. Macrophage depletion in advanced NFs reduced tumor size, highlighting their functional relevance [25]. In our study, we used CD163 as a marker of M2-like macrophages [46]. Given their plasticity, TAMs might promote both tumor development and immune surveillance at different stages, a hypothesis warranting further study. It is important to note that in their study, Zhang et al. showed that the presence of CD163 + macrophages has prognostic value in MPNSTs, suggesting a role in immune evasion [26]. Only a few studies have focused on the expression of ICs in NF1-associated tumors at the protein level, mostly focusing on PD-1 and PD-L1, and to the best of our knowledge, none included cases of ANF/ANNUBPs [20, 21]. Several IC molecules were expressed in ANF/ANNUBPs in our study. CD3 + T cells expressed PD-1, while CD163 + macrophages expressed both Tim-3 and its ligand Gal9. The expression of PD-1 by T cells infiltrating MPNSTs was highlighted in the study by Zhang et al. suggesting that it is maintained during malignant transformation. These data raise the possibility that immunotherapy could bolster anti-tumor immunity and delay/prevent malignant progression of ANF/ANNUBPs. Case reports suggest anti-PD-1 therapy may benefit some MPNST patients, including two cases with metabolic responses to pembrolizumab [39, 40]. However, clinical trials remain scarce due to the rarity of MPNST. A phase II trial of pembrolizumab in MPNSTs ended early (NCT02691026), and a phase I trial testing neoadjuvant nivolumab plus ipilimumab is still ongoing (NCT04465643).

Tim-3 (encoded by HAVCR2) and Gal9 (LGALS9) are promising targets for cancer immunotherapy. Tim-3, originally found on Th1 and CD8 + T cells [47], also regulates immune activity in other cells [48]. Inactivating HAVCR2 mutations are linked to severe inflammatory syndromes (promotion of hemophagocytic syndrome) in subcutaneous T-cell lymphomas [49], emphasizing its IC function. Tim-3/Gal9 interactions are involved in immune responses across various cancers [50], including gliomas [51] and pancreatic tumors [52], probably because Gal9 induces M2 macrophage polarization [53, 54]. We found HAVCR2 among the top overexpressed genes in ANF/ANNUBPs versus pNFs, cNFs and MPNSTs. HAVCR2 and LGALS9 were co-expressed in CD163 + macrophages, suggesting a potential autocrine/paracrine loop, as previously described in acute myeloid leukemias [55]. Gal9 may further drive M2 polarization, promoting immune evasion—a hypothesis requiring functional validation.

PRC2 loss, a hallmark of HG-MPNSTs [17, 56], is absent in ANF/ANNUBPs and ANNUBPs-wip in our high-depth targeted sequencing (500 ×), confirming previous reports [18, 57]. In agreement, PRC2 alterations are likely late events in MPNST development [19]. However, rare malignant subclones might exist within ANF/ANNUBPs and be controlled by immune cells. While we did not observe SMARCA2 alterations in ANF/ANNUBPs and ANNUBPs-wip, Pemov et al. reported such changes in some cases [16]. Yan et al. have shown that PRC2 inactivation in MPNSTs leads to immune evasion by reducing antigen presentation and immune infiltration [58]. Using RNA sequencing, Cortes-Ciriano et al. showed that MPNSTs with PRC2 inactivation had lower immune cell scores and lower levels of activation of the adaptative immune system [59], in agreement with the findings of Zhang et al., which found lower TILs and TAMs levels in MPNSTs with PRC2 inactivation [26]. Suppiah et al. identified 2 MPNST subgroups: one with PRC2 alterations and SHH pathway activation, and another with Wnt pathway activation, the latter enriched in immune cells [19]. Therefore, it is conceivable that the loss of PRC2 function contributes to immune escape at the MPNST stage, as previously suggested by others [60]. In our series, despite the small sample size involved, we also observed that MPNSTs with PRC2 inactivation have lower levels of infiltration by TAMs and TILs than those with preserved H3K27me3 expression. Furthermore, it is possible that p16 also contributes to tumor escape, as in our series of MPNSTs, it appeared to act synergistically with PRC2 loss of function in reducing TAMs and TILs infiltration levels. However, this hypothesis needs to be verified by studying a larger cohort of tumors.

Beyond the potential role of TAMs and TILs in immune surveillance in ANF/ANNUBPs, our findings raise many other pathophysiological questions. Whether NF1 haploinsufficiency may alter immune cell function is an interesting question. Brosseau et al. found hyperproliferative T cells in NF1 patients, suggesting enhanced immunity [61]. While macrophage alterations due to NF1 haploinsufficiency are unexplored, they could affect tumor progression. ANF/ANNUBPs are frequent in NF1 but rare sporadically, implying a role for host immune context. We did not observe more T cells and macrophages in sporadic ANF/ANNUBPs versus sporadic NFs and MPNSTs, but the sample size limits conclusions. Dodd et al. used a haploinsufficient Nf1 ± mouse model and found increased proportion of hematopoietic cells (CD45 +) and faster tumor development, although this model lacks the dysplastic stage [62]. Our scRNAseq and Nanostring analyses suggest active immune cells in ANF/ANNUBPs, including high CCND1 expression in macrophages and MAPK pathway enrichment. It should be noted that a greater infiltration of immune cells, primarily T lymphocytes, has been observed in low-grade gliomas compared to high-grade gliomas in patients with NF1. This further underscores the potential role of immune cells in regulating malignant transformation in the context of NF1 deficiency [63].

In conclusion, we propose that immune surveillance in ANF/ANNUBPs constrains tumor progression, and IC targeting might provide an immunopreventive strategy. However, given the uncertain nature of ANF/ANNUBPs and the potential toxicity of immunotherapies, identifying biomarkers to stratify risk is essential. Future treatments may aim to enhance local immune responses without systemic immunosuppression, offering hope for managing unresectable ANF/ANNUBPs. Because the Prss56Cre/+, Nf1fl/fl, R26tdTom/+ mouse model reproduces the immune cell peak with the expression of various ICs that we observed in human ANF/ANNUBPs, it could be used for future in vivo preclinical studies.
