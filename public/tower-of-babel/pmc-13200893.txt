Trigeminal Neuralgia After a Cerebrospinal Fluid Leak: A Case Report
Authors: Fu-Wei Su, Hsinlin T. Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage can be difficult to localize and may result in unexplained intracranial hypotension with intractable positional headache. Loss of CSF cushioning can cause brain sagging, leading to compression and traction of cranial nerves within the brain cisterns and mimicking Chiari malformation. This case report describes a patient presenting with trigeminal facial pain and positional headache who was ultimately diagnosed with a thoracic spinal CSF–venous fistula. Both headache and facial pain resolved following surgical intervention. This report highlights the importance of advanced diagnostic imaging and multidisciplinary management.

Trigeminal neuralgia (TN) is typically attributed to vascular compression of the trigeminal nerve but may also arise from infections, autoimmune conditions, or space-occupying lesions.1–3 Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage is commonly associated with positional headaches localized to the occipital, temporal, or frontal regions. Rarely, CSF leakage can manifest as unilateral facial pain or mimic other cranial neuropathies.4 In rare instances, CSF leakage causes an acquired Chiari malformation, where pressure gradient changes induce brain sagging, compression, and traction of cranial nerves.5

We describe a rare case of TN attributed to CSF leakage and an acquired Chiari malformation, outlining our diagnostic reasoning and the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying TN after a CSF leak. Written Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) authorization was obtained from the patient, including permission to publish relevant clinical details and imaging.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 45-year-old man with a history of obstructive sleep apnea was referred for intractable left-sided TN that developed after a fall one year prior, superimposed on a five-year history of positional left occipital headaches. He described paroxysmal, sharp, stabbing pain over the left midface and jaw within the trigeminal nerve distribution, exacerbated by activities like chewing and tooth brushing. His daily positional occipital headaches improved with recumbency.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the brain revealed radiographic features of markedly decreased CSF volume and spontaneous intracranial hypotension (SIH). These included diffuse pachymeningeal enhancement, engorgement of the pituitary gland, effacement of basal cisterns, and brainstem sagging with 8 mm cerebellar tonsillar ectopia below the foramen magnum, consistent with a Chiari I malformation (Figure 1A, 1B). Moreover, images demonstrated vascular contact of the left trigeminal nerve by the left superior cerebellar artery (Figure 1C, 1D). Whole-spine MRI and conventional computed tomography myelography (CTM) were unrevealing for a CSF leak.

B, Markedly reduced CSF volume with brain sagging (white arrowheads). C, Superior cerebellar artery (yellow arrow) in contact with the left trigeminal nerve (white arrow). D, Reduced CSF volume in the prepontine cistern, with suspected neurovascular conflict involving the trigeminal nerve (white arrow) and the superior cerebellar artery (yellow arrow).

Due to the intractable and agonizing nature of the facial pain, the patient underwent a left-sided percutaneous balloon compression (PBC) of the Gasserian ganglion as a palliative intervention. Although this procedure provided significant immediate relief from the facial pain by interrupting the neural pain signaling pathways, it did not address the underlying CSF volume depletion, as evidenced by the persistence of his positional occipital headaches.

Neuroradiology was consulted for digital subtraction myelography (DSM) and photon-counting detector CTM to further evaluate for CSF leakage. DSM performed in the right lateral decubitus position demonstrated a small contrast outpouching at the 10th thoracic (T10) spinal nerve root and an area of irregular contrast enhancement (Figure 2A). Subsequent photon-counting detector CTM in the same position showed an irregular outpouching involving the right T10 nerve root sleeve (Figure 2B), along with rapid contrast accumulation in the right renal collecting system (Figure 2C). Although no definitive dural CSF leakage points or a paraspinal fistula were identified, the combination of the nerve root sleeve abnormality and asymmetric rapid contrast drainage to the right renal system raised the possibility of a right-sided CSF–venous fistula at the T10–T11 level.

B, CTM showing an outpouching involving the right T10 nerve root sleeve (white arrow). C, Contrast in the right renal collecting system. These findings raise concern for a right-sided CSF–venous fistula at the T10–T11 level (yellow arrow).

The patient underwent a T9–T10 laminectomy to explore suspected pathology. A dissection plane was developed around the right T10 nerve root, revealing a venous fistula along the distal axilla within the T10 foramen. This venous channel was then cauterized with bipolar forceps and sharply divided with microscissors.

The patient experienced rapid and sustained resolution of his occipital headaches and “brain fog” following the surgery. Facial pain remained controlled. As an expected sequela of the PBC, the patient experienced mild residual paresthesia in the tongue and left mandibular branch of the trigeminal nerve. At a two-week follow-up, he reported only mild frontal headaches and incisional soreness, which improved steadily. He resumed regular physical activity with minimal discomfort two months postoperatively. A repeat MRI demonstrated improvement in the pachymeningeal enhancement, indicating the resolution of intracranial hypotension.

DISCUSSION

TN typically results from neurovascular conflict at the root entry zone (REZ), where the trigeminal nerve emerges from the pons of the brainstem.1–3 Although CSF leakage rarely causes facial pain,4,6–8 Atkinson et al reported seven cases of acquired Chiari malformation secondary to spontaneous spinal CSF leakage and SIH, one of whom presented with facial pain.5 In addition, Cheshire et al reported a patient who presented with SIH and TN caused by a cervical nerve root sleeve leak.9 MRI images revealed cerebellar tonsillar ectopia. A potential mechanism for TN was that CSF volume depletion decreased brain buoyancy, resulting in brain sagging and altering the anatomical proximity between adjacent blood vessels and the trigeminal nerve REZ.

The facial pain of the current case resolved following surgical ligation of the CSF-venous fistula. Similar cases of pressure gradient-induced TN were reported by Badary et al, who reviewed 19 TN cases associated with Chiari malformation, 4 of whom had concurrent hydrocephalus with enlarged lateral and third ventricles, exerting a significant downward force that displaced the brainstem caudally. Their TN improved after ventriculoperitoneal shunting to relieve brainstem crowding and downward shifting.10 Taken together, a downward CSF pressure gradient, whether driven by elevated pressure pushing downward (hydrocephalus) or by reduced CSF buoyancy leading to brain sagging (CSF leak), can result in similar neurovascular crowding, and subsequent compression and traction of cranial nerves.

Papanastassiou et al outlined four hypothesized pathomechanisms for these rare cases of TN10,11: (1) increased neurovascular contact at the REZ, (2) stretching of the trigeminal nerve leading to demyelination, (3) localized microischemia, and (4) a downward anatomical shift that causes crowding around the foramen magnum, resulting in direct compression of the brainstem.10,11 Given this mechanical etiology, surgery aims to relieve neural compression. Accordingly, Badary et al reported that 14 of the 19 reviewed patients underwent decompressive surgery or shunting, and all achieved improvement or resolution of their TN.10

Our case highlights a remote T10 CSF-venous fistula causing intracranial hypotension, brain sagging, and symptomatic TN. This distinction is clinically critical, as CSF-venous fistulas typically respond poorly to epidural blood patches and require targeted surgical ligation.6,7 Fistula closure normalized CSF pressure gradient, structurally reversing the Chiari configuration and relieving TN, as confirmed by postoperative MRI.

We considered that anatomical asymmetry was likely the cause of the patient’s unilateral TN. In support of our hypothesis, Badary et al reported that only 2 of their 19 reviewed cases (10.5%) of Chiari-associated TN cases present bilaterally.10 In addition, Liu et al demonstrated that TN patients have significantly smaller posterior fossae and cerebellopontine cisterns on the painful side, compared to the healthy side.12 Therefore, as systemic brain sagging exacerbates baseline crowding, the mechanical threshold for nerve compression is breached asymmetrically, exclusively affecting the side with less anatomical reserve.

Localizing a CSF leak remains challenging.6,8 Although whole-spine MRI can seldom definitively localize the leak, it is usually the first imaging step to evaluate the leak type and guide advanced myelography based on the presence or absence of a spinal longitudinal epidural collection (SLEC).7,8,13,14 SLEC-positive scans suggest ventral or diverticular leaks, whereas SLEC-negative scans strongly indicate a CSF-venous fistula. Because fistulas drain directly into veins without forming epidural fluid, standard MRI or CTM often appears deceptively normal.13 Detecting fistulas requires advanced modalities—like DSM for real-time temporal resolution and photon-counting detector CTM for 3D spatial resolution—performed by an expert neuroradiologist using lateral decubitus positioning and provocative maneuvers (eg, Valsalva). Given this diagnostic complexity, expert neuroradiological consultation is crucial for optimal planning.7,8,13,14

Taken together, in patients with refractory TN and positional headaches, evaluation for a CSF leak is warranted. In selected cases, repair of the leak may relieve both the Chiari malformation and facial pain, obviating the need for craniocervical dural decompression.
