Clinical subtypes and prognosis of new-onset atrial fibrillation in critically ill patients
Authors: Yuanhui Sheng, Xixi Liao, Feng Li, Shan Xu, Dan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNew-onset atrial fibrillation (NOAF) is a common cardiovascular complication in critically ill patients and is consistently associated with adverse outcomes. However, substantial heterogeneity exists in its clinical presentation and prognosis. This study aimed to identify distinct clinical subtypes of NOAF and evaluate their prognostic and management-related implications.MethodsAdult NOAF patients were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database. Background

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is one of the most common cardiac arrhythmias and a major contributor to cardiovascular morbidity and mortality worldwide [1]. Over the past 30 years, the global prevalence of AF has continued to rise, imposing an increasing clinical burden [2]. New-onset atrial fibrillation (NOAF) defined as AF occurring for the first time in patients without a prior history of the condition, frequently develops during acute hospitalization and critical illness [3]. In the intensive care unit (ICU), NOAF is particularly common, with reported incidence rates ranging from 5% to 46% depending on underlying conditions and illness severity [[4], [5], [6]]. NOAF occurs most frequently in patients with sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome, or following cardiac surgery [[7], [8], [9]]. NOAF is consistently associated with adverse outcomes, including higher in-hospital mortality, prolonged mechanical ventilation, longer ICU stays, and increased thromboembolic risk [[10], [11], [12]].

Despite growing recognition of its prognostic significance, NOAF in critically ill patients continues to pose substantial challenges for clinical risk assessment and management. Importantly, accumulating evidence suggests that NOAF in critically ill patients represents a clinically heterogeneous condition rather than a uniform arrhythmic entity. Substantial variability exists in onset patterns, ventricular rate, hemodynamic impact, treatment response, and clinical outcomes across individuals [13]. However, current diagnostic definitions rely primarily on electrocardiographic criteria and do not adequately capture the underlying clinical and pathophysiological complexity of NOAF, potentially obscuring clinically meaningful subgroups with distinct prognoses and therapeutic responsiveness. The development of NOAF in critical illness is multifactorial and may involve systemic inflammation, volume overload, acid-base disturbances, and electrolyte imbalance [14], with considerable interpatient variability in triggers and disease trajectories [15]. Nevertheless, most prior studies have treated NOAF as a homogeneous clinical entity [[16], [17], [18]] and emerging evidence suggests that a “one-size-fits-all” management strategy may be suboptimal [19].

To address this unmet clinical need, data-driven phenotyping approaches have been increasingly applied in critical care to characterize heterogeneous syndromes such as sepsis, ARDS, and acute kidney injury [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]]. These approaches leverage multidimensional clinical and laboratory data to identify subgroups with distinct biological profiles and prognostic trajectories. However, to date, no studies have systematically applied such methods to characterize clinical subtypes of NOAF in critically ill patients.

Accordingly, we conducted a retrospective analysis of a large cohort of ICU patients who developed NOAF during hospitalization using data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) and MIMIC-III databases. By integrating routinely available clinical and laboratory variables, we sought to identify reproducible NOAF subtypes and to compare their clinical characteristics, management patterns, and prognostic outcomes. Through this approach, we aimed to elucidate the intrinsic heterogeneity of NOAF in critically ill patients and to provide clinically interpretable insights that may support individualized risk stratification and inform future phenotype-guided management strategies.

Methods

Data source and study design

This was a retrospective, data-driven cohort study conducted using two large, publicly available critical care databases, the MIMIC-IV (version 2.2) and MIMIC-III. Access to the database and data extraction were performed after completion of the required training course and certification from the National Institutes of Health (Certification No. 36743986).

Study population and diagnostic criteria

Patients with NOAF were identified from the MIMIC-III and MIMIC-IV database. NOAF was defined based on hourly rhythm documentation in nursing notes, as the transition from sinus rhythm at ICU admission to new-onset atrial fibrillation occurring at least 2 h later. Exclusion criteria were [1]: age <18 years [2]; ICU stay <24 h. For patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the first admission was included in the analysis.

Patients meeting the criteria from the MIMIC-IV 2.2 database were adopted as the discovery set. Clinical subtypes were determined using unsupervised learning. Subsequently, the identified clinical subtypes were employed as outcome labels and randomly divided into a training set and an internal validation set at a ratio of 7:3 to establish the predictive model for clinical subtypes. Patients meeting the criteria from the MIMIC-III database served as the external validation set.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality. Secondary outcomes included the length of ICU stay, in-hospital stay, ICU mortality, and in-hospital mortality, 90-day mortality and 365-day mortality.

Data extraction and subtyping variables

All variables were extracted using Structured Query Language (SQL). Extracted data included demographic information (age, weight), laboratory results (complete blood count, liver/kidney function, coagulation profile), blood gas analysis, vital signs, comorbidities (Hypertension, diabetes, chronic heart failure, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI),etc.), severity scores (Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA)), organ support (ventilation, vasoactive use, and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT)), pharmacologic management (β-blockers, diltiazem, propofol, dexmedetomidine, magnesium sulfate, and potassium chloride) and clinical outcomes (ICU mortality, in-hospital mortality, 28-day mortality, 90-day mortality and 365-day mortality). Variables with >30% missing data were excluded. Missing values were imputed 20 times using the mice package with both Random Forest (RF) and Predictive Mean Matching (PMM) methods; the averaged imputed value was used for analysis. Outliers were removed using the Local Outlier Factor (LOF) algorithm.

For clustering analysis, variables from the first 24 h of ICU admission were considered. Feature selection was based on clinical relevance and biological plausibility, ensuring that the absolute correlation coefficient between any two continuous variables was <0.6.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using STATA 16.0, Python (3.12.3), and R (4.4.0). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests according to data distribution. Categorical variables were summarized as counts (%) and compared using χ2 tests.

Correlation analysis was performed for parameters within the first 24 h of ICU admission (Python Seaborn library). Based on correlation coefficients, 30 variables were selected for consensus k-means, k-means, and self-organizing map (SOM) clustering (R package ConsensusClusterPlus). The optimal number of clusters (k) representing NOAF subtypes was determined using the elbow method, cumulative distribution function (CDF) plot, and consensus matrix. Dimensionality reduction and visualization were performed using Principal Component Analysis (PCA), t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP).

Differences among identified subtypes were analyzed using one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Kaplan-Meier survival curves for 28-day mortality were generated (Python lifelines library), and the log-rank test was applied to compare survival across subtypes. To assess the predictive value of subtypes, an Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model was developed using the consensus k-means subtypes as labels and internally validated. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and area under the curve (AUC) values were calculated. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were employed to interpret feature contributions [26].

All analyses were implemented using Python libraries (pandas, numpy, matplotlib, missingno, rpy2, scikit-learn, umap, xgboost, shap) and R packages (ConsensusClusterPlus, mice).

Results

Study population and cohort characteristics

After a stepwise screening process, 8,472 critically ill patients with NOAF were included, comprising 5,554 patients from the MIMIC-IV database and 2,918 from MIMIC-III (Fig. 1). The pattern of missing data among NOAF patients in the MIMIC-IV database is presented in Supplementary Fig. 1. Missing values were imputed using the MICE approach, and the kernel density plot confirmed the reliability of the imputation process (Supplementary Fig. 2). Patients from the MIMIC-IV cohort who met the inclusion and exclusion criteria were categorized into survivor and non-survivor groups according to 28-day mortality. The baseline characteristics of the two groups were summarized in Supplementary Table 1, revealed significant differences in demographics, laboratory parameters, vital signs, blood gas, comorbidities, disease severity scores, and pharmacologic treatments.

Derivation process of subtypes

In this study, critically ill patients with NOAF meeting the criteria from the MIMIC-IV database were included for unsupervised clustering analyses. Using 30 clinically relevant demographic and laboratory variables (Supplementary Fig. S3), unsupervised clustering identified four distinct NOAF subtypes. The four-cluster solution was further supported by the Consensus Matrix and CDF plots, which indicated favorable cluster stability (Supplementary Fig. S4). The identified subtypes were designated as Subtype A, Subtype B, Subtype C, and Subtype D, respectively. Dimensionality-reduction visualizations using PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP demonstrated distinct distributions among the four subtypes (Fig. 2).

Using PCA, t-SNE, and UMAP methods to visualize the phenotype of the MIMIC dataset through Consensus K-means, K-means and SOM analysis. A-C: Consensus K-means, D-F: K-means, G-I: SOM. Circles represent individual patients.

Clinical features, management, and outcomes across NOAF subtypes

Distinct clustering profiles were observed across the four NOAF subtypes. Subtype A represented 30.30% of the cohort and was characterized by a relatively favorable physiological profile, including the lowest heart rate, highest arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO2), and lowest lactate levels. Subtype B (34.5%) demonstrated relatively lower disease severity, as reflected by the lowest SOFA, together with marked hypomagnesemia. Subtype C (19.7%) was characterized by impaired oxygenation and prominent hematologic stress features, including lower PaO2, markedly elevated red cell distribution width (RDW), and reduced hemoglobin levels, together with a higher comorbidity burden as reflected by the highest CCI among all subtypes. Subtype D (15.5%) represented the most severe clinical phenotype, characterized by pronounced metabolic derangement and multi-organ dysfunction, including markedly elevated lactate and creatinine, severe acid-base disturbances, and the highest SOFA scores among all subtypes. Comparisons of baseline clinical characteristics across subtypes are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Visualization using bar plots and radar charts further highlighted the distinct distribution patterns of representative clinical variables across the four NOAF subtypes (Fig. 3).

Beyond the clustering variables, additional clinical characteristics differed substantially across the four NOAF subtypes (Table 1). Subtype A patients were predominantly admitted to the cardiac ICU (90.1%) and exhibited a predominant cardiac comorbidity, including congestive heart failure (39.3%) and prior myocardial infarction (25.5%). This subtype also had the highest proportions of patients undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) and valve surgery. Pre-ICU antiarrhythmic therapy exposure was also most frequently observed in Subtype A. Subtype B included a larger proportion of patients from mixed ICUs (35.5%) and was characterized by higher prevalences of sepsis (19.6%), pneumonia (21.6%), and a moderate burden of comorbid malignancy (16.1%). Subtype C demonstrated the greatest comorbidity burden and showed further enrichment of infectious conditions, including sepsis and pneumonia. Subtype D was mainly composed of medical ICU patients and had the highest prevalence of sepsis among all subtypes. Use of organ support varied markedly across subtypes. Mechanical ventilation and vasopressor use were most frequent in Subtype A, whereas the intensity of organ support increased from Subtype B to Subtype D. Clinical outcomes differed accordingly, with mortality increasing progressively across subtypes and reaching the highest level in Subtype D.

Association between NOAF subtypes and clinical outcomes

Kaplan-Meier survival curves demonstrated significant differences in survival among the four NOAF subtypes. Subtype A exhibited the lowest 28-day mortality (4.9%), followed by Subtypes B and C, whereas Subtype D exhibited the highest 28-day mortality (42.7%). Survival differences among subtypes were statistically significant according to the log-rank test (P < 0.001). Similar survival patterns were observed in the 90-day and 365-day Kaplan-Meier analyses, consistent with the 28-day findings (Fig. 4A).

Survival outcomes and multivariable Cox regression analysis across the four NOAF subtypes. A. Kaplan-Meier survival curves for 28-day, 90-day, and 365-day mortality among the four NOAF subtypes. B. Multivariable Cox regression analysis showing adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for mortality across NOAF subtypes under three models. Model I. unadjusted; Model II. adjusted for gender, weight and admission type and CCI; Model III. further adjusted for SAPS II score, ventilation, CRRT, vasoactive drug and pre-ICU antiarrhythmic therapy.

Using Subtype A as the reference, Cox proportional hazards models showed that the remaining subtypes were associated with progressively higher risks of mortality at 28, 90, and 365 days. In the unadjusted model (Model I), the hazard ratios for 28-day mortality were 3.38 (95% CI, 2.65-4.32) for Subtype B, 7.14 (95% CI, 5.60-9.09) for Subtype C, and 11.28 (95% CI, 8.88-14.34) for Subtype D (all P < 0.001). These associations remained significant after adjustment for demographic characteristics and comorbidities (Model II). After further adjustment for disease severity scores and treatments (Model III), the results remained robust, with adjusted hazard ratios of 4.24 (95% CI, 3.20-5.23) for Subtype B, 5.38 (95% CI, 4.05-7.14) for Subtype C, and 5.98 (95% CI, 4.48-7.99) for Subtype D, all significantly higher than Subtype A (all P < 0.001) (Fig. 4B). Hazard ratios for 90-day and 365-day mortality are also presented in Fig. 4B.

Overall, NOAF subtypes were independently associated with short- and long-term mortality in critically ill patients, with Subtype D consistently demonstrating the poorest prognosis.

Pharmacologic management patterns and outcome associations across NOAF subtypes

Marked differences in pharmacologic management were observed across the four NOAF subtypes, including heart rate control strategies, sedative use, and electrolyte supplementation (Supplementary Table S3). Subtype A, which predominantly consisted of post-cardiac surgery patients, showed the highest use of amiodarone, propofol, and electrolyte therapy. Subtype B was characterized by more frequent use of diltiazem and magnesium sulfate. Subtype C demonstrated intermediate use of sedative agents, whereas Subtype D had the highest utilization of midazolam and dexmedetomidine (all P < 0.001).

We next explored associations between pharmacologic treatments and 28-day mortality using Cox proportional hazards models. In the overall NOAF population, use of β-blockers, diltiazem, propofol, dexmedetomidine, magnesium sulfate, and potassium chloride was associated with a lower risk of 28-day mortality, whereas midazolam use was associated with a higher mortality risk (all P < 0.05).

Subtype-specific analysis revealed heterogeneous associations. In Subtype A, β-blocker and propofol use were associated with lower 28-day mortality, while midazolam use was associated with increased mortality risk. In Subtype B, diltiazem and magnesium sulfate use were associated with lower 28-day mortality. In Subtype C, propofol administration and electrolyte supplementation (magnesium and potassium) were associated with reduced mortality risk. In Subtype D, diltiazem, dexmedetomidine, and potassium chloride use were associated with lower 28-day mortality (Fig. 5).

Development and validation of a simplified prediction model for NOAF subtypes

To facilitate practical identification of NOAF subtypes, supervised machine learning models were developed based on the subtype labels derived from unsupervised clustering. Using the training cohort, XGBoost classifiers were constructed to predict each NOAF subtype. SHAP analysis was applied to quantify the relative contribution of individual features to subtype discrimination (Fig. 6A). In internal validation, the full model incorporating 30 variables demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance, with area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) values of 0.99, 0.96, 0.97, and 0.99 for Subtypes A, B, C, and D, respectively (Fig. 6B), indicating accurate classification of NOAF subtypes.

Each dot represents one patient, with SHAP values on the x-axis and color indicating feature magnitude (red = high, blue = low). B. ROC curves for the complete and simplified models in the training cohort. C. SHAP summary plots for the simplified six-variable model (RDW, PaO2, bicarbonate, creatinine, respiratory rate, magnesium). D. Kaplan-Meier survival curves for the four NOAF subtypes in the external validation cohort (MIMIC-III).

Based on these six variables, a parsimonious supervised XGBoost classifier was further constructed and externally validated in the MIMIC-III cohort (Fig. 6C). Baseline characteristics of the external validation cohort are presented in Supplementary Tables S3 and S4. The simplified model effectively distinguished the four NOAF subtypes in the external cohort, with consistent differences in clinical characteristics and outcomes across subtypes (Supplementary Tables S5 and S6). Kaplan-Meier survival analyses for 28-day, 90-day, and 365-day mortality in the external cohort demonstrated survival patterns comparable to those observed in the training cohort (Fig. 6D). Multivariable Cox regression analyses further confirmed consistent associations between NOAF subtypes and mortality across training and validation cohorts, supporting the robustness and generalizability of the simplified prediction model (Supplementary Table S7).

Discussion

NOAF is a common arrhythmia and serves as an established predictor of adverse outcomes in critically ill patients. However, growing evidence suggests that NOAF represents a clinically heterogeneous condition, which may contribute to the substantial variability in clinical presentation, management, and outcomes. Prior studies have primarily focused on the association between NOAF and complications such as stroke and organ dysfunction [10,27], or on predicting its incidence [[28], [29], [30]], while largely overlooking its intrinsic biological diversity. Using data from a large, retrospective ICU cohort in the MIMIC database, we applied unsupervised clustering to identify four reproducible NOAF subtypes based on 30 routinely available clinical and biological variables. These subtypes exhibited distinct clinical profiles, management patterns, and prognostic trajectories, providing a clinically interpretable framework for bedside subtyping and offering new insights into the pathophysiological heterogeneity of NOAF.

Our clustering analysis identified four clinically distinct NOAF subtypes with progressively worse outcomes. Subtype A predominantly consisted of post-cardiac surgery patients and was characterized by preserved physiological homeostasis and the lowest mortality. Subtype B was characterized by relatively lower disease severity, a relatively higher prevalence of malignancy and marked hypomagnesemia, and was associated with moderately increased mortality. Subtype C was characterized by anemia, impaired oxygenation, inflammation-related stress, and the highest comorbidity burden, accompanied by worse outcomes. Subtype D represented the most critically ill phenotype, with pronounced metabolic derangements as well as multi-organ dysfunction, and exhibited the highest short- and long-term mortality. Mortality increased stepwise from Subtype A to D at 28, 90, and 365 days, indicating a progressive gradient of disease severity. Importantly, multivariable Cox regression analyses confirmed that these subtypes were independently associated with mortality, even after adjustment for established severity and comorbidity scores, suggesting that our data-driven phenotyping captures multidimensional risk patterns not fully reflected by conventional scoring systems.

The progressively worsening clinical patterns observed across NOAF subtypes may reflect underlying differences in pathophysiological stress and organ vulnerability. Rather than representing discrete disease entities, the identified subtypes delineate a continuum of physiological derangement characterized by escalating inflammatory stress, impaired oxygen delivery, metabolic imbalance, and organ dysfunction. Subtype A appears to reflect transient sympathetic activation and stress following surgery, leading to favorable outcomes [31,32]. Notably, pre-ICU antiarrhythmic therapy exposure was most frequently observed in this subtype, suggesting that perioperative cardiovascular management may partially contribute to the lower mortality observed in this group [33]. Subtype B may be associated with moderate systemic stress, accompanied by hypomagnesemia and a relatively higher burden of comorbid malignancy, which together may contribute to arrhythmogenic vulnerability [[34], [35], [36]]. Although total calcium levels were also mildly reduced across subtypes, serum calcium in critically ill patients may be substantially influenced by hypoalbuminemia and systemic illness [37]. In contrast, magnesium demonstrated greater inter-subtype variability and has been more consistently linked to atrial electrophysiologic instability and atrial fibrillation in previous studies [38]. Subtype C combines chronic comorbidity burden with acute hypoxic and hematologic stress, which may partially explain its adverse prognosis [39,40]. Subtype D may correspond to severe critical illness with sepsis-associated metabolic derangements, profound multi-organ dysfunction, consistent with its markedly poor outcomes [[41], [42], [43]]. These interpretations should be viewed as hypothesis-generating and warrant further mechanistic investigation.

Furthermore, we developed a simplified subtype classification model based on six routinely available variables identified through SHAP analysis: RDW, PaO2, bicarbonate, creatinine, respiratory rate, and magnesium. This simplified model demonstrated excellent discriminative performance in both the training and external validation cohorts (MIMIC-III), achieving AUCs of 0.89-0.96. The consistent results across datasets indicate that the model is robust and generalizable, with strong potential for clinical translation. By enabling rapid and interpretable bedside subtyping, it provides a feasible framework for early risk stratification of NOAF in critically ill patients.

Previous studies have applied clustering approaches to AF populations outside the ICU setting. For example, Cui et al. classified patients with persistent AF based on epicardial adipose tissue phenotypes, identifying distinct patterns of atrial fibrosis [44]. Zhang et al. identified five AF subtypes from a large UK electronic health record cohort by integrating cardiovascular, metabolic, and mental health variables, revealing heterogeneity in comorbidities and outcomes [45]. Similarly, Essa et al. used unsupervised clustering to define AF phenotypes that were significantly associated with clinical outcomes [46]. Collectively, these studies underscore the heterogeneity of AF in stable or chronic care settings. In contrast, our study focuses on NOAF in critically ill patients, a population characterized by acute physiological stress, systemic inflammation, metabolic derangements and organ instability. Unlike prior AF subtyping efforts that primarily emphasized chronic structural remodeling or long-term cardiovascular risk, our analysis captures acute pathophysiological processes and rapidly evolving comorbidity burdens in the ICU setting. Therefore, while methodologically aligned with previous clustering studies, our work extends phenotypic classification into the critical care context and provides novel insights into the biological heterogeneity of NOAF and its potential implications for clinical management.

This study further demonstrated that pharmacologic management (heart rate control, sedation, and electrolyte supplementation) was associated with heterogeneous prognostic patterns across NOAF subtypes. Treatment-outcome associations varied substantially by subtypes. In Subtype A, treatment with β-blockers and propofol was associated with lower mortality, whereas midazolam use was associated with higher mortality. These associations likely reflect differences in clinical context and illness severity rather than direct pharmacologic effects, as Subtype A patients were predominantly post-cardiac surgery patients who typically require only short-term sedation, with prolonged midazolam use serving as a marker of more severe postoperative conditions. In Subtype B, magnesium sulfate and diltiazem use were associated with lower mortality, potentially related to improved heart rate control and stabilization of atrial electrophysiology [47,48]. Among Subtype C patients, magnesium and potassium supplementation was similarly associated with lower mortality, highlighting the importance of correcting electrolyte imbalance in the setting of hypoxia and systemic stress [49]. For Subtype D, the most critically ill phenotype, diltiazem and dexmedetomidine use was associated with more favorable outcomes, possibly reflecting their hemodynamic tolerability and sympatholytic properties [[50], [51], [52]]. However, these treatment-outcome associations should be interpreted cautiously. Our findings do not imply causal treatment effects but rather underscore phenotype-specific heterogeneity in observed treatment–outcome relationships and support the concept of phenotype-aware NOAF management.

This study has several notable highlights. First, the identified NOAF subtypes were derived independently of outcome variables, thereby more accurately reflecting the intrinsic biological heterogeneity. Second, we developed a simplified classification model based on six routinely available variables, which is clinically feasible for early risk stratification and bedside application in the ICU. Third, we observed heterogeneous treatment-outcome associations across NOAF subtypes. However, these findings provide new evidence-based support for precision, phenotype-guided management of NOAF in critically ill patients.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, as it is based on a retrospective database, the potential for selection bias and residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Second, although the clustering and simplified predictive models were internally and externally validated, further prospective and multicenter validation is warranted to confirm their robustness and generalizability. Third, the associations between pharmacologic interventions and clinical outcomes are observational and should be interpreted cautiously. Future research should aim to incorporate multicenter data to enhance model robustness and external validity, and to integrate multi-omics approaches for mechanistic exploration. In addition, the use of trajectory-based or growth mixture models may help capture the dynamic evolution of NOAF phenotypes over time.

Conclusion

In summary, this study represents the first large-scale retrospective analysis of ICU patients with NOAF, identifying four clinically and biologically distinct subtypes that highlight the marked heterogeneity of this condition. We further developed and externally validated a simplified six-variable classification model capable of accurately distinguishing these subtypes and revealing significant differences in prognosis and therapeutic response. Together, these findings establish robust evidence for phenotype-based risk stratification in critically ill patients with NOAF, offering a conceptual and practical foundation for precision management and bridging the gap between machine learning derived phenotypes and precision clinical practice in the ICU.

As this was a retrospective analysis using de-identified patient data, no ethical concerns or issues related to clinical trial participants were implicated. No additional institutional review board approval was required for this analysis.
