Failure mechanisms of the Exactech Equinoxe hybrid cage glenoid in anatomic TSA: a retrieval and radiographic case series
Authors: Nathaniel L. Bailey, Moreica Pabbruwe, Alan Kop, Stefan Bauer, William G. Blakeney
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlenoid loosening is a well-documented failure mechanism in anatomic shoulder arthroplasty. Implant manufacturers have developed innovative designs to address these issues, with hybrid glenoid components emerging as a potential solution. This study investigates the Exactech Equinoxe glenoid (Exactech Inc., Gainesville, FL, USA), a hybrid component that has shown higher midterm revision rates in national registry data.MethodsWe conducted an investigation of failed Equinoxe Cage Glenoid implants revised between November 2022 and July 2025 and analyzed at a tertiary arthroplasty retrieval center. Clinical data, radiographs, and intraoperative findings were reviewed. Radiographs were assessed for radiolucency (Lazarus score), implant position, and evidence of fracture or metallic debris. Retrieved components underwent macroscopic inspection and evaluation of wear modes. Polyethylene (PE) oxidation was quantified through white-banding assessment and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy. Periprosthetic tissues, when available, underwent inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry to evaluate metal ion presence.ResultsEleven failed hybrid glenoid components were analyzed at a median of 5.8 years after implantation. Radiographic loosening was present in 82% of cases (median Lazarus score 5). All retrieved components demonstrated macroscopic PE wear; 82% showed cracking and delamination. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy confirmed subsurface oxidation in all tested components. Peripheral peg fixation was limited, with 100% of pegs demonstrating minimal bony ongrowth and 45% showing polishing consistent with micromotion. In contrast, 100% of central metal cages exhibited bony ingrowth. Metal debris or staining was noted in 55% of cases, and tissue samples demonstrated elevated metal ions consistent with cage or peg wear.ConclusionFailed Equinoxe Cage Glenoid implants demonstrated consistent patterns of PE oxidation and inadequate peripheral peg fixation, contributing to loosening and mechanical failure at the PE–metal interface. These findings provide a potential mechanism for the higher revision rate observed in national registry data and highlight the importance of ongoing postmarket surveillance of new implant designs.

Anatomic total shoulder arthroplasty (aTSA) is an effective treatment for glenohumeral osteoarthritis in patients with an intact rotator cuff.9 Its use continues to rise, with a 228% increase in shoulder arthroplasty procedures reported in Australia between 2008 and 2023.2 Despite improvements in implant design and surgical technique, glenoid component loosening remains the leading cause of aTSA failure.4

The glenoid component is critical to the long-term success of these procedures. While cemented all-polyethylene (PE) glenoid components have long been considered the gold standard because of their predictable fixation and long-term survivorship, concerns over cement durability and radiographic loosening have driven interest in newer fixation strategies, including uncemented metal-backed and hybrid components.4,13,17, 18, 19 Hybrid designs combine PE and metal components to enhance both immediate fixation and biological ingrowth.

One such design is the Exactech Equinoxe Cage Glenoid (Exactech Inc., Gainesville, FL, USA), which utilizes a central porous titanium cage with peripheral cemented pegs. Early studies on this design suggested promising results, with revision rates as low as 3.5% at 5 years.10,11 However, recent data from the Australian Orthopedic Association National Joint Replacement Registry (AOA NJRR) contradict these findings, showing a significantly higher 5-year revision rate of 11.4%, more than double that of other glenoid components used in aTSA.3 This discrepancy has raised concerns regarding implant design, material behavior, and mechanisms of failure.

In parallel, Exactech issued recalls for a range of PE components, including glenoid components, due to packaging that allowed exposure to oxygen during storage, potentially accelerating oxidation and embrittlement of the PE.23,27 Oxidation-related degradation has been implicated in catastrophic PE failure in other arthroplasty systems, yet has not been studied in this hybrid glenoid design.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to characterize the mechanisms of failure in retrieved Equinoxe Cage Glenoid implants using radiographic assessment, retrieval analysis, and PE oxidation testing. We hypothesized that early failure of this implant is multifactorial, involving PE degradation, wear, and inadequate peripheral peg fixation, leading to loosening and fracture at the PE–metal interface.

Methods

Study population

This case series reviewed 11 failed Equinoxe Cage Glenoid implants (Exactech Inc., Gainesville, FL, USA) retrieved during revision surgeries between November 2022 and July 2025. Components were analyzed at the Centre for Implant Technology and Retrieval Analysis, Royal Perth Hospital, and root cause analysis was performed to identify patterns of failure and contributing factors.

Patient demographics

Standardized proforma questionnaires were reviewed for patient demographics, including age, gender, comorbidities, activity level, and primary indication for aTSA. The record was also used to identify reasons for implant removal, observations at removal, and contributing factors to failure.

Radiographic analysis

Post-operative radiographs were assessed by a senior upper limb surgeon for appropriate placement, inclination, and version. They were determined to be acceptable or unacceptable to identify cases where early surgical placement could be a contributing factor. Off-axis drilling of the peripheral pegs relative to the central cage was measured by taking the angle between the long axis of the central cage and each peg. The largest angle was taken, and the measurement repeated 3 times; the median was then reported. Pre-revision radiographs were assessed for osteolysis, implant fracture, and metallic debris. Radiolucent lines around the glenoid component were graded by zone and width, then scored independently by 2 authors from 0 to 5 according to Lazarus et al15 on both post-operative and pre-revision radiographs. Retrieval analysis

Retrieved implants underwent macroscopic evaluation and advanced testing. All retrieved devices were submitted with a standardized proforma questionnaire filled by surgeons, including intraoperative findings and reasons for revision. The implants were then sent to Centre for Implant Technology and Retrieval Analysis and photographed as received. Retrieved implants were assessed for 7 modes of wear: cracking and delamination, focal destruction, metal debris and metallosis, bony ingrowth or ongrowth, polishing, and fretting suggestive of loosening, modes of failure and stress risers.

White banding assessment and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR): a sagittal cut was made through each PE glenoid using a Buhler Saw (Metaserv, Lake Bluff, IL, USA). Thin sections, (200 μm) were then obtained using new, sharp microtome blades (Polycut S, Reichert Jung, Heidelberg, Germany). Thin sections were utilized for both white banding and FTIR analyses. White bands are formed by production of ketones combined with high subsurface stresses in the PE, and in a thin section, a white band is a visual indicator of oxidation, the subsurface corresponding to an area of highest oxidation.

The oxidation level in each glenoid component, determined through absorbance spectra, was measured with FTIR (Thermo Scientific, Nicolet iN10 Mx, Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA). Oxidation assessments were conducted as a function of depth on a thin section of each PE glenoid component. Sixty-four scans per 50-μm depth interval, with a wavelength interval of 8 cm−1 and an aperture of 150 μm2, were taken. The analysis of oxidation in the samples was carried out based on American Society for Testing and Materials F2102-01.25 The oxidation index (OI) is defined as the ratio of the area of the carbonyl absorption peak(s) centered near 1,720 cm−1 to the area of the absorption peak(s) near 1,370 cm−1. However, this method has faced criticism due to potential overestimation of OI from absorbed lipids during component implantation; hence, the oxidation of each sample was determined by ketone peak height rather than OI as specified in the standard.6 The reported oxidation level of each thin section was defined as the measured ketone (1,718 cm-1) peak height normalized to the 1,368 cm-1 peak height giving a ketone index (KI); a value above 1.2 is considered an indicator of oxidation.7 This approach enables a separate evaluation of absorbed species (ester, 1,738 cm−1) and oxidation products in assessing bearing oxidation.

Analysis of metal ion levels in periprosthetic tissue received with some of the explants was performed using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry to quantify concentrations of specific metals such as titanium (Ti), aluminum, cobalt (Co), and chromium (Cr), which are present in Ti and CoCr alloys used to manufactured the central peg/peripheral pegs in the glenoid and humeral head, respectively.16

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize findings. Data were compared with published literature and AOA NJRR data.

Ethics

This study was conducted under institutional ethical review approval (MP 0129/20). It was performed in accordance with the ethical standards in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki.12

Discussion

This retrieval study demonstrates a consistent pattern of mechanical failure in retrieved Equinoxe Cage Glenoid prostheses, providing an explanation for the high revision rate observed in registry data. Recently published data from the AOA NJRR has revealed that revisions/100 observation years for the Equinoxe Cage Glenoid are 2.29 as compared to 0.92 for all other stemmed anatomic shoulder replacements.3 The failed implants showed gross wear and oxidation of the PE glenoid, as well as poor fixation of the peripheral pegs. It is likely that PE and metal debris lead to wear induced osteolysis, which then causes loosening of the implant. This then creates a sheer stress at the well-fixed central peg, which eventually leads to fracture. Failure of the implants can be associated with patient, surgical, and design and material factors. The majority of these implants failed early after implantation in young, active males. These patient factors combined with oxidation of the PE, as well as problems with the design of the peripheral pegs, have led to early failure of these implants.23,27

Patient demographics in this series differed from those reported for the broader stemmed aTSA population in Australia.3 The median age at primary implantation in our cohort (63 years) was younger than registry averages (68.7 years), and a greater proportion of recipients were male (63% in our cohort vs. 43.1% in the registry).3 While our data do not allow us to determine whether age, sex, or activity level independently contributed to failure, it is possible that these factors, combined with PE oxidation and limited peripheral fixation, may have influenced implant performance. The registry does not report activity level, and therefore any association between patient factors and failure should be interpreted cautiously.

The implants all failed early and were revised at median 5.8 years. The most common reason for revision was loosening. The AOA NJRR reports the main reasons for revision of all stemmed aTSA to be rotator cuff insufficiency and instability/dislocation, with loosening ranking third at 13.9%.3

All implants showed evidence of oxidative degradation. This was confirmed in 4 cases by FTIR analysis and white band testing. This specific testing was performed on later samples as it became evident that oxidative damage could be part of the failure mechanism. This testing could not be added retrospectively to previous samples, as they were not stored in a way to prevent further oxidation and testing would not be reliable. This testing is to be viewed as an adjunct to the characteristic visual changes of oxidative damage. In 2021, Exactech issued recalls for Equinoxe implants, as well as many others, due to a potential packaging defect that allowed storage without an oxygen barrier, potentially leading to accelerated oxidation and subsequent material embrittlement.23,27 A study by Ansari et al1 examined catastrophically failed PE glenoid components from 3 different manufacturers retrieved during revision surgeries. Their findings confirmed elevated oxidation levels as a key driver of PE embrittlement and subsequent failure. The cracking and wear characteristics they described resemble those observed of the PE bearing surface observed in our study. By contrast, PE glenoid components examined by Ansari et al1 lack the PE–metal interface of hybrid glenoid components and so they did not observe failure at this point.

Elevated tissue metal levels were demonstrated in 100% (n = 4) of cases tested. Testing was performed on samples where adequate tissue was received with the implant. This testing is to be viewed as an adjunct to the characteristic visual changes of metallosis, tissue staining, and debris. Due to the inability to representatively sample tissue, levels of metal ions are only indicative of their presence and not their concentration. However, presence of the Ti alloy suggests wear of the central cage and Co/Cr metals are indicative of wear of the humeral head articulating on the cage, which was confirmed on observation. In 55% (n = 6) of cases, there was evidence of PE and metal debris, tissue staining, or metallosis as observed during implant retrieval. Metallosis can trigger an inflammatory response, leading to chronic synovitis, bone resorption, and progressive implant loosening.14 Wear-generated debris is a key contributor to periprosthetic osteolysis, often resulting in aseptic loosening of the implant and necessitating revision surgery.8,24 Radiolucent lines/osteolysis was observed pre-revision, on radiographs, in 82% (n = 9) of cases. We note that radiolucent lines are seen around the peripheral pegs with minimal or no occurrence around the central cage, particularly the tip. It is likely that oxidation and particulate deposition has led to wear induced osteolysis around already poorly fixed peripheral pegs. Loosening and movement is evidenced by lack of bony or cement attachment and polishing of these peripheral pegs. Loosening and movement of the implant promoted additional osteolysis. Without attachment at the peripheral pegs all the force is transferred to the well-fixed central cage. This creates a stress riser at the PE–cage interface, which eventually results in shear failure at this point.

A recent article by Monir et al reported the failure of this implant due to shear stress at the PE–cage junction, attributing the failure primarily to surgical error. Specifically, they proposed that inadequate fixation of the peripheral pegs could result from off-axis drilling during implantation, potentially compromising stability.22 To allow for direct comparison, our study also evaluated off-axis drilling. Monir et al reported a mean misalignment of 4.8° (range: 3°-6°), which was slightly higher than our measured median value of 3.0° (range: 0.6°-6.9°). The lower misalignment observed in our cohort may indicate that other factors contributed more substantially to early failure compared with Monir et al’s findings. However, potential inaccuracies in measurement methods and the uncertain clinical significance of these differences warrant cautious interpretation.

In Western Australia, the retrieval laboratory received 9 revisions of 108 implanted glenoid components in this timeframe giving a failure rate of at least 8.3%. We cannot confirm that we received all failed glenoid components from Western Australia within the data collection period. Two implants were received from interstate and were not included in this revision figure. The 5-year revision rate of all other aTSA glenoid systems used in Australia is 4.2%.3 The United Kingdom's National Joint Registry reports theirs to be 3.6%.26 Previous literature reports revision of the Exactech Equinoxe platform to be 3.5%-6.4% at approximately 5 years follow-up.10,11 This is in stark contrast to the AOA NJRR–reported 5-year revision rate for Equinoxe Cage Glenoid components of 11.4% and of all-PE Equinoxe variants at 9.3%.3 At 8 years, the revision rate for the Equinoxe Cage Glenoid components increase to 16.4% and the all-PE Equinoxe glenoid components to 14.1%, which is significantly higher than other nonmodular metal-backed glenoid components (6.6%) and other all-PE glenoid components (5.1%).3 The high failure rate in the Exactech all-PE glenoid raises the question of whether these implants are also failing early secondary to oxidation issues, as it cannot be explained by the same design flaws as the hybrid glenoid component. Both devices were stored in the same nonconforming packaging, suggesting both would be susceptible to the same oxidative degradation and predisposition to cracking, delamination, and fracture. Moreover, the PE used in Exactech shoulder systems is direct compression–molded and not heat-treated after sterilization. It relies on the manufacturing method and integrity of its packaging to prevent storage and in vivo oxidation; a failure of the packaging likely resulted in accelerated in vivo oxidation.

Another potential explanation for the difference in survivorship between the 2 Exactech implants could be a difference in demographics. In this study, the Equinoxe Cage Glenoid recipients were younger, possibly more active and failure occurred in a disproportionately male group. It is conceivable that this component may have been selected for younger, more active patients due to its theoretical advantage in short- and long-term fixation. Differences in survivorship could also be due to implant design, such as poor attachment of peripheral pegs, as previously described. Recent iterations of the hybrid glenoid (Laser Cage Glenoid) now incorporate 3D-printed mini-cage pegs intended to improve fixation. These presumably allow for bony ingrowth and stronger fixation at these peripheral sites increasing overall stability. Further study is needed to see what effect this design change will have on outcomes.

Compared to other hybrid glenoid component designs, revision rates quoted in the literature vary significantly. The LIMA Hybrid TT Glenoid component (LimaCorporate, Villanova di San Daniele, Udine, IT) 5-year revision is reported at 11.7%. This may be indicative of a broader problem with hybrid glenoid components in general; however, this is only a small study.21 The Zimmer Biomet Comprehensive shoulder system (Warsaw, IN, USA) is reported as 2.3%-3.3% at mean follow-up of approximately 5 years.5,20 The LIMA design entered the market around 2018 compared to a 2011 release date for the Exactech and Zimmer glenoid components. The Depuy Inhance (DePuy Synthes, Raynham, MA, USA) does not yet have long-term data published, as it was only recently released in 2022. Further long-term revision rates are needed for these implants to allow for comparison.

Limitations

This study is limited by its small sample size. Like all retrieval studies, it only analyses revised implants with retrieved failed components. It does not look at well-functioning implants. However, this study identifies consistent issues with the Equinoxe Cage Glenoid, which may explain its poor performance in the registry. While this study focuses on one hybrid glenoid, there is much variation in implant design and our findings are not generalizable to other hybrid implants. These results point to the importance of future prospective research to guide improvements in hybrid implant design, as well as postmarket surveillance.

Conclusion

Failed Exactech Equinoxe Cage Glenoid implants demonstrated consistent patterns of PE oxidation and inadequate peripheral peg fixation, contributing to loosening and mechanical failure at the PE–metal interface. These findings provide a potential mechanism for the higher revision rate observed in national registry data and highlight the importance of ongoing post-market surveillance of new implant designs.
