Short-term prevention of perimenstrual migraine with rimegepant
Authors: Florian Frank, Alois Schiefecker, Katharina Kaltseis, Michael Eller, Gregor Broessner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMenstrually-related migraine (MRM) affects approximately 31–52% of women with migraine and are characterized by increased frequency, severity, and reduced response to conventional therapy during the perimenstrual window. Traditional short-term prevention with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or triptans often fails due to intolerance or contraindications. Rimegepant, a calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) receptor antagonist with indication for both acute and preventive therapy, may offer a novel short-term prophylactic option for perimenstrual migraine.MethodsThis hypothesis-generating case series includes five women, aged 22–42 years, with episodic migraine and regular menstrual cycles, who received rimegepant 75 mg orally disintegrating tablet every other day, initiated two days prior menstruation and continued until two days post-menstruation. Patients recorded daily headache diaries, and key outcomes included reduction of perimenstrual migraine days, overall migraine frequency, tolerability, and adverse events. Follow-up was conducted after 3–4 cycles.ResultsRimegepant prevented perimenstrual attacks in 17/20 cycles (85%) with no treatment-related adverse events in this small cohort. However, sustained monthly migraine day reduction occurred in only one patient (20%). Three patients (60%) experienced delayed migraine onset 3–7 days post-menstruation, suggesting “temporal displacement” rather than complete prevention. All patients reported high satisfaction with perimenstrual symptom control.ConclusionsShort-term rimegepant was well tolerated and prevented anticipated perimenstrual migraine attacks in most cases. The observed temporal displacement of migraine supports complex hormonal and CGRP-independent mechanisms in menstrual migraine. Introduction

Menstrually-related migraine (MRM) represent a substantial clinical burden, affecting 31–52% of women with migraine, with the highest prevalence among premenopausal women [1]. According to the International Classification of Headache Disorders (ICHD-3), MRM are defined as migraine attacks occurring within the perimenstrual window (from two days before menstruation to the third day of menstruation). Pure menstrual migraine (MM) is diagnosed when attacks occur exclusively around menstruation [2] (Table 1).

Traditional management of menstrual migraine includes acute therapy with NSAIDs or triptans, as is the case in non-menstrual migraine. However, due to the often longer duration of perimenstrual attacks, long-acting formulations are preferred. For severe cases, and in patients with frequent menstrual and non-menstrual attacks, prophylactic treatment may be necessary. In menstrual migraine, short-term prevention can be an option for patients who do not respond adequately to standard prophylactic treatment or who experience insufficient relief from acute therapy. This treatment is typically initiated several days before menstruation [3]. However, common regimens, such as frovatriptan 2.5 mg bid or naproxen 550 mg bid often only show insufficient response and are limited by gastrointestinal, vascular, or medication overuse risks. Real-world data suggest that only 21.1% of women with menstrual migraine actually use preventive therapy, while the majority rely on acute or over-the-counter (OTC) options alone [4].

CGRP pathway antagonists, such as rimegepant, have emerged as promising alternatives. Rimegepant is approved for both acute and preventive treatment of episodic migraine, without evidence for triggering medication overuse headache. Phase-3 studies showed reduction in mean monthly migraine days by 1.1 days versus placebo over a 12-week period, with a favorable safety profile. Still, evidence in the context of short-term, event-based prevention in menstrual migraine is scarce [5, 6]. Limited data are available on monoclonal CGRP antibodies, showing mixed results regarding their efficacy in menstrual migraine [7, 8].

Methods

Study design

This case series was conducted at the headache outpatient clinic of the Medical University of Innsbruck between June 2023 and August 2025 in compliance with the local ethics committee. We identified suitable patients among women of childbearing age who presented to our tertiary headache center with a history of regular and predictable menstrual cycles. All participants were required to have an established diagnosis of migraine with or without aura according to ICHD-3 criteria for at least twelve months prior to study entry. Patients needed to demonstrate a stable pattern of perimenstrual migraine attacks occurring within the defined perimenstrual window of two days before to three days after menstruation onset. Several exclusion criteria were applied to ensure a homogeneous study population and minimize confounding factors. We excluded patients with current or recent medication overuse headache within six months of presentation, as this condition could interfere with treatment response assessment. Women with frequent episodic migraine, defined as ten or more monthly migraine days averaged over the three months prior to consultation, were also excluded to focus specifically on patients with predominantly perimenstrual patterns. Additional exclusion criteria included concurrent or recent use of migraine preventive medications within the preceding three months, current treatment with lasmiditan for any indication, ongoing short-term preventive therapies, or treatment with monoclonal CGRP antibodies.

Treatment protocol

Rimegepant was prescribed as a 75 mg orally disintegrating tablet to be taken every other day according to a standardized protocol (Table 2). Treatment initiation started two days before the expected onset of menstruation based on each patient’s individual cycle pattern, continuing until two days after menstruation began. This regimen typically resulted in administration of four doses per menstrual cycle. Patients were instructed to maintain their established acute migraine treatments as needed for breakthrough attacks while continuing the rimegepant preventative protocol. All participants were provided with standardized paper headache diaries and instructed in their proper completion to ensure accurate prospective data collection. These diaries captured daily headache occurrence, intensity using a visual analog scale, associated symptoms, medication use, and menstrual timing. Patients were specifically trained to distinguish between different headache types and to record all relevant clinical details.

Variables collected

During the initial outpatient consultation, all patients underwent comprehensive headache assessment following our standard clinical protocol. After obtaining written informed consent, we collected comprehensive demographic information, detailed medical history including previous migraine treatments, and documented any relevant comorbidities or concomitant medications.

This evaluation included administration of validated questionnaires comprising the Migraine Disability Assessment Scale (MIDAS) to quantify functional disability and the Headache Impact Test-6 (HIT-6) to assess migraine-related disability impact. Patients completed a detailed headache calendar covering the three months preceding their visit, with particular attention to perimenstrual attack patterns and associated menstrual symptoms. Prospective data collection utilized standardized headache diaries that recorded daily headache occurrence, pain intensity, associated symptoms, acute medication use, and precise menstrual timing.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the proportion of menstrual cycles in which perimenstrual migraine prevention was achieved, defined as either complete absence of migraine attacks within the perimenstrual window (two days before to three days after menstruation onset) or a reduction of at least 50% in perimenstrual migraine days compared to baseline. Secondary outcomes included changes in overall monthly migraine days, improvements in MIDAS and HIT-6 disability scores from baseline to follow-up (3–4 months), treatment tolerability and adverse events. Data are presented as absolute numbers, percentages, means with standard deviation (SD), or ranges when appropriate. All outcomes were assessed after a follow-up period of four completed menstrual cycles treated with rimegepant per patient.

Results

Five women with a mean age of 31.8 years (range 22–42 years) provided fully analyzable data. Due to low adherence to the headache diary during baseline, three women were excluded from the study before the initiation of rimegepant treatment phase. Two patients were classified as having pure menstrual migraine, while three patients had menstrually-related migraine according to the ICHD-3 criteria (Table 1). None of the participants were using hormonal contraception at the time of study entry, and all had established patterns of regular menstrual cycles with predictable timing. The baseline migraine burden varied among participants, with monthly migraine days ranging from three to six days per month. Patient histories revealed multiple previous unsuccessful treatment attempts of their menstrual attacks with conventional therapies, including various NSAIDs and triptans, often limited by tolerability issues or inadequate efficacy. Three patients had never received migraine preventive therapy, while two had unsuccessful trials with conventional preventive medications including beta-blockers, anticonvulsants, or CGRP monoclonal antibodies (Table 3).

Treatment data and response patterns

Rimegepant demonstrated efficacy (defined as ≥ 50% reduction of migraine days in the perimenstrual window) in preventing anticipated perimenstrual migraine attacks across the study population. Of the twenty total menstrual cycles (4 cycles per patient) observed during the treatment period, perimenstrual migraine attacks were averted in seventeen cycles, representing an overall prevention rate of 85% (Fig. 1). All patients reported initial success with rimegepant in eliminating their typical perimenstrual migraine pattern during the first treatment cycles. However, the impact on overall monthly migraine burden revealed more complex response patterns than initially anticipated (Fig. 2). Only one patient, a 32-year-old woman with pure menstrual migraine, experienced sustained reduction in monthly migraine days, achieving 50% reduction from baseline during the treatment period. This patient’s response was characterized by both prevention of perimenstrual attacks and maintenance of this benefit throughout the observation period without temporal displacement of symptoms. The remaining four patients exhibited an unexpected phenomenon that we termed “temporal displacement”. These women successfully prevented their anticipated perimenstrual migraine attacks but subsequently developed migraine episodes occurring three to seven days post-menstruation. This pattern was particularly striking in a patient with previously pure menstrual migraine, where post-menstrual attacks represented entirely novel outside their historical pattern. Consequently, while these patients achieved their primary goal of perimenstrual symptom control, their overall monthly migraine frequency remained essentially unchanged. Overall, the delayed migraine attacks were reported to be identical to the usual migraine attacks in terms of duration, intensity, associated symptoms as well as acute medication use. The tolerability profile of rimegepant in. No patient reported treatment-related adverse events during the entire observation period. This finding was particularly noteworthy given that the perimenstrual period is typically associated with increased gastrointestinal sensitivity and reduced medication tolerance in many women. Treatment adherence was 100%, documented across all patients and cycles. No patient discontinued treatment due to tolerability concerns (Fig 1).

Subjective patient satisfaction and disability measurements

Despite the temporal displacement phenomenon observed in most patients, satisfaction with treatment was uniformly high. MIDAS and HIT-6 improved by mean 2.0 ± 1.0 and 2.4 ± 1.7, respectively. All participants expressed strong appreciation for the reliable control of their predictable and often severe migraine episodes. The ability to plan personal and professional activities around expected menstrual periods without fear of debilitating migraine attacks represented an improvement in quality of life for these women. Patients noted that while they continued to experience the same amount of migraine days per month, the elimination of perimenstrual attacks allowed for better overall migraine management and reduced anticipatory anxiety.

Discussion

This case series provides the first reported experience with rimegepant for short-term perimenstrual migraine prevention in clinical practice. The observed 85% success rate for preventing anticipated perimenstrual attacks compares favorably with published efficacy data for established short-term prevention strategies. Historical studies report that frovatriptan 2.5 mg twice a day (BID) leads to approximately 55–62% migraine-free cycles, while naproxen 550 mg BID shows a modest but significantly varying prophylactic effect in older studies [9–11]. The performance of rimegepant in our preliminary series, combined with its tolerability profile, might suggest potential advantages over conventional approaches. The safety profile observed in our patients aligns closely with data from large-scale clinical trials and post-marketing surveillance studies [12]. The absence of any treatment-related adverse events in our perimenstrual application, despite the potentially challenging physiological context, supports the favorable risk-benefit profile of this approach.

The most intriguing finding from our series was the “temporal displacement” observed in 60% of patients. While rimegepant successfully prevented hormonally triggered perimenstrual attacks, migraine episodes subsequently emerged in the post-menstrual phase, typically occurring one week after menstruation. This observation suggests complex pathophysiological mechanisms that extend beyond simple CGRP receptor antagonism. Several hypotheses may explain this phenomenon. The declining estrogen levels that characterize the late luteal phase and early menstruation may activate migraine pathways that are only partially dependent on CGRP signaling. Alternative neurochemical cascades, potentially involving prostaglandin release, nitric oxide signaling, or other neuropeptide systems, might become predominant when CGRP pathways are blocked [13, 14]. The pharmacokinetic properties of rimegepant, with its relatively short half-life of approximately 11 h, may also contribute to this effect if the vulnerable period for hormonally triggered migraine extends beyond the current dosing window. Another consideration involves the complex interplay between estrogen withdrawal and sensitization of trigeminovascular pathways. Recent research suggests that menstrual migraine involves distinct pathophysiological mechanisms compared to non-menstrual attacks, potentially explaining why CGRP antagonism provides only partial protection [7].

Clinical implication

Despite the temporal displacement phenomenon, the potential role of rimegepant in short-term prevention remains an intriguing consideration, as suggested by preliminary observations. The predictable nature of menstrual cycles allows women to anticipate their highest-risk period and plan accordingly. Patients in our series emphasized that controlling perimenstrual attacks, even with subsequent delayed episodes, provided significant lifestyle benefits including improved work attendance, social participation, and reduced anticipatory anxiety. This contributed to a slight reduction in MIDAS and HIT-6 scores, even in the absence of a reduction in monthly migraine days. Rimegepant may be particularly valuable for specific patient populations. Women with contraindications to triptans due to cardiovascular risk factors, those with previous medication overuse headache, or patients who experience significant gastrointestinal intolerance to NSAIDs represent ideal candidates. The dual indication of rimegepant for both acute and preventive treatment offers additional flexibility, allowing patients to use the same medication for breakthrough attacks while maintaining preventive efficacy.

Interestingly, one patient in our series had previously failed treatment with erenumab, a CGRP receptor monoclonal antibody, yet responded to rimegepant for short-term prevention. This observation highlights potential differences between small molecule CGRP antagonists and monoclonal antibodies in the specific context of hormonally triggered migraine. The pharmacokinetic differences, with rimegepant providing acute receptor blockade compared to the sustained but potentially less complete blockade achieved with antibodies, may account for these differential responses.

Limitations

Several important limitations must be acknowledged in interpreting these results. Most importantly, the small sample size of five patients limits generalizability and precludes statistical analysis of potential predictive factors for treatment response. The open-label design introduces potential bias, as both patients and investigators were aware of treatment assignment. The relatively short followup period of three to four cycles may not capture longer-term efficacy patterns or delayed adverse effects.

The homogeneous demographic characteristics of our patient population, consisting entirely of Caucasian Austrian women, may limit applicability to more diverse populations. Additionally, the lack of a control group prevents definitive attribution of observed effects to rimegepant rather than natural variation in migraine patterns or placebo effects.

These preliminary findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and do warrant validation in larger, randomized, placebo-controlled trials specifically designed to evaluate rimegepant for perimenstrual migraine prevention. Such studies should include diverse patient populations, longer follow-up periods, and detailed mechanistic investigations to better understand the temporal displacement phenomenon.
