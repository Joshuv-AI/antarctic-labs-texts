The Spiked Helmet Sign
Authors: Arjun Patel, Nisha Palla, Maria Abraham, Luka Petrovic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCritically ill patients often develop electrocardiographic (ECG) changes that are difficult to interpret, making it challenging to distinguish primary cardiac pathology from ECG manifestations of systemic illness. The spiked helmet sign (SHS) is a recognized pattern in this context, and real-time identification may be challenging.Case SummaryA 45-year-old man with HIV/AIDS admitted for pneumonia developed septic shock, stress-induced cardiomyopathy, and multiorgan failure. Telemetry monitoring revealed a wide complex rhythm, initially interpreted as ventricular tachycardia, and synchronized cardioversion was performed without effect. A 12-lead electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm with ST-segment elevations occurring before the QRS complex, consistent with SHS rather than a ventricular rhythm. Coronary angiogram was deferred, given clinical features not supporting acute coronary syndrome. The patient developed worsening multiorgan failure and died shortly thereafter.DiscussionThe case illustrates the importance of recognition of the SHS in critically ill patients as a marker of ominous prognosis in systemic illness, rather than primary cardiac pathology.Take-Home MessageRecognition of SHS is important to avoid unnecessary cardiac intervention and redirect management toward extracardiac causes of deterioration.Key words: acute heart failure, electrocardiogram, myocardial ischemia

History of Presenting Illness

A 45-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 1-month history of progressive dyspnea, fatigue, and decreased oral intake. On presentation, the patient was noted to be in respiratory distress with worsening hypoxemia.

Initial work-up revealed severe cystic lung disease, which was thought to be secondary to an opportunistic infection in the setting of AIDS. Worsening respiratory failure ensued in the emergency department, ultimately requiring endotracheal intubation, initiation of vasopressor support, and broad-spectrum antibiotics for septic shock.

Past Medical History

The patient's history was significant for HIV/AIDS (viral load: 23,185 cp/mL, CD4 count: 34), seizure disorder, schizoaffective disorder, and gastroesophageal reflux disease. Previous records and family revealed that the patient was not adherent to his medications.

Investigations

During hospitalization, the patient's course was complicated by altered mental status, prompting a noncontrast computed tomography scan of the head, which revealed an acute, large right-sided subdural hematoma without midline shift.

Transthoracic echocardiogram revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 30% with apical wall motion abnormalities, concerning for Takotsubo cardiomyopathy.

After 3 days of broad-spectrum antibiotics, the patient was weaned off vasopressors. Given his transthoracic echocardiogram findings, the decision was made to initiate carvedilol 6.25 mg twice a day for guideline-directed medical therapy. Shortly thereafter, the patient's hemodynamics deteriorated.

His vasopressor requirements increased to epinephrine 10 μg/min, norepinephrine 30 μg/min, phenylephrine 150 μg/min, and vasopressin 0.03 U/min. Dobutamine 5 μg/kg/min was started for suspected cardiogenic component. Ventilator support was escalated to a positive end-expiratory pressure of 12 cm H2O and fraction of inspired oxygen of 100%. Laboratory evaluation at this time demonstrated severe mixed acidosis, elevated lactate, and worsening renal failure as detailed in Table 1. Later that evening, telemetry monitoring revealed a wide complex rhythm at approximately 100 beats per minute, initially suspected to be ventricular tachycardia (VT), and cardioversion was attempted without change in rhythm. The patient was also started on an amiodarone drip. A 12-lead electrocardiogram obtained subsequently demonstrated ST-segment elevations beginning before the onset of the QRS complex, consistent with the spiked helmet sign (Figures 1 and 2). There was absence of precordial concordance, atrioventricular dissociation, capture, or fusion beat and morphologic criteria that would support the diagnosis of VT. Coronary angiogram was not pursued, given low clinical suspicion for acute coronary syndrome.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis included the following: ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, VT, accelerated idioventricular rhythm, noncardiac critical illness, and electrolyte derangements.

Management

The patient was started on low-dose carvedilol for guideline-directed medical therapy, given his echocardiography findings. He subsequently developed worsening hypotension, for which he was started on dobutamine and vasopressors due to concern for concomitant cardiogenic shock developing secondary to the negative ionotropic effects of beta blockers and ongoing septic shock.

After the development of a wide complex rhythm noted on telemetry, the patient underwent synchronized cardioversion for presumed slow VT without clinical or electrocardiographic improvement and was therefore initiated on continuous amiodarone infusion. Review of the postcardioversion electrocardiogram demonstrated absence of precordial concordance, atrioventricular dissociation, capture, or fusion beat and morphologic criteria that would support the diagnosis of VT; therefore, further cardioversions were deferred and amiodarone was discontinued. The ST-segment elevations that were initially concerning for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, prompting initiation of unfractionated heparin, were further recognized as electrocardiographic findings consistent with the spiked helmet sign. These are characterized by ST-segment elevation preceding the onset of the QRS complex, rather than at the J-point, and are reflective of severe systemic illness rather than acute myocardial ischemia. Anticoagulation was therefore discontinued, especially given the risk of worsening bleed in the setting of recent subdural hematoma, and management was redirected toward aggressive supportive care of the patient's underlying shock and progressive respiratory failure.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Despite aggressive supportive care, the patient developed worsening shock with escalating vasopressor requirements. Further evaluation revealed severe hypoxemia and metabolic acidosis, suggesting progressive systemic collapse. The patient rapidly deteriorated and died shortly thereafter. No additional follow-up was possible.

Discussion

The spiked helmet sign is a distinctive electrocardiographic finding characterized by ST-segment elevation that originates before the onset of the QRS complex.1, 2, 3 Morphologically, this pattern is marked by slurring or notching at the J-point, followed by a downsloping ST-segment and broad, deeply inverted T waves, most often observed in the inferior leads. This configuration reflects superimposition of the late T-wave from the preceding cardiac cycle onto the subsequent QRS complex, often in the setting of QT prolongation and relative tachycardia.1 The resulting dome-and-spike configuration closely resembles the historical German military spiked helmet, from which the sign derives its name.

Although the exact pathophysiology of the spiked helmet sign remains incompletely understood, several mechanisms have been proposed. Acute increases in intrathoracic or intra-abdominal pressure resulting in pulsatile epidermal stretch appear to play a significant role, as resolution of the pattern has been observed after decompression of pneumothoraces, reduction of ventilatory pressures, or relief of abdominal distention.2,3 These observations suggest that the mechanical stretch of these structures may induce transient repolarization abnormalities, with anterior lead involvement more frequently associated with thoracic pathology and inferior lead involvement with abdominal processes.3 Sympathetic overstimulation has also been implicated, particularly in critically ill patients experiencing hypoxia, shock, or catecholamine excess. Many reported cases demonstrate concomitant tachycardia and QT prolongation at the time of spiked helmet sign appearance, supporting a catecholamine-mediated effect on ventricular repolarization rather than true transmural ischemia.2,3 Pulsatile diaphragmatic contraction has also been proposed as an additional contributor; however, evidence supporting this mechanism remains limited.3

As discussed, this sign is most often seen in critically ill patients, frequently in the setting of relative tachycardia. Although it has been associated with ventricular fibrillation, malignant ventricular arrhythmias are uncommon, and most patients die from noncardiac causes.3 Prior studies have demonstrated a high in-hospital mortality among patients with this pattern, reflecting severe underlying systemic illness rather than primary cardiac disease.3

This present case demonstrates how spiked helmet sign can be present diffusely and be easily mistaken for ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction or VT on telemetry. Although the patient had severely reduced left ventricular systolic function, laboratory evaluation revealed worsening hypoxemia and metabolic acidosis, suggesting that circulatory collapse rather than primary myocardial ischemia was the main driver of his clinical deterioration. Recognition of the electrocardiographic features of this sign and its implication as a secondary marker rather than a primary cardiac issue was critical in redirecting management away from potentially harmful and invasive cardiac interventions, such as further cardioversions, unnecessary anticoagulation, or cardiac catheterization, and toward treatment of progressive systemic illness.

Conclusions

This ominous electrocardiographic finding should prompt us to urgently explore possible noncardiac etiologies for clinical deterioration. Our patient had a cardiomyopathy that was likely not the culprit of the patient's acute decline because laboratory evaluation revealed worsening acidosis and hypoxemia in the setting of septic shock.
