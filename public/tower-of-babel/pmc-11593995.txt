Effects of Haptoglobin on Early Brain Injury, Vasospasm, and Lymphatic Drainage After Subarachnoid Hemorrhage in Mice
Authors: Fumihiro Kawakita, Hideki Nakajima, Yume Suzuki, Mai Nampei, Hiroki Oinaka, Hidenori Suzuki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Erythrolysis releases free Hb (hemoglobin), which is one of the most upstream and important molecules causing early brain injury and cerebral vasospasm after subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH). The purpose of this study was to investigate if a Hb scavenger protein Hp (haptoglobin) supplementation prevents early brain injury and cerebral vasospasm and influences the lymphatic drainage mechanism in an established SAH model of mice by a blood injection into the prechiasmatic cistern.METHODS:This study consisted of 4 parts, with a total of 317 C57BL/6 male mice undergoing sham or SAH modeling, randomly followed by 24-hour intracerebroventricular infusion of no vehicle, 30.1 mg/mL BSA, 30.1 mg/mL Hp (100%; a mixture of Hp1-1, Hp2-1, and Hp2-2), 50% Hp, 25% Hp, and 12.5% Hp solutions from 30 minutes postmodeling. The effects were evaluated at 24 and 48 hours postmodeling.RESULTS:The 100%Hp decreased mortality and brain edema until 48 hours post-SAH and suppressed post-SAH neurological impairments, Hb infiltrations into the perivascular spaces and brain tissues, fibrinogen-positive microthrombi formation, microglial activation, and caspase-dependent neuronal apoptosis, as well as large-vessel vasospasm on India-ink angiography at 24 hours compared with vehicle-treated SAH mice. The Hp solutions up to 50% concentrations also prevented post-SAH neurological impairments, and those up to 25% concentrations suppressed post-SAH neuronal apoptosis and vasospasm development until 48 hours. Immunohistochemical staining of deep cervical and mandibular lymph nodes demonstrated that Hb was increased in the lymphatic sinus after SAH and was further increased by Hp administration in SAH animals.CONCLUSIONS:This study first showed that an Hp concentrate prevented early brain injury and cerebral vasospasm by inhibiting Hb penetration into brain tissues and increasing lymphatic drainage of free Hb in an established SAH model of mice.

Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) due to aneurysmal rupture remains the most devastating cerebrovascular disorder despite advances in the management. SAH-induced brain injuries consist of early brain injury (EBI) and delayed cerebral ischemia (DCI). The pathophysiology of EBI and DCI is complex, multifactorial, and incompletely elucidated.1,2 However, there is no doubt that blood breakdown products spread into the subarachnoid space play an important role for both brain injuries.1,2 Within several days post-SAH, extravasated erythrocytes are degraded by immunologic and nonimmunological processes and lysed to release free Hb (hemoglobin) into the subarachnoid space,3 which penetrates into the brain parenchyma through the perivascular space.2 Hb and its degradation byproducts induce multiple pathologies, including neuroinflammation, microthrombosis, and cerebral vasospasm, causing EBI and DCI.1,2 Thus, early elimination and detoxification of free Hb in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) are reasonable treatment strategies against EBI and DCI after aneurysmal SAH.4

Hp (haptoglobin) is a natural antagonist for extracellular Hb toxicity.5 Free Hb is immediately and irreversibly bound by Hp, forming a Hb-Hp complex to prevent a series of Hb-driven neuropathic reactions.5 The liver and reticuloendothelial system mainly synthesize Hp, which diffuses into the CSF from the plasma to a limited extent.5 As a result, endogenous CSF concentrations of Hp are insufficient to provide protection against Hb-induced neurotoxic effects after SAH.4 A few animal studies compared the effects of injecting free Hb and Hb-Hp complexes into the CSF and showed neuroprotective effects of Hp against Hb-induced toxic reactions.6–8 The aim of this animal study was to examine neuroprotective effects of Hp in a manner closer to clinical practice, in which Hp was intrathecally administered after creating a SAH model, and to investigate the effects of Hp infusion on EBI, vasospasm, and lymphatic drainage of Hb after SAH.

Methods

The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article and the Supplemental Material.

Animals

Our institution’s Animal Ethics Review Committee approved all procedures, which were performed according to the Animals in Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments guidelines. C57BL/6 mice (male; age, 10−12 weeks; weight, 25−30 g; SLC, Hamamatsu, Japan) were kept on 12-hour light and dark cycles in temperature- and humidity-controlled rooms with free access to food and water. We calculated the study group sizes necessary to ensure that all data sets provide an 80% power level for the detection of the anticipated differences between groups with a 2-tailed alpha level of 0.05. After modeling, mice were randomized until final group sizes reached the planned experimental number by drawing lots. Data collection and analyses were performed by an investigator blinded to study groups.

Drug

Undiluted Hp solution (30.1 mg or 20 units/mL, 100%; 1-unit Hp binds to 1 mg Hb; molecular weight [MW], Hp1-1=86 kDa, Hp2-1=indeterminate, Hp2-2≥200 kDa) from human plasma was provided by the Japan Blood Products Organization (Tokyo, Japan). Hp solution was diluted to 50%, 25%, and 12.5% concentrations by adding the vehicle (sterile physiological saline). Bovine serum albumin (Alb; Nacalai Tesque, Kyoto, Japan) was also diluted with saline to the same concentration as 100% Hp.

SAH Model and Study Protocol

SAH models were produced by a prechiasmatic cisternal injection of autologous blood as previously described.9 Animals were anesthetized by an intraperitoneal injection of 250 μg/g body weight (BW) of midazolam (4 mg/kg BW), medetomidine (0.75 mg/kg BW), and butorphanol tartrate (4 mg/kg BW) combination anesthetics. After fixation of the head in a stereotactic frame, a burr hole was drilled 4.5 mm anterior to the bregma and 0.5 mm to the right. A 27-gauge spinal needle was inserted at a 40° angle through the burr hole into the prechiasmatic cistern, and then 100 μL of nonheparinized autologous blood, which was collected from the tail, was manually injected for over 15 seconds. In the sham operation, 100 μL physiological saline was injected similarly into the prechiasmatic cistern.

After modeling, another burr hole was drilled 0.2 mm posterior to the bregma and 1.0 mm to the right. A ventricular cannula connected to an ALZET minipump (Model No. 2001D; Durect Corp, Cupertino, CA), which was filled with drug solution and placed subcutaneously, was inserted into the right lateral ventricle to a depth of 2.5 mm through the burr hole.7 The drug solution was intracerebroventricularly and continuously infused at a rate of 8 μL/h regardless of the animal’s BW for 24 hours from 30 minutes postmodeling. Body temperature was kept constant at 37 °C, and blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation were measured noninvasively from the tail during surgery. All animals were used, and no exclusions were made.

This study consisted of 4 experiments (Table S1). The first experiment was performed to check for nonspecific reactions to intracerebroventricular infusion of saline and protein on SAH severity and brain injuries. Mice were randomly divided into the following groups: Sham without intracerebroventricular infusion, sham+saline, sham+Alb, SAH without intracerebroventricular infusion, SAH+saline, and SAH+Alb groups (n=4, respectively). Neurobehavioral tests, SAH grade, and brain water content (BWC) were evaluated 24 hours postmodeling.

In the second experiment, to assess undiluted Hp solution’s neuroprotective effects, 122 mice were randomly divided into the sham+saline (n=30), sham+Hp (n=24), SAH+saline (n=38), and SAH+Hp (n=30) groups. Neuroscore, SAH severity, and BWC were evaluated at 24 and 48 hours postmodeling. India-ink angiography, Western blotting, and immunohistochemical staining, including double immunolabeling for the terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) and MAP-2 (microtubule-associated protein 2) were assessed at 24 hours postmodeling.

The third experiment was conducted to elucidate the dose-response relationships of Hp against Hb’s toxicities. SAH mice received intracerebroventricular infusion of saline or 100% to 12.5% concentrations of Hp solution and were evaluated with neuroscores at 24 and 48 hours and India-ink angiography at 48 hours postmodeling.

In the fourth experiment, to confirm neuroprotective effects of Hp and to examine the relationship between Hp’s neuroprotective effects and the lymphatic drainage of Hb, 111 mice were randomly divided into the sham+saline (n=20), SAH+saline (n=25), SAH+Hp (n=20), SAH+50% Hp (n=24), and SAH+25% Hp (n=22) groups, followed by the assessment of neuroscore, SAH severity, and India-ink angiography at 24 hours, and neuroscore, SAH severity, TUNEL staining of neurons, and Hb staining of deep cervical and mandibular lymph nodes (LNs) at 48 hours postmodeling.

Neurobehavioral Test

Neurobehavior was blindly assessed using the modified Garcia’s neuroscore system.10 Neurological function was evaluated using the sum of the 6 neuroscores.

SAH Severity or Grades

SAH grades were calculated blindly at each sacrifice as previously described.10 The amount of SAH was graded from 0 to 3 for each of the 6 parts of the basal and prepontine cisterns and evaluated as the severity of SAH per animal using the total score.

Brain Edema

Brain edema was assessed using the wet/dry method to measure BWC.11 From euthanized mice under deep anesthesia, the left and right cerebral hemispheres and cerebellum were quickly harvested and weighed (wet weight), followed by being dried in an oven at 105 °C for 72 hours to measure the dry weight. Each part’s BWC was calculated by the following formula: [(wet weight−dry weight)/wet weight]×100%.

India-Ink Angiography

India-ink angiography was performed as previously reported.12 Briefly, the right atrium was cut to allow perfusate to flow out, and then 50 mL of PBS, 15 minutes of 10% formalin, and 10 minutes of 3.5% gelatin-India-ink solution were infused through the cannula inserted into the ascending aorta at an injection pressure of 60 to 80 mm Hg. After mice were refrigerated at 4 °C for 24 hours to solidify the gelatin within cerebral arteries, the brains were removed, photographed with a scale, and stored in 10% neutral-buffered formalin. An experienced person blindly measured the smallest lumen diameter within the precommunicating segment of the anterior cerebral artery, sphenoidal segment of the middle cerebral artery, intradural internal carotid artery (ICA), and basilar artery 3 times using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD) and determined the mean value per segment.

Western Blot Analysis

Western blotting was performed using the right (blood-injection side) cerebral hemisphere as previously described.10 The polyvinylidene difluoride membrane onto which proteins were transferred was blocked with 5% Alb for 2 hours at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit polyclonal anti-Hb (LS-B12169, 1:2000; LSBio, Seattle, WA), monoclonal anti-Iba-1 (ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (ab178847, 1:1000; Abcam, Cambridge, MA), and polyclonal anti-cleaved caspase-3 (ab214430, 1:2500; Abcam) antibodies. As a loading control, β-tubulin (2146, 1:4000; Cell Signaling Technology, Danvers, MA) was blotted on the same membrane. Immunoreactive bands were detected with a chemiluminescence reagent kit (ECL Prime; Amersham Bioscience, Arlington Heights, IL) and quantified by densitometry with ImageJ software. Each protein level was expressed as a ratio of β-tubulin levels for normalization.

Immunohistochemical Staining

The brains and deep cervical and mandibular LNs fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin were used for immunohistochemical staining as previously described.10 Sections were cut to a thickness of 4 µm; for brains, sections were coronally cut at 1.0 mm posterior to the bregma. Antigen retrieval with heat was performed in 1 mmol/L ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (pH 8.0) at 80 °C for 20 minutes. After quenching any endogenous peroxidase activity, sections were blocked with 10% normal goat serum for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated with rabbit polyclonal anti-Hb (LS-B12169, 1:100; LSBio), monoclonal anti-Iba-1 (ab178847, 1:2000; Abcam), and polyclonal anti-fibrinogen (ab27913, 1:500; Abcam) antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with biotinylated goat anti-rabbit polyclonal immunoglobulin G (1:200; Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA) and then with an avidin-biotin-horseradish peroxidase complex (Vectastain Elite ABC Rabbit HPR Kit; Vector Laboratories). The sections were visualized by diaminobenzidine/hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) solution and counterstained with hematoxylin for light microscopic examination. Expression levels of each protein were evaluated on 4 continuous pictures of the right (blood-injection side) temporal cortex magnified ×200 and 1 microscopic field of deep cervical and mandibular LNs magnified ×100 and ×40, respectively, and the relative quantity of the proteins was calculated by Image Pro Plus 6.0 software (Media Cybernetics, Rockville, MD): Hb expression levels in LNs were calculated on a pixel-by-pixel basis to correct for differences in LN size.

Double Immunolabeling for TUNEL and MAP-2

TUNEL staining was performed using a commercially available kit (ver. 15; Roche, Mannheim, Germany).11 Four micrometer-thick coronal sections at 1.0 mm posterior to the bregma were incubated with proteinase K, TUNEL, 3% H2O2, and peroxidase solutions in order, and diaminobenzidine was used as the chromogen. Heat-induced antigen retrieval and inactivation of peroxidases by 3% H2O2 were performed to prevent cross-reaction with the following immunostaining.11 After being blocked with normal goat serum, the sections were incubated with rabbit polyclonal anti-MAP-2 antibody (ab32454, 1:1000; Abcam), biotinylated goat anti-rabbit polyclonal immunoglobulin G (1:200; Vector Laboratories), and an avidin-biotin complex in order. Alkaline phosphatase-mediated reaction colored the sections with BCIP/NBT as chromogen (Vectaistain Elite ABC Rabbit AP Kit, SK-5400; Vector Laboratories). Cells with oval nuclei and prominent nucleoli were used as reference findings for intact neurons, and pyknotic, amorphous, fragmented, and shrunken nuclei were considered to indicate degenerating neurons morphologically suggesting apoptosis.10 TUNEL-positive neurons were counted in 4 consecutive microscopic fields of the right temporal cortex magnified ×400, and the percentage of TUNEL-positive neurons was calculated using the formula: (number of TUNEL-positive neurons/total number of neurons)×100%.10

Statistics

Data were analyzed using SPSS, version 26 (IBM Corp, Chicago, IL). χ2 or Fisher exact tests were used to compare the mortality. Neuroscore and SAH grade were expressed as medians ±25th to 75th percentiles and compared by Mann-Whitney U tests or Kruskal-Wallis tests followed by Mann-Whitney U test post hoc tests with Bonferroni correction as appropriate. Other values were described as means±SD and analyzed using unpaired t tests or 1-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Student-Newman-Keuls post hoc tests as appropriate. The Kaplan-Meier method was used for cumulative survival rates with the log-rank test for assessing the statistical differences among SAH study groups. A P<0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Neuroprotective Effects of Hp

The first experiment demonstrated that 24-hour intracerebroventricular infusion of saline or Alb had no effects on neuroscore, SAH severity, or brain edema in both sham-operated and SAH-operated animals (Figures S1 through S3). Therefore, in subsequent experiments, vehicle (saline)-treated mice were used as a control group.

In the second experiment, a total of 126 mice underwent sham operation or SAH modeling, and 4 SAH mice died before randomization (Table S1). Physiological parameters were not significantly different among the groups. No sham-operated animals (n=54) died within the 48-hour observation period. The 48-hour mortality of the SAH+saline mice (21.1%, 8/38 animals) was significantly higher than that of the SAH+Hp group (0%, 0/30 animals). SAH grading scores of the remaining animals were not significantly different between the groups at both 24 and 48 hours (Figure S4).

At 24 hours postmodeling, SAH caused neurological impairments associated with increased BWC in the bilateral cerebral hemispheres and TUNEL-positive neurons in the right temporal cortex compared with sham-operated animals. The Hp infusion significantly prevented all of them compared with the SAH+saline group (Figure 1; Figure S5). At 48 hours postmodeling, although there were no significant differences in neuroscores among the groups, BWC in the left cerebral hemisphere was increased after SAH and suppressed by the Hp infusion (Figure S6).

Western blot analyses showed that the Hp infusion prevented post-SAH upregulation of cleaved caspase-3 in the right cerebral hemisphere at 24 hours; taken together with the TUNEL staining results, the Hp infusion inhibited caspase-dependent neuronal apoptosis (Figures 1C and 2). As Western blotting also suggested that the Hp infusion significantly suppressed post-SAH increases in Hb infiltration into brain tissues and Iba-1-positive microglial activation, Hb toxicities-related pathologies were further investigated at 24 hours postmodeling. India-ink angiography demonstrated that the Hp infusion did not influence the diameter of cerebral arteries in sham-operated mice but significantly prevented post-SAH vasospasm development of bilateral anterior cerebral arteries, middle cerebral arteries, and ICAs (Figures S7 and S8). Immunohistochemistry demonstrated post-SAH increases in Hb infiltration into the perivascular spaces and brain tissues, microglial activation, and fibrinogen-positive microthrombi formation, all of which were prevented in the SAH+Hp group (Figure S9).

Dose-Response Relationship of Hp on Cerebral Vasospasm After 48 Hours

The third experiment was conducted to determine the minimum concentration of Hp that suppressed vasospasm at 48 hours using 52 SAH mice. The mortality, physiological parameters, and SAH grades were not significantly different among the SAH groups (Table S1; Figure S10).

Hp solutions up to 50% concentrations significantly prevented post-SAH neurological impairments at 24 hours and vasospasm of bilateral anterior cerebral arteries, bilateral middle cerebral arteries, and the right ICA at 48 hours, while Hp solutions up to 25% concentrations significantly suppressed post-SAH neurological impairments at 48 hours and vasospasm of the left ICA at 48 hours compared with the saline administration group (Figures S11 and S12). Thus, undiluted, 50%, and 25% concentrations of Hp solutions were selected for the following experiment.

Reconfirmation of Neuroprotective Effects of Hp and the Role of Lymphatic Drainage

In experiment 4, there were no significant differences in physiological parameters and SAH grades among the study groups, although 48-hour mortality of SAH mice tended to be lower in the Hp-treated group (Table S1; Figure S13).

Undiluted and a 50% concentration of Hp solution-treated SAH mice showed a significant improvement in neuroscores and vasospasm of bilateral anterior cerebral arteries, middle cerebral arteries, and ICAs, and a 25% concentration of Hp also suppressed post-SAH vasospasm of the left ICA compared with the saline-treated SAH mice at 24 hours (Figure 3; Figure S14). Moreover, Hp solutions up to 50% and 25% concentrations significantly prevented post-SAH aggravation of neuroscores and increases in TUNEL-positive neurons in the right temporal cortex at 48 hours, respectively (Figure 4).

Survival Benefit From Hp Administration

No side effects were observed from Hp administration throughout the study. Considering the results from experiments 2 to 4 together, Kaplan-Meier plots revealed a significant improvement in survival of SAH animals treated with Hp (Figure 6).

Discussion

This study first showed that post-SAH intrathecal administration of a clinically available Hb scavenger Hp inhibited not only neurological impairments and cerebral vasospasm but also brain edema, microglial activation, microthrombi formation, and neuronal apoptosis, and that the neuroprotective effects of Hp were associated with decreased infiltration of free Hb into brain parenchyma and enhanced lymphatic drainage of free Hb. The pathophysiological mechanisms of secondary brain injury after SAH are complex, but free Hb has been thought to be a potent driver of multiple pathologies, including neuroinflammation, microthrombosis, neuronal apoptosis, and cerebral vasospasm, causing EBI in an acute phase and DCI in a delayed phase.1,2 The Hp treatment is promising to block Hb toxicity and to prevent or reduce both EBI and DCI after SAH.

Hp is a natural Hb-scavenging protein and irreversibly binds free Hb to prevent its auto-oxidation or toxicity.5 However, Hp is mainly synthesized by the liver and the reticuloendothelial system and scarcely diffuses into the CSF; therefore, endogenous CSF concentrations of Hp are much lower than the amount of free Hb released by erythrolysis after SAH.13 Thus, intrathecal administration of Hp to form Hb-Hp complexes before free Hb initiates a series of pathological reactions is considered to be an effective Hb-scavenging strategy. In an old preliminary study using dogs, large-vessel vasospasm that occurred 3 days after a cisternal injection of free Hb was reversed by a cisternal injection of Hp (phenotype 2-2 predominant).14 In a recent study using healthy sheep and mice in which free Hb or Hb-Hp (phenotype 2-2 predominant) complexes were intracerebroventricularly injected, large Hb-Hp complexes interrupted free Hb infiltration from the CSF compartment into cerebral arterial or arteriolar walls and brain tissues within 2 hours postinjection and prevented free Hb-induced hyperacute large-vessel and microvascular vasospasm.8 In contrast, 3-day bi-daily intracerebroventricular injections of either free Hb or Hb-Hp (unknown Hp phenotypes) complexes in healthy sheep showed similar findings as to microglial activation, iron deposition in the brain, and microvascular vasospasm between the 2 groups, although neurobehavior was improved and perivascular histiocytic infiltrates tended to be enhanced in the Hb-Hp group.6 Another study using healthy mice showed that 2-week intracerebroventricular infusion of Hb-Hp (phenotype 1-1 predominant) complexes reduced 2-week infusion of free Hb-induced toxicities on astrocytes, microglia, and synapses, as well as microvascular vasospasm by inhibiting diffusion of free Hb into the brain parenchyma, but failed to attenuate large-vessel vasospasm.7 The somewhat inconsistent results of these previous studies may be explained by the MW of the administered Hb-Hp complex, which was synthesized in vitro. That is, Hb-Hp complexes are formed with MWs between 162 kDa in the simplest dimeric form and 1760 kDa in the highest-order polymer, and the larger the MW, the harder they are to enter the brain and vessel walls.3 Hp2-2 forms larger Hp polymers, of which MWs ranged from 199 kDa (tetramers) to >1000 kDa (>20 mers) compared with Hp1-1, whereas Hp1-1 produces dimers with a MW of 89 kDa.15 On the contrary, lower MWs of Hp1-1 may have more neuroprotective, antioxidative, and anti-inflammatory qualities, possibly by better clearance of Hb-Hp complexes from the subarachnoid space compared with Hp2-2.16 Thus, all phenotypes of Hp may contribute to Hb-scavenging and detoxification strategies and were included in the Hp solution used in this study.

In the early stages of SAH, some extravasated erythrocytes may exit the central nervous system via the meningeal lymphatic system.17 However, most extravasated erythrocytes undergo osmotic cell lysis by activated complements and other mechanisms, or phagocytosis by microglia and CSF/blood-derived, meningeal, and perivascular macrophages as the days pass.3,18 Erythrophagocytes detoxify Hb and may leave the intracranial space across the nasal epithelium and along cranial and spinal nerves leading to deep cervical and mandibular LNs, via meningeal lymphatic vessels along the dural sinuses, by direct transport to the vascular system across the arachnoid villi, and by crossing back into the vascular system through the disrupted blood-brain barrier or the blood-CSF barrier within the choroid plexus.17–20 On the contrary, hyperphagic macrophages engulfing ≥2 erythrocytes undergo cell death, resulting in releases of deleterious hemes and irons into the extracellular spaces.5 In addition, the glymphatic system is impaired with aging,20 and meningeal or sinus-associated lymphatic drainage is blocked possibly by blood clots after SAH.2,17 Therefore, free Hb is sustained in the CSF for a long time by not only slow clearance but also continuous and gradual erythrolytic releases from the blood clots.18 Erythrocytes and free Hb in the subarachnoid space can enter the intraparenchymal perivascular space.3,5 In the CSF, free Hb shifts from tetramers to dimers (32 kDa), which can penetrate into brain tissues along the perivascular space to induce pathologies such as microvascular vasospasm, microthrombosis, cortical spreading depolarization, and seizure, as well as into the arterial walls to cause large-vessel vasospasm18,21,22; molecules with a MW ≤100 kDa can distribute across tissue barriers between the CSF and the brain, such as the pia mater, ependymal cell layer, and glia limitans,23 and penetrate deep into brain tissues from the perivascular space through endfoot-endfoot gaps between adjacent astrocytes, while molecules with a MW >200 kDa are limitedly cleared from the perivascular space.3,5 Hb dimers irreversibly bind to Hp at a ratio of 1 Hb dimer per Hp monomer subunit to antagonize Hb toxicity,18 but the reactions to inhibit the action of nitric oxide, a representative vasodilator, remain the same between free Hb and Hb-Hp complexes.23 Hb-Hp complexes are endocytosed by microglia and macrophages through the scavenger receptor CD163.3 Under pathological conditions such as SAH, neurons also upregulate CD163, but this leads to cell death when neurons endocytose the complexes.5 Thus, it is important to form a higher MW of Hb-Hp2-2 complex in the CSF that cannot enter the interstitium of the brain or the nitric oxide compartments in the walls of blood vessels, while it is better to form a smaller MW of Hb-Hp1-1 complex that is advantageous for clearance once free Hb has entered them. The Hp solution used in this study contained both Hp1-1 and Hp2-2 and effectively scavenged the slowly hemolyzed and liberated Hb, resulting in suppressed penetration into brain tissues and augmented lymphatic drainage of Hb, by which multiple pathologies underlying EBI and large-vessel vasospasm were prevented.

This study has some limitations, including that the optimal ratio of Hp phenotypes, dosage, and duration of administration are unknown. However, this experimental study showed for the first time that clinically available Hp solution administered after SAH was effective against EBI and cerebral vasospasm. It is awaited that this result will be clinically proven.
