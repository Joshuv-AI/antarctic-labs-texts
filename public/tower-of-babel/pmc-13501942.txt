Association Between Anterior Hippocampal Gyrification and Episodic Memory Performance in Neurotypical Young Adults
Authors: Nima Talaei, Jordan DeKraker, Bradley G. Karat, Ali R. Khan, Stefan Köhler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe hippocampus is critical for episodic memory. The relationship between its structural variability and individual differences in episodic memory performance among neurotypical young adults, however, remains unclear. Previous studies have not consistently found associations between hippocampal volume and episodic memory or other aspects of hippocampally dependent performance in this population. Here, we moved beyond volumetric approaches and tested whether more fine‐grained morphometric features, particularly gyrification, are related to behavioral performance. To obtain these measures, we used HippUnfold, a deep learning‐based segmentation tool that models the hippocampus as a folded cortical ribbon and uniquely quantifies local variability in folding patterns, including in the hippocampal head. We focused on an open‐access dataset with high‐resolution MRI scans and multiple behavioral measures on hippocampal‐dependent tasks, including episodic and semantic components of the Autobiographical Interview. To improve segmentation quality for the high‐resolution images in this dataset, we trained an nnU‐Net segmentation model for HippUnfold that was optimized for high‐resolution T2w images. Volumetric and surface‐based measures were computed for 186 participants (97 female). We found no reliable associations between hippocampal volumetric measures, including subfield volumes, and behavioral performance. In contrast, surface‐based analysis of hippocampal gyrification revealed significant bilateral clusters in the anterior hippocampus, both of which were associated with measures of episodic but not semantic autobiographical memory. The right hemisphere cluster was located in the subiculum and CA1, whereas the left hemisphere cluster was primarily situated in the subiculum. These findings demonstrate the utility of surface‐based hippocampal morphometry for linking hippocampal structure to behavioral performance. They suggest that even in neurotypical young adults individual differences in episodic memory are tied to structural hippocampal variation.

Introduction

A large body of literature supports the idea that the hippocampus plays a central role in episodic memory. Additional evidence implicates this structure in scene imagination, spatial navigation, and future thinking (Moscovitch et al. 2016). Lesion studies have shown that damage to the hippocampus impairs the ability to recall autobiographical episodes, imagine new experiences, and navigate spatial environments (Moscovitch et al. 2016). This notion has been further supported by functional MRI studies demonstrating hippocampal activation during tasks involving these cognitive functions (Zeidman and Maguire 2016). This body of work has also motivated extensive efforts to determine whether variations in hippocampal structure underlie individual differences in these abilities, in the absence of neurological conditions (Clark et al. 2020; Griesbauer et al. 2022; Palombo et al. 2018; Poppenk and Moscovitch 2011). While prior research has shown a reliable link between hippocampal gray matter volume and extreme spatial navigation ability, such as the enlarged posterior hippocampi observed in London taxi drivers (Griesbauer et al. 2022), in the general population, the relationship between hippocampal gray matter volume and individual differences in cognition is less clear (Weisberg et al. 2019). Notably, one major study recently questioned whether any reliable associations between hippocampal volume and performance measures exist in neurotypical young adults (Clark et al. 2020). These authors selected experimental tasks in episodic memory, scene imagination, spatial navigation, and future thinking that had previously revealed hippocampal engagement in fMRI and/or lesion research. In a large sample (N = 217), they found no significant relationships between performance measures and hippocampal gray matter volume, even when considering anterior and posterior sections separately. Given increasing evidence suggesting functional differentiation of hippocampal subfields, one possible explanation for these findings lies in the limitations of not considering subfield‐specific volumes (Olsen et al. 2012). Another explanation relates to the nature of volumetric analyses more broadly, which provide only a coarse measure that may obscure finer‐grained morphological features. It remains unclear whether other morphometric measures, particularly those capturing local structural variations, such as gyrification and thickness, are associated with individual differences in performance on hippocampal‐dependent tasks.

Gyrification, in particular, is a promising yet understudied feature of hippocampal anatomy. While neocortical gyrification has been extensively investigated and linked to cognitive abilities, such as general intelligence and working memory (Gautam et al. 2015; Gregory et al. 2016; Luders et al. 2008), the role of hippocampal gyrification in cognition has received limited attention. Emerging evidence suggests that the hippocampus, particularly its anterior subdivision, exhibits significant individual differences in folding patterns (Ding and Van Hoesen 2015). A small number of studies that considered variation in hippocampal dentations relied on manual or semi‐automated methods to assess hippocampal gyrification (Beattie et al. 2017; Kilpattu Ramaniharan et al. 2023). Recent developments in hippocampal segmentation techniques, by contrast, allow for fully automated quantification of these features with greater anatomical precision, including in its anterior subsection (DeKraker et al. 2021, 2022). HippUnfold, a deep learning‐based tool that models the hippocampus as a folded cortical ribbon, enables surface‐based morphometric analyses that preserve the anatomical continuity of subfields and allow for the measurement of local thickness and gyrification across the entire structure (DeKraker et al. 2018, 2023). This methodological advance offers an opportunity to move beyond traditional volumetric analyses and examine whether these surface‐based features are associated with behavioral performance in neurotypical young adults.

In the current study, we investigated the relationship between morphological measures derived from HippUnfold and performance on hippocampally dependent tasks in the publicly available dataset collected by Clark and Maguire (2023). We focused on their main hippocampally dependent performance measures on four experimental tasks in episodic autobiographical memory, scene imagination, future thinking, and navigation. To determine the specificity of any observed relationship, we also included a measure of semantic memory (external details in the Autobiographical Interview [AI]), which is generally viewed as non‐hippocampal dependent in the literature. HippUnfold was used to segment the hippocampus from high‐resolution T2‐weighted MRI scans and to derive volumetric (including for subfields) and surface‐based measures of morphology. Our main goal was to determine whether automated measures of gyrification would provide new insight as to how variations in hippocampal morphology relate to individual differences in hippocampally dependent cognition.

Materials and Methods

Overview of Dataset

We used data from an open‐access dataset that includes cognitive and multimodal MRI data from 217 healthy adults (mean age = 29 years, range = 20–41) (Clark and Maguire 2023). This dataset was designed to allow for examination of individual differences in cognition, with a particular focus on memory and the hippocampus. The protocol contained extensive cognitive assessments, including a set of real‐world tasks probing hippocampal‐dependent cognitive functions, including but not limited to episodic memory. Neuroimaging data consist of multimodal MRI acquisitions, including high‐resolution T2‐weighted structural scans for 201 participants, which were used in the current study. The dataset also includes manually segmented hippocampal subfields for all participants for whom T2‐weighted structural scans are available.

MRI Data

We employed T2‐weighted structural MRI scans from 201 participants in the Clark and Maguire (2023) dataset, acquired on three different 3 Tesla scanners using a high‐resolution 3D T2‐weighted turbo spin echo sequence. All scanners were Siemens Magnetom TIM Trio systems, each equipped with a 32‐channel head coil. They were located at the same imaging center, running identical software. The scans have a spatial resolution of 0.52 × 0.52 × 0.5 mm, with 104 partitions and a matrix size of 384 × 328. Imaging parameters include a repetition time (TR) of 3200 ms and an echo time (TE) of 353 ms. Each participant underwent three T2‐weighted scans that were co‐registered and averaged to generate the final structural image. These scans were used for manual hippocampal subfield segmentation that was included in the published dataset. Segmentation was conducted by two researchers following the Dalton et al. (2017) protocol using ITK‐SNAP software (Yushkevich et al. 2006). The segmented subfields include the dentate gyrus (DG)/CA4, CA2/3, CA1, subiculum, pre/parasubiculum, and the uncus. Full details on MRI acquisition parameters and manual segmentation can be found in Clark and Maguire (2023).

Behavioral Data

The dataset includes data from four real‐world cognitive tasks assessing autobiographical memory recall, scene imagination, future thinking, and navigation. These cognitive domains were selected based on their hippocampal dependencies established in prior literature (Clark et al. 2020; Clark and Maguire 2023). Autobiographical memory was assessed with the widely used AI (Levine et al. 2002), in which participants describe specific personal memories from early childhood, teenage years, adulthood, and the past year. Responses were transcribed and scored for the inclusion of internal as well as external detail, following established protocols (Levine et al. 2002). Internal details refer to the episodic components of specific autobiographical memories, that is, information directly related to the specific event being recalled. We will refer to them as episodic details in the remainder of this article. They include descriptions of what happened (nature of event), when it happened (time), where it took place, how it was perceived (sensory details), and the thoughts or emotions experienced and now recalled. These details are considered hippocampal‐dependent and reflect the richness of memory recall. External details, by contrast, reflect non‐episodic aspects of the narrative, including general knowledge that is semantic in nature, repetitions, details from other events, and off‐topic content. The main outcome measure for episodic‐memory performance in this task is the total number of episodic details recalled. Scene imagination was evaluated using the Scene Construction Task (Hassabis et al. 2007). In this task, participants receive verbal prompts (e.g., “Imagine you are by the swimming pool of a luxury hotel”) and generate detailed descriptions of the imagined scene. The task was designed to assess scene construction rather than autobiographical memory retrieval. Following the original Hassabis et al. (2007) procedure, participants were instructed not to describe a remembered event or any part of one, but instead to construct a novel imagined scene in response to the verbal prompt. Responses were analyzed using the Experiential Index, which quantifies scene richness, spatial coherence, and vividness. For both the scene imagination and future thinking tasks, higher Experiential Index scores indicate more richly imagined, vivid, and spatially coherent constructions. As in Clark et al. (2020), this measure served as the main outcome measure for scene imagination. Future thinking was examined using the same procedure as the scene construction task. In this case, it requires participants to imagine and describe three future events, occurring in the near future (e.g., the upcoming weekend), medium‐term future (e.g., next Christmas), and the distant future (e.g., a college reunion 10 years after graduation). Data from this task were analyzed using the same scoring framework as for the Scene Construction Task. Navigation ability was assessed using a task described by Woollett and Maguire (2010), which features video footage of two overlapping routes through an unfamiliar town. Following video exposure, participants are tested on landmark recognition, spatial relationships, and route ordering. The main outcome measure employed for this task was the overall navigation score, which is derived from multiple subcomponents, including clip recognition, scene recognition, proximity judgments, route knowledge, and sketch mapping (Clark and Maguire 2023). Similar to the navigation task, the scored data from all other tasks includes other subcomponents. In the current study, we only focused on the main outcome measure from each task in our analyses, as they arguably reflect the most robust overall estimate of participants' performance. Moreover, this choice ensured reliance on a limited number of statistical comparisons in our inferences. For the AI, however, we also included the measure for external (semantic) details in our analyses in order to examine whether hippocampal morphology would contribute differentially to episodic and semantic memory.

Hippocampal Segmentation Using HippUnfold

To segment the hippocampus and extract morphological measures from MRI images, we used HippUnfold version 1.5.3, a deep learning‐based segmentation tool designed to model the hippocampus as a folded cortical ribbon. This tool facilitates subfield segmentation and provides morphometric measures from MRI data. Unlike traditional volumetric approaches, HippUnfold employs a topological framework that accounts for interindividual variation in hippocampal folding, while ensuring accurate inter‐subject alignment (DeKraker et al. 2022).

As a first major step, HippUnfold employs the nnUNet deep learning architecture (Isensee et al. 2021) to segment hippocampal gray matter from surrounding structures. After performing hippocampal segmentation, HippUnfold maps the hippocampus into an unfolded common coordinate space, enabling surface‐based morphometric analysis (DeKraker et al. 2023). This transformation is achieved by solving Laplace's equation along the anterior–posterior (AP), proximal–distal (PD), and inner‐outer (IO) axes, generating an intrinsic geodesic coordinate system. The resulting Laplace fields, ranging from 0 to 1 along each axis, provide a standardized framework for indexing hippocampal subfields while preserving their topological continuity. Subfield boundaries are then defined in unfolded space using a reference atlas derived from histology, which are mapped back into each subject's native folded space to produce topologically consistent subfield segmentations (DeKraker et al. 2022). Within this unfolded representation, HippUnfold defines three surfaces: inner, mid‐thickness, and outer surfaces, which correspond to the white, mid‐thickness, and pial surfaces in the cortex. These surfaces were generated at a surface density of 0.5 mm, meaning that the average spacing between adjacent vertices is approximately 0.5 mm, providing a high‐resolution mesh for morphometric analysis. Hippocampal thickness is computed as the distance between the inner and outer hippocampal surfaces at each vertex. Gyrification is quantified at each vertex as the ratio of the native 3D surface area to the unfolded 2D surface area, capturing the degree of folding within the surface (see DeKraker et al. 2022, for further detail).

To ensure the accuracy of hippocampal morphological feature extraction, particularly gyrification, the deep learning segmentation of hippocampal gray matter made use of the synthseg_v0.2 model as opposed to the default T2w model. The conventional T2w model was trained with lower‐resolution 0.7 mm isotropic data (HCP Young Adult dataset), thus when applied to the higher‐resolution data used in this study (~0.5 mm isotropic, nearly 3× smaller voxel volumes), the segmentation can produce over‐smoothed hippocampal boundaries, potentially underestimating complex structures such as the uncus and tail (DeKraker et al. 2020, 2023). In contrast, the HippUnfold synthseg_v0.2 model leverages prior knowledge of hippocampal folding patterns by incorporating images synthesized from high‐resolution ex vivo hippocampal segmentations during its training, making it well‐suited for capturing hippocampal gyrification, in particular in its head and tail. The segmentation model is trained using SynthSeg (Billot et al. 2023), a method that generates synthetic MR images from labeled segmentations while applying randomized contrast and intensity variations, making the network MRI contrast‐agnostic. nn‐UNet (Isensee et al. 2021) is then used to introduce extensive structural augmentations, including random deformations, smoothing, anisotropic rescaling, and standard MRI artifacts, improving the model's generalization across different datasets. The trained nnUNet segmentation model is integrated into the HippUnfold pipeline that we utilized in the current study and enabled with the “‐‐force‐nnunet‐model synthseg_v0.2” command‐line option. More information about the model and its training process can be found on its GitHub page.

Quality Control: Manual Inspection of HippUnfold Segmentations

As mentioned earlier, the synthseg_v0.2 model is trained on images synthesized from high‐resolution ex vivo hippocampal segmentations, enhancing its ability to capture individual variations in gyrification. The trade‐off, however, is that the use of ex vivo data and the lack of anatomical variability in the surrounding tissue of the synthetic images can contribute to a higher rate of segmentation errors, particularly in regions where nearby structures resemble the hippocampus. To mitigate this concern, we conducted a systematic manual inspection of all participants' segmentations to identify and exclude cases with errors that could affect downstream analyses. Our manual inspection was guided by anatomical criteria described by Ding and Van Hoesen (2015), which served as the foundation for the segmentation approach used in HippUnfold (DeKraker et al. 2022). The paper provides a detailed cytoarchitectural and chemo‐architectural delineation of hippocampal subfields and their corresponding macro‐anatomical landmarks. Segmentations that exhibited severe misalignment with hippocampal boundaries or failed to capture critical subfield distinctions, as described by Ding and Van Hoesen (2015), were excluded. We then used the resulting sample of high‐quality segmentations as training labels for an updated nnUNet model, with the goal of improving segmentation performance for this dataset and increasing the number of participants with high‐quality segmentations available for the brain‐behavior analyses.

Training a New Segmentation Model

Using these quality‐controlled segmentations as training labels, we trained an updated nnUNet segmentation model tailored to the high‐resolution T2‐weighted images in this dataset (Isensee et al. 2021). Training followed the standard procedure described in the nnUNet documentation (nnUNet GitHub page). Preprocessed coronal‐oblique T2‐weighted images and their corresponding segmentation labels were used as training data, with the left and right hemispheres treated as separate samples. Following standard nnUNet preprocessing and dataset integrity checks, we trained a 3D model using five‐fold cross‐validation. Across the five cross‐validation folds, the model achieved stable mean validation Dice scores ranging from 0.735 to 0.759. These scores fall within the range considered good for hippocampal subfield segmentation, particularly given that the mean was computed across all segmentation labels, including small anatomical structures that are expected to yield lower Dice scores (DeKraker et al. 2022). The resulting model, which we named maguire_T2w, was then applied to the full dataset to generate improved segmentations. The trained model is publicly available online (Zenodo page) and has been incorporated into HippUnfold version 1.5.3. Once again, the new segmentations were systematically quality controlled, and any segmentations that did not meet our anatomical criteria were excluded from further analyses.

Volumetric Analyses

We used volumetric measures derived from HippUnfold to investigate the relationships between hippocampal volume measures and behavioral performance measures. Specifically, we examined relationships to anterior and posterior hippocampal volume, as well as to subfield volumes in both hemispheres. The subfield volumes included the subiculum (Sub), CA1, CA2, CA3, CA4, and the DG estimated by HippUnfold, along with their anterior and posterior subdivisions (Figure 1). The anterior and posterior hippocampus were defined based on the Laplace field generated by HippUnfold, which quantifies the geodesic distance from the hippocampal–amygdalar transition area to the posterior terminus of the indusium griseum (see Leferink et al. 2024, for the same approach). This field ranges from zero to one across the hippocampal gray matter (DeKraker et al. 2018, 2022). We used the Laplace field to divide the hippocampus into anterior and posterior regions, with 0 marking the anterior boundary and 1 marking the posterior boundary. Values between 0 and 0.5 were classified as anterior, while those between 0.5 and 1 were classified as posterior. We also applied the same Laplace field definition to examine the relationships between anterior and posterior subdivisions of each subfield and our behavioral measures. We controlled for age, sex, scanner, and intracranial volume in assessing whether each volumetric measure was related to behavioral task performance.

Surface‐Based Analyses

Critically, in addition to volumetric measures, we utilized surface‐based morphometric features computed by HippUnfold, including local hippocampal thickness and gyrification, to investigate their relationships with our behavioral measures. For DG, we only focused on gyrification given the challenges that arise from its unique morphology for thickness measurement. To conduct these analyses, we utilized surface‐based linear models implemented in BrainStat v.0.4.2 (Larivière et al. 2023; https://brainstat.readthedocs.io), which enable mass‐univariate modeling of surface‐based neuroimaging data while correcting for spatial correlation. For each behavioral measure, we fitted independent linear models for gyrification and thickness measures on the left and right hippocampal surfaces. The models included the behavioral variable of interest as the main predictor while controlling for potential confounds, including age, sex, scanner, and intracranial volume. To meet the assumptions of Random Field Theory (RFT) regarding the Gaussian distribution of the data, and to reduce noise across vertices, we applied Gaussian smoothing to both hippocampal and DG surface measures using Connectome Workbench (Van Essen et al. 2013) prior to further analysis. Surface measures were smoothed with a 3 mm Gaussian kernel. We then used the SLM function in BrainStat to apply RFT, define clusters based on a cluster‐forming threshold, and correct for multiple comparisons using the False Discovery Rate (FDR). This approach allowed for a vertex‐wise examination of how hippocampal thickness and gyrification relate to behavioral performance.

Results

Quality Control of Segmentations

After applying HippUnfold to the dataset using the synthseg_v0.2 model, careful manual quality control was performed on all participants' segmentations based on the anatomical criteria described by Ding and Van Hoesen (2015). We visually assessed the segmentations in axial, sagittal, coronal, and 3D views using ITK‐SNAP (Yushkevich et al. 2006), and segmentations were excluded if they exhibited structural inconsistencies, such as holes within hippocampal subfields, overextension into adjacent regions, or errors affecting hippocampal gray matter coverage, including cases where hippocampal structures extended beyond their expected anatomical boundaries or failed to fully capture the gray matter. As a result of this process, we retained 93 participants with high‐quality segmentations, corresponding to 46% of the available participants with high‐resolution T2w images (55 female, mean age = 29.83 years, SD = 5.23), and trained our new model maguire_T2w on this subsample. The list of participants included as the training set, along with examples of common issues that led to the exclusion of some segmentations from the training set, is provided in the supplemental material (Figures S1 and S2). We then applied the maguire_T2w model to the full dataset of 201 participants and performed another round of quality control on the resulting segmentations. This time, only 15 participants had segmentations with issues that required exclusion, resulting in a final sample of 186 participants, corresponding to 92% of the available participants with high‐resolution T2w images (97 female; mean age = 29.08 years, SD = 5.67). Volumetric and surface‐based analyses were then performed on this large sample as our main analyses. The same brain‐behavior analyses were also conducted in the original 93‐participant training subsample, as well as in the independent 93‐participant subsample on which the updated segmentation model was not trained. These results are reported in the Supporting Information for completeness (Table S4; Figures S3–S6).

Volumetric Analysis

In the first stage of the volumetric analysis, we examined the relationships between anterior and posterior hippocampal volumes and behavioral measures, including episodic and external (semantic) details from the AI, as well as performance scores on the Navigation, Scene Imagination, and Future Thinking tasks. For this purpose, we focused on ordinary least‐squares regression while controlling for age, sex, scanner, and intracranial volume (as covariates of no interest). Multiple comparisons were controlled using FDR correction within each stage of the volumetric analyses. Overall, 20 statistical tests were conducted, and no statistically significant associations were found between performance on any behavioral measure and anterior or posterior hippocampal volume in each hemisphere (Table S1).

Next, we conducted similar analyses to examine the relationships between performance on each behavioral task and the volume of each hippocampal subfield (separated by hemisphere) as estimated by HippUnfold, including the Subiculum, CA1, CA2, CA3, CA4, and DG. Out of 60 statistical tests, we found no statistically significant relationships between various subfields and behavioral performance. We next examined whether the anterior or posterior subdivisions of each hippocampal subfield were associated with the behavioral measures. As described in the Methods section, we used Laplace's field along the AP axis, generated by HippUnfold, to divide the hippocampal volume into two equally sized anterior and posterior regions. We then examined the associations between the anterior and posterior subdivisions of each subfield and the behavioral performance on each task. Across 120 analyses, we identified one statistically significant association and four associations that approached significance (Table 1). However, after applying FDR correction separately within each hemisphere (correcting for 60 comparisons per hemisphere), none of these associations remained statistically significant. Surface‐Based Analysis

HippUnfold models the hippocampus and DG as distinct surfaces, providing surface‐based morphometric analysis of both structures. We analyzed thickness and gyrification measures from the hippocampal surfaces, and gyrification measures from the DG surfaces to examine whether local estimates of these morphometric features correlate with behavioral performance. For localization and statistical inference, we relied on random‐field theory. We applied cluster‐forming thresholds of 0.01 and 0.001 for RFT and subsequently used FDR correction across clusters to control for Type I errors (see Larivière et al. 2023 for further justification). Surface‐based plots were generated using the HippoMaps toolbox (DeKraker et al. 2025) to illustrate the spatial pattern of associations between hippocampal morphology and behavioral measures.

Significant clusters were identified in both the right and left anterior hippocampus, showing positive correlations between gyrification and episodic detail scores (Figure 2). In the right anterior hippocampus, a significant cluster showed a positive correlation with episodic details (corrected p = 0.026, cluster size = 400) (Figure 2B). A second significant cluster was identified in the left hippocampus, where gyrification was also positively correlated with episodic details (corrected p = 0.041, cluster size = 220) (Figure 2E). No other significant associations were observed between DG gyrification and behavioral measures, nor were any clusters detected for hippocampal thickness.

To establish the robustness of these findings, we also applied a more stringent cluster‐forming threshold of 0.001. This decision was motivated by broader concerns in neuroimaging that clusterwise inference can be sensitive to the choice of cluster‐forming threshold, with more stringent thresholds often providing better false‐positive control in some settings (Eklund et al. 2016). With this adjustment, the right anterior hippocampal cluster associated with episodic details remained statistically significant (corrected p = 0.011, cluster size = 169), whereas the left hippocampal cluster was no longer significant, although it approached significance (corrected p = 0.054, cluster size = 51). Overall, these findings indicate positive associations between anterior hippocampal gyrification and episodic details, with the strongest and most consistent effect observed in the right hippocampus. Notably, surface‐based analyses using segmentations from the synthseg_v0.2 model, which served as the training data for the maguire_T2w model, revealed a similar right‐hemisphere cluster (Figures S4 and S5).

To further characterize the significant clusters associated with episodic details, we identified the peak vertex within each cluster. In the significant right hippocampal cluster, the peak vertex was vertex 3405, which showed a significant positive association of r = 0.261 (p = 0.000327; see Figure 2C) with episodic detail scores. In the significant left hippocampal cluster, the peak vertex was vertex 4929, which also exhibited a significant positive correlation of r = 0.181 (p = 0.0137) with episodic detail scores (see Figure 2F). Importantly, direct statistical comparisons using Williams' t‐test for dependent overlapping correlations confirmed that these associations were highly specific, with gyrification showing a significantly stronger relationship with internal (i.e., episodic) details than with external details at both the right (p = 0.001) and left (p = 0.023) peak vertices.

For descriptive purposes, we also visualized differences in gyrification across participants. Towards this end, we ranked participants based on their gyrification values at vertex 3405 in their right hippocampal surfaces and visually compared those with the highest and lowest values. Figure 3A shows the four participants with the highest gyrification values and the four with the lowest values from multiple angles. Differences in gyrification patterns are clearly apparent in this comparison, with high‐gyrification participants exhibiting more pronounced folding. To further illustrate these differences, Figure 3B displays a coronal view of two participants, one with high gyrification and one with low gyrification.

Subfield Localization of Surface‐Based Results

To better understand the regional specificity of the observed gyrification effects, we examined the subfield localization of the identified clusters in the surface‐based analyses. To achieve this, we used the group‐based hippocampal surfaces with the significant clusters, as well as the unfolded subfield atlas surfaces produced by HippUnfold. We utilized Connectome Workbench (Van Essen et al. 2013) to overlay the subfield atlas onto each surface containing a significant cluster. The visualization revealed that the cluster in the right hemisphere fell within CA1 and the subiculum (Figure 4B), whereas the cluster in the left hemisphere was largely situated in the subiculum (Figure 4D). For further visualization, Figure 4 also shows the right hippocampus of a single participant (Participant 8) with a high gyrification value at vertex 3405 in the unfolded space (Figure 4A), as well as the left hippocampus of another participant (Participant 49) with a high gyrification value at vertex 4929 (Figure 4C). These individual high‐gyrification patterns largely mirror the group‐level findings in terms of subfield localization.

Discussion

Volumetric analyses have long been the primary method for investigating individual differences in hippocampal structure and their relationship to behavior (Maguire et al. 2000, 2006; Poppenk and Moscovitch 2011). The increased availability of high‐resolution MRI data and recent advances in hippocampal segmentation, including the development of fully automated segmentation methods such as HippUnfold (DeKraker et al. 2022), now offer complementary approaches that allow us to capture variability in fine‐grained hippocampal morphology through surface‐based metrics. Here, we investigated whether hippocampal morphological features, specifically gyrification and thickness, are associated with performance on hippocampally‐dependent tasks in episodic autobiographical memory, scene imagination, spatial navigation, and future thinking. Our study utilized a publicly available dataset (Clark and Maguire 2023) from the general population of neurotypical adults. Notably, one major study that used the same dataset and relied on volumetric analyses reported no robust associations between hippocampal volume and performance on any of the hippocampal‐dependent tasks (Clark et al. 2020). Although further analyses revealed a relationship between posterior CA2/3 volume and autobiographical memory scores in a subset of low‐performing participants, the authors acknowledged significant challenges in interpreting this result (Clark et al. 2023). The present findings, which incorporated surface‐based morphological measures alongside volumetric analyses, revealed significant relationships between anterior hippocampal gyrification and episodic memory, without targeted subsampling based on performance.

Our surface‐based analysis of hippocampal gyrification revealed bilateral significant positive associations between anterior hippocampal gyrification and episodic detail scores, with the stronger effect in terms of both cluster size and significance threshold observed in the right hemisphere. The cluster in the right hippocampus was localized to CA1 and the subiculum, whereas the second cluster in the left hippocampus was largely situated in the subiculum. Importantly, these associations emerged despite the absence of significant relationships between volumetric measures, including subfield‐level volumes, and behavior after correction for multiple comparisons. One might ask whether a finer‐grained voxel‐wise approach, such as voxel‐based morphometry (VBM), could also have revealed this effect. Notably, a prior VBM‐based analysis of the current data set did not show any association between hippocampal measures and the episodic memory scores (Clark et al. 2020). VBM treats each voxel as a single gray‐matter density value, that is, a composite measure in which local hippocampal gyrification and thickness are still collapsed. Moreover, the whole‐brain 3D registration used in VBM cannot fully accommodate the complex folding patterns of the anterior hippocampus (Ding and Van Hoesen 2015; DeKraker et al. 2021). HippUnfold, by contrast, unfolds the hippocampal sheet into a two‐dimensional coordinate system that preserves each participant's native topology and generates vertex‐wise maps of gyrification and thickness, thereby retaining the fine‐scale anatomical detail needed to detect subtle structure‐behavior relationships that VBM is prone to miss (see Goto et al. 2022, for a review of the use of VBM and surface‐based morphometry in the neocortex). Overall, our findings demonstrate that associations between variability in structure and behavior may still be present when volumetric measures fail to reveal them, provided that sufficiently sensitive morphometric measures are used.

Gyrification, as quantified by HippUnfold, reflects the degree of surface folding within the hippocampus and is defined as the ratio of the native 3D surface area to the corresponding 2D unfolded surface area (DeKraker et al. 2022). Consequently, high gyrification does not necessarily indicate a greater number of visible digitations, but rather represents increased surface area in the region. Increased gyrification can also arise through tighter folding and hence elongation of existing gyri without increasing the number of distinct digitations (Duvernoy et al. 2013). Nevertheless, in some cases, such variability may correspond to a greater number of visible digitations (See Figure 3B). In the neocortex, cortical folding is widely regarded as an adaptive structural feature related to optimization of information‐processing capacity, within the volume restrictions of the skull (Rakic 2009). Numerous studies have investigated the mechanisms underlying the emergence of folding in the neocortex. One suggestion is that folding occurs to support the integration of long‐range associative circuits across cortical regions, reducing wiring costs while maintaining rapid communication between distributed hubs (Van Essen 1997). Others propose that highly folded regions often arise in areas where neurogenesis is selectively amplified, leading to increased neuronal populations (Xing et al. 2021). Additionally, biophysical models suggest that during development, differential tangential growth of the cortical gray matter relative to the underlying white matter mechanically favors surface expansion over increases in cortical thickness, promoting the emergence of a folded morphology (Tallinen et al. 2014, 2016). Whether these principles extend to gyrification in the hippocampus remains largely unexplored at present. Inasmuch as the hippocampus is an allocortical structure consisting of three rather than six cortical layers (Duvernoy et al. 2013), it remains unclear whether its folding follows the neocortical mechanisms described. Although the precise mechanistic and developmental basis of hippocampal gyrification remains currently unclear, the location of the present brain‐behavior relationship in the anterior hippocampus also invites a functional interpretation. Much of the literature on hippocampal long‐axis specialization has emphasized the involvement of the posterior hippocampus in episodic memory representations of fine‐grained perceptual detail, whereas the anterior hippocampus has often been linked to coarser or gist‐like representations (Poppenk et al. 2013). However, emerging evidence also points to the involvement of the anterior hippocampus in the construction of richly detailed episodes during autobiographical retrieval. Building on Conway's Self‐Memory System framework (Conway 2005), various investigations distinguish an early construction stage of autobiographical retrieval, during which participants search for and recover the general aspects of a specific memory, from a later elaboration stage, during which they re‐experience and describe the event in detail (e.g., Holland et al. 2011; McCormick et al. 2015). In a recent fMRI study, Audrain et al. (2022) found strong anterior hippocampal activity during the construction stage of autobiographical retrieval, while previous analyses of the same dataset showed posterior hippocampal activity during the elaboration stage (Gilmore et al. 2021). Although fMRI activity does not necessarily map directly onto MRI‐derived morphometric measures, our findings are in line with Audrain et al.'s interpretation of a unique contribution of the anterior hippocampus to the constructive process that may be conceptually based (Sheldon et al. 2019) and support the production of perceptual episodic detail during subsequent elaboration. Our findings hint that anterior hippocampal morphology is relevant to this process, an idea that deserves further investigation in future studies.

In contrast to the extensive literature on hippocampal volume and its relationship to behavior, relatively few studies have examined the association between hippocampal gyrification and behavior. Beattie et al. (2017) developed a visual rating scale to quantify the prominence of gyrification, focusing specifically on the inferior surface of the CA1 and subiculum subfields. They found that greater gyrification in CA1 and subiculum within the hippocampal body was associated with better episodic memory performance as measured by verbal delayed recall and visual recognition tasks in neuropsychological assessments. This study, however, excluded large parts of the anterior hippocampus, that is, the hippocampal head, from their analysis. As the authors acknowledge, this is a significant limitation. Yang et al. (2024), using large‐scale automated dentation analysis, reported that gyrification is most prominent in the hippocampal head, followed by the body and then the tail. Our findings highlight the behavioral relevance of this prominent structural variability. Given the anatomical specificity we observed, they also provide support for the concept of long‐axis functional specialization within the hippocampus (Genon et al. 2021; Poppenk et al. 2013).

Another notable aspect of our results is the behavioral specificity of the relationships we observed. Anterior hippocampal gyrification was found to be related to the number of episodic details in autobiographical memory but not to several other measures of performance on purportedly hippocampally dependent tasks. We recognize that null results are challenging to interpret, but one possible explanation that deserves targeted examination in future research is that our findings point to a high degree of functional specialization in the surface area that is reflected in anterior hippocampal gyrification. In line with this interpretation, the lack of an association between external details and our volumetric or surface measures is consistent with the broader literature showing that the hippocampus is critical for episodic memory, with semantic memory being more dependent on other temporal‐lobe structures (Moscovitch et al. 2016; Renoult et al. 2019). However, this distinction should not be treated as absolute. For example, prior work has shown anterior hippocampal involvement during semantic retrieval when retrieval depends on autobiographical or schema‐based associations (Sheldon et al. 2016). Importantly, the external‐detail score in the AI would not isolate this form of structured autobiographical‐semantic retrieval because it captures a heterogeneous set of non‐episodic details, including semantic information, repetitions, and off‐topic details (Levine et al. 2002). The interpretation of the future thinking results also requires some caution. As noted in the Methods section, we used the Experiential Index also employed by Clark and Maguire (2020) in their original study to evaluate future thinking, which is the same scoring framework used for the Scene Construction Task. This measure captures the richness, vividness, and spatial coherence of imagined events, but it does not isolate episodic details in the same way as episodic detail scoring in the AI. Therefore, the absence of an association between our morphological measures and future thinking may not be surprising.

An additional methodological contribution of the present study was the training and application of an updated segmentation model for HippUnfold that was tailored to the high‐resolution images available in this dataset and provided 186 high‐quality segmentations. The initial application of the synthseg_v0.2 model produced accurate segmentations for only a subset of these participants (N = 93). Our new maguire_T2w model improved segmentation performance and allowed us to retain a much larger final sample. Proper anatomically informed visual quality control was critical in this process because surface‐based measures such as gyrification depend strongly on accurate delineation of hippocampal boundaries, which in HippUnfold is performed by the nnUNet model. Thus, the present findings highlight not only the sensitivity of surface‐based morphometry but also the importance of quality control when applying automated segmentation tools to datasets with image characteristics that may differ from those used during model training. Where necessary, dataset‐specific optimization of the segmentation model may be useful, as models included with a software package may not always be optimal for a given dataset.

To conclude, we found that anterior hippocampal gyrification is significantly associated with the episodic aspect of autobiographical memory. This study demonstrates that surface‐based measures of hippocampal morphology, specifically gyrification, can reveal meaningful structure–behavior relationships that are not detectable through conventional volumetric analyses. Our findings highlight the utility of surface‐based measures, particularly gyrification, for detecting subtle anatomical features that support memory performance in the neurotypical population.
