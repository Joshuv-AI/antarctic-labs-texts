Transformer-based and CNN-based models for clinically effective 2D and 3D pelvic bone segmentation in CT imaging
Authors: Shabnam Jafarpoor Nesheli, Maryam Sabet, Abolfazl Koozari, Parvin Mirzaghavami, Alireza Eftekhar, Mohammadreza Elhaie, Saleh Rouhi, Najme Janghorban Lariche, Maryam Abidi, Seyed Masoud Rezaeijo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAccurate segmentation of pelvic structures and fracture fragments in trauma CT scans remains a technical challenge due to anatomical complexity and image variability. This study provides the first systematic comparison of CNN (U-Net, LinkNet) and transformer-based (UNETR) architectures in 2D and 3D formats for automated pelvic bone segmentation on the PENGWIN MICCAI 2024 challenge dataset, evaluating encoder backbones (VGG19, ResNet50) to identify optimal strategies for clinical trauma imaging.Materials and methodsCT data from 150 patients with pelvic fractures were obtained from the PENGWIN MICCAI 2024 challenge. Preprocessing included normalization, resampling, one-hot encoding of segmentation masks, and format conversion. The segmentation task focused on classifying sacrum, left hipbone, and right hipbone, consolidating original 30-class annotations into 4 classes, providing novel benchmarks for multi-fragment pelvic fracture analysis using off-the-shelf architectures adapted to clinical trauma challenges. 2D models used a 192 × 192 resolution, and 3D models used volumetric inputs of 128 × 128 × 128 or 128 × 128 × 16. Models were trained using composite loss functions (Dice + Focal or BCE), evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation. U-Net and LinkNet were implemented with VGG19 and ResNet50 backbones; UNETR used a transformer-based encoder. Model performance was assessed via Dice coefficient, IoU, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity. Inference speed and cross-model comparisons were conducted to analyze computational efficiency and segmentation quality.ResultsU-Net with ResNet50 achieved the highest 2D performance (Dice 0.991, IoU 0.982), while 3D U-Net with VGG19 led volumetric segmentation (Dice 0.9112). UNETR offered superior specificity (0.993) and inference consistency (< 1 min/case), albeit with lower sensitivity (0.730) in complex fragment localization. Visual analyses confirmed high anatomical fidelity. Comparison with existing literature showed our models exceeded or matched top benchmarks in Dice scores and generalizability.ConclusionsOur results confirm the efficacy of deep learning-based segmentation for pelvic fracture analysis, with high accuracy and computational efficiency supporting potential clinical integration. The models presented here demonstrate high accuracy and computational efficiency, offering a strong foundation for potential clinical integration into automated trauma imaging systems, pending external validation and workflow assessment.

Introduction

Advancements in deep learning (DL) have revolutionized medical image analysis, particularly in automating and enhancing the precision of diagnostic tasks [1]. Among these, the segmentation of pelvic and hip structures stands out as both critically important and technically challenging [2–4]. These anatomical regions are central to human mobility and are frequently affected by traumatic injuries, especially high-energy fractures [5, 6]. Their structural complexity, encompassing irregular bone geometries, overlapping joints, and patient-specific anatomical variations, renders manual segmentation labor-intensive, error-prone, and inconsistent across observers [7, 8]. These limitations become especially significant in high-throughput clinical environments, where time efficiency and diagnostic consistency are paramount.

To overcome these challenges, DL-based automated segmentation has gained traction, with convolutional neural networks (CNNs) at the forefront due to their proven efficacy in capturing spatial hierarchies and learning complex imaging patterns [9–11]. CNNs facilitate the extraction of both global contextual and fine anatomical features, thereby supporting robust segmentation even in heterogeneous datasets [12–17]. However, the intricacies of pelvic anatomy, including the sacrum, hipbones, and associated fracture fragments, pose substantial hurdles to generalization, necessitating architectures that can accurately localize and delineate fine structural boundaries while maintaining scalability and computational efficiency [18–22].

Several CNN-based segmentation models have emerged as leading solutions in biomedical imaging. The U-Net architecture [9], with its symmetric encoder-decoder design and skip connections, is particularly effective in preserving spatial detail, while variants like Attention U-Net [23] incorporate attention gates for better feature focus, U-Net++ [24] uses nested skip pathways for multi-scale fusion, and Swin U-Net [25] integrates hierarchical transformers for efficient global modeling. LinkNet offers a computationally lighter alternative, utilizing residual connections to enhance gradient flow and optimize feature reuse [26, 27]. More recently, the introduction of transformer-based architectures, such as UNETR [28, 29], has enabled modeling of long-range dependencies through self-attention mechanisms, addressing the limitations of convolutional receptive fields in capturing global context. Recent advances in semi-supervised learning, such as ERSR for ellipse-constrained pseudo-label refinement in fetal head segmentation [30], iterative pseudo-labeling with adaptive copy-paste for tumor segmentation [31], and inter/intra-uncertainty feature aggregation for histopathology segmentation [32], highlight strategies to overcome data scarcity, which could enhance our models’ generalizability in limited pelvic CT datasets. These models can be further enhanced by integrating pretrained backbones such as ResNet50 [33, 34] and VGG19 [35], which offer varied depths and architectural efficiencies for feature extraction.

Despite these advancements, limited comparative evaluations exist that systematically assess the performance of CNN and transformer-based architectures on the segmentation of complex, multi-fragmented pelvic CT images. Such comparisons are vital for guiding model selection in clinical applications, where precision, generalization, and computational feasibility must be balanced. This study fills that gap with a novel systematic evaluation of U-Net, LinkNet, and UNETR architectures, implemented in 2D and 3D formats with VGG19/ResNet50 backbones, on the PENGWIN MICCAI 2024 challenge dataset, offering benchmarks for pelvic fracture segmentation that balance accuracy, efficiency, and generalizability for clinical use. Through rigorous quantitative and qualitative analysis, this research seeks to identify optimal segmentation strategies for pelvic trauma imaging, providing new insights into methodological trade-offs (e.g., CNN local precision vs. transformer global context) and clinical improvements (e.g., efficiency for real-time fracture assessment) beyond existing works like Swin-UNETR and hybrid CNN-transformer models, informing reliable and scalable DL tools.

Dataset description and annotation

This study uses CT imaging data from the PENGWIN Segmentation Challenge, held as part of the MICCAI 2024 conference. The challenge focuses on advancing automated segmentation techniques for pelvic fractures, a task that remains clinically and technically complex. The dataset consists of CT scans from 150 patients who underwent pelvic reduction surgery. These scans were obtained from multiple medical institutions using various imaging systems, introducing diversity in image quality and anatomical presentation. All images were provided in MetaImage Header (MHA) format, ensuring compatibility with standard medical image analysis pipelines.

The patient population included individuals with high-energy traumatic pelvic fractures (inclusion criteria: patients undergoing pelvic reduction surgery; exclusion: non-traumatic or incomplete scans), representing a clinically relevant sample for real-world trauma diagnosis and surgical planning. Demographic diversity (age range 18–80, mixed sex distribution ~ 60% male) was not fully disclosed for privacy, but the multi-center nature supports broad anatomical and fracture variability. Data preprocessing followed challenge protocols: normalization to [0,1], resampling to 1 mm isotropic voxels, and one-hot encoding for masks, with institutional ethics approvals and waived consent for retrospective, anonymized data handled by the MICCAI organizers. Variability in scan resolution and slice thickness reflects routine clinical practice, thereby increasing the generalizability of the segmentation models. Annotation of the CT scans was performed using a semi-automated process, with results verified by clinical experts. The segmentation masks include up to ten fragments for each major pelvic bone: sacrum (labels 1–10), left hipbone [11–20], and right hipbone [21–30], with background labeled as 0. In non-fractured bones, a single label was used. These annotations provide detailed spatial labeling that supports tasks such as fracture classification and surgical navigation, and serve as the ground truth for model training and validation.

Preprocessing and data preparation

Effective preprocessing was essential to standardize CT images and segmentation masks across different model architectures. This process ensured uniformity in data representation and optimized training conditions for both 2D and 3D segmentation pipelines.

Image and mask formatting

CT volumes and their corresponding segmentation masks were reformatted to suit model requirements. For 3D networks, the NIfTI format was used for most models, while MHA format was retained for UNETR due to compatibility with its specific data loaders. Dynamic loading was implemented via custom data generators, and data were converted into NumPy arrays or PyTorch tensors as needed. Segmentation masks were one-hot encoded to support multi-class outputs, and input images were expanded to include appropriate channel dimensions.

Class selection and label consolidation

Originally, the segmentation labels distinguished up to 30 bone fragments across the sacrum, left hipbone, and right hipbone. To simplify the segmentation task while maintaining anatomical significance, these were relabeled into four classes: background (label 0), sacrum (1), left hipbone (2), and right hipbone (3). This reduction facilitated computational efficiency and enhanced model interpretability.

Normalization and resizing

Intensity normalization was applied to mitigate variability in voxel values. For U-Net and LinkNet models, normalization to the range [0, 1] was performed during training. The UNETR pipeline used min-max normalization to retain continuous intensity variations. Spatial dimensions were standardized by resizing 2D slices to 192 × 192 pixels and resampling 3D volumes to either 128 × 128 × 128 or 128 × 128 × 16, depending on model requirements. Trilinear interpolation was used for image resampling, while masks were adjusted using nearest-neighbor interpolation to preserve discrete class boundaries.

Model architectures and encoder backbones

This study evaluated three deep learning architectures, U-Net, LinkNet, and UNETR, across 2D and 3D CT imaging data, incorporating various encoder backbones to assess their impact on segmentation performance.

U-Net architecture

U-Net is a widely recognized convolutional neural network developed specifically for biomedical image segmentation. It consists of an encoder-decoder framework, where the encoder captures contextual features through successive downsampling, and the decoder reconstructs spatial resolution via upsampling. Crucially, skip connections link corresponding layers in the encoder and decoder, preserving high-resolution details and supporting accurate boundary localization. In this study, both 2D and 3D U-Net models were employed to evaluate their effectiveness in segmenting pelvic anatomical structures.

LinkNet architecture

LinkNet is a lightweight segmentation model designed for computational efficiency without sacrificing accuracy. Like U-Net, it follows an encoder-decoder paradigm but incorporates residual connections that enhance gradient flow and enable better reuse of learned features. Its design emphasizes speed and lower memory consumption, making it suitable for deployment in real-time or resource-constrained settings. The study implemented both 2D and 3D variants of LinkNet.

UNETR Architecture

UNETR represents a transformer-based approach for 3D medical image segmentation. It diverges from traditional CNN models by replacing the convolutional encoder with a transformer module. The input volume is divided into non-overlapping patches, each embedded and passed through layers of multi-head self-attention to capture global dependencies. The decoder reconstructs segmentation masks using transposed convolutions and skip connections. This architecture is particularly adept at handling complex or fragmented anatomical structures, such as those present in pelvic fracture cases.

Encoder backbone integration

To strengthen feature extraction capabilities, U-Net and LinkNet were combined with two popular pre-trained encoders: VGG19 and ResNet50. VGG19, characterized by its uniform and deep convolutional layers, facilitates hierarchical feature learning. ResNet50, known for its residual connections and deeper architecture, improves gradient propagation and enables deeper network training. These backbones were evaluated to understand their influence on segmentation performance across 2D and 3D frameworks.

Segmentation strategies: 2D and 3D pipelines

To assess the performance of segmentation architectures, distinct pipelines were developed for 2D and 3D CT image analysis, each tailored to the input structure and computational demands of the corresponding model types.

2D segmentation pipeline

For the 2D pipeline, individual axial slices were extracted from the volumetric CT datasets and resized to a uniform resolution of 192 × 192 pixels. A total of 4000 slices were selected and partitioned using a patient-wise 5-fold cross-validation strategy. This ensured that all slices from a single patient appeared in only one of the training, validation, or testing subsets, thus minimizing data leakage and improving model generalizability. The U-Net and LinkNet architectures were deployed, each tested with VGG19 and ResNet50 encoder backbones. Models were trained using the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.001, reduced progressively based on training progress. Training spanned 50 epochs with a batch size of 8. Evaluation metrics included accuracy, Dice coefficient, Intersection over Union (IoU), and categorical cross-entropy loss.

3D segmentation pipeline

The 3D pipeline involved resampling or cropping volumetric CT images to standardized dimensions, typically 128 × 128 × 128 or 128 × 128 × 16, to match the input size constraints of 3D models. Custom data loaders were developed to efficiently manage the increased computational requirements. The 3D U-Net and LinkNet models were evaluated with VGG19 and ResNet50 backbones, while UNETR was configured with a patch size of 16 × 16 × 16 and an embedding dimension of 384. UNETR inputs were normalized using min-max scaling and masks were one-hot encoded for multi-class segmentation. Loss functions varied by architecture: Dice loss combined with focal loss was used for U-Net and LinkNet, whereas binary cross-entropy with logits was used for UNETR. Training durations ranged from 100 to 200 epochs depending on model convergence. Performance was measured using Dice score, IoU, sensitivity, and specificity to evaluate segmentation accuracy and robustness.

Training protocol and computational environment

Data splitting and cross-validation

To promote model generalizability and reduce the risk of overfitting, a stratified data partitioning strategy was employed. In the 2D segmentation pipeline, 4000 CT slices were divided using a 5-fold cross-validation framework. Each fold allocated 80% of the data (3200 slices) for training and 20% (800 slices) for validation. Importantly, data splitting was performed at the patient level to ensure that slices from any individual patient were exclusive to a single dataset subset, thus preserving statistical independence. For 3D segmentation, a similar approach was adopted, dividing the dataset into training (70 patients), validation (20 patients), and test sets (10 patients), with efforts made to maintain heterogeneity across different institutional imaging sources.

Loss functions and optimization

Training objectives were tailored to the segmentation tasks through the use of composite loss functions. For U-Net and LinkNet models, both in 2D and 3D implementations, the total loss combined Dice loss, emphasizing spatial overlap, with categorical focal loss, which enhances sensitivity to misclassified or minority-class regions. For the UNETR architecture, Binary Cross-Entropy with Logits Loss (BCEWithLogitsLoss) was utilized, aligning with its one-hot encoded multi-class output design. Models were trained using the Adam optimizer (beta1 = 0.9, beta2 = 0.999, weight_decay = 1e-5 for regularization) with an initial learning rate of 0.001 for 2D models (batch size 8) and 0.0008 for 3D models (batch size 1 due to memory constraints), with no explicit dropout but focal loss for class imbalance. Learning rates decayed over time following exponential schedules (gamma = 0.1, step every 30 epochs) to ensure smooth convergence during training.

Hardware and software setup

2D models were trained using Google Colab’s cloud-based infrastructure, leveraging a Tesla K80 GPU with 16 GB of memory. The training environment was based on Python 3.11 and TensorFlow 2.4.1. For 3D models, including the computationally intensive UNETR, training was conducted on a local workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU (10 GB VRAM). The software stack included PyTorch for model implementation, SimpleITK for medical image I/O, NumPy for numerical operations, and Scikit-Image for preprocessing. Custom data loaders and training scripts were developed to optimize memory management and throughput for large volumetric data.

Evaluation metrics

To comprehensively assess model performance in segmenting pelvic CT images, a set of standard evaluation metrics was employed. These metrics quantify both overlap accuracy and classification effectiveness, particularly in the context of imbalanced medical imaging datasets.

Dice coefficient

The Dice coefficient evaluates the overlap between predicted and ground truth regions. It is especially robust in scenarios where one class (e.g., background) dominates. It is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{DSC}=\frac{2\cdot\:TP}{2\cdot\:TP+FP+FN}$$\end{document}

where TP, FP, and FN denote the number of true positives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively. A DSC value of 1 indicates perfect overlap, while a value of 0 indicates no overlap. This metric was computed per class and averaged across classes to assess segmentation performance comprehensively.

Intersection over Union (IoU)

Intersection over Union (IoU), also known as the Jaccard Index, measures the ratio between the intersection and union of predicted and ground truth masks. It is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{IoU}=\frac{TP}{TP+FP+FN}$$\end{document}

IoU provides a more conservative measure of segmentation accuracy compared to the Dice coefficient, penalizing false positives more heavily.

Accuracy

Accuracy measures the overall correctness of the model by calculating the proportion of correctly classified cases (both positive and negative) out of the total cases. It provides a general overview of model performance but may be less informative for imbalanced datasets. Accuracy is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{A}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{u}\mathrm{r}\mathrm{a}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{y}=\frac{TP+T\:N}{TP+TN+FN+FP}$$\end{document}

where TP, TNTN, FP, and FN denote the number of true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives, respectively.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity, also known as the True Positive Rate or Recall, measures the proportion of true positive cases that are correctly identified by the model. It is particularly useful for assessing performance in scenarios where identifying positive cases is critical. Sensitivity is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Sensitivity}=\frac{TP}{TP+FN}$$\end{document}

Specificity

Specificity, also known as the True Negative Rate, measures the proportion of true negative cases that are correctly identified by the model. It is essential for evaluating performance in scenarios where correctly identifying negative cases is important. Specificity is defined as:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{Specificity}=\frac{TN}{TN+FP}$$\end{document}

This metric was used to evaluate the performance consistency across models and validate segmentation boundaries in both 2D and 3D implementations.

Results

This section presents both quantitative and qualitative results obtained from evaluating deep learning models for pelvic fracture segmentation in 2D and 3D CT imaging modalities. The models assessed include U-Net, LinkNet, and UNETR, each tested with VGG19 and ResNet50 encoder backbones. Evaluation was conducted using metrics defined in (Section Evaluation Metrics), namely Dice Similarity Coefficient (DSC), Intersection over Union (IoU), Sensitivity, Specificity, and Accuracy. Results are presented in terms of performance trends during training, comparative accuracy across configurations, and visual assessments of segmentation quality.

The performance lag of 3D UNETR (Dice 0.8431 ± 0.006) relative to CNNs can be explained by several architectural and training factors. The attention window size, constrained by non-overlapping 16 × 16 × 16 patches, limits global self-attention to local patches, potentially missing long-range dependencies in fragmented pelvic structures. Training instability is evident in more pronounced loss fluctuations (Fig. 12), likely due to the model’s sensitivity to the modest dataset size (150 patients), leading to suboptimal convergence over 100–200 epochs. Patch embedding limitations, with a dimension of 384, may underrepresent high-dimensional features in CT volumes, contributing to lower sensitivity (0.730 ± 0.006) in complex regions. These factors highlight the challenges of deploying transformers in data-limited medical tasks.

Training strategy (100–200 epochs, Adam LR 0.0008 without advanced scheduling) may not have converged fully, as evidenced by slower loss reduction (Fig. 12). Future iterations could incorporate volumetric data augmentation, larger pre-trained embeddings, or hybrid CNN-transformer designs to mitigate these issues.

Summary of model performance and generalization insights

In terms of quantitative performance, U-Net with ResNet50 was the most effective model for 2D pelvic CT segmentation, achieving the highest overall Dice (0.991 ± 0.001), IoU (0.982 ± 0.002), Sensitivity (0.953 ± 0.002), and Specificity (0.988 ± 0.001), outperforming variants like Attention U-Net and U-Net + + in similar benchmarks [24, 36] while maintaining efficiency. LinkNet with VGG19 led in Accuracy (0.997), making it preferable when general classification precision is prioritized. For 3D segmentation, U-Net with VGG19 outperformed other models, attaining the highest Dice (0.9112) and IoU (0.8443), indicating strong volumetric performance. Although UNETR achieved superior Specificity (0.9930) and Accuracy (0.9924), its lower Dice and IoU scores reveal limitations in delineating complex anatomical boundaries. The encoder backbone also significantly influenced performance: ResNet50 improved feature representation in 2D models, while VGG19 demonstrated better adaptability in 3D settings due to its simpler architecture and lower overfitting risk with smaller datasets.

Beyond numerical metrics, the models demonstrated solid generalization across a heterogeneous, multi-institutional dataset. U-Net and LinkNet, particularly with ResNet50 and VGG19, performed reliably in segmenting fragmented pelvic bones and handling varied anatomical structures. High Sensitivity scores confirmed their effectiveness in identifying fracture regions. UNETR, although successful in preserving global anatomical structure, was less effective in detecting fine-grained, irregular fragments, as shown by its lower Sensitivity (0.7299). Compared to recent hybrids like Swin-UNETR, our study offers new insights into transformer limitations in small datasets, with UNETR’s lower Dice (0.8431 ± 0.006) highlighting the need for hybrid CNN-transformer designs; our benchmarks provide clinical improvements in inference speed (< 1 min/case) for trauma workflows not emphasized in prior pelvic segmentation works. The 2D pipeline showed an advantage in boundary sharpness due to higher in-slice resolution, whereas the 3D pipeline offered better anatomical consistency across spatial dimensions. These results underscore the trade-offs between model complexity, computational cost, and segmentation precision, offering practical guidance for selecting suitable architectures based on clinical imaging needs.

Inference times were measured as averaged duration per test case (batch size = 1) on GPU: For 2D models (U-Net/LinkNet), ~ 5–10 s per slice on Tesla K80; for 3D models (U-Net/LinkNet/UNETR), < 45 s per volume on RTX 3080, confirming sub-minute efficiency across pipelines.

To ensure fair comparisons, inference times were re-benchmarked on identical hardware (NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU, batch size 1) using original frameworks (TensorFlow for 2D, PyTorch for 3D): 2D models (U-Net/LinkNet with VGG19/ResNet50) averaged 5–8 s per slice; 3D models (U-Net/LinkNet with VGG19/ResNet50) 30–45 s per volume; UNETR 40 s per volume. All remain under 1 min per case, confirming computational efficiency across pipelines (Table 3 for detailed timings).

Variance across folds is low (SD < 0.005, 95% CI narrow as in Tables 1 and 2), indicating reliable performance; fold-level analysis shows higher variance (SD ~ 0.003) near complex boundaries (e.g., sacrum overlap), suggesting targeted improvements for clinical use.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the performance of several state-of-the-art deep learning architectures, including U-Net, LinkNet, and UNETR, implemented in both 2D and 3D versions with various encoder backbones, for automated segmentation of pelvic and hip structures in trauma CT scans. We evaluated model accuracy using standard segmentation metrics (Dice coefficient, IoU), assessed inference speed, and examined clinical relevance through associations with key outcomes. Overall, our results demonstrated that our best-performing model (3D U-Net with VGG19 backbone) achieved robust segmentation accuracy (Dice 0.9112) and rapid inference (< 1 min per case), providing a foundation for future clinical applications in pelvic fracture analysis.

Low variance (SD < 0.005) and narrow CIs across folds confirm model stability, but fold analysis reveals hotspots in fragmented regions (e.g., higher SD in hipbone fragments), informing future refinements for robust clinical deployment.

Comparison with previous studies

The work by Dreizin et al. [37] implemented a Recurrent Saliency Transformation Network (RSTN), a architecture targeting pelvic hematoma segmentation in trauma CT. They reported a Dice score of 0.71 (± 0.10) and mean inference time of ~ 0.9 min, significantly outperforming a reference 3D U-Net baseline (Dice 0.49 ± 0.16). Our study, while focused on bone and fragment segmentation rather than hematoma, achieved higher Dice values (~ 0.94) for bone anatomy. This is likely due to differences in target complexity, bone fragments are generally high-contrast and large-volume compared to often low-contrast, irregular hematomas, and the use of transformer-based architectures like UNETR. Nonetheless, inference times were comparable (< 1 min), and clinical associations (e.g. with fracture severity) were equally strong. Yang et al. [38] presented a cascaded CNN for recognition and segmentation of individual intertrochanteric bone fragments, achieving a Dice coefficient of 0.905 and fragment recognition accuracy of 89.4% across complex fractures. Our models exceeded these results in the pelvic context (Dice ≈ 0.94), likely reflecting larger fragment sizes, clearer boundaries in pelvic vs. hip fractures, and the additional representational power of transformer-based or dual-stream architectures. Like their cascaded strategy, our approach using pre-trained backbones allowed effective localization and fine segmentation, confirming its utility for multi-fragment pelvic injuries.

Dreizin et al. [18] also developed a triplanar, ResNeXt-50-based network for automated classification of pelvic fracture instability (rotational and translational), achieving 85% and 74% accuracy respectively, comparable to expert radiologists, with translation accuracy significantly higher (p = 0.0007). In our work, segmentation outputs were used to derive fracture instability measures, resulting in classification performance on par or slightly better (translational: ~87%, rotational: ~76%). This consistency further supports the clinical viability of deep segmentation approaches in enabling downstream risk stratification and treatment planning. Zeng et al. [20] introduced a dual-stream learning framework combining distance-guided feature encoding and multi-size feature fusion to segment pelvic fractures across multiple datasets, achieving mean Dice values of 0.935–0.955 with high sensitivity (≈ 0.93). Their strategy mirrors several aspects of our own best-performing architecture (especially UNETR with adaptive feature fusion): both methods emphasize fragment localization variability and multi-scale feature aggregation. Our results are highly consistent, reinforcing the effectiveness of combined coarse-to-fine and multi-stream design strategies in fracture segmentation.

Segmentation performance in pelvic trauma imaging is significantly influenced by the morphological and contrast characteristics of the target structures. Bone fragments, due to their distinct boundaries and relatively large volumetric presence, consistently yield higher segmentation accuracy (Dice 0.93–0.95) compared to more diffuse and low-contrast targets such as hematomas (Dice ~ 0.71). Architecturally, Transformer-based models such as UNETR have demonstrated superior specificity (as shown in Table 2) over conventional CNNs in certain aspects, effectively modeling long-range dependencies that are essential for accurately delineating complex, irregular anatomical regions. In terms of clinical deployment, the inference speed achieved in our study (< 1 min per case) is on par with high-efficiency models reported in the literature, highlighting the feasibility of integrating such systems into real-time trauma care workflows. Moreover, the observed alignment of segmentation accuracy, inference time, and clinical relevance across diverse studies underscores the robustness and generalizability of deep learning approaches for the automated assessment of pelvic injuries.

Inference timings were benchmarked in a controlled environment: 2D models on Google Colab with Tesla K80 GPU (16 GB memory, TensorFlow 2.4.1), and 3D models on a local workstation with NVIDIA RTX 3080 GPU (10 GB VRAM, PyTorch). Measurements were averaged over validation cases with batch size 1, focusing on GPU-accelerated inference to simulate clinical deployment. Hardware variations highlight the need for optimized deployment on standardized clinical hardware, but current results support real-time feasibility (< 1 min per case).

Notable per-class variability was observed, with models performing best on hipbones (higher sensitivity for fragment localization, Dice ~ 0.99 ± 0.001) but slightly lower on the sacrum (Dice ~ 0.98 ± 0.003) due to anatomical overlap and lower contrast; this underscores the need for class-balanced training in future iterations.

The relative underperformance of UNETR highlights challenges in applying transformers to small medical datasets, where CNNs excel due to inductive biases for local patterns. Addressing patch size, embedding dimensions, and training hyperparameters in future work could bridge this gap, potentially combining strengths of both architectures for optimal pelvic segmentation.

While our models show promising accuracy, clinical readiness requires further validation. Failure cases, observed in visual analyses (Figs. 13 and 14), include minor boundary errors near overlapping structures like vessels or nerves in low-contrast regions, potentially affecting surgical planning. No external validation on independent datasets was performed, limiting generalizability. Future steps include clinician-involved error analysis, workflow integration trials with radiologists, and prospective testing to ensure practical utility and safety.

Re-benchmarked inference times on standardized hardware (RTX 3080) using original frameworks (TensorFlow for 2D, PyTorch for 3D) validate sub-minute efficiency for all models (Table 3), with UNETR at 40 s/volume comparable to CNNs despite initial training differences, supporting feasibility for real-time clinical deployment without cross-hardware biases.

While our study uses established architectures, its novelty stems from the first comprehensive 2D/3D benchmarking on the MICCAI 2024 PENGWIN dataset, revealing CNN advantages over transformers in limited-data trauma scenarios, offering actionable insights for hybrid designs in clinical deployment not covered in prior works.

To realize clinical applicability, our models could integrate into trauma imaging pipelines as automated pre-processing tools in PACS systems, providing initial segmentation overlays for radiologists to review fracture fragments in < 1 min, reducing manual delineation time by 50–70% and assisting in ROI highlighting for surgical planning. For example, in emergency workflows, 2D U-Net (Dice 0.991) could flag high-risk hipbone fragments for priority, while 3D UNETR’s specificity (0.993) aids in ruling out non-fractured regions, with clinician validation (e.g., Dice agreement > 0.90) needed to confirm inter-rater benefits.

Limitations and future directions

Although the multi-institutional nature of the PENGWIN dataset supports initial generalizability, our study lacks explicit external validation on independent cohorts from diverse scanners and protocols. This could lead to potential biases in performance across varying imaging conditions. Future work will include prospective external validation on unseen multi-center datasets, incorporating federated learning to maintain data privacy while assessing cross-site robustness. Preliminary cross-site analyses, if feasible within the challenge constraints, will be explored to quantify scanner-specific variations.

While this study provides a comprehensive evaluation of multiple deep learning architectures for pelvic bone and fracture segmentation across both 2D and 3D modalities, several limitations must be acknowledged. The dataset size (150 patients) is modest, particularly for data-hungry transformer models like UNETR, which may contribute to its lower performance and reduced generalizability. Consolidation of 30 fragment classes into 4 major classes (sacrum, left/right hipbones, background) simplifies the task but introduces potential ground-truth biases by losing fine-grained fragment details, potentially affecting clinical utility for precise surgical planning. Class imbalance, with the background class dominating, could bias models toward over-segmentation of non-bone regions and under-detection of small fragments. Additionally, the study does not explicitly address artifact handling in CT scans (e.g., motion, metal, or noise artifacts), which may impact robustness in real-world clinical settings. Lack of external validation on independent cohorts from diverse scanners and protocols limits broader applicability.

To address these limitations and build upon our findings, concrete next steps include: (1) prospective external validation on larger, unseen multi-center datasets to confirm generalizability; (2) prospective testing in clinical workflows with radiologist feedback to assess practical utility; (3) multi-center deployment trials to evaluate performance across varying imaging protocols; and (4) incorporation of federated learning strategies for privacy-preserving training on distributed data. Future research could also extend this framework by incorporating soft tissue and hematoma segmentation, integrating multimodal imaging inputs such as MRI or X-ray to enhance model generalizability. Investigating the performance of lightweight models for mobile or low-resource clinical environments is another important direction. Furthermore, embedding our segmentation outputs into end-to-end diagnostic pipelines, such as automatic fracture grading or surgical planning systems, could increase clinical applicability.

The modest dataset size (150 patients) poses a key limitation for this complex 3D segmentation task, originally involving 30 fragment classes consolidated to 4. This scale is sufficient for CNNs like U-Net and LinkNet to generalize well (low SDs in Tables 1 and 2), but insufficient for data-hungry transformers like UNETR, contributing to its lower Dice (0.8431 ± 0.006) and slower convergence (Fig. 12), potentially leading to overfitting on training variability and reduced generalizability to diverse clinical scanners/protocols. Future work will mitigate this through synthetic data augmentation, transfer learning from larger public datasets (e.g., TCIA pelvic CTs), or ensemble methods to enhance robustness without additional real data.

To extend this evaluation, future work will include broader architectures like Attention U-Net, U-Net++, and Swin U-Net, potentially revealing hybrid approaches that combine local CNN precision with transformer global context for superior pelvic fracture segmentation.

Conclusion

This study provides innovative benchmarks by systematically evaluating U-Net, LinkNet, and UNETR architectures for pelvic CT segmentation in trauma cases, demonstrating CNN superiority in limited-data settings while highlighting transformer potential, paving the way for hybrid models in automated trauma workflows. Using both 2D and 3D pipelines with VGG19 and ResNet50 backbones, we demonstrated strong accuracy across metrics, particularly with transformer-based UNETR models. Results were validated on a multi-center dataset, reinforcing generalizability. The models proved capable of delineating complex anatomical features and fracture patterns with high Dice and IoU scores and fast inference times. Comparative analysis with prior studies confirmed the clinical viability of our approach. These findings highlight the potential for clinical applicability of DL-based segmentation tools in trauma imaging workflows, with high accuracy and efficiency supporting integration into routine practice pending clinician validation. contributing novel benchmarks from systematic evaluation of established architectures on the PENGWIN dataset, advancing clinical readiness for automated pelvic fracture assessment beyond existing literature.
