Connection of hypocoercivity and hypocontractivity via the Cayley transform
A. Arnold, S. Egger, V. Mehrmann, E.A. Nigsch

Abstract
The concepts of hypocoercivity and hypocontractivity and their relationship are studied for semi-dissipative continuous-time and discrete-time evolution equations in a Hilbert space setting. New proofs for the characterization of the short-time decay of the solution from the initial value are presented, that in particular characterize the constants in the leading terms of the solution when expanded in time. Maximally coercive/contractive representations of hypocoercive and hypocontractive semi-dissipative systems are presented, as well as the effect of different representations on the error estimates for the numerical solution.



1
Introduction

We are interested in applying time-discretization methods to linear time-invariant initial value problems,

\dot{x}=-B_{c}x,\quad x(0)=x^{0},\quad t>0,

(1.1)

where
B_{c}
is either a complex matrix or a bounded linear operator on an infinite dimensional, separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. We are particularly interested in
(semi-)dissipative systems
, i.e. systems where
B_{H}
, the Hermitian part of
B_{c}
, is positive (semi-)definite. For simplicity of the presentation we always assume here that
B_{c}
has a trivial kernel. Otherwise, the space should be restricted to
(\ker B_{c})^{\perp}
, assuming that
-B_{c}
is semi-dissipative (see also §I of
[
25
]
).

Analogously, we study discrete-time systems (that may or may not arise from a time-discretization of (
1.1
)) which have the form

x_{k+1}=B_{d}x_{k},\quad x_{0}=x^{0},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0},

(1.2)

where
B_{d}
is a bounded linear operator on
\mathcal{H}
.
We are particularly interested in systems that are semi-contractive: We call such a system
(semi-)contractive
if
\sigma_{\max}(B_{d})\,(\leq)<1
. Here,
\sigma_{\max}(B_{d})
is the largest singular value (the
spectral norm
) of
B_{d}
. In analogy to the continuous-time case, we always assume that
B_{d}
does not have the eigenvalue
1
. Otherwise, the space should be restricted to
(\ker(B_{d}-I))^{\perp}
, assuming that
B_{d}
is semi-contractive. A detailed study of the properties of such systems and their relationship in the finite dimensional case has been presented in
[
3
]
.

In this paper new proofs for the characterization of the short-time decay of the solutions from
the initial value are presented for both systems. It is shown that essentially the same proof technique can be employed in both systems. The constants in the leading terms of the solution are determined when it is expanded in time in the neighborhood of the initial value. When the discrete-time system arises from the implicit midpoint discretization (scaled Cayley-transform, Crank-Nicholson scheme) of a continuous-time system, it is shown that the norm of the continuous-time solution operator is approximated to a higher order than expected from the order of the discretization.

Since the representation of a linear system as a semi-dissipative or semi-contractive system is not unique, but can be modified by a change of basis, we discuss the construction of maximally coercive/contractive representations of hypocoercive and hypocontractive systems and the effect of different representations
on the error estimates for the numerical solution.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we present the notation and recall the previously derived decay results for continuous-time systems.
Section
3
presents a new proof of the short-time decay in the continuous-time case. The relationship between the hypocoercivity index of a continuous-time system and the hypocontractivity index
of the system discretized in time via the implicit midpoint rule, which leads to the scaled Cayley transform of the system operator, is studied in Section
4
. Section
5
studies the concept of hypocontractivity, and the corresponding hypocontractivity index, as finite difference approximation of the corresponding hypocoercivity concept and index.
Maximally coercive/contractive representations via a change of basis of hypocoercive, respectively hypocontractive systems, as coercive respectively contractive systems are discussed in Section
6
as well as the effect of these transformations on the short-time decay.

2
Notation and preliminaries

In this section we introduce the notation and recall the previous initial-time decay results.

For a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, by

\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
we denote the set of bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
. For an operator
B\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, its spectrum is denoted by
\Lambda(B)
, its spectral radius by
\rho(B)
and its set of singular values by
\sigma(B)
. Moreover,
B^{*}
denotes the adjoint of
B
. The positive definite square root of a self-adjoint positive semidefinite operator
R
is denoted as
\sqrt{R}
.
For the matrix case
\|\cdot\|
denotes the spectral norm of vectors and matrices, otherwise the Hilbert space norm.
We make frequent use of the sets
\mathbb{S}:=\{x\in\mathcal{H}\,:\,\|x\|=1\}
,

\mathbb{H}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Re(z)<0\}
, and
\mathbb{D}\coloneqq\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\lvert z\rvert<1\}
.

The concepts of semi-dissipativity and hypocoercivity for operators in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
are defined as follows, see
[
4
,
5
,
25
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
B_{c}=B_{H}-B_{S}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
B_{H}\coloneqq\frac{B_{c}+B_{c}^{*}}{2}
and

B_{S}\coloneqq-\frac{B_{c}-B_{c}^{*}}{2}
.

(i)

-B_{c}
is called
semi-dissipative
(or, equivalently,
B_{c}
is called
semi-accretive
) if
B_{H}\geq 0
, i.e.,
B_{H}
is positive semidefinite.

(ii)

B_{c}
is called
hypocoercive
if there exist constants
C\geq 1
and
\lambda>0
such that the solutions to (
1.1
) satisfy

\forall x^{0}\in\mathcal{H},\quad\forall t\geq 0:\quad\|x(t)\|\leq Ce^{-\lambda t}\|x^{0}\|.

(2.1)

(iii)

Let
-B_{c}
be semi-dissipative. The
hypocoercivity index
(HC-index)
m_{HC}
of
B_{c}
is defined as the smallest integer
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(if it exists) such that

\sum_{j=0}^{m}(B_{c}^{*})^{j}B_{H}\,B_{c}^{j}\geq\kappa I

(2.2)

for some
\kappa>0
.

We recall that the minimality of
m
implies for the case
\dim(\mathcal{H})<\infty
that the matrix
\sum_{j=0}^{m_{HC}-1}(B_{c}^{*})^{j}B_{H}\,B_{c}^{j}
has a non-trivial kernel (see (2.2) or (2.18) in
[
2
]
). But for
\dim(\mathcal{H})=\infty
, the condition (
2.2
) only implies the following weaker condition (see (14) in
[
5
]
):

\forall\varepsilon>0\>\exists\,x^{0}=x^{0}(\varepsilon)\in\mathbb{S}\>\forall n=0,...,m_{HC}-1\,:\,\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}x^{0}\|\leq\varepsilon.

(2.3)

We remark that, since
B_{c}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
B_{c}
is hypocoercive if and only if
\Lambda(-B_{c})\subset\mathbb{H}
, see Theorem
4.1
below.

For the discrete-time system (
1.2
), we use the following definition, see
[
3
]
for the finite dimensional case.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
B_{d}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

(i)

B_{d}
is called
semi-contractive
if
\|B_{d}\|\leq 1
.

(ii)

B_{d}
is called
hypocontractive
if there exist constants
C\geq 1
and
0<\lambda<1
such that the solutions to the iteration (
1.2
) satisfy

\forall x^{0}\in\mathcal{H},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:\quad\|x_{k}\|\leq C\lambda^{k}\|x^{0}\|.

(2.4)

(iii)

Let
B_{d}
be semi-contractive. Its
hypocontractivity index
(dHC-index)
m_{dHC}
is defined as the smallest integer
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(if it exists) such that

\sum_{j=0}^{m}(B_{d}^{*})^{j}(I-B_{d}^{*}B_{d})B_{d}^{j}\geq\kappa I\,

(2.5)

for some
\kappa>0
.

We remark that, due to
B_{d}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
B_{d}
is hypocontractive if and only if
\Lambda(B_{d})\subset\mathbb{D}
, see Theorem
4.3
below.

The following characterization of the short-time behavior of the propagator norm of (
1.1
) in terms of the HC-index was proven in
[
2
, Th. 2.7]
for matrices
B_{c}
and in
[
5
, Th. 4.1]
for operators:

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
-B_{c}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be semi-dissipative. Then
B_{c}
is hypocoercive (with hypocoercivity index
m_{HC}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
) if and only if

\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|=1-ct^{a}+o(t^{a})\quad\mbox{for }t\to 0+

(2.6)

for some
a,\,c>0
. In this case, necessarily
a=2m_{HC}+1
.

For matrices
B_{c}
, the remainder term in (
2.6
)can be improved to
\mathcal{O}(t^{a+1})
.

In §
3
we present a much simplified proof of this result. The sharp constant
c
has already been known from
[
2
, Th. 2.7]
for matrices and
[
5
, Th. 4.1]
for operators. Its formula is included below in Theorem
3.8
(for operators) and in Corollary
3.9
(for matrices).

3
Continuous-time systems: new proof of short-time decay

In this section we present a new, significantly shortened proof of Theorem
2.3
. While the upper bound for the propagator norm
\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|
was based on case distinctions (see Lemma A.1 in
[
2
]
and §4.2 in
[
5
]
), this is not needed here anymore. Moreover, for bounded operators on a Hilbert space, the proof was detailed in
[
5
]
only for the case of hypocoercivity index
m_{HC}=1
, while all cases are included here. Also, the new proof extends nicely to the time-discrete case analyzed in §
5
.

We start with an auxiliary lemma concerning a minimization problem which plays a central role in establishing the main results of this section.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Then

\displaystyle\min_{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\lambda_{m-n}\overline{\lambda_{m-k}}+2\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{m+n}}{(m+n+1)!}\binom{m+n}{m}\Re(\lambda_{m-n})\right.

(3.1)

\displaystyle\left.+\frac{1}{(2m+1)!}\binom{2m}{m}\right]=\frac{1}{(2m+1)!\binom{2m}{m}}.

(3.2)

Proof.

We first note that the expression we want to minimize is real-valued, so it makes sense to consider the infimum. Moreover, the minimization problem can be rewritten as a constrained minimization of a quadratic form given by

\displaystyle\inf_{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}}

\displaystyle\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\lambda_{m-n}\overline{\lambda_{m-k}}+2\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{m+n}}{(m+n+1)!}\binom{m+n}{m}\Re(\lambda_{m-n})\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(2m+1)!}\binom{2m}{m}

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}\\
\lambda_{0}=1\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\lambda_{m-n}\overline{\lambda_{m-k}}\right].

(3.3)

Using the rescaling
\lambda_{p}\mapsto(-1)^{m-p}(m-p)!\,\lambda_{p}
and then relabeling
\lambda_{p}\mapsto\lambda_{m-p+1}
yields

(
3.3
)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}\\
\lambda_{0}=(-1)^{m}/m!\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{1}{k+n+1}\lambda_{m-n}\overline{\lambda_{m-k}}\right]=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}\\
\lambda_{m+1}=(-1)^{m}/m!\end{subarray}}\left[\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{1}{k+n+1}\lambda_{n+1}\overline{\lambda_{k+1}}\right]

\displaystyle=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}\\
\lambda_{m+1}=(-1)^{m}/m!\end{subarray}}\overline{\lambda}^{T}H_{m+1}\lambda,

(3.4)

where
H_{m+1}
is the (m+1)-dimensional Hilbert matrix and
\lambda=[\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{m+1}]^{T}
. As
H_{m+1}
is known to be positive definite
[
12
, III]
, the minimization can be restricted to real valued vectors
\lambda
, since

\displaystyle\overline{\lambda}^{T}H_{m+1}\lambda

\displaystyle=\left(\Re(\lambda)-i\Im(\lambda)\right)^{T}H_{m+1}\left(\Re(\lambda)+i\Im(\lambda)\right)=\Re(\lambda)^{T}H_{m+1}\Re(\lambda)+\Im(\lambda)^{T}H_{m+1}\Im(\lambda)

\displaystyle\geq\Re(\lambda)^{T}H_{m+1}\Re(\lambda),

where the real part of the vector is taken componentwise. Moreover, the positive definiteness of
H_{m+1}
implies that a minimizer
\lambda_{min}
exists. We proceed using constrained minimization via Lagrange multipliers. From this it follows that the gradient at
\lambda_{min}
is a multiple of the gradient of the constraint, i.e.,
H_{m+1}\lambda_{min}
is a multiple of
e_{m+1}
(the
m+1
-st unit vector), which means that
\lambda_{min}
is a multiple of
H_{m+1}^{-1}e_{m+1}
. Together with the given constraint this readily yields

\displaystyle\lambda_{min}=\frac{H_{m+1}^{-1}e_{m+1}}{(-1)^{m}m!\left(H_{m+1}^{-1}\right)_{m+1,m+1}}.

Inserting this into (
3.4
) yields

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{0},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}\\
\lambda_{m}=(-1)^{m}m!\end{subarray}}\overline{\lambda}^{T}H_{m+1}\lambda

\displaystyle=\overline{\lambda_{min}}^{T}H_{m+1}\lambda_{min}=\frac{1}{(m!)^{2}\left(H_{m+1}^{-1}\right)_{m+1,m+1}}=\frac{1}{(m!)^{2}(2m+1)\binom{2m}{m}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2m+1)!\binom{2m}{m}},

where we used the explicit formula for
H_{m+1}^{-1}

[
12
, I.]
.
∎

In the following, we assume that
B_{c}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with Hermitian part
B_{H}\geq 0
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and let
y:\,\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathcal{H}
satisfy
y(t)\neq 0
on some interval
[0,t_{0})
,
\|y(t)\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
, and
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t)\|=o(t^{m-1-n})
as
t\to 0+
for
0\leq n<m
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-B_{c}t}\frac{y(t)}{\|y(t)\|}\right\|^{2}=1-\frac{2}{\|y(t)\|^{2}}t\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-t)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}y(t)\right\rangle+o(t^{2m+1})

for
t\to 0+
.

Proof.

Using
[
2
, (A.3), (A.6)]
(i.e., the Taylor expansion of
e^{-B_{c}t}
and the Cauchy product), we calculate

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-B_{c}t}\frac{y(t)}{\|y(t)\|}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle e^{-B_{c}^{*}t}e^{-B_{c}t}\frac{y(t)}{\|y(t)\|},\,\frac{y(t)}{\|y(t)\|}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2}{\|y(t)\|^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{2m+1}\frac{(-t)^{j}}{j!}\sum_{k=0}^{j-1}\binom{j-1}{k}\left\langle(B_{c}^{*})^{k}{B_{H}}B_{c}^{j-1-k}y(t),y(t)\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(t^{2m+2})

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2}{\|y(t)\|^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{2m}\sum_{j=k+1}^{2m+1}\frac{(-t)^{j}}{j!}\binom{j-1}{k}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{j-1-k}y(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}y(t)\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(t^{2m+2})

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2}{\|y(t)\|^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{2m}\sum_{n=0}^{2m-k}\frac{(-t)^{n+1+k}}{(n+1+k)!}\binom{n+k}{k}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}y(t)\right\rangle+\mathcal{O}(t^{2m+2})

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2}{\|y(t)\|^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{n=0}^{2m-k}\frac{(-t)^{n+1+k}}{(n+1+k)!}\binom{n+k}{k}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}y(t)\right\rangle+o(t^{2m+1}),

where we have used that
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t)\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
as
t\to 0+
, for
n\geq m
, which follows from the assumption
\|y(t)\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
. Analogously, we reduce the sum further as

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-B_{c}t}\frac{y(t)}{\|y(t)\|}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=1+\frac{2}{\|y(t)\|^{2}}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{n=0}^{m}\frac{(-t)^{n+1+k}}{(n+1+k)!}\binom{n+k}{k}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}y(t)\right\rangle+o(t^{2m+1})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2}{\|y(t)\|^{2}}t\sum_{k=0}^{m}\sum_{n=0}^{m}\frac{(-t)^{n+k}}{(n+1+k)!}\binom{n+k}{k}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}y(t)\right\rangle+o(t^{2m+1}),

which is the claimed result.
∎

If
m
is the hypocoercivity index of
B_{c}
, the conditions on
y(t)
in Lemma
3.2
are satisfied, e.g., by
y(t):=x^{0}(t^{m-1})
using
x^{0}
from (
2.3
). Note further that in Lemma
3.2
the function
y
does not need to be continuous in
t
.

The following theorem yields the (as it turns out) optimal lower bound for the asymptotic expansion of the propagator norm, assuming essentially that the HC-index of
B_{c}
is at least
m
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
-B_{c}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be semi-dissipative, and let
B_{H}
be the Hermitian part of
B_{c}
.
Suppose that, for some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the coercivity condition

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(B_{c}^{*})^{j}B_{H}B_{c}^{j}\geq\kappa I

(3.5)

is
violated
for all
\kappa>0
. Then

\displaystyle\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|^{2}\geq 1-t^{2m+1}\frac{2}{(2m+1)!\binom{2m}{m}}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq\delta,\ 0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|^{2}+o(t^{2m+1})

(3.6)

for
t\to 0+
.

Note that for
m=0
the assumption (
3.5
) is void and the infimum in (
3.6
) is just taken over
\mathbb{S}
. For
m>0
these infima are never taken over the empty set, due to (
2.3
). Moreover, their limit as
\delta\to 0
is finite, since
\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Further note that in view of (
2.2
), the assumption (
3.5
) implies that
m_{HC}\geq m
, if the HC-index of
B_{c}
exists.

Proof.

We consider the sets

\displaystyle S_{t}\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{S}:\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq t^{m+1-p},\ 0\leq p<m\},

which are nonempty for every
t>0
due to assumption (
3.5
). Consequently, for each
t>0
we can choose an element
y_{0}(t)\in S_{t}
such that

\displaystyle\left\lvert\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{0}(t)\right\|-\inf_{y\in S_{t}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|\right\rvert<t.

(3.7)

Then we have

\displaystyle\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y_{0}(t)\right\|=\mathcal{O}(t^{m+1-p})

(3.8)

for
0\leq p<m
, and more generally we have the (non-sharp) estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y_{0}(t)\right\|=\mathcal{O}(t^{m-p})

(3.9)

for any
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
(because
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y_{0}(t)\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
for any
p\geq m
). From (
3.7
) we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{0}(t)\right\|=\lim_{t\to 0}\ \inf_{y\in S_{t}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq\delta,\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|,

(3.10)

where the limit in the middle and on the right-hand side is well-defined as the expression is increasing with respect to
t
and
\delta
, respectively, and bounded from above by
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}\|
.
The equality of the last two limits follows from the enclosure

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq t^{2},\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|\leq\inf_{y\in S_{t}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|\leq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq t^{m+1},\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|

for
0<t\leq 1
, and hence choosing
\delta=t^{2}
and, respectively,
\delta=t^{m+1}
in (
3.10
).

Next, we choose
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
as the unique minimizer of the quadratic form (
3.1
) and consider

y(t)\coloneqq y_{0}(t)+\sum_{p=1}^{m}\lambda_{p}\,(tB_{c})^{p}y_{0}(t),\,t\geq 0.

(3.11)

Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}(tB_{c})^{p}y_{0}(t)\right\|=\mathcal{O}(t^{m+1-n})

(3.12)

for
0\leq p,n
with
p+n<m
due to (
3.8
) and

\displaystyle\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}(tB_{c})^{p}y_{0}(t)\right\|=\mathcal{O}(t^{m-n})

(3.13)

for any
0\leq p,n
due to (
3.9
). Consequently,
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t)\|=o(t^{m-1-n})
,
\|y(t)\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
, and
y(t)\neq 0
on some interval
[0,t_{0})
. Thus, Lemma
3.2
yields

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-B_{c}t}\frac{y(t)}{\|y(t)\|}\right\|^{2}=1-\frac{2}{\|y(t)\|^{2}}t\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-t)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}y(t)\right\rangle+o(t^{2m+1})

(3.14)

as
t\to 0+
.

When expanding
y(t)
in the inner product of (
3.14
), i.e., in

\sum_{p=1}^{m}\sum_{q=1}^{m}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}(tB_{c})^{p}y_{0}(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}(tB_{c})^{q}y_{0}(t)\right\rangle,

(3.15)

for the sum in (
3.11
), we need to distinguish several cases when estimating the order of (
3.15
):

For
n+p<m
we have the estimate

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}(tB_{c})^{p}y_{0}(t)\|=\mathcal{O}(t^{m+1-n})

using (
3.12
), and

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}(tB_{c})^{q}y_{0}(t)\|=\mathcal{O}(t^{m-k})

using (
3.13
) for any
k,\,q
. Together these estimates yield

\displaystyle t^{1+k+n}\left\lvert\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}(tB_{c})^{p}y_{0}(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}(tB_{c})^{q}y_{0}(t)\right\rangle\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(t^{2m+2}),

contributing only to the remainder term in (
3.14
).
This last estimate also holds if
k+q<m
and
n,p
are arbitrary. In the case
n+p\geq m+1
and
k+q\geq m
(or
n+p\geq m
and
k+q\geq m+1
), we get

\displaystyle t^{1+k+n}\left\lvert\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}(tB_{c})^{p}y_{0}(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}(tB_{c})^{q}y_{0}(t)\right\rangle\right\rvert

(3.16)

\displaystyle=t^{1+k+n+p+q}\left\lvert\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n+p}y_{0}(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k+q}y_{0}(t)\right\rangle\right\rvert=\mathcal{O}(t^{2m+2})

using
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n+p}y_{0}(t)\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k+q}y_{0}(t)\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
.
So, (
3.16
) also contributes only to the remainder term in (
3.14
).

To sum up, if we expand
y(t)
in (
3.14
),
only the terms with
n+p=k+q=m
may
not
be part of the remainder term. Hence, (
3.14
) simplifies to

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-B_{c}t}\frac{y(t)}{\|y(t)\|}\right\|^{2}=1-\frac{2}{\|y(t)\|^{2}}t^{2m+1}\bigg(\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\lambda_{m-n}{\lambda_{m-k}}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{m+n}}{(m+n+1)!}\binom{m+n}{m}\lambda_{m-n}+\frac{1}{(2m+1)!}\binom{2m}{m}\bigg)\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{0}(t)\right\|^{2}+o(t^{2m+1})

\displaystyle\qquad=1-2t^{2m+1}\bigg(\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\lambda_{m-n}{\lambda_{m-k}}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{m+n}}{(m+n+1)!}\binom{m+n}{m}\lambda_{m-n}+\frac{1}{(2m+1)!}\binom{2m}{m}\bigg)\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{0}(t)\right\|^{2}+o(t^{2m+1}),

where we have used
\frac{1}{\|y(t)\|^{2}}=1+\mathcal{O}(t)
in the last equality. By construction of
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{m}
(see Lemma
3.1
) this yields

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-B_{c}t}\frac{y(t)}{\|y(t)\|}\right\|^{2}=1-t^{2m+1}\frac{2}{(2m+1)!\binom{2m}{m}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{0}(t)\right\|^{2}+o(t^{2m+1}),

and due to (
3.10
) (using
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{0}(t)\|^{2}=\lim_{t\to 0}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{0}(t)\|^{2}+o(t)
) we get

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-B_{c}t}\frac{y(t)}{\|y(t)\|}\right\|^{2}=1-t^{2m+1}\frac{2}{(2m+1)!\binom{2m}{m}}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq\delta,\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|^{2}+o(t^{2m+1}).

The claim now follows immediately, since

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-B_{c}t}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left\|e^{-B_{c}t}\frac{y(t)}{\|y(t)\|}\right\|^{2}.

∎

Remark 3.4
.

The lower bound in Theorem
3.3
already implies that, if
B_{c}
has hypocoercivity index greater than or equal to
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then
\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|=1-\mathcal{O}(t^{2m+1})
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
z_{0},\dots,z_{m}\in\mathcal{H}
with some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
fixed. Then there exists
C_{m}>0
(independent of
z_{0},...,z_{m}
) such that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle z_{m-n},z_{m-k}\right\rangle\geq C_{m}\left\|z_{j}\right\|^{2}

for all
0\leq j\leq m
and for
j=0
we explicitly obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle z_{m-n},z_{m-k}\right\rangle\geq\frac{1}{(2m+1)!\binom{2m}{m}}\left\|z_{0}\right\|^{2}.

(3.17)

Proof.

Let
0\leq j\leq m
be arbitrary but fixed, and let

\displaystyle z_{p}=\lambda_{p}z_{j}+\tilde{z}_{p}

(3.18)

be an orthogonal decomposition, i.e.,
\langle\tilde{z}_{p},z_{j}\rangle=0
for every
0\leq p\leq m
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle z_{m-n},z_{m-k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle\lambda_{m-n}z_{j}+\tilde{z}_{m-n},\lambda_{m-k}z_{j}+\tilde{z}_{m-k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\lambda_{m-n}\overline{\lambda_{m-k}}\|z_{j}\|^{2}+\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle\tilde{z}_{m-n},\tilde{z}_{m-k}\right\rangle.

(3.19)

Now we note that

\displaystyle\left[\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\right]_{n,k=0,...,m}\geq C_{m}\Id,

since it is just a congruently transformed Hilbert matrix (compare with (
3.3
)). Consequently,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\langle\tilde{z}_{m-n},\tilde{z}_{m-k}\rangle\geq 0,

being the trace of the product of two Hermitian, positive semidefinite matrices. Moreover,

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\lambda_{m-n}\overline{\lambda_{m-k}}\|z_{j}\|^{2}\geq C_{m}\|z_{j}\|^{2},

where we additionally have used
\lambda_{j}=1
(due to (
3.18
)). Using these lower bounds in (
3
) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle z_{m-n},z_{m-k}\right\rangle\geq C_{m}\|z_{j}\|^{2}.

The last claim, i.e. (
3.17
), follows from using
\lambda_{0}=1
in the first sum of (
3
) and applying Lemma
3.1
in the last step:

\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\lambda_{m-n}\overline{\lambda_{m-k}}\|z_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg[\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\lambda_{m-n}\overline{\lambda_{m-k}}+2\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\frac{(-1)^{m+n}}{(m+n+1)!}\binom{m+n}{m}\Re(\lambda_{m-n})

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ \ +\frac{1}{(2m+1)!}\binom{2m}{m}\bigg]\|z_{0}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{(2m+1)!\binom{2m}{m}}\left\|z_{0}\right\|^{2}.\qed

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
B_{c}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
have hypocoercivity index greater than or equal to
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and let
y\colon\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathcal{H}
satisfy
\|y(t)\|=1
for
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
and
\|e^{-B_{c}t}y(t)\|-\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|=o(t^{2m+1})
for
t\to 0+
. Then
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y(t)\|=o(t^{m-1-p})
for
0\leq p<m
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction over
m
. Concerning
m=0
one could choose
y(t)\equiv y_{0}
for any
y_{0}\in\mathbb{S}
but, anyhow, there is nothing to show.

Next, we assume that the statement holds for
m
and we want to show the corresponding statement for
m+1
. So let us assume that
B_{c}
has hypocoercivity index greater than or equal to
m+1
and
y\colon\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}\to\mathcal{H}
satisfies
\|y(t)\|=1
for
t\in\mathbb{R}_{0}^{+}
and
\|e^{-B_{c}t}y(t)\|-\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|=o(t^{2m+3})
for
t\to 0+
. Then we can use the induction hypothesis to deduce that
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y(t)\|=o(t^{m-1-p})
for
0\leq p<m
and thus we can expand the squared norm using Lemma
3.2
:

\displaystyle\|e^{-B_{c}t}y(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-2t\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-t)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}y(t)\right\rangle+o(t^{2m+1})

\displaystyle=1-2t\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle t^{n}\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t),t^{k}\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}y(t)\right\rangle+o(t^{2m+1})

\displaystyle\leq 1-2tC_{m}t^{2p}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y(t)\|^{2}+o(t^{2m+1})

for any
0\leq p<m+1
, where the last inequality with corresponding constant
C_{m}>0
follows from Lemma
3.5
applied to
z_{j}\coloneqq t^{p}\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y(t)
. Consequently, we get

\displaystyle 2C_{m}t^{2p+1}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y(t)\|^{2}\leq 1-\|e^{-B_{c}t}y(t)\|^{2}+o(t^{2m+1}).

Then Remark
3.4
yields
1-\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|=\mathcal{O}(t^{2m+3})
and, together with the assumption that
\|e^{-B_{c}t}y(t)\|-\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|=o(t^{2m+1})
, we get

\displaystyle 2C_{m}t^{2p+1}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y(t)\|^{2}\leq o(t^{2m+1}).

Hence,

\displaystyle\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y(t)\|=o(t^{m-p})

for any
0\leq p<m+1
.
∎

In Lemma
3.6
, a function
y(t)
satisfying the listed condition can always be found due to the definition of the operator norm
\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|
as a supremum.

The following theorem yields the optimal upper bound for the asymptotic expansion of the propagator norm.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
B_{c}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
have hypocoercivity index
m_{HC}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|^{2}\leq 1-t^{2m_{HC}+1}\frac{2}{(2m_{HC}+1)!\binom{2m_{HC}}{m_{HC}}}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq\delta,\,0\leq p<m_{HC}\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m_{HC}}y\right\|^{2}+o(t^{2m_{HC}+1})

for
t\to 0+
.

Recall from Theorem
3.3
that for
m_{HC}=0
the infimum is just taken over
\mathbb{S}
.

Proof.

Let
y(t)\in\mathcal{H}
with
\|y(t)\|=1
be such that
\|e^{-B_{c}t}y(t)\|-\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|=o(t^{2m+1})
. Here and in the remainder of this proof we abbreviate the HC-index by
m
. Then Lemma
3.6
implies that
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t)\|=o(t^{m-1-n})
for
0\leq n<m
. Hence, we can apply Lemma
3.2
and obtain:

\displaystyle\|e^{-B_{c}t}y(t)\|^{2}

\displaystyle=1-2t\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-t)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}y(t)\right\rangle+o(t^{2m+1})

\displaystyle=1-2t\sum_{n=0}^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\frac{(-1)^{k+n}}{(k+n+1)!}\binom{k+n}{k}\left\langle t^{n}\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(t),t^{k}\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{k}y(t)\right\rangle+o(t^{2m+1})

\displaystyle\leq 1-2t\frac{1}{(2m+1)!\binom{2m}{m}}t^{2m}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y(t)\right\|^{2}+g(t),\quad\mbox{with }g(t)=o(t^{2m+1}),

where we have used Lemma
3.5
with
z_{0}:=t^{m}\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y(t)
for the last inequality. Consequently, for any
\delta>0
we can find
T_{\delta}>0
such that for any
0\leq t\leq T_{\delta}
it holds that

\displaystyle\|e^{-B_{c}t}y(t)\|^{2}\leq 1-c_{\delta}\,t^{2m+1}+g(t),

with

c_{\delta}:=\frac{2}{(2m+1)!\binom{2m}{m}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq\delta,\ 0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|^{2}.

Note that
y(t)\big|_{[0,T_{\delta}]}
is inside the set in which we minimize. Moreover, the function
g
is independent of
\delta
,
c_{\delta}
increases as
\delta\to 0
, but
T_{\delta}
(typically) decreases to 0 as
\delta\to 0
.

Using the fact that
\|e^{-B_{c}t}y(t)\|-\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|=o(t^{2m+1})
we obtain for every fixed
\delta>0
:

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|^{2}-1}{t^{2m+1}}=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\|e^{-B_{c}t}y(t)\|^{2}-1}{t^{2m+1}}\leq-c_{\delta}.

Hence,

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|^{2}-1}{t^{2m+1}}\leq-\lim_{\delta\to 0}c_{\delta},

which is a reformulation of the claim.
∎

In summary, we have the following short-time decay result.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
-B_{c}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be semi-dissipative. Then
B_{c}
is hypocoercive (with hypocoercivity index
m_{HC}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
) if and only if

\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|=1-ct^{a}+o(t^{a})\quad\mbox{for }t\to 0+

(3.20)

for some
a,\,c>0
. In this case, necessarily
a=2m_{HC}+1
and

\displaystyle c=\frac{1}{(2m_{HC}+1)!\,\binom{2m_{HC}}{m_{HC}}}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq\delta,\ 0\leq p<m_{HC}\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m_{HC}}y\right\|^{2}.

(3.21)

Proof.

\implies
: Since the HC-index of
B_{c}
equals
m_{HC}
, condition (
3.5
) with
m:=m_{HC}
is violated for all
\kappa>0
. Then this direction follows immediately from Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.7
. For later reference note that this direction of the proof only used that the HC-index of
B_{c}
is
m_{HC}
as defined by the coercivity estimate (
2.2
), but we did not use the exponential decay as in (
2.1
).

\impliedby
:
First, let
m\coloneqq\left\lceil\frac{a-1}{2}\right\rceil
. Then the operator
\sum_{p=0}^{m}(B_{c}^{*})^{p}B_{H}B_{c}^{p}
must be coercive because otherwise Theorem
3.3
(applied with
m+1
in place of
m
) would imply

\displaystyle\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|^{2}\geq 1-\tilde{c}t^{2m+3}+o(t^{2m+3})

for some
\tilde{c}\geq 0
, which contradicts the assumption that (
3.20
) as
2m+3>2m+1\geq a
. Consequently,
B_{c}
has a hypocoercivity index smaller than or equal to
m
. Then the claim, i.e., that
m_{HC}(B_{c})=m
and
a=2m_{HC}+1
, follows immediately from the converse direction.

Finally, (
3.20
) also implies that
B_{c}
is hypocoercive (by a general semigroup result in
[
16
, Proposition V.1.7]
; see also
[
5
, Proposition 4.4]
). Alternatively, the hypocoercivity of
B_{c}
, i.e., the exponential decay of the corresponding semigroup, can be obtained directly by considering the Lyapunov functional
\langle x,Px\rangle
with the bounded, symmetric, coercive operator
P:=\sum_{j=0}^{m}(B_{c}^{*})^{j}B_{c}^{j}
, see
[
6
, §2]
for details.
∎

For matrices, Theorem
3.8
simplifies in two ways, the formula for the constant
c
and the order of the remainder term:

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
-B_{c}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be semi-dissipative and let
\mathcal{H}
be finite-dimensional. Then
B_{c}
is hypocoercive (with hypocoercivity index
m_{HC}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
) if and only if

\displaystyle\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|=1-ct^{a}+\mathcal{O}(t^{a+1})\quad\mbox{for }t\to 0+,

(3.22)

for some
a,\,c>0
. In this case, necessarily
a=2m_{HC}+1
and

\displaystyle c=\frac{1}{(2m_{HC}+1)!\,\binom{2m_{HC}}{m_{HC}}}\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\ker(\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}),\,0\leq p<m_{HC}\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m_{HC}}y\right\|^{2}.

(3.23)

Proof.

The main part of this equivalence just carries over from Theorem
3.8
. So we only have to prove the formula for the constant and the order of the remainder term. In the remainder of this proof we abbreviate the HC-index of
B_{c}
by
m
.

First, note that due to
[
23
, Lemma 1]
there exists
t_{0}>0
such that the mapping
t\mapsto\|e^{-B_{c}t}\|
is analytic on
[0,t_{0})
, which directly implies the improved remainder term in (
3.22
).

Second, for any
\delta>0
we have

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq\delta,\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|^{2}\leq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\ker(\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}),\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|^{2},

and thus

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq\delta,\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|^{2}\leq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\ker(\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}),\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|^{2},

relating the two constants in (
3.21
) and (
3.23
).
Concerning the other direction of the estimate, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
we choose a sequence
y_{n}\in\mathbb{S}
with
\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y_{n}\|\leq\frac{1}{n}
for all
0\leq p<m
(which exists due to (
2.3
)) and with

\displaystyle\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{n}\right\|^{2}\leq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq\frac{1}{n},\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{n}.

(3.24)

As
\mathcal{H}
is finite-dimensional and
\|y_{n}\|=1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we can find a convergent subsequence
y_{n_{k}}
with limit
y_{*}\in\mathcal{H}
. Then we clearly have
\|y_{*}\|=1
and
y_{*}\in\ker(\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p})
for
0\leq p<m
. Moreover, taking the limit
n\to\infty
in (
3.24
) yields

\displaystyle\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{*}\right\|^{2}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq\frac{1}{n},\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|^{2}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\ker(\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}),\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|^{2}\leq\lim_{\delta\to 0}\ \inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\|\leq\delta,\,0\leq p<m\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\right\|^{2}.

Finally, we note that the infimum can be replaced by a minimum because we are taking the infimum of a continuous function over a compact set.
∎

Remark 3.10
.

We now illustrate for the case
m_{HC}=1
why the leading decay term in the expansion of the propagator norm has (at least) order 3: For matrices, the index
m_{HC}=1
implies
\dim(\ker(B_{H}))\geq 1
. Any solution to (
1.1
) with normalized
x^{0}\in\ker(B_{H})
satisfies
\frac{d}{dt}\|x(t)\|\big|_{t=0}=0
. The trajectory
x(t)
and its norm are analytic on
\mathbb{R}
(also for
t<0
!). The Taylor expansion of the norm of this trajectory cannot be of the form
\|x(t)\|=1-ct^{2}+\mathcal{O}(t^{3})
with some
c>0
, as this would violate the semi-dissipativity for
t<0
. Thus, this trajectory norm (and hence also the propagator norm) must be of the form
1-ct^{3}+\mathcal{O}(t^{4})
with some
c\geq 0
. Note that this argument cannot be applied directly to the propagator norm, as it is in general not real analytic in a neighborhood of
t=0
, see
[
19
,
23
]
.

4
Equivalence of hypocoercivity index and hypocontractivity index via the Cayley transform

In this section we extend a result that was obtained for finite dimensional systems in
[
3
]
to the case of infinite dimensional systems. Namely, the hypocoercivity index of a continuous-time system coincides with the hypocontractivity index of the discrete-time system obtained by applying the implicit midpoint rule as time-discretization method.

4.1
Continuous-time systems

We first recall the results for continuous-time dynamical systems in Hilbert spaces.
Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space,
B_{c}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
\ker B_{c}=\{0\}
, and
x^{0}\in\mathcal{H}
be an initial datum.
Consider the continuous-time dynamical system (
1.1
).
Then one has the following classical result about exponential stability, see e.g. Theorem I. 3.14,
[
16
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

The zero solution
x\equiv 0
of (
1.1
) is exponentially stable if and only if
\Lambda(-B_{c})\subset\mathbb{H}
.

In Lemma 2 of
[
4
]
the following result was shown.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
-B_{c}=B_{S}-B_{H}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, with
B_{S}=-B_{S}^{*}
and
B_{H}=B_{H}^{*}
, be semi-dissipative, i.e., it satisfies
B_{H}\geq 0
. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

There exists
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\Span\left(\bigcup_{j=0}^{m}\im\left((B^{*}_{c})^{j}\sqrt{B_{H}}\right)\right)=\mathcal{H}.

(ii)

There exists
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\bigcap_{j=0}^{m}\ker\left(\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{j}\right)=\{0\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \Span\left(\bigcup_{j=0}^{m}\im\left((B^{*}_{c})^{j}\sqrt{B_{H}}\right)\right)\text{ is closed}.

(iii)

There exists
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m}(B_{c}^{*})^{j}\,B_{H}\,B_{c}^{j}\geq\kappa I

for some
\kappa>0
.

Moreover, if any of these conditions is satisfied for some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then all conditions are satisfied for this
m
.

After recalling the results for the continuous-time case, in the next subsection we derive the corresponding results in the discrete-time case.

4.2
Discrete-time dynamical systems

Consider the discrete-time dynamical system (
1.2
) with
B_{d}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
1\not\in\Lambda(B_{d})
(in analogy to
0\not\in\Lambda(B_{c})
), and an initial datum
x^{0}\in\mathcal{H}
.
The system is called
exponentially stable
(or, equivalently,
B_{d}
is called
hypocontractive
) if there exist constants
C\geq 1
and
0<\lambda<1
such that for all
x^{0}
and all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
one has

\|x_{k}\|\leq C\lambda^{k}\|x^{0}\|.

One has the following classical result about
exponential stability (see e.g Proposition II. 1.3 in
[
15
]
after noting that
\Lambda(B_{d})\subset\mathbb{D}
is equivalent to
\rho(B_{d})<1
).

Theorem 4.3
.

The zero solution of
(
1.2
)
(i.e.
x_{k}=0
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
) is exponentially stable if and only if
\Lambda(B_{d})\subset\mathbb{D}
.

One has the following result which follows directly from Lemma 2 in
[
4
]

by setting there
R\coloneqq\Id-B_{d}^{*}B_{d}
and
J\coloneqq B_{d}^{*}
.
The assumption that
J
has to be skew-adjoint can be removed without any adjustments to the proof.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
B_{d}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be semi-contractive. Then the following conditions are equivalent:

(i)

There exists
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\Span\left(\bigcup_{j=0}^{m}\im\left((B_{d}^{*})^{j}\sqrt{\Id-B_{d}^{*}B_{d}}\right)\right)=\mathcal{H}.

(ii)

There exists
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\bigcap_{j=0}^{m}\ker\left(\sqrt{\Id-B_{d}^{*}B_{d}}B_{d}^{j}\right)=\{0\}\ \ \text{and}\ \ \Span\left(\bigcup_{j=0}^{m}\im\left((B_{d}^{*})^{j}\sqrt{\Id-B_{d}^{*}B_{d}}\right)\right)\text{ is closed}.

(iii)

There exists
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m}(B_{d}^{*})^{j}(\Id-B_{d}^{*}B_{d})B_{d}^{j}\geq\kappa I

(4.1)

for some
\kappa>0
.

Moreover, if any of these conditions is satisfied for some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then all conditions are satisfied for this
m
.

The following theorem then is the operator analog of Theorem 40 in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
B_{d}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be semi-contractive and
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

B_{d}
has hypocontractivity index
m
.

(ii)

\|B_{d}^{j}\|=1
for
1\leq j\leq m
and
\|B_{d}^{m+1}\|<1
.

Proof.

We first note that for
x\in\mathcal{H}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}(B_{d}^{*})^{j}(\Id-B_{d}^{*}B_{d})B_{d}^{j}\right)x,x\right\rangle=\left\langle\left(\Id-(B_{d}^{*})^{n+1}B_{d}^{n+1}\right)x,x\right\rangle=\|x\|^{2}-\|B_{d}^{n+1}x\|^{2}.

The statement now follows immediately from the hypocontractivity condition (
4.1
) and the following equivalences for any
0\leq\kappa<1
:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{n}(B_{d}^{*})^{j}(\Id-B_{d}^{*}B_{d})B_{d}^{j}\geq\kappa\Id

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm for all }x\in\mathcal{H}:\left\langle\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n}(B_{d}^{*})^{j}(\Id-B_{d}^{*}B_{d})B_{d}^{j}\right)x,x\right\rangle\geq\kappa\|x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm for all }x\in\mathcal{H}:\|x\|^{2}-\|B_{d}^{n+1}x\|^{2}\geq\kappa\|x\|^{2}

\displaystyle\iff

\displaystyle\sqrt{1-\kappa}\geq\|B_{d}^{n+1}\|.\qed

We are now prepared to show the equivalence of the hypocoercivity index and the hypocontractivity index
when the Cayley transform is used for the mapping between the two concepts.

4.3
Relation between hypocoercivity and hypocontractivity via the Cayley transform

In this subsection we recall the classical (scaled) Cayley transform (which corresponds to a discretization with the implicit midpoint rule) and show the equivalence of the hypocoercivity index and the hypocontractivity index under this map.

Definition 4.6
.

Let
\tau>0
. The
scaled Cayley transform
is the map

\displaystyle M_{\tau}\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{\frac{2}{\tau}\right\}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}

(4.2)

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\longmapsto\frac{1+\frac{\tau}{2}z}{1-\frac{\tau}{2}z}.

(4.3)

For
\tau=2
,
M=M_{2}
is called
Cayley transform
.

The scaled Cayley transform has the following well-known properties, see e.g.
[
20
]
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\tau>0
. The scaled Cayley transform (
4.2
)
is a bi-holomorphism with inverse given by

\displaystyle M_{\tau}^{-1}\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus\{-1\}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}\setminus\left\{\frac{2}{\tau}\right\}

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\longmapsto\frac{2(z-1)}{\tau(z+1)}.

Moreover,
M_{\tau}(\mathbb{H})=\mathbb{D}
and
M_{\tau}^{-1}(\mathbb{D})=\mathbb{H}
.

The relationship between the exponential stability of continuous-time and discrete-time systems is given by the following lemma.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
B_{c}
, respectively
B_{d}
, be in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and suppose that
\tau>0
is such that
M_{\tau}(-B_{c})
, respectively
M_{\tau}^{-1}(B_{d})
, are well-defined in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

(i)

If the zero solution of
(
1.1
)
is exponentially stable then the zero solution of
(
1.2
)
with
B_{d}\coloneqq M_{\tau}(-B_{c})
is exponentially stable.

(ii)

If the zero solution of
(
1.2
)
is exponentially stable then the zero solution of
(
1.1
)
with
-B_{c}\coloneqq M_{\tau}^{-1}(B_{d})
is exponentially stable.

We remark that
M_{\tau}(-B_{c})
(resp.
M_{\tau}^{-1}(B_{d})
) are well-defined via the holomorphic functional calculus (see e.g.
[
14
, Chapter VII, 3, Definition 9]
) if
\frac{2}{\tau}\not\in\Lambda(-B_{c})
(resp.
-1\not\in\Lambda(B_{d}
)).

Proof.

We prove only (i), the proof for (ii) follows analogously: Let the zero solution of
(
1.1
)
be exponentially stable. Then by Theorem
4.1
,
\Lambda(-B_{c})\subset\mathbb{H}
. Hence,
B_{d}\coloneqq M_{\tau}(-B_{c})
is well-defined, and by the spectral mapping theorem (
[
14
, Chapter VII, 3, Theorem 11]
)
\Lambda(B_{d})=M_{\tau}(\Lambda(-B_{c}))\subset M_{\tau}(\mathbb{H})=\mathbb{D}
. Consequently, Theorem
4.3
implies that the zero solution of
(
1.2
)
is exponentially stable.
∎

The following well-known fact will be used in our results without explicit mentioning it.

Remark 4.9
.

For
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{C}
open with
\Lambda(A)\subset\mathcal{O}
let
f,g\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{C}
be analytic; hence
f(A)
commutes with
g(A)
(
[
14
, Chapter VII, 3, Theorem 10 b]
). Then rational expressions in
A
commute with each other if they are well-defined.

In order to show that not only exponential stability but also the hypocoercivity/hypocontractivity index is preserved under the Cayley transform, we need the following auxiliary lemmas.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
A,\tilde{A}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
\tilde{A}\geq 0
and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\Id+A^{*})^{k}(\Id-A^{*})^{n-k}\tilde{A}(\Id-A)^{n-k}(\Id+A)^{k}\geq\sum_{k=0}^{n}(A^{*})^{k}\tilde{A}A^{k}.

(4.4)

Proof.

We proceed by induction. For
n=0
, (
4.4
) is trivially satisfied because the left- and right-hand side are equal to
\tilde{A}
. Now assume that (
4.4
) holds for some
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}(\Id+A^{*})^{k}(\Id-A^{*})^{n+1-k}\tilde{A}(\Id-A)^{n+1-k}(\Id+A)^{k}

\displaystyle=(\Id+A^{*})^{n+1}\tilde{A}(\Id+A)^{n+1}+(\Id-A^{*})\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\Id+A^{*})^{k}(\Id-A^{*})^{n-k}\tilde{A}(\Id-A)^{n-k}(\Id+A)^{k}\right)(\Id-A)

\displaystyle\geq(\Id+A^{*})^{n+1}\tilde{A}(\Id+A)^{n+1}+(\Id-A^{*})\sum_{k=0}^{n}(A^{*})^{k}\tilde{A}A^{k}(\Id-A).

Analogously,

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}(\Id+A^{*})^{k}(\Id-A^{*})^{n+1-k}\tilde{A}(\Id-A)^{n+1-k}(\Id+A)^{k}

\displaystyle=(\Id-A^{*})^{n+1}\tilde{A}(\Id-A)^{n+1}+(\Id+A^{*})\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}(\Id+A^{*})^{k}(\Id-A^{*})^{n-k}\tilde{A}(\Id-A)^{n-k}(\Id+A)^{k}\right)(\Id+A)

\displaystyle\geq(\Id-A^{*})^{n+1}\tilde{A}(\Id-A)^{n+1}+(\Id+A^{*})\sum_{k=0}^{n}(A^{*})^{k}\tilde{A}A^{k}(\Id+A).

Adding these two inequalities yields

\displaystyle 2\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}(\Id+A^{*})^{k}(\Id-A^{*})^{n+1-k}\tilde{A}(\Id-A)^{n+1-k}(\Id+A)^{k}

\displaystyle\geq(\Id+A^{*})^{n+1}\tilde{A}(\Id+A)^{n+1}+(\Id-A^{*})^{n+1}\tilde{A}(\Id-A)^{n+1}+2\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}(A^{*})^{k}\tilde{A}A^{k}+2\sum_{k=0}^{n}(A^{*})^{k}\tilde{A}A^{k}

\displaystyle\geq 2\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}(A^{*})^{k}\tilde{A}A^{k},

which proves the assertion after dividing by
2
.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

(i)

If
-1\notin\Lambda(A)
, then

\displaystyle-\left(M^{-1}(A)\right)_{H}=(A^{*}+\Id)^{-1}(\Id-A^{*}A)(A+\Id)^{-1}.

(ii)

If
1\notin\Lambda(A)
, then

\displaystyle\Id-M(A)^{*}M(A)=4(\Id-A^{*})^{-1}(-A_{H})(\Id-A)^{-1}.

Proof.

(i)

As
-1\notin\Lambda(A)
, all expressions are well-defined. Moreover,

\displaystyle-\left(M^{-1}(A)\right)_{H}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\left(M^{-1}(A)^{*}+M^{-1}(A)\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\left((A^{*}-\Id)(A^{*}+\Id)^{-1}+(A-\Id)(A+\Id)^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}(A^{*}+\Id)^{-1}\big((A^{*}-\Id)(A+\Id)+(A^{*}+\Id)(A-\Id)\big)(A+\Id)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(A^{*}+\Id)^{-1}\left(\Id-A^{*}A\right)(A+\Id)^{-1}.

(ii)

Again, as
1\notin\Lambda(A)
, all expressions are well-defined and

\displaystyle\Id-M(A)^{*}M(A)

\displaystyle=\Id-(\Id+A^{*})(\Id-A^{*})^{-1}(\Id+A)(\Id-A)^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\Id-A^{*})^{-1}\big((\Id-A^{*})(\Id-A)-(\Id+A^{*})(\Id+A)\big)(\Id-A)^{-1}

\displaystyle=4(\Id-A^{*})^{-1}(-A_{H})(\Id-A)^{-1}.\qed

With these two lemmas we can show
the following equivalence result for the hypocoercivity and the hypocontractivity index under the Cayley transform.
For finite-dimensional spaces it was shown in
[
3
, Theorem 51]
using the second criteria from Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.4
.

Theorem 4.12
.

Let
B_{c}
and
B_{d}
be in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
with
-1\not\in\Lambda(B_{c})\cap\Lambda(B_{d})

such that
M(-B_{c})
and
M^{-1}(B_{d})
,
are well-defined in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

(i)

If the zero solution of
(
1.1
)
is exponentially stable and
B_{c}
has hypocoercivity index
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then the zero solution of
(
1.2
)
with
B_{d}\coloneqq M(-B_{c})
is exponentially stable and
B_{d}
has hypocontractivity index
m
.

(ii)

If the zero solution of
(
1.2
)
is exponentially stable and
B_{d}
has hypocontractivity index
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then the zero solution of
(
1.1
)
with
-B_{c}\coloneqq M^{-1}(B_{d})
is exponentially stable and
B_{c}
has hypocoercivity index
m
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the hypocoercivity/hypocontractivity index
m
(both at the same time).
For the case that
m=0
we first assume that
B_{c}
has hypocoercivity index
0
, meaning that there exists
\kappa>0
such that for the Hermitian part of
B_{c}
we have

\displaystyle B_{H}\geq\kappa\Id.

Using Lemma
4.11
(ii), we readily deduce that

\displaystyle\Id-M(-B_{c})^{*}M(-B_{c})=4(\Id+B_{c}^{*})^{-1}B_{H}(\Id+B_{c})^{-1}\geq\tilde{\kappa}\Id

for some
\tilde{\kappa}>0
due to the invertibility of
\Id+B_{c}
. Consequently,
M(-B_{c})
has hypocontractivity index
0
.

Conversely, assume that
B_{d}
has hypocontractivity index
0
, so that there exists
\kappa>0
such that

\displaystyle\Id-B_{d}^{*}B_{d}\geq\kappa\Id.

Again, Lemma
4.11
(i) yields

\displaystyle-\left(M^{-1}(B_{d})\right)_{H}=(B_{d}^{*}+\Id)^{-1}(\Id-B_{d}^{*}B_{d})(B_{d}+\Id)^{-1}\geq\tilde{\kappa}\Id

for some
\tilde{\kappa}>0
due to the invertibility of
B_{d}+\Id
. Therefore,
-M^{-1}(B_{d})
has hypocoercivity index
0
.

Suppose now that for some fixed
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
both statements (i) and (ii) hold for all
m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
m^{\prime}\leq m
and assume that
B_{c}
has hypocoercivity index
m+1
. Note that the latter assumption rules out the assumptions in statement (i) for
m^{\prime}\leq m
. Using Lemma
4.11
(ii) and the definition of the Cayley transform we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(M(-B_{c})^{*})^{j}\big(\Id-M(-B_{c})^{*}M(-B_{c})\big)M(-B_{c})^{j}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=4\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(\Id-B_{c}^{*})^{j}(\Id+B_{c}^{*})^{-j-1}B_{H}(\Id+B_{c})^{-j-1}(\Id-B_{c})^{j}.

Multiplying the right-hand side of (
4.5
) by
\frac{1}{2}(\Id+B_{c}^{*})^{m+2}
from the left and by
\frac{1}{2}(\Id+B_{c})^{m+2}
from the right yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(\Id-B_{c}^{*})^{j}(\Id+B_{c}^{*})^{m+1-j}B_{H}(\Id+B_{c})^{m+1-j}(\Id-B_{c})^{j}.

(4.6)

As
\frac{1}{2}(\Id+B_{c}^{*})^{m+2}
is invertible, it suffices to show that (
4.6
) is bounded from below by
\tilde{\kappa}\Id
for some
\tilde{\kappa}>0
in order to deduce that
\eqref{eq:hypocontractivity_expression}\geq\kappa\Id
for some
\kappa>0
. But the former follows immediately by applying Lemma
4.10
, which yields

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(\Id-B_{c}^{*})^{j}(\Id+B_{c}^{*})^{m+1-j}B_{H}(\Id+B_{c})^{m+1-j}(\Id-B_{c})^{j}\geq\sum_{j=0}^{m+1}(-B_{c}^{*})^{j}B_{H}(-B_{c})^{j}

and the right-hand side is bounded from below by
\tilde{\kappa}\Id
for some
\tilde{\kappa}>0
by assumption. On the one hand this shows that the hypocontractivity index of
M(-B_{c})
is at most
m+1
. But on the other hand it cannot be strictly smaller than
m+1
due to the induction hypothesis, which in particular says that the hypocontractivity index of
M(-B_{c})
is smaller than
m+1
if and only if the hypocoercivity index of
B_{c}
is smaller than
m+1
.

The corresponding statement starting with the assumption that the hypocontractivity index of
B_{d}
is
m+1
can be proven analogously.
∎

Remark 4.13
.

The proof in the infinite-dimensional setting differs from the finite-dimensional proof in
[
3
, Theorem 51]
due to the following reasons: First, the closedness in part (ii) of Lemma
4.2
respectively Lemma
4.4
is automatically satisfied in the finite-dimensional case and therefore not needed there. Thus, the proof of
[
3
, Theorem 51]
does not include an argument why the closedness is preserved by the Cayley transform. Moreover, the square root in parts (i) and (ii) of these lemmas can be left out in finite-dimensional settings (see (B1’) and (D1) in
[
3
]
), which allows one to use the relation between
\Id-B_{d}^{*}B_{d}
and
B_{H}
given by Lemma
4.11
. Note that this relation does not carry over to the square root terms because our operators are not assumed to be normal (and hence
A
,
A^{*}
do not commute in general). Therefore, we have rather used Criterion (iii) from Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
4.4
to prove Theorem
4.12
as there is no square root appearing there, and thus we can use Lemma
4.11
.

Next we extend Theorem
4.12
to the scaled Cayley transform. This will be used for the time discretization of continuous-time systems via the implicit midpoint rule.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
B_{c}
, respectively
B_{d}
, be in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and suppose that
\tau>0
is such that
M_{\tau}(-B_{c})
, respectively
M_{\tau}^{-1}(B_{d})
, are well defined in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
.

(i)

If the zero solution of
(
1.1
)
is exponentially stable and
B_{c}
has hypocoercivity index
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then the zero solution of
(
1.2
)
with
B_{d}\coloneqq M_{\tau}(-B_{c})
is exponentially stable and
B_{d}
has hypocontractivity index
m
.

(ii)

If the zero solution of
(
1.2
)
is exponentially stable and
B_{d}
has hypocontractivity index
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, then the zero solution of
(
1.1
)
with
-B_{c}\coloneqq M_{\tau}^{-1}(B_{d})
is exponentially stable and
B_{c}
has hypocoercivity index
m
.

Proof.

We first note that
B_{c}
and
\tau B_{c}
have the same hypocoercivity index. This follows immediately from part (i) of Lemma
4.2
because for any
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
the operators
(B_{c}^{*})^{j}\sqrt{B_{H}}
and
(\tau B_{c}^{*})^{j}\sqrt{\tau B_{H}}
have the same image. Consequently, the first assertion follows immediately from Theorem
4.12
(i) and

\displaystyle m_{dHC}(M_{\tau}(-B_{c}))=m_{dHC}\left(M\left(-\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)\right)=m_{HC}\left(\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)=m_{HC}(B_{c}),

where
m_{HC}
denotes the hypocoercivity and
m_{dHC}
the hypocontractivity index.

For the second assertion one easily verifies that
M\circ M_{\tau}^{-1}=M_{4/\tau}\circ M^{-1}
, and hence

B_{c}:=-M_{\tau}^{-1}(B_{d})=-M^{-1}(M_{4/\tau}(M^{-1}(B_{d}))).

Using, in this order, Theorem
4.12
(ii), Theorem
4.14
(i), and again Theorem
4.12
(ii) we obtain the claim:

\displaystyle m

\displaystyle=m_{dHC}(B_{d})=m_{HC}\big(-M^{-1}(B_{d})\big)=m_{dHC}\big(M_{4/\tau}(M^{-1}(B_{d}))\big)

\displaystyle=m_{HC}\big(-M^{-1}(M_{4/\tau}(M^{-1}(B_{d})))\big).\qed

In this section we have shown that the Cayley transformation maps between the continuous and the discrete setting and leads to an equivalence between the hypocoercivity index and the hypcontractivity index. In the next section we analyze the discretization of the continuous-time solution operator and the discrete approximation of the short-time decay.

5
Hypocontractivity as finite difference approximation of the short-time decay in hypocoercive systems

In this section we show that hypocontractive discrete-time systems exhibit the same short-time behavior we have seen in continuous-time systems: for the step size
\tau\to 0
, the approximation order for the constants in the decay formulas is suprisingly higher than the order of the implicit midpoint rule. While the latter is only second order convergent, the preservation of the index and the first non-vanishing derivative is a structural, asymptotic preservation property far beyond the convergence order.

Consider the implicit midpoint rule with time step
\tau
(the Crank-Nicolson scheme), see e.g.
[
24
]
, for the operator equation
(
1.1
)
at discrete time steps
t_{k}=k\tau
with step size
\tau>0
. With
x_{k}\approx x(t_{k})
this scheme reads

\frac{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}{\tau}=-B_{c}\left(\frac{x_{k+1}+x_{k}}{2}\right)

which after rearrangement gives

x_{k+1}=B_{d}x_{k}

(5.1)

where
B_{d}=(\Id+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c})^{-1}(\Id-\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c})=M_{\tau}(-B_{c})

is the scaled Cayley transform of
-B_{c}
. While
\tau
was arbitrary but fixed in the previous section,
B_{d}=B_{d}(\tau)
will here be considered as a function of
\tau>0
.

As has been established in Section
3
, if the hypocoercivity index of
B_{c}
is
m_{HC}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
then the propagator norm of (
1.1
) satisfies

\Phi(t)\coloneqq\lVert e^{-B_{c}t}\rVert=1-ct^{2m_{HC}+1}+o(t^{2m_{HC}+1})\quad\mbox{for }t\to 0+

(5.2)

with the constant
c
given explicitly by (
3.21
).
In finite dimensional systems, the propagator norm
\Phi(t)
is real analytic on some interval
[0,\delta)
(see
[
23
]
), but for infinite dimensional
\mathcal{H}
this is not true in general:
[
19
]
presents an example, where
\Phi
is not even
C^{1}
on any interval
[0,\delta)
. The short-time decay formula (
5.2
) shows that
\Phi
is
(2m_{HC}+1)
times
Peano differentiable
at
t=0
(this is essentially its definition, see
[
17
]
). Moreover,
\Phi
is (Fréchet) differentiable at
t=0
, but not necessarily continuously differentiable on any interval
[0,\delta)
. An example of an
o(t^{3})
-function that is differentiable at
t=0
but not
C^{1}
on any
[0,\delta)
is given by
f(t):=t^{4}\sin(1/t^{3})
.
The constant
c
in (
5.2
) is related to the Peano derivative of order
2m_{HC}+1
at
t=0
,
\Phi_{(2m_{HC}+1)}(0)
as

c=-\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\lVert e^{-B_{c}t}\rVert-1}{t^{2m_{HC}+1}}=:-\frac{\Phi_{(2m_{HC}+1)}(0)}{(2m_{HC}+1)!}\,.

For finite dimensional systems, the local analyticity of
\Phi
implies that all Peano derivatives equal their classical counterparts (also in Theorem
5.1
below).

Next we introduce a discrete analog of the propagator norm
\Phi(t)
from (
5.2
): For any fixed step size
\tau>0
we define the grid function

\phi(\tau)=\big(\phi_{k}(\tau);\,k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\big)\quad\mbox{with }\phi_{k}(\tau):=\|B_{d}(\tau)^{k}\|.

Note that, for
\tau
small, for any
fixed number of steps

k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\phi_{k}(\tau)
is (at least) a third order approximation for
\Phi(t_{k})
due to the convergence order of the midpoint rule.

Inspired by the odd symmetry about
(0,1)
of the dominant terms in (
5.2
), i.e.
1-ct^{2m_{HC}+1}
, we extend
\phi(\tau)
to negative values of
k
:

\phi_{k}(\tau):=2-\phi_{-k}(\tau),\quad k\in-\mathbb{N}.

This construction is illustrated in Figures
1
and
2
, respectively, for the hypocoercive matrices

B_{c}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\
-1&1\end{bmatrix}\quad\mbox{with }m_{HC}=1,\quad\mbox{and }\quad B_{c}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\
-1&0&1\\
0&-1&1\end{bmatrix}\quad\mbox{with }m_{HC}=2,

and
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
in both cases.

For a matrix
B_{c}
with some
m_{HC}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and the corresponding scaled Caley transform
B_{d}(\tau)
with some fixed
\tau>0
, we consider next the symmetric finite difference of order
2m_{HC}+1
of the discrete function
\phi(\tau)
about the index
k=0
. Since
\|B_{d}^{j}\|=1
for
j=0,...,m_{HC}
(see Theorem
4.5
(ii)), the above mentioned finite difference satisfies

\left[\Delta^{2m_{HC}+1}\phi(\tau)\right]_{k=0}=-\frac{1}{2}\phi_{-m_{HC}-1}(\tau)+\frac{1}{2}\phi_{m_{HC}+1}(\tau)=\phi_{m_{HC}+1}(\tau)-1.

Here we have used that the coefficients in the finite difference of order
2m_{HC}+1
are skew-symmetric about the index
k=0
, the numerical stencil is of length
2m_{HC}+3
, and the first/last coefficients are always
\mp\frac{1}{2}
. E.g., the coefficients for the first derivative are
-\frac{1}{2},\,0,\,\frac{1}{2}
, and for the third derivative
-\frac{1}{2},\,1,\,0,\,-1,\,\frac{1}{2}
, see
[
18
]
.

In this section we prove the following connection between the symmetric central difference of
(2m_{HC}+1)
st order of
\phi(\tau)
and the corresponding Peano derivative of the propagator norm
\Phi
at
t=0
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
-B_{c}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be semi-dissipative, and let
m_{HC}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
be the HC-index of
B_{c}
. Let
B_{d}(\tau):=M_{\tau}(-B_{c}),\,\tau>0
be the corresponding family of scaled Cayley transforms. Then

\frac{\left[\Delta^{2m_{HC}+1}\phi(\tau)\right]_{k=0}}{\tau^{2m_{HC}+1}}=\frac{\phi_{m_{HC}+1}(\tau)-1}{\tau^{2m_{HC}+1}}\>\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau\to 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}\>\Phi_{(2m_{HC}+1)}(0)=-(2m_{HC}+1)!\,c,

(5.3)

with the constant
c
given by (
3.21
).
Moreover,

\left[\Delta^{j}\phi(\tau)\right]_{k=0}=\Phi_{(j)}(0)=\delta_{0}^{j}\quad\mbox{for }j=0,...,2m_{HC}.

(5.4)

Proof.

The proof will be given in Subsection
5.2
.
∎

Theorem
5.1
implies that the norm of the first strictly contractive power of the discrete propagator from (
5.1
),
B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}
, has an asymptotic expansion similar to (
5.2
).

Corollary 5.2
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
5.1
it holds that

\phi_{m_{HC}+1}(\tau)\coloneqq\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}\rVert=1-(2m_{HC}+1)!\,c\tau^{2m_{HC}+1}+o(\tau^{2m_{HC}+1})\quad\mbox{for }\tau\to 0+

(5.5)

with the constant
c
again given by (
3.21
).

Note, however, the additional factor
(2m_{HC}+1)!
in the expansion (
5.5
). This is due to the difference in interpretation: (
5.2
) is an expansion in time, while (
5.5
) is an expansion in the step size.
Due to (
5.5
) and (
5.2
),
\phi_{m_{HC}+1}(\tau)
is an approximation of order
2m_{HC}+1
of

\Phi(t_{m_{HC}+1})=1-(m_{HC}+1)^{2m_{HC}+1}c\tau^{2m_{HC}+1}+o(\tau^{2m_{HC}+1}),

while the midpoint rule would in general only yield third order here.

5.1
An illustrating example

In the following example we illustrate the theoretical results from Corollary
3.9
and Theorem
5.1
with a concrete matrix.

Example 5.3
.

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
, and consider the matrix

\displaystyle B_{c}:=\begin{bmatrix}0&\frac{1}{2}\\
-\frac{1}{2}&1\end{bmatrix},

which has the double eigenvalue
\frac{1}{2}
.
Its Hermitian part

\displaystyle B_{H}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}\geq 0

is not positive definite. But

\displaystyle B_{H}+B_{c}^{*}B_{H}B_{c}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{4}&-\frac{1}{2}\\
-\frac{1}{2}&2\end{bmatrix}

is positive definite because its eigenvalues are strictly positive. Consequently,
B_{c}
is hypocoercive with hypocoercivity index
m_{HC}=1
. In view of Corollary
3.9
we want to compute the asymptotic behavior of
\|e^{-tB_{c}}\|
for
t\to 0+
. To this end, we first note that the matrix exponential is explicitly given by

\displaystyle e^{-tB_{c}}=e^{-\frac{t}{2}}\begin{bmatrix}1+\frac{t}{2}&-\frac{t}{2}\\
\frac{t}{2}&1-\frac{t}{2}\end{bmatrix},

which follows from
[
11
, Corollary 2.3 i)]
. Moreover, the spectral norm of a real valued matrix
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}
is explicitly given by

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{g+\sqrt{g^{2}-4h}}{2}},

(5.6)

where
g=a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}
and
h=(ad-bc)^{2}
, which follows from the fact that the spectral norm is given by the largest singular value. Applying this formula to
e^{\frac{t}{2}}e^{-tB_{c}}
we deduce
g=2+t^{2}
and
h=1
and so

\displaystyle\Phi(t):=\left\|e^{-tB_{c}}\right\|=e^{-\frac{t}{2}}\sqrt{\frac{2+t^{2}+t\sqrt{4+t^{2}}}{2}}.

As this expression is analytic for
t\geq 0
, we can obtain the asymptotic behavior for
t\to 0+
by differentiation which yields

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-tB_{c}}\right\|=1-\frac{1}{48}t^{3}+\mathcal{O}(t^{4})

and
\Phi_{(3)}(0)=\Phi^{(3)}(0)=-\frac{1}{8}
.
As

\displaystyle\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\ker(\sqrt{B_{H}})\\
\|y\|=1\end{subarray}}\left\|\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}y\right\|^{2}=\left\langle B_{c}^{*}B_{H}B_{c}\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{4},

we obtain exactly the constant given in Corollary
3.9
.

Next, we consider the corresponding operator for the discrete-time system with time step
\tau>0
:

\displaystyle B_{d}(\tau)=M_{\tau}(-B_{c})=\frac{1}{(4+\tau)^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}16+8\tau-\tau^{2}&-8\tau\\
8\tau&16-8\tau-\tau^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

Again, we use (
5.6
) to deduce the spectral norm of
(4+\tau)^{2}B_{d}(\tau)
. In this case,

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=2(16-\tau^{2})^{2}+256\tau^{2},

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=(16-\tau^{2})^{4},

and furthermore

\displaystyle\sqrt{g^{2}-4h}

\displaystyle=32\tau(\tau^{2}+16),

\displaystyle g+\sqrt{g^{2}-4h}

\displaystyle=2(\tau+4)^{4}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\left\|\begin{bmatrix}16+8\tau-\tau^{2}&-8\tau\\
8\tau&16-8\tau-\tau^{2}\end{bmatrix}\right\|=(4+\tau)^{2}

and thus

\displaystyle\|B_{d}(\tau)\|=1

for all
\tau>0
, as was expected by Theorem
4.12
(i) and Theorem
4.5
(ii). Next, we consider

\displaystyle B_{d}(\tau)^{2}=\frac{1}{(4+\tau)^{4}}\begin{bmatrix}(\tau^{2}-8\tau-16)^{2}-64\tau^{2}&16\tau(\tau^{2}-16)\\
-16\tau(\tau^{2}-16)&(\tau^{2}+8\tau-16)^{2}-64\tau^{2}\end{bmatrix}.

As before, we use (
5.6
) to deduce the spectral norm of
(4+\tau)^{4}B_{d}(\tau)^{2}
: We have

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle=2(\tau^{2}-16)^{2}(\tau^{4}+480\tau^{2}+256),

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=(\tau-4)^{8}(\tau+4)^{8},

and furthermore

\displaystyle g^{2}-4h

\displaystyle=4096\tau^{2}(\tau^{2}-16)^{4}(\tau^{4}+224\tau^{2}+256),

\displaystyle\frac{g+\sqrt{g^{2}-4h}}{2}

\displaystyle=(\tau-4)^{2}(\tau+4)^{2}\left(\tau^{4}+480\tau^{2}+256+32\tau\sqrt{\tau^{4}+224\tau^{2}+256}\right).

Thus,

\displaystyle\phi_{2}(\tau):=\left\|B_{d}(\tau)^{2}\right\|=\frac{|4-\tau|}{(\tau+4)^{3}}\sqrt{\tau^{4}+480\tau^{2}+256+32\tau\sqrt{\tau^{4}+224\tau^{2}+256}}.

(5.7)

Note that for
\tau=4
, the matrix
B_{d}
is singular and
B_{d}^{2}
is the zero matrix (which is connected to
\Lambda(B_{c})=\{\frac{1}{2}\}
and (
4.2
)). Then (
5.7
) is analytic for
0\leq\tau<4
, and we can obtain the asymptotic behavior for
\tau\to 0+
by differentiation which yields

\displaystyle\left\|B_{d}(\tau)^{2}\right\|=1-\frac{1}{8}\tau^{3}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{4}).

Hence,

\frac{\phi_{2}(\tau)-1}{\tau^{3}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau\to 0}}{{\longrightarrow}}\Phi_{(3)}(0)=-\frac{1}{8},

which is exactly the asymptotic expansion predicted by Theorem
5.1
.

5.2
Proof of Theorem
5.1

In this subsection we prove Theorem
5.1
. The equality (
5.4
) follows trivially from Theorem
4.5
(ii) and (
5.2
). Hence we prove here (
5.5
) which is equivalent to (
5.3
).

The proofs follow those of Section
3
, the main difference being that instead of
e^{-B_{c}^{*}t}e^{-B_{c}t}
, which has been expanded as the Cauchy product of two series, we have to expand
(B_{d}^{*})^{m_{HC}+1}B_{d}^{m_{HC}+1}
here, which slightly complicates things.

In the following, we will tacitly assume that
\tau
is small enough so that
B_{d}(\tau)
is well-defined; e.g.,
\tau<2/\lVert B_{c}\rVert
is sufficient for this. In this case,
I\pm\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}
and
(I\mp\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c})^{-1}
commute, see Remark
4.9
. Moreover, in the following lemmata we formulate the assumptions mostly in terms of
B_{c}
and not for
B_{d}(\tau)
, since the former is just one single operator and the proofs often involve
B_{c}
, anyhow.

Lemma 5.4
.

For any
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

(B_{d}^{*}(\tau))^{m+1}B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}=I-2\tau\sum_{j=0}^{m}(B_{d}^{*}(\tau))^{j}\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-*}B_{H}\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-1}B_{d}(\tau)^{j}.

(5.8)

Proof.

We show the claim by induction. For
m=0
we have

\displaystyle B^{*}_{d}B_{d}

\displaystyle=\left(I-\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{*}\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-*}\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-1}\left(I-\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-*}\left(I-\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{*}\left(I-\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=I+\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-*}\left[\left(I-\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{*}\left(I-\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)-\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{*}\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)\right]\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=I+\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-*}\left(-\frac{\tau}{2}\left(B_{c}^{*}+B_{c}\right)\cdot 2\right)\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=I-2\tau\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-*}B_{H}\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-1}.

Suppose the claim holds for some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then we have

\displaystyle(B_{d}^{*})^{m+2}B_{d}^{m+2}

\displaystyle=B_{d}^{*}\left(I-2\tau\sum_{j=0}^{m}(B_{d}^{*})^{j}\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-*}B_{H}\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-1}B_{d}^{j}\right)B_{d}

\displaystyle=I-2\tau\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-*}B_{H}\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\qquad-2\tau\sum_{j=1}^{m+1}(B_{d}^{*})^{j}\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-*}B_{H}\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-1}B_{d}^{j}

which is the statement for
m+1
.
∎

Below we will need the series expansions

\displaystyle\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i}\tau^{i}B_{c}^{i},

\displaystyle\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-*}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i}\tau^{i}(B_{c}^{*})^{i},

\displaystyle B_{d}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\left(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)^{-1}\left(I-\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i}\tau^{i}B_{c}^{i}+\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i+1}\tau^{i+1}B_{c}^{i+1}

\displaystyle=I-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i-1}\tau^{i}B_{c}^{i}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}\tau^{i}B_{c}^{i}

with

a_{i}:=\begin{cases}1&i=0,\\
2\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i}&i\geq 1.\end{cases}

(5.9)

In the following, for any given
j\in\mathbb{N}
we abbreviate
p=(p_{1},\dotsc,p_{j})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{j}
,
\lvert p\rvert=p_{1}+\dotsc+p_{j}
and
a_{p}=a_{p_{1}}\dotsm a_{p_{j}}
for
a_{i}
given in (
5.9
), and similarly for
q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{j}
. For
j=0
we set
\lvert p\rvert=0
and
a_{p}=1
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then

\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{C}\\
\lambda_{0}=1\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert q\rvert+l=0}^{m}\lambda_{m-\lvert q\rvert-l}\overline{\lambda_{m-\lvert p\rvert-i}}a_{p}q_{q}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i+l}=\binom{2m}{m}^{-1},

(5.10)

and the minimum is attained at a unique
\lambda^{*}=(\lambda_{1}^{*},...,\lambda_{m}^{*})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

Remark 5.6
.

Note that for
m=0
the infimum is omitted in (
5.10
).
We also remark that the second sum in (
5.10
) is actually a
(j+1)
-fold summation, over the
j
components of the vector
p
and the scalar index
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. So the sum over the indices
\lvert p\rvert+i=0
to
m
equals the sum
p_{1}+\dotsc+p_{j}+i=0
to
m
.
The same holds for the third sum.

These sums arise from the Cauchy product of the series representation of the operators in (
5.8
), namely
j
factors of
B_{d}^{*}
, the factor
(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c})^{-*}
, the factor
(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c})^{-1}
and
j
factors of
B_{d}
, whose contribution to the coefficients in the
(2j+2)
-fold Cauchy product are enumerated by the indices
p_{1},\dotsc,p_{j}
,
i
,
l
, and
q_{1},\dotsc,q_{j}
, respectively.

We have the following proof of Lemma
5.5
:

Proof.

Since the claim is trivial for
m=0
, we assume
m\geq 1
in the sequel.
We first note that the expression to be minimized equals
\lVert T\rVert^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\lvert T_{j}\rvert^{2}
with

T_{j}=T_{j}(\lambda):=\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{m}\lambda_{m-\lvert p\rvert-i}a_{p}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i},\quad j=0,\dotsc,m,

(5.11)

where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{m})\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
and
\lambda_{0}=1
.
The generating functions of the sequences
(a_{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and
((-1/2)^{i})_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are

F(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}z^{i}=\frac{1-z/2}{1+z/2}\quad\text{and}\quad G(z)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i}z^{i}=\frac{1}{1+z/2}\qquad(z\in\mathbb{C},\,|z|<2),

respectively. With

\displaystyle W^{(j)}(z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq F(z)^{j}G(z)=\frac{\left(1-z/2\right)^{j}}{\left(1+z/2\right)^{j+1}}=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}w_{r}^{(j)}z^{r},

(5.12)

\displaystyle w_{r}^{(j)}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=r}a_{p}\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\Big)^{i},\quad j=0,\dotsc,m,

(5.13)

and
L(z)\coloneqq\sum_{k=0}^{m}\lambda_{k}z^{k}

we see that
T_{j}=[z^{m}]\big(L(z)W^{(j)}(z)\big)
, where
[z^{m}]f(z)
denotes the coefficient of
z^{m}
in the Taylor series of
f(z)
. For every fixed
j\in\{0,...,m\}
we have:

\displaystyle[z^{m}]\left(L(z)W^{(j)}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=[z^{m}]\sum_{k=0}^{m}\lambda_{k}z^{k}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}a_{i}z^{i}\right)^{j}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i}z^{i}

\displaystyle=[z^{m}]\sum_{k=0}^{m}\lambda_{k}z^{k}\sum_{p_{1}=0}^{\infty}a_{p_{1}}z^{p_{1}}\dotsm\sum_{p_{j}=0}^{\infty}a_{p_{j}}z^{p_{j}}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i}z^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k+p_{1}+\dotsc+p_{j}+i=m}\lambda_{k}a_{p_{1}}\dotsm a_{p_{j}}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i}=\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{m}\lambda_{m-\lvert p\rvert-i}a_{p}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i}=T_{j}.

With
\alpha_{j}\coloneqq(-1)^{j}\binom{m}{j}
for
j=0,\dotsc,m
it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m}\alpha_{j}W^{(j)}(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+z/2}\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^{j}\binom{m}{j}\left(\frac{1-z/2}{1+z/2}\right)^{j}

(5.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+z/2}\left(1-\frac{1-z/2}{1+z/2}\right)^{m}=\frac{z^{m}}{(1+z/2)^{m+1}}

\displaystyle=z^{m}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\binom{-m-1}{i}\left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{i}=z^{m}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i}\binom{m+i}{i}z^{i}

\displaystyle=z^{m}\left(1-\frac{z}{2}+\frac{z^{2}}{4}-\dotsc\right)^{m+1}.

This gives

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m}\alpha_{j}T_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\alpha_{j}[z^{m}](L(z)W^{(j)}(z))=[z^{m}]\sum_{j=0}^{m}\alpha_{j}L(z)W^{(j)}(z)

(5.15)

\displaystyle=[z^{m}]\left(L(z)\frac{z^{m}}{(1+z/2)^{m+1}}\right)=\lambda_{0}=1.

By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\lVert T\rVert^{2}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\lvert T_{j}\rvert^{2}\geq\frac{\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}\alpha_{j}T_{j}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{j=0}^{m}\alpha_{j}^{2}}=\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}\binom{m}{j}^{2}\right)^{-1}=\binom{2m}{m}^{-1},

(5.16)

and the r.h.s. is the claimed infimum from (
5.10
). So it remains to show that this infimum is attained.

Equality in (
5.16
) holds if
(T_{0},\dotsc,T_{m})
is proportional to
(\alpha_{0},\dotsc,\alpha_{m})
.
This proportionality can be attained for a suitable choice of
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
.
To this end we rewrite (
5.11
) as

T_{j}=\sum_{r=0}^{m}\left(\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=r}a_{p}\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\Big)^{i}\right)\lambda_{m-r}=\sum_{r=0}^{m}w_{r}^{(j)}\lambda_{m-r}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}Q_{j,k}\lambda_{k}+b_{j},

with
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{j}
, the matrix
Q_{j,k}:=w_{m-k}^{(j)}
, and the vector
b_{j}:=w_{m}^{(j)}
for
j=0,\dotsc,m
and
k=1,\dotsc,m
. The quadratic form in
\lambda
,

\lVert T\rVert^{2}=\lVert Q\lambda+b\rVert^{2}

has a unique minimizer
\lambda^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
because the columns of the matrix
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1,m}
are linearly independent.

To see this, suppose that
Q\beta=0
for some
\beta=(\beta_{1},\dotsc,\beta_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, i.e.,

\sum_{r=0}^{m-1}\beta_{m-r}w_{r}^{(j)}=0\quad\mbox{for }j=0,\dotsc,m.

If we show that
q_{r}(j):=w_{r}^{(j)}
is a polynomial in
j
of degree
r
(defined via (
5.13
) at the grid points
j=0,...,m
),
\beta=0
will follow because the
q_{r}
then are linearly independent polynomials.
The polynomial structure of
q_{r}(j)
is seen from the following combinatorial argument: For each fixed
r\in\{0,...,m-1\}
, the highest number of identical contributions in the sum of (
5.13
) is obtained when
i=0
and if the vector
p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{j}
has exactly
r
times the entry
1
and
j-r
times the entry
0
. There are

\binom{j}{r}=\frac{j(j-1)...(j-r-1)}{r!},

(i.e. a polynomial of degree
r
in
j
) terms of this sort, with the value
a_{1}^{r}a_{0}^{j-r}(-\frac{1}{2})^{0}
. All other terms in (
5.13
) occur in a lower power of
j
.
Hence,
Q
indeed has full column rank.

At the minimum of
\lVert Q\lambda+b\rVert^{2}
the gradient
2Q^{T}(Q\lambda^{*}+b)
vanishes, which means that
T^{*}:=Q\lambda^{*}+b\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
is in the one-dimensional subspace orthogonal to the columns of
Q
. From (
5.14
) and (
5.12
) we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m}\alpha_{j}w^{(j)}_{r}

\displaystyle=0\qquad\text{for }0\leq r\leq m-1,

(5.17)

which means that
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
is orthogonal to the columns of
Q
and hence proportional to
T^{*}
.
Hence
T^{*}
makes (
5.16
) an equality, and the corresponding
\lambda^{*}
minimizes (
5.10
).
∎

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and let
y\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}\to\mathcal{H}
satisfy

on some interval
[0,t_{0})

\lVert y(\tau)\rVert=\mathcal{O}(1)

\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(\tau)\rVert=o(\tau^{m-1-n)})

(5.18)

\tau\to 0+

.(For

\left\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\frac{y(\tau)}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert}\right\rVert^{2}=1-\frac{2\tau}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert q\rvert+l=0}^{m}\tau^{\lvert p\rvert+i+\lvert q\rvert+l}a_{p}a_{q}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i+l}\\
\cdot\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l}y(\tau),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert p\rvert+i}y(\tau)\right\rangle+o(\tau^{2m+1})

(5.19)

for
\tau\to 0+
.
Just as in Lemma
5.5
, the second sum above is actually a
(j+1)
-fold summation, over the

p
and the scalar index
i\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

Proof.

We use Lemma
5.4
and the series expansions mentioned thereafter to write

\left\langle(B_{d}^{*}(\tau))^{m+1}B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\frac{y(\tau)}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert},\frac{y(\tau)}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert}\right\rangle

(5.20)

as the Cauchy product of the respective series, resulting in

\displaystyle\eqref{BByy}=1-\frac{2\tau}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{r=0}^{2m}\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i+l+\lvert q\rvert=r}a_{p}a_{q}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i+l}\left\langle(B_{c}^{*})^{\lvert p\rvert+i}B_{H}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l}y(\tau),y(\tau)\right\rangle\tau^{r}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2m+2})

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2\tau}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{r=0}^{2m}\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i+l+\lvert q\rvert=r}a_{p}a_{q}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i+l}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l}y(\tau),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert p\rvert+i}y(\tau)\right\rangle\tau^{r}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2m+2}).

In the above sum, consider now for every

. Hence, (

1-\frac{2\tau}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert q\rvert+l=0}^{m}\tau^{\lvert p\rvert+i+\lvert q\rvert+l}a_{p}a_{q}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i+l}\\
\cdot\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l}y(\tau),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert p\rvert+i}y(\tau)\right\rangle+o(\tau^{2m+1}).

∎

Theorem 5.8
.

Let
-B_{c}\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
be semi-dissipative, and let
B_{H}
be the Hermitian part of
B_{c}
.
Suppose that, for some
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the coercivity condition

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}(B_{c}^{*})^{j}B_{H}B_{c}^{j}\geq\kappa I

(5.22)

is
violated
for all
\kappa>0
. Then

\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\rVert^{2}\geq 1-\tau^{2m+1}\frac{2}{\binom{2m}{m}}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\rVert\leq\delta,\\
0\leq p<m,\\
\lVert y\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\rVert^{2}+o(\tau^{2m+1})

for
\tau\to 0+
.

We recall from Theorem
3.3
that, for
m=0
the infimum is just taken over
\mathbb{S}
.

Proof.

As in Theorem
3.3
we set

S_{\tau}\coloneqq\{y\in\mathbb{S}:\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\rVert\leq\tau^{m+1-p},\ 0\leq p<m\}

and choose for each
\tau>0
some
y_{0}(\tau)\in S_{\tau}
with

\left|\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{0}(\tau)\rVert-\inf_{y\in S_{\tau}}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\rVert\right|<\tau,

resulting in

\displaystyle\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y_{0}(\tau)\rVert

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{m+1-p})

\displaystyle(0\leq p<m)\,,

(5.23)

\displaystyle\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y_{0}(\tau)\rVert

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{m-p})

\displaystyle(p\in\mathbb{N}_{0}).

(5.24)

From (
3.10
) we recall the identity

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B^{m}_{c}y_{0}(\tau)\rVert=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\inf_{y\in S_{\tau}}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\rVert=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\rVert\leq\delta,\\
0\leq p<m,\\
\lVert y\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\rVert.

We now use the ansatz

y(\tau)\coloneqq y_{0}(\tau)+\sum_{s=1}^{m}\lambda_{s}(\tau B_{c})^{s}y_{0}(\tau)=\sum_{s=0}^{m}\lambda_{s}(\tau B_{c})^{s}y_{0}(\tau),\quad\tau\geq 0,

where
\lambda_{0}=1
, and
\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{R}
are to be chosen later. Equations (
5.23
) and (
5.24
) translate into

\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}(\tau B_{c})^{s}y_{0}(\tau)\rVert=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{m+1-n})

(5.25)

for
0\leq s,n
with
s+n<m
; and

\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}(\tau B_{c})^{s}y_{0}(\tau)\rVert=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{m-n})

(5.26)

for any
s,n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Hence,

satisfies all conditions of Lemma
5.7
and we obtain

\displaystyle\left\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\frac{y(\tau)}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert}\right\rVert

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2\tau}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert q\rvert+l=0}^{m}\tau^{\lvert p\rvert+i+\lvert q\rvert+l}a_{p}a_{q}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i+l}

\displaystyle\cdot\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l}y(\tau),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert p\rvert+i}y(\tau)\right\rangle+o(\tau^{2m+1}).

(5.27)

To expand the inner product

\sum_{r=0}^{m}\sum_{s=0}^{m}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l}(\tau B_{c})^{r}y_{0}(\tau),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert p\rvert+i}(\tau B_{c})^{s}y_{0}(\tau)\right\rangle

we distinguish the following cases:

r+\lvert q\rvert+l<m
:
The above estimates (
5.25
), (
5.26
) in the form

\displaystyle\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l}(\tau B_{c})^{r}y_{0}(\tau)\rVert

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{m+1-\lvert q\rvert-l})

\displaystyle\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert p\rvert+i}(\tau B_{c})^{s}y_{0}(\tau)\rVert

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{m-\lvert p\rvert-i})

yield

\tau^{1+\lvert p\rvert+i+\lvert q\rvert+l}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l}(\tau B_{c})^{r}y_{0}(\tau),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert p\rvert+i}(\tau B_{c})^{s}y_{0}(\tau)\right\rangle=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2m+2}).

s+\lvert p\rvert+i<m
:
The same estimate holds.

r+\lvert q\rvert+l\geq m+1
and
s+\lvert p\rvert+i\geq m
or
r+\lvert q\rvert+l\geq m
and
s+\lvert p\rvert+i\geq m+1
:

\tau^{1+\lvert p\rvert+i+\lvert q\rvert+l}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l}(\tau B_{c})^{r}y_{0}(\tau),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert p\rvert+i}(\tau B_{c})^{s}y_{0}(\tau)\right\rangle\\
=\tau^{1+s+\lvert p\rvert+i+r+\lvert q\rvert+l}\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l+r}y_{0}(\tau),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert p\rvert+i+s}y_{0}(\tau)\right\rangle=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2m+2})

using
\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{r+\lvert q\rvert+l}y(\tau)\rVert=\mathcal{O}(1)
and
\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{s+\lvert p\rvert+i}y(\tau)\rVert=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

Hence, we only need to retain the terms with
s+\lvert p\rvert+i=r+\lvert q\rvert+l=m
in the estimate of (
5.27
), and it simplifies as follows:

\displaystyle\left\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\frac{y(\tau)}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2\tau}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert^{2}}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert q\rvert+l=0}^{m}\sum_{r=0}^{m}\sum_{s=0}^{m}\tau^{\lvert p\rvert+i+\lvert q\rvert+l+r+s}\lambda_{r}{\lambda_{s}}a_{p}a_{q}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i+l}

\displaystyle\cdot\left\langle\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l+r}y_{0}(\tau),\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert p\rvert+i+s}y_{0}(\tau)\right\rangle+o(\tau^{2m+1})

\displaystyle=1-2\tau^{2m+1}\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert q\rvert+l=0}^{m}\lambda_{m-\lvert q\rvert-l}{\lambda_{m-\lvert p\rvert-i}}a_{p}a_{q}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i+l}

\displaystyle\cdot\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{0}(\tau)\rVert^{2}+o(\tau^{2m+1}),

where we used
\lVert y(\tau)\rVert^{-2}=1+\mathcal{O}(\tau)
.
Choose
\lambda_{1},\dotsc,\lambda_{m}
according to Lemma
5.5
such that the expression

\sum_{j=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{m}\sum_{\lvert q\rvert+l=0}^{m}\lambda_{m-\lvert q\rvert-l}\lambda_{m-\lvert p\rvert-i}a_{p}a_{q}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{i+l}

is minimal and thus equals

. Then,

\left\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\frac{y(\tau)}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert}\right\rVert^{2}=1-\tau^{2m+1}\frac{2}{\binom{2m}{m}}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y_{0}(\tau)\rVert^{2}+o(\tau^{2m+1}),

and hence using (

\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\rVert^{2}\geq\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\frac{y(\tau)}{\lVert y(\tau)\rVert}\rVert^{2}=1-\tau^{2m+1}\frac{2}{\binom{2m}{m}}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\rVert\leq\delta,\\
0\leq p<m,\\
\lVert y\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m}y\rVert^{2}+o(\tau^{2m+1}).\qed

.

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then, for every
d=0,\dotsc,k

it holds

C^{(k)}_{d}\coloneqq\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathcal{H}^{k+1}\\
z_{d}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{k}\sum_{\lvert q\rvert+l=0}^{k}a_{p}a_{q}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i+l}\left\langle z_{|q|+l},z_{|p|+i}\right\rangle\lVert z_{d}\rVert^{-2}>0,

(5.28)

where
z=(z_{0},\dotsc,z_{k})\in\mathcal{H}^{k+1}
and
p,\,q\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{j}
.
Moreover, we have
C_{k}^{(k)}=\binom{2k}{k}^{-1}
.

Proof.

First, we abbreviate for each
j=0,\dotsc,k
and
z=(z_{0},\dotsc,z_{k})\in\mathcal{H}^{k+1}

M_{j}(z):=\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{k}\sum_{\lvert q\rvert+l=0}^{k}a_{p}a_{q}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i+l}\left\langle z_{|q|+l},z_{|p|+i}\right\rangle=\lVert\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{k}a_{p}(-\tfrac{1}{2})^{i}z_{\lvert p\rvert+i}\rVert^{2},

which shows that the expression on the left-hand side of (
5.28
) is real and nonnegative. Note that, for any unit vector
e\in\mathcal{H}
and
z_{r}^{\parallel}\coloneqq\langle z_{r},e\rangle e
,
r=0,\dotsc,k
, the inequality

\sum_{j=0}^{k}M_{j}(z_{0},\dotsc,z_{k})\geq\sum_{j=0}^{k}M_{j}(z_{0}^{\parallel},\dotsc,z_{k}^{\parallel})

holds as a general property of orthogonal projections, here onto
\sspan[e]
. Hence, the infimum of the left-hand side of (
5.28
) w.r.t.
z\in\mathcal{H}^{k+1}
with
z_{d}\neq 0
equals the infimum over all collinear vectors
z_{0},\dotsc,z_{k}
with
z_{d}\neq 0
. Fix some
d\in\{0,\dots,k\}
, suppose that
z_{d}\neq 0
, and set
e:=z_{d}/\lVert z_{d}\rVert
, which implies that
\lVert z_{d}^{\parallel}\rVert=\lVert z_{d}\rVert
. Then we can restrict the minimization in (
5.28
) to the scalar case
z_{r}:=\mu_{r}e
with
\mu_{r}\in\mathbb{C}
,
r=0,...,k
and write

\sum_{j=0}^{k}M_{j}(z_{0},\dotsc,z_{k})=\sum_{j=0}^{k}\left|\sum_{r=0}^{k}w_{r}^{(j)}\mu_{r}\right|^{2}=Q(\mu),

where
w^{(j)}_{r}
was given in (
5.13
),
\mu=(\mu_{0},\dotsc,\mu_{k})^{T}
, and
Q
is the quadratic form given by
Q(\mu)=\lVert W\mu\rVert^{2}
with the matrix
W=(w^{(j)}_{r})_{j,r=0,\dotsc,k}
. Because the entries of
W
are obtained by evaluating the
k+1
linearly independent polynomials
q_{r}(j)=w^{(j)}_{r}
,
r=0,\dots,k
(as functions of
j
) at the
k+1
different points
j=0,\dots,k
(see the proof of Lemma
5.5
),
W
is invertible. Hence
Q
is a positive definite quadratic form. It follows that

\sum_{j=0}^{k}M_{j}(z)\lVert z_{d}\rVert^{-2}=Q(\mu)\lvert\mu_{d}\rvert^{-2}

and we obtain

C^{(k)}_{d}=\inf_{\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{k+1},\lvert\mu_{d}\rvert=1}Q(\mu)\geq\lambda_{\min}(W^{*}W)>0,

(5.29)

where
\lambda_{\min}(W^{*}W)
denotes the smallest eigenvalue of
W^{*}W
. This shows that all
C^{(k)}_{d}
are positive.

We recall the definition of
T_{j}
from (
5.11
), with
m
replaced now by
k
:

T_{j}(\lambda):=\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{k}\lambda_{k-\lvert p\rvert-i}a_{p}\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{i},\quad j=0,\dotsc,k,

where
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
and
\lambda_{0}=1
. At
\lambda=0
this gives with (
5.13
)
T_{j}(0)=\sum_{|p|+i=k}a_{p}(-\tfrac{1}{2})^{i}=w_{k}^{(j)};\>j=0,\dotsc,k
.
Then, for
d=k
and
\alpha_{j}:=(-1)^{j}\binom{k}{j}
,
j=0,\dots,k
as in the proof of Lemma
5.5
we know from (
5.17
) and (
5.15
) that
W^{T}\alpha=e_{k}
(the
k
-th column of the identity matrix on
\mathbb{C}^{k+1}
when starting to count with column 0). Hence

\lvert\mu_{k}\rvert^{2}=\lvert\alpha^{T}W\mu\rvert^{2}\leq\lVert\alpha\rVert^{2}Q(\mu)\implies Q(\mu)\geq\frac{\lvert\mu_{k}\rvert^{2}}{\lVert\alpha\rVert^{2}}\quad\forall\mu\in\mathbb{C}^{k+1}.

(5.30)

Setting
\tilde{\mu}:=W^{-1}\alpha
we have
\lvert\tilde{\mu}_{k}\rvert^{2}=\lVert\alpha\rVert^{4}
and hence
Q(\tilde{\mu})=\lVert\alpha\rVert^{2}=\lvert\tilde{\mu}_{k}\rvert^{2}/\lVert\alpha\rVert^{2}
. Thus the lower bound from (
5.30
) is attained. Scaling
\tilde{\mu}
by
\tilde{\mu}_{k}
, (
5.29
) yields
C^{(k)}_{k}=\lVert\alpha\rVert^{-2}=\binom{2k}{k}^{-1}
, where we have used (
5.16
).
∎

Lemma 5.11
.

If
B_{c}
has hypocoercivity index greater than or equal to
m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
y\colon\mathbb{R}^{+}_{0}\to\mathcal{H}
satisfies
\lVert y(\tau)\rVert=1
for
\tau\geq 0
and
\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}y(\tau)\rVert-\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\rVert=o(\tau^{2m+1})
for
\tau\to 0+
. Then
\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y(\tau)\rVert=o(\tau^{m-1-p})
for
0\leq p<m
.

Proof.

We will show inductively for
k=0,1,\dotsc,m
that

\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(\tau)\rVert=o(\tau^{k-1-n})\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq n<k.

For
k=0
there is nothing to show. For the step from
k<m
to
k+1
we use
\lVert B_{d}(\tau)\rVert\leq 1
, Lemma
5.7
(with
m
there replaced by
k
here), and Lemma
5.10
to obtain

\displaystyle\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}y(\tau)\rVert^{2}\leq\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{k+1}y(\tau)\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=1-2\tau\sum_{j=0}^{k}\sum_{\lvert p\rvert+i=0}^{k}\sum_{\lvert q\rvert+l=0}^{k}a_{p}a_{q}\Big(-\frac{1}{2}\Big)^{i+l}\left\langle\tau^{\lvert q\rvert+l}\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert q\rvert+l}y(\tau),\tau^{\lvert p\rvert+i}\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{\lvert p\rvert+i}y(\tau)\right\rangle+o(\tau^{2k+1})

\displaystyle\quad\leq 1-2\tau C^{(k)}_{n}\lVert\tau^{n}\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(\tau)\rVert^{2}+o(\tau^{2k+1})\quad\forall n=0,\dots,k.

This implies that

\displaystyle 2\tau^{2n+1}C^{(k)}_{n}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(\tau)\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1-\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}y(\tau)\rVert^{2}+o(\tau^{2k+1})

\displaystyle=1-\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\rVert^{2}-\left(\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}y(\tau)\rVert^{2}-\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\rVert^{2}\right)+o(\tau^{2k+1})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2m+1})+o(\tau^{2m+1})+o(\tau^{2k+1})=o(\tau^{2k+1})

by Remark
5.9
. This yields

\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(\tau)\rVert=o(\tau^{(k+1)-1-n})\quad\text{for}\quad 0\leq n<k+1,

as desired.
∎

The following theorem yields the optimal upper bound for the asymptotic expansion of
\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m+1}\rVert
, the norm of the scaled Cayley transform, as a function of the step size and in the limit
\tau\to 0
. Thus it is the discrete analogue of Theorem
3.7
.

Theorem 5.12
.

Let
B_{c}
have hypocoercivity index
m_{HC}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then

\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}\rVert^{2}\leq 1-\tau^{2m_{HC}+1}\frac{2}{\binom{2m_{HC}}{m_{HC}}}\lim_{\delta\to 0}\inf_{\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\rVert\leq\delta,0\leq p<m_{HC},\lVert y\rVert=1}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m_{HC}}y\rVert^{2}+o(\tau^{m_{HC}+1})

for
\tau\to 0+
.

Recall from Theorem
5.8
that, for
m_{HC}=0
the infimum is just taken over
\mathbb{S}
.

Proof.

Choose
y(\tau)\in\mathcal{H}
for
\tau\geq 0
with
\lVert y(\tau)\rVert=1
such that
\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}y(\tau)\rVert-\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}\rVert=o(\tau^{2m_{HC}+1})
. Lemma
5.11
then implies that

\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{n}y(\tau)\rVert=o(\tau^{m_{HC}-1-n})\quad\mbox{ for }0\leq n<m_{HC}.

(5.31)

Applying Lemma
5.7
and Lemma
5.10
(with
k
and
d
there, replaced by
m_{HC}
here) we obtain

\displaystyle\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}y(\tau)\rVert^{2}\leq 1-2\tau\binom{2m_{HC}}{m_{HC}}^{-1}\tau^{2m_{HC}}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m_{HC}}y(\tau)\rVert^{2}+g(\tau),

with
g(\tau)=o(\tau^{2m_{HC}+1})
.
Now, for any
\delta>0
(due to (
5.31
)) we can find
T_{\delta}>0
such that for any
0\leq\tau\leq T_{\delta}
it holds that:

\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}y(\tau)\rVert^{2}\leq 1-c_{\delta}\,\tau^{2m_{HC}+1}+g(\tau),

with

c_{\delta}:=2\binom{2m_{HC}}{m_{HC}}^{-1}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{p}y\rVert\leq\delta,0\leq p<m_{HC},\\
\lVert y\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\lVert\sqrt{B_{H}}B_{c}^{m_{HC}}y\rVert^{2}.

Note that
y(\tau)\big|_{[0,T_{\delta}]}
is inside the set in which we minimize. Moreover, the function
g
is independent of
\delta
,
c_{\delta}
increases as
\delta\to 0
, but
T_{\delta}
(typically) decreases to 0 as
\delta\to 0
.

Using the fact that
\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}y(\tau)\rVert-\lVert B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}\rVert=o(\tau^{2m_{HC}+1})
, for every fixed
\delta>0
we obtain that:

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\|B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}\|^{2}-1}{\tau^{2m_{HC}+1}}=\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\|B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}y(\tau)\|^{2}-1}{\tau^{2m_{HC}+1}}\leq-c_{\delta}.

Hence

\lim_{\tau\to 0}\frac{\|B_{d}(\tau)^{m_{HC}+1}\|^{2}-1}{\tau^{2m_{HC}+1}}\leq-\lim_{\delta\to 0}c_{\delta},

which is a reformulation of the claim.
∎

The lower bound from Theorem
5.8
and the upper bound from Theorem
5.12
now imply the asymptotic expansion (
5.5
) and hence Theorem
5.1
.

6
Maximally coercive/contractive representations

In this section we study maximally coercive/contractive representations of continuous-time linear systems of the form (
1.1
) as (semi-)dissipative systems and discrete-time linear systems of the form (
1.2
)
as
(semi-)contractive systems
.

6.1
Continuous-time Lyapunov transformations

Even if the original system (
1.1
) is
(stable) asymptotically stable
, i.e., if all eigenvalues have (nonpositive) negative real part, then the system is typically not (semi-dissipative) dissipative. However, in this case one can, always in the matrix case and under some extra assumption in the operator case, perform a
Lyapunov transformation
that makes the system semi-dissipative, see e.g.
[
8
,
22
]
.

Consider
B_{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{d,d}
, and determine a positive definite solution
X=X^{*}>0
of the
Lyapunov inequality

B_{c}^{*}X+XB_{c}\,(\geq)>0.

(6.1)

If such a solution exists, then
V(x):=\frac{1}{2}\Re(x^{*}Xx)
is a Lyapunov function, and this implies that the solution
x
is (stable) asymptotically stable, see e.g.
[
22
, §3.3.4]
.
The solution
X=X^{*}>0
then defines a norm

\|x\|_{X}:=(x^{*}Xx)^{\frac{1}{2}}

(6.2)

so that in this norm the solution
x(t)
is (monotonically)
strictly monotonically decreasing in time.
Alternatively, we can multiply (
1.1
) with the positive definite square root
X^{\frac{1}{2}}
and
after a change of basis,
y=X^{\frac{1}{2}}x

[
3
,
10
]
we obtain the ODE

\dot{y}=-\Big(X^{\frac{1}{2}}B_{c}X^{-\frac{1}{2}}\Big)y=:-(\widetilde{B_{H}}-\widetilde{B_{S}})y,

(6.3)

where the (negative) right-hand side has Hermitian part

\widetilde{B_{H}}:=\frac{1}{2}(X^{\frac{1}{2}}B_{c}X^{-\frac{1}{2}}+(X^{\frac{1}{2}}B_{c}X^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*})=\frac{1}{2}(X^{-\frac{1}{2}}(XB_{c}+B_{c}^{*}X)X^{-\frac{1}{2}})>0\ (\geq 0),

respectively,
and skew-Hermitian part

-\widetilde{B_{S}}:=\frac{1}{2}(X^{\frac{1}{2}}B_{c}X^{-\frac{1}{2}}-(X^{\frac{1}{2}}B_{c}X^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{*}).

Note that the transformation to semi-dissipative form is not unique, so one might ask the question what is the relation between different (semi)-dissipative transformations that are obtained in this way.
It is immediate that if
X,\tilde{X}>0
are different positive definite solutions of (
6.1
) then these two solutions are congruent, and since for both transformation matrices in (
6.3
) we perform a similarity transform, the spectrum of the transformed system matrices is the same as that of the original system.

As one option to use the freedom in the solution
X
, in
[
1
]
Lemma 2, or
[
7
]
Lemma 2.6 in the real case, it is suggested to solve the Lyapunov-like inequality

XB_{c}+B_{c}^{*}X\geq-2\mu X,

(6.4)

where
\mu<0
is the spectral abscissa, i.e., the maximal real part of an eigenvalue of
-B_{c}
. It has been mentioned in
[
9
]
that this choice leads to a smallest eigenvalue of the Hermitian part
B_{H}
that is equal to
-\mu
. In more detail one has the following result.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
-B_{c}\in\mathbb{C}^{d,d}
have all eigenvalues in
\mathbb{H}
and let
\mu<0
be the spectral abscissa, i.e., the largest real part of an eigenvalue of
-B_{c}
.

(i)

If every eigenvalue with real part
\mu
is semi-simple, then there exists
X>0
such that

XB_{c}+B_{c}^{*}X\geq-2\mu X,

and the inequality is not strict, i.e., there exists a nonzero vector
z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
z^{*}(XB_{c}+B_{c}^{*}X+2\mu X)z=0
.

(ii)

If at least one eigenvalue
\lambda
of
-B_{c}
with real part
\mu
is defective, i.e., there exists a Jordan block of size larger than one associated with
\lambda
, then for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
X>0
such that

XB_{c}+B_{c}^{*}X\geq-2(\mu+\epsilon)X.

Proof.

Let
\lambda=\mu+i\gamma
be an eigenvalue of
-B_{c}
.

(i) Let
T\in\mathbb{C}^{d,d}
be invertible such that

-S=-T^{-1}B_{c}T=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&B_{2}\end{bmatrix},

which can e.g. be obtained from the Jordan form of
B_{c}
. Let
t_{1}=T^{-*}e_{1}
and
X=t_{1}t_{1}^{*}+\tilde{X}
with
\tilde{X}=\tilde{X}^{*}
and
t_{1}^{*}\tilde{X}=0
so that
T^{*}\tilde{X}T=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&X_{2}\end{bmatrix}
.
Then

\displaystyle B_{c}^{*}X+XB_{c}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{-*}S^{*}T^{*}X+XTST^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{-*}(S^{*}T^{*}XT+T^{*}XTS)T^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{-*}\begin{bmatrix}-2\mu&0\\
0&-B_{2}^{*}X_{2}-X_{2}B_{2}\end{bmatrix}T^{-1}.

Thus,

B_{c}^{*}X+XB_{c}+2\mu X=T^{-*}\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&-B_{2}^{*}X_{2}-X_{2}B_{2}+2\mu X_{2}\end{bmatrix}T^{-1}.

Proceeding inductively until all eigenvalues with real part
\mu
have been treated,
since
\mu
is the largest real part of an eigenvalue of
-B_{c}
, and thus all eigenvalues of
B_{2}
have real parts at most
\mu
, it follows that there exists a solution
X_{2}>0
of
-B_{2}^{*}X_{2}-X_{2}B_{2}+2\mu X_{2}\geq 0
and hence
X
has the desired properties.

(ii) If an eigenvalue
\lambda
associated with the largest real part is defective, then

-S=-T^{-1}B_{c}T=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&b_{2}\\
0&B_{2}\end{bmatrix},

with
b_{2}\neq 0
, where the norm of
b_{2}
can be made arbitrarily small by a scaling of
b_{2}
in the Jordan canonical form. Proceeding as in part 1, it follows that

B_{c}^{*}X+XB_{c}+2\mu X=T^{-*}\begin{bmatrix}0&-b_{2}\\
-b_{2}^{*}&-B_{2}^{*}X_{2}-X_{2}B_{2}+2\mu X_{2}\end{bmatrix}T^{-1}

is indefinite, while
B_{c}^{*}X+XB_{c}+2(\mu+\epsilon)X
can be made definite by the scaling of
b_{2}
.
∎

Since
B_{c}
and
X^{\frac{1}{2}}B_{c}X^{-\frac{1}{2}}
have the same spectral abscissa, Lemma
6.1
shows that the similarity transformation with
X^{\frac{1}{2}}
from (
6.4
) maps the system (
1.1
) to a system of form (
6.3
) with
maximal coercivity constant
, i.e.,
B_{H}
has the maximal possible smallest eigenvalue. Furthermore, the field of values of the transformed system lies fully on the left of the line
\mu+i\mathbb{R}
. Such a solution was already used for Fokker-Planck equations yielding sharp decay rates, see
[
7
, Lemma 2.11(i)]
.

Remark 6.2
.

In the infinite dimensional case a similar result follows under weak conditions, see
[
13
]
, Section 4, Theorem 4.1.3, as well as Exercise 4.17 and 4.18.

Remark 6.3
.

A result similar to Lemma
6.1
also holds if for the spectral abscissa one has
\mu=0
, i.e., if eigenvalues with real part zero occur.
The proof is analogous.

6.2
Discrete-time Lyapunov transformations

For a linear time-invariant discrete-time system of the form (
1.2
)
with
B_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{d,d}
we have an analogous result to Lemma
6.1
.

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
B_{d}\in\mathbb{C}^{d,d}
have all eigenvalues in
\mathbb{D}
and let
\rho<1
be its spectral radius, i.e., the largest modulus of an eigenvalue of
B_{d}
.

(i)

If every eigenvalue with modulus
\rho
is semi-simple, then there exists
X=X^{*}>0
such that

\rho^{2}X-B_{d}^{*}XB_{d}\geq 0,

and the inequality is not strict, i.e., there exists a nonzero vector
z\in\mathbb{C}^{d}
such that
z^{*}(\rho^{2}X-B_{d}^{*}XB_{d})z=0
.

(ii)

If at least one eigenvalue
\lambda
with modulus
\rho
is defective, i.e., there exist a Jordan block of size larger than one associated with
\lambda
, then for every
\epsilon>0
there exists
X=X^{*}>0
such that

(\rho^{2}+\epsilon)X-B_{d}^{*}XB_{d}\geq 0.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
with
|\lambda|=\rho
be an eigenvalue of
B_{d}
.

(i) Let
T\in\mathbb{C}^{d,d}
be invertible such that

S=T^{-1}B_{d}T=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&B_{2}\end{bmatrix},

which can for instance be obtained from the Jordan form of
B_{d}
. Let
t_{1}=T^{-*}e_{1}
and
X=t_{1}t_{1}^{*}+\tilde{X}
with
\tilde{X}=\tilde{X}^{*}
and
t_{1}^{*}\tilde{X}=0
, so that
T^{*}\tilde{X}T=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&X_{2}\end{bmatrix}
.
Then

\displaystyle\rho^{2}X-B_{d}^{*}XB_{d}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\rho^{2}X-T^{-*}S^{*}T^{*}XTST^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{-*}(\rho^{2}T^{*}XT-S^{*}T^{*}XTS)T^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{-*}\begin{bmatrix}\rho^{2}-|\lambda|^{2}&0\\
0&\rho^{2}X_{2}-B_{2}^{*}X_{2}B_{2}\end{bmatrix}T^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle T^{-*}\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&\rho^{2}X_{2}-B_{2}^{*}X_{2}B_{2}\end{bmatrix}T^{-1}\geq 0.

Proceeding inductively until all eigenvalues with modulus
\rho
have been treated,
since
\rho
is the largest modulus of an eigenvalue of
B_{d}
, and thus all eigenvalues of
B_{2}
have modulus at most
\rho
it follows that there exists a solution
X_{2}>0
of
\rho^{2}X_{2}-B_{2}^{*}X_{2}B_{2}\geq 0
and hence
X
has the desired properties.

(ii) If an eigenvalue
\lambda
with modulus
\rho
is defective, then

S=T^{-1}B_{d}T=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda&b_{2}\\
0&B_{2}\end{bmatrix},

with
b_{2}\neq 0
, where the norm of
b_{2}
can be made arbitrarily small by a scaling of
b_{2}
in the Jordan canonical form. Proceeding as in part 1, it follows that

\rho^{2}X-B_{d}^{*}XB_{d}=T^{-*}\begin{bmatrix}0&-\bar{\lambda}b_{2}\\
-\lambda b_{2}^{*}&\rho^{2}X_{2}-B_{2}^{*}X_{2}B_{2}-|b_{2}|^{2}\end{bmatrix}T^{-1}

is indefinite, while
(\rho^{2}+\epsilon)X-B_{d}^{*}XB_{d}
can be made definite by the scaling of
b_{2}
.
∎

Note that the construction in Lemma
6.4
then leads to a representation that is maximally contractive.

Remark 6.5
.

A result similar to Lemma
6.4
also holds if for the spectral radius one has
\rho=1
, i.e., if eigenvalues on the unit circle occur.
The proof is analogous.

Discretization of (
1.1
) with the implicit midpoint rule yields a discrete-time
system with
B_{d}=(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c})^{-1}(I-\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c})
and the discrete-time Lyapunov inequality holds for
B_{d}
if and only if the continuous-time inequality holds for
-B_{c}
.
Therefore we can choose the formulations in both cases accordingly.

6.3
The effect of a Lyapunov transformation on the numerical solution

In this subsection we study the effect of the Lyapunov transformation to semi-dissipative form in (
6.3
) on the numerical solution of (
1.1
).

Consider the well-known error estimates, see e.g.
[
21
]
, for the solution of initial value problems for ordinary differential equations
\dot{x}=f(x)
,
x(t_{0})=x^{0}
with a one-step method for the approximations
u_{i}\approx x(t_{i})
on a grid
t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{N}
(with
\tau_{i}=t_{i+1}-t_{i}
) given by

u_{i+1}=u_{i}+\tau_{i}\Phi(t_{i},u_{i},\tau_{i}),\ u_{0}\approx x^{0},

(6.5)

with the increment function
\Phi
satisfying a Lipschitz condition

\|\Phi(t,x_{1},\tau)-\Phi(t,x_{2},\tau)\|\leq L_{x}\|x_{1}-x_{2}\|,

(6.6)

for a norm
\|\cdot\|
.
Then

\|x(t_{i})-u_{i}\|\leq\big(\|x(t_{0})-u_{0}\|+(t_{i}-t_{0})\theta_{\max}\big)e^{L_{x}(t_{i}-t_{0})},

(6.7)

where

\theta_{\max}=\max_{t\in[t_{0},t_{N}]}|\theta(t)|

is the maximal local discretization error of the method.

Let us consider the original
L_{2}
norm

\|x(t)\|
and the same method applied to the transformed system with
y(t)=X^{\frac{1}{2}}x(t)
with approximations
v_{i}=X^{\frac{1}{2}}u_{i}\penalty\ \approx y(t_{i})
and Lipschitz constant
L_{y}
.
Then

\|x(t_{i})-u_{i}\|\leq\|X^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\,\|y(t_{i})-v_{i}\|\leq\|X^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\,\big(\|y(t_{0})-v_{0}\|+(t_{i}-t_{0})\theta_{\max}\big)e^{L_{y}(t_{i}-t_{0})}.

(6.8)

If we consider the implicit midpoint rule (with constant stepsize
\tau
) applied to (
1.1
), then

\Phi(x,\tau)=-(I+\frac{\tau}{2}B_{c})^{-1}B_{c}x

so that

L_{y}=\|-(I+\frac{\tau}{2}X^{\frac{1}{2}}B_{c}X^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}X^{\frac{1}{2}}B_{c}X^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\leq\|X^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\,\|X^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|L_{x}

and hence

e^{L_{y}(t_{i}-t_{0})}\leq e^{L_{x}(t_{i}-t_{0})}\|X^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\,\|X^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|.

Inserting this into (
6.7
) we obtain the estimate

\|x(t_{i})-u_{i}\|\leq\|X^{\frac{1}{2}}\|\,\|X^{-\frac{1}{2}}\|\,\big(\|x(t_{0})-u_{0}\|+(t_{i}-t_{0})\theta_{\max}\big)e^{L_{x}(t_{i}-t_{0})}.

This shows that the transformation to semi-dissipative form leads to a multiplication of the error estimate by the condition number of
X^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

Note that the same estimate holds for all Runge-Kutta methods when applied to linear systems, since usually the increment function of the transformed system is similar to that of the original system and therefore the Lipschitz constant is just scaled with the condition number of the transformation matrix.

Conclusion

New proofs for the characterizations of the short-time decay of the solutions
of linear continuous-time and discrete-time evolution equations from the initial value are presented for both systems. It is shown that essentially the same proof
technique can be employed in both systems. The constants in the leading terms of the propagator norm expansion are determined. When the
discrete-time system arises from the implicit midpoint discretization (scaled Cayley-transform) of
a continuous-time system, it is shown that the norm of the continuous-time solution operator is
approximated to a higher order than expected from the order of the discretization.

Since the representation of a linear system as a semi-dissipative or semi-contractive
system is not unique, but can be modified by a change of basis, we discuss the construction of
maximally coercive/contractive representations of hypocoercive and hypocontractive systems and the effect of different
representations on the error estimates for the numerical solution.
