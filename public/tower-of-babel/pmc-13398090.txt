Integrated transcriptomic, metabolomic, and physiological analyses underlying differential responses to potassium deficiency in sesame
Authors: Yiming Xu, Biqing Zhang, Ziming Wu, Sheng Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPotassium (K) is an essential macronutrient for plant growth and stress resistance, and K deficiency severely limits sesame (Sesamum indicum L.) yield and oil quality. Despite substantial genotypic variation in K tolerance among sesame cultivars, the molecular mechanisms underlying this variation remain poorly characterized. Here, an integrated physiological, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analysis was performed on two contrasting sesame cultivars (K sensitive ZZ9 and K tolerant WZM6) grown under low K (LK, 0.2 mmol L− 1) and adequate K (CK, 2 mmol L− 1) conditions to identify key traits and genes associated with K tolerance. Physiological analysis showed that low K stress inhibited plant growth, reduced leaf K content, and altered photosynthetic and sucrose metabolism-related enzyme activities in both cultivars. WZM6 maintained better performance with 34.3% less reduction in plant height, 29.5% less loss in leaf K content, and 12.8% reduction in Rubisco activity. Transcriptomic profiling identified 513 and 195 unique differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in ZZ9-LK vs. ZZ9-CK and WZM6-LK vs. WZM6-CK, respectively. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed that DEGs in ZZ9 were enriched in protein metabolic processes and mRNA surveillance pathway, while WZM6 DEGs were associated with small molecule metabolism, intracellular transport, and carbon/amino acid metabolism. Key K transporter genes (HAK5, POT5, POT6) and nitrate transporters (NRT2.1) were more highly upregulated in WZM6, contributing to its superior K uptake capacity. Metabolomic analysis detected 294 differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs), with 102 and 96 cultivar specific DAMs in ZZ9 and WZM6, which were enriched in plant secondary metabolite biosynthesis, ABC transports and biosynthesis of amino acids. Integrated transcriptomic-metabolomic analysis highlighted glutamate biosynthesis (GS/GOGAT pathway) and sucrose metabolism as core regulatory networks, with WZM6 maintaining stable glutamine accumulation and sucrose synthase activity under low K stress. Therefore, WZM6 achieves K tolerance through enhanced K uptake via transporters, targeted transcriptional regulation, and metabolic remodeling of carbon-nitrogen metabolism. Our findings provide valuable genetic resources and candidate genes for breeding K efficient varieties, which is essential for improving yield stability and reducing K fertilizer inputs in K deficient fields.Keywords: Sesame, Potassium, Metabolomic and transcriptomics, Glutamate biosynthesis, Regulator networks

Introduction

Sesame (Sesamum indicum L.) is an ancient and economically oilseed crop cultivated worldwide for its high quality oil and nutritional value [1, 2]. Potassium (K) is indispensable for plant physiological functions, from maintaining turgor pressure to activating enzymes crucial for growth and stress adaptation. Large areas of the agricultural land of the world are deficient in potassium which include 3/4 of the paddy soils of China [3], posing a significant threat to sesame yield and oil quality [4, 5]. Sesame is a crop with high potassium demand, requiring an average of 6.3 kg of K₂O per 100 kg of seeds produced. Rational application of potassium fertilizer can increase sesame yield by an average of 13.1% [5]. As a pivotal macronutrient, K is integral to a multitude of physiological processes, including enzyme activation, osmotic regulation, and stress resistance [6]. While K fertilization is a common practice, its low use efficiency in agriculture not only escalates production costs but also poses environmental risks such as soil degradation and water pollution, which is incompatible with the goal of sustainable agriculture [7]. Intriguingly, substantial genotypic variation in potassium efficiency exists among sesame varieties, presenting a genetic resource for improvement [8]. K efficient varieties typically possesses well-developed root systems, strong root activity, active physiological metabolism, higher potassium uptake capacity, and faster translocation rates [9]. They can maintain relatively high photosynthetic efficiency and carbon assimilation even under low potassium supply or when leaf potassium content is reduced [10]. Research has shown that two cotton varieties with similar yield levels under K sufficient conditions can exhibit nearly a 70% difference in yield in K deficient soil, mainly due to lower net photosynthetic rate, excessive nitrogen accumulation, and hindered export of photosynthetic products in the potassium sensitive variety [11]. However, previous research has primarily focused on phenotypic and physiological responses, leaving a critical gap in our understanding of the systemic molecular mechanisms that confer high potassium use efficiency.

In recent years, multi-omics technologies have been widely applied to dissect the molecular mechanisms underlying plant tolerance to K deficiency. Plants have evolved complex adaptive mechanisms to cope with low K stress, including enhanced K⁺ uptake and transport, metabolic reprogramming, and activation of sucrose distribution [1, 12]. Studies in soybean [7], wheat [13], and canola [14] have revealed that low-K tolerance involves coordinated regulation of ion transporters, sugar metabolism, osmolyte accumulation, and membrane lipid composition. In model plants such as Arabidopsis and rice, several K transporter genes (e.g., HAK, KAT, KEA) have been confirmed to play crucial roles in maintaining K homeostasis under low-K conditions [15, 16].

Despite these advances, research on sesame’s response to low K stress remains largely limited to phenotypic and physiological levels, such as photosynthetic characteristics and antioxidant enzyme activities. There is a lack of systematic multi-omics studies to comprehensively elucidate the molecular regulatory networks and metabolic pathways underlying sesame’s low K tolerance, which hinders the development of molecular breeding technologies for K efficient sesame varieties. However, K deficiency in soils is a common abiotic stress that limits sesame yield and quality. Therefore, it is urgent to explore the genotype specific responses of sesame to low-K stress at the transcriptional and metabolic levels, which will lay a theoretical foundation for improving sesame potassium utilization efficiency through genetic modification.

To address this, we conducted an integrated analysis of the physiological, transcriptome and metabolome in two distinct sesame varieties (K tolerant WZM6 and K sensitive ZZ9) under K deficient conditions. Our research aims to (1) compare the differences in growth parameters, K accumulation, and key enzyme activities between the two varieties under low K and adequate K conditions; (2) identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) associated with low K tolerance in sesame; (3) dissect the key biological pathways involved in low K stress response through functional enrichment analysis of DEGs and DAMs. The insights gained from this study are expected to accelerate the development of novel sesame genotypes with enhanced potassium acquisition and utilization, paving the way for more sustainable and resilient sesame cultivation.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and treatments

The hydroponic experiment was conducted at the science and technology building of Jiangxi agricultural university with K sensitive variety Zhongzhi No. 9 (ZZ9, developed by sexual hybridization between Xinjiang black sesame and Zhongzhi No. 7) and the K tolerant variety Changzhi No. 6 (WZM6, developed by sexual hybridization between Xingan black sesame and Wuning black sesame) set as test materials provided by Jiangxi Agricultural University. The two cultivars are conventional sesame varieties widely planted in Jiangxi, with ZZ9 derived from a high-oil breeding line, while WZM6 was selected from a landrace with known adaptation to low fertility soils. Prior to the experiment, sesame seeds were surface sterilized with 5% sodium hypochlorite for 20 min, rinsed three times with deionized water, and then germinated in clean silica sand. After the first pair of true leaves had fully expanded, uniformly healthy seedlings were selected and transferred to plastic containers (40 cm × 26 cm × 15 cm). The containers were lined with black plastic bags on the sides and bottom to maintain the root zone in darkness. Seedlings were fixed on planting baskets using foam strips. Each container was planted 24 seedlings and filled with 14 L of modified Hoagland nutrient solution prepared with deionized water, which was renewed every three days. Two potassium treatments were established as low K (0.2 mmol L− 1) and adequate K (2 mmol L− 1). The pH of the nutrient solution was adjusted to 6.5. Three biological replicates were included for each treatment. After 20 days of treatment, the seedlings were harvested for sampling.

Phenology and growth parameters

Sesame plants with uniform growth traits were selected and separated into roots, stems, and leaves. The samples were deactivation at 105 °C for 30 min, then dried in an oven at 80 °C to constant weight for the determination of plant height, root and shoot biomass. Leaf K content was measured by H2SO4-H2O2 method with atomic absorption spectrophotometry [17].

Physiological and biochemical assays

Six uniformly growing sesame seedlings were selected from each group. The third pair of fully expanded true leaves were collected, washed, and cut into halves. One half of the leaf samples was stored at − 80 °C for the determination of enzyme activities, including phosphate synthase (SPS), sucrose synthase (SuSy), and soluble acid invertase (SAI), while the other half was deactivated at 105 °C and then dried at 70 °C for the measurement of sucrose and starch content following the method described by Wang et al. [18]. Rubisco activity was determined using ultraviolet spectrophotometry. Root vigor was measured by TTC method [19]. All determinations were performed with three biological replicates and three technical replicates.

RNA-seq and transcriptome analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the third pair of fully expanded true leaves using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality and integrity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA) and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent, USA), respectively. The cDNA library construction and sequencing were performed at Guangzhou Genedenovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Clean reads were aligned to the sesame reference genome (version v1.0, available at https://sesame-bioinfo.org) using HISAT2. Raw sequencing reads were filtered using Fastp software [20] to remove low-quality reads (Q < 20), reads with > 10% ambiguous bases (N), and reads consisting entirely of adenine residues, to obtain high-quality clean data. Clean reads were aligned to the sesame reference genome using HISAT2 software [21], and gene expression levels were calculated as Fragments Per Kilobase of transcript per Million mapped reads (FPKM) values [22]. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using DESeq2 with thresholds of |log₂(FC)| ≥ 1 and adjusted P < 0.05. GO (Gene Ontology) and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) enrichment analyses were performed using cluster profiler to identify functional enrichment of DEGs.

Metabolomic profiling

Metabolites were extracted from third pair of fully expanded true leaves samples (100 mg) with 1.0 mL of 70% aqueous methanol. The extracts were analyzed using a UPLC-ESI-MS/MS system (Shimadzu, Japan) coupled with an API 4500 QTRAP mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX, USA). The UPLC separation was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm), and the MS/MS detection [23] was conducted in positive and negative ion modes with multiple reaction monitoring (MRM). Metabolites were identified based on the Biomarker Metabolite Database and public databases (HMDB, MoToDB). Differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) were screened with variable importance in projection (VIP) ≥ 1 and |log₂(FC)| ≥ 1. KEGG enrichment analysis of DAMs was performed using the MetaboAnalyst 5.0 online platform [24], with the threshold of P < 0.05 for significantly enriched pathways.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates. Statistical significance was determined by one-way ANOVA using SPSS 20.0, with P < 0.05considered significant. Graphs were plotted using Origin 2021 software.

Results

Phenotypic, root vigor, and leaf K changes in ZZ9 and WZM6 under low K stress

To investigate the phenotypic and physiological responses of ZZ9 (K sensitive cultivar) and WZM6 (K tolerant cultivar) to low K (LK) stress, we compared plant growth, root activity, and K accumulation under different K conditions. As shown in Fig. 1A, low K stress markedly inhibited the growth of sesame. Low K stress suppressed ZZ9 growth more severely than WZM6. The plant height (Fig. 1B) was decreased by 34.3% under LK in ZZ9 while WZM6’s plant height showed no significant difference between LK and CK. In addition, compared to CK, the root (Fig. 1C) and shoot biomass (Fig. 1D) was declined by 52.7% and 38.0% respectively under LK in ZZ9, while reduced by 36.1% and 21.9% in WZM6. Root activity differed significantly between cultivars under LK (Fig. 1E). In ZZ9, low K increased root activity by 36.7%. In WZM6, root activity showed no significant difference between LK and CK. Leaf K content directly reflects plant K acquisition efficiency (Fig. 1F). Under LK, leaf K content of ZZ9 decreased sharply by 46.7%, while WZM6 retained a significantly higher leaf K content by reducing 29.5%.

Physiological and biochemical traits in response to low K stress

Under low K treatment, the sucrose (Fig. 2A) and starch (Fig. 2B) contents of both varieties increased significantly compared with the K sufficient condition, with inter-treatment differences reaching significant levels. Compared with the WZM6, the ZZ9 exhibited greater increases in sucrose (61.2%) and starch (38.1%) contents under low K treatment. As shown in Fig. 2C, compared with the K sufficient condition, low K stress significantly reduced leaf Rubisco activity by 40.0% and 12.8% in ZZ9 and WZM6, respectively. SPS, SuSy, and SAI play important roles in plant sucrose metabolism, with SuSy and SAI being significantly affected by the interaction between variety and K level. Under K sufficient conditions, sesame seedlings exhibited higher activities of SPS (Fig. 2D) and SuSy (Fig. 2E), but lower SAI activity (Fig. 2F). The WZM6 showed minimal overall changes in SPS activity, with no significant alterations in SuSy and SAI activities. In contrast, the ZZ9 exhibited significant changes in SPS, SuSy, and SAI activities. Compared with the K sufficient treatment, the SPS and SuSy activities in ZZ9 leaves decreased by 24.0% and 7.9%, respectively, while SAI activity increased by 8.1%.

Transcriptome profiling of ZZ9 and WZM6 under low K stress

To characterize the genome-wide transcriptional responses to low K stress between the K sensitive cultivar ZZ9 and K tolerant cultivar WZM6, RNA sequencing was performed on leaf samples from four groups (ZZ9-CK, ZZ9-LK, WZM6-CK, WZM6-LK) with three biological replicates per group. The Pearson correlation heatmap (Fig. 3A) demonstrated high reproducibility among replicates, validating the reliability of transcriptomic data. The Venn diagram (Fig. 3B) characterized the distribution of differentially expressed genes (DEGs; |log₂(FC)| ≥ 1, p < 0.05) across groups. Results showed that WZM6-LK and ZZ9-LK possessed 195 and 513 unique DEGs, while WZM6-CK and ZZ9-CK only had 105 and 18 unique DEGs. Volcano plots (Fig. 3C, D) visualized DEG dynamics with ZZ9 exhibited extensive transcriptional reprogramming under LK and majority of them were upregulated. There are only 116 of DEGs downregulated. In contrast, WZM6 displayed a more extensive transcriptional adjustment, characterized by a greater abundance of DEGs and more prominent downregulated gene clusters (666 of DEGs), reflecting a more robust transcriptomic adjustment that aligns with its K-tolerant phenotype.

GO enrichment analysis revealed cultivar specific biological processes. In ZZ9 (Fig. 4A), DEGs were predominantly enriched in cellular protein metabolic process (GO:0044267), protein modification process (GO:0036211), and macromolecule modification (GO:0043412), indicating that protein metabolism regulation is a central low K response in the K sensitive cultivar. In WZM6 (Fig. 4B), DEGs were enriched in small molecule metabolic process (GO:0044281), intracellular transport (GO:0046907), and organelle organization (GO:0006996), reflecting a broader metabolic and cellular organizational adaptation in the K tolerant cultivar. KEGG pathway enrichment further clarified cultivar specific mechanisms. ZZ9’s DEGs (Fig. 4C) were enriched in mRNA surveillance pathway, biosynthesis of amino acids, and nucleocytoplasmic transport, suggesting post-transcriptional regulation and amino acid metabolism as key pathways response to low K stress. WZM6’s DEGs (Fig. 4D) were enriched in biosynthesis of amino acids, carbon metabolism, and endocytosis, indicating that coordinated regulation of carbon/amino acid metabolism and membrane transport underpins its K tolerance.

Comparative metabolomic analysis of ZZ9 and WZM6 under low K stress

The reliability of metabolomic data was validated via Pearson correlation analysis (Fig. 5A) and biological replicates within the same treatment exhibited high correlation coefficients, confirming consistent metabolic profiles across replicates. Volcano plots showed (Fig. 5B) quantified differentially accumulated metabolites (DAMs) abundance (|log₂(FC)| ≥ 1, P < 0.05) in different cultivars. Low K stress induced 59 upregulated and 89 downregulated metabolites in ZZ9, reflecting a predominantly repressive metabolic response. Concurrently, low K stress triggered 75 upregulated and 67 downregulated metabolites, with more upregulated metabolites indicating a more active metabolic adaptation. A Venn diagram (Fig. 5C) further characterized DAM specificity that only 46 DAMs (18.85%) were shared between the two cultivars, while 102 (41.8%) and 96 (39.34%) were unique to ZZ9 and WZM6, respectively, indicating highly genotype-specific metabolic reprogramming under low K stress. KEGG pathway enrichment revealed divergent metabolic pathway regulation between cultivars. In ZZ9 (Fig. 5D), DAMs were most enriched in biosynthesis of plant secondary metabolites (29 metabolites), biosynthesis of plant secondary metabolites (17 metabolites), ABC transporters (11 metabolites), and Biosynthesis of amino acids (10 metabolites). In WZM6 (Fig. 5E), DAMs were also mainly concentrated in the above four pathways with 31, 16, 11 and 11 metabolites, respectively. In addition, under low potassium stress, 6 metabolites related to carbon metabolism and 2 metabolites related to nitrogen metabolism were also identified in the WZM6 cultivar.

Integration of the transcriptomic and metabolomic profiles

A comparative analysis of transcriptional and metabolic responses to low K showed several common enriched pathways, including carbon metabolism and biosynthesis of amino acids (Fig. 6). Glutamate is important for plant growth and development. The contents of some metabolites related to the glutamate biosynthesis pathway increased significantly in ZZ9, including citric acid (log2FC = 0.33), Oxo-glutaric acid (log2FC = 0.35), malic acid (log2FC = 0.23), and succinic acid (log2FC = 0.30). The glutamine content in ZZ9 decreased significantly (log2FC = 0.79), whereas significant increased (log2FC = 0.83) in WZM6. DEGs related to the glutamate biosynthesis pathway were upregulated more profound in ZZ9 than in WZM6. The ncbi_105156948 and ncbi_105165713 encoding glutamine synthetase (GS) had up-regulated expression levels (log2FC = 4.32 and log2FC = 3.41, respectively) in ZZ9 and (log2FC = 2.07 and log2FC = 2.41, respectively) in WZM6. Moreover, the expression levels of the DEGs ncbi_105159899 encoding glutamate synthase (GOGAT) were significantly up-regulated (log2FC = 3.73) in ZZ9 and (log2FC = 3.68) in WZM6. Consequently, enhanced expression of GS/GOGAT genes accompanied by increased glutamine accumulation may facilitate nitrogen reassimilation and carbon-nitrogen balance, supporting photosynthetic efficiency and growth under low K stress. Carbohydrates, such as sucrose, fructose, and glucose 1-phosphate were increase in ZZ9. The ncbi_105161960 encoding sucrose synthetase (SS) was upregulated while ncbi_105172940 and ncbi_105173847 encoding alkaline/neutral invertase (INV) was downregulated, indicating a shift toward carbohydrate storage rather than utilization.

Discussion

Soil K depletion is a growing threat to global agriculture, driven by intensive crop production and low K fertilizer use efficiency. Exploiting genotypic variation in K tolerance is an effective strategy to improve K use efficiency without excessive fertilizer input [3, 25]. This study systematically dissected the physiological, transcriptional, and metabolic responses of two sesame cultivars with contrasting K tolerance, and uncovering key mechanisms underlying their differential adaptation to low K stress.

The physiological differences between the two cultivars highlight the importance of phenotypic variation in K tolerance. Low K stress commonly causes growth inhibition, biomass reduction, photosynthetic suppression, and nutrient imbalance in various crops. In peanut [26], cotton [27], and rapeseed [28], K sensitive genotypes show more severe growth retardation, lower leaf K content, and weaker photosynthetic capacity under K deficiency compared to tolerant genotypes. Similarly, in the present study, low K stress severely inhibited plant height, root and shoot biomass, and leaf K accumulation in the K sensitive sesame cultivar ZZ9, while the K tolerant WZM6 maintained relatively stable growth and higher K retention. The superior root growth and higher leaf K content in WZM6 under low K conditions indicate enhanced K acquisition and utilization capacity, which is a characteristic of K efficient genotypes. Rubisco activity, a key indicator of photosynthetic efficiency, was drastically reduced in ZZ9 but slightly affected in WZM6, indicating better photosynthetic protection in the tolerant cultivar. This is consistent with findings in soybean [29] and rapeseed [28]. Meanwhile, significantly increased accumulation of sucrose and starch in ZZ9 under low K stress reflected disrupted carbon metabolism, this finding aligns with the results reported by Hu et al. [30], where low potassium led to more pronounced changes in sucrose content in K sensitive cultivars. K deficiency increases the activity of starch synthase, leading to elevated starch content and altering the partitioning of photosynthetic products between sucrose and starch [31]. The stability of sucrose metabolism-related enzymes (SPS, SuSy, SAI) in WZM6 further supports its superior metabolic regulation under nutrient stress, which is critical for sustaining yield in K deficient fields [32]. These physiological differences confirm that WZM6 possesses stronger stress resistance and more stable homeostasis under low K stress, laying a physiological foundation for its K tolerance.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed distinct gene expression patterns between cultivars. The larger number of DEGs in ZZ9 (513 unique DEGs) compared to WZM6 (195 unique DEGs) suggests that the sensitive cultivar undergoes more extensive transcriptional reprogramming to cope with low K stress, which may indicate metabolic imbalance. In contrast, WZM6 undergoes targeted and efficient transcriptional adjustment, with DEGs enriched in small molecule metabolism and intracellular transport, pathways directly related to K uptake and stress adaptation, reflecting a more economical and effective transcriptional regulatory mechanism. K transporters play a central role in plant K uptake and homeostasis under low K conditions, and the sesame specific expression pattern of K transporter genes is the core molecular basis for the superior low K tolerance of WZM6. The significant upregulation of HAK5, a core high-affinity K transporter [33], was higher in WZM6 (log2FC = 4.57) than in ZZ9 (log2FC = 3.18) directly enhances the high affinity K uptake capacity of WZM6, which is the main reason for its higher leaf K content under low K stress. This result is consistent with the function of OsHAK5 in rapeseed in low K tolerance [34], and confirms the conserved role of HAK5 in plant K uptake across different species. The POT family genes (POT5, POT10) were also more highly upregulated in WZM6, further enhancing its K uptake and translocation capacity. KEA family proteins encode K⁺ efflux antiporters that mediate K⁺ efflux from cells [35], and the high expression of KEA genes in ZZ9 than in WZM6 leads to excessive K⁺ efflux from leaf cells, which exacerbates K deficiency and is an important molecular reason for its K sensitivity, this is a novel finding specific to sesame low K stress responses. NRT family genes encode high affinity nitrate transporters that mediate nitrate uptake [36, 37], and their upregulation under low K stress can maintain normal nitrogen uptake while promoting K uptake through the synergistic regulation of ion transporters. The upregulation of NRT family genes in both cultivars under low K stress indicates the existence of K and N uptake crosstalk in sesame, and the higher induction of NRT genes in WZM6 reflects its more coordinated nutrient uptake strategy.

Metabolomic profiling revealed that WZM6 exhibited an active metabolic response to low K stress (75 upregulated DAMs) compared with ZZ9 (59 upregulated DAMs), and the highly genotype specific DAMs (only 18.85% shared) indicate that sesame low K tolerance is mediated by cultivar specific metabolic reprogramming. The enrichment of plant secondary metabolite biosynthesis in both cultivars suggests that secondary metabolism (e.g., flavonoids, phenolics) plays a conserved role in K stress adaptation, possibly by regulating redox homeostasis or providing osmo-protectants [38]. In addition, phenylalanine derived metabolites such as flavonoids are involved in stress resistance and cell wall reinforcement [39], while enhanced carbon metabolism ensures energy supply for adaptive processes such as K transporter synthesis and root growth, and potentially improving root soil interactions for K uptake, which is the energy basis for the superior low K tolerance of WZM6. Therefore, WZM6 maintained better growth performance in plant height, root and shoot biomass than the sensitive ZZ9. The superior root growth and higher leaf K content in WZM6 under low K conditions indicate enhanced K acquisition and utilization capacity, which is a characteristic of K efficient genotypes [27]. In contrast, ZZ9’s enrichment in oxidative phosphorylation suggests mitochondrial dysfunction under low K stress, leading to insufficient ATP supply for K uptake and other physiological processes [15], which is an important metabolic reason for the K sensitivity of ZZ9.

Integrated multi-omics analysis revealed coordinated regulation of glutamate biosynthesis and sucrose metabolism as the core regulatory networks for sesame low K tolerance, and the coordinated regulation of carbon-nitrogen metabolism based on these two pathways is the key molecular mechanism for WZM6 to adapt to low K stress. Although ZZ9 showed higher upregulation of GS and GOGAT genes, it only accumulated precursor metabolites and had a significant decrease in glutamine content, indicating a blocked glutamate biosynthesis pathway under low K stress. Glutamine is a key intermediate in nitrogen metabolism that participates in osmotic regulation and stress signaling [40], and its decrease in ZZ9 leads to nitrogen metabolism disorder and impaired stress signaling, exacerbating K sensitivity. In contrast, WZM6 maintained stable GS/GOGAT gene expression and a significant increase in glutamine content, which ensures normal nitrogen metabolism, maintains cell osmotic balance, and activates stress signaling pathways. In WZM6, enhanced GS/GOGAT expression together with increased glutamine accumulation likely facilitates nitrogen reassimilation and C-N balance, supporting photosynthetic efficiency under low K stress. Consequently, the Rubisco activity, a key determinant of photosynthetic efficiency, was less reduced in WZM6 than in ZZ9, suggesting that the tolerant cultivar retains better photosynthetic capacity under K limitation. This result is consistent with the general characteristics of K efficient crop varieties [27, 28]. Sucrose metabolism, a central hub of carbon allocation, showed distinct patterns with ZZ9 accumulated more sucrose and starch, implying that the sensitive cultivar may rely on carbohydrate storage as a passive adaptive strategy. meanwhile, WZM6 maintained stable enzyme activities and gene expression, indicating efficient carbon utilization for growth rather than storage [41]. This aligns with Hu et al. [30] who reported that low potassium led to more pronounced changes in sucrose content in K sensitive cultivars. Under low K stress, the activities of SPS and SuSy in sesame leaves decreased, while SAI increased, suggesting inhibited sucrose synthesis and enhanced sucrose hydrolysis, which may impair sucrose supply to sink organs [42, 43]. The synergistic stability of the GS/GOGAT pathway and sucrose metabolism in WZM6 achieves the coordinated regulation of carbon-nitrogen metabolism, which balances energy supply, nutrient homeostasis, and stress response, and is a key candidate pathway of its superior low K tolerance. This finding has not been previously reported in sesame, and supplements the understanding of the carbon-nitrogen metabolism regulatory mechanism in plant low K tolerance.

Summarily, the K tolerant sesame cultivar WZM6 exhibits superior physiological performance, targeted transcriptional regulation of K transporters and metabolic pathways, and coordinated carbon-nitrogen metabolism under low K stress. These findings advance our understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying K tolerance in sesame and provide candidate genes and metabolic markers for breeding K efficient varieties, which is crucial for reducing reliance on K fertilizers and promoting sustainable agriculture. However, the translation of hydroponic results to field conditions requires consideration. While hydroponics allows precise control of K availability, field soils vary in K content, texture, and other properties affecting plant K uptake [44]. Future studies should evaluate the performance of WZM6 and other K tolerant genotypes in K deficient fields through agronomic trials to assess yield stability, oil quality, and K use efficiency under different fertilizer regimes.

Conclusion

In this study, an integrated physiological, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analysis was performed on two contrasting sesame cultivars to uncover the core mechanisms of sesame low K tolerance. The key findings are summarized as follows: (1) The K tolerant cultivar WZM6 maintained superior growth, as evidenced by higher root biomass, higher leaf K content, and more stable photosynthetic and sucrose metabolism related enzyme activities under low K stress compared to the K sensitive ZZ9. (2) WZM6 showing enhanced expression of K transporter genes (HAK5, POT5, POT6) and enrichment in metabolic and transport pathways, while ZZ9 exhibited extensive transcriptional reprogramming related to protein metabolism. (3) Metabolic reprogramming in WZM6 prioritizes phenylalanine metabolism and carbon metabolism, providing osmo-protection and energy for stress adaptation, while ZZ9 exhibits metabolic imbalance linked to oxidative phosphorylation. (4) Integrated multi-omics analysis highlighted coordinated regulation of glutamate biosynthesis and sucrose metabolism as key pathways underlying K tolerance. These results uncover the genetic and metabolic basis of K tolerance in sesame, promoting sustainable oilseed production in K deficient agricultural systems.
