Personalized Accelerated Physiologic Dual Rate Pacing for Cardiac Amyloidosis
Authors: Markus Meyer, Valmiki Maharaj, Sneha Nandy, Rebecca Cogswell, Jop H. van Berlo, Mathew S. Maurer, Lisa Von Wald, Henri Roukoz
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
A 78-year-old man with cardiac amyloidosis developed sinus bradycardia with symptomatic hypotension. Three months after receiving an advanced biventricular pacemaker system with continuous accelerated rate pacing (daytime: 70 beats/min; nighttime: 90 beats/min), his health status and symptoms were markedly improved.

History of Presentation

Here we report the case of a 78-year-old African American man with a 5-year history of wild-type transthyretin amyloid cardiomyopathy and paroxysmal atrial fibrillation who was treated with tafamidis for the past 4 years. He was evaluated in the emergency department and admitted to the cardiology service for worsening hypotension with severe lightheadedness and recurring falls that increasingly precluded normal activities of daily living (NYHA functional class IV). Chronotropic incompetence and a lack of orthostatic rise in heart rate was demonstrated. Serial 12-lead electrocardiograms over the preceding 2 years suggested progressive cardiac conduction system disease that resulted in sinus node dysfunction, prolonged atrioventricular conduction, and a left bundle branch block, as shown in Figure 1A. The clinical presentation was consistent with transthyretin amyloid infiltration into the sinus node and conduction system. As shown in Figure 3, the patient was provided with an advanced biventricular pacing system that combined a left bundle branch area lead with a left ventricular coronary sinus lead, which is coined left bundle branch–optimized cardiac resynchronization therapy (LOT-CRT). Initially, there appeared to be little benefit from the pacing intervention, and the patient could only take few assisted steps with a walker. In addition, his systolic blood pressures remained below 110 mm Hg despite starting midodrine. However, over the course of several weeks, his self-reported health status continuously improved. At 3 months, the patient had resumed all previous activities of daily living (NYHA functional class I/II) and reported that his performance status was at a level last held 12 to 18 months ago. This was reflected in a 39-point improvement in the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire and a normalization of blood pressure as shown in Table 1. At the 3-month time point, the highly sensitive troponin level had improved, but the N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) level remained elevated. The echocardiogram suggested some regression in left ventricular mass. The principal physical limitation that remained was from back pain that predated the diagnosis of cardiac amyloidosis.

Rationale for Heart Rate Modifications in Cardiac Amyloidosis

Heart rate lowering

It is a common misperception that pharmacological heart rate lowering is beneficial in patients with restrictive cardiomyopathies by providing more time for ventricular filling.4,5 The 2023 treatment recommendations for the management of cardiac amyloidosis were the first to acknowledge that the well-being of these patients depends on higher heart rates and that beta-blockers have detrimental effects.6 Although slowing of the heart rate was presumed to be beneficial, it is in fact a problem because the prolongation of diastolic filling can only be accomplished at higher filling pressures, particularly in stiff ventricles. Conversely, higher heart rates abbreviate ventricular filling, which shifts the pressure–volume relationship leftward toward lower filling pressures. The ensuing reduction in stroke volume is compensated by the increase in rate.7 These effects help explain the symptomatic improvements in our patient; however, higher heart rates have been associated with an increased risk of death in observational studies. Because higher heart rates are likely a result of chronic illnesses, it is inappropriate to infer that accelerated pacing has the same risk.

Heart rate elevation

The rise in heart rate with physical activity enables exercise, and pacing above the resting heart rate can markedly reduce cardiac filling pressures and symptoms.8 Therefore, accelerated pacing effectively reduces cardiac filling pressures in patients with restricted filling by moving the ventricular pressure–volume relationship leftward and downward. Nevertheless, there are several points to consider before engaging in this approach. First, acute pacing studies in patients with heart failure and preserved ejection fraction confirmed that pacing up to 125 beats/min can normalize elevated left ventricular end-diastolic pressures.8 Second, permanent heart rate elevations above normal will inevitably induce some eccentric remodeling as part of an innate myocardial response with associated reductions in ejection fraction.3 Finally, pacing from the conduction system is emerging as a hemodynamically superior pacing modality that emulates normal myocardial activation, thereby minimizing the risk of pacing-induced cardiomyopathy.

The added left bundle area lead will increase the safety margin of continuous accelerated pacing. The question remains by how much should the heart rate be raised? Body height and resting heart rates have a linear relationship (ie, to explain why women have higher heart rates).9 This association was used to calculate a personalized heart rate. Continuous pacing at the resting heart rate in a patient with a normal ejection fraction should not induce much cardiac remodeling but may still reduce cardiac filling pressures at night when heart rates tend to be lower. In patients with small cavity sizes and higher ejection fractions, accelerated rate pacing enhances the reduction in filling pressures while increasing chamber volumes by eccentric remodeling over time. This myocardial adaptation is accompanied by left ventricular wall thinning and resultant improvements in tissue compliance.3,10 Prolonged and repetitive changes in heart rate will inevitably trigger these remodeling mechanisms that reorganize the extracellular matrix that in our case will include amyloid deposits. In a pig model of stage B heart failure and preserved ejection fraction, this innate molecular program involved cytokines such as interleukin-6 and chemokines like C-C motif ligand 2 that were upregulated 10- and 17-fold, respectively, 2 hours after changing the pacing rate.3 In the myocardium, these mediators are part of a myokine response that stimulates resident immune cells and attracts circulating cells to orchestrate the extracellular matrix reorganization. By only allowing 6 hours of higher rate pacing (90 beats/min between 11:30 pm and 5:30 am), the risk of inducing eccentric remodeling with a reduction in ejection fraction is low while preserving the hemodynamic and tissue reorganization benefits that may help reduce the burden off amyloid deposits over time. Although Figure 2B suggests that the thickness of the proximal septum and ventricular mass may have regressed at 3 months, this change is well within the margin of error for echocardiographic assessments. However, in patients with heart failure and preserved ejection fraction, the structural effect of prolonged accelerated pacing was clearly evident in a blinded assessment of echocardiograms.10

It cannot be overemphasized that our patient received an advanced physiological pacing system with the in-build redundancy of left bundle area pacing to allow for the safe delivery of accelerated pacing, as shown in Figure 3. Traditional right ventricular pacing would carry an inherent risk of added mechanical dyssynchrony with higher filling pressures that increase the risk for heart failure and atrial fibrillation. It is notable that the QRS duration was significantly shortened with pacing to suggest that ventricular activation was indeed resynchronized when compared with the intrinsic left bundle branch block, as shown in Figure 1. Although the effects of CRT and accelerated pacing cannot be separated in our case, it appears less likely that CRT alone would provide such a profound clinical benefit in a patient with a preserved ejection fraction.

Efficacy of Accelerated Pacing in Other Patient Groups With Restricted Filling

The myPACE trial challenged the “lower heart rate is better” paradigm by testing the effects of continuous personalized accelerated physiological pacing in 107 patients with heart failure and preserved ejection fraction with pre-existing pacemakers. When compared with the standard lower-rate setting of 60 beats/min, accelerated pacing improved health status, quality of life, NT-proBNP levels, and physical activity and reduced the burden of atrial fibrillation.1 In an accelerated pacing study in 7 patients with nonobstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy with pacemakers, 3 months of myPACE+ improved their NYHA functional class, health status, and NT-proBNP levels. Median peak Vo2 rose from 14 to 17 mL/kg/min, and pacemaker-detected daily physical activity increased by 1 hour. The left atrial volume index significantly decreased to suggest effective atrial decongestion.2 Similar to our patient with transthyretin amyloid amyloidosis with a history of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation, no pacemaker-detected arrhythmias were documented.

Summary

Personalized accelerated physiological pacing with rate changes may not only improve baseline hemodynamics and symptoms, but could present an opportunity to reduce the burden of myocardial amyloidosis by normalizing myocardial tissue properties. Larger and longer studies with multimodal assessments of functional, biomarker, health status, and structural measures are needed to test this hypothesis.
