LONP2 Enhances Cervical Cancer Growth and Metastatic Potential by Upregulating PLS3 and Activating PI3K/AKT Signaling
Authors: Ying Yang, Jing Sun, Liu Tian, Jing Liao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to investigate the role of LONP2 in cervical cancer progression and elucidate the underlying signaling mechanisms.MethodsHeLa cervical cancer cells were transduced with lentiviral vectors to overexpress LONP2 or simultaneously overexpress LONP2 and knock down plastin 3 (PLS3). Protein expression was assessed by Western blotting. Cell viability, apoptosis, migration, and invasion were evaluated using CCK-8 assays, flow cytometry, and Transwell assays, respectively. The involvement of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway was examined by detecting the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT. A nude mouse xenograft model was established to validate the in vitro findings.ResultsLONP2 overexpression significantly increased PLS3 protein expression in cervical cancer cells. Functionally, LONP2 promoted cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumor growth while suppressing apoptosis. Knockdown of PLS3 markedly attenuated these tumor-promoting effects both in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, LONP2 overexpression enhanced the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT without significantly affecting total PI3K or AKT expression, indicating activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Importantly, silencing PLS3 effectively reversed LONP2-induced PI3K/AKT pathway activation. Consistent findings were observed in xenograft tumors, where PLS3 knockdown suppressed tumor growth and reduced p-PI3K and p-AKT expression.ConclusionLONP2 promotes cervical cancer progression by upregulating PLS3 expression and activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. The LONP2/PLS3/PI3K-AKT signaling axis represents a novel mechanism driving cervical cancer progression and may serve as a potential therapeutic target for cervical cancer treatment.

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains the fourth most common malignancy and one of the leading causes of cancer-related death among women worldwide. Approximately 527,600 new cases and 265,600 deaths are reported annually, with the majority occurring in developing countries.1 Although the implementation of human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccination, cervical cytology screening, and HPV DNA testing has significantly improved prevention and early detection, the burden of cervical cancer remains substantial, particularly in China, where both incidence and mortality continue to rise and the disease is increasingly affecting younger women.2 Accumulating evidence indicates that cervical carcinogenesis is a complex, multistep process involving the interaction of viral, genetic, and molecular factors. Persistent infection with high-risk HPV is recognized as the primary etiological factor;3 however, HPV infection alone is insufficient to drive malignant transformation. Multiple oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes have been shown to participate in cervical cancer initiation and progression through diverse biological pathways.4 Nevertheless, the molecular mechanisms underlying cervical cancer progression remain incompletely understood.

Lon proteases are evolutionarily conserved ATP-dependent proteases responsible for maintaining cellular protein homeostasis through the degradation of damaged or misfolded proteins. The Lon protease family consists of two members, LONP1 and LONP2, which are predominantly localized in mitochondria and peroxisomes, respectively.5 To date, most studies have focused on LONP1, and increasing evidence has demonstrated its involvement in cancer development and progression. For example, LONP1 is significantly upregulated in gastric cancer and functions as a key regulator of tumor metabolic reprogramming.6 Elevated LONP1 expression has also been reported in colorectal cancer, where it is associated with tumor invasion and poor clinical outcomes.7 In cervical cancer, LONP1 overexpression has been identified as an adaptive mechanism that supports tumor cell bioenergetics, proliferation, and survival, whereas its inhibition suppresses tumor growth-related phenotypes.8 Given the high structural similarity between LONP1 and LONP2, particularly within their conserved functional domains,5 it is conceivable that LONP2 may exert analogous oncogenic functions. However, the biological role and molecular mechanisms of LONP2 in cervical cancer remain largely unexplored.

Our previous study demonstrated that LONP2 is markedly upregulated in cervical cancer tissues and is closely associated with pathological type, tumor grade, clinical stage, and poor patient prognosis. Functional analyses further revealed that LONP2 promotes cervical cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion while suppressing apoptosis, suggesting that LONP2 acts as a potential oncogenic factor in cervical cancer.9 However, the downstream molecular mechanisms responsible for these effects have not yet been elucidated.

Plastin 3 (PLS3) is an actin-bundling protein implicated in cytoskeletal remodeling, cell motility, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and tumor progression in multiple malignancies. Previous studies have reported that aberrant PLS3 expression contributes to tumor invasion, metastasis, and unfavorable clinical outcomes.10,11 Moreover, activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway has been identified as a major downstream event associated with PLS3-mediated tumor progression.12 These findings prompted us to investigate whether PLS3 serves as a downstream effector of LONP2 in cervical cancer.

In the present study, we explored the molecular mechanism by which LONP2 promotes cervical cancer progression. We demonstrate that LONP2 upregulates PLS3 expression, leading to activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and enhancement of cervical cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumor growth. Our findings identify a previously unrecognized LONP2/PLS3/PI3K-AKT regulatory axis in cervical cancer and provide new insights into the molecular basis of cervical cancer progression. These results suggest that targeting the LONP2–PLS3 signaling cascade may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for cervical cancer.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

The human cervical cancer cell line HeLa was obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin solution. Cells were maintained in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2 at 37°C.

Cell Transfection

Short hairpin RNA targeting PLS3 (shPLS3) and a scrambled negative control (shNC) were synthesized by Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). The target sequences were as follows: shPLS3: 5′-GCCCTGGTAATCTTACAGTTA-3′, shNC: 5′-CCTAAGGTTAAGTCGCCCTCG-3′

The oligonucleotides were cloned into the pSLenti-U6-[shRNA]-CMV-EGFP-F2A-Puro-WPRE lentiviral vector according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For LONP2 overexpression, the full-length human LONP2 coding sequence was inserted into the pcSLenti-EF1-mCherry-P2A-BSR-CMV-LONP2-3×FLAG-WPRE vector.

Recombinant lentiviruses, including LV-LONP2, LV-NC, LV-shPLS3, and LV-shNC, were packaged and produced by Obio Technology (Shanghai, China). HeLa cells were transduced with the indicated lentiviral vectors, and transfection efficiency was verified by Western blot analysis.

Western Blot Analysis

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, China). Cells were washed twice with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and lysed on ice for 30 min. Lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C, and the supernatants were collected for protein quantification.

Equal amounts of protein (50 μg) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. After blocking, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against LONP2 (18035-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:500), PLS3 (DF13459, Affinity, 1:500), PI3K (ab191606, Abcam, 1:1000), phosphorylated PI3K (p-PI3K; ab182651, Abcam, 1:800), AKT (AF0836, Affinity, 1:800), phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT; AF0832, Affinity, 1:400), and β-actin (100166-MM10, Sino Biological, 1:1000). Subsequently, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (ab205718 or ab205719, Abcam, 1:4000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Beyotime, China). Briefly, HeLa cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. The culture medium was then replaced with 100 μL of 10% CCK-8 working solution, followed by incubation at 37°C for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated independently three times.

Flow Cytometric Analysis of Apoptosis

Cell apoptosis was evaluated using a PE Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (559763, BD Biosciences, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, cells were harvested, washed twice with cold PBS, and resuspended in 500 μL binding buffer. Subsequently, 5 μL Annexin V-PE and 10 μL 7-AAD were added, and the samples were incubated in the dark at 4°C for 10 min. Apoptotic cells were analyzed using a BD FACScan flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and at least 1 × 106 events were collected for each sample.

Transwell Migration and Invasion Assays

Cell migration and invasion assays were performed using 24-well Transwell chambers with 8-μm pore-size polycarbonate membranes (Corning, USA). For migration assays, 1×105 transfected cells suspended in 100 μL serum-free medium were seeded into the upper chambers. For invasion assays, the upper chambers were precoated with Matrigel (1 mg/mL; Corning, USA) before cell seeding. The lower chambers were filled with 600 μL DMEM containing 10% FBS.

After incubation for 48 h at 37°C, non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were removed with cotton swabs. Cells that migrated or invaded through the membrane were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 20 min, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and photographed under a light microscope (Olympus, Japan). Cells were counted in three randomly selected fields for each membrane.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Paraffin-embedded xenograft tumor tissues were sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. After deparaffinization, rehydration, antigen retrieval, and blocking, tissue sections were incubated with primary antibodies against LONP2 (1:100), PLS3 (1:50), PI3K (1:50), p-PI3K (1:50), AKT (1:50), and p-AKT (1:50). Sections were subsequently incubated with the corresponding secondary antibodies for 30 min at 37°C and counterstained with hematoxylin.

Immunostained sections were independently evaluated by two pathologists blinded to the experimental groups using the German Immunoreactive Score (IRS) system.12 Staining intensity was scored as follows: negative = 0, weak = 1, moderate = 2, and strong = 3. The percentage of positively stained tumor cells was scored as: 0% = 0, 1–10% = 1, 11–50% = 2, 51–80% = 3, and 81–100% = 4. The final IHC score was calculated as the product of the staining intensity score and the percentage score.

In vivo Xenograft Assay

All animal experiments were approved by the Experimental Animal Ethics Committee of Xinjiang Medical University (Approval No. IACUC-JT-20240415-99). Female BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-h light/dark cycle at 22 ± 2°C and 60–80% relative humidity. HeLa cells transduced with LV-NC, LV-LONP2, orLV-LONP2+LV-shPLS3 were harvested and subcutaneously injected into the right axillary region of the mice (n = 6 per group). Tumor length (L) and width (W) were measured every 5 days using digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated according to the formula: V = (L × W2)/2.

At the experimental endpoint (28 days after tumor cell inoculation), mice were deeply anesthetized by inhalation of isoflurane (5% for induction and 2–3% for maintenance in oxygen) to minimize pain and distress. Following confirmation of an adequate depth of anesthesia by the absence of pedal withdrawal and corneal reflexes, the animals were euthanized by cervical dislocation performed by trained personnel. Death was confirmed by the absence of respiration and heartbeat prior to tissue collection. Xenograft tumors were then excised, photographed, weighed, and subjected to histopathological and immunohistochemical analyses. All anesthesia and euthanasia procedures were conducted in accordance with the recommendations of the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA) Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals (2020 Edition) and institutional animal welfare regulations.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed independently at least three times. Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 19.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons among multiple groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

PLS3 Mediates LONP2-Induced Activation of the PI3K/AKT Signaling Pathway

Previous studies have reported that PLS3 promotes tumor progression through activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Therefore, we investigated whether the LONP2-mediated upregulation of PLS3 contributes to PI3K/AKT pathway activation in cervical cancer cells.

Western blot analysis revealed that overexpression of LONP2 did not significantly alter the total protein levels of PI3K or AKT. However, the levels of phosphorylated PI3K (p-PI3K) and phosphorylated AKT (p-AKT) were markedly increased in LV-LONP2-transduced cells (Figure 3E and F). Notably, silencing PLS3 significantly reduced the expression of p-PI3K and p-AKT in LONP2-overexpressing cells, while total PI3K and AKT levels remained largely unchanged. These results suggest that LONP2 activates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway primarily through the upregulation of PLS3.

Mice implanted with LV-LONP2-transduced cells developed significantly larger tumors than those in the Control and LV-NC groups, as evidenced by increased tumor weight and tumor volume (Figure 4B and C). In contrast, knockdown of PLS3 markedly suppressed tumor growth in LONP2-overexpressing xenografts, resulting in significantly reduced tumor weight and volume.

To further investigate the underlying mechanism, immunohistochemical staining was performed on xenograft tissues. As shown in Figure 4D and E, LONP2 overexpression increased PLS3 expression and was accompanied by elevated levels of p-PI3K and p-AKT. Conversely, PLS3 knockdown substantially reduced p-PI3K and p-AKT expression in LONP2-overexpressing tumors. Consistent with the in vitro findings, total PI3K and AKT expression exhibited relatively minor changes among the experimental groups.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that LONP2 promotes cervical cancer growth in vivo through a mechanism involving PLS3-dependent activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway.

Discussion

LONP2 is a member of the highly conserved Lon protease family and functions as an ATP-dependent protease involved in maintaining protein homeostasis through the degradation of oxidized or damaged proteins.5,13–15 Compared with the extensively studied mitochondrial protease LONP1, the biological functions of the peroxisomal protease LONP2 remain poorly understood, particularly in cancer. Although our previous study demonstrated that LONP2 is overexpressed in cervical cancer and is associated with adverse clinicopathological characteristics and poor prognosis,9 the molecular mechanisms underlying its oncogenic activity have not been elucidated. In the present study, we provide evidence that LONP2 promotes cervical cancer progression through a PLS3-dependent mechanism and identify activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway as a key downstream event.

Functional analyses demonstrated that LONP2 overexpression significantly enhanced cervical cancer cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and xenograft growth while suppressing apoptosis. These findings are consistent with our previous observations and further support a tumor-promoting role of LONP2 in cervical cancer.9 More importantly, rescue experiments revealed that silencing PLS3 markedly attenuated the effects of LONP2 on malignant cellular phenotypes both in vitro and in vivo, indicating that PLS3 serves as a critical downstream mediator of LONP2-driven tumor progression.

PLS3 is an actin-bundling protein belonging to the plastin family and plays important roles in cytoskeletal remodeling, cell motility, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition.16–18 Tight regulation of PLS3 expression is essential for normal cellular function, whereas dysregulated PLS3 expression has been implicated in the development and progression of multiple malignancies.19 Elevated PLS3 expression has been associated with poor prognosis in non-small cell lung cancer and has been linked to enhanced tumor aggressiveness and therapeutic resistance in several cancer types.20–22 Moreover, oncogenic roles of PLS3 have also been reported in gastric cancer and colorectal cancer, where it promotes tumor invasion, migration, and metastatic dissemination.23–25 Consistent with these observations, our study demonstrated that LONP2 overexpression significantly increased PLS3 protein expression, whereas knockdown of PLS3 effectively reversed the tumor-promoting effects of LONP2. These findings suggest that PLS3 is an important effector molecule involved in LONP2-mediated cervical cancer progression.

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is one of the most frequently activated oncogenic pathways in human cancers and plays a central role in regulating cell proliferation, survival, metabolism, migration, and therapeutic resistance.12 Aberrant activation of PI3K/AKT signaling has been reported in a wide variety of malignancies, including cervical cancer.26,27 In HPV-positive cervical cancer cells, activation of this pathway contributes to virus–host interactions and promotes tumor progression.28 Furthermore, dysregulation of PI3K/AKT signaling has been closely associated with treatment resistance and unfavorable clinical outcomes.29 Therefore, identification of upstream regulators of this pathway remains of considerable interest.

Previous bioinformatic analyses have suggested a potential association between PLS3 expression and PI3K/AKT signaling activity in pancreatic cancer.30 In the present study, we experimentally validated this relationship in cervical cancer cells. We found that LONP2 overexpression significantly increased the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT without substantially affecting total PI3K or AKT expression, indicating activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. Notably, knockdown of PLS3 effectively abrogated LONP2-induced phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT, both in cultured cells and xenograft tumors. These findings support a model in which LONP2 promotes cervical cancer progression by upregulating PLS3 expression and subsequently activating PI3K/AKT signaling.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, although our data demonstrate that LONP2 positively regulates PLS3 expression, the precise molecular mechanism linking LONP2 to PLS3 remains unclear. Whether LONP2 directly regulates PLS3 transcription, protein stability, or other upstream signaling events warrants further investigation. Second, all functional experiments were performed using a single cervical cancer cell line, and validation in additional cervical cancer models would strengthen the generalizability of our findings. Third, clinical validation of the LONP2–PLS3 signaling axis in patient specimens was beyond the scope of the present study and should be addressed in future studies.

Despite these limitations, our findings identify a previously unrecognized LONP2/PLS3/PI3K-AKT signaling axis in cervical cancer and provide new mechanistic insights into the oncogenic functions of LONP2. These results suggest that targeting this signaling cascade may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for cervical cancer.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the present study demonstrates that LONP2 promotes cervical cancer progression by upregulating PLS3 expression and activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. Functional inhibition of PLS3 attenuated LONP2-induced cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumor growth, highlighting the critical role of PLS3 in mediating the oncogenic effects of LONP2. Collectively, our findings reveal a novel LONP2/PLS3/PI3K-AKT regulatory axis in cervical cancer and provide potential molecular targets for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies.
