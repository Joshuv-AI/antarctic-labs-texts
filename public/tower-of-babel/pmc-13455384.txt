Microhardness and structural changes in calcium silicate-based sealers after short-term exposure to EDTA and organic acids: an in vitro study
Authors: Sedanur Candas Gulcu, Suleyman Serdar Pazarlioglu, Hasan Gokce, Nevin Kartal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCalcium silicate–based root canal sealers are widely used because of their bioactivity and favorable properties. However, their resistance to chemical challenge and the role of chelating and acidic solutions in retreatment remain unclear. This study evaluated the effects of short-term EDTA and organic acids exposure on the microhardness and structure of two sealers (CeraSeal and TotalFill BC Sealer) and explored the underlying mechanisms.MethodsA total of 160 disc-shaped samples (80 per sealer) were prepared and incubated at 37 °C and 100% relative humidity. Vickers microhardness was measured weekly. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses were performed at weekly intervals using different samples, starting from the first measurable microhardness and continuing during material maturation. The final microhardness was defined as the plateau in value.After the final microhardness was reached, the samples were immersed for 5 min in 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid, 10% citric acid, 10% formic acid, or distilled water to simulate retreatment conditions. Postexposure microhardness, phase changes, and surface morphology were evaluated via Vickers hardness testing, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM), respectively. Statistical analyses included the Shapiro–Wilk test, t tests or nonparametric equivalents, one-way and Welch’s ANOVA, and linear mixed model analysis (p < 0.05).ResultsCeraSeal appeared to reach its final microhardness earlier than TotalFill BC Sealer did, whereas TotalFill BC Sealer tended to exhibit higher ultimate microhardness values. Exposure to the test solutions significantly reduced the microhardness of both sealers (p < 0.001), with the greatest reductions observed in the formic acid (58%) and citric acid (52%) groups. XRD and SEM analyses suggested dissolution of the calcium silicate hydrate and calcium carbonate phases, increased surface porosity, and a possible tetragonal-to-monoclinic zirconia transformation in TotalFill BC Sealer, whereas no clear evidence of zirconia phase transformation was observed in CeraSeal.ConclusionsWithin the limitations of this in vitro study, TotalFill BC Sealer presented higher final microhardness values; however, it was more susceptible to structural degradation following exposure to the test solutions, which was associated with a marked reduction in microhardness values. In contrast, CeraSeal, despite demonstrating a lower final microhardness, exhibited comparatively smaller percentage reductions after solution exposure, suggesting relatively greater resistance to solution-induced degradation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08683-y.Keywords: Calcium silicate sealer, Microhardness, Organic acids, XRD, SEM, Retreatment

Introduction

Successful endodontic treatment relies on effective chemomechanical debridement of the root canal system, followed by obturation with materials that provide long-term sealing and biological compatibility [1]. In recent years, calcium silicate–based root canal sealers have been increasingly preferred because of their bioactivity, dimensional stability, and ability to set in moist environments [2]. These materials form hydration products such as calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) and hydroxyapatite, which enhance the sealing ability and interfacial adaptation to dentin [3].

Despite their favorable clinical performance, the retreatment of canals filled with calcium silicate–based sealers remains a significant challenge. These materials penetrate dentinal tubules and undergo progressive crystallization over time, which increases their mechanical resistance and limits retrievability [4]. In cases of endodontic failure, incomplete removal of residual sealer may impair disinfection by restricting the penetration of irrigants and intracanal medicaments into complex canal anatomies [5]. Therefore, strategies that facilitate the weakening and removal of these materials are clinically relevant.

Chemical adjuncts are frequently employed during retreatment and may facilitate the removal of root canal filling materials [6]. While several organic solvents are effective for treating gutta-percha, their cytotoxicity and limited efficacy against bioceramic sealers restrict their clinical applicability [7, 8]. In contrast, solutions such as ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), citric acid, and formic acid interact with calcium-containing phases and hydration products, suggesting a potential role in altering the structural integrity of calcium silicate–based sealers [9, 10]. However, comparative data regarding their effectiveness and the mechanisms responsible for solution-induced structural degradation remain limited.

Microhardness is a clinically relevant parameter that reflects a material’s resistance to deformation and provides insight into its structural integrity and susceptibility to chemical degradation [11].

Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate whether short-term exposure to clinically relevant test solutions facilitates the degradation of calcium silicate–based root canal sealers and to determine which solution is more effective in promoting structural degradation of these materials. Furthermore, this study aimed to compare sealers with different chemical compositions in terms of their setting and maturation behavior, final microhardness, and susceptibility to solution-induced degradation to determine whether compositional differences influence their mechanical properties and resistance to chemical degradation. The setting and maturation behavior, microhardness development, and structural alterations of the CeraSeal and TotalFill BC Sealer were investigated following exposure to 17% EDTA, 10% citric acid (CA), and 10% formic acid (FA). The null hypothesis was that exposure to different chemical environments would not significantly affect the setting characteristics, mechanical properties, or crystalline stability of the tested sealers, irrespective of their compositional differences.

Materials and methods

This laboratory study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Laboratory Studies in Endodontology (PRILE) 2021 guidelines [12]. A PRILE 2021 flowchart illustrating the study design and reporting framework is provided in Supplementary Figure S1.

Materials

Two commercially available premixed calcium silicate–based root canal sealers were evaluated: CeraSeal (Meta Biomed, Cheongju, Korea; lot no. CSL2409021) and TotalFill BC Sealer (FKG Dentaire, La Chaux-de-Fonds, Switzerland; lot no. 23004SP). Materials were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

The test solutions used in the study included 17% EDTA (Cerkamed, Poland), 10% citric acid prepared from 99% citric acid powder (Ak-Bel Kimya, Bursa, Turkey), and 10% formic acid prepared from 85% formic acid solution (Ak-Bel Kimya, Bursa, Turkey). Dilution procedures to obtain the final concentrations were performed by Deren Chemicals (Turkey).

Sample preparation and setting conditions

To standardize the specimen dimensions, a total of 80 samples were prepared for each sealer group via custom-designed plastic molds (5 mm in diameter and 2 mm in height) fabricated via three-dimensional printing. Prior to use, the molds were cleaned with ethyl alcohol and fixed onto glass slabs. The sealers were injected into the molds according to the manufacturers’ instructions. A glass slide was gently pressed over the molds to remove excess material and obtain flat and uniform surfaces. The samples were then transferred for incubation.

All the samples were stored at 37 °C and 100% relative humidity until the final microhardness was reached. The final microhardness was considered to be reached when the weekly microhardness values no longer clearly increased but reached a plateau.

Experimental groups

For each sealer, eight samples were used for weekly microhardness measurements during the maturation period. The same samples were measured at each time point to monitor the progression of microhardness until the final value was reached.

Following the completion of the maturation period, an additional thirty-two samples from each sealer group were allocated to four subgroups (n = 8) for exposure to the test solutions: 10% citric acid, 10% formic acid, 17% EDTA, and distilled water (control). The samples were distributed equally among the experimental groups.

The remaining forty samples in each sealer group were allocated for X-ray diffraction (XRD) analyses at different time points during the setting period and after exposure to the test solutions, as well as for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analyses following solution exposure.

To ensure accurate structural characterization and to avoid potential interference from mechanical testing, separate samples were used for each analytical technique and time point.

Microhardness testing

Microhardness measurements were performed via the Vickers hardness test. A diamond pyramid indenter was applied to the upper surface of each sample under a load of 100 × g for 10 s. Three indentations were made on each sample, with a minimum distance of 1 mm between adjacent indentations to prevent interaction effects, and the mean value was recorded. Microhardness measurements were performed on coded specimens by an operator blinded to the solution groups.

Weekly measurements were conducted on the same samples throughout the maturation period until the final microhardness was reached. Following exposure to the test solutions, the same testing protocol was applied to assess changes in microhardness.

XRD analysis

The crystalline phase evolution of the sealers was analyzed by XRD at weekly intervals using different samples at each time point, starting from the first measurable microhardness and continuing during material maturation. For each time point, one sample per group was analyzed. Although the measurements were performed weekly, representative diffraction patterns obtained at selected intervals (two-week periods) are presented to better illustrate the phase evolution trends. Rietveld refinement was performed only at these representative time points, and the refinement quality was assessed via the weighted profile R factor (Rwp) to ensure an acceptable fit. The corresponding residual plots and Rwp values are provided in Supplementary Figure S2.

XRD analyses were performed via a PANalytical X’Pert Pro diffractometer with Cu–Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) operated at 45 kV and 40 mA over a 2θ range of 10–90°. Phase identification was carried out via HighScore Plus software in combination with the ICDD database. The quantitative phase composition and structural variations were further evaluated via Rietveld refinement.

After determination of the final microhardness, the samples exposed to the test solutions were analyzed via the same protocol to assess the phase transitions and changes in crystallinity.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

The surface morphology of the sealers was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) (Thermo Scientific Quattro S, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Analyses were conducted under high-vacuum conditions (5.81 × 10⁻⁴ Pa) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and a working distance of 10 mm. Images were obtained at a magnification of ×3000. Prior to SEM examination, all the samples were sputter-coated with a thin gold–palladium layer (~ 10 nm) to improve the surface conductivity. SEM observations were performed to qualitatively compare the baseline surface morphology and postexposure alterations following solution exposure.

All samples were coded prior to analysis, and the operators performing SEM and XRD analyses were blinded to group allocation.

Application of test solutions

Test solutions (17% EDTA, 10% citric acid, 10% formic acid, and distilled water) were applied to the upper surface of each sample via a micropipette (20 µL per sample), ensuring complete coverage of the 5 mm diameter surface without overflow. Solutions were applied without agitation and were not renewed during the 5-minute exposure period to standardize the contact conditions. All procedures were performed at room temperature to minimize potential pH fluctuations.

Following exposure, the samples were rinsed with 10 mL of distilled water, gently air-dried, and stored at 37 °C and 100% relative humidity for 24 h to allow surface re-equilibration prior to further analyses.

Statistical analysis

To verify the adequacy of this sample size for the primary outcome of the study (microhardness values), a sensitivity power analysis was conducted via G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf). This analysis confirmed that a sample size of n = 8 per experimental group provided 80% power (1–β) at a 0.05 alpha level to detect a large effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.40) via an F test framework.

The normality of the data distribution was assessed via the Shapiro–Wilk test. For comparisons between two independent groups, the independent samples t test was applied for normally distributed data, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used otherwise. Comparisons among the four solution groups were performed via one-way ANOVA for parametric data and Welch’s ANOVA when the homogeneity of variance was violated, followed by Tukey’s HSD test or Tamhane’s T2 post hoc test, as appropriate. For dependent group comparisons, the paired samples t test or Wilcoxon signed-rank test was applied. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

To evaluate the effects of multiple variables and their interactions, including the longitudinal within-sample data from weekly measurements (time effect), a linear mixed model (LMM) was employed. The detailed fixed-effects results are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Results

Microhardness progression of the CeraSeal and TotalFill BC Sealer over time

The initial microhardness of CeraSeal became measurable in the third week following placement and increased progressively until it reached a plateau at week 9. In contrast, the TotalFill BC Sealer exhibited measurable microhardness from week 5 onward and reached its final microhardness at week 14. At the final measurement time point, TotalFill BC Sealer demonstrated significantly higher microhardness values (95.5 ± 1.4 VHN) than did CeraSeal (34.1 ± 1.5 VHN) (p < 0.001).

Evolution of crystalline phases in root canal sealers (XRD findings)

XRD analysis revealed progressive hydration in both sealers over time, characterized by an increase in the calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) content. In CeraSeal, increased C–S–H formation was accompanied by gradual changes in zirconia polymorphism, with a relative increase in the tetragonal phase. In TotalFill BC Sealer, XRD analysis revealed progressive hydroxyapatite formation and a stable zirconia phase distribution throughout the maturation period (Figs. 2 and 3; Tables 1 and 2).

Effect of test solutions on microhardness

The baseline microhardness values did not differ significantly among the solution groups for either sealer (p > 0.05). Following exposure to the test solutions, the microhardness decreased significantly in all the groups (p < 0.001). The greatest reductions were observed in the citric acid and formic acid groups, whereas EDTA caused a moderate decrease. Distilled water resulted in the smallest changes in the microhardness (Table 3). Within-group comparisons confirmed statistically significant reductions in microhardness following solution exposure for both sealers (p < 0.001).

Discussion

Calcium silicate–based root canal sealers are known to form hydroxyapatite-like crystals at the dentin interface and to penetrate dentinal tubules after setting. While these properties improve the sealing ability and mechanical performance, they also increase the difficulty of removing these materials from the canal [13, 14]. Previous studies have shown that calcium silicate– and MTA-based materials exhibit increased solubility and reduced microhardness under acidic conditions [15–17]. Recent studies have reported that chelating and acidic solutions may facilitate the removal of calcium silicate–based sealers from root canals and help reestablish apical patency [9, 18]. Therefore, acidic solutions should be evaluated not only in terms of their effects on dentin but also in terms of their effects on the removal of calcium silicate–based sealers from the canal.

In this context, the present study evaluated the resistance of CeraSeal and TotalFill BC Sealer to 17% EDTA, 10% citric acid, and 10% formic acid, as well as the degradation mechanisms induced by these solutions. The experimental model was not designed to directly simulate clinical retreatment procedures or actual sealer removal under canal-like conditions but rather to investigate the structural response of fully set sealers to exposure to the test solutions. Accordingly, the study design was designed to reflect the possible behavior of these materials when exposed to chelating and acidic irrigants within the root canal environment. The exposure time was limited to 5 min to represent clinically relevant irrigation durations and to minimize dentin damage, as it has been reported that exposure times exceeding 10 min may adversely affect dentin structure [17].

Microhardness is an important parameter that reflects a material’s resistance to deformation and provides indirect information about material properties [11]. A decrease in microhardness is associated with material softening and may facilitate its removal from the canal [19]. Therefore, the microhardness test was used to evaluate the effects of acidic environments and to assess changes in the structural integrity of the materials. To better simulate clinical conditions, only one surface of each sample was exposed to the test solution, and the samples were incubated at 37 °C and 100% relative humidity to ensure complete setting and maturation [19, 20].

Clear differences were observed between the two sealers in terms of their setting behavior and resistance to solution-induced degradation. CeraSeal reached its final microhardness earlier, whereas TotalFill BC Sealer presented higher final microhardness values. These differences may be explained by compositional variations affecting hydration kinetics [21]. These findings suggest that aluminum-containing phases in CeraSeal accelerate early hydration reactions and support C–S–H gel formation [22]. In contrast, phosphate-containing phases in TotalFill BC Sealer may promote hydroxyapatite formation over time, contributing to microstructural densification and increased mechanical strength [23, 24].

Both materials showed a significant reduction in microhardness after exposure to the test solutions, indicating that calcium silicate–based sealers are susceptible to chemical degradation. The greatest reductions in microhardness were observed in the citric acid and formic acid groups, whereas the effect of EDTA was more limited. In the present study, the pH values of the test solutions were not directly measured; therefore, the following mechanistic interpretations should be considered literature-based. Citric acid has been reported in the literature as a tricarboxylic organic acid that can exhibit pH values in the range of approximately 0.8–1.9 depending on its concentration [25]. Formic acid, the simplest carboxylic acid, has a pKa of approximately 3.75 and is capable of creating an acidic environment [26]. The observed effects may be related to the acidic nature of the citric acid and formic acid solutions, as acidic environments have been shown to increase calcium dissolution and destabilize calcium-containing hydration products in cement-based materials [27]. However, the greater effect observed with formic acid cannot be explained solely by its acidity. Owing to its smaller molecular size, formic acid may diffuse more effectively into the material matrix and interact beyond the surface [28]. EDTA, on the other hand, is a calcium-chelating agent [29], and it has been reported that its pH in dental formulations is near neutral to slightly alkaline [30]; this may explain its relatively limited ability to destabilize hydration phases. These findings are consistent with the literature and suggest that, under short-term exposure, organic acids may be more effective than chelating agents in degrading the cement-like matrix of calcium silicate–based sealers [27].

Structural analyses provided insight into the mechanisms underlying the observed reduction in mechanical strength. The XRD findings revealed that acidic degradation is associated primarily with the dissolution of calcium silicate hydrate and carbonate-containing hydration products. In TotalFill BC Sealer, the characteristic changes observed in the XRD patterns after exposure to the test solutions are consistent with a possible tetragonal-to-monoclinic zirconia phase transformation, characterized by a decrease in the tetragonal peak at approximately 2θ ≈ 30° and an increase in monoclinic peaks in the 2θ ≈ 28–31° range [31]. Considering the reinforcing role of the tetragonal phase, its destabilization may explain the pronounced reduction in microhardness observed in this material [32]. In contrast, no significant zirconia phase transformation was detected in the CeraSeal samples, suggesting the presence of a more stable zirconia structure.

SEM analysis revealed findings consistent with increased surface porosity and erosion, particularly after exposure to citric and formic acids. In contrast, the distilled water group did not present the pronounced porosity or structural deterioration observed under acidic conditions. This suggests that the reduction in microhardness in the distilled water group may be related to more limited and superficial changes. In this study, SEM analysis was performed qualitatively and based on visual observations; therefore, the findings should be considered indicative rather than definitive evidence of microstructural changes.

One of the main strengths of the present study is its integrated methodological approach. The combined use of microhardness testing with XRD and SEM analyses allowed for a more comprehensive evaluation of the effects of test solutions on calcium silicate–based sealers. This approach enables not only the assessment of the extent of changes in mechanical properties but also the elucidation of the underlying degradation mechanisms.

In clinical practice, retreatment procedures may be required months or even years after the initial root canal treatment; this is supported by previous experimental studies simulating both short- and long-term aging conditions [33, 34]. Therefore, the assessment of the final microhardness is clinically relevant. In this context, monitoring the temporal evolution of the microhardness during the maturation process via XRD provided a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between the mechanical properties and phase development, representing an additional strength of the present study.

However, this study has several limitations. The disk-based experimental model does not fully replicate the clinical root canal environment. Although the incubation conditions were standardized, they do not completely reflect the actual moisture conditions of intraradicular dentin. In addition, actual retreatment procedures or sealer removal under canal-like conditions have not been directly evaluated. The qualitative nature of SEM analysis and the absence of semiquantitative or quantitative surface analyses represent further limitations. In addition, the pH values of the test solutions were not directly measured; therefore, mechanistic interpretations regarding acidity should be considered literature-based. These factors should be considered when interpreting the clinical applicability of the findings.

Finally, on the basis of the findings obtained, exposure to test solutions resulted in a significant reduction in microhardness and measurable crystallographic and microstructural changes in both calcium silicate–based sealers; therefore, the null hypothesis was rejected.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, exposure to test solutions resulted in significant reductions in microhardness and induced measurable crystallographic and microstructural alterations in calcium silicate–based root canal sealers. Although citric acid and formic acid had greater effects than EDTA did, this trend may be associated with the acidic properties reported for these solutions in previous studies. The present findings provide a quantitative assessment of these changes across different sealers and relate them to material-specific structural characteristics, particularly differences in composition and phase stability. These results indicate that the response to acidic challenge is material dependent and may influence the effectiveness of sealer removal during retreatment. Further studies evaluating longer exposure times and clinically relevant retreatment conditions are warranted.
