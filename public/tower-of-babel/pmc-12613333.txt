Artificial intelligence in orthopaedic education, training and research: a systematic review
Authors: Bibek Banskota, Rajan Bhusal, Prkash Kumar Yadav, Ashok Kumar Banskota
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) increasingly transforms orthopedic education and research, offering novel surgical training and scientific advancement approaches. However, these AI applications' scope, impact, and limitations require comprehensive evaluation. Aim to systematically review AI technologies' practical applications, benefits, limitations, and future directions in orthopedic education and research.MethodsThis systematic review was registered with PROSPERO (CRD420251064596). A comprehensive search of PubMed, Embase, and Scopus was conducted. Studies reporting AI technologies—such as machine learning, virtual reality (VR) simulation, generative AI, and adaptive learning systems—were included in orthopedic training or research. Due to heterogeneity in study designs and outcome measures, data were extracted and synthesized narratively.ResultsAI technologies consistently improve training efficiency, personalized learning, and objective skill assessments. VR simulation and machine learning-based feedback tools enhanced technical proficiency and significantly reduced learning curves. Adaptive learning platforms enabled tailored educational pathways. However, generative AI applications remain nascent, with notable concerns regarding content accuracy and bias. Across studies, key limitations included data bias, over-reliance on automated systems, high implementation costs, and a lack of longitudinal and real-world validation. Most studies were limited to simulation-based environments with insufficient evidence on clinical skill transfer. Ethical, curricular, and regulatory considerations remain underdeveloped.ConclusionsAI holds considerable potential to advance orthopedic education and research by enabling more efficient, equitable, and personalized training. However, its integration must be guided by rigorous validation, ethical standards, and stakeholder collaboration. Hybrid human–AI training models and standardized evaluation metrics are essential to realizing AI's full potential in orthopedics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08162-y.Keywords: Artificial Intelligence, Orthopedic Education, Surgical Training, Machine Learning, Virtual Reality Simulation, Orthopedic Research

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) rapidly transforms orthopedics in clinical care, education, and research. As machine learning (ML), computer vision, predictive analytics, and robotic systems mature, they are being applied across all orthopedic domains – trauma, spine, sports medicine, arthroplasty, Pediatrics, and oncology – to train future surgeons and advance research. These tools can analyze vast "big data" sets and images, enhancing diagnostics and generating predictive models for patient outcomes [1]. In parallel, AI-powered platforms create adaptive learning modules and simulations for trainees. In healthcare, AI has generally improved diagnostic accuracy, risk prediction, and robotic surgery, and it is increasingly used in medical education, especially surgical training​ [2]. In orthopedic training, these advances are being harnessed by all stakeholders – from medical students and residents to fellows, faculty, and researchers – to augment traditional teaching, objective skill assessment, and research methodology.

AI in Orthopaedic Education and Skills Training. AI-based educational tools are emerging throughout the training spectrum. Virtual reality (VR) and augmented reality (AR) surgical simulators, often coupled with AI analytics, let trainees practice complex procedures (e.g., arthroscopy, trauma management, joint replacement) in immersive, risk-free settings [3]​. Recent studies show that residents using VR simulators outperform controls in procedure accuracy, speed, and confidence. Computer vision and ML can objectively track instrument motions and generate novel performance metrics. For example, ML algorithms applied to virtual reality surgical tasks can accurately distinguish novice from expert techniques and provide automated safety, efficiency, and precision feedback [4]. These automated feedback systems overcome the subjective bias of traditional apprenticeship models by giving trainees personalized, data-driven skill assessments​ [5]. Robotic surgery systems (e.g., robot-assisted arthroplasty or spinal navigation) also serve educational roles, as their AI-driven guidance and haptic feedback help teach residents implant planning and execution. In didactics, large language models (LLMs) like ChatGPT and related generative AI tools are beginning to assist learners by generating case vignettes, explanations, and adaptive quizzes. Early studies suggest LLMs can answer some orthopedic board-style questions. Still, currently with moderate accuracy (e.g., ChatGPT v3.5 answered only ~ 45% of upper-extremity exam questions correctly versus 51–76% for trainees)​ [6]. Thus. At the same time, generative AI holds promise for on-demand learning and literature summarization; its clinical reasoning still lags behind human trainees.​[6]Faculty and curriculum directors are beginning to integrate these tools for personalized learning paths and competency-based assessment, but human oversight remains essential. Computer vision algorithms can process medical images (radiographs, CT, MRI) at scale—for example, classifying fractures, segmenting anatomy, or measuring joint alignments—with accuracy rivaling experts' [7]​. Such tools speed up outcome research and may even detect subtle imaging features (e.g., early osteoarthritis or tumor margins) that humans might miss. Predictive analytics and ML models use large clinical datasets to forecast patient outcomes and optimize care paths. Studies have shown that artificial neural networks accurately predict postoperative length of stay, discharge disposition, and functional recovery in joint arthroplasty ​ [8]. These models can help educators and researchers identify factors affecting learning curves and patient outcomes. Researchers employ AI for data mining and literature review: natural language processing can scan thousands of publications and Electronic Health Record (EHR) notes, aiding in hypothesis generation.

Key stakeholders are involved in all these domains. Trainees (students, residents, fellows) interact with AI-driven learning modules and high-fidelity simulators to build skills. Faculty use AI feedback systems to assess surgical techniques and tailor teaching objectively. Researchers employ AI for sophisticated analyses of large data and imaging sets. AI technologies are increasingly integrated into orthopedic education and research. [9]. Machine learning, computer vision, predictive analytics, and robotics are transforming how surgeons are trained – from virtual simulations and objective skill feedback to intelligent tutoring systems – and how orthopedic research is conducted – from imaging analysis to outcome prediction. This review will examine recent advances in these areas, spanning all orthopedic subspecialties and stakeholder roles, to chart practical pathways for using AI to train tomorrow's surgeons.

Methodology

Search strategy and selection criteria

This systematic review was conducted and reported per the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (see Table 1: PRISMA flow chart). The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420251064596. A comprehensive literature search was performed across three electronic databases—PubMed, Embase, and Scopus covering studies published between 2010 and 2025. We restricted the start date of the search to 2010 because this period marks the beginning of widespread and impactful applications of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) in healthcare. Before 2010, the literature was sparse; most studies were exploratory or theoretical. From 2010 onward, advances in computing power, the availability of large datasets, and the development of deep learning frameworks led to a significant increase in high-quality research and practical implementations in clinical settings. [10]. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary MeSH terms and free-text keywords related to artificial intelligence, machine learning, orthopedic education, virtual reality, surgical training, and orthopedic research. Representative terms included "Artificial Intelligence" [MeSH], "Machine Learning" [MeSH], and "Education, Medical" [MeSH], along with keywords such as "orthopedic education," "surgical training," and "orthopedic research." Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to link AI- and ML-related terms with medical education and training-related terms. Searches were limited to articles published in English. Summary of the studies included

Eligibility criteria

We pre-defined inclusion and exclusion criteria to guide study selection. Included studies were peer-reviewed original research articles and review articles published in English from 2010 onward, reporting applications of AI or ML in orthopedic education, surgical training, or related research involving orthopedic trainees or surgeons. Exclusion criteria were editorials, studies outside the domain of AI and unrelated to training/education, conference abstracts without full data, and publications before 2010. These criteria ensured a focused sample directly relevant to training the next generation of surgeons, as recommended for systematic reviews ​ [11].

Selection process

All citations identified in the search were imported into Mendeley reference management software and de-duplicated. Two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts for relevance against the eligibility criteria​. Articles deemed potentially relevant were retrieved in full text. The same two reviewers then independently assessed each full text for final inclusion. Discrepancies at either stage were resolved through discussion, and if needed, a third reviewer adjudicated. With consensus resolution, this dual-review process minimized bias and aligned with evidence-based standards for evidence synthesis​. A PRISMA-style flow diagram was prepared to document the number of records identified, screened, excluded, and included at each stage, and twenty eligible papers were selected.

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of all identified studies and conducted full-text reviews to determine eligibility based on predefined inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies during the selection process were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer. Studies were included if they reported on the application of AI technologies in orthopedic education or research and presented measurable outcomes related to training effectiveness, skill acquisition, or health research advancement. Only peer-reviewed original articles and review articles, published in English, were considered; editorials, conference abstracts without full text, and studies outside the orthopedic domain were excluded. For data extraction, the same two reviewers independently extracted information from the included studies, comparing results to ensure accuracy and consistency. Consensus resolved disagreements, and data fields were iteratively refined to capture emerging themes. The final dataset was checked for completeness and clarity before synthesis, with this rigorous process aimed at minimizing errors and reducing bias.

Synthesis of findings

Given the heterogeneity of study types, outcomes, and interventions, we conducted a qualitative, reflexive thematic synthesis of the extracted data. Findings from included studies were systematically collated, and key concepts and results were identified through open coding. Codes were then iteratively reviewed, refined, and grouped into higher-order themes through a reflexive process, ensuring that the interpretation remained closely grounded in the primary data while allowing for the emergence of novel insights. Throughout the analysis, we engaged in reflexive discussions among the research team to examine potential biases, challenge assumptions, and enhance analytical rigor. The synthesized findings were presented in a narrative format, explicitly linking individual study results to overarching conclusions of the review. [12]. Consistent with established guidelines, evidence was summarized and compared in a coherent narrative highlighting recurring patterns, contrasts, and gaps. Themes were developed inductively, enabling a transparent and rigorous synthesis that remained truthful to the original studies while providing new interpretive perspectives [13].

Quality assessment

We adopted PRISMA-recommended reporting practices​ [14] and recent guidelines for systematic reviews in medical education [15]. Screening and extraction were conducted in duplicate to reduce error​. Findings were integrated through a structured thematic synthesis​. The risk of bias in the included studies was assessed using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS), which evaluates study quality based on three domains: selection (up to 4 stars), comparability (up to 2 stars), and outcome/exposure assessment (up to 3 stars), for a maximum score of 9. This tool allowed for standardized appraisal of methodological quality across the selected studies [16].

Results

The systematic search across PubMed, EMBASE, and Scopus identified 1,166 records published between 2010 and 2025. After automated de-duplication using reference management software, 865 unique records remained. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts, excluding 133 records for not meeting eligibility criteria. The proportion of exclusions at the title/abstract stage appears lower than typical; this reflects the broad nature of initial AI-related search terms capturing studies potentially relevant to orthopedic education. Of the 732 full texts retrieved, 99 could not be obtained, mainly conference abstracts without full data. The remaining 633 articles were assessed in full, and 613 were excluded for reasons including wrong outcome (n = 293), wrong study design (n = 215), and other reasons (n = 105). Twenty studies met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1).

The analysis revealed six key themes that illustrate the integration and impact of artificial intelligence (AI) in orthopedic education and training. These themes are: (a) AI-driven simulation and Virtual Reality in Orthopaedic Training, (b) Adaptive Learning Platforms and Personalized Education Pathways, (c) Computer Vision and Objective Skill Assessment, (d) AI Applications in Orthopaedic Research, (e) Predictive Analytics for Surgical Education and Outcome Prediction, and (f) Challenges and Ethical Considerations in AI Integration. Each theme is described in detail below, highlighting its relevance, current applications, and potential implications for the future of orthopedic education.

AI-Driven simulation and virtual reality in orthopaedic training

Recent work shows that immersive simulation and VR systems can significantly enhance technical skills. For example, a systematic review found that orthopedic trainees using VR simulators generally outperformed controls on key proficiency measures [17].​ Trainees using VR showed better arthroscopy skills and shorter operative times, and reported confidence that VR-acquired skills would transfer to the OR​ [18]. AR/VR platforms also allow the practice of rare procedures – e.g., arthroplasty and fracture fixation – in a risk-free setting​. Generative AI techniques can further enrich these simulations: embedding AI "virtual tutors" or assistants in VR improved learners' comprehension of anatomy and provided dynamic, context-sensitive guidance [19].​ In addition, AI-enhanced simulators can generate a wide range of surgical scenarios (from routine cases to complex complications) with automatic feedback, accelerating learning curves and building trainee confidence​. Emerging AR-based training tools (e.g., an AR-guided MIS simulator) demonstrate excellent accuracy and reproducibility in tasks such as K-wire or screw insertion [20]​, indicating that these approaches can safely augment hands-on practice. Overall, evidence from peer-reviewed studies suggests that AI-driven simulation/VR offers a modern, flexible training adjunct that improves skill acquisition [21].

While AI-driven simulation holds great promise, addressing challenges within each specific application is essential. A key concern is the high cost, which could limit access and create a digital divide [21]. Over-reliance on simulations might also cause a disconnect from real-world clinical variables. The transferability of skills from virtual to physical environments must be continuously validated to ensure patient safety. Finally, the ethical implications of using AI to evaluate trainees must be considered to prevent algorithmic bias from affecting career progression. These technologies should be used thoughtfully alongside traditional methods, focusing on equitable access and rigorous validation [19].

Adaptive learning platforms and personalized education pathways

AI-driven learning platforms are beginning to deliver truly adaptive, individualized education in orthopedics. Recent reviews note that generative models and machine-learning systems can tailor content and pacing to each trainee's needs [22]. For example, large language models can function as on-demand "virtual tutors," providing 24/7 answers, explanations, and interactive case scenarios customized by user performance​. AI tools can analyze a learner's past performance and engagement to identify weaknesses, then dynamically adjust question difficulty or suggest focused modules​. In practical terms, systems like Med-PaLM or ChatGPT have demonstrated excellent accuracy in answering medical questions and could serve as personalized question and answer tutors​. These platforms refine their output by continually incorporating student feedback, creating an iterative loop that hones individualized study plans​. Such adaptive systems promise higher engagement and knowledge retention than one-size-fits-all curricula. At the same time, authors caution that AI-generated content must be carefully curated (to avoid errors or bias) and integrated under human guidance​.

There's also a risk of algorithmic bias, as the AI's training data may not be diverse, leading to biased feedback or content that disadvantages certain groups. Another concern is the potential for over-reliance on AI, which could hinder the development of a trainee's independent clinical reasoning and critical thinking skills. Furthermore, the "black box" nature of many AI models makes it difficult to understand how they arrive at certain recommendations, raising questions of transparency and accountability [23]​.

Computer vision and objective skill assessment

AI and computer-vision methods are enabling objective measurement of surgical performance. Modern VR simulators augmented with real-time computer vision can identify instruments and anatomy and quantify metrics like completion time, accuracy, and handling errors [24]. For example, vision-enhanced simulators track trainees' motion data and use machine learning to classify skill levels based on movement patterns [25]. ​In one study, AI analyzed novice versus expert motions in VR knee arthroscopy to grade performance automatically [26]. These systems generate rich data-driven feedback, pinpointing specific deficiencies (e.g., poor instrument handling) for targeted improvement. Beyond simulators, prototype computer-vision scoring tools have been shown to correlate strongly with expert ratings​in non-orthopedic surgical studies. In orthopedics, AR/VR systems can similarly embed built-in assessment: for instance, an MR hip arthroplasty simulator using patient-specific 3D models allowed precise tracking of component placement with low workload​. These technologies offer a quantitative, reproducible alternative to subjective skill checklists. Trainees benefit from immediate, objective feedback on performance, while educators can monitor progression over time [27].

AI applications in orthopaedic research

Artificial intelligence is also transforming how orthopedic research is conducted. Recent studies highlight that AI-powered tools can dramatically accelerate literature review, data analysis, and evidence synthesis. Generative AI models (e.g., ChatGPT/GPT-4) have been used to refine search strategies, summarize large bodies of papers, and even draft coherent narrative syntheses.​[28]AI can extract key data from hundreds of studies or clinical notes, perform advanced statistical analyses, and generate tables or figures – tasks that traditionally require extensive manual effort [29]. Such tools make interpreting large datasets (e.g., national registry biobank data) more ​ feasible. Orthopedic residents report that AI assistance speeds up writing and literature review, providing quick evidence summaries while they focus on critical interpretation. In addition, machine learning is being applied to biomechanical and imaging research – for instance, automated detection of fracture lines or implant loosening – enabling new study designs that would be infeasible by hand [30]. AI is a "research assistant" that enhances productivity and insight generation [28].

The reviewed studies highlight that while AI tools can rapidly synthesize large volumes of information and improve research efficiency, their accuracy and reliability remain a major concern. Reported issues include inaccuracies, omissions, and biases in AI-generated summaries and analyses, which risk misleading researchers and compromising evidence-based practice if left unchecked. These findings underscore the necessity of rigorous validation, human oversight, and critical appraisal before AI-generated outputs can be safely integrated into orthopaedic research workflows [31].

Predictive analytics for surgical education and outcome prediction

Another growing trend is using predictive models to forecast educational and clinical outcomes. Machine learning algorithms have been trained on student performance data to predict exam success in education. For example, ensemble ML models (random forest, boosting, etc.) accurately predicted medical students' results on high-stakes licensing exams [32]. By analogy, such models could identify orthopedic trainees at risk of failing certification or guide personalized remediation plans. On the clinical side, numerous studies have applied AI to predict which patients will achieve meaningful improvement after surgery. A recent systematic review found that ML algorithms (e.g., neural nets, random forests) can predict the achievement of minimal clinically important differences after spine, joint, and sports surgeries with fair-to-good accuracy (c-statistics ~ 0.6–0.9) [33] Once validated, these outcome predictors could inform risk-adjusted training; for instance, educators might emphasize complication-avoidance techniques for cases where AI predicts high risk.

Challenges and ethical considerations in AI integration

Despite these advances, studies repeatedly caution about significant challenges and ethical issues. Key concerns include AI's accuracy and bias: generative models often produce inconsistent or incorrect answers, and unrecognized errors in training data can bias outputs [23]. The black-box nature of many algorithms also limits explainability in high-stakes settings [34]. Over-reliance on AI is a critical worry – one study warns that long-term dependence on algorithmic support may erode trainees' critical thinking and hands-on experience [35]. Practical barriers loom: implementing advanced simulators and AI systems requires substantial resources and infrastructure, and high costs can exacerbate disparities between well-funded programs and others [36]. Data privacy and academic integrity are further issues. Using real patient images or records for AI training raises patient confidentiality concerns, and students may be tempted to misuse AI (e.g., submitting AI-generated answers)​ [37]. Finally, there are regulatory and curricular hurdles: faculty need training to oversee AI tools, and consensus guidelines are only emerging. In summary, while AI has transformative potential, its integration must be accompanied by rigorous validation, human oversight, and ethical safeguards [31].

Discussion

Across orthopedic education and research, AI technologies consistently aim to enhance training efficiency, personalization, and objectivity. Our review found that diverse AI tools – from VR simulators to machine-learning tutors – share these goals. For example, virtual-reality training often shortens learning curves, with VR-trained residents completing arthroplasty tasks significantly faster than controls [29].​. Despite shortened learning curves, there is Conflicting evidence on VR training’s impact on orthopedic skill acquisition, particularly the lack of significant OSATS improvements. Some studies show VR reduces procedure time and enhances proficiency [38]. Others report no significant OSATS differences between VR and control groups [39].

This variability likely reflects differences in VR platforms, training protocols, participant experience, duration, procedure type, and outcome measures [40]. Interventions emphasizing speed and procedural steps may shorten completion time without improving OSATS, which assesses broader competencies. Moreover, while valuable, VR benefits such as reduced anxiety and increased confidence are not captured in OSATS [29].

Subgroup or sensitivity analyses stratified by VR system, training intensity, trainee experience, and procedure complexity could help explain these discrepancies. Standardized outcome metrics and large multicenter RCTs are needed to validate findings and reduce methodological heterogeneity.[40]AI-enhanced training platforms should be viewed as valuable supplements to traditional orthopedic education rather than replacements for established training methodologies. Integrating AI tools into existing curricula offers opportunities to optimize learning while preserving the essential human elements of surgical training and mentorship that remain fundamental to developing competent orthopedic surgeons.

Similarly, AI-driven analytics provide personalized feedback, identifying individual performance metrics and adapting to each learner's needs [41]. These adaptive systems standardize assessment: machine-learning classifiers can distinguish expertise levels and generate objective feedback without human bias​ [42]. Generative AI likewise promises customized learning content, creating case scenarios or explanations tailored to the trainee's level​ [18]. In short, as reported in our Results, studies across modalities routinely highlight improved efficiency and individualized learning (adaptive curricula or real-time explanations. [1]. Importantly, all AI applications can provide objective skill assessments, breaking reliance on traditional apprenticeship 26 s [41], and often create a safer practice environment.

Despite these shared benefits, AI applications differ markedly in maturity, accessibility, and risk profiles. High-fidelity simulators (VR/AR) are relatively mature and supported by multiple clinical studies.​ [29], whereas newer tools (like generative AI chatbots) are still being validated in orthopedic contexts​ [1]. For instance, VR systems have FDA/CE-approved devices and structured RCTs, while evidence for generative models is mostly preliminary. The resources required vary: immersive VR or AR demands specialized hardware and significant costs [18].​, whereas AI tutors or chatbots run on standard devices. This affects accessibility – learners in resource-rich settings may use advanced simulators, while others only have generic AI apps. The nature of outputs differs, too: VR/AR provides hands-on motor skills training, machine learning can offer video-based coaching or imaging analysis, and generative AI offers textual/image content. Risks also diverge: VR training poses little safety risk and can improve patient outcomes by reducing trainee errors​ [43], whereas generative AI risks inaccurate or biased information and academic misuse [44]​.

The strengths of AI-based education and research are evident across studies. AI-enhanced training boosts skill acquisition: most VR studies reported that trainees made fewer errors and improved technical skills after AI-guided practice [45]. ​ For example, an arthroplasty VR meta-analysis found significantly shorter procedure times for VR-trained residents​, indicating faster mastery of techniques. Similarly, AI-driven assessment (e.g., the "Virtual Operative Assistant") enabled recognition of expert vs. novice performance, highlighting teachable metrics and delivering automated, objective feedback​ [25]. These methods free educators to focus on higher-level teaching.

Regarding research impact, AI can accelerate discovery: machine-learning models analyze large clinical data sets to predict outcomes (e.g., hospital stay, complication risk) with high accuracy [4].​Such capabilities speed up evidence generation and inform curricula (e.g., personalized risk calculators taught to students). Across domains, AI tools facilitate individualized learning paths, engaging students actively. For instance, VR requires active participation, promoting deeper learning and motivation​ [46]. These strengths imply that AI can improve what and how orthopedic trainees learn and make research more efficient by handling complex data.

Nonetheless, limitations pervade the current literature. Data bias and generalizability are major concerns: many AI models are trained on limited, homogeneous data, so they may not perform well for all populations [47].​ Generative AI models like ChatGPT have demonstrated inaccurate or incomplete knowledge – for example, they scored far below residents on orthopedic exam questions​ [48] – reflecting knowledge gaps. Many tools also lack thorough validation: objective assessment scores (like OSATS) often showed no significant improvement with VR compared to controls​ [49], and high heterogeneity in study designs limits firm conclusions. Technology costs and complexity hinder adoption [50]. In practice, few programs can integrate these advanced simulators widely. There is also a risk of over-reliance on AI: automated feedback can be effective, but without human context, it may fail (learners can feel disconnected) [51]​. Ethical and practical issues arise, too – concerns about academic honesty, access inequity, and patient privacy have been noted [52].​Finally, the current evidence often comes from short-term, small-scale studies. As the ARVR review highlights, it reduces procedure time and error. Such capabilities speed up evidence generation and inform curricula (e.g., personalized risk calculators taught to students). Across domains, AI tools facilitate individualized learning paths, engaging students actively. Fortes are promising, but OSATS and long-term competency data are lacking [53].​ In short, while AI tools show potential, the evidence is constrained by bias, limited validation, and incomplete outcome data.

These limitations point to key evidence gaps. Notably, no/limited longitudinal studies or RCTs demonstrate that AI-acquired skills transfer to the operating room. One systematic review notes that "a paucity of literature exists to evaluate the effect of a longitudinal VR curriculum on direct patient care performance"​ [29]. In our review, most studies assessed short-term gains in simulation metrics; almost none followed trainees into real surgery. Similarly, robust comparisons between AI-based curricula and standard teaching are scarce. For example, Mirchi et al. have initiated a randomized trial of AI-integrated training vs. control [54], but such efforts remain uncommon. The literature also lacks standardized outcome measures, making synthesis difficult.

Furthermore, few publications address ethical, curricular, or regulatory frameworks for AI in orthopedics [55]. Questions of data privacy, informed consent for AI use, and how to train faculty on AI tools are largely unexplored. Cost-effectiveness analyses are similarly absent. Thus, the field urgently needs high-quality, longitudinal trials, implementation studies, and consensus guidelines on AI deployment in education. Ethically, AI in orthopedics must be assessed through autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice. Respecting autonomy is complex when patients may not fully understand AI’s role, requiring informed consent processes that clearly explain AI’s involvement, benefits, and risks.[56]Beneficence supports AI use for improving diagnostic accuracy and surgical precision. However, this must be balanced with non-maleficence, given risks such as misinformation (hallucination) and overreliance, which can impair clinical judgment [57].

In clinical practice, AI can aid preoperative planning and navigation, yet errors or biases in algorithms may cause harm if not carefully overseen. Accountability for AI-related errors remains unclear, highlighting the need for surgeons to retain autonomy and critically interpret AI outputs rather than unthinkingly rely on them [2].

Regulatory efforts recommend lifecycle-based frameworks involving ongoing evaluation from development through implementation, ensuring safety, transparency, and adaptability. Existing standards, such as HIPAA and GDPR, must be tailored for orthopedics to address data security, algorithmic bias, and equitable access [56].

Looking ahead, several future directions and recommendations emerged, and platforms must be integrated with expert teaching and not used in isolation​. As Mirchi et al. suggest, combining AI-based feedback with instructor mentorship may yield the best outcomes. Second, rigorous validation studies are essential. Trial designs like the ongoing RCT by Mirchi et al​ [41]. Should be replicated in orthopedics to confirm that simulator gains predict real-world performance. Third, continual advancement of AI methods is needed. Explainable AI and deep learning could improve assessment granularity [58]., and feeding these models more orthopedic-specific data may overcome current knowledge limits.

Implications for future research and practice

For future research, longitudinal studies and randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are needed to assess how AI-acquired skills translate into real-world surgical performance and patient outcomes, alongside rigorous comparative evaluations of AI-enhanced versus traditional curricula. Strict verification should be prioritized through validation across diverse orthopedic contexts, standardized outcome measures, and reproducibility testing, supported by explainable AI (XAI), larger orthopedic-specific datasets, and ethical safeguards addressing bias, privacy, and equity. For educational practice, a staged mixed model is recommended: early integration of adaptive platforms and automated assessments, intermediate use of VR/AR simulation with AI-driven feedback and faculty debriefings, and advanced supervised clinical exposure supported by AI-based performance monitoring. Across all stages, strict oversight—including expert review of generative AI outputs, audits of assessments, and alignment with accreditation standards—must ensure accuracy, fairness, and safety, with institutional capacity-building and equitable access central to implementation. Ultimately, AI should complement—not replace—human-centered orthopedic training and mentorship, guided by robust evidence.

Limitations of this systematic review

This review has several limitations. Restricting inclusion to English-language publications risks language bias and may exclude relevant international research. The search was limited to major databases (PubMed, Embase, Scopus), potentially missing grey literature. Significant methodological heterogeneity in included studies (AI interventions, designs, outcomes) precluded meta-analysis. Variable study quality (assessed by the NOS in Table 2) impacts evidence strength. Finally, the focus on orthopedic education and research limits generalizability to other specialties.

Conclusion

AI in orthopedic education and research offers powerful advantages but faces significant hurdles. Similar benefits – efficiency, personalization, and objectivity – span most AI applications, yet differences in technology maturity and risk must be acknowledged. Strengths such as improved skill acquisition and individualized learning have been demonstrated, but limitations (bias, cost, validation gaps) and evidence gaps (long-term outcomes, standardization) temper enthusiasm. The field should pursue hybrid models, rigorous evaluation, and ethical integration of AI into curricula. By addressing these challenges head-on, educators can harness AI's promise to train more competent, confident surgeons for the future.
