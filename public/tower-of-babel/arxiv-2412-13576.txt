Parallel Graver Basis Extraction for Nonlinear Integer Optimization
Wenbo Liu, Akang Wang, Wenguo Yang

Abstract
The augmentation scheme provides a nontraditional approach to nonlinear integer programming by iteratively refining incumbent solutions along objective-improving directions from the Graver basis. Its main computational bottleneck, however, lies in the practical difficulty of accessing such directions. To address this challenge, we develop a massively parallel heuristic for approximating Graver basis, extracting promising directions by optimizing nonconvex continuous problems using parallelizable first-order methods. Experiments on QPLIB and MINLPLib instances show that our method achieves comparable performance to advanced solvers.

Introduction

We consider a nonlinear integer program of the form

\displaystyle\underset{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}{\min}

\displaystyle\quad f(x)

(1a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\quad Ax=b

(1b)

\displaystyle\quad l\leq x\leq u,

(1c)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a real-valued function, and
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n},b\in\mathbb{Z}^{m},l,u\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
define linear constraints and variable bounds.
Let
S\coloneqq\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:Ax=b,l\leq x\leq u\}
denote the feasible region
, and let
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)\coloneqq\{g\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}:Ag=0\}
denote the
integer kernel
of
A
.
Such problems arise in real-world applications like portfolio selection
[
19
]
and resource allocation
[
18
]
, but are computationally challenging due to their
\mathcal{NP}
-hard complexity. General-purpose solvers rely on branch-and-cut methods, which are expensive due to systematic solution enumeration.

A nontraditional approach for tackling nonlinear integer programs of form (
1
) involves augmenting via the Graver basis. Given
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we say that
x\sqsubseteq y
if
x_{i}y_{i}\geq 0
and
|x_{i}|\leq|y_{i}|
for all
i=1,2,\dots,n
, and the Graver basis
\mathcal{G}(A)
consists of all
\sqsubseteq
-minimal elements in
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)\setminus\{0\}
.
The augmentation scheme begins with an initial solution
\bar{x}
and iteratively refines it by moving along feasible, objective-reducing directions drawn from
\mathcal{G}(A)
.

The Graver augmentation procedure is shown to achieve an optimal solution within polynomially many steps for separable convex objectives
[
9
]
.
Furthermore, the Graver basis is applicable to various nonlinear integer programs including convex integer maximization
[
5
]
and minimization of quadratic or higher degree polynomial functions lying in suitable cones
[
16
]
.

An immediate question is how to access the Graver basis for practical augmentation.
This difficulty arises from its intrinsic intractability: the size of
\mathcal{G}(A)
may grow exponentially, and deciding membership in
\mathcal{G}(A)
is
\mathcal{NP}
-complete
[
11
]
.
Exact methods, including Pottier’s algorithm
[
21
]
and the project-and-lift algorithm
[
10
]
, are inherently sequential and thus poorly scalable.
Recent works
[
1
,
14
]
employs quantum annealing to generate diverse elements of
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
, but still depends on Pottier’s procedure to ensure
\sqsubseteq
-minimality.

In this work, we shift from exact computation to the approximation of Graver basis.

This is performed by optimizing unconstrained, non-convex continuous problems.
The objective function is carefully designed through an isomorphism to
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
, and first-order methods are employed for parallel optimization, initiated from diversified starting points.
During this process, near-minimal elements of
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
are progressively extracted to form this approximate basis.
This set of directions is then applied in multiple augmentations from diverse initial solutions, each striving for optimality.
We refer to this entire process as
M
ulti-start
A
ugmentation via
P
arallel
E
xtraction (MAPE), a
massively parallel
algorithm for solving nonlinear integer programs.
MAPE exhibits three notable characteristics: (i) it does not rely on general-purpose solvers, while ensuring feasible returned solutions;
(ii) it is highly parallelizable with GPU acceleration;
(iii) it is reusable, as the parallel extraction needs to be performed only once to handle instances with identical constraint matrices but varying objectives and right-hand sides.

The distinct contribution of our work can be summarized as follows:

•

We
extract diverse Graver basis elements
by optimizing unconstrained problems, demonstrating how continuous techniques can be applied to discrete problems.

•

We introduce MAPE, a
massively parallel
algorithm for solving nonlinear integer programs,
building upon the partial Graver basis.

•

We conduct extensive computational experiments on
MINLPLib and
QPLIB instances, with results showing that our approach performs comparably to state-of-the-art general-purpose solvers.

The Graver Augmentation

Definition 2.1
.

A set
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is called a
test set
(or optimality certificate) for model (
1
) if, for any non-optimal
\bar{x}\in S
, there exists
g\in\mathcal{T}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that: (i)
\bar{x}+\lambda g\in S
and (ii)
f(\bar{x}+\lambda g)<f(\bar{x})
.

Given an instance of model (
1
) and a set
\mathcal{T}
of directions, the augmentation algorithm begins with an initial feasible solution and repeatedly selects the best augmenting direction from
\mathcal{T}
to improve the incumbent, until no such a direction exists.
We outline this augmentation procedure in Algorithm
1
.

Algorithm 1

Augmentation Scheme

1:

INPUT:
An optimization problem in model (
1
), a finite set
\mathcal{T}
and an initial feasible solution
\bar{x}\in S

2:

OUTPUT:
A solution
x^{*}\in S
.

3:

while

\mathcal{A}\coloneqq\{(g,\lambda):g\in\mathcal{T},\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},\bar{x}+\lambda g\in S,f(\bar{x}+\lambda g)<f(\bar{x})\}\neq\emptyset

do

4:

(\bar{g},\bar{\lambda})\leftarrow\underset{(g,\lambda)\in\mathcal{A}}{\arg\min}\;f(\bar{x}+\lambda g)

5:

\bar{x}\leftarrow\bar{x}+\bar{\lambda}\bar{g}
.

6:

return

\bar{x}

It can be easily shown that Algorithm
1
always returns an optimal solution to model (
1
) whenever
\mathcal{T}
is a test set.

The following proposition characterizes an important special case where
\mathcal{G}(A)
forms a test set.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

The Graver basis
\mathcal{G}(A)
forms a test set for model (
1
) when the objective function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is separable convex, i.e., when
f(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{n}f_{j}(x_{j})
where each
f_{j}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is convex.

Proposition
2.1
establishes that for separable convex objectives, the Graver augmentation is guaranteed to reach an optimal solution to model (
1
). Moreover, as shown in
[
9
]
, the number of required augmenting steps is polynomially bounded in the input size.

3
MAPE: Multi-start Augmentation via Parallel Extraction

To facilitate practical augmentation, we develop a parallel approach for extracting Graver basis elements, anchored in four fundamental conditions:

(C1)

Kernel condition:
Ag=0
;

(C2)

Integrality:
g\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
;

(C3)

Minimality:
g
is
\sqsubseteq
-minimal;

(C4)

Bound consistency:
l-u\leq g\leq u-l
.

Conditions (C
(C1)
) and (C
(C2)
) characterize elements of
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
, and (C
(C3)
) ensures non-redundant directions that define
\mathcal{G}(A)
. Notably,
\mathcal{G}(A)
depends solely on the linear constraints (
1b
), whereas the variable bounds (
1c
) admit further pruning of invalid directions through (C
(C4)
).
Existing approaches like
[
1
]
strictly enforce (C
(C2)
) and (C
(C4)
) via quantum bit encoding, followed by quantum annealing to minimize
\|Ag\|_{2}^{2}
for kernel condition, and finally apply the Pottier’s algorithm for minimality.
In contrast, we propose an alternative scheme that strictly enforces conditions (C
(C1)
) and (C
(C2)
), and addresses both (C
(C3)
) and (C
(C4)
) through a
unified criterion
.
Our approach first characterizes the integer kernel
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
through its lattice basis representation, which inherently satisfies both the kernel condition and integrality requirement.
We then develop a
parallel extraction
method that minimizes an unconstrained, non-convex continuous problem designed to promote direction minimality and bound consistency, progressively collecting near-minimal elements during the optimization.

The resulting set of directions serves as an approximate Graver basis, enabling practical multi-start augmentation for nonlinear integer programs,
which we designate as MAPE.

3.1
Characterization of
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)

For any full-column-rank matrix
B=(B_{1},...,B_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, denote by
\mathcal{L}(B)\coloneqq\left\{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}z_{i}B_{i}:z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\right\}
the lattice generated by
B
.
We now construct a basis for
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
of dimension
d\coloneqq n-m
.

Lemma 3.1

(
[
13
]
)
.

For
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
with full row rank, there exists a unimodular matrix
K\coloneqq(D|B)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times(m+d)}
such that

AK=(H|0)
is of Hermite normal form.

The matrix
K
can be computed in polynomial time
[
13
]
.
Its last
d
columns, denoted by
B\coloneqq(B_{1},\ldots,B_{d})
, form a basis of
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
.

Theorem 3.2
.

\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)=\mathcal{L}(B).

Proof.

For any
Bz\in\mathcal{L}(B)
where
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, Lemma
3.1
gives
AB=0
, hence
ABz=0
. Since
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times d}
by Lemma
3.1
, we have
Bz\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Thus,
Bz\in\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
.

For any
g\in\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
, consider
K^{-1}g\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
(since
K\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times(m+d)}
is unimodular and
g\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
). Decompose:

K^{-1}g=\binom{w}{z}\quad\text{with }w\in\mathbb{Z}^{m},z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}

Then:

0=Ag=AKK^{-1}g=(H|0)\binom{w}{z}=Hw

The invertibility of
H
implies
w=0
. Therefore:

g=K\binom{0}{z}=Bz\in\mathcal{L}(B).

Therefore,
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)=\mathcal{L}(B).

∎

Theorem
3.2
indicates an isomorphism between
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
and
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
via
g=Bz
, and the inverse is given by
z=(B^{\top}B)^{-1}B^{\top}g
.
Therefore, every element
g\in\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
can be obtained via its integer coordinate in the space of
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

3.2
Parallel Extraction of the Graver Basis

Our approach enforces condition (C
(C1)
) through the parameterization
g=Bz
, where
B
denotes a basis for the integer kernel
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
constructed following
[
13
]
.
When necessary, the basis may be further refined using the LLL algorithm (see
A
for details).
Theorem
3.2
establishes that condition (C
(C2)
) can be satisfied through simple rounding of
z
.
However, condition (C
(C3)
) presents greater challenges.
To address this, we develop a unified criterion that simultaneously incorporates conditions (C
(C3)
) and (C
(C4)
) by identifying
short
directions. This leads to the optimization problem:

\underset{z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\backslash\{0\}}{\min}\|Bz\|.

(2)

Problem (
2
) represents the shortest vector problem, which is known to be
\mathcal{NP}
-hard
[
20
]
.
However, instead of exclusively seeking the shortest vector, our goal is to generate a diverse collection of short vectors through a highly parallelizable implementation.
To this end, we optimize a non-convex continuous surrogate of Problem (
2
), reformulated as the objective function in (
3
).
In this formulation, we employ the
\ell_{1}
-norm, motivated by the well-established success of LASSO
[
23
]
in promoting sparsity.
The model includes two additional components: (i) a penalty term that drives
z
toward integer values in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, and (ii) a term that penalizes solutions near the origin through an inverse
\ell_{\infty}
-norm, with
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
as tuning parameters.

The
surrogate problem
is formulated as
:

\underset{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\min}\;\Phi(z)\coloneqq\|Bz\|_{1}+\lambda_{1}\cdot\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}(z_{i}-\lfloor z_{i}\rfloor)(\lceil z_{i}\rceil-z_{i})+\lambda_{2}\cdot\max\left\{\frac{1}{\|z\|_{\infty}}-1,0\right\}.

(3)

Given Problem (
3
), our parallel extraction method performs simultaneous optimization from multiple diverse initial points through three main phases.
First, we generate
N
uniformly distributed random points
g\sim\text{Uniform}(l-u,u-l)
to ensure diversity in initialization. These points are projected via the transformation
z\coloneqq(B^{\top}B)^{-1}B^{\top}g
to obtain valid starting points for model (
3
).
The optimization then proceeds using first-order methods, particularly Adam
[
15
]
, selected for their computational efficiency and native GPU parallelization support. During optimization, intermediate solutions are periodically processed through rounding and mapping to the integer kernel via
B\lceil z\rfloor
, yielding a comprehensive collection of candidate directions in
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)\setminus\{0\}
.
This parallel extraction framework is formally described in Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

Parallel Extraction

1:

INPUT:

A\in\mathbb{Z}^{m\times n}
, number of epochs
T
, number of initial points
N

2:

OUTPUT:
the collected direction set
\mathcal{G}

3:

\mathcal{G}\leftarrow\emptyset

4:

Compute the HNF of
A
with
AK=(H|0)

5:

B\leftarrow K_{:,m+1:n}

6:

for

i=1,2,...,N

do

# in parallel

7:

Generate a sample
g\sim\text{Uniform}(l-u,u-l)

8:

z^{0}\leftarrow(B^{\top}B)^{-1}B^{\top}g

9:

for

t=1,2,...,T

do

10:

z^{t}\leftarrow update(z^{t-1},\nabla\Phi(z^{t-1}))
# a single-step update

11:

if

B\lceil z^{t}\rfloor\in[l-u,u-l]

then

12:

\mathcal{G}\leftarrow\mathcal{G}\cup\{B\lceil z^{t}\rfloor\}

13:

return

\mathcal{G}

3.3
Multi-start Augmentation via Parallel Extraction

Section
3.2
provides a
parallel
approach for Graver basis extraction.
Building upon this, we introduce the MAPE algorithm for solving nonlinear integer programs. In particular, as the parallel extraction only yields a
partial
Graver basis, MAPE compensates by performing multiple augmentations in parallel.

Initial feasible solutions to model (
1
) are obtained by solving the following problem
:

\underset{x\in\mathbb{[}l,u]}{\min}\|Ax-b\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda_{3}\cdot\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}-\lfloor x_{i}\rfloor)(\lceil x_{i}\rceil-x_{i})

(4)

where
\lambda_{3}
is a hyper-parameter that balances integrality against satisfaction of linear constraints, and any optimal solution to Problem (
4
) is feasible for model (
1
).

Starting from diversified initial points, Problem (
4
) can be optimized in parallel using Adam
, as discussed in Section
3.2
.
Let
S_{0}\subseteq S
denote the set of feasible solutions to model (
1
).
Beginning from each
\bar{x}\in S_{0}
, MAPE performs
|S_{0}|
parallel augmentations via the extracted partial Graver basis and finally returns the best solution found.

3.4
Extension to Cases with Linear Inequalities

Consider the presence of additional linear inequality constraints

Gx\geq h
, where
G\in\mathbb{Z}^{m^{\prime}\times n}
and

h\in\mathbb{Z}^{m^{\prime}}
, in Problem (
1
).
Introducing slack variables
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{m^{\prime}}
,
the problem can be reformulated as

\displaystyle\underset{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{n},s\in\mathbb{Z}^{m^{\prime}}}{\min}

\displaystyle f(x)

(5)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
G&-I_{m^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x\\
s\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}b\\
h\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle l\leq x\leq u,\;s\geq 0

To characterize the integer kernel of the constraint matrix, we have the following result.

Corollary 3.3
.

\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}\left(\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
G&-I_{m^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}\right)=\mathcal{L}\left(\binom{B}{GB}\right),

where
B\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times d}
is a basis of
\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}(A)
.

Accordingly, MAPE searches for
short
directions in

\mathrm{Ker}_{\mathbb{Z}}\!\left(\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
G&-I_{m^{\prime}}\end{pmatrix}\right)
.
The corresponding surrogate problem becomes

\displaystyle\underset{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\min}\;\left\|\binom{B}{GB}z\right\|_{1}+\lambda_{1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}(z_{i}-\lfloor z_{i}\rfloor)(\lceil z_{i}\rceil-z_{i})+\lambda_{2}\cdot\max\left\{\frac{1}{\|z\|_{\infty}}-1,0\right\}

(6)

Compared with the standard surrogate problem (
3
),
the objective in (
6
) differs only through the additional term

\|GBz\|_{1}
, which penalizes the inequality constraints and promote the minimality condition (
(C3)
).

Experiments

4.1
Setup

4.1.1
Datasets

We evaluated the proposed method on two established benchmark libraries: (i) QPLIB
[
6
]
, a comprehensive collection of quadratic programming instances, and (ii) MINLPLib
[
3
]
, a standard repository of mixed-integer nonlinear programming models.
Maximization problems were reformulated as minimization problems by negating the objective function.
To construct a representative and meaningful testbed, we applied the following selection criteria: (i) the instance conforms to the structure of Model (
1
) or (
5
), namely linear constraints with integer variables; (ii) the instance is nontrivial, excluding cases solvable to optimality within 10 seconds by the commercial solvers used in our experiments; and (iii) the instance is of moderate size, restricted to at most 700 variables for QPLIB and 500 variables for MINLPLib. This procedure resulted in 53 instances.
The resulting testbed captures a diverse and challenging class of nonlinear integer programs, spanning problem sizes from tens to several hundred variables, varying constraint densities, and both convex and nonconvex objective functions.

4.1.2
Baselines

In the experiments, we denote our algorithm by MAPE and compare it with state-of-the-art nonlinear integer programming solvers, including
(i) Gurobi 12.0.0
[
7
]
;
(ii) CPLEX 22.1.0
[
12
]
; and (iii) SCIP 9.1.1
[
2
]
.

We also consider advanced heuristics for integer quadratic programs (iv) LS-IQCQP
[
8
]
and its successor (v) QSeek.

Each baseline solver was run with a time limit of
3,600
seconds and granted access to all available threads of the testing machine.

For MAPE, we perform augmentations from
200
starting points and conducts
200,000
parallel extractions.
The hyper-parameters
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{3}
are set to
0.85
,
1
and
0.1
, respectively.
The optimization of unconstrained problems are conducted via Adam with a learning-rate of
0.003
(with a PyTorch implementation on GPUs).
Our code is available at
https://github.com/NetSysOpt/MAPE
.

4.1.3
Evaluation Configuration

All our experiments were conducted on an Nvidia GeForce RTX 3090 GPU and an 12th Gen Intel(R) Core(TM) i9-12900K CPU
with 24 threads
, using Python 3.11.5 and PyTorch 2.7.0.

The last ten columns present the performance (

4.2.2
Analysis

The computational results are summarized in Table
1
.
MAPE outperforms CPLEX and SCIP on 90% of the instances, and
surpasses QSeek and LS-IQCQP on more than 66% and 93% of comparable cases, respectively
.
While Gurobi attains optimal or best-known solutions for more than half of the instances within seconds, MAPE still delivers superior performance on 20% of the instances and prominently surpasses all baseline solvers on instances
“2492”
,
“2733”
,
“3307”
,
“3883”
and
“7164”
.

Notably, whereas these general-purpose solvers incorporate sophisticated components such as presolve routines and primal heuristics, MAPE relies on a lightweight implementation of only a few hundred lines of Python code while still producing high-quality solutions. Its GPU-based design also suggests potential complementarity with CPU-based solvers such as Gurobi. Overall, MAPE provides a competitive alternative to state-of-the-art solvers.

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we propose MAPE, a heuristic approach designed to extract the Graver basis elements and solve nonlinear integer programs through a multi-start augmentation procedure.
Unlike traditional approaches that struggle with sequential processing, MAPE excels in parallelism, enabling faster execution.
Experimental results on public datasets demonstrate MAPE’s ability to swiftly deliver high-quality solutions, outperforming state-of-the-art solvers. Finally, MAPE does not rely on any general-purpose solver or sophisticated presolving procedures, and
could be massively paralleled on GPUs
.
