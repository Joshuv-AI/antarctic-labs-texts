Unlike common pneumonia, COVID-19 is a risk factor for multiple cardiovascular diseases: A two-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Chengjia Li, Huijun Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the differences between COVID-19 and past common forms of pneumonia and to determine if COVID-19 acts as a contributing factor in various cardiovascular diseases (CVDs). We retrieved large-sample genome-wide association study data from the Open GWAS database related to COVID-19, bacterial pneumonia (BP), viral pneumonia (VP), stable angina (SA), unstable angina (UA), heart failure (HF), ischemic heart disease (IHD), atrial fibrillation (AF), and myocardial infarction (MI). We selected single-nucleotide polymorphisms with strong correlations as instrumental variables (P < 5E-06), and set the threshold for the F-statistic to be over 10. Five statistical methods were used for analysis including inverse variance weighted, Mendelian randomization-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode, with inverse variance weighted as the primary method. We assessed the reliability of our results through heterogeneity, pleiotropy, and sensitivity testing; Our analysis probed the relationship between COVID-19, BP, VP, and 6 CVDs. COVID-19 infection was found to enhance the incidence of SA, UA, HF, and MI (SA: odds ratio [OR], 1.12; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.04–1.20; P = .002; UA: OR, 1.14; 95% CI, 1.01–1.29; P = .041; HF: OR, 1.12; 95% CI, 1.03–1.23; P = .012; MI: OR, 1.11; 95% CI, 1.02–1.25; P = .032). There was no significant effect on the incidence of AF or IHD (P > .05), and no pleiotropy or sensitivity issues were found in the results. In contrast, neither past common BP nor VP was found to contribute to the progression of these 6 CVDs (P > .05). Unlike past common BP or VP, COVID-19 was found to increase the risks of SA, UA, HF, and MI, with no evidence supporting an increased risk for AF or IHD following COVID-19 infection.

1. Introduction

Since 2019, the emergence of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) and its various mutating forms have continuously brought profound adverse effects to the global public health system.[1,2] Clinically, many COVID-19 patients have been observed to suffer from a range of persistent physiological and psychological sequelae after clinical recovery, leading to chronic public health burdens.[3,4] The phenomenon of “Long COVID” has been increasingly mentioned in recent years, signifying its emergence as a new public health challenge.[5] A study by the U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs showed that, although vaccination reduced the risk of death (HR = 0.66, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.58–0.74) and severe sequelae (HR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.82–0.89) in COVID-19 patients, long-term, Long COVID still presents with multisystem damage.[3] Similarly, other respiratory infections, such as bacterial and viral pneumonias (VP), are also known to produce prolonged health effects. For instance, bacterial pneumonia (BP), commonly caused by Streptococcus pneumoniae, and VP, often resulting from influenza or respiratory syncytial virus, can lead to lingering symptoms like fatigue, weakness, and respiratory complications postinfection. Studies indicate that pneumonia of both origins may induce systemic inflammation that contributes to cardiovascular risks and other long-term health impacts.

A prospective observational study conducted in 17 countries across Africa, Asia, Europe, and South America found that 46.5% of patients reported experiencing multiple lingering symptoms, such as fatigue, headache, shortness of breath, and muscle/joint pain, within 6 to 12 months after discharge.[6] Another recent study indicates that approximately 65 million people worldwide experience long COVID, with chest pain and heart palpitations being common symptoms. In patients with long COVID symptoms, trace levels of pro-inflammatory factors can still be detected 18 months after infection, which may be the culprit behind cardiovascular symptoms.[7] Although multiple signs suggest that long COVID and other forms of pneumonia may affect the cardiovascular system, more evidence is needed to effectively assess this impact.

Due to the interference of confounding factors in clinical research (such as BMI, smoking, etc), the relationship between COVID-19 and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) lacks stable speculative results, making it difficult to compare the long-term cardiovascular consequences caused by COVID-19 with those resulting from past common pneumonias (viral or bacterial). The long-term consequences of previous COVID-19 infections have not gained significant attention. Mendelian randomization (MR) has been widely used in epidemiological studies of causal relationships in recent years. The fundamental principle of MR is that the gametes from the parental generation are randomly allocated to the offspring, which will not be affected by environmental confounders and reverse causality when discussing the relationship between diseases. Therefore, MR can provide more reliable results than observational studies. To date, no studies have used MR to explore the differences between COVID-19 and past common pneumonias. This study uses large-sample genome-wide association study (GWAS) data based on COVID-19, BP, VP, UA, SA, heart failure (HF), ischemic heart disease (IHD), atrial fibrillation (AF), and myocardial infarction (MI), and conducts a two-sample MR study to explore the differences between COVID-19 and other common pneumonias.

2. Methods

2.1. Overview of study design

This study has adhered to the guidelines outlined by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) in the design of the experiments and the manuscript preparation. All GWAS data employed in the MR analysis were obtained from previously published studies, which had already attained informed consent from participants and ethical approval from review boards, hence no additional ethical clearance was required for this study. We selected strongly associated single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNPs) as instrumental variables (IVs) representative of the exposure factors to examine the association with CVDs outcomes. The 3 core assumptions of MR studies are as follows: (1) IVs are robustly associated with the exposure factor; (2) IVs influence the outcome solely through the exposure factor and not directly associated with the outcome; (3) IVs are not affected by confounding factors that may affect the exposure–outcome relationship (Fig. 1).

2.2. Data sources

The aggregated data for COVID-19, BP, VP, UA, SA, HF, IHD, AF, and MI were sourced from publicly accessible GWAS available in the open GWAS database (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), with all population data derived from European descendants. The COVID-19-related IVs were derived from the COVID-19 Host Genetics Initiative, which began in 2020 and aims to elucidate host GWAS data. This study included 1588,783 participants, all of European descent. Data related to BP and VP were sourced from publicly available datasets collected by the European Bioinformatics Institute (EBI), with all participants being of European descent. These datasets include 16,380,385 and 16,380,366 SNPs, respectively. SNP data for 6 CVDs also came from EBI, and detailed data can be accessed via the provided URL.

2.3. Selection and validation of SNPs

Following the core assumptions for selecting IVs, a P-value threshold of < 5E-06 was set to identify SNPs significantly associated with COVID-19, BP, and VP. We performed clumping using R studio software, with parameters set to r2 = 0.01 and kb = 5000 to remove linkage disequilibrium.[8] All SNPs were uploaded to PhennoScanner V2 (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) to download the associated phenotype data, discarding any confounders directly related to CVDs. The F-values (F = β²/SE²) of the remaining SNPs were calculated, setting an F-value threshold of 10 to ensure a strong association. Relevant SNPs for each cardiovascular outcome were extracted and integrated, with the MR-PRESSO algorithm then utilized to remove outliers.

2.4. MR analysis

The “TwoSampleMR” package in R Studio software was utilized to analyze the causal relationships between COVID-19, BP, VP, and various CVD outcomes. To ensure the robustness and reliability of the results, we selected multiple statistical methods, each with its own unique advantages, to address potential biases and assumption violations in MR analysis. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method served as the main analysis technique, complemented by MR-Egger, weighted median, simple mode, and weighted mode methods. The IVW method is known for its robustness in MR analysis. This method assumes that all IVs strictly adhere to the core underlying assumptions, and during regression analysis, the intercept is not considered, while the inverse of the variance of the outcome is used as weight in the fitting analysis.[9,10] The MR-Egger method’s framework depends on the IVW but incorporates an intercept term during regression, relaxing the assumptions to only fulfill the InSIDE requirement: the instrument’s pleiotropy should not correlate with the causal effect estimate.[11] According to the principle of the weighted median method, if over 50% of the weight comes from valid SNPs, then the weighted median can produce an estimate consistent with the causal effect.[12] The simple mode is a median ratio estimator, but its estimation power diminishes if there is a significant variation in effect estimates among individual SNPs.[13,14] The weighted mode method provides a single causal effect estimation from multiple genetic variant IVs, with lesser bias and lower Type I error risk compared to other methods. Although the estimation power of weighted mode is not as strong as that of IVW and weighted median estimator methods, it is superior to the MR-Egger method.[15] If an analysis shows statistical significance with the IVW method, and while other methods may not be statistically significant, if they convey a similar direction to that of IVW, the overall results are considered to be statistically meaningful.

2.5. Reliability evaluation

Heterogeneity, pleiotropy, and sensitivity analyses were conducted to detect potential biases in the study. We initiated by using the “mr_heterogeneity” function to assess data heterogeneity through the Cochran Q statistic. Subsequently, the “mr_pleiotropy_test” function was employed, leveraging the MR-Egger intercept method to evaluate data pleiotropy. Finally, the “mr_leaveoneout” function was applied, removing SNPs one by one to assess whether the remaining SNPs point to the same result, thus analyzing if the outcomes were influenced by a single SNP.

3.2. Two-sample MR results

IVW results indicated that COVID-19 contributes to the incidence of SA, UA, HF, and MI (SA: odds ratio [OR], 1.12, 95% CI, 1.04–1.20, P = .002; UA: OR, 1.14, 95% CI, 1.01–1.22, P = .041; HF: OR, 1.12, 95% CI, 1.03–1.23, P = .012; MI: OR, 1.11, 95% CI, 1.02–1.25, P = .032); however, the positive associations observed with IHD and AF, although indicative of increased risk (OR > 1), were not statistically significant (P > .05) (Fig. 2). No promotional effects of BP or VP were found on any of the 6 heart disease outcomes assessed, with all results being nonsignificant (P > .05). Heterogeneity was identified in the positive outcomes using the Cochran Q test, and MR-Egger test, yet no pleiotropy was detected, with results presented in Table 3. The statistical results were unaffected by the removal of any single SNP using the leave-one-out method, affirming the robustness of the findings, all MR analysis, and robustness test results can be accessed in the Data S1, Supplementary Materials, http://links.lww.com/MD/O238. 4. Discussion

According to the World Health Organization, as of October 20, 2024, the cumulative number of confirmed COVID-19 infections worldwide has reached 776,696,616 (https://data.who.int/dashboards/covid19/cases). Further in-depth studies and clinical observations suggest that COVID-19 may not only cause prominent lung damage and hypoxemia but may also involve multiple systems and organs throughout the body. However, it is still unclear whether COVID-19 differs from common bacterial or VP in affecting long-term CVD outcomes.[16] This study is the first to use MR to compare the effects of COVID-19 and common pneumonia on various CVDs. We included up to 9 GWAS datasets in a two-sample MR design, hypothesizing correlations between COVID-19 or common pneumonia and various CVDs. Results indicate a causative effect of COVID-19 on the incidence of certain CVDs such as SA, UA, HF, and MI, while no association was found with AF and IHD. Common pneumonias did not affect any of the 6 CVD outcomes, suggesting that COVID-19 should be treated with greater caution compared to common pneumonias. These results are robust, based on the same ethnic data, though the underlying mechanisms still require further exploration.

Studies have indicated that BP and VP can elevate the incidence of CVDs. BP, such as infections caused by S pneumoniae, can trigger a systemic inflammatory response, leading to endothelial dysfunction, a procoagulant state, and accelerated progression of atherosclerosis, thereby increasing the risk of cardiovascular events.[17] VP, such as infections caused by the influenza virus, has also been linked to the occurrence of acute cardiovascular events. A large cohort study demonstrated a significantly increased risk of MI within the first week following influenza infection, with the risk being 6 times higher than baseline levels.[18] The inflammatory response and immune activation triggered by these infections can lead to cardiovascular complications such as myocardial injury, arrhythmias, and HF.[19]

However, these effects are typically most pronounced during the acute phase of infection, and the long-term cardiovascular risk remains unclear. Some studies suggest that in patients recovering from BP and VP, cardiovascular risk gradually decreases back to baseline levels after the infection resolves.[20] In contrast, the results of this study indicate a promotive causal relationship between COVID-19 and certain CVDs. In comparison, previously common BP or VP did not show a significant impact on these cardiovascular outcomes after adjusting for confounding factors. This finding suggests that COVID-19 may have unique pathophysiological mechanisms in triggering CVDs.

During discussions of Long COVID, central vascular damage symptoms are relatively common, such as elevated levels of N-terminal-prohormone brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP).[21,22] Researchers have reported complications including arrhythmias, MIs, or venous thromboembolisms; these findings have been gathered via electrocardiograms, echocardiogram imaging, or patient self-reports.[23,24] However, due to sparse data, the association between COVID-19 and long-term CVD still urgently requires verification.[5] It remains undetermined whether the cardiovascular events widely reported are a result of the respiratory system’s attack by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), thereby secondary affecting the cardiovascular system, or if they directly lead to serious CVD outcomes. The findings of this study indicate that although prior common clinical viral or BP may attack the respiratory system and indirectly affect the circulatory system, they are unlikely to cause serious CVD outcomes on a large scale, post elimination of confounding factors. Conversely, SARS-CoV-2 might possess a direct mechanism in addition to the indirect cardiovascular damage from common hypoxemia. Although recent studies have proposed various mechanisms, the precise direct pathophysiological pathway by which SARS-CoV-2 induces cardiac damage is yet to be fully elucidated.

Firstly, the formation of a cytokine storm may be one of the crucial mechanisms causing microvascular endothelial damage, a hypercoagulable state, and myocardial injury.[25] The novel coronavirus, SARS-CoV-2, the pathogen behind COVID-19, is an enveloped single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus. The spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 can bind to angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) on the host cell surface, and with assistance and catalysis from transmembrane serine protease 2, it can invade the cell.[26,27] ACE2 is a membrane-bound aminopeptidase, playing a pivotal role in the cardiovascular and immune systems. It is involved in regulating heart functions and the incidence and progression of diseases such as hypertension and diabetes. Alveolar epithelial cells highly express ACE2 and are directly exposed to the external environment, thus becoming the primary target for SARS-CoV-2, leading to respiratory symptoms. Once SARS-CoV-2 invades the host cells via ACE2, it induces an imbalanced immune response in the body: low-level expression of type I and III interferons, while chemokines and IL-6 are highly expressed.[28] This phenomenon suggests that the antiviral immune response in patients with COVID-19 may be suppressed while simultaneously experiencing mass activation of inflammatory factors. Thus, ACE2 is not only the gateway for SARS-CoV-2 to enter host cells but may also activate immune cells in the lungs, producing an abundance of inflammatory factors. The excessive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines can often lead to a cytokine storm, a fatal state associated with an overactive immune response related to systemic inflammation. Persistent systemic inflammation can cause vasculitis, increasing the risk of thrombosis, a hypercoagulable state, ischemia, and cell death.[29] A study of COVID-19 patients showed that serum biomarkers of inflammation and cytokine levels had significantly increased, especially IL-6, C-reactive protein, tumor necrosis factor-alpha, interleukin-2 receptor (IL-2R), and ferritin.[30] The higher the levels of these markers, the more severe the pneumonia symptoms and the worse the prognosis. Severe pneumonia patients’ plasma levels of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, interferon-inducible protein-10, monocyte chemoattractant protein, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha were all significantly increased compared to milder cases, indicating that the cytokine storm is closely related to the severity of the disease.

Secondly, direct myocardial damage by the virus could be another specific mechanism leading to cardiovascular complications. The myocardium, small intestinal epithelial tissues, and testicular cells are considered cell types most likely to be infected by SARS-CoV-2.[31] This suggests that SARS-CoV-2 can directly invade cardiac myocytes, leading to myocardial cell necrosis. Viral nucleic acids have been detected in myocardial tissues via real-time quantitative reverse-transcription PCR (qRT-PCR), immunohistochemical staining, and observation through electron microscopy, which shows not only the presence of SARS-CoV-2 RNA but also viral particles in the heart,[32] indicating that SARS-CoV-2 is not exclusively localized to the lungs but can also be found in other organs/tissues. In vitro experiments have demonstrated that the virus can invade cardiomyocytes and cause disruption of myofibrillar sarcomeres.[33] This evidence indicates that the myocardial injury experienced by COVID-19 patients may result from the direct infiltration of the virus.[34]

Additionally, oxidative stress plays a significant role in the promotion of CVD by SARS-CoV-2 infection. Oxidative stress, a commonly measured indicator in the field of CVDs, occurs when there is an overproduction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) or when the antioxidant system fails. On one hand, the entry of SARS-CoV-2 into the body induces an increase in inflammatory markers such as IL-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha, which can indirectly lead to the overproduction of ROS by the mitochondria,[35] and the production of ROS can, in turn, promote the formation of inflammasomes, suggesting that oxidative stress and inflammation mutually reinforce each other in the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection.[36] On the other hand, SARS-CoV-2 can downregulate ACE2, inhibiting the hydrolytic function of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidases, thus further accelerating ROS accumulation.[37] Also, the S1 spike protein of SARS-CoV-2 can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction in cardiomyocytes by weakening mitochondrial respiration to boost glycolysis.[38] This mitochondrial dysfunction can contribute to the exacerbation of cardiovascular damage, adding another layer of complexity to the direct effects of SARS-CoV-2 infection on the cardiovascular system.

These factors: cytokine storm, direct viral damage to cardiomyocytes, and oxidative stress, provide a multifaceted illustration of how SARS-CoV-2 can contribute to cardiovascular complications. Further research is needed to completely understand these mechanisms and their implications for patient care and potential therapeutic targets to manage the cardiovascular consequences of COVID-19.

Research findings suggest that vaccination is the only preventive measure discovered so far that has a reliable effect against Long COVID. A recent study in Israel compared individuals who had received 2 doses of the COVID-19 vaccine to unvaccinated individuals and found that the former group had a significantly lower proportion of long-term illness symptoms 4 months after infection.[39] The study also revealed that the vaccine not only alleviated symptoms in infected individuals but also made the symptoms of Long COVID in the vaccinated population statistically indistinguishable from those who were never infected with the virus.[40] Another study in the United States found that vaccination significantly reduced the risk of symptoms persisting more than 28 days after infection. Research in the UK yielded similar results, showing a 35% reduction in the incidence of long-term COVID-19 symptoms after 12 weeks postinfection.[41] Some views hold that vaccines may reduce the proportion of Long COVID by preventing occurrences of COVID-19 infection. Nonetheless, even with the large increases in infections caused by the Omicron variant, vaccination still demonstrated effectiveness in reducing the incidence of Long COVID. A recent study conducted in Japan indicates that receiving a COVID-19 vaccination 14 to 149 days before contracting COVID-19 can reduce the incidence of 15 diseases, including pneumonia and acute upper respiratory tract infections, with this effect remaining stable across all 3 follow-up periods. This could be due to the vaccination reducing the severity of COVID-19 symptoms and the virus’s pathological effects, thereby lowering the incidence rate of Long COVID.[42]

The effectiveness of vaccines in preventing Long COVID implies that boosting immunity can reduce the risk of enduring COVID complications. In the later stages of the pandemic, with the possibility of people being infected multiple times, how to reduce the incidence of Long COVID is an issue worthy of attention.[3] The ongoing research and findings highlight the importance of widespread vaccine adoption and the necessity for public health initiatives to combat both the acute and long-term effects of COVID-19.[43]

4.1. Study strengths and limitations

Despite this study providing strong evidence for a causal relationship between COVID-19 and CVDs, several limitations remain. First, although the MR method can reduce the impact of confounding factors, its effectiveness depends on the strength and validity of the selected genetic instruments, which may lead to issues with weak IVs. Second, the GWAS data included are primarily from European populations, potentially limiting the applicability of the results to other ethnic groups or regions and reducing the study’s generalizability. Third, due to limitations in data sources, information on disease severity, treatment interventions, and long-term follow-up of COVID-19 patients was unavailable, which may affect the accurate assessment of the long-term impact on CVDs. Finally, while potential mechanisms were explored, there is a lack of direct experimental data to support these findings, and further basic research is needed to elucidate the specific mechanisms by which COVID-19 affects the cardiovascular system.

5. Conclusion

We found that, unlike common bacterial or VP, COVID-19 can increase the risk of SA, UA, HF, and MI. There is no evidence to support that COVID-19 increases the risk of AF or IHD. Therefore, a history of COVID-19 should be considered a risk factor for patients with SA, UA, HF, and MI in the future. Additionally, the public should actively receive vaccinations to prevent cardiovascular risks.
