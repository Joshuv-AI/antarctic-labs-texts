Stable Differentiable Modal Synthesis for Learning Nonlinear Dynamics
Victor Zheleznov, Stefan Bilbao, Alec Wright, Simon King

Abstract
Modal methods are a long-standing approach to physical modelling synthesis. Extensions to nonlinear problems are possible, including the case of a high-amplitude vibration of a string. A modal decomposition leads to a densely coupled nonlinear system of ordinary differential equations. Recent work in scalar auxiliary variable techniques has enabled construction of explicit and stable numerical solvers for such classes of nonlinear systems. On the other hand, machine learning approaches (in particular neural ordinary differential equations) have been successful in modelling nonlinear systems automatically from data. In this work, we examine how scalar auxiliary variable techniques can be combined with neural ordinary differential equations to yield a stable differentiable model capable of learning nonlinear dynamics. The proposed approach leverages the analytical solution for linear vibration of system’s modes so that physical parameters of a system remain easily accessible after the training without the need for a parameter encoder in the model architecture. As a proof of concept, we generate synthetic data for the nonlinear transverse vibration of a string and show that the model can be trained to reproduce the nonlinear dynamics of the system. Sound examples are presented.



1  INTRODUCTION

The aim of physical modelling synthesis is to generate sound by numerically solving ordinary or partial differential equations (ODEs/PDEs) that describe the dynamics of an acoustic system. Throughout its long history, various simulation techniques have been employed, including modal synthesis
[
23
]
, finite-difference time-domain methods
[
3
]
and port-Hamiltonian methods
[
19
]
. Numerical stability of such approaches has always been a critical topic in the literature, which has seen a resurgence due to the scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) technique
[
2
,
32
,
30
]
. Under the non-negativity condition on potential energy, the SAV technique provides a way to construct explicit and provably stable numerical solvers for general classes of nonlinear systems.

More recently, machine learning approaches have seen an increasing interest in numerical simulation
[
6
,
28
,
18
,
20
]
, including for modelling of distributed musical systems
[
25
,
22
,
7
,
17
,
21
,
8
]
, and can yield differentiable models that are well-suited for learning from data while respecting physical priors
[
9
]
. In contrast with classical approaches, machine learning methods usually operate without stability guarantees. Extrapolation in time beyond intervals seen during training is a significant challenge and can lead to a rapid degradation in solution accuracy
[
25
,
22
,
7
]
. In addition to stability concerns, a common drawback of machine learning approaches is that sampling rate and physical parameters of a system, affecting pitch, timbre and other sonic characteristics, can not be changed after training
[
25
,
22
,
7
,
21
,
8
]
, or the model architecture relies on a parameter encoder to condition the solution
[
17
]
, leading to more trainable parameters and the requirement of a larger dataset containing ground truth data for all desired configurations of a system.

In this work, we aim to more deeply integrate the physics of a distributed system into a machine learning framework. We use modal decomposition to construct a system of finite dimension and separate the linear and nonlinear parts of the problem. Then, we replace only a dimensionless memoryless nonlinearity that describes coupling between the modes with a neural network. In particular, we employ gradient networks (GradNets)
[
5
]
that allow an interpretation in terms of a closed-form and non-negative potential function. Finally, we use neural ordinary differential equations (NODEs)
[
6
]
in combination with the SAV technique to yield a stable differentiable model that can learn nonlinear dynamics from data. As a result, physical parameters remain easily accessible and the model generalises to physical parameters, sampling rates and time scales not seen during training.

2  NONLINEAR TRANSVERSE STRING VIBRATION

The general equation of motion describing the transverse nonlinear vibration of a string in a single polarisation is:

\mathcal{L}u=\mathcal{F}+\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{e}}.

Here
u=u(x,t)\!\!:[0,L]\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denotes the transverse displacement of a string of length
L
and depends on spatial coordinate
x
in
\mathrm{m}
and time
t
in
\mathrm{s}
. Initial conditions are assumed to be zero. The string is assumed to be simply supported at both ends, implying the following boundary conditions:

u(0,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}u(0,t)=u(L,t)=\partial_{x}^{2}u(L,t)=0,\;\forall t\in\mathbb{R}^{+}.

Output is assumed to be drawn directly from the string displacement at position
x_{\mathrm{o}}
as
w(t)=u(x_{\mathrm{o}},t)
.

2.1  Linear Vibration

The linear part of the string vibration is encapsulated in the operator
\mathcal{L}
, defined as:

\mathcal{L}=\rho A\partial_{t}^{2}-T_{0}\partial_{x}^{2}+EI\partial_{x}^{4}+2\sigma_{0}\rho A\partial_{t}-2\sigma_{1}\rho A\partial_{t}\partial_{x}^{2},

where
\partial_{t}
and
\partial_{x}
represent partial derivatives with respect to
x
and
t
, respectively. Physical parameters that appear in
\mathcal{L}
are: the material density
\rho
in
\mathrm{kg{\cdot}m^{-3}}
; the string cross-sectional area
A=\pi r^{2}
in
\mathrm{m^{2}}
for a string of radius
r
; the tension
T_{0}
in
\mathrm{N}
; Young’s modulus
E
in
\mathrm{N{\cdot}m^{-2}}
; and moment of inertia
I=\frac{1}{4}\pi r^{4}
in
\mathrm{m^{4}}
. Frequency-independent and dependent loss is characterised by parameters
\sigma_{0}\geq 0
and
\sigma_{1}\geq 0
, respectively. See
[
3
]
for more on these terms in the context of linear string vibration.

2.2  Nonlinearity

Nonlinear dynamics of the string are described in a force density
\mathcal{F}
that can be expressed through the potential
\mathcal{V}
:

\mathcal{F}(x,t)=\frac{EA-T_{0}}{2}\partial_{x}\big(\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(\xi)\big),\quad\xi\triangleq\partial_{x}u,

where prime denotes a derivative of a scalar function.
A general model for the potential
\mathcal{V}
is given by Morse and Ingard
[
24
]
and includes both longitudinal and transverse motion of a string in two polarisations. In this work we neglect the longitudinal motion and one of the two polarisations, leading to the following nonlinear function
[
2
]
:

\mathcal{V}(\xi)=\Big(\sqrt{1+\xi^{2}}-1\Big)^{2}.

The potential (
2.2
) can be approximated by Taylor series as
\frac{1}{4}\xi^{4}
which corresponds to the model used in our previous work
[
36
]
. Further simplification can lead to a Kirchhoff-Carrier model
[
15
,
4
]
, where the nonlinearity is averaged over the length of the string. Compared to the Kirchhoff-Carrier model, which adequately reproduces only the pitch glide effect, the potential (
2.2
) is capable of capturing other perceptually important effects such as phantom partials.

2.3  Plucking Excitation

The string is excited by a pointwise external force
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{e}}
, which can be modelled as:

\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{e}}(x,t)=\delta(x-x_{\mathrm{e}})f_{\mathrm{e}}(t),

where
\delta(x-x_{\mathrm{e}})
is the Dirac delta function at the excitation location
x_{\mathrm{e}}
. The driving function
f_{\mathrm{e}}(t)
resembles a pluck of a string and is of the following form
[
1
]
:

f_{\mathrm{e}}(t)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}f_{\mathrm{amp}}\big[1-\cos\big(\frac{\pi t}{T_{\mathrm{e}}}\big)\big],\quad&t\in[0,T_{\mathrm{e}}]\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

Here
f_{\mathrm{amp}}
is the excitation amplitude in
\mathrm{N}
and
T_{\mathrm{e}}
is the excitation duration in
\mathrm{s}
. The excitation starting time is assumed to be zero.

2.4  Equation Scaling

In view of using the string model (
2
) for dataset generation, it is useful to reduce the number of physical parameters to the smallest possible set. We employ spatial scaling by introducing normalised variables for position
\hat{x}=\frac{x}{L}\in[0,1]
and displacement
\hat{u}=\frac{u}{L}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2}\hat{u}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{2}\partial_{\hat{x}}^{2}\hat{u}-\kappa^{2}\partial_{\hat{x}}^{4}\hat{u}-2\sigma_{0}\partial_{t}\hat{u}+2\hat{\sigma}_{1}\partial_{t}\partial_{\hat{x}}^{2}\hat{u}+{}

\displaystyle{}+\gamma^{2}\frac{\alpha^{2}-1}{2}\partial_{\hat{x}}\big(\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(\xi)\big)+\delta(\hat{x}-\hat{x}_{\mathrm{e}})\hat{f}_{\mathrm{e}}(t),

(4)

where

\gamma=\frac{1}{L}\sqrt{\frac{T_{0}}{\rho A}}
,

\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{EA}{T_{0}}}
,

\kappa=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sqrt{\frac{EI}{\rho A}}
,

\hat{\sigma}_{1}=\frac{\sigma_{1}}{L^{2}}

and

\hat{f}_{\mathrm{e}}(t)=\frac{1}{\rho AL^{2}}f_{\mathrm{e}}(t)
.

Thus, we have reduced a set of physical parameters
\{L,\rho,A,\allowbreak T_{0},E,I,\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1}\}
to a set of only five parameters
\{\gamma,\kappa,\alpha,\sigma_{0},\hat{\sigma}_{1}\}
. In the following sections we omit the “hat” while referring to the scaled string model (
4
).

3  MODAL DECOMPOSITION

The solution to equation (
4
) can be decomposed into a set of modes, yielding a finite-dimensional system when truncated to finite order
M
. The transverse displacement
u
is rewritten as a superposition of modal displacements
\mathbf{q}(t)=[q_{1}(t),\dots,q_{M}(t)]^{\mathrm{T}}
:

u(x,t)=\sum\limits_{m=1}^{M}\Phi_{m}(x)q_{m}(t)=\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}(x)\mathbf{q}(t),

where modal shapes
\Phi_{m}(x)=\sqrt{2}\sin(m\pi x),\;m=1,\dots,M
correspond to the solution of the eigenvalue problem for a stiff string under simply supported boundary conditions.

Substituting (
3
) into (
4
), left-multiplying by
\bm{\Phi}
and taking an
L^{2}
inner product over the interval
[0,1]
, we obtain the following second-order system of ODEs:

\ddot{\mathbf{q}}+2\bm{\Sigma}\dot{\mathbf{q}}+\bm{\Omega}^{2}\mathbf{q}=\nu^{2}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})+\bm{\Phi}(x_{\mathrm{e}})f_{\mathrm{e}}(t),

where
\nu=\gamma\sqrt{\frac{\alpha^{2}-1}{2}}
is treated as an independent parameter and matrices
\bm{\Sigma}
and
\bm{\Omega}
are defined using a
M\times M
diagonal matrix
\mathbf{B}
of modal wavenumbers
[\mathbf{B}]_{mm}=m\pi,\;m=1,\dots,M
as:

\bm{\Sigma}=\sigma_{0}+\sigma_{1}\mathbf{B}^{2},\quad\bm{\Omega}^{2}=\gamma^{2}\mathbf{B}^{2}+\kappa^{2}\mathbf{B}^{4}.

The system (
3
) can be rewritten in the first-order form using modal velocities
\mathbf{p}(t)=[p_{1}(t),\dots,p_{M}(t)]^{\mathrm{T}}
as:

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{p}\\
\dot{\mathbf{p}}=-2\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{p}-\bm{\Omega}^{2}\mathbf{q}+\nu^{2}\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})+\bm{\Phi}(x_{\mathrm{e}})f_{\mathrm{e}}(t)\end{cases}

3.1  Spectral Method

To obtain a computationally efficient expression for the dimensionless nonlinearity
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})\!\!:\mathbb{R}^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
, we employ a spectral method
[
35
,
34
]
to calculate spatial derivatives
\partial_{x}
on the grid
x_{l+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\frac{1}{M+1}\big(l+\frac{1}{2}\big),\;l=0,\dots,M
.

First, we calculate spatial derivatives in a modal domain by multiplying modal displacements
\mathbf{q}
by their respective wavenumbers
\mathbf{B}
. Second, we use the discrete cosine transform (DCT) to obtain spatial derivatives
\bm{\xi}=[\xi_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},\dots,\xi_{M+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}]^{\mathrm{T}}
on the grid
\{x_{l+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\}_{l=0}^{M}
. Finally, we apply the elementwise nonlinear function
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(\bm{\xi})=\allowbreak[\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(\xi_{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}),\dots,\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(\xi_{M+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})]^{\mathrm{T}}
, perform the inverse transform and another spatial differentiation in a modal domain. The closed-form expression for
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})
takes the form:

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})=-\frac{1}{\sqrt{M+1}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}\mathcal{V}^{\prime}(\bm{\xi}),\quad\bm{\xi}\triangleq\sqrt{M+1}\mathbf{C}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{q},

where
\mathbf{C}
is a truncated
M\times(M+1)
matrix for the orthonormal DCT-II:

[\mathbf{C}]_{ml}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{M+1}}\cos\bigg(\frac{\pi}{M+1}m\bigg(l+\frac{1}{2}\bigg)\bigg),\quad\vbox{\Let@\restore@math@cr\default@tag\halign{\hfil$\m@th\scriptstyle#$&$\m@th\scriptstyle{}#$\hfil\cr m&=1,\dots,M\\
l&=0,\ldots,M\crcr}}.

Using the fundamental theorem for line integrals
[
29
]
, it is possible to derive a potential
V(\mathbf{q})
for the dimensionless nonlinearity
\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})
:

\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{q})=-\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V(\mathbf{q}),\quad V(\mathbf{q})=\frac{1}{M+1}\sum\limits_{l=0}^{M}\mathcal{V}\big(\xi_{l+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big).

4  NUMERICAL SOLVER

4.1  Quadratisation

To guarantee stability of numerical simulation, we make use of the SAV technique
[
2
,
32
]
. SAV methods are based on quadratisation of the potential
V(\mathbf{q})
through transformation
\psi\triangleq\sqrt{2V(\mathbf{q})+\epsilon}
where
\epsilon>0
is a gauge constant. This transformation is well defined if the potential
V(\mathbf{q})
(
3.1
) is non-negative which is the case for the chosen nonlinear function (
2.2
).

After quadratisation, we append the auxiliary variable
\psi
to the state vector and rewrite the system (
3
) as follows:

\begin{cases}\dot{\mathbf{q}}=\mathbf{p}\\
\dot{\mathbf{p}}=-2\bm{\Sigma}\mathbf{p}-\bm{\Omega}^{2}\mathbf{q}-\nu^{2}\mathbf{g}\psi+\bm{\Phi}(x_{\mathrm{e}})f_{\mathrm{e}}(t)\\
\dot{\psi}=\mathbf{g}^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{p}\end{cases}

where
\mathbf{g}\triangleq\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{std}}(\mathbf{q})=\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}\psi
.

Furthermore, we add a control term to the system (
4.1
) as described by Risse et al.
[
30
]
to reduce the drift between numerical values of
\psi
and
\sqrt{2V(\mathbf{q})+\epsilon}
in discrete time. This results in modification of the coupling term
\mathbf{g}
:

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}\triangleq\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{std}}(\mathbf{q})+\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{mod}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},\psi),

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{mod}}(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{p},\psi)=-\lambda_{0}\Big(\psi-\sqrt{2V(\mathbf{q})+\epsilon}\Big)\frac{\sign(\mathbf{p})}{\sign(\mathbf{p})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{p}}.

4.2  Time Discretisation

We choose a time step
k
in
\mathrm{s}
, yielding a sampling rate
f_{\mathrm{s}}=\frac{1}{k}
, and approximate state variables by the time series
\mathbf{q}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
,
\mathbf{p}^{n}
and
\psi^{n}
on interleaved grids
t^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\big(n+\frac{1}{2}\big)k
and
t^{n}=nk
for
n=0,\dots,N-1
.
Using the difference operator
\delta_{t+}a^{n}=\frac{a^{n+1}-a^{n}}{k}
and the averaging operator
\mu_{t+}a^{n}=\frac{a^{n+1}+a^{n}}{2}
, we define the scheme as
[
30
]
:

\begin{cases}\delta_{t+}\mathbf{q}^{n-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\mathbf{p}^{n}\\
\begin{aligned} \delta_{t+}\mathbf{p}^{n}=&-2\bm{\Sigma}\mu_{t+}\mathbf{p}^{n}-\bm{\Omega}^{2}\mathbf{q}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}-{}\\
&{}-\nu^{2}\mathbf{g}^{n}\mu_{t+}\psi^{n}+\bm{\Phi}(x_{\mathrm{e}})f_{\mathrm{e}}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\end{aligned}\\
\delta_{t+}\psi^{n}=(\mathbf{g}^{n})^{\mathrm{T}}\mu_{t+}\mathbf{p}^{n}\end{cases}

where

\mathbf{g}^{n}=\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{std}}\big(\mathbf{q}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big)+\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{mod}}\big(\mu_{t+}\mathbf{q}^{n-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},\mathbf{p}^{n},\psi^{n}\big)

and

f_{\mathrm{e}}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=f_{\mathrm{e}}\big(t^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big)
.

Numerical energy for the scheme (
4.2
) is conserved and takes the form:

E^{n}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{p}^{n})^{\mathrm{T}}\mathbf{p}^{n}+\frac{1}{2}\big(\mathbf{q}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big)^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\Omega}^{2}\mathbf{q}^{n-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\frac{\nu^{2}}{2}(\psi^{n})^{2}.

The second term in
E^{n}
can be bounded to guarantee non-negativity of numerical energy, resulting in the same stability condition
[\bm{\Omega}]_{MM}<\frac{2}{k}
as for the linear system
[
2
]
.

Defining
\mathbf{q}^{n}\triangleq\mu_{t+}\mathbf{q}^{n-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, the scheme (
4.2
) can be written in an update form
(\mathbf{q}^{n},\mathbf{p}^{n},\psi^{n})\rightarrow(\mathbf{q}^{n+1},\mathbf{p}^{n+1},\psi^{n+1})
:

\begin{cases}\mathbf{q}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\mathbf{q}^{n}+\tfrac{k}{2}\mathbf{p}^{n}\\
\begin{aligned} \mathbf{p}^{n+1}&=\Big[\mathbf{I}+k\bm{\Sigma}+\tfrac{k^{2}\nu^{2}}{4}\mathbf{g}^{n}(\mathbf{g}^{n})^{\mathrm{T}}\Big]^{-1}\times{}\\
&{}\times\Big[\Big(\mathbf{I}-k\bm{\Sigma}-\tfrac{k^{2}\nu^{2}}{4}\mathbf{g}^{n}(\mathbf{g}^{n})^{\mathrm{T}}\Big)\mathbf{p}^{n}+{}\\
&{}+k\Big(-\bm{\Omega}^{2}\mathbf{q}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}-\nu^{2}\mathbf{g}^{n}\psi^{n}+\bm{\Phi}(x_{\mathrm{e}})f_{\mathrm{e}}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\Big)\Big]\end{aligned}\\
\mathbf{q}^{n+1}=\mathbf{q}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\tfrac{k}{2}\mathbf{p}^{n+1}\\
\psi^{n+1}=\psi^{n}+k(\mathbf{g}^{n})^{\mathrm{T}}\tfrac{\mathbf{p}^{n+1}+\mathbf{p}^{n}}{2}\end{cases}

The inverse appearing in (
4.2
) can be easily computed with the Sherman-Morrison formula
[
33
]
. Using (
3
), we obtain an audio output as
w^{n}=\bm{\Phi}^{\mathrm{T}}(x_{\mathrm{o}})\mathbf{q}^{n}
.

5  DIFFERENTIABLE MODEL

5.1  Neural Ordinary Differential Equations

NODEs can be defined through the following first-order system:

\frac{d\mathbf{y}}{dt}=\mathbf{h}_{\theta}(\mathbf{y},t),\quad\mathbf{y}(0)=\mathbf{y}_{0}.

Here
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}(t)\!\!:\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}
is an unknown function of time
t
,
\mathbf{y}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
is an initial condition and
\mathbf{h}_{\theta}(\mathbf{y},t)\!\!:\mathbb{R}^{K}\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{K}
is a neural network where
\theta
denotes the set of all learnable parameters and
K
denotes the state dimension. Generally, a simple architecture such as a multilayer perceptron is chosen for
\mathbf{h}_{\theta}(\mathbf{y},t)
. Chen et al.
[
6
]
have showed that the system (
5.1
) in combination with a numerical solver, labelled as ODENet, can be trained from data to reproduce dynamics of a target system.

Assume a target trajectory
\{\mathbf{y}_{0},\mathbf{y}^{1},\dots,\mathbf{y}^{N-1}\}
sampled on the time grid
\{t^{n}\}_{n=0}^{N-1}
. Given a predicted trajectory
\{\mathbf{y}_{0},\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{1},\dots,\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{N-1}\}
by a numerical solution to the initial value problem (
5.1
), i.e., a forward pass of the ODENet, we can construct an objective function
J(\theta)
such as mean squared error (MSE):

J(\theta)=\MSE(\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{n},\mathbf{y}^{n})\triangleq\frac{1}{KN}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{y}}^{n}-\mathbf{y}^{n}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},

where
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{2}
is the Euclidean norm. We search for a local minimum of
J(\theta)
using gradient-based optimisation techniques where the gradient
\nabla_{\theta}J
can be computed using the backpropagation algorithm
[
31
]
through internal operations of a numerical solver or the adjoint sensitivity method
[
27
,
6
]
. In most cases the objective function
J(\theta)
will be averaged for a finite set of target trajectories before each optimisation step.

5.2  Extension for Modal Synthesis

In the case of modal synthesis, there is a known ODE structure (
3
) which can serve an inductive bias for a NODEs framework
[
13
,
16
]
. In particular, we parametrise only a dimensionless memoryless nonlinear function
\mathbf{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})\!\!:\mathbb{R}^{M}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{M}
with a neural network, yielding a system of physics-informed NODEs:

\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\mathbf{q}}\\
\dot{\mathbf{p}}\end{bmatrix}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\hphantom{-}\mathbf{0}\hphantom{{}^{2}}&\hphantom{-}\mathbf{I}\hphantom{\bm{\Sigma}}\\
-\bm{\Omega}^{2}&-2\bm{\Sigma}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{q}\\
\mathbf{p}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathclap{\text{Linear vibration}}}{}+{}\nu^{2}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathclap{\text{Neural network}}}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\
\bm{\Phi}(x_{\mathrm{e}})\end{bmatrix}f_{\mathrm{e}}(t)}_{\mathclap{\text{Excitation}}}

As mentioned earlier, initial conditions for a state vector
\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{T}}=[\mathbf{q}^{\mathrm{T}},\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{T}}]
are assumed to be zero. To compute a forward pass of the physics-informed ODENet, we:

▶

set
\bm{\Sigma},\bm{\Omega},\nu,\bm{\Phi}(x_{\mathrm{e}})
in (
5.2
) using physical parameters of a target solution;

▶

precompute
f_{\mathrm{e}}^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=f_{\mathrm{e}}\big(t^{n+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\big),\;n=0,\dots,N-1
using (
2.3
) and excitation parameters of a target solution;

▶

use the numerical solver (
4.2
) as in the case of a regular system to produce a predicted trajectory.

The formulation (
5.2
) has strong implications. The exact expression for linear vibration exploits the periodic, harmonic and lossy nature of a musical system. Thus, we aid the optimisation process by constraining the space of possible solutions and improve interpretability of the model. Furthermore, the neural network
\mathbf{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})
is memoryless and dimensionless, thus does not depend on physical parameters of a system and external excitation. Theoretically, these parameters can be changed after the training as long as range of displacements
\mathbf{q}
stays the same as in a training dataset to simulate other configurations of a system.

5.3  Gradient Networks

The nature of the SAV technique (Section
4.1
) constrains possible network architectures for
\mathbf{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})
as we require existence of a closed-form and non-negative potential
V_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})
. To overcome this, we employ GradNets
[
5
]
that directly parametrise gradients of various function classes. In particular, we use the following architecture:

\mathbf{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})=-\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{T}}[\bm{\alpha}\odot\sigma(\mathbf{z})],\quad\mathbf{z}=\bm{\beta}\odot\mathbf{W}\mathbf{q}+\mathbf{b},

where
\odot
denotes the Hadamard product. Learnable parameters are the
H\times M
weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
, bias
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}
and scaling vectors
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}
and
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{H}
where
H
is the hidden dimension. An activation function
\sigma(x)\!\!:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is applied elementwise as
\sigma(\mathbf{z})=[\sigma(z_{1}),\dots,\sigma(z_{H})]^{\mathrm{T}}
.

We can notice a strong resemblance between the GradNet (
5.3
) and the spectral method (Section
3.1
). The weight matrix
\mathbf{W}
acts as a transform to a hidden space where we apply the elementwise nonlinear function
\sigma(\mathbf{z})
before performing an “inverse” transform with the transposed weight matrix
\mathbf{W}^{\mathrm{T}}
. Moreover, the potential (
3.1
) arising from the spectral method is expressed as a sum of convex ridge functions and the GradNet (
5.3
) with a monotonically-increasing activation function
\sigma
and non-negative scaling vectors
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
can universally approximate gradients of such functions
[
5
]
.

Assuming existence of an antiderivative function
\phi(x)\!\!:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
so that
\sigma=\phi^{\prime}
, the closed-form expression for
V_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})
takes the form:

\mathbf{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})=-\nabla_{\mathbf{q}}V_{\theta}(\mathbf{q}),\quad V_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})=\sum_{i=1}^{H}\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\beta_{i}}\phi(z_{i}).

If
\phi
is a non-negative function, the potential
V_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})
is also non-negative. The scaling vectors can be redefined to avoid ill-conditioning from division.

6  EVALUATION

The physics-informed ODENet (Section
5
) was implemented in the PyTorch framework
[
26
]
and evaluated in the case of nonlinear transverse string vibration. The training was conducted on a cloud server equipped with NVIDIA GeForce RTX 2080 Ti GPUs with
\mathrm{12}

\mathrm{GB}
VRAM. The source code used for dataset generation and training is available in the accompanying GitHub repository
2
2

2

https://github.com/victorzheleznov/jaes-modal-node
.

For the parametrisation of
\mathbf{f}_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})
we used the GradNet (
5.3
) with hidden dimension
H=1000
. A leaky rectified linear unit (Leaky ReLU) was used as an activation
\sigma
which is a monotonically-increasing function with a non-negative antiderivative
\phi
. Kaiming initialisation
[
12
]
was used for the initial weights
\mathbf{W}
and the biases
\mathbf{b}
were initialised to zero. Logarithms of scaling vectors
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\beta}
were used as learnable parameters to enforce non-negativity and were initialised around zero using a normal distribution.

The training loss was the MSE (
5.1
) taken over the state vector
\mathbf{y}^{\mathrm{T}}=[\mathbf{q}^{\mathrm{T}},\mathbf{p}^{\mathrm{T}}]
, excluding the auxiliary variable
\psi
arising from the SAV method. This implied that the potential
V_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})
could be shifted by a constant relative to the target potential (
3.1
) since dynamics of the system and the training loss would not be affected. Backpropagation was performed using internal operations of the numerical solver (
4.2
), i.e., the “discretise-then-optimise” method, which is generally a preferred approach due to its gradient accuracy, speed and straightforward implementation
[
13
]
. Calculation of the control term
\mathbf{g}_{\mathrm{mod}}
was excluded from the computational graph as it was found to significantly compromise training by steering the potential
V_{\theta}(\mathbf{q})
to incorrect values of the auxiliary variable
\psi
. The Adam optimiser
[
14
]
was used with default parameters. Three separate datasets for training, validation and testing were used (Section
6.1
). The training was performed for 2000 epochs using a cluster of four GPUs to parallelise batches of data. The resulting model was chosen based upon the lowest loss obtained on the validation dataset.

For training we used a variation of the teacher forcing technique
[
11
]
by splitting up a target trajectory into
\mathrm{1}

\mathrm{ms}
segments and providing true initial conditions for each segment to the ODENet. Since the numerical solver (
4.2
) is given as a one step update we had access to both displacement and velocity of the target numerical solution at each time step, and thus initial conditions for each segment. The auxiliary variable was initialised as
\psi_{0}=\sqrt{2V_{\theta}(\mathbf{q}_{0})+\epsilon}
using initial displacements
\mathbf{q}_{0}
to produce a consistent initial condition
[
30
]
. In addition, the excitation function (
2.3
) was shifted in time to reflect a new starting point for integration. The main reason for using this technique was to speed up training as the number of integration steps in the numerical solver is significantly reduced. These integration steps can not be parallelised in time. Moreover, the likelihood of vanishing and exploding gradient problems during optimisation is also reduced by this technique as backpropagation on long time series is avoided
[
13
]
.

6.1  Datasets

Three datasets were independently generated using the nonlinear transverse string model (Section
2
). The training and test datasets both consisted of 60 trajectories and the validation dataset consisted of 20 trajectories. Each trajectory included both displacement and velocity information for each mode of a string. The number of modes was equal to
M=75
. Taking into account the effect of stiffness, this covered the
\mathrm{10}

\mathrm{kHz}
range for the lowest considered fundamental frequency at
\mathrm{61{.}74}

\mathrm{Hz}
. Oversampling by a factor of two for standard sampling rates
\mathrm{44{.}1}

\mathrm{kHz}
and
\mathrm{48}

\mathrm{kHz}
was used to avoid aliasing due to the nonlinear effects for high amplitude excitations.

Simulation parameters used for the datasets are provided in Table

T_{\mathrm{sim}}

\frac{\gamma}{2}
, spanned a half-octave range from
\mathrm{61{.}74}

\mathrm{Hz}
(B1 note) to
\mathrm{87{.}31}

\mathrm{Hz}
(F2 note). For validation and testing, the next half-octave range from
\mathrm{87{.}31}

\mathrm{Hz}

\mathrm{123{.}47}

\mathrm{Hz}
(B2 note) was used. In addition, the simulation duration was increased to account for a longer decay time due to a smaller damping parameter

\kappa

6.2  Results

\mathbf{x}^{n}

\MSE_{\mathrm{rel}}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{n},\mathbf{x}^{n})\triangleq\frac{\sum_{n}\left\lVert\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{n}-\mathbf{x}^{n}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{n}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}^{n}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}},

where
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{n}
is a prediction given by the model.

\tilde{\mathbf{q}}^{n}

As can be seen, metrics for the validation and test datasets have the same order of magnitude as for the training dataset and do not degrade for unseen simulation parameters. This suggests that the sampling rate and duration of simulation can be changed after the training and the model generalises to unseen physical parameters as long as the range of displacements

7  CONCLUSION

We have presented here a differentiable modal synthesis model that is capable of learning nonlinear dynamics from data. The proposed approach separates the linear vibration of a system from the nonlinear coupling between the modes described by a dimensionless potential function. We employed gradient networks (GradNets)
[
5
]
that universally approximate gradients of such functions and made use of the scalar auxiliary variable (SAV) technique
[
2
,
32
,
30
]
to ensure numerical stability in simulation. For the case study of nonlinear transverse string vibration, we have shown that the sampling rate and duration of simulation can be easily changed after the training and the model generalises to unseen physical parameters, resulting in a flexible and controllable sound synthesis approach.

Future work will be focused on using this framework for learning from acoustic recordings of string instruments. This would require improvements in the training methodology, most notably in the training loss, as we would not have the reference data for displacements and velocities of each mode. It is theorised that the proposed approach could be used to expand the timbral range of a real instrument in a digital domain and synthesise sounds which were not part of the recorded data by changing physical parameters after the training. In addition, physics-informed architectures like that presented here have the potential to address problems where the underlying physical phenomena are not fully understood, e.g., the bowed string in musical acoustics
[
10
]
.

APPENDIX

A.1  Adjustment for Excitation Amplitude

We consider the following initial value problem for a harmonic oscillator under the driving function
f_{\mathrm{e}}(t)
(
2.3
):

\ddot{q}+\omega^{2}q=f_{\mathrm{e}}(t),\quad q(0)=0,\quad\dot{q}(0)=0.

Using the Laplace transform, we can obtain terms of the solution oscillating at the frequency
\omega
:

\displaystyle q(t)\approx

\displaystyle-f_{\mathrm{amp}}\frac{\big(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{e}}}{2}\big)^{2}}{2\big[\big(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{e}}}{2}\big)^{2}-\omega^{2}\big]\omega^{2}}\cos(\omega t)+{}

\displaystyle{}+f_{\mathrm{amp}}\frac{\big(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{e}}}{2}\big)^{2}-2\omega^{2}}{2\big[\big(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{e}}}{2}\big)^{2}-\omega^{2}\big]\omega^{2}}\cos(\omega(t-T_{\mathrm{e}})),

where
\omega_{\mathrm{e}}\triangleq\frac{2\pi}{T_{\mathrm{e}}}
.
Under the assumption
\omega_{\mathrm{e}}\gg\omega
, we can approximate
\cos(\omega(t-T_{\mathrm{e}}))\approx\cos(\omega t)-\omega T_{\mathrm{e}}\sin(\omega t)
:

\displaystyle q(t)\approx

\displaystyle-f_{\mathrm{amp}}\frac{1}{\big(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{e}}}{2}\big)^{2}-\omega^{2}}\cos(\omega t)-{}

\displaystyle{}-f_{\mathrm{amp}}\frac{\big[\big(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{e}}}{2}\big)^{2}-2\omega^{2}\big]T_{\mathrm{e}}}{2\big[\big(\frac{\omega_{\mathrm{e}}}{2}\big)^{2}-\omega^{2}\big]\omega}\sin(\omega t).

Amplitude of the displacement
q(t)
is dominated by the second term and can be approximated as
f_{\mathrm{amp}}\frac{T_{\mathrm{e}}}{2\omega}
. Thus, we can keep the range of displacement
q(t)
roughly the same by linearly scaling the excitation amplitude
f_{\mathrm{amp}}
with the frequency
\omega
.
