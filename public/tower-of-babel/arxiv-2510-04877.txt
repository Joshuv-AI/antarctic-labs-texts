The tetrahedral Horn problem and asymptotics of 𝑼 ⁡ ( 𝒏 ) \boldsymbol{U(n)} 𝟔 ​ 𝒋 \boldsymbol{6j} symbols
Anton Alekseev 1, Matthias Christandl 2, Thomas C. Fraser 2

Abstract
. Horn’s problem is concerned with characterizing the eigenvalues ( a , b , c ) (a,b,c) of Hermitian matrices ( A , B , C ) (A,B,C) satisfying the constraint A + B = C A+B=C and forming the edges of a triangle in the space of Hermitian matrices. It has deep connections to tensor product invariants, Littlewood-Richardson coefficients, geometric invariant theory and the intersection theory of Schubert varieties. This paper concerns the tetrahedral Horn problem which aims to characterize the tuples of eigenvalues ( a , b , c , d , e , f ) (a,b,c,d,e,f) of Hermitian matrices ( A , B , C , D , E , F ) (A,B,C,D,E,F) forming the edges of a tetrahedron, and thus satisfying the constraints A + B = C A+B=C , B + D = F B+D=F , D + C = E D+C=E and A + F = E A+F=E . Here we derive new inequalities satisfied by the Schur-polynomials of such eigenvalues and, using eigenvalue estimation techniques from quantum information theory, prove their satisfaction up to degree k k implies the existence of approximate solutions with error O ⁡ ( ln ⁡ k / k ) O(\ln k/k) . Moreover, the existence of these tetrahedra is related to the semiclassical asymptotics of the 6 ​ j 6j -symbols for the unitary group U ⁡ ( n ) U(n) , which are maps between multiplicity spaces that encode the associativity relation for tensor products of irreducible representations. Using our techniques, we prove the asymptotics of norms of these 6 ​ j 6j -symbols are either inverse-polynomial or exponential depending on whether there exists such tetrahedra of Hermitian matrices.

The case of

Beyond the superficial connection between the imposed constraints and the resulting geometry depicted in

Our contributions

In this paper, we provide new necessary inequalities for the tetrahedral Horn problem and moreover prove their sufficiency.
In order to describe these conditions, we make the following simplifying assumption.
Denote by
\mathcal{H}^{+}_{n}\subset\mathcal{H}_{n}
the set of non-negative Hermitian matrices, and by
\mathbb{R}^{+}_{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\geq}_{n}
the corresponding set of possible eigenvalues.
We claim that the tetrahedral Horn problem can be reduced to the problem of characterizing the subset of non-negative eigenvalues:

(9)

\Tetra^{+}(n)\coloneqq(\mathbb{R}^{+}_{n})^{6}\cap\Tetra(n).

This restriction to non-negative eigenvalues is justified because
\Tetra(n)
is closed under certain affine transformations (for more details see
23
in
Appendix

A
).
Additionally, we define the following distance measure:

(10)

D\!\left(\!\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&b&c\\
d&e&f\end{array}\!\right)\coloneqq\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}C=A+B\\
F=B+D\\
E=C+D\\
E=A+F\end{subarray}}\begin{array}[]{c}[\left\lVert a-\eig A\right\rVert_{1}^{2}+\left\lVert b-\eig B\right\rVert_{1}^{2}+\left\lVert c-\eig C\right\rVert_{1}^{2}+\\
\left\lVert d-\eig D\right\rVert_{1}^{2}+\left\lVert e-\eig E\right\rVert_{1}^{2}+\left\lVert f-\eig F\right\rVert_{1}^{2}]^{\frac{1}{2}},\end{array}

where
\left\lVert x\right\rVert_{1}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|x_{i}\right|
is a distance measure which emerges naturally from the probabilistic techniques used in this paper.
Note that
D\!\left(\!\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&b&c\\
d&e&f\end{array}\!\right)\geq 0
with
D\!\left(\!\begin{array}[]{ccc}a&b&c\\
d&e&f\end{array}\!\right)=0
only if
(a,b,c,d,e,f)\in\Tetra(n)
.

Our methods rely on the representation theory of the group
G=U(n)
.
We denote by
\mathbb{Z}^{\geq}_{n}=\{(\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n})\mid\lambda_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\}

G=U(n)

by
\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{n}=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{\geq}_{n}\mid\lambda_{n}\geq 0\}

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{n}

\lambda
by
|\lambda|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}
, and the product of the hook lengths of boxes in
\lambda

H_{\lambda}=\prod_{b\in\lambda}h_{\lambda}(b)
(the hook length of box
b

The irreducible representations
V_{\lambda}
corresponding to positive dominant weights are polynomial, and they extend to the matrix algebra
\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{C})

It will be convenient to use rescaled characters of representations,

(11)

\varphi_{\lambda}(X)\coloneq\frac{1}{H_{\lambda}}\chi_{\lambda}(X),

extended to
X\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{C})

Note that if
x=\eig X

\chi_{\lambda}(X)

Irreducible representations

(12)

V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta}\cong\bigoplus_{\gamma}C_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\otimes V_{\gamma},

C_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\coloneqq\Hom(V_{\gamma},V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta})

Note that a necessary condition for

The associativity isomorphism between triple tensor products

\bigoplus_{\phi}C^{\epsilon}_{\alpha\phi}\otimes C^{\phi}_{\beta\delta}

Here we note a necessary condition for

\delta&\epsilon&\phi\end{array}\!\right\}\neq 0

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\alpha|+|\beta|&=|\gamma|,\quad|\beta|+|\delta|=|\phi|,\\

For

We will always assume that the space

All representations

The tensor product of two irreducible representations admits a unique decomposition of the form

(20)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:tensor_{p}roduct}V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta}\cong\bigoplus_{\gamma}C_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\otimes V_{\gamma},

where

Hermitian structures on

We let
\Pi_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}
denote the corresponding orthogonal projection operator.
Using this notation, we have for all

(21)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:operatorproduct}\pi_{\alpha}(g)\otimes\pi_{\beta}(g)\cong\bigoplus_{\gamma}\id_{C_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}}\otimes\pi_{\gamma}(g),

Triple tensor products can be decomposed in two alternative ways:

(22)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta})\otimes V_{\delta}&\cong\bigoplus_{\gamma,\epsilon}(C_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\otimes C_{\gamma\delta}^{\epsilon})\otimes V_{\epsilon},\\
V_{\alpha}\otimes(V_{\beta}\otimes V_{\delta})&\cong\bigoplus_{\phi,\epsilon}(C_{\alpha\phi}^{\epsilon}\otimes C_{\beta\delta}^{\phi})\otimes V_{\epsilon}.\end{split}

We let
\Pi_{(\alpha\beta)\delta}^{\gamma,\epsilon}
and
\Pi_{\alpha(\beta\delta)}^{\epsilon,\phi}
denote the corresponding orthogonal projection operators.
Since the composition of these two projectors is a
G

V_{\epsilon}
and itself, Schur’s Lemma implies
\Pi_{(\alpha\beta)\delta}^{\gamma,\epsilon}\Pi_{\alpha(\beta\delta)}^{\epsilon,\phi}
acts as the identity on

and as the map

(23)

\left\{\!\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\delta&\epsilon&\phi\end{array}\!\right\}:C_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}\otimes C_{\gamma\delta}^{\epsilon}\to C_{\alpha\phi}^{\epsilon}\otimes C_{\beta\delta}^{\phi},

called the

-symbol for

, on the multiplicity spaces.
Concretely, our normalization convention for

(24)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:prod_{p}roj_{6}j}\Pi_{(\alpha\beta)\delta}^{\gamma,\epsilon}\Pi_{\alpha(\beta\delta)}^{\epsilon,\phi}\simeq\left\{\!\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\delta&\epsilon&\phi\end{array}\!\right\}\otimes\id_{V_{\epsilon}}

where

Recall that for Hermitian vector spaces

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left\lVert O\right\rVert_{\infty}&=\max_{v\in V_{1},\left\lVert v\right\rVert_{V_{1}}=1}\left\lVert O(v)\right\rVert_{V_{2}},\\
\left\lVert O\right\rVert_{2}&=\Tr_{V_{1}}[O^{\dagger}O]^{\frac{1}{2}}=\Tr_{V_{2}}[OO^{\dagger}]^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

2.1.3.
The function
\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}
and the binomial theorem

It is convenient to introduce the following functions of two matrices,

(37)

\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)=\Tr_{V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta}}(\Phi_{\alpha}(X)\otimes\Phi_{\beta}(Y))\Pi_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}.

Proposition 4
.

For
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{n}
and
X,Y\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{C})
, we have

(38)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:varphi_{p}roduct_{s}um}\varphi_{\alpha}(X)\varphi_{\beta}(Y)=\sum_{\gamma}\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y).

For
X,Y\in\mathcal{H}^{+}_{n}
, the function
\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)
takes non-negative real values.

Proof.

To prove the summation identity, note that

(39)

\sum_{\gamma}\Pi_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}=\id_{V_{\alpha}}\otimes\id_{V_{\beta}},

which then implies

(40)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\gamma}\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)&=\sum_{\gamma}\Tr_{V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta}}(\Phi_{\alpha}(X)\otimes\Phi_{\alpha}(Y))\Pi_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma},\\
&=\Tr_{V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta}}(\Phi_{\alpha}(X)\otimes\Phi_{\alpha}(Y)),\\
&=\varphi_{\alpha}(X)\varphi_{\beta}(Y).\end{split}

For the claim of positivity, note that
\Pi_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}
is a orthogonal projection operator which implies
\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)
is the trace of the orthogonal restriction of the non-negative Hermitian operator
(\Phi_{\alpha}(X)\otimes\Phi_{\alpha}(Y))
over the subspace
(C_{\alpha\beta}\otimes V_{\gamma})\subseteq V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta}
, meaning
\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)\geq 0
as claimed.
∎

Theorem 5

(Binomial theorem)
.

For
X,Y\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{C})
and
\gamma\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{n}
, we have

(41)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:varphi_{s}um_{s}um}\varphi_{\gamma}(X+Y)=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y).

Proof.

Let
k=|\gamma|
and let
\rho_{k}
denote the representation of the monoid
(S_{k}\times\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
on the space
(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes k}
, and
\Pi^{\gamma}
the orthogonal projector to the summand
W_{\gamma}\otimes V_{\gamma}
in the Schur-Weyl decomposition
Eq.

35
.
Recall that
\dim(W_{\lambda})=|\lambda|!/H_{\lambda}
.
Then we have for all
k
and
X\in\Mat_{n}(\mathbb{C}))
,

(42)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi_{\lambda}(X)=\frac{\dim(W_{\lambda})}{k!}\Tr_{V_{\lambda}}[\pi_{\lambda}(X)]=\frac{1}{k!}\Tr_{(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes k}}[\Pi_{\lambda}\rho_{k}(X)].\end{split}

Moreover, we have

(43)

(X+Y)^{\otimes k}=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{1}{l!(k-l)!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\rho_{k}(\sigma)(X^{\otimes l}\otimes Y^{\otimes(k-l)})\rho_{k}(\sigma^{-1})

which is a non-commutative counterpart of the binomial decomposition.
We then expand

(44)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\varphi_{\gamma}(X+Y)\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{k!}\Tr_{(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes k}}\Pi^{\gamma}(X+Y)^{\otimes k},\\
&\quad=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{l=0}^{k}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}\frac{1}{l!(k-l)!}\Tr_{(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes k}}\Pi^{\gamma}\rho_{k}(\sigma)(X^{\otimes l}\otimes Y^{\otimes(k-l)})\rho_{k}(\sigma^{-1}).\end{split}

Here we make use of cyclicity of the trace and the
S_{k}
-equivariance of
\Pi^{\gamma}
, i.e.,
\rho_{k}(\sigma^{-1})\Pi^{\gamma}\rho_{k}(\sigma)=\Pi^{\gamma}
to obtain

(45)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi_{\gamma}(X+Y)=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{1}{l!(k-l)!}\Tr_{(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes k}}\Pi^{\gamma}(X^{\otimes l}\otimes Y^{\otimes(k-l)}).\end{split}

Now we freely insert operator identities
\sum_{\alpha\vdash k-l}\Pi^{\alpha}=\id_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}^{\otimes k-l}
and
\sum_{\beta\vdash l}\Pi^{\beta}=\id_{\mathbb{C}^{n}}^{\otimes l}
to obtain the claimed result:

(46)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi_{\gamma}(X+Y)&=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{1}{|\alpha|!|\beta|!}\Tr_{(\mathbb{C}^{n})^{\otimes k}}\Pi^{\gamma}(\Pi^{\alpha}X^{\otimes l}\otimes\Pi^{\beta}Y^{\otimes(k-l)}),\\
&=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\frac{1}{H_{\alpha}H_{\beta}}\Tr_{V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta}}\Pi_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}(\pi_{\alpha}(X)\otimes\pi_{\beta}(Y)),\\
&=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\Tr_{V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta}}\Pi_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}(\Phi_{\alpha}(X)\otimes\Phi_{\beta}(Y)),\\
&=\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\phi_{\alpha\beta}^{\gamma}(X,Y).\end{split}

∎

For

From the definition of

2.1.5.
Estimates for

Here we derive bounds on the magnitude of the quantity

To begin we need the following proposition.

Proposition 7
.

Let
P,Q
be two orthogonal projections, and
\Omega
be a non-negative Hermitian operator.
Then,

(50)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:cauchy_{s}chwarz_{i}neq_{d}ouble_{p}roj}\left|\Tr(Q\Omega P)\right|\leq\left\lVert PQ\right\rVert_{\infty}(\Tr Q\Omega)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Tr P\Omega)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Proof.

Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the Hilbert-Schmidt norm, we obtain

(51)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\left|\Tr(Q\Omega P)\right|&=\left|\Tr(PQ\Omega P)\right|,\\
&=\left|\Tr(PQ\Omega^{\frac{1}{2}})(\Omega^{\frac{1}{2}}P)\right|,\\
&\leq(\Tr PQ\Omega QP)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Tr P\Omega P)^{\frac{1}{2}},\\
&=(\Tr PQ\Omega Q)^{\frac{1}{2}}(\Tr P\Omega)^{\frac{1}{2}}.\end{split}

Now let
\{v_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
denote a set of orthonormal eigenvectors for the operator
Q\Omega Q
with eigenvalues
\omega_{j}\geq 0
.
Then,

(52)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Tr(PQ\Omega Q)&=\Tr(QP^{2}Q)(Q\Omega Q),\\
&=\sum_{j}\omega_{j}\left\lVert PQv_{j}\right\rVert^{2},\\
&\leq\left\lVert PQ\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\sum_{j}\omega_{j},\\
&=\left\lVert PQ\right\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\Tr(Q\Omega).\end{split}

This completes the proof.
∎

Applying this proposition to the definition of
\varphi^{\alpha\beta\delta}_{\gamma\epsilon\phi}(X,Y,Z)

Proposition 8
.

Let

\left|\varphi^{\alpha\beta\delta}_{\gamma\epsilon\phi}(X,Y,Z)\right|\leq\left\lVert\left\{\!\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\delta&\epsilon&\phi\end{array}\!\right\}\right\rVert_{\infty}\varphi^{\alpha\beta\delta}_{\_\epsilon\phi}(X,Y,Z)^{\frac{1}{2}}\varphi^{\alpha\beta\delta}_{\gamma\epsilon\_}(X,Y,Z)^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Let

Under the assumption that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left|\Tr[\Pi_{\alpha(\beta\delta)}^{\epsilon,\phi}\Omega_{\alpha\beta\delta}^{X,Y,Z}\Pi_{(\alpha\beta)\delta}^{\gamma,\epsilon}]\right|\\
&\qquad\leq\left\lVert\Pi_{(\alpha\beta)\delta}^{\gamma,\epsilon}\Pi_{\alpha(\beta\delta)}^{\epsilon,\phi}\right\rVert_{\infty}\Tr[\Pi_{(\alpha\beta)\delta}^{\gamma,\epsilon}\Omega_{\alpha\beta\delta}^{X,Y,Z}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\Tr[\Pi_{\alpha(\beta\delta)}^{\epsilon,\phi}\Omega_{\alpha\beta\delta}^{X,Y,Z}]^{\frac{1}{2}}\end{split}

2.2.1.
Measures on finite sets

Recall that for a finite set

For two measures

(58)

K(p,q)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\left|p_{i}-q_{i}\right|.

Another measure of similarity between distributions

and

(59)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:Bhattacharyya_{c}oefficient}\BC(p,q)=\sum_{i=1}^{r}\sqrt{p_{i}q_{i}}.

For all distributions

lies in the interval

, and
\BC(p,q)=1
if and only if

.
Recall the following relation between
the Kolmogorov distance and the Bhattacharyya coefficient (see
e.g.

[

, Proposition 5, Equation (41)]

(60)

1-\BC(p,q)\leq K(p,q)\leq\sqrt{1-\BC(p,q)^{2}}.

This implies
1

1

Here we used the inequality
\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\leq\exp(-x^{2}/2)
which holds for all

.

(61)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:BC_{n}orm_{e}stimate}\BC(p,q)\leq\sqrt{1-K(p,q)^{2}}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}K(p,q)^{2}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8}\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}\right).

We extend the Bhattacharyya coefficient to non-normalized non-negative tuples where

In that case, the

Bhattacharyya coefficient satisfies

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:bhattacharyya_{v}s_{d}istance}\frac{\BC(p,q)}{\tau}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{8\tau^{2}}\left\lVert p-q\right\rVert_{1}^{2}\right).

Finally, the Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence is defined as

Proposition 12

(Eigenvalue estimation)

Proof.

From

What remains is to bound the multinomial distribution

To this end, note that

\displaystyle\begin{split}m_{x}(\lambda)&=\frac{k!}{\Tr[x]^{k}}\frac{x_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}}{\lambda_{1}!}\cdots\frac{x_{n}^{\lambda_{n}}}{\lambda_{n}!},\\

Appendix A
Symmetries of tetrahedral eigenvalues

There are numerous types of symmetries present in the statement of the tetrahedral Horn problem.
The purpose of this section is to describe the scaling, inversion, tetrahedral, and shifting symmetries of the problem.

Proposition 17

(Scaling)
.

Let
(a,b,c,d,e,f)\in\mathbb{R}^{\geq}_{n}
and let
s\in(0,\infty)
be a positive real number.
Then
(sa,sb,sc,sd,se,sf)\in\Tetra(n)
if and only
(a,b,c,d,e,f)\in\Tetra(n)
.

Proof.

The statement follows from the fact that
\eig(sX)=s\eig(X)
holds for all Hermitian matrices
X
and positive scalars
s
and that each of the constraints in
Eq.

3
are homogeneous, e.g., we have
sA+sB-sC=0
if and only if
A+B-C=0
because
s\neq 0
.
∎

One may wonder whether it is possible to extend the scaling symmetry to negative values for
s
.
Indeed, this can be accomplished, provided one accounts for the fact that negating a Hermitian matrix both negates and reverses the sorting order of its eigenvalues.

Proposition 18

(Inversion)
.

Let
(a,b,c,d,e,f)
be a tuple of candidate eigenvalues.
Given any list of non-increasing eigenvalues
x=(x_{1}\geq\cdots\geq x_{n})
, let
x^{*}
denote the inverted list of resorted and negated eigenvalues:
x^{*}=(-x_{n}\geq\cdots\geq-x_{1})
.
Then
(a,b,c,d,e,f)\in\Tetra(n)
if and only if
(a^{*},b^{*},c^{*},d^{*},e^{*},f^{*})\in\Tetra(n)
.

Proof.

The statement holds because
\eig(-X)=x^{*}
whenever
\eig(X)=x
and moreover the tuple of matrices
(A,B,C,D,E,F)
satisfies
Eq.

3
if and only if the tuple of negated matrices
(-A,-B,-C,-D,-E,-F)
satisfies
Eq.

3
.
∎

The inversion symmetry
18
is just one element belonging to a group of
48
symmetries consisting of inversions of eigenvalues,
x\rightarrow x^{*}
, or permutations of elements in the tuple
(a,b,c,d,e,f)
.

Proposition 19

(Signed permutation)
.

The following transformations preserve
\Tetra(n)
:

(140)

\displaystyle\begin{split}T_{1}\coloneqq(a,b,c,d,e,f)&\mapsto(a,f,e,d^{*},c,b),\\
T_{2}\coloneqq(a,b,c,d,e,f)&\mapsto(c,e^{*},d^{*},f,b,a^{*}).\end{split}

Moreover,
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
generate a finite group of order
48
with presentation

(141)

\langle T_{1},T_{2}\mid T_{1}^{2},T_{2}^{4},(T_{1}T_{2})^{6},(T_{2}T_{1}T_{2}T_{1}T_{2})^{2}\rangle.

Proof.

To begin, notice that both
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are invertible (indeed the first operation has order two, while the second operation has order four).
The claim that these two operations generate a finite group of order
48
can be checked explicitly using computer software.
Now suppose
(a,b,c,d,e,f)
are the eigenvalues of a tuple of Hermitian matrices
(A,B,C,D,E,F)
satisfying
Eq.

3
.
Then the tuple
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime},C^{\prime},D^{\prime},E^{\prime},F^{\prime})=(A,F,E,-D,C,B)
(which has the eigenvalues
(a,f,e,d^{*},c,b)
) also satisfies the tetrahedral conditions because

(142)

\displaystyle\begin{split}A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}-C^{\prime}&=(A+F-E)=0,\\
B^{\prime}+D^{\prime}-F^{\prime}&=-(B+D-F)=0,\\
C^{\prime}+D^{\prime}-E^{\prime}&=-(C+D-E)=0,\\
A^{\prime}+F^{\prime}-E^{\prime}&=(A+B-C)=0.\end{split}

Therefore,
(a,b,c,d,e,f)\in\Tetra(n)
implies
(a^{*},c,b,d,f,e)\not\in\Tetra(n)
also.
Moreover, as the operation

Remark 20

Proposition 21

(Shifting symmetry)
.

Let
(a,b,c,d,e,f)\in(\mathbb{R}^{\geq}_{n})^{6}
and let
x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}
be fixed.
Define shifted eigenvalues
(\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\tilde{c},\tilde{d},\tilde{e},\tilde{f})
by

(147)

\displaystyle\tilde{a}_{i}\coloneqq a_{i}+x,\qquad

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{i}\coloneqq b_{i}+y,\qquad

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{i}\coloneqq c_{i}+x+y,

\displaystyle\tilde{d}_{i}\coloneqq d_{i}+z,\qquad

\displaystyle\tilde{e}_{i}\coloneqq e_{i}+x+y+z,\qquad

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{i}\coloneqq f_{i}+y+z.

Then
(\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\tilde{c},\tilde{d},\tilde{e},\tilde{f})\in\Tetra(n)
if and only if
(a,b,c,d,e,f)\in\Tetra(n)
.

Proof.

Given a Hermitian matrix
A
with eigenvalues
\eig(A)=a
and a constant
w\in\mathbb{R}
, the eigenvalues of
A+wI
(where
I
is the identity matrix) are

(148)

\eig(A+wI)=(a_{1}+w\geq\cdots\geq a_{n}+w).

The claim then follows because the tuple of matrices

(149)

\displaystyle\tilde{A}\coloneqq A+xI,\qquad

\displaystyle\tilde{B}\coloneqq B+yI,\qquad

\displaystyle\tilde{C}\coloneqq C+(x+y)I,

\displaystyle\tilde{D}\coloneqq D+zI,\qquad

\displaystyle\tilde{E}\coloneqq E+(x+y+z)I,\qquad

\displaystyle\tilde{F}\coloneqq F+(y+z)I,

have eigenvalues
(\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\tilde{c},\tilde{d},\tilde{e},\tilde{f})
and satisfy the tetrahedral constraints in
Eq.

3
if and only if
(A,B,C,D,E,F)
satisfy the tetrahedral constraints in
Eq.

3
.
∎

As a corollary of the shifting symmetries (
21
) one concludes that to solve the tetrahedral Horn problem, in a certain sense, it suffices to solve the tetrahedral Horn problem merely for traceless matrices (
22
) or, alternatively, for non-negative or positive matrices (

Corollary 22

(Traceless eigenvalues)
.

Let
(a,b,c,d,e,f)
be a tuple of candidate eigenvalues and for any list of

denote the traceless list of eigenvalues obtained by shifting

by

Then
(a,b,c,d,e,f)\in\Tetra(n)
if and only if (i) their traces
({\Tr[a]},{\Tr[b]},{\Tr[c]},{\Tr[d]},{\Tr[e]},{\Tr[f]})
are tetrahedral (they satisfy
Eq.

4
) and (ii)
(a^{0},b^{0},c^{0},d^{0},e^{0},f^{0})\in\Tetra(n)

Proof.

This “only if” portion of the statement follows from both the tetrahedral trace conditions (

) and applications of the shifting symmetry

with
(x,y,z)
values

(150)

x=-n^{-1}\Tr(A),\qquad y=-n^{-1}\Tr(B)\qquad z=-n^{-1}\Tr(D).

The “if” portion of the statement follows from the shifting symmetry applied to the negated
(x,y,z)
values.
∎

The next corollary of the shifting symmetry proves that to solve the tetrahedral Horn problem it suffices to solve the tetrahedral Horn problem for non-negative (or even positive) eigenvalues only.
Indeed, one can take the shifting parameters
(x,y,z)
appearing in

to be sufficiently large.
The following corollary establishes, in a certain sense, the minimum shifting required to ensure non-negative (or positive) eigenvalues for all six Hermitian matrices.

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:min_{e}ig_{i}neqs}c_{n}\geq a_{n}+b_{n},\qquad f_{n}\geq b_{n}+d_{n},\qquad e_{n}\geq d_{n}+c_{n},\qquad e_{n}\geq a_{n}+f_{n}.

be such that

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:positive_{s}hift}x\geq-a_{n},\qquad y\geq-b_{n},\qquad z\geq-d_{n},

and let

be shifted eigenvalues given by

Then

(153)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:positive_{s}hift_{i}ff}(a,b,c,d,e,f)\in\Tetra(n)\iff(\tilde{a},\tilde{b},\tilde{c},\tilde{d},\tilde{e},\tilde{f})\in\Tetra^{+}(n).

Remark 24
.

It is worth emphasizing that our main theorem,
1
, provides necessary and sufficient conditions for determining membership in
\Tetra^{+}(n)
.
Nevertheless, by a simple shifting of eigenvalues,
23
demonstrates how any characterization of
\Tetra^{+}(n)
also provides a characterization of
\Tetra(n)
.
However, while
\Tetra(n)
has numerous symmetries, including the discrete group of order
48
described by
19
, the subset
\Tetra^{+}(n)\subseteq\Tetra(n)
has fewer symmetries;
indeed, the only element in the aforementioned discrete group which preserves non-negativity of eigenvalues is the permutation
(a,b,c,d,e,f)\leftrightarrow(d,b,f,a,e,c)
.
This difference explains why the inequalities from
1
do not directly respect the symmetries of
\Tetra(n)
.

Remark 25
.

Although this section listed a handful of symmetries of the tetrahedral Horn problem, there remains the possibility of additional symmetries which are unknown at this time.
For example, in the case of
2\times 2
Hermitian matrices, an additional class of symmetries on the set of edge-lengths
(a,b,c,e,d,f)
of tetrahedra, known as the

, hold
[
97
,
98
,
54
]
.
If there exists a tetrahedron with edge lengths

(156)

(s_{1}-a,s_{1}-b,c,s_{1}-d,s_{1}-e,f)\quad\text{where}\quad s_{1}=\frac{a+b+d+e}{2}.

Combining this symmetry with the permutational symmetries given by

(noting eigenvalues of traceless

Hermitian matrices are invariant under negation) one concludes the co-existence of a family of six tetrahedra with edge lengths given by

(157)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&(a,b,c,d,e,f),\\
&(s_{1}-a,s_{1}-b,c,s_{1}-d,s_{1}-e,f),\\
&(a,s_{3}-b,s_{3}-c,d,s_{3}-e,s_{3}-f),\\
&(s_{2}-a,b,s_{2}-c,s_{2}-d,e,s_{2}-f),\\
&(s_{2}-d,s_{1}-e,s_{3}-f,s_{2}-a,s_{1}-b,s_{3}-c),\\
&(s_{1}-d,s_{3}-e,s_{2}-f,s_{1}-a,s_{3}-b,s_{2}-c),\end{split}

where

(158)

s_{1}=\frac{a+b+d+e}{2},\quad s_{2}=\frac{a+c+d+f}{2},\quad s_{3}=\frac{b+c+e+f}{2}.

Appendix B
Results for the original Horn problem

The purpose of this section is to describe how our methods can also be used to prove the following result which pertains to the original Horn problem.

Theorem 27
.

Let
(a,b,c)\in(\mathbb{R}^{+}_{n})^{3}
be a triple satisfying the trace conditions.
Then
(a,b,c)\in\Horn^{+}(n)
if and only if for all
(\alpha,\beta)\in(\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{n})^{2}
,

(159)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:tri_{i}ff}\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\gamma\\
c^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}>0\end{subarray}}\varphi_{\gamma}(c).

Furthermore, if these inequalities are satisfied for all pairs
(\alpha,\beta)\in(\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{n})^{2}
with
|\alpha|+|\beta|=k
, then

(160)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:tri_{d}ist_{b}ound}\frac{D(a,b;c)}{\Tr[c]}\leq 2\sqrt{5}n\sqrt{\frac{\ln(k\!+\!1)}{k}}.

where
D(a,b;c)\coloneqq\min_{(A,B)\in\mathcal{O}_{a}\times\mathcal{O}_{b}}\left\lVert\eig(A+B)-c\right\rVert_{1}
.

The proof of this theorem is divided into two parts.
First we prove the necessity of
Eq.

159
in
Section

B.1
.
Then afterwards we prove the distance bound in
Eq.

160
which in turn implies the sufficiency of
Eq.

159
upon taking the limit as
k\to\infty
.

B.1.
Necessary conditions

The following proposition follows directly from the binomial theorem (
5
) and pertains to sums of the quantity
\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)
.

Proposition 28
.

Let
X,Y
be
n\times n
matrices. Then the following identities hold:

(161)

\displaystyle\forall\alpha,\beta:\quad\sum_{\gamma}\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\alpha}(X)\varphi_{\beta}(Y),

(162)

\displaystyle\forall\gamma:\quad\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\gamma}(X+Y).

Proof.

The first identity involving a summation over
\alpha
and
\beta
is the result of taking the trace of both sides of
5
.
For the second identity, involving a summation over
\gamma
, follows from the identity
\sum_{\gamma\vdash|\alpha|+|\beta|}\Pi^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}=\id_{V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta}}
:

(163)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi_{\alpha}(X)\varphi_{\beta}(Y)&=\Tr[\Phi_{\alpha}(X)\otimes\Phi_{\beta}(Y)],\\
&=\sum_{\gamma}\Tr[\Pi^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\Phi_{\alpha}(X)\otimes\Phi_{\beta}(Y))],\\
&=\sum_{\gamma}\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y),\end{split}

where the last equality is just the definition of
\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)
.
Once again, the condition that
|\gamma|=|\alpha|+|\beta|
is implicitly present.
∎

Under the assumption that the matrices
X
and
Y
are non-negative, we can conclude that the real number
\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)
is also non-negative because
\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)=\Tr[\Pi^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}(\Phi_{\alpha}(X)\otimes\Phi_{\beta}(Y))]
where
\Pi^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}
is a projection operator and
\Phi_{\alpha}(X),\Phi_{\beta}(Y)\geq 0
because
X,Y\geq 0
.
Moreover, we note that
\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)=0
whenever the Littlewood-Richardson coefficient
c^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}
vanishes.
These observations imply the following necessary condition for compatibility of eigenvalues for the original Horn problem.

.

Let
(a,b,c)\in(\mathbb{R}^{+}_{n})^{3}
.

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b),

\displaystyle\forall\gamma:\quad\sum_{\alpha,\beta}\Gamma^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\gamma}(c).

The claim follows directly from

B.2.
Distance bound & sufficiency

Here we prove the distance bound stated in
27
.
To begin we fix an
arbitrary
pair
(\alpha,\beta)
with total size
|\alpha|+|\beta|=k
and derive multiple bounds on the product
\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b)
.
Afterwards, we will sum over all such pairs and then obtain the claimed bound on
D(a,b;c)
.

First we bound
\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b)
in two ways and then apply the eigenvalue separation lemma (specifically
Eq.

79
).
The first bound on
\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b)
is obtained from relaxing
Eq.

159
by considering a Young diagram
\gamma^{*}
of size
k
which maximizes
\varphi_{\gamma^{*}}(c)
among those
\gamma
such that
c^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}>0
for the given
(a,b,c)
.
As there are at most
(k\!+\!1)^{n}
Young diagrams of size
k
, we conclude

(170)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:first_{a}b_{b}ound}\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b)\leq(k\!+\!1)^{n}\varphi_{\gamma^{*}}(c).

The second bound on
\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b)
is obtained from
14
which states that for any
\gamma
such that
c^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}>0
,

(171)

\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b)=\frac{\dim V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta}}{c^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}\dim V_{\gamma}}\begin{array}[]{c@{}}\expect\\
{\scriptscriptstyle X\sim\mathcal{O}_{x}}\\
{\scriptscriptstyle Y\sim\mathcal{O}_{y}}\end{array}\,\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y).

To relax this into a usable bound we (i) bound the dimensions by
\frac{\dim V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta}}{c^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}\dim V_{\gamma}}\leq\dim(V_{\alpha}\otimes V_{\beta})\leq(k\!+\!1)^{2\binom{n}{2}}
, then (ii) bound the average over
X\in\mathcal{O}_{x}
and
Y\in\mathcal{O}_{y}
with a maximum, then (iii) apply
30
to get
\varphi^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma}(X,Y)\leq\varphi_{\gamma}(X+Y)
and then finally (iv) specialize to the optimal
\gamma\mapsto\gamma^{*}
to produce

(172)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:second_{a}b_{b}ound}\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b)\leq(k\!+\!1)^{2\binom{n}{2}}\begin{array}[]{c@{}}\mathrm{max}\\
{\scriptscriptstyle X\in\mathcal{O}_{a}}\\
{\scriptscriptstyle Y\in\mathcal{O}_{b}}\end{array}\varphi_{\gamma^{*}}(X+Y).

Then we combine the two bounds on
\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b)
from
Eq.

170
and
Eq.

172
and apply the eigenvalue separation bound
Eq.

79
to obtain

(173)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b)&\leq\sqrt{(k\!+\!1)^{n^{2}}\begin{array}[]{c@{}}\mathrm{max}\\
{\scriptscriptstyle X\in\mathcal{O}_{a}}\\
{\scriptscriptstyle Y\in\mathcal{O}_{b}}\end{array}\varphi_{\gamma^{*}}(X+Y)\varphi_{\gamma^{*}}(c)},\\
&\leq(k\!+\!1)^{\frac{n^{2}}{2}}(k\!+\!1)^{\binom{n}{2}}\begin{array}[]{c@{}}\mathrm{max}\\
{\scriptscriptstyle X\in\mathcal{O}_{a}}\\
{\scriptscriptstyle Y\in\mathcal{O}_{b}}\end{array}\frac{\BC(\eig(X+Y),c)^{k}}{k!},\\
&\leq(k\!+\!1)^{n^{2}-\frac{n}{2}}\frac{\Tr[c]^{k}}{k!}\exp\left(-\frac{k}{8\Tr[c]^{2}}D(a,b;c)^{2}\right),\end{split}

where the last inequality follows from
Eq.

63
and the definition
D(a,b;c)=\begin{array}[]{c@{}}\mathrm{min}\\
{\scriptscriptstyle X\in\mathcal{O}_{a}}\\
{\scriptscriptstyle Y\in\mathcal{O}_{b}}\end{array}\left\lVert\eig(X+Y)-c)\right\rVert_{1}
.

Now we sum both sides of
Eq.

173
over all pairs
(\alpha,\beta)
with total size
|\alpha|+|\beta|=k
and use that there are at most
(k\!+\!1)^{2n}
such pairs to obtain

(174)

\frac{\Tr[c]^{k}}{k!}\leq(k\!+\!1)^{n^{2}+\frac{3n}{2}}\frac{\Tr[c]^{k}}{k!}\exp\left(-\frac{k}{8\Tr[c]^{2}}D(a,b;c)^{2}\right),

where the left-hand side follows from the trace condition
\Tr[c]=\Tr[a]+\Tr[b]
and

(175)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:binomial_{t}race}\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\vdash\ell\\
\beta\vdash k-\ell\end{subarray}}\varphi_{\alpha}(a)\varphi_{\beta}(b)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{k}\frac{\Tr[a]^{\ell}}{\ell!}\frac{\Tr[b]^{k-\ell}}{(k-\ell)!}=\frac{(\Tr[a]+\Tr[b])^{k}}{k!},

whereas the extra factor of
2n
in the exponent on the right-hand side is obtained because there are at most
(k\!+\!1)^{n}
Young diagrams with
n
rows and size at most
k
and we are summing over a pair
(\alpha,\beta)
with total size
|\alpha|+|\beta|=k
.
Rearranging then establishes an upper bound on

of the form

(176)

\frac{D(a,b;c)}{\Tr[c]}\leq\sqrt{8\left(n^{2}+\frac{3}{2}n\right)\frac{\ln(k\!+\!1)}{k}},

which implies

\sqrt{8\left(n^{2}+\frac{3}{2}n\right)}\leq 2\sqrt{5}n
for all

Appendix C
The large
n
limit & an entropic inequality

The purpose of this section is to show how to leverage the inverse polynomial bound on
\left\lVert\left\{\!\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\delta&\epsilon&\phi\end{array}\!\right\}\right\rVert
provided by
2
to derive inequalities on the entropies of the eigenvalues.
Our strategy will be to prove an upper bound on the
6j
symbol which depends only on the dimensions of the underlying irreducible representations and the multiplicity spaces between them.
By combining this upper bound with the lower bound provided by
2
(specifically the
n
-independent bound in
Eq.

17
), we will prove the following.

Theorem 32
.

Let
(a,b,c,d,e,f)\in\Tetra^{+}(n)
be tetrahedral eigenvalues.
Then

(177)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:entropic_{i}neq}s(c)+s(f)-s(b)-s(e)\geq 0,

where

(178)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:little_{s}{}_{d}efn}s(x)\coloneqq-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\ln x_{i}.

We begin with our upper bound on the
6j
symbol.

Lemma 33
.

Let
(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta,\epsilon,\phi)\in(\mathbb{Z}^{+}_{n})^{6}
.
Then

(179)

\left\lVert\left\{\!\begin{array}[]{ccc}\alpha&\beta&\gamma\\
\delta&\epsilon&\phi\end{array}\!\right\}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\leq\left(c_{\alpha,\phi}^{\epsilon}c_{\beta,\delta}^{\phi}c_{\alpha,\beta}^{\gamma}c_{\gamma,\delta}^{\epsilon}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\dim(V_{\gamma}^{n})\dim(V_{\phi}^{n})}{\dim(V_{\beta}^{n})\dim(V_{\epsilon}^{n})}.

Proof.

Here we provide a diagrammatic proof of the claimed inequality.
For the sake of clarity, we abbreviate the irreducible representation labels,
V_{\lambda}
, and omit the multiplicity space labels on the intermediate steps.

(180)

The left equality follows from the definition of the
6j
symbol.
The middle inequality follows from an application of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality with respect to the annotated contraction.
The right equality follows from applying Schur’s lemma to components of the diagram mapping
V_{\beta}
to itself on the left and
V_{\epsilon}
to itself on the right.
Note that the factors of
\dim(V_{\epsilon})^{2}
appearing on left-most and right-most sides of the above inequality cancel producing the claimed inequality.
∎

An immediate application of
33
is to combine it with
2
.
If
(a,b,c,d,e,f)
are tetrahedral eigenvalues, then by
33
and
2
there exists a converging sequence of Young diagrams
(\alpha_{k},\beta_{k},\gamma_{k},\delta_{k},\epsilon_{k},\phi_{k})
such that

(181)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:combined_{b}ound}\frac{1}{q(k)}\leq\left(c_{\alpha_{k},\phi_{k}}^{\epsilon_{k}}c_{\beta_{k},\delta_{k}}^{\phi_{k}}c_{\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}}^{\gamma_{k}}c_{\gamma_{k},\delta_{k}}^{\epsilon_{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{\dim(V_{\gamma_{k}}^{n})\dim(V_{\phi_{k}}^{n})}{\dim(V_{\beta_{k}}^{n})\dim(V_{\epsilon_{k}}^{n})}.

).
Remarkably, the Littlewood-Richardson coefficients

c_{\beta_{k},\delta_{k}}^{\phi_{k}}

c_{\alpha_{k},\beta_{k}}^{\gamma_{k}}

defining the group

(assuming

This observation provides us with an opportunity to optimize the above bound by varying the value of
n
.

In particular, it will useful to better understand how the dimension,
\dim(V_{\lambda})
, of the irreducible representation

of

becomes much larger than the number of rows of
\lambda
.
In general, for fixed
\lambda
, the function sending

One can find estimates for
\dim(V_{\lambda})
in the regime where

{|\lambda|}

Here we use an elegant presentation of this scaling, which relates the asymptotics of

Proposition 34

Let
\lambda

(182)

\frac{\dim(V_{\lambda})}{n^{{|\lambda|}}}=\frac{\dim(W_{\lambda})}{{|\lambda|}!}\prod_{(i,j)\in\lambda}\left(1+\frac{j-i}{n}\right).

The statement follows from comparing the Weyl dimension formula for
\dim(V_{\lambda})

183

(183)

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:frob_{d}im}\dim(W_{\lambda})=\frac{{|\lambda|}!\Delta(\lambda+\rho)}{\prod_{i=1}^{n}(\lambda+\rho)_{i}!}.

That this formula for

For additional details, see also

∎

From

Corollary 35

Let

\addcontentsline{lla}{section}{\numberline q:balanced_{s}izes}{|\gamma|}+{|\phi|}={|\beta|}+{|\epsilon|}.

From

∎
