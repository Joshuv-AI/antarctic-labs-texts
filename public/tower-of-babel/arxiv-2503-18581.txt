Range strongly exposing operators between Banach spaces
Geunsu Choi, Helena del Río, Audrey Fovelle, Mingu Jung, Miguel Martín

Abstract
. We introduce a new class of bounded linear operators, called range strongly exposing ( RSE , in short) operators, which form a natural intermediate class: weaker than absolutely strongly exposing operators, yet stronger than both uniquely quasi norm-attaining and classical norm-attaining operators. Several foundational results on norm-attaining operators are extended to the RSE setting. Among our main contributions, we establish that for every infinite-dimensional Banach space Y Y , there exists a Banach space X X such that the RSE operators from X X to Y Y are not dense—an RSE analogue of a result by Acosta (1999) which applies only when Y Y is strictly convex. We also show that the Radon-Nikodým property of Y Y is sufficient to obtain that RSE operators from L 1 ​ ( μ ) L_{1}(\mu) to Y Y are dense and that this is also necessary if μ \mu is not purely atomic. This extends and sharpens classical results by Uhl (1976). As a consequence, we prove that the set of RSE operators between L 1 ​ ( μ ) L_{1}(\mu) and L 1 ​ ( ν ) L_{1}(\nu) is dense if and only if at least one of the measures μ \mu or ν \nu is purely atomic, in contrast with the classical result by Iwanik (1979) which guarantees the denseness of norm-attaining operators for all measures μ \mu and ν \nu . We also prove that weakly compact operators from any C ⁡ ( K ) C(K) space can always be approximated by (weakly compact) RSE operators, thereby strengthening a result of Schachermayer (1983). Additionally, we present several improvements of more recent results concerning finite-rank operators and Γ \Gamma -flat operators which give, in particular, RSE versions of classical results on compact operators by Johnson–-Wolfe (1979). Finally, we discuss RSE counterparts of results by Zizler and Lindenstrauss on the denseness of operators whose adjoints attain their norm.

Let

into

It is said that

attains its norm
(or that

into

fails to be dense in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
, and that
\NA(X,Y)
is dense if either

\ell_{\infty}

\NA(X,Y)

for every target space

has

\NA(X,Y)
is dense in

for every domain space

[
7

[
24

by showing that

must have the RNP if any renorming of

has property A. We refer the reader to the expository papers
[

,
14
,

]
and the recent papers
[

,
13
,
20
,
28
,
29
]
and references therein for more information and background. Let us mention that both properties A and B are of isometric nature, that is, depend on the concrete norm we are using in the involved spaces, and that every separable Banach space can be renormed to have property A and every Banach space can be renormed to have property B.

To relate Bourgain’s work with the concept we are introducing in this paper, let us describe it more in detail. What was actually proved in
[
7
]
is that the set of all absolutely strongly exposing operators (denoted by
\ASE(X,Y)
) is dense in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
when
X
has the RNP. Recall that
T\in\ASE(X,Y)
if there exists
x_{0}\in S_{X}
such that whenever a sequence
(x_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
satisfies that
\|Tx_{n}\|\to\|T\|
, one can find a subsequence
(x_{\sigma(n)})\subseteq(x_{n})
and
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
such that
x_{\sigma(n)}\to\theta x_{0}
. That is,
T
attains the norm at a unique element (up to rotation) in the strong way that all sequences in
B_{X}
approximating the norm have to converge (up to rotation) to this element. This is, of course, a very strong concept. For instance, the point at which
T
attains its norm has to be a strongly exposed point of
B_{X}
(immediately) and the denseness of
\ASE(X,Y)
for a non-zero
Y
implies the denseness of the set of strongly exposing functionals in
X^{*}

[
28
, Proposition 1.5]
(see Subsection
1.1
for the definitions of strongly exposed points and strongly exposing functionals). We refer the reader to the recent paper
[
28
]
for extensions of Bourgain’s work. Although Bourgain’s positive result is both strong and useful when
X
has the RNP, the concept of absolutely strongly exposing operator turns out to be highly restrictive when the space
X
fails the RNP and is entirely useless when
B_{X}
has no strongly exposed points. This occurs in classical Banach spaces such as infinite-dimensional
C(K)
spaces and
L_{1}(\mu)
spaces when
\mu
is atomless.

Let us now turn to property B, the counterpart to property A for range spaces. This property is also connected to the RNP: If every equivalent renorming of
Y
has property B, then
Y
has the RNP
[
7
,
24
]
. However, unlike property A, the converse is false; as observed by Gowers
[
22
]
, the Banach spaces
\ell_{p}
fail to have property B for
1<p<\infty
. Actually, the same happens for
\ell_{1}

[
4
]
, for all strictly convex infinite-dimensional Banach spaces
[
3
]
, and for
\ell_{p}
-sums of finite-dimensional Banach spaces for
1<p<\infty

[
20
]
. Nevertheless, there remains a way to connect the RNP of a target space with its role as a ‘universal range’ for the density of a kind of norm-attaining operators in a weaker sense. For this purpose, the notion of quasi norm-attainment was introduced in
[
12
]
as follows. An operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)

quasi attains its norm
(we write
T\in\QNA(X,Y)
) if there exists a sequence
(x_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
and
y_{0}\in\|T\|S_{Y}
such that
Tx_{n}\to y_{0}
(that is, if
\overline{T(B_{X})}\cap\|T\|S_{Y}\neq\emptyset
). It was shown in the aforementioned paper
[
12
]
that if
Y
has the RNP, then
\QNA(X,Y)
is dense in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
for every Banach space
X
and, conversely, the RNP is necessary to get that
\QNA(X,Y^{\prime})
is dense in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y^{\prime})
for every Banach space
X
and every equivalent renorming
Y^{\prime}
of
Y
. It was actually proved in
[
12
]
that if
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
is a strong Radon-Nikodým operator (see the definition in Subsection
1.1
, observe that in the case when
Y
has the RNP, then every operator in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
is strong Radon-Nikodým), then
T
can be approximated by uniquely quasi norm-attaining operators. We say that an operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)

uniquely quasi attains its norm
(and write
T\in\UQNA(X,Y)
) if there exists
y_{0}\in\|T\|S_{Y}
such that whenever a sequence
(x_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
satisfies
\|Tx_{n}\|\to\|T\|
, one can find a subsequence
(x_{\sigma(n)})\subseteq(x_{n})
and
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
such that
Tx_{\sigma(n)}\to\theta y_{0}
. Assuming that either the domain space
X
or the range space
Y
has the RNP (or that we are dealing with compact or weakly compact operators), the set of uniquely quasi norm-attaining (respectively, compact or weakly compact) operators between
X
and
Y
is dense in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)

[
12
, Corollaries 3.10 and 3.11]
. This notion is less useful than the one of absolutely strongly exposing operator, as it does not imply the operator to attain its norm. Instead, it provides a “supremum value” that is unique (up to rotations) rather than an actual “maximum value”.

In this paper, we introduce the following notion of norm attainment, which is weaker than being absolutely strongly exposing, but stronger than the mere norm attainment and than being
\UQNA
.

Definition 1.1
.

A bounded linear operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
is said to
strongly exposes its range
(or that it is
range strongly exposing
, writing
T\in\RSE(X,Y)
), if there exists
x_{0}\in S_{X}
such that whenever
(x_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
satisfies
\|Tx_{n}\|\to\|T\|
, one can find a subsequence
(x_{\sigma(n)})\subseteq(x_{n})
and
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
such that
Tx_{\sigma(n)}\to\theta Tx_{0}
. Sometimes, we will simply say that
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
is an
\RSE
operator when
T
belongs to the class
\RSE(X,Y)
.

The motivation behind the terminology “
\RSE
” stems from the following geometric observation: If
T\in\RSE(X,Y)
witnessed by
x_{0}\in S_{X}
, then
Tx_{0}
is indeed a strongly exposed point of
\overline{T(B_{X})}
(see Lemma
2.9
). This strongly exposed point lies in the range space, and therefore the letter R in
\RSE
is understood to signify the range space. As expected, the letters “SE” stand for “strongly exposing”. It will turn out that being a range strongly exposing operator is equivalent to being both uniquely quasi norm-attaining and norm-attaining (in the classical sense) simultaneously, Proposition
2.3
. Let us mention that the denseness of both
\NA(X,Y)
and
\UQNA(X,Y)
does not guarantee the denseness of
\RSE(X,Y)
, see Example
3.4
. Observe that while an operator in the
\RSE
class may attain its norm at many elements of the domain space, the operator has a unique (up to rotation) “strong maximum value” (in the sense that the image of any sequence approximating the norm of the operator has a subsequence which converges to a rotation of the maximum value).

The aim of this paper is to compare this new class of operators with previously studied notions of norm-attaining operators and to deepen the understanding of the interaction between the geometry of Banach spaces and norm-attaining theory. As we will see, the denseness of
\RSE
operators has stronger consequences than the denseness of the norm-attaining operators.

For a nonempty bounded subset

A point

A point

\NA(X,Y)
is not always contained in the closure of
\UQNA(X,Y)
.

The next example also shows that the implication (iii) cannot be reversed even when both the domain and range spaces are of finite dimension (greater than one):

Example 2.2
.

For every
n\geq 2
, let
Y
be a Banach space with
\dim Y\geq 2
. Then,

\RSE(\ell_{1}^{n},Y)\neq\mathcal{L}(\ell_{1}^{n},Y)=\NA(\ell_{1}^{n},Y).

Indeed, take two linearly independent vectors
u,v\in S_{Y}
such that for any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{K}
with
|\alpha|+|\beta|=1
,
\|\alpha u+\beta v\|=1
if and only if
\alpha=0
or
\beta=0
(this is clearly possible in every Banach space of dimension at least two). Let
T\colon\ell_{1}^{n}\rightarrow Y
be defined by
T(e_{1})=u
,
T(e_{2})=v
, and
T(e_{j})=0
for
j>2
. Then, it is clear that
T\not\in\RSE(\ell_{1}^{n},Y)
.

Nevertheless, there is a significant relationship between the properties in Figure
1
: being
\RSE
is equivalent to simultaneously belonging to the classes
\NA
and
\UQNA
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. Then,

\RSE(X,Y)=\NA(X,Y)\cap\UQNA(X,Y).

Proof.

One way is clear. Let
T\in\NA(X,Y)\cap\UQNA(X,Y)
. Then, there exists
x_{0}\in S_{X}
such that
\|Tx_{0}\|=\|T\|
. Let
(x_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
be any subsequence satisfying
\|Tx_{n}\|\to\|T\|
. Since
T\in\UQNA(X,Y)
, there exists
y_{0}\in\|T\|S_{Y}
,
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, and a subsequence
(x_{\sigma(n)})\subseteq(x_{n})
such that
Tx_{\sigma(n)}\to\theta y_{0}
. Similarly, for the constant sequence
(x_{0})\subseteq B_{X}
, we have
Tx_{0}\to\theta^{\prime}y_{0}
for some
\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}
. This shows that
Tx_{\sigma(n)}\to\theta\overline{\theta^{\prime}}Tx_{0}
, hence
T\in\RSE(X,Y)
.
∎

Let us remark that Example
3.4
, which appears in the next section, will show that the denseness of both
\NA(X,Y)
and
\UQNA(X,Y)
does not guarantee the denseness of
\RSE(X,Y)
.

The next observation provides some sufficient conditions for the reverse implications of (i), (ii), and (v) in Figure
1
to hold. Item (a) below appeared already in
[
12
, Remark 1.5]
and we include it for the sake of completeness.

Remark 2.4
.

Let
X
,
Y
be Banach spaces and let
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
satisfy that
T(B_{X})
is closed.

(a)

T\in\QNA(X,Y)
if and only if
T\in\NA(X,Y)
;

(b)

T\in\UQNA(X,Y)
if and only if
T\in\RSE(X,Y)
.

If, in addition,
T
is a monomorphism, then

(c)

T\in\UQNA(X,Y)
if and only if
T\in\ASE(X,Y)
.

Proof.

Indeed, if
T\in\QNA(X,Y)
then
T(B_{X})\cap\|T\|S_{Y}=\overline{T(B_{X})}\cap\|T\|S_{Y}\neq\emptyset
, so
T
attains its norm. Moreover, if
T\in\UQNA(X,Y)
then

T\in\UQNA(X,Y)\cap\NA(X,Y)=\RSE(X,Y).

For (c), suppose that
T\in\UQNA(X,Y)
and let
(x_{n})\subseteq S_{X}
be a sequence such that
\|Tx_{n}\|\to\|T\|
. Therefore, there exist
y_{0}\in\|T\|S_{Y}
and a subsequence
(x_{\sigma(n)})\subseteq(x_{n})
and
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
such that

Tx_{\sigma(n)}\to\theta y_{0}.

Since
T
is a monomorphism, we have
x_{\sigma(n)}\rightarrow x_{0}:=\theta\,T^{-1}(y_{0})
.
∎

Another way to pass from being range strongly exposing to being absolutely strongly exposing is to attain the norm at a LUR point.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. If
T\in\RSE(X,Y)
satisfies that
\|Tx_{0}\|=\|T\|
for
x_{0}\in S_{X}
and
x_{0}
is strongly exposed by every functional attaining its norm on it (for instance, if
x_{0}
is a LUR point), then
T\in\ASE(X,Y)
.

Proof.

For simplicity, we may assume that
\|T\|=1
. Suppose that
(x_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
is a sequence such that
\|Tx_{n}\|\to 1
. Then, there exists a subsequence
(x_{\sigma(n)})\subseteq(x_{n})
and
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
such that
Tx_{\sigma(n)}\to\theta Tx_{0}
. Take
y_{0}^{*}\in S_{Y^{*}}
so that
y_{0}^{*}(Tx_{0})=1
. Then,
T^{*}y_{0}^{*}(x_{0})=1
and
T^{*}y_{0}^{*}\bigl(\overline{\theta}x_{\sigma(n)}\bigr)\to 1
. Since
x_{0}
is strongly exposed by
T^{*}y_{0}^{*}
, we obtain that
(x_{\sigma(n)})
converges to
\theta x_{0}
.
∎

A useful method used in
[
11
,
28
]
to obtain
\ASE
operators is to consider bounded linear operators that attain their norm at strongly exposed points. Concretely, if
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
attains its norm at
x_{0}\in\operatorname{str-exp}(B_{X})
and
\varepsilon>0
, then there exists
S\in\ASE(X,Y)
such that
\|Sx_{0}\|=\|S\|
and
\|S-T\|<\varepsilon
(see
[
11
, Proposition 3.14]
and
[
28
, Lemma 1.2]
). The following lemma follows a similar approach and will be useful in establishing the denseness of
\RSE(X,Y)
and
\UQNA(X,Y)
based on the denseness of
\NA(X,Y)
and
\QNA(X,Y)
, respectively.

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
X
,
Y
be Banach spaces and
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
.

(a)

If
T\in\QNA(X,Y)
towards
y_{0}\in\|T\|S_{Y}
and
y_{0}\in\operatorname{str-exp}\bigl(\overline{T(B_{X})}\bigr)
, then
T\in\overline{\UQNA(X,Y)}
.

(b)

If
T\in\NA(X,Y)
and
\|Tx_{0}\|=\|T\|
for some
x_{0}\in S_{X}
such that
Tx_{0}\in\operatorname{str-exp}\bigl(\overline{T(B_{X})}\bigr)
, then
T\in\overline{\RSE(X,Y)}
.

Proof.

(a) Fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
y_{0}^{*}\in{Y^{*}}
be a strongly exposing functional of
\overline{T(B_{X})}
at
y_{0}
such that
\|y_{0}^{*}\|<\frac{1}{\|T\|}
. Observe that

(1)

\sup\bigl\{|y_{0}^{*}(y)|\colon y\in\overline{T(B_{X})}\bigr\}=|y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})|=y_{0}^{*}(y_{0}).

Now, we define
S\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
as

(2)

Sx=Tx+\varepsilon y_{0}^{*}(Tx)\frac{y_{0}}{\|y_{0}\|}.

Then, we have
\|S-T\|\leq\varepsilon\|y_{0}^{*}\|\|T\|<\varepsilon
and, applying (
1
),

\|Sx\|\leq\|T\|+\varepsilon|y_{0}^{*}(Tx)|\leq\|y_{0}\|+\varepsilon y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})

for every
x\in B_{X}
, so
\|S\|\leq\|y_{0}\|+\varepsilon y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})
. In fact, we will prove that
\|S\|=\|y_{0}\|+\varepsilon y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})
.
Choose a sequence
(x_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
such that
Tx_{n}\to y_{0}
, and we have

\lim_{n}Sx_{n}=y_{0}+\varepsilon y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})\frac{y_{0}}{\|y_{0}\|}=\left(1+\varepsilon\frac{y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})}{\|y_{0}\|}\right)y_{0}=:z_{0}.

Observe that
\|z_{0}\|=\|y_{0}\|+\varepsilon y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})
; so
\|S\|=\|y_{0}\|+\varepsilon y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})
.

Finally, to prove that
S\in\UQNA(X,Y)
take a sequence
(u_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
such that
\|Su_{n}\|\to\|S\|
. Then by (
1
)

\|S\|=\lim_{n}\left\|Tu_{n}+\varepsilon y_{0}^{*}(Tu_{n})\frac{y_{0}}{\|y_{0}\|}\right\|\leq\|T\|+\varepsilon y_{0}^{*}(y_{0}).

In particular, this implies that
\lim_{n}|y_{0}^{*}(Tu_{n})|=y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})
; so there exists a sequence
(\theta_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{T}
such that
\re y_{0}^{*}(T(\theta_{n}u_{n}))\to y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})
. As
y_{0}^{*}
strongly exposes
\overline{T(B_{X})}
at
y_{0}
, we have
T(\theta_{n}u_{n})\to y_{0}
, and then
S(\theta_{n}u_{n})\to z_{0}
, as we wanted.

(b) If
T\in\NA(X,Y)
then, in particular,
T\in\QNA(X,Y)
. So, we can follow the proof of (a) and consider
S\in\UQNA(X,Y)
as in (
2
) with
y_{0}=Tx_{0}
.
Note that
\|S-T\|<\varepsilon
, and, in this case,
\|Sx_{0}\|=\|S\|
.
Thus,
S\in\NA(X,Y)
; therefore
S\in\RSE(X,Y)
thanks to Proposition
2.3
.
∎

Remark 2.7
.

Let us comment that in the proof of the lemma above, in both cases we construct operators
S\in\RSE(X,Y)
which are arbitrarily close to the given operator
T
, such that
T-S
is of rank-one and satisfying that
\overline{S(B_{X})}\subseteq\beta\overline{T(B_{X})}
for some
\beta>0
.

As a direct consequence of Lemma
2.6
, we have the following corollary:

Corollary 2.8
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. Suppose that
S_{Y}=\operatorname{str-exp}(B_{Y})
(in particular, if
Y
is LUR). Then

(a)

\NA(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{\RSE(X,Y)}
;

(b)

\QNA(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{\UQNA(X,Y)}
.

The next result complements the relationship between UQNA and RSE operators with the strongly exposed points of the (closure) of the image of the unit ball. Notice the parallelism with
[
28
, Lemma 1.2]
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. Then

(a)

If
T\in\UQNA(X,Y)
towards
y_{0}\in\|T\|S_{Y}
then
y_{0}\in\operatorname{str-exp}\bigl(\overline{T(B_{X})}\bigr)
and it is strongly exposed by any
y^{*}_{0}\in S_{Y^{*}}
such that
\re y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})=\|y_{0}\|
.

(b)

If
T\in\RSE(X,Y)
and
x_{0}\in S_{X}
satisfies
\|Tx_{0}\|=\|T\|
, then
Tx_{0}\in\operatorname{str-exp}\bigl(\overline{T(B_{X})}\bigr)
and it is strongly exposed by any
y_{0}^{*}\in S_{Y^{*}}
such that
\re y_{0}^{*}(Tx_{0})=\|T\|
.

Proof.

(a) Let
y_{0}^{*}\in S_{Y^{*}}
such that
\re y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})=\|y_{0}\|=\|T\|
. Observe that we have

\|T\|=\re y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})\leq\sup\bigl\{\re y_{0}^{*}(z)\colon z\in\overline{T(B_{X})}\bigr\}\leq\|T\|

and, therefore,

\sup\bigl\{\re y_{0}^{*}(z)\colon z\in\overline{T(B_{X})}\bigr\}=\|T\|.

Now, suppose that
(y_{n})\subseteq\overline{T(B_{X})}
is a sequence such that

\lim_{n}\re y_{0}^{*}(y_{n})=\sup\{\re y_{0}^{*}(z)\colon z\in\overline{T(B_{X})}\}=\|T\|.

Find
x_{n}\in B_{X}
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
so that
\|y_{n}-Tx_{n}\|\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
; thus we have

(3)

\lim_{n}\re y_{0}^{*}(Tx_{n})=\|T\|

which, in particular, implies that
\|Tx_{n}\|\rightarrow\|T\|
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.
Thus, there exists a subsequence
(x_{\sigma(n)})\subseteq(x_{n})
and
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
such that
Tx_{\sigma(n)}\to\theta y_{0}
. Applying (
3
), we have that

\|T\|=\lim_{n}\re y_{0}^{*}(Tx_{\sigma(n)})=\re\theta\,y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})=\re\theta\,\|T\|

so
\theta
must be
1
. In fact, every subsequence of
(Tx_{n})
must contain a further subsequence converging to
y_{0}
, so we finally have
\|Tx_{n}-y_{0}\|\rightarrow 0
; hence
\|y_{n}-y_{0}\|\rightarrow 0
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

(b) The proof is almost identical with (a), by applying
\sup\{\re y_{0}^{*}(Tx)\colon x\in B_{X}\}=\|T\|
.
∎

is the injectivization of
T
.

Let us mention that
T\in\UQNA(X,Y)
if and only if
\tilde{T}\in\UQNA(X/\ker T,Y)
, a fact that which can be deduced from the argument in

\tilde{T}\in\UQNA(X/\ker T,Y)

\tilde{T}\in\ASE(X/\ker T,Y)
by Remark

Remark 2.10
.

Given
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
, we have

T\in\UQNA(X,Y)\iff\tilde{T}\in\UQNA(X/\ker T,Y)\,\xLeftarrow{(\diamondsuit)}\,\tilde{T}\in\ASE(X/\ker T,Y)

and the implication
(\diamondsuit)
can be reversed when
T
has closed range.

Two related important observations in the same line are the following.

Proposition 2.11
.

Given
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
,
R\in\mathcal{L}(X/\ker T,Y)
, if
R\in\ASE(X/\ker T,Y)
with
\|R(q(x_{0}))\|=\|R\|
for some
x_{0}\in B_{X}
, then
R\circ q\in\RSE(X,Y)
.

Proof.

If
(x_{n})\subset B_{X}
is such that
\|R(q(x_{n}))\|\to\|R\|
, then passing to a subsequence,
q(x_{n})\to\theta q(x_{0})
for some
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
, and therefore
R(q(x_{n}))\to\theta R(q(x_{0}))
. This shows that
R\circ q\in\RSE(X,Y)
at
x_{0}
.
∎

The implication in Proposition
2.11
can be reversed for
R=\tilde{T}
when
T
has closed range. In fact, if
T=\tilde{T}\circ q\in\RSE(X,Y)
then, in particular,
T\in\UQNA(X,Y)
. This is equivalent to
\tilde{T}\in\ASE(X/\ker T,Y)
as we observed in Remark
2.10
.

Remark 2.12
.

Notice that when
R\in\ASE(X/\ker T,Y)\text{ with }\|R(q(x_{0}))\|=\|R\|
and
q(x_{0})\in B_{X/\ker T}
, the point
x_{0}
can be chosen from the closed unit ball
B_{X}
in the following situations:

(a)

when
\ker T
is proximinal, i.e.,
q(B_{X})=B_{X/\ker T}
.

(b)

when
(\ker T)^{\perp}\subseteq\NA(X)
.

In fact, the case (a) is somewhat obvious. As for the case (b), note that
\|R^{*}\|=\|R^{*}(y_{0}^{*})\|
for some
y_{0}^{*}\in B_{Y^{*}}
. Moreover,
x_{0}^{*}:=(R\circ q)^{*}(y_{0}^{*})=q^{*}(R^{*}(y_{0}^{*}))
vanishes on
\ker T
. By hypothesis, this implies that
x_{0}^{*}\in\NA(X)
; so
|x_{0}^{*}(x_{0})|=\|x_{0}^{*}\|
for some
x_{0}\in B_{X}
. It follows that
\|R\|=\|R(q(x_{0}))\|
.

From the discussion above, we can deduce the following results regarding approximation by
\RSE
operators.

Proposition 2.13
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces, and let
\mathcal{I}
be an operator ideal. If
T\in\mathcal{I}(X,Y)
satisfies one of the following conditions:

(a)

\ker T
is proximinal and
X/\ker T
has the RNP;

(b)

(\ker T)^{\perp}\subseteq\NA(X)
,

then
T\in\overline{\mathcal{I}(X,Y)\cap\RSE(X,Y)}
.

Proof.

(a): Fix
\varepsilon>0
. Since
X/\ker T
has the RNP, there is
R\in\ASE(X/\ker T,Y)
so that
R\in\mathcal{L}(X/\ker T,Y)
,
Q:=R-\tilde{T}
is of finite-rank, and
\|R-\tilde{T}\|<\varepsilon
, where
T=\tilde{T}\circ q
. Finally, notice from Remark
2.12
that the proximinality of
\ker T
yields that
R\circ q\in\RSE(X,Y)
with
\|R\circ q-T\|<\varepsilon
. Finally, note that
R\circ q=Q\circ q+T\in\mathcal{I}(X,Y)
.

(b): Observe that
(\ker T)^{\perp}\subseteq\NA(X)
implies that
X/\ker T
is reflexive. Thus, as in (a), we can find
R\in\ASE(X/\ker T,Y)
so that
\|R-\tilde{T}\|\approx 0
. Finally, we deduce from the item (b) of Remark
2.12
that
R\circ q\in\mathcal{I}(X,Y)\cap\RSE(X,Y)
and
\|R\circ q-T\|\approx 0
.
∎

In particular, item (a) of Proposition
2.13
shows that if
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
has closed range,
\ker T
is proximinal, and
Y
has the RNP, then
T\in\overline{\RSE(X,Y)}
. We note that item (b) improves
[
30
, Proposition 3.15]
, which proves that
T\in\overline{\NA(X,Y)}
under the same assumption.

We can also take advantage of the observations above to obtain a result similar to Lemma
2.6
.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces, and let
\mathcal{I}
be an operator ideal. If
T\in\mathcal{I}(X,Y)
attains its norm at some
x_{0}\in S_{X}
with
q(x_{0})\in\operatorname{str-exp}(B_{X/\ker T})
, then
T\in\overline{\mathcal{I}(X,Y)\cap\RSE(X,Y)}
.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{T}\in\mathcal{L}(X/\ker T,Y)
be the operator induced by
T\in\mathcal{I}(X,Y)
. Since
\tilde{T}
attains its norm at
q(x_{0})
which is a strongly exposed point, by
[
28
, Lemma 1.2]
, there exists
R\in\ASE(X/\ker T,Y)
such that
\|R(q(x_{0}))\|=\|R\|
,
Q:=R-\tilde{T}
is of finite-rank, and
\|R-\tilde{T}\|<\varepsilon
. So,
R\circ q\in\mathcal{I}(X,Y)
and
\|R\circ q-T\|<\varepsilon
. Notice from Proposition
2.11
that
R\circ q\in\RSE(X,Y)
.
∎

As a consequence, we obtain the following result on finite-rank operators.

Corollary 2.15
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces, and
T\in\FR(X,Y)\cap\NA(X,Y)
. If
X/\ker T
is strictly convex, then
T\in\overline{\FR(X,Y)\cap\RSE(X,Y)}
.
Therefore, if
X^{*}
is smooth, then

\FR(X,Y)\cap\NA(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{\FR(X,Y)\cap\RSE(X,Y)}.

Proof.

Let
T\in\FR(X,Y)\cap\NA(X,Y)
be given. Since
X/\ker T
is strictly convex, every point in
S_{X/\ker T}
is an exposed point. By finite-dimensionality of
X/\ker T
, we conclude that
S_{X/\ker T}=\operatorname{str-exp}(B_{X/\ker T})
. Proposition
2.14
shows that
T\in\overline{\FR(X,Y)\cap\RSE(X,Y)}
and completes the proof.

For the last part, just observe that if
X^{*}
is smooth, then every quotient of
X
is strictly convex.
∎

The last goal in this section will be to show that
\UQNA
is a
G_{\delta}
subset of
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
. Recall that the same is true for the set
\ASE(X,Y)
(see, for instance, the proof of
[
7
, Theorem 5]
). Let us mention that it can be easily observed that
\RSE(X,Y)
is not always residual, even being dense. Indeed, note that
\RSE(c_{0},\mathbb{K})=\NA(c_{0},\mathbb{K})=\ell_{1}\cap c_{00}
(cf. Remark
2.1
and
[
28
, Example 1.9]
).

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. Then, the set
\UQNA(X,Y)
is a
G_{\delta}
subset of
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
.

Before giving a proof of this result, we state the elementary lemma which can be proved easily.

Lemma 2.17
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. For every
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
, define

\widetilde{S}(T,\eta)=\{Tx\colon x\in B_{X}\text{ with }\|Tx\|>\|T\|-\eta\}.

Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
the set

A_{\varepsilon}=\{T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)\colon\exists\,y\in Y,\eta>0\text{ such that }\,\widetilde{S}(T,\eta)\subseteq\mathbb{T}U(y,\varepsilon)\}

is open, where
U(y,\varepsilon)
denotes the set
\{z\in Y\colon\|z-y\|<\varepsilon\}
.

Proof of Proposition
2.16
.

Consider, for each
\varepsilon>0
, the set
A_{\varepsilon}
as in Lemma
2.17
which is an open subset in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
.
We will prove that

\UQNA(X,Y)=\bigcap\nolimits_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n^{-1}}.

First, suppose that
T\in\UQNA(X,Y)
towards
y_{0}\in\|T\|S_{Y}
. If we had
T\notin A_{n_{0}^{-1}}
for some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
, then in particular we would have

\widetilde{S}\left(T,k^{-1}\right)\not\subseteq\mathbb{T}U(y_{0},n_{0}^{-1})

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In other words, there exists
z_{k}\in B_{X}
such that
Tz_{k}\in\tilde{S}(T,\frac{1}{k})
and
Tz_{k}\notin\mathbb{T}U(y_{0},n_{0}^{-1})
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
\|Tz_{k}\|\to\|T\|
, there exists a subsequence
(z_{\sigma(k)})\subseteq(z_{k})
such that
Tz_{\sigma(k)}\to\theta y_{0}
for some
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
. So we would have
Tz_{\sigma(k)}\in\theta U(y_{0},n_{0}^{-1})
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a contradiction. Therefore,
\UQNA(X,Y)\subseteq\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n^{-1}}.

On the other hand, suppose that
T\in\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}A_{n^{-1}}
. Then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist
y_{n}\in Y
and
\delta_{n}>0
such that

(4)

\widetilde{S}(T,\delta_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{T}U\left(y_{n},n^{-1}\right).

We may assume that
\delta_{n}\to 0
decreasingly, and noting that
\tilde{S}(T,\eta)=\mathbb{T}\tilde{S}(T,\eta)
, we can find from (
4
) a sequence
(x_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
such that

(5)

\|Tx_{n}\|>\|T\|-\delta_{n}\text{ and }\|Tx_{n}-y_{n}\|<n^{-1}

for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Now, we will prove that
(Tx_{n})
converges to an element of
\|T\|S_{Y}
. For
m>n
we have
\|Tx_{m}-y_{m}\|\leq m^{-1}
and

Tx_{m}\in\tilde{S}(T,\delta_{m})\subseteq\tilde{S}(T,\delta_{n})\subseteq\mathbb{T}U\left(y_{n},n^{-1}\right).

Thus, there exists
\alpha_{m,n}\in\mathbb{T}
such that
\|\alpha_{m,n}Tx_{m}-y_{n}\|<n^{-1}
and therefore

(6)

\|\alpha_{m,n}y_{m}-y_{n}\|\leq\|\alpha_{m,n}y_{m}-\alpha_{m,n}Tx_{m}\|+\|\alpha_{m,n}Tx_{m}-y_{n}\|<\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{n}

if
m>n
. Write
z_{1}=y_{1}
and
z_{k}=\beta_{k}y_{k}
, where we define

\beta_{k}=\alpha_{k,k-1}\cdots\alpha_{3,2}\alpha_{2,1}\in\mathbb{T}

for each
k\geq 2
.
Then, we have that

{\|z_{k+1}-z_{k}\|=|\alpha_{k,k-1}\cdots\alpha_{2,1}|\|\alpha_{k+1,k}y_{k+1}-y_{k}\|<\frac{1}{k+1}+\frac{1}{k}}

by (
6
), so
(z_{k})
is a Cauchy sequence and hence
\lim_{k}z_{k}=\lim_{k}\beta_{k}y_{k}=z_{0}
for some
z_{0}\in Y
. Passing to a subsequence we can assume that
(\beta_{k})
converges so that
y_{k}\to\theta z_{0}
for some
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
.
From (
5
) we have that
Tx_{k}\to\theta z_{0}
and therefore

\|z_{0}\|=\lim\nolimits_{k}\|y_{k}\|=\lim\nolimits_{k}\|Tx_{k}\|=\|T\|,

so
\theta z_{0}\in\|T\|S_{Y}
.

Finally, suppose that
(u_{n})\subseteq B_{X}
is a sequence satisfying
\|Tu_{n}\|\to\|T\|
. Then, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
\sigma(n)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
Tu_{\sigma(n)}\in\tilde{S}(T,\delta_{n})
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, applying (
4
) there exists
\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{T}
such that

\|Tu_{\sigma(n)}-\gamma_{n}y_{n}\|<n^{-1}.

Passing again to a subsequence we may assume that
\gamma_{n}
converges to
\gamma\in\mathbb{T}
. As
\lim_{n}y_{n}=\theta z_{0}
we finally see that
(Tu_{\sigma(n)})
converges to
\gamma\theta z_{0}
.
∎

Observe that the above proposition, combined with Proposition
2.3
, will enable us to obtain not only the denseness but also the residuality of
\RSE(X,Y)
given that
\UQNA(X,Y)
is dense and
\NA(X,Y)
is residual in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
. As we will see in Example

, the mere denseness of

is not enough.

Corollary 2.18
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. If
\UQNA(X,Y)
is dense and

is residual, then
\RSE(X,Y)
is residual.

It is worth mentioning that we do not know if the fact that

is residual actually implies that

is dense, see (Q3) in
[

, §4]
, and that this is the case when

are separable

.

3.1.
Nonexistence of a
\RSE
Universal Infinite-Dimensional Range Space

Let us directly enunciate the result in the title.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
Y
be an infinite-dimensional Banach space. Then, there is a Banach space
X
such that
\RSE(X,Y)
is not dense in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
. Moreover, such space
X
can be chosen independently of the choice of
Y
.

Recall that Acosta
[
3
]
showed that no
strictly convex
infinite-dimensional Banach space has Lindenstrauss’ property B. Our proof actually follows the same general strategy as Acosta’s. However, our goal is to demonstrate that the strict convexity assumption on the range space can be removed in this setting, when working with
\RSE
operators instead of norm-attaining operators. In that paper, a Banach space was constructed as a universal domain in which the set of norm-attaining operators is not dense. For convenience, we present that space here along with the properties we will need.

Example 3.2

(
[
3
, Lemma 2.1, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Let
w=(w_{n})\in\ell_{2}\backslash\ell_{1}
be a decreasing sequence of positive scalars with
w_{1}<1
and let
Z(w)
be space of sequences
z
of scalars satisfying that

\|z\|:=\|(1-w)z\|_{\infty}+\|wz\|_{1}<\infty,

which is a Banach space when endowed with the above formula as norm.
We denote by
(e_{n})
the sequence of coordinate functionals in
Z(w)^{*}
given by

e_{n}(z)=z(n)\quad\forall z\in Z(w),\ \forall n\in\mathbb{N}

and define
X(w):=\overline{\spann}\{e_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq Z(w)^{*}
.
The following properties hold:

(i)

(e_{n})
is a one-unconditional normalized basis of
X(w)
.

(ii)

X(w)^{*}\equiv Z(w)
and
B_{X(w)}=\overline{\operatorname{conv}}(E)
,
where

E=\left\{\theta_{m}(1-w_{m})e_{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}w_{i}e_{i}\colon m,n\in\mathbb{N},\;\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{T},\,\forall i\right\}.

(iii)

Given
x_{0}\in S_{X(w)}
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
, there are
n\geq N
and
\delta_{n}>0
such that
\|x_{0}\pm\lambda e_{n}\|\leq 1
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{K}
so that
|\lambda|\leq\delta_{n}
.

The crucial point in which we will use
\RSE
operators in the proof of the theorem is isolated in the following easy lemma, which we will also use later on.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. If
S\in\RSE(X,Y)
attains its norm at
x_{0}\in S_{X}
and
\|x_{0}+z\|,\|x_{0}-z\|\leq 1
for some
z\in X
, then
S(z)=0
.

Proof.

Take
y_{0}^{*}\in S_{Y^{*}}
such that
\re y_{0}^{*}(Sx_{0})=\|Sx_{0}\|=\|S\|
. By Lemma
2.9
, we have that
y_{0}^{*}
strongly exposes
\overline{S(B_{X})}
at
Sx_{0}
. Now, we have that

\displaystyle\|S\|

\displaystyle=\re y_{0}^{*}(Sx_{0})=\re y_{0}^{*}\left(\frac{1}{2}(Sx_{0}+Sz)+\frac{1}{2}(Sx_{0}-Sz)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\re y_{0}^{*}\left(S(x_{0}+z)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\re y_{0}^{*}\left(S(x_{0}-z)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|S\|\|x_{0}+z\|+\frac{1}{2}\|S\|\|x_{0}-z\|.

As
y_{0}^{*}
strongly exposes
\overline{S(B_{X})}
at
Sx_{0}
, it follows that
S(x_{0}\pm z)=Sx_{0}
, hence
S(z)=0
as desired.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Consider a sequence
w\in\ell_{2}\backslash\ell_{1}
satisfying the conditions required in the above example and let
X=X(w)
. By the Dvoretzky–Rogers’ theorem (see
[
15
, Theorem 1.2]
), there exists a sequence
(y_{n})\subseteq S_{Y}
such that
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}w_{n}y_{n}
converges unconditionally, so the set

A:=\left\{\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\theta_{n}w_{n}y_{n}\colon\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{K},\,|\theta_{n}|\leq 1,\,\forall n\right\}

is bounded (use
[
15
, Theorem 1.9]
, for instance). Define
T\colon\spann\{e_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subset X(w)\to Y
by
T(e_{n})=y_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Observe that

\left\|T\left(\theta_{m}(1-w_{m})e_{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}w_{i}e_{i}\right)\right\|\leq 1+\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}w_{i}y_{i}\right\|\leq 1+\sup_{y\in A}\|y\|<\infty

for all
n,m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\theta_{i}\in\mathbb{T}
. Hence
T
is bounded on the set
E
of Example
3.2
and, therefore,
T
can be extended to a bounded linear operator from
X(w)
to
Y
, which we will also denoted by
T
. Let us show that
\|T-S\|\geq 1
whenever
S\in\RSE(X(w),Y)
, finishing thus the proof. Indeed, suppose that there exists
S\in\RSE(X(w),Y)
which attains its norm at
x_{0}\in S_{X(w)}
. Use (iii) in Example
3.2
to find
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta_{n}>0
such that

\|x_{0}+\delta_{n}e_{n}\|,\|x_{0}-\delta_{n}e_{n}\|\leq 1.

It follows from Lemma
3.3
that
Se_{n}=0
. Since
\|e_{n}\|=1
, we have

\|T-S\|\geq\|Te_{n}-Se_{n}\|=\|y_{n}\|=1.\qed

As a first consequence, we obtain that the fact that
\NA(X,Y)
and
\UQNA(X,Y)
are dense does not imply that
\RSE(X,Y)
is, despite the fact that the latter is the intersection of the other two sets. Even more, the fact that
Y
has property
\beta
(which implies property B and so the denseness of
\NA(X,Y)
for every
X

[
33
]
) and the RNP (which implies that
\UQNA(X,Y)
is residual for every
X
) is not enough to ensure the denseness of
\RSE
operators. The following example illustrates this point:

Example 3.4
.

There is a Banach space
Y
with Lindenstrauss’ property
\beta
and the RNP, and a Banach space
X
such that
\RSE(X,Y)
is not dense in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
.

Indeed, it suffices to consider an equivalent renorming of
\ell_{2}
(say) with Lindenstrauss’ property
\beta

[
37
]
, and apply Theorem
3.1
.

We finish this subsection by noting that an analogue of Theorem
3.1
for domain spaces does not hold, since Banach spaces with the RNP satisfy the
\RSE
version of property A
[
7
]
; that is, they serve as universal domain spaces for the denseness of
\RSE
operators (actually, for
\ASE
operators).

3.2.
Operators from
L_{1}(\mu)
-Spaces

In the present subsection, we will restrict our focus to finite measures even though some results may hold without this restriction. This is because the discussion on the analytic version of the RNP is always done only for finite or even probability measures, for instance, in
[
16
]
. We actually refer to this book for background.

We start by proving that every strong RN operator from
L_{1}(\mu)
to a Banach space
Y
can be approximated by elements of
\RSE(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
. This improves the classical result of Uhl
[
40
, Theorem 1]
, which shows that operators from
L_{1}(\mu)
to a Banach
Y
with the RNP which attain their norm (in the classical sense) are dense. Our proof closely follows Uhl’s original argument.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a finite measure space,
Y
a Banach space and
T\in\mathcal{L}(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
be a strong RN operator. Then, for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
S\in\RSE(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
such that
\|T-S\|\leq\varepsilon
. Moreover, there is
\beta>0
such that

\overline{S(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}\subseteq\beta\overline{T(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}.

We will use some well-known facts that we state here for commodity of the reader.
Recall that an operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
is
representable
if there exists an essentially bounded Bochner integrable function
g\colon\Omega\to Y
such that

T(f)=\int_{\Omega}fg\,d\mu

for all
f\in L_{1}(\mu)
.

Lemma 3.6
.

(
[
19
, Lemma 5.4]
and
[
16
, Theorem III.1.5]
)

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a finite measure space and
Y
a Banach space. Then
T\in\mathcal{L}(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
is an RN operator if and only if
T
is representable. Moreover,
Y
has the RNP with respect to
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
if and only if every bounded linear operator from
L_{1}(\mu)
to
Y
is representable.

We will also need the following geometrical lemma whose proof is straightforward.

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
C
be a subset of a Banach space
X
. If
x_{0}
is a denting point of
\overline{\operatorname{conv}}(C)
, then
x_{0}\in\overline{C}
. In particular,
\operatorname{str-exp}(\overline{\operatorname{conv}}(C))\subseteq{\overline{C}}
.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
be a strong RN operator and
\varepsilon>0
. By Lemma
3.6
, we can find an essentially bounded Bochner integrable function
g\colon\Omega\to Y
such that

T(f)=\int_{\Omega}fg\,d\mu,\quad\forall f\in L_{1}(\mu).

Then there exists a countably valued
\mu
-measurable function
h\colon\Omega\to Y
such that
\esssup\|g-h\|_{Y}<\frac{\varepsilon}{6}
and

h=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}z_{i}\chi_{E_{i}},

where
z_{i}\in Y
,
E_{i}\in\Sigma
,
\mu(E_{i})>0
and
E_{i}\cap E_{j}=\emptyset
for
i\neq j
(see the paragraph before
[
16
, Corollary II.1.3]
). Consider

h_{1}:=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}y_{i}\chi_{E_{i}},\,\text{ where }\,y_{i}:=\frac{1}{\mu(E_{i})}\int_{E_{i}}g\,d\mu=T\left(\frac{\chi_{E_{i}}}{\mu(E_{i})}\right),\,\forall i\in\mathbb{N}.

Then, for every
i\in\mathbb{N}
we have that

\begin{split}\|h_{1}-h\|_{E_{i}}&=\left\|\frac{1}{\mu(E_{i})}\int_{E_{i}}gd\mu-z_{i}\right\|=\left\|\frac{1}{\mu(E_{i})}\int_{E_{i}}(g-h)d\mu\right\|\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{6}.\end{split}

This shows that
\esssup\|h_{1}-h\|_{Y}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{6}
; therefore
\esssup\|g-h_{1}\|_{Y}<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
.

Now, define
T_{1}\in\mathcal{L}(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
by

T_{1}(f)=\int_{\Omega}fh_{1}\,d\mu,\quad\forall f\in L_{1}(\mu).

Then, we have that
\|T_{1}-T\|<\frac{\varepsilon}{3}
and

\overline{T_{1}(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}=\overline{\operatorname{aconv}}\{y_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}\subseteq\overline{T(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}.

Indeed, notice firstly that
y_{n}=T\left(\frac{\chi_{E_{n}}}{\mu(E_{n})}\right)=T_{1}\left(\frac{\chi_{E_{n}}}{\mu(E_{n})}\right)
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Secondly, observe that if
f\in B_{L_{1}(\mu)}
one has

T_{1}(f)=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}y_{i}\int_{E_{i}}fd\mu

and
\sum\limits_{i=1}^{\infty}\left|\int_{E_{i}}f\,d\mu\right|\leq\int_{\Omega}|f|d\mu\leq 1
, so we have
T_{1}(f)\in\overline{\operatorname{aconv}}\{y_{n}\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\}
. This, in particular, shows that
T_{1}
is a strong RN operator.

On the other hand, we can assume that
T_{1}\neq 0
(otherwise the proof would be trivial) and let
\beta=\sup\|y_{i}\|>0
. We can choose
i_{0}
such that
\beta-\|y_{i_{0}}\|<\varepsilon/4
and
\alpha>1
such that

\varepsilon/4<(\alpha-1)\|y_{i_{0}}\|<\varepsilon/3.

Observe that

(7)

\beta<\|y_{i_{0}}\|+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}<\alpha\|y_{i_{0}}\|.

Next, define
T_{2}\in\mathcal{L}(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
by

T_{2}(f):=\int_{\Omega}fh_{2}\,d\mu,\,\,\forall f\in L_{1}(\mu),\,\text{ where }\,h_{2}:=\sum_{i\neq i_{0}}y_{i}\chi_{E_{i}}+\alpha y_{i_{0}}\chi_{E_{i_{0}}}.

Since
(\alpha-1)\|y_{i_{0}}\|<\varepsilon/3
, we obtain that
\|T_{1}-T_{2}\|<\varepsilon/3
. Note also that
T_{2}(\chi_{E_{i_{0}}}/\mu(E_{i_{0}}))=\alpha y_{i_{0}}
. Arguing as before, observe that

\overline{T_{2}(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}=\overline{\operatorname{aconv}}(\{y_{i}\colon i\neq i_{0}\}\cup\{\alpha y_{i_{0}}\})\subseteq\alpha\,\overline{T_{1}(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})},

so
\|T_{2}\|=\alpha\|y_{i_{0}}\|=\|T_{2}(\chi_{E_{i_{0}}}/\mu(E_{i_{0}}))\|
, and
T_{2}
is a strong RN operator.

By Lemma
2.6
, if we prove that
\alpha y_{i_{0}}
is a strongly exposed point of
\overline{T_{2}(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}
we will have that
T_{2}\in\overline{\RSE(L_{1}(\mu),Y)}
, which gives us an
S\in\RSE(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
such that
\|T_{2}-S\|<\varepsilon/3
and therefore
\|T-S\|<\varepsilon
, finishing the proof.

Since
\overline{T_{2}(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}
is an RNP set, it is the closed convex hull of its strongly exposed points (see
[
8
, Theorem 3.5.4]
). Therefore, there exists a strongly exposed point
z\in\overline{T_{2}(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}
such that

\|z\|>\|y_{i_{0}}\|+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

By Lemma
3.7
, the point
z
belongs to the set
\overline{\mathbb{T}(\{y_{i}\colon i\neq i_{0}\}\cup\{\alpha y_{i_{0}}\})}
. However, by the inequality (
7
), the only possibility is
z\in\mathbb{T}\{\alpha y_{i_{0}}\}
. In particular,
y_{i_{0}}
is a strongly exposed point, as we wanted.

Finally, the moreover part follows since

\overline{T_{2}(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}\subseteq\alpha\overline{T_{1}(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}\subseteq\alpha\overline{T(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})},

and the operator
S
is obtained from
T_{2}
using Lemma
2.6
and it is shown in Remark
2.7
that
S
can be taken in such a way that

\overline{S(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}\subseteq\alpha^{\prime}\overline{T_{2}(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}.

Therefore,
\beta=\alpha\alpha^{\prime}
works.
∎

We would like to emphasize two consequences of Theorem
3.5
. The first one follows immediately since every operator whose range space has the RNP is obviously a strong RN operator; the second one follows since weakly compact operators are strong RN and we can use the moreover part of the theorem to get that the approximating operators can be taken to be weakly compact.

Corollary 3.8
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a finite measure space and let
Y
be a Banach space with the RNP. Then,
\RSE(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
is dense in
\mathcal{L}(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
.

Corollary 3.9
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a finite measure space and let
Y
be a Banach space. Then,

\mathcal{W}(L_{1}(\mu),Y)=\overline{\mathcal{W}(L_{1}(\mu),Y)\cap\RSE(L_{1}(\mu),Y)}.

The analogous result to this last one for compact operators also holds and can be proved in the same way, but we will also get it with a completely different argument in Corollary
3.20
.

Our next aim is to show that, actually, Corollary

characterizes the RNP of the range space

when the measure

has the RNP and, actually,

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a finite measure space which is not purely atomic and let

be a Banach space. If
\RSE(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
is dense in
\mathcal{L}(L_{1}(\mu),Y)

has the RNP.

For the proof of Theroem
3.10
, we need the following preliminary result from measure theory which we highlight here for the reader’s convenience.

Lemma 3.11

(
Proof of
[
16
, Lemma III.1.4]
)
.

If
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
is a finite measure space and

defines a bounded linear operator from

to

\|T\|=\operatorname{ess\penalty\ sup}\|g\|_{Y}
.

The next preliminary result allows to pass the denseness of
\RSE
operators from de domain space to an
L
-summand. The analogous results for other types of norm-attaining operators are well-known.

Lemma 3.12
.

Let
X
,
Y
be Banach spaces and suppose that
M
is an
L
-summand of
X
(that is,
X=M\oplus_{1}N
for some closed subspace
N
of

). If
\RSE(X,Y)
is dense in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
, then
\RSE(M,Y)
is dense in
\mathcal{L}(M,Y)
.

Proof.

Consider
T\in\mathcal{L}(M,Y)
and define
\tilde{T}\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
by
\tilde{T}(m,n)=T(n)
for
(n,m)\in X=M\oplus_{1}N
, which satisfies that
\|\tilde{T}\|=\|T\|
. For a given
\varepsilon>0
with
\varepsilon<\|T\|
, we find
\tilde{S}\in\RSE(X,Y)
witnessed by
(m_{0},n_{0})\in S_{X}
such that
\|\tilde{T}-\tilde{S}\|<\varepsilon
, and define
S\in\mathcal{L}(M,Y)
by
S(m)=\tilde{S}(m,0)
for
m\in M
which clearly satisfies that
\|T-S\|<\varepsilon
. Then,
\|S\|=\|\tilde{S}\|
. Indeed, the inequality
\|S\|\leq\|\tilde{S}\|
is immediate and, conversely,

\|\tilde{S}\|=\max\{\|\tilde{S}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{M}\|,\|\tilde{S}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{N}\|\}\leq\max\{\|S\|,\|\tilde{T}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{N}\|+\|\tilde{T}-\tilde{S}\|\}=\|S\|.

Now, if we have a sequence
(m_{k})
in
B_{M}
such that
\|S(m_{k})\|\to\|S\|
, then

\|\tilde{S}(S)(m_{k},0)\|\to\|\tilde{S}\|

and, therefore, there is a subsequence
(m_{\sigma(k)})
and
\theta\in\mathbb{T}
such that

\tilde{S}(m_{\sigma(k)},0)\to\theta\tilde{S}(m_{0},n_{0}).

In particular,
S(m_{\sigma(k)})\to\theta S(m_{0})
. This shows that
S\in\RSE(M,Y)
, finishing the proof.
∎

The next result include the technical details that we need to use in the proof of Theorem
3.10
. It provides the “
\RSE
version” of
[
40
, Lemma 2]
, but there is no need to assume that the range space is strictly convex.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
be a finite measure space and let
Y
be a Banach space. If
T\in\RSE(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
, then there exists a set
E_{0}\in\Sigma
with
\mu(E_{0})>0
,
g\in L_{\infty}(\mu)
with
|g|=1
on
E_{0}
and
y_{0}\in Y
with
\|y_{0}\|=\|T\|
such that

T(f\chi_{E_{0}})=\left(\int_{E_{0}}fg\,d\mu\right)y_{0},\,\text{ }\forall f\in L_{1}(\mu).

Proof.

If
\|T\|=0
there is nothing to prove. Otherwise, let
f_{0}\in S_{L_{1}(\mu)}
such that
\|Tf_{0}\|=\|T\|
and
y_{0}^{*}\in S_{Y^{*}}
so that

y_{0}^{*}(Tf_{0})=\|T\|.

Then,
y_{0}^{*}
strongly exposes
\overline{T(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}
at
Tf_{0}
. Choose
h\in L_{\infty}(\mu)
so that
\|h\|_{\infty}=\|T\|
and

y_{0}^{*}(Tf)=\int_{\Omega}fh\,d\mu

for all
f\in L_{1}(\mu)
. Let
E_{0}
be the support of
f_{0}
(
\mu(E_{0})>0)
as
\|f_{0}\|>0
), then it must be
h=\overline{\sgn}f_{0}\|T\|
on
E_{0}
, where
\overline{\sgn}f_{0}(t)=\frac{\overline{f_{0}}(t)}{|f_{0}(t)|}
for
t\in E_{0}
and
\overline{\sgn}f_{0}(t)=0
elsewhere. Therefore, for
f\in L_{1}(\mu)

y_{0}^{*}(T(f\chi_{E_{0}}))=\int_{E_{0}}f\overline{\sgn}f_{0}\|T\|\,d\mu.

Next, suppose that
E\subseteq E_{0},E\in\Sigma
and
\mu(E)>0
. Then

y_{0}^{*}T\left(\frac{\chi_{E}}{\mu(E)}\sgn f_{0}\right)=\int_{E_{0}}\frac{\chi_{E}}{\mu(E)}\sgn f_{0}\overline{\sgn}f_{0}\|T\|d\mu=\|T\|

and

y_{0}^{*}T\left(\frac{\chi_{E_{0}}}{\mu(E_{0})}\sgn f_{0}\right)=\int_{E_{0}}\frac{\chi_{E_{0}}}{\mu(E_{0})}\sgn f_{0}\overline{\sgn}f_{0}\|T\|d\mu=\|T\|.

Hence, as
y_{0}^{*}
strongly exposes
\overline{T(B_{L_{1}(\mu)})}
, it must be

(8)

\frac{T\left(\chi_{E}\sgn f_{0}\right)}{\mu(E)}=\frac{T\left(\chi_{E_{0}}\sgn f_{0}\right)}{\mu(E_{0})}=Tf_{0}:=y_{0}.

Now, let
f\in L_{1}(\mu)
be a simple function,
\varepsilon>0
and
\varphi\in L_{1}(\mu)
another simple function so that
\|\overline{\sgn}f_{0}-\varphi\|_{\infty}<\varepsilon
. We have that
T(f\chi_{E_{0}})=T(f\overline{\sgn}f_{0}\sgn f_{0})
and therefore

\begin{split}\|T(f\chi_{E_{0}})-T(f\varphi\sgn f_{0}\chi_{E_{0}})\|&\leq\|T\|\|f\overline{\sgn}f_{0}\sgn f_{0}-f\varphi\sgn f_{0}\chi_{E_{0}}\|\\
&=\|T\|\int_{E_{0}}|f||\overline{\sgn}f_{0}-\varphi|\,d\mu<\|T\|\|f\|\varepsilon.\end{split}

Choose
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}\in\Sigma
disjoint so that

f=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\chi_{A_{i}}\text{ and }\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\beta_{i}\chi_{A_{i}}.

Then

\begin{split}T(f\varphi\sgn f_{0}\chi_{E_{0}})&=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}T(\chi_{A_{i}\cap E_{0}}\sgn f_{0})\\
&=\sum_{\mu(A_{i}\cap E_{0})>0}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\frac{T(\chi_{A_{i}\cap E_{0}}\sgn f_{0})}{\mu(A_{i}\cap E_{0})}\mu(A_{i}\cap E_{0})\\
&=\sum_{\mu(A_{i}\cap E_{0})>0}\alpha_{i}\beta_{i}\mu(A_{i}\cap E_{0})y_{0}\quad{(\text{by \eqref{eq:subset_of_E}})}\\
&=\left(\int_{E_{0}}f\varphi d\mu\right)y_{0}.\end{split}

Hence, for every
\varepsilon>0

\displaystyle\left\|T(f\chi_{E_{0}})-\left(\int_{E_{0}}f\overline{\sgn}f_{0}d\mu\right)y_{0}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|T(f\chi_{E_{0}})-\left(\int_{E_{0}}f\varphi d\mu\right)y_{0}\right\|+\left\|\left(\int_{E_{0}}f\varphi d\mu\right)y_{0}-\left(\int_{E_{0}}f\overline{\sgn}f_{0}d\mu\right)y_{0}\right\|

\displaystyle<\|T\|\|f\|\varepsilon+\|y_{0}\|\left|\int_{E_{0}}f(\overline{\sgn}f_{0}-\varphi)\,d\mu\right|\leq 2\|T\|\|f\|\varepsilon,

so

T(f\chi_{E_{0}})=\left(\int_{E_{0}}f\overline{\sgn}f_{0}\,d\mu\right)y_{0}.

As simple functions are dense, we obtain the equality for every
f\in L_{1}(\mu)
.
∎

We are now able to present the pending proof.

Proof of Theorem
3.10
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{L}(L_{1}(\mu),Y)
and
\varepsilon>0
. Define a class
\mathcal{M}
of measurable sets in
\Sigma
by the condition that
E\in\mathcal{M}
if there exists an essentially bounded Bochner integrable function
g(E,\varepsilon)\colon\Omega\to Y
such that

\left\|T(f\chi_{E})-\int_{E}fg\,d\mu\right\|\leq\varepsilon\|f\chi_{E}\|_{1}

for every

.
Note that
\mathcal{M}
is closed by taking subsets that are measurable. Indeed, if
E\in\mathcal{M}
and
A\subseteq E
is measurable, then given
f\in L_{1}(\mu)
,

\displaystyle\left\|T(f\chi_{A})-\int_{A}fg(E,\varepsilon)d\mu\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|T(f\chi_{A}\chi_{E})-\int_{E}f\chi_{A}g(E,\varepsilon)d\mu\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\|f\chi_{A}\chi_{E}\|_{1}=\varepsilon\|f\chi_{A}\|_{1}.

Let
\alpha=\sup\{\mu(E)\colon E\in\mathcal{M}\}
and
(E_{n})\subseteq\mathcal{M}
a sequence such that
\lim_{n}\mu(E_{n})=\alpha
. Write
A_{1}=E_{1},A_{2}=E_{2}\setminus E_{1},\ldots,A_{n}=E_{n}\setminus\cup_{i=1}^{n-1}E_{i}
. Then
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
if
i\neq j
,
\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}=\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}E_{n}
and
\mu\left(\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\right)\geq\mu(E_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, so
\mu\left(\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\right)\geq\alpha
. As
A_{n}\subseteq E_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
there exists a sequence
(g_{n})
of essentially bounded Bochner integrable functions
g_{n}\colon\Omega\to Y
such that for every
f\in L_{1}(\mu)

\left\|T(f\chi_{A_{n}})-\int_{A_{n}}fg_{n}d\mu\right\|\leq\varepsilon\|f\chi_{A_{n}}\|_{1}.

Then,

\left\|\int_{A_{n}}fg_{n}\,d\mu\right\|\leq\|T(f\chi_{A_{n}})\|+\varepsilon\|f\chi_{A_{n}}\|_{1}\leq(\|T\|+\varepsilon)\|f\|_{1}.

By Lemma
3.11

\operatorname{ess\penalty\ sup}\|g_{n}\chi_{A_{n}}\|_{Y}=\sup_{\|f\|_{1}\leq 1}\left\|\int_{A_{n}}fg_{n}\,d\mu\right\|\leq\|T\|+\varepsilon

and therefore
\sup_{n}\operatorname{ess\penalty\ sup}\|g_{n}\chi_{A_{n}}\|_{Y}\leq\|T\|+\varepsilon
Defining
g\colon\Omega\to Y
by

g(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lcc}g_{n}(t)&\text{if}&t\in A_{n}\\
\\
0&\text{if}&t\notin\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\end{array}\right.

we have that
\operatorname{ess\penalty\ sup}\|g\|_{Y}\leq\|T\|+\varepsilon
and if
f\in L_{1}(\mu)
, then

\begin{split}\left\|T(f\chi_{\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}})-\int_{\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}}fg\,d\mu\right\|&\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\|T(f\chi_{A_{n}})-\int_{A_{n}}fg_{n}\,d\mu\right\|\\
&\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\varepsilon\|f\chi_{A_{n}}\|=\varepsilon\|f\chi_{\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}}\|_{1}\leq\varepsilon\|f\|_{1}\end{split}

Therefore,
\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\in\mathcal{M}
, and so
\mu(\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n})=\alpha
.

Next, we will show that
\alpha=\mu(\Omega)
. Suppose, on the contrary, that
\alpha<\mu(\Omega)
. Set
B_{0}=\Omega\setminus\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}
and consider
L_{1}(B_{0})
, the space of integrable functions in
L_{1}(\mu)
supported on
B_{0}
, that is,

L_{1}(B_{0})=\{f\in L_{1}(\mu)\colon f=0\text{ a.e. on }\Omega\setminus B_{0}\}.

Then,
L_{1}(B_{0})
is an
L
-summand in
L_{1}(\mu)
via the band projection
f\mapsto f\chi_{B_{0}}
. Define
T_{1}\colon L_{1}(B_{0})\to Y
by
T_{1}:=T\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{L_{1}(B_{0})}
. By Lemma
3.12
, we have that
\RSE(L_{1}(B_{0}),Y)
is dense in
\mathcal{L}(L_{1}(B_{0}),Y)
. Thus, there exists
T_{2}\in\RSE(L_{1}(B_{0}),Y)
satisfying that
\|T_{1}-T_{2}\|\leq\varepsilon
. By Lemma
3.13
, there exist
y_{1}\in Y
,
B_{1}\subseteq B_{0}
,
\mu(B_{1})>0
and
h\in L_{\infty}(\mu)
with
|h|=1
on
B_{1}
such that

T_{2}(f\chi_{B_{1}})=\left(\int_{B_{1}}fh\,d\mu\right)y_{1},\,\forall f\in L_{1}(B_{0}).

Writing

, we have

\begin{split}\left\|T(f\chi_{B_{1}})-\int_{B_{1}}fg^{\prime}d\mu\right\|&\leq\|T_{1}(f\chi_{B_{1}})-T_{2}(f\chi_{B_{1}})\|\\
&\leq\|T_{1}-T_{2}\|\|f\chi_{B_{1}}\|\leq\varepsilon\|f\chi_{B_{1}}\|,\end{split}

so

. Now, set
\tilde{g}=g+g^{\prime}
, then

\displaystyle\left\|T(f\chi_{\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\cup B_{1}})-\int_{\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\cup B_{1}}f\tilde{g}\,d\mu\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left\|T(f\chi_{A_{n}})-\int_{A_{n}}fg_{n}\,d\mu\right\|+\left\|T(f\chi_{B_{1}})-\int_{B_{1}}fg^{\prime}\,d\mu\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\|f\chi_{A_{n}}\|+\varepsilon\|f\chi_{B_{1}}\|=\varepsilon\|f\chi_{\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n}\cup B_{1}}\|,

\alpha=\mu(\Omega)=\mu(\cup_{n=1}^{\infty}A_{n})
and

\left\|T(f)-\int_{\Omega}fg\,d\mu\right\|\leq\varepsilon\|f\|_{1}

. Finally, let

We have proved so far that every operator from

Corollary 3.14
.

Let

and

two finite measures. Then,

If

has the RNP and therefore the denseness of

. For the converse, if

∎

that

3.3.
Weakly Compact Operators from
C(K)
-Spaces

Another significant result on norm-attaining operators on classical spaces was obtained by Schachermayer
[
38
]
. In his paper, he proved that for every compact Hausdorff space
K
and every Banach space
Y
,
\mathcal{W}(C(K),Y)
is equal to the norm-closure of the set
\mathcal{W}(C(K),Y)\cap\NA(C(K),Y)
. The following theorem improves this result by replacing
\NA
with
\RSE
.

Theorem 3.15
.

Let
K
be a compact Hausdorff space and
Y
be a Banach space.
Then,
\mathcal{W}(C(K),Y)\cap\RSE(C(K),Y)
is dense in
\mathcal{W}(C(K),Y)
.

Proof.

We follow the argument of the proof of
[
38
, Theorem B]
. Let
S\in\mathcal{W}(C(K),Y)
be such that
\|S\|=1
and let
\varepsilon>0
be given. Since
S\in\mathcal{W}(C(K),Y)
, it is a strong RN operator. By
[
12
, Remark 3.4]
, there exists
T\in\mathcal{W}(C(K),Y)
such that

(i)

T\in\UQNA(C(K),Y)
towards some
y_{0}
;

(ii)

\|S-T\|<\varepsilon
;

(iii)

T^{*}
attains its norm at some
y_{0}^{*}\in S_{Y^{*}}
which strongly exposes
\overline{T(B_{C(K)})}
at
y_{0}
with
\sup\re y_{0}^{*}(T(B_{C(K)}))=1
.

Put
\mu_{0}:=T^{*}(y_{0}^{*})
. Applying
[
38
, Lemma 3.1]
to the weakly compact subset
T^{*}(B_{Y^{*}})
, there exists a bounded linear operator
S\colon C(K)^{*}\to C(K)^{*}
with
\|S\|=1
such that

(i’)

\|S\mu-\mu\|\leq\varepsilon
for every
\mu\in T^{*}(B_{Y^{*}})
;

(ii’)

there exists
f_{0}\in{C(K)}
such that
\|f_{0}\|=1
and
\langle f_{0},S\mu_{0}\rangle=\|\mu_{0}\|=1
.

Let
A={(ST^{*})^{*}}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{C(K)}=T^{**}S^{*}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{C(K)}\colon C(K)\to Y
(here,
A(C(K))\subset Y
by weak-compactness of
T
). First, note that
A^{*}=ST^{*}
and

\|A-T\|=\|A^{*}-T^{*}\|=\|ST^{*}-T^{*}\|\leq\varepsilon.

By the part (b) of Lemma
2.6
, if we show that
A\in\NA(C(K),Y)
at
f_{0}
and that
Af_{0}
is a strongly exposed point of
\overline{A(B_{C(K)})}
, then there exists
Q\in\RSE(C(K),Y)
such that
\|A-Q\|<\varepsilon
and
A-Q
is a rank-one operator. It follows that
Q\in\mathcal{W}(C(K),Y)\cap\RSE(C(K),Y)
and
\|Q-S\|\leq\|Q-A\|+\|A-T\|+\|T-S\|<3\varepsilon
.

To this end, observe that
\|A\|\leq 1
and

\|Af_{0}\|\geq y_{0}^{*}(Af_{0})=(A^{*}y_{0}^{*})(f_{0})=(ST^{*}y_{0}^{*})(f_{0})=\langle f_{0},S\mu_{0}\rangle=1.

Moreover,

\displaystyle 1=y_{0}^{*}(Af_{0})

\displaystyle\leq\sup\re y_{0}^{*}\bigl(\overline{T^{**}(B_{C(K)^{**}})}\bigr)\quad\bigl(\text{since }A(B_{C(K)})\subseteq\overline{T^{**}(B_{C(K)^{**}}})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sup\re y_{0}^{*}\bigl(\overline{T(B_{C(K)})}\bigr)\quad\bigl(\text{since }\overline{T^{**}(B_{C(K)^{**}}})=\overline{T(B_{C(K)})}\bigr)

\displaystyle=\re y_{0}^{*}(y_{0})=1\quad(\text{due to \ref{eq:y_0_strongly_exposed}})

As
y_{0}^{*}
strongly exposes
\overline{T(B_{C(K)})}
at
y_{0}
, it follows that
y_{0}=Af_{0}
and that
y_{0}^{*}
strongly exposes
\overline{A(B_{C(K)})}
at
Af_{0}
, as desired.
∎

3.4.
Finite-Rank and Compact Operators

Let us start recalling that there are compact operators which cannot be approximated by norm attaining ones
[
34
]
and that the existence of finite-rank operators of the same kind is an old open problem. We send the interested reader to
[
35
,
30
]
for more information. Therefore, our proofs of the denseness of
\RSE
compact operators of
\RSE
finite-rank operators require additional conditions to hold. Even more, in the case of compact operators, with the same ideas as those used in Subsection
3.1
, we may prove the following result which is the
\RSE
version of
[
34
, Proposition 6]
, where the strict convexity of the space
Y
was required. Recall also that every Banach space can be renormed with Lindenstrauss’ property
\beta

[
37
]
and that this property implies that
\NA(X,Y)\cap\comp(X,Y)
is dense in
\comp(X,Y)
for every Banach space
X
(see
[
35
, Proposition 4.1]
).

Proposition 3.16
.

For every infinite-dimensional Banach space
Y
failing the approximation property, there is a Banach space
X
and a compact linear operator from
X
to
Y
which cannot be approximated by
\RSE
operators.

Proof.

As
Y
fails the approximation property, a result of Grothendieck combined with Enflo’s example (cf.
[
32
, Theorem 1.e.5 and Theorem 2.d.6]
), ensures the existence of a closed subspace
X
of
c_{0}
and a compact operator
T\colon X\to Y
which cannot be approximated by finite-rank operators (see
[
26
, Theorem 18.3.2]
). To complete the proof, it is enough to prove that
\RSE(X,Y)\subseteq\FR(X,Y)
. Indeed, if
S\in\RSE(X,Y)
attains its norm at
x_{0}\in S_{X}
, being
X
a closed subspace of
c_{0}
, it follows that
Z:=\overline{\spann}\{z\in X\colon\|x_{0}\pm z\|\leq 1\}
is of finite-codimension (see
[
35
, Lemma 2.2]
for the easy proof). By Lemma
3.3
, it follows that
S(Z)=0
, so
S
has finite-rank.
∎

Regarding the denseness of finite-rank norm-attaining operators, one of the main consequences in
[
30
]
establishes that if a Banach space
X
satisfies that
\NA(X)
contains sufficiently many linear subspaces, then the set
\FR\cap\NA
of finite-rank norm-attaining operators on
X
is dense in the ideal
\FR
of finite-rank operators. In the next theorem, we improve upon these results by replacing
\FR\cap\NA
with
\FR\cap\RSE
. The proof is closely related to the one of
[
30
, Theorem 3.3]
, using the technical results we have presented in Section
2
.

Theorem 3.17
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. Suppose that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, every
\varepsilon>0
and all
x_{1}^{*},\ldots,x_{n}^{*}\in B_{X^{*}}
, there are
y_{1}^{*},\ldots,y_{n}^{*}\in B_{X^{*}}
such that
\|x_{i}^{*}-y_{i}^{*}\|<\varepsilon
, and
\spann\{y_{1}^{*},\ldots,y_{n}^{*}\}\subseteq\NA(X)
. Then,

\FR(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{\FR(X,Y)\cap\RSE(X,Y)}.

Proof.

Let
T\in\FR(X,Y)
and write
T=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{*}\otimes y_{i}
for some
x_{i}^{*}\in X^{*}
and
y_{i}\in Y
,
i=1,\ldots,n
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, by assumption, we can find
z_{1}^{*},\ldots,z_{n}^{*}\in X^{*}
such that
\spann\{z_{1}^{*},\ldots,z_{n}^{*}\}\subseteq\NA(X)
and
R:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}^{*}\otimes y_{i}
satisfies that
\|R-T\|<\varepsilon
. Then
(\ker R)^{\perp}\subseteq\spann\{z_{1}^{*},\ldots,z_{n}^{*}\}\subseteq\NA(X)
.
Since
X/\ker R
is finite-dimensional, we can find
\tilde{S}\in\ASE(X/\ker R,Y)
such that
\|\tilde{S}-\tilde{R}\|<\varepsilon
. Let
S:=\tilde{S}\circ q\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
, where
q\colon X\rightarrow X/\ker R
is the canonical quotient map. Since
(\ker{S})^{\perp}\subseteq\NA(X)
and
S
is of finite-rank, we have that
\|Sx_{0}\|=\|(\tilde{S}\circ q)(x_{0})\|=\|S\|
for some
x_{0}\in S_{X}
(see
[
30
, Corollary 2.6]
). Finally, Proposition
2.11
shows that
S\in\RSE(X,Y)
, finishing the proof.
∎

The following main consequence follows directly.

Corollary 3.18
.

Let
X
be a Banach space such that there is a norm dense linear subspace of
X^{*}
contained in
\NA(X)
. Then, for every Banach space
Y
, we have:

As Theorem

4.
Range Spaces with Quasi-ACK Structure

In this section we will provide denseness results of
\RSE
operator in the case of specific target Banach spaces
Y
—namely, those with the so-called quasi-ACK structure—under the hypothesis of the denseness of
\NA(X)\cap\operatorname{smo}({X^{*}})
in
X^{*}
.

The notion of quasi-ACK structure was introduced in
[
13
]
as a generalization of ACK
ρ
structure
[
9
]
and property quasi-
\beta

[
1
]
. A Banach space
Y
with a quasi-ACK structure serves as a universal range space, ensuring the denseness of norm-attaining operators from
X
into
Y
within a specific operator ideal class—namely, the
\Gamma
-flat operator ideal—for any arbitrary domain space
X
.

Let us present a formal definition of quasi-
\ACK
structure. A Banach space
X
is said to have
quasi-
\ACK
structure
if there exist a
1
-norming set
\Gamma\subseteq B_{X^{*}}
and a function
\rho\colon\Gamma\to[0,1)
such that for any
e^{*}\in\operatorname{ext}(B_{X^{*}})
, there exists
\Gamma_{e^{*}}\subseteq\Gamma
satisfying that

(i)

e^{*}\in\overline{\mathbb{T}\Gamma_{e^{*}}}^{w^{*}}
,

(ii)

\sup_{x^{*}\in\Gamma_{e^{*}}}\rho(x^{*})<1
,

(iii)

for every
\varepsilon>0
and a nonempty relatively
w^{*}
-open subset
U
of
\Gamma_{e^{*}}
, there are a nonempty subset
V\subseteq U
,
x_{1}^{*}\in V
,
e\in S_{X}
,
F\in\mathcal{L}(X)
such that

(i’)

\|F(e)\|=\|F\|=1
,

(ii’)

x_{1}^{*}(F(e))=1
,

(iii’)

F^{*}(x_{1}^{*})=x_{1}^{*}
,

(iv’)

If we let
V_{1}:=\{x^{*}\in\Gamma\colon\|F^{*}(x^{*})\|+(1-\varepsilon)\|x^{*}-F^{*}(x^{*})\|\leq 1\}
, then
|x^{*}(F(e))|\leq\rho(x_{1}^{*})
for any
x^{*}\in\Gamma\setminus V_{1}
,

(v’)

\dist(F^{*}(x^{*}),\operatorname{aco}\{0,V\})<\varepsilon
for all
x^{*}\in\Gamma
,

(vi’)

|v^{*}(e)-1|\leq\varepsilon
for all
v^{*}\in V
.

We also recall the notion of
\Gamma
-flat operators: a function
f
from a topological space
\mathcal{T}
to a metric space

is called
openly fragmented
if for every nonempty open subset
U\subseteq\mathcal{T}
and any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a nonempty open subset

with
\diam(f(V))<\varepsilon.
Given a set
\Gamma\subseteq Y^{*}
and
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)

is
\Gamma
-flat
if
T^{*}\mathord{\upharpoonright}_{\Gamma}\colon(\Gamma,w^{*})\to X^{*}
is openly fragmented. It is known that every Asplund operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
is
\Gamma
-flat for every subset
\Gamma\subseteq B_{Y^{*}}
(
[
9
, Example A]
), and if
(\Gamma,w^{*})
is a discrete topological space, then every operator
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
is
\Gamma_{0}
-flat for any
\Gamma_{0}\subseteq\Gamma
(
[
9
, Example C]
).

It was observed in
[
9
]
that
C(K)
spaces, uniform algebras, and Banach spaces with property
\beta
(introduced in
[
33
]
) have ACK
ρ
structure. Besides, the property is stable by
\ell_{\infty}
-sums and by taking injective tensor products. Property quasi-
\beta
was introduced in
[
1
]
as a generalization of property
\beta
which still implies property B. As mentioned earlier, the notion of quasi-ACK structure is more general. Examples of Banach spaces having quasi-ACK structure include those with ACK
ρ
structure as well as those with property quasi-
\beta

[
13
, Remark 2.2]
. Moreover, there is a Banach space that has quasi-ACK structure, while failing to have both ACK
ρ
structure and property quasi-
\beta

[
13
, Proposition 3.6]
.

The next is the most general result that we are able to obtain. Even though the hypotheses seem to be very technical, we will be able to deduce many interesting consequences.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
X
and

Lemma 4.2

Let

Remark 4.3
.

Let

(a)

By definition,

is dense in

Finally, note that if

However, the denseness of certain

(b)

We have the following.

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X\text{ is strictly convex}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X^{*}\text{ is smooth}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\NA(X)\subseteq\operatorname{smo}(X^{*})}

As mentioned at the beginning of this section, Banach spaces with property quasi-

5.
RSE Analogues of Zizler’s and Lindenstrauss’s Results on Adjoint Operators

We start by showing that there is no analogous results for
\RSE
operators to Zizler’s
[
41
]
and Lindenstrauss’
[
33
]
on the denseness on the set of operators whose adjoint or biadjoint attain their norm.

Example 5.1
.

The sets

(a)

\{T\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{1},\ell_{1})\colon T^{*}\in\RSE(\ell_{\infty},\ell_{\infty})\}
,

(b)

\{T\in\mathcal{L}(c_{0},c_{0})\colon T^{**}\in\RSE(\ell_{\infty},\ell_{\infty})\}
,

are not dense in
\mathcal{L}(\ell_{1},\ell_{1})
and
\mathcal{L}(c_{0},c_{0})
, respectively.

Proof.

For (a), observe that, since the set of isomorphisms is an open set, if the set in (a) were dense, it would exists an isomorphism
S\in\mathcal{L}(\ell_{1},\ell_{1})
such that
S^{*}\in\RSE(\ell_{\infty},\ell_{\infty})
; being
S^{*}
also an isomorphism, Remark
2.4
would give that
S^{*}\in\ASE(\ell_{\infty},\ell_{\infty})
, but this is impossible since the unit ball of
\ell_{\infty}
fails to contain strongly exposed points. The argument for (b) is completely analogous.
∎

It is then clear that we need conditions on
X
,
Y
, or on the involved operator to approximate it by operators whose adjoint are
\RSE
. The most general result we are able to present in this line deals with operators with closed range and such that their image of the unit ball have the Asplund property. Let us first introduce the needed definitions.

Let
D\subseteq X
be a bounded subset and
F\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
any function. Then
F
is
D
-differentiable at

x\in X
if there exists
f\in X^{*}
such that

\lim_{t\rightarrow 0+}\sup_{d\in D}\left|\frac{F(x+td)-F(x)}{t}-f(d)\right|=0.

In this case,
f
is called the
D
-gradient
of
F
at
x
. A bounded subset
D
of
X
is said to have the
Asplund property
if each convex continuous function
F\colon X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is
D
-differentiable on a residual subset of
X
.
It is known
[
8
, Theorem 5.3.5]
that
T\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
is an Asplund operator (i.e.,

The result can be directly applied to Asplund operators to get the following nice consequence.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces, and let
\mathcal{I}
be an operator ideal contained in
\mathcal{A}
. If
T\in\mathcal{I}(X,Y)
has closed range, then given
\varepsilon>0
there exists
S\in\mathcal{I}(X,Y)
such that
\|S-T\|<\varepsilon
and
S^{*}\in\RSE(Y^{*},X^{*})
.

Proof.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be given and consider
\Phi:=\|\cdot\|_{X^{*}}\colon T^{*}(B_{Y^{*}})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. By Proposition
5.2
, we can find
x_{0}\in S
such that
\Phi+\text{Re}\,x_{0}
strongly exposes
T^{*}(B_{Y^{*}})
at some
T^{*}y_{0}^{*}
and
\|Tx_{0}\|<\varepsilon
. Define
R\in\mathcal{L}(Y^{*},X^{*})
by

Ry^{*}=T^{*}y^{*}+\frac{T^{*}y_{0}^{*}}{\|T^{*}y_{0}^{*}\|}y^{*}(Tx_{0}),\quad y^{*}\in Z^{*}.

Notice that
R
is weak-star to weak-star continuous, so
R=S^{*}
for some
S\in\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
. Since
S-T
is a rank-one operator, we have
S\in\mathcal{I}(X,Y)
. Moreover,
\|R\|=(\Phi+\re x_{0})(T^{*}y_{0}^{*})
. Now,
if
(y_{n}^{*})\subseteq B_{Y^{*}}
is such that
\|Ry_{n}^{*}\|\rightarrow\|R\|
, then

\|R\|=[\Phi+\re x_{0}](T^{*}y_{0}^{*})\geq[\Phi+\text{Re}\,x_{0}](T^{*}y_{n}^{*})\geq\|Ry_{n}^{*}\|\to\|R\|;

so
(T^{*}y_{n}^{*})
converges to
T^{*}y_{0}^{*}
. It follows that

Ry_{n}^{*}\rightarrow T^{*}y_{0}^{*}+\frac{T^{*}y_{0}^{*}}{\|T^{*}y_{0}^{*}\|}y_{0}^{*}(Tx_{0})=Ry_{0}^{*}\text{ and }\|Ry_{0}^{*}\|=\|R\|;

so
R\in\RSE(Y^{*},X^{*})
.
∎

Observe that
T\in\mathcal{A}(X,Y)
if and only if

in

proved in

.

If we deal with finite-rank operators, then the Asplundness condition and the closed rank condition automatically hold, so we get the following immediate consequence of Corollary
5.4
.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces. Then,

\mathcal{FR}(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{\{R\in\mathcal{FR}(X,Y)\colon R^{*}\in\RSE(Y^{*},X^{*})\}}.

If, moreover,
X^{*}
or
Y
has the approximation property, then

\comp(X,Y)\subseteq\overline{\{R\in\mathcal{FR}(X,Y)\colon R^{*}\in\RSE(Y^{*},X^{*})\}}.

Let us observe that the adjoint of a compact operator is always norm-attaining, and that compact operators can be always approximated by
\UQNA
operators
[
12
, Corollary 3.11]
, but it is not clear whether when one starts with the adjoint of a compact operator, the
\UQNA
approximating operators can also be taken as adjoint operators, which is exactly what the previous corollary ensures.

Our last result in this section deals with the relation of the
\UQNA
and
\RSE
operators and Fréchet differentiability points, similar to the known relation in the case of
\ASE
operators. Recall that
T
is a point of Fréchet differentiability in
\mathcal{L}(X,Y)
if and only if
T\in\ASE(X,Y)
at
x_{0}\in S_{X}
and

is a point of Fréchet differentiability in

(see
[

, Proposition 1.6]
or
[
23
, Theorem 3.1]
). Moreover,

is a Fréchet differentiability point if and only if
T^{*}
is a Fréchet differentiability point. We can also relate the notions of
\UQNA
and
\RSE
to Fréchet differentiability in a similar way as follows.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
X
and
Y
be Banach spaces and

. Then

(a)

towards

We only prove (b), being (a) completely analogous.
(\Rightarrow)
