Sarsasapogenin Preserves Podocyte Integrity and Attenuates Renal Injury in Experimental IgA Nephropathy
Authors: Meltem Kumaş, Dilek Canlar Akar, Mehmet Gül, Erkan Karadaş, Selen Kum Özşengezer, Kadri Kulualp, Orhan Yavuz, Osman Yilmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIgA nephropathy (IgAN) is a progressive glomerular disease marked by mesangial IgA deposition, inflammation, podocyte injury, and fibrogenesis. Although understanding of its pathophysiology has advanced, effective disease‐modifying therapies are still limited. Sarsasapogenin (SAR), a natural steroidal sapogenin, demonstrates anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and antifibrotic properties by modulating signaling pathways such as NF‐κB and TGF‐β. The present study investigated the renoprotective effects of SAR in an experimental model of IgAN. IgAN was induced in Sprague–Dawley rats using bovine serum albumin (BSA), carbon tetrachloride (CCl4), and lipopolysaccharide (LPS). SAR was administered at 60 mg/kg/day from week 7 through week 12. Renal function, histopathological changes (Oxford MEST‐C classification), and immunohistochemical, ultrastructural, and molecular alterations were assessed. Induction of IgAN resulted in significant proteinuria, renal dysfunction, mesangial IgA deposition, and podocyte alterations, accompanied by increased expression of IL‐6, TGF‐β1, SMAD3, fibronectin, and α‐SMA. Masson's trichrome staining did not reveal excessive collagen deposition, indicating early fibrogenic activity rather than established fibrosis. SAR treatment significantly reduced proteinuria and improved renal function, accompanied by decreased IL‐6‐mediated inflammation and reduced activation of the TGF‐β1/SMAD3 pathway. SAR also preserved podocyte‐associated proteins, including nephrin and podocin, and lowered pre‐fibrotic markers. Ultrastructural analysis confirmed preservation of podocyte architecture, with reduced foot process fusion and mesangial matrix expansion. Overall, sarsasapogenin modulated multiple targets in experimental IgAN by reducing inflammation and early fibrogenesis while supporting podocyte integrity. These findings indicate that SAR is a promising therapeutic candidate for early IgAN, although further mechanistic and long‐term studies are warranted.Keywords: experimental model, IgA nephropathy, podocyte injury, renal fibrosis, sarsasapogenin, TGF‐β/SMAD signaling

Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide and a leading cause of chronic kidney disease and end‐stage renal failure [1, 2]. It is defined by mesangial deposition of IgA‐containing immune complexes, which triggers inflammatory, cellular, and fibrotic responses that impair renal function over time [3, 4, 5, 6, 7]. Given the heterogeneous clinical course and the risk of progressive renal dysfunction, there is a clear need for effective disease‐modifying therapies.

The pathogenesis of IgAN is currently explained by the “multi‐hit hypothesis,” which involves increased circulating levels of galactose‐deficient IgA1 (Gd‐IgA1), formation of anti‐glycan autoantibodies, and subsequent deposition of immune complexes within the mesangium [2, 8, 9]. This deposition triggers mesangial cell activation, engagement of the complement pathway, and the release of pro‐inflammatory mediators, such as interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) and tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), ultimately promoting glomerular injury and disease progression [10]. In parallel, growth factors including transforming growth factor‐β1 (TGF‐β1) and platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF) promote extracellular matrix accumulation and fibrotic remodeling [11, 12].

In addition to mesangial changes, podocyte injury plays a critical role in IgAN progression. Loss of slit diaphragm proteins such as nephrin and podocin disrupt the glomerular filtration barrier, resulting in proteinuria and faster renal decline [9, 13, 14]. These changes often occur before overt fibrosis, indicating a potential early therapeutic window to modify disease progression.

Natural bioactive compounds are increasingly studied for their multi‐target effects and favorable safety profiles. Sarsasapogenin (SAR), a steroidal sapogenin from Anemarrhena asphodeloides, has demonstrated anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant, and antifibrotic properties by modulating pathways such as NF‐κB and TGF‐β/SMAD [15, 16, 17, 18]. Although sarsasapogenin has demonstrated renoprotective effects in several experimental kidney injury models, its potential role in IgA nephropathy remains unclear.

Since inflammatory activation, early fibrogenic responses, and podocyte injury play central roles in IgAN progression, modulation of these interconnected pathways may provide therapeutic benefit.

This study investigated the effects of sarsasapogenin in an experimental model of IgA nephropathy, focusing on inflammation, early fibrogenesis, and podocyte integrity. It was hypothesized that sarsasapogenin would attenuate IL‐6‐mediated inflammatory responses, suppress the TGF‐β1/SMAD3 signaling axis, reduce the expression of fibrotic markers, and preserve podocyte‐associated proteins, thereby improving renal function and structural integrity in IgA nephropathy.

Material and Methods

Animals and Ethical Approval

Male Sprague–Dawley rats, five weeks old and weighing approximately 150 ± 10 g, were obtained from Nesa Experimental Animals Laboratory (Work Permit No. 193, Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry, Ankara, Turkey). Upon arrival, the animals acclimatized to laboratory conditions for one week prior to initiation of the experimental protocol. Consequently, the rats were approximately six weeks old at the onset of IgAN induction. This specific age and sex were chosen to ensure methodological consistency with the previously established experimental IgAN model that utilized male Sprague–Dawley rats of similar age and BSA‐, CCl4‐, and LPS‐based induction protocols [19]. All animals were maintained under standard laboratory conditions, including a 12‐h light/dark cycle, controlled room temperature (22°C–25°C), and unrestricted access to standard chow and water throughout the study.

All experimental procedures complied with institutional ethical guidelines and received approval from the Animal Experiments Local Ethics Committee of Dokuz Eylul University (Approval No. 33/2023). No mortality occurred in any experimental group during the study.

Experimental Design and Establishment of the IgAN Model

Rats were assigned to experimental groups using a computer‐generated randomization sequence. A total of 17 rats underwent the IgAN induction protocol. Prior to initiating SAR treatment, three IgAN‐induced rats were sacrificed to confirm the successful establishment of the IgAN model. Immunohistochemical examination of kidney tissues confirmed mesangial IgA deposition, verifying successful model induction. These rats were used exclusively for model validation and were not included in the final experimental analyses.

Following model validation, the remaining IgAN‐induced rats were allocated to either the vehicle‐treated IgAN group (IgAN group, n = 7) or the SAR‐treated IgAN group (IgAN+SAR group, n = 7). Healthy animals were similarly allocated to the vehicle‐treated control group (Control group, n = 7) or the SAR‐treated group (SAR group, n = 7). Sample sizes were determined based on previous experimental IgAN studies employing comparable animal models and outcome measures. Histopathological, immunohistochemical, and quantitative analyses were performed by blinded investigators. No animals or samples were excluded from the final analysis.

The IgAN model was established by sequential administration of bovine serum albumin (BSA; Cat. No. V900933), lipopolysaccharide (LPS; Cat. No. L2637), and carbon tetrachloride (CCl4; Cat. No. 319961), all obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) as previously described in the literature [20].

BSA was dissolved in 0.4 mL distilled water and administered intragastrically at a dose of 400 mg/kg every other day for 6 weeks. CCl4 (0.1 mL), dissolved in 0.3 mL castor oil (Cat. No. 259853, Sigma‐Aldrich), was injected subcutaneously once weekly for a total of 9 weeks. LPS (0.05 mg), dissolved in 0.2 mL saline, was administered via tail vein injection during weeks 6 and 8. Control animals received equivalent volumes of distilled water and saline instead of BSA and LPS, respectively.

SAR was administered to rats in the SAR and IgAN+SAR groups at 60 mg/kg/day via intragastric gavage from week 7 through week 12. SAR was suspended in 1% carboxymethyl cellulose sodium (CMC‐Na; Cat. No. C5678, Sigma‐Aldrich). The dosage was selected based on a previous study [21]. Rats in the Control and IgAN groups received 1% CMC‐Na alone during the same treatment period. Sample Collection

Twenty‐four‐hour urine samples were collected at weeks 8, 10, and 12 using metabolic cages and stored at −20 C until analysis. At the end of the experiment, all rats were euthanized by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (90 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Blood samples and kidney tissues were collected from deeply anesthetized animals. Half of the left kidney was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for histopathological evaluation. Approximately 1 mm3 of cortical tissue from the remaining portion was fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde for transmission electron microscopy. The renal cortex from the right kidney was dissected from the medulla and stored at −80 C for subsequent Western blot analysis.

Biochemical Analyses

Spectrophotometric Analysis

Both urinary and serum levels of urea, creatinine, and total protein were measured using spectrophotometric methods based on the principle of determining solute concentration from light absorbance and the reaction medium color intensity. All analyses were performed with commercial diagnostic kits (Chronolab, Spain) according to the manufacturer's instructions: total protein (PICC059e, V2017/5), urea (PICC018e, V2021/6), and creatinine (PICC030e, V2023/9). Absorbance values were recorded using a calibrated spectrophotometer, and results were expressed in mg/dL.

ELISA

Serum concentrations of tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α; Cat. No. E0764Ra), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6; Cat. No. E0135Ra), platelet‐derived growth factor (PDGF; Cat. No. E0691Ra), transforming growth factor‐β1 (TGF‐β1; Cat. No. E1688Ra), and SMAD3 (Cat. No. E1259Ra) were quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (Bioassay Technology Laboratory, BTLAB, Zhejiang, China) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. All assays were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility and analytical accuracy. Whole blood was collected via cardiac puncture under general anesthesia and centrifuged at 3,500 rpm for 10 min to obtain serum. The separated serum samples were aliquoted and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Prior to the assay, all reagents and samples were equilibrated to room temperature. For each target protein, standards and serum samples were added to 96‐well microplates precoated with specific monoclonal antibodies. After incubation and washing steps, biotin‐conjugated detection antibodies and horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐labeled streptavidin were sequentially added. The colorimetric reaction was developed using tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate and terminated with the supplied stop solution. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Allsheng AMR‐100, China). Cytokine concentrations were calculated from standard calibration curves generated for each analyte and expressed as picograms per milliliter (pg/mL).

Histopathology and MEST‐C Evaluation

Formalin‐fixed and paraffin‐embedded kidney sections (4 μm thick) were prepared for routine light microscopy. Histological staining was performed to evaluate distinct structural components of renal tissue. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was used to assess overall renal morphology and cellular architecture. Periodic scid‐Schiff (PAS) staining revealed mesangial matrix expansion and an intact glomerular basement membrane. Masson's trichrome staining was used to detect and quantify collagen deposition, an indicator of interstitial fibrosis. Reticulin staining was utilized to examine the organization and integrity of glomerular capillary loops and the interstitial reticular framework.

All sections were examined using a light microscope (Zeiss AX10, Carl Zeiss). Histopathological scoring was performed independently by two blinded pathologists to ensure objective evaluation. Histological lesions were graded exclusively according to the Oxford MEST‐C scoring system to align experimental findings with human IgA nephropathy (IgAN) pathology, thereby enabling a translational interpretation of disease stage. For MEST‐C assessment, mesangial hypercellularity (M0/M1), endocapillary proliferation (E0/E1), segmental sclerosis (S0/S1), tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis (T0/T1/T2), and crescents (C0/C1/C2) were recorded for each animal. For robust quantitative analysis, at least 20 glomeruli per rat were evaluated to determine glomerular scores (M, E, S, and C). Additionally, a minimum of 30 non‐overlapping cortical fields per kidney were analyzed at 200× magnification to quantify tubulointerstitial damage (T score). Immunohistochemistry

Paraffin‐embedded kidney sections (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval in Tris‐EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) at 95°C for 20 min. Following endogenous peroxidase blocking (3% H2O2, 10 min) and nonspecific binding inhibition (3% BSA in TBS, 30 min), sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against IgA (1:32.000), nephrin (1:100), podocin (1:400), fibronectin (1:600), and α‐SMA (1:5.000). HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 30 min, followed by DAB visualization and Mayer's hematoxylin counterstaining. Images were quantified using ImageJ by two blinded observers. For each animal, at least 20 glomeruli were analyzed. The positive staining area fraction (%) was measured using the Color Deconvolution and Thresholding tools, with results expressed as the mean percentage of positively stained areas per glomerulus.

Transmission Electron Microscopy

Kidney samples (~1 mm3) were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4) for 24 h at 4°C, post‐fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (1 h), and dehydrated through a graded acetone series. Samples were then embedded in Araldite CY212 epoxy resin. Representative glomeruli were identified in toluidine blue‐stained semi‐thin sections (~1 μm). Subsequently, ultrathin sections (70–90 nm) were cut with a diamond knife, collected on copper grids, and contrasted with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Ultrastructural examination was performed using a ZEISS Libra 120 transmission electron microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) operated at 80 kV.

Western Blot Analysis

Total protein was extracted from renal cortex tissues using an appropriate lysis buffer containing protease inhibitors, and protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Cat. No. T9300A). Equal amounts of protein (50 μg per sample) were separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes using the iBlot 2 dry blotting system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for podocin and the Bio‐Rad wet transfer system for nephrin. Membranes were blocked with 5% non‐fat dry milk in Tris‐buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween‐20 for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against nephrin (Invitrogen, Cat. No: PA5‐20330; 1:750) and podocin (Proteintech, Cat. No: 20384‐1‐AP; 1:1,000). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with appropriate horseradish peroxidase (HRP)‐conjugated secondary antibodies (Elabscience, Wuhan, China) for 2 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system and captured with a gel imaging system. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ (NIH, USA), and protein expression levels were normalized to glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal loading control.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (GraphPad Software Company). Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Comparisons among groups were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and analyzed using Fisher's exact test. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Validation of the Experimental IgA Nephropathy Model

The experimental protocol successfully established characteristic pathological features of IgAN in rats (Figure 1a). Repeated administration of bovine serum albumin, carbon tetrachloride, and lipopolysaccharide induced a reproducible IgAN phenotype characterized by prominent mesangial IgA accumulation. Immunohistochemical analysis showed minimal IgA immunoreactivity in the control and SAR groups, whereas the IgAN group exhibited marked mesangial IgA deposition within the glomerular tufts (Figure 1b). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in the IgA‐positive area in the IgAN group compared with both the control and SAR groups. Notably, SAR treatment markedly attenuated mesangial IgA accumulation in IgAN rats, indicating a substantial reduction in immune complex deposition (Figure 1c). These findings confirm the successful induction of the IgAN model and demonstrate the modulatory effect of SAR on glomerular IgA deposition.

Sarsasapogenin Ameliorates Renal Dysfunction and Proteinuria in Experimental IgA Nephropathy

Longitudinal analysis of urinary biomarkers revealed a progressive decline in renal function during the development of IgAN (Figure 2a–c). In the IgAN group, urinary urea levels decreased over time, especially in the later stages, while SAR treatment partially restored urea excretion (Figure 2a). Urinary creatinine levels also declined in IgAN rats but were significantly improved with SAR administration (Figure 2b). Additionally, urinary protein excretion increased progressively in the IgAN group, peaking at week 10; SAR treatment significantly reduced proteinuria at weeks 10 and 12 (Figure 2c).

Serum renal function markers were significantly altered in the IgAN group (Figure 2d,e). Serum urea and creatinine concentrations were substantially higher in the IgAN group than in the control and SAR groups, indicating impaired renal function. SAR treatment significantly reduced both serum urea and creatinine levels, although these values did not fully return to those observed in the healthy control group (Figure 2d,e). Notably, SAR administration alone did not significantly affect renal functional parameters in healthy animals. These results demonstrate that SAR alleviated renal dysfunction and reduced proteinuria in experimental IgAN.

The effects of SAR on inflammatory and profibrotic signaling in IgA nephropathy (IgAN) were investigated by assessing serum inflammatory and profibrotic mediators and renal fibrosis‐related histopathological markers (Figure 3).

Serum TNF‐α concentrations showed only modest differences among the experimental groups (Figure 3a). In contrast, serum IL‐6 levels were significantly elevated in the IgAN group compared with the control and SAR groups. SAR treatment significantly reduced IL‐6 levels, indicating attenuation of the inflammatory response (Figure 3b).

Profibrotic mediators were also altered following IgAN induction. Serum TGF‐β1, SMAD3, and PDGF levels were significantly elevated in the IgAN group compared with the healthy groups. SAR treatment attenuated these increases, with the most pronounced reductions observed for TGF‐β1 and SMAD3 (Figure 3c–e). These findings suggest suppression of profibrotic signaling.

Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated markedly increased fibronectin and α‐SMA expression within the glomeruli of IgAN rats (Figure 3f). Quantitative analysis confirmed significant increases in the fibronectin‐ and α‐SMA‐positive staining areas in the IgAN group compared with the control and SAR groups. SAR treatment significantly reduced the expression of both markers, although values remained higher than those of the healthy groups (Figure 3g,h).

Collectively, these findings indicate that SAR attenuates inflammatory and profibrotic responses and reduces extracellular matrix accumulation in experimental IgAN.

Sarsasapogenin Attenuates Histopathological Alterations and Glomerular Lesions in Experimental IgA Nephropathy

Histopathological examination revealed characteristic glomerular lesions in the IgAN group, whereas SAR treatment partially mitigated these pathological alterations (Figure 4). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining showed increased glomerular cellularity and narrowing of the glomerular capillary lumen in IgAN rats compared with the control and SAR groups (Figure 4a). Periodic acid–Schiff (PAS) staining indicated mesangial matrix expansion and distortion of glomerular architecture in the IgAN group. These histopathological changes were less evident in the IgAN+SAR group (Figure 4a).

Masson's trichrome staining did not detect significant collagen accumulation, consistent with the early stage of disease in this experimental model (Figure 4a). Similarly, reticulin staining revealed only mild alterations in the glomerular capillary framework, with no evidence of advanced structural remodeling (Figure 4a). Semi‐quantitative assessment using the Oxford MEST‐C classification demonstrated significantly higher frequencies of mesangial hypercellularity (M1), endocapillary hypercellularity (E1), segmental sclerosis (S1), tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis (T1), and crescent formation (C1) in the IgAN group compared with the control and SAR groups (Figure 4b–f). SAR treatment significantly reduced the frequencies of mesangial hypercellularity and endocapillary hypercellularity and was associated with lower frequencies of segmental sclerosis, tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis, and crescent formation (Figure 4b–f).

Sarsasapogenin Preserves Podocyte Integrity in Experimental IgA Nephropathy

To further investigate the effects of SAR on podocyte integrity, the ultrastructure of podocytes and the expression of slit diaphragm‐associated proteins were evaluated using transmission electron microscopy (TEM), immunohistochemistry, and Western blot analysis (Figure 5).

TEM analysis demonstrated preserved podocyte ultrastructure in both the control and SAR groups. In contrast, the IgAN group exhibited focal foot process effacement and disruption of podocyte architecture (Figure 5a). The IgAN+SAR group showed partially preserved podocyte morphology, with less pronounced foot process effacement than in untreated IgAN rats.

Immunohistochemical staining revealed strong nephrin and podocin expression in the glomeruli of the control and SAR groups (Figure 5b). In contrast, nephrin and podocin immunoreactivity was markedly reduced in the IgAN group. Quantitative analysis confirmed significant reductions in the areas of nephrin‐ and podocin‐positive staining in IgAN rats. SAR treatment significantly increased the expression of both proteins, although levels remained lower than those observed in the healthy groups (Figure 5c,d).

Western blot analysis produced results consistent with the immunohistochemical findings (Figure 5e,f). Nephrin and podocin protein expression was significantly lower in the IgAN group than in the control and SAR groups. Administration of SAR significantly increased the expression of both proteins in IgAN rats, indicating partial preservation of slit diaphragm‐associated proteins.

Collectively, these findings indicate that SAR preserves podocyte structure and partially restores nephrin and podocin expression in experimental IgAN.

Discussion

IgAN, the most prevalent primary glomerulonephritis worldwide, is a chronic progressive disease characterized by its propensity to drive 20%–40% of patients toward end‐stage renal disease (ESRD) within 10–20 years. The pathogenesis of IgAN is globally conceptualized through the multi‐hit hypothesis, a complex cascade encompassing the production of galactose‐deficient IgA1 (Gd‐IgA1), the emergence of targeted autoantibodies, and the subsequent deposition of these macromolecular immune complexes within the renal mesangium [1, 2].

Mesangial immune complex deposition promotes mesangial cell activation and contributes to a local inflammatory and profibrotic environment characterized by increased IL‐6, TGF‐β1, and angiotensin II production. Ultimately, this paracrine milieu is closely linked to alterations in the structural integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier, potentially mediated by a pathological mesangial–podocyte crosstalk [22]. Given the limited efficacy and unfavorable systemic toxicities often associated with conventional immunosuppressive therapies, there is an urgent translational imperative to develop novel therapeutics that simultaneously modulate these interconnected pathways. In the present study, we demonstrated that SAR, a multitarget natural steroidal sapogenin, shows significant renoprotective effects, preserving podocyte architecture and attenuating markers of the TGF‐β1/SMAD3 fibrotic axis. Our findings support our initial hypothesis, illustrating that SAR targets key pathological components of early‐stage disease progression.

A crucial finding of this study is that the established experimental model effectively replicates key pathophysiological signatures of human IgAN. The pronounced mesangial IgA deposition, coupled with robust proteinuria and clinical renal dysfunction, establishes the structural and functional validity of our model. Utilizing the Oxford MEST‐C scoring system, the clinical gold standard for prognostic stratification in human biopsies, we demonstrated that our model effectively mirrors the active, proliferative phase of the disease, as evidenced by the high prevalence of mesangial hypercellularity (M1) and endocapillary proliferation (E1) [2, 3, 4].

Intriguingly, the limited degree of tubulointerstitial fibrosis (T0) and the absence of established crescentic or sclerotic lesions confirm that this model captures an early‐to‐moderate window of IgAN. Clinical paradigms indicate that this specific stage, while dominated by hyperactive cellular inflammation and acute podocyte stress, represents a crucial therapeutic window in which irreversible structural scarring has not yet consolidated, rendering it highly responsive to targeted molecular interventions [22].

Importantly, our data unveil a profound dissociation between molecular priming and histopathological manifestation. Despite the absence of marked collagen deposition on Masson's trichrome staining, IgAN rats exhibited increased α‐SMA and fibronectin expression. This discrepancy suggests activation of early fibrogenic pathways before the development of overt histological fibrosis. Fibronectin and α‐SMA act as immediate‐early molecular anchors correlated with myofibroblast differentiation and subsequent matrix scaffolding [23]. Intercepting the disease process at this pre‐fibrotic threshold offers an important opportunity to restore renal function before irreversible architectural distortion occurs.

Proteinuria remains universally accepted as one of the most powerful, independent clinical predictors of long‐term renal decline and poor survival outcomes in IgAN [2]. In our experimental cohort, the baseline IgAN phenotype was marked by severe elevations in BUN, serum creatinine, and 24‐h urinary protein excretion. Notably, SAR treatment was associated with a striking, time‐dependent reduction in proteinuria, most prominently at weeks 10 and 12, which directly correlated with the stabilization of systemic renal functional parameters, thereby highlighting its potential utility in maintaining filtration barrier integrity under high‐stress conditions.

At the cellular level, a major structural component of the filtration barrier is the podocyte, whose injury and subsequent foot‐process effacement serve as a primary biophysical substrate for proteinuria in IgAN [13, 24]. Under pathological conditions, the deposition of IgA immune complexes in the adjacent mesangium is suggested to induce remote podocyte injury via a paracrine signaling loop. Our transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis captured this apparent mesangial–podocyte crosstalk, demonstrating that the untreated IgAN group exhibited evident yet focal podocyte alterations, primarily characterized by localized areas of foot process effacement and minor architectural variations, rather than diffuse or catastrophic ultrastructural decay. This specific ultrastructural presentation confirms that the model accurately represents an early, non‐destructive stage of podocyte stress.

Notably, SAR administration was associated with qualitative structural preservation, with treated IgAN rats exhibiting well‐maintained interdigitation patterns and a visible reduction in focal foot process fusion. This ultrastructural appearance provides a consistent qualitative correlation that aligns closely with the prominent quantitative protection observed in nephrin and podocin expression levels via immunohistochemistry and Western blot analyses. The preservation of podocyte protein components appears to be a key pathway in SAR‐mediated stabilization of the glomerular filtration barrier.

Oxford MEST‐C evaluation further demonstrated that SAR reduced active glomerular lesions, particularly mesangial and endocapillary hypercellularity [25]. SAR treatment was associated with a targeted suppression of M1 and E1 lesions, with their prevalence decreasing from 100% in the untreated disease group to 28.6%. Because M1 and E1 signify active, inflammatory, and hypercellular flares that clinically dictate the initiation of aggressive immunosuppression in human patients, SAR's capacity to attenuate these lesions warrants further investigation. Conversely, the minor, nonsignificant changes observed in S1 and T1 lesions imply that chronic, structurally consolidated scars may be inherently more resistant to short‐term therapeutic modulation, further reinforcing the rationale that SAR may act primarily as an early‐interceptive agent rather than a late‐stage rescue molecule.

Mechanistically, activated mesangial cells propagate chronic lesions by releasing a wave of pro‐inflammatory and profibrotic cytokines, including IL‐6, TNF‐α, PDGF, and TGF‐β1 [26]. In this study, SAR treatment significantly suppressed serum IL‐6 levels, whereas its effects on PDGF and TNF‐α did not reach statistical significance. This selectivity suggests that SAR may act through specific molecular checkpoints rather than inducing a nonspecific systemic suppression. These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting the anti‐inflammatory effects of SAR in experimental models of tissue injury [27, 28].

Importantly, the suppression of the TGF‐β1/SMAD3 signaling axis in SAR‐treated rats was accompanied by reduced expression of the profibrotic markers α‐SMA and fibronectin, suggesting attenuation of early fibrogenic responses in experimental IgAN [29, 30]. Although histologically detectable collagen deposition was not observed, these molecular changes indicate that SAR may interfere with the early stages of fibrogenesis before irreversible structural fibrosis develops. Similar renoprotective effects through modulation of the TGF‐β1/SMAD pathway have been reported for other natural compounds, including oxymatrine and rutin, in experimental IgAN models [20, 31].

In summary, these findings support further investigation of sarsasapogenin as a potential therapeutic strategy for early‐stage IgA nephropathy. By attenuating inflammatory and early fibrogenic responses while preserving podocyte integrity, SAR may help slow disease progression and preserve renal function.

Limitations of the Study

Despite the promising structural and molecular insights obtained, several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the experimental duration was limited to 12 weeks, which captures the early‐to‐moderate stage of pathogenesis but precludes the evaluation of SAR's long‐term efficacy or potential late‐stage toxicities over extended periods. Second, while sample sizes were statistically sufficient to confirm significant associations and histopathological trends, larger animal cohorts would enhance the generalizability of the outcomes. Third, although the modulation of the TGF‐β1/SMAD3 and IL‐6 pathways was clearly demonstrated, a definitive causal mechanism remains to be established through specific gene knockout models or in vitro receptor‐blocking assays. Finally, while the Oxford MEST‐C framework provides an invaluable translational bridge, differences in metabolic and physiological profiles between rodents and human patients necessitate cautious interpretation when extrapolating these preclinical findings to clinical settings.

Conclusion

In summary, the present study demonstrates that sarsasapogenin (SAR) confers renoprotective effects in an experimental rat model of early‐to‐moderate IgA nephropathy. SAR administration resulted in reduced proteinuria, improved renal function, attenuation of inflammatory and early fibrogenic responses, and preservation of podocyte integrity. These outcomes were associated with suppression of the TGF‐β1/SMAD3 signaling pathway and decreased expression of profibrotic markers, indicating that SAR may modulate key pathogenic mechanisms underlying early IgAN progression. These results support further investigation of sarsasapogenin as a potential therapeutic strategy for IgA nephropathy.
