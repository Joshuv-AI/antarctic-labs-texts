Unravelling Cyclic First-Order Arithmetic
Graham E. Leigh, University of Gothenburg Dominik Wehr, University of Gothenburg

Abstract
Cyclic proof systems for Heyting and Peano arithmetic eschew induction axioms by accepting proofs which are finite graphs rather than trees. Proving that such a cyclic proof system coincides with its more conventional variants is often difficult: Previous proofs in the literature rely on intricate arithmetisations of the metamathematics of the cyclic proof systems. In this article, we present a simple and direct embedding of cyclic proofs for Heyting and Peano arithmetic into purely inductive, i.e. ‘finitary’, proofs by adapting a translation introduced by Sprenger and Dam for a cyclic proof system of μ ​ FOL \mu\mathrm{FOL} with explicit ordinal approximations. We extend their method to recover Das’ result of C ​ Π n ⊆ I ​ Π n + 1 \mathrm{C}\Pi_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{I}\Pi_{n+1} for Peano arithmetic. As part of the embedding we present a novel representation of cyclic proofs as a labelled sequent calculus.

Introduction

Cyclic proof systems admit derivations whose underlying structures are finite
graphs, rather than finite trees.
Such proof systems are especially well-suited to logics and theories which
feature fixed points or (co-)inductively defined objects.
Compared to conventional ‘inductive’ proof systems, cyclic proof systems often do not
require explicit induction rules, which are instead simulated by cycles. This
makes cyclic proof systems particularly well-suited to proof theoretic
investigations and applications in automated theorem proving, which are both
often hampered by induction rules.

There are two common methods for proving that a given cyclic proof system
corresponds to a particular logic or theory.
The usual method is via direct soundness and completeness arguments.
This approach is often not well-suited to arithmetical
theories.
In such cases, equivalence is instead established via proof theoretic translations
between
the cyclic and a conventional inductive proof system.
Translating inductive proofs into cyclic proofs
is usually straightforward whereas the opposite direction requires considerable effort.

For Peano and Heyting arithmetic, two general strategies for obtaining such
translations are present in the literature.
Simpson’s approach
[
16
]
,
later refined by Das
[
7
]
,
formalises a soundness argument for cyclic proofs in subsystems of
second-order arithmetic, using reflection and conservativity results to
‘return to’ the first-order setting. Berardi and
Tatsuta
[
2
,
3
]

instead rely on Ramsey-style order-theoretic principles which can be
formalised in Heyting arithmetic. Both
approaches involve the formalisation of complex mathematical
concepts in first- and second-order arithmetic.

The literature contains other translation methods, although not applied to
arithmetical theories. Of significance to this article is a method first
employed by Sprenger and Dam
[
18
]
for
first-order logic with fixed points
\mu\mathrm{FOL}
. This approach unfolds a cyclic
proof until inductive hypotheses can simply be ‘read off’ the proof’s structure.
The inductive proof resulting from this procedure thus naturally corresponds to
an unfolding of the initial cyclic proof.

In this article, we adapt Sprenger and Dam’s ‘unfolding’ method to present a proof translation between
the cyclic and inductive proof system for Heyting arithmetic.
The strategy can be adapted to Peano
arithmetic with minor adjustments.
Our construction of the inductive hypothesis in the translated proof refines
those employed by Sprenger and Dam, which allows recovery Das’ logical
complexity result: In cyclic
\mathrm{PA}
, proofs with
\Pi_{n}
cycles correspond to

\mathrm{I}\Pi_{n+1}
-proofs. We obtain a similar result for
\mathrm{HA}
.
As part of the proof, we introduce a novel syntactic representation of the
cyclic proofs resulting from the unfoldings called

stack-controlled cyclic proofs
. These allow a clear description the
process of extracting invariants from such proofs
and thus lead to a simpler argument for
the translation from cyclic proofs to inductive proofs.
While we only present a stack-controlled system for Heyting arithmetic, the
notion readily generalises to cyclic proof systems for other logics.
Furthermore, we provide detailed summaries of previous approaches to translating
cyclic proof systems for Peano or Heyting arithmetic to inductive proof systems.

Roughly, the translation of

We only consider finite, non-cyclic derivations in
\mathrm{SHA}
to be proofs. That is,
a proof in
\mathrm{SHA}
is
a pair
\pi=(T,\rho)
consisting of a finite tree
T
and a function

\rho:\mathrm{Nodes}(T)\mapsto\mathcal{R}
, assigning instances of
\mathrm{SHA}
-derivation rules to nodes of

T
. If
t\in\mathrm{Nodes}(T)
and
\rho(t)
is a rule with
n

\Lambda_{1}\mid\Gamma_{1}\Rightarrow\delta_{1},\ldots,\Lambda_{n}\mid\Gamma_{n}\Rightarrow\delta_{n}
then
\mathrm{Chld}(t)
must be
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
such that the conclusion of the
\rho(t_{i})
is
\Lambda_{i}\mid\Gamma_{i}\Rightarrow\delta_{i}
. We consider axiomatic sequents as premiseless derivation rules.
Every rule but
Pop

may only applied to derive sequents well-formed sequents.
We call the conclusion of the rule labelling the root of
T
the
endsequent
.
If such a
proof with endsequent
\varepsilon\mid\Gamma\Rightarrow\delta

exists then
\Gamma\Rightarrow\delta
is
provable in stack-controlled Heyting arithmetic
,
denoted
\mathrm{SHA}\vdash\Gamma\Rightarrow\delta
.

Formally,
\mathrm{SHA}
is not a cyclic proof system because its derivations are
non-cyclic trees. However, through the

- and
Bud
-rules, it retains a
‘cyclic character’. In this sense, it can be considered as a mid-point between
the fully cyclic system
\mathrm{CHA}
and the fully non-cyclic system
\mathrm{HA}
.

5
Unfolding Cyclic Arithmetic

This section covers the translation of
\mathrm{CHA}
-proofs into
\mathrm{SHA}
-proofs. The
translation consists of two parts. First,
\mathrm{CHA}
-proofs are transformed into

\mathrm{CHA}
-proofs of the same end-sequent in a normal-form which simplifies the
‘interaction between cycles’ within a proof. The
\mathrm{CHA}
-proofs in this normal-form
are in fact essentially unfoldings of the original
\mathrm{CHA}
-proofs, meaning computation
content is preserved. This first step is presented in

Section

5.1
and directly follows Sprenger and
Dam
[
18
]
, who show the analogous
result for
\mu\mathrm{FOL}
, first-order logic extended with least and greatest fixed
points. Their proof operates purely at the level of cyclic proofs and their
traces, not interacting with the logical properties of
\mu\mathrm{FOL}
at all, and thus
readily transfers to
\mathrm{CHA}
and
\mathrm{CPA}
. In the second step,
\mathrm{CHA}
-proofs in
said normal-form are translated
\mathrm{SHA}
-proofs, which we present in
Section

5.2
.

We begin by fixing a concrete notion of
tree
to work with in this section.
For
s,t\in\omega^{*}
, we write
s<t
if
s

is a strict prefix of
t
, and
s\leq t
if either
s<t
or
s=t
.
A
tree

T\subseteq\omega^{*}
is a non-empty set of sequences
which is closed under prefixes: if
s\in T
and
t<s
then
t\in T
.
We denote by

[s,t]
the path from
s
to
t
, i.e.
[s,t]\coloneq\{u\in\omega^{*}\mid s\leq u\leq t\}
.
We denote the extension of
s\in\omega^{*}
with
n\in\omega
by
s\cdot n
. Concatenation of sequences is denoted by juxtaposition such that
s\leq st
for all
s,t\in\omega^{*}
.
Each
t\in T
is called a
node
and the nodes
in
\mathrm{Chld}_{T}(t)\coloneq\{t\cdot i\in T~|~i\in\omega\}
are called the

children
of
t
. We omit the parameter
T
, writing
\mathrm{Chld}(t)
,
whenever this does not cause any confusion.
A node
t
is a
leaf
of
T
if
\mathrm{Chld}(t)=\emptyset

and an
inner node
otherwise. We write
\mathrm{Leaf}(T)
and
\mathrm{Inner}(T)
for the set of leaves and inner nodes of
T
, respectively.

A
cyclic tree
is a pair
C=(T,\beta)
of a finite tree
T
and
a partial function
\beta\colon\mathrm{Leaf}(T)\to\mathrm{Inner}(T)
mapping some leaves of
T

onto inner nodes of
T
.
If
t\in\mathrm{dom}(\beta)
one calls it a
bud
and
\beta(t)
its
companion
.
For a bud
t\in\mathrm{dom}(\beta)
, we call the path
[\beta(t),t]
the
local
cycle
of
t
and denote it by
\gamma_{C}(t)
. We omit the parameter
C
,
writing
\gamma(c)
, whenever this causes no confusion.

Cyclic trees
(T,\beta)
which satisfy
\beta(t)<t
for every
t\in\mathrm{dom}(\beta)
are
said to be in
cycle normal-form
. Every cyclic tree can be ‘unfolded’
into cycle normal-form, although at an super-exponential size
increase
[
4
, Theorem 6.3.6]
. We will thus
assume, without loss of generality, that all cyclic trees in this section are in
cycle normal-form. Note that cycle normal-form is not the normal-form of

\mathrm{CHA}
-proofs with which
Section

5.1
is concerned.

Fix a cyclic tree
C=(T,\beta)
. A
finite path

p
through
C
is a
non-empty sequence
p\in T^{+}
such that for every
i<|p|-1
we have

p_{i+1}\in\mathrm{Chld}(p_{i})
if
p_{i}\in\mathrm{Inner}(T)
and
p_{i}\in\mathrm{dom}(\beta)
with
p_{i+1}=\beta(p_{i})
otherwise. We say
p

starts at

p_{0}
and
ends at

p_{|p|-1}
. An
infinite branch
through
C

is an infinite sequence
t\in T^{\omega}
with
t_{0}=\varepsilon
and such
that for each
i\in\omega
either
t_{i+1}\in\mathrm{Chld}(t_{i})
or
t_{i}\in\mathrm{dom}(\beta)
and
t_{i+1}=\beta(t_{i})
. If
C
is the underlying cyclic tree
of a
\mathrm{CHA}
-proof
(C,\rho)
, this induces an infinite sequence

(\Gamma_{i}\Rightarrow\delta_{i})_{i\in\omega}
of sequents with
\lambda(t_{i})=\Gamma_{i}\Rightarrow\delta_{i}
, which we use interchangeably with
(t_{i})_{i\in\omega}
to denote an infinite branch.

5.1
Induction Orders

Sprenger and Dam
[
18
]
work with a
cyclic proof system using induction orders, a soundness condition of cyclic proofs different from the
global trace condition given in
Definition

2.4
. We begin by transferring this soundness
condition to the setting of Heyting arithmetic.

Induction orders are characterised in terms of the strongly connected subgraphs of cyclic
proofs, which can, in turn, be identified with a subset
\eta\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(\beta)
.

Definition 5.1
.

Fix a cyclic tree
C=(T,\beta)
and let
\eta\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(\beta)
.

1.

The set of nodes
C[\eta]\coloneq\bigcup_{s\in\eta}\gamma(s)
is
said to be part of
C

covered
by the cycles corresponding to the buds in
\eta
.

2.

The set
\eta
is
strongly connected
if
there a finite path
p
through
C
which (1) starts and ends at the same
node and (2) visits precisely the nodes in
C[\eta]
, i.e.
p
visits a node
t\in T
if and only
if
t\in C[\eta]
.

3.

A strongly connected
\eta
is said to be
B
-maximal
for
B\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(\beta)
if
\eta\subseteq B
and there
is no
\eta\subsetneq\eta^{\prime}\subseteq B
which is strongly connected.

4.

Let
p
be an infinite path through
C
then
\mathrm{Inf}(p)\coloneq\{s\in T~|~p_{i}=s\text{ infinitely often}\}
.

The strongly connected components of a cyclic tree are closely linked to their infinite
branches: For any infinite branch through a cyclic tree, the nodes visited
infinitely often by it are precisely
C[\eta]
for a strongly connected
\eta
.
A proof of
Lemma

5.2
, for an equivalent definition of
strongly connected component, result can be found in
[
13
, Lemma
5.3]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
t\in T^{\omega}
be an infinite branch of a cyclic tree
C=(T,\beta)
.
Then there exists a strongly connected component
\eta
of
C
such that
\text{Inf}(t)=C[\eta]
.

A key property of

B
-maximal strongly connected components is that they
are disjoint iff they are distinct.

Lemma 5.3
.

For a cyclic tree
C=(T,\beta)
let
B\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(\beta)
and let

\eta,\eta^{\prime}\subseteq B
be
B
-maximal strongly connected components. Then either
\eta=\eta^{\prime}
or
\eta\cap\eta^{\prime}=\emptyset
.

Proof 5.4
.

Suppose there was
s\in\eta\cap\eta^{\prime}
. Then, w.l.o.g. there exists paths

p
and
p^{\prime}
starting and ending at
s
such that
p

and
p^{\prime}
visit precisely the nodes of
C[\eta]
and
C[\eta^{\prime}]
,
respectively. ‘Pasting together’
p
and
p^{\prime}
yields a path from
s
to

s
visiting precisely the elements of
C[\eta]\cup C[\eta^{\prime}]=C[\eta\cup\eta^{\prime}]
, meaning
\eta\cup\eta^{\prime}
is strongly connected. By the

B
-maximality of
\eta
and
\eta^{\prime}
it then follows that
\eta=\eta\cup\eta^{\prime}=\eta^{\prime}
.

Having established some properties of strongly connected components, we now define induction orders.

Definition 5.5
(Induction order).

Let
\pi=(C,\rho)
be a
\mathrm{CHA}
-pre-proof.

\mathrel{\preceq}\;\subseteq\mathrm{dom}(\beta)\times\mathrm{dom}(\beta)
together with a mapping

6.2
Peano arithmetic

A proof system
\mathrm{PA}
of Peano arithmetic can be obtained by modifying
\mathrm{HA}
in the usual manner:
Considering multi-conclusion sequents
\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
instead of
single-conclusion sequents and replacing the intuitionistic logical rules of

\mathrm{HA}

\mathrm{CPA}
for Peano

\mathrm{CHA}

Theorem

6.1
extends to Peano arithmetic in a straightforward manner:

Theorem 6.3
.

If
\mathrm{CPA}\vdash\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
then
\mathrm{PA}\vdash\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta
.

.

Let
\varphi^{*}

\mathrm{CHA}\vdash\Gamma^{*},\neg\Delta^{*}\Rightarrow\bot

, one obtains
\mathrm{HA}\vdash\Gamma^{*},\neg\Delta^{*}\Rightarrow\bot

\mathrm{PA}\vdash\Gamma\Rightarrow\Delta

Definition 6.6

Let

1.

\mathrm{C}\Theta

It remains to scrutinise the translation of

\mathrm{HA}
-proof in

\widehat{I}_{\Lambda}

6.4
Proof size complexity

Another result of Das
[
7
]
relates
\mathrm{CPA}
and

\mathrm{PA}
in terms of proof size complexity: they show that translating from
\mathrm{CPA}

to
\mathrm{PA}
need only incur a singly-exponential size-increase, although this
‘proof size efficient’ translation maps
\mathrm{C}\Sigma_{n}
proofs to
\mathrm{I}\Sigma_{n+2}
, rather
than
\mathrm{I}\Sigma_{n+1}
.

Our translation contains two sources of significant blow-up in proof size: (1) a
super-exponential blow-up when transforming arbitrary
\mathrm{CHA}
-proofs into

\mathrm{CHA}
-proofs of cycle normal form (see
[
4
, Theorem
6.3.6]
) and (2) an exponential blow-up
transforming injective
\mathrm{CHA}
-proofs in cycle normal form to
\mathrm{CHA}
-proofs with

\sqsubseteq^{*}
induction orders via
Lemma

5.12
. All other translation
steps only incur polynomial size increase. Thus, we only provide an
improvement on Das’ translation witnessing
\mathrm{C}\Sigma_{n}\subseteq\mathrm{I}\Sigma_{n+1}
, which exhibits a
non-uniform size increase.

6.5
Applicability of the method

We conjecture that both the method of Sprenger and Dam, and our refinement
thereof, are more widely applicable
for translating cyclic into inductive proofs, even for logics unrelated to
arithmetic.
As we see it, there are three major requirements on a cyclic proof system
and its logic to be amenable to the translation methods:

Internalising progress

The relations
<
and

\leq
are key to the formulation of the inductive hypotheses. They are used
to express which inductive hypothesis is ‘ready’ to be applied and for which a
progress step must yet occur, both in Sprenger and Dam’s method and our
refinement.
Transferring the methods to
other logics requires formulating suitable inductive hypotheses which will
likely rely on analogous methods of ‘internalising’ the progress of the cyclic
proof system in the logic.
At this stage, however, it is unclear how the idea of internalisation can
be formulated in a general sense that is applicable to systems that lack
the explicit expressivity to reason about approximations of fixed points,
for example, type systems and modal logics.

Cut

The treatment of companions, both in Sprenger and
Dam
[
18
]
and in our method, relies on
the
Cut
-rule to introduce a new inductive hypothesis. This seems likely to
be a key feature of the translation method, meaning any system amenable to it
must feature a
Cut
-rule or
Cut
-admissability.

Induction orders

To carry out the method as presented by Sprenger
and Dam
[
18
]
or us, all cyclic proofs
of the system must be justifiable by induction orders, or it must at least be
possible to transform them in such a way that they always become justifiable by
induction orders. For trace conditions in
which the objects traced along branches (e.g. variables, terms, formulas) always
have a unique successor, as in
\mathrm{CHA}
, an argument analogous to

Lemma

5.6
can likely be carried out. It is known that not all cyclic
proof systems exhibit this property, for example those that allow
‘contractions’ in their traces such as
Simpson’s
[
16
]
cyclic proof system
for PA. A possible path to extending our method to
such cases is provided by the work of Afshari and
Leigh
[
1
]
: They provide a translation from
cyclic to inductive proofs for the modal
\mu
-calculus. Rather than relying on
induction orders, their translation is obtained via reset proof systems, another
soundness condition for cyclic proof system. Similarly to Sprenger and Dam’s
approach, the method can be split into two parts: (1) bringing arbitrary cyclic
proofs into a combinatorial normal-form and (2) translating proofs in said
normal-form into inductive proofs. While part (2) of their method was found to
contain a mistake (see
[
11
]
), part (1) remains correct. Indeed, their notion of
normal-form bears a close resemblance to
\sqsubseteq^{*}
induction orders.
As Leigh and
Wehr
[
13
]
demonstrates, most cyclic proof
systems admit equivalent reset systems, so adapting the method presented here to
the setting of reset-proofs should yield a method that is more widely applicable.
