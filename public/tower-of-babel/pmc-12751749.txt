Exploring the experience and needs of postoperative rehabilitation management for elderly patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures after percutaneous vertebroplasty: a qualitative study
Authors: Wei Kang, Junwei Shi, Yang Long, Li Li, Wei Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPercutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) is a critical intervention for rapidly alleviating pain and restoring vertebral stability in elderly patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures postoperative rehabilitation management, covering both in-hospital and postdischarge phases, has received growing attention due to shortened in-hospital recovery periods and the ongoing challenge of managing underlying osteoporosis. However, elderly patients face dual challenges: fragmented adherence to in-hospital rehabilitation due to shortened stays, and inconsistent engagement in postdischarge rehabilitation due to socioeconomic barriers and osteoporosis management gaps. In October 2024, this study investigated comprehensive rehabilitation management protocols spanning from hospital admission to postoperative follow-up in elderly osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture patients. This research aims to elucidate patients' perspectives on rehabilitation management, ultimately facilitating enhanced postoperative functional recovery and mitigating osteoporotic fracture recurrence.MethodWe implemented a qualitative descriptive methodology incorporating face-to-face semistructured interviews. Twenty-four patients were consecutively recruited, with 20 participants meeting the inclusion criteria and completing qualitative investigations in the Orthopedics departments of a tertiary hospital in Shanghai between October and December 2024. Follow-up interviews were conducted with 15 participants through in-person outpatient visits and five via telephone consultations. Three trained researchers administered six predetermined interview questions to each participant through these dual modalities. All interviews were audio-recorded and systematically analyzed via content analysis with NVivo software (version 12).ResultsSix key themes were identified regarding patients' postoperative rehabilitation experiences and needs: establishment of rehabilitation beliefs, heightened perceived benefits of external support, physical dysfunction, negative rehabilitation beliefs, low-quality management environments, aging and comorbidities. The findings revealed both facilitating and hindering factors in postoperative rehabilitation management while emphasizing the importance of establishing personalized multidisciplinary teams in rehabilitation management.ConclusionThis study suggests that clear guidance regarding postoperative rehabilitation exercises and multidisciplinary management protocols including individualized exercise plans, collaborative multidisciplinary rehabilitation strategies, and the development of smart devices may be important for healthcare providers in supporting patients' rehabilitation management. Based on participants' experiences, these approaches show potential for contributing to the alleviation of postoperative movement-related anxiety and fear of falls.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-025-06667-1.Keywords: Rehabilitation management, Aged Population, Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, PVP (Percutaneous Vertebroplasty), Osteoporosis

Background

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, as a leading cause of osteoporotic fractures in elderly individuals, have emerged as a critical public health crisis in aging societies worldwide. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately one-fifth of women and 5% of men aged 50 years or older will experience at least one osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture in their lifetime [1]. In Asian populations, the disease burden is exacerbated by lower peak bone mass, higher prevalence of vitamin D deficiency, and increased fall risk compared to Western cohorts, leading to significantly higher incidence rates among elderly Asian women [2]. In China, a rapidly aging nation, the prevalence of OVCFs in adults aged ≥ 60 years reaches 12.2%, with over 1.8 million new cases annually. The direct medical costs amount to 2.8 billion RMB, where indirect societal costs—including long-term care expenses and productivity losses—continue to escalate exponentially [3, 4].

These fractures, typically triggered by low-energy trauma such as falls, coughing, or bending, extend beyond localized skeletal damage. Kyphotic deformities may reduce pulmonary capacity by up to 15% and increase the risk of chronic pain syndromes, elevating one-year all-cause mortality by 2.3-fold and severely impairing activities of daily living [5]. Although percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP) can rapidly stabilize vertebral structures through cement injection and alleviate acute pain, it remains a palliative intervention that fails to address the underlying osteoporotic pathology. Alarmingly, 47.1% of osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture patients experience secondary fractures within six months post-PVP,, which is attributed to inadequate rehabilitation management [6], a figure that increases to 61.8% in those aged over 75 years with comorbidities such as diabetes or chronic kidney disease, underscoring the urgent need for systematic postoperative care frameworks [7].

To address this challenge, the International Osteoporosis Foundation (IOF) updated its clinical guidelines in 2020, emphasizing multidimensional postoperative management strategies that integrate pharmacological therapy, nutritional support, exercise interventions, and fall prevention education [8]. However, high-quality evidence supporting specific rehabilitation protocols remains limited. Systematic reviews indicate a paucity of randomized trials on optimal exercise prescriptions, bracing duration, or long-term adherence strategies for elderly osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture patients post-PVP [9]. This evidence gap contributes to inconsistent clinical implementation: in China’s primary healthcare institutions, only 58.3% of patients adhere to osteoporosis medications, fewer than 30% receive individualized exercise prescriptions, and 42% of caregivers participate in structured training programs [10].

This "surgery-rehabilitation disconnect" reflects fundamental limitations in current research paradigms—an overreliance on the biomedical model that narrows outcome assessments to biochemical markers or radiological parameters [11], neglecting the multilevel interactions between individual behaviors and environmental factors emphasized by socioecological theories. Social psychological barriers—including medication nonadherence, which increases cognitive decline, intergenerational power imbalances in family decision-making driven by the culture of filial piety, and urban–rural disparities in rehabilitation resource accessibility—collectively undermine treatment confidence and compliance, ultimately compromising recovery outcomes [12].

However, despite growing recognition of these challenges, there remains a critical gap in the literature. While quantitative studies have documented functional outcomes, medication adherence rates, and system-level barriers, and while the importance of multidisciplinary care is acknowledged, a comprehensive and in-depth qualitative exploration focused explicitly on the lived experiences, perceived needs, and the complex interplay of personal, social, and environmental factors influencing postoperative rehabilitation management from the unique perspective of elderly OVCF patients themselves after PVP is notably lacking. Existing research has not sufficiently captured the nuanced, dynamic, and contextually rich realities of this vulnerable population navigating their recovery journey.

Against this backdrop, and to address this significant knowledge gap, our study employs interpretative phenomenological analysis (IPA) through purposive sampling of 20 elderly patients (≥ 60 years) post-PVP, conducting semistructured in-depth interviews to explore their rehabilitation experiences and needs, aiming to provide practical and theoretical insights for optimizing postoperative care models.

Materials and methods

This study was conducted in the Orthopedics departments of a tertiary hospital in Shanghai, China from October to December 2024, where participants received postoperative rehabilitation adhering to standardized protocols of the Chinese Orthopedic Association Spine Surgery Group and Chinese Association of Rehabilitation Medicine [13]; this encompassed progressive functional exercises including ankle pumps, early ambulation, and core-back strengthening via gluteal bridges with three-point, two-point, and five-point spinal stabilization techniques, alongside mandatory use of semirigid thoracolumbar orthoses such as Spinomed® or custom TLSO for 6–8 weeks post-PVP with weaning guidance based on pain and function during outpatient follow-up combined with osteoporosis management and fall prevention education.

Study design

This qualitative descriptive study adopted Husserlian principles to explore the essence of postoperative rehabilitation experiences [14]. The methodology requires bracketing researchers' preconceptions while focusing on participants' subjective realities and contextual interactions [15]. By the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines [16], the design enabled systematic examination of interpersonal and environmental influences on rehabilitation processes. This study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) 32-item checklist. A completed checklist detailing our compliance with each criterion is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Methods

A semistructured interview framework rooted in naturalistic inquiry principles [17]was implemented to capture participants' subjective perspectives. This qualitative approach facilitated direct insights from elderly patients regarding their rehabilitation needs [18].

Participant recruitment

To capture a broad spectrum of experiences and needs regarding postoperative rehabilitation management, twenty elderly osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture patients (aged ≥ 60 years) were recruited through purposeful sampling with a maximum variation strategy [19] from the hospital This approach was chosen to intentionally recruit participants with diverse characteristics relevant to the research question, ensuring rich and varied perspectives on the phenomenon under study. Recruitment continued iteratively until thematic saturation was reached, meaning new interviews ceased to yield substantially new insights or themes related to the research questions. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) radiologically confirmed osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures treated with PVP; (2) ≥ 3 months postdischarge; (3) intact cognitive capacity; and (4) hemodynamic stability. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) severe organ dysfunction/malignancy; (2) cognitive/communicative impairments; (3) concurrent participation in other trials.

Before recruitment, we established a detailed sampling frame targeting maximum variation across key clinical sociodemographic and rehabilitation dimensions, including fracture level, pain intensity, time since surgery, age, education, residence, family support, and community resource access. Furthermore, the type of fractures for all enrolled patients was independently assessed and classified by two senior orthopedic physicians according to the guidelines for osteoporotic vertebral fractures of the German Society for Orthopedics and Traumatology (DGOU) [20]. Data collection and analysis

Twenty elderly osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture patients were enrolled in this investigation. Semistructured interviews were conducted via an empirically developed protocol created by our research team. The team comprises two nurses, a chief orthopedic doctor, and two graduate nurses. They have extensive professional experience in post-operative rehabilitation after percutaneous vertebroplasty and elderly care, and possess extensive expertise in post-PVP rehabilitation and gerontological care. The interview guide underwent pilot testing with three participants, with iterative refinements to optimize question clarity and thematic coverage (see Table 1). A candidate specializing in qualitative research, with no prior experience with participants and no financial conflicts of interest, served as the primary interviewer. Trust was established through the interviewer's consistent participation in daily ward rounds and follow-up clinics alongside attending physicians.

Semistructured interview outline

The data were collected via 15 in-person interviews conducted in private hospital settings and five telephone consultations, each lasting 26–45 min. Digital recordings were obtained following written consent, with data collection terminating upon reaching theoretical saturation. To enhance methodological rigor and data credibility, this study employed triangulation through three complementary approaches: data source triangulation involved collecting detailed field notes during all face-to-face and telephone interviews to document contextual observations such as participants' nonverbal expressions, environmental barriers, and family interactions, alongside researcher reflections, while also supplementing interview data with clinical records including pain scores and comorbidity profiles to contextualize self-reported experiences; investigator triangulation was achieved through three researchers independently coding transcripts and field notes using NVivo 12, followed by iterative discussions to resolve discrepancies and finalize themes; methodological triangulation integrated Braun & Clarke’s thematic analysis [22] focusing on semantic patterns with Colaizzi’s phenomenological approach [23] exploring lived-experience depth, ensuring comprehensive interpretation through the convergence of these distinct analytical frameworks.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Tongren Hospital Ethics Committee (NO.2025–010-01). The Helsinki Declaration observed the principle of protecting research subjects. After being given the study's introduction, each participant gave written informed consent. We reassured each participant that they could withdraw from the study without penalties. Data confidentiality was maintained in accordance with the privacy ordinance.

Data credibility

The credibility of the data was ensured via Lincoln and Guba’s (1985) four criteria for trustworthiness [24]. Credibility was established through prolonged engagement and regular team meetings to discuss data interpretation and reach a consensus on emerging findings. Interviewers implemented real-time validation by paraphrasing key statements for immediate participant confirmation. Transferability was ensured through thick descriptions of participant contexts, supported by direct quotations from transcripts and observational details from field notes… Dependability and confirmability were maintained via an audit trail documenting coding and categorization processes.

Quality control methods

Prior to data collection, a trusting relationship was established with patients to secure their cooperation. During data coding, interviewers engaged in continuous self-reflection to bracket personal biases and actively explore participants’ perspectives, avoiding the influence of their backgrounds, values, or professional identities. Researchers maintained post-interview reflective journals to ensure methodological rigor and objectivity. We also used Field notes as a critical tool for reflexivity, allowing researchers to bracket preconceptions by documenting immediate post-interview reflections on potential biases.

Results

Twenty participants (14 females, 6 males) aged 63–82 years were interviewed. The clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 2. Six core themes and 20 subthemes were identified, which were ultimately categorized into facilitators and barriers influencing post-PVP rehabilitation management in Fig. 1.

Facilitators and barriers in the post-PVP rehabilitation management of elderly patients with OVCFs

Facilitators

Establishment of rehabilitation beliefs

Studies have shown that the establishment of rehabilitation beliefs is the key to promoting behavioral transformation in patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures.

"I thought that injecting bone cement would be fine. Later, the doctor told me that if I did not exercise, my muscles would atrophy and I would be more likely to fall again. Then I realized that relying solely on surgery was not enough." (Interviewee 4, female,64 years old.

"Before, I did not think the fracture was severe. I know the bone has become brittle, and another fall might be more severe. So no matter how painful it is, I have to move." (Interviewee 6, female,65 years old.)

Most patients believe that the decline in life ability after a fracture is a kind of "constraint," and they feel guilty for the sacrifices they have made for their family. They urgently hope to regain self-care ability and regain the freedom of movement. Patients who underwent rapid perioperative rehabilitation after medical education and guidance were more compliant with postoperative home-based rehabilitation. Early postoperative activities reduced patients' fear of movement, and these patients could complete the rehabilitation exercises guided by the rehabilitation therapist exactly.

The results of this study indicate that exercise plays a core role in the postoperative rehabilitation of osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures and the prevention of secondary fractures. During home-based rehabilitation, there are differences in patients' exercise cognition. Some patients reduce activities due to a lack of exercise knowledge or fear of secondary fractures. Professional guidance from physical therapists is crucial for patients' exercise confidence and rehabilitation effects. However, there is a lack of continuous support during the family-based rehabilitation stage. Patients need adaptive strategies such as gradual exercise plans, assistive tools, and social support interventions. The high daily activity level before surgery enables patients to return to society quickly after surgery. After positive physical activity, the activity endurance and muscle strength recovery caused by exercise promoted patients' positive feedback during their rapid recovery.

High sense of benefit from external support

Many patients experience anxiety, depression, and even self-isolation in the early postoperative period, which can increase pain, limited mobility, and concerns about secondary fractures. Emotional support from family members (such as companionship, comfort, and encouragement) helps patients maintain a positive attitude toward rehabilitation.

"Every time I practiced walking, I was terrified of falling, but my family members held me and encouraged me, saying it is okay to take it slow. This made me feel much more at ease." (Interviewee 6, female,65 years old.)

"Before the surgery, the doctor told me that I could not just lie still after the operation and needed to start moving around as soon as possible. Otherwise, my muscles would deteriorate. This reminder made me more proactive in moving around after the surgery." (Interviewee 14, female,74 years old.)

"The doctor taught me how to move slowly and get up from the bed without straining my back. These are truly important." (Interviewee 5, female,80 years old.)

Most patients believe that the treatment plan of injecting denosumab once every six months is superior to taking bisphosphonates orally every day. Adjusting the treatment method has increased patients' trust in the doctor and compliance with anti-osteoporosis treatment. This enables patients to deal with osteoporosis treatment as minimally as possible, enhancing their ability and willingness to adhere to a broader rehabilitation program.

"The doctor asked me to come to the outpatient clinic once every six months for a denosumab injection, the kind that is injected into the belly. It is very quick and effective. Although I can not remember exactly how often I return, the doctor reminds me every time during the follow-up. I think the doctors here are very professional." (Interviewee 17, female,65 years old.)

Patients face risks of functional limitations, pain, and refracture after the surgery. Scientific rehabilitation guidance can reduce postoperative complications and promote functional recovery.

"The doctor told me which actions I could take and which I could not. This gave me more confidence when recovering at home and made me less worried about doing the wrong movements." (Interviewee 18, female,71 years old.)

Barriers

Physical function disorders

Research results have shown that some patients experience a recurrence of low back pain after discharge. Among the 20 patients, three still experienced low back pain symptoms after discharge, which was related to the recurrence of lumbar intervertebral disc protrusion at the same time as the fracture, sensitivity to pain, or insufficient pain management after surgery. Some patients said that although the surgery relieved the pain, their low back still felt weak, making it difficult for them to complete daily self-care and healthy social activities.

"The surgery went well on the first day. I did not feel any pain at all. However, after resting at home for one day, the low back suddenly started to hurt again. I went for a check-up, and the doctor told me that the lumbar intervertebral disc protrusion had also occurred." (Interviewee 2, female,70 years old.)

This excessive fear increases the risk of muscle atrophy and falls, which can increase the risk of prolonged bed rest, forming a paradoxical obstacle in prevention. Moreover, the lack of targeted muscle exercise programs after discharge leads to a decline in muscle function, making it difficult for some patients to complete daily activities. Patients deliberately reduce the ability to walk and exercise, increasing fear of refall and fracture.

"The doctor told me to move, but I am afraid to move and get a fracture again. Later, the rehabilitation therapist told me to take it slowly, to sit and move my hands and feet first, and then stand up little by little. I dared to start trying then." (Interviewee 19, male,64 years old.)

Owing to concerns about drug side effects, such as gastrointestinal discomfort and addiction, some patients do not take painkillers as prescribed by their doctors or lack knowledge of nonpharmacological pain management, resulting in poor pain control and affecting home-based rehabilitation.

"The doctor told me to take painkillers, but after taking them, I had stomach discomfort, so I tried to hold it in and not take them." (Interviewee 13, female,69 years old.)

Most patients tend to rely on lumbar support devices, such as still wearing them for several months after surgery, being insecure about taking them off, lacking guidance on the use of lumbar support devices, and not knowing when to take them off. Long-term wearing of lumbar support devices can lead to atrophy of the lumbar and back muscles, a lack of muscle support for the spine, and instability, affecting the recovery of patients' quality of life.

Negative rehabilitation beliefs

The results of this study indicate that poor health literacy and insufficient knowledge often lead patients to lack initiative in the rehabilitation process. Some patients believe that osteoporosis is just a part of aging and that fractures are accidental events. At the same time, they mistakenly believe that after PVP surgery, "fractures have been cured", downplaying the problem of osteoporosis and ignoring the multiple factors of the risk of refall, thus not taking additional preventive measures.

"This time I fell because of vertigo, not because of brittle bones. I will be careful and will not fall again." (Interviewee 19, male,64 years old.)

"My floor is quite flat, so I do not think I will fall again. I do not need to install handrails." (Interviewee 8, male,65 years old.)

Some patients have previously consumed calcium carbonate-type calcium supplements, which often cause gastrointestinal discomfort symptoms. They stopped taking the medication on their own.

"Calcium tablets cause constipation easily. I just stopped taking them. I do not know if they were useful anyway." (Interviewee 10, female,63 years old.)

Although remote medical care provides convenience for rehabilitation, some elderly patients, owing to unfamiliarity with smart devices, difficulty in operation, and lack of network resources, have difficulty obtaining rehabilitation information through the internet or mobile applications, limiting the accessibility of rehabilitation knowledge.

"I can not use my phone to watch those rehabilitation videos. I always have to wait for my child to come and help me watch." (Interviewee 7, male,77 years old.)

Low quality of rehabilitation environment

The results of this study indicate that the insufficient rehabilitation support from the medical system and the low-level rehabilitation environment make it difficult for patients to carry out home-based rehabilitation.

Some patients reported that they could receive guidance from physical therapists in hospitals or rehabilitation institutions, but lacked continuous professional guidance at home, resulting in a decline in exercise execution rates.

"During hospitalization, someone would guide me every day. However, after I got discharged, I had no idea how to practice anymore." "The hospital is too far away. It takes a whole day to travel back and forth. Therefore, I have not gone for follow-up check-ups after the surgery." (Interviewee 6, female,65 years old.)

"The rehabilitation department in the hospital is hard to make an appointment with. There is no rehabilitation guidance in the community hospital either. Therefore, I had to practice at home by myself." (Interviewee 3, female, 68 years old).

"The doctor did not tell me in detail how to recover. I thought I would be fine after the surgery. I never expected that I would need to pay attention for a long time." (Interviewee 5, female,80 years old.)

The results of this study indicate that the social environment affects patients' compliance with rehabilitation and may also increase the risk of postoperative complications and refractures. Some patients lack the necessary rehabilitation equipment at home, which affects their postoperative functional recovery and increases the risk of falls.

"My home is on the fifth floor without an elevator. The doctor said I should move around more, but going up and down stairs is impossible." (Interviewee 4, female,64 years old.)

"The doctor suggested installing handrails, but I must buy them myself. My family thought it was unnecessary and did not install them." (Interviewee 8, male,65 years old.)

Some patients have difficulty going to the hospital for regular check-ups and rehabilitation treatment, which increases restricted transportation conditions. This affects their rehabilitation progress and the implementation of fracture prevention measures. Owing to work or other reasons, some elderly patients' children cannot provide sufficient care and support, resulting in the patients lacking assistance during rehabilitation.

"The hospital is too far. Taking the bus twice makes it inconvenient to move around. So, I just do not go." (Interviewee 10, female,63 years old.)

"My family thinks I am fine after the surgery, and there is no need to spend time on rehabilitation. They will not remind me to exercise." (Interviewee 2, female,70 years old.)

Studies have shown that patients have insufficient understanding of the severity of osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures and the rehabilitation needs after PVP surgery before the operation, resulting in low postoperative rehabilitation compliance.

Although some patients receive postoperative health education, increased factors such as age, educational level, and memory decline can lead to individual differences in their understanding and application of information. Some patients reported after surgery that they had difficulty remembering all the doctor's suggestions or could not correctly execute them because they did not fully understand the guidance content.

"The doctor mentioned some rehabilitation methods, but sometimes I can not remember them. I do not get reminded at home either." (Interviewee 16, female,80 years old.)

"Sometimes when I go for a check-up, I want to ask the doctor, but the queue is too long, so I do not ask. I figured out how to do it myself at home." (Interviewee 12, female,82 years old.)

Aging and comorbidities

Some patients, influenced by traditional concepts, believe that their physical functions will inevitably deteriorate as they age. Therefore, they believe that special attention should not be paid to the treatment of osteoporosis.

"We did not have anyone taking these bone-boosting medications in our generation. We all lived to our seventies and eighties anyway." (Interviewee 7, male,77 years old.)

Elderly patients often have a comorbid state with multiple chronic diseases, which interferes with their postoperative rehabilitation and management of osteoporosis.

"My heart is not good. I can only walk slowly after the surgery because I get out of breath after walking just two steps." (Interviewee 8, male,65 years old.)

The treatment of some comorbidities affects bone metabolism. Patients tend to focus on obvious or life-threatening symptoms. The presence of comorbidities complicates patients' daily lives and makes it difficult for them to continue postoperative home-based rehabilitation.

"Take a pill after breakfast, another one after dinner. I often cannot figure out which is for calcium supplementation or blood pressure treatment. (laugh)" (Interviewee 9, female,66 years old.)

The frailty of elderly patients is often accompanied by muscle loss. Sarcopenia affects the recovery of patients' physical functions. For example, a decrease in the content of lumbar muscles increases the risk of re-fracturing of the spine.

Discussion

Consistent with our research aim to explore patients' rehabilitation experiences, the barriers identified reveal critical gaps in current post-PVP care. Among them, the core obstacles are physical functional impairment, difficulties in bone density assessment, fear of movement, and reliance on braces. Notably, the posterior spinal muscles, as the key posterior tension band for maintaining trunk posture, significantly decrease in strength with age, and are especially prominent in female patients [25]. Strengthening this muscle group improves lumbar lordosis and posture control and reduces the risk of acute and chronic pain and progressive vertebral collapse. Studies have confirmed that the impact of back extension strength and lumbar range of motion on patients' quality of life exceeds traditional indicators such as bone density [26]. There is a significant correlation between back muscle strength, the number of osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures, the thoracic lordosis angle, and the frequency of falls [27], suggesting that exercise plans targeting spinal extensor muscle weakness, spinal mobility, and posture alignment can simultaneously improve muscle strength, physical function, pain and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [28]. However, we were wary of misleading interpretations of brace improvements in posterior extensor strength: pain relief may involve inflated baseline muscle strength assessments, and long-term bracing dependence can lead to disuse atrophy of core muscles [29]. The study revealed that 40% of participants lacked access to continuous rehabilitation guidance, directly impeding exercise adherence. This supports the implementation of multidisciplinary follow-up systems to bridge hospital-to-home transitions. Our findings also indicate that patients' fear of movement and reliance on braces stem from perceived muscle weakness and postural instability. This highlights the need for rehabilitation programs to address psychological barriers and provide safe, graded exercise guidance aligned with individual functional limitations.

Fear of movement and reliance on braces form another closed-loop obstacle: although traditional spinal braces stabilize the spine through axial support, reduce paraspinal muscle load, and promote fracture healing [30], their rigid design is prone to cause compliance reduction, limited chest expansion, and muscle atrophy complications. The fear of movement caused by postoperative pain and muscle weakness intensifies the patient's overreliance on braces. Studies have shown that semirigid braces (such as Spinomed) or weighted braces (PTSs) can improve the thoracic kyphosis angle and back muscle strength within 3–6 months [31], especially for patients with kyphosis exceeding 60° and pain scores below 5 points [27]. However, it is necessary to be vigilant that prolonged use (> 3 months) may delay muscle recovery [32]. Gradual brace weaning combined with resistance training is supported by trials in elderly osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture patients [33], aligning with our participants' needs for personalized support. Patients expressed dependence on braces due to fear of falls, yet long-term use may exacerbate muscle atrophy. This underscores the importance of developing patient-centered support strategies that balance safety with functional independence, particularly through structured weaning protocols and community-based fall prevention programs.

Participants with more severe fracture types (OF3) are likely to experience greater initial instability and pain, which directly contributes to the heightened fear of movement and stronger dependence on spinal orthoses that we identified in our themes of "Physical Function Disorders" [26]. Conversely, patients with stable OF2 fractures might perceive a quicker recovery and consequently underestimate their long-term rehabilitation needs, potentially explaining the cognitive gaps and behavioral non-adherence encapsulated in our "Negative Rehabilitation Beliefs" theme. Patients with OF3 fractures warrant more intensive, closely supervised, and psychologically-informed programs focused on gradual brace weaning and core stability building [27]. For those with OF2 fractures, interventions must emphasize education and motivational strategies to instill the necessity of sustained exercise and osteoporosis management, reinforcing that minimal initial symptoms do not equate to a low risk of secondary fractures [7].

Bone mineral density (BMD) assessment, a key component of osteoporosis management and a crucial factor informing postoperative rehabilitation planning, faces significant implementation challenges: the rapid discharge after surgery limits the timing of DXA testing, and patients resist examinations due to increased pain caused by body position changes. Moreover, alternative assessment methods such as the Hounsfield unit measurement of CTs have operational complexity. Quantitative ultrasound (QUS) and radiofrequency echographic multispectrometry (REMS) are convenient but cannot fully replace the clinical value of DXA [34]. The dependence of the FRAX tool on bone mineral density data also contributes to these limitations. These limitations result in patients’ insufficient awareness of their bone status, undermining their motivation to engage in prescribed rehabilitation exercises to improve muscle strength and balance. Furthermore, the lack of timely BMD data may hinder clinicians’ ability to tailor exercise intensity and weight-bearing recommendations within the rehabilitation program. Therefore, integrating strategies to overcome BMD assessment barriers, such as opportunistic CT or simplified QUS during the perioperative period, is essential within a comprehensive postoperative rehabilitation framework to enable personalized exercise prescriptions and address functional decline and fracture prevention.

Refracturing after osteoporotic fracture may lead to increased mortality and complications such as pulmonary infection and urinary tract infection [35]. High-quality rehabilitation integrating structured exercise therapy, functional training, and dietary interventions can shorten hospital stays, enhance functional recovery, and attenuate bone loss [9]. Crucially, evidence confirms that such multimodal rehabilitation significantly reduces secondary fracture risk. For instance, progressive resistance training combined with balance exercises improves muscle strength and postural stability, directly lowering fall incidence [27, 31], while calcium/vitamin D supplementation and antiresorptive therapy mitigate bone resorption [7]. Meta-analysis further demonstrate that comprehensive rehabilitation programs reduce refracture rates by 19–35% in elderly OVCF cohorts [7, 9].However, the rehabilitation compliance of patients is generally low, and their cognitive misunderstandings and behavioral deviations constitute the main obstacles: some patients ignore the long-term management of osteoporosis because of the rapid pain relief achieved by minimally invasive surgery and attribute fractures to accidental falls. They mistakenly believe that they can recover on their own by just resting in bed, which leads to a decrease in postoperative treatment compliance. Moreover, long-term immobilization actually aggravates bone loss. In addition, dietary intervention faces challenges in terms of cultural adaptation. Although patients are aware that calcium intake is insufficient, the calcium intake rate among the elderly individuals in China is less than 20%, and the average consumption of dairy products is only 30% of the recommended value [36], their preference for traditional dietary structures and lactose intolerance make it difficult for them to accept dairy calcium supplementation. Intervention should be carried out through nonlactose calcium sources such as dark green vegetables and soy products, and combined with personalized nutrition guidance. Notably, the weak electronic health literacy of the elderly group further restricts the effect of rehabilitation [37]. Although digital platforms can provide standardized rehabilitation guidance videos, elderly patients have low acceptance of electronic media, and the overload of online health information can easily exacerbate cognitive confusion. Simplifying the operation process of intelligent devices through community internet training, combing government policy support to enhance the digital health literacy of the elderly population, and optimizing the design of remote medical services to form a multidimensional rehabilitation support system to bridge the information acquisition gap and enhance the initiative of rehabilitation are recommended.

At present, for the postoperative care of patients after percutaneous vertebroplasty (PVP), although the guidelines recommend continuous rehabilitation exercise and osteoporosis management to prevent secondary fractures, the postoperative rehabilitation of patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures still faces multiple challenges. Evidence-based medical evidence shows that there is currently no widely recognized postoperative rehabilitation program that can effectively improve the prognosis of patients [9], and there are also deficiencies in preventive measures for secondary fractures. In the long-term management of osteoporosis, the fracture liaison service (FLS) model advocated by the International Osteoporosis Foundation has been proven to reduce the rate of secondary fractures through multidisciplinary collaboration, such as fall risk assessment, drug treatment and patient education [38]. However, its promotion in China is limited by the shortage of professional talent and the uneven distribution of medical resources. Clinical research further revealed that 40% of patients reported that they did not receive systematic postoperative rehabilitation guidance from medical staff (8/20 participants). This lack of education directly weakens patients' awareness of the necessity of rehabilitation, and studies have shown that patients' rehabilitation beliefs are highly correlated with their behavioral compliance [39]. Therefore, it is urgent to establish a hospital-led multidisciplinary collaboration mechanism: integrating resources from spinal surgery, rehabilitation departments, endocrinology departments and community nursing, formulating a continuous rehabilitation plan from the perioperative period to the follow-up stage, and enhancing patients' understanding through diverse educational means,such as video tutorials and mobile applications. Notably, elderly patients in China are more inclined toward home-based rehabilitation rather than professional institutions, which requires the design of the plan to take into account the operability in the family setting, such as joint guidance from community nurses. Moreover, economic burden is one of the core factors hindering the quality of rehabilitation, especially in terms of long-term drug treatment, environmental adaptation for aging and follow-up costs.

Owing to their limited income, elderly patients find it difficult to afford the high costs of home fall prevention renovations,such as slip-resistant flooring and handrail installation, which are crucial for preventing secondary fractures. The concept of active aging, it is recommended to assist in completing risk assessment and environmental modification through government subsidies, community support and public welfare projects, while exploring the feasibility of covering part of the rehabilitation costs with medical insurance. In summary, building a high-quality rehabilitation management system requires breaking through four barriers: standardizing of the plan, integrating resources, strengthening of education and providing economic support. Through multilevel collaboration, seamless connections from the hospital to home can be achieved, ultimately improving patients' quality of life and reducing the burden on society's medical care.

Postoperative rehabilitation for elderly patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures requires comprehensive management of comorbidities, perceptions of frailty, and systemic intervention strategies. Studies have shown that comorbidities are prevalent among elderly patients. Among the 20 patients in this study, 55% (11 patients) had two or more chronic diseases (including osteoporosis), and most urgently needed guidance from the multidisciplinary team for rehabilitation measures under the condition of multiple comorbidities. A survey covering 12,496 elderly patients with osteoporotic fractures in China revealed that 71.01% had multiple comorbidities, and that psychological pressure caused by multiple medications significantly weakened the rehabilitation effect [40]. This comorbidity often intertwines with the perception of frailty, and patients tend to attribute osteoporosis to the inevitability of aging, generating negative emotions, and thereby reducing their enthusiasm for rehabilitation. Therefore, it is urgent to establish a multidisciplinary team including a psychologist, collaborate with community medical institutions, and implement a comprehensive management model covering fall risk assessment, personalized rehabilitation plans, and drug adjustments through telemedicine technology. In particular, attention should be given to psychological intervention for patients to break the vicious cycle of "frailty-negative behavior".

At the specific rehabilitation implementation level, early mobilization guided by professionals and sustained family support emerged as core facilitators, directly reflecting patients' expressed needs. Participants emphasized that personalized exercise progression initiated during hospitalization and continued at home reduced fear of movement, while active caregiver involvement provided critical emotional security. Conversely, the absence of multidisciplinary continuity and insufficient family engagement were key barriers. These findings underscore the necessity of integrating family-centered strategies and seamless professional support into rehabilitation frameworks.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. The sample, drawn from specific regional orthopedic centers, risks regional bias and may not fully represent national elderly patient diversity, limiting transferability. Designed for a specific patient category, transferability is also constrained. Despite excluding diagnosed cognitive impairment and simplifying interview questions, participants' advanced age may affect recall accuracy, while potential selective reporting bias exists as highly educated patients' responses might mask lower-literacy needs. As clinician-researchers, inherent perspectives on rehabilitation efficacy could influence thematic emphasis, despite bracketing. Without member checking, nuanced interpretations of colloquial expressions may be missed. Sole reliance on patient interviews lacks contextual depth; caregiver and staff perspectives, crucial for understanding rehabilitation barriers, were not included.

Conclusion

This qualitative study elucidated two facilitators and four barriers in post-PVP rehabilitation for elderly osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture patients within urban Shanghai hospitals.Medical staff should formulate personalized exercise protocols with smart monitoring, build multidisciplinary FLS models integrating osteoporosis management,and develop culturally adapted e-health tools. However, recruitment from a single tertiary center, potential recall bias in elderly participants, and exclusion of caregiver perspectives limit transferability to rural or resource-limited settings. Future work must validate these strategies through multi-center trials across diverse healthcare systems, incorporating caregiver-clinician triangulation to optimize generalizability.
