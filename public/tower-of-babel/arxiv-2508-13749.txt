Order Optimal Regret Bounds for Sharpe Ratio Optimization in the Bandit Setting
Mohammad Taha Shah, Sabrina Khurshid, Gourab Ghatak

Abstract
In this paper, we investigate the problem of sequential decision-making for Sharpe ratio (SR) maximization in a stochastic bandit setting. We focus on the Thompson Sampling (TS) algorithm, a Bayesian approach celebrated for its empirical performance and exploration efficiency, under the assumption of Gaussian rewards with unknown parameters. Unlike conventional bandit objectives focusing on maximizing cumulative reward, Sharpe ratio optimization instead introduces an inherent tradeoff between achieving high returns and controlling risk, demanding careful exploration of both mean and variance. Our theoretical contributions include a novel regret decomposition specifically designed for the Sharpe ratio, highlighting the role of information acquisition about the reward distribution in driving learning efficiency. Then, we establish fundamental performance limits for the proposed algorithm SRTS in terms of upper bound on regret. We also derive the matching lower bound and show the order-optimality. Our results show that Thompson Sampling achieves logarithmic regret over time, with distribution-dependent factors capturing the difficulty of distinguishing arms based on risk-adjusted performance. Empirical simulations show that our algorithm significantly outperform existing algorithms.

I-B

Contributions

•

Regret decomposition for
SR
objectives:
We introduce a novel regret decomposition tailored to the Sharpe ratio. Unlike classical bandits, where regret is driven only by uncertainty in the mean,
SR
regret involves coupled uncertainties in both mean and variance. Our decomposition separates these effects, yielding explicit sensitivity constants that quantify how estimation errors in the two parameters propagate into regret. This decomposition is the foundation of our analysis, and highlights the structural difference between
SR
and reward-only objectives.

•

Fundamental bounds on
SRTS
:
In the classical setting, where tail and moment bounds on reward distributions are assumed to be known, specialized algorithms can be designed such that the probability of error decays at least logarithmically in the horizon. In contrast, we establish distribution-dependent information-theoretic bounds on the performance of Thompson Sampling under the
SR
objective. Our results show that
SRTS
achieves logarithmic regret in stable stochastic regimes, matching the best possible rates while remaining distribution-agnostic. This illustrates both the strength of Bayesian exploration and the limitations of classical specialized algorithms when applied outside their assumptions.

•

Matching upper and lower bounds:
Finally, we prove that the logarithmic regret achieved by
SRTS
is asymptotically tight. Specifically, we show that lower bounds for SR regret match our upper bounds up to constant factors. This result places
SR
optimization within the same asymptotic landscape as cumulative-reward bandits, but with instance-dependent constants that capture the additional complexity of risk-aware learning. In doing so, we provide the first sharp characterization of the fundamental difficulty of
SR
bandits.

•

Extensive empirical validation:
To complement our theory, we conduct a broad set of simulations Gaussian bandits. The experiments consistently show that
SRTS
outperforms state-of-the-art UCB algorithms across a wide range of risk-tolerance parameters, demonstrating both robustness and practical superiority of the proposed approach.

Problem Formulation

Consider a
MAB
setting with
K\geq 2
arms, indexed by
i\in[K]:=\{1,\dots,K\}
. The problem is defined across a time horizon of duration

and at each time
t\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}
, the player selects one arm and observes its reward. By pulling an arm
i
at time

is obtained from the distribution

X_{i,t}\sim\nu_{i}
, which is independent of
X_{j,t}\sim\nu_{j},\forall{j\in[K]\backslash\{i\}}
implying the reward samples are independent across arms and time slots. A policy
\pi
orchestrates the action of the player at each time instant and is driven by the historical data. Let
s_{i,n}
represent the number of times an arm
i
has been pulled until (and including) time
n
. Here, we aim to identify the arm that efficiently balances its anticipated reward and variability. The reward being associated with return and variability with risk, we focus on the
SR
, which is the ratio of return and risk. A greater
SR
signifies an improved risk-adjusted return of a portfolio. Under traditional settings,
SR
is defined as
\frac{\mu}{\sigma}
, i.e., the ratio of mean to standard deviation of a random variable. In this work, instead of following the traditional definition, we define
SR
as

Definition 1
.

The
Sharpe ratio
of an arm
i
with mean
\mu_{i}
, variance
\sigma^{2}_{i}
, and risk tolerance
\rho
is

\displaystyle\xi_{i}=\frac{\mu_{i}}{L_{0}+\rho\sigma^{2}_{i}},

where
L_{0}
is the regularization term and is same for all the arms.

The regularization term is a necessary constant to stabilize the value of estimated
SR
. For example, in variance estimation, we see that the estimate of variance can become extremely small, thus leading to a large value of
SR
, which in turn affects the performance of the
TS
algorithm. Thus we use the regularization term to have a stable estimate of
SR
. The values of
L_{0}
lies in the range of
(0,1]
, although one can increase it to
\infty
, but for
(0,1]
it serves the purpose of stabilization and simplifies our analysis. Using this definition offers a tractable approach to deriving regret guarantees and designing algorithms in a simplistic manner. Without loss of generality, we assume that the optimal arm is unique and arm
1
is the optimal one. i.e.,
\xi_{1}=\max_{i\in[K]}\xi_{i}
. The empirical
SR
for an arm
i
is calculated from the i.i.d. samples
\{X_{i,q}\}_{q=1}^{t}
drawn from its distribution

, and given is as

\displaystyle\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\xi}_{i,t}=\frac{\rho\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,t}}{L_{0}+\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}^{2}_{i,t}}

(1)

where
\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,t}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{q=1}^{t}X_{i,q}
and
\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}^{2}_{i,t}=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{q=1}^{t}(X_{i,q}-\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,t})^{2}
. It is important to note that employing a biased estimate of variance does not have any impact on regret guarantees.

Let
\Delta_{i}=\xi_{1}-\xi_{i}
denote the sub-optimality gap of
SR
of any arm
i
and the first arm. Also, let
\Lambda_{i,j}=\mu_{i}-\mu_{j}
, denote the gap between the mean of arm
i
and
j
. We use
\pi(t)
to denote the arm that is pulled at time
t
. For a learning policy
\pi\left(\cdot\right)
and the reward process
\{X_{\pi(t),t}\}_{t=1}^{n}
, we define the
SR
experienced by the player as
algorithmic empirical
SR
and is given as

\displaystyle\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\xi}_{n}(\pi)=\frac{\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{n}(\pi)}{L_{0}+\rho\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}^{2}_{n}(\pi)}

(2)

where
\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{n}(\pi)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{\pi(t),t}
and
\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}^{2}_{n}(\pi)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}(X_{\pi(t),t}-\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{n}(\pi))^{2}
. This results in the formulation of the regret for each policy
\pi
, which is the difference between the policy’s

(in short for algorithmic empirical

) and its optimal

. The
algorithmic mean
(in short for algorithmic empirical mean), i.e. the mean reward of the player, is rewritten as

\displaystyle\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{n}(\pi)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{t=1}^{n}X_{\pi(t),t}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}

II-A

The Role of Arm Pull Counts
s_{i,n}

A central quantity in our analysis is
s_{i,n}
, the number of times arm
i
is selected by a policy
\pi
up to round
n
. Both the algorithmic mean and variance in (
2
) and (
3
) decompose naturally in terms of
\{s_{i,n}\}_{i=1}^{K}
. Intuitively, the regret of a policy depends on how often it misallocates pulls to sub-optimal arms, which is exactly what
s_{i,n}
quantifies. In the worst case,
\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]\leq n
for any
i
, since no arm can be pulled more than
n
times.

Beyond expectations, understanding the variability of
s_{i,n}
is also important: if the variance of pull counts is too large, concentration arguments for regret bounds become fragile. To address this, we invoke the Efron–Stein inequality.

Lemma 1

(Variance of arm pull counts)
.

Let
s_{i,n}=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\!\left(\pi(t)=i\right)
denote the number of times arm
i
is pulled by a policy
\pi
up to round
n
. Then the variance of
s_{i,n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[s_{i,n}]\leq\frac{n}{2}.

Proof.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{n}
be independent random variables taking values in some space
\mathcal{X}
, and let
f\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\right)
be a square-integrable function. Let
X_{1}^{\prime},\dots,X_{n}^{\prime}
be independent copies of
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
, and define:

\displaystyle f^{(i)}=f\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{i-1},X_{i}^{\prime},X_{i+1},\dots,X_{n}\right),

then the Efron-Stein inequality states
\mathbb{V}\left[f\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\right)\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(f-f^{(i)}\right)^{2}\right]
.

For
s_{i,n}=f\left(X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\right)=\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\left(X_{t}=i\right)
(where
X_{t}=A_{t}
is the arm pulled at time
t
), we apply Efron-Stein inequality on
\mathbb{V}\left[s_{i,n}\right]
. If we change
X_{t}
to
X_{t}^{\prime}
(leaving other
X_{j}
fixed), the maximum change in
s_{i,n}
is
\left|f(X)-f(X^{(t)})\right|\leq 1
, because at most one count
\mathbb{I}(X_{t}=i)
can flip (from
0
to
1
or vice versa). Substituting into Efron-Stein inequality, we get,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[s_{i,n}]\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(s_{i,n}-s_{i,n}^{(t)}\right)^{2}\right]\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{t=1}^{n}1=\frac{n}{2}.

Thus, the variance of the arm pull count
s_{i,n}
is at most linear in
n

∎

Applying the Efron-Stein inequality to the function
f(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})=s_{i,n}
, where
X_{t}
denotes the arm selected at round
t
, shows that the effect of perturbing any single
X_{t}
is bounded by
1
. Summing over all
n
steps yields
\mathbb{V}[s_{i,n}]\leq n/2
. Importantly, this holds despite dependence in the sequence

, since the Efron-Stein argument isolates the local effect of each perturbation. Lemma
1
establishes that fluctuations in
s_{i,n}
are well controlled. Thus, when decomposing regret into terms proportional to
\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]
, we do not need to worry about pathological instability in the allocation of pulls. In fact, the bound reinforces the intuition that the primary driver of regret is
how frequently
sub-optimal arms are played, rather than higher order randomness in the sequence of pulls.

Step 1:

\displaystyle|f(x)-f(y)|\leq L|x-y|\quad\forall x,y.

\displaystyle|f(D)-f(D^{\prime})|\leq\left(\sup_{\xi\geq L_{0}}|f^{\prime}(\xi)|\right)|D-D^{\prime}|\leq\frac{1}{L_{0}^{2}}|D-D^{\prime}|.

L_{0}^{-2}-

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[f(D)]=\mathbb{E}\left[(f(D)-\mathbb{E}[f(D)])^{2}\right].

IV
Thompson Sampling algorithm for the Gaussian Sharpe ratio
MAB

The proposed algorithms can effectively solve the risk-averse MAB problem in two extreme scenarios (e.g., large or small
\rho
). However, it is difficult to decide on a suitable threshold for a player to choose the algorithm she needs because she does not know the true means and variances (and neither does she know
\rho
). In this section, we propose a combined or unified Thompson Sampling algorithm to address this problem. The player chooses a prior over the set of feasible bandits parameters for both the mean and precision. In each round, the player samples a pair of parameters from each posterior and plays an arm according to the optimal action under these parameters. The
SRTS
algorithm, shown in Algorithm 1, uses the conjugate prior of a Gaussian which is parametrized by the mean and precision. When
\rho
is small, the mean-variance MAB problem reduces to a variance minimization problem. When
\rho=0
, the problem reduces to the standard reward maximization problem. In this case,
SRTS
is consistent with Mean TS. Hence, we expect that
SRTS
performs well over all
\rho\in\mathbb{R}
. We show that this is indeed the case in Theorem 2.

V
Regret analysis for Gaussian bandits

We use
SRTS
as an example to demonstrate the key steps in proving regret bounds. There are two main difficulties in analyzing the regret for
SRTS
. First, the Thompson sample of the
SR
is not sub-Gaussian, since it involves a ratio of random quantities (mean over variance), and hence exhibits heavier tails. This makes it necessary to derive sufficiently tight lower and upper bounds on the tail probability of the posterior distribution. The second difficulty arises from the hierarchical nature of the model parameters in
SRTS
: the posterior draws for the mean and variance come from different distributions, and their dependence complicates the analysis compared to standard
MAB
.

We note that in
SRTS
, the Thompson samples of the mean
\theta_{i,t}
and variance
\sigma_{i,t}^{2}
are drawn separately, and the
SR
statistic is
\hat{\xi}_{i,t}=\frac{\theta_{i,t}}{L_{0}+\frac{\rho}{\tau_{i,t}}}
, where
L_{0}>0
and
\rho\geq 0
are scaling parameter and variance sensitive parameters respectively.
L_{0}
ensures stability of the denominator. Deriving sharp concentration bounds for
\hat{\xi}_{i,t}
is challenging because the numerator is sub-Gaussian while the denominator involves the inverse-Gamma (or Gamma) distribution, which is only sub-exponential.

From Theorem
1
, we know that it suffices to bound the expected number of times a suboptimal arm is chosen,
\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]
. Let the Thompson sample of the mean-variance pair for arm
i
at time

(\theta_{i,t},\sigma_{i,t}^{2})
. Define the
SR
sample as above, and denote the optimal arm’s Sharpe ratio as
\xi_{1}
. We introduce the event

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{i}(t):=\Big\{\hat{\xi}_{i,t}\leq\xi_{1}-\varepsilon\Big\},

(20)

which corresponds to the case where the Thompson sample of arm
i
’s
SR
is
\varepsilon
smaller than that of the optimal arm at period
t
.

Intuitively, the event
\mathcal{G}_{i}(t)
occurs with high probability once arm
i
has been sufficiently explored, since its posterior distribution concentrates around its true
SR
. However, the Thompson sampling algorithm does not necessarily avoid choosing
i
even when
\mathcal{G}_{i}(t)
holds, because the randomized draws may still produce spuriously high Sharpe ratio samples for arm
i
. Thus, with small but non-negligible probability, arm
i
is selected even under
\mathcal{G}_{i}^{c}(t)
.

The regret decomposition for
SRTS
can be organized into two complementary regimes:

•

Exploration regime
— when posterior uncertainty is still large, so the concentration event
\mathcal{G}_{i}(t)
may fail with non-negligible probability. In this phase, the algorithm frequently samples suboptimal arms in order to reduce uncertainty about their Sharpe ratios.

•

— once sufficient evidence has been gathered,
\mathcal{G}_{i}(t)
holds with high probability, meaning that the posterior sample of arm
i
’s
SR
is clearly worse than that of the optimal arm. Nevertheless, due to the inherent randomness of Thompson sampling, arm
i
can still be chosen with small probability, contributing a residual but controlled amount to the regret.

This decomposition ensures that the total expected number of pulls of a suboptimal arm can be bound by analyzing (i) the rate at which posterior concentration eliminates the exploration regime, and (ii) the small probability of residual selection in the exploitation regime. Let

\displaystyle H_{i,q}:=\mathbb{P}_{t}\left(\mathcal{G}_{i}^{c}(t)\mid s_{i,t}=q\right)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{R}_{n}(\pi)\right]\geq\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}\left(s_{i}\geq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\right)\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\left(\Delta_{i}-\frac{\rho\xi_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{L_{0}}\right)-A_{7}

Proof.

We attain regret decomposition as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{R}_{n}(\pi)\right]=n\mathbb{E}\left[\xi_{1}-\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\xi}_{n}(\pi)\right]

\displaystyle=n\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\frac{\mu_{1}}{L_{0}+\rho\sigma^{2}_{1}}-\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}}{L_{0}+\rho\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}_{i,s_{i,n}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j\neq i}s_{i,n}s_{j,n}\left(\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}-\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{j,s_{j,n}}\right)^{2}\right)}\right]

\displaystyle=n\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]\frac{\mu_{1}}{L_{0}+\rho\sigma^{2}_{1}}-\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}}{L_{0}+\rho\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}_{i,s_{i,n}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j\neq i}s_{i,n}s_{j,n}\left(\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}-\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{j,s_{j,n}}\right)^{2}\right)}\right]\right)

(28)

First, we find the upper bound on the expectation term i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}}{L_{0}+\rho\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}_{i,s_{i,n}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j\neq i}s_{i,n}s_{j,n}\left(\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}-\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{j,s_{j,n}}\right)^{2}\right)}\right]=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}\right]\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{L_{0}+\rho\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}_{i,s_{i,n}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j\neq i}s_{i,n}s_{j,n}\left(\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}-\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{j,s_{j,n}}\right)^{2}\right)}\right]+

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}},\frac{1}{L_{0}+\rho\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}_{i,s_{i,n}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j\neq i}s_{i,n}s_{j,n}\left(\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}-\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{j,s_{j,n}}\right)^{2}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_{i}\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]}{L_{0}}+

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}\right]\mathbb{V}\left[\frac{1}{L_{0}+\rho\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}_{i,s_{i,n}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j\neq i}s_{i,n}s_{j,n}\left(\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}-\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{j,s_{j,n}}\right)^{2}\right)}\right]}

(29)

From (
18
)

\displaystyle\sqrt{\mathbb{V}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}\right]\mathbb{V}\left[\frac{1}{L_{0}+\rho\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}s_{i,n}\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}_{i,s_{i,n}}^{2}+\frac{1}{2n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\sum_{j\neq i}s_{i,n}s_{j,n}\left(\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{i,s_{i,n}}-\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\mu}_{j,s_{j,n}}\right)^{2}\right)}\right]}\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{A_{7}}{n}\right)

where
A_{7}>0
is some constant. Thus (
28
) becomes,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{R}_{n}(\pi)\right]\geq n\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]\frac{\mu_{1}}{L_{0}+\rho\sigma^{2}_{1}}-\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_{i}\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]}{L_{0}}-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{A_{7}}{n}\right)\right)

Adding and subtracting
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\xi_{i}\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{R}_{n}(\pi)\right]\geq n\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]\xi_{1}+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\xi_{i}\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\xi_{i}\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]-\frac{1}{L_{0}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mu_{i}\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{A_{7}}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=n\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]\left(\Delta_{i}-\frac{\rho\xi_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{L_{0}}\right)-\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{A_{7}}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]\left(\Delta_{i}-\frac{\rho\xi_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{L_{0}}\right)-A_{7}

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{i=1}^{K}\mathbb{P}\left(s_{i}\geq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\right)\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\left(\Delta_{i}-\frac{\rho\xi_{i}\sigma_{i}^{2}}{L_{0}}\right)-A_{7}

where the proof of
\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]\geq\mathbb{P}\left(s_{i}\geq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\right)\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}
follows from Markov’s inequality and is discussed in detail in next subsection.
∎

VI-A

Proof of
\mathbb{E}[s_{i,n}]\geq\mathbb{P}\left(s_{i}\geq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\right)\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}

We start with a couple of basic definitions.

Definition 3
.

The
{\rm KL}
divergence between 2 distributions
\nu
and
\tilde{\nu}
is given by

\displaystyle I(\nu,\nu^{\prime})=\mathbb{E}_{\nu}\bigg[\log\frac{f_{\nu}(X)}{f_{\tilde{\nu}}(X)}\bigg],

where
E_{\nu}
denotes the expectation operator with respect to
\nu
,
f_{\nu}(X)
is the probability density function of
\nu
and
f_{\tilde{\nu}}(X)
is the probability density function of
\tilde{\nu}
.

Definition 4
.

A policy
\pi
is
\alpha
-consistent
0<\alpha<1
, if for one parameter distributions and for all
i\neq*
,
\mathbb{E}[s_{i}(n)]\leq n^{\alpha},
where
*
represents the optimal arm.

Specifically, we assume that the distribution of the arm
i
is given by
f(.;\theta_{i})
and the distribution model
\mathcal{F}=(f(.;\theta_{1}),...,f(.;\theta_{K}))
can be represented by

{\Theta}=(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{K})
. The parameters
\theta_{i}
take values from a set
\mathcal{U}
satisfying the following regularity conditions (similar
to that in
[
5
]
).

Assumption 1
.

For any
\theta
,
\lambda
, and
\lambda^{\prime}\in\mathcal{U}
, and for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists a
\zeta>0
such that
0<\xi(\lambda^{\prime})-\xi(\lambda)<\zeta
implies
|I(f(.;\theta),f(.;\lambda^{\prime}))-I(f(.;\theta),f(.;\lambda))|<\epsilon
. This condition ensures that small changes in the SR of distributions result in small changes in their KL-divergences. Thus, implies that the parameter space
\mathcal{U}
is sufficiently dense to allow fine distinctions between different arms.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\Theta
be the given distribution model, and let

i
denote the index of a suboptimal arm under
\Theta
. Under Assumption 1, number of times arm
i
is played under
\pi
for any constant
C_{1}<1-\alpha
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[s_{i}(n)\geq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\bigg]=1.

Furthermore, with Assumption 2, there exists
n_{o}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[s_{i}(n)\geq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\bigg]\geq C_{2},\quad\textit{for all}\quad n>n_{0}

where constant
0<C_{2}<1
is independent of
n
and
\mathcal{F}
.

Proof.

Assume a distribution model
\mathcal{F}
, such that the first arm is optimal, denoted by
\ast
. We construct a new distribution model
\mathcal{F}^{i}
by changing
f_{i}
to
\tilde{f}_{i}
where
i\neq\ast
is optimal. Thus

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}=(f_{1},f_{2},....f_{i}...f_{K})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{F}^{i}=(f_{1},f_{2},....\tilde{f}_{i}....f_{K})

such that
\left(\zeta>\tilde{\xi}_{i}-\xi_{*}>0\right)
and

\left|I(f_{i},f_{*})-I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})\right|\leq\epsilon
for arbitrary small
\epsilon
using assumption 1.
Let
\gamma
be the log-likelihood ratio between the
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{i}\colon\gamma=\log\frac{f_{i}\left(X_{i}\left(t_{i}(1)\right)\right)\dots f_{i}\left(X_{i}\left(t_{i}(s_{i})\right)\right)}{\tilde{f}_{i}\left(X_{i}\left(t_{i}(1)\right)\right)\dots\tilde{f}_{i}\left(X_{i}\left(t_{i}(s_{i})\right)\right)}
where
t_{i}(s_{i})
is the time step when the
s_{i}
-th observation of arm
i
occurred. We show that it is unlikely to have
s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}
under two different scenarios for
\gamma
, i.e,
\gamma>C_{3}\log n
and
\gamma<C_{3}\log n
.

Case 1
:
\gamma>C_{3}\log n
for a constant
C_{3}>C_{1}
.
We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})},\gamma>C_{3}\log n\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})},\sum_{\tau=1}^{s_{i}}\log\frac{f_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}{\tilde{f}_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}>C_{3}\log n\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[\max_{t\leq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}}}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\log\frac{f_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}{\tilde{f}_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}>\frac{C_{3}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\Bigg[\max_{t\leq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}}}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\log\frac{f_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}{\tilde{f}_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}>\frac{C_{3}}{C_{1}}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}\bigg]

By the strong law of large numbers
\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\log\frac{f_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}{\tilde{f}_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}\rightarrow I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})
a.s as
t\rightarrow\infty
. Thus,

\max_{t\leq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}}}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\log\frac{f_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}{\tilde{f}_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}\rightarrow I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})
a.s as
n\rightarrow\infty
. So, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\left[\max_{t\leq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}}}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\log\frac{f_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}{\tilde{f}_{i}(X_{k}(\tau))}>\frac{C_{3}}{C_{1}}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}\right]\rightarrow 0

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Conclusion:
For
\gamma>C_{3}\log n
, by strong law of large numbers, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})},\gamma>C_{3}\log n\bigg]\rightarrow 0,\,\text{as}\,n\rightarrow\infty

(30)

Also, using Chernoff bound,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}[s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})},\gamma>C_{3}\log n]
is upper bounded for finite
n
as

\displaystyle\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}n^{-aI(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})\frac{(C_{3}-C_{1})^{2}}{C_{1}}}

(31)

Here
a
is the Chernoff bound constant. This result, (
31
) is obtained from (Eq.(52) of
[
38
]
) and is given below.

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})},\gamma>C_{3}\log n\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})},\sum_{s=1}^{s_{i}}\log\frac{f_{i}(X_{i}(s))}{\tilde{f}_{i}(X_{i}(s))}>C_{3}\log n\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[\max_{t<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}},\sum_{s=1}^{t}\log\frac{f_{i}(X_{i}(s))}{\tilde{f}_{i}(X_{i}(s))}>C_{3}\log n\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\log\frac{f_{i}(X_{i}(s))}{\tilde{f}_{i}(X_{i}(s))}-\frac{1}{t}C_{1}\log{n}>\frac{1}{t}C_{3}\log{n}-\frac{1}{t}C_{1}\log{n}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[\frac{1}{t}\sum_{s=1}^{t}\log\frac{f_{i}(X_{i}(s))}{\tilde{f}_{i}(X_{i}(s))}-I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})>\frac{1}{t}C_{3}\log{n}-\frac{1}{t}C_{1}\log{n}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=1}^{\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}}\exp(\frac{-a_{1}(C_{3}-C_{1})^{2})\log^{2}n}{t})

(32)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}n^{-aI(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})\frac{(C_{3}-C_{1})^{2}}{C_{1}}}

(33)

(
32
) holds because
I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})>\frac{1}{t}C_{1}\log{n}
and (
33
) holds according to Chernoff bound.

Case 2
:
\gamma\leq C_{3}\log n
.
By Markov’s Inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}^{i}}\left[s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}^{i}}\left[n-s_{i}\geq n-\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}\right]\frac{\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}^{i}}[n-s_{i}]}{n-\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}}

(34)

\displaystyle\text{Also,}\;\;\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}[S(n)]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}^{i}}[\mathbb{I}_{S(n)}e^{\gamma}],

(35)

where
S(n)
is the set of all the observations over
n
that satisfy a particular event and
\gamma
is the log-likelihood ratio between
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{F}^{i}
. Finally, (
35
) is an application of Radon-Nikodym theorem
[
39
]
.
Using (
34
) and (
35
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\left[s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})},\gamma\leq C_{3}\log n\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I\left(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i}\right)},\gamma\leq C_{3}\log n\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}^{i}}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I\left(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i}\right)},\gamma\leq C_{3}\log n\right\}\times e^{\gamma}\right]

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}n^{C_{3}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}^{i}}\left[\mathbb{I}\left\{s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})},\gamma\leq C_{3}\log n\right\}\right]\leq n^{C_{3}}\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}^{i}}\left[{s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I\left(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i}\right)}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{n^{C_{3}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}^{i}}\left[n-s_{i}\right]}{n-\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{Kn^{C_{3}+\alpha}}{{n-\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}}}

(36)

Step (a) follows from substituting
\gamma\leq C_{3}\log{n}
in
e^{\gamma}
. The last inequality is due to
\alpha
-consistency. The split on
\gamma
controls the event that a suboptimal arm is sampled too little. Case
1
uses concentration of the empirical log–likelihood ratio around
I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})
; Case
2
transports probabilities across models using the likelihood–ratio identity and then applies
\alpha
-consistency to upper bound
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{F}^{i}}\left[n-s_{i,n}\right]
. Together, these force
s_{i,n}\gtrsim\frac{C_{1}}{I(f_{i},f_{*}}\log n
with constant probability precisely the ingredient needed in the regret lower bound.

From (
30
), (
36
) and the fact that
{I(f_{i},f_{*})}-{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}
can be arbitrarily small, for
C_{3}<1-\alpha
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\bigg]\rightarrow 0\,\text{as}\,n\rightarrow\infty

\displaystyle\text{Equivalently,}\;\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[s_{i}\geq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\bigg]\rightarrow 1\,\text{as}\,n\rightarrow\infty

Using (
31
) and (
36
), we conclude, for
C_{3}<1-\alpha
, when Assumption 2 is satisfied,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[s_{i}<\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}n^{-aI(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})\frac{(C_{3}-C_{1})^{2}}{C_{1}}}+\frac{Kn^{C_{3}+\alpha}}{{n-\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},\tilde{f}_{i})}}}.

Thus, there is a
n_{0}
such that for
n>n_{0}
,
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{F}}\bigg[s_{i}\geq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\bigg]\geq C_{2}
,
for some constant
C_{2}>0
independent of
n
and
\mathcal{F}
. We emphasize that constant
C_{1}
and
C_{3}
are chosen to satisfy
C_{1}<C_{3}<1-\alpha
.

∎

Theorem 3
.

Consider the
MAB
problem where the objective is to optimize the SR of the observed rewards. Let
\pi
be an
\alpha
-consistent
policy and
\Theta=\left\{\left\{\theta_{i}\right\}_{i=1}^{K}\colon\theta_{i}\in\mathcal{U}\right\}
be the distribution model. Under Assumption 1, for any constant

C_{1}<1-\alpha
,
0<C_{2}<1
, with
f_{i}
and
f_{*}
as the reward distribution of the i-th arm and the optimal arm respectively, then,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{s_{i}(n)}{\log n}\geq\sum_{i=1,i\neq*}^{K}\frac{C_{1}C_{2}}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}.

Proof.

We need to establish a lower bound on expected sub-optimal pulls, i.e,
\mathbb{E}[s_{i}]
, which is a straightforward consequence of Lemma 2. By using Markov’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[s_{i}]\geq\mathbb{P}\left[s_{i}\geq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\right]\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}

\displaystyle\text{So,}\;\;\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[s_{i}]}{\log n}

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{P}\left[s_{i}\geq\frac{C_{1}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}\right]\frac{C_{1}}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}=\frac{C_{1}}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}

Similarly, by Markov’s inequality, we have, when assumption 2 is satisfied, there is
n_{0}\in n
such that for all
n>n_{0}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[s_{i}]\geq\frac{C_{1}C_{2}\log n}{I(f_{i},f_{*})}

∎

The lemma and theorem together imply that, for any
\alpha
–consistent policy, the cumulative regret under the
SR
objective is
at least
logarithmic in
n
, with arm wise constants determined by the
SR
adjusted gaps and the KL information numbers. Combined with our
\log n
upper bound for
SRTS
under Gaussian rewards, this certifies the
order–optimality
of
SRTS
in the model–specific regime. The structure and constants mirror those established in the paper’s model–specific analysis for related risk–aware bandit objectives, reinforcing that a Thompson sampling style algorithm can achieve the fundamental
\log n
rate.

IX
Conclusion

In this paper, we study the problem of
SR
maximization via Thompson sampling for Gaussian bandits. We investigate the performance of Thompson Sampling in this risk-sensitive context and introduce a novel regret decomposition tailored to the
SR
, which explicitly incorporates information gains regarding both mean and variance. Building on this decomposition, we derive the first information-theoretic upper bound on the regret of the proposed algorithm,
SRTS
. We further establish a corresponding lower bound, demonstrating that
SRTS
is order-optimal. Our theoretical analysis shows that Thompson Sampling achieves logarithmic regret over time, where the constants reflect the intrinsic difficulty of of estimating risk-sensitive differences among arms. Our findings enhance the relevance of Bayesian bandit algorithms for applications involving risk-sensitive objectives.

Lemma 5

(Gaussian Left-Tail Bound)
.

Let
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,1/q)
, with
q>0
. Then for any
a<\mu
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq a)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{s}{2}(\mu-a)^{2}\right).

Proof.

We use the Chernoff method. For any
\lambda<0
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq a)=\mathbb{P}(e^{\lambda X}\geq e^{\lambda a})\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}]}{e^{\lambda a}}\quad\text{(by Markov's inequality)}.

Now compute the moment-generating function (MGF) of
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,1/s)
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}]=\exp\left(\lambda\mu+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2s}\right).

So,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq a)\leq\exp\left(\lambda(\mu-a)+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2s}\right).

To tighten the bound, choose
\lambda<0
that minimizes the exponent. The minimizing value is:

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}=-s(\mu-a).

Substituting this in, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq a)\leq\exp\left(-s(\mu-a)^{2}+\frac{s^{2}(\mu-a)^{2}}{2s}\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{s}{2}(\mu-a)^{2}\right).

∎

Lemma 6

(Gamma Left-Tail Bound)
.

Let
X\sim\Gamma(\alpha,\beta)
, where
\alpha>0
is the shape parameter,
\beta>0
is scale parameter. Then for any
a<\alpha\beta
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq a)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(\alpha\beta-a)^{2}}{4\alpha\beta^{2}}\right).

Proof.

We use the Chernoff method with the Laplace transform of the Gamma distribution. For any
\lambda>0
, by Markov’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq a)=\mathbb{P}(e^{-\lambda X}\geq e^{-\lambda a})\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}[e^{-\lambda X}]}{e^{-\lambda a}}=e^{\lambda a}\cdot(1+\lambda\beta)^{-\alpha},

where we used the Laplace transform of the Gamma distribution:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[e^{-\lambda X}]=(1+\lambda\beta)^{-\alpha},\quad\text{valid for }\lambda>0.

Define the exponent:

\displaystyle f(\lambda):=\lambda a-\alpha\log(1+\lambda\beta).

To minimize the bound, find the optimal
\lambda^{\ast}>0
minimizing
f(\lambda)
. Differentiate:

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(\lambda)=a-\frac{\alpha\beta}{1+\lambda\beta},\quad f^{\prime\prime}(\lambda)=\frac{\alpha\beta^{2}}{(1+\lambda\beta)^{2}}>0.

Setting derivative to zero:

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha\beta}{1+\lambda^{\ast}\beta}\Rightarrow 1+\lambda^{\ast}\beta=\frac{\alpha\beta}{a}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\lambda^{\ast}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\beta}\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{a}-1\right)=\frac{\alpha}{a}-\frac{1}{\beta}.

Plug
\lambda^{\ast}
back into
f(\lambda)
:

\displaystyle f(\lambda^{\ast})=\lambda^{\ast}a-\alpha\ln(1+\lambda^{\ast}\beta)=\alpha-\frac{a}{\beta}-\alpha\ln{\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{a}\right)}.

So the bound becomes:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq a)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(\alpha-\frac{a}{\beta}-\alpha\ln{\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{a}\right)}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\alpha\left(\frac{a}{\alpha\beta}+\ln{\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{a}\right)}-1\right)\right)

Let
x=\frac{\alpha\beta}{a}
, then we use the inequality
\frac{1}{x}+\log{x}-1\geq\frac{(x-1)^{2}}{2x}
for
x>1
, thus the bound results in:

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\alpha\left(\frac{a}{\alpha\beta}+\log{\left(\frac{\alpha\beta}{a}\right)}-1\right)\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(\alpha\beta-a)^{2}}{2a\beta}\right)

Thus tail upper bound on Gamma distribution is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq a)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(\alpha\beta-a)^{2}}{2a\beta}\right)\quad\text{in Shape-Scale form},

or

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X\leq a)\leq\exp\left(-\beta\frac{\left(\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-a\right)^{2}}{2a}\right)\quad\text{in Shape-Rate form}.

∎

For
\alpha=\frac{q}{2}
,
\beta=\frac{2}{q\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}_{1}^{2}}
and
a=\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\varepsilon}
we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\tau_{1,t}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{1}^{2}+\varepsilon}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{q\left(\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}_{1}^{2}-(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\varepsilon)\right)^{2}}{4\accentset{\rule{3.27773pt}{0.5pt}}{\sigma}_{1}^{2}(\sigma_{1}^{2}+\varepsilon)}\right).

Lemma 7

(Two-Sided Tail Bounds for Non-Standard Gaussian)
.

Let
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu,\sigma^{2})
be a Gaussian random variable with mean
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and variance
\sigma^{2}>0
. For any
x>\mu
, the tail probability
P(X>x)
satisfies the following inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}{\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{2}+4}}\leq\mathbb{P}(X>x)\leq\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}{\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{2}+\frac{8}{\pi}}}.

Proof.

We derive the bounds by standardizing
X
and applying known inequalities for the standard normal distribution. Let
Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. The tail probability can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(X>x)=\mathbb{P}\left(Z>t\right)=\Phi^{c}\left(t\right),

where
t=\frac{t-\mu}{\sigma}
and
\Phi^{c}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-x^{2}/2}\,dx
is the CCDF of the standard normal distribution. From
[
40
]
the following tight two-sided inequality holds for
\Phi^{c}(t)
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t+\sqrt{t^{2}+4}}<\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{2}}\exp\left(\frac{t^{2}}{2}\right)\Phi^{c}(t)<\frac{1}{t+\sqrt{t^{2}+\frac{8}{\pi}}}.

Substitute
t
with
\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}{\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{2}+4}}\leq\Phi^{c}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)\leq\frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right)}{\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}+\sqrt{\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^{2}+\frac{8}{\pi}}}.

∎

The original inequality for
\Phi^{c}(t)
is known to be sharp for all
t>0
. Since the transformation
t\mapsto\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}
is bijective for
x>\mu
, the bounds remain sharp for
P(X>x)
.

Lemma 8

(Two-sided Mills-type bounds for the Gamma distribution)
.

Let
X\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)
with shape
\alpha>1
and rate
\beta>0
. For any
x>\frac{\alpha-1}{\beta}
integral
\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta t}\,{\rm d}t
satisfies the two-sided bounds

\displaystyle\frac{x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}}{\beta}\leq\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta t}\,{\rm d}t\leq\frac{x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}}{\beta-\frac{\alpha-1}{x}}.

Consequently, the probability obeys

\frac{\beta^{\alpha-1}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\leq\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\leq\frac{\beta^{\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\!\left(\beta-\dfrac{\alpha-1}{x}\right)}.

(38)

Proof.

Define
\phi(t)=t^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta t}
for
t>0
. Then

\displaystyle\phi^{\prime}(t)=-\Big(\beta-\frac{\alpha-1}{t}\Big)\,\phi(t).

Integrating this identity over
[x,\infty)
yields the representation

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}\phi(t)\,dt=\int_{x}^{\infty}-\frac{\phi^{\prime}(t)}{\beta-\frac{\alpha-1}{t}}\,{\rm d}t.

For
t\geq x
the denominator satisfies

\displaystyle\beta-\frac{\alpha-1}{t}\geq\beta-\frac{\alpha-1}{x}>0,

This gives the upper bound

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}\phi(t)\,{\rm d}t\leq\frac{1}{\beta-\frac{\alpha-1}{x}}\int_{x}^{\infty}-\phi^{\prime}(t)\,{\rm d}t=\frac{\phi(x)}{\beta-\frac{\alpha-1}{x}}.

(39)

For the lower bound note that for
t\geq x
and
\alpha\geq 1
we also have
\beta-\frac{\alpha-1}{t}\leq\beta
, hence
\frac{1}{\beta-\frac{\alpha-1}{t}}\geq\frac{1}{\beta}
. Thus

\displaystyle-\frac{\phi^{\prime}(t)}{\beta-\frac{\alpha-1}{t}}\geq-\frac{\phi^{\prime}(t)}{\beta},

and gives the lower bound as

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}\phi(t)\,{\rm d}t\geq\frac{1}{\beta}\int_{x}^{\infty}-\phi^{\prime}(t)\,{\rm d}t=\frac{\phi(x)}{\beta}.

(40)

Multiplying both sides of (
39
) and (
40
) by the normalizing factor
\beta^{\alpha}/\Gamma(\alpha)
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\beta^{\alpha-1}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\leq\mathbb{P}(X\geq x)\leq\frac{\beta^{\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}}{\Gamma(\alpha)\!\left(\beta-\dfrac{\alpha-1}{x}\right)}.

∎

Remark 1

(Asymptotics and tightness)
.

As
x\to\infty
the prefactor
\dfrac{1}{\beta-\frac{\alpha-1}{x}}=\dfrac{1}{\beta}\big(1+O(1/x)\big)
,
so the two-sided bounds in (
39
) and (
40
) are of the same asymptotic order:

\displaystyle\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta t}\,{\rm d}t\sim\frac{x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x}}{\beta},\qquad x\to\infty,

and hence the bounds (
38
) are asymptotically tight (the multiplicative gap tends to
1
).
