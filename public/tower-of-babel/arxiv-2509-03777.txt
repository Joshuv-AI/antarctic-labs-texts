Quadrature Domains and the Faber Transform
Andrew J. Graven, Nikolai G. Makarov

Abstract
We present a framework for reconstructing any simply connected, bounded or unbounded, quadrature domain Ω \Omega from its quadrature function h h . Using the Faber transform, we derive formulae directly relating h h to the Riemann map for Ω \Omega . Through this approach, we obtain a complete classification of one point quadrature domains with complex charge. We proceed to develop a theory of weighted quadrature domains with respect to weights of the form ρ a ​ ( w ) = | w | 2 ​ ( a − 1 ) \rho_{a}(w)=|w|^{2(a-1)} when a > 0 a>0 (“power-weighted” quadrature domains) and the limiting case of when a = 0 a=0 (“log-weighted” quadrature domains). Furthermore, we obtain Faber transform formulae for reconstructing weighted quadrature domains from their respective quadrature functions. Several examples are presented throughout to illustrate this approach both in the simply connected setting and in the presence of rotational symmetry.

Introduction

The purpose of this article is to construct and analyze certain classes of quadrature domains (QDs) using the Faber transform. In particular, we will provide explicit representations for the Riemann map associated to simply connected quadrature domains and weighted quadrature domains. We will also cover several applications of this approach to questions of the existence and uniqueness for QDs and weighted QDs.

Quadrature domains are an important class of planar domains, characterized by their admission of exact quadrature identities. They arise naturally across a wide array of topics in analysis and mathematical physics, including moment problems, approximation theory, random matrix theory, potential theory, Hele-Shaw flow, the Schwarz reflection, and conformal dynamics

We shall begin with what is undoubtedly the most well-known example of a quadrature domain: the disk. Recall the mean value property for functions analytic in the disk asserts that

\int_{\mathbb{D}_{r}(w_{0})}fdA=r^{2}f(w_{0}),

1.1
Bounded Quadrature Domains

We refer to a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
as a classical bounded quadrature domain with respect to the analytic test class
\mathcal{F}
if there exists a finite collection of
c_{k}\in\mathbb{C}
,
a_{k}\in\Omega
, and
m_{k}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
for which

\int_{\Omega}fdA=\sum_{k}c_{k}f^{(m_{k})}(a_{k})

(1.2)

for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
. This is equivalent, via the residue theorem, to the existence of
h\in\text{Rat}(\Omega)
for which

\int_{\Omega}fdA=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw

(1.3)

for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
. In particular, taking
h(w)=\sum_{k}\frac{c_{k}m_{k}!}{(w-a_{k})^{m_{k}+1}}
reproduces Equation
1.2
. Such an

is referred to as a
quadrature function
for
\Omega
.
h
may or may not be unique when it exists - this depends crucially on the choice of test class. For example,
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
then
\Omega
has a unique quadrature function
h\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
, provided that one exists. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 1.0.1

(Bounded quadrature domain)
.

A bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{C}
, equal to the interior of its closure, is called a
bounded quadrature domain
(BQD) if there exists an
h\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
such that Equation
1.3
holds for all
f\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
. This is denoted by
\Omega\in\text{QD}(h)
.

The simplest non-trivial example of a bounded QD is the cardioid
\Omega=\left\{z+\frac{z^{2}}{2}:z\in\mathbb{D}\right\}
, which admits a quadrature identity involving both
f
and its first derivative:

\int_{\Omega}fdA=\dfrac{3}{2}f(0)+\dfrac{1}{2}f^{\prime}(0).

(1.4)

The formula just below Equation
1.3
combined with the quadrature identity (

\Omega\in\text{QD}\left(h\right)
for

1.2
Unbounded Quadrature Domains

Parallel to the theory of bounded quadrature domains, is that of unbounded quadrature domains. It turns out that Equation
1.3
, where the area integral is understood in the sense of its
Cauchy principal value
:

\int_{\Omega}fdA:=\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{\Omega\cap\mathbb{D}_{r}}fdA,

is a much more natural starting point in this case. A QI in the sense of Equation
1.2
still exists, but includes a linear combination of the Laurent coefficients of
f
at
\infty
.

More care is required when it comes to the choice of test class
\mathcal{F}
for unbounded quadrature domains. On the one hand,
\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
cannot be used because it isn’t integrable; on the other hand, the integrable test class
L_{a}^{1}(\Omega)=L^{1}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
is too small to ensure uniqueness of the quadrature function: If
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
, then
f\in O(w^{-3})
about
\infty
. Hence if
h\in\text{Rat}(\Omega)
is a quadrature function for
\Omega
, then so is
h+\alpha
for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
because

\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)(h(w)+\alpha)dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw+\alpha\underset{\infty}{\text{Res}}(f)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw.

We can recover uniqueness in this sense, however, if we consider the intermediate test class
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
(
L^{1}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{A}(\Omega)\subset\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)\subset\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
). In particular, if
\Omega
is an unbounded domain for which there exists an
h\in\text{Rat}(\Omega)
satisfying Equation
1.3
for all
f\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
, then
h
is unique (apply the quadrature identity to the Cauchy kernel
\xi\mapsto(\xi-w)^{-1}
). This motivates the following definition.

Definition 1.0.2

(Unbounded quadrature domain)
.

An unbounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
, equal to the interior of its closure, is called an
unbounded quadrature domain
(UQD) if there exists an
h\in\text{Rat}(\Omega)
such that Equation
1.3
holds for all
f\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
. This is denoted by
\Omega\in\text{QD}(h)
.

Example 1.1

(The ellipse)
.

If
\Omega\in\text{QD}\left(\alpha w\right)
is unbounded and simply connected and
|\alpha|<1

\varphi(z)=c(z+\overline{\alpha}z^{-1})
is a Riemann map for
\Omega
(where

is the conformal radius of
\Omega

\Omega
is the complement of an ellipse centered at the origin of eccentricity
\frac{2\sqrt{|\alpha|}}{|\alpha|+1}

In particular, if

1.3
Quadrature Domains

We refer to UQDs and BQDs collectively as quadrature domains (QDs), characterized by the existence of a quadrature function. In particular,

Definition 1.0.3

(Quadrature domain)
.

A domain
\Omega\subseteq\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is called a
quadrature domain
if there exists a rational function
h
such that
\Omega\in\text{QD}(h)
. This is denoted by
\Omega\in\text{QD}
. We furthermore denote by
d_{\Omega}:=\deg(h)
the
order
of
\Omega
.

As previously discussed, the uniqueness of the quadrature function associated to a given quadrature domain is well established. However, the inverse problem of existence and uniqueness for quadrature domains associated to a given quadrature function isn’t so straightforward. Non-uniqueness has been exhibited for multiply connected regions: consider the disk and the complement of an annulus with the same inner radius (Varchenko & Etingof (
[
5
]
, §2.3) provide another example). On the other hand, it is conjectured that simply connected QDs are indeed uniquely associated to their quadrature function (modulo conformal radius). Aharonov & Shapiro (1976) established that disks are the unique one point QDs among all bounded finitely connected domains
[
9
]
. They established in the same paper a similar result for the cardioid. See
[
10
]
, Theorem 1.1, for a discussion of additional known uniqueness results.

1.3.1
The Schwarz Function

A useful tool for approaching these questions arises from a characteristic property of quadrature domains: they admit a
Schwarz function

S
. In particular, a domain
\Omega
is a QD if and only if there exists a function
S\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
such that
S(w)\dot{=}\overline{w}
.
S
is unique when it exists and admits a unique representation is in terms of the quadrature function and the
Cauchy transform
of
\Omega_{\ast}
,
C^{\Omega_{\ast}}
. The Schwarz function associated to a quadrature domain
\Omega\in\text{QD}(h)
can be expressed as
[
6
]

S(w)=C^{\Omega_{\ast}}(w)+h(w)\dot{=}\overline{w}.

(1.5)

C^{U}
is defined for measurable
U\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
with compact boundary and is given by

C^{U}(w)=\int_{U}\dfrac{dA(\xi)}{w-\xi}.

(1.6)

A handful of equivalent characterizations of quadrature domains, tied together by the Schwarz function equation
1.5
, are given in Lemma
1.1
.

Lemma 1.1
.

Let
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a domain equal to the interior of its closure. Then the following are equivalent

1.

there exists
h\in\text{Rat}(\Omega)
for which
\Omega\in\text{QD}(h)
,

2.

there exists
h\in\text{Rat}(\Omega)
for which
\left.C^{\Omega}\right|_{\Omega_{\ast}}=h
,

3.

there exists
S\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
for which
S(w)\dot{=}\overline{w}
.

The proof of this equivalence is standard
[
11
,
12
]
.

The statement
\left.C^{\Omega}\right|_{\Omega_{\ast}}=h
has a natural physical interpretation.
\overline{C^{\Omega}}
corresponds to the logarithmic electrostatic field due to a uniform charge distribution on
\Omega
, whereas
\overline{h}
corresponds to the electrostatic field due to a collection of point charges (and multipoles) at the poles of
h
. Thus the above equality tells us that the electrostatic field in
\Omega_{\ast}
due to a uniform charge distribution on
\Omega
is equal to that of a finite collection of point charges (and multipoles) in
\Omega
. Figure
3
illustrates this phenomenon in the case of the cardioid.

1.3.2
Boundary Regularity of QDs

It turns out that the regularity of the boundary of QDs is essentially determined by the associated Schwarz function. This is a direct consequence of the celebrated Sakai regularity theorem (
[
13
]
Theorem 5.2 and
[
12
]
§3.2). Fix a domain
\Omega
and
w_{0}\in\partial\Omega
.
w_{0}
is called a
regular
point if there exists
\epsilon>0
for which
\Omega\cap\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(w_{0})
is a Jordan domain and
\partial\Omega\cap\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(w_{0})
is a simple real analytic arc. A point which is not regular is called a
singular
point.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
S
is a local Schwarz function at a singular point
w_{0}
, then
w_{0}
is either a (conformal) cusp, a double point, or a degenerate point.

A function
S:\text{Cl}(\Omega)\cap\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(w_{0})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is
local Schwarz function
at
w_{0}\in\partial\Omega
if
S\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega\cap\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(w_{0}))
and
S(w)=\overline{w}
on
\partial\Omega\cap\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(w_{0})
. A
degenerate point
is a point
w_{0}
for which
\Omega^{c}\cap\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}(w_{0})
is a proper subset of a real analytic curve. See
[
12
]
, §3.2 for the definitions of cusps and double points.

As we require quadrature domains to equal to the interior of their closure, we may neglect the degenerate case. Combining this with the fact that a Schwarz function restricts to a local Schwarz function about every boundary point, we obtain the following regularity theorem for QDs,

Theorem 1.3
.

If
\Omega
is a quadrature domain then
\partial\Omega
has finitely many singular points, each of which is either a cusp or a double point.

1.4
The Inverse and Direct Problems for Simply Connected QDs

A central problem in the theory of quadrature domains is that of reconstructing a QD given its quadrature function. This is referred to as the
inverse problem
for quadrature domains. Complementary to this is the
direct problem
, which is concerned with determining the quadrature function associated to a given quadrature domain.

For simply connected domains, these problems may be reduced to relating the quadrature function of the domain and its Riemann map. A classic result in the theory of quadrature domains provides a characterization of simply connected QDs in terms of the associated Riemann map.

Theorem 1.4

(
[
9
]
)
.

A simply connected domain is a quadrature domain iff it has a rational Riemann map.

Hence, for simply connected QDs, the inverse and direct problems reduce to the determination of the relationship between the finitely many poles and coefficients of the quadrature function and the finitely many poles and coefficients of the Riemann map. Formulae relating the Riemann map of a given simply connected QD with its quadrature identity have appeared in various forms going as far back as Davis
[
6
]
(1974) in the case of bounded classical QDs. More recently, Ameur, Helmer, and Tellander (2021) apply a form of this identity (
[
10
]
Equation 2), referred to as the “master formula”, to the uniqueness problems for bounded classical QDs. This “master formula” represents the Riemann map in terms of the quadrature measure
\mu=\overline{\partial}h
:

\varphi(z)=\overline{\varphi_{\ast}\mu}\left(\dfrac{z}{\overline{\varphi^{\prime}(\lambda)}(1-z\overline{\lambda})}\right),

(1.7)

where
\varphi_{\ast}\mu
is the pushforward measure.

Chang
[
14
]
considers an essentially equivalent formula in terms of the
Faber transform
, an isomorphism of analytic functions on the disk with those on another bounded simply connected domain. We provide generalizations of this approach to unbounded domains (
1.6
) and some classes of weighted domains (§
3
, §
4
). After fixing some conventions and notation regarding the Riemann map, we will proceed with a brief proof of Theorem
1.4
, followed by an exposition of the method for classical BQDs and UQDs.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded simply connected domain. The Riemann mapping theorem asserts that
\Omega
is conformally equivalent to the disk, and that the associated conformal map is unique up to a rotation and recentering. In particular, for each
w_{0}\in\Omega
there exists a unique biholomorphism
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega
for which
\varphi(0)=w_{0}
and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)>0
, so that

\varphi(z)=w_{0}+\varphi^{\prime}(0)z+O(z^{2})

We refer to
\varphi
as
the
Riemann map associated to
\Omega
and
w_{0}
. On the other hand, when
\Omega
is unbounded, there exists a unique biholomorphism
\varphi:\mathbb{D}_{\ast}\rightarrow\Omega
for which
\varphi(\infty)=\infty
and
\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)>0
, so that

\varphi(z)=cz+f_{0}+\left<z^{-1}\right>.

In this case, too, we refer to
\varphi
as
the
Riemann map associated to
\Omega
.

1.5
Solving the Inverse and Direct Problems via the Faber Transform

A central goal of this paper is to demonstrate a method of reconstructing the Riemann map associated to a simply connected QD from its quadrature function and vice-versa (i.e. to solve the inverse and direct problems for simply connected QDs). Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
provide characterizations of this method for bounded and unbounded domains respectively. We denote by
\Phi_{\varphi}
the
Faber transform
associated to the Riemann map
\varphi
.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded and simply connected domain with quadrature function
h\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
and Riemann map
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega
. In this case,

h=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\varphi^{\#}\right]\right)

(1.8)

and

\varphi=\varphi(0)+\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}(h)^{\#}.

(1.9)

In particular, Equations
1.8
and
1.9
respectively solve the direct and inverse problems for BQDs. Note that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\varphi^{\#}=\varphi^{\#}-\overline{\varphi(0)}
, so
h=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\varphi^{\#}-\overline{\varphi(0)}\right)
is an equivalent form of Equation
1.8
. Theorem
1.6
covers the analogous result for UQDs.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
\Omega
be an unbounded and simply connected domain with quadrature function
h\in\text{Rat}(\Omega)
and Riemann map
\varphi:\mathbb{D}_{\ast}\rightarrow\Omega
. In this case,

h=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\varphi^{\#}\right]\right)

(1.10)

and

\varphi(z)=cz+\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}(h)^{\#}(z).

(1.11)

To make sense of these formulae, a formal definition of the Faber transform is needed. This, along with a discussion of the various properties of the Faber transform is the topic of the next section.

1.5.1
The Faber Transform

Recall that if
\Omega
is a bounded simply connected domain and
w_{0}\in\Omega
, then by “the Riemann map associated to
\Omega
and
w_{0}
” we refer to the unique biholomorphism
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega
for which
\varphi^{\prime}(0)>0
and
\varphi(0)=w_{0}
. We furthermore denote by
\psi
the inverse of
\varphi
.

If
\Omega
is a domain with a rectifiable boundary on which a function
f
integrable, then we denote by
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}\left[f\right]
the
Cauchy projection
of
f
onto
\Omega
,

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}\left[f\right](w):=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{f(\xi)}{\xi-w}d\xi.

(1.12)

It follows from the residue theorem that if
f\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega_{\ast})
, then
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}\left[f\right]
is a rational function with the same poles as
f
. The Cauchy projection,
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega}f
, is also referred to as the “analytic part” of
f
in
\Omega
.

Definition 1.6.1

(Interior Faber transform)
.

The
interior Faber transform
associated to
\Omega
and
\varphi
(as above) is an isomorphism
\Phi_{\varphi}:\mathcal{A}_{0}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega_{\ast})
given by

\Phi_{\varphi}(f)(w):=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[f\circ\psi\right](w)=\oint_{\partial\Omega_{\ast}}\dfrac{f\circ\psi(\xi)}{\xi-w}d\xi.

(1.13)

If
f\in C^{0}(\partial\mathbb{D})
then the Sokhotski-Plemelj theorem implies
f=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[f\right]+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[f\right]
, so

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[f\circ\psi\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[f\right]\circ\psi+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[f\right]\circ\psi\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[f\right]\circ\psi\right]=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}f\right).

(1.14)

The analogous result holds for the inverse transform,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[f\circ\varphi\right]=\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}f\right)
, under the additional assumption that
\partial\Omega
is piecewise
C^{1}
.

If the domain under consideration is unbounded, then it has an associated
exterior Faber transform
. Recall that if
\Omega
is an unbounded simply connected domain, then by “the Riemann map associated to
\Omega
” we refer to the unique biholomorphism
\varphi:\mathbb{D}_{\ast}\rightarrow\Omega
for which
\varphi(\infty)=\infty
and
\varphi^{\prime}(\infty)=c>0
, where
c>0
is the conformal radius of
\Omega
.

Definition 1.6.2

(Exterior Faber transform)
.

The exterior Faber transform associated to
\Omega
and
\varphi
(as above) is an isomorphism
\Phi_{\varphi}:\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})\rightarrow\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{\ast})
defined precisely as in Equation
1.13
. The inverse transform is given by
\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}(f)=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[f\circ\varphi\right]
.

Moreover note that because
\partial\mathbb{D}
is
C^{1}
, the Sokhotski-Plemelj theorem implies that for each
f\in C^{0}(\partial\mathbb{D})
,

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[f\circ\psi\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[f\right]\circ\psi+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[f\right]\circ\psi\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[f\right]\circ\psi\right]=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}f\right).

(1.15)

The analogous result holds for the inverse transform,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[f\circ\varphi\right]=\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}f\right)
, under the additional assumption that
\partial\Omega
is piecewise
C^{1}
.

Note that, in contrast to the interior case,
\mathbb{C}[z]\subseteq\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
so one can consider the Faber transform of polynomials. The
Faber polynomials
associated to
\Omega
(or
\varphi
) are the polynomial basis

We now take a brief detour into logarithmic potential theory. We will see that solutions to the associated “obstacle problem” are, in some sense, dual to quadrature domains. In our case, if

Power-Weighted Quadrature Domains

We will begin our study of weighted quadrature domains by discussing the instructive example of
power-weighted quadrature domains
(PQDs). Informally, a PQD is a domain
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
if it admits a quadrature identity in which the integral over the domain is taken with respect to the weight
\rho_{a}(w)=|w|^{2(a-1)}
for some
a>0
. The choice of
\rho_{a}
is motivated by the correspondence between PQDs and local droplets of potentials of the form

Q(w)=\frac{|w|^{2a}}{a^{2}}-2\text{Re}(H(w)).

The exact nature of this correspondence is detailed in Lemmas
3.1
,
3.18
, and
3.19
. Before a deeper study of PQDs, we begin with a brief discussion of weighted quadrature domains more generally.

3.1
Weighted Quadrature Domains

As the name suggests, a
\rho-

\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
satisfying a quadrature identity in which the area integral is taken with respect to the weight
\rho
. Consider
\rho(w)=|w|^{4}=\frac{1}{4}\Delta\frac{|w|^{6}}{9}
as a first example.

Example 3.1
.

If

|w^{3}-1|^{2}\dot{=}c^{6}

\overline{w}^{3}\dot{=}\frac{c^{6}}{w^{3}-1}+1

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(w)|w|^{4}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\frac{w^{2}}{3}\overline{w}^{3}dw

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\frac{1}{3}w^{2}\left(\frac{c^{6}}{w^{3}-1}+1\right)dw

Applying the residue theorem and the fact that the third roots of unity are not in
\Omega
, we find

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(w)|w|^{4}dA(w)

\displaystyle=-\frac{f_{3}}{3},

where

We observe in Figure

As for classical quadrature domains, the existence of a

is equivalent to the existence of a rational function

(e.g

Definition 3.0.1

(Weighted quadrature domain)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a domain equal to the interior of its closure and
\rho
a weight function on
\Omega
.
\Omega
is a
\rho-
weighted quadrature domain with respect to the
\rho-
integrable analytic test class
\mathcal{F}
iff there exists a rational function
h
for which

\int_{\Omega}f\rho dA=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw

(3.1)

holds for all
f\in\mathcal{F}
. This is denoted by
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{\rho}(h;\mathcal{F})
.

While this definition is very general, one can still recover analogs of many of the results for classical QDs - at least in certain special cases. The following lemma provides an analogue of the Schwarz function equation (
1.5
) for a broad class of radially symmetric weights.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{\rho}(h;\mathcal{F})
be a bounded domain where
\rho=\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial w\partial\overline{w}}=\frac{1}{4}\Delta F
for some real valued
F\in C^{2}(\text{Cl}(\Omega))
, radially symmetric with respect to the origin. If
\mathcal{A}(\Omega)\subseteq\mathcal{F}
, then there exists
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
such that

h(w)\dot{=}\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial w}+G(w).

(3.2)

The same holds when
\Omega
is unbounded, with each
\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
above replaced by
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Let
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{\rho}(h;\mathcal{F})
be a bounded domain with
\rho=\frac{1}{4}\Delta F
, where
F\in C^{2}(\text{Cl}(\Omega))
is radially symmetric with respect to the origin.
By assumption if
f\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
then
f\in\mathcal{F}
, so applying the quadrature identity and Green’s theorem, we obtain

\displaystyle\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\left(h(w)-\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial w}\right)dw

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw-\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial w}dw

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}f\rho dA-\int_{\Omega}f\rho dA=0

Therefore there exists
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
such that
h\dot{=}\frac{\partial F}{\partial w}+G
. The proof in the unbounded case is entirely analogous.
∎

This generalized Schwarz function equation naturally leads to the notion of a
generalized Schwarz function
,
S_{\Omega,F}:=h-G\dot{=}\frac{\partial F}{\partial w}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
. For example if
\Omega
is a bounded domain in
\text{QD}_{|w|^{2(a-1)}}(h;\mathcal{A}(\Omega))
, so that

for some

, then the lemma implies
S_{\Omega,a^{-2}|w|^{2a}}(w):=h(w)-G(w)\dot{=}a^{-1}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}
for some
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
(see Equation
3.3
). In the remainder of this section, we explore various families of PQDs and their properties.

,

,
10
, and
11
). It appears that this phenomenon isn’t unique to PQDs, but is generic among WQDs with a singular weight.

As in the classical case, the choice of test class
\mathcal{F}
is of great importance when it comes to the uniqueness of the quadrature function. For example if
\Omega
were unbounded then for each
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
,

\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\left(\alpha+h(w)\right)dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw

whenever
f\in L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{a})
and
a\geq 1
. That is,
\text{QD}_{\rho_{a}}(h;L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{a}))=\text{QD}_{\rho_{a}}(h+\alpha;L_{a}^{1}(\Omega;\rho_{a}))
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
. This isn’t the case, however, when
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)

because if
w\in\Omega_{\ast}
then
\xi\mapsto(\xi-w)^{-1}\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
, so

\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\alpha+h(\xi)}{\xi-w}d\xi=\alpha+\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{h(\xi)}{\xi-w}d\xi\neq\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{h(\xi)}{\xi-w}d\xi.

Applying Lemma
3.1
with
F(w)=\frac{|w|^{2a}}{a^{2}}
, we find that there exists
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
(
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
when
\Omega
is unbounded) for which

h(w)\dot{=}\dfrac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}+G(w).

(3.3)

Note this implies that when
\Omega
is bounded (resp. unbounded),
h
is uniquely associated to
\Omega
if we restrict to the class
\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
(resp.
\text{Rat}(\Omega)
): If
\Omega
is a PQD with
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
(resp.
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
) two such quadrature functions
h_{1},h_{2}
then

h_{1}(w)-h_{2}(w)\dot{=}\left(\dfrac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}+G_{1}(w)\right)-\left(\dfrac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}+G_{2}(w)\right)=G_{1}(w)-G_{2}(w).

Hence
h_{1}-h_{2}
continues analytically into
\Omega
. So when
\Omega
is bounded,
h_{1}-h_{2}\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{A}(\Omega)=\{0\}
, and when
\Omega
is unbounded,
h_{1}-h_{2}\in\text{Rat}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)=\{0\}
, so
h_{1}=h_{2}
. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 3.1.1

(Power-weighted quadrature domain)
.

Take
a>0
and let
\Omega
be a bounded (resp. unbounded) domain equal to the interior of its closure. We say that
\Omega
is a PQD iff there exists
h\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
(resp.
\text{Rat}(\Omega)
) for which

\int_{\Omega}f\rho_{a}dA=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw

(3.4)

is satisfied for all
f\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
(resp.
A_{0}(\Omega)
). This is denoted by
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(h)
.

In the language of general weighted quadrature domains, this is equivalent to the statement that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{\rho_{a}}(h;\mathcal{A}(\Omega))
with
h\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
when
\Omega
is bounded, and
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{\rho_{a}}(h;\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega))
,
h\in\text{Rat}(\Omega)
when
\Omega
is unbounded. We also denote by
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}
that there exists an
h
for which
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(h)
. There are several equivalent characterizations of PQDs in the spirit of Lemma
1.1
:

Theorem 3.2
.

If
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is a domain equal to the interior of its closure and
a>0
then the following are equivalent

1.

There exists a rational
h
for which
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(h)
.

2.

There exists a
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
(
\mathcal{A}_{0}
when
\Omega
is unbounded) and a rational
h
for which
h(w)\dot{=}\frac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}+G(w)
.

3.

There exists a function
S_{a}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
for which
S_{a}(w)\dot{=}\frac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}
. This is the generalized Schwarz function associated to
\Omega
.

Moreover in this case
h=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[S_{a}\right]
, and
G
is minus the
\rho_{a}-
weighted Cauchy transform of
\Omega^{c}
,

G(w)=\int_{\Omega^{c}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{2(a-1)}}{\xi-w}dA(\xi).

(3.5)

The generalized Schwarz function
S_{a}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
is unique when it exists as a consequence of its equality with
\frac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}
on
\partial\Omega
. We’ll see in the next section that simply connected PQDs admit an additional characterization in terms of their Riemann map.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

\mathbf{(1)\implies(2)}
: This is immediate from Equation
3.3
(via Lemma
3.1
).

\mathbf{(2)\implies(3)}
: Rearranging, we find that
\frac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}\dot{=}h(w)-G(w)=:S_{a}(w)
, which is the generalized Schwarz function for
\Omega
because
h-G\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
. Also,
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}S_{a}=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[h-G\right]=h
.

\mathbf{(3)\implies(1)}
: Finally, if
\Omega
is a bounded domain for which such an
S_{a}
exists, and
f\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}f(w)|w|^{2(a-1)}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\frac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)S_{a}(w)dw

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[S_{a}(w)\right]dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw,

So
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(h)
. The argument in the unbounded case is completely analogous.

Finally, we verify Equation
3.5
: First, if
\Omega
is unbounded then for each
w\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle G(w)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{G(\xi)d\xi}{\xi-w}=-\oint_{\partial\Omega_{\ast}}\dfrac{h(\xi)-\frac{1}{a}\overline{\xi}|\xi|^{2(a-1)}}{\xi-w}d\xi=\oint_{\partial\Omega_{\ast}}\dfrac{\frac{1}{a}\overline{\xi}|\xi|^{2(a-1)}}{\xi-w}d\xi=\int_{\Omega^{c}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{2(a-1)}}{\xi-w}dA(\xi).

The first equality is from Cauchy’s integral formula, the second follows from part (2) of the lemma, the third follows from the fact that
h\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega_{\ast})
, and the final equality is Green’s formula.
On the other hand, if
\Omega
is bounded then
\Omega_{\ast}
is unbounded, so we consider the integral in terms of its Cauchy principal value. In particular, we have by Green’s theorem that for each
w\in\Omega
,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{c}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{2(a-1)}}{\xi-w}dA(\xi)

\displaystyle:=\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{\Omega^{c}\cap\mathbb{D}_{r}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{2(a-1)}}{\xi-w}dA(\xi)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\oint_{\partial(\Omega^{c}\cap\mathbb{D}_{r})}\dfrac{\frac{1}{a}\overline{\xi}|\xi|^{2(a-1)}}{\xi-w}d\xi

Integrating over each component of the boundary separately, and applying the fact that
\frac{1}{a}\overline{\xi}|\xi|^{2(a-1)}=h(\xi)-G(\xi)
on
\partial\Omega
, we find

\int_{\Omega^{c}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{2(a-1)}}{\xi-w}dA(\xi)=\oint_{\partial\Omega_{\ast}}\dfrac{h(\xi)-G(\xi)}{\xi-w}d\xi+\lim_{r\to\infty}\dfrac{r^{2a}}{a}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{r}}\dfrac{\xi^{-1}}{\xi-w}d\xi.

Applying Cauchy’s theorem and that
h\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
, we obtain

\int_{\Omega^{c}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{2(a-1)}}{\xi-w}dA(\xi)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{G(\xi)}{\xi-w}d\xi+\lim_{r\to\infty}\dfrac{r^{2a}}{a}\cdot 0=G(w).

∎

3.3
Boundary Regularity of PQDs

Recall that the boundary regularity of
\Omega\in\text{QD}
(Theorem
1.3
) was a direct consequence of the existence of an associated Schwarz function and Sakai’s regularity theorem
1.2
, which characterizes the types of singularities which can occur on a curve admitting a local Schwarz function. Unfortunately, Sakai’s result can’t be applied directly to PQDs, as they don’t generally admit a Schwarz function. However, a recent result of Vardakis and Volberg
[
21
]
(Corollary 2.5) sufficiently generalizes Sakai’s result for our purposes.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain,
p
a polynomial,
F
a function analytic in a neighborhood of
\text{Cl}(\Omega)
, and
T\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
. Suppose that for all
w\in\partial\Omega
, we have

T(w)=p(\overline{w})F(w).

Then for each
w_{0}\in\partial\Omega
such that
p^{\prime}(\overline{w_{0}})\neq 0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that
p^{-1}\left(\frac{T}{F}\right)
is a local Schwarz function at
w_{0}
.

In particular, if
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(h)
is a bounded PQD, Theorem
3.2
tells us that there exists a
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
such that
a(h(w)-G(w))=\overline{w}^{a}w^{a-1}
. If we let
r(w)=\prod_{k}(w-a_{k})^{n_{k}}
(where the
a_{k}
are the poles of
h
and
n_{k}
their respective orders), then

p(\overline{w})F(w):=\overline{w}^{a}(w^{a-1}r(w))\dot{=}ar(w)(h(w)-G(w))=:T(w),

where
T
is analytic in
\Omega
,
F(w):=w^{a-1}r(w)
is analytic in a neighborhood of
\text{Cl}(\Omega)
, and
p(w)=w^{a-1}
is a polynomial. This choice of
T
,
p
, and
F
satisfy the conditions of Lemma
3.3
, and we find that
\Omega
admits a local Schwarz function at every point
w_{0}\in\partial\Omega
, except possibly at
w_{0}=0
. We conclude that if
\Omega
is a bounded PQD then for each
\epsilon>0
,
\partial\Omega\setminus\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}
has finitely many singular points, each of which is either a cusp or a double point.
On the other hand, if
\Omega
is an unbounded PQD, then choose
w_{1}\notin\text{Cl}(\Omega)
and consider the bounded domain
\widetilde{\Omega}=(\Omega-w_{1})^{-1}
, so
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
satisfies

\dfrac{1}{h((w+w_{1})^{-1})-G((w+w_{1})^{-1})}=a\overline{(w+w_{1})}^{a}(w+w_{1})^{a-1}

for some
G\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
. If we let
r(w)=\prod_{k}(w-a_{k})^{n_{k}}
(where the
a_{k}
are the poles of the left hand side and
n_{k}
their respective orders), then

p(\overline{w})F(w):=a(\overline{w}+\overline{w_{1}})^{a}((w+w_{1})^{a-1}r(w))\dot{=}\dfrac{r(w)}{h((w+w_{1})^{-1})-G((w+w_{1})^{-1})}=:T(w),

where
T
is analytic in
\widetilde{\Omega}
,
F(w):=((w+w_{1})^{a-1}r(w))
is analytic in a neighborhood of
\text{Cl}(\widetilde{\Omega})
, and
p(w)=a(w+\overline{w_{1}})^{a}
is a polynomial. This choice of
T
,
p
, and
F
satisfy the conditions of Lemma
3.3
, and we find that
\widetilde{\Omega}
admits a local Schwarz function at every point of
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
, except possibly at
-w_{1}
. The map
w\mapsto(w-w_{1})^{-1}
is univalent in a neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
, so this implies that
\Omega
admits a local Schwarz function at every point of
\partial\Omega
, except possibly at
0
. In summary,

Lemma 3.4
.

If
\Omega
is a PQD then for each
\epsilon>0
,
\partial\Omega\setminus\mathbb{D}_{\epsilon}
has finitely many singular points, each of which is either a cusp or a double point.

To constrain the behavior at the origin, we require an extra ingredient, that
(\partial\Omega)^{a}
is a subset of an algebraic curve:

Theorem 3.5
.

If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}
for some
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, there exists a non-constant polynomial
p
, irreducible over
\mathbb{C}
, with real coefficients such that
p(x,y)=0
for all
x+iy\in\partial\Omega^{a}
. (c.f.
[
9
]
, Theorem 3). In particular,
\partial\Omega
is a subset of the
a
th root of an algebraic curve.

Proof of Theorem
3.5
.

By Theorem
3.2
,
\Omega
admits a generalized Schwarz function,
S_{a}
which is analytic in
\Omega
except at finitely many poles such that
T(w):=\frac{S_{a}(w)}{\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}}\dot{=}\overline{w}^{a}
. Thus

f(w):=\frac{1}{2}(w^{a}+T(w)),\text{ and }g(w):=\frac{1}{2i}(w^{a}-T(w))

are analytic except at finitely many poles, and admit continuous real extensions to
\partial\Omega
,
f(w)\dot{=}\text{Re}(w^{a})
,
g(w)\dot{=}\text{Im}(w^{a})
. Now suppose
\Omega
is bounded. We apply Theorem 2 of
[
9
]
:

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{C}
be a bounded domain and suppose
f
and
g
are holomorphic in
\Omega
except at finitely many poles. Further suppose that
f
and
g
admit real, continuous, extensions to
\partial\Omega
. Then there exists a non-trivial polynomial
p(x,y)
, irreducible over
\mathbb{C}
, with real coefficients such that
p(f(z),g(z))=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

Therefore there exists a real non-constant,
\mathbb{C}
-irreducible polynomial
p
such that
p(f(w),g(w))=0
on
\partial\Omega
. Thus,
p(\text{Re}(w^{a}),\text{Im}(w^{a}))=0
on
\partial\Omega
. This is equivalent to
p(x,y)=0
for all
x+iy\in\partial\Omega^{a}
. The argument is similar, but slightly more delicate in the unbounded case; see the proof of Theorem 3 in
[
9
]
.
∎

Theorem
3.5
straightforwardly implies that
\partial\Omega
has finitely many singular points, so we conclude

Theorem 3.6
.

If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}
for some
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
then
\partial\Omega
has finitely many singular points, where each non-zero singular point is either a cusp or a double point.

3.4
Simply Connected Power-Weighted Quadrature Domains with a=2

As a first step towards generalizing Theorems
1.5
and
1.6

and

. We further divide our discussion into, first, the case of unbounded PQDs and, second, the case of bounded PQDs.

3.4.1
Unbounded PQDs

Suppose that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{2}
is a simply connected unbounded PQD and quadrature function
h
and Riemann map
\varphi:\mathbb{D}_{\ast}\rightarrow\Omega
. Further suppose that
0\notin\text{Cl}(\Omega)
. Composing
\varphi
with the Schwarz function equation for PQDs,
h(w)\dot{=}\frac{1}{2}w\overline{w}^{2}+G(w)
(Theorem
3.2
), we obtain

\overline{\varphi}^{2}\dot{=}\left(\frac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{2}w}\right)\circ\varphi

Expanding
\varphi
in series,
\varphi(z)=cz+f_{0}+\left<z^{-1}\right>
, we see that

\displaystyle\varphi^{2}(z)

\displaystyle=c^{2}z^{2}+2cf_{0}z+(f_{0}^{2}+2cf_{1})+\left<z^{-1}\right>

\displaystyle=\mathring{W_{2}}(z)+(f_{0}^{2}+2cf_{1})+\left<z^{-1}\right>

where
\mathring{W_{2}}(z):=W_{2}(z)-W_{2}(0)
and
W_{2}
is the second inverse Faber polynomial (Equation
1.18
) so, in the disk,
(\varphi^{2})^{\#}=\mathring{W_{2}}^{\#}+(f_{0}^{2}+2cf_{1})+\left<z^{-1}\right>
. Therefore
(\varphi^{2})^{\#}-\mathring{W_{2}}^{\#}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
which implies

(\varphi^{2})^{\#}-\mathring{W_{2}}^{\#}=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[(\varphi^{2})^{\#}-\mathring{W_{2}}^{\#}\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[(\varphi^{2})^{\#}\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\overline{\varphi}^{2}\right],

where the last line follows from the fact that
\overline{\varphi}\dot{=}\varphi^{\#}
. Rearranging and substituting the Schwarz function equation, we obtain

\displaystyle(\varphi^{2})^{\#}

\displaystyle=\mathring{W_{2}}^{\#}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\overline{\varphi}^{2}\right]=\mathring{W_{2}}^{\#}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\left(\frac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{2}w}\right)\circ\varphi\right].

Equation
1.15
(using the boundary regularity from Theorem
3.6
) implies

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\left(\frac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{2}w}\right)\circ\varphi\right]=\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\frac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{2}w}\right]\right)=\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(2\frac{h(w)-h(0)}{w}\right).

Hence, we obtain an implicit solution to the inverse problem,

\varphi^{2}=\mathring{W_{2}}+2\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\dfrac{h(w)-h(0)}{w}\right)^{\#}.

(3.6)

Moreover, as the Faber transform of a rational function is rational, we conclude that

Corollary 3.6.1
.

If
0\notin\Omega
is a simply connected unbounded domain then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{2}
iff the square of its Riemann map extends to a rational function.

The reverse direction follows from the fact that
\frac{w}{2}(\varphi^{2})^{\#}\circ\psi(w)
is a generalized Schwarz function for
\Omega
(Theorem
3.2
). Reflecting Equation
3.6
and applying the Faber transform to both sides of the equation, yields a partial solution to the direct problem,

\dfrac{h(w)-h(0)}{w}=\dfrac{1}{2}\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\left(\varphi^{2}-\mathring{W_{2}}\right)^{\#}\right)(w).

(3.7)

Note that this leaves
h(0)
undetermined.

Another, more direct, formula for
h

\varphi

\frac{1}{2}\varphi\overline{\varphi}^{2}\dot{=}(h-G)\circ\varphi

\mathbb{D}
) and applying Equation
1.15
yields

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\varphi\overline{\varphi}^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\left(h-G\right)\circ\varphi\right]\right)=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[h-G\right]=h

Noting that
\overline{\varphi}\dot{=}\varphi^{\#}
and again applying Equation
1.15
, we obtain a solution to the direct problem

h(w)=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}\varphi(\varphi^{2})^{\#}\right]\right)(w)=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}w(\varphi^{2})^{\#}\circ\psi(w)\right]

(3.8)

Finally, consider the arrangement of the Schwarz function equation,
\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}\overline{\varphi}^{2}\dot{=}(w(h(w)-G(w)))\circ\varphi
. Taking the Faber transform of this (after projecting onto the part analytic in
\mathbb{D}
) and applying Equation
1.15
yields

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}\overline{\varphi}^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\left(w(h(w)-G(w))\right)\circ\varphi\right]\right)=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[wh(w)-wG(w)\right]=wh(w)+\underset{\infty}{\text{Res}}G.

One can show via Equation

\Omega^{c}

h(w)=\dfrac{1}{w}\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}(\varphi^{2})^{\#}\right]\right)(w)-\dfrac{t}{w}=\dfrac{1}{w}\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}w^{2}(\varphi^{2})^{\#}\circ\psi(w)\right]-\dfrac{t}{w}

(3.9)

3.4.1.1  Linear PQDs Not Containing Zero

We’ll now apply these results to characterize the set of

having a linear quadrature function. The conclusion of this analysis is summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.7
.

Fix
\alpha_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
,
\alpha_{1}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}

\Omega
is unique modulo conformal radius unless
\alpha_{0}=0

\pm\Omega

\Omega

\displaystyle\varphi^{2}(z)

\displaystyle=\mathring{W_{2}}^{\#}(z)+2\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}(1)=c^{2}z^{2}+2cf_{0}z+2.

\displaystyle\alpha_{0}+w

\displaystyle\varphi^{2}(z)

0

\varphi

\displaystyle h(w)

3.4.2
Simply Connected Bounded PQDs with a=2

Suppose that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{2}(h)

\varphi

3.2
)
h(w)\dot{=}\frac{1}{2}w\overline{w}^{2}+G(w)
(for some
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
), we obtain
\overline{\varphi}^{2}\dot{=}\left(\frac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{2}w}\right)\circ\varphi
. Expanding
\varphi
in series,
\varphi(z)=\varphi(0)+\left<z\right>
, we see that
(\varphi^{2})^{\#}=\left(\varphi^{2}(0)+\left<z\right>\right)^{\#}=\overline{\varphi^{2}(0)}+\left<z^{-1}\right>
. Therefore
(\varphi^{2})^{\#}-\overline{\varphi^{2}(0)}\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
which implies

(\varphi^{2})^{\#}-\overline{\varphi^{2}(0)}=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[(\varphi^{2})^{\#}-\overline{\varphi^{2}(0)}\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[(\varphi^{2})^{\#}\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\overline{\varphi}^{2}\right],

where the last line follows from the fact that
\overline{\varphi}\dot{=}\varphi^{\#}
. Rearranging and substituting the Schwarz function equation, we obtain

\displaystyle(\varphi^{2})^{\#}

\displaystyle=\overline{\varphi^{2}(0)}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\overline{\varphi}^{2}\right]=\overline{\varphi^{2}(0)}+\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\left(\frac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{2}w}\right)\circ\varphi\right].

Equation
1.14
(using the boundary regularity from Theorem
3.6
) implies

\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\left(\frac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{2}w}\right)\circ\varphi\right]=\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\frac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{2}w}\right]\right)=\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(2\frac{h(w)-h(0)}{w}\right).

Hence, we obtain an implicit solution to the inverse problem,

\varphi^{2}=\varphi^{2}(0)+2\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\dfrac{h(w)-h(0)}{w}\right)^{\#}.

(3.10)

Reflecting and applying the Faber transform to both sides of the equation, yields a partial solution to the direct problem,

\dfrac{h(w)-h(0)}{w}=\dfrac{1}{2}\Phi_{\varphi}\left((\varphi^{2})^{\#}-\overline{\varphi^{2}(0)}\right)(w).

(3.11)

Moreover, the constraint
h(\infty)=0
implies
h(0)
is equal to the residue at
\infty
of the right hand side.

Another, more direct, formula for
h
can be obtained by composing
\varphi
with a different arrangement of the Schwarz function equation,
\frac{1}{2}\varphi\overline{\varphi}^{2}\dot{=}(h-G)\circ\varphi
. Taking the Faber transform of this (after projecting onto the part analytic in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
) and applying Equation
1.14
yields

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\varphi\overline{\varphi}^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\left(h-G\right)\circ\varphi\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[h-G\right]=h

Noting that
\overline{\varphi}\dot{=}\varphi^{\#}
and again applying Equation
1.14
, we obtain a solution to the direct problem

h(w)=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}\varphi(\varphi^{2})^{\#}\right]\right)(w)=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}w(\varphi^{2})^{\#}\circ\psi(w)\right]

(3.12)

\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}\overline{\varphi}^{2}\dot{=}(wh(w)-wG(w))\circ\varphi
. Taking the Faber transform of this (after projecting onto the part analytic in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
) and applying Equation
1.14
yields

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\frac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}\overline{\varphi}^{2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\left(wh(w)-wG(w)\right)\circ\varphi\right]\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[wh(w)-wG(w)\right]

\displaystyle=wh(w)+\underset{\infty}{\text{Res}}h.

By applying the quadrature identity to the constant function shows
-\underset{\infty}{\text{Res}}h=t:=\int_{\Omega}|w|^{2(a-1)}dA(w)
, the weighted area of
\Omega
. Hence,

h(w)=\dfrac{1}{w}\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}\varphi^{2}(\varphi^{2})^{\#}\right]\right)(w)+\dfrac{t}{w}=\dfrac{1}{w}\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{1}{2}w^{2}(\varphi^{2})^{\#}\circ\psi(w)\right]+\dfrac{t}{w}

(3.13)

We are now prepared to consider two different generalizations of the Riemann mapping characterization and formulae for simply connected classical QDs (Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
) to PQDs for arbitrary
a>0
. In particular, we exhibit Faber transform formulae in terms of both sum (Mittag-Leffler) and product (inner-outer/Nevanlinna) decompositions of the Riemann map
\varphi
.

Each of these approaches come with their own benefits and drawbacks. The sum representation yields relatively simple formulae, but only applies for integer values of
a
. On the other hand, the product representation yields more specific and unwieldy formulae, but applies for all
a>0
. We will begin with a discussion of the simpler sum representation.

3.5
Faber Sum Identities for PQDs

Fix
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and let
\Omega
be a simply connected unbounded PQD with quadrature function
h
and Riemann map
\varphi:\mathbb{D}_{\ast}\rightarrow\Omega
. Note that
\varphi^{a}(z)
can be rewritten as
p+g
, where
p\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
is a polynomial of degree
a
and
g\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
. Recalling the definition of Faber polynomials (Equation
1.17
), we find that
p
is, in fact, the
a
th inverse Faber polynomial
W_{a}
,

\displaystyle p

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[p\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\varphi^{a}-g\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\varphi^{a}\right]=W_{a}.

As a consequence,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[(\varphi^{a})^{\#}\right]=(\varphi^{a})^{\#}-\mathring{W_{a}}^{\#}\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D})
, where
\mathring{W_{a}}:=W_{a}-W_{a}(0)
, so we may compute its Faber transform:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left((\varphi^{a})^{\#}-\mathring{W_{a}}^{\#}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[(\varphi^{a})^{\#}\right]\right).

Note that
(\varphi^{a})^{\#}\dot{=}\overline{\varphi^{a}}
, so rearranging Equation
3.3
and substituting, we obtain

(\varphi^{a})^{\#}\dot{=}\left(\dfrac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}}\right)\circ\varphi,

for some
G\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then, as
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}
and the Faber transform only respect the boundary values of their argument, we may substitute to obtain

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left((\varphi^{a})^{\#}-\mathring{W_{a}}^{\#}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[\left(\dfrac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}}\right)\circ\varphi\right]\right).

We recognize the argument of the RHS as the inverse Faber transform. Applying Equation
1.15
yields

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left((\varphi^{a})^{\#}-\mathring{W_{a}}^{\#}\right)(w)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\left(\dfrac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}}\right)\circ\varphi\circ\psi(w)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}}\right].

Applying the inverse Faber transform to both sides of the above equation and rearranging, we obtain

\varphi^{a}(z)=\mathring{W_{a}}(z)+\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}}\right]\right)^{\#}(z).

(3.14)

Furthermore, the Faber transform of a meromorphic function is rational, so we may conclude that
\varphi^{a}\in\text{Rat}(\mathbb{D})
. This proves the backward direction of the first of the two main theorems of this section:

Theorem 3.8
.

Take
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and let
\Omega
be an unbounded simply connected domain. Then there exists
r\in\text{Rat}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
for which
\varphi^{a}(z)=W_{a}(z)-W_{a}(0)+r^{\#}(z)
is a Riemann map for
\Omega
if and only if
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(h)
for some
h\in\text{Rat}(\Omega)
.

Theorem 3.9
.

Take
a\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and let
\Omega
be a bounded simply connected domain. Then there exists
r\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\mathbb{D})
for which
\varphi^{a}(z)=\varphi^{a}(0)+r^{\#}(z)
is a Riemann map for
\Omega
if and only if
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(h)
for some
h\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
.

In the course of the proof of Theorem
3.9
, we also obtain the following pair of equations relating
h
and
\varphi
.

Corollary 3.9.1
.

Whenever
r
and
h
, as in Theorems
3.8
and
3.9
, exist, they satisfy the following pair of identities

\displaystyle r(z)=\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}}\right]\right)(z),\;\;\;\;\;\;\;\;h(w)=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{1}{a}w^{a-1}(\varphi^{a})^{\#}\circ\psi(w)\right].

(3.15)

Where
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
when
\Omega
is bounded and
G\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
when
\Omega
is unbounded.

Note that the
G(w)
term in the left equation drops out whenever
0\notin\Omega
. We now prove Theorem
3.9
and Corollary
3.9.1
. The proof of Theorem
3.8
will be omitted, as it is analogous.

Proof of Theorem
3.9
and Corollary
3.9.1
.

For the forward direction, suppose there exists
r\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\mathbb{D})
for which
\varphi^{a}(z)=\varphi^{a}(0)+r^{\#}(z)
. Then note that for
w\in\partial\Omega
,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{a}w^{a-1}\overline{w}^{a}=\dfrac{1}{a}w^{a-1}\overline{\varphi^{a}\circ\psi(w)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{a}w^{a-1}\overline{\left(\varphi^{a}(0)+r^{\#}\circ\psi(w)\right)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{a}w^{a-1}\left(\overline{\varphi^{a}(0)}+r\circ\psi(w)\right)=:S_{a}(w)

extends meromorphically to
\Omega
, so
S_{a}
is a generalized Schwarz function for
\Omega
. Thus
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(h)
for some
h\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
by Theorem
3.2
.

For the reverse direction, suppose that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(h)
for some
h\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
. Then, as
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\overline{\varphi^{a}(0)}\right]=0
and
(\varphi^{a})^{\#}-\overline{\varphi^{a}(0)}\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
, we find that
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[(\varphi^{a})^{\#}\right]=(\varphi^{a})^{\#}-\overline{\varphi^{a}(0)}
, so

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left((\varphi^{a})^{\#}-\overline{\varphi^{a}(0)}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[(\varphi^{a})^{\#}\right]\right).

Rearranging Equation
3.3
and substituting as we did in the bounded case, we obtain

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left((\varphi^{a})^{\#}-\overline{\varphi^{a}(0)}\right)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}\left[\left(\dfrac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}}\right)\circ\varphi\right]\right).

We recognize the argument of the RHS as the inverse Faber transform. Applying Equation
1.14
yields

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left((\varphi^{a})^{\#}-\overline{\varphi^{a}(0)}\right)(w)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\left(\dfrac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}}\right)\circ\varphi\circ\psi(w)\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{h(w)-G(w)}{\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}}\right].

Taking the inverse Faber transform of both sides and noting that the RHS is meromorphic in
\Omega
, so that its inverse Faber transform is rational, we obtain the desired result.

Finally, we verify the formula for the quadrature function. By Theorem
3.2
,
h(w)\dot{=}\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}\overline{w}^{a}+G(w)
for some
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
, so

h(w)=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega^{\ast}}\left[h(w)\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega^{\ast}}\left[\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}\overline{w}^{a}+G(w)\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega^{\ast}}\left[\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}\overline{w}^{a}\right],

where the first equality follows from the fact that
h\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega_{\ast})
and the last from the fact that
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
. Thus, noting that
\overline{w}^{a}\dot{=}(\varphi^{a})^{\#}\circ\psi(w)
we find that
h(w)=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega^{\ast}}\left[\frac{1}{a}w^{a-1}(\varphi^{a})^{\#}\circ\psi(w)\right]
. The argument for unbounded PQDs is analogous.
∎

As a demonstration of the utility of this approach, we consider a simple one point PQD for
a=2
.

3.5.1
One Point PQDs with a=2 not Containing Zero

Let
0\notin\Omega\in\text{QD}_{2}(h)
be a simply connected bounded domain with
h(w)=\frac{\alpha}{w-1}
for some
\alpha>0
. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\Omega
be the unique Riemann map associated to
\Omega
for which
\varphi(0)=1
and
\varphi^{\prime}(0)>0
. Then by Theorem
3.9
and corollary
3.9.1
, there exists
r\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\mathbb{D})
for which
\varphi^{2}=1+r^{\#}
is a Riemann map for
\Omega
and
r
is given by Equation
3.15
for some
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
. Thus,

\displaystyle r(z)

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[2\dfrac{\frac{\alpha}{w-1}-G(w)}{w}\right]\right)(z)=2\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{\alpha}{w-1}-\dfrac{\alpha+G(w)}{w}\right]\right)(z)

\displaystyle=2\alpha\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\dfrac{1}{w-1}\right)(z)=\dfrac{2\alpha\psi^{\prime}(1)}{z-\psi(1)},

where the last equality follows from Equation
1.17
. Also
\psi(1)=\varphi^{-1}(1)=0
and
\psi^{\prime}(1)=\varphi^{\prime}(0)^{-1}
, so
r(z)=\frac{2\alpha}{\varphi^{\prime}(0)z}
, and we find
\varphi^{2}(z)=1+\frac{2\alpha}{\varphi^{\prime}(0)}z
. Thus

2\varphi^{\prime}(0)=2\varphi(0)\varphi^{\prime}(0)=(\varphi^{2})^{\prime}(0)=\frac{2\alpha}{\varphi^{\prime}(0)},

so
\varphi^{\prime}(0)=\sqrt{\alpha}
, and we conclude

\varphi^{2}(z)=1+2\sqrt{\alpha}z.

In summary,

Theorem 3.10
.

If
\alpha>0
and
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{2}\left(\frac{\alpha}{w-1}\right)
is a simply connected domain not containing
0
, then
\Omega
is the unique connected component of the square root of
\mathbb{D}_{2\sqrt{\alpha}}(1)
containing
1
.

In §
3.7
we provide a classification of simply connected one point PQDs more generally.

3.6
Faber Product Identities for PQDs

As the title of this subsection suggests, this portion of the paper is concerned with formulae obtained by decomposing the Riemann map into a product. Out of a host of possible factorizations, those using
Blaschke factors
proved by far the most fruitful. For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{D}
, the associated Blaschke factor is defined by

b_{\lambda}(z):=\frac{\overline{\lambda}}{|\lambda|}\frac{z-\lambda}{\overline{\lambda}z-1}.

(also
b_{0}(z):=z
by standard convention).
b_{\lambda}
satisfies the following important properties:

1.

|b_{\lambda}(z)|=1
for all
z\in\partial\mathbb{D}
,

2.

b_{\lambda}^{\#}(z)=b_{\lambda}(z)^{-1}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
,

3.

\lim_{|\lambda|\to 1}b_{\lambda}(z)=1
,

4.

\lim_{|z|\to\infty}b_{\lambda}(z)=|\lambda|^{-1}>0
.

When
|\lambda|>1
, we also call
b_{\lambda}
a Blaschke factor (in the exterior disk).

Let
\Omega
be a simply connected domain and
\varphi
the associated Riemann map (with the standard normalization). We define the function
\varphi_{\rm out}
as follows. If
\infty\notin\Omega
then

\displaystyle\varphi(z)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\rm out}(z),\quad

\displaystyle\text{when }0\notin\Omega,

(3.16)

\displaystyle\varphi(z)

\displaystyle=b_{z_{0}}(z)\varphi_{\rm out}(z),\quad

\displaystyle\text{when }0\in\Omega,

where
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}
is the unique root of
\varphi
. On the other hand, if
\infty\in\Omega
then

\displaystyle\varphi(z)

\displaystyle=z\varphi_{\rm out}(z),\quad

\displaystyle\text{when }0\notin\Omega,

(3.17)

\displaystyle\varphi(z)

\displaystyle=zb_{z_{0}}(z)\varphi_{\rm out}(z),\quad

\displaystyle\text{when }0\in\Omega,

where
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
is the unique root of
\varphi
. The formulae (
3.16
) and (
3.17
) give the inner-outer factorization of
\varphi
. It is trivial to see that
\varphi
belongs to the meromorphic Nevanlinna class, which consists of ratios of bounded holomorphic functions. Thus
\varphi_{\rm out}
is exactly the
outer part
of
\varphi
in the sense of the Nevanlinna theory. The key property of
\varphi_{\rm out}
for our purposes is the fact that it is (by construction) finite, non-zero, and analytic, and hence
\varphi_{\rm out}^{a}
is single-valued and analytic.

On the other hand, we denote by
\varphi_{\rm in}:=\frac{\varphi}{\varphi_{\rm out}}
the
inner part
of
\varphi
. Equations
3.16
and
3.17
yield by inspection

\displaystyle\varphi_{\rm in}(z)

\displaystyle=1,\quad

\displaystyle\text{when }\infty\notin\Omega,\;0\notin\Omega,

(3.18)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\rm in}(z)

\displaystyle=b_{z_{0}}(z),\quad

\displaystyle\text{when }\infty\notin\Omega,\;0\in\Omega,

\displaystyle\varphi_{\rm in}(z)

\displaystyle=z,\quad

\displaystyle\text{when }\infty\in\Omega,\;0\notin\Omega,

\displaystyle\varphi_{\rm in}(z)

\displaystyle=zb_{z_{0}}(z),\quad

\displaystyle\text{when }\infty\in\Omega,\;0\in\Omega.

In particular,
\varphi_{\rm in}
is a product of Blaschke factors (and their reciprocals). This leads to the key property of
\varphi_{\rm in}
for our purposes: that
|\varphi_{\rm in}(z)|=1
for all
z\in\partial\mathbb{D}
and
\varphi_{\rm in}^{\#}(z)=\varphi_{\rm in}(z)^{-1}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
.

For the remainder of this discussion, fix
a>0
and let
\Omega
be a simply connected domain with Riemann map
\varphi=\varphi_{\rm in}\varphi_{\rm out}
(unless otherwise stated). In this case, we obtain the following simple characterization of simply connected PQDs.

Theorem 3.11
.

\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}
iff
\varphi^{a}_{\rm out}
extends to a rational function.

Proof of Theorem
3.11
.

For the reverse direction, suppose that
\varphi_{\rm out}^{a}
extends to a rational function. Then, as
|\varphi_{\rm in}|\dot{=}1
, we find that
\left|\varphi\right|^{2a}=\left|\varphi_{\rm in}\right|^{2a}\left|\varphi_{\rm out}\right|^{2a}\dot{=}\left|\varphi_{\rm out}^{a}\right|^{2}\dot{=}\varphi_{\rm out}^{a}(\varphi_{\rm out}^{a})^{\#}
. Hence,

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\left|\varphi\right|^{2a}\circ\psi(w)}{aw}\dot{=}\dfrac{\left((\varphi_{\rm out}^{a})^{\#}\varphi_{\rm out}^{a}\right)\circ\psi(w)}{aw}=:S_{a}(w)

is meromorphic in
\Omega
, and thus a generalized Schwarz function associated to
\Omega
and
a
. We conclude by Theorem
3.2
that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}
.

Now we only need demonstrate that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}
implies
\varphi_{\rm out}^{a}
extends to a rational function. Note that it is necessary and sufficient to show that
r:=(\varphi_{\rm out}^{a})^{\#}=\left(\frac{\varphi^{\#}}{\varphi_{\rm in}^{\#}}\right)^{a}
extends to a rational function. By construction,
r
is finite, non-zero, analytic (defined in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
if
\Omega
is bounded and
\mathbb{D}
if
\Omega
is unbounded), and extends continuously to the boundary. Hence for each
w\in\partial\Omega
,

\displaystyle r\circ\psi(w)

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{\varphi^{\#}\circ\psi(w)}{\varphi_{\rm in}^{\#}\circ\psi(w)}\right)^{a}

\displaystyle=\left(\overline{w}\varphi_{\rm in}\circ\psi(w)\right)^{a}

\displaystyle=aw\left(\dfrac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}\right)\left(\dfrac{\varphi_{\rm in}\circ\psi(w)}{w}\right)^{a}

If
\Omega
is bounded then, by the definition of interior the Faber transform, we have
\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}r\right)(z)=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[r\circ\psi(w)\right]
. Moreover by Theorem
3.2
,
\frac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}\dot{=}h(w)-G(w)
. Hence, by Equation
1.14
,

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}r\right)(w)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[aw(h(w)-G(w))\left(\dfrac{\varphi_{\rm in}\circ\psi(w)}{w}\right)^{a}\right].

Setting
Y(w):=a\left(\frac{\varphi_{\rm in}\circ\psi(w)}{w}\right)^{a}\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
, we have
wG(w)Y(w)\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
so

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}r\right)(w)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[wh(w)Y(w)\right]-\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[wG(w)Y(w)\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[wh(w)Y(w)\right].

(where the last equality follows from Theorem
3.2
). As
r\in\mathcal{A}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
is bounded,
r-r(\infty)\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
, so
\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}_{\ast}}r=r-r(\infty)
. Applying the inverse Faber transform, we obtain

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=r(\infty)+\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[wh(w)Y(w)\right]\right).

The argument of the inverse Faber transform here is in
\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
, so its transform is rational, and we may conclude that
r
is rational.

On the other hand, if
\Omega
is unbounded then by reasoning analogous to the bounded case, we find that

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left(r\right)(w)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[w(h(w)-G(w))Y(w)\right].

As
wG(w)Y(w)\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
,
G(w)=O(w^{-1})
and
Y(w)=O(1)
about
\infty
, we find that

\displaystyle\Phi_{\varphi}\left(r\right)(w)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[wh(w)Y(w)\right]-\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[wG(w)Y(w)\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[wh(w)Y(w)\right]+C

for some
C\in\mathbb{C}
. Applying the inverse Faber transform, we obtain

\displaystyle r

\displaystyle=\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[wh(w)Y(w)\right]\right)+C

The argument of the inverse Faber transform here is in
\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
, so its transform is rational, and we conclude that
r
is rational. The desired conclusion follows.
∎

It follows immediately that if
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}
then there exists a rational function
r
such that

\varphi(z)=\varphi_{\rm in}(z)r^{\#}(z)^{\frac{1}{a}}.

(3.19)

We furthermore obtain a formula for
h
in terms of this representation of
\varphi
, hence solving the direct problem for PQDs. In particular if
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(h)
then by Theorem
3.2
,

h(w)\dot{=}\dfrac{|w|^{2a}}{aw}+G(w)=\dfrac{|\varphi|^{2a}\circ\psi(w)}{aw}+G(w)\dot{=}\dfrac{\left(rr^{\#}\right)\circ\psi(w)}{aw}+G(w).

Noting that
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[G\right]=0
and

Theorem 3.21

Before continuing on to our discussion of Monomial PQDs, we state the following univalence criterion particularly applicable to PQDs.

Lemma 3.22
.

Take
a>0
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
nonzero constants, and assume
\varphi:\mathbb{D}_{\ast}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is given by
\varphi(z)=z\left(\left(1-\frac{z_{1}}{z}\right)\ldots\left(1-\frac{z_{k}}{z}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}
. Then,

1.

If
k\leq 2a
then
\varphi
is univalent if and only if each
|z_{j}|\leq 1
. In this case,
\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
is starlike with respect to infinity.

2.

If
k>2a
, then
\varphi
is univalent and
\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
is starlike with respect to infinity if and only if
|z_{j}|\leq 1
and

a+\text{Re}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\dfrac{z_{j}}{z-z_{j}}\right)>0,\;\;\;\forall z\in\mathbb{D}_{\ast}.

Proof of lemma
3.22
.

This is a straightforward consequence of the following result:

Let
a>0
,
k,l\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and,
\{z_{j}\}_{j=1}^{k}
,
\{w_{j}\}_{j=1}^{l}
nonzero constants. If
\varphi:\mathbb{D}_{\ast}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is given by

\varphi(z)=z\left(\dfrac{\left(1-\frac{z_{1}}{z}\right)\ldots\left(1-\frac{z_{k}}{z}\right)}{\left(1-\frac{w_{1}}{z}\right)\ldots\left(1-\frac{w_{l}}{z}\right)}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}

then
\varphi
is univalent and
\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
is starlike with respect to infinity if and only if each
|z_{j}|,|w_{j}|\leq 1
and

a+\text{Re}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\dfrac{z_{j}}{z-z_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{l}\dfrac{w_{j}}{z-w_{j}}\right)>0,\;\;\;\forall z\in\mathbb{D}_{\ast}.

It follows from Lemma
3.17
that an analytic function
f:\mathbb{D}_{\ast}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is univalent and
\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
is starlike with respect to infinity if and only if
\text{Re}\left(z\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}\right)>0
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
. Moreover, by the chain rule,
\text{Re}\left(z\frac{f^{\prime}(z)}{f(z)}\right)>0
if and only if
\text{Re}\left(z\frac{(f^{a})^{\prime}(z)}{f^{a}(z)}\right)>0
. Thus, its sufficient to show that each
|z_{j}|,|w_{j}|\leq 1
and

a+\text{Re}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\dfrac{z_{j}}{z-z_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{l}\dfrac{w_{j}}{z-w_{j}}\right)>0,\;\;\;\forall z\in\mathbb{D}_{\ast}

if and only if
\varphi
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
and
\text{Re}\left(z\frac{(\varphi^{a})^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{a}(z)}\right)>0
in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
.

First, note that each
|z_{j}|,|w_{j}|\leq 1
if and only if
\varphi
is analytic in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
. Second, we have that

\varphi^{a}(z)=z^{a-k+l}\frac{\left(z-z_{1}\right)\ldots\left(z-z_{k}\right)}{\left(z-w_{1}\right)\ldots\left(z-w_{l}\right)},

so

\displaystyle\text{Re}\left(z\frac{(\varphi^{a})^{\prime}(z)}{\varphi^{a}(z)}\right)

\displaystyle=\text{Re}\left(z\dfrac{a-k+l}{z}+\sum_{j=1}^{k}\dfrac{z}{z-z_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{l}\dfrac{z}{z-w_{j}}\right)=a+\text{Re}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\dfrac{z_{j}}{z-z_{j}}-\sum_{j=1}^{l}\dfrac{w_{j}}{z-w_{j}}\right).

The desired result follows.
∎

3.8
Classification of Basic Monomial PQDs

In Example
3.2
, we showed that the image
\Omega
of
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
under the map

\varphi(z)=cz\left(1-\dfrac{\gamma}{z}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}

(3.30)

is a PQD with quadrature function
\alpha=-\frac{c^{2a-1}}{a}\overline{\gamma}
whenever
|\gamma|<1
,
a,c>0
, and
\varphi
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
.

Using this result as a starting point, our aim in this section is to fully classify the simply connected domains
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(\alpha)
. We refer to such domains as
basic monomial PQDs
. It turns out that all such domains not containing zero admit a Riemann map in the form of Equation
3.30
. The situation is slightly more complicated when
0\in\Omega
, so we deal with this case last.

3.8.1
Basic Monomial PQDs Not Containing Zero

Theorem
3.23
below provides a characterization of basic monomial PQDs not containing zero.

Theorem 3.23
.

Take
a>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
. There exists a simply connected domain
\Omega
, not containing zero, of conformal radius
c
for which
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(\alpha)
if and only if either

1.

0<a<\frac{1}{2}
and
|\gamma|\leq\frac{a}{1-a}
,

2.

a>\frac{1}{2}
and
|\gamma|\leq 1
,

3.

a=\frac{1}{2}
and
|\alpha|\leq 2
.

where
\gamma=-\frac{a\overline{\alpha}}{c^{2a-1}}
. In this case,
\Omega
is unique modulo conformal radius, and
\Omega=\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
with
\varphi
univalent in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
and given by Equation
3.30
.

Proof of Theorem
3.23
.

We proved in Example
3.2
that if
\varphi
(Equation
3.30
) is a Riemann map for
\Omega
, then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(\alpha)
, where
\alpha=-\frac{c^{2a-1}}{a}\overline{\gamma}
.

Conversely, suppose
\Omega\not\ni 0
is a simply connected domain in
\text{QD}_{a}(\alpha)
for some
a>0
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
(necessarily unbounded because
\infty
is a quadrature node). Then by Theorem
3.14
there exists a rational function
r
for which
\varphi(z)=zr^{\#}(z)^{\frac{1}{a}}
is a Riemann map for
\Omega
and

r=a\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\alpha w\left(\dfrac{\psi(w)}{w}\right)^{a}\right]\right)+\dfrac{a}{c^{a}}t.

Also note that the argument of the Faber transform grows linearly about
\infty
, so there exists
c_{0}\in\mathbb{C}
s.t.
w\left(\frac{\psi(w)}{w}\right)^{a}-\frac{w+c_{0}}{c^{a}}\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
, which implies

\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[\alpha w\left(\dfrac{\psi(w)}{w}\right)^{a}\right]=\dfrac{\alpha}{c^{a}}\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[w+c_{0}\right]+\alpha\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[w\left(\dfrac{\psi(w)}{c^{-1}w}\right)^{a}-\dfrac{w+c_{0}}{c^{a}}\right]=\dfrac{\alpha}{c^{a}}(w+c_{0}).

Substituting this into the above expression and computing the Faber transform, we obtain

\displaystyle r^{\#}(z)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{a\overline{\alpha}}{c^{a}}\Phi_{\varphi}^{-1}\left(w+c_{0}\right)^{\#}(z)+\dfrac{a}{c^{a}}t=a\overline{\alpha}\dfrac{cz^{-1}+\overline{f_{0}}}{c^{a}}+\dfrac{a}{c^{a}}t=\dfrac{a\overline{\alpha}}{c^{a-1}}z^{-1}+c_{1}.

Considering the asymptotics,
\varphi(z)=cz+O(1)
, we find that
c_{1}=c^{a}
, so

\varphi(z)=cz\left(1-\dfrac{\gamma}{z}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}.

Now all there is to do is demonstrate that
\varphi
is univalent if and only if one of conditions 1-3 in the theorem is satisfied. By Lemma
3.22
, when
a\geq\frac{1}{2}
,
\varphi
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
if and only if
|\gamma|\leq 1
. Furthermore, when
a=\frac{1}{2}
,
\gamma
is independent of
c
with
-2\gamma=\overline{\alpha}
, so
\varphi
is univalent iff
|\alpha|\leq 2
.

On the other hand, if
a<\frac{1}{2}
then Lemma
3.22
tells us that
\varphi
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
if and only if
|\gamma|\leq 1
and
a+\text{Re}\left(\frac{\gamma}{z-\gamma}\right)>0
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
. Rotating
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
by taking
z\mapsto-\frac{\gamma}{|\gamma|}z
yields
a-|\gamma|\text{Re}\left(\frac{1}{z+|\gamma|}\right)
which is minimized as
z\to 1
. Thus we obtain the condition
|\gamma|\leq\frac{a}{1-a}
. Or, equivalently
0<c\leq c_{\ast}
, where
c_{\ast}=\sqrt[2a-1]{|\alpha|(1-a)}
. Thus when
0<a<\frac{1}{2}
,
\varphi
is univalent iff
|\gamma|\leq\frac{a}{1-a}
.
∎

Basic Monomial PQDs Containing Zero

Theorem 3.24
.

Take

(3.31)

Proof of Theorem
3.24
.

Let

with quadrature function

\varphi(z)=zb_{z_{0}}(z)\left(|cz_{0}|^{a}\left(1-\dfrac{\overline{z_{0}}^{-1}}{z}\right)\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}

is a Riemann map for
\Omega
for some
z_{0}\in\mathbb{D}_{\ast}

\varphi
is univalent in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}

implies

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=h(w)=\dfrac{1}{aw}\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[|cz_{0}|^{a}\left(1-\dfrac{\overline{z_{0}}^{-1}}{z}\right)|cz_{0}|^{a}\left(1-zz_{0}^{-1}\right)\right]\right)(w)-\dfrac{t}{w}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{|cz_{0}|^{2a}}{aw}\Phi_{\varphi}\left(\mathcal{C}_{\mathbb{D}}\left[1+|z_{0}|^{-2}-zz_{0}^{-1}-\dfrac{\overline{z_{0}}^{-1}}{z}\right]\right)(w)-\dfrac{t}{w}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{z_{0}^{-1}|cz_{0}|^{2a}}{aw}c^{-1}w-\dfrac{\widetilde{t}}{w}.

hence

. This implies that

\Omega\ni 0

with
\varphi
as above.

Regarding the univalence of
3.31
, we apply Lemma
2.2
, which tells us it is sufficient to check injectivity on the boundary. Suppose there existed
z\neq w\in\partial\mathbb{D}
for which
\varphi(z)=\varphi(w)
. Then if we take
\eta=-\gamma^{\frac{1}{2a-1}}
,

\displaystyle|\varphi(z)|

\displaystyle=c\left|\frac{z+\eta}{z+\eta^{-1}}\left(1+\frac{\eta^{-1}}{z}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}\right|=c\eta\left|\left(1+\frac{\eta^{-1}}{z}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}\right|=c\eta\left|z+\eta^{-1}\right|^{\frac{1}{a}},

and

and expanding, we find that
\cos(\theta_{1})=\cos(\theta_{2})
, so
w=\overline{z}=z^{-1}
. Thus

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\varphi(z^{-1})}{\varphi(z)}=cz^{-1}\frac{z^{-1}+\eta}{z^{-1}+\eta^{-1}}\left(1+z\eta^{-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}\left(cz\frac{z+\eta}{z+\eta^{-1}}\left(1+\frac{\eta^{-1}}{z}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}}\right)^{-1}=\frac{\left(1+z^{-1}\eta^{-1}\right)^{2-\frac{1}{a}}}{\left(1+z\eta^{-1}\right)^{2-\frac{1}{a}}}.

Thus
(1+z^{-1}\eta^{-1})^{2-\frac{1}{a}}=(1+z\eta^{-1})^{2-\frac{1}{a}}
.

a>\frac{1}{2}

\partial\mathbb{D}

, so

. However recall that

which, in both cases, implies that

. Thus

is univalent in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
when

then

isn’t even analytic in

\varphi
is univalent in

3.9
Classification of Monomial PQDs

In this section, we are interested in characterizing PQDs with a monomial quadrature function - that is,
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(\alpha kw^{k-1})
for some
a>0
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Firstly note that we can wlog assume that
\alpha>0
because if
S_{a}
is the associated generalized Schwarz function then

\widetilde{S_{a}}(w):=\left(\frac{|\alpha|}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}S_{a}\left(w\left(\frac{|\alpha|}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\right)\dot{=}\left(\frac{|\alpha|}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\dfrac{1}{a}\overline{w\left(\frac{|\alpha|}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}}\left|w\left(\frac{|\alpha|}{\alpha}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\right|^{2(a-1)}=\dfrac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2(a-1)}

is a generalized Schwarz function for
\widetilde{\Omega}:=\left(\frac{\alpha}{|\alpha|}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\Omega
, so it is a PQD. It’s then straightforward to verify that
\widetilde{\Omega}\in\text{QD}_{a}(|\alpha|kw^{k-1})
.

Theorem 3.25
.

Fix
a>0
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. If
0\notin\Omega
is a
\mathbb{Z}_{k}-
rotationally symmetric domain
(e^{\frac{2\pi i}{k}}\Omega=\Omega)
, then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{a}(\alpha kw^{k-1})
iff
\Omega^{k}\in\text{QD}_{\frac{a}{k}}(\alpha k^{2})
. In this case,
\Omega=\widetilde{\varphi}(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
, where

\widetilde{\varphi}(z)=cz\left(1-\frac{\gamma_{k}}{z^{k}}\right)^{\frac{1}{a}},\;\;\;\;\gamma_{k}=-\frac{a\overline{\alpha}k}{c^{2a-k}}.

(3.32)

is univalent in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
.

Toward proving this, we note the following relationship between the
k=1
case and the general case:

Lemma 3.26
.

Fix
a>0
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
, and
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{\frac{a}{k}}(\alpha k^{2})
then
\{w:w^{k}\in\Omega\}\in\text{QD}_{a}\left(\alpha kw^{k-1}\right)
.

Proof of lemma
3.26
.

Suppose that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{\frac{a}{k}}(\alpha)
and define
\widetilde{\Omega}:=\{w:w^{k}\in\Omega\}
. By Theorem
3.2
,
\Omega
admits a generalized Schwarz function
S_{\frac{a}{k}}(w)\dot{=}\frac{k}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2\left(\frac{a}{k}-1\right)}
, so

S_{a}(w):=\dfrac{1}{k}w^{k-1}S_{\frac{a}{k}}(w^{k})\dot{=}\dfrac{1}{k}w^{k-1}\dfrac{k}{a}\overline{w}^{k}|w|^{2\left(a-k\right)}=\dfrac{1}{a}\overline{w}|w|^{2\left(a-1\right)}

is a generalized Schwarz function for
\widetilde{\Omega}
. Thus, by Theorem
3.2
,
\widetilde{\Omega}\in\text{QD}_{a}
and

\widetilde{h}(w)=\mathcal{C}_{\widetilde{\Omega}_{\ast}}\left[S_{a}(w)\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\widetilde{\Omega}_{\ast}}\left[\dfrac{1}{k}w^{k-1}S_{\frac{a}{k}}(w^{k})\right]=\mathcal{C}_{\widetilde{\Omega}_{\ast}}\left[\frac{\alpha}{k}w^{k-1}+\frac{1}{k}w^{k-1}G(w^{k})\right]=\frac{\alpha}{k}w^{k-1}.

The result follows from the substitution
\alpha\mapsto\alpha k^{2}
.
∎

It is not true however that every element of
\text{QD}_{a}(\alpha kw^{k-1})
can be represented this way. To wit,

Example 3.3
.

Consider the map
\varphi:\mathbb{D}_{\ast}\rightarrow\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
given by

\varphi(z):=cz\dfrac{z+u}{z+u^{-1}}\sqrt{\left(1-\frac{2\gamma}{u^{3}}z^{-1}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{u}z^{-1}\right)}=c\left(z+u\right)\sqrt{\dfrac{z-2\gamma u^{-3}}{z+u^{-1}}}

where
\gamma=-\frac{2\alpha}{c^{2}}
,
u=\sqrt{\sqrt{\gamma\left(\gamma+4\right)}-\gamma}
, and
c,\alpha>0
. One can show that this map is univalent in
\mathbb{D}_{\ast}
precisely when
\frac{2\alpha}{c^{2}}=-\gamma\geq\frac{5\sqrt{13}-1}{4}\approx 4.257
via Lemma
2.2
and solving the equation
\varphi^{2}(z)=\varphi^{2}(\xi)
over
|z|=|\xi|=1
(taking its conjugate, we obtain
\varphi^{2}(z^{-1})=\varphi^{2}(\xi^{-1})
, so we may eliminate
\xi
to obtain a polynomial equation in
z
with coefficients depending only on
\gamma
).

Then note that
\varphi_{\rm out}^{2}(z)=r^{\#}(z)=u^{2}c^{2}\left(1-\frac{2\gamma}{u^{3}}z^{-1}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{u}z^{-1}\right)
has the form required by Theorem
3.11
, so
\Omega:=\varphi(\mathbb{D}_{\ast})
is a PQD when
\varphi
is univalent. Applying Equation
3.20
with
r(z)=u^{2}c^{2}(1-2\gamma u^{-3}z)(1+u^{-1}z)
we obtain

\displaystyle h(w)

, so that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{D}_{\sqrt{2}}(\sqrt{2})_{\ast}}f(w)|w|^{2}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{\sqrt{2}}(\sqrt{2})_{\ast}}f(w)w\overline{w}^{2}dw

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{\sqrt{2}}(\sqrt{2})_{\ast}}f(w)w\left(\dfrac{2}{w-\sqrt{2}}+\sqrt{2}\right)^{2}dw

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{\sqrt{2}}(\sqrt{2})_{\ast}}f(w)\left(\dfrac{2\sqrt{2}}{(w-\sqrt{2})^{2}}+\dfrac{6}{w-\sqrt{2}}+2\sqrt{2}+w\right)dw

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{\sqrt{2}}(\sqrt{2})_{\ast}}f(w)\left(2\sqrt{2}+w\right)dw,

confirming the identity.

4
Log-Weighted Quadrature Domains

We now shift our discussion to
log-weighted quadrature domains
(LQDs). These are WQDs with respect to the “logarithmic” weight
\rho_{0}(w):=|w|^{-2}=\frac{1}{4}\Delta\frac{\ln^{2}|w|^{2}}{2}
, and as we will see in Lemma
4.7
, they can be understood in certain cases as a limit of PQDs (
\rho_{a}(w)=|w|^{2(a-1)}
) as
a\to 0^{+}
.

Unlike
\rho_{a}
for
a>0
,
\rho_{0}
has a non-integrable singularity at the origin, so
\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
aren’t
\rho_{0}-
integrable function spaces when
0\in\Omega
. For the purposes of this article, we restrict our consideration to the non-singular (
0\notin\text{Cl}(\Omega)
) case in which
\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
are integrable.
We shall implicitly assume that
0\notin\text{Cl}(\Omega)
for the remainder of this article.
A manuscript covering the singular case is under preparation.

The central result of this section is Theorem
4.9
, which generalizes the Faber transform formulae of Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
to LQDs. We also obtain classification results for null LQDs (Theorem
4.4
), one point LQDs (Theorem
4.10
), and monomial LQDs (§
4.5
).

Definition 4.0.1

(Log-weighted quadrature domain)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
be a bounded (resp. unbounded) domain, equal to the interior of its closure, for which
0\notin\text{Cl}(\Omega)
. We say that
\Omega
is a log-weighted quadrature domain iff there exists an
h\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)
(resp.
\text{Rat}(\Omega)
) such that

\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw,

(4.1)

for each
f\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
(resp.
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
). This is denoted by
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
.

Equivalently, we say that
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
iff
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{\rho_{0}}(h;\mathcal{A}(\Omega))
when
\Omega
is bounded or
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{\rho_{0}}(h;\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega))
when
\Omega
is unbounded. We also denote by
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}
that there exists an
h
for which
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
.

4.1
Basic Properties of LQDs

We begin by deriving an analog of the coincidence equation (
1.5
) for LQDs. Fix a bounded domain
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
. Then, applying Lemma
3.1
with
F(w)=\frac{1}{2}\ln^{2}|w|^{2}
, we find that there exists
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
for which

h(w)\dot{=}\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}+G(w).

(4.2)

The same argument and conclusion holds for unbounded LQDs by simply exchanging
\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
and
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Equation
4.2
provides us with a straightforward method of proof that
h
is unique. Suppose
\Omega
is an LQD with quadrature functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
then

h_{1}(w)-h_{2}(w)\dot{=}\left(\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}+G_{1}(w)\right)-\left(\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}+G_{2}(w)\right)=G_{1}(w)-G_{2}(w).

Therefore
h_{1}-h_{2}
continues analytically into
\Omega
. So when
\Omega
is bounded,
h_{1}-h_{2}\in\text{Rat}_{0}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{A}(\Omega)=\{0\}
, and when
\Omega
is unbounded,
h_{1}-h_{2}\in\text{Rat}(\Omega)\cap\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)=\{0\}
, so
h_{1}=h_{2}
. Hence, under this definition,
h
is uniquely associated to
\Omega
.

Equation
4.2
moreover captures one of several equivalent characterizations of LQDs in the spirit of Theorem
3.2
:

Theorem 4.1
.

If
\Omega\subset\widehat{\mathbb{C}}
is a domain then the following are equivalent

1.

There exists a rational
h
for which
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
.

2.

There exists a
G\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
(
\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
when
\Omega
is unbounded) and a rational
h
for which Equation
4.2
holds.

3.

There exists a function
S_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
for which
S_{0}(w)\dot{=}\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}
. This is the generalized Schwarz function associated to
\Omega
.

Moreover in this case
h=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[S_{0}\right]
.

The generalized Schwarz function
S_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
is unique when it exists as a consequence of its equality with
\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}
on
\partial\Omega
.

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

\mathbf{(1)\implies(2)}
: This follows from the application of Lemma
3.1
above.

\mathbf{(2)\implies(3)}
: Rearranging, we find that
\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}\dot{=}h(w)-G(w)=:S_{0}(w)
, which is the generalized Schwarz function for
\Omega
because
h-G\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
. Also,
\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}S_{0}=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[h-G\right]=h
.

\mathbf{(3)\implies(1)}
: Finally, if
\Omega
is a bounded domain for which such an
S_{0}
exists, and
f\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
, then

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\frac{\log|w|^{2}}{w}dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)S_{0}(w)dw

\displaystyle=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[S_{0}(w)\right]dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)h(w)dw,

(
h:=\mathcal{C}_{\Omega_{\ast}}\left[S_{0}\right]
is rational because
S_{0}\in\mathcal{M}(\Omega)
). So
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
. The argument in the unbounded case is completely analogous.
∎

4.1.1
Boundary Regularity of LQDs

Fix
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
so, by Theorem
4.1
, Equation
4.2
is satisfied on
\partial\Omega
. Rearranging, we obtain

\overline{w}\dot{=}g(w)e^{wh(w)}.

(4.3)

where
g(w)=w^{-1}e^{-wG(w)}\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
(
\mathcal{A}_{0}
when
\Omega
is unbounded). Hence
S(w):=g(w)e^{wh(w)}
is (almost) a Schwarz function for
\Omega
, the only obstruction to this being the fact that
S
has essential singularities at the poles of
h
so it is not meromorphic in
\Omega
. However, this is more than enough to establish the boundary regularity of LQDs.

In particular, Sakai’s regularity theorem
1.2
requires only the existence of a
local Schwarz function
. Thus, by applying the same reasoning as in the classical case (§
1.3.2
), we obtain a regularity theorem for LQDs along the same lines as that for QDs,

Theorem 4.2
.

If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}
then
\partial\Omega
has finitely many singular points, each of which is either a cusp or a double point.

4.1.2
Invariance Under Inversion

LQDs are particularly well-behaved under inversion, which is essentially a consequence of the fact that
(w^{-1})^{\ast}\frac{dA(w)}{|w|^{2}}=\frac{|-w^{-2}|^{2}dA(w)}{|w^{-1}|^{2}}=\frac{dA(w)}{|w|^{2}}
, where
(w^{-1})^{\ast}
is the pullback by
w\mapsto w^{-1}
. In particular,

Lemma 4.3
.

If
\Omega
is a bounded domain, then
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
iff
\Omega^{-1}\in\text{QD}_{0}(-h(w^{-1})w^{-2})
.

Proof of Lemma
4.3
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain. If
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(h)
then for each
f\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega^{-1})
,
f(w^{-1})\in\mathcal{A}(\Omega)
, so

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega^{-1}}\dfrac{f(w)}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{f(w^{-1})}{|w|^{2}}dA(w)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w^{-1})h(w)dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega^{-1}}f(w)h(w^{-1})(-w^{-2})dw

This implies the backwards direction as well because
\widetilde{h}(w)=-h(w^{-1})w^{-2}
iff
-\widetilde{h}(w^{-1})w^{-2}=h(w)
.
∎

4.1.3
Null LQDs

We begin with a classification of null LQDs (domains
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(0)
), summarized by the following theorem.

Theorem 4.4
.

A domain
\Omega
is a null LQD iff it is an exterior disk centered at the origin.

Proof of Theorem
4.4
.

Let
\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}(0)
. We first note that
\Omega
must be unbounded because otherwise we may take
1
as a test function which implies

0<\int_{\Omega}|w|^{-2}dA(w)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}1\cdot 0dw=0,

a contradiction.

Then
\Omega
must be unbounded, so Equation
4.2
yields
\ln|w|^{2}\dot{=}-wG(w)
for some
G\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
. If
q=\underset{\infty}{\text{Res}}G
, then
\widetilde{G}(w):=-wG(w)-q\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
, and

\ln\left|w\right|^{2}\dot{=}-wG(w)\;\;\;\;\implies\;\;\;\;\left|w\right|^{2}e^{-q}\dot{=}e^{\widetilde{G}(w)},

where
e^{\widetilde{G}(w)}-1\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
. Thus there exists
U\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
and
A\in\mathbb{C}
for which
|w|^{2}\dot{=}U(w)+A
. Thus if
f\in\mathcal{A}_{0}(\Omega)
,

\int_{\Omega}f(w)dA(w)=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\overline{w}dw=\oint_{\partial\Omega}f(w)\dfrac{U(w)+A}{w}dw.

As
0\notin\Omega
this equals
0
. In particular,
\Omega
is a (classical) null quadrature domain centered at
0
. Thus
\Omega
is an exterior disk centered at the origin (see e.g.
[
9
]
). Conversely, it is straightforward to show that
(\mathbb{D}_{r})_{\ast}\in\text{QD}_{0}(0)
for each
r>0
.
∎

4.1.4
Potential Theory for LQDs

As in the discussion of classical one point QDs (
2
), it is instructive to consider the situation from the perspective of potential theory. In particular, we have the following lemmas relating the two.

Lemma 4.5
.

If
K\subset\mathbb{C}
is a compact subset such that
K^{c}
is a finite disjoint union of LQDs,
\Omega_{j}\in\text{QD}_{0}(h_{j})
and a disk centered at the origin
\Omega_{0}=\mathbb{D}_{r}
for some
r>0
, then
K
is a local droplet. Furthermore, the external potential associated to the droplet is given by

Q(w)=\dfrac{1}{2}\ln^{2}|w|^{2}-2\text{Re}(H(w)),\;H(w)=2\text{Re}\left(\int_{w_{l}}^{w}h(\xi)d\xi\right)+\text{const}(w_{l})\text{ in some open nbhd of }K_{l}

(where
h=\sum h_{j}+\frac{\ln|r|^{2}}{w}
and
w_{l}
is any point in the
l
th connected component of
K
,
K_{l}
). In particular,
Q
is unique up to several additive constants.

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

Let
K^{c}=\Omega_{\infty}\cup\bigcup_{j=0}^{N}\Omega_{j}
be a disjoint union where
\Omega_{j}\in\text{QD}_{0}(h_{j})
for
j>1
and
\Omega_{0}=\mathbb{D}_{r}
for some
r>0
. Also let
h=\sum_{j}h_{j}+\frac{\ln|r|^{2}}{w}
. Applying Frostman’s theorem (lemma
1.8
), it is sufficient to construct an open nbhd
\mathcal{O}\supset K
and harmonic
H:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\frac{\partial H}{\partial w}=h
and

\dfrac{1}{2}\ln^{2}|w|^{2}-H(w)+U^{\mu}(w)=0,\;\forall w\in K,

(4.4)

where
d\mu=\mathbbm{1}_{K}\frac{\Delta Q}{2}dA
. We shall choose
\mathcal{O}
as an
\epsilon-
nbhd of
K
for a to be determined value of
\epsilon
. We require that
\mathcal{O}
satisfies each of the following

1.

h
is holomorphic in
\mathcal{O}
,

2.

each connected component of
\mathcal{O}
contains precisely one connected component of
K
,

3.

every loop in
\mathcal{O}
is homotopic to a loop in
K
.

By assumption,
\partial K
has finitely many singular points. Thus there exists
\epsilon>0
s.t. (2) and (3) hold.
Note that, for each
\Omega_{j}
and each
w\in(\Omega_{j})_{\ast}
, the quadrature identity tells us that for each
j>0

C_{Q}^{\Omega_{j}}(w)=\int_{\Omega_{j}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{-2}}{w-\xi}dA(\xi)=\oint_{\partial\Omega_{j}}\dfrac{h_{j}(\xi)}{w-\xi}d\xi=\oint_{\partial(\Omega_{j})_{\ast}}\dfrac{h_{j}(\xi)}{\xi-w}d\xi=h_{j}(w),

(see Equation
1.6
for the initial discussion of
C_{Q}
.)
and this extends to
\partial\Omega_{j}
. Thus, for each
w\in\bigcap_{j}\Omega_{j}^{c}=K
,

C_{Q}^{\Omega\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w)=\int_{\Omega}\dfrac{|\xi|^{-2}}{w-\xi}dA(\xi)=\sum_{j>1}\int_{\Omega_{j}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{-2}}{w-\xi}dA(\xi)=\sum_{j>1}C_{Q}^{\Omega_{j}}(w)=\sum_{j}h_{j}(w)=h(w)-\dfrac{\ln|r|^{2}}{w}.

But then note that

\displaystyle C_{Q}^{\mathbb{D}_{r}^{c}}(w)=\int_{\mathbb{D}_{r}^{c}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{-2}}{w-\xi}dA(\xi)

\displaystyle=\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\mathbb{D}_{R}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}\dfrac{|\xi|^{-2}}{w-\xi}dA(\xi)=\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}-\lim_{R\to\infty}\oint_{\partial(\mathbb{D}_{R}\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r})}\dfrac{\ln|\xi|^{2}}{\xi(\xi-w)}d\xi

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}-\lim_{R\to\infty}\left(\ln|R|^{2}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{R}}\dfrac{1}{\xi(\xi-w)}d\xi-\ln|r|^{2}\oint_{\partial\mathbb{D}_{r}}\dfrac{1}{\xi(\xi-w)}d\xi\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}-\lim_{R\to\infty}\left(0-\ln|r|^{2}\left(-\dfrac{\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{D}_{r}^{c}}(w)}{w}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}-\dfrac{\ln|r|^{2}}{w}\mathbbm{1}_{\mathbb{D}_{r}^{c}}(w)=\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}-\dfrac{\ln|r|^{2}}{w}

So

\displaystyle C_{Q}^{K}(w)+C_{Q}^{\Omega\setminus\mathbb{D}_{r}}(w)

\displaystyle=C_{Q}^{\mathbb{D}_{r}^{c}}(w)=\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}-\dfrac{\ln|r|^{2}}{w}

on
K
. Thus
C_{Q}^{K}(w)+h(w)=\frac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}
on
K
. Then note that if
\gamma
is a loop in
K
,

\displaystyle 2\text{Re}\left(\oint_{\gamma}h(w)dw\right)=\oint_{\gamma}h(w)dw+\overline{\oint_{\gamma}h(w)dw}

\displaystyle=\oint_{\gamma}\left(\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}-C_{Q}^{K}(w)\right)dw+\oint_{\gamma}\left(\overline{\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}-C_{Q}^{K}(w)}\right)d\overline{w}

\displaystyle=\oint_{\gamma}\partial\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\ln^{2}|w|^{2}+U^{K}(w)\right)+\oint_{\gamma}\overline{\partial}\left(\overline{\dfrac{1}{2}\ln^{2}|w|^{2}+U^{\mu}(w)}\right)

\displaystyle=\oint_{\gamma}d\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\ln^{2}|w|^{2}+U^{\mu}(w)\right)=0.

So, by Morera’s theorem,
h
is holomorphic in
K
and thus in
\mathcal{O}
because
h
is a sum of quadrature functions on the components of
K^{c}
, so, by (3), this result extends to
\mathcal{O}
.

As a consequence, if we pick a point
w_{l}
in each connected component
\mathcal{O}_{l}
of
\mathcal{O}
and set

H(w):=2\text{Re}\left(\int_{w_{l}}^{w}h(\xi)d\xi\right)+\dfrac{1}{2}\ln^{2}|w_{l}|^{2}+U^{\mu}(w_{l}),\;\forall w\in\mathcal{O}_{l},

is a real, well-defined harmonic function in
\mathcal{O}
and
\frac{\partial H}{\partial w}=h
on
K
. Finally, we need to verify Equation
4.4
. By construction, the identity holds at each
w_{l}
. Furthermore, we have

\dfrac{\partial}{\partial w}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\ln^{2}|w|^{2}-H+U^{\mu}\right)=\dfrac{\ln|w|^{2}}{w}-h-C^{K}=0\;\text{ on }K,

and

\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\overline{w}}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\ln^{2}|w|^{2}-H+U^{\mu}\right)=0\;\text{ on }K.

Thus Equation
3.27
holds.

An additional consequence of this (from Frostman’s theorem
1.8
and Equation
4.4

4.3
Faber Transform Method for LQDs

We would like to obtain Faber transform-based formulae for the Riemann maps associated to LQDs.
As with PQDs, a key step in obtaining such formulae is making an appropriate choice of decomposition of the Riemann map. However, unlike with PQDs, it appears that only the product (inner-outer/Nevanlinna) decomposition and not the sum (Mittag-Leffler) decomposition yields tractable formulae. The inner-outer factorization is, of course, precisely that given in §
3.6
, Equation
3.18

\varphi=\varphi_{\rm in}\varphi_{\rm out}

\displaystyle\varphi_{\rm in}(z)

\displaystyle=z,\quad

\Omega\in\text{QD}_{0}

Proof of Theorem
