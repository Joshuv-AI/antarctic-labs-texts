Icariin has the potential to induce the differentiation of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells into brown fat cells via PDE5A inhibition
Authors: Yao Lu, Xiangfeng Xu, Sitong Liu, Zhaozheng Wang, Kai Jiang, Juan Gu, Li Fang, Rong Shen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs) possess the ability of adipogenic differentiation. Icariin (ICA) is a prenylated flavonol glycoside with diverse pharmacological activities and has been reported to promote osteogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. Nevertheless, the effects of ICA on BMMSC adipogenic differentiation into brown fat cells are still unclear. This study aimed to explore the effects and mechanistic basis of ICA on the differentiation of BMMSCs into brown fat cells.MethodsOil Red-O staining assay was applied to detect the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs after induction. RT-qPCR and Western blot were conducted to detect the expression of lipogenic markers PPARγ and FABP4 as well as the brown fat biomarkers BMP7, PGC-1α, and UCP1 in BMMSCs. Moreover, phosphodiesterase-5A (PDE5A) expression in BMMSCs treated with ICA was measured by RT-qPCR and Western blot.ResultsICA promoted the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs and increased the expression levels of lipogenic markers PPARγ and FABP4 and the brown fat biomarkers BMP7, PGC-1α, and UCP1 during the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. Furthermore, PDE5A was identified as a target of ICA, and its expression was reduced by ICA treatment. Moreover, PDE5A inhibition enhanced BMP7, PGC-1α, and UCP1 levels in BMMSCs. Additionally, overexpression of PDE5A notably reversed the effects of ICA in the differentiation of BMMSCs into brown fat cells.ConclusionICA induces the differentiation of BMMSCs into brown fat cells via PDE5A inhibition, highlighting the therapeutic value of ICA for treating obesity-related diseases.Keywords: Icariin, Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, Brown fat cells, PDE5A

Introduction

Obesity is characterized by excess body fat mass mainly stored in the adipose tissue [1]. The adipose organ is composed of white adipose tissue (WAT) and brown adipose tissue (BAT) [2]. WAT is dedicated to storing chemical energy as triglycerides, while BAT dissipates chemical energy in the form of heat [2]. Brown fat cells are the main component of BAT, and apart from classic brown adipocytes, BAT also consists of beige adipocytes. The process of beige adipocyte stimulation and development in WAT is called “browning”, which may lead to reduced adverse effects caused by WAT and improve metabolic health [3,4]. BAT plays a key role in energy homeostasis and has high metabolic activity, and activation of BAT shows beneficial effects on adiposity [3]. Therefore, identifying the mechanisms that stimulate the development of BAT in humans can provide a new therapeutic approach to reducing obesity and its related diseases.

Brown adipocyte contains mitochondria fuelled with the surrounding lipid droplets for energy dissipation and heat production for which they express thermogenic markers such as PPARγ, PGC-1α and UCP1 [5]. Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) belongs to a master modulator of adipogenesis [6]. Fatty acid-binding protein 4 (FABP4) is a target gene of PPARγ and belongs to the fatty acid-binding protein family. FABP4 is reported to be highly expressed in adipose tissue and plays a critical role in lipid metabolism and inflammatory responses [7]. PPARγ can promote adipocyte differentiation by activating adipocyte differentiating regulator FABP4, along with enhancing its expression to proceed with fat deposition [8]. PPAR-γ can also activate PPAR-γ coactivator-1α (PGC-1α) to promote the expression of brown-selective genes such as uncoupling protein-1 (UCP-1) [9]. Moreover, bone morphogenetic protein-7 (BMP-7) is reported to induce the PGC-1α and elevate the expression of UCP-1 and PPAR-γ to activate brown adipogenesis [10,11]. In addition, the WAT browning is accompanied by UCP-1 upregulation, increasing the energy consumption, and attenuating the inflammatory response in adipose tissues [12].

Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMMSCs) are mesodermal cells [13] with multi-directional differentiation potential, which is regulated by ligands and receptors such as PPARγ, BMP and others [14]. BMMSCs adipogenesis is regarded as one of the leading causes for obesity [15]. Bone marrow adipose tissue is evidently increased in type-2 diabetes patients, and their BMMSCs constitutively express adipogenic markers and differentiate into adipocytes compared with non-diabetic individuals [16]. Studies have also reported that BMMSCs can differentiate into brown adipocytes [17,18]. Restoring osteo-adipogenic differentiation balance and promoting the osteogenesis of BMMSCs can alleviate obesity, which might provide clues for anti-obesity therapy [19].

Traditional Chinese medicines (TCMs) has been reported to affect the biological activities of BMSCs [20]. Icariin (ICA) is a prenylated flavonol glycoside and an active component derived from a common herb (Herba Epimedii) in TCM [21]. The pharmacological effects of ICA mainly include anti-inflammation, antioxidant, anti-depression, cardiovascular protection properties and others [21,22]. Several studies have revealed the therapeutic effect of ICA on osteoporosis, chronic nephritis, asthma, hepatitis, and cardiovascular problems [23]. Moreover, ICA is reported to regulate the proliferation and differentiation of BMSCs in different diseases. For example, ICA promotes BMSC osteoblastic differentiation in osteoporosis via the estrogen pathway [24]. ICA can also restore the osteogenic-adipogenic differentiation balance of BMMSCs in osteonecrosis patients [25]. However, the underlying mechanisms of ICA involved in adipose differentiation of BMMSCs into brown fat cells remain to be studied.

In this study, our objective was to explore the effects of ICA on the differentiation of BMMSCs into brown fat cells, as well as to elucidate the mechanistic basis of the regulation of adipogenic differentiation of ICA, which may deepen the understanding of the pharmacological activities of ICA on BMMSCs and provide a promising therapeutic agent for obesity treatment.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

The hBMMSCs were purchased from Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (#CM-H166; Wuhan, China) and cultured in DMEM with 10 % FBS (Gibco, Auckland, NZ) in a humidified atmosphere of 5 % CO2 at 37 °C. After adipogenic differentiation, cells were treated with 1 μM, 10 μM, 50 μM, or 100 μM of ICA (Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) for 24 h.

Adipogenic differentiation

Cells were seeded in six-well plates and incubated in DMEM (Gibco) together with an adipogenic induction mixture comprising 10 % FBS, 1 % Pen-Strep, 10 mg/mL insulin, 1 mM dexamethasone and 0.5 mM 3-isobutyl-1-methylxanthine (IBMX) (Sigma-Aldrich) for 14 days [26]. Medium was replaced 3 times per week.

Cell transfection

To silence PDE5A expression, cells were transfected with PDE5A siRNA (si-PDE5A; 10 μM, 20 μM, 50 μM, or 100 μM; Genechem, Shanghai, China) as well as its negative control siRNA (si-NC; Genechem) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Full-length sequence of PDE5A was inserted into pcDNA3.1 vector (Geenseed Biotech, Guangzhou, China) and transfected into cells using Lipofectamine 3000 for establishing the PDE5A overexpressed cell line, and the empty pcDNA3.1 vector served as negative control (NC). After 48 h of transfection, cells were collected and induced adipogenic differentiation.

Reverse transcription quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells using 1 mL of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen). Next, the reverse transcription was performed with a Prime Script™ RT reagent kit (Takara Biotechnology, China). Subsequently, qPCR was performed using a SYBR Premix Ex Taq TM II Kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan) on an ABI 7500 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Gene expression was calculated with the 2−ΔΔCt method normalized to GAPDH. The primer sequences are given in Table 1.

Western blot

Cells were lysed in the RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher, USA) to extract the total protein. The concentration of protein was measured using BCA assay (Byotime, Shanghai). The protein samples were loaded onto 10 % SDS-PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 5 % nonfat milk, membranes were incubated with primary antibodies including PPARγ (Abcam, ab178860, 1/1000), UCP1 (Abcam, ab234430, 1/1000), BMP7 (Abcam, ab129156, 1/1000), PGC-1α (Abcam, ab176328, 1/1000), PDE5A (Abcam, ab259945, 1/1000) and GAPDH (Abcam, ab8245, 1/1000) at 4 °C overnight. GAPDH served as a loading control. Next morning, membranes were incubated with the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Abcam, ab6728, 1/2000) for 1 h. Finally, the ECL chemiluminescent detection reagent (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Shanghai, China) was applied to visualize the proteins. The relative densities of protein bands were analyzed by ImageJ software (v1.8.0; National Institutes of Health).

Oil Red-O staining assay

Cells were rinsed with PBS, and then fixed with 4 % formaldehyde for 10 min. Next, cells were washed with 3 % isopropanol, and dyed with filtered Oil Red-O staining solution (Servicebio technology Co., Ltd, Wuhan, China) for 1 h. The formation of adipogenic differentiation was observed by microscopy (Olympus, Japan) [27].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SD from 3 independent repeats. The difference between the groups was analyzed with Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc analysis. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

ICA promotes the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs

The 2D and 3D chemical structures of ICA obtained from pubchem (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) are shown in Fig. 1A and B. After induction of adipogenic differentiation, BMMSCs were treated with different concentrations of ICA (1 μM, 10 μM, 50 μM, and 100 μM). Subsequently, we detected the impact of ICA on adipogenic capacity of BMMSCs. Oil red O staining assay was applied to measure the lipid droplets accumulation of BMMSCs. We found that, treatment of BMMSCs with different doses of ICA evidently promoted the lipid droplet accumulation in BMMSCs in a concentration dependent manner when compared to the control cells (Fig. 1C). Additionally, RT-qPCR was used to measure lipogenic markers such as PPARγ and FABP4 expression level in BMMSCs treated with different concentrations of ICA. We observed that, in comparison to control cells, PPARγ and FABP4 mRNA levels in BMMSCs were significantly increased in a dose-dependent manner (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1D–E). This data was further confirmed by Western blot assay that depicted the similar results to that of RT-qPCR (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1F–G). Collectively, these results indicate that ICA promotes the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs.

ICA promotes the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. (A–B) The 2D and 3D chemical structures of ICA were obtained from the PubChem database. (C) Oil red O staining assay was applied to measure adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs treated with ICA (1 μM, 10 μM, 50 μM, 100 μM). (D–E) RT-qPCR and (F–G) Western blot results of PPARγ and FABP4 levels in BMMSCs treated with ICA. *p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001.

ICA promotes the expression of brown fat biomarkers during the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs

In the next step, we evaluated the impact of ICA treatment on brown fat biomarkers (BMP7, PGC-1α, UCP1) during the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. After adipogenic differentiation and treatment of BMMSCs with ICA (1 μM, 10 μM, 50 μM, or 100 μM), we assessed the gene and protein expression levels of UCP1, BMP7, and PGC-1α. Data demonstrated that BMMSCs treated with 10 μM, 50 μM, or 100 μM of ICA resulted in elevation in the mRNA and protein levels of UCP1, BMP7, and PGC-1α (P < 0.001) (Fig. 2A–F). Notably, treatment of BMMSCs with 1 μM of ICA also increased the protein levels of UCP1 and BMP7 (P < 0.001) but not PGC-1α (Fig. 2D–F). These data suggest that ICA facilitates the expression of brown fat biomarkers in BMMSCs.

ICA promotes the expression of brown fat biomarkers during the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. (A–C) RT-qPCR and (D–F) Western blot results of UCP1, BMP7, and PGC-1α mRNA and protein levels in BMBSCs treated with ICA. *p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001.

PDE5A is a target of ICA during the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs

We then explored the molecular targets of ICA involved in adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. To this end, Swiss Target Prediction database (http://swisstargetprediction.ch/) was used to predict the potential targets of ICA (Fig. 3A). We found that among the possible targets of ICA, phosphodiesterase-5A (PDE5A) ranks first with the highest probability (Fig. 3B). Thus, we chose PDE5A for the subsequent assays. Western blot assay showed that PDE5A protein levels were significantly decreased when BMMSCs were treated with 10 μM (P < 0.001), 50 μM (P < 0.05), or 100 μM (P < 0.001) of ICA (Fig. 3C). Of note, treatment of BMMSCs with 10 μM of ICA had the most significant inhibitory effect on PDE5A level. Therefore, we then treated BMMSCs with 10 μM of ICA to evaluate PDE5A level at 0 h, 12 h, 24 h, or 48 h. The outcomes illustrated that PDE5A protein level was markedly downregulated at 12, 24 and 48 h (P < 0.001), and was most significantly decreased at 24 h (Fig. 3D). Meanwhile, we also evaluated PDE5A expression at mRNA level via RT-qPCR, and we found that treatment of BMMSCs with 1 μM of ICA had no significant (P˃0.05) effect on PDE5A (Fig. 3E). However, when BMMSCs were treated with 10 μM, 50 μM or 100 μM of ICA, it had a significant inhibitory effect on PDE5A mRNA expression (P < 0.001), and 10 μM of ICA showed the most significant inhibitory effect (Fig. 3E). Additionally, we also explored the effect of 10 μM of ICA treatment on PDE5A gene expression level at different time points. Results showed that PDE5A expression was decreased by 10 μM of ICA at 12 h, 24 h, or 48 h (P < 0.001) (Fig. 3F). Taken together, this data highlights that ICA mediates adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs via targeting PDE5A.

PDE5A is a target of ICA during the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. (A–B) The target classes of ICA were predicted from the Swiss Target Prediction database. PDE5A belongs to the phosphodiesterase target class and ranks first. (C–D) Western blot and (E–F) RT-qPCR results of PDE5A protein and mRNA levels in BMMSCs treated with/without different concentrations of ICA or with 10 μM ICA at different time points. *p < 0.05, ***p < 0.001.

PDE5A inhibition enhances the expression of brown fat biomarkers during the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs

The effect of PDE5A on the expression of brown fat biomarkers in BMMSCs was further evaluated. After adipogenic differentiation, BMMSCs were transfected with different concentrations of si-PDE5A (10 μM, 20 μM, 50 μM, or 100 μM). Oil red O staining assay illustrated that, compared with the control BMMSCs, knock down of PDE5A notably promoted lipid droplet accumulation in BMMSCs (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, BMMSCs transfected with si-PDE5A (20 μM, 50 μM, or 100 μM) showed the high mRNA levels of UCP1 (P < 0.01) and BMP7 (P < 0.001) (Fig. 4B–C). Meanwhile, PGC-1α expression was elevated by the transfection of si-PDE5A (10 μM, 20 μM, 50 μM, or 100 μM) in BMMSCs (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4D). Western blot assay also demonstrated that UCP1 (P < 0.001), BMP7 (P < 0.05), and PGC-1α (P < 0.001) protein levels were notably increased by si-PDE5A transfection compared to the control BMMSCs (Fig. 4E–F). Overall, these data suggest that PDE5A inhibition enhances the expression of brown fat biomarkers during the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs.

PDE5A inhibition enhances the expression of brown fat biomarkers during the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. (A) Oil red O staining assay was employed to evaluate adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs transfected with si-PDE5A (10 μM, 20 μM, 50 μM, 100 μM). (B–D) RT-qPCR and (E–F) Western blot results of UCP1, BMP7, and PGC-1α mRNA and protein levels in BMMSCs transfected with si-PDE5A. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

PDE5A is essential for ICA-mediated adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs

Lastly, rescue assays were performed to validate the effects of PDE5A and ICA on the differentiation of BMMSCs into brown fat cells. To this end, BMMSCs were transfected with pcDNA3.1-PDE5A to overexpress PDE5A in BMMSCs and then treated these cells with 10 μM of ICA. Results revealed that ICA treatment promoted lipid droplet precipitation compared to the control BMMSCs, while overexpressing PDE5A in BMMSCs reversed this effect (Fig. 5A). We then evaluated the expression of brown fat biomarkers (BMP7, PGC-1α, UCP1) in these cells. We found that UCP1, BMP7, and PGC-1α expressions at mRNA and protein levels in BMMSCs were increased by ICA treatment and this effect was reversed by PDE5A overexpression (P < 0.001) (Fig. 5B–C). This data indicates that PDE5A plays a key role in BMMSCs to differentiate into adipocytes by ICA.

PDE5A is essential for ICA-mediated adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. (A) Oil red O staining assay was employed to evaluate adipogenic differentiation of ICA-treated BMMSCs transfected with pcDNA-NC or pcDNA-PDE5A. (B) RT-qPCR and (C) Western blot results of UCP1, BMP7, and PGC-1α mRNA and protein levels in BMMSCs of different groups. **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Discussion

Obesity is a chronic metabolic disease that arise from the interplay of multiple factors [28]. The unbalance between WAT and BAT is the characteristic of obesity [29]. Obesity causes various metabolic disorders and is closely related to the occurrence and development of diabetes and cardiovascular diseases [29]. BAT exerts the crucial function in energy expenditure and preventing obesity [30]. In one study, BAT and brown fat cells have been confirmed to play a key role in obesity [31].

ICA is a prenylated flavonol glycoside that is extracted from the medical plant Herba Epimedii [32]. Its assorted pharmacological function has been confirmed in different human diseases [33]. For example, ICA alleviates diabetic nephropathy via repressing the NF-κB signaling pathway [32]. ICA treatment can reduce the blood glucose level in diabetic rats and protects pancreatic function [34]. ICA exerts the cardioprotective effect against ischemia/reperfusion injury via activating PI3K-Akt signaling pathway [35]. Importantly, FNDC5 is proved to protect against obesity by inducing browning in WAT, and ICA has been shown to promote FNDC5 expression by regulating AMPK pathway, highlighting the important role of ICA and is expected to become an anti-obesity drug [36].

BMMSCs have been demonstrated to transform into adipocytes under the certain induction condition [37]. Thus, we selected the BMMSCs to conduct our study. As reported previously, ICA can induce osteogenic differentiation of BMMSCs [38], but whether ICA has effect on adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs is obscure. In our study, we found that 1 μM, 10 μM, 50 μM, or 100 μM of ICA notably facilitated lipid droplet establishment in BMMSCs, which suggest that ICA may promote the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. PPARγ is the member of nuclear receptors family and it is primarily expressed in adipose tissues [39]. FABP4 is a subtype of fatty acid-binding protein family and is responsible for generating mature adipocytes [40]. In this study, we also demonstrated that the levels of lipogenic markers PPARγ and FABP4 in BMMSCs were significantly increased by ICA treatment, suggesting that ICA promoted the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs.

We further evaluated the impact of ICA on brown fat biomarkers (UCP1, BMP7, and PGC-1α) during the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. BMP7 is the member of bone morphogenetic protein family, and it is revealed to promote brown fat cell differentiation and thermogenesis [41]. PGC-1α is the gene involved in energy metabolism and expressed in fat tissues, skeletal muscle tissues and so on [42]. PGC-1α has been shown to have the ability to induce mitochondrial biogenesis and adaptive thermogenesis in brown fat cells [[42], [43], [44]]. Thus, it is the crucial regulator of brown adipocyte differentiation. UCP1 is called thermogenin and is responsible for modulating energy consumption and glucose homeostasis of brown fat [45]. UCP1 is the critical regulator of thermogenesis in BAT. In line with the above literatures, this study observed that the levels of UCP1, BMP7, and PGC-1α were significantly elevated in BMMSCs treated with ICA after adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs. These data suggested that ICA promoted the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs into brown fat cells.

PDE5A belongs to the family member of phosphodiesterase superfamily [46]. PDE5A catalyzes the dissociation of cGMP into the inactive 5′-GMP triggering assorted cell activities [47]. The expression of PDE5A in adipocytes and skeletal muscle cells has been validated [48]. Accumulating studies have indicated that PDE5A is involved in the regulation of different human diseases. For example, PDE5A can repress proteasome activity to initiate insulin resistance in C2C12 myotubes, which indicates that PDE5A may facilitate the progression of obesity and diabetes [49]. Recent research has revealed that PDE5A inhibition is conducive to improve the insulin resistance in diabetes patients [50]. PDE5A inhibition alleviates visceral adiposity via miR-22/SIRT1 pathway [51]. PDE5A is related to pathological progression of proteinuria in childhood IgA nephropathy [52]. PDE5A inhibition suppresses pulmonary fibrosis and pulmonary hypertension via inhibiting oxidative stress injury [53]. Consistent with these previous studies, herein, we also discovered that PDE5A was the possible target of ICA. Furthermore, PDE5A expression at mRNA and protein levels was inhibited by ICA treatment. Knockdown of PDE5A markedly increased adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs and promoted UCP1, BMP7, and PGC-1α levels, suggesting that PDE5A inhibition could facilitate differentiation of brown fat cells. In rescue assay, we observed that the promoting function of ICA on brown fat cell differentiation was markedly reversed by PDE5A upregulation.

There are also some limitations in our study. First, we did not perform in vivo experiments to explore the effect of ICA on obesity, and the effects of ICA on the fat accumulation in whole body are unclear. Second, whether ICA induced the differentiation of BMMSCs into brown fat cells via some certain signaling pathway was not exlored Therefore, more relevant experiments will be carried out in the further study. Despite these limitations, our study revealed the promoting effects of ICA on the adipogenic differentiation of BMMSCs and identified the role of PDE5A as the target of ICA in regulating BMMSCs adipogenic differentiation. Thus, deepening the understanding of the underlying mechanism of ICA on BMSC differentiation may contribute to its application in clinic.

Conclusion

The results of this study demonstrated that ICA could induce the differentiation of BMMSCs into brown fat cells via PDE5A inhibition, suggesting that ICA may become the promising therapeutic agent for obesity related diseases.
