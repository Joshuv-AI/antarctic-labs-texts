Minimmit: Fast Finality with Even Faster Blocks
Brendan Kobayashi Chou, Andrew Lewis-Pye, Patrick O’Grady

Abstract
. Minimmit is a new protocol for State-Machine-Replication (SMR) that extends the ‘2-round finality’ approach of protocols such as Alpenglow to further reduce latency, by allowing for faster progression through ‘views’. This preliminary draft provides motivation and pseudocode, together with proofs of consistency and liveness. An updated draft with a proof of optimistic responsiveness, suggested optimizations, and experiments, is to follow.



1.
Motivation

Solana’s recent announcement of their transition to the Alpenglow protocol
(
alpen
)
has sparked renewed interest in State-Machine-Replication (SMR) protocols that achieve
2-round finality
under the assumption that
5f+1\leq n
, where
n
is the number of processors and
f
bounds the number of processors that may exhibit (Byzantine) faults. In these protocols, instructions are organized into
views
, each led by a different designated leader, and 2-round finality means that a leader’s proposal can be finalised after just a single round of voting, meaning two rounds overall (one round for the proposal and one for voting).

This document describes Minimmit, which differs from other protocols with 2-round finality
(
fba
;
SBFT
;
alpen
;
hydran
;
kudzu
)
by foregoing the
slow path
that is used by most previous protocols when 2-round finality fails, and by allowing processors to proceed immediately to the next view upon receiving a quorum of votes that is smaller than that required for finality. The idea behind this approach is that, in a globally distributed network of processors with a range of connection speeds, receiving a smaller quorum of votes (
2f+1
votes) will often take significantly less time than waiting for the larger quorum (
n-f
votes). Requiring the smaller quorum for view progression therefore reduces view latency (time between views). This also reduces transaction latency, i.e., the time it takes to finalise transactions, since transactions wait for less time before being included in a block.

Evidence for reduced latency
. To evaluate the performance of Minimmit, we implemented a deterministic network simulator
1
1

1

https://github.com/commonwarexyz/monorepo/blob/v0.0.58/examples/estimator/README.md
that runs custom workloads over configurable network topologies. This simulator provides each participant the opportunity to drive the simulation (i.e. propose a block) while collecting telemetry by region and role. To model realistic network conditions, we apply latency and jitter to message broadcasts using empirical measurements of AWS inter-region performance over the public internet from the past year.

We tested both Simplex and Minimmit on a globally distributed network of 50 participants uniformly assigned across ten “virtual” regions: us-west-1, us-east-1, eu-west-1, ap-northeast-1, eu-north-1, ap-south-1, sa-east-1, eu-central-1, ap-northeast-2, and ap-southeast-2. Under these conditions, Simplex achieves notarization (and view transition) in 183.61ms (with standard deviation
\sigma
=30.39ms) and finalisation in 289.45ms (
\sigma
=25.98ms). Minimmit achieves view transition in 120.46ms (
\sigma
=25.10ms) and finalisation in 199.16ms (
\sigma
=32.52ms), a 34.4% reduction in view latency and a 31.2% reduction in finalisation latency.

The reduction in view latency directly translates to reduced transaction latency for users. Consider a transaction submitted immediately after block construction at “height”
h
. This transaction must await inclusion in a block of height
h+1
and for that block’s subsequent finalisation. In recently proposed protocols operating under the
n\geq 5f+1
assumption—such as Kudzu
(
kudzu
)
, Alpenglow (fast-path), and Hydrangea
(
hydran
)
—fast view progression requires
n-f
votes. Consequently, transaction finalisation requires waiting for both the completion of the current view (199.16ms) and the finalisation of the subsequent block containing the transaction (199.16ms), totalling 398.32ms in this model. In contrast, Minimmit’s view progression threshold of
2f+1
enables the same transaction to achieve finalisation in 120.46ms + 199.16ms = 319.62ms, representing a 19.7% reduction in end-to-end latency.

The Setup

We consider a set
\Pi=\{p_{0},\ldots,p_{n-1}\}
of

Processors that never become corrupted by the adversary are referred to as

Cryptographic assumptions
. Our cryptographic assumptions are standard for papers on this topic. Processors communicate by point-to-point authenticated channels. We use a cryptographic signature scheme, a public key infrastructure (PKI) to validate signatures, and a collision resistant hash function
H
.

In the full version of the paper, we will also consider optimisations of the protocol that use threshold signatures and erasure codes.
We assume a computationally bounded adversary. Following a common standard in distributed computing and for simplicity of presentation (to avoid the analysis of negligible error probabilities), we assume these cryptographic schemes are perfect, i.e., we restrict attention to executions in which the adversary is unable to break these cryptographic schemes.

The partial synchrony model

Correct processors begin the protocol execution before GST and are not assumed to have synchronised clocks. For simplicity, we do assume that

The intuition

In this section, we give an informal account of the intuition behind the protocol design.

One round of voting
. We aim to specify a standard form of
view-based
protocol, in which each view has a designated
leader
. If a view has a correct leader and begins after GST, the leader should send a block
b
to all other processors, who will then send a signed
vote
for
b
to all others. Upon receipt of
n-f
votes for
b
(by distinct processors), our intention is that processors should then be able to immediately finalise
b
: this is called
2-round finality

(
fba
)
(one round to send the block and one round for voting).
5f-1\leq n
is necessary and sufficient

3

3

See
https://decentralizedthoughts.github.io/2021-03-03-2-round-bft-smr-with-n-equals-4-f-equals-1/
for 2-round finality: we make the assumption that
5f+1\leq n
for simplicity. We’ll call a set of
n-f
votes for a block an
L-notarization
(where ‘L’ stands for ‘large’).

When to enter the next view?
We specified above that a single L-notarization should suffice for finality. However, as well as achieving 2-round finality, we also wish processors to proceed to the next view immediately upon seeing a smaller set of votes, which we’ll call an
M-notarization
(where the ‘M’ stands for ‘mini’). As explained in Section
1
, the rationale behind this is that, in realistic scenarios, receiving
n-f
votes will generally take much longer than receiving a significantly smaller set of votes (of size
2f+1
, say). Proceeding to view
v+1
immediately upon seeing an M-notarization for
b
in view
v
therefore speeds up the process of block formation: processors can begin view
v+1
earlier, and then finalise
b
upon later receiving the larger notarization.

How many votes should we require for an M-notarization? Note that, so long as correct processors don’t vote for more than one block in a view, the following will hold:

(X1)

If
b
for view
v
receives an L-notarization, then no block
b^{\prime}\neq b
for view

To see this, suppose towards a contradiction that

b^{\prime}\neq b

. Then
|P\cap P^{\prime}|\geq(n-f)+(2f+1)-n=f+1
. So,

contains a correct processor, which contradicts the claim that correct processors don’t vote for two blocks in one view.

So, if we set an M-notarization to be a set of

for view

can receive an L-notarization. In particular, it is useful to see things from the perspective of the leader,

. If
p_{i}
has seen an M-notarization for

in view

, and so long as

resends this to other processors,

Nullifications
. Since it may be the case that no block for view

(X2)

So long as (X2) holds, any processor receiving a nullification for view

Defining valid proposals so as to maintain consistency

(i)

Ensuring liveness
. To ensure liveness, we must first guarantee that if all correct processors enter a view, then they all eventually leave the view. To this end we allow that, while correct processors will not vote in any view

(b)

•

The conditions above also suffice to ensure that if all correct processors enter a view

, then they all eventually leave the view. Towards a contradiction, suppose all correct processors enter view
v
, but it is not the case that they all leave the view. Since correct processors forward nullifications and notarizations upon receiving them, this means that no correct processor leaves view
v
. Each correct processor eventually receives, from at least

or a nullify
(v)

p_{i}
is a correct processor that votes for a view

p_{i}
receives messages from at least

(v)

sends a nullify
(v)

(v)

Once we have ensured that correct processors progress through the views, establishing liveness just amounts to showing that each correct leader after GST finalises a new block. This now follows easily, using the reasoning outlined in (i) and (ii) above, where we explained that (after GST) processors are guaranteed to receive all the messages they need to verify the validity of a block proposed by a correct leader. The formal proof appears in Section

.

Intuition summary (informal)
:

•

In each view, the leader proposes a block and all processors then vote (one round of voting). Correct processors vote for at most one block in each view, which ensures (X1).

•

An L-notarization suffices for finalization, while an M-notarization or a nullification suffices to move to the next view.

•

Processors only vote for the block
b
for view
v
, with parent
b^{\prime}
for view
v^{\prime}
, if they have seen a notarization for
b^{\prime}
and nullifications for all views in
(v^{\prime},v)
. Using (X1) and (X2), this suffices for safety.

•

To ensure progression through the views, processors send nullify
(v)
messages upon timing out (
2\Delta
after entering the view) before voting, or upon receiving proof that no block for view
v
will receive an L-notarization. This ensures (X2) is satisfied.

•

Once we are given that nodes progress through views, liveness follows from the fact that each correct leader after GST will finalise a new block: correct processors will vote for the leader’s proposal because the leader themself will have sent all messages required to verify the validity of the block.

Lemma 5.1 (One vote per view).

Correct processors vote for at most one block in each view, i.e., if
p_{i}
is correct then, for each

, there exists at most one

such that

sends a message

Proof.

Recall that
\bot
is a default value, different than any block. Each correct processor’s local value
\mathtt{notarized}
is initially set to
\bot

\bot
upon entering any view (lines
16
and

). A correct processor

will only vote for a block if

(lines

and

either sets

(line

and
19
).
∎

Lemma 5.2 ((X1) is satisfied).
