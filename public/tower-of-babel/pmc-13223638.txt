Navigating through the care of children with autism spectrum disorder: parents’ lived experiences in a Ghanaian Regional Hospital
Authors: Crescensia Baarkoh, Akua Afriyie Nkrumah, Richard Kwaku Bawah, Solomon Suglo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutism spectrum disorder is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder marked by difficulties in social interaction, communication impairments and repetitive behaviours. Depending on the degree of the illness and each person’s particular strengths and problems, autism can have a variety of effects on both individuals and families. Caring for a child with autism is complex and demanding, placing considerable emotional and social burdens on parents.AimThis study explored the lived experiences of parents caring for children living with autism spectrum disorder at the Greater Accra Regional Hospital.MethodAn explorative qualitative research approach was employed in this study. A purposive sampling technique was used to interview 10 parents of children with autism spectrum disorder. Data were collected with an interview guide and analysed using thematic content analysis.ResultsThe findings of this study underscore the profound emotional, social, psychological and financial challenges faced by parents caring for children with autism. Many participants reported experiences of stigma, spiritual accusations and marital difficulties associated with caregiving. Despite these adversities, parents demonstrated resilience by adopting various coping strategies, including behavioural and lifestyle adaptations, reliance on faith and spiritual support and importantly, consciously disregarding negative societal attitudes.ConclusionThese findings highlight the urgent need for comprehensive and coordinated support systems, including psychological counselling, marital therapy and rehabilitation support services, to assist parents in navigating the complex challenges of caring for children with autism spectrum disorder. Implementing such interventions has the potential to reduce parental burden, mitigate stigma and enhance the overall well-being of both children with autism and their caregivers.Keywords: Caregivers, Child, Child Health

Introduction

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder marked by difficulties in social interaction, communication impairments and repetitive behaviours.1 ASD consists of a spectrum of symptoms reflecting impaired social communication, restricted and repetitive behaviours that range in severity from mildly impairing to severe impairment.2 It is identified as a collection of interconnected disorders of different aetiologies.3 ASD presents with highly diverse characteristics, ranging from individuals with intellectual impairments and minimal language skills to those with superior cognitive and language abilities who nonetheless struggle with social communication.1 These social communication challenges often appear in the pragmatic aspects of interaction, including maintaining appropriate speaking volume, observing suitable interpersonal distance and interpreting or adjusting to others’ gestures and facial expressions.2 The wide variability and complexity of ASD are influenced by developmental factors, such as age and cognitive level, as well as environmental conditions, including the availability of support services like individualised education programmes and speech, language or behavioural interventions.1 4

A report from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention shows that the global estimate of autistic children has risen over the years to 1 in 36 children.5 The WHO estimates that 1 in 160 people worldwide has an autism spectrum disorder, which results in more than 7.6 million people living with a lifetime disability and accounts for 0.3% of the global disease burden.6 ASD affects more than 5 million Americans, with an estimated prevalence of approximately 1.7% in children.7 The estimated prevalence of ASD has been increasing in the USA, from 1.1% in 2008 to 2.3% in 2018, which is likely associated with changes in diagnostic criteria, improved performance of screening and diagnostic tools, and increased public awareness.1 In Africa, population surveillance data reveal late-stage diagnoses, suggesting significant barriers to screening and surveillance.8

Depending on the degree of the illness and each person’s particular strengths and problems, ASD can have a variety of effects on both individuals and families.9 10 Common early signs and symptoms of ASD in a child’s first 2 years of life include no response to name when called, no or limited use of gestures in communication and lack of imaginative play.1 Some individuals with ASD may have high-functioning abilities and be able to live independently, while others may require ongoing support and supervision throughout their lives.11 12

Caring for a child with ASD is complex and demanding, placing considerable emotional, social and practical burdens on parents and siblings, in addition to requiring substantial financial resources.8 The high cost of therapies and treatments often intensifies parental stress and feelings of burden. Family dynamics, including parental roles, available support systems and broader community structures, further shape parents’ experiences and influence their ability to cope.13 Parents of children with ASD frequently encounter stigma and negative social judgments stemming from their children’s behavioural and communication difficulties, which contribute to an increased caregiver load.14 Compared with caregivers of children who have other developmental conditions, intellectual impairments or physical disabilities, parents of children with autism encounter a distinct set of challenges, including greater risks of marital strain, job loss, financial hardship, caregiver burnout, poor mental health and reduced quality of life.9 15 16 In Ghana, these challenges are particularly pronounced; parents often experience significant stress and isolation driven by concerns about behavioural deficits, stigma and socio-cultural interpretations surrounding autism.9 17 In response to these pressures, parents adopt a variety of coping strategies to manage the demands of caregiving.14 Common coping mechanisms include seeking medical care, accepting the child’s condition, pursuing spiritual support and relying on assistance from family and community networks.18 19

Although global research on caregivers of children with autism is steadily expanding, there remains a notable lack of studies from sub-Saharan Africa and other developing regions, including Ghana. Most Ghanaian literature focuses on developmental conditions such as cerebral palsy and intellectual disability,20 21 resulting in a substantial gap regarding the lived experiences of parents raising a child with autism. It is therefore essential to address this gap by conducting a qualitative investigation into the experiences and coping strategies of parents of autistic children in Ghana. Such insights are critical for developing context-specific interventions and support systems that promote both parental well-being and the developmental outcomes of children with ASD. This study, therefore, explored the experiences of parents as they navigate the complexities of caring for children living with ASD in Ghana.

Methods

Study design

An exploratory qualitative research design was adopted for this study, as it enabled the researchers to gain an in-depth understanding of the lived experiences of parents of children with ASD.

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Greater Accra Regional Hospital (GARH), one of the leading public healthcare facilities in the Greater Accra Region. GARH, popularly known as Ridge Hospital, is located within the Accra Metropolitan area in North Ridge and occupies approximately 15.65 acres of land. Established in 1928, the hospital has since been refurbished into an ultra-modern 620-bed facility offering a comprehensive range of specialty services. On average, the hospital serves about 800 outpatients and 250 inpatients daily. The facility also operates a highly efficient paediatric neurodevelopmental clinic that provides advanced services to children with ASD and other neurodevelopmental conditions, as well as support for their parents.

Study population

Parents at the Greater Accra Regional Hospital’s paediatric neurodevelopmental clinic constituted the study’s population.

Sampling and sample size

Participants were recruited using a purposive sampling technique. Medical staff at the paediatric neurodevelopmental clinic served as points of contact during scheduled clinic sessions. Parents who met the eligibility criteria were approached and subsequently scheduled for interviews at times and locations convenient to them. Recruitment and interviews continued consecutively until the 10th participant was interviewed, at which point data saturation was achieved. At this point, no new themes and insights emerged from the data.

Data collection tool and procedures

Data were collected through in-depth, face-to-face interviews using a semi-structured interview guide. The guide comprised open-ended questions designed to elicit rich and detailed accounts of participants’ experiences with ASD. It consisted of two sections: Section A captured participants’ demographic information, while Section B included key open-ended questions exploring parents’ caregiving experiences and coping strategies in caring for children living with ASD. Interviews were conducted at participants’ homes or other private locations of their choice to ensure privacy and confidentiality. Parents who agreed to participate were contacted after the initial clinic session and interviewed at their scheduled times and locations. Prior to each interview, the study’s purpose and procedures were clearly explained, and written informed consent, including consent for audio recording, was obtained before the interviews commenced.

Methodological rigour

Several strategies were employed to ensure methodological rigour and enhance the credibility and trustworthiness of the study findings. Credibility was strengthened through prolonged engagement with participants, reflexivity, awareness of researcher bias, peer debriefing, member checking, explicit reporting and consistency in qualitative data collection and analysis. Data collection was conducted over a 3-month period, allowing sufficient time to confirm the appropriateness of participant selection. To enhance the reliability of the data collection instrument, a pilot test was conducted with five participants across two different facilities. Transferability was addressed by providing detailed descriptions of the study setting, context, participant characteristics and the systematic procedures used to generate the findings. Dependability was ensured through meticulous documentation of the research process, including verbatim interview transcripts and transparent coding procedures. Confirmability was achieved through careful auditing of the research methods, findings and participants’ narratives to minimise potential researcher bias and maintain objectivity throughout the study. Reflexivity in this study involved a conscious and continuous examination of the researchers’ roles, assumptions and potential influence on the research process. The researchers acknowledged that their professional backgrounds and prior knowledge of the subject could shape interactions with the participants, as well as the interpretation of study findings. To minimise these biases, the researchers maintained a reflective stance throughout data collection and analysis by regularly questioning their perspectives and decisions.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection. Audio-recorded interviews were transcribed verbatim and reviewed repeatedly by the research team to ensure accuracy and promote familiarity with the data. All transcripts were anonymised using unique codes to protect participants’ confidentiality. Thematic content analysis was employed to identify, analyse and report patterns in the data into themes and subthemes. The analytical process began with in-depth immersion in the transcripts, followed by coding statements and sentences with similar meanings into themes. Initially, relevant segments of the dataset were assigned codes that captured key ideas and salient features (thus, statements or sentences with similar meanings). These codes were then grouped and organised into potential themes in line with the study objectives. The emerging themes were carefully reviewed and refined to ensure clarity, internal consistency and clear distinctions between them. This process was supported by continuous reference to the original data to preserve participants’ meanings. Each theme was clearly defined and appropriately labelled to reflect its core essence. The analysis culminated in the development of the study report, which was enriched with representative and compelling participant quotations to substantiate the findings.

Of the participants, eight (8) were female and two (2) were male, and all were biological parents of children living with ASD. Participants’ ages ranged from 26 to 53 years. Experiences of parents caring for children living with ASD

Emotional stress

The majority of the parents reported experiencing profound emotional stress while caring for their children. Many expressed that on several occasions they had to suppress their emotions and cry inwardly because others refused to offer support and, in some instances, labelled or stigmatised them due to the children’s repetitive behaviours. Some parents indicated that the emotional distress they endured led them to contemplate suicide. In response, one participant stated:

I feel like I’m not myself most of the time, but I have to be with my child all the time since no one has any support for me. I cry at night because I’m so depressed all the time, and I wonder what I did wrong to deserve this. (P2)

Financial stress

The majority of participants reported that parenting a child with autism imposed a substantial financial burden on their families. Many indicated that the daily expenses associated with caring for a child living with ASD were more than twice those of a child without ASD, making the situation financially draining. Some parents further explained that they frequently had to borrow money or seek financial assistance from family members and friends in order to meet the daily needs of their children with ASD. In response, a participant stated:

Hmm, caring for my child who has ASD is challenging. Since I am a single parent, I must constantly borrow money from family and friends. I use half of my income to cover his expenses and some of it to seek out spiritual guidance. (P9)

Marital stress

Most of the female participants reported experiencing marital challenges from their husbands, including spiritual accusations, threats of divorce, denial of their children, and in some cases, abandonment of both the mother and child. In response, one of the participants stated:

At first, my husband accused me of spoiling our autistic child and failing to properly train him, which is why, unlike his siblings, he is unable to do simple things expected of him, like using the toilet on his own. As a result, he plays and cares for my other children, but refuses to interact with our autistic child. He now claims that I have the boy under a spell. (P1)

In contrast, one female participant described her husband as highly supportive in caring for their child with ASD. She highlighted his consistent expressions of love for her and their children, as well as his ongoing emotional and financial support. The participant stated that:

Honestly speaking, my husband has been supportive; he hasn’t let my daughter’s health hinder our relationship. Continually assuring me of his love, he assists me in caring for all the kids. My only issue is that we need more time for ourselves. (P10)

Superstitious and religious accusations

The study found that parents of children with ASD often experience shame due to superstitious and religious accusations, as they are frequently blamed for their children’s condition. Some participants reported that family members and others accused them of witchcraft, bewitching their children or using their children in money rituals. A participant expressed:

My husband has stopped eating my food, and he accused me of bewitching his son. So, he doesn’t eat when I cook. Even the extended family accused me of witchcraft. (P1)

Stigma by association

Most participants reported that neighbours often prevented their children from playing or interacting with children living with ASD. Others noted that when they conducted business with their children present, customers sometimes refused to purchase their goods. One of the participants said:

Whenever I am with my son, and I try to sell things on the streets of Accra, people avoid us and don’t buy from us. Because of that, I have to leave my son home alone, who is at the age of 7, for six hours to get money for the family. I used to have a good relationship with a neighbor who lived about four blocks away. But I have noticed that my neighbor has stopped talking to me ever since she saw my son and realized he had autism. My neighbor ignores me whenever I wave and say hello to her; she will just quickly enter her house when she notices I am coming. (P1)

Parents’ worry about children’s inability to access mainstream education

The majority of parents reported that it was very difficult for their children with ASD to access mainstream education. Some parents indicated that mainstream schools did not provide the necessary support their children required, forcing them to withdraw their children and enrol them in private schools that cater to special educational needs. In response, a participant stated:

Since my son’s mainstream preschool was not providing him with the necessary support, we began looking for a special needs school for him. Finding one was quite tough; it took us almost two years of searching and waiting before we discovered the private institution for special needs where he is currently enrolled. Because of its distance from our home and its high tuition, the majority of parents who couldn’t afford it withdrew their children. (P3)

Coping mechanisms of parents with children living with ASD

Adaptive behaviours and lifestyle changes

Parents described how they had to modify their lifestyles to accommodate and manage their children’s condition. Many indicated that they had to leave their regular jobs to dedicate sufficient time to care for their children. A participant expressed:

I had to stop working for my employers so that I would have time for my son and properly manage his condition, and now I am self-employed and work from home. (P2)

Faith in God and spiritual assistance

Having faith in God or Allah and seeking spiritual support were among the coping mechanisms employed by the participants. Most reported that they believed only God or Allah could heal their children and expressed trust that their children might 1 day experience divine intervention. Others mentioned that they consistently prayed for their children, hoping for miraculous outcomes. One of the participants expressed:

I believe all will be okay since we have been going for prayers and also invited pastors to pray and drive away any bad omen at our home. People might assume that we don’t care if we don’t go, and they might believe the rumors that the father actually used his child for money rituals. As a result, we go for prayers so that people can see that we are not afraid. Seeking spiritual guidance also helps to reassure us that the child is not possessed … (P3)

Disregard for negative attitudes

Most participants reported that their struggles had shaped their responses to people who sought to embarrass them publicly. Some indicated that negative attitudes from others had even led them to experience suicidal thoughts or consider harming or concealing their children. However, they redirected their energy toward positive actions, such as learning more about the condition and taking the initiative to educate those who had previously embarrassed them. One of the participants expressed:

I would be miserable and possibly commit suicide if I paid attention to what people said. There is nothing I can do to stop him from being my child. I won’t conceal him, leave him in the community, or poison him slowly like I’ve heard people do. The most I can do for him is to be very patient with him and to love him deeply. (P9)

Discussion

The study explored the lived experiences of parents of children with ASD, focusing on the challenges they face in caregiving as well as the coping strategies they employ to manage these demands. The parents’ ages ranged from 26 to 53 years, with the majority (60%) between 30 and 50 years. This suggests that most participants had substantial long-term experience caring for children with ASD, making them well-positioned to provide detailed insights into their caregiving experiences and the strategies they use to maintain composure and nurture their children over time. Furthermore, the majority of participants (80%) were female, highlighting that caregiving responsibilities often fall disproportionately on mothers, as fathers may reject or disengage from caring for children with ASD, thereby placing additional burdens on female caregivers.

This study revealed that parents of children with ASD face substantial emotional, financial and marital challenges in caring for their children. Many participants reported experiencing emotional distress, often feeling isolated and left to manage their children without adequate social support. These findings are consistent with those of Agyemang22 and Ten Hoopen and colleagues,23 who also reported that parents and caregivers experience significant emotional stress when caring for children with ASD. Regarding financial challenges, most parents indicated that raising a child with autism imposed a heavy financial burden. Reported expenses included the costs of specialised education, medical care and seeking spiritual support. These experiences align with global evidence highlighting the financial strains faced by parents of children with ASD,24 25 largely driven by the medical and educational needs of their children. The financial strain described by parents points to significant inadequacies in health and social protection systems in Ghana and many other developing countries. The high out-of-pocket costs for specialised education, therapy and medical care indicate limited integration of ASD-related services into mainstream healthcare and educational frameworks. In Ghana, health insurance coverage for developmental and neurobehavioural conditions is often minimal; hence, families are compelled to rely on private services or informal care pathways, including spiritual interventions. This not only exacerbates financial vulnerability but also reinforces inequities, as access to appropriate care becomes contingent on socioeconomic status.

The study also found that parents experienced high levels of marital stress. Some participants reported having gone through divorce, while others described threats of divorce, spousal rejection and neglect. These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that raising a child with ASD can negatively impact family dynamics and marital satisfaction.26 27 Husbands were sometimes found to blame their spouses for the child’s condition and showed less empathy toward their autistic children compared with non-autistic children. Furthermore, some parents reported being accused of contributing to their children’s condition, particularly through spiritual or supernatural means. Such accusations were often devastating for parents and reflected the social stigma associated with ASD. These findings corroborate those of Allotey14 and Eshun,28 who noted that parents and caregivers of children with ASD frequently face stigmatisation due to ingrained societal beliefs. In Ghana, where Christianity, Islam and indigenous African traditional religions are widely practised, beliefs in supernatural powers are common, leading to accusations that parents are unwittingly responsible for their children’s condition. Consequently, parents of children with ASD often encounter discrimination, social rejection and cultural blame within their communities. The attribution of autism to supernatural or spiritual causes reflects deeply rooted cosmologies in which illness is often interpreted through moral or metaphysical lenses in Ghana and most African countries. Such interpretations can intensify stigma, as parents, particularly mothers, may be perceived as responsible for the condition through perceived wrongdoing or spiritual failings. This not only compounds emotional distress but also influences care-seeking behaviour, often diverting families toward spiritual or traditional healing systems, neglecting orthodox medicine. The tendency for husbands to blame their spouses, withdraw support or exhibit less empathy reflects entrenched gender norms that position women as primary caregivers in Ghana. This dynamic exacerbates the psychosocial burden on mothers, who may experience isolation not only from the broader community but also within their own households. The reported instances of marital breakdown further illustrate how ASD can destabilise family structures, particularly in the country of Ghana, where patriarchal norms limit shared caregiving responsibilities.

The study also revealed that most participants reported experiencing stigmatisation, with neighbours often preventing their children from playing or interacting with children living with ASD. Others noted that when conducting business with their children present, customers sometimes refused to purchase their goods. These findings align with previous studies,29 30 which documented discrimination and social isolation experienced by parents of children with autism. The refusal of customers to engage with parents accompanied by their children highlights how stigma translates into tangible economic disadvantage for parents/caregivers living with children with ASD. This dynamic extends beyond social rejection into livelihood disruption, particularly for informal workers who rely on daily interactions for income. Again, this study also revealed that parents face significant challenges in accessing mainstream education for their children with ASD. Many parents were unable to enrol their children in publicly funded special schools or regular educational institutions due to high costs, discrimination and limited boarding facilities. This is consistent with the findings of Abubakar and colleagues,31 who reported that parents of children with ASD in Kenya often lack opportunities to secure basic education for their children. The exclusion of children with ASD in Ghana from play, commerce and schooling reflects weak implementation of inclusive systems rather than mere interpersonal prejudice. With these considerable caregiving challenges, policymakers and lawmakers in Ghana have established laws and regulations aimed at facilitating inclusive education and enabling children with ASD or other disabilities to participate in societal activities similarly to children without disabilities. Although Ghana has enacted progressive legal frameworks such as the Persons with Disability Act, 2006 (Act 715) and committed to international instruments like the United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, the lived realities described by participants suggest a significant disconnect between policy and practice. Limited availability of trained special educators, inadequate infrastructure and insufficient funding for inclusive education constrain the operationalisation of these policies.

Despite the significant burdens associated with caring for children with ASD, parents employed various strategies to manage these challenges. The study identified key coping strategies, including adaptive behaviours and lifestyle changes, reliance on faith and spiritual support, and disregarding negative societal attitudes. Many participants reported placing trust in God or seeking spiritual guidance as a source of strength, which aligns with findings by Gona and colleagues,30 who noted that parents and caregivers of children with autism often turn to spiritual healing. The reliance on faith and spiritual coping mechanisms highlights the central role of religion in shaping resilience, especially in settings where formal psychosocial support services are limited. Trust in God and engagement with spiritual practices can provide emotional relief, meaning-making and a sense of hope amid uncertainty. However, this also reflects a gap in access to structured mental health and counselling services for caregivers in the Ghanaian healthcare system.

Additionally, most parents described altering their daily routines and lifestyles, such as quitting their jobs or changing careers, to dedicate sufficient time to care for their children. These decisions were often influenced by difficulties in accessing basic education for their children, challenges in hiring caregivers and societal stigma. To further cope with these circumstances, some parents limited their participation in social gatherings to reduce exposure to stigmatising situations. These findings are consistent with Vernhet and colleagues32 and Gona and colleagues,30 who observed that parents often adjust their living conditions to manage the demands of caring for children with autism. This implies that the absence of accessible respite care, specialised childcare services and inclusive educational institutions in Ghana places the burden of care entirely on families. As a result, what appears to be ‘adaptive behaviour’ is, in many cases, a form of compelled adaptation, in which parents have limited alternatives. This has long-term implications for household economic stability, career progression and social mobility, particularly in Ghana, where social protection mechanisms for caregivers are weak. The study also revealed that many parents actively ignored or avoided negative societal judgments. Consistent with Vernhet and colleagues32 and Hidayah and Lestari,33 parents maintained a positive outlook regarding their children’s well-being despite societal negativity. By focusing on the strengths and potential of their children, parents were able to cope with stigma and other adverse attitudes. These findings highlight that maintaining an optimistic perspective and focusing on positive aspects of their children’s lives are essential strategies for parents to manage the challenges of raising a child with ASD.

Conclusion

This study examined the lived experiences of parents caring for children with ASD, revealing multiple interconnected challenges, including emotional stress, financial strain, marital difficulties, stigma, superstitious and religious accusations and limited access to mainstream education. These findings highlight the substantial burden associated with raising a child with autism. Despite these challenges, parents demonstrated resilience through various coping strategies, such as adapting their lifestyles, relying on faith and spiritual support and deliberately ignoring negative societal attitudes. These strategies helped parents maintain emotional stability and continue providing care for their children.

Implications for policy, clinical practice and community-based interventions

The Government of Ghana and its relevant agencies, such as the Ministry of Health, the Ministry of Gender and Social Protection, and the National Council on Disability, should prioritise the integration of ASD services into the National Health Insurance Scheme to reduce out-of-pocket costs. There is also a need to increase funding for inclusive education, enforce existing disability laws and support the establishment of regional autism support and rehabilitation centres to improve equitable access. Multidisciplinary teams (eg, paediatricians, psychologists, speech therapists) should be strengthened and caregiver counselling services incorporated into routine care to address the emotional and psychological needs of parents. Community awareness campaigns should be implemented to reduce stigma and improve understanding of ASD, leveraging local leaders and faith-based organisations. Support groups for parents should be established to provide peer support and shared coping strategies. Such coordinated interventions could help alleviate parental burden, reduce stigma and promote the overall well-being of both children and their parents.

Limitation

Participants were recruited from a single regional hospital, potentially limiting the representation of experiences from parents in rural areas or other healthcare settings. Additionally, the study relied on self-reported data, which may be influenced by recall bias or social desirability bias, particularly when addressing sensitive topics such as marital challenges, stigma or suicidal thoughts. Furthermore, the sample was predominantly female (80%), which may under-represent the perspectives of fathers or male caregivers.
