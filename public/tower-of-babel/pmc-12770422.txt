Hierarchical human activity recognition with fusion of audio and multiple inertial sensor modalities
Authors: Tuğce Alara Yılmaz, Hakan Yekta Yatbaz, Enver Ever, Adnan Yazici
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In everyday life, individuals engage in a multitude of activities, and recent technological advancements have facilitated the development of Artificial Intelligence systems that analyse these activities through data in various contexts. Human activity recognition, an essential Artificial Intelligence application in healthcare, detects deviations from normal activities, such as falls, which may indicate health issues. The widespread adoption of mobile and wearable technology enables the development of personalized activity recognition solutions. Given the critical importance of these Artificial Intelligence applications in healthcare, designing systems with high recognition accuracy is imperative. Moreover, these systems must be lightweight to ensure they operate seamlessly on end-user devices like smartphones without compromising their primary functions. Our research introduces innovative input representations and advanced methods in data fusion and multimodal learning that surpass previous methods in recognition accuracy while reducing computational and memory demands. We propose a streamlined neural network model that creatively integrates inertial and audio sensor data to generate color-coded image representations. This implemented Artificial Intelligence system was tested using a complex, publicly available activity recognition dataset organized hierarchically. Compared to earlier studies, our solution demonstrates significant performance enhancements, achieving a 91% balanced accuracy rate in activity recognition and offering substantial improvements in Central Processing Unit and memory efficiency.

Introduction

In exploring the diverse range of human behaviors, there lies a potential for technological advancements to capitalize on these patterns in developing supportive systems. The effectiveness of these systems hinges on their ability to accurately and autonomously identify human activities. Human Activity Recognition (HAR) applications are increasingly used in diverse areas, including healthcare1–3, sports4 smart homes5, content-based video analysis6, and surveillance7. The automation of activity recognition enhances overall quality. Many solutions have been proposed for HAR, utilizing sensors like inertial sensors, medical sensors (e.g., electrocardiographs), and multimedia sensors (cameras, microphones, etc.). Despite the extensive research in HAR, challenges remain in achieving high accuracy, mobility, discretion, and adapting to complex, everyday activities. Furthermore, HAR systems should recognize context autonomously while respecting user privacy, operating without user interaction or imparting a sense of surveillance. As mobile devices are integral to daily life, HAR modules should be compatible with these devices and designed to be lightweight to conserve battery life and not impede the primary device’s functions.

Our research addresses these challenges by presenting a compact model that integrates a unique input representation and introduces new techniques in combining and learning from multiple modes, specifically focusing on inertial and audio data. This model hierarchically recognizes human activities using sensors in mobile devices. We introduce novel image-based representations for inertial and audio sensors, employing early fusion. The color-coding approach for inertial sensors follows the method in8, while various techniques, such as Mel Spectrogram or Log Mel Spectrogram (LMSPC) images, are explored for audio data. The study also examines the individual contributions of audio and inertial sensors to activity recognition. We used the public, complex ExtraSensory dataset9 for evaluation, designed for realistic multi-label HAR scenarios. To ensure a fair comparison with existing literature and address the multi-label nature of the dataset, we adopted a hierarchical representation of the dataset’s activity labels, similar to10,11. Our experiments demonstrate that our model surpasses current state-of-the-art accuracy with a lightweight framework regarding the number of parameters and memory required.

In this study, we present several key contributions: first, we introduce a novel multi-modal learning method with an early fusion strategy that effectively integrates audio and sensory data within a lightweight framework, optimizing both parameter count and memory usage. Second, we develop a dual-strategy evaluation framework for HAR to assess performance across uniform and hierarchical activity classes. Our model achieves 90% accuracy for user-specified labels at the same hierarchical level, surpasses existing models by 4% to 7% in complex multiclass classification tasks, and reaches an average accuracy of 85% in scenarios where smartphone placement is critical. Lastly, we validate the lightweight nature of our approach by comparing it with other models based on floating point operations (FLOPs), parameters, and inference time.

The structure of the remainder of this paper is as follows. “Related work” section discusses the state-of-the-art research in human activity recognition with mobile devices. “Proposed framework” section elaborates on the proposed model and its design specifics. “Performance evaluation” section outlines the experimental setup and findings for activity recognition through inertial and acoustic sensors. In “Discussion” section, we analyze our results and compare them with the current body of research. The paper concludes with “Conclusion” section.

Public datasets.

The study heavily relies on the ExtraSensory dataset9, which encompasses recordings of 60 participants engaged in everyday activities, free from external monitoring. This dataset offers a comprehensive array of sensor data, including accelerometers, gyroscopes, magnetometers, audio, location, and phone usage statistics. These readings are transformed into 225 unique features through feature engineering, focusing on the mean values across three dimensions and 13 Mel Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCC) channels extracted from inertial sensor data using both time and frequency domain analysis.

The ExtraSensory dataset’s design reflects the diversity and unpredictability of human behavior, without predefined activity durations, acknowledging individual variations. With 51 multi-label annotations, it captures the contextual nature of activities hierarchically categorized into physical activities (h1), contextual information (h2), and phone status (h3). In this study, we use the representations <H1>, <H2>, <H3> when defining new contexts.

Given the possibility of simultaneous activities, a hierarchical categorization is imperative. To address the multi-label problem while preserving labelled information, a new format expressing combinations with single labels is employed. The chosen activities are based on their contribution range across the dataset and their mutual exclusivity with other user-defined activities at the same hierarchy level.

Sensor based HAR studies

Sensor-based HAR, applied in domains like smart homes, healthcare, and elderly monitoring, capitalizes on the expansion of wireless sensor networks and the influx from wearable, object, and environmental sensors21,22. These applications are summarized in Table 2.

Employing the ExtraSensory dataset, a study9 conducted HAR for 25 behavioral contexts using logistic regression, focusing on balanced accuracy due to imbalanced activity data. It achieved 77% balanced accuracy with 5-fold and 78% with Leave-One-Out (LOO) cross-validation. Another research23 explored feature selection in elderly care with wearable and ambient sensors, obtaining a 98% accuracy using a Support Vector Machine (SVM) with a hierarchical feature selection.

Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are frequently utilized in HAR; for instance, Mario24 applied a three-layer CNN to waist-mounted accelerometer data, generating monochrome and colored images with F1 scores up to 94%. A shallow CNN on the University of California, Irvine (UCI) Machine Learning Repository dataset25 realized 97% accuracy with 10-fold validation. Studies26 and 27 investigated CNN kernel configurations and introduced a Seq2Dense U-Net model, respectively. The Inception network28 achieved up to 95% F1 scores across various datasets. Beyond the above CNN-based models, recent studies also evaluate on ExtraSensory. A multiscale cross-modal interactive fusion network (MCIFN) extracts per-modality multiscale features, fuses them via bidirectional cross-modal attention. It refines the fused representation with a hybrid attention block. The study reports performance using balanced accuracy, recall, and specificity due to label imbalance, and does not predict context labels36. In another work37, a deep polynomial neural network (DPNN) is proposed with separate single-label classifiers for locomotion and for localization. It achieves accuracy of approximately 0.92 for both tasks on ExtraSensory dataset. Finally, a dynamic context-aware system (DCSHAR) incrementally integrates inertial signals with selected contextual inputs for activity-only recognition; on a 7-class ExtraSensory subset. It reports 0.9148 accuracy and 0.7548 Macro-F1 while reducing execution time relative to a static variant38.

For efficiency, Teng et al.33 focused on lightweight CNNs, enhancing training with local loss, achieving 98% accuracy on the Wireless Sensor Data Mining (WISDM) dataset. To address limited training data, Huang et al.31 proposed two-stage CNNs, improving activity recognition through data augmentation.

Activity complexity categorization in HAR influences labelling and processing, as demonstrated in32. Activities are classified as simple or complex, with complex activities first analyzed by a CNN for feature extraction and then simple activities recognized by an long short term memory (LSTM) model. Table 2 differentiates datasets and activity complexities.

A deep belief network (DBN) in34 examined the impact of hidden layer configurations. The study35 compared time and frequency domain features, reporting 96% and 97% accuracy for single-layer feedforward neural network (SFLN) and LSTM models.

Audio data is also integral to HAR. Jung and Chi30 utilized audio data, extracting melspectrograms for experiments, achieving an overall F1 score of 87%, with Talking recognized at 93%. Additionally, Stork et al.39 applied classical machine learning (ML) algorithms to audio data, with an ensemble voting method reaching a 92% F1 score.

Audio processing for HAR

Mel Frequency Cepstral Coefficients (MFCCs) play a crucial role in audio signal processing for HAR, derived through a sequence of signal processing steps. Initially, audio waveforms are segmented into frames with a Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) applied to convert these into frequency domain representations. The resulting Power Spectrum, indicating frequency magnitudes, is log-scaled to create the Log Power Spectrum (Log-PS), highlighting harmonic content.

To convert frequencies into the mel scale, a suitable number of mel bands are chosen, typically between 32 and 64. These mel filter banks transform the Log-PS into the Mel Spectrogram (MSPC) through matrix multiplication, illustrating frequency variations over time. Subsequently, a Discrete Cosine Transform (DCT) condenses the Mel Spectrogram into a set of real-valued cepstral coefficients, forming the MFCC matrix, which usually consists of 12 to 13 coefficients.

In our study, we employ Mel Spectrograms, which are preferred for deep learning-based audio analysis and MFCCs which are generally used for conventional machine learning techniques in HAR. Also, we employ Log Melspectrograms during performance evaluation. The choice between Mel Spectrograms and MFCCs depends on the specific requirements of the employed learning method. Initially, we applied mel-scaling to the waveform signal, converting it into a linear form known as the Mel Spectrogram. We then took the logarithm of the Power Spectrum (PS) to produce the Log Mel Spectrogram, which captures continuous frequencies with harmonic components. Finally, we used the DCT to transform the Log Mel Spectrogram into a matrix of real-valued cepstral coefficients, resulting in MFCCs. This process is crucial for extracting meaningful features from audio signals for subsequent analysis.

Multi-label classification

In HAR, multi-label classification captures simultaneous activities effectively. The9 study, utilizing the ExtraSensory dataset, gathered data via smartphone and smartwatch sensors from 60 participants across 5000 hours. This study explored both single-sensor classifiers and sensor fusion techniques. The highest single-sensor accuracy (0.76) was observed with phone state sensors, while early fusion in sensor fusion reached 0.86 accuracy in leave-one-out (LOO) experiments. Audio sensors led to balanced accuracy for single sensors (0.70), and both averaging and learned weight methods in late fusion achieved 0.80. The50 study also used the ExtraSensory dataset, implementing early and late sensor fusion, along with a multiple-layer perceptron (MLP) for context recognition, achieving a balanced accuracy of 0.762 with five-fold cross-validation.

Research in51 applied boosting algorithms to HAR, outperforming Neural Networks, SVMs, MLP, and logistic regression-based methods with an F1 score of approximately 0.88. In52, a pre-trained CNN feature extractor was used, achieving F-scores of 0.5277 and 0.2142 for inertial and audio sensors, respectively, with five-fold cross-validation.

Multi-label classification in HAR was explored in10, focusing on coarse and fine-grained levels. Their Coarse-to-Fine Human Activity Recognition (C2FHAR) approach, integrating Decision Tree, Random Forest, and Neural Networks, achieved up to 0.83 and 0.86 balanced physical activities and phone placement accuracies. The Context-aware human activity recognition (CAHAR) framework in11 combined behavioural context with physical activities, using classifiers like random forest and K-nearest neighbor (kNN), achieving average balanced accuracies of 0.80 for physical activities and 0.77 for behavioural context with 10-fold cross-validation. Ehatisham-ul Haq and Azam53 presented another scheme using classifiers like Decision Tree and kNN, obtaining balanced accuracies up to 0.83 with 5-fold cross-validation. Alternatively in54, active learning strategies are investigated for HAR, achieving a balanced accuracy of around 0.79 with less labeled data, comparable to supervised methods.

Finally, in recent attempts to tackle multi-label prediction for CA-HAR, a deep heterogeneous contrastive hyper-graph method (DHC-HGL) is proposed to model inter-label structure across activity and placement using contrastive objectives55. Authors present results with MCC and Macro-F1, including activity-category averages around Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) of 0.855 and 0.923 Macro-F1 on ExtraSensory Dataset. In addition, a label-semantics method (SEAL) encodes activity and placement labels with semantic representations to guide joint prediction is proposed in56, which achieves Activity MCC of 0.922, 0.959 Macro-F1, Context MCC of 0.977 and 0.988 Macro-F1.

In this research, our methods excel in accuracy, both in single hierarchy classification without grouping and in multiclass classification involving intricate activity-context pairings, while demanding less memory and computational resources for real-time activity monitoring.

Dataset description and pre-processing techniques

In our study, we selected the9 dataset for its comprehensive coverage of activities, contexts, phone positions, and multi-label annotations. This dataset, comprising multi-modal sensory data from 60 users, was ideal due to its extensive participant range and detailed contextual information. Unique to this dataset is the inclusion of audio information, which enhances HAR systems. For instance, in-home settings, audio signals can help identify specific activities such as “Talking” or “Watching TV”.

The dataset comprises unprocessed and processed inertial sensor data and statistical features extracted from it. Raw audio data is omitted for privacy reasons, but the dataset includes MFCCs as a numerical audio representation. Additionally, it features 26 extra audio-derived characteristics from smartphones, encompassing the mean of the first 13 MFCC channels and the standard deviations of the remaining 13, averaging over 400 frames.

The dataset faces imbalance issues due to varying user contributions and uncontrolled collection settings. To mitigate this, we use a sliding window method with 20 data points per sample and 50% overlap between windows, aligning with established practices. This approach helps balance the dataset, especially for minority classes and ensures uniform input feature lengths. We also cluster feature data by user and activity. Labels are applied to activities with hierarchical definitions at each hierarchy level. Data is segmented by activity or context per user, then grouped by sensor readings, forming clusters for each activity and individual. Following clustering, windowing is applied to create images.

Each sensor in our dataset displays hardware-specific distribution ranges, with variations for different activities. Typically, activities show consistent patterns: minor sensory changes indicate low-effort activities, while significant fluctuations suggest high-effort actions. Image generation from sensor data

This study addresses the challenge of visualizing actions or contexts in HAR systems by converting activities into “activity images.” These RGB images visually represent daily activities or contexts, aiding the model more efficiently extracting activity recognition patterns. In our approach, each pixel’s numerical value, typically in the [0, 255] range, is derived from sensor data after normalization. This data is then mapped to pixel values, with individual adjustments for each person and sensor channel, preserving natural behavior patterns.

We map the X, Y, and Z channels to RGB pixel channels for inertial sensors (like smartphone accelerometers, gyroscopes, magnetometers, and smartwatch accelerometers). This technique, inspired by the study in8, uses four inertial sensors represented in an image row, as shown in Fig. 2a for the activity “Lying Down”. Audio sensors, however, differ as MFCC sequences and Mel-scaled Power Cepstral Coefficients (MSPCs)/Log-Mel Power Spectral Coefficients (LMPSCs) have more channels. We represent these as grayscale images, later converting them to RGB for early fusion by assigning each grayscale value to all R, G, and B channels. Proposed lightweight model

To train a HAR classification model, we use fused color-coded sensor data images. Our proposed CNN architecture includes 4 convolutional layers, each with 32 output channels and a 3x3 kernel. Following each convolutional layer is a 1x2 max pooling layer, and all these layers use the Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function. The network then includes a flattened layer to reshape feature dimensions before a fully connected layer with 512 hidden units, followed by another with 256 hidden units. The final layer, matching the number of classes, uses a softmax activation function suitable for multi-class issues. We employ stochastic gradient descent (SGD) as an optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0001 and a momentum of 0.9. The loss function is categorical cross-entropy Loss, and we train for 100 epochs. An early stop condition is implemented to halt training when validation loss no longer decreases, optimizing for robust predictions. The parameters of CNN were chosen through a meticulous process of hyperparameter tuning. This involved experimenting with various configurations to identify the optimal settings that enhance the model’s performance. Key parameters such as learning rate, batch size, number of epochs, and the architecture of the CNN layers were adjusted iteratively. By evaluating the model’s accuracy and loss metrics on validation data, these parameters are fine-tuned to achieve the best possible recognition performance.

Performance analysis

To assess model performance, we employ two complementary evaluation protocols that align with the three-level label hierarchy (H1–H3): H1 represents primary physical activities, H2 refers to placement-independent contexts (PIC), and H3 covers context–placement combinations (PDC). In the main-group-independent (MGI) setting, each hierarchy level is classified separately, without conditioning on other levels. For instance, H1 predicts six activities, H2 predicts 15 PIC classes aggregated across all activities, and H3 predicts 28 PDC classes pooled across all activity and placement combinations. In contrast, the main-group-dependent (MGD) setting conditions predictions on the main activity. Here, the model first determines the H1 activity and then classifies its related H2 contexts (four PIC groups: PIC-1 to PIC-4) and H3 context–placement pairs (four PDC groups: PDC-1 to PDC-4). Finally, per-class modality patterns are visualized in figures, while their corresponding averaged results are summarized in the tables where relevant.

At hierarchy level two, multiclass classification is more challenging due to the complexity of activity-context combinations treated as classes. This level features two testing methods: independent and dependent on the main group. The independent method constructs 15 detailed activity classes from various contexts and activities, achieving an 81% balanced accuracy with 5-fold cross-validation, about 4% higher than11, thanks to inertial and audio sensor fusion. In contrast, the dependent method assesses four main groups, each yielding distinct scores. This results in an 83% balanced accuracy, which is 7% better than53.

The hierarchical structure of the classifier ends at level three, where smartphone placement plays a critical role in labelling activity-context pairs. Due to the lack of user-labeled data, “running, exercise, phone on pocket” is not included in the pre-processed data. Therefore, level three does not consider this user-defined context. In their study10, researchers utilized three sensor modalities, incorporating multiple features in data processing. They achieved 86% accuracy in level three, main group independent test grouping. While this study’s results at the same configuration did not surpass this score, incorporating audio data enhanced overall system performance when combined with inertial sensors. In main group-dependent test cases, the proposed solution outperforms state-of-the-art solutions by a significant margin. The average score, based on iteration mean results for four activity-dependent groups, reached 85%, almost 2% higher than the reported results in53 (83%). Notably, “sitting” and “standing” scored very high, whereas the remaining two scored extremely low. In contrast, this study’s results for the three groups are very close, with a slightly lower result for “walking”.

The proposed solution, designed for real-world HAR, effectively processes data from individuals engaged in routine activities, not predefined scripts. A 20-row window setting enhances activity flow representation while handling the imbalanced dataset. The model concatenates multiple simultaneous activities into a single class for practical application. Inertial sensors in smartphones and smartwatches provide necessary data without extra devices, while audio data, even in smaller segments, is effective alone or in combination with inertial sensors. Both MFCC and MSPC perform well, with varying superiority in certain cases.

The study employs a compact deep-learning model, occupying 1.9 to 6.2 MB of memory, dependent on the number of activities and data size, ranging from inertial to audio and fusion images, all with a 20-pixel width and three channels. Conclusion

This study introduces a novel solution for recognizing real-world human activities. Our methodology employs a lightweight model that leverages changes in spatial sensory information to predict user behavior. To address the challenges of multi-label classification, we adopt a hierarchical framework spanning three levels: activity, context, and placement. Additionally, we combat the issue of class imbalance in the dataset by implementing a class-weight approach, emphasizing the significance of thorough data analysis and preprocessing. Our research demonstrates a promising advancement in transforming sensor data into image format, benefiting both inertial and audio sensors. Furthermore, our investigation underscores the enhanced performance achieved by incorporating audio sensors, enabling predictions not only for contexts but also for physical activities and smartphone placement.

During the model evaluation phase, we conducted two sets of experiments: one without label grouping and the other involving grouping labels based on their primary physical activity. Although the results at the hierarchical level were similar for both approaches, it is worth noting that, on average, slightly better results were obtained when labels were grouped than the non-grouping cases.
