Intertwined fluctuations and isotope effects in the Hubbard-Holstein model on the square lattice from functional renormalization
Aiman Al-Eryani, Sabine Andergassen, Michael M. Scherer

Abstract
Electron-electron and electron-phonon interactions are responsible for the formation of spin, charge, and superconducting correlations in layered quantum materials. A paradigmatic model for such materials that captures both kinds of interactions is the two-dimensional Hubbard-Holstein model with a dispersionless Einstein phonon. In this work, we provide a detailed analysis of the magnetic, density, and superconducting fluctuations at and away from half-filling. To that end, we employ the functional renormalization group using the recently introduced extension of the single-boson exchange formulation. More precisely, we go beyond previous approaches to the model by resolving the full frequency dependence of the two-particle vertex and taking into account the feedback from the electronic self-energy. We perform broad parameter scans in the space of Hubbard repulsion, electron-phonon coupling strength, and phonon frequency to explore the leading magnetic, density, and superconducting susceptibilities from the adiabatic to the anti-adiabatic regime. Our numerical data reveal that self-energy effects lead to an enhancement of the d d -wave superconducting susceptibility towards larger phonon frequencies, in contrast to earlier isotope-effect studies. At small phonon frequencies, large density contributions to the s s -wave superconducting susceptibility change sign and eventually lead to a reduction of s s -wave superconductivity with increasing electron-phonon coupling, signaling the breakdown of Migdal-Eliashberg theory. We analyze our findings systematically, employing detailed diagnostics of the intertwined fluctuations and pinning down the various positive and negative isotope effects of the physical susceptibilities. Our results pave the way for a more comprehensive understanding of the interplay of electron-electron and electron-phonon interactions in correlated quantum materials.

Introduction

Electron-electron and electron-phonon interactions are key players in controlling correlation effects across different material classes.
For example, in normal metals, the electron-phonon coupling mediates a retarded attractive interaction between the electrons, leading to Cooper pairing and superconductivity at low temperatures.
According to Bardeen-Cooper-Schrieﬀer (BCS) theory
[
1

, the superconducting transition temperature is proportional to the Debye frequency, which in turn is inversely proportional to the square root of the mass of ion cores constituting the crystal lattice, i.e.,
T_{c}\sim 1/\sqrt{M_{\mathrm{ion}}}

This phenomenon is typically referred to as the isotope effect and was known before BCS theory
[
2

3

However, in the presence of nesting or Van Hove points, the electron-phonon coupling can also induce different types of charge or bond order, depending on which phonon excitations dominate the coupling to the electronic degrees of freedom; see, e.g., Refs.
[

,

,

,

for the two-dimensional (2D) case.

Naturally, the electron-phonon-mediated attraction competes with the repulsive Coulomb interaction between electrons.
While the latter is well screened in ordinary metals, it is the game changer in strongly correlated materials.
There, it can give rise to competing spin- and charge-density wave instabilities, or even unconventional superconductivity as, e.g., found in the paradigmatic Hubbard model
[

II.2
Aspects
of the Hubbard-Holstein model

Effect of phonon-induced charge fluctuations on
\chi^{\mathrm{SC}}
.

Including the self-energy together with the full frequency dependence of the vertex within the so-called Katanin substitution, constitutes a scheme that encapsulates and goes beyond

In particular, it accounts for the inter-channel feedback in an unbiased fashion. For small phonon frequencies

in terms of the charge density susceptibility, see Eq. (

The fluctuation diagnostics provides a detailed analysis of the influence of the effective retarded interaction resulting from the phonons on the interplay of electronic fluctuations, see Figs.

and

III
Hubbard-Holstein model

Figure 1:
Representation of Hubbard-Holstein model. Electrons hop with amplitude
t
to their nearest neighbors and with amplitude
t^{\prime}
to their second-nearest neighbors. They interact instantaneously via the Hubbard repulsion
U
. Lattice sites vibrate with frequency
\omega_{0}
and the effect of an electron visiting a vibrating site leaves an imprint felt by an electron passing by that site at a later time. This induces an effective retarded interaction of strength
V_{H}
between the electrons.

The Coulomb interaction couples electrons in a solid with each other and to the ion cores that constitute the crystal lattice.
To first order in the displacement from their equilibrium positions, the ions oscillate in different vibrational modes which – when quantized – give rise to phonon branches.
A general model that describes a system of interacting electrons coupled to phonons is given by the Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathclap{\mathbf{k}\sigma}}(\epsilon(\mathbf{k})-\mu_{0})c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}+\sum_{\mathclap{\mathbf{q}j}}\omega_{j}(\mathbf{q})\left(b^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}j}b_{\mathbf{q}j}+\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\sum_{\mathbf{q}j}g^{j}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}\sigma}c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\left(b_{\mathbf{q}j}+b^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{q}j}\right)

\displaystyle+U\sum_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}\uparrow}c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}-\mathbf{q}\downarrow}c_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}\downarrow}c_{\mathbf{k}\uparrow}\,.

(1)

where
c^{(\dagger)}_{\mathbf{k},\sigma}
and
b^{(\dagger)}_{\mathbf{q}j}
are the annihilation (creation) operators for the electrons and phonons respectively;
\epsilon(\mathbf{k})
and
\omega_{j}(\mathbf{q})
are the dispersion relations of the electrons and the phonons in branch
j
, respectively. On a square lattice, the bare electronic dispersion is given by

\displaystyle\epsilon({\bf k})=-2t(\cos{k_x}+\cos{k_y})-4t^{\prime}\cos{k_x}\cos{k_y}\,.

(2)

It describes electrons hopping on a lattice with nearest-neighbor and next-nearest-neighbor hopping
amplitude
t
and
t^{\prime}
.
The electrons interact locally and instantaneously via a repulsive interaction
U
, while the phonons in branch
j
couple to an electron-hole bilinear with the coupling constant
g^{j}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}
.
In the following, we consider the paradigmatic case of a constant electron-phonon coupling
g
and a
single dispersionless phonon of frequency
\omega_{0}
, which is often referred to as the Hubbard-Holstein model
[
22
,
85
]
, see Fig.
1
for an illustration.
The case of an acoustic phonon that couples to the charge density (
g^{j}_{\mathbf{q}\mathbf{k}}=g_{\mathbf{q}}
) is discussed in Appendix
A
.

In a field theoretic treatment, the phonons appearing quadratically in the Hamiltonian (
1
) can be integrated out.
This yields a frequency-dependent effective electron-electron interaction,

\displaystyle V_{0}(i\Omega):=U+V_{H}\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{(i\Omega)^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}}=U-V_{H}\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{\Omega^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}}\,,

(3)

with bosonic Matsubara frequency
\Omega=2\pi m/\beta
,
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, and a phonon-mediated electron-electron interaction strength given by the ratio

\displaystyle V_{H}:=2\frac{|g|^{2}}{\omega_{0}}\,;

(4)

see Appendix
B
for details.
Physically, both the phonon frequency and the electron-phonon coupling depend on the ion mass
M_{\mathrm{ion}}
as

\displaystyle\omega_{0}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{\mathrm{ion}}}}\quad\text{and}\quad g^{2}\sim\frac{1}{\sqrt{M_{\mathrm{ion}}}}\,.

(5)

Therefore,
V_{H}=2|g|^{2}/\omega_{0}

is independent of
M_{\mathrm{{ion}}}
.
As a consequence, a variation of
M_{\mathrm{ion}}
affects only
\omega_{0}
in the interaction (
3
).
Hence, at fixed
V_{H}
, two important
limits for the influence of the phonons can be distinguished:

1.

The
anti adiabatic limit
for a vanishing mass
M_{\mathrm{{ion}}}\rightarrow 0
with a diverging phonon frequency
\omega_{0}\sim 1/\sqrt{M_{\mathrm{ion}}}\rightarrow\infty
. In this limit, Eq. (
3
) yields an effective Hubbard interaction with

\displaystyle V_{0}(i\Omega)\rightarrow U_{\textrm{eff}}:=U-V_{H}\,.

(6)

2.

The
adiabatic limit
for
M_{\mathrm{ion}}\rightarrow\infty
. Here, one has to consider the
i\Omega=0
contribution separately, for which the effect of the phonons does not vanish

\displaystyle V_{0}(i\Omega)\rightarrow\begin{cases}U_{\mathrm{eff}}&\text{for }\;i\Omega=0\\
U&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(7)

We see that besides two situations in which the phonons can be neglected in the adiabatic limit, i) at low temperatures
T\ll\omega_{0}
and ii) for
V_{H}\ll U
, the lattice is soft to thermal fluctuations and phonons will proliferate even in presence of a large
M_{\textrm{ion}}
.

In this study, the adiabatic and anti adiabatic limits will be considered by very small and very large values of
\omega_{0}
respectively, while keeping
V_{H}
fixed.
Note that
V_{H}=U
represents a special situation, corresponding to
U_{\mathrm{eff}}=0
.
We will thus distinguish between repulsive
U_{\mathrm{eff}}>0
and attractive effective interactions
U_{\mathrm{eff}}<0
.

IV
Functional renomalization

Let
\psi
and
\bar{\psi}
denote the Grassmann fields corresponding to the fermion creation and annihilation operators
c
and
c^{\dagger}
.
For brevity, we use the combined frequency-momentum index
k=(i\nu,\mathbf{k})
and we denote the spin index by
\sigma
.
We denote the integration over this index
\int_{k}\equiv\int_{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{i\nu}
. Here, the normalization by the Brillouin-zone volume is absorbed into the integral sign over momentum and the normalization by the factor of
\beta
is absorbed in the sign representing the sum over the Matsubara frequencies. Integration over the spin indices just means a sum over the two spin orientations
\sigma_{l}\in\{\uparrow,\downarrow\}
.
The starting point is the Euclidean microscopic action

\displaystyle S[\bar{\psi},\psi]=

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{\overset{k_{1}k_{2}}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}}\bar{\psi}_{\sigma_{1}k_{1}}G^{-1}_{0,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}(k_{1},k_{2})\psi_{\sigma_{2}k_{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle\int\limits_{\overset{k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}k_{4}}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}}}\hskip-11.38109ptV_{0}\,\psi_{\sigma_{1}k_{1}}\bar{\psi}_{\sigma_{2}k_{2}}\psi_{\sigma_{3}k_{3}}\bar{\psi}_{\sigma_{4}k_{4}}\,,

(8)

where
G_{0}
denotes the free fermionic propagator and
V_{0}=V_{0,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})
represents the bare two-particle interaction.

In the Hubbard-Holstein model [cf. Eq. (
1
)], by virtue of its translation invariance and spin-SU(2) symmetry, the free fermionic propagator is given by

\displaystyle G_{0,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}(k_{1},k_{2})

\displaystyle=G_{0}(i\nu_{2}-i\nu_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}-\mathbf{k}_{1})\delta_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}

(9)

with

\displaystyle G_{0}(i\nu,\mathbf{k})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i\nu-\epsilon({\bf k})+\mu_{0}}\,.

(10)

Here,
\epsilon(\mathbf{k})
is the free electron dispersion [cf. Eq. (
2
)],
\nu=(2m+1)\pi/\beta
,
\ m\in\mathbb{Z}
the fermionic Matsubara frequency, and
\beta=1/T
the inverse temperature.
The bare interaction is given by

\displaystyle V_{0,\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}}(k_{1},

\displaystyle k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})=\delta(k_{1}-k_{2}+k_{3}-k_{4})

\displaystyle\times\left[V_{0}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})\delta_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}\delta_{\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.\quad-V_{0}(k_{1},k_{4},k_{3},k_{2})\delta_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{4}}\delta_{\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}}\right]\,,

(11)

with
V_{0}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})=V_{0}(i\nu_{2}-i\nu_{1})
defined by Eq. (
3
).

The quantum effective action includes all quantum and statistical fluctuations of the system defined in Eq. (
8
) and can be written as

\displaystyle\Gamma[\bar{\psi},\psi]=

\displaystyle-\int\limits_{\overset{k_{1}k_{2}}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}}\bar{\psi}_{\sigma_{1}k_{1}}G^{-1}_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}(k_{1},k_{2})\psi_{\sigma_{2}k_{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\int\limits_{\overset{k_{1}k_{2}k_{3}k_{4}}{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}}}\hskip-11.38109ptV\,\psi_{\sigma_{1}k_{1}}\bar{\psi}_{\sigma_{2}k_{2}}\psi_{\sigma_{3}k_{3}}\bar{\psi}_{\sigma_{4}k_{4}}+\ldots\,,

(12)

with
V=V_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})
and further higher-order vertices represented by the dots.
The full fermionic Green’s function for the electron reads

\displaystyle G_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}}(k_{1},k_{2})=G(i\nu_{2}-i\nu_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2}-\mathbf{k}_{1})\delta_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}}\,,

(13)

\displaystyle\text{with}\ G(i\nu,\mathbf{k}):=\Big(i\nu-\epsilon({\mathbf{k}})+\mu-\Sigma(i\nu,\mathbf{k})\Big)^{-1}\,.

(14)

Here, we introduced the electron self-energy
\Sigma(i\nu,\mathbf{k})
. Further, we have redefined the chemical potential
\mu
such that it includes the Hartree contribution of the electronic self-energy and that the self-energy decays to zero at large frequencies
\Sigma(i\nu\rightarrow\pm\infty)=0
.
Thus
\mu=0
corresponds to half filling
n=1/2
for
t^{\prime}=0
and the noninteracting Van Hove filling to
\mu=4t^{\prime}
.

The fRG method introduces a scale dependence, inducing a flow that interpolates between the microscopic action and the quantum effective action.
To introduce the scale dependence we deform the bare Green’s function and introduce a scale parameter
\Lambda
into the free propagator, i.e.,
G_{0}\rightarrow G_{0}^{\Lambda}
, such that the scale-dependent quantum effective action
\Gamma^{\Lambda}
fulfills
\Gamma^{\Lambda_{\textrm{init}}}=S
and
\Gamma^{\Lambda_{\textrm{final}}}=\Gamma
.
Variation of
\Gamma^{\Lambda}
with respect to
\Lambda
leads to an exact functional flow equation with one-loop structure, describing the evolution from the microscopic initial action
S
to the final effective action
\Gamma
as a function of
\Lambda

[
86
,
58
,
59
]
.

In this work, we use a smooth frequency cutoff
[
87
]
supplemented by a scale-dependent shift in the chemical potential
\delta\mu^{\Lambda}
, which will be discussed below.
Then, the modified fermionic propagator is given by

\displaystyle G^{0,\Lambda}(\mathbf{k},i\nu):=\frac{\Theta^{\Lambda}(i\nu)}{G_{0}^{-1}(\mathbf{k},i\nu)+\delta\mu^{\Lambda}}\,,

(15)

with

\displaystyle\Theta^{\Lambda}(i\nu)=\frac{\nu^{2}}{\nu^{2}+\Lambda^{2}}\,.

(16)

being the cutoff function.

In the context of strongly correlated fermion systems, an expansion of
\Gamma^{\Lambda}
in the Grassmann fields yields an infinite hierarchy of ordinary differential equations for the one-particle irreducible vertex functions
[
58
]
.
In general, this cannot be solved exactly and one has to resort to truncation schemes.
In the so-called level-2 truncation, only the flows of the self-energy
\Sigma^{\Lambda}(k)
contributing to the scale-dependent propagator
G^{\Lambda}(k)
and the spin-SU(2) resolved two-particle interaction vertex
V^{\Lambda}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})
, cf. Eq. (
11
), are considered and will be discussed below.
The ones of the three- and higher-order particle interaction vertices are neglected.
This approximation includes all corrections up to second order in the bare two-particle interaction
\mathcal{O}(V_{0}^{2})
, and partially further corrections up to infinite loop orders generated during the flow.

IV.1
Electronic self-energy flow and frequency dependence

With SU
(2)
spin symmetry, the flow equation for the self-energy is given by

\displaystyle\hskip-5.69046pt\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}\Sigma^{\Lambda}(k)=\!\int_{p}\!S^{\Lambda}(p)\!\left(V^{\Lambda}(p,k,k,p)\!-\!2V^{\Lambda}(k,k,p,p)\!\right),

(17)

where
S^{\Lambda}(p)=\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{G}^{\Lambda}(p)|_{\Sigma=\text{const.}}
defines the single-scale propagator

\displaystyle S^{\Lambda}=G^{\Lambda}\left[\dot{\Theta}^{\Lambda}(G_{0}^{-1}+\delta\mu^{\Lambda})-\Theta^{\Lambda}\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}(\delta{\mu})^{\Lambda}\right]G^{\Lambda}\,,

(18)

(we have suppressed momentum indices for convenience of notation).
The inclusion of the electronic self-energy flow results in a Fermi-surface deformation along the flow, which leads to a flowing of the filling.
The chemical potential shift is introduced to compensate for this, following a procedure given in Refs.
[
88
,
77
]
. Specifically, the shift in the chemical potential is determined implicitly by inverting

\displaystyle\!\!n(\delta\mu^{\Lambda})\!=\!\sum_{\nu\mathbf{k}}\frac{e^{i\nu 0^{+}}}{G_{0}^{-1}(\mathbf{k},i\nu)\!+\!\delta\mu^{\Lambda}\!-\!\Theta^{\Lambda}(i\nu)\Sigma^{\Lambda}(\mathbf{k},i\nu)}\,,

(19)

where the filling
n
is fixed.
Numerically, this is done by a root finding algorithm.
The
\Lambda
-dependent change of the chemical potential imposed by this condition has to be taken into account in the single-scale propagator (
18
).
For this, the derivative in the
j
-th integration step
\Lambda_{j}
of the flow is computed by the finite difference

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}(\delta\mu)^{\Lambda_{j}}=\frac{\delta\mu^{\Lambda_{j}}-\delta\mu^{\Lambda_{j-1}}}{\Lambda_{j}-\Lambda_{j-1}}\,.

(20)

This ensures that our results will have a consistent filling.
We note that interactions generally shift the Van Hove singularity, thereby altering the filling at which it occurs.
In this study when we say we are at the Van Hove filling, we refer to the noninteracting Van Hove filling.

The self-energy is a relevant term in the RG flow, even at weak coupling, and we therefore include it in our approach.
Moreover, while power counting arguments suggest the frequency dependence to be irrelevant at weak coupling, it substantially affects the results on a quantitative level
[
89
,
88
]
.
In the one-particle irreducible scheme, no flow in the imaginary part of the self-energy is generated when the frequency dependence of the vertex is neglected
[
58
]
.
Efforts have been made toward a full frequency treatment
[
88
]
which includes the high-frequency asymptotics
[
90

IV.2
Flow of the phonon self-energy

In the electron-phonon system, a bare bosonic Green’s function can be ascribed to the phonon

\displaystyle D_{0}(q)=\frac{2\omega_{0}}{(i\Omega)^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}}\,,

(21)

with
q=(i\Omega,\mathbf{q})
a compound bosonic index, where
i\Omega
is a bosonic Matsubara frequency and
\omega_{0}
the phonon frequency appearing in the Hamiltonian (
1
).
Likewise, we define a full phonon Green’s function

\displaystyle D(q)=\frac{1}{D_{0}^{-1}(q)-\Xi(q)}\,,

(22)

with a phonon self-energy
\Xi
. Having integrated out the phonons in the problem, we expect the effects of the phonon renormalization to be implicitly present in the electron problem, as long as the frequency-dependent part of the vertex is allowed to flow.
Since we include the full frequency dependence of the vertex, we therefore implicitly include also the flow of the phonon self-energy. In fact, since the phonons in the Hubbard-Holstein model couple to the density operators, it is possible to extract the explicit phonon self-energy
\Xi
from the charge density response function of the system
\chi^{\mathrm{D}}
and thereby calculate also the renormalized phonon dispersion, see Sec.
V.4
for more details.
We note that as a post-processing computation, this applies also for other approaches than the fRG.

IV.3
Flow of the vertex

For the flow of the vertex function
V^{\Lambda}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})
, it is convenient to perform a decomposition of the vertex and calculate the flow equations of the constituents.
In this work, we utilize the recently introduced SBE decomposition
[
74
]
in which the diagrams are classified in terms of their reducibility with respect to the bare interaction.
Reducible diagrams can be interpreted as representing the exchange of a single boson corresponding to a collective fermionic excitation.
Diagrams that are not reducible, instead, can be further classified in terms of two-particle reducibility or reducibility with respect to the removal of a pair of Green-function lines (
GG
-reducibility).

Applying the SBE decomposition to systems with extended interactions requires special care
[
77
,
94
]
.
In particular, we split the bare vertex into a bosonic part
\mathcal{B}
and a fermionic part
\mathcal{F}
as will be discussed now.

IV.3.1
\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{F}
splitting

The generalization of the SBE to extended interactions requires to split the bare interaction
V_{0}
into a bosonic and a fermionic part as

\displaystyle V^{\mathrm{X}}_{0}(q,k,k^{\prime})=\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{X}}(q)+\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{X}}(q,k,k^{\prime})\,,

(23)

in the spin-diagonalized basis, for the momentum-frequency conventions we refer to Appendix
C
.
The index
\mathrm{X}\in\{\mathrm{M},\mathrm{D},\mathrm{SC}\}
stands for the physical channel, i.e., the magnetic/spin channel (M), the density/charge channel (D), and the superconducting/pairing channel (SC).
The above splitting is determined uniquely by

\displaystyle\lim_{i\nu,i\nu^{\prime}\rightarrow 0}\int_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}V^{\mathrm{X}}_{0}(q,k,k^{\prime})=\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{X}}(q).

(24)

In particular, this ensures
\mathcal{B}

(i) depends only on a single bosonic transfer momentum-frequency
q
and (ii) contains the local and instantaneous part of the bare interaction
V_{0}
.
The bare interaction of our problem (
3
) is local in space, but retarded in time.
In order to identify the instantaneous part, we Fourier transform and evaluate at
\tau=0
,

\displaystyle V_{0}(\tau\!=\!0)=\sum_{i\Omega}V_{0}(i\Omega)=U\!-\!V_{H}=U_{\textrm{eff}}\,,

(25)

where we have identified it with the effective interaction in the discussion of Section
III
.
Isolating this local part, the bare interaction (
3
) can be written as

\displaystyle V_{0}(i\Omega)=U_{\textrm{eff}}+V_{H}\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\Omega^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}}\,,

(26)

with
V_{H}
and
U_{\textrm{eff}}
as in Eqs. (
4
) and (
6

Translating this interaction into physical channels, we obtain the following natural splittings

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{SC}}(q)=U_{\textrm{eff}}\quad\mbox{}\quad

(27a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{D}}(q)=U_{\textrm{eff}}+2V_{H}\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\Omega^{2}+\omega^{2}_{0}}\quad\mbox{}\quad

(27b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{M}}(q)=-U_{\textrm{eff}}

(27c)

and

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{SC}}(k,k^{\prime})=V_{H}\frac{(\nu-\nu^{\prime})^{2}}{(\nu-\nu^{\prime})^{2}+\omega^{2}_{0}}

(28a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{D}}(k,k^{\prime})=-V_{H}\frac{(\nu-\nu^{\prime})^{2}}{(\nu-\nu^{\prime})^{2}+\omega^{2}_{0}}

(28b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{M}}(k,k^{\prime})=-V_{H}\frac{(\nu-\nu^{\prime})^{2}}{(\nu-\nu^{\prime})^{2}+\omega^{2}_{0}}\,.

(28c)

The two-particle irreducible part is given by the bare interaction

The SBE decomposition of the vertex

IV.3.3
High-frequency asymptotics

An important aspect in the numerical treatment and storage of the objects
\lambda^{\mathrm{X}}
and
M^{\mathrm{X}}
is their behavior at high frequencies.
In general, in the
\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{F}
splitted SBE it can be nontrivial, i.e.,
\lambda^{\mathrm{X}}
and
M^{\mathrm{X}}
fail to decay to zero for large

or
i\nu^{\prime}

[

]

For
k^{(\prime)}=(i\nu^{(\prime)},\mathbf{k}^{(\prime)})
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{\mathrm{X},\text{asympt}}_{\mathbf{k}}(q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\nu\rightarrow\pm\infty}\lambda^{\mathrm{X}}_{k}(q)

(32a)

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{X},\text{asympt}1}_{\mathbf{k}k^{\prime}}(q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\nu\rightarrow\pm\infty}M^{\mathrm{X}}_{kk^{\prime}}(q)

(32b)

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{X},\text{asympt}1^{\prime}}_{k\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\nu^{\prime}\rightarrow\pm\infty}M^{\mathrm{X}}_{kk^{\prime}}(q)

(32c)

\displaystyle M^{\mathrm{X},\text{asympt}2}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}(q)

\displaystyle=\lim_{i\nu^{\prime},i\nu\rightarrow\pm\infty}M^{\mathrm{X}}_{kk^{\prime}}(q)\,,

(32d)

where the last limit corresponds to taking the two limits simultaneously.
For the specific case of the Hubbard-Holstein interaction, a further simplification occurs: The asymptotics have only
s
-wave contributions; i.e., they are constant in
\mathbf{k}
and
\mathbf{k}^{\prime}
, because
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{X}}
is. For the bosonic frequencies, the asymptotics are simply those of their bare values

\displaystyle\lim_{i\Omega\rightarrow\pm\infty}w^{\mathrm{X}}(i\Omega)=\lim_{i\Omega\rightarrow\pm\infty}\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{X}}(q)=U_{\textrm{eff}}^{X}

(33a)

\displaystyle\lim_{i\Omega\rightarrow\pm\infty}\lambda_{k}^{\mathrm{X}}(q)=1

(33b)

\displaystyle\lim_{i\Omega\rightarrow\pm\infty}\hskip-5.69054ptM^{\mathrm{X}}_{kk^{\prime}}(q)=0\,.

(33c)

Knowing the asymptotic behavior of these objects is essential as it allows us to compute the quantities on finite frequency windows.
Outside
these windows, the objects are substituted by their asymptotes.
The least complicated objects

are also the most important ones: They are directly related to the susceptibilities under investigation in this work.
They are also the most sizable contributions when

is chosen to be the largest.
The fermion-boson coupling

is more complicated in comparison, depending on two frequencies, and therefore it comes second in the size of the window on which it is computed.
Finally
M^{\mathrm{X}}
is computed on the smallest frequency window.
More details are presented in Appendix

The decomposition of the vertex into channels has the advantage that the essential components

In the pure Hubbard model the inclusion of the rest function leads only to small quantitative changes of the results away from the Fermi-surface instabilities, both at weak
[
75
]
and strong coupling
[
76

For a systematic analysis of the relevance of the rest functions at a finite phonon frequency
\omega_{0}

D

We find that approaching the adiabatic limit, the impact of the rest functions becomes quantitatively more and more important.
However, qualitatively, e.g., on the type of the leading susceptibility, the results remain unaffected by their inclusion.
This is in contrast to our findings for the extended Hubbard model

For this reason, we here take into account the rest function in almost all calculations, the only exception is the large parameter scans presented in Figs.

For the latter, we expect small quantitative changes upon inclusion of the rest functions, but qualitative features will remain unaltered.

IV.4
Contributions from the three-particle vertex and Migdal-Eliashberg theory

We now comment on the partial inclusion of three-particle vertex contributions in the so-called Katanin substitution
[
78
]
, which allows to recover the ME theory.
In the flow equation (

17

In the standard level-2 truncation, the derivative of the self-energy

Here, we consider the Katanin correction
[
78
]
including the self-energy insertions in Eq. (

It leads to an improved fulfillment of the Ward identities, since it partly takes into account the flow of the three-particle vertex

The Katanin correction is particularly important in the fRG treatment of electron-phonon models in order to fully enclose the physics of ME theory
[
108
,
109
]
(see Appendix
E
).
In fact, the unbiased treatment of fluctuations from all channels on equal footing characteristic of the fRG will allow us

V.3
Phonon-induced density fluctuations effects

We now explore the impact of the phonons on the electronic problem.
To that end, we denote the density susceptibility in absence of phonons (
V_{H}=0
) as
\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}}=\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}}(q)
.
Then, introducing phonons induces a renormalization of the susceptibility, which in random phase approximation (RPA) is given by

\displaystyle\chi^{\mathrm{D}}_{\textrm{RPA}}=\frac{\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}}}{1+\absolutevalue{g}^{2}D_{0}\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}}}\,,

(44)

with
D_{0}
being the bare phonon propagator [cf. Eq. (
21
)]. In the fRG, the RPA is recovered by neglecting the flow of the fermion-boson coupling, i.e.,
\lambda^{D}\equiv 1
.
The inverse reads

\displaystyle(\chi^{\mathrm{D}}_{\textrm{RPA}})^{-1}

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}})^{-1}+V_{H}\frac{\omega_{0}^{2}}{(i\Omega)^{2}-\omega_{0}^{2}}\,.

(45)

For
i\Omega=0
, the inverse density susceptibility hence has a linear dependence on the phonon-induced effective electron-electron coupling

\displaystyle(\chi^{\mathrm{D}}_{\textrm{RPA}})^{-1}=(\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}})^{-1}-V_{H}\,.

(46)

Deviations from the RPA can then be assigned to contributions from other channels, which feed back into the density channel through renormalization of
\lambda^{\mathrm{D}}
.
We discuss this interchannel feedback in the following.

V.4
Phonon softening and
lattice instability

Lattice vibrations affect electronic correlations, and vice versa.
Although, in our treatment, we have integrated out the phonons, we can still extract information on phonon renormalization from a solution of the purely electronic problem.
A general effect induced by phonon renormalization is the phenomenon of softening, where the phonon dispersion
\omega(q)
is renormalized to
\omega_{\textrm{eff}}(q)
.
In the RPA,
\omega_{\textrm{eff}}(\mathbf{q})
can be estimated
[
117
,
118
]
through the electronic corrections to the bare phonon propagator, cf. Eq. (
21
), as

\displaystyle D_{\textrm{RPA}}

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{0}}{1+\absolutevalue{g}^{2}\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}}D_{0}}=\frac{2\omega_{0}}{(i\Omega)^{2}-\omega^{2}_{\textrm{RPA,eff}}}

(47)

with the effective dispersion

\displaystyle\omega_{\textrm{RPA,eff}}:=\omega_{0}\sqrt{1-2\absolutevalue{g}^{2}\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}}(q)/\omega_{0}}\,.

(48)

The effective dispersion
\omega_{\textrm{RPA,eff}}(i\Omega=0,\mathbf{q})
vanishes if

\displaystyle 2\absolutevalue{g}^{2}\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}}(i\Omega=0,\mathbf{q})/\omega_{0}=1

(49)

or equivalently
V_{H}\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}}(i\Omega=0,\mathbf{q})=1
.
This is indeed possible as
V_{H}\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}}
is unbounded, implying that for sufficiently large
g
(or small
\omega_{0}
),
\omega_{\textrm{RPA,eff}}
will become imaginary.
Therefore, it has been suggested
[
118
,
119
]
that Eq. (
49
) corresponds to a lattice instability even if
\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}}
is finite.
However, a more accurate treatment of the phonon renormalization within renormalized ME theory
[
116
]
was shown to prevent its occurrence.
Here, we illustrate that this problem does not appear for the exact expression of the phonon self-energy for which we present a consistent derivation in the following.

Figure 9:
Density susceptibility determined diagrammatically from the phonon self-energy:
The phonon self-energy sums up all diagrams that are
D_{0}
irreducible, i.e., the chain of
\Xi
with
D_{0}
to produce all
D
amputated diagrams that are
D_{0}
reducible [see Eq. (
50
)].
The diagrams generated this way
describe a process beginning and ending with a phonon. But since the diagrams are
D
-amputated, that can only be if the process begins and ends with bare electron-phonon vertex
g
. If we strip off the
g
factors, we end up with the full set of diagrams that make up the density
susceptibility, as the phonons couple to the density operator.

The central observation is that the density susceptibility
\chi^{D}
can be determined diagrammatically from the phonon self-energy
\Xi
as

\displaystyle-\chi^{\mathrm{D}}

\displaystyle=\absolutevalue{g}^{-2}(\Xi+\Xi\circ D_{0}\circ\Xi+\cdots)=\frac{\absolutevalue{g}^{-2}\Xi}{1-D_{0}\Xi}\,,

(50)

see Fig.
9
for a diagrammatic representation.
Inverting the above relation, we obtain an exact expression for the phonon self-energy from
\chi^{D}

[
116
,
120
]

\displaystyle\Xi=\frac{-|g|^{2}\chi^{\mathrm{D}}}{1-|g|^{2}\chi^{\mathrm{D}}D_{0}}\,.

(51)

In the Dyson equation for the full phonon propagator
D^{-1}=D_{0}^{-1}-\Xi
, we can then identify the renormalized phonon dispersion by

\displaystyle D=\frac{2\omega_{0}}{(i\Omega)^{2}-\omega_{\textrm{eff}}^{2}}

(52)

with

\displaystyle\omega_{\textrm{eff}}(q):=\omega_{0}\sqrt{1+2\Xi(q)/\omega_{0}}\,.

(53)

Note that the phonon self-energy is a monotonic function of
\chi^{\mathrm{D}}(i\Omega=0,\mathbf{q})>0
.
In particular, we have
\Xi(\chi^{\mathrm{D}}\rightarrow 0)=0
and
\Xi(\chi^{\mathrm{D}}\rightarrow\infty)=-\omega_{0}/2
.
Hence, the phonon self-energy is bounded in the normal phase

\displaystyle-\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}<\Xi(i\Omega=0,\mathbf{q})\leq 0\,.

(54)

For the renormalized phonon dispersion (
53
), this implies that (i)
\omega_{\textrm{eff}}(i\Omega=0,\mathbf{q})\leq\omega_{0}
, a property termed as phonon softening, and (ii) for finite
g
, it vanishes only in correspondence to
\chi^{\mathrm{D}}\rightarrow\infty
.
This means that in the Hubbard-Holstein model there is no dissociation between a lattice instability and an electronic density instability.

In contrast, for the RPA phonon self-energy which can be read off from Eq. (
47
), we obtain

\displaystyle\Xi_{\textrm{RPA}}=-|g|^{2}\tilde{\chi}^{\mathrm{D}}_{\textrm{RPA}}=\Xi+\mathcal{O}(g^{4})\,,

(55)

since
\chi^{\mathrm{D}}=\tilde{\chi}^{D}
in lowest order in
g
.
As
\Xi_{\textrm{RPA}}
is not bounded, the RPA renormalized phonon dispersion becomes imaginary for large electron-phonon couplings.
Evidently, this is an artifact of the RPA.

The proper inclusion of the phonon renormalization is relevant not only for a quantitatively accurate, but also for a correct qualitative description.
On one hand, the phonon softening may affect the low energy description of a system.
For example, it has been shown that a system which from purely electronic considerations can host a spin liquid, may not be able to do so in the presence of soft phonon modes
[
121
]
. On the other hand, it allows to capture the suppression of
\chi^{\mathrm{SC}}
by density fluctuations which is absent without phonon renormalization
[
115
]
.

VI
Intertwined fluctuations and isotope effects

VI.1
Fluctuation diagnostics

The magnitude of an order-parameter propagator (minus its bare value) can be taken as a measure of the strength of fluctuations.
Hence, to systematically analyze the fluctuations of the model, we introduce magnetic, density, and superconducting order-parameter fields, i.e.,
\vec{m},\rho
, and
\Delta
, respectively.
To that end, one may split the SU(2)-resolved bare interaction as

\displaystyle V_{0}(k_{1},k_{2}

\displaystyle,k_{3},k_{4})=V_{0,k_{1}k_{2}}^{\mathrm{pp}}(k_{1}+k_{3})+V_{0,k_{1}k_{4}}^{\mathrm{ph}}(k_{2}-k_{1})

\displaystyle+V_{0,k_{1}k_{2}}^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}}}(k_{3}-k_{2})-2V_{0}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})\,.

(56)

Writing the first three terms each in physical channels as in Eq. (
101
), splitting
V_{0}^{\mathrm{X}}=\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{X}}+\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{X}}
, and finally applying three Hubbard-Stratonovich transformations for each of the
\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{X}}
yields the following action
[
98
]

\displaystyle S_{\text{bos}}\left[\psi,\bar{\psi},\Delta,\Delta^{*},\rho,\vec{m}\right]=

\displaystyle-\int_{k,\sigma}\bar{\psi}_{\sigma}(k)(i\nu+\mu-\epsilon_{k})\psi_{\sigma}(k)-2U_{\textrm{eff}}\int_{k,k^{\prime},q}\bar{\psi}_{\uparrow}(k+q)\bar{\psi}_{\downarrow}(k^{\prime}-q)\psi_{\downarrow}(k^{\prime})\psi_{\uparrow}(k),

\displaystyle+\int_{q}\Delta^{*}(q)\frac{1}{\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{SC}}(q)}\Delta(q)+\frac{1}{2}\int_{q}\rho(-q)\frac{1}{\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{D}}(q)}\rho(q)-\frac{1}{2}\int_{q}\vec{m}(-q)\cdot\frac{1}{\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{M}}(q)}\vec{m}(q)

\displaystyle+\int_{k,q}\Delta(q)\bar{\psi}_{\uparrow}\left(\frac{q}{2}+k\right)\bar{\psi}_{\downarrow}\left(\frac{q}{2}-k\right)+\int_{k,q}\Delta^{*}(q)\psi_{\downarrow}\left(\frac{q}{2}-k\right)\psi_{\uparrow}\left(\frac{q}{2}+k\right)

\displaystyle+\int_{k,q,\sigma}\rho(q)\bar{\psi}_{\sigma}\left(k+\frac{q}{2}\right)\psi_{\sigma}\left(k-\frac{q}{2}\right)+\int_{k,q,\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\vec{m}\cdot\bar{\psi}_{\sigma}\left(k+\frac{q}{2}\right)\vec{\tau}_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}\psi_{\sigma^{\prime}}\left(k-\frac{q}{2}\right).

(57)

Adding a regulator to the fermionic part of the theory and deriving the equations for the one-particle irreducible vertices while truncating the flow of vertices with
N_{f}+2N_{b}>4
, where
N_{f}
,
N_{b}
is the number of fermionic, bosonic legs, yields the SBE flow equations
[
76
,
98
]
reported in Eq. (
35
).
These flow equations are equivalent to the fermionic flow equations in the level-1 truncation.
Thus the flow equations in this bosonisation scheme are free of ambiguities in the decoupling and treat all channels on equal footing, see also Ref.
[
98
]
.
Note that the bare values of the bosonic propagators
w_{0}^{\mathrm{X}}=\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{X}}
and fermion-boson couplings
\lambda^{\mathrm{X}}_{0}=1
in Eq. (
57
) coincide with the initial values of the SBE flow Eq. (
37
).
Interactions between the different bosons are mediated by the fermions through the fermion-boson couplings in Eq. (
57
) and thus the interchannel correlations between the different types of bosons are reflected in the renormalization of the fermion-boson couplings.
Indeed, freezing their flow to their initial value
\lambda^{\mathrm{X},\Lambda=0}=1
yields single-channel ladder resummations for the bosonic propagators
w^{\mathrm{X}}
.

The importance of bosonic fluctuations is determined by the difference
w^{\mathrm{X}}-w^{\mathrm{X}}_{0}
which is directly proportional to the
s
-wave susceptibility
\chi^{\mathrm{X}}
by Eq. (
31
).
The question we address in this section is the following:
How do the different fluctuations, encoded in the susceptibilities, change with the phonon frequency
\omega_{0}\sim 1/\sqrt{M_{\textrm{ion}}}
?
The straightforward answer is provided already in Figs.
4
and
5
.
However, in the bare action
\omega_{0}
enters only the density bare bosonic propagator
w^{\mathrm{D}}_{0}=\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{D}}
, whereas
w^{\mathrm{SC}}_{0}=\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{SC}}
and
w^{\mathrm{M}}_{0}=\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{M}}
do not depend on it.
Therefore, the influence of
\omega_{0}
on the superconducting and magnetic susceptibilities can only arise from the interplay between the channels, requiring a more nuanced answer to the above question.

[

\displaystyle\bar{\nabla}^{\mathrm{X}}:=\nabla^{\mathrm{X}}-U^{\mathrm{X}}_{\textrm{eff}}\,.

VII
Conclusions and outlook

Using the SBE formulation of the fRG, we have presented a detailed analysis of the Hubbard-Holstein model on the square lattice, both at half filling and finite doping.
In contrast to previous studies, the full frequency dependence of the vertex as well as the electron and phonon self-energies are accounted for.
We computed the (
s
- and
d
-wave) superconducting
\chi^{\mathrm{SC}}
, density
\chi^{\mathrm{M}}
, and magnetic
\chi^{\mathrm{M}}
susceptibilities in the
(U,V_{H})
plane (in the SBE approximation) and investigated their evolution with
\omega_{0}
from the adiabatic to the anti adiabatic limit.
We found that the screening induced by the self-energy is responsible for changing the sign of the isotope effect in the
d
-wave superconducting susceptibility
\chi^{\mathrm{dSC}}
, see Fig.
6
.
The role of the phonon-self energy, implicitly included through the renormalization of the density susceptibility, has been shown to be responsible for the nonmonotonicity of the
s
-wave
\mathrm{SC}
susceptibility as a function of the electron-phonon coupling, indicating a regime where ME theory is not valid, see Figs.
7
and
8
.
We also showed how using the exact expression of the phonon self-energy prevents the occurrence of an unphysical lattice instability without a charge-density wave.
Performing a fluctuation diagnostics, we furthermore analyzed the influence of phonons on electronic fluctuations and their interplay.
We have shown how the retardation of the phonon-mediated interaction reverses the role of the density fluctuations from being supportive to being destructive to
s
-wave superconductivity.
In addition, we supplemented the numerical results by an analytical derivation of the diagnostic matrices which allowed us to identify the key diagrams driving the influence of phonons in the different fluctuation channels.
One important conclusion that we can draw from these fluctuation-diagnostics results is
that phonon-induced charge fluctuations can suppress superconductivity even for increasing electron-phonon coupling.

On the methodological side, we illustrated that the SBE formulation of the fRG provides a suitable tool to study the complex interplay of the different fluctuation channels in the various regimes of the Hubbard-Holstein model.
In particular, the fRG description goes beyond ME theory.
Despite the substantial reduction of the computational effort provided by the SBE approximation, the application to multi-orbital systems remains challenging within a full frequency-dependent treatment of both, the vertex as well as the self-energy.
Here, promising candidates for a more efficient frequency treatment are the IR
[
124
,
125
]
or DLR
[
126
,
127
]
bases, in addition to the recently introduced quantic tensor trains
[
128
]
and tensor cross interpolation
[
129
]
.

Future directions of this work include the extension to the strong-coupling regime by exploiting the dynamical mean-field theory
[
130
,
131
]
solution as a starting point for the fRG flow
[
132
,
133
,
76
]
, the investigation of Peierls bond phonons
[
134
,
135
]
which couple to the hopping instead of the charge density, and the exploration of hexagonal-lattice models as relevant to many correlated moiré materials.

Appendix A
Acoustic phonons

In the seminal work
[
136
]
, it was argued within the framework of (Unrenormalized) Migdal-Eliashberg theory that it is the short-wavelength phonons that primarily mediate the electron-electron attraction relevant for
s
-wave superconductivity. Consequently, a model of optical/Einstein phonons was argued to be a better approximation for superconductivity in metals. As we have seen in the main text, the role of inter-channel feedback, particularly the influence of density fluctuations, plays a crucial role. A question that remains then is, to what extent would considering an acoustic/Debye phonon instead, that is gapless at
\mathbf{q}=0
, influence our conclusions?

To that end, we consider the additional case of an acoustic phonon and briefly comment on it.
We will show that the results are qualitatively the same as the ones discussed in the main text.

We consider an acoustic phonon with dispersion

\displaystyle\omega(\mathbf{q})=\frac{\omega_{0}}{2}\left(\absolutevalue{\sin(\bfq_x)}+\absolutevalue{\sin(\bfq_ y)}\right).

(81)

From Eq. (
88
), it follows that the electron phonon coupling
g(\mathbf{q})
takes the form

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{q})\sim\frac{(\mathbf{q}_{x}+\mathbf{q}_{y})V_{\textrm{el-ion}}(\mathbf{q})}{\sqrt{\omega(\mathbf{q})}}\,.

(82)

Integrating out this phonon as in appendix
B
yields an effective electron-electron interaction with a coupling strength
V_{H}(\mathbf{q})=2g^{2}(\mathbf{q})/\omega(\mathbf{q})
. Assuming that the electron-ion interaction is strongly screened such that
V_{\textrm{el-ion}}
can be approximated by a constant, we have
g(\mathbf{q})\sim\sqrt{|\mathbf{q}|}
. In the proximity of
\mathbf{q}=0
, we have
\omega(\mathbf{q})\sim\absolutevalue{\bfq}
, and thus we obtain
V_{H}(\mathbf{q})\sim\text{const.}
Therefore, the gaplessness at
\mathbf{q}=0
will not show up as a feature for

Appendix B
Effective interaction of the Hubbard-Holstein model

In this section, we briefly recall the derivation of the phonon-mediated effective electron-electron interaction in the Hubbard-Holstein model.
As a starting point one considers the ions at positions
\mathbf{R}
oscillating around their equilibrium positions
\mathbf{R}^{*}
with displacement
\mathbf{u}
in analogy to a system of coupled harmonic oscillators. The linear mode analysis of the equations of motion in momentum space yields the eigenvalue problem

\displaystyle D(\mathbf{q})\mathbf{e}_{j}(\mathbf{q})=\omega_{j}^{2}\mathbf{e}_{j}(\mathbf{q})\,,

(83)

where the eigenvectors
\mathbf{e}_{j}
describe the normal modes or the polarization of the ion oscillations identified by the phonon type or branch and
D

is the so-called dynamical matrix.
Its components are given by

\displaystyle D_{ab}(\mathbf{q}):=\sum_{{\mathbf{R}}\in\Lambda}\frac{1}{M_{\textrm{ion}}}\Phi_{ab}({\mathbf{R}})\exp(i\bfq\cdot{\bfR})\,,

(84)

with
\Phi_{ab}
the components of the Hessian of the ion-ion potential
V_{\textrm{ion-ion}}
.
Note that Eq. (
84
) implies that
\omega_{j}\propto 1/\sqrt{M_{\textrm{ion}}}
.

The components of the ion displacement from the equilibrium position are related to the phonon operators by

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}=\sum_{\mathbf{q}j}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2M_{\textrm{ion}}\omega_{j}(\mathbf{q})}}\mathbf{e}_{j}(\mathbf{q})e^{i\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{R}^{*}}\left(b_{\mathbf{q}j}+b^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{q}j}\right).

(85)

The Holstein electron-phonon interaction is then determined by the linear expansion of the electron-ion interaction [neglecting terms of
\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{u}^{2})
beyond the the phonon dispersion]

\displaystyle\!\!V_{\textrm{el-ion}}({\bf{r}}\!-\!\mathbf{R})=

\displaystyle V_{\textrm{el-ion}}({\bf{r}}\!-\!\mathbf{R}^{*})\!-\!\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla V_{\textrm{el-ion}}({\bf{r}}\!-\!\mathbf{R})\,,

(86)

where
\bf{r}
indicates the position of the electron.
In second quantization, the Hamiltonian reads

\displaystyle H_{\textrm{el-ph}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{k}\sigma\mathbf{q}j}\frac{\mathbf{e}_{j}(\mathbf{q})}{\sqrt{2M_{\textrm{ion}}\omega_{j}(\mathbf{q})}}\cdot\bra{\bfk+ \bfq, \sigma}\nabla V_{\textrm{el-ion}}\ket{\bfk, \sigma}

\displaystyle\qquad\times c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{q}\sigma}c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma}\left(b_{\mathbf{q}j}+b^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{q}j}\right).

(87)

The final expression is obtained by identifying

\displaystyle g^{j}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}:=\frac{\mathbf{e}_{j}(\mathbf{q})}{\sqrt{2M_{\textrm{ion}}\omega_{j}(\mathbf{q})}}\cdot\bra{\bfk+ \bfq, \sigma}\nabla V_{\textrm{el-ion}}\ket{\bfk, \sigma}\,,

(88)

with
g^{j}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}\propto 1/\sqrt{M_{\textrm{ion}}\omega_{j}(\mathbf{q})}\propto 1/\sqrt[4]{M_{\textrm{ion}}}
.

Integrating out the phonons, that couple to the electrons through the (modified)
displacement operators

A_{\mathbf{q}j}=b_{\mathbf{q}j}+b^{\dagger}_{-\mathbf{q}j}

and
A^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}j}=A_{-\mathbf{q}j}
, one obtains a purely electronic Hamiltonian.
In the path integral formulation of the coherent-state fields for
c^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},c_{\mathbf{k}\sigma},A^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{q}j},A_{\mathbf{q}j}
, the action reads

\displaystyle S[\bar{\psi},\psi,\phi^{*},\phi]=

\displaystyle-\int_{k\sigma}\bar{\psi}_{k\sigma}G_{0}^{-1}(k\sigma)\psi_{k\sigma}

\displaystyle+\int_{qj}\phi^{*}_{qj}D_{0}^{-1}(qj)\phi_{qj}

\displaystyle+\int_{k\sigma qj}g^{j}_{\mathbf{k}\mathbf{q}}\bar{\psi}_{k+q\sigma}\psi_{k\sigma}\phi_{qj}+\textrm{c.c.},

(89)

where we introduced
q=(\mathbf{q},i\Omega)
and
k=(\mathbf{k},i\nu)
. For simplicity, we restrict ourselves to a single phonon mode and drop the branch index in the following. The electronic bare propagator is determined by

\displaystyle G_{0}(k)=\int_{\tau}e^{i\nu\tau}\expectationvalue{\mathrm{T} c_k(\tau) c^\dagger_k}_{0}=\frac{1}{i\nu-e(\mathbf{k})},

(90)

while the phononic propagator is given by

\displaystyle D_{0}(q)=

\displaystyle-\int_{\tau}e^{i\Omega\tau}\expectationvalue{\mathrm{T} A_{q}(\tau) A_{-q}(0)}_{0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\tau}e^{i\Omega\tau}\expectationvalue{\mathrm{T} (b_{\bfq j}(\tau) + b^\dagger_{-\bfq j}(\tau))(b_{-\bfq j} + b^\dagger_{\bfq j}) }

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\tau}e^{i\Omega\tau}\left(\expectationvalue{\mathrm{T} b_{\bfq j}(\tau)b^\dagger_{\bfq j}}_{0}-\expectationvalue{\mathrm{T} b^\dagger_{-\bfq j}(\tau)b_{-\bfq j}}_{0}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{i\Omega-\omega(\mathbf{q})}+\frac{1}{-i\Omega-\omega(\mathbf{q})}=\frac{2\omega(\mathbf{q})}{(i\Omega)^{2}-\omega^{2}(\mathbf{q})},

(91)

where the terms violating the particle number conservation vanish.
We can hence integrate out the
\phi
fields by completing the square in the action.
Holstein phonons arising from lattice distortions do not depend on
k
in general.
Neglecting the
k
-dependence, the phonon-mediated electron-electron interaction reads

\displaystyle V_{H}(q)=\frac{2\absolutevalue{g_{\bfq}}^{2}\omega(\mathbf{q})}{(i\Omega)^{2}-\omega^{2}(\mathbf{q})}\,.

(92)

For Einstein or optical phonons, a dispersionless phonon mode
\omega(\mathbf{q})=\omega_{0}
and constant electron-phonon coupling
g_{\mathbf{q}}=g
is used. Then, the above expression simplifies to

\displaystyle V_{H}(i\Omega)=-V_{H}\frac{\omega^{2}_{0}}{\Omega^{2}+\omega^{2}_{0}}\,,

(93)

with
V_{H}=2\absolutevalue{g}^{2}/\omega_{0}
controlling the coupling strength. The phonon frequency
\omega_{0}>0
sets the relevant scale of the phonon dynamics with respect to the electron one.

We finally note that different conventions for the definition of the electron-phonon coupling
g
exist in the literature. In our definition we have considered
g
to be the coupling constant of the electrons to the modified displacement operators
A
which are directly related to the phonon operators
b^{(\dagger)}
. In some works,
\tilde{g}
was taken to be the coupling constant of the electrons to the positions
\mathbf{X}:=\mathbf{u}/\sqrt{M_{\textrm{ion}}}
. The two conventions are related simply by
\tilde{g}=g\sqrt{2\omega_{0}}
, with
V_{H}=\tilde{g}^{2}/\omega_{0}^{2}
in that case.

The two-particle vertex operator of a U
(1)
invariant system in the quantum effective action
reads

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\sum V_{\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}\sigma_{4}}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})c_{\sigma_{1}}(k_{1})c^{\dagger}_{\sigma_{2}}(k_{2})c_{\sigma_{3}}(k_{3})c^{\dagger}_{\sigma_{4}}(k_{4})\,,

with the only nonvanishing terms corresponding to

(98b)

(99b)

can be used to diagonalize

In the following, we provide an explanation for the quantitative failure of the SBE approximation near the adiabatic limit. The systematic increase of the importance of the multiboson contributions in that regime can be understood from the fact that the bosonic parts of the bare interaction essentially vanish

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}^{\mathrm{X}}\sim V_{H}-V_{H}\frac{\Omega^{2}}{\Omega^{2}+\omega_{0}^{2}}\rightarrow-\delta_{\Omega,0}\,,

(105)

whereas the fermionic parts become essentially constant

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{X}}\sim V_{H}\frac{(\nu-\nu^{\prime})^{2}}{(\nu-\nu^{\prime})^{2}+\omega^{2}_{0}}\rightarrow V_{H}(1-\delta_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}})\,.

(106)

In particular, for finite frequencies
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{SC}}=-\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{D}}=-\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{M}}\sim V_{H}
. Thus, the importance of ladder diagrams of
\mathcal{F}
will exceed the importance of ladders of
\mathcal{B}
. Since these ladders are contained in
M^{\mathrm{X}}
, it becomes important in proximity of the adiabatic limit. A possible way out without including the flow of the full rest functions is to use simplified flow equations for
M^{\mathrm{X}}
that generate only the
\mathcal{F}
ladders

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{M}^{\mathrm{X},{\mathcal{F}\text{-lad}}}_{nn^{\prime}}(q,i\nu,i\nu^{\prime})=

\displaystyle-\text{Sgn}\mathrm{X}\sum_{\mathclap{i\nu^{\prime\prime},m,m^{\prime}}}\mathcal{F}_{nm}^{\mathrm{X}}(i\nu-i\nu^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\times\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Pi}^{\mathrm{X}}_{mm^{\prime}}(q,i\nu^{\prime\prime})\mathcal{F}_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{X}}(i\nu^{\prime\prime}-i\nu^{\prime})\,.

(107)

If we approximate
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{SC}}(i\nu-i\nu^{\prime})=-\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{D}}(i\nu-i\nu^{\prime})\sim-\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{M}}(i\nu-i\nu^{\prime})\sim V_{H}\Theta_{\omega_{0}}(|i\nu-i\nu^{\prime}|)
, with
\Theta_{\omega_{0}}
a step function at
\omega_{0}
, the above equation simplifies near the adiabatic limit

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{M}^{\mathrm{X},{\mathcal{F}\text{-lad}}}_{\mathrm{sw},\mathrm{sw}}(q,i\nu,i\nu^{\prime})\approx-\text{Sgn}\mathrm{X}(V_{H})^{2}\sum_{\mathclap{i\nu^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{\omega_{0}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}}}\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Pi}^{\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{sw},\mathrm{sw}}(q,i\nu^{\prime\prime})\,,

(108)

where
\mathcal{R}^{\omega_{0}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}=\{i\nu^{\prime\prime}\in[-i\nu_{\text{max}},i\nu_{\text{max}}]\ |\ \ \omega_{0}<\absolutevalue{i\nu''-i\nu}\ \text{and}\ \omega_{0}<\absolutevalue{i\nu''-i\nu'}\}
with
[-\nu_{\text{max}},\nu_{\text{max}}]
the maximum frequency window. Note that
\mathcal{R}^{\omega_{0}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}
is the set of all frequencies within this window except for a few with a distance of
\omega_{0}
from
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
. Thus, sufficiently close to the adiabatic limit
\omega_{0}<\pi T
, the sums for different
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
can be calculated cumulatively

\displaystyle\ \ \ \sum_{\mathclap{i\nu^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{R}^{\omega_{0}}_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}}}\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Pi}^{\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{sw},\mathrm{sw}}(q,i\nu^{\prime\prime})=\left(\sum_{\mathclap{i\nu^{\prime\prime}}}\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Pi}^{\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{sw},\mathrm{sw}}(q,i\nu^{\prime\prime})\right)-

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Pi}^{\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{sw},\mathrm{sw}}(q,i\nu)-\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Pi}^{\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{sw},\mathrm{sw}}(q,i\nu^{\prime})+\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Pi}^{\mathrm{X}}_{\mathrm{sw},\mathrm{sw}}(q,i\nu)\delta_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}\,,

(109)

where the first term containing the sum is independent of
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}
.
With an efficient calculation of the fermionic
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{X}}
-ladders contributions we expect that the quality of the SBE approximation can be recovered also in presence of soft phonons.

Appendix E
Migdal-Eliashberg theory

Here, we review the derivation of the
ME equation from the fRG flow equations, see Refs.
[
108
]
for the symmetric and Ref
[
109
]
for the symmetry-broken state.
In the symmetric phase, the unrenormalized ME equation for the self-energy reads

\displaystyle\Sigma_{\text{ME}}(k)=\int_{k^{\prime}}G(k^{\prime})V_{0}(k^{\prime}-k)

(110)

and is solved self-consistently for
V_{0}
defined in Eq. (
3
).
In the fRG, the unrenormalised ME is obtained by approximating the vertex to contain only forward scattering contributions in the
{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}}
channel

\displaystyle V(k_{1},k_{2},k_{3},k_{4})\approx

\displaystyle\delta(k_{2}-k_{3})\delta(k_{1}-k_{4})\times

\displaystyle V^{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}}_{k_{1},k_{2}}(q=k_{3}-k_{2})\,.

(111)

Plugging this ansatz for the vertex into the self-energy flow equation, Eq. (
17
), yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}\Sigma(k)

\displaystyle=\int_{k^{\prime}}S(k^{\prime})V(k^{\prime},k,k,k^{\prime})\,,

(112)

where the other term in the flow equation
vanishes
(the nonvanishing elements have zero measure).

The flow equation for the vertex reads

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}V^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}}}_{k_{1},k_{2}}(q_{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}}=0)=

\displaystyle\ \mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}V(k_{1},k_{2},k_{2},k_{1})

\displaystyle=\int_{p}V(k_{1},p,p,k_{1})

\displaystyle(\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{G})(p)G(p)V(p,k_{2},k_{2},p)\,.

(113)

In terms of the SBE decomposition and the flow Eqs. (
35
), this equation is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}w^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}

\displaystyle=\left(w^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}\right)^{2}\sum_{m,m^{\prime},\nu}\lambda_{m}^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}(i\nu)\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ph}}_{mm^{\prime}}(q=0,\nu)\lambda^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}_{m^{\prime}}(i\nu)

(114a)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}\lambda^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}_{n}(i\nu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,m^{\prime},\nu^{\prime}}\lambda^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}_{m}(\nu^{\prime})\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Pi}^{\mathrm{ph}}_{mm^{\prime}}(q=0,i\nu^{\prime})\mathcal{I}^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}_{m^{\prime}n}(i\nu^{\prime},i\nu)

(114b)

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{M}^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}_{nn^{\prime}}(i\nu,i\nu^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,m^{\prime},\nu^{\prime\prime}}\mathcal{I}^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}_{nm}(i\nu,i\nu^{\prime\prime})\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Pi}^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\mathrm{FS}}_{mm^{\prime}}(i\nu^{\prime\prime})\mathcal{I}^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}_{m^{\prime}n^{\prime}}(i\nu^{\prime\prime},i\nu^{\prime})\,,

(114c)

where we use form-factor notation and “
{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}}
,FS” stands for “forward scattering in the
{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}}
channel”; i.e. (suppressing fermionic arguments)
w^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}=-w^{\mathrm{M}}(q=0)
,
\lambda^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}=-\lambda^{\mathrm{M}}(q=0)
,
M^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}=-M^{\mathrm{M}}(q=0)
,
\Pi^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}=-\Pi^{\mathrm{M}}(q=0)
and the irreducible vertex

\mathcal{I}^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}:=V^{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}}(q=0)-\nabla^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}=M^{{\overline{\mathrm{ph}}},\textrm{FS}}-\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{M}}
.
Besides these, the flow of the other components and other
q
values is neglected.

The derivative appearing in the right hand side of Eq. (
113
) is a total derivative (due to the Katanin-substitution).
Therefore it admits a ladder solution

\displaystyle V

\displaystyle(k_{1},k_{2},k_{2},k_{1})=V_{0}(k_{1},k_{2},k_{2},k_{1})

\displaystyle+\int_{p}V(k_{1},p,p,k_{1})G(p)G(p)V_{0}(p,k_{2},k_{2},p),

(115)

which we may insert into Eq. (
112
) to find

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Sigma}(k)

\displaystyle=\int_{k^{\prime}}S(k^{\prime})V_{0}(k^{\prime},k,k,k^{\prime})+\int_{k^{\prime},p}S(k^{\prime})V(k^{\prime},p,p,k^{\prime})G(p)G(p)V_{0}(p,k,k,p)

\displaystyle=\int_{k^{\prime}}S(k^{\prime})V_{0}(k^{\prime},k,k,k^{\prime})+\int_{p}\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Sigma}(p)G(p)G(p)V_{0}(p,k,k,p)=\int_{k^{\prime}}\left(S(k^{\prime})+G(k^{\prime})\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{\Sigma}(k^{\prime})G(k^{\prime})\right)V_{0}(k^{\prime},k,k,k^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{k^{\prime}}\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda}{G}(k^{\prime})V_{0}(k^{\prime},k,k,k^{\prime})\,.

(116)

Integrating this equation from the initial scale
\Lambda_{\textrm{init}}
to the final scale
\Lambda_{\textrm{final}}
, we recover Eq. (
110
).
