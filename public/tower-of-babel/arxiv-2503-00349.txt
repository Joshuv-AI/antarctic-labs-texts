Convergence of energy-based learning in linear resistive networks
Anne-Men Huijzer, Thomas Chaffey, Bart Besselink, Henk J. van Waarde

Abstract
Energy-based learning algorithms are alternatives to backpropagation and are well-suited to distributed implementations in analog electronic devices. However, a rigorous theory of convergence is lacking. We make a first step in this direction by analysing a particular energy-based learning algorithm, Contrastive Learning, applied to a network of linear adjustable resistors. It is shown that, in this setup, Contrastive Learning is equivalent to projected gradient descent on a convex function with Lipschitz continuous gradient, giving a guarantee of convergence of the algorithm for a range of stepsizes. This convergence result is then extended to a stochastic variant of Contrastive Learning.



I
Introduction

Backpropagation is the most popular method of training artificial neural networks. However, while artificial neural networks are inspired by biological nervous systems, it has long been observed that backpropagation is not biologically plausible
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
. Several biologically plausible alternatives to backpropagation have been proposed in the literature, among them so-called
energy-based learning algorithms

[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
]
. These algorithms apply to energy-based models, which come equipped with some generalized notion of energy, and associate to each input a minimum of this energy. The basic idea is to probe the system in two states, one free and one
clamped
, or dictated by the training data, and use the energy difference between these states as a cost function. An iterative procedure is then applied to minimise this cost function. Several clamping mechanisms and iterative procedures have been defined, among them Contrastive Learning
[
4
,
12
,
5
]
, Equilibrium Propagation
[
7
]
, Coupled Learning
[
9
]
and Temporal Contrastive Learning
[
13
]
. These algorithms all resemble gradient descent, where the gradient of the cost function is replaced by a quantity which may be computed in a distributed manner across a network.

The energy-based learning paradigm is particularly suited to learning in analog electronic devices, as they have a natural notion of generalized energy: the heat dissipated by electrical resistance (in this case, a power rather than energy). Learning in analog electronics was first investigated in the 1980s
[
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
, and has seen a recent resurgence. This is, in part, due to the ability of analog circuits to perform inference many times faster than conventional neural networks
[
20
,
21
,
22
]
. Energy-based learning algorithms offer two advantages over backpropagation in this context: firstly, gradients are estimated by probing the circuit, removing the expensive gradient computations of backpropagation and automatically compensating for any device inconsistencies; secondly, updates are made in a distributed fashion, without any centralized information, allowing the learning algorithms to be implemented directly in the analog circuit, avoiding any transport delay with a central processor
[
22
,
23
,
11
,
24
]
.

The fact that energy-based algorithms resemble gradient descent raises the question of whether these algorithms do, in fact, minimize any sensible loss function. This question was first addressed by Hinton
[
5
]
in the context of Deterministic Boltzmann Machines, where he showed that contrastive learning is equal to gradient descent in the limit of infinitesimal step size.
It was shown soon after by Movellan
[
4
]
that contrastive learning, applied to Hopfield neural networks, performs gradient descent for any step size. Similar arguments, either approximate or exact, have been made for Equilibrium Propagation
[
7
]
and Coupled Learning
[
9
]
. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no known guarantees of convergence for these algorithms.

In this paper, we treat
Contrastive Learning

[
4
,
12
,
5
]
, applied to a network of linear resistors with adjustable conductances, where two sets of node potentials correspond to the input and output, respectively. This mimics the experimental setup of
[
25
]
. Contrastive Learning works by measuring the voltage drop across each resistor when output potentials are free or clamped to a desired value dictated by the training data, and uses the difference in dissipated power between these two states as a cost function. The conductance of each resistor is adjusted according to the difference in squared voltage between these two states.
Our main result, Theorem
4
, proves that in this setting, Contrastive Learning is guaranteed to converge for a specific set of conductances. This result is developed via a set of auxiliary results, showing in particular that the contrastive cost function is convex (Lemma
5
) and its gradient is Lipschitz continuous (Lemma
7
). We furthermore extend this result to stochastic gradient descent, proving convergence of a stochastic variant of Contrastive Learning in Theorem
9
.

Our results rely on connections between learning theory, electrical circuit theory, and distributed convex optimization. A class of optimization algorithms known as
consensus algorithms
rely on alternating an averaging step across a network with a local update step
[
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
]
. This has some similarity with the situation studied here: the electrical interconnection allows information from local updates to be shared between resistors. In particular, the voltage across each resistor depends not only on its conductance, which is updated based upon local information, but also on the conductances of the other resistors in the network. This “physics-based” communication between resistors enables the convergence to a global objective by means of local updates. The connection between distributed optimization and electrical networks is classical
[
30
,
31
]
, and has recently been exploited to develop circuit simulation algorithms
[
32
,
33
]
. The analysis of optimization algorithms using control theory is a subject of recent interest
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
, and our results represent a similar approach applied to distributed learning algorithms. Our approach also bears some similarity to the use of passivity-based tools to study convergence in population games
[
37
,
38
,
39
]
.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. In Section
II
, we introduce the circuit structure and formulate the learning problem. In Section
III
, we introduce the Contrastive Learning algorithm. Our main convergence result is stated and proved in Section
IV
. In Section
V
, this result is extended to the stochastic case. In Section
VI
, several computational examples are given.

II-B

Problem statement

We consider a network of linear resistors whose conductance values are adjustable, i.e., the entries of the vector of conductances
g
can be varied, mimicking the experimental setup of
[
25
]
. We impose the physical constraint that conductance values are always greater than some lower bound
\epsilon>0
, determined by the hardware implementation. For
\epsilon>0
, we define the set
\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}\subset\mathds{R}^{B}
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}:=\{x\in\mathds{R}^{B}\,|\,x_{k}\geq\epsilon,\,k=1,2,\ldots,B\},

(11)

and we require that
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
.

In this setup, for a given vector of conductances
g
, it follows from (
7
) that the vector of output potentials
p_{O}
equals

\displaystyle p_{O}(g)=-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}p_{I},

(12)

where we recall that
G=\diag(g)
. Similarly, it can be obtained by (
8
) that the voltages in the network satisfy

\displaystyle v(g)=D^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}I\\
-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}\end{pmatrix}p_{I}.

(13)

Given the vector
p_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{I}}
of input potentials and a vector
p_{O}^{D}\in\mathds{R}^{N_{O}}
of
desired
output potentials, this paper aims to determine conductances
g^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
such that
p_{O}(g^{*})=p_{O}^{D}
. We will assume this to be possible.

Assumption 1
.

There exists a vector of conductances
g^{D}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
such that

\displaystyle p_{O}^{D}=-(D_{O}G^{D}D_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}G^{D}D_{I}^{\top}p_{I}

(14)

where
G^{D}=\diag(g^{D})
.

We stress that
p_{O}^{D}
is given, but
g^{D}
is unknown.

In other words, in this paper, we are interested in learning a desired mapping between input and output potentials by adjusting the conductances of the resistors in the network. We want to realise these updates without any central processing, i.e., by implementing the updates on the conductance values using only local controllers for each resistor. These local controllers will be designed so that they only make use of local conductance and voltage measurements. The learning objective is formalized as follows.

Objective 1
.

Consider a connected graph
\mathcal{G}
and let
p_{I}\in\mathds{R}^{N_{I}}
and
p_{O}^{D}\in\mathds{R}^{N_{O}}
. Find a sequence
\bigl(g^{t}\bigr)_{t\in\mathds{N}}
in
\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
where
, for
k=1,2,\dots,B
,

g_{k}^{t+1}
is
a function of

g_{k}^{t}
and
v_{k}\bigl(g^{t}\bigr)
, such that
g^{t}\to g^{*}
for some
g^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
satisfying
p_{O}(g^{*})=p_{O}^{D}
.

Note that, by Assumption
1
, there exists at least one
g^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
satisfying
p_{O}(g^{*})=p_{O}^{D}
. This assumption represents a form of well-posedness: there must exist a circuit configuration which realises the mapping we are trying to learn.

A classical experimental setup often used to meet Objective
1
is Contrastive Learning. In this paper, we will study this algorithm and formally prove its convergence.

\displaystyle Q(g)=\bigl(v^{D}\bigr)^{\top}Gv^{D}-v(g)^{\top}Gv(g).

Problem 1
.

Find a minimiser

\displaystyle\min_{g}

\displaystyle\>Q(g)

\displaystyle\>g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}.

A classical result of Movellan

is distributed in

for all branches

Lemma 1
.

Given the cost function
Q
defined in (

\nabla Q\bigl(g\bigr)=\bigl(v^{D}\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(v(g)\bigr)^{2}.

(17)

Proof.

Let
S(p_{I},p_{O}(g),g)
denote the total power dissipated by the network for a given set of conductances

10

\displaystyle Q(g)=\bigl(v^{D}\bigr)^{\top}Gv^{D}-S(p_{I},p_{O}(g),g).

Its gradient with respect to
g
can be computed as

\displaystyle\nabla Q\bigl(g\bigr)=\bigl(v^{D}\bigr)^{2}-\nabla S(p_{I},p_{O}(g),g)

and equals

\displaystyle\nabla Q(g)=\bigl(v^{D}\bigr)^{2}-\left(\frac{\partial p_{O}}{\partial g}\right)^{\top}\frac{\partial S(p_{I},p_{O}(g),g))}{\partial p_{O}}-\bigl(v(g)\bigr)^{2}.

It follows from Remark
1
that
\partial S(p_{I},p_{O}(g),g)/\partial p_{O}=0
, leading to the desired result (

A standard approach to solve Problem
1
is to perform (projected) gradient descent on

, using the update rule

\displaystyle g^{t+1}=P_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}\left(g^{t}-\gamma\nabla Q\left(g^{t}\right)\right),

where at each time-step

, the gradient of

is computed by (

Here,

denotes the vector of conductances at the time-step

and

to the closest vector

\displaystyle P_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}(g):=\arg\min_{\hat{g}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}\|\hat{g}-g\|

(19)

and ensures that

for all time-steps

This algorithm is illustrated in Figure

at a branch

v^{D}_{k}
and voltage

at that branch. Furthermore, updates on the voltages in the network are an immediate consequence of the physics of the electrical network and do not require supervision. The algorithm (

Algorithm 1

Contrastive Learning

Let

be given. Repeat the following steps for each time-step

1.

Compute

\displaystyle g^{t+1}=P_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}\Bigl(g^{t}-\gamma{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q\left(g^{t}\right)}\Bigr).

(20)

2.

Determine

by computing (

Algorithm

. However, proving the convergence of the contrastive learning algorithm is non-trivial and will be the main contribution of this paper.

IV
Convergence of Contrastive Learning

In the previous section, we derived a Contrastive Learning algorithm for learning in resistor networks with adjustable conductance values. In this section, we show that this algorithm generates a sequence of iterates satisfying Objective
1
.
We show that the cost function
Q
and its gradient satisfy the convexity and Lipschitz continuity properties necessary to apply standard results on the convergence of projected gradient descent, e.g.,
[
26
,
\lx@sectionsign
2.4.3]
.

IV-A

Preliminaries on fixed-point iterations

We begin by introducing some technical machinery. Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathds{R}^{B}
. A function
f
is said to be Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{C}
if there exists
K\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\|f(x)-f(y)\|\leq K\|x-y\|

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
. We call
f
non-expansive if it is Lipschitz continuous with
K=1
. Moreover,
f
is called
\theta
-averaged if we can write
f(x)=(1-\theta)x+\theta\tilde{f}(x)
for some non-expansive function
\tilde{f}
and some
\theta\in(0,1)
. Averaged functions satisfy the following property.

Lemma 2
.

(
[
26
, Thm. 27]
) Let
f_{1}:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
and
f_{2}:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
be
\theta_{1}
- and
\theta_{2}
-averaged functions with
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in(0,1)
. Then the composition of
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, i.e.,
f_{1}\circ f_{2}
, is
\theta
-averaged with

\displaystyle\theta=\frac{\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}-2\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}{1-\theta_{1}\theta_{2}}.

Next, let
\mathcal{C}
be convex, i.e., for all
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
and
\theta\in(0,1)
we have
\theta x+(1-\theta)y\in\mathcal{C}
. Then,
f:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is called convex if

\displaystyle f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\leq\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y)

for all
x,y\in\mathcal{C}
and
\theta\in(0,1)
.

A fixed-point iteration (FPI) is an algorithm of the form

\displaystyle x^{t+1}=f(x^{t})

(21)

for some function
f:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
, time-step
t=0,1,2,\ldots
, and starting point
x^{0}\in\mathcal{C}
. A vector
x\in\mathcal{C}
is a fixed point of
f
if
x=f(x)
. The set of fixed points of
f
is denoted by

\displaystyle\Fix f=\{x\in\mathcal{C}\,|\,x=f(x)\}.

When
f
is averaged, the FPI (
21
) is called a Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration and converges to a fixed point if one exists. This is recalled in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a nonempty closed convex subset of
\mathds{R}^{B}
. Assume
f:\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}
is
\theta
-averaged with
\theta\in(0,1)
and
\Fix f\neq\emptyset
. Then, (
21
) with any starting point
x^{0}\in\mathcal{C}
converges to a fixed point, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}x^{t}=x^{*}

for some
x^{*}\in\Fix f
.

Proof.

We recall that an
\theta
-averaged function
f
with
\theta\in(0,1)
equals
f=(1-\theta)I+\theta\tilde{f}
for some non-expansive function
\tilde{f}
. It follows by statement
iii)
in
[
41
, Theorem 5.14]
that the iteration

\displaystyle x^{t+1}=x^{t}+\theta(\tilde{f}(x^{t})-x^{t})

(22)

converges to a point in
\Fix\tilde{f}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}x^{t}=x^{*}

for some
x^{*}\in\Fix\tilde{f}
. Then, since (
22
) equals (
21
) and
\Fix\tilde{f}=\Fix f
, we obtain the desired result.

IV-B

Rephrasing contrastive learning as a fixed-point iteration

We introduce some new notation to write (
20
) in the form of an FPI.
We define
T:\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
as

\displaystyle T(g):=P_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}\Bigl(g-\gamma{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q(g)}\Bigr)

(23)

where
\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
is defined in (
11
) and
\gamma>0
. Using this new notation, (
20
) can be written as the FPI

\displaystyle g^{t+1}=T(g^{t})

(24)

with
t=0,1,2,\ldots
indicating the time-step, and the starting point
g^{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
. The set of fixed points of
T
reads

\displaystyle\Fix T=\{g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}\,|\,g=T(g)\}.

Now, by using known results on the convergence of FPIs, we will show that the
iteration (
24
) converges to a point in
\Fix T
, assuming
\Fix T
is
non-empty, and determine that the iteration satisfies Objective
1
.

IV-C

The main result

In this section, we show that the FPI (
24
) converges towards a point in the set
\Fix T
and the resulting sequence of conductances satisfies Objective
1
. This is formalized in the following theorem.

Theorem 4
.

Define

\displaystyle K:=\frac{2}{\varepsilon}\left(\|D_{I}\|+\sqrt{N_{I}N_{O}}\|D_{O}\|\right)^{2}\|p_{I}\|^{2}.

(25)

The algorithm (
24
) with any starting point
g^{0}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
and any
\gamma\in(0,2/K)
converges to a fixed point of
T
, that is,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}g^{t}=g^{*}

(26)

for some
g^{*}\in\Fix T
. Moreover,
g^{*}
is such that
p_{O}(g^{*})=p_{O}^{D}
.

We will prove the above result by showing that
T
is an averaged function and, hence, that (
24
) defines a Krasnosel’skiĭ-Mann iteration. Its convergence then follows by Theorem
3
. To show that
T
is averaged, we note that
T
is the composition of the functions

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(g):=g-\gamma{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q(g)}

(27)

and
P_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}
as defined in (
19
). Hence, if we can show that both
\tilde{T}
and
P_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}
are averaged, then by Theorem
2
the function
T
is averaged as well. Moreover, after showing the convergence of (
24
), we will use results from convex optimization to show that the algorithm converges to a point satisfying Objective
1
.

We observe that
\tilde{T}
has the form of one step of gradient descent. If it can
be shown that
Q
is convex and
\nabla Q
is Lipschitz continuous, then, in a similar manner to
the proof of convergence of gradient descent in
[
26
, Section 2.4.3]
, it can be proven that
\tilde{T}
is averaged. We will
prove these properties by showing that the
Hessian
\nabla^{2}Q
is
positive semidefinite and bounded on
\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
.
The
Hessian
\nabla^{2}Q:(0,\infty)^{B}\rightarrow\mathds{R}^{B\times B}
can be computed as

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}Q(g):=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\nabla Q}{\partial g_{1}}(g)&\frac{\partial\nabla Q}{\partial g_{2}}(g)&\ldots&\frac{\partial\nabla Q}{\partial g_{B}}(g)\end{bmatrix}.

(28)

We are now able to derive the following result.

Lemma 5
.

The cost function
Q
is convex and its Hessian is given by

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla^{2}Q}(g)=2\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)W(g)\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)

(29)

for any
g\in(0,\infty)^{B}
, where
W
is defined as

\displaystyle W(g):=D_{O}^{\top}(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}.

(30)

Proof.

The proof can be found in Section
A-A
.

Next, we want to show that the function
\nabla Q
is Lipschitz continuous on

\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
. Since
\nabla Q
is differentiable on
\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
it suffices
to show that the
Hessian
\nabla^{2}Q
, given by (
29
), is bounded on
\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
. To prove this, we start by showing that the matrix
-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}
has the following properties.

Lemma 6
.

If
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
, then the matrix
-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}

a)

has only nonnegative entries,

b)

is row-stochastic, i.e.,
-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}\mathds{1}=\mathds{1}
,

c)

has all its entries between
0
and
1
.

Proof.

See Section
A-B
for the proof.

We are now able to prove the following result.

Lemma 7
.

The
gradient
\nabla Q
is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathcal{C}_{\varepsilon}
with constant
K
given by (
25
).

Proof.

We will show that
\nabla Q
is Lipschitz continuous with constant
K
by proving that the Hessian (
28
) is bounded with constant
K
on
\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
, i.e.,
\|\nabla^{2}Q(g)\|\leq K
for all
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
, see
[
46
, Lemma 3.1]
.

By Lemma
5
we have that

\nabla^{2}Q
is positive semidefinite and given by
(
29
). Hence,
\|\nabla^{2}Q(g)\|=\lambda_{\max}(\nabla^{2}Q(g))
and it follows by the Courant-Fischer Theorem
[
47
, Theorem 4.2.6]
that we can write

\displaystyle\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla^{2}Q(g)}\|=\max_{x\in\mathds{R}^{B},\,\|x\|=1}x^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla^{2}Q(g)}x.

(31)

Let
x\in\mathds{R}^{B}
be such that
\|x\|=1
, then establishing a bound on
x^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla^{2}Q(g)}x
will lead to a bound on
\|{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla^{2}Q(g)}\|
. Substitution of (
29
) in
x^{\top}\nabla^{2}Q(g)x
gives

\displaystyle x^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla^{2}Q(g)}x=2x^{\top}\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)W(g)\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)x

which, by using that
W
is symmetric and hence diagonalizable, can be bounded as

\displaystyle x^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla^{2}Q(g)}x\leq 2\lambda_{\max}\bigl(W(g)\bigr)\|\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)x\|^{2}.

(32)

We are left to show that both
\lambda_{\max}\bigl(W(g)\bigr)
and
\|\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)x\|
are bounded for all
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
.

Firstly, we will derive a bound on
\lambda_{\max}\bigl(W(g)\bigr)
. We note that, since
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
, we can write

\displaystyle D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top}\geq\epsilon D_{O}D_{O}^{\top}.

Then, since both
D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top}
and
D_{O}D_{O}^{\top}
are symmetric and positive definite, we can apply
[
42
, Proposition 8.6.6]
to obtain

\displaystyle(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}(D_{O}D_{O}^{\top})^{-1}.

It follows that
W
can be bounded as

\displaystyle W(g)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}D_{O}^{\top}(D_{O}D_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}.

implying that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\bigl(W(g)\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}\lambda_{\max}\left(D_{O}^{\top}(D_{O}D_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}\right).

We observe that the matrix
D_{O}^{\top}(D_{O}D_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}
is an orthogonal projection matrix, hence its eigenvalues are either
0
or
1
. It follows that

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\left(D_{O}^{\top}(D_{O}D_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}\right)=1,

hence
\lambda_{\max}\bigl(W(g)\bigr)
is bounded as

\displaystyle\lambda_{\max}\bigl(W(g)\bigr)\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon}.

(33)

for all
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
.

Secondly, we obtain a bound on
\|\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)x\|
. We start by noticing that this norm can be bounded as

\displaystyle\|\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)x\|\leq\|\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)\|\|x\|\leq\|\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)\|,

where the last inequality follows from
\|x\|=1
. Then, since the absolute values of the diagonal entries in a diagonal matrix equal its singular values, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)\|=\max_{1\leq r\leq B}|v_{r}(g)|

which, by definition of the Euclidean norm, is bounded from above as

\displaystyle\|\diag\bigl(v(g)\bigr)\|\leq\|v(g)\|.

Moreover,
\|v(g)\|
can be bounded, using (
53
) and the triangle inequality, as

\displaystyle\|v(g)\|\leq\bigl\|D_{I}^{\top}p_{I}\bigr\|+\bigl\|-D_{O}^{\top}(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}p_{I}\bigr\|

which can be bounded by the product of matrix and vector norms as

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\|v(g)\|\leq\\
&\hskip 8.53581pt\Bigl(\bigl\|D_{I}^{\top}\bigr\|+\bigl\|D_{O}^{\top}\bigr\|\bigl\|-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}\bigr\|\Bigr)\|p_{I}\|.\end{aligned}

(34)

Then, by using Lemma
6
we will derive an upper bound on
\bigl\|-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}\bigr\|
. By definition, this norm can be expressed as

\displaystyle\left\|-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}\right\|=

\displaystyle\qquad\max_{x\in\mathds{R}^{N_{I}},\|x\|=1}\left\|-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}x\right\|,

which equals

\displaystyle\max_{x\in\mathds{R}^{N_{I}},\|x\|=1}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{N_{I}}-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}e_{k}x_{k}\right\|.

Consecutively applying the triangle inequality and using the homogeneity of the norm leads to

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\left\|-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}\right\|\leq\\
&\,\max_{x\in\mathds{R}^{N_{I}},\|x\|=1}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{I}}|x_{k}|\left\|-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}e_{k}\right\|.\end{aligned}

(35)

Lemma
6
implies that the matrix
-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}
has all its entries between
0
and
1
, hence

\displaystyle\left\|-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}e_{k}\right\|\leq\sqrt{N_{O}}.

Moreover, the Euclidean and
1
-norm are related as

\displaystyle\max_{x\in\mathds{R}^{N_{I}},\|x\|=1}\sum_{k=1}^{N_{I}}|x_{k}|\leq\max_{x\in\mathds{R}^{N_{I}},\|x\|=1}\sqrt{N_{I}}\|x\|=\sqrt{N_{I}}.

Then, by substitution of the above inequalities in (
35
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}\right\|\leq\sqrt{N_{I}N_{O}}.

(36)

Finally, combining (
32
), (
33
), (
34
), and (
36
) leads to

\displaystyle x^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla^{2}Q(g)}x

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{\epsilon}\left(\|D_{I}^{\top}\|+\sqrt{N_{I}N_{O}}\|D_{O}^{\top}\|\right)^{2}\|p_{I}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\epsilon}\left(\|D_{I}\|+\sqrt{N_{I}N_{O}}\|D_{O}\|\right)^{2}\|p_{I}\|^{2}

for all
x\in\mathds{R}^{B}
with
\|x\|=1
, hence by (
31
) we conclude that
h
is Lipschitz continuous with constant (
25
).

To show that the Contrastive Learning algorithm satisfies Objective
1
, we are left to prove that minimisers
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
of
the cost function
Q
are such that the vector of output potentials
p_{O}(g)
equals the vector of desired output potentials
p_{O}^{D}
. Here, we define a minimiser of
Q
as a point
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
satisfying
Q(g)=Q^{*}
where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q^{*}}=\min_{\bar{g}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}(\bar{g})
. We can now derive the following result.

Lemma 8
.

Any minimiser
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
of
Q
satisfies
p_{O}(g)=p_{O}^{D}
.

Proof.

We will show this result by using two consecutive steps. First, we will show that every minimiser
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
of
Q
is such that
\nabla Q(g)=0
. Second, we prove that this implies that
p_{O}(g)=p_{O}^{D}
.

We start by recalling that
\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
is a convex set and that by Lemma
5

the cost function
Q
is convex. It is then a standard result in convex optimization, see
[
44
, Section 3.1.3]
, that a point
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
is a minimiser of
Q
if and only if

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g)^{\top}(\bar{g}-g)\geq 0\text{ for all }\bar{g}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}.

(37)

Next, we recall that by assumption
1
there exists
g^{D}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
such that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g^{D})=0
. Since
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g^{D})=0
, it follows from Lemma
1
and (
13
) that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(\beta g^{D})=0
for any
\beta>1
. Therefore, we can assume without loss of generality that
g^{D}\in\text{int }\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
, where
\text{int }\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
is the interior of
\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\text{int }\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}:=\{x\in\mathds{R}^{B}\,|\,x_{k}>\epsilon,k=1,2,\ldots,B\}.

Obviously,
g^{D}\in\text{int }\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
satisfies (
37
) and is thus a minimiser of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}
.

We assume that
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
is another minimiser of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}
. We notice that if
g\in\text{int }\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
, then
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g)=0
. Namely, in the case that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g)\neq 0
, we can pick
\bar{g}=g-\alpha{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g)
with
\alpha>0
sufficiently small such that
\bar{g}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
. With this choice of
\bar{g}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
we derive
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g)^{\top}(\bar{g}-g)=-\alpha{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g)^{\top}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g)<0

which contradicts (
37
). Next, we suppose that
g\notin\text{int }\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
. By the convexity of
\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}
, we have that

\displaystyle\alpha g+(1-\alpha)g^{D}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}

and

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}(\alpha g+(1-\alpha)g^{D})\leq\alpha{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}(g)+(1-\alpha){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}(g^{D})

for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Moreover, as both
g
and
g^{D}
are minimisers of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}
, i.e.,
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}(g)={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}(g^{D})={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}^{*}
with
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}^{*}=\min_{\bar{g}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}(\bar{g})
, we can write

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}(\alpha g+(1-\alpha)g^{D})\leq{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}^{*}.

Hence,
\alpha g+(1-\alpha)g^{D}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
is a minimiser of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
. We observe that by the assumption
g^{D}\in\text{int }\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
it follows that
\alpha g+(1-\alpha)g^{D}\in\text{int }\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
for all
\alpha\in[0,1)
. Then, by using the result we have shown before, we obtain that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(\alpha g+(1-\alpha)g^{D})=0
for all
\alpha\in[0,1)
. Finally, by the continuity of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}
, we conclude that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(\alpha g+(1-\alpha)g^{D})=0
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
and hence
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g)=0
for all minimisers
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}
.

We are left to show that
p_{O}(g)=p_{O}^{D}
for all minimisers
g\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}
. To show this, we again use that
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(\alpha g+(1-\alpha)g^{D})=0
for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
and any minimiser

of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}
. This implies that

\displaystyle\bigl(v\bigl(\alpha g+(1-\alpha)g^{D}\bigr)\bigr)^{2}=\bigl(v^{D}\bigr)^{2}

(38)

for all
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Suppose that there exists a branch

such that

with

and introduce

\displaystyle f(\alpha):=v_{k}\bigl(\alpha g+(1-\alpha)g^{D}\bigr).

We note that

is a continuous function,
f(0)=v_{k}^{D}
, and

. Hence, by the intermediate value theorem there exists an

such that

. However, this contradicts (

for all

. Finally, as the graph
\mathcal{G}
is assumed to be connected we have that

, hence it follows by (

of
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}
is such that

.

.

Proof of Theorem
4

The function

) and

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(g)=(1-\theta_{2})g+\theta_{2}\left(g-\frac{2}{K}h(g)\right)

. Note, that the assumption

. Moreover, Lemma

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \bigl({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g)-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g^{\prime})\bigr)^{\top}&\bigl(g-g^{\prime}\bigr)\\

\displaystyle\left\|\Bigl(g-\frac{2}{K}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g)\Bigr)-\Bigl(g^{\prime}-\frac{2}{K}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\nabla Q}(g^{\prime})\Bigr)\right\|^{2}=\|g-g^{\prime}\|^{2}-

-averaged. Finally, Theorem

. Moreover, as

is satisfied.

The proof of Theorem
4
relies on Assumption
1
: there must exist a
g^{*}
such that

for convergence of the algorithm to be guaranteed. This seems restrictive, as it would be reasonable for a circuit to be able to
approximate
a mapping without being able to realise it precisely. Guaranteeing convergence in the absence of Assumption
1
is an interesting problem that we leave for future research.

V
Stochastic Contrastive Learning

So far, we have treated Objective
1
, which is to learn a mapping from a single input vector
p_{I}
to a single output vector
p_{O}^{D}
. In practice, however, we will most often have a collection of
n
input-output pairs, gathered in the matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{I}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}p_{I1}&p_{I2}&\cdots&p_{In}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathds{R}^{N_{I}\times n}

(41)

\displaystyle\mathbf{p}_{O}^{D}

\displaystyle:=\begin{bmatrix}p_{O1}^{D}&p_{O2}^{D}&\cdots&p_{On}^{D}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathds{R}^{N_{O}\times n}.

In this setting, given

, we define

\mathbf{p}_{O}(g):=\begin{bmatrix}p_{O1}(g)&p_{O2}(g)&\cdots&p_{On}(g)\end{bmatrix},

(42)

where

p_{O\ell}(g)=-(D_{O}GD_{O}^{\top})^{-1}D_{O}GD_{I}^{\top}p_{I\ell}

for all
\ell=1,2,\dots,n
. Now, our goal is to find a vector of conductances
g^{*}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
such that
\mathbf{p}_{O}(g^{*})=\mathbf{p}_{O}^{D}
. We will assume this to be possible, i.e., we make the blanket assumption that there exists some
g^{D}\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
satisfying
\mathbf{p}_{O}(g^{D})=\mathbf{p}_{O}^{D}
.

In the case that the size
n
of the data set is small, Contrastive Learning can be extended in a straightforward manner by averaging the cost functions
Q
over the training data set. As
n
becomes large, however, computing the gradient of this aggregate cost becomes expensive. In this section, we propose an alternative, a stochastic version of Contrastive Learning (see Algorithm
2
), and prove its convergence.

Let

be given. Let

be a nonincreasing sequence of positive stepsizes. Repeat the following steps for each time-step

1.

Select
\ell\in\{1,2,\dots,n\}

2.

Determine

by (

) with

.

Determine

Compute

\displaystyle g^{t+1}=P_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}\Bigl(g^{t}-\gamma_{t}\Bigl(\bigl(v_{\ell}^{D}\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(v_{\ell}(g^{t})\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)\Bigr).

(43)

A function
f:\mathds{R}^{n}\to\mathds{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
is called an extended-valued function. The (effective) domain of an extended-valued function
f
is the set
\dom f:=\{x\in\mathds{R}^{n}\;|\;f(x)<\infty\}
. The range of
f
is denoted
\operatorname{ran}f
. An extended-valued function
f
is called proper if it never takes the value
-\infty
and is finite somewhere. The epigraph of an extended-valued function
f
is the set
\operatorname{epi}f:=\{(x,\alpha)\in\mathds{R}^{B}\times\mathds{R}\;|\;f(x)\leq\alpha\}
. An extended-valued function is called closed if its epigraph is a closed set. Convexity of
f
is equivalent to convexity of its epigraph. The subdifferential of a convex (extended-valued) function
f
at
x
is the set

\displaystyle\partial f(x):=\{z\in\mathds{R}^{n}\;|\;f(y)\geq f(x)+z^{\top}(y-x),

\displaystyle\text{for all }y\in\mathds{R}^{n}\}.

We let
F:\mathds{R}^{n}\rightrightarrows\mathds{R}^{m}
denote a (possibly) multi-valued map from
\mathds{R}^{n}
to
\mathds{R}^{m}
. The set of zeros of such a map
F
is denoted by
\zer(F):=\{x\;|\;0\in F(x)\}
.

Let
v_{\ell}(g)
be defined by (
13
) with
p_{I}=p_{I\ell}
,
v^{D}_{\ell}
be given by (
15
) with
p_{I}=p_{I\ell}
and
p_{O}^{D}=p_{O\ell}^{D}

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}Q}_{\ell}:(0,\infty)^{B}\to\mathds{R}
by

(44)

We then define the aggregate function

\displaystyle\mathbf{h}(g)

\displaystyle:={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\nabla Q_{\ell}}

(45)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{l=1}^{n}\bigl(v^{D}_{\ell}\bigr)^{2}-\bigl(v_{\ell}(g)\bigr)^{2}.

(46)

We furthermore let
\mathds{I}_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}:\mathds{R}^{B}\to\mathds{R}\cup\{\pm\infty\}
be the indicator function of the set
\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}
, defined as

\mathds{I}_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}(x):=\begin{cases}0&x\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}\\
\infty&x\notin\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}.\end{cases}

(47)

The subdifferential of

\mathds{N}_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}:\mathds{R}^{B}\rightrightarrows\mathds{R}^{B}

\mathds{N}_{\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}}(x):=\begin{cases}\emptyset&x\notin\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}\\
\{y\;|\;y^{\top}(z-x)\leq 0\;\forall\;z\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}\}&x\in\mathcal{C}_{\epsilon}.\end{cases}

(48)

For each
\ell
, we define

to be the Lipschitz constant of

given by Lemma

p_{I}=p_{I\ell}

and

\displaystyle x^{\top}(g-g^{\ast})>0.

(50)

By assumption, there exists
g^{\ast}\in\zer(\partial f)

and

\displaystyle x^{\top}(g-y)\geq f(g)-f(y).

(51)

In particular, we can let

,
and

. We claim that, in this case,

VII
Conclusions

In this paper, we have proved convergence of Contrastive Learning when applied to a network of linear adjustable resistors. Our proof relies on
showing the convexity of the contrastive cost function, and the Lipschitzness of its gradient.
The results extend in a natural way to stochastic variants of gradient descent.

There are several promising areas for future research. Firstly, the linear network of this paper is restrictive, and an extension to nonlinear networks would allow the results to be applied to more realistic neuromorphic systems. Secondly, we anticipate the results can be extended to other energy-based learning algorithms, such as Equilibrium Propagation. This will involve the analysis of both different cost functions and optimization algorithms other than projected gradient descent, but subject to similar network constraints. Finally, applying other optimization methods to Problem 1 may yield new energy-based learning algorithms with attractive properties.
