Beyond pulmonary hypertension: expanding the therapeutic frontier in chronic thromboembolic disease
Authors: Leo Gozdecki, Stanley Swat, Manreet Kanwar
Published: 2026

Venous thromboembolism, comprising both pulmonary embolism (PE) and deep vein thrombosis, is the third most common vascular pathology worldwide after myocardial infarction and stroke (1). With the increased use of more sensitive diagnostic imaging and an aging population, the incidence of acute PE has continued to rise over last decades (2). Given these trends, it would be logical to presume the sequelae of acute PE, specifically chronic thromboembolic pulmonary disease with (CTEPH) or without (CTED) pulmonary hypertension, will similarly become more common in the future. Up to half of patients who suffer an acute PE will have persistent symptoms despite appropriate anticoagulation(3) and 2–5% will develop CTEPH. The estimated incidence of CTED is at least as high as CTEPH and potentially higher (4-6). Traditionally, CTEPH has been the focus of management strategies in both registries and clinical trials, with CTED being managed ‘conservatively’, despite many of these patients experiencing significant dyspnea particularly with exercise (7,8). Consequently, determining the ideal treatment strategy for patients with CTED has gained significant attention over the last 10 years.

Although pulmonary thromboendarterectomy (PTE) has been firmly established as treatment of choice in eligible patients with CTEPH, the role of PTE in CTED is less well defined. PTE for CTED is currently given a class IIb indication (level of evidence C) in the 2022 European Society of Cardiology (ESC)/European Respiratory Society (ERS) pulmonary hypertension guidelines (9,10). Current data is limited to single center experiences exploring various endpoints including measures of quality of life and symptomatic outcomes, surgical mortality and complications, hemodynamics and non-invasive cardiopulmonary exercise parameters (Table 1). Another key question is how to best identify patients with CTED who have a favorable risk-benefit surgical profile and would therefore benefit from PTE.

Bird et al. aimed to better address these unresolved questions with their experience at the University of California San Diego by assessing the safety and immediate efficacy of PTE for CTED (11). In this retrospective analysis, 163 patients with CTED and New York Heart Association functional class II–III symptoms who underwent PTE from 2009–2022 were compared against 870 unmatched patients with CTEPH who also underwent PTE. The procedural and post-operative results demonstrated that PTE was a safe option for carefully selected patients with CTED. There were no deaths or ECMO requirements in the CTED group and post-operative bleeding was similar (5% vs. 9%, P=0.12). Patients with CTED had significantly lower rates of reperfusion lung injury, circulatory arrest times, intensive care unit (ICU) length of stay, time on ventilator and supplemental oxygen needs on discharge, compared to patients with CTEPH. While the achievement of 0% surgical mortality is the major highlight of this study, the excellent post-operative morbidity outcomes should not be overlooked. Lower lengths of ICU stay and time on ventilator would suggest these patients were appropriately selected for surgery which is important for both quality outcomes and healthcare resource utilization. Although quality of life parameters and patient reported outcomes measures were not assessed in this study, one could speculate the lack of need for supplemental oxygen on discharge would be a major point of satisfaction for patients.

Importantly, this study expands on the hemodynamic abnormalities seen in CTED and corroborates the findings of previously published cohorts (Table 1). By definition, the hemodynamic derangements in CTED are milder in comparison to CTEPH, which was re-demonstrated in this study [mean pulmonary artery pressure (MPAP) 23 vs. 42 mmHg and pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) 2.2 vs. 6.1 Woods units (WU), respectively]. By definition, these patients have normal or near-normal resting PVR, creating uncertainty regarding the physiologic basis of their symptoms. The current study demonstrates that many CTED patients exhibit abnormal exercise hemodynamics characterized by reduced pulmonary artery compliance (CPA) and impaired vascular reserve despite relatively preserved resting measurements. Moreover, surgical intervention was associated with substantial improvement in CPA, suggesting that vascular stiffness and impaired compliance may represent a critical component of disease burden that is not adequately captured by conventional hemodynamic metrics. After PTE, there was a significant improvement in MPAP (decrease of 4 mmHg from 23 to 19 mmHg), PVR (decrease of 0.61 WU) and CPA (increase of 1.2 mL/mmHg to a normal level >4 mL/mmHg). This is important for two reasons. First, contemporary prognostication data demonstrated a continuous relationship between all-cause mortality and MPAP beginning at 19–20 mmHg which prompted a change in the definition of pulmonary hypertension at the 6th World Symposium on Pulmonary Hypertension in 2018 (18). The decrease in MPAP to 19 mmHg post-PTE seen in this study may suggest an improvement in mortality risk for these patients, but further studies will be needed given the overall mortality risk of CTED is known to be low. Secondly, this study suggests that the symptomatic and exercise improvement seen in patients with CTED post-PTE is largely driven by increases in CPA as opposed to a decrease in PVR. Prior hemodynamic investigations studying the pulmonary vasculature resistance-compliance relationship have demonstrated that dramatic decreases in CPA occur well before a clinically significant rise in PVR is observed (19-21). This suggests a low normal or mildly decreased CPA is the harbinger of early pulmonary vascular disease and a very low CPA is a marker of advanced disease. As such, CPA should be considered an indicator that reflects CTED disease status. Together, these findings provide a physiologic framework supporting why benefits of PTE in this population are observed despite relatively mild abnormalities in the traditional hemodynamics measures of MPAP and PVR. Additional questions remain unanswered, including why some patients develop limiting symptoms with only mild hemodynamic abnormalities while others do not. It also remains to be seen if PTE in this patient population limits progression to CTEPH or offers a survival benefit.

Notably, of the 99 patients with CTED undergoing exercise right heart catheterization in this study, only 63% met current criteria for exercise induced pulmonary hypertension [MPAP/cardiac output (CO) slope >3 mmHg/min/L] and 69% met the previous criteria (exercise MPAP >30 mmHg). On the other hand, the average CPA at rest was 3.6 mL/mmHg and decreased to 2.7 mL/mmHg with exercise, which normally should remain stable or >4 mL/mmHg (21). Seventy-eight percent of this cohort had a decrease in CPA with exercise. After PTE, mean resting CPA increased to a normal level of 4.8 mL/mmHg. Exercise testing post-PTE was not collected. This raises the question: should pre-operative resting and exercise CPA be more heavily considered, as opposed to traditional hemodynamics, when deciding which patients with CTED should be offered PTE? Additional future investigation into identifying an ideal CPA cutoff to estimate the risk-benefit profile with PTE is warranted. The study is retrospective, subject to referral and selection bias, and limited to a highly experienced center. The absence of a medically managed comparison group prevents assessment of the true magnitude of benefit attributable to surgery. Although immediate hemodynamic improvements are compelling, clinicians and patients are ultimately most interested in outcomes that matter directly to daily life, including exercise capacity, symptom burden, quality of life, and long-term survival, which were not assessed. Previous studies (Table 1) have evaluated functional capacity [New York Heart Association functional class and six-minute walk distance (6MWD)] in CTED pre- and post-PTE. These studies have demonstrated largely favorable results. No studies have compared functional status in CTED patients undergoing PTE versus non-operative management. This is an area primed for future investigation. Furthermore, as balloon pulmonary angioplasty continues to evolve as a treatment option for chronic thromboembolic vascular disease, comparative effectiveness studies will become increasingly important.

For decades, the management of chronic thromboembolic pulmonary vascular disease has been largely defined by the presence or absence of PH. As our understanding of CTED continues to evolve, future management may increasingly focus not only on preventing pulmonary hypertension but also on restoring pulmonary vascular reserve, improving right ventricular-pulmonary arterial coupling, and addressing the physiologic abnormalities responsible for exercise intolerance. The finding in this study reinforces the safety of surgery in carefully selected CTED patients at experienced centers and provide reassurance for clinicians who remain hesitant to recommend intervention in the absence of resting pulmonary hypertension. In doing so, we may finally move beyond a definition of disease based solely on resting hemodynamics and toward one centered on patient symptoms, functional capacity, and quality of life.
