Pediatric brain tumor patients display altered immune activation and reduced lymphopoiesis at the onset of disease
Authors: Marco Rosichini, Giada Del Baldo, Carmen Dolores De Luca, Francesca Benini, Shirley Genah, Maria Vinci, Alfredo Cerimele, Marianna Coccetti, Sara Flamini, Rita Carsetti, Antonella Cacchione, Andrea Carai, Angela Mastronuzzi, Franco Locatelli, Enrico Velardi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Optimal immune function is crucial in preventing cancer development and growth and for the success of anti-cancer therapies. Here, we characterized the peripheral immunological status of 83 steroids-naïve pediatric patients with central nervous system neoplasia at the disease onset. Tumors were classified into low-grade gliomas (LGG), high-grade gliomas (HGG), medulloblastoma, and other tumors. We revealed that glioma patients showed an altered lymphocyte pool. T-cells of HGG patients shifted from naïve to effector memory phenotype. LGG patients exhibited T-cell central memory expansion and higher T-cell activation. Interestingly, HGG patients displayed reduced thymic function. Furthermore, LGG and HGG patients showed reduced activated B-cells and suboptimal B-cell formation. Our data demonstrate that glioma patients have reduced lymphopoiesis at the disease onset, which could contribute to the systemic lymphopenia characterizing these patients. This study offers novel insights into the immunological status of brain tumor patients which may help in designing more effective treatments.

Introduction

Brain tumors are the deadliest childhood cancer, and, after leukemia, represent the second most frequent malignancy, with incidence varying by country from 1.12 to 5.14 cases per 100,0001–4. Although diagnostic and therapeutical approaches have improved over time, several factors still hinder the efficacy of treatments. These include the difficulties of complete mass removal and the immunosuppressive/tolerogenic environment created by the tumor itself5. In fact, brain tumors hamper immune cell infiltration and recognition by creating an anti-inflammatory microenvironment and by inducing tolerance and exhaustion of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes6,7. Interestingly, several brain tumors have been associated with systemic immunosuppression, particularly restricted to the T-cell compartment, characterized by peripheral lymphopenia and T-cell senescence7–11. The origins of the peripheral immunosuppression linked to brain cancers are not fully understood, as well as its connection with cancer progression and patient response to treatments.

Lymphocytes are essential effector cells in the immune response to tumors, and T-cell-mediated anti-cancer immunity plays a critical role in improving survival outcomes. A diverse pool of T-cells capable of recognizing and mounting an immune response against a wide array of antigens derived from tumor cells and pathogens is generated in the thymus during the process of T-cell receptor (TCR) recombination12. An impaired thymic function has been associated with reduced generation of naïve T-cells and TCR repertoire restriction13. This is reflected in dampened immune surveillance resulting in reduced ability to recognize tumor antigens and increased risk of opportunistic infections. In fact, a broader TCR repertoire not only increases the chances of antigen recognition and initiation of an immune response, but it has also been correlated with a better response to cancer immunotherapy14,15. To which extent thymic activity may play a role in influencing the peripheral T-cell lymphopenia observed in brain tumor patients has not been clarified yet. Interestingly, previous studies showed thymocyte maturation impairment in mouse models of glioblastoma16,17. Furthermore, reduced thymic output of CD8 T-cells has been shown to correlate with poor outcomes in glioblastoma patients8.

Despite the great heterogeneity in central nervous system (CNS) tumors, lymphopenia has been observed as a common feature in different clinical courses1,10,11,18,19. While standard therapy-related lymphopenia has been associated with shorter overall survival20, it is still not completely understood if the immune suppression is already present at the time of diagnosis, a scenario that could limit the use and development of immune therapies for this patient population. This can have important therapeutic implications given that, even though lymphopenia can reduce adverse effects, it is also associated with reduced efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors21. In contrast to the literature on adults, there is less information available on the occurrence of systemic immune suppression in pediatric patients with CNS tumors. Given that robust immune function is crucial in controlling cancer initiation and growth and for the success of anti-cancer therapies22, we reasoned that a better understanding of patient’s immune system status at the onset of the disease would guide in designing more effective and personalized treatments, and in identifying patients who would benefit from the use of immunotherapy treatments.

Therefore, in the present study, we characterized the peripheral blood immunological status of steroid-naive patients with brain tumor cancers at the time of diagnosis and assessed the endogenous process of de novo T- and B-cell formation to seek potential mechanisms contributing to the systemic lymphopenia observed in those patients.

Methods

Patient and donor characteristics

A total of 269 pediatric patients were admitted to the neuro-oncology unit at Bambino Gesù Children’ Hospital between January 2020 and February 2022. All patients were eligible for surgery and underwent resection or biopsy of their brain tumor. Blood samples were collected at the time of surgery. The medical records of the patients included: demographic information, clinical data, occurrence of other diseases, brain tumor histotype, steroid assumption at disease onset, and complete blood count at diagnosis. Patients were included in this study depending on the following criteria: (1) CNS brain tumor verified by histopathology according to 2021 WHO brain tumor classification18; (2) free of steroids for at least six months; (3) patients without concomitant immunodeficiency or autoimmune disease; (4) patients without second tumors or arrival at progression/recurrence; (4) the presence of complete blood count (CBC) at diagnosis surgery; (5) completed informed consent. Seventy-eight patients arrived at progression/recurrence and 3 patients had a second tumor. Brain tumor was not confirmed by histopathology in 3 patients. One hundred patients had received steroids or other immunosuppressive drugs at diagnosis and 5 patients had a diagnosis of immunodeficiency or autoimmune disease. These cases were excluded from the analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1). Our study was exploratory, single-center, with no a priori sample size calculation. The study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Bambino Gesù Children’s Hospital and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from patient’s parents or legal guardian.

Blood sample processing and storage

An average of two ml of peripheral blood was collected in EDTA tubes for patients and healthy donors (HD). Pharm Lyse™ lysing solution (cat. 555899, BD life sciences) was used to isolate peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). After separation, PBMCs were resuspended in CryoStor cell cryopreservation medium (cat. C2874, Merck), transferred at -80°C for 24 h and then stored in liquid nitrogen until use.

Flow cytometry

Monoclonal antibodies used for multi-parametric flow-cytometry analyses are listed in Supplementary Table 2. Data were collected using a BD FACSymphony™ A5 Cell Analyzer (BD Life Sciences). Manual gating and unsupervised clustering analysis were performed using FlowJo v10.8 Software (LCC).

TREC and KREC quantification

Genomic DNA was extracted from PBMCs using QIAamp DNA Micro Kit (Qiagen), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. T-cell receptor excision circles (TRECs) and k-deleting recombination excision circles (KRECs) were quantified to evaluate T- and B-cell neogenesis, as previously described23,24. Briefly, a first PCR reaction was performed with a mix containing primers, 0.5 to 1 μg of genomic DNA, 200 mM each 2’-deoxynucleoside 5’-triphosphate (dNTP), 2.5 mM MgCl2, 1x buffer, and 1.25 U of Platinium Taq polymerase (ThermoFisher) in 20 μL (3 minutes at 95 °C followed by 18 cycles of 95 °C for 15 seconds, 60 °C for 30 seconds, and 68 °C for 30 seconds). Quantification was made on a QuantStudio 12K Flex real-time PCR system, in duplicate with a second reaction containing 4 μL of a 1/200 or 1/2000 dilution of the first PCR product; primer; probes for albumin, signal joint (sj) TRECs (sjTRECs), coding joint (cj) KRECs (cjKRECs), sjKRECs, or 1 of the Db-Jb segments; and Takyon Low Rox master mix (Eurogentec, Seraing, Belgium) in a final volume of 10 μL (40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 seconds and 60°C for 1 minute). The sum of the 10 Db-Jb segments was finally multiplied by 1.3 to extrapolate for all of the 13 existing Db-Jb segments. Values were normalized for the genomic copy number by using albumin gene quantification. Data were expressed per 150,000 PBMCs. All primers and probes were purchased from Merck (Supplementary Table 3).

Results

Glioma patients display constricted lymphocyte pool

According to the selection criteria, 83 patients were included in the study and divided into four cohorts18: low-grade gliomas (LGG, n = 44, 53%), high-grade gliomas (HGG: n = 16, 19%), medulloblastoma (n = 9, 11%), and other CNS tumors (n = 14, 17%). The median age of the patients was 11.78 (IQR 5.88–15.44) and 57.8% were males. Detailed characteristics of patients and their tumors are reported in Supplementary Table 1. Twenty-one age-matched, HD with a median age of 10 years (IQR 6.1-13.5), from whom any clinical conditions were excluded, were included as a control group. The demographic information of patients and donors are listed in Table 1. While whole white blood cell (WBC) counts were not significantly different among the study cohorts (Fig. 1A), we observed a decrease in the absolute lymphocyte counts in the HGG group and a reduction in the relative proportion of lymphocytes in both LGG and HGG patients, when compared to the age-matched control group, whose blood count values were in line with previously published studies25–27 (Fig. 1B). This was reflected by an increase in the number of neutrophils and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) in both LGG and HGG tumor patients (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B). Complete evaluation of blood cells additionally revealed a decrease in circulating eosinophils in LGG and HGG cohorts, consistent with previous reports28 (Supplementary Fig. 2C). No major differences were found in monocytes or basophils (Supplementary Fig. 2D, E). To assess more in details the differences observed in the lymphocytes and NLR in association with specific tumur type, we focused on LGG and HGG subgroups in which at least four patients were present in the specific tumor histology. Collectively, these results are consistent with previous observations and indicate that HGG and LGG patients are characterized by decreased levels of lymphocytes which correlate with tumor grade.

T-CELL compartment shifts from naïve toward memory phenotype in glioma patients

Given the differences identified in LGG and HGG patients, we aimed at further characterizing the lymphocyte subpopulations in these two cohorts by multiparametric flow-cytometry analysis. Analyzing the T-cell compartment in LGG and HGG patients, we found no major differences in CD3+, TCRαβ+, CD4+ nor CD8+ T-cell counts (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). We performed uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) analysis to assess the overall distribution of CD4+ T-cell subsets according to their expression of a panel of T-cell differentiation and activation markers (Supplementary Table 2). Interestingly, in both patient cohorts, we observed a shift from naïve toward a memory phenotype, compared to the age-matched control group (Fig. 1C). In particular, LGG patients were characterized by an expansion of the central memory (CM, CD197+CD45RA-) T-cell population, highlighted by a rise in both absolute value and frequency (Fig. 1D and Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). Among the subtypes, except for dysembryoplastic neuroepithelial tumor (DNET) and pilocytic astrocytoma, all other LGG subsets displayed CM expansion (Supplementary Fig. 5A). On the other hand, HGG patients showed an increase in the effector memory (EM, CD197-CD45RA-) CD4+ T-cell subset, which was accompanied by trends toward a decrease in CD4+ naïve (CD197+CD45RA+) population (Fig. 1E, F). Importantly, a significant drop in recent thymic emigrants (RTE), defined as CD31+ naïve CD4 T-cells, was recorded in HGG patients (Fig. 1G).

Similarly to the CD4+ T-cell cluster, the UMAP analysis revealed a shift toward a memory phenotype in the tumor groups also in the CD8+ T-cell subsets (Fig. 1H). CM CD8+ T-cells were expanded in LGG patients (Fig. 1I and Supplementary Fig. 5B). Of interest, HGG patients were characterized by higher proportion of EM and reduced naïve cells within the CD8+ T-cell pool, when compared to the LGG group (Fig. 1J, K). Since we observed alteration in the T-cell subsets within the patient cohorts, we aimed to further investigate the activation status of these cells. We found an increase in the frequency of cells expressing the activation markers CD69 and HLA-DR in CD4+ and CD8+ populations of the LGG patients (Fig. 2A–D). In the LGG group, we also found an increase in CD38+ fraction of CM T-cells, although this phenotype was only restricted to CD8+ T-cells (Fig. 2C). Conversely, we did not find any sign of T-cell activation in the HGG cohort, as assessed by the evaluation of the CD69, HLA-DR and CD38 surface markers (Fig. 2A–D).Fig. 2Assesment of peripheral T-cell activation in HGG and LGG tumor patients and healthy controls.CD69, HLA-DR and CD38 positive cells in CD4+ Central Memory, CM (A), and Effector Memory, EM (B), and CD8+ CM (C) and EM (D) T-cells in HD (blue), LGG (green) and HGG (red) tumor patients. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.

Taken together, these results indicate that treatment-naïve glioma patients are characterized by a shift toward a memory T-cell phenotype and that T-cells of LGG patients display an enhanced T cell activation status.

Glioma patients are characterized by alterations in the B- and NK-cell compartments

Given the reduced lymphocyte counts in HGG and the decrease in the relative proportion of lymphocytes in both LGG and HGG patients (Fig. 1B), we also characterized the B- and NK-cell populations. Analyzing the B-cell compartment, we observed that the total CD19+ B-cell number remained comparable between the cohorts (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B). Further analysis of B-cell subpopulations revealed a decreased frequency of activated B-cells in both patient cohorts (Fig. 3A). Except for glioneuronal tumors, the other LGG and HGG subgroups displayed a significant reduction in activated B cells compared to the age-matched controls (Supplementary Fig. 6C). On the other hand, we found an increase in the frequency of naïve B-cells in HGG patients, while the LGG cohort displayed a certain degree of heterogeneity (Fig. 3B and Supplementary Fig. 6D). We also observed a significant reduction in the frequency of the CD38+ CD24+ transitional B-cells in HGG patients, compared to the LGG group (Fig. 3C). Evaluating the NK-cell compartment, we observed a drop in NK-cell counts in both tumor cohorts, although reaching significance only in the LGG cohort (Supplementary Fig. 7A, B). As peripheral NK cells differ in the expression of CD56 and CD57 markers along their maturation pathway 29, we analyzed early CD56bright, mature CD56dim, along with terminally differentiated CD56dim CD57+ NK-cells (Supplementary Fig. 6A). LGG patients exhibited an increase in the frequency of CD56bright NK cells (Fig. 3G). On the other hand, these patients were also characterized by a decrease in both CD56dim and CD56dim CD57+ NK cells (Fig. 3J, K).

Thus, brain tumor patients, and in particular LGG, are characterized by reduced levels of activated B-cells and CD56dim NK cells, two critical subsets associated with anti-tumor activity 29–31. Together these data highlighted that together with a shift toward a memory T-cell phenotype, glioma patients also exhibited altered B- and NK-cell compartments.

T- and B-cell neogenesis is impaired in glioma patients

Given that we detected a reduction in the frequency of naïve T-cells in the HGG cohort, which was particularly pronounced in CD4+ T-cell RTE (Fig. 1F, G), we examined if this effect was associated with a reduced egress or formation of T-cells from the thymus, which has been observed in other CNS disorders16,17. To assess thymic T-cell neogenesis, we quantified TRECs in the peripheral blood of patients and HD23,24. Beta-TRECs (βTREC) and signal joint-TRECs (sjTREC) represent extra-chromosomal DNA molecules generated during the rearrangement of the TCR beta and the TCR alpha loci, respectively32. HGG tumor patients displayed reduced thymic functionality, as demonstrated by the drop in the levels of both βTREC and sjTREC, when compared to both LGG patients and healthy controls (Fig. 4A, B). Analyzing the different LGG and HGG subgroups, we found a particularly evident defect in sjTRECs in DMG H3K27-altered and DNET patients (Supplementary Fig. 8A, B). Considering our results demonstrating reduced frequency of neo-synthetized transitional B-cells in HGG patients (Fig. 3C), to gain additional insights into the process of B-cell formation in our cohorts of patients we evaluated the endogenous process of B-cell neogenesis by quantifying the KRECs. In agreement with the flow-cytometry analysis (Supplementary Fig. 6B), we observed no differences in terms of total B-cell levels in tumor patients, measured by coding joint KREC (cjKREC) quantification (Fig. 4C). On the other hand, by quantifying signal joint KREC (sjKREC), which reflect newly generated B-cells, we found that both LGG and HGG tumor patients showed a significant reduction in B-cell neogenesis (Fig. 4D). Further stratifying patients by the specific tumor subgroups, we found that in DNET, glioneuronal tumor and DMG H3K27-altered the decreased levels of sjKREC were particularly evident (Supplementary Fig. 8C, D). We extended the KREC and TREC quantification to the medulloblastoma and the other CNS patients of our study cohort (Supplementary Fig. 8). While we observed reduced levels of TRECs in medulloblastoma patients (Supplementary Fig. 8A), this result was not confirmed by the sjTREC evaluation (Supplementary Fig. 8B), leaving doubts on the actual defect in T-cell development in this patient group. On the other hand, and similarly to other brain tumor patients, we revelead that medulloblastoma patients were also characterized by a significant drop in sjKRECs (Supplementary Fig. 8D).

Discussion

Robust immune function is crucial to provide protection against cancer and in offering the ideal conditions for the success of anti-cancer immunotherapies. Brain tumors represent the leading cause of cancer-related deaths during childhood33. In recent years, much effort has been dedicated to improve early diagnostic tools, which now integrate histologic, ultrastructural, genetic and genomic features1. This comprehensive approach is of paramount importance to efficiently characterize the extreme heterogeneity of cancers affecting the CNS, which differ in terms of clinical behaviour, treatments, and prognosis. Such improvement in both diagnostic and treatment tools has been reflected by amelioration in the survival of patients, with LGG patients now showing more than 70% 5-year progression-free survival34,35. On the other hand, patients with HGG still display poor outcomes, with 70–90% of patients dying within 2 years from diagnosis36, further highlighting the need to identify early predictive markers for tumor histology and progression. The immune system actively controls the CNS to ensure its homeostasis; however, the profound immunosuppressive nature of gliomas negatively affects the function of T lymphocytes. Although immunotherapy remains an attractive and promising therapeutic approach, a deeper understanding of the immunological status of the patients is key to increase the success chance of the therapy.

It is widely accepted that the innate and adaptive immune systems play a crucial role in immune surveillance37. During the initial stages of tumor development, neoplastic cells may induce inflammatory responses, resulting in the local infiltration of innate immune cells, such as macrophages and other antigen-presenting cells, along with NK cells38. Tumor antigens presented by local antigen-presenting cells and the tumor cells themselves may further stimulate specific cytotoxic T-cell responses and humoral B-cell responses, aimed at controlling tumor development and growth39. Nevertheless, the immune microenvironment of high-grade tumors gradually shifts towards immunosuppression, fostering immune tolerance, tumor growth, and progression. Tumor-associated macrophages represent the predominant population of immune cells in both LGG and HGG40,41, but less is known about the contributions of other immune cell populations, such as T-cells, to glioma pathobiology. It has been suggested that T lymphocytes have the capacity to serve as both promoters and inhibitors of glioma growth: these contrasting effects may arise from variations in T-cell subpopulations (CD4+ versus CD8+ T-cells), their functional status within the tumor (exhausted versus cytotoxic), or their interactions with other non-cancer cell types42. These characteristics may vary with the state of the tumor (initiation and maintenance), the particular location of the tumor and the use of target therapies43,44. While an increasing number of studies have focused on characterizing the immunological milieu in the tumor microenvironment, a broad characterization of peripheral immunity in different pediatric CNS cancers still leaks.

Investigating a cohort of pediatric patients with brain tumors at the disease onset, we found that patients with LGG and HGG showed significant alterations in their immunological status. We observed elevated NLR in LGG and similar trends in HGG. These data are in agreement with previous studies showing an elevated ratio of peripheral neutrophils-to-lymphocytes in other forms of solid cancers45–47, which may be linked to the inflammatory response at the tumor microenvironment. Importantly, NLR could represent a prognostic variable for response to immune checkpoint inhibitors for solid cancers21. High NLR has been associated with poorer overall survival in patients with glioma48–51, and it has been shown to correlate with the tumor size as well as with the disease severity 52–55. While we found high NLR in glioma patients, we did not observe association with the disease severity, presumably due to limited sample size of our cohort.

Our data also demonstrated that LGG and HGG patients were characterized by a shift from naïve to memory T phenotype. We found that CM CD4+ and CD8+ T-cells were significantly increased in LGG, while the HGG were characterized by a greater proportion of EM CD4 T-cells and reduction of the naïve T-cell pool. We also observed that LGG patients were characterized by higher expression of the T-cell activation markers CD69 and HLA-DR when compared to HD. On the other hand, no changes in T-cell activation were found in HGG patients. Our data are consistent with the concept that more aggressive neoplasia is associated with a reduced capacity of the immune system to respond or be activated by tumor antigens, as different tumor-intrinsic properties (including localization, microenvironment, and genetics) differentially shape the immune response. This is consistent with previous studies showing that the immune microenvironment in HGG is largely “immune cold” and tends to be less responsive to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy 56. Consistent with this, we also found that brain tumor patients, and in particular LGGs, displayed reduced levels of activated B-cells and CD56dim NK cells, two critical subsets associated with anti-tumor activity 29,30.

Studying the endogenous process of T and B-cell neogenesis, we found reduced thymic and medullary lymphopoiesis in CNS tumor patients, particularly evident in HGG, which may contribute to the systemic lymphopenia characterizing these patients. Indeed, by KREC quantification, we found a decrease in B-cell neogenesis in both tumor cohorts compared to the age-matched control group. Interestingly, and in agreement with the quantification of RTE by flow-cytometry, we observed a drastic drop in thymic output that was exclusive of the HGG cohort. As it represents the primary site of T-cell formation, the thymus has a pivotal role in immunity in both childhood and adulthood. Sustained thymic output is reflected in a highly diverse peripheral T-cell pool, capable of efficiently recognizing and mounting an immune response against tumor antigens. In agreement with a crucial role in tumor immunity, it has been recently reported that patients who undergo thymectomy have an increased incidence of cancer during adulthood12. Of note, CNS tumors were among the cancers whose frequency mostly increased in thymectomized patients12. Another study demonstrated that impaired thymic function is correlated with poor outcomes in adult patients affected by glioblastoma8.

Immunomodulatory therapies hold promise not only in hematologic malignancies but also in solid tumors57–60. Currently, several clinical trials are ongoing to evaluate efficacy of various forms of immunotherapies which include the use of checkpoint inhibitors, CAR T-cells, peptide and cell based vaccines56,61. At our center, a phase one clinical trial with GD2 CAR T cells (NCT05298995) is currently active for the treatment of pediatric and young adult patients with relapsed/refractory brain tumors. However, immunotherapy has been significantly less successful in treating CNS malignancies than other cancer types, and gliomas remain notoriously hard to treat. Glioma patients often exhibit significant local and systemic immunosuppression, limiting the efficacy of these therapeutic strategies. In glioblastoma patients vaccinated with dendritic cells, various immune markers, such as high CD8+T-cells, low PD-1+/CD8+ ratio, and reduced expression of PD-L1 and CTLA-4, were linked to better therapy responses and longer survival62,63. In phase II trials, patients with lower PD-1+/CD8+ ratios or low PD-L1 expression had significantly longer median overall survival64. Despite these findings, no definitive predictive biomarkers for immunotherapy responses in glioblastoma have been prospectively validated62–64.

Given that a prerequisite for successful cancer immunotherapy is the presence of an intact and functioning immune system, our study provides novel insights into the immunological status of LGG and HGG which could help in designing more effective immunotherapeutic treatments. In addition, besides its role in fostering tumor surveillance, a broad T-cell pool is strongly involved in reacting against pathogens. Brain tumor-related lymphopenia is often associated with high risk of viral and bacterial infections, which contribute to morbidity in these patients9. Furthermore, tumor-associated lymphopenia is further worsened by anti-cancer treatments (steroids and chemo-/radio-therapy) thus increasing the risk of infections and impacting survival65–69. The exploration of strategies to enhance lymphocyte count recovery after extended lymphopenia represents a key focus area in clinical research70. In addition, as pre-treatment lymphopenia is a well-known prognostic marker in several tumors71–75, strategies to enhance T-cell immunity, perhaps through the recovery of thymic function, are an attractive option to improve patients’ outcomes13. Based on available clinical data for the treatment of severe lymphopenia in cancer and non-cancer patients, rhIL-7 can represent a potent therapeutic candidate for immune reconstitution70.

Our study has some limitations. First, it is a retrospective cohort study conducted at a single site, and the findings need validation in other cohorts. Additionally, the sample size was limited and the patient groups were heterogeneous, primarily due to the stringency of our inclusion criteria, making generalizing our findings difficult. On the other hand, the strength of our study is that we focused on pediatric patients who, at the time of blood sample collection, did not receive steroids, which heavily influence leukocyte counts and relative distribution. Future studies are needed to further validate and expand our findings and correlate patient immunological status at the time of tumor diagnosis with the response to therapies and clinical outcomes. Moving forward, it will also be of particular interest to correlate the immunological profile of the tumor microenvironment with the peripheral blood of HGG and LGG patients, focusing not only on T-cells, but also on B and NK cells which also showed significant alterations in their activation and maturation stages in our study.
