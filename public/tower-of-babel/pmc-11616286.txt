Morphological data of the superior vena cava predicted by multiple linear regression equations
Authors: Yuan Wang, Jia Fu, Hao Y Li, Shi J Du, Yun Y Zhang, Hao Y Wang, Jing W Wang, Jian F Mao, Hong T Zhang, Cheng Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the main surface markers in the hemodialysis puncture catheterization pathway and the relative spatial position of venous vessels in the puncture results, providing data support for venous puncture. Seventy cadavers were studied; angles of vertical axis with superior vena cava, left and right brachiocephalic vein at the confluence and circumference of superior vena cava were the primary parameters. Bivariate correlation analysis was used to determine in relation to other body parameters, and a regression equation was also obtained. The angle of the vertical axis with the superior vena cava at the confluence was linearly correlated with the distances between the coracoid processes and the jugular notch to coracoid processes. The angle of the vertical axis with left brachiocephalic vein was linearly correlated with the thickness of thorax and the length from the upper pericardial margin to the confluence. The angle of the vertical axis with right brachiocephalic vein was correlated with clavicular midline length, acromial distance, intersection point of clavicle to clavicular midline, confluence to clavicular midline, superior edge of the blood vessels to superior edge of clavicle at the right intersection, and superior edge of pericardium to confluence. There is a linear relationship between the superior vena cava circumference and the distance from the superior vena cava to the aorta. A number of parameters influence angles of the vertical axis with superior vena cava, left and right brachiocephalic veins and the superior vena cava circumference which can provide guidance for intubation.Keywords: Superior vena cava, Brachiocephalic vein, Hemodialysis, Venous intubation

Introduction

Kidney failure is one of the leading causes of death worldwide, with more than two million people suffering from end-stage kidney disease [1]. As chronic kidney disease (CKD) has become a global epidemic, medical providers around the world are increasingly confronted with kidney disease-related problems and are eager to reverse this troubling trend [2]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) and CKD are comprehensive public health problems affecting millions of people and have rapidly increased in prevalence in recent years. Both diseases are associated with high morbidity and mortality [3].

CKD is a significant medical problem worldwide, rapidly increasing in incidence corresponding with the increases in hypertension and diabetes [4]. One of the effective treatments is hemodialysis; however, patients undergoing hemodialysis have a high risk of cardiovascular lesions [5]. Currently, there are approximately two million people on hemodialysis treatment; the number will continue to grow with the ageing of the population [6].

Unfortunately, despite technical advances in hemodialysis, the mortality of patients suffering end-stage renal disease (ESRD) is 10 to 30-fold higher than that of the general population [7]. A cluster of data in 2011 from the Maghreb showed that the mortality rate among patients varied between countries, ranging from 6% in Morocco to 10.4% in Tunisia and 12% in Algeria. The number of kidney dialysis patients in South Korea has also increased dramatically in recent years [8]. Intriguingly, Japan substantially outperforms other countries in the survival of patients undergoing dialysis. The unadjusted 5-year survival was 60% in Japan, 39% in the USA, and 41% in Europe for patients starting dialysis in the 2004–2008 period [9–11]. Where available, the data indicate high survival rates in countries with populations of predominantly Asian ethnicity: the unadjusted 5-year survival was 52% in Malaysia (2004–2008) and 44% in Taiwan (2000–2009). To our knowledge, national dialysis outcome data are not yet available for China or South Asian countries, although efforts are underway to collect the data [12].

For hemodialysis patients, central venous insertion is usually chosen although it is associated with mechanical complications, such as arterial injury and hematoma. Blood vessels used for central venous catheters include the jugular, subclavian, and femoral veins. Among them, the internal jugular vein (IJV) is the most preferred. The left internal jugular vein is slightly smaller than the right internal jugular vein [13]. Most clinicians choose the right instead of the left internal jugular vein to avoid vascular injury due to the uncertain angle between the left internal jugular vein and the subclavian vein [14]. Inserting from the left internal jugular vein and the subclavian vein is more likely to damage the vascular wall, resulting in chylothorax or chylothorax leakage [15]. Because the right internal jugular vein is thick and almost in line with brachiocephalic vein and superior vena cava, puncture and intubation of internal jugular vein should be performed on the right side [16]. The pleural parietal on the right is lower than that on the left, so there is less chance of pneumothorax during an internal jugular vein puncture. The left internal jugular vein and the subclavian vein are farther from the junction of the superior vena cava and the right atrium, and the possibility of successfully placing the catheter is much lower than on the right side. The left internal jugular vein and the subclavian vein merge to form the brachiocephalic vein which inserts into the superior vena cava at a right angle, a deep vein catheter against the superior vena cava at such an angle may cause injury of the superior vena cava.

Generally, the left internal jugular vein is thinner than the right. In addition, the left internal jugular vein overlaps with the carotid artery to a great extent, which predisposes the artery to damage. Normally, in order to avoid the above problems, the left internal jugular vein and subclavian vein should be avoided for puncture unless necessary. However, in patients undergoing long-term hemodialysis, central vein stenosis or occlusion often occurs [17]. Right subclavian catheterization can also cause intimal hyperplasia, resulting in clinically significant stenosis or occlusion in more than 40% of patients.

Thickening of the venous wall caused by an increase in the number of smooth muscle cells after prolonged catheterization has been reported. Patients with stenosis from the right internal jugular vein puncture have to choose the left subclavian vein puncture. Due to the confluence of the left internal jugular vein, the subclavian vein and the brachiocephalic vein having an uncertain angle, the puncture is more difficult and with greater risk.

We measured the angle of the vertical axis of the superior vena cava and left and right brachiocephalic veins at the confluence, to predict the relationship between the angle and body surface data to provide useful data for patients with central vein occlusion to reduce the risk of damage associated with wrong puncture location.

In summary, although there are currently clinical methods such as ultrasound and CT to assist with catheterization, it is still possible for patients to experience surgical sequelae such as arteriovenous fistula, endothelial injury, and arterial dissection. Therefore, if there is more comprehensive data to further support this, we can better avoid the occurrence of these injuries. The aim of this study is to explore the factors that affect the angle between the vertical axis of the superior vena cava and the confluence of the left and right brachiocephalic veins, providing reference for possible venous catheterization in hemodialysis patients.

Materials and methods

Research subjects

All 80 study cadaver specimens were obtained from the Human Anatomy Laboratory, Zhengzhou University. Because of damage to the superior vena cava from dissection, one specimen was excluded; therefore, 70 sets of experimental data were available for analysis.

Data collection

Thoracotomy was performed on the cadavers to measure and collect general parameters, which included height, thorax thickness, nipple spacing, clavicular midline spacing, coracoid spacing, acromion spacing, the distance from the jugular notch to the coracoid, left and right clavicle intersection distance at the midline, jugular notch to thyroid cartilage distance, intersection point of the blood vessel edge to the right supraclavicular edge distance, jugular notch to the supraclavicular edge distance, left and right head arm clavicle intersection to the length of the manubrium, pericardium edge to the length of the sternum, left and right brachiocephalic vein circumference, superior vena cava circumference., the distance from the superior vena cava to the atrium, the distance from the superior vena cava to the aorta, the distance from the superior vena cava to the right apex of the lung, the angle from the superior vena cava to the aorta, the angle from the superior vena cava to the right apex of the lung, the longest diameter of the atrium and the circumference of the aorta.

Data measurement

Four basic parameters were collected: height, chest thickness (measured along the cross-section from the xiphoid process point), coracoid process distance, highest point of coracoid process, and distance from the jugular vein incision. The measurement point for beak distance remains unchanged.

After measuring the distance of the coracoid process and placing the thumb pin in position, we continued to use this point. Other parameters were measured as follows:

Distance between the midline of clavicle; acromion process distance; jugular notch to coracoid process; distance from the point of intersection of right cephalic arm clavicle to midline; distance from confluence point to central line; distance from confluence point to thyroid cartilage; distance from upper edge of blood vessel to upper edge of clavicle at right intersection; distance from confluence point to upper clavicle; length from the point of intersection of the clavicle of the left forearm to the point of confluence; length from the point of intersection of right cephalic arm clavicle to the point of confluence; length from pericardium upper edge to confluence point; Circumference of left brachiocephalic vein; circumference of right brachiocephalic vein; circumference of superior vena cava in 49 human specimens (shown in Fig. 1 and Fig. 2 and Table 1). Later we measured the distance from the superior vena cava to the atrium, the distance from the superior vena cava to the aorta, the distance from the superior vena cava to the right apex of the lung, the angle from the superior vena cava to the aorta, the angle from the superior vena cava to the right apex of the lung, the longest diameter of the atrium and the circumference of the aorta in 21 human specimens.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 21.0 statistical software was used for data analysis. The mean ± standard deviation (MSD) was calculated to measure the data that were consistent with normal distribution, while median (quartile spacing) was used for non-normally distributed data. Single-factor correlation was analyzed by Spearman or Pearson simple linear correlation. Multiple linear regression analysis was applied to the correlation factors. Test level α = 0.05.

Linear correlation analysis

The angle of the vertical axis of superior vena cava at the confluence had a linear correlation with the following parameters: the coracoid process spacing and the jugular notch to coracoid process spacing (R = 0.046, -0.144; P value was 0.017, 0.016, respectively). The angle of the vertical axis at the confluence site and the left brachiocephalic vein was correlated with the thickness of thorax and the length from the upper pericardial margin to the confluence site (R value was 0.279, -0.072; P values were 0.010, 0.030, respectively), the angle of the vertical axis at the confluence point and the right brachiocephalic vein was correlated with the clavicular midline length, acromial distance, the distance between the clavicle intersection point and the midline of the right brachiocephalic vein, the distance between the confluence point and the midline of the right brachiocephalic vein, the distance between the superior edge of the vessels and the superior edge of the clavicle, and the distance of the superior edge of the pericardium to the confluence point were correlated (R values were 0.285, -0.044, -0.107, -0.399, 0.372, 0.045, respectively; P values were 0.003, 0.016, 0.024, 0.001, 0.008, 0.014, respectively); there is a linear relationship between the superior vena cava circumference and the distance from the superior vena cava to the aorta (R = 0.672 respectively. Results of multiple linear regression analysis

The angle of the vertical axis of superior vena cava and the left and right brachiocephalic veins at the confluence point and height were used as independent variables for further multiple linear regression analysis, with the inclusion variable level α = 0.10. The test level for excluding variables was α = 0.20 using the step forward method for statistical analysis. Other parameters (coracoid process space, distance of the jugular notch to coracoid process) that predicted the angle of the vertical axis of the superior vena cava at the confluence, were entered into the regression equation, these parameters are the main factors influencing the angle of the vertical axis of the superior vena cava at the confluence. The following expression was obtained: Y (the angle of the vertical ax-is of the superior vena cava at the confluence) = 9.800 + 0.061X1—0.126X2. The parameters (thorax thickness, the length from the upper margin of the pericardium to the confluence) that predicted the angle between the left brachiocephalic vein and the vertical axis at the confluence, were entered into the regression equation, these are the main factors influencing the angle between the left brachiocephalic vein and the vertical axis at the confluence. The equation is expressed as: Y (the angle between the left brachiocephalic vein and the vertical axis of the confluence) = 63.148 + 0.225X1—0.236X2. Discussion

In this study, we measured the angle between the vertical axis of superior vena cava and left and right brachiocephalic veins at the confluence together with the height of cadaver specimens and studied the relationship between them. The maximum superior vena cava diameter was significantly correlated with height (Pearson's r = 0.28, P = 0.008) [18]. The results showed that parameters, such as coracoid process spacing and jugular notch to coracoid process spacing were linearly correlated with superior vena cava circumference. Thorax thickness, length from pericardium upper edge to confluence point were linearly correlated with the angle of right brachiocephalic vein with the vertical axis at the confluence. Clavicular midline spacing, acromial peak spacing and other parameters, were all positively correlated with the angle of the right brachiocephalic vein with the vertical axis at the confluence.

Because different hemodialysis patients have different body sizes, parameters such as acromion spacing, distance from the left and right intersections of the brachiocephalic vein and other parameters also vary. For patients who are thin and have narrow acromion spacing, the circumference of the superior vena cava is relatively narrow. Therefore, we can predict the angle of the vertical axis of the superior vena cava and the left and right brachiocephalic veins by observing and measuring the acromion distance and other parameters of the patients to provide support for safer venous cannulation.

Knowing the angle of the vertical axis of the superior vena cava with left and right brachiocephalic veins is important for hemodialysis patients when venous intubation is to be performed, as this information helps reduce the risk associated with the serious complications that greatly contribute to total mortality in kidney disease [19–21]. Vascular access complications remain an important cause of morbidity and mortality in patients on hemodialysis [22, 23]. Long-term use of catheters is also associated with complications [24]. We can predict the angle of the vertical axis of the superior vena cava with the left and right brachiocephalic veins based on the equation.

For patients with central venous obstruction caused by long-term venous catheterization, the left subclavian vein puncture is the alternative option. With the left subclavian vein and the internal jugular vein converging into brachiocephalic vein at an uncertain angle, and differences in body parameters, the blind puncture in left subclavian vein could easily cause failure of intubation and complications such as pneumothorax and chylothorax [25]. Therefore, we can predict the circumference of the superior vena cava by measuring body surface parameters and vena-related data. Since there are differences between cadaver specimens and living humans, further human studies are needed to evaluate the circumference of the superior vena cava.

For central venous obstruction in hemodialysis patients, we usually choose the right internal jugular vein needle, so through body surface data we can predict the angle of the vertical axis of the superior vena cava and right brachiocephalic vein at the confluence. The body surface data provides for more accurate insertion of the needle, so as to reduce errors that could lead to complications such as pneumothorax.

In conclusion, the distance between the coracoid processes and the jugular notch to the coracoid process are the main factors that influence the angle of the vertical axis of the superior vena cava at the confluence. The thickness of thorax and the length from the upper margin of pericardium to the confluence were the main factors influencing the angle of the vertical axis with the left brachiocephalic vein at the confluence. The clavicular midline, acromial distance, the distance between the clavicular intersection and the midline, the distance between the confluence and the midline, the distance between the superior edge of the vessels at right intersection and the superior edge of the clavicle, and the distance of the superior edge of the pericardium to the confluence are the main factors influencing the angle of the vertical axis of the confluence and the right brachiocephalic vein, and these factors showed a linear correlation.

As reported by Peres and Czepizak it was recommended that right infraclavicular subclavian catheters should be inserted at a height of 10–2 cm, right internal or external jugular catheters at a height of 10 cm, and left external jugular catheters at a height of 10 + 4 cm. However, the insertion method that was used in these studies was the puncture method, not vessel incision [26, 27]. Therefore, before intubation, we should measure and evaluate these parameters to provide guidance for intubation.

Limitation

This article aims to collect data on the surface and internal blood vessels of the catheterization pathway in order to avoid damage to patients and subsequent complications caused by improper operation during hemodialysis catheterization. The data used in this article are all from human specimens, and there may be some errors compared to humans. Therefore, the data in this article is only used as auxiliary data reference during catheterization, and more clinical data support is needed in the future.
