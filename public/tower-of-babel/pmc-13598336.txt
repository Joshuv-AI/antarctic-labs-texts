Leveraging an Antimicrobial Stewardship Collaborative and Urgent Care Center Medication-dispensing Kiosks to Support Shorter Antibiotic Durations: a Multisite, Retrospective Evaluation
Authors: Timothy P Gauthier, Ethan A Lobo, Corey M Frederick, Harrison Phan, Lauren C Russell, Michael DeCoske, Richard Levine, Yenny Ceballos, Ernesto Sanz-Martinez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOutpatient medication dispensing kiosks that contain antibiotics are proliferating. This study aimed to evaluate how their implementation in urgent care centers (UCCs) alongside antimicrobial stewardship program (ASP) collaborative engagement can influence antibiotic prescribing.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 20 UCCs, evaluating antibiotic electronic prescriptions (eRx) ordered in January–June 2019 (preintervention period) versus January–June 2024 (postintervention period). In late 2019, kiosks containing antibiotics were placed in UCCs, with initial kiosk inventories based on historical eRx volumes. Serendipitously, an outpatient ASP collaborative was established around the same time. Between the preintervention/postintervention periods, numerous programmatic interventions were implemented to promote rational antibiotic prescribing, including kiosk inventory modifications, education, and stakeholder engagement. Data on eRx prescribing for 12 antibiotics were obtained. The primary outcome was antibiotic days of therapy (DOT) per eRx. Secondary outcomes included antibiotic-specific changes, kiosk utilization, and an estimate of the intervention’s impact.ResultsIn the preintervention period, 38 593 eRx were included with 325 400 DOT, as compared to 45 608 eRx with 355 502 DOT in the postintervention period (8.4 vs 7.8 DOT/eRx, respectively; P < .001). In the preintervention period 8649 eRx (22%) were routed to a kiosk, compared with 25 414 eRx (56%) in the postintervention period (P < .001). An estimated 33 340 DOT were avoided in the postintervention period.ConclusionsEngagement of an antimicrobial stewardship collaborative along with implementation of UCC kiosks containing antibiotics may support prescriber practices that align with stewardship principles and safer outpatient antibiotic use. For healthcare systems considering kiosk adoption, early and sustained ASP involvement in inventory decisions appears critical, which the contents of this article may serve to support as a template.Keywords: antibiotic stewardship, antibiotics, antimicrobial stewardship, kiosk, outpatient

To address the threat of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) to public health worldwide it is necessary to engage in antimicrobial stewardship efforts across all healthcare settings. According to the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 80%–90% of human antibiotic use occurs in the outpatient setting, and at least 28% of antibiotics prescribed in the outpatient setting are not needed [1]. It is no surprise that outpatient antimicrobial stewardship has emerged as a critical area of opportunity to fight the progression of AMR, with numerous review articles, regulatory interest, and a call to action published in recent years [2–4]. The strength of evidence supporting outpatient stewardship interventions is low to moderate, and there is a need to better understand how to promote rational antibiotic use in this realm.

Prescribing behaviors in outpatient care may be influenced by a variety of factors, including medication availability. One tool that has emerged in urgent care centers (UCCs) is decentralized automated dispensing systems (referred to herein as “kiosks”), which house a finite inventory of nonnarcotic prepackaged prescription medications. In Florida, these systems were first authorized for use in 2019, motivated by their potential to improve convenience and medication access [5]. Kiosks have been shown to reduce prescription abandonment rates, with 1 study finding that 100% of antibiotic prescriptions sent to a UCC kiosk were retrieved within the same day, compared with just 54% of those sent to a community pharmacy (P < .001)] [6, 7]. Kiosks containing antibiotics have the potential to support more rapid treatment initiation and enhance patient satisfaction. However, there is a dearth of literature investigating the impact of kiosks on clinical care as it relates to the use of antimicrobials.

The relevance of medication kiosks within the healthcare landscape is likely to increase. According to the Urgent Care Association's white paper on the urgent care industry [8], the number of UCCs in the United States in 2014 was 7220, increasing each year to 14 382 as of 2023. According to a 2024 report, kiosk technology is poised for expanded use within the US market over the coming years, with an expected market valuation of $758 million (USD) by 2033, compared with the 2024 valuation of $516 million [9]. Kiosk systems are used in Asia and Europe, with one company reporting >4 million prescriptions dispensed as of 2020 [10]. Adoption of kiosks within UCCs is likely to influence medication prescribing practices, including for antibiotics. Given the finite space within kiosks, engagement of antimicrobial stewardship programs (ASPs) and leveraging of the CDC’s Core Elements of Antibiotic Stewardship along with the published literature may support optimal inventories and, consequently, influence prescribing decisions. Medication-dispensing kiosks may influence prescribing behavior through structural constraints, such as finite inventory and predefined quantities, which can function as prescribing “defaults.” When aligned with antimicrobial stewardship principles, these constraints may serve as a novel mechanism to promote guideline-concordant antibiotic durations.

The current study aimed to evaluate how implementation of medication kiosks in UCCs along with ASP collaborative engagement can influence antibiotic prescribing. We secondarily aimed to develop a framework for other healthcare systems to leverage as they adopt such technologies and make inventory decisions about what medications, doses, and durations of therapy will be available.

METHODS

Practice Description

In late 2019, an outpatient ASP collaborative was launched to promote best practices regarding the use of antimicrobials in ambulatory care settings within a large healthcare system (southern Florida) that includes 11 hospitals and >100 outpatient centers. This collaborative included stakeholders from UCCs, emergency medicine, primary care, and pharmacy services, supported by a pharmacist and a physician with infectious diseases expertise. A meeting cadence was established as every other month, including a priority item for each calendar year, development of educational materials, establishing order sets, standing items, and other emerging issues (eg, coronavirus disease 2019 [COVID-19]). The ASP collaborative annual goals included focusing on fluoroquinolones (2020), acute respiratory tract infection management (2021–2022), urinary tract infection (2023–2024), and acute bronchitis (2025). Education to prescribers was provided electronically, via text messaging, at staff meetings, and as part of free open-access on-demand continuing medical education [11]. Provider-specific data was disseminated through UCC leadership to engage those with the most opportunities for improvement.

Kiosks containing select antibiotics had been placed within system UCCs in late 2019, provided by InstyMeds (Minneapolis, Minnesota) for a cash price of $8 per antibiotic prescription. Electronic prescriptions (eRx) sent to kiosks were not able to modify the antibiotic dose or volume of tablets to be dispensed, but instructions for dosing frequency and duration could be edited when entering the prescription details. If the former occurred, patients were instructed on the proper disposal of excess medication. The inventory of the machines was initially developed from gross prescription volume data without ASP engagement. As the ASP collaborative matured, kiosk inventory management eventually become a standing every-other-month meeting item. This included periodic kiosk utilization reviews, analysis of electronic antibiotic prescribing patterns, and approval for kiosk inventory item addition, deletion, or modification. UCC-wide ASP initiatives, such as a “shorter is better” educational campaign, aligned well with efforts to provide evidence-based antibiotics for common ailments [12, 13]. Study Design

This was a retrospective quality improvement project that analyzed eRx data from 20 UCCs operating within a single healthcare system. Study periods were defined as January–June 2019 (before ASP involvement) versus January–June 2024 (after ASP involvement). Prescription data were extracted from Cerner Discern Analytics 2.0, which provided information on eRx from UCCs, including oral antibiotics. Variables captured in the report included drug name, quantity, duration, dose, frequency, route of administration, encounter date, encounter location, prescribing provider, provider specialty, encounter identification number, and whether prescriptions were routed to a kiosk or a community pharmacy.

The primary study outcome was antibiotic days of therapy (DOT) prescribed per eRx. Secondary outcomes included total DOT/eRx by drug, total number of eRx in each time frame, number of eRx by drug, number of eRx routed to kiosks versus community pharmacies, estimated DOT avoided in the postintervention period, and total number of UCC visits for any reason. To estimate the DOT avoided in the postintervention period, the expected postintervention DOT were calculated. This was done by multiplying the DOT/eRx rate observed during the preintervention period against the number of eRx in the postintervention period for each antibiotic individually, after which each result was summed to give a cumulative expected postintervention DOT. Comparing this expected postintervention DOT with the actual postintervention DOT allowed for an estimate of impact. Given the retrospective preintervention/postintervention design and concurrent implementation of multiple stewardship initiatives, this study was not designed to attribute causality to any single intervention.

All UCC amoxicillin, amoxicillin–clavulanic acid, cefdinir, cefuroxime, cephalexin, ciprofloxacin, clindamycin, doxycycline, levofloxacin, metronidazole, nitrofurantoin, and sulfamethoxazole-trimethoprim (SMX/TMP) eRx for adults in the preintervention/postintervention windows were eligible for inclusion. Levofloxacin was never in the kiosks but was included as it was associated with the internal fluroquinolone campaign. Doxycycline and clindamycin were in the kiosks during the preintervention and postintervention periods but were never modified. Exclusion criteria included encounters missing data for drug name, duration, or quantity, prescriptions for oral solutions, nonoral dosage forms, and prescriptions written for a “once” dose. Azithromycin, fluconazole, oseltamivir, and valacyclovir were not included in this analysis because minimal impact on their DOT was anticipated given their typical indications and durations of treatment. The project was approved and deemed quality assurance by the local institutional review board, and patient consent was not required

Statistics

Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel pivot tables to develop basic descriptive statistics. A 2-tailed t test for independent samples was used to evaluate differences in the median DOT per encounter between study periods. Statistical significance was defined as P < .05. A sample size was calculated with the assistance of a nurse scientist using G*Power software. Based on a small effect size (Cohen d = 0.2; α = .05; power, 0.8), a minimum sample of 786 prescriptions (394 per group) was determined to be required. Analyses did not adjust for clustering by UCC or prescriber, which may have resulted in underestimation of variance

RESULTS

There were 167 901 total UCC patient encounters for any reason in the preintervention period, compared with 196 158 in the postintervention period (an increase of 17%). Of the 43 874 antibiotic eRx in the preintervention period and 52 360 in the postintervention period, the common reasons for exclusion were patient <18 years old (in 3617 and 6422 respectively), report field missing drug name, duration, or quantity (1331 and 1320, respectively), and nonoral dosage form or oral suspension product (332 and 145, respectively). Included eRx in the analysis totaled 38 593 in the preintervention period and 45 608 in the postintervention period, with 325 400 and 355 502 DOT in each group, respectively. The preintervention period had 8.4 DOT/eRx and the postintervention period, 7.8 DOT/eRx (P < .001). In the preintervention period 8649 of 38 593 eRx (22%) were routed to a kiosk, compared with 25 414 of 45 608 (56%) in the postintervention period (P < .001).

DISCUSSION

Kiosks within UCCs offer a contemporary strategy for enhancing patient access and may have a meaningful impact on antimicrobial durations. In the current study, we estimated that the kiosks and ASP engagement supported a meaningful reduction in antibiotic DOT prescribed into the community with substantial use of kiosks. This supports reduced potential for toxic effects and collateral damage of antibiotic use (ie, AMR and Clostridioides difficile infection). The interdisciplinary ASP collaborative with UCC medical leadership support was no doubt critical to the success of this work, as it served as a focal point when making kiosk inventory modifications, some of which took considerable time and convincing to enable or disable. Findings from this study have important implications for supporting antimicrobial stewardship in the outpatient urgent care setting and may help establish a foundational approach to kiosk inventory management.

The first modification to the kiosks made in 2020 added a 3-day ciprofloxacin option for lower urinary tract infection, while retaining an existing 10-day option for pyelonephritis. This corresponded with a local campaign in 2020, which aimed to reduce fluoroquinolone prescribing. The work ultimately produced a 46% reduction in fluoroquinolone prescriptions from UCCs when comparing 2022 with 2019 [14]. Other fluoroquinolones are still intentionally kept out of the kiosks to discourage their use, out of concern for antibiotic resistance and potential toxic effects. The success of the fluoroquinolone campaign and literature from the shorter-is-better literature helped garner leadership support to replace the 10-day ciprofloxacin option with a 7-day option in 2022 [12]. Removal of ciprofloxacin is currently being considered, and it is probable that other organizations may want to exclude fluoroquinolones from the start.

The change in nitrofurantoin duration from 7 to 5 days in the kiosks is supported by guidelines and was suggested to the ASP collaborative in October 2020, but it was not implemented until 2022 as it took time for all stakeholders to agree [15]. This change had a clear impact on prescribing patterns, as the nitrofurantoin DOT/eRx rate went from 7 days in the preintervention to 5 days in the postintervention period. This change was rapidly endorsed locally by leadership, is likely a simple initiative most organizations can adopt, and may be an easy early win.

Cefdinir was not initially in the kiosks but was added in 2022 and then ultimately removed after the postintervention period. The addition was secondary to provider prescribing volume and preference, with the eventual removal supported by ASP educational efforts and current literature shared with prescribers, such as the article entitled “Things we do for no reason: prescribe cefdinir for treatment of common infections” [16]. With nearly 20 000 cefdinir DOT in the postintervention period, it is hoped that the current kiosk inventory will shift much of that to shorter-course cefuroxime, but that remains to be seen. With cefuroxime, the 7- and 10-day options in the postintervention period probably contributed to the reduction in the durations selected, given that cefuroxime changed by a rate of 1.8 DOT/eRx, aligning with more preference for shorter courses for community-acquired infections [13].

Less than a 10% shift in DOT/eRx was seen with amoxicillin, amoxicillin–clavulanic acid, and cephalexin despite shorter courses being made available for each of these antibiotics. Note, however, that these are all antibiotics with a high volume of use, totaling >200 000 DOT in the postintervention period. These are examples of changes that may not achieve a large shift in DOT/eRx but may produce a meaningful change in DOT given the total volume of use. On the topic of cephalexin, the ASP collaborative is currently considering placing cefadroxil in the kiosks as a first-generation cephalosporin with more convenient dosing frequency, although this change would not reduce the DOT.

For doxycycline there was a substantial increase in prescription volume between the preintervention and postintervention periods, which was driven by updates to the CDC sexually transmitted infection guidelines preferring doxycycline over azithromycin for chlamydia [17]. A shorter doxycycline course is being considered to align with current guideline recommendations for community-acquired pneumonia [18]. With clindamycin there was almost no change in volume or DOT/eRx rate. The ASP collaborative is currently considering adding a 7-day duration for clindamycin to the kiosk.

In 2024 the healthcare system's 11 hospitals made the switch from thrice-daily to twice-daily metronidazole dosing for most patients, which prompted the most recent kiosk inventory that supports a duration of either 7 or 10 days [19]. Metronidazole remains among the antibiotics with the lowest volume of use in the kiosks, which is predictable given the routine infection types encountered within UCCs. The lower frequency leads to a reduced risk of toxicity and allows for more convenient dosing, although this unlikely to have an impact on AMR at a low eRx rate. When implementing kiosks, organizations should account for inpatient initiatives (eg, twice-daily metronidazole), potentially leveraging the machines to promote consistency in practice across care areas.

For SMX/TMP, a 3-day duration was added to the initial 7-day duration, to support guideline-directed treatment of cystitis [15]. This appeared to have minimal impact on durations of SMX/TMP (−3% change in DOT/eRx), and even in 2026 this remains a low-use option for us. In addition, rates of Escherichia coli resistance to SMX/TMP in our 2024 UCC antibiogram show 66% susceptibility, understandably leading UCC providers to have limited enthusiasm for SMX/TMP as an empiric option.

This study is strengthened by its novelty, large sample, and broad scope. It succeeds in laying the groundwork for fundamental kiosk management, and the current kiosk inventory may be helpful to others, but there are several important limitations. First, eRx were routed to a kiosk or community pharmacy, but we could determine the abandonment rate or whether the medication was taken when picked up or if this potential increased waste production. Second, data could not capture antibiotic indications or the appropriateness of therapy. While we are enthusiastic about new technology to enhance the patient experience in a time of illness, it is unknown whether kiosks may positively or negatively contribute to inappropriate antibiotic use and prescribing. While the kiosks do include a variety of options for symptomatic management of acute respiratory infections, it is unlikely that they would improve delayed prescribing (ie, delayed antibiotic eRx filling) [20].

Third, 3% pre-intervention and 2.5% post-intervention eRx were excluded from the analysis due to missing data. These are low rates but could have affected the results. Fourth, sometimes the quantity of medication in the bottle from the kiosk exceeds what the patient requires. It is unknown how often patients take the correct duration, properly dispose of these excess pills, or save them for later. The efforts made in this work to reduce antibiotic durations support mitigation of this concern, but they do not completely resolve it, and this continues to be a journey rather than a final destination for inventory management to support ASP efforts. Fifth, we are unable to separate the impact of the stewardship initiatives from that of the kiosk changes. Broader national shifts toward shorter antibiotic durations, updated clinical guidelines, and practice changes related to the COVID-19 pandemic may also have contributed to observed differences independent of the interventions studied. Finally, comparing durations between prescriptions from the kiosks and those not from the kiosks may have provided additional insights not included here.

In conclusion, engagement of an antimicrobial stewardship collaborative along with implementation of UCC kiosks containing antibiotics may support prescriber practices that align with stewardship principles and safer outpatient antibiotic use. For healthcare systems considering kiosk adoption, early and sustained ASP involvement in inventory decisions appears critical, which the contents of this article may serve to support as a template. Absent such engagement, kiosk technology alone may risk reinforcing suboptimal prescribing practices.
