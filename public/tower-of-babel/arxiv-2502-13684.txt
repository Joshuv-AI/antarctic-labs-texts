Divyansh Agrawal, Plamen Stefanov

Abstract
. We study the ray transform L L over null (light) rays in the pseudo-Euclidean space with signature ( n ′ , n ′′ ) (n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}) , n ′ ≥ 2 n^{\prime}\geq 2 , n ′′ ≥ 2 n^{\prime\prime}\geq 2 . We analyze the normal operator L ′ ​ L L^{\prime}L , derive an inversion formula, and prove stability estimates. We show that the symbol p ⁡ ( ξ ) p(\xi) is elliptic but singular at the light cone ℒ \mathcal{L} with conormal singularities there. We analyze L L as a Fourier Integral Operator as well. Finally, we compare this to the Minkowski case.

1.
Introduction

The purpose of this article is to study the light ray transform in the pseudo-Euclidean space. We consider
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n=n^{\prime}+n^{\prime\prime}
, with
n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}\geq 2
. Throughout this article, we identify
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime\prime}}
, and denote the points in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by
x=(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime\prime}}
, similarly for vectors and covectors. We assume that the metric
g
is given by

g=\mathrm{diag}(\underbrace{-1,\dots,-1}_{n^{\prime}},\underbrace{1,\dots,1}_{n^{\prime\prime}}),

and, in particular, has signature
(n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime})
.
The case
n^{\prime}=1
(or
n^{\prime\prime}=1
but not
n^{\prime}=n^{\prime\prime}=1
which is not interesting) gives us the Minkowski metric, and the light ray transform for it has been studied already even for more general Lorentzian metrics, see, e.g.,
[
17
,
2
,
13
,
18
,
21
,
22
,
20
,
23
]
.
Consider the operator

(1.1)

Lf(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}f\,\mathrm{d}s,

which integrates
f
along null-lines (also called light line/ray) with respect to
g
, satisfying
g(\dot{\gamma},\dot{\gamma})=0
,
\dot{\gamma}\not=0
, and, say,
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Recall that any line in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
can be parameterized by

(1.2)

t\mapsto x+t\theta,\quad\text{for }x,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

For such a line to be a null line, we require
g(\theta,\theta)=0
,
\theta\not=0
, or
|\theta^{\prime}|=|\theta^{\prime\prime}|\not=0
. Thus, we have the following parameterization for the light ray transform:

(1.3)

Lf(x,\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x+t\theta)\,\mathrm{d}t\quad\text{~ for ~}x,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\text{~with~}|\theta^{\prime}|=|\theta^{\prime\prime}|\not=0,

and we require below
x\cdot\theta=0
and
|\theta^{\prime}|=|\theta^{\prime\prime}|=1
.
Thus, the transform studied here is the restriction of the usual ray transform to null-lines with respect to
g
, and a generalization of the Light ray transform. The problem of recovering
f
from the knowledge of
Lf
is formally overdetermined by
(2n-3)-n=n-3
variables since
Lf
depends on
n-1+n-2
variables, see (
2.5
) below, and
f
depends on
n
variables.

The transform
L
appears naturally as the bicharacteristic one, restricted to functions
f(x)
on the base, related to the generalized ultra-hyperbolic operator
P=\Delta_{x^{\prime}}-\Delta_{x^{\prime\prime}}
, see
[
16
]
. In particular, its inversion would recover a compactly supported potential
V
from Cauchy data for
P+V
on a boundary of a smooth domain containing
\supp V
by
[
16
, Theorem 1.2]
.
Also,
L
belongs to the class of the generalized Radon transforms, formulated first by Guillemin
[
7
,
8
]
. Restricted versions of them to
n
-dimensional complexes of curves has been studied microlocally in
[
5
,
6
,
4
]
. Its analytic version has been studied recently in
[
14
]
establishing support theorems. The same transform has been studied in
[
11
]
, where it is shown that
L
is injective on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
using the Fourier Slice Theorem.

In sections
3
,
4
, we analyze the normal operator
L^{\prime}L
and show that it is a convolution, hence a Fourier multiplier with some
p(\xi)
which is positively homogeneous of order
-1
, continuous but singular at the light cone
\mathcal{L}
in
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
with conormal singularities but still elliptic when either
n^{\prime}\geq 3
or
n^{\prime\prime}\geq 3
. In section
4.3
we compute the principal symbol of
p
as a conormal distribution at
\mathcal{L}
. When
n^{\prime}=n^{\prime\prime}=2
,
p(\xi)
has a logarithmic blow-up at
\mathcal{L}
; still elliptic in the sense that it has a lower bound
C/|\xi|
. In both cases, we present an inversion formula and prove a stability estimate which requires a modified Sobolev space when
n^{\prime}=n^{\prime\prime}=2
. The symbol
p
does not seem to have a closed-form formula, except in some particular dimensions (see section
3.4
), and we present several representations.

In section
5
, we analyze
L
briefly as a Fourier Integral Operator (FIO) to fit it into the generalized Radon transform framework of Guillemin. We do not get deeply into this analysis since the singularity of
p(\xi)
at the light cone suggests the need to use a specialized calculus which is beyond the scope of this work. We refer to the remarks following Theorem
5.1
. Finally, in section
6
, we give a brief comparison to the Minkowski case.

2.
Setup and uniqueness

2.1.
Setup

We call the cone

\mathcal{L}:=\{(\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}|\;|\theta^{\prime}|=|\theta^{\prime\prime}|\not=0\}

the
light cone
(in
T\mathbb{R}^{n}
); and we use the same notation for the cone
|\xi^{\prime}|=|\xi^{\prime\prime}|\not=0
in the cotangent bundle since
g^{-1}
is the co-metric formally looking the same as
g
. If we start with
\theta
not restricted to be of fixed length, and non-restricted
x
in (
1.3
), we would have, for
a\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
,

(2.1)

Lf(x,a\theta)=\frac{1}{|a|}Lf(x,\theta).

Due to this scaling property and the fact that
|\theta^{\prime}|=|\theta^{\prime\prime}|
, we impose the restriction
|\theta^{\prime}|=|\theta^{\prime\prime}|=1
. Thus, we now have

(2.2)

\theta=(\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}.

We trivially have, analogous to the corresponding property of the X-ray transform, for
x,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
s\in\mathbb{R}

(2.3)

Lf(x+s\theta,\theta)=Lf(x,\theta).

We take
x\perp\theta
. Our operator now is defined as

(2.4)

Lf(x,\theta)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x+t\theta)\,\mathrm{d}t,

where
\theta=(\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}
,
x=(x^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}}\times\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime\prime}}
with
x\cdot\theta=0
. The variety of those
(x,\xi)
will be called
\Sigma
, i.e.,

(2.5)

\Sigma:=\left\{(x,\theta)|\;x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1},\;x\cdot\theta=0\right\}.

One can think of
\Sigma
as the normal bundle of
\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}
, with the standard smooth structure on it. We equip it with the measure
\,\mathrm{d}H_{\theta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})
, where
\,\mathrm{d}H_{\theta}(x)
is the standard Lebesgue measure on the plane
\theta^{\perp}
and
\,\mathrm{d}S(\cdot)
denotes the surface measure on the unit spheres in the corresponding variable.

2.2.
Fourier Slice Theorem

We now proceed to derive the Fourier Slice theorem for the operator (
2.4
). To fix our notation, we recall the Fourier transform. For
f
in the Schwartz class
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the Fourier transform is defined as

(2.6)

\mathcal{F}f(\xi)=\widehat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

and the inverse Fourier transform is given as

(2.7)

\mathcal{F}^{-1}f(x)=\widecheck{f}(x)=(2\pi)^{-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}f(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

We similarly define the Fourier and inverse Fourier transforms on a hyperplane through the origin, say
\theta^{\perp}
, denoted
\mathcal{F}_{\theta^{\perp}}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\theta^{\perp}}
respectively. More precisely, for
x,\xi\in\theta^{\perp}
,

(2.8)

\begin{split}\mathcal{F}_{\theta^{\perp}}f(\xi)&=\int_{\theta^{\perp}}e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}H_{\theta}(x),\\
\mathcal{F}^{-1}_{\theta^{\perp}}f(x)&=(2\pi)^{1-n}\int_{\theta^{\perp}}e^{\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}f(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}H_{\theta}(\xi),\end{split}

where
\,\mathrm{d}H_{\theta}
denotes the natural
n-1
dimensional (Lebesgue) measure on
\theta^{\perp}
.

Theorem 2.1

(Fourier Slice Theorem)
.

For every
f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
n^{\prime}\geq 2
,
n^{\prime\prime}\geq 2
,

(2.9)

\hat{f}(\xi)=\int_{\theta^{\perp}}e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}Lf(x,\theta)\,\,\mathrm{d}H_{\theta}(x),\quad\text{whenever $\xi\perp\theta$, $\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}$}.

The theorem is just a restricted version of the classical Fourier Slice Theorem.

2.3.
Consequences of the Fourier Slice Theorem

We immediately obtain the following implications of the Fourier Slice Theorem, see also
[
11
]
.

Corollary 2.2

(Uniqueness)
.

f\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
Lf=0
imply
f=0
.

Proof.

We follow
[
11
]
here.
Given
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we want to show that the equation
\xi^{\prime}\cdot\theta^{\prime}+\xi^{\prime\prime}\cdot\theta^{\prime\prime}=0
has a solution
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}
. Assume, without loss of generality, that
|\xi^{\prime}|\leq|\xi^{\prime\prime}|
. Choose any
\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}
. Then
|\xi^{\prime}\cdot\theta^{\prime}|\leq|\xi^{\prime}|\leq|\xi^{\prime\prime}|
, hence we can find
\theta^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}
so that
\xi^{\prime\prime}\cdot\theta^{\prime\prime}=-\xi^{\prime}\cdot\theta^{\prime}
.

By Theorem
2.1
,
\hat{f}(\xi)=0
for every
\xi
, which completes the proof.
∎

3.
The normal operator

3.1.
The adjoint and the normal operators

The operator
L:C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C_{0}(\Sigma)
is continuous, see
[
3
, sec. 2.8]
. Then it has a sequentially continuous transpose
L^{\prime}:\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Sigma)\to\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. We compute
L^{\prime}
below on the dense set
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Sigma)
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}\geq 2
. The transpose of
L
is given by

(3.1)

L^{\prime}\phi(z)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\phi(z-{2^{-1}}(z\cdot\theta)\theta,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime}),\quad\forall\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Sigma).

The normal operator is

(3.2)

\displaystyle L^{\prime}Lf(x)

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}f\left(x+t\theta\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime}),\quad\forall f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Proof.

For every
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Sigma)
, we have

(3.3)

\begin{split}\langle Lf,\phi\rangle&=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\int_{\theta^{\perp}}Lf(x,\theta)\phi(x,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}H_{\theta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\int_{\theta^{\perp}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x+t\theta)\phi(x,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}H_{\theta}(x)\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})\\
&=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(z)\phi(z-{2^{-1}}(z\cdot\theta)\theta,\theta)\,\mathrm{d}z\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime}).\end{split}

This proves (
3.1
). To prove (
3.2
), write

(3.4)

\displaystyle L^{\prime}Lf(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(x-{2^{-1}}(x\cdot\theta)\theta+t\theta\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})

(3.5)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(x+t\theta\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime}).

Let us split the integral in
t
variable into
\{t<0\}
and
\{t>0\}
. Since
n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}\geq 2
, we can make the changes of variables
\theta^{\prime}\mapsto-\theta^{\prime}
and
\theta^{\prime\prime}\mapsto-\theta^{\prime\prime}
to obtain (
3.2
).
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Theorem
3.1
makes sense when (only) one of
n^{\prime}
or
n^{\prime\prime}
equals
1
as well with the integral over
\mathbb{S}^{0}=\{-1,1\}
interpreted as a sum, see
[
20
]
.

We can extend
L
to the space of compactly supported distributions
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by duality, as usual.

3.2.
The Schwartz kernel of the normal operator

Let
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Let us define
\phi_{\epsilon}(t)=\epsilon^{-1}\phi(t/\epsilon)
,
\epsilon>0
. Then
\phi_{\epsilon}\to\delta
in
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, as
\epsilon\to 0
.
For
f\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\displaystyle L^{\prime}Lf(x)

\displaystyle=2\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{\epsilon}(s)f(x+((t+s)\theta^{\prime},t\theta^{\prime\prime}))\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime}).

In the
(t,\theta^{\prime\prime})
integral, make the change of variables
y^{\prime\prime}=t\theta^{\prime\prime}
to get

\displaystyle L^{\prime}Lf(x)

\displaystyle=2\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{\epsilon}(s)\frac{f(x^{\prime}+(|y^{\prime\prime}|+s)\theta^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}+y^{\prime\prime})}{|y^{\prime\prime}|^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=2\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\phi_{\epsilon}(s-|y^{\prime\prime}|)\frac{f(x^{\prime}+s\theta^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}+y^{\prime\prime})}{|y^{\prime\prime}|^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\,\mathrm{d}s\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=2\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}}}\phi_{\epsilon}(|y^{\prime}|-|y^{\prime\prime}|)\frac{f\left(x^{\prime}+y^{\prime},x^{\prime\prime}+y^{\prime\prime}\right)}{|y^{\prime}|^{n^{\prime}-1}|y^{\prime\prime}|^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime\prime},

where in the last equality, we switched to polar coordinates in the first variable. Next,

(3.6)

\displaystyle L^{\prime}Lf(x)

\displaystyle=2\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime\prime}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n^{\prime}}}\phi_{\epsilon}\left(|y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}|-|y^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime\prime}|\right)\frac{f\left(y\right)}{|y^{\prime}-x^{\prime}|^{n^{\prime}-1}|y^{\prime\prime}-x^{\prime\prime}|^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}y^{\prime\prime}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=2\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}f\ast\frac{\phi_{\epsilon}(|\cdot^{\prime}|-|\cdot^{\prime\prime}|)}{|\cdot^{\prime}|^{n^{\prime}-1}|\cdot^{\prime\prime}|^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}.

We want to interpret the limit above as a delta function. Set
F(x)=|x^{\prime}|-|x^{\prime\prime}|
. Then
\nabla F=(x^{\prime}/|x^{\prime}|,-x^{\prime\prime}/|x^{\prime\prime}|)
. Therefore,
F
is smooth, with
|\nabla F|^{2}=2
, in some neighborhood of
\{F=0,\,x\not=0\}
not containing the origin. Then away from
x=0
, we have
\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\phi_{\epsilon}(|x^{\prime}|-|x^{\prime\prime}|)=\delta(|x^{\prime}|-|x^{\prime\prime}|)
, which, in particular, is positively homogeneous w.r.t.
x
of order
-1
. Then the convolution kernel in (
3.7
) is positively homogeneous of order
-n+1
(away from
x=0
). It has a unique extension to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
as such a distribution, see
[
9
, Theorem 3.2.3]
. On the other hand,
(
3.2
) implies that the Schwartz kernel of
L^{\prime}L
is positively homogeneous of order
-n+1
, therefore it equals that extension. Indeed, given a function
h
, denote temporarily
h_{\lambda}=h(\lambda x)
,
\lambda>0
. Then by (
3.2
),
L^{\prime}Lf_{\lambda}=\lambda^{-1}(L^{\prime}Lf)_{\lambda}
,
\forall\lambda>0
. This implies that the convolution kernel
K
of
L^{\prime}L
satisfies
K_{\lambda}=\lambda^{1-n}K
, as claimed. With that understanding, we replace the limit above by a delta (with the understanding that we made sense of the whole expression below, not just of its numerator) to get the following.

Theorem 3.3
.

L^{\prime}L
is a convolution with

2\frac{\delta(|x^{\prime}|-|x^{\prime\prime}|)}{|x^{\prime}|^{n^{\prime}-1}|x^{\prime\prime}|^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}.

The denominator above can be replaced by
|x^{\prime}|^{n-2}
or by
|x^{\prime\prime}|^{n-2}
.

3.3.
The normal operator as a Fourier multiplier

Since
L^{\prime}L
is a convolution, it is a Fourier multiplier with
p(\xi)
equal to the Fourier transform of its convolution kernel. In particular,
p
is positively homogeneous of degree
-1
. We will compute
p
in the following way.
By (
3.5
), for
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\displaystyle\widehat{L^{\prime}Lf}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}(L^{\prime}Lf)(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(x+t\theta\right)\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x.

The multiple integral is not absolutely convergent in general. In order to make it such, we introduce the factor
e^{-\epsilon t^{2}/2}
and eventually we will take the limit
\epsilon\to 0+
. Then the limit of the modified integral would equal the integral above, both considered as distributions in the
\xi
variable (as we will see below, we always get a locally
L^{1}
function of
\xi
but when
n^{\prime}=n^{\prime\prime}=2
, it is not bounded). Indeed,
\langle\widehat{L^{\prime}Lf},\psi\rangle=\langle L^{\prime}Lf,\hat{\psi}\rangle
for every
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, with the pairing on the right represented by an absolute convergent integral. Then regularizing the Schwartz kernel of
L^{\prime}L
as above creates a family convergent to
\langle L^{\prime}Lf,\hat{\psi}\rangle
by the dominated convergence theorem.
With the change of variable
x\mapsto x-t\theta
, we obtain

\displaystyle\widehat{L^{\prime}Lf}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\mathrm{i}x\cdot\xi+\mathrm{i}t\theta\cdot\xi-\epsilon t^{2}/2}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\widehat{f}(\xi)\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{\mathrm{i}t\theta\cdot\xi-\epsilon t^{2}/2}\,\mathrm{d}t\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\widehat{f}(\xi)\lim_{\epsilon\to 0+}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}(2\pi/\epsilon)^{1/2}e^{-(\xi\cdot\theta)^{2}/(2\epsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\hat{f}(\xi)p(\xi),

with the last identity defining
p(\xi)
as a distribution. The limit is a delta at
\xi\cdot\theta
multiplied by
2\pi
, thus the multiplier that we are looking for is given by

(3.8)

p(\xi)=2\pi\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\delta(\xi^{\prime}\cdot\theta^{\prime}+\xi^{\prime\prime}\cdot\theta^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime}).

The function
\psi:=\xi\cdot\theta
has differential

\,\mathrm{d}_{\theta}\psi=(\xi^{\prime}-(\xi^{\prime}\cdot\theta^{\prime})\theta^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime}-(\xi^{\prime\prime}\cdot\theta^{\prime\prime})\theta^{\prime\prime})

on
\mathbb{S}_{\theta^{\prime}}^{n^{\prime}-1}\times\mathbb{S}_{\theta^{\prime\prime}}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}
vanishing on the null set when
\xi^{\prime}=\lambda\theta^{\prime}
,
\xi^{\prime\prime}=-\lambda\theta^{\prime\prime}
with some
\lambda
(and then
|\xi^{\prime}|=|\xi^{\prime\prime}|
). Thus
\delta(\psi)=\delta(\xi\cdot\theta)
is a distribution with respect to
\theta\in\mathbb{S}_{\theta^{\prime}}^{n^{\prime}-1}\times\mathbb{S}_{\theta^{\prime\prime}}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}
smoothly depending on the parameter
\xi\not\in\mathcal{L}
. Then its action on each test function (and (
3.8
) is such) is a smooth function of
\xi\not\in\mathcal{L}
as well. This follows, for example, by regarding
\delta(\xi\cdot\theta)
as the pullback of
\delta
under the map
\theta\mapsto s=\xi\cdot\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, see
[
3
, Theorem 7.2.1]
.

The delta above is not correctly defined as a function of
\theta
as a pullback for
\xi
on the light cone but the meaning of (
3.8
), as already mentioned, is

(3.9)

\langle p,\phi\rangle=2\pi\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}}\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\int\delta(\xi\cdot\theta)\phi(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime}),\quad\forall\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

The inner integral above is in distribution sense (a Radon transform), defining a smooth function of
\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}\times\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}
.

Formally applying the Funk-Hecke theorem

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n-1}}\phi(x\cdot\theta)\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta)=|\mathbb{S}^{n-2}|.|x|^{2-n}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|x|^{2}-t^{2})_{+}^{(n-3)/2}\phi(t)\,\mathrm{d}t,\quad n\geq 2,

we find

(3.10)

\displaystyle p(\xi)

\displaystyle=C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}|\xi^{\prime}|^{2-n^{\prime}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{2-n^{\prime\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}-u^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime}-3)/2}(|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{2}-v^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime\prime}-3)/2}\delta(u+v)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v

(3.11)

\displaystyle=C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}|\xi^{\prime}|^{2-n^{\prime}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{2-n^{\prime\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}-u^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime}-3)/2}(|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{2}-u^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime\prime}-3)/2}\,\mathrm{d}u,

where
C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}=2\pi|\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-2}|.|\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-2}|
. Equation (
3.11
) actually holds in distribution sense as it can be seen by applying the Funk-Hecke theorem to the action of
p
on test functions. It defines a function positively homogeneous of order
-1
in
\xi
as we established earlier, and (
3.11
) holds in the way written for
\xi^{\prime}\not=0
,
\xi^{\prime\prime}\not=0
. The cases of one of them only being zero can be handled as a limit.

We have, using the evenness of the integrand in (
3.11
),

(3.12)

\displaystyle p(\xi)

\displaystyle=C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}|\xi^{\prime}|^{2-n^{\prime}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{2-n^{\prime\prime}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}-u^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime}-3)/2}(|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{2}-u^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime\prime}-3)/2}\,\mathrm{d}u

(3.13)

\displaystyle=2C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}|\xi^{\prime}|^{2-n^{\prime}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{2-n^{\prime\prime}}\int_{0}^{\infty}(|\xi^{\prime}|^{2}-u^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime}-3)/2}(|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{2}-u^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime\prime}-3)/2}\,\mathrm{d}u.

Set

(3.14)

\kappa=|\xi^{\prime}|/|\xi^{\prime\prime}|.

Then
\kappa-1
(or
\kappa^{2}-1
) is a defining function of the light cone
\mathcal{L}
. We use the notation
p=p_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}
below. With the substitution
u=|\xi^{\prime}|s
, we have

(3.15)

p_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}(\xi)=2C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-s^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime}-3)/2}(1-\kappa^{2}s^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime\prime}-3)/2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

Examples

We consider several special cases of
n^{\prime}
and

Example 3.4
.

Consider

first. Then

p_{3,3}(\xi)=16\pi^{3}\frac{\min(|\xi^{\prime}|,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|)}{|\xi^{\prime}|.|\xi^{\prime\prime}|}=\frac{16\pi^{3}}{\max(|\xi^{\prime}|,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|)}

since in this case, each factor in the integral (

Since
|\xi|/\sqrt{2}\leq\max(|\xi^{\prime}|,|\xi^{\prime\prime}|)\leq|\xi|
, we have

\frac{16\pi^{3}}{|\xi|}\leq p_{3,3}(\xi)\leq\frac{16\sqrt{2}\pi^{3}}{|\xi|}.

In particular, this shows that the singularity at
\xi=0
is locally integrable, thus contributing to a smoothing operator. Next,
p
is continuous but not smooth, even after cutting away a neighborhood of
\xi=0
; therefore not a symbol of a pseudo-differential operator. However,

is smooth away from the light cone
\mathcal{L}

Its behavior as

(but not of its

-derivatives) is like
\sim|\xi|^{-1}
. In particular, one gets

(3.16)

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}/C\leq\|L^{\prime}Lf\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall f\in L^{2}(\Omega),

see (
4.8
) since
|\xi|p_{3,3}(\xi)
has positive lower and upper bounds. Here, we view
L^{2}(\Omega)
as a subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by using an extension as zero. The proof is the same as in
[
20
]
.

Example 3.5
.

We will investigate
p_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}
when one of the dimensions is

, say

. Note that

Let us assume first that

\displaystyle p_{n^{\prime},3}(\xi)

\displaystyle=2C_{n^{\prime},3}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\int_{0}^{1}(1-s^{2})^{(n^{\prime}-3)/2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=2C_{n^{\prime},3}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\int_{0}^{\pi/2}\cos^{n^{\prime}-2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}\Gamma((n^{\prime}-1)/2)}{\Gamma(n^{\prime}/2)}C_{n^{\prime},3}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1},

where

(3.19)

\displaystyle p_{n^{\prime},3}(\xi)

\displaystyle=2C_{n^{\prime},3}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\int_{0}^{1/\kappa}(1-s^{2})^{(n^{\prime}-3)/2}\,\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle=2C_{n^{\prime},3}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\int_{0}^{\arcsin(1/\kappa)}\cos^{n^{\prime}-2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\theta,\quad\kappa>1.

Comparing to (

p_{n^{\prime},3}(\xi)=2C_{n^{\prime},3}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\begin{cases}G(\pi/2)&\text{if $\kappa\leq 1$},\\
G(\arcsin(1/\kappa))&\text{if $\kappa>1$},\end{cases}

\arcsin(1/\kappa)

with the substitution

4.1.
Properties of
p(\xi)

We recall the definition of the hypergeometric function

(4.1)

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(a)_{k}(b)_{k}}{(c)_{k}}\frac{z^{k}}{k!},

where
(a)_{k}:=a(a+1)\dots(a+k-1)
when
k\geq 1
, and
(a)_{0}=1
. We collect some properties in the following see
[
1
, Theorems 2.1.3, 2.2.2]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

[
1
]

The series (
4.1
) converges absolutely when
|z|<1
and
c\not\in\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}
. It has the following behavior as
z\to 1-
.

(a)

If
\Re(c-a-b)>0
, then

{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;1)=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(c-a-b)}{\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)}.

In particular,
{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)
is it continuous up to
z=1
from the left by Abel’s theorem.

(b)

If
c=a+b
, then

\lim_{z\to 1-}\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)}{-\log(1-z)}=\frac{\Gamma(a+b)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}.

(c)

If
\Re(c-a-b)=0
and
c\neq a+b
, then

\lim_{z\to 1-}(1-z)^{a+b-c}\left({}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)-\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(c-a-b)}{\Gamma(c-a)\Gamma(c-b)}\right)=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(a+b-c)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}.

(d)

If
\Re(c-a-b)<0
, then

\lim_{z\to 1-}\frac{{}_{2}F_{1}(a,b;c;z)}{(1-z)^{c-a-b}}=\frac{\Gamma(c)\Gamma(a+b-c)}{\Gamma(a)\Gamma(b)}.

We recall the definition of the Beta function as well:

\mathcal{B}(z_{1},z_{2})=\frac{\Gamma(z_{1})\Gamma(z_{2})}{\Gamma(z_{1}+z_{2})}.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}\geq 2
.
L^{\prime}L
is a Fourier multiplier with
p(\xi)
given by (
3.8
), which also can be expressed as the integral (
3.11
).

(a) We have
p\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. It is smooth away from the light cone
\mathcal{L}
and away from the origin, and it is positively homogeneous of order
-1
.

(b) If
n^{\prime}\geq 3
or
n^{\prime\prime}\geq 3
,
p
is continuous across the light cone
\mathcal{L}
(away from the origin), and

(4.2)

\frac{1}{C|\xi|}\leq p(\xi)\leq\frac{C}{|\xi|}

with some
C=C(n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime})>0
.

(c) When
n^{\prime}=n^{\prime\prime}=2
, (
4.2
) holds outside any conic neighborhood of the light cone, i.e., for
||\xi^{\prime}|-|\xi^{\prime\prime}||\geq c_{0}|\xi|
with any
c_{0}>0
. Inside that set, for
0<c_{0}\ll 1
, we have

(4.3)

\frac{1}{C|\xi|}\leq\frac{p_{2,2}(\xi)}{-\log\frac{||\xi^{\prime}|-|\xi^{\prime\prime}||}{|\xi|}}\leq\frac{C}{|\xi|}.

Proof.

Consider (a) first.
The smoothness away from
\mathcal{L}\cup\{0\}
was proved in the analysis of (
3.8
) above. On the other hand, since the convolution kernel of
L^{\prime}L
is positively homogeneous of order
-n+1
,
p
must be positively homogeneous of order
-1
. The regularity claim follows from (b) and (c) below (which do not use (a)). Indeed, they imply that
p(\xi)
is integrable on the unit sphere. Since such a homogeneous distribution
p(\xi)=|\xi|^{-1}p(\xi/|\xi|)
, of order
-1
, has a unique extension to the whole
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, that extension coincides with its restriction to
\xi\not=0
considered as an almost everywhere defined function. It is in
L^{1}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
since
n\geq 4
.

Consider (b) now.
Since
p\in L^{1}_{\rm loc}
is smooth away from the light cone, it is enough to consider the behavior of
p
as
\xi
tends to the latter. The integral in (
3.11
) is absolutely convergent and defines a locally bounded (and, as we prove below, a continuous) function of
\xi=(\xi^{\prime},\xi^{\prime\prime})\not=0
near the light cone (and hence everywhere away from the origin). Also,
p(\xi)>0
for
\xi^{\prime}\not=0
and
\xi^{\prime\prime}\not=0
but representation (
3.11
) is not convenient to establish positivity when one of those factors vanishes. In that case, say when
\xi^{\prime}=0
, but
\xi^{\prime\prime}\neq 0
, we use (
3.8
) to write

\displaystyle p(0,\xi^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=2\pi|\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}|\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-1}}\delta(\theta^{\prime\prime}\cdot\xi^{\prime\prime})\,\mathrm{d}S(\theta^{\prime\prime})

\displaystyle=2\pi|\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}||\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-2}|\int_{\mathbb{R}}\delta(|\xi^{\prime\prime}|t)(1-t^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime\prime}-3)/2}\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=2\pi|\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime}-1}||\mathbb{S}^{n^{\prime\prime}-2}|\frac{1}{|\xi^{\prime\prime}|},

where we invoke Funk-Hecke theorem to get the second equality.
This proves estimate (
4.2
) in case (b).

To prove the continuity of
p
away from the origin in case (b), we can apply the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem to (
3.15
). We will do something different however, which is of its own interest: we derive an “explicit” formula for
p
using hypogeometric functions. That allows us to treat case (c), as well.

For now, we consider the general case
n^{\prime}\geq 2
and
n^{\prime\prime}\geq 2
.
Recall that
\kappa=|\xi^{\prime}|/|\xi^{\prime\prime}|

when
\xi^{\prime\prime}\not=0
, see (
3.14
).
Recall (
3.15
):

(4.4)

\displaystyle p(\xi)=2C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-s^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime}-3)/2}(1-\kappa^{2}s^{2})_{+}^{(n^{\prime\prime}-3)/2}\,\mathrm{d}s.

The substitution
t=s^{2}
further yields

(4.5)

p(\xi)=C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{-1/2}(1-t)_{+}^{(n^{\prime}-3)/2}(1-\kappa^{2}t)_{+}^{(n^{\prime\prime}-3)/2}\,\mathrm{d}t.

When
\kappa\in(0,1)
, we get

p(\xi)=C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\mathcal{B}\Big(\frac{1}{2},\frac{n^{\prime}-1}{2}\Big)\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{3-n^{\prime\prime}}{2},\frac{1}{2};\frac{n^{\prime}}{2};\kappa^{2}\Big),\quad\kappa\in(0,1),

where in the last line, we used the Euler’s Integral Representation of the Hypergeometric function (see
[
1
, Theorem 2.2.1]
).

Since for the coefficients
a,b,c
, see Proposition
4.1
, we have
c-a-b=(n^{\prime}+n^{\prime\prime}-4)/2\geq 1/2
, we can take the limit
\kappa\to 1-
to get

\displaystyle\lim_{\kappa\to 1-}p(\xi)

\displaystyle=C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\mathcal{B}\Big(\frac{1}{2},\frac{n^{\prime}-1}{2}\Big)\frac{\Gamma(n^{\prime}/2)\Gamma((n^{\prime}+n^{\prime\prime}-4)/2)}{\Gamma((n^{\prime}+n^{\prime\prime}-3)/2)\Gamma((n^{\prime}-1)/2)}

\displaystyle=C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\frac{\Gamma(1/2)\Gamma((n-4)/2)}{\Gamma((n-3)/2)}

\displaystyle=C_{n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\mathcal{B}\Big(\frac{1}{2},\frac{n-4}{2}\Big).

This expression is symmetric with respect to
|\xi^{\prime}|
and
|\xi^{\prime\prime}|
(note that we can replace
|\xi^{\prime\prime}|
and
|\xi^{\prime}|
when
\kappa=1
), therefore, the limit
\kappa\to 1+
would yield the same result. Therefore,

is continuous across the light cone away from the origin.

We proceed with the proof of (c) now. We have
n^{\prime}=n^{\prime\prime}=2
.
For
\kappa\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle p_{2,2}(\xi)

\displaystyle=8\pi^{2}|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\,{}_{2}F_{1}\Big(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2};1;\kappa^{2}\Big),

which is also

Therefore, by Proposition

\displaystyle\lim_{\kappa\to 1-}\frac{p_{2,2}(\xi)}{-\log(1-\kappa^{2})}=8\pi|\xi^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}.

As

4.2.
Inversion and stability

Corollary 4.4

(Inversion)
.

Let
n^{\prime},n^{\prime\prime}\geq 2
. For every
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, we have

f=p(D)^{-1}L^{\prime}Lf,

where the operator
p(D)
on the right is defined via the Fourier transform, as follows

p(D)^{-1}L^{\prime}Lf\coloneqq\mathcal{F}^{-1}p^{-1}\mathcal{F}L^{\prime}Lf.

The proof is straightforward in case (b) of Theorem
4.2
. Indeed, then
\widehat{L^{\prime}Lf}=p\hat{f}
with
\hat{f}
smooth and
p\in L^{1}_{\textrm{\rm loc}}
satisfying (
4.2
). Then we just divide by
p
.

In case (c), when
n^{\prime}=n^{\prime\prime}=2
, the symbol
p_{2,2}
is singular at
\mathcal{L}
, and
p_{2,2}^{-1}
extends continuously as zero there. For
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\hat{f}
is smooth, and then
p_{2,2}\hat{f}
would have the same singularity, at worst, as
p_{2,2}
does, at
\mathcal{L}
. Then
p_{2,2}^{-1}(\xi)p_{2,2}(\xi)\hat{f}(\xi)
equals
\hat{f}(\xi)
away from
\mathcal{L}
. Therefore, they equal almost everywhere, which is enough to determine
\hat{f}
as an element of
L^{2}
. We are essentially using the fact that
\supp\hat{f}
cannot be in
\mathcal{L}
.

To formulate a stability estimate, in the case
n^{\prime}=n^{\prime\prime}=2
we need a modified Sobolev space. To this end, define the norm

\|h\|_{H_{2,2}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}^{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{4}}\sigma^{2}(\xi)|\hat{h}(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi,\quad\sigma(\xi):=\frac{\langle\xi\rangle}{-\log\frac{||\xi^{\prime}|-|\xi^{\prime\prime}||}{e|\xi|}},

where
\langle\xi\rangle=(1+|\xi|^{2})^{1/2}
. Since
||\xi^{\prime}|-|\xi^{\prime\prime}||\leq|\xi|
, the denominator in
\sigma
has a lower bound
1
, therefore
0\leq\sigma(\xi)\leq\langle\xi\rangle
but
\sigma
is not elliptic. We define the space
H_{2,2}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
as the completion of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{4})
under that norm. It is a Hilbert space.

Theorem 4.5
.

(a) When
n^{\prime}=n^{\prime\prime}=2
, we have

(4.7)

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}/C\leq\|L^{\prime}Lf\|_{H^{1}_{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{4})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall f\in L^{2}(\Omega).

(b) When either
n^{\prime}\geq 3
or
n^{\prime\prime}\geq 3
, we have

(4.8)

\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}/C\leq\|L^{\prime}Lf\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\quad\forall f\in L^{2}(\Omega).

Proof.

We start with (
4.7
). We take Fourier transforms of
f
and
L^{\prime}Lf
. Then the first inequality follows from
p(\xi)\sigma(\xi)\geq 1/C
, which is true in a conic neighborhood of the light cone
\mathcal{L}
by (
4.6
), and outside it in a trivial way. For the second inequality in (
4.7
), we can use
p(\xi)\sigma(\xi)\leq C
for
|\xi|\geq 1
. In the ball
|\xi|\leq 1
however,
p
has a singularity at the origin, while
\sigma
does not compensate for it; it compensates for the logarithmic factor only. To deal with the singularity at
\xi=0
, we proceed as in the proof of
[
15
, Theorem II.5.1]
. Denote by
q(\xi)
the function in (
4.6
), before we take the limit there. It is positively homogeneous of order
0
, and has positive lower and upper bounds. We have
p\sigma=q\langle\xi\rangle/|\xi|
, and then
1/C\leq|\xi|p\sigma\leq C
. Then cutting
\widehat{L^{\prime}Lf}
to
|\xi|\leq 1
, we are left with estimating

\int_{|\xi|\leq 1}|\xi|^{-2}|\hat{f}(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi

which can be bounded by
C_{s}\|f\|^{2}_{H^{s}}
for every
s
, see p. 44 of the proof of

5.
L
as a generalized Radon transform

We will fit

This framework starts with picking a connected manifold

(5.1)

We parameterize

making it a

Consider the diagram

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Z}
