Abstract
. We study the graph-theoretic properties of the trace of random walks on pseudorandom graphs. We show that for any ε > 0 \varepsilon>0 , there exists a constant C C such that the cover time of an ( n , d , λ ) (n,d,\lambda) -graph G G with d / λ ≥ C d/\lambda\geq C is at most ( 1 + ε ) ​ n ​ log ⁡ n (1+\varepsilon)n\log n , meaning the expected number of steps needed to reach all vertices at least once is at most ( 1 + ε ) ​ n ​ log ⁡ n (1+\varepsilon)n\log n regardless of the starting vertex. Furthermore, we prove that with high probability, the trace of a random walk of length ( 1 + ε ) ​ n ​ log ⁡ n (1+\varepsilon)n\log n on G G is Hamiltonian, regardless of the starting vertex. These results also hold for random d d -regular graphs with sufficiently large d d . These findings answer two questions proposed by Frieze, Krivelevich, Michaeli, and Peled [PLMS, 2018]. Notably, our results imply a bound on a stronger version of the cover time: with high probability, all vertices are covered after ( 1 + ε ) ​ n ​ log ⁡ n (1+\varepsilon)n\log n steps, regardless of the starting vertex. Our proofs rely on the spectral properties of the adjacency matrix and the graph expansion. All results are asymptotically optimal.



1.
Introduction

Let
G
be a simple undirected connected graph. A
simple random walk
on
G
is a stochastic process where, starting from a given vertex, each step consists of moving to a neighbor chosen uniformly at random. The set of edges traversed by this walk is called the
trace
. Random walks are fundamental objects in combinatorics, probability theory, and theoretical computer science. For a comprehensive treatment, we refer the reader to the textbook by Levin and Peres
[
20
]
or the survey by Lovász
[
21
]
.

In this paper, we study the graph formed by the trace of a simple random walk on a finite graph. This line of research was initiated by Frieze, Krivelevich, Michaeli, and Peled in
[
13
]
, who focused on the case where the base graph is random. Motivated by these results, in
[
13
]
, they asked to extend the inquiry to deterministic graphs that exhibit random-like properties, known as pseudorandom graphs. A prominent class of pseudorandom graphs is that of spectral expanders, or
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs. An
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph is defined as a
d
-regular graph on
n
vertices where the second largest eigenvalue in absolute value is at most
\lambda
. The expander mixing lemma shows that
\lambda
governs the edge distribution: a smaller
\lambda
implies that the edge distribution of
G
closely resembles that of the random graph
G(n,d/n)
. For a detailed introduction, we refer the reader to the survey by Krivelevich and Sudakov
[
19
]
.

We focus our attention on the case where
G
is an
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph. Let
\Gamma
denote the subgraph on the vertex set of
G
induced by the trace of the random walk. A fundamental question concerns the structural properties of
\Gamma
, particularly its connectivity. This inquiry is intrinsically linked to the
cover time
,
T(G)
, defined as the expected number of steps required to visit every vertex, maximized over all possible starting vertices. Equivalently,
T(G)
corresponds to the expectation of the hitting time for the property that
\Gamma
becomes connected. The cover time is a classical parameter in the study of random walks. A seminal result by Feige
[
10
]
establishes a universal lower bound of
(1-o(1))n\log n
for any connected graph on
n
vertices. For Erdős-Rényi graph, random regular graph and more generally random graph with a fixed degree sequence, asymptotically sharp bounds have been proven
[
1
,
6
,
5
,
15
,
7
]
. In particular, Frieze, Krivelevich, Michaeli, and Peled
[
13
]
demonstrated that for random graphs
G(n,p)
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon}
such that if
p\geq C_{\varepsilon}\log n/n
, the trace
\Gamma
generated by a random walk of length
(1+\varepsilon)n\log n
is not merely connected, but Hamiltonian with high probability.

Given that
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs exhibit quasi-random behavior analogous to the random graph
G(n,d/n)
, it is natural to inquire whether the structural properties of the random walk trace keep in this setting. Specifically, we ask whether a random walk of length
(1+\varepsilon)n\log n
yields a Hamiltonian trace on an
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph. This question was raised by Frieze, Krivelevich, Michaeli, and Peled
[
13
]
.
Recall that the connectivity threshold for
G(n,p)
is
(1+o(1))\log n/n
where as for the trace to be Hamiltonian, one needs
C_{\varepsilon}\log/n
where
C_{\varepsilon}
is a big constant depending on
\varepsilon
. For
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph, the connectivity holds when
d/\lambda>1
. Thus, analogously, one would expect the answer holds when
d/\lambda>C_{\varepsilon}
for some big constant
C_{\varepsilon}
.

1.1.
Our results

By the expander mixing lemma,
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs represent a class of graphs with expansion properties. The asymptotic behavior of random walks in this setting was investigated in the foundational work of Broder and Karlin
[
3
]
, who established that for any
d
-regular expander

T(G)=\Theta(n\log n)

. Our first contribution is a refinement of this bound for

For every

We now introduce a strengthrening of the cover time, termed the

In the context of random graphs

Again this is asymptotically optimal by the general lower bound of the cover time. In fact, we obtain

The connection between spectral expansion and the Hamiltonicity in pseudorandom graphs is well-established as in

Let

We remark that both results are asymptotically optimal due to the general lower bound on the cover time. Our proof strategy is as follows.
First, since the second largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix of an
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph is bounded away from
d
, we can control the transitional probability going from any vertex
u
to another vertex

at step

by using the spectral decomposition of the transition matrix.
We show that after
(1+\varepsilon)n\log n
steps, each vertex is visited
\Theta(\log n)
times with high probability. Then, we use these visit counts to establish the expansion properties of the trace graph
\Gamma
, which ensures the existence of a Hamiltonian cycle.

Central to our proof of
Theorem

1.3
is the lemma that the trace of the random walk visits every vertex with logarithmic frequency. Specifically,
Lemma

3.1
establishes that there exists a constant
\rho=\rho(\varepsilon)>0
such that with high probability, every vertex is visited at least
\rho\log n
times. This phenomenon is intimately connected to the concept of
blanket time
in the probability theory, a parameter introduced by Winkler and Zuckerman
[
24
]
. Let
\pi
denote the stationary distribution, where
\pi_{v}=\deg(v)/(2|E(G)|)
. For a parameter
\delta\in(0,1)
, the
\delta
-blanket time, denoted
t_{bl}(G,\delta)
, is defined as the minimum time
t
required for the walk to visit every vertex
v
at least
\delta t\pi_{v}
times. While it is immediate that
t_{bl}(G,\delta)\geq T(G)
, Winkler and Zuckerman conjectured that the blanket time is, in fact, within a constant factor of the cover time; that is,
t_{bl}(G,\delta)\leq C_{\delta}T(G)
for any
0<\delta<1
and any graph
G
.
Kahn, Kim, Lovász and Vu
[
16
]
showed that
t_{bl}(G,\delta)=O(T(G)(\log\log n)^{2})
before the conjecture was affirmatively settled in a celebrated work of Ding, Lee, and Peres
[
8
]
.
Our results imply that there exists a
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)\in(0,1)
for which
t_{bl}(G,\delta)\leq(1+\varepsilon)n\log n
, which is the same as the cover time up to a
1+o(1)
factor. In general, it is unclear for which graphs the same conclusion hold. In the concluding remarks we discuss and propose further questions along this direction.

2.2.
Basics of random walks

Let
G
be a connected simple graph. A simple random walk on
G
starting at vertex
v_{0}\in V(G)
is an infinite random sequence
(v_{0},v_{1},\dots)
, where at each step
i
, the walk move from
v_{i}
to
v_{i+1}
, which is a uniformly random chosen neighbor of
v_{i}
. We denote such move by
v_{i}\rightarrow v_{i+1}
. The simple random walk has the Markov property, meaning the probability distribution at each step depends only on the current position and not on the history.

Let
P=(P_{i,j})_{i,j=1}^{n}
be the probability transition matrix, where
P_{i,j}=\mathbb{P}(v_{i}\rightarrow v_{j})=1/\deg(v_{i})
if
\{i,j\}\in E(G)
and
0
otherwise. Equivalently
P=D^{-1}A
, where
D
is the diagonal matrix with
D_{i,i}=\deg(v_{i})
and
A
is the adjacency matrix.
Note that the probability a random walk takes
t
steps to reach vertex
v
from vertex
u
equals to
(P^{t})_{u,v}
.
The
stationary distribution
for a random walk
X
on
G
is a vector
\pi\in[0,1]^{n}
such that
P\pi=\pi
. It is known that if
G
is connected and non-bipartite, then
\pi
exists and is unique (see e.g.
[
21
]
).
For
S\subseteq V(G)
, let
\pi(S)=\sum_{v\in S}\pi_{v}
.
The
total variation distance
between
X
and
\pi
is define as

d_{\mathrm{TV}}(X_{t},\pi)=\sup_{S\subseteq V(G)}|\mathbb{P}(X_{t}\in S)-\pi_{S}|=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{v\in V(G)}|\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=v)-\pi_{v}|\,.

Let
Y
be the stationary walk which is a simple random walk such that
\mathbb{P}(Y_{0}=v)=\pi_{v}
for every
v\in V(G)
.
There exists a standard coupling between
X
and
Y
such that for every
t
, we have

(2.1)

\mathbb{P}(\exists s\geq t:X_{s}\neq Y_{s})\leq d_{\mathrm{TV}}(X_{t},\pi)\,.

Now we define several well-known parameters of the random walk.
For a vertex
v\in V(G)
, let
C_{v}
denote the expected number of steps for a simple random walk staring from
v
to reach every vertex at least once.
The
cover time
is defined as
C_{G}=\max_{v\in V(G)}C_{v}
.
The
hitting time

H(u,v)
is defined as the expected number of steps for a random walk to reach
v
from
u
.
For
\xi\in(0,1)
, the
\xi
-mixing time
is defined as

\tau(\xi)=\min\{t\geq 0:d_{\mathrm{TV}}(X_{s},\pi)<\xi\;\;\forall s\geq t\}\,.

It is well-known that for expanders, the mixing time is small. For
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs, a quantitative version is as follows (see for instance
[
20
, Chapter 12]
)

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
G
be an
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with
\lambda<d
. Then,

\tau(\xi)\leq\frac{\log n/2+\log(1/2\xi)}{1-\lambda/d}\,.

Assuming
d/\lambda\geq 100
, we have

\tau(1/n)\leq 10\log n\,.

2.3.
Electrical networks

One may think of a (undirected, simple) graph as an electrical network where each edge carry
1
Ohm of electrical resistance. The
effective resistance
between two vertices
u,v
, denoted as
R_{u,v}
, is the amount of current flowing between
u
and
v
if we exert
+1
volt of voltage at
u
and
-1
at
v
.
Effective resistance is nonnegative.
One may compute the effective resistance by the series and parallel rules. Another, more systematic way, is to calculate effective resistance using the Laplacian matrix.

Recall the Laplacian matrix
L
of a graph
G
is defined to be
D-A
where
D
is the diagonal matrix with
D_{i,i}=\deg(v_{i})
and
A
is the adjacency matrix and the pseudo-inverse of
L
, denoted as
L^{\dagger}
, is the inverse of
L
restricted to the subspace orthogonal to the span of all-
1
vector. Let
e_{i}
be the
i
-th standard basis. Then, the effective resistance can be expressed as (see
[
17
]
):

(2.2)

R_{v_{i},v_{j}}=(e_{i}-e_{j})^{T}L^{\dagger}(e_{i}-e_{j})=L^{\dagger}_{i,i}+L^{\dagger}_{j,j}-2L^{\dagger}_{i,j}\,.

A standard lower bound for
R_{u,v}
is as follows (see e.g.
[
15
]
):

Proposition 2.2
.

For a graph
G
and any two vertices
u,v\in V(G)
, we have

R_{u,v}\geq\frac{1}{d_{u}+1}+\frac{1}{d_{v}+1}\,.

The effective resistance, hitting time and cover time are closely related.

S
is a symmetric real square matrix, its spectral decomposition can be written as
S=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}\phi_{i}\phi_{i}^{T}
, where
\mu_{i}=\lambda_{i}/d\in[-1,1]
are eigenvalues of
S
and
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
forms an orthonormal basis.
Observe that the stationary distribution of
X
is
\pi=(1/n,\dots,1/n)
,
\mu_{1}=1
and
\phi_{1}(v)=(1/\sqrt{n},\dots,1/\sqrt{n})
.

Using the fact that

P^{t}=D^{-1/2}S^{t}D^{1/2}=S^{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}^{t}\phi_{i}\phi_{i}^{T}\,,

we conclude

\mathbb{E}[N]=\frac{T}{n}+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\sum_{i=2}^{n}\mu_{i}^{t}\phi_{i}(u)\phi_{i}(v)=\frac{T}{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\mu_{i}(1-\mu_{i}^{T})}{1-\mu_{i}}\phi_{i}(u)\phi_{i}(v)\,.

By Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and orthonormality of
\phi_{i}
, we know

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\sum_{i=2}^{n}\phi_{i}(u)\phi_{i}(v)}}\right|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\phi_{i}(u)^{2}}}\right)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=2}^{n}\phi_{i}(v)^{2}}}\right)\leq 1\,.

Moreover, using the fact that
|\mu_{i}|=|\lambda_{i}|/d\leq\lambda/d\leq 1/C
for all
i\geq 2
, we conclude

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\mu_{i}(1-\mu_{i}^{T})}{1-\mu_{i}}}}\right|\leq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{\mu_{i}}{1-\mu_{i}}}}\right|\leq\frac{1/C}{1-1/C}\leq 2/C\,.

Thus,
\mathbb{E}[N]\geq T/n-2/C
.

Now we compute
\mathbb{E}[N^{2}]
. Observe that

\mathbb{E}[N^{2}]=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{T}I_{t}}}\right)^{2}}}\right]=\mathbb{E}[N]+2\sum_{1\leq t_{1}<t_{2}\leq T}\mathbb{E}[I_{t_{1}}I_{t_{2}}]\,.

Using the Markov’s property, we have

\mathbb{E}[I_{t_{1}}I_{t_{2}}]=\mathbb{P}(\{X_{t_{1}}=v\}\cap\{X_{t_{2}}=v\}\mid X_{0}=u)=\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{1}}=v\mid X_{0}=u)\cdot\mathbb{P}(X_{k}=v\mid X_{0}=v)\,,

where
k=t_{2}-t_{1}\geq 1
.

Let
R_{v}(T)=\sum_{t=1}^{T}(P^{t})_{v,v}
. Since
R_{v}(T)
is monotone non-decreasing in
T
, we conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[N^{2}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[N]+2\sum_{t_{1}=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{1}}=v\mid X_{0}=u)\sum_{k=1}^{T-t_{1}}\mathbb{P}(X_{k}=v\mid X_{0}=v)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[N]+2\sum_{t_{1}=1}^{T}\mathbb{P}(X_{t_{1}}=v\mid X_{0}=u)\cdot R_{v}(T-t_{1})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}[N]+2\mathbb{E}[N]\cdot R_{v}(T)=(1+2R_{v}(T))\cdot\mathbb{E}[N]\,.

By a similar computation as for
\mathbb{E}[N]
, we know that

R_{v}(T)=\frac{T}{n}+\sum_{i=2}^{n}\frac{\mu_{i}(1-\mu_{i}^{T})}{1-\mu_{i}}\phi_{i}(v)^{2}\leq\frac{T}{n}+\frac{2}{C}\,.

Applying
Proposition

2.8
and using
T=n/\sqrt{C}
for a sufficiently large
C
, we conclude

\mathbb{P}(N>0)\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[N]^{2}}{\mathbb{E}[N^{2}]}\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}[N]}{2R_{v}(T)+1}\geq\frac{T/n-2/C}{2(T/n+2/C)+1}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{C}}(1-0.1\varepsilon)\,,

as desired.
∎

We have the following immediate corollary for the stationary walk:

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. There exists a constant
C=C(\varepsilon)>0
such that the following holds: Let
G
be a
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with
d/\lambda\geq C
and
T=n/\sqrt{C}
. Then, for any vertex
v\in V(G)
, the probability a stationary walk visits
v
at least once within
T
steps is at least
(1-0.1\varepsilon)/\sqrt{C}
.

Proof.

Recall that the stationary distribution for a
d
-regular graph is the uniform distribution over all vertices
(1/n,\dots,1/n)
. Also, the stationary walk
Y
and the simple random walk
X
have the same distribution conditionally on having the same starting vertex.
Since
Lemma

3.2
holds for every starting vertex
u\in V(G)
, the conclusion follows by integrating over the starting vertex.
∎

We are now ready to prove
Lemma

3.1
:

Proof of
Lemma

3.1
.

Let
X
denote the simple random walk and
Y
denote the stationary walk.
Recall
L=(1+\varepsilon)n\log n
and
T=n/\sqrt{C}
. Let
b=\tau(1/n)
. By
Theorem

2.1
, we know
b\leq 10\log n
.
We divide
X
into consecutive segments of length
10\log n+T
and treat each segment separately. For simplicity, the subscript here are with respect to the starting point in each segment.
Within each segment, by the definition of the mixing time,
we know
d_{\mathrm{TV}}(X_{t},Y_{t})\leq 1/n
for any
t\geq 10\log n
. Using (
2.1
), we conclude

\mathbb{P}(\exists 10\log n\leq s\leq 10\log n+T:X_{s}\neq Y_{s})\leq 1/n\,.

Fix an arbitrary vertex
v\in V(G)
.
By Markov’s property and
Corollary

3.3
, for each segment, the walk visits
v
with probability at least
(1-0.1\varepsilon)/\sqrt{C}-1/n\geq(1-0.2\varepsilon)/\sqrt{C}
. Thus, by
Proposition

2.9
, the probability
v
is visited fewer than
\rho\log n
times within the
L
steps is at most

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\operatorname{Bin}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{L}{10\log n+T},\frac{1-0.2\varepsilon}{\sqrt{C}}}}\right)\leq\rho\log n}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\rho\log n\cdot\binom{L/(10\log n+T)}{\rho\log n}((1-0.2\varepsilon)/\sqrt{C})^{\rho\log n}(1-(1-0.2\varepsilon)/\sqrt{C})^{L/(10\log n+T)-\rho\log n}

\displaystyle\quad=o(n^{-1-0.1\varepsilon})\,,

if we choose
\rho
sufficiently small and
C
sufficiently large.
The conclusion follows from a union bound over all vertices
v\in V(G)
.
∎

3.2.
C-expander

The goal of this section is to prove the following:

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. There exists a constant
C=C(\varepsilon)>0
such that the following holds: Let
G
be a
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with
d/\lambda\geq C
and
L=(1+\varepsilon)n\log n
.
Let
\Gamma
be the trace graph obtained by a random walk of length
L
starting from any vertex. Then,

whp

\Gamma
is a
C^{\prime}
-expander for
C^{\prime}=\log\log C
.

Proof.

To prove a graph is
C
-expander, we need to verify the expansion and joinedness properties. By
Lemma

3.1
, there exists a constant
\rho>0
such that
\gamma(v)\geq\rho\log n
for all
v\in V(G)
.

We start by proving the expansion property. Suppose for contradiction there exists a set
A\subseteq V(G)
of size
a\leq n/(2C^{\prime})
such that
|N_{\Gamma}(A)|\leq C^{\prime}a
. For
v\in A
, let
d_{v}\coloneqq|N_{\Gamma}(v)|
. Note that the existence of such a set
A
implies for every vertex
v\in A
, every time the random walk exits
v
, it goes to one of the
d_{v}
neighbors of
v
in
N_{\Gamma}(v)
, which happens with probability
d_{v}/d
independently. Using the independence between exits and that
\gamma(v)\geq\rho\log n
, we conclude the probability for a fixed outcome of
A
and
N_{\Gamma}(A)
satisfying the conditions above to happen is at most

\prod_{v\in A}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{d_{v}}{d}}}\right)^{\rho\log n}=\biggl(\prod_{v\in A}\frac{d_{v}}{d}\biggr)^{\rho\log n}\,.

By
Lemma

2.5
,
|N_{\Gamma}(A)|\leq C^{\prime}a
and
\lambda\leq d/C
, we know

\sum_{v\in A}d_{v}\leq e_{G}(A,N_{\Gamma}(A))\leq\frac{d}{n}C^{\prime}a^{2}+\frac{d\sqrt{C^{\prime}}}{C}a\,.

When
a\geq 10n/(C\sqrt{C^{\prime}})
, we have
\sum_{v\in A}d_{v}\leq 1.1dC^{\prime}a^{2}/n
and otherwise we have
\sum_{v\in A}d_{v}\leq 11d\sqrt{C^{\prime}}a/C
. Applying the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means, we know
\prod_{v\in A}d_{v}\leq(\sum_{v\in A}d_{v}/a)^{a}
.
Thus,

\prod_{v\in A}d_{v}\leq\begin{cases}(1.1dC^{\prime}a/n)^{a}&\text{if }a\geq 10n/(C\sqrt{C^{\prime}})\\
(11d\sqrt{C^{\prime}}/C)^{a}&\text{otherwise}\par\end{cases}

When
a\geq 10n/(C\sqrt{C^{\prime}})
, the number of such sets
A
and
N_{\Gamma}(A)
is upper bounded by
2^{2n}
. Using the fact that
a\leq n/(2C^{\prime})
, the probability there exists such set
A
is at most

2^{2n}(1.1C^{\prime}a/n)^{a\rho\log n}\leq 2^{2n}\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{10\rho}{C\sqrt{C^{\prime}}}\log_{2}(1.1/2)\cdot n\log n}}\right)=o(1)\,.

Otherwise, the number of such sets
A
and
N_{\Gamma}(A)
is upper bounded by
\binom{n}{a}\binom{da}{C^{\prime}a}
. Thus, the probability there exists such set
A
is at most

\binom{n}{a}\binom{da}{C^{\prime}a}(11\sqrt{C^{\prime}}/C)^{a\rho\log n}\leq\exp\bigl(a\log n+C^{\prime}a\log(d/C^{\prime})+\log(11\sqrt{C^{\prime}}/C)\rho a\log n\bigr)=o(1)\,,

since
C^{\prime}=\log\log C
and
C
is sufficiently large. In both cases, the expansion property is verified.

Now we verify the joinedness property. Suppose for contradiction there exists two disjoint sets
A
of size
a
and
B
of size
b
where
a=b=n/2C^{\prime}
and that
e_{\Gamma}(A,B)=0
.
By
Lemma

2.5
and
\lambda\leq d/C
and
C^{\prime}=\log\log C
, we know

e_{G}(A,B)\geq\frac{d}{n}ab-\lambda\sqrt{ab}=\frac{dn}{4(C^{\prime})^{2}}-\frac{\lambda n}{2C^{\prime}}\geq\frac{dn}{10(C^{\prime})^{2}}\,.

Let
A^{\prime}=\{v\in A:N_{G}(v)\cap B\geq d/(10C^{\prime})\}
. Then,

\frac{dn}{10(C^{\prime})^{2}}\leq e_{G}(A,B)\leq d|A^{\prime}|+\frac{d}{10C^{\prime}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{n}{2C^{\prime}}-|A^{\prime}|}}\right)\implies|A^{\prime}|\geq\frac{n}{30(C^{\prime})^{2}}\,.

Recall that each vertex is visited at least
\rho\log n
times. Thus, the probability
e_{\Gamma}(A,B)=0
is at most the probability none of the edges between
A^{\prime}
and
B
survives, which is at most

(1-1/(10C^{\prime}))^{\rho n\log n/(30(C^{\prime})^{2})}\leq\exp\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(-\frac{\rho}{9000(C^{\prime})^{3}}n\log n}}\right)=o(2^{-2n}).

It remains to union bound over all such
A
and
B
for which there are at most
2^{2n}
possibilities.
∎

Proof of
Theorem

1.3

We are now ready to prove
Theorem

1.3
:

Proof of
Theorem

1.3
.

Let
C_{0}
be as in
Theorem

2.7
.
From
Lemma

3.4
, we know
whp

\Gamma
is a
C^{\prime}
-expander. By taking

4.
Concluding remarks

One of the key tools we applied was
Theorem

2.7
, which requires only
C
-expanders. One may wonder if
Theorem

1.3
is true with
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs replaced by
C
-expanders. This is unfortunately (very) incorrect:

Proposition 4.1
.

For any
C\leq 1.1n/\log n
, there exists a
C
-expander for which the cover time is
an\log n
for some
a>1
.

Proof sketch.

Consider a complete graph on
n-2
vertices together with two special vertices
u,v
, each connecting to
C
vertices of the complete graph and their neighborhoods are disjoint. Clearly, the graph is a
C
-expander. However, note that the expected number of steps needed to go from
u
to
v
is at least
1+(n-1)/C)\cdot(n-1)>n\log n
. Indeed, the
1
comes from going from
u
to the clique. Then, in each step, it will have at most a
C/(n-1)
chance of landing in
N(v)
and thus in expectation
(n-1)/C
steps are needed to land in
N(v)
. Starting from a vertex in
N(v)
, each time there is a
1/(n-1)
chance of going to
v
so in expectation it takes
n-1
such trials. Clearly, the expected number of steps taken to go from
u
to
v
is a lower bound on the cover time, which finishes the proof.
∎

One natural strengthening of
Theorem

1.3
would be to consider following hitting time problem: Let
\tau_{HC}(G)
be the minimum
t\in\mathbb{N}
for which the trace graph
\Gamma
becomes Hamiltonian and
\tau_{1}
the minimum
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\Gamma
has minimum degree
1
.
For
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs with
d/\lambda\geq C
or a random
d
-regular graph for
d
sufficiently large, is it true that
whp

\tau_{HC}=\tau_{1}+1
? Note that
\tau_{HC}\geq\tau_{1}+1
is necessary since a Hamilton cycle uses two incident edges from each vertex.
For complete graph the corresponding hitting time result is known
[
13
]
.

Another natural direction is to consider relations between cover time, strong cover time and the blanket time. For
(n,d,\lambda)
-graph with
d/\lambda
sufficiently large, in
Theorem

1.2
, we have established that the cover time and the strong cover time differ only by a factor of
1+o(1)
. In general, can the strong cover time always be controlled by the cover time by a constant factor?
As mentioned in the introduction,
Lemma

3.1
implies the existence of a
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)\in(0,1)
such that the
\delta
-blanket time
t_{bl}(G,\delta)
and the cover time differ only a factor of
1+o(1)
for
(n,d,\lambda)
-graphs with
d/\lambda
sufficiently large. Are there other natural graph classes that the same conclusion holds? When the cover time of a graph is
(1+o(1))n\log n
(almost match the general lower bound
(1-o(1))n\log n)
, does such
\delta
necessarily exist?
