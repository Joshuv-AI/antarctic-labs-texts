Location and habitat specificity structure benthic microbial assemblages in a temperate seagrass ecosystem
Authors: Gabriel A. Serrano, Alejandro De Santiago, Tiago J. Pereira, Mirayana Marcelino Barros, Holly M. Bik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSeagrass meadows are globally distributed coastal ecosystems that provide habitats for diverse species and facilitate ecosystem services, such as carbon storage and nutrient cycling. However, anthropogenic activities and climate change are causing seagrass decline worldwide, broadly impacting ecosystem functioning. Although macrofaunal communities in seagrass habitats are relatively well studied, benthic microbial assemblages (including microeukaryote groups such as protists and microbial metazoa) remain underexplored. In this study, we used environmental DNA (eDNA) metabarcoding to characterize assemblages of bacteria/archaea (16S rRNA gene), microeukaryotes (18S rRNA amplified from raw sediment), and meiofauna (18S rRNA amplified from sieved and Ludoxed sediment fractions) in seagrass fields and bare sediment habitats at three sites along an estuarine gradient in Bodega Harbor, California. We found that microbial and microeukaryote communities were primarily structured by location, with sediment properties (sand vs silt/clay proportions) being the main driver of community assemblages. Habitat type was a secondary factor influencing benthic fauna, with bare sediments typically displaying higher biodiversity than seagrass beds, except at Westside Park mudflats, where bare sediments showed significantly reduced diversity compared to all other sampling locations. The differential recovery of nematode bioindicator taxa, including Terschellingia, Daptonema, Viscosia, Microlaimus, and Spilophorella, suggested that abiotic factors, such as physical disturbance, pollution, or oxygen dynamics, may influence the success of specific taxa at each site. Finally, nematode-bacterial co-occurrence networks confirmed known symbiotic associations and suggest additional taxonomic relationships to explore in future studies. Our findings underscore the importance of environmental gradients and microhabitat features in shaping benthic biodiversity in temperate seagrass ecosystems.IMPORTANCESeagrass meadows, which sustain a wide variety of plant and animal life, are vital coastal habitats. These submerged ecosystems support biodiversity, coastal protection, biogeochemical cycling, and carbon storage. However, seagrass habitats worldwide are declining due to pressures from climate change and human activity. This study characterizes the microbial and microeukaryote communities (including microbial animals with a body size <1 mm) that inhabit and surround temperate seagrass meadows in Bodega Harbor, California. Our findings demonstrate that location is a primary factor that determines microbial communities inhabiting sediments at each site, with habitat type (bare sediment vs seagrass beds) exerting a secondary influence on the biodiversity and distribution of microbial species. By using nematode bioindicator taxa and nematode-bacterial co-occurrence patterns, our results also suggest specific environmental factors that shape sediment biodiversity, including tidal cycles, oxygen, sulfur, and decaying organic matter. A comprehensive understanding of the variation within benthic communities associated with seagrass habitats can significantly enhance conservation and restoration efforts for seagrass meadows.

INTRODUCTION

Seagrass habitats are distributed worldwide in shallow coastal waters and span tropical, temperate, and boreal regions, globally covering a total area of approximately 277,000 km2 (1–3). These highly productive ecosystems support a diverse array of species, from microorganisms to large animals, and are therefore recognized as biodiversity hotspots. Seagrasses offer shelter and food (i.e., nursery grounds) for many economically significant species of fish, crustaceans, and mollusks. They also deliver key ecosystem services, including carbon storage, sediment stabilization and accretion, and coastal protection (1, 2, 4). However, despite their high societal value, seagrasses are declining globally due to various stressors linked to anthropogenic activities and climate change, especially in the intertidal zones of low-inflow estuaries in temperate regions (4–6).

A variety of abiotic and biotic factors influence biodiversity and community structure in intertidal estuaries, including the presence of seagrasses. Studies have found that fish and benthic invertebrate communities differ between seagrass and bare sediments in both temperate and tropical regions, including changes in species abundance and biomass (7–9). Grazing and bioturbation by larger organisms can also impact seagrass density and spatial distribution, which may, in turn, affect assemblages of smaller-sized benthic species (10, 11). Other abiotic factors, such as those related to daily tide cycles and seasonality (e.g., temperature, salinity, water depth, sunlight exposure), contribute to species richness and spatial patterns of biodiversity by creating environmental gradients (12, 13). At times, these factors result in more pronounced differences among sites along the estuary than between microhabitats (i.e., seagrass vs bare sediment), highlighting the importance of spatial scale variation (12). In other cases, the differences among sites within an estuary can be just as significant as those observed between microhabitats (14, 15). In other words, abiotic factors can promote larger differences between estuarine sites versus habitat types themselves.

Most research on seagrass habitats has focused on the macrophytes themselves, including factors that control seagrass distribution and abundance, or their associations with macrofauna and megafauna such as fishes, crustaceans, and polychaetes (16). Studies of intertidal seagrass habitats have generally shown that seagrass meadows support higher macroinvertebrate diversity, richness, and density than unvegetated areas (16–18). However, Barnes (13) reported little to no effect of seagrass on benthic invertebrates near the mouth of the Kynsa estuary in South Africa, although differences between seagrass and bare sediment communities were shown to increase upstream as a result of local environmental gradients (13, 19). In terms of microscopic fauna, seagrass meadows often exhibit high meiofauna abundance and diversity, but diversity levels and species distributions are often correlated with dissolved oxygen and chlorophyll a (20, 21). Intertidal seagrass habitats have also been shown to increase the stability and richness of microbial communities (22). By creating microhabitats on plant surfaces and within seagrass patches (23, 24), generalist and specialist microbes can inhabit distinct physical niches and play key roles in biogeochemical cycling (25–28). Additionally, both emergent and submerged seagrass beds are known to vertically structure microbial and macroinvertebrate communities (27, 29), suggesting that the three-dimensional nature of benthic seagrass habitats may be a key aspect that contributes to high microbial biodiversity.

Understanding the ecological interactions between microorganisms and seagrasses is crucial for effective conservation measures (8, 16, 30, 31). However, a significant gap remains regarding benthic microbes in intertidal seagrass meadows, resulting in a poor understanding of prokaryotic and microeukaryotic biodiversity, microhabitat structure, and species co-occurrence in these habitats. Few studies have examined prokaryote and eukaryote co-occurrence in aquatic environments (27, 32–34), and even fewer have addressed these interactions specifically in seagrass habitats (35). While some correlations between prokaryotes and eukaryotes have been observed, the underlying drivers remain unclear. Recent advances in environmental DNA (eDNA) metabarcoding offer a promising approach to filling these knowledge gaps in seagrass ecosystems, particularly in mapping the spatial distribution and interactions of bacterial/archaeal and microeukaryote communities. Studies using multiplexed eDNA metabarcoding—such as nuclear 16S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes for bacteria and archaea, nuclear 18S rRNA genes or mitochondrial cytochrome oxidase subunit I (COI) for eukaryotes, and nuclear ITS rRNA gene regions for fungi—have begun to explore the scope of seagrass biodiversity and spatial habitat patterns, facilitating deeper insights into the distribution and relationships among prokaryotic and eukaryotic assemblages (36, 37).

In this study, we used 16S rRNA and 18S rRNA eDNA metabarcoding to characterize bacterial/archaeal and microeukaryote communities associated with seagrass and adjacent bare sediments in Bodega Harbor, northern California, USA. Bodega Harbor is a shallow, natural harbor on the Pacific Ocean, located on the eastern side of Bodega Head and formed from a geological depression within the San Andreas Fault. Bodega Harbor is protected from the larger marine water mass of Bodega Bay by a narrow sandy spit of land, with a small water channel on the western side at Campbell Cove. Ecologically, Bodega Harbor is characterized by expansive mudflats and seagrass habitats, and local benthic habitats are dominated by eelgrass (Zostera marina) and sea lettuce (Ulva lactuca). Through replicated sampling across known environmental gradients in this temperate seagrass habitat, we assessed whether community patterns for microbes and microeukaryotes reflected differences among sites across the estuary or specific microhabitats within sites. Here, we provide a significant focus on benthic meiofaunal species, a phylogenetically diverse group of marine invertebrates with a body size of <1 mm (e.g., nematodes, tardigrades, copepods, kinorhynchs, and some protist groups such as foraminifera [38]). Most meiofaunal groups are understudied due to high biodiversity and difficult taxonomy, and their spatial patterns and ecological roles are still poorly known compared to other faunal groups in seagrass habitats. We also evaluated the relationships between microbial and microeukaryote communities, focusing on nematode bioindicators and co-occurrences of bacteria and nematodes to identify potential markers of habitat types and generate hypotheses about potentially novel symbioses.

RESULTS

Environmental characterization of Bodega Harbor

We assessed the environmental variables of seagrass and bare sediment samples at three distinct sites at the Bodega Harbor (Fig. 1). Analysis of environmental variables grouped samples by site and habitat, and primarily highlighted differences in sediment properties (Fig. 2A). Mason’s Marina, located in the inner harbor, exhibited higher total organic carbon (TOC), silt %, and clay %, especially in seagrass habitats. Westside Park (mid harbor) and Campbell Cove (outer harbor) were characterized by higher sand % (Table S1). Differences between seagrass and bare sediment habitats varied by site, with Westside Park showing the most pronounced differences: bare sediments had higher sand %, while seagrass had higher TOC and sulfate. PC1 and PC2 together explained 99.06% of the total variation in sediment samples (Fig. 2A). Significant differences were found for all environmental variables between sites, habitats, and habitats within sites (Tables S1 and S4).

Patterns of alpha- and beta-diversity in microbial and microeukaryote communities

We compared alpha-diversity between bare sediment and seagrass habitats within sites (Fig. S1). For microbes (16S), there were no significant differences for observed, Shannon, and Simpson diversity estimates between the two habitats (Fig. S1A). Westside Park displayed significantly higher evenness in seagrass, while Mason’s Marina had significantly higher evenness in bare sediments (Fig. S1A). For microeukaryotes (18S raw sediment), bare sediments had significantly higher observed diversity compared to seagrass at Campbell Cove and Westside Park (Fig. S1B). Additionally, bare sediments at Campbell Cove had significantly higher Shannon and Simpson diversity than seagrass (Fig. S1B). For the meiofauna data set (18S Ludox), bare sediments had higher observed diversity compared to seagrass in all three sites (Fig. S1C). At Westside Park, seagrass had significantly higher Shannon, Simpson, and evenness compared to bare sediments (Fig. S1C), while at Mason’s Marina, seagrass had significantly lower Shannon and Simpson diversity (Fig. S1C). Overall, alpha-diversity was consistently higher in Campbell Cove than in Mason’s Marina across all data sets, except for observed and Shannon in the 18S raw sediment data set (Fig. S2 and Table S3). Westside Park showed highly variable values, although it had the lowest means for all metrics in the meiofauna data set. When comparing habitats, bare sediments had higher observed and Shannon diversity in every data set. Furthermore, for microeukaryotes (18S raw sediment), bare sediment exhibited significantly higher Simpson (Table S3).

Microbial communities (16S data set) showed a strong separation by sampling sites, suggesting an environmental gradient from outer to inner sites at Bodega Harbor (Fig. 2B). Microeukaryotes (18S raw sediment) were also strongly structured by sampling sites, with slightly more overlap between Campbell Cove and Westside Park samples (Fig. 2C). Meiofaunal communities (18S Ludox) showed a similar pattern but with much more overlap among sample sites (Fig. 2D). Regardless of the data set, bare sediment communities from Westside Park strongly differed from all other samples. PERMANOVA analysis and pairwise tests detected significant differences among sites and between habitats for all data sets (Table S4).

Among bacterial families, Desulfocapsaceae and Desulfosarcinaceae dominated across sites, though Desulfosarcinaceae was less common in Westside Park bare sediments (Fig. S3A). Sulfurovaceae was the most abundant in Westside Park seagrass, but nearly absent in Westside Park bare sediments. Pirellulaceae and Rhodobacteraceae were more abundant in Westside Park bare sediments, whereas Halieaceae was slightly more prevalent at Mason’s Marina.

Overall, there were 1,618 differentially abundant predicted functions among sites, 64 related to sulfur cycling (Table S5). Pathways for dissimilatory sulfate reduction (DSR; e.g., dissimilatory sulfite reductase, adenylyl-sulfate reductase, and thiosulfate reductase) were enriched across samples, except at Westside Park bare sediments (Fig. S5). In contrast, pathways for glycosaminoglycan degradation (e.g., iduronate-2-sulfatase and N-acetylgalactosamine-4-sulfatase) were more abundant in Westside Park bare sediments. Dimethyl sulfide:cytochrome c2 reductase, a key enzyme in sulfur metabolism, was consistently elevated in Westside Park bare sediments and also enriched at Mason’s Marina. Select sulfur-associated predicted functions were used to analyze patterns in sulfur-utilizing, host-associated bacterial genera (Table S5). Westside Park bare sediments had the lowest abundances of sulfur-utilizing host-associated taxa, but the highest Candidatus Thiobios (an ectosymbiont of protists [39, 40]) abundance (Fig. 5A and Table S5). Sequences matching other potential endosymbionts and Thiotrichaceae dominated other sites and habitats.

Major microeukaryote (18S rRNA raw sediment) and meiofauna taxa (18S rRNA Ludox)

The 18S rRNA raw sediment data set was dominated by Metazoa, though other eukaryote groups were also abundant (Fig. 3B). The parasitic protist order Eugregarinorida was notably more abundant in raw sediment samples compared to the Ludox data set. Bacillariophyceae diatoms were also commonly found in raw sediment samples, especially in bare sediment habitats. The dinoflagellate group Suessiaceae was particularly abundant in the inner estuary at Mason’s Marina, while the Phytomyxea parasite Maullinia was more common in seagrass habitats, especially at Campbell Cove.

Differential abundance analysis detected differences among microeukaryotes between sites and among habitats. Enriched taxa included Gomphonema rosenstockianum (diatom) and Pirsonia formosa (Stramenopiles) at Campbell Cove, dinoflagellate species Bysmatrum subsalsum and Biecheleria brevisulcata at Mason’s Marina, Lecudina (Alveolates), Oblongichytrium sp. (Stramenopiles), and DSGM-81 at Westside Park bare sediments, and Minidiscus sp. (diatom) at Westside Park seagrasses (Fig. S6). Metazoa also dominated the 18S Ludox reads (Fig. S3B), followed by dinoflagellate Gonyaulacales, which was widespread except at Westside Park. Fungi Dikarya were found primarily in seagrass habitats across sites, and the dinoflagellate Peridiniales was most abundant at Campbell Cove.

Taxonomic composition of nematode communities

A total of 772 ASVs assigned to the phylum Nematoda were recovered from the 18S Ludox data set, representing 92 genera and 44 families. The most abundant families were Linhomoeidae, Chromadoridae, and Oncholaimidae (Fig. S3C). Although Anticomidae and Enchelidiidae were commonly detected, they were generally less abundant overall. Oncholaimidae, along with other putative Enoplida taxa that could not be assigned to lower taxonomic levels, was primarily present in bare sediments at Westside Park. Among genera, the most abundant were Ptycholaimellus, Terschellingia, and Viscosia (Fig. 3C). While Anticoma was frequently detected across sites, it was comparatively less abundant. Viscosia, Calyptronema, and Enoplida were primarily abundant in bare sediments at Westside Park. Daptonema and Metalinhomoeus were more prevalent in seagrass habitats at Campbell Cove and Westside Park.

Differential abundance analysis detected differences among nematode genera between sites and among habitats. Enriched taxa at Campbell Cove included Achromadora, Metalinhomoeus, Terschellingia, and Ptycholaimellus, with the latter two also enriched in Mason’s Marina (Fig. 4B). Genera enriched at Westside Park included Anticoma, Enoplida, and Viscosia, especially in bare sediments. Additionally, Acanthonchus, Paramonhystera, and Daptonema were especially enriched in Westside Park seagrass. At Mason’s Marina, unknown Monhysteridae, Microlaimus, and Spilophorella were more common. Seven unique ASV sequences identified as nematodes known to possess chemosynthetic bacterial symbionts (i.e., Astomonema, Eubostrichus, and Robbea) were also detected in Westside Park bare sediments (Fig. 5B). In terms of ASVs with taxonomic assignments to these known chemosynthetic nematode genera in Bodega Harbor, there were five unique ASVs assigned to Astomonema (92.916%–93.733% BLAST match), one Eubostrichus ASV (98.898% BLAST match), and two Robbea ASVs (97.507%–98.895% BLAST match).

Co-occurrence network between bacteria/archaea and nematodes

The co-occurrence network, built using correlations (r ≥ |0.4|) and significant (P-value < 0.05) correlations between microbial and nematode genera, is composed of 230 nodes and 768 edges (Table S6). We focused our analysis on positive correlations to explore instances where shared environmental preferences or host-microbe relationships may exist between specific microbes and nematodes. This subnetwork contained 203 nodes and 434 edges, with 31 nodes representing nematode genera and 172 nodes corresponding to bacteria/archaea orders (Fig. 6). The network’s average degree was 4.276, with a modularity of 0.462, indicating a moderate to high level of modular structure. This suggests the presence of well-defined groups of co-occurring bacterial and nematode genera. Supporting this, a large hub in the network included multiple nematode taxa such as Enoplida, Viscosia, and Astomonema, with smaller hubs made up of one or two nematode taxa. These patterns may reflect microhabitat structures, selective associations, or environmental gradients. Overall, we identified 99 unique bacterial/archaea orders, with Flavobacteriales (33), Rhizobiales (21), Cellvirbionales (18), Bacteroidales (17), and Desulfobacterales (17) showing the highest degree values. Among nematode nodes, Enoplida (92) had the greatest number of connections, followed by Viscosia (64), Astomonema (37), Terschellingia (36), and Ptycholaimellus (26). Correlation coefficients for the network ranged from 0.4 (Calomirolaimus—d142 V3 [Acidobacteriota]) to 0.95 (Enoplida—Flavobacteriales).

DISCUSSION

Location and benthic environment determine community assemblages

Our eDNA study reveals a pronounced environmental gradient across Bodega Harbor, evidenced by differences in sediment composition and the grouping of benthic communities by site (outer, Campbell Cove; mid, Westside Park; inner, Mason’s Marina) rather than by habitat type (bare sediments vs seagrass beds). Environmental analyses revealed a dominance of coarse sediment (sand %) at the mouth (Campbell Cove) and midpoint (Westside Park) of Bodega Harbor, while the innermost site contained significantly higher fractions of fine sediments (silt % and clay %), and TOC (Table S1), and these sediment grain size differences explained >95% of the differences between sites within Bodega Harbor (PC1; Fig. 2A). This aligns with established estuarine dynamics: high-energy conditions near the mouth retain coarser particles, while finer sediments are transported and deposited upstream (41). Similarly, higher TOC concentrations are typically found further from estuary mouths (8, 42), and in our study, high TOC was associated with higher organic matter and reduced water flow at Mason’s Marina (43). Environmental variables also indicated the influence of benthic habitat type, with seagrass areas exhibiting higher fine sediment content, sulfate, and TOC (Fig. 2A and Table S1), likely due to lowered water velocity that limits sediment resuspension (44). Additionally, macrophyte detritus is known to enhance carbon storage and influence sulfate reduction rates, raising TOC and sulfate levels in seagrass ecosystems (31, 45, 46).

Spatial patterns for microbial, microeukaryote, and meiofaunal communities reinforce the role of site-driven environmental filtering. Microbial communities exhibited distinct shifts moving from sandy, lower-TOC sites (Campbell Cove and Westside Park) to the fine-grained, organic matter-rich sediments at Mason’s Marina, likely reflecting adaptations to nutrient and biogeochemical gradients (47), as well as community assembly based on sediment grain size (48, 49) (Fig. 2B). Microeukaryotes showed a similar trend, though there was more overlap between Campbell Cove and Westside Park seagrass assemblages (Fig. 2C). This overlap likely reflects vegetation influence (50, 51), but overall, microeukaryote communities were shaped more strongly by site differences, which aligns with prior research supporting greater similarity between habitats within the same site (8, 52). The isolation of Mason’s Marina in ordinations suggests stronger environmental filtering, likely influenced by much higher TOC and finer sediments at this location compared to all other sites.

Meiofauna assemblages showed more substantial overlap across the three sites in Bodega Harbor, and meiofaunal species were the only group to show loose clustering by bare sediment vs seagrass habitat types (Fig. 2D). Within sites, alpha-diversity patterns also often showed significant differences between bare sediment versus seagrass habitat types, particularly at Westside Park and Mason’s Marina where all four metrics were significant (Fig. S1 and Table S3), although patterns were not consistent in terms of which habitat exhibited higher biodiversity. Environmental filtering is known to be a key factor influencing the distribution of larger-body metazoan meiofaunal species such as nematodes (53, 54). In seagrass ecosystems, differences in meiofauna and nematode communities have previously been observed at multiple spatial scales, and these variations are known to be linked to increased organic matter and sediment stability from macrophytes (9, 55, 56).

Our findings indicate that benthic assemblages are primarily influenced by location within Bodega Harbor, suggesting that both prokaryotes and microeukaryotes preferentially respond to broader estuarine patterns over microhabitat variation. Sediment properties such as grain size and organic matter content are known to be the most critical environmental factors determining the composition, abundance, and diversity of meiofauna in benthic ecosystems worldwide (57–59), and our results confirm that these site-specific factors are equally important in intertidal seagrass habitats. However, microhabitat also appeared to play a secondary role in structuring benthic assemblages within Bodega Harbor. For example, microeukaryotes and meiofauna typically showed lower diversity in seagrass compared to bare sediments, although this was not always significant (Fig. S1B and C). Notably, Westside Park seagrasses—featuring higher sulfate and finer sediments—exhibited higher meiofauna diversity except for measures of richness. In estuaries, habitat effects have been shown to be strongest where environmental stress is highest, typically upstream (13). In Bodega Harbor, microeukaryote and meiofauna communities at near-mouth seagrass beds (Campbell Cove) had lower diversity than bare sediment samples; however, the difference in diversity between bare sediments and seagrass becomes less pronounced toward the inner estuary (Mason’s Marina) (Fig. S1B and C and Table S3). Taken together, our results suggest that both location and habitat specificity play a role in structuring benthic prokaryotic and microeukaryote communities, most likely governed by a complex suite of factors that include organic matter content, sulfate levels, and variations in sediment particle size and the availability of interstitial spaces among sediment particles.

Distinction of benthic assemblages in intertidal mudflats at Westside Park

Bare sediments in Westside Park (at the midpoint of Bodega Harbor) stood out as the most distinct benthic community in both 16S and 18S metabarcoding data sets, always grouping strongly and separately from all other samples (Fig. 2B through D). These bare sediment samples were collected much farther away from seagrass beds compared to all other sites, and visual differences in this mudflat habitat are even obvious in satellite photos of Westside Park (solid blue diamond in Fig. 1A). Westside Park bare sediments were collected higher up the beach slope, and this site was characterized by mudflats exhibiting ripple marks and wave-like undulations in sediments that became exposed during low tide (Holly Bik, personal communication). In this study, bare sediments at Westside Park encompassed both “low mudflat” samples (sediment cores collected in the ripple troughs, where organic matter and detritus became concentrated) and “high mudflat” samples (sediment cores collected at the peak of a sediment ripple). This slightly farther mudflat site was chosen for coring because no clear unvegetated sediments were visible next to the Westside Park seagrass habitat during sample collection. Our initial data analysis suggested that high vs low mudflat sediment cores did not exhibit distinct benthic community assemblages, and because of this similarity, we ultimately grouped all cores into a single “bare sediments” category at Westside Park.

Our results highlight the unique features of these intertidal mudflat habitats in estuaries. Westside Park bare sediment samples had the highest sand %, and the lowest clay %, silt %, sulfate, and TOC—this granulometry somewhat resembled grain size proportions seen in Campbell Cove, but it was markedly different from geographically closer Westside Park seagrass samples (Fig. 2A and Table S1). At Westside Park bare sediments, the abiotic factors associated with tidal cycles (e.g., temperature, salinity, sediment moisture fluctuations) likely drive the observed community differences more strongly than the measured sediment properties. Previous studies have shown that intertidal mudflats display unique zonation patterns correlated with elevation and tidal exposure; for instance, higher elevated mudflats exposed to greater wave energy tend to retain more coarse sand and less fine sediment (60, 61), in line with the site characteristics seen in Westside Park bare sediment habitats. In contrast, bare sediments at Campbell Cove and Mason’s Marina, located directly adjacent to seagrass beds, may benefit from seagrass-related sediment stabilization and fine particle trapping (44), resulting in different hydrodynamic forces than at Westside Park bare sediments.

The distinction of Westside Park bare sediments is further supported by the prokaryotic and microeukaryote assemblages, which were observed in this habitat. Our ALDEx2 analyses revealed a statistically significant enrichment of ASVs with taxonomic assignments to nitrogen cycling bacteria (Nitrospiraceae, Nitrosococcaceae, Nitrosomonadaceae, and Nitrosopumilaceae) and, to a lesser extent, methane cycling bacteria (Methylophagaceae, Methylomonadaceae, and Methyloligellaceae) in Westside Park bare sediments (Fig. 4A). The dominance of these bacterial taxa at Westside Park is in line with previous studies showing that marine mudflats are often hotspots for microbial methanogenesis (62–64). In contrast, all other sites within Bodega Harbor—including bare sediment habitats at Campbell Cove and Mason’s Marina—showed a statistical enrichment of bacterial ASVs with taxonomic assignments to known sulfur-cycling groups, including Desulfobacteraceae, Thiotrichaceae, and Desulfatiglandaceae (Fig. 4A). The comparative depletion of sulfur-cycling bacterial ASVs in Westside Park bare sediments further underscores the distinct biogeochemical processes likely occurring within this intertidal mudflat habitat. This lack of sulfur-cycling pathways at Westside Park bare sediments was further supported by predicted gene functions from PICRUSt2 analyses, where DSR pathways—including thiosulfate reductase, adenylyl-sulfate reductase, and dissimilatory sulfite reductase—were highly enriched in seagrass habitats and adjacent bare sediments, but overall lacking in Westside Park bare sediments (Fig. S5). Generally, seagrass beds are known for high sulfur cycling due to oxygen production and increased organic matter content (22, 65), and the higher signal from sulfur-associated bacterial ASVs enriched in bare sediments directly adjacent to seagrasses at Campbell Cove and Mason’s Marina is more characteristic of the expected microbial metabolisms associated with seagrass ecosystems (22, 66).

Meiofauna diversity and community composition were also quite distinct within Westside Park bare sediments. Westside Park was the only site in Bodega Harbor where meiofauna assemblages in bare sediments exhibited significantly lower alpha-diversity than seagrass habitats at the same site (Fig. S1C); this pattern was significant across all four alpha-diversity metrics. At other sites, the biodiversity levels between bare sediments were not significantly different (Campbell Cove) or bare sediments showed significantly higher biodiversity across several alpha-diversity metrics (Mason’s Marina). Meiofaunal assemblages in Westside Park bare sediments were dominated by nematode ASVs with taxonomic assignments to the genus Viscosia and the group “Enoplida V17” (Fig. 3C and Fig. 4B). In ALDEx2 analyses examining differential abundance of ASVs, both Viscosia and Enoplida V17 were statistically enriched only in Westside Park bare sediments and statistically depleted at all other sites, including all other bare sediment habitats. The nematode order Enoplida (which includes the nematode genus Viscosia within family Oncholaimidae) is a highly diverse clade of marine nematodes and includes some of the largest-bodied marine species, many of which are facultative predators or scavengers (67, 68). Viscosia and other Enoplids (e.g., Enoplolaimus litoralis) are commonly found in sandy intertidal sediments (69–71). While these nematodes typically inhabit the upper sediment layers, they may move deeper when bioturbation occurs or in search of prey (72–74). Their swimming behavior enables rapid movement within sediments, alternating between active and inactive, which facilitates dispersal through tidal currents (75). Thus, the environmental conditions in Westside Park bare sediments—characterized by sandy sediments with larger interstitial spaces and low organic matter—may favor these larger predatory nematodes over deposit-feeding types (76, 77).

Nematode bioindicators of environmental stress and sediment quality

More broadly, the nematode taxa recovered across all sites in Bodega Harbor support the role of sediment properties and habitat quality in structuring benthic assemblages in seagrass ecosystems. Nematodes have been touted as ideal bioindicators for monitoring environmental pollution and disturbance in aquatic ecosystems, as their small size and rapid generation time allow nematode communities to respond quickly to chemical and physical environmental stressors (59, 78), including oil spills and heavy metal contamination (79, 80). For eDNA ASVs with taxonomic assignments to Nematoda, genus-level taxonomy can be used to infer relative sensitivity or resilience to environmental perturbation, and these genus-specific nematode responses are well known and based on scientific syntheses spanning several decades of empirical studies (59).

In Bodega Harbor, nematode ASVs with taxonomic assignments to the genera Microlaimus and Spilophorella were statistically more abundant in Mason’s Marina habitats but depleted in other sites (Fig. 4B). Microlaimus is a commonly used nematode bioindicator, known to be able to persist in environments with hydrocarbon pollution, such as mariculture and harbors (59, 81–83). Similarly, Spilophorella has been previously recovered as a dominant member of the nematode community in polluted sediments across Bohai Bay, China, with the relative abundance of one Spilophorella sp. increasing at the most contaminated sites (84). Although we did not measure sediment contaminant levels directly in this study, these nematode bioindicators suggest that chemical pollution and hydrocarbon contamination from the nearby marina could be higher in this area of Bodega Harbor with lower water flow. ASVs with taxonomic assignments to two other nematode genera, Terschellingia and Ptycholaimellus, were found to be statistically depleted in Westside Park bare sediments (Fig. 4B) but statistically more abundant at all other sites, including all other bare sediment habitats. Notably, Terschellingia is resistant to anthropogenic pollution (59, 77, 85, 86), but remains sensitive to physical disturbance and harsh environments, supporting their lower abundances in Westside Park bare sediments, which are presumed to have more harsh abiotic conditions and be subjected to higher wave action and exposure during low tides. Thus, in our data set, nematode bioindicators such as Terschellingia appear to suggest a distinct signature of physical disturbance and abiotic stress compared to potential environmental pollutants in Bodega Harbor sediments.

The nematode genera Metalinhomoeus and Daptonema were notably enriched in Westside Park seagrass beds as well as both habitat types in Campbell Cove (Fig. 4B), but depleted in Westside Park bare sediments and largely absent from Mason’s Marina (Fig. 3C and 4B). Daptonema is one of the most tolerant nematode genera known, resistant to anthropogenic disturbances (59, 86–88) and typically found in environments contaminated with diesel and metals (59, 82, 87). Although Daptonema spp. are typically able to thrive in fine/silty sediments; experimental work has shown that they are highly sensitive to decreased oxygen levels and partially sulfidic conditions (89), which likely explains the exclusion of Daptonema nematodes from exposed and oxygen-depleted mudflats at Westside Park and very high sulfate conditions at Mason’s Marina. In contrast, Metalinhomoeus tolerates high organic matter enrichment (90) and can survive even long-term anoxia (>300 days [91]), suggesting either an unknown sensitivity of this nematode genus to high sulfide, variable species-specific sensitivity patterns, or another unknown biological or abiotic factor which may be impacting the exclusion of Metalinhomoeus from certain habitats in Bodega Bay.

Finally, Achromadora nematodes were observed to be statistically enriched in only at Campbell Cove, in both bare sediments and seagrass beds at this site. Similarly, Dichromadora was enriched at both habitats in Campbell Cove and Mason’s Marina, but exhibited minimal abundance overall at Westside Park seagrasses or bare sediments. The higher abundance of Achromadora and Dichromadora at these sites could not be readily explained by any obvious biological or environmental factor, although Achromadora is typically known as an exclusively limnetic-terrestrial nematode (92) and its presence in Campbell Cove could potentially be explained by wind transport or runoff from nearby soils. In summary, nematode bioindicator data provide further support for the sediment properties and abiotic factors structuring benthic assemblages at each site in Bodega Harbor, and provide preliminary evidence for other key factors that may be impacting benthic seagrass habitats, such as hydrocarbon pollution or hypoxic sediment conditions.

Correlations between eukaryotes and bacteria in benthic seagrass ecosystems

In the present study, our paired 16S and 18S metabarcoding data sets allowed for a unique exploration of potential symbiotic relationships in seagrass ecosystems, both in terms of known symbioses and identification of potentially novel eukaryotic-bacterial interactions. Seagrass beds are hotspots for sulfur cycling, harboring a diversity of sulfur-utilizing bacteria and creating unique biogeochemical conditions that are known to promote symbiotic relationships between bacteria and eukaryotic hosts, including ciliate protists, gutless oligochaetes, flatworms, stilbonematid nematodes, and lucinid clams (93, 94). In each case, the eukaryotic host species harbors chemoautotrophic bacteria (either as ectosymbionts attached outside the body or endosymbionts housed within body tissues) that utilize sulfur to provide nutrition to the host, and in the case of ectosymbionts, offers additional physical protection from toxic hydrogen sulfide molecules. In many cases, the same phylogenetic clade of bacterial symbionts has independently associated with a wide variety of animal hosts. For example, Candidatus Thiosymbion bacteria form a coat of ectosymbiotic bacteria on the outside of the body of stilbonematid nematodes, and other species of Ca. Thiosymbion are endosymbionts of Olavius species gutless oligochaetes (94, 95).

As expected, our PICRUSt2 analyses of predicted gene functions in bacteria confirmed an abundance of sulfur-based metabolic pathways in most Bodega Harbor habitats. Predicted genes for DSR pathways—including thiosulfate reductase, adenylyl-sulfate reductase, and dissimilatory sulfite reductase—were enriched in seagrass habitats and adjacent bare sediments, except for Westside Park bare sediments (Fig. S5). The DSR pathway, which can oxidize water-insoluble sulfur particles, is a key function among symbiotic sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (96). In addition to predicted gene functions, we also utilized ASV taxonomy assignments to identify known eukaryote-associated bacterial symbionts that likely contribute to these sulfur cycles (Fig. 5A). For instance, Candidatus Thiobios (ectosymbiont of the marine ciliate Zoothamnium niveum) and Candidatus Thiodiazotropha (symbiont of lucinid clams such as Loripes lucinalis) were identified in our 16S rRNA data set and were correctly predicted as key taxa that contribute to DSR pathways in our PICRUSt2 analysis (Table S5) (96–100). Additionally, we also recovered taxa with ASV taxonomy assignments to the SUP05 cluster, including Thiotrichaceae (e.g., Thiothrix, Cocleimonas, and Leucothrix), Sulfurovum spp., and unclassified endosymbionts of the gastropod Alviniconcha and thyasirid clams Thyasira and Maorithyas hadalis (97–101). These SUP05 taxa were similarly predicted to be strong contributors to DSR pathways. Thus, our benthic eDNA approach was able to identify well-known signatures of symbiosis in seagrass ecosystems using only small volumes of sediment, without directly sampling for larger invertebrates or protistan symbioses.

We also carried out preliminary analyses to examine potential signatures of nematode-bacterial symbioses, based on the well-known occurrence of nematodes harboring endo- or ectosymbiotic bacteria in seagrass ecosystems (e.g., nematode subfamilies Stilbonematinae and Astomonematinae [94, 95]), and the common occurrence of other marine nematode genera within the nematode family Oncholaimidae where there is emerging evidence for novel bacterial symbioses with Pseudoaltromonas bacteria that form biofilms within the mouth, esophagus, and ovaries of host nematodes (102). Notably, ASVs assigned to nematode genera with known chemosynthetic symbionts (i.e., Astomonema, Eubostrichus, and Robbea) were detected only in Westside Park bare sediments (Fig. 5B). Oncholaimid nematodes, known for hosting Pseudoalteromonas symbionts and other as-yet-unidentified host-associated bacteria (102–104), were also statistically enriched in Westside Park bare sediments and comprised a significant proportion of the overall nematode community in this specific habitat (Fig. 3C). The higher sand content and increased interstitial spaces in Westside Park bare sediments, along with more variable sediment oxygen dynamics during tidal cycles, may offer an ideal habitat for these nematodes and their symbiont bacteria compared to other sites within Bodega Harbor (105). Thus, our data indicate that mudflat habitats within Bodega Harbor may be a hotspot for animal-bacterial symbioses, particularly for microbial metazoa such as nematodes.

Furthermore, nematodes with known symbionts were prominent in our nematode-bacterium co-occurrence network (Fig. 6). For example, the nematode genera Viscosia (Oncholaimidae) and Astomonema (Astomonematinae) exhibited 64 and 37 positive connections with bacterial ASVs, respectively (Fig. 6). Another Stilbonematid nematode genus, Robbea, had eight significant connections. All three nematodes shared bacterial co-occurrences with Microbulbifer (Cellvibrionales) and PAUC26f (Acidobacteriota) (Table S6). Although these bacterial ASVs do not represent the primary known Ca. Thiosymbion bacterial ectosymbionts in these nematode genera, we hypothesize that these statistically significant co-occurring ASVs may suggest bacterial biomarkers for suitable nematode habitat, or even potentially new co-symbionts of nematodes. Microbulbifer, known for degrading polysaccharides and producing broad-spectrum antibacterial alkanoyl imidazoles, is a known host-associated bacterial group that has been previously isolated from sponges and sea urchins (106–110). Similarly, PAUC26f is another sponge-associated bacterium, although its symbiotic function and evolutionary adaptations remain unknown (111). Other bacterial orders known to contain symbiotic and host-associated taxa (112) such as Bdellovibrionales, Rhizobiales, Legionellales, and Polyangiales showed notable co-occurrence patterns with nematodes from the genera Astomonema, Robbea, and Viscosia. One bacterium, Muricauda (Flavobacteriales), uniquely co-occurred with Viscosia and other Enoplid nematodes, and previous work has shown that this bacterial group is known to improve coral and clam resilience to stress via antioxidant carotenoid production (113–115).

Many additional nematode genera appeared prominently in bacterial co-occurrence networks, including almost all of the statistically significant nematode bioindicator taxa discussed above, such as nematodes from the genera Microlaimus, Spilophorella, Terschellingia, Ptycholaimus, Daptonema, Metalinhomoeus, Achromadora, and Dichromadora. Uniquely, Microlaimus and Spilophorella shared a connection with Thiogranum (Ectothiorhodospirales), a chemolithoautotrophic bacterium often linked to high concentrations of heavy metals and nutrients (116). The co-occurrence of these nematodes and Thiogranum suggests that these taxa may be adapted to habitats shaped by active sulfur cycling and high organic matter turnover at sites such as Mason’s Marina (81, 82, 117). Interestingly, the nematode Diplolaimelloides that was found only in seagrass habitats (Fig. 3C) co-occurred with Campylobacterales (e.g., sulfur-oxidizing genera Sulfurimonas and Sulfurovum) and Oceanospirillales (aerobic Marinomonas). In other marine invertebrate hosts, Campylobacterales are well-described symbionts known to possess complete sulfur-oxidizing complexes and can fix carbon dioxide via the reverse tricarboxylic acid (rTCA) and Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) pathways using electrons from reduced sulfur (104, 118), also acquiring metabolites like vitamin B6 and molybdenum cofactor from hosts (118–121). Oceanospirillales bacteria are known to have symbiotic relationships with sea urchins, corals, mussels, and bone-eating worms, providing hydrocarbon degradation (122, 123). Putative nematode-bacterium associations such as Diplolaimelloides–Oceanospirillales may support seagrass resilience and carbon sequestration (1, 2, 4, 124). Finally, associations with sulfur-oxidizing Sulfurimonas and Sulfurovum bacteria are known to provide carbohydrates and amino acids to host invertebrates or bacterivores (100, 117, 125–127), potentially aiding environmental adaptation in sulfide-rich seagrass sediments. Further studies are needed to confirm these host-associated relationships among microscopic benthic invertebrates and investigate potential ramifications for ecosystem function in seagrass ecosystems worldwide. Bacterial symbioses in meiofaunal metazoan groups remain severely underexplored (128), with most work focusing on species with known associations with chemosynthetic bacteria (127). Symbiosis in seagrass ecosystems may be much more prevalent than currently known, and our data suggest some intriguing associations that warrant further exploration. Although we cannot draw any definitive conclusions from co-occurrence networks based on metabarcoding ASVs, these results lay the groundwork for hypothesis generation and more targeted -Omics work in future studies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study area

Bodega Harbor, located in northern California, USA (38°19′59″N 123°02′53″W), is approximately 105 km north of San Francisco. This small low-inflow estuary (~5 km2) features extensive intertidal mudflats and limited fringing salt marshes. Finer sediment and higher organic content are found in the inner part of the harbor, whereas sandy sediment dominates near the harbor mouth (128). The estuary is relatively shallow (~2 m of depth at high tide) and connects to the Pacific Ocean via a dredged channel in the northern part of Bodega Harbor (129). The system is strongly affected by the adjacent ocean, with up to 80% water exchange during each tidal cycle (129, 130). Freshwater input from small streams is minimal and occurs mostly during the winter rainy season. Mean summer water temperatures range from 15.9 to 18.1°C (43, 130).

Sampling

Sediment samples were collected at low tide from Bodega Harbor on 7 July 2018 across three different sites: Mason’s Marina (inner estuary), Westside Park (mid estuary), and Campbell Cove (outer estuary). At each site, four sediment cores were taken from vegetated (seagrass) and non-vegetated (bare sediment) habitats using a 10 × 10 cm PVC core (Fig. 1). Samples were placed in Ziploc bags, kept cool in an ice chest, and transported to the laboratory, where they were stored at −80°C until processing. Vegetated sediments at Mason’s Marina and Westside Park were covered in eelgrass (Zostera marina), while those at Campbell Cove also contained sea lettuce (Ulva lactuca). At Westside Park, no clearly unvegetated sediments were found adjacent to the seagrass habitat, so “bare sediments” here refer to both low and high mudflat samples collected at a location slightly farther away from seagrass beds. In the laboratory, sediment cores were split in half for molecular procedures and sediment analysis. Sediment characterization measured sand (sand %), silt (silt %), and clay (clay %), TOC, and sulfate sulfur (SO4-S). All sediment analyses were performed at the UC Davis Analytical Laboratory (Table S1). Sediment samples for molecular work were processed two weeks after collection.

Extraction of meiofauna from sediment samples

To characterize meiofauna communities, with an emphasis on marine nematodes, sediment samples were washed using filtered artificial seawater (Instant Ocean, Spectrum Brands, Inc., USA) over a 45 μm sieve. Organisms were extracted from the sieved sediment by flotation using Ludox TM-50 (specific gravity of 1.15 [131]), following protocols described in Somerfield and Warwick (132). To minimize cross-contamination, sieves and glassware were sterilized and thoroughly washed between each core. The equipment was washed with Milli-Q ultrapure water, soaked in a 10% Sodium Metabisulfite solution (Pro Supply Outlet, CA, USA) for 1 h, and rinsed in Milli-Q ultrapure water before processing the next sample (133). The final volume in each Falcon tube was adjusted to 20 mL; a 1 mL aliquot of the meiofauna community was set aside for morphological identification and DNA barcoding of nematodes as described in Pereira et al. (134). DNA sequences are available in GenBank under accession numbers PV244184–PV244287 (135). The remaining sample in each Falcon tube was centrifuged at 5,000 rpm for 2 min to concentrate the organisms into a single pellet. After centrifugation, the supernatant was carefully discarded, and community DNA was extracted from the entire pellet (see below).

Metabarcoding of microbial and microeukaryote communities

DNA was extracted from raw sediment (0.25 g of sediment) and Ludox-processed samples in triplicate using the ZymoBIOMICS DNA Miniprep Kit (Catalog Nos. D4300, Zymo Research Corp, Irvine, CA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. To monitor potential kit contamination, blank controls containing water were included during the DNA extraction. Lysates were stored at −80°C until PCR amplification. For microbes, the V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified with primers 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) (136). For the microeukaryotes (raw sediment and Ludox), a 400-bp segment of the V1–V2 hypervariable regions of the 18S rRNA gene was amplified using primers F04 (5′-GCTTGTCTCAAAGATTAAGCC-3′) and R22 (5′-GCCTGCTGCCTTCCTTGGA-3′). We chose to focus on the V1-V2 hypervariable region for the 18S rRNA metabarcoding approach because this primer set is 100% conserved in nematodes and provides recovery of >20 meiofaunal phyla, in addition to broad but patchy recovery of many fungi and protist groups (137). The V1–V2 region is thus more useful for benthic eDNA studies compared to V4 or V9, which are more commonly utilized in zooplankton and protist studies (138–140). All PCRs were performed in a sterilized laminar-flow hood. PCRs (25 µL total volume) consisted of 1 µL of DNA template, 0.5 µL of each primer (10 µM), 10 µL of Platinum Hot Start PCR Master Mix (2×) (Thermo Fisher), and 13 µL of molecular-grade water. Positive (ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard, Zymo Research, Irvine, CA) and negative controls (molecular-grade water) were included. PCR amplification followed the protocols described in Pereira et al. (134). DNA concentrations were quantified with a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer with the Qubit dsDNA HS (High Sensitivity) Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and normalized before library pooling. Libraries were purified using magnetic beads and size-selected (300–700 bp) on a BluePippen (Sage Science). Sequencing was carried out at the UC Davis Genomics Core on the Illumina MiSeq Platform (2 × 300 bp PE) in two runs. All wet lab protocols and bioinformatics scripts are freely available on GitHub (https://github.com/BikLab/bodega-bay-eDNA).

Processing Illumina data using DADA2

Raw Illumina data sets were demultiplexed using a custom script to handle dual-barcode combinations. Primer sequences were trimmed with the cutadapt plugin (141). ASVs were denoised and estimated using DADA2 (142) within QIIME2 v2024.2 (143) with default parameters and by truncating forward and reverse reads at 250 bp and 200 bp, respectively. Taxonomy for 16S ASVs was assigned using the BLAST + consensus taxonomy classifier (144) with default parameters (top 10 hits, minimum confidence of 0.80, and a minimum consensus of 0.51), with the 16S rRNA SILVA 138 SSURef NR99 database as reference (145). For 18S ASVs, sequence reads were truncated at 209 bp (forward) and 230 bp (reverse), and taxonomy was assigned using the BLAST+ top hit approach (144) with a minimum confidence of 0.90 as recommended for meiofaunal communities with limited representation in reference databases (135). A custom reference data set (https://github.com/BikLab/MeioDB-18SrRNA) was used for 18S ASVs, composed of the SILVA 138 SSURef NR99 database with added short (~350 bp) and large (~700 bp) fragments covering the V1–V2 regions of the 18S rRNA retrieved from NCBI, improving the accuracy and precision of nematode taxonomy assignments (135).

Bioinformatics and statistical analysis

Samples with low read count or suspected errors from DADA2 denoising were excluded from downstream analysis (Table S2). Potential contaminants were identified using the R package decontam v1.28 (146) with the prevalence method and a contaminant classification threshold of 0.5 (Table S2). In addition to blanks and controls, samples “MM.SG.1.3” and “CC.SG.3.1” were removed from the 16S rRNA raw sediment data set, and “CC.B.1.1.RS,” “CC.B.3.3.RS,” and “WP.MFL.2.1.1.RS” from the 18S rRNA raw sediment data set due to low read counts and suspected naming errors. For the 16S rRNA data, reads identified as mitochondrial, chloroplast, or unassigned at the domain level were removed. For the 18S rRNA data (Ludox and raw sediment), reads annotated as “humans,” unassigned, Charophyta (seagrass), and Ulva (green algae) were excluded to focus on microeukaryotes. Bar plots for 16S and 18S rRNA data sets were generated at multiple taxonomic ranks, and less abundant taxa were grouped as “Other.” Known sulfur-utilizing taxa with host associations were further visualized. Meiofauna and nematode diversity were analyzed in greater detail in the Ludox 18S data set, including BLAST (144) and phylogenetic placement (using SILVA ACT [147, 148]) of nematode ASVs where host genera are known to be obligately associated with chemosynthetic bacterial symbionts (Astomonema, Eubostrichus, and Robbea).

Quality-filtered ASV tables were used to assess the microbial and microeukaryote community compositions across habitats (seagrass and bare sediments) and sites (i.e., Campbell Cove, Westside Park, and Mason’s Marina). Alpha-diversity metrics—observed diversity, Shannon diversity H′ (log2), inverted Simpson (D), and Pielou’s evenness (J’)—were calculated using phyloseq v1.52 (149) and compared among habitats and sites. A Kruskal-Wallis (KW) test, followed by Wilcoxon pairwise analyses, was performed. The 16S rRNA sample “CC.SG.3.2” (Campbell Cove seagrass) habitat was excluded from the alpha-diversity analysis due to excessive reads (420,654) compared to the data set mean (21,256 ± 8,172). Rarefaction was not carried out for alpha-diversity analyses because it is not considered appropriate for eDNA data sets based on statistical principles (150), as rarefied counts can result in a high rate of false positives and/or discard samples that can be accurately analyzed by alternative methods. We have thus followed the recommendations of the phyloseq package developers in choosing not to rarefy data for alpha-diversity analyses.

Beta-diversity was assessed using Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and ASV relative abundances and visualized via PCoA with phyloseq (150). Permutational analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) was used to test for differences (P-value < 0.05) among sites and habitats. PCA using Euclidean distance matrix based on standardized environmental variables was used to characterize sites and habitats. To identify taxa (16S, bacteria; 18S raw sediment, microeukaryotes; and 18S Ludox, nematodes) that contribute most to community differences among sites and between habitats, differential abundance analyses were performed at different taxonomic ranks using the ALDEx2 v1.40 (151) after CLR transformation. Significance was assessed by KW tests (P < 0.05), and false discovery rates (FDRs) were estimated via the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure (152). Functional profiles of bacterial communities (16S rRNA) were predicted with PICRUSt2 v2.6.2-foss-2023a (153), with sulfur cycling/metabolism pathways analyzed separately as above.

To examine co-occurrence between bacteria (16S data set) and nematode genera (18S Ludox data set) in 82 shared samples, networks were constructed using the Spearman correlation coefficient (r ≥ |0.4|, P < 0.05 after FDR-corrected via the BH procedure) with the HMSC v.5.2-3 package. A prevalence threshold (≥5%) was applied, and taxa were agglomerated at the genus level and transformed to relative abundances. Networks were visualized with Gephi v0.10.1 (154) using the Fruchterman–Reingold algorithm (155); nodes represented bacteria (colored, labeled at the order level) and nematodes (labeled by taxon). For this study, all visualizations were created in ggplot2 v3.5.2 (156) using R version 4.5.0 (157).
