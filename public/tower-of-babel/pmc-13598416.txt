ABM/P-15-enhanced bone graft in oncologic cervical spine reconstruction: two case reports and narrative discussion
Authors: Henrique Ribeiro Rodrigues Neto, Federico Cardahi, Kaveh Mozafari-Lorestani, William Kubik, Julien Montreuil, Anthony Bozzo, Ahmed Aoude
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBone consolidation in musculoskeletal oncology is challenging after pathological fracture, radiotherapy, repeated surgery, extensive reconstruction, or compromised vascularity. Successful healing also requires segmental mechanical stability. Anorganic bone mineral/P-15 (ABM/P-15) bone grafts provide an osteoconductive mineral scaffold with a synthetic collagen-mimetic peptide surface, but their use in oncologic cervical spine reconstruction remains sparsely described.Case DescriptionAt the McGill University Health Centre, a tertiary referral center, we report two complex cervical oncologic reconstructions in which ABM/P-15 was used as one component of multicomponent fusion constructs. A young adult male with metastatic solitary fibrous tumor, prior radiotherapy, upper cervical nonunion, and pathologic fracture underwent revision occiput-to-T2 fusion with local autograft and ABM/P-15. A young adult female with recurrent cervical chordoma after prior resection and proton-beam radiotherapy underwent combined anterior and posterior tumor resection, instrumentation, and fibular graft reconstruction augmented with ABM/P-15. In both cases, rigid reconstruction plus adjunctive grafting was followed by CT and radiographic evidence of bridging bone or host-graft incorporation without implant loosening or pseudoarthrosis at approximately 12 months. Standardized Visual Analog Scale (VAS)/Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) and Neck Disability Index (NDI) scores were not collected.ConclusionsThese two cases document radiographic fusion after complex oncologic cervical reconstructions in which ABM/P-15 was used as an adjunct. The perceived rationale was to provide an osteoconductive, collagen-mimetic surface in compromised host biology, but these cases do not establish that ABM/P-15 independently caused fusion or improved oncologic safety. The findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Two complex oncologic cervical reconstructions treated with rigid stabilization, structural/autogenous grafting, and adjunctive anorganic bone mineral/P-15 (ABM/P-15) demonstrated computed tomography evidence of bridging bone or host-graft incorporation at approximately 12 months, without implant loosening or pseudoarthrosis.

What is known and what is new?

• Prior radiotherapy, recurrent tumor, repeated surgery, compromised host biology, and insufficient segmental stability can increase the risk of nonunion or hardware failure.

• These two cases add descriptive clinical experience with ABM/P-15 used as one adjunct within mechanically stable, multicomponent oncologic cervical reconstructions.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• ABM/P-15 should be discussed as a possible adjunct rather than a proven determinant of fusion in this setting. No practice recommendation follows from these two cases; prospective oncology-specific studies with standardized fusion and clinical-outcome assessment are needed.

Introduction

Bone grafting has a long history in skeletal and spinal reconstruction (1,2). In spinal oncology, tumor-related bone destruction and mechanical instability are important considerations in treatment planning (3,4). Available grafting approaches include autograft, allograft, synthetic substitutes, and biologic adjuncts. Allograft use requires attention to tissue banking and disease-transmission risk (5,6). Hydroxyapatite and recombinant human bone morphogenetic protein-2 have been investigated in tibial fracture settings (7,8), but these studies do not directly establish their performance in oncologic cervical reconstruction.

Anorganic bone mineral/P-15 (ABM/P-15) bone grafts combine anorganic bone mineral with a synthetic 15-amino-acid peptide sequence designed to mimic the cell-binding domain of type I collagen. The mineral phase functions as an osteoconductive scaffold, while the peptide surface is intended to support osteoblast adhesion and matrix formation (9-12). This mechanism differs from bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), which are osteoinductive cytokines intended to drive bone formation through growth-factor signaling. Clinical evidence for ABM/P-15 comes primarily from degenerative cervical and lumbar spine populations; oncology-specific evidence remains limited and indirect (13-18).

We present two cases of complex cervical spine reconstruction in oncologic patients where ABM/P-15 was used as one component of a broader reconstructive strategy. This manuscript is framed as two clinical case reports with a focused narrative literature discussion, and is not intended to be a systematic review or PRISMA-structured evidence synthesis.

For this report, successful healing/fusion was defined operationally as continuous bridging trabecular bone or host-graft continuity on follow-up computed tomography (CT) across the intended fusion surfaces, accompanied by maintained segmental mechanical stability and absence of pseudoarthrosis, implant loosening, or hardware failure. This definition was informed by CT-based fusion-assessment concepts including the Glassman grading approach, although that lumbar posterolateral fusion scale was not formally applied to these heterogeneous cervical oncologic reconstructions (19). We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0119/rc).

Case presentation

For both cases, the authors confirmed that the biologic adjunct was a Cerapedics ABM/P-15 product, described as i-FACTOR/PearlMatrixTM, with PearlMatrix representing the same/rebranded product name. A 10-cc product volume was used in each case. The supplied documentation describes porcine-derived anorganic bone mineral particles coated with P-15 peptide and carried in bovine type I collagen. The product was hydrated with sterile saline or autogenous bone marrow aspirate, placed at the graft site, used with autograft or autogenous bone marrow aspirate, did not directly contact tumor-bed margins, and was not subject to institutional or cost restriction.

Radiographic fusion was assessed retrospectively on CT obtained at 6 and 12 months. Two evaluators reviewed the imaging and agreed regarding fusion status. Fusion was described when bridging trabecular bone or host-graft continuity was seen across the intended fusion surfaces, with maintained alignment and no radiographic pseudoarthrosis, implant loosening, or hardware failure. Dynamic radiographs were not used for the formal fusion assessment, the Glassman or another validated grading scale was not formally applied, and no formal interobserver-agreement statistic was calculated. Case 1: metastatic solitary fibrous tumor with upper cervical nonunion and pathologic fracture

A young adult male with metastatic solitary fibrous tumor had a long oncologic and surgical history. Earlier treatment included intracranial surgery in 2013, cervical decompression/fusion and adjuvant neck radiotherapy in 2019, axillary lymph-node dissection for metastatic solitary fibrous tumor in 2020, and revision cervical surgery for failed hardware in 2021. In 2022, imaging again raised concern for recurrence, and systemic therapy consultation was arranged.

He presented in July 2023 with severe cervical pain, inability to maintain his head upright, and marked functional limitation. Examination demonstrated upper cervical tenderness and clinical instability without motor or sensory deficit. CT showed a lytic lesion involving the left C1 lamina/transverse process with a pathologic fracture and C1–C2 nonunion; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed adjacent soft-tissue disease with foraminal extension and mild thecal sac compression. After multidisciplinary review, revision stabilization was recommended.

On July 14, 2023, he underwent removal of prior implants and revision occiput-to-T2 posterior instrumentation. Local autograft from spinous processes was placed along the prepared posterolateral fusion beds, and the reported Cerapedics ABM/P-15 preparation was used as an adjunct at the fusion surfaces. The response materials state that the product did not contact tumor-bed margins. The patient remained neurologically intact postoperatively and began early physiotherapy.

At follow-up, radiographs at 3 months showed preserved alignment, CT at 6 months demonstrated bilateral bridging bone formation and progressive graft incorporation, and CT/radiographs at approximately 12 months showed continuous trabecular bone across the instrumented segments without pseudoarthrosis, implant loosening, or hardware failure (Figures 1-3). Visual Analog Scale (VAS)/Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) and Neck Disability Index (NDI) scores were not collected. The patient was reported to be neurologically intact, have no mechanical neck pain, and returned to playing basketball after having been unable to sit upright before surgery.

Case 2: recurrent cervical chordoma treated with fibular reconstruction and ABM/P-15

A young adult female had recurrent cervical chordoma after prior surgery and radiotherapy. In June 2019 she underwent posterior C1–C6 surgery followed by an anterior approach with tumor removal. Proton-beam radiotherapy was arranged in Boston in September 2019. In 2022 she developed right-arm numbness and neck symptoms, and subsequent imaging showed progression involving the C2 and C4 foramina with surrounding lymph nodes. After tumor-board review and biopsy planning, en bloc resection was recommended.

In April 2023, she underwent anterior tumor resection, including partial C2–C4 corpectomy, with anterior and posterior stabilization. The previous fibular graft was preserved and reshaped, anterior fixation was provided with plate/screws spanning C4–C7 while preserving the prior C4–C5 plate, and grafting was performed at the intervertebral and host-graft interfaces. Posterior instrumentation included C2 pedicle screws, C3–C6 lateral mass screws, bilateral rods, and a structural fibular allograft strut from C1 to C5, augmented with the reported ABM/P-15 material. The response materials state that the product did not contact tumor-bed margins.

The immediate postoperative course was stable, with improved pain and upper-limb strength, 48 hours of intensive-care monitoring, adequate analgesia, early physiotherapy, and no vascular or infectious complication. Radiographs at 3 months showed maintained hardware position; CT at 6 months showed progressive bridging bone formation at host-graft interfaces; and CT/radiographs at approximately 12 months showed graft integration without pseudoarthrosis or implant failure (Figures 4,5). VAS/NRS and NDI scores were not collected. The patient was reported to be neurologically intact and without mechanical neck pain.

Cancer-treatment review found no systemic therapy recommended for this patient and no chemotherapy, immunotherapy, bisphosphonate, denosumab, kinase inhibitor, or targeted systemic therapy. In April 2023, resection margins were positive. By October 2024, imaging showed a new left-sided cervical lesion concerning for local recurrence; no additional surgery, radiotherapy, or systemic therapy was recommended, and palliative care was pursued. She subsequently developed hydrocephalus requiring ventriculoperitoneal shunting and multiple shunt revisions in January 2025.

Discussion

These cases describe radiographic fusion or graft incorporation after two high-risk cervical oncologic reconstructions in which ABM/P-15 was used as an adjunct. The observations are clinically relevant because both patients had features associated with impaired fusion biology, including prior radiotherapy, recurrent tumor, repeat surgery, and extensive reconstruction. However, they should be interpreted as initial observations rather than evidence of efficacy: both patients underwent multicomponent reconstruction with rigid fixation, autograft and/or fibular graft material, and multidisciplinary perioperative care. The contribution of ABM/P-15 cannot be isolated.

Mechanical stability is also central to successful healing. A biologic adjunct cannot compensate for persistent motion, inadequate load sharing, implant loosening, or failed alignment. In both cases, rigid instrumented stabilization and structural/autogenous grafting were essential elements of the reconstruction, and the perceived role of ABM/P-15 was limited to providing an osteoconductive scaffold with a collagen-mimetic cell-attachment surface in a compromised healing environment. This rationale remains theoretical in oncologic cervical reconstruction and should not be read as proof of an independent graft effect.

The mechanism of ABM/P-15 should also be described precisely and distinguished from BMP-based strategies. The anorganic bone mineral component provides an osteoconductive scaffold. The P-15 peptide is designed to mimic the cell-binding domain of type I collagen and may support osteoblast attachment and matrix production. By contrast, BMPs act through growth-factor signaling and are generally described as osteoinductive in selected approved indications. ABM/P-15 therefore should not be assumed to be osteoinductive in oncologic reconstruction, nor does this two-case experience demonstrate comparative benefit over autograft, allograft, BMPs, or other biologic strategies.

The oncologic context is important. Radiotherapy, local recurrence, systemic therapy, and compromised tissue quality can affect bone healing and survival independently of any graft material. In the solitary fibrous tumor case, pazopanib therapy and multiple radiation courses are potential confounders when interpreting fusion. In the chordoma case, later local progression and hydrocephalus reflected the aggressive oncologic course; this case report cannot determine whether ABM/P-15 had any favorable or unfavorable oncologic effect. Statements about oncologic safety or tumor progression should therefore be limited to descriptive reporting.

The cases are heterogeneous in diagnosis, prior treatment, reconstruction type, systemic therapy exposure, and oncologic course. They should not be pooled as an efficacy series. Instead, they suggest that standardized reporting of biologic implant details, construct stability, fusion criteria, oncologic treatment exposure, and patient-reported outcomes is essential if ABM/P-15 or related graft materials are studied prospectively in spine oncology.

Limitations

This report is limited by its two-case retrospective design, substantial case heterogeneity, lack of a control group, and inability to isolate the effect of ABM/P-15 from fixation, autograft, allograft/fibular graft reconstruction, radiotherapy history, systemic therapy, construct stability, or host biology. Fusion assessment was retrospective rather than prospectively protocolized; it was based on CT imaging at 6 and 12 months, was reviewed by two evaluators who agreed, did not use dynamic radiographs, did not formally apply the Glassman or another validated fusion grading scale, and did not include a formal interobserver-agreement statistic. VAS/NRS, NDI, and prospectively collected functional outcome scores were not available.

Conclusions

Two complex oncologic cervical reconstructions using ABM/P-15 as an adjunct within mechanically stable, multicomponent constructs demonstrated radiographic fusion or host-graft incorporation at approximately 12 months. These cases are descriptive and hypothesis-generating. They do not establish independent graft efficacy, comparative benefit, or oncologic safety, and they support the need for standardized prospective evaluation in oncologic spine reconstruction.
