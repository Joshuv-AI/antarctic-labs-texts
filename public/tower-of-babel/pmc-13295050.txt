Predicting Surfactant Oil–Water Interfacial Tension Using Gated Message-Passing Graph Neural Networks
Authors: Suiyang Liu, Yanrong Cui, Jie Wang, Hualei Xu, Houshun Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Using surfactants to manipulate the interfacial tension (IFT) of oil–water systems represent a critical strategy for enhanced oil recovery (EOR). However, predicting the physical properties of surfactants based on their molecular structure remains a challenging task, as conventional machine learning methods struggle to capture the coupled interactions between molecular structures and environmental parameters, while existing graph neural networks predominantly focus on single-molecule representations and overlook the characteristics of the system environment. Accordingly, a Gated Message-passing Graph Neural Network with an Attention Mechanism (Gated-MPNN-AT) is proposed to integrate molecular graph structures and environmental features, aiming to achieve accurate prediction of interfacial tension in surfactant–oil–water systems. The model dynamically controls the message passing process through a dual gated mechanism, adopts a Cross-Attention mechanism to achieve the in-depth interaction between molecular topological features and environmental parameters, and designs a hybrid robust loss function to handle the IFT data with cross-order-of-magnitude distribution. The research results show that the prediction accuracy of the model is better than that of traditional machine learning methods (such as Random Forest (RF) and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)) and some graph neural network methods (such as Graph Convolutional Network (GCN), and Graph Attention Network (GAT)). Ablation experiments have confirmed that the gated mechanism increases the coefficient of determination (R 2) by 4.8%, and the Cross-Attention fusion strategy reduces the mean absolute error (MAE) by 21.3%. Meanwhile, the model has good generalization ability and strong anti-interference ability against abnormal IFT data.

Introduction

Enhanced oil recovery (EOR) technology, as a key approach to improving the recovery efficiency of depleted oil reservoirs, focuses on effectively regulating the interfacial tension (IFT) of the crude oil–water system, thereby reducing capillary pressure and optimizing the fluid displacement process. In the EOR strategy, the injected water containing surfactants has attracted much attention because it can significantly reduce the oil–water IFT and improve the reservoir wettability. However, its effectiveness highly depends on the molecular structure, concentration of the surfactant and environmental parameters. − Nevertheless, accurately predicting the IFT value of surfactant containing systems poses a severe challenge. Although traditional experimental methods are the standard for measuring IFT, they are costly, time-consuming, and are particularly difficult to apply on a large scale under the high-temperature and high-pressure reservoir conditions. Although existing machine learning models have made progress in the prediction of IFT, most of them focus on simplified component systems. Liu et al. used various machine learning methods to build models for predicting the IFT between oil and water. Ricardo et al. established a prediction model for the air–water IFT using the Random Forest (RF) algorithm. It is worth noting that the chemical structure of surfactant molecules has a decisive influence on IFT, while traditional regression models based on input parameters have difficulty in capturing the nonlinear relationships of molecular-level interactions. In recent years, Graph Neural Networks (GNN) have provided new ideas for solving such problems due to their excellent ability in processing graph-structured data. , GNN can encode molecular structures as graphs for representation. Through the message passing mechanism, it can automatically learn the correlation between molecular features and molecular properties, so as to more accurately quantify the molecular structure effect of surfactants. − Brozos et al. collected data on 108 surfactant mixtures and developed a graph neural network to predict the critical micelle concentration (cmc) of surfactants at different temperatures. Hödl et al. collected a data set of 1,624 surfactants and established a multitask graph neural network, enabling the model to predict multiple properties of surfactants. This indicates that graph neural networks show great potential in the prediction of surfactant properties.

However, most of the existing studies focus on the molecular properties of surfactants with insufficient attention paid to other environmental parameters in the system. As a result, it is difficult to fully capture the influence of the combined effects of the molecular structure and various environmental parameters on the properties. This study collected and sorted out the experimental data of surfactants tested in oil–water systems during the oil extraction process and established an IFT prediction model based on surfactant molecular graphs, system composition, and operating conditions. , By integrating the molecular structure information with experimental environmental parameters, a graph neural network architecture capable of reflecting the structure-performance relationship was constructed to achieve the regression prediction of IFT. Additionally, multiple experimental tests were designed to demonstrate that the prediction performance and generalization ability of the optimized model were superior to those of traditional models. This research reduced the cost and development time of surfactants for enhanced oil recovery, contributing to efficient and stable production of oil and gas resources.

A critical limitation in compiling this multisource data set is the inconsistent reporting of critical micelle concentration values. The majority of the 112 source articles did not provide CMC data for the surfactants under study, and among those that did, the reported values for the same surfactant often varied across different literature sources due to differences in experimental methods, temperature, salinity, and purity. Consequently, it was not feasible to classify each data point precisely relative to its CMC, nor to normalize concentrations by CMC as a reduced variable. Instead, absolute concentration in wt % was used as a continuous input feature, and the model implicitly learns the concentration–IFT relationship as an averaged trend across the full data set. This simplification means that the model does not explicitly distinguish between pre-CMC and post-CMC adsorption regimes, and its predictions should be interpreted as effective equilibrium IFT values rather than mechanistically decomposed into micellization-controlled and nonmicellization-controlled contributions. The implications of this limitation for model interpretation and applicability are discussed further in Section .

Data Preprocessing

Molecular Descriptors of Surfactant Preprocessing

The processing of the molecular structure of surfactants is a fundamental step in the entire process. Converting molecular descriptors into graph structures and calculating molecular fingerprints are two mainstream preprocessing paradigms. Among them, the molecular fingerprint is a more traditional and widely used representation method. It “hashes” or “folds” the entire molecule into a fixed-length binary or integer vector through a set of predefined rules or substructure lists. The graph structure transformation retains the native representation of the complete topological relationship. This approach aims to directly and losslessly map the essential structures of molecules. Its core idea is to regard a molecule as a graph, where atoms serve as nodes and chemical bonds act as edges. In this paper, graph structure transformation is usually a better choice. Since interfacial behavior highly depends on the amphiphilic structure of surfactant molecules, the precise spatial arrangement of functional groups, and the orientation of molecules at the interface, all of which require a fine modeling of the complete topology and local chemical environment of the molecule.

The process of converting molecular descriptors to a graph structure is shown in Figure . First, the input SMILES string is processed using the RDKit tool (version 2025.03.6) to convert it into a molecular object that contains the complete connection relationships between atoms and chemical bonds. Subsequently, the molecular object is deconstructed: the algorithm traverses each atom and generates a high-dimensional feature vector on the basis of its chemical properties. This vector encodes key physicochemical descriptors, such as atomic type, hybridization state, formal charge, and aromaticity, forming the node features in the graph structure. Simultaneously, the system traverses all chemical bonds, records the connection pairs between atoms, establishes a complete adjacency relationship, and constitutes the edges in the graph.

Flowchart of SMILES preprocessing.

Finally, the feature vectors of all nodes are integrated into a feature matrix, and the connection relationships among atoms are organized into an edge index tensor. These two parts of the data are encapsulated into a standardized graph data structure, which fully preserves the topological connections and atomic-level properties of the molecule, providing a standardized and information-lossless input for graph neural networks.

The choice of molecular representation fundamentally affects the model’s ability to capture structure–property relationships. Molecular fingerprints, such as the extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP), encode the presence of predefined substructures as fixed-length binary vectors and are widely used in traditional QSAR modeling. However, they suffer from three limitations for surfactant IFT prediction: (i) hashing collisions may map distinct chemical environments to the same bit, losing fine-grained information; (ii) they are inherently local, with a predefined radius that cannot capture long-range dependencies between the hydrophilic head and hydrophobic taila critical determinant of amphiphilicity and interfacial orientation; and (iii) fingerprints are generated by fixed rules and cannot be learned or adapted to the target property. In contrast, graph representations preserve the complete molecular topology and enable iterative message passing, allowing the model to aggregate information from distant atoms and learn task-relevant features end to end. Therefore, this study adopts graph structure transformation as the primary molecular encoding strategy for all graph neural network models.

IFT Preprocessing

Since the original IFT data usually have the characteristics of spanning multiple orders of magnitude, being right-skewed in distribution, and having extreme values, directly using the raw values for training would lead to insufficient fitting in the small value region and overfitting in the large value region for the model. To address this issue, we designed a systematic two-stage preprocessing process.

First, apply the log1p transformation to the original values to compress the dynamic range. 1 ylog1p=log(1+yoriginal) where y log1p is the transformed value and y original is the original value.

Next, z-score standardization was applied. 2 ynormalized=ylog1p−μlog1pσlog1p where μlog1p is the mean after the log1p transformation and σlog1p is the standard deviation after the log1p transformation. It should be particularly emphasized that this transformation is only used to match the loss function during training. When evaluating the model and generating the final output, the predicted values will be restored to the original physical scale through a strict inverse transformation (inverse standardization → inverse log1p). All reported error metrics are calculated on the original scale, ensuring the physical intuitiveness and comparability of the evaluation results.

Model Description

This paper proposes a gated message passing neural network with an attention mechanism (Gated-MPNN-AT) model for IFT prediction. The overall structure of the model network is listed in Figure . First, the model converts the molecular SMILES string into graph-structured data, encoding atoms as node features and chemical bonds as edge connections. Meanwhile, it transforms the experimental environment parameters into structured vectors through multilevel feature engineering. Subsequently, the molecular graph data are input into the Gated-MPNN module, and the topological features of the molecular graph are extracted through multiple layers of gated message passing layers. The environmental features were encoded by a fully connected network. Then, the node features are aggregated into a graph-level representation through the attention pooling mechanism and fused with an environmental feature vector. The fused features undergo nonlinear transformation through a multilayer perceptron and ultimately output the predicted value of IFT. This model consists of four core modules: the graph feature extraction module, the environmental feature encoding module, the attention feature fusion module, and the IFT regression module. During the training process, the model uses a robust loss function based on the standardized IFT values for optimization to enhance the prediction stability and accuracy of the model across a wide range of IFT values.

Graph Feature Extraction Module

In the field of molecular graph feature extraction, the MPNN provides a general and powerful framework. This framework abstracts the forward propagation process of graph neural networks into two stages: message-passing and node update. It learns node representations by iteratively aggregating neighbor information and has become the standard paradigm for processing relational data, such as molecular graphs. On this basis, the Gated-MPNN introduces mechanisms such as the Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) to dynamically control the information flow, aiming to enhance the model’s ability to model deep dependencies and alleviate the oversmoothing problem.

The graph feature extraction module is shown in Figure . In our graph feature extraction module, the input of the module is the molecular graph G = {V, E, A}, where each atomic node v i ∈V has an initial feature vector X i , and each edge e ij ∈ E represents the chemical bond between atoms. The processing flow contains three core steps as the starting point of message passing. First, a projection network is used to map the original atomic features into a unified high-dimensional feature. 3 hi(0)=ReLU(WpXi+bp) where X i is the original features of the i-th atom, W p is the projection weight matrix; b p is the bias vector, h i (0) is the initialized feature vector, and ReLU is the rectified linear unit, which is a neural network activation function.

where m j→i is the message vector sent from node j to node i; m i is the aggregated message vector of node i; h i (l–1) and h j (l–1) are the feature vectors of the i-th and j-th atoms at layer l–1, respectively; MLP is the Multilayer Perceptron; and N(i) is the set of neighbor nodes of node i.

The update of messages uses a dual-gating update mechanism. Nodes are updated by combining their own states at the previous time step h i (l–1)and the aggregated information m i (l). We have designed a dual-gating mechanism: first, a GRU is used for preliminary fusion, and then an adaptive gating network is employed to finely adjust the retention ratio of the old and new information. 6 hi(l)=Gate(l)·GRU(hi(l−1),mi(l))+(1−Gate(l))·hi(l−1) where GRU­() is the Gated Recurrent Unit and Gate(l) is the adaptive gating scalar of the i-th layer.

This design enables the model to dynamically control the information flow and stably learn the deep dependencies.

After message passing, the final node features h i (L) containing rich local chemical environments are obtained. To perform property prediction, the node features need to be aggregated into a graph-level vector representing the entire molecule. Instead of using simple averaging or summation, attention pooling is adopted, allowing the model to automatically learn the weights α i of the contributions of different atoms to IFT. 7 hgraph=∑i=1Nαihi(L),αi=exp(aTtanh(Whi(L)))∑j⁡exp(aTtanh(Whj(L))) where L is the number of layers in message passing; tanh denotes the hyperbolic tangent activation function; α i represents the attention weight of the i-th atom; and h graph is the attention weight of the i-th atom.

This design enables the model to focus on the key molecular structures that have a greater impact on the IFT.

Environmental Feature Encoding Module

In the environmental feature extraction module, the input of the module is the environmental feature vector standardized through feature engineering. The core of this module is a two-layer fully connected neural network, which maps the preprocessed environmental feature vector to a unified hidden space through nonlinear transformation. 8 eencoded=W2·LeakyReLU(W1e+b1)+b2 where W 1 and W 2 are the weight parameters of the first and second layers; b 1 and b 2 are the bias parameters of the first and second layers; and LeakyReLU is an improved ReLU activation function.

After passing through a concise and efficient two-layer MLP structure, normalization and dropout layers are applied to maintain the stability of the training. The encoded environmental features e encoded in the output will be fused with the molecular graph features in the subsequent processes and jointly participate in the final prediction.

Feature Fusion Module and Prediction Module

The network structure of the feature fusion module is shown in Figure . In this module, there are two key inputs: the graph-level feature vector h graph from the graph feature extraction module and the environmental feature vector e encoded from the environmental feature encoding module. To achieve an in-depth interaction between the two types of molecular structure features and environmental condition features, this module adopts the cross-attention fusion mechanism. This mechanism enables the two types of features to use each other as context for information query and alignment through a bidirectional multihead attention layer. The calculation process is as follows. 9 hattended=Attention(hgraph,eencoded,eencoded) 10 eattended=Attention(eencoded,hgraph,hgraph) where Attention­(Q, K, V) is the standard attention function. The first attention layer takes the molecular features as the query and the environmental features as the key and value; the second attention layer takes the environmental features as the query and the molecular features as the key and value. The outputs of the two attention layers are merged, pooled, and fused through gating to generate the final fused feature, f fused. This design enables the model to adaptively determine the influence weights of the molecular structure and environmental conditions on interfacial tension based on specific input samples.

After passing through three fully connected layers, the dimension is gradually reduced from high to low, and the final prediction result is the output.

Loss Function

In view of the characteristics of IFT data, such as wide span, skewed distribution, and the presence of extreme values, directly using standard loss functions such as mean squared error (MSE) or mean absolute error (MAE) may lead to unstable training or prediction biases. In this paper, a piecewise robust loss function based on the standardized space is adopted to ensure that the model can achieve a stable and accurate prediction performance across the entire range of the IFT values.

Considering that the original IFT values follow an approximately standard normal distribution after log1p + z-score normalization, this loss function operates directly in the normalized space. Its core form combines the advantages of the Huber loss and the MSE. 14 LHuber={12(ŷ−y)2,|ŷ−y|≤δδ·|ŷ−y|−12δ,otherwise 15 LMSE=(ŷ−y)2 16 Lbase=α·LHuber(ŷ,y)+(1−α)·LMSE(ŷ,y) where ŷ and y are the model’s predicted values and the true target values respectively; and α and δ are the hyperparameters.

The traditional MSE loss overemphasizes large value errors on a wide range of data, while the MAE loss has sparse gradients in the extremely small value region. The module proposed in this paper balances the advantages of these two losses in the standardized space through the weighted ratio of α, ensuring that the model can make robust and accurate predictions for both small and large values.

Hyperparameter Optimization

During the development of the IFT prediction model, to improve the model performance and efficiently determine the optimal hyperparameter combination, the Bayesian optimization method, a commonly used hyperparameter tuning method, was introduced. This method is a global optimization strategy based on a sequential model. Its core lies in guiding the search direction in the parameter space by constructing a probabilistic surrogate model of the objective function so as to obtain excellent hyperparameter configurations with fewer evaluation times.

In this paper, Bayesian optimization is applied to the tuning of the key hyperparameters of graph neural networks. The hyperparameter settings of the model are listed in Table . The optimization process starts with a set of randomly initialized hyperparameter combinations. Each time the training and validation of the model are completed, the set of hyperparameters and their corresponding performance indicators are recorded and used to update the surrogate model. Subsequently, on the basis of the updated surrogate model, the acquisition function selects the next point with the greatest expected improvement in the parameter space for the next round of evaluation. After multiple rounds of iteration, the surrogate model’s fitting of the relationship between parameters and performance gradually becomes more accurate, thus, guiding the search to quickly converge to the high-performance region. Finally, through Bayesian optimization, a superior combination of hyperparameters was efficiently obtained.

Data Set Settings

This data set contains 2184 valid data points, each recording the complete experimental conditions and the measured equilibrium IFT value. Considering that IFT values span several orders of magnitude with a right-skewed distribution (as shown in Section ), a stratified splitting strategy was adopted to ensure that the training and test sets have consistent distributions across the full range. Specifically, the data were first sorted by the log1p-transformed IFT values (as defined in eq ) and divided into 10 strata according to deciles. Within each stratum, samples were randomly assigned to the training set (80%) and the test set (20%). The resulting training set contains 1747 samples, and the test set contains 437 samples.

The 8:2 training–test split follows the common practice in machine learning and cheminformatics, where this ratio balances sufficient training data with a reliably sized hold-out set for evaluation. , With 2184 data points, the 80% training allocation ensures adequate learning of complex molecular representations, while the 20% test set yields 437 samples for robust statistical estimation. Although a 7:3 split or cross-validation are valid alternatives, the 8:2 ratio was preferred here because the data set size is large enough to ensure training stability without compromising test-set reliability. Furthermore, the stratified splitting strategy guarantees distribution consistency between sets, minimizing sensitivity to the exact split proportion. In addition to random splitting, a leave-one-type-out protocol was implemented to evaluate generalization to structurally distinct surfactants. Surfactants were classified into anionic, cationic, nonionic, and zwitterionic categories via SMARTS pattern matching of headgroup moieties. Three experiments were conducted, each withholding one class as the test set, while the remaining data were randomly split into 85% training and 15% validation subsets. The held-out classes were anionic surfactants with 573 data points, cationic surfactants with 431 data points, and nonionic surfactants with 222 data points. Zwitterionic surfactants, along with Gemini, biosurfactant, and polymeric types, were excluded as hold-out classes due to insufficient sample sizes of fewer than 80 data points each but were retained in the training pool when applicable.

Comparison Model

To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the Gated-MPNN-AT model, this paper selects six baseline models for comparison. These models encompass traditional machine learning models, namely, RF and XGBoost, , as well as graph neural networks covering both classic and recent mainstream architectures, including Graph Convolutional Network (GCN), Graph Attention Network (GAT), the standard MPNN, and Directed Message-Passing Neural Network (D-MPNN).

D-MPNN, which serves as the core architecture of Chemprop, updates messages along directed bonds rather than atoms, thereby avoiding redundant information loops during propagation and achieving state-of-the-art results on various molecular property prediction benchmarks. Including this recent architecture ensures that the comparison reflects the current mainstream standards in molecular property prediction.

All baseline models use the same training-test set division and are trained under the same preprocessing. For machine learning models, the SMILES strings of surfactants were converted into 1024-bit extended-connectivity fingerprints (ECFP4, radius 2) using RDKit , as the model input. This fingerprint type and dimensionality were chosen because ECFP4 captures circular atom neighborhoods up to a diameter of four bonds, providing a comprehensive representation of the local chemical environments relevant to surfactant amphiphilicity while maintaining a fixed-length vector suitable for RF and XGBoost. For graph neural networks, the same atomic feature encoding scheme and environmental feature vectors were used. To ensure a fair comparison, all models used Bayesian optimization for hyperparameter tuning to achieve their respective optimal performance.

The selection of these baseline models was guided by three criteria: representativeness in their respective methodological categories, established performance in molecular property prediction tasks, and fair comparability to the proposed architecture. RF and XGBoost were chosen as representatives of traditional machine learning because they are ensemble methods that have demonstrated strong predictive capability in cheminformatics and petroleum engineering applications. , Compared with single decision trees, they mitigate overfitting through bagging and boosting mechanisms, respectively, and have been widely adopted as benchmarks in recent IFT prediction studies. , Other classical algorithms such as support vector regression or single-layer neural networks were not included because prior work , has shown that they underperform ensemble methods on similar physicochemical regression tasks. For graph neural networks, GCN, GAT, standard MPNN, and D-MPNN were selected because they span the mainstream design space of message-passing paradigms: spectral convolution, attention-based aggregation, edge-conditioned message passing, and directed bond-level message passing, with D-MPNN specifically serving as the core architecture of Chemprop and having established state-of-the-art results on multiple molecular property benchmarks by propagating messages along directed bonds to avoid redundant information loops. Collectively, these four architectures cover the foundational and current standard approaches in molecular representation learning, and excluding other variants such as GraphSAGE or Transformer-based models is justified by the fact that GAT, MPNN, and D-MPNN already subsume or outperform these approaches on small-to-medium molecular data sets, while more recent architectures such as Graphormer or equivariant GNNs might offer competitive performance, their substantially higher computational cost and data requirements exceed the scope of this benchmark, which focuses on practical applicability under standard laboratory and reservoir conditions.

Evaluation Indicators

The coefficient of determination (R 2) and the mean absolute error (MAE) are selected as the core indicators. They measure the model’s ability to capture data variance and the absolute scale of prediction errors, respectively, and serve as benchmarks for evaluating the global performance in regression analysis.

Given that the IFT experimental data often spans several orders of magnitude, a single absolute error indicator makes it difficult to fairly compare the performance of models on samples of different magnitudes. Therefore, the Symmetric Mean Absolute Percentage Error (sMAPE) and the Mean Relative Error (MRE) are additionally introduced as supplements. sMAPE assigns relatively balanced weights to the prediction errors of high and low values, while MRE directly reflects the average deviation ratio of the predicted values relative to the true values. 17 R2=1−∑i=1n(yi−yi^)2∑i=1n(yi−y̅)2 18 MAE=∑1n|yi−yi^| 19 sMAPE=1n∑1n2·|yi−yi^||yi|+|yi^|+ϵ×100% 20 MRE=1n∑i=1n|yi−yi^||yi|+ϵ where y i , yi^ , and y̅ are the true value, predicted value, and average value, respectively; n is the number of samples; and ϵ is a constant.

This performance hierarchy is not solely attributable to architectural sophistication; the underlying molecular representations also play a decisive role. The performance gap between traditional machine learning and GNN models is not due solely to architectural differences. Molecular fingerprints compress structural information into fixed-length vectors, which lose topological context and long-range dependencies that are critical for surfactant amphiphilic structures. In contrast, graph representations preserve complete molecular topology, enabling the model to learn the spatial configurations of functional groups and their coupling with environmental parameters. This suggests that graph-based representations offer inherent advantages over fingerprints for the IFT prediction tasks.

The attention weight distribution keeps oscillating before 150 iterations, resulting in unstable gradient update directions. Although the convergence cost increases the training time by 35%, the final R 2 of Gated-MPNN-AT reaches 0.9213 and the loss drops to 0.087, which proves that this convergence overhead has exchanged for a significant improvement in prediction accuracy.

Generalization Assessment via Leave-One-Type-Out Validation

Table summarizes the model performance under both random-split in-distribution and leave-one-type-out class-extrapolation validation scenarios. The most favorable extrapolation performance is observed when anionic surfactants are held out, yielding a test R 2 of 0.65 and the lowest test MRE of 75.6%, presumably because the model can leverage abundant cationic and nonionic training data to infer anionic behavior through shared alkyl tail chemistry. By contrast, nonionic surfactants are the most challenging to extrapolate, producing a test R 2 value of 0.58 and the highest test MRE of 85.4%, attributable to their diverse headgroup architectures and strong temperature-dependent hydration effects that differ fundamentally from those of ionic surfactants.

Conclusions

In this paper, the Gated-MPNN-AT model was developed to predict the IFT of surfactantoil–water systems. This model uses the gated message passing neural network to extract the structural features of molecular graphs and adaptively fuses environmental parameters with the attention mechanism. By designing a robust loss function, it solves the problem of low prediction accuracy in IFT prediction, which is caused by the complex molecular structure effect, strong nonlinear coupling of multiscale environments, and the data distribution spanning multiple orders of magnitude. The main conclusions are as follows.(1)The Gated-MPNN-AT model achieves high prediction accuracy and strong generalization ability. These demonstrate a more robust prediction capability for the IFT value range spanning multiple orders of magnitude.(3)The gated mechanism enhances the message screening ability of the MPNN and avoids information redundancy and gradient vanishing in the deep networks. Limitations and Future Perspectives

Limitations

Although the integration of the gated mechanism and attention mechanism improves the accuracy, it increases the number of model parameters by ∼40% and extends the training time by 35%. The model needs 200 epochs to converge, which requires relatively high computational resources. As a purely data-driven predictor, the model does not explicitly embed thermodynamic constraints such as CMC phase boundaries or temperature-induced transitions, meaning that its predictions represent effective equilibrium IFT values within the observed data range rather than mechanistic extrapolations beyond it.

The current data set is derived from the integration of articles. Although it has undergone strict standardization processing, there are problems with heterogeneous data such as differences in measurement methods, inconsistent experimental conditions, difficulty in quantifying measurement errors, and the data distribution may be unbalanced. Additionally, the environmental representation is simplified, with salinity treated as NaCl concentration alone and the system assumed to be single-component, which constrains the model to moderate conditions and surfactant types covered in the training data.

Future Perspectives

In the future, our work will focus on building a higher-quality experimental database and exploring the fusion strategy of lightweight architectures and physical information. This approach aims to reduce the computational cost while enhancing the generalization ability and interpretability of the model under harsh reservoir conditions.
