Maternal mortality in Assam’s informal economy: the household burden of precarious work
Authors: Kanchan Devi, Vandana Upadhyay
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaternal mortality remains a pressing public health concern globally, with low- and middle-income countries bearing a disproportionate burden. Despite notable progress, India continues to record high maternal mortality rates (MMR), particularly in socio-economically marginalized regions such as Assam. This study investigates maternal mortality among informal worker households in Assam using household-level data and estimates the Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR) based on WHO’s standard methodology. The findings reveal an estimated MMR of 675.68 per 100,000 live births substantially higher than both the national average (97) and the state average (195). This discrepancy reveals the compounded vulnerabilities faced by women in informal employment, who lack access to adequate maternal healthcare, nutrition, and social protection. The study contextualizes India’s maternal health performance relative to countries with comparable levels of economic and social development, where India’s ranking remains significantly low. The results highlight the need for targeted interventions to strengthen maternal healthcare access and delivery, particularly for women in precarious informal employment. The study also calls for improved data collection and policy attention toward this under-researched population to effectively reduce preventable maternal deaths.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02157-0.Keywords: Maternal Mortality, Assam, Informal Workers, Factors, Health

Background

Maternal mortality continues to be a critical public health concern in India despite decades of policy focus and economic progress [10]. The issue is compounded by sharp disparities in the utilization of maternal healthcare services both across and within states [2]. Although maternal health outcomes have improved globally, low- and middle-income countries, including India, continue to lag behind [1]. Reducing maternal mortality thus remains a central goal for health systems worldwide, particularly in developing nations [7].

High maternal mortality rates in such contexts are not only a marker of weak health systems but also reflect persistent violations of women’s fundamental rights to life and healthcare [14]. As per UNICEF [14], India's maternal mortality ratio (MMR) in 2017 stood at 112, placing the country 120th among 186 nations. While the national MMR declined from 113 in 2016–2018 to 103 in 2017–2019 [21], significant interstate disparities remain. In Assam, the MMR fell from 215 to 205 during the same period, yet the state continues to rank among those with the highest MMR, alongside Rajasthan, Uttar Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Bihar, Odisha, and Chhattisgarh [16]. In contrast, southern Indian states have demonstrated stronger performance in reducing maternal mortality [16], driven in part by more equitable access to antenatal care, institutional deliveries, and postnatal services [8].

The low utilization of maternal healthcare services in Assam further illustrates the challenges. Only 45% of pregnant women in the state received antenatal care, compared to the national average of 55% (AHS, 2012–13). Moreover, the percentage of home deliveries is substantially higher in Assam (78%) than the national average (52.4%) [5]. Postnatal care rates remain equally concerning, with only 16% of mothers receiving care within 48 h of delivery, compared to the national average of 41.2% [12].

A critical but often overlooked dimension of India’s maternal mortality challenge lies in the vulnerability of women employed in the informal sector. Informal employment is pervasive across Indian states, and Assam has seen a relatively faster growth in informal jobs compared to the national trend 2.46% overall, with 3.87% among females (NSSO, 55th and 68th rounds). Women working in informal sectors agriculture, domestic labour, construction face structural barriers such as lack of job security, erratic incomes, and limited or no access to maternity benefits or employer-supported care [3, 9]. These constraints result in delayed antenatal check-ups, inadequate care during delivery, and poor postnatal support [. The physically demanding nature of informal work further exacerbates the risks of pregnancy-related complications, as many women are compelled to continue working throughout pregnancy [13].

Importantly, maternal deaths among informal workers often go underreported, obscuring the full extent of the crisis [17]. Comparative international evidence shows that countries with similar levels of income and social development such as Sri Lanka and Bangladesh, have achieved significantly better maternal health outcomes than India, primarily due to stronger community health infrastructure and more inclusive policies [10].

In this context, the present study seeks to examine the factors contributing to maternal mortality among informal sector workers in Assam, a state that typifies the intersection of health inequities and informal employment. The study also aims to situate India’s maternal mortality burden within broader global trends, particularly in relation to nations with similar economic and social development levels.

Methodology

Data sources

The study utilizes both primary and secondary data sources. Secondary data are obtained from publications by the World Health Organization, the Reserve Bank of India, the World Bank and other relevant government reports.

To collect primary data, a structured questionnaire was developed, targeting respondents who were informal workers, regardless of gender. The identification of informal workers was based on the definition provided by the International Labour Organization (ILO). Data were collected at both the individual and household levels.

In addition to households of informal workers, the study identified eligible respondents based on specific criteria. These criteria included mothers who had given birth during the reference period (2015–2020) and husbands whose spouses had passed away during pregnancy, childbirth, or within 42 days postpartum. Maternal mortality was defined as any maternal death occurring within a household during pregnancy, childbirth, or up to two months following delivery. For this purpose, only women of reproductive age were considered. Since direct information could not be obtained from deceased mothers, the study relied on knowledgeable household members, particularly the spouse, to provide relevant information.

Sampling procedure

Purposive sampling was employed to identify and select informal workers across three districts of Assam i.e. Dhubri, Sonitpur, and Dhemaji, representing Lower, Northern, and Upper Assam, respectively. Selection was based on a computed health index incorporating indicators such as infant mortality, under-five mortality, maternal mortality, life expectancy, and death rate. The index categorized districts into three performance tiers: better-performing, moderate-performing, and lower-performing. Accordingly, Dhemaji (better-performing, Upper Assam), Sonitpur (moderately performing, Northern & Middle Assam), and Dhubri (poorly performing, Lower Assam) were chosen.

Given the geographic distribution of high- and low-performing districts, a clustered sampling approach was adopted. To capture spatial variability, two development blocks per district, one nearest and one farthest from the district headquarters were selected. A total of 600 informal workers (500 rural, 100 urban) were surveyed using a multi-stage sampling technique. The sample included 246 workers from Dhubri, 232 from Sonitpur, and 122 from Dhemaji, with further stratification based on gender and area of residence.

The data collection process began with the question: "Has any live birth occurred in the household between 2015 and 2020?" Only individuals who responded affirmatively were considered as the ultimate sampling units. Of the total surveyed, 241 informal workers reported the occurrence of a live birth during the reference period. Consequently, the final sample comprised 241 informal workers or households of informal workers.

To ensure a comprehensive understanding of the subject while minimizing recall bias, the study adopted a five-year reference period. However, to mitigate potential distortions caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, the reference period excluded 2017/18 and other years that may have been significantly affected by the crisis.

Results

Despite notable global progress in reducing maternal mortality, India continues to face significant challenges, highlighting how adverse socio-economic conditions and regional disparities continue to shape maternal health outcomes. Numerous studies have shown that social determinants, including poverty, poor nutrition, inadequate health infrastructure, and limited access to quality maternal care, remain key drivers of elevated maternal mortality rates [3].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), HIV/AIDS-related complications remain among the leading causes of maternal fatalities globally, and maternal mortality is the second-largest cause of death worldwide among women of reproductive age [10]. In India, this challenge is compounded by the widespread prevalence of anaemia, particularly among women in vulnerable and underserved groups. Dasgupta et al. [4] report that anaemia is the leading cause of death among women of reproductive age in the country, and Kalaivani [19] notes that it contributes to over 40% of all maternal deaths annually. The situation is particularly alarming in Assam, where 69.7% of women of reproductive age are anaemic, the highest prevalence among Indian states.

While India has made national-level progress, reflected in the decline of its maternal mortality ratio (MMR) from 113 in 2016–18 to 103 in 2017–19 [21], significant interstate disparities persist. For example, Assam’s MMR decreased only marginally, from 215 in 2016 to 205 in 2017 [18], remaining substantially above the national average and pointing to persistent structural and healthcare access gaps in socio-economically disadvantaged regions [14].

Maternal deaths remain alarmingly high worldwide (Table 1). According to the Meh el al. [10], in 2020 one mother died every two minutes from preventable causes related to pregnancy and childbirth. These deaths are largely concentrated in low- and middle-income countries and are primarily driven by poor socio-economic conditions and persistent inequalities in access to maternal healthcare services. While developed countries have achieved substantial reductions in maternal mortality through strategic interventions and robust health systems, many low- and middle-income nations continue to struggle, showing only modest declines over time. Approximately 99% of all maternal deaths occur in developing countries, where structural barriers, including inadequate medical care, high prevalence of infectious diseases, elevated total fertility rates, and systemic healthcare disparities, sustain an unacceptably high burden of maternal mortality.

The disproportionately high MMR observed in this cohort may be attributed to a combination of adverse socio-economic conditions, limited access to quality maternal healthcare services, and the precarious working and living environments typical of informal sector households. However, it is important to note that the relatively small sample size may have inflated this estimate, warranted cautious interpretation while emphasising the need for larger-scale investigations.

Discussion

Maternal mortality in Assam has remained a persistent public health challenge over the decades and continues to be a significant contributor to the lower life expectancy observed among women in the state. Despite various policy interventions aimed at reducing maternal deaths, the Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR) in Assam remains among the highest in the country.

Our study estimated the MMR among households of informal workers in the study area to be 675.68 per 100,000 live births, which is alarmingly higher than both the state average of 195 and the national average of 97 per 100,000 live births. These findings indicate a disproportionate burden of maternal deaths among women belonging to informal worker households. This elevated MMR emphasises the layered vulnerabilities faced by this population group, rooted in socio-economic disadvantage, lack of healthcare access, and insufficient maternal care.

A comparison with other countries of similar economic and social development further emphasizes India’s relative underperformance in maternal health. For instance, countries such as Sri Lanka and Bangladesh, with comparable or even lower per capita incomes, report significantly lower MMRs. According to the Meh et al. [10], Sri Lanka’s MMR stands at around 29 per 100,000 live births, while Bangladesh reports 123 per 100,000 live births, both substantially lower than the national Indian average. These contrasts suggest that economic development alone does not determine maternal outcomes; rather, effective health systems, nutritional support, and targeted maternal health interventions play crucial roles.

Moreover, the data indicate that most maternal deaths recorded in our study area were attributed to pregnancy-related complications (over 85%), corroborating the broader literature that identifies such complications as a primary cause of maternal mortality. The risk is compounded in populations with constrained access to preventive and emergency maternal health services, often the case among informal sector workers.

While institutional deliveries are typically associated with lower maternal mortality due to the presence of skilled healthcare providers [20], our data show that 57.1% of maternal deaths occurred in hospitals, suggesting that many women reach healthcare facilities too late or in critical condition. This highlights the need for not only increasing access to healthcare but also strengthening the continuum of care from antenatal to postnatal periods.

Additionally, all reported maternal deaths occurred among women who did not receive food supplements during pregnancy, emphasizing the crucial role of maternal nutrition. This aligns with earlier studies, including Nguyen et al. [11], which emphasize the impact of nutritional deprivation on maternal outcomes.

Although our study is limited by a small sample size and a lack of integration of conceptual models of maternal mortality [15], it offers important insights. Future research should embed such empirical evidence within broader theoretical frameworks to comprehensively understand the systemic, structural, and social determinants of maternal mortality.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence of a disproportionately high burden of maternal mortality among informal worker households in Assam, with an estimated Maternal Mortality Ratio (MMR) of 675.68 per 100,000 live births, substantially higher than both the state and national averages. These findings suggest that socio-economic vulnerability and systemic gaps in healthcare delivery may be contributing to poorer maternal health outcomes in this population.

Several factors appear to be associated with the elevated risk, including pregnancy-related complications, delays in reaching or receiving care, maternal age, nutritional deficits during pregnancy, and postpartum illness. The observation that maternal deaths occurred despite hospital deliveries points to possible concerns about the quality, timeliness, or adequacy of maternal care services rather than simply physical access to facilities. While India has made significant progress in reducing maternal mortality overall, regional and population-level disparities persist. Comparisons with countries such as Sri Lanka and Bangladesh, which have achieved lower maternal mortality despite similar income levels, emphasis the potential value of improving the effectiveness and equity of healthcare systems.

This study emphasizes the urgent need for targeted public health strategies that not only strengthen maternal healthcare infrastructure but also address the nutritional, informational, and systemic needs of informal worker households. Interventions must focus on early antenatal registration, continuity of care, emergency obstetric support, and nutritional supplementation. Community-based awareness programs can play a vital role in promoting early health-seeking behaviour and reducing delays in treatment.

This study highlights the need for targeted public health measures that could strengthen maternal healthcare infrastructure and address the nutritional, informational, and systemic needs of informal worker households. Strategies such as early antenatal registration, continuity of care, emergency obstetric support, nutritional supplementation, and community-based awareness programs may help reduce maternal health risks.

However, these findings should be interpreted with caution. The study is based on a relatively small sample size and lacks a formal conceptual framework, which limits the generalizability of results and restricts the ability to establish causal relationships. Further research using larger, representative samples and theoretically grounded approaches is necessary to validate these findings and to explore the structural and institutional determinants of maternal mortality among informal workers.
