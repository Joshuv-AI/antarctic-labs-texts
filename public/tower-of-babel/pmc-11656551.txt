Efficacy and safety of domperidone and metoclopramide on human milk production in postpartum mothers: a bayesian network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Si Si, Ge Zhao, Guang Song, Jing Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBreastfeeding is the most advantageous nutrition for infants because of its many health benefits. However, lactation insufficiency is a prevalent issue among women, particularly those who give birth prematurely. Galactagogues, such as domperidone and metoclopramide, have been reported and may be beneficial for lactation insufficiency. However, the optimal pharmacological galactagogue remains unclear.MethodsThe PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, ClinicalTrials.gov, and Cochrane Library databases were searched from their inception to April 23, 2023. The primary aim of this research was to assess the efficacy and safety of domperidone and metoclopramide using Bayesian network meta-analysis. The efficacy outcome was the increased breast milk volume (in mL/day). The safety outcome was the frequency of maternal drug-related adverse events during the study period.ResultsSeventeen randomized controlled trials were included in the final analyses. Preterm mothers who took domperidone had a greater increase in breast milk volume than those who took metoclopramide or placebo (domperidone vs. metoclopramide: MD = 82.84, 95% CI: 37.04–118.95; domperidone vs. placebo: MD = 88.30, 95% CI: 59.48–118.62). However, in the term mothers, domperidone did not show the above benefit compared with metoclopramide or placebo (domperidone vs. metoclopramide: MD = 93.67, 95% CI: -186.11–375.59; domperidone vs. placebo: MD = 126.25, 95% CI: -49.91–304.55). Both in preterm and term mothers, metoclopramide was no better than the placebo. There was no difference in the frequency of maternal drug-related adverse outcomes among domperidone, metoclopramide, and placebo.ConclusionDomperidone increased the daily breast milk volume in mothers of preterm infants, without serious adverse events. However, this conclusion is limited due to the small sample sizes in the studies analyzed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-024-07027-4.

Introduction

Breastfeeding is the most advantageous nutrition for the newborn infant, especially for those preterm [1]. The advantages of breastfeeding have been well documented, including the psychological benefits of maternal–infant bonding, increases immunity and protection against certain infections, and improves neurodevelopmental outcomes [2]. Breastfeeding also could reduce the maternal risk of breast and ovarian cancers [3], diabetes [4], and coronary heart diseases [5].

Referring to the World Health Organization, the exclusive breastfeeding is advised for infants until they reach the age of six months [6]. However, lactation insufficiency is a prevalent issue among women, particularly those who give birth prematurely [7]. Several factors will lead to lactation insufficiency, such as irregular hormone levels, whether they had a prior breastfeeding history, maternal pain (including painful nipples) [8]. According to the latest research data, almost 50% of mothers in the United States and two-thirds of women worldwide claimed “breastmilk insufficiency” as a primary reason for discontinuing breastfeeding before six months [9].

Although infant formula can alleviate insufficient breast milk supply to some extent, formula may also bring some risks, including eczema, upset stomach, runny nose, and necrotizing enterocolitis in premature babies [10]. Various strategies to address concerns about insufficient milk supply include breastfeeding education, appropriate lactation counseling, other nonpharmacological interventions (such as skin contact, initiating the process of milk expression shortly after childbirth, frequent pumping, and relaxation techniques), and galactagogues, which may be beneficial. The definition of galactagogue is a substance that induces, maintains, or increases milk production in humans and other animals [11]. Galactagogues can be synthetic or plant-derived. Various herb-derived galactagogues have been reported in the literature [11]. Nonetheless, there is a scarcity of scientific data available to demonstrate their efficacy. Therefore, pharmacological galactagogues are usually recommended after non-pharmacological strategies have failed.

Several pharmacological galactagogues have been studied in postpartum mothers. Domperidone and metoclopramide have been extensively investigated as pharmacological galactagogues [12]. Others, such as sulpiride, only had limited evidence of efficacy as pharmacological galactagogues [12]. Meanwhile, the results were inconsistent among the studies which evaluated the efficacy and safety of domperidone and metoclopramide as galactagogues in postpartum mothers. The purpose of this study was to provide an extensive assessment of these two mainstream galactagogues using network meta-analysis (NMA).

Methods

Registration

A pre-registered protocol was demonstrated on the PROSPERO website (Registered ID: CRD42023420746). This review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses statement (Supplemental file) [13].

Search strategy

PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, ClinicalTrials.gov, and Cochrane Library databases were searched (from database inception to April 23, 2023) to identify the available literature by two independent investigators (S.S. and G.Z.). The keywords contained: “Domperidone” or “Metoclopramide”; “Galactogogues”, “Breast Feeding”, “Lactation”, “Milk, Human”, “Lactation Disorders” or other relevant terms (Supplemental Table S1). Additionally, we examined the citations of papers to find other relevant literature.

Eligibility criteria

Original studies were eligible if the following criteria were met: (I) randomized controlled trials (RCTs); (II) full text available in English; and (III) comparing the efficacy and safety of domperidone or metoclopramide with placebo on human milk production in postpartum mothers.

Original studies were ineligible for the following reasons: (I) reviews, observational studies, case-control studies, or case reports; (II) without data for calculating the changes in human milk production or the risk of maternal adverse events in domperidone, metoclopramide, or placebo groups; (III) laboratory animal studies. In the case of multiple papers from the same research, the publication containing the largest number of cases and the most pertinent information was chosen.

Outcomes

The efficacy outcome was the increased breast milk volume (in mL/day), which was reported as the change from baseline (mean difference, MD). The safety outcome was the frequency of maternal drug-related adverse events during the study period, including headache, dry mouth, abdominal cramping, diarrhea, dizziness, and depression.

Selection process, data extraction and quality assessment

Individual studies from our retrieval were first screened based on titles and abstracts. If a judgment could not be made based on titles and abstracts, we proceeded to read the full text. Both the screening process and data extraction were performed independently by two investigators (S.S. and G.Z.). Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with the senior reviewers (J.L.).

For each eligible study, two independent investigators (S.S. and G.S.) extracted data including: the first author, published year, country, eligibility criteria, number of participants in each group, gestational age, details of interventions (startup time, dose, and duration), and outcomes.

The Cochrane Collaboration’s tool was used to evaluate each trial before data statistics [14]. Two authors (S.S. and G.S.) independently assessed the quality of the studies included. Any disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion or consultation with the senior reviewer (J.L.). The quality evaluation charts were generated using the “robvis” package of the R software.

Statistical analysis

The mean difference (MD) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were the changes of volume on human milk production in postpartum mothers among different intervention groups. Odds ratios (ORs) were used to report the risk of maternal and neonatal adverse events. Random-effects and consistency models were used. We assessed inconsistencies using the node-splitting method, and inconsistencies were reported when Bayesian P values < 0.05. All outcomes were ranked according to the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) (worst = 0%; best = 100%). Publication bias was evaluated by using a funnel plot. Stata (version 16.0; StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA) and R software (version 4.0.2; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria) with the “gemtc” package were used in this NMA.

Results

Baseline of involved studies

410 potentially relevant publications were retrieved from five databases. After screening and reading the full-text articles, 17 RCTs were included in our final analyses (Fig. 1) [15–31].

Efficacy of galactagogues

16 RCTs reported the increased breast milk volume (in mL/day) [15, 17–31]. Before subgroup analysis, we found that mothers who took domperidone had a greater increase in breast milk volume than those who took metoclopramide or placebo (domperidone vs. metoclopramide: MD = 78.13, 95% CI: 12.28–145.91; domperidone vs. placebo: MD = 103.55, 95% CI: 59.18– 148.36; Fig. 3 and Supplemental Table S3). There was no difference between metoclopramide and placebo (MD = 25.38, 95% CI: -34.10–82.92; Fig. 3).

Subgroup analysis of efficacy

Stratified by gestational week (Preterm/term subgroup analysis)

12 RCTs reported the efficacy outcome in mothers with preterm infants [18–23, 25–27, 29–31]. Preterm mothers who took domperidone had a greater increase in breast milk volume than those who took metoclopramide or placebo (domperidone vs. metoclopramide: MD = 82.84, 95% CI: 37.04–118.95; domperidone vs. placebo: MD = 88.30, 95% CI: 59.48–118.62; Fig. 4A and Supplemental Table S4). There was no difference between metoclopramide and placebo (MD = 3.95, 95% CI: -22.52–46.59; Fig. 4A).

Subgroup analysis of efficacy. (A) Stratified by gestational week (preterm or term); (B) Stratified by different daily doses; (C) Stratified by different cumulative doses during trials

5 RCTs reported the efficacy outcome in mothers with term infants [15, 17, 24, 28, 30]. There was no difference among domperidone, metoclopramide, and placebo (Fig. 4A and Supplemental Table S5).

Stratified by different daily doses

According to the different daily doses, all trials received domperidone have been divided into 2 groups: “domperidone 30 mg/day” (9 trials [17–19, 21, 23, 25–28]) and “domperidone > 30 mg/day” (2 trials [24, 31]). All doses of metoclopramide taken were 30 mg per day [15, 18, 20, 22, 23, 29, 30].

Therefore, the results revealed both “domperidone 30 mg/day” and “domperidone > 30 mg/day” were more effective than placebo in increasing breast milk volume (“domperidone 30 mg/day” vs. placebo: MD = 100.52, 95% CI: 50.49–150.31; “domperidone > 30 mg/day” vs. placebo: MD = 120.35, 95% CI: 3.01–240.43; Fig. 4B). Meanwhile, “domperidone 30 mg/day” was more efficacious than “metoclopramide 30 mg/day” for increasing breast milk volume (MD = 75.81, 95% CI: 6.13–147.15; Supplemental Table S6).

There was no difference between “metoclopramide 30 mg/day” and placebo (MD = 24.59, 95% CI: -36.93–84.64; Fig. 4B).

Stratified by different cumulative doses during trials

We used 210 mg (30 mg/day × 7 days) as a boundary for the cumulative dose and divided domperidone and metoclopramide into four groups: “domperidone > 210 mg” (5 trials [17, 21, 23, 26, 31]), “domperidone 210 mg” (6 trials [18, 19, 24, 25, 27, 28]), “metoclopramide > 210 mg” (4 trials [15, 20, 22, 23]), and “metoclopramide 210 mg” (3 trials [18, 29, 30]).

After analysis, we found that both “domperidone > 210 mg” and “domperidone 210 mg” were more effective than placebo in increasing breast milk volume (“domperidone > 210 mg” vs. placebo: MD = 150.80, 95% CI: 80.55–216.73; “domperidone 210 mg” vs. placebo: MD = 74.42, 95% CI: 21.47–129.06; Fig. 4C). Meanwhile, there was no difference between “domperidone > 210 mg” and “domperidone 210 mg” (MD = 76.54, 95% CI: -13.39–160.33; Supplemental Table S7).

There was no difference among “metoclopramide > 210 mg”, “metoclopramide 210 mg”, and placebo (Fig. 4C and Supplemental Table S7).

Safety of galactagogues

There was no difference in the frequency of maternal drug-related adverse outcomes among domperidone, metoclopramide, and placebo (Fig. 5 and Supplemental Table S8). Four trials investigated the neonatal adverse effects: three trials did not reveal any drug-related adverse events [17, 21, 30], and one trial found there was no difference of the frequency of neonatal adverse events between domperidone and placebo [26].

Discussion

This study reviewed the efficacy and safety of domperidone and metoclopramide as galactagogues in postpartum mothers through network analysis. Our paper involved 17 trials and nearly 800 women, which significantly surpassing previous meta-analyses [2, 32–34]. We also compared the efficacy and safety between domperidone and metoclopramide as galactagogues, which was analyzed and published in a meta-analysis for the first time. In this first comprehensive NMA, we found domperidone was more efficacious than metoclopramide and placebo in improving breast milk insufficiency. This benefit was only revealed in mothers with preterm infants. Orally domperidone 30 mg/day for seven days (cumulative doses = 210 mg) had maximal efficacy based on available data for improving breast milk insufficiency. Meantime, domperidone did not increase the maternal drug-related adverse outcomes, compared with metoclopramide and placebo.

Domperidone

Domperidone and metoclopramide are the most commonly used galactagogues. Both domperidone and metoclopramide are dopamine antagonists which could block the dopamine 2 receptors in lactotrophic cells of the anterior pituitary [35, 36]. Our analysis revealed an average increase in volume of 103.55 mL/day in the domperidone group compared to the placebo group for mothers with breast milk insufficiency. This finding was consistent with recent systematic reviews [2, 32, 33]. Domperidone is thought to work by blocking the inhibitory effects of dopamine-mediated prolactin secretion, thereby increasing milk production. Several pharmacologic studies have shown that domperidone can significantly elevate prolactin levels [17, 19, 37]. In the involved studies, 81.8% of studies (9/11) used domperidone 30 mg/day (10 mg orally, administered 3 times daily), one study used 40 mg/day (10 mg orally, administered 4 times daily) [24] and one study used 60 mg/day (20 mg orally, administered 3 times daily) [31]. The duration of domperidone varied between four and twenty-eight days. Currently, there is no definitive dosage recommendation for domperidone to improve breastmilk insufficiency. None of studies investigated the sustained efficacy of domperidone beyond a duration of six weeks [38]. Therefore, the recommended of domperidone in recent research is 10 mg orally three times daily for one to two weeks, without convincing evidence to substantiate a higher dosage [27, 39].

The ability of domperidone to traverse the blood-brain barrier is limited, and its excretion into breast milk is modest. However, some transient adverse effects of domperidone (including abdominal cramps, dry mouth, headache, diarrhea, and dizziness) have been reported [12, 39]. Our analysis demonstrated there were no differences in the frequency of maternal drug-related adverse events after using domperidone compared with the placebo. In 2004, the US Food and Drug Administration issued a warning that breastfeeding women not use domperidone because of an increased risk of cardiac arrhythmia and sudden death. However, a recent study of domperidone for postpartum lactation found no cases of ventricular arrhythmias in women with no prior history [40]. All cases of ventricular arrhythmia have been observed among women with a previous history [12]. Therefore, in postpartum women with a history of arrhythmia, the use of domperidone for lactation should be avoided [12].

Metoclopramide

Metoclopramide is another widely used galactagogue. Early clinical trials in the pharmacological treatment for improving breastmilk insufficiency often focused on metoclopramide. Our analysis revealed there was no difference in the increased breast milk volume between metoclopramide and placebo for mothers with breast milk insufficiency. This finding was consistent with two recent systematic reviews [33, 34]. The difference in lactation efficacy between domperidone and metoclopramide, which are both dopamine antagonists, may be due to the following two reasons: First, metoclopramide is more lipid soluble, has lower molecular weight, higher protein binding than domperidone [41]. Second, the medication duration of metoclopramide is too short. A recent meta-analysis found that two-week metoclopramide therapy didn’t elevate prolactin levels, but three-week therapy did [42]. However, in the studies involved in our analysis, the duration of metoclopramide was 9.3 ± 5.1 days (only one study > two weeks [16]).

One reason for the limited duration of metoclopramide administration is concern about its potential side effects. Unlike domperidone, metoclopramide does readily cross the blood-brain barrier, and has the potential to cause extrapyramidal side effects and other serious maternal adverse events, including depression [23]. The prolonged use of metoclopramide is associated with an elevated frequency of tardive dyskinesia [43]. Therefore, the experts’ recommended dosage (less than five days, less than 10 mg three times daily) is not sufficient to improve breast milk insufficiency.

Limitation

Several limitations are still in our study. First, due to the small sample sizes in the studies analyzed, our conclusions are limited. Second, the included studies were heterogeneous in the details of interventions, including the treatment initiation, the treatment durations, and the dosage of medicine. Third, not all non-pharmacological breastfeeding strategies in the included studies were well described. Fourth, eligibility criteria varied widely across studies (details in Supplemental Table S2).

Future directions

Domperidone and metoclopramide were evaluated in this study. However, some other pharmacological galactagogues have been reported, such as sulpiride, growth hormone, recombinant human prolactin, thyrotropin releasing hormone, oxytocin, and metformin [12]. Due to the scarcity of studies on these drugs, our NMA did not include them. More RCTs on these drugs for postpartum women needed to be carried out in the future.

Although some literature has reported the use of domperidone for lactation in couples who are adopting or same-sex couples [44], and transgender women [45–47], more study is needed.

Conclusion

Domperidone increased the daily breast milk volume in mothers of preterm infants, without serious adverse events. However, this conclusion is limited due to the small sample sizes in the studies analyzed. The use of domperidone for breast milk stimulation must be prescribed by a physician, and potential side effects should be considered. Women should receive appropriate guidance on ways to enhance breast milk production in hospital and in the 30 days following hospital discharge, before considering to medication to increase milk production.
