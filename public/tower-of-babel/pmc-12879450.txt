Grade 4 thrombocytopenia with PD-1 inhibitors following progression of PD-L1 inhibitor therapy in small cell lung cancer: a case report and literature review
Authors: An Wang, Yi Dong, Dongxiao Wu, Yibing Bai, Xuhui Yang, Haitao Tao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) are now a standard treatment for small cell lung cancer (SCLC). In certain cases, cross-line ICI therapy may still provide clinical benefit after disease progression; however, reports detailing the associated adverse events—particularly hematologic toxicities—remain scarce. We present a case of grade 4 immune thrombocytopenia that occurred in a patient with extensive-stage SCLC shortly after switching from durvalumab (anti-PD-L1) to camrelizumab (anti-PD-1) upon progression. The diagnosis was confirmed by strongly positive anti-platelet antibodies (4+) after excluding other causes. Combination therapy with high-dose methylprednisolone and intravenous immunoglobulin, supplemented with recombinant human thrombopoietin (rhTPO) in accordance with Chinese clinical guidelines, led to rapid platelet recovery within one week. No recurrence occurred during subsequent non-ICI chemotherapy, strongly supporting a probable association with camrelizumab. To our knowledge, this represents the first documented case of severe thrombocytopenia occurring in the context of and likely triggered by camrelizumab following durvalumab-to-camrelizumab sequence in SCLC, which highlights the potential for severe irAEs upon switching ICI classes.Keywords: Small cell lung cancer, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Thrombocytopenia, Durvalumab, Camrelizumab, Case report

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Small cell lung cancer (SCLC), known for its aggressive behavior and poor prognosis, has a five-year survival rate of less than 7% [2]. For extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC), immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) combined with chemotherapy are the standard first-line treatment [3, 4]. However, treatment strategies after first-line progression remain challenging. Some studies have explored the cross-line application of ICIs, suggesting potential benefits [5–7], and this strategy is sometimes employed in clinical practice. Nonetheless, safety data, particularly concerning the sequential application of different ICIs, are scarce.

The expanded use of ICIs has uncovered a spectrum of immune-related adverse events (irAEs). While dermatological, gastrointestinal, and endocrine toxicities predominate [8], hematological irAEs, especially thrombocytopenia, are exceedingly rare [9, 10]. In the CASPIAN trial, only 5% of patients experienced grade ≥ 3 irAEs, with thrombocytopenia being particularly uncommon [4]. The pathophysiological consequences and risk stratification of sequential PD-L1 to PD-1 inhibitor transitions represent uncharted territory, an emerging concern as patients may receive multiple lines of immunotherapy.

Herein, we report a case of grade 4 thrombocytopenia that occurred shortly after the administration of camrelizumab (anti-PD-1) following durvalumab (anti-PD-L1) failure in ES-SCLC. This case underscores the potentially unpredictable and severe irAEs that may arise when switching ICI classes, emphasizing the need for heightened vigilance and cautious management.

Case presentation

A 70-year-old patient presented in September 2020 after the incidental discovery of a space-occupying lesion in the left lower lobe of the lung on imaging. Contrast-enhanced chest computed tomography (CT) revealed a 5-cm soft tissue mass in the left lower lobe and multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the mediastinum and bilateral hilum. An emission CT (ECT) bone scan showed increased metabolism in the left third anterior rib and the eighth rib, raising suspicion of metastasis, although corresponding CT findings were inconclusive. The patient declined a positron emission tomography (PET) scan. Baseline brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed no evidence of intracranial metastases. Bronchoscopy with biopsy confirmed the diagnosis of SCLC. The patient was thus diagnosed with SCLC (cT2N2Mx, with possible bone metastasis).

Treatment course and thrombocytopenia onset

The initial treatment plan, considering a potential limited-stage diagnosis, was concurrent chemoradiotherapy. The patient received two cycles of etoposide and cisplatin chemotherapy on October 21 and November 13, 2020. During this time, the patient developed a pulmonary embolism (PE), and oral rivaroxaban was initiated for anticoagulation. Rivaroxaban was discontinued on November 25, 2020, due to an episode of hemoptysis. The chemotherapy schedule was also interrupted as a result of the PE and hemoptysis. The patient subsequently underwent thoracic radiotherapy (60 Gy in 30 fractions) targeting the primary lung lesion and the regional lymph nodes. However, the patient discontinued radiotherapy due to cough and fatigue and declined to restart it.

Three months later, in February 2021, the patient experienced recurrent hemoptysis. A repeat CT scan showed enlargement of the primary lesion. The patient was admitted to our hospital for further management. A PET-CT scan was performed, revealing the primary lesion (approx. 5.4 cm) with associated lymph node metastases; the disease was still considered potentially amenable to local therapy. However, given the patient’s persistent refusal of further lung radiotherapy and considering the previous response to chemotherapy, we decided to reintroduce systemic therapy. After discussion with the patient about the potential risks and benefits of various strategies, including the off-label use of immunotherapy in this context, a decision was made to administer the same chemotherapy regimen (etoposide/cisplatin) combined with durvalumab (1000 mg IV, Q3W). The patient received four cycles of this combination therapy from February to April 2021, achieving a best response of stable disease (SD) with a 16.3% reduction in the primary lesion (from 51.5 mm to 43.1 mm).

Persistently refusing conventional radiotherapy, the patient required an alternative local modality. After multidisciplinary discussion and thorough counseling, the patient provided written informed consent for a non-standard option: CT-guided percutaneous implantation of radioactive iodine-125 seeds into the left hilar lymph nodes in June 2021. Durvalumab maintenance therapy (1000 mg IV, Q3W) was continued from June 2021 to April 2022. During maintenance therapy, surveillance imaging identified a slow progression of a right adrenal gland metastasis. This lesion was treated with stereotactic body radiotherapy (SBRT, 50 Gy in 10 fractions) in April 2022, which achieved excellent local control.

Despite this, a comprehensive reassessment in May 2022 revealed clear systemic disease progression, evidenced by growth of the primary lung lesion, enlargement of lymph nodes, and a new pleural effusion. Given the patient’s prior prolonged clinical benefit from durvalumab (nearly one year), a strategy of continuing immunotherapy beyond progression with a switch to a different ICI class was discussed. After extensive counseling on the limited evidence and potential risks, including unique irAEs, the patient consented to proceed. A second-line combination regimen of apatinib, albumin-bound paclitaxel, and camrelizumab (200 mg IV, D1, Q3W) was initiated on May 7, 2022.

Follow-up blood routine examinations performed before, 7 days, and 14 days after the first cycle of this new regimen were within normal limits. However, on June 1, 2022 (23 days after the first dose of camrelizumab and immediately before the planned second cycle), a routine blood test revealed severe, isolated thrombocytopenia with a platelet count of 27 × 10⁹/L, without concurrent anemia or neutropenia. Recombinant human thrombopoietin (TPO, 15,000 U/day sc) was administered immediately. Notably, consecutive attempts at platelet transfusion matching were unsuccessful due to incompatibility. A qualitative platelet antibody assay (Sanquin Reagents B.V., The Netherlands) returned a strongly positive result (4+). Four days later (June 5, 2022), the platelet count further declined to a nadir of 14 × 10⁹/L, confirming grade 4 thrombocytopenia.

A comprehensive diagnostic workup was urgently undertaken to exclude other causes. Serological tests for hepatitis B virus, hepatitis C virus, human immunodeficiency virus, and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) were all negative. A broad panel of autoimmune antibodies was largely negative, except for a weak positive anti-Scl-70 antibody, which was of uncertain clinical significance in the absence of scleroderma symptoms. The patient has had no prior history of thrombocytopenia, including during first-line chemotherapy. Crucially, the temporal association—onset three weeks after the first and only one dose of camrelizumab, coupled with the strong anti-platelet antibody positivity and the exclusion of other probable causes—supported a diagnosis of drug-induced immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), which was likely attributable to camrelizumab.

Management and recovery

Management was challenging due to the patient’s history of hemoptysis, the rapid platelet decline, and the inability to transfuse compatible platelets, necessitating a rapid therapeutic intervention. Based on guidelines for high-grade immune thrombocytopenia, first-line therapy with high-dose corticosteroids was initiated. Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) was added for its rapid effect in blocking Fc-receptor-mediated platelet destruction. Treatment commenced on June 2, 2022, with intravenous methylprednisolone (80 mg daily, ~ 1.6 mg/kg) and IVIG (20 g daily). The patient’s platelet count responded promptly, rising to 68 × 10⁹/L by June 5 and normalizing to 163 × 10⁹/L by June 7, 2022. The methylprednisolone dose was subsequently tapered and discontinued.

Following recovery, the camrelizumab was permanently discontinued. The patient received three additional cycles of the combination therapy with albumin-bound paclitaxel and apatinib alone. No recurrence of thrombocytopenia was observed during this treatment or throughout a subsequent 2-month follow-up period, with platelet counts remaining stable between 150 and 300 × 10⁹/L. This sustained recovery without further ICI exposure further supports the relation between camrelizumab and ITP. The best response to the non-ICI therapy was stable disease (SD) with a reduction of 21.2% in the target lesion (from 51.8 mm to 40.8 mm). Discussion

Summary of clinical presentation

This case details a severe (grade 4) immune thrombocytopenia in a 70-year-old patient with ES-SCLC that emerged following the first dose of camrelizumab (anti-PD-1), which was administered after prolonged prior treatment with durvalumab (anti-PD-L1). The thrombocytopenia emerged specifically after the first dose of camrelizumab (anti-PD-1), following prolonged prior treatment with durvalumab (anti-PD-L1). The diagnosis of ICI-induced ITP was supported by the strong positivity for anti-platelet antibodies, elevated IL-6 levels, and, crucially, the systematic exclusion of alternative causes, including viral infections, other medications (rivaroxaban had been stopped months prior), bone marrow failure (given isolated thrombocytopenia), and underlying autoimmune disease. The lack of thrombocytopenia during previous chemotherapy or after cycles containing only apatinib/albumin-paclitaxel helps exonerate these agents. The rapid and complete response to corticosteroids and IVIG is highly characteristic of antibody-mediated ITP.

ICI-induced ITP is a rare but serious hematologic irAE. In a retrospective study of 2360 melanoma patients treated with ICIs, less than 1% of patients developed thrombocytopenia after ICIs [11]. Immune thrombocytopenia is the major type of immune-related hematological toxicity, accounting for 25 to 29% of hematological irAEs [12, 13]. The median time to onset of immune-associated thrombocytopenic purpura has been reported to be 41 days, with death in 11% of cases [3]. This warrants considerable attention in clinical practice.

Pathogenic mechanisms and the context of sequential ICI therapy

The mechanism of ITP is not yet fully understood, but it usually involves abnormal activation of immune cells and inflammatory responses mediated by cytokines. In our case, the patient had strongly positive anti-platelet antibodies and elevated IL-6 levels, which normalized as platelet counts recovered. A primary mechanism underlying ITP is the production of platelet antibodies [14]. The strongly positive antibodies suggest the presence of autoantibodies against platelets in the body, which may lead to thrombocytopenia by promoting platelet destruction or inhibiting megakaryocyte production. Additionally, elevated serum IL-6 levels further support the role of cytokines in the pathogenesis. Abnormally elevated IL-6 may enhance immune activation and inflammatory responses, thereby exacerbating platelet destruction [15]. Reports have shown that IL-6 receptor antagonists (such as tocilizumab) are effective in some patients [16], which also confirms the importance of this pathway.

The potential for sequential ICI therapy to increase the risk or severity of irAEs is an emerging concern. Case reports have described severe toxicities, such as pneumonitis and myocarditis, in patients who underwent sequential treatment with anti-PD-1 and anti-PD-L1 therapies [17, 18]. Proposed mechanisms for such sequence-dependent toxicity include epitope spreading, sustained immune cell modulation, and altered cytokine profiles following initial ICI exposure [17]. In our patient, thrombocytopenia occurred immediately after the first dose of sequential therapy and followed an insidious course. Although platelet counts were normal at 7 and 14 days after treatment, a rapid and asymptomatic drop was observed at the 23-day follow-up. The nearly year-long exposure to durvalumab may have primed the immune system, leading to an accelerated and severe adverse event upon PD-1 inhibition. While camrelizumab is the most immediate causative suspect, the unique temporal profile suggests that the sequential switch from PD-L1 to PD-1 inhibition may have created an immune context that predisposed the patient to this severe and rapid-onset irAE. This potential interaction warrants further investigation.

Management strategy: bridging clinical guidelines and case experience in ICI-Induced thrombocytopenia

The overall management of this case was guided by the principles outlined in international and Chinese guidelines from the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), and Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) for high-grade (≥ 3) hematologic irAEs [19–21]. Faced with grade 4 thrombocytopenia and a significant bleeding risk (history of hemoptysis), our immediate goal was to achieve a rapid platelet increase. Key steps included: (1) permanent discontinuation of camrelizumab, (2) urgent hematology consultation, and (3) initiation of first-line therapy with high-dose corticosteroids. The addition of IVIG was justified, given the high bleeding risk (history of hemoptysis) and the need for a rapid platelet increase, which aligns with guideline recommendations for severe or life-threatening cases.

The use of recombinant human thrombopoietin (rhTPO) in this context warrants specific discussion. While the guidelines from the ESMO and ASCO frequently mention TPO-RAs in their discussions of second-line treatment options [19, 20], it is crucial to note that rhTPO is explicitly recommended and widely utilized in Chinese clinical practice, as endorsed by CSCO guidelines (2025) and Chinese expert consensus documents [22, 23]. For this particular patient, who experienced acute, severe thrombocytopenia, we adhered to Chinese clinical practices and opted for the more rapidly acting rhTPO as an emergency intervention. Additionally, to address the high risk of bleeding and potential antibody-mediated mechanisms, we combined this with IVIG and high-dose corticosteroids, which are known for their rapid onset of action. This combined strategy successfully induced a rapid and sustained response, consistent with the treatment objectives. The successful reintroduction of non-ICI chemotherapy without recurrence further confirms the diagnosis and management approach.

Limitations and future directions

As a single case report, this study has inherent limitations. First, a bone marrow biopsy was not performed, which could have provided further confirmation by assessing megakaryocyte numbers and excluding other dysplastic processes. Quantitative testing and follow-up monitoring of platelet antibody levels were not available. Secondly, and more importantly, while preclinical data and case reports (including ours) suggest a potential risk, no prospective or retrospective clinical study to date clearly demonstrates an association between sequential ICI and an increased risk of irAEs. Therefore, our hypothesis regarding the sequential therapy remains speculative and requires validation in larger cohorts.

Future efforts should therefore focus on generating more systematic data to determine whether sequential ICI therapy is indeed associated with an elevated risk of severe irAEs, and to accurately characterize its incidence and clinical spectrum. This could be achieved through multicenter registries and well-designed cohort studies. Prospective studies incorporating longitudinal immune profiling could help identify predictive biomarkers for patients at high risk of severe toxicity upon ICI switching.

Conclusion

This case illustrates a severe, life-threatening episode of thrombocytopenia that occurred in the high-risk context of sequential ICI administration in SCLC. It underscores the critical importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for unusual irAEs when utilizing sequential ICI strategies outside standard guidelines. A thorough diagnostic workup to exclude other causes is paramount. While high-dose corticosteroids and IVIG can be highly effective, proactive monitoring, patient counseling, and cautious decision-making are essential when considering such therapeutic sequences. Further studies are needed to characterize the incidence and mechanisms of hematologic toxicities associated with sequential immunotherapy.
