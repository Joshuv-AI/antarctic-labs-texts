Standardized Emergency Medical Care in Mass Participation Endurance Events: Race Emergency Medicine Course (REMC) Educational Program
Authors: Luca Carenzo, Stéphane Bermon, Xavier Bigard, Korin B Hudson, Frederic Garrandes, Sergio Migliorini, Matthew D Sedgley, Chris Troyanos, Cloé Pustel, Paolo Emilio Adami
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWorld-wide, endurance races, including road races, trail races, cycling events, and triathlons, attract millions of participants each year. While these events promote health, fitness, and exercise, there exists significant risk for morbidity and mortality. The World Academy of Endurance Medicine (WAEM) created the Race Emergency Medicine Course (REMC) to train and certify race organizers and medical directors regarding planning and response for medical emergencies at endurance events. The course includes asynchronous e-learning modules, in person lectures, hands-on simulation sessions, and race-day observation.Methods 599 participants from 20 courses conducted in Europe, Asia, North and South America were surveyed. A 5-point Likert scale was utilized to assess participants’ impressions regarding the format, quality and effectiveness of the course. A paired t-test was utilized to evaluate participants’ self-assessments of their confidence in managing critical siutations both before and after participating in the course. Descriptive statistics were used to evaluate responses to open-ended questions.ResultsParticipants rated the overall REMC 4.7 out of 5. Pre-course e-learning modules were rated 4.4 out of 5 while the lectures, hands-on sessions, and race-day observation activity scored 4.6 out of 5. Overall self-confidence in managing race-related critical situations increased from a mean score of 5.0 to 6.1 on a 7-point scale (p < 0.01). 73% of participants indicated that they plan to modify their practice based on what they learned.ConclusionThe REMC is a valuable educational tool to promote medical care and safety at endurance events.

Background

Millions of individuals worldwide participate annually in mass sporting events such as road races (marathons, half-marathons), triathlons, and cycling events. For instance, in 2022, approximately 3 million people took part in 200 World Athletics label Road races globally not considering the myriads of national, regional and local races spontaneously organized [1]. Marathons and similar endurance events are associated with low mortality rates, but morbidity is relatively common and encompasses a range of medical conditions. Mortality rates are approximately 0.9 per 100,000 participants, with sudden cardiac arrest being the leading cause of death, particularly in males and during the final stages of the race [2–5]. Morbidity is relatively common in endurance sporting events, with up to 2–4% of participants requiring medical attention during or after the event. Common medical issues include exercise-associated collapse (EAC), musculoskeletal injuries, dehydration, and thermal injuries, with heat-related illnesses becoming a growing concern in an increasingly warmer climate [6–10]. A comprehensive review on medical encounters indicates that EAC is the most frequently observed condition, accounting for up to 60% of all medical encounters. This condition is usually self-limited and benign in most cases. Musculoskeletal injuries, including acute sprains, strains, and exacerbations of chronic conditions such as stress fractures, dehydration and skin-related problems like blisters and abrasions are the next most common issues depending on the kind and type of event [11]. Effective medical management of mass participation sporting events mandates professional planning and comprehensive training to ensure participant safety and efficient response to medical incidents. A well-structured medical plan should include a central command structure, integration with community resources, and strategically placed medical facilities to provide timely care [12–16]. Recognizing the unique challenges of providing medical support at mass-participation endurance events, the World Academy for Endurance Medicine (WAEM, powered by World Athletics) developed the Race Emergency Medicine Course (REMC) program. The goal of this initiative is to support clinical decision-making in athletes’ care during endurance events, based on a critical analysis of existing literature and best practices. The aim of this paper is to present the scientific rationale and first three-years of experience with this educational program.

Methods

The REMC is an educational program developed by the WAEM, a global leader in endurance and race emergency medicine [17]. Established in 2022 as a joint venture between the World Athletics Health & Science Department and the former International Institute for Race Medicine, WAEM’s mission is to promote the health and safety of athletes participating in endurance events through education, research, and the development of best practices for the delivery of emergency care. Thanks to a successful partnership between World Athletics, World Triathlon, and the International Cycling Union (UCI), all activities developed by the WAEM, are supported and recommended by all three international sports federations [18]. In particular, the REMC plays a key role in training and certifying medical staff for mass-participation endurance events. The REMC aims to enhance the skills of healthcare professionals responsible for medical support at mass-participation endurance events, such as marathons and triathlons. Medical Directors of World Athletics Label Road Races are required to complete the REMC at least once every three years as part of their continuing medical education. The REMC follows a tiered structure, with Level 1 aimed primarily at medical directors but open to anyone interested in the curriculum. Level 2 offers a more in-depth focus, exclusively for World Athletics race medical directors. Upon completing Level 2, participants may advance to the REMC Train-the-Trainer course, becoming eligible to serve as candidate trainers or fully certified REMC instructors.

The course curriculum for Level 1 integrates epidemiology of endurance mass participation events, public health, event planning, emergency preparedness, team management, as well as evidence-based clinical care protocols and hands-on simulation. In terms of planning and management, participants are expected to be able to assess risk factors for endurance related injuries and illnesses using baseline data, design a comprehensive medical response plan that includes resource estimation and allocation for large endurance events, and outline intervention strategies aimed at reducing the risk of fatal and near-fatal medical incidents during such events [12–20]. They should also be able to adopt innovative approaches to enhance traditional epidemiological methods for collecting data on medical encounters and understand the core principles of mass-casualty management and triage [21–23]. Regarding clinical care, participants are expected to be able to apply internationally recognized medical algorithms to manage common endurance sports-related illnesses and injuries, encountered during events and provide medical care onsite [24, 25]. Specific clinical competencies include being able to formulate a strategy to assess differential diagnoses and deliver appropriate treatment for athletes who collapse during or after endurance events, perform pre-hospital diagnosis and treatment for the top three leading causes of death in sport i.e. sudden cardiac arrest, exertional heat stroke, and exercise-associated hyponatremia [26–33].

Detailed structure of the level 1 training

The REMC Level 1 is structured using a blended learning approach, with an online e-Learning theoretical component followed by an in-person two days hands-on and observation workshop organized around a major endurance event around the world. The content of the course has been developed to be suitable for Athletics, Triathlon and Cycling events, as recommended by the three International Sports Federations. The course content and structure remained the same in all locations, as well as the logistical requirements and set up. In some countries, where English language knowledge was not so diffuse, the presence of a simultaneous interpreter was provided to to facilitate participants’ interaction and ensure full content understanding. Participants attending REMC Level 1 courses can receive Continuing Medical Education (CME) credits, as these courses are recognized by the European Accreditation Council for Continuous Medical Education. The REMC Course Level 2, available from 2025 will follow a similar structure to Level 1, with e-Learning, an in-person workshop on day 1, and race day observation on day 2. The course focuses on professionalizing experienced medical directors through interactive learning, experience sharing, and advanced topics like Mass Disaster Medicine, Risk Mitigation, and Contingency Planning [34–38]. Trainer-led case presentations foster critical thinking, and discussions encourage diverse, evidence-based dialogue [39–42]. The course concludes with one-on-one mentoring, where participants receive feedback on their previously submitted medical operation plans. Additionally, optional, sport-specific modules address unique needs for endurance sports (e.g. drowning prevention and management of triathlon or early concussion diagnosis for cycling). Day 2 provides field observation to apply newly acquired skills directly.

Data collection for REMC level 1

This is a descriptive study centered on participant’s satisfaction, participant’s self-perception of effectiveness, and knowledge gain. We used the first two of Kirkpatrick’s four levels of evaluation to assess this. Kirkpatrick’s tool is a model for assessing training programs. The first level explores participants’ satisfaction with the training program: overall event quality, and effectiveness of the training format. The second expresses the knowledge gain obtained with the educational process measured as the difference between pre- and post-event assessment of participant confidence in managing specific scenarios [43, 44].

All participants in the REMC program were asked to complete an anonymous online feedback form via a SurveyMonkey® webpage at the conclusion of both the first and second days of the in-person workshop. Completion of the form was requested to formally conclude the training and receive the participation certificate.

The questionnaire encompassed the following sections: (1) overall evaluation of the course, including its format and teaching quality; (2) assessment of the training format’s effectiveness; and (3) self-assessed confidence levels in managing critical care cases post-simulation. Sections one and two utilized a 5-point Likert scale, while section three employed a 7-point semantic differential scale ranging from “1 - not confident at all” to “7 - very confident.”

Open-ended questions were used to gather participants’ feedback and reflections on what they planned to implement or improve after completing the course. These responses were subsequently collected and analyzed thematically. Descriptive statistics were summarized using mean Pre-post- differences in self-perception regarding confidence were tested using the paired t-test [45]. Data analysis was conducted using GraphPad PRISM V10 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Formal ethical approval for this study was waived by the Independent Review Board Comitato Etico Territoriale Lombardia 5 (protocol 47/2025) and all study procedures were performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Before starting the course, participants were informed about the purpose of the surveys (course evaluation and research on educational outcomes), the voluntary nature of participation, anonymity, and data handling procedures. In addition, all attendees had previously signed the course participation consent, which included acceptance of privacy terms (including photo/video) and consent to the use of de-identified data for research purposes related to the course.

Results

Descriptive and quantitative data

Between January 1, 2022, and December 31, 2024, a total of 20 REMC Level 1 courses were conducted globally, covering regions including Europe, Asia, North America, and South America. These sessions collectively attracted 599 participants, comprising 74% medical doctors, 4% nurses, 4% paramedics, and 18% from other professions, including athletic trainers, physiotherapists, and race directors. No exclusion criteria were applied and no surveys were discarded becuse incomplete or invalid. Participants’ operating countries are presented in Fig. 2. Participants regarded the REMC as a valuable learning experience, giving it an overall rating of 4.7 out of 5. Similarly, the knowledge presented in the e-learning modules was rated as useful, relevant, and engaging, with a score of 4.4 out of 5. The lectures and hands-on sessions, as well as the race day observation activity received comparable ratings, also scoring 4.6 out of 5. Additionally, participants found the time allotted for each activity appropriate, and they noted that the speakers demonstrated in-depth knowledge of the topics presented. Participants’ self-assessed confidence in managing specific competencies significantly improved immediately following the event, as measured by pre- and post-event questionnaires across all competencies. Overall self-confidence in managing these competencies increased from a mean score of 5.0 to 6.1 on a 7-point scale (p < 0.01). Detailed results for each competency are provided in Table 1. Finally, 73% of participants indicated that they plan to modify their practice based on what they learned. No data is available on the REMC Level 2 as the first edition is scheduled in 2025.

Thematic analysis of participants’ feedback and reflections

Enhanced medical and safety planning

A recurring theme involves the importance of thorough pre-event medical planning. Participants repeatedly emphasized the need to conduct risk analysis and prepare medical plans tailored to the event’s specific needs, such as the race route, weather conditions, and athlete profiles. Comments highlighted the necessity of identifying probable incidents (e.g., heat stroke, hypothermia) and proactively preparing solutions, including cooling measures, ambulance and medical tent zoning, and the deployment of emergency response teams.

Heat-related illnesses and cooling strategies

Management of heat-related illnesses was a prominent focus. Participants suggested introducing or enhancing the use of ice baths, immersion cooling stations, and heat illness treatment decks at strategic locations, particularly at the finish line. Emphasis was placed on having a formalized system for heat stroke management, with a more aggressive treatment approach and adequate supplies such as rectal thermometers for precise diagnosis and treatment.

Staffing and volunteer organization

Participants frequently noted the need for better staff and volunteer management, including clearer roles, improved training, and deployment strategies. Several suggested forming specialized teams, such as sweep teams and first aid groups, and ensuring that all personnel were educated on common issues like heat-related illnesses. Recommendations also included increasing the number of medical volunteers and providing structured training sessions for them.

Communication and collaboration

Effective communication systems and collaboration with key stakeholders, including race directors and local emergency services, were highlighted. Participants expressed a desire for clearer channels of communication to ensure that medical plans and protocols are well-coordinated and understood by all involved parties.

Improving facilities and equipment

Another common theme was the need for better medical facilities and access to modern equipment. Suggestions included setting up split medical facilities (e.g., triage at the finish line separate from the main medical area), improving storage of medical supplies like ice, and ensuring the availability of advanced diagnostic tools (such as point-of-care laboratory devices) for conditions like hyponatremia, severe hypoglycemia, severe dehydration or acute myocardial infarction Some participants mentioned using their newfound knowledge to advocate for better-equipped medical stations in their respective regions.

Education and evidence-based practice

Participants acknowledged the importance of education, both for themselves and for event stakeholders. They expressed an intent to learn more, adopt a more evidence-based approach to race management, and educate athletes and staff about crucial topics, such as proper hydration and heat illness prevention. This theme also included the desire to share insights gained from the course with their teams and to incorporate structured pre-event simulations.

Structural and logistical improvements

Feedback often touched on the importance of improving the structural organization of events. Participants proposed better staging areas, more organized start and finish areas, and a focus on crowd management to ensure smooth flow and safety for participants and spectators alike.

Application of new knowledge and continuous improvement

Finally, many participants expressed a desire to apply their newly acquired knowledge to future events, highlighting specific actions they would take to improve medical support and race organization. Comments reflected a commitment to continuous improvement, such as thinking deeper, analyzing more details, and striving to exceed previous standards of care and safety.

Discussion

The implementation of the REMC by the WAEM has been a step forward in enhancing the preparedness of healthcare professionals to provide effective medical care during endurance events. The program achieved high participant satisfaction and showed statistically significant improvements in self-reported confidence across multiple clinical competencies. This underlines the REMC’s role in addressing the unique medical challenges associated with mass participation sporting events, particularly in the areas of heat-related illnesses, exercise-associated collapse, and race-day risk management. It is important to highlight that, although designed for healthcare professionals, the goal of the REMC program is to ensure the safety and well-being of athletes [46]. The REMC’s structured curriculum, which includes e-Learning modules and hands-on workshops, aligns with established recommendations for medical coverage at endurance events, and has the objective of improving healthcare staff knowledge, abilities and skills in this field.

Effective medical management of mass participation sporting events requiring professional planning and comprehensive training to ensure participant safety and efficient response to medical incidents. A well-structured medical plan should include a central command structure, integration with community resources, and strategically placed medical facilities to provide timely care [47–50]. Previous studies have highlighted the importance of pre-event all-hazards planning, risk assessment, and immediate response capabilities to reduce morbidity and mortality in endurance events [51–54]. The REMC’s tiered structure and emphasis on real-world case-studies and simulations align with recommendations from the literature on best practices for medical management at such events [25]. While studies such as those from the Twin Cities Marathon and the Chicago Marathon emphasize the high frequency of minor medical issues like musculoskeletal injuries, REMC’s focuses specifically on more severe conditions, reflecting an integrative approach to participant safety [6, 7]. Of particular interest is the thematic analysis of participant feedback that revealed that even experienced medical professionals often lacked familiarity and confidence in managing exertional heat stroke and implementing whole-body cold water rapid cooling as a treatment modality. This gap is notable given that rapid cooling including whole body cold water imersion is widely recognized as the gold standard for rapidly reducing core body temperature in EHS cases [55]. Several participants acknowledged that, prior to the course, they were unaware of the efficacy of whole-body cold water rapid-cooling or had reservations about its application, particularly concerning safety and practical implementation during endurance events. This apprehension aligns with findings in the literature, where healthcare providers have expressed concerns regarding the logistics, potential complications of whole-body cold water rapid cooling, the mandatory use of a rectal thermometer despite their proven effectivenesses [32]. The course was instrumental in enhancing participants’ knowledge and confidence and clarifying doubts about this and other lifesaving interventions. Post-course evaluations indicated a significant increase in self-assessed competence in all explored areas. The REMC program’s global reach and standardized curriculum are key strengths, providing a consistent framework for medical care across diverse geographical contexts. The use of hands-on workshops and race day observations enhances practical skill development.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, it relies primarily on participant self-assessments, which may introduce bias and cannot directly demonstrate improved clinical performance or patient outcomes. Nevertheless, within the context of REMC Level 1, self-reported impressions remain an important measure, as they reflect whether the course content was perceived as relevant, applicable, and aligned with the professional responsibilities of race medical directors and healthcare providers. Second, the present analysis only covers Level 1 of the program, as data from REMC Level 2 are not yet available. Finally, long-term follow-up is lacking, and it remains to be determined whether the reported increase in confidence translates into sustained practice changes or measurable improvements in event medical outcomes.

Conclusions

The REMC program is a valuable educational tool, promoting improved medical care and athletes’ safety at endurance events. Future research should explore the long-term impact of the course on participant outcomes and examine the effectiveness of Level 2 training. Further studies may also investigate how insights from the program influence race management and medical protocols globally. More ambitiously, it would be interesting to assess the impact of REMC programs on the incidence of accidents and illnesses during competition, their progression, and time to recover.
