Rapid Parallel Magnetic Resonance Relaxometry on Multiwell Plates with a Benchtop Scanner
Authors: Hans Gaensbauer, Yanmeng Yang, Daniel Roxby, David Bono, Jongyoon Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nuclear magnetic resonance relaxometry measurements have a diverse array of applications in medicine, biology, and chemistry, and they are valuable for monitoring cell cultures, diagnosing diseases, and detecting defects in pharmaceutical products. However, relaxometry workflows typically require laborious procedures and large sample volumes, which are both slow and destructive. This makes it difficult to use relaxometry in applications such as drug screening and cell culture development, where it has significant potential to be useful. In this work, we describe and demonstrate a low-cost benchtop system that can perform relaxometry measurements on 16 wells of a 96-well culture plate in one shot, enabling rapid, nondestructive scanning of multiple groups without any sample preparation. We demonstrate the application of this multiwell scanner for iron measurement and parallel measurements.

Introduction

Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) relaxometry has the potential to fill a key gap as a nondestructive quality monitor in biomanufacturing and a noninvasive medical diagnostic tool. For example, measurements of the T 2 relaxation time can be used to quickly quantify the amount of iron in a biological sample, quantify diabetic oxidative stress in blood serum samples, detect malaria in blood samples, and characterize senescence and differentiation efficiency in stem cell cultures. −

In addition to its applications in monitoring cell cultures, relaxometry has been shown to detect freezing, exposure to oxygen, and the formation of protein aggregates in vaccines. − Because the relaxation properties of the sample are determined by complex interactions between water molecules and the large molecules in drugs and vaccines, even very subtle chemical changes are reflected in the T 2 relaxation time.

NMR relaxometry measurements use the same mechanism exploited by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to produce contrast between different tissue types. As in MRI, the detected signals come from water molecules in the sample, which are polarized with a strong static magnetic field (B 0) and excited by a pulsed radiofrequency field (B 1) that is applied perpendicular to B 0. In biological samples, magnetic perturbers such as dissolved iron and oxygen (and to a lesser extent proteins and other macromolecules) create variations in the magnetic field strength within the sample and a loss of signal coherence over time. This in turn causes the received signal to decay exponentially with a time constant (known as T 2) that is dependent on the form and concentration of these perturbers. In practice, imperfections in the field produced by benchtop permanent magnets like those used here accelerate the loss of signal coherence to the point where the true decay cannot be measured. To get around this, Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (GPMG) experiments employ “refocusing” pulses to periodically reverse the effect of these persistent imperfections so that the sample-driven decay can be measured.

A typical benchtop relaxometry system is designed to accept small samples, which are placed one at a time inside the homogeneous region within the magnet. This is straightforward for a small number of samples but becomes prohibitively time-consuming for studies that require testing many samples and conditions, as is the case for many biological experiments. While autosamplers can automate sample loading and unloading, it is generally necessary for a technician to prepare each sample for measurement, for example, by loading it into an NMR or microcapillary tube. This presents another challenge when working with precious samples or live cell cultures, as they are effectively destroyed when they are removed from the culture vessel.

In this work, we demonstrate parallelized NMR relaxometry measurements using radiofrequency hardware that can scan 16 wells from a 96-well culture plate in a benchtop magnet weighing only 13 kg. 96-well culture plates are the gold standard for efficient sample handling in biological work: they are low cost, and many imaging and fluidic manipulation systems (e.g., autosamplers) and other instruments are designed explicitly to support them. This approach enables two significant improvements over existing NMR technologies: it can measure multiple samples at the same time, and it can measure the contents of multiwell plates in situ.

While it is possible to use MRI to take relaxation time measurements of a 96-well plate (Figure S1), the size and cost of even the relatively small animal scanners with bores large enough to accept a 90 mm by 130 mm well plate are far larger and more expensive than benchtop relaxometry systems because of the need to support gradients. In addition, it is difficult to simultaneously image the well plate while measuring the decay of each well independently: in order to match the number of echoes used to estimate the T 2 relaxation time in a conventional benchtop CPMG sequence, the scanner would need tens or even hundreds of excitations that would need to be spaced far enough apart in time for the signal to return to equilibrium. Finally, MRI techniques for T 2 measurements generally require longer echo times to allow sufficient time to acquire enough points to reconstruct the subject between each echo. This makes them more sensitive to magnet homogeneity than CPMG sequences with short echo times and ultimately lowers the sensitivity to low concentrations of iron or other relaxation agents. A better solution is to use a receive coil array with a dedicated receive coil for each well, removing the need to use additional echoes and a gradient field system for spatial contrast. This also enables the use of a smaller benchtop permanent magnet, reducing the total system cost from several million to several thousand USD. This approach is similar to that used by the NMR-MOUSE (Magritek GmbH, Aachen, Germany), but with many parallel readout channels which are explicitly designed for multiwell plates. Furthermore, the use of a single-sided magnet in the NMR-MOUSE produces a large magnetic field gradient, making it unsuitable for measuring the very long T 2 relaxation times typical of biological samples since the relaxation caused by diffusion through the gradient during the experiment dominates even for homogeneities on the order of 100 ppm.

Figure shows a schematic diagram of the system developed in this work. While the polystyrene used to construct multiwell plates does not produce an NMR signal, the sample is often confined to a small cylindrical volume at the bottom of each well. To achieve field strengths of a few thousand Gauss, the poles of permanent magnets are typically only a few centimeters apart, meaning that for the well plate to fit, the bottom surface of the well plate must be parallel to the magnet poles. This in turn means that the most common cylindrical solenoid transceiver coils are unsuitable for this application because the axis of the cylindrical sample is parallel to the applied static field and the B 1 field must be perpendicular to this axis.

Materials and Methods

Magnet and Transmit Coils

This work uses a 0.19T permanent magnet developed for an educational miniaturized MRI system. This magnet has a 152 mm diameter bore surrounded by an iron yoke, leaving a 127 mm × 127 mm × 51 mm available volume within the magnet. Because planar or surface coils produce inhomogeneous fields when used as transmit coils, we chose to use a single large solenoid to generate the applied B 1 field.

The 50 mm distance between the pole pieces constrains the diameter of a cylindrical transmit solenoid to 50 mm, far smaller than the standard 90 mm width of a 96-well plate. To fit the entire well plate, we instead use a rectangular solenoid measuring 140 mm × 127 mm by 32 mm. The solenoid is widened to fill the entire available space within the magnet so that contributions to B 1 from the vertical edges of the coil are minimized, leaving a winding geometry that can be described as two parallel sheets of current, each of which produces a homogeneous field within the bore of the solenoid. The coil is wound around a 3D printed coil former using 5 parallel strands of 660-strand 46-gauge Litz wire (Mike’s Electronic Parts 660/46-Litz) to reduce the turn count (Figure S2), which is necessary to keep the coil self-resonance frequency above the Larmor frequency. To drive the transmit solenoid with a low-cost radio power amplifier, it must be tuned to resonate at the proton resonance frequency of 8.05 MHz and matched to a 50 Ω source with an L network consisting of two high-voltage air-variable capacitors.

A significant concern with large transmit coils in permanent magnet systems is the formation of eddy currents in the conductive metal yoke of the magnet. In our testing, this eddy current effect was observed as a reduction in the field strength of our solenoid once it was fitted to the magnet. The presence of the yoke, which acts as a shorted turn on an air-core transformer formed by the yoke and the solenoid, dramatically changes the tuning of the transmit coil. For this reason, the tuning circuit is kept outside of the magnet and the tuning and matching procedure is performed with the solenoid fitted inside the magnet. At the low transmit duty cycles required for even very frequent measurements, the resistive heating caused by eddy currents in the yoke is small enough to be ignored, and it is sufficient to increase the transmit power to obtain the desired B 1. After constructing the transmit coil, we checked B 1 homogeneity by confirming that the pulse amplitude required to produce a 180° flip is the same across all 16 wells.

Receive Coil Array

Because the field produced by the sample is parallel to the bottom of the plate and because the entire length of the receive coil must sit below the well plate, the optimum coil geometry is not obvious. To identify the coil geometry with the highest sensitivity per turn, we compared the ratio of the coil sensitivity to the total coil length for several candidate field geometries (shown in detail in Figure S3) using the “receive field” approach described by Haacke et al. After comparing an offset planar spiral, a small rectangular solenoid made from 2 layers of a printed circuit board, and a butterfly coil, we identified that a butterfly coil produced the highest sensitivity to the bottom 0.5 mm of a well per turn. This butterfly coil was then further optimized by computing the flux through the coil’s plane, modeling the magnetized sample as a spatial distribution of microscopic dipoles. The mathematical framework for this two-stage optimization is described in the Supporting Information, and the results are shown in Figure . We could then compute the flux captured per unit length of the coil for every possible length and width of the butterfly coil to find optimum values at 8.6 mm by 6.5 mm. Fortuitously, these values are smaller than the 9 mm well spacing on standard 96-well plates, allowing one of these coils to be placed under each well without any overlap.

Because the resistance of the coil is not significantly larger than the rest of the tuned circuit resistance, higher turn counts produce better SNR until the self-resonance frequency of the coil falls below the Larmor frequency. These coils are implemented as 3 concentric coils, repeated across the three top layers of a 4-layer printed circuit board for ease of manufacture and to ensure the best consistency between coils (Figure ). We selected a winding pattern that avoids the use of blind vias to minimize the cost of these coil arrays. Additional images showing the 3D coil layout are listed in Figure S4.

(a) The receive coil array. The receive coils are implemented as a single 4-layer printed circuit board. To reduce eddy current effects, each coil is completely differential inside the magnet. The field-detector coil is visible at the top. (b) A 3D view of one coil. Each coil has 9 turns and occupies 3 layers of the board. By moving away from the coil to switch between layers, it is possible to use only standard vias that traverse all the board layers, thereby reducing costs. (c) A photo of the 16-well readout setup, including the magnet, the well plate bracket, the receive coil array, and the multiplexer/tuner circuit. The transmit coil is hidden inside the magnet.

Each coil is set up as a differential-balanced source, so that there are no grounded wires that might change the tuning of adjacent coils or allow eddy currents to flow. The coils are all connected to external tuning and switching networks that tune the coils to resonance during reception and detune them during transmission (Figure S5). This is required because any current induced in the highly resonant receiving coils during transmission will distort B 1 through the sample.

The switching circuit is based on the differential PIN diode switch described by Xu et al. During transmission, the diodes are forward-biased, connecting the “detuning” capacitor to the resonant circuit and shifting its resonant frequency away from the Larmor frequency. During reception, the diodes are strongly reverse-biased, presenting a high impedance that removes the “detuning” capacitor, bringing the coil to resonance. Because the diodes do not participate in the coil resonance during receiving, it is possible to use a generic silicon rectifier rather than the more traditional PIN diodes to reduce the physical footprint of the array of switching circuits. After the switching section, the differential resonant circuits are matched to a differential 50 Ω impedance with series capacitors. The signal is then converted to a single-ended signal with a balun and fed into a multiplexer that selects which coil is connected to the relaxometry console.

Relaxation Time Measurement and Pulse Sequence Parameters

This work used a custom NMR console that can run pulse sequences written with pulseq and a custom power amplifier operating at 20 W peak output power. This power level creates a B 1 amplitude of 200 μT, which corresponds to a 180° pulse time of 50 μs. To scan multiple wells in parallel, the console is programmed to switch to the next coil after each pair of echoes. Because a typical experiment contains thousands of echoes, the reduction in the number of echoes received per well by a factor of 16 still produces hundreds of echoes, far more than is necessary to obtain a good least-squares fit for determining the T 2 time.

It is possible to improve the bandwidth of the refocusing pulses by using a smaller flip angle and a correspondingly shorter pulse (Figure S6). The CPMG experiment compensates for flip-angle error for even echoes, and the exponential fit is unaffected after the odd echoes are discarded. Each experiment requires the coherent sum of two scans with a 180° phase difference between the refocusing pulses to suppress stimulated echoes arising from flip-angle imperfections. Furthermore, a sufficiently long echo time provides temporal separation between each spin echo and any unwanted ringing after refocusing pulses. Because shorter echo times yield better refocusing for samples with significant diffusion, the optimal echo time is system-dependent for these experiments, we use an echo time of 1 ms, which is about twice T 2* for the most homogeneous wells.

Field Measurement and Shimming

Because the dimensions of the 4 × 4 grid of wells are much larger than those of a typical (single) sample, the total variation in magnetic field over the detection volume is significantly higher than it is for a single sample. As a result, different wells have different resonance frequencies, and the excitation and refocusing pulses must have sufficient bandwidth to cover the entire frequency range spanned by the well plate if the entire grid is to be measured at once. To use a lower power transmitter, it is possible to measure the center frequency for each well beforehand and then run a separate CPMG experiment for each well where the excitation frequency has been set to the correct value for that well (for the 20 W transmit power used in this work, this is not necessary). The center frequency of each well can be measured quickly using the same interecho switching techniques used to make simultaneous T 2 measurements. For calibration, a 5 ms echo time allows each well to be measured long enough to resolve the Larmor frequency to within 200 Hz.

Without shimming, hard pulses at the maximum output power of our amplifier cannot refocus all wells simultaneously. To address this issue, we shim the magnet using a single-channel linear shim coil that is embedded below the receive coil array (Figure S7). This coil is driven with a benchtop linear power supply and can be rotated to match the orientation of the linear field error. Residual nonlinear error is further shimmed using small neodymium magnets, which are placed on the poles of the permanent magnet (Figure S8). These shimming approaches reduce the total frequency variation across the wells from 8 kHz to below 2.5 kHz (Figure ) and are only performed once while setting up the instrument.

Results and Discussion

Iron Quantification with Standard Solutions

The sensitivity and repeatability of the system were characterized by measuring the known concentrations of iron in 2% nitric acid in every well. The acidic environment ensures that all the iron in the sample is converted to paramagnetic Fe3+. 2.5 mL samples were prepared by serial dilution of a 1 mM solution of Fe3+ with 2% nitric acid. Sixteen samples (150 μL each) of each concentration were loaded into a 4 by 4 grid within a polycarbonate well plate (Thermo Fisher 442404), which was sealed using an adhesive cover and loaded into the magnet. Figure shows the mean and standard deviation for single-shot CPMG T 2 measurements of every sample concentration in every well with 5000 echoes total, 2 scans, and an echo time of 1 ms. Each well plate measurement takes 5 s and produces 156 echoes per well after discarding odd echoes. For concentrations below 3 μM, the measured relaxation rate is equal to the measured relaxation rate of pure water; this is visible as a flattening of the calibration curve after the 3 μM sample. Below 3 μM, variations in the local field homogeneity cause the diffusion of the sample between echoes to dominate the measured T 2 decay, rather than interactions with paramagnetic iron. At higher concentrations, these effects are insignificant. The single-shot geometric coefficient of variation (GCV) across the 16 wells over all 13 concentrations is 0.14, which is comparable to the GCV across repeated measurements taken from the same well (0.12). This indicates that there is good consistency between wells and that more accurate measurements can be produced by averaging multiple scans as in other instruments.

Relaxometry measurements on a serial dilution of iron in nitric acid from 1 mM to 125 nM. Each concentration is measured in every well with a single multiplexed CPMG sequence with two scans. This graph shows the mean and standard deviation of the measurements at each concentration. The system can measure the iron concentration down to about 3 μM.

Monitoring Plasma Oxidation with Hydrogen Peroxide

As a demonstration of a parallelized measurement, we have measured the T 2 relaxation time of samples of human plasma treated with hydrogen peroxide and ascorbic acid as described by Wierzuchowska et al. , (Figure ). Hydrogen peroxide is a strong oxidizer that converts Fe2+ in the plasma to paramagnetic Fe3+, causing a rapid concentration-dependent decrease in the relaxation times of the solution. Plasma has some inherent antioxidant properties, and after the initial drop, the T 1/T 2 relaxation times begin to increase toward an equilibrium point. The addition of ascorbic acid, which is an antioxidant, accelerates the recovery of the relaxation time. Similar experiments have been used to quantify the inherent antioxidant activity of human plasma samples, as a surrogate diagnostic tool for diabetes.

Samples of human plasma treated with two different concentrations of hydrogen peroxide, with and without the addition of ascorbic acid. High concentrations of hydrogen peroxide cause a steep drop in the T 2 relaxation time of the sample. The addition of ascorbic acid accelerates the subsequent recovery of the T 2 relaxation time.

These experiments lend themselves to parallelization because they require several samples to be measured repeatedly over a period of time. For systems that can only handle one sample, either the samples need to be swapped for each time-point (as in Peng et al.) or run at completely different times (as in Wierzuchowska et al. , ). In this measurement, 16,180 μL samples of fresh plasma are loaded into a single well plate. 20 μL of 0.58 M ascorbic acid solution in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) is added to half of these samples, while the other half are given 20 μL of PBS with no ascorbic acid. These 16 samples are then treated with 4 different concentrations of hydrogen peroxide to bring the final hydrogen peroxide concentrations to 0.3%, 0.075%, 0.019%, and 0.005%. This gives 2 samples each of 8 conditions. These samples are measured every 30 s for 30 min with a CPMG pulse sequence (5000 echoes and 300 μs echo time). For clarity, Figure shows the mean and standard deviation for only the highest and lowest concentrations of hydrogen peroxide with and without ascorbic acid; a graph showing all 4 concentrations is available in Figure S9. The T 2 relaxation times behave similarly to the T1 measurements presented by Wierzuchowska et al. , This experiment clearly demonstrates our ability to carry out multiple parallel measurements repeatedly without any manual intervention, a critically important functionality in many bioanalytical chemistry and diagnostics, enabled by our instrumentation.

Conclusions

In this work, we have demonstrated parallel NMR measurements on the contents of 96-well plates using dedicated radiofrequency hardware that is well suited for use with low-cost, permanent magnet systems. This system can measure the contents of 16 wells with a single CPMG echo train, which reduces the measurement time from minutes to a mere seconds. By moving the well plate forward and backward within the magnet, it is possible to measure 48 of the wells in a standard 96 well plate in three acquisitions. Furthermore, the ability to measure the contents of multiwell plates in situ means that no additional sample loading is required to perform a measurement. Together, these improvements allow for a dramatic speedup: the 208 sample calibration measurements presented in Figure took 9 min, compared to over 10 h with a single-sample, capillary-based system that takes 3 min to load, run, and unload. Because the experiments are very fast (<10 s) and the sample is not removed from the well plate, we can measure the T 2 time for samples of growing cells and other biological samples without destroying the samples. The ability to parallelize these measurements opens the door to automated high-throughput parallel biology experiments, which are not practical with conventional benchtop relaxometry systems that require the manual loading of samples held in glass tubes.
