Directed evolution of a staphylophage under biofilm and planktonic conditions
Authors: Carlos Valdivia, Pilar Domingo-Calap
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The rise of multidrug-resistant bacteria, particularly biofilm-forming pathogens such as Staphylococcus epidermidis, highlights the urgent need for alternative antimicrobial strategies. Phage therapy, which uses phages to selectively infect and lyse bacterial cells, offers a promising solution. In this study, we evolved the lytic phage vB_Sep_Steph1 under both biofilm and planktonic conditions, using varying initial phage inoculum titers. Whole-genome sequencing of evolved populations revealed recurrent condition-dependent mutations in holins and structural genes with putative depolymerase activity—critical for host recognition and biofilm degradation. Phenotypic improvements in traits such as antibacterial efficacy and replicative fitness were observed to be highly dependent on both the presence of biofilm and the initial phage titer during evolution. Furthermore, some evolved phage lineages could delay bacterial resistance better than the ancestral strain. These findings support the utility of directed phage evolution to improve therapeutic efficacy and robustness, particularly against biofilm-associated infections.

Introduction

The rise of antibiotic resistance is a major global health concern. The overuse of antibiotics, driven by their widespread availability and production, has led to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant bacteria1. By 2050, it is estimated that 10 million people will die annually from infections caused by bacteria that are resistant to current antibiotics2,3. In 2019, multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria were associated with approximately 5 million deaths, with 1.27 million directly caused by MDR infections2. A particularly effective survival strategy involves forming biofilms, microbial communities encased in a self-produced matrix of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), extracellular DNA (eDNA), and proteins4,5. These complex architectures protect bacteria from antimicrobials, immune responses, and environmental stress. Biofilm development follows a five-stage process: reversible attachment, irreversible attachment, maturation I (cluster formation), maturation II (microcolony formation), and dispersion6–8. Biofilms are especially problematic in clinical settings, where they form on medical devices such as catheters and prosthetic joints, often leading to persistent and hard-to-treat infections. The dense bacterial environment within biofilms also promotes horizontal gene transfer, facilitating the spread of virulence and resistance determinants9. Among biofilm-forming pathogens, Staphylococcus epidermidis is a leading cause of device-associated infections. Although typically a harmless skin commensal, S. epidermidis poses a serious threat to immunocompromised individuals and neonates, frequently causing hospital-acquired infections10–12. Furthermore, treatment is increasingly challenging, as clinical isolates commonly exhibit methicillin resistance (MRSE), and rising vancomycin resistance is further compromising treatment efficacy13.

Bacteriophages or phages, viruses that infect and lyse bacteria, represent a promising alternative to antibiotics, particularly against biofilm-associated infections. Phages naturally thrive in biofilm-dominated environments and have developed mechanisms to penetrate and replicate within these protective structures14. The phage infection cycle is initiated when receptor-binding proteins (RBPs) adsorb to specific bacterial surface receptors. This interaction constitutes the first determinant to infection and where most of the selective pressure for phage evolution resides. Following successful adsorption, the viral genome is injected into the host, and replication proceeds upon evasion of bacterial defence mechanisms14–16. Lytic phages, which promptly kill their hosts by bursting the bacterium, are of particular interest for therapeutic applications17. A significant limitation, however, is their generally narrow host range, which is often restricted to closely related bacterial isolates. This specificity can be broadened through strategies such as phage cocktails, mutagenesis, or directed evolution. Directed evolution, in which phages are cultured under selective pressures, can enhance other traits such as virulence, anti-biofilm activity, shortened lysis time, and resistance evasion18–20. During evolution, the accumulation of mutations is shaped by selection pressures, which are strongly influenced by environmental conditions, playing a crucial role in determining the evolutionary trajectory of the phages19,21. Notably, adaptive mutations often arise in structural proteins like RBPs and depolymerases22, able to degrade capsules or the biofilm matrix to facilitate infection. Because of this enzymatic activity, phage-derived enzymes, including depolymerases and endolysins, can also be used independently of whole phages as therapeutic agents23–25.

In this study, we designed an experimental evolution setup to study the adaptation of a lytic S. epidermidis phage, vB_Sep_Steph1, under biofilm and planktonic conditions. Evolution passages were performed with both low and high initial phage inoculum, as this is a parameter that has been shown to influence phage evolution before19. Our results revealed condition- and titer-dependent mutational patterns (Supplementary Table 1), particularly in genes encoding structural components involved in receptor recognition and biofilm degradation. For some lineages, these mutations were associated with improved bacterial killing and delayed resistance emergence. This work underscores the power of directed evolution to enhance phage efficacy and highlights the importance of environmental context in shaping adaptive trajectories.

Results

Directed evolution experimental design for phage optimization

We evolved twelve independent lineages of phage Steph1 through 20 serial passages against S. epidermidis ATCC 35984 across four distinct experimental conditions, as outlined in Fig. 1. These evolution conditions were defined by a combination of (1) initial phage inoculum—either low (10³ PFU) or high (10⁵ PFU)—and (2) bacterial growth mode — biofilm or planktonic. Thus, three different lineages were established for each condition: lineages A, B, and C were evolved in biofilm conditions with low inoculum; DEF under biofilm conditions with high inoculum; GHI under planktonic conditions with low inoculum; and JKL under planktonic conditions with high inoculum. These titers corresponded to low multiplicities of infection (MOI: ~0.01 for high inoculum, ~0.0001 for low inoculum, representing phage to bacteria ratios). Every condition included three different lineages. Negative controls were included throughout the experiment to validate the absence of cross-contamination among lineages. We observed titer fluctuations in the low-inoculum, biofilm-evolved lineages, which stabilized between passages 7 and 12 (Supplementary Table 2). Phage fitness is dependent on the evolutionary context

To assess the impact of experimental evolution on phage replication dynamics, phage amplification was performed under biofilm and planktonic conditions and compared to the ancestral phage. Phage amplification was done similarly to the experimental evolution, and 4 h post infection (hpi) phage supernatants were titrated for all the lineages, including the ancestral phage (Fig. 2). Interestingly, lineages evolved under high inoculum, biofilm conditions reached higher titers than the planktonic counterparts, for both biofilm-forming (t-test, P = 0.013) and planktonic-forming (t-test, P = 0.002) conditions. Indeed, when cross-testing lineages in the opposing biofilm state, lineages D (t-test P = 0.043) and F (t-test P = 0.027) successfully led to significant higher titers compared to the ancestral phage after planktonic amplification. Evolved phages increase lytic activity

To evaluate antibacterial efficacy, both ancestral and evolved phages were tested for their ability to reduce bacterial load following a 4-h incubation (Fig. 3). Lytic activity was assessed under two sets of conditions, those that matched the evolution environment for each lineage and the opposing biofilm-forming state. At high inoculum, the ancestral phage significantly reduced S. epidermidis counts in both planktonic and biofilm states (t-test, P < 0.003), achieving reductions of over two orders of magnitude. In contrast, no significant reduction was observed at low inoculum, planktonic conditions (t-test, P > 0.078). However, this changed for the evolved phages, as all lineages significantly reduced bacterial load across conditions (t-test, P ≤ 0.033), demonstrating overall improved lytic efficacy. Directed evolution can delay the emergence of phage-resistant bacteria

Phages that can target resistant bacterial variants may have an evolutionary advantage, enabling prolonged suppression of bacterial populations over time20,26. To assess this, we monitored the long-term inhibitory capacity of evolved phages using bacterial growth curves, under the same conditions of the experimental evolution. Cultures were challenged with either the ancestral or evolved phages at 2.5 × 106 PFUs/mL, and bacterial growth was tracked as optic density at 620 nm (OD620) in a plate reader over 49.5 h (Fig. 4). Across all conditions, phage-resistant variants emerged heterogeneously. In the biofilm forming state, the ancestral phage was able to control bacterial population until 9 hpi, when phage-resistance emerged, reaching a lower OD620 by the end of the experiment (t-test, P = 0.046). Lineages evolved under low inoculum further inhibited bacterial growth until around 22 hpi, when resistant variants arose (heterogeneously) across replicates. Those evolved at high inoculum achieved a similar short-term reduction, but also maintained the bacterial culture at low OD620 until the end of the experiment, controlling the emergence of resistant variants. We quantitatively evaluated phage performance using three metrics: the normalized area under the curve (nAUC), the centroid index (CI)—which reflects lytic efficiency based on OD variations—and the final optical density value. All biofilm lineages successfully reduced the nAUC (t-test, P < 0.021), but only lineages evolved at high inoculum significantly increased the CI (t-test, P < 0.008) and lowered the OD620 at 49.5 hpi (t-test, P < 0.014). Structural proteins and a holin represent mutational hotspots during phage evolution

To elucidate the genetic basis of phage adaptation, we sequenced the ancestral and the evolved phages (passages 0 and 20) and compared the mutational landscape (Supplementary Table 3). While mutations were distributed across the genome, a significant clustering (Poisson test with FDR correction, Q ≤ 0.018) was observed within a ~ 10 kb structural region spanning positions 92,788 to 102,574 (Fig. 5). This region encompasses CDSs 152 to 158, although no mutations were detected in CDS_0153 or CDS_0155. Remarkably, 42 of the 66 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) identified across all lineages localized within this region. Of these, 37 mutations were concentrated in four CDSs (CDS_0152, CDS_0154, CDS_0156, and CDS_0158), all predicted to encode tail fibers or other virion structural proteins with putative depolymerase activity (Fig. 6). Approximately 70% of mutations present at low (>5%) and moderate (>30%) frequencies were found in CDSs annotated with “tail” or “head and packaging” functions. Only a subset of mutations reached fixation (frequency > 90%) by the end of evolution, predominantly in CDS_0156, and notably, none fixed in lineages evolved under low inoculum biofilm conditions. Both biofilm presence and phage inoculum influenced mutational patterns: high-frequency mutations in CDS_0156 were exclusive to planktonic-evolved genomes. Under planktonic conditions, high inoculum lineages accumulated mutations almost exclusively within this hotspot, whereas low inoculum lineages exhibited more dispersed mutations. (Supplementary Table 4). A secondary hotspot emerged in high-inoculum biofilm lineages (Poisson test with FDR correction, Q = 0.003), with moderate to high-frequency mutations detected in CDS_0185, encoding a holin protein outside the canonical phage lytic cassette. Mutation frequencies in this gene reached up to 88%. Additionally, large deletions, observed as regions of no coverage, were present in lineages A, B, G, and I (Supplementary Table 5). These deletions primarily affected regions encoding hypothetical proteins, membrane proteins, a nucleotide pyrophosphohydrolase, a homing endonuclease, and a DNA-binding protein. Notably, loss of the DNA-binding protein gene was shared among genomes A, B, F, and I. Discussion

Bacteria with biofilm-forming activity possess a significant threat that requires urgent solutions13,27. Phage therapy offers a promising option, with directed evolution potentially enhancing its effectiveness. Previous studies have demonstrated the potential of phage evolution as a strategy to enhance activity against biofilm-forming bacteria20,26. Interestingly, some reports have shown that low viral titers can actually promote biofilm formation28,29, underscoring the importance of achieving sufficiently high phage concentration for therapeutic efficacy. However, attaining high titers can be challenging for certain phages, and experimental evolution may provide a means to overcome these limitations. To date, most evolutionary studies have focused on expanding host range and delaying the emergence of resistance under planktonic conditions21,22,26. Given that biofilm formation profoundly alters bacterial physiology and surface structures, we hypothesized that phages may adapt differently depending on whether their bacterial hosts form biofilms or growth under planktonic states.

Here, we evolved a staphylophage under different conditions of biofilm formation and phage inoculum. Our initial inoculums varied from 103 to 105 PFUs, which translates to MOIs of approximately 0.0001 and 0.01, respectively. In our experiment, evolved lineages in biofilm conditions significantly reduced the emergence of phage-resistant bacterial variants, suggesting an interesting approach for phage training for therapeutics. In fact, our evolved phages can reduce biofilm formation even at 103 PFUs. Recently, a phage belonging to the same family as Steph1 (PM448), was used for a prosthetic knee S. epidermidis infection successfully. This phage was initially selected for its strong lytic capacity, maintaining a clear culture for up to 40 h at an MOI of 100, and for 24 h at an MOI of 10. Remarkably, several evolved lineages in this study were able to supress bacterial growth for comparable durations at MOIs as low as 0.1. Even though resistant variants eventually emerged under our in vitro conditions, previous in vivo studies have shown that synergistic interactions between phages, antibiotics, and the immune system can collectively prevent relapse and promote infection clearance30–32. However, optic density readings can be affected by bacterial clumping, EPS production, or pelleted bacteria. Biofilms can be complex and stratified, and other approaches could be more adequate to measure biofilm growth more accurately. Methods like crystal violet staining measure total biomass, which includes not only living cells but also EPS and dead cells33. Microcalorimetry or RT-qPCR allow for accurate measure of phage-bacterial dynamics, without being biased by bacterial aggregates34,35. These have given good results when monitoring phage-bacteria dynamics in recent evolution works26. However, none of these methods accurately depict in vivo conditions, and in the end still require experimental confirmation. To circumvent the problems resulting from optic density readings and get a broader picture of the infection in its early stages, we evaluated the killing and amplification capacity of the evolved lineages and compared it against the ancestral phage at 4 hpi. Directed evolution tended to improve lytic activity, and observed effects were more marked under planktonic conditions, as observed previously36,37. On the other hand, phage amplification was only slightly increased for biofilm lineages when compared to planktonic ones. As expected, for both conditions, higher MOI showed increased lytic activity.

The integration of genomic and phenotypic data highlights that structural protein mutations are the main drivers of enhanced phage performance. The predominance of mutations in tail fibers and other structural protein genes aligns closely with the improved antibacterial phenotypes observed in evolved phage lineages, as previously reported20,22. These proteins are directly involved in host recognition, attachment, and biofilm degradation, critical steps for infection and lysis. In addition, the high frequency of mutations in genes associated with depolymerase activity suggests adaptive modification of phage adsorption and biofilm-targeting mechanisms. This has been extensively described as these mutations can allow for adaptation against new receptors or a more specific binding or access to their ligand38,39. Notably, mutations fixed in CDS_0156 were primarily found in planktonic-evolved lineages, which also showed enhanced killing. Interestingly, structural modeling of the related phage phi812 tail, belonging to the same subfamily as Steph1, revealed the RBP1 homolog functions as the main adsorption determinant, recognizing and binding to lipo(teichoic) acids on the bacterial cell surface40. This supports the idea that mutations in Steph1’s RBP1 could modulate receptor recognition and host interaction during adaptation. The secondary hotspot in the holin gene observed in biofilm evolved lineages might be related to adaptation in lysis timing, allowing increased phage fitness in the biofilm context. Our biofilm-forming conditions involved minimal mixing, as opposed to the well-mixed planktonic growth, and thus, lower availability of hosts. Previous work has pointed that phages evolved in spatially structured populations are led to evolve towards slower, weaker adsorptions in order to diffuse better. This improves overall local productivity, without maximizing infectivity41. Following existing models, an increased infectivity (potentially explained by mutations in RBPs) would be more beneficial under well-mixed conditions and high availability of hosts, while biofilm lineages would be benefited by increasing the local density of susceptible hosts42. In contrast, modulating or nullifying the affinity of the holin could result in slower lysis and increased burst sizes. This might be an adaptation to biofilm conditions, environments that are spatially limited and where bacterial metabolism can be slowed43,44.

Collectively, our genomic and phenotypic data support a model where the evolutionary trajectory of Steph 1 is shaped by mutations in two key genetic loci: the RBP1 and the holin. We propose that mutations in RBP1 drive enhanced virulence and lytic activity, whereas mutations in the holin primarily modulate replication dynamics and amplification efficiency, probably due to modification of the lysis time. This is evidenced by the observation that planktonic-evolved lineages, which evolved under well-mixed conditions with high host availability, accumulated and fixed mutations predominantly in RBP1 and exhibited increased lytic activity. In contrast, biofilm-evolved lineages, adapting to spatially structured and host-limited environments, converged on mutations in the holin gene, likely adjusting lysis timing to optimize burst size. This strategy enhances local progeny production without maximizing immediate killing, consistent with evolutionary models for structured populations41. Regarding genomic deletions observed in the evolved lineages, most affected genes with unknown function, and were located outside the structural hotspot. This pattern was also reported previously26, and it may reflect a streamlining process, as shorter genomes can replicate more efficiently45. Importantly, whole-population sequencing limits our ability to correlate specific mutations to individual phenotypic traits. Moreover, whether these evolved lineages altered adsorption time or modified infection dynamics as a consequence of evolution remains an open question for future investigation.

In summary, our study demonstrates that the evolutionary dynamics of phage Steph1 are strongly shaped by the host environment, driving distinct adaptive routes in planktonic versus biofilm conditions. Mutations in key functional genes such as the receptor-binding protein and the holin underpin these divergent strategies, reflecting trade-offs between virulence and fitness. These findings highlight how environmental structure constrains and directs phage evolution, with implications for optimizing therapeutic phage design and evolution-based approaches. Understanding how phages adapt to complex bacterial lifestyles, including biofilms, will be essential for developing robust phage therapies against complex MDR infections.

Methods

Phage and bacteria

The phage vB_Sep_Steph1 (Steph1), a previously isolated lytic S. epidermidis phage in our laboratory46 was selected for the experimental evolution. S. epidermidis ATCC 35984, a genome-sequenced clinical MRSE isolate47, was used as a host. Cultures were grown in LB media at 37 °C, 250 rpm agitation, and aerobic conditions. LB medium was prepared with 10 g/L tryptone (TRYN-00P-500, Labkem), 5 g/L yeast extract (YEEX-1MB-500, Labkem), 10 g/L NaCl (SOCH-00A-500, Labkem), and supplemented with 0.42 g/L CaCl₂ (C/1400/53, Fisher Scientific). Agar (AGAG-00P-500, Labkem) was added to 15 or 7.5 g/L to prepare agar plates or top agar for overlays, respectively. After coincubation of phage and bacteria, the phage lysate was obtained by serial centrifugation of the culture and filtration through a 0.22 µm filter. Bacteria-free lysate was kept at 4 °C for short-term storage and −70 °C for medium-term storage. For phage quantification, bacterial overnight was mixed with serial dilutions of phage lysate using the double-layer technique48.

Bacterial stock preparation for phage evolution

Overnight bacterial culture was diluted in fresh media in 500 mL flasks to an initial OD600 of ≈0.001, and incubated at 37 °C and 250 rpm agitation until an OD600 ≈ 0.05. At this point, the incubation was stopped by transferring the culture to 50 mL tubes on ice. The culture was then centrifuged at 4 °C and the pellet was resuspended in 0.6 mL of media. Concentrated cultures were diluted in glycerol to a final concentration of 20% (v/v), and quickly frozen in liquid nitrogen for their stocking at −70 °C.

Phage evolution design

Experimental evolution of the phage was carried out during 20 passages under four different conditions, differentiated by the biofilm production status and the phage inoculum used. Biofilm growing conditions included the use of LB 1% glucose and centrifugation at 200 g for 2 min before static incubation in a treated well-24 plate. Planktonic growing conditions lacked glucose and were incubated at 850 rpm agitation using 2 mL round-bottom tubes. To initiate each evolution passage, a stock of the bacterial culture was thawed and diluted in 3 mL of LB, to achieve a standard OD600 of approximately 0.05 at the beginning of each passage. Then, aliquots of 500 µL were prepared and inoculated with 10 µL of phage (either inoculating 103 or 105 PFUs for low and high inoculum lineages, respectively). Phage lysates from each passage were aliquoted and frozen, with one aliquot subsequently thawed and titrated to inoculate the next passage with the selected titer. All conditions were incubated for 22 h at 37 °C.

Evaluation of lytic activity and phage fitness

Evolved phages were tested to evaluate their ability to reduce bacterial load 4 hpi. For planktonic lineages, bacteria were counted by standard plate colony counting. For biofilm lineages, wells were washed twice before scrapping the biofilm, and then sonicated in PBS 1X at 37 kHz (Elmasonic p60H sonic bath) for 2 min and plated for counting. In addition, bacteria-free supernatants were titrated to determine phage amplification. For both the killing and fitness assays, evolved lineages were compared to the ancestral phage. All the experiments were performed in triplicate (three independent biological replicates, each made up of three technical replicates). Statistical significance was evaluated by unpaired t-test in R (v4.4.0)49.

Bacterial growth curves

To assess bacteria-phage dynamics, growth curves were performed. Optical density readings at 620 nm (Multiskan FC Microplate Photometer) were taken at regular intervals of 30 min for a total of 49.5 h after facing bacteria with the evolved phages. 125 μL were inoculated in 96-well plate plates at a concentration of 2.5 × 106 PFUs/mL in LB supplemented with either 0% glucose (with agitation) or 1% glucose (without agitation), depending on the biofilm-forming status of each lineage. Growth curves were generated using GraphPad Prism version 10.3.1 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA). Three independent replicates composed each of two technical replicates were used. The area under the growth curve (AUC) was calculated for each condition using the trapezoidal rule in R (v4.4.0)49. Then, AUC was normalized by first subtracting the AUC of the negative control (LB medium) from all sample values. CI was calculated with the Centroid_Index_Calculator software50. Here, for figure representation, the average of replicates was calculated after substituting negative values with 0, as they lack a meaningful interpretation. Last OD620 values (t = 49.5 h), nAUC, and CI were used to compare the evolved lineages against the ancestor phage. Statistical significance was assessed using unpaired t test in R (v4.4.0)49.

Phage sequencing

High titer phage lysates were centrifuged at 80,000 × g for 2 h, supernatants were carefully removed and pellet were resuspended in SM buffer and kept at −70 °C. 180 μL of each lysate were added to 20 μL of DNAse buffer 10X, 4 μL of DNAse turbo, and 2 μL of benzonase and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to digest any bacterial DNA. Then another 2 μL of DNAse were added and left for incubation in the same conditions to ensure total removal of the host’s DNA. To stop the reaction, 6 μL of EDTA 0.5 M were added and incubated for 10 min at 75 °C. Afterwards, 20 μL of SDS 10% and 10 μL of proteinase K (20 mg/mL) were added to digest the viral capsids at 55 °C for 45 min. Genome concentration and purification were carried out using a Maxwell ®RSC (Promega), and concentrations were assessed using a Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega). Samples with a concentration over 0.2 ng/ μL were sequenced using Illumina platform.

Bioinformatic analysis of the phage genomes

Paired-end reads were checked through FastQC v0.12.151 and assembled de novo using Unicycler v0.5.052. Contigs were manually assembled where necessary and the resulting consensus genomes were refined with Pilon v1.2453 and manually revised with IGV v.2.16.254. Assembled genomes were then annotated using PHOLD v0.1.455. To detect CDSs with potential depolymerase activity, DePP v1.0.056, DepoScope57 and Phage DPO (galaxy v0.1.0)58 were used with default parameters. For Phage DPO and DePP, hits over 80% prediction fidelity were considered. PhageRBPdetect v359 was used to detect RBPs. For data representation, ancestral phage lineage was visualized with the R package gggenomes. To identify SNPs in the evolved lineages LoFreq v2.1.560 was used. Obtained SNPs were filtered according to the following criteria: Only SNPs with an allele frequency > 0.05 and at least 30 reads covering that position were considered. Each position had to be covered both by forward and reverse reads and indels inside repetitive sequences (HRUN > 5) were discarded. Lastly SNPs already present in the control unevolved phage were not considered. The effect of those mutations was checked through SnpEff v5.261 and manually revised with IGV. To identify mutational hotspots, sliding-window scan across the genome were performed. Mutation counts in any lineage within each 2.5 kb window (step = 250 pb) were compared to the expected uniform distribution using a Poisson model. Windows were considered when they contained at least one hit not included in an adjacent window. Significance was assessed via p-values, adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR; q < 0.05). All analyses were implemented in R (v4.4.0)49.
