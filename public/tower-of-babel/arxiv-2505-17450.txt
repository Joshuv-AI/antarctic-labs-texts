Decoherence by black holes via holography
Shoichi Kawamoto, Da-Shin Lee, Chen-Pin Yeh

Abstract
In this note, we reexamine decoherence effects in quantum field theories with gravity duals. The thought experiment proposed in DSW_22 ; DSW_23 , which reveals novel decoherence patterns associated with black holes, is also manifest in the perspective of the boundary theory. In particular, we consider a moving mirror coupled to quantum critical theories characterized by a dynamical exponent z z that are dual to asymptotically Lifshitz geometries. The interference experiment takes place on the boundary, where a superposition of two spatially separated quantum states of a mirror is maintained for a finite time τ 0 \tau_{0} before recombination. We find that the interaction with a quantum field at finite temperature, arising from the presence of a Lifshitz black hole, leads to a constant decoherence rate. In contrast, for the zero-temperature case corresponding to pure Lifshitz spacetime, the decoherence rate vanishes in the large-time limit τ 0 → ∞ \tau_{0}\to\infty . Remarkably, in this zero-temperature regime, the decoherence exhibits a power-law decay at large τ 0 \tau_{0} as z → ∞ z\rightarrow\infty , a behavior reminiscent of the decoherence patterns seen in extremal black hole geometries. In addition, we investigate the decoherence of one particle in an EPR pair constructed holographically. Our results indicate that causality plays a crucial role in determining whether the entanglement leads to the suppression of decoherence in the other particle.



I
introduction

There has been a longstanding debate over whether black hole evaporation violates the principles of quantum mechanics. A central challenge lies in the lack of consensus, even among those who accept the validity of quantum mechanics, on how information is recovered in Hawking radiation. The potential loss of quantum information is often associated with decoherence. In this context, the black hole information problem can be viewed as persistent decoherence induced by the black hole on nearby quantum systems, thereby violating the unitarity of quantum evolution.
Quantum decoherence typically arises due to interactions with an environment. Recently, Danielson, Satishchandran, and Wald (DSW) proposed a thought experiment demonstrating that quantum superpositions maintained outside a Killing horizon will decohere at a constant rate
DSW_22
;
DSW_23
. Their argument primarily relies on causality. They showed that when long-range fields, such as electromagnetic or gravitational fields, are entangled with quantum superposition states, soft mode radiation can carry information across the horizon into the black hole, leading to a steady decoherence rate.
This decoherence effect, like Hawking radiation, appears to be related to the global structure of spacetime horizons. It can be analyzed using quantum field theory in curved spacetime, where the past state (in-vacuum) and the future state (out-vacuum) may differ
Gralla_24
. More recently, it has been shown that the DSW effect can also be understood locally as quantum decoherence in a thermal environment
Biggs_24
;
Chen_24
;
DSW_24
. These findings suggest that black holes can be modeled semiclassically as quantum systems in thermal equilibrium.
Nevertheless, it remains an intriguing question how the DSW effect manifests in a fully quantum-mechanical description of black holes. Extending beyond the semiclassical regime is challenging due to the limited tools available. In this short paper, we aim to investigate the DSW effect using the local method in a holographic framework, which is believed to offer a fully quantum description of black holes.

In parallel with the study of the DSW effect, we consider an interference experiment involving a particle (or a mirror) in a coherent superposition of two localized states. These states depart from the same initial point and later recombine after following two distinct, non-overlapping paths,
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. Similar interference experiments have been studied in
Ford_93
;
Ford_97
, where the influence of vacuum electromagnetic-field fluctuations on electron coherence have been analyzed. These studies showed that both the phase shift and decoherence effects could be expressed through double worldline integrals involving the commutator and anti-commutator of gauge potentials in the vacuum state, which are related to the retarded and Hadamard Green’s functions, respectively.
In the framework of open quantum systems
Breuer
, environment-induced effects on the system can be systematically described using the influence functional formalism developed by Feynman and Vernon
Feynman
, constructed from Green’s functions defined in the in-in formalism
SC
. One notable application of this approach is the analysis of quantum Brownian motion in a thermal bath
CAD
. The influence functional generally admits a decomposition into real and imaginary components: the real part, involving the Hadamard function, encodes decoherence, while the imaginary part, associated with the retarded Green’s function, corresponds to phase shifts
Hsiang_06
. These two effects are connected via the fluctuation-dissipation theorem.
In this note, we reexamine the decoherence of a moving mirror interacting with strongly coupled quantum critical fields using the influence functional derived from holographic duality
Yeh_15
;
mirror
. In the holographic setup, a particle or extended object (referred to as a mirror) is modeled by a string or brane propagating in an asymptotically Lifshitz geometry. The influence functional for the interference experiment, conducted at the boundary, is obtained from the on-shell action of the string or brane in the bulk. The environmental fields at zero and finite temperatures are dual to pure Lifshitz spacetimes and Lifshitz black holes, respectively. In the finite temperature case, we interpret the environmental decoherence as arising from the black hole in the gravitational dual. We find that the resulting decoherence pattern agrees with that predicted by the DSW effect.
At zero temperature, we also observe consistency with the DSW result in the extremal black hole limit when the Lifshitz scaling exponent is taken to infinity. Additionally, we investigate decoherence arising from Unruh effects in the holographic setting by considering accelerating string endpoints
Xiao_08
;
Yeh_22_1
. Here we find a constant decoherence rate, analogous to that observed in finite-temperature environments.

In the next section, we outline the setup of the interference experiment and identify the decoherence function and phase shift as derived from the influence functional.
In Section
III
, we present the calculation of the holographic influence functional and use it to analyze the resulting decoherence effect.
Section
IV
explores a holographic description of the Unruh effect and its implications for decoherence.
Finally, we summarize our findings and conclusions in Section
V
.

II
review of influence functional

In this section, we briefly review the influence functional formalism describing how environmental degrees of freedom affect a quantum particle. We also introduce phase shifts and decoherence in an interference experiment where the particle is in a superposition of two spatially separated trajectories
Hsiang_06
. This formalism can be naturally extended to holographic settings as demonstrated in
Son_09
;
Yeh_15
.

The environment-induced effects on the system (which is a particle with position variable
q
) can be captured by a linear coupling to an environment field
F
, with the full Lagrangian given by

L(q,F)=L_{q}[q]+L_{F}[F]+qF\,.

(1)

The evolution of the density matrix of the system-environment is obtained by the time evolution operator
U(t_{f},t_{i})
as

\rho(t_{f})=U(t_{f},t_{i})\,\rho(t_{i})\,U^{-1}(t_{f},t_{i})\,.

(2)

We consider the case in which the initial density matrix at time
t_{i}
can be factorized as

\rho(t_{i})=\rho_{q}(t_{i})\otimes\rho_{{F}}(t_{i})\,,

(3)

and the environment field
F
is in thermal equilibrium at temperature
T=1/\beta
with the density matrix

\rho_{{F}}(t_{i})

\rho_{F}(t_{i})=e^{-\beta H_{F}}\,,

(4)

where
H_{F}
is the Hamiltonian of the environment field. The zero-temperature limit corresponds to taking
T\rightarrow 0
.
The reduced density matrix for the system is obtained by tracing out the environmental degrees of freedom.
In the position basis, it can be expressed as

\displaystyle\langle q_{f}|\rho_{r}|\tilde{q}_{f}\rangle\equiv\rho_{r}({q}_{f},\tilde{{q}}_{f},t_{f})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\!d{q}_{1}\,d{q}_{2}\left[\int^{{q}_{f}}_{{q}_{1}}\!\!\mathcal{D}{q}^{+}\!\!\int^{\tilde{{q}}_{f}}_{{q}_{2}}\!\!\mathcal{D}{q}^{-}\;\exp\biggl\{i\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}dt\left(L_{q}[{q}^{+}]-L_{q}[{q}^{-}]\right)\biggr\}\right.

(5)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.\,\times\,\mathcal{F}[\,q^{+},q^{-}]\right]\rho_{q}({q}_{1},{q}_{2},t_{i})\,,

by using the so-called influence functional

\mathcal{F}[\,q^{+},q^{-}]=\exp\Big\{\mathcal{W}[\,q^{+},q^{-}]+i\,\Phi[\,q^{+},q^{-}]\Bigr\}\,.

(6)

Here, the real functionals
\mathcal{W}
and
\Phi
are given by

\displaystyle\Phi[\,q^{+},q^{-}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int\!dt\!\!\int\!dt^{\prime}\Bigl[\,q^{+}(t)-q^{-}(t^{\prime})\Bigr]G_{R}(t-t^{\prime})\Bigl[\,q^{+}(t)+q^{-}(t^{\prime})\Bigr]\,,

(7)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}[\,q^{+},q^{-}]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int\!dt\!\!\int\!dt^{\prime}\Bigl[\,q^{+}(t)-q^{-}(t^{\prime})\Bigr]G_{H}(t-t^{\prime})\Bigl[\,q^{+}(t)-q^{-}(t^{\prime})\Bigr]\,,

(8)

where the retarded Green’s function
G_{R}(t-t^{\prime})
and the Hadamard function
G_{H}(t-t^{\prime})
of the environmental field are defined, respectively, as

\displaystyle G_{R}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-i\,\theta(t-t^{\prime})\,\bigl<\left[{{F}}(t),{{F}}(t^{\prime})\right]\bigr>\,,

(9)

\displaystyle G_{H}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,\bigl<\left\{{{F}}(t),{{F}}(t^{\prime})\right\}\big>\,.

(10)

They obey the fluctuation-dissipation relation

G_{H}(\omega)=-\coth\bigg[\frac{\omega}{2T}\bigg]\operatorname{Im}G_{R}(\omega)\,

(11)

in the frequency space. At zero temperature
T\to 0
, the relation reduces to

G_{H}(\omega)=-\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)\operatorname{Im}G_{R}(\omega)\,.

(12)

We now apply the influence functional formalism outlined above to the interference experiment described below. Suppose that the initial state of the particle is a coherent superposition of two localized states with the initial density matrix given by

\displaystyle\rho_{q}(t_{i})=(|\psi_{1}(t_{i})\rangle+|\psi_{2}(t_{i})\rangle)(\langle\psi_{1}(t_{i})|+\langle\psi_{2}(t_{i})|)

(13)

where
|\psi_{1}(t)\rangle
and
|\psi_{2}(t)\rangle
represent wave packets propagating along the two non-overlapping paths
{C}_{1}
and
{C}_{2}
separately as shown in Fig.
1
. We assume that the wave packet is sharply peaked in its position and momentum and the effect of wavefunction spreading is negligible. Under this approximation, the leading contribution to decoherence can be obtained by evaluating the influence functional along prescribed classical trajectories. The diagonal components of the reduced density matrix then become

\rho_{r}({q}_{f},{q}_{f},t_{f})=\bigl|\psi_{1}({q}_{f},t_{f})\bigr|^{2}+\bigl|\psi_{2}({q}_{f},t_{f})\bigr|^{2}+2\,e^{\mathcal{W}[\,\bar{q}^{+},\bar{q}^{-}]}\;\operatorname{Re}\left\{e^{i\,\Phi[\,\bar{q}^{+},\bar{q}^{-}]}\psi_{1}^{\vphantom{*}}({q}_{f},t_{f})\,\psi_{2}^{*}({q}_{f},t_{f})\right\}\,,

(14)

where the
\mathcal{W}
and
\Phi
functionals are evaluated along the classical trajectories,
{C}_{1}=\bar{q}^{+}
and
{C}_{2}=\bar{q}^{-}
. The last term in Eq. (
14
) gives the interference effect;
\Phi
gives the phase shift in the interference pattern, while
\mathcal{W}
determines the strength of the interference. When
\mathcal{W}
is negative and large, the interference pattern will disappear. Thus, we refer to
\mathcal{W}
as the decoherence functional which characterizes the decoherence effect.

Here we consider the prescribed trajectories
\bar{q}^{\pm}=\pm\zeta(t)
for the localized states. The path function
\zeta(t)
is chosen to take the form,

\zeta(t)={\ell}_{0}\,e^{-\frac{t^{2}}{\tau_{0}^{2}}}\,,

(15)

where
2\ell_{0}
is the effective path separation and
2\tau_{0}
is the effective flight time. The decoherence functional from (
8
) is obtained as

\mathcal{W}=-\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\;G_{H}(\omega)\bigg|\int dt\,\zeta(t)\,e^{-i\omega t}\bigg|^{2}<0\,.

(16)

Since these two prescribed classical trajectories are symmetric with respect to the initial position, we have

and will not see any phase shift, i.e.,

Decoherence from Lifshitz spacetimes

In this section, we consider an

-dimensional moving mirror coupled to an environmental field of the theory of

where the scaling symmetry (

;

18

(19)

III.1
Zero Temperature

In the zero-temperature case, the probe brane (or string) has static solutions with one endpoint fixed at the boundary. The quantum fluctuation of the string endpoint (which is interpreted as the motion of a Brownian particle from the boundary point of view) is holographically described by small perturbations around the static configurations. The action of these perturbations up to the quadratic terms is given by
Yeh_15

\displaystyle S\simeq-\frac{T_{n+1}S_{n}}{2}\int dr\,dt\,\bigg(r^{z+n+3}\partial_{r}X\partial_{r}X-\frac{\partial_{t}X\partial_{t}X}{{r^{z-n-1}}}\bigg)\,,

(20)

where
S_{n}
is the volume of the brane, and

represents one of the transverse displacement modes of the brane around its static configuration, parameterized by the metric time and radius in (
18

Yeh_15

Son_09

31

;

(21)

is the Hankel function of the first kind,

G_{R}(\omega)={m(i\omega)^{2}+\gamma(-i\omega)^{\alpha}}+{\cal{O}}(\omega^{2}/r_{b}^{2z})\,,

(22)

(23)

and

\gamma=\frac{T_{n+1}S_{n}}{(2z)^{\alpha-1}}\frac{\Gamma(1-\frac{\alpha}{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{\alpha}{2})}\,.

(24)

Thus, the interaction with the environmental field not only gives a mass

(25)

In the small

G_{H}(\omega)=\frac{\pi\,T_{n+1}S_{n}}{(2z)^{\alpha-1}\,\Gamma^{2}(\frac{\alpha}{2})}\,\omega^{\alpha}+{\cal{O}}(\omega^{\alpha+2}/r_{b}^{z(\alpha+2)})\,.

(26)

Notice that the obtained retarded Green’s function and the Hadamard function obey the zero-temperature fluctuation-dissipation theorem (

Thus, in the small

\mathcal{W}\simeq-\frac{\pi\,T_{n+1}S_{n}\,\Gamma(\frac{1+\alpha}{2})}{(2)^{(1+\alpha)/2}\,\Gamma^{2}(\frac{\alpha}{2})}\,\frac{\ell_{0}^{2}}{(\tau_{0}z)^{\alpha-1}}+{\mathcal{O}}(1/r_{b}^{2z(\alpha+2)}\tau_{0}^{2(\alpha+2)})\,.

(27)

Since
\alpha>1

\tau_{0}\rightarrow\infty
, we have

. It implies that the decoherence of the system with the supraohmic dissipative dynamics can be vanishingly small for the adiabatic motion of the quantum states. This is consistent with the findings in

While in their case, the environment is the electromagnetic fluctuations (and gives

III.2
Finite Temperature

In this section, we consider the decoherence effect arising from a finite temperature environment that is dual to a Lifshitz black hole.
The background metric for a Lifshitz black hole in
(d+1)
dimensions is

\displaystyle S^{(T)}\simeq-\frac{T_{n+1}S_{n}}{2}\int dr\,dt\,\bigg(r^{z+n+3}f(r)\partial_{r}X\partial_{r}X-\frac{\partial_{t}X\partial_{t}X}{{r^{z-n-1}f(r)}}\bigg)\,,

The relevant Green’s functions of the environmental field, which couples to the mirror (or particle) in the interference experiment, can also be obtained using the holographic influence functional method
mirror

IV
decoherence due to Unruh effect

As demonstrated in
Chen_24

IV.1
decoherence of an accelerating charged particle

The dynamics of the charged particle with charge
Q
and trajectory
x^{\mu}
obeys the relativistic Abraham-Lorentz-Dirac (ALD) equation where the radiation reaction force term can be written using the proper time

f^{\mu}=\frac{Q^{2}}{6\pi}\left[\frac{d^{3}x^{\mu}}{d\tau^{3}}-\frac{dx^{\mu}}{d\tau}\left(\frac{d^{2}x^{\nu}}{d\tau^{2}}\frac{d^{2}x_{\nu}}{d\tau^{2}}\right)\right]\,.

(36)

Consider a small perturbation
\epsilon^{\mu}(\tau)
around the uniformly accelerating trajectory
x^{\mu}=z^{\mu}+\epsilon^{\mu}
where the unperturbed trajectory satisfies
\ddot{z}^{\mu}=a^{2}z^{\mu}
and
\epsilon^{\mu}z_{\mu}=0

f^{\mu}=\frac{Q^{2}}{6\pi}\left[\frac{d^{3}\epsilon^{\mu}}{d\tau^{3}}-a^{2}\frac{d\epsilon^{\mu}}{d\tau}\right]\,,

(37)

which leads to the damping coefficient in frequency space
\omega

\gamma=\frac{Q^{2}}{6\pi}(a^{2}\omega+\omega^{3})\,.

(38)

Thus, for a uniformly accelerating charge, the dissipative dynamics is ohmic and then gives rise to a constant decoherence rate as discussed earlier. However, for a static charge in a zero temperature environment, the damping effect becomes supraohmic and from (
27

for an adiabatic motion of the particle as
\tau_{0}\rightarrow\infty
. It is important to note that being in a non-inertial frame is crucial for the DSW effect. We can see this by the case of a static charge in a thermal environment. From finite temperature fluctuation-dissipation relation (
11

\tau_{0}^{-1}

V
Concluding remarks

In this note, we investigate the decoherence effects of a moving mirror (or particle) influenced by quantum critical theories with the dynamical exponent
z
using holography. The holographic dual is given by the Lifshitz geometry. We study the nonequilibrium motion of the mirror (or particle) by the holographic influence functional, whose real part involves the retarded Green’s function
G_{R}
, responsible for the phase shifts, while its imaginary part involves the Hadamard function
G_{H}
for decoherence. The
G_{R}
and the
G_{H}
obtained this way also obey the fluctuation-dissipation theorem.
We study the decoherence effect in the interference experiment, where the superposition of two mirror(or particle) states that hold spatially separated for a time scale
\tau_{0}
before being recombined. For the pure Lifshitz spacetime, which is dual to the zero temperature environment, the obtained retarded Green’s function gives supraohmic dissipation and it induces the decoherence functional that vanishes in large
\tau_{0}
for an arbitrary finite
z
. As
z\rightarrow\infty
, both of the Green’s functions
G_{R}
and
G_{H}
have a linear
\omega
dependence, and this results in the
\ln\tau_{0}
dependence in the decoherence influence functional. This can be compared with the DSW effect in the case of extremal black holes. For the Lifshitz black hole background, which is dual to the finite temperature environment, the dissipation dynamics of the mirror becomes ohmic, and this gives a constant decoherence rate. This is also consistent with the DSW effect in finite-temperature black holes. In this work, we also consider the decoherence of one of the EPR particles, which are built up through holography. The presence of the entangled particle gives the suppression in the decoherence for the one that is in the interference experiment, when two particles are in causal contact. When the particles in the EPR pair are out of causal contact, then the decoherence pattern is not so different from the case with just one particle.

The results in current work imply that the holographic description of black holes is consistent with the perspective that black holes can be described by ordinary quantum systems. Our results are also consistent with the semiclassical description of black holes in the studies of the DSW effect. This may not be so surprising, since we have used the large-
N
holographic dictionary here. In this respect, it will be interesting to study how the finite-
N
corrections change the decoherence patterns. Furthermore, the constant decoherence rate cannot continue indefinitely, if the system in the interference experiment has more degrees of freedom than those of the black hole. The question is how decoherence terminates when we go beyond the semiclassical description of black holes. We don’t know the answer yet, but the hint may come from our study of the decoherence in holographic EPR pairs, where the decoherence of a particle due to thermal environment is suppressed when it is in causal contact with the other entangled particle.
