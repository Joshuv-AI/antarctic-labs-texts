Basic Bronchoscopy Competence Achieved During a One-Day Workshop for a Multidisciplinary Critical Care Team: A simulation-based study
Authors: B Jayakrishnan, Saif Musabah Al-Mubaihsi, Hasan Al-Sayegh, Ahmed Zaher Al-Abri, Issa Saif AlQarshoubi, Jehan Al-Abri, Sami M Bennji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:Fibreoptic bronchoscopy has become a key procedure in critical care, transitioning from pulmonologists to intensivists. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of a one-day simulation-based training programme in acquiring basic bronchoscopy skills.Methods:A one-day workshop was conducted at Sultan Qaboos Comprehensive Cancer Care and Research Centre, University Medical City, Muscat, Oman, in October 2024 with didactic lectures, small group skill stations, virtual bronchoscopy simulations and hands-on training using manikins and a novel, cost-effective, 3D-printed airway model. The pre-course knowledge of the attendees and their gain in skill were assessed with a 50-question survey tool.Results:A total of 61 participants attended the workshop; 53 attendants completed the pre- and post-training survey of which 38 were medical doctors (MDs) and 15 were non-MDs (respiratory therapists and nurses) whose average ages were 41.3 ± 8.6 years and 34.3 ± 5.5 years, respectively. Prior experience with bronchoscopy was indicated by 65.8% of the MDs and 60% of the non-MDs. Among the MDs, the pre-test post-test average correct response improved from 53.5% to 85.4% (gain of 31.9 percentage points; P <0.001). Non-MDs improved in a similar way from 54.2% to 86.2% (32 percentage point increase; P <0.001). The maximum gains were in sampling (MDs = 46.5%, non-MDs = 48.9%) and critical care practices (MDs = 36.2%, non-MDs = 38.3%). The participants were highly satisfied with scores ranging from 89.5–100%.Conclusion:The one-day simulation-based bronchoscopy workshop significantly enhanced intensivists’ knowledge and practical skills. The manikin and the custom designed 3D-model were valuable tools in teaching bronchoscopy and furthering clinician confidence and competency. These results indicate the potential of brief, focused training in bronchoscopy in critical care, particularly utilising cost-effective, portable training tools in resource-limited areas.Keywords: Bronchoscopy, Competence, Medical Education, Training, Workshop

1. Introduction

Fibreoptic bronchoscopy (FOB) has become a routine procedure in critical care settings, gradually transitioning from the exclusive domain of pulmonologists to intensivists. The introduction of portable, single-use flexible bronchoscopes has significantly simplified the procedure, facilitating its adoption by intensivists in the intensive care unit (ICU).[1,2] However, this shift does not replace more complex procedures that are done by pulmonologists in dedicated bronchoscopy suites.[3] Detailed anatomical knowledge and advanced technical skills are necessary to perform a successful bronchoscopic procedure.[4] Due to the complexity of the procedure, which necessitates proficiency in both cognitive and motor skills, proper training and assessment are essential.[5] Various simulation-based modalities are used worldwide, ranging from virtual reality simulators and low-fidelity models to animal models, manikins and cadavers. Recently, low-cost, 3D custom-printed airway casts have emerged as practical alternatives to expensive commercial lung models.[6]

While bronchoscopy is an essential part of training for pulmonology and cardiothoracic surgery residents, the training pathway for this procedure is less structured for residents in intensive care and anaesthesia.[7] Despite this, they have a foundational advantage due to their familiarity with the anatomy of upper airways, sedation, mechanical ventilation and emergency interventions. Consequently, under the correct guidance, they can rapidly achieve competency in bronchoscopy techniques, such as identification of segments, lavage, removal of a foreign body, biopsy and endobronchial interventions. Short courses are now being conducted all over the world to train in basic bronchoscopy or advanced interventional procedures. Most include didactic, interactive lectures followed by small group learning skill stations.[7,8,9] Even a short introductory course such as a one-day workshop with simulators showed significant improvement in resident performance.[10]

This study used a pre-test/post-test model to assess the effectiveness of a one-day bronchoscopy course for the intensivists. The workshop was comprised of didactic and interactive lectures, followed by 4 hours of hands-on training using manikins and a novel cost-effective 3D-printed airway model. The pre-course knowledge of the attendees and their gain in skill were assessed with a 50-question survey tool as a part of the evaluation of the course.

2. Methods

The Head, Neck & Thoracic Program, along with the Medical Subspecialties unit, Sultan Qaboos Comprehensive Cancer Care and Research Centre (SQCCCRC), University Medical City, Muscat, Oman, conducted a workshop on ICU bronchoscopy on 2 separate days on the 14th and 16th of October 2024.

During the workshop, special emphasis was placed on the bronchoscopic and radiological identification of different lung segments. The faculty included consultants in pulmonology, anaesthesia and radiology, along with experienced endoscopy nurses and a senior cytology technologist. The techniques of suctioning, bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL), endobronchial biopsies, bronchoscopic intubation and sedation protocols were covered in the theoretical sessions and subsequently demonstrated using anatomical lung models. The lectures were followed by a 4-hour hands-on practical exercise [Supplementary Table 1]. The salient features of the workshop included the use of 5 manikins with anatomically accurate tracheobronchial segments as well as a novel 3D-printed tubular model specifically developed to practice lavage and biopsy training [Fig. 1,Fig. 2]. The placement of endobronchial blockers and bronchoscopy-assisted intubation were also demonstrated. Two brands of single-use flexible bronchoscopes were used during the workshop: the Ambu® aScope™ (Ambu A/S, Copenhagen, Denmark) and the Pentax® Medical ONE Pulmo™ (Hoya Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), each paired with the respective LCD monitor. Additionally, a reusable conventional bronchoscope (BF-P290, Olympus Corporation, Japan), along with its full stack and video display system, was made available for demonstration and familiarisation with standard equipment.

Participants also had access to all necessary accessories, they participated in a virtual bronchoscopy simulation online and viewed looping videos showcasing bronchoscopies performed at SQCCCRC to simulate a live experience.

3D-modelling is the process of constructing a digital representation of an object in 3 dimensions (width, height and depth) using specialised software. It is the development of a virtual form by the controlling points, edges and surfaces in a 3D space. The 3D model obtained can be viewed and oriented in any desired manner. This study utilised fused deposition modelling, the popular 3D printing technology using a thermoplastic filament polylactic acid (PLA), for creating objects layer by layer using a material extrusion 3D printer (P1S, Bambu Lab, Shenzhen, China) equipped with a 0.4 mm stainless steel nozzle, operated via Bambu Studio slicer software. The heated standard PLA filament (1.75 mm diameter, natural/white) was extruded through a nozzle moving along the X and the Y axes to form the layers with the build platform descending in the Z axis on completion of each layer. The printing parameters were as follows: layer height = 0.2 mm; infill density = 100%; nozzle temperature = 220°C; build plate temperature = 55°C; and print speed = standard preset (approximately 200 mm/s). These settings were selected to balance print quality, structural integrity and printing time, and are readily reproducible using default configurations on the specified printer. A non-anatomical tubular model was developed to facilitate instruction in bronchoscope manoeuvrability and procedures. The diameters and angulations of the model were adapted from commercially available training manikins.

A 50-question survey tool was designed as a Google Form (Google LLC, Mountain View, California, USA) and the delegates were asked to scan the QR code displayed on site. The pretest questionnaire containing 23 questions, in addition to the demographic information, which were completed during registration on the morning of the workshop. The same questions on the knowledge in the pre-course survey were repeated along with questions on their gain in knowledge, confidence and satisfaction in the post-workshop 27 item assessment form. The pretest questionnaire was piloted on the first 5 registrants in the morning, before the survey was opened. As the questionnaire was administered using Google Forms (Google LLC), modifications could be implemented as required; however, none were necessary, likely due to the clarity and relevance of the questions and the highly educated participant cohort.

A group of delegates who chose to stay beyond the scheduled workshop hours for additional practice were informally assessed for their newly acquired skills. To support continued learning and reinforce knowledge retention, all delegates were provided with a comprehensive set of course materials, including copies of all PowerPoint (Office 365, Microsoft Corp, Redmond, Washington, USA) lectures. Additionally, links were shared to a free bronchoscopy atlas, selected book chapters focused on ICU bronchoscopy and virtual bronchoscopy learning software. These resources were provided for the purpose of promoting post-course self-learning and facilitating the consolidation of the knowledge and skills obtained during the workshop.

Descriptive statistics were performed to summarise the characteristics of the study population. Medians and ranges were calculated for continuous variables; frequencies and percentages were reported for categorical variables. The McNemar test was used to evaluate changes in paired categorical variables. The exact McNemar test was used for all paired comparisons with 0 cell counts. The averages of correct answer responses were calculated and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to assess differences between pre- and post-training scores, both overall and within individual sections. All statistical analyses were performed using the R statistical software (R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). A P value of ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3. Results

A total of 61 participants attended the course, 32 on the first and 29 on the second day. There were 44 medical doctors (MD) and 17 respiratory therapists or nurses (non-MD). Of the attendants, 54 healthcare providers participated in the pre-training survey; finally, a total of 53 providers (38 MDs and 15 non-MDs) were included in the study (1 was excluded for not completing the post-training survey). Mean age of the MDs and non-MDs were 41.3 ± 8.6 years and 34.3 ± 5.5 years, respectively. The majority of attendees in both groups had more than 5 years of post-qualification experience and 60.0% of the non-MDs and 65.8% of the MDs had assisted in bronchoscopy procedures at least once [Table 1].

Post-training survey results demonstrated a significant improvement in participants’ knowledge of bronchoscopy and related interventions. Among MDs, the average percentage of correct responses increased from 53.5% before the workshop to 85.4% after, reflecting an overall gain of 31.9 percentage points (P <0.001). The greatest improvement was observed in the domains of sampling (46.5%), followed by critical care practices (36.2%), technique (25.8%) and anatomy (24.3%). Analysis of individual questions revealed consistent gains across all topics with the gain in anatomy ranging from 18.4 to 34.2%, technique from 5.3 to 39.4%, sampling from 39.5 to 55.2% and critical care practices from 18.4 to 50%. These changes were statistically significant (P <0.001 each) [Table 2].

4. Discussion

The one-day workshop on bronchoscopy showed considerable enhancement in knowledge and skills in the participants. This model offers a possible and effective method of enhancing procedural competency in critical care teams, allowing for safer and more effective airway management during care in the ICU. It is a notable accomplishment especially with a trend of the intensivists performing bronchoscopy in the ICU on their own. The indications for bronchoscopy in the ICU are numerous; in a recent study, microbiologic sampling of the respiratory tract (43.6%), secretion clearance (30.4%), bronchoscopy-guided percutaneous tracheostomy (11.7%), management of atelectasis (8.7%) and haemoptysis (5.4%) were the most common conditions.[11]

Procedural skills are defined as “the mental and motor activities required to execute a manual task”.[12] Peyton's teaching approach, consisting of the following 4 steps: demonstration, deconstruction, comprehension and performance, is an effective teaching approach for skill acquisition of procedural skills in health professions education.[13,14] Gagne's model considers the internal and external conditions of learning to create a practical, stepwise series of instructions for many topics, including teaching procedural skills.[12] The current workshop design was based on these evidence-based teaching methodologies. Simulation-based training (SBT) offers a patient-safe training setting, improved skill acquisition, decreased errors and the potential for repetitive practice.[15] A systematic review by Gerretsen et al. evaluated 14 studies on SBT for flexible bronchoscopy and confirmed its effectiveness in skill acquisition, although design variations in the studies hindered conclusive inferences.[16] Regardless, their conclusions support the current study's results.

Acquiring the practical skills, knowledge and experience needed to perform flexible bronchoscopy proficiently in the critical care setting can be a challenging task. A clear knowledge of airway anatomy is needed to perform a bronchoscopy procedure.[4] The main limitation is correct identification of segments in a branching maze of tubular structures where the segments (10 on the right and 8 on the left) look alike. Proper segment identification is essential for targeted interventions, particularly in cases of focal pulmonary infections. Some programmes provide didactic lectures with hands-on supervision whilst others incorporate simulation training with supervisors completing checklists at certain milestones of training.[8] The current program combined both approaches and the MD's knowledge of bronchial anatomy improved considerably with the post-workshop responses achieving a value of 65.8–100%.

From a practical viewpoint, each of the virtual reality simulators, low-fidelity models, animal models, manikin and human cadavers have advantages and disadvantages in terms of upfront costs, maintenance, availability, reusability and portability.[17] Some advantages of a wet lab simulation are the anatomical similarity and the feeling of a real bronchoscopy procedure, while ethical issues in the use of animals, cost and potential damage to the bronchoscopy equipment are the disadvantages. Low-fidelity models range from simple, non-anatomical labyrinths to more modern simulators. The non-anatomical labyrinth models like a ‘12-hole clock model’ help develop dexterity but lack anatomical representation.[18] 3D custom-printed airway casts are much less expensive than commercial models with almost equal efficiency.[6,19,20] Computer-based virtual reality simulators provide detailed imaging for orientation and anatomical training but cannot replicate procedures such as BAL, biopsy or foreign body removal. Given the availability of manikins for teaching anatomy, the current authors developed a non-anatomical tubular model using 3D printing to facilitate instruction in manoeuvrability and procedures such as BAL and biopsies. The advantages of this model include low design and printing costs (approximately USD 40), portability, durability, minimal maintenance requirements, partial replication of airway structures with satisfactory visual representation and the capability to perform repeated procedures, including the instillation of saline. To facilitate practice of biopsy techniques, small pieces of red strawberries were placed inside the tubes and were replaced often. Strawberries were selected because they can be readily cut using biopsy forceps, mimicking the tactile feeling of tissue sampling during an actual biopsy, while their vivid red appearance simulates a bleeding lesion. Participants reported that the model was valuable for enhancing scope manoeuvrability skills and procedural confidence.

Unlike pulmonology or cardiothoracic training, intensive care and anaesthesia residency programmes often lack structured bronchoscopy training.[7] However, physicians from these specialties possess baseline competencies in airway management, sedation and mechanical ventilation, which facilitates faster upskilling with focused training. Solidoro et al. proposed a structured framework for ICU-based bronchoscopy training, combining theoretical and practical modules using a mix of simulators and biological models.[21] The current one-day workshop followed a similar structure and yielded notable improvements in knowledge, up to 55.2% gain for MDs and up to 80% for non-MDs with post-workshop responses reaching 100% in many instances in the areas of anatomical knowledge, technique, sampling and ICU related issues. The participants also gained skill in manoeuvring the airways and doing BAL or simple biopsy. Interestingly, these gains were consistent across different professional backgrounds, including both MDs and non-MDs. In a broader interdisciplinary role for the procedure, emerging evidence also supports physiotherapy-led bronchoscopy for airway clearance in select ICU settings such as with sedated patients when other physiotherapy interventions may not be appropriate or successful.[22]

In the future, the integration of artificial intelligence into SBT platforms is anticipated to further enhance learning outcomes.[23,24] Experts now recommend that bronchoscopy trainees undergo simulator-based training and pass objective assessments before clinical practice.[9,25] Moreover, bronchoscopy manipulation skills may decay over time, necessitating periodic practice to maintain an acceptable level of proficiency. Frequently, insufficient exposure to bronchoscopy stems from clinical factors such as limited time availability, a scarcity of patients in ICUs who require bronchoscopy and hesitance due to lack of experience.[7] Common barriers to ongoing training include cost, limited access to simulators, time constraints and the absence of structured programmes. Accruing low-cost, portable models—such as the 3D-printed version used in the current workshop—can help overcome these barriers and provide a sustainable solution for skill retention. It is essential to note, that simulation-based training can provide fundamental bronchoscopy skills; however, it cannot replace learning bronchoscopy on real patients under supervision. In clinical practice, patients may cough, have secretions that obscure the endobronchial view, or develop complications such as desaturation, arrhythmias or biopsy-induced bleeding, with inter-individual variability to sedation further contributing to procedural complexity.

This study is subject to some limitations. First, the design of the study assessment was limited, as the primary objective was to conduct the training and workshop rather than a rigorous evaluation. Second, selection bias may have influenced the post-workshop skill assessment as it was conducted informally among a highly motivated subgroup who chose to remain after the session. Third, while the training model was effective for teaching airway manoeuvring, lavage and biopsy techniques, a more anatomically accurate representation of tracheobronchial segments could have heightened its realism and educational value. Fourth, though overall knowledge improved, some questions showed a slight reduction in corresponding responses following training. These differences might stem from the misinterpretation of newly introduced concepts, varying levels of confidence or variability in the small sample size. Finally, even though study materials were provided for self-paced study, long-term follow-up was not conducted to check skill retention or initiation of similar service by the participants in their workplace.

5. Conclusion

A one-day, simulation-based bronchoscopy workshop resulted in significant improvements in theoretical knowledge and technical competency in intensivists. Both manikins and the custom-designed 3D airway model were instrumental in teaching bronchoscopy techniques effectively. The increasing popularity of single-use flexible bronchoscopes in ICUs—due to their portability, economic value, lower infection risk and convenience in handling—supports the growing practice of intensivists performing bronchoscopy. Short, concise training courses can enhance clinician confidence and skill in basic bronchoscopy and facilitate safer and more effective patient care in the ICU. Moreover, cost-effective and portable training equipment, such as the current study's 3D model, presents a feasible way to enhance and sustain bronchoscopy skills, especially in resource-limited areas.
