Stable Spectral-Volume Methods
Lena Schadt

Abstract




Abstract

A novel approach for the stabilization of the Spectral-Volume (SV) method based on Dafermos’ entropy rate criterion is presented. The method is an adaption of an already existing approach for the stabilization of the Discontinuous-Galerkin (DG) method. It employs the same estimates for the maximal possible entropy dissipation rate as the DG version. However, a new way to compute the discrete conservative filter had to be derived due to the differences of the underlying schemes. The resulting modified SV scheme even satisfies the entropy inequality. Tests are carried out for Burgers’ equation and for the Euler equations of gas dynamics.

Introduction

The partial differential equations we aim to solve numerically are one-dimensional hyperbolic systems of conservation laws

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u\left(x,t\right)+\partial_{x}f\left(u\left(x,t\right)\right)

\displaystyle=0,\quad

\displaystyle\left(x,t\right)\in\Omega\times\left[0,\infty\right[

(1.1)

\displaystyle u\left(x,0\right)

\displaystyle=u_{0}\left(x\right),\quad

\displaystyle x\in\Omega

with
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}
. The function
u:\Omega\times\left[0,\infty\right[\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the unknown vector of state variables and
f:\mathbb{R}^{m}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a given flux function. A function
u
is called a
classical solution
of (
1.1
) if
u
is a
C^{1}
-function that satisfies (
1.1
) pointwise. Unfortunately, it is well known that in many cases classical solutions to conservation laws break down. Consequently, one introduces the concept of weak solutions
[
8
,
15
]
.

Let
D=\mathbb{R}\times\left[0,\infty\right[
, and
C_{0}^{\infty}\left(D\right)
denote the space of all smooth functions with compact support. A function
\varphi
is termed a testfunction if
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}\left(D\right)
. For brevity, we denote
u=u\left(x,t\right)
and
\varphi=\varphi\left(x,t\right)
. Multiplying (
1.1
) by
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}\left(D\right)
and integrating over
D
yields

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi\partial_{t}u+\varphi\partial_{x}f\left(u\right)\,dxdt=0.

(1.2)

By performing an integration by parts and considering the compact support of
\varphi
, we obtain

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}u\partial_{t}\varphi+f\left(u\right)\partial_{x}\varphi\,dxdt=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi\left(x,0\right)u\left(x,0\right)\,dx.

(1.3)

This formulation is valid even if
u\in L^{\infty}_{loc}\left(D\right)
only. A function u is termed a weak solution of (
1.1
) if (
1.3
) holds for all
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}\left(D\right)
. We say that
u
satisfies (
1.1
) in the weak sense
[
15
]
.

Unfortunately, weak solutions are not unique [6, example 2.3]. Therefore, we will introduce entropy
conditions as an additional constraint on the solution. These conditions should enable us to pick out the physically relevant weak solutions.

A convex function
U:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is an entropy, if there exists a function
F:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, called entropy flux, such that

\displaystyle U^{\prime}\left(u\right)f^{\prime}\left(u\right)=F^{\prime}\left(u\right)

(1.4)

holds
[
15
]
. An admissable pair of entropy and entropy flux will be refered to as entropy-entropy flux pair
\left(U,F\right)
. Note that
U^{\prime}=\left(\nabla U\right)^{T}
,
F^{\prime}=\left(\nabla F\right)^{T}
and
f^{\prime}
denotes the jacobian matrix of
f
.

If a scalar conservation law is considered as with the linear advection equation, every convex function
U:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
could be selected as entropy. A corresponding entropy flux would then be given by
[
15
]

F\left(u\right)=\int U^{\prime}\left(u\right)f^{\prime}\left(u\right)du.

(1.5)

Note further that equation (
1.4
) is overdetermined if
m>2
. Hence, it is possible that no entropy-entropy flux pair exists at all. Fortunately, in the case of the Euler equations of gasdynamics suitable entropy- entropy flux pairs are known
[
9
,
19
]
.

If
u
is a classical solution of (
1.1
) it satisfies another conservation law as well. Multiplying (
1.4
) by
\partial_{x}u
and using the chain rule we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}U\left(u\right)+\partial_{x}F\left(u\right)=0

(1.6)

In general, one can show that for an entropy-entropy flux pair
\left(U,F\right)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}U\left(u\right)+\partial_{x}F\left(u\right)\leq 0

(1.7)

holds in the weak sense
[
15
]
. For
m=1
the additional condition given by equation (
1.7
) enforces the uniqueness of the solution to (
1.1
)
[
14
]
, if it holds for a suitable family of entropies.
For
m\geq 2
on the other hand, equation (
1.7
) yields no guaranty for a unique solution
[
6
]
. Therefore, we use a different entropy criterion.

Let
u
be a weak solution of (
1.1
) and
U
an admissable entropy. The total entropy of
u
at time
t
is then defined as:

\displaystyle E_{u}\left(t\right)=\int U\left(u\left(x,t\right)\right)\,dx.

(1.8)

Dafermos’ entropy rate criterion
[
4
]
states that the total entropy of the relevant weak solution
\tilde{u}
should reduce faster than the total entropy of any other existing weak solution
u
:

\displaystyle\forall t>0:\quad\frac{dE_{\tilde{u}}}{dt}\leq\frac{dE_{u}}{dt}.

(1.9)

See
[
3
,
2
,
5
]
for theoretical examples where Dafermos’ criterion is able to reduce the amount of admissible solution. We will use this criterion in chapter
2
as basis for the stabilization approach.

1.1
Short Introduction to the FV Method

We start by a brief introduction to the Finite-Volume (FV) method
[
8
]
as a fundamental conceptṪhe Spectral-Volume method is essentially based on this simpler and better known method.

First, the domain
\Omega=\left[a,b\right]
is divided into
N
volumes
V_{i}
called cells

\left[a,b\right]=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}V_{i},\quad V_{i}=\left[x_{i-1/2},x_{i+1/2}\right],\quad i=1,...,N.

(1.10)

To keep things simple, we assume an equidistant partition and denote the length of one cell by
h=x_{i+1/2}-x_{i-1/2},\,i=1,...,N
. The center of each cell is given by

x_{i}=\frac{x_{i-1/2}+x_{i+1/2}}{2},\quad i=1,...,N,

(1.11)

and the cell average of
u
in the
i
-th volume will be denoted by an upper bar

\bar{u}_{i}\left(t\right)=\frac{1}{h}\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}u\left(x,t\right)dx.

(1.12)

The FV method can be derived from an integration of equation (
1.1
) over one volume
V_{i}

\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}\partial_{t}u\left(x,t\right)+\partial_{x}f\left(u\left(x,t\right)\right)dx=0.

(1.13)

If we assume
u
to be sufficiently smooth, the fundamental theorem of calculus yields

h\cdot\frac{d\bar{u}_{i}}{dt}\left(t\right)+\left(f_{i+1/2}\left(t\right)-f_{i-1/2}\left(t\right)\right)=0,

(1.14)

where

f_{i+1/2}\left(t\right)=f\left(u\left(x_{i+1/2},t\right)\right).

(1.15)

Equation (
1.14
) is equivalent to

\frac{d\bar{u}_{i}}{dt}\left(t\right)=\frac{1}{h}\left(f_{i-1/2}\left(t\right)-f_{i+1/2}\left(t\right)\right).

(1.16)

and specifies the time evolution of the cell averages in all volumes. Intuitively equation (
1.16
) states that the amount of a conserved quantity within a volume
V_{i}
only changes by its flux across the boundary. Unfortunately, the equation can’t be integrated directly. That is because we have no access to nodal values of
u
at volume boundaries and are thus unable to evaluate the flux at these points. Instead, we approximate the flux at volume boundaries by a numerical flux
f^{*}
, that depends on approximations of the left-hand and right-hand limits
u_{l}
and
u_{r}
of
u
at a given volume boundary. The most simple way to approximate the one-sided limits is to use the cell averages
u_{l}=\bar{u}_{i}
and
u_{r}=\bar{u}_{i+1}
of the adjacent volumes directly. To get better approximations of
u_{l}
and
u_{r}
often a reconstruction step is added. That implies that for every step in time
u
is approximated in each volume by a polynomial of low degree. The coefficients of the polynomial are determined by the cell averages of the surrounding volumes. The one-sided limits
u_{l}
and
u_{r}
are then calculated by an evaluation of the reconstruction polynomials at the volume boundaries.

1.2
Basic Idea of the SV Method

As with the FV method, the fundamental concept behind Spectral-Volume methods
[
24
]
is to partition the spatial domain into so-called spectral volumes (SV). However, for the SV method each spectral volume is further subdivided into control volumes (CV). Cell-averaged data from these control volumes is used to reconstruct a high-order polynomial approximation within each spectral volume. In contrast to the FV method, the reconstruction polynomial remains continuous throughout the entire spectral volume. Consequently, we can use the analytical flux function within each spectral volume. Only at the SV boundaries do we need to employ numerical fluxes to address the arising Riemann problems.

In this chapter, all vectors of cell averages and nodal values are presented in bold typeface, as are all matrices. Cell averages are denoted by an upper bar, as in
\bar{u}
. Generally, we will use
i
as an index for the SVs and
j
as an index for the CVs. Besides, a superscript star will indicate the numerical flux and the numerical entropy flux, as in
f^{*}
and
F^{*}
.

1.3
Spatial Grid

We will now look at the concept of Spectral-Volume methods
[
24
]
in more detail. As with the FV method, we opt for a partition
\{x_{i+1/2}\}_{i=0}^{N}
of the domain
\Omega=\left[a,b\right]
to divide it into N spectral volumes
S_{i}
, where
x_{1/2}=a
and
x_{N+1/2}=b

\left[a,b\right]=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}S_{i},\quad S_{i}=\left[x_{i-1/2},x_{i+1/2}\right],\quad i=1,...,N.

(1.17)

In all numerical tests, we choose an equidistant partition, although this selection is not mandatory. However, as there are no specific requirements for the partition, we will proceed with the special case of equidistant spectral volumes for simplicity. Let
h=x_{i+1/2}-x_{i-1/2},\,i=1,...,N
represent the length of one spectral volume and

x_{i}=\frac{x_{i-1/2}+x_{i+1/2}}{2},\quad i=1,...,N

(1.18)

be the center of each SV. The subsequent step involves determining the number of control volumes into which each spectral volume will be subdivided. This decision influences the numerical order of accuracy in space, as we utilize the cell-averages of all control volumes within one spectral volume for the reconstruction. For a desired accuracy of order k, each spectral volume
S_{i}
should be divided into
k
control volumes using a second partition
\{x_{i,j+1/2}\}_{j=0}^{k}
with
x_{i,1/2}=x_{i-1/2}
and
x_{i,k+1/2}=x_{i+1/2}
. That is because the reconstruction polynomial within each SV will be of order
k-1
if
k
control volumes are selected. The
j
th CV of
S_{i}
will be denoted by

C_{i,j}=\left[x_{i,j-1/2},x_{i,j+1/2}\right],\quad i=1,...,N,\quad j=1,...,k

(1.19)

and
h_{j}=x_{i,j+1/2}-x_{i,j-1/2},\,i=1,...,N
will be the length of the jth control volume. A visualization of the construction of the grid is depictured in figure
1
.

Figure 1
:
Partition into spectral volumes and further subdivision into control volumes.

In contrast to the partition of
\left[a,b\right]
into spectral volumes, we aim to employ a non-equidistant partition to subdivide each spectral volume into control volumes. This choice is motivated by the potential for the reconstruction polynomial to exhibit strong oscillations when equal length control volumes are utilized. This phenomenon is well-known in polynomial interpolation on equidistant grid points and is mitigated by adopting a grid that becomes denser towards the edges. Therefore, in all numerical tests, Gauss-Lobatto points
[
11
,
7
]
are used for the subdivision of the SVs into CVs.

Using a linear transformation, we obtain the partition
\{x_{i,j+1/2}\}_{j=0}^{k}
from the Gauss-Lobatto points
\tilde{x}_{j+1/2}
by

\displaystyle x_{i,j+1/2}=x_{i}+\frac{h\cdot\tilde{x}_{j+1/2}}{2},\quad i=1,...N,\quad j=0,...,k,

(1.20)

where
h
was defined as the length of one SV.

1.4
Polynomial Reconstruction

Now that we’ve established the grid for our computations, let’s delve into the core of the spectral volume method—polynomial reconstruction.
At the onset of each numerical test, the initial condition
u\left(x,0\right)=u_{0}\left(x\right)
needs to be transformed into a cell-averaged initial vector
\mathbf{\bar{u}}
with components

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{h_{j}}\int_{x_{i,j-1/2}}^{x_{i,j+1/2}}u_{0}\left(x\right)dx,\quad i=1,...,N,\quad j=1,...,k.

(1.21)

The integral can be approximated numerically, for exammple, using Gaussian quadrature
[
12
]
. Further, the components of
\mathbf{\bar{u}}
should be aranged in a SV-wise order:

\displaystyle\mathbf{\bar{u}}=\left(\mathbf{\bar{u}_{1}},...,\mathbf{\bar{u}_{N}}\right)=\left(\bar{u}_{1,1},\bar{u}_{1,2},...,\bar{u}_{1,k},\bar{u}_{2,1},...,\bar{u}_{N,k}\right)

(1.22)

Subsequently, we proceed by calculating the reconstruction polynomials from the cell-averaged inital condition. The reconstruction is repetitive in every time step. Hence, we consider the reconstruction problem for the SV method in general: Given the cell-averaged state variables for all the control volumes in one spectral volume
S_{i}
, construct a polynomial
p_{i}\left(x\right)
of degree at most
k-1
within
S_{i}
such that it is a smooth
k
th order accurate approximation to
u\left(x\right)
inside
S_{i}
:

\displaystyle p_{i}\left(x\right)=u\left(x\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(h^{k}\right),\quad i=1,...,N.

(1.23)

Once we have the reconstruction polynomials, they can be employed to calculate approximations of
u\left(x\right)
at the CV boundaries.

u_{i,j+1/2}:=p_{i}\left(x_{i,j+1/2}\right)=u\left(x_{i,j+1/2}\right)+\mathcal{O}\left(h^{k}\right),\quad j=0,...,k,\quad i=1,...,N

(1.24)

It is important to note, that we obtain two different values for
u
at the SV boundaries, because two different reconstruction polynomials intersect there.

The reconstruction problem has already been solved
[
1
, Problem 2.1]
. In problem 2.1 it is shown that for
i=1,...,N
, there exists a unique polynomial
p_{i}\left(x\right)
of degree at most
k-1
in
S_{i}
, whose cell averages in every control volume coincide with the given cell averages
\bar{u}_{i,j}

\bar{u}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{h_{j}}\int_{x_{i,j-1/2}}^{x_{i,j+1/2}}p_{i}\left(x\right)dx,\quad j=1,...,k,\quad i=1,...,N.

(1.25)

Hence this reconstruction aligns with the conservation law, as the averaged integral of the reconstruction over each control volume reproduces the cell averages.

We will now outline a simple way to construct
p_{i}\left(x\right)
numerically. The occuring matrices should have a low condition number. For this purpose, we will use the reliable Legendre polynomials
[
12
]
for the reconstruction process. We express every polynomial
p_{i}\left(x\right)
as a combination of the first
k
Legendre polynomials
L_{m},\,m=0,...,k-1
, using the inverse linear transformation of (
1.20
) to bring each spectral volume back into the interval
\left[-1,1\right]

\displaystyle p_{i}\left(x\right)=l_{i,0}\cdot L_{0}\left(\frac{2\left(x-x_{i}\right)}{h}\right)+...+l_{i,k-1}\cdot L_{k-1}\left(\frac{2\left(x-x_{i}\right)}{h}\right),

(1.26)

\displaystyle\quad x\in S_{i},\quad i=1,...,N.

Hence,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h_{j}}\int_{x_{i,j-1/2}}^{x_{i,j+1/2}}p_{i}\left(x\right)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{j}}\int_{x_{i,j-1/2}}^{x_{i,j+1/2}}\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}l_{i,m}\cdot L_{m}\left(\frac{2\left(x-x_{i}\right)}{h}\right)dx

(1.27)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}l_{i,m}\cdot\frac{1}{h_{j}}\int_{x_{i,j-1/2}}^{x_{i,j+1/2}}L_{m}\left(\frac{2\left(x-x_{i}\right)}{h}\right)dx.

The substitution
s=\frac{2\left(x-x_{i}\right)}{h}
yields

\displaystyle\bar{u}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{h_{j}}\int_{x_{i,j-1/2}}^{x_{i,j+1/2}}p_{i}\left(x\right)dx=\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}l_{i,m}\cdot\frac{1}{\tilde{h}_{j}}\int_{\tilde{x}_{j-1/2}}^{\tilde{x}_{j+1/2}}L_{m}\left(x\right)dx

(1.28)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{x}_{j+1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\left(x_{i,j+1/2}-x_{i}\right)}{h},

(1.29)

\displaystyle\tilde{h}_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{h_{j}}{h}=\frac{2\left(x_{i,j+1/2}-x_{i,j-1/2}\right)}{h}=\tilde{x}_{j+1/2}-\tilde{x}_{j-1/2}

(1.30)

Examining (
1.28
) for every
p_{i}
, we obtain a system of linear equations to calculate the coefficients
l_{i,m},\,m=0,...,k-1
. This system can be expressed using a
k\times k
matrix
\mathbf{M}
, with entries defined as follows:

M_{j,m}=\frac{1}{\tilde{h}_{j}}\int_{\tilde{x}_{j-1/2}}^{\tilde{x}_{j+1/2}}L_{m}\left(x\right)dx,\quad j=1,...,k,\quad m=0,...,k-1.

(1.31)

The resulting linear system reads:

\mathbf{\bar{u}_{i}}=\mathbf{M}\cdot\mathbf{l_{i}}\quad\Leftrightarrow\quad\mathbf{l_{i}}=\mathbf{M^{-1}}\cdot\mathbf{\bar{u}_{i}},

(1.32)

where for fixed
i

\mathbf{\bar{u}_{i}}
is the vector of the
(\bar{u}_{i,j})_{j=1}^{k}
and
\mathbf{l_{i}}
is the vector of the coefficients
(l_{i,m})_{m=0}^{k-1}
. To save computational cost, we calculate
\mathbf{M^{-1}}
once and use it for every time step. It is worth noting that
\mathbf{M}
has to be non-singular, as the reconstruction polynomial
p_{i}\left(x\right)
is unique. Ultimately, we need to compute the approximate solution of
u\left(x\right)
at each CV boundary to update the state variable at the next time level:

\displaystyle u_{i,j+1/2}

\displaystyle=p_{i}\left(x_{i,j+1/2}\right)=\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}l_{i,m}\cdot L_{m}\left(\frac{2\left(x_{i,j+1/2}-x_{i}\right)}{h}\right)

(1.33)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{k-1}l_{i,m}\cdot L_{m}\left(\tilde{x}_{j+1/2}\right).

Let
\mathbf{u_{i}}
be the vector of the reconstructed values
u_{i,j+1/2}
. Equation (
1.33
) can be written in a compact matrix vector form:

\mathbf{u_{i}}=\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{l_{i}},\quad A_{j,m}=L_{m}\left(\tilde{x}_{j+1/2}\right),\quad j=0,...,k,\quad m=0,...,k-1.

(1.34)

Altogether, the approximate nodal value at the CV boundaries is given by

\mathbf{u_{i}}=\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{l_{i}}=\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{M^{-1}}\cdot\mathbf{\bar{u}_{i}}.

(1.35)

Defining the reconstruction matrix as:

\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{M^{-1}}

(1.36)

(
1.35
) yields:

\mathbf{u_{i}}=\mathbf{C}\cdot\mathbf{\bar{u}_{i}}.

(1.37)

The reconstruction process is illustrated stepwise in figure
2
.

Figure 2
:
Polynomial reconstruction:
(a) Cell averages,
(b) Reconstruction polynomial,
(c) Reconstructed boundary values.

One can see the reconstruction within one spectral volume that is divided into four CVs. Initially cell averages are given for all four control volumes. In a first step the coefficients of the reconstruction polynomial are calculated via a multiplication of these cell averages by
\mathbf{M^{-1}}
. Subsequently, the reconstruction polynomial is evaluated at all CV boundaries via a multiplication of the vector of coefficients by the matrix
\mathbf{A}
. In practice, the two steps are of course combined in one multiplication of the given cell averages by the reconstruction matrix
\mathbf{C}
.

1.5
The fundamental SV scheme

The fundamental spectral volume scheme presented in this section aims to provide a semi-discrete version of the given problem, allowing for numerical time integration. Analogue to the derivation of the FV method we start by integrating our differential equation (
1.1
) over one control volume
C_{i,j}
:

\displaystyle\int_{x_{i,j-1/2}}^{x_{i,j+1/2}}\partial_{t}u\left(x,t\right)+\partial_{x}f\left(u\left(x,t\right)\right)dx=0.

(1.38)

If
u
is sufficiently smooth, the fundamental theorem of calculus yields:

\displaystyle h_{j}\cdot\frac{d\bar{u}_{i,j}}{dt}\left(t\right)+\left(f_{i,j+1/2}\left(t\right)-f_{i,j-1/2}\left(t\right)\right)=0,

(1.39)

where

\displaystyle f_{i,j+1/2}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=f\left(u\left(x_{i,j+1/2},t\right)\right)\quad\text{and}

(1.40)

\displaystyle\frac{d\bar{u}_{i,j}}{dt}\left(t\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h_{j}}\cdot\frac{d}{dt}\int_{x_{i,j-1/2}}^{x_{i,j+1/2}}u\left(x,t\right)dx.

(1.41)

Equation (
1.39
) is equivalent to

\frac{d\bar{u}_{i,j}}{dt}\left(t\right)=\frac{1}{h_{j}}\left(f_{i,j-1/2}\left(t\right)-f_{i,j+1/2}\left(t\right)\right).

(1.42)

Like equation (
1.16
) of the FV method, this equation can’t be integrated directly, because we have no access to nodal values of
u
at SV boundaries. Therefore, we introduce a numerical flux
f^{*}
that approximates the flux at all cell volume boundaries

\displaystyle f_{i,j+1/2}^{*}\approx f(u(x_{i,j+1/2},t)).

(1.43)

The concrete definition of
f^{*}
remains to be determined. As mentioned in the last section, we can use the analytical flux at the interior CV boundaries of a SV, due to the continuity of the reconstruction within every SV, i.e.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} f_{i,j+1/2}^{*}=f\left(u_{i,j+1/2}\right),\quad j=1,...,k-1.\end{aligned}

(1.44)

At the SV boundaries, on the other hand, we obtain two reconstructed values of the state variable. For example, at
x_{i+1/2}
the SV
S_{i}
adjoins
S_{i+1}
. Hence one value
u_{i,k+1/2}
is due to the reconstruction in
S_{i}
and another value
u_{i+1,1/2}
due to the reconstruction in
S_{i+1}
. In all numerical tests the local Lax-Friedrichs flux is used to calculate the numerical flux at SV boundaries. It is a simplification of the HLL flux introduced by Harten, Lax and van Leer
[
10
]
.

\displaystyle f^{HLL}\left(u_{l},u_{r}\right)=\begin{cases}f\left(u_{l}\right),&\quad a_{l}<0\\
-\frac{a_{l}}{a_{r}-a_{l}}\cdot f\left(u_{r}\right)+\frac{a_{r}}{a_{r}-a_{l}}\cdot f\left(u_{l}\right)+\frac{a_{l}\cdot a_{r}}{a_{r}-a_{l}}\left(u_{r}-u_{l}\right),&\quad a_{l}<0<a_{r}\\
f\left(u_{r}\right),&\quad a_{r}>0\end{cases}

(1.45)

where
a_{l}
and
a_{r}
are lower and upper bounds for the smallest and largest signal speeds respectively. The special choice of
c_{max}=max\{|a_{l}|,|a_{r}|\}
and afterwards setting
a_{r}=c_{max},a_{l}=-c_{max}
leads to the simpler local Lax-Friedrichs flux:

\displaystyle f^{LLF}\left(u_{l},u_{r}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\left(f\left(u_{l}\right)+f\left(u_{r}\right)\right)-c_{max}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{r}-u_{l}\right).

(1.46)

To gain an intuitive understanding of the numerical flux, see figure
3
.

2
Entropy Stabilization

In this chapter, we provide an overview of an entropy rate stabilization approach for the Discontinuous Galerkin (DG) method as presented by Klein
[
13
]
and demonstrate its adaption to the Spectral-Volume scheme.
After grasping the basic idea of the stabilization method in section
2.1
, we start by an estimate for the highest possible entropy dissipation speed in section
2.2
. In the following chapter a direction for the dissipation is constructed in a physically motivated manner based on the heat equation. The correction size for the required entropy dissipation will be calculated in section
2.4
. Finally, in section
2.5
, we present the stabilized SV scheme. During this chapter, we exclusively use cell averages. Hence, we no longer employ the upper bar introduced in the previous chapter. Still all vectors of cell averages are presented in bold typeface, as are all matrices.

2.1
Basic Idea

As we use cell averages for our spectral volume method, we will in general approximate an integral over
S_{i}
by

\int_{S_{i}}g\left(u\right)\,dx\approx\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}g\left(u_{i,j}\right),

(2.1)

where
g:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a given function, for example, an entropy
U
. Similarly, a discretisation of the inner product will be given by

\int_{S_{i}}f\left(u\right)g\left(u\right)\,dx\approx\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}g\left(u_{i,j}\right)f\left(u_{i,j}\right)=:\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{S_{i}}.

(2.2)

Thus, for a numerical approximation of the total entropy in one SV
S_{i}
at time
t
, we obtain

E_{u,i}\left(t\right)=\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}U\left(u\left(x,t\right)\right)\,dx\approx\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}U\left(u_{i,j}\left(t\right)\right).

(2.3)

Note, that the discretizations for the integral and scalar product on one SV are not exact. This can be seen as follows:

The approximations (
2.1
) and (
2.2
) are obviously exact if
u=const
. On the other hand if we consider
u
to be linear, for example
u=x
and
g\left(u\right)=u^{2}
, the approximation is not exact as can be shown by a simple calculation:

\displaystyle\int_{S_{i}}g\left(u\right)\,dx=\int_{x_{i-1/2}}^{x_{i+1/2}}x^{2}\,dx=\frac{1}{3}\left(x_{i+1/2}^{3}-x_{i-1/2}^{3}\right)

(2.4)

but

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}g\left(u_{i,j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(x_{i,j+1/2}-x_{i,j-1/2}\right)\left(\frac{x_{i,j-1/2}+x_{i,j+1/2}}{2}\right)^{2}.

(2.5)

For convenience, we assume
x_{i-1/2}=-1,\,x_{i+1/2}=1
and four equidistant control volumes. Then

\displaystyle\int_{S_{i}}g\left(u\right)\,dx=\frac{1}{3}\left(x_{i+1/2}^{3}-x_{i-1/2}^{3}\right)=\frac{2}{3}\neq 0.625=\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}g\left(u_{i,j}\right).

(2.6)

Now, we have all necessary definitions to start our exploration of the stabilization method. The procedure is as follows:

1.

Calculate the time derivatives
\frac{du_{i,j}}{dt}
of all cell averages.

2.

Estimate the highest possible entropy dissipation speed
\sigma_{i+1/2}
at the interface of
S_{i}
and
S_{i+1}
.

3.

Compute a correction direction
v_{i}
.

4.

Determine the necessary size
\lambda_{i}
of the correction to achieve that

\frac{d\tilde{u}_{i,j}}{dt}=\frac{du_{i,j}}{dt}+\lambda_{i}v_{i}

(2.7)

satisfies

\frac{dE_{\tilde{u},i}}{dt}=\left\langle\frac{dU}{du},\frac{du}{dt}+\lambda_{i}v_{i}\right\rangle_{S_{i}}\leq\sigma_{i}+F_{i-1/2}^{*}-F_{i+1/2}^{*},

(2.8)

where
F^{*}
is a numerical entropy fux. In hope for an entropy inequality, we employ the (local) Lax-Friedrichs flux for
F^{*}
as well
[
21
,
20
]
, i.e.

F^{*}_{i+1/2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(F\left(u_{i,k}\right)+F\left(u_{i+1,1}\right)\right)-c_{max}\cdot\frac{1}{2}\left(u_{i+1,1}-u_{i,k}\right).

(2.9)

For the first step, equation (
1.42
) from the previous chapter is applicable. In chapter
2.1
it was established that entropy dissipation can only occur in the absence of smooth solutions, as the entropy equality (
1.6
) holds otherwise. Consequently, dissipation does not transpire within the spectral volumes, given the smoothness of the reconstruction polynomials. Instead, dissipation is dictated by the SV boundaries where distinct reconstruction polynomials intersect.

2.2
Estimate for the highest possible entropy dissipation

Given the occurence of local Riemann problems at SV boundaries in every time step, we investigate a general Riemann problem with an initial condition given by:

u_{0}\left(x\right)=\begin{cases}u_{l},\quad x<0,\\
u_{r},\quad x>0.\end{cases}

(2.10)

From
[
13
, Lemma 2]
we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1

Given bounds
a_{l},a_{r}
on the fastest signal speed to the left and to the right, respectively. Let
M\geq\max\{|a_{l}|,|a_{r}|\}
. The entropy dissipation speed of a Riemann problem solution on the interval
\theta=\left]-M,M\right[
is bounded from below by

\sigma^{\theta}\geq\left(a_{r}-a_{l}\right)U\left(u_{lr}\right)+a_{l}U\left(u_{l}\right)-a_{r}U\left(u_{r}\right)+F\left(u_{l}\right)-F\left(u_{r}\right),

(2.11)

where

u_{lr}=\frac{a_{r}u_{r}-a_{l}u_{l}+f\left(u_{l}\right)-f\left(u_{r}\right)}{a_{r}-a_{l}}.

(2.12)

The proof can be found in the literature
[
13
, Lemma 2]
.

To align with the used local Lax-Friedrichs flux, we should adapt this inequality by choosing
c_{max}=\max\{|a_{l}|,|a_{r}|\}
and subsequaently updating
a_{r}=c_{max},a_{l}=-c_{max}
. This leads to

u_{lr}=\frac{u_{l}+u_{r}}{2}+\frac{f\left(u_{l}\right)-f\left(u_{r}\right)}{2c_{max}}

(2.13)

and (
2.11
) simplifies to

\sigma^{\theta}\geq c_{max}\left(2U\left(u_{lr}\right)-U\left(u_{l}\right)-U\left(u_{r}\right)\right)+F\left(u_{l}\right)-F\left(u_{r}\right).

(2.14)

This lower bound will inevitably overstate the highest possible entropy dissipation speed, given its negative nature. However, aiming for the most dissipative weak solution, we will employ this lower bound as an approximation of the highest possible entropy dissipation speed
[
21
,
20
]
.

To incorporate the entropy dissipation speed approximation in our SV scheme, we apply (
2.14
) on all local Riemann problems given by the reconstructed values of
u
at the SV boundaries. This leads to

\displaystyle\sigma_{i+1/2}=

\displaystyle c_{max}\left(2U\left(u_{m}\right)-U\left(u_{i,k+1/2}\right)-U\left(u_{i+1,1/2}\right)\right)

(2.15)

\displaystyle+F\left(u_{i,k+1/2}\right)-F\left(u_{i+1,1/2}\right)

with

u_{m}=\frac{u_{i,k+1/2}+u_{i+1,1/2}}{2}+\frac{f\left(u_{i,k+1/2}\right)-f\left(u_{i+1,1/2}\right)}{2c_{max}}

(2.16)

using periodic adjustments at the boundaries if necessary.

2.3
Entropy dissipation direction

Our objective now is to determine a direction to correct the time derivative of the SV scheme, ensuring entropy dissipation.
In accordance with the DG version
[
13
]
the correction direction will be based on the construction of operators that can regularize a solution
u
. These operators, termed filters, take the form of special Hilbert-Schmidt operators
K
.

Definition 2.2

(Filter):
An operator
K:L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)\rightarrow L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
is designated as filter if it is an integral operator satisfying

\left[Ku\right]\left(x\right)=\int_{\Omega}k\left(x,y\right)u\left(y\right)\,dy,\quad\text{with}\quad\forall x\in\Omega:\int_{\Omega}k\left(x,y\right)\,dy=1.

(2.17)

The kernel
k
is required to have bounded Hilbert-Schmidt norm. The pointwise evaluation of
\left[Ku\right]
conforms to a weighted average of
u
.

Of particular interest are conservative filters, ensuring the preservation of our basic SV scheme’s conservation property when applied to cell averages. Fubini’s theorem provides:

Theorem 2.3

(Conservative filter):

A filter
K:L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)\rightarrow L^{2}\left(\Omega\right)
is conservative, i.e.

\int_{\Omega}\left[Ku\right]\left(x\right)\,dx=\int_{\Omega}u\left(x\right)\,dx,

(2.18)

if the corresponding kernel
k
has mass one, i.e.

\forall y\in\Omega:\quad\int_{\Omega}k\left(x,y\right)\,dx=1.

(2.19)

For the complete proof, refer to
[
13
, Lemma 4]
.

Our aspiration is for the filter
K
to impart a smoothing effect and to dissipate entropy. The selection for an appropriate filter
K
is guided by the following theorem:

Theorem 2.4

(Dissipative filter):

A filter
K
is deemed dissipative if for all convex entropy functions
U

E_{Ku}=\int_{\Omega}U\left(\left[Ku\right]\left(x\right)\right)\,dx\leq\int_{\Omega}U\left(u\left(x\right)\right)\,dx=E_{u}

(2.20)

holds. For a conservative filter the above inequality holds if the corresponding kernel is non-negative, i.e.

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in\Omega:k\left(x,y\right)\geq 0.

(2.21)

If the corresponding kernel
k
of a filter
K
satisfies (
2.21
) we refer to the filter as positive. The proof can be found in the literature
[
13
, Theorem 1]
.

Evidently, the continuous filter cannot be applied directly on our numerical SV scheme. The next step involves modifying these concepts for discrete application. The discrete version of the continuous conservative, positive and hence dissipative filter is defined as follows:

Definition 2.5

(Discrete conservative and positive filter):

We designate a matrix
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
as a filter if

\forall j\in\{1,...,k\}:\quad\sum_{l=1}^{k}Y_{jl}=1

(2.22)

holds by analogy with definition
2.2
. It is said to be conservative, if

\forall l\in\{1,...,k\}:\quad\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}Y_{jl}=h_{l}

(2.23)

is satisfied, see theorem
2.3
. Further we call it positive if

\forall j,l\in\{1,...,k\}:\quad Y_{jl}\geq 0

(2.24)

holds corresponding to theorem
2.4
.

This definition may seem at odds with the continuous version of the filter
K
, especially if we consider the matrix
\mathbf{Y}
to be the discrete representation of the integral kernel
k
. The contradiction is resolved by considering the relation

Y_{jl}=h_{l}\tilde{Y}_{jl}

(2.25)

and interpreting
\mathbf{\tilde{Y}}
as the discrete version of the integral kernel
k
. Consequently, the integral operator
K
simplifies to a mere matrix-vector multiplication in the discrete case:

\left[Ku\right]\left(x\right)=\int_{\Omega}k\left(x,y\right)u\left(y\right)\,dy\quad\leftrightarrow\quad v_{i,j}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}h_{l}\tilde{Y}_{jl}u_{i,l}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}Y_{jl}u_{i,l}.

(2.26)

The relation (
2.23
) arises from

\displaystyle\int_{S_{i}}\left[Ku\right]\left(x\right)\,dx\approx\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}\sum_{l=1}^{k}Y_{jl}u_{i,l}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}u_{i,l}\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}Y_{jl}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}h_{l}u_{i,l}=\int_{S_{i}}u\left(x\right)\,dx

Theorem 2.6

Analogous to theorem
2.3
, we establish that a discrete filter
Y
that is positive and conservative is also dissipative in the sense that

\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}U\left(v_{i,j}\right)\leq\sum_{l=1}^{k}h_{l}U\left(u_{i,l}\right),\quad v_{i,j}=\sum_{l=1}^{k}Y_{j,l}u_{i,l}.

(2.27)

That means, a filter is dissipative if Jensen’s inequality holds in a discrete sense.

Proof: By Jensen’s inequality and definition
2.5
we get

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}U\left(v_{i,j}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}U\left(\sum_{l=1}^{k}Y_{j,l}u_{i,l}\right)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{l=1}^{k}h_{j}Y_{j,l}U\left(u_{i,l}\right).

The result follows by using again definition
2.5

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sum_{l=1}^{k}h_{j}Y_{j,l}U\left(u_{i,l}\right)=\sum_{l=1}^{k}U\left(u_{i,l}\right)\left(\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}Y_{j,l}\right)=\sum_{l=1}^{k}h_{l}U\left(u_{i,l}\right).

As we will see later, we can construct positive, conservative filters using so-called filter generators:

Definition 2.7

(Conservative and positive filter generator):

Let
G\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
a square matrix. we call
G
a filter generator if

\forall j\in\{1,...,k\}:\sum_{l=1}^{k}G_{jl}=0

(2.28)

holds. It will be conservative if

\forall l\in\{1,...,k\}:\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}G_{jl}=0

(2.29)

is satisfied. Further we call it positive, if

\forall l\in\{1,...,k\},\quad\forall j\in\{1,...,l-1,l+1,...,k\}:\quad G_{jl}\geq 0

(2.30)

holds.

Generators and filters have the following connection
[
13
, Lemma 5]
:

Theorem 2.8

(Generators and filters):

\mathbf{G}\text{ conservative as generator}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{I}+\Delta t\mathbf{G}\text{ conservative as filter.}

(2.31)

Let further
\Delta t\max_{l}|G_{ll}|\leq 1
. Then it follows

\mathbf{G}\text{ positive as generator}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\mathbf{Y}\text{ positive as filter.}

(2.32)

The proof can be found in the literature
[
13
, Lemma 5]
.

As in
[
13
]
, we will construct a filter generator based on the heat equation, thus possessing a direct physical interpretation. However, applying the procedure for the DG method directly to the SV scheme is not feasible as the DG procedure relies on the nodal basis of the DG method, whereas the SV approach is based on cell averages. Therefore, as an initial step, we introduce a finite volume discretization of the heat equation.

Finite volume discretization of the heat equation:

Consider the heat equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\Delta u

(2.33)

for an arbitrary spectral volume
S_{i}
in conjunction with homogenous Neumann boundary conditions. As the index
i
does not matter for our derivation, we will denote
u_{j}=u_{i,j}
for brevity. The heat equation (
2.33
) can be rewritten as conservation law

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}=0

(2.34)

with flux function

f\left(\nabla u\right)=-\nabla u.

(2.35)

Therefore, we define the discrete heat flux
f^{h}
as

f^{h}_{0+1/2}=f^{h}_{k+1/2}=0,\quad f^{h}_{j+1/2}=-2\cdot\frac{u_{j+1}-u_{j}}{h_{j+1}+h_{j}},\quad j=1,...,k-1.

(2.36)

An semidiscrete version of the heat equation is then given by

\frac{du_{j}}{dt}=\frac{f^{h}_{j-1/2}-f^{h}_{j+1/2}}{h_{j}},\quad j=1,...,k.

(2.37)

This can be expressed in matrix-vector form:

\frac{d\mathbf{u}}{dt}=\mathbf{H}\mathbf{u}.

(2.38)

Theorem 2.9

The matrix
\mathbf{H}
defined by (
2.37
) and (
2.38
) satisfies all requirements of a conservative, positive filter generator. Therefore, it will be used for all numerical computations and we denote
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{G}
.

Proof: The matrix
\mathbf{H}
is by construction a tridiagonal matrix and the non-zeros entries are given by:

\displaystyle H_{11}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{h_{1}\left(h_{1}+h_{2}\right)},\quad

\displaystyle H_{12}=\frac{2}{h_{1}\left(h_{1}+h_{2}\right)},

(2.39)

\displaystyle H_{kk-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{h_{k}\left(h_{k-1}+h_{k}\right)},\quad

\displaystyle H_{kk}=-\frac{2}{h_{k}\left(h_{k-1}+h_{k}\right)},

and for
j=2,...,k-1
we have

\displaystyle H_{jj-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{h_{j}\left(h_{j-1}+h_{j}\right)},\quad H_{jj+1}=\frac{2}{h_{j}\left(h_{j}+h_{j+1}\right)},

(2.40)

\displaystyle H_{jj}

\displaystyle=-\frac{2}{h_{j}}\left(\frac{1}{h_{j-1}+h_{j}}+\frac{1}{h_{j}+h_{j+1}}\right).

Thus, the matrix
\mathbf{H}
satisfies

\forall j\in\{1,...,k\}:\sum_{l=1}^{k}H_{jl}=0

(2.41)

which means
\mathbf{H}
is a filter generator. Secondly,
\mathbf{H}
is conservative, because
\forall l\in\{2,...,k-1\}:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}H_{jl}

\displaystyle=h_{l-1}H_{l-1l}+h_{l}H_{ll}+h_{l+1}H_{l+1l}

\displaystyle=h_{l-1}\cdot\frac{2}{h_{l-1}\left(h_{l-1}+h_{l}\right)}-h_{l}\cdot\frac{2}{h_{l}}\left(\frac{1}{h_{l-1}+h_{l}}+\frac{1}{h_{l}+h_{l+1}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+h_{l+1}\cdot\frac{2}{h_{l+1}\left(h_{l}+h_{l+1}\right)}

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{1}{h_{l-1}+h_{l}}-\left(\frac{1}{h_{l-1}+h_{l}}+\frac{1}{h_{l}+h_{l+1}}\right)+\frac{1}{h_{l}+h_{l+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=0

and the same is true for
l=1
and
l=k

\mathbf{H}
is positive as

\forall l\in\{1,...,k\},\quad\forall j\in\{1,...,l-1,l+1,...,k\}:\quad H_{jl}\geq 0

(2.42)

holds.

Note, that we have no problems with the positivity of
\mathbf{H}

for the DG version. The next theorem shows, that the matrix

2.

3.

4.

Multiply all rows by

Using theorem

Theorem 2.11

If
\mathbf{u}
is non-constant with admissable strictly convex entropy
U
and the nullspace of
\mathbf{H}
consists only of constants, then

\left\langle\frac{dU}{du},\mathbf{H}\mathbf{u_{i}}\right\rangle_{S_{i}}<0.

(2.45)

If
U
is merely convex, only
\leq
applies in the inequality above.
Keeping theorem
2.10
in mind the proof can be found in the literature
[
13
, Lemma 6]
.

Note, that we don’t have to assume that the nullspace of
\mathbf{H}=\mathbf{G}
consists only of constants as in the DG case
[
13
, Lemma 6]
, because of theorem
2.10
.

2.4
Correction size

After calculating the suitable direction
\mathbf{v_{i}}=\mathbf{Hu_{i}}=\mathbf{Gu_{i}}
, we continue with
the procedure outlined for the the DG version
[
13
, chapter 3.1]
to compute an appropriate value of the correction size
\lambda_{i}
in two steps. First, we calculate

\lambda_{ED}^{i}=\max\left(0,-\frac{\left\langle\frac{dU}{du},\frac{du}{dt}\right\rangle_{S_{i}}-\left(F^{*}_{i-1/2}-F^{*}_{i+1/2}\right)}{\left\langle\frac{dU}{du},\mathbf{v_{i}}\right\rangle_{S_{i}}}\right)

(2.46)

to enforce the per spectral volume entropy dissipativity:

\left\langle\frac{dU}{du},\frac{du}{dt}+\lambda^{i}_{ED}\mathbf{v_{i}}\right\rangle_{S_{i}}\leq F^{*}_{i-1/2}-F^{*}_{i+1/2},

(2.47)

In the second step, we enforce a suffiently high enough entropy rate. This is achieved by splitting the estimated entropy dissipation rate of the adjacent Riemann problems. In general, every Riemann problem is connected with an approximation of the highest possible entropy rate
\sigma^{\theta}
. The entropy rate approximations are then split between the two adjecent spectral volumes, leading to two additional parts of the correction size, one from the left and another from the right adjacent Riemann problem of the SV under consideration.

\displaystyle\lambda^{i}_{ER,l}=\max\left(0,\frac{\sigma_{i-1/2}}{\left\langle\frac{dU}{du},\mathbf{v_{i-1}}\right\rangle_{S_{i-1}}+\left\langle\frac{dU}{du},\mathbf{v_{i}}\right\rangle_{S_{i}}}\right),

(2.48)

\displaystyle\lambda^{i}_{ER,r}=\max\left(0,\frac{\sigma_{i+1/2}}{\left\langle\frac{dU}{du},\mathbf{v_{i}}\right\rangle_{S_{i}}+\left\langle\frac{dU}{du},\mathbf{v_{i+1}}\right\rangle_{S_{i+1}}}\right).

All parts are then added together:

\lambda^{i}_{sum}=\lambda^{i}_{ED}+\lambda^{i}_{ER,l}+\lambda^{i}_{ER,r}.

(2.49)

In a last step, we introduce an upper limit
\lambda_{max}
and compute

\lambda^{i}=\min\left(\lambda_{max},\lambda^{i}_{sum}\right)

(2.50)

to enforce the limit dictated by theorem
2.8
. For a clarification of the notation used, note that

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{dU}{du},\frac{du}{dt}\right\rangle_{S_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}\frac{dU}{du}\left(u_{i,j}\right)\cdot\frac{f_{i,j-1/2}^{*}-f_{i,j+1/2}^{*}}{h_{j}},

(2.51)

\displaystyle\left\langle\frac{dU}{du},\mathbf{v_{i}}\right\rangle_{S_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{k}h_{j}\frac{dU}{du}\left(u_{i,j}\right)\cdot v_{i,j},

(2.52)

where

\mathbf{v_{i}}=\mathbf{Gu_{i}},\quad\mathbf{v_{i}}=\left(v_{i,1},...,v_{i,k}\right).

(2.53)

3.4
Convergence Analysis

Finally, we will discuss the convergence speed of our modified SV scheme. As an example, we employed the modified SV scheme to calculate the numerical solution of the Euler equations with initial conditions:

\rho_{0}\left(x\right)=1+\varepsilon\left(x\right),\quad v_{0}\left(x\right)=1.0,\quad p_{0}\left(x\right)=1.0,\quad\varepsilon\left(x\right)=e^{-\frac{\left(x-5\right)^{2}}{2}}.

(3.14)

First, consider a generalized form of these initial conditions:

\tilde{\rho_{0}}\left(x\right)=\psi\left(x\right),\quad\tilde{v_{0}}\left(x\right)=v_{c},\quad\tilde{p_{0}}\left(x\right)=p_{c},

(3.15)

where
v_{c},p_{c}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\psi\left(x\right)
is some given smooth function. The solution of this initial value problem is given by

\tilde{\rho}\left(x,t\right)=\psi\left(x-v_{c}t\right),\quad\tilde{v}\left(x,t\right)=v_{c},\quad\tilde{p}\left(x,t\right)=p_{c}.

(3.16)

Therefore, the special case of
v_{c}=1.0
,
p_{c}=1.0
and
\psi(x)=1+\varepsilon(x)
in problem (
3.14
) provides

\rho\left(x,t\right)=1+\varepsilon\left(x-t\right),\quad v\left(x,t\right)=1.0,\quad p\left(x,t\right)=1.0.

(3.17)

The numerical solution was computed at
t=10
for
N_{CV}=4
and
N_{SV}=\{10,11,12,...,22\}
corresponding to
N=\{40,44,...,88\}
cells. Subsequently, the
L^{1}
and

Despite using a third order Runge-Kutta method for time integration, the observed convergence speed falls between orders 4 and 5, see figure

4
Summary

In this work, we modified the SV scheme proposed by Wang
[
24
]
by incorporating an entropy stabilization method suggested by Klein for the DG method
[
13
]
. We started with an overview of the basic SV scheme and its numerical implementation, utilizing Gauss-Lobatto grid points, polynomial reconstruction, and a third-order Runge-Kutta method for time integration.

An initial numerical test exposed challenges with the direct applicability of the SV scheme, as spurious oscillations emerged in the numerical solution. To adress this issue, we introduced and adapted Kleins’ entropy stabilization method from the DG method. Notably, the SV version of the stabilization method differed in its construction of the correction direction. As for the DG method, the direction for the SV scheme was constructed based on the time evolution of the heat equation, ensuring physical relevance. However, the SV method relies on cell averages instead of point values used in the DG version. Thus, the direction for the SV scheme was calculated by a finite volume discretization of the heat equation. This procedure appeared to be even simpler than the one used for the DG method.

The adapted SV scheme was then applied to various test cases to verify its practical applicability and an impovement over the basic SV scheme. Revisiting the initial test case revealed a significant reduction in spurious oscillations. Numerical tests of the Burgers’ equation with different initial conditions illustrated that the adapted SV scheme can handle shock formation and the developement of rarefaction waves in the solution. Two different shock tube tests further demonstrated satisfactory results, with sharp shocks and contact discontinuities in the numerical solution and no spurious smearings at the edges of rarefaction waves. Finally, a convergence analysis revealed that the order of convergence matches the order of the original SV scheme.

For future enhancements, the scheme could benefit from exploring alternative spectral volume partitionings beyond the currently employed Gauss-Lobatto points. Additionally, a different discretization of the
L^{2}
scalar product on control volumes is worth considering.

Moreover, stabilization methods, different from the one introduced in this work, are undoubtedly possible to stabilize the SV scheme. For instance, stabilization methods correcting the numerical flux itself, rather than the time derivative of the state variable, could be considered
[
22
,
23
]
.
