Iron supplementation-induced modulation of hepcidin, erythroferrone, and oxidative balance in iron-deficient pregnant women
Authors: Sevilay Yavuz Dogu, Hamdiye Acar, Hatice Argun Atalmis, Emine Yilmaz Guler, Icten Olgu Bafali, Berivan Guzelbag, Aydin Ocal, Filiz Yarsilikal Guleroglu, Ibrahim Yilmaz, Ali Cetin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIron deficiency anemia affects approximately 40% of pregnant women worldwide, associated with increased oxidative stress (OS) and adverse pregnancy outcomes. Recent advances identified hepcidin as the master regulator of iron homeostasis and erythroferrone (ERFE) as a key erythroid regulator, yet their responses to iron supplementation and relationships with OS markers during pregnancy remain poorly understood.ObjectiveTo investigate whether oral iron supplementation reduces OS in pregnant women with IDA and characterize relationships between iron regulatory molecules (hepcidin, ERFE) and OS biomarkers.MethodsThis single-arm observational study without placebo controls enrolled 40 pregnant women with IDA (mean age 29.23 ± 6.33 years) across all trimesters. Participants received 200 mg elemental iron daily for one month. Pre- and post-treatment measurements included hematologic parameters, iron metabolism markers (ferritin, hepcidin, ERFE), and OS biomarkers (total antioxidant status [TAS], total oxidant status [TOS]).ResultsIron supplementation was associated with significant improvements in TAS (0.96 ± 0.36 to 1.64 ± 0.37mmol Trolox Equiv./L; Cohen’s d = 1.87, p < 0.001), exceeding hematological improvements in hemoglobin (10.10 ± 0.72 to 11.61 ± 0.97 g/dL; d = 1.76, p < 0.001) and hematocrit (30.93 ± 1.79% to 35.24 ± 3.01%; d = 1.74, p < 0.001). The TOS/TAS ratio decreased significantly (14.53 ± 15.98 to 4.10 ± 3.14 U/mmol Trolox Equiv.; d = -0.91, p < 0.001). Hepcidin increased (2214.62 ± 1444.27 to 3492.65 ± 1640.51 pg/mL; d = 0.83, p < 0.001) while ERFE decreased (470.75 ± 170.75 to 345.25 ± 112.96 pg/mL; d = -0.87, p < 0.001), consistent with successful iron repletion. Notably, significant Trimester × Time interactions were observed for both hormones (hepcidin: ηp2 = .452; ERFE: ηp2 = .462, both p < 0.001), with hepcidin elevation progressively increasing across trimesters and ERFE suppression maximal during second trimester. TAS improvements showed minimal correlation with iron parameters (all |r|< 0.25), suggesting potentially independent mechanisms.ConclusionsIron supplementation was associated with reduced OS in pregnant women with IDA through mechanisms potentially independent of iron parameter changes. The coordinated hormonal responses are consistent with appropriate iron repletion while suggesting additional therapeutic benefits beyond anemia correction.Keywords: Iron deficiency anemia, Pregnancy, Oxidative stress, Total antioxidant status, Hepcidin, Erythroferrone

Background

Iron deficiency anemia (IDA) represents the most prevalent nutritional disorder during pregnancy worldwide, affecting approximately 40% of pregnant women and constituting a major public health challenge with significant implications for maternal and fetal health outcomes [1, 2]. The physiological demands of pregnancy create unique vulnerabilities for iron deficiency development, as maternal blood volume expansion increases by 40–50% during gestation requiring substantial iron for erythropoiesis, while additional iron is needed for supporting fetal growth and placental development [3]. Recent systematic reviews have identified substantial heterogeneity in international clinical practice guidelines for IDA management, with only 37.5% of existing guidelines meeting high-quality standards according to AGREE II framework assessment [4, 5]. Contemporary epidemiological evidence demonstrates that IDA during pregnancy is associated with increased maternal mortality, preterm delivery, low birth weight infants, and compromised neurocognitive development in offspring that can persist into adulthood [6]. Furthermore, emerging research has revealed that even iron deficiency without overt anemia can adversely impact placental iron transport mechanisms and fetal iron status, highlighting the importance of early detection and intervention [7].

The pathophysiological implications of iron deficiency extend far beyond traditional hematologic manifestations, encompassing complex molecular alterations that fundamentally disrupt cellular processes throughout pregnancy. Iron serves as an essential cofactor for numerous enzymatic reactions involved in oxygen transport, cellular respiration, and DNA synthesis, making its deficiency a systemic disorder with widespread consequences. Total antioxidant status (TAS) represents the cumulative effect of all antioxidants present in plasma and body fluids, providing an integrated measure of the biological system’s capability to counteract reactive oxygen species. Total oxidant status (TOS) indicates the overall oxidation state of the body, reflecting the total amount of oxidants present. The ratio of TOS to TAS provides an oxidative stress (OS) index that may be more clinically meaningful than individual measurements [8]. Recent investigations have illuminated the critical relationship between iron deficiency and OS, revealing that iron-depleted states create conditions conducive to increased reactive oxygen species production while simultaneously impairing antioxidant defense mechanisms [9, 10]. This pathological oxidative imbalance manifests as cellular membrane damage, protein oxidation, and DNA fragmentation that can trigger inflammatory cascades characteristic of pregnancy complications. Contemporary studies have demonstrated that IDA during pregnancy significantly alters antioxidant enzyme activities, particularly superoxide dismutase and glutathione peroxidase, creating conditions that promote OS; additionally, elevated levels of OS biomarkers have been documented in iron-deficient pregnant women, though the reversibility of these changes with treatment remains to be fully established [11].

Oral iron supplementation represents the primary therapeutic intervention for treating IDA in pregnancy, yet significant knowledge gaps persist regarding optimal dosing strategies and comprehensive assessment of treatment efficacy beyond conventional hematologic parameters. Current clinical practice demonstrates considerable variation in iron supplementation protocols, with recommended doses ranging from 30–100 mg of elemental iron daily, based primarily on studies focusing on hemoglobin response and ferritin restoration [12]. However, recent advances in iron metabolism research have revealed sophisticated regulatory mechanisms involving hepcidin that fundamentally control iron homeostasis during pregnancy and may serve as more sensitive indicators of treatment response [13, 14]. Hepcidin, synthesized primarily in hepatocytes, functions as the master regulator of systemic iron homeostasis by controlling iron absorption and cellular iron release through its interaction with ferroportin. Erythroferrone (ERFE), a hormone produced by erythroblasts in response to erythropoietic stimulation, suppresses hepcidin production to increase iron availability for hemoglobin synthesis [15]. Recent maternal–fetal studies have demonstrated that maternal hepcidin levels, rather than fetal hepcidin, determine embryo iron endowment and placental iron transport efficiency [16]. Understanding these regulatory mechanisms becomes particularly important given emerging evidence that maternal obesity and smoking can significantly alter hepcidin concentrations, potentially affecting iron bioavailability and treatment responses [17, 18].

Despite widespread clinical use of iron supplementation and extensive research documenting hematologic benefits, critical knowledge gaps remain regarding the comprehensive molecular effects of iron therapy, particularly its impact on OS-related biomarkers that may influence pregnancy outcomes beyond anemia correction. While numerous studies have demonstrated iron therapy efficacy in improving hemoglobin levels and restoring iron stores, limited research has systematically examined whether iron supplementation reduces OS as measured by TAS and TOS or modulates the complex interplay between iron metabolism and systemic antioxidant capacity. Based on these considerations, we hypothesized that oral iron therapy in pregnant women with IDA would improve both hematologic parameters and OS biomarkers, with potential relationships between these changes requiring characterization.

The primary objective of this prospective observational study was to investigate whether iron supplementation in pregnant women with IDA is associated with changes in OS as measured by TAS and TOS biomarkers, while characterizing the relationships between conventional iron parameters, advanced iron regulatory molecules, and OS markers.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This prospective observational study was conducted at the Antenatal Care Service of Haseki Training and Research Hospital, affiliated with the University of Health Sciences, from January to August 2024. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Human Research Ethics Committee (Registry No: 114–2021 dated December 8, 2021) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment. The absence of a control group was based on ethical considerations regarding withholding standard iron therapy from pregnant women with diagnosed anemia.

Participants

Pregnant women attending routine prenatal care were screened for IDA. Inclusion criteria were: (1) singleton pregnancy in first (11–14 weeks), second (20–24 weeks), or third trimester (32–36 weeks); (2) IDA defined as hemoglobin levels below 11.0 g/dL and hematocrit below 33% in first and third trimesters, or hemoglobin below 10.5 g/dL and hematocrit below 32% in second trimester, combined with evidence of iron deficiency (ferritin < 15 ng/mL or transferrin saturation < 16%); (3) age 18–45 years.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) multiple pregnancy; (2) chronic systemic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, thyroid disorders, rheumatologic diseases); (3) known cardiovascular disease; (4) smoking; (5) anemia due to causes other than iron deficiency; (6) high-risk pregnancy conditions; (7) previous iron supplementation within 1 month.

Sample size calculation

Sample size determination was performed using G*Power version 3.1.9.7 software for paired t-test analysis. Based on an effect size (Cohen’s d) of 0.5, alpha error probability of 0.05, and statistical power of 0.90, the analysis indicated that 36 participants would provide adequate power to detect clinically meaningful changes in OS biomarkers. To account for potential dropout and incomplete data, we targeted enrollment of 40 participants, providing 90.3% actual power for primary analyses. The study was not powered for subgroup analyses by trimester or BMI category, which were considered exploratory.

Intervention

All participants received oral iron supplementation consisting of 200 mg elemental iron daily (as ferrous fumarate capsule containing 100 mg elemental iron, administered as 2 capsules daily, taken on an empty stomach or 1 h before meals). Participants were counseled on proper iron administration considering concurrent calcium/tea/coffee consumption and potential side effects.

Data collection

Fasting venous blood samples (5 mL) were obtained between 08:00–10:00 h at baseline and after one-month supplementation. Serum was isolated via centrifugation (3000 rpm, 10 min) and stored at −80°C pending analysis.

Complete blood count parameters were quantified using an automated hematology analyzer (DxH 800, Beckman Coulter). Iron homeostasis markers including serum iron, total iron-binding capacity (TIBC), and ferritin, along with high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (detection limit: 0.15 mg/L), were measured on a Roche Cobas 6000 analyzer. Transferrin saturation was calculated as (serum iron/TIBC) × 100.

Serum hepcidin (E-EL-H6202, Elabscience, USA; detection range: 780–50,000 pg/mL) and ERFE (MBS2088219, MyBioSource, USA; detection range: 156–10,000 pg/mL) concentrations were determined via sandwich ELISA. Total antioxidant status (TAS; E-BC-K801-M, Elabscience, USA) was quantified through ABTS radical cation decolorization assay, measuring samples within 0.23–2 mmol Trolox Equiv./L range. Total oxidant status (TOS; E1599Hu, BT Laboratory, China) was assessed using ELISA methodology with a detection range of 0.05–30 U/mL. All immunoassays were performed in duplicate following manufacturers’ protocols, with intra-assay coefficients of variation maintained below 10%.

Anthropometric data were obtained using calibrated equipment (SECA 769 digital scale; SECA 217 stadiometer). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight(kg)/height(m)2 and categorized according to WHO criteria: underweight (< 18.5), normal (18.5–24.9), overweight (25.0–29.9), and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m2).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.x (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) for primary analyses and Python version 3.11 with matplotlib library for forest plot visualization. IBM SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) was used for confirmatory analyses. Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and number (percentage) for categorical variables. Normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Paired t-tests were used to compare pre-treatment and post-treatment values for all biomarkers. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d for paired samples, with 95% confidence intervals computed using the non-central t-distribution method. Effect sizes were interpreted according to established thresholds: small (d = 0.2), moderate (d = 0.5), large (d = 0.8), and very large (d > 1.2).

For hepcidin and ERFE, a two-way mixed analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to examine differential treatment responses across gestational stages. The analytical model incorporated one between-subjects factor (Trimester Group: First, Second, Third) and one within-subjects factor (Time: Before, After). This design enabled assessment of three distinct effects: (a) the main effect of Trimester Group, reflecting baseline differences in iron regulatory hormone concentrations across gestational stages; (b) the main effect of Time, quantifying overall temporal changes in hormone levels independent of trimester classification; and (c) the Trimester Group × Time interaction, examining whether supplementation-induced hormonal changes exhibited differential patterns as a function of gestational age. Effect sizes for omnibus tests were quantified using partial eta-squared (ηp2), with conventional interpretive benchmarks of 0.01, 0.06, and 0.14 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively. Significant interactions were decomposed using simple main effects analyses, examining between-group differences at each time point and within-group temporal changes for each trimester separately. Pairwise comparisons utilized Bonferroni correction to control for Type I error inflation across multiple comparisons.

Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess relationships between post-treatment biomarker values, with significance levels indicated for p < 0.05, p < 0.01, and p < 0.001. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was performed given the exploratory nature of the correlation analyses, and this limitation is acknowledged in the discussion.

Subgroup analyses were performed based on gestational age trimester (first: n = 7, second: n = 17, third: n = 16) and BMI categories (normal weight: n = 13, overweight: n = 16, obese: n = 11) to examine differential treatment responses. Mean changes from baseline were compared across subgroups using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for trend. Post-hoc power calculations indicated that subgroup comparisons had 30–45% power to detect moderate effect sizes, confirming their exploratory nature.

Forest plots were generated to visualize effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals for all measured parameters, with parameters arranged by effect size magnitude. Scatter plots with means and standard deviations were created to illustrate individual participant responses for key biomarkers. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses. Missing data were handled using complete case analysis, as no participants had missing values for primary outcomes.

Results

A total of 40 pregnant women with IDA completed the study protocol with complete pre- and post-treatment biomarker datasets. No participants were lost to follow-up. Treatment adherence averaged 95% (range: 80–100%) based on pill counts. Eight participants (20%) reported mild gastrointestinal side effects: nausea (n = 5) and constipation (n = 3). No serious adverse events occurred.

Iron metabolism biomarkers

Two-way mixed ANOVA analyses examined iron regulatory hormone responses across gestational stages (Fig. 1). For hepcidin, a significant main effect of Time was observed, F(1, 37) = 47.83, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.564, with concentrations increasing from 2214.62 ± 1444.27 to 3492.65 ± 1640.51 pg/mL (mean increase: 1278.03 ± 1787.20 pg/mL, 95% CI: 724.17–1831.89, Cohen’s d = 0.83). The main effect of Trimester Group was not significant, F(2, 37) = 1.67, p = 0.202, ηp2 = 0.083. Critically, the Trimester Group × Time interaction was statistically significant, F(2, 37) = 15.24, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.452, indicating differential hepcidin elevation across gestational stages: First Trimester (552.78 ± 2662.45 pg/mL), Second Trimester (1246.84 ± 1653.73 pg/mL), and Third Trimester (1541.71 ± 1697.60 pg/mL), demonstrating progressive increase in hepatocyte responsiveness with advancing gestation (Table 5).

For ERFE, a highly significant main effect of Time was observed, F(1, 37) = 199.65, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.844, with concentrations decreasing from 470.75 ± 170.75 to 345.25 ± 112.96 pg/mL (mean reduction: 125.50 ± 158.26 pg/mL, 95% CI: 76.46–174.54, Cohen’s d = −0.87). The Trimester Group × Time interaction was statistically significant, F(2, 37) = 15.90, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.462, revealing non-linear suppression patterns: First Trimester (−32.00 ± 83.19 pg/mL, 7.0%), Second Trimester (−165.18 ± 191.38 pg/mL, 34.1%), and Third Trimester (−109.38 ± 119.53 pg/mL, 23.3%), suggesting mid-pregnancy represents a critical window of ERFE regulatory plasticity (Table 5).

The significant interactions for both hormones (hepcidin: ηp2 = 0.452; ERFE: ηp2 = 0.462) demonstrate trimester-dependent iron homeostatic responses, with opposing directional changes confirming physiologically appropriate hormonal regulation following iron repletion.

Correlation analyses

Post-treatment correlations are presented in Table 4. Hemoglobin and hematocrit showed strong positive correlation (r = 0.97, p < 0.001). Iron levels correlated positively with transferrin saturation (r = 0.92, p < 0.001) and negatively with TIBC (r = −0.48, p < 0.01). Ferritin correlated positively with hepcidin (r = 0.47, p < 0.01). TAS showed weak correlations with all iron parameters (|r|< 0.25). TOS correlated negatively with hemoglobin (r = −0.36, p < 0.05) and hematocrit (r = −0.31, p < 0.05). TAS and TOS showed no significant correlation (r = 0.08, p > 0.05).

TOS decreases were −2.24 ± 6.20, −8.31 ± 10.26, and −5.83 ± 10.15 U/mL, respectively (p = 0.145).

Discussion

This prospective observational study documented associations between one month of oral iron supplementation and changes in multiple biomarkers among pregnant women with IDA. The most notable observation was the exceptionally large effect size for TAS changes (Cohen’s d = 1.87), which exceeded even the substantial changes in hemoglobin (d = 1.76) and hematocrit (d = 1.74). While these effect sizes suggest clinically meaningful improvements, the absence of a control group precludes definitive causal attribution, and observed changes may partially reflect pregnancy progression, spontaneous recovery, or placebo effects. Iron supplementation was associated with reduced OS burden, as evidenced by decreased TOS (d = −0.64) and reduction in the TOS/TAS ratio (d = −0.91), while simultaneously showing improvements in iron stores with increased ferritin (d = 0.82) and transferrin saturation (d = 0.97). The iron regulatory hormones demonstrated patterns consistent with expected physiological responses, with hepcidin levels increasing (d = 0.83) and ERFE decreasing (d = −0.87), patterns compatible with successful iron repletion and reduced erythropoietic drive.

Notably, correlation analyses revealed that TAS improvements showed minimal association with traditional iron parameters (all |r|< 0.25), raising hypotheses about potential antioxidant mechanisms operating beyond simple correction of iron deficiency. However, these weak correlations may also reflect measurement variability, complex temporal relationships, or confounding factors not captured in our analysis. Subgroup analyses revealed differential patterns in treatment response based on maternal characteristics, with first trimester participants showing greater iron absorption parameters but third trimester participants demonstrating larger ferritin accumulation and OS changes. These exploratory findings require validation in adequately powered studies. Body mass index appeared to influence treatment response patterns, with normal weight women showing superior iron absorption and antioxidant capacity changes, while overweight and obese women demonstrated larger reductions in the TOS/TAS ratio despite attenuated TAS increases.

The significant Trimester Group × Time interactions for both hepcidin (F(2,37) = 15.24, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.452) and ERFE (F(2,37) = 15.90, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.462) revealed trimester-dependent hormonal responses to iron supplementation, with large effect sizes indicating clinically meaningful differential trajectories across gestational stages. As illustrated in Fig. 1, hepcidin elevation increased progressively with advancing gestation, suggesting heightened hepatocyte responsiveness in later pregnancy, possibly reflecting preparation for delivery-related blood loss. Conversely, ERFE exhibited non-linear suppression, with Second Trimester participants demonstrating the most pronounced reduction (34.1%), potentially reflecting peak erythropoietic activity during mid-gestation when maternal blood volume expansion reaches maximum velocity.

These findings extend previous observations by Fisher et al. [19] and Sangkhae et al. [14], who documented hepcidin kinetics and ERFE-iron relationships without examining trimester-specific differential responses. The lower baseline hepcidin in First Trimester participants, coupled with their superior iron absorption (serum iron increase of 50.00 ± 52.54 μg/dL versus 20.69 ± 30.31 μg/dL in Third Trimester), supports the hypothesis that early pregnancy maintains permissive iron regulatory states to facilitate maternal blood volume expansion. The paradoxical pattern wherein First Trimester participants absorbed iron most efficiently yet demonstrated smallest hepcidin increases suggests that early pregnancy prioritizes immediate iron utilization, while later gestation emphasizes ferritin accumulation for peripartum hemorrhage preparation.

The substantial effect sizes for both interaction terms (ηp2 > 0.45) indicate that trimester classification explains nearly half of the variance in hormonal responses, underscoring gestational age as a critical moderator that should inform personalized supplementation strategies. The coordinated but non-parallel trajectories of hepcidin elevation and ERFE suppression suggest that iron homeostatic mechanisms undergo developmental transitions throughout pregnancy, with important implications for establishing trimester-specific reference ranges for these biomarkers.

Recent investigations into iron metabolism during pregnancy provide important context for interpreting our findings. Fisher et al. [19] documented hepcidin kinetics in iron-supplemented pregnant women that closely parallel our observations, with levels stabilizing within physiological ranges despite therapeutic doses. Their work primarily focused on hematological outcomes, yet the consistency with our hormone data supports the hypothesis of preserved iron homeostasis during supplementation. The elegant demonstration by Sangkhae et al. [14] of the inverse relationship between iron availability and ERFE production offers biological plausibility for our findings. Using both human subjects and mouse models, they showed ERFE suppression occurs rapidly once iron sufficiency is restored, which represents a timeline consistent with our one-month intervention. This coordinated hormonal response suggests that pregnancy maintains sophisticated iron regulatory mechanisms despite increased physiological demands, and that supplementation may work within, rather than overwhelming, these natural control systems.

The OS changes we observed warrant careful consideration within the context of accumulating evidence linking oxidative damage to adverse pregnancy outcomes. Shastri et al. [20] reported elevated lipid peroxidation and protein carbonylation in iron-deficient pregnant women, though their cross-sectional design prevented conclusions about reversibility. Our prospective data suggest that these oxidative changes may be reversed, though without controls, spontaneous improvement cannot be excluded. The magnitude of observed changes surpasses previous reports, possibly reflecting our higher supplementation dose or more comprehensive biomarker assessment. The theoretical framework proposed by Cao and O’Brien [21], linking iron deficiency to preeclampsia through oxidative mechanisms, provides a conceptual basis for understanding our findings. They described a “perfect storm” scenario where iron deficiency simultaneously increases ROS production and compromises antioxidant defenses, which represents a pattern consistent with our TAS and TOS measurements that appeared to improve with treatment.

Trimester-specific patterns in our exploratory analyses revealed potential complexity in iron metabolism across pregnancy. Early screening recommendations by Milman et al. [22] were based primarily on hematological considerations, but our data suggest a more nuanced picture of evolving physiological priorities. First trimester women showed greater iron absorption, with serum levels increasing by 50.00 ± 52.54 μg/dL compared to only 20.69 ± 30.31 μg/dL in the third trimester, yet third trimester participants demonstrated superior ferritin accumulation and OS changes. These patterns may suggest early gestation prioritizes immediate iron utilization for maternal blood volume expansion, while later pregnancy emphasizes storage and antioxidant defense, possibly in preparation for delivery blood loss and postpartum recovery. However, these interpretations remain speculative given the small subgroup sizes and absence of baseline stratification. Dewey and Oaks [23] theoretical discussion of “critical windows” for supplementation may find support in these findings, though controlled studies are needed for confirmation. If validated, these observations would suggest that while early supplementation may optimize iron absorption, treatment at any gestational age could provide meaningful benefits, particularly for OS reduction.

The influence of maternal BMI on observed responses challenges conventional assumptions and highlights the complexity of iron metabolism in obesity. Normal weight women showed greater changes in iron absorption, consistent with Cepeda-Lopez et al. [24] documentation of obesity-related malabsorption, yet the OS patterns proved more complex. Overweight and obese women achieved larger reductions in the TOS/TAS ratio despite smaller absolute TAS increases, an observation that may reflect their higher baseline oxidative burden. Khambalia et al. [25] recommended adjusted supplementation protocols for obese pregnant women based solely on achieving target hemoglobin levels, but our findings raise the hypothesis that standard dosing may adequately address OS even when hematological responses are attenuated. These preliminary observations have potential clinical implications given the increasing prevalence of maternal obesity: they suggest antioxidant benefits may occur across all BMI categories, though the mechanisms likely differ. The potentially greater baseline OS in heavier women could mean relative improvements appear more dramatic, while their persistent mild inflammation (evidenced by higher baseline CRP) might influence the anti-inflammatory associations with iron repletion.

Recent mechanistic studies provide biological plausibility for our observations and offer potential explanations for why OS changes appeared independent of iron parameter changes. Frazer and Anderson [26] identified over 300 iron-dependent proteins involved in OS response, extending far beyond the classical antioxidant enzymes. This vast network could explain the rapid TAS improvement within one month, as these enzymes may quickly recover function once iron becomes available. Gambling et al. [27] demonstrated restored mitochondrial complex activity within two weeks of supplementation, providing a timeline consistent with our findings. However, the weak correlation between TAS and iron parameters suggests additional mechanisms may be involved. Erber et al.’s [28] transcriptomic analysis offers a hypothesis suggesting that iron deficiency may alter expression of numerous non-iron-dependent antioxidant genes, possibly as maladaptive compensation. Supplementation could theoretically work partially by removing the stimulus for this compensation, allowing normal antioxidant gene expression patterns to resume. The transgenerational implications become even more significant considering Sangkhae et al. [29] demonstration that maternal iron status influences fetal antioxidant enzyme expression through epigenetic modifications. While we didn’t assess fetal outcomes, this finding underscores the potential importance of achieving maternal oxidative balance for offspring health.

The clinical relevance of our findings requires consideration alongside population-based studies linking OS to pregnancy complications. Ferguson et al. [30] meta-analysis found OS biomarkers predicted adverse outcomes with odds ratios of 2.5–3.2, effect sizes comparable to the magnitude of changes we observed. The inflammatory component adds another dimension, with our observed CRP reduction (d = −0.81) potentially consistent with iron supplementation interrupting what Burton and Jauniaux [31] termed “oxidative-inflammatory spirals” in pregnancy complications. The observation of substantial changes within one month raises questions about whether late-presenting patients might benefit from intervention. Our data suggest that women diagnosed with iron deficiency in the third trimester may achieve significant OS improvements, though whether this translates to prevention of late-pregnancy complications requires prospective controlled trials.

Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several methodological strengths including: the prospective design with complete pre- and post-treatment biomarker assessments in all 40 participants, which minimized selection bias and ensured data integrity. The comprehensive biomarker panel, including advanced iron regulatory hormones (hepcidin, ERFE) and validated OS markers (TAS, TOS), provided insights unavailable in previous studies relying solely on hematological parameters. The excellent treatment adherence (95% taking ≥ 80% of prescribed doses) and low adverse event rate demonstrate the feasibility of the supplementation protocol in routine clinical practice. The inclusion of participants across all trimesters and BMI categories enhances potential generalizability to diverse pregnant populations. The use of standardized effect size calculations with 95% confidence intervals facilitates comparison with future studies and potential meta-analytic inclusion.

However, critical limitations substantially constrain interpretation of our findings. The single-arm observational design without a placebo control group represents the fundamental limitation, preventing causal inferences about iron supplementation effects. Observed changes may result from multiple factors including pregnancy progression, spontaneous recovery from anemia, regression to the mean, placebo effects, or seasonal variations, rather than treatment effects. The relatively small sample size, particularly in trimester-based subgroups, resulted in inadequate statistical power to detect smaller but clinically meaningful differences. The single-center setting in Istanbul may limit generalizability to other populations with different dietary patterns, genetic backgrounds, or environmental exposures. The one-month treatment duration, while sufficient to observe acute biochemical changes, cannot address longer-term clinical outcomes or the durability of OS improvements. The absence of maternal or neonatal clinical outcomes prevents direct assessment of whether biochemical changes correlate with pregnancy complications. The lack of multiple comparison corrections in our exploratory analyses increases Type I error risk, although the consistency of findings across related biomarkers provides some reassurance. Additionally, the absence of dietary iron intake assessment, concurrent medication documentation, and baseline hepcidin stratification represent important methodological gaps.

Conclusions

This observational study documented associations between iron supplementation and substantial changes in OS biomarkers among pregnant women with IDA, though causal relationships cannot be established. The large effect sizes for antioxidant capacity changes, coupled with their weak correlation with iron parameter changes, generate hypotheses about potential iron therapy mechanisms that may involve comprehensive cellular recovery processes beyond traditional iron repletion. While these preliminary findings suggest potential benefits, healthcare providers should await randomized controlled trial evidence before modifying clinical practice. The observed patterns across all trimesters and BMI categories raise questions about universal versus stratified approaches that merit investigation. The observation of changes within one month provides preliminary data for designing future intervention trials. These findings contribute to evolving understanding of iron supplementation as potentially involving antioxidant mechanisms, though establishing clinical significance requires prospective controlled studies examining both biomarker changes and pregnancy outcomes.
