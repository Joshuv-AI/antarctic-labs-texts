Sensing the shape of a surface by tightly surface-bound filaments
Authors: Handuo Shi, Jeffrey Nguyen, Jordan Alexander Huang, Zemer Gitai, Joshua Shaevitz, Benjamin P. Bratton, Ajay Gopinathan, Gregory Grason, Kerwyn Casey Huang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe protein MreB, a homolog of eukaryotic actin, regulates the shape of bacteria like Escherichia coli by guiding nascent cell-wall insertion based on local curvature cues. However, the mechanism by which a nanometer-scale MreB filament “senses” the micron-scale curvature of the cell wall has remained a mystery. We introduce a biophysical model of the energetics of twisted and bent filaments bound to curved surfaces, which predicts that localization of filaments like MreB is sensitive to both mean and Gaussian curvature. The model captures experimentally measured curvature enrichment patterns and explains how MreB naturally localizes to saddle-shaped regions without energy-consuming processes. Beyond cell shape regulation, our work suggests design principles for synthetic systems that can sense and respond to surface shape.

Understanding the mechanisms that enable sensing and control of cell shape in living organisms is a fundamental challenge. Known mechanisms are typically based on the regulation of proteins and their assemblies whose nanometer sizes are much smaller than the micron-scale dimensions of cells. In bacteria, cell shape is thought to be regulated by the spatial pattern of membrane-bound cytoskeletal filaments (1). The localization of a stiff filament depends on the mismatch between the geometry of the cell surface and the preferred filament conformation, which includes its propensity to curve and to twist (2). The local geometry of a surface is characterized by two principal curvatures, which can be expressed as a mean curvature (average magnitude) and a Gaussian curvature (the product of the two curvatures, whose sign indicates whether the surface is spherical or saddle-like). Protein or filaments can thus bind to surfaces that most closely match their intrinsic curvatures; for instance, amphipathic helices prefer positive Gaussian curvature (3) while α-synuclein generates negative Gaussian curvature (presumably due to its geometric preference) (4). Filament curvature and twist collectively interact with both mean and Gaussian curvature, yet many models of filament energetics consider them separately, despite their strong covariation in common bacterial cell shapes such as rods.

Bacteria such as Escherichia coli maintain rod-like shape by remodeling their rigid peptidoglycan cell wall during growth (5). In many species, the actin homolog MreB forms short filaments that move along the cell periphery to guide cell wall insertion (6–8), and its localization and movement are correlated with the local cell wall curvature (9–12). MreB is enriched in regions of both low mean and Gaussian curvature (9, 11), particularly in aberrantly shaped spheroplasts lacking a complete cell wall (12). By mediating feedback between the local cell wall geometry and cell wall synthesis, MreB helps to robustly maintain a rod-like morphology at the cellular scale. However, it remains unclear whether MreB senses mean or Gaussian curvature directly or whether its localization is driven by its movement (13).

In many bacteria, MreB forms antiparallel double protofilaments that appear straight in crystal structures (14) but adopt multiple twist states in molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (15, 16). A previous biophysical model incorporating membrane and filament bending as well as a membrane pinning term can prescribe the orientation and length of MreB (17). However, this model assumed a perfect cylinder and neglected filament twist. Other models have also focused on filament bending (13, 18–20). Much like actin, the asymmetric cross-section and potential for twisting in MreB double protofilaments suggests that both bending and twist should be considered on equivalent footing in energetic models. In actin, bending and twist are strongly coupled (21), and twist regulates actin filament stability and assembly dynamics (22). A biophysical model considering the requirement that a twisted MreB filament straightens to align membrane binding sites with the surface successfully predicted changes in filament size and orientation across a range of MreB mutants with different intrinsic twists (16), suggesting that the anisotropy of surface binding and twisting are critical components of the energy landscape. However, this study again assumed a perfect cylindrical cell body, leaving unanswered the critical question of how variation in local surface geometry affects filament localization.

Here, we develop a model for filament localization based on the generic compatibility between the natural shape of filaments, their resistance to bending and twisting, and the arbitrary geometry of the surface to which they bind. We find that the coupling between twist and bending determines localization. Using values of key mechanical parameters extracted from MD simulations, our model quantitatively predicts an MreB enrichment profile consistent with experimental measurements for physiologically relevant filament lengths. We demonstrate experimentally that enrichment is independent of MreB movement or its binding history. Taken together, our findings indicate that MreB localization is an equilibrium phenomenon that depends on the covariance of mean and Gaussian curvature; that is, on the interdependence of curvatures within biologically relevant cell shapes and their fluctuations.

Results

Shape Compatibility between Twisted Filaments and the Surface to Which They Bind Determines a Curvature-Dependent Binding Landscape.

To explore how surface geometry and filament mechanics determine localization, we consider a generic model for the elastic energy of intrinsically twisted and bent anisotropic filaments that strongly adsorb to a surface of arbitrary mean (H) and Gaussian (KG) curvatures. As a simplified model of filaments like MreB associating to an approximately cylindrical cytoplasmic membrane (whose rod-shaped geometry is determined by the cell wall), we consider short elastic rods with anisotropic local cross-section (Fig. 1A), whose wider dimension mediates surface binding. We assume that the filament bends easily in one direction (Fig. 1A) with intrinsic curvature k0 while it is intrinsically straight and substantially stiffer to bending in the orthogonal direction. Combinations of H and KG satisfying Eq. 5 correspond to geometries with surface curvature and twist that perfectly match the filament’s preferred bend and twist, while deviations from this perfect fit result in strain and increase the elastic energy of the bound filament. As an example, Fig. 1C highlights three locations in the curvature landscape for a filament with α=π/6 bound to a i) saddle, ii) cylinder, and iii) sphere (Fig. 1B). For the saddle with H=0, there is a specific Gaussian curvature, KG=-Ω02, for which the elastic energy in Eq. 4 is zero. The same filament bound to a cylinder and sphere experiences increasing elastic energy due to overbending and untwisting (Fig. 1B). Note that perfect fit is generically possible for all values of H, yet the location of the perfect fit is skewed toward negative KG values (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B), implying a generic preference for filament binding to surfaces with negative Gaussian curvature. For small α in particular, negative KG is favored due to the existence of straight (i.e., asymptotic) lines along which the surface normal twists. Thus, the shape of strongly binding filaments selects surface geometries with a specific linear relationship between mean and Gaussian curvature.

To assess how closely E. coli cells follow toroidal geometry, we imaged a population of log-phase cells and segmented the cell outlines (Fig. 2B and Methods). Although precise three-dimensional geometry is difficult to measure (24, 25), cells appear approximately rod-shaped and do not exhibit regions with large curvature compared to the inverse of the cylindrical radius. We therefore assume that the cell centerline lies in the imaging plane, which is reasonable given the large mechanical pressure from the coverslip and the agarose pad during imaging. We then estimated the principal curvatures from the measured curvatures within and perpendicular to the imaging plane (Methods). For each contour point, we first obtained its osculating circle (radius R) in the imaging plane. The radius of the osculating circle perpendicular to the imaging plane (r) was defined by the intersection of the local contour normal vector and the cell centerline (Fig. 2B). The mean and Gaussian curvature of this point are therefore, respectively H=R+r2Rr and KG=1Rr. This strategy was previously used for analyzing MreB enrichment as a function of curvature (11). Similar to bent tubes (Fig. 2A), KG and H in individual cells were approximately linear, except for the hemispherical poles with high KG and H (Fig. 2C). Across cells with different widths (hence different average r), the slope of the line relating KG and H was ∼2r in the cell body (Fig. 2D). Across the entire population, since cell width was narrowly distributed, most surface geometries had KG=0, representing cell bodies; a small subset near the poles had high KG and H (Fig. 2E). Taken together, even with cell poles and local deformations away from rods, the mean and Gaussian curvatures in E. coli are strongly correlated and confined to a limited space that provides a framework for model predictions.

Inferring the Mechanical Properties of MreB Polymers from MD Simulations.

We next sought to validate our model using MreB, which forms short (diffraction-limited) filaments that bind to the inner cytoplasmic membrane and guide cell wall insertion (8). Since it is challenging to experimentally measure the mechanical properties of MreB, MD simulations have been used to estimate intrinsic bending, twist, and the associated moduli for cytoskeletal filaments including MreB (26, 27), the tubulin homolog FtsZ (28), and actin (29).

Crystal structures show MreB as straight antiparallel double protofilaments (14), but MD simulations revealed substantial conformational variability. MreB hydrolyzes ATP (30), which influences monomer conformation and bending of a single protofilament (26). In a double protofilament, the bending translates into a nucleotide-dependent twist (16). Filament bending and twist are further affected by point mutations (16), binding to the MreB-interacting protein RodZ (27), and the small molecule MreB inhibitor A22 (16).

To capture MreB properties over long time scales, we analyzed two replicate simulations of a 4 × 2 MreB double protofilament initialized from a crystal structure of Caulobacter crescentus MreB (CcMreB, PDB: 4CZJ, Fig. 3A and SI Appendix, Table S1) (15). The two simulations, 2.7 µs and 2 µs long, were performed with the double protofilament surrounded by water (Fig. 3 A, Bottom). At each time step, we calculated the intrinsic curvature and twist of the polymer (Methods). Filament twist shifted among multiple steady states (15), as identified by a change-point analysis algorithm (31, 32). Together, the two simulations exhibited four twist states, with mean intersubunit twist angles of 12.2°, 9.3°, 4.9°, and 2.4° (Fig. 3B), while filament bending remained at ~2.0°, primarily perpendicular to the membrane-binding interface (Fig. 3C). Because MreB filaments are stable for tens of seconds in vivo (8), they likely experience similar conformational changes.

We estimated the twist modulus of the MreB double protofilament from the MD simulation as C=kBTσω2, where kB is the Boltzmann constant, T is the absolute temperature, and σω is the SD of the twist angle ω (29). From the twist angle trajectory of replicate ATP-1 (Fig. 3B), larger sampling windows corresponded to smaller values of C (Fig. 3D). In the case of actin, the experimentally measured twist modulus was close to the estimated value from the largest sampling window (29), likely because larger sampling windows captured more physiologically relevant dynamics. We therefore extrapolated the estimates of C and found the baseline of the exponential fit to be 1.0±0.5×103 kBTnm (Fig. 3D); this value is very close to the value of C estimated from the largest sampling window (1.1×103kBTnm). We similarly estimated the bending modulus as B=2.6±0.5×103 kBTnm (Fig. 3E). Replicate ATP-2 had similar mechanical properties, with C=1.6±0.1×103 kBTnm and B=2.8±0.3×103 kBTnm. We further compared these values with short simulations (~100 ns) using an E. coli MreB (EcMreB) homolog (16). Our extrapolation method worked poorly with the short simulations, but nonetheless the estimated C and B for a fixed 50-ns sampling window were comparable, with C50 ns CcMreB=4.5±1.9×103 kBTnm and C50 ns EcMreB=3.5±1.3×103 kBTnm, B50 ns CcCcMreB=4.7±2.0×103 kBTnm and B50 ns EcMreB=4.5±1.8×103 kBTnm. Thus, EcMreB exhibits similar mechanical properties as CcMreB. In the rest of this work, we use values derived from CcMreB for future calculations, since most of our simulations were performed with CcMreB.

A recent study showed that physiologically relevant potassium concentrations (500 mM) reduce filament bending in MreB from a thermophilic Gram-positive species (33). Therefore, we repeated our CcMreB simulations in the presence of 500 mM KCl. In these simulations (~100 ns and ~250 ns for two replicates), MreB bending and twist angles remained similar as in the original simulations (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Using a 50-ns window, the corresponding C and B remained similar to the original simulations without potassium: CKCl,50 ns=5.2±1.8×103 kBTnm and BKCl,50ns=5.8±1.3×103 kBTnm. Thus, in our simulation system, additional potassium ions do not seem to alter filament mechanical properties.

While experimental measurements for C and B in MreB or other actin homologs are lacking, we compared our estimates to in vitro measurements of C and B for actin filaments: The measured C ranges from 2.3×10-27 N m2 (34) to 8×10-26 N m2 (35), corresponding to 0.6×103 kBTnm to 2×103 kBTnm; and the persistence length of actin filaments was ~9 µm across multiple studies (36–38), equivalent to B=104 kBTnm. Thus, our estimated C for MreB is comparable to that of actin, while B is ~four-fold lower. The lower bending modulus for MreB could be due to its unique filament structure, in which the intraprotofilament interfaces occur at the same position in the two protofilaments (14), creating a “hinge” that is relatively easy to bend. In contrast, the intraprotofilament interfaces are offset in the two protofilaments of actin (29), potentially strengthening its protofilaments.

Since MreB protofilament conformation can be affected by nucleotide binding and interacting proteins (16, 27), we analyzed other simulations from our previous study (15) of MreB double protofilaments bound to ADP or RodZ (SI Appendix, Table S1). These simulations, initiated from the same crystal structure, either replaced the binding nucleotide with ADP, or constructed a homology model for the cytoplasmic tail of C. cresentus RodZ [RodZ(1–87)] from the cocrystal structure of Thermotoga maritima MreB and RodZ (PDB ID: 2WUS) and aligned it to each MreB subunit (16). ADP binding reduced twist, and RodZ-binding caused an opposite, right-handed (negative) twist (15). Thus, ATP hydrolysis or RodZ binding alters the conformation of MreB protofilaments by reducing filament twist and therefore potentially affects MreB’s function in geometric sensing. Across these simulations, intersubunit twist angles ranged from −7.1° to 12.4° and bending angles from 0.1° to 2.7° (Fig. 3F), with no correlations between them (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Given an MreB monomer length of ~5 nm (14), these values correspond to twist per unit length ω = −0.0246 to 0.0433 rad/nm, and curvature k = 0.0004 to 0.0095 rad/nm for MreB double protofilaments. The bending and twist moduli of these protofilaments were similar to those of the ATP-bound protofilaments. These parameters provide the foundation for predicting MreB localization using our filament energetics model.

Model Prediction of MreB Curvature Enrichment.

Using local geometry measurements (Fig. 2B) and the estimated conformational and mechanical properties of MreB protofilaments (Fig. 3 B–F), we used our model to predict MreB localization and compared these predictions to experimental observations. We imaged an E. coli strain expressing mreB fused to the fast-folding fluorescent protein msfGFP as the sole copy, and quantified MreB abundance as a function of the local mean and Gaussian curvatures (Methods). Consistent with previous studies (9, 11, 27), MreB was enriched at lower curvatures (Fig. 4A). Since mean and Gaussian curvatures are largely correlated within the cell, the curvature dependency holds true for both mean and Gaussian curvatures (Fig. 4 A, Bottom and Left Insets).

To compare model results with experimental measurements, we calculated the landscape of MreB elastic energy per unit length at each accessible combination of mean and Gaussian curvatures following Eq. 4, with intermediate twist and bending angles (twist angle 7.5° and bending angle 1.0°, corresponding to ω = 0.0262 rad/nm, k = 0.0035 rad/nm, Fig. 3F). We assumed that MreB filaments always fully bind to the membrane, and therefore that membrane binding energy is constant. Thus, the minimized local energy is E∗H,KG,L=Lminθfθ,H,KG, where L is the MreB filament length, which we assumed for this analysis to be constant. MreB localization preference is therefore set by a Boltzmann distribution[6]pH,KG,L∝e-E∗kBT=e-L minθ fθ, H, KGkBT,

in which the only unknown parameter is L. For each combination of H and KG accessible in cells (Fig. 2E), we calculated the corresponding localization preference at that geometry using Eq. 6, which is a function of L. We then calculated the optimal value of L that minimizes the mean square error between the model and experiment, yielding a filament length L = 47 nm, which is a reasonable match to experimental measurements (Fig. 4B). Importantly, the successful fit depends on the coupling of H and KG; if KG is fixed at 0 (representing a perfect cylinder), then the model predicts that filament elastic energy would be higher at lower values of H (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) and hence that MreB is enriched at higher mean curvature, opposite to our measurements. Due to the constraint of the surface geometry, the model predicts that MreB filaments indeed deform from the strain-free conformation to bind to the membrane (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). When projected onto the mean or Gaussian curvature, the model matched the experimental measurements well (Fig. 4 B, Bottom and Left Insets). However, closer inspection revealed a quantitative difference at low curvatures. For instance, at zero Gaussian curvature, MreB was enriched at lower mean curvatures in experimental measurements (Fig. 4A), opposite to the model prediction (Fig. 4B), introducing ~17% variations between experiment and model. We note that in such cases with zero Gaussian curvature, the regions with relatively high or low mean curvatures correspond to different widths, thus are likely measured from different cells across a range of widths (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). Wider cells typically had higher MreB concentrations (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B), which could affect MreB localization due to competition for binding sites or interfilament interactions not considered in our model. Variations in cell width led to 10 to 20% variation in MreB concentration (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B), sufficient to cause the discrepancies observed between model and experiment at a fixed Gaussian curvature, but the variation is relatively small compared to the overall range of MreB abundance across all accessible geometries.

We further explored how twist and bending angles affect the overall fit and estimated filament length L by performing the same fit with different ω and k values (Fig. 3F). The length diagram is symmetric in ω, since left- and right-handed twists can generate the same extent of shape selection. For large k and small absolute ω, such that filament curvature dominates over twist, fits were particularly poor, characterized by L→0 and large fit residues (Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). The poor fit was because the elastic energy cost to unbend the filament becomes too large, and hence, higher cell surface curvatures are energetically more favorable (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). Nonetheless, most combinations of ω and k yielded reasonable agreement with experimental data and predicted MreB filament lengths ranging from tens to a few hundred nm (Fig. 4C), consistent with in vivo measurements (16). Varying the bending and twist moduli B and C altered fit values for L quantitatively but did not change the overall curvature localization pattern (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 E and F). Many of the ω and k values of individual simulation states (Fig. 3F) yielded reasonable fits and predicted similar ranges of filament lengths (Fig. 4 C–E), although some with relatively low twist but large bending resulted in L→0 with poor fits (Fig. 4C). These states may be relevant to stressful conditions, as starved cells exhibit MreB enrichment at high-curvature cell poles (27). Thus, although some conformational states lead to unrealistic enrichment results in fast-growing cells, they may be relevant when cells encounter physiological stresses.

Filament Twist Is Critical for Curvature Sensing.

We then asked how incorporating filament twist in our model affects its performance. Without twist (ω = 0), the model predicts enrichment of MreB at higher curvatures when k > 0.0016 rad/nm (bending angle >0.46°; Fig. 4C and SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). MreB bending angle was >0.46° in most MD simulations (Fig. 3F), suggesting that intrinsic twist is critical for MreB enrichment at lower curvatures. In the regime with low bending angles, while filament bending alone can explain the qualitative pattern of MreB enrichment, the best model fit exhibits large errors (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C), and the range of predicted filament length changes rapidly with small changes in bending angles (Fig. 4F). Bending angle can fluctuate by ~0.6° in MD simulations (Fig. 3 C, Right), and without twist these fluctuations can change filament length by >300 nm (Fig. 4 F, Inset). Therefore, intrinsic twist permits wider ranges of bending angles and enables more robust geometry sensing when bending angle fluctuates.

Curvature-Dependent Localization of MreB Is a Statistical Behavior Independent of MreB Motion.

During steady-state growth, MreB filaments in E. coli (8) and Bacillus subtilis (6, 7) move in an approximately circumferential direction, and the speed correlated with the rate of cell-wall insertion and therefore bacterial growth. Disrupting insertion using the PBP2 inhibitor mecillinam slows MreB motion (8). It has been proposed that such circumferential motion of MreB around a curved tube could by itself bias localization to negative curvature, since the inwardly curved side of the bent tube/toroid (with negative curvature) would accumulate a higher MreB density (19, 20). This proposed mechanism assumes that the curvatures on opposing sides of the cell body are anticorrelated, as in a perfectly curved toroid. We sought to test this critical assumption of anticorrelated curvature on opposing sides of the cell body. We treated cells with the division inhibitor cephalexin to prevent division sites and excluded cell poles from our analyses. Along the cell body, we did not observe any correlations of curvatures on the opposing sides (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A), consistent with our previous observations (11). In fact, for ~50% of the cell body, the curvatures on the opposing sides were either both positive or both negative, forming local bulges and indentations (Fig. 5A). For cells not treated with cephalexin, future division sites generated some correlation when considering only negative contour curvatures, although again no correlation was observed across all curvatures (SI Appendix, Fig. S6B). Therefore, unlike an idealized bent tube (Fig. 2A), real E. coli cells do not exhibit negatively correlated curvatures on opposite sides of the cell body, and circumferential motion alone does not explain MreB localization.

We therefore sought to test whether MreB exhibits a curvature preference in the absence of motion. We grew exponential-phase E. coli cells in a microfluidic flow cell and treated them with mecillinam, which effectively halted MreB movement, as evidenced by high MreB fluorescence autocorrelation (Fig. 5B). While maintaining the presence of mecillinam, we treated the cells with A22 (40) to rapidly depolymerize MreB (Fig. 5 C and D). After 15 min, we washed away A22 and observed MreB repolymerization onto the membrane. The re-formed MreB filaments did not move due to the continued presence of mecillinam (Fig. 5 C and D). The precise locations of MreB filaments before and after A22 treatment were not correlated (Fig. 5E), indicating that MreB adopts a different pattern upon repolymerization. Nonetheless, the statistical profile of MreB enrichment as a function of curvature remained essentially the same (Fig. 5F), indicating that motion is not necessary for MreB’s bias to low mean curvature. Thus, MreB localization can be explained by its energetic preference for local surface geometries with low mean and Gaussian curvature, which is dictated by the mechanical properties of MreB filaments.

Discussion

Bacterial cells face a balance between maintenance of their cell size and shape and the benefits of modulating their morphology to adapt to changing growth conditions. Components that define the pattern of cell-wall synthesis must respond to any shape defects and reorganize downstream machinery to reinforce the preferred shape of a cell as it grows. Here, we demonstrated that the equilibrium energetics of MreB filaments are sufficient to recapitulate the observed localization of MreB to negative curvatures (Fig. 4), which acts as a negative feedback against bending fluctuations. Our results focused on exponentially growing cells, in which shape homeostasis may be particularly important; it will be intriguing to determine whether our energetic model is sufficient to predict MreB localization in other physiological states in which cell shape is altered.

Although the circumferential movement of MreB has been proposed to play a role in its observed localization to negative curvatures, we demonstrated that the statistical distribution of MreB localization is preserved even when motion is fully suppressed (Fig. 5), indicating that motion is not required and suggesting that MreB energetics alone may be sufficient for MreB localization. A mechanochemical model of cell wall growth (41) revealed that MreB movement can help to maintain a rod-like shape by smoothing out variations in cell-wall insertion when small morphological defects occur (8). However, it is challenging to rationalize the role of movement when cells severely deviate from a rod-like shape; for instance, in regions with variable cell width, movement can retain memory of the local geometry and hence could end up propagating such defects. A simulation framework that incorporates MreB movement and cell-wall dynamics under large perturbations and in abnormally shaped cells (12) will be informative in understanding the role of MreB movement in rod-shape regulation.

While our model can quantitatively predict most features of the observed pattern of MreB localization (Fig. 4), additional variables should be considered to comprehensively understand MreB properties. Our model consistently predicts higher MreB abundances in the polar regions than observed (Fig. 4 B and D), possibly due to mechanisms actively excluding MreB from the poles, such as the enhanced concentration of anionic phospholipids (42). Moreover, the mechanical parameters we extracted from MD simulations (Fig. 3) may not completely reflect the properties of MreB. A recent in vitro study of MreB from a thermophilic Gram-positive bacterium, which shares 55.2% identity with EcMreB and 56.9% identity with CcMreB, showed that physiologically relevant potassium concentrations can reduce filament bending (33). To predict whether potassium would have a similar effect on CcMreB, we carried out MD simulations with 500 mM KCl and observed no significant changes to filament conformation or mechanical properties (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Nonetheless, other ions or solutes in the cytosol could modulate the mechanical properties of MreB filaments. Other MreB-interacting proteins like RodZ (25) and PBP2 (10) may also alter its energetic landscape. Moving forward, more accurate biochemical measurements of MreB and protein structural insights provided by cryoelectron microscopy experiments and protein structure prediction tools like AlphaFold (43) will provide a more comprehensive picture of MreB filament properties. Specifically, the hydrolysis from ATP to ADP in MreB filaments reduces filament twist, and binding of the cytoplasmic tail of RodZ can even reverse the chirality of twist (Fig. 3F). It remains to be discovered how hydrolysis in vivo modulates the conformational states and localization of MreB. Also, the ratio of copy numbers between MreB and RodZ depends on cellular growth rate (27), suggesting that cells actively modify the fraction of RodZ-bound MreB subunits across growth phases, which in turn modulates MreB’s geometric preference and thereby impacts cell shape. Therefore, accurate biochemical measurements of MreB in vivo are needed to identify the MreB filament hydrolysis and RodZ-binding state, which our model predicts would alter the filament bending and twist angles. Moreover, better structural models, including the interaction of MreB and full-length RodZ (with its membrane-bound domains) as well as other Rod complex members like MreC, MreD, RodA, and PBP2 (44), may improve characterization of MreB filament properties in its native environment. Such interactions may modulate the energetic landscape of MreB in a growth-dependent fashion (27) and shed light on how MreB integrates with other cellular components to dynamically regulate cell shape.

Beyond the limitations of MD simulations, the statistical properties of MreB localization can realistically only be measured across many cells. MreB abundance can vary across cells (e.g., as a function of cell width; SI Appendix, Fig. S4B), which can alter its localization and affect the accuracy of model predictions. To calculate the energy landscape of filament localization (Fig. 1), we assume that MreB filaments are of fixed length and short compared to variations in surface geometry, and that MreB strongly adsorbs to the membrane. In reality, variation in filament length due to (de)polymerization would allow a single filament to sample different local geometries with modified energy landscapes. Moreover, weaker membrane adsorption could produce filaments that are only partially membrane bound, potentially with nonuniform morphologies in very long filaments (2, 45), or could limit polymerization length due to energetic competition between polymerization and membrane binding (16). Previous models considering partial membrane binding have focused on cases with zero Gaussian curvatures (i.e., perfect cylinders) (2, 16). In such a simplified scenario, given the parameters for MreB protofilaments in our study, we estimate the critical membrane binding potential for MreB is close to Vc=π2Kω024=1.7kBTnm (2). From MD simulations of membrane-bound double protofilaments, MreB is estimated to have a membrane binding potential of V=4 kBTnm (16), larger than Vc. Thus, our assumption of a fully membrane-bound filament likely holds for MreB. However, this assumption can break down for other filaments. The tubulin homolog FtsZ forms much longer filaments and has a relatively small membrane binding potential and hence may not fully adhere to the membrane (2), thus requiring a modified model in which the local geometry along the filament can vary. We expect that future enhancements to our model incorporating more realistic factors will further enhance predictions of cytoskeletal protein localization patterns, potentially in more complex geometries (12).

Is twist important beyond MreB? Evolutionarily, twist is a natural state of many polymers regardless of function (46, 47), and our study shows that a twisted polymer has intrinsic geometric preferences (Fig. 1 and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). Thus, cells may be able to appropriate polymers with suitable twist states for geometric sensing. In the case of MreB, point mutations can alter filament twist, which in turn changes cell morphologies and the pattern of MreB localization (9, 16). In addition, changes to filament bending and twist when bound to surfaces can expose moieties responsible for other downstream processes (48), and filament untwisting could enhance parallel filament–filament interactions (49). These downstream effects could lead to autocatalytic amplification of the geometric sensing induced by filaments. The approach used in this study can also be generalized to other membrane-interacting systems with intrinsic twist and/or bend. For instance, the actin homolog FtsA directly binds to the membrane and anchors other divisome components including the tubulin homolog FtsZ. FtsA single protofilaments exhibit similar conformational changes as MreB single protofilaments (50), and a recent study showed that FtsA can also form antiparallel double protofilaments (51). Thus, our model can potentially reveal the curvature-sensing properties of FtsA and how it guides the location of the divisome. Twist can be introduced into FtsZ filaments via point mutations, altering their localization and resulting in perturbations to cell division and shape (52). The archaeal cytoskeletal protein crenactin, a closer homolog of actin than MreB, forms helical structures along cell peripheries to maintain rod-like cell shape (53). Crenactin filaments exhibit large right-handed twist in MD simulations (50), suggesting a potential link between its filament conformation and curvature-dependent localization. In eukaryotes, the endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT) proteins assemble on negatively curved membranes to facilitate membrane fission. During this process, ESCRT-III subunits assemble as a double-stranded helical complex. The binding partners of ESCRT-III can change filament structure and thereby allow it to sample a wide variety of targeted membrane geometries (54). In all these cases, intrinsic twist and bend of these membrane-associated filaments potentially underline their geometry-sensing properties. Future studies will provide insights into whether localization of these and other filaments is also determined by energetics or requires factors such as movement (55, 56).

The general physical principles demonstrated in our work also empower de novo material designs. Our model of surface binding of filaments with intrinsic twist and curvature is generically applicable to engineering of shape sensors with novel functions. Engineered filaments with unique shapes, elasticities, and substrate binding properties can be used to measure and target specific material interfaces. Current protein design tools can rationally engineer such filaments (57), and their localization can be tested in complex geometries beyond bent toroids using microfluidic devices (58). Ultimately, twisted biopolymers such as DNA origami as well as shape-programmable polymer filaments and spiral nanosheet ribbons (59, 60) could be rationally designed to localize to distinct interfacial environments and even modify membrane geometry (61). Polymers and microfilaments that localize to mesoporous membranes may facilitate selective filtration, and proteins with geometric preferences can serve as scaffolds for tissue engineering. These applications highlight the opportunities for applying polymer physics models such as ours to geometry-sensing material functions.

Methods

Strains and Media.

Strains used in this study are E. coli MG1655 (CGSC #6300) and MG1655 csrD::Km, mreB’::msfGFP-mreB’’ (11, 24). For experiments in Figs. 2 B–E, 4A, and 5A, cells were cultured in lysogeny broth (LB; 10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L NaCl, and 5 g/L yeast extract) and imaged at 37 °C on 1% agarose pads with LB. For experiments in Fig. 5 B–F, cells were cultured at 37 °C in M63 minimal media [2 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 13.6 g/L KH2PO4, 0.0005 g/L FeSO4, and 0.25 g/L MgSO4·7H2O] supplemented with glucose and casamino acids, and imaging was performed in microfluidic channels constructed from two pieces of double-stick tape sandwiched between a coverslip and a glass slide. Cells were adhered to the surface by pretreating the coverslip with 0.1% poly-L-lysine solution for 5 min before adding cell suspension.

Imaging.

For experiments in Figs. 2 B–E, 4A, and 5A, cells from exponentially growing cultures were placed on agarose pads and immediately imaged with a Nikon Ti-E inverted microscope (Nikon Instruments) using a 100X (1.40 NA) oil immersion objective and a Neo 5.5 sCMOS camera (Andor). Images were acquired using µManager v. 1.4 (62).

For experiments in Fig. 5 B–E, images were acquired on a Nikon N-SIM microscope with a 100X (1.49 NA) objective and an Andor DU-897 EMCCD in Slide 3D-SIM reconstruction mode. For experiments in Fig. 5F, images were acquired on a custom monolithic aluminum microscope with a 100X (1.49 NA) objective (Nikon), an Andor DU-897 EMCCD, and custom LabView (National Instruments) software. Three-dimensional images were acquired with a z-stack step of 100 nm, and three-dimensional shape reconstructions were performed using in-house MATLAB software (https://github.com/PrincetonUniversity/shae-cellshape-public) (63).

Single-Cell Segmentation and MreB Enrichment Analyses.

Segmentation and enrichment analyses were performed as described previously (11). Briefly, along the cell contour, image fluorescence along normal vectors of the segmented contour was integrated at ~1 pixel intervals. MreB enrichment profiles as a function of Gaussian curvature in Fig. 5F were calculated from three-dimensional measurements using code available at https://github.com/PrincetonUniversity/shae-cellshape-public. The shape of individual cells was reconstructed by minimizing the image difference between an observed focal stack and the forward convolution of a shape with the observed microscope blur function. More information can be found in SI Appendix.

MreB Autocorrelation Analyses.

For Fig. 5B, 20 images were acquired every 10 s, and individual cell regions were manually cropped out. Pixels were defined as “within a cell” if there was intensity in the pixel above background at any point during the time lapse. These within-cell regions were converted into a one-dimensional list of intensities, preserving their spatial positioning. After background subtraction, photobleaching was corrected by fitting the total intensity in the cell to a single exponential decay. After bleach correction, the autocorrelation curve for each cell was calculated by averaging all pairwise correlation coefficients with a given time lag. For example, for the 10-s time lag, the average correlation coefficient for all nineteen one-lag comparisons (frame 1-2, 2-3, etc.) was calculated. Error bars were estimated via bootstrapping within each individual cell. To compute an ad hoc likelihood that mecillinam treatment resulted in a different autocorrelation decay, we performed a permutation analysis by randomly shuffling the labels and calculating the sum of squared difference between the resulting clusters.

MD Simulations.

The simulations ATP-1, ATP-2, ADP-1, ADP-2, ATP-RodZ(1–87)-1, ATP-RodZ(1–87)-2, EcMreB-1, and EcMreB-2 are from previously published data (16).

The simulations KCl-1 and KCl-2 were performed using the MD package NAMD3.0 Alpha (64). The simulations used the CHARMM36 force field (65), including CMAP corrections (66). The TIP3P model was used for water molecules (67), and long-range electrostatic forces were evaluated by means of the particle–mesh Ewald summation approach with a grid spacing of <1 Å. The integration time step was 2 fs (68). Constant temperature (T = 310 K) and constant pressure (1 atm) were maintained using Langevin dynamics (69) and the Langevin piston algorithm (70). Setup, analysis, and rendering of the simulation systems were performed with the software VMD (71).

Filament Twist and Bending.

For each MD simulation frame, a local coordinate system was defined by three unit vectors d1,d2,d3 as previously described (16). For an MreB double protofilament, d3 is largely parallel to the filament, d2 is perpendicular to the surface of MreB membrane binding, and d1=d3×d2. Twist angle is the rotation angle around d3 between the middle two MreB doublets. Positive values denote left-handed twist. Total bending of the filament is calculated by fitting the centers of mass of the four doublet pairs. Bending angle is the projection of total bending to the rotation around d2. The rotation around d1 is relatively small and fluctuates around zero.

Identifying Simulation Twist States.

Twist states were defined using the change-point analysis algorithm Steppi (31, 32). For our analyses, parameters were chosen as follows (15): state level μ=unfixed, level slope α=0, nearest-neighbor coupling ϵ=0.6, noise “stiffness” k=0.2.
