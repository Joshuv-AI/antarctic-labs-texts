Strength enhancement of calcium carbide residue-stabilized clay with perlite powder and water treatment sludge
Authors: Atbin Mohammadi Darestani, Mahyar Arabani, Payam Zanganeh Ranjbar, Meghdad Payan, Mahdi Salimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Clay soils, particularly kaolinite, often exhibit low strength and high deformation, necessitating stabilization for geotechnical applications. This study investigates the potential of calcium carbide residue (CCR) and its partial substitution with perlite powder (PP) and water treatment sludge (WTS) to enhance the strength and microstructure of kaolinite clay. To capture both mechanical response and structural evolution, unconfined compressive strength (UCS) and ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) tests were conducted at 7, 28, and 56 days of curing. The microstructural changes were also investigated using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analyses to reveal cementitious gel formation. The findings show that the addition of 4% CCR increased UCS by about 10 times to 1395 kPa, while partial replacement with 25% PP and 10% WTS further enhanced UCS by nearly 14 times, reaching 1783 and 1744 kPa, respectively. Similarly, UPV increased from approximately 280 m/s in untreated clay to over 1500 m/s in stabilized blends, indicating a denser and more cohesive microstructure. The results of secant modulus (E50) and energy absorption capacity (Eu) followed the same trend as UCS. Partial replacement of CCR with 25% PP led to the highest long-term improvements, producing E50 of 1021 MPa and Eu of 535 kJ/m3, while substitution with 10% WTS also yielded substantial gains, reaching an E50 of 1011 MPa and an Eu of 520 kJ/m3. These enhancements corresponded to increases of up to 18 times and 9 times over untreated soil, confirming greater stiffness but a shift toward more brittle behavior. Microstructural analysis revealed the formation of tightly bonded cementitious gels and superior particle packing, which directly account for the pronounced improvements in the strength and stiffness of the stabilized samples. Overall, the results demonstrate that CCR, particularly when partially substituted with PP or WTS, presents a strong potential as a sustainable alternative to conventional cement-based stabilizers, offering significant improvement in soil performance along with environmental benefits.

Introduction

Kaolinite soil is a prominent earthen material widely used in the construction field, characterized by low resistance to shear and tension strengths, along with a propensity for volumetric changes. These inherent properties hinder its performance in various engineering applications1. As a member of the clay soil group, kaolinite often requires stabilization to improve its suitability for geo-structural purposes. Its poor performance under applied stresses is primarily due to its relatively loose microstructure, which contributes to its instability. To address these weaknesses, stabilization techniques are crucial for enhancing the structural behavior of kaolinite, making it more favorable for engineering uses2. Stabilization can effectively increase the soil’s shear strength or modify its shrink-swell behavior, thereby increasing the structural load capacity of subgrades used in pavements and foundations3–5. Consequently, exploring a range of stabilizing agents is essential to optimize the chemical reactions involved and achieve more durable and resilient soil structures. Conventional stabilizers, such as cement and lime, produce cementitious compounds that promote the binding of soil particles, thereby improving the mechanical properties of the soil. This process enhances the stability and strength of the soil, making it more suitable for construction applications6.

Despite the availability and low cost of these materials, their utilization has detrimental environmental impacts, including the generation of elevated CO2 levels. This issue is particularly critical in geotechnical engineering, where groundworks, a key component of construction projects, require substantial amounts of energy, thereby amplifying the overall environmental footprint7,8. In this context, several eco-friendly alternatives to traditional binders have been investigated, including fly ash, calcium carbide residue (CCR), water treatment sludge (WTS), perlite powder (PP), steel slags, and silica fume, all of which offer promising potential for reducing environmental impact while substantially enhancing soil performance9–13. Among these alternatives, CCR stands out as a by-product derived from acetylene gas manufacturing, characterized by a considerable concentration of Ca(OH)2. Given that clay soils are abundant in natural pozzolans, specifically silica and alumina, CCR can be effectively employed as a stabilizing agent for these soils14. With its beneficial physical, chemical, and mineral composition, CCR presents itself as a sustainable and practical solution for soil stabilization, offering both economic and functional advantages15,16.

Another promising material is WTS, which is a residual by-product generated during the decantation and filtration stages of raw water treatment processes. This material primarily consists of suspended solids, organic matter, and various impurities that have been removed from the water, in addition to residual treatment chemicals such as coagulants and flocculants. Due to its composition and physical properties, WTS has attracted increasing attention as a potential resource for valorization in various environmental and industrial applications, thereby contributing to waste minimization and sustainable material management17. Recent studies have extensively investigated the feasibility of repurposing WTS as a sustainable soil stabilizer, aiming to enhance the geotechnical properties of soils such as shear strength, bearing capacity, and compressibility. This approach not only provides an environmentally responsible method for managing WTS disposal but also offers a cost-effective alternative to conventional soil stabilization materials, thereby supporting circular economy principles in geotechnical engineering4,18,19. Notably, this approach carries significant environmental implications, as it offers a green alternative to conventional chemical stabilizers that are often linked to harmful environmental effects20.

In addition to CCR and WTS, PP has also attracted attention due to its favorable pozzolanic characteristics. PP is a vitreous volcanic rock rich in SiO₂ and Al₂O₃, and its amorphous, non-crystalline structure allows it to chemically react with calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂) in the presence of water to form cementitious binding phases that enhance soil stability21–24. Owing to its lightweight and porous nature, PP finds widespread application across multiple industries, including construction, petrochemicals, and horticulture. In the construction sector, expanded perlite (EP) is commonly incorporated as an additive in cementitious materials, either as a partial substitute for conventional aggregates or as a lightweight filler. Moreover, it serves as a potential precursor material for the synthesis of geopolymers, which are increasingly recognized for their sustainability and performance characteristics in construction applications25.

Recent studies have demonstrated the significant impact of lightweight and pozzolanic additives on the performance of cementitious composites in soil stabilization26,27. Their findings indicate that incorporating such materials can substantially enhance mechanical properties, including compressive and tensile strengths, while improving durability and thermal efficiency. These results highlight the dual role of lightweight and pozzolanic materials in simultaneously enhancing mechanical performance and thermal behavior, providing strong justification for exploring similar stabilization strategies in sustainable soil improvement. Research by Latifi et al.28 on sustainable CCR use in soil stabilization investigated a significant enhancement in unconfined compressive strength (UCS) upon the addition of CCR to the soil mixture. Their results showed that after 28 days of curing, the UCS increased approximately 3.8-fold, reaching 460 kPa. After 60 days, the strength further increased 5.3-fold to 640 kPa, and after 90 days of curing, the UCS reached 700 kPa, corresponding to a 5.8-fold improvement. Bawab et al.29 reviewed the use of CCR in construction materials and soil stabilization and found that incorporating CCR at low replacement levels (5–10%) generally improves mechanical performance and material properties. The findings emphasize CCR’s potential in enhancing strength and cohesion in treated soils, while also identifying gaps for future research on durability and long-term performance. Moreover, Yu et al.30 investigated the use of CCR-based stabilizers and demonstrated that they significantly enhance the UCS of treated soils. Laboratory tests showed that CSC-stabilized soils achieved much higher UCS values compared to lime-stabilized soils, and field trials confirmed these improvements, indicating stronger and more cohesive soil behavior. These findings highlight the potential of CCR as an effective and sustainable soil stabilizer. Similarly, a study by Saldanha et al.15 explored the use of CCR as a sustainable chemical additive for soil improvement. The researchers concluded that CCR primarily consists of portlandite minerals and exhibits features similar to commercial lime, while being non-hazardous. Furthermore, they found that CCR displays remarkable physical, mineralogical, and chemical properties, making it a cost-efficient and environmentally benign alternative for soil stabilization. In another study, Horpibulsuk et al.31 investigated the employment of CCR as a cementing agent for problematic soils and found that it enhances the chemical bonding between clay particles. They also reported that CCR has a high Ca(OH)2 content of approximately 76.7%, making it suitable for improving problematic clayey soils, particularly those with high levels of natural pozzolanic materials. Takao et al.32 examined the viability of incorporating WTS as a partial replacement for lime and cement in the stabilization of silty sand, suggesting potential applications in pavement construction. Based on California Bearing Ratio (CBR) evaluations, the optimal mixture composition was determined to be 15% WTS in combination with soil, sludge and lime. Calik and Sadoglu33 conducted a study to examine the effects of perlite, both alone and in combination with lime, on the classification, shear strength, and long-term stability of expansive soils rich in smectite clay minerals. Their UCS tests showed that lime-treated samples incorporating over 30% PP possessed satisfactory durability and shear resistance. Moreover, Hosniyeh et al.34 demonstrated that incorporating perlite into problematic soils significantly enhanced soil performance. The addition of PP increased UCS and shear strength, while substantially reducing swelling and consolidation settlement. These findings indicate that PP is an effective pozzolanic additive for improving the strength, stability, and durability of fine or saline soils.

A critical literature review reveals that while the individual effects of CCR, PP, or WTS on soil stabilization have been documented, no prior research has explored their combinatorial impact or the microstructural mechanisms governing their synergy in clayey soils. The present study uniquely addresses this gap by evaluating the impact of these hybrid additives on UCS and ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) over short, medium, and long-term curing periods (7, 28, and 56 days). Using mechanical tests and advanced microstructural techniques, including scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), this research not only quantifies performance improvements but also provides micro-level evidence of gel formation and structural enhancement. The novel combination of these waste by-products offers a sustainable solution to conventional soil stabilization methods, effectively balancing engineering performance with ecological considerations in geotechnical applications. In this study, after incorporating CCR into clay soil at concentrations ranging from 1 to 16%, mechanical test results are analyzed to identify the most beneficial inclusion percentage. This optimal content is then replaced with varying proportions of PP (i.e., 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%) and WTS (i.e., 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, 25%). Finally, microstructural analyses are conducted on the optimal mixtures to confirm the structural improvements and the formation of cementitious gels resulting from the inclusion of these waste materials.

Calcium carbide residue (CCR)

The CCR was sourced from the Sapragas factory located in Karaj, Iran. Initially, the material was oven-dried at 110 °C and subsequently sieved through a No. 40 mesh to achieve a uniform particle size. The processed material exhibited a specific gravity of 2.24, indicative of its relatively low unit weight. The chemical composition and PSD curve of the CCR are presented in Table 2 and Fig. 1, respectively.

Water treatment sludge (WTS)

The WTS used in this investigation was collected as a wet mixture from a refinery located in Rasht, Iran. In the initial preparation phase, the sludge underwent oven drying at 110 °C for one full day. Following this drying process, the material was pulverized and subsequently passed through a No. 40 mesh sieve. The processed WTS exhibited a specific gravity of 2.41. The chemical properties and PSD curve of WTS are presented in Table 2 and Fig. 1, respectively. In chemical composition of the WTS in Table 2, All oxide contents fall within the typical range reported for WTS from municipal water treatment plants. The LOI value of 19.8% indicates moderate volatile content, mainly from carbonates and minor organics, which did not adversely affect the pozzolanic reactions or the mechanical behavior of the stabilized soil. Moreover, any trace impurities present in the WTS are expected to be immobilized within the cementitious matrix formed during stabilization, ensuring environmentally safe reuse of this by-product.

Perlite powder (PP)

The PP utilized in this study was sourced from a manufacturing site positioned in Tehran, Iran. The sample was placed in an oven prior to use and the PP used in this study was extremely fine and passed completely through a No. 200 (0.075 mm) sieve. With a specific gravity of 1.30, the material is considered lightweight in geotechnical terms. The chemical composition of PP and its PSD are detailed in Table 2 and Fig. 1, respectively.

Sample preparation & mix proportions

Initially, different percentages of CCR (1%, 2%, 4%, 8%, and 16%) were incorporated into the soil, followed by standard compaction tests according to ASTM D69838 to determine the OMC and MDD. The selection of CCR contents (1%, 2%, 4%, 8%, and 16%) was guided by previous research and aimed to capture both the increasing and decreasing trends in strength development. Studies such as Saldanha et al.15 have reported an optimum CCR content of around 6%, while Latifi et al.28 used similar ranges to define performance boundaries. Accordingly, the chosen dosages covered sub-optimum to supra-optimum levels to evaluate mechanical performance, pozzolanic activity, and economic feasibility. For PP and WTS, substitution levels were selected to systematically examine their influence on CCR-based stabilization. PP replacements up to 100% and WTS up to 25% were tested to fully capture both beneficial and adverse effects and to determine the optimum substitution level for each material.

After that, the materials were placed in an oven set to 110 °C for 24 h to eliminate moisture. Following this 24-h oven exposure, the materials were allowed to reach equilibrium with the ambient temperature. Based on the results obtained from the compaction test, CCR was blended with the dry soil using a spatula, and water was then added to ensure uniform distribution throughout the soil and additives. The mixture was then compacted in three successive layers within the UCS mold before being ejected using a hydraulic jack. A cylindrical specimen (38 mm × 76 mm) was prepared and subsequently wrapped in plastic to retain moisture. It was then placed in a controlled environment with a minimum temperature of 25 °C for curing, subjected to curing periods of 7, 28, and 56 days. After completing the curing periods, the samples were subjected to UPV testing in accordance with ASTM C59740 and UCS testing following ASTM D216639 to determine the optimal percentage of CCR through strength analysis.

In the next phase, PP and WTS were introduced as replacements for CCR, utilizing substitution percentages of 0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% for PP, and 0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% for WTS, based on the previously established optimum CCR content. Compaction tests were conducted again to ascertain the MDD and OMC for these new mixtures. In accordance with the results, a comparable pattern, as previously described, was observed during the UCS tests, and the optimal percentages of CCR–WTS and CCR–PP were determined. Testing procedure

A comprehensive experimental program was designed to evaluate the geotechnical behavior and microstructural characteristics of the stabilized soil mixtures. The testing procedure was carried out in two main phases, focusing on mechanical, ultrasonic, and microstructural analyses.

In the first phase, the mechanical properties of the stabilized mixtures were assessed using the standard Proctor compaction, UPV, and UCS tests. Following ASTM D69838, the mixtures underwent compaction tests to determine their respective OMC and MDD values. For the UPV testing, procedures followed the ASTM C59740 guidelines. Given the non-destructive nature of UPV, the specimens were first subjected to ultrasonic measurements prior to compressive strength testing. The weight and length of each specimen were measured, and their surfaces were lubricated to enhance signal propagation and ensure reliable measurements. The time required for the compression wave to traverse each specimen was recorded, and the UPV was calculated using the formula provided by Mandal et al.41:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$UPV = \frac{L}{\Delta t}$$\end{document}

Following the UPV tests, the UCS tests were conducted in accordance with ASTM D216639, where cylindrical specimens were subjected to unconfined axial compression until failure. At a constant deformation rate of 1.27 mm/min, the specimens were compressed up to 15% axial strain while recording the maximum compressive resistance.

In addition to mechanical testing, a series of microstructural and chemical analyses were conducted on the optimized samples, which were ground into a fine powder. These tests were performed to explore the interactions within the stabilized matrix. Surface morphology and elemental composition were analyzed using SEM–EDS, while chemical bonding characteristics were examined through FTIR-XRD analysis also revealed the crystalline structures formed during stabilization. Results and discussion

Compaction tests

Figure 4 illustrates the compaction test results for samples stabilized with CCR, CCR-PP, and CCR-WTS. As shown in Fig. 4a, increasing the CCR content in the soil mixtures results in a noticeable rise in OMC accompanied by a decrease in MDD. This phenomenon is predominantly due to the density difference, as CCR has a lower specific gravity than the base kaolinite material. The addition of CCR introduces more voids and requires greater moisture for effective compaction; hence, the OMC increases. This pattern aligns well with the findings of Latifi et al.28, confirming that substituting heavier soil particles with lighter CCR results in such compaction behavior.Fig. 4MDD and OMC trends for the samples treated by (a) CCR, (b) CCR-PP, and (c) CCR-WTS.

MDD and OMC trends for the samples treated by (a) CCR, (b) CCR-PP, and (c) CCR-WTS.

In Fig. 4b, the effect of incorporating PP into the CCR-stabilized soil is examined. Similar to the CCR-only samples, the addition of PP results in higher OMC and reduced MDD. This behavior is largely due to the inherent properties of PP, which is a lightweight material with a relatively high surface area and cohesive nature42. PP also has a significant silica and alumina content23, contributing to its water absorption capacity. The combined influence of low specific gravity and high water affinity causes the CCR-PP mixtures to require more water for optimal compaction while yielding a lower dry density.

In contrast, Fig. 4c demonstrates a different trend when CCR is partially replaced with WTS. Here, the MDD increases while the OMC decreases. This contrasting trend results from WTS having a greater specific gravity than both CCR and PP. WTS particles are generally denser and less porous, leading to a more compact mixture with lower water demand. The decrease in OMC suggests improved compaction efficiency, while the increase in MDD indicates a denser final structure. This observation is consistent with previous research43, which highlighted the beneficial effects of WTS on enhancing the physical properties of treated soils.

Unconfined compression tests

Stress–strain behavior

Figure 5 present the stress–strain behavior of untreated soil and stabilized specimens with 4% CCR, as well as with varying percentages of PP and WTS. In Fig. 5a, the untreated soil exhibits a very low UCS and a relatively large failure strain, indicating ductile but weak behavior. Upon stabilization with 4% CCR, there is a significant enhancement in strength and a reduction in failure strain. Furthermore, the specimen cured for 28 days exhibited higher peak strength but a slightly lower strain at failure compared to the 7-day cured specimen. This observation aligns with the common understanding that longer curing periods allow more complete pozzolanic reactions and cementitious bond formation, leading to denser and stiffer soil matrices44. As curing progresses, the formation of calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) and calcium aluminate hydrate (C–A–H) gels densifies the soil matrix, reduces porosity and plastic deformation, and increases overall stiffness45.Fig. 5Stress–strain curves for (a) untreated soil and soils treated with CCR, (b) 7-day soil samples treated with CCR-PP, (c) 28-day soil samples treated with CCR-PP, (d) 7-day soil samples treated with CCR-WTS samples, (e) 28-day soil samples treated with CCR-WTS.

Stress–strain curves for (a) untreated soil and soils treated with CCR, (b) 7-day soil samples treated with CCR-PP, (c) 28-day soil samples treated with CCR-PP, (d) 7-day soil samples treated with CCR-WTS samples, (e) 28-day soil samples treated with CCR-WTS.

Figure 5b and Fig. 5c illustrate the effect of incorporating various amounts of PP into the 4% CCR-treated soil. A moderate addition of PP (e.g., 25%) slightly increases UCS and enhances ductility. However, beyond 25%, there is a noticeable decrease in compressive strength and an increase in failure strain. This behavior can be attributed to the excessive addition of PP disrupting the continuity of the soil matrix. The lightweight and porous nature of PP, while initially beneficial for enhancing soil workability and reducing weight, eventually leads to a reduction in structural integrity when added in large amounts46,47. Higher PP content results in a weaker composite with a more flexible structure, causing a reduction in peak stress and an increase in strain at failure.

Figure 5d and Fig. 5e illustrate the effect of different percentages of WTS on the UCS behavior of the 4% CCR-stabilized soil. Similar to the behavior observed with PP, low percentages of WTS (5–10%) result in a slight increase in compressive strength and improved post-peak ductility. However, when the WTS content exceeds 10%, there is a significant decrease in UCS, and the material becomes more ductile. This phenomenon can be explained by the presence of organic matter and fine particles in WTS, which increase the plasticity of the soil matrix48–50.

At higher percentages of WTS, the soil matrix becomes more flexible, reducing the overall stiffness of the composite material. The introduction of these fine and potentially organic particles might absorb some deformation but lack the necessary strength properties to enhance compressive resistance. Overall, these results demonstrate that both curing time and the optimal content of reinforcing materials are crucial factors in balancing strength and ductility in stabilized soils. An excessive amount of flexible inclusions, although beneficial for strain capacity, may severely impair composite strength. In the case of PP (beyond 25%) and WTS (beyond 10%), the composite becomes more ductile but significantly weaker, highlighting the need for careful control over the quantities of these materials in soil stabilization.

UCS

Figure 6 depicts the impact of CCR content on the UCS of soil across varying CCR concentrations and curing durations. The addition of 0–4% CCR increased the UCS of untreated soil. The UCS data indicate that the optimal CCR content for stabilizing kaolinite soil is 4%, in agreement with Saldanha et al.15. The incorporation of 4% CCR leads to a substantial increase in UCS to 1229.52 kPa and 1395.2 kPa at 28 and 56 days of curing, respectively. The results indicate that UCS gains predominantly occur during the first 28-day curing period, with minimal strength development observed between days 28 and 56, demonstrating that stabilization reactions are largely completed within the first month.Fig. 6Development of UCS with varying CCR content over different curing periods.

Development of UCS with varying CCR content over different curing periods.

The time-dependent strength enhancement in stabilized soils results from cementitious compound formation that binds soil particles and reduces porosity, progressively improving mechanical properties throughout the stabilization period51. This range is identified as the active zone. Moreover, the UCS increase can be attributed to the presence of both calcium hydroxide [Ca(OH)₂] and calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) in CCR. The calcium hydroxide fraction readily reacts with the reactive silica and alumina in kaolinite through pozzolanic reactions, forming cementitious hydrates such as C–S–H and C–A–H, thereby further improving strength and durability52,53. C–S–H enhances soil cohesion and decreases porosity, thereby improving the durability of stabilized soil and ensuring longer-lasting layers. The pozzolanic reaction also contributes to reducing soil permeability54.

Beyond the active zone, specifically for CCR contents between 4–16%, the UCS begins to decrease, and this region is classified as the inert zone31,55. The reduction in UCS can be ascribed to the increased positive surcharge, which causes repulsive forces among the soil particles within the mixture. This repulsion disrupts the inter-particle cohesion, leading to a reduction in the total strength of the soil51,56,57.

The UCS enhancement effect of PP as a CCR replacement is shown in Fig. 7, where a 25% PP blend yields peak strengths of 1418.1 kPa (after 28 days) and 1783.8 kPa (after 56 days). The improvement in UCS can be attributed to the unique properties of PP, particularly its porous structure, which enhances particle distribution within the soil matrix and increases overall cohesion. At lower substitution percentages, PP primarily acts as a structural enhancer in kaolinitic soil by filling the voids between finer particles, thereby improving particle packing, compaction, and inter-particle cohesion, which contributes to increased UCS. Additionally, since both PP and clay contain silica, the interaction between silica in these materials and the calcium in CCR at lower PP percentages promotes the formation of strength-enhancing gels, further reinforcing the soil structure23,58.Fig. 7Development of UCS with varying PP replacement content over different curing periods.

Development of UCS with varying PP replacement content over different curing periods.

However, the positive effects of PP on UCS diminish as the substitution percentage increases beyond a certain threshold. One significant factor contributing to this reduction in strength is the decrease in soil density and cohesion. When PP content exceeds this threshold, the overall soil density decreases, impairing its compactness. This loss of compaction disrupts the dense structure responsible for high strength, ultimately causing a decline in UCS59. Moreover, while clay typically exhibits high resistance due to its adhesive properties, increasing amounts of PP reduce soil cohesion. This occurs because the porous structure of PP does not facilitate strong bonds between soil particles, unlike other materials that promote more cohesive interactions60. As a result, reduced cohesion weakens the overall soil structure and decreases its strength33.

Furthermore, the reduction in strength can also be attributed to changes in capillary tension within the soil matrix. While PP initially enhances soil strength by filling voids between finer particles and improving particle packing and cohesion, the introduction of larger particle sizes at higher contents leads to decreased capillary tension, which is a key factor in maintaining soil strength.

61. In addition, the excessive void spaces created by the porous nature of PP further weaken compressive bonds between soil particles, diminishing the soil’s ability to resist pressure. Consequently, while adding PP up to a certain percentage improves soil strength, exceeding this level causes a substantial decrease. In summary, incorporating PP into the soil enhances UCS when the substitution percentage is limited to 0–25%. Beyond this threshold, structural integrity is compromised, resulting in reduced strength due to decreased compaction, cohesion, and increased void spaces.

Figure 8 illustrates the impact of replacing CCR with WTS at various substitution percentages and curing times on the UCS of the soil. The data reveal a clear trend: within the substitution range of 0–10%, UCS increases. However, beyond the 10% substitution level, UCS begins to decline. This behavior can be attributed to the rapid hydration process occurring at lower substitution levels, which promotes more significant and pronounced chemical reactions between the soil and WTS62.Fig. 8Development of UCS with varying WTS replacement content over different curing periods.

Development of UCS with varying WTS replacement content over different curing periods.

WTS is typically rich in key compounds such as Ca, Mg, Al, and Si, which are known to benefit soil structure. These compounds, both inorganic and organic, participate in reactions that form new bonds with clay particles63. These bonds, particularly cationic bonds involving Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ ions, help enhance soil structure by improving cohesion and, consequently, strength. The formation of such bonds leads to a noticeable increase in UCS64. Furthermore, the addition of WTS can reduce the plasticity of the clay, as mineral components within the sludge reduce the clay’s tendency to swell. This reduction in swelling potential results in less volumetric change and contributes to the overall strength improvement of the soil65.

However, as the percentage of WTS increases beyond 10%, a decline in UCS is observed. This reduction in strength can be explained by several factors. WTS contains a considerable amount of water and organic matter, which can increase the soil’s moisture content. With higher sludge content, the soil absorbs more water, causing greater swelling in the clay. Under these more moist conditions, the soil weakens, as excess moisture leads to the separation of clay particles. This reduction in particle cohesion results in lower UCS66. Moreover, at elevated WTS percentages, the physical and chemical properties of the soil may be altered to such an extent that clay particles begin to form a slurry rather than stronger, cohesive structures. This shift diminishes the overall soil strength.

Furthermore, the organic matter present in WTS, particularly at higher substitution levels, can decompose in the presence of excess moisture. This decomposition leads to the breakdown of organic materials, further reducing bonding between soil particles and weakening the soil structure67. At lower substitution levels, Ca2⁺ and Mg2⁺ ions effectively form robust bonds between clay particles, enhancing soil strength. However, at higher levels, the concentration of these ions may diminish soil cohesion and interact with other heavy metals and organic materials present in the sludge, thereby exacerbating the weakening effect68. In conclusion, the optimal percentage of WTS for enhancing soil strength lies at the 10% substitution level. Beyond this threshold, the detrimental effects of increased moisture and organic content in the sludge outweigh the benefits of enhanced soil cohesion, leading to a decrease in UCS.

Eu and E50

Figure 9 illustrates the variation of the secant modulus at 50% of ultimate stress (E50) and the energy absorption capacity (Eu) for mixtures stabilized with CCR, PP, and WTS at curing times of 7 and 28 days. The results show a clear and significant relationship between the strength and stiffness of the mixtures, with trends in Eu and E50 closely mirroring the behavior of UCS. This provides a deeper understanding of the interplay between material strength, stiffness, and deformability. As UCS increased, both Eu and E50 also exhibited corresponding increases, highlighting that stronger materials tend to be more rigid and less prone to significant deformation before failure. This is characteristic of brittle materials, where an increase in strength leads to a decrease in ductility, resulting in more abrupt fractures69,70. The alignment of Eu and E50 values with UCS further underscores the direct correlation between strength and stiffness in these stabilized soil mixtures.Fig. 9Eu and E50 trends for stabilized samples with PP replacement after (a) 7 days, and (b) 28 days, and WTS replacement after (c) 7 days, and (d) 28 days.

Eu and E50 trends for stabilized samples with PP replacement after (a) 7 days, and (b) 28 days, and WTS replacement after (c) 7 days, and (d) 28 days.

As can be seen, the untreated clay soil exhibited very low stiffness and toughness, with baseline values of 58 MPa (E50) and 60 kJ/m3 (Eu). Incorporation of 4% CCR substantially improved both moduli, raising them to 320 MPa and 289 kJ/m3 after 7 days, and further to 760 MPa and 310 kJ/m3 after 28 days. These results correspond to increases of more than 5 times in E50 and nearly 5 times in Eu compared with untreated soil, demonstrating the efficiency of CCR in enhancing soil stiffness. This enhancement is attributed to pozzolanic reactions between CCR and water, leading to the formation of cementitious gels (i.e., C–S–H). As the curing period progressed, these reactions became more complete, further improving the soil’s mechanical properties.

When part of the CCR was replaced with 25% PP (optimum content), additional gains were observed. After 7 days, values reached 419 MPa (E50) and 315 kJ/m3 (Eu), which represent improvements of about 31% and 9% compared with CCR alone at the same curing time (Fig. 9a). After 28 days, the moduli rose dramatically to 1021 MPa and 535 kJ/m3, equivalent to 34% and 73% higher than CCR alone and nearly 18-fold and ninefold increases compared with untreated soil, as shown in Fig. 9b. These findings confirm that perlite plays a significant role in long-term stiffness development, owing to its filler effect and pozzolanic activity.

Similarly, the inclusion of 10% WTS (optimum level) produced clear enhancements as per Fig. 9c, d. After 7 days, E50 and Eu increased to 488 MPa and 305 kJ/m3, which are approximately 52% and 6% higher than the 4% CCR mixture, respectively. After 28 days, the values reached 810 MPa and 436 kJ/m3, corresponding to 7% higher E50 and 41% higher Eu than CCR alone, and roughly 14-fold and sevenfold increases compared to untreated soil. These results demonstrate the positive role of WTS in improving short- to medium-term stiffness and toughness, largely due to its fine particle size and reactive alumino-silicate phases. The overall trend across all mixtures (Figs. 9a–d) indicates that both E₅₀ and Eu increase with additive content up to an optimum level (25% PP and 10% WTS), after which the values decline. This behavior reflects a balance between the beneficial effects of filler action, pozzolanic reactions, and matrix densification, and the detrimental effects of excessive additive contents that disrupt the soil structure and increase porosity. Importantly, the evolution of both moduli closely followed UCS trends, confirming that improvements in strength are directly linked to enhanced stiffness. At the same time, the higher stiffness values were associated with a more brittle stress–strain response, highlighting the trade-off between strength gain and ductility in CCR-based stabilization systems.

Ultrasonic tests

Figure 10 illustrates the evolution of UPV across various stabilized mixtures at different curing durations. The experimental findings demonstrate that mixtures containing 4% CCR, combined with 25% PP and 10% WTS as partial replacements, achieved the highest UPV values, reaching approximately 1350, 1550, and 1530 m/s, respectively (Fig. 10, b, and c). This clearly highlights the beneficial role of incorporating optimal proportions of these additives in enhancing the compactness and structural quality of the soil. The data further reveal that the most substantial improvement in UPV occurred during the first 28 days of curing, followed by only marginal enhancements up to day 56. This trend suggests that the critical phase of microstructural development and pore refinement predominantly takes place during the early curing period. During these initial weeks, intensive chemical reactions such as hydration, pozzolanic activity, and crystallization are most active, leading to significant transformations in the internal architecture of the soil28,71,72.Fig. 10Variation of UPV for samples treated with (a) CCR, (b) CCR-PP, and (c) CCR-WTS after 7, 28 and 56 days of curing.

Variation of UPV for samples treated with (a) CCR, (b) CCR-PP, and (c) CCR-WTS after 7, 28 and 56 days of curing.

Specifically, after 28 days, the formation of a viscous gel phase, resulting from the interaction between soil minerals and stabilizing agents, evolves into a framework of suspended crystalline particles. These particles progressively occupy the void spaces within the soil matrix, resulting in a denser, more continuous internal structure. The filling of pores reduces overall porosity, enhances particle-to-particle contact, and strengthens the matrix, collectively facilitating a more efficient and faster propagation of ultrasonic waves. Such improvements align with previous research emphasizing the role of microstructural densification in enhancing mechanical and acoustic properties73,74.

In contrast, the decrease in UPV observed at higher replacement levels can be attributed to the onset of microstructural imperfections. When the dosage of stabilizing materials exceeds the optimal range, it often leads to excessive formation of reaction products and an imbalance in the soil–binder system. This imbalance promotes the generation of additional voids and weak interfaces within the soil skeleton, adversely affecting its compactness. These defects act as scattering points for ultrasonic waves, impeding their transmission and resulting in lower UPV values75. Thus, careful optimization of stabilizer content is essential to maintain the integrity and coherence of the soil matrix and to prevent degradation of both mechanical and acoustic performance. Among all mixtures, the formulation incorporating 4% CCR with 25% PP and 10% WTS consistently exhibited superior mechanical properties and structural uniformity. This demonstrates the effectiveness of synergistic additive combinations in producing a well-compacted, durable, and eco-friendly stabilized soil.

Microstructural studies

FTIR

FTIR analysis results for both untreated soil and samples with optimized additive combinations are presented in Fig. 11. Significant variations in absorption peak intensities were observed across multiple spectral regions, with a prominent peak in the 3600–3900 cm−1 range corresponding to hydroxyl (-OH) groups. In the CCR, CCR–PP, and CCR–WTS stabilized samples, peaks at 3617, 3691, and 3904 cm−1 were detected, associated with the formation of C–S–H and C–A–H gels. Previous studies, such as Madejova et al.76 and Eisazadeh et al.77 obtained similar results, indicating that the observed peaks correspond to hydroxyl groups, which are associated with the presence of cementitious gels in the soil. Our findings are consistent with their results.Fig. 11FTIR analysis of untreated versus CCR-PP/WTS-stabilized kaolinite soil.

FTIR analysis of untreated versus CCR-PP/WTS-stabilized kaolinite soil.

C–S–H is produced through the reaction between calcium and silica and is commonly observed in cementitious systems. These gels exhibit a hydrated structure, where water is physically and chemically integrated into silicate and calcium bonds. Additionally, the peak observed at 778 cm−1 is assigned to Si–O–Al bonds within aluminosilicate compounds, while the peak at 912 cm−1 corresponds to Al–OH vibrations. The absorption at 1028 cm−1 is attributed to bending vibrations of Si–O linkages within the mineral framework. This signal is more pronounced in the untreated soil and the CCR–PP mixture, reflecting their higher content of these structural units compared to the CCR–WTS sample. The presence of such oxygen-bridged silicon–aluminum networks plays a key role in reinforcing the soil matrix, as their interconnected frameworks are known to enhance mechanical strength and overall structural stability. Consistent findings were reported in the studies of Fateh et al.44 and Latifi et al.78 suggesting that such modifications lead to an enhancement of the soil structural properties.

Therefore, the presence of peaks in the 950–1100 cm−1 range indicates silicate structures in the soil, which can improve resistance to external pressures and mechanical stresses. In the optimized combinations, peaks at 1415 and 1416 cm−1 correspond to the stretching vibrations of C–O bonds in carbonate compounds. In CCR combinations with PP or WTS, CCR can react with atmospheric CO₂ to form CaCO₃, which is responsible for the observed peaks in this region. As a stable mineral, CaCO₃ strengthens the soil structure and enhances particle cohesion, thus improving resistance to physical changes such as shrinkage and swelling. Previous investigations, including that of Bagriacik et al.79 have demonstrated that calcite formation enhances the cohesive properties of the soil, thereby improving its overall mechanical behavior. Our findings are in full accordance with these results, indicating a complete consistency with the observations reported in earlier studies.

XRD

Figure 12 presents the XRD patterns of untreated kaolinite soil and kaolinite stabilized with CCR, PP, and WTS. In the untreated kaolinite soil, characteristic peaks were observed at 12.3°, 20.7°, 24.8°, and 35.5°, typical of kaolinite’s crystalline structure. These sharp, intense peaks indicate that the kaolinite in the sample is well-crystallized and stable under normal conditions80. The addition of CCR, PP, and WTS stabilizers led to a notable attenuation of the characteristic kaolinite peaks in the XRD patterns, indicating structural modifications and partial loss of crystallinity. The interaction between CCR and kaolinite promotes the formation of secondary mineral phases, including C–S–H and C–A–H, which transform the original clay structure. Furthermore, CCR and PP may deposit on soil particle surfaces, potentially forming CaCO₃ that can cover primary mineral phases and contribute to the observed reduction in peak intensities. The decreased proportion of primary minerals, combined with alterations in hydrogen bonding on the clay surfaces, further modifies the soil’s microstructure through chemical reactions. These mineralogical changes are consistent with previous studies conducted by Latifi et al.81 and Heirani et al.82, which also reported that the formation of C–S–H and related hydration products, along with partial dissolution or coverage of clay minerals, leads to decreased XRD peak intensities and contributes to the improved mechanical performance of stabilized soils.Fig. 12XRD analysis of untreated versus CCR-PP/WTS-stabilized kaolinite soil.

XRD analysis of untreated versus CCR-PP/WTS-stabilized kaolinite soil.

The reduction in the intensity of the primary diffraction peaks in the stabilized soils, particularly in the CCR–PP and CCR–WTS mixtures, relative to the untreated kaolinite can be attributed to microstructural and mineralogical transformations induced by the stabilization process. The incorporation of CCR and WTS promotes pozzolanic reactions between calcium-rich residues and the reactive silica and alumina in the soil, leading to the formation of poorly crystalline hydration products such as C–S–H and C–A–H. These newly formed phases partially replace or obscure the original clay mineral peaks, such as those observed near )28° and 48°(, resulting in the observed decline in peak intensities80. This transformation mechanism is consistent with previous studies that were reported by Goodarzi et al.83 and Salimi et al.84, where the addition of lime or calcium-based stabilizers similarly caused a decrease in the diffraction intensity of kaolinite peaks due to pozzolanic consumption of the clay minerals and the formation of new crystalline and amorphous phases such as C–S–H and gibbsite. Overall, the XRD results suggest that the stabilizing agents induce significant structural changes in the kaolinite-based soils, including the breakdown of kaolinite and the formation of new mineral phases such as C–S–H and C–A–H gels. These changes contribute to the overall enhancement of the soil’s stability and structural integrity.

SEM

The natural microstructure of kaolinite exhibits a distinct layered arrangement that generates considerable voids between adjacent particles. SEM observations reveal that the particles are irregularly distributed rather than forming compact, plate-like sheets, resulting in the development of pores and interparticle voids. These structural discontinuities significantly reduce the bearing capacity and mechanical strength of kaolinite in its untreated state. The weak interparticle bonding and limited cohesion among the fine clay particles lead to low shear strength and poor resistance to deformation under applied stresses. Consequently, in the absence of stabilizing additives, the soil exhibits pronounced compressibility and susceptibility to excessive settlements under both static and dynamic loading conditions. The incorporation of additives such as CCR, PP, and WTS effectively fills the voids between soil particles and enhances interparticle cohesion (Figs. 13b–d). These stabilizers improve the soil’s elastic modulus and shear strength by reducing pore volume and forming a denser, more resilient matrix87. The stabilization process significantly transforms the soil fabric, shifting it from an open, porous structure to a compact and flocculated configuration. Crystalline bonding materials were observed in samples subjected to longer compaction durations, indicating the progressive formation of cementitious products. The original wavy morphology of the untreated soil gradually disappeared as new crystalline and gel-like phases developed, coating particle surfaces and occupying pore spaces. This microstructural densification strengthens interparticle bonds, reduces void ratios, and results in marked improvements in UCS and these results are consistent with those reported in previous studies reported by Latifi et al.28.

Compared to the untreated clay, which exhibited a loose and highly porous microstructure, the stabilized samples show a compact and well-integrated fabric with noticeably reduced pore spaces. This densification can be attributed to the progressive infilling of voids by cementitious products generated through pozzolanic reactions between CCR and the reactive components of the clay. The resulting hydration products, primarily C–S–H and C–A–H, form as fine crystalline or gel-like substances that coat the particles, bridge adjacent grains, and significantly improve interparticle bonding and cohesion (Fig. 13b). Consequently, the soil develops a stronger and more stable matrix, reflecting substantial enhancement in its mechanical behavior. These findings are in close agreement with those of previous studies18,31,82,88, which likewise reported that the formation of C–S–H and C–A–H gels leads to pore filling, particle bonding, and overall strengthening of stabilized clay soils.

It is also noticeable that the clay particles, influenced by the chemical reactions with CCR and PP, are more uniformly connected, with thin layers of cementitious materials coating them. These coatings help bind the soil particles together, forming a stronger structure. In various areas of the image, fine PP particles may be observed. Due to their insulating properties and low density, these particles can fill the voids between soil particles, assisting in the creation of a denser structure. This contributes to lower porosity and increased UCS of the soil. In the initial clay soil, the particles may have been arranged irregularly and scattered; however, in the images with the additives, the particles are more regularly connected, forming a more cohesive structure.

The presence of voids in the CCR–PP mixture can be attributed to several factors (Fig. 13c). PP, as a naturally occurring and lightweight mineral, possesses a highly porous crystalline structure that contains numerous air cavities. When added to clay soil, these intrinsic pores remain within the matrix and appear as hollow regions in the SEM micrographs. Furthermore, the limited bonding capacity between PP and clay particles, along with the evaporation of moisture during curing, contributes to the formation of additional voids. Hence, the inherent porosity and weak interfacial adhesion of PP explain the residual voids observed in the stabilized samples, which are consistent with the findings reported in studies by Akbulut et al.26 and Varga et al.89. WTS, as an active material, plays a crucial role in this process. It not only enhances particle cohesion but, due to its organic and inorganic content, may also contribute to the formation of adhesive materials and mineral complexes within the soil. These changes are visible in the SEM images, demonstrating the structural improvements in the soil, which have led to enhanced mechanical properties as a result of the combination of CCR-WTS (Fig. 13d).

However, significant changes were observed in the concentrations of Ca and carbon (C), particularly in the CCR, CCR-PP, and CCR-WTS stabilized samples. The observed rise in carbon content in the CCR-PP and CCR-WTS mixtures can be attributed to the high LOI of CCR, which contains residual carbon and other impurities originating from the acetylene production process. Additionally, the WTS component is known to contain substantial amounts of organic matter and C, which become incorporated into the soil matrix during stabilization. The increased concentrations of Ca and C in the CCR-based stabilized mixtures, especially those including PP and WTS, lead to both chemical and physical improvements in the treated soils. While Ca contributes to strength development through the formation of durable cementitious phases, C enhances the microstructure by improving interparticle bonding and reducing pore spaces. These modifications collectively result in significantly improved strength, durability, and mechanical resistance of the stabilized kaolinite soils.

Conclusion

This work primarily examined how the incorporation of perlite powder (PP) and water treatment sludge (WTS) as partial replacements for calcium carbide residue (CCR) modifies the engineering response of clay soil, quantified through systematic unconfined compression strength test (UCS) and ultrasonic pulse velocity test (UPV) testing. The experimental program began by blending varying proportions of CCR (1%, 2%, 4%, 8%, 16%) with the clay, followed by curing periods of 7, 28, and 56 days. The optimal CCR content was identified based on mechanical performance and then partially replaced with different percentages of PP (0%, 25%, 50%, 75%, 100%) and WTS (0%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, 25%). This phase led to the identification of optimum CCR-PP and CCR-WTS combinations. Finally, microstructural analyses, including Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), X-ray diffraction (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS), were performed on the optimized mixtures.
