3D printed hydrogel scaffold incorporating keratin and melatonin-loaded nanomicelles for cartilage tissue engineering
Authors: Ahmed F Hameed, Nissan S Oraibi, Wildan Mohammed Awad, Suha Mousa Alawsi, Mastafa H Al-Musawi, Huda Muhammed Muzher, Mohammed Hayder Asker, Fariborz Sharifianjazi, Ketevan Tavamaishvili, Maryam Vesal, Negin Azad, Marjan Mirhaj, Mohamadreza Tavakoli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArticular cartilage possesses limited self-repair capacity due to its avascular nature, necessitating advanced tissue engineering strategies for effective regeneration. This study presents the fabrication and evaluation of a novel 3D-printed hydrogel scaffold composed of chitosan (Cs), carrageenan (Crg), and keratin (Kr), integrated with melatonin (Mel)-loaded nanomicelles to stimulate cartilage repair. The nanomicelles exhibited a mean diameter of 133.36 ± 13.69 nm, and their incorporation into the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold enabled a sustained Mel release profile, reaching 88.0 ± 5.5% cumulative release over 264 h. The Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold demonstrated an equilibrium swelling ratio of 391.6 ± 26.3% after 72 h in distilled water and a degradation rate of 47.0 ± 5.5% after 28 days in PBS. In vitro assays using ATDC5 chondrogenic cells revealed superior cell viability and adhesion on the Mel-containing scaffold. Alcian blue and Safranin O staining confirmed increased proteoglycan synthesis of rBMSCs on the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold and gene expression analysis via qRT-PCR indicated significantly elevated levels of COL II, ACAN, and SOX9, confirming enhanced chondrogenic differentiation. The combined presence of Kr and Mel contributed to the scaffold’s regenerative efficacy by improving cell adhesion and modulating cellular processes involved in chondrogenic differentiation. These findings suggest that the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold holds strong potential for application in cartilage repair and regeneration.Keywords: 3D printed scaffold, Cartilage tissue engineering, Keratin, Melatonin, NanomicellesSubject terms: Biotechnology, Materials science

Introduction

Articular cartilage is a specialized connective tissue found at the termini of bones within synovial joints. It consists predominantly of chondrocytes embedded in a structured extracellular matrix (ECM) and serves to minimize friction while enabling smooth and efficient joint articulation1. Cartilage tissue is susceptible to damage resulting from diverse etiologies, including acute sports-related injuries, chronic inflammatory conditions such as arthritis, and external mechanical trauma. Cartilage lacks both vascular and neural networks, relying exclusively on diffusion of oxygen and essential nutrients from the surrounding synovial fluid to sustain cellular viability. This avascular nature significantly limits its regenerative capacity following structural damage2.

Conventional strategies for restoring damaged cartilage encompass techniques such as microfracture (which stimulates marrow-derived repair), autologous tissue grafting, and autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI). While these approaches have demonstrated partial efficacy, they are often constrained by issues such as mismatched tissue properties, poor integration with surrounding cartilage, and complications arising from donor site harvesting. Tissue engineering offers a promising approach for cartilage regeneration by incorporating chondrogenic cells within or atop biodegradable scaffold structures, aiming to reconstruct functional tissue at sites of defect3. The regenerative potential of tissue-engineered scaffolds can be significantly enhanced by incorporating bioactive molecules and biomimetic structural features. Accordingly, their fabrication necessitates highly adaptable and precision-controlled processing techniques to ensure functional efficacy. Three-dimensional (3D) printing, or additive manufacturing, enables the precise fabrication of complex 3D structures by depositing materials layer by layer based on computer-aided design (CAD) models. This technology has transformed biomedical engineering by facilitating the production of advanced devices such as drug delivery systems, diagnostic models, biosensors, tissue-engineered constructs, and artificial organs. Its versatility supports the creation of biologically inspired architectures and the customization of multifunctional, high-performance scaffolds4.

Hydrogel-based scaffolds, especially those derived from natural biomaterials, have gained prominence in cartilage tissue engineering due to their biocompatibility, bioactivity, and ECM-like microstructure. When combined with 3D printing, they enable precise fabrication of constructs that support cell growth, proliferation, and phenotype maintenance5. Chitosan (Cs) is a naturally derived cationic polysaccharide obtained through the deacetylation of chitin, primarily sourced from marine crustaceans such as shrimp and crab. Structurally composed of D-glucosamine and N-acetylglucosamine units, it closely mimics the architecture of glycosaminoglycans (GAGs) and the ECM in cartilage tissue. This molecular similarity, combined with its intrinsic biocompatibility, biodegradability, and low immunogenicity, makes Cs a compelling candidate for scaffold fabrication. Moreover, its ability to interact with cytokines and growth factors enables modulation of cellular behaviors including adhesion, proliferation, and phenotype preservation5,6. Cs, as a cationic polysaccharide, can form polyelectrolyte complexes with anionic polysaccharides through physical interactions, resulting in hydrogel networks without the need for chemical crosslinkers5. Carrageenan (Crg) is a linear, sulfated polysaccharide extracted from red seaweed, characterized by its strong negative charge and polyanionic nature. Its molecular structure closely resembles GAGs present in the ECM of cartilage, making it highly suitable for cartilage tissue engineering applications7. Owing to its excellent biocompatibility, high water retention capacity, viscoelastic properties, and unique gelling behavior, Crg-based hydrogels have been widely explored in cartilage tissue engineering, wound healing, drug delivery, and regenerative medicine8–10. Due to its polyanionic nature, Crg can readily form polyelectrolyte complexes with cationic polysaccharides such as Cs11,12. Keratin (Kr) is another natural polymer that exhibits a collagen-mimetic molecular structure, effectively replicating the architecture of native cartilage ECM. It contains bioactive peptide motifs such as Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) and Leu-Asp-Val (LDV) that facilitate integrin-mediated cell adhesion. Moreover, its inherent water stability addresses a major limitation commonly associated with protein-based biomaterials, enhancing its suitability for scaffold-based biomedical applications13,14.

Incorporating a localized drug delivery strategy into scaffold-based tissue engineering can significantly enhance regenerative outcomes by providing sustained biochemical cues within the defect site15. Melatonin (Mel) (N-acetyl-5- methoxytryptamine, C13H16N2O2) is a hormone derived from tryptophan and primarily secreted by the pineal gland during nighttime. Mel exhibits notable cytoprotective and anti-aging properties due to its antioxidant activity and ability to neutralize free radicals, contributing to a healthier tissue environment. Exogenously administered melatonin can suppress acute inflammation by regulating inflammatory cytokines and reducing pro-apoptotic signals such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α)16,17. It has been reported that Mel promotes chondrogenic differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and significantly enhances the expression of genes involved in cartilage formation under normal differentiation conditions, making it a promising candidate for therapeutic applications in cartilage regeneration18. However, Mel’s hydrophobicity, poor aqueous solubility, and short biological half-life pose significant challenges for its effective incorporation into hydrogel-based scaffolds commonly utilized in cartilage tissue engineering19. Therefore, encapsulating Mel within amphiphilic nanocarriers such as nanomicelles, followed by their integration into hydrogel-based scaffolds, appears to be a promising strategy.

Based on the aforementioned rationale, this study aims to fabricate a 3D-printed Cs–Crg/Kr hydrogel scaffold incorporated with Mel-loaded nanomicelles. This composition was selected owing to the high biocompatibility of Cs and Crg and their GAG‑like structural features that resemble the native cartilage ECM, while Kr provides complementary ECM-mimetic and cell-adhesive properties. In addition, Cs and Crg have the ability to crosslink and form a stable hydrogel with suitable gelation behavior for 3D printing, thereby offering an appropriate platform for cartilage tissue engineering. Unlike previously reported Kr-containing scaffolds or Mel-releasing hydrogels, the present design uniquely integrates Cs–Crg polyelectrolyte complexes with Kr and Mel-loaded nanomicelles within a 3D-printed architecture. This combination enhances Mel solubility and stability, provides synergistic bioactivity through ECM-mimetic and cell-adhesive motifs, and enables sustained Mel release, collectively promoting superior chondrogenic differentiation. Accordingly, this study introduces a previously unexplored multifunctional scaffold and evaluates its physical properties, cytocompatibility, and in vitro chondrogenic performance for cartilage tissue engineering.

Materials and methods

Materials

Soluplus® was obtained from BASF (Ludwigshafen, Germany). Chitosan (Cs), with a deacetylation degree of 85% and medium molecular weight, was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Also, Kappa-carrageenan (Crg, ∼401 kDa), keratin (Kr; 98% purity), melatonin (Mel; ≥98% purity), and dimethyl thiazole diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) were also sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Genipin was supplied by Iota-Chem (China), and TRIzol reagent was obtained from Invitrogen (USA). Alcian blue and Safranin-O staining dyes were purchased from Merck (Germany), and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was provided by Bioidea (Iran).

Synthesis and characterization of Mel-loaded nanomicelles

Nanomicelles were prepared using the thin film hydration method, following protocols established in previous studies20,21. Briefly, 1.7% w/v Soluplus® was fully dissolved in 10 mL ethanol in a round-bottom flask at ambient temperature for 5 h. Subsequently, Mel was added to the solution at a concentration of 0.05% w/v (5 mg) and dissolved completely22. The resulting solution was then subjected to solvent evaporation by incubating the flask overnight at 40 °C, resulting in the formation of a thin film on the flask’s inner surface. This thin film was rehydrated with 10 mL of distilled water, followed by sonication for 20 min to aid dispersion. Finally, the suspension was stirred mechanically at 450 rpm for 20 min to ensure homogeneity of the nanomicellar solution. A schematic representation of the synthesized nanomicelle structure and the encapsulated Mel within it is presented in Fig. 1.

Schematic representation of the synthesized nanomicelle encapsulating Mel within its core.

To evaluate the structural features of the nanomicelles, Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM, TESCAN Mira3, Czech Republic) was utilized. For this purpose, a small aliquot (20 µL) of the nanomicelle suspension was carefully dropped onto a silicon wafer and allowed to dry at ambient conditions. Once dried, the sample was adhered to SEM specimen holders using double-sided tape. Prior to imaging, the samples were coated with a thin gold film via sputter coating under vacuum to enhance electron conductivity. The size of nanomicelles was quantified using ImageJ software based on images obtained from FE-SEM (n = 20). Additionally, the particle size distribution was measured through dynamic light scattering (DLS) using a Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (UK), with all measurements performed in triplicate. Samples were sonicated for 20 min before analysis to ensure uniform dispersion. Zeta potential values were recorded using the same instrument’s zeta potential mode, with each sample undergoing 20 individual scans. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation.

Fabrication of scaffolds

A variety of polymeric formulations were preliminarily tested in pilot experiments to assess their suitability for scaffold fabrication, with the optimal concentrations selected based on their printability and structural integrity during the 3D printing process. To prepare the 3D-printed scaffolds, a 3% w/v Cs solution was first formulated in 1% acetic acid. Separately, a 5% Crg solution was prepared in distilled water under vigorous stirring at 40 °C for 4 h to ensure complete dissolution. These two solutions were then combined in a 1:1 volumetric ratio at room temperature, resulting in a uniform mixture. For the Cs-Crg/Kr formulation, Kr was incorporated at 5 wt% into the blend. Subsequently, for preparation of the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel formulation, Mel-loaded nanomicelles were added to achieve a final Mel concentration of 150 µg/mL in the polymeric solution23, and the stirring was continued for another 30 min. Gelation was initiated by the gradual addition of genipin with concentration of 1 wt%24 under continuous stirring, maintained for an additional 30 min. The resulting pre-crosslinked hydrogel was loaded into the cartridge of an extrusion-based 3D bioprinter. Scaffolds were fabricated following a pre-designed STL file featuring dimensions of 10 mm × 10 mm × 3 mm and composed of alternating layers oriented at 0° and 90°. 3D printing was conducted using a nozzle with an internal diameter of 300 μm at a speed of 5 mm/s. Post-printing, the constructs were immersed in a calcium chloride solution for 2 h and subjected to freeze-drying to finalize the scaffold preparation. Depending on the specific formulation of the 3D printing solutions (inks), three types of scaffolds were ultimately fabricated, namely Cs-Crg, Cs-Crg/Kr, and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel. Characterization of scaffolds

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM; LEO435VP, UK) was employed to investigate the morphology and microstructural features of the freeze-dried 3D-printed scaffolds. Prior to imaging, a thin gold coating was applied to the scaffold surfaces to enhance conductivity. SEM analysis was performed at an accelerating voltage of 18.0 kV. Quantitative measurements of strut thickness and pore diameter were obtained using ImageJ software.

To assess the swelling behavior of the scaffolds, specimens measuring 10 × 10 mm were initially weighed to determine their dry mass (W0). Subsequently, each sample was immersed in 30 mL of distilled water maintained at 37 °C. At predetermined time points—12, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 96, and 120 h—the scaffolds were retrieved, gently blotted to remove surface moisture, and reweighed to obtain their swollen mass (WS). Swelling was calculated using Eq. (1)25, and the experiment was performed in triplicate using independently fabricated scaffolds.

Mechanical properties

Compression testing was performed on scaffolds (n = 3) with dimensions of 10 × 10 mm using a universal testing machine (Zwick, Germany) equipped with a 500 N load cell. The specimens were subjected to uniaxial compression at a crosshead speed of 3 mm/min. Compressive strength and elastic modulus values were subsequently determined from the corresponding stress–strain curves.

Biodegradability

To investigate the biodegradation behavior, scaffold specimens measuring 10 × 10 mm were prepared. Each sample was initially weighed to determine its baseline dry mass (W0), then immersed in 10 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated at 37 °C for durations of 1, 3, 5, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days. At each designated time point, the scaffolds were retrieved, freeze-dried, and subsequently reweighed to obtain their post-degradation dry mass (Wt) using a high-precision laboratory balance (accurate to five decimal places). The extent of degradation, expressed as weight loss, was calculated using Eq. (2)26. All measurements were performed in triplicate using independently fabricated scaffold samples.

Additionally, to investigate the morphological changes of the scaffolds during the degradation process, samples were retrieved from PBS after 28 days, dried, and coated with a thin layer of gold, and subsequently analyzed using SEM.

Drug encapsulation efficiency and release

Encapsulation efficiency (EE) was assessed by dissolving 10 mg of Mel-loaded nanomicelles in 20 mL of ethanol and incubating the solution at room temperature for 48 h. Following incubation, a sample of the supernatant containing Mel was collected, and its concentration was quantified using UV–Vis spectrophotometry at a wavelength of 278 nm. At scheduled intervals—12, 24, 72, 120, 168, 216, and 264 h—3 mL samples were withdrawn from the incubation medium. The amount of Mel released was quantified by UV–Vis spectrophotometry, with absorbance recorded at 278 nm27.

Cytocompatibility

Cellular compatibility was investigated using the ATDC5 chondrogenic cell line, obtained from the Pasteur Institute Cell Bank (Iran). Cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 5% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. For cellular assays, cells at passage three were selected. Prior to seeding, scaffolds (10 × 10 × 3 mm) were sterilized under ultraviolet light for 30 min per side and placed into individual wells of a 24-well plate. A suspension containing 5 × 104 cells was carefully pipetted onto each scaffold (n = 3) and incubated under standard conditions. To assess cell viability, the MTT assay was performed on days 1 and 7. At each time point, the culture medium was removed and replaced with 600 µL of fresh medium containing 60 µL MTT reagent (2.5 mg/mL). After 4 h of incubation, the medium was discarded and scaffolds were transferred to new wells. Formazan crystals formed by metabolically active cells were solubilized using 400 µL of isopropanol containing 0.04 M hydrochloric acid. The resulting solution was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 2 min, and 200 µL of the supernatant was collected for absorbance measurement at 570 nm using a microplate reader28.

Morphological assessment and evaluation of cell adhesion were performed by seeding 5 × 104 ATDC5 cells onto each scaffold (n = 3), followed by a 72-hour incubation under standard culture conditions. Post-incubation, scaffolds were gently rinsed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove non-adherent cells. Cellular fixation was carried out using 3% glutaraldehyde for 4 h at 4°C. Subsequently, samples underwent graded ethanol dehydration and were air-dried at ambient temperature. To prepare the surface for imaging, a thin gold layer was sputter-coated onto each scaffold, and SEM was employed to visualize cell morphology and attachment.

To assess the viability of ATDC5 cells adhered to the scaffolds after 72 h of culture, a dual acridine orange/propidium iodide (AO/PI) staining protocol was employed. Stock solutions of AO (670 mmol/L) and PI (750 mmol/L) were prepared in Dulbecco’s solution and maintained at 4 °C in the dark to preserve stability. For working solution preparation, 0.01 mL of AO and 1.0 mL of PI were mixed, diluted tenfold with Dulbecco’s solution, and sterilized by passage through a 0.22-µm membrane filter. Cell-seeded scaffolds (n = 3) were incubated in the AO/PI mixture for 3 min, followed by immediate fluorescence microscopy examination. Viable cells were identified by green fluorescence resulting from AO uptake, whereas non-viable cells exhibited red fluorescence due to PI incorporation. Quantification of viable cell density was performed using ImageJ software analysis of the acquired images.

In vitro chondrogenic differentiation

For in vitro evaluation of chondrogenic differentiation, rat bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (rBMSCs), obtained from the Pasteur Institute Cell Bank (Iran), were used. The chondrogenic medium used throughout the differentiation process consisted of high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 50 µM ascorbic acid, 10 ng/mL transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), 100 nM dexamethasone, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 1% insulin-transferrin-selenium (ITS) supplement, and 50 µM L-proline29,30.

To assess chondrogenic differentiation under in vitro conditions, rBMSCs were seeded onto scaffolds assigned to different experimental groups (n = 3 per group). Each scaffold was placed into a well of a 6-well culture plate and incubated in chondrogenic differentiation medium, with media renewal performed every three days. After a 14-day culture period, scaffolds were harvested, rinsed thoroughly with PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde at 4 °C overnight. Fixed samples were subsequently embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 5 μm slices using a microtome. The sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated, then stained with 0.1% Safranin O for 10 min to detect sulfated GAGs. For Alcian Blue staining, sections were pretreated with 3% acetic acid for 3 min and subsequently stained with 1% Alcian Blue for 30 min to visualize acidic polysaccharides within the ECM. Finally, the sections were examined under light microscopy (BX51; Olympus, Japan). Quantitative assessments were performed by analyzing the intensity of blue and red staining in the microscopic images using ImageJ software31,32.

RNA extraction and quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) were employed to evaluate the expression of cartilage-specific genes, including collagen type II (COL II), SRY-box transcription factor 9 (SOX9), and aggrecan (ACAN). To do this, rBMSCs were seeded onto the fabricated scaffolds and cultured for 7 and 21 days. After the culture period, the cells were enzymatically detached from the scaffolds using trypsin and collected. Total RNA was then extracted from the harvested cells using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Subsequently, complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using a commercial cDNA synthesis kit (AmpliSens, Russia), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, qRT-PCR was performed on a Rotor-Gene Q system (QIAGEN, Germany) using SYBR Green Master Mix and gene-specific primers listed in Table 133. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as the internal control gene. All reactions were run in triplicate. The relative expression levels of cartilage-specific genes were calculated using the comparative threshold cycle method (2-ΔΔCt).

Statistical analysis

All statistical evaluations were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1 and SPSS version 16.0. Depending on the structure of the experimental setup, both one-way and two-way ANOVA were employed to analyze differences between groups, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test for post hoc analysis. Results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Levels of significance were denoted as follows: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, *p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001, indicating increasing degrees of statistical reliability.

Results and discussion

Physical and morphological characterization

In this study, Soluplus®-based nanomicelles were employed for the encapsulation of Mel and were prepared using the thin film hydration method. FE-SEM was employed to investigate the morphological characteristics of Mel-loaded nanomicelles. As shown in Fig. 3a, the nanomicelles exhibit a predominantly spherical shape with relatively uniform size distribution. The nanomicelles exhibited a favorable size distribution with overall uniformity, although they displayed a slight tendency toward aggregation. The mean particle diameter based on FE-SEM images was determined to be approximately 93.88 ± 27.05 nm, aligning with the optimal size range for enhanced drug delivery performance34. These morphological attributes validate the successful synthesis of the nanomicelles and underscore their suitability for biomedical applications, particularly in targeted therapeutic systems.

(a) FE-SEM images of Mel-loaded nanomicelles at two different magnifications, and (b) DLS analysis results.

The size distribution and surface charge of the nanomicelles were characterized using DLS, as illustrated in Fig. 3b. The DLS results indicated that Mel-loaded nanomicelles possessed an average hydrodynamic diameter of 133.36 ± 13.69 nm and a zeta potential of − 2.1 ± 0.4 mV, suggesting efficient self-assembly into nanoscale carriers. As mentioned, imaging via FE-SEM revealed slightly smaller particle dimensions, which can be attributed to the absence of the hydration shell in dry-state measurements. Although Soluplus®-based micelles generally possess zeta potentials close to neutrality, they demonstrate exceptional colloidal stability due to the presence of a steric protective layer. Soluplus® is an amphiphilic copolymer without ionic charge, incorporating polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains that serve as the hydrophilic segment. During the self-assembly process, these PEG moieties orient toward the surrounding aqueous medium, forming a hydrated outer shell around the micellar core. This shell acts as a steric barrier, preventing particle aggregation by creating spatial separation between micelles, rather than relying on electrostatic repulsion. Such steric stabilization plays a pivotal role in maintaining dispersion stability, even in systems with minimal surface charge.

The microstructural and morphological characteristics of the 3D-printed scaffolds, namely Cs-Crg, Cs-Crg/Kr, and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel, were investigated using SEM imaging (Fig. 4a). The images reveal that all scaffolds exhibited a porous architecture characterized by nearly spherical and interconnected pores, with struts arranged in a layered configuration at 0° and 90° orientations. It appears that the Cs-Crg scaffold exhibited a lower degree of structural order compared to the other two scaffolds, as evidenced by irregularly sized pores and thicker struts, which also indicate insufficient control over ink deposition by the printer. Figure 4b–c presents the average diameters of the struts and pores across the different scaffolds. In the Cs-Crg, Cs-Crg/Kr, and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffolds, the strut diameters were measured as 673.1 ± 25.4, 488.2 ± 50.4 and 509.8 ± 81.3 μm, while the corresponding pore sizes were 361.1 ± 40.1, 506.9 ± 47.3, and 549.0 ± 75.4 μm, respectively. These results indicate that the incorporation of Kr into the Cs-Crg scaffold led to a significant reduction in strut thickness, followed by a corresponding significant increase in pore size (p < 0.05). The incorporation of Kr may have increased the concentration of the polymeric printing ink, which could in turn elevate its viscosity. Such a rise in viscosity might restrict the material flow through the nozzle under constant pressure, potentially resulting in the formation of finer struts and larger, well‑defined pores within the scaffold structure35. Moreover, the reduced nozzle output improved the control over ink deposition by the printer, leading to a more uniform and well-organized printed scaffold. Notably, the incorporation of Mel-loaded nanomicelles did not induce any significant structural alterations in the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold when compared to the Cs-Crg/Kr scaffold (p > 0.05). It has been reported that cartilage tissue engineering scaffolds support optimal cell infiltration and chondrogenesis, with pore sizes ranging from 300 to 600 μm, and particularly around 500 μm36. Therefore, the fabricated scaffolds were structurally capable of supporting cell adhesion, infiltration, and chondrogenesis.

(a) SEM images of the 3D-printed Cs-Crg, Cs-Crg/Kr, and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffolds, (b) Quantitative analysis of the strut diameters of the scaffolds, and (c) Quantitative analysis of the pore diameters of the scaffolds.

Cartilage is a highly hydrated tissue, containing more than 80% water, and its water content serves as a key indicator of tissue health37,38. Consequently, scaffolds intended for cartilage tissue engineering should demonstrate substantial water uptake and swelling ability to closely replicate the properties of native cartilage. As illustrated in Fig. 5a, all the fabricated scaffolds exhibited excellent water uptake, with the swelling rate increasing up to 72 h and reaching equilibrium thereafter. Following 96 h, the swelling rate began to decrease, which can be attributed to the hydrolytic degradation of the polymer chains and the hydrogel network structure. The equilibrium swelling of the Cs-Crg, Cs-Crg/Kr, and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffolds, after 72 h of immersion in distilled water, was measured to be 353.0 ± 32.0, 387.5 ± 17.8, and 391.6 ± 26.3%, respectively. Owing to their composition of cross-linked hydrogels, the scaffolds were expected to exhibit substantial swelling behavior39.

Mechanical properties

The compressive mechanical properties of the 3D-printed scaffolds were evaluated to assess their structural stability and suitability for supporting cartilage tissue regeneration. The compressive stress–strain curves of different scaffolds are presented in Fig. 5b, and the numerical values of compressive yield strength and elastic modulus are summarized in Table 2. The fabricated scaffolds exhibited a compressive yield strength in the range of 0.74–0.81 MPa and an elastic modulus in the range of 954.7–1048.4 kPa, with no statistically significant differences in their compressive properties (p > 0.05). Based on previous reports indicating that the compressive strength of cartilage tissue ranges from 0.01 to 3 MPa40 and its elastic modulus is approximately 400 kPa41, the results of mechanical testing demonstrated that the 3D-printed constructs possessed compressive properties within the desirable range and could thus be considered effective scaffolds for cartilage tissue engineering.

Biodegradability

During the initial phase of implantation, a tissue-engineered scaffold needs to preserve its structure while progressively degrading to allow eventual replacement by the host tissue42. To estimate and predict scaffold degradation in practice, the in vitro weight loss of the three 3D-printed scaffolds (Cs-Crg, Cs-Crg/Kr, and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel) was measured in PBS at 37 °C over 28 days (Fig. 5c). Overall, the scaffolds exhibited a linear increase in degradation rate up to day 28, with the weight loss reaching 36.6 ± 3, 44.7 ± 2.1, and 47.0 ± 5.5% for Cs-Crg, Cs-Crg/Kr, and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffolds, respectively. The presence of Kr and Mel-loaded nanomicelles led to slightly higher degradation rates in the Cs-Crg/Kr and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffolds compared to the Cs-Crg scaffold. The enhanced degradation observed in the Cs-Crg/Kr and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffolds was likely due to the presence of Kr and nanomicelles, which disrupted the cross-links between Cs and Crg as well as the polyelectrolyte complexes they formed. This effect, combined with the larger pore sizes of these scaffolds compared to the Cs-Crg scaffold, facilitated greater water penetration into the network and accelerated hydrolytic degradation. This is further supported by the results shown in Fig. 5a, where water uptake and swelling of the Cs-Crg/Kr and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffolds were higher than those of the Cs-Crg scaffold.

To evaluate the morphology and structural changes of the scaffolds during degradation, SEM images were obtained on day 28 of the process and are shown in Fig. 5d. As can be seen, the surfaces of all scaffolds underwent noticeable erosion and delamination throughout the degradation process. In particular, the scaffold containing Mel-loaded nanomicelles exhibited a wrinkled surface with prominent grooves, likely resulting from the release of the nanomicelles and the disruption of the hydrogel network cross-links.

Drug encapsulation efficiency and release

In this study, hydrophobic Mel was first encapsulated within Soluplus® nanomicelles and then incorporated into the 3D-printed Cs-Crg/Kr scaffold to enable localized and controlled release of the drug for promoting chondrogenic differentiation. The encapsulation efficiency (EE) of melatonin in Soluplus®-based nanomicelles was found to be 67.2%, with an encapsulation capacity (EC) of 2.36%. The release profile of Mel from the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold is illustrated in Fig. 5e. As evident from the release profile, Mel exhibited a rapid release rate during the initial 120 h, reaching approximately 58.8 ± 6.3% of the total loaded amount. Thereafter, the release continued at a slower and more sustained rate, culminating in 88.0 ± 5.5% cumulative release by 264 h.

The biphasic release behavior can be primarily attributed to the sequential processes of scaffold swelling and degradation, which together regulate the diffusion and sustained release of the encapsulated Mel. In the initial phase, during the first 120 h, the scaffold absorbed water and underwent rapid swelling, which facilitated the release of nanomicelles and the Mel encapsulated within them, especially those located near the surface. This phase accounted for the majority of the drug release. As time progressed, the scaffold began to degrade gradually, allowing deeper-embedded nanomicelles to be released in a sustained manner. This extended release continued up to 264 h, ultimately resulting in about 88.0% cumulative release. Figure 5f schematically illustrates the two key mechanisms driving Mel release from the 3D-printed Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold, namely, hydrogel matrix swelling and degradation.

Cytocompatibility

To assess the biocompatibility of the 3D-printed scaffolds and investigate the response of ATDC5 chondrogenic cells upon interaction with different scaffold compositions, an MTT assay was conducted on days 1 and 7 following cell seeding. The corresponding results are illustrated in Fig. 6a. No signs of cytotoxicity were observed in any of the scaffold groups under investigation, and all scaffolds demonstrated excellent biocompatibility throughout the study. On day 1, the beneficial effect of Kr was clearly evident, as cell viability on the Cs-Crg/Kr and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffolds was significantly higher compared to the Cs-Crg scaffold (p < 0.05). However, the presence of Mel-loaded nanomicelles did not result in a statistically significant improvement in cell viability on the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold relative to the Cs-Crg/Kr scaffold on day 1 (p > 0.05). By Day 7, the Cs-Crg/Kr scaffold maintained its superior cell viability compared to the Cs-Crg scaffold (p < 0.05). Meanwhile, the sustained release of Mel from the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold led to a significantly higher viability of ATDC5 cells relative to both Cs-Crg (p < 0.01) and Cs-Crg/Kr (p < 0.05) scaffolds.

Figure 6b shows SEM images of ATDC5 cells adhered to the surface of different scaffold formulations. As shown, cells exhibited favorable attachment and maintained a normal morphology across all scaffold types, indicating appropriate surface compatibility. However, cell density appeared to be higher on scaffolds containing Kr compared to the Cs-Crg scaffold. Notably, the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold not only supported greater cell density but also seemed to promote enhanced ECM synthesis. In this group, cells appeared to be embedded within the ECM, suggesting elevated matrix deposition and active cellular remodeling.

To gain deeper insight into cell–scaffold interactions, Live/Dead staining was conducted after 72 h of culture (Fig. 6c–d). Consistent with the trends observed in the MTT assay and SEM images of cell adhesion, all scaffolds supported satisfactory cell viability. Nevertheless, the Cs‑Crg/Kr scaffold exhibited a significantly higher density of viable cells compared with the Cs‑Crg scaffold (p < 0.05). Furthermore, incorporation of Mel-loaded nanomicelles into the Cs‑Crg/Kr scaffold markedly enhanced cell survival and proliferation, as evidenced by the Cs‑Crg/Kr‑Mel group showing significantly greater viable cell density than the Cs‑Crg/Kr scaffold (p < 0.05).

The enhanced cell adhesion and proliferation on Kr-containing scaffolds can be attributed to the presence of specific cell-binding motifs within Kr, such as leucine–aspartic acid–valine (LDV) and arginine–glycine–aspartic acid (RGD)14,43. These motifs are known to interact with integrin receptors on the cell surface, thereby facilitating stronger cell–matrix interactions and promoting cellular attachment. The superior cellular response observed on Mel-loaded scaffold can be attributed to Mel’s multifaceted bioactivity. It is reported that Mel inhibits the release of inflammatory cytokines, thereby fostering a more favorable microenvironment for cell proliferation and reducing cellular senescence and apoptosis. Additionally, its ability to modulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and upregulate antioxidant gene expression contributes to enhanced cell survival and function44.

In vitro chondrogenic differentiation

After 14 days of culture, rBMSCs seeded on the scaffolds were subjected to histological staining using Safranin O and Alcian blue to assess chondrogenic ECM deposition. Safranin O specifically stains sulfated GAGs in cartilage tissue, producing a red coloration indicative of proteoglycan-rich ECM. Alcian blue binds to acidic polysaccharides such as hyaluronic acid and chondroitin sulfate, yielding a blue signal that reflects the presence of cartilage-specific ECM components. The use of these stains provides a reliable qualitative and quantitative assessment of chondrogenic differentiation and matrix production by rBMSCs on the scaffolds45. Representative images of Safranin O and Alcian blue staining, along with corresponding quantitative analyses, are presented in Fig. 6e–h. All scaffold groups exhibited varying degrees of proteoglycan production, indicating successful cell differentiation and chondrocyte formation. However, statistically significant differences were observed in the positively stained areas for both staining methods among the different scaffold groups. Based on Safranin O and Alcian blue staining, the following order of chondrogenic activity was established among the different scaffolds: Cs-Crg < Cs-Crg/Kr < Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel. The increased production of proteoglycans is attributed to enhanced cell proliferation, adhesion, and differentiation45, which is consistent with the results of the MTT assay and SEM images demonstrating cell attachment.

To further evaluate the chondrogenic potential of the fabricated scaffolds, qRT-PCR was performed after 7 and 21 days of rBMSCs culture to assess the expression levels of key cartilage-specific genes, including COL II, SOX9, and ACAN, and the results are presented in Fig. 7. A significant difference in the expression levels of all evaluated genes was observed between the fabricated scaffolds and the control group at both day 7 and day 14, indicating the high chondrogenic potential of the scaffolds. On day 7, the expression levels of COL II and SOX9 on the Cs‑Crg/Kr scaffold were significantly higher than those on the Cs‑Crg scaffold (p < 0.01 and p < 0.05, respectively), whereas ACAN expression showed no significant difference between the two scaffolds (p > 0.05). The expression levels of COL II, SOX9, and ACAN on the Mel‑containing scaffold (Cs‑Crg/Kr‑Mel) were significantly higher than those observed on the other scaffolds at day 7. After 21 days, the expression levels of COL II were higher in scaffolds containing Kr compared to the Cs-Crg scaffold (p < 0.05). However, no significant difference was observed between the COL II expression levels in the Cs-Crg/Kr and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffolds (p > 0.05). A similar expression pattern was observed for SOX9 and ACAN, with the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold showing the highest transcriptional levels. Importantly, both genes were significantly upregulated in Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel compared to Cs-Crg/Kr (p < 0.01), highlighting the enhanced chondrogenic activity induced by Mel incorporation.

Expression levels of chondrogenic genes (COL II, ACAN, SOX9) in rBMSCs after (a) 7 days and (b) 21 days of culture on different scaffolds. All tests were conducted with three replicates for each sample.

As mentioned, at day 7 COL II expression was significantly higher on the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold compared to all other samples, whereas by day 21 it converged to levels comparable to the Cs-Crg/Kr scaffold. Based on these observations, it may be inferred that Mel had accelerated early chondrogenic differentiation, leading to an initial rise in COL II, SOX9, and ACAN expression. After 21 days, SOX9, as the main transcription factor expressed throughout chondrogenesis, and ACAN, as a marker of cartilage‑specific ECM deposition in the late stage46, remained elevated in the Mel-containing group. While COL II expression plateaued in both Cs-Crg/Kr and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffolds, SOX9 and ACAN levels were significantly higher in the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold, indicating that Mel may help maintain the chondrogenic phenotype and stabilize proteoglycan-rich ECM during later stages of differentiation.

The chondrogenic behavior observed in the scaffolds based on Cs and Crg was fully anticipated, given their excellent biocompatibility, high water absorption capacity, and structural resemblance to the GAGs naturally present in the cartilage ECM7,47. The Kr-containing scaffolds demonstrated improved cellular behavior and enhanced chondrogenic differentiation, which was attributed to the intrinsic properties of Kr, including its excellent biocompatibility and the presence of bioactive RGD and LDV motifs that promoted cell adhesion and signaling13. The addition of Mel-loaded nanomicelles provided further biological cues that enhanced the chondrogenic potential and cellular responses of the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold. As reported in the literature, Mel not only preserves the viability of MSCs, but also promotes their chondrogenic differentiation through modulation of the TGF-β signaling pathway, which is critical for cell proliferation and cartilage regeneration16,23. Furthermore, Mel has been reported to enhance hypertrophic maturation of MSC-derived chondrocytes via activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, thereby contributing to improved cartilage matrix formation and repair48. Previous studies have also reported that melatonin induces the upregulation of chondrogenic gene expression, including COL II, SOX9, and ACAN18,49,50.

Conclusion

In this study, customizable Cs-Crg, Cs-Crg/Kr, and Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel hydrogel scaffolds were fabricated via 3D printing to establish engineered platforms for cartilage tissue regeneration. The addition of Kr and Mel-loaded nanomicelles endowed the Cs-Crg scaffold with improved structural and physicochemical characteristics, including favorable swelling behavior, controlled degradation, and sustained drug release. The Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold supported ATDC5 chondrogenic cell viability and adhesion, and promoted proteoglycan synthesis, as evidenced by Alcian blue and Safranin O staining. Moreover, elevated expression of COL II, ACAN, and SOX9 confirmed its capacity to induce chondrogenic differentiation. Overall, the Cs-Crg/Kr-Mel scaffold is introduced as an optimized construct tailored for cartilage tissue engineering. Future studies may focus on more detailed rheological characterization and enhanced assessments of scaffold printability. In addition, to ensure clinical performance, further assays on chondrogenic activity and extracellular matrix formation, together with preclinical in vivo evaluations, will be required.
