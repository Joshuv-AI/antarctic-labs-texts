Enhanced emulsifying properties of corn starch nanoparticles modified with purple corn cob anthocyanins as a Pickering emulsion gel stabilizer
Authors: Cheng Guo, Zhaofeng Liang, Weiping Jin, Guoyan Yang, Wenjing Huang, Jinling Li, Bin Li, Wangyang Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The strong hydrophilicity of corn starch nanoparticles (CSNPs) limits their application as a Pickering emulsion gel stabilizer. In this study, CSNPs were modified with purple corn cob anthocyanins (PCCAs), a type of anthocyanin derived from processing waste. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction analyses confirmed that PCCAs were embedded in CSNPs via both hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. The three-phase contact angle increased from 51.77 ± 3.18° to 67.43 ± 1.08° after loading, suggesting the PCCA-modified CSNPs (PCCA-CSNPs) were more suitable than the CSNPs for stabilizing oil/water emulsion. The Pickering emulsion gel stabilized by PCCA-CSNPs (PCSPEGs) exhibited better storage stability than the Pickering emulsion gel stabilized by CSNPs (CSPEGs). A comparison of the rheological properties, creep-recovery properties, and interfacial properties between CSPEGs and PCSPEGs revealed that the combined effects of the more stable interfacial layer and the increased viscosity of the continuous phase of the emulsion gel contributed to the improved stability of PCSPEGs. Thus, PCCA-CSNPs represent a green, rapid, and economical hydrophobic modification method for CSNPs, and the stabilization mechanism of PCSPEGs was elucidated in this work.

Introduction

A Pickering emulsion gel is an emulsion gel formed from solid particles, instead of traditional surfactants, and it is an emerging emulsification technology (Geng et al., 2023). The solid particle stabilizers in Pickering emulsion gels are irreversibly adsorbed on the oil/water (O/W) interface, effectively reducing the interfacial energy and forming a rigid film. These properties inhibit the instability of the emulsion, including the droplet coalescence, Ostwald ripening, and gravity separation (Low et al., 2020). Compared with traditional emulsion gels, Pickering emulsion gels have the advantages of low cost, environmental protection, high stability, and low environmental sensitivity (Xiao, Li, et al., 2016). In recent years, Pickering emulsion gels have attracted growing attention in food processing, food texture regulation, and functional ingredient delivery (Li et al., 2020; Madadlou et al., 2016).

The use of natural macromolecular nanoparticles, such as starch, to prepare Pickering emulsion gel is gaining traction in food and cosmetics research, due to their non-toxicity, biocompatibility, and biodegradability (Feng et al., 2022; Li et al., 2024). Corn starch, which is widely available and inexpensive, serves as an ideal raw material for preparing food-grade nanoparticles to stabilize Pickering emulsion gel (Ramos et al., 2025). However, natural corn starch exhibits strong hydrophilicity due to its polyhydroxy structure, resulting in poor inherent emulsifying properties. Therefore, the hydrophobic modification of corn starch can effectively improve the emulsifying property of corn starch particles (Feng et al., 2022). The combination of starch and natural bioactive substances provides a green and safe method for hydrophobic modification of starch, especially in complex environmental conditions such as in thermal processing and acidic food products where natural bioactive substances stability is a challenge. Furthermore, this method can overcome the limitations of single-component particles (Abdul Hadi et al., 2020). Importantly, using the composite particles to stabilize Pickering emulsions is an effective strategy to achieve dual delivery of active substances and improve loading and delivery efficiency (Wei et al., 2022).

Purple corn cob is the remaining part of purple corn (Zea mays L.) after removing grains. It is almost black because of its high content of anthocyanins (ACNs) (Yang and Zhai, 2010). Compared with other ACN-rich crops, such as blueberries and purple sweet potatoes, purple corn cobs have the advantages of abundance, low cost, and convenient transportation and storage (Chung et al., 2016). However, currently, the purple corn cob is mostly used as a fuel source, resulting in a large loss of ACN resources.

The polyhydroxy and glycosidic bonds in ACN's molecular structure make it hydrophilic, while the benzene rings endow it with a certain degree of hydrophobicity (Riaz et al., 2016). The right-handed helical structure of starch has natural compatibility with the hydrophobic skeleton of ACNs. The helical cavity provides a hydrophobic microenvironment for ACNs to embed into the helix through van der Waals forces, forming typical V-type inclusion complexes (Li et al., 2023; Putseys et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2023). Amoako and Awika, (2019) demonstrated that amylose can encapsulate sorghum proanthocyanidins in the hydrophobic core of the helical structure, forming a V-type crystalline complex with high thermal stability. Tong et al., (2020) also indicated that ACNs could be effectively encapsulated by amylopectin through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, which protect ACNs from heat, oxidation, reduction reactions, and metal ions. Thus, we propose that the self-assembly between purple corn cob anthocyanins (PCCAs) and corn starch could effectively improve the emulsifying property of corn starch particles and act as a stabilizer in the preparation of Pickering emulsion gels.

In this study, the corn starch nanoparticles (CSNPs) were prepared via a solvent displacement method, and PCCAs were incorporated during the preparation process to obtain PCCA-modified corn starch nanoparticles (PCCA-CSNPs). The CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs were characterized using dynamic light scattering (DLS) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The interactions between corn starch and PCCA were analyzed using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) and X-ray diffraction (XRD). Additionally, the differences in stability, rheological properties, and creep properties between the Pickering emulsion gel stabilized by CSNPs (CSPEGs) and the Pickering emulsion gel stabilized by PCCA-CSNPs (PCSPEGs) were systematically compared. Furthermore, the interfacial microstructures and properties of the two Pickering emulsion gels were investigated using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM), cryo-scanning electron microscopy (cryo-SEM), dynamic interfacial tension measurement, and three-phase contact angle analysis. Compared with other starch-polyphenol composite particles, the polyphenol used in this method was completely obtained from industrial waste, which greatly reduces the production cost. The solvent displacement method effectively shortened the self-assembly time of starch-polyphenol and improved the encapsulation efficiency of polyphenol. Additionally, the PCCA-CSNPs with good emulsification can achieve dual delivery of both hydrophilic (the anthocyanins in the particle matrix) and lipophilic (encapsulated in the oil phase) bioactive compounds. The primary aim of this study was to develop a green, efficient, and economical method for hydrophobic modification of corn starch particles, and the mechanism by which the modified CSNPs stabilize the Pickering emulsion gel was investigated.

Materials and methods

Materials

Purple corn was purchased from a local market (Wushang Market, Wuhan, China). Corn starch (Purity≥90 %; Mw = 4.74 × 104 kDa; 26.1 % amylose content, 73.9 % amylopectin content) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Rapeseed oil was purchased from Daodaoquan Cereal & Oil Co. Ltd. (Yueyang, China). Other chemicals used in this study were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagents Co. Ltd. (Beijing, China). Milli-Q purified water was used in all experiments.

Extraction and purification of PCCAs

Purple corn cobs were crushed mechanically and passed through a 60-mesh sieve. The purple corn cobs were extracted in a 1:30 mass-to-liquid ratio of 80 % (v/v) ethanol (pH 3) at 45 °C for 50 min. The extract was filtered through a Büchner funnel and concentrated by rotary evaporator at 45 °C. The concentrated solution was lyophilized to obtain the crude extract. The crude extract (2.0 g diluted in 10 mL Milli-Q purified water) was loaded onto a column (2.6 × 20 cm) packed with AB-8 macroporous resin (a bead size of 0.3–1.25 mm) as the stationary phase for purification (Carrera et al., 2023). It was washed with 150 mL 70 % ethanol solution acidified to pH = 2 with 0.1 M HCl solution. Each fraction with 5 mL was collected. The obtained purified PCCAs was lyophilized after evaporation of the organic solvent, and the final concentration of ACNs in PCCAs was 38.37 mg/g.

Preparation of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs

The CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs were prepared using the solvent displacement method (Liu et al., 2022). Corn starch (0.45 g) and PCCAs powder (7.5 mg) were dissolved in 13.5 mL and 1.5 mL of DMSO, respectively. The corn starch DMSO solution was placed in a boiling water bath for 20 min with 500 r/min magnetic stirring. Then the PCCAs DMSO solution was added and incubated in the water bath for 10 min. The mixed solution was added dropwise at a rate of 1 mL/min into anhydrous ethanol (15 mL) at room temperature, and magnetic stirring was maintained at 500 r/min for 20 min to achieve homogeneous dispersion of nanoparticles. The precipitates were collected after standing and layer separation. They were washed three times with anhydrous ethanol to remove residual DMSO, and then dispersed in 15 mL Milli-Q purified water. Finally, the PCCA-CSNPs were obtained by lyophilization. The CSNPs were prepared by replacing the PCCAs DMSO solution in the above method with DMSO.

Determination of encapsulation efficiency (EE)

The EE of ACNs was determined according to the method of Cui et al. (2022). In detail, the PCCA-CSNP anhydrous ethanol dispersion was centrifuged at 10,000 r/min for 5 min. The unencapsulated ACNs were separated in the supernatant and quantified by the pH-differential method (Wang et al., 2016) using an ultraviolet–visible spectrometer (U-T3C, Yi Spectrum, China) at 520 and 700 nm. The initial content of ACNs in PCCAs DMSO solution was also quantified by the pH-differential method. The EE was calculated using the following equation:(1)EE(%)=ACN0−ACNuACN0×100,

where ACN0 is the initial content of ACNs, and ACNu is the content of unencapsulated ACNs.

Particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and ζ-potential measurement

The particle size, PDI, and ζ-potential of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs were determined using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano-ZS (Malvern Instruments, UK). Samples were dispersed in Milli-Q purified water, and ultrasonic treatment (100 W and 40 KHz) was applied for 10 min before measurement (Huang et al., 2021).

TEM

The micromorphology of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs was observed using TEM (JEM-F2100, JEOL, Japan). The samples were prepared by dropping the solution onto copper grids coated with carbon.

FTIR

A Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometer (Frontier, PerkinElmer Ltd., UK) was used to obtain the infrared spectra. The spectra within the range of 400–4000 cm−1 were acquired with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 64 scans.

XRD

The wide-angle XRD spectra of corn starch, PCCAs, CSNPs, and PCCA-CSNPs were determined using an X-ray diffractometer with a Cu-Kα radiation source (Rigaku SmartLab SE, Japanese Science, Japan). The spectral scanning was performed from 5° to 50° (2θ) at 2°/min, and the current and voltage were 40 mA and 40 kV, respectively. The relative crystallinity was calculated using MDI Jade software (Material Data Inc., Livermore, CA, USA) (Li et al., 2023).

Three-phase contact angle measurement

Three-phase contact angle was measured at 25 °C using a drop shape analysis system (JY-PHA, Chengde Youte Testing Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Homogenous films of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs were prepared by depositing the dispersion (1.5 % w/v) on glass slides, which were dried at room temperature (Sharkawy et al., 2019). Then, 2 μL of Milli-Q purified water was dropped onto the film surface and photographed after 20 s. The Laplace-Young equation was applied to fit the profile data of the droplet. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Preparation of Pickering emulsion gels

The CSNP and PCCA-CSNP aqueous dispersions with different concentrations (4 %, 6 %, and 8 % w/v) were used to prepare Pickering emulsion gels. The oil phase (rapeseed oil) proportion was set to 30 %, 40 %, and 50 %. The oil-water mixture was sheared using a high-speed disperser (T18 digital ULTRA TURRAX, IKA Instruments Ltd., Germany) at 12,000 r/min for 3 min at room temperature to produce Pickering emulsions. The Pickering emulsions formed gels after standing at room temperature for 30 min. The Pickering emulsion gels stabilized by CSNPs and the Pickering emulsions stabilized by PCCA-CSNPs are abbreviated as CSPEGs and PCSPEGs, respectively.

Optical microscopic observation of Pickering emulsion gels

The microstructure of the Pickering emulsion gel was observed with an optical microscope (WV-CP470, Panasonic, Japan). The Pickering emulsion gel was diluted 10 times with Milli-Q purified water and shaken for 1 min to disperse evenly (Huang et al., 2021). The images were acquired at a magnification of 20 × .

Droplet size measurement

The droplet size of the Pickering emulsion gel was measured using a laser diffraction particle analyzer (Mastersizer 2000; Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). The refractive indices were 1.47 for rapeseed oil and 1.33 for water (Wang et al., 2025). All measurements were performed in triplicate.

Measurement of rheological properties

The rheological properties of the Pickering emulsion gel were determined using a Kinexus Pro rheometer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK) at 25 °C with a 40 mm plate geometry. For the steady flow assays, the apparent viscosity was measured versus shear rate (ranging from 0.1 to 100 s−1) with an angle of 4° and a fixed gap of 1 mm (Bi et al., 2021). The frequency sweep measurements were conducted at a constant strain amplitude of 0.4 %, with a fixed gap of 1 mm. The strain amplitude (0.4 %) was determined to be within the linear viscoelastic region (LVR) of the material. The storage moduli (G') and loss moduli (G") of the Pickering emulsion gels were measured over a frequency range of 0.1–10 Hz (Zhang et al., 2020).

Measurement of creep-recovery properties

The creep-recovery properties of the Pickering emulsion gel were determined using a DHR-2 rheometer (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) at 25 °C with a 40 mm parallel-plate flow chamber. A constant shear stress of 10.0 Pa was continuously applied to the sample, and the sample strain was recorded for 600 s as a creep curve. Then, the applied stress was rapidly removed, and the sample strain was recorded for 600 s as a recovery curve (Sharkawy et al., 2021). The relative recovery was calculated using the following equation:(2)Relativerecovery(%)=Jm−JeJm×100

where Jm (Pa−1) is the maximum creep, and Je (Pa−1) is the equilibrium creep compliance after recovery.

CLSM

CLSM experiments were performed according to a previous study (Yu et al., 2023) using an FV1200 CLSM (Olympus, Japan). A mixed fluorescent dye solution was used to label the oil (0.5 mg/mL Nile Red in Milli-Q purified water) and starch (0.5 mg/mL fluorescein isothiocyanate in Milli-Q purified water). One microliter of mixed fluorescent dye solution was mixed fully with 1 μL of Pickering emulsion gel, and the stained specimens were placed on microscope slides and covered with coverslips. The fluorescent dyes were excited at 488 nm and 543 nm for Nile Red and fluorescein isothiocyanate, respectively.

Cryo-SEM

The interfacial microstructures of the Pickering emulsion gels were observed using a cryo-SEM (Regulus 8220, Hitachi, Japan). Samples were placed on aluminum specimen mounts, frozen in liquid nitrogen slush, and transferred to a preparation chamber for sublimation gold plating under vacuum. Then, the samples were transferred to the scanning electron microscopy chamber for observation at −140 °C, with an accelerating voltage of 5–30 kV (Xiao, Wang, et al., 2016).

Dynamic interfacial tension measurement

Dynamic interfacial tension was measured via the hanging drop method, using an interfacial rheometer (DSA30S, KRÜSS, Germany). The CSNP and PCCA-CSNP dispersions (1.6 % w/v) were formed by submerging a syringe inside a quartz vessel filled with rapeseed oil. The interfacial tension between the dispersion and rapeseed oil was calculated within 3000 s from the pendant drop shape using the Laplace-Young equation (Sharkawy et al., 2021).

Statistical analysis

All results are reported as the mean ± standard deviation of at least three repeated measurements. Graphs were plotted using Origin 2024b, and one-way ANOVA was performed using SPSS 21.0 for significant differences (P < 0.05) in experimental data.

Results and discussion

Physicochemical properties of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs

Characterization of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs

As shown in Fig. 1, the particle size of PCCA-CSNPs (153.83 ± 6.23 nm) was significantly larger than that of CSNPs (97.98 ± 0.44 nm). This was consistent with the structural remodeling pattern of nanoparticles upon loading of bioactive substances reported in the literature (X. Wang et al., 2020). The possible structural cause was that the anthocyanins molecules were encapsulated in the CSNPs through hydrophobic stacking between the glucose anhydride rings of starch and the aromatic rings of anthocyanins (Escobar-Puentes et al., 2020). The PDI values of the two nanoparticles were both less than 0.3, indicating that the particle size distributions were uniform. Additionally, there was no significant difference between the ζ-potentials of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs. This suggests that the loading of PCCAs did not change the surface charge of CSNPs. Notably, the PCCA-CSNPs exhibited a high EE (72.81 ± 0.41 %) of ACNs, demonstrating good potential for active ingredient delivery.Fig. 1(A) Flow chart of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs preparation. (B) Particle sizes, PDI, ζ-potentials and (C) transmission electron microscopy images of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs.Fig. 1

(A) Flow chart of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs preparation. (B) Particle sizes, PDI, ζ-potentials and (C) transmission electron microscopy images of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs.

The TEM images provided a visualization of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs (Fig. 1C). These two nanoparticles exhibited typical spherical geometrical configurations, which were similar to other starch-based nanoparticles (Feng et al., 2019; Yuan et al., 2021). Starch molecules exhibit flexibility in DMSO. Upon the addition of anhydrous ethanol, the solubility of starch decreases, prompting the molecules to aggregate and initiate self-assembly. During this process, the starch molecular chains undergo rearrangement, favoring the formation of spherical structures that minimize surface energy (Marta et al., 2023). Additionally, the TEM images showed that the particle size of PCCA-CSNPs was larger than that of CSNPs, which was consistent with the DLS results.

Analysis of molecular interaction during the formation of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs

Fig. 2A shows the FTIR spectra of PCCAs, CSNPs, and PCCA-CSNPs. For PCCAs, the peak at 3265.8 cm−1 corresponds to O-H stretching vibrations, and the peaks observed at 1733.6 cm−1 and 1393.8 cm−1 are related to C-H and C=O stretching vibrations in the aromatic ring skeleton, respectively. Furthermore, the peak at 1218.2 cm−1 is caused by the stretching of the benzopyran aromatic ring, representing a typical flavonoid (Cai et al., 2019; Pereira et al., 2015; Zang et al., 2021). For CSNPs, the wide characteristic peak at 3310.8 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibrations of O-H. The peaks observed at 1652.3 cm−1 and 1420.8 cm−1 correspond to the amide I band (C=O stretching) and the asymmetrical stretching of COOH, respectively (Remanan and Zhu, 2021).Fig. 2(A) Fourier-transform infrared spectra and (B) X-ray diffraction pattern of PCCAs, CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs. (C) Three-phase contact angles of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs.Fig. 2

(A) Fourier-transform infrared spectra and (B) X-ray diffraction pattern of PCCAs, CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs. (C) Three-phase contact angles of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs.

After CSNPs were loaded with PCCAs, the characteristic peaks of PCCAs at 1733.6 cm−1, 1393.8 cm−1, and 1218.2 cm−1 disappeared, confirming that PCCAs were encapsulated by starch. Additionally, the wide characteristic peak of CSNPs at 3310.8 cm−1 shifted to 3434.6 cm−1, and the peaks at 1652.3 cm−1 and 1420.8 cm−1 shifted to 1641.5 cm−1 and 1415.4 cm−1, respectively. This suggests both hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions between PCCAs and CSNPs, and these synergistic forces maintain the structural stability of the complex.

Changes of starch structure during the formation of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs

Fig. 2B summarizes the XRD spectra of PCCAs, CSNPs, and PCCA-CSNPs. The intense and sharp diffraction peaks of PCCAs at 2θ of 14.1°, 18.1°, 19.5°, and 26.1° show that PCCAs was in a highly crystalline form. However, the characteristic diffraction peaks of PCCAs disappeared after loading with CSNPs, indicating that PCCAs was effectively embedded in CSNPs in an amorphous form. This structural evolution feature is closely related to the intermolecular interactions and spatial site-barrier effects in the nanocomposite system (Zhong et al., 2024). Additionally, the CSNPs showed diffraction peaks at 2θ of 13.2° and 19.9°, with a typical V-type crystalline structure (Escobar-Puentes et al., 2020). The PCCA-CSNPs also had obvious diffraction peaks of V-type crystalline structure at 2θ of 19.9°, suggesting that the V-type crystalline structure was maintained during the loading process. Notably, the crystallinities of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs were 12.60 % and 14.55 %, respectively. They were both significantly lower than the crystallinity of corn starch (36.95 %). This might be due to the gradual disintegration of the crystalline region of starch in DMSO, resulting in structural change similar to gelatinization (Zheng et al., 2025). Moreover, anthocyanins can form hydrogen bonds and other interactions with starch molecules, which limits the movement of starch molecular chains and makes starch molecular chains more easily arranged into ordered crystal structures (Miskeen et al., 2019). This may cause the crystallinity of PCCA-CSNPs to be slightly higher than that of CSNPs.

Differences in interfacial wettability between CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs

Solid particles used as Pickering emulsion stabilizers must have appropriate interfacial wettability to achieve effective anchoring at the oil-water interface (Wang et al., 2023). The interfacial wettability of particles can be quantified by the three-phase contact angle, the value of which directly determines the interface orientation tendency of particles. Particles with a three-phase contact angle below 90° were appropriate for stabilizing O/W emulsions, and those above 90° were appropriate for W/O emulsions (Dickinson, 2017).

As shown in Fig. 2C, the three-phase contact angle of CSNPs was 51.77 ± 3.18°, indicating high hydrophilicity. This may lead to insufficient adsorption free energy of the particles at the oil-water interface, thus affecting their interfacial stabilization efficiency (Sharkawy et al., 2019). However, the three-phase contact angle of PCCA-CSNPs significantly increased to 67.43 ± 1.08°, indicating the introduction of more hydrophobic ACNs. This reduced the overall hydrophilicity of the particles, enhancing their adsorption capacity at the oil-water interface (X.-Y. Wang et al., 2020). These results suggest that the PCCA-CSNPs are more suitable than the CSNPs for stabilizing O/W emulsions.

Visual appearance and microstructures of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs

Fig. 3A presents the appearances of Pickering emulsion gels prepared with different stabilizer concentrations and oil-to-water ratios after 24 h storage. As the stabilizer concentrations increased and the oil phase proportion dropped, the emulsion layer increased and the precipitation decreased gradually. Thus, the emulsifying stability increased gradually. The Pickering emulsion gels prepared with a stabilizer concentration of 8 % (w/v) and an oil phase proportion of 30 % showed the best emulsion stability, with no evidence of oil-water separation.Fig. 3(A) Visual appearances of Pickering emulsion gels prepared with different stabilizer concentrations and oil-to-water ratios after 1 d storage. (B) Visual appearances of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs after 60 d storage. (C) Microscopy images and droplet diameters of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs after 1d and 60 d storage.Fig. 3

(A) Visual appearances of Pickering emulsion gels prepared with different stabilizer concentrations and oil-to-water ratios after 1 d storage. (B) Visual appearances of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs after 60 d storage. (C) Microscopy images and droplet diameters of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs after 1d and 60 d storage.

Notably, the microstructures of the fresh CSPEGs and PCSPEGs were significantly different. The CSPEGs exhibited droplets of heterogeneous size, whereas the droplets in the PCSPEGs were relatively smaller and more uniform (Fig. 3B). After 60 d of storage, obvious phase separation was observed in the CSPEGs, suggesting poor stability. By contrast, the PCSPEGs maintained good physical stability without phase separation and exhibited better storage stability. Additionally, the coalescence stability of the Pickering emulsion gels during storage can be evaluated by the changes in droplet size (Chen et al., 2019). The average droplet diameter of CSPEGs increased from 48.02 ± 1.40 μm to 60.03 ± 7.01 μm after 60 d of storage, while that of PCSPEGs remained constant (Fig. 3C). The strong hydrophilicity of CSNPs prevented effective adsorption at the oil-water interface to stabilize the droplet surface. The lower hydrophilicity of PCCA-CSNPs enabled more nanoparticles to adsorb, preventing coalescence and resulting in reduced droplet size.

Rheological properties of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs

The apparent viscosity test and frequency sweep tests were conducted to obtain an insight into the difference mechanism of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs. As shown in Fig. 4A, the apparent viscosities of the two Pickering emulsion gels decreased sharply when the shear rate increased from 0.1 to 100 s−1, illustrating a typical shear thinning behavior and a non-Newtonian fluid property (Li et al., 2021). The reduction in viscosity reflected deflocculating of the droplets and the destruction of the internal structure, as the shearing process hindered the interactions between nanoparticles and droplets (Xi et al., 2019). To compare the difference in apparent viscosities of the two Pickering emulsion gels, the following Power-Law model was used to perform nonlinear regression analysis on the non-Newtonian fluid properties:(3)τ=K·γnwhere τ (Pa) represents the stress at a certain shear rate; K (Pa·s) is the consistency index; γ (s−1) is the shear rate; and n (dimensionless) represents the flow characteristic index.Fig. 4Apparent viscosities of (A) CSPEGs and PCSPEGs, (B) CSNPs aqueous solution and PCCA-CSNPs aqueous solution. (C) Storage moduli G′ and loss moduli G′′, (D) loss factor tan δ, (E) creep recovery curves, (F) compliances of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs.Fig. 4

Apparent viscosities of (A) CSPEGs and PCSPEGs, (B) CSNPs aqueous solution and PCCA-CSNPs aqueous solution. (C) Storage moduli G′ and loss moduli G′′, (D) loss factor tan δ, (E) creep recovery curves, (F) compliances of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs.

From Fig. 4A, the R2 values of both fits were higher than 99 %, indicating high accuracy. The consistency index K of PCSPEGs was greater than that of CSPEGs, and the flow characteristic index n of PCSPEGs was less than that of CSPEGs. This indicates that the apparent viscosity of PCSPEGs increased, leading to higher stability due to the formation of a network structure that restricts the emulsion droplet movement and prevents aggregation and coalescence (Sharkawy et al., 2021). Notably, the addition of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs made the continuous phase (water) viscous (Fig. 4B). Starch will undergo similar gelatinization changes when heated in DMSO solution. Therefore, the solubility of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs was enhanced, and the dissolved starch molecules were entangled with each other to increase the viscosity. The increase in continuous phase viscosity was also related to the interaction between starch molecules and nanoparticles. More hydrogen bonds could be formed between the starch molecules after loading PCCA, and the interaction between starch molecules and PCCA-CSNPs was stronger than that between starch molecules and CSNPs, thus the viscosity of PCCA-CSNPs aqueous solution was higher than that of CSNPs aqueous solution. Moreover, the higher viscosity of the PCCA-CSNP aqueous solution could also be due to slight differences in the effective concentration or the size of the dissolved polymeric fraction. It was another key factor for the difference in apparent viscosity between the two emulsion gels.

The network structure strength of the Pickering emulsion gel can be determined from the storage moduli (G′) and the loss moduli (G′′) (Feng et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 4C–G′ values were higher than G′′ values for both Pickering emulsion gels, implying the formation of an elastic gel-like network (Zhou et al., 2024). The loss tangent (tan δ) is defined as the ratio of the G′′ to the G′, which represents the elastic or viscous behavior of emulsions. As shown in Fig. 4D, the tan δ values of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs were both <0.1, indicating that the emulsion gels were both in a weak gel state. Additionally, the PCSPEGs showed higher G′ and G′′ values than the CSPEGs in the frequency range from 0.1 to 10 Hz, reflecting increased mechanical strength of the Pickering emulsion gel network structure (Li et al., 2019). This was attributed to the loading of PCCAs, which increased the binding sites of nanoparticles at the oil-water interface and enhanced the inter-droplet hydrogen bonding and intermolecular interactions. Thus, the stronger interaction led to the formation of a more compact three-dimensional elastic gel-like structure and restricted the droplet movement, effectively preventing the emulsion's aggregation and destabilization (Shahbazi, Jäger, Chen, et al., 2021).

Creep-recovery properties of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs

The creep-recovery analysis is commonly used to evaluate the transient behavior of the viscoelastic properties by rapidly deforming a sample via applied stress (Shahbazi, Jäger, Ettelaie, et al., 2021). As shown in Fig. 4E, when Pickering emulsion gels were subjected to prompt and constant stress, deformation increased over time, and the consequent elimination of stress resulted in a reduction in the strain. In the creep phase (0–600 s), the peak deformation of PCSPEGs was significantly lower than that of CSPEGs, indicating lower flowability and stronger creep resistance. In the recovery phase (601–1200 s), the stress was eliminated, the deformation of Pickering emulsion gels decreased with time, and the relative recovery could reflect the creep-recovery ability of the emulsion gel (Bi et al., 2021). In comparison with CSPEGs, the relative recovery of PCSPEGs increased from 7.21 ± 0.54 % to 14.55 ± 0.70 %. The higher percent recovery is related to a higher elasticity and a stronger network structure (Wei et al., 2024). Additionally, the compliance of PCSPEGs was lower than that of CSPEGs (Fig. 4F), which also indicated that the structural strength of PCSPEGs was stronger. The results of the creep-recovery experiments agreed well with the results of previously discussed rheological experiments.

Interfacial properties of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs

To further clarify the role of CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs in the emulsification process, CLSM was performed to visualize the interfacial microstructure of the Pickering emulsion gel. The oil phase was stained by Nile red shown as red, and starch was stained by fluorescein isothiocyanate white shown as green. As shown in Fig. 5A, a yellow interfacial layer was clearly observed, which is a characteristic of Pickering stabilization. This suggests the adsorption of CSNPs or PCCA-CSNPs on the oil droplet surface (Huang et al., 2021). Moreover, the CLSM images clearly exhibited the smaller size and more uniform shape of oil droplets in PCSPEGs than those in CSPEGs. This result is consistent with the droplet optical microscopy and droplet size measurement data.Fig. 5(A) Confocal laser scanning microscopy images and (B) Cryo-SEM images of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs. The morphology of the oil-water interface was further observed using cryo-SEM. As shown in Fig. 5B, there was a honeycomb-like structure in the continuous phase of both Pickering emulsion gels. The honeycomb-like structure is derived from the starch network structure and related to the close packing of emulsion droplets (Wang et al., 2025). The smaller and more uniform oil droplets in the PCSPEGs could be more closely packed. Therefore, the PCCA-CSNPs formed a more compact honeycomb-like structure than the CSNPs in the continuous phase, which was responsible for the higher apparent viscosity, G′, G′′, and relative recovery of the Pickering emulsion gel.

As shown in Fig. 5B, the rough droplet surface was observed for both Pickering emulsion gels, indicating that the CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs were absorbed at the oil-water interface. Subsequently, dynamic interfacial tension measurements were used to compare the adsorption degree of these two nanoparticles at the oil-water interface. As shown in Fig. 5C, the interfacial tension for CSNPs and PCCA-CSNPs at the oil-water interface gradually decreased with adsorption time, confirming that they were adsorbed at the interface (Costa et al., 2018). The interfacial tension of CSNPs showed a rapid decline within the first 500 s, followed by a slower decrease and reached a constant value after 3000 s. However, the decrease in the interfacial tension of PCCA-CSNPs was much slower, and it reached a constant value after 7000 s. Therefore, to maintain consistency between the two nanoparticles, only the data from the first 3000 s was retained, which resulted in a near-linear decline in the interfacial tension of PCCA-CSNPs. Throughout the measurement, the PCCA-CSNPs showed lower interfacial tension values than the CSNPs, indicating that the PCCA-CSNPs were more readily adsorbed at the interface (Wei and Huang, 2019). This might be due to the exposure of more hydrophobic areas after loading PCCA, which increases the surface adsorption capacity.

Mechanism of stability difference between CSPEGs and PCSPEGs

The interfacial layer and the viscosity of the continuous phase are two key factors affecting the stability of CSPEGs and PCSPEGs. When the CSNPs were used as an emulsion stabilizer, they increased the viscosity of the continuous phase; however, the strong hydrophilicity limited their interfacial activity, leading to insufficient adsorption free energy of the particles at the oil-water interface. The improvement in continuous phase viscosity alone is not enough to sufficiently stabilize CSPEGs. After the CSNPs were modified with PCCA, the introduction of more hydrophobic sites associated with ACNs reduced the hydrophilicity of CSNPs. This enhanced the adsorption of the stabilizer at the oil-water interface, forming a stable interfacial layer and uniform, small droplets. Additionally, the PCCA-CSNPs can also increase the viscosity of the continuous phase. Conclusion

The strong hydrophilicity of CSNPs limits their application as a Pickering emulsion gel stabilizer. In this study, the CSNPs were prepared using the solvent displacement method and were further modified by loading with PCCAs. The FTIR and XRD spectra revealed that PCCAs were embedded in CSNPs via both hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, and the crystallinities of both nanoparticles were significantly reduced compared to corn starch. Additionally, the three-phase contact angle of nanoparticles significantly increased after loading PCCAs, suggesting that the PCCA-CSNPs are more suitable than the CSNPs for stabilizing O/W emulsions. After 60 d of storage, the PCSPEGs exhibited better storage stability than the CSPEGs. A comparison of the rheological properties, creep-recovery properties, and interfacial properties between CSPEGs and PCSPEGs revealed that the combined effects of the more stable interfacial layer and the increased viscosity of the continuous phase of emulsion gels contributed to the improved stability of PCSPEGs. In summary, this study has presented a green, fast, and economical modification method for corn starch for the preparation of a stable O/W Pickering emulsion gel. Furthermore, this emulsion stabilizer loaded with ACNs may enable the co-delivery of ACNs and other bioactive substances in the same emulsion.
