Evaluation of the effect of brinzolamide 1% ophthalmic suspension on nystagmus using videonystagmography
Authors: Rehab Rashad Kassem, Hussein Sherif Hamdy, Dina Hossameldin Hassanein, Randa Mohamed Abdel-Moneim El-Mofty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:To evaluate the effect of topical brinzolamide (Azopt) eye drops on nystagmus using videonystagmography in infants and children.Methods:Setting: Institutional (Cairo University Hospitals). Study population: Both eyes of 40 patients, presenting with nystagmus, with a maximal age of 14 years, were included in this prospective, nonrandomized, unmasked study. Intervention: Clinical evaluation and videonystagmography were carried out on entry, at 1 month, and 6 months of Azopt instillation and 1 month after its discontinuation. Azopt was stopped at 1 month in case of deterioration. Main outcome measures: The primary outcome of the study was the change in nystagmus intensity at 4 weeks of Azopt treatment, based on videonystagmography.Results:Mean age was 5.7 ± 3.3 years. There was a significant decrease in mean nystagmus amplitude from 8.39 ± 7.67, on entry, to 5.95 ± 5.31, 5.27 ± 4.64, and 4.18 ± 3.65 degrees at 1 month, 6 months, and 1 month after discontinuation (P < 0.05). There was a significant decrease in slow phase velocity (SPV) from 29.53 ± 22.19, on entry, to 20.43 ± 18.62 degrees/second at 6 months, and in nystagmus intensity from 1018.4 ± 1530.9, on entry, to 319.3 ± 322.05 at 6 months (P < 0.05). Decrease in nystagmus frequency and visual improvement were insignificant (P > 0.05). At 1 month, nystagmus intensity decreased in 62.5%, but increased in 32.5%. No side effects were documented.Conclusion:Azopt eye drops significantly reduced nystagmus in infants and children, without side effects. Worsening of nystagmus was, however, noted in around one-third of the cases, so close follow-up is essential for timely discontinuation.

Nystagmus is an involuntary rhythmic oscillation of the eyes, which leads to reduced visual acuity due to the excessive motion of images on the retina. Nystagmus can be grouped into infantile nystagmus, which usually appears in the first 3–6 months of life, and acquired nystagmus, which appears later.[12]

Various interventions have been tried to alleviate nystagmus, whether surgical or medical.[12] The role of carbonic anhydrase (CA) inhibitors, as a therapy in infantile nystagmus, was previously documented. Oral acetazolamide was found to act both centrally and peripherally to dampen nystagmus.[3] To avoid the systemic side effects of oral acetazolamide, a topical CA inhibitor, brinzolamide, was tried and proved effective in improving visual acuity, foveation, and nystagmus waveforms.[4567]

Most previous studies evaluated the use of brinzolamide for a short duration in a small sample of adults.[456] The present study was conducted to evaluate the effect of brinzolamide on infantile nystagmus, using videonystagmography (VNG), in a larger sample of pediatric population, with extended usage reaching 6 months. Further assessment was done 1 month after its cessation.

Methods

This is a prospective study conducted at the Ophthalmic Department of Children’s Hospital of Cairo University, and the Audiology Department of Cairo University, between November 2020 and May 2025, to evaluate the effect of topical brinzolamide (Azopt) eye drops on nystagmus, using videonystagmography. The study was approved by the scientific committee, the Council of the Ophthalmic Department, and the Research Ethics Committee of Cairo University, gaining ethical committee approval number N-469-2023. The study conformed to all local laws and to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Consent was obtained from all guardians before the participation of their children.

Inclusion criteria comprised: patients aged 6 months to 14 years of age, presenting with infantile nystagmus. Exclusion criteria involved any of the following: refusal to participate, Azopt instillation for less than 1 month or not at all after its prescription, and/or lack of VNG on entry and at least once during the 6-month treatment period.

Patients underwent a complete ophthalmological evaluation by the first author, as follows. History taking, inquiring for: age, age at onset, previous glasses, occlusion or surgery, general or neurological illnesses, or delayed milestones, previous neuroimaging or electrophysiology. Anterior and posterior segment evaluation was performed on entry. Evaluation of extraocular muscles was done in terms of ocular alignment and ocular motility. The following were performed on entry and on each follow-up visit (at 1 and 6 months of Azopt treatment, and 1 month after cessation). Best corrected visual acuity was measured binocularly with compensatory head posture and with a straightened head and monocularly. Detailed evaluation of nystagmus was carried out to determine its direction (horizontal, vertical, oblique, rotatory, or torsional), type whether pendular or jerky (and fast phase direction for the latter), amplitude (small, moderate, or large), speed (slow, moderate, or fast), whether it dampens with convergence or not, and whether there is a null zone and/or abnormal head posture or not.

Electrophysiological evaluation (Electroretinography (ERG) and Visual evoked potential (VEP)) was done by the last author as indicated, mainly in cases with best corrected visual acuity below 6/12 without a settled diagnosis for the cause. Magnetic resonance imaging was ordered as indicated, mainly in those with nonhorizontal nystagmus, neurological abnormalities, optic disc abnormalities (especially pallor or cupping), abnormal VEP, or poor vision in the absence of retinal abnormalities clinically and by ERG.

Videonystagmography was done by the second author on entry, at 1 month and 6 months of Azopt treatment, and 1 month after cessation of Azopt. Eye movements were recorded using Cyclops Balance Eye Videonystagmography (Cyclops Medtech Pvt Ltd, Bengaluru, Karnataka 560062, India). The system has a linear range of ± 25° horizontally and ± 15° vertically. The system sampling frequency was 60 Hz, and the gaze position accuracy error was 0.25°–1° on average. After explaining the procedure, the subject was seated on a chair with a headrest, 1 meter from the stimulus screen. At this distance, the target subtended <0.1° of visual angle. The room light was turned off during the recording to minimize extraneous visual stimuli and thus avoid distraction. The machine was autocalibrated first for proper seeing of the eye tracker’s software threshold levels (e.g., pupil and corneal reflection) and imaging optics (e.g., eye camera focus and contrast), after which the actual examination started. The target appeared on the screen for a duration of 30 s in abnormal head posture and for 1 min without abnormal head posture, during which the eye movement recordings were made.

Based on VNG, nystagmus slow phase velocity (SPV, degrees/second), amplitude (A, degrees), frequency (F, Hz), and fast phase direction (D, degrees) were recorded. Nystagmus intensity (I) was used for easy comparisons and calculated as follows:

I = SPV × A × F

The treatment regimen with brinzolamide (Azopt) eye drops was as follows. One drop was instilled three times daily for 2 weeks, followed by twice daily for another 2 weeks, at the end of which VNG was repeated. If there was no improvement in nystagmus, based on the 4-week VNG, treatment was discontinued. On the other hand, if there was improvement documented by the 4-week VNG, Azopt was continued twice per day, for 6 months, at the end of which VNG was done, and repeated after 1 month of cessation. This was the study endpoint.

The primary outcome of the study was the change in nystagmus intensity at 4 weeks of Azopt treatment, based on videonystagmography. A successful outcome was considered an improvement in nystagmus intensity by at least 10% from the base level. Secondary outcomes involved subjective and clinical change in nystagmus and visual acuity, change in head posture, and any recorded adverse effects of Azopt.

Statistical analysis of the results was performed. Data were statistically described in terms of mean ± standard deviation (±SD), median, range, or frequencies (number of cases) and percentages when appropriate. Because the sample is large enough, comparison of numerical variables between the different time points was done using a paired t-test. On the other hand, because the groups are quite small, comparisons vs intensity outcome were performed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. For comparing categorical data, the Chi-square (χ2) test was performed. An exact test was used instead when the expected frequency is less than 5. Correlation between various variables was done using the Pearson moment correlation equation for linear relation of normally distributed variables and the Spearman rank correlation equation for nonnormal variables/nonlinear monotonic relation. Two-sided P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. IBM SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) release 22 for Microsoft Windows was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

Out of 73 patients initially screened or enrolled in the study, 40 patients were included in this prospective study, evaluating the effect of topical brinzolamide (Azopt) eye drops on nystagmus. The remaining 33 were excluded due to: lack of VNG on entry or at least once during the 6-month period of Azopt treatment (due to no show or child noncooperation), Azopt instillation for less than 1 month, or loss of follow-up after prescription of Azopt. Among the 40 included patients, 17 were males (42.5%), and 23 were females (57.5%). Age ranged from 0.75 to 14 (mean: 5.7 ± 3.3) years. Nystagmus was idiopathic in six patients (15%). General disorders recorded were (N, %) as follows: albinism (15, 37.5%), dwarfism (1, 2.5%), renal abnormalities (1, 2.5%), and Down’s syndrome with dysthyroidism and cardiac abnormalities (1, 2.5%). Neurological abnormalities were documented in seven patients (17.5%). Ophthalmological abnormalities were detected in 34/40 (85%), including albinism (15, 37.5%), other fundus abnormalities (17, 42.5%), pseudophakia (5, 12.5%), glaucoma (3, 7.5%), ptosis (3, 7.5%), and microcornea (1, 2.5%). The spherical equivalent of refractive errors was −1.24 ± 5.191 (−21.3 to +7.5) diopters for right eyes and −1.27 ± 5.427 (−22 to + 8.5) diopters for left eyes. Orthotropia was documented in 25/40 (62.5%), whereas the rest (15, 37.5%) had horizontal and/or vertical deviations. Abnormal head posture was present in 30 cases (75%). Electrophysiological abnormalities were detected in 16/18 patients, and MRI abnormalities in 4/9 patients who were tested.

Clinical evaluation of nystagmus revealed the following. Nystagmus, recorded on entry into the study, was horizontal in 33 (82.5%), oblique in 4 (10%), rotatory in 2 (5%), and vertical in 1 (2.5%). It was jerky in 18 (45%), pendular in 17 (42.5%), combined jerky and pendular in 2 (5%), and shimmering in 3 (7.5%). Nystagmus speed was fast in 19 (47.5%), moderate in 16 (40%), slow in 4 (10%), and slow alternating with fast in 1 (2.5%). Nystagmus amplitude was small in 14 (35%), moderate in 17 (42.5%), large in 8 (20%), and moderate alternating with large in 1 (2.5%).

All 40 patients received Azopt eye drops for at least 1 month, whereas 14 (35%) continued for at least 6 months. Azopt was used for a maximum of 27 months, with a mean duration of usage of 4.6 ± 5.4 months. Among the 26 patients who discontinued Azopt before 6 months, 13 were instructed to stop Azopt due to worsening of nystagmus (10 patients), or negligible improvement in (3 patients). The remaining 13 opted to stop Azopt due to financial burdens, unavailability of Azopt, lack of subjective improvement, or preference for surgery, or were lost to follow-up before 6 months.

Subjective improvement was documented in 21 patients (52.5%) and no change in 7 (17.5%), whereas the rest could not be determined. Parents reported improvement in terms of decreased nystagmus, whereas one reported a cessation of head nodding, and another reported better child balance with less frequent falls. Abnormal head posture improved in 8/30 cases presenting with abnormal head posture (27%). No side effects were documented in any patient. One parent, however, reported child agitation while on Azopt, but this patient was excluded from the study due to a lack of pre-treatment videonystagmography.

According to the paired samples test, improvements in nystagmus amplitude were statistically significant when comparing entry amplitude to 1 and 6 months of Azopt administration and to 1 month after Azopt discontinuation (P = 0.037, 0.013, and 0.021, respectively). Moreover, entry nystagmus SPV and intensity improved significantly after 6 months of Azopt instillation (P = 0.014 and 0.020, respectively). All other changes in nystagmus parameters and visual acuity were, however, statistically insignificant (P > 0.05). Outcomes of Azopt eye drops for treatment of nystagmus

One month after Azopt usage, vision improved in 8/13 (61.5%), remained the same in 2/13 (15.4%), and worsened in 3/13 (23.1%) patients, in whom vision could be measured by regular charts. Among patients with presumably idiopathic nystagmus, 2/3 (66.7%) showed improvement, and 1/3 (33.3%) remained the same. Among those with sensory or neurological abnormalities, 6/10 (60%) showed improvement, 1/10 (10%) remained the same, and 3/10 (30%) deteriorated.

Outcome of Azopt treatment (nystagmus intensity at 1 month) showed insignificant relation to all tested parameters (P > 0.05); these included: sex, age, age at onset, etiology of nystagmus (idiopathic, visual affection, and neurological), previous extraocular muscle surgery, general or neurological diseases, electrophysiological or MRI abnormalities, anterior or posterior segment abnormalities, refractive errors, visual acuity, strabismus, abnormal head posture, clinical nystagmus criteria (direction, type, amplitude, speed, and presence of null zone), or waveform.

Discussion

The present study prospectively evaluated the role of brinzolamide (Azopt) eye drops for the treatment of nystagmus, over a period of 1 to 6 months, and 1 month after cessation. On VNG, there was significant improvement in mean nystagmus amplitude, SPV, and intensity. Nystagmus intensity improved in 62.5%, but worsened in 32.5%, after 1 month of Azopt instillation.

Topical brinzolamide is a safe medicament widely used for treating glaucoma. In addition, CA inhibitors (both oral and topical) have been shown to improve the nystagmus waveforms and nystagmus acuity function in infantile nystagmus syndrome (INS).[34] Its effects are due to reversible CA enzyme inhibition, which diminishes the formation of bicarbonate ions and interferes with NA-K ATPase.[8]

The direct cause of INS in all patients is loss or miscalibration of smooth-pursuit damping, due to afferent, central, or peripheral causes. Afferent causes involve defective visual input. Central causes involve the brain cells responsible for the dampening of smooth pursuit. Peripheral causes involve defective proprioception. Proprioception is a function of the extraocular muscles’ proprioceptors that provide continuous signals to the brain about eye position and movement.[3] These proprioceptives are found distally at the tendinoscleral junction (enthesis)[9] and proximally at the musculotendinous junction.[10] Normal functioning of the proprioceptive neurons is essential for ocular alignment and stability, and their malfunction can cause nystagmus.[341112]

One of the precipitating factors for loss of smooth-pursuit dampening, with resultant INS, is an “inherited channelopathy” in a subset of central or peripheral neurons. This channelopathy affects ion channels, with affection of sodium-potassium ATPase membrane-bound system. A chanellopathy of the enthesial neurons affects proper proprioception, thereby inducing nystagmus. It is postulated that CA inhibitors (acetazolamide) affect this channelopathy (by interfering with the sodium-potassium ATPase membrane-bound system) in central or peripheral neurons, therefore acting centrally and peripherally.[34]

The potential role of CA inhibitors as a therapy in INS was incidentally noted and reported by Thurtell et al. (2010)[3] in an elderly patient, when oral acetazolamide, given for three consecutive days to prevent altitude sickness, improved foveation and INS waveforms over a broadened range of gaze angles. To avoid the systemic side effects of oral acetazolamide, Dell‘Osso et al. (2011)[4] subsequently explored the role of the topical CA inhibitor, brinzolamide, administered for 3 days in the same patient, and reported similar therapeutic effects.

Several studies evaluating brinzolamide as a treatment option for INS ensued. Hertle et al., in 2015,[5] prospectively enrolled five cases of INS ≥ 18 years, to compare the effect of brinzolamide (Azopt) vs placebo, each given for 3 days, and noted a statistically significant improvement in visual acuity, expanded nystagmus acuity function (NAFX) score, and INS waveform characteristics.

In 2017, Nieves‐Moreno et al.[8] conducted a retrospective study on 14 patients with congenital nystagmus of a mean age of 14 ± 11.29 (range: 6–35 years). The authors reported a slight improvement in nystagmus. However, a slight increase in the frequency of nystagmus was found, which was statistically significant in certain gazes. The authors reported statistically significant differences in pre- and post-treatment binocular near visual acuity, yet only two of the patients noticed improvement in visual acuity. The authors concluded that there was a slight improvement in nystagmus, which was not cosmetically appreciable by patients in most cases, and was not related to a significant improvement in visual acuity or in patient quality of life. In contrast, in the present study, 52.5% of parents reported subjective improvement with Azopt, whether cosmetic or functional. Vision improved in 61.5% of those tested by regular charts. The mean improvement in vision, in those who were old enough to be tested by the regular charts, was, nevertheless, insignificant.

In 2018, Aygit et al.[6] conducted a retrospective study to evaluate brinzolamide in 23 nystagmus patients of mean age 12.6 ± 5.5 years (4 to 21 years), of whom 18 had idiopathic INS and 5 had oculocutaneous albinism. The authors reported a reduction of nystagmus in 22.7%, an improvement in vision in 40.9%, and reduced abnormal head position in 18.3% of cases, whereas 18.1% showed no improvement, after 1 week of brinzolamide therapy. Quantitative analysis was not done for nystagmus in this study, and the assessment was based on video recordings. In contrast, in the present study, only six (15%) patients had idiopathic INS, and oculocutaneous albinism was present in 15 patients (37.5%). Reduction of nystagmus was documented in 62.5%, vision improved in 61.5%, and head posture improved in 20%. Although the present study included more patients with ocular or neurological abnormalities than idiopathic ones, the rate of visual improvement was higher than the former study including mostly cases of idiopathic nystagmus. This, plus the lack of significant statistical correlation between outcome and the type of nystagmus (whether idiopathic or associated with other abnormalities), supports the assumption that the effect of Azopt is not affected by the presence or absence of ocular or neurological abnormalities. This agrees with Hertle and DellÓsso (2013),[13] who considered “idiopathic” as a misnomer derived from the mistaken designations of “sensory” and “motor” congenital nystagmus,[14] and that the various treatment options can improve vision in cases with or without associated sensory deficits.

In 2024, Yadav et al.[7] conducted a prospective study to evaluate the effect of brinzolamide vs placebo on visual function and waveforms in INS. They recruited 29 children, 10 years or older (17.65 ± 7.22 years) with idiopathic INS. One group received a placebo for 3 months, followed by a 1-week wash-out period, then brinzolamide, three times daily, for another 3 months. The order was reversed in the second group. A significant improvement was noted in INS waveform characteristics, mean NAFX score (P < 0.001), and mean binocular visual acuity (P < 0.001) with topical brinzolamide in comparison with baseline as well as placebo. No significant change was noted in head position and stereopsis. No side effects were reported. NAFX, although a valuable tool to evaluate the pure effect of nystagmus on vision, was not possible in the present study due to its unavailability.

Previous studies evaluated the role of CA inhibitors in adults, with the exception of that of Aygit et al. (2018),[6] which evaluated cases 4 to 21 years of age, and that of Yadav et al. (2024),[7] that evaluated children older than 10 years of age with a mean age of 17.65 ± 7.22 years. The current study is the first to evaluate the role of topical CA inhibitors (brinzolamide) in infants and children as young as 0.75 years of age (with a mean age of 5.7 ± 3.3 years), and confines all cases to the pediatric age limit of 14 years.

Normal visual development requires appropriate visual stimulation. Visual development is most active and vulnerable during the first 3 months of life, which is termed the critical period of visual development.[15] Visual acuity development continues up to 7 to 8 years of age, but development is slower, and plasticity is progressively less in later childhood. Abnormal visual stimulation during early visual development can result in permanent damage to visual centers in the brain. Early treatment of pediatric eye disorders is, therefore, important to promote normal visual development.[16]

Considering the importance of visual development during the early years, and, more importantly, during the early months of life, the current study was conducted to evaluate the effect of Azopt on nystagmus starting in infancy. The study included cases of a mean age of 5.7 ± 3.3 (0.75 to 14) years, with 34 cases (85%) at the age of 8 or less. Limitations of this, however, were the lack of cooperation at very young ages, necessitating exclusion of some cases due to failure to perform videonystagmography. More importantly, there was the uncertainty regarding the cause of nystagmus improvement in these very young age groups, whether it was due to the effect of Azopt or spontaneous with child growth, especially at the 6-month follow-up. Nevertheless, an improvement at 1 month of Azopt prescription would likely to be related to the effect of the latter, rather than child growth. Similarly, changes compared between follow-up visits at 6 months and 1 month after stop are likely to be related to Azopt rather than age, although 1 month after stop vs entry could be related to age.

In the present study, there was significant and continuous improvement in mean nystagmus amplitudes over the 6 months of Azopt instillation, and even at 1 month after Azopt cessation, there was further improvement (P < 0.05). On the other hand, mean nystagmus SPV, frequency, and intensity, as well as vision, although improved over the 6-month period of Azopt consumption, this improvement weaned off after Azopt cessation, but was still better than at baseline. The effect of Azopt, therefore, persists after its discontinuation in the short-term. Further studies are warranted to test if the effect persists in the long-term as well.

In the present study, nystagmus intensity improved in 62.5% of cases at 1 month of Azopt instillation, but worsened in 32.5%. At 6 months, 12.5% further improved, and 20% worsened. One month after stopping, 10% further improved, whereas 15% worsened. In a previous study, Aygit et al.[6] reported reduction in nystagmus in 22.7% of cases, after 1 week of Azopt prescription. Their lower improvement rate might be attributed to the evaluation after only 1 week in their study vs 1 and 6 months in the present study. This explanation is supported by the fact that further improvements were documented in the present study at 6 months vs 1 month. Extended durations of Azopt prescriptions are warranted, but under close follow-up for weaning off the effect or worsening.

The present study reported an increase (worsening) in nystagmus intensity with Azopt in around one-third of the cases (13 cases, 32.5%) at 1 month compared with baseline. Moreover, eight cases that showed initial improvement at 1 month showed weaning off of effect at 6 months, of whom three became even worse than at entry. None of the previous studies reported worsening of nystagmus, except Nieves‐Moreno et al.,[8] who reported a slight but significant increase in nystagmus frequency with treatment. Interestingly, in the present study, the increase in nystagmus SPV and amplitude was noted in 30% of cases at 1 month, whereas the increase in frequency was noted in 42.5% of cases. This agrees with the previous study that documented an increase in nystagmus frequency with Azopt.

Attempts to find a relation between the outcome of Azopt and various patients’ characteristics revealed no significant relation to any. One might assume that lack of effect in certain patients could be attributed to the presence of another cause for nystagmus rather than a channelopathy. This, however, does not explain the “worsening” of nystagmus encountered in one-third of the cases. An explanation could not be found.

Limitations of the present study included the high rate of drop-out at 6 months, of 62.5% (25 cases). Among these, 42.5% (17 cases) were due to discontinuation of Azopt because of worsening or no improvement. The remaining eight cases (20%) discontinued treatment due to the unavailability of Azopt and/or posing a financial burden, as all study cases belonged to low-income families. Further research evaluating the value of other types of brinzolamide eye drops (as Azarga) is warranted to tackle the problem of eye drops unavailability. Another limitation is that accurate visual acuity measurements using charts were not feasible due to the very young age of many children (17 cases), so only fix and follow responses were documented. Visual changes, therefore, could not be evaluated in these cases. Moreover, NAFX was not possible due to its unavailability, so discrimination between the effect of nystagmus vs other associated pathologies on vision could not be determined. Lastly, patients were examined 1 month after cessation, whereas a longer follow-up (preferably 6 months after cessation) would have been more valuable.

Conclusion

Azopt was found to be promising and safe for the treatment of nystagmus in infants and children. There was a significant reduction in nystagmus on VNG at 1 month, with further significant reduction at 6 months. It is, therefore, advisable to prolong its usage for 6 months. This effect persisted 1 month after cessation, although insignificantly decreased. Nystagmus, however, worsened in around one-third of the cases with Azopt, so careful monitoring of patients is necessary during treatment.
