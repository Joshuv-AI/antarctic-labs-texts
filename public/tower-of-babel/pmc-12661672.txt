Reliability and validity of the Persian version of the Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment-Patella (VISA-P) Questionnaire
Authors: Aynollah Naderi, Hossein Shahrokhi, Hossein ChoromZadeh, Ebrahim Ebrahimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatellar tendinopathy symptoms and functional limitations should be evaluated using validated, language-specific assessment tools. Cultural adaptation of these tools can improve their psychometric properties across languages. The VISA-P questionnaire is the only condition-specific tool for measuring symptom severity, but a validated Persian version is not available.PurposeTo translate, culturally adapt, and evaluate the psychometric properties of the VISA-P questionnaire for use among Persian-speaking athletes.Study designCase–control observational study with a test–retest subgroup; Level of evidence, 3.MethodsThe VISA-P was translated into Persian following international guidelines: forward translation, synthesis, back-translation, expert review, pretesting, and validation. Sixty Persian-speaking athletes (30 with patellar tendinopathy, 30 healthy controls) participated in this cross-sectional study. Psychometric evaluation followed COSMIN standards assessing structural validity (exploratory factor analysis), construct validity (Spearman correlation with the Blazina classification), known-groups validity (comparison between symptomatic and healthy athletes using the Mann–Whitney U test)), internal consistency (Cronbach’s α), and test–retest reliability (ICC over 24–48 h). Face validity, feasibility, and interpretability were also assessed.ResultsThe VISA-P-Pr demonstrated good face and content validity. Factor analysis revealed a two-factor structure explaining 87.1% of variance with strong item loadings. Construct validity was supported by a strong negative correlation with the Blazina classification (rs = –0.75, p < .001), and known-groups validity showed significantly lower scores in athletes with patellar tendinopathy compared to healthy controls (median 65.0 vs 98.5, p < .001). Test–retest reliability was excellent in the tendinopathy group (ICC = 0.91) with good internal consistency across both assessments (Cronbach’s α = 0.88 and 0.89).ConclusionThe VISA-P-Pr is a reliable and valid instrument with psychometric properties comparable to the original VISA-P, suitable for clinical and research use among Persian-speaking athletes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-025-06464-x.Keywords: Jumper’s knee, Cross-cultural, Psychometrics, Validation, Reliability

Introduction

Patellar tendinopathy is a prevalent overuse injury in sports characterized by repetitive jumping and landing, such as volleyball and basketball. It arises from excessive loading of the knee extensor mechanism, leading to cumulative microtrauma within the patellar tendon and localized pain at its insertion [1–4]. The condition is particularly common among athletes who rely on explosive lower-limb power, with reported prevalence rates of 24.8% in volleyball players and 20.8% in basketball players [5, 6].

Accurate clinical diagnosis primarily relies on physical examination. Palpation exhibits higher sensitivity (98%) than the Royal London Hospital test (88%), although the latter demonstrates slightly superior specificity (98% vs. 94%) [7]. Combining these assessments with ultrasound imaging enhances diagnostic confidence and allows evaluation of tendon morphology and thickness [8]. Conservative management—typically involving eccentric and isometric loading exercises combined with physiotherapy—has proven effective in most cases [4, 9]. However, approximately 10% of patients require advanced interventions, such as injection therapies or surgical procedures [3, 10–13].

Among noninvasive options, eccentric training programs and radial extracorporeal shock wave therapy (rESWT) have consistently shown long-term benefits in pain reduction and improvements in VISA-P scores [4, 9]. For refractory cases, High-Volume Image-Guided Injection (HVIGI)—which delivers a mixture of bupivacaine, hydrocortisone, and saline near the patellar tendon—has demonstrated marked improvements in VISA-P outcomes and may represent a promising adjunct pending further controlled research [11, 13]. Overall, a comprehensive approach integrating meticulous clinical assessment, imaging, and targeted interventions is essential for the effective management of patellar tendinopathy [4].

Despite available treatments, patellar tendinopathy remains a significant clinical challenge. Approximately one-third of affected athletes fail to return to sport within six months [14], and more than half eventually retire prematurely due to persistent symptoms within 15 years [15]. Thus, early diagnosis and effective management are critical to preserving athletic performance and preventing chronic disability.

While imaging techniques such as ultrasound and MRI can assist in evaluating anterior knee pain, their utility in diagnosing patellar tendinopathy is limited. There is a well-documented weak correlation between clinical symptoms and ultrasonographic findings [16], and the high cost and limited availability of MRI—particularly in resource-constrained settings—further restrict its routine use [17]. Consequently, clinical evaluation remains the cornerstone of diagnosis, emphasizing the need for reliable, cost-effective, and condition-specific assessment tools.

To address this need, the Victorian Institute of Sport in Australia developed the VISA-P (Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment–Patellar) questionnaire in 1998—a brief, self-reported instrument designed to evaluate symptom severity in athletes with patellar tendinopathy and to monitor treatment outcomes [18]. As the only condition-specific outcome measure currently available for this pathology, the VISA-P has gained widespread acceptance and has been validated in numerous studies as a robust and responsive tool for assessing disease severity.

However, self-report questionnaires must undergo rigorous translation and cultural adaptation to ensure their validity and interpretability across different linguistic and cultural contexts. Simple translation is insufficient, as linguistic, idiomatic, and contextual differences can alter item meaning and response patterns. To maintain measurement equivalence, a standardized methodological approach is required. Following established guidelines for cross-cultural adaptation [19], the VISA-P has been successfully adapted and validated in multiple languages, including Spanish [20], Italian [21], Dutch [22], German [23], Swedish [24], Brazilian [25], Korean [26], Turkish [27], French [28], Greek [29], Canadian [30], Chinese [31], and Arabic [32].

Despite significant advancements in the diagnosis and management of patellar tendinopathy worldwide, the absence of a validated Persian version of the VISA-P scale (VISA-P-Pr) remains a major barrier to effective clinical assessment and research among Persian-speaking populations. Accurate evaluation of symptom severity and functional limitations depends on valid and reliable patient-reported outcome measures. Without a culturally and linguistically adapted tool, clinicians and researchers risk collecting inconsistent or misleading data, undermining treatment monitoring and intervention evaluation—one of the primary sources of error in tendinopathy studies [33]. A standardized instrument like the VISA-P, when appropriately adapted, enhances data quality, facilitates longitudinal tracking, and enables meaningful interpretation of treatment outcomes. Moreover, its availability would support the inclusion of Persian-speaking athletes in multinational research, improving representation and allowing for better integration of findings through systematic reviews and meta-analyses [8, 34].

This study aims to address this gap by translating, culturally adapting, and evaluating the psychometric properties of the VISA-P for Persian-speaking athletes with patellar tendinopathy. We hypothesize that the VISA-P-Pr will demonstrate strong measurement properties, providing a reliable and clinically valuable tool.

Materials and methods

Study design

This cross-sectional observational study was designed and reported in accordance with established methodological guidelines. The COSMIN (Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments) checklist was used as the primary framework to guide the design, statistical analysis, and interpretation of all psychometric evaluations, including reliability, validity, and interpretability of the translated instrument [35]. The STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist was followed to ensure transparent reporting of the cross-sectional design [36], and the CHERRIES (Checklist for Reporting Results of Internet E-Surveys) guidelines were applied to support rigorous conduct and reporting of the online data collection process [37], as all surveys were administered electronically via a secure web-based platform. The study was conducted from June 1 to August 25, 2025, in Shahrood, Sabzevar, and Tehran . Ethical approval was obtained from Hakim Sabzevari University (approval code: IR.HSU.REC.1404.011), and all participants provided informed consent prior to participation.

Participants

This cross-sectional study recruited a total of 60 Persian-speaking athletes—30 with clinically diagnosed patellar tendinopathy and 30 asymptomatic controls—over a six-week period (June 1–July 15, 2025) through sports clubs and physiotherapy clinics in Shahrood, Sabzevar, and Tehran , using a combination of in-person and online outreach to ensure a representative sample. The sample size was justified by psychometric guidelines recommending 5–10 participants per item for the 8-item VISA-P questionnaire [38], providing adequate power for factor analysis, reliability testing, and other key validation metrics. Participants in the tendinopathy group were diagnosed based on hallmark clinical criteria, including localized tenderness at the inferior pole of the patella and pain during provocative loading tasks such as single-leg decline squats, jumping, and landing, with symptoms persisting over the past three months [39, 40]. The healthy control group consisted of individuals with no history or current symptoms of anterior knee pain. The equal allocation between groups strengthened known-groups validity and reduced confounding in comparative analyses, aligning with COSMIN recommendations and supporting a robust, culturally relevant assessment of the Persian version of the VISA-P in sports rehabilitation research.

Eligibility criteria were: (1) age 18–50 years, (2) active participation in sports, (3) willingness to participate, (4) literacy in Persian, and (5) ability to provide informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: history of lower limb fracture or knee surgery, pain in other joints, systemic, rheumatological, or neurological disorders, and insufficient Persian proficiency. Imaging (MRI or ultrasonography) was performed at the clinician’s discretion in some cases to confirm tendon pathology or rule out structural abnormalities, but was not required for inclusion.

All participants attended an in-person baseline assessment at designated clinics, completing a demographic questionnaire (age, sex, sport type and level, affected limb) and the Persian version of the VISA-P questionnaire, along with classification using the Blazina system. To evaluate test–retest reliability, the VISA-P was re-administered 24–48 h later via a secure online platform. A subgroup of athletes with patellar tendinopathy (n = 17) completed the questionnaire a third time five weeks after initiating treatment to assess responsiveness [25, 41]. The fixed sequence of administrations (baseline in person, retest online, responsiveness after treatment) ensured methodological consistency and minimized order effects.

First phase: translation process of the VISA-P questionnaire

The process of translating and culturally adapting the VISA-P questionnaire into Persian was conducted based on two well-established international frameworks: the “Guidelines for the Process of Cross-Cultural Adaptation of Self-Report Measures [19]” and the guidelines provided by the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) [42]. The goal was to ensure both conceptual and functional equivalence with the original English version. Initially, two independent translators—one an expert in English language and the other a sports medicine specialist—translated the questionnaire into Persian. One translator was informed about the study's objectives, while the other was not, in order to minimize bias. These translations were then reviewed in a video conference with the translators and the research team, resulting in a unified Persian version. This version was subsequently back-translated into English by two bilingual translators, fluent in Persian and unfamiliar with the original version. The back-translations were compared with the original English version to identify any discrepancies. A committee of experts, including the translators, research team members, and clinical specialists, was then formed to finalize the pre-final Persian version. To assess clarity and comprehension, this version was administered to a distinct group of 30 Persian-speaking athletes without symptoms of tendinopathy, who were not included in the second phase’s validation sample, ensuring independent cohorts for adaptation and psychometric evaluation. Participants completed the questionnaire and took part in structured interviews to provide feedback on each item. Analysis of their responses indicated that all items were well understood; however, minor wording revisions were made to two questions to enhance fluency. Finally, the Persian version of the VISA-P questionnaire was finalized after thorough review by the translation team and approval by the original author of the questionnaire. This version was then used for psychometric evaluation.

Second phase: evaluation of the measurement properties of the VISA-P

Validity

Face validity assesses whether questionnaire items appear relevant, clear, and comprehensible to experts and users. In this study, it was evaluated through semi-structured interviews with participants (n = 15, athletes with patellar tendinopathy) using a think-aloud protocol for qualitative feedback on clarity, relevance, and comprehensibility of each VISA-P-Pr item. The research authors also conducted a consensus-based review in a committee meeting to assess these attributes.

Content validity assesses whether a tool comprehensively captures all key aspects of the concept it measures [43]. In this study, content validity of the VISA-P-Pr was evaluated by a dedicated expert panel, distinct from the first phase’s translation committee, comprising clinical specialists (orthopedic surgeons and physiotherapists) and two patient representatives with a history of patellar tendinopathy. The panel used a standardized form to assess the relevance, clarity, and representativeness of each VISA-P-Pr item, ensuring the inclusion of the patient perspective.

This study employed exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with varimax rotation to assess the structural validity of the Persian version of the VISA-P-Pr, aiming to determine whether its items coherently and meaningfully load onto one or more underlying factors. Before extracting factors, data suitability was evaluated using the Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy (with values ≥ 0.60 considered acceptable) and Bartlett’s test of sphericity (p < 0.05 indicating sufficient correlations among items). Factors were extracted through principal axis factoring and retained based on eigenvalues greater than one, scree plot inspection, and conceptual interpretability. Items with factor loadings of 0.40 or above were deemed significant contributors [44].

Construct validity refers to the degree to which a measurement instrument’s scores are consistent with theoretical expectations regarding their relationships with other relevant variables [45, 46]. In this study, construct validity of the Persian version of the VISA-P-Pr was evaluated by examining its correlation with the Blazina classification system, a widely recognized, condition-specific staging tool for patellar tendinopathy [47]. Although no universal gold standard exists for assessing this condition, the Blazina scale is clinically valuable for evaluating the impact of tendon pain on functional performance during athletic activities. It has been used in previous validation studies of the VISA-P, including the Greek, German, and Canadian English versions, where it demonstrated strong, inverse correlations with VISA-P scores. Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to assess the relationship between the VISA-P-Pr and the Blazina scale. Given the expected inverse relationship—higher pain severity (worse Blazina stage) corresponding to lower VISA-P scores—a moderate to strong negative correlation (r ≤ o.50) was hypothesized, supporting adequate construct validity.

Known-groups validity, a form of construct validity, evaluates the ability of a measurement instrument to distinguish between groups that are expected to differ in the construct being measured based on theoretical or empirical evidence. For this assessment to be meaningful, the groups must be clearly distinct in their clinical or functional status, and the expected differences should be supported by previous research. In this study, the known-groups validity of the VISA-P-Pr was assessed by comparing scores between two predefined groups: athletes with clinically diagnosed patellar tendinopathy and healthy athletes without knee pain or a history of tendon injury. Based on previous validation studies of the VISA-P in other languages [23, 48], it was hypothesized that individuals with patellar tendinopathy would report significantly lower (worse) VISA-P-Pr scores than healthy controls, reflecting greater symptom severity and functional limitation. Independent t test was used to evaluate the differences between the two groups.

Reliability

Test–retest reliability evaluates the stability of a questionnaire by assessing the consistency of results when administered to the same individuals on two separate occasions, assuming no change in their health status [49]. In this study, the VISA-P-Pr was completed twice within a 24- to 48-h interval. This timeframe was chosen to reduce the likelihood of actual clinical changes while minimizing the risk of participants recalling their previous responses. Test–retest reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, model 2,1), with a minimum acceptable value of 0.70. Measurement precision was assessed using the Standard Error of Measurement (SEM), calculated as SEM = SD × √ (1 − r). The Minimal Detectable Change (MDC), indicating the smallest change that exceeds measurement error, was computed using the formula MDC = SEM × 1.96 × √2. Both SEM and MDC are reported in the same units as the original measurement.

To evaluate internal consistency, Cronbach’s alpha (α) was calculated, with values between 0.70 and 0.95 deemed acceptable in line with COSMIN guidelines, reflecting satisfactory inter-item correlation without redundancy [49, 50]. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted, in which each item was sequentially removed and the alpha coefficient recalculated. This procedure allowed for the identification of items that may undermine the scale’s internal consistency and informed considerations for future refinement.

Responsiveness

Responsiveness reflects an instrument’s capability to detect meaningful clinical changes over time, even subtle shifts in a patient's condition. The responsiveness of the VISA-P-Pr was assessed in a subset of 17 individuals with patellar tendinopathy who agreed to follow-up and were undergoing physical therapy treatments. These interventions were not standardized, aligning with routine clinical practice. Participants completed the VISA-P-Pr a third time, five weeks after the initial assessment (T2), to evaluate changes corresponding to their clinical progress. The magnitude of change was quantified using effect sizes, calculated as the difference between follow-up and baseline means divided by the baseline standard deviation. Effect sizes were interpreted as trivial (≤ 0.20), small (0.21–0.49), moderate (0.50–0.79), large (≥ 0.80). Moderate to large changes were anticipated among participants who experienced clinical improvement, indicating the tool’s ability to capture individual progress [51].

Feasibility and interpretability

Feasibility was evaluated by assessing the rate of missing responses, which reflects participant burden and the ease of completing the questionnaire. A low proportion of missing data indicates that the VISA-P is practical, user-friendly, and well-tolerated by the target population. Interpretability was assessed within the group of athletes diagnosed with patellar tendinopathy by analyzing floor and ceiling effects, defined as the percentage of participants scoring the minimum (0) or maximum (10) on each item. A floor or ceiling effect was considered present if more than 15% of participants scored at either extreme. These effects may indicate limited discriminative ability at the severe or mild ends of the symptom spectrum and could influence the instrument’s content validity and responsiveness [52].

Measurement instruments

VISA-P questionnaire

The VISA-P questionnaire (Victorian Institute of Sport Assessment – Patellar Tendon) is an 8-item self-reported tool designed to assess symptoms and functional limitations in individuals with patellar tendinopathy (Persian and English versions are attached). It is widely used in clinical and sports research to measure disease severity and monitor response to treatment. Of the 8 items, six are scored using a Likert scale ranging from 0 to 10, where a score of 10 indicates no pain or disability, and a score of 0 represents maximum symptom severity. Item 7 also uses a Likert-style format but includes four fixed response options (0, 4, 7, or 10) based on the individual's ability to perform jumping activities. Item 8 consists of three categorical options (8a, 8b, or 8c), of which only one must be selected, reflecting the level of pain experienced during sports activities. This item is scored from 0 to 30. The total VISA-P score is calculated by summing the scores of all items and ranges from 0 to 100. A score of 100 indicates full function and absence of symptoms, whereas a score of 0 reflects the highest degree of symptom severity and functional limitation [18].

Blazina classification system

The Blazina classification system, introduced in 1973, is a widely used clinical framework for staging patellar tendinopathy based on pain severity and functional limitations during physical activity [47]. It outlines five stages, ranging from no pain (Stage 0) to complete tendon rupture (Stage 4). In this study, after obtaining informed consent and confirming eligibility, participants' demographic data were collected, and their condition was classified according to the Blazina stages (Table 1) [53] This classification aided in evaluating construct validity—through correlation with VISA-P-Pr scores—and in assessing symptom severity across clinical stages.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (Version 29.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics (mean ± SD for continuous variables; frequencies and percentages for categorical variables) were used to summarize participants’ demographic, clinical, and questionnaire data. Structural validity was evaluated via exploratory factor analysis (EFA) with varimax rotation; factors were extracted based on eigenvalues > 1, and items with factor loadings ≥ 0.40 were retained. Construct validity was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (rs) between the total VISA-P-Pr score and the Blazina classification system, with correlations interpreted as weak (rs ≤ 0.35), moderate (0.36 ≤ rs < 0.67), or strong (0.68 ≤ rs < 1.0) [54]. Known-groups validity was examined using the Mann–Whitney U test to compare scores between participants with patellar tendinopathy and healthy controls. Reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s α for internal consistency (acceptable: 0.70–0.79; good: 0.80–0.89; excellent: ≥ 0.90) and the intraclass correlation coefficient ICC(2,1) with absolute agreement for test–retest reliability (poor: < 0.50; moderate: 0.51–0.75; good: 0.76–0.90; excellent: > 0.90) [55]. Responsiveness was assessed in the treatment subgroup (n = 17) using paired t-tests and standardized response mean (SRM). Floor and ceiling effects were considered present if more than 15% of participants scored the minimum (0) or maximum (100) total score. Missing data were minimal (< 2%) and addressed through listwise deletion. No adjustments for confounders were made, as groups were clinically distinct and matched on inclusion criteria. No interaction or sensitivity analyses were conducted, as the study was not powered for these. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05 for all tests.

Results

No major problems arose during translation; minor discrepancies were resolved through consensus, and the expert committee approved the final VISA-P-Pr version and its cross-cultural adaptation process. From 83 screened athletes (45 with patellar tendinopathy, 38 healthy), 60 (30 with patellar tendinopathy, 30 healthy) met inclusion criteria and completed the VISA-P-Pr questionnaire twice, 24–48 h apart, with all included in the analyses. Eighteen were excluded due to declining participation (10), not meeting diagnostic criteria (7), or incomplete baseline data (6). The participants had a mean age of 26.9 years (SD = 4.9), with 40 men (66.7%). Of these, 17 were receiving treatment for patellar tendinopathy and also completed the questionnaire five weeks after the first assessment. All demographic and clinical data (age, sex, height, weight, BMI, affected side, level of sport) were complete with no missing values. Validity

The VISA-P-Pr questionnaire demonstrated good face validity as rated by both the expert committee and participants, and its content validity was considered well addressed. Exploratory factor analysis (EFA) identified a clear two-factor structure accounting for 87.1% of the total variance (Factor 1: 64.36%, Factor 2: 22.66%), with initial eigenvalues of 5.15 and 1.81, respectively, indicating that two dominant constructs effectively capture the majority of item variance. Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (χ2(28) = 305, p < 0.001), confirming sufficient inter-item correlations, and the overall Kaiser–Meyer–Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.869, reflecting meritorious sampling adequacy, with individual MSA values ranging from 0.642 to 0.940, all above the recommended 0.50 threshold. All items loaded strongly on their respective factors (0.81–0.94), demonstrating low item uniqueness and strong construct associations, with no cross-loadings above 0.35, ensuring interpretability and supporting the retention of all items based on primary loadings (Table 3). Construct validity was supported by a strong negative correlation with the modified Blazina classification (rs = –0.75, 95% CI [–0.86, –0.52], p < 0.001) among athletes with patellar tendinopathy. Known-groups validity was supported, as athletes with patellar tendinopathy scored significantly lower (mean = 60.5, SD = 13.8) than healthy controls (mean = 97.6, SD = 3.0; t = 14.30, p < 0.001). A large between-group difference was observed, with a Cohen’s d of 3.70 (95% CI [2.85, 4.53]), indicating that the scores of the tendinopathy group were substantially and consistently lower than those of the control group.

Reliability

The results of the test–retest reliability analysis are presented in Tables 4 and 5. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) for the VISA-P-Pr was 0.964 (95% CI: 0.89–0.99) overall, with ICC values of 0.91 (95% CI: 0.83–0.96) in athletes with patellar tendinopathy and 0.72 (95% CI: 0.50–0.86) in healthy control athletes, indicating excellent test–retest reliability in the patellar tendinopathy group and moderate reliability in the healthy control group. The standard error of measurement (SEM) was 1.68 for healthy controls and 4.02 for those with patellar tendinopathy. The minimal detectable change at the 95% confidence level (MDC₉₅) was calculated as 4.66 for healthy athletes and 11.14 for athletes with patellar tendinopathy. Internal consistency was good at both assessment points (Cronbach’s α = 0.91 and 0.92 overall), with Cronbach’s α values of 0.88 and 0.89 in athletes with patellar tendinopathy, and 0.86 and 0.87 in healthy control athletes at the first and second assessments, respectively.

Responsiveness

During the assessment of responsiveness, a significant improvement was observed in the tendinopathy group between the first and third administrations of the VISA-P-Pr, t(16) = 4.79, p < 0.001. The mean change in VISA-P-Pr score was 12.29 (SD = 10.56) points. The standardized response mean (SRM) was 1.19, 95% CI [0.56, 1.80], indicating a large responsiveness.

Feasibility and interpretability

The VISA-P-Pr showed excellent feasibility and good interpretability, with a very low missing response rate (1.8%), indicating strong acceptability. Floor and ceiling effects were minimal (0% and 4.0% on average), and no item exceeded the 15% threshold, confirming an adequate score range and good discriminative ability.

Discussion

The findings of this study indicated that the VISA-P questionnaire was successfully translated and culturally adapted into Persian, and its Persian version (VISA-P-Pr) demonstrated adequate content and conceptual equivalence with the original version. Psychometric analyses revealed that this instrument has favorable face and content validity, satisfactory internal consistency, excellent test–retest reliability, and appropriate ability to discriminate between healthy individuals and patients with patellar tendinopathy in the Persian-speaking athletic population.

The Persian version of the VISA-P-Pr, following a rigorous translation and cultural adaptation process, demonstrated a robust two-dimensional factor structure, excellent reliability, strong construct and known-group validity, and adequate responsiveness. Exploratory factor analysis revealed two factors explaining 87.1% of total variance: Factor 1 (64.36%) comprised items 1–6 assessing pain and symptoms during daily and functional activities, while Factor 2 (22.66%) included items 7–8 capturing physical participation and tolerance to sports activity. Factor loadings were high (0.81–0.94) with no problematic items, indicating strong factorial validity. In line with the Greek and Spanish adaptations, the Persian VISA-P demonstrated a robust two-factor structure reflecting pain/symptoms and functional participation [20, 29]. Notably, our model accounted for 87.1% of total variance with exceptionally high factor loadings (0.81–0.94) and no problematic items, suggesting stronger factorial validity than reported in the Spanish version (76.1% variance, loadings > 0.60) [20] and avoiding the instability observed in the Swedish version, which required a three-factor solution due to low communality of one item [24]. These findings highlight the cultural adaptability of the VISA-P and the psychometric strength of the Persian version.

The construct validity of the VISA-P-Pr was strongly supported by a significant negative correlation with the modified Blazina classification (rs = –0.75, 95% CI [–0.86, –0.52]), indicating that lower scores correspond to greater clinical severity of patellar tendinopathy. This result aligns with previous cross-cultural adaptations, including the Traditional Chinese version (r = –0.899), German (ρ = –0.81), and Greek (ρ = –0.839), all demonstrating similarly strong associations with the Blazina scale [23, 29, 48]. While the Blazina classification is limited to four clinical stages, it remains a widely used, condition-specific reference in VISA-P validation studies [47]. Further support comes from the Japanese version, showing moderate to strong correlations with Roels’ classification and VAS measures (r = –0.52 to –0.86), as well as the Arabic (r = 0.63 with SF-36), Brazilian Portuguese (r = 0.60 with Lysholm), and Spanish versions (ρ = 0.897 with Kujala; ρ = 0.782 with Cincinnati), all confirming meaningful associations with established functional and symptom measures [20, 25, 32, 56]. Known-group validity was also confirmed, with athletes with patellar tendinopathy scoring lower than healthy controls, consistent with prior translations [20, 23, 24, 26, 29, 32, 48], collectively supporting the VISA-P’s convergent validity and cross-cultural applicability.

Our study demonstrated that the VISA-P-Pr exhibits strong psychometric properties, with good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.88), well within the recommended range of 0.7–0.95 [52], aligning with prior cross-cultural adaptations that reported acceptable to excellent reliability across diverse populations [20, 24–26, 29, 32, 48, 56, 57]. Test–retest reliability was excellent among athletes with patellar tendinopathy (ICC = 0.91) and moderate in healthy controls (ICC = 0.72), yielding an overall ICC of 0.964, indicating high stability and reproducibility over time. The slightly lower ICC in healthy participants likely reflects a ceiling effect due to clustered high scores (98.5 ± 3.0), which reduces variability and may underestimate reliability. A 24- to 48-h interval between assessments minimized clinical changes, aligning with recommended intervals for such evaluations [58]. Across translations with intervals ranging from 30 min to 2 weeks, test–retest reliability consistently ranged from good to excellent (ICC = 0.74–0.99) [22, 28], further supporting the VISA-P-Pr’s robustness for evaluating patellar tendinopathy in both symptomatic and asymptomatic populations [20, 22–26, 29, 32, 48, 56, 57].

In our study, the VISA-P-Pr showed SEMs of 1.68 for healthy controls and 4.02 for athletes with patellar tendinopathy, with MDC₉₅ values of 4.66 and 11.14, respectively. These findings are comparable to those reported in other VISA-P adaptations, reflecting consistency in measurement precision across populations. For example, the Brazilian Portuguese version reported SEM of 5.2 and MDC90 of 12.2 in patients [25], while the Traditional Chinese version had SEMs of 1.43 (control) and 4.85 (patients) with MDCs of 3.98 and 13.45, closely aligning with our results[48]. Similarly, German and Greek adaptations demonstrated higher SEM and MDC in patient groups compared to controls, indicating that measurement error is greater in symptomatic populations, which is expected due to higher score variability [23, 29]. These comparisons suggest that the VISA-P-Pr exhibits robust reliability and sensitivity, consistent with international versions, and that its SEM and MDC are within acceptable ranges for detecting clinically meaningful changes in both healthy and symptomatic Persian-speaking athletes.

The VISA-P-Pr demonstrated strong responsiveness in Persian-speaking athletes with patellar tendinopathy, evidenced by significant improvements in post-intervention scores (mean change = 12.29 ± 10.56) and a large SRM (1.19), confirming its sensitivity to clinical changes. These results are consistent with other cross-cultural adaptations, such as the Brazilian Portuguese version (effect size = 0.97) [25] and the Spanish version (mean change = 15.23 ± 13.01; standardized response mean = 1.148) [20], both of which demonstrated good responsiveness. Taken together, these findings supporting its utility for monitoring treatment outcomes in both clinical practice and research, with the high effect size in our study further confirming its reliability in Persian-speaking populations.

The VISA-P-Pr showed excellent feasibility and good interpretability, with a low missing response rate (1.8%) and minimal floor (0%) and ceiling (4.0%) effects, indicating a suitable score range and strong ability to discriminate between individuals. These findings align with previous cross-cultural adaptations: the Brazilian Portuguese version reported 4% at score extremes [25], the Filipino version had 12.5% maximum scores (no minimum) [57], the Japanese version showed no floor or ceiling effects [56], and the Spanish version reported 6.7% maximum scores (no minimum) [20], with all individual items below standard thresholds. Overall, the results confirm the questionnaire’s high acceptability, robust psychometric performance, and cross-cultural applicability.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. The study employed convenience sampling with a relatively small sample size (30 athletes with patellar tendinopathy and 30 healthy athletes) recruited from a single region in Iran. This limited sample size may increase the risk of type II error and restrict the generalizability of the results to broader populations. Additionally, a larger sample size could further strengthen the evaluation of the Persian VISA-P’s psychometric properties, particularly for the healthy control group, where moderate test–retest reliability (ICC = 0.72) was observed, potentially due to limited score variability. The participant cohort was predominantly male (66.7%), which may limit the applicability of the findings to female athletes or more diverse populations. Eligibility for the symptomatic group was determined through clinical assessment, including localized tendon tenderness and pain during functional tests such as single-leg decline squats, jumping, and landing [39, 40]. However, the heterogeneous nature of patellar tendinopathy suggests that incorporating imaging modalities, such as Doppler ultrasound or MRI, could have provided more objective confirmation of tendon pathology, potentially enhancing the accuracy of group classification. Finally, the lower test–retest reliability in the healthy control group (ICC = 0.72) may be attributed to a ceiling effect (mean score ≈ 98.5), where reduced score variability likely underestimated the reliability of the Persian VISA-P in asymptomatic individuals.

Several measures were implemented to minimize potential sources of bias. Standardized inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied to ensure a homogeneous sample and reduce selection bias. The translation and cross-cultural adaptation of the VISA-P-Pr followed international guidelines, minimizing linguistic and cultural bias, while psychometric evaluation adhered to COSMIN standards to ensure methodological rigor. The inclusion of a healthy control group alongside athletes with patellar tendinopathy allowed meaningful group comparisons and supported known-groups validity. Validated instruments (VISA-P-Pr, Blazina classification) were used to reduce measurement bias, and data collection was performed by trained assessors following a standardized protocol to limit observer bias. To reduce recall and reporting bias, participants completed questionnaires independently, while test–retest reliability data were collected within 24–48 h to prevent clinical changes from influencing results. Missing data were minimal (< 2%) and handled using listwise deletion, thereby limiting bias due to incomplete information. Finally, statistical analyses were conducted by an independent researcher blinded to participants’ group status to minimize analytical bias.

Conclusion

The VISA-P was successfully culturally adapted and validated for use in Persian-speaking athletes. The psychometric properties of the VISA-P-Pr are comparable to the original English version and other internationally adapted versions, enhancing overall confidence in the tool. VISA-P-Pr can be recommended as a reliable instrument for assessing the impact of symptoms on function and sports performance in Persian-speaking athletes with patellar tendinopathy.
