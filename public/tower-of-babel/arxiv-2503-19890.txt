Observability of linear systems on the Heisenberg Lie group
Victor Ayala, Thiago Matheus Cavalheiro, Alexandre J. Santana

Abstract
In control theory, understanding the observability property of a system is crucial for effectively managing and controlling dynamical systems. This property empowers us to deduce the internal state of a system from its outputs over time, even when direct measurements are impossible. By harnessing observability, we can accurately estimate the complete state of a system and reconstruct its dynamics using just limited information. In this work, we will find conditions for observability of linear systems in the three dimensional Heisenberg group ℋ \mathcal{H} . Considering the homomorphisms between the group and its simply connected subgroups, whose kernel is denoted by K K , we will find sufficient conditions for observability on the system using a quotient space ℋ / K \mathcal{H}/K as the output.



1
Introduction

In the context of control theory, the observability property of a system relates to the ability to infer the internal state of a dynamical system based on its outputs over time. This property is critical for designing systems in which the full state space cannot be directly measured but must be estimated through available outputs. Moreover, observability enables us to reconstruct the entire dynamics from limited information.

A compelling example comes from the renowned Pontryaguin’s Maximum Principle,
[
13
]
which earned him the Lenin Prize in Russia in the early sixties.

Consider the challenge of stopping a train at a railway station as quickly as possible.

This situation represents a linear control system on the Euclidean plane. In this scenario, the first variable,
x(t)
, indicates the distance from the train to the station
(0,0)
at time
t
, while the second one, its derivative,
y(t)
, represents the velocity. The control mechanism is based on acceleration. Therefore, the optimal problem reduces to taking any initial condition on the plane to the origin in minimum time.

Remarkably, we can fully reconstruct an optimal trajectories on the plane by using the projection onto the x-axis as the output, from a curve of distances. This means that the information provided by a one-dimensional subspace allows us to recover the entire optimal curve in the plane for any initial position of the train. In contrast, the velocity alone does not provide sufficient information.

This example underscores the importance of observability: it enables us to develop effective solutions with fewer variables while highlighting the critical components of the system. Embracing this concept enhances efficiency and sharpens our focus on what truly matters in control systems.

The dynamic of the train example is determined by a real matrix of order two and a column vector. This model can be readily extended to n-dimensional Euclidean space as follows.

Consider the linear control system

\Sigma:\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\dot{x(t)}&=&Ax(t)+Bu(t)\\
y(x(t))&=&Cx(t)\end{array}\right.,

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
,
B\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
C\in\mathbb{R}^{l\times n}
, with
l<n
and

.

The variable
y
is the output map defined on
\mathbb{R}^{l}
and computed by the matrix

. Let

be the solution of the system starting in

, and control

.

The system

is

is global observable (observable), if it is observable in any element

There exists a computable algebraic characterization of this property, as follows,

\Sigma
is observable if and only if
\hbox{rank}(\mathcal{O})=n
.
Here,
\mathcal{O}=[C\hbox{ }CA\hbox{ }...\hbox{ }CA^{n-1}]^{T}

Preliminaries and Notations

As we saw in the introduction, the observability property of a linear control system
\Sigma
on the Euclidean space its determined by the pair

(A,y)
, i.e., the drift and the output map.
The flow of
A
in
\Sigma

, which is an automorphism. In the Lie group context this matrix is replaced by a linear vector fields, which means by definition that its flows is a 1-parameter groups of Aut(G), the Lie group of G-automorphisms, exactly as in the Euclidean case. In a more algebraic way, it is worth to mention that the Lie bracket between the drift and any left invariant vector field gives a new element of the Lie algebra, in both cases.
On the other hand, the linear map
C
is changed by a projection from the group to a homogeneous space of
G
.

Denotes by
\mathfrak{X}(G)
the Lie algebra of all smooth vector fields on
G

\mathfrak{g}
related to
\mathfrak{X}(G)
appears in
[
4
]
, and is defined by

\text{norm}_{\mathfrak{X}(G)}(\mathfrak{g})=\{\mathfrak{X}{}\in\mathfrak{X}(G)\mid\text{ad}(\mathcal{X})(Y)=[\mathcal{X},Y]\in\mathfrak{g},\ \forall Y\in\mathfrak{g}\}.

A vector field
\mathcal{X}\in\text{norm}_{\mathfrak{X}(G)}(\mathfrak{g})
is called affine. And, if
\mathcal{X}(e)=0
then it is linear, where

stands for the identity element of
G
. It is important to note that for any constant admissible control in either the Euclidean or Lie group context, all vector fields in these systems belong to the normalizer.

Consider

a subgroup of

\pi_{K}:G\longrightarrow G/K

The pair

Now, denote by

3-

.

According to
[

Consider

The 3-dimensional Heisenberg Lie group

In this main section, we begin by reviewing key facts about the Heisenberg Lie group
\mathcal{H}
. Then, after studying the fixed points of the linear vector field, describing the closed and simply connected subgroups
H

3.1

Linear systems on
\mathcal{H}

Consider the Heisenberg group

\mathcal{H}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}1&y&z\\
0&1&x\\
0&0&1\end{bmatrix}\in GL_{3}(\mathbb{R}):x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}\right\}

endowed with the matrix product. Then
\mathcal{H}
is a
3-
dimensional Lie group, whose Lie algebra is given by

\mathfrak{h}=\left\{\begin{bmatrix}0&y&z\\
0&0&x\\
0&0&0\end{bmatrix}\in\mathfrak{gl}_{3}(\mathbb{R}):x,y,z\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

with the usual matrix Lie bracket. In particular,
\mathcal{H}
is diffeomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the product

(x,y,z)\cdot(w,s,t)=(x+w+yt,y+s,z+t)

(1)

and the Lie algebra being
\mathbb{R}^{3}
itself, with the Lie bracket

[(x,y,z),(a,b,c)]=(0,0,ya-bx).

According to Jouan
[
7
]
, the derivations of
\mathfrak{h}
, in the basis
(X,Y,Z)
are given by

D=\begin{bmatrix}a&b&0\\
c&d&0\\
e&f&a+d\end{bmatrix}.

Also, the linear vector fields are expressed in the form

\mathcal{X}(x,y,z)=\left(ax+by,cx+dy,ex+fy+(a+d)z\right)

which generates the following system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\dot{x}&=&ax+by\\
\dot{y}&=&cx+dy\\
\dot{z}&=&ex+fy+(a+d)z\end{array}\right.

(2)

whose solution with initial condition
(x,y,z)
is given by

\varphi(t,(x,y,z))=\left(e^{tA}(x,y),\left\langle e^{t(a+d)}\int_{0}^{t}e^{s(A-(a+d)I_{2})^{T}}(e,f)ds,(x,y)\right\rangle+e^{t(a+d)}z\right)

(3)

where
A=\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{bmatrix}
.

Remark 3.1
.

It is clear that
e^{tA}(x,y)
is the solution of the first and the second lines in the linear system (
2
). Now, for the third line, derivating the map

f(t)=\left\langle e^{t(a+d)}\int_{0}^{t}e^{s(A-(a+d)I_{2})^{T}}(e,f)ds,(x,y)\right\rangle+e^{t(a+d)}z,

we get

f^{\prime}(t)=\langle e^{t(a+d)}e^{t(A-(a+d)I_{2})^{T}}(e,f),(x,y)\rangle+(a+d)f(t).

If we suppose that

e^{tA}=\begin{bmatrix}g_{11}(t)&g_{12}(t)\\
g_{21}(t)&g_{22}(t)\end{bmatrix},

we get
e^{t(a+d)}e^{t(A-(a+d)I_{2})^{T}}=(e^{tA})^{T}
and

(e^{tA})^{T}(e,f)=(g_{11}(t)e+g_{21}(t)f,g_{12}(t)e+g_{22}(t)f).

Therefore

\langle(e^{tA})^{T}(e,f),(x,y)\rangle=e(g_{11}(t)x+g_{12}(t)y)+f(g_{21}(t)x+g_{22}(t)y)=ex(t)+fy(t),

as claimed.

In particular, considering
\alpha
and
\beta
the roots of the characteristic polynomial of
A

e^{tA}=s_{0}(t)I+s_{1}(t)A,

(1-\alpha t)e^{t\alpha},&\alpha=\beta\end{array}\right.

(5)

and

s_{1}(t)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\dfrac{e^{t\alpha}-e^{t\beta}}{\alpha-\beta},&\alpha\neq\beta\\
te^{t\alpha},&\alpha=\beta\end{array}\right.

Conclusion and future research

In this article, we introduced a precise method for determining the observability property of general pairs within the Heisenberg three-dimensional Lie group. This technique utilizes homomorphisms between the group and its simply connected subgroups, resulting in a quotient space as the output. Under certain assumptions about the system and the subgroup in question, we established several results that can be valuable for analyzing the system’s dynamics from the perspective of the output space.

To avoid impractical scenarios, we identified subgroups in which the system cannot be observed, as well as some unusual cases where the system exhibits instabilities, as discussed in Remark (
3.4
). This analysis uses results referenced in Section 2.

For future research, we strongly believe that the techniques employed here could also be valuable for examining observability in non-nilpotent solvable affine three-dimensional Lie groups, which will be our next focus.
