Abstract
. We give another bit of evidence that forcing axioms provide proper framework for rigidity of quotient structures, by improving the OCA lifting theorem proved by the author in late 20th century and greatly simplifying its proof. In the assumptions of this theorem, Martin’s Axiom is weakened to MA ⁡ ( σ − linked ) \MAsigma and, in case of countably generated ideals, completely removed. (In case of the Frechét ideal this was done by De Bondt, Vignati, and the author.) We also extend the conclusion of author’s 2004 lifting theorem from a lifting result for countably 3204-determined ideals to one for countably 80-determined ideals and also weaken PFA \PFA to OCA_T \OCAT and MA ⁡ ( σ − linked ) \MAsigma in its assumptions.

1.1.
Approximations to ideals and ldeals countably determined by closed approximations

For subsets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
consider their ‘Minkowski union’,

\mathcal{A}\,\underline{\cup}\,\mathcal{B}=\{A\cup B\mid A\in\mathcal{A},B\in\mathcal{B}\}.

The notion of a closed approximation to an ideal first appeared in
[
14
]
.

Definition 1.1
.

A set
{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
is
hereditary
if
A\subseteq B\in{\mathcal{K}}
implies
A\in{\mathcal{K}}
.
If
{\mathcal{K}}
and
\mathcal{L}
are families of subsets of
\mathbb{N}
, then

{\mathcal{K}}\,\underline{\cup}\,\mathcal{L}=\{K\cup L:K\in{\mathcal{K}}\text{ and }L\in\mathcal{L}\}

and

{\mathcal{K}}^{k}={\mathcal{K}}\,\underline{\cup}\,\dots\,\underline{\cup}\,{\mathcal{K}}=\{A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup\dots\cup A_{k}:A_{i}\in{\mathcal{K}}\text{ for $i\leq k$}\}.

Note that
{\mathcal{K}}\,\underline{\cup}\,\operatorname{Fin}=\bigcup_{n}\{A\mid A\setminus n\in{\mathcal{K}}\}
. We say that
{\mathcal{K}}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
is an
approximation to
{\mathcal{I}}
if it is hereditary and
{\mathcal{I}}\subseteq{\mathcal{K}}\,\underline{\cup}\,\operatorname{Fin}
. An approximation is closed if it is closed (as a subset of the Cantor space).

We will be primarily interested in closed approximations.
The following definition comes from
[
8
]
.

Definition 1.2
.

An ideal
{\mathcal{I}}
is
countably determined
by closed approximations
if there are closed hereditary
sets
{\mathcal{K}}_{n}
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
) such that

(a)

{\mathcal{I}}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}({\mathcal{K}}_{n}\,\underline{\cup}\,\operatorname{Fin}).

If in addition for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(b)

{\mathcal{I}}=\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}({\mathcal{K}}_{n}^{d}\,\underline{\cup}\,\operatorname{Fin})

we say that
{\mathcal{I}}
is
countably
d
-determined
by closed (analytic, etc.) approximations.
If there are closed hereditary
{\mathcal{K}}_{n}
such that (b) holds for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
,
we say that
{\mathcal{I}}
is
strongly countably determined

by
{\mathcal{K}}_{n}
(
n\in\mathbb{N}
).

Clearly, every ideal countably determined by closed approximations
is
F_{\sigma\delta}
.
Our Theorem
1.4
, will be proven
for ideals that are countably 80-determined by closed approximations.
No care is taken to assure the optimality of this constant, in particular because all known

d

1.2.
Nonmeager hereditary sets

Definition 1.5
.

If
{\mathcal{X}}
is a family of subsets of
\mathbb{N}

by
\hat{\mathcal{X}}
we denote the
downwards closure
(or, the
hereditary closure
) of
{\mathcal{X}}
, i.e.,

\hat{\mathcal{X}}=\bigcup_{a\in{\mathcal{X}}}{\mathcal{P}}(a)
, and we
say that
{\mathcal{X}}
is
hereditary
if

{\mathcal{X}}=\hat{\mathcal{X}}
.

The following is
[
7
, Theorem 3.10.1]
. It is a slight strengthening of results of Jalali–Naini (
[

]
) and Talagrand (
[

Theorem 1.6
.

(1)

If
{\mathcal{X}}
is a hereditary subset of
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
,
then it
is nonmeager
if and only if for every sequence
s_{i}
of disjoint finite sets of
integers there is an infinite
a\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that

\bigcup_{i\in a}s_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}}
.

(2)

The family of nonmeager hereditary subsets of
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
is closed
under taking finite intersections of its elements.

(3)

The family of nonmeager hereditary subsets
of
{\mathcal{P}}(\mathbb{N})
which are closed under finite changes of their elements
is closed under taking countable intersections. ∎

The following refinement of Theorem
1.6
(
3
) will be useful.

Lemma 1.7
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is a hereditary nonmeager set, then there exists
k
such that for every
s\Subset[k,\infty)
the set

\mathcal{H}[s]=\{a\subseteq\mathbb{N}\mid s\cup a\in\mathcal{H}\}

is hereditary and nonmeager.
In particular, the intersection of
\mathcal{H}
with every nonempty open subset of
\mathcal{P}([k,\infty))
is relatively nonmeager, and the set
\mathcal{H}\,\underline{\cup}\,\mathcal{P}(k)
is hereditary, nonmeager, and closed under finite changes of its elements.

The set
\mathcal{H}\,\underline{\cup}\,\mathcal{P}(k)
is clearly hereditary for every
k
. Assume it is meager for all
k
. By Theorem
1.6
, we can recursively find an increasing sequence
k(j)
and
s(j)\subseteq[k(j),k(j+1))
such that
\mathcal{H}[s(j)]
is meager for all
j
.

Thus
\bigcup_{j}\mathcal{H}[s(j)]
is a meager hereditary set, and by Theorem
1.6
there is a sequence of disjoint finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
,
t(j)
, for
j\in\mathbb{N}
, such that the set

\{a\subseteq\mathbb{N}\mid(\exists^{\infty}j)t(j)\subseteq a\}

is disjoint from
\bigcup_{j}\mathcal{H}[s(j)]
. Recursively find increasing sequences
m(j),n(j)
such that
u(j)=s(m(j))\cup t(n(j))
are pairwise disjoint.

By Theorem
1.6
there is an infinite
X\subseteq\mathbb{N}
such that
a=\bigcup_{j\in X}u(j)\in\mathcal{H}
. Fix
j\in X
. Then
a\in\mathcal{H}[s(j)]
and also
a\notin\bigcup_{i}\mathcal{H}[s(i)]
; contradiction.

For the second claim, it suffices to prove that the intersection of
\mathcal{H}
with every basic open subset of
\mathcal{P}([k,\infty))
is relatively nonmeager. Since
\mathcal{H}
is hereditary, this is equivalent to
\mathcal{H}[s]
being nonmeager for every
s\Subset[k,\infty)
.
∎

Corollary 1.8
.

If
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})

is a nonmeager hereditary subset of

and

Since

is comeager, there are

such that

are disjoint and
\mathcal{X}\supseteq\{X\mid(\exists^{\infty}n)X\cap I(n)=s(n)\}

there is an infinite

such that

. Partition

\displaystyle\textstyle A_{0}=\bigcup_{n\in d_{0}}I(n),\quad A_{1}=\mathbb{N}\setminus A_{0},\quad C_{0}=\bigcup_{n\in d_{0}}s(n),\quad C_{1}=\bigcup_{n\in d_{1}}s(n).

, hence both

Infinite sets
A
and
B

A

almost

For

For

Definition 1.9
.

A family
{\mathcal{A}}

An example of a tree-like family that is also a perfect subset of

is given as follows.

Example 1.10

(Perfect tree-like almost disjoint family)
.

Suppose that
J(s)
, for

, are pairwise disjoint nonempty finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
. For

Then

for

. Therefore the family

\mathcal{A}\{J_{s}\}=\{J(f)\mid f\in\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}\}

.

We say that

1.4.
Martin’s Axiom

We assume that the reader is familiar with Martin’s Axiom
\MA
and its weakening

[

For distinct
a

\displaystyle\Delta(a,b)

\displaystyle=\min\{n\mid a(n)\neq b(n)\},

\displaystyle a\wedge b

If

\Delta(Z)=\{a\wedge b\mid a,b\text{ are distinct elements of $Z$}\}.

Equivalently,

\Delta(Z)=\{s\in\{0,1\}^{<\mathbb{N}}\mid[s^{\frown}j]\cap Z\neq\emptyset\text{ for }j=0,1\}

The set
\Delta(Z)
is a tree with respect to

-th level is
\{s\in\Delta(Z)\mid|\{t\in\Delta(Z)\mid t\sqsubset s\}|=i+1\}\}

. Since

If
