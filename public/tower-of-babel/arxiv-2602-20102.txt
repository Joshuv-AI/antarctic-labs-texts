BarrierSteer : LLM Safety via Learning Barrier Steering
Thanh Q. Tran, Arun Verma, Kiwan Wong, Bryan Kian Hsiang Low, Daniela Rus, Wei Xiao

Abstract
Despite the state-of-the-art performance of large language models (LLMs) across diverse tasks, their susceptibility to adversarial attacks and unsafe content generation remains a major obstacle to deployment, particularly in high-stakes settings. Addressing this challenge requires safety mechanisms that are both practically effective and supported by rigorous theory. We introduce BarrierSteer , a novel framework that formalizes response safety by embedding learned non-linear safety constraints directly into the model’s latent representation space. BarrierSteer employs a steering mechanism based on Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) to efficiently detect and prevent unsafe response trajectories during inference with high precision. By enforcing multiple safety constraints through efficient constraint merging, without modifying the underlying LLM parameters, BarrierSteer preserves the model’s original capabilities and performance. We provide theoretical results establishing that applying CBFs in latent space offers a principled and computationally efficient approach to enforcing safety. Our experiments across multiple models and datasets show that BarrierSteer substantially reduces adversarial success rates, decreases unsafe generations, and outperforms existing methods.

Introduction

The rapid scaling of large language models (LLMs)
(
Anthropic 2023
;
Google 2023
;
OpenAI 2023
;
Touvron et al. 2023
)
has outpaced our ability to ensure their safety. Although these models achieve remarkable performance across a wide range of tasks, such as code generation
(
Chen et al. 2021
)
, mathematical reasoning
(
Wei et al. 2022
)
, and question answering
(
Taori et al. 2023
)
, among others
(
Zhao et al. 2023
)
, they remain vulnerable to adversarial attacks
(
Zou et al. 2023
;
Cao et al. 2024
)
and unintended generation of harmful, biased, or toxic content
(
Gehman et al. 2020
;
Chua et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025
)
.
Such failures can lead to serious real-world consequences, including the spread of misinformation and the reinforcement of systemic biases, undermining public trust and posing significant legal and ethical risks for deploying LLMs in high-stakes applications
(
Hung et al. 2023
;
Fareed et al. 2025
)
. Existing safety alignment approaches largely rely on empirical heuristics rather than rigorous verification or robustness guarantees, leaving models susceptible to unsafe behavior.
To address this gap, it is crucial to develop safety mechanisms that are not only effective in practice but also grounded in strong theoretical foundations, enabling predictable and reliable behavior in high-stakes deployment settings.

Existing safety approaches for large language models are largely heuristic, relying on alignment techniques based on preference feedback

)
that provide limited robustness and lack formal safety guarantees. In this work, we introduce

(
Ames et al. 2017
;
Glotfelter et al. 2017
;
Xiao & Belta 2021
)
that provides provable guarantees on safe behavior.
CBFs transform nonlinear safety constraints into constraints that are linear in the control decision variables, allowing efficient enforcement during inference. Unlike alignment-based methods that target specific prompts and can be circumvented through adversarial manipulation
(
Zou et al. 2023
;
Cao et al. 2024
)
,
BarrierSteer
steers latent response trajectories in real time, making it inherently more robust to adversarial inputs.

Moreover, whereas heuristic safety filters often incur a substantial trade-off between safety and utility,
BarrierSteer
adopts an inference-time steering mechanism that preserves the base model’s parameters and original capabilities. From a computational perspective,
BarrierSteer
avoids the high cost of solving nonlinear constrained optimization problems at inference time by leveraging an efficient steering strategy with constraint merging
(
Rabiee & Hoagg 2025
;
Wang et al. 2025
)
. This design enables
BarrierSteer
to enforce multiple, potentially conflicting safety constraints within a unified constrained Markov decision process (CMDP) framework
(
Altman 2021
)
. By anchoring safety directly in the latent representation space,
BarrierSteer
maintains predictable and robust behavior even under adversarial inputs or distributional shifts.

BarrierSteer
formalizes response safety by embedding explicit, nonlinear safety constraints, learned via neural networks, directly into the latent representation space of LLMs. Instead of solving expensive constrained optimization problems at inference time,
BarrierSteer
uses a principled steering mechanism grounded in CBFs, enabling high-precision detection and real-time prevention of unsafe response trajectories.
By formulating safe LLM response generation as a CMDP,
BarrierSteer
efficiently enforces multiple safety requirements through constraint merging. This approach preserves the base model’s utility while providing provable safety guarantees in latent space.
We establish theoretical guarantees and demonstrate empirically that
BarrierSteer
offers a principled and computationally efficient solution for enforcing response safety.
Extensive experiments across diverse models and datasets show that
BarrierSteer
substantially reduces adversarial attack success rates and unsafe generations, while consistently outperforming state-of-the-art baselines.

Related Work

LLM safety.

Ensuring the safety and alignment of LLMs has become a central challenge in modern AI research. Recent surveys
(
Anwar et al. 2024
;
Dalrymple et al. 2024
)
provide comprehensive overviews of the rapidly evolving landscape of LLM safety. In particular, prior work characterizes foundational challenges spanning scientific understanding, model development, and broader sociotechnical considerations
(
Anwar et al. 2024
)
. Other work advocates for principled system-level design approaches capable of delivering quantifiable safety guarantees
(
Dalrymple et al. 2024
)
. Despite these efforts, existing guidelines and high-level principles have not yet translated into universally effective, practically deployable, or enforceable mechanisms for ensuring LLM safety in real-world settings.

Existing techniques for LLM safety.

A major line of work aimed at operationalizing high-level safety principles is language model alignment. Within this paradigm, recent research has explored a range of formulations for enforcing safety, most notably through multi-objective alignment frameworks. Training-time approaches such as
Safe-RLHF

(
Dai et al. 2023
)
,
MODPO

(
Zhou et al. 2024
)
, and
Constrained DPO

(
Liu et al. 2024
)
typically incorporate safety considerations into reward modeling or preference optimization. Although effective empirically, these methods often lack explicit interpretability of the learned safety concepts and provide limited mechanisms for correcting unsafe behavior after deployment. In a complementary direction, recent work
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
proposes enforcing safety constraints directly in the representation space. This approach enables post-hoc safety control without retraining and allows for interpretable analysis of how individual polytope boundaries correspond to specific safety concepts.
In contrast, our approach offers several advantages: (i) it supports non-linear safety constraints through CBFs; (ii) it avoids inference-time constrained optimization by admitting efficient closed-form solutions; and (iii) supports modular composition of multiple safety constraints without requiring retraining or complex geometric operations.

Control barrier functions for machine learning.

In state of the art of control theory, set invariance enforced via Control Barrier Functions (CBFs)
(
Ames et al. 2017
;
Glotfelter et al. 2017
;
Xiao & Belta 2021
)
is a standard approach for ensuring the safety of nonlinear dynamical systems. CBFs transform nonlinear safety constraints into a sequence of quadratic programs that can be solved efficiently online, providing both computational efficiency and provable safety guarantees. As a result, CBFs have been widely adopted in machine learning and control systems.
Early applications of barrier functions in safe reinforcement learning (RL)
(
Achiam et al. 2017
;
Tessler et al. 2018
)
incorporated safety as soft constraints through reward shaping, which does not provide strict safety guarantees.
More recent advances in differentiable optimization
(
Amos & Kolter 2017
)
have enabled CBFs to be integrated directly into neural networks as safety-guaranteed layers
(
Pereira et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2023a
;
Xiao et al. 2023a
)
, primarily for neural network–based robotic control. CBFs have also been extended to sequential models
(
Xiao et al. 2023b
)
and diffusion generative models
(
Xiao et al. 2025
)
to enforce safety during generation.
However, existing works typically assume that safety constraints are known
a priori
, an assumption that often does not hold for complex systems such as large language models (LLMs).
While recent work has explored the use of control barrier functions (CBFs) to steer large language models
(
Miyaoka & Inoue 2025
)
, these approaches derive barrier functions from large pretrained safety classifiers and enforce them via constrained optimization, incurring substantial computational overhead that limits practical scalability.
We address this limitation by learning safety constraints directly from demonstrations and proposing an efficient inference-time mechanism to enforce these learned constraints during LLM response generation.

3
Preliminaries

This section provides background on formulating LLM safety as a constrained optimization problem. We first describe how safety constraints can be learned from data. We then introduce Control Barrier Functions, a control-theoretic tool to enforce safety guaranties in dynamical systems.

3.1
Learning Constraints for LLM

We formulate LLM safety as a sequential decision-making problem in which models must account for the long-term consequences of each token generation. Constrained Markov decision processes (CMDPs) provide a natural framework for safe LLM response generation, allowing harmful behaviors to be explicitly modeled as constraints.

Constrained Markov decision processes.

An infinite-horizon discounted constrained Markov decision process (CMDP)
(
Altman 2021
)
is defined by the tuple
(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{U},r,\{c_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K},p,\gamma,\rho)
, where
\mathcal{H}
is the state space,
\mathcal{U}
is the action space,
r:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the reward function, and
\forall k,\ c_{k}:\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{U}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are cost functions corresponding to
k
safety constraints. The transition dynamics are given by
p(\cdot\mid x,a)
,
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor, and
\rho
denotes the initial state distribution.
The goal is to find a policy
\pi
that maximizes expected reward subject to cost constraints:
\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}c_{k}(h_{t},u_{t})]\leq\xi_{k},\ \forall k
, where
\xi_{k}
specifies the allowable budget for constraint
k
.
These constraints can also be written in terms of value functions:
\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[v_{k}(h_{0})\right]\leq\xi_{k}
, where
v_{k}(h)
denotes the expected discounted cumulative cost starting from state
h
under policy
\pi
.

Autoregressive language generation as an MDP.

In autoregressive language generation, the state space
\mathcal{H}
is defined as the set of all token sequences, while the action space
\mathcal{U}
coincides with the vocabulary
\mathcal{V}
.
LLM induces a stochastic policy
\pi(u\mid h)
that specifies a distribution over next-token conditioned on the currently generated tokens. We contend that safe and ethical language generation cannot be adequately characterized by likelihood maximization alone; it requires adherence to intrinsic safety constraints (e.g., avoiding hateful or toxic content). This perspective naturally leads to a CMDP formulation, in which generation quality is optimized subject to explicit safety constraints.

Learning constraints from demonstrations.

Our main result builds on the observation that, in CMDPs, a conservative estimate of the feasible set
\mathcal{C}
can be learned solely from trajectory demonstrations. Specifically, it has been shown that given a dataset of trajectories generated by
N
safe policies
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{N}
, together with a suitable feature representation, one can construct a conservative approximation of
\mathcal{C}
as the set of all convex combinations of the corresponding feature expectations
(
Lindner et al. 2024
)
.
Motivated by this geometric characterization, recent work such as
SaP

(
Chen et al. 2025
)
proposes learning safety constraints directly as an a convex polytope (intersection of linear half-spaces) within the representation space of LLMs.

In the context of LLMs, we treat intermediate representations
h
as demonstrations to learn safety constraint boundaries. We follow the standard feature extraction pipeline commonly adopted in recent representation engineering work
(
Arditi et al. 2024
;
Chen et al. 2025
)
.
Specifically, we are given a labeled dataset
\mathcal{D}=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where each
x_{i}
is a token sequence and
y_{i}\in\{-1,+1\}
denotes its safety label, with
+1
indicating safe sequences and
-1
indicating unsafe ones.
Such labels are typically obtained from human annotations reflecting criteria such as ethical compliance, toxicity, and privacy preservation
(
Yuan et al. 2024
)
, or from the outcomes of adversarial evaluation procedures.

To extract features, we perform a forward pass of the pre-trained model on each input sequence and collect intermediate hidden representations.
Let
\bar{\pi}^{l}(x)
denote the hidden state corresponding to input
x
at layer
l
.
Each data point
x_{i}
is associated with a feature vector
h_{i}=\bar{\pi}^{l}(x_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}
, yielding a dataset of feature–label pairs
\{(h_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
In practice, sentence-level safety is determined using the representation corresponding to the last token of the sequence.

Limitations of geometric constraints.

While
SaP
provides a learnable framework for modeling safety constraints, it relies on projecting the latent representation onto a learned polytope by solving a constrained optimization problem at inference time. Concretely, given a latent state
h\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{h}}
, the method computes a projected safe representation by solving the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\min_{h}\|\bar{\pi}^{l}(x)-h\|_{1}

s.t.
h
satisfies the learned safety constraints.

This approach has following limitations:

•

Linearity:

SaP
impose linear decision boundaries, which limits their capacity to represent complex, non-linear safety manifolds.

•

Inference-time optimization:
Enforcing safety requires solving constrained optimization problems at inference time, which typically lack closed-form solutions and introduce additional computational overhead and latency.

•

Limited modularity:
Composing or extending safety constraints is non-trivial, often necessitating retraining.

These limitations motivate the use of CBFs, which provide a more flexible and computationally efficient alternative.

There is a three-stage pipeline of

: (i) extracting intermediate latent representations from a pre-trained LLM and constructing an LLM-specific safety dataset with binary safety labels; (ii) learning expressive, non-linear safety constraints in the latent space; and (iii) enforcing these constraints at inference time via CBF-based steering to prevent unsafe generation trajectories, without modifying the underlying model parameters.

3.2
Control Barrier Functions

Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) provide a rigorous and principled framework for enforcing safety in dynamical systems.
We consider affine control systems with state
h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, control input
u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and dynamics of the form:

\dot{h}=f(h)+g(h)u,

(1)

where
f
and
g
are assumed to be locally Lipschitz.
We aim is to ensure the system state
h(t)
remains within a prescribed safe set
\mathcal{C}
, defined as the
0
-superlevel set of a continuously differentiable function
b:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, as follows:

\mathcal{C}=\{h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:b(h)\geq 0\}.

(2)

Definition 1 (Control Barrier Function).

A function
b
is a control barrier function (CBF) for the system on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
if there exists an extended class
\mathcal{K}
function
\beta
(a strictly increasing function that passes through the origin) such that

\forall h\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\!\sup\limits_{u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\dot{b}(h,u)\geq-\beta(b(h)).

This condition
\sup_{u}[L_{f}b(h)+L_{g}b(h)u+\beta(b(h))]\geq 0
guarantees the existence of a control input that can maintain system safety.
Given a control barrier function
b
, any control input
u
satisfying
\dot{b}(h,u)\geq-\beta(b(h))
for all
h\in\mathcal{C}
renders the system safe
(
Ames et al. 2017
;
Xiao & Belta 2021
)
. For simplicity, we consider linear class
\mathcal{K}
functions in this paper, i.e.,
\beta(b(h))=\alpha b(h),\alpha>0
.

Crucially, CBFs are composable, admit efficient closed-form solutions in certain scenarios, and are capable of enforcing highly complex non-linear safety constraints.

4
LLM Safety via Steering with Learned Safety Constraints

In this section, we present our approach for learning safety constraints from demonstration data and demonstrate the utility of our approach for steering unsafe output generation.

As illustrated in
Fig.

2
, our approach consists of three high-level stages: (i) learning control barrier functions that characterize safe regions of the latent state space; (ii) extracting latent representations and dynamics from a pre-trained LLM using labeled data; and (iii) applying the learned control barrier functions at inference time to steer the model’s generation toward the safe region.

4.1
Learning Control Barrier Functions

A key challenge in applying control barrier functions to LLMs is that safety constraints are not known
a priori
and must instead be inferred from data. In contrast to prior approaches that rely on linear approximations or concept encoders
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
, we learn highly non-linear safety constraints directly in the latent representation space and subsequently enforce these constraints via control barrier functions during LLM inference.

We formulate safety boundary estimation as a classification problem and learn a collection of barrier functions
\{b_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
, parameterized by neural network weights
\theta=\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{K}\}
. Given a dataset of safe samples
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{safe}}
and unsafe samples
\mathcal{X}_{\mathrm{unsafe}}
, we jointly train these functions by minimizing a loss function composed of two terms:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{\text{safe}}}(\theta)+\lambda_{\textnormal{\text{unsafe}}}\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{\text{unsafe}}}(\theta).

(3)

where the parameter
\lambda_{\text{unsafe}}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
is a hyperparameter that controls the penalty associated with safety violations.
The
safe set loss
ensures that safe states satisfy
all
constraints (
b_{k}(h)\geq 0,\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
):

\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{\text{safe}}}(\theta)=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{X}_{\textnormal{\text{safe}}}}\sum_{k=1}^{K}[-b_{k}(h)]_{+}.

(4)

Conversely, the
unsafe set loss
ensures that unsafe states violate
at least one
constraint (
\min_{k}b_{k}(h)\leq-\varepsilon
):

\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{\text{unsafe}}}(\theta)=\sum_{h\in\mathcal{X}_{\textnormal{\text{unsafe}}}}[\min_{k}b_{k}(h)+\varepsilon]_{+}.

(5)

In these expressions, the operator
[\cdot]_{+}=\max(0,\cdot)
denotes the standard hinge loss, and
\varepsilon
defines the safety margin.
Minimizing
\mathcal{L}(\theta)
encourages the intersection of the learned safety constraints to correctly classify safe and unsafe states. Moreover, distinct control barrier functions can be learned from separate datasets or for different safety categories, as discussed in Section
4.3
.

4.2
Representation Steering with Latent Dynamics

Once the safety constraints are learned, we can leverage them to verify and actively steer the model’s generation.
Obtaining
b_{k}
allows us to directly check if a proposed next-token state lies within the safe set
\mathcal{C}=\{h\mid b_{k}(h)\geq\delta,k\in\{1,\dots,K\}\}
, where
\delta
denotes the safety threshold. While any
\delta\geq 0
strictly enforces the safety of the LLM, in practice, we set
\delta=0
for simplicity.
If the model’s original trajectory violates the safety margin (i.e.,
\exists k,b_{k}(h)<\delta
),
we dynamically adjust the latent state
h
within the generation loop to enforce safety.

In order to enforce the learned CBFs
\{b_{k}(h)\geq\delta\}_{k=1}^{K}
, we have to know the dynamics of the latent state
h
. We approximate the local evolution of the hidden state as
\dot{h}=(h_{t}-h_{t-1})/\Delta t
, where
\Delta t
is the time step (default
\Delta t=1
). This empirical approximation has been successfully leveraged to enforce CBFs in continuous generative processes, such as diffusion models
(
Xiao et al. 2025
)
.
By modeling the latent-state evolution as a controllable dynamical system
\dot{h}=u
, we seek a control input
u
that maintains safety while minimizing deviation from the original trajectory. Specifically, we compute the optimal
BarrierSteer
control
u
by solve the following Quadratic Program (QP):

\displaystyle u^{*}

\displaystyle=\underset{u}{\text{argmin}}\ \|u-\frac{h_{t}-h_{t-1}}{\Delta t}\|^{2}

(6)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\nabla b_{k}(h_{t-1})^{\top}u\!+\!\alpha(b_{k}(h_{t-1})-\delta)\geq 0,

\displaystyle k\in\{1,\dots,K\}.

The state is then updated as
h^{\prime}_{t}=h_{t-1}+u^{*}\Delta t
, and the state after steering becomes
h_{t}\leftarrow h^{\prime}_{t}
. This avoids the need for computationally expensive iterative solvers like projected gradient descent during inference.
Furthermore, the parameter
\alpha
in
Eq.

6
controls the steering strength. A larger value of
\alpha
enforces a more aggressive correction toward the safe set
\mathcal{C}
. While this enhances formal safety guarantees, it may induce a significant deviation from the nominal control input. This fundamental trade-off between safety and task performance is empirically validated across various benchmarks, as reported in Table
2
.

4.3
Closed-form Steering via Composing CBFs

Real-world safety specifications often involve multiple, possibly conflicting, constraints (e.g.,“be helpful” AND “be harmless”).
We can formalize these requirements as satisfying
all
constraints simultaneously.
However, directly solving the quadratic program in
Eq.

6
is still inefficient for LLMs as the dimension of
u
(the same as the one of
h
) is usually very large in the latent space. In order to address this, we propose a closed-form
BarrierSteer
as follows.

Recall that
\{b_{k}(h)\}_{k=1}^{K}
is a set of learned CBF boundaries corresponding to safe sets
\mathcal{C}_{k}
.
A system satisfying all constraints must remain in the intersection
\bigcap_{k=1}^{K}\mathcal{C}_{k}
.
We propose to merge these constraints into a single, continuously differentiable CBF.
The intersection of safe sets can be captured by a smooth under-approximation of the minimum function using the Log-Sum-Exp (LSE) formula:

B(h)=-\frac{1}{\kappa}\ln\left(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}}e^{-\kappa(b_{k}(h)-\delta)}\right),

(7)

where
\kappa>0
is a smoothing parameter.
Crucially, this composed barrier
B(h)
remains continuously differentiable, and the satisfaction of
B(h)\geq 0
implies the satisfaction of
b_{k}(h)-\delta\geq 0,\forall k\in\{1,\dots,K\}

(
Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004
)
.
Under appropriate assumptions on the latent space, we have the following theorem establishing the safety guarantee for the proposed
BarrierSteer
.

Theorem 1
.

Given a set of learned safety constraints
\{b_{k}(h)\geq\delta\}_{k=1}^{K}
, if the initial hidden state
h_{0}
is in the safe set
\mathcal{C}=\{h\mid b_{k}(h)\geq\delta,k\in\{1,\dots,K\}\}
, then the
BarrierSteer
control obtained via the composing method
Eq.

7
ensures the state
h
stays in the safe set
\mathcal{C}
for all
t\geq 0
(i.e., safety guarantees for LLM); otherwise, the hidden state
h
will be exponentially stabilized towards
\mathcal{C}
.

The proof for Theorem
1
is given in Appendix Sec.
B
.

Alternatively, to further reduce computational cost while maintaining safety effectiveness, one can employ a greedy heuristic: identify the Top-
k
(e.g.,
k=2
) most violated constraints at the current step and solve the safety filter considering only these constraints.
In practice, addressing the most critical violations often implicitly satisfies the remaining constraints or sufficiently mitigates immediate safety risks.
Under this restriction, when the number of active safety constraints is no greater than two, the quadratic program in
Eq.

6
admits a closed-form
BarrierSteer
solution for the control input
u
, given by:

\displaystyle u^{*}=\frac{h_{t}-h_{t-1}}{\Delta t}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{1}(h_{t-1})\hat{g}_{1}(h_{t-1})

(8)

\displaystyle+\lambda_{2}(h_{t-1})\hat{g}_{2}(h_{t-1}),

where
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\hat{g}_{1},\hat{g}_{2}
are given in the Appendix Sec.
A
, in which the derivation process of the closed-form solution is also given. These three methods of composing CBFs lead to three versions of
BarrierSteer
:

•

BarrierSteer
(QP): Directly solves the QP in
Eq.

6
.

•

BarrierSteer
(Top-2): Selects the two most violated constraints to derive a fast, closed-form steering solution.

•

BarrierSteer
(LSE): Compose all constraints into a single CBF via the LSE
Eq.

7
formula to derive a closed-form steering solution via Equation
Eq.

8
.

Datasets and evaluation setup.

We evaluate our approach using two primary safety benchmarks. First, we utilize
HarmBench

(
Mazeika et al. 2024a
)
as a standardized framework to generate automated adversarial prompts. We select 320 distinct harmful behaviors to generate test cases for training the safety barriers, while reserving a test set of 80 harmful behaviors to evaluate. This behavior-level split ensures that the evaluated method must generalize to defend against the underlying adversarial attack mechanism rather than memorizing certain harmful prompts. Second, we use
WildGuardMix

(
Han et al. 2024
)
, a large-scale dataset comprising harmful prompts across 14 risk categories. For each category, we sample 400 samples, splitting them into 320 for training and 80 for evaluation. We use these prompts to collect datasets of safe and unsafe activations, denoted as
\mathcal{X}_{\text{safe}}
and
\mathcal{X}_{\text{unsafe}}
respectively. For all experiments, we quantify safety via the HarmBench classification model, which evaluates the semantic safety of the generated responses.

Baselines.
We compare
BarrierSteer
against popular representation steering methods.
Activation Addition

(
Turner et al. 2023
)
modifies the hidden state
h
by adding a steering vector
r
derived from the difference-in-means
(
Rimsky et al. 2024
)
of safe and unsafe activations:

r=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}_{\textnormal{\text{safe}}}|}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{X}_{\textnormal{\text{safe}}}}h-\frac{1}{|\mathcal{X}_{\textnormal{\text{unsafe}}}|}\sum_{h\in\mathcal{D}_{\textnormal{\text{unsafe}}}}h

The steering is defined as
h^{\prime}=h+\alpha r
, where
\alpha
represents the steering coefficient. Meanwhile,
Directional Ablation

(
Arditi et al. 2024
)
removes the harmful feature by projecting
h
onto the orthogonal complement of
r
:
h^{\prime}=h-r(r^{\top}r)^{-1}r^{\top}h
.

Finally, we evaluate our approach against
SaP

(
Chen et al. 2025
)
, the most relevant baseline as it also constructs safety boundaries within the activation space. A fundamental distinction lies in the geometric formulation:
SaP
is restricted to a convex polytope defined by the intersection of linear half-spaces. In contrast,
BarrierSteer
benefits from the expressive capacity of non-linear neural networks to represent highly complex constraints. This gap in representation capacity directly impacts the ability to filter unsafe responses while preserving the safe ones, as shown in our subsequent experiments.

6
Conclusion

In this work, we presented
BarrierSteer
, a principled framework for improving the safety of large language models by enforcing explicit non-linear safety constraints directly in latent representation space. By using Control Barrier Functions as a steering mechanism during inference,
BarrierSteer
steers unsafe response trajectories towards safe responses while preserving the original model parameters and capabilities. The latent-space CBFs offer an efficient method for safety enforcement, even under multiple simultaneous constraints via constraint merging. Empirically,
BarrierSteer
consistently reduces adversarial success rates and unsafe generations across models and datasets, outperforming existing safety baselines. These results suggest that CBF-based methods offer a scalable way for building LLMs with provable safety guarantees.

Limitations

Dependence on supervised safety labels.

BarrierSteer
depends on supervised learning from labeled safe and unsafe latent representations, making its effectiveness depend on high-quality, comprehensive annotations. Any gaps or biases in the training data lead to missing or skewed safety barriers, limiting generalization to unseen behaviors. Consequently, out-of-distribution harmful behaviors may bypass the learned CBFs and remain undetected.

Latent-space vs. semantic safety.

BarrierSteer
enforces safety only in latent representation space, creating a gap between latent safety and human-interpretable semantic safety. A latent state deemed safe can still produce unsafe or misaligned outputs. As a result, the method’s “provable safety” guarantees apply to hidden representations, not to human-aligned safety of the generated text.

Approximate latent dynamics assumption.

BarrierSteer
approximates hidden-state evolution as a simplified continuous dynamical system, even though LLMs are not true dynamical systems. This ignores key transformer properties such as attention, inter-layer interactions, and token-dependent nonlinearities. Consequently, the theoretical safety guarantees rely on assumptions that may not fully hold in practice, limiting their real-world validity.

Unavoidable safety–utility trade-off.

While
BarrierSteer
manages the safety–utility trade-off better than baseline methods, it does not remove it. Increasing the steering strength
\alpha
improves safety but can cause performance degradation. This demonstrates that stronger safety constraints inherently limit model capability, reaffirming the fundamental tension between safety and utility.

Dataset Collection and Feature Extraction.
Following the HarmBench evaluation protocol, we utilize a corpus of 400 distinct behaviors. For the training and evaluation of safety constraints, we partition this into 320 behaviors for training and 80 behaviors for the final safety evaluation. For each attack category, we generate a single test case per behavior.

To construct the training dataset, we perform a forward pass on the 320 training behaviors and extract the per-token hidden state representations at layer
L=20
. The dimensionality of the training set is thus
N_{\text{sentences}}\times N_{\text{tokens}}
. Ground truth labels are assigned at the sentence level: if a model’s output for a given behavior is flagged as unsafe, all token representations in the sentence are labeled as unsafe, and conversely for safe output. Safety evaluation of generated responses is performed using the
HarmBench-Llama-2-13b-cls
validation model.

Baselines Settings.

To ensure a reproducible environment, all inference and safety evaluations are conducted using greedy decoding with a temperature setting of
T=0
. For the
Activation Steering
baseline, we employ a steering strength of
\alpha=1
. Both the proposed method and the SaP baseline are trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate
\eta=10^{-2}
and a batch size of 512.

For the
Safety Polytope (SaP)
baseline, we implement the framework as specified by the authors, utilizing a Concept Encoder that projects hidden states into a
16,384
-dimensional sparse representation space. We fix the number of facets to
K=30
, as empirical results suggest diminishing returns beyond this count
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
. Model-specific hyperparameters (e.g., margin parameters and regularization weights) for Llama2-7B, Ministral-8B, and Qwen2-1.5B are adopted directly from the original work.

Neural CBF Architecture.

In our proposed framework, we utilize a multi-head architecture consisting of
K=4
Control Barrier Functions (CBFs) to maintain a parameter count approximately equivalent to the SaP baseline. The architecture for each constraint is defined as follows:

•

Input Projection Layer
: A linear transformation mapping the hidden states
d_{\varphi}
to a
2048
-dimensional space, followed by Layer Normalization and a GELU activation function.

•

Hidden Layers
: A sequence of three dense blocks with output dimensions of
1024
,
512
, and
256
respectively. Each block consists of a
nn.Linear
layer,
nn.LayerNorm
, and a
GELU
non-linearity.

•

Output Head
: A final
nn.Linear
layer that collapses the
256
-dimensional representation into a single scalar value representing the constraint satisfaction level
h(x)

C.2
Computational Benchmarking.

To assess the inference latency and efficiency of the proposed framework, we perform a benchmarking study using
100,000
hidden state activations extracted from the adversarial unsafe dataset. The experiment is conducted on an NVIDIA H200 GPU with a batch size of 1 to simulate real-time token generation constraints. We maintain consistent model architectures as described in Section
C.1
, comparing SaP with
K=30
facets against our
BarrierSteer
with
4
Neural CBF heads. Crucially, the architectures are designed such that the total parameter count remains equivalent across both methods. Following the implementation details in
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
, the SaP baseline employs
100
gradient-based optimization iterations during the steering phase to ensure activation convergence.

C.3
Modular Composition of Safety Barriers

Consistent with the adversarial attack protocol, we sample 400 behaviors for each of the 14 safety categories. This corpus is partitioned into 320 behaviors for training the individual safety constraints and 80 behaviors for the final evaluation of safety. A single test case is generated per behavior for each attack category. During inference, we evaluate performance across a total of 1,120 samples (
80\times 14
) and report the harmfulness rate as determined by the
HarmBench-Llama-2-13b-cls
validation model.
In this experimental setup, each category-specific safety constraint is parameterized by the neural CBF architecture described in Section
C.1
. To isolate the effects of composition, we utilize exactly one CBF (
K=1
) per semantic category. For a fair comparison across all methodologies, we maintain a constant steering strength
\alpha=1
during inference. All reported results are averaged over 5 independent runs.
