Protocol for integrative transcriptomic/metabolomic analysis: A case study on factors influencing the oyster-POMS disease
Authors: Leo Duperret, Alejandro Valdivieso, Bruno Petton, Benjamin Morga, Nicole Faury, Julien de Lorgeril, Charlotte Corporeau, Lionel Degremont, Sylvain Henry, Andrei Turtoi, Juliette Pouzadoux, Océane Romatif, Gaelle Courtay, Cristián J Monaco, Eve Toulza, Arnaud Lagorce, Jeremie Vidal-Dupiol, Guillaume Mitta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDeciphering the molecular mechanisms underlying complex biological interactions requires comprehensive approaches that capture these interactions across multiple regulatory and functional levels. Here, we present a protocol applied to the host-pathogen-environment interactions underlying Pacific oyster mortality syndrome (POMS) in Magallana gigas. We describe steps from individual transcriptomic and metabolomic workflows using WGCNA and FELLA packages, to their integration in a biologically meaningful framework. This protocol aims to move omics integration beyond statistical correlations toward functional enrichment with cross-validation at the system level.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Duperret et al.1Subject areas: Bioinformatics, RNAseq, Metabolomics

Before you begin

Pacific Oyster Mortality Syndrome (POMS) is a polymicrobial and multifactorial disease affecting the oyster Magallana gigas, a marine species of significant economic interest. While the molecular mechanisms underlying the polymicrobial manifestation of this disease has already been deciphered,2 the effect of food availability on POMS was recently elucidated. For this, we used two full-sib oyster families with distinct genetic backgrounds selected on their high susceptibility to POMS. At 3 months-old, “recipient” oysters were divided into two batches and followed a strict diet regime: fed ad libitum (permissive condition) or starved (non-permissive condition) for 16 days prior the infection. Infection was done in mesocosm, to mimic natural disease transmission using infected oysters (“donors”) injected with OsHV-1 μVar virus placed in the same environment as naïve recipient oysters. At different hours post-cohabitation (0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48 and 72 hpc) a pool of ten oysters per family was sampled for RNA and metabolites extraction. RNA-sequencing was performed to analyze differentially expressed genes, and targeted metabolomics (LC/MS and GC/MS) was conducted to quantify relative metabolite abundance. Targetted metabolomic analysis quantified 346 metabolites across four metabolite classes: General Metabolites (GM, n = 107 covering amino acid metabolism, urea cycle, neurotransmitter metabolism, redox/cofactor metabolism); Nucleotides (n = 20); Poly-Unsaturated Fatty Acids (PUFAs, n = 151 including prostaglandins, leukotrienes, and oxylipins); and the Tricarboxylic Acid cycle (TCA) intermediates (n = 13). The list of targeted metabolites can be retrieved in Table S1: List of metabolites identified for the targeted metabolomics analysis.

The protocol described here was focused on the combined analysis of RNA-seq and metabolomics data obtained in naïve oysters, after 16 days of acclimation just before POMS infection. The effect of food availability on the infection kineticis published in the original research paper.1

This protocol provides a comprehensive, end-to-end workflow, spanning from (i) single omics data analyses to (ii) the integration of both. Rather than relying solely on the mathematical correlations between omics, the pipeline combines functional enrichment with cross-validation to produce biologically meaningful, robust and reproducible results. This approach yields a systemic understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms and biological interactions underlying complex biological processes.

Innovation

Previous studies have integrated multi-omics data by identifying statistical associations between large omics datasets (e.g., RNA-seq, metabolomics, proteomics), relying primarily on correlation-based approaches such as Pearson or Spearman coefficients. We propose here a strategy aimed at cross-validating outcomes from two independent omics analyses (RNA-seq and metabolomics), thereby integrating datasets with greater biological insight. This approach is based on (i) the identification of co-expressed genes and metabolites significantly associated to the phenotype of interest using the WGCNA pipeline; (ii) functional enrichment analyses of the identified features from each -omics, (iii) identification of the genes involved in the identified metabolic pathways, and (iv) the analysis of their respective biological role within these pathways associate with the phenotype. This rational enable to integrate cross-validated results from two omics layers.

Institutional permissions

Ethical approval was not required for this study in accordance with local legislation and institutional requirements, as there is no regulatory framework in Europe governing the use of bivalve molluscs in scientific research (cf. European Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for scientific purposes). Nevertheless, the 3R principle (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) was applied throughout the study,1 notably by minimizing the number of animals used, and were anesthetized prior to manipulation in order to minimize potential stress.

Selection of the soft power threshold to achieve a scale-free topology for WGCNA analyses

A scale-free topology is characterized by a network structure in which most features (genes/metabolites) have few connections, whereas a small proportion of highly connected features act as hubs. Here, when applied to the transcriptomics data of,1 the red horizontal line indicates the threshold of 0.8 for the signed scale-free topology fit index, and a soft-thresholding power of 27 was selected for subsequent network construction.

Expected outcomes

The integration of both transcriptomics and metabolomics data generated a more comprehensive understanding of the studied phenomenon. Instead of relying on a single biological layer, this approach provides a deeper insight into the biological system while cross-validating results obtained at the transcriptome and metabolome level. This integrative strategy is expected to identify genes involved in metabolite-associated pathways, thereby highlighting coordinated molecular responses consistent with the observed metabolic changes and physiological state of the animal.

Limitations

This multi-omics integration can be applied on every organism at the condition that their metabolic map have been characterized and made publicly available on the KEGG database. Since the datasets used include metabolomic data, this protocol can be useful for integration with other omics dataset (epigenetic, genetic, proteomic), enabling the identification of epigenetics/genes/proteins involved in the studied phenomenon. Any omics dataset could be integrated, as long as a biologically meaningful link can be found. In the case of this protocol (RNA-seq and metabolomics), the so-called link was the identification of metabolic pathways where gene of interests were involved in.

Note: One way to integrate epigenomic data with transcriptomics and metabolomics would be to follow an approach similar to.16 Differentially Methylated Cytosines (DMCs) located within gene body regions can be used to identify a specific list of genes impacted by the trait of interest, identified by WGCNA. Because these genes retain the same KEGG gene identifiers as those used for transcriptomic data, this DMC-derived gene list can be mapped onto KEGG pathways and linked to compound IDs using the exact same integration strategy described in this protocol for the transcriptomics-metabolomics workflow.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Poor RNA-seq data quality due to low sequencing depth, RNA degradation, or contamination may reduce the reliability of gene expression estimates.

Potential solution

Perform strict quality control (e.g., read trimming, removal of low-quality reads) and verify mapping rates. Samples with poor sequencing metrics, qualified as outliers, should be excluded before downstream analyses.

Problem 2

When computing a scale-free topology in an unsupervised signed network using WGCNA, it may occur that no soft-thresholding power yields a scale-free topology fit index (R2) above 0.8 (see Step 5). Although achieving a scale-free topology is recommended for network construction, this criterion is not always strictly met depending on the dataset.

Potential solution

If this occurs, it is advisable to check both the filtering of the raw data and the normalization step (Step 4). Excessive noise in the dataset, such as lowly expressed or highly variable genes, can reduce gene–gene correlations and prevent the network from approximating a scale-free topology.

Problem 3

In WGCNA analysis (related to both individual omics analyses) the grey module should not be considered in downstream analyses. As discussed in Step 7, the grey module contains all genes that were not assigned to any co-expression module (e.g., genes showing low correlation with other genes in the dataset or belonging to clusters that are too small).

Potential solution

If more than 40–50% of genes are in the grey module, one should try to modify network characteristics (e.g., filter dataset, soft power threshold, minimal size of the modules).

Problem 4

The WGCNA analysis (related to both individual omics analyses) may output no significantly correlated modules with the trait of interest (even when an appropriate soft-thresholding power has been selected).

Potential solution

Before modifying network parameters, verify that the dataset contains a sufficient number of samples and that data preprocessing steps (e.g., normalization and filtering) were appropriately performed. Increasing module sensitivity by adjusting parameters such as the minModuleSize or mergeCutHeight may help identify relevant modules. However, these adjustments should be applied cautiously, as forcing the detection of very small modules relative to the sample size may lead to results that are not biologically representative.

Problem 5

Suspected batch effects, especially in the metabolomic analysis (related to Individual omics analysis: computational analysis of the metabolomics data). This can be due to samples being processed on different time points. This problem can be a major source of inconsistency during the analysis.

Potential solution

In MetaboAnalyst, you can fix this problem with.

>mSet <- InitDataObjects(“conc”, “stat”, FALSE)

>mSet <- Read.BatchDataTB(mSet, “#csv file containing the raw data”, “row”, “lods”)

>mSet <- PerformBatchCorrection(mSet)

After, you may want to confirm that samples cluster primarily according to biological conditions rather than technical factors.
