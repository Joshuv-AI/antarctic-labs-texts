Magnitude, pattern, birth outcome, and determinants of congenital anomalies among newborns in Ethiopia
Authors: Tihitena Negussie Mammo, Samuel Negash, Tigist Chalachew, Ayalew Tizazu, Amezene Robele, Belachew Dejene, Miliard Derbew, Yimer Seid
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCongenital anomalies, encompassing structural or functional defects present at birth, pose a significant global health challenge. Addressing congenital anomalies can contribute significantly to further reducing neonatal mortality and advancing overall child health in Ethiopia.ObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the magnitude, pattern, birth outcome, and determinants of congenital anomalies among newborns in Ethiopia.MethodsEmploying a facility-based cross-sectional design over one month, the study enrolled 813 deliveries attending two public and private maternal and child health centers. We used consecutive sampling of all eligible deliveries who fulfils the inclusion criteria during the study period and meticulously screened for congenital anomalies using a well-designed pictorialized data collection tool by training pediatricians and midwives. To determine the strength of the association and identify independent predictors of birth defects, the adjusted odds ratio (AOR) and 95% confidence interval were computed using binary logistic regression analysis.ResultsForty-six neonates (5.66%, 95% CI: 4.17%–7.47%) had one or more congenital anomalies detected on physical examination. Of these, 36 had a single anomaly, while 10 had multiple malformations. There were a combined 60 anomalies among the 46 newborn babies. The majority of the patients had central nervous system anomalies (31.7%), followed by genitourinary system (25.4%) and musculoskeletal system (22.2%) anomalies. The odds of birth defects increased by 3.73 for women attending a private hospital compared to those attending a public hospital (AOR 3.73, 95% CI 1.67, 8.32). The odds of birth defects were 1.97 times greater for males than for females (AOR 1.97, 95% CI 0.99, 3.91; p = 0.051), which is clinically or practically significant even if the p value is slightly above the cutoff point, 0.05. The odds of birth defects were found to be 9.3 times greater among neonates with very low birth weight and low birth weight than among neonates with appropriate birth weight (AOR 9.29, 95% CI 3.41, 25.31). Primipara women had 3.64 times greater odds of delivering babies with birth defects than did multipara women (AOR 3.64, 95% CI 1.45, 9.13). In this study, perinatal mortality was markedly higher among newborns with congenital anomalies (37%) compared to those without anomalies (1%), and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.001).ConclusionIn conclusion, this study provides important insights into the prevalence, patterns, birth outcomes, and predictors of congenital anomalies. The high anomaly rates highlight the need for comprehensive prenatal care, including early diagnosis, risk factor monitoring, and targeted interventions for private MCH clinics, male neonates, low birth weight infants, and primipara women. Further longitudinal research and collaboration with the Ministry of Health to establish a congenital anomaly surveillance system can improve the understanding and management of these conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06403-z.Keywords: Birth defect, Congenital anomaly, Birth outcome, Newborns in ethiopia

Introduction

Congenital anomalies, encompassing structural or functional defects present at birth, pose a significant global health challenge. The incidence of this disease ranges from 3 to 7% of live births worldwide, with a notable prevalence of severe cases in low- and middle-income countries, where 94% of such anomalies occur [1–4]. The contributing factors include higher birth rates, limited prenatal care, nutritional deficiencies, and infections [5]. This high incidence of anomalies results in an alarming 240,000 new-born deaths annually, positioning congenital anomalies as the fourth leading cause of perinatal mortality [1]. The majority of these deaths occur in developing countries, where challenges such as home birth, lack of awareness about potential interventions, exposure to hypothermia during travel to healthcare facilities, and delays in diagnosing nonvisible anomalies contribute to elevated mortality rates [1, 5].

Despite progress in meeting certain sustainable development goals, the absence of focused attention on congenital anomalies in Ethiopia requires urgent action [6, 7]. There is no congenital anomaly surveillance system in Ethiopia and that most available data are from retrospective, hospital-based pediatric admissions, which underestimate the true prevalence. Establishing a birth cohort is pivotal not only for understanding the magnitude and patterns of congenital anomalies but also for identifying determinants that may inform preventive strategies. Our study addresses this gap by conducting prospective screening at birth in both public and private facilities with high volume of delivery, capturing anomalies that may be missed in admission-based studies that provides insight into the nuanced landscape of congenital anomalies among neonates in Addis Ababa and contributes essential data for public health planning and intervention development. Ultimately, addressing congenital anomalies can contribute significantly to further reducing neonatal mortality and advancing overall child health in Ethiopia.

Methods

Study setting

This prospective observational study, conducted at Gandhi Memorial General (public) Hospital and Grace (private) Maternal and Child Health Center in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, represents a comprehensive investigation of congenital anomalies among neonates. The two institutions were purposively selected to represent contrasting settings, one large public referral hospital (Gandhi Memorial) and one private maternal and child health center (Grace), to explore possible differences in prevalence and risk factors between public and private care.

Study design and sampling method

Employing a facility-based cross-sectional design over one month, the study enrolled 813 deliveries attending the two centers and meticulously screened for congenital anomalies using a well-designed pictorialized data collection tool and trained pediatricians and midwives. Cases are included based on the selection criteria mentioned below.

Inclusion Criteria: All live births and stillbirths of ≥ 24 weeks gestation, with fetal viability with full organ formation, during the study period whose mothers gave written informed consent. Late term abortion is included since structural deformity could be the main reason for late fetal demise.

The sampling procedure is consecutive sampling of all eligible births who fulfills the inclusion criteria during the one-month study period in both facilities.

However, during the one-month study period, all 813 births that met the inclusion criteria in both facilities were enrolled in the study.

Data collection process

Data were collected by trained pediatricians and midwives. Before starting, they received a half day training on the study objectives and how to properly use a structured data collection form.

During the training, participants received a pictorial checklist, which was developed by the team from pictures accessed freely online, with hands-on demonstration to facilitate understanding of congenital anomalies during the physical examination (See the guideline on the supplementary document). The congenital anomalies and other clinical characteristics were assessed during pregnancy and immediately after birth through a physical examination. All findings from these examinations were carefully recorded in the structured form. Additional information, such as biomedical test results and radiologic measurements, was obtained from medical records. The newborns’ health outcomes was ascertained by follow-up phone calls when the infants reached 28 days of age. These calls were used to capture important information, including whether the newborn had died or required early surgery. Consistency of anomaly detection was assessed through daily supervision by the principal investigator and cross-checking of selected cases to ensure uniform application of definitions and recording standards.

Variables and measurements

The primary outcome focused on the occurrence and types of birth defects, while the secondary outcome encompassed perinatal mortality. The study adopted a clear operational definition of congenital anomalies, considering any gross developmental defect apparent upon inspection or through physical examination at birth or within the first few hours of life Both major and minor anomalies were included. If more than one system was affected by a congenital anomaly, a composite diagnosis was made where possible; if not, the infant was classified as having multiple congenital abnormalities (MCAs). Thus, each affected infant was recorded only once but for specific anomalies each tallied separately.

Data quality assurance

To make sure the data collected was accurate and reliable, several steps were taken. First, the data collection tool was tested among 10 participants in a similar health facility (Black Lion hospital) before the actual study to check if the questions were clear and worked as intended. Next, all data collectors were given proper training so they could collect the information consistently and correctly. During the data collection period, the principal investigator closely supervised the process and reviewed the work every day to identify and correct any problems quickly. In addition, special care was taken during the stages of coding, data entry, and analysis to reduce errors and ensure the information was handled properly.

Data analysis

The Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 26 was used to analyze the data. A descriptive analysis was performed to evaluate the clinical characteristics, treatment patterns, and outcomes. Continuous variables are expressed as the mean (SD) when normally distributed or as the median (IQR) when not normally distributed. Categorical variables are presented as the frequency and percentage. Bivariate and multivariable binary logistic regression models were fitted to identify the contributing factors to birth defects. To determine the strength of the association between predictors and birth defects, the crude odds ratio (COR) and adjusted odds ratio (AOR), together with the 95% confidence interval (CI), were computed. Clinically relevant variables and predictors with a P value < 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were subsequently considered in the multivariable model. A P value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant. In multicollinearity testing, all the independent variables had tolerance statistics above 0.3 and a VIF below 2.86, indicating the absence of multicollinearity. The fitness of the multivariable model was shown to be satisfactory according to the Hosmer and Lemeshow tests, for which the P value was 0.939.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Institutional Review Board of the College of Health Sciences, Addis Ababa University (Protocol Number: 075/18/SURG). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The objectives of the study were explained to mothers or caregivers, and participation was entirely voluntary. Written informed consent was obtained from all mothers or caregivers prior to enrollment.

The study involved all 813 births that met the inclusion during the one-month study period in both facilities. The majority (648; 79.7%) were from a public health facility, and the remaining 165 (20.3%) were from a private hospital. The sex distribution was balanced, with 52% females and 48% males and no cases of sexual ambiguity. The average gestational age was 39 weeks and 2 days, with a deviation of ± 2 weeks and 6 days. Maternal and paternal sociodemographic and anthropometric data

The mean maternal age was 28 ± 5 years, and 97% of the mothers were married. Predominantly, mothers were between 25 and 34 years (61.6%) and 24 years and younger (27.2%), while 11.2% were 35 years and older. A significant proportion (53.9%) of the participants were working with an average household income of 8006 euros, and the rest were housewives. The average pregnancy weight gain was 12.6 kg (± 4.25 kg), ranging from − 4 kg to 31 kg. The average number of births given by a woman was two (± 1), ranging from one to six births.

Magnitude and pattern of congenital anomalies

One or more congenital anomalies were detected in forty-six neonates (5.66%, 95% CI: 4.17%–7.47%) via physical examination. Of these, 36 had a single anomaly, while 10 had multiple malformations. There were a combined 60 anomalies among the 46 newborn babies. The majority of the patients had central nervous system anomalies (31.7%), followed by genitourinary system (25.4%) and musculoskeletal system (22.2%) anomalies (Table 2).

Birth outcomes

A total of 813 babies born at the two sites were followed for their 28 days perinatal outcome. Among the 796 deliveries, whose GA data is recorded, term deliveries were more common as in 699 (87.8%), the remaining 57 (7.2%) were late preterm, 23 (3%) were post term, and 17 (2%) were early preterm or delivered during the second trimester. A total of 681 (83.8%) infants had appropriate weights, whereas 67 (8.2%) were classified as low birth weight (VLBW), 15 (1.8%) as very low birth weight (LBW), and 50 (6.2%) as macrocosmic.

Stillbirth was noted in 20 (2.5%) of all 813 deliveries, and 63 (7.7%) newborns required special care. Among the babies who had congenital anomalies, 14 (30.4%) were stillborn, and 18 (39.2%) needed special care (p ≤ 0.001).

During the 28-day follow-up, an additional 4 infants died form those born alive (neonatal death) and this makes the total perinatal mortality as 24 (3%). Perinatal mortality was 37% among babies with congenital anomalies, compared to only 1% among babies without anomalies (p < 0.001), indicating a very strong association between congenital anomalies and perinatal death (Tables 3 and 4).

Maternal risk factors associated with congenital anomalies

Table 3 shows maternal and paternal risk factors associated with birth defects (congenital anomalies). Accordingly, a proportional number of male (48%) and female (52%) babies were represented in this study. Birth weights within the normal range seen in 21 (45.7%) babies who had congenital anomalies, and in 660 (86%) babies had no congenital anomalies. Nine (19.6%), 12 (26%), and 4 (8.7%) babies with congenital anomalies exhibited VLBW, LBW, and macrocosmic, respectively. We combined the appropriate-weight and macrocosmic groups to meet the logistic regression requirement of having more than 10 observations per category and the occurrence of VLBW and LBW was significantly higher among babies with congenital anomalies compared to those without anomalies (p ≤ < 0.001). A significant number of babies with congenital anomalies (17; 37%) were preterm (p ≤ 0.001). The majority (33;71.7%) of babies with congenital anomalies were from the primipara (p ≤ 0.001).

Among the examined risk factors, 92 mothers (11%) presented with chronic medical illnesses, infections, or pregnancy-induced conditions. Seven (15.2%) mothers with congenital anomalies had a history of chronic or pregnancy-related medical illnesses or infections. Chronic hypertension was observed in 25 mothers, while 33 experienced pregnancy-induced illnesses such as hypertension (PIH), preeclampsia, or gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Chronic infections included seven cases of HIV sero-reactivity and four cases of positivity for HBSAg. Pregnancy-related infections, such as urinary tract infections (UTIs), affected five mothers. The additional conditions included type 2 diabetes mellitus in six patients, epilepsy in two patients, asthma in three patients, and cardiac illnesses in two patients. Anemia affected 12 (1.5%) mothers, with only three taking Haem-UP for mild anemia. There was no significant relationship between maternal medical conditions and the development of anomalies (p = 0.390).

Eighty-three (10.2%) mothers had never taken folic acid during their current pregnancy, 70 (8.6%) had taken folic acid before pregnancy or within six weeks of pregnancy, and 660 (81.2%) had taken folic acid six weeks after pregnancy. The majority of the mothers of babies with congenital anomalies did not consume folic acid 14 (30.4%) or took it six weeks after pregnancy 26 (56.6%) (p = < 0.001).

Only 415 (58.2) women had a total weight gain greater than 11.5 kg during pregnancy. Among those babies with congenital anomalies, 16 (48.55) mothers exhibited a total weight gain of 11.5 kg or less throughout the course of their pregnancy.

No mothers reported nutritional deficiencies. Only 3 (6.5%) babies with congenital anomalies had some behavioral or work-related chemical exposure during pregnancy, including alcohol and tobacco use (p = 0.011). Three neonates had a first-degree history of congenital anomalies (one from the father and two from previous deliveries of siblings).

A history of stillbirth in previous pregnancies was noted for 11 (1.4%) mothers, none of whom had congenital anomalies on the current delivery.

Prenatal screening through antenatal ultrasound was conducted in 96.7% of the mothers, with only 22.9% having their initial ultrasound during the first trimester. Among the infants with congenital anomalies, abnormalities were detected in only 22 (48.9%) infants during fetal examination. Of the abnormalities observed via ultrasound, polyhydramnios was observed in 4 of the fetuses with anomalies, and oligohydramnios was detected in 5 fetuses with anomalies. Fourteen (1.7%) fetuses had intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR). Of those 46 babies with congenital anomalies, 3 (6.8%) had IUGR (p = 0.008).

Of the 46 mothers of babies with congenital anomalies, only six (14%) had one to eight ANC visits; the remaining 37 (86%) had more than eight ANC visits.

Binary logistic regression analysis

To choose predictor variables for the multivariable logistic regression model, a bivariate analysis was conducted on 12 variables: place of delivery, neonatal sex, birth weight, gestational age (GA), intrauterine growth retardation (IUGR), maternal age, parity, folic acid intake, total weight gain during pregnancy, number of antenatal care (ANC) visits, maternal medical conditions, and chemical exposure. Of these variables, four variables, maternal age (p value = 0.627), total weight gain during pregnancy (p value = 0.425), number of ANC visits (p value = 0.976), and maternal medical conditions (p value = 0.390), were excluded from the multivariable model. The remaining eight variables were included in the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis, with p values less than 0.25. Among the eight variables included in the multivariable model, four variables, namely, place of delivery, sex, birth weight, and parity, remained significantly different at the 0.05 level.

Accordingly, the odds of having a birth defect increase by 3.73 for women attending a private hospital compared to those attending a public hospital (AOR 3.73, 95% CI 1.67, 8.32). The odds of birth defects were 1.97 times greater for males than for females (AOR 1.97, 95% CI 0.99, 3.91; p = 0.051). The odds of birth defects were found to be 9.3 times greater among neonates with very low birth weight and low birth weight than among neonates with appropriate birth weight (AOR 9.29, 95% CI 3.41, 25.31). Primipara women had 3.64 times greater odds of delivering babies with birth defects than did multipara women (AOR 3.64, 95% CI 1.45, 9.13) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this pilot study involving 813 newborns, 5.7% were born with congenital anomalies, a prevalence at the higher end of rates reported globally as 3–6%, exceeding those observed in most developed and developing countries [1, 8, 9]. This prevalence is even greater than that reported in previous prevalence studies performed in LMICs, including Ethiopia, which were mainly retrospective in pediatric patients admitted to neonatal or pediatric wards or those visiting hospitals for any illness [10–14]. Despite the absence of a standard surveillance registry in Ethiopia, in some studies, including ours, which is performed by evaluation during delivery, the prevalence of congenital anomalies has also increased [15, 16]. Unfortunately, the majority of the studies are not representative of overall congenital anomalies at the community level since the studies are mainly performed in patients admitted at the hospital level or with deliveries at health facilities; these findings warrant representative study in the future to determine the exact prevalence and risk of congenital anomalies.

In patients with congenital anomalies (CAs), the birth outcome can be severely impacted, resulting in high mortality or morbidity, demanding special perinatal care. In the present study, there was a high rate of stillbirths; 20/813 (2.5%) of whom 14/46 (30%) had CAs. Early neonatal death was 24 (3%) out of all live births, which led to an overall perinatal mortality of 44/813 (5.4%). This finding is comparable with those of higher studies that reported greater stillbirth and early neonatal mortality in the presence of C [8, 13, 17–20]. In addition, a greater number of babies with CAs were born preterm and had a low birth weight, which is supported by different studies [21].

The pattern of anomalies observed in our study, with central nervous system (CNS) anomalies predominating, followed by urological anomalies, aligns with findings in studies such as those in India [19] but differs from previous studies conducted in Ethiopia [12]. This highlights the variability in congenital anomaly patterns across different geographical locations.

A distinct feature of our study is the low rate of detection of anomalies during prenatal ultrasound check-ups, which deviates from the practices observed in high-income countries [19]. This low detection rate underscores potential gaps in prenatal screening and the need for improved strategies to enhance the identification of specific anomalies. Early detection is crucial for facilitating timely management decisions such as termination or planned delivery, as well as referrals for specialized care.

In this study, visiting a private hospital was found to be a significant predictor of congenital anomalies (p = 0.001). Women with higher-risk pregnancies or more complex cases may often prefer private hospitals since private clinics offer more advanced or comprehensive prenatal screening services. This can lead to earlier and more frequent detection of congenital anomalies, which might not be as thoroughly assessed in some public or less specialized facilities.

In our study, neonates with low and very low birth weights were found to be at risk of congenital anomalies (p < 0.001), which is consistent with the findings of studies conducted in different parts of Ethiopia [22, 23].

Among the identified risk factors, first pregnancy was identified as a significant independent contributor (p = 0.006). These findings are also consistent with those of other studies [22, 24, 25]. There was also a greater risk of congenital anomalies in male neonates (p = 0.051), which is consistent with the findings of previous studies conducted in China [22, 23, 26]. This difference might be attributed to the many congenital anomalies linked to sex chromosomes and X-linked genetic disorders. Some congenital anomalies, such as those in the genitourinary system, are more common in males.

Interestingly, unlike findings in many studies [16, 27, 28], in our study, maternal age was not found to be a determining factor for congenital anomalies. This discrepancy may be caused by the small population of women older than 35 years with congenital anomalies in our study. However, these findings are supported by a large population-based study conducted in British Columbia and China [26, 29]. Although the findings from this population-based analysis of no association between the incidence of birth defects of unknown etiology and advancing maternal age may provide some reassurance to women who choose to delay childbearing, it is essential to recognize that this conclusion is based on the specific findings of this study and may not be universally applicable. In addition, unlike findings in many studies [16, 30–33], folic acid intake and maternal medical condition did not emerge as determining factors for congenital anomalies in our study.

The observed high burden of congenital anomalies may be influenced by multiple contextual factors. Nutritional deficiencies, particularly folic acid deficiency, are known contributors to certain congenital anomalies, and late or absent supplementation during pregnancy may increase risk which is seen in our cases too (On the bivariate analysis though it is not independent factor). Pregnancy-related complications, including maternal infections and hypertension, may also play a role. Chronic maternal illnesses and pregnancy-related complications, while more frequent in our cohort, did not reach statistical significance but could still contribute to higher rates. Additionally, tertiary centers often receive more severe or complex cases, which may inflate the apparent overall burden. Recognizing these factors is important for guiding future research, raising awareness, and informing targeted secondary prevention strategies, including early detection, education, and appropriate nutrient supplementation.

Our study included multiple congenital malformations, whereas earlier research concentrated on particular congenital anomalies, such as neural tube defects, which could account for the disparity. Hence, these findings do not necessarily negate the potential benefits of folic acid, and we need to underscore the need for increased awareness and interventions regarding the importance of folic acid supplementation, especially in populations where its uptake is suboptimal.

Additionally, efforts to enhance prenatal screening practices and surveillance systems in Ethiopia could contribute to improved detection rates and better management of congenital anomalies. Further research is warranted to explore the factors contributing to the observed differences in anomaly patterns and risk factors between this study and previous research in Ethiopia and other regions.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The inclusion of both private and government health centers enhances the study’s representativeness. Studying at birth will help in picking all patients to obtain real value beyond a retrospective study or a study performed in a pediatric ward only.

The limitation of this study is that it was only performed in Addis Ababa and only at the health facility, which lacks geographic representation. Some variables, including gestational age, IUGR and parity had slightly fewer observations than the total study population due to missing information in medical records. Although the proportion of missing data was small (< 5%), this may have introduced minor bias in the analysis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study contributes valuable insights into the prevalence, patterns, birth outcomes and predictors of congenital anomalies within the studied population. The high incidence and dismal outcome of congenital anomalies emphasizes the need for comprehensive prenatal care, including early diagnosis, vigilant monitoring of risk factors, and targeted interventions focused on private MCH clinics, male and low birth weight neonates and primipara women to improve neonatal outcomes. Further research and collaboration across healthcare disciplines, as well as the Ministry of Health, to establish a surveillance system for congenital anomalies can enhance our understanding and management of congenital anomalies in the country.
