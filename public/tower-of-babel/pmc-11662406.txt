Twelve-lead ambulatory ECG recording using a seven-electrode recorder: An alternative method for electrophysiological evaluation
Authors: Carlos Arthur Hansel Diniz da Costa, Gabriela Menichelli Medeiros Coelho, Rhanniel Theodorus Helhyas Oliveira Shilva Gomes Villar, Enia Lúcia Coutinho, Claudio Cirenza, Angelo Amato Vincenzo de Paola
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
IntroductionConventional three-lead ambulatory electrocardiogram recording (3L-AECG) is used for the quantitative diagnosis of arrhythmias. However, the lack of crucial information, such as QRS morphology and orientation, renders the 3L-AECG incomplete for planning electrophysiological interventions. The 12-lead AECG (12L-AECG) merges the temporal resolution 3L-AECG with the spatial resolution of the standard electrocardiogram (S-ECG). Although it provides more detail, it is not widely used. This study aimed to verify whether the seven-electrode 12L-AECG and S-ECG have similar waveforms.MethodsA questionnaire consisting of 240 side-by-side comparisons (12 leads from 20 patients) was created. These consisted of a QRS registered using the 12L-AECG and a QRS from the same patient, registered using the S-ECG. The questionnaire was submitted to cardiologists trained in electrophysiology. For each comparison, the evaluator assigned “similar” or “different” depending on their own judgment.ResultsFive cardiologists completed the questionnaire, resulting in 1200 answers. The AECG-12 was similar to the ECG in 84.50 % of the instances (95 % confidence interval [CI] 83.20–86.50). The interobserver agreement was moderate (0.542, p < 0.001). The similarity between specific leads ranged up to 98 % (95 % CI 92.96–99.76). No significant differences were found among patients (p = 0.407).ConclusionThe seven-electrode 12L-AECG and S-ECG produced comparable waveforms. This similarity supports the use of 12L-AECG for accurate arrhythmia tracking and assists in planning electrophysiological procedures.

Introduction

Ambulatory electrocardiogram recording, also known as Holter recording, was named after its creator [1]. Currently, it is a cornerstone test in the field of cardiology. This method has improved dramatically since its introduction. It was originally designed to detect premature ventricular beats (PVC). Over time, it has gained the ability to analyze pacemaker behavior, ST segment deviations, and QT variations [2].

While this method quantifies premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) in terms of frequency and coupling, its three leads do not provide as much detail as the twelve leads of the standard 12-lead electrocardiogram (S-ECG). Its applications in precision scenarios, such as planning catheter ablation, are limited. For example, it is impossible to plan a procedure that does not determine whether a PVC arises from the right or left ventricular outflow tract.

The 12-lead ambulatory electrocardiogram recording (12L-AECG) was introduced in 1966 by Mason and Likar [3]. However, owing to differences in the QRS amplitudes and mean electrical axis, it underwent some modifications. These corrections have improved its quality. As a result, the 12L-AECG became very similar to the S-ECG in terms of the final recording [4,5]. Thus far, only the ten-electrodes Mason and Likar system and the five-electrodes EASI system have been tested in a clinical scenario.

This study aimed to compare the similarity between the seven-electrodes 12L-AECG and S-ECG according to the perception of trained cardiologists.

Methods

Patient population

Informed consent was obtained from all patients. This study was approved by the ethics committee of the Federal University of São Paulo (CAAE: 64353722.5.0000.5505). Twenty patients were enrolled using convenience sampling. Patients were enrolled when they returned to the office for regular follow-up. No patients with acute disease or decompensation were enrolled. All patients had either an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD) or cardiac resynchronization therapy–defibrillator (CRT-D). All patients were regularly followed-up at our hospital. All comorbidities were treated according to the current guidelines. The mean ejection fraction was 48.65 % (48.65 ± 12.14). The average age was 60.85 years (60.85 ± 12.44). The patients exhibit a wide range of pathologies, mainly Chagas disease. By the time of data acquisition, most patients did not receive any type of ventricular pacing support (device operating in AxVs – atrial sense or pace followed by an intrinsic ventricular sensed event). Four patients (20 %) presented with normal QRS complexes. We defined a normal QRS as one that was narrow (duration <120 milliseconds), with a mean axis between −30 and + 90°, without pathological Q waves, without notches, without ST deviation following it, and not resulting from pacing. Recording the exams

The 12L-AECG were obtained using a DMS 300-8 seven-electrode recorder (D.M. Software, Los Angeles, CA, USA).

Silver chloride (AgCl) electrodes were used (Maxicor, Pinhais, BR). They consisted of a central conductive core surrounded by a white adhesive frame. By electrode position, we refer specifically to the location of the central conductive core, as the adhesive frame does not contribute to signal acquisition. Six electrodes were placed on the patient's chest, and one electrode was placed on the back. One electrode was placed immediately below the articulatio sternoclavicularis. One electrode was placed on the left parasternal line adjacent to the processus xiphoides. Four electrodes were placed at the level of the tenth rib, one on each hemiclavicular line and one on each anterior axillary line. The last electrode was placed on the left paraspinal line adjacent to the angular superior scapulae. The electrodes were placed by a highly experienced nurse who has attended the service for many years. The electrode placement is shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Electrode placement on the patient's body. Six electrodes were placed on the chest and one on the back.Fig. 1

Electrode placement on the patient's body. Six electrodes were placed on the chest and one on the back.

The sampling rate was 4096 per second with an 8-bits resolution. S-ECG recordings were performed using a DMS HD system (DM Software, Los Angeles, CA, USA) with the electrodes placed in the standard position. Standard calibration (25 mm/s and 10 mm/mV) and 60 Hz filtering were applied during data acquisition.

The 12L-AECG were reconstructed using a proprietary algorithm based on Frank's leads.

Questionnaire elaboration

Traces of each patient (both 12L-AECG and S-ECG) were collected, paired, and anonymized by the main author.

Twelve questions were developed for each participant. Each question displayed a pair of QRS, one recorded by the 12L-AECG and the other by the S-ECG. Both QRS came from the same lead and patient. For example, the first question pair was created using patient number 1's traces. The pair consisted of a QRS from lead 1, recorded using the 12L-AECG, and a QRS from lead 1, recorded using the S-ECG.

If the patient was under ventricular pacing support at the time of recording, only the paced QRS was used. The pairs did not consist of native and a paced QRS.

This resulted in 240 pairs (12 pairs for each of the 20 patients). A simple binary question regarding whether the QRS was similar or different was posed for each pair. The questionnaire was designed in Portuguese; therefore, “similaridade” meant similar and “diferença” meant different. The questionnaire was compiled using Google Forms. Answering the questionnaire

The questionnaire was sent via e-mail to cardiologists with expertise in electrophysiology.

They evaluated each pair and determined whether the QRS were similar. This interpretation was purely subjective. Amplitude or duration measurements were not obtained. There was no time limit for answering the questions. After each of the 240 pairs was evaluated, the questionnaire was considered complete and sent back to the authors via Google Forms.

Similarity definition

For the purposes of this study, similarity was defined as the percentage of times the evaluators considered the 12L-AECG and S-ECG recordings to be similar. In other words, when they checked “semelhança” in the form.

Question's score definition

Whenever an evaluator considered a pair to be similar, a score of one was assigned. A score of zero was assigned if the pair was considered different. The question's score is defined as the sum of each of its scores. In other words, the number of times its pair was considered similar.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative variables are expressed as counts, percentages, means, and medians. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Rater agreement on the comparison between the 12L-AECG and S-ECG recordings was evaluated using the Fleiss Kappa method. The Fleiss Kappa method was chosen instead of Cohen's Kappa because five raters were involved. This test is appropriate for checking the agreement between multiple raters evaluating two different methods (in our case, surface S-ECG and 12L-AECG). The confidence intervals (CI) for binary data were evaluated using a binomial distribution. We compared the data between the two groups according to the score of each question. For comparisons involving non-normally distributed data, we employed the Kruskal–Wallis test (non-parametric ANOVA). For the post-hoc analysis, we employed Dwass-Steel-Critchlow-Flinger pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. The open-source Jamovi software was used for the analysis (Jamovi Project (2023), Sydney, Australia).

Results

Questionnaire results

In total, five cardiologists trained in electrophysiology answered the questionnaire, resulting in 1200 direct comparisons between the S-ECG and 12L-AECG. Among those comparisons, 1014 (84.50 %, [95 % CI 83.20–86.50]) were considered similar and 186 different. The interobserver agreement was moderate according to the Landis and Koch classification [6] for the Fleiss Kappa coefficient (0.542, p < 0.001). Further analysis revealed that interobserver agreement varied depending on whether the QRS complexes were normal or abnormal. For abnormal QRS, the correlation was moderate according to the Kappa coefficient (0.522, p < 0.001). However, for normal QRS, the correlation was substantial (0.617, p < 0.001). Each question's score ranged from 0 to 5 (Supplementary Material). The scores were not normally distributed (W = 0.596, p < 0.001). The mean score was 4.22 and the median was 5.

Similarity among leads

Each lead was evaluated 100 times, five times for each of 20 patients. When they were evaluated separately, the similarity was on average 85 ± 9.19 %. It ranged from 98 % [95 % CI 92.96–99.76] to 68 % [95 % CI 57.90–77.00]. There was a significant difference between leads (p = 0.027). However, using pairwise comparisons, we were unable to identify the leads responsible for this difference (Supplementary Material). Therapeutic implications

One enrolled patient presented with recurrent ventricular tachycardia (VT) during follow-up, indicating the need for an electrophysiological study. One tachycardia episode was recorded using 12L-AECG. This served as the basis for our mapping strategy.

During the procedure, clinical VT was easily induced and matched with 12L-AECG recordings (Fig. 3). We confirmed clinical VT by simultaneous interrogation of the patient's implantable cardioverter-defibrillator in the electrophysiology laboratory. Discussion

The main finding of this study was that 12L-AECG recordings were similar to those of the ECG. Even some trivial information, such as the side of the branch block and the mean electrical axis, are hidden variables for the 3L-AECG, but are crystal clear to the 12L-AECG. This information is of the utmost importance to electrophysiologists. In principle, all ambulatory electrocardiogram recording devices should use 12-lead configuration as reported elsewhere [7].

Some authors have proven the feasibility of derived twelve-lead electrocardiograms in clinical practice using the Mason-Likar [8,9], EASI [10,11] and other [12,13] systems. The Mason-Likar system uses ten electrodes to acquire data, and the EASI system uses five electrodes. Although different 12L-AECG systems have been studied over the years, to our knowledge, the seven-electrode system has never been properly compared with S-ECG.

Interobserver agreement differed significantly when QRS complexes were subclassified as 'normal' or 'abnormal.' Agreement was considerably higher for the normal QRS group. We hypothesize that evaluators were more rigorous when presented with an abnormal QRS. It is well recognized that when physicians analyze a particular exam, they tend to focus their attention on abnormal findings. This phenomenon has been extensively studied in the field of radiology, where it is referred to as ‘satisfaction of search’ [14], or colloquially, as ‘happy eye syndrome.’ We believe that a similar phenomenon occurred in this study, with evaluators concentrating more attention on the abnormal waveforms. This more rigorous analysis led to inferior agreement in the abnormal QRS group.

The S-ECG remains the gold standard for arrhythmia analyses. However, the use of fewer electrodes is more comfortable for patients. In addition, fewer electrodes are less prone to dislodgement. The odds of a failed test owing to electrode dislodgement were lower when working with fewer electrodes. Lead dislodgement is rarely reported in the literature. In our study, no instances of lead dislodgement were observed. At our center, we secure the electrodes with adhesive plasters after placement, making lead failure exceedingly rare. Further studies are required to assess this reduction. However, due to the rarity of such events, a substantially large patient cohort would be necessary to detect a statistically significant difference.

The relatively poor performance of leads V1 and V2 was further analyzed by the authors. Given the crucial information provided by these leads, such results could be misleading in situations such as distinguishing VT from supraventricular tachycardia or in localizing specific VT sites. We concluded that, although differences in notches, amplitudes, and slew rates were observed, the QRS polarity remained consistent. In other words, a positive QRS always remained positive, and a negative QRS always remained negative. We believe that the low scores of V1 and V2 were a consequence of the evaluators' rigorous analysis and not due to computational or lead placement errors.

As observed in this study, 12L-AECG can be used not only for clinical understanding but also as a basis for planning catheter ablation. The qualitative aspects of tracing, such as the precise QRS complex morphology, bestow the 12L-AECG with an unpaired advantage over the 3L-AECG. This unique advantage distinguishes them from one another.

Limitations

Our study used a small conveniently sampled population. This was used to avoid creating an extensively long questionnaire. We considered 240 questions, which were enough to obtain reasonable results. A longer questionnaire would likely cause evaluators to lose focus because of the long answering times. This small population may have led to potential uncertainties in the results.

Because only electrophysiologists evaluated the results, they may have focused on details that general cardiologists may not have noticed (e.g., notches and slew rates). This may have lowered the similarity rate, because the analyses were very strict.

This study was designed as a subset of a larger study evaluating recipients of implantable cardioverter-defibrillators. Consequently, it included only patients with structural heart diseases, and no healthy participants were enrolled. This limitation holds potential for future studies to analyze the performance of the 12L-AECG in healthy individuals. The same applies to patients with idiopathic ventricular arrhythmias. We believe these results would be consistent.

Our 12L-AECG recordings were reconstructed using a proprietary algorithm provided by the manufacturer. We reached out to the manufacturer to obtain more detailed information about the algorithm's specifics; however, they did not disclose comprehensive data on its steps and transformations. This information could have helped us better understand some of our results. For example, we were unable to identify why the limb leads outperformed the chest leads, particularly V1 and V2, given that all electrodes were placed in the same manner by the same nurse.

We observed significant variations in the similarity rates among patients, with differences reaching up to 30 % in some cases. Several recognized factors can interfere with the signal acquisition of ambulatory electrocardiograms, including body movement, environmental noise, and skeletal myopotentials [7]. Additional factors, such as body habitus or body mass index, may also have influenced the recordings. However, our study did not collect data on daily activity, environmental conditions, or patient biometrics. This information might have provided insight into the discrepancies observed between patient results.

The final limitation of this study is its subjectivity. Objective similarity measurements such as the mean absolute deviation and correlation coefficient are available [15]. Although these measurements exist, they are mostly confined to electrophysiology laboratories. The daily routine of the cardiologist, and even that of the electrophysiologist, does not involve such measurements. The use of these techniques may have affected the internal validity of the study. However, they had no impact on the external validity.

Conclusion

The seven-electrode method for 12L-AECG produced tracings that were similar to those of the S-ECG. It may be feasible to use 12L-AECG as a method for planning ventricular tachycardia ablation.
