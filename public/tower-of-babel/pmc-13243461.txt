Influence mechanism of pre-oxidation on the secondary oxidation characteristics of coal: insights from TG-DSC-FTIR analysis
Authors: Shaoqiang Yun, Ju Zhao, Hailong Wu, Yujie Li, Min Cai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe secondary oxidation behavior of coal represents a critical process governing its spontaneous combustion propensity and pollutant emissions. To elucidate the influence mechanism of pre-oxidation on the secondary oxidation characteristics of coal, this study selected raw coal from Dananhu, Xinjiang (DNH) as the research subject. Pre-oxidized coal samples (DNH-80) were prepared via temperature-programmed heating to 80 °C with isothermal holding for 1 h. The thermal behavior, reaction kinetics, and Gas emissions characteristics of both coal samples were systematically investigated using thermogravimetry–differential scanning calorimetry-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (TG-DSC-FTIR) at three heating rates (5, 8, and 12 K/min). The results demonstrate that pre-oxidation treatment significantly altered the physicochemical properties of coal: proximate analysis revealed a substantial decrease in moisture content from 11.56% to 1.38%, accompanied by significant increases in fixed carbon and volatile matter contents. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy further confirmed substantial microstructural alterations, characterized by a marked increase in oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., carbonyl C=O) and a reorganization of aliphatic structures. Kinetic analysis revealed that the apparent activation energy of pre-oxidized coal during the main combustion stage (conversion degree of 0.2–0.6) was systematically lower than that of raw coal, indicating a marked enhancement in reactivity. Thermal analysis data further confirmed that pre-oxidized coal exhibited more intense combustion behavior, with its maximum mass loss rate (DTGmax) increasing by 3.0–10.1% and total heat release increasing by 6.6–7.2%. Gas emissions analysis demonstrated that pre-oxidized coal produced sharper CO2 release peaks, with cumulative total release increasing by 7.27–10.13% relative to raw coal. This study elucidates the mechanism by which pre-oxidation intensifies the secondary oxidation process of coal through reducing activation energy and altering reaction process, providing essential theoretical foundation and experimental data for accurately assessing the spontaneous combustion risk and environmental impact of pre-oxidized coal.Keywords: Coal spontaneous combustion, Pre-oxidation, Reaction process, Gas emissions, Kinetic analysisSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Coal, as the cornerstone of the energy system in China and globally, is persistently threatened by spontaneous combustion hazards during mining, storage, and stockpiling. This issue constitutes a critical impediment to safe production, causing resource losses and environmental pollution1–3. Coal spontaneous combustion not only directly reduces utilizable coal resources but also releases toxic gases such as carbon monoxide and sulfur dioxide, as well as greenhouse gases including carbon dioxide and methane, further exacerbating the risks of mine fires and even explosions4–6. Notably, even after spontaneous combustion phenomena in coal stockpiles or goaf areas are temporarily extinguished through human intervention, residual coals remain susceptible to secondary oxidation upon subsequent exposure to air7. Field observations demonstrate that such “pre-oxidized coal,” which has undergone low-temperature oxidation, often exhibited a shorter spontaneous combustion induction period and enhanced re-ignition propensity under storage or re-mining conditions compared with raw coal samples. However, there is currently a lack of in-depth research on the kinetic behavior and gas emission characteristics during the secondary oxidation process of pre oxidized coal. Therefore, the system’s revelation of the reaction kinetics and gas product generation mechanism during the secondary oxidation process of pre oxidized coal is of great practical significance for scientifically evaluating its re ignition risk and improving coal fire prevention theory.

The modification of coal chemical structure by pre-oxidation treatment is pivotal in regulating its subsequent oxidation behavior. At the microscopic level, numerous studies have confirmed that pre-oxidation significantly influences the composition and distribution of functional groups in coal. Duan et al.8 demonstrated through pre-oxidation experiments at varying temperatures that this process promotes the consumption of hydroxyl groups (–OH) while enhancing the transformation of aliphatic chain structures such as methyl (–CH3) and methylene (–CH2) groups and oxygen-containing functional groups. Guo et al.9 further noted that with increasing pre-oxidation temperature, aromatic hydrocarbon structures in coal gradually decrease, while aliphatic hydrocarbon content exhibits an initial increase followed by a decline, wherein ether bonds (–C–O–) decrease whereas carbonyl (–C=O) and carboxyl (–COO–) groups markedly increase. These findings align with Jo et al.10, who reported decreased aliphatic hydrocarbon and ether bond contents upon pre-oxidation, and Xu et al.11, who proposed that pre-oxidation enhances the oxidation reactivity of aliphatic hydrocarbon structures. Additionally, Xu et al.12 emphasized that the influence of pre-oxidation temperature and mechanical conditions on coal spontaneous combustion behavior is primarily achieved by regulating the quantity of oxygen-containing functional groups and coal permeability. Zhang et al.13 discovered that photo-thermal synergistic effects facilitate the conversion of aliphatic structures to oxygen-containing functional groups and cause pore structure evolution from micropores and mesopores toward macropores, providing the basis for enhanced macroscopic reactivity of pre-oxidized coal.

In terms of macroscopic reaction performance, pre-oxidized coal exhibits distinct oxidation characteristics and thermodynamic behaviors compared to raw coal. Xu et al.14 explicitly stated that pre-oxidation treatment enhances coal oxidation reactivity. Mahidin et al.15 revealed that moisture and volatile matter contents gradually decrease with increasing oxidation temperature. Regarding gas emission behavior, conflicting viewpoints persist in the literature: Zhang et al.16 reported that gas product concentrations during the spontaneous combustion of oxidized coal are lower than those of raw coal; conversely, Wen et al.17 and Deng et al.18 observed higher carbon monoxide generation rates, oxygen consumption rates, and heat release intensities during the secondary oxidation stage of pre-oxidized coal. Complementary to these studies, research on Zonguldak coals has demonstrated that online CO monitoring can effectively indicate the onset of low-temperature oxidation activity, and that gas content and spontaneous combustion liability are closely linked to coal rank and petrographic properties19,20. Similarly, investigations on Turkish lignites have revealed that gas adsorption and desorption characteristics are influenced by coal quality parameters and petrographic composition21–23. These findings underscore the importance of integrating gas emission analysis with microstructural characterization—an approach adopted in the present study.

Concerning energy release and reaction kinetics, Wang et al.24,25 argued that parameters including exothermic behavior, gas product distribution, and apparent activation energy of pre-oxidized coal undergo significant alterations. Lv et al.26, however, proposed divergent opinions, suggesting that the exothermic temperature of pre-oxidized coal may exhibit hysteresis, with both heat release and maximum heat release power being lower than those of raw coal. Guo et al.9 further refined this understanding from a reaction stage perspective, proposing that pre-oxidation exerts more pronounced effects on the moisture evaporation and oxygen adsorption stages of coal, while its influence on the high-temperature combustion stage is relatively limited. These divergent conclusions reflect the significant impacts of coal properties, pre-oxidation conditions, and experimental methodologies on results, underscoring the urgency and necessity of conducting multi-dimensional, systematic experimental investigations into the secondary oxidation behavior of pre-oxidized coal.

To address these knowledge gaps, this study investigates raw coal (DNH) from the Dananhu mining area in Xinjiang. Pre-oxidized samples (DNH-80) were prepared using temperature-programmed oxidation at 80 °C. Combined thermogravimetry–differential scanning calorimetry-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (TG-DSC-FTIR) analysis was conducted at three heating rates (5, 8, and 12 K/min) to systematically compare the thermal behavior, reaction kinetics, and gas emission characteristics between raw and pre-oxidized coal. The research focuses on the following aspects: (i) analyzing the influence of pre-oxidation on proximate analysis, characteristic temperatures of combustion stages, and heat release behavior; (ii) investigating the variation patterns of kinetic parameters during secondary oxidation based on the Starink method; (iii) elucidating the release characteristics and cumulative emissions of gases such as CO2 in different temperature intervals using online infrared spectroscopy; and (iv) characterizing the evolution of key functional groups via FTIR to establish the microstructural basis for the observed macro-behavioral changes. Through these investigations, this study aims to clarify the intrinsic mechanisms by which pre-oxidation intensifies the secondary oxidation process of coal from microstructural, energetic, and procedural perspectives, providing theoretical foundations and data support for the scientific assessment of spontaneous combustion risk and pollution prevention in pre-oxidized coal. It should be noted that the present study focuses on a single coal sample from the Dananhu mining area; therefore, the generalizability of the conclusions to other coal types requires further investigation. While similar instrumental techniques have been used in previous studies8, the present work distinguishes itself through full-range iso-conversional kinetic analysis with validation, quantitative CO2 emission assessment, and explicit correlation between microstructural evolution and macro-behavioral changes.

Experimental sample preparation and apparatus

Sample preparation

The coal sample for testing was collected from the Dananhu mining area in Hami City, Xinjiang. After obtaining the raw coal on-site, it was immediately sealed in airtight bags and transported to the laboratory for storage. Prior to testing, the coal sample was crushed and screened to obtain a 1–3 mm particle size fraction, designated as DNH, for the pre-oxidation experiment. One kilogram of coal was placed in a steel container (10 cm diameter × 22 cm height). Air was continuously introduced from the bottom of the container at a constant flow rate of 100 mL/min. The coal sample was heated to 180 °C at a rate of 1 °C/min, with gas samples collected and analyzed at 10 °C intervals. The experimental results indicate that when the coal temperature exceeds 70 ℃, the concentration of carbon monoxide in the container increases significantly, suggesting that the critical temperature of this coal sample is approximately 70 ℃. To obtain fully oxidized samples, 80 °C was selected as the preparation temperature for pre-oxidized coal samples in this study. The choice of 80 °C was based on the following considerations. First, online gas monitoring during preliminary experiments showed a significant increase in CO concentration when the coal temperature exceeded 70 °C, indicating the transition to an active chemical reaction stage; thus 70 °C was taken as the critical temperature. Second, selecting a temperature 10 °C above the critical point allows the coal to undergo a stable low-temperature pre-oxidation while avoiding irreversible pyrolysis or structural collapse, thereby simulating the state of coal that has been “critically activated” under actual engineering conditions. Subsequently, under identical experimental conditions, 1 kg of raw coal was programmed to heat to 80 °C and held isothermally at this temperature for one hour, ultimately yielding the pre-oxidized coal sample designated as DNH-80. Both raw and pre-oxidized coal samples were further ground to pass through a 100-mesh sieve for subsequent characterization. The proximate and ultimate analysis results for raw and pre-oxidized coal are presented in Table 1. Notably, following pre-oxidation, the moisture content was substantially reduced, while volatile matter and fixed carbon contents increased significantly, which is expected to profoundly affect the combustion and Gas emissions processes.

TG-DSC-FTIR coupled experiments

Thermal analysis experiments were conducted on a Netzsch STA 449 F3 simultaneous thermogravimetric-differential scanning calorimeter (TG-DSC) using uncovered Al2O3 crucibles under dry air atmosphere at a flow rate of 100 mL min⁻1. Linear heating rates were programmed at 5, 8, and 12 K/min over a temperature range of 30–700 °C. Baseline corrections were performed using empty crucibles under identical conditions and automatically subtracted. Sample mass was controlled at (10.0 ± 0.2) mg. Evolved gas analysis (EGA) was implemented by coupling with a Bruker Tensor II Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (FTIR). The transfer line and gas cell were both maintained at 200 °C to prevent condensation of high-boiling-point components. IR spectra were recorded over the range of 4000–650 cm⁻1 with a resolution of 4 cm⁻1 at a collection rate of 1 spectrum s⁻1. All experimental data were synchronously acquired via Netzsch Proteus and Bruker OPUS software suites and temporally aligned to enable real-time correlation between mass changes and Gas emissions.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) experiment

To investigate the characteristic changes in microscopic functional groups of coal following pre-oxidation, FTIR spectroscopy was performed on both raw and pre-oxidized coal samples. The prepared coal samples were thoroughly mixed and ground with spectroscopic-grade potassium bromide (KBr) at a mass ratio of approximately 1:100 in an agate mortar until the particle size was less than 2 μm. Subsequently, an appropriate amount of the mixed powder was placed in a pellet die and compressed at approximately 12 MPa for 1–2 min to form uniform, transparent pellets for analysis. Spectra were collected over a wavenumber range of 4000–650 cm⁻1 with a resolution of 4 cm⁻1 and 32 scans. Following data acquisition, peak deconvolution and assignment were performed based on characteristic functional group absorptions to identify the types of functional groups and their quantitative changes.

Results and discussion

Microstructural functional group changes in raw and pre-oxidized coal

The FTIR spectra obtained for the raw and pre-oxidized coal samples are shown in Fig. 1. The results indicate that while the shapes and positions of the absorption peaks remain largely similar, the intensities of these peaks have undergone noticeable changes. This suggests that pre-oxidation did not alter the types of functional groups present in the coal but significantly affected their relative abundances. Therefore, to more precisely determine the changes in the microscopic functional groups of the coal after pre-oxidation, peak deconvolution was performed using PeakFit software. The spectra were fitted, and the peaks were assigned to different functional groups based on established correlations13, with the results presented in Fig. 2.

Comparison of FTIR curves between raw and pre-oxidized coal: (a) DNH, (b) DNH-80.

Comparison of functional group content between raw and pre-oxidized coal.

As clearly shown in Fig. 2, the relative contents of the microscopic functional groups in the DNH coal changed significantly after pre-oxidation. The most pronounced changes occurred in the region of oxygen-containing functional groups. The relative content of associated hydroxyl groups (-OH) dropped sharply from 21.69% to 14.04% after pre-oxidation. This directly confirms that the pre-oxidation process effectively removed physically adsorbed water and some bound water from the coal, which is consistent with the changes in moisture content observed in the proximate analysis. Simultaneously, oxygen-containing functional groups in higher oxidation states increased significantly: the carbonyl group (C=O) increased from 5.59% to 6.54%, the carboxyl group (–COO–) increased substantially from 0.41% to 0.84%, and the ether/phenol group (C-O) increased from 13.95% to 16.89%. This pattern of “one decrease and three increases” clearly reveals the chemical nature of pre-oxidation: some unstable hydroxyl groups are consumed during low-temperature oxidation and are converted into oxygen-containing functional groups such as C=O and –COO–, which possess poorer thermal stability but higher chemical reactivity.

Regarding the hydrocarbon structures, the relative contents of aliphatic methyl (–CH3) and methylene (–CH2–) groups increased from 9.67% and 5.88% to 12.48% and 7.45%, respectively. This does not necessarily imply an absolute increase in the total amount of aliphatic chains but is more likely due to the selective cleavage and reorganization of long aliphatic chains or naphthenic structures during pre-oxidation, generating more terminal methyl groups and short-chain methylene groups. These smaller aliphatic fragments are more volatile and easier to oxidize in thermal environments, thereby enhancing the reactivity of the coal at lower temperature stages. Conversely, the aromatic C–H (Ar–CH) content decreased significantly from 1.43% to 0.41%, and the aromatic skeleton (C=C) also showed a slight decrease (from 21.53% to 20.54%). This indicates that pre-oxidation also affected the condensed aromatic core of the coal, particularly through substantial oxidation and substitution of hydrogen atoms on the benzene ring side chains, converting them into oxygen-containing functional groups. This process makes the peripheries of the originally chemically inert aromatic nuclei more reactive.

Overall, pre-oxidation promotes the generation of more reactive oxygen-containing functional groups in coal, which may significantly influence its subsequent combustion reactions and gas generation.

The oxidation reaction process of raw coal and pre-oxidized coal

Thermogravimetric analysis was conducted on raw coal and 80℃ pre-oxidized coal under dry air atmosphere to elucidate the influence of pre-oxidation on the thermal reaction process. The results are presented in Fig. 3 and Table 2. The characteristic temperatures are defined as moisture evaporation temperature T₁, pyrolysis onset temperature T2, ignition temperature T3, temperature at maximum mass loss rate T₄, and burnout temperature T₅27,28. Both DNH and DNH-80 exhibit four consecutive stages in their TG-DTG profiles: moisture evaporation, pyrolysis, combustion, and burnout. This indicates that pre-oxidation does not alter the fundamental nature of coal-oxygen reactions. However, closer examination of the temperature intervals reveals distinct differences. In the initial stage, raw coal DNH undergoes significant mass loss before 100℃ due to moisture evaporation (Mad=11.56%), whereas DNH-80 pre-oxidized coal shows substantially reduced mass loss because most moisture had been removed during pre-oxidation (Mad=1.38%). This dehydration behavior is consistent with studies on the drying performance of low-grade coals under controlled thermal conditions29, which reported that low-temperature pre-treatment effectively removes both surface and capillary-bound water, thereby altering the subsequent thermal response. Upon entering the pyrolysis stage, aliphatic hydrocarbons and oxygen-containing functional groups of relatively low abundance begin to be gradually oxidized, decomposed, and volatilized, resulting in slight heat dissipation. When the temperature exceeds approximately 350℃, both samples enter the rapid combustion stage, where the cracking and combustion of aliphatic and aromatic compounds cause sharp mass reduction. Regarding the maximum mass loss rate (DTGmax), pre-oxidized coal exhibits varying degrees of change compared with raw coal. For instance, at a heating rate of 5 K/min, the DTGmax of DNH is 3.18%/min, lower than that of DNH-80 (3.50%/min). Analysis combined with Table 1 demonstrates that the increased fixed carbon and volatile matter contents after pre-oxidation enhance reactant availability, thereby promoting combustion intensity. More fundamentally, the enrichment of oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., -C=O) and the reorganization of aliphatic structures as revealed in Section “Microstructural functional group changes in raw and pre-oxidized coal” provide more active sites and readily oxidizable precursors, which directly contribute to the observed higher mass loss rates (DTGmax). Overall, the reaction intervals of DNH-80 shift toward higher temperatures, particularly evidenced by the significant delay of the stages marking intense fixed carbon combustion and complete burnout. Nevertheless, the combustion intensity shows a certain degree of enhancement relative to DNH. This increase in burnout temperature (T₅) can be attributed to the combined effects of increased reaction load and altered reaction pathway. Firstly, the higher fixed carbon content in DNH-80 (Table 1) inherently requires a longer duration for complete conversion. Secondly, the enriched oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., C=O) as proven in Section “Microstructural functional group changes in raw and pre-oxidized coal” promote a more intense and consumptive reaction in the early stages (reflected by the higher DTGmax), which may alter the pore structure and subsequently moderate the diffusion of oxygen to the residual char core in the later stage, further leads to a lag in the burnout temperature.

Further quantitative analysis of the characteristic temperature data illuminates the aforementioned transition in reaction process. As summarized in Table 2, the influence of pre-oxidation on the four-stage characteristic temperatures exhibits significant differences and can be categorized into two distinct groups. The first group includes moisture evaporation temperature T₁, pyrolysis onset temperature T2, and ignition temperature T3, which are generally elevated in pre-oxidized coal compared with raw coal. For example, at 5 K/min, T2 increases from 191.3 °C to 202.6 °C, representing an elevation of 11.3 °C, thereby demonstrating the pronounced effect of pre-oxidation on the early stages of spontaneous combustion. This delay may be attributed to the substantial removal of moisture and the consumption of some highly reactive groups during pre-oxidation (Section “Microstructural functional group changes in raw and pre-oxidized coal”), which requires additional thermal input to initiate the secondary oxidation of the remaining, more stable yet activated matrix. The second group comprises temperature at maximum mass loss rate T₄ and burnout temperature T₅, which undergo relatively minor alterations; under the same heating rate, T₄ only marginally decreases from 390.7 °C to 390.1 °C. This discrepancy is primarily attributed to the fact that the dominant reactions in the high-temperature stage of coal spontaneous combustion become the combustion of volatile matter and fixed carbon, whose total contents undergo negligible changes, thus limiting the impact of pre-oxidation. Pre-oxidation treatment significantly affects the thermal reaction process of coal, predominantly manifested by the reduction of moisture evaporation, pyrolysis, combustion, and burnout temperatures, along with enhanced reaction rates during the pyrolysis and burnout stages. Moreover, pre-oxidation exerts a more pronounced influence on the early phases of spontaneous combustion, while its effect on the high-temperature combustion stage is relatively limited.

Comparison of exothermic effects of raw coal and pre-oxidized coal

The DSC curves presented in Fig. 4 comprehensively elucidate the complete heat flow evolution from room temperature to 700 °C for both DNH and DNH-80, demonstrating significant correspondence with TG. Overall, the DSC profiles of both coal types can be delineated into four distinct stages: initial endothermic stage, slow exothermic stage, intense exothermic stage, and attenuation stage, with pre-oxidation systematically influencing the thermal effect intensity of each stage.

Heat flow curves of raw and pre-oxidized coal samples: (a) DNH, (b) DNH-80.

During the initial heating process, the DSC curves exhibit a weak endothermic peak with heat flow values of approximately − 0.5 to − 2.0 mW/mg, with peak temperatures occurring in the 60–120 °C interval. This endothermic peak precisely corresponds to the first mass loss stage of the TG curve (mass loss of ~ 1.5–12.0 wt%), attributed to the evaporation enthalpy of inherent moisture (adsorbed water) and some crystalline water in coal. As temperature exceeds 180 °C, the DSC curves transition from endothermic to exothermic, with heat flow values gradually increasing from near-baseline to 2–5 mW/mg, forming a broad “shoulder” platform. This corresponds to the flat plateau of the pyrolysis process in the TG curve, indicating that the heat release at this time mainly comes from the pyrolysis of low molecular mass volatiles (alkanes, aromatics) and a small amount of oxygen chemical adsorption. When temperature surpasses 350 °C, the DSC curves rise exponentially, reaching a peak in the 400–420 °C interval and forming the main peak. During this stage, heat flow values surge from 5 mW/mg to the maximum, with the maximum heat flow of DNH-80 consistently exceeding that of DNH and exhibiting a sharper peak shape. This intensified and sharper exothermic profile is a direct macroscopic manifestation of the activation of the microstructure. The higher concentration of oxygen-containing functional groups (–C=O, –COO–) and more reactive aliphatic fragments facilitate a more concurrent and vigorous oxidation reaction upon reaching their activation temperatures. This corresponds to the third mass loss stage of the TG curve—combustion stage (400–500 °C), where substantial volatiles are released and ignited while fixed carbon begins heterogeneous oxidation. Beyond the peak temperature, the DSC curves display asymmetric attenuation, with heat flow values gradually returning to baseline. This stage corresponds to the burnout interval (> 500 °C) of the TG curve, characterized by slow oxidation of residual char. Overall, the reaction intervals of pre-oxidized coal (DNH-80) shift toward higher temperatures compared with raw coal, particularly evidenced by the significant delay of stages marking intense fixed carbon combustion and complete burnout, although the combustion intensity itself is enhanced. Conversely, the exothermic peak of pre-oxidized coal exceeds that of raw coal; for instance, at 5 K/min, the maximum exothermic peak of DNH is 10.14 mW/mg, whereas DNH-80 increases to 10.90 mW/mg, indicating that the combustion process, particularly fixed carbon combustion, is enhanced after pre-oxidation. This aligns with the variations observed in thermogravimetric data and Table 1.

To further quantify the heat release characteristics of both coal samples, the DSC curves obtained at different heating rates (Fig. 4) were integrated to obtain the comparative heat release values illustrated in Fig. 5. The results clearly demonstrate that the heat output of pre-oxidized coal samples exhibits marked enhancement compared with raw coal. The heat release values for DNH coal under the three heating rates are 9735 J/g, 9682 J/g, and 9696 J/g, respectively, whereas those for DNH-80 increase to 10,435 J/g, 10,382 J/g, and 10,338 J/g, representing increases of 7.19%, 7.23%, and 6.62%, respectively. This further confirms that the combustion process is enhanced after pre-oxidation, with the oxidation-combustion reaction with oxygen being promoted, consequently leading to increased heat release.

Figure 8 reveals the nonlinear evolution of activation energy with conversion degree, profoundly reflecting the multi-stage nature of coal combustion. For raw coal, the activation energy profile exhibits a distinctive three-stage pattern of “rapid decrease–slow increase–rapid decrease” (Fig. 8). In the initial conversion stage (α ≈ 0.05–0.15, corresponding to temperatures of ~ 200–300 °C), the activation energy is initially high and decreases sharply. This may come from the initial cracking of large molecular cross-linking structures or specific oxygen-containing bridging bonds with high bond energy and poor thermal stability in the raw coal. The early decomposition of this structure requires overcoming significant energy barriers; However, once cleavage occurs, the number of active sites in the reaction system increases, leading to a rapid decrease in the activation energy of subsequent reactions.

Comparison of activation energy results between raw and pre-oxidized coal.

As combustion progresses into the main combustion stage (α = 0.3–0.6), the activation energy of raw coal shows a gradual increase. This behavior is characteristic of coal combustion, where components with higher reactivity (such as aliphatic side chains and less stable functional groups) are preferentially consumed, while the residual material becomes progressively enriched in less reactive, more stable condensed aromatic rings and fixed carbon, resulting in increased reaction difficulty.

In the later combustion stage (α > 0.7), raw coal exhibits a rapid decrease in activation energy—an anomalous phenomenon most likely attributable to the decomposition of inherent carbonate minerals in lignite. Carbonates (e.g., calcite, CaCO3) decompose at high temperatures (typically > 600 °C) to release CO2, which is a strongly endothermic reaction that manifests as significant mass loss in thermogravimetric curves. This decomposition reaction generally possesses a characteristic and relatively low activation energy. When combustion proceeds to its later stage, concurrent fixed carbon combustion (high activation energy) and carbonate decomposition (low activation energy) cause the apparent activation energy to be “pulled down” by the latter, resulting in a declining trend.

For pre-oxidized coal, its kinetic behavior presents a striking contrast to that of raw coal. The most prominent feature is that its activation energy remains consistently and significantly lower than that of raw coal throughout the majority of the conversion range. This systematic reduction in apparent activation energy can be directly traced back to the microstructural alterations detailed in Section “Microstructural functional group changes in raw and pre-oxidized coal”. The incorporation of numerous oxygen-containing functional groups (especially –C=O and –COO–) and the creation of structural defects during pre-oxidation effectively lower the energy barrier for the coal-oxygen reaction. These groups act as preferential sites for oxidation, initiating chain reactions at lower apparent activation energies. This “pre-activation” effect fundamentally enhances the overall combustion reactivity of coal and systematically reduces the average energy barrier required for reaction. At the same time, it was observed that DNH-80 did not show a significant decrease in activation energy between conversion rates of 0.05–0.15, which further proves that pre oxidation removed the originally unstable macromolecular cross-linking structures or specific bridge bonds with high bond energy in lignite. The relatively stable activation energy when α < 0.7 indicates that pre-oxidation treatment homogenizes the coal structure, resulting in similar energy barriers for reactions occurring at different conversion degrees. Only at the final combustion stage (α > 0.7) does the activation energy of pre-oxidized coal decrease rapidly. This also explains the hysteresis of the burnout temperature T5 in Section “The oxidation reaction process of raw coal and pre-oxidized coal”, which is due to the stabilization of the activation energy caused by the homogenization of coal structure, leading to a continuous and stable reaction over a wider temperature range, rather than a rapid end after a violent explosion. Given that combustion of pre-oxidized coal is more intense and occurs earlier, less fixed carbon may remain at high temperatures, making the contribution of carbonate mineral decomposition to the apparent activation energy more dominant and causing a similar declining phenomenon. The results in Fig. 7 indicate that the activation energy of the coal sample significantly decreases after pre oxidation treatment, which explains the increase in mass loss rate and heat release observed in thermogravimetric and differential thermal experiments.

Gas emissions characteristics of raw coal and pre-oxidized coal

Figure 9 shows the 3D Fourier transform infrared spectra of gases released during oxidation and combustion of raw coal and pre oxidized coal samples at different heating rates. Due to the predominance of carbon as the main organic compound in the two coal samples, the CO2 produced during combustion occupies an absolute dominant position in the entire gas product, with sharp absorption peaks in the range of 2345 cm⁻1 to 2360 cm⁻1 as shown in the figure. Further observation reveals that the release of CO2 exhibits distinct stage characteristics with increasing temperature. Before about 300 °C, the absorption peak of CO2 remains at an extremely low level, indicating that the CO2 content produced during water evaporation and pyrolysis is extremely low. However, as the temperature increased to 350 °C, the release of CO2 sharply increased, reaching peak intensity in the range of 400–500 °C, which is highly synchronized with the intense exothermic stage in DSC curves and the combustion stage in TG curves, indicating that CO 2 mainly comes from the intense combustion of volatile matter and fixed carbon.

To more clearly analyze the temperature-dependent trend of CO2 release, the absorption peak intensity curve at 2353.9 cm⁻1 was extracted and analyzed from the 3D spectrum, with results shown in Fig. 10. Its vertical axis represents the instantaneous change in absorbance, which can be used to quantify CO2 release levels. At low temperatures (< 300 °C), only a faint CO2 signal was detected. This corresponds to the moisture evaporation zone in thermogravimetric analysis and the transition phase from initial endothermic to slow exothermic shifts in the differential scanning calorimetry curve. CO2 released during this stage primarily originates from the thermal decomposition of unstable oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., carboxyl groups) within the coal molecular structure. Upon entering the main combustion zone (300–500 °C), the CO2 release rate surges sharply, forming a steep emission peak. Notably, the CO2 release peak for pre-oxidized coal is sharper than that of raw coal, with significantly higher peak absorbance. This phenomenon aligns closely with TG-DTG and DSC analyses: first, it corresponds to the higher maximum mass loss rate (DTGmax in Table 2) observed in pre-oxidized coal, indicating more intense mass loss; second, it directly correlates with the stronger main exothermic peak in the DSC curve (Fig. 2) and the higher total heat release (Fig. 5). The underlying driver for these synchronized enhancements is the pre-activated coal structure, which enables a more concentrated and efficient conversion of carbon to CO2 during the main combustion stage. Collectively, these evidences demonstrate that pre-oxidation promotes more concentrated and intense oxidation reactions during the main combustion stage, significantly enhancing the conversion efficiency of carbon to CO2 per unit time. In the late combustion stage (> 500 °C), the CO2 release rate gradually declines, corresponding to the burnout stage in the thermogravimetric curve and reflecting the slow oxidation process of residual coke.

Consequently, coals that undergo autothermal heating followed by secondary oxidation exhibit a far stronger thermal zone gas effect than raw coal, significantly increasing the environmental damage risk associated with coal spontaneous combustion. The increased terminal methyl groups (–CH3) of aliphatic chains and the activated aromatic edges in the pre-oxidized coal may provide more precursors for the formation of light aromatic hydrocarbons such as benzene and toluene. Conclusions

This study combined thermal analysis, kinetic calculations, and gas-phase infrared monitoring to investigate how pre-oxidation treatment affects coal’s secondary oxidation behavior and gas release patterns. Specific findings are as follows:

FTIR analysis revealed that 80 °C pre-oxidation chemically “pre-activated” the coal matrix, significantly increasing the concentration of reactive oxygen-containing functional groups (e.g., C=O, –COO–) while reducing moisture and reorganizing aliphatic structures. This microstructural modification laid the foundation for all subsequent changes in macroscopic properties.

Pre-oxidation did not alter the fundamental oxidation process of coal but significantly reduced moisture content while increasing fixed carbon and volatile matter content, thereby modifying thermal reaction characteristics. Pre-oxidized coal exhibited higher initial characteristic temperatures (T₁, T2, T3) than raw coal, along with a greater maximum mass loss rate. During combustion, pre-oxidized coal demonstrated stronger exothermic peaks and higher cumulative heat release.
