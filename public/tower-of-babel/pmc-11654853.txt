Catheter‐Based Renal Denervation for Resistant Arterial Hypertension: 10‐Year Real‐World Follow‐Up Data
Authors: Sofie Brouwers, Giulia Botti, Matthias Verbesselt, Lucio Addeo, Marta Belmonte, Kostas Bermpeis, Dario Tino Bertolone, Chiara Valeriano, Michele Mattia Viscusi, Eric Wyffels
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis analysis of real‐world data examines the efficacy, safety, and long‐term outcomes of renal denervation in hypertensive patients for up to 10 years. Sixty‐five consecutive patients underwent renal denervation (RDN) (single operator) for uncontrolled resistant hypertension. Efficacy was defined as the interindividual change of office (OBPM) and ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) at 1, 6, 12 months, 2–4 and 5–10 years after RDN. Medication changes, renal function, and device generation disparities were analyzed. Of these patients, 42 received RDN with a first‐generation device, while 23 underwent the procedure with a second‐generation device. Baseline demographics included a predominantly male cohort (57.6%) with an average age of 60.3 years. The mean number of medications at baseline was 4.3. OBPM at baseline was 169.0/87.4 mmHg, and ABPM at baseline was 153.4/88.4 mmHg. Post‐procedure, significant reductions in systolic blood pressure (SBP) were observed in both OBPM and ABPM at 1 month (OBPM 147.9/82.8 mmHg; ABPM 141.3/83.0 mmHg [SBP, p < 0.001]), sustained up to 10 years (OBPM 153.1/84.3 mmHg; ABPM 138/80.1 mmHg [SBP, p < 0.001]). After 1 year around half of patients had a controlled OBPM and 24 h ABPM < 135/85 mmHg, which was associated with a higher number of ablation spots (31.5±14.8 vs. 15.5 ± 6.5, p = 0.002) and occurred more often when treated with a second‐generation device (2 [12.5%] vs. 7 [77.8%], p = 0.002). Renal function displayed a minor physiological decline over 5–10 years. No major complication occurred. Renal denervation demonstrated sustained significant reductions in systolic OBPM and ABPM up to 10 years post‐procedure. Controlled blood pressure at 1 year was associated with a higher number of mean ablated spots and the use of a second‐generation device. The procedure exhibited a favorable safety profile, indicating its viability in managing hypertension without significant renal function compromise.Keywords: arterial hypertension, device‐based treatment, renal denervation, treatment

Introduction

Hypertension is the most prevalent cardiovascular risk factor, affecting over 1.2 billion people worldwide [1]. There is a continuous relationship between the increase in blood pressure (BP) and the risk of stroke, peripheral artery disease, myocardial infarction, heart failure, and chronic kidney disease [2]. Despite many treatment options, most patients with hypertension do not reach blood pressure targets [1], as defined in current guidelines [3, 4], underlining an important unmet global need. Moreover, it is well known that every reduction in blood pressure is important to reduce major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE). Over the last decade, renal denervation (RDN) has become the third pillar for the treatment of arterial hypertension [5], after lifestyle measures and combination drug therapy. Moreover, supported by several randomized sham‐controlled trials [6, 7, 8, 9], renal denervation was recently upgraded to a class II recommendation in all patients with eGFR > 40 mL/min who have uncontrolled blood pressure despite pharmacological therapy [3, 4]. The second‐generation RDN devices and accumulating anatomical insights (i.e., more distal denervation) led to more complete ablation and more reproducible results [10, 11]. Follow‐up data from the Global SYMPLICITY Registry, the SPYRAL HTN‐ON MED trial, and the RADIANCE‐HTN SOLO trial demonstrated the efficacy of RDN for at least 3 years [6, 9, 12], and lately maintained blood pressure lowering effect of RDN for at least 5 years and even 10 years post RDN in the absence of escalating antihypertensive drug therapy over time was shown [13, 14]. We assessed the long‐term effects of single‐operator RDN up to 10 years postprocedure and correlated the outcomes with the used ablation techniques and the number of ablation points in our center.

Methods

All patients undergoing renal denervation for resistant hypertension at our center from 2011 to 2023 were prospectively included in an internal registry. The ESC Guidelines definition of resistant hypertension was used: persistent hypertension despite the use of three or more antihypertensive drugs, including a diuretic in the maximally tolerated dose [4]. Secondary hypertension was excluded, and all patients underwent ambulatory blood pressure measurement (ABPM) before the procedure. All cases were performed by one single operator. Two cohorts were identified: patients belonging to the first cohort (2011–2014) were treated with the Simplicity Ardian device or the Vessix device, whereas patients from the second cohort (2019–2023) were treated with the Simplicity Spiral device.

All patients were included in a systematic follow‐up program, with regular follow‐up at 1 month, 6 months, 1 year, anytime between 2 and 4 years, and between 5 and 10 years (Figure 1). Office BP was always monitored, and ABPM was performed if allowed by the patients at these timepoints. There was no limitation to the modification of prescribed medical therapy, and alterations were left to the discretion of the treating physician.

All patients provided written informed consent. All study procedures were conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki.

Denervation Procedure

The specifics for each used device have been extensively described elsewhere [15, 16]. All procedures were performed under deep sedation or general anesthesia which was left at the discretion of the anesthesiology team. A baseline renal angiogram was performed to assess the anatomy and distribution of the major vessels. The aortic portion immediately adjacent to the renal arteries was also investigated to visualize the presence of polar arteries or large accessory arteries.

With the first‐generation Simplicity Ardian device, the operator targeted the proximal segment of the renal arteries, from the ostium to the bifurcation, and aimed at delivering a minimum of 4 and a target of 8 ablation spots; the polar arteries or large accessory arteries were not treated. The device has one monopolar tip delivering 6–8 W resulting in deep burns (ablation depth >3–5 mm). The Vessix device generally allowed for a higher mean number of ablation spots (8‐12). Radiofrequency ablation could be repeated while pulling back the balloon from the bifurcation to the ostium. The operator performed additional ablations in the presence of large additional arteries (accessory or polar arteries with minimum diameter 3–4 mm). This device exists of a balloon with eight bipolar electrodes delivering lower energy levels (>1 W) resulting in more superficial energy field (ablation depth < 3 mm). With the new generation Simplicity Spiral device, the operator aimed at performing the highest possible number of ablation spots, in all arteries with a diameter > 3 mm, including arteries beyond the bifurcation, as well as polar and accessory arteries. It exists of four monopolar tips (6–8 W), again resulting in a deep energy field (ablation depth > 3–5 mm).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation and non‐normally distributed variables as median and interquartile range. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Comparison of continuous data was performed using Student t‐test, Mann–Whitney U test, ANOVA, or Kruskal–Wallis, as appropriate. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 defined statistical significance. The analysis was conducted using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA)

Results

A total of 65 patients were included in the present analysis; 58.4% were male and the mean age was 60.3 ± 12.5. The baseline characteristics are described in Table 1. The mean BMI was 28.9 ± 3.9, 29.2% of patients suffered from diabetes mellitus, and 15.4% had atrial fibrillation. The mean creatinine at baseline was 1.1 ± 0.4 mg/dL. The mean number of blood pressure lowering medications used by each patient was 4.3 ± 1.5.

During follow‐up, ABPM systolic pressure, OBPM systolic pressure, and MAP decreased significantly and steadily, as described in Table 3. APBM systolic blood pressure (SBP) dropped already at one month and was significantly lower at all following timepoints (baseline: 153.4 ± 11.3 mmHg; 1 month: 141.3 ± 16.5 mmHg; 6 months: 146.4 ± 17.0 mmHg; 1 year: 135.6 ± 15.2 mmHg; 2–4 years: 136.0 ± 13.7 mmHg; 5–10 years: 138.0 ± 15.1 mmHg; p < 0.001). Similarly, OBPM SBP decreased significantly. Both ABPM and OBPM diastolic blood pressure (DBP) decreased during follow‐up, although not significantly. These results are shown in Figure 2. The highest rate of patients with controlled BP (i.e. <130/80 mmHg at ABPM 24 h mean and <140/90 mmHg at OBPM) was detected in 2–4 year follow‐up interval for the ABPM measurement (n = 5/13, 38.5%), and in the 5–10 year follow‐up interval for the OBPM measurement (n = 15/24, 62.5%). The 2–4‐year follow‐up interval was also the timeframe in which the highest average ABPM SBP drop was detected (Figure 2c), whereas the highest OBPM SBP drop was reported in the 5–10‐year interval. Of note, seven patients died during long‐term follow‐up.

Patients who had controlled BP at 1 year had a significantly higher number of mean ablated spots (31.5 ± 14.8 vs. 15.5 ± 6.5, p = 0.002). Significantly more patients treated with a second‐generation device achieved controlled BP at 1 year than first‐generation patients (2 [12.5%] vs. 7 [77.8%], p = 0.002). No significant differences in blood pressure reduction between the two first‐generation ablation catheters were seen.

The number of medications was comparable across the whole follow‐up interval. The kidney function displayed a natural minor decline over 5–10 years (baseline 1.09 ± 0.43 to 1.30 ± 0.73 mg/dL at 5–10 years)

Discussion

This study provides further insights into the long‐term efficacy and safety of radiofrequency renal denervation in uncontrolled resistant hypertensive patients, extending up to 10 years postprocedure. RDN data on the very long‐term effects are still rather limited and to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study analyzing the efficacy and safety of radiofrequency RDN from a single operator comparing the two device generations. The main findings of our study can be summarized as follows: (1) patients affected by uncontrolled resistant hypertension treated with renal denervation experienced a steady and persistently significant reduction of SBP; (2) a higher number of ablation spots and use of a second‐generation device was associated with improved achievement of controlled blood pressure; and (3) RDN procedure did not compromise renal function.

One of the most important findings is the consistency in blood pressure reduction over time, with significant decreases observed as early as 1‐month postprocedure and maintained up to the 10‐year mark. These results align with previous studies demonstrating the efficacy of RDN in lowering blood pressure beyond the months and years postprocedure 14. The sustained reduction in SBP is significant for OPBM as well as for ABPM. In addition, there were no significant changes in the number of medications, with more than 50% of the patients having a controlled OBPM and 24 h ABPM < 135/85 mmHg from 1 year after the intervention and onwards.

Another important finding is the favorable safety profile of RDN, with no major complications reported throughout the whole follow‐up period, especially in the short term, when no procedure‐related safety issues were encountered. In the mid‐ to long‐term no complications were seen, especially regarding renal function compromise. A minor natural reduction in kidney function was observed, with an average of 0.2 mL/min/1.73 m2 over 5–10 years. The average decline of renal function in hypertension varies widely depending on comorbidities and blood pressure control as well as baseline estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) with an average decline of 0.5 to 2.7 mL/min/1.73 m2 annually [17, 18, 19].

The study also addresses the impact of device generation and ablation techniques on treatment outcomes. Achieving a controlled BP at 1 year after RDN was associated with a higher number of mean ablated spots and was obtained more frequently by patients treated with a second‐generation device. Moreover, all ablations were performed by a single operator, therefore excluding any inter‐operator variability. Thus, our data underscore the importance of the expertise of the operator and focus on the number of ablation spots which was significantly improved with the use of second‐generation device, as highlighted in the most recent clinical consensus paper [5].

Our study carries some limitations that must be acknowledged. First, the sample size is relatively small, however, data available on long‐term safety and efficacy of RDN is scarce. Second, follow‐up data is not complete for all patients, both because of patient unavailability and because some patients have not yet reached all time points of follow‐up (i.e., second generation). Additionally, minor in‐hospital adjustments to antihypertensive medications were made to optimize blood pressure control and align treatment regimens with our institutional practices. Notably, increases in medications were balanced by reductions, minimizing the overall impact. While these changes may have slightly affected early postprocedural blood pressure readings (as shown in Table 2), they are unlikely to influence the long‐term effects of renal denervation. Finally, the study was not designed in a randomized controlled fashion and a control cohort was not available.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study adds to the growing body of evidence supporting the long‐term efficacy and safety of renal denervation in resistant hypertensive patients. The sustained reduction in office as well as ambulatory blood pressure observed up to 10 years postprocedure, along with the favorable safety profile, highlights the potential of RDN as a valuable therapeutic option for managing resistant hypertension. Moreover, the number of ablated spots and the use of a second‐generation device are important factors for achieving controlled blood pressure after RDN.
