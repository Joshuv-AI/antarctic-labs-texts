Ribosomal RNA operon copy number: A trait‐informed framework to close the microbial cultivation gap
Authors: Yuxiao Huang, Qingliu Wu, Chang Liu, Pugang Yu, Hao Li, Shengyue Tang, Fusuo Zhang, Shuangjiang Liu, Bin Ni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The vast majority of microbes remain uncultured, constraining functional validation and limiting microbiome‐based applications. We show that ribosomal RNA operon (rrn) copy number—a stable, phylogenetically conserved genomic trait—is strongly correlated with cultivation outcomes across ecosystems. Comparative analyses of rhizosphere, human gut, and mouse gut microbiomes reveal that rhizosphere communities contain a higher proportion of high‐rrn taxa, which are preferentially recovered under nutrient‐rich, short‐term cultivation, whereas low‐rrn taxa are systematically underrepresented. By linking rrn distributions to cultivation success, we propose trait‐informed strategies for media design, incubation regimes, co‐culture approaches, and preservation methods. This framework provides a predictive, generalizable path to bridge sequencing and cultivation, facilitating biodiversity conservation, sustainable agriculture, and microbiome‐based therapeutics.

For over a century, microbiologists have faced the “great plate count anomaly”: microscopic counts of microbes far exceed colony‐forming units [1, 2]. Despite advances in sequencing, single‐cell genomics, and high‐throughput culturomics, most taxa remain uncultured, limiting functional validation, biobank development, and applied innovations in agriculture, medicine, and biotechnology [3, 4, 5]. Conventional methods—favoring copiotrophic generalists in nutrient‐rich, short‐term incubations—consistently miss slow‐growing oligotrophs, which often play key roles in ecosystem function [6, 7, 8]. To bridge this gap, we propose moving from empirical trial‐and‐error to a predictive, trait‐informed approach.

In macroecology, functional traits—quantitative measures of form, physiology, or behavior—help explain and are correlated with ecological patterns. In microbial ecology, the ribosomal RNA operon (rrn) copy number is a particularly informative genomic trait [9, 10, 11]. It reflects an organism's capacity for ribosome synthesis, influencing growth rate and nutrient response. Importantly, rrn copy number is stable within lineages, phylogenetically structured, and estimable from community sequencing data. We argue that rrn copy number can bridge genomic profiling and cultivation strategy design, enabling targeted approaches to recover underrepresented taxa.

Ribosome production capacity sets an upper limit on protein synthesis and maximum growth rate. Taxa with high rrn copy numbers can rapidly assemble ribosomes and exploit transient nutrient pulses, a hallmark of copiotrophic life strategies [9, 10, 11]. In contrast, low‐rrn taxa grow slowly and allocate resources toward efficiency, stress tolerance, or specialized metabolism typical of oligotrophy. These divergent strategies create predictable cultivation biases: high‐rrn taxa are readily recovered under nutrient‐rich, short‐term incubations, whereas low‐rrn taxa often require longer incubation, lower nutrient availability, or co‐culture approaches. By profiling rrn distributions in a target microbiome, researchers can anticipate these biases and tailor media composition, incubation regimes, and preservation methods accordingly. In this study, we extend rrn‐based trait insights from individual species to community‐level cultivation outcomes, and we propose a trait‐informed framework that links genomic profiling directly to actionable strategies for recovering uncultured microbial diversity.

COMMUNITY COMPOSITION AND TRAIT DISTRIBUTIONS IN THE RHIZOSPHERE

We profiled soybean (Glycine max) and maize (Zea mays) rhizosphere bacterial communities using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing (Figures 1A,B, 2A), classifying ASVs into six rarity groups: always abundant taxa (AAT), conditionally rare or abundant taxa (CRAT), conditionally abundant taxa (CAT), moderate taxa (MT), conditionally rare taxa (CRT), and always rare taxa (ART) [12]. Dominant phyla included Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria, with crop‐specific differences: soybean favored Proteobacteria and Firmicutes, whereas maize showed higher Actinobacteria (Figure S1A,B,E,F).

High‐rrn copy numbers clustered in abundant groups (AAT, CRAT, CAT), while rare groups (ART, CRT) carried low rrn values (Figure 2B,C). This separation aligns with ecological strategies: abundant groups consisted of fast‐growing copiotrophs, whereas rare groups included slow‐growing specialists, many from Acidobacteria and Verrucomicrobia.

CULTIVATION PATTERNS AND THRESHOLD EFFECTS

High‐throughput cultivation in 10% TSB medium revealed a strong positive correlation between rrn copy number and bacterial cultivability (R² = 0.97 in soybean and R² = 0.99 in maize), corresponding to the proportion of ASVs successfully isolated from the rhizosphere of both crops (Figure S1D,H and Tables S1, S2). Beyond this threshold, additional rrn copies provided diminishing returns, likely reflecting physiological constraints unrelated to ribosome number [13]. At the phylum level, outcomes reflected this trend: Proteobacteria (generally high‐rrn) exhibited high cultivability; Firmicutes (very high rrn) dominated AAT groups; Actinobacteria (intermediate rrn) showed genus‐dependent variation (Figure 1B, Figure S1C,D,G,H).

PRESERVATION BIASES

Fresh samples captured the broadest diversity. Cold storage without cryoprotectant reduced Proteobacteria and enriched Actinobacteria, while glycerol partially restored Proteobacteria recovery. Low‐rrn taxa declined under all storage regimes, highlighting compounding effects of cultivation and preservation biases (Figure S2).

CROSS‐ECOSYSTEM COMPARISON: RHIZOSPHERE Versus GUT MICROBIOMES

We compared rhizosphere communities with human and mouse gut microbiomes as ecological benchmarks to evaluate rrn‐based trait distributions across ecosystems [14, 15, 16]. Clear ecosystem‐specific patterns emerged: rhizosphere communities exhibited higher mean rrn values (~6–8), with many taxa carrying more than nine copies, whereas gut communities had lower means (~3–5) and were dominated by oligotrophic lineages (Figure 2A–E).

These trait profiles were correlated with cultivation outcomes. Gut communities, enriched in low‐rrn taxa, displayed substantially lower cultivability under standard nutrient‐rich, aerobic conditions. Mouse gut communities, although exhibiting slightly higher rrn copy numbers than the human gut, remained considerably less cultivable than their rhizosphere counterparts (Figure 2F–K), particularly among abundant taxa (Figure S3). These differences reflect habitat‐specific selective pressures: rhizospheres are dynamic, nutrient‐pulsed, and structurally heterogeneous, favoring opportunistic, high‐rrn strategists; gut environments are chemically complex, host‐regulated, and relatively stable, selecting for efficient, syntrophic specialists.

CULTIVATION BIAS AND TRAIT‐BASED CORRECTIONS

Across all tested ecosystems, rrn copy number was positively correlated with cultivability, with cultivated taxa consistently exhibiting higher rrn copy numbers than their uncultured counterparts (Figure 2L,M). This predictable bias enriches fast‐growing copiotrophs while excluding low‐rrn oligotrophs (Figure 2N,O and Table S3). The omission of low‐rrn taxa is ecologically significant: slow‐growing genera such as Variovorax, Ensifer, and Paenibacillus contribute to nitrogen cycling, modulate phytohormones, and suppress plant disease [17, 18]. We propose rrn‐informed corrections to cultivation protocols: 1. Media design: Use oligotrophic or root exudate–based media to support low‐rrn taxa. Employ chemically defined media to reduce the dominance of fast growers. 2. Incubation regimes: Extend incubation periods for slow growers. Apply fluctuating nutrient conditions to mimic natural environments. Adjust temperature and oxygen levels to match ecological niches. 3. Co‐culture and in situ cultivation: Use diffusion chambers or iChips to preserve natural microbial interactions [19, 20]. Pair target taxa with helper strains for cross‐feeding or detoxification. 4. Preservation methods: Apply cryoprotectants and slow cooling to minimize selective losses. Minimize freeze–thaw cycles for sensitive, low‐rrn taxa. This trait‐based framework links rrn profiling to actionable cultivation strategies, providing a systematic approach to recover both abundant and elusive microbes.

Most microbial taxa remain uncultured—a challenge known as the “great plate count anomaly”—which constrains functional validation, microbial applications, and biodiversity conservation. Conventional cultivation methods disproportionately enrich fast‐growing, nutrient‐responsive taxa, leaving slow‐growing specialists underrepresented. Ribosomal RNA operon (rrn) copy number, however, provides a functional trait to anticipate these biases and guide corrective strategies. We propose rrn‐informed approaches to media formulation, incubation, and preservation that improve recovery of both abundant and elusive microbes. Integrating rrn profiling into isolation workflows enables researchers to systematically adjust protocols and capture underrepresented diversity. Although rrn is a strong predictor under the tested conditions, it should be considered alongside other genomic features—including genome size, GC content, and metabolic potential—for more comprehensive guidance. Future studies should probe causality by manipulating rrn copy number in model strains and by assessing how environmental and host contexts shape rrn–cultivability relationships. By linking trait‐based theory to practical cultivation, this framework narrows the gap between sequencing and experimentation, advancing efforts in sustainable agriculture, next‐generation probiotics, and microbial resource conservation.
