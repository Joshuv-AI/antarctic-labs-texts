The pseudometric topology induced by upper asymptotic density
Jonathan M. Keith

Abstract
. Upper asymptotic density induces a pseudometric on the power set of the natural numbers, with respect to which 𝒫 ⁡ ( ℕ ) {\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}}) is complete. The collection 𝒟 {\mathcal{D}} of sets with asymptotic density is closed in this pseudometric, and closed subsets of 𝒟 {\mathcal{D}} are characterised by a generalisation of an additivity property (AP0).



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 11B05; Secondary 54E50

1.
Introduction

The
asymptotic density
of a set
A\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}
is defined to be

\nu(A):=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\lvert A\cap n\rvert}{n},

if that limit exists. (The
n
in the numerator represents the set
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
, a common shorthand in the literature on asymptotic density, as for example in
[
1
]
.) The collection of sets with asymptotic density is denoted
{\mathcal{D}}
. If the limit in the above definition is replaced by the limit inferior or limit superior, one obtains the
lower asymptotic density

\nu^{-}(A)
and
upper asymptotic density

\nu^{+}(A)
, respectively. Both
\nu^{-}
and
\nu^{+}
extend
\nu
to
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
. These set functions are important in several mathematical disciplines, including number theory (see, for example, their role in numerous theorems and proofs in
[
17
]
). Key papers exploring the properties of asymptotic density (and its extensions) include
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
,
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
20
]
.

Asymptotic density is additive in the sense that for disjoint
A,B\in{\mathcal{D}}
, the set
A\cup B
is in
{\mathcal{D}}
with
\nu(A\cup B)=\nu(A)+\nu(B)
. Lower asymptotic density is superadditive (
\nu^{-}(A\cup B)\geq\nu^{-}(A)+\nu^{-}(B)
), whereas upper asymptotic density is subadditive (
\nu^{+}(A\cup B)\leq\nu^{+}(A)+\nu^{+}(B)
). Much of the literature cited above is concerned with additive extensions of
\nu
to
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
. A highlight of this literature is the identification of necessary and sufficient conditions for the
L^{p}
spaces induced by such an additive extension to be complete
[
1
,
11
]
.

This paper investigates the pseudometric topology induced on
{\mathcal{D}}
by the upper asymptotic density, via the pseudometric

d(A,B):=\nu^{+}(A\triangle B),

defined for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
. Note that one does not obtain a pseudometric on
{\mathcal{D}}
if
\nu^{+}
is replaced by
\nu
in this definition, because
A\triangle B
is not necessarily an element of
{\mathcal{D}}
for arbitrary
A,B\in{\mathcal{D}}
. A pseudometric is obtained if
\nu^{+}
is replaced by an additive extension
\overline{\nu}
of
\nu
to
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, and it is possible (assuming the ultrafilter principle) to choose
\overline{\nu}
so that
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is complete (this follows from Theorem 1 of
[
1
]
and Theorem 1 of
[
7
]
). However, it is not known whether
\overline{\nu}
can be chosen so that
{\mathcal{D}}
is also closed in the induced topology.
With
d
defined as above, the following theorem is proved in Section
2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

({\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}}),d)
is complete and
{\mathcal{D}}
is closed.

Moreover, the closed subsets of
{\mathcal{D}}
can be characterised in terms of a property that generalises AP0, as defined by Mekler
[
16
]
and others. In the context of asymptotic density, AP0 can be stated as follows: for every increasing sequence
(A_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathcal{D}}
, there is
A\in{\mathcal{D}}
such that
\nu(A_{i}\setminus A)=0
for all
i
, and
\nu(A)=\lim_{i\rightarrow\infty}\nu(A_{i})
. An additive extension
\overline{\nu}
of
\nu
to
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
has AP0 if the above statement holds with
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
in place of
{\mathcal{D}}
and
\overline{\nu}
in place of
\nu
. One reason AP0 is an important property is that it characterises those additive extensions of
\nu
for which
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
is complete with respect to the pseudometric
d_{\overline{\nu}}(A,B):=\overline{\nu}(A\triangle B)
(see Theorem 1 of
[
7
]
).

The generalisation of AP0 can be expressed in terms of quotients. Let
[A]
be the equivalence class of
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
, that is

[A]:=\{B\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}}):d(A,B)=0\}.

Define

{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/d:=\{[A]:A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})\}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad{\mathcal{D}}/d:=\{[A]:A\in{\mathcal{D}}\}.

Then
d([A],[B]):=d(A,B)
defines a metric on
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/d
or
{\mathcal{D}}/d
. (In fact
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/d
is also a Boolean quotient, since the null sets of
\nu^{+}
form an ideal in
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
.) The following theorem is proved in Section
3
.

Theorem 1.2
.

A set
{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq{\mathcal{D}}/d
is closed if and only if the following condition holds: for every sequence
(A_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathcal{E}}
such that the nets

\{\nu^{+}(\vee_{k=i}^{j}A_{k}):i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\{\nu^{-}(\vee_{k=i}^{j}A_{k}):i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}

converge to a common limit
L
, the net
\{\vee_{k=i}^{j}A_{k}:i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}
converges to some
A\in{\mathcal{E}}
with
\nu^{+}(A)=L
.

Here the directed set
\{(i,j):i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}
has the product order:

(i,j)\leq(k,l)\iff i\leq k\mbox{ and }j\leq l.

The operator
\vee
represents the least upper bound in the partial order on
{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})/d
given by

[A]\leq[B]\iff\nu^{+}(A\setminus B)=0.

The functions
\nu^{+}
and
\nu^{-}
are given by
\nu^{+}([A]):=\nu^{+}(A)
and
\nu^{-}([A]):=\nu^{-}(A)
respectively. These functions can be shown to be well defined. The property described in Theorem
1.2
is equivalent to AP0 in the case
{\mathcal{E}}={\mathcal{D}}/d
and
(A_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is increasing.

The closed subsets of
{\mathcal{D}}
are then the inverse images under the quotient map
A\mapsto[A]
of closed subsets of
{\mathcal{D}}/d
.

2.
Completeness of
({\mathcal{D}},d)

Theorem
1.1
can be proved by a diagonalisation argument. Let
(B_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
be a Cauchy sequence in
({\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}}),d)
, and define
\epsilon_{k}:=\nu^{+}(B_{k}\triangle B_{k+1})
for each
k
, so that
\epsilon_{k}\rightarrow 0
. Without real loss of generality, it may be supposed
\epsilon_{k}>0
for all
k
, since either
(B_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
has a subsequence with this property, or if no such subsequence exists, the sequence trivially converges. Define a putative limit for
(B_{k})_{k=1}^{\infty}
, of the form:

B:=\cup_{k=1}^{\infty}\left(B_{k}\cap(n_{k-1},n_{k}]\right),

where
n_{0}=-1
and
n_{k}>n_{k-1}
for each
k
. Let
n_{1}
be the smallest positive integer such that
\nu_{n}(B_{1}\triangle B_{2})<\nu^{+}(B_{1}\triangle B_{2})+\epsilon_{1}
for all
n\geq n_{1}
, where

\nu_{n}(A):=\frac{\lvert A\cap n\rvert}{n}

for all
A\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}})
and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Iteratively define
n_{k}
(for
k\geq 2
) to be the smallest positive integer such that:

(1)

\nu_{n}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})<\nu^{+}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})+\epsilon_{k}
for all
n\geq n_{k}
and
j\leq k
, and

(2)

\frac{n_{k-1}}{n_{k}}<\epsilon_{k}
.

Observe that

(2.1)

|\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B)-\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k})|<\epsilon_{k}

for
1\leq j\leq k
. This is trivially true in the case
j=k=1
, since
\nu_{n_{1}}(B_{1}\triangle B)=\nu_{n_{1}}(B_{1}\triangle B_{1})=0
. In the case
1<j=k
, note

\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{k}\triangle B)=\frac{1}{n_{k}}n_{k-1}\nu_{n_{k-1}}(B_{k}\triangle B)<\epsilon_{k}

and
\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{k}\triangle B_{k})=0
. In the case
j<k
, first observe that

\displaystyle\nu_{n}(B_{j}\triangle B)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\left(n_{k}\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B)+n\nu_{n}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})-n_{k}\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})\right)

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\frac{n_{k}}{n}\left(\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B)-\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})\right)+\nu_{n}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1}),

for
1\leq j\leq k
and
n_{k}\leq n\leq n_{k+1}
. Now put
n=n_{k+1}
in (2.2) and note
|\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B)-\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})|\leq 1
and
n_{k}/n_{k+1}<\epsilon_{k+1}
, thus obtaining

|\nu_{n_{k+1}}(B_{j}\triangle B)-\nu_{n_{k+1}}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})|<\epsilon_{k+1}

for
1\leq j\leq k
.

Another consequence of (2.2) is that
\nu_{n}(B_{j}\triangle B)\leq\nu^{+}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})+4\epsilon_{k}
. To see this in the case
\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B)-\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})\leq 0
, observe that (2.2) gives

\nu_{n}(B_{j}\triangle B)\leq\nu_{n}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})<\nu^{+}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})+\epsilon_{k}

using the first criterion for selecting
n_{k}
. If
\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B)-\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})>0
, then (2.2) gives

\displaystyle\nu_{n}(B_{j}\triangle B)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B)-\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})\right)+\nu_{n}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\left(\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B)-\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k})\right)+\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{k}\triangle B_{k+1})+\nu_{n}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})

\displaystyle<\epsilon_{k}+\left(\nu^{+}(B_{k}\triangle B_{k+1})+\epsilon_{k}\right)+\left(\nu^{+}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})+\epsilon_{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\nu^{+}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})+4\epsilon_{k},

where the second line uses the triangle inequality
\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k})\leq\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})+\nu_{n_{k}}(B_{k}\triangle B_{k+1})
and the third line uses (2.1) and the first criterion for selecting
n_{k}
. It follows that

\sup_{n_{k}\leq n\leq n_{k+1}}\nu_{n}(B_{j}\triangle B)\leq\nu^{+}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})+4\epsilon_{k}.

Taking the limit superior as
k\rightarrow\infty
yields

\nu^{+}(B_{j}\triangle B)\leq\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\nu^{+}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})

and thus

\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}\nu^{+}(B_{j}\triangle B)\leq\limsup_{j\rightarrow\infty}\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\nu^{+}(B_{j}\triangle B_{k+1})=0.

Hence
d(B_{j},B)\rightarrow 0
as
j\rightarrow\infty
, and
({\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{N}}),d)
is complete.

Since
\nu^{+}
and
\nu^{-}
are continuous, their coincidence set is closed (see Proposition
3.5
below and Proposition 6.9 of
[
9
]
). That is,
{\mathcal{D}}
is closed.

3.
Closed subsets of
{\mathcal{D}}

A more general result than Theorem
1.2
is proved in this section. Some new terminology and notation will facilitate the exposition. In what follows,
I_{A}
represents the indicator function of a set
A
. A
field
is a family of sets containing the empty set
\emptyset
and closed under complements and finite unions.

Definition 3.1
.

Consider a set
X
, a field
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(X)
, and set functions

\mu^{+}:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow[0,~1]\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\mu^{-}~:~{\mathcal{F}}~\rightarrow~[0,~1]

with
\mu^{+}(\emptyset)=\mu^{-}(\emptyset)=0
and
\mu^{+}(X)=\mu^{-}(X)=1
.

(1)

\mu^{+}
is
subadditive
if

I_{A}\leq I_{B}+I_{C}\implies\mu^{+}(A)\leq\mu^{+}(B)+\mu^{+}(C)

for
A,B,C\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

(2)

\mu^{-}
is
superadditive
if

I_{A}\geq I_{B}+I_{C}\implies\mu^{-}(A)\geq\mu^{-}(B)+\mu^{-}(C)

for
A,B,C\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

(3)

\mu^{-}
is
co-subadditive with respect to
\mu^{+}
if

I_{A}\leq I_{B}+I_{C}\implies\mu^{-}(A)\leq\mu^{-}(B)+\mu^{+}(C)

for
A,B,C\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

(4)

\mu^{+}
is
co-superadditive with respect to
\mu^{-}
if

I_{A}\geq I_{B}+I_{C}\implies\mu^{+}(A)\geq\mu^{+}(B)+\mu^{-}(C)

for
A,B,C\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

(5)

The pair
(\mu^{+},\mu^{-})
is
conjugate
if all four of the above properties hold.

By these definitions, subadditive and superadditive set functions are necessarily monotone, that is,

A\subseteq B\implies\mu^{+}(A)\leq\mu^{+}(B)

for
A,B\in{\mathcal{F}}
, and similarly for
\mu^{-}
.

It will also be useful to say that a set function
\mu^{+}:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow[0,1]
is
countably subadditive
if

I_{A}\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}I_{A_{k}}\implies\mu^{+}(A)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu^{+}(A_{k})

for any
A,A_{1},A_{2},\ldots\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

Using the properties of the
\limsup
and
\liminf
operators, it is straightforward to verify that lower and upper asymptotic density form a conjugate pair that agree on
{\mathcal{D}}
(and only on
{\mathcal{D}}
, where they also agree with
\nu
).

Definition 3.2
.

Consider a set
X
, a field
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(X)
, and a subadditive function
\mu:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow[0,1]
. Define

d_{\mu}(A,B):=\mu(A\triangle B)

for all
A,B\in{\mathcal{F}}
.

It is straightforward to show that
d_{\mu}
satisfies the axioms of a pseudometric. In particular, the triangle inequality follows from the subadditivity of
\mu
.

The key new theorem in this section can now be stated.

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider a set
X
, a field
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(X)
, an arbitrary set
{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq{\mathcal{F}}
, and conjugate set functions
(\mu^{+},\mu^{-})
defined on
{\mathcal{F}}
and agreeing on
{\mathcal{E}}
. The pseudometric space
({\mathcal{E}},d_{\mu^{+}})
is complete if and only if the following condition holds: for every sequence
(A_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathcal{E}}
such that the nets

\{\mu^{+}(\cup_{k=i}^{j}A_{k}):i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}\mbox{ and }\{\mu^{-}(\cup_{k=i}^{j}A_{k}):i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}

converge to a common limit
L
, the net
\{\cup_{k=i}^{j}A_{k}:i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}

converges to some
A\in{\mathcal{E}}
with
\mu^{+}(A)=L
.

Here and in what follows, the notation
d_{\mu^{+}}
is used to represent both a pseudometric on
{\mathcal{F}}
and its restriction to
{\mathcal{E}}
.

Theorem
1.2
follows immediately from Theorem
3.3
, since a subspace of
{\mathcal{D}}
is complete if and only if its image under the quotient map is complete. Theorem
1.2
is framed in terms of the quotient space
{\mathcal{D}}/d
because only in a Hausdorff space is a complete subspace necessarily closed.

The proof of Theorem
3.3
requires two propositions.

Proposition 3.4
.

Consider a set
X
, a
\sigma
-field
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(X)
, and a countably subadditive set function
\mu:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow[0,1]
. The pseudometric space
({\mathcal{F}},d_{\mu})
is complete.

Proof.

Consider a Cauchy sequence
(A_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathcal{F}}
. Without loss of generality, it may be assumed
\mu(A_{i}\triangle A_{j})<2^{-i}
for
i\leq j
, since one can always choose a subsequence with this property, and a Cauchy sequence has the same limits as any convergent subsequence. Define
A:=\cup_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\cap_{j=i}^{\infty}A_{j}\in{\mathcal{F}}
. Then

\mu(A_{i}\triangle A)\leq\sum_{j=i}^{\infty}\mu(A_{j}\triangle A_{j+1})<2^{1-i},

for each
i
, implying
A_{i}\rightarrow A
. Hence
({\mathcal{F}},d_{\mu})
is complete.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

Consider a set
X
, a field
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(X)
, a subadditive set function
\mu^{+}:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow[0,1]
, and a set function
\mu^{-}:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow[0,1]
that is co-subadditive with respect to
\mu^{+}
. Then
\mu^{+}
and
\mu^{-}
are uniformly continuous with respect to
d_{\mu^{+}}
.

Proof.

It follows from subadditivity of
\mu^{+}
that

(3.1)

\lvert\mu^{+}(A)-\mu^{+}(B)\rvert\leq\mu^{+}(A\triangle B)

and from cosubadditivity of
\mu^{-}
that

(3.2)

\lvert\mu^{-}(A)-\mu^{-}(B)\rvert\leq\mu^{+}(A\triangle B)

for any
A,B\in{\mathcal{F}}
. This implies uniform continuity of
\mu^{+}
and
\mu^{-}
.
∎

Theorem
3.3
can now be proved. First suppose
({\mathcal{E}},d_{\mu^{+}})
is complete, and consider
(A_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathcal{E}}
such that

\{\mu^{+}(B_{ij}):i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}\mbox{ and }\{\mu^{-}(B_{ij}):i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}

converge to a common limit
L
, where
B_{ij}:=\cup_{k=i}^{j}A_{k}
for each
i
and
j
. Given
\epsilon>0
, there is
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
such that
\lvert\mu^{+}(B_{ij})-L\rvert<\epsilon
and
\lvert\mu^{-}(B_{ij})-L\rvert<\epsilon
for all
i,j\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
N\leq i\leq j
. Consider
i,j,k,l\in{\mathbb{N}}
with
N\leq i\leq j
and
N\leq k\leq l
. Define
m:=\min\{i,k\}
and
M:=\max\{j,l\}
, and note
B_{ij}\subseteq B_{mM}
and
B_{kl}\subseteq B_{mM}
. Then

\displaystyle\mu^{+}(B_{ij}\triangle B_{kl})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mu^{+}(B_{mM}\triangle B_{ij})+\mu^{+}(B_{mM}\triangle B_{kl})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu^{+}(B_{mM}\setminus B_{ij})+\mu^{+}(B_{mM}\setminus B_{kl})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mu^{+}(B_{mM})-\mu^{-}(B_{ij})+\mu^{+}(B_{mM})-\mu^{-}(B_{kl})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\lvert\mu^{+}(B_{mM})-L\rvert+\lvert\mu^{-}(B_{ij})-L\rvert+\lvert\mu^{-}(B_{kl})-L\rvert

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 4\epsilon,

where the third line uses the co-superadditivity of
\mu^{+}
. Hence
\{B_{ij}:i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}
is a Cauchy net containing the Cauchy sequence
(A_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, and both sequence and net converge to some
A\in{\mathcal{E}}
. Moreover,

\lvert\mu^{+}(A)-L\rvert\leq\lvert\mu^{+}(A_{i})-\mu^{+}(A)\rvert+\lvert\mu^{+}(A_{i})-L\rvert

for any
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
. But
i
can be chosen so that the summands on the right-hand side of the preceding inequality are arbitrarily small, since
\lvert\mu^{+}(A_{i})-\mu^{+}(A)\rvert\leq\mu^{+}(A_{i}\triangle A)
. Hence
\mu^{+}(A)=L
.

For the converse, let
S
be the Stone space of the Boolean algebra
{\mathcal{F}}
and let
\phi:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow{\mathcal{C}}
be the canonical Boolean isomorphism mapping
{\mathcal{F}}
onto the field
{\mathcal{C}}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}(S)
comprised of the clopen (ie. simultaneously open and closed) subsets of
S
. (It is not necessary to be familiar with Stone spaces in order to understand what follows: it is sufficient to know that such an isomorphism exists for some compact topological space
S
.) The proof involves first defining a countably subadditive set function
\hat{\mu}^{+}:{\mathcal{P}}(S)\rightarrow[0,1]
and a set function
\hat{\mu}^{-}:\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}\rightarrow[0,1]
, where
\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}
is the closure of
{\mathcal{C}}
with respect to
d_{\hat{\mu}^{+}}
. The essential properties of these set functions are:

(1)

\mu^{+}=\hat{\mu}^{+}\circ\phi
and
\mu^{-}=\hat{\mu}^{-}\circ\phi
;

(2)

\hat{\mu}^{+}
agrees with
\hat{\mu}^{-}
on
\overline{\phi({\mathcal{E}})}
(where
\phi({\mathcal{E}})
is the image of
{\mathcal{E}}
under
\phi
); and

(3)

(\overline{\phi({\mathcal{E}})},d_{\hat{\mu}^{+}})
is complete.

For each
B\in{\mathcal{P}}(S)
, define

\hat{\mu}^{+}(B):=\inf\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu^{+}(A_{i}),

where the infimum is over sequences
(A_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathcal{F}}
such that
B\subseteq\cup_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\phi(A_{i})
. (This infimum always exists because
B\subseteq\phi(X)=S
.) For
A\in{\mathcal{F}}
, it is immediate from the definition that
\hat{\mu}^{+}(\phi(A))\leq\mu^{+}(A)
. Moreover, any sequence
(A_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
in
{\mathcal{F}}
with
\phi(A)\subseteq\cup_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\phi(A_{i})
contains a finite subsequence
(A_{i_{k}})_{k=1}^{n}
with
\phi(A)\subseteq\cup_{k=1}^{n}\phi(A_{i_{k}})
, since
S
is compact and elements of
{\mathcal{C}}
are both open and closed. Then
A\subseteq\cup_{k=1}^{n}A_{i_{k}}
and

\mu^{+}(A)\leq\sum_{k=1}^{n}\mu^{+}(A_{i_{k}})\leq\sum_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}\mu^{+}(A_{i}).

Hence,
\mu^{+}(A)\leq\hat{\mu}^{+}(\phi(A))
and
\mu^{+}=\hat{\mu}^{+}\circ\phi
.

Note
d_{\hat{\mu}^{+}}(A,B)=d_{\mu^{+}}(\phi^{-1}(A),\phi^{-1}(B))
for
A,B\in{\mathcal{C}}
. It follows that
\mu^{-}\circ\phi^{-1}
is a uniformly continuous map from
{\mathcal{C}}
to
[0,1]
, using Proposition
3.5
. Thus
\mu^{-}\circ\phi^{-1}
has a uniformly continuous extension
\hat{\mu}^{-}:\overline{{\mathcal{C}}}\rightarrow[0,1]
(see Proposition 12.13 of
[
9
]
). Then
\mu^{-}=\hat{\mu}^{-}\circ\phi
. Moreover,
\hat{\mu}^{+}(B)=\hat{\mu}^{-}(B)
for any
B\in\overline{\phi({\mathcal{E}})}
, since this is true for any
B\in\phi({\mathcal{E}})
, and the coincidence set of
\hat{\mu}^{+}
and
\hat{\mu}^{-}
must be closed (see Proposition 6.9 of
[
9
]
).

Also note
\hat{\mu}^{+}
is countably sub-additive, by an argument used in the proof of Lemma III.5.5 of
[
6
]
. Thus
({\mathcal{P}}(S),d_{\hat{\mu}^{+}})
is complete by Proposition
3.4
, hence
(\overline{\phi({\mathcal{E}})},d_{\hat{\mu}^{+}})
is also complete.

Now consider a Cauchy sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}\subseteq{\mathcal{E}}
. The sequence
(\phi(A_{n}))_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is Cauchy in
\phi({\mathcal{E}})
, and thus converges to some
B\in\overline{\phi({\mathcal{E}})}
. Choose a subsequence
(\phi(A_{n_{i}}))_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
such that
\hat{\mu}^{+}(\phi(A_{n_{i}})\triangle B)<2^{-i}
for all
i
. Then for any
i\leq j
,

\hat{\mu}^{+}((\cup_{k=i}^{j}\phi(A_{n_{k}}))\triangle B)\leq\sum_{k=i}^{j}\hat{\mu}^{+}(\phi(A_{n_{k}})\triangle B)<2^{1-i}.

Using the inequalities (3.1) and (3.2), it follows that the nets

\{\hat{\mu}^{+}(\cup_{k=i}^{j}\phi(A_{n_{k}})):i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}\mbox{ and }\{\hat{\mu}^{-}(\phi(\cup_{k=i}^{j}A_{n_{k}})):i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}

converge to a common limit
L:=\hat{\mu}^{+}(B)=\hat{\mu}^{-}(B)
. Since
\mu^{+}=\hat{\mu}^{+}\circ\phi
and
\mu^{-}=\hat{\mu}^{-}\circ\phi
, these nets are precisely

\{\mu^{+}(\cup_{k=i}^{j}A_{n_{k}}):i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}\mbox{ and }\{\mu^{-}(\cup_{k=i}^{j}A_{n_{k}}):i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}.

By assumption, the net

\{\cup_{k=i}^{j}A_{n_{k}}:i,j\in{\mathbb{N}},i\leq j\}

converges to some
A\in{\mathcal{E}}
, and in particular the sequence
(A_{n_{i}})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges to
A
. The original Cauchy sequence
(A_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
must therefore converge to
A
, implying
({\mathcal{E}},d_{\mu^{+}})
is complete. This completes the proof.

Note that one can replace the unions with intersections in Theorem
3.3
, and the result remains true, with a similar proof. Thus Theorem
1.2
also remains true if the least upper bounds are replaced by greatest lower bounds.

4.
Applications

A reviewer of this paper invited speculation regarding potential applications of the above results “in number theory or other parts of mathematics”.
The author is currently investigating properties of the monotone integral induced by upper asymptotic density, defined in the manner of Greco
[
8
]
, and in particular the relationship between that integral and the
Cesàro mean
, defined as

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{a_{1}+\cdots+a_{n}}{n}

for certain sequences of real numbers
(a_{i})_{i\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. The author hopes the above results will facilitate that investigation, and subsequently lead to insights in the theory of ergodic processes, where Cesàro means play an important role.

Number theorists are interested in whether certain classes of natural numbers have asymptotic density. For example, the class of
abundant numbers
, consisting of natural numbers
n
for which the sum of divisors is at least
2n
, is known to have asymptotic density (see Theorem 7.16 of
[
17
]
), and moreover that asymptotic density has been estimated
[
5
]
. Conceivably, Theorem
1.1
might facilitate proof that a given class of natural numbers has asymptotic density, by first expressing it as the limit of a sequence of classes with asymptotic density. Theorem
1.2
may provide a method of estimating the asymptotic density of such a class, as the limit of a net of the form described in that theorem.
