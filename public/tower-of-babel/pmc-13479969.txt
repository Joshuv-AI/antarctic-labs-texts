Dysregulation of exosomal miRNAs in A2 astrocytes under oxygen-glucose deprivation is associated with neuronal pyroptosis: a role for miR-139-5p in targeting NLRP3
Authors: Guoliang Ma, Xiaokuan Qin, Bo Xu, Xiongjie Tang, Zhizhuang Wang, Dongsheng Yuan, Renkun Zhao, Penghui Li, Liguo Zhu, He Yin, Bowen Yang, Xin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundA2 astrocyte exosomes are closely associated with neuronal pyroptosis. Reduced expression of miR-139-5p was detected in A2 astrocyte exosomes under oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) conditions. We postulate that miR-139-5p intensifies neuroinflammation by modulating the expression of neuronal NLRP3 pyroptosis protein.ObjectiveTo identify differentially expressed exosomal miRNAs from A2 astrocytes under OGD conditions and to evaluate their potential roles in neuronal pyroptosis.MethodsAdminister interleukin-4 (IL-4) to microglia, harvest the supernatant to induce A2 astrocytes, expose them to oxygen deprivation models to isolate exosomes, then co-culture these exosomes with both healthy neurons and neurons subjected to OGD conditions to assess pyroptosis levels in the neurons. Subsequently, execute miRNA sequencing on exosomes from various circumstances to analyze differentially expressed microRNAs and their associated pathways. Finally, the differential targeting relationship between miR-139-5p and NLRP3 was validated using a dual luciferase reporter assay.ResultsOxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) reduced A2 astrocyte viability, with 1 h selected as the optimal duration (p < 0.001). Exosomes from OGD-treated astrocytes were internalized by neurons, decreasing viability (p < 0.001) and increasing pyroptosis-related proteins (p < 0.01). miRNA sequencing identified 133 differentially expressed miRNAs (62 upregulated, 71 downregulated), with miR-139-5p significantly downregulated. Dual-luciferase assays confirmed that miR-139-5p directly targets NLRP3 (p < 0.001).ConclusionsOf five pyroptosis-associated exosomal miRNAs (miR-214-3p, miR-22-5p, miR-139-5p, miR-382-3p, and miR-365-5p), miR-139-5p and miR-382-3p were downregulated under OGD, and miR-139-5p directly targeted NLRP3. Thus, exosomal miRNAs, particularly miR-139-5p, warrant further therapeutic investigation in neurodegenerative diseases, including degenerative cervical myelopathy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13018-026-06966-2.Keywords: A2 Astrocyte, Oxygen-glucose deprivation model, Exosomes, miR-139-5p, Neuronal pyroptosis

Introduction

Degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM) is the predominant cause of spinal cord dysfunction [1]. DCM encompasses many acquired and congenital conditions associated with cervical spine degeneration, including ligament hypertrophy and/or calcification, intervertebral disc degeneration, and alterations in bone tissue. These injuries lead to spinal canal narrowing, resulting in chronic spinal cord compression and subsequent dysfunction. The increasing prevalence of DCM, attributed to an aging population, requires timely diagnosis and intervention to avert irreversible disability [2]. There are two main categories of DCM pathogenesis: static and dynamic causes [3]. These components together create the ischemic-hypoxic milieu, which is the main cause of DCM. This then sets off inflammatory responses and pyroptosis [4, 5]. Recent research demonstrates that pyroptosis is pivotal in the pathophysiology of neurodegenerative disorders, including DCM. Pyroptosis is predominantly a form of programmed cell death mediated by the NLRP3 inflammasome. The NLRP3 receptor interacts with the adaptor protein ASC and pro-caspase-1 to form an inflammasome complex. This activates caspase-1. Caspase-1 cuts the GSDMD protein to make holes in the membrane, which makes cells swell because they become more permeable. At the same time, it encourages the activation and release of inflammatory cytokines, which leads to a cytokine storm and cell death [6].

Astrocytes are important for many brain activities in the central nervous system, and they also help nearby neurons get the nutrients they need [7]. Studies show that hypoxic-ischemic circumstances turn astrocytes into A1 and A2 subtypes [8]. A1 astrocytes promote neuronal mortality through mechanisms such as the upregulation of classical complement cascade components, whereas A2 astrocytes secrete neurotrophic factors that suppress neuronal pyroptosis, thereby providing neuroprotective effects [9].

Exosomes are vesicles enclosed by a membrane, measuring approximately 40 to 100 nanometers in diameter. Those found in physiological fluids serve a vital function in cellular communication. Research demonstrates that exosomes play a pivotal role in neurodegenerative diseases [10].Notably, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have demonstrated significant therapeutic potential in mitigating systemic inflammatory responses; for instance, MSC-derived EVs are being explored as a prospective therapy to mitigate the cytokine storm and ARDS in COVID-19 patients due to their potent immunomodulatory and regenerative capabilities [11].Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are useful for studying metabolic processes in osteoporosis and identifying therapeutic targets, although it remains challenging to predict whether in vitro responses are applicable in vivo [12]. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) are useful for investigating inflammatory processes, identifying therapeutic targets, and assessing drug efficacy in rheumatoid arthritis (RA), although it remains difficult to determine whether in vitro responses translate to in vivo settings [13]. Recent studies have shown that cell-free stem cell–derived secretomes, containing growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular vesicles including exosomes, exert therapeutic effects by promoting cell proliferation, migration, and tissue repair [14]. In pathological situations, astrocyte exosomes can precipitate neuronal pyroptosis and spinal cord damage [15–17]. Acute cytokine responses brought on by inflammatory factors activated during neuronal pyroptosis attract and activate astrocyte exosomes, exacerbating inflammatory damage to the spinal cord and causing a cascade of neuronal pyroptosis [18], quickening DCM’s advancement. Prior research has shown that A2-exo reduces cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury by inhibiting neuronal pyroptosis under physiological settings. Its effects on neurons under conditions of oxygen-glucose deprivation, however, are still unknown [19].Exosome therapy significantly improves histological outcomes in osteoarthritis compared to placebo, with enhanced efficacy in early-stage disease and a once-weekly dosing schedule [20].

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are significant and thoroughly researched non-coding RNAs that regulate approximately two-thirds of human genes [21]. miR-106a-5p is downregulated in postmenopausal osteoporosis, correlates with ferritin and bone mineral density, and protects against iron accumulation-induced osteoblast dysfunction by targeting PTEN, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic biomarker [22]. CRNDE sponges miR-29a-3p to promote osteogenic differentiation and fracture healing, and serum CRNDE serves as a potential diagnostic and predictive biomarker for delayed fracture healing [23].Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), as another class of regulatory small RNAs, can modulate gene expression involved in tissue repair and regeneration, although their precise targets and mechanisms remain incompletely understood [24]. Diverse cell and tissue populations can produce exosomes with unique miRNA profiles, transmitting them to specific target cells to exert their effects [25]. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) are useful for studying metabolic processes in osteoporosis and identifying therapeutic targets, although it remains challenging to predict whether in vitro responses are applicable in vivo [26]. MicroRNAs play critical roles in regulating tissue repair and inflammation; however, their clinical application is limited by the lack of efficient delivery systems [27].MicroRNAs play critical roles in regulating gene expression across various diseases and hold significant diagnostic and therapeutic potential; however, their clinical application is limited by challenges in effective delivery systems [28].Recent studies demonstrate that miRNAs, as essential post-transcriptional regulators of gene expression, significantly influence the initiation and advancement of spinal cord damage [29]. Numerous miRNA expression profiles have been documented to link with the course of DCM [30, 31]. miR-204-5p protects against intervertebral disc degeneration by targeting SSRP1 and regulating the NF-κB pathway, as revealed by Mendelian randomization, experimental validation, and bioinformatics analysis [32]. MiRNAs are prone to degradation in the spinal tissue microenvironment. Exosomes, distinguished by a lipid bilayer architecture, protect their contents from enzymatic degradation and other mechanisms, hence ensuring the integrity and stability of encapsulated miRNAs [33]. Moreover, exosomes possess the ability to traverse the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and facilitate targeted delivery to recipient cells [34]. Thus, miRNAs contained in exosomes from various cellular origins can influence DCM injury and repair by regulating gene expression and cellular activities.

This study generated an in vitro model of pyroptosis utilizing A2 astrocytes and neuronal cells. The aim was to examine the function of A2 astrocyte exosomes (A2-Exos) in neuronal pyroptosis and to analyze genes and pathways related to this phenomenon by microRNA sequencing (miRNA-Seq). Proteomic profiling of synovial fluid exosomes identifies C3, C4B, APOM, MMP3, and DPYSL2 as potential diagnostic biomarkers for osteoarthritis, independent of age [35]. Bioinformatics study and dual luciferase reporter experiments identified miR-139-5p as the principal molecule facilitating A2-Exos intervention in neuronal pyroptosis. These findings may provide a basis for future studies exploring therapeutic strategies targeting spinal cord damage and other neuroinflammation-related neurological disorders.

All reagents were used according to the manufacturers’ instructions.

Cultivation of A2-reactive astrocytes and neuronal cells

rat primary astrocytes (CP-R137, Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), rat spinal cord neuronal cells (CP-R144, Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), and rat primary microglia (CP-R110, Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) that were previously purchased. To produce A2-reactive astrocytes, microglia were activated with 40 ng/mL IL-4 for three days [36], and the supernatant was collected. A2-reactive astrocytes were the end result of the three-day induction phase.

Identification of A2 astrocytes

Immunofluorescence labeling was used to detect the cell-specific antigen S100A10 to describe the induced A2 astrocytes. Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for an hour at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and then blocked with 5% BSA. The cells were then incubated for one hour at room temperature in the dark with Alexa Fluor 488-labeled secondary antibody (1:500 dilution) after being treated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit anti-S100A10 primary antibody (1:200 dilution). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Finally, slides were sealed with anti-fade mounting medium, and images were acquired using a laser confocal microscope.

Establishment of the oxygen-glucose deprivation model (OGD model)

Cells were grown until they were 80–90% full, and then the media was sucked off, and the cells were gently washed three times with PBS. Cells were resuspended in glucose-free HBSS medium and transferred to a triple-gas anaerobic chamber. The gas inlet was connected to a mixture of 95% N2, 4% CO2, and 1% O2. Continuous gas flow was maintained to deplete oxygen within the chamber, ensuring an oxygen concentration below 1%. Control group cells were cultured under standard conditions. Model group cells were treated under hypoxic and glucose-deprived conditions for 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 8 h. Cells were collected post-treatment for subsequent experimental analysis.

MTT assay for cell viability detection

Seed cell suspensions from each group into 96-well plates at equal proportions and incubate at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator for 24 h. After that, take all the medium off the plates and put new medium on. Put 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL) in each well and let them sit in the dark for 4 h. Carefully suck off the supernatant, then add 150 µL of DMSO and mix until the MTT crystals are gone. Finally, use a microplate reader to measure the absorbance at 490 nm, write down the results, and figure out how many cells are still alive.

Exosome extraction and characterization

To extract exosomes from healthy and oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD)-treated (1 h) A2 reactive astrocytes, exosome isolation was first performed using the ExoQuick™ Kit (System Biosciences, Palo Alto, CA). Brief steps are as follows: To get rid of cellular debris, the cell culture supernatant was first centrifuged at 3,500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and then at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. Collect the supernatant and sequentially add reagents according to the kit instructions. Centrifuge the sample again to isolate exosomes. Collect the pellet and resuspend it in PBS. Subsequently, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and Western blot (WB) analysis were used to describe the exosomes.

Exosome RNA extraction

Total RNA from exosomes was extracted using the TRIzol™ LS reagent combined with the RNeasy column purification method. Brief steps are as follows: Take 20 µL of exosome suspension, add 1 mL of TRIzol™ LS reagent, and let stand at room temperature for 5 min. Add 140 µL of chloroform, mix well for 15 s, let it sit at room temperature for 3 min, and then spin it at 4 °C and 12,000 × g for 15 min. Meticulously transfer the upper aqueous layer to a new tube, incorporate 1.5 volumes of isopropanol, and mix completely. Thereafter, aliquot the mixture into an RNeasy purification column. Sequentially wash with 700 µL of Buffer RWT and 500 µL of Buffer RPE. Conclude by eluting the RNA with 30 µL of RNase-free water. Immediately store the isolated RNA at − 80 °C and evaluate its quality and concentration with an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100.

RNA library construction and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from exosome samples using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity and concentration of RNA were assessed using the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Small RNA libraries were constructed using the QIAseq miRNA Library Preparation Kit (Qiagen). Briefly, RNA samples were subjected to adapter ligation, reverse transcription, and PCR amplification to generate libraries enriched for small RNA species.The libraries were purified, quantified, and sequenced on an Illumina platform using single-end sequencing. Each group included n = 3 biological replicates, with an average sequencing depth of approximately 10–15 million reads per sample.Quality control analysis showed that Q30 values exceeded 90%, and mapping rates were above 80%, indicating high-quality sequencing data.No rRNA depletion step was performed, as small RNA sequencing inherently enriches for miRNAs and other small RNA species.Sequencing was conducted by Shanghai Xiaoyan Biotechnology Co., Ltd. to guarantee the acquisition of high-precision data.

miRNA data analysis

Raw reads were first quality-assessed using FastQC. Subsequently, Fastp software removed adapter sequences, poly N, and low-quality reads to obtain high-quality clean reads. Clean reads were aligned against the reference genome (Rattus norvegicus, Ensembl release 108, mRatBN7.2) and the miRBase database using Bowtie software. The Rfam database (version 14.9) was employed to identify and exclude non-miRNA small RNA sequences such as rRNA, tRNA, snRNA, and snoRNA. Finally, the miRDeep2 software was used for the identification and quantitative analysis of known miRNAs. Based on the miRNA quantification results, we used the DESeq2 and EdgeR tools in R to compare the blank control group to the oxygen-glucose deprivation group in terms of differential expression. The screening criteria for differentially expressed miRNAs were: p-value < 0.05 and |log2(Fold Change)| ≥ 1.

Target gene prediction and GO/KEGG enrichment analysis

To examine the biological roles of miRNAs with differential expression, we predicted and analyzed their potential target genes. Target prediction was performed by integrating results from multiple online databases, including miRanda [37], TargetScan [38], and miRWalk [39], with the intersection of predictions serving as a high-confidence candidate target gene set. The enrichGO function in the clusterProfiler software package (version 4.10) was used to perform Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis on the candidate target gene set, covering three categories: Biological Process (BP), Cellular Component (CC), and Molecular Function (MF). Using the entire rat proteome as background, significance was assessed via one-sided Fisher’s exact test (hypergeometric test) with Benjamini-Hochberg multiple testing correction. GO entries with FDR < 0.05 and ≥ 3 annotated genes were defined as significantly enriched. Results were visualized after simplification. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of candidate target gene sets was performed using the enrich KEGG function from the clusterProfiler software package. Statistical methods and significance thresholds were consistent with the GO analysis. Pathways with fewer than 3 genes were excluded to reduce estimation errors.

Western blot

Identification of pyroptosis-associated proteins, including NLRP3, GSDMD, Caspase-1, and ASC, using Western blot analysis. Neuronal cells were collected, and total proteins were extracted on ice utilizing a lysis buffer (Sigma-Aldrich). Proteins were resolved by 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently deposited onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes (Millipore). These membranes were blocked using 5% skim milk for two hours and subsequently incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: NLRP3 (1:2000, ab270449, Abcam), GSDMD (1:2000, ab209845, Abcam), ASC (1:2000, ab309497, Abcam), Caspase-1 (1:2000, ab179515, Abcam), and GAPDH (1:3000, AF7021, Affinity) overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was washed and incubated with goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:3000, S0001, Affinity). The spots were then visualized using Novex™ Enhanced Chemiluminescent Reagent (Thermo Scientific), with results normalized to GAPDH. Protein signals were subsequently detected via chemiluminescence (ECL), and target protein bands were quantified using ImageJ software.

qRT-PCR detection of multiple differentially expressed miRNAs including miR-139-5p

qRT-PCR was employed to quantify multiple differentially expressed miRNAs identified in sequencing results. Total RNA from exosomes was extracted using TRIzol™ LS Reagent. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcription using miRNA-specific stem-loop primers and the HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase kit. Using the ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix kit, qPCR reactions were done on a QuantStudio 5 real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR apparatus. U6 snRNA functioned as the housekeeping gene. We used the 2 − ΔΔCt approach to figure out the relative expression levels of miR-139-5p and other miRNAs that were expressed differently. Unpaired t-tests were used to statistically compare the groups, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Dual-luciferase assay

Bioinformatics analysis (TargetScan and miRWalk) predicted a putative binding site for miR-139-5p within the coding sequence (CDS) region of NLRP3 mRNA. The wild-type (WT) sequence containing the predicted binding site (5′-TGCTGGATCGTGTGCACTGG-3′) and the corresponding mutant (MUT) sequence with substitutions in the seed region (5′-TGCTGGATCGTCACGTGACG-3′) were synthesized and cloned into the psiCHECK-2 luciferase reporter vector. HEK-293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates and co-transfected with 500 ng of WT or MUT reporter plasmid and 50 nM miR-139-5p mimics or negative control (NC) using Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24–48 h of transfection, luciferase activities were measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity, and the relative luciferase activity was expressed as the Firefly/Renilla ratio. All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.3.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). All experiments were conducted with at least three independent biological replicates (n = 3), and each measurement was performed in triplicate unless otherwise stated. Prior to statistical analysis, the normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. When the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were satisfied (p > 0.05), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons; otherwise, appropriate non-parametric tests were applied. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of A2 astrocytes

S100A10 serves as a specific marker for A2-reactive astrocytes. Immunofluorescence staining showed positive expression of S100A10 in IL-4-treated astrocytes (Fig. 1A), indicating the presence of A2-like astrocytes. In addition, morphological observations revealed distinct cellular characteristics among microglia, astrocytes, and A2 astrocytes (Fig. 1B). These results suggest that A2-reactive astrocytes were successfully induced.

Impact of OGD duration on A2 astrocyte activity and determination of optimal modeling duration

This study established an A2 astrocyte oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) model using different exposure durations (1 h, 2 h, 4 h, and 8 h) to determine an appropriate injury condition. MTT assays showed a time-dependent decrease in cell viability with increasing OGD duration (Fig. 2). Compared with the control group, 1 h of OGD resulted in a significant reduction in cell viability (***p < 0.001), while longer durations (2–8 h) led to a more pronounced decrease.Considering that 1 h OGD induced a measurable but moderate reduction in cell viability, it was selected as the experimental condition for subsequent analyses.

Extraction and characterization of A2 astrocyte-derived exosomes

To confirm the successful isolation of exosomes, comprehensive characterization was performed in accordance with the MISEV guidelines. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that particles isolated from both the control (NC) and OGD-treated (OGD-1 h) groups exhibited typical cup-shaped or spherical vesicles with a bilayer membrane structure (Fig. 3A).Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) demonstrated that the particle size distribution predominantly ranged from 50 to 300 nm (Fig. 3B–C). The peak diameter was approximately 165 nm in the NC group, with a concentration of 4.3 × 108 particles/mL, whereas in the OGD group, the peak diameter was approximately 172 nm with a concentration of 5.1 × 108 particles/mL, indicating a slight increase in exosome secretion following OGD stimulation.Furthermore, Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of canonical exosomal markers, including CD9, CD63, and TSG101, in both groups (Fig. 3D).Collectively, these morphological, physical, and biochemical characteristics confirm that the isolated particles are bona fide exosomes and exhibit comparable quality between the NC and OGD groups.

OGD-derived A2 astrocyte exosomes can be uptaken by neurons and induce pyroptosis

To investigate the effects of A2 astrocyte-derived exosomes on neurons following OGD injury, we isolated exosomes from A2 astrocytes in control and OGD groups and co-cultured them with healthy or OGD-treated rat neurons. First, exosomes were labeled with PKH-67 fluorescent dye. Immunofluorescence labeling demonstrated that labeled exosomes were effectively swallowed by neurons, validating the capacity of neurons to internalize exosomes from various groups (Fig. 4A). Results demonstrate that under physiological conditions, exosomes derived from A2 astrocytes promote neuronal cell viability (Fig. 4B), while exosomes obtained under oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) conditions markedly diminish neuronal survival rate (p < 0.001), indicating a toxic effect of OGD-derived exosomes on neurons (Fig. 4C). To ascertain if this toxicity was linked to neuronal pyroptosis, we employed Western blot analysis to evaluate the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins in neurons co-cultured with various groups of exosomes. The findings demonstrated that, relative to the control group, the expression levels of pyroptosis marker proteins NLRP3, Caspase-1, ASC, and GSDMD were significantly increased in neurons subjected to OGD-derived exosomes (p < 0.01). The findings suggest that OGD-treated A2 astrocytes can trigger neuronal pyroptosis through the exosomal pathway, a process possibly associated with the activation of the previously reported pyroptosis-related proteins (Fig. 4D).

Hierarchical clustering analysis further demonstrated distinct miRNA expression profiles between the two groups (Fig. 5C).

Subsequent Gene Ontology functional annotation and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were conducted on the differentially expressed miRNAs. Gene Ontology analysis revealed (Fig. 6A) that differentially expressed miRNAs predominantly engaged in biological activities including “enhancement of cell chemotaxis towards fibroblasts” and “regulation of phosphorus metabolism.” KEGG analysis suggested (Fig. 6B) that these miRNAs were significantly enriched in pathways closely associated with pyroptosis [40, 41], including the “PI3K/Akt signaling pathway” and “calcium ion signaling pathway.” This indicates that differentially expressed miRNAs may influence neuronal pyroptosis by regulating these pathways.

Based on miRNA sequencing results and published literature, we first selected five miRNAs that have been closely associated with pyroptosis: miR-214-3p [42], miR-22-5p [43], miR-139-5p [44], miR-382-3p [45], and miR-365-5p [46] (the predicted binding sites for these five miRNAs are provided in Supplementary Table 2). Considering the post-transcriptional regulatory properties of miRNAs, we identified differentially expressed miRNAs that exhibited higher expression in the control group and were significantly downregulated in the OGD group. By integrating differential expression analysis with target gene prediction and published literature, two candidate miRNAs (miR-382-3p and miR-139-5p) were selected for further validation using qRT-PCR (Fig. 6C). Although both miRNAs showed significant downregulation under OGD conditions, miR-139-5p was selected for subsequent functional analysis based on a combination of factors, including its predicted targeting relationship with NLRP3 and previously reported involvement in pyroptosis and inflammatory pathways [44, 47].Therefore, we hypothesize that the reduced expression of miR-139-5p in A2 astrocyte-derived exosomes under OGD conditions may contribute to neuronal pyroptosis by relieving the inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway.

Dual luciferase reporter assay validates miR-139-5p targeting and inhibition of NLRP3

To determine whether miR-139-5p directly targets and regulates NLRP3, this study employed a dual luciferase reporter assay for validation. Bioinformatics prediction identified a potential binding site for miR-139-5p within the coding sequence (CDS) region of the NLRP3 gene. To this end, reporter plasmids containing the wild-type binding sequence and a corresponding site-mutant sequence were constructed (Fig. 7A). These were co-transfected into HEK-293T cells with either a miR-139-5p mimic or a negative control (NC). Results showed that in the wild-type reporter vector, the miR-139-5p mimic significantly inhibited luciferase activity (p < 0.001), whereas this inhibitory effect was absent in the mutant vector (Fig. 7B). These results provide direct molecular evidence for the function of miR-139-5p in controlling neuronal pyroptosis by demonstrating that it specifically targets the NLRP3 coding sequence.

Discussion

DCM has many different pathophysiological pathways, but one of the main causes of neuronal death and neurological dysfunction is the hypoxic-ischemic microenvironment brought on by prolonged compression. The precise regulation mechanisms of astrocytes and their exosomes in the hypoxic-ischemic milieu of DCM are still poorly known, despite prior research demonstrating the critical role pyroptosis plays in neurodegenerative disorders. This study employs miRNA sequencing technology to elucidate the molecular mechanism through which A2-type reactive astrocytes, under oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) conditions, intensify neuronal pyroptosis by downregulating miR-139-5p levels in exosomes, consequently alleviating the inhibition on neuronal NLRP3. This discovery contests the traditional perspective that A2-type astrocytes predominantly provide neuroprotection, indicating that changes in their exosomal content may trigger functional reversal in particular clinical circumstances.

Astrocytes can develop into either neurotoxic A1-type or neuroprotective A2-type in response to ischemia or inflammatory stimuli [48]. Wang et al. demonstrate that A2-type astrocytes often enhance the expression of neurotrophic factors, facilitating neuronal survival and tissue regeneration [49, 50]. The latest study by Chen et al. corroborates this perspective, demonstrating that A2 astrocyte exosomes induce autophagy in vascular endothelial cells through the delivery of miR-5121, thus facilitating the repair of the blood-spinal cord barrier after traumatic spinal cord damage [51].

Our findings, however, indicate that under normal settings, A2 exosomes confer a protective benefit via enhancing neuronal activity. Subsequent to OGD treatment (mimicking the DCM microenvironment), A2 exosomes convert into neurotoxic agents, markedly increasing the expression of pyroptosis-related proteins (NLRP3, Caspase-1, GSDMD) in neurons. This finding aligns with the research conducted by Kun Leng et al., which indicates that under particular inflammatory or damage stimuli, extracellular vesicles generated by astrocytes can modify their contents, transitioning from supportive to pathogenic states and potentially initiating cascading inflammatory responses [52]. Our data suggest that even A2-type astrocytes, which are phenotypically recognized as protective, experience pathogenic transformation within the exosomes they release under hypoxic-ischemic stress. This finding indicates that in therapeutic strategies aimed at DCM, solely promoting astrocyte polarization toward the A2 phenotype may be inadequate. Equal emphasis should be placed on maintaining the stability of their secretory function within ischemic environments.

As primary mediators of intercellular communication, variations in exosome function are frequently ascribed to differences in their encapsulated miRNA profiles [53]. In this study, high-throughput sequencing demonstrated that OGD treatment caused a substantial reduction of miR-139-5p expression in A2 exosomes. Previous research on spinal cord injury (SCI) has shown that miRNA expression profiles are closely linked to the pathological processes occurring after injury [54]. Liu et al. demonstrated that the targeted delivery of specific miRNAs through exosomes can effectively facilitate spinal cord injury repair [55]. Specifically concerning miR-139-5p, although its tumor-suppressing role in cancer biology has been extensively documented, its function within the nervous system remains comparatively underexplored [56–58]. Our study addresses this lacuna by affirming that miR-139-5p is a vital molecule for preserving neuronal homeostasis. The depletion of this molecule under OGD conditions results in A2 exosomes losing their intrinsic anti-inflammatory properties.

The NLRP3 inflammasome serves as the central mediator of pyroptosis [59]. Its overactivation results in Caspase-1-mediated cleavage of GSDMD, inciting severe inflammatory reactions and cellular apoptosis [60]. While numerous miRNAs, including miR-27 A and miR-21, are recognized for their regulatory role on NLRP3 [61, 62], Our dual luciferase reporter assay demonstrates that miR-139-5p selectively interacts with the coding sequence (CDS) of NLRP3 mRNA, thereby inhibiting its translation.miR-217 is upregulated in postmenopausal osteoporosis and promotes disease progression by targeting OPG and regulating the OPG/RANKL/RANK signaling pathway [63].

Based on the enhanced PI3K/Akt and calcium signaling pathways identified through KEGG pathway analysis, we propose that miR-139-5p may play a pivotal role within the regulatory network. PRP-exos combined with cyclic peptide-modified β-TCP scaffold promotes cartilage repair via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, as revealed by miRNA sequencing analysis [64].In typical A2 exosomes, the abundant miR-139-5p persistently inhibits NLRP3 expression upon internalization into neurons, thereby sustaining minimal levels of pyroptosis. Following the depletion of miR-139-5p resulting from ischemia-hypoxia, this suppression is alleviated, resulting in increased NLRP3 expression and subsequent neuronal pyroptosis. This indicates that delivering miR-139-5p through engineered exosomes could function as a targeted therapeutic approach to reduce neuronal injury in DCM.Human adipose stem cell-derived exosomes prevent the progression of osteonecrosis of the femoral head by inhibiting osteonecrosis and cartilage damage, suggesting their potential as a regenerative therapy for early-stage ONFH [65].

This work predominantly utilizes in vitro cellular models that effectively replicate the interactions between A2 astrocytes and neurons in hypoxic-ischemic circumstances. It should be noted that exosomes contain a complex cargo of miRNAs, and the regulation of neuronal pyroptosis is unlikely to be mediated by a single miRNA. Although miR-139-5p was identified as a key candidate in this study, other differentially expressed miRNAs may also contribute to the observed effects. Therefore, the regulatory mechanism is likely to involve coordinated actions of multiple miRNAs.A major limitation of this study is the lack of direct functional validation using gain- or loss-of-function approaches (e.g., miR-139-5p mimics or inhibitors in astrocytes). On the other hand, the other three candidate factors related to cell pyroptosis were not individually verified by qRT-PCR. Future studies will address this by manipulating miR-139-5p expression in exosome-producing cells to confirm its causal role.Nonetheless, the in vivo milieu of DCM is more intricate, encompassing various components including mechanical compression and vascular remodeling. Subsequent study must confirm the therapeutic effectiveness of miR-139-5p-engineered exosomes in animal models of DCM and examine their capability to traverse the blood-spinal cord barrier (BSCB). Lyophilized human umbilical cord stem cell-derived exosomes preserve their characteristics and significantly improve the viability, proliferation, migration, and gene expression of chronically injured ACL cells after hydration, offering a ready-to-use biomaterial for clinical application [66]. Meta-analysis confirms that miRNAs are promising non-invasive biomarkers for osteoarthritis with high diagnostic accuracy (AUC 0.90), particularly in Asian populations and for downregulated genes [67]. Furthermore, the potential synergistic contribution of other differentially expressed miRNAs in A2 exosomes to this mechanism necessitates further investigation. HCG18 promotes spinal tuberculosis progression by sponging hsa-miR-146a-5p to activate the TGF-β1/SMADs pathway, leading to nucleus pulposus cell dysfunction and intervertebral disc destruction [68]. Although our results support the involvement of pyroptosis, other forms of cell death, such as apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis, cannot be excluded and warrant further investigation.

Conclusion

This study reveals a potential intercellular communication mechanism in the pathophysiology of degenerative cervical myelopathy (DCM). Under oxygen-glucose deprivation conditions, A2 reactive astrocytes release exosomes with altered miRNA profiles. Among five pyroptosis-associated miRNAs identified from the literature (miR-214-3p, miR-22-5p, miR-139-5p, miR-382-3p, and miR-365-5p), miR-139-5p and miR-382-3p were significantly downregulated in OGD-treated exosomes. Upon uptake by neurons, these exosomes were associated with enhanced NLRP3 inflammasome activation and increased neuronal pyroptosis. Dual-luciferase reporter assay confirmed that miR-139-5p directly targets the NLRP3 coding region. Importantly, while miR-139-5p represents one prominent candidate, the observed effects are likely mediated by the coordinated actions of multiple exosome-derived miRNAs rather than a single factor. These results provide new insights into the complex regulatory roles of astrocyte-derived exosomes in neuroinflammatory conditions and highlight miR-139-5p as a potential candidate for further investigation in the context of spinal cord damage and related neurological disorders.
