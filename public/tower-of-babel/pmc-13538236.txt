Estimated health burden of environmental noise exceeds that of ambient air pollution in Ireland
Authors: Jon Paul Faulkner, Enda Murphy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo quantify population health risks attributable to environmental noise in Ireland and to compare two assessment approaches, EU harmful-effects estimation and burden of disease (BoD) in terms of interpretability, data requirements and policy applicability.Subjects and methodsPopulation exposure to road, rail and aircraft noise in Dublin and Cork was modelled using 2017 strategic noise mapping outputs and CNOSSOS-EU. Health impacts were estimated using harmful-effects metrics (attributable cases; numbers highly annoyed and highly sleep disturbed) and BoD metrics (years lived with disability, YLD; disability-adjusted life years, DALY). Uncertainty and sensitivity were assessed by propagating parameter uncertainty in exposure–response functions and by testing the influence of key exposure-model assumptions on attributable outcomes.ResultsIn 2017, environmental noise exposure was associated with 256 attributable cases of ischaemic heart disease (IHD) and 3,445 IHD DALYs; 296,471 cases of high annoyance (HA) and 5,866 HA YLDs; and 109,951 cases of high sleep disturbance (HSD) and 4,780 HSD YLDs. In total, 14,091 DALYs were attributable to noise exposure, exceeding the burden estimated for ambient air pollution and some dietary risks in comparable population-health assessments.ConclusionEnvironmental noise represents a substantial yet under-recognised public health risk in Ireland. Harmful-effects estimation provides efficient, policy-ready outputs for END reporting and action planning, while BoD estimation supports cross-risk prioritisation in public health. The findings support stronger integration of noise within national environmental health governance and routine health risk assessment.

Introduction

In recent years, significant progress has been made demonstrating that environmental noise is not only a nuisance but a measurable determinant of adverse health outcomes, including adverse cardiovascular effects and sleep disturbance [1, 10, 22]. In response, the World Health Organization (WHO) updated its Environmental Noise Guidelines for the European Region [26]. At EU level, the Environmental Noise Directive (END; 2002/49/EC) [5] was subsequently supplemented by Commission Directive (EU) 2020/367 [7], which operationalises health impact assessment within END reporting by specifying methods for estimating harmful effects for road traffic noise-related ischaemic heart disease (IHD), and for estimating high annoyance (HA) and high sleep disturbance (HSD) across all major transport sources (i.e. road, rail, aircraft). This update is important because it introduces a harmonised “harmful effects” approach alongside the earlier WHO environmental burden of disease (BoD) approach [25], which has direct implications for how END mapping outputs are translated into public-health metrics.

However, despite the policy salience of Commission Directive (EU) 2020/367, there remains limited empirical work demonstrating its application in national contexts or examining how harmful effects estimates compare with BoD estimates when both are implemented on the same exposure model. Existing European-scale assessments provide valuable benchmarks e.g., [8], but cross-country comparability can be influenced by methodological choices (including how underlying health statistics are operationalised) and by reliance on END submissions that are produced using diverse national modelling inputs, coverage rules, and quality-assurance practices [15]. Relatedly, Khomenko et al., [12] report substantial heterogeneity in the underlying END map quality across European cities, which underscores the importance of evaluating health-risk methods using exposure inputs that are internally consistent within the study design.

The present study addresses these gaps by applying both harmful effects and BoD approaches to a harmonised national exposure model for Ireland. Using CNOSSOS-EU acoustic modelling and standard END exposure band outputs, the study estimates IHD, HA and HSD burdens attributable to road, rail and aircraft noise (where applicable), and compares: (i) the magnitude and interpretability of outputs across methods (cases vs. YLD/YLL/DALY); (ii) the data requirements and practical implementability of each approach; and (iii) the sensitivity of results to key exposure-model assumptions. By keeping the exposure model fixed and systematically documenting the calculation steps, the analysis isolates methodological differences between harmful effects and BoD estimation and clarifies the implications for END reporting and for national noise-health risk assessment practice.

The paper proceeds as follows. The Methods section describes the CNOSSOS-EU exposure modelling and the calculation procedures for harmful effects and BoD estimation, including uncertainty and sensitivity analysis. The Results section reports national and local-authority estimates for IHD, HA and HSD, alongside uncertainty ranges and sensitivity findings. The Discussion interprets methodological differences and situates findings within the international literature, before the Conclusion summarises implications for END mapping, action planning, and future research.

Methods

Estimating population exposure

In evaluating both the harmful effects and burden of disease (BoD) methodologies, the study employed data obtained from the strategic noise mapping (SNM) process conducted under the END. In estimating national exposure statistics in the Irish case study, the CNOSSOS-EU calculation method was applied. For road traffic sources, the relevant Local Authority (LA) supplied the necessary datasets used in the third round (Round 3) of the strategic noise mapping (SNM) process conducted in 2017. This facilitated the generation of acoustic models calculated using the CNOSSOS-EU method (see [16]; p. 31–38).1 Cork’s agglomeration is managed by Cork City Council (covering 187 km² with 125,657 residents in 2017), whereas Dublin’s agglomeration is divided among four separate local authority areas. Analysis of Ireland’s 2017 round 3 data revealed significant variations in the coverage of road source polylines (excluding buffer zones) and corresponding traffic flow data across local authority areas. For instance, the Dublin City Council dataset covered 97% of road sources compared to Ordnance Survey Ireland’s PRIME2 dataset, which is the official digital road database of Ireland, while datasets from South Dublin, Fingal, Dún Laoghaire-Rathdown (DLRD), and Cork City Council covered only 59%, 39%, 23%, and 31% respectively. Noise models for these regions were created for the entire road network using systematically estimated road traffic flows based on round 3 data (refer to [16]; p. 34–37). Predictor-LimA software was employed to develop the acoustic models.

Acoustic models for rail sources within the Dublin agglomeration were also developed using the CNOSSOS-EU methodology, and datasets provided by the appropriate authorities. The 2017 rail source dataset was provided by Irish Rail for evaluating the Irish Rail network, while Transport Infrastructure Ireland supplied the data for assessing the Luas Tram Rail network. These models were developed for both the Irish Rail network (national rail system) and the Luas tram network.2 For the Irish Rail system, vehicles were classified according to the categories defined in the Predictor-LimA database: Cat. 14 (Mk4), Cat. 15 (Freight), Cat. 8 (DMU), Cat. 19 (ICR), and Cat. 10 (EMU). For Luas Tram Rail, vehicles were classified as per Predictor-LimA database Cat. 20 (Urban Subway). Again, Predictor LimA was utilised to generate models.

In accordance with CNOSSOS-EU methodology, noise levels were assessed at the most exposed façade for single-dwelling residential buildings, while for multi-dwelling buildings, noise levels were evaluated at each façade, as specified in Directive (EU) 2015/996 L168/93 [6]. Harmful effect and burden of disease calculation

In calculating harmful effects and BoD the quantitative basis for assessment is population risk. In epidemiology research estimation of relative risk (RR) is used to generate the population attributable fraction (PAF) which is applied in the estimation of noise-induced cardiovascular disease cases in the assessment of harmful effects and noise-induced cardiovascular disease disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for BoD estimation. Likewise, the absolute risk (AR) associated with HA and HSD is incorporated into both the assessment of harmful effects and the estimation of BoD.

In 2018 the WHO updated its existing exposure-response relationship formulations for negative health impacts caused by environmental noise. In relation to cardiovascular and metabolic impacts, the van Kempen et al. [22] developed exposure–response curves derived from meta-analyses, providing relative risk (RR) estimates for the incidence, prevalence, and mortality of noise-induced hypertension, IHD, stroke, and both type 1 and type 2 diabetes. van Kempen et al., [22] also calculated the natural logarithm of the RR and its variance per 10 dB(A) to quantify the association between environmental noise exposure and cardiovascular diseases.

It should be noted that Commission Directive (EU) 2020/367 does not consider the assessment of harmful effects relating to railway and aircraft noise-induced IHD, or environmental noise-induced hypertension, stroke, diabetes, other metabolic health outcomes, cognitive impairment in children, mental health and wellbeing, hearing impairment, tinnitus, and adverse birth outcomes, because a common method for determining a definitive method for assessing these harmful effects has not yet been established. Hence, these assessments were also not considered in the present analysis.

The methodological contribution of this work lies in applying harmful effects and BoD approaches to a harmonised CNOSSOS-EU national exposure model, enabling direct comparison of the two risk assessment methods within a consistent population exposure framework. The exposure modelling, population assignment, and integration of CNOSSOS-EU with WHO/BoD and EU harmful effects metrics represent the authors’ original contribution.

Uncertainty and sensitivity analysis

To address uncertainty and to support reproducibility, the study quantified uncertainty in health-impact estimates arising from epidemiological parameters and disability weighting assumptions, and tested sensitivity to key exposure-model assumptions. For IHD, uncertainty was represented using the reported confidence interval for the exposure–response relationship [22] and propagated to attributable fractions and attributable outcomes. For HA and HSD, uncertainty was represented using the uncertainty in the exposure–response functions reported in the underlying meta-analyses [1, 10] and propagated to estimated numbers of affected individuals. For BoD outputs that use disability weights (DW) and duration assumptions, uncertainty was further represented using the published DW ranges and duration assumptions applied in the assessment. Uncertainty was reported as 95% uncertainty intervals around central estimates, derived by parameter sampling and re-calculation of outcomes.

In addition, a sensitivity analysis was conducted, focusing on exposure-model completeness for road traffic noise, given documented variation in road source coverage across END mapping submissions [15]. Specifically, we compared health-impact results generated using Round 3 road source inputs as supplied by local authorities with results generated using a reconstructed full road network exposure model (with systematically estimated flows, following the procedure described in [16]).

Discussion

International overview

Although cross-country comparisons can be challenging, they remain valuable for interpreting and contextualizing findings. It is important to acknowledge that such comparisons may be influenced by a range of factors, including: (1) differences in assessment methodologies; (2) approaches to estimating population exposure; (3) selected counterfactual values; (4) the nature and distribution of population exposure; (5) variability in disease incidence rates; (6) demographic and health characteristics of the populations studied; and (7) methodological differences in strategic noise mapping, such as the selection and classification of noise sources. Overall, Irish estimates fall within the range reported in European and national studies, but cross-country comparison is sensitive to, firstly, the exposure-model inputs used for END reporting, and secondly, differences in health-statistics operationalisation and counterfactual choices across studies. For road and rail, Irish results are broadly consistent with patterns reported for Germany and WHO European averages, while differing from some national and city-level studies in the relative balance between annoyance and sleep disturbance. The markedly lower aircraft-related estimates relative to European-scale figures likely reflect incomplete availability of aircraft exposure data outside agglomerations in the Irish END Round 3 submissions; these estimates should therefore be interpreted as conservative pending updated national coverage.

Differences across outcomes partly reflect how endpoints translate from exposure to “affected” status and then into disability-weighted burden. HA yields the largest affected counts because the annoyance response increases steeply across common exposure ranges, but it is assigned a comparatively low disability weight, producing moderate YLD per affected person. HSD affects fewer people but has a higher disability weight, yielding YLD totals that can approach those of HA. For IHD, DALY totals are dominated by YLL because the attributable fraction applies to mortality as well as morbidity, and premature mortality produces large life-year losses per death. This divergence between “affected counts” and DALY-based burden is therefore expected and provides complementary policy signals: prevalence of exposure-related disturbance versus severity-weighted health loss.

Environmental noise relative to ambient air pollution

Positioning environmental noise within a BoD framework clarifies its salience for regulatory prioritisation. In Ireland (2017), the estimated noise-attributable burden (14,091 DALYs) exceeded the corresponding estimate reported for ambient air pollution (10,685 DALYs), indicating that transport noise can represent a leading environmental health risk in the context where air-quality governance is well established. This comparison should be interpreted as a call for prioritisation rather than a definitive ranking, because estimates depend on harmonised inputs and uncertainty ranges. Nevertheless, it supports integrating END outputs into standard environmental health assessments and aligning noise action planning with broader transport and public-health policy objectives.

Harmful effects and burden of disease: comparative analysis

Commission Directive (EU) 2020/367 introduced an EU-level harmful-effects assessment approach that complements the longer-established use of BoD metrics in environmental health. In practice, the two approaches provide different but compatible decision-support outputs. Harmful-effects estimation yields attributable cases that are readily interpretable and can be calculated at sub-national scale where suitable incidence-rate and population data are available, supporting spatial targeting in END action planning. BoD estimation requires additional inputs (including disability weights, duration assumptions, and mortality/life-expectancy data to derive YLD and YLL), but produces DALY-based metrics that enable comparison across endpoints and risk factors within public health planning frameworks.

From a practical standpoint, method choice should be guided by decision context. Where the aim is local prioritisation (e.g., identifying hotspots, evaluating candidate interventions within specific local authority areas, or communicating “numbers affected”), harmful-effects outputs are often the most direct. Where the aim is cross-sector prioritisation (e.g., situating noise alongside air pollution or other risk factors in national health strategy), BoD metrics are more informative because they translate heterogeneous outcomes into a common health-loss unit (DALYs). In applied settings, reporting both—cases for communicability and spatial actionability, and DALYs for cross-risk comparison—provides the most robust basis for policy decisions.

Finally, both approaches remain sensitive to the completeness and resolution of exposure inputs used for END reporting. Where exposure submissions omit portions of the road network or other sources, both attributable cases and DALY-based estimates will be conservative. Routine reporting of uncertainty and sensitivity ranges (for example, across exposure–response confidence intervals and disability-weight assumptions) is therefore essential for transparent interpretation and for tracking change over successive mapping rounds.

Limitations of study

Concerning study limitations, the findings from this analysis are based on the assumption that the relative risk (RR) statistics from van Kempen et al., [22] and the absolute risk (AR) statistics from Basner and McGuire [1] and Guski et al. [10] are relevant to the Irish context. Furthermore, discounting the potential inaccuracies caused by degree of error, an additional source of inaccuracy is associated with exposure to multiple sources. Both the harmful effects and BOD assessments omit consideration of multiple noise sources. Consequently, failing to quantify the cumulative health impacts from various sources may lead to a significant underestimation (see [18]). Additionally, inaccuracies arise because air pollution, which is not currently incorporated into noise assessment methodologies, and the interaction of multiple pollutants are not accounted for. Moreover, this analysis focused primarily on exposure to environmental noise at residential dwellings; therefore, the adverse health impacts in workplace settings, recreational areas, schools, and hospitals were not evaluated. Furthermore, the acoustic models representing an entire road network were developed using real-time road traffic data and estimated flows from Ireland’s round 3 data (see [16]; p. 34–38), introducing an indeterminate degree of error. Finally, the results indicated that, in contrast to the clear trends observed for road traffic and railway noise, the aircraft noise data from round 3 statistics submitted under the END were markedly different when compared internationally and were substantially lower than the European average [25]. This discrepancy suggests that there may be value in reassessing population exposure statistics for aircraft noise in the Irish context.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that applying EU harmful-effects and WHO burden-of-disease approaches to a harmonised CNOSSOS-EU exposure model yields complementary evidence for noise governance. Harmful-effects estimation provides directly interpretable affected counts that support efficient reporting and spatial targeting within action plans, while BoD metrics translate impacts into YLD, YLL, and DALYs to enable prioritisation across risks. In Ireland (2017), the estimated noise-attributable burden exceeded the corresponding estimate for ambient air pollution, underscoring the need to treat transport noise as a central component of integrated environmental health governance and regulatory prioritisation. Continued improvements in END input coverage and routine uncertainty/sensitivity reporting will strengthen the credibility and policy utility of national noise-health assessment.
