Habenular structural–functional dysconnectivity in bipolar disorder: evidence from multimodal imaging and transcriptomic integration
Authors: Meng-Xuan Qiao, Wei Wei, Meng Zhou, Ming-li Li, Ya-min Zhang, Xiao-Jing Li, Wei Deng, Wan-Jun Guo, Qiang Wang, Hua Yu, Tao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBipolar disorder (BD) is a highly heritable condition characterized by recurrent shifts between manic and depressive states. Here we investigated the potential involvement of the habenula because it plays a central role in negative affect and behavioral regulation.MethodsWe investigated bilateral habenular volume and seed-based resting-state functional connectivity in a discovery cohort (78 BD, 102 controls) and an independent replication cohort (72 BD, 85 controls). Associations among habenular features, clinical symptoms, and molecular correlates were examined by integrating pathway-specific polygenic risk scores and brain-wide gene expression data from the Allen Human Brain Atlas.ResultsAcross both cohorts, BD was associated with reduced bilateral habenular volume and increased rs-FC between the habenula and right precentral gyrus. Habenular volume correlated positively with severity of mania symptoms and negatively with severity of symptoms of anxiety and somatization. Polygenic risk scores linked the altered volume to dopaminergic pathways and altered connectivity to serotonergic pathways, while transcriptomic data linked the altered connectivity to changes in expression of synaptic membrane structures, transporter complexes, and other proteins involved in synaptic transmission.ConclusionsStructural, functional and transcriptomic data identify the habenula as a critical neural hub in BD and therefore important for understanding pathogenesis and clinical manifestations.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08216-5.Keywords: Bipolar disorder, Habenular volume, Resting-state functional connectivity, Biological mechanisms, Polygenic risk score

Introduction

Bipolar disorder is a chronic, severe psychiatric condition that affects approximately 2% of the global population [1]. The disorder involves recurring episodes of mania, typically associated with elevated dopaminergic activity; and episodes of depression, associated with reduced dopaminergic activity [2]. Parallel dysregulation of serotonergic activity appears to contribute to affective instability and exacerbation of depressive symptoms [3]. These imbalances in monoaminergic neurotransmission may impair functional coordination between limbic and cortical regions, thereby destabilizing emotion-regulation circuits [4–6].

Studies of the habenula in depression imply its involvement in bipolar disorder. The habenula regulates monoaminergic neurotransmission [7] by receiving afferent input from regions such as the prefrontal cortex, amygdala and nucleus accumbens, and sending efferent projections to the raphe nuclei and ventral tegmental area [8, 9]. Reduced habenular volume in individuals with depression correlates with severity of their anhedonia [10, 11], and neuromodulation that sends major depressive disorder into remission increases gray matter volume in the habenula [12]. Depression appears to involve aberrant functional connectivity between the habenula and limbic/sensorimotor networks, which may impair emotional regulation and sensory integration [13–16]. The demonstrated role of the habenula in mood dysregulation in major depressive disorder implies that it may contribute to similar dysregulation in bipolar disorder.

To examine this question, we compared the volume and functional connectivity of the habenula between individuals with bipolar disorder and controls, and further assessed their associations with clinical symptom severity. To begin to identify molecular pathways that may explain disorder-associated alterations, we integrated our structural and functional findings with pathway-related polygenic risk scores and transcriptomic profiles in habenula. Elucidating how changes in gene expression contribute to bipolar disorder is critical given that its heritability lies between 70% and 90% and 10–15% of first-degree relatives of affected individuals develop mood disorders [17, 18].

Methods

Participants

The study was approved by the Ethics Committees of the two medical centers involved. All participants provided written informed consent after receiving a comprehensive explanation of the study procedures, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

The discovery cohort comprised individuals who were diagnosed between 2014 and 2018 with bipolar disorder but not with other Axis I or Axis II psychiatric disorders, based on the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV Axis I Disorders, at the Mental Health Center of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Chengdu, China). Such individuals were enrolled only if they reported having at least six years of formal education, no history of alcohol or substance abuse or dependence, and no electroconvulsive therapy during the preceding six months; and only if they scored at least 70 on the Chinese version of Wechsler’s intelligence test [19]. The same inclusion criteria were used to recruit a validation cohort of individuals with bipolar disorder at Hangzhou Seventh People’s Hospital (Hangzhou, China) between 2022 and 2024.

Healthy controls were recruited from the local communities around the two hospitals and enrolled if they were between 16 and 55 years old; had no history of psychiatric disorders, including substance use disorders and other addictive disorders, as assessed by the Non-Patient Edition of the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-IV or DSM-5; reported no history of diabetes, thyroid dysfunction, hyperlipidemia, hepatic disease, epilepsy, stroke, systemic lupus erythematosus or other major chronic condition; and reported no first-degree relatives with psychiatric disorders.

All participants were right-handed Han Chinese, and none had contraindications to magnetic resonance imaging. Controls were matched to individuals with bipolar disorder in terms of age, sex, and years of education in the discovery cohort.

Assessment of manic and depressive symptoms

Manic symptoms in individuals with bipolar disorder were assessed using the validated Chinese version of the Young Mania Rating Scale [20]. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the validated Chinese version of the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale [21].

Magnetic resonance imaging and volume analysis

All members of the discovery cohort underwent magnetic resonance imaging on a 3.0-T Achieva scanner (Philips Healthcare, Amsterdam, Netherlands) equipped with an eight-channel phased-array head coil. All members of the validation cohort underwent imaging on a 3.0-T MAGNETOM Prisma system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Foam padding and earplugs were used to reduce head motion and scanner noise. During imaging, participants were asked to clear their minds and not think about anything in particular. Acquisition parameters are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Structural data were registered into standard space using FreeSurfer 6.0.0 (http://surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu), allowing calculation of total intracranial volume. Left and right habenula were manually delineated at the voxel level by a trained analysist, who was blinded to whether individuals had been diagnosed with bipolar disorder. Images were segmented manually using ITK-SNAP 3.8.0 (www.itksnap.org) following a validated anatomical protocol [22]. Habenula volume was calculated using the command “fslstats [-k] < input> [-V]” in FSL 6.0 (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/).

Resting-state functional connectivity

Functional magnetic resonance imaging data were preprocessed using FreeSurfer and FSL in the following steps: (1) correction for slice acquisition timing with the stc_sess function; (2) correction for head motion via rigid-body realignment using the mc_sess function; (3) temporal band-pass filtering within the range of 0.01–0.08 Hz; (4) regression of nuisance variables, including six motion parameters, white matter signal, ventricular signal, global signal, and their derivatives with respect to time; (5) linear alignment of functional data to each participant’s high-resolution anatomical image using boundary-based registration; and (6) spatial smoothing with a Gaussian kernel of 6 mm for the full width at half-maximum.

Seed-based whole-brain resting-state functional connectivity was analyzed on the manually delineated left and right habenula. The mean time series for each habenula seed was extracted from the preprocessed functional magnetic resonance imaging data. Strength of functional connectivity was expressed in terms of Pearson correlation coefficients between the mean habenula time series and the time series of all other brain voxels. The resulting correlation maps were converted to Z-score maps using Fisher’s r-to-z transformation in order to normalize data for subsequent group-level statistical analyses.

Pathway-specific polygenic risk scoring

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples from individuals with bipolar disorder in the discovery cohort, then genotyped using the Asian Screening Array chip (see Supplementary Methods). Data were subjected to rigorous quality control within PLINK 1.9 (www.cog-genomics.org/plink).

Polygenic risk scores were calculated specifically for dopaminergic and serotonergic pathways (Supplementary Table 1) that we selected from the Gene Ontology collection in the MSigDB database (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org/gsea/msigdb/human/search.jsp). We adopted the exact Gene Ontology nomenclature for these pathways (e.g., distinguishing general serotonin receptor signaling from G protein-coupled serotonin receptor signaling) to ensure methodological precision and biological specificity. Scores were calculated using PRS-CSx [23] based on genome-wide association studies of European and East Asian individuals with bipolar disorder [24] and reference linkage disequilibrium panels from phase 3 of the 1000 Genomes Project (http://github.com/getian107/PRScsx). Pathway-specific polygenic risk scores took into account all polymorphisms within pathway-related genes as well as within 30 kb up- and downstream of each gene, where regulatory regions may lie. These pathway‑specific polygenic risk scores integrate the potential pathogenic influence of polymorphisms affecting genes whose proteins participate in defined molecular pathways [25]. When combined with brain imaging, such scores can suggest possible molecular bases for observed imaging anomalies.

Spatial transcriptome in the brain

Spatially resolved data on gene expression in the brain were obtained from the Allen Human Brain Atlas (http://human.brain-map.org), which was built from six adult donors. Data on expression of genes detected by 58,692 probes at coordinates that had been registered to standard Montreal Neurological Institute space [26] were preprocessed using the “abagen” toolbox (https://github.com/rmarkello/abagen) with default settings. The expression profiles of 15,631 genes were mapped to 338 brain regions defined by the Schaefer-400 cortical parcellation atlas and were ultimately included in the present study.

Associations between functional connectivity and gene expression

Partial least-squares correlation analysis was conducted to examine the spatial correspondence between aberrant resting-state functional connectivity patterns of the bilateral habenula and regional gene expression profiles derived from the Allen Human Brain Atlas. The analysis was performed using the myPLS toolbox (https://github.com/danizoeller/myPLS/).

Partial least-squares correlation is a multivariate statistical technique that identifies latent variables by maximizing the shared covariance between two data matrices. In the present study, the first data matrix consisted of T-value maps representing resting-state functional connectivity involving the habenula, whereas the second data matrix consisted of gene expression levels across brain regions. The cross-covariance matrix between the two data matrices was decomposed using singular value decomposition, yielding a set of latent variables, each characterized by a singular value and a pair of corresponding singular vectors.

Latent variables were assessed for statistical significance by testing their singular values in 10,000 permutations, and variables associated with Ppermutation < 0.05 were considered significant. The contribution of each participant to a given latent variable was quantified using subject-wise latent variable scores. Whether a given feature contributed significantly to a given latent variable was assessed through bootstrap resampling, and a feature associated with a | bootstrap ratio | > 3 was defined to contribute significantly. Genes that met this definition were assessed for overlap with genes linked to bipolar disorder in a recent genome-wide association study [27].

Enrichment of Gene Ontology terms among genes associated with altered functional connectivity involving the habenula

Gene symbols were converted to ENTREZ IDs using the “org.Hs.eg.db” annotation package, and Gene Ontology term enrichment was assessed among genes with |BSR| > 3 that were associated with altered functional connectivity involving the habenula using the enrichGO function in the “clusterProfiler” package in R 4.3.1 (www.r-project.org). The resulting Penrichment values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, and terms were considered significant if their adjusted P < 0.05. The 20 terms with the smallest adjusted P values were included in the final analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed statistically as described here and in the Supplementary Materials using R 4.3.1, and results were considered significant if associated with two-tailed P < 0.05, unless otherwise noted. The significance of clinicodemographic differences between individuals with bipolar disorder and healthy controls was assessed using independent-samples t tests for normally distributed continuous variables, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for non-normally distributed continuous variables, and chi-squared tests for categorical variables.

Differences in habenula volumes between individuals with bipolar disorder and healthy controls were assessed for significance using analysis of covariance in which age, sex, education, body mass index and total intracranial volume were treated as covariates. Differences in resting-state functional connectivity were assessed using two-sample t tests in which age, sex, education, body mass index, and head-motion parameters were controlled. The same analytical procedures were applied when comparing individuals with bipolar disorder in manic/hypomanic episodes or depressive episodes with healthy controls.

Partial correlations of volume or functional connectivity of the habenula with total score or subscores on the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale or with total score on the Young Mania Rating Scale were explored. These correlation analyses controlled for age, sex, education, body mass index, and total intracranial volume. Scores from the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale were included as covariates in analyses involving Young Mania Rating Scale and vice versa, in order to account for overlap in how the two instruments assess mood symptoms.

Partial correlations of volume or functional connectivity of the habenula with polygenic risk scores specifically for dopaminergic or serotonergic pathways were also explored. These analyses controlled for age, sex, education, body mass index, and the 10 genetic principal components.

To control for multiple testing, corrections were applied separately within each set of analyses. For imaging analyses, significance was defined as P < 0.001 at the voxel level or P < 0.05 after Gaussian random field correction at the cluster level. For correlation analyses between habenula-related measures and clinical variables, as well as for polygenic risk score analyses, p values were adjusted using the false discovery rate method within each family of tests.

Bipolar disorder is associated with altered volume and functional connectivity of the habenula

Bipolar disorder was associated with significantly smaller volumes of left and right habenula in the discovery cohort (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Table S2) and validation cohort (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Table S2). The disorder was also associated with significantly stronger resting-state functional connectivity between the bilateral habenula and the right precentral gyrus in the discovery cohort (Fig. 1C) and validation cohort (Fig. 1D) (Supplementary Table S3).

When individuals with bipolar disorder in the discovery cohort were classified into subgroups who were in depressive or manic/hypomanic states, we found that the depressive subgroup showed significantly smaller volumes of the bilateral habenula than the manic/hypomanic subgroup or healthy controls (see Supplementary SFigure1). The manic/hypomanic subgroup, but not the depressive subgroup, showed significantly stronger resting-state functional connectivity between the bilateral habenula and right precentral gyrus. Similar results were observed in the validation cohort, except that the volume differences did not reach statistical significance.

Associations of habenula volume with clinical features in the discovery cohort

In the discovery cohort, left habenular volume correlated positively with severity of manic symptoms as measured on the Young Mania Rating Scale after adjusting for age, sex, education, total intracranial volume, and total score on the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (r = 0.392, PFDR = 0.001; Fig. 2A). Conversely, right habenula volume correlated negatively with severity of anxiety or somatization symptoms as measured by the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale after adjusting for age, sex, education, total intracranial volume, and total score on the Young Mania Rating Scale (r = − 0.354, PFDR = 0.027; Fig. 2B). No significant associations were detected between severity of clinical symptoms and resting-state functional connectivity involving the habenula.

In the validation cohort, neither volume nor functional connectivity of habenula correlated significantly with severity of clinical symptoms.

Altered functional connectivity of the habenula in bipolar disorder is associated with changes in gene expression

As a complementary approach, we examined spatial correlations between brain-wide gene expression patterns and abnormalities in habenular resting-state functional connectivity to identify molecular pathways potentially underlying habenula connectivity alterations in bipolar disorder.

In the discovery cohort, gene expression correlated significantly with connectivity involving the left habenula (r = 0.361, FDR-corrected PPermutation < 0.001; Fig. 4A-B) and right habenula (r = 0.305, FDR-corrected PPermutation < 0.001; Fig. 5A-B). In both hemispheres, the genes associated with altered functional connectivity were enriched in the Gene Ontology cellular components of synaptic membranes, transporter complexes and glutamatergic synapses, as well as in Gene Ontology biological processes of regulation of chemical synaptic transmission and trans-synaptic signaling (Figs. 4C and 5C). Similar results were observed in the validation cohort (see Supplementary SFigure2).

Among the genes associated with altered functional connectivity involving the habenula, 335 overlapped with known genetic risk factors for bipolar disorder (Figs. 4D and 5D). In the left habenula, 21 genes correlated positively with connectivity T-values, and the correlation was strongest for LIMK2 (r = 0.235, P < 0.001; Fig. 4E). Conversely, 33 genes correlated negatively with T-values, most strongly SHISA9 (r = − 0.270, P < 0.001; Fig. 4F). In the right habenula, 17 genes correlated positively with T-values, most strongly ZNF644 (r = 0.219, P < 0.001; Fig. 5E), while 23 genes correlated negatively, most strongly TP53BP1 (r = − 0.239, P < 0.001; Fig. 5F). Similar results were observed in the validation cohort (see Supplementary SFigure3).

Discussion

This controlled study involving discovery and external validation cohorts strongly implicates habenular dysfunction in bipolar disorder. In our cohorts, the disorder was associated with significantly smaller bilateral habenula and elevated resting-stage functional connectivity between the habenula and right precentral gyrus. Moreover, habenular volume correlated positively with the severity of manic symptoms and negatively with severity of anxiety and somatization symptoms. We explored potential molecular drivers behind these changes using pathway-specific polygenic risk scoring and spatial transcriptomic data, which suggested that the observed reduction in habenular volume may reflect dysfunction in dopaminergic pathways, while the elevated functional connectivity with the right precentral gyrus may reflect dysfunction in serotonergic pathways and altered expression of genes involved in synaptic membrane structures, transporter complexes, and synaptic transmission. These findings, which span the levels from molecular pathways to brain morphology, may guide future studies to clarify how habenular dysfunction contributes to bipolar disorder and how the condition might be treated.

Among our individuals with bipolar disorder, those suffering a depressive episode had even smaller bilateral habenula than those suffering a manic/hypomanic episode and healthy controls. Consistent with previous high-resolution MRI studies demonstrating reduced habenular volume in unmedicated bipolar depression [28]. Our findings suggest that habenular structural alterations are closely tied to bipolar mood states. This raises the possibility that habenular volume may serve as a potential biomarker for bipolar episodes. However, its precise utility in predicting illness polarity and severity must be validated by future longitudinal studies.

The inverse association of habenula volume with somatic anxiety aligns with previous reports linking reduced habenula volume to anhedonia [29], which may be broadly compatible with the proposed role of the habenula in negative affect regulation [30]. Conversely, the positive correlation with manic symptoms may reflect the habenula’s involvement in regulating arousal and controlling impulses and aggressive behaviors [31, 32]. Notably, these associations differed between the left and right habenula, which may point to possible lateralized involvement [10]. However, current evidence regarding left–right asymmetry of the habenula in mood disorders remains limited and heterogeneous [33, 34]. Accordingly, these lateralized findings should be interpreted with caution. However, the associations between habenular measures and symptom severity were not reproduced in the replication cohort. This inconsistency may partly reflect differences in sample composition between the two cohorts. In particular, the replication cohort included a higher proportion of male patients and a higher level of educational attainment. These differences may be relevant, as sex is known to influence brain structure and function in bipolar disorder [35], and educational attainment may reflect cognitive reserve that buffers illness-related burden [36]. In addition, detailed information on medication status was not available in the replication cohort. As previous studies have reported reduced habenula volume in untreated patients with bipolar depression [28], possible differences in treatment exposure between the two cohorts may also have influenced the observed symptom-related associations. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Future studies with larger, better-matched samples, more detailed medication data, and sex-stratified analyses will be important to determine whether these associations reflect true clinical heterogeneity or cohort-specific effects. While extensive preclinical and clinical literature robustly links the habenula to the regulation of midbrain dopaminergic pathways [2, 37]. However, in the present study, we did not observe a significant association between altered resting-state functional connectivity of the bilateral habenula and right precentral gyrus and the polygenic risk score for the dopaminergic synapse pathway. This suggests that the specific functional coupling between the habenula and this sensorimotor cortical region may be more distinctly driven by serotonergic, rather than dopaminergic, genetic risk profiles in this cohort.

Like the habenula, the precentral gyrus contributes to regulating emotions and controlling impulses and responses [38–40]. The enhanced resting-state functional connectivity between the habenula and precentral gyrus in our individuals in the manic/hypomanic subgroup is similar to that observed in individuals with treatment-resistant depression and suicidal ideation [41–43], which in both groups may reflect stress-related neural hypersensitivity. In major depressive disorder, resting-state functional connectivity between habenula and precentral gyrus is associated with less dynamic connectivity with broader sensorimotor networks, which may underlie behavioral withdrawal from aversive stimuli [44]. These considerations suggest that heightened connectivity between the habenula and precentral gyrus is a neural marker of maladaptive emotional regulation and motor reactivity in mood disorders.

The transcriptomic integration results showed that the elevated functional connectivity between habenula and right precentral gyrus in our study was associated with signaling mediated by serotonin receptor and G protein–coupled receptors. This pattern may be broadly consistent with prior evidence showing that tryptophan depletion, which reduces central serotonin synthesis, can increase habenular metabolic activity [45, 46]. However, this interpretation remains tentative, and these transcriptomic associations should not be regarded as direct evidence of causal serotonergic mechanisms. Genes whose dysregulation was associated with the altered resting-state functional connectivity of the habenula in bipolar disorder encoded synaptic membranes, transporter complexes, and many were involved in synaptic transmission and trans-synaptic signaling. Our transcriptomic findings provide convergent evidence that molecular systems governing synaptic architecture and chemical signaling may underpin the observed habenular functional dysconnectivity in bipolar disorder [8, 47]. Specifically, the enrichment of genes involved in synaptic membrane integrity and transporter complexes suggests that the fundamental machinery of neuronal communication is compromised within the habenula [48, 49]. As a critical hub for integrating multimodal information (aversive, reward, and arousal), such molecular disturbances could disrupt the fine-tuned balance of neurotransmitter release and reuptake [50, 51]. Notably, the overlap between our imaging-derived gene profiles and bipolar disorder-linked risk genes underscores a potential causal link between genetic vulnerability and regional circuit dysfunction. The identification of key modulators, such as LIMK2 (involved in actin cytoskeleton remodeling and dendritic spine morphogenesis) and SHISA9 (an AMPA receptor auxiliary subunit), points toward specific mechanisms of synaptic plasticity and glutamatergic signaling that may render the habenula dysfunctional [52, 53]. Furthermore, the prominent enrichment in serotonergic pathways aligns with the “classic” monoamine hypothesis while providing a localized anatomical substrate (the habenula-precentral circuit) for emotional dysregulation in bipolar disorder [34, 54].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, we could not apply myelin-sensitive automated segmentation of the habenula because of the limited quality of T2-weighted magnetic resonance images in our study. Instead, we segmented manually, which may have made the image interpretation more subjective. Second, our participants were not euthymic, preventing us from examining habenular volume and connectivity across the full spectrum of mood states in bipolar disorder and from determining whether observed changes depended on the mood state. Third, our cross-sectional study prevents us from examining causality in the observed associations among volume, functional connectivity and gene expression. That will require studies that perform neuroimaging and molecular analyses over time in bipolar disorder. Fourth, the associations involving polygenic risk scores did not survive false discovery rate correction. These results should therefore be interpreted cautiously and regarded as exploratory. Moreover, the replication cohort had imbalances in sex and education; despite adjustment, residual confounding may have compromised the robustness of the replication results. A further limitation is that the effects of psychotropic medications were not fully controlled. Medication dosage and duration were not systematically recorded, and medication could not be included as a covariate in the analyses. Although exploratory analyses within the patient group did not show significant medication-related differences, these results should be interpreted cautiously.

Despite these limitations, the present study provides preliminary evidence linking bipolar disorder to altered volume and functional connectivity of the habenula, with the volume alterations associated with dysregulation of dopaminergic pathways and connectivity alterations associated with dysregulation of serotonergic pathways. Our multilevel analysis integrating brain imaging, genetic risk scoring and gene expression identifies potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets related to the habenula for treating bipolar disorder.

Conclusions

In summary, the integration of structural, functional, and transcriptomic data across discovery and validation cohorts identifies the habenula as a critical neural hub in bipolar disorder. Structural and functional alterations of the habenula were associated with clinical symptom severity and linked to dopaminergic and serotonergic pathways, as evidenced by pathway-specific polygenic risk scoring and genes involved in synaptic structure and neurotransmission. These findings provide convergent evidence for the central role of the habenula in the pathophysiology of bipolar disorder and offer important insights into the biological mechanisms underlying its clinical manifestations.
