XEN‑45 implantation for refractory secondary glaucoma
Authors: Kun Lv, Zhiqiao Liang, Kuankuan Wu, Yao Ma, Kangyi Yang, Wenbo Liu, Huijuan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo evaluate the efficacy and safety of XEN-45 gel stent in different types of refractory secondary glaucoma (SG).MethodsThis retrospective analysis included 39 patients (42 eyes) who underwent XEN-45 gel stent implantation for medically uncontrolled SG using two approaches, ab externo closed-conjunctiva (AEC) and ab externo open-conjunctiva (AEO). The intraocular pressure (IOP) and numbers of antiglaucoma medications were compared between preoperative and postoperative values. The requirement for revision, bleb needling and further glaucoma surgery were recorded.ResultsIOP was decreased significantly from 31.79 ± 10.62 mmHg to 14.06 ± 4.45 mmHg (p < 0.001) by the final visit (averaging 13.74 ± 7.96 months post-surgery) and the number of antiglaucoma medications was reduced from 3.67 ± 0.87 to 0.36 ± 0.82 (p < 0.001). Twenty-three eyes (54.8%) required postoperative needling. Twelve eyes (28.6%) required open conjunctival bleb revision. At the final follow-up, 33 eyes (78.6%) achieved complete success, defined as an IOP of 18 mmHg or lower without medications, and 36 eyes (85.7%) reached qualified success, maintaining an IOP of 18 mmHg or lower with medications, while 2 eyes (4.76%) needed trabeculectomy. There were no vision-threatening and ocular infection complications intraoperatively or postoperatively.ConclusionsXEN-45 gel stent provides a minimally invasive option for eyes with SG and requires close monitoring and timely postoperative management. The incidence of adverse events following the procedure is rare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-025-04452-7.Keywords: XEN-45 gel stent, Secondary glaucoma, Minimal invasive glaucoma surgery

Introduction

Glaucoma, a leading cause of irreversible vision loss, is classified into primary and secondary forms [1]. Secondary glaucoma (SG) results from various underlying pathophysiological mechanisms causing elevated intraocular pressure (IOP), stemming from diverse causes such as inflammation, post-surgical complications, trauma, lens-related conditions, drug reactions, tumors, and others [2]. The condition of elevated pressure without optic nerve damage is termed ‘ocular hypertension’; if left untreated, it progresses to glaucomatous optic neuropathy, a form of SG [3]. Currently, the mainstay of glaucoma management involves reducing IOP to halt further damage to the optic nerve [4, 5]. Eliminating any secondary factors and utilizing medical therapy serve as the initial treatment strategy for SG. Surgical options are reserved for cases where IOP remains uncontrolled despite maximal medicinal treatment, laser therapy proves ineffective, or both.

Traditional filtering surgeries like trabeculectomy (TE) and drainage implants lower IOP by creating a pathway from the anterior chamber to the subconjunctival space [6]. However, due to significant tissue disruption, these procedures pose risks of complications such as hypotony and bleb leaks, leading to higher rates of additional surgeries [7–9]. Minimally invasive glaucoma surgeries (MIGS) have been developed to offer safer, more predictable outcomes.

XEN-45 Gel Stent (Allergan, an Abbvie company, Irvine, CA, USA), FDA-approved in 2016, is designed for subconjunctival filtration to reduce IOP [10]. Made from cross-linked porcine gel, it is 6 mm long with a 45 μm lumen. Preloaded in a special inserter, it is injected from the anterior chamber through the blocked trabecular meshwork. Compared to traditional filtration surgeries, XEN-45 gel stents cause less invasive trauma to sclera, iris and conjunctiva with a more rapid recovery and less complications [11, 12]. Multiple surgical techniques are available for implanting the XEN-45 gel stent, including ab interno closed-conjunctiva (AIC), ab interno open-conjunctiva (AIO), ab externo closed-conjunctiva (AEC), and ab externo open-conjunctiva (AEO) [13]. The majority of studies to date have utilized the ab interno implantation method [14]. The ab externo technique, as an alternative off-label approach involving stent inserted beneath the conjunctiva from outside the eye, with (AEO) or without (AEC) conjunctival dissection, has become increasingly popular due to its simplicity, minimal intraocular manipulation, and no corneal incisions [15].

XEN-45 gel stents have been effectively employed in managing both primary and secondary open-angle glaucoma (OAG) that remains uncontrolled despite previous medical interventions or failed surgeries [16, 17]. Although its effectiveness has been proven in refractory SG such as uveitic, pseudoexfoliative, and steroid-induced glaucoma [11, 18–20], comparative analyses of XEN-45 implantations across various SG forms are still scarce. This study includes a diverse range of SG cases, such as uveitic glaucoma, steroid-induced glaucoma, traumatic glaucoma, pigmented glaucoma, neovascular glaucoma, glaucoma due to elevated episcleral venous pressure and so on. The purpose of this study is to present the data of XEN-45 gel stent implantation for the treatment of refractory SG in a Chinese patient population.

Method

Study design

This study includes records of all patients or eyes scheduled for XEN-45 gel stents implantation from May 2022 to December 2024 at the glaucoma clinic of a university-based hospital. Prior to surgery, written informed consent was obtained from all enrolled patients, for these under 16 years of age, written consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians. All research adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Hospital.

Participants

Patients were eligible for inclusion in the study if they met the following baseline criteria: (1) A diagnosis of SG with evidence of disease progression; (2) Uncontrollable IOP exceeding 21 mmHg, or normal IOP maintained with at least three anti-glaucoma medications; (3) Patients with healthy, mobile conjunctiva in at least one quadrant. Previous intraocular surgeries and laser procedures were allowed, provided they occurred more than three months prior to the study enrollment; (4) Participants were required to have at least 6 months of follow-up for inclusion in the analysis. Glaucoma was diagnosed as chronically elevated IOP (> 21 mmHg) and with glaucomatous optic neuropathy and visual field defect. SG is a heterogeneous group of diseases characterized by elevated IOP caused by identifiable factors, leading to glaucomatous optic neuropathy. In this study, the etiologies of SG included uveitis, steroid-induced, trauma, pigment dispersion and post-vitrectomy.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) Patients with any active ocular disease, such as corneal, iris, lens, vitreous, retinal, or optic nerve diseases; (2) Patients with a history of intolerance or allergy to formaldehyde, glutaraldehyde, or porcine-derived products; (3) Patients with severe systemic conditions, including end-stage cardiovascular, kidney, pulmonary diseases, or advanced stage tumors.

Ophthalmologic examinations

General information recorded included patient demographic information such as age, sex, diagnosis, eye operated on. All subjects underwent a comprehensive ocular examination, including baseline best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), IOP measurement using Goldmann applanation tonometry (Haag-Streit, Koniz, Switzerland), detailed slit-lamp biomicroscopy and gonioscopy (Volk Optical, Inc., Mentor, OH, USA), glaucoma diagnosis, number of glaucoma medications used, degree of visual field loss, and prior glaucoma surgeries and procedures. Optical Coherence Tomography (OCT) analysis (Spectralis HRA + OCT; Heidelberg Engineering GmbH, Heidelberg, Germany) was employed to detect retinal nerve fiber layer defects, while glaucomatous visual field defects were evaluated using visual field testing (Humphrey Field Analyzer; Carl Zeiss Meditec, Inc., Dublin, CA, USA).

Surgical technique

All Xen-45 implantations were performed by the same experienced surgeon (H.J.W) under topical anesthesia with oxybuprocaine. Our study utilized two ab externo implantation methods: AEC and AEO. The choice between AEC and AEO approaches was based primarily on conjunctival conditions, such as prior scarring and implant quadrant suitability, rather than glaucoma severity or IOP level.

AEC: A 6 − 0 vicryl suture was placed in the superior peripheral cornea as a traction suture to infraduct the eye. The conjunctiva was marked 6 mm posterior to the limbus and 2.5 mm posterior in the superior area using a caliper (except for 3 eyes in the inferotemporal quadrant). A 0.1 ml dose of 0.02% MMC (0.2 mg/mL) was injected subconjunctivally space. The Xen-45 injector was introduced through the conjunctiva 6 mm posterior to the limbus, advanced through the subconjunctival space, and inserted into the intrascleral space 3 mm posterior to the limbus. The placement XEN-45 gel stent was assessed to ensure 1 mm within the anterior chamber and 2–3 mm projected above the sclera. Adjustments were made via conjunctival manipulation if needed, and the injection site was checked for leaks. The procedure concluded with the removal of the corneal traction suture.

AEO: This involved creating a fornix-based conjunctival flap and blunt dissection of the subconjunctival space. The conjunctiva and Tenon’s capsule were dissected towards the conjunctival sac to expose the sclera. The XEN-45 gel stent injector was placed bevel-up on the sclera 2.5 mm from the limbus and advanced to the limbus. After confirming the needle’s position above the iris plane and clear of the cornea, the injector was carefully removed. The conjunctiva was then closed at the limbus with two 10 − 0 nylon wing sutures, and 0.1 ml of 0.2 mg/mL of MMC was injected subconjunctivally.

Needling and 5-FU injections

In instances where an increase in IOP was attributed to conjunctival scarring and fibrotic tissue obstructing the subconjunctival outflow of the XEN-45 gel stent during follow-up, a needling procedure was undertaken. Administered under topical anesthesia, a 1 ml syringe needle was carefully inserted through the conjunctiva into the bleb to gently release episcleral adhesions surrounding the stent. Following this intervention, 0.1 ml of 5-Fluorouracil (5-FU) (5 mg in 0.1 ml) was injected into the subconjunctival space to prevent further scarring, complemented by the application of antibiotic drops. Post-procedure care included administering both antibiotic and steroid eye drops four times daily, with the antibiotic treatment concluding after three days and the steroid being gradually reduced over the course of four weeks.

Outcome measures

According to the recommendations of the World Glaucoma Association’s guidelines [21], complete success in this study was defined as maintaining an IOP between 6 mmHg and 18 mmHg without the need for IOP-lowering medications. Qualified success was achieved when the criteria for complete success were met but with the use of ocular hypotensive medications. Surgical failure was identified by one or more of the following: (1) Persistent IOP elevation above 18 mmHg; (2) The requirement for additional glaucoma surgeries due to complications or uncontrolled IOP, such as TE or extraction of the XEN-45 gel stent. However, interventions such as needling and laser iridotomy were not considered indicative of failure in this study; (3) Loss of light perception vision.

As three patients underwent bilateral surgery, an additional one-eye analysis was performed by including only one randomly selected eye per patient to account for bias. The secondary outcome measures included reduced mean IOP compared to baseline, the number of individual antiglaucoma agents used.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 22.0 software (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). All collected data were processed to calculate the mean ± standard deviation. Mean values, along with 95% confidence intervals, were determined for all postoperative intervals. For data adhering to a normal distribution, parametric testing via the Student’s t-test was utilized, while the Mann-Whitney test was applied to data that did not follow a normal distribution. Statistical significance was established at a p-value of less than 0.05. Bonferroni correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons. For the six comparisons of intraocular pressure and medication count across the six time points (postoperative day 1, week 1, month 1, month 3, month 6, and year 1), the adjusted significance level was set at α = 0.0083 (0.05 / 6). For patients who received bilateral XEN implantations, data from both eyes were included into the analysis. Surgical success was evaluated using the Kaplan-Meier survival estimates.

Results

In our study, XEN-45 gel stent implantation was performed on 42 eyes from 39 patients (21 female, 18 male). The average age of the participants was 43.1 ± 19.53 years, and they were followed for an average duration of 13.74 ± 7.96 months, ranging from 6 to 32 months. Complications

Postoperative adverse events were generally mild or moderate and transient, resolving without sequelae. Hypotony, defined as an IOP lower than 6 mmHg, was observed in 5 eyes (11.9%) on the first day post-surgery but resolved by the one-week follow-up without impacting implant functionality. No persisting hypotony complications were observed, including in the eye with a final IOP of 5 mmHg. Bleb leakage, noted in two eyes (4.8%) during the two-month follow-up, were successfully managed with resuturing and implant repositioning, thereafter, maintaining stable bleb function and IOP levels. A choroidal detachment occurred in one eye (2.4%), which did not involve the macula and resolved spontaneously. In cases where the iris contacted the XEN-45 gel stent, YAG iridotomy was performed postoperatively in five eyes (Table 2).

Twelve eyes (28.6%) required open conjunctival bleb revision about 4.97 months post-implantation due to impaired drainage, likely caused by occlusion of the stent lumen by Tenon’s fascia or subconjunctival scarring; four of these eyes had undergone AEO implantation.

Of these 14 eyes (60.9%) underwent AEC and 9 (39.1%) underwent AEO. Needling was completely successful in 17 eyes (73.9%) achieving a mean IOP ≤ 18 mmHg, while two eyes required a secondary TE. One of the two patients was a 69-year-old man (patient 2 in Fig. 2) with a history of ocular blunt and diagnosed with post-traumatic angle-recession glaucoma. He underwent needling 9 times due to subconjunctival scarring and uncontrolled IOP, eventually requiring XEN-45 gel stent extraction and TE at 11 months postoperatively. Another patient, a 33-year-old female (patient 4 in Fig. 2) with a rheumatic disease history and chronic oral glucocorticoid therapy, experienced Tenon’s fascia or conjunctival scarring. She underwent a conjunctival bleb revision at 2 months postoperatively for impaired drainage. Unfortunately, at 5 months, she required TE, during which a broken stent was discovered in the subconjunctival space. Fortunately, neither patient experienced vision loss during follow-up. Representative cases

Case 1:

A 46-year-old male patient was admitted to clinic with a diagnosis of refractory SG in his right eye, which had previously undergone vitrectomy for retinal detachment. Over the past four years, he underwent multiple glaucoma surgeries, including trabectome combined with phacoemulsifcation and intraocular lens implantation, TE and Ahmed glaucoma valve implantation. However, due to filtration bleb leakage, he required multiple revision surgeries. At the time of evaluation, his BCVA was 0.02, and the IOP was 48 mmHg in the right eye. Given his extensive history of surgical intervention, a XEN-45 gel stent was strategically implanted in the inferior temporal quadrant to avoid areas with previous surgical scarring (illustrated in Fig. 3). Over a follow-up duration of 13 months, he required two needling procedures but ultimately achieved a stable IOP of 14.4 mmHg.

Case 2:

A six-year-old girl was admitted to our clinic with a 7-month history of persistent, refractory steroid-induced glaucoma in her left eye. She had previously undergone a gonioscopy-assisted transluminal trabeculotomy (GATT) two months prior at another facility to manage ocular hypertension. At presentation in our department, her BCAV was 0.8, and her IOP continued to remain in the 40–50 mmHg range with 5 hypotensive medications. Visual field test revealed tubular vision in the affected eye. After explaining the potential risks and benefits, a XEN-45 gel stent implantation was performed. Following the procedure, her IOP stabilized at 7–10 mmHg during the first 4 weeks, without the need for glaucoma medications. Two years post-surgery, her IOP was effectively managed within the range of 9–13 mmHg, supported by a well-functioning filtrating bleb (illustrated in Fig. 4), and an improvement in her visual field was observed.

Case 3:

A 56-year-old male patient was admitted to our clinic with a history of 18-month of redness and proptosis in his left eye. On examinations, his BCAV was 1.0, and the IOP was 25 mmHg in the affected eye. Corkscrew hyperaemia and dilated scleral vessels were observed. Digital subtraction angiography showed a tentorial dural arteriovenous fistula being supplied by the left posterior cerebral artery and posterior meningeal artery. Despite receiving gamma knife radiosurgery and hypotensive medication, his symptoms persisted without notable improvement, accompanied by worsening optic nerve and visual field damage. Finally, a XEN-45 gel stent implantation was undertaken. At the last follow-up, 14 months postoperatively, his IOP had decreased to 10.5 mmHg, and the corkscrew hyperaemia was gradually alleviated (illustrated in Fig. 5).

Discussion

This retrospective study highlights the effectiveness of XEN-45 gel stent implantation in significantly reducing IOP in eyes with refractory SG, including a substantial proportion with prior surgical interventions. The study observed a marked decrease in IOP from baseline to the most recent follow-up, alongside a notable reduction in the reliance on antiglaucoma medications. Sight-threatening complications may be associated with XEN-45 implantation, including persistent hypotony and bleb-related ocular infection did not occur in our study. These findings suggest that the XEN-45 gel stent offers a safe and effective treatment option for a broad spectrum of glaucoma patients.

Since its initial introduction, several reports have been published on the efficacy and safety profile of the XEN-45 gel stent for the treatment of various types of glaucoma. However, most of the available studies included both primary and secondary OAG, mainly including uveitic glaucoma, pseudoexfoliation glaucoma and normal tension glaucoma [11, 19, 22–25]. Our research team has evaluated the efficacy and safety of XEN-45 gel stent for OAG and different types of SG patients in China, showing significant reduction in IOP and the use of glaucoma medications with a high success rate (73.9%) [26]. To our knowledge, this study was the first detailed report on the outcomes of XEN-45 gel stent implantation across a diverse spectrum of SG cases, employing AEC and AEO surgical approaches. Uniquely, our research includes two eyes of SG associated with elevated episcleral venous pressure, which has not been documented in the literature. The surgeries for both patients achieved complete success.

The XEN-45 gel stent was implanted in the inferotemporal quadrant in three eyes with prior failed TE, and all of them showed a durable IOP-reduction without needling procedure. Düzgün et al. reported similar outcomes of inferonasal implantation with no vision loss or sight-threatening complication [17]. We suggest that the XEN-45 stent implantation in the inferior quadrant can be considered a viable option for glaucoma patients with a history of failed filtering surgery who require further IOP lowering.

The relatively high needling rate observed in this study (more than 50% of eyes requiring at least one needling procedure) likely reflects both the challenging nature of refractory SG and our proactive approach to managing early fibrosis. Although this rate is higher than those reported in some studies of primary OAG, it is consistent with outcomes in other complex glaucoma subtypes and previous studies involving uveitic population. For instance, Sng et al. reported a needling rate of 41.7% within the first 12 months postoperatively in uveitic glaucoma, along with a bleb revision rate of 20.8% [27]. Similarly, studies by Tan et al. and Rauchegger et al. documented needling rates of 53% and 62%, respectively, in patients with OAG and pseudoexfoliation glaucoma [23, 28], aligning more closely with our findings.

Several risk factors may contribute to this elevated intervention rate, including young age, intraocular inflammation, and a history of prior glaucoma surgery which are prevalent in our study cohort [29, 30]. The role of subconjunctival fibrosis is particularly relevant in uveitic eyes, the predominant diagnosis in our cohort, where aberrant wound healing responses can compromise bleb function [31]. Midha et al. have further demonstrated that elevated IOP in the early postoperative period stretches the bleb, stimulating fibroblast proliferation and fibrosis. They advise prompt intervention, such as needling, if IOP rises into the high teens or above [32]. They draw a conclusion that needling revision in failing XEN gel stent bleb is a relatively safe and efficient procedure to restore filtration, with a durable IOP-reduction potential [33]. Importantly, evidence suggests that early intervention with needling can effectively restore aqueous outflow and improve long-term outcomes. In our series, needling proved to be a safe and, effective procedure, with 73.9% of treated eyes achieving an IOP ≤ 18 mmHg thereafter. And needling should be considered as a part of routine postoperative treatment.

The need for open revision surgery was primarily driven by significant subconjunctival fibrosis or encapsulation that could not be adequately addressed with needle revision alone. Twelve eyes (28.6%) in our study required open conjunctival bleb revision, which is similar to previously reported rates in the literature. For instance, studies by Evers et al. [34] and Sng et al. [27] reported comparable revision rates of 24% and 21%, respectively, in cohorts with uveitis-related glaucoma. We speculate that fibrosis was exacerbated by prior surgeries or the underlying inflammatory nature of the glaucoma subtypes included in this study (e.g., uveitic glaucoma).

In contrast to TE, the XEN-45 gel stent implantation involves minimal disruption of the subconjunctival and episcleral tissues. The elevated IOP observed immediately after surgery was attributed to potential obstructions of the stent by Tenon’s capsule, blood, or exudates [35, 36]. Our study has honed the surgical approach, reducing the surgical duration to an average of 12.07 ± 8.52 min, a significant improvement over previous studies [37, 38]. Shorter duration minimized intraoperative bleeding and rendered conjunctival dissection unnecessary, ensuring stable IOP levels from the very first postoperative day.

The comparison between the two procedures have been widely debated. In our study, IOP, medication reduction and success rate were comparable between AEC and AEO groups, with no significant difference observed at any time point, which is comparable to the conclusions of Tan et al. and Gallardo et al. [14, 39]. While Helwe et al. and Do et al. reported more favorable success rates, lower needling rates and favorable IOP control in AEO group and hypothesized that opening the conjunctiva and Tenon’s capsule allows for more consistent placement of the XEN-45 gel stent reducing the risk of occlusion at the expense of longer procedure time [40, 41]. While there was a higher incidence of early hypotony and inflammation in the AEO group compared to AEC which may be caused by increased tissue disruption and a higher chance of subclinical microfluidic leaks [40]. However, the comparative results may be underpowered due to the limited sample size and uneven group distribution.

In our study, two patients necessitated additional TE due to uncontrolled postoperative IOP, one of which was a male patient with post-traumatic angle recession glaucoma and narrow palpebral fissure. Mermoud et al. have identified angle recession as a risk factor for the failure of filtering surgeries, with fibrosis around the bleb area as a primary cause [42]. Lee et al. reported that higher height and larger central bleb extension were significantly correlated with better long-term success rates following surgical revisions, and a bleb with higher height suggested less scarring in the subconjunctival space, making it a predictor of successful outcomes [43]. In this instance, the narrow palpebral fissure may have constrained bleb expansion, resulting in increased scarring. The other patient, a 33-year-old female with a rheumatic condition, was on chronic oral glucocorticoid therapy. The exact cause of her failure remains unclear. We speculate that repeated needling procedures could have disrupted the subconjunctival positioning of the XEN-45 gel stent, potentially leading to partial amputation of the implant and contributing to its failure.

There are limitations to this study. First, this study may be limited by its retrospective nature, relatively small sample size and no control group to compare the XEN-45 gel stent with TE or glaucoma valve surgeries in SG patients. Surgery was performed by a single surgeon, experienced in the technique at a single tertiary center Second, this study is the non-randomized selection of surgical approach based on conjunctival status rather than disease severity may have introduced selection bias, limiting the comparability between the AEC and AEO groups. Third, attrition over the 12-month follow-up period, leading to a reduced sample size could potentially introduce bias. Our study included two pediatric cases (one aged 6 and one aged 11 years). Although both showed favorable postoperative outcomes in IOP reduction and tolerability, we emphasize that the safety and long-term stability of this procedure in younger patients require further investigation through larger studies with extended follow-up.

To confirm the efficacy and safety of XEN-45 gel stent for various types of glaucoma and to further compare it with other glaucoma surgeries, our research team is embarking on a prospective multi-center with larger and more heterogeneous cohort study. To further validate the efficacy and safety of XEN-45 gel stent therapy across various glaucoma types, our team is currently embarking on a larger prospective study.

Conclusion

This retrospective study provided data on outcomes of the XEN-45 gel stent for medically uncontrolled SG, demonstrating that XEN-45 implantation significantly reduced IOP and medication use, with an average follow-up of 13.74 months. Needling was required in 54.8% eyes with generally good outcomes. Early postoperative monitoring and timely management of the filtration bleb are critical to ensuring optimal outcomes. Ab externo technique of implantation was employed in our study, showing that IOP and medication reduction was comparable between AEC and AEO groups. Further research with larger cohorts and extended follow-up is essential to elucidate the long-time efficacy and safety of XEN-45 gel stent in complex SG patients.
