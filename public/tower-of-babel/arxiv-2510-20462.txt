Global bifurcation of solutions to elliptic systems with system and domain symmetries
Piotr Stefaniak

Abstract
. We study parameterized elliptic systems on symmetric domains with additional system symmetries. We prove the existence of continua of nontrivial solutions bifurcating from the constant branch determined by a critical point of the potential, without assuming nondegeneracy, via the degree for equivariant gradient maps. Our assumptions are formulated in terms of the right–hand side. When the domain is a compact symmetric space, the bifurcating solutions break symmetry at every nonzero level. Under additional assumptions on the right–hand side, the continua are unbounded.

Primary 35B32; Secondary 35J47, 35B06, 58J05

1.
Introduction

We study global bifurcations of weak solutions to nonlinear elliptic systems on domains with symmetries, where the systems themselves also carry symmetries. For a compact Riemannian manifold
M
(possibly with boundary) and a potential
F:\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
, we consider

(1.1)

-\Delta_{M}u=\nabla_{u}F(u,\lambda),

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, with Neumann boundary conditions when
\partial M\neq\emptyset
. The precise assumptions used throughout are collected in Subsection
3.1
.

To study global bifurcation we assume that, for each
\lambda
, the potential
F
has a critical point; thus there is a family of trivial (constant) solutions, and we look for continua of nontrivial solutions that emanate from it. The assumptions on (
1.1
) place the problem in a variational setting: we associate to (
1.1
) a
C^{2}
functional
\Phi:\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
whose critical points are weak solutions, with
\nabla_{u}\Phi
a completely continuous perturbation of the identity on a Hilbert space
\mathbb{H}
. This is the natural framework for the Leray–Schauder degree and the Rabinowitz alternative (see
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
) can then be used to detect global bifurcation via degree jumps.

However, the symmetries of the domain and of the system are inherited by
\mathbb{H}
and by
\Phi
. In the presence of symmetries the classical (nonequivariant) degree may lose sensitivity: for an equivariant completely continuous perturbation of the identity, the Leray–Schauder degree equals the degree of its restriction to the fixed–point subspace, see for example
[
5
]
. Thus only the fixed–point contribution is seen, while changes in the complementary directions are not recorded. This motivates the use of an equivariant degree, which retains symmetry information and can detect bifurcations missed by the classical degree; the cost is algebraic, as computations take place in an Euler ring of a compact Lie group rather than in
\mathbb{Z}
.

The critical point at the trivial branch is not assumed to be nondegenerate. In the literature, the degenerate case is often handled when the critical point is a strict local minimum/maximum, or more generally when the Brouwer degree of
\nabla_{u}F(\cdot,\lambda)
on a small ball is nonzero. Here we exploit system symmetries and use the degree for equivariant gradient maps to cover these cases and more: the equivariant degree may remain nontrivial even when the Brouwer degree vanishes, still forcing bifurcation; see Remark
3.1
for the discussion.

Computationally, the Euler ring is, in general, difficult to handle, since its multiplicative structure is not fully known. For tori (abelian groups), however, the situation is much better: explicit multiplication rules are available, see
[
6
]
. We therefore pass to maximal tori in the symmetry groups of the domain and of the system and carry out all computations in the Euler ring of their product (again a torus). The key tools are the tensor product of representations (Lemma
2.1
) and its decomposition into torus representations (Lemma
2.3
).

As a first step to study the bifurcation problem we identify the admissible parameter values - those at which continua of nontrivial solutions may bifurcate from the trivial family, see Lemma
3.3
. This follows from the implicit function theorem when the critical point of
F
is nondegenerate, and from the symmetric structure of Laplace eigenspaces in the degenerate case.
To locate actual bifurcation points we introduce an equivariant bifurcation index defined via the degree for equivariant gradient maps; as usual, nontriviality of this index yields global bifurcation (Theorem
3.4
). The heart of the matter is to show that this index is nontrivial in our setting (Theorems
3.5
and
3.7
).
If the assumptions of Lemma
3.3
are not satisfied, Theorem
3.9
provides a local–global alternative driven by the equivariant degree: when the degree cannot be defined at some parameter, a local bifurcation occurs; if the degree is defined on both sides and changes value, a global bifurcation occurs at an intermediate parameter.

We next turn our attention to compact symmetric spaces
M=G/H
. Exploiting the irreducibility of Laplace–Beltrami eigenspaces in this setting, we show that global bifurcation occurs at every candidate parameter value, with the possible exception of
\lambda=0
, and that at each nonzero level the bifurcating solutions break symmetry: their isotropy group is strictly smaller than
G
, see Theorem
3.14
. In particular, for
G=\mathrm{SO}(n)
and
H=\mathrm{SO}(n-1)
(so
M=S^{n-1}
), this yields bifurcation of nonradial solutions from the radial (constant) ones.

The last result concerns the structure of the bifurcating continua. In Theorem
3.16
we prove that, under assumptions on the eigenspaces of the Hessian of
F
and with
0
nondegenerate, every bifurcating continuum is unbounded. The key ingredient is Lemma
3.15
: highest weights of Laplace–Beltrami eigenspaces, combined with the spectral assumptions on the Hessian, determine which weights may occur in the kernel of the Hessian of the functional and which cannot. This, in turn, identifies the nonzero coefficients in the bifurcation indices and shows that their sum cannot vanish, thereby excluding one of the alternatives in the Rabinowitz alternative (Theorem
3.12
).

After this introduction, the article is organized as follows. In Section
2.1
we recall basic notions of representation theory for compact Lie groups, then restrict to torus actions and record the decomposition rules needed later. Subsection
2.2
reviews the Euler ring of a torus and establishes several algebraic identities used in the bifurcation-index computations. Subsection
2.3
summarizes the degree for equivariant gradient maps and its properties.
In Subsection
3.1
we introduce the elliptic system (
3.1
)–(
3.2
), list the standing assumptions (B1)–(B6) and set up the variational and symmetry framework. In Subsection
3.2
we analyze the bifurcation problem, deriving the necessary condition and proving sufficient conditions. In Subsection
3.3
we establish symmetry breaking for the bifurcating solutions. Finally, in Subsection
3.4
we specialize to compact Riemannian symmetric spaces, where we prove global bifurcation with symmetry breaking and the unboundedness of the bifurcating continua.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section we collect the basic tools used later in the paper. Most of the material is standard, but we also include several auxiliary lemmas tailored to our setting: in particular, we describe the decomposition of tensor products of torus representations and establish algebraic identities in the Euler ring that will be used in the bifurcation index computations.

2.1.
Elements of representation theory

Let
G
be a compact Lie group. An orthogonal
G
-representation is a pair
(\mathbb{V},\rho)
with
\mathbb{V}
a finite-dimensional real vector space and
\rho\colon G\to O(\mathbb{V})
a continuous homomorphism. We do not distinguish between the representation and its underlying space, writing simply
\mathbb{V}
. When clear, we denote the action either by
gv
or by
g\cdot v
, with
gv=g\cdot v=\rho(g)v
for
g\in G
,
v\in\mathbb{V}
. A
G
-representation is called irreducible if it has no nonzero proper
G
-invariant subspace (i.e., the only
G
-invariant subspaces are
\{0\}
and
\mathbb{V}
).

Two
G
-representations
(\mathbb{V},\rho_{1})
and
(\mathbb{W},\rho_{2})
are equivalent if there exists a linear isomorphism
T:\mathbb{V}\to\mathbb{W}
which is
G
-equivariant, i.e. such that

T\circ\rho_{1}(g)=\rho_{2}(g)\circ T
for all
g\in G
.
We denote this by
\mathbb{V}\approx_{G}\mathbb{W}
.

Let
\Gamma
and
G
be compact Lie groups. Let
(\mathbb{W},\rho_{1})
be a finite-dimensional orthogonal
\Gamma
-representation and
(\mathbb{V},\rho_{2})
a finite-dimensional orthogonal
G
-representation.
Put
N=\dim\mathbb{W}
and equip the orthogonal direct sum
\bigoplus_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{V}
with the
(\Gamma\times G)
–action

(\gamma,g)\cdot(v_{1},\dots,v_{N})=\rho_{1}(\gamma)\bigl(\rho_{2}(g)v_{1},\dots,\rho_{2}(g)v_{N}\bigr),

i.e. apply
\rho_{2}(g)
componentwise, then let

\rho_{1}(\gamma)
act on the list of components via its action on
\mathbb{W}
.
On the tensor product
\mathbb{W}\otimes\mathbb{V}
we take the product action

(\gamma,g)\cdot(w\otimes v)=(\rho_{1}(\gamma)w)\otimes(\rho_{2}(g)v).

Lemma 2.1
.

With the above notation, the orthogonal
\Gamma\times G
–representations
\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{V}
and
\mathbb{W}\otimes\mathbb{V}
are equivalent.

Proof.

Fix an orthonormal basis
(w_{1},\dots,w_{N})
of
\mathbb{W}
and define an isomorphism

T:\mathbb{W}\otimes\mathbb{V}\longrightarrow\bigoplus_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb{V},\qquad T(w_{j}\otimes v):=(0,\dots,0,\ \text{$v$ in the $j$-th slot}\ ,0,\dots,0),

extended linearly. From the definition of the
\Gamma\times G
–actions,
it easily follows that
T
is
\Gamma\times G
–equivariant.

∎

2.1.1.
Torus representations

We now restrict attention to torus actions, which will be used in the sequel.
Let
T^{r}
be a torus of rank
r
. For
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
denote by
\mathbb{R}[1,m]
the real two–dimensional orthogonal
T^{r}
–representation in which

e^{i\phi}=(e^{i\phi_{1}},\dots,e^{i\phi_{r}})\in T^{r}
acts on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as the rotation through the angle
\langle m,\phi\rangle
, where
\phi=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{r})\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
:

\mathbb{R}[1,m]=\Bigl(\mathbb{R}^{2},\ \phi\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}\cos\langle m,\phi\rangle&-\sin\langle m,\phi\rangle\\
\sin\langle m,\phi\rangle&\ \cos\langle m,\phi\rangle\end{pmatrix}\Bigr).

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
put
\mathbb{R}[k,m]:=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{k}\mathbb{R}[1,m]
. In the case
m=0
we write
\mathbb{R}[k,0]:=\mathbb{R}^{k}
with the trivial action.

As is standard (see, for example,
[
2
, Proposition II.8.5]
), every real irreducible
T^{r}
–representation is equivalent to
\mathbb{R}[1,m]
for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and
\mathbb{R}[1,m]\approx_{T^{r}}\mathbb{R}[1,m^{\prime}]
precisely when
m^{\prime}=\pm m
.
Consequently, every finite–dimensional orthogonal
T^{r}
–representation decomposes as follows.

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\mathbb{V}
be a finite–dimensional orthogonal
T^{r}
-representation. Then there exist
m_{1},\dots,m_{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\setminus\{0\}
and integers
l\geq 0
,
k_{0}\geq 0
,
k_{1},\dots,k_{l}\geq 1
such that

\mathbb{V}\approx_{T^{r}}\mathbb{R}[k_{0},0]\oplus\mathbb{R}[k_{1},m_{1}]\oplus\cdots\oplus\mathbb{R}[k_{l},m_{l}].

For
m=(m_{1},\dots,m_{r})\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and
n=(n_{1},\dots,n_{l})\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}
, by
(m,n)
we understand
(m_{1},\dots,m_{r},n_{1},\dots,n_{l})\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+l}
. This convention will be used throughout the paper.

The following lemma records the
T^{r+l}
–decomposition of a tensor product of torus representations.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathbb{W}
be a
T^{r}
–representation and
\mathbb{V}
a
T^{l}
–representation with

\mathbb{W}\approx_{T^{r}}\mathbb{R}[l_{0},0]\oplus\bigoplus_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\setminus\{0\}}\mathbb{R}[l_{m},m],\qquad\mathbb{V}\approx_{T^{l}}\mathbb{R}[k_{0},0]\oplus\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}\setminus\{0\}}\mathbb{R}[k_{n},n].

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{W}\otimes\mathbb{V}

\displaystyle\approx_{T^{r+l}}

\displaystyle\mathbb{R}[k_{0}l_{0},(0,0)]\oplus\bigoplus_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\setminus\{0\}}\mathbb{R}[k_{0}l_{m},(m,0)]\oplus\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}\setminus\{0\}}\mathbb{R}[k_{n}l_{0},(0,n)]

\displaystyle\oplus\bigoplus_{m\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\setminus\{0\},n\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}\setminus\{0\}}\bigl(\mathbb{R}[k_{n}l_{m},(m,n)]\oplus\mathbb{R}[k_{n}l_{m},(m,-n)]\bigr).

Proof.

By distributivity of
\otimes
it suffices to show, for
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}
,

\mathbb{R}[1,m]\otimes\mathbb{R}[1,n]\approx_{T^{r+l}}\begin{cases}\mathbb{R}[1,(m,n)]\oplus\mathbb{R}[1,(m,-n)]&m\neq 0,\ n\neq 0,\\
\mathbb{R}[1,(m,0)]&n=0,\ m\neq 0,\\
\mathbb{R}[1,(0,n)]&m=0,\ n\neq 0,\\
\mathbb{R}[1,0]&m=0,\ n=0.\end{cases}

For
m\neq 0
,
n\neq 0
, define
T^{r+l}
–equivariant maps

T_{\pm}:\mathbb{R}[1,(m,\pm n)]\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}[1,m]\otimes\mathbb{R}[1,n]

by

\displaystyle T_{+}(e_{1})=e_{1}\otimes e_{1}-e_{2}\otimes e_{2},\quad

\displaystyle T_{+}(e_{2})=e_{1}\otimes e_{2}+e_{2}\otimes e_{1},

\displaystyle T_{-}(e_{1})=e_{1}\otimes e_{1}+e_{2}\otimes e_{2},\quad

\displaystyle T_{-}(e_{2})=e_{1}\otimes e_{2}-e_{2}\otimes e_{1},

where
e_{1},e_{2}
is the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Their images are
T^{r+l}
–invariant, orthogonal, and
the direct sum of their ranges equals
\mathbb{R}[1,m]\otimes\mathbb{R}[1,n]
,
giving the first case. The remaining cases are immediate. Collecting multiplicities in the distributive expansion yields the stated decomposition.
∎

2.2.
Euler ring of a torus

Let
\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})
be the set of closed subgroups of
T^{r}
. For
H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})
let
T^{r}/H
denote the orbit space, and write
T^{r}/H^{+}
for the pointed space obtained by adjoining a disjoint base point. Let
\chi_{T^{r}}(X)
denote the
T^{r}
–equivariant Euler characteristic of a
T^{r}
–CW complex
X
. The Euler ring of the torus
U(T^{r})
is the free
\mathbb{Z}
–module generated by the elements
\chi_{T^{r}}(T^{r}/H^{+})
with
H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})
. The multiplication in
U(T^{r})
is induced by the smash product of pointed
T^{r}
–CW complexes.
For tori this product is explicit (see
[
6
]
): if
H,H^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})
and
H^{\prime\prime}:=H\cap H^{\prime}
, then

(2.1)

\chi_{T^{r}}(T^{r}/H^{+})\star\chi_{T^{r}}(T^{r}/H^{\prime+})=\begin{cases}\chi_{T^{r}}(T^{r}/H^{\prime\prime+}),&\dim H+\dim H^{\prime}=\dim T^{r}+\dim H^{\prime\prime},\\[2.0pt]
\Theta,&\dim H+\dim H^{\prime}<\dim T^{r}+\dim H^{\prime\prime},\end{cases}

with unit
\mathbb{I}=\chi_{T^{r}}\bigl((T^{r}/T^{r})^{+}\bigr)
and zero element
\Theta
.

Directly from (
2.1
) we get:

Corollary 2.4
.

Let
X,Y\in U(T^{r})
with
Y
a
\mathbb{Z}
–linear combination of terms
\chi_{T^{r}}(T^{r}/H^{+})
with
\dim H\leq r-2
.
Then
X\star Y
is again a
\mathbb{Z}
–linear combination of terms
\chi_{T^{r}}(T^{r}/H^{+})
with
\dim H\leq r-2
.

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
define

H_{k}:=\{e^{i\phi}\in T^{r}:e^{i\langle k,\phi\rangle}=1\}.

Arguing as in
[
13
, Remark 2.9]
, every
H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})\setminus\{T^{r}\}
is an intersection of codimension-
1
subgroups.
In the next lemma we show that any
H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})\setminus\{T^{r}\}
can be written as the intersection of exactly
\mathrm{codim}H
such subgroups
H_{k}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})
be of codimension
s
. Then there exist

k_{1},\dots,k_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\setminus\{0\}
such that

H=H_{k_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap H_{k_{s}}.

Proof.

Let

H=H_{l_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap H_{l_{p}}
, where
l_{1},\ldots,l_{p}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}\setminus\{0\}
.
Let
R\in M_{p\times r}(\mathbb{Z})
have rows
l_{1},\dots,l_{p}
. Then

H=\{e^{i\phi}\in T^{r}:R\phi\in(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{p}\}.

By the Smith normal form, see, for example,
[
15
, Theorem 6.6]
, there exist unimodular
P\in\mathrm{GL}_{p}(\mathbb{Z})
and

Q\in\mathrm{GL}_{r}(\mathbb{Z})
such that

D=PRQ=(D_{ij})_{1\leq i\leq p,1\leq j\leq r},\qquad D_{ij}=\begin{cases}d_{i}&\text{if }i=j\leq\rho,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}\quad d_{i}\geq 1.

Put
\theta=Q^{-1}\phi
. Since
P
is unimodular, the condition
R\phi\in(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{p}

is equivalent to
D\theta\in(2\pi\mathbb{Z})^{p}
, i.e.
d_{j}\theta_{j}\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
for

j=1,\dots,\rho
. Hence, using the
\theta
–coordinates,

H=\{e^{i\theta_{1}}\in S^{1}:e^{id_{1}\theta_{1}}=1\}\times\ldots\times\{e^{i\theta_{\rho}}\in S^{1}:e^{id_{\rho}\theta_{\rho}}=1\}\times T^{r-\rho},

so
\mathrm{codim}H=\rho
. Since
\mathrm{codim}H=s
, we conclude
\rho=s
.
For
j=1,\dots,s
set
k_{j}:=d_{j}(Q^{-1})^{T}e_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
, where
e_{j}
is the
j
-th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{r}
,
and note that
d_{j}\theta_{j}\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
iff
\langle k_{j},\phi\rangle\in 2\pi\mathbb{Z}
.
Hence
H=H_{k_{1}}\cap\ldots\cap H_{k_{s}}
.
∎

We now turn to intersections of the form
(H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}
, which will play a central role in the bifurcation index calculations. We begin by recording their dimension.

Lemma 2.6
.

If
n\neq 0
, then
\dim\big((H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}\big)=l+\dim H-1
.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.5
there exist
k_{1},\dots,k_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
with

H=\bigcap_{j=1}^{s}H_{k_{j}}
, where
s=\mathrm{codim}H
. Define

F\colon\mathbb{R}^{r}\times\mathbb{R}^{l}\to\mathbb{C}^{s+1},\quad F(\phi,\psi)=\bigl(e^{i\langle k_{1},\phi\rangle}-1,\dots,e^{i\langle k_{s},\phi\rangle}-1,\ e^{i(\langle m,\phi\rangle+\langle n,\psi\rangle)}-1\bigr).

Then
F^{-1}(0)=(H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}
. At any
(\phi,\psi)\in F^{-1}(0)
, the real Jacobian
F^{\prime}(\phi,\psi)
has rows

(k_{1},0),\ \dots,\ (k_{s},0),\ (m,n).

Thus, over
\mathbb{R}
,
F^{\prime}(\phi,\psi)
has rank
s+1
: the first
s
rows have rank
s
(since
s=\mathrm{codim}H
) and the last row has a nonzero
\psi
–part because
n\neq 0
. The implicit function theorem yields

\dim F^{-1}(0)=(r+l)-(s+1)=l+(r-s)-1=l+\dim H-1.

∎

Using the above lemma with (
2.1
), we immediately obtain:

Corollary 2.7
.

If
n\neq 0
, then

\chi_{T^{r+l}}\bigl((T^{r}/H\times T^{l})^{+}\bigr)\star\chi_{T^{r+l}}\bigl((T^{r}/H_{(m,n)})^{+}\bigr)\neq\Theta
.

We are now going to show that a sum of elements as in the above corollary is never trivial, provided
n\neq 0
. To that end we will need the following:

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
r,l\geq 1
, let
H,H^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})
and
m,m^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
,
n,n^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}
.

(1)

If exactly one of
n,n^{\prime}
is zero, then

(H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}\neq(H^{\prime}\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})}.

(2)

If
n\neq 0
and
n^{\prime}\neq 0
and

(H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}=(H^{\prime}\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})},

then
H=H^{\prime}
.

Proof.

(1) Assume without loss of generality that
n=0
and
n^{\prime}\neq 0
. Then

(H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,0)}=(H\cap H_{m})\times T^{l},

so in particular
\{e\}\times T^{l}\subset(H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,0)}
. On the other hand,

(H^{\prime}\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})}\cap(\{e\}\times T^{l})=\{e\}\times H_{(0,n^{\prime})},

which is a proper subset of
\{e\}\times T^{l}
because
n^{\prime}\neq 0
. Hence the intersections cannot be equal.

(2) Assume
n\neq 0
and
n^{\prime}\neq 0
and suppose

(H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}=(H^{\prime}\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})}.

Let
\pi_{1}:T^{r}\times T^{l}\to T^{r}
be the projection onto the first factor. For
n\neq 0
one has

\pi_{1}\big((H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}\big)=H.

Indeed,

(H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}\subseteq H\times T^{l}\Rightarrow\pi_{1}\big((H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}\big)\subseteq\pi_{1}(H\times T^{l})=H.

Conversely, for every
e^{i\phi}\in H
the surjectivity of
T^{l}\to S^{1}
,
e^{i\psi}\mapsto e^{i\langle n,\psi\rangle}
(since
n\neq 0
), provides
e^{i\psi}\in T^{l}
with
e^{i\langle n,\psi\rangle}=e^{-i\langle m,\phi\rangle}
, hence
(e^{i\phi},e^{i\psi})\in(H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}
, and applying
\pi_{1}
gives
e^{i\phi}\in\pi_{1}((H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)})
. Since
e^{i\phi}\in H
was arbitrary, this shows
H\subseteq\pi_{1}((H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)})
.
Similarly,

\pi_{1}\big((H^{\prime}\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m^{\prime},n^{\prime})}\big)=H^{\prime}.

Taking
\pi_{1}
of the assumed equality gives
H=H^{\prime}
, as claimed.
∎

The next lemma will be used to establish nontriviality of the bifurcation index in the next section.

Lemma 2.9
.

Consider the elements of
U(T^{r+l})
defined by

A=\sum_{H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})\setminus\{T^{r}\}}n_{H}\,\chi_{T^{r+l}}\bigl(T^{r+l}/(H\times T^{l})^{+}\bigr),\quad B=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+l}\setminus\{0\}}q_{(m,n)}\,\chi_{T^{r+l}}\bigl(T^{r+l}/H_{(m,n)}^{+}\bigr).

Assume all nonzero
q_{(m,n)}
have the same sign and that
q_{(m_{0},n_{0})}\neq 0
for some
(m_{0},n_{0})
with
n_{0}\neq 0
. If
A\neq\Theta
, then
A\star B\neq\Theta
.

Proof.

Fix
(m_{0},n_{0})
with
n_{0}\neq 0
and
q_{(m_{0},n_{0})}\neq 0
. Choose
H
with
n_{H}\neq 0
and set

K_{H}:=(H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m_{0},n_{0})}\subset T^{r+l}.

The coefficient of
\chi_{T^{r+l}}(T^{r+l}/K_{H}^{+})
in
A\star B
equals the sum of
n_{H^{\prime}}q_{(m,n)}
over all
H^{\prime}\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})\setminus\{T^{r}\}
and
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+l}\setminus\{0\}
with
(H^{\prime}\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}=K_{H}
. By Lemma
2.8
, this forces
H^{\prime}=H
, so the coefficient is
n_{H}\sum q_{(m,n)}
taken over all
(m,n)
with
(H\times T^{l})\cap H_{(m,n)}=K_{H}
. This sum contains
q_{(m_{0},n_{0})}
and all nonzero
q_{(m,n)}
have the same sign, hence the sum is nonzero; since
n_{H}\neq 0
, the coefficient is nonzero. Therefore
A\star B\neq\Theta
.
∎

2.3.
The degree

Let
\mathbb{V}
be a finite-dimensional orthogonal
T^{r}
-representation, let
\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{R})
be
T^{r}
-invariant, and let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{V}
be open, bounded,
T^{r}
-invariant with
\partial\Omega\cap(\nabla\varphi)^{-1}(0)=\emptyset
. Gęba has defined in
[
7
]
, for any compact Lie group
G
, a degree
\deg^{\nabla}_{G}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)
for
G
-equivariant gradient maps. Rybicki has extended this to the infinite-dimensional setting (see
[
20
]
): if
\mathbb{H}
is a separable Hilbert space being an orthogonal
G
-representation and
\Phi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{H},\mathbb{R})
is
G
-invariant with
\nabla\Phi(u)=u-\nabla\eta(u)
, where
\nabla\eta\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
is
G
-equivariant and completely continuous, then for any open, bounded,
G
-invariant
\Omega\subset\mathbb{H}
with
\partial\Omega\cap(\nabla\Phi)^{-1}(0)=\emptyset
one obtains a degree
\deg^{\nabla}_{G}(\nabla\Phi,\Omega)
. In this paper we specialize to
G=T^{r}
and keep the notation
\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r}}
in both the finite- and infinite-dimensional cases; context will indicate which one is meant.

We record the basic computational tool for
\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r}}(-\mathrm{Id},B(\mathbb{V}))
.
Write
B(\mathbb{V})
for the unit ball of
\mathbb{V}
and set
S^{\mathbb{V}}=B(\mathbb{V})/\partial B(\mathbb{V})
.
By
[
7
]
,

\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B(\mathbb{V})\bigr)=\chi_{T^{r}}\bigl(S^{\mathbb{V}}\bigr).

Combining this identification with
[
6
, Thm. 3.2]
yields the following theorem.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let
\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{R}[k_{0},0]\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\mathbb{R}[k_{i},m_{i}]
be a
T^{r}
-representation. Then

\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B(\mathbb{V})\bigr)=(-1)^{k_{0}}\left(\mathbb{I}-\sum_{i=1}^{s}k_{i}\chi_{T^{r}}(T^{r}/H_{m_{i}}^{+})\right)+\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{V}),

where
\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{V})
is a
\mathbb{Z}
-linear combination of terms
\chi_{T^{r}}(T^{r}/H^{+})
with
\dim H\leq\dim T^{r}-2
.

Since
\mathbb{R}[k_{i},m_{i}]
are even dimensional,
(-1)^{k_{0}}=(-1)^{\dim\mathbb{V}}
, hence, for
\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{R}[k_{0},0]\oplus\bigoplus_{i=1}^{s}\mathbb{R}[k_{i},m_{i}]
,

(2.2)

\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B(\mathbb{V})\bigr)=(-1)^{\dim\mathbb{V}}\left(\mathbb{I}-\sum_{i=1}^{s}k_{i}\chi_{T^{r}}(T^{r}/H_{m_{i}}^{+})\right)+\mathcal{O}(\mathbb{V}).

Remark 2.11
.

Let
\mathbb{V}
be a
T^{r}
-representation,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{V}
a
T^{r}
-invariant set, and
\varphi\in C^{1}(\mathbb{V},\mathbb{R})
a
T^{r}
-invariant function with
\partial\Omega\cap(\nabla\varphi)^{-1}(0)=\emptyset
. Viewing
\mathbb{V}
as a
T^{r+l}=T^{r}\times T^{l}
-representation by letting
T^{l}
act trivially, by the definition of the degree, if

\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r}}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)=a_{0}\chi_{T^{r}}(T^{r}/T^{r})+\sum_{H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})}a_{H}\chi_{T^{r}}(T^{r}/H),

then

\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r+l}}(\nabla\varphi,\Omega)=a_{0}\chi_{T^{r+l}}(T^{r+l}/T^{r+l})+\sum_{H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})}a_{H}\chi_{T^{r+l}}\bigl(T^{r+l}/(H\times T^{l})\bigr).

3.
Main result

In this section we state and prove the main results. We begin by introducing the system and the standing assumptions, then describe the variational structure and formulate the bifurcation problem under consideration. Next we establish global bifurcation and symmetry–breaking theorems. We conclude with the case of a symmetric space, where we prove a stronger structural statement on the unboundedness of the bifurcating sets of solutions.

3.1.
Setting and assumptions

We study bifurcations of weak solutions to

(3.1)

-\Delta_{M}u=\nabla_{u}F(u,\lambda)\quad\text{in }M\setminus\partial M,

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,
M
is a compact smooth Riemannian manifold and
-\Delta_{M}
is the Laplace–Beltrami operator. When
\partial M\neq\emptyset
we impose the Neumann boundary condition

(3.2)

\partial_{\nu}u=0\quad\text{on }\partial M,

where
\nu
is the outward unit normal vector. Thus (
3.1
)-(
3.2
) covers both cases, the boundary condition is void if
\partial M=\emptyset
.

We work under the following assumptions.

(B1)

M
is a compact smooth Riemannian manifold with a smooth isometric action of an infinite compact Lie group
G
, so that
-\Delta_{M}
is
G
–equivariant.

(B2)

\mathbb{R}^{p}
is an orthogonal representation of a torus
T^{r}
.

(B3)

F\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
is
T^{r}
-invariant in the first variable, i.e.
F(\tau u,\lambda)=F(u,\lambda)
for all
\tau\in T^{r}
,
u\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. There exist
C>0
and

s\in\begin{cases}[1,(n+2)/(n-2))&\text{if }n\geq 3,\\
[1,+\infty)&\text{if }n=2,\end{cases}

such that

|\nabla^{2}_{u}F(u,\lambda)|\leq C(1+|u|^{s-1})\quad\text{for all }(u,\lambda)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}.

(B4)

There exists
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
\nabla_{u}F(u_{0},\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. After the change of variables
v=u-u_{0}
we assume
u_{0}=0
.

(B5)

There exists a symmetric matrix
A
such that

\nabla^{2}_{u}F(0,\lambda)=\lambda A
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

(B6)

There exists
\delta>0
such that
B_{\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\cap(\nabla_{u}F(\cdot,\lambda))^{-1}(0)=\{0\}
and

\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r}}\bigl(\nabla_{u}F(\cdot,\lambda),B_{\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\bigr)\neq\Theta\in U(T^{r})

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Here
B_{\delta}(X)
denotes the open ball of radius
\delta
in a normed space
X
.

Remark 3.1
.

Assumption
(B6)
may appear technical, but it is easy to verify in standard situations. Following the argument of
[
13
, Remark 4.1]
, we note:

(i)

If
0
is a nondegenerate critical point of
F(\cdot,\lambda)
, then for
\delta>0
small the degree

\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r}}\bigl(\nabla_{u}F(\cdot,\lambda),B_{\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\bigr)

is nontrivial, hence
(B6)
holds.

(ii)

More generally, if the Brouwer degree
\deg_{B}\bigl(\nabla_{u}F(\cdot,\lambda),B_{\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{p}),0\bigr)
is nonzero (for example, when
0
is a strict local minimum or maximum), then the
T^{r}
–equivariant degree is nontrivial, so
(B6)
holds.

As noted in
[
13
, Remark 4.3]
(treated there for
r=1
; the same reasoning extends to any
r
), condition
(B6)
is not limited to these cases.

Moreover, when the original symmetry is
S^{1}
, we may enlarge the symmetry group by embedding
S^{1}\subset T^{r}
and letting the complementary torus act trivially. As in Remark
2.11
, nontriviality of the
S^{1}
–equivariant degree then lifts to nontriviality of the
T^{r}
–equivariant degree. In particular,
(B6)
covers the
S^{1}
condition
(a3.2)
from
[
13
]
; the converse generally fails, so
(B6)
is strictly more general.

Remark 3.2
.

The potential
F
may be symmetric under any compact Lie group
\Gamma
, not necessarily a torus. One could try to replace
(B6)
by nontriviality of the
\Gamma
–equivariant degree. However, nontriviality does not, in general, pass to a maximal torus under restriction and we would then have to work in the Euler ring of a product with
\Gamma
, whose multiplicative structure is generally not explicit.
For this reason we impose
(B6)
at the torus level requiring the
T^{r}
–equivariant degree to be nontrivial. This guarantees that later computations can be carried out in the Euler ring of a torus, where the structure is explicit.

We study weak solutions of (
3.1
)-(
3.2
) as critical points of an associated functional on a Sobolev space.
Let
M
be as in (B1). Denote by
H^{1}(M)
the Sobolev space with inner product

\langle v,w\rangle_{H^{1}(M)}=\int_{M}(\nabla v(x),\nabla w(x))\,dM(x)+\int_{M}v(x)\cdot w(x)\,dM(x).

Here
(\cdot,\cdot)
is the Riemannian inner product and
\cdot
is the Euclidean product on
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Set

\mathbb{H}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}H^{1}(M)
, with

\langle u,v\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\langle u_{i},v_{i}\rangle_{H^{1}(M)}
.

Weak solutions of (
3.1
)–(
3.2
) are in one-to-one correspondence with critical points of

\Phi(u,\lambda)=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{M}|\nabla u(x)|^{2}\,dM(x)-\int_{M}F(u(x),\lambda)\,dM(x),\qquad(u,\lambda)\in\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{R}.

By the standard argument (see
[
18
]
),
\nabla_{u}\Phi
is a completely continuous perturbation of the identity. A routine computation gives

\nabla_{u}\Phi(u,\lambda)=u-L_{\lambda A}u+\nabla_{u}\eta(u,\lambda),

where
L_{\lambda A}\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
is defined by

\langle L_{\lambda A}u,v\rangle_{\mathbb{H}}=\int_{M}\bigl(u(x)+\lambda Au(x)\bigr)\cdot v(x)\,dM(x),\qquad u,v\in\mathbb{H},

and
\nabla_{u}\eta(\cdot,\lambda)\colon\mathbb{H}\to\mathbb{H}
is compact with
\nabla_{u}\eta(0,\lambda)=0
and
\nabla_{u}^{2}\eta(0,\lambda)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. The operator
L_{\lambda A}
is bounded, self-adjoint, and completely continuous on
\mathbb{H}
.

By (B1)–(B3),
\mathbb{H}
is an orthogonal
T^{r}\times G
–representation with action
((\tau,g)\cdot u)(x)=\tau u(g^{-1}x)
for
\tau\in T^{r}
,
g\in G
,
u\in\mathbb{H}
,
x\in M
. With this action the functional
\Phi
is invariant and the gradient is equivariant:

\nabla_{u}\Phi((\tau,g)\cdot u,\lambda)=(\tau,g)\cdot\nabla_{u}\Phi(u,\lambda)\quad\text{for }\tau\in T^{r},\ g\in G,\ \lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

Let
\sigma(-\Delta_{M})=\{0=\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}<\cdots\}
be the spectrum of the Laplace–Beltrami operator on
M
(with Neumann boundary condition if
\partial M\neq\emptyset
), and write
\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})
for the corresponding eigenspace. Let
\sigma(A)
be the spectrum of
A
. Arguing as in
[
9
, Lemma 3.2]
,

(3.3)

\sigma\bigl(\mathrm{Id}-L_{\lambda A}\bigr)=\left\{\frac{\beta_{k}-\lambda\alpha_{j}}{1+\beta_{k}}:\ \alpha_{j}\in\sigma(A),\ \beta_{k}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})\right\}.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{0}(\lambda)=\ker(\mathrm{Id}-L_{\lambda A})
,

\mathbb{H}_{0}=\bigoplus_{i=1}^{p}\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(0)
and set
\mathcal{V}(\lambda)=\mathcal{V}_{0}(\lambda)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{\perp}
. By (
3.3
) and Lemma
2.1
,

(3.4)

\mathcal{V}(\lambda)\approx_{T^{r}\times G}\bigoplus_{\alpha_{j}\in\sigma(A)}\ \bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{k}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})\setminus\{0\}\\
\beta_{k}=\lambda\alpha_{j}\end{subarray}}\bigl(\mathbb{V}_{A}(\alpha_{j})\otimes\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})\bigr),

where
\mathbb{V}_{A}(\alpha_{j})
is the eigenspace of
A
with eigenvalue
\alpha_{j}
.

Set

(3.5)

\Lambda=\Bigl\{\frac{\beta_{k}}{\alpha_{j}}:\ \alpha_{j}\in\sigma(A)\setminus\{0\},\ \beta_{k}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})\Bigr\}.

Then, for
\lambda\neq 0
,
\mathcal{V}(\lambda)\neq\{0\}
if and only if
\lambda\in\Lambda
.

Let
\mathcal{W}_{0}(\lambda)
be the direct sum of the eigenspaces of
\operatorname{Id}-L_{\lambda A}
corresponding to negative eigenvalues and set
\mathcal{W}(\lambda)=\mathcal{W}_{0}(\lambda)\cap\mathbb{H}_{0}^{\bot}
. By (
3.3
) and Lemma
2.1
,

\mathcal{W}(\lambda)\approx_{T^{r}\times G}\bigoplus_{\alpha_{j}\in\sigma(A)}\ \bigoplus_{\begin{subarray}{c}\beta_{k}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})\setminus\{0\}\\
\beta_{k}<\lambda\alpha_{j}\end{subarray}}\bigl(\mathbb{V}_{A}(\alpha_{j})\otimes\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})\bigr),

which is finite-dimensional for each fixed
\lambda
. Moreover, for
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,

(3.6)

\mathcal{W}(\lambda_{0})=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\bigoplus\limits_{\lambda\in\Lambda\cap(0,\lambda_{0})}\mathcal{V}(\lambda),&\lambda_{0}>0,\\
\{0\},&\lambda_{0}=0,\\
\displaystyle\bigoplus\limits_{\lambda\in\Lambda\cap(\lambda_{0},0)}\mathcal{V}(\lambda),&\lambda_{0}<0.\end{cases}

3.2.
Global bifurcations

Consider the weak-solution equation for (
3.1
)-(
3.2
)

(3.7)

\nabla_{u}\Phi(u,\lambda)=0.

By (B4), the zero function is a solution for every
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
. Set

\mathcal{T}=\{0\}\times\mathbb{R}\subset\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{R}

and

\mathcal{N}=\{(u,\lambda)\in\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{R}:\nabla_{u}\Phi(u,\lambda)=0,\ u\neq 0\}.

We call
\mathcal{T}
the set of trivial solutions and
\mathcal{N}
the set of nontrivial solutions. Let
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})
denote the connected component of
\mathcal{T}\cup\mathcal{N}
that contains
(0,\lambda_{0})\in\mathcal{T}
.

A point
(0,\lambda_{0})
is a global bifurcation point if
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})\neq\{(0,\lambda_{0})\}
and either
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})
is unbounded in
\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{R}
or
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})\cap\mathcal{T}
contains a point distinct from
(0,\lambda_{0})
.
A local bifurcation from
(0,\lambda_{0})
occurs if
(0,\lambda_{0})
is an accumulation point of nontrivial solutions of (
3.7
).

For a
G
–representation
\mathbb{W}
denote its fixed-point subspace by
\mathbb{W}^{G}=\{w\in\mathbb{W}:\ g\cdot w=w\ \text{for all }g\in G\}
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume
(B1)
–
(B6)
and let
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
be such that
(0,\lambda_{0})
is a local bifurcation point of weak solutions of (
3.1
) and (
3.2
). If at least one of the following holds:

(N1)

\det A\neq 0
, i.e.
0
is a nondegenerate critical point of
F(\cdot,\lambda)
for all
\lambda\neq 0
,

(i)

\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})^{G}=\{0\}
for every
\beta_{k}\neq 0
,

then
\displaystyle\lambda_{0}=\frac{\beta_{k}}{\alpha_{j}}
for some
\beta_{k}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})\setminus\{0\}
and some
\alpha_{j}\in\sigma(A)\setminus\{0\}
.

In case (N1) the conclusion follows from the implicit function theorem together with (
3.3
). In case (N2) the same necessary condition is obtained by the argument of
[
11
, Lemma 3.5]
.

By the lemma, every bifurcation parameter
\lambda_{0}\neq 0
lies in
\Lambda
, defined in (
3.5
), so under the above assumptions
\Lambda
is the set of candidate local (and hence potential global) bifurcation values. The case
\lambda_{0}=0
is not covered by the lemma; we therefore include it among the candidates (it can arise only when
\beta_{k}=0
).

Let us now turn the attention to the necessary condition for global bifurcation.
Let
T^{l}
be a maximal torus of
G
. Since
G
is an infinite compact Lie group, we have
l>0
, see
[
2
, Chapter IV]
.
Fix the torus

\mathbb{T}:=T^{r}\times T^{l}
and
\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda
. By the necessary condition, there exists
\varepsilon>0
with
[\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon,\lambda_{0}+\varepsilon]\cap\Lambda=\{\lambda_{0}\}
. Hence there exists
\delta>0
such that

\nabla_{u}\Phi(\cdot,\lambda_{0}\pm\varepsilon)^{-1}(0)\cap B_{\delta}(0,\mathbb{H})=\{0\},

so the degrees
\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}(\nabla_{u}\Phi(\cdot,\lambda_{0}\pm\varepsilon),B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H}))
are well defined. Define the bifurcation index

(3.8)

\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\lambda_{0})=\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\nabla_{u}\Phi(\cdot,\lambda_{0}+\varepsilon),B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H})\bigr)-\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\nabla_{u}\Phi(\cdot,\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon),B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H})\bigr)\ \in\ U(\mathbb{T}).

The index
\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\lambda_{0})
measures the change of the
\mathbb{T}
–equivariant degree as
\lambda
crosses
\lambda_{0}
. By the Rabinowitz-type global bifurcation theorem
[
20
, Theorem 4.9]
, a nontrivial change forces global bifurcation from
(0,\lambda_{0})
. Thus we obtain:

Theorem 3.4
.

If
\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\lambda_{0})\neq\Theta\in U(\mathbb{T})
, then
(0,\lambda_{0})
is a global bifurcation point.

We will use the bifurcation index and Theorem
3.4
to study global bifurcations of (
3.7
).
The next result is one of the main theorems of this paper and provides a sufficient condition for global bifurcation at the values
\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Assume
(B1)
–
(B6)
. Fix
\beta_{k_{0}}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})\setminus\{0\}
and
\alpha_{j_{0}}\in\sigma(A)
, and set
\lambda_{0}=\beta_{k_{0}}/\alpha_{j_{0}}
.
Suppose that
\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k_{0}})
is a nontrivial
T^{l}
-representation.
If, moreover
(N1)
or
(N2)
holds,
then
(0,\lambda_{0})
is a global bifurcation point.

Proof.

For
\varepsilon>0
as above, set
\Phi_{\pm}(u)=\Phi(u,\lambda_{0}\pm\varepsilon)
and let

\mathcal{L}_{\pm}=\nabla^{2}_{u}\Phi_{\pm}(0)=\mathrm{Id}-L_{(\lambda_{0}\pm\varepsilon)A}
.
By the choice of
\varepsilon
, the degrees
\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}(\nabla_{u}\Phi_{\pm},B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H}))
, where
\delta>0
is sufficiently small, are well-defined.
By Theorem
3.4
, it suffices to show that the index
\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\lambda_{0})
from (
3.8
) is nonzero.

Arguing as in the proof of Theorem 3.1 in
[
13
]
, we obtain

(3.9)

\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\nabla\Phi_{\pm},B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H})\bigr)=\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl((\nabla\Phi_{\pm})|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}},B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H}_{0})\bigr)\star\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\pm}\!\mid_{\mathbb{H}_{0}^{\perp}},B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H}_{0}^{\perp})\bigr),

so the computation splits into the (possibly degenerate) block on
\mathbb{H}_{0}
and an isomorphism on its
\mathbb{T}
–invariant complement.

To compute the first factor, note that on
\mathbb{H}_{0}
we have

(\nabla\Phi_{\pm})|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}}(u)=\nabla_{u}F(u,\lambda_{0}\pm\varepsilon).

By homotopy invariance of the equivariant degree and (B6), since
\lambda_{0}\neq 0
, the degrees

\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r}}\bigl(\nabla_{u}F(\cdot,\lambda_{0}\pm\varepsilon),B_{\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\bigr)

do not depend on the chosen level. By (B6) there exist integers
n_{0}
and
\{n_{H}\}_{H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})\setminus\{T^{r}\}}
, not all zero and only finitely many nonzero, such that

\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r}}\bigl(\nabla_{u}F(\cdot,\lambda_{0}\pm\varepsilon),B_{\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\bigr)=n_{0}\,\mathbb{I}+\sum_{H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})\setminus\{T^{r}\}}n_{H}\,\chi_{T^{r}}\bigl((T^{r}/H)^{+}\bigr)\in U(T^{r}).

By Remark
2.11
, this lifts to

\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl((\nabla\Phi_{\pm})|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}},B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H}_{0})\bigr)=n_{0}\,\mathbb{I}+\sum_{H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})\setminus\{T^{r}\}}n_{H}\,\chi_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl((\mathbb{T}/(H\times T^{l}))^{+}\bigr)\in U(\mathbb{T}).

On the other hand, by
[
20
, Thm. 4.7]
,

\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\mathcal{L}_{\pm}\mid_{\mathcal{R}_{2}},B_{\delta}(\mathcal{R}_{2})\bigr)=\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B_{\delta}(\mathcal{W}(\lambda_{0}\pm\varepsilon))\bigr).

From now on we assume
\lambda_{0}>0
; the case
\lambda_{0}<0
is analogous.
Since
\lambda_{0}>0
, by (
3.6
) and (
3.9
),

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\lambda_{0})=

\displaystyle\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl((\nabla\Phi_{\pm})|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}},B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H}_{0})\bigr)\star\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B_{\delta}(\mathcal{W}(\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon))\bigr)

\displaystyle\star\bigl(\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B_{\delta}(\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0}))\bigr)-\mathbb{I}\bigr)

and since
\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B_{\delta}(\mathcal{W}(\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon))\bigr)
is invertible in
U(\mathbb{T})
(see, e.g.,
[
8
, Thm. 3.11]
,
[
12
, Thm. 2.1]
), the nontriviality of
\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\lambda_{0})
reduces to

(3.11)

\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl((\nabla\Phi_{\pm})|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}},B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H}_{0})\bigr)\ \star\ \bigl(\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B_{\delta}(\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0}))\bigr)-\mathbb{I}\bigr)\neq\Theta.

Since
\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k_{0}})
is a nontrivial
T^{l}
–representation, the tensor product
\mathbb{V}_{A}(\alpha_{j_{0}})\otimes\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k_{0}})
is a nontrivial
\mathbb{T}
–representation and consequently so is
\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})
. Moreover, there exists
\mathbb{R}[1,(m,n)]\subset\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})
with
n\neq 0
. Applying Theorem
2.10
to
\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})
yields

\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B_{\delta}(\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0}))\bigr)=(-1)^{q_{0}}\Bigl(\mathbb{I}-\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+l}\setminus\{0\}}q_{(m,n)}\,\chi_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathbb{T}/H_{(m,n)}^{+})\Bigr)+\mathcal{O}\bigl(\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})\bigr),

where
q_{(m,n)}\in\mathbb{Z}
with at least one
q_{(m,n)}\neq 0
for some
n\neq 0
, and where
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0}))
is a
\mathbb{Z}
–linear combination of
\chi_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathbb{T}/H^{+})
with
\dim H\leq\dim\mathbb{T}-2
.

To finish the proof, assume first that
n_{0}\neq 0
. By Corollary
2.4
, the codimension-1 part of the product in (
3.11
) is

n_{0}\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+l}\setminus\{0\}}q_{(m,n)}\,\chi_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathbb{T}/H_{(m,n)}^{+})+\bigl((-1)^{q_{0}}-1\bigr)\sum_{\mathrm{codim}H=1}n_{H}\,\chi_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\mathbb{T}/(H\times T^{l})^{+}\bigr)\in U(\mathbb{T}),

where
n_{H}\in\mathbb{Z}
(possibly all zero). Since there exists
(m,n)
with
n\neq 0
and
q_{(m,n)}\neq 0
, and codimension-1 subgroups coming from
T^{r}
appear in the second summand as

, this element is nonzero. Hence
\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\lambda_{0})\neq\Theta
.

When
n_{0}=0
,

\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl((\nabla\Phi_{\pm})|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}},B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H}_{0})\bigr)=\sum_{H\in\overline{\mathrm{sub}}(T^{r})\setminus\{T^{r}\}}n_{H}\chi_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\mathbb{T}/(H\times T^{l})^{+}\bigr).

Let
c_{0}
be the smallest codimension of
H
for which

.

If
\dim\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})
is odd, the coefficient of
\mathbb{I}
in

\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}(-\mathrm{Id},B_{\delta}(\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})))-\mathbb{I}
equals
-2
. Hence, using Corollary
2.4
, the codimension-
c_{0}
part of the product in (

-2\sum_{\mathrm{codim}H=c_{0}}n_{H}\chi_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\mathbb{T}/(H\times T^{l})^{+}\bigr).

By the choice of

this sum is nonzero, and consequently
\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\lambda_{0})\neq\Theta
.

Suppose now that
\dim\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})
is even.
Applying Lemma
2.9
with

A=\sum_{\mathrm{codim}H=c_{0}}n_{H}\chi_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\mathbb{T}/(H\times T^{l})^{+}\bigr),\qquad B=\sum_{(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}^{r+l}\setminus\{0\}}q_{(m,n)}\chi_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathbb{T}/H_{(m,n)}^{+}),

and using
q_{(m,n)}\neq 0
with
n\neq 0
as above, we obtain that the product in (
3.11
) is nontrivial. Hence
\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\lambda_{0})\neq\Theta
.
Using Theorem
3.4
we finish the proof.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

If
(N2)
holds, then
\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})^{G}=\{0\}
for every
\beta_{k}\neq 0
. Hence
\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})
is a nontrivial
G
–representation and, if

is connected, by
[

, Lemma 3.3]
, a nontrivial

–representation. Thus, under
(N2)
and for connected

is satisfied for all
\beta_{k}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})\setminus\{0\}
.

In the absence of the nontrivial

–representation assumption on

, a parity condition on the dimension of
\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})
suffices to force a nonzero bifurcation index. In particular, if

is odd, then global bifurcation occurs. Namely, we have the following:

Theorem 3.7
.

Assume
(B1)
–
(B6)
. Fix
\beta_{k_{0}}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})\setminus\{0\}
and
\alpha_{j_{0}}\in\sigma(A)
, and set
\lambda_{0}=\beta_{k_{0}}/\alpha_{j_{0}}
.
Assume that

is odd.
If, moreover,
(N1)
or
(N2)
holds,
then
(0,\lambda_{0})
is a global bifurcation point.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
3.5
, it suffices to show

(3.12)

\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl((\nabla\Phi_{\pm})|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}},B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H}_{0})\bigr)\ \star\ \bigl(\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B_{\delta}(\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0}))\bigr)-\mathbb{I}\bigr)\neq\Theta,

with the same notation as before.

By (B5) and Remark

there exist integers

and

, not all zero, only finitely many nonzero, such that

Since

If

) holds.

If

, let

be the minimal codimension for which some

-2\sum_{\mathrm{codim}H=c_{0}}n_{H}\chi_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(\mathbb{T}/(H\times T^{l})^{+}\bigr)\neq 0,

again proving (

∎

Remark 3.8
.

If
\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k_{0}})
is a trivial
G
–representation and
\dim\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})
is even, the above results can still be refined. This would require a careful treatment of the degree in
(B6)
and a detailed analysis of intersections of subgroups of a torus. The refinement is feasible but technical and adds little insight, so we omit it.

If the symmetry group
G
is finite, it contains no nontrivial torus. In this case we are effectively confined to the
T^{r}
–equivariant framework and computations in
U(T^{r})
. One can still detect global bifurcation as in the theorem above, but the simple separation via the coefficient of
\chi_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathbb{T}/H_{(m,n)}^{+})
appearing in one factor and not in the other for some

is no longer available. Consequently, one must examine the structure of torus subgroups more closely to enforce bifurcation, and the resulting criteria for global bifurcation become substantially more technical.

We conclude this section with the case where the criterion of Lemma
3.3
fails. In this situation there is no a priori “suspected” level, but one can still track the variation of the equivariant degree. If the degree cannot be defined at some
\lambda
(because nontrivial solutions accumulate at the trivial branch), then a local bifurcation occurs; if the degree is defined on both sides of a value and can be computed, a change in the degree necessarily signals a global bifurcation somewhere in between.

Let us formalize this. Arguing as in
[
11
, Theorem. 3.8]
and Theorem
3.5
and
3.7
, we obtain the following alternative.

Theorem 3.9
.

Assume
(B1)
–
(B6)
and fix
\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda
.
Let
\alpha_{j_{0}}\in\sigma(A)\setminus\{0\}
and
\beta_{k_{0}}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})\setminus\{0\}
satisfy
\lambda_{0}=\beta_{k_{0}}/\alpha_{j_{0}}
.
Assume that
\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k_{0}})
is a nontrivial
T^{l}
–representation or that
\dim\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})
is odd.
For every
\varepsilon>0
such that

(\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon,\lambda_{0}+\varepsilon)\cap\Lambda=\{\lambda_{0}\},

at least one of the following holds:

(i)

a local bifurcation of solutions of (
3.7
) occurs from
(0,\lambda)
for every
\lambda\in(\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon,\lambda_{0})
or for every
\lambda\in(\lambda_{0},\lambda_{0}+\varepsilon)

(ii)

a global bifurcation of solutions of (
3.7
) occurs from
(0,\widehat{\lambda})
for some
\widehat{\lambda}\in(\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon,\lambda_{0}+\varepsilon)
.

Remark 3.10
.

For
\lambda_{0}\neq 0
, in the proofs of Theorems
3.5
and
3.7
we use that the degrees

\deg^{\nabla}_{T^{r}}\bigl(\nabla_{u}F(\cdot,\lambda_{0}\pm\varepsilon),\,B_{\delta}(\mathbb{R}^{p})\bigr)

are independent of the choice of level
\lambda_{0}\pm\varepsilon
. For
\lambda_{0}=0
this invariance may fail. Following these proofs, it still follows that if

then global bifurcation occurs at
(0,0)
, provided the assumptions of Lemma
3.3
are satisfied.
If
0
is a nondegenerate critical point of
F(\cdot,\lambda)
for all
\lambda\neq 0
(i.e.,
(N1)
holds), then, by the linearization property of the degree, the question reduces to whether the numbers (counting multiplicities) of negative and positive eigenvalues of

have different parity, which is precisely to say that

is odd.
Finally, if the necessary condition of Lemma
3.3
fails, then bifurcation occurs as in Theorem
3.9
: locally for
\lambda
near
\lambda_{0}
, or globally at some
\widehat{\lambda}\in(\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon,\lambda_{0}+\varepsilon)
.

3.3.
Symmetry breaking

We now discuss (N2) and its implications for the symmetry of bifurcating solutions. We say that symmetry breaking occurs at a bifurcation point
(0,\lambda_{0})
if, in some neighbourhoods
U\subset\mathbb{H}
of
0
and
I\subset\mathbb{R}
of
\lambda_{0}
, every nontrivial solution
(u,\lambda)\in U\times I
of (
3.7
) satisfies
G_{u}\neq G
, where
G_{u}=\{g\in G:\ g\cdot u=u\}
denotes the isotropy group of
u
.

Theorem 3.11
.

Assume
(B1)
–
(B6)
and
(N2)
, and let
\lambda_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
be such that
(0,\lambda_{0})
is a bifurcation point of weak solutions of (
3.1
)-(
3.2
). Then symmetry breaking occurs at
(0,\lambda_{0})
.

Proof.

Assume, to the contrary, that there exists a nontrivial solution
(u,\lambda)
with
\lambda
near
\lambda_{0}\neq 0
and
G_{u}=G
. Then
u\in\mathbb{H}^{G}\subset\mathbb{H}_{0}
by (N2), so
u
is constant. By (B6), the only constant solution in a neighborhood of
0
is
u=0
, a contradiction. Hence every nontrivial solution near
(0,\lambda_{0})
has
G_{u}\neq G
, i.e., symmetry breaking occurs.
∎

Under (N2), when
\lambda_{0}=0
any bifurcation - if it occurs - is confined to
\mathbb{H}_{0}
, hence to constant solutions
u(x)\equiv u_{0}
with
\nabla_{u}F(u_{0},\lambda)=0
. Thus no symmetry breaking can occur at
(0,0)
. Indeed, by standard equivariant Lyapunov–Schmidt arguments (cf.
[
4
]
and
[
10
, Lemma 3.11]
), any solution
(u,\lambda)
near
(0,0)
has the same isotropy group as some element of
\mathcal{V}_{0}(0)
, and
\mathcal{V}_{0}(0)\subset\mathbb{H}_{0}
consists of
G
–invariant functions.

3.4.
Elliptic systems on a symmetric space

In this section we specialize (
3.1
) to compact Riemannian symmetric spaces.
Let
G
be a compact, connected, semisimple Lie group and let
H\subset G
be
the fixed-point subgroup of an involutive automorphism of
G
. Then

M:=G/H
is a compact Riemannian symmetric space endowed with its canonical

G
-invariant metric.

On such manifolds the eigenspaces of
-\Delta_{M}
are well understood, which
allows us to verify the assumptions of Theorems
3.5

and
3.11
and thereby locate global bifurcation points at
which symmetry breaking occurs. Moreover, using the algebraic description of
these eigenspaces as
G
-representations (irreducibility and highest weights),
we refine the global bifurcation theorem to obtain unboundedness of the
bifurcating continua. To that end, let us recall the following
\mathbb{T}
–equivariant
version of the Rabinowitz alternative (cf.
[
20
, Thm. 4.9]
),
together with its summation formula:

Theorem 3.12
.

If
\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\lambda_{0})\neq\Theta
in
U(\mathbb{T})
, then a global bifurcation of
solutions to (
3.7
) occurs from
(0,\lambda_{0})
. Moreover, if the
continuum
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})
is bounded, then the set
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})\cap\mathcal{T}

is finite and

(3.13)

\sum_{(0,\widehat{\lambda})\in\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})\cap\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\widehat{\lambda})=\Theta\in U(\mathbb{T}).

In particular, if the individual indices
\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\widehat{\lambda})

are known, (
3.13
) can be used to deduce that
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})

is unbounded whenever the sum in (
3.13
) cannot vanish.

It is known that for each
\beta_{k}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})
the corresponding eigenspace

\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})
is an irreducible
G
-representation, and eigenspaces
attached to distinct eigenvalues are nonequivalent, see for example
[
14
, Theorem 5.7.2]
.
With this in mind, we collect the basic spectral features of the
Laplace–Beltrami operator on a compact symmetric space.

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
M=G/H
be a compact Riemannian symmetric space. Then:

(1)

For each
\beta\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})\setminus\{0\}
, the eigenspace

\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta)
is a finite-dimensional, irreducible, nontrivial
G
-representation.

(2)

\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(0)^{G}=\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(0)
and

\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta)^{G}=\{0\}
for
\beta>0
.

(3)

For every
\beta_{k}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})
there exists
\nu_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}

such that
\mathbb{R}[1,\nu_{k}]\subset\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})
while

\mathbb{R}[1,\nu_{k}]\not\subset\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{m})
for any

\beta_{m}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})
with
\beta_{m}<\beta_{k}
.

In particular,
\nu_{k}
in (3) is the highest weight of the irreducible

G
-representation
\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})
; see for example

[
22
, Proposition 2.6]
.

As a consequence of Lemma
3.13
together with Theorems
3.5
and
3.11
we obtain:

Theorem 3.14
.

Assume
(B1)
–
(B6)
and let
M=G/H
be a symmetric space with
G
connected. Fix
\beta_{k_{0}}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{M})
and
\alpha_{j_{0}}\in\sigma(A)\setminus\{0\}
, and set
\lambda_{0}=\beta_{k_{0}}/\alpha_{j_{0}}\in\Lambda
.
Then, if
\beta_{k_{0}}\neq 0
, then
(0,\lambda_{0})
is a global bifurcation point at which symmetry breaking occurs.

We next ask when the continua from Theorem
3.14
are unbounded. For this we assume the following property of the eigenspaces of
A
:

(E)

For every
\alpha_{j}\in\sigma(A)
there exists
\mu_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
such that

\mathbb{R}[1,\mu_{j}]\subset\mathbb{V}_{A}(\alpha_{j})
and

\mathbb{R}[1,\mu_{j}]\not\subset\mathbb{V}_{A}(\alpha_{i})
for any
\alpha_{i}\in\sigma(A)
with
\alpha_{i}\neq\alpha_{j}
.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
M
be a compact symmetric space. Suppose that the eigenvalues of
A
satisfy
(E)
.
Fix
\lambda_{0}=\beta_{k_{0}}/\alpha_{j_{0}}\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
. Then:

(1)

If
\mu_{j_{0}}\neq 0
, then

\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},\nu_{k_{0}})]\oplus\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},-\nu_{k_{0}})]\subset\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0}).

(2)

If
\mu_{j_{0}}=0
, then

\mathbb{R}[1,(0,\nu_{k_{0}})]\subset\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0}).

Moreover, if
\lambda_{0}>0
, then for every
\lambda<\lambda_{0}
,

\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},\pm\nu_{k_{0}})]\not\subset\mathcal{V}(\lambda),

while if
\lambda_{0}<0
, the same conclusion holds for every
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
.

Proof.

Fix
\lambda_{0}=\beta_{k_{0}}/\alpha_{j_{0}}\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
. In particular,
\beta_{k_{0}}\neq 0
.
By (E) and Lemma
3.13
(3) there exist

\mu_{j_{0}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and
\nu_{k_{0}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}\setminus\{0\}
with

\mathbb{R}[1,\mu_{j_{0}}]\subset\mathbb{V}_{A}(\alpha_{j_{0}})
and

\mathbb{R}[1,\nu_{k_{0}}]\subset\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k_{0}}),

such that
\mathbb{R}[1,\mu_{j_{0}}]
does not occur in
\mathbb{V}_{A}(\alpha_{j})
for
j\neq j_{0}
and

\mathbb{R}[1,\nu_{k_{0}}]
does not occur in
\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})
for
\beta_{k}<\beta_{k_{0}}
.
Using the definition of
\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})
and
Lemma
2.3
, we obtain

\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},\nu_{k_{0}})]\oplus\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},-\nu_{k_{0}})]\subset\mathbb{V}_{A}(\alpha_{j_{0}})\otimes\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k_{0}})\subset\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})

if
\mu_{j_{0}}\neq 0
, while, if
\mu_{j_{0}}=0
, then

\mathbb{R}[1,(0,\nu_{k_{0}})]\subset\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0}).

To finish the proof, suppose that
\lambda\in\Lambda
and
\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},\nu_{k_{0}})]\subset\mathcal{V}(\lambda)
or
\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},-\nu_{k_{0}})]\subset\mathcal{V}(\lambda)
. Then, by Lemma
2.3
,
\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},\pm\nu_{k_{0}})]
must come from a tensor summand
\mathbb{V}_{A}(\alpha_{j})\otimes\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})
with
\beta_{k}=\lambda\alpha_{j}
and with
\mathbb{R}[1,\mu_{j_{0}}]\subset\mathbb{V}_{A}(\alpha_{j})
and
\mathbb{R}[1,\pm\nu_{k_{0}}]\subset\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})
. Since
\mathbb{R}[1,\nu_{k_{0}}]\approx_{T^{l}}\mathbb{R}[1,-\nu_{k_{0}}]
, the latter is equivalent to
\mathbb{R}[1,\nu_{k_{0}}]\subset\mathbb{V}_{-\Delta_{M}}(\beta_{k})
. Hence
\beta_{k}\geq\beta_{k_{0}}
and, by (E),
j=j_{0}
. Therefore, if
\lambda_{0}>0
(so
\alpha_{j_{0}}>0
), then
\lambda=\beta_{k}/\alpha_{j_{0}}\geq\beta_{k_{0}}/\alpha_{j_{0}}=\lambda_{0}
, and if
\lambda_{0}<0
, then
\lambda=\beta_{k}/\alpha_{j_{0}}\leq\beta_{k_{0}}/\alpha_{j_{0}}=\lambda_{0}
. This proves the claim.

∎

Combining Lemma
3.15
with the
\mathbb{T}
–equivariant Rabinowitz alternative and its summation formula (Theorem
3.12
) shows that the bifurcation indices along
\mathcal{T}
do not cancel, and hence the continua through the bifurcation points cannot be bounded. Let us make this precise in the following theorem.

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
M
be a compact symmetric space. Assume
(B1)
–
(B6)
and
(E)
. Suppose that
0
is nondegenerate. Then for every
\lambda_{0}\in\Lambda\setminus\{0\}
the point
(0,\lambda_{0})
is a global bifurcation point and the set
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})
is unbounded in
\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{R}
. If, additionally,
p
is odd, the set
\mathcal{C}(0)
is also unbounded in
\mathbb{H}\times\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

Throughout the proof we use the notation of Theorem
3.5
.
Fix
\lambda_{0}=\beta_{k_{0}}/\alpha_{j_{0}}\neq 0
. Without loss of generality assume
\lambda_{0}>0
; the case
\lambda_{0}<0
is completely analogous. By Theorem
3.14
,
(0,\lambda_{0})
is a global bifurcation point. We will show that the continuum
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})
is unbounded.

Assume, to the contrary, that
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})
is bounded. Then, by Theorem
3.12
, the set
\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})\cap\mathcal{T}
is finite and

(3.14)

\sum_{(0,\widehat{\lambda})\in\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})\cap\mathcal{T}}\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\widehat{\lambda})=\Theta\in U(\mathbb{T}).

Without loss of generality, assume
\lambda_{0}=\max\{\widehat{\lambda}:(0,\widehat{\lambda})\in\mathcal{C}(\lambda_{0})\cap\mathcal{T}\}

Fix
\mu_{j_{0}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
and
\nu_{k_{0}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{l}\setminus\{0\}
as in (E) and Lemma

(3).
By Lemma
3.15
,
\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},\nu_{k_{0}})]\subset\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})
and
\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},\nu_{k_{0}})]\not\subset\mathcal{V}(\lambda)

We are going to show that the coefficient of
\chi_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathbb{T}/H_{*}^{+})

Recall that (see (

\displaystyle\mathcal{BIF}_{\mathbb{T}}(\lambda_{0})=

\displaystyle\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl((\nabla\Phi_{\pm})|_{\mathbb{H}_{0}},B_{\delta}(\mathbb{H}_{0})\bigr)\star\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B_{\delta}(\mathcal{W}(\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon))\bigr)

\displaystyle\star\bigl(\deg^{\nabla}_{\mathbb{T}}\bigl(-\mathrm{Id},B_{\delta}(\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0}))\bigr)-\mathbb{I}\bigr).

By (

), since
\lambda_{0}>0
,

\mathcal{W}(\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon)=\bigoplus_{\lambda\in\Lambda\cap(0,\lambda_{0})}\mathcal{V}(\lambda).

Thus
\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},\nu_{k_{0}})]\not\subset\mathcal{W}(\lambda_{0}-\varepsilon)

\chi_{\mathbb{T}}(\mathbb{T}/H_{*}^{+})

.

Hence, using (

) and (

that is, the

is the multiplicity of
\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},\nu_{k_{0}})]
in
\mathcal{V}(\lambda_{0})
.

On the other hand, since
\mathbb{R}[1,(\mu_{j_{0}},\nu_{k_{0}})]
does not occur in any

, no other bifurcation index

. Hence the sum in (

is unbounded.

For
\lambda_{0}=0
, if

is odd, Theorem

implies that either

is unbounded or else
\mathcal{C}(0)
meets
