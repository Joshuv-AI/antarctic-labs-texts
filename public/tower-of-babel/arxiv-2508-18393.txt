A Group-Theoretic Perspective on the PPT and Realignment Entanglement Criteria
Tobias C. Sutter, Christopher Popp, Beatrix C. Hiesmayr

Abstract
Entanglement is a key feature in many quantum technologies, including secure communication protocols and quantum computing. However, detecting it in mixed quantum states remains a challenging task. While the PPT and realignment criteria are well-established tools for entanglement detection in general, and are especially effective in Bell-diagonal states, their connection to the underlying group structure of this state family has not been fully explored. In this work, we analyze the PPT and realignment criteria for Bell-diagonal states from a group-theoretic point of view. Our results demonstrate that the group structure of Bell-diagonal states provides a clear framework for analyzing and computing these two entanglement detection criteria. This unified perspective offers new insights into the mathematical and physical properties of entanglement in structured quantum systems, like the connection of the PPT criterion to experimentally accessible entanglement witnesses, thus opening a path for extending the group formalism to more complex quantum systems.



1
Introduction

Many quantum technologies, including quantum computing
[
1
]
, cryptography
[
2
]
, and teleportation
[
3
]
, rely on entanglement as a fundamental resource.
As these technologies advance, a deeper understanding of entanglement becomes increasingly essential for creating and manipulating resource states.
Still, one of the major open problems in quantum information theory is determining whether a given bipartite quantum state is separable or entangled
[
4
]
.
For mixed quantum states, which arise naturally in experiments due to decoherence, this so-called
separability problem
is provably hard when considering systems of arbitrary dimensions
[
5
,
6
]
.
Nevertheless, efficient solutions may exist for fixed local dimensions or particular state families.

In the special case of pure states of dimension
d_{A}\times d_{B}
, the separability problem has a conclusive and efficiently computable answer
[
7
]
.
Furthermore, it is also solved for general qubit-qubit (
d_{A}=d_{B}=2
) and qubit-qutrit (
d_{A}=d_{B}-1=2
) systems, because the PPT criterion
[
8
,
9
]
is necessary and sufficient for entanglement in these dimensions.
However, for higher dimensions (
d_{A}d_{B}>6
), the PPT criterion ceases to be necessary for entanglement, leading to the notion of PPT-entangled states
[
10
]
.
Physically, such states are relevant as they cannot be distilled to maximally entangled form using local operations and classical communication
[
11
]
.
More generally, there exist multiple other efficiently computable criteria for entanglement detection that are necessary
or
sufficient (cf.
[
4
,
12
]
).
They aim to approximate a solution to the separability problem in higher dimensions, albeit in a one-sided manner.
One prominent example is the realignment (or cross-norm) criterion
[
13
,
14
]
.

Besides the possibility of fixing the dimensions of the subsystems, a different way to obtain further results is to consider specific state families that bear desirable properties while still being experimentally relevant.
One such family of bipartite quantum states is Bell-diagonal states
[
15
,
16
]
.
From a physical point of view, they appear naturally by considering (generalized) Pauli errors.
Thus, they are intimately connected to applications like error correction and entanglement distillation by their mathematical group structure related to the Weyl operators
[
17
,
18
]
.

Regarding their entanglement structure, Ref.
[
19
,
20
]
found that the set of Bell-diagonal qutrit-qutrit states (
d_{A}=d_{B}=3
) contains a significant share of PPT-entanglement, namely about 6%.
This means that the PPT criterion is relatively ineffective for detecting entanglement in this subclass of states, being successful only for roughly 60% of all states (see Fig.
1
).
In contrast, the realignment criterion appears to be particularly effective in this dimension, detecting around 62% of all states as entangled.
Importantly, this involves about 10% of the states where the PPT criterion fails.
Further numerical results in Ref.
[
21
]
suggest that the group structure of Bell-diagonal states crucially affects the entanglement class.
This leads to the central question of how the properties of Bell-diagonal states are related to the performance of the PPT and realignment criteria.
Our contribution investigates this relationship analytically.

]
.
For qubits (

.

The work is organized as follows.
Sec.
2
formalizes the PPT and realignment criteria.
In Sec.
3
, we introduce Bell-diagonal states with a focus on their group properties inherited by the Weyl matrices.
We furthermore compute their Bloch vectors and find that they carry information about subgroups of the Weyl-Heisenberg group.
Based on this, we derive an explicit version of the realignment criterion in Sec.
4
, unveiling its dependence on the subgroups for Bell-diagonal qutrits in Sec.
4.1
.
Sec.
5
shows that the PPT criterion for Bell-diagonal qutrits can also be understood in terms of the subgroups of the Weyl-Heisenberg group.
We furthermore derive a corresponding NPT-witnesses for this subset of states, and prove a statement that can guide the search for PPT-entangled Bell-diagonal qutrits.
Finally, Sec.
6
summarizes the results and gives an outlook on future research directions.

2
PPT and Realignment Criteria

Let
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of linear operators mapping the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
into itself, and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}):=\{\rho\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})\,|\,\rho\geq 0,\mytr(\rho)=1\}
be the set of density matrices on
\mathcal{H}
.
For bipartite systems, we set
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
, for which
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
can be partitioned into the sets SEP and ENT that denote separable and entangled states, respectively.
Hence,
\mathrm{SEP}\cup\mathrm{ENT}=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
.
Any state
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
can be written as

\displaystyle\rho=\sum_{i,j=0}^{d_{A}-1}\sum_{k,l=0}^{d_{B}-1}\rho_{ik,jl}|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes|k\rangle\langle l|\;,

(1)

where
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})=d_{A}
and
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})=d_{B}
, and
\{|i\rangle\langle j|\}_{i,j=0}^{d_{A}-1}
and
\{|k\rangle\langle l|\}_{k,l=0}^{d_{B}-1}
constitute a basis of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
, respectively.

We define the partial transpose acting on the second subsystem, denoted by a superscript
\Gamma
, by its action on the basis states,

\displaystyle(|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes|k\rangle\langle l|)^{\Gamma}

\displaystyle:=(\mathbbm{1}_{A}\otimes T_{B})(|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes|k\rangle\langle l|)

\displaystyle=|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes|l\rangle\langle k|\;,

(2)

where
\mathbbm{1}_{A}
is the identity map on
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and
T_{B}
is the transposition map on
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
.
The action of
\Gamma
extends to general states of the form (
1
) by linearity.

The PPT criterion (also known as Peres-Horodecki criterion) is a powerful sufficient criterion for entanglement:

Theorem 2.1

(PPT Criterion
[
9
,
8
]
)
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
, and
\rho^{\Gamma}
be the partial transpose of
\rho
.
Then

\displaystyle\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0\Longrightarrow\rho\in\mathrm{ENT}\;.

(3)

If
\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0
, i.e., it is not positive semidefinite, we say it is NPT entangled and write
\rho\in\mathrm{NPT}
, and otherwise
\rho\in\mathrm{PPT}
.
This admits another partition of the space of bipartite density operators, namely
\mathrm{PPT}\cup\mathrm{NPT}=\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
.
By Thm.
2.1
it holds that
\rho\in\mathrm{NPT}\Longrightarrow\rho\in\mathrm{ENT}
, but the converse is only true for
d_{A}d_{B}\leq 6

[
9
,
10
]
.
Hence,
\mathrm{SEP}\neq\mathrm{PPT}
for higher-dimensional states, which crucially includes qutrit-qutrit systems with
d_{A}=d_{B}=3
.

The realignment criterion (also known as cross-norm criterion) is another sufficient criterion for entanglement.
It is based on the realignment (or shuffle) operation on
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
, which is defined by its action on the basis states as

\displaystyle\hat{R}(|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes|k\rangle\langle l|):=|i\rangle\langle k|\otimes|j\rangle\langle l|\;,

(4)

and extends to general
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
by linearity.
The realignment criterion reads:

Theorem 2.2

(Realignment Criterion
[
13
,
14
]
)
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
and define the realigned state
\rho_{R}:=\hat{R}(\rho)
.
Then

\displaystyle\mytr\left(\sqrt{\rho_{R}^{\dagger}\rho_{R}}\right)>1\Longrightarrow\rho\in\mathrm{ENT}\;.

(5)

Note that
\mytr(\sqrt{\rho_{R}^{\dagger}\rho_{R}})
corresponds to the trace norm of
\rho_{R}
(i.e., the sum of its singular values).
Computing the norm scales as
\mathcal{O}(\min(d_{A}^{4}d_{B}^{2},d_{A}^{2}d_{B}^{4}))
because
\rho_{R}
is a matrix of size
d_{A}^{2}\times d_{B}^{2}

[
23
]
.
The realignment criterion detects some PPT entanglement, even though it does not detect all NPT entangled states.
Hence, it is neither stronger nor weaker than the PPT criterion.

Furthermore, the realignment criterion can be expressed in terms of the correlation tensor
T_{a,b}=\mytr(\rho\,(G_{a}\otimes G_{b}))
, where
\{G_{a}^{A}\}_{a=0}^{d_{A}^{2}-1}
and
\{G_{b}^{B}\}_{b=0}^{d_{B}^{2}-1}
form orthonormal matrix bases of
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
and
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
, respectively.
The normalization condition is
\mytr(G_{a}^{A}(G_{a^{\prime}}^{A})^{\dagger})=\delta_{a,a^{\prime}}
and
\mytr(G_{b}^{B}(G_{b^{\prime}}^{B})^{\dagger})=\delta_{b,b^{\prime}}
.
With this, Thm.
2.2
can be expressed as
[
24
]

\displaystyle\|T\|_{\mytr}>1\Longrightarrow\rho\in\mathrm{ENT}\;,

(6)

where
\|T\|_{\mytr}=\mytr(\sqrt{TT^{\dagger}})
is the trace norm of
T
.

We conclude this section by noting three similarities between these two entanglement criteria.
(i) Both are based on permutations of the basis states.
(ii) The defining operations represent generally non-physical transformations of the state
\rho
: The transposition is a positive but not completely positive map, and realignment does not preserve hermiticity.
(iii) Specific other permutations of the basis elements, similar to (
2
) and (
4
), recover the criteria:
First, both are trivially invariant under exchanging
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, i.e., mapping
\rho\mapsto\mathds{F}\,\rho\,\mathds{F}^{\dagger}
where
\mathds{F}=\sum_{i=0}^{d_{B}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{d_{A}-1}|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes|j\rangle\langle i|
.
Furthermore, for the PPT criterion, we can equivalently take the partial transposition on the first subsystem
\rho^{T_{A}}
, acting as
(|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes|k\rangle\langle l|)^{T_{A}}=|j\rangle\langle i|\otimes|k\rangle\langle l|
on the basis states.
This leaves the criterion unchanged as the spectrum of
\rho^{\Gamma}=(\rho^{T_{A}})^{T}
is unaffected by transposition.
Regarding the realignment criterion, we note that
\mathds{F}_{A}\hat{R}(\rho)
,
\hat{R}(\rho)\mathds{F}_{B}
,
\mathds{F}_{A}\hat{R}(\rho)\mathds{F}_{B}
,
\hat{R}(\rho)^{T}
,
\mathds{F}_{A}\hat{R}(\rho)^{T}
,
\hat{R}(\rho)^{T}\mathds{F}_{B}
,
\mathds{F}_{A}\hat{R}(\rho)^{T}\mathds{F}_{B}
define different permutations of the basis states, where
\mathds{F}_{X}=\sum_{i=0}^{d_{X}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{d_{X}-1}|i\rangle\langle j|\otimes|j\rangle\langle i|
with
X\in\{A,B\}
are the flip operators on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
.
As the singular values of
\hat{R}(\rho)
are invariant under taking the transposition and/or multiplying it by unitaries from the left or right, these permutations again lead to the realignment criterion.

3
Bell-Diagonal States with Weyl-Structure

Let
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
with
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})=\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})=d
be the Hilbert space of a bipartite qudit system with equal local dimensions.
Maximally entangled states
|\psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
, i.e., pure states satisfying
\mytr_{A}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)=\mytr_{B}(|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|)=\frac{1}{d}\mathbbm{1}_{d}
, are called Bell states on
\mathcal{H}
.
A set of
d^{2}
linearly independent Bell states on
\mathcal{H}
is referred to as a Bell basis (of
\mathcal{H}
).
A particularly useful one is generated using the
d
-dimensional unitary Weyl-Heisenberg operators

\displaystyle W_{k,l}=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega^{jk}\,|j\rangle\langle j+l|

(7)

where
k,l\in\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
and
\omega:=e^{\frac{2\pi i}{d}}
, and, throughout this paper, variables taking values in
\{0,1,\dots,d-1\}
are understood as elements of
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}:=\mathbbm{Z}/d\mathbbm{Z}
, i.e., the ring of integers with addition and multiplication modulo
d
.
The matrices (
7
) satisfy the so-called Weyl relations

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} W_{i,j}W_{k,l}&=\omega^{jk}\,W_{i+k,j+l}\;,\\
W_{k,l}^{\dagger}&=\omega^{kl}W_{-k,-l}=W_{k,l}^{-1}\;,\\
W_{k,l}^{\ast}&=W_{-k,l}\;,\\
W_{k,l}^{T}&=\omega^{-kl}W_{k,-l}\;,\end{aligned}

(8)

where
W_{k,l}^{\ast}
,
W_{k,l}^{T}
, and
W_{k,l}^{\dagger}
denote the complex conjugate, transpose, and conjugate transpose of
W_{k,l}
, respectively.
Writing
W_{k,l}=Z^{k}X^{l}
with
Z=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}\omega^{j}|j\rangle\langle j|
and
X=\sum_{j=0}^{d-1}|j\rangle\langle j+1|
, the indices
k
and
l
in
W_{k,l}
can be identified with phase and shift operations, respectively.
Applying
W_{k,l}
to one half of the maximally entangled state
|\Omega_{0,0}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}|i\rangle\otimes|i\rangle
yields

\displaystyle|\Omega_{k,l}\rangle

\displaystyle=(W_{k,l}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d})|\Omega_{0,0}\rangle\;,

(9)

with which the set
\{|\Omega_{k,l}\rangle\}_{k,l=0}^{d-1}
constitutes a complete Bell basis
1
1

1

This is also called the standard or “magic” Bell basis due to its special properties given by the Weyl relations (
8
), and to distinguish it from the generalization considered in Ref.
[
20
]
. This generalization, however, lacks many desirable features of the standard Bell basis, e.g., the generating unitaries generally do not form a group.
.
Alternatively,
|\Omega_{k,l}\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\mathrm{vec}(W_{k,l})
, where the vectorization
\mathrm{vec}(\cdot)
is defined by its action on the computational basis states of
\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
by
\mathrm{vec}(|i\rangle\langle j|)=|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle
(which extends to general operators
M\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H})
by linearity).
The set of states that are diagonal in this basis, also known as Bell-diagonal states, is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{d}:=\left\{\rho=\sum_{k,l=0}^{d-1}c_{k,l}\,P_{k,l}\;\Big|\;\sum_{k,l=0}^{d-1}c_{k,l}=1,\;c_{k,l}\geq 0\right\}\;,

(10)

where
P_{k,l}:=|\Omega_{k,l}\rangle\langle\Omega_{k,l}|
are the projectors onto the Bell states (
9
).
Mathematically, equation (
10
) defines a simplex, which, due to the group structure inherited by the Weyl relations (
8
), is sometimes referred to as a “magic” simplex
[
16
]
.

3.1
Group Structure of
\mathcal{M}_{d}

Bell-diagonal states
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
possess an underlying group structure
2
2

2

An example of the following group-theoretic considerations for
d=3
is presented in App.
A
.
that is known to have a significant impact on their entanglement class
[
20
,
21
]
.
In Sec.
4.1
and
5
, we show that this group property is strongly related to entanglement detection via the PPT and realignment criteria for
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{3}
.
To formalize this, we can arrange the
d^{2}
coefficients
c_{k,l}
in (
10
) in a
d\times d
coefficient matrix
C=(c_{k,l})_{kl}
.
For example, for
d=3
, this matrix is given by

\displaystyle C=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0,0}&c_{0,1}&c_{0,2}\\
c_{1,0}&c_{1,1}&c_{1,2}\\
c_{2,0}&c_{2,1}&c_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}\;.

(11)

\mathbbm{Z}_{3}^{2}

.

.

Similarly, we can identify the
d^{2}
basis states
P_{k,l}
in (
10
) with points in a discrete phase space
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
(cf. Ref.
[
25
]
; see Fig.
2
) where the mapping
P_{k,l}\mapsto(W_{i,j}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d})\,P_{k,l}\,(W_{i,j}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d})=P_{k+i,l+j}
is an automorphism.
In particular,
P_{k,l}=(W_{k,l}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d})\,P_{0,0}\,(W_{k,l}^{\dagger}\otimes\mathbbm{1}_{d})
, and thus each
P_{k,l}\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
can be uniquely associated to one
W_{k,l}
.
We define the projective Weyl-Heisenberg group as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{d}:=\{W_{k,l}\}_{k,l\in\mathbbm{Z}_{d}}\cong\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}\;,

(12)

where we identify elements that differ only up to a phase
3
3

3

More precisely, let
\mathcal{V}_{d}:=\{\omega^{m}W_{k,l}\}_{m,k,l\in\mathbbm{Z}_{d}}
be the Weyl-Heisenberg group
[
26
]
with the abelian normalizer
\mathcal{N}_{d}=\{\omega^{m}\mathbbm{1}_{d}\}_{m\in\mathbbm{Z}_{d}}
. Then,
\mathcal{W}_{d}
is the factor group
\mathcal{V}_{d}/\mathcal{N}_{d}
.
Thus, there is a slight abuse of notation in (
12
): The elements of
\mathcal{W}_{d}
are not matrices
W_{k,l}
but rather equivalence classes
[W_{k,l}]
with
W_{k,l}\sim W_{i,j}\Longleftrightarrow W_{k,l}=\omega^{m}W_{i,j}
for some
m\in\mathbbm{Z}_{d}
.
.
Here,
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}=\mathbbm{Z}_{d}\times\mathbbm{Z}_{d}
is the direct product of two copies of the cyclic group
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}
.
Consequently, the structure of
\mathcal{M}_{d}
is inherited from the group structure of
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
, which is the central object of the following considerations.

Let
\mathcal{S}_{d}
be the set of
d
-element subgroups of
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
.
Each
S\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
gives rise to a line or sublattice in
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
via the coset
S(i,j)=\{(k,l)+(i,j)\,|\,(k,l)\in S\}
for some
(i,j)\in\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
(cf.
[
16
,
25
]
and Fig.
2
).
For
S\in\mathcal{S}_{d}
, we define the set of all cosets by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(S):=\{S(i,j)\,|\,(i,j)\in\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}\}\;.

(13)

As each point
(i,j)\in\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
appears exactly once per
\mathcal{C}(S)
, we call
\mathcal{C}(S)
a “striation” of
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
generated by
S\in\mathcal{S}_{d}

[
27
]
(cf. Fig.
3
).

:

\mathbbm{Z}_{3}^{2}

\mathcal{C}(S_{0,1})

The set of all cosets in
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{d}:=\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{d}}\mathcal{C}(S)

(14)

Bell-diagonal subgroup states
\{\rho_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{d}}
are highly symmetric separable states in
\mathcal{M}_{d}

[
16
]
.
For every coset
\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{d}
, they are defined by having
c_{k,l}=\frac{1}{d}
for
(k,l)\in\ell
, while all other
c_{k,l}
are zero, i.e.,

\displaystyle\rho_{\ell}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{(k,l)\in\ell}P_{k,l}\;.

(15)

They form the outermost separable states in
\mathcal{M}_{d}
, i.e., separable states with highest purity
[
16
]
, and the convex hull of all subgroup states is known as the (separable) kernel polytope.

3.2
Bloch Vector of
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
and the Discrete Fourier Transform

As the Weyl-Heisenberg operators (
7
) constitute a basis for the space of
d
-dimensional density matrices
[
26
]
, any bipartite qudit state can be written as

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{d-1}\beta_{ij,kl}\,W_{i,j}\otimes W_{k,l}\;,

(16)

where
\beta_{ij,kl}
are the elements defining the generalized Bloch vector (also known as correlation tensor).
Using
\beta_{ij,kl}=\mytr\left(\rho\,(W_{i,j}\otimes W_{k,l})^{\dagger}\right)
and
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\beta_{ij,kl}=\sum_{x,y=0}^{d-1}c_{x,y}\,\omega^{yi-xj}\,\delta_{i,-k}\delta_{jl}\;.

(17)

With this, we can write
[
28
]

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}B_{j,i}\,W_{i,j}\otimes W_{i,j}^{\ast}\;,

(18)

where we defined the (reduced) Bloch vector

\displaystyle B_{j,i}:=\beta_{ij,(-i)j}=\sum_{k,l=0}^{d-1}c_{k,l}\,\omega^{il-jk}\;.

(19)

The form of
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
in (
18
) can equivalently be derived by applying the Weyl twirl channel to the state (
16
) (see Ref.
[
20
]
).

Note that the Bloch matrix
B:=(B_{j,i})_{ji}
is obtained from the coefficient matrix
C=(c_{k,l})_{kl}
by a discrete Fourier transform (DFT) (cf. Ref.
[
28
]
).
In particular, with the unitary DFT matrix

\displaystyle F:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}\omega^{-ij}|i\rangle\langle j|\;,

(20)

we get
B=d\,FCF^{\dagger}
.
Consequently, the Bloch vector
B
of
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
carries information about the discrete phase space
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
and the Weyl-Heisenberg group
\mathcal{W}_{d}
: Coset probabilities in
C
get mapped to their respective annihilator subgroup cosets via the DFT (cf. Thm. 2.7.1 in Ref.
[
29
]
).
In other words, while
C
contains the immediate spatial distribution of
\rho
in the phase space
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
, the Bloch vector
B
contains its spectral information.

As preparation for the next section, we conclude here with a short discussion of the properties of
B
concerning the entanglement class of
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
, which is not affected by specific symmetry transformations of
C

[
16
]
.
For example, for prime dimension
d
, we can understand
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
as the affine plane
\mathrm{AG}(2,d)

[
30
]
, where these symmetries are given by the affine group
\mathrm{AGL}(2,d)
(acting as
x\mapsto Ax+b
with
A\in\mathrm{GL}(2,d)
and
x,b\in\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
).
However, as
B
generally changes under these mappings, the information about the entanglement class contained in
B
must be invariant under such transformations as well.

4
Realignment Criterion for Bell-Diagonal States

The realignment criterion (Thm.
2.2
) can be used to detect entangled quantum states, but it lacks a clear operational and geometric meaning.
In this section, we present the realignment criterion for Bell-diagonal qudit-qudit states
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
in a form that admits a clear geometric interpretation in terms of the Bloch vector:

Theorem 4.1
.

The realignment criterion for
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
is given by

\displaystyle\|B\|_{1}>d\Longrightarrow\rho\in\mathrm{ENT}\;,

(21)

where
B=(B_{j,i})_{ji}
is the Bloch vector of
\rho
as defined in (
19
), and
\|B\|_{1}=\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}\left|B_{j,i}\right|
denotes the entry-wise 1-norm.

Before we prove the theorem, two remarks are in order.
First, note that this theorem reduces the computational complexity of computing the realignment criterion for
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
compared to Thm.
2.2
.
Particularly, as
B
is proportional to the DFT of
C
, evaluating (
21
) scales only as
\mathcal{O}(d^{2}\log(d))
instead of
\mathcal{O}(d^{6})
for (
5
) (cf. Ref.
[
23
]
).
Second, (
21
) is equivalent to (
6
) if the correlation tensor is diagonal (as is the case for Bell-diagonal states; cf. (
18
)), in which case its trace norm reduces to the entry-wise 1-norm.
The extra factor
d
in (
21
) is due to the unnormalized Weyl matrices satisfying
\mytr(W_{i,j}W_{k,l}^{\dagger})=d\,\delta_{i,k}\delta_{j,l}
.

To prove Thm.
4.1
, we need the following lemma:

Lemma 1
.

The realigned operator of
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\rho)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k,l=0}^{d-1}c_{k,l}\,W_{k,l}\otimes W_{k,l}^{\ast}\;.

(22)

Its eigenvectors are
|\Omega_{i,j}\rangle
for each
(i,j)\in\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
with respective eigenvalues
\frac{1}{d}B_{j,i}=(FCF^{\dagger})_{ji}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k,l=0}^{d-1}c_{k,l}\,\omega^{il-jk}
, where
F
is defined in (
20
).

Proof.

Using (
7
) and (
9
), we write
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
as

\displaystyle\rho=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{d-1}c_{k,l}\,\omega^{k(i-j)}|i-l\rangle\langle j-l|\otimes|i\rangle\langle j|\;.

(23)

With (
4
), the realigned operator
\rho_{R}:=\hat{R}(\rho)
evaluates to

\displaystyle\rho_{R}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{d-1}c_{k,l}\,\omega^{k(i-j)}\,|i-l\rangle\langle i|\otimes|j-l\rangle\langle j|

(24)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k,l=0}^{d-1}c_{k,l}\,W_{k,l}\otimes W_{k,l}^{\ast}\;.

(25)

The eigenvectors of
W_{k,l}\otimes W_{k,l}^{\ast}
are the Bell states
|\Omega_{i,j}\rangle
in (
9
) with respective eigenvalue
\omega^{il-jk}

[
20
]
.
Hence, we obtain

\displaystyle\rho_{R}\,|\Omega_{i,j}\rangle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k,l=0}^{d-1}c_{k,l}\,\omega^{il-jk}|\Omega_{i,j}\rangle\;.

(26)

∎

Proof of Thm.
4.1
.

Using Lemma
1
, we can diagonalize
\rho_{R}
via the unitary
U=\sum_{k,l=0}^{d-1}(|k\rangle\otimes|l\rangle)\langle\Omega_{k,l}|
to obtain
U\rho_{R}U^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}B_{j,i}|i\rangle\langle i|\otimes|j\rangle\langle j|
, and thus

\displaystyle\mytr\left(\sqrt{\rho_{R}^{\dagger}\rho_{R}}\right)=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i,j=0}^{d-1}|B_{j,i}|\;.

(27)

Inserting this into Thm.
2.2
completes the proof.
∎

4.1
Realignment Criterion for Bell-Diagonal Qutrits (
d=3
)

Thm.
4.1
gives a geometric entanglement condition based on the Bloch vector of
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{d}
.
Because the Bloch vector
B
in (
19
) can be obtained by the DFT of the coefficient matrix
C
, it encodes the structure of the discrete phase space
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
.
In particular,
B
contains information about the subgroups of
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
.

To exemplify this, we present the explicit version of Thm.
4.1
for Bell-diagonal qutrit states
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{3}
.
After some simplifications using the subgroups
S\in\mathcal{S}_{3}
and the corresponding cosets
\mathcal{C}(S)
presented in App.
A
, (
21
) can be written as

\displaystyle\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{3}}\sqrt{6\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}(S)}\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\ell}c_{i,j}\right)^{2}-2}\;>2\;\Rightarrow\;\rho\in\mathrm{ENT}\;.

(28)

In words: First,
\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{3}}
sums over all subgroups
S\in\mathcal{S}_{3}
generating horizontal, vertical, diagonal, and anti-diagonal striations
\mathcal{C}(S)
; second,
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}(S)}
sums over all cosets
\ell
contained in the striation
\mathcal{C}(S)
; third,
\sum_{(i,j)\in\ell}
sums the coefficients
c_{i,j}
of each coset
\ell\in\mathcal{C}(S)
.
Consequently, the subgroup structure of
\mathbbm{Z}_{3}^{2}
contained in the Bloch vector (
19
) is crucial for entanglement detection in
\mathcal{M}_{3}
using the realignment criterion.

Furthermore, (
28
) entails a measurement procedure to obtain the realignment value of
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{3}
.
This requires projecting the state for each
S\in\mathcal{S}_{3}
onto the different coset subspaces using the projection operators in
\Pi(S)=\{\sum_{(i,j)\in\ell}P_{i,j}\,|\,\ell\in\mathcal{C}(S)\}
.
In this way, each subgroup gives rise to a positive operator-valued measure (POVM), corresponding to the striation
\mathcal{C}(S)
.

5
PPT Criterion for Bell-Diagonal Qutrits (
d=3
)

For bipartite qubit states
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{2}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{2})
, the PPT criterion (Thm.
2.1
) is necessary and sufficient for entanglement
[
9
]
, and it even holds that
[
31
]

\displaystyle\rho\in\mathrm{ENT}\Longleftrightarrow\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0\Longleftrightarrow\det(\rho^\Gamma)<0\;.

(29)

For general qudit-qudit states
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbbm{C}^{d_{A}}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d_{B}})
with
d_{A}d_{B}>6
, these equivalences fail and we get the weaker statement

\displaystyle\rho\in\mathrm{ENT}\Longleftarrow\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0\Longleftarrow\det(\rho^\Gamma)<0\;.

(30)

In this section, we show that for Bell-diagonal qutrit-qutrit states
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{3}
, the equivalence for the second implication in (
30
) is restored, namely,
\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0\Longleftrightarrow\det(\rho^\Gamma)<0
.
In contrast, even for Bell-diagonal qutrit states, the first implication cannot be promoted to an equivalence because of the existence of PPT-entangled states in
\mathcal{M}_{3}
, i.e.,
\mathrm{ENT}\cap\mathrm{PPT}\cap\mathcal{M}_{3}\neq\varnothing
.
Furthermore, our result underscores that the subgroup structure of
\mathbbm{Z}_{3}^{2}
plays a crucial role in distinguishing between
\mathrm{PPT}\cap\mathcal{M}_{3}
and
\mathrm{NPT}\cap\mathcal{M}_{3}
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{3}
and let
\rho^{\Gamma}
be the partial transpose of
\rho
.
It holds that

\displaystyle\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0\Longleftrightarrow\det(\rho^\Gamma)<0\;.

(31)

Explicitly, we have

\displaystyle\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0\;\Longleftrightarrow\;3\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{3}}\left(\prod_{(i,j)\in\ell}c_{i,j}\right)<\sum_{k,l=0}^{2}c_{k,l}^{3}\;.

(32)

The idea of the proof is to reduce the spectrum of the partial transposition of
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{3}
to the spectrum of a
3\times 3
matrix
A_{0}
with only one non-contradicting assignment of the signs of its eigenvalues if
\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0
.

Proof.

We use (
23
) to compute the partial transposition
\rho^{\Gamma}:=(\mathbbm{1}\otimes T)(\rho)
and apply the basis transformation
U=\sum_{i,j=0}^{2}(|i\rangle\otimes|j\rangle)\langle\Omega_{i,j}|
, finding that
\rho^{\Gamma}
is block-diagonal in the Bell basis, i.e.,

\displaystyle U\rho^{\Gamma}U^{\dagger}=\sum_{m=0}^{2}|m\rangle\langle m|\otimes A_{m}\;,

(33)

where we defined

\displaystyle A_{m}:=\frac{1}{9}\sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{2}\omega^{j(m-k)-i(l+j)}c_{k,l}W_{i,j}\;.

(34)

The hermitian blocks
A_{m}
are known to be unitarily equivalent for odd dimension
d

[
16
]
, and we have
A_{m}=W_{m,0}^{\dagger}A_{0}W_{m,0}
for
m\in\{0,1,2\}
.
Hence, each
A_{m}
has the same eigenvalues and thus the spectrum of
A_{0}
completely determines the spectrum of
\rho^{\Gamma}
.
Also, the multiplicity of each eigenvalue of
\rho^{\Gamma}
must be divisible by 3.
Furthermore,
\mytr(A_{0})=\frac{1}{3}\sum_{k,l=0}^{d-1}c_{k,l}=\frac{1}{3}
and, because
\det(\rho^\Gamma)=\prod_{m=0}^{2}\det\left(A_{m}\right)=\det(A_0)^{3}
, we have

\displaystyle\det(\rho^\Gamma)<0\Longleftrightarrow\det(A_0)<0\;.

(35)

The spectrum of
A_{0}
is restricted as follows:
Let us denote the minimal and maximal eigenvalues of a hermitian matrix
M
by
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(M)
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(M)
, respectively.
The qutrit Bell basis projectors
P_{k,l}=|\Omega_{k,l}\rangle\langle\Omega_{k,l}|\in\mathcal{M}_{3}
satisfy
\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(P_{k,l}^{\Gamma})=-\frac{1}{3}
and
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(P_{k,l}^{\Gamma})=\frac{1}{3}
for all
k,l\in\{0,1,2\}
.
Applying Weyl’s inequalities
[
32
]
to
\rho^{\Gamma}
for
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{3}
yields

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(\rho^{\Gamma})

\displaystyle=\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\left(\sum_{k,l=0}^{2}c_{k,l}P_{k,l}^{\Gamma}\right)

(36)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{k,l=0}^{2}c_{k,l}\,\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}\left(P_{k,l}^{\Gamma}\right)=-\frac{1}{3}\;,

(37)

and similarly
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\rho^{\Gamma})\leq\frac{1}{3}
, i.e., the spectrum of
\rho^{\Gamma}
and
A_{0}
is contained in
[-\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3}]
.

Now, let us show that only
\det(A_0)<0
(and thus
\det(\rho^\Gamma)<0
) is consistent with
\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0
.
The partial transpose of any
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
with
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{A})=d_{A}
and
\dim(\mathcal{H}_{B})=d_{B}
has at most
n_{-}=(d_{A}-1)(d_{B}-1)
negative eigenvalues
[
33
,
34
]
.
For
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{3}
we have
d_{A}=d_{B}=3
and thus
n_{-}\leq 4
.
By the multiplicity restriction on the eigenvalues of
\rho^{\Gamma}
, we conclude that either
n_{-}=0
or
n_{-}=3
.
The case
n_{-}=0
is equivalent to
\rho^{\Gamma}\geq 0
and
\det(\rho^\Gamma)\geq 0
.
For
n_{-}=3
we have
\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0
, and
A_{0}
has exactly one negative eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}<0
.
This leaves three distinct cases for the other two non-negative eigenvalues
\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{3}
of
A_{0}
.
First, if
\lambda_{2}=\lambda_{3}=0
, we obtain
\mytr(A_{0})=\lambda_{1}<0
, which contradicts
\mytr(A_{0})=\frac{1}{3}
.
Second, if
\lambda_{2}>\lambda_{3}=0
we have
\frac{1}{3}=\mytr(A_{0})=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
, and thus
\lambda_{2}=\frac{1}{3}-\lambda_{1}>\frac{1}{3}
.
But this contradicts that the spectrum of
A_{0}
is contained in
[-\frac{1}{3},\frac{1}{3}]
.
The third and only consistent case for
\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0
is
\lambda_{1}<0<\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
with
\det(A_0)=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}\lambda_{3}<0
.
Thus, we have shown that
\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0\Longrightarrow\det(A_0)<0
.
Together with (
35
) and the (trivial) implication
\det(\rho^\Gamma)<0\Longrightarrow\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0
, we get the equivalence (
31
).
Finally, the explicit formula (
32
) is obtained by computing
\det(A_0)
using (
34
).
∎

Thm.
5.1
has interesting implications regarding the geometric properties of the sets
\mathrm{PPT}\cap\mathcal{M}_{3}
and
\mathrm{NPT}\cap\mathcal{M}_{3}
.
To see this, let us define the boundary
\partial(\mathrm{PPT})
of PPT as states
\rho\in\mathrm{PPT}\cap\mathcal{M}_{3}
such that every
\epsilon
-neighborhood around
\rho
is neither contained in PPT nor NPT.
As
\det(\rho^\Gamma)
is a continuous function of the coefficients
c_{k,l}
, boundary states
\rho\in\partial(\mathrm{PPT})
satisfy
\det(\rho^\Gamma)=0
.
Using (
32
), this is equivalent to

\displaystyle\rho\in\partial(\mathrm{PPT})\;\Longrightarrow\;3\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{3}}\left(\prod_{(i,j)\in\ell}c_{i,j}\right)=\sum_{k,l=0}^{2}c_{k,l}^{3}\;.

(38)

Simple examples include the separable subgroup states
\{\rho_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{3}}
(cf. Sec.
3
).
We can reformulate this as a complete geometric characterization of the set
\mathrm{PPT}\cap\mathcal{M}_{3}
by tangential hyperplanes, i.e., entanglement witnesses, for which we define

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{NPT}}=\sum_{i,j=0}^{2}\kappa_{i,j}P_{i,j}\;,

(39)

\displaystyle\kappa_{i,j}=-c_{i,j}^{2}+\sum_{\{(i,j),(k,l),(m,n)\}\in\mathcal{C}_{3}}c_{k,l}\,c_{m,n}\;.

(40)

The explicit form of
\kappa_{i,j}
is presented in App.
C
.
If the coefficients
c_{k,l}
in
\rho
and
\kappa_{i,j}
coincide, it holds that
\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0\Longleftrightarrow\mytr(\rho\,W_{\mathrm{NPT}})<0
and
\rho\in\partial(\mathrm{PPT})\Longrightarrow\mytr(\rho\,W_{\mathrm{NPT}})=0
.
In this regard,
W_{\mathrm{NPT}}
can be considered a “PPT-optimal” entanglement witness.
We note, however, that
W_{\mathrm{NPT}}
can be the zero matrix if all
\kappa_{i,j}
vanish, as is the case for all subgroup states
\{\rho_{\ell}\}_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{3}}
; or it can become a positive operator, as is the case for the maximally mixed state with
c_{k,l}=\frac{1}{9}
for all
k,l\in\{0,1,2\}
.
Strictly speaking,
W_{\mathrm{NPT}}
does not define an entanglement witness in those cases.

The Weyl-covariant
[
35
]
positive map
\Lambda_{\mathrm{NPT}}
corresponding to
W_{\mathrm{NPT}}
via the Choi-Jamiołkowski isomorphism
[
36
,
37
]
is given by

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{NPT}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=3\mytr_{B}\left(W_{\mathrm{NPT}}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{A}\otimes\rho^{T}\right)\right)

(41)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{2}\kappa_{i,j}W_{i,j}\,\rho\,W_{i,j}^{\dagger}\;,

(42)

where
\mytr_{B}(\cdot)
denotes the partial trace over the second subsystem.

Lastly, using Thm.
5.1
and the fact that subgroup states are separable
[
16
]
, we arrive at the following statement
(the proof is presented in App.
B
):

Proposition 5.1
.

There is no PPT-entangled
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{3}
with coefficients
c_{k,l}
corresponding to at least one coset
\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{3}
equal to zero.

This serves as a good guiding principle for finding PPT-entangled states in
\mathcal{M}_{3}
.
For example, the Bell-diagonal states that are defined by the coefficient matrix

\displaystyle C=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{3}-x&\frac{1}{3}-x&\frac{1}{3}-x\\
2x&x&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\;,

(43)

with
x\in[0,1/3]
, satisfy the premise of the proposition and are separable for
x=0
(reducing to case 3b in App.
B
) and NPT-entangled for
x\in(0,1/3]
(cf. case 5 in App.
B
).
On the contrary, Ref.
[
38
]
considered allegedly similar-looking states that are (up to the symmetries of
\mathcal{M}_{3}
; cf. Sec.
3.2
) equivalent to

\displaystyle C=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{3}-x&\frac{1}{3}-x&\frac{1}{3}-x\\
2x&0&0\\
x&0&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

(44)

These states do not satisfy the requirement of the proposition as no coset
\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{3}
has coefficients
c_{k,l}
equal to zero.
Using (
28
) and (
32
), it can be checked that this state is PPT-entangled for
x\in(0,\frac{2}{15}]
.

6
Conclusion and Outlook

The goal of this work was to answer the question of how the group structure of Bell-diagonal states relates to the PPT and realignment criteria.
To this end, we leveraged the symmetry properties of this state family to provide a unified approach connecting these criteria to the group-theoretic features of the system.

Thm.
4.1
shows that the realignment criterion for Bell-diagonal states is strongly linked to the geometry of the Bloch ball.
As the Bloch vector of Bell-diagonal states is obtained by the discrete Fourier transform of their defining coefficient matrix, it contains information about the subgroups of
\mathbbm{Z}_{d}^{2}
.
For the specific case of Bell-diagonal qutrits, we find that in the 8-dimensional coefficient space of
c_{k,l}
, the inequality for the realignment criterion is at most quadratic in
c_{k,l}
.
In contrast, the PPT set is defined by a cubic inequality by Thm.
5.1
.
Additionally, this explicit version of the PPT criterion for Bell-diagonal qutrit states
\rho\in\mathcal{M}_{3}
unveils that the equivalence
\det(\rho^\Gamma)<0\Longleftrightarrow\rho^{\Gamma}\ngeq 0
holds, which is generally not valid for all states of the same dimension.
Consequently, our results not only demonstrate that the group structure of Bell-diagonal states simplifies the analysis of two specific entanglement detection criteria but also provide deeper insights into the mathematical and physical properties of this state family.

While our analysis focuses on Bell-diagonal states and is limited to
d=3
for the PPT criterion, adapting this framework to other dimensions or more general classes of quantum states remains an open challenge.
Extending our treatment of the PPT criterion for Bell-diagonal qudits for
d>3
is not straightforward, as the spectral properties of
U\,\rho^{\Gamma}\,U^{\dagger}
do not satisfy the requirements used in the proof of Thm.
5.1
.

Going forward, it is furthermore an open problem whether for general bipartite qudit states, an entanglement criterion based on the entry-wise 1-norm of the Bloch vector, similar to Thm.
4.1
, can be derived.
Ref.
[
39
,
40
]
present a sufficient criterion for separability, reading
\|\beta\|_{1}\leq 2\Longrightarrow\rho\in\mathrm{SEP}
for
\beta
as in (
16
), but to the authors’ knowledge, no corresponding sufficient entanglement criterion exists.
A naive approximation using
\|\beta\|_{1}\leq d^{2}\,\|\beta\|_{2}
for
\beta\in\mathbbm{C}^{d^{4}}
yields that
\rho
is entangled if
\|\beta\|_{1}>d^{3}
.
However, the maximal value for pure states
|\psi\rangle\in\mathbbm{C}^{d}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{d}
is also
\|\beta\|_{1}=d^{3}
.
A quick numerical optimization for
d=3
yields
\|\beta\|_{1}\lesssim 25.9735
for general pure states
|\psi\rangle\in\mathbbm{C}^{3}\otimes\mathbbm{C}^{3}
, whereas separable pure states seem to satisfy
\|\beta\|_{1}\leq 25
.
The discrepancy of
25.9735<27=3^{3}
stems from the fact that equality in
\|\beta\|_{1}\leq d^{2}\,\|\beta\|_{2}
holds if and only if
\beta
and the vector
(1,\dots,1)^{T}
are linearly dependent.
However, this cannot be as
\frac{1}{d^{2}}\sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{d-1}W_{i,j}\otimes W_{k,l}
does not represent a valid quantum state.

To conclude, by bridging the gap between group theory and entanglement detection, this work provides a novel perspective on the structure of quantum states, specifically for qutrit-qutrit systems, paving the way for further advancements in quantum information science.

Appendix B

5.1

that preserve the entanglement class of

[

,
25

c_{k,l}\neq 0

c_{k,l}>0

\sum_{k,l=0}^{2}c_{k,l}=1

\mathcal{M}_{3}
, we can shift any one
c_{k,l}
to
c_{0,0}

\displaystyle C_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\

0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\;,

(56)

for which (
32
) yields
0<1
, and thus
\rho\in\mathrm{NPT}
.

Case 2:

If two
c_{k,l}>0
, we can locally shift one coefficient
c_{k,l}
to
c_{0,0}
and the other one to
c_{0,1}
, so this case reduces to

\displaystyle C_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0,0}&c_{0,1}&0\\

0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\;.

(57)

Equation (
32
) becomes
0<c_{0,0}^{3}+c_{0,1}^{3}
, which is satisfied.

\rho\in\mathrm{NPT}
.

Case 3:

Three coefficients
c_{k,l}>0
reduce to two distinct cases after utilizing the symmetries of
\mathcal{M}_{3}
.
The first one is given by

\displaystyle C_{3a}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0,0}&c_{0,1}&0\\
c_{1,0}&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\;,

(58)

with (
32
) being
0<c_{0,0}^{3}+c_{0,1}^{3}+c_{1,0}^{3}

\displaystyle C_{3b}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0,0}&c_{0,1}&c_{0,2}\\

[

]

Case 4:

c_{k,l}>0

\displaystyle C_{4a}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0,0}&c_{0,1}&c_{0,2}\\

\displaystyle\underbrace{3\,c_{0,0}\,c_{0,1}\,c_{0,2}-c_{0,0}^{3}-c_{0,1}^{3}-c_{0,2}^{3}}_{\leq 0\text{ by \eqref{eq:det_A_0_3ckl}}}\;\underbrace{-c_{1,0}^{3}}_{<0}<0\;,

\displaystyle C_{4b}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0,0}&c_{0,1}&0\\

(63)

\rho\in\mathrm{NPT}

c_{k,l}
to either (
61
) or (
63

\mathcal{M}_{3}
, the only possibility with one complete line of zeros is

\displaystyle C_{5}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0,0}&c_{0,1}&c_{0,2}\\
c_{1,0}&c_{1,1}&0\\

(64)

This gives

\displaystyle\underbrace{3\,c_{0,0}\,c_{0,1}\,c_{0,2}-c_{0,0}^{3}-c_{0,1}^{3}-c_{0,2}^{3}}_{\leq 0\text{ by \eqref{eq:det_A_0_3ckl}}}\;\underbrace{-c_{1,0}^{3}-c_{1,1}^{3}}_{<0}<0\;,

Accounting for the symmetries of
\mathcal{M}_{3}

\displaystyle C_{6}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{0,0}&c_{0,1}&c_{0,2}\\

\displaystyle\hskip 17.07164pt+\,\underbrace{3\,c_{1,0}\,c_{1,1}\,c_{1,2}-c_{1,0}^{3}-c_{1,1}^{3}-c_{1,2}^{3}}_{\leq 0\text{ by \eqref{eq:det_A_0_3ckl}}}<0\;.
