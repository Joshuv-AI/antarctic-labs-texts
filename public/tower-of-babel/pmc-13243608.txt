Suicide literacy and its association with attitude towards seeking professional psychological help among Iranian nursing students
Authors: Hossein Namdar Areshtanab, Farnaz Ramani, Araz Jadidoleslami, Hadiseh Tabrizi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDue to high educational and clinical stress, nursing students face mental issues. Suicide literacy is defined as a set of knowledge and skills to recognize, assess, and appropriately respond to suicidal thoughts. However, there is ongoing debate regarding nursing students’ attitudes toward seeking professional psychological help and the extent to which they actually utilize these mental health services. The objective of this study was to examine suicide literacy and its association with attitude towards seeking professional psychological help among nursing students. A descriptive design conducted on 500 nursing students in Tabriz faculty of nursing and midwifery, Iran in 2025. A stratified sampling approach selected the participants. Tools for data gathering comprised a demographic-social questionnaire and the literacy of suicide scale (LOSS), and attitude towards seeking professional psychological help (ATSPPH-SF). Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Pearson correlation, and multiple linear regression to assess relationships and identify predictors of help‑seeking attitudes. The findings of the research indicated that the student`s the mean Literacy of Suicide and ATSPPH scores was12.61 ± 4.51 (range: 0–26) and 16.31 ± 3.75 (range: 0–30), respectively. Suicide literacy was significantly and positively correlated with attitudes toward seeking professional psychological help (r = 0.185, p ≤ 0.001), indicating that higher levels of knowledge were associated with more favorable attitudes. In the multiple linear regression model, suicide literacy and gender remained a significant predictor of help‑seeking attitudes), explaining a modest proportion of variance in attitudes (adjusted R² = 0.119). Although nursing students’ suicide literacy and help‑seeking attitudes were generally low, greater suicide literacy was associated with more positive attitudes toward professional help‑seeking. These findings suggest that educational interventions targeting suicide knowledge may support improved readiness to seek psychological support. Future studies using longitudinal designs are recommended to clarify causal pathways and examine additional predictors such as stigma and perceived social support.Keywords: Attitude, Help-seeking behavior, Mental health, Nursing students, Suicide literacySubject terms: Health care, Health occupations, Medical research, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Mental health is a key determinant of holistic health that affects all aspects of life, especially among students. Research shows that approximately 35% of students experience at least one mental disorder during their lifetime1. Entering university brings about major social, family, and individual changes and is considered a sensitive period that can lead to problems such as social isolation, substance abuse, anxiety, depression, and suicidal thoughts2. Among 15–29-year-olds, suicide ranks as the third most common cause of death. The global burden of suicide falls disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries, accounting for 73% of cases3. Furthermore, research indicates that 22.3% of college students experience suicidal thoughts, with 3.2% attempting suicide during their lifetime4. A study showed that the prevalence of suicidal behaviors among Iranian university students is 95.57%5. Among university students, nursing students, due to their clinical nursing training, are exposed directly to critical patients, the risk of infection, fear of error, and evaluation anxiety, leading to severe stress, increased anxiety, and emotional instability6. Nursing students, both as learners in the health system and as future health care providers, are on the front lines of identifying and managing individuals who are at risk of suicide or suicidal thoughts and require appropriate knowledge and attitudes regarding suicide and psychological help-seeking7. If nursing students, as future nurses, are aware of suicidal thoughts or attempts in advance, they can take steps to increase their own and patients’ safety in the early stages. Suicide literacy is defined as understanding the causes of suicide, risk factors, warning signs/symptoms, and appropriate treatment and prevention. It functions as a foundational knowledge construct that modifies perceptions and beliefs about suicide and mental health services8. Inadequate recognition of suicidal signs and symptoms can result in a failure to apprehend the seriousness of the risk or a minimization of professional help’s importance9. Young people, in particular, often refrain from seeking such assistance, motivated by apprehension about disclosing vulnerability and prevailing negative attitudes toward professional psychological support10. Stigma associated with mental disorders, suicidal behavior, and psychological services significantly contributes to insufficient help-seeking behavior11. Higher levels of suicide literacy are associated with lower levels of suicide-related stigma, as individuals with accurate knowledge are less likely to endorse stereotypes or misconceptions about suicide12.

Literacy contributes directly to more favorable attitudes toward seeking psychological help by reducing uncertainty, fear, and misconceptions about treatment and care13. Both general stigma and self-stigma in help seeking diminish willingness to access professional support, even among those who recognize a need for help. Stigma may mediate the relationship between literacy and help-seeking attitudes, where lower stigma facilitates more positive dispositions toward professional help. Hence, cultivating a culture of seeking professional mental health assistance is an indispensable measure in preventing suicide11. Studies have shown that there is insufficient awareness of suicide among health care providers and medical students14,15.

In Iran, most studies in Iran have been conducted on suicide literacy in the general population and medical residents16,17. While most studies in this field have been on suicidal thoughts in nursing students18,19.

Suicide prevention strategies should prioritize improving suicide literacy, which encompasses understanding its nature, risk factors, symptoms, and available treatments, since such knowledge positively influences attitudes and help-seeking behavior20. Given that suicide literacy and professional help-seeking behaviors among nursing students as future nurses are important not only for improving the mental health of nursing students, but also for the quality of care provided to patients. The results of the present study can be useful for nursing students, nursing professors, educational policy makers, and counseling and mental health services in universities.

Materials and methods

Study design

In 2025, The research was designed as a cross-sectional study involving 500 nursing students at Tabriz faculty of nursing and midwifery, Iran. This faculty is the oldest nursing faculty in Iran. The sample size was calculated based on a study of Jahanbakhsh et al. and considering d = 0.1 S, S = 4.31, and 95% confidence interval and a margin of error of 5% and the probability of a 10% attrition in samples equal to 500 participants16. The inclusion criteria were a full-time undergraduate (from first to final year) or postgraduate nursing student; and no self-reported history of serve mental disorders. Exclusion criteria were having a history of participating in relevant training courses and non-completion of questionnaires or incomplete data that would make the study results unreliable. By using stratified random sampling, 500 nursing students were selected. The target population was divided into specific classes based on academic year. Then, in each class, students were selected by simple random sampling to obtain a representative sample of each class. Data were collected through a standard self-report questionnaire, and confidentiality of responses was ensured to reduce the potential limitation of lack of psychological self-disclosure.

Measurements

Tools for data gathering comprised three questionnaires: demographic information, The Literacy of Suicide Scale (LOSS) and Attitude towards seeking professional psychological help (ATSPPH-SF).

Demographic information questionnaire

The questionnaire collected data on age, gender, marital status, semester of study, region of residence, Grade Point Average (GPA), interest in the nursing profession, number of siblings, family income, family structure, as well as personal or family history of psychiatric disorders and psychotherapy.

The literacy of suicide scale (LOSS)

This study employed the Suicide Literacy Questionnaire (SLQ), originally developed by Calear, to assess participants’ suicide literacy levels21. The scale consists of 26 items rated on a 3point response format “Correct,” “Incorrect,” and “Don’t know”—organized into four domains: (a) signs and symptoms of suicide (5 items), (b) nature and reasons for suicidal thoughts and behaviors (10 items), (c) risk factors (7 items), and(d) treatment and prevention (4 items).Scoring follows a binary system: correct answers receive 1 point, while incorrect and “Don’t know” responses receive 0. The total score ranges from 0 to 26, with higher scores reflecting greater suicide-related knowledge. The questionnaire was originally validated among Australian medical students, demonstrating acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.71). The study utilized the Persian-language version of the SLQ, which has also been validated, showing strong reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.82) and good test-retest reliability (ICC = 0.79)22. In current research, the scale’s content and face validity were qualitatively affirmed by 10 expert faculty members. Internal consistency reliability was evaluated through a pilot study with 30 students, the reliability of the questionnaire through Kuder- Richardson − 21(KR-21 reported in the present study it was 0.84.

Attitude towards seeking professional psychological help (ATSPPH-SF)

The Attitudes Toward Seeking Professional Psychological Help Scale (ATSPPH-SF), developed by Fischer and Farina in 1995, was administered to assess participants’ attitudes toward receiving professional psychological services—including counseling, therapy, and pharmacological treatment from psychologists, therapists, or psychiatrists. The scale comprises 10 items rated on a 4‑point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“disagree”) to 3 (“agree”). Five items (2, 4, 8, 9, and 10) are reverse‑scored, whereas the remaining items are scored directly23. The total score ranges from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating a more positive attitude toward seeking professional psychological help. Based on established cut‑off criteria, attitudes were classified into three levels: low (20–23.3), moderate (23.3–26.7), and high (26.7–30), determined by dividing the total possible score range into equal intervals. The scale has demonstrated strong psychometric properties, including robust test-retest reliability, temporal stability, and internal consistency24. Furthermore, its validity and goodness‑of‑fit have been confirmed in the Iranian context16.

In this study, the scale’s content and face validity were qualitatively affirmed by 10 expert faculty members. Internal consistency was assessed in a pilot study involving 30 students. In the present study, the questionnaire demonstrated good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.87.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Vice-Chancellor for Research and the Ethics Committee of Tabriz University of Medical Sciences (Code: IR.TBZMED.REC.1404.083). All participants received verbal and written information regarding the study’s purpose, data confidentiality, and their right to withdraw. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion. The study adhered to the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed statistically with SPSS 16. Initially, descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) were calculated. The Shapiro–Wilk test verified the normal distribution of quantitative data. Subsequently, in order to examine the intensity and direction of the linear relationship between two quantitative variables, Pearson correlation test, and to examine the independent effect of suicide literacy on attitude towards seeking professional help in the presence of other controlling variables, multiple linear regression was used. All analyses were performed at a significance level of < 0.05.

In order to examine the intensity and direction of the linear relationship between two quantitative variables, Pearson correlation test, and to examine the independent effect of suicide literacy on attitude towards seeking professional help in the presence of other controlling variables, multiple linear regression was used. Statistically significant (P < 0.05) variables were then entered into the multiple linear model to determine the effect of independent variables on the dependent variable (ATSPPH).

Results

The results demonstrated that the majority of participants were female, single, undergraduate, first year, native, uninterested in nursing, income equal to expenses, no history of psychiatric illness in the family, single and without self-history of psychological problems. The mean age and GPA were 22.15 (2.98) and 16.61 (1.23), respectively (Table 1). The mean and standard deviation of suicide literacy was 12.61 (4.51) from the score range of 0–25 and the mean attitude towards seeking psychological help was 16.31 (3.75) from the score range of 0–30. A significant positive correlation was found between suicide literacy and attitude towards seeking psychological help (r=0.185, P = 0.000) (Table 2). Factors related to attitude towards seeking psychological help in nursing students were examined with multiple linear regression (R2= 0.119, f = 4.014, p < 0.001). Significant factors predictive of attitude toward seeking psychological help in students were gender, having a brother or sister, and suicide literacy (Table 3).

Discussion

The current study assessed nursing students’ Suicide literacy and attitude towards seeking professional psychological. The results of the present research align with research by Sarikaya et al., Arafat et al., and Kacan, which reported a relatively low level of suicide literacy among nursing students8,25,26. In contrast, several other studies have documented higher levels of suicide knowledge in similar student populations, a finding inconsistent with the results reported here21,27. These differences reflect differences in educational curricula, individual experience, and contextual factors may influence suicide literacy across different settings. Findings of the current study revealed that the majority of participants had a negative attitude towards seeking professional psychological help. Results of studies of Sarikaya et al., Arafat et al. and Kacan indicated that nursing students had a negative attitude seeking professional psychological help of suicide which is consistent with our findings8,25,26. The Iranian context is characterized by cultural and societal stigmas surrounding mental health, which often intertwine with discussions on the topic and can actively discourage help-seeking behavior28. Psychometric studies in the Iranian context have shown that the components of “stigma,” “shame,” and “cultural inhibition” play a role in attitudes toward seeking professional help29. Also, stigma around suicide and mental disorders has been widely reported in the Iranian population, such that this stigma can cause fear of labeling, social shame, and avoidance of health services, ultimately preventing people from seeking help30.

The reluctance to toward seeking professional psychological help is driven by a complex interplay of determinants, including negative attitudes toward help-seeking, practical constraints, and pervasive sociocultural barriers such as stigma, concerns regarding judgment and information disclosure, the tendency to minimize personal symptom severity and low perceived treatment utility31.

Suicide literacy and its correlation with attitude towards seeking professional psychological help among nursing students, which is in line with previous results32–34. Conversely, Prior studies have reported no significant association, which contrasts with the findings of the present study35,36. Possible reasons for the differences include sample size, study instruments, and the influence of strong mediating or moderators such as level of internalized stigma, gender, culture beliefs, access to services, or social support.

The current study found that attitudes toward professional help-seeking were significantly more positive among female students than among males37-39. This is consistent with established literature indicating that women are often more comfortable with emotional disclosure, a key predictor of openness to psychological support37. This study is subject to several limitations that should be acknowledged when interpreting its findings. First, the reliance on self-reported data introduces the risk of recall bias, which may affect the accuracy of the responses. Although measures were taken to mitigate this such as detailed explanations of the study objectives and allowing sufficient time to complete the questionnaires this potential bias cannot be fully eliminated. Second, as a descriptive correlational study, the design does not support causal inferences between the variables examined. Finally, participants’ emotional states at the time of questionnaire completion could have influenced their responses. To minimize this, respondents were instructed to complete the measures in a calm and low-stress environment; nevertheless, this factor remains a potential source of bias. Lack of psychological self-disclosure may cause some responses to not accurately reflect the true situation. To minimize this, confidentiality of responses was ensured to reduce the potential limitation of lack of psychological self-disclosure.

The study was conducted at the Tabriz School of Nursing and Midwifery, and the findings may not be generalizable to all nursing students in Iran or other countries.

The findings of this study could have important implications for nursing education and nursing management. Identifying the level of suicide literacy and attitudes toward help-seeking among students will allow educational planners to design targeted workshops, training courses, and prevention programs. At the nursing management level, these data can help develop policies to support the mental health of nursing staff and students and guide nursing managers in creating supportive environments for identifying and referring patients with suicidal thoughts. Also, identifying groups with negative attitudes toward professional help can provide a basis for cultural and psychological interventions. Suggestions for future study include conducting longitudinal studies to examine changes in suicide literacy and attitudes toward professional help-seeking over time and the impact of targeted training. Expanding the study to other nursing schools in the country to increase the generalizability of the findings. Using qualitative methods such as in-depth interviews to better understand barriers to professional help-seeking and related cultural factors. Examining the effects of educational interventions and prevention workshops on suicide literacy and attitudes toward professional help empirically.

Conclusion

The co‑occurrence of limited suicide literacy and negative help‑seeking attitudes among nursing students highlights a potential gap in current nursing education. These findings suggest that existing curricula may not adequately address suicide‑related knowledge and help‑seeking skills. Integrating evidence‑based suicide education and structured mental health support into nursing programs may improve students’ preparedness and attitudes toward seeking professional help.
