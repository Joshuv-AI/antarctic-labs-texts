Approximation by Uniformly distributed sequences
Gerardo González Robert, Mumtaz Hussain, Nikita Shulga, Benjamin Ward

Abstract
. We consider approximation properties of real points by uniformly distributed sequences. Under some assumptions on the approximation functions, we prove a Khintchine-type 0 0 - 1 1 dichotomy law. We establish a new connection between uniform distribution and the ubiquity property. Namely, we show that a bound on the discrepancy of the sequence implies the ubiquity property, which helps to obtain divergence results. We further obtain Hausdorff dimension results for weighted sets. The key tools in proving these results are the weighted ubiquitous systems and weighted mass transference principle introduced recently by Kleinbock & Wang, and Wang & Wu respectively.



1.
Introduction and main results

Fix a natural number
n
and let
\omega=(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence of points in
[0,1]^{n}
(that is,
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}=(\omega_{j,1},\dots,\omega_{j,n})\in[0,1]^{n}
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
). Let
\psi_{i}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be a monotonic decreasing function for each
1\leq i\leq n
and set
\Psi=(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{n})
. We wish to analyse the question of the approximation of a point
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n}
by elements of our chosen fixed sequence
\omega
from a metrical point of view. For this purpose, we consider the set

W_{\omega}(\Psi):=\left\{\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}:|x_{i}-\omega_{j,i}|<\psi_{i}(j)\,\quad(1\leq i\leq n)\quad\text{ for i.m. }j\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\,.

Basically, this is a set of points in an
n
-dimensional unit cube, which are coordinate-wise
\psi_{i}
-approximable by a fixed sequence
\omega
. One can easily present degenerate examples of sequences
\omega
for which the set
W_{\omega}(\Psi)
has Lebesgue measure
0
. But what are the conditions on
\omega
under which we can guarantee this happens? And under which conditions on
\omega
can we guarantee that
W_{\omega}(\Psi)
has full Lebesgue measure?

In this article, we present an approach to tackle such questions and provide some partial results. Surprisingly, the framework of ubiquitous systems can be connected to the setting of approximation with sequences
\omega
by imposing some natural assumptions on the distribution properties of the sequences
\omega
with standard definitions of uniform distribution and discrepancy.

We recall a few basic definitions from uniform distribution theory, see for example
[
18
]
. By a
rectangle

R
in
[0,1]^{n}
we mean a set of the form

R=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n}:a_{i}\leq x_{i}<b_{i}\quad(1\leq i\leq n)\,\right\}

for some
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n}),(b_{1},\dots,b_{n})\in[0,1]^{n}
with
a_{i}<b_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,n
. For any rectangle
R
and any

, define

A(R;N,\omega):=\#\{1\leq j\leq N:\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}\in R\}\,.

Denote the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{n}
by
\lambda_{n}
. For

, we write
\lambda=\lambda_{1}
.

Definition 1.1

(Uniformly distributed sequence)
.

A sequence
\omega=(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j})_{j\geq 1}
of points in

is a
uniformly distributed sequence on

[0,1]^{n}
, denoted
\omega
is a

on
[0,1]^{n}
, if for any
n
-dimensional rectangle

we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{A(R;N,\omega)}{N}=\lambda_{n}(R)\,.

That is, a sequence
\omega
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]^{n}
if for any rectangle
R
the sequence spends time in
R
proportionate to the volume of
R
.
Much is known about

when

is a sequence of independent identically distributed uniform random variables. Initiated by Fan and Wu

8

[

As a classical example of a uniformly distributed sequence, fix some
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
and consider the sequence
\omega=(\{n\alpha\})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, where

The metric properties of

is well distributed
[

[

]
(his definition of well-distributed sequence differs from the classic one). In the following section, we begin with a general statement in which we consider no other conditions on

Definition 1.2

(Discrepancy, Star-discrepancy)
.

Let
\omega
be a sequence of points in
[0,1]^{n}
.

i.

The
discrepancy
of
\omega
is the function
D:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
N\mapsto D_{N}(\omega)
, given by

D_{N}=D_{N}(\omega):=\sup_{R\subseteq[0,1]^{n}}\left|\frac{A(R;N,\omega)}{N}-\lambda_{n}(R)\right|,

where the supremum is taken over all rectangles
R\subseteq[0,1]^{n}
.

ii.

The
star-discrepancy
of
\omega
is the function
D^{*}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
N\mapsto D_{N}^{*}(\omega)
, given by

D_{N}^{*}=D_{N}^{*}(\omega):=\sup_{\{\boldsymbol{0}\}\subset R\subseteq[0,1]^{n}}\left|\frac{A(R;N,\omega)}{N}-\lambda_{n}(R)\right|,

where the supremum is taken over all rectangles
R\subseteq[0,1]^{n}
of the form
[0,t_{1})\times\cdots[0,t_{n})
for some
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}>0
.

It is well known
[
18
, Theorem 1.3]
that for any sequence
\omega
in
[0,1]^{n}
and any

we have

D_{N}^{*}(\omega)\leq D_{N}(\omega)\leq 2^{n}D_{N}^{*}(\omega).

It is clear, see
[
18

, that
\omega
is a uniformly distributed sequence in

if and only if
D_{N}\to 0
(and by the above observation also

) as

. To relate the notion of discrepancy to the framework of ubiquitous systems, it is crucial to understand how fast the discrepancy decays to zero. For this reason, we introduce the following definition.

Definition 1.3

(
({\mathcal{N}},v)
-Discrepancy satisfying sequence)
.

Let
\omega=(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence of points in
[0,1]^{n}
. We say that
\omega
is a
({\mathcal{N}},v)
-Discrepancy satisfying sequence
, which we write as

-d.s.s, if there exists a strictly increasing sequence

of positive integers and a monotonic decreasing function

with

as

such that

D_{N_{i}}(\omega)<v(N_{i})\quad\text{ and }\quad\limsup_{i\to\infty}N_{i-1}v(N_{i})<1\quad(i\in\mathbb{N}).

Clearly, every uniformly distributed sequence is a
(\mathcal{N},v)
-d.s.s, since

as
N\to\infty
one can choose an increasingly sparse sequence

satisfying the conditions of Definition
1.3
. However, the sparsity of this sequence will become important in our main theorem. So it is desirable to pick
{\mathcal{N}}
as dense as possible, while still satisfying the conditions of the definition.

In order to state our most general result, recall a function
f
is
c
-regular with respect to a sequence
{\mathcal{N}}=(N_{j})_{j\geq 1}
if, for constant

f(N_{i+1})\leq cf(N_{i})

for all sufficiently large

. We prove the following Khintchine-type divergence statement.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\Psi=(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{n})
be an n-tuple of monotonic decreasing functions. Suppose that
\omega=(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{i})_{i\geq 1}

({\mathcal{N}},v)
-d.s.s. for

-regular for

\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

\min\{\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{n}\}>0,\quad\sum_{j=1}^{n}\tau_{j}=1

\psi_{j}(N)\leq v(N)^{\tau_{j}}\quad(1\leq j\leq n).

Then

\lambda_{n}(W_{\omega}(\Psi))=1\quad\text{\rm if }\quad\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}v(N_{j})^{-1}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{i}(N_{j})=\infty.

iii.

We have

\lambda_{n}\left(R\setminus\bigcup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}}\Delta(\mathbf{y};\mathbf{r}_{k})\right)<\varepsilon.

The following result shows that any ball
B\subseteq[0,1]^{n}
can be covered, up to a Lebesgue-null set, by rectangles centred on a finite subset of the sequence
\omega
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\rho
and
\omega
satisfy the conditions stated in Proposition
3.1
. For any ball
B\subseteq[0,1]^{n}
we have

\lim_{k\to\infty}\lambda_{n}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\Delta(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j};\rho(N_{k}))\right)=\lambda_{n}(B).

Proof.

Take any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Let
K
be the natural number obtained from Proposition
3.3
applied on
R=B
and
\mathbf{r}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\rho(N_{k})
. Take
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq K}
and let
\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{C}(k)
be the corresponding finite set. For every
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}
, we have

\left|\frac{A_{N_{k}}\left(\omega;\Delta\left(\mathbf{y};\frac{\rho(N_{k})}{2}\right)\right)}{N_{k}}-\lambda_{n}\left(\Delta\left(\mathbf{y};\frac{\rho(N_{k})}{2}\right)\right)\right|\leq D_{N_{k}}<v(N_{k}).

Hence, since

\lambda_{n}\left(\Delta\left(\mathbf{y};\frac{\rho(N_{k})}{2}\right)\right)=v(N_{k}),

we have

A_{N_{k}}\left(\omega;\Delta\left(\mathbf{y};\frac{\rho(N_{k})}{2}\right)\right)\geq 1\,.

So, for each
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}
, there exists

\omega(\mathbf{y})\in\{\boldsymbol{\omega}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\omega}_{N_{k}}\}\cap\Delta\left(\mathbf{y};\frac{\rho(N_{k})}{2}\right)\,.

Fix one such point
\omega(\mathbf{y})
for each
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}
. Note that

\Delta\left(\mathbf{y};\frac{\rho(N_{k})}{2}\right)\subseteq\Delta\left(\omega(\mathbf{y});\rho(N_{k})\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\Delta(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j};\rho(N_{k}))\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{n}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}}\Delta(x(\mathbf{y});\rho(N_{k}))\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{n}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{C}}\Delta\left(\mathbf{y};\frac{\rho(N_{k})}{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle>(1-\varepsilon)\lambda_{n}(B).

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we conclude

\liminf_{k\to\infty}\lambda_{n}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\Delta(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j};\rho(N_{k}))\right)\geq\lambda_{n}(B).

The result follows, because we have for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
that

\lambda_{n}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{k}}\Delta(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j};\rho(N_{k}))\right)\leq\lambda_{n}(B).

∎

Lemma 3.5
.

For every
\delta,\eta>0
there exists
K_{0}=K_{0}(\delta)\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq K_{0}}
we have

\lambda_{n}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{k-1}}\Delta(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j};\rho(N_{k}))\right)<(1+\delta)(1+\eta)^{n}N_{k-1}v(N_{k})\lambda_{n}(B).

Proof.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
be such that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq K}

\begin{cases}\lambda_{n}\left(\Delta(B;(1+\eta)\rho(N_{k})\right)<\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\lambda_{n}(B),\\
D_{N_{k-1}}<\frac{\delta}{2}\lambda_{n}(B).\end{cases}

Such
K
exists, because
\rho(N_{k})\to 0
as
k\to\infty
and
\omega
is uniformly distributed (and so
D_{N_{k}}\to 0
). Then, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq K}
we have

\left|\frac{A_{N_{k-1}}(\omega;\Delta(B;(1+\eta)\rho(N_{k})))}{N_{k-1}}-\lambda_{n}\left(\Delta(B;(1+\eta)\rho(N_{k}))\right)\right|<\frac{\delta}{2}\lambda_{n}(B),

so

A_{N_{k-1}}(\omega;\Delta(B;(1+\eta)\rho(N_{k}))\leq\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)N_{k-1}\lambda_{n}(B)=(1+\delta)N_{k-1}\lambda_{n}(B).

Note that if
\Delta(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j};\rho(N_{k}))\cap B\neq\varnothing
, then
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j}
is contained in
\Delta(B;(1+\eta)\rho(N_{k}))
and so

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{j=1}^{N_{k-1}}\Delta(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j};\rho(N_{k}))\right)

\displaystyle\leq A_{N_{k-1}}\left(\omega;\Delta(B;(1+\eta)\rho(N_{k}))\right)\lambda_{n}\left(\Delta(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{1};\rho(N_{k}))\right)

\displaystyle<(1+\delta)\lambda_{n}(B)(1+\eta)^{n}N_{k-1}v(N_{k}),

in the last inequality, we have used
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}(N)=v(N)
.
∎

Proof of Proposition
3.1
.

Since
\limsup_{k\to\infty}N_{k-1}v(N_{k})<1
, we can pick
k_{0}
large enough so that
N_{k-1}v(N_{k})<1-\gamma
for all
k\geq k_{0}
. Now choose small values
c,\varepsilon,\delta,\eta>0
such that

1-\gamma<\frac{1-\varepsilon-c}{(1+\delta)(1+\eta)^{n}}\,.

Combining Lemma
3.4
and Lemma
3.5
with
\varepsilon,\delta,\eta>0
chosen as above, for every large
k
we have that

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{N_{k-1}\leq j\leq N_{k}}\Delta\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j},\rho(N_{k})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{n}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq N_{k}}\Delta\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j},\rho(N_{k})\right)\right)-\lambda_{n}\left(B\cap\bigcup_{1\leq j\leq N_{k-1}}\Delta\left(\boldsymbol{\omega}_{j},\rho(N_{k})\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq(1-\varepsilon)\lambda_{n}(B)-(1+\delta)(1+\eta)^{n}N_{k-1}V(N_{k})\lambda_{n}(B)

\displaystyle\geq c\lambda_{n}(B),

as required.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

we conclude the

.

3.5.
Proof of Theorems
1.11
and
1.12

For the lower bound of the Hausdorff dimension and the divergent counterpart of the Hausdorff measure theory, we have the following theorem. It is a particular case of a result by Wang and Wu
[
22
, Theorem 3.1-3.2]
on weighted ubiquitous systems.

Theorem 3.6

(
[
22
]
)
.

Let
\omega
be a sequence in
[0,1]^{n}
which is locally ubiquitous with respect to
(l_{k})_{k\geq 1}
,
(u_{k})_{k\geq 1}
, and
\rho=(\rho^{a_{1}},\ldots,\rho^{a_{n}})
for some function
\rho:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.
Then, if
\Psi=(\rho^{a_{1}+t_{1}},\dots,\rho^{a_{n}+t_{n}})
for some
\textbf{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
,

\dimh W(\Psi)\geq\min_{A_{i}\in A}\left\{\#\mathcal{K}_{1}+\#\mathcal{K}_{2}+\frac{\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{K}_{3}}a_{j}-\sum\limits_{j\in\mathcal{K}_{2}}t_{j}}{A_{i}}\right\}=s,

where
A=\{a_{i},a_{i}+t_{i},1\leq i\leq n\}
and
\mathcal{K}_{1},\mathcal{K}_{2},\mathcal{K}_{3}
are a partition of
\{1,\dots,n\}
defined as

\mathcal{K}_{1}=\{j:a_{j}\geq A_{i}\},\quad\mathcal{K}_{2}=\{j:a_{j}+t_{j}\leq A_{i}\}\backslash\mathcal{K}_{1},\quad\mathcal{K}_{3}=\{1,\dots n\}\backslash(\mathcal{K}_{1}\cup\mathcal{K}_{2}).

Furthermore, for any ball
B\subset[0,1]^{n}
we have

(4)

\mathcal{H}^{s}(B\cap W_{\omega}(\Psi))=\mathcal{H}^{s}(B).

Now let us first prove the upper bounds in Theorems
1.11
and
1.12
.

3.5.1.
Upper bound

For any
K\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the cover

\bigcup_{j>K}\Delta(\omega_{j},\Psi(j))\supset W_{\omega}(\Psi)\,.

Fix
1\leq k\leq n
. Each rectangle
\Delta(\omega_{j},\Psi(j))
can be covered by

\asymp\prod_{i=1}^{n}\max\left\{1,j^{\tau_{k}-\tau_{i}}\right\}=j^{\sum_{i:\tau_{k}>\tau_{i}}(\tau_{k}-\tau_{i})}

balls of radius
j^{-\tau_{k}}
. So

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{s}\left(W_{\omega}(\Psi)\right)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{j>K}j^{\sum_{i:\tau_{k}>\tau_{i}}(\tau_{k}-\tau_{i})}(j^{-\tau_{k}})^{s}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j>K}j^{-s\tau_{k}+\sum_{i:\tau_{k}>\tau_{i}}(\tau_{k}-\tau_{i})}\to 0

as
K\to\infty
for any

s>\frac{1+\sum_{i:\tau_{k}>\tau_{i}}(\tau_{k}-\tau_{i})}{\tau_{k}}\,.

Hence

\dimh W_{\omega}(\Psi)\leq\frac{1+\sum_{i:\tau_{k}>\tau_{i}}(\tau_{k}-\tau_{i})}{\tau_{k}}

Since the above argument remains true for each
1\leq k\leq n
, we have the required upper bound. This gives the required upper bound for both Theorem
1.11
and
1.12
.

3.5.2.
Lower bound

As stated above, for any low discrepancy sequence we can associate the above pair
({\mathcal{N}},v)
to ensure that
\omega
is a
({\mathcal{N}},v)
-d.s.s and so, by Proposition
3.1

\omega
is locally ubiquitous with respect to
{\mathcal{N}}
and
\rho
as in (
3
).
Hence, Theorem
3.6
is applicable in the lower bound of Theorem
1.11
. Note that strictly speaking the functions
\Psi
in Theorem
1.11
should be of the form

\Psi(N)=\left(\left(\frac{(\log N)^{n}}{N}\right)^{\tau_{1}},\dots,\left(\frac{(\log N)^{n}}{N}\right)^{\tau_{n}}\right)\,.

However, it is easily seen that for any
\theta>0
and any
0<\varepsilon<\theta
we have

\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)^{\theta}<\left(\frac{(\log N)^{n}}{N}\right)^{\theta}<\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)^{\theta-\varepsilon}

for
N
sufficiently large, and so the Hausdorff dimension bound is the same.

From the conditions of Theorem
1.12
, we can choose a sequence of natural numbers over which
D_{N}(\omega)\ll N^{-1}
. Take a suitably sparse subsequence of these integers to be
{\mathcal{N}}
such that
N_{k-1}N_{k}^{-1}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
. Paired with the function
v(N)=cN^{-1}
, where
c
is the implied constant in the condition of Theorem
1.12
, we have that
\omega
is a
({\mathcal{N}},v)
-d.s.s for such pair.
Hence,
\omega
is locally ubiquitous with respect to
{\mathcal{N}}
and
\rho=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n})
determined by

\rho_{i}(N)=\rho(N)^{a_{i}}=N^{-a_{i}}\quad(1\leq i\leq n)\,,

for vector
\boldsymbol{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=1\,.

We now consider both lower bound results in tandem. It remains to show the formula given in Theorem
3.6
produces the lower bound of Theorem
1.11
and
1.12
.
In order to apply Theorem
3.6
, for any
\mathbf{a}=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=1
and
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{n})
as in Theorem
1.11
and
1.12
, let us define

\textbf{t}=(t_{1},\dots,t_{n})=(\tau_{1}-a_{1},\dots,\tau_{n}-a_{n})\,.

Consider the two cases:

i.

Suppose that
\tau_{i}\geq\frac{1}{n}
for all
1\leq i\leq n
. Then set

a_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\quad(1\leq i\leq n)\,.

Now, if
A=a_{i}
for any
1\leq i\leq n
we have that
\mathcal{K}_{1}=\{1,\dots,n\}
and so the lower bound formula is
n
in this case. If
A=\tau_{j}
for some
1\leq j\leq n
then

\mathcal{K}_{1}=\emptyset\,,\quad\mathcal{K}_{2}=\{i:\tau_{j}\geq\tau_{i}\}\,,\quad\mathcal{K}_{3}=\mathcal{K}_{2}^{c}\,,

and so

\displaystyle\dimh W_{\omega}(\Psi)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{1\leq j\leq n}\left\{\#\mathcal{K}_{2}+\frac{\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{2}^{c}}a_{i}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{2}}t_{k}}{\tau_{j}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{1\leq j\leq n}\left\{\#\mathcal{K}_{2}+\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{2}}(a_{i}+t_{i})}{\tau_{j}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{1\leq j\leq n}\left\{\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{2}}(\tau_{j}-(a_{i}+t_{i})}{\tau_{j}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq\min_{1\leq j\leq n}\left\{\frac{1+\sum_{i:\tau_{j}\geq\tau_{i}}(\tau_{j}-\tau_{i})}{\tau_{j}}\right\}.

ii.

Assume that there exists
\tau_{j}
such that
\tau_{j}<\frac{1}{n}
. Without loss of generality, suppose that

\tau_{1}>\tau_{2}>\dots>\tau_{n}\,.

We want to choose
1\leq u\leq n
that solves

u\times\widetilde{D}+\sum\limits_{u<i\leq n}\tau_{i}=1

for some
\widetilde{D}>0
with
\tau_{u}>\widetilde{D}
. Pick

\widetilde{D}=\frac{1-\sum_{u\leq i\leq n}\tau_{i}}{u}

and note that

\tau_{1}>\tau_{2}>\dots>\tau_{u}>\widetilde{D}\geq\tau_{u+1}>\dots>\tau_{n}.

Hence pick

a_{i}=\begin{cases}\widetilde{D}\quad(1\leq i\leq u),\\
\tau_{i}\quad(u+1\leq i\leq n).\end{cases}

For
A=a_{i}
with
1\leq i\leq u
we have

\mathcal{K}_{1}=\{1,\dots,u\},\quad\mathcal{K}_{2}=\{u+1,\dots,n\},\quad\mathcal{K}_{3}=\emptyset,

and for
A=a_{i}=\tau_{i}
with
u+1\leq i\leq n

\mathcal{K}_{1}=\{1,\dots,i\},\quad\mathcal{K}_{2}=\{i+1,\dots,n\},\quad\mathcal{K}_{3}=\emptyset.

In both cases, we have a lower bound formula of
n
.

For each
A=a_{j}+t_{j}=\tau_{j}
with
1\leq j\leq u
we have that

\mathcal{K}_{1}=\emptyset,\quad\mathcal{K}_{2}=\{j,\dots,n\}=\{i:\tau_{j}\geq\tau_{i}\},\quad\mathcal{K}_{3}=\mathcal{K}_{2}^{c},

Notice these are the same sets as in the conclusion of case
i)
, and so we obtain the same lower bound, thus the proof of Theorem
1.11
and
1.12
is complete.
