A digital health governance agenda for sexual and reproductive health and rights
Authors: Claudia A Lopes, Arthur Saitabau, Niharika Rustagi, Rajat Khosla
Published: 2024

The need for digital health governance for SRHR

The increasing reliance on digital technologies in healthcare has the potential to either exacerbate existing inequities in health systems or mitigate them, depending on how these technologies are developed and implemented. As the United Nations (UN) Special Rapporteur on right to health pointed out, “digital technologies can perpetuate racism, sexism, ableism or discrimination based on sexual orientation or gender identity, among others”.* Echoing these concerns, The Lancet and Financial Times Commission on governing health futures† emphasised the need to address inequities of the digital divide and ensure data and child protection, digital safety and a human rights-based approach to digital health.1 The World Health Organization (WHO), in its guidance on digital interventions and AI for health,2–4 underscores the importance of considering the ethical, legal and human rights implications relating to issues of data governance, algorithmic transparency and explainability, inclusiveness and equity, responsibility and accountability. Furthermore, WHO’s Call for Health Data Governance as a global public good in September 20215 highlights the urgent need for a global health data governance framework to ensure that health data must be collected, shared and stored in line with internationally approved standards and used ethically and equitably.

This commentary presents a reflection on digital governance aspects, towards a governance agenda for digital health applications and sexual and reproductive health and rights (SRHR). Digital SRHR facilitates access to health information, education and promotion; supports screening and triage of health conditions; tailors treatment and care regimens; monitors personal and population health; assists in health system management needs; and accelerates clinical research and drug discovery.6 Digital tools and platforms have played a pivotal role in breaking down societal taboos and stigma surrounding SRHR topics, fostering open dialogues and promoting greater understanding.7 By creating conversational digital spaces that offer anonymity, individuals are encouraged to participate and seek assistance without the fear of judgment, thereby enhancing accessibility to vital resources.8,9 Moreover, digital audience segmentation facilitates the tailored delivery of SRHR information, ensuring its relevance and effectiveness for diverse communities.10,11

However, digital technologies for SRHR present specific challenges. First, due to the sensitive nature of SRHR issues such as sexual transmitted diseases, gender-based violence, and safe abortion, individuals are inclined to use digital solutions as they potentially protect privacy and confidentiality. Therefore, digital users’ privacy and data security are of utmost importance.12–14 Second, in certain contexts where SRHR issues are stigmatised, online platforms are the preferred channel to access health information.15 However, these digital spaces can also create echo chambers that perpetuate harmful beliefs or attitudes, propagate misinformation and provide opportunities for targeted disinformation campaigns. Taboo topics related to SRHR are therefore more difficult to debunk through peer conversations and advice from health professionals. Third, AI introduces additional complexities, as governments and organisations may exploit it to restrict access to SRHR health services such as safe abortion and sexual education.16–18 AI can also be used to mine data to identify gender-diverse minorities and potentially may lead to the persecution of individuals who access those services in certain legal contexts.

In the absence of proper governance mechanisms, control of personal health data from digital technologies by governments and big-tech companies poses a risk to the bodily autonomy of women, girls and gender-diverse individuals. The following examples illustrate the adverse impact of unregulated and unethical use of digital health technologies for SRHR.

Data from period-trackers has been shared with third parties containing information about menstrual cycles, physical activity, weight, height, blood pressure, mood, and other health data.19 These data sources can be sold, shared or leaked, influencing an individual’s access to healthcare, work, finance/insurance, citizenship or social protection.20,21

For women and girls online, dissemination of inaccurate and deceptive information through targeted campaigns and conversational bots complicates their comprehension and perspectives in relation to SRHR. A recent study analysing the top 100 most viewed TikTok videos tagged #IUD showed personal experiences with negative sentiments, scepticism towards healthcare professionals and moderately or highly inaccurate scientific claims.22 Such depictions contribute to the perpetuation of myths and misconceptions surrounding modern contraceptives, which, in turn have been associated with low contraceptive uptake among youth.23

Social media companies segment their users to redirect them to sites promoting inaccurate SRHR information. For example, it has been reported that pro-life groups based in the United States disseminate disinformation regarding pregnancy and family planning, primarily targeting women in Africa and Latin America.24,25 Law enforcement is turning to social media platforms and commercial data from online providers for abortion-related medication to build cases to prosecute women seeking abortions.26

Inequities observed in the management of breast cancer linked to the underrepresentation of Hispanic, Asian, and Native women, result from imbalanced training data within Google’s AI system for analysing mammograms. This AI model consequently bears the potential to perpetuate pre-existing biases with less diagnostic accuracy and treatment efficacy ascribed to underrepresented groups.27 It is noteworthy that AI is also providing potential opportunities to address this challenge, for example, generative adversarial networks (GAN) learn the underlying distribution of the input data and generate realistic samples to replace missing data and fill the gaps in training datasets.28

Despite the merits of digital SRHR, there are inherent risks associated with mis/disinformation on digital spaces, threats to bodily autonomy, data leakages, and the rise of automated decision-making processes, underscoring the need for robust ethical frameworks and stringent safeguards to protect individuals’ rights and privacy.

Digital health governance initiatives

Several calls for digital health governance have led to a plethora of initiatives at different levels within the UN system and at the regional level, most notably within the context of the ongoing discussions on the Global Digital Compact (GDC) to be adopted in 2024. The GDC is expected to outline “shared principles for an open, free and secure digital future for all”.29 Responding to these calls, the WHO’s Global Strategy for Digital Health 2020–202530 identifies the need to “strengthen governance for digital health at global, regional and national levels” as a key strategic priority. Digital health governance can be defined as a multidimensional approach to regulating, coordinating, and ensuring the responsible and ethical use of digital technologies in healthcare. It relates to laws, policies and guidelines for ethical data collection and meaningful consent, product validation, health data privacy and security, and algorithmic transparency and accountability.31

The regulatory dimension of digital health encompasses technical aspects from the transferability or interoperability of health data to global standards for quality assurance of digital health innovations. Several global principles and Acts apply to digital health data such as the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in Europe, the framework on Digital Health Data Governance for ASEAN countries,32 the Standards for Data Protection for the Ibero-American States33 and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)34 in the United States of America. In addition, several organisations are responsible for developing global standards for deployment of Digital Health Technologies, such as the WHO with the International Telecommunications Union (ITU)35 and the International Organisation for Standardization (ISO).36

In 2021, UNESCO introduced the Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence37 encompassing specific standards for health and social well-being that were adopted by 193 member states. In the same year, the WHO Ethics and Governance of Artificial Intelligence for Health was published that includes principles such as respect for human rights, transparency, accessibility, scalability, replicability, interoperability, privacy, security and confidentiality. This framework provides guidance for the development, implementation, and evaluation of digital health programmes globally.

While different initiatives have been established to regulate digital health technologies and data, resources necessary to ensure compliance and effective implementation are far from adequate, especially in lower- and middle-income countries (LMICs).38–40 Addressing these governance gaps requires the development of localised policy frameworks tailored to specific national and regional needs. Localisation should be accompanied with flexibility, as in Carnicero and Serra’s paper41 emphasising the importance of adaptive regulatory frameworks that keep pace with technological advances while supporting moving public health goals. Moreover, Auerbach et al.42 provide key recommendations for digital health governance, including improving transparency, encouraging stakeholder engagement, and fostering ethics and equity in health technology practices.

Adapting technology to fit local contexts can significantly improve the relevance and impact of digital health interventions, thereby creating digital health ecosystems that are equitable and effective in improving health outcomes in LMICs. Digital health policy frameworks have been developed by governments and international organisations to guide the adoption and integration of digital health initiatives in health systems. Examples of digital policy frameworks from LMICs include India’s National Digital Health Mission, which aims to leverage a national digital health infrastructure to improve implementation and scale up digital health interventions.43 In Kenya, digital health technologies are improving access to healthcare services and facilitating the achievement of universal health coverage.44 South Africa has also seen significant contributions from digital health innovations, particularly in addressing healthcare challenges through mobile technology.45

All these guidelines apply to digital interventions across health areas. However, there are health area-specific guidelines such as the UK’s National Health Service (NHS) Digital Mental Health Standards; the American Psychiatric Association’s (APA) guidelines for telepsychiatry; mHealth Alliance’s Maternal and Child Health mHealth Compendium; and the United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) Digital Health Toolkit for Maternal, Newborn, and Child Health. These frameworks provide guidance for developing, implementing, and evaluating digital health interventions in specific health areas, ensuring that technology solutions are tailored to the needs of patients and healthcare providers within those domains, showing that there is a precedent for SRHR.

Key components of digital health governance for SRHR

Ensuring digital health is ethically and responsibly applied to promote SRHR demands collaborative endeavours involving a wide range of stakeholders from policymakers, businesses, funders, developers, healthcare professionals, and civil society. We aim for a digital future characterised by robust data privacy frameworks, meaningful informed consent for data retention and use, mechanisms to combat online dis/information, personalised health decision-making, inclusive technology development, and localised digital governance principles that accompany technological advancements. These principles would shape a more encompassing, ethically grounded, and equitable digital SRHR landscape. As the first step towards a digital SRHR governance agenda, the WHO Human Reproduction Programme (HRP)46 published a technical brief on use of AI for SRHR where four policy and operational considerations were put forward:

Revisit data protection regulations and redress mechanisms. To safeguard against potential digital breaches resulting from the collection and commercialisation of data for AI purposes, it is imperative to strengthen data protection laws. This involves implementing clear limitations on data use, robust privacy protections, opt-out mechanisms, and transparent redress procedures. While regulations for AI and data are still evolving, ensuring informed consent processes and empowering individuals to control their SRHR data collection and usage are essential. Stakeholders handling health data must adhere to clear limitations to uphold the principle of “do no harm” and data minimisation requirements for health apps.

Concluding remarks

If the potential of digital technology for enhancing SRHR is to be realised, we must ensure the widest possible public debate about alternative approaches to data governance that need to be implemented by governments and technology providers. We must create spaces to articulate how different communities and people are taking back control and providing alternative narratives to data governance that include communal data ownership and concepts such as “data commons”, “data trusts”, “data cooperatives”, “data coalitions” and “data unions”.

As the datafication of our lives intensifies, and sensitive personal data about our bodies is bought and sold without meaningful consent – i.e. without the ability to restrict, negotiate, or withdraw consent – and fundamentally, without the power and agency to say no, we must reimagine safeguards that are driven by public purpose, not private profit, and build a culture of data justice and equity.

There are undeniable power asymmetries between global and national data controllers and processors and individual data subjects. Given the ubiquity of technology-facilitated gender-based violence, and gender biases in big data sets and the algorithms that use them, we need to position women’s and girls’ rights at the centre of these debates: for example, by using UN engagement mechanisms such as the Global Digital Compact.‡
