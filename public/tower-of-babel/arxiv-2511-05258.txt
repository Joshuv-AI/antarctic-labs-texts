Convex semidefinite tensor optimization and quantum entanglement
Liding Xu, Ye-Chao Liu, Sebastian Pokutta

Abstract
The cone of positive-semidefinite (PSD) matrices is fundamental in convex optimization, and we extend this notion to tensors, defining PSD tensors, which correspond to separable quantum states. We study the convex optimization problem over the PSD tensor cone. While this convex cone admits a smooth reparameterization through tensor factorizations (analogous to the matrix case), it is not self-dual. Moreover, there are currently no efficient algorithms for projecting onto or testing membership in this cone, and the semidefinite tensor optimization problem, although convex, is NP-hard. To address these challenges, we develop methods for computing lower and upper bounds on the optimal value of the problem. We propose a general-purpose iterative refinement algorithm that combines a lifted alternating direction method of multipliers with a cutting-plane approach. This algorithm exploits PSD tensor factorizations to produce heuristic solutions and refine the solutions using cutting planes. Since the method requires a linear minimization oracle over PSD tensors, we design a spatial branch-and-bound algorithm based on convex relaxations and valid inequalities. Our framework allows us to study the white-noise mixing threshold, which characterizes the entanglement properties of quantum states. Numerical experiments on benchmark instances demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed methods. Key words: Tensor Optimization Cutting-Plane Methods Branch-and-Bound Algorithms Quantum Entanglement Convex Relaxations

Introduction

In quantum information science, a (quantum) state is represented by a density matrix, which is a Hermitian, positive-semidefinite (PSD) matrix with unit trace. The density matrix of a pure state has rank one. A pure state of a multipartite system is separable if and only if its density matrix can be written as a tensor product of rank-one density matrices corresponding to pure states of the individual subsystems. A mixed (non-pure) state is separable if its density matrix is a convex combination of density matrices of separable pure states. States that are not separable are called entangled. Detecting entanglement in mixed states is hard; however, it is central to quantum information science and impacts many areas of physics
29
;
55
.

Mathematically, separability of (quantum) states is closely related to decompositions of tensors. Canonical polyadic decomposition (CPD) is the most well-known tensor decomposition. Given a vector
\mathbf{d}=(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
, an order-
m
tensor
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}\times\cdots\times d_{m}}
admits a CPD, if
\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i\in[r]}\lambda_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i1}\mtens\cdots\mtens\mathbf{x}_{im}
,
where
\mathbb{F}
is the field
\mathbb{R}
of real numbers or the field
\mathbb{C}
of complex numbers. The minimum
r
in CPD of
\mathbf{y}
is its CPD rank.
If all
modes

\mathbf{x}_{ij}
(
j\in[m]
) in each
component

\mathbf{x}_{i1}\mtens\cdots\mtens\mathbf{x}_{im}
are identical to a vector
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and all
d_{j}=d
, then CPD is symmetric (under permutation of indices).

Separable states are tensors that have more restrictive decompositions. We define the following cone of (order-
2m
) PSD tensors:

\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})\coloneqq\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}^{d_{1}\times d_{1}\cdots\times d_{m}\times d_{m}}\mid\mathbf{y}=\sum_{i\in[r]}\mathbf{x}_{i1}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{i1}^{\dagger}\cdots\mathbf{x}_{im}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{im}^{\dagger}\},

(1)

where
\mathbf{x}^{\dagger}
denotes the conjugate transpose of
\mathbf{x}
.
Any tensor in
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
, when reshaped as an order-2 tensor, yields a large PSD matrix of size
\bar{d}^{2}
with
\bar{d}=\prod_{j\in[m]}d_{j}
. The set
\sep(\mathbf{d})
of separable states consists of all unit-trace reshaped tensors in
\SOST_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{d})
:

\sep(\mathbf{d})\coloneqq\{\mathbf{y}\in\SOST_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{d})\mid\tr(\mathbf{y})=1\}.

(2)

Identification of separable states is difficult. For
unipartite
systems (
m=1
), the set of separable states is simply the set of density matrices. For
multipartite systems
, however, separable states form a strict subset of density matrices. In addition, the set
\sep(\mathbf{d})
is
computationally intractable
: there are no known efficient algorithms for separation, projection, linear minimization, or even membership testing. The membership testing problem is already strongly
NP
-hard even for bipartite systems (
m=2
)
34
. The linear minimization problem
\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\sep(\mathbf{d})}\langle\chi,\mathbf{y}\rangle
, known as the

NP
-hard
30
.

We consider a generalization of semidefinite programming (SDP):
semidefinite tensor optimization (SDTO)
, a conic optimization problem over the cone of PSD tensors. The SDTO can be written as:

\text{opt}\coloneqq\min\{\langle\mathbf{c},\mathbf{z}\rangle:\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z},\;A(\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{a}\in\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})\},

(SDTO)

where
\mathcal{Z}
is a tractable convex cone,
A
is a linear map,
\mathbf{a}
is a constant tensor. The intractability of
\sep(\mathbf{d})
implies that SDTO is also
NP
-hard. When
m=1
and
\mathcal{Z}
is a PSD cone, (
SDTO
) becomes an SDP, for which off-the-shelf solvers are available. However, algorithms for tackling the general problem (
SDTO
) are scarce. We adapt established SDP strategies and address the nonconvexity introduced by PSD tensor factorizations.

Our first contribution is an iterative refinement (IR) algorithm for (
SDTO
). IR alternates between a heuristic stage – where a lifted alternating direction method of multipliers (LADMM) solves a factorized nonconvex reformulation to find heuristic solutions – and a refinement stage that tightens and certifies bounds via a cutting-plane (CP) algorithm. LADMM and CP warm-start one another: LADMM outputs accelerate CP, while CP polishes the accuracy of heuristic solutions and provides numerically reliable bounds on
opt
. The IR algorithm produces a non-increasing sequence of upper bounds, and it can also provide lower bounds and certified optima.

Our second contribution is a spatial branch-and-bound (sBB) algorithm that serves as a linear minimization oracle (LMO) inside the CP algorithm. The convex relaxations in the sBB algorithm are constructed from the intersections of the following building blocks:

•

Factorizable DPS constraints obtained by combining the Doherty–Parrilo–Spedalieri (DPS) convex outer approximations of separable bipartite states
26
with McCormick factorization for multilinear monomials
2
;

•

SDP McCormick inequalities generated via Kronecker product of two matrix constraints
3
;

•

LP McCormick inequalities that support efficient branching rules.

We show that these convex sets and inequalities can be derived from a
unified framework
that generalizes the classical
Reformulation–Linearization Technique (RLT)

62
for polynomial optimization. Moreover, the convex outer approximations of separable states also give a convex relaxation of SDTO.

We evaluate the proposed algorithms and existing methods on a benchmark problem: determining the white-noise mixing threshold of an entangled state—that is, the minimal amount of white-noise admixture required to make the state separable, or equivalently, its random robustness
70
under a complete depolarizing channel:

S(\phi\|\mathbf{Id})=\min\{\mathbf{z}\geq 0:\rho(\mathbf{z};\phi,\mathbf{Id})\coloneqq(\mathbf{Id}/\bar{d}-\phi)\mathbf{z}+\phi\in\sep(\mathbf{d})\},

(Threshold)

where the normalized identity matrix
\mathbf{Id}/\bar{d}
represents the covariance of the white noise. The interpolated state
\rho(\mathbf{z};\phi,\mathbf{Id})
lies on the line segment joining
\mathbf{Id}/\bar{d}
and
\phi
. The threshold value indicates the separability/entanglement of
\phi
.

1.1
Related work

The problem of certifying entanglement or separability has been studied through several complementary approaches. A well-known example is the positive partial transpose (PPT) criterion
42
;
57
, which is necessary and sufficient for bipartite systems of
\mathbf{d}=(2,2)
or
(2,3)
, but only necessary in higher dimensions.
The DPS hierarchy
26
;
54
extends the PPT criterion and provides nested convex outer approximations of separable states for bipartite systems. From the dual perspective, the DPS hierarchy corresponds to a sum-of-squares hierarchy for a homogeneous Hermitian polynomial over spheres
30
. Although the DPS constraints admit physical interpretations, the hierarchy lacks finite-convergence guarantees
31
. The DPS constraints are primarily used as entanglement certification (YES answers to entanglement). In addition, the DPS hierarchy can produce lower bounds on white-noise mixing thresholds for bipartite states; see an implementation in Ket.jl
4
.

The inner approximations of separable states and alternating SDP-based refinement are combined to find separability certification (NO-answer to entanglement)
56
and upper bounds on white-noise mixing thresholds. As an alternative, the geometric reconstruction (GR) method
61
finds a separability certification for a target state
\phi
expressed as a convex combination of two separable states
\rho_{c}
and
\rho_{x}
. In the trial step, the GR method performs a line search to generate an extrapolated state
\rho(\mathbf{z};\phi,\mathbf{Id})
with
\mathbf{z}<0
. It then applies the conditional-gradient (CG) method (a.k.a. the Frank-Wolfe method)
18
;
32
;
59
to minimize the distance from the extrapolated state to the separable set
\sep(\mathbf{d})
. The pure separable state returned by the last call of the LMO in this CG procedure becomes
\rho_{c}
. In the reconstruction step, the method forms a parallelogram with vertices
\rho_{x}
,
\mathbf{Id}/\bar{d}
,
\rho_{c}
, and the extrapolated state
\rho(\mathbf{z};\phi,\mathbf{Id})
, such that the target state
\phi
lies on the line segment joining
\rho_{c}
and
\rho_{x}

44
. Finally, the method verifies
\rho_{x}
inside an inner approximation ball of
\sep(\mathbf{d})
, which implies the separability of
\phi
.

Recently, a primal-dual GR method has been proposed
51
to construct upper and lower bounds on the white-noise mixing threshold. This method first applies the CG algorithm to obtain an upper approximation of the distance from the target state
\phi
to the separable set
\sep(\mathbf{d})
. It then uses the gap estimation from
68
, together with a simplicial-partition-based LMO equipped with error bounds, to simultaneously find a lower bound on this distance. Whenever the lower bound is strictly positive, the CG-based entanglement detection yields a entanglement certification for the state
\phi
.

Positive-semidefinite and positive-definite tensors were introduced for symmetric tensors in
60
(via eigenvalue-based definitions) and extended to non-symmetric tensors in
43
. These notions differ from the factorization-based definition of PSD tensors, which is closely related to CPD; see
40
;
41
;
47
. Most tensor problems are
NP
-hard in the worst case
39
. It is believed that CPD exhibits a computational-to-statistical gap
6
, with efficient algorithms for symmetric CPD expected to exist for random tensors with ranks up to
\mathcal{O}(d^{m/2})
. Notable CPD algorithms include Jennrich’s simultaneous diagonalization
24
;
50
, alternating least squares
50
, the tensor power method
49
, and optimization-based approaches
64
. The tensor power method reconstructs components by solving subproblems of the form
\max\langle T,\bmtens_{j\in[m]}\mathbf{x}\rangle
. For symmetric CPD with orthogonal components, local minima of the component subproblem are provably close to global minima
33
.

Manifold optimization
1
;
14
is popular for problems with smooth reparameterizations and has been applied to CPD
19
;
27
. Reparameterization alone does not guarantee global optimality of stationary points
53
. When

m=1
, the symmetric CPD reduces to the eigenvalue decomposition, which is commonly used to project a matrix onto the PSD cone. In the Burer-Monteiro factorization for SDP
21
, the costly projection is avoided by optimizing over low-rank factors, resulting in a nonconvex problem. Under certain equality constraints, all local minima of the factorized formulation are global
22
; this result can be extended to nonlinear objectives and some structured constraints
46
. The augmented Lagrangian method (ALM) is a standard tool for constrained optimization
11
and can handle SDP constraints that are not manifold-reparameterizable
27
;
72
;
71
. The alternating direction method of multipliers (ADMM) is widely used for convex problems with partially separable structure

.

2
Reformulations and relaxations of SDTO

In this section, we consider the optimization problem (
SDTO
). As discussed in the introduction,
\mathcal{Z}
is tractable but
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
is not. Unlike classical SDPs, only lower and upper bounds for (
SDTO
) are generally computable. We discuss the problem with the following running example.

Example 1
.

Recall the white-noise mixing threshold problem (
Threshold
).
This is an instance of (
SDTO
) with
\langle\mathbf{c},\mathbf{z}\rangle=\mathbf{z}
,
\mathcal{Z}=\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
A(\mathbf{z})=(\mathbf{Id}/\bar{d}-\phi)\mathbf{z}
,
\mathbf{a}=\phi
, and
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})=\SOST_{\mathbb{C}}(\mathbf{d})
. Note that
\tr(A(\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{a})=\tr(\mathbf{a})
is always equal to one, and this implies that
A(\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{a}\in\sep(\mathbf{d})
. We may view
S(\phi\|\mathbf{Id})
as a line-search problem over a convex set. In principle, since
\sep(\mathbf{d})
is convex, we could apply a line-search method. In this work, we formulate it as an SDTO problem and solve it via more general-purpose algorithms.

2.1
Parameterization and duality

We discuss a smooth parameterization of
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
. Recall that the factorization size
r
is the number of components in the tensor decomposition. We call
r
a
compact
factorization size if every tensor in
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
admits a decomposition with at most
r
components. We have the following loose upper bound on the compact factorization size.

Proposition 1
.

If
\dim\bigl(\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})\bigr)=D
, then there exists a compact factorization size
r\leq 2D+1
.

Proof.

Carathéodory’s theorem states that if a point
x
lies in the convex hull
\conv(S)
with
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{D}
, then
x
is a convex combination of at most
D+1
extreme points (or generating points) in
S
. Take
S
as
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
, and realize the complex tensor as a real tensor, so we can embed
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
in
\mathbb{R}^{2D}
. Note that
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
is generated from rank-1 tensors. Thus, a tensor in
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
can be expressed as a sum of at most
2D+1
rank-1 tensors.
∎

Definition (
1
) yields the factorization-based parameterization of PSD tensors. We can construct a smooth surjective map
\Psi:\mathbb{F}^{r\bar{d}}\to\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})\subseteq\herm^{\bar{d}}
such that

\mathbf{x}=(\mathbf{x}_{11},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{rm})\mapsto\Psi(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i\in[r]}\mathbf{x}_{i1}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{i1}^{\dagger}\cdots\mathbf{x}_{im}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{im}^{\dagger},

(3)

where
\mathbf{x}_{ij}
is in the vector space
\mathbb{F}^{d_{j}}
equipped with the standard (Hermitian) inner product, and the factorization size
r
is compact. Recall that a matrix
\mathbf{y}
is PSD (equivalently,
\mathbf{y}=[\mathbf{u}_{1}\dots\mathbf{u}_{r}][\mathbf{u}_{1}\dots\mathbf{u}_{r}]^{\top}
), if
\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\mathbf{y}\mathbf{v}=\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v}\mtens\mathbf{v}^{\dagger}\rangle=\sum_{i\in[r]}(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{\top}\mathbf{v})^{2}
is nonnegative for any vector
\mathbf{v}
. Given
\mathbf{y}\in\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
, for vectors
\mathbf{v}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{v}_{m}
,
\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{v}_{1}\mtens\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots\mathbf{v}_{m}\mtens\mathbf{v}_{m}^{\dagger}\rangle
equals
\sum_{i\in[r]}(\mathbf{x}_{i1}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{1})^{2}\cdots(\mathbf{x}_{im}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{m})^{2}
, which is non-negative. This shows that
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
is indeed a generalization of PSD matrices.

The space
\mathbb{F}^{r\bar{d}}
together with
\Psi
is a so-called lift of
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
in the context of manifold optimization
53
. Thus, any optimization problem involving
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
can be transformed into a lifted nonconvex optimization problem in the space
\mathbb{F}^{r\bar{d}}
. We will show that heuristic solutions for the lifted problem can be found by the LADMM in
Section

3.1
.

We next consider the duality for (
SDTO
).
Let
A^{*}
be the adjoint of the linear map
A
, let
\mathcal{Z}^{*}
be the dual cone of
\mathcal{Z}
, and let
\SOST^{*}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
be the dual cone of
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
. Assume a standard regularity condition (e.g., Slater’s condition) holds so that there is no duality gap between (
SDTO
) and its dual:

\text{opt}=\max\{\langle-\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y}^{*}\rangle\mid\mathbf{y}^{*}\in\SOST^{*}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d}),\;-A^{*}(\mathbf{y}^{*})+\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{Z}^{*}\}.

(DSDTO)

Unlike PSD cones,
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
is not self-dual.

Proposition 2
.

The extreme rays of
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
are precisely the rank-one product tensors

\{\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}_{1}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots\mathbf{x}_{m}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{m}^{\dagger}\}.

The dual cone can be written as

\SOST^{*}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})=\{\mathbf{y}^{*}\mid\langle\mathbf{y}^{*},\mathbf{y}\rangle\geq 0\ \forall\ \mathbf{y}\in\ext(\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d}))\},

and for
m\geq 2
this dual cone strictly contains
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
.

Proof.

We first show that every tensor of the form

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}_{1}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots\mathbf{x}_{m}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{m}^{\dagger}

generates an extreme ray of
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
.
Suppose
\mathbf{y}
can be decomposed as
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{y}_{1}+\mathbf{y}_{2}
with
\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2}\in\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
.
Since
\mathbf{y}
is a rank-one PSD product tensor, any decomposition into PSD product terms must be proportional. Hence there exist
\alpha,\beta\geq 0
such that
\mathbf{y}_{1}=\alpha\mathbf{y}
and
\mathbf{y}_{2}=\beta\mathbf{y}
.
This proves that
\mathbf{y}
spans an extreme ray of
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
. Conversely, let
\mathbf{y}\in\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
be an extreme ray.
By definition,
\mathbf{y}
is a conic combination of elements of the form

\mathbf{x}_{1}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots\mathbf{x}_{m}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{m}^{\dagger}
.
If more than one such term appears with a positive coefficient, then
\mathbf{y}
can be expressed as the sum of two non-collinear elements of the cone, contradicting extremality.
Hence
\mathbf{y}
must itself be of the form above.
This establishes

\ext(\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d}))\;=\;\Bigl\{\mathbf{y}\;\big|\;\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{x}_{1}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots\mathbf{x}_{m}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{m}^{\dagger}\Bigr\}.

Next, consider the dual cone.
By definition,
\SOST^{*}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})=\{\mathbf{y}^{*}\;\mid\;\langle\mathbf{y}^{\ast},\mathbf{y}\rangle\geq 0,\;\forall\mathbf{y}\in\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})\}.

Since a closed convex cone is generated by its extreme rays, it suffices to test positivity against
\ext(\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d}))
.
Therefore,

\SOST^{*}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})=\{\mathbf{y}^{*}\;\mid\;\langle\mathbf{y}^{\ast},\mathbf{y}\rangle\geq 0,\;\forall\mathbf{y}\in\ext(\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d}))\}.

Finally, we show that inclusion is strict when
m\geq 2
.
For
m=1
,
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(d)
is the cone of PSD matrices, which is self-dual.
For
m\geq 2
, however,
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
is the cone of separable tensors, while its dual consists of block-positive tensors (operators that are nonnegative on all product vectors).
It is a standard fact that block-positivity strictly relaxes separability: there exist block-positive operators that are not separable; see the example in
57
.
∎

Unlike the cone of PSD matrices,
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
is not self-dual.
In quantum information theory, the element of
\SOST^{*}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
is called
entanglement witness
.
An entanglement witness
\mathbf{y}^{*}
is an operator that is nonnegative on all separable states and negative on at least one entangled state, thereby certifying entanglement when detected.
They can be analytically constructed for arbitrary pure states
15
but often fall short when dealing with general (mixed) entangled states
10
;
9
;
58
, which is

\mathbf{y}^{*}=\sigma-\rho+\langle\sigma,\rho-\sigma\rangle\mathbf{Id}\,,

(4)

where
\rho
is the target entangled state, and
\sigma
is its closest separable state in
\sep(\mathbf{d})
(normalization omitted).
The main difficulty lies in finding
\sigma
(which can be as hard as solving SDTO) and parametrizing
\rho
.

Remark 1
.

The map
\Psi(\mathbf{x})
of a point
\mathbf{x}
in the lift
\mathbb{F}^{r\bar{d}}
is defined as the sum of rank-1 extreme rays; any point in
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
is the sum of its extreme rays. This pairing implies that one can freely convert a point in the lift to a finite subset of extreme rays, and vice versa.

2.2
Lower and upper bounds for SDTO

We present methods for constructing lower and upper bounds for the problem (
SDTO
).

Lemma 1
.

Any feasible point
\mathbf{y}^{*}
in (
DSDTO
) provides a lower bound
\langle-\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y}^{*}\rangle
for
opt
. Any feasible point
\mathbf{y}
in (
SDTO
) provides an upper bound
\langle\mathbf{c},\mathbf{y}\rangle
for
opt
.

Proof.

The feasible point in the dual problem gives a dual lower bound, and the feasible point the primal problem gives a dual upper bound.
∎

We will use LADMM to find heuristic solutions for the nonconvex lifted problems, where
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
is replaced by the smooth map
\Psi
from a more tractable set
\mathbb{F}^{r\bar{d}}
. This heuristic method gives us a heuristic upper bound in the following senses.

Let
\mathbf{x}
be an heuristic solution to the lifted problem of (
SDTO
):

\min\{\langle\mathbf{c},\Psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle\mid\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}^{r\bar{d}}\;,\mathbf{z}\in\mathcal{Z}\;,A(\mathbf{z})+\mathbf{a}=\Psi(\mathbf{x})\},

(LSDTO)

then
\text{ub}_{\text{heur}}=\langle\mathbf{c},\Psi(\mathbf{x})\rangle
is a heuristic upper bound on
opt
.

We present a relaxation method to compute numerically reliable lower and upper bounds.
We can rewrite (
DSDTO
) in a semi-infinite programming formulation:

\text{opt}=\max\{\langle-\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y}^{*}\rangle\mid\forall\mathbf{y}\in\ext(\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})),\;\langle\mathbf{y}^{\ast},\mathbf{y}\rangle\geq 0\;,-A^{*}(\mathbf{y}^{*})+\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{Z}^{*}\}

(SemiDSDTO)

(
SemiDSDTO
) has an infinite number of linear constraints.

A tractable approximation of (
SemiDSDTO
) is to replace the infinite number of linear constraints with a finite number of linear constraints, which can be done by generating a finite subset
\mathcal{P}
from
\ext(\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d}))
. This leads to a finite-dimensional convex relaxation for (
SemiDSDTO
) and an upper bound on
opt

\text{opt}\leq\text{ub}_{\text{relax}}\coloneqq\max\{\langle-\mathbf{a},\mathbf{y}^{*}\rangle\mid\forall\mathbf{p}\in\mathcal{P},\;\langle\mathbf{y}^{\ast},\mathbf{p}\rangle\geq 0\;,-A^{*}(\mathbf{y}^{*})+\mathbf{c}\in\mathcal{Z}^{*}\}

(RelDSDTO)

Thus, a viable strategy is to solve the finite-dimensional convex relaxation (
RelDSDTO
) to obtain an upper bound and refine the relaxation by the CP algorithm
48
. The separation subproblem of (
RelDSDTO
) asks for a linear constraint
\langle\mathbf{y}^{\ast},\mathbf{p}\rangle\geq 0
violated by a given
\mathbf{y}^{\ast}
, so we can find such a constraint by solving the problem
\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\ext(\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d}))}\langle\mathbf{y}^{\ast},\mathbf{y}\rangle
. Since
\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})
is convex, this problem is equivalent to a linear minimization problem:

\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\SOST_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbf{d})}\langle\mathbf{y}^{\ast},\mathbf{y}\rangle.

(LM)

This problem is simpler than (
SDTO
), but still requires LMO.

Proposition 3
.

Let
\mathbf{y}^{\ast}
be a solution of the relaxed dual (
RelDSDTO
) and let
\underline{b}
be a lower bound for (
LM
). Then,
\text{lb}_{\text{relax}}\coloneqq\text{ub}_{\text{relax}}+\underline{b}
is a lower bound on
opt
.

Proof.

4
Branch-and-bound LMO

For

-hard—subproblems. This idea underlies methods such as Dantzig–Wolfe decomposition for integer programming
69

17

NP

Algorithm

2
is based on an LMO.

In
51
, the LMO constructs a simplicial partition of the pure product states and searches for the best solution within this a priori partition. Below, we describe an sBB-based LMO, in which the partition is realized lazily through the branching rule. We assume that readers are familiar with sBB; see
7
for the general approach and
23
for a similar pipeline in complex nonconvex second-order polynomial optimization. We summarize only the modules essential in our implementation: convex relaxations, branching rules, and heuristics.

W.l.o.g., we can normalize the tensor in the separation subproblem (
LM
) to unit trace. We focus on the complex field
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{C}
(the real case
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{R}
is analogous and simpler). With this normalization, the separation problem (
LM

\min_{\mathbf{y}\in\sep(\mathbf{d})}\langle\chi,\mathbf{y}\rangle.

(BSS)

Omitting the constraint
\tr(\mathbf{y})=1
in (
2

\sep(\mathbf{d})
yields the unnormalized BSS (which is unbounded); the two formulations are equivalent up to scaling, and we focus on the normalized one.

4.1

The sBB algorithm requires a convex outer approximation

In definition (

Our convex outer approximations are based on an equivalent, often more convenient, lifted representation of separable states.

Proposition 5
.

\sep(\mathbf{d})\;=\;\conv\bigl\{\bmtens_{k\in[m]}\mathbf{X}_{k}\;:\;\mathbf{X}_{k}\in\dmat^{d_{k}}\ \text{for all }k\in[m]\bigr\}.

Proof.

The inclusion
\sep(\mathbf{d})\subseteq\conv\{\bmtens_{k}\mathbf{X}_{k}\}

\rho=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\rho_{i}
be any convex combination with
\rho_{i}=\bmtens_{k}\mathbf{X}_{ik}
and
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
,
\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}=1

By the spectral theorem, each density matrix

admits a decomposition into rank-1 density matrices:

\mathbf{X}_{ik}\;=\;\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}\beta_{ikj}\,v_{ikj}v_{ikj}^{\dagger},\qquad\beta_{ikj}\geq 0,\ \sum_{j}\beta_{ikj}=1,

where

is a convex combination of pure product states (rank-1 tensor products). Hence

Thus,
in this section, we lift the tensor product
\mathbf{x}_{k}\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\dagger}
to
\mathbf{X}_{k}
.
This proposition implies that any convex constraints satisfied by tensors of the form

yield a convex outer approximation of
\sep(\mathbf{d})

B

\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}}=\rho_{A}\mtens(\rho_{B})^{\mtens\ell},

(12)

for
\ell\geq 1
.

The extended state
\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}}
satisfies the following constraints:

(a)

Positivity:
\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}}\succeq 0
.

(b)

Partial-trace projection: tracing out the systems
B_{2},\ldots,B_{\ell}
from
\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}}
recovers the original bipartite state:

\tr_{B_{[2:\ell]}}(\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}})=\rho_{A}\mtens\rho_{B}=\rho_{AB}.

(13)

Here
\tr_{B_{[2:\ell]}}
denotes the partial trace over subsystems
B_{2},\dots,B_{\ell}
and this operator is linear.

(c)

Symmetry: the extension is symmetric under permutations of the
\ell
copies of subsystem
B
. Equivalently, if
\Pi
is the orthogonal projector onto the symmetric subspace of
{\herm^{d_{B}}}^{\otimes\ell}
, then

(\mathbf{Id}\otimes\Pi)\,\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}}\,(\mathbf{Id}\otimes\Pi)=\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}}.

(14)

(d)

PPT: for any subset of the
B
-copies, the partial transpose with respect to that subset yields a PSD matrix. In particular, for any
s=1,\ldots,\ell
and any choice of
s
copies,

\mathsf{T}_{B_{[s]}}(\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}})\succeq 0,

(15)

where
\mathsf{T}_{B_{[s]}}
denotes the partial transpose on the selected
B
-subsystems; see (
27
) for the elementwise definition.

The PSD variable
\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}}
lives in an extended space of dimension
(d_{A}d^{\ell}_{B})^{2}
. Define

\dps_{\ell}(d_{AB})\;=\;\{\,w_{AB}\in\dmat^{d_{AB}}:\ \exists\ \rho_{AB_{[\ell]}}\ \text{satisfying }\eqref{eq:extensionpartialtracecond},\ \eqref{eq:extensionsymmetrycond},\ \eqref{eq:extensionpptcond}\,\}.

(16)

We refer to the collection of conditions above as the level-
\ell
DPS constraints, which are completely determined by the configuration
(d_{A},d_{B},\ell)
. The DPS hierarchy yields a nested family of convex outer approximations of
\sep(d_{AB})
:

\sep(d_{AB})\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\dps_{\ell}(d_{AB})\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\dps_{1}(d_{AB}).

(17)

The DPS constraints are often stated without explicitly enforcing unit-trace constraints on the extended variables; unit-trace constraints on the extended variables are often added in practice.

Remark 4
.

The DPS hierarchy can also be used to construct convex outer approximations for separable multipartite states by grouping subsystems into a bipartition. For example, writing a tripartite state as the bipartition
\rho_{ABC}=\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{BC}
allows one to relax it as a bipartite system for the pair
(A^{\prime}=A,B^{\prime}=BC)
. Thus, the DPS constraints corresponding to the configuration
(d_{A^{\prime}},d_{B^{\prime}},\ell)
give a convex outer approximation of
\mathcal{C}
for separable multipartite states. This approach is available in Ket.jl.

4.1.2
Factorizable DPS outer approximations for multipartite systems

We build convex outer approximations of separable multipartite states via factorization.
Because the tensor product is associative, there are several ways to evaluate the expression
\mathbf{y}=\bmtens_{k\in[m]}\mathbf{X}_{k}

\displaystyle 1,

\displaystyle\dps_{\ell}((d_{k_{A}},d_{k_{B}})),

4.1.3
Complex McCormick inequalities for multipartite systems

The conventional sBB method typically branches on univariate or bilinear expressions so that the relaxations of these expressions tighten as variable ranges shrink
7
. The DPS constraints derived from unnormalized separable states do not depend on variable bounds. Therefore, we choose to work with the normalized BSS formulation, which allows us to derive variable bounds and LP relaxations that depend on those bounds.

The bilinear equation
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
generally does not hold in the DDPS system (
19
), and we propose valid LP inequalities that relax the entry-wise bilinear equation in
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
. These linear inequalities can be tightened as variable bounds are reduced by the branching rule that enforces the bilinear equation.

For any Hermitian matrix
\mathbf{Z}
, we separate real and imaginary parts and write
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{i}\mathbf{V}
, where
\mathbf{U}
is real symmetric and
\mathbf{V}
is real skew-symmetric. We first state a useful implication about density matrices.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{U}+\mathbf{i}\mathbf{V}\in\herm^{d}
. Consider the following statements:

1.

\mathbf{Z}\succeq 0
and
\tr(\mathbf{Z})=1
;

2.

\mathbf{Z}\succeq 0
and
\mathbf{Z}\preceq\mathbf{Id}
;

3.

every
2\times 2
principal submatrix
\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}+\mathbf{i}\mathbf{V}^{\prime}
of
\mathbf{Z}
satisfies
\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\succeq 0
and
\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\preceq\mathbf{Id}
;

4.

for every
2\times 2
principal submatrix
\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}=\mathbf{U}^{\prime}+\mathbf{i}\mathbf{V}^{\prime}
of
\mathbf{Z}
we have
0\leq U^{\prime}_{ii}\leq 1
,
V^{\prime}_{ii}=0
for
i\in\{1,2\}
, and
-1\leq V^{\prime}_{ij}\leq 1
,
-1\leq U^{\prime}_{ij}\leq 1
for
i\neq j
.

Then the implications
1\implies 2\implies 3\implies 4
hold.

Proof.

If
\mathbf{Z}\succeq 0
and
\tr(\mathbf{Z})=1
, diagonalize
\mathbf{Z}
by a unitary
T
so that
T\mathbf{Z}T^{\dagger}
is diagonal with entries in
[0,1]
. Hence
T\mathbf{Z}T^{\dagger}\preceq\mathbf{Id}
, and by congruence with a unitary we get
\mathbf{Z}\preceq\mathbf{Id}
. This proves
1\!\implies\!2
.

If
\mathbf{Z}\succeq 0
and
\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}\succeq 0
, then every principal
2\times 2
submatrix of both matrices is PSD, so
2\!\implies\!3
.

Assume
3
holds. Note that
U^{\prime}_{ii}\geq 0,V^{\prime}_{ii}=0
(
i\in\{1,2\}
) follows from the Hermitian PSD of
\mathbf{U}^{\prime}+\mathbf{i}\mathbf{V}^{\prime}
. Let
E_{i}
be the diagonal matrix with only
i
-th diagonal being one, then
U^{\prime}_{ii}=\tr[\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}E_{i}]\leq\tr[\mathbf{Id}E_{i}]=1
. Let the all-ones matrix be
E
, then
0=\tr[0E]\leq\tr[\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}E]\leq\tr[\mathbf{Id}E]=2
. Since
\tr[\mathbf{V}^{\prime}E]=0
,
\tr[\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}E]=\tr[\mathbf{U}^{\prime}E]=U^{\prime}_{11}+U^{\prime}_{22}+2U^{\prime}_{12}
. Then, we have
-(U^{\prime}_{11}+U^{\prime}_{22})/2\leq U^{\prime}_{12}\leq(2-(U^{\prime}_{11}+U^{\prime}_{22}))/2
. Let
E^{\prime}=[1,1;-1,1]
, then
0=\tr[0E^{\prime}]\leq\tr[\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}E^{\prime}]\leq\tr[\mathbf{Id}E^{\prime}]=2
. Then,
\tr[\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}E]=\tr[\mathbf{U}^{\prime}E]=U^{\prime}_{11}+U^{\prime}_{22}+2V^{\prime}_{12}
. Then, we have
-(U^{\prime}_{11}+U^{\prime}_{22})/2\leq V^{\prime}_{12}\leq(2-(U^{\prime}_{11}+U^{\prime}_{22}))/2
. Then, the assumption that
\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\succeq 0
and
\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}\preceq\mathbf{Id}
implies that

\begin{split}Z^{\prime}\in\herm^{d}&\implies 0\leq V^{\prime}_{ii}\leq 0,\\
\tr[0E_{i}]\leq\tr[\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}E_{i}]\leq\tr[\mathbf{Id}E_{i}]&\implies 0\leq U^{\prime}_{ii}\leq 1,\\
\tr[0E]\leq\tr[\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}E]\leq\tr[\mathbf{Id}E]&\implies-1\leq\frac{-U^{\prime}_{11}-U^{\prime}_{22}}{2}\leq U^{\prime}_{12}\leq\frac{2-(U^{\prime}_{11}+U^{\prime}_{22})}{2}\leq 1,\\
\tr[0E^{\prime}]\leq\tr[\mathbf{Z}^{\prime}E^{\prime}]\leq\tr[\mathbf{Id}E^{\prime}]&\implies-1\leq\frac{-U^{\prime}_{11}-U^{\prime}_{22}}{2}\leq V^{\prime}_{12}\leq\frac{2-(U^{\prime}_{11}+U^{\prime}_{22})}{2}\leq 1.\end{split}

(21)

Thus,
3\implies 4
.
∎

We now consider entries of
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathbf{U}_{k}+\mathbf{i}\mathbf{V}_{k}
, which satisfy condition 1 in
Theorem

1
. Let
\overline{\mathbf{U}}_{k},\overline{\mathbf{V}}_{k}
(resp.
\underline{\mathbf{U}}_{k},\underline{\mathbf{V}}_{k}
) denote entry-wise upper (resp. lower) bounds implied by condition 4. Define the index mapping
\delta(i_{A},i_{B})=i_{A}d_{k_{B}}+i_{B}
. The entries of
\mathbf{Z}_{k}
can be written as bilinear products of entries of
\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
:

\begin{split}&Z_{k,\delta(i_{A},i_{B}),\delta(j_{A},j_{B})}\\
&\qquad=(U_{k_{A},i_{A}j_{A}}+\mathbf{i}V_{k_{A},i_{A}j_{A}})(U_{k_{B},i_{B}j_{B}}+\mathbf{i}V_{k_{B},i_{B}j_{B}})\\
&\qquad=U_{k_{A},i_{A}j_{A}}\,U_{k_{B},i_{B}j_{B}}-V_{k_{A},i_{A}j_{A}}\,V_{k_{B},i_{B}j_{B}}\\
&\qquad\quad+\mathbf{i}\bigl(V_{k_{A},i_{A}j_{A}}\,U_{k_{B},i_{B}j_{B}}+U_{k_{A},i_{A}j_{A}}\,V_{k_{B},i_{B}j_{B}}\bigr).\end{split}

(22)

The convex envelope of a real bilinear term
W_{A}W_{B}
over the box
[\underline{W}_{A},\overline{W}_{A}]\times[\underline{W}_{B},\overline{W}_{B}]
is given by McCormick inequalities

:

\displaystyle W_{A}W_{B}

\displaystyle\geq\overline{W}_{B}W_{A}+\overline{W}_{A}W_{B}-\overline{W}_{A}\overline{W}_{B},

(23a)

\displaystyle W_{A}W_{B}

\displaystyle\geq\underline{W}_{B}W_{A}+\underline{W}_{A}W_{B}-\underline{W}_{A}\underline{W}_{B},

(23b)

\displaystyle W_{A}W_{B}

\displaystyle\leq\underline{W}_{B}W_{A}+\overline{W}_{A}W_{B}-\overline{W}_{A}\underline{W}_{B},

(23c)

\displaystyle W_{A}W_{B}

\displaystyle\leq\overline{W}_{B}W_{A}+\underline{W}_{A}W_{B}-\underline{W}_{A}\overline{W}_{B}.

(23d)

These inequalities can also be obtained via the scalar RLT procedure in
62
. Denote the sets of affine under- and over-estimators of

by
\underline{W_{A}W_{B}}
and
\overline{W_{A}W_{B}}
, respectively.

For brevity set

u_{A}=U_{k_{A},i_{A}j_{A}},\quad u_{B}=U_{k_{B},i_{B}j_{B}},\quad v_{A}=V_{k_{A},i_{A}j_{A}},\quad v_{B}=V_{k_{B},i_{B}j_{B}},

and

\mu_{AB}=U_{k,\delta(i_{A},i_{B}),\delta(j_{A},j_{B})},\quad\nu_{AB}=V_{k,\delta(i_{A},i_{B}),\delta(j_{A},j_{B})}.

Applying McCormick to the four real bilinear terms
u_{A}u_{B}
,

,
v_{A}u_{B}
, and

and combining them yields a collection of linear inequalities (aggregated McCormick inequalities):

\displaystyle\forall a\in\underline{u_{A}u_{B}},\ \forall a^{\prime}\in\overline{v_{A}v_{B}}:\quad\mu_{AB}\geq a(u_{A},u_{B})-a^{\prime}(v_{A},v_{B}),

(24a)

(24b)

(24c)

\displaystyle\forall a\in\overline{u_{A}v_{B}},\ \forall a^{\prime}\in\overline{v_{A}u_{B}}:\quad\nu_{AB}\leq a(u_{A},v_{B})+a^{\prime}(v_{A},u_{B}).

(24d)

Relaxing the entry-wise equations in
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
by these inequalities yields an LP relaxation for the BSS problem.

4.1.4
Tensor RLT procedure and inequalities

We now introduce valid SDP constraints derived from a tensor RLT procedure for PSD variables. The construction generalizes the scalar RLT and is based on tensor products.

The tensor RLT on two SDP constraints appears in
3
;
65
. We first review and apply it to the proposed relaxations, and then extend it for multiple SDP constraints. Let
F(\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}})
and
H(\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}})
be two linear matrix-valued maps acting on PSD variables
\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}
and
\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
. If

F(\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}})\succeq 0
and
H(\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}})\succeq 0
, then by Proposition 1.1 of
3
the tensor product

F(\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}})\mtens H(\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}})\succeq 0
also holds. The linearization step rewrites

F(\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}})\mtens H(\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}})
as a linear map in the variables
\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}
,
\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
and the auxiliary variable
\mathbf{Z}_{k}
representing
\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
, yielding a valid SDP inequality. The tensor product
\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
is then replaced by the auxiliary PSD variable
\mathbf{Z}_{k}
(the so-called RLT variable).

Applying this construction to the PSD variables appearing in the dichotomy DPS relaxation leads to the following tensor McCormick inequalities:

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{k}

\displaystyle\preceq\mathbf{Id}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}},

(25a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{k}

\displaystyle\preceq\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Id},

(25b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}_{k}

\displaystyle\succeq\mathbf{Id}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}+\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Id},

(25c)

which are valid whenever
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
and the factors are density matrices.

Proposition 7
.

Given density matrices
\mathbf{Z}_{k},\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}},\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
, the tensor McCormick inequalities (
25
) are valid consequences of the equality
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
.

Proof.

By
Theorem

1
we may use the factor constraints

\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\succeq 0,\ \mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}\succeq 0,\ \mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\succeq 0,\ \mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}\succeq 0
.
Taking tensor products of suitable factor combinations yields PSD constraints such as

\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}\succeq 0,\qquad(\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}})\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}\succeq 0,\qquad\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens(\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}})\succeq 0,

and so on. Expanding these tensor-factor constraints and then linearizing the terms that involve
\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
produces matrix inequalities of the form (
25
). Thus (
25
) follows from the factor constraints and the identity
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
.
∎

One can show that the tensor McCormick inequalities are stronger than the aggregated (entrywise) McCormick inequalities (
24
).

Theorem 2
.

If density matrices
\mathbf{Z}_{k},\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}},\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
satisfy the tensor McCormick inequalities (
25
), then the aggregated McCormick inequalities (
24
) also hold.

Proof.

By symmetry it suffices to derive (
24a
) from (
25
). The aggregated McCormick inequalities are linearizations of bilinear factor constraints such as

(u_{A}-\underline{u}_{A})(u_{B}-\underline{u}_{B})\geq 0
and similar expressions for the imaginary parts. According to the proof in
Theorem

1
, each scalar bound like
u_{A}\geq\underline{u}_{A}
is implied by trace inequalities applying suitable projections against
0\preceq\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\preceq\mathbf{Id}
and
0\preceq\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}\preceq\mathbf{Id}
; likewise for
u_{B}
. Suppose
u_{A}\geq\underline{u}_{A}
is implied by
\tr[E_{1}\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}]+\tr[E_{2}(\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}})]+\tr[E_{2}\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}]+\tr[E_{4}(\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}})]\geq 0
and
u_{B}\geq\underline{u}_{B}
is implied by
\tr[E_{5}\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}]+\tr[E_{6}(\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}})]+\tr[E_{7}\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}]+\tr[E_{8}(\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}})]\geq 0
. Thus,
(u_{A}-\underline{u}_{A})(u_{B}-\underline{u}_{B})\geq 0
is implied by the product inequality
(\tr[E_{1}\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}]+\tr[E_{2}(\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}})]+\tr[E_{2}\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}]+\tr[E_{4}(\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}})])(\tr[E_{5}\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}]+\tr[E_{6}(\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}})]+\tr[E_{7}\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}]+\tr[E_{8}(\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}})])\geq 0
. Using rules
\tr[D_{1}\mathbf{Z}_{1}]\tr[D_{2}\mathbf{Z}_{2}]=\tr[(D_{1}\mtens D_{2})(\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{2})]
, we can rewrite the product inequality as the sum of trace inequalities applying suitable projections against tensor McCormick inequalities in (
25
).
∎

Note that scalar bounds implied by
\mathbf{Id}\succeq\mathbf{Z}\succeq 0
do not yield RLT inequalities stronger than those produced by the tensor RLT. Therefore, the convex outer approximation
\mathcal{C}
is defined by constraints in the DDPS system (
18
) and the scalar and tensor McCormick inequalities.

We now present the tensor RLT procedure on multiple SDP constraints, thus fully generalizing the Sherali-Adams RLT for scalar polynomials
63
. Consider the alphabet of matrix symbols

\mathfrak{Z}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{Z}_{k},\mathbf{Id}_{k}\}_{k\in N},

and its extension

\bar{\mathfrak{Z}}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{Z}_{k},\mathbf{Z}_{k}^{\top},\mathbf{Id}_{k},\tr(\mathbf{Z}_{k}),\tr(\mathbf{Z}_{k}^{\top})\}_{k\in N},

where
\mathbf{Z}_{k}^{\top}
denotes the transpose. Let
\langle\mathfrak{Z}\rangle
be the family of finite ordered multisets over
\mathfrak{Z}
. Each multiset
\mathfrak{S}\in\langle\mathfrak{Z}\rangle
gives rise to a tensor monomial
\bmtens_{\mathbf{Z}\in\mathfrak{S}}\mathbf{Z}
, and the monomials are in general noncommutative.

We extend the partial trace and partial transpose maps to act on tensor monomials. For
M\in\bmtens_{k\in[m]}\mathbb{C}^{d_{k}\times d_{k}}
and a subset
S\subseteq[m]
, the partial trace
\tr_{S}
and partial transpose
\mathsf{T}_{S}
are defined entry-wise by the usual index contractions and index swaps, respectively:

\displaystyle\tr_{S}\bigl([M_{i_{1},j_{1},\dots,i_{m},j_{m}}]\bigr)

\displaystyle=[\sum_{i_{k},j_{k}:k\in S}M_{i_{1},j_{1},\dots,i_{m},j_{m}}],

(26)

\displaystyle\mathsf{T}_{S}\bigl([M_{i_{1},j_{1},\dots,i_{m},j_{m}}]\bigr)

\displaystyle=[M_{\bar{i}_{1},\bar{j}_{1},\dots,\bar{i}_{m},\bar{j}_{m}}],

(27)

where
\bar{i}_{k}=\begin{cases}i_{k}\quad k\notin S\\
j_{k}\quad k\in S\end{cases}
, and
\bar{j}_{k}=\begin{cases}j_{k}\quad k\notin S\\
i_{k}\quad k\in S\end{cases}
(indices are swapped on the subsystems in
S
).

The following rewriting lemma is useful.

Theorem 3
.

Any tensor monomial
\bar{\mathfrak{S}}\in\langle\bar{\mathfrak{Z}}\rangle
can be written as
\tr_{S_{A}}\!\bigl(\mathsf{T}_{S_{B}}(\mathfrak{S})\bigr)
for some
\mathfrak{S}\in\langle\mathfrak{Z}\rangle
and suitable index sets
S_{A},S_{B}
.

Proof.

Replace every occurrence of a trace symbol
\tr(\cdot)
by an explicit partial trace over the corresponding subsystem (this produces
\tr_{S_{A}}
). Then, replace every transpose symbol by a corresponding partial transpose
\mathsf{T}_{S_{B}}
. The remaining object is a tensor monomial in the original alphabet
\mathfrak{Z}
; thus the original monomial equals
\tr_{S_{A}}\bigl(\mathsf{T}_{S_{B}}(\mathfrak{S})\bigr)
for some
\mathfrak{S}\in\langle\mathfrak{Z}\rangle
.
∎

The tensor RLT constructs valid SDP inequalities by the following steps, applied to a finite multiset
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
of factor constraints drawn from

\mathcal{F}=\{\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z},\mathbf{Z}^{\top},\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}^{\top},\tr(\mathbf{Z})-1,\tr(\mathbf{Z}^{\top})-1:\mathbf{Z}\in\mathfrak{Z}\}.

1.

(Constraint-factor) Form the tensor product
\bmtens_{f\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}f
, which is PSD (or zero) by construction.

2.

(Product expansion) Expand the tensor product as a linear combination of monomials in
\bar{\mathfrak{Z}}
:

\bmtens_{f\in\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}f\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{i}\,\bar{\mathfrak{S}}_{i}.

3.

(Monomial rewriting) Apply Theorem
3
to write each
\bar{\mathfrak{S}}_{i}
as
\tr_{S_{A_{i}}}\bigl(\mathsf{T}_{S_{B_{i}}}(\mathfrak{S}_{i})\bigr)
with
\mathfrak{S}_{i}\in\langle\mathfrak{Z}\rangle
.

4.

(Linearization) Introduce an auxiliary matrix variable
X_{\mathfrak{S}_{i}}
for each monomial
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
and replace
\mathfrak{S}_{i}
by
X_{\mathfrak{S}_{i}}
. This yields SDP constraints of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}a_{i}\,\tr_{S_{A_{i}}}\!\bigl(\mathsf{T}_{S_{B_{i}}}(X_{\mathfrak{S}_{i}})\bigr)\succeq(\,=)\ 0,\qquad X_{\mathfrak{S}_{i}}\succeq 0.

These matrix inequalities are tensor RLT inequalities. The procedure generates many useful constraints; we illustrate it with a short example.

Example 4
.

Take
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=(\mathbf{Z}_{1},\mathbf{Id}-\mathbf{Z}_{2},\tr(\mathbf{Z}_{3}^{\top})-1)
. Expanding yields

\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mtens\mathbf{Id}\mtens\tr(\mathbf{Z}_{3}^{\top})-\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{2}\mtens\tr(\mathbf{Z}_{3}^{\top})-\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mtens\mathbf{Id}+\mathbf{Z}_{1}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{2}.

Rewriting and linearizing produce, for instance,

\tr_{\{3\}}\mathsf{T}_{\{3\}}(X_{\mathbf{Z}_{1},\mathbf{Id},\mathbf{Z}_{3}})-\tr_{\{3\}}\mathsf{T}_{\{3\}}(X_{\mathbf{Z}_{1},\mathbf{Z}_{2},\mathbf{Z}_{3}})-X_{\mathbf{Z}_{1},\mathbf{Id}}+X_{\mathbf{Z}_{1},\mathbf{Z}_{2}}=0,

a valid tensor RLT equality in the lifted variables
X_{\cdot}
.

Finally, many DPS constraints can be recovered from tensor RLT constructions. For instance:

(a)

Positivity
: Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=(\rho_{A})\cup(\rho_{B})_{k\in[\ell]}
, then the corresponding RLT inequality is
\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}}\succeq 0
.

(b)

Partial Trace Projection
: Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})\cup(\tr(\rho_{B})-1)_{k\in[2:\ell]}
, then the corresponding RLT equality is
\tr_{B_{[2:\ell]}}(\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}})-\rho_{AB}=0
.

(c)

PPT
: Let
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=(\rho_{A})\cup(\rho^{\top}_{B})_{k\in[s]}\cup(\rho_{B})_{k\in[s+1:\ell]}
, then the corresponding RLT inequality is
\mathsf{T}_{B_{[s]}}(\rho_{AB_{[\ell]}})\succeq 0
.

Hence the tensor RLT provides a unified framework that subsumes DPS-type constraints and generates additional structured SDP inequalities useful for relaxations of multipartite separability.

4.2
Branching

We now describe the branching rule used in our sBB algorithm.
At an open node of the sBB search tree, the branching rule selects an entry of some intermediate variable
\mathbf{Z}_{k}
and tightens its bounds when creating the subnodes. A good branching choice should tighten the relaxations in the subnodes so that the parent node’s relaxation solution becomes infeasible in at least one subnode; otherwise the subnodes will inherit the same lower bound and the tree search will not make progress. Such a branching rule is called violating, and it is important for the sBB algorithm to make consistent progress.

We propose a violating branching rule that targets the relaxed equation
\mathbf{Z}_{k}=\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}\mtens\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
. Let the values of
\mathbf{Z}_{k},\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}},\mathbf{Z}_{k_{B}}
in the parent node’s relaxation solution be the reference values. The rule evaluates a violation score for each entry and then selects the entry with the highest score as the branching candidate. For instance, suppose we branch on the real entry
u_{A}=U_{k_{A},i_{A}j_{A}}
of
\mathbf{Z}_{k_{A}}
. Given its current bounds
[\underline{u_{A}},\overline{u_{A}}]
and its reference value
u^{\prime}_{A}\in[\underline{u_{A}},\overline{u_{A}}]
, we create two subnodes with bounds
[\underline{u_{A}},u^{\prime}_{A}]
and
[u^{\prime}_{A},\overline{u_{A}}]
. The McCormick inequalities (
24
) are updated in each subnode using the new bounds.

The proposed rule is similar in spirit to strong branching for mixed-integer programs: we simulate splitting at the reference value for each candidate entry and estimate how much the reference values are violated by the updated McCormick inequalities (
24
). Concretely, consider the scalar variables that appear in a single entry relation (
22
):
the real and imaginary parts
u_{A},v_{A},u_{B},v_{B}
and the linearized variables
\mu_{AB},\nu_{AB}
, with reference values
u^{\prime}_{A},v^{\prime}_{A},u^{\prime}_{B},v^{\prime}_{B},\mu^{\prime}_{AB},\nu^{\prime}_{AB}
. Splitting at
u^{\prime}_{A}
produces two subnodes; for each subnode we compute the amount by which the reference point violates the McCormick constraints. The per-constraint violations are

\displaystyle\max\Bigl(\max_{a\in\underline{u_{A}u_{B}},\,a^{\prime}\in\overline{v_{A}v_{B}}}\bigl(a(u^{\prime}_{A},u^{\prime}_{B})-a^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{A},v^{\prime}_{B})\bigr)-\mu^{\prime}_{AB},\,0\Bigr),

(28a)

\displaystyle\max\Bigl(\mu^{\prime}_{AB}-\max_{a\in\underline{u_{A}u_{B}},\,a^{\prime}\in\overline{v_{A}v_{B}}}\bigl(a(u^{\prime}_{A},u^{\prime}_{B})-a^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{A},v^{\prime}_{B})\bigr),\,0\Bigr),

(28b)

\displaystyle\max\Bigl(\max_{a\in\underline{u_{A}v_{B}},\,a^{\prime}\in\underline{v_{A}u_{B}}}\bigl(a(u^{\prime}_{A},v^{\prime}_{B})+a^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{A},u^{\prime}_{B})\bigr)-\nu^{\prime}_{AB},\,0\Bigr),

(28c)

\displaystyle\max\Bigl(\nu^{\prime}_{AB}-\max_{a\in\overline{u_{A}v_{B}},\,a^{\prime}\in\overline{v_{A}u_{B}}}\bigl(a(u^{\prime}_{A},v^{\prime}_{B})+a^{\prime}(v^{\prime}_{A},u^{\prime}_{B})\bigr),\,0\Bigr).

(28d)

Summing these four terms yields the total violation for a given subnode. Let the sums for the two subnodes be
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
, respectively.

Proposition 8
.

If
s_{1}>0
and
s_{2}>0
, then branching on
u_{A}
renders the reference point infeasible in both subnodes.

Proof.

By construction,
s_{i}>0
implies that at least one McCormick inequality from (
24
) is violated at the reference point under the subnode bounds; hence, the reference point is infeasible in that subnode. If both
s_{1}>0
and
s_{2}>0
, infeasibility holds in both subnodes.
∎

We combine the two subnode scores into a single violation score to prioritize branching candidates. Empirically we use

s\;=\;\tfrac{5}{6}\min(s_{1},s_{2})\;+\;\tfrac{1}{6}\max(s_{1},s_{2}),

so the violating branching rule selects the variable with the largest
s
.

This violating rule applies generally to bilinear programs where McCormick relaxations are used. Other branching strategies exist for complex variables and PSD constraints (e.g., rules that force the rank of a PSD matrix to one)
23
, but those typically estimate objective change and are not directly tailored to bilinear McCormick structures as used here.

4.3
Heuristics

The sBB algorithm also needs heuristics for finding solutions that provide upper bounds for the BSS problem; we use a simple alternating heuristic.

The alternating heuristic iteratively updates rank-1 density matrices
\{\mathbf{X}_{k}\}_{k\in[m]}
that provide a solution
\mathbf{y}=\bmtens_{k\in[m]}\mathbf{X}_{k}
for (
BSS
). In each iteration, the heuristic fixes
m-1
density matrices and maximizes the objective
\langle\chi,\bmtens_{k\in[m]}\mathbf{X}_{k}\rangle
with respect to the remaining free density matrix. Given
\{\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{k}\}_{k\in[m]}
from the previous iteration and assuming the index
k^{\prime}
is free, the restricted BSS problem is equivalent to the following SDP problem:

\max_{\forall k\in[m]/\{k^{\prime}\},\mathbf{X}_{k}=\mathbf{X}^{\prime}_{k},\mathbf{X}_{k^{\prime}}\in\dmat^{d_{k^{\prime}}}}\langle\chi,\bmtens_{k\in[m]}\mathbf{X}_{k}\rangle=\max_{\mathbf{X}_{k^{\prime}}\in\dmat^{d_{k^{\prime}}}}\langle\chi_{k^{\prime}},\mathbf{X}_{k^{\prime}}\rangle,

(29)

where
\chi_{k^{\prime}}
is a tensor contraction of
\chi
on the
k^{\prime}
-th mode.
Note that the optimal solution
\mathbf{X}_{k^{\prime}}
must be a rank-1 matrix
\mathbf{x}\mtens\mathbf{x}^{\dagger}
, where
\mathbf{x}
is the unit eigenvector corresponding to the most negative eigenvalue of the matrix
\chi_{k^{\prime}}
.

We initialize the alternating heuristic with solutions of the convex relaxation at the search-tree nodes. We choose the free index
k^{\prime}
cyclically and stop the alternating heuristic when it converges or when it reaches the iteration limit. This heuristic was first introduced in
61
for separability certification and applied in
51
as well.

5
Numerical experiments

In this section, we present the numerical experiments of the existing and proposed algorithms on the white-noise mixing
threshold problem. The data, detailed results, and source code are available on the online repository:
https://github.com/ZIB-IOL/TensorOpt4Entanglement
.

5.1
Development environment.

All experiments were conducted on a cluster equipped with Intel Xeon Gold 5122 CPUs, operating at 3.60 GHz with 96 GB of RAM. Each test was executed on a single thread with a memory limit of 10GB RAM. All algorithms are implemented in the Julia language and JuMP modelling language. The following solvers and software packages were used: Mosek (for solving LPs and SDPs), Manopt
8
(for using quasi-Newton method), and Ket.jl
4
(for generating quantum states and computing lower bounds on white-noise mixing
threshold).

5.2
Problem instances.

Our benchmark consists of the following instances:

•

Greenberger–Horne–Zeilinger (GHZ) states
35
: GHZ states are typical examples of maximally entangled multipartite states. Its white-noise threshold is analytically known as
1-1/(1+2^{m-1})
for
m\geq 2

28
.

•

Dicke states
25
: Dicke states describe the highly entangled state of an ensemble with fixed energy excitation.
Their entanglement is maximally persistent and robust under particle losses
36
.

•

Cluster states
20
: Cluster states describe a type of entangled states in lattices with Ising-type interactions. Their entanglement is more robust to noise and local operations. For
m=2
and
m=3
, the cluster states are equivalent to the Bell state and GHZ state (up to local transformations), respectively.

Note that all the instances are states of
m
-partite systems, where the dimension of each subsystem is
2\times 2
.

5.3
Algorithms implementations and details

We implement the following algorithms:

•

SDP-based alternating optimization (Alt-SDP): our implementation follows
56
. The method represents separable states as a sum of
r
tensor components, each a Kronecker product of subsystem density matrices (see
Proposition

5
). In each iteration, one density matrix per component is left free and updated, so each update reduces to an SDP and yields an upper bound on the white-noise mixing threshold. Our implementation sets
r
the same as the factorization size in the other algorithms (the same baseline).

•

Primal-dual geometric reconstruction (PDGR) method: We also report the best available upper and lower bounds computed by PDGR
51
, which is backed by the advanced CG algorithms implemented in FrankWolfe.jl
12
. In the trial step, the method applies a line search with the CG-based entanglement detection to find entangled and interpolated states
\rho(\mathbf{z};\phi,\mathbf{Id})
for
1\geq\mathbf{z}\geq 0
(defined in (
Threshold
)). The corresponding
\mathbf{z}
establishes lower bounds on the white-noise mixing threshold. During each line-search iteration, the PDGR method also tries to reconstruct a separable state
\rho(\mathbf{z}^{*};\phi,\mathbf{Id})
between
\rho(\mathbf{z};\phi,\mathbf{Id})
and
\mathbf{Id}/\bar{d}
using the separability certification of GR. The corresponding
\mathbf{z}^{*}
are upper bounds on the white-noise mixing threshold.

•

LADMM (
Algorithm

1
): the quasi-Newton solver from Manopt is used for solving lifted nonconvex subproblems. LADMM typically returns a heuristic solution of (
Threshold
) and therefore a heuristic upper bound together with a feasibility residual
\|\mathbf{y}-A(\mathbf{z})-\mathbf{a}\|_{2}
. Following
Remark

3
, we optionally run one CP iteration to refine the LADMM output and obtain a nearby feasible point with a certified upper bound.

•

Cutting-plane (CP
Algorithm

2
): see
Example

2
for the LPs solved inside the CP routine. The CP algorithm uses the tailored sBB method as the LMO for the BSS problem and provides both upper and lower bounds on the white-noise mixing threshold.

•

Iterative refinement (IR
Algorithm

3
): the IR alternates between LADMM and CP to produce tightened upper and (Lagrangian) lower bounds on the white-noise mixing threshold.

•

DPS: we use Ket.jl to obtain lower bounds provided by (
10
) with the DPS hierarchy (see
Remark

4
).

•

DDPS+: we construct the relaxation (
10
) by the proposed (first-level) DDPS outer approximation (
19
) with scalar McCormick inequalities (
24
) and tensor McCormick inequalities (
25
), which provides lower bounds.

Experimental settings and parameters are as follows.

•

Time limits and tolerances: time limits are set to 1/2/3 hours for
m=3/4/5
, respectively. Numerical tolerances are
10^{-6}
unless stated otherwise.

•

Factorization size and initialization: the factorization size
r
is set to 256/512/652 for
m=3/4/5
, respectively. We generate
r

\mathbf{x}_{1}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\dagger}\cdots\mtens\mathbf{x}_{m}\mtens\mathbf{x}_{m}^{\dagger}

•

CP and sBB LMO: within the IR algorithm, the CP subroutine iteration limit is set to the factorization size; the standalone CP routine has no iteration limit and terminates when upper and lower bounds coincide. For the sBB LMO we set the node limit to 120/100/1 for
m=3/4/5

Before 90% of the global time limit is reached, the sBB stops as soon as a violated cut is found; if no solution is found within the node limit, we fall back to the alternating heuristic with several random starts. After 90% of the time limit, the CP routine focuses on improving dual lower bounds by running the sBB up to a tighter node limit of 70/50/15 for

•

LADMM settings: in the IR algorithm, the LADMM iteration limits are 10/10/4 for

(30)

DPS parameters: we set the number of extended copies as large as allowed by Ket.jl. Concretely, for

5.4.3
Analysis of hard instances

For
m=5
, the scalability limitations become apparent: we must restrict the factorization size and rely on low-rank approximations. The IR algorithm remains the most effective at decreasing the upper bound (particularly relative to Alt-SDP), but it is less effective at improving the (Lagrangian) lower bound than the pure CP procedure. Recall that the Lagrangian lower bound is computed as
\text{lb}_{\text{relax}}=\text{ub}_{\text{relax}}+\underline{b}
, where
\underline{b}
is the sBB LMO’s lower bound for the BSS separation subproblem. Averaged values of these quantities (the gap-closing CP iteration statistics:
\text{ub}_{\text{relax}}
,
\text{lb}_{\text{relax}}
, and
\underline{b}
) are reported in
Table

. Although IR yields a smaller
\text{ub}_{\text{relax}}
, its associated
\underline{b}
is substantially more negative than that obtained by the CP-only approach, leading to a worse (smaller)
\text{lb}_{\text{relax}}
.

This lower‑bound deterioration highlights a shortcoming of the IR for large-scale problems: we reuse LADMM’s tensor factorization of
\mathbf{x}
and the corresponding extreme components
R(\mathbf{x})
to warm-start the CP. However, compared to the standalone CP, the CP subroutine in the IR is not run for a sufficient number of iterations, and the CP is still far from convergence. In our CP implementation, termination occurs when the LMO returns a nonnegative optimal value (no violated cuts). Otherwise, a violated cut (negative value) is added. When the CP subroutine is not converging, the LMO’s optimal solution can be unstable and the optimal value
b
as well as its lower bound
\underline{b}
can be very negative. These observations suggest that, for large-scale problems, CP subroutines need many iterations to converge.

5.4.4
Summary

The Alt-SDP heuristic is easy to implement and computationally fast, but it provides no lower bounds and may miss small objective improvements that more expensive methods can capture. LADMM (lifted ADMM) often attains slightly better objectives than simple alternating updates and typically yields near-feasible solutions with small residuals (around
10^{-4}
). However, achieving high-accuracy convergence is extremely difficult (taking hours for larger instances). In addition, LADMM is sensitive to parameter tuning, can violate coupling constraints, and usually requires a CP crossover or refinement step to turn heuristic solutions into certified feasible solutions.

The PDGR method is more problem-specific, since it is designed for the line-search formulation. It can provide the best lower bounds and competitive upper bounds for small instances (
m<5
). For larger-scale cases (
m=5
), the CP/IR pipeline with the sBB LMO offers a reliable approach to numerically certified upper bounds and feasible solutions, while DDPS+ improves the SDP-based lower bounds produced by the bipartite DPS hierarchy. The principal computational bottleneck for these methods lies in the LMO: as
m
increases, solution quality degrades and running times grow substantially. In addition, the general-purpose algorithms (CP/LADMM/IR) take more running time than the problem-specific algorithms (Alt-SDP/PDGR).

6
Conclusion

In this work, we study convex optimization over the cone of PSD tensors, a natural generalization of SDP with broad examples in quantum information theory. We leverage strategies inspired by modern SDP algorithms and incorporate new nonconvex structures arising in PSD tensors. Our work bridges the gaps between general-purpose algorithms in computational optimization and problem-specific algorithms in computational physics on a standard benchmark problem.

We develop the LADMM algorithm to tackle the lifted nonconvex formulation of SDTO. In addition, we design a CP algorithm that iteratively refines solutions of the LADMM algorithm, together with a tailored sBB method serving as the LMO. We then integrate these two approaches into an IR framework that alternates between primal and dual updates to generate upper and lower bounds on the optimal value.

Our numerical experiments on benchmark white-noise threshold instances show that the IR method consistently yields the best upper bounds and scales to larger problem sizes. However, SDTO problems remain substantially more challenging than standard SDPs, as the dimension grows exponentially with the number of subsystems (modes). The tailored PDGR method, for example, does not scale to these larger instances. This demonstrates the need for further research on more efficient algorithms and implementations for large-scale SDTO problems, particularly general-purpose approaches that can extend beyond the white-noise threshold setting.

Since many convex optimization problems in quantum information involving density matrices can be reformulated in terms of separable states, a broad class of such problems can be expressed as SDTOs. Thus, our framework is not restricted to the white-noise threshold problem. In particular, it can be extended to incorporate operator convex/concave functions acting on PSD tensors, such as quantum (relative) entropy
38
.
