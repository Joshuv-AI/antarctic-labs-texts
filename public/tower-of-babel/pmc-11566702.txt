Introduction

Granulosa cell tumors (GCTs) account for 3–5 % of all malignant ovarian tumors, however, are the most common type among tumors originating from the sex cords and ovarian stroma, constituting 70 % of it. Based on histological and clinical differences, two types of GCTs can be distinguished: juvenile and adult. The juvenile type is less common, comprising about 5 % of cases, and typically occurs in girls and young women. The majority of GCT cases are of the adult type, primarily affecting women in their perimenopausal age (45–60 years), although cases have been described at any age [1]. The juvenile type generally has a favorable prognosis. The adult type is characterized by a slow disease progression but has a tendency for distant recurrences over time, sometimes even many years after the initial diagnosis.

Since GCTs are hormonally active, their symptoms mainly result from elevated estrogen levels. However, the clinical presentation can vary and include symptoms related to a pelvic or abdominal mass. The basis of GCTs therapy is surgery, but the extent of treatment should take into account the patient's age, reproductive plans, and hormonal status. Given the diversity of patients, treatment should be individualized, focusing on the patient's needs. Based on the cases presented from our practice, in this single-center retrospective study we wanted to demonstrate how heterogeneous GCTs are as a group and what the course of the disease may look like.

This work has been reported in line with the PROCESS criteria [2].

Case 1

A 29-year-old woman was admitted to the department for endocrinological diagnosis due to secondary amenorrhea and infertility. She had one previous pregnancy that ended with a natural birth at age 26. Hormonal tests revealed hyperprolactinemia, but gynecological ultrasound showed no abnormalities. The patient was treated with bromocriptine, and menstruation resumed, but anovulatory cycles persisted. Several attempts were made to stimulate ovulation with clomiphene citrate, but pregnancy was not achieved. A hysterosalpingography suggested intrauterine adhesions, which were removed during hysteroscopy. The patient did not opt for assisted reproduction methods.

At age 34, a routine gynecological visit revealed a suspicious lesion in the left ovary. Ultrasound showed a cystic mass measuring 16.5 × 10.4 × 16.8 cm with heterogeneous echogenicity. Laparotomy was performed, during which the left adnexa with the tumor and part of the greater omentum were removed. In the intraoperative histopathological examination, a lesion originating from the ovarian stroma cells was diagnosed, with suspicion of granulosa cell tumor. The tumor was staged as FIGO IA. Due to the patient's reproductive plans, a fertility-sparing operation was performed. The intraoperative diagnosis was confirmed in the final examination. Patient was qualified for adjuvant chemotherapy (BEP regimen: bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin) – three courses were administered. A PET scan showed no metastases, and follow-up visits revealed no recurrence.

Six months after completing chemotherapy, the patient conceived spontaneously, and at 39 weeks, delivered via cesarean section. A second pregnancy followed two years later, also resulting in a healthy delivery. Subsequent follow-ups has shown no signs of recurrence.

Case 2

A 58-year-old woman presented to her family doctor with abdominal distension and gastric symptoms: bloating, constipation, feeling of “fullness”, and general symptoms: chronic fatigue, overall malaise. A gynecological examination revealed a large cystic-solid tumor, most likely originating from the adnexa, completely filling the lesser pelvis and partially the abdominal cavity (Fig. 1). According to the patient's history, vaginal hysterectomy due to uterine fibroids was performed several years ago, with the last gynecological check-up more than five years earlier. Preoperative testing, including the ROMA test, indicated a 56.1 % risk of malignancy (HE 4: 54.5 pmol/L; Ca125: 301.0 U/mL). Laparotomy revealed a tumor measuring 28 × 21 × 18 cm arising from the left ovary, which was removed along with the left adnexa, right adnexa, and greater omentum. The lesion was removed in its entirety (Fig. 2, Fig. 3). The tumor was staged as FIGO IA. Intraoperative histopathological examination revealed a lesion with uncertain malignant potential. Granulosa cell tumor was diagnosed in the final examination. The patient was referred to an oncological center for further treatment. Postoperative imaging showed an indeterminate lesion near the left external iliac vessels, measuring 22 × 17 × 25 mm, not enhancing after contrast administration, but not excluding a tumor focus. Adjuvant chemotherapy was initiated: carboplatin plus paclitaxel. After four courses, the patient was in poor general condition and experienced ocular and neurological complications. Reassessment was decided - no pathological changes were described in the subsequent tomography. Further chemotherapy was discontinued. Case 3

A 47-year-old woman presented to the emergency department with symptoms of acute abdomen. Ultrasound revealed a mass near the uterus and a significant amount of fluid in the Douglas pouch (Fig. 4). Due to suspicion of bleeding into the abdominal cavity, urgent laparotomy was decided. Laparotomy uncovered a ruptured, bleeding tumor of the left ovary, about 8 cm in size, with approximately 1500 mL of blood in the abdominal cavity. The left adnexa were removed. The right adnexa were macroscopically unchanged. No other pathological changes were observed. The tumor was staged as FIGO IC2. Due to the urgent nature of the operation and the life-threatening condition, tumor markers were not measured preoperatively, only basic laboratory tests were conducted. There was also no possibility of performing an intraoperative histopathological examination.Fig. 4Ultrasound image of the tumor in the patient from case no. 3.Fig. 4

Postoperative histopathological examination revealed a granulosa cell tumor with high mitotic activity and a high proliferative index. A second laparotomy was performed for a radical hysterectomy with removal of the right adnexa and omentum. Subsequently, the patient was referred to an oncological center for further treatment.

Discussion

Regardless of the type, GCTs exhibit varied clinical presentations. These hormonally active tumors are often associated with elevated estrogen levels, leading to symptoms like precocious puberty in prepubescent girls [3] and abnormal uterine bleeding in menstruating or postmenopausal women. About 45 % of patients present with abnormal bleeding [4]. Estrogen-induced endometrial hyperplasia occurs in 25–50 % of cases, with hormone-dependent endometrial cancer developing in 5–13 % of cases [5]. Rarely, high estrogen levels can lead to thromboembolic events [6]. In rare cases (<3 %), GCTs may secrete androgens, causing virilization symptoms like hirsutism, acne, clitoromegaly and menstrual irregularities ranging from irregular, rare menstruation to secondary amenorrhea [7].

GCTs also cause a syndrome of symptoms related to the presence of a tumor in the abdominal cavity (10–20 %): abdominal pain, bloating, constipation, and ascites [4]. For others, the first manifestation may be an acute abdomen due to tumor torsion or rupture, while about 20 % of patients remain asymptomatic and are diagnosed incidentally.

Most GCTs (70–90 %) are diagnosed at FIGO stage I, limited to one ovary, and have a favorable prognosis. The most important prognostic factor is the stage of advancement, along with the patient's age, cytoreduction level, tumor size, and histological evaluation. Stage I GCTs have a near 100 % five-year survival rate, with long-term survival ranging from 75 to 90 % [8]. However, recurrences often occurring many years after diagnosis. The longest recorded time to recurrence is 37 years [9]. Advanced-stage (III-IV) cases have a poor prognosis, similar to ovarian cancer, with five-year survival rates below 20 %.

Inhibin B and anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH) are useful markers in monitoring GCTs, as they can aid in the early detection of recurrence or residual disease [10]. Elevated estrogen levels may also help diagnose GCTs.

Surgery remains the cornerstone of GCT treatment. Ovarian cancer must be considered in the differential diagnosis, making intraoperative histopathological examination crucial. The extent of surgery depends on the disease stage and follows a similar protocol to ovarian cancer. Since granulosa cell tumors are typically diagnosed at an early stage, the most common procedure is total abdominal hysterectomy with bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, often without the need to remove disseminated lesions. The degree of cytoreduction and the presence of residual disease are independent unfavorable prognostic factors. In advanced cases, maximal cytoreduction is essential [11]. In such cases, beyond the aforementioned hysterectomy with adnexectomy, surgery should follow the approach used for ovarian cancer: removal of all visible lesions in the peritoneum, the omentum, pelvic and para-aortic lymph nodes (at least on the side of the tumor), and any lesions on adjacent organs (such as the colon or bladder). However, this type of surgery is rarely performed in GCT due to its indolent nature. Complete tumor removal is important to prevent spread, as rupture can lead to dissemination. For young patients with early-stage disease, a fertility-sparing surgery should be performed, limited to unilateral adnexectomy, allowing for the preservation of both fertility and hormonal function [12].

Due to the rarity of GCTs and their prolonged course, prospective studies on chemotherapy are limited. Chemotherapy is mainly used in advanced-stage disease or in cases of recurrence, with response rates of 60–80 %. The BEP regimen (bleomycin, etoposide, cisplatin) is commonly used [13], though bleomycin's toxicity is a concern, particularly for older patients. Carboplatin and paclitaxel are alternative chemotherapy options, offering similar efficacy with lower toxicity [14]. The use of adjuvant chemotherapy in Stage I patients is debated, with no evidence showing better outcomes than surgery alone. It may help patients with risk factors (tumor size >10 cm, rupture, high mitotic index), but more research is needed. Similarly, while the tumor is radiation-sensitive, there's no clear proof that adjuvant radiotherapy benefits early-stage patients [15].

Biological therapies, including bevacizumab, and hormonal treatments like aromatase inhibitors are also being explored, but more studies are needed to determine their efficacy.

Conclusion

Granulosa cell tumors do not constitute a homogeneous group. Based on the presented cases, they can manifest in different ways and have variable disease courses. It is important to consider GCT in the differential diagnosis of acute abdomen and pelvic masses. Surgery remains the mainstay of therapy; however, therapy should always be individualized. Particular attention should be given to young patients, for whom fertility-sparing surgery can be performed. Given the tendency for late recurrences, long-term follow-up is crucial for all GCT patients.
