Awareness, knowledge, attitudes, and practices toward health research ethics and research ethics committees among healthcare postgraduate students, faculty, and professionals in The Gambia
Authors: Thomas Senghore, Effua Usuf, Jainaba Sey-Sawo, Henry Silverman, Veronica P. S. Njie-Carr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Until recently, there has been no formal research ethics training program in The Gambia, despite decades of conducting research. This study aimed to evaluate the awareness, knowledge, attitudes, and practices of research ethics and Research ethics committees (RECs) among health professionals across various health cadres before the commencement of a postgraduate certificate program. A cross-sectional survey was conducted using an online questionnaire. Chi-square tests and multivariable binary logistic regression models were used to examine the associations between independent variables and knowledge, attitudes, and practices of research ethics and RECs. Among 147 participants, 42.1% were unaware of the availability of ethics training programs in The Gambia. Over two-thirds were aware of the research ethics principles (69.7%), the presence of RECs in their institutions (61.2%), and their functions (61.8%). About half (47.9%) demonstrated adequate knowledge of research ethics, while 45.8% and 44.4% had a positive attitude toward research ethics and RECs, respectively. Alarmingly, 85.5% exhibited low levels of research ethics practice. Having an undergraduate degree or higher showed statistically significant odds of a positive attitude toward research ethics compared to holding a college diploma or lower (aOR 2.98; 95% CI: 1.09–8.18, p = 0.034). The low level of practice and average knowledge and attitude of the participants toward research ethics and RECs is likely a reflection of the lack of formal research ethics training programs. Greater awareness and local training opportunities are needed.

Background

Health research activity in Africa has proliferated over the past decades, due to increased international collaborations and funding in response to addressing the health challenges in the continent (Collins et al., 2013; Samb et al., 2009; Wilson et al., 2020). Much of this research is conducted in settings marked by structural inequalities, limited institutional resources, and power imbalances between local researchers and externally funded partners (Chatfield et al., 2021). External sponsorship, often from institutions in high-income countries, can influence research priorities, study design, and decision-making processes in Africa (Kmietowicz, 2002). Thus, increasing ethical challenges related to participant protection, informed consent, benefit sharing, and local ownership of research data and outputs. These challenges highlight the need for strong and effective ethical oversight mechanisms within African research settings. Nonetheless, concerns have been raised about health research regulatory oversight and research ethics capacity in Africa (Chaudhry et al., 2022; Nabyonga-Orem et al., 2021; Sexton et al., 2016). Additionally, studies have demonstrated the knowledge gap among investigators on the ethics of conducting health research (Chongwe et al., 2020; Gangbo et al., 2022; Ogunrin et al., 2016). In line with international standards, Research Ethics Committees (RECs), also called Institutional Review Boards (IRBs) in some countries, review health research. Therefore, the responsibility of protecting research participants from harm and exploitation rests on the RECs and, ultimately, the investigators. This necessitates that REC members and investigators be well-informed about the ethical principles and guidelines involved in conducting health research.

The history and development of research ethics occurred during periods of unethical research, from which the current understanding of research ethics is derived. For instance, the unethical Nazi experiments conducted during the Second World War led to the Nuremberg trials that subsequently influenced formulations of the Nuremberg Code in 1946, the Declaration of Helsinki in 1964 (Carlson et al., 2004), and the International Ethics Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects (CIOMS) (CIOMS, 2016). These guidelines set out conditions for the ethical conduct of research on human subjects internationally and thus influence global research ethics. Despite the existence of these international guidelines, reports continue to document ethical challenges and research integrity concerns in Africa (Amoakoh-Coleman et al., 2023; Bain et al., 2022; Dzamesi et al., 2025). In 1998 and 2005, the World Health Organization (WHO) Regional Committee for Africa and the United Nations Education, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO), respectively, passed resolutions to commit member states to develop and incorporate research ethics regulations into national legislations and policies. Nonetheless, in many African countries, concerns remain as to whether appropriate structures and policies are in place to protect the safety and rights of research participants and the scientific integrity and rigor of research studies (Chaudhry et al., 2022; Dzamesi et al., 2025; Orievulu et al., 2024).

The Gambia has over 70 years of health research history (Umberto DA, 2018). Like many African countries, health research has been growing dramatically, with efforts to follow international ethical standards. In practice, all health-related research involving human participants is expected to undergo ethical review and approval through established national and institutional mechanisms guided by standard operating procedures and internationally recognized ethical principles. Health research ethics oversight is mainly provided by two scientific and three ethics committees; The Medical Research Council Unit, The Gambia (MRCG) at London School of Hygiene and Tropical Medicine (LSHTM), Scientific Coordinating Committee (SCC) reviews submitted research protocols, which once approved are forwarded to The Gambia Government/MRCG Joint Ethics Committee (GG/MRCG JEC) (MRCG@LSHTM, 2020). The SCC and GG/MRCG JEC have standard procedures for providing ethical reviews. The Research and Publication Committee (RePubliC) operates under the School of Medicine and Allied Health Sciences (SMAHS) at the University of The Gambia (UTG), and reviews health-related proposals from students and faculty at the UTG and other health academic institutions prior to review by GG/MRCG JEC or the newly established research ethics committees at the UTG and Edward Francis Small Teaching Hospital. The structures of the RePubliC and the UTG Ethics Committee are evolving, and there are ongoing efforts to standardize procedures. Recently, other new institutional review boards have been established. In addition to externally funded research, there is a growing body of internally driven research, led by local academic and research institutions. However, disparities in funding, limited infrastructure, and research capacity contribute to low research output and access to ethics review resources across institutions. The National Research and Innovative Fund (NRIF) is a newly established statutory public institution mandated to finance, coordinate, and strengthen research and innovation in support of national development priorities (NRIF, 2026). Although research ethics committees are increasing in The Gambia, there is currently no fully established national research ethics regulatory board (Batchilly et al., 2023; MoHERST, 2024) to provide centralized governance, coordination, and quality oversight across institutions. However, national efforts are on the way through the National Research Policy 2024–2029 (MoHERST, 2024) to establish a National Research Ethics Committee, which will oversee institutional RECs across the country, standardize ethical guidelines, and strengthen compliance with best practices in research ethics.

Furthermore, most hospitals do not have RECs. Although most staff in the MoH do not lead research projects because of limited capacity related to knowledge, they are often involved in recruiting, collecting data, and obtaining participant consent. These activities require a basic understanding of ethical principles in health research ethics and research integrity. Up till October 2023, there were no established in-country research ethics training programs at any level. The undergraduate and graduate programs at the UTG and similar institutions teach research methods with limited components of research ethics related to the responsible conduct of research, consenting research participants, and submitting study protocols to the RECs. Also, where topics in research ethics are covered, the contents are limited based on anecdotal reports as reported by attendees during an Ethics summer workshop in 2022, which was funded by the President Global Impact Fund (PGIF), University of Maryland, Baltimore (UMB, 2020). Under a Fogarty International Center, National Institutes of Health (NIH) grant, the PGIF program was expanded to the introduction of a postgraduate certificate program on research ethics and methodology offered at the UTG since October 2023. While evidence exists on investigators’ awareness, knowledge, attitudes, and practices of research ethics and RECs in developing countries (Al Demour et al., 2019; El-Dessouky et al., 2011; Munoli et al., 2017; Ogunrin et al., 2016; Tarboush et al., 2020), many countries, including The Gambia, have no such data.

To fill this gap, we conducted a quantitative descriptive study to evaluate the awareness, knowledge, attitudes, and practices of postgraduate students, faculty members, and health professionals on Research Ethics and RECs in The Gambia before the commencement of a postgraduate research ethics and methodology certificate program.

Methods

Study design, participants, and sample size

In this cross-sectional study, we recruited postgraduate students, faculty, and health professionals from health care, academic, and research institutions across The Gambia. A sample size of 387 was considered adequate for the study using the formula for calculating sample size for cross-sectional studies (Pourhoseingholi et al., 2013). The desired sample size n=Z2P1−Qd2, where “Z” represents the critical value for the level of confidence, set at 1.96 for a 95% confidence level; “P” represents the anticipated prevalence of the outcome (adequate knowledge of research ethics, in the study population), conservatively estimated to be 40%, consistent with similar studies in comparable settings (Adeleye and Ogundiran, 2013); and “d” represents the level of desired precision, which was set at 5%. Five percent of the sample size was added to account for the attrition rate.

Recruitment and data collection procedures

We received scientific and ethics approval for the study protocol from the GG/MRCG JEC. Participants gave informed consent online or on hard copies. Initially, participants were recruited using a multi-stage cluster sampling method. However, due to a low response rate, we transitioned to convenience and snowball sampling to boost participation. Participants were reached using a multifaceted approach. Institutional human resource officers and administrators disseminated the study invitation to eligible staff and students on behalf of the research team through institutional communication channels, including email and WhatsApp. Individuals who voluntarily expressed interest in participating shared their phone numbers or email addresses with the research team, and invitations to complete the questionnaire were then sent. Additional participants were contacted through professional WhatsApp groups in which members of the research team participated. We also encouraged potential participants to share the invitation with contacts who met the inclusion criteria. The web link and QR code of the Qualtrics survey were included in the recruitment correspondence. Due to internet challenges for the participants, we also had a research assistant visit health institutions to recruit participants and distribute hard copies of the questionnaire to those who had not already completed it online.

Participants who agreed to participate online by clicking the web link or scanning the QR code were taken to the online questionnaire to complete the items, which were automatically saved in Qualtrics©, an Online Survey Platform. Participants using hard copies signed the information sheet, and data were uploaded to Qualtrics following completion.

To ensure quality control during the recruitment and data collection processes, one investigator was designated to periodically monitor and review participant traffic, collected data, and the response rate. The team sent out emails and text reminders to potential participants using flyers and text messages. Messages were sparse initially but increased in frequency to monthly for the last three months before we stopped data collection.

Instrument

A questionnaire adapted from Than et al. (2020) was originally developed and used in Myanmar among postgraduate students. It was modified for the present study to fit the local context and objectives. The instrument comprised five sub-scales with categorical and Likert-type items. Face validity was established through expert review to ensure the clarity, relevance, and appropriateness of the items. Internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s Alpha. The reliability coefficient alpha values were 0.53, 0.27, 0.77, 0.57, and 0.79 for Awareness, Knowledge, Attitude toward Research Ethics, Attitude toward RECs, and Practice of Research Ethics, respectively. Lower alpha coefficients were attributed to a limited number of items in the sub-scales. The sub-scales on the questionnaire cover knowledge and awareness of Research Ethics and RECs; attitudes toward Research Ethics and RECs; and practice of Research Ethics.

Awareness is defined as recognition and familiarity derived from either personal perception or from information acquired through external sources (Than et al., 2020), specifically relating to key aspects of research ethics infrastructure and guidance within their context. The awareness sub-scale contains four items in which participants were required to respond with “yes,” “no,” or “uncertain” to questions relating to their awareness of any research ethics training program in the country, their familiarity with the ethical principles, the presence of a REC/IRB in their institution, and awareness of REC functions. Knowledge is conceptually defined as the extent to which individuals understand key concepts, principles, and guidelines related to research ethics. The knowledge sub-scale contains four items assessing participants’ knowledge of the importance of ethics, the research ethics principles, and guidelines, as well as informed consent. The questions contained multiple-choice items requiring one answer. For each item, the correct response was accorded a maximum of two points, and the wrong response was accorded 0 points. A knowledge score is computed by summing up all the points obtained on each item, and the median score was used as a cutoff (DeCoster et al., 2011) to determine the level of knowledge. Those scoring below the median were determined to have inadequate knowledge levels, while those scoring above were determined to have adequate knowledge. Attitude is defined as an individual’s judgment about the degree of like or dislike for research ethics and RECs (Than et al., 2020). The sub-scale on attitude toward Research Ethics comprises of 17 Likert-scale questions assessing attitudes on several issues bordering around ethics training, informed consent, data protection, integrity, and the need for ethical rules for governing research. The attitudes toward RECs sub-scale comprises of ten items, ranging from the need for RECs in academic and research institutions, reviewing of research by RECs, to training of RECs on bioethics. Practice is conceptually defined as the extent to which individuals apply research ethics principles and guidelines in their actual research activities. The practices sub-scale is measured based on seven items focusing on the procedure for obtaining informed consent from research participants, submission of protocol for REC review, involvement in REC work, and thesis supervision. The attitude and practice levels were determined following a similar procedure used to determine the knowledge level.

Statistical analysis

Data were downloaded from Qualtrics and imported into IBM SPSS Statistics (version 20), cleaned, and prepared for analysis. Baseline data were summarized as means with standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The knowledge, attitudes, and practices responses were combined into a composite score, and the median scores were used as the cutoff to determine levels of knowledge, attitudes, and practices of research ethics and RECs. The awareness items captured conceptually distinct domains of awareness rather than a single unified construct and were therefore only analyzed descriptively at the item level to provide a more accurate representation of the responses. Chi-square tests and multivariable binary logistic regression models were used to examine the associations between independent variables and knowledge, attitudes, and practices of research ethics and RECs. All tests were two-sided, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of participants

One hundred and sixty-four participants completed the questionnaire (44% response rate). Table 1 shows the sociodemographic characteristics of the participants and key study variables. Most of the participants were between the ages of 20 and 35 years (n = 113, 68.9%), men (n = 95, 57.9%), and had undergraduate degrees (n = 80, 48.8%). About 40% (n = 66) were nurses, and almost half (n = 80, 48.8%) had 1–5 years of clinical experience. Over half reported that they never attended a research ethics course (n = 87, 53.0%) or workshop (n = 121, 73.8%).

Awareness, knowledge, attitudes, and practices of research ethics and RECs

Table 2 shows information on awareness of research ethics and RECs. Most (n = 64, 42.1%) of the participants reported that they were unaware about the availability of a Research Ethics training for all academics in The Gambia, and 69.7% (n = 106) reported being familiar with the ethical principles governing the conduct of human subject research. About two-thirds reported that their institutions had a REC (n = 93, 61.2%), and they were fully aware of the function of RECs (n = 94, 61.8%).

Tables S1–S4 (Supplementary information) present participants’ responses regarding knowledge, attitudes, and practices of research ethics and RECs. The levels of Knowledge, Attitudes, and Practices of Research Ethics and RECs are shown in Figure 1. After computing the scores, the levels of knowledge, attitude, and practice of research ethics and RECs, about half (47.9%, n = 64) of the participants were categorized as having adequate knowledge of research ethics. Under half of the participants had a positive attitude toward research ethics (n = 60, 45.8%) and RECs (n = 56, 44.4%), while 85.5% (n = 100) were categorized as having a low level of research ethics practice.

Association between demographic characteristics, knowledge, attitudes, and practices of research ethics and RECs

In the chi-square analysis of demographic and key study variables, none of the demographic variables or attendance of a research/medical ethics course or workshop was associated with knowledge of research ethics. However, factors such as education (p = 0.021), current position (p = 0.015), attendance of a course on Research/Medical Ethics (p = 0.011), and attendance of a workshop on research/medical ethics (p = 0.006) were associated with positive attitudes toward research ethics. Age (p = 0.043), education (p = 0.047), attendance of a course (p = 0.021), and workshop (p = 0.003) on research/medical ethics were associated with attitudes toward RECs. Attendance of a course (p = 0.013), and workshop (p = 0.011) on research/medical ethics was associated with research ethics practice. Table 3 shows the bivariate analysis of factors associated with knowledge, attitude, and practice of Research Ethics and RECs.

Table 4 shows the results of the multivariable binary logistic regression analysis. After adjusting for confounders (associated variables with p < 0.05 from the Chi-square analysis in Table 3), only those with an undergraduate degree or higher (aOR 2.98; 95% CI: 1.09–8.18, p = 0.034) had higher statistically significant odds of having a positive attitude toward research ethics compared to those with a college diploma or lower. None of the factors in the multivariable analysis were associated with attitudes toward RECs and practice of research ethics (p > 0.05).

Discussion

This study was undertaken to evaluate the awareness, knowledge, attitudes, and practices of postgraduate students, faculty members, and health professionals on Research Ethics and RECs before the commencement of a postgraduate certificate program in health research ethics and methodology in The Gambia. Our findings indicate moderate to fair awareness of research Ethics and RECs. However, over half of the participants had inadequate knowledge, negative attitudes, and low practices of research ethics and RECs. These findings demonstrate the dire need for formal research ethics training to build capacity and prepare scholars to conduct research responsibly with scientific rigor and integrity.

Training in research ethics is an essential component of a robust research infrastructure to ensure that RECs and researchers are equipped to review and conduct research under established principles and guidelines (CIOMS, 2016; WHO, 2011). Participants in our study reported unawareness or uncertainty about a research ethics training program in the country. This was consistent with the reality at the time of data collection, as no institution offered such formal training programs. It was also evident from our results that more than half of the participants reported not having attended any training or course on research ethics or medical ethics. These findings highlight a significant gap in research ethics capacity-building efforts in the country. The absence of structured training programs in research ethics may lead to a limited understanding and application of ethical standards necessary for protecting the rights and welfare of research participants. Therefore, our findings underscore the need for the development and implementation of a research ethics training Program, which our team has implemented since 2023 through an NIH, Fogarty-funded Program (RePORTER, 2024).

We noted that over two-thirds of the participants reported being aware of the ethical principles governing research, the presence of a REC in their institution, and the function of the RECs. Across Africa, studies report variable, often limited awareness of research ethics principles and the functions of RECs among health professionals. Regional reviews indicate that insufficient formal training in research ethics, limited resources, and weak institutional support are key contributors to these gaps in awareness and ethical practice (Chaudhry et al., 2022; Silaigwana and Wassenaar, 2015). Beyond Africa, similar variability has been observed. For example, studies from Myanmar (Than et al., 2020) and Pakistan (Qadri et al., 2024) reported moderate awareness of research ethics principles and REC functions, while higher levels of familiarity have been documented among researchers in India, reflecting differences in institutional research capacity and training exposure (Shivakumar, et al., 2024). Notwithstanding, familiarity with research ethics principles and RECs’ function is a positive indication of the level of awareness among the participants. Awareness of the ethical principles and functions of RECs is crucial as it ensures that researchers are cognizant of the ethical standards that guide the responsible conduct of research.

Our findings reveal significant variability in participants’ knowledge of research ethics. While over eighty percent can identify the core ethical principles and understand the concept of informed consent, more than half do not understand the importance of research ethics and cannot adequately identify the research ethics guidelines. Comparable findings have been reported across other African settings (Ateudjieu et al., 2022; Verster and Amugune, 2016). Studies in Benin and Cameroon showed that researchers and health professionals demonstrate good awareness of fundamental ethical principles, such as informed consent, but have limited knowledge of national and international research ethics guidelines, ethics review processes, and regulatory frameworks (Ateudjieu et al., 2022; Gangbo et al., 2022).

When we evaluated the overall knowledge level, we found that about half of the participants had inadequate knowledge of research ethics. Similar knowledge gaps have been reported among health professionals in Africa, where deficiencies in knowledge of ethical principles and national regulatory requirements, together with unmet research ethics training needs, have been documented (Adeleye and Ogundiran, 2013; Gangbo et al., 2022; Senghore and Omotosho, 2025). Outside the region, a study from China similarly reported that less than half of participants were knowledgeable about research ethics guidelines (Liu et al., 2023), suggesting that inadequate ethics knowledge is not unique to Africa. A study among physicians in Lebanon (Azakir et al., 2020) also found similar results, but a lower proportion was found among faculty in Saudi Arabia (Qusada, 2023). The variability of our findings with those from the above studies may be explained by the differences in participants’ characteristics, research experience, and educational level. Our participants included faculty, postgraduate students, and healthcare professionals, most of whom only had undergraduate degrees with little to no experience in research, while those from the Chinese and Myanmar studies included only postgraduate students. These findings suggest a lack of fundamental understanding of critical elements and a gap in knowledge of key concepts relevant to ethical research practice.

Understanding researchers’ attitudes toward research ethics is important in fostering a culture of responsible research practice. In this study, we found that about half of the participants demonstrated a negative attitude toward research ethics, which is both intriguing and concerning. Such negative attitudes may contribute to poor adherence to ethical standards necessary for safeguarding participants’ rights and ensuring the credibility of research output. Several factors may explain these findings. Education and training significantly influence an individual’s understanding of the ethical norms in research (Andersson et al., 2022). However, as shown in the results, over half of the participants did not receive any training on research ethics or medical ethics, which may partly account for the unfavorable attitudes observed. In addition, the prominence of young and inexperienced researchers in our sample may have influenced these attitudes, and limited mentoring and inadequate exposure to ethical guidance can make navigating ethical challenges more difficult (Zhao et al., 2022).

Attitudes toward RECs were similarly negative among many participants, possibly reflecting the practical challenges researchers encounter in complying with ethical review processes. Although this study did not investigate the specific reason underlying these perceptions, previous studies from Africa have documented prolonged ethics review timelines, delays in feedback to researchers, and administrative and resource constraints affecting RECs (Mrisho and Essack, 2021; Ssali et al., 2020). These systemic challenges, together with publication pressure and constrained research funding, may contribute to negative perceptions of RECs and increase the risk of ethical lapses if adequate institutional support is lacking (Adeleye and Adebamowo, 2012; Zhao et al., 2022). For early career researchers in particular, these challenges may create a perception of RECs as barriers rather than supportive mechanisms for ethical research conduct. Such perceptions can undermine researchers’ engagement with RECs and weaken trust in the ethical oversight process, highlighting the need for more supportive, efficient, and educational REC systems (Zhao et al., 2022).

The practice of research ethics was low. This may be because of participants’ limited knowledge of research ethics and experience conducting research. As shown in our data, over two-thirds did not receive training beyond the undergraduate level, and over half did not receive research ethics training. For this group of novice researchers, understanding research ethics can be challenging. They may be familiar with basic ethical concepts, such as requiring research participants to fill out informed consent forms, but not fully grasp more complex issues. For example, obtaining valid informed consent, obtaining ethical clearance, or familiarity with guidelines for conducting human subject research, including the responsible conduct of research and handling data responsibly. Novice researchers often lack the guidance and mentorship needed to recognize the importance of adherence to ethical research practice. Research methods training at the undergraduate level often briefly touches on ethical concerns without providing depth for beginners to practice effectively.

We assessed the relationship between demographic factors and knowledge, attitudes, and practices of research ethics and RECs, and found that only participants’ educational level was associated with attitudes toward research ethics. Those with an undergraduate degree or higher were over four times more likely to have positive attitudes toward research ethics. This is not surprising because training at this level often includes aspects of research ethics that can help with a better understanding of ethical principles and standards, thus influencing their attitudes toward research ethics (Resnik, 2020). Than et al. (2020) also found a similar significant association between educational level and attitudes toward research ethics among postgraduate students (Than et al., 2020). This finding may highlight the importance of training in research ethics early on in one’s career.

Our findings may also be viewed within the broader context of structural inequalities and power imbalances in health research in Africa. Limited local funding and reliance on external sponsors can constrain how institutions and researchers address ethical issues. Additionally, knowledge gaps in research ethics may further increase risks related to informed consent, community engagement, and fair benefit sharing. Strengthening ethics awareness among health professionals and students, together with improving REC capacity, is therefore essential. Enhanced ethics training and institutional support can help ensure that ethical standards are locally relevant and effectively applied.

Our study has some limitations; the cross-sectional nature limits the inference of causality. We used convenience and snowball sampling methods to select participants, which are liable to selection bias. The data collection began during the COVID-19 pandemic, which impacted our recruitment and estimated sample size. Consequently, the achieved sample size was smaller than the calculated sample size, leading to small numbers in some professional categories. This may have reduced the statistical power of the study and limited the generalizability of the findings, particularly when interpreting subgroups. However, we extended the recruitment time to include all regions and made allowance for hard copies of self-administered questionnaires. Therefore, findings should be considered exploratory and descriptive, with prevalence estimates interpreted cautiously. A considerable number of our participants had limited research experience, which may impact the reliability of their responses. From a psychometric perspective, some of the scales used in this study demonstrated low reliability coefficients, indicating potential limitations in internal consistency. Further research should focus on refining and validating measurement tools in similar contexts, using larger and more diverse samples and mixed-methods designs to improve reliability and interpretability. Despite these limitations, the study identified the need for research ethics training and capacity development in The Gambia.

Conclusion

This study highlights critical issues about awareness, knowledge, attitudes, and practices of research ethics and RECs in The Gambia. Addressing these issues requires a concerted effort from academic and research institutions, RECs, and researchers. Increasing awareness through training programs, fostering positive attitudes toward research ethics and RECs. Enhancing the practical applications of the ethical principles and guidelines in research may be an essential step to protecting research participants from harm and exploitation. Beyond research ethics training, other efforts may be required to improve attitudes and practices of research ethics at both the national and institutional levels. Such efforts should include the establishment of research ethics governance structures, regulations, guidelines, and policies, as well as investment in research ethics education. Further studies may be required to explore the specific barriers to a positive attitude and good research ethics practices.
