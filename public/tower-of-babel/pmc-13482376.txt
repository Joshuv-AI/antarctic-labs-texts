A multimodal radiomics, deep learning, and pathomics signature for predicting the prognosis in central conventional chondrosarcoma
Authors: Qiushi Su, Jie Wu, Ben Li, Yanmei Wang, Yuchao Xu, Yunqing Chen, Ning Wang, Feng Hou, Guangjie Yang, Pei Nie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Prognostic tools remain limited for central conventional chondrosarcoma.•A multimodal signature integrated CT radiomics, deep learning, and pathomics.•The signature predicted progression-free survival in independent cohorts.•The signature may support postoperative risk stratification.

Introduction

Chondrosarcoma (CS) is generally considered the second most common primary malignant bone tumor after osteosarcoma, with an estimated annual incidence of approximately 2–4 cases per million people [1,2]. It is characterized by the formation of hyaline cartilage tumor tissue [3]. Central conventional CS arises within the medullary cavity and represents the predominant subtype of CS [4]. Central conventional CS was divided into low-grade and high-grade according to histopathology. According to the 2020 World Health Organization (WHO) classification, low-grade cartilaginous lesions arising in the long and short tubular bones of the appendicular skeleton are termed atypical cartilaginous tumors (ACTs) and are classified as intermediate tumors [5]. Intralesional curettage is commonly used for ACTs, by contrast, wide surgical resection remains the preferred treatment for central conventional CS other than ACTs [6]. Although surgical treatment achieves favorable outcomes in many patients with central conventional CS, postoperative disease progression remains clinically relevant concerns. Previous studies have reported local recurrence rates of approximately 19–24% and metastatic rates of approximately 14–15% [7]. These findings highlight the need for reliable risk stratification tools to support individualized postoperative assessment.

Prognostic assessment of CS traditionally relies on clinical and pathological factors, including age, tumor size, location, surgical margin status, and histological grade [8]. These factors are valuable but may not fully reflect intratumoral heterogeneity. Computed tomography‌ (CT) and histopathology are essential for diagnosis and disease assessment [9]. Several conventional imaging features, including endosteal scalloping, cortical destruction and calcification shape, have been linked to tumor aggressiveness and histological grade [10]. However, conventional visual assessment remains partly subjective and provides limited quantitative information on tumor heterogeneity.

Omics technologies provide a high-throughput and quantitative way to characterize tumor heterogeneity from imaging and pathological data. They have been increasingly applied to prognostic prediction in various malignancies [[11], [12], [13]]. However, single-modality models may provide only a partial view of tumor biology. Multimodal integration can combine complementary information from different data sources and may improve prognostic assessment [14,15]. To our knowledge, prognostic signatures integrating multimodal features have rarely been explored in CS.

Therefore, this study aims to develop and validate a multimodal feature-integrated signature (MS) that integrates radiomics, deep learning (DL), and pathomics features from preoperative CT and postoperative whole slide imaging (WSI) to predict prognosis in central conventional CS patients and to explore its potential value for postoperative risk stratification.

Materials and methods

Patients

This multicenter retrospective study was reviewed and approved by the institutional ethics committees. Given the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

We searched the pathology databases of two medical institutions from January 2013 to January 2023 to recruitment patients. The inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) postoperative pathology confirmed central conventional CS. All diagnoses centrally reviewed according to the 2020 WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumors (5th edition); 2) availability of whole slide imaging (WSI) of hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained tissue sections; 3) availability of a preoperative non-enhanced CT scan performed. The exclusion criteria included: 1) CT or WSI images deemed unevaluable due to severe artifacts or poor technical quality; 2) missing follow-up data; 3) history of synchronous or metachronous other primary malignant tumors; 4) ACTs. Following the TRIPOD statement guidelines [16], patients from the Affiliated Hospital of Qingdao University (n = 117) were used as the training cohort, while patients from Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University (n = 49) served as an independent external validation cohort. Follow-up

Follow-up was generally performed every 6 months during the first 2 years after surgery and annually thereafter, according to routine clinical practice. Follow-up data were collected through electronic medical records or telephone interviews. The endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from surgery to the first occurrence of any of the following events: histologically or radiologically confirmed local recurrence, distant metastasis, death from any cause, or the last follow-up for censored cases. The final follow-up date was August 31, 2024.

CT scan parameters and image segmentation

CT examination images were obtained from two medical institutions, and detailed scan parameters are provided in Appendix Table S1.

Two radiologists (Reader 1 and Reader 2, with 7 and 13 years of experience in musculoskeletal radiology, respectively) independently layer by layer segmented the three-dimensional (3D) regions of interest (ROI) using ITK-SNAP software (Version 3.8.0; https://www.itksnap.org). The following principles were followed for segmentation: 1) the outermost layer of cortical bone destruction or cartilage matrix calcification along the edge of the tumor was delineated, including all visible tumor parenchymal components; 2) avoid the inclusion of obvious peripheral edema zone, reactive bone sclerosis zone, and adjacent normal vascular or nerve structures. Reader 1 completed the initial segmentation of all ROIs, and Reader 2 re-segmented 30 randomly selected images across all cohorts. After the extraction of radiomics features, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated, and features with an ICC > 0.80 were considered to demonstrate good reliability and reproducibility [17]. The radiologists were blinded to clinical outcomes during whole segmentation process.

Pathological section preparation and image selection

H&E-stained sections were prepared from formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples for all patients. After a comprehensive review of all available slides, a musculoskeletal pathologist with 9 years of experience selected one representative FFPE block per patient containing the most viable and histologically representative tumor area. One H&E-stained section from the selected block was subsequently digitized for WSI. The selection criteria included: 1) high tumor cellularity; 2) representative histological features, including chondroid matrix and tumor-cell morphology; and 3) absence of extensive necrosis, hemorrhage, or processing artifacts. The selected slides were scanned using a Pannoramic SCAN whole-slide scanner (3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) with a 40× objective. Only the selected single WSI from each patient was used for subsequent pathomics analysis. Following WSI acquisition, the ROI for pathomics feature extraction was manually selected using ImageScope software (version 12.3.3.5048; Aperio Technologies®). To ensure biological representativeness, ROI selection adhering to following criteria: 1) encompassing the area with the highest tumor cell density and most characteristic chondroid morphology; 2) avoiding regions with significant necrosis, hemorrhage, calcification, or tissue folding artifacts. Each ROI was cropped to a standardized field of view of 1000 × 1000 pixels (with one pixel corresponding to 0.504 μm) to ensure dimensional consistency for downstream analysis. The pathologist was blinded to clinical outcomes during whole review.

Image preprocessing and omics feature extraction

To ensure reproducibility and robustness of radiomics features, standardized preprocessing was applied to all CT images. First, image resampling was conducted to achieve isotropic voxels with a uniform size of 1 × 1 × 1 mm³ using PyRadiomics (version 3.7), thereby normalizing spatial resolution across all patients for feature extraction. Subsequently, gray-level discretization was then performed using a fixed bin width (25 Hounsfield Unit) to reduce the number of intensity levels and standardize the calculation of texture features. A total of 1244 radiomics features were extracted using the PyRadiomics. The detailed description of the radiomics features is provided in Appendix S1.

For DL feature extraction, each CT scan was converted into 3 windowed image channels: a bone window to emphasize osseous structures, a soft-tissue window to assess adjacent soft-tissue involvement, and a standard window to provide general anatomical context. These windowed images were normalized to a grayscale range of 0–255. The specific rationale and functions of these 3 window settings are provided in Appendix S2. For each 3D tumor ROI, the largest cross-sectional slice was selected, as it is often considered to contain the most representative and aggressive tumor components. The selected slice was cropped into a tumor-centered square region and resized to 224 × 224 pixels to meet the input requirements of the chosen architecture. The processed images from the 3 window settings were then fed into a ResNet152 model pre-trained on the ImageNet dataset, with the final classification layer removed. DL features were extracted from the penultimate layer, yielding a total of 6144 features (3 windows × 2048 features per window) per tumor.

Pathomics features were extracted using the software CellProfiler (version 4.0.7; https://cellprofiler.org). The extraction process involved the following steps: First, the “Unmix Colors” module was used to separate H&E-stained images into hematoxylin and eosin components. The “IdentifyPrimaryObjects” and “IdentifySecondaryObjects” modules were then applied to automatically identify nuclei and cytoplasm. Subsequently, the “ObjectSizeShape”, “Texture”, “ObjectIntensity”, and “ObjectIntensityDistribution” modules were used to extract additional pathological features. Finally, the mean and standard deviation of these features were calculated, resulting in a total of 451 pathomics features per patient. Detailed information about pathomics features is provided in Appendix S3.

Development and validation of prognostic prediction signature

Feature selection was performed exclusively in the training cohort and involved 4 steps. First, features with good reproducibility were retained. Second, Pearson correlation analysis was used to reduce feature redundancy (correlation coefficient < 0.9). Third, univariable Cox proportional hazards regression was used for initial feature screening, and features with P < 0.05 were retained as candidate features. Finally, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression with 10-fold cross-validation was applied to select the optimal prognostic features. The validation cohort was not used for feature screening, signature development, coefficient estimation, or parameter tuning.

The selected features were used to development the radiomics signature (RS), deep learning signature (DS), pathomics signature (PS), and MS using multivariable Cox regression analysis. The selected features and their corresponding coefficients were combined to calculate the risk score for each patient. X-tile software [18] (version 3.6.1; Rimm Lab, Yale School of Medicine) was used to select a cutoff value for the continuous risk score of each prognostic signature in the training cohort. Patients with risk scores greater than or equal to the cutoff were classified as high-risk, whereas those with scores below the cutoff were classified as low-risk. The training-derived cutoff for each signature was subsequently applied unchanged to the validation cohort without further optimization. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis was conducted to assess the association between the risk score and PFS. The concordance index (C-index) and hazard ratio (HR) were used to evaluate the predictive performance of the 4 signatures for PFS. Calibration curves were generated to compare predicted and observed survival probabilities.

Signature interpretability

To improve the interpretability and transparency of the MS, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was performed. SHAP estimates the contribution of each feature to the model output by calculating its average marginal contribution across different feature combinations [19], thereby providing a consistent measure of feature importance. This method has been increasingly used to interpret artificial intelligence-based medical prediction models [20]. SHAP analysis was performed at both global and individual levels. For global interpretation, the mean absolute SHAP value of each feature was calculated to rank its overall contribution to the MS. For individual interpretation, waterfall plots were generated to show how specific features shifted the model output toward higher or lower predicted risk.

Statistic

SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM) was used to perform the Mann-Whitney U test, independent samples t-test, and chi-square (χ²) test. R software (version 3.3.3; https://www.r-project.org) was used for ICC analysis, Pearson correlation analysis, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression, LASSO Cox regression, multivariable Cox regression, calibration plots, C-index and Kaplan-Meier survival analysis, A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Development and validation of prognostic prediction signature

After dimension reduction selection, 6 radiomics features, 17 DL features and 4 pathomics features were used to development RS, DS and PS, respectively. A total of 15 features (including 4 radiomics features, 7 DL features and 4 pathomics features) were selected to construct MS. The development details and risk score calculation formula and specific thresholds are shown in Appendix S4 and Fig. S1.

As a supplementary feature-selection robustness analysis, Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to the P values obtained from univariable Cox regression for candidate features entering MS feature screening in the training cohort. The FDR-adjusted q values of the 15 final features retained in the MS are reported in Appendix Table S2 to assess the potential risk of false-positive feature selection. All final features included in the MS showed FDR-adjusted q values < 0.10.

Discussion

Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of treatment for patients with central conventional CS. Although favorable outcomes can be achieved in many patients, postoperative progression remains clinically relevant concerns. However, central conventional CS is characterized by substantial intratumoral heterogeneity, and traditional clinicopathological factors may not fully capture this complexity. This limits their ability to provide individualized postoperative risk assessment. In this study, we developed and evaluated the MS for PFS prediction in patients with central conventional CS. The MS showed potential prognostic value and may support postoperative risk stratification. Nevertheless, the modest overall sample size and limited number of progression events may have affected the stability of feature selection and model coefficients, which may increase the risk of overfitting. The relatively wide CI of the C-index in the validation cohort further indicates uncertainty in the estimated signature performance. Therefore, the performance estimates should be interpreted with caution, and the MS should be regarded as an exploratory prognostic stratification tool. Further validation in larger prospective multicenter cohorts is required before clinical implementation.

Radiomics converts medical images into high-throughput quantitative features and provides additional information on tumor heterogeneity that may not be fully captured by visual assessment [21,22]. Many previous radiomics studies in CS focused on differential diagnosis. For example, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based radiomics has been used to differentiate skull base chordoma from CS [23]. More recent studies have extended radiomics to prognostic prediction in CS. Li et al. developed a CT-based intratumoral and peritumoral radiomics model for predicting PFS in patients with CS, and the combined radiomics signature achieved a C-index value of 0.800 in the test cohort [24]. Another CT-based radiomics nomogram showed value for predicting both histological grade and recurrence-related outcomes in CS [25]. These studies suggest that radiomics features may provide useful prognostic information in CS. In the present study, 6 radiomics features were selected to construct the RS, most of which were texture features. These features may capture may capture CT-based phenotypic heterogeneity related to the spatial distribution of attenuation values [26]. In CS, such imaging heterogeneity may partly reflect variations in cartilage matrix, mineralization, and lobular tumor architecture [27]. However, these biological correlates were not directly validated in the present study. Accordingly, radiomics features should be regarded as quantitative components of the prognostic signature rather than independently validated biological biomarkers. Further spatially matched radiologic-pathologic and molecular analyses are needed to clarify their biological correlates. The RS showed potential prognostic value, with a C-index of 0.735 in the validation cohort. Although its discrimination was lower than that of the MS, the RS provided complementary quantitative imaging information alongside the DL and pathomics features in the multimodal framework.

Based on manually delineated tumor regions, DL feature extraction can provide high-dimensional image representations that may complement handcrafted radiomics features [28]. In CS, DL-related studies remain limited and have mainly focused on lesion classification or histological grading. For example, Gassert et al. developed a CT-based DL model to classify enchondroma, ACT, and high-grade CS [29]. A recent study developed a DL-radiomics model for CS and reported an AUC of 0.879 for histological grade prediction and a C-index of 0.805 for recurrence-free survival prediction in the test cohort [30]. In the present study, 17 DL features were selected to construct the DS. The DS showed potential prognostic value, with a C-index of 0.767 in the validation cohort. The selected DL features represent task-adaptive, high-dimensional latent image representations and may encode hierarchical spatial patterns ranging from local textures to higher-order morphological configurations [31]. However, they cannot be directly mapped to specific histopathological or molecular processes, and their biological correlates remain uncertain.

Pathomics enables quantitative characterization of histological morphology from routine H&E-stained WSI, including tissue architecture and cellular-level features such as nuclear shape, size, texture, and spatial arrangement [32,33]. Previous studies have shown the prognostic potential of pathomics in several malignancies [34], and recent sarcoma-related studies have also explored digital pathology for survival prediction [35,36]. However, pathomics-based prognostic research in CS remains scarce. To our knowledge, this is the first study to incorporate pathomics features into a prognostic signature for CS. In our study, 4 pathomics features were selected to construct the PS, which showed potential prognostic value with a C-index of 0.711 in the validation cohort. Most of the selected features were nuclear shape features, suggesting that quantitative nuclear morphology may provide prognostic information [33]. These features may be related to nuclear atypia, which is considered during the histopathological assessment of CS [8]. However, the specific pathological and biological correlates of the selected features still need to be further verified in future studies.

Compared with previous studies, the present study integrated radiomics, DL, and pathomics features to construct the MS and showed higher C-index values than the single-modality signatures, suggesting the potential complementary value of multimodal feature integration. This may be because CT-derived radiomics and DL features characterize macroscopic imaging heterogeneity, whereas pathomics features provide quantitative information on histological morphology. SHAP analysis further provided global and individual-level explanations of feature contributions, which may helped improve the transparency of the MS. However, SHAP characterizes the contribution of individual features to model predictions rather than establishing causal biological mechanisms or clinical decision rules.

By integrating preoperative CT with postoperative WSIs, the MS may provide supplementary prognostic information for postoperative risk stratification after surgical resection. Its application is therefore confined to the postoperative setting and is not intended for purely preoperative risk prediction or surgical decision-making. Moreover, the present study did not include a prospective decision-impact analysis or assess whether MS-guided management improves clinical outcomes. The MS should be regarded as an exploratory prognostic stratification tool rather than a stand-alone clinical decision-making tool, and its risk stratification results should not currently be used to modify treatment strategies or follow-up intervals. Prospective decision-impact studies are needed to determine whether the MS improves clinical decision-making and patient outcomes.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, although this study employed a multicenter design with an independent external validation cohort, the modest overall sample size and limited number of progression events may have affected the robustness and generalizability of the proposed signature. The retrospective cohort assembly and data collection may also have introduced selection bias and unmeasured confounding, potentially affecting the observed prognostic associations and the performance estimates of the signature. Therefore, further validation in larger prospective multi-institutional cohorts with consecutive patient enrollment is required before clinical implementation. Second, CT images were acquired using different scanners and acquisition and reconstruction protocols across institutions. Although voxel resampling and gray-level discretization were applied to standardize voxel geometry and radiomics feature calculation, residual scanner- and protocol-related variability may still have affected radiomics feature stability and cross-institutional generalizability. Future multicenter studies should adopt more standardized imaging protocols and evaluate feature harmonization approaches, such as ComBat. Third, the signature was developed using non-enhanced CT and postoperative WSIs, whereas MRI and contrast-enhanced CT were not included. Although non-enhanced CT and histopathological images provide complementary information on osseous morphology and microscopic tissue architecture, MRI may provide additional characterization of bone marrow involvement and soft-tissue extension, whereas contrast-enhanced CT may capture tumor enhancement characteristics. The absence of these modalities may have limited comprehensive tumor phenotyping. Future studies should incorporate MRI and contrast-enhanced CT and assess whether their inclusion provides incremental prognostic value. Fourth, the MS was not compared with an established clinicopathological prognostic model. Consequently, the incremental prognostic value of the MS beyond conventional clinicopathological assessment remains uncertain and should be evaluated against prespecified clinicopathological benchmarks in larger prospective cohorts. In addition, pathomics features were derived from a single WSI per patient, which may not fully capture spatial histological heterogeneity across the entire tumor and may introduce slide selection bias. Future studies should incorporate multiple slides or spatially distinct tumor regions to improve the representation of intratumoral histological heterogeneity.

Conclusion

In conclusion, The MS integrating radiomics, DL, and pathomics features showed promising prognostic value for prognostic prediction in central conventional CS. It may provide useful support for postoperative risk stratification. Further prospective validation study is needed before its use in individualized postoperative management.
