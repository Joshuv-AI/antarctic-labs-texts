A note on potential gains in precision of radiation risk estimates from joint analysis
Authors: Richard Sposto, Munechika Misumi, John Cologne
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In estimating radiation-related risk of cancer and other diseases based on the RERF Life Span Study (LSS), joint analyses can be performed where multiple health outcome endpoints are combined in the same model, allowing some parameters to be estimated in common among all endpoints with possible increase in precision of radiation risk and other model parameter estimates. Using as a basis excess relative risk (ERR) and excess absolute risk (EAR) models of the type commonly used in analysis of LSS data at RERF, we use maximum likelihood theory to compute the asymptotic relative standard error of endpoint-specific radiation effect and other parameter estimates using joint analyses as compared to traditional independent analysis. We show that some gains in precision of endpoint-specific radiation risk parameter estimates can be achieved by sharing effect modifier and other model parameters, but only small or negligible gains in precision are achieved for endpoint-specific background modifying or effect modifying parameters when other model parameters are shared. The magnitude of the precision gain for radiation risk estimates depends on the number of endpoints, the baseline incidence rate of the endpoint, and the type of model being used.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-76920-x.

Introduction

The Life Span Study (LSS) of the Radiation Effects Research Foundation (RERF) is used to estimate the added risk of cancer incidence or cancer and non-cancer mortality due to whole-body exposure to ionizing radiation1–4. Analysis of LSS data can provide precise estimates of radiation risk for omnibus disease endpoints, such as all solid cancer incidence or mortality, and for some specific diseases. However, the precision of radiation risk estimates for rarer endpoints can be poor. For example, in Fig. 1 of LSS mortality report 14 4, the bounds of the 95% confidence interval for excess relative risk at 1 Gy (ERR1Gy) for all-cause mortality or all solid cancer mortality are within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:$$\end{document} 20% of the estimated ERR1Gy. For stomach cancer, the most prevalent solid cancer cause of death comprising 29% of all solid cancer deaths, the bounds are within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:$$\end{document} 50% of the estimate. However, for rarer cancers which had a significant dose response, for example esophageal cancer, which comprises 3.1% of solid cancer deaths, the precision is much poorer–confidence bounds are more than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:\:$$\end{document}100% of the ERR estimate. Similarly, in Table 11 of the Preston et al.5 analyses of solid cancer incidence, for those exposed at age 30, the 95% confidence bounds for ERR1Gy were within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:$$\end{document} 35% and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:$$\end{document} 38% for all solid cancer and stomach cancer, respectively, but more than \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\pm\:$$\end{document} 90% for esophageal cancer.

One strategy that has been proposed for analysis of multiple disease endpoints is to perform joint analysis6 whereby in a single analysis individual endpoints are pooled but are nevertheless distinguished from each other as separate endpoints. The advantages cited for this approach are (1) the ability to share parameters among different endpoints; (2) availability of significance tests for differences among endpoints; (3) use of endpoint-appropriate radiation organ dose estimates; and (4) the ability to formulate unified models. (1) and (4) are related and imply that joint analysis will involve estimation of fewer parameters compared to completely independent endpoint-specific analyses, with a possible gain in precision for estimation of some parameters, most importantly dose response and effect modifier parameters, especially for more rarely occurring endpoints.

A number of authors have used joint analysis of multiple endpoints in various contexts, many from RERF. Brenner et al.1 used joint analysis of four subclasses of solid tumor to analyze formally whether dose response curvature observed in all solid cancers combined was similar within disease subclass. Cologne et al.7 used joint analysis to investigate the effect of background misspecification on the curvature of male dose response. Hsu et al.8 used joint analysis to examine heterogeneity in temporal patterns and dose response shape in incidence analysis of four leukemia subtypes. Sugiyama et al.9 used joint analysis to test differences in ERRs and effect modifier parameters for incidence of proximal vs. distal colon cancer. Brenner et al.10 tested differences in radiation effects for CNS tumors of different histological origins. Sakata et al.11 explored heterogeneity of radiation effect in oral cavity/pharyngeal cancer, esophageal cancer and stomach cancer. Outside of RERF, Webb et al.12 looked at differences in the effect of parental mental health on subtypes of fatal birth defects, and Richardson et al.13 reported an INWORKS analysis of multiple cancer mortality endpoints using both standard maximum likelihood and hierarchical Bayesian analysis. In addition to these applications of joint analysis, Sposto and Cullings14 discussed the equivalence of joint analysis to traditional analysis in analysis of aggregate endpoints such as all combined solid cancer, and also discussed implications of the use of organ specific doses in these analyses. None of these reports has addressed directly the possibility of improving precision by using joint analysis.

This note describes a theoretical evaluation of the possible impact on precision of radiation risk estimates and other model parameters resulting from joint analysis in the context of the LSS.

In the current evaluation we will compute the ratio of asymptotic standard errors of the parameters–Eq. (1)–estimated in a joint analysis in eight different scenarios. These scenarios are shown in Table 3. Scenario 1 is one where only the background intercept parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{B}_{1}$$\end{document} differ among endpoints, but all other parameters are identical. In contrast, scenario 8 is one in which no parameters are shared among endpoints. This analysis is equivalent to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:J$$\end{document} completely independent endpoint-specific analyses. The ratio of asymptotic standard errors, for each Scenario 1 through 7 relative to Scenario 8, reflects the possible gain in precision when various parameters are shared, as compared to completely independent analyses. For each scenario, the appropriate design matrix for unshared parameters is expanded similarly to that shown in Table 2C, with corresponding expansion of the parameters vectors. The vector of asymptotic standard errors \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:SE$$\end{document} is then computed using the person-year (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{P}_{s})$$\end{document} and covariate values \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left({X}^{s}\right)$$\end{document} from the original LSS data, along with the assumed-known population parameter values in Table 1.

In this computation, all parameter values in Table 1 are assumed identical for all endpoints, with the exception of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{B}_{1}$$\end{document}, where the quantity \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\text{ln}}\left( {\frac{{6\left( {J - j + 1} \right)^{2} }}{{J\left( {J + 1} \right)(2J + 1)}}} \right),\:\:j = 1, \cdots \:,J$$\end{document}, is successively added to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{B}_{1}$$\end{document} parameter in Table 1 to create a new parameter vector \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{B}_{1}$$\end{document} with dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:(1\times\:J)$$\end{document} whose corresponding hypothetical incidence rates will add up to the original total incidence rate. This formula is based on the sum of squared integers \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:j=1,\cdots\:,J$$\end{document}, so that the fraction in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\text{l}\text{n}(\cdot\:)$$\end{document}, when evaluated and summed, adds up to 1. Adding this quantity to the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{B}_{1}$$\end{document} parameter creates an artificial but plausible example of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:J$$\end{document} different endpoints with a range of baseline hypothetical incidence rates from high to low, where the individual rates add up to the incidence rate that obtains when all causes are aggregated. This quadratic equation was selected because it approximates the pattern of incidence rates for individual cancer types as shown, for example, in Appendix Table C1 of Grant et al.2, where there are comparatively fewer cancer types with higher incidence rate compared to those with lower incidence.

By assuming that all parameter values in Table 1 are identical for all endpoints with the exception of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{B}_{1}$$\end{document}, we create a situation where endpoints differ only in the overall incidence rate. Here a joint analysis under Scenario 1 would be a correct and most parsimonious model for data arising from this formulation. Other scenarios represent models that would be correct (would fit) but that are overparametrized, presumably incurring a penalty in terms of precision.

The RERF cohorts have a highly skewed dose distribution, with most person-years of follow-up among those at zero or low dose. The above computations were repeated but using an artificially constructed person-year table where the total number of person-years was the same but where person-years were redistributed so that higher doses were made to be approximately as equally represented in the cohort as lower doses. The purpose here was to investigate the robustness of our conclusions to a situation where the dose distribution was not as highly skewed as in the RERF cohort. This change had a small quantitative but not qualitative effect on the results (data not shown).

Discussion

Using asymptotic theory, we have shown that use of joint analysis can in some circumstances result in gains in precision of radiation risk estimates and other parameters. For radiation risk-related parameters, the maximum gain is achieved when all effect modifying parameters are assumed identical for all endpoints. Potential gains in precision were somewhat larger for ERR models than for EAR models. For the intercept parameters, which affect the overall predicted incidence rates, there can be substantial gains in precision if background modifying parameters are shared. Precision gains for effect modifying or background modifying parameters were comparatively smaller.

Although we have used radiation dose to the colon in this paper, in joint analysis when dose response parameters are estimated separately for each endpoint, organ doses specific to the endpoint can be used, as discussed in 6 and 14. Whether use of endpoint-specific organ doses is sensible if common dose response parameters are estimated requires careful consideration.

The results presented herein are in the context of a model and data set that is used specifically in the RERF Life Span Study analyses or other similar analyses from RERF. The RERF cohorts have a highly skewed dose distribution and a unique distribution of age at exposure and attained age. Although not perfectly generalizable to other cohorts whose exposure distribution or sample makeup differ, we think that our general findings would nevertheless obtain.

In our investigation we only looked at a few patterns of sharing–e.g. sharing all EM parameters, sharing all BM parameters, sharing both EM and BM, etc. We did not, however, investigate degrees of sharing within each of these blocks of parameters. For example, there are 10 different parameters in the BM parameter block, and one could investigate the effect of sharing all of them or none of them (as we did), but there are also 1,022 other subsets of these parameters that could be shared or not shared which we did not investigate. It is not clear to what extent the gradient of precision gains is roughly linear between all shared and none shared as a function of the number of these parameters that are shared.

The results are also computed under the assumption that the true underlying model is known and is in fact identical (except for the intercept) among different endpoints. In application, the decisions about whether there is commonality between models for different endpoints are data driven. Thus a more parsimonious model than is justified might be fitted to the data, imposing some bias in parameter estimations. As with many statistical procedures, there is a tradeoff between precision and potential bias. Although this manuscript focuses on the precision gain only, joint analysis does allow formal tests for differences in background and dose-effect modification among endpoints in the context of endpoint-appropriate radiation doses and thus may yield more unified models. The advantage and/or disadvantage of the joint model may have to be evaluated in more comprehensive settings. Achieving the correct balance cannot be guided by statistical considerations alone but requires substantive knowledge.

In our review of literature on previous use of joint analysis, this method was used primarily to investigate heterogeneity of a parameter or parameters among different disease types or classes, and not explicitly to improve precision. In the paper by Richardson and colleagues13, the authors used traditional maximum likelihood of multiple cancer mortality endpoint in addition to a hierarchical Bayesian approach. The former is interesting in the context of the current paper, since for each endpoint they fit a stratified background linear excess relative risk model without effect modification. We show that maximum gain in precision is achieved by sharing effect modifier (EM) parameters, with much smaller benefit of sharing background modifier parameters. Since implicitly Richardson et al. have shared all EM parameters (there is none), there is no further improvement in precision that can be obtained unless background parameters are shared. We did not investigate the extent to which precision is improved if, instead of a completely stratified background for each endpoint (none of very many parameters is shared) whether use of a modelled background as in our case, whether or not some parameters are shared, could improve precision of the dose response parameter estimate. Their hierarchical Bayesian analysis was a different way to improve precision in the endpoint-specific analysis, using normal priors on the linear dose response parameter in the context of the stratified analysis, yielding much improved precision for rarer endpoints (smaller credible intervals), albeit with shrinkage of the dose response estimates toward the overall mean.
