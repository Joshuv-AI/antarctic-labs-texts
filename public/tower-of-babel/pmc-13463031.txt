A Global 3D Chlorophyll Concentration Dataset (1997–2024): 4 km Spatial Resolution, Monthly Scale, 0–220 m
Authors: Yanfeng Wen, Peng Chen, Delu Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOceanic chlorophyll-a (Chl-a) 3D distribution is critical for quantifying marine primary productivity, ecosystem dynamics, and the oceanic carbon cycle. However, existing global 3D Chl-a datasets have coarse spatial resolution (≥0.25°), limiting their ability to resolve mesoscale and sub-mesoscale processes. We present MEGO-C-M (Multi-model Ensemble for Global-Ocean Chlorophyll-a - Monthly Gridded Dataset), a high-resolution global 3D Chl-a dataset on a 0.0416° (~4.6 km) grid with monthly coverage (October 1997–November 2024). Derived via the MEGO-C machine-learning framework (trained on 144,936 quality-controlled BGC-Argo profiles and co-located satellite/physical reanalysis predictors), the model is applied to monthly predictor fields to generate gridded Chl-a across 27 vertical levels. Validation against independent BGC-Argo profiles shows strong agreement (R² = 0.818, RMSE = 0.254 log₁₀(mg·m−3), bias = −0.001 log₁₀(mg·m−3)). The dataset captures vertical Chl-a structures and large-scale seasonal variability, per-grid-cell ensemble spread. It is distributed in NetCDF format and assigned a digital object identifier (DOI) for persistent access: 10.57760/sciencedb.30693.

Background & Summary

Chlorophyll-a (Chl-a), the primary light-absorbing pigment in phytoplankton, is a central variable in ocean biogeochemistry and climate studies1–3. Its three-dimensional (3D) distribution underpins our understanding of marine primary productivity, ecosystem structure, and the functioning of the ocean’s biological carbon pump4,5. However, obtaining accurate, high-resolution global estimates of subsurface Chl-a remains a longstanding observational challenge. Satellite ocean color missions provide globally consistent surface Chl-a data, but they are limited to the upper optical layer (~20 m) and often suffer from cloud contamination6,7. On the other hand, in-situ observations from the Biogeochemical-Argo (BGC-Argo) network offer invaluable vertical profile information, yet their sparse spatial coverage restricts their ability to characterize basin-wide or mesoscale variability8–10.

To overcome this observational asymmetry, hybrid reconstruction approaches have been developed to fuse satellite and in-situ data using statistical or machine learning models11–13. However, existing global 3D Chl-a products typically offer coarse spatial resolutions (≥0.25°, ~28 km), which are insufficient to resolve submesoscale features critical for vertical nutrient flux and biological dynamics14–16.

To address this gap, we present MEGO-C-M17, a new global 3D chlorophyll-a dataset constructed using the MEGO-C ensemble learning framework18–21. The dataset spans the period from October 1997 to November 2024, providing monthly, 3D fields of Chl-a at 1/24° (~4.6 km) horizontal resolution and 27 vertical levels from the surface to 200 m. MEGO-C-M17 integrates BGC-Argo profiles with satellite remote sensing and physical-biogeochemical reanalysis inputs, combining high vertical accuracy with fine-scale horizontal detail22–24. The ensemble architecture provides not only high-resolution reconstructions but also robust per-grid-cell ensemble spread.

This paper describes the complete MEGO-C-M17 data processing pipeline, including source data preparation, model training, spatiotemporal collocation, vertical grid design, and ensemble reconstruction. In accordance with FAIR data principles, we also provide extensive validation using independent BGC-Argo profiles and a global ensemble-spread (dispersion) assessment. The dataset is distributed in NetCDF format and complies with the Climate and Forecast (CF) metadata conventions to improve interoperability and reuse, and it is available under DOI: [10.57760/sciencedb.30693]. MEGO-C-M17 aims to support a wide range of applications in ocean carbon cycle research, ecosystem modeling, climate diagnostics, and operational oceanography.

Methods

Data Sources and Pre-processing

The MEGO-C-M17 dataset was developed by integrating quality-controlled in-situ vertical profiles with a suite of gridded satellite and physical reanalysis datasets (Table 1). All third-party datasets are formally cited in the References and summarized in Table 2, and were used in compliance with the corresponding terms and licenses. These inputs span a range of spatial and temporal resolutions and were used at different stages of the reconstruction workflow. Specifically, daily collocations were used to train the machine-learning model, while monthly-averaged fields were employed to generate the final gridded product. Finally, we used the CMEMS MultiOBS/SOCA 3D product25 as an established baseline to benchmark the validation results of MEGO-C-M against independent BGC-Argo matchups. In-situ BGC-Argo Data

The core observational basis for MEGO-C-M17 consists of 144,936 quality-controlled vertical profiles from the global Biogeochemical-Argo (BGC-Argo) array10, collected between October 2005 and December 2024 (Fig. 1). Each profile contains observations of temperature (TEMP), salinity (PSAL), and chlorophyll-a fluorescence (CHLA) within the upper 200 m, with pressure (PRES) used to derive geometric depth10,26.

A multi-step quality control (QC) protocol was applied to all profiles prior to model training. First, we used the Argo QC flags provided with the profiles and defined a “good-quality” observation as one with QC = 1 (good), 2 (probably good), or 5 (value changed during QC/delayed-mode adjustment). Only profiles that contained at least one depth level within 0–200 m where TEMP, PSAL, CHLA, and PRES were all simultaneously available and satisfied this QC criterion were retained. Second, a 3-point moving average filter was applied to remove spikes. Third, raw fluorescence measurements were converted to lab-calibrated chlorophyll-a concentrations using an established empirical relationship derived from high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) co-locations14,27–29:

In addition, all variables from the BGC-Argo10 profiles and external predictor datasets were subjected to a physical plausibility screening (range test) based on documented valid ranges and common community QC practice. Thresholds were defined according to the physical meaning and operational definition of each variable, with the primary aim of removing gross outliers, missing-value encodings, and occasional retrieval artefacts prior to collocation and model training (rather than truncating the natural variability), and are summarized in Table 2. To unify the vertical axis across all profiles, sea pressure was converted to geometric depth using the TEOS-10 routine gsw_z_from_p30, which accounts for latitude-dependent gravity variation. The resulting Argo profile dataset10 maintains consistent sampling frequency throughout the annual cycle (Fig. 2a), exhibits a nearly uniform vertical sampling density between 10 and 200 m (Fig. 2b), and provides broad spatial coverage across all major ocean basins and marginal seas (Fig. 2c). A hemispheric sampling imbalance remains, with the Northern Hemisphere receiving greater float density and QC-passed profiles due to deployment patterns and environmental constraints (e.g., sea-ice) in the south31,32.

Satellite and Reanalysis Data

To complement the in-situ BGC-Argo profiles10 (Fig. 2), we compiled a suite of satellite and reanalysis datasets representing key ocean surface and subsurface conditions. These include satellite ocean color (Rrs, Kd(490), ZSD, SPM)33,34, sea level anomaly (SLA)35,36, surface wind (uwnd, vwnd, anomalies)37–39, and ocean physical reanalysis fields (temperature, salinity, mixed-layer depth)40,41, as listed in Table 1.

All non-Argo predictor fields were spatiotemporally collocated to individual BGC-Argo profiles10 using the nearest grid cell in space and a resolution-dependent strategy in time: exact day for daily products, nearest six-hour interval for CCMP winds38, and calendar month for monthly anomalies39. After collocation, gross outliers were removed using physically defined plausibility ranges (Table 3). Whenever available, we followed the variable definitions and metadata/quality guidance in the upstream product documentation—for example, Copernicus Marine ocean-colour documentation for CHL/Kd(490)/ZSD/SPM/Rrs (including multi-water-type processing spanning Case-1 and optically complex Case-2 conditions)42,43 and Copernicus Marine sea-level documentation for SLA44—and we adopted Argo community QC conventions for profile variables45. Negative or extreme Rrs values, which can arise from atmospheric-correction limitations especially in optically complex waters, were treated as invalid and removed by the range test46. For satellite ocean color variables, only values with a relative uncertainty ≤ 20% were retained to ensure reliability. The full names and units of all variables listed in Table 3 are compiled in Table 4.

Benchmark dataset

As a benchmark, we used the CMEMS (Copernicus Marine) MultiOBS/SOCA global 3D biogeochemical product25, which provides observation-based gridded fields of chlorophyll-a. This product is widely used as an established reference for large-scale 3D biogeochemical analyses. In our study, we employed (i) the official monthly MultiOBS fields available for 2010 to enable a strict month-to-month comparison, and (ii) the corresponding weekly MultiOBS fields (matched by nearest-week sampling) to extend the benchmark evaluation beyond 2010. The MEGO-C-M Reconstruction Framework.

We designed a structured five-step workflow encompassing data integration, preprocessing and feature engineering, ensemble model training, reconstruction of the MEGO-C-M dataset, and verification of the MEGO-C-M dataset17. First, we compiled two key categories of input data: (i) gridded satellite and reanalysis products providing surface environmental variables—including sea-surface optical properties, sea level anomaly, wind, and physical oceanographic fields—and (ii) quality-controlled BGC-Argo10 vertical profiles of temperature, salinity, and chlorophyll-a concentration. After spatial and temporal collocation, we applied a rigorous quality control procedure and performed feature engineering steps such as log-transformation, standardization, and normalization to prepare the dataset for model input.

Next, we implemented a heterogeneous ensemble learning framework comprising multiple candidate models, including XGBoost, CatBoost, Random Forest, KNN, MLP and ANN. These models were evaluated via cross-validation, and the final ensemble (MEGO-C) was constructed using a model stacking strategy (see the “Ensemble Model Development” section). Once trained, the ensemble model was applied to monthly environmental predictor fields to generate the global three-dimensional chlorophyll-a reconstructions. To further improve physical consistency with observations, a bias correction module was incorporated for the GLORYS12V1 salinity field41.

Following dataset generation, we conducted a comprehensive performance assessment of the reconstructed fields, including both the global domain and twelve representative oceanic regions. This evaluation considered spatial, vertical, and seasonal variations, and was complemented by diagnostic statistics such as profile-level root mean squared error (RMSE), bias, and coefficient of determination (R²).

Following collocation and quality control, the dataset was randomly split at the float level into a training set and an independent validation set, such that the resulting validation profiles account for approximately 15% of all available profiles.To prepare the data for machine learning, we derived a total of 22 input features from the original 18 environmental variables (Table 3). In addition, specific preprocessing strategies were applied to certain variables. Depth values were clipped to our study range (0–200 m), and then linearly scaled to the range [–1, 1] to standardize vertical positioning across profiles. Geographic coordinates (latitude and longitude) and time (day-of-year) were transformed into radians and encoded using trigonometric functions (sine and cosine), allowing the model to capture cyclic patterns while avoiding artificial discontinuities at spatial or temporal boundaries52–54.

We selected input predictors to provide complementary constraints on the optical environment, water-mass state, dynamical forcing, and spatiotemporal context that jointly shape three-dimensional chlorophyll variability. Specifically, optical proxies include multispectral remote-sensing reflectance (Rrs(412), Rrs(443), Rrs(490), Rrs(555), Rrs(670)) together with bulk clarity/attenuation indicators (Kd(490), ZSD) and particulate loading (SPM), providing complementary descriptors of the surface apparent optical properties and optical-water-type variability (from Case-1 to optically complex Case-2 conditions). Environmental (water-mass) descriptors comprise TEMP and PSAL, and the mixed-layer depth proxy mlotst, which reflect hydrographic conditions relevant to vertical structure and biological response. Dynamical forcing and mesoscale context are represented by SLA, wind components (uwnd/u, vwind/v), and surface current anomalies (uanom, vanom), which modulate advection, mixing, and bloom development. Spatiotemporal information is provided by LATITUDE, LONGITUDE, and JULD (day since reference), and vertical position is explicitly encoded by DEPTH to support subsurface reconstruction. To capture periodic variability and avoid discontinuities (e.g., across the dateline and at the start/end of the annual cycle), LONGITUDE and JULD are additionally transformed into sine–cosine pairs.

Ensemble Model Development

We adopted a stacked ensemble learning framework (also known as “stacked generalization”) to maximize predictive accuracy and generalization skill. This framework consists of two modeling levels:

Level-1 (Base Learners): A diverse set of ten regression models was evaluated as base learners, spanning three major algorithmic families:

Tree ensembles: Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Categorical Boost (CatBoost)55–57;

Tree ensembles: Random Forest (RF), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Categorical Boost (CatBoost)55–57;

Neural networks: Feedforward artificial neural network (ANN), and multilayer perceptron (MLP)15,50,58;

Neural networks: Feedforward artificial neural network (ANN), and multilayer perceptron (MLP)15,50,58;

Distance-based models: k-nearest neighbours (KNN)59–61;

Distance-based models: k-nearest neighbours (KNN)59–61;

Each base learner was trained and evaluated via 5-fold cross-validation. Based on their performance (R², RMSE), the six most accurate and complementary base models were selected for meta-learning.

Notably, the base learners used in the ensemble were screened from a pool of ten candidate models. Candidate models were benchmarked under the same training protocol, and a spatially stratified evaluation was further conducted by partitioning the global ocean into 1.5° × 1.5° tiles to compare model skill across heterogeneous regimes. The final base-learner set was selected to balance overall accuracy and spatial complementarity. Global Reconstruction of MEGO-C-M

Once trained, the final MEGO-C ensemble model was applied to a global set of predictor variables to reconstruct monthly chlorophyll-a fields. While the model was trained using daily-scale inputs, the production-phase inputs were constructed from monthly-averaged satellite and reanalysis products to match the desired temporal resolution of the final dataset. To ensure methodological consistency, the same set of 22 predictor features was derived using identical preprocessing and feature engineering procedures.

One key consideration during global reconstruction was the known bias in the GLORYS12V1 salinity field (so)41,62,63. To correct this, we trained an XGBoost-based bias-correction model using daily co-locations between GLORYS12V141 and in-situ BGC-Argo10 PSAL observations (2005–2024), with spatiotemporal coordinates (latitude, longitude, depth, time) as auxiliary inputs14,64,65. In addition, to enable an independent quality check of the bias-corrected salinity fields, we randomly held out 15% of the co-located profiles as an independent validation subset during 2005–2024; these withheld profiles were excluded from model fitting and were used only for post-correction evaluation. The trained correction model was then directly applied to the monthly salinity fields to align them with in-situ observations15.

In addition to this shift from daily to monthly inputs and the salinity correction step, another difference lies in the treatment of satellite optical variables. Specifically, the threshold for acceptable relative uncertainty was relaxed from 20% (used during model training) to 80% during production, in order to maximize spatial coverage while maintaining acceptable data quality. All other preprocessing and feature engineering procedures—such as log-transformation and cyclical encoding—were preserved exactly as in the training phase.

The final set of monthly predictors, including the corrected salinity and all other environmental drivers, was resampled to a uniform 1/24° grid (~4.6 km) and passed to the trained MEGO-C model. This produced the MEGO-C-M dataset17: a monthly, global, three-dimensional chlorophyll-a product with 27 vertical levels (0–220 m), covering the period October 1997 to November 2024. Because the model architecture and data processing were held consistent, the reconstructed fields retain the calibrated accuracy and robustness established during training, without introducing distributional drift.

Validation and Evaluation of MEGO-C-M Data Quality

To systematically assess the quality of the MEGO-C-M dataset17, we carried out a three-pronged evaluation encompassing:

Overall Comparison with In-situ BGC-Argo Observations

We first aggregated the original BGC-Argo profiles10 from 2005–2024 into a gridded, monthly-scale format for direct comparison. Chlorophyll-a (Chl-a) observations were binned onto the native MEGO-C-M output grid7,17,66. Specifically, observation timestamps were rounded to the nearest calendar month, and geographic coordinates were standardized to the target grid. Each profile was assigned to a grid cell based on latitude and longitude indices, while depth matching was performed with a tolerance of Δz = 1 m. Within each grid cell and month, observations were robustly aggregated using the median, and the number of contributing profiles was recorded28,67–69. This section aims to assess the performance of the MEGO-C-M17 monthly product by comparing it with in-situ BGC-Argo10 chlorophyll profiles at collocated space–time matchups and harmonized depth levels. The goal is to quantify the product’s consistency with independent observations (e.g., accuracy, bias, and correlation) and to diagnose whether MEGO-C-M can reliably reproduce the observed vertical chlorophyll structure across different concentration regimes, providing a product-level evaluation relevant to end users.

Corresponding 27-layer vertical profiles from the MEGO-C-M predictions17 were then extracted for each matched grid cell and month. These predictions were mapped to physical units using a consistent transformation. Most evaluation metrics—including the coefficient of determination (R²), root-mean-square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and bias—were computed in log₁₀-transformed space using log₁₀(Chl-a + 1e−6), ensuring numerical stability. In contrast, the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) was calculated in linear space for interpretability in absolute percentage terms. No additional spatial or temporal interpolation was applied, ensuring strict structural consistency between matched observations and model outputs.

To further evaluate model performance across varying oceanographic conditions, we performed stratified analysis by grouping the matched samples along four key oceanographic dimensions: latitude bands (e.g., tropics, subtropics, polar zones), ocean basins (e.g., Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Arctic, Southern), depth intervals (e.g., 0–20 m, 20–40 m, …, 220–240 m), and seasons (DJF, MAM, JJA, SON). Within each group, we recomputed the full set of evaluation metrics to identify possible performance differences due to key climatic and oceanographic dimensions.

Independent validation and benchmark setup

To enable an unbiased evaluation of the MEGO-C-M17 monthly product and a fair benchmark against an existing 3D product, we defined an independent validation protocol prior to any dataset-wide comparisons. After spatiotemporal collocation and quality control, profiles were assigned to training and validation at the float level, such that the resulting validation profiles account for approximately 15% of all available profiles. This float-wise split reduces spatiotemporal leakage associated with repeated sampling by the same platforms and provides a more conservative estimate of out-of-sample performance.

Two matchup-based benchmark experiments were then configured using in-situ BGC-Argo10 as the common reference. (1) 2010 monthly overlap: because the CMEMS MultiOBS/SOCA product25 provides official monthly fields only for 2010, we performed a strict month-to-month comparison by sampling MEGO-C-M17 (2010) and MultiOBS-month25 (2010) at identical BGC-Argo matchups. (2) Independent-period evaluation (2021-07–2022-11): for profiles in the float-wise validation subset within this period, MEGO-C-M17 values from the corresponding month were compared with BGC-Argo10 observations; in parallel, the existing product was evaluated using MultiOBS weekly fields25 by matching each BGC-Argo10 profile date to the nearest weekly field.

For both experiments, matchups were constructed with a consistent spatiotemporal and vertical collocation scheme. Observation timestamps were rounded to the nearest calendar month for monthly-field comparisons (MEGO-C-M17 and MultiOBS-month25), whereas the weekly benchmark used the nearest-week sampling as noted above. Geographic coordinates were standardized to the target grid, and each profile was assigned to a grid cell using latitude/longitude indices (ilat, ilon). Vertical matching was performed relative to target depth levels with a tolerance of Δz = 1 m. When multiple observations occurred within the same grid cell and time bin, values were robustly aggregated using the median, and the number of contributing profiles was recorded. This protocol yields a reproducible, observation-anchored validation and benchmarking framework that is applied in the Results section before presenting dataset-wide diagnostics based on all available profiles.

Spatial and Temporal Structure Evaluation

Beyond pointwise accuracy metrics, we further assessed the spatial and temporal structure fidelity of MEGO-C-M by combining (i) a dataset-level, multi-year diagnostic against the full BGC-Argo archive and (ii) structure-oriented evaluations of large-scale patterns and mesoscale-sensitive features. Specifically, we collocated MEGO-C-M17 with all available BGC-Argo10 profiles and quantified rolling-window skill in log10(Chl-a) space (R², RMSE, and bias) to summarize the long-term numerical behavior of the dataset. This analysis is reported as a dataset-level quality diagnostic rather than an independent validation, because the full BGC-Argo10 record may partially overlap with data used during product development.

To examine the large-scale spatial and vertical patterns of chlorophyll-a distribution, we visualized the global three-dimensional fields in 2023. This temporal sampling allowed us to qualitatively inspect seasonally varying structures in both horizontal and vertical dimensions. As shown in Fig. 5 and Table 8, we further delineated 12 representative oceanic regions, spanning various climatic zones and biogeochemical regimes77,78. A resolution-controlled self-consistency experiment was conducted to assess the mesoscale-to-submesoscale spatial detail preserved by MEGO-C-M17. Technical Validation

This section first provides a complementary dataset-level diagnostic by contrasting MEGO-C-M17 with all available BGC-Argo10 profiles to examine coverage, internal consistency, and potential ensemble-spread accumulation along the production chain. We then conduct a strict independent validation of the MEGO-C-M17 monthly product using the independent BGC-Argo10 hold-out set, and perform an apples-to-apples benchmark against the existing CMEMS MultiOBS/SOCA monthly product25 for 2010, the only overlap year for the official monthly fields. This demonstrates that the correction process successfully mitigates the systematic errors in the original salinity field, ensuring the reliability of physical inputs used for chlorophyll-a reconstruction.

Spatio-temporal Variability and Ensemble-spread

To rigorously quantify the predictive ensemble-spread of the MEGO-C-M dataset17, we leveraged the ensemble dispersion generated by the six base learners within the stacking framework. For each spatiotemporal grid cell, the median absolute deviation (MAD) was computed across the six individual predictions. This metric offers a robust and scale-consistent estimate of intra-ensemble variability, and—crucially—was calculated directly in linear space (mg·m−3), thereby reflecting absolute ensemble-spread magnitudes without transformation distortion. The year 2023 was selected as a representative analysis period.

Figure 21 presents the monthly evolution of MAD-based ensemble-spread across 12 representative ocean regions in depth–time space. Each panel shows vertically resolved ensemble-spread profiles, aggregated across the horizontal extent of each study area. Overall, ensemble-spread levels remain low across most months and depths, with particularly small values (median < 0.005 mg·m−3) observed in oligotrophic subtropical gyres (e.g., panels a and g), where optical and ecological conditions are relatively stable and well captured by the training data101,117.

Depth–time distribution of ensemble-spread (MAD) for 12 representative ocean regions in 2023. Each panel shows monthly median ensemble-spread profiles derived from six base models. Median (med) and 90th percentile (p90) values are shown for reference.

By contrast, elevated ensemble-spread is observed in regions characterized by strong environmental gradients or sparse in-situ sampling. For instance, the Kuroshio Extension, North Atlantic Subpolar Zone, and Southern Ocean (panels b, f, and l) show peak MAD values near the surface and around the deep chlorophyll maximum (DCM) depth. These peaks likely reflect enhanced mesoscale activity, mixing dynamics, and observational scarcity. Marginal seas such as the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal (panels i and j) exhibit seasonally modulated ensemble-spread maxima, consistent with monsoon-driven variability118. Summary statistics (median and 90th percentile MAD) displayed in each panel confirm that even in complex regions, ensemble-spread rarely exceeds 0.04 mg·m−3, attesting to the ensemble’s predictive robustness.

To further explore the global spatial patterns of ensemble-spread, Fig. 22 displays CoV for six representative depth layers (0, 29, 55, 92, 155, and 186 m), averaged over 2023. This normalization accounts for regional differences in Chl-a concentrations, allowing for meaningful cross-domain comparison. The CoV maps reveal coherent ensemble-spread structures: values are lowest (<0.03) in subtropical gyres and mid-latitude open ocean, moderate (0.04–0.06) in productive coastal zones and equatorial upwelling regions, and highest (>0.06) in polar oceans and marginal seas, including the Arabian Sea and Southern Ocean119. At greater depths, spatial coherence weakens, and ensemble-spread decreases, consistent with attenuated vertical gradients and diminished signal strength. Importantly, in the representative year examined here (randomly selected), the spatial patterns do not show obvious signatures of artificial discontinuities or edge-related artifacts, suggesting that the ensemble fields are visually consistent across basins and depth layers for this case. We note that this is a qualitative artifact check based on a single-year visualization; to facilitate broader inspection, we provide the full set of monthly, depth-resolved ensemble-spread (CoV) fields for the entire record as part of the data release120.

Together, these results indicate that the ensemble-derived dispersion in MEGO-C-M17 is generally low across most open-ocean regions and exhibits physically interpretable, geographically coherent spatial patterns. Variability is primarily driven by oceanographic complexity and observation sparsity—rather than by instability or inconsistencies in the model architecture. This reinforces the scientific reliability of MEGO-C-M17 and supports its application in biogeochemical modeling, data assimilation, and ensemble-spread-aware ecological forecasting.

Data Limitations

The comprehensive validation presented in the previous sections highlights the high quality and reliability of the MEGO-C-M dataset17. However, like any data product derived from statistical models, it possesses inherent limitations that users should consider.

Regional caveats (polar oceans). Model performance and ensemble dispersion show regional variability, with notably higher ensemble spread in the Arctic and Southern Oceans. This likely reflects historically sparse BGC-Argo sampling in ice-covered/remote regions and additional challenges for high-latitude ocean-colour retrieval (clouds, low sun angles, sea ice). Users should apply additional caution for quantitative analyses in these polar regions.

Scope of independent validation. Validation primarily relies on a float-level held-out subset of BGC-Argo, and the fully independent hold-out period is restricted to July 2021–November 2022. While MEGO-C-M shows consistently higher skill than the baseline benchmark within this period, fully independent diagnostics across the entire temporal span are not currently available. Additional comparisons against truly independent ship-based datasets (e.g., GLODAP/GO-SHIP) would provide further verification and are left for future work.

Temporal heterogeneity and hindcast. We treat 1997–2004 as a hindcast interval because substantial BGC-Argo coverage becomes available mainly after 2005; estimates for this period should be interpreted cautiously. Long-term diagnostics also indicate weaker and more variable matchup-based skill during 2005–2013, whereas skill becomes higher and more stable after ~2013 as sampling improves. As further shown in Fig. 13, the early BGC-Argo Chl-a profiles were not only sparse but also regionally clustered, suggesting reduced spatial and environmental representativeness during 2005–2013. Thus, the weaker early-period skill likely reflects the combined effects of sparse sampling, regional sampling imbalance, potential uncertainties in early BGC-Argo bio-optical observations and upstream predictors, and reduced generalization to under-represented regimes. Substituting alternative upstream ocean-colour inputs (e.g., OC-CCI) would require re-harmonizing predictors and re-assessing sensitivity, effectively defining a new product configuration.

Reference-data uncertainty and interpretation of “uncertainty”. BGC-Argo chlorophyll-a is derived from in situ fluorescence and is sensitive to correction procedures (e.g., non-photochemical quenching, sensor drift, inter-sensor consistency), which can introduce region-/season-dependent biases that propagate into both training and evaluation. In addition, the provided CoV/ensemble spread should be interpreted as an internal, model-based dispersion metric rather than a calibrated error bound, and it does not necessarily capture shared model biases or uncertainties inherited from upstream predictors (e.g., GlobColour and other environmental fields). Users are encouraged to interpret CoV as relative confidence information and to consider independent data where available.
