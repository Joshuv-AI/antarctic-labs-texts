Conservative Management of Atrioventricular Groove Hematoma in Mitral Valve Repair Surgery: A Case Report
Authors: Xia Zhang, Yangzhao Zhou, Zhaoshun Yuan, Jianming Li, Kang Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAn accidental injury to the myocardial tissue below the posterior annulus occurred during mitral valve repair surgery, leading to the formation of an atrioventricular groove hematoma. By evaluating the size of the communication orifice and monitoring the bleeding tendency, we successfully managed the complication conservatively, thus preventing cardiac rupture and the necessity for reoperation.

Summary Conservative management of mitral valve repair surgery–related atrioventricular groove hematoma is feasible with stable hemodynamics, no active bleeding, and absent hematoma expansion on serial imaging.Closely monitoring hematoma/fistula size and hemodynamic changes with echocardiography provides a crucial basis for formulating a rational treatment strategy.

Introduction

Hematoma formation in the atrioventricular groove constitutes a rare but clinically critical complication of cardiac valve surgery. Improper management of this condition may lead to catastrophic intraoperative complications, including massive hemorrhage and myocardial rupture, with particular surgical relevance during mitral valvuloplasty and prosthetic valve replacement procedures [1]. This case report presents a unique clinical scenario of progressive atrioventricular groove hematoma following surgical correction of rheumatic mitral stenosis with concomitant atrial fibrillation. Through detailed documentation of the clinical evolution, therapeutic interventions, and longitudinal outcomes, this study integrates comprehensive literature analysis to systematically explore the pathogenesis, diagnostic challenges, treatment algorithms, and postoperative surveillance strategies associated with this surgical complication.

Case Presentation

This is a case of a 51‐year‐old female patient with an extensive history of valvular heart disease over 40 years, experiencing palpitations for the past 8 years, and a recent exacerbation of symptoms lasting half a month. Despite these persistent symptoms, she did not seek specialized medical care. Eight years ago, she started experiencing palpitations and dyspnea upon exertion, which temporarily subsided with rest. During the past half‐month, her condition deteriorated significantly. The frequency and severity of her symptoms escalated. She was then diagnosed with rheumatic valvular disease, specifically mitral valve stenosis complicated by atrial fibrillation, at a local hospital. Considering the severity of her condition and the need for surgical intervention, she was transferred to our department for further treatment.

Following exclusion of coronary heart disease and completion of a comprehensive preoperative evaluation, the patient successfully underwent a combined mitral valve repair (MVP) and maze procedure under general anesthesia with cardiopulmonary bypass support. Intraoperative examination revealed a dilated left atrium with the mitral valve apparatus demonstrating diffuse leaflet thickening, localized retraction of the anterior leaflet, and nodular calcific deposits at the leaflet edges. Notably, both anterolateral and posteromedial commissures exhibited fibrotic fusion with restricted valve mobility. Preoperative ultrasound measurements revealed a mitral valve orifice area of approximately 1.16 cm2. The biatrial radiofrequency ablation (Cox maze IV procedure) was performed. Subsequently, we excised the fibrous nodules on the valve leaflets' surface, thinned the thickened mitral valve leaflets, incised both commissures near the valve annulus, and intermittently sutured a 32‐mm complete mitral annuloplasty ring to complete the mitral valve plasty. Intraoperative restoration of sinus rhythm was achieved, and post‐repair transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed satisfactory leaflet coaptation and the absence of residual regurgitation. The mitral valve transvalvular flow rate was 2 m/s, and the transvalvular pressure was 16 mmHg.

Upon successful weaning from cardiopulmonary bypass, direct visual inspection revealed a 5‐cm hematoma originating from the basal posterior left ventricular wall at the junction of the left atrial appendage and ventricle, tracking along the atrioventricular groove. Notably, no active hemorrhage was identified intraoperatively (Figure 1A). TEE demonstrated a 4‐mm fistulous tract connecting the posterior mitral annulus to the hematoma cavity, with sonographic dimensions measuring 3.4 × 1.7 cm. Hemodynamic stability was maintained throughout with preserved biventricular contractility (LVEF 55%) and normotensive circulation (MAP 70–80 mmHg). Serial echocardiographic surveillance over 90 min showed no progression of hematoma size or new pericardial effusion. Given the stable clinical presentation and the absence of hemodynamic compromise, a conservative management strategy was adopted. The patient was transferred to the cardiac surgical ICU for continuous hemodynamic monitoring and protocolized surveillance echocardiography. In the ICU, the patient's hemodynamics remained stable, and they awoke naturally. Over the subsequent days, continuous echocardiographic monitoring was conducted to evaluate the size of the atrioventricular groove hematoma and any potential changes in the fistula. After confirming that there was no significant change in the size of the hematoma, the endotracheal tube was successfully extubated on postoperative Day 3 (POD 3) following standard weaning parameters, and the patient was discharged without additional complications on POD 8 post‐surgery.

Discharge and Follow‐Up Evaluation

The echocardiography results upon discharge showed the following: mitral valve orifice area of 2.49 cm2, mitral valve transvalvular blood flow velocity of 2.2 m/s, pressure gradient of 19 mmHg, atrioventricular groove hematoma size of 3.1 cm × 1.7 cm, and communication size between the hematoma and the left ventricle of 0.4 cm (Figure 1B), with a flow velocity of 4.2 m/s and a pressure gradient of 70 mmHg. Left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) value was 77%. Cardiac CT revealed a cystic cavity in the left atrioventricular groove, filled with contrast agent, with a size of 2.3 cm × 1.6 cm. The rupture was located below the posterior mitral valve of the left ventricle. The CT reconstruction images showed that the size of the hematoma had significantly decreased compared to intraoperative observations (Figure 1C). The electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with a heart rate of 80 beats per minute, occasionally accompanied by atrial premature contractions. Upon discharge, the patient was prescribed oral ACE inhibitors, diuretics, and nitrate drugs. Oral warfarin was administered for six months for anticoagulation, and regular coagulation monitoring (prothrombin time [PT]/international normalized ratio [INR]) was performed to guide the warfarin dosage. The patient's postoperative recovery was satisfactory, and no special discomfort was reported.

Throughout the discharge period, echocardiography was performed continuously to monitor changes in the size of the atrioventricular groove hematoma and cardiac alterations (specific values are detailed in Table 1). Seventeen months post surgery, the review indicated good mitral valve closure, with a mitral valve orifice area of 2.21 cm2, a transvalvular flow velocity of 2 m/s, a pressure gradient of 16 mmHg, and a left ventricular EF value of 73%. The atrioventricular groove showed no signs of abnormal hematoma or cysts. The electrocardiogram revealed sinus rhythm with incomplete right bundle branch block, suggesting that the atrioventricular groove hematoma had resolved and healed, the MVP was satisfactory, and cardiac function was normal with sinus rhythm.

Disscussion

The occurrence of an atrioventricular groove hematoma following mitral valve surgery is uncommon, but it can represent a form of cardiac rupture. Based on the characteristic anatomical predilection of the rupture site, it should be classified as a Type I cardiac rupture according to the postoperative cardiac rupture classification system [2]. Depending on the severity of the injury, it can manifest as hematoma, cardiac rupture, hemorrhage, etc. [3, 4]. Treatment options can be divided into conservative management [3] and surgical correction [4, 5]. The outcome can range from spontaneous resolution to successful repair, while severe cases may result in patient death due to uncontrollable bleeding or cardiac function damage.

The posterior left ventricular wall corresponding to the posterior mitral annulus is a common site for cardiac rupture. Common causes of rupture include (1) excessive resection of valve leaflet tissue, (2) removal of severely calcified valves, (3) excessive traction and accidental damage to the annulus, (4) implantation of an oversized valve prosthesis, (5) female patients, (6) elderly patients, and (7) patients undergoing reoperation. During mitral valve surgery, special attention should be paid to avoiding these factors to reduce the risk of cardiac rupture. If a cardiac rupture occurs, prompt diagnosis and treatment are crucial to minimize patient morbidity and mortality. Patients with rheumatic heart disease, particularly those with mitral stenosis, are at an increased risk of left ventricular rupture due to several factors. These include the typically smaller size of the left ventricle, the common occurrence of valve calcification, and the pathological changes caused by the rheumatic disease itself that lead to myocardial fragility. Most ruptures occur during the surgical procedure, but there have also been reports of ruptures discovered several years after mitral valve surgery [6]. In this particular case, a review of the surgical record revealed that the cause of the atrioventricular groove hematoma was likely due to accidental insertion of the tip of a right‐angled clamp into the fragile myocardium at the root of the posterior mitral valve annulus during the exploration of the mitral valve annulus position (Figure 1D). This led to the formation of an early hematoma within the atrioventricular groove myocardium corresponding to the posterior mitral valve annulus root after the heart resumed beating. Therefore, the cause of the hematoma in this patient was intraoperative exploration that resulted in damage to the fragile myocardium beneath the annulus, leading to the formation of an early postoperative hematoma. It is important to maintain strict technical precision to avoid injuring the vulnerable tissues beneath the annulus.

Dealing with atrioventricular groove hematomas after they occur is indeed a challenging task for surgeons. Kirklin's book mentions that when a small‐ to moderate‐sized atrioventricular groove hematoma forms and there is no significant ongoing bleeding, when the patient's hemodynamics remain stable, and when there is no significant change in the size of the hematoma over time, the hematoma itself may not require intervention [7]. The approach described by Yushi Kavase et al. [8] in managing atrioventricular groove hematomas during mitral valve surgery is indeed a systematic and concise decision‐making framework for surgeons. By considering multiple factors such as the location, depth, and size of the hematoma; the presence of ongoing bleeding; intraoperative ultrasound findings; the patient's myocardial contractility; and hemodynamic status, surgeons can make informed decisions about the management of atrioventricular groove hematomas. In most cases, atrioventricular groove hematomas can be managed conservatively. Surgical intervention is only necessary when there is a significant risk of bleeding or when specific criteria are met, such as the formation of a pseudoaneurysm larger than 1 cm or a hematoma that has gradually enlarged by more than 0.5 cm within 6 months [9].

In this case, the initial size of the hematoma was approximately 3.5 cm, with a fistula size of 0.4 cm and a pressure difference of 70 mmHg. However, due to the absence of obvious bleeding, a conservative approach was decided during the operation. The aim was to control the left ventricular volume load and closely monitor changes in hematoma size and pericardial drainage within the first three POD days. The tracheal intubation and mediastinal drainage tube were removed only after ensuring stability of the patient's condition. During the perioperative period, hematoma resolution was primarily assessed by the absence of expansion, no new pericardial effusion, and a reduction in hematoma size or pressure gradient. After discharge, patients underwent regular follow‐up with TTE. When TTE showed significant shrinkage or disappearance of the hematoma, cardiac CT was performed to confirm complete healing. Following discharge, follow‐up echocardiograms were conducted to monitor the hematoma, which gradually diminished and had disappeared by the second postoperative month. In this case, despite the relatively large size of the hematoma and the notable pressure gradient, the patient remained stable under conservative management, and the hematoma resolved rapidly within two months postoperatively. We attribute this outcome, which distinguishes it from other reported cases, to the fact that the patient underwent valve repair surgery. During the procedure, the sutures were placed along the annulus using an interrupted mattress fashion, avoiding penetration through the injured myocardial tissue. In contrast, most other cases may occur during valve replacement surgery, where sutures must pass through the compromised myocardium. Subsequent cardiac contraction can then lead to enlargement of the initial injury due to suture traction, potentially resulting in cardiac rupture. At the 17‐month follow‐up, the patient's postoperative cardiac function was satisfactory, with significant expansion of the mitral valve orifice after repair surgery. The heart rhythm remained sinus. This case demonstrates the effectiveness of a conservative approach in managing atrioventricular groove hematomas, particularly when there is no significant risk of bleeding and when close monitoring and follow‐up can be ensured.

Conclusion

Our case report details the conservative management of an atrioventricular groove hematoma complicating MVP for rheumatic mitral stenosis. Our treatment strategy achieved satisfactory results, but the etiology of the hematoma highlights the need for extreme delicacy and minimal exploration during valve exploration procedures for female patients with rheumatic heart disease. This approach aims to reduce the risk of atrioventricular groove hematoma and left ventricular rupture at the annular position. Additionally, we observed that blunt trauma to the root of the annulus during valvuloplasty might have a higher potential for recovery compared to sharp penetrating injuries caused by sutures during valve replacement.

To summarize, we clearly delineate the clinical criteria that support conservative management: (1) stability of the hematoma with no signs of expansion in serial imaging; (2) absence of new or increasing pericardial effusion; (3) hemodynamic stability throughout observation; (4) no evidence of rupture progression or worsening pressure gradient across the affected area.

The combination of various factors aids in making scientifically reasonable treatment decisions. It is important to note that a conservative treatment approach should be considered when close and frequent imaging surveillance and immediate surgical readiness can be ensured. When coupled with rigorous follow‐up observations, this conservative approach can safely and effectively manage atrioventricular groove hematomas, leading to favorable outcomes.
