Prenatal humid heat exposure and cord blood extracellular vesicle microRNA profiles in a Ghanaian Pregnancy Cohort: GRAPHS
Authors: Alison Lee, Seyram Kaali, Michelle Li, Mohammed Mujtaba Nuhu, Elena Colicino, Musah Osei, Darby Jack, Sule Awuni, Steven N Chillrud, Haotian Wu, Abigail Gaylord, Cascade Tuholske, Kwaku Poku Asante
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHumid heat exposures over pregnancy impact birth outcomes but mechanisms are poorly described. We leveraged the Ghana Randomized Air Pollution and Health Study (GRAPHS) to examine associations between maximum shaded wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) and cord blood extracellular vesicle (EV)-associated microRNA. We also considered heat index (HI) and dry air temperature as secondary exposures. Cord blood was collected at delivery and EVs isolated and RNAs sequenced and small RNA libraries constructed. We assigned participants’ trimester and whole pregnancy average heat exposures using satellite reanalysis heat exposures at the level of participant community of residence. We then performed linear regression to examine associations between prenatal heat exposures and log2-transformed miRNA counts and considered significance with an unadjusted P-value ≤ .01 and |beta| > 0.2. In 44 participants, we identified 91 microRNAs that were detected in at least 70% of samples. We identified that miR-146b-5p (β = 0.94 [95% CI = 0.27, 1.6], per 1-degree increase in WBGT); miR-381-3p (β = 2.23 [1.3, 3.15]), miR-378a-3p (β = −1.4, [−2.42, −0.39]), and miR-744-5p (β = 1.51 [0.46, 2.57]); and miR-126-3p (β = −0.75 [−1.2, −0.29]) were associated with first-, second-, and third-trimester WBGT, respectively. miR-1307-3p (β = 2.27 [0.61, 3.94] per 1-degree increase in WBGT) was associated with WBGT averaged over the whole pregnancy. Similar findings were observed with HI. In the GRAPHS cohort, we identified that humid-heat exposure over pregnancy alters the cord blood EV-associated miRNA profiles.Keywords: heat exposure, microRNA, cord blood extracellular vesicles, prenatal, wet bulb globe temperature

Introduction

Greenhouse gas emissions are driving rises in global temperatures, including higher frequency, intensity, and duration of heat events [1]. While a warmer climate impacts health everywhere, pregnant persons and particularly those living in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are especially vulnerable to the health impacts of heat exposure. The biological mechanisms through which heat experienced over pregnancy impact pregnancy outcomes are poorly understood. Maternal–fetal biochemical communication via circulating microRNA (miRNA) is a promising pathway that has been found to be altered with other maternal exposures [2, 3]. To our knowledge, the impact of maternal heat exposure over pregnancy on circulating extracellular vesicle (EV)-associated miRNA in cord blood is not known.

The perinatal period represents an important window of susceptibility to heat for both the pregnant person and the developing fetus. Heat can lead to dehydration and impair uterine blood flow, placental perfusion, and can ultimately affect fetal development [4]. Fetal and placental metabolism increase endogenous maternal heat production which, combined with increasing maternal body mass index over pregnancy, impairs thermoregulation [4]. These impacts may be especially detrimental to pregnancy in Ghana, where small-scale farming and physical labor are common and climate adaptation strategies, such as heat early warning systems and cooling centers, are not commonly available.

The impacts of heat over pregnancy on birth outcomes have been described in many settings. Our group found that higher humid heat exposure, measured as shaded wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) and heat index (HI), were independently associated with lower birth weight, shorter birth length, shorter gestational length. and higher odds of preterm birth [5]. These data support findings from other groups, largely in high-income countries [6]. Recent work by Zhu et al. focused specifically on the impacts of heat over pregnancy on birth outcomes in LMICs and identified that heat exposure as compared to the optimal temperature range was associated with increased risk of lower birth weight and low birth weight, including in sub-Saharan Africa [7].

MiRNAs are small non-coding RNAs that regulate many biological processes, including those implicated as heat-sensitive pathways [8, 9]. MiRNAs can be sorted into EVs and released into maternal and fetal circulations where they can impact gene expression in distant cells. Work by our group and others have found that the miRNA content of EVs is sensitive to maternal exposures including psychosocial stress and maternal health conditions such as asthma [2, 3]. We hypothesize that the profile of these circulating EV-encapsulated miRNAs, which are measurable in cord blood at delivery, is altered by prenatal heat exposures, with potential downstream health implications.

We leveraged the Ghana Randomized Air Pollution and Health Study (GRAPHS), a pregnancy cohort in the Bono East region of Ghana, which recruited pregnant persons from 2013 to 2014 [10], a subset of whom had cord blood collected at birth [11, 12]. GRAPHS analyses have identified that higher humid-heat exposures over pregnancy are associated with worse birth anthropometry and birth outcomes [5]. To investigate the whether maternal heat exposure may impact EV-encapsulated miRNAs as a potential mechanistic pathway, we leveraged banked cord blood plasma samples from n = 44 pregnancies, isolated EVs, and quantified the miRNA content. We then explored associations with humid heat over pregnancy and these EV-miRNA profiles.

Results

The cohort in this analysis was composed of the 44 GRAPHS participants with valid cord blood-derived EV-miRNA samples. Participant characteristics are described in Table 1; Supplemental Table 1 compares the characteristics of those included in this analysis and those who were not. Those included versus not included did not differ significantly (P ≤ .05). Overall participants were a median age of 25.2 years (interquartile range, IQR, 22.3–32.7) and had a normal body mass index (median 22.4, IQR 21.3, 23.7) at enrollment. Many participants had no formal education (n = 16, 36.4%). In this subset, only one pregnancy resulted in a preterm delivery. Distributions of exposure variables are shown in Supplemental Table 2.

Associations between trimester- and pregnancy-averaged WBGT and cord blood EV-miRNAs

We identified one miRNA associated with trimester-averaged WBGT in the first trimester (miR-146b-5p, β = 0.94 [95% CI = 0.27, 1.6], Fig. 1B), three miRNAs with the second trimester (miR-381-3p, β = 2.23 [1.3, 3.15]; miR-378a-3p, β = −1.4, [−2.42, −0.39]; miR-744-5p, β = 1.51 [0.46, 2.57], Fig. 1C), and one miRNA with the third trimester (miR-126-3p, β = −0.75 [−1.2, −0.29], Fig. 1D). For the models with WBGT averaged over the whole pregnancy, we identified one miRNA upregulated with prenatal heat (miR-1307-3p, β = 2.27 [0.61, 3.94], Fig. 1A). Associations between monthly average WBGT and cord blood EV-miRNAs

Sensitivity models examining trimester and monthly average dry air temperature

Similar to WBGT and HI, second-trimester average dry air temperature was associated with miR-381-3p (β = 0.84 [0.35, 1.33], Fig. 3). Notably, first- and third-trimester dry air temperature was associated with miR-192-5p (β = 0.83 [0.25, 1.42] and β = 0.99 [0.40, 1.59]), respectively, which previously was not associated with either WBGT or HI trimester averages. We did not identify associations between the whole pregnancy averages of dry air temperature models and miRNA. Supplemental Figure 3 and Supplemental Table 8 have the volcano plots and regression results for these models.

The models of monthly average dry air temperature identified temporal associations with miRNAs that differed from the identified monthly average WBGT and HI associations. For example, associations were identified with months 3, 8, 9, and 10 dry air temperature and miR-146b-5p (month 3: β = 0.58 [0.27, 0.90]; month 8: β = −0.45, [−0.74, −0.15]; month 9: β = −0.54 [−0.81, −0.26]; month 10: β = −0.51 [−0.82, −0.20]), in addition to the associations with months 1 and 2 dry air temperature (month 1: β = 0.42 [0.11, 0.73]; month 2: β = 0.51 [0.22, 0.80]), concordant with those observed for WBGT and HI. Associations with dry air temperature monthly averages and let-7d-3p and let-7b-5p were observed; previously no associations were observed between these miRNAs and WBGT and HI (Supplemental Fig. 5 for a visual summary of all three measures; the dry air temperature-specific results are in Supplemental Fig. 4 and Supplemental Table 9).

KEGG pathway analysis

Finally, we performed a KEGG pathway analysis on the 5 miRNA (miR-146b-5p, miR-381-3p, -378a-3p, -744-5p, and -126-3p) identified by the primary analysis of trimester-averaged WBGT. This was done using the DIANA miRPath version 4, using the TarBase version 8 targets, which are experimentally supported. We identified 64 pathways involving these miRNAs, shown in the heat map in Fig. 4. The top pathways were cellular senescence (miR-126-3p, miR-378a-3p) and cell cycle (miR-378a-3p, miR-146b-5p).

Discussion

Leveraging a pregnancy cohort in Ghana, we identified that humid-heat exposure alters cord blood EV-miRNA expression and the changes in EV-miRNA expression may vary with the timing of humid-heat exposures. We note that humid-heat exposure may upregulate some miRNAs while downregulating others, and KEGG pathway analyses suggest key pathways of cellular senescence and cell cycle pathways. These findings begin to elucidate how heat exposure, including humid-heat exposure, over pregnancy worsens maternal-child health outcomes.

EV-miRNAs are transported in the blood where they may impact distant organ function. These circulating miRNAs are critical regulators of numerous pathways and biological processes [13]. For example, animal models establish that in the developing fetus, these EV-encapsulated miRNAs may infiltrate developing organs and alter fetal gene expression [14, 15]. EV-encapsulated placental miRNAs also regulate interrelated systems important in intergenerational environmental exposure programming, including immune [16–20] and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis [21–23] function and oxidative stress (OS) [24, 25]. Our data suggest that newborns born to mothers with higher heat exposure have altered profiles of EV-encapsulated miRNAs in cord blood, which could impact newborn health and development.

We identified that higher WBGT exposure over pregnancy was associated with changes in cord blood EV-miRNA expression, with upregulation of three miRNAs including miR-146b-5p with higher first trimester; and miR-381-3p and miR-744-5p with higher second trimester WBGT exposures. miR-146b-5p has been identified in immune responses and inflammatory disease, and in oncologic processes. For example, miR-146b-5p has been identified as a hypoxia-induced regulator and mouse models suggest that it can be upregulated in response to ischemic injury such as cardiac infarction [26]. In pregnancy, miR-146b-5p has been shown to be upregulated in unexplained miscarriages, perhaps through inhibiting trophoblast proliferation, migration, invasion, and implantation [27]. miR-381-3p has been identified as an oncogenic regulator with overexpression accelerating cell proliferation and ultimately poorer tumor prognosis [28]. Conversely, increased expression of miR-744-5p in epithelial ovarian cancer models appears protective, inhibiting proliferation, migration, and invasions of cancer cells [29, 30].

We also note lower expression of miR-378a-3p with higher second-trimester and miR-126-3p with higher third-trimester WBGT average exposures. miR-378a-3p has been identified as critical for glucose homeostasis [31] and, although understanding of its role in pregnancy is limited, a recent study of preeclampsia identified that miR-378a-3p overexpression could increase trophoblast ability to proliferate, migrate, invade, and decrease trophoblast apoptosis [32]. miR-126-3p is also involved in glucose regulation with changes in expression observed in circulating EVs in maternal third-trimester blood [33]. These same miRNAs plus others were identified in the monthly analyses as well.

In our analyses, we present results from regressions utilizing different measures of heat exposure including humid-heat, such as WBGT and HI, and dry air temperature. Debate remains in the literature on the most appropriate exposure to use [34]. Dry air temperature may not predict skin temperature and does not take into account the effect of humidity on the body’s ability to cool as relative humidity (RH) increases [34, 35]. In using WBGT, HI, and dry air temperature, our analysis provides insights into how three commonly used heat stress metrics perform when assessing prenatal heat exposure in an understudied geography like Ghana.

As noted in Fig. 3, we do identify consistency among most of the WBGT and HI results, e.g. with associations identified between miR-744-5p, miR-381-3p, miR-378a-3p, and miR-126-3p. This is to be expected because our formulation of WBGT is a direct transform of HI. Nonetheless, by using these two humid-heat stress metrics our findings contribute to a growing literature on the interoperability of these two metrics [36]. Furthermore, dry air temperature results find similarities with associations, with miR-381-3p however identifies associations with miR-192-5p that are not seen with the humid-heat measures. While our findings do not identify the mechanisms that explain the observed differences between humid-heat and dry-heat exposure, by independently assessing the associations across multiple metrics, our results can be compared to a wider range of studies, many of which only use a single metric [37]. Continued research is needed to understand which heat measure best represents physiologic heat stress although similarity in findings among the humid-heat measures and miRNA support the associations.

Strengths of these findings include novel data from a well-characterized longitudinal Ghanaian pregnancy cohort with satellite reanalysis data assigned at community of residence over gestation and ability to control for multiple hypothesized confounders. We test multiple temperature measures and find similarity among results with different humid-heat measures. We also note that in this rural Ghanian cohort, access to residential cooling infrastructure such as air conditioners is extremely limited and largely unavailable at the household level; therefore, ambient measures may be more representative of actual exposure as compared to in a high-income country where climate control technology is prevalent. However, we also acknowledge that persons living in extreme heat may be better able to cope with higher temperatures [38, 39] and therefore these results may not be applicable to populations from different temperate regions. We also acknowledge that due to limited statistical power given by the small sample size and large number of biomarkers, we employed two joint conditions for declaring statistical significance (raw P < 0.01 and absolute beta > 0.2). This statistical approach provided a comprehensive list of potential biological signals (or candidate biomarkers) that can be foundation for future hypothesis-driven studies. Overall our results suggest that cord blood EV-miRNA may be a mechanistic pathway key to the intergenerational effects of heat exposure over pregnancy and support the replication of this work in a larger cohort. Future, larger studies can also consider gene target analyses.

In summary, heat exposure over pregnancy has been demonstrated to have health impacts for both mother and child. Our study adds mechanistic data to these observations, suggesting that prenatal heat exposure may alter EV-miRNA cargo, measurable in cord blood at delivery. Further study is needed to replicate these findings and understand the health programming implications.

Materials and methods

The GRAPHS cohort

The GRAPHS cohort has been described elsewhere [40]. Briefly, GRAPHS was a cluster randomized cookstove intervention trial designed to understand if a cleaner stove intervention to lower household air pollution exposure would improve birth weight and reduce pneumonia risk over the first year of life. In 2013–14, GRAPHS randomized 1414 pregnant persons into one of two intervention arms—liquefied petroleum gas stove or improved biomass stove—or control where the pregnant person continued to cook using the traditional three-stone open fire stove. We used ultrasound at study screening to confirm an intrauterine, singleton pregnancy and estimate gestational age [41]. Participant community of residence was recorded at GRAPHS study enrollment and updated over pregnancy. The data reported herein include the study participants for whom cord blood was collected at delivery and EVs were isolated and the miRNA cargo sequenced. This study was approved by the Kintampo Health Research Centre (KHRC) Institutional Ethics Committee and the institutional research boards of the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai and Columbia University. Written informed consent was obtained from all pregnant persons prior to the commencement of study activities.

Climate variables

We use daily shaded maximum WBGT, daily maximum HI, and daily maximum dry air temperature data derived from CHIRTS-daily Tmax [42] and RH generated from hourly dew-point temperature (Td) from ERA5 climate reanalysis data [43]. Available at 5-km spatial resolution, CHIRTS-daily has been shown to be highly accurate dry air temperature data for data-sparse countries like Ghana [42]. Following Tuholske et al. [44], we first estimate HI with dry air temperature and RH following the US National Weather Service’s procedure [45]. We then transform HI to WBGT using an established quadratic relationship that assumes fixed wind speed 0.05 m/s and no radiated heat [46].

Heat stress metric selection is unsettled in the literature [34]. While shaded WBGT is a well-established heat stress [46] metric used in heat epidemiological research [47], it does not capture the additional influences of wind nor radiated heat measured by outdoor WBGT. We present WBGT as our primary metric to be consistent with and comparable to the aforementioned heat and birth outcomes study the GRAPHS cohort [5]. By performing sensitive analysis using HI and dry air temperature, we provide a more comprehensive understanding of associations between heat stress and miRNA in LMICs. Our findings can thus be compared to similar studies to a wider range of studies.

We assigned a latitude and longitude to each participant at the level of the study community. Using the ultrasound-derived dates of conception and the date of delivery and the participant-specific community location, we assigned WBGT, HI, and dry air temperature for each day of gestation. We then averaged the heat variable over the entire pregnancy and over each trimester. We defined trimester one as weeks 0–13 gestation, trimester two as weeks 14–27 gestation, and trimester three as weeks 28 through the date of delivery. We also calculated average exposure per month gestation, with every 4 weeks being counted as a month.

Cord blood collection, EV isolation, and miRNA sequencing

Immediately following delivery, cord blood was collected into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) tubes and transported on IsoRack cool packs (Iso Therm-System, Hamburg, Germany) to the KHRC Clinical Laboratory where they were centrifuged and the plasma aliquoted and stored at −80°C. Following collection of all samples, the samples were shipped to the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai on dry ice with box temperature tracking (World Courier).

EVs were isolated from plasma samples using ExoQuick® ULTRA EV Isolation Kit for Serum and Plasma. RNA was then extracted from EVs using the Qiagen miRNeasy Micro Kit. The samples were sequenced and small RNA libraries were constructed using the NEBNext Small RNA library kit. The samples were pooled and run on a MiSeq before rebalanced and run on the NovaSeq. The final size selection range for the library was 120–135 bp. Sequencing data were processed using previously described workflow [48]. Data were mapped using the ExceRpt small RNA sequencing data analysis pipeline on the Genboree Workbench (http://genboree.org/site/exrna_toolset/). Mapping parameters specified a minimum read length of 15 nucleotides and 0 mismatches allowed, with the rest on default. We removed all samples with failed library preparation, defined as <100 000 total input reads (one plasma sample), and samples with low abundance of mapped miRNA reads (seven plasma samples, including the one above), defined as <10 000 total miRNA reads. Reads that were not aligned to a single unique miRNA due to sequence similarities were collapsed into a single category that represented multiple miRNAs. In total, 44 plasma samples passed QC.

Covariates

On study enrollment, we ascertained maternal age (continuous variable). We queried for any past medical history by the questionnaire. A composite binary variable to represent maternal health was defined by whether the participant reported on enrollment any of the following in their medical history including e.g. asthma, hypertension, sickle cell disease, and whether the participant had malaria over pregnancy, defined via placental histopathology examination for malaria as previously described [49]. All pregnant persons were non-smokers; however, we assessed secondhand smoke by the questionnaire. Specifically we asked, “Does anybody in the household smoke?” and “Does anybody in the compound smoke?” both with yes/no responses. The date of delivery was recorded within 24 h of delivery and used to categorize season of delivery into rainy season versus dry season, with rainy season defined as April, May, June, September, and October and dry season as all other months. Fetal sex was determined at delivery.

Statistical analyses

The cord blood-derived miRNA counts were log base 2 transformed and normalized to counts per million, as is standard. As an initial screen, we only analyzed the 91 miRNAs that were detected in at least 70% of samples; Supplemental Table 3 has a full list of the miRNAs.

Our general model approach was to fit regression models by regressing the sequencing results from each cord blood EV-miRNA against different windows of prenatal heat exposure. For our primary analysis, we fit linear regressions between each of the 91 miRNA counts and the trimester averages of WBGT, with all three trimesters in one model, adjusting for prior medical diagnoses (see Table 1), secondhand tobacco smoke exposure, age at enrollment, season of delivery (rainy or dry), and fetal sex. We fit the same models for trimester averages of secondary exposure measures, HI, and dry air temperature. We also regressed each of the 91 miRNA counts against whole pregnancy averages of each heat exposure, adjusting for the same covariates. As a secondary analysis, we examined heat exposures at a monthly level by regressing each miRNA count against monthly averages of each heat exposure considered separately and adjusted for the same list of covariates. As we previously have done, due to the small number of participants, we used a less strict threshold to identify miRNAs that were associated with prenatal heat exposure: unadjusted P-value ≤ .01 and |beta| > 0.2, where beta is the effect size of the heat average [2]. We also report the P-value corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure in the regression result supplementary tables. Finally, we included identified miRNA for the primary analysis in a KEGG pathway analysis using DIANA miRPath version 4.

Statistical software

We performed all analyses using R version 4.3.1 using the base stats package. For the KEGG pathway analysis we employed the DIANA miRPath V4 with analysis parameters as follows: direct TarBase v8.0 targets, did not include LncRNA targets, and selected miRBase-v22.1 annotation. We merged using pathways union and classic analysis as the testing method and set the P-value threshold of .05 with FDR correction.
