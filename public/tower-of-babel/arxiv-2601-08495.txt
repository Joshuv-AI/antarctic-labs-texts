Toolchain for shuttling trapped-ion qubits in segmented traps Preprint: AIP/123-QED
A. Conta, S. Bogino, F. Köhncke, F. Schmidt-Kaler, U. G. Poschinger

Abstract
Scalable trapped-ion quantum computing requires fast and reliable transport of ions through complex, segmented radiofrequency trap architectures without inducing excessive motional excitation. We present a numerical toolchain for the systematic generation of time-dependent electrode voltages enabling fast, low-excitation ion shuttling in segmented radiofrequency traps. Based on a model of the trap electrode geometry, the framework combines an electrostatic field solver, efficient unconstrained optimization, waveform postprocessing, and dynamical simulations of ion motion to compute voltage waveforms that realize prescribed transport trajectories while respecting experimental constraints such as voltage limits and bandwidth. The toolchain supports arbitrary trap geometries, including junctions and multi-zone layouts, and allows for the flexible incorporation of optimization objectives. We provide a detailed assessment of the accuracy of the framework by investigating its numerical stability and by comparing measured and predicted secular frequencies. The framework is optimized for numerical performance, enabling rapid numerical prototyping of trap architectures of increasing complexity. As application examples, we apply the framework to the transport of a potential well along a linear, uniformly segmented trap, and we compute a solution for shuttling a potential well around the corner of an X-type trap junction. The presented approach provides an extensible and highly efficient numerical foundation for designing and validating transport protocols in current and next-generation trapped-ion processors.

The current maturation of quantum computer platforms entails the demand of increasingly complex methods and tools for designing hardware components and control protocols for operations on the physical level. Such tools are crucial prerequisites for the realization of platforms offering genuine quantum advantage for meaningful computational tasks, and their development and deployment requires combined expertise from physics, engineering, mathematics and computer science.

The shuttling operations are performed by applying suitable pre-computed voltage waveforms, generated by a multichannel arbitrary waveform generator
Kaushal et al. 2020

The waveforms have to be designed such that the residual motion of the ions after completion of the operation is small, as subsequent radiation-driven gate operations require localization of the ions on small length scales, for example given by optical wavelengths or tight foci of laser fields mediating the gates. On the other hand, it is desirable to execute the shuttling operations as fast as possible, in order to reduce the operational overhead
Pino et al. 2021
;
Hilder et al. 2022

The toolchain presented in this work serves the purpose of computing suitable voltage waveforms for enabling shuttling operations at high speed and precision, informed on the hardware resources of a given platform. The task of finding voltage waveforms for a particular shuttling operation can be formulated as follows: One seeks to determine a temporally ordered sequence of sets of electrode voltages, such that confining potential wells move within the trap in a predefined way. This task can be broken down into two mostly decoupled problems: First, one has to find voltage sets which place potential wells at given locations within the trap. Second, actual time-dependent voltage waveforms have to be generated from the voltage set sequence. The waveforms determine the dynamics of the shuttling operations and have to be generated in view of a suitable tradeoff between speed and residual motion. For this, one typically distinguishes between
adiabatic
shuttling, where the voltage waveforms lead to sufficiently slow movement of the potential wells to ensure low motional excitation, and
diabatic
shuttling
Bowler et al. 2012
;
Walther et al. 2012
, where control techniques are applied. A variety of such techniques exists and has been studied for the purpose of ion shuttling, such as optimal control
Schulz et al. 2006
, invariant-based inverse engineering
Torrontegui et al. 2011
;
Palmero et al. 2015
or bang-bang control
Alonso et al. 2013
. These techniques are beyond the scope of this work, and we refer to
Fürst et al. 2014
for a comparative overview. However, such control strategies are usually formulated in terms of equilibrium positions and secular frequencies as the control parameters, while in practice, the accessible control parameters are the electrode voltages. Putting fast and accurate motion control protocols into practice therefore requires a numerical ‘gearbox’ for translating between the hardware-accessible and physical-layer controls, which is precisely the toolchain presented in this work.

In this work, we present a method for unambiguous local expansion of trap potentials into spherical harmonics, based on the concept of spherical
t

This provides the foundation for the next step, namely the computation of shuttling solutions from a suitable parametrization of the desired shuttling operation. Based on related concepts and ideas from earlier work

Here, a multi-target optimization is controlled by penalty functions in a way which allows for problem-specific and hardware-informed generation of viable shuttling solutions. The numerical efficiency of the entire pipeline is optimized to a degree of performance which allows for its direct integration into the transpiler stack of a shuttling-based trapped-ion platform

II
Confinement properties from spherical harmonics expansion of electrostatic potentials

We consider a segmented trap consisting of electrodes
n=1\ldots N
. Note that some scenarios, it is convenient to group physical trap electrodes into electrode sets. In the following, we always refer to
electrodes
, but implicitly include this option. Applying a voltage of +1 V to electrode
n
and biasing all other electrodes to 0 V gives an electrostatic potential - the dimensionless
unit potential
- denoted
\phi_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})
. The total electrostatic potential for an arbitrary applied voltage set
\{V_{n}\}
is then given by linear superposition

\Phi(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})=\sum_{n}V_{n}\phi_{n}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}).

(1)

The unit potentials are computed for a given trap geometry by using an electrostatics solver, which computes an influence matrix describing the electrostatic potential at a surface element, induced from a surface charges carried by any other surface element. The inversion of the influence matrix for a given trap geometry then allows for computing the surface charges on the electrode surface elements for given applied electrode voltages. Finally, with the surface charges for unit voltage settings available, the corresponding unit potentials can be computed for any point within the trap volume via Green’s functions
Singer et al. 2010
. We note that for the case of planar electrodes geometries, direct computation of the potentials and fields via a gapless plane approximation is also possible
Wesenberg 2008
;
Wesenberg 2009
.

Computing voltage ramps for generating viable shuttling solutions requires the capability of computing electric fields, potential curvatures and eventually higher-order derivatives of the unit potentials. The accurate computation of such derivatives can be cumbersome, as approaches based finite difference methods or fitting to model functions can lead to hardly controllable systematic errors. Furthermore, extracting derivatives from potential values computed on a three-dimensional grid can be numerically demanding: Consider a shuttling path composed of
T
intermediate support points, and assume that for each point along the path, the unit potentials on a regular grid of
L^{3}
vertices ought to be computed for extracting the required derivatives - this requires invoking an electrostatics solver on
\mathcal{O}(L^{3}TN)
points in total. We therefore emphasize that a numerical toolchain for computing shuttling solutions ultimately requires an efficient and accurate method for computation of all required derivatives.

Our framework is based on the fact that the confinement properties - i.e. equilibrium positions, secular trap frequencies and orientations of the secular modes - are only of interest along predefined
shuttling paths
.
Therefore, the framework is based on a representation of the trap potentials which is illustrated in Fig.
2
: For a given trap geometry and a given shuttling operation, a shuttling path comprised of a finite number of support points is defined via a suitable parametrization, and suitable representations of trap potentials are computed at each of these points. Note that a shuttling path may consist of a fixed single position, for example for a shuttling operation such as ion crystal rotation
Kaufmann et al. 2017
, throughout which the orientation of a potential ellipsoid is changed, but not its location. The shuttling paths are set up on a global trap coordinate system
\hat{x},\hat{y},\hat{z}
, on which the trap geometry is defined and the electrostatics solver operates. Then, a finite set of support points
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}_{t},\;t=1\ldots T
is placed along the path, with the index
t
labeling
sequence steps
. The shuttling operation is realized by a sequence of electrode voltage sets consisting of
voltage samples

V_{n,t}
, such that for each sequence step
t
, a combined electrostatic and ponderomotive potential ellipsoid is obtained. Ideally, the ellipsoid at sequence step
t
is centered at
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}_{t}
and has specified orientations and axes lengths, which determine the orientations and frequencies of the secular modes. A local coordinate system
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\hat{x}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\hat{y}}}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\hat{z}}}^{\prime}
is attached to each support point, which allows for convenient specification of the semi-axes. As exemplified in Fig.
2
, arranging the primed axes to be tangential and perpendicular to a shuttling path is convenient for specifying a potential ellipsoid which remains aligned with the path throughout the shuttling operation.

II.1
Multipole expansion of the unit potentials using spherical designs

The method presented here relies on real regular solid spherical harmonics used as basis functions to expand all unit potentials around each point along the shuttling path. These are defined as

R_{l,m}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})=\frac{1}{r^{l}}\begin{cases}Y_{l,0}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})&m=0\\
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(Y_{l,m}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})+(-1)^{m}Y_{l,-m}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})\right)&m>0\\
\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{-2}}\left(Y_{l,m}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})-(-1)^{m}Y_{l,-m}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})\right)&m<0\end{cases}

(2)

with
r=|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}|
and the
Y_{l,m}
being the complex-valued standard spherical harmonics. Note that similarly to the standard spherical harmonics, the regular solid spherical harmonics are orthonormal with respect to integration over the surface of a unit sphere,

\int_{\mathbb{S}^{2}}R_{l,m}(1,\Omega)R_{l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(1,\Omega)d\Omega=\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}

(3)

and are also solutions of the Laplace equation, i.e.
\Delta R_{l,m}=0
. Note that for our purposes, the
R_{l,m}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})
need to be explicitly specified in Cartesian coordinates. We require an as-small-as possible set of
K
points arranged on the surface of the unit sphere:

\mathcal{P}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{K}\in\mathbb{S}^{2}\},

(4)

on which we evaluate the unit potential of interest. From the resulting potential values, we seek to obtain an unambiguous expansion of the unit potential around a given support point. An expansion up to order
L
comprises
(L+1)^{2}
basis functions. The point set
\mathcal{P}
would be an exact spherical
t
-design with
t=2L
if the orthogonality relation Eq.
3
is preserved upon evaluation on the finite point set:

\sum_{k=1}^{K}R_{l,m}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{k})R_{l^{\prime},m^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{k})\propto\delta_{ll^{\prime}}\delta_{mm^{\prime}}

(5)

which is a sufficient condition for reliable extraction of the expansion coefficients from the potential values evaluated on point set
\mathcal{P}
. Generating spherical
t
-designs can be cumbersome, furthermore less points than contained in an exact
t
-design may actually be sufficient to achieve good accuracy for a given expansion order
L
. Therefore, we use spherical Fibonacci grids to generate point sets
\mathcal{P}
with good uniform and equidistant distribution on the unit sphere, see Appendix
A
for details.

We evaluate the spherical basis functions at all points of the set
\mathcal{P}
:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{l,m}=\left(R_{l,m}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{1}),\ldots,R_{l,m}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{K})\right)^{T},

(6)

and arrange these in a
(L+1)^{2}\times K
matrix
V
comprised of
(L+1)^{2}

K
-dimensional row vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{l,m}
, or respectively of
K

(L+1)^{2}
-dimensional column vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}_{i}
:

V=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{0,0}^{T}\\
\ \boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{1,-1}^{T}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{4,4}^{T}\end{pmatrix}=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}_{(L+1)^{2}}\right).

(7)

With
\mathcal{P}
failing to be a proper spherical 8-design, Eq.
5
does not hold and consequently, the column vectors are not orthogonal:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}_{j}\not\propto\delta_{ij}.

(8)

From the Gram matrix

G=\{g_{ij}\}=\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}_{j}\},

(9)

we obtain a transformed matrix

U=G^{-1/2}V=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{(L+1)^{2}}\right),

(10)

which is comprised of orthonormal column vectors:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{j}=\delta_{ij}.

(11)

We can use these to expand the unit potential
\phi
for any electrode (or electrode set) at any point
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}
, specified in the trap coordinate system. Let
\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}})
be the
SO(3)
rotation matrix which rotates the global coordinate axes
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\hat{x}}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\hat{y}}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\hat{z}}}
onto local axes
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\hat{x}^{\prime}}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\hat{y}^{\prime}}},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\hat{z}^{\prime}}}
and
\kappa
a suitably chosen scaling parameter (typically a small fraction of a reference trap geometry parameter). We apply a Poincaré transform to the design points from set
\mathcal{P}
Eq.
4
to obtain a set
\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}_{K}\}
of
K
points in the trap coordinate system, distributed on the surface of a sphere of radius
\kappa
and centered at
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}
:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}_{k}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}+\kappa\;\mathcal{R}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}})\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{k}.

(12)

Using the electrostatics solver, we evaluate the unit potential of interest at these points and arrange the results in a
K
-element vector
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\phi}}
:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\phi}}=\{\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}_{1}),\ldots,\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{q}}_{K})\}.

(13)

We can now directly project out the components of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\phi}}_{n}
in the orthonormal basis given by the column vectors of
U
, obtaining an
(L+1)^{2}
-dimensional column vector of expansion coefficients:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{c}}}=(\tilde{c}_{i})^{T}=(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\phi}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{u}}_{i})^{T}.

(14)

In the next step, we seek to obtain expansion coefficients uniquely and unambiguously pertaining to the spherical basis functions. To that end, we revert the orthonormalization to obtain a new coefficient vector from the coefficient vector
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{c}}}
:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{c}}=\left(G^{1/2}\right)^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{c}}}.

(15)

Finally, we have to rescale the coefficients with the power of the sphere radius
\kappa
, pertaining to the respective value of

c_{i}\leftarrow c_{i}/\kappa^{l_{i}}.

(16)

As a result, we obtain an expansion of
\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})
in the spherical basis around
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}
with respect to the primed axis system:

\phi(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{(L+1)^{2}}c_{i}\;R_{l_{i},m_{i}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}^{\prime}).

(17)

II.2
Electrostatic, ponderomotive and total trap potentials

In this section, we establish how to generally characterize confinement in terms of residual forces and Hessians for combined electrostatic and ponderomotive forces and general trap geometries. Here and in the following, we denote all quantities derived from unit-voltage potentials with small letters (
\phi,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}},h
) and quantities scaled with applied trap voltages with capital letters (
\Phi,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}},H
). Implicitly, all these quantities are evaluated at a support point
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}
, to which a local coordinate system (
x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}
) with basis vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\epsilon}}_{s}
is attached, see Fig.
2
.

The unit voltage electric field (in units of
m^{-1}
) generated by electrode (set)
n
and expressed in a local coordinate system, reads

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{n}=-\boldsymbol{\nabla}^{\prime}\;\phi_{n}=-\sum_{s}\partial_{s}\phi_{n}\;\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{s}.

(18)

The unit voltage Hessian matrix (in units of
\text{m}^{-2}
) containing all second derivatives of the unit potential for electrode
n
, is given by

h_{n}=\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\nabla}}^{\prime}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\nabla}}^{\prime}\right)\phi_{n}=\sum_{r,s}\partial_{r}\partial_{s}\phi_{n}\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\epsilon}}_{r}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\epsilon}}_{s}.

(19)

The total electrostatic field and Hessian at support point
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}
with electrode voltages
\{V_{n}\}
applied are given by

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{dc}}=\sum_{n}V_{n}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{n}\qquad H_{\text{dc}}=\sum_{n}V_{n}h_{n}.

(20)

Confinement in a radiofrequency (rf) trap requires at least one additional oscillatory electric field, which generates a time-averaged
ponderomotive
potential (or pseudo-potential). We consider a trap with rf electrodes supplied with a single rf signal at fixed voltage amplitude
V_{\text{rf}}
and drive frequency
\Omega
, which generates the electrostatic unit potential
\phi_{\text{rf}}
, the unit electric field
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}
and the unit Hessian
h_{\text{rf}}
. The resulting ponderomotive potential for a particle with charge
Q
and mass
m
in an electric field
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{rf}}=V_{\text{rf}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}
oscillating at frequency
\Omega
, expressed in Volts for convenience, is given by:

\Phi_{\text{rf}}=\frac{Q\;V_{\text{rf}}^{2}}{4m\Omega^{2}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}\right)=\frac{\alpha_{\text{rf}}}{2}\sum_{s}\left(\partial_{s}\phi_{\text{rf}}\right)^{2},

(21)

where we have lumped constants into the prefactor (units of
Vm^{2}
)

\alpha_{\text{rf}}=\frac{Q\;V_{\text{rf}}^{2}}{2m\Omega^{2}}.

(22)

Note that we specify ponderomotive forces as
effective electric fields
, which is convenient for treating these on the same footing as electrostatic forces. An inhomogeneous oscillating field gives rise to a ponderomotive force, expressed as an effective electric field (in units of
V/m
):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{rf}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\nabla}}^{\prime}\Phi_{\text{rf}}

(23)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\alpha_{\text{rf}}\sum_{r,s}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\epsilon}}_{r}\;\partial_{s}\phi_{\text{rf}}\;\partial_{r}\partial_{s}\phi_{\text{rf}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{\text{rf}}h_{\text{rf}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}.

The last equality is obtained by using Eqs.

\omega_{u}\ll\Omega

The total effective electric field (in V/m) is given by the sum of the electrostatic and ponderomotive fields:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{rf}}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{dc}}=\alpha_{\text{rf}}h_{\text{rf}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}+\sum_{n}V_{n}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{n}.

The combined Hessian pertaining to all dc and rf electrodes (in

H=H_{\text{rf}}+H_{\text{dc}}=\alpha_{\text{rf}}\left(\tilde{h}_{\text{rf}}^{(a)}+\tilde{h}_{\text{rf}}^{(b)}\right)+\sum_{n}V_{n}h_{n}.

It holds that

{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}}

(27)

If the unit vector

(31)

II.3
Forces and curvatures from multipole expansion

Once the spherical expansion coefficients are obtained for all relevant electrodes at a given position
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}
and with respect to the chosen axes system, the total forces and potential curvatures can be computed.
To that end, we compute the derivatives of the basis functions
R_{i}
at the origin of their proper coordinate system
x,y,z
(see Fig.
2
), which leads to constant coefficients not depending on
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}
. Weighing these coefficients with the expansion coefficients computed in the primed system attached to
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}
then gives the required derivatives for these coordinates. From computing forces and curvatures, including the ponderomotive potential, an expansion order of
L=3
is sufficient. For points
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}
restricted to the rf-null, an expansion order of
L=2
is sufficient. In the following, all expansions coefficients
c_{i}
implicitly depend on the electrode index
n
and the support point
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{p}}
.

For computing the unit voltage electric field Eq.
18
for an electrode of interest, only
l=1
components need to be taken into account:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{n}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=2}^{4}c_{i}\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\nabla}}\;R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})|_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}=0}

(32)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{3}{4\pi}}\left(c_{4},c_{2},-c_{3}\right)^{T}.

For computing the unit voltage Hessians, only the five coefficients pertaining to
l=2
need to be taken into account:

h_{r,s}^{(n)}=\sum_{i=5}^{9}c_{i}\;\partial_{r}\partial_{s}\,R_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}})|_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}=0}

(33)

which gives

h^{(n)}=-\sqrt{\frac{15}{4\pi}}\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}c_{7}-c_{9}&c_{5}&c_{8}\\
c_{5}&\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}c_{7}+c_{9}&c_{6}\\
c_{8}&c_{6}&-\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}c_{7}\end{pmatrix}.

(34)

Note that the rf electrode unit voltage fields
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}
and Hessians
h_{\text{rf}}
have the same expansion coefficient representations as the corresponding dc quantities.

The ponderomotive force Eq.
23
computes from the expansion coefficients as

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{rf}}=\frac{3\sqrt{5}}{4\pi}\alpha_{\text{rf}}\begin{pmatrix}c_{3}c_{5}-\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}c_{2}c_{7}-c_{4}c_{8}\\
-c_{2}c_{5}-c_{4}c_{6}+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}}c_{3}c_{7}+c_{3}c_{9}\\
c_{3}c_{6}+\tfrac{2}{\sqrt{3}}c_{4}c_{7}-c_{2}c_{8}\end{pmatrix}.

(35)

Explicit expressions for both parts of the pseudopotential Hessian
H_{\text{rf}}
Eq.
24
in terms of the expansion coefficients are given in Appendix (Eqs.
87
,
88
).

III
Computing shuttling solutions

Given the established capability to accurately and efficiently compute derivatives of the unit potentials for all trap electrodes at any given position within the trap volume, we now describe how these can be used to compute sequences of voltage sets - termed
shuttling solutions
- providing the basis for realizing a given shuttling operation. This means that each sequence step
t=1\ldots T
, we seek to establish
w=1\ldots W

potential wells
centered at positions
\boldsymbol{r}_{w,t}
specified in the trap coordinate system. For any given well
w
, the set of well positions
\{\boldsymbol{r}_{w,t}\}
defines a
shuttling path
(see Fig.
2
). Note that
t
and
T
do not specify actual timings, but nevertheless refer to temporally ordered sequences. Throughout a shuttling process, two well positions can emerge from (or converge to) the same location, which realizes splitting (or merging) operations.

We emphasize at this point that a shuttling solution is just an ordered sequence of electrode voltage sets suitable for quasi-static realization of a shuttling operation, it does neither take actual physical dynamics into account, nor do the steps
t
correspond to actual timings. The subsequent computation of actual
voltage waveforms
based on concrete hardware parameters and ion dynamics is covered further below in Sec.
VII
.

A potential well being characterized by a confining potential ellipsoid, we also specify the orientation of its main axis and the curvatures along these. The task now consists of determining voltage levels
V_{n,t}
for each dc electrode
n
and sequence step
t
, which realize the desired shuttling operation as good as possible, informed on limited resources such as bandwidth and voltage range. In the following, we express the task of determining optimal voltage set sequences for a given shuttling process as an unconstrained quadratic optimization problem, consisting of several penalty terms
\mathcal{F}^{(p)}
. Such problem is generally equivalent to solving a linear system, which allows for straightforward and efficient numerical solution, without resorting to numerical optimization procedures.

III.1
Shuttling penalties

Each penalty consists of a penalty kernel
\mathcal{F}^{(p)}_{u,t,w}
and a weight vector
\mathcal{W}_{u,t,w}^{(p)}
, and the products of these are summed over the sequence step
t
, wells
w
and spatial directions
u={x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}}
:

\mathcal{F}^{(p)}=\sum_{u,t,w}\mathcal{W}_{u,t,w}^{(p)}\mathcal{F}^{(p)}_{u,t,w}.

(36)

The weight factors
\mathcal{W}_{u,t,w}^{(p)}
serve to set the relative importance of the penalty, and may depend on
u,t,w
. The quality of the optimization results may strongly depend on the management of the penalty terms via the weight factors, which is facilitated by separating the weights into a constant scaling factor and dimensionless factors which may depend on
u,t,w
:

\mathcal{W}_{u,t,w}^{(p)}=\mathcal{W}_{0}^{(p)}W_{u,t,w}^{(p)}.

(37)

For determining suitable penalty scaling factors, we need to fix reference secular frequencies
\omega_{\text{ref},u}
, which can be the preset secular frequencies at the beginning of the shuttling operation.

In the following, we individually discuss the different relevant penalty terms.

III.1.1
Well position penalty

For establishing a confining potential well at position
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w,t}
, the total force evaluated at
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w,t}

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{w,t}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w,t}),

(38)

has to vanish:

||\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{w,t}||^{2}\overset{\text{!}}{=}0.

(39)

We define the corresponding penalty kernel to be the weighted sum of the squared residual force components along directions
u
:

\mathcal{F}^{(1)}=\sum_{u,w,t}\mathcal{W}^{(1)}_{u,w,t}E_{u,w,t}^{2}.

(40)

where residual force components (specified as an effective electric field in
V/m
) are obtained from Eq.
25
. This penalty serves for positioning minima of the
combined
electrostatic and ponderomotive potential. Unless enforced by symmetry or appropriate grouping of trap electrodes, the minima may not coincide, leading to excess micromotion, i.e. rapid oscillations of the ion driven by a nonzero rf field. Throughout shuttling operations, transient excess micromotion may be tolerated, as it does not necessarily lead to residual motion upon completion of the shuttling. For shuttling solutions with potential wells moving outside the rf null, the criteria listed in Appendix
B
should be tested a posteriori to check the validity of the pseudopotential approximation. Furthermore, noise cross-coupling from the rf field may lead to increased heating outside of the rf null
Blakestad et al. 2009
;
Blakestad et al. 2011
;
Taniguchi et al. 2025
, the effect of which may be estimated once the relevant noise spectral densities are known.

We pin down a reference scale for controlling the penalty weight by fixing reference position deviations
\Delta u
and computing the magnitude the penalty would assume for this deviation at a preset secular frequency
\omega_{\text{ref},u}
:

\displaystyle QE_{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-m\omega_{\text{ref},u}^{2}\Delta u

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\mathcal{F}^{(1)}_{u,w,t}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m^{2}}{Q^{2}}\omega_{\text{ref},u}^{4}\Delta u^{2}.

(41)

Now, if we choose

\mathcal{W}^{(1)}_{0}=\frac{Q^{2}}{m^{2}\omega_{\text{ref},u}^{4}\Delta u^{2}},

(42)

we get one unit of penalty 1 if at step
t
, well
w
is off by
\Delta u
along direction
u
at secular frequency
\omega_{\text{ref},u}
.

III.1.2
Confinement penalty

It is generally beneficial to keep secular frequencies constant throughout a shuttling operation, or at least to maintain as much confinement as possible, in order to suppress excitation from inertia and squeezing. Moreover, background-noise induced heating can drastically increase at low secular frequencies
Brownnutt et al. 2015
. We therefore seek to fix the secular frequencies
\omega_{u}
and the orientation of the main axes of the confining potential at all well positions
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w,t}
, by pinning down the entries of the total Hessian Eq.
26
, evaluated at each
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w,t}
:

H_{w,t}=H(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w,t}).

(43)

The corresponding penalty, for each time step
t
and well
w
, is then given by the squared Frobenius norm of the difference of the actual total Hessian
H
and the desired one
H^{(set)}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\{uu^{\prime}\},w,t}\mathcal{W}^{(2)}_{u,u^{\prime},w,t}||H_{w,t}-H_{w,t}^{(set)}||^{2}_{F}.

(44)

It can be advantageous to orient the primed coordinate system used for computing the potential coefficients at each desired well position along the desired axes (see Fig.
2
). Upon choosing desired secular frequencies
\omega_{\text{ref},u}
, the desired total Hessian will then assume diagonal form:

H^{(set)}_{u,u^{\prime}}=\frac{m}{Q}\omega_{\text{ref},u}^{2}\delta_{uu^{\prime}}.

(45)

Note that depending on the shuttling operation of interest, the preset secular frequencies
\omega_{\text{ref},u}
may (but must not) depend on
w,t
.

For determining a suitable scaling factor for the weights, we assume diagonal actual and desired Hessians. The penalty kernel from Eq.
44
simplifies to

\displaystyle H_{w,t}-H_{w,t}^{(set)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{m}{Q}\left(\omega_{u}^{2}-\omega_{\text{ref},u}^{2}\right)\delta_{uu^{\prime}}

(46)

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle 2\frac{m}{Q}\omega_{\text{ref},u}\Delta\omega_{u}.

Fixing reference deviations
\Delta\omega_{u}
between actual and desired secular frequencies, and setting the penalty scaling factor to

\mathcal{W}^{(2)}_{0}=\frac{Q^{2}}{4m^{2}\omega_{\text{ref},u}^{2}\Delta\omega^{2}}

(47)

yields one unit of penalty 2 when the secular frequency along
u
is deviates from the reference frequency by
\pm\Delta\omega
.

III.1.3
Voltage penalty

The moduli of the voltage samples are generally to kept small in order to comply with the voltage limits of the employed waveform generator. The corresponding penalty reads

\mathcal{F}^{(3)}=\sum_{n^{\prime},t^{\prime}}\mathcal{W}^{(3)}_{n,t}V_{n,t}^{2}.

(48)

The trap unit potentials are generally of long-range type. However, it is desirable to preclude that trap electrodes far away from a desired well position
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w,t}
are taken into account for the generation of the confining potential. The penalty vector
W^{(3)}_{n,t}
can be conveniently used for this purpose by including an
activation function
: Let

D_{n,w,t}=||\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w,t}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{n}||

(49)

be a suitable measure of distance between a well position with
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{R}}_{n}
being a suitable specification of the location of electrode (set)
n
within the trap coordinate system. Then, a ‘bathtub’ type activation function

\mathcal{W}^{(3)}_{n,t}=\mathcal{W}^{(3)}_{0}\min_{w}\begin{cases}\mathcal{L}\quad&D_{n,w,t}\geq\mathcal{D}_{2}\\
\mathcal{L}\frac{D_{n,w,t}-\mathcal{D}_{1}}{\mathcal{D}_{2}-\mathcal{D}_{1}}\quad&\mathcal{D}_{1}\leq D_{n,w,t}<\mathcal{D}_{2}\\
1\quad&D_{n,w,t}<\mathcal{D}_{1},\end{cases}

(50)

with a very large value
\mathcal{L}\gg 1
and distances
\mathcal{D}_{2}<\mathcal{D}_{1}
gradually activates segment
n
if at least one well is to be positioned in the vicinity of segment
n
.

III.1.4
Voltage difference penalty

The moduli of the voltage samples are supposed to be as small as possible to comply with hardware resources, see Sec.
VII.2
further below. The corresponding penalty does not depend on
u,w
and reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{(4)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\sum_{t=2}^{S}\mathcal{W}^{(4)}\left(V_{n,t}-V_{n,t-1}\right)^{2}.

(51)

As this penalty corresponds to a global hardware-imposed bandwidth limitation, we may take
\mathcal{W}^{(4)}
to be independent of
n,u,t,w
.

III.1.5
Fixed voltage set penalty

For some practically relevant situations, it may be required to employ fixed (calibrated or separately precomputed) voltage configurations. After terminating a shuttling operation, one might typically seek to obtain a confinement situation with precisely calibrated properties for driving sensitive quantum gate operations. Another example is the precise tuning of the critical point of vanishing harmonic confinement for a separate or merge operation
Kaufmann et al. 2014
;
Ruster et al. 2014
. For simplicity, we consider a single such voltage set
\{\hat{V}_{n}\}
. The penalty reads

\mathcal{F}^{(5)}=\sum_{n,t}\mathcal{W}^{(5)}_{n,t}\left(V_{n,t}-\hat{V}_{n}\right)^{2}.

(52)

For enforcing the reference voltage set at time step
\hat{t}
, the penalty weights need to assume large nonzero values only for
\hat{t}
. The penalty term can also straightforwardly be extended to allow for distinct voltage sets applied at different sequence steps.

Note that for
\hat{V}_{n}=0
, this penalty becomes identical to the voltage penalty Eq.
48
, such that by proper choice the weight factors, both penalties could be merged. However, as both penalties serve different purposes, is is beneficial in terms of usability and extensibility to keep them separated in the numerical framework.

III.2
Computing optimal solutions

An optimum voltage ramp is generated by finding a stationary point of the total penalty function:

\sum_{p}\partial_{V_{n,t}}\mathcal{F}^{(p)}\overset{\text{!}}{=}0.

(53)

We bundle all voltage samples into a
NT
-component vector
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}

V_{n,t}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}_{(n,t)}\qquad(n,t)=N(t-1)+n.

(54)

Setting up a
NT\times NT
matrix
A
and a vector
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{b}}
with entries

\displaystyle A_{(n,t),(n^{\prime},t^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p}\partial_{v_{(n^{\prime},t^{\prime})}}\partial_{v_{(n,t)}}\mathcal{F}^{(p)}

\displaystyle b_{(n,t)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{n^{\prime},t^{\prime}}A_{(n,t),(n^{\prime},t^{\prime})}-\sum_{p}\partial_{v_{(n,t)}}\mathcal{F}^{(p)}

(55)

allows for casting Eq.
53
into a linear system:

A\boldsymbol{\mathsf{v}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{b}},

(56)

the solution of which provides optimum voltage sets for the shuttling task. The indexing convention Eq.
54
is useful as it renders
A
to be band-diagonal. The voltage and voltage difference penalties can be used to enforce solutions which are compatible with available hardware resources, but also render
A
to be well-conditioned if the corresponding penalty scaling factors are sufficiently large.
We now introduce shorthand notations such as e.g.

e_{u,n}^{(w,t)}=e_{n,u}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w,t}),

(57)

and similarly for all fields and Hessians, and conclude this section by providing explicit expressions for the entries of
A
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{b}}
:

\displaystyle A_{(n,t),(n^{\prime},t^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Eq.\ref{eq:penalty1}}}{=}

\displaystyle\sum_{u,w}\mathcal{W}^{(1)}_{u,w,t}e_{u,n}^{(w,t)}e_{u,n^{\prime}}^{(w,t)}\overset{\text{Eq. \ref{eq:penalty2}}}{+}\sum_{u,u^{\prime},w}\mathcal{W}^{(2)}_{u,u^{\prime},w,t}h_{u,u^{\prime},n}^{(w,t)}h_{u,u^{\prime},n^{\prime}}^{(w,t)}

(58)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Eq. \ref{eq:penalty3}}}{+}

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(3)}_{n,t}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{tt^{\prime}}\overset{\text{Eq. \ref{eq:penalty4}}}{+}\mathcal{W}^{(4)}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\times\begin{cases}\delta_{t^{\prime},1}-\delta_{t^{\prime},2}\quad\text{for}\quad&t=1\\
\delta_{t^{\prime},S}-\delta_{t^{\prime},S-1}\quad\text{for}\quad&t=S\\
2\delta_{t^{\prime},t}-\delta_{t^{\prime},t-1}-\delta_{t^{\prime},t+1}\quad&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Eq. \ref{eq:penalty5}}}{+}

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}^{(5)}_{n,t}\delta_{nn^{\prime}}\delta_{tt^{\prime}},

\displaystyle b_{(n,t)}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Eq. \ref{eq:penalty1}}}{=}

\displaystyle-\sum_{u,w,t}\mathcal{W}^{(1)}_{u,w,t}e_{u,n}^{(w,t)}E_{\text{rf},u}^{(w,t)}

(59)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{Eq. \ref{eq:penalty2}}}{-}

\displaystyle\sum_{u,u^{\prime},w}\mathcal{W}^{(2)}_{u,u^{\prime}}h_{u,u^{\prime},n}^{(w,t)}\left(H_{\text{rf},u,u^{\prime}}^{(w,t)}-H^{(set,w,t)}_{u,u^{\prime}}\right)\overset{\text{Eq. \ref{eq:penalty5}}}{+}\mathcal{W}^{(5)}_{n,t}\hat{V}_{n}.

Numerical pipeline

In this section, we describe the components of our numerical framework and assess the accuracy of representation of the trap potentials discussed in Sec.
II
.

IV.1
Step-by-step procedure for computing shuttling solutions

We describe how the framework can be employed for the particular use case of computing shuttling solutions for a given trap geometry.

Construct Fibonacci grids is constructed on expansion spheres of radius

IV.2
Numerical accuracy of the multipole expansion

We now assess the accuracy of the parts of the pipeline which are used to compute the expansion coefficients

for representing the trap potentials by comparing computed and measured secular frequencies. The secular frequencies can be determined using laser spectroscopy, with relative accuracy and precision levels in the

range or even below, and therefore provide an excellent benchmark for validation of the numerical methods. We measure the axial and radial secular frequencies of a single
{}^{40}\text{Ca}^{+}
in the linear, uniformly segmented trap employed e.g. in Refs.

The trap drive level

Three parameters determine the accuracy of the computed secular frequencies:

•

The tesselation of the trap geometry mesh used by the electrostatics solver in step

•

The radius

of the expansion sphere used in step

of the multipole expansion may become insufficient as higher order anharmonic terms become dominant. For too small values of

•

The number of grid points

used in step

As

IV.3
Performance

Our framework allows for a wide range of possible applications based on its numerical performance, which becomes particularly relevant for trap geometries of increasing complexity. For the use case of computing shuttling solutions for a given trap geometry, the parametrization of the task in terms of the penalty vectors may have to be adapted until a satisfactory solution is found, which is facilitated by rapid computation of the solutions. More advanced use cases such as parametric trap design studies are only viable if the numerical framework features sufficient performance along the entire pipeline.

VII
Postprocessing: From shuttling solutions to voltage waveforms

The shuttling solutions
V_{n,t}
computed according to Sec.
III
have to be post-processed in order to obtain actual
voltage waveforms
, which can be transferred to a multichannel arbitrary waveform generator (AWG)
Kaushal et al. 2020
. The AWG, downstream electric circuitry as well as the trapped ions themselves are characterized by hardware parameters, i.e. sampling rate, bandwidth and inertia, which entails the need for matching the shuttling solutions to the hardware via postprocessing. The following discussion relies on the fact that all quantities defined with respect to sequence step
t
, such as the well positions
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w,t}
, the expansion coefficients
c_{i}
and the voltage samples
V_{n,t}
can be turned into continuous functions via spline interpolations. Conversely, from an interpolation, a discrete sequence of values can be obtained.

VII.1
Mapping

Having continuous shuttling solutions
V_{n}(\tau),\tau=0\ldots 1
with
V_{n}(0)=V_{n,1},V_{n}(1)=V_{n,T}
available from interpolation of a discrete shuttling solution, the next step is to determine a transfer function
f\colon(0,1)\to(0,1)
with
f(0)=0,f(1)=1
such that mapped continuous shuttling solutions are computed from

\tilde{V}_{n}(\tau)=V_{n}\left(f(\tau)\right).

(61)

Various methods are available for design of such transfer functions, such such as invariant-based inverse engineering
Torrontegui et al. 2011
;
Palmero et al. 2015
, where the final motional excitation is suppressed by harnessing the availability of closed-form solutions of the dragged parametric harmonic oscillator
Lewis and Riesenfeld 1969
. Optimal control methods such as Krotov, GRAPE or CRAB approaches
Koch et al. 2022
may also be employed, where optimal solutions are computed based on numerical solution of the classical
Schulz et al. 2006
or quantum dynamics
Singer et al. 2010
. These methods are beyond the scope of this work, we refer to
Fürst et al. 2014
for a comparative overview.

For shuttling operations in the adiabatic regime, it is generally sufficient to choose a simple sigmoid-type transfer function providing gradual acceleration and deceleration, such as e.g.

f(\tau)=\sin^{2}\left(\frac{\pi\tau}{2}\right).

(62)

After mapping, discrete waveforms are computed via resampling:

V_{n,t}=\tilde{V}_{n}\left(\left(t-\tfrac{1}{2}\right)\Delta\tau\right)\quad t=1\ldots T,\;\Delta\tau=\frac{1}{T}.

(63)

Mind that we now use
V_{n,t}
to denote the mapped, resampled voltages, and the step number
T
may differ to the number of steps used for computing the original shuttling solution.

VII.2
Filter inversion

The voltage waveforms are supplied to the trap electrodes by an AWG, which will typically have output noise levels which would lead to non-tolerable heating of stored ions. Moreover, upon executing shuttling ramps, switching the output voltage levels leads to sharp spikes - termed glitches - which may also lead to large motional excitation. Passive or active filters in the signal lines are therefore required, see
Kaushal et al. 2020
for details. However, such filters will lead to delay and distortion of the signals, which degrades the control over the confinement properties. In particular, substantial buffer times may have to be added after shuttling operations in order to include the filter-induced ring-off behavior, which can have a detrimental impact on the overall system performance. While the voltage difference penalty Eq.
51
used in the computation of the shuttling solution already reduces the required bandwidth, it remains beneficial to compute
pre-ramps
from a mapped shuttling solution and the known properties of the filters, such that the output signal of the filter coincides as good as possible with the desired signal.

For the following discussion, we assume that nonlinearities of the AWG output stages remain negligible, and the effective linear filter is given by the output characteristics of the AWG and additional downstream circuitry. The filter is described by a discrete, finite convolution kernel and is therefore a finite impulse response (FIR) filter. Considering an input waveform
\{V_{i}\}
(the electrode index is omitted) consisting of
T
samples and kernel
\{k_{j}\}
of length
K
, the output waveform
\{s^{\prime}_{j}\}
is given a convolution of the input waveform with the FIR kernel:

V^{\prime}_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{K}k_{j}V_{i-j+1}\qquad i=1\ldots T.

(64)

It has to hold that
\sum_{j=1}^{K}k_{j}=1
, such that the convolution of a constant waveform leaves the output level unaffected. Our aim is now to cast Eq.
64
into the form of a matrix-vector product. We have to take into account possible ring-off behavior at the trailing edge, beyond
i=T
, and also additional samples before
i=1
might be required for producing steep leading edges. We cast the input waveform into vector format and add some ’scratch space’ by padding with
S
copies of its first sample at the leading edge and
S
copies of the last sample at the trailing edge:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}=V_{1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{1}}_{S}\;;\;(V_{1},\ldots,V_{T})\;;\;V_{T}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{1}}_{S}

(65)

where
{}^{\prime};^{\prime}
denotes stacking of vectors, and
V_{i}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{1}}_{S}
denotes a vector consisting of
S
times element
V_{i}
. The new waveform length is
M=T+2S
, and we assume that
S
is chosen large enough such that
M>K
. The padding is justified for practical purposes, assuming the signal source remains idle sufficiently long before and after the waveform. This being fulfilled, we can safely describe the first
K
samples of the filtered waveform by a set of kernel vectors
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}^{(m)}\;(m=K\ldots 1)
which have a range of elements cumulated at the left (past kausal) end:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}^{(m)}=\left(\sum_{j=K-m+1}^{K}k_{j}\;,\;k_{K-m+1},\;\ldots\;,k_{1}\right).

(66)

The length of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}^{(m)}
is
K-m+1
. This allows for writing the convolution Eq.
64
in matrix format:

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}^{\prime}=\mathcal{K}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}

(67)

with the
M\times M
kernel matrix
\mathcal{K}
, specified in terms of its row vectors:

\mathcal{K}=\begin{pmatrix}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}^{(K)}\;;\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}_{M-1}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}^{(K-1)}\;;\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}_{M-2}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}^{(2)}\;;\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}_{M-K+1}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}^{(1)}\;;\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}_{M-K}\\
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}_{1}\;;\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}^{(1)}\;;\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}_{M-K-1}\\
\vdots\\
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}_{M-K}\;;\;\boldsymbol{\mathsf{k}}^{(1)}\end{pmatrix}

(68)

Fig. 11:
Filter inversion for a
\sin^{2}
-type sigmoid waveform: The main panel shows a desired

sample waveform (solid green) of

type including padding with

w=0.1
was used.

The inversion task now lies in finding an input ramp
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}}
such that a specified ramp
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}
is reproduced at the filter output, i.e.
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}=\mathcal{K}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}}
. Attempting to solve directly by inversion of
\mathcal{K}
, i.e.
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}}=\mathcal{K}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}
yields unsatisfactory results due to the fact that realistic low-pass filters lead to large condition numbers of
\mathcal{K}
, such that the inversion is not numerically stable and the solutions contain extreme voltage levels and bandwidth. We therefore need to include a suitable regularization. One approach is to enforce solutions minimizing the mean squared differences between consecutive voltage samples. The corresponding function we seek to minimize is therefore

\mathcal{F}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(V_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{K}_{ij}\tilde{V}_{j}\right)^{2}+w\sum_{i=2}^{M}(\tilde{V}_{i}-\tilde{V}_{i-1})^{2}

(69)

where
w>0
is a hyperparameter weighing the difference penalty. We compute the derivative of
\mathcal{F}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{V}}})
with respect to sample
\tilde{V}_{p}
:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathcal{F}}{d\tilde{V}_{p}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\sum_{i=1}^{M}\mathcal{K}_{ip}\left(V_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{M}\mathcal{K}_{ij}\tilde{V}_{j}\right)

(70)

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle 2w(1-\delta_{p,1})(\tilde{V}_{p}-\tilde{V}_{p-1})

\displaystyle-

\displaystyle 2w(1-\delta_{p,M})(\tilde{V}_{p+1}-\tilde{V}_{p}).

A stationary point of
\mathcal{F}
is given if all derivatives vanish:

\frac{d\mathcal{F}}{d\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{0}}.

(71)

Defining

W_{ij}=\begin{cases}\delta_{i,j}-\delta_{i,j-1}&i=1\\
\delta_{i,j}-\delta_{i,j+1}&i=M\\
2\delta_{i,j}-\delta_{i,j-1}-\delta_{i,j+1}&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(72)

Eq.
71
can be stated as

\left(\mathcal{K}^{T}\mathcal{K}-\lambda W\right)\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}}=\mathcal{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}.

(73)

from which we obtain the solution

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}}=\left(\mathcal{K}^{T}\mathcal{K}-W\right)^{-1}\mathcal{K}^{T}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{V}}.

(74)

The kernel itself can be directly determined by recording the response to a unit voltage step on an oscilloscope, measuring at the output of a filter channel. This requires that no downstream passive components, such as additional capacitors near the trap, significantly affect the signals at the trap electrodes. The timing resolution is limited by the oscilloscope, therefore the resampling step of the mapped shuttling solution Eq.
63
should match the resolution at which the step response is recorded. The kernel elements are then obtained by taking the differences of the recorded step response samples.

Results from the filter inversion method are shown in Fig.

. A toy model kernel causing delay and distortion consisting of
K=70
elements is applied to a

voltage waveform with
T=50
samples. The chosen regularization weight
w=0.1
leads a smooth resulting pre-ramp as computed from Eq.
74
, and relative deviations between desired and filtered pre-ramp
\mathcal{K}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{V}}}
remaining below the
10^{-3}
level. Moreover, most of the used scratch space (
S=25
) can be safely discarded a-posteriori, as the relative sample levels also remain below
10^{-3}
. The final postprocessing step consists of resampling the pre-ramp to the timing resolution of the AWG.

The capabilities of the filter inversion method are ultimately limited by the slew-rate of the AWG output stages, i.e. the maximum voltage difference between consecutive samples
Kaushal et al. 2020
. Pre-ramps containing sample differences approaching the slew-rate limitation will lead to nonlinear signal distortions, such that the description in terms of a linear FIR filter breaks down.

VII.3
Mechanical simulation

As a complementary tool, mechanical simulation of ion trajectories may be used for different purposes, e.g. to validate shuttling waveforms, to compute figure-of-merit metrics for closed-loop optimization of trap geometries or shuttling paths, or for identifying suitable total shuttling durations used in the mapping step for adiabatic shuttling. It is important to state that the scale separation between the ground-state wavefunction sizes for trapped ion (few nm to few tens of nm) and the size of control electrodes of (few tens to few hundreds of
\mu
m) renders the electrostatic and ponderomotive potentials to be almost perfectly harmonic across the extent of wavefunction. Therefore, for most scenarios, simulation of the classical point-mass dynamics is sufficient, even if ground state wavefunction and sub-quantum excitation energies are considered, see
Fürst et al. 2014
.

Within our framework, ion trajectories based on the multipole expansions of the trap potential along a shuttling path and a voltage waveform are computed using velocity Verlet integration
Swope et al. 1982
. The required prerequisites are as follows:

•

Interpolations of all dc potential expansion coefficients up to order
l=2
and rf expansion coefficients up to order
l=3
. Once the problem enforces motion only along the shuttling path,
l=1
is for the dc potentials and
l=2
for the rf potentials is sufficient.

•

Interpolation of all voltage waveforms, namely the filtered pre-ramps

\mathcal{V}_{n}(\tau)=\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}\left[\mathcal{K}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\tilde{V}}}_{n}\right](\tau)

(75)

where
\mathcal{I}_{\mathcal{T}}\left[\cdot\right]
denotes interpolation of a sample waveform scaled to final time
\mathcal{T}
.

The velocity Verlet algorithm requires computing the acceleration
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{a}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}},t)
acting on an ion of mass
m
and charge
Q
at any position
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}
in the trap coordinate system, taken at any time
\tau=0\ldots\mathcal{T}
. For the general case where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}
can lie outside of a shuttling path, we first compute the shortest vector
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{d}
connecting to a point
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{p}
within the path, such that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{p}+\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{d}
. The acceleration is then computed from evaluating the electrostatic (Eq.
18
) and effective ponderomotive (Eq.
23
) force fields as well as the ponderomotive (Eq.
24
) and electrostatic (Eq.
19
) Hessians at
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{p}
:

\frac{m}{Q}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{a}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}},t)=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{rf}}-H_{\text{rf}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{d}+\sum_{n}\mathcal{V}_{n}(t)\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{n}-h_{n}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{d}\right).

(76)

For multi-ion scenarios, the Coulomb repulsions ought to be taken into account as well. We note that the force in Eq.
76
acting tangential to a shuttling path includes anharmonicities of the trap potential up to arbitrary order, limited by the accuracy of the interpolations. Perpendicular to the shuttling path, the electrostatic and ponderomotive forces are included only to first order. In cases where it remains unsure whether the pseudopotential approximation is valid, it is possible to analyze the validity via mechanical simulations resolving the trap drive. This requires replacing the acceleration due to the rf field by

\frac{m}{Q}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{a}}_{\text{rf}}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}},t)=V_{\text{rf}}\left(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}-h_{\text{rf}}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{d}\right)\;\cos\left(\Omega t+\phi_{\text{rf}}\right).

(77)

Simulation results such as final excitation energies significantly depending on the rf phase
\phi_{rf}
indicate that the pseudopotential approximation may not be legitimate.

VIII
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work we have presented a numerical framework for the efficient and accurate computation of multipole expansions of electric potentials in segmented ion traps or electrode geometries serving other purposes. Combined electrostatic and ponderomotive forces as well as secular trap frequencies can be computed from the multipole coefficients. Based on this, we have shown how shuttling solutions and post-process voltage waveforms can be computed using an integrated pipeline of hardware-informed, efficient and versatile methods. The method for computing shuttling solutions was illustrated using the simple scenario of moving a single potential well along the axis of a linear segmented trap, and a more complex task consisting of moving a potential well around the corner of an X-type junction in a stacked-wafer trap.

The framework can be adapted and extended to address a variety of tasks in the context of shuttling operations. The numerical efficiency of the framework allows for parametric trap design studies, where trap geometry parameters can be systematically optimized. It is even possible to perform automated closed-loop design optimization by adapting electrode geometry parameters according to the quality of shuttling solutions for a given shuttling task. \displaystyle z_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2k}{K-1}\qquad r_{k}=\sqrt{1-z_{k}^{2}}\quad\phi_{k}=k\pi(3-\sqrt{5})

\displaystyle x_{k}

Appendix B
Validity limits for nonzero micromotion

Our framework for computing shuttling solutions is based on simple superposition of electrostatic and ponderomotive forces, which relies is valid for trap operation parameters sufficiently deep in the stability regime of the trap, i.e. for all secular frequencies ranging way below the trap frequency. This is however only valid for potential well located at the rf null of the trap. The well-positioning penalty
\mathcal{F}^{(1)}
Eq.
40
only requires the total effective force to vanish at a well location, which can lead to well positions with nonzero (actual, not effective) rf field
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{rf}}
. Then, a number of further sufficient validity criteria eventually need to be considered:

•

The excess micromotion must be small compared length scales on which the ponderomotive and electrostatic forces change. For a potential well with nonzero rf field amplitude
|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{rf}}|
, the micromotion amplitude is given by

r_{\mu}=\frac{Q}{m\Omega^{2}}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{rf}}|=\frac{QV_{\text{rf}}}{m\Omega^{2}}|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}|

(79)

and should fall below the characteristic length scales on which the fields vary:

\displaystyle r_{\mu}\ll\frac{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{dc}}|}{\left|\nabla|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{dc}}|\right|}

(80)

\displaystyle r_{\mu}\ll\frac{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{rf}}|}{\left|\nabla|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{rf}}|\right|}

(81)

•

The net effect of the electrostatic forces over a micromotion cycle should remain negligible, which translates to

\frac{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{dc}}|}{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{rf}}|}\ll 1

(82)

•

For a shuttling process with dynamic potential well positions
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w}(\tau)
, the micromotion envelope seen by each well should be slowly varying:

\left|\frac{d}{d\tau}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w}(\tau)\right|\ll\omega\;r_{\mu}

(83)

with
\omega
being the minimum secular frequency.

All criteria can be evaluated by computing fields, field gradients and secular frequencies based on the multipole expansions around the static well positions
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w,t}
or dynamic well positions
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{r}}_{w}(\tau)
as discussed in Sec.
II.3
. For Eq.
80
, we note that

\left|\nabla|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{dc}}|\right|=\frac{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{dc}}H_{\text{dc}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{dc}}}}{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{E}}_{\text{dc}}|}

(84)

which can be evaluated using Eqs.
20
,
32
and
34
. For condition Eq.
81
, one may use

\left|\nabla|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\mathcal{E}}}_{\text{rf}}|\right|=V_{\text{rf}}\frac{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}h_{\text{rf}}^{2}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}}}{|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{e}}_{\text{rf}}|}

(85)

also in conjunction with Eqs.
32
and
34
.

Note that the validity criteria listed here are sufficient conditions, and a shuttling solution partially violating the requirements may still work out in practice.

It is also important to note that an ion located a position where the ponderomotive force exhibits a gradient, it experiences additional heating due to electric field noise at the drive frequency offset by the secular frequency along direction
u

Blakestad et al. 2009
;
Blakestad et al. 2011
:

\dot{\bar{n}}_{u}=\frac{Q^{2}}{4m\hbar\omega_{u}}\left(\partial_{u}\Phi_{\text{rf}}\right)^{2}\frac{S_{V_{\text{rf}}}(\Omega+\omega_{u})}{V_{\text{rf}}^{2}},

(86)

where
\bar{n}_{u}
is the mean phonon number for the base secular mode along direction
u
, and
S_{V_{\text{rf}}}
is the voltage noise spectral density of the trap drive voltage.
