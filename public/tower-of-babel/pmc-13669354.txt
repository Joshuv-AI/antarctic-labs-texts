From compliance to competence: a multi-level model for AI governance in undergraduate medical education
Authors: Juan José Gualda-Gea, Lourdes Estefanía Barón-Miras, Maria Jesús Bertran, Alberto L. Garcia-Basteiro, Guillermo Mena, Marta Aldea, Gemina Santana, Jaume Grau, Andrés Prat Marin, Isabel Torá-Rocamora, Antoni Trilla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AI's fast-paced irruption in medicine and education has challenged undergraduate medical colleges, with institutions documenting policies emphasizing immediate conduct restrictions, while strategic oversight, infrastructure and evaluation remain less consistently developed. We propose a multi-level framework for AI governance in undergraduate medical education based on accountability, competence and co-responsibility across institutions, educators and students, designed to turn a fast-evolving technology from a threat into an opportunity for collective progress.

From reaction to proaction in AI governance

Artificial intelligence (AI) has already entered classrooms, clinical settings and examination halls1. Generative tools are now capable of drafting patient cases, simulating diagnostic reasoning and providing personalised training2. Yet the governance of these technologies in the university remains largely reactive, designed to prevent misconduct rather than to build capacity3. Recent evidence from US medical schools and Swedish higher education institutions suggests that institutional responses remain limited and uneven, often focusing on academic integrity while strategic oversight, infrastructure and implementation receive less attention4,5.

Medicine has been impacted by disruptive technologies before, and its educational reaction has frequently been uneven. History offers several examples of tools that reshaped not only what physicians could do, but which skills they retained. The internet and, later, electronic health records broadened the availability, access and quality of health information. Yet, digitalization has made us dependable of computers, networks and electricity. Documentation is a leading driver of healthcare workers' burnout, being a cautionary example of technology imposed on clinicians rather than designed along with them6,7. Besides, digitalization has been unevenly implemented across territories and across countries, widening even more health inequity gaps8. Another example can be medical imaging, which revolutionised diagnosis, yet overreliance on it has been repeatedly linked to the loss of physical examination and bedside skills9. Imaging is increasingly ordered before, or instead of, clinical reasoning. How many times has a scan been requested “to see what it shows” instead of testing a preconceived hypothesis? This is precisely the pattern that AI threatens to boost, and precisely, the reason a governance framework must differentiate between competencies that could safely benefit from AI assistance and those that must remain AI-independent10,11.

The challenge extends beyond academic integrity. As AI becomes embedded in healthcare, physicians must not only know how to use it but also when and whether to trust it, navigating the risks of automation bias and algorithmic opacity12. Without structured governance, universities risk producing doctors used to using these tools but unable either to harness their full potential or to recognize when they are being misled by them13. Governance should therefore evolve from reactive control mechanisms into a proactive process of education, reflection, and shared responsibility, aligned with global ethical principles and regulatory requirements14.

The missing layer of governance

Existing ethical and regulatory frameworks define safeguards such as privacy, fairness, safety and human oversight14,15 while professional and educational competency frameworks specify what clinicians, educators and learners should know and be able to do12.

Within universities, governance remains fragmented and inconsistent. Evidence from U.S. medical schools suggests that few institutions maintain dedicated oversight structures for educational AI, while regular audits of fairness, reliability, and transparency remain uncommon4,16. Where directives exist, they often seek to minimize risk rather than cultivate capability. This reactive approach leaves educators and students in a moral “grey zone”, uncertain where legitimate learning assistance ends, and academic dishonesty begins3,17,18.

Anchoring governance through accountability, competence and co-responsibility

Hereby, we propose a framework structured around three interconnected core implementation principles:

Accountability

Trust but verify. Every AI tool integrated into medical education must face systematic scrutiny. Does it achieve its stated educational objectives? Does it introduce hidden harms, such as algorithmic bias, skill atrophy, or false confidence in flawed outputs? Accountability answers through regular audits, transparent performance metrics, and honest reckoning when tools fail. Operationally, this requires a body that assesses the proposed tools and the implementation of AI tool proposals, similar to classical biomedical Health Technology Assessment Committees. On the other hand, project leaders should define the intended use, review evidence, identify advantages, relevance, and benefits and, if accepted, monitor outcomes. Potential ethical risks must be listed, and all parts of the project must commit to preserve ethical integrity.

Competence

AI must empower, not diminish. Critical clinical judgement remains the irreducible foundation of medical education. Algorithmic tools should handle routine tasks to create space for higher-order clinical reasoning, strengthening rather than replacing essential skills. Judgement should be sharpened rather than autonomy eroded. Competence should therefore be demonstrated through observable performance of a defined task under stated conditions and against predefined criteria, rather than inferred solely from course attendance, learner confidence, or the quality of an AI-assisted product.

Co-responsibility

A pact, not a prosecution. AI governance in medical education requires shared commitment rather than top-down control. Universities provide infrastructure and guidance; educators model judicious integration; students engage tools with informed intentionality. Success emerges from collective ownership. Responsibilities must be specified along the different phases of implementation and follow-up, for both staff and users: approval, supervision, disclosure, verification and escalation. The institution retains final responsibility for the systems and the assessment conditions it authorises.

Operational tool implementation algorithm

To operationalise the conceptual framework for specific AI uses, we propose a risk-proportionate decision algorithm embedded within broader technical and non-technical governance mechanisms (Fig. 2). The proponent first defines the intended use, after which the institutional AI governance body assigns a risk tier and specifies the minimum evidence and safeguards required. A proposed use level (sandbox-only, supervised/restricted, or routine use) is then defined together with its scope, required human oversight, educational outcomes, stop criteria, and accountable decision owner. Before proceeding, the proposed use must satisfy a non-negotiable floor encompassing legality, privacy, safety, non-discrimination, academic integrity, and meaningful human review. If this floor is not met, the use should be modified and reassessed where feasible or discarded when remediation is not possible. Uses that meet the floor proceed to a controlled pilot with predefined scope, duration, monitoring, and stop criteria. Pilot outcomes and safeguards are subsequently reviewed; unsuccessful uses may again be modified and reassessed or discarded. Successful uses proceed to implementation, documentation, monitoring, and periodic reassessment under a revocable institutional AI-entrustment decision.Fig. 2Risk-proportionate decision pathway for AI governance in undergraduate medical education.A proposed AI use progresses from definition of its intended purpose through risk classification, specification of minimum evidence and safeguards, and definition of a proposed use level and its conditions. The pathway also makes trade-offs explicit. Where potential educational benefit conflicts with potential harm, responses may include data-minimised deployment, modification of the intended use, an approved alternative, or non-use. Where AI-supported learning or assessment threatens learner independence, curricula should identify which capabilities must remain independently demonstrable and assess AI-free performance separately from the ability to collaborate safely with AI. Rethinking evaluation methods

Parallel to competency review, evaluation methodologies must be fundamentally reconsidered. Each competency requires individual scrutiny. There will be no philosopher's stone, no unique answer. Yet one essential question must guide the process: Must future physicians perform this skill independently of AI assistance? This simple question carries profound implications that demand honest confrontation.

Consider a straightforward example: cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). Few would contest that physicians must perform CPR without algorithmic support. Nonetheless, this clarity dissolves when applied across the broader spectrum of clinical skills. The CPR example nevertheless illuminates a critical principle: if competency requires AI-independent performance, evaluation cannot occur in AI-accessible contexts.

This constraint introduces significant pedagogical challenges. AI-free evaluation environments will become increasingly scarce as these technologies become embedded not only in conventional digital devices but also in wearable and environmental technologies, including smart glasses. Consequently, traditional assessment methodologies risk obsolescence. Authentic evaluation will also require clear expectations, professional integrity, direct observation, and assessment designs that make students’ reasoning processes visible.

For competencies where AI assistance is appropriate, assessment criteria must be reconceived. The evaluative question transforms: not “Can the student solve this problem?” but “Can the student effectively collaborate with AI while maintaining clinical judgement?” These demands assessing prompt engineering, output validation, algorithmic limitation recognition, and the synthesis of human expertise with computational support.

At the individual level

Professional responsibility for AI literacy

AI literacy is not only an educational opportunity but also an emerging professional duty for students and educators. Safe use requires sufficient understanding of a tool’s indications, limitations, potential harms, and appropriate conditions of use13. Students should progress from passive users to accountable professionals who critically appraise AI outputs, recognize inappropriate use, and retain responsibility for decisions informed by them. Educators should model transparent practice, acknowledge uncertainty, and maintain human oversight18.

Engagement and transparency in AI-supported learning

The educational value of generative AI depends not only on output quality but also on how openly and actively students engage with it. Students should understand its purpose, disclose its contribution when appropriate, and remain intellectually engaged in the learning process18. Transparent use allows educators to distinguish legitimate assistance from substitution of effort and enables meaningful feedback on how students formulate questions, evaluate outputs, and integrate information. AI should therefore support active learning rather than hindering how knowledge and conclusions were produced28.

Empowerment, initiative, and creativity

Students' capacity to learn, reason, and create independently should be protected. Students must be empowered to decide when AI adds value or weakens learning, rather than following the path of least cognitive resistance29. The objective is to master AI without becoming mastered by it: using the technology to extend curiosity, initiative, and creative exploration while retaining the confidence and competence to work without its assistance. Educational practice should engage original questioning, independent problem-solving, and thoughtful experimentation. This requires particular attention to the risks of cognitive offloading, dependency, and algorithmic overconfidence, as well as to AI’s still-uncertain effects on long-term memory and foundational clinical reasoning30,31.

Policy and educational implications

Policies supported by this framework include institutionally licensed AI environments, controlled pilots, and separate AI-free and AI-supported assessments. These approaches can improve equity, data protection, controlled innovation and preservation of independent competence, but require funding, infrastructure, monitoring and assessment redesign. Institutions should adapt them to local capacity without weakening non-negotiable safeguards.

Infrastructure and cybersecurity

AI implementation can expose educational and health information systems to privacy, security, and integrity risks affecting students, patients, research participants, and institutional partners14. Institutions should use approved environments with access controls, data minimization, logging, and security review, and keep sensitive data out of unapproved external models. Secure computational sandboxes can isolate experimentation from operational systems and confidential data.

Interdisciplinary collaboration

Responsible integration requires collaboration among medical educators, clinicians, computer scientists, cybersecurity and data-protection specialists, ethicists, and learners3,32. These partnerships should guide tool selection, evaluation, and co-design to ensure technical robustness, pedagogical value, and clinical relevance.

Challenges to implementation

Comprehensive governance requires more than institutional enthusiasm. Weak implementation structures, unstable funding, and unequal access may widen disparities between institutions16. Proprietary systems can restrict transparency and independent scrutiny3,14. Governance must therefore remain iterative, with policies, risk assessments, and oversight mechanisms reviewed as technologies and evidence evolve. The framework’s feasibility is bounded by resource constraints, and these vary sharply between institutions. Evidence from low-resource settings is clear: binding barriers are rarely algorithmic. They are infrastructural8. Unreliable electricity, intermittent connectivity, and ageing hardware routinely defeat tools that perform well elsewhere. Data pose a second barrier. Where records remain paper-based or fragmented, the structured digital substrate that fuels AI simply does not exist. Namely, a new perfect AI programme that can predict Intensive Care Unit health outcomes cannot be reasonably implemented in a setting where vital signs are registered on paper. Workforce and financing complete the picture: overburdened staff that cannot validate these tools, and reliance on proprietary systems and short-term funding leaves pilots to lapse once external support ends. Crucially, these constraints fall unevenly, widening the gap between well and under-resourced colleges8,33.

Governance must recognize and adapt to this reality. Institutions must engage stakeholders in the process and perform a sincere mapping of needs. Tackling these barriers requires a two-front strategy: secure or better the foundations if possible (e.g. Power, connectivity, digital records…); and creative solutions that engage the community (e.g. Scheduling compute-heavy tasks, as model runs and downloads; during stable supply windows, Offline AI tools with local data catching; preferring open-source tool and sustainable and long-term public-private partnerships; a “train the trainer” philosophy that cascades AI literacy, that invites both educators and students to learn and validate tools; consortia with local technical colleges that could benefit and supply technical skills and maintenance; and progressive migration to interoperable digitalization of data, in non-proprietary information systems).

Conclusions

AI governance in undergraduate medical education must urgently move beyond reactive compliance towards proactive capacity building. This requires coordinated action across institutional, curricular, and individual levels, with clear accountability, shared responsibility, and safeguards proportionate to educational risk. Governance should preserve competencies that require independent performance while enabling transparent and critically supervised AI-supported learning and academic monitoring. Unequal access, lack of protected training time, funding and the need for a multidisciplinary approach are challenges that must be strategically addressed. As a conceptual and untested framework, its feasibility, acceptability and effects on learning, equity and safety require empirical evaluation across institutions with differing resources and digital maturity. Its ultimate purpose is to ensure that AI strengthens clinical judgement, learner agency, and patient-centred care.
