Floral origin influences physicochemical, volatiles, and taste profiles of Apis mellifera honey in Northern Thailand
Authors: Gerry Renaldi, Arriya Tadtan, Napatsawan Chaimahawan, Natthanicha Banpacha, Hiran Janpeng, Terd Disayathanoowat, Wanalee Sangpimpa, Rajnibhas Sukeaw Samakradhamrongthai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Honey is a complex natural product whose composition and quality are influenced by floral origin and geographical location. This study aimed to characterize the properties of Apis mellifera honeys from different botanical origins in Northern Thailand. Fourteen honey samples, including mimosa (SH), lychee (CH), multifloral (FH), and longan (LH), were analyzed for physicochemical properties. Phytochemical profiling was conducted using Ultra-High-Performance Liquid Chromatography–Quadrupole Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC-QTOF/MS), while volatile and taste profiles were evaluated using electronic nose (E-nose) and electronic tongue (E-tongue) systems. All samples met Codex Alimentarius quality standards, with moisture below 20%, acidity below 50 meq/kg, and hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) below 80 mg/kg. Longan honey exhibited relatively higher total phenolic content (TPC) (51.32 mg GAE/100 g), indicating a sample-specific observation. Phytochemical profiling tentatively identified 55 compounds, predominantly saccharides (34.85%) and organic acids (30.30%). A total of 146 VOCs were identified, mainly esters (22.22%), alcohols (15.69%), and aldehydes (14.38%). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed partial separation of honey samples according to botanical origin; however, no distinct clustering was observed, suggesting overlapping compositional characteristics. These findings indicate that variability in Northern Thai honeys is influenced by both floral origin and post-harvest factors, providing insights for quality assessment and authentication.

Introduction

Honey is legally defined as a natural sweet substance produced by Apis mellifera bees from floral nectar or plant secretions, which are transformed through enzymatic activity, dehydration, and maturation within honeycombs, as described in Council Directive 2001/110/EC1,2. Beyond its traditional role as a sweetener, honey has gained increasing attention due to its compositional complexity, nutritional value, sensory properties, and functional applications in food, cosmetic, and health-related products3.

Honey is primarily composed of sugars but also contains minor constituents such as amino acids, proteins, organic acids, and phenolic compounds, many of which contribute to its biological activities, including antioxidant and antimicrobial effects. These non-volatile components are commonly characterized using chromatographic techniques, providing detailed insights into honey composition. In addition, volatile organic compounds (VOCs), although present at trace levels, play a crucial role in defining honey aroma and taste4. The composition and relative abundance of VOCs are strongly influenced by floral origin, enzymatic activity, microbial interactions, and post-harvest conditions5. Due to this variability, VOC profiling has been widely applied for honey authentication and classification according to botanical and geographical origin6–8. Complementary to these approaches, sensor-based technologies such as electronic nose (e-nose) and electronic tongue (e-tongue) systems have been increasingly used to evaluate aroma and taste profiles by capturing overall sensory patterns. These systems enable rapid and non-destructive analysis; however, their performance may be limited by sensor sensitivity, selectivity, and dependence on calibration models, which can affect classification accuracy4,6.

Based on these compositional and sensory attributes, honey is commonly classified according to its botanical origin as monofloral or multifloral. Monofloral honey is predominantly derived from a single floral source and is characterized by distinctive sensory properties, whereas multifloral honey originates from multiple nectar sources and exhibits more complex but less specific profiles2,7. In commercial markets, blended honey is also widely available, reflecting the variability in physicochemical and sensory characteristics among different sources.

Given that honey composition is largely governed by botanical origin, detailed characterization is essential for ensuring authenticity and quality2,9. Northern Thailand, with its rich botanical diversity and varied agro-ecological conditions, provides a unique environment that is expected to influence honey composition and sensory properties. However, studies integrating physicochemical properties, VOC profiles, and sensory characteristics of honeys from this region remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to characterize the physicochemical properties, volatile compound profiles, and taste characteristics of Apis mellifera honeys from different botanical origins in Northern Thailand.

Results

Physicochemical properties

Correlation analysis

The correlation heatmap of physicochemical properties is presented in Fig. 1. Strong positive correlations were observed among total phenolic content (TPC), color, and free acidity. Honey color showed significant positive correlations with TPC (r = 0.69–0.87). An inverse relationship between pH and free acidity was also observed, where lower pH values corresponded to higher acidity. A weak positive correlation was detected between pH and moisture content (MC, r = 0.23). Phytochemical profile of honey sample by UHPLC-QTOF/MS

In the present study, a total of 55 compounds (Table 2) were tentatively identified across all samples, including 23 saccharides (41.82%), 20 organic acids (36.36%), 8 amino acids (14.55%), 3 phenolic compounds (5.45%), and 1 furan derivative (1.82%). Discussion

The moisture content of the honey samples remained below the Codex Alimentarius maximum limit of 20 g/100 g10, indicating acceptable quality and reduced susceptibility to fermentation. Variations in moisture content are influenced by botanical origin, harvesting period, environmental conditions, processing, and storage practices, and this parameter plays a critical role in determining honey’s viscosity, crystallization behavior, and overall stability11–13. Lower moisture levels are generally associated with improved shelf life, reduced microbial spoilage, and enhanced resistance to fermentation and granulation12. In addition to moisture, color is a key quality attribute that is primarily determined by floral origin and strongly influences consumer preference7. The honey samples in this study ranged from water white to light amber12,14, consistent with previous reports showing that floral source and geographical origin significantly affect honey color3,12. While lighter honeys are often preferred in global markets, darker honeys may be valued for their distinct sensory and bioactive properties15,16. The pH values observed in this study reflect the naturally acidic nature of honey, typically ranging from 3.5 to 5.5 due to the presence of organic acids, which contribute to microbial stability and influence texture and shelf life7,17,18. These findings are consistent with previous studies on A. mellifera honey19,20. Similarly, free acidity values were well below the Codex Alimentarius limit of 50 meq/kg10, indicating good freshness and minimal fermentation, and are influenced by various factors, including organic acid composition, harvesting season, and floral source21, that are also comparable to honey from other region, such as Ethiopia and Brazil12,20. The 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) content, an important indicator of honey freshness and thermal degradation, was also below the maximum limit of 80 mg/kg for tropical regions10, confirming minimal heat damage and appropriate storage conditions12,13,22. Taken together, the physicochemical data indicate acceptable honey quality, with observed variations primarily attributable to differences in floral source and environmental influences.

The phenolic content of honey has also been widely reported to vary depending on geographical and botanical origin23,24. Comparable ranges have been documented in monofloral and multifloral honeys from different regions. For instance, TPC values of 13.13–75.54 mg GAE/100 g have been reported across five monofloral honeys, with Bombax ceiba L. honey exhibiting the highest level24. Similarly, monofloral honeys from Sicily showed TPC values ranging from 16.5 to 133.3 mg GAE/100 g25, while honeys from Turkey ranged from 16.02 to 120.04 mg GAE/100 g26. Lower ranges have been observed in eucalyptus and wildflower honeys from Brazil (11.37–54.01 mg GAE/100 g)27, whereas polyfloral Cuban honeys exhibited an average TPC of 54.30 mg GAE/100 g28. These findings are consistent with the TPC values obtained in the present study.

Pearson correlation analysis is commonly used to evaluate the strength and direction of linear relationships among physicochemical parameters in honey29. Strong positive associations between total phenolic content (TPC), color, and acidity have been widely reported, reflecting the contribution of phenolic compounds and related pigments to both color intensity and acid-related characteristics24,30. Darker honeys are typically associated with higher levels of antioxidant compounds, including flavonoids, carotenoids, and Maillard reaction products31. In contrast, pH generally shows an inverse relationship with acidity, as the accumulation of organic acids contributes to increased free acidity and lower pH values19. These acidity-related parameters are influenced by multiple factors, including floral origin, mineral composition, and enzymatic activity32. Moisture content, however, is often weakly associated with other physicochemical properties, as it is primarily governed by external factors such as harvesting practices and environmental humidity rather than the intrinsic chemical composition of nectar29,33. This explains why parameters related to stability, such as fermentation risk, are largely independent of phenolic content, color, or acidity. Similarly, 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) typically shows limited correlation with most compositional parameters, as its formation is mainly driven by heat exposure and storage duration rather than botanical origin13. Consequently, HMF is widely regarded as an indicator of honey freshness and processing conditions rather than inherent compositional variability8.

Honey composition is strongly influenced by the botanical origin of nectar22. Sugars constitute the predominant chemical class, consistent with their role as the primary constituents of honey34. Variations in sugar composition across honey types reflect differences in floral sources, supporting previous observations of botanical-dependent sugar profiles34,35. Fructose is typically the dominant monosaccharide in most honey varieties and contributes to key properties such as sweetness and hygroscopicity34. Organic acids are also important contributors to honey quality, influencing physicochemical stability and sensory characteristics, and are frequently used as indicators of freshness and authenticity36,37. Gluconic acid is widely recognized as the principal organic acid, formed through enzymatic oxidation of glucose during honey maturation38. Other detected acids, such as shikimic acid, are associated with plant metabolic pathways and may contribute to biological activities, including antioxidant and antimicrobial effects39. In addition to sugars and organic acids, honey contains a range of minor constituents, including amino acids, phenolic compounds, and other organic compounds, which vary depending on botanical origin34. Among amino acids, proline is widely recognized as the predominant compound in honey and is often used as an indicator of honey maturity and authenticity40. The furan derivative HMF is also commonly detected in honey and is primarily formed through sugar degradation and Maillard reactions during thermal processing or prolonged storage. However, HMF concentration alone cannot be considered a definitive indicator of heat treatment severity, as its formation is influenced by multiple factors, including sugar composition, organic acid content, pH, moisture content, and floral origin22.

Following the compositional analysis of non-volatile compounds, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) represent another important fraction contributing to honey quality, particularly its aroma profile41. The composition and abundance of VOCs are strongly influenced by botanical origin, bee metabolism, environmental factors, and processing and storage conditions5,42,43. Among these, alcohols are characterized by the presence of a hydroxyl (–OH) group and are often associated with fermentation processes and thermal degradation, with their levels increasing under prolonged storage or elevated temperatures44. Aldehydes are also commonly detected and may arise from lipid oxidation, Maillard reactions, sugar degradation, as well as Strecker degradation and enzymatic transformations occurring in honey45. Terpenes, a diverse class of plant-derived compounds, are also widely distributed in honey and often linked to floral origin41, although compounds such as limonene, while proposed as markers for citrus honey, have also been identified in other floral types including pine, acacia, and chestnut honeys42,46. Carboxylic acids and esters further contribute to honey aroma41, with acids potentially formed through enzymatic transformations and esters generated via esterification reactions between acids and alcohols47–49. In addition, ethers and ketones represent minor yet relevant contributors to the volatile profile; for example, ambroxide is associated with woody and amber-like aromas50, while ketones have been reported to vary considerably in concentration across different honey types48.

Building on the chemical composition of volatile and non-volatile compounds, the sensory characteristics of honey are closely linked to its underlying molecular profile. The taste of honey is largely determined by its botanical origin, which influences both its chemical composition and sensory attributes51. Sourness is an important factor affecting consumer preference and purchasing behavior52 and may be associated with changes in water activity during crystallization. Such changes can facilitate the growth of osmophilic yeasts, including Saccharomyces spp., potentially leading to fermentation and increased acidity53. Bitterness, although less commonly dominant in honey, may arise from specific bioactive compounds. For instance, the presence of eugenol has been associated with bitter taste perception and may contribute to the sensory profile of certain samples. Eugenol is a phenolic compound commonly found in spices such as clove, cinnamon, and nutmeg and is known to impart characteristic bitter and pungent notes54.

To further explore relationships among the measured attributes, PCA was applied to the dataset, as it summarizes variability across samples into principal components29. In this study, physicochemical parameters were first included to represent compositional characteristics related to honey quality and stability. Subsequently, volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were incorporated to account for aroma-related variation. For VOC analysis, the top 40% of compounds were selected based on their relevance to key honey aroma descriptors, including sweet, acidic, pungent, waxy, and plant-based notes55. Finally, taste attributes were considered to capture sensory variation associated with consumer perception. This integrated approach allows for a more comprehensive evaluation of honey samples by combining compositional, aromatic, and sensory dimensions.

The present study demonstrated that Apis mellifera honeys from different floral sources in Northern Thailand exhibited distinct physicochemical, volatiles, and taste characteristics. All samples satisfied international quality standards, indicating good freshness and low risk of fermentation. Significant positive correlations among color, acidity, and total phenolic content suggested that darker honeys tend to contain higher phenolic content. Phytochemical profiling of the honey samples identified 55 distinct metabolites, indicating a complex chemical composition dominated by saccharides and organic acids, along with amino acids and phenolic compounds. Volatile profiling revealed that esters, alcohols, and aldehydes were the predominant classes of aroma-active compounds, contributing to the sensory identity of each honey type. Based on the Pearson correlation heatmap, HMF showed weak correlation with most quality indicators, suggesting a stronger dependence on post-harvest handling than botanical origin. PCA analysis differentiated honeys according to floral source; however, these findings should be interpreted with caution due to the limited sample size. In particular, the observations for longan honey are based on a single sample and should be considered preliminary, especially regarding its relatively higher phenolic content. Further studies incorporating multiple independent longan honey samples are necessary to confirm these findings and establish more reliable compositional markers. Overall, this study provides initial insights into the variability of A. mellifera Northern Thai honeys and highlights the need for expanded sampling to strengthen conclusions related to specific floral origins.

Methods

Raw materials and chemicals

The 14 honey samples from different brands, including 5 mimosa honey (SH), 3 lychee honey (CH), 5 multifloral honey (FH), and 1 longan honey (LH), were commercially obtained from retail markets in Chiang Mai, Thailand. All samples were labeled as 100% pure honey, with their botanical origin reported by the respective brands, and were produced in Chiang Mai. The harvesting periods varied depending on the flowering season of each botanical source. After arrival at the Faculty of Agro-Industry, Chiang Mai University, Thailand, the honey samples were stored in a dark place at room temperature (27 ± 2 °C) and analyzed within 2 months. All chemicals used in this study were of analytical grade.

Physicochemical properties measurement

The moisture content of the honey samples was determined with an indirect refractometric method11 using a digital refractometer at 20 °C. The refractive index of each sample was recorded, and the corresponding moisture percentage was calculated using the Wedmore conversion table, and the measurement was conducted in triplicate.

Honey color was calculated by using Pfund scale and correlating it with the value of optical density obtained after measurement at 560 nm12. The Pfund color grader provides continuous readings across the full honey color range, from light to dark. The optical density of each pure honey sample was measured using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite 200 Pro, Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland), and glycerol was used as the blank.

The pH and free acidity of the honey samples were determined according to AOAC Official Method 962.1956. For pH determination, 10 g of honey was dissolved in 75 mL of distilled water, and the pH was measured using a pH meter (model S220, Mettler Toledo AG, Greifensee, Switzerland). Free acidity was measured by potentiometric titration, in which 0.05 M NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich Co., St. Louis, MO, USA) was added to the honey solution, with ultrapure water prepared using a Milli-Q water purification system (Millipore, Paris, France) serving as the blank. Free acidity was expressed as milliequivalents (meq) of acid per kilogram of honey.

HMF was determined according to the AOAC official method 980.2356. A clarified solution of 0.50 g of honey was prepared using 0.05 mL Carrez I (a solution containing 2.19 g zinc acetate dihydrate (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.30 g glacial acetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved to a final volume of 10 mL with distilled water) and 0.05 mL Carrez II (a solution containing 1.06 g potassium ferrocyanide (Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved to a final volume of 10 mL with distilled water). The absorbance of this solution was measured at 284 nm (Abs284nm) and 336 nm (Abs336nm) using a microplate reader (Tecan Infinite 200 Pro, Tecan Group Ltd., Männedorf, Switzerland). The honey sample was replaced with sodium bisulfite (Sigma Aldrich) and used as a blank. After subtraction of the absorbances of the samples from the blank, the HMF was calculated using Eq. Phytochemical profiling by ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UHPLC-QTOF/MS)

The phytochemical profiling of the honey samples was done using UHPLC-QTOF/MS. The analysis was performed using a Bruker Elute UHPLC (Bruker Daltonics GmbH & Co. KG, Bremen, Germany) equipped with a Bruker Intensity Solo 1.8 C18-2 (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) maintained at 35 °C and connected to a Bruker Impact II QTOF-MS detector. The honey sample for the phytochemical profiling was prepared according to the “dilute and shoot” method57. Two mL of a mixture of acetonitrile and water (1:1 v/v) was added to the sample prior to being vortexed for about 5 min, or until the honey is completely dissolved, then filtered through a 0.45 μm nylon syringe filter. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (Solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in methanol (Solvent B). The gradient program for mobile phase B was as follows: 1%, 1–10 min; 99%, 10–15 min; 1%, 15–20 min. The flow rate was maintained at 0.2 mL/min, with an injection volume of 2 μL per sample. Mass spectrometry was conducted in both positive ([M−H]+) and negative ([M−H]−) electrospray ionization (ESI) modes. The QTOF-MS was operated with the following parameters: capillary voltage of 3.6 kV, nebulizer pressure at 2.2 bar, drying gas flow of 10.0 L/min at 220 °C. Full-scan mass spectra were acquired over a mass range pf m/z 100–1000 at a scan rate of 3 spectra/s.

Untargeted qualitative analysis of phytochemicals in honey samples was performed on LC-QTOF/MS. The phytochemicals were tentatively identified and characterized based on their m/z value in positive and negative ionization modes using Bruker MetaboBASE Personal Library 2.0 and Bruker NIST 2020 MS Spectral Library in Bruker MetaboScape software. Compounds with a MS/MS score more than equal to 800.0 and mass error less than equal to 10 ppm were only selected for characterization and m/z verification purpose.

Volatile organic compounds identification using electronic nose (E-nose)

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) were analyzed using a Heracles II electronic nose system (Alpha M.O.S., Toulouse, France) equipped with a MXT-5 column (10 m × 0.18 mm × 0.40 µm) and flame ionization detectors (FID). Prior to analysis, samples were incubated at 60 °C for 5 min to allow headspace equilibration. A 5 mL aliquot of headspace gas was injected at a flow rate of 125 µL/s, with the injector temperature set at 200 °C. The trap temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the split flow rate was fixed at 10 mL/min. The column oven temperature program began at 50 °C, increased to 80 °C at 1 °C/s, then ramped to 250 °C at 3 °C/s, and held at 250 °C for 21 s. The detector temperature was maintained at 260 °C. All analyses were performed in triplicate. The VOCs were tentatively identified using the AroChemBase sensory analysis software module based on library matching. Compounds with a pertinence index ≥80% were selected and further screened according to their reported sensory descriptors in the literature. Semi-quantitative analysis was performed using peak area normalization, and results were expressed as hexanal equivalents based on a calibration curve constructed with hexanal at concentrations ranging from 0.001 to 100 μL/mL.

Taste profiling using electronic tongue (E-tongue)

Taste characteristics were evaluated using an Astree electronic tongue system (Alpha M.O.S., Toulouse, France) fitted with a #6 sensor array comprising seven sensors. Sensors AHS, CTS, NMS, ANS, and SCS are associated with human taste perceptions of sourness, saltiness, umami, sweetness, and bitterness, respectively, whereas PKS and CPS contribute to the overall taste pattern. A 1% (w/v) honey solution was prepared by dissolving the honey sample in distilled water at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C). Data acquisition was performed over 140 s with a sampling interval of 1 s. Following each measurement, the sensors were rinsed with deionized water to prevent carryover effects. The mean sensor response recorded between 120 and 140 s, corresponding to the signal stabilization phase, was used for data analysis. All measurements were conducted in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

All results were calculated and analyzed using SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Differences among samples were evaluated using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) for mean comparison. Homogeneity of variance was assessed prior to analysis. Pearson correlation analysis was used to examine relationships among physicochemical properties. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed and visualized using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (McGill University, Montreal, Canada).
