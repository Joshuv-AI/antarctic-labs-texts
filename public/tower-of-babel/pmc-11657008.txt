Pathway analysis of the impact of family function and self-efficacy on depression and anxiety in patients undergoing in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer
Authors: Fangliang Zou, Yi Fang, Yanshan Lin, Zikai Feng, Shiying Cai, Jiliang Huang, Shaoyan Zheng, Jue Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatients undergoing assisted reproductive technology (ART) experience significant psychological distress due to infertility, with depression and anxiety being the most common manifestations. This study investigates the influence of family support and self-efficacy on the mental health of patients undergoing in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET). The aim is to assess the direct and indirect effects of family function and self-efficacy on depression and anxiety in IVF-ET patients through pathway analyses, thereby providing novel insights for improving patients’ psychological well-being.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted from March to July 2021, employing convenience sampling to recruit 291 participants from a tertiary care hospital’s reproductive medicine center. Data were collected using the Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS), General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), Family APGAR index (APGAR), and a Demographic Characteristics Form. Descriptive analysis, Pearson correlation analysis, and multiple linear regression analysis were performed. A Structural Equation Model (SEM) was utilized for pathway analysis to evaluate the direct and indirect influences of family function and self-efficacy on anxiety and depression.ResultsThe scores for the SAS and SDS were 46.15 ± 7.35 and 51.71 ± 8.65, respectively. Multiple regression analysis indicated that family function, self-efficacy, and economic status significantly predicted anxiety and depression. Path analysis revealed that economic status directly (β=-0.447, -0.232) and indirectly (β=-0.066, -0.068) affected anxiety and depression, while family function both directly and indirectly affected depression (β=-0.323, -0.104), directly affected anxiety (β=-0.351), and self-efficacy directly influenced only depression (β=-0.509).ConclusionThe findings underscore the pivotal role of a supportive family environment and self-efficacy in alleviating anxiety and depression among IVF-ET patients. The mediating role of family function between economic status and mental health highlights the importance of integrated support systems. Enhancing family function and self-efficacy as part of comprehensive care for individuals undergoing ART is crucial for promoting patient well-being.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-024-02246-y.

Introduction

In recent years, the incidence of infertility has risen globally, affecting approximately one in four couples worldwide [1]. According to the World Health Organization, an estimation of 8–12% global couples are infertile [2], with rates in China reaching 15.6% and increasing with age [3]. The progression of assisted reproductive technology (ART) have prompted an increasing number of women to opt for in vitro fertilization-embryo transfer (IVF-ET) as a therapeutic intervention [4]. Individuals undergoing IVF-ET often face substantial psychological distress due to infertility, rigorous medical procedures, high costs, and unpredictable outcomes [5]. Beyond treatment challenges, individuals with infertility frequently face societal and familial pressures, which exacerbate their risk of developing psychological conditions such as depression, anxiety, and diminished self-esteem. Notably, depression and anxiety are the most observed, impacting the quality of life for both genders and potentially influencing the success of pregnancies [6–9].

The role of family function is pivotal to the psychological well-being of patients undergoing IVF-ET [10, 11]. Women dealing with infertility often subject to increased anxiety and fear due to societal pressures, family conflicts, or even domestic violence [12, 13]. Research indicates that women in couples experiencing infertility often anticipate a higher degree of family adaptation but are frequently disappointed with actual support and unity they receive [14]. There is an inverse relationship between robust family support and the incidence of depression, anxiety, and stress, and higher levels of self-efficacy also significantly alleviate psychological distress [11]. Women with higher self-efficacy are less prone to depression, anxiety, and stress, as enhanced self-efficacy helps to counteract negative emotions and lessen stress associated with infertility [15].

The Concentric Sphere Family Environment Theory (CSFET), proposed by Prof Hohashi in 2005 [16], is a theoretical framework that centers on the impact of the family environment on the welfare of the family system. It delineates how the family system is influenced by the emotional states of its members, such as their feelings, moods, and thoughts, with a particular focus on the “cascading effect” of emotions, where a change in one family member’s emotions can trigger emotional shifts in others. Additionally, the Self-Determination Theory (SDT), formulated by Deci and Ryan [17], elucidates how human behavior is propelled by self-motivation and self-determination, advocating the necessity of fulfilling three fundamental psychological needs, i.e. autonomy, relatedness, and competence [17]. This theory underscores the importance of harnessing internal resources to actively confront challenges and enhance emotional regulation self-efficacy; conversely, diminished emotional regulation self-efficacy can impede the experience of positive emotions and the dispelling of negative ones, potentially leading to an elevated risk of depressive disorders [18].

Beyond family function and self-efficacy, factors such as age, economic status, and literacy are also pivotal. Numerous studies have established that education and economic status significantly shape mental health outcomes [19, 20]. Economic status influences healthcare access, stress levels, and mental health overall. Lower economic status is associated with increased psychological distress, including anxiety and depression, attributed to financial strain, lower literacy, and limited access to support and healthcare services [21, 22]. The substantial costs associated with IVF-ET treatments can intensify the stress experienced by patients from lower economic backgrounds [23]. Conversely, a higher economic status typically provides individuals with superior resources to manage stress, including access to psychological counseling, social support, and a sense of financial security, which may mitigate the emotional burden of infertility treatments [24]. Therefore, the present study not only investigates the interplay between family function, self-efficacy with and anxiety and depression but also incorporates general variables such as age, economic status and literacy to assess their collective impact on anxiety and depression.

Although the above theories and studies have extensively examined family function and self-efficacy in various contexts, such as spinal cord injuries, acquired brain injuries, and postpartum depression, there is limited research on their synergistic impact on IVF-ET patients [25–27]. Most previous studies have concentrated on these factors individually, often overlooking their potential synergies. This study aims to evaluate the direct or indirect influences of family function and self-efficacy on depression and anxiety experienced by IVF-ET patients through pathway analyses, with the goal of generating new strategies to ameliorate the negative psychological states of these patients. We propose the following hypothesis: (a) self-efficacy exerts a direct negative influence on anxiety and depression; (b) family function mediates the relationship between self-efficacy and anxiety/depression; (c) family function directly affects anxiety and depression negatively (Fig. 1).

Materials and methods

Design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from March to July 2021 at a tertiary care hospital’s reproductive medicine center in Guangdong Province, China. We employed a convenience sampling method to recruit patients undergoing assisted conception. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a diagnosis of infertility in accordance with the World Health Organization criteria and having initiated the assisted reproduction cycle; (2) currently undergoing controlled ovulation induction treatment; (3) capable and willing to complete the questionnaire; (4) absence of history of psychiatric disorders. Patients who discontinued treatment due to acute illnesses such as colds or fevers, or for personal reasons, were excluded from the study. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (Approval No. 2021-016), and verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The questionnaire comprised 55 variables. Following Kendall’s rough estimation method [28] for determining sample size, it is suggested that the sample size should be 5–10 times the number of variables, which translates to a range of 275–550 cases. After accounting for a 10% attrition rate and potential biases associated with convenience sampling, we adjusted the estimated sample size to 303 individuals. A sample size of 200 or more was deemed adequate for structural equation modeling [29]. A total of 303 questionnaires were distributed, and 291 were completed validly, resulting in a response rate of 96%.

The questionnaire encompassed several standardized measures, including the Self-rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS), General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES), Family Apgar Index (APGAR), along with demographic questions developed for this study. The surveys were administered in person by two investigators who had received uniform training. Following a detailed explanation of the study’s objectives and obtaining informed consent, participants were asked to complete the questionnaires independently. The investigators did not interfere with the completion of the questionnaires, intervening only to offer necessary clarifications or instructions. Their responsibilities included collecting the completed questionnaires and promptly addressing possible omissions.

Measures

Demographic characteristics form

The self-designed questionnaire gathers basic demographic information including the patient’s age, education level, average monthly household income, marital status, number of embryos transferred, and place of residence.

Self-rating anxiety scale (SAS)

The Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS), developed by Zung [30], comprises 20 items on a 4-point Likert scale: 1 (‘never or occasionally’), 2 (‘sometimes’), 3 (‘often’), and 4 (‘always’). Scores of 50 to 59 suggest mild anxiety, 60 to 69 indicate moderate anxiety, and 70 to 100 indicate severe anxiety. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was 0.880 for the Chinese version [31] and 0.741 for the current study.

Self-rating Depression Scale (SDS)

Also developed by Zung [32], the Self-Rating Depression Scale (SDS) consists of 20 items on a 4-point Likert scale with the same rating criteria as the SAS. A score of 53 marks the threshold for depressive symptoms; 53 to 62 indicates mild depression, 63 to 72 indicates moderate depression, and 73 to 100 indicates severe depression. The normative mean for the Chinese version of the SDS was 41.88 ± 10.57 [33], providing a better visualization of patients’ subjective feelings of anxiety and their changes during treatment. The reliability test by Peng et al. reported an overall Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.784 for the Chinese version [34]. The Cronbach’s alpha for the current study was 0.786.

General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES)

Developed by Ralf Schwarzer [35] and revised by Wang et al. [36], the GSES comprises 10 items scored on a 4-point Likert scale. Responses range from 1 (‘does not meet’), 2 (‘somewhat meets’), 3 (‘not quite meets’), to 4 (‘very much does not meet’). The total score is the average of all item scores, with higher scores indicating greater self-efficacy. This scale assesses an individual’s confidence in managing various challenges and new situations. It has been used and validated in a Chinese population, with an internal consistency coefficient of 0.87 [36]. The Cronbach’s alpha for the current study was 0.935.

Family APGAR index (APGAR)

The Family APGAR Index, developed by Smilkstein [37], assesses five dimensions: adaptability, cooperation, adulthood, affectivity, and closeness. It uses a 3-point Likert scale with 2 (‘often’), 1 (‘sometimes’), and 0 (‘seldom’) assigned to each item. Total scores from 0 to 3 indicate severe family dysfunction, 4–6 indicate moderate dysfunction, and 7–10 indicate good family function, reflecting satisfaction with family relationships. The Chinese version of the scale was localized and validated by Lv et al., with Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.830 to 0.857 [38]. The Cronbach’s alpha for the current study was 0.796.

Statistical analysis

Data entry was performed by two individuals using EpiData 3.1, followed by subsequent analysis conducted with SPSS 21.0 and AMOS 26.0. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using Q-Q plots and P-P plots. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation (SD) were used to summarize patient data. Continuous measurements were expressed as Mean ± SD, while categorical data were presented as percentages. Differences in anxiety and depression scores among IVF-ET patients with varying demographic characteristics were examined using t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Pearson’s correlation analysis was used to investigate the relationships between anxiety, depression, family function, and self-efficacy. Further, the impact of these variables on psychological well-being was assessed through multiple linear regression analysis and Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) for pathway analysis. The maximum likelihood method was employed for model parameter estimation, and model fitness was evaluated based on the following criteria [39]: χ2/DF < 3.0, RMSEA < 0.08, and IFI, TLI, and CFI > 0.9, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 303 questionnaires were distributed, with 291 valid responses, yielding a response rate of 96%. The participants, all of whom were infertility patients, were aged between 22 and 42 years, with an average age of 31.13 ± 4.11 years. The majority (57.0%) had completed at least junior college education. Additionally, 94.8% of the patients (276 individuals) were in their first marriage, 230 had undergone single embryo transfers, 89.1% reported a monthly household income below 10,000 yuan, and 76.3% resided in urban areas.

Comparison of anxiety and depression scores with sociodemographic variables

Independent samples t-tests and one-way ANOVAs were used to compare anxiety scores among patients with different demographic characteristics. The factors influencing anxiety in infertility patients showed statistically significant differences in anxiety scores based on age (F = 1.762, P < 0.05), education level (F = 1.835, P < 0.05), marital status (F = 1.801, P < 0.05), and economic status (F = 1.593, P < 0.05). Similarly, depression scores varied significantly with respect to age (F = 1.536, P < 0.05), education level (F = 2.574, P < 0.05), reproductive status (F = 2.989, P < 0.05), economic status (F = 2.523, P < 0.05), and residence (F = 1.561, P < 0.05), as detailed in Table 1.

Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analysis

Among infertility patients, 56.4% (164 individuals) reported good family function, 36.4% (106 individuals) experienced moderate family dysfunction, and 7.2% (21 individuals) suffered from severe family dysfunction. The self-efficacy score was 2.39 ± 0.61, and the family function score was 6.97 ± 2.33. In terms of psychological assessments, the anxiety score was 46.15 ± 7.35, and the depression score was 51.71 ± 8.65. The prevalence rates for mild, moderate, and severe anxiety were 26.8%, 2.4%, and 0.7%, while for depression the rates were 31.9%, 7.9%, and 0.6%, respectively. Correlation analysis revealed a negative correlation between family function and self-efficacy scores with the levels of anxiety and depression. Additionally, self-efficacy was positively correlated with family function (Table 2).

The results indicated no multicollinearity among the variables, with family function and economic status sequentially entered the anxiety regression equation (Table 3). For depression, family function, self-efficacy, and economic status were sequentially added to the regression model (Table 4).

Pathway analysis of anxiety and depression in infertility patients

The variables identified as statistically significant through stepwise regression analysis were used to construct an initial model, including economic status, family function, self-efficacy, anxiety, and depression total scores. Using the maximum likelihood method, a well-fitting equation-corrected model was developed. The analysis revealed a χ2/DF of 2.006, RMSEA of 0.003, IFI, TLI and CFI all equal to 1.000 (Fig. 2). Economic status had a direct positive effect on family function (β = 0.261, P < 0.05) and had direct negative effects on anxiety and depression (β=-0.447, -0.232, respectively, P < 0.05). Family function had direct passive effects on anxiety and depression (β = 0.323, 0.351, respectively, P < 0.05) and a direct positive effect on self-efficacy (β = 0.330, P < 0.05). Self-efficacy had a direct positive effect on depression, (β = 0.509, P < 0.05), but no direct effect on anxiety (P > 0.05). In the pathway where economic status affected anxiety and depression, family function played a partial mediating role, and in the pathway where family function affected depression, self-efficacy also played a partial mediating role (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the impact of family function and self-efficacy on anxiety and depression among women undergoing IVF-ET for infertility. Using pathway analysis, we explored the interplay among these factors, with findings that partially supported our hypotheses: economic status and family function are identified as influential factors on anxiety and depression in infertile women undergoing IVF-ET; both economic status and family function have direct effects on anxiety and depression, with economic status potentially influencing these psychological states through the mediation of family function; family function also impact depression through the mediation of self-efficacy. Improved economic status can ameliorate depression and anxiety in IVF-ET patients by fostering better family function. These results align with the CSFET, which posits that a supportive family environment and home care setting are conducive to the realization of personal values and the consolidation of family functions.

Shaped by China’s social, cultural, and traditional perspectives, childbearing has become an ethical imperative and a widely embraced value of procreation [40]. Female infertility patients, compared to their male counterparts, tend to experience heightened psychosocial stress and are more susceptible to psychosocially induced physical and mental health issues, such as anxiety and depression [41]. In our study, 291 infertility patients were enrolled, with 29.9% exhibiting anxiety and 45.4% exhibiting depression, rates that exceed the 25.2% and 31.3% prevalence of anxiety and depression, respectively, reported by a recent cross-sectional study in China [41]. These disparities may stem from varying economic and cultural conditions and significant social pressures across different regions [42], which can lead to varying degrees of anxiety and depression in infertile women. Furthermore, the prevalence of anxiety and depression among infertile couples may vary at different stages of the IVF-ET process [43].

The comparison of anxiety and depression scores with socio-demographic variables revealed statistically significant correlations with age, education, marital status, economic status, number of transplants, and place of residence. In line with previous research [44–46], older age and lower education levels are associated with increased psychological stress during infertility treatments, indicating a complex interplay between personal characteristics and psychological outcomes for IVF-ET patients. Economic challenges can intensify psychological and interpersonal strains, often prompting patients to discontinue treatment [47]. In contrast, affluent patients report lower anxiety and depression levels due to reduced financial strain and improved access to medical and psychological support services [48]. The number of embryo transfers and place of residence were uniquely linked to depression scores, resonating with Bhamani’s study [49], which found a significant association between depression, the number of treatments, and the patient’s place of residence, particularly in areas with limited access to high-quality infertility treatments.

Correlation analyses further indicated that family function was positively associated with self-efficacy and negatively associated with anxiety and depression, which is consistent with Zhou’s findings [50] that robust family function bolsters resilience and coping mechanisms against stress, mitigating negative emotional symptoms. High self-efficacy is also linked to lower levels of psychological distress [51].

The pathway analysis conducted in this study partially substantiated our hypotheses, revealing that economic status and family function exert direct negative effects on anxiety and depression. Furthermore, economic status was found to influence family function, thereby indirectly impacting anxiety and depression. Prior research has highlighted that financial strain can compel numerous patients to interrupt or forgo treatment, consequently augmenting their susceptibility to anxiety and depression [24, 52]. Family support, encompassing coping assistance, emotional reinforcement, problem-sharing, and participation in decision-making, has been recognized as a protective factor in predicting anxiety and depression among women with infertility [53], and is crucial in alleviating anxiety and depression in this patient cohort [54]. Dysfunctional family dynamics can contribute to anxiety and depression through a multifaceted network of influences, with robust correlations observed between family function, anxiety, and depressive symptoms. Positive family relationships can mitigate stress and diminish negative emotions [55], while familial support bolsters patients’ courage and confidence to confront challenges, thereby ameliorating negative emotions. In contrast, poor family function, challenging economic conditions, and strained relationships may restrict available support, impeding patients’ ability to effectively mitigate negative emotions. The substantial pressure from these sources can precipitate adverse emotions such as inferiority, despair, fear, depression, and loneliness in women with infertility. Li et al. [56] identified social relationships as a pivotal mechanism linking household economic status to emotional well-being. A family’s economic status significantly affect its functionality; financial limitations can undermine stability and diminish the capacity to provide support, thereby exacerbating stress and contributing to anxiety and depression. Intense financial stress may erode the hope for conception, further intensifying the emotional burden on these patients.

Self-efficacy is known to play a protective role in managing negative emotions and stress [57]. When family function is robust, it can adequately fulfill emotional and social needs, offer care and support, enhance individuals’ sense of self-efficacy, and enable them to employ positive coping strategies in stressful situations, thus mitigating adverse effects on mental health. Moreover, individuals with higher self-efficacy are generally more adept at managing negative emotions and utilizing positive coping mechanisms, leading to reduced anxiety and depression, and establishing a virtuous cycle [58]. Although self-efficacy directly influenced depression, our findings did not reveal any direct or indirect effects of self-efficacy on anxiety. Compared to Scholten’s study [25], which found that self-efficacy moderated the relationship between family function and both depression and anxiety, our study identified self-efficacy as a mediator only for depressive symptoms. This could be attributed to differences in the frequency of instances, sources, and measurement tools utilized. The findings of this study enhance the understanding of the multifaceted factors influencing the mental health of infertility patients. Developing individualized and comprehensive care strategies for these patients necessitates an integrated approach that considers economic status, family function, self-efficacy, as well as psychological well-being. Integrating family counseling and psychosocial interventions to boost self-efficacy can effectively alleviate anxiety and depression, leading to improved outcomes and greater psychological resilience in women undergoing IVF-ET.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, due to its cross-sectional design, the study does not allow for the establishment of causal relationships or the examination of temporal dynamics between variables. Secondly, the sample was drawn from a single tertiary hospital fertility center in Guangdong Province, China, using convenience sampling, which limits the representativeness of the findings and may restrict their generalizability to other regions. Additionally, as all data were self-reported via patient-completed questionnaires, there is potential for recall bias in responses to certain questions. It is suggested that in future research, multicenter, large-sample, random sampling methods should be employed. Lastly, the psychological mechanisms of infertility patients are intricate and likely influenced by a multitude of factors that warrant further investigation in future studies.

Conclusions

Our study demonstrates the significant roles of family function and self-efficacy in alleviating psychological distress among IVF-ET patients. Robust family support and individual self-efficacy were found to associate with reduced anxiety and depression. The mediating role of family function between economic status and mental health underscores the need for comprehensive support systems. These findings suggest that interventions aimed at enhancing family function and self-efficacy could improve psychological outcomes for IVF-ET patients. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs to clarify the causal effects of these variables, and efforts should be made to fully engage family support systems to promote the mental health of IVF-ET patients.
