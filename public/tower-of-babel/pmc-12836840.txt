Coix lacryma-jobi root extract exhibits self-toxicity, with butyl palmitate being the primary toxic substance
Authors: Yi Cheng, Guiqin Zhou, Lei Li, Min Liu, Dailing Liu, Yinying Liu, Jiana Shu, Juan Xiang, Bi Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoix lacryma-jobi growth is often inhibited by continuous cropping due to autotoxic effects. The autotoxic effects and decomposition of aqueous extracts from different parts of C. lacryma-jobi plants and impact of their decomposed matter are unclear. The present study tested aqueous extracts from shoots, roots, and decomposed residues at four concentrations to assess their autotoxic properties. The extracts and decomposition products inhibited seed germination and seedling growth in a concentration-dependent manner. At concentrations of ≥ 0.1 g mL-1, the extracts significantly suppressed seed germination and impaired seedling growth. Specifically, root and shoot lengths, plant height, stem diameter, leaf area, and root activity were all reduced. Concurrently, leaf superoxide dismutase, peroxidase, and catalase activities decreased significantly, whereas proline and malondialdehyde contents increased significantly. The inhibitory potency of the materials tested followed this order: roots > shoots > decomposition products. To isolate the specific autotoxic compounds, we employed a series of solvents for extraction and fractionation. The acidic fraction obtained from an n-butanol partition of a methanol root extract is strongly inhibitory to C. lacryma-jobi seed germination. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis revealed 22 allelochemical compounds. Subsequent bioassays on seven selected candidates revealed that five compounds—hexadecane, dibutyl phthalate, vanillin, butyl palmitate and palmitic acid —exhibited significant, dose-dependent autotoxic effects on seed germination. Their order of inhibitory potency was: butyl palmitate > vanillin > palmitic acid > hexadecane > dibutyl phthalate.Keywords: Allelochemicals, Autotoxicity, Butyl palmitate, Coix lacryma-jobi, Germination, GC-MS

Background

Coix lacryma-jobi L. is an annual herb of the Poaceae family that is widely cultivated in East and Southeast Asia, particularly in China, Japan, Thailand, and Myanmar [1, 2]. Coix seeds contain numerous bioactive ingredients with anti-tumor, anti-diabetic, antioxidant, anti-allergic, and anti-melanoma properties, and they can improve metabolic syndrome and reduce blood pressure [3, 4]. Guizhou Province has the largest planting area and production in China, exceeding 16,000 ha and approximately 33,000 tons [5], respectively, and is a prominent processing and sales center within China and across Southeast Asia [6]. The Coix industry has expanded substantially in Guizhou due to continued development based on its nutritional and medicinal value. However, the concentration of production areas and a reduction in foxtail millet cultivation have led to continuous cropping which results in poor growth, severe disease or insect infestation, premature senescence, and significantly lower yields and quality [7, 8]. These factors have adversely impacted the healthy and sustainable development of the Coix industry.

Obstacles to continuous cropping arise from complex plant–soil–microbial systems, including physical and chemical degradation of the rhizosphere soil, nutrient imbalances, soil-borne diseases, and crop autotoxicity [9, 10]. Autotoxicity is commonly observed in both natural and agricultural ecosystems where living or decaying plants release chemicals that inhibit the growth and development of the same species [11, 12]. Several studies have shown that eucalyptus leaves, bark, and roots release volatile allelochemicals into the environment via volatilization, leaching by rain, and soil adsorption. These compounds interact with environmental biotic and abiotic factors and influence plant growth [13, 14]. The inhibitory allelopathic effects of aqueous extracts derived from Artemisia annua branches and leaves on the germination of their own seeds and those of other plant species were more potent than those of root extracts (REs) [13, 15, 16]. Aqueous extracts from Lantana camara leaves inhibited germination and seedling growth and reduced seedling survival rate in a dose-dependent manner [17]. Decomposed solutions from Salvia miltiorrhiza roots, stems, and leaves contained various substances capable of inhibiting plant germination or growth, with higher concentrations derived from roots than from stems and leaves [18]. The rhizosphere soil, root exudates, decomposing plant wastes, and leachates derived from Imperata cylindrica—one of the world’s ten most invasive weed species—inhibit mycorrhizal colonization in various woody plant species [19, 20]. Allelochemical accumulation in the soil can promote imbalances in soil nutrients and dysfunctions in microbial activity, thereby hindering continuous plant cropping [9, 21–23]. Six compounds were isolated from the rhizosphere of Lanzhou Lily. Among them, dibutyl phthalate, decyl trans-p-coumarate, 7-oxo-sitosterol, 6-hydroxystigmast-4-en-3-one, and β-sitosterol-3-O-glucoside primarily inhibited the seedling growth of Lactuca sativa and Lanzhou lily [24]. Some phenolic acids found in the root secretions and rhizosphere soils of American ginseng are potential allelopathic substances [25]. Ferulic acid, total saponins, and Radix notoginseng extract can inhibit plant growth [26]. Continuous cropping becomes feasible on the same land for numerous years once allelochemicals have been eliminated [27, 28], highlighting the importance of understanding their composition.

Although numerous studies have suggested that autotoxicity impairs seed germination and seedling growth, few reports have addressed the autotoxicity of Job’s tears. Here, we tested the hypothesis that C. lacryma-jobi induces autotoxicity, thereby affecting seed germination and seedling growth and diminishing crop yield. Our objectives were to (i) validate the autotoxicity and identify differences among Coix plants and decomposers and (ii) identify allelochemicals in the most potent constituents, thereby establishing a theoretical foundation for developing mitigation technologies and sustainable production practices for Coix.

Materials and methods

Preparing aqueous Coix plant and decomposer extracts (DEs)

In Chengbei Town, Xingren City, Guizhou Province (25°26′N, 105°04′E), a C. lacryma-jobi field under continuous planting was selected. The planted variety, Xingren Xiaobaike (Coix lacryma-jobi L.), is a landrace variety extensively cultivated in Xingren City and was provided by the Institute of Subtropical Crops, Guizhou Academy of Agricultural Sciences. The stems, leaves, and roots from five randomly selected C. lacryma-jobi plants were collected, washed, and air-dried naturally.

The air-dried roots and shoots were ground. Simultaneously, 1 kg of dried Coix plants was added to a plastic bucket with a plant: unplanted soil: distilled water mass ratio of 1:1:5. The bucket opening was covered with a perforated plastic cloth and allowed to naturally decompose for 15 days with intermittent stirring every 5 days. Next, 1 g of C. lacryma-jobi root, shoot, and decomposer samples were each added to 5 mL of sterile water (1:5 w/v). The mixture was soaked in a sterile container, sealed, and incubated at 40 °C for 24 h with shaking every 12 h. The solutions were then centrifuged at 6400 × g for 3 min. The supernatant was then filtered through a 0.45 μm organic membrane filter to remove fine particles and reduce microbial load. The resulting clarified stock solutions ( original concentration of 0.2 g mL− 1) were collected and diluted in aqueous medium to concentrations of 0.025, 0.05, and 0.1 g mL− 1. All solutions were refrigerated at 4 °C for subsequent use.

Determining the biological activities of the aqueous extracts

Seed-germination experiments were conducted using the double-layer filter paper method. The filter paper was sterilized, and in each Petri dish contained 50 peeled Xingren Xiaobaike seeds that were full, uniform in size, and mature. The seeds were sterilized in a 2% sodium hypochlorite solution for 2 min, rinsed several times with sterile water, and evenly arranged in the dishes. The experiment was designed using a two-factor, completely randomized design with four concentrations (0.025, 0.05, 0.1, and 0.2 g mL-1) of aqueous root extracts (REs), shoot extracts (SEs), and decomposer extracts (DEs). Distilled water served as the control (CK), resulting in 13 treatments (n = 5). Initially, 10 mL of treatment solution was added to each culture dish, placed in a 25 °C incubator with constant temperature and lighting. Each culture dish was exposed to a 12 h light: 12 h dark cycle with 85% humidity. Thereafter, the evaporation loss of leachate was replenished using the constant weight method every 24 h. Seed germination was recorded daily after the appearance of sprouts [29]. Seed germination (radicle extension of 2 mm from the seed coat) was recorded daily for 8 days. Effects of aqueous extracts on the growth and physiological characteristics of Coix seedlings

Seedling experiments were conducted in plastic pots (18 cm diameter, 15 cm height) filled with 1 kg soil and sown with 10 presoaked Xingren Xiaobaike seeds (13 treatments; 15 pots/treatment). At the two-leaf stage, four seedlings were placed in each pot, and 100 ml aqueous extract was applied to each pot. Equal amounts of water and aqueous extracts were added every 5 days over 30 days. Watering was adjusted based on weather conditions and soil-moisture levels to maintain the soil-moisture content at 80% of the field capacity. At 30 days post-treatment, various agronomic traits, root vitality, leaf protective enzyme activity, and the contents of osmotic adjustment substances were measured. The root: shoot ratio, relative growth rate, response index, and synthetic allelopathic effect index were also determined.

Plant height was measured from the base of the main stem to the top of the plant. Stem diameter was measured at the middle of the first internode of the main stem using a Vernier caliper. Dry weights (n = 3) were measured after dehydration at 105 °C for 30 min and drying to a constant weight at 80 °C, and then the stems and leaves were separated. Fully expanded leaf areas were determined using an LI-3100 C desktop leaf area meter. The root: shoot ratio was calculated as root biomass/(stem biomass + leaf biomass). The relative growth rate was calculated as (lnW2 − lnW1)/(t2 − t1), where W1 and W2 are the initial and final dry weights, respectively, and t1 and t2 are the initial and final times (days), respectively.

At 30 days post-treatment, three representative plants from each treatment group were selected for measurement. On clear days between 09:00 and 12:00, the net photosynthetic rate (Pn), stomatal conductance (Gs), intercellular CO2 concentration (Ci), and transpiration rate (Tr) of the second fully expanded leaf from the top of the plant were determined using an LI-6400XT portable photosynthetic instrument (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA).

Root activity was measured using a modified triphenyl tetrazolium chloride method [35]. Superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity was determined by monitoring the photochemical reduction of nitro blue tetrazolium [36]. Catalase activity (CAT) was determined by monitoring H2O2 oxidation at 240 nm [37]. Peroxidase activity (POD) was performed using guaiacol substrate [38]. Malondialdehyde (MDA) concentrations were determined by conducting TBA assays [39], which involved recording the absorbances of the supernatants at 600, 532, and 450 nm. Proline content was determined using the acid ninhydrin colorimetric method [40].

The response index was calculated as described previously [41] and used to derived the synthetic allelopathic effect index. Two formulas were used to calculate the response index based on the relationship between the treatment (T) and control (CK) groups. When T ≥ CK, the response index was calculated as 1 − CK/T. When T < CK, the response index was calculated as T/CK − 1 [41]. The synthetic allelopathic effect index was derived from the response index. A positive response index indicated a stimulatory effect, and a negative response index indicated an inhibitory effect. We calculated the seed-germination index response during germination and the seedling-growth index response of young plants. The synthetic allelopathic effect index was computed as the average response index across different test parameters within each treatment. A synthetic allelopathic effect index > 0 indicated a promoting effect, and a synthetic allelopathic effect index < 0 indicated an inhibitory effect. The absolute value of the index reflected the intensity of the effect.

Isolating autotoxic compounds from Coix roots and assaying their allelopathic activities

Root samples (100 g) were added to separate 500 mL solutions of distilled water, 80% ethanol, and methanol. The mixtures were incubated in triplicate at 40 °C in a culture box for 24 h. After combining the replicate extracts, the resulting solutions were concentrated under reduced pressure at 45 °C and adjusted to 500 mL. The mother liquor was diluted with distilled water to prepare four concentrations (0.2, 0.1, 0.05, and 0.025 g mL− 1) of REs for biological assays. The most bioactive extract (0.2 g mL− 1) was selected from the C. lacryma-jobi root system, and triplicate extractions were performed with equal volumes of n-butanol, ethyl acetate, ether, dichloromethane, and petroleum ether. The extracts were pooled, concentrated to 500 mL, transferred to a brown reagent bottle, and n-butanol, ethyl acetate, ether, chloroform, petroleum ether, and methanol phases were extracted. Each extract was concentrated at 45 °C under reduced pressure until dryness and dissolved in distilled water to 500 mL for subsequent biological assays. The organic phase with the most potent active component was adjusted to pH 3 using 1 M HCl and extracted with an organic solvent. The remaining aqueous phase was adjusted to pH 12 using 1 M NaOH and extracted with an organic solvent (alkaline fraction). Subsequently, the residual aqueous phase was further adjusted to pH 7 using 1 M HCl and extracted with an organic solvent (neutral fraction). The acidic, alkaline, and neutral organic extracts were concentrated under reduced pressure to 1 mL (at 45 °C) and filtered through a 0.45 μm organic membrane. After dissolution in distilled water, the extracts were diluted to 500 mL for biological assays, revealing the most active component for gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) analysis.

GC–MS analysis of allelopathic components

Analysis was performed on an Agilent 7890 A gas chromatograph coupled with a 5975 C mass selective detector (Agilent Technologies, USA). Separation was achieved using an Agilent HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). The injector temperature was maintained at 250 °C, and the samples (1 µL) were injected in split mode with a split ratio of 20:1. High-purity helium was used as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.0 mL·min⁻¹. The GC temperature program was set as follows: the initial oven temperature was held at 60 °C for 3 min, then increased to 180 °C at a rate of 5 °C·min⁻¹ and held for 3 min, and finally raised to 300 °C at 5 °C·min⁻¹ and maintained for 5 min. The mass spectrometer was operated in the electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV. The ion source temperature was set to 230 °C, and the quadrupole temperature was 150 °C. The mass scan range was set from m/z 29 to 650, with a scanning frequency of 2 scans per second. Data were processed using an Agilent MSD Chemical Workstation (G1701EA; Agilent Technologies, USA). Substances were identified via computer retrieval using the NIST08 and WILEY275 MS databases. Relative concentrations were determined using the peak-area normalization method [42, 43].

Determining the biological activities of potential autotoxic substances

The purified compounds were first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and diluted to 8, 4, and 2 µg mL− 1 in distilled water. An equal volume of DMSO was added to distilled water as a control (final DMSO% < 1%). The exogenous-addition method was employed to evaluate the effects on the germination of C. lacryma-jobi seeds, thereby verifying their allelopathic properties.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were repeated thrice for each assay. Data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 18.0 (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A normal distribution (Kolmogorov–Smirnov test) and homogeneity variance (Levene’s test) were tested for all raw data. For multiple comparisons, we used Fisher’s protected least significant difference at a 5% level of probability. Figures were created using OriginLab 2024.

Results

Effects of aqueous RE, SEs, and DEs on C. lacryma-jobi seed germination and seedling growth

Seed germination

Higher concentrations of aqueous REs, SEs, and DEs exhibited stronger inhibition on seed germination (Fig. 1). With increasing RE concentration, the germination index, germination potential, root length, and shoot length gradually decreased. At 0.05 g mL-1, all parameters in the RE treatment were lower than those in the CK group, although the decreases in germination index and root length were not statistically significant. Germination potential and shoot length were significantly reduced by 8.47 and 17.58%, respectively. At an RE concentration of 0.1 g mL-1, the germination index, germination potential, and root and shoot length were significantly decreased (P < 0.05) by 15.71, 14.29%, 29.15, and 52.73%, respectively. A significant decrease (10.67%) in germination rate was observed at the highest concentration 0.2 g mL-1). The trends and degrees of activity of the SE and DE were similar to those of the RE. At concentrations ≥ 0.1 g mL-1, the germination index and germination potential in the SE and DE treatments were 15.75 and 20.60%, and 15.79 and 15.04% lower than those in the CK group, respectively. The root and shoot lengths also decreased significantly (P < 0.05) by 35.53 and 24.41%, and 32.70 and 30.86%, respectively.

Soluble sugar, soluble protein, protease, and α-amylase activities during germination

Synthetic allelopathic effect index

To comprehensively assess the autotoxic effects of the aqueous REs, SEs, and DEs on seed germination and seedling growth of the same species, we determined the synthetic allelopathic effect index by calculating the allelopathic response index (Fig. 5). The synthetic allelopathic effect indexes of the RE, SE, and DE treatments between 0.025 and 0.2 g mL-1 were uniformly negative, indicating inhibition. For the same type of aqueous extract, the synthetic allelopathic effect index decreased as the concentration increased, reaching a minimum value at 0.2 g mL-1. At 0.025 g mL-1, no significant differences were observed in the synthetic allelopathic effect indices of RE, SE, and DE, although at 0.05 g mL-1, SE exhibited significantly lower effects than DE. At concentrations of 0.1 and 0.2 g mL-1, the synthetic allelopathic effect indexes followed the order: RE < SE < DE (P < 0.05). This suggests that the autotoxic effects of the aqueous extract from C. lacryma-jobi roots on seed germination and seedling growth are more pronounced compared to those of the aqueous extracts from shoots or decomposed material.

Studying the autotoxicity of the C. lacryma-jobi root system

Separation of autotoxic substances in the C. lacryma-jobi root system

The inhibitory effects of various C. lacryma-jobi root extracts on seed germination were concentration-dependent. Higher concentrations resulted in more pronounced inhibitory effects (Fig. 6). At extract concentrations between 0.025 and 0.05 g mL-1, the germination index, potential, and rate of C. lacryma-jobi seeds were not significantly different from those of the CK group. However, the root and shoot lengths decreased significantly, with the methanol extract solution showing the greatest reduction. When the concentration of extract solution increased to 0.1 g mL-1, the germination index, germination rate, root length, and shoot length were all significantly lower than those of the CK group. Discussion

Autotoxicity of C. lacryma-jobi aqueous extracts and their decomposed substances on C. lacryma-jobi seed germination and seedling growth

The stems, leaves, roots, and flowers of numerous crops produce allelopathic autotoxic substances. These compounds infiltrate the soil via rain leaching and crop-residue decomposition. With extended continuous cropping, these substances progressively accumulate in the soil, markedly impacting subsequent crops [44–46]. The current findings indicate that aqueous extracts from C. lacryma-jobi plants and their decomposition products significantly inhibited C. lacryma-jobi seed germination. As the concentrations of aqueous REs, SEs, and DEs increased, the inhibitory effects on seed germination and on root and shoot lengths became more pronounced. Furthermore, at high concentrations, the protease and α-amylase activities in germinating C. lacryma-jobi seeds decreased markedly, as did the levels of soluble proteins and sugars. Substantial water is absorbed into the environment during seed germination. This water contained allelopathic autotoxic substances derived from C. lacryma-jobi plants. As the aqueous extract concentration increased, the contents of these allelopathic autotoxic substances increased. These substances inhibit enzymatic activity in seeds and impede the transformation of internal substances necessary for germination [43], thereby reducing the vigor and uniformity of C. lacryma-jobi seed germination.

Aqueous extracts from various organs also inhibited the growth of C. lacryma-jobi seedlings. As the aqueous RE and SE concentrations increased, the growth parameters of C. lacryma-jobi seedlings (plant height, stem diameter, leaf area, root dry weight, root: shoot ratio, and relative growth rate) progressively declined. This finding is consistent with previous data [47]. During seedling growth, as the RE-treatment duration and concentration increased, the allelopathic autotoxic substances gradually increased during seedling growth, which inhibited seedling growth and development. Plants undergo a series of physiological changes to adapt to such stress conditions [48]. Photosynthesis, which serves as the physiological and metabolic basis of plant yield, is particularly susceptible to allelopathic autotoxicity [49]. In this study, as the concentration of the aqueous extract increased, the Pn, Gs, and Tr values of C. lacryma-jobi seedlings progressively decreased, whereas the Ci value gradually increased. The reduced photosynthetic capacity may be attributed to leaf stomata closure and diminished CO2 utilization by mesophyll cells. These findings suggest that aqueous C. lacryma-jobi REs inhibited photosynthesis in C. lacryma-jobi seedlings, which aligns with Wang et al. [49], who observed similar inhibition in Medicago truncatula treated with root exudates, and with Zhang et al. [50], who reported comparable effects in melon seedlings treated with root aqueous extracts. Furthermore, we found that higher concentrations of aqueous extracts led to an initial increase followed by a subsequent decrease in CAT, SOD, and POD activities in the leaves of Coix seedlings. These data suggest that low concentrations of aqueous extracts from different organs can stimulate the activities of antioxidant enzymes, leading to increased enzyme activity and preservation of the plasma membrane integrity. Concentrations above 0.1 g mL− 1 intensified stress, reducing antioxidant enzyme activity, which failed to neutralize excess reactive oxygen species. This led to membrane lipid peroxidation, increased membrane permeability, and elevated levels of osmotic adjustment substances such as proline and MDA, disrupting normal plant metabolism [43, 46].

The inhibitory effects of aqueous extracts from different plant organs on seed germination and seedling growth, as well as the sensitivity of each seedling growth index, varied significantly. Our results demonstrate that at identical concentrations, the autotoxic effects followed the order: roots > shoots > decomposed substances. Additionally, the radicle of C. lacryma-jobi seeds and the root system of seedlings were more sensitive to the aqueous extracts of each organ than the young leaves. Variations in autotoxicity in different parts of C. lacryma-jobi may result from distinct types and accumulation levels of autotoxic compounds produced in each part [11]. During growth, roots and shoots exhibit high metabolic activity, facilitating the release of autotoxic substances and resulting in more pronounced autotoxicity. Furthermore, during plant growth, roots must absorb various environmental substances to support normal development of the aboveground parts. Being in closest contact with the soil, the root system is likely the primary site of allelochemical action and, thus, the most affected.

Isolation and characterization of autotoxic compounds from C. lacryma-jobi roots

Organic solvent-based extraction is the most commonly used method to separate autotoxic compounds. Typically, this process involves extracting crude extracts or root exudates with various polar organic solvents, such as methanol, n-butanol, dichloromethane, chloroform, and ethyl acetate, following the principle of “like dissolves like” for the initial separation [51]. Subsequent separation of compounds from these solvents can be achieved by adjusting the pH, leveraging the differential solubility of compounds under acidic and alkaline conditions. You [52] extracted alcohol from continuously cropped Panax notoginseng soil using petroleum ether, ethyl acetate, and n-butanol. Growth experiments with P. notoginseng seedlings showed that the ethyl acetate fraction exhibited a pronounced autotoxic effect, whereas the petroleum ether, n-butanol, and water fractions displayed concentration-dependent bidirectional effects. Furthermore, constructing a fingerprint of the ethyl acetate fraction from P. notoginseng rhizosphere soil and analyzing its chemical components identified ginsenoside-Rh1, a compound with potential autotoxic properties. Yuan [53] conducted repeated extractions of Rehmannia glutinosa roots using 80% ethanol. The extract was subsequently concentrated under reduced pressure at 40 °C. Equal volumes of petroleum ether, ethyl acetate, and n-butanol were used for the sequential extractions. Biological assays revealed that the autotoxicity of the ethyl acetate fraction was significantly stronger than those of the n-butanol and petroleum ether fractions. Five allelochemicals (benzoic acid, vanillin, vanillic acid, ferulic acid, and p-hydroxybenzoic acid) were isolated from the ethyl acetate fraction and identified; ferulic acid was relatively abundant. Tang [54] employed the ether acid–base extraction method to separate and identify decomposition products from Pinellia ternata plants, revealing that allelochemicals were predominantly present in the acidic fraction. GC-MS analysis showed that 2,6-di-tert-butyl-p-cresol, erucamide, and 2,2′-methylenebis (4-methyl-6-tert-butylphenol) collectively accounted for 60.35% of all allelochemicals in the extract, a substantial proportion. Our results showed that among three extraction solvents tested (water, 80% ethanol, and methanol), the methanol extract most strongly inhibited seed germination. Subsequently, various organic solvents (n-butanol, ethyl acetate, ether, dichloromethane, and petroleum ether) were used to isolate autotoxic compounds from the methanol extract of Coix roots and evaluate their bioactivities. We found that n-butanol fraction contained more potent autotoxic compounds than the other fractions. Furthermore, among the acidic, neutral, and alkaline fractions of the n-butanol extract, the acidic fraction was the most potent inhibitor of Coix seed germination.

We identified 22 compounds via GC-MS analysis. Notably, hexadecane, dibutyl phthalate, vanillin, pentadecane, heneicosane, butyl palmitate, and palmitic acid are potentially autotoxic allelochemicals. Palmitic acid (a well-documented autotoxic compound) has been identified in root exudates from numerous plants with continuous cropping obstacles [46, 55]. Geng et al. [56] analyzed root exudates from pepper plants and confirmed the allelopathic effects of dibutyl phthalate, a component identified in the exudates, on lettuce growth. Huang et al. [57] investigated the effects of exogenous additives, such as benzoic acid and palmitic acid, on ginseng seeds. Their findings revealed that these compounds significantly inhibited seed root and hypocotyl growth, with benzoic and palmitic acid being particularly effective. Additionally, benzoic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, cinnamic acid, and palmitic acid were detected in cucumber root exudates and significantly inhibited cucumber growth [58]. Similarly, pea root exudates inhibited radicle elongation. GC-MS analysis revealed the presence of benzoic acid, p-hydroxybenzoic acid, vanillic acid, cinnamic acid, and other phenolic acids in pea root exudates [59, 60]. These compounds may also exhibit significant autotoxic activity as components of C. lacryma-jobi root exudates. Here, exogenous chemicals were added to simulate the effects of potential allelochemicals, including hexadecane, dibutyl phthalate, vanillin, pentadecane, hentriacontane, butyl palmitate, palmitic acid, and mixtures thereof on C. lacryma-jobi seed germination. Within a concentration range of 2–8 mg·L− 1, these substances exhibited inhibitory effects on seed germination in a concentration-dependent manner (except for pentadecane and hentriacontane). Furthermore, their autotoxicity on C. lacryma-jobi followed the order butyl palmitate > vanillin > palmitic acid > hexadecane > dibutyl phthalate. The varying effects of these seven substances at different concentrations suggest that the severity of the autotoxicity contributing to the continuous cropping obstacle in C. lacryma-jobi is concentration-dependent. Therefore, controlling the levels of autotoxic substances in the soil may be crucial for mitigating the autotoxic effects on C. lacryma-jobi.

The impact of autotoxic substances on various growth parameters of C. lacryma-jobi is likely complex, encompassing both synergistic and antagonistic interactions among different autotoxic compounds. Biological activity assays that focus on individual substances may not adequately capture their effects on C. lacryma-jobi growth. Future studies should consider employing multiple-substance mixtures in bioassays to gain a better understanding of the pathways and mechanisms whereby autotoxic substances influence C. lacryma-jobi growth.

Conclusions

In summary, the autotoxic effect of aqueous C. lacryma-jobi root extract on seed germination and seedling growth was more pronounced than that of the shoot and decomposition products. Further separation of autotoxic substances from C. lacryma-jobi roots using various extracts and extractants, along with determination of their biological activities, revealed that the methanol extract, followed by n-butanol organic extraction and isolation of the acidic fraction, exhibited the strongest autotoxic activity. The potential allelochemicals identified (including hexadecane, dibutyl phthalate, vanillin, butyl palmitate, and palmitic acid) exerted significant autotoxic effects on the germination of C. lacryma-jobi seeds. The intensity of these effects correlated positively with the concentration; generally, higher concentrations resulted in stronger autotoxicity. Additionally, the order of the autotoxic effects of these exogenously added substances on C. lacryma-jobi seed germination was as follows: butyl palmitate > vanillin > palmitic acid > hexadecane > dibutyl phthalate.
