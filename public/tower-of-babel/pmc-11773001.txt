RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTOR 22 is a key modulator of the root hair growth responses to fungal ethylene emissions in Arabidopsis
Authors: Rafael Jorge León Morcillo, Jesús Leal-López, Alberto Férez-Gómez, Lidia López-Serrano, Edurne Baroja-Fernández, Samuel Gámez-Arcas, Germán Tortosa, Leonel E López, José Manuel Estevez, Verónica G Doblas, Laura Frías-España, María Dolores García-Pedrajas, Jorge Sarmiento-Villamil, Javier Pozueta-Romero
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh), exposure to volatile compounds (VCs) emitted by Penicillium aurantiogriseum promotes root hair (RH) proliferation and hyper-elongation through mechanisms involving ethylene, auxin, and photosynthesis signaling. In addition, this treatment enhances the levels of the small signaling peptide RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTOR 22 (RALF22). Here, we used genetics to address the role of RALF22 in fungal VC-promoted RH growth and to identify the bioactive fungal VC. We found that RHs of ralf22 and feronia (fer-4) plants impaired in the expression of RALF22 and its receptor FERONIA, respectively, responded weakly to fungal VCs. Unlike in wild-type roots, fungal VC exposure did not enhance RALF22 transcript levels in roots of fer-4 and ethylene- and auxin-insensitive mutants. In ralf22 and fer-4 roots, this treatment did not enhance the levels of ERS2 transcripts encoding one member of the ethylene receptor family and those of some RH-related genes. RHs of ers2-1 and the rsl2rsl4 double mutants impaired in the expression of ERS2 and the ethylene- and auxin-responsive ROOT HAIR DEFECTIVE 6-LIKE 2 and 4 transcription factors, respectively, weakly responded to fungal VCs. Moreover, roots of plants defective in photosynthetic responsiveness to VCs exhibited weak RALF22 expression and RH growth responses to fungal VCs. VCs of ΔefeA strains of P. aurantiogriseum cultures impaired in ethylene synthesis weakly promoted RH proliferation and elongation in exposed plants. We conclude that RALF22 simultaneously functions as a transcriptionally regulated signaling molecule that participates in the ethylene, auxin, and photosynthesis signaling-mediated RH growth response to fungal ethylene emissions and regulation of ethylene perception in RHs.

Introduction

Secreted, small (ca. 5 kDa) RAPID ALKALINIZATION FACTOR (RALF) peptide hormones play important roles in multiple processes, including root and root hair (RH) growth and development (Bergonci et al. 2014; Du et al. 2016; Murphy et al. 2016; Zhu et al. 2020), response and tolerance to biotic and abiotic stresses (Atkinson et al. 2013; Zhao et al. 2018), regulation of plant–microbe interactions (Tang et al. 2022), and modulation of immune responses (Stegmann et al. 2017; Tang et al. 2022; He et al. 2023). In Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana (L.) Heynh), RALF peptides comprise a family of 37 members and practically all of them have the same function of regulating cell expansion (Do Canto et al. 2014). The majority of these small peptides are ligands of plasma membrane-localized protein complexes involving FERONIA (Zhao et al. 2018; Abarca et al. 2021), a receptor-like kinase that regulates growth, development, and responses to environmental changes (Haruta et al. 2014; Zhu et al. 2020; Martínez-Pacheco et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2023) through mechanisms involving calcium signaling (Feng et al. 2018), interaction with hormones (e.g. auxins, abscisic acid, jasmonic acid, brassinosteroids, and ethylene) (Deslauriers and Larsen 2010; Yu et al. 2012; Chen et al. 2016; Li et al. 2022), and the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) (Duan et al. 2010). There are numerous studies analyzing aspects of this complex signaling network (Blackburn et al. 2020; Abarca et al. 2021). However, the functional role of the majority of RALF peptides remains largely unknown.

In nature, plants interact with microorganisms by exchanging chemical signals through the phytosphere (Huang et al. 2019; Singh et al. 2023). Such interactions are important for plant productivity, fitness, and capacity to adapt to environmental changes (Stringlis et al. 2018; He et al. 2022). Microorganisms emit a large number of volatile compounds (VCs) with molecular masses of <300 Da that promote plant growth, nutrient acquisition, and photosynthesis (Zhang et al. 2008, 2009; Sánchez-López et al. 2016; García-Gómez et al. 2020). These compounds may cause massive lateral root (LR) formation and RH growth, thus potentially improving the root's capacity to explore for water and minerals and host microorganisms (Gutiérrez-Luna et al. 2010; Garnica-Vergara et al. 2016; García-Gómez et al. 2019, 2020; Verbon et al. 2023). Using a “box-in-box” cocultivation system in which plants are grown in the vicinity of microbial cultures covered with charcoal filters, we have recently shown that VCs with molecular masses of <45 Da (hereinafter designated as “small VCs”) from the fungal phytopathogen Penicillium aurantiogriseum enhance plant growth and photosynthesis and promote changes in root system architecture (RSA), including LR proliferation and shortening, and proliferation and “hyper-elongation” of RHs on LRs (García-Gómez et al. 2019, 2020). These morphological changes involved ethylene, auxin, and enhanced photosynthesis signaling and were associated with enhanced ROS accumulation due to upregulated expression of enzymes involved in ROS production at both transcript and protein levels (García-Gómez et al. 2020).

A striking change in the proteome of roots of small fungal VC-treated adult plants involved strong upregulation of RALF22 (At3g05490) (García-Gómez et al. 2020). RALF22 accumulates mainly in roots (https://pax-db.org/protein/3702/AT3G05490.1), participates with FERONIA in plant immunity (He et al. 2023), has structural and signaling roles in RH cell wall assembly (Schoenaers et al. 2024), and is involved in the transduction of cell wall signals to regulate plant growth and salt stress tolerance (Zhao et al. 2018). To investigate the possible involvement of RALF22 in the ethylene, auxin, and enhanced photosynthesis signaling-mediated RSA changes promoted by small fungal VCs, in this work we characterized the LR and RH growth responses to fungal VCs of ralf22 and feronia (fer-4) plants impaired in RALF22 and FERONIA expression, respectively. Furthermore, we characterized the response to fungal VCs of RALF22 transcript levels in roots of ethylene- and auxin-insensitive mutants and roots of a mutant defective in photosynthetic responsiveness to small fungal VCs, and those of ethylene-responsive transcripts involved in RH formation and elongation in ralf22 and fer-4 roots. Moreover, we characterized the RSA response of plants exposed to VCs emitted by ΔefeA strains of P. aurantiogriseum impaired in ethylene biosynthesis. Collectively, our findings showed that RALF22 is a key determinant of the RH ethylene perception and the ethylene, auxin, and photosynthesis signaling-mediated RH proliferation and elongation responses to fungal ethylene emissions.

Results

RALF22 and FERONIA are required for the fungal VC-promoted proliferation and hyper-elongation responses of RHs

In the absence of small fungal VCs, ralf22-2 and fer-4 plants exhibited similar to wild-type (WT) rosette and root growth (Supplementary Fig. S1) and LR number and length phenotypes (Fig. 1, A and B, Supplementary Fig. S2). RHs on primary roots (PRs) and LRs of fer-4 plants were shorter and less abundant than on WT LRs (Fig. 1C), whereas those of ralf22-1 and ralf22-2 plants exhibited a size and abundance similar to WT (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Fig. S3). Small fungal VCs promoted a similar to WT rosette and root growth in ralf22-1 and ralf22-2 plants, and their effect in fer-4 plants was weaker than in WT plants (Supplementary Fig. S1). Fungal VCs also promoted LR proliferation and shortening similar to the WT in ralf22-1, ralf22-2, and fer-4 plants (Fig. 1B, Supplementary Fig. S2), strongly indicating that RALF22 and FERONIA do not mediate the LR response to fungal VCs. Notably, unlike in WT plants and ralf1 and ralf34-1 knockout mutants with null expression of RALF peptides closely related to RALF22, small fungal VCs weakly promoted RH proliferation and elongation on PRs and LRs in ralf22-1, ralf22-2, and fer-4 plants (Fig. 1C, Supplementary Fig. S2, Supplementary Fig. S3). The weak response of ralf22-2 RHs to fungal VCs could be restored to WT by the ectopic expression of RALF22 under the control of the RALF22 promoter (Supplementary Fig. S3). We next conducted confocal microscope analyses of promRALF22::mCherry-RALF22mature plants expressing mCherry-tagged mature RALF22. In conformity with Schoenaers et al. (2024), these plants accumulated mCherry-RALF22 in RHs (Fig. 1D). Furthermore, small fungal VCs promoted strong accumulation of mCherry-RALF22 in RHs (Fig. 1D).

The ethylene and auxin signaling-mediated RH growth response to small fungal VCs involves RALF22

Small fungal VC-promoted RH proliferation and hyper-elongation involve ethylene and auxin signaling (García-Gómez et al. 2020). The promoter region of RALF22 contains several putative cis-elements for key transcription factors of the ethylene signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. S4), suggesting a molecular link between ethylene signaling and transcriptional control of RH development. To investigate the possible involvement of RALF22 in the ethylene and auxin signaling-mediated RH growth responses to fungal VCs, we first analyzed by RT-qPCR the levels of RALF22 transcripts and those of other RALF genes in roots of WT plants cultured in the absence or presence of fungal VCs. We also analyzed the expression of RALF22 transcripts in the roots of the etr1-3 and ein2-5 ethylene-insensitive mutants, the auxin-insensitive aux1-T mutant, and the eir1 ethylene-insensitive and auxin efflux carrier mutant. In addition, because FERONIA has been suggested to be indirectly involved in ethylene responsiveness in hypocotyls (Deslauriers and Larsen 2010), we characterized the RALF22 expression response of fer-4 roots to fungal VCs. Furthermore, we measured the levels of transcripts encoding proteins involved in ethylene perception (e.g. ETR1, ETR2, ERS1, and ERS2) and downstream signaling (e.g. EIN2, EIN3, and CTR1), responsiveness (e.g. ERF1 and ERF4) and synthesis (e.g. CAS-C1 and ACO2) in roots of WT and ralf22-2 plants cultured with/without fungal VCs. Moreover, we characterized the response of RHs of WT, ralf22, and fer-4 plants to exogenous application of the ethylene precursor aminocyclopropane carboxylic acid (ACC) and the auxin indole-3-acetic acid (IAA).

As shown in Fig. 2A, exposure to small fungal VCs enhanced the RALF22 transcript levels, but strongly reduced or did not alter those of other RALF genes. Furthermore, this treatment enhanced RALF22 expression in WT roots, but not in etr1-3, ein2-5, aux1-T, eir1, and fer-4 roots (Fig. 2B), and promoted changes similar to those in the WT in the expression of ETR1, ETR2, ERS1, CTR1, EIN2, EIN3, CAS-C1, ACO2, ERF1, and ERF4 in ralf22-2 roots (Supplementary Fig. S5). Moreover, exposure to small fungal VCs enhanced the ERS2 transcript levels in WT roots, but not in ralf22-2 and fer-4 roots (Fig. 2C). This indicated that ERS2 could be involved, at least in part, in the RALF22-mediated RH response to fungal VCs. In conformity with this idea, we found that the RH proliferation and elongation responses of ers2-1 plants were weaker than those of WT plants (Fig. 2D). As expected, exogenous ACC and IAA application promoted the elongation of RHs in WT plants (Fig. 2E). However, these compounds exerted a weak effect on RH length in ralf22 and fer-4 plants (Fig. 2E).

The small fungal VC-promoted upregulation of RH-related ROS biosynthesis enzymes is RALF22- and FERONIA-dependent

The RH proliferation and elongation responses to small fungal VCs are ROS (e.g. O2− and H2O2)-dependent processes that are associated with enhanced levels and activities of the NADPH oxidase RHD2 and class III apoplastic peroxidases (e.g. PRX1, PRX38, and PRX44) (García-Gómez et al. 2020). Some of these functions are required for RH growth (Foreman et al. 2003; Martin et al. 2022; Marzol et al. 2022) and are transcriptionally regulated by the RHD6 transcription factor required for RH initiation and the RH length-determining, ethylene- and the auxin-responsive ROOT HAIR DEFECTIVE 6-LIKE 2 and 4 (RSL2 and RSL4, respectively) transcription factors (Yi et al. 2010; Song et al. 2016; Mangano et al. 2017; Feng et al. 2017). We hypothesized that the RH responses to fungal VCs involve, at least in part, RALF22- and FERONIA-mediated upregulation of RSL2, RSL4, RHD2, RHD6, and PRX expression. To test this idea, we analyzed by confocal microscopy the GREEN FLUORESCENT PROTEIN (GFP) fluorescence response to small fungal VCs of transcriptional promPRX44:GFP reporter plants. We also analyzed by RT-qPCR the response to small fungal VCs of RSL2, RSL4, RHD2, RHD6, PRX1, and PRX44 transcript levels in roots of WT, ralf22-2, and fer-4 plants. Furthermore, we characterized the RH growth and RALF22 expression responses to fungal VCs of the hairless rhd6-1 and rsl2-1rsl4-1 mutants. As shown in Fig. 3A, small fungal VC exposure enhanced the GFP fluorescence in the RHs of promPRX44:GFP. This treatment upregulated RHD6 expression in both WT and ralf22 roots (Fig. 3B), strongly indicating that the RALF22-mediated RH response to fungal VCs does not involve RHD6 action. In line with this presumption, small fungal VCs promoted a similar to WT RH elongation in rhd6-1 plants (Fig. 3C). Exposure to small fungal VCs strongly enhanced the levels of RSL2, RSL4, RHD2, PRX1, and PRX44 transcripts in WT roots, but not in ralf22-2 and fer-4 roots (Fig. 3B). This indicated that RSL2 and RSL4 could be involved in the RALF22-mediated RH response to fungal VCs. In conformity with this idea, we found that rsl2-1rsl4-1 plants were unresponsive in terms of RH elongation (Fig. 3C) and RALF22 expression (Fig. 3D) to fungal VCs.

Small VCs emitted by P. aurantiogriseum strains impaired in ethylene biosynthesis weakly promote RH proliferation and elongation

Several factors strongly indicated that the RALF22- and FERONIA-dependent RH growth response to small P. aurantiogriseum VCs could be triggered, at least partly, by fungal ethylene emissions, namely: (i) the molecular mass of ethylene is 28.05 g/mol and thus can be considered as a “small VC”; (ii) some root morphological changes promoted by fungal VCs, including root shortening and RH proliferation and elongation (Fig. 1), are similar to those observed in plants exposed to exogenously supplied ethylene (Pitts et al. 1998; Negi et al. 2008; Street et al. 2015) or ethylene released by some fungal species (Splivallo et al. 2009; Contreras-Cornejo et al. 2015); (iii) small fungal VCs enhance the expression of ethylene-inducible genes including RSL2, RSL4, PRX1, PRX44, RHD2, ERS1, ERS2, CAS-C1, ACO2, ERF1, and ERF4 (Fig. 2C, Fig. 3, Supplementary Fig. S5); and (iv) RSA and RALF22 expression in roots of the etr1-3 and ers2-1 mutants impaired in the expression of ethylene receptors weakly responded to P. aurantiogriseum VCs (García-Gómez et al. 2020) (Fig. 2, B and C). To test this hypothesis, we analyzed GUS expression in roots of plants harboring the ethylene-inducible EBS:GUS marker cultured in the absence or presence of adjacent cultures of P. aurantiogriseum. Furthermore, we produced ΔefeA strains of P. aurantiogriseum with null ethylene-forming enzyme (EFE) expression (Supplementary Fig. S6) and characterized root responses to VCs emitted by these fungi. As shown in Fig. 4A, roots of EBS:GUS plants grown with adjacent fungal cultures showed greater GUS expression in vascular tissues and RHs than controls, strongly indicating that P. aurantiogriseum emits ethylene. In line with this idea, ethylene concentration in the headspace of growth boxes containing WT fungal cultures reached values of ca. 0.15 parts per million, which were exceedingly higher than those of growth boxes containing ΔefeA cultures (Fig. 4B). This provided strong evidence that P. aurantiogriseum produces ethylene mainly through mechanisms involving EFE action. Like WT P. aurantiogriseum cultures, small VCs of ΔefeA cultures promoted rosette and root growth (Supplementary Fig. S7, A and B). Notably, compared with small VCs of WT P. aurantiogriseum cultures, those of ΔefeA cultures weakly promoted LR proliferation and shortening (Supplementary Fig. S7C) and RH proliferation and elongation (Fig. 4, C and D).

RHs of plants defective in photosynthetic responsiveness to VCs weakly respond to small fungal VCs

Fungal VCs enhance photosynthesis and soluble sugar content in leaves (Sánchez-López et al. 2016; García-Gómez et al. 2019). Because exogenously supplied sugars promote RH proliferation and elongation (Jain et al. 2007; Mishra et al. 2009; Karve et al. 2012), we hypothesized that the RALF22- and FERONIA-mediated RH growth responses to small fungal VCs are due, at least in part, to enhanced signaling of photosynthates (mainly sugars) imported from leaves. To test this hypothesis, we analyzed by RT-qPCR the response to small fungal VCs of RALF22 transcript levels in roots of cfbp1 plants defective in photosynthetic responsiveness to VCs (Ameztoy et al. 2021) cultured with or without supplementary sucrose. Moreover, we characterized the RH growth responses of cfbp1, ralf22-2, and fer-4 plants to fungal VCs and sucrose.

Without sucrose supplementation, small fungal VC exposure strongly enhanced RALF22 transcript levels in WT roots, but did so weakly in cfbp1 roots (Fig. 5A). Sucrose supplementation enhanced RALF22 expression in roots of WT and cfbp1 plants, especially when exposed to fungal VCs (Fig. 5A). In WT and cfbp1 plants not exposed to fungal VCs, sucrose supplementation promoted RH elongation and proliferation (Fig. 5, B and C, Supplementary Fig. S8), but had no effect on ralf22 and fer-4 plants (Supplementary Fig. S9). Without sucrose supplementation, the RH growth responses of cfbp1 plants to small fungal VCs were weaker than those of WT plants (Fig. 5, B and C, Supplementary Fig. S8). Notably, unlike in ralf22-2 and fer-4 plants, fungal VCs strongly promoted RH proliferation and elongation in cfbp1 plants cultured in a sucrose-containing medium (Fig. 5, B and C, Supplementary Fig. S9).

Discussion

RALF22, but not other members of the RALF family, and FERONIA are involved in the fungal VC-promoted RH proliferation and elongation responses

Results presented in this work showing that RHs of ralf22 plants weakly respond to fungal VCs (Fig. 1) apparently conflict with previous studies showing that (i) exogenous application of different RALFs slightly promotes RH elongation (Gjetting et al. 2020; also confirmed in our lab) and (ii) RALF1 mediates RH development in PRs of young seedlings (Du et al. 2016; Zhu et al. 2020). However, unlike RALF22, fungal VCs did not enhance the protein content of other members of the RALF family in roots (cf. Supplemental Table 1 in García-Gómez et al. 2020). Furthermore, fungal VCs enhanced the RALF22 transcript levels in RHs but strongly reduced or did not alter those of other RALF genes (Fig. 1D, Fig. 2A). Moreover, the RH growth response of ralf1 and ralf34-1 plants to fungal VCs was similar to that of WT RHs (Supplementary Fig. S3). Collectively, our data provide strong evidence that (i) RALF22, but not other members of the RALF family, is an important transcriptionally regulated determinant of the fungal VC-promoted RH growth response and (ii) the RH growth response to fungal VCs involves mechanisms that prevent functional redundancy of RALF22 with other RALFs.

In the context of seedling and root growth, FERONIA is involved in the perception and/or signaling pathway of the majority of the RALF peptides (Abarca et al. 2021). In this work, we found that fer-4 mutant plants exhibited a similar to ralf22 weak responsiveness of RHs to fungal VCs (Fig. 1), which strongly indicates that the RALF22-dependent RH growth response to fungal VCs involves FERONIA (Fig. 6). This idea is consistent with Schoenaers et al. (2024) who showed that, besides its capacity to bind LEUCINE REPEAT EXTENSIN proteins to form a complex essential for RH cell wall integrity, RALF22 induces the formation of a complex with LORELEI-LIKE-GPI-ANCHORED PROTEIN 1 and FERONIA. Notably, fungal VCs did not enhance RALF22 transcript levels in fer-4 plants (Fig. 2B). It thus appears that, in addition to its role in the perception and/or signaling of RALF22, FERONIA may play a role in fine-tuning transcriptional feedback regulation of RALF22 expression in response to fungal VCs (Fig. 6).

Suggested hypothetical model for RALF22-driven regulation of the RH growth response to fungal ethylene emissions. According to this model, RH proliferation and elongation responses to fungal VCs are initiated by the binding of fungal ethylene to membrane-bound receptors and the incorporation of photosynthetically produced sugars (mainly sucrose) into the RH. Downstream ethylene and sugar signaling mechanisms transcriptionally upregulate the expression of RALF22, RSL2, and RSL4. FERONIA modulates the expression of RSL2 and RSL4, which in turn modulate the transcription of RH-related genes including those encoding RHD2, PRX1, and PRX44 involved in the apoplastic ROS production for RH elongation. The RALF22-dependent RH proliferation and elongation responses to fungal VCs are mainly triggered by fungal ethylene emissions

RSA changes promoted by small VCs emitted by P. aurantiogriseum including root shortening and RH proliferation and elongation are similar to those triggered by exogenous ethylene application (Pitts et al. 1998; Negi et al. 2008; Street et al. 2015). In this work, we found that the RH and LR growth responses promoted by VCs of ΔefeA P. aurantiogriseum cultures impaired in ethylene biosynthesis were weaker than those promoted by VCs of WT P. aurantiogriseum cultures (Fig. 4, Supplementary Fig. S7). Therefore, both the RALF22-dependent RH proliferation and elongation and the RALF22-independent LR proliferation responses to fungal VCs are triggered, at least partly, by fungal ethylene emissions. In addition to ethylene, P. aurantiogriseum emits small VCs including carbon monoxide, nitric oxide, and hydrogen sulfide (García-Gómez et al. 2019). Some of these compounds are very reactive with proteins and are known to act as signaling molecules that promote RH proliferation and elongation when exogenously applied in low concentrations (Guo et al. 2009; Liu et al. 2017; Li et al. 2018; Lombardo and Lamattina 2018; for a review, see Gámez-Arcas et al. 2022). Therefore, it is likely that part of the RH response to P. aurantiogriseum volatile emissions is not solely due to ethylene, but also to other small VCs such as carbon monoxide, nitric oxide, and hydrogen sulfide.

RALF22 mediates the ethylene- and auxin signaling-driven RH growth responses to fungal ethylene emissions

Ethylene signaling increases ROS accumulation to drive RH initiation and elongation through mechanisms involving transcriptional activation of RSL4- and RSL2-mediated upregulation of class III PRXs and NADPH oxidases (Song et al. 2016; Feng et al. 2017; Mangano et al. 2017; Martin et al. 2022). Some of these ROS-producing enzymes (e.g. PRX1, PRX38, PRX44, and RHD2) are strongly upregulated by P. aurantiogriseum small VCs (cf. Supplemental Table 1 in García-Gómez et al. 2020). In a previous work, we demonstrated that the RH growth response to fungal VCs is dependent on ethylene and auxin signaling and ROS, as RHs of etr1-3, aux1-T, eir1, and rhd2 plants weakly responded to fungal VCs (García-Gómez et al. 2020). Several factors indicate that the ethylene and auxin signaling- and ROS-driven RH growth responses to fungal ethylene emissions are mediated by RALF22 and FERONIA as schematically illustrated in Fig. 6. First, ethylene-containing fungal VCs enhanced RALF22 and PRX44 expression in RHs (Fig. 1D, Fig. 3A). Second, the promoter region of RALF22 contains several putative cis-elements for key transcription factors of the ethylene signaling pathway (Supplementary Fig. S4). Third, ethylene promotes RH development through mechanisms involving modulation of auxin distribution in epidermis of root tip (Rahman et al. 2002; Chen et al. 2022). Fourth, unlike in WT roots, fungal VCs did not enhance RALF22 transcript levels in roots of the etr1-3 and ein2-5 ethylene-insensitive mutants and the eir1 and aux1-T auxin-insensitive mutants (Fig. 2B). Fifth, the application of ethylene-containing fungal VCs upregulated the levels of ethylene-inducible and RH-related RSL2, RSL4, RHD2, PRX1 and PRX44 transcripts in roots of WT plants, but not in ralf22-2 and fer-4 roots (Fig. 3B). Sixth, exogenous ACC and IAA applications promoted the elongation of RHs in WT plants, but not in ralf22-2 and fer-4 plants (Fig. 2E).

rsl2-1rsl4-1 plants were unresponsive in terms of RH elongation to fungal VCs (Fig. 3C). This strongly indicated that the RALF22- and FERONIA-dependent, ethylene- and auxin-signaling- and ROS-driven response to fungal ethylene emissions is mediated by RSL2 and RSL4 (Fig. 6). RSL4 expression is transcriptionally regulated by RHD6 (Yi et al. 2010). However, unlike rsl2-1rsl4-1 plants, RHs of rhd6-1 plants exhibited a WT-like growth response to fungal VCs (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, unlike in ralf22 and fer-4 plants, exogenous application of ACC and auxin promotes RH elongation in rhd6-1 plants (Yi et al. 2010). Therefore, the RSL2 and RSL4-mediated RH growth response to fungal ethylene emissions is regulated by RALF22 and FERONIA, but not by RHD6. FERONIA positively regulates RSL4 expression (Zhu et al. 2020; Lu et al. 2024). The facts that, unlike in WT roots, ethylene-containing fungal VCs did not enhance the levels of RSL4 and RSL2 transcripts in ralf22-2 and fer-4 mutants (Fig. 3B), and those of RALF22 in rsl2-1rsl4-1 plants (Fig. 3D) strongly indicated that RSL4 and RSL2 expression in fungal VC-exposed roots is subject to transcriptional regulation by RALF22 and FERONIA through mechanisms involving a positive feedback loop as schematically illustrated in Fig. 6.

RALF22 and FERONIA positively regulate the perception of fungal ethylene emissions

ERS2 is one member of the ethylene receptor family (Hua et al. 1998), which interacts with other proteins involved in ethylene signaling and activates the dominant ethylene receptor (ETR1) (Gao et al. 2008; Berleth et al. 2019; https://thebiogrid.org/24784/summary/arabidopsis-thaliana/ers2.html). ERS2 also interacts with RHS2, a RH-specific protein required for RH growth (Won et al. 2009). Unlike in WT roots, fungal VCs did not enhance ERS2 transcript levels in roots of ralf22-2 and fer-4 plants (Fig. 2C). Furthermore, this treatment weakly promoted RH proliferation and elongation responses in ers2-1 plants (Fig. 2D). These data show that (i) ERS2 is an important determinant of the RALF22-mediated RH response to fungal ethylene emissions and (ii) ERS2-mediated fungal ethylene perception is positively and transcriptionally regulated by RALF22 and FERONIA in RHs (Fig. 6). The discovery that RALF22 positively modulates ERS2 expression suggests that plants have evolved RALF22-dependent mechanisms to ensure efficient fungal ethylene perception and RH growth to prepare the plant to host the microorganism, raising questions regarding the evolution of those mechanisms and their ecological importance.

Sugar-mediated enhanced photosynthesis signaling is an important determinant of the RH response to fungal VCs mediated by RALF22

Soluble sugars photosynthetically produced in leaves act as hormone-like signaling molecules that are transported to roots to support and adjust growth and development according to the shoot demand (Freixes et al. 2002; Tong et al. 2022). Fungal VCs increase photosynthetic activity and soluble sugar content (Sánchez-López et al. 2016; García-Gómez et al. 2019). Here, we provided strong evidence that the RALF22-mediated RH proliferation and hyper-elongation responses to small fungal VCs involve, at least partly, sugar-mediated signaling of enhanced photosynthesis. First, fungal VC-promoted enhancement of RALF22 transcripts in roots of cfbp1 plants cultured without sucrose supplementation was much weaker than in roots of WT plants (Fig. 5A). Second, sucrose supplementation enhanced RALF22 expression in WT and cfbp1 roots, especially in VC-exposed plants (Fig. 5A). Third, the RH proliferation and elongation responses to fungal VCs of cfbp1 plants cultured without sucrose were weaker than those of WT plants (Fig. 5, B and C). The weak responses of cfbp1 RHs to fungal VCs could be reverted to being much more similar to the WT by exogenous sucrose supplementation (Fig. 5, B and C). Fourth, in the absence of fungal VCs, sucrose supplementation promoted a ca. three-fold increase of RH length in WT plants, but not in ralf22-2 plants (Supplementary Fig. S9).

In roots of plants not exposed to ethylene-containing fungal VCs, sucrose supplementation promoted a strong enhancement of RALF22 transcript levels (Fig. 5A), but weak RH proliferation and elongation responses when comparing with roots of plants exposed to fungal VCs (Fig. 5, B and C). This indicated that enhanced RALF22 transcription promoted by sugar feeding is not the sole important player in the fungal VC-promoted RH growth responses. Ethylene synthesis is enhanced by sucrose feeding (Seneweera et al. 2003; Jeong et al. 2010). Under conditions of elevated photosynthetic CO2 fixation, ethylene and sugar production increases (Dhawan et al. 1981; Pan et al. 2019; Smet et al. 2020; Ahammed and Li 2022) and RHs elongate (Wang et al. 2009; Niu et al. 2011). Therefore, although sugars and ethylene often act in an antagonistic manner to each other (Yanagisawa et al. 2003; Price et al. 2004; Karve et al. 2012; Tong et al. 2022), we hypothesize that the RALF22- and FERNONIA-mediated response of RHs to fungal VCs is due, at least partly, to the synergistic actions of signaling of enhanced photosynthetic production of sugars and of fungal ethylene (Fig. 6).

RHs of ralf22 plants not exposed to fungal VCs develop RHs with sizes similar to those of WT plants (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. S3), strongly indicating that, under noninduced conditions, RALF22 is not a key determinant of RH development. This finding apparently conflicts with Schoenaers et al. (2024) who showed that ralf22 RHs are shorter than those of WT plants. However, Schoenaers et al. (2024) conducted their work using plants cultured in a sucrose-containing medium. This sugar promotes RH growth when exogenously supplied to plants (Jain et al. 2007; Mishra et al. 2009; Karve et al. 2012) (Supplementary Fig. S9) and upregulates RALF22 expression (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, the RH proliferation and elongation responses to exogenously supplied sucrose are RALF22- and FERONIA-dependent (Supplementary Fig. S9). This would explain why, under the culture conditions used by Schoenaers et al. (2024), but not under the conditions used in our study, RHs of ralf22 plants are shorter than those of WT plants.

Materials and methods

Fungal cultures and growth conditions

P. aurantiogriseum (CECT 20226) was cultured in small Petri dishes (35 × 10 mm, Sarstedt, Ref. 82.1135.500) containing solid MS (Phytotechlab M519) medium supplemented with 90 mm sucrose. ΔefeA P. aurantiogriseum strains lacking the entire ORF of the EFE-encoding efeA gene were produced using the OSCAR method (Paz et al. 2011). The deletion construct was generated with the updated vector pOSCAR-HSVtk (Sarmiento-Villamil et al. 2020), which contains the HSVtk gene to prevent ectopic integration events of the T-DNA that do not result in gene deletion and 2 Kbp DNA homologous to each flanking region of efeA (Supplementary Fig. S10A). Agrobacterium tumefaciens-mediated transformation of P. aurantiogriseum was performed as described by Mullins et al. (2001) with minor variations (Supplementary Fig. S10B). Transformants were selected on PDA plates supplemented with 150 μg/mL hygromycin and 25 μg/mL 5-fluoro-29-deoxyuridine (5FU), for the positive selection of transformants and negative selection of those with ectopic integration of the T-DNA, respectively. Deletion of efeA was confirmed by PCR analysis (Supplementary Fig. S6). Primers used to amplify the efeA flanking sequences to generate the deletion construct, and to confirm gene deletion by PCR were designed using the genomic sequence of P. aurantiogriseum strain IBT356559 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCA_019977855.1/), and are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Plants and growth conditions

Plants used in this work are listed in Table 1. The pRALF22:RALF22 plasmid construct was produced using Gateway technology as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S11 and confirmed by sequencing. Primers used for PCR amplification of RALF22 and the RALF22 promoter are listed in Supplementary Table S2. The pRALF22:RALF22 plasmid construct was transferred to A. tumefaciens EHA105 cells by electroporation and utilized to transform Arabidopsis plants as described by Clough and Bent (1998). Plants were cultured in Petri dishes (92 × 16 mm, Ref. 82.1472.001, Sarstedt) containing sucrose-free half-strength MS medium in growth chambers providing “long-day” 16-h light (90 µmol photons sec−1 m−2), 22 °C/8-h dark, 18 °C cycles.

Cocultivation assays and sampling

Effects of small fungal VCs on plants were investigated using the “plasticized PVC wrap and charcoal filter-based box-in-box” cocultivation system described in García-Gómez et al. (2019) and Gámez-Arcas et al. (2022). Because fungal VCs exert a negative effect on growth of very young seedlings (Sánchez-López et al. 2016), we used mature plants cultured in vitro on sucrose-free medium. Briefly, cultures of 14 d after sowing plants and fungal cultures in unlidded Petri dishes were placed in sterile plastic growth boxes sealed with PVC wrap. After 1 wk of cocultivation, roots were harvested, immediately freeze-clamped, and ground in liquid nitrogen.

Headspace analysis of ethylene content

For measurement of the ethylene contents in the plasticized PVC wrap-sealed growth boxes containing P. aurantiogriseum cultures, the sealed growth boxes were carefully drilled with a syringe. Ethylene content was measured using a HP 4890D Agilent Technologies gas chromatography instrument equipped with a flame ignition detector, two chromatography columns (HP-PLOT/Q column [19095P-04] and HP-MOLESIEVE [19095P-MS0]), and a PAL RSI 85 autosampler injection device. Helium was used as gas carrier (flow: 5 mL min−1; pressure: 11.065 psi) and the thermal conditions were 200 °C (injector), 40 °C (columns), and 250 °C (detector).

Root morphological analysis

RSA analyses were conducted as described in García-Gómez et al. (2020) using plants grown in vertical position in the absence or presence of adjacent P. aurantiogriseum cultures. After 7 d of cocultivation, the numbers and lengths of the plants’ LRs and RHs were measured using a ZEISS SteREO Discovery.V12 stereomicroscope (Zeiss, Germany). To measure RH length, photomicrographs in a region of 5 mm from the LR tips were captured employing an Axiocam 503 color camera (Zeiss, Germany). The digital images obtained were used for RH length measurement using the ImageJ software (http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/).

Real-time quantitative PCR

Transcript levels in roots of plants cultured on vertical plates were measured as described in García-Gómez et al. (2020) using the primers listed in Supplementary Table S3. Comparative threshold values were normalized to ACTIN2 internal control.

Confocal laser scanning microscopy

Roots of ralf22-2XpRALF22::mCherry-RALF22mature and Col-0 pPRX44:GFP plants were imaged using a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany). Filters for wavelength selection were used for the visualization of the following fluorophores (excitation/emission): mCherry (587 nm/550 to 650) and GFP (488 nm/500 to 854,533 nm). Image processing was performed using ZEN 3.4 (Zeiss, Germany).

GUS expression analysis

Expression of the GUS reporter gene was monitored using the histochemical staining assay described by Jefferson et al. (1987).

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise indicated, the data provided in this study are means (±SE) obtained from 3 to 4 independent experiments, with three biological replicates (each a pool of 4 to 12 plants) for each experiment. The significance of differences was statistically evaluated with Student's t-tests using the SPSS package. Differences were considered significant if P < 0.05.
