Detection of circulating tumor cells and microemboli in the blood of lung adenocarcinoma in situ patients case reports
Authors: Yubin Huang, Houlu Zhang, Daming Fan, Zerui Wang, Xuanzhe Qin, Xiaoming Sun, Liangming Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBased on the pathological characteristics of lung adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), conventional histopathological understanding suggests that non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells should not penetrate the basement membrane to enter systemic circulation. When cancer cells enter the blood circulation and form circulating tumor cells (CTCs) or even circulating tumor microemboli (CTM), they may trigger cancer metastasis. CASE PRESENTATION: In three AIS patients, we detected heterogeneous populations of CTCs and CTM in peripheral blood using the CTCBIOPSY® system-a finding that possibly contradicts established AIS pathomorphological criteria.ConclusionOur findings suggest that CTCs/CTM may possess occult metastatic potential, indicating a dormant risk of micrometastasis even in AIS. Therefore, we believe that CTCs/CTM can serve as essential preoperative examination indicators for evaluating the prognosis of lung cancer patients and determining their treatment plans.

Introduction

With the increasing implementation of comprehensive CT screening programs, a growing number of lung cancers—including adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS)—are being diagnosed and treated at early stages, leading to a marked decline in lung cancer mortality rates [1]. Histopathologically defined as a localized, non-invasive neoplasm, AIS exhibits tumor cells strictly confined to alveolar structures without breaching the basement membrane, and demonstrates absence of stromal, vascular, or pleural invasion [2]. Current clinical evidence indicates that the 5-year survival rate for AIS approaches 100%, and this pathological stage universally portends an excellent prognosis. Circulating tumor cells (CTCs) and their clusters, defined as malignant cells shed from primary tumors that intravasate into the bloodstream [3]. Current evidence recognizes CTCs as a validated biomarker for disease progression in lung cancer and other malignancies, with their detection associated with adverse clinical outcomes [4, 5]. Circulating tumor microemboli (CTM) is conventionally defined as cohesive aggregates of ≥ 3 tightly adherent circulating tumor cells [6]. Compared to single CTC, the presence of CTMs portends a higher risk of distant metastasis [6]. This enhanced aggressiveness is further amplified through the formation of CTC-neutrophil clusters, which synergistically promote vascular extravasation and metastatic niche colonization via neutrophil-derived proteases (e.g., MMP-9) and cytokine signaling (IL-6/IL-8) [7, 8]. While AIS patients may harbor sparse CTCs in peripheral blood, no published evidence exists to date confirming the presence of CTMs in AIS. However, we detected CTCs/CTMs in three pathologically confirmed AIS cases—a finding that may have significant implications for refining prognostic assessment in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma management.

Case report

Respectively, there were three patients who were found to have pulmonary nodules measuring 10 × 7 mm, 6 × 5 mm, and 6 × 5 mm in size (Figs. 1a, 2a and 3a). All of them presented with the chief complaint of having detected pulmonary nodules several months prior. The patients had no history of alcohol abuse, no smoking history, no family history of genetic diseases, or a previous history of malignant tumors. Before the surgery, we collected 5 mL of peripheral venous blood samples from each patient. CTC isolation was carried out using the CTCBIOPSY® device (Wuhan YZY Medical Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) [9]. This system employs positive pressure to drive blood samples through an 8-µm pore filter membrane, effectively retaining CTCs/CTMs, leukocytes, and other large-diameter cells while allowing the passage of erythrocytes. Following Wright’s staining of cells, subsequent microscopic identification of CTCs requires concurrent satisfaction of three morphological criteria: (1) cell diameter > 16 μm, (2) nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio > 0.8, and (3) nuclear atypia [10, 11]. Furthermore, CTM is defined as cohesive aggregates comprising ≥ 3 tightly adherent CTCs [12]. Subsequently, all patients underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic pulmonary wedge resection under general endotracheal anesthesia. Intraoperative frozen section analysis confirmed the diagnosis of AIS, and no lymph node dissection was performed. The final pathological examinations all indicated AIS (Figs. 1b, 2b and 3b), with tumor-free surgical margins (1.5 cm away from the resection edge). Peripheral blood analysis revealed the presence of multiple CTCs and CTMs (Figs. 1c and d, 2c and d and 3c and d), with quantitative data detailed in Table 1. The patient has now completed over 5 years of postoperative surveillance with no clinical or radiological evidence of tumor recurrence, as confirmed by serial CT assessments per institutional follow-up protocols.

Discussion

Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), constituting 40% of NSCLC, represents the most prevalent histologic subtype [13]. AIS, classified as the earliest stage in the ADC continuum, is pathologically defined by neoplastic cells strictly confined to native alveolar structures with lepidic growth pattern, absence of stromal, vascular, or pleural invasion, and consequently demonstrates exceptional prognosis [14]. Current evidence from the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) database confirms a 100% 5-year survival rate following complete surgical resection of AIS lesions [15]. Notably, some authorities contend that AIS should not be categorized as conventional malignancy given its non-invasive biological behavior [16].

CTCs and CTM are widely regarded as pivotal mediators of cancer dissemination, originating from primary tumor shedding into the vasculature and facilitating distant metastatic spread. Mechanistically, a subset of CTCs demonstrates direct metastatic competency through active vascular intravasion, while a minor population may establish dormancy as disseminated tumor cells (DTCs) in distant niches [17]. In clinical practice, CTCs and CTM enumeration serve as prognostic biomarkers for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [18, 19]. Moreover, they constitute independent determinants of progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) in stage IV NSCLC, as validated by multivariate Cox regression analyses [20]. Notably, CTCs involvement in oncogenesis may precede clinical manifestations of advanced disease [21, 22]. Seminal work by Ilona Krol et al. has documented CTCs presence in early-stage breast cancer patients, indicating their involvement in disease progression during initial oncogenesis [23]. The presence of CTCs in healthy individuals or those with non-malignant conditions is exceedingly rare. A seminal study has demonstrated that CTCs can be detected in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) patients prior to the clinical manifestation of early stage NSCLC, indicating their potential as pre-diagnostic biomarkers [24]. CTM is referred to as circulating tumor microemboli, which are defined as groups of tumor cells (comprising more than two or three cells) moving together in the bloodstream [25]. Compared with CTCs, CTMs are more likely to resist initial cell death after homing to distant organs, thereby promoting metastasis [26]. This establishes that CTCs and CTMs are pathognomonic of malignant processes and should not be present in individuals without active neoplasia. The roles of CTCs, particularly CTMs, in the development of early-stage lung cancer, especially in pre-invasive lesions such as AIS, have not been fully explored in peer-reviewed literature.

We detected CTCs and CTMs in preoperative blood samples from three AIS patients. Crucially, these specimens were obtained prior to surgical intervention to eliminate potential confounding from iatrogenic tumor cell displacement. Comprehensive preoperative diagnostic workup, also excluded concomitant malignancies in all three patients. We have discovered the presence of CTCs and CTMs in patients with AIS. All three patients underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic wedge resection, a strategy supported by national comprehensive cancer network (NCCN) guidelines for pure ground-glass opacity nodules ≤ 2 cm, which demonstrates equivalent 5-year survival rates compared to lobectomy [27, 28]. To our knowledge, this represents the first documented evidence of CTMs in histologically confirmed AIS, contradicting its characterization as a ‘non-metastasizing’ entity. Intriguingly, in these three cases we observed that the larger tumors yielded higher numbers of detectable circulating cells as detailed in Table 1. Although statistical significance was not reached due to limited sample size, this trend aligns with established evidence demonstrating that CTCs and CTM enumeration correlates proportionally with primary tumor burden in LUAD [29].

The biological implications of CTCs/CTM detection in these cases remain enigmatic. Current evidence demonstrates that CTCs/CTM enumeration correlates positively with tumor size in LUAD, wherein larger tumors yield higher CTCs counts. Moreover, CTCs levels in LUAD patients increase proportionally with EGFR mutation burden, as quantified by digital PCR [29, 30]. Preoperative profiling of hotspot mutations (BRAF, KRAS, EGFR, and PIK3CA) in CTCs provides clinically actionable guidance for predicting tumor recurrence [31]. All three patients have undergone structured postoperative surveillance with no current evidence of locoregional recurrence or distant metastasis. However, the absence of short-term recurrence does not preclude the possibility of late metastatic progression, particularly considering the documented capacity of CTCs/CTMs for prolonged dormancy [32]. Consequently, it remains uncertain whether tumor recurrence will occur in the future. These cases illuminate a gap in early lung cancer biology: the potential for pre-metastatic niche formation during histologically “indolent” stages, necessitating urgent investigation into CTC cluster-targeted therapies to intercept micrometastatic seeding.

However, our study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Although Wright’s staining allows for assessment of cytomorphologic features and serves as a feasible initial screening method, its specificity is limited [33, 34]. Definitive immunophenotypic verification requires specific detection of epithelial markers (e.g., CK8/18/19) and leukocyte markers (CD45) through immunofluorescence (IF) or immunocytochemistry (ICC) staining [35, 36]. In subsequent investigations, unequivocal confirmation of circulating tumor cell identity through multiparameter immunofluorescence profiling represents a prerequisite for CTC/CTM research.

Summary

Our findings provide evidence of CTCs/CTM in AIS patients, challenging the paradigm of AIS as a strictly localized entity. The presence of CTCs/CTM may signify occult biological aggressiveness, potentially heralding adverse clinical outcomes. With advancing CTCs/CTM research, CTCs/CTM profiling could emerge as a critical preoperative biomarker for stratifying recurrence risk in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma, enabling precision surveillance and adjuvant intervention strategies.
