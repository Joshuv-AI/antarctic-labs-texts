Association of exercise type and levels with depressive symptoms in a Korean population
Authors: Jae Ho Park, Joong-Yeon Lim, Hyun-Young Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study examined whether the relationship between exercise and depressive symptoms varied by exercise type. It also explored how the duration of the training period and the weekly time spent on each exercise type were associated with depressive symptoms.MethodsData from 19,112 participants in nationwide Korean cohorts were analyzed. Based on their participation in regular exercise, participants were divided into the following groups: “never-exercise,” “leisure-time walking (performing at usual pace),” and “structured exercise.” The structured exercise group was further classified into “aerobic exercise,” “resistance exercise,” and “sports.” The training period and weekly exercise time for each group were analyzed to determine the optimal training level of exercise associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms. Multiple logistic regression models were used to estimate this risk.ResultsCompared with the never-exercise group, leisure-time walking, aerobic exercise, resistance exercise, and sports were associated with 19%, 41%, 40%, and 46% lower risks of depressive symptoms, respectively. Furthermore, aerobic exercise, resistance exercise, and sports were associated with 30%, 30%, and 41% reductions in risk, respectively, compared with leisure-time walking. When considering training levels, performing leisure-time walking, aerobic exercise, resistance exercise, and sports for at least 150 min/week for at least one year was associated with a risk reduction of 31%, 48%, 45%, and 57%, respectively, compared with the never-exercise group. Across all exercise groups, engaging in ≥ 300 min/week was not associated with additional benefits.ConclusionsWhile leisure-time walking was associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms, structured exercise provided greater benefits. However, engaging in any form of exercise for at least 150 min/week for at least one year is recommended to reduce this risk.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13102-025-01449-z.

Background

Depression is a serious mental health condition that increases the risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD), CVD-specific mortality, all-cause mortality, and even suicide attempts [1, 2]. Depressive symptoms refer to emotional, cognitive, and physical changes indicative of depression. Meta-analytical evidence suggests that individuals with subthreshold depressive symptoms—those below the diagnostic threshold for depression—have about two to three times the risk of incident depression compared with those without depressive symptoms [3, 4]. Early interventions to reduce the risk of depressive symptoms are therefore essential for preventing depression and related life-threatening conditions.

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends at least 150 min/week of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise to reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety [5]. A recent meta-analysis found that adults who met these recommendations had a 25% lower risk of depression than those reporting no activity [6]. However, whether low-intensity activities such as leisure-time walking are also associated with a reduced risk of depressive symptoms remains controversial. A recent meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) found that walking interventions carried out at a moderate intensity or guided pace significantly reduced depressive symptoms, whereas self-paced walking had no significant effect [7]. In contrast, another meta-analysis reported that the risk of developing depression decreased by approximately 9% for every 1,000-step increase in daily steps [8]. Given that the threshold for moderate-intensity walking speed is 5 km/h [9], whereas the speed for leisure-time walking, such as strolling, typically ranges from 3.2 to 4 km/h [10], it is necessary to further investigate whether this exercise type alone is sufficient to reduce the risk of depressive symptoms.

Although the WHO and the American College of Sports Medicine recommend resistance exercise training at least 2 days per week to improve musculoskeletal fitness and overall health [5, 11], its mental health benefits have been studied less extensively than those of aerobic exercise training. Previous meta-analytical evidence has shown a significant relationship between regular resistance exercise training and reduced depressive symptoms [12], but more recent research has reported mixed results. A recent epidemiological study reported no association between regular resistance exercise training and reduced risk of incident depression in middle-aged or older adults [13], while an RCT found no improvement in depressive symptoms after 24 weeks of resistance exercise training in older women [14]. Further studies are needed to clarify the relationship between these factors. In particular, research examining training levels (i.e., training period and time per week) is necessary to offer resistance exercise recommendations for depressive symptoms, similar to those established for aerobic exercise.

Because loneliness, social isolation, and reduced social participation are significantly linked to an increased risk of depressive symptoms [15, 16], sports participation, which involves social interactions, such as competition and cooperation, may provide greater mental health benefits than individual exercise [17]. However, most research on the direct associations between sports participation and depressive symptoms has focused primarily on adolescents. A recent meta-analysis found that adolescents who participated in sports reported significantly lower levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms than nonparticipants [18]. However, to strengthen current guidelines, it is important to determine whether sports participation also confers greater benefits for depressive symptoms in adults compared with other exercise types, such as leisure-time walking, aerobic exercise, and resistance exercise.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine whether the benefits of reducing depressive symptom risk vary by exercise type. Additionally, to support the evidence base for exercise-specific guidelines, we analyzed the relationship between the training period and the amount of time spent per week for each type of exercise and the risk of depressive symptoms.

Methods

Study participants

This was a population-based cross-sectional study designed to examine the associations between exercise type, exercise level, and depressive symptoms in Korean adults. Data were obtained from the Korean Genome and Epidemiology Study (KoGES), a large-scale consortium project comprising six prospective cohort studies that investigate the environmental and genetic etiologies of non-communicable diseases in Korea [19]. For this study, we used data from 2004 to 2013 from the KoGES Health Examinee study, which included 173,195 urban residents aged 40–79 years, as well as data from the seventh wave of the KoGES Ansan and Anseong study (2013–2014), which included 5,906 participants aged 50–82 years living in Ansan (an urban area) and Anseong (a rural area). Trained healthcare professionals conducted face-to-face surveys and physical examinations. Detailed information on these cohort studies has been reported previously [19].

Of the 179,101 participants, those with missing data on depressive symptoms (n = 149,177), exercise type or level (n = 9,359), or covariates (n = 447), as well as participants engaging in multiple exercise types (n = 1,006), were excluded. After these exclusions, 19,112 participants (11,539 women) remained for the final analysis (Additional file 1). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Institute of Health, Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency (approval no. KDCA-2024-02-12-R-04).

Measurement of exercise type and levels

The exercise type and level performed by the participants were measured using the Minnesota Leisure-Time Physical Activity Questionnaire, a self-report questionnaire [20]. The validity and reliability of this questionnaire have been well established in previous studies [21, 22]. Based on whether they engaged in regular exercise, they were divided into the following groups: “never-exercise (not performing exercise),” “leisure-time walking (performing at usual pace),” and “structured exercise.” Leisure-time walking was defined as reporting participation in walking or strolling. The structured exercise group was further classified based on the characteristics of the exercises performed into the following subgroups: “aerobic exercise,” “resistance exercise,” and “sports.” Aerobic exercise is defined as continuous and rhythmic activities that increase breathing and heart rate while engaging large muscle groups (e.g., brisk walking, jogging, running, cycling, swimming, dancing, or jump rope). Resistance exercise refers to any training program involving muscle contraction against external resistance, including body weight, weight machines, barbells, and dumbbells. Sports participation is a type of exercise that involves competing for victory under set rules, including ball games, racket sports, and combat sports.

To examine the presence of an inversely graded dose–response association between training levels and the risk of depressive symptoms, the training period and time per week for each exercise group were analyzed. Based on training time per week, participants were categorized into one of the following subgroups: “<150 min/week,” “150–299 min/week,” and “≥300 min/week.” According to the training period, participants were classified into the following subgroups: “<12 months” and “≥12 months.” As shown in Fig. 1, participants were also assigned to one of four subgroups based on the combination of training period and exercise time per week.

Definition of depressive symptoms

To assess depressive symptoms, the KoGES Health Examinee and KoGES Ansan and Anseong study used the Korean versions of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-K) and Geriatric Depression Scale-Short Form (SGDS-K), respectively. Both the CES-D-K and the SGDS-K are valid and reliable instruments for screening depressive symptoms in the Korean population [23, 24]. Depressive symptoms were defined as a score of ≥ 16 on the 20-item CES-D-K or a score of ≥ 6 on the 15-item SGDS-K [25, 26]. Participants were categorized into two groups, “non-depressive symptoms” (those without depressive symptoms) and “depressive symptoms” (those with depressive symptoms), for comparison.

Covariates

Sociodemographic and health-related factors, including age, sex, marital status, educational level, household income, drinking and smoking habits, body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, blood pressure (BP), hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and laboratory parameters, were included in our analyses. Marital status was categorized as “divorced/widowed/single” or “married/partnered.” Educational level was classified as elementary school or lower, middle school or high school, or college or higher. Household income was categorized as “<1,” “1–<2,” “2–<3,” “3–<4,” and “≥4” million KRW/month (million Korean won per month). Drinking and smoking habits were classified as “never,” “former,” or “current.”

Anthropometric data including height, body weight, and waist circumference were measured by trained healthcare professionals using standardized protocols. BMI was calculated as body weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2), expressed in kg/m2. Systolic BP (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP) were measured as the average of two readings from the arm with the highest SBP after the participant rested for 5 min in a seated position. Blood samples were collected for biochemical assays after 8 h of overnight fasting, and total cholesterol (T-Chol), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), triglyceride (TG), and fasting blood glucose (FBG) levels were measured. Hypertension was defined based on a previous physician diagnosis, current use of antihypertensive drugs, SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg. Diabetes mellitus was identified based on a previous physician diagnosis; current use of antidiabetic medications (insulin or oral hypoglycemic agents); FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL; glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5%; or 2-hour blood glucose ≥ 200 mg/dL in the oral glucose tolerance test. Detailed information on the biochemical analyses has been reported in a previous study [19].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, United States). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participants’ characteristics. Continuous and categorical variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and absolute frequencies and percentages (%), respectively. Chi-squared tests were used to determine intergroup differences in sex, marital status, educational level, household income, drinking and smoking habits, regular exercise, and the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and depressive symptoms. Independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to compare age, BMI, waist circumference, SBP, DBP, T-Chol, HDL-C, TG, and FBG levels between groups. When ANOVA revealed significant differences between groups, Scheffé’s test was used for post hoc comparisons.

Odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for depressive symptom prevalence were estimated using multiple logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, marital status, educational level, household income, drinking, smoking, waist circumference, T-Chol level, hypertension, and diabetes mellitus. Subgroup analyses were performed to investigate the relationship between performing leisure-time walking or structured exercise and the risk of depressive symptoms, considering age (< 65 vs. ≥65 years), sex (male vs. female), marital status (divorced/widowed/single vs. married/partnered), educational level (≤ middle school vs. ≥high school), household income (< 3 vs. ≥3 million KRW/month), current drinking habits (yes vs. no), smoking status (never vs. ever), hypertension status (yes vs. no), and diabetes mellitus status (yes vs. no). The p-value for interaction was estimated to assess the consistency of the associations across subgroups. All tests were two-tailed, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

This study included 19,112 participants (11,539 women). Table 1 shows participants’ characteristics according to their depressive symptom status. Compared with the non-depressive symptom group, the depressive symptom group had a significantly higher mean age and waist circumference, as well as greater proportions of females, individuals with low education level (≤ elementary school), low household income (< 1 million KRW/month), those living without a partner (divorced, widowed, or single), never smokers, never-exercisers, and those with hypertension or diabetes mellitus. Conversely, the depressive symptom group had lower T-Chol and HDL-C levels and a lower proportion of current drinkers.

Additional file 2 presents participants’ characteristics based on the exercise type. Compared with both the never-exercise group and the leisure-time walking group, the structured exercise group was younger and had a markedly lower proportion of females, individuals with low education level (≤ elementary school), low household income (< 1 million KRW/month), those living without a partner (divorced, widowed, or single), never drinkers, never smokers, and individuals with depressive symptoms, but had higher T-Chol and HDL-C. In addition, compared with the never-exercise group, participants in the structured exercise group had significantly lower waist circumference and DBP, but had higher FBG. However, compared with the leisure-time walking group, the structured exercise group had higher SBP and DBP but lower prevalence of hypertension and diabetes mellitus. Characteristics of participants in each structured exercise subgroup are presented in Additional file 3.

Table 2 shows the association between exercise type and the risk of depressive symptoms. Compared with the never-exercise group, performing leisure-time walking, aerobic exercise, resistance exercise, and sports was significantly associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms by 19% (p < 0.01), 41% (p < 0.0001), 40% (p < 0.001), and 46% (p < 0.001), respectively. Furthermore, all structured exercise types were associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms than leisure-time walking. Specifically, compared with the leisure-time walking group, aerobic exercise, resistance exercise, and sports were markedly associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms by 30% (p < 0.0001), 30% (p < 0.05), and 41% (p < 0.01), respectively.

Performing all types of exercise for at least 150 min/week was associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms (Table 3). Compared with the never-exercise group, only aerobic exercise performed for less than 150 min/week was associated with a 29% lower risk of depressive symptoms (p < 0.01). Performing leisure-time walking, aerobic exercise, resistance exercise, and sports for 150–299 min/week was associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms by 29% (p < 0.01), 43% (p < 0.0001), 43% (p < 0.05), and 55% (p < 0.05), respectively. Compared with the never-exercise group, performing leisure-time walking, aerobic exercise, resistance exercise, and sports for ≥ 300 min/week was associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms by 29% (p < 0.01), 47% (p < 0.0001), 41% (p < 0.01), and 54% (p < 0.01), respectively. After adjustment for covariates, exercising ≥ 300 min/week was not significantly associated with additional benefits compared to 150–299 min/week in leisure-time walking (OR = 1.02, 95% CI 0.75–1.38, p = 0.90), aerobic exercise (OR = 0.93, 95% CI 0.72–1.19, p = 0.55), resistance exercise (OR = 1.06, 95% CI 0.58–1.95, p = 0.84), and sports (OR = 1.02, 95% CI 0.40–2.55, p = 0.97).

Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine whether the association between leisure-time walking or structured exercise and the risk of depressive symptoms remained consistent across various subgroups, including age, sex, marital status, educational level, household income, current drinking habits, smoking status, hypertension status, and diabetes mellitus status. The significance of the relationship between leisure-time walking and reduced risk of depressive symptoms differed among some subgroups (Additional file 4). The protective benefit of engaging in leisure-time walking against depressive symptoms was significant only in participants aged ≥ 65 years (p < 0.01), females (p < 0.01), those with middle school education or lower (p < 0.01), non-drinkers (p < 0.01), never smokers (p < 0.001), those with hypertension (p < 0.01), and those without diabetes mellitus (p < 0.01). However, a significant interaction was observed only in the age subgroup (p for interaction < 0.01). The benefit of structured exercise in reducing the risk of depressive symptoms was consistent across all subgroups (Additional file 5). However, significant interactions were observed for two subgroups, and the beneficial association with lower depressive symptoms was greater in participants aged ≥ 65 years (p for interaction < 0.0001) and non-drinkers (p for interaction < 0.01).

Discussion

Our findings indicate that while leisure-time walking was associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms, structured exercise (i.e., aerobic exercise, resistance exercise, or sports) was associated with a 30% or greater reduction in risk compared to leisure-time walking. Importantly, regardless of exercise type, exercising for at least 150 min/week for one year or more was significantly associated with reduced risk. However, across all exercise types, engaging in 300 min/week or more was not associated with additional benefits. Taken together, engaging in any structured exercise for at least 150 min/week, sustained for at least one year, appears to be an effective strategy to help prevent depression.

Current guidelines recommend at least 150 min/week of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise training to reduce depressive symptoms [5]. A recent study reported that moderate-intensity walking, performed three times per week for 150 min/week, significantly reduced depressive symptoms [27]. However, the speed for typical leisure-time walking, such as strolling (3.2–4 km/h), does not meet the moderate-intensity threshold of 5 km/h [9, 10]. Thus, further research is required to clarify whether low-intensity exercise effectively reduces depressive symptoms. A recent meta-analysis reported that moderate-intensity walking markedly reduced depressive symptoms, whereas self-paced walking had no significant effect [7]. Notably, this finding did not consider training levels; therefore, further research is required to clarify this relationship. In our study, regular leisure-time walking was associated with a 19% lower risk compared with the never-exercise group, and when training levels were considered, a 31% reduction in the risk was observed among those who engaged in leisure-time walking for ≥ 150 min/week for at least one year. Research examining the relationship between long-term low-intensity leisure-time walking and depressive symptoms is scarce; therefore, our findings are meaningful. Supporting this, recent meta-analytical evidence indicates that walking 5,000 steps/day or more is associated with fewer depressive symptoms [8]. Taken together, considering that the benefits of leisure-time walking for depressive symptoms in our study were greater in the elderly group (Additional file 4), engaging in long-term walking for ≥ 150 min/week may be an effective alternative for individuals unable to perform higher-intensity exercise due to socioeconomic or health limitations.

Our study found that both exercise types recommended in the current guidelines (aerobic and resistance exercise) were associated with a ≥ 40% lower risk of depressive symptoms compared with the never-exercise group, and a ≥ 30% lower risk compared with leisure-time walking. Specifically, compared with the never-exercise group, engaging in aerobic exercise for less than 150 min/week was associated with a 29% lower risk, whereas performing this exercise for 150 min/week or more was associated with a more than 40% lower risk. This aligns with a recent meta-analysis reporting that performing 75 min/week of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise reduced depression risk by 18%, while performing 150 min/week reduced risk by 25% [6]. Other meta-analytical evidence indicates that performing approximately 215–245 min/week of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise yields the greatest improvements in adults with major depressive disorder [28]. Similarly, in our study, performing ≥ 300 min/week of aerobic exercise yielded no significant benefit compared with performing 150–299 min/week. Unlike aerobic exercise, the antidepressant effects of resistance exercise remain unclear. Although a meta-analysis has shown a significant association between resistance exercise training and reduced depressive symptoms [12], recent studies have shown inconsistent results. For example, in a group where approximately 80% of the participants had performed resistance exercise for ≥ 1 year, a four-year follow-up found no significant reduction in the risk of incident depression compared with the inactive group [13]. However, this study did not consider training time per week. In our study, resistance exercise performed for ≥ 1 year was not associated with a reduced risk of depressive symptoms if performed for less than 150 min/week. Consistently, recent RCTs have found that 12–24 weeks of resistance exercise training, performed 3 times per week for 30–60 min/session, did not significantly reduce depressive symptoms in older adults [14, 29]. Considering previous findings and our results, regardless of which of the two traditional exercise types is chosen, performing both aerobic and resistance exercise appears to require at least 150 min/week over a sustained period is likely necessary to effectively improve depressive symptoms.

Loneliness, social isolation, and reduced social participation are strongly associated with increased risk of depressive symptoms [15, 16]. Thus, sports participation, which involves cooperation and competition, may provide additional mental health benefits compared to individual exercise [17]. Our study found that regular sports participation was associated with a 46% lower risk of depressive symptoms compared with the never-exercise group, and with a 41% lower risk compared with leisure-time walking. However, there is a lack of studies examining the association between sports participation and depressive symptoms in adults. Our results are consistent with the findings of a recent meta-analysis showing that adolescents who participated in sports had significantly lower levels of anxiety and depression compared with non-participants [18]. Evidence directly comparing aerobic or resistance exercise (traditionally recommended exercise types) with sports participation in relation to depressive symptoms among adults is limited, but in our study, sports participation yielded the lowest risk of depressive symptoms among structured exercise types, though not significantly greater than aerobic or resistance exercise. However, as only 3.99% of the participants engaged in regular sports, comparisons of the risk of depressive symptoms between aerobic exercise (23.78%) and resistance exercise (4.66%) may not have reached statistical significance. A similar pattern was consistently observed in a previous study. According to meta-analytic evidence on global adult sports participation, individual exercises such as walking, running, cycling, swimming, and resistance exercise consistently rank among the top five, whereas participation in most sports activities remains below 5% [30]. Given these low participation rates, RCTs that directly compare antidepressant effects while controlling for exercise intervention type are warranted. Importantly, our study found that, like other exercise types, sports participation was associated with a significantly lower risk of depressive symptoms when performed for at least 150 min/week over a period of more than one year. A recent adolescent study found that compared with those who participated in sports three or more times per week, the severity of depressive symptoms was 1.8 times higher in those participating 1–2 times per week and 2.1 times higher among non-participants [31]. Another study reported that regular participation in team sports among children and adolescents significantly improved mental health indicators, such as anxiety, withdrawal, attention problems, and depression, compared to non-participation [32]. Combining previous findings with ours, regular sports participation for at least 150 min/week or 3 times per week is significantly associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms across age groups. However, as evidence in middle-aged and older adults remains scarce, further studies are needed to confirm the antidepressant effects of sports participation.

Neuroplastic adaptation, anti-inflammatory effects, and mental health benefits are key mechanisms underlying the antidepressant effects of regular exercise. First, mature brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) is essential for neuronal survival and growth, and is initially synthesized as a precursor called pro-BDNF, which plays an antagonistic role in neuronal function by inducing neuronal atrophy and apoptosis [33, 34]. Prior studies show the levels of mature BDNF and its receptors are negatively correlated with depression severity, while the concentrations of pro-BDNF and its receptors are positively correlated [35, 36]. This indicates that an imbalance in the ratio of mature BDNF to pro-BDNF is associated with depressive symptoms. Recent meta-analyses suggest that resistance exercise training, aerobic exercise training, and high-intensity interval training significantly increase mature BDNF levels, thereby serving as protective factors against depression [37]. Second, elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines are well-documented in depression. Recent meta-analyses have reported associations between increased levels of inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein and interleukin-6, and depression risk [38, 39]. Conversely, resistance exercise training, aerobic exercise training, and high-intensity interval training significantly reduced the levels of interleukin-6, C-reactive protein, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha [40]. Lastly, beyond these physiological mechanisms, sports participation confers additional mental health benefits. Participation in group exercise or team sports is associated with reduced depressive symptoms, possibly through greater social connectedness, self-esteem, and life satisfaction [17, 41, 42]. Taken together, these findings suggest that regular exercise may help prevent the onset of depression by reducing inflammation, increasing mature BDNF levels, and supporting mental well-being.

A major strength of our study is the use of large, nationwide cohorts representative of the general middle-aged and older Korean population. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, because our study used a cross-sectional design, causality could not be inferred. Second, as our study focused on the Korean population, the findings may not be generalizable beyond the Korean populations. Third, exercise type and level were assessed using a self-administered questionnaire, which may have introduced recall bias, potentially leading to under- or overestimation of participants’ exercise levels. Fourth, although the CES-D-K and SGDS-K have been validated for detecting depressive symptoms in Korean populations, integrating two cohorts that used different assessment instruments introduces measurement heterogeneity and the possibility of outcome misclassification, which may have affected the estimated prevalence. Lastly, as detailed information on sedentary behavior and physical activity related to occupation and transportation was unavailable, these potential confounders could not be accounted for in the present study.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that while leisure-time walking is associated with a lower risk of depressive symptoms, structured exercise (aerobic exercise, resistance exercise, or sports) is associated with a significantly greater reduction in risk. Notably, regardless of the specific type of exercise, engaging in any form of exercise for 150 min/week or more for at least one year was consistently linked to a reduced risk of depressive symptoms. These findings reinforce the recommendation of regular exercise training as an effective strategy associated with reduced risk of depression.
