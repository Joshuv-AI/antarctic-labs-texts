Health Literacy and Psychological Well-Being of Occupational Drivers in Central India
Authors: Prasheelkumar P. Gupta, Mohammad Ilyas, Manoj Sahu, Talha Saad, Maneesh Jain, Satyendra Mishra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Occupational drivers in India face unique challenges, including long working hours, irregular schedules, and limited access to healthcare resources. Understanding these aspects is crucial for developing targeted interventions to improve their overall health outcomes.Methods:A cross-sectional study was conducted among 300 male occupational drivers aged 21–60 years in Central India. Participants were selected using stratified random sampling. Data collection involved a structured questionnaire comprising three sections: sociodemographic information, the Health Literacy Questionnaire, and the General Health Questionnaire-12 (GHQ-12) to assess psychological well-being. Statistical analyses included descriptive statistics, Pearson’s correlation, and linear regression using SPSS version 25.Results:The mean health literacy score was 26.4 ± 6.8, indicating moderate health literacy levels. The mean GHQ-12 score was 15.7 ± 4.9, with 46% of participants scoring above the threshold suggestive of psychological distress. A significant negative correlation was found between health literacy and psychological distress (r = −0.612, P < 0.001), indicating that lower health literacy is associated with higher psychological distress among drivers.Conclusion:The study highlights a significant association between health literacy and psychological well-being among occupational drivers in Central India. Interventions aimed at improving health literacy could potentially enhance psychological well-being in this population.

INTRODUCTION

Occupational drivers play a critical role in the transportation and logistics infrastructure of India, ensuring the movement of goods and passengers across vast geographic regions. Despite their importance, drivers—particularly those operating long-haul or commercial vehicles—are exposed to multiple occupational hazards, including irregular work hours, poor diet, substance use, sleep deprivation, and psychosocial stressors.[1] These factors cumulatively impact not only their physical health but also their psychological well-being.

Health literacy, defined as the capacity to obtain, process, and understand basic health information and services needed to make appropriate health decisions, is essential for maintaining health and preventing disease.[2] Among occupational drivers, low health literacy is frequently associated with poor disease awareness, inadequate utilization of healthcare services, and unhealthy coping behaviors. Simultaneously, the chronic stressors encountered in this profession—such as extended time away from family, demanding schedules, and high road traffic accident risks—have been linked with higher rates of anxiety, depression, and other psychological morbidities.[3]

There is a paucity of region-specific data, especially from Central India, that examines the dual burden of low health literacy and compromised mental well-being in this high-risk occupational group. This study seeks to assess the levels of health literacy and psychological well-being among occupational drivers in Central India, with the goal of informing targeted interventions and policy reforms that can improve their overall quality of life and occupational safety.

RESULTS

Out of 300 occupational drivers surveyed, the majority (34%) were in the 31–40 age group. Most participants had completed secondary education (37.3%), and a significant portion (59.3%) reported current substance use. Nearly, 57% reported working 8–12 h/day.

The mean health literacy score was 26.4 (±6.8), indicating moderate health literacy overall. However, 40% of drivers scored below the 25th percentile, indicating limited capacity to understand and use health-related information. The mean GHQ-12 score was 15.7 (±4.9), with 46% of drivers scoring above the clinical cutoff (≥15), suggestive of poor psychological well-being or distress.

A statistically significant negative correlation (r = −0.612, P < 0.001) was observed between health literacy and GHQ-12 scores, indicating that drivers with lower health literacy experienced higher psychological distress [Tables 1–3].

DISCUSSION

The present study highlights the interlinked challenges of low health literacy and compromised psychological well-being among occupational drivers in Central India. The findings reveal that a substantial proportion of drivers possess limited health literacy and experience elevated levels of psychological distress, reinforcing the need for targeted health promotion strategies tailored to this vulnerable group.

The mean health literacy score of 26.4 (±6.8) indicates that while some drivers can navigate health-related information, a significant subset struggles with understanding, interpreting, and applying such knowledge. This aligns with earlier research suggesting that individuals in transport occupations, especially in low- and middle-income countries, often have minimal access to structured health education, exacerbated by irregular work schedules and limited formal schooling.[14]

Psychological well-being, as measured by GHQ-12, was also notably affected. Nearly half of the participants showed scores suggestive of moderate-to-severe distress. The high prevalence of substance use (59.3%) and extended working hours (more than 8 h/day for 80% of drivers) are known contributors to poor mental health in this population.[35] Previous studies have documented high rates of anxiety, depression, and burnout among commercial and long-distance drivers due to occupational fatigue, social isolation, and high-pressure environments.[6]

Importantly, a significant inverse correlation (r = −0.612, P < 0.001) between health literacy and psychological distress suggests that enhancing health literacy may serve as a protective factor for mental well-being. Individuals with better comprehension of health information may adopt healthier coping strategies, seek timely care, and reduce reliance on maladaptive behaviors such as substance use.[7]

The findings support global evidence that health literacy is a critical determinant of mental and physical health outcomes and must be addressed as a public health priority.[8]

CONCLUSION

Culturally tailored educational interventions, regular health checkups at transport hubs, and mobile mental health outreach programs could be instrumental in mitigating the risks faced by drivers. Future research should explore longitudinal trends and evaluate the effectiveness of structured health literacy programs in improving both knowledge and psychological resilience among this occupational group.
