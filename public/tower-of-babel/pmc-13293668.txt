Metrics That Matter: Evaluating the Correlates of Pretransplant Performance in US Kidney Transplant Programs
Authors: Jessica L Harding, Stephen O Pastan
Published: 2026

See Clinical Research on Article 106570

For the more than 90,000 patients currently awaiting a deceased donor kidney transplant in the United States,1 regulatory oversight of transplant programs has focused almost exclusively on post-transplant graft and patient survival — a framework that necessarily overlooked what happens to candidates before they ever reach the operating room. The introduction of two new Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network pretransplant performance metrics—the offer acceptance rate ratio (OARR),2 implemented in July 2023, and the pretransplant mortality rate ratio (PMRR),3 implemented in July 2024—represents a major shift in how transplant program accountability is conceptualized. In the article by Parvathinathan et al.4 titled “Correlates of Pretransplant Outcomes in United States Adult Kidney Transplant Programs”, the authors examine program-level correlates of OARR and PMRR across US adult kidney transplant programs between 2015 and 2020, before their adoption. The primary goal of this paper is to characterize pre-existing program-level heterogeneity in these metrics to establish a benchmark against which the impact of these metrics on future transplant program behavior can ultimately be assessed.

Using data from the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients covering 220 US adult kidney transplant programs, the authors evaluate the cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between program-level characteristics—most notably waitlist size—and OARR and PMRR. OARR and PMRR are recalculated annually and represent the ratio of observed to expected rates based on national waitlist candidate and donor organ characteristics. The key finding is that a larger waitlist size is associated with worsening performance on both metrics. Specifically, for every 100 additional patients on the waitlist, the OARR decreased by approximately 1.4% and the PMRR worsened comparably.4 The authors observed substantial between-program variation in both metrics, consistent with previous literature documenting up to a 9.8-fold variation in transplantation probability across centers even within the same geographic area.5 Programs with larger waitlists, most commonly high-volume centers, tended to perform worse on OARR— a pattern that may reflect the logistical and operational challenges of managing large cohorts of candidates with heterogeneous clinical profiles. The study thus raises an important question—do these new metrics inadvertently penalize programs that accept the sickest and most complex patients?

The concern is not merely academic. Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network oversight inquiries are triggered when a program’s OARR falls below a defined threshold, creating reputational and regulatory consequences. If waitlist size is a structural confounder of OARR performance, as this study suggests, then programs that serve large populations may face regulatory scrutiny despite providing high-quality, equitable care. This dynamic has clear equity implications, as follows: programs with smaller, more select waitlists may achieve better metric performance not by providing better care, but by limiting access—an unintended perverse incentive that could deepen disparities in transplant access.

These findings arrive at a pivotal moment in the evolution of transplant program oversight. The Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network’s expanded monitoring framework—now encompassing four risk-adjusted metrics spanning the full transplant trajectory, from waitlist management through 1-year post-transplant graft survival—reflects a growing recognition that pretransplant outcomes are not simply a function of how long someone waits, but of how actively and effectively a program manages candidates toward the goal of transplantation. The observation that larger programs perform worse on OARR and PMRR even after adjustment for patient case-mix points less to the intrinsic risk profile of their patients and more to the operational challenges of managing large, heterogeneous waitlists. This creates a timely opportunity for the transplant community to band together and develop creative solutions, including approaches adapted from other fields, to strengthen system performance, improve resource allocation, and promote both efficiency and equity in access to transplantation.

Despite the concern of larger programs being disproportionality penalized, the practical magnitude of the waitlist size effect in the current study deserves scrutiny. An association of 1.4% fewer organs and more deaths per 100 additional waitlisted patients is statistically significant but clinically modest. Only 32% of US adult kidney transplant programs have waitlists exceeding 500 patients, meaning the majority of programs have a structurally limited capacity to meaningfully change their OARR by manipulating waitlist size. This raises the possibility that waitlist size, although a statistically significant correlate, may not be the primary driver of program-level variation in pretransplant performance, and that other unmeasured programmatic factors (e.g., staffing, communication and waitlist management protocols, and offer review systems) may carry greater explanatory weight.

The goal of this study was a “broad, hypothesis generating ‘first look’ …. at possible responses to the implementation of the OARR and PMRR metrics” and opens several important avenues for future investigation. First, though current metrics adjust for case-mix (i.e., centers with more complex patients should not be penalized solely for having sicker case-mix), residual disadvantage likely remains from unmeasured severity or social risk that is not included in the adjustment. Stratifying by patient complexity or social vulnerability indices may help in distinguishing poorly performing programs (because of organizational factors) from those facing intrinsic structural disadvantage (because of more complex waitlist candidates). Second, longitudinal studies examining whether the implementation of the OARR and PMRR metrics in 2023 and 2024 has actually changed program behavior—particularly whether programs reduce waitlist size, improve active patient management, or both—will be essential to evaluating whether these metrics achieve their intended goals without unintended consequences. Third, given the known variation in psychosocial listing criteria across transplant programs, future work should examine whether programs with more inclusive listing practices, such as accepting patients with complex psychosocial profiles, are systematically disadvantaged on these metrics, and whether current risk-adjustment models could adequately account for this. Finally, qualitative or mixed-methods studies exploring how transplant programs operationally respond to OARR and PMRR inquiries would shed light on whether the regulatory mechanism is functioning as intended, or whether it is instead catalyzing defensive practices that protect metric performance at the expense of patient access.

The work by Parvathinathan et al.4 makes a timely and valuable contribution to an evolving policy landscape. As the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network continues to refine its monitoring framework, ensuring that pretransplant performance metrics are equitable in their application will be as important as the metrics themselves.
