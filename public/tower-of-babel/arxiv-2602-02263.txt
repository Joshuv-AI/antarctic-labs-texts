On The Spectral Theory of Isogeny Graphs and Quantum Sampling of Hard Supersingular Elliptic Curves
David Jao and Maher Mamah

Abstract




Abstract.
In this paper we study the problem of sampling random supersingular elliptic curves
with unknown endomorphism rings. This task has recently attracted significant
attention, as the secure instantiation of many isogeny-based cryptographic
protocols relies on the ability to sample such “hard” curves. Existing
approaches, however, achieve this only in a trusted-setup setting.

We present the first provable quantum polynomial-time algorithm that samples a random hard
supersingular elliptic curve with high probability. Our algorithm
runs heuristically in

\tilde{O}\!\left(\log^{4}p\right)

quantum gate complexity and in
\tilde{O}\!\left(\log^{13}p\right)
under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. As a consequence, our algorithm gives a secure instantiation of the CGL hash function and other cryptographic primitives.

Our analysis relies on a new spectral delocalization result for supersingular

\ell
-isogeny graphs: we prove the Quantum Unique Ergodicity conjecture, and we further provide numerical evidence for complete eigenvector delocalization; this theoretical result may be of independent interest. Along the way, we prove a stronger
\varepsilon
-separation property for eigenvalues of isogeny graphs than that predicted in the quantum money protocol of Kane, Sharif, and Silverberg, thereby removing a key heuristic assumption in their construction.

1.
Introduction

Isogeny-based cryptography is a leading candidate for post-quantum cryptography: it aims to provide
schemes that remain secure against quantum adversaries while relying only on classical algorithms and
implementations. The central hardness assumption in this area is the
isogeny path problem
:
given two supersingular elliptic curves
E
and
E^{\prime}
, find an isogeny
\varphi:E\to E^{\prime}
(i.e., a homomorphism) connecting them. As a comparatively young area, the field
has also attracted substantial cryptanalytic attention.

One of the earliest constructions of cryptographic primitives from isogenies is the
CGL hash function of Charles, Goren, and Lauter
[
12
]
. It is defined via random walks on the
supersingular
\ell
-isogeny graph, which is known to be Ramanujan. The CGL hash function is often termed

provable
in the sense that breaking collision resistance implies solving an assumed hard problem.
In the relevant instantiation, the underlying hard problem is the
endomorphism ring problem
for
supersingular curves, denoted
EndRing
: given a supersingular curve
E
, compute its ring of endomorphisms
\mathrm{End}(E)
. The reduction proceeds via the intermediate
OneEnd
problem of computing a single nontrivial endomorphism of
E

[
36
]
.

By the known computational equivalence between the

supersingular isogeny path
problem and
EndRing

[
17
,
46
,
31
]
, initializing the
CGL hash function from a curve whose endomorphism ring is already known renders the construction
insecure. Indeed, if the starting curve
E_{0}
has a known endomorphism ring
\mathrm{End}(E_{0})
, then taking a
sufficiently long random walk in an
\ell
-isogeny graph yields an output curve
E
that is close to
uniformly distributed. Nevertheless, the walk itself reveals an explicit isogeny path from
E_{0}
to
E
,
which—by the equivalence of the two problems—enables computation of
\mathrm{End}(E)
, which then allows one to compute arbitrary paths, i.e. collisions.
Consequently, a central problem in isogeny-based cryptography is the generation of a supersingular elliptic curve whose
endomorphism ring is unknown, commonly referred to as a
hard curve
. This assumption underlies the
security of several foundational constructions, including the CGL hash function
[
12
]
and Sterner’s commitment scheme
[
43
]
and other primitives that implicitly rely on the ability to generate such curves.

Despite its importance, there is no known efficient algorithm—classical or quantum—that can sample guaranteed hard curves. Several approaches to this problem have been proposed. Methods based on generating polynomials for
supersingular
j
-invariants require exponential time and space
[
34
]
. Other classical and
quantum proposals
[
9
,
16
]
, building on ideas from
[
22
]
, offer promising directions
but remain heuristic and incomplete as they do not provide rigorous guarantees on the induced output distribution, and rely
on simplifying assumptions in their analyses.

[

At present, the only known method to guarantee this property relies on a trusted setup. In particular, Basso
et al.

[
4
]
proposed a distributed trusted-setup protocol in which multiple parties jointly generate a curve. Each participant proves in zero knowledge that they correctly applied an isogeny in a chain, and the resulting curve is guaranteed to be hard provided that at least one participant behaves honestly.

This motivates a natural question: can a single honest party, say Alice, generate a hard supersingular elliptic curve—unknown
both to herself and to any adversary—without relying on any trusted setup? Note that
certifying hardness, i.e. proving that a given curve has an unknown endomorphism ring, lies beyond current techniques.

If Alice is instead given a
potentially
hard curve by an untrusted party, she may perform a
sufficiently long random walk from this curve in an isogeny graph and output the resulting curve to be used in a protocol,
without revealing the traversed path. In this way, neither party learns the endomorphism ring of the
final curve, and this is guaranteed to Alice,
since at least she should be able to trust herself
!

Contributions and organization.

In this paper we answer the above question in the affirmative by presenting the first provable quantum polynomial-time sampling algorithm together with a
detailed gate-complexity analysis. While our approach is inspired by the general perspective of

[
22
,
9
]
, our technical results allow us to
control the induced output distribution. Beyond establishing quantum polynomial-time solvability, a
central goal of this work is to replace the key heuristic assumptions appearing in prior proposals by
rigorous conditional statements; in particular, our main sampling algorithm
1
is provable under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis.
Finally, to facilitate future implementations, we state our heuristic assumptions with explicit and
instantiable parameter choices, so that the sampler can be concretely evaluated once fault-tolerant
quantum hardware becomes available. We note that our results extend naturally to sampling hard curves with

N
-level structure, however, we do not pursue this direction here and leave a detailed treatment to future
work.

The organization of the paper is as follows. Section
2
reviews the required background on
supersingular elliptic curves, isogeny graphs, and the Deuring correspondence.
Section
3
develops the analytic tools needed for our security and distributional analysis.
In particular, we revisit the classical correspondence between supersingular isogeny graphs and modular
forms to derive sup-norm bounds for eigenvectors, which in turn imply strong delocalization. We then
prove an
\varepsilon
-separation result for eigenvalues of
\ell
-isogeny graphs under the Generalized Riemann
Hypothesis, thereby removing a key heuristic assumption appearing in

[
23
,
22
,
9
,
16
]
. Finally, Section
4
assembles these ingredients to present and
analyze our quantum sampling algorithm.

We note that the simplest way to guarantee hardness is for some party to prepare the uniform superposition

Preliminaries

2.1.
Background on Elliptic curves.

We refer the reader to
[
41
]
for a detailed discussion on this topic. An elliptic curve
E
over a field
K
of characteristic
p>3
is defined by the equation
y^{2}=x^{3}+Ax+B
for
A,B\in K^{\times}
with
4A^{3}+27B^{2}\neq 0
. The
K
-rational points of
E
are
(x,y)\in K
satisfying the aforementioned equation along with an identity called the point at infinity
\infty
.

An isogeny
\varphi:E_{1}\rightarrow E_{2}
defined over
K
is a non-constant map which is also a group homomorphism that takes
\infty_{1}
to
\infty_{2}
. The degree of an isogeny is the degree of
\varphi
as a rational map. Every isogeny of degree
n>1
can be factored into a composition of isogenies of prime degrees such that the product of the degrees equals n. If
deg(\varphi)=d
then there exist a unique isogeny called the dual isogeny denoted
\hat{\varphi}:E_{2}\rightarrow E_{1}
such that
\varphi\hat{\varphi}=[d]
where
[d]:E_{2}\rightarrow E_{1}
is the multiplication-by-
d
map. An isomorphism is an isogeny
\iota:E_{1}\rightarrow E_{2}
of degree 1. We say that
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
are isomorphic over
k
(an extension of
K
) if there is an isomorphism between them that is defined over
k
.

The
j
invariant of an elliptic curve
E
as defined by the above Weierstrass equation is
j(E)=\frac{265\cdot 27\cdot A^{3}}{4A^{3}+27B^{2}}
. Any two elliptic curves that are isomorphic over
\overline{\mathbb{F}}_{p}
have the same
j
-invariant. Over a smaller field (or a lower-degree extension), the converse
need not hold: there may be several distinct
K
-isomorphism classes sharing the
same
j
-invariant. Since our applications require an explicit description of

\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
-isomorphism classes of elliptic curves, we will use the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
p\neq 2,3
and set
K=\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
. Fix
\alpha\in K^{*}
a generator of
K^{*}
.
We represent
K
-isomorphism classes of elliptic curves over
K
by pairs
(j,b)\in K\times\mathbb{Z}

with

b\in\{0,1\}\ \textnormal{if }(j\neq 0,1728),\qquad b\in\{0,1,2,3\}\ \textnormal{if }(j=1728),\qquad b\in\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}\ \textnormal{if }(j=0).

Define the curve attached to
(j,b)
by

\begin{array}[]{rll}\text{if }j\neq 0,1728:&E_{j,b}:\ y^{2}=x^{3}+\alpha^{2b}\dfrac{3j}{1728-j}\,x+\alpha^{3b}\dfrac{2j}{1728-j},\\[10.00002pt]
\text{if }j=1728:&E_{1728,b}:\ y^{2}=x^{3}+\alpha^{b}x,\\[3.99994pt]
\text{if }j=0:&E_{0,b}:\ y^{2}=x^{3}+\alpha^{b}.\end{array}

Then
E_{j,b}\cong_{K}E_{j,b^{\prime}}
iff
b\equiv b^{\prime}\pmod{2}
for
j\neq 0,1728
,

b\equiv b^{\prime}\pmod{4}
for
j=1728
, and
b\equiv b^{\prime}\pmod{6}
for
j=0
.

Proof.

See
[
41
, Corollary X.5.4.1]
.
∎

An isogeny with a degree that is coprime to
p
is uniquely determined by its kernel. Given the kernel, the isogeny can be computed in polynomial time with respect to the degree of the isogeny and
\log(p)
using Vélu’s formula
[
44
]
. In fact we have an asymptotically faster algorithm due to
[
5
]
which runs in time
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\ell})
.

Endomorphism ring and Supersingular versus Ordinary curves
An isogeny from
E
into itself is called an endomorphism of
E
. If
E
is defined over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
then an endomorphism of
E
will be defined over a finite extension of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. The set of endomorphisms of
E
with the zero map form a ring under pointwise addition and composition which is called the endomorphism ring of
E
and denoted by End(
E
). If End(
E
) is isomorphic to an order in a imaginary quadratic field then the curve is said to be
ordinary
. Otherwise if End(
E
) is isomorphic to a maximal order in a Quaternion algebra in
B_{p,\infty}
(defined in section
2.2
) then
E
is said to be
supersingular
. Every supersingular elliptic curve over a field of characteristic
p
has an isomorphic curve that is defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
because the j-invariant of such a curve is in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
. So without loss of generality we can assume there exists a canonical base curve
E
over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
.

Up to
k
-isomorphism, all supersingular elliptic curves are defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
which we denote by
\mathcal{S}_{p}
, with approximately
\left\lfloor\frac{p}{12}\right\rfloor
such curves. Let
\ell
be a prime different from
p
. The supersingular
\ell
-isogeny graph over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
, denoted
\mathcal{G}(p,\ell)
, consists of vertices corresponding to these supersingular elliptic curves (up to isomorphism), with an edge connecting
E_{1}
to
E_{2}
for each
l
-isogeny from
E_{1}
to
E_{2}
. This graph is regular with degree
\ell+1
since each curve
E
possesses
\ell+1
subgroups
H
of order
\ell
, each generating an isogeny with kernel
H
. The
\ell
-isogeny graph is Ramanujan, meaning that random walks on this graph converge rapidly to the uniform distribution, and any two curves in the graph are linked by an isogeny of degree
\ell^{m}
with
m=O(\log p)
(see
[
26
]
, Theorem 79).

Supersingular elliptic curves with level structure.

For background on this topic, we refer the reader to Arpin
[
3
]
.
Let
p
be a prime and let
N
be a square-free integer coprime to
p
.
Define
\mathcal{S}_{p}(N)
to be the set of equivalence classes of pairs
(E,G)
,
where
E
is a supersingular elliptic curve over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
and

G\subseteq E[N]
is a cyclic subgroup of order
N
.
We say two pairs
(E_{1},G_{1})
and
(E_{2},G_{2})
to be equivalent if and only if
there exists an isomorphism
\iota:E_{1}\to E_{2}
such that
\iota(G_{1})=G_{2}
.
Elements of
\mathcal{S}_{p}(N)
are thus supersingular elliptic curves equipped with
level-
N
structure. When talking about the
\ell
-isogeny graph with
N
-level structure, we will denote it by
\mathcal{G}(p,\ell,N)
.

Motivated by the role of endomorphism rings in isogeny-based cryptography, we
associate to such a pair
(E,G)
a distinguished suborder of
End(E)
.
Namely, for
(E,G)\in\mathcal{S}_{p}(N)
we define

\mathcal{O}(E,G)\;=\;\{\alpha\in End(E):\alpha(G)\subseteq G\},

the order consisting of endomorphisms of
E
that preserve the subgroup
G
. The size of the set is roughly

|\mathcal{S}_{p}(N)|\approx|\mathcal{S}_{p}|\prod_{\ell|N}(\ell+1)\ll\frac{p}{12}\cdot N\log\log N.

2.2.
Quaternion Algebras and the Deuring Correspondence

For a detailed account on the arithmetic of quaternion algebras we refer the reader to
[
45
]
. For
a,b\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}
, let
B(a,b)
denote the quaternion algebra over
\mathbb{Q}
, with basis
1,i,j,ij
, i.e.

B(a,b)=\mathbb{Q}+\mathbb{Q}i+\mathbb{Q}j+\mathbb{Q}ij,

such that
i^{2}=a
,
j^{2}=b
and
ij=-ji
.
The quaternion algebra
B
has a
canonical involution
that sends
\alpha=x_{1}+x_{2}i+x_{3}j+x_{4}ij
to
\bar{\alpha}=x_{1}-x_{2}i-x_{3}j-x_{4}ij
, and we define both the reduced trace and norm of an element
\alpha
in B by

\textnormal{Trd}(\alpha)=\alpha+\bar{\alpha}=2x_{1},~~~~\textnormal{Nrd}(\alpha)=\alpha\bar{\alpha}=x_{1}^{2}-ax_{2}^{2}-bx_{3}^{2}+abx_{4}^{2}.

We say
\Lambda
is a lattice in
B
if
\Lambda=\mathbb{Z}x_{1}+\mathbb{Z}x_{2}+\mathbb{Z}x_{3}+\mathbb{Z}x_{4}
where the
x_{i}
’s form a basis for the vector space
B
over
\mathbb{Q}
.

If
I\subset B
is a lattice, then the reduced norm of
I
, Nrd
(I)=\gcd(\textnormal{Nrd}(\alpha)|\alpha\in\Lambda)
. We associate to
\Lambda
the normalised quadratic map

q_{\Lambda}:\Lambda\rightarrow\mathbb{Z},\quad\begin{array}[]{c}\alpha\mapsto\frac{\textnormal{Nrd}(\alpha)}{\textnormal{Nrd}(\Lambda)}\end{array}

and notice that
\frac{\textnormal{Nrd}(\alpha)}{\textnormal{Nrd}(\Lambda)}\in\mathbb{Z}
as
\textnormal{Nrd}(\Lambda)|\textnormal{Nrd}(\alpha)
. The quaternion algebra
B
is an inner product space with respect to the bilinear form

\langle x,y\rangle=\frac{1}{2}(\textnormal{Nrd}(x+y)-\textnormal{Nrd}(x)-\textnormal{Nrd}(y)),

and the basis
\{1,i,j,ij\}
is an orthogonal basis with respect to this inner product.

An order
\mathcal{O}
in
B
is a full rank lattice that is also a subring. It is maximal if it is not contained in any other order. For an lattice
\Lambda
we define the
left order
and
right order
of
\Lambda
to be

\mathcal{O}_{L}(\Lambda)=\{\alpha\in B|~\alpha\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda\},\quad\mathcal{O}_{R}(\Lambda)=\{\alpha\in B|~\Lambda\alpha\subseteq\Lambda\}\}.

If
\mathcal{O}
is a maximal order and
I
is a left
\mathcal{O}
-ideal, then
\mathcal{O}_{R}(I)
is also a maximal order. Given two maximal order
\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
, then there exist a lattice
I
, called a connecting ideal, such that
\mathcal{O}_{L}(I)=\mathcal{O}
and
\mathcal{O}_{R}(I)=\mathcal{O}^{\prime}
. Additionally, we say left
\mathcal{O}
-ideals
I
and
J
are equivalent if there exists an
\alpha\in B^{\times}
such that
I=\alpha J
. The set of classes of this equivalence relation is called the
left
-ideal class set of
\mathcal{O}
and is denoted by Cl
(\mathcal{O}
).

The endomorphism rings of supersingular elliptic curves in characteristic
p

are maximal orders in the unique quaternion algebra
B_{p,\infty}
over
\mathbb{Q}

ramified exactly at
p
and
\infty
. Equivalently, the localizations

B_{p,\infty}\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}_{p}
and
B_{p,\infty}\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{R}
are division
algebras, while for every prime
\ell\neq p
we have a splitting into matrix algebras

B_{p,\infty}\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}\cong M_{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{\ell}).

An Eichler order
\mathfrak{O}
is an intersection
\mathfrak{O}=\mathcal{O}_{1}\cap\mathcal{O}_{2}
for two (not necessary distinct) maximal orders
\mathcal{O}_{1}
and
\mathcal{O}_{2}
. The level
N
of an Eichler order is given by its index in one of the maximal orders whose intersection defines the order, i.e.
N=[\mathcal{O}_{1}:\mathfrak{O}]=[\mathcal{O}_{2}:\mathfrak{O}]
(this index will be the same for either order). Trivially, a maximal order is an Eichler order of level
N=1
.

The Deuring Correspondence.

For a detailed account of on the Deuring correspondence, see chapter 42 of
[
45
]
. Let
E
be a supersingular elliptic curve defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
, Deuring showed that endomorphism rings of supersingular elliptic curves are in bijection with maximal order in
B_{p,\infty}
up to conjugation with norms and traces on the endomorphisms side correspond to norms and traces in the quaternion algebra.

Deuring’s correspondence establishes that, for a supersingular elliptic curve
E
defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
, every isogeny
\varphi:E\to E^{\prime}
of degree
n
can be associated with a left ideal
I
in
\mathrm{End}(E)
whose norm is
n
. According to Kohel’s work
[
26
, Section 5.3]
, every nonzero left ideal of
\mathrm{End}(E)
arises from such an isogeny.

To construct an isogeny from a given left ideal, one considers a nonzero integral left ideal
I\subset\mathrm{End}(E)
and defines
E[I]
as the scheme-theoretic intersection of the kernels of all endomorphisms
\alpha\in I
, i.e.

E[I]=\bigcap_{\alpha\in I}\mathrm{ker}(\alpha).

This subgroup then determines an isogeny
\varphi_{I}:E\to E/E[I]
. We also have for each isogeny
\varphi:E\rightarrow E^{\prime}
, the corresponding ideal which is given by

I_{\varphi}=\mathrm{Hom}(E^{\prime},E)\varphi
, and its a left
\mathrm{End}(E)
-ideal and a right
\mathrm{End}(E^{\prime})
-ideal.
Interestingly,
I_{\varphi_{I}}=I
and
\varphi_{I_{\varphi}}=\varphi
. Moreover, if the reduced norm of
I
is coprime to
p
,
\varphi
is separable, and
E[I]=\{P\in E(\bar{\mathbb{F}}_{p^{2}})~\colon~\alpha(P)=0,~\forall\alpha\in I\}
.

.
Conversely, every Eichler order of level
N
is isomorphic to

.

See Theorem 3.7 and proposition 3.8 of

]

\ell

B_{\ell}=(b_{ij})
is defined by

b_{ij}=\#\Bigl\{\,J\subseteq I_{j}:\mathrm{nrd}(J)=\ell\,\mathrm{nrd}(I_{j})\ \text{and}\ [J]=[I_{i}]\Bigr\}.

Equivalently,
B_{\ell}
acts on the space of complex-valued functions
A(\mathcal{O})=\{f:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathbb{C}\}
by

(B_{\ell}f)([I])=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}J\subseteq I\\
\mathrm{nrd}(J)=\ell\,\mathrm{nrd}(I)\end{subarray}}f([J]),

which is independent of the chosen representative of the class
[I]
.

Under Deuring’s correspondence,
B_{\ell}
(with respect to the basis of
\delta
-indicator functions) agrees with the
A_{\ell}
as an adjacency operator.
At vertices with extra automorphisms (notably
j=0
and
j=1728
), the resulting operator is not
self-adjoint for the standard Euclidean inner product, i.e. the matrix
A_{\ell}
isn’t symmetric. Let
w(E,G)=|\mathrm{Aut}(E)/2|\in\{1,2,3\}
where
\mathrm{Aut}(E,G)=\{u\in\mathrm{Aut}(E):u(G)=G\}
and set

D=\mathrm{diag}(w(E,G))_{(E,G)\in\mathcal{S}_{p}(N)}
. Then

A_{\ell}^{\prime}=D^{1/2}\,B_{\ell}\,D^{-1/2},

(1)

is a real symmetric matrix; see
[
45
, § 40.1.9 & 40.1.10]
.
In particular,
B_{\ell}
and
A_{\ell}^{\prime}
are similar and therefore have the same spectrum. Moreover the operators
\{A_{\ell}^{\prime}\}
form a family of commuting operators and thus have a simultaneous eigenbasis.

When
p\equiv 1\pmod{12}
, no supersingular curve has
j
-invariant
0
or
1728
, so
w(E)=2

for all
E\in\mathcal{S}
and hence
A_{\ell}=B_{\ell}
. This congruence condition is often imposed in
isogeny-based cryptography to avoid such pathological automorphism cases, as the
\ell
-isogeny graph would contain multi-edges and loops. For more details on the identity (
1
) see appendix A of
[
36
]
.

3.
Automorphic Forms

In this section, we review several aspects of the theory of automorphic forms. We focus in particular on quaternionic automorphic forms, viewed both as finite-dimensional vector spaces of functions on quaternionic ideal class sets and as functions on 3-spheres. We then recall the classical correspondence between these two perspectives and establish the sup-norm bounds and the
\varepsilon
-separation of Hecke eigenvalues required for our applications. These results will play a fundamental role in our subsequent analysis.

3.1.
Space of Quaternionic forms

The notation in this section is somewhat involved; we refer the reader to
[
15
,
32
]
for background and further details. Throughout,
B
denotes the definite quaternion algebra
B_{p,\infty}
over
\mathbb{Q}
and
\mathcal{O}
an Eichler order of level
N
coprime to
p
. Denote by
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})=\{[I_{1}],\cdots,[I_{h}]\}
the set of invertible left
\mathcal{O}
-ideal classes in
B
and
h=|\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})|
the class number of
\mathcal{O}
. Note that
h=|\mathcal{S}_{p}|
.

Let
\hat{\mathbb{Z}}=\prod_{p}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
be the profinite completion of
\mathbb{Z}
, and for an abelian group
G
set

\hat{G}=G\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
\mathbb{A}_{f}=\prod_{p}^{\prime}\mathbb{Q}_{p}
be the ring of finite adeles of
\mathbb{Q}
, and write

\hat{B}=B\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{A}_{f}
and
\hat{\mathcal{O}}=\mathcal{O}\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}\hat{\mathbb{Z}}
. For
N\geq 1
define

H=H(N)=(1+N\hat{\mathcal{O}})\cap\hat{\mathcal{O}}^{\times}\subset\hat{B}^{\times},

a compact open subgroup of
\hat{B}^{\times}
. The double coset space

B^{\times}\backslash\hat{B}^{\times}/H

is finite and is in natural correspondence with
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})
, where
B^{\times}
is embedded diagonally in
\hat{B}^{\times}
. Choosing representatives

x_{1},\dots,x_{h}
, we identify a function
f
on
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})
with a function on
\hat{B}^{\times}
that is
left
B^{\times}
-invariant and right
H(N)
-invariant, and write

f([I_{i}])=f(x_{i})=f(B^{\times}xH)
for
x\in\hat{B}^{\times}
.

The locally compact group
\hat{B}^{\times}

admits a Haar measure
\mu
, that is, a measure invariant under both left and right
translations, finite on compact subsets, and nontrivial on open subsets. The
space of automorphic forms of level
H
is

A(\mathcal{O}):=L^{2}\!\left(B^{\times}\backslash\hat{B}^{\times}/H\right)\cong\{\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathbb{C}\},

a finite-dimensional Hilbert space of dimension
h
, equipped with the Petersson
inner product

\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle=\int_{x\in B^{\times}\backslash\hat{B}^{\times}/H}\varphi(x)\,\overline{\psi(x)}\,\mu(xH).

Let
\ell
be a prime unramified in
B
, so that
\mathcal{O}_{\ell}\simeq M_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{\ell})
.
Let
a_{\ell}\in\hat{B}^{\times}
be the adele equal to
1
at all places
\neq\ell

and to
\begin{pmatrix}\ell&0\\
0&1\\
\end{pmatrix}
at
\ell
.
Writing

Ha_{\ell}H=\bigsqcup_{i}x_{i}H,

the Hecke operator
T_{\ell}:A(\mathcal{O})\to A(\mathcal{O})
is defined by

T_{\ell}(\varphi)(x)=\sum_{x_{i}H\in Ha_{\ell}H/H}\varphi(B^{\times}xx_{i}H),

with adjoint

T_{\ell}^{*}(\varphi)(x)=\sum_{x_{i}H\in Ha_{\ell}^{-1}H/H}\varphi(B^{\times}xx_{i}H).

For
\ell
prime to
N
, the adelic Hecke operators
T_{\ell}
are a commuting family of self-adjoint operators with respect to the Petersson inner product.

The averaging property of these Hecke operators resembles, to a great extent, the Brandt operators in the quaternionic side of isogeny graphs. In fact, as the next proposition shows, they are indeed identical.

Proposition 3.1
.

The Hecke operator
T_{\ell}
defined with respect to the indicator
\delta
-functions is equal to the Brandt matrix
B_{\ell}
.

Proof.

Choose representatives
x_{1},\dots,x_{h}\in\hat{B}^{\times}
for

B^{\times}\backslash\hat{B}^{\times}/H
, corresponding to the left
\mathcal{O}
-ideal classes

[I_{1}],\dots,[I_{h}]
. We identify
\varphi\in A(\mathcal{O})
with the column vector

\big(\varphi(x_{1}),\dots,\varphi(x_{h})\big)^{t}
, and let
\delta_{[I_{j}]}
be the function
supported on
[I_{j}]
(equivalently on the coset
B^{\times}x_{j}H
).

Write the finite right-coset decomposition

Ha_{\ell}H=\bigsqcup_{r}y_{r}H.

Then for any
i,j
we have

\displaystyle(T_{\ell}\delta_{[I_{j}]})(x_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{r}\delta_{[I_{j}]}(B^{\times}x_{i}y_{r}H)

\displaystyle=\#\{\,r:\;B^{\times}x_{i}y_{r}H=B^{\times}x_{j}H\,\}.

(
\ast
)

Hence the
(i,j)
entry of the matrix of
T_{\ell}
in the delta-basis is exactly the
cardinality in
(\ast)
.

Now use the standard local double-coset computation in
GL_{2}(\mathbb{Q}_{\ell})
(elemntary divisor theorem).
Since
H_{\ell}\simeq GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{\ell})
and
a_{\ell}=diag(\ell,1)
at
\ell
, one has

H_{\ell}\begin{pmatrix}\ell&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}H_{\ell}=\left(\bigsqcup_{i\in\mathbb{Z}/\ell\mathbb{Z}}\begin{pmatrix}1&i\\
0&\ell\end{pmatrix}H_{\ell}\right)\;\sqcup\;\begin{pmatrix}\ell&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}H_{\ell}.

Extending each representative by
1
at all primes
\neq\ell
gives a decomposition

Ha_{\ell}H=\bigsqcup_{r}y_{r}H
with
\ell+1
cosets.

Under the standard bijection

B^{\times}\backslash\hat{B}^{\times}/H\;\cong\;\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})
,
right-multiplication by such a
y_{r}
changes the local lattice only at
\ell
in a way
that corresponds to passing from a left ideal
I_{i}
to an
\ell
-neighbor left ideal

J\subset I_{i}
of (relative) reduced norm
\ell
. Since
(y_{r})_{q}=1
for
q\neq\ell
, the ideal attached to
x_{i}y_{r}
agrees with
I_{i}

away from
\ell
, and because
(y_{r})_{\ell}\in\mathcal{O}_{\ell}^{\times}\begin{pmatrix}\ell&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\mathcal{O}_{\ell}^{\times}

it yields precisely an
\ell
-neighbor
J\subset I_{i}
with
\mathrm{Nrd}(I_{i}^{-1}J)=\ell
.

Consequently, for fixed
i
, the multiset of classes represented by
B^{\times}x_{i}y_{r}H

as
r
varies is exactly the multiset of ideal classes
[J]
with

J\subset I_{i}
and
\mathrm{Nrd}(I_{i}^{-1}J)=\ell
.

Therefore the count in
(\ast)
equals

\#\{\,J\subset I_{i}:\ \mathrm{Nrd}(I_{i}^{-1}J)=\ell,\ [J]=[I_{j}]\,\},

which is precisely the defining
(i,j)
-entry of the Brandt matrix
B_{\ell}
.
Thus the matrix of
T_{\ell}
in the delta-basis is
B_{\ell}
.
∎

3.2.
The sup-norm problem of Hecke eigenforms

Here we study the so-called sup-norm problem. Let
T_{\ell}
be a Hecke operator on

A(\mathcal{O})=L^{2}\!\left(B^{\times}\backslash\hat{B}^{\times}/H\right).

Since
T_{\ell}
is self-adjoint with respect to the Petersson inner product, it admits
an orthonormal eigenbasis (of
eigenforms
) consisting of functions

\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathbb{C}
. A classical question in analytic number theory concerns the

sup-norm
of such forms. Simply put, for a normalized Hecke eigenform

\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathbb{C},\qquad[I_{i}]\longmapsto\varphi([I_{i}]),

or equivalently a function on
B^{\times}\backslash\hat{B}^{\times}/H
, the sup-norm problem is
to obtain an upper bound for

\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\;=\;\max_{1\leq i\leq h}\,|\varphi([I_{i}])|.

The sup-norm problem fits into the broader circle of ideas known as
arithmetic quantum chaos
:
one expects high-energy Hecke eigenfunctions to behave “randomly” and, in particular, to avoid
strong concentration. Quantitatively, this is reflected both in bounds for the
L^{\infty}
-norm and
in statements about the distribution of the
L^{2}
-mass (“energy”) of eigenfunctions

[
40
,
21
]
. In this direction, Rudnick and Sarnak initiated a systematic
study of quantum limits for arithmetic eigenstates and formulated the
quantum unique
ergodicity
(QUE) philosophy in the arithmetic setting
[
39
]
.

On arithmetic
surfaces, Lindenstrauss proved arithmetic QUE for compact congruence surfaces (via measure
classification)
[
28
]
, and Soundararajan ruled out “escape of mass” for the
modular surface; together these establish the Rudnick–Sarnak QUE conjecture in that case

[
42
]
. In the holomorphic setting, Holowinsky and Soundararajan proved the
Rudnick–Sarnak mass equidistribution conjecture for Hecke eigenforms
[
19
]
.

In the definite quaternionic setting, the operators
T_{\ell}
are realized by Brandt operators on the
finite set
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})
, so an eigenform
\varphi:\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathbb{C}
may be viewed as an eigenvector of a
regular “Brandt graph.” From this perspective, bounding
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
is a quantitative

delocalization
statement: it prevents the eigenvector from concentrating on a small subset
of vertices. This viewpoint parallels the delocalization and quantum ergodicity theory for eigenvectors
on large regular graphs
[
2
]
.

Some of the first sup-norm bounds in our setting of automorphic forms on

X\;=\;B^{\times}\backslash\hat{B}^{\times}/H,

are due to Blomer and Michel
[
7
, Theorem 1]
. Specifically, for an

L^{2}
-normalized Hecke eigenform
\varphi
they prove that there exists

\delta<1/60
such that

\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\ll\mathrm{Vol}(X)^{-\delta}\;=\;|\mathrm{disc}(\mathcal{O})|^{-\delta/2}\;\asymp\;(pN)^{-\delta},

using that
\mathrm{Vol}(X)=\mathrm{disc}^{1/2}(\mathcal{O})=pN
where
\mathcal{O}\subset B_{p,\infty}
again is an Eichler order of level
N
. This was subsequently strengthened
in
[
8
, Theorem 1]
, where they refined the exponent and obtained, for any

\varepsilon>0
,

\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\ll_{\varepsilon}\mathrm{Vol}(X)^{-1/6+\varepsilon}\;=\;(pN)^{-1/6+\varepsilon}.

The sharpest sup-norm bound currently known in this setting is due to Khayutin–Nelson–Steiner
[
25
]
, which we record for later use.

Theorem 3.2
.

[
25
, Corollary 2.3]

Let
\varphi\in A(\mathcal{O})
be an
L^{2}
-normalized Hecke eigenform. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\ll_{\varepsilon}\mathrm{Vol}(X)^{-1/4+\varepsilon}.

Remark.
Norms in
[
25
]
are taken with respect to a
probability measure

(i.e. total mass
1
). In our setting
X=B^{\times}\backslash\hat{B}^{\times}/H
is finite,
and the Haar-induced measure
\mu
satisfies

\mathrm{Vol}(X)=\int_{X}1\,d\mu.

We set the probability measure
d\mu_{\mathrm{prob}}:=\mathrm{Vol}(X)^{-1}d\mu
, so that

\int_{X}1\,d\mu_{\mathrm{prob}}=1
. Consequently,

\|\varphi\|_{2,\mathrm{prob}}^{2}=\int_{X}|\varphi|^{2}\,d\mu_{\mathrm{prob}}=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(X)}\int_{X}|\varphi|^{2}\,d\mu=\frac{1}{\mathrm{Vol}(X)}\|\varphi\|_{2}^{2},

and switching between these normalizations rescales
\varphi
(hence
\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
)
by a factor
\mathrm{Vol}(X)^{1/2}
.

We now prove our main delocalization theorem of eigenvectors of supersingular
\ell
-isogeny graph. For the sake of emphasizing the characteristic
p
we are working over and the structure of the quaternion algebra, we will denote by
\mathcal{G}(p,\ell,N)
the supersingular
\ell
-isogeny graph with
N
-level structure where again
(N,p)=1
.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
A_{\ell}
be the weighted adjacency matrix of the supersingular
\ell
-isogeny graphs with level structure
\mathcal{G}(p,\ell,N)
given by
1
.
If
v
is a normalized eigenvector of
\{A_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
, then

\|v\|_{\infty}\;=\;\max_{E\in\mathcal{G}(p,\ell)}|v(E)|\;\ll_{\varepsilon}\;(pN)^{-1/4+\varepsilon},

where
v(E)
denotes the
(E,G)^{th}
entry of
v
.

Proof.

Write
v
in the
\delta
-indicator basis indexed by the vertices of
\mathcal{G}(p,\ell,N)
.
By the identity (
1
), the vector
w:=D^{-1}v
is an eigenvector of the Brandt matrix
B_{\ell}

in the indicator basis of
\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})
, where
D
is the weighted diagonal matrix. By
Proposition
3.1
,
w
corresponds to a Hecke eigenform
\varphi\in A(\mathcal{O})
satisfying

\varphi([I_{i}])=w([I_{i}])
for all
i
. Hence, by Theorem
3.2
,

\|w\|_{\infty}\;=\;\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\;=\;\max_{[I_{i}]\in\mathrm{Cl}(\mathcal{O})}|\varphi([I_{i}])|\;\ll_{\varepsilon}\;(pN)^{-1/4+\varepsilon}.

Finally, since
v=Dw
and
\|D\|_{\infty}=3
, we obtain

\|v\|_{\infty}\leq\|D\|_{\infty}\,\|w\|_{\infty}\ll_{\varepsilon}(pN)^{-1/4+\varepsilon},

as claimed.
∎

This theorem establish a strong delocalization property of the isogeny graphs: an
L^{2}
-normalized eigenvector of the
adjacency operator cannot concentrate a large proportion of its mass on a small set of vertices.
Equivalently, the eigenmodes of the random walk on
\mathcal{G}(p,\ell)
are forced to be spread
across many vertices rather than exhibiting sharp spikes.

However, we expect more from these isogeny graphs. As pointed out in

[
8
, p. 4]
, the strongest conceivable upper bound for the sup-norm is

\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\ll_{\varepsilon}\mathrm{Vol}(X)^{-1/2+\varepsilon}=(pN)^{-1/2+\varepsilon}.

From a graph-theoretic perspective, such a bound would imply not merely strong
delocalization, but
complete delocalization
of eigenvectors. This so-called
sup-norm bound
,
first highlighted in
[
8
]
and subsequently echoed in
[
25
]
, remains
a long-standing and widely believed open problem in analytic number theory. While
no proof is currently known, we provide supporting evidence for the following
heuristic in the case of the regular
\ell
-isogeny graph, that is when
N=1
, which is the case of interest.

Heuristic 1

(Eigenvector delocalization)
.

Let
A_{\ell}
denote the weighted adjacency matrix of the supersingular
\ell
-isogeny
graph
\mathcal{G}(p,\ell)
for all
\ell\neq p
. Then any
L^{2}
-normalized simultaneous eigenvector
v
of
A_{\ell}

satisfies

\|v\|_{\infty}\;\ll\;\frac{\log p}{\sqrt{p}}.

In Figure
1
, we computed the sup-norm of each eigenvector of
A_{\ell}
for
\ell=2,3,5
, then took the maximum over all of them and plotted the results. The matrices were computed using Sage’s built-in functions
BrandtModule(
p,\ell
)
and
hecke_matrix
, and subsequently analyzed in MATLAB for improved stability and efficiency. As shown, the computations indicate that the sup-norm satisfies the conjectured heuristic, with implicit constants ranging roughly between
0.5
and
2.5
.

Figure 1:
Sup-norm values versus
\frac{\log p}{\sqrt{p}}
for
\ell=2,3,5
.

We now record the following useful theorem, which asserts that eigenvectors of the weighted adjacency matrices
A_{\ell}
are completely delocalized,
on average
.
For a vector
v
, we write
v(x)
for its
x
-th coordinate.

Theorem 3.4

(Fourth Moment bound)
.

Let
A_{\ell}
denote the weighted adjacency matrices of the graph
\mathcal{G}(p,\ell,N)
, and let
n
be the number of vertices.
For any two vertices
x,y\in\mathcal{G}(p,\ell,N)
, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(v_{i}(x)^{2}-v_{i}(y)^{2}\big)^{2}\ll_{\varepsilon}(pN)^{-1+\varepsilon},

where
\{v_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is a simultaneous orthonormal basis of eigenvectors of
\{A_{\ell}\}
.

Proof.

This follows directly from Theorem 2.1 of
[
25
]
, since the weighted adjacency matrices
A_{\ell}
satisfy the same hypotheses in the elliptic curve setting. The proof proceeds exactly as in Theorem
3.3
.

∎

3.3.
\varepsilon
-Separation of Hecke Eigenvalues

Fix a prime
p
and let

A_{\ell_{1}},\ldots,A_{\ell_{r}}

denote the weighted adjacency matrices of the supersingular
\ell_{i}
-isogeny graphs over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
as in
1
, so that they are real and symmetric.

For each prime
\ell
, define

U_{\ell}:=e^{iA_{\ell}/\sqrt{\ell}}.

Let
v_{k}
and
v_{j}
be simultaneous normalized eigenvectors of all
A_{\ell_{i}}
(and hence of all
U_{\ell_{i}}
).
For each eigenvector
v_{j}
, define the associated eigenvalue vector

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}:=(\lambda_{1,j},\lambda_{2,j},\ldots,\lambda_{r,j}),

where
\lambda_{i,j}
denotes the eigenvalue of
v_{j}
under

A_{\ell_{i}}
.

Definition 3.5
.

We say that the operators
\{A_{\ell}\}
are
\varepsilon
-separated
if for any two distinct
simultaneous eigenvectors
v_{k}
and
v_{j}
,

\lVert\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}\rVert_{2}\geq\varepsilon.

Our main result in this subsection is theorem
3.6
and proposition
3.7
which provide strong lower bounds on

\lVert\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{k}-\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}\rVert_{2}
.
The operators
U_{\ell}
are introduced for applications: the matrices
A_{\ell}
are symmetric, hence
U_{\ell}
is unitary and can be interpreted
as a quantum gate.

By the Jacquet–Langlands correspondence, the Brandt (Hecke) operators acting on the
supersingular module can be identified with Hecke operators acting on weight
2

modular forms of level
p
equipped with the Petersson inner product.
Concretely, for each prime
\ell\neq p
, the (symmetrized) adjacency matrix
A_{\ell}

of the supersingular
\ell
-isogeny graph coincides with the
\ell
-th Brandt matrix,
and hence with the Hecke operator
T_{\ell}
acting on
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(p))
.
In particular, the eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue
\ell+1
spans the
Eisenstein component, while its orthogonal complement identifies with the space of
cuspidal forms, which is the relevant case for our purposes.
As a result, the spectrum of
A_{\ell}
on the cuspidal subspace (space of eigenvectors orthogonal to the Eisenstein one) agrees with the set of
Hecke eigenvalues of a simultaneous cuspidal eigenbasis of
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(p))
.

Thus, if
\{v_{j}\}
denotes a basis of simultaneous eigenvectors for the commuting family

\{A_{\ell}\}_{\ell\neq p}
restricted to the cuspidal subspace, then each
v_{j}

corresponds to a normalized weight
2
Hecke cusp eigenform

f_{j}\in S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(p))
, and for every prime
\ell\neq p
we have

A_{\ell}f_{j}=a_{\ell}(f_{j})\,f_{j}\qquad\text{and}\qquad U_{\ell}f_{j}=e^{i\frac{a_{\ell}(f_{j})}{\sqrt{\ell}}}\,f_{j}.

It is therefore natural to associate to
f_{j}
the
Hecke tag vector

\mathbf{t}_{j}:=\bigl(\lambda_{\ell_{1}}(f_{j}),\ldots,\lambda_{\ell_{r}}(f_{j})\bigr),\qquad\lambda_{\ell}(f):=\frac{a_{\ell}(f)}{\sqrt{\ell}}\in[-2,2].

The bound
\lambda_{\ell}(f)\in[-2,2]
follows from Deligne’s theorem
[
14
,
37
]
.

The
eigenvalue separation problem
asks for an explicit length
r
and a usable
separation threshold
\varepsilon>0
such that distinct eigenforms yield distinct (and
numerically distinguishable) tags:

f_{j}\neq f_{k}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\bigl\|\mathbf{t}_{j}-\mathbf{t}_{k}\bigr\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon.

This is practically important, as it allows one to descend to unique common one-dimensional
eigenspaces indexed by their tags. In particular, public-key quantum money constructions
based on commuting Brandt–Hecke operators require eigenvalue tags that are sufficiently
well separated to be identifiable from approximate measurements. All existing works,
however, rely on heuristic separation assumptions; see, for example,

[
23
]
,
[
22
]
,
[
9
]
and
[
16
]
.

At the level of
uniqueness
(without quantitative separation), it is known that there exists
a bound
r
such that for any two simultaneous Hecke eigenforms
f,g\in S_{2}(p)
,
equality of Hecke eigenvalues up to
r
forces
f=g
.
Analytic multiplicity-one results show that for distinct Hecke eigenforms
f\neq g

there exists an index
n\ll\,p\log p
with
a_{n}(f)\neq a_{n}(g)
, and assuming the Generalized
Riemann Hypothesis for the relevant Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions one may take

r=O\!\bigl((\log p)^{2}(\log\log p)^{4}\bigr),

with
C=16
in the case of weight
2
and prime level
p
for
p
sufficiently large

[
18
, Thms. 2–3]
.

By multiplicativity and the Hecke relations, a discrepancy at such an index
n
implies a
difference in the Hecke eigenvalues at some prime
\ell\mid n
, and hence

\mathbf{t}_{f}\neq\mathbf{t}_{g}
once sufficiently many primes are included.
However, these results certify only non-equality and do not provide a usable quantitative lower
bound on
\|\mathbf{t}_{f}-\mathbf{t}_{g}\|_{2}
, as the resulting gap may be
arbitrarily small. Our main result of this section is the following quantitative separation of eigenvalues.

Theorem 3.6

(
GRH
)
.

Let
p
be a prime, and let
f_{k}
and
f_{j}
be two distinct normalized and simultaneous cuspidal
Hecke eigenforms in
S_{2}(\Gamma_{0}(p))
.
Assume the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for the relevant Rankin–Selberg
L
-functions.
If
Y
and
X
satisfy
X\gg Y\gg(\log p)^{4}
, and

\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{r}
are the primes in the interval
[Y,X]
, then

\|\mathbf{t}_{k}-\mathbf{t}_{j}\|_{2}\geq 1.

In order to prove Theorem
3.6
, we introduce several tools from analytic number theory.
We refer the reader to sections 1.6 and 1.8 of
[
11
]
. We note that the following discussion can also be examined from a representation-theoretic perspective. We
Let
f_{k}
and
f_{j}
be normalized cuspidal Hecke eigenforms of weight
2
and level
p

(with trivial nebentypus).

The Rankin–Selberg
L
-function
L(s,f_{k}\times\overline{f_{j}})
is the degree
4

L
-function
whose Euler product (for
\real(s)>1
) is given by

L(s,f_{k}\times\overline{f_{j}})=\prod_{\ell}\prod_{i,j=1}^{2}\Bigl(1-\alpha_{\ell,i}(f_{k})\,\overline{\alpha_{\ell,j}(f_{j})}\,\ell^{-s}\Bigr)^{-1},

where
\{\alpha_{\ell,1}(f_{k}),\alpha_{\ell,2}(f_{k})\}
and
\{\alpha_{\ell,1}(f_{j}),\alpha_{\ell,2}(f_{j})\}

are the Satake parameters at
\ell\nmid p
(so

\alpha_{\ell,1}(f_{k})+\alpha_{\ell,2}(f_{k})=\lambda_{\ell}(f_{k})
and

\alpha_{\ell,1}(f_{k})\alpha_{\ell,2}(f_{k})=1
, and similarly for
f_{j}
).
Equivalently, the logarithmic derivative of
L(s,f_{k}\times\overline{f_{j}})
encodes the correlations

\lambda_{\ell}(f_{k})\lambda_{\ell}(f_{j})
:

-\frac{L^{\prime}}{L}(s,f_{k}\times\overline{f_{j}})=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\Lambda(n)\,b_{k,j}(n)}{n^{s}},\qquad b_{k,j}(\ell)=\lambda_{\ell}(f_{k})\lambda_{\ell}(f_{j})\ \ (\ell\nmid p),

(2)

where
\Lambda
is the von Mangoldt function.

Analytically,
L(s,f_{k}\times\overline{f_{j}})
admits analytic continuation and a functional equation
[
20
, chapter 5]
.
Moreover, if
f_{k}\neq f_{j}
then
L(s,f_{k}\times\overline{f_{j}})
is entire, whereas

L(s,f_{k}\times\overline{f_{k}})=\zeta(s)\,L(s,\mathrm{sym}^{2}f_{k})

has a simple pole at
s=1
, and similarly for
f_{j}
.
These facts imply a sharp distinction between
diagonal
and
off-diagonal
prime sums:
the diagonal terms have a main term of size
X
, while the off-diagonal correlation has no main term. We are now ready to prove theorem
3.6
.

Proof.

Fix parameters
1\leq Y<X
and define the interval second moment

S(Y,X):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y<\ell\leq X\end{subarray}}\bigl(\lambda_{\ell}(f_{k})-\lambda_{\ell}(f_{j})\bigr)^{2}\log\ell.

Expanding gives

S(Y,X)=\sum_{Y<\ell\leq X}\lambda_{\ell}(f_{k})^{2}\log\ell+\sum_{Y<\ell\leq X}\lambda_{\ell}(f_{j})^{2}\log\ell-2\sum_{Y<\ell\leq X}\lambda_{\ell}(f_{k})\lambda_{\ell}(f_{j})\log\ell.

Under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for the relevant Rankin–Selberg
L-functions, the corresponding prime number theorem is proved in full generality in
[
20
, Theorem 5.15]
( see also
[
38
]
). A similar unconditional version is obtained in
[
29
]
. By the discussion above, this implies that
(for
i\in\{j,k\}
)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\leq T\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\ell}(f_{i})^{2}\log\ell=T+O\!\bigl(T^{1/2}\log^{2}(QT)\bigr),\qquad\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\leq T\end{subarray}}\lambda_{\ell}(f_{k})\lambda_{\ell}(f_{j})\log\ell=O\!\bigl(T^{1/2}\log^{2}(QT)\bigr),

where
Q
is the analytic conductor (here
Q\asymp p^{2}
).
Subtracting the estimates at
T=X
and
T=Y
yields

\sum_{Y<\ell\leq X}\lambda_{\ell}(f_{i})^{2}\log\ell=(X-Y)+O\!\bigl(X^{1/2}\log^{2}(QX)+Y^{1/2}\log^{2}(QY)\bigr),

and similarly

\sum_{Y<\ell\leq X}\lambda_{\ell}(f_{k})\lambda_{\ell}(f_{j})\log\ell=O\!\bigl(X^{1/2}\log^{2}(QX)+Y^{1/2}\log^{2}(QY)\bigr).

Consequently,

S(Y,X)=2(X-Y)+O\!\bigl(X^{1/2}\log^{2}(QX)\bigr).

In particular, if
X\gg Y\gg(\log p)^{4}
,
then the main term dominates and we may ensure

S(Y,X)\geq c(X-Y)

with
c>1
by taking the implicit constants sufficiently large.

On the other hand, if
|\lambda_{\ell}(f_{k})-\lambda_{\ell}(f_{j})|<c_{0}<\sqrt{c}
for all primes
Y<\ell\leq X
,
where
c_{0}
is picked such that

S(Y,X)<c_{0}^{2}\sum_{Y<\ell\leq X}\log\ell<c(X-Y),

we get a contradiction. In other words, if the individual point-wise distance of eigenvalues is less than a constant, then the average needs be also smaller. Hence there exists a prime
\ell\in(Y,X]
with
\ell\nmid p
such that

|\lambda_{\ell}(f_{k})-\lambda_{\ell}(f_{j})|\geq 1.

Since the tag set
\{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r}\}
contains all primes in
(Y,X]
, then this implies

\|\mathbf{t}_{k}-\mathbf{t}_{j}\|_{2}\geq 1
.
∎

Quantum Sampling of Hard Curves

In this section, we present the main application of the theory developed in this work: the generation of a
random supersingular elliptic curve with unknown endomorphism ring.
The mains results of this section are theorems
4.2
and
4.3
.

We emphasize that, throughout this section, we focus on supersingular elliptic curves without level
structure. The results are therefore stated in this setting, but can be adapted in a
manner to incorporate level structures if required.

We start by reviewing some fundamentals of quantum computing, a standard reference is
[
24
, Ch. 3-7]
.

Qubit.
A qubit
\ket{x}=\alpha\ket{0}+\beta\ket{1}
is a
2
-dimensional unit vector with coefficients in
\mathbb{C}
, i.e.,
x=(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{C}^{2}
, satisfying the normalization constraint

|\alpha|^{2}+|\beta|^{2}=1
.
When neither
\alpha
nor
\beta
is equal to
0
, we say that
\ket{x}
is in

superposition
.

An
n
-qubit quantum register

\ket{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha_{i}\ket{i}

is a unit vector in
\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{\otimes n}
, where

\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
and
\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}|\alpha_{i}|^{2}=1
.
We call the set
\{\ket{0},\ket{1},\ldots,\ket{2^n-1}\}
the

computational basis
.
When
\ket{x}
cannot be written as a tensor product of single-qubit states,
we say that
\ket{x}
is
entangled
.

Measurement.

Unless otherwise stated, measurements are performed in the computational basis.
After measuring a quantum register

\ket{x}=\sum_{i=0}^{2^{n}-1}\alpha_{i}\ket{i}

in the computational basis, the state collapses to
\ket{i}
with probability

|\alpha_{i}|^{2}
.

Quantum Algorithms.

A quantum algorithm
\mathcal{A}
is a sequence of unitary operations
U_{i}
,
where unitary operations map unit vectors to unit vectors and preserve the
normalization constraint of quantum registers.
A quantum oracle algorithm
\mathcal{A}^{O}
is defined similarly, except that it
may query the oracle
O
after (or before) executing a unitary
U_{i}
.
Since quantum computation must be reversible, we model an oracle

O:X\rightarrow Y
by a unitary transformation
U_{O}
acting as

U_{O}:\ket{x}\ket{y}\mapsto\ket{x}\ket{y \oplus O(x)}.

We only consider sequential quantum algorithms.

Quantum Phase Estimation.
It is the primitive that, given a unitary
U
and an eigenstate
\lvert\psi\rangle

such that
U\lvert\psi\rangle=e^{2\pi i\lambda}\lvert\psi\rangle
for an unknown eigenvalue
\lambda\in[0,1)
,
outputs an
m
-bit estimate
\tilde{\lambda}
of
\lambda
. The standard circuit prepares an
m
-qubit control
register in
\lvert+\rangle^{\otimes m}
, applies controlled powers
U^{2^{k}}
(for
k=0,\dots,m-1
) to imprint
phases
e^{2\pi i2^{k}\lambda}
via phase kickback, then applies the inverse quantum Fourier transform

QFT_{m}^{-1}
to the control register and measures in the computational basis. The measurement yields

\tilde{\lambda}
concentrated near
\lambda
with additive error

\lvert\tilde{\lambda}-\lambda\rvert=O(2^{-m})
(with high probability), using
O(2^{m})
controlled-
U

applications in the straightforward implementation. Given some state
\ket{\psi}
we denote by
\mathrm{QPE}^{(\varepsilon)}_{U}\!\big(\ket{\psi_i}\big)
the resulting entangled state after applying QPE to
U\ket{\psi}
up to precision
\varepsilon
.

In what follows, our quantum states live in the Hilbert space
\mathbb{C}^{K}
,
where
K
is the size of the set of all elliptic curves defined over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
. We denoted by
V_{n}
the subspace generated by the supersingular curves over

\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
. Using Proposition
2.1
, we index the computational basis by
pairs
(j,b)
, where
j\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
is the
j
-invariant and
b
denotes the
corresponding twisting data. Thus, the computational basis is

\{\ket{(j,b)}\}
.

We note that, although this parametrization is straightforward, it has not been
made explicit in prior work. Making it concrete plays an important role in our
analysis. In principle, one could instead represent curves by their Weierstrass
coefficients
(a,b)
; however, this would require approximately twice as many qubits.

We now present the high-level idea of our sampling algorithm; the remainder of this section is devoted to
analyzing its correctness and performance.
Let
\ket{E_0}

H_{\ell}:=\frac{A_{\ell}}{\sqrt{\ell}}.

Applying the unitary operator
e^{itH_{\ell}}
to
\ket{E_0}

The family of matrices
\{A_{\ell}\}

Repeating this procedure sequentially for the unitaries

\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_{1,i}}\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_{2,i}}\cdots\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_{r,i}}\ket{\phi_i}.

Finally, measuring the eigenstate register in the vertex basis

\{\ket{(j,b)}\}
produces a supersingular elliptic curve sampled according
to the induced distribution.

In the rest of this section we analyze:
(i) the quantum gate complexity of
QuantumSampling
, i.e., the number of elementary gates (and in particular
controlled-

invocations) required to reach the target precision; and
(ii) the induced output distribution on curves obtained by measuring the final vertex register. We start by analyzing the correctness and running time of our algorithm.

Proposition 4.1
.

Given primes
p
and
\ell\nmid p
, the gate complexity to execute one call to Quantum Phase estimation
\mathrm{QPE}_{\,e^{iH}}^{\varepsilon}
with failure probability
\eta
, where
H=A_{\ell}/\sqrt{\ell}
, is

\tilde{O}\!\left(\log\left(\frac{1}{\eta}\right)\left[\ell^{5/4}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{3/2}\;+\;\ell^{3/4}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}\Big(\log\frac{1}{\eta}+\log\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big)\right]\right).

Proof.

Since the eigenvalues of
H
lie in
[-2,2]
(Deligne’s Bound
[
14
]
), the eigenphases of
U
are in
[-2,2]

(as
2<\pi
), hence there is no modular ambiguity between an eigenphase and the
corresponding eigenvalue.

Fix a target additive phase accuracy
\varepsilon
and an overall failure probability
\eta
.
Standard QPE uses

m\;:=\;\left\lceil\log_{2}\!\Big(\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\Big)\right\rceil

phase bits, and thus requires controlled applications of the powers

U^{2^{j}}=e^{iH2^{j}}
for
j=0,1,\dots,m-1
. Equivalently, one execution of QPE requires
implementing controlled Hamiltonian evolutions for times

t_{j}\;=\;2^{j}\qquad(0\leq j\leq m-1).

To reduce the failure probability to at most
\eta
, we repeat QPE

R=\Theta(\log(1/\eta))
times and output the median estimate.

We implement each
e^{iHt}
using sparse-Hamiltonian simulation as in
Berry–Childs–Kothari
[
6
]
. Their cost is expressed in terms of

\tau(t)\;:=\;d\,\|H\|_{\max}\,t,

where
d
is the sparsity and
\|H\|_{\max}=\max_{x,y}|H_{x,y}|
.
For
H=A_{\ell}/\sqrt{\ell}
, we have
d=\ell+1
and
\|H\|_{\max}=1/\sqrt{\ell}
, hence

\tau(t)\;=\;(\ell+1)\frac{t}{\sqrt{\ell}}\;=\;\kappa\,t,\qquad\kappa\;:=\;\frac{\ell+1}{\sqrt{\ell}}.

We allocate simulation error per controlled evolution as

\displaystyle\delta_{\mathrm{sim}}\;:=\;\frac{\eta}{10Rm},

so that over the
m
controlled evolutions and
R
repetitions, a union bound
ensures that the total probability of any simulation failure is at most
\eta/10
.
Thus

\log\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathrm{sim}}}\;=\;\log\!\Big(\Theta(Rm/\eta)\Big)\;=\;O\!\Big(\log(1/\eta)+\log m\Big).

Specializing
[
6
, Theorem 3]
with
\alpha=1
, each evolution
e^{iHt}
can be implemented
within error
\delta_{\mathrm{sim}}
using

O\!\Big(\tau(t)^{3/2}+\tau(t)^{1/2}\log(1/\delta_{\mathrm{sim}})\Big)

oracle queries. Therefore, the
j
-th controlled power costs

Q_{j}\;=\;O\!\Big((\kappa 2^{j})^{3/2}+(\kappa 2^{j})^{1/2}\log(1/\delta_{\mathrm{sim}})\Big).

Summing over
j=0,\dots,m-1
and using geometric-series bounds yields

Q_{\mathrm{QPE}}\;=\;O\!\left(\kappa^{3/2}\,2^{3m/2}\;+\;\kappa^{1/2}\,\log(1/\delta_{\mathrm{sim}})\,2^{m/2}\right).

Since
m=\lceil\log_{2}(4/\varepsilon)\rceil
implies

, we obtain

Q_{\mathrm{QPE}}\;=\;O\!\left(\kappa^{3/2}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{3/2}\;+\;\kappa^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}\log\frac{1}{\delta_{\mathrm{sim}}}\right).

Finally, repeating
R=\Theta(\log(1/\eta))
times gives the total query complexity

Q_{\mathrm{total}}\;=\;O\!\left(\log\frac{1}{\eta}\left[\kappa^{3/2}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{3/2}\;+\;\kappa^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)^{1/2}\Big(\log\frac{1}{\eta}+\log\log\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big)\right]\right).

Using the square-root Velu algorithm of
[
5
, Appendix A]
to compute isogenies, the gate cost of one coherent call to compute the isometry

\ket{E}\mapsto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\ell+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell+1}\ket{E}\ket{E_i},

where the curves
E_{i}

\ell
-neighbors of
E
, is
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{\ell})
. Then the gate complexity to execute one QPE is

the result then follows.

We now turn to the problem of studying the distribution of the output curve. Conceptually, starting from the initial vertex state
\ket{E_0}
, each application of quantum phase estimation (QPE) progressively dephases the state by eliminating coherence between eigenspaces of the adjacency operators. After sufficiently many applications, each surviving component lies in a one-dimensional joint eigenspace common to all operators
A_{\ell}
. We now gather our theoretical machinery from previous sections to prove the following key theorem.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and let
E^{\prime}/\mathbb{F}_{p^{2}}
be any supersingular elliptic curve.
For any
\varepsilon>0
, the output
E
of Algorithm
1

0<\Pr[E=E^{\prime}]\;\ll_{\varepsilon}\;p^{-1+\varepsilon}

(3)

Proof.

By the
\varepsilon
-separation assumption, the joint QPE tag

\ket{\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i}=\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_{1,i}}\cdots\ket{\tilde{\lambda}_{r,i}}

uniquely identifies the common eigenvector
\ket{\phi_i}
and the tag states are orthonormal, i.e.,

\langle\tilde{\mathbf{t}_{j}}\mid\tilde{\mathbf{t}_{i}}\rangle=\delta_{ij}.

Hence the final state can be written as

\ket{\Psi}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\,\ket{\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i}\ket{\phi_i},\qquad\alpha_{i}=\phi_{i}(E_{0})=\innerproduct{\phi_i}{E_0}.

Let
\ket{E'}
be any vertex basis state. Measuring only the vertex register yields

\Pr[E=E^{\prime}]=\big\|(\mathbf{1}_{\mathrm{tags}}\otimes\bra{E'})\ket{\Psi}\big\|^{2}=\left\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\innerproduct{E'}{\phi_i}\,\ket{\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i}\right\|^{2}.

Expanding the squared norm and using orthonormality of the tag states, all cross terms vanish, giving

\Pr[E=E^{\prime}]=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\alpha_{i}|^{2}\,\big|\innerproduct{E'}{\phi_i}\big|^{2}.

(4)

In particular, (
4

\Pr[E=E^{\prime}]>0

.
Indeed, write
\rho:=\Tr_{\mathrm{tags}}(\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi})=\sum_{i}|\alpha_{i}|^{2}\ket{\phi_i}\bra{\phi_i}
,
so that
\Pr[E=E^{\prime}]=\bra{E'}\rho\ket{E'}
. Let

K:=\operatorname{span}\{A_{\ell}^{k}\ket{E_0}:k\geq 0\}

be the Krylov subspace generated by
A_{\ell}
for some prime
\ell\nmid p
. By spectral decomposition,

K=\operatorname{span}\{\ket{\phi_i}:\alpha_{i}\neq 0\}

and hence
\ker(\rho)=K^{\perp}
. If
\bra{E'}\rho\ket{E'}=0
, then
\ket{E'}\in\ker(\rho)=K^{\perp}
, so

\bra{E'}A_{\ell}^{k}\ket{E_0}=0
for all
k\geq 0
. But note that

\bra{E'}A_{\ell}^{k}\ket{E_0}=\#\{\textnormal{paths in }\mathcal{G}(p,\ell)\textnormal{ from }E_{0}\textnormal{ to }E^{\prime}\textnormal{of length }k\},

and since
\mathcal{G}(p,\ell)
is connected and
A_{\ell}
has
nonnegative weights, there exists
k
such that

(A_{\ell}^{k})_{E^{\prime},E_{0}}=\bra{E'}A_{\ell}^{k}\ket{E_0}>0,

a contradiction.

Notice that if we apply theorem
3.3
to (
4
) we obtain
\Pr[E=E^{\prime}]\ll_{\varepsilon}p^{-1/2+\varepsilon}
(since
\sum|\alpha_{i}|^{2}=1)
; however we can do much better.

Recall that
n:=|\mathcal{S}_{p}|\asymp p
, and define

r_{E}:=\big(|\phi_{1}(E)|^{2},\dots,|\phi_{n}(E)|^{2}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad u:=\Big(\tfrac{1}{n},\dots,\tfrac{1}{n}\Big).

Then (
4
) may be written as

r_{E_{0}}\cdot r_{E^{\prime}}=\big(u+(r_{E_{0}}-u)\big)\cdot\big(u+(r_{E^{\prime}}-u)\big),

and the cross terms cancel, yielding

\Pr[E=E^{\prime}]=\|u\|_{2}^{2}+(r_{E_{0}}-u)\cdot(r_{E^{\prime}}-u).

By Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

|(r_{E_{0}}-u)\cdot(r_{E^{\prime}}-u)|\leq\|r_{E_{0}}-u\|_{2}\,\|r_{E^{\prime}}-u\|_{2},

and Theorem
3.4
(fourth-moment bound), which states

\sup_{x,y}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\phi_{i}^{2}(x)-\phi^{2}(y))^{2}\ll_{\varepsilon}p^{-1+\varepsilon},

implies
\|r_{E}-u\|_{2}^{2}\ll_{\varepsilon}p^{-1+\varepsilon}
uniformly in
E
.
Since
\|u\|_{2}^{2}=n^{-1}
, we obtain

\Pr[E=E^{\prime}]=n^{-1}+O_{\varepsilon}(p^{-1+\varepsilon})\ll_{\varepsilon}p^{-1+\varepsilon}.

∎

Remark 3
.

As suggested by Heuristic
1
, the exponent
\varepsilon
could plausibly be replaced by a logarithmic factor (e.g.
\log^{2}p
). At present, however, unconditional results only achieve this bound up to a power loss.

Remark 4
.

We note that
[
16
, section 5.1]
relies on the heuristic assumption that the
\ell
-isogeny adjacency matrices have simple spectrum (i.e., one-dimensional eigenspaces) in order to prove sampling from the limiting distribution of the continuous-time quantum walk. In our setting, this assumption is unnecessary: we do not require convergence to the limiting distribution. Instead, it suffices for our purposes to obtain a suitable bound on the induced distribution, which is all that we use.

We now discuss both implications and limitations of theorems
4.3
and
4.2
. First, observe that our procedure neither computes nor uses an explicit isogeny path from the
starting curve
E_{0}
to the output curve
E
. The quantum state evolves continuously via Hamiltonian
simulation and is subsequently dephased by repeated applications of QPE, rather than by traversing a
discrete walk in the isogeny graph. Moreover, the post-measurement state corresponds to a superposition
of approximate eigenvalue labels associated with the eigenvectors contributing to the measurement of

E
. These eigenvalue approximations do not encode local path information; instead, they reflect global
spectral properties of the graph.

In addition, the superposition eigenvalue approximations obtained via QPE are not expected to be helpful in reconstructing isogeny
paths. Even if they were, eigenvalues of the supersingular
\ell
-isogeny graph are known to be equidistributed in the
interval
[-2\sqrt{\ell},\,2\sqrt{\ell}]

-
see
[
35
]
-
and within the QPE precision

1/(2\sqrt{r})=O(1/\mathrm{polylog}(p))
there are exponentially many eigenvalues compatible with any
given approximation
\tilde{\lambda}
. Consequently, the final measurement outcome is determined solely
by the projection onto eigenvectors whose vertex amplitudes are exponentially small, as implied by our
sup-norm bounds. In other words, our algorithm can be thought of preparing a superposition state of eigenvectors of the
A_{\ell}^{\prime}s
and then merely measure it. This information therefore should provide no meaningful advantage for recovering isogeny
paths or computing endomorphism rings.

Secondly, the bound (
3
) on the output distribution
guarantees the hardness of the sampled curve from an information-theoretic
perspective. Recall that the min-entropy of the output distribution is

H_{\infty}(E)\;:=\;-\log_{2}\!\left(\max_{E^{\prime}\in\mathcal{S}}\Pr[E=E^{\prime}]\right).

Any pointwise bound of the form

\Pr[E=E^{\prime}]\;\ll\;p^{-\delta}\qquad\text{for some }\delta>0

implies

H_{\infty}(E)\;\geq\;\delta\log_{2}p-O(1),

and therefore that the output is information-theoretically hidden among

\Omega(p^{\delta})
candidates.

In particular, in our unconditional analysis we obtain such a bound with

\delta=1-\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
, yielding an output
distribution that is nearly uniform.

Practical parameters.

Under the plausible eigenvector delocalization heuristic
1
,
the output distribution is close to uniform over
\mathcal{S}
up to
polylogarithmic factors. In this regime,

H_{\infty}(E)\;\gtrsim\;\log_{2}p-2\log_{2}\log p,

and thus one may take
p\approx 2^{\lambda}
while retaining
\lambda
bits
of security in the sense of min-entropy, up to lower-order
\log\log p
terms.

More generally, our bound

\Pr[E=E^{\prime}]\;\ll\;p^{-1+\varepsilon}

implies that for any fixed subset
T\subseteq\mathcal{S}
of size

|T|=O(p^{\alpha})
with
\alpha<1
, we have

\Pr[E\in T]\;\ll\;p^{-(1-\alpha)+\varepsilon}.

For cryptographic sizes of
p
, this probability is negligible for
sufficiently small
\varepsilon
.

This observation is particularly relevant for subsets of curves with known
additional structure. For instance, the set of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-rational
supersingular curves, which has cardinality
O(p^{1/2})
, admits a quadratic
speedup for endomorphism ring computation over generic algorithms
[
33
]
,
although the resulting complexity remains exponential. One may further apply Kuperberg’s algorithm
[
27
]
to
obtain subexponential time at the expense of subexponential space, which
significantly limits its practical relevance.

A similar discussion applies to the set of curves admitting an
M
-small
endomorphism, that is, an endomorphism of degree at most
M
. The endomorphism rings of such curves are computable in
time polynomial in
\log p
and
M
; see
[
30
]
. Since the number of such
curves is
\ll M^{3/2}
, the probability that a randomly sampled curve lies in
this set is again negligible.
