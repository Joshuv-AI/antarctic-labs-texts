Filled space and filled time illusions vary similarly with analogous stimulus parameters
Authors: Tadas Surkys
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Visual filled space (Oppel-Kundt) and auditory filled time illusions distort the perception of spatial width and temporal length, respectively. Although these two illusions occur in different physical domains, space and time, their construction principles are analogous, raising the question of whether they are related. In this study, visual and auditory stimuli were constructed in a comparable manner and presented in psychophysical experiments to investigate these distortions. The paired results revealed correlations in illusion strength change across various stimulus parameter changes. Illusion strength increased with the number of fillers, saturating at about six fillers. Relative illusion strength decreased with increasing interval length. Reversing stimulus order had little effect. Introducing gaps reduced illusion strength, but only for visual stimuli. These findings support the notion that related perceptual processes, possibly grounded in closely related neural structures, underlie both illusions.

Introduction

Perceptual illusions of filled space and filled time pertain to different domains of the physical world but share similar construction principles. Both types of illusions occur when the perceptually filled interval, whether temporal or spatial, appears longer than an empty one (Fig. 1). For example, a visual space interval marked by dots or lines and filled with visual elements appears longer than an empty one. Similarly, the filled time illusion manifests when a time period filled with continuous or dividing sounds stimuli appears longer than an empty interval1–3. The version of the illusion with dividing sounds is also referred to as the divided time illusion.

It is known that common brain structures are associated with both time and space perception4,5. A Theory of Magnitude (ATOM), introduced by Walsh6, posits that the brain represents the perceived magnitude of quantities—distance, duration, number, intensity—via a partly shared network. An fMRI activation-likelihood meta-analysis showed that spatial and temporal tasks jointly activate the intraparietal sulcus, pre-supplementary motor area (pre-SMA), right frontal operculum, and bilateral anterior insula. In all regions except the insula, time-selective activation peaks transit to space-selective to adjacent peaks, forming smooth gradients and based on this data the Gradient Theory of Magnitude (GradiATOM) was formulated7. This anatomical overlap suggest that illusions based on common perceptual information processing principles are likely to occur. Consistent with this prediction, a recent study created a distracter-induced time-duration illusion by applying the centroid-extraction principle—the same mechanism that explains Müller-Lyer-type distortions in visual space8,9.

In this study, I investigate the Oppel-Kundt illusion as a specific case of the filled space illusion. The typical Oppel-Kundt figure consists of two intervals marked by three vertices, with one interval filled with regularly arranged stripes. Previous studies indicate that the magnitude of this illusion increases with the number of filling stripes up to a certain point before it saturates or decreases slightly. Eventually, the illusion can be induced with any continuous luminance contour that fills the gap10,11. The absolute Oppel-Kundt illusion strength rises with the width of the filled interval12, although the relative strength decreases.

Analogously, in the domain of filled time illusions, the perception of duration can be affected by the number of discrete stimuli within the interval, with more dividers making the duration appear longer1. However, similar to the Oppel-Kundt illusion, the relative strength of the filled time illusion decreases as the duration of the interval increases13.

Auditory stimuli were chosen for the temporal tasks and visual stimuli for the spatial tasks because each sense offers its greatest precision in the matching dimension. The auditory system can resolve differences of only a few milliseconds, whereas visual timing thresholds are typically ten‑fold larger; vision, in turn, provides minute spatial acuity that audition cannot approach14,15. Using the modality that is most accurate for each dimension reduces sensory noise and yields more reliable psychometric estimates of illusion strength. This choice also aligns with the historical record: most filled-time studies have used sounds and most Oppel-Kundt work has used visual patterns, so the present design remains comparable with—and directly extendable from—previous research.

Building on earlier work, this study has two objectives. First, it delivers what appears to be the first within‑observer, parameter‑matched comparison of the Oppel‑Kundt (filled‑space) and divided‑time (filled‑time) illusions, placing the discretely divided and continuously filled temporal variants side‑by‑side with their spatial counterparts. Second, it traces how illusion magnitude changes when the number of fillers is extended from 0 to 20 stripes or sound bursts, revealing the full growth‑and‑saturation function that previous studies only hinted at1,13. The width or duration of discretely filled intervals is varied, and an explicit gap is introduced between filled and empty sub-intervals—a condition not tested previously for either illusion. This manipulation probes whether the illusion requires contiguous input. The design also checks whether reversing the order of filled and empty parts alters the effect —a factor not previously tested for the time illusion. Guided by the Gradient Theory of Magnitude (GradiATOM), I hypothesise that a single magnitude‑coding mechanism will yield parallel growth, saturation, and gap‑sensitivity profiles across vision and audition; consistent divergences would instead point to modality‑specific processes arising from the functional gradients. To test this hypothesis, illusion strength was measured for continuously filled, discretely divided, and gap‑separated configurations in both domains while manipulating divider number, interval length, and stimulus order, with all other parameters held constant for the same group of observers.

Methods

To assess how different factors influence the strength of filled space and filled time illusions, key stimulus parameters were varied, including the number of fillers, interval lengths, stimulus order, and the presence of gaps. Detailed procedures for participant recruitment, stimuli, data collection procedures, and data analysis are outlined below.

Participants

A total of 16 participants (13 females and 3 males, aged 19–45 years) were recruited for the psychophysical experiments. Of the 16 participants, three produced unstable fits in the auditory task, leaving 13 usable data sets for analysis in both modalities. Of these 13, ten volunteers completed the three main experiments—divider-number series, gap insertion, and order reversal—in both modalities. A smaller subset of four observers additionally participated in the filled-interval-length experiment. All participants had self-reported normal or corrected-to-normal vision and normal hearing. They provided informed consent before participation, adhering to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. The experimental protocols were approved by the Kaunas Regional Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (permission number BE9-5).

Auditory stimuli

Auditory stimuli were generated using MATLAB (MathWorks, R2020a) and presented via the Fame DT-790 headphones in a separate room. The auditory stimuli (Fig. 1) consisted of two time intervals, marked by three short sound signals. When a gap was introduced, the two intervals were marked by four sounds. The reference interval was filled with equally distributed sound bursts or, in case of control condition, was empty. Reference interval duration was 1 s, except during experiments where different durations were tested. All sounds were 1,000-Hz sinusoidal waves with an intensity of approximately 60 decibels. Sound bursts were of 30-ms duration. The loudness of each sound rose and fell linearly over 3.4 ms.

In the first group of the experiments, stimuli were presented with different numbers of filling sound bursts (1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 13, 20). In the case of a fully filled interval, a continuous tone was used, and there were no separate short sound bursts to mark the start and end of the first interval. The control stimuli consisted of no filling (0) sounds.

All the remaining experiments used six filler sound bursts. The illusion magnitude was tested at different durations (0.5 s, 0.75 s, 1 s, 1.5 s, 2 s) of the filled interval. In the experiments with reversed stimuli (Fig. 1b), the first interval was empty, and the second interval contained filling sounds. In the experiments with separated intervals (Fig. 1c), a gap of 1.5 s was introduced between the reference and test intervals.

Visual stimuli.

All visual stimuli were presented on a Philips 243 s monitor with a diagonal of 60.5 cm and 1080 × 1920 pixels. The monitor was placed at 160 cm from the observer head at which one screen pixel subtended 0.5 × 0.5 min of arc. The monitor’s gamma was corrected using an OptiCAL RS232 photometer. The stimuli were presented monocularly on a background of 0.1 cd/m2 luminance. A chin rest was used to minimize head movements. The Oppel-Kundt illusory figure (Fig. 1) consisted of vertical stripes each of 1 × 15 arc min and 55 cd/m2 luminance.

In all experiments, the figure had two horizontally aligned intervals on screen marked by three (Fig. 1a and b) or four vertical stripes (Fig. 1c). The reference interval was of 120 arc min (2 arc degrees) width on the screen, except when different lengths were tested.

In the experiments with a variable number of fillings the left part was filled with stripes and at different experiment sessions had different numbers of filling stripes, i.e., 0 (control), 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 9, 13, 20, or fully filled i.e., uniform band.

In the remaining experiments, stimuli had 6 filling stripes. Illusion magnitudes were measured at different (60, 90, 120, 180, 240 arc min) reference interval lengths. In the experiments with reversed stimuli (Fig. 1b), the figures were flipped so that the left interval served as the test interval, and the right interval was the reference interval filled with stripes. And there were experiments with introduced gap between filled and empty intervals (Fig. 1c). The gap interval was of 180 arc min.

Procedure.

Experiments were run with PsyExTool 0.819 (graphical interface) in MATLAB R2020a. Illusion strength was obtained with the Psi adaptive procedure16 from Palamedes Toolbox 1.1118, which chose each comparison value so as to minimise the entropy of the joint posterior over the threshold (µ) and slope (β) of a four-parameter logistic psychometric function. Guess (γ) and lapse (λ) rates were both fixed at 0.02. On every trial the algorithm presented an interval that was equally likely to fall above or below the current µ estimate, ensuring balanced sampling. Participants made a two-alternative forced-choice response (“first interval longer”/“second interval longer”) by pressing the corresponding arrow key. Each person first completed at least five minutes of practice with both the visual and auditory stimuli.

In each experiment session, a single reference stimulus part configuration was presented 17 times with adaptively selected lengths for the variable test part. Each experiment was repeated a minimum of three times which led to 51 measurements for each stimuli configuration. The exception was the experiments with different filled-part lengths, which were repeated five times, yielding 85 measurements per configuration. The order of the experiments was randomized. Participants completed experiments on at least four separate days. Participants always completed the visual (filled space) experiments before the auditory (filled time) experiments; however, within each modality block, the individual sessions were presented in a different randomized order for each participant.

As of visual experiments, the viewing duration was not limited. After each response, the screen remained blank for one second before the next configuration was presented. In the adaptive two-alternative forced-choice procedures width of the test part varied in steps of 2.4 arc min. At the beginning, participants were taught and during experiments reminded to occasionally pause, blink, or look aside to avoid adaptation effects.

In the auditory experiments, to minimize data noise from potentially missed stimuli, participants were allowed to replay the stimulus before making a decision. The length of the test part varied in steps of 0.02 s. After the participant’s response, the onset of the next stimulus occurred after 2.6 s.

Consequently, it was not possible to estimate thresholds and the results from these three participants were excluded, leaving data from 13 participants for further analysis. While the number of participants was limited, it was sufficient for detecting general trends and significant effects in illusion strength across the experimental conditions. The consistency of the results across participants supports the reliability of the observed phenomena.

To analyze data the Palamedes Toolbox 1.1118 and PsyExTool 0.819 in the environment of MathWorks MATLAB (2020a) were used. Descriptive statistics, probability, and correlation analyses were performed using Microsoft 365 Excel, Makie.jl in Julia language20,21.

The data passed the Shapiro-Wilk test (p > 0.05), confirming the assumption of normality. Paired samples t-tests were used to compare the illusion strength between different experimental conditions within subjects. An F-test for equality of variances was used to compare the variability of illusion strength between groups. Effect sizes were calculated for every statistical test: Cohen’s d was computed for all paired-samples t-tests, and partial η² was reported for each F-test using η²ₚ.

Results

To explore the relationship between filled space and filled time illusions, psychophysical experiments were conducted to examine how various stimulus parameters influence illusion strength in both modalities. The following results detail the findings on how changes in the number of fillers, interval lengths, stimulus order, and the introduction of gaps affect the perception of spatial and temporal intervals. In each trial, participants compared two intervals: a reference interval, which remained constant, and a test interval, which varied adaptively throughout the experimental session.

Illusion strength variation with number of fillers

Illusion strength was first explored by varying the number of fillers. Results from all ten participants indicated a similar trend: the illusion magnitude increased as the number of fillers increased, whether for time (Fig. 2, A) or space (Fig. 2, V) length illusions. For the auditory stimuli, the illusion strength saturated at approximately six fillers, where the test interval perceptually matched the reference interval being 0.6 s longer on average (mean over n = 10 participants, t(9) = 6.29, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.41, 0.87], d = 1.99). For the visual stimuli, the illusion strength also saturated at around six fillers, with the filled interval appearing 32 arc minutes (n = 10, t(9) = 12.53, p < 0.001, 95% CI [26.14, 37.66], d = 3.96) longer on average. Fully filled intervals with continuous sound or a single filled rectangle in visual stimuli produced results consistent with these trends. The correlation coefficient between the averaged illusion strength curves of both modalities was r = 0.9 (p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.65, 0.97]).

Effect of filled interval length on illusion strength

The results showed that illusion strength, represented by the ratio of overestimation size to filled part length, decreased (n = 4) with increasing interval length of the filled part for both types of stimuli. The decrease for the time illusion was steeper than for the filled space illusion (Fig. 3). For the auditory (time) condition the mean illusion strength fell from 0.98 (range 0.69–1.13) at 0.5 s to 0.23 (range 0.14–0.32) at 2 s. For the visual (space) condition the corresponding decline was milder, dropping from 0.34 (range 0.21–0.65) at 60 arc min to 0.15 (range 0.12–0.20) at 240 arc min.

Impact of gap introduction and stimuli reversal

Inserting a gap between the reference filled and test empty intervals did not significantly affect time misperception but reduced the visual illusion strength (Fig. 4, Vg). For visual stimuli with six fillers, illusion strength decreased from a mean of 0.26 to 0.19 when a 1.5° gap was introduced. This reduction was statistically significant (t(9) = 5.15, p < 0.001, 95% CI [0.043, 0.111], d = 1.63). In contrast, for the auditory stimuli, the illusion strength decreased only slightly (Fig. 4, Ag) from 0.65 to 0.62, and this difference was not statistically significant (t(9) = 0.27, p = 0.80, 95% CI [–0.18, 0.23], d = 0.09).

Reversing the order of filled and empty intervals had little impact on illusion strength (Fig. 4, Ar and Vr). For auditory stimuli, the mean strength remained stable (from 0.65 to 0.63; t(9) = − 0.56, p = 0.59, 95% CI [–0.27, 0.16], d = − 0.18). For visual stimuli, the strength changed slightly from 0.26 to 0.29, but the difference was not significant (t(9) = 1.17, p = 0.27, 95% CI [–0.03, 0.08], d = 0.37).

Correlation and variability

The correlation between individual variations in illusion strength for stimuli with six fillers across both modalities was weak (r = 0.31, n = 13, p = 0.3, 95% CI [−0.29, 0.74]). Participants who exhibited a stronger (or weaker) filled space illusion did not consistently show a stronger (or weaker) filled time illusion (Fig. 5).

The coefficient of variation, which measures relative variability by dividing the standard deviation by the mean, was larger for auditory stimuli than for visual stimuli, both having six fillers (0.44 vs. 0.22, F(12, 12) = 19.31, p < 0.01, n = 13, η²ₚ = 0.95). For control stimuli with no fillers, the standard deviation of illusion strength proportions was twice as large for auditory stimuli (0.08 vs. 0.04, F(12, 12) = 3.93, p = 0.02, n = 13, η²ₚ = 0.80).

Discussion

The exploration of filled time and filled space effects using comparable stimuli parameters across auditory and visual domains revealed more detailed phenomenological parallels between these perceptual modalities. In both cases, the illusion magnitude increased with the number of fillers, reaching saturation at around 5–6 fillers. Studies on filled space, particularly the Oppel-Kundt illusion, have repeatedly shown similar findings22,23. Buffardi1 conducted experiments on the filled time illusion, varying the number of dividers. He found that the illusion intensified as the number of dividers increased, but his study was limited to a maximum of five dividers. Our experiments extend this research by providing new data on the illusion’s manifestation across a wider range of numbers of filling sounds.

The results confirm that the relative strength of the divided time illusion decreases with increasing stimulus duration13. For the visual Oppel-Kundt illusion, the absolute illusion strength increases with the length of the filled interval12, but the relative strength decreases. Based on the obtained results of the present study, within the chosen range of intervals, the time illusion decreases approximately twice as fast as the space illusion.

Changing the order of empty and filled intervals did not significantly alter the illusion’s mean strength. From previous research, it is known that visual space anisotropy, where individuals tend to misjudge the midpoint of an interval, sums linearly with illusions of spatial extent introducing asymmetry in the results12. There exists a Time Order Error effect, where the duration of the second time interval is overestimated compared to the first. This effect is typically positive for shorter durations and negative for longer time intervals24. However, durations around one second, as in this study, are indifferent to Time Order Errors. The results of the present study align with this, as no significant asymmetry was observed.

The introduction of the gap between filled and empty intervals reduced the strength of both illusions, but for visual stimulus, it was more pronounced. This indicates that the visual illusion relies more on continuity, while the auditory illusion is less affected by interruption.

Group-level parallels in growth, saturation, and gap sensitivity suggest common perceptual processing principles which align with ATOM/GradiATOM; the modest individual correlation is expected when two closely neighboring—but not identical—neural maps generate the effects.

The illusion strength in the auditory domain was twice as high as in the visual domain, and the variability among participants was also much greater in the auditory domain for both the illusory stimuli and the control conditions. Participants reported feeling more uncertainty and discomfort when comparing time intervals, whether they were dealing with illusory stimuli or control conditions.

There are several limitations warrant mention. The time-illusion analysis is based on 13 observers, with smaller subsets for the gap/reversal (n = 10) and interval-length (n = 4) tests, so statistical power is modest. Because audition was used only for time and vision only for space, modality is confounded with domain; whether the same patterns hold for visual timing or auditory spacing remains to be seen. Finally, blocks were always run in a visual-first order, which may have introduced order effects despite within-block randomization.

In general, manifestations of studied illusions across most conditions closely mimicked each other. In previous research close similarities were observed while comparing Müller-Lyer type visual illusion and analogous newly constructed time illusion8. Both findings reduce the likelihood of coincidence, supporting the hypothesis that there are common principles underlying estimation of perceptual time and space intervals. Moreover, the existence of overlapping structures that process both spatial and time related signals7 would suggest that these phenomena arise from closely related neural networks.
