Epidemiology and Clinical Characteristics of Community-Acquired Pneumonia in Hospitalized Patients at a Single Tertiary Center in Korea
Authors: Kyeo Rae Ju, Sung Yoon Lim, Yeonhoon Jang, Hyunju Lee, Se Young Jung, Sooyoung Yoo, Joon Yhup Lee, Kyul Kim, Sejin Nam, Ho Young Lee, Eu Suk Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCommunity-acquired pneumonia (CAP) frequently requires empirical antibiotic treatment owing to its nonspecific symptoms and the need for urgent intervention. Understanding the epidemiology of patients hospitalized with CAP at a regional level is essential for developing effective treatment strategies. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the epidemiological and clinical characteristics of CAP in patients admitted to a tertiary care hospital in Korea.MethodsUtilizing a common data model, we selected patients aged 18 years and older who were admitted to Seoul National University Bundang Hospital from January 2005 to July 31, 2019. These patients presented with pneumonia-related symptoms, such as fever or a respiratory symptom, and had a pneumonia diagnosis code within 7 days of admission. The medical records of these patients were retrospectively reviewed. Patients without pneumonia, who had been hospitalized within 14 days before the start of the study, and those with pulmonary tuberculosis were excluded.ResultsOut of 7,147 patients with pneumonia-related symptoms, 4,083 were included in the study. The mean age of the patients was 70.1 years, with 66.7% being male. The most prevalent comorbidities were chronic lung disease (42.6%), malignancy (24.4%), and immunocompromised conditions (26.5%). Klebsiella pneumoniae was the most commonly identified pathogen in sputum cultures, followed by Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 3.9%. The factors significantly associated with increased in-hospital mortality were male sex, immunocompromised status, presence of dyspnea, unstable vital signs, low hemoglobin levels, and high C-reactive protein levels.ConclusionIn patients hospitalized with CAP in a tertiary care center in this study, gram-negative bacteria and the traditionally known S. pneumoniae were commonly found. This finding appeared related to patient age and underlying conditions such as chronic lung disease. Furthermore, male sex and an immunocompromised state were found to be significant risk factors for in-hospital mortality along with worse clinical indices.Keywords: Pneumonia, Community-Acquired Infection, Hospitalization, Etiology, Mortality, Common Data Model

INTRODUCTION

Pneumonia remains a leading cause of mortality among infectious diseases worldwide, notwithstanding advancements in diagnostic modalities and antibiotic therapies.1 In Korea, it was the third leading cause of death in 2021, making it the infectious condition responsible for the most deaths. The increase in pneumonia-related mortality is attributed mainly to the growing prevalence of chronic diseases, compounded by the demographic shift toward an aging population.2 This trend highlights the marked impact of age-related vulnerability and comorbidities on the severity and outcomes of pneumonia, underscoring the need for targeted interventions in these high-risk groups.3,4

Based on the clinical circumstances, pneumonia is traditionally categorized into community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP), and healthcare-associated pneumonia (HCAP). This distinction is driven by the variability in causative agents and clinical manifestations of each type. However, in the 2016 guidelines by the Infectious Diseases Society of America and the American Thoracic Society, it was proposed that HCAP could be managed similarly to CAP, anticipating that it may be reclassified under CAP in the future.5 While some patients with CAP can be effectively treated with empirical antibiotics in a community setting, those requiring admission to tertiary care hospitals often present with distinctive characteristics that set them apart from the general population with CAP.6,7,8

Given the nonspecific clinical and radiological manifestations of pneumonia and the challenges in rapidly identifying causative bacteria, empirical antibiotic treatment is mostly initiated before strain identification. This approach is inevitable as prompt antibiotic administration is key in treating pneumonia.9 Consequently, understanding the epidemiology of the causative pathogen infection varies by region, time, and clinical setting, and it is essential to inform treatment strategies and ensure the judicious use of antibiotics. However, there is a significant lack of epidemiological data, especially regarding patients admitted to tertiary hospitals, complicating effective empirical antibiotic selection and treatment.10,11 This absence of epidemiological information on prevalent causative organisms is increasingly critical considering the population’s swift aging and the simultaneous surge in chronic diseases.3,4,6 Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the epidemiological and clinical features of CAP in patients requiring admission to a tertiary care hospital.

METHODS

Study population

A retrospective analysis included patients aged 18 years or older admitted to Seoul National University Bundang Hospital from January 01, 2005, to July 31, 2019, and was conducted using a common data model (CDM). Patients who initially presented with fever or respiratory symptoms—cough, sputum production, or dyspnea—which eventually resulted in a pneumonia diagnosis code within 7 days of admission were selected. Pneumonia diagnoses were identified using ICD-10 codes commonly associated with CAP, including J12–J18. The clinical data warehouse system also collected the demographic information; underlying diseases; vital signs (recorded within 48 hours of admission, using the maximum or minimum values, depending on the variable); laboratory and microbiology test findings (obtained within ± 7 days of admission, with values closest to admission dates prioritized); radiologic findings from the time points closest to the admission both before and after hospitalization; admission date and duration; and clinical outcome. To confirm cases of CAP, the electronic medical records were reviewed retrospectively to collect the patients’ epidemiological data and clinical data including symptoms and physical examination findings.

For this study, CAP was defined by both clinical and radiological criteria. To establish a diagnosis of CAP based on radiographic findings, there had to be new or worsening infiltrates, consolidation, or pleural effusion indicative of pneumonia within the first 48 hours of admission. Furthermore, to establish a diagnosis of CAP based on clinical findings, patients with a fever—or a history of fever—needed to exhibit at least two of the following symptoms: a new or exacerbated cough, increased sputum production, abnormal breath sounds upon auscultation, and dyspnea. Patients who were discharged within the 14 days prior to admission due to an acute illness, as well as patients who developed pneumonia more than 72 hours after admission, were excluded. Additionally, 91 patients who were initially diagnosed with CAP were later confirmed to have pulmonary tuberculosis and were therefore excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1).

Chronic lung disease was defined as bronchial asthma, chronic obstructive lung disease, or interstitial lung disease, among other chronic lung diseases. A patient with immunocompromised status was defined as having received immunosuppressants such as steroids, azathioprine, and tacrolimus within 6 months (Supplementary Table 1). Severe pneumonia was defined as that requiring admission to the intensive care unit (ICU).

Microbiological evaluation

Sputum Gram stain and culture tests were performed for 93.8% (3,832) of the patients, and blood culture tests were performed for 92.8% (3,792). Pleural effusion culture test results were collected if available. In some cases, a bronchoalveolar lavage fluid test using bronchoscopy was performed for a culture test. Pathogens identified by blood culture or other sterile site culture tests, including pleural effusion, were defined as confirmed causative agents of pneumonia when no other cause of infection existed. According to the US National Healthcare Safety Network recommendation,12 Candida species, coagulase-negative staphylococci, and Enterococcus species were excluded from consideration as causative microorganisms of pneumonia. When multiple pathogens were isolated from different or the same clinical specimens, causative agents were determined based on the specimen type, considering sterility, Gram stain results, semi-quantitative culture findings, and the patient’s clinical response to antibiotic therapy. Two physicians—specialists in respiratory and infectious diseases—independently assessed these cases. If disagreement occurred, consensus was reached through a joint review of sputum quality, microbiologic data, and treatment response. To avoid duplication, a single primary pathogen was assigned in the final analysis whenever possible. Serological tests for Mycoplasma pneumoniae were performed. Results were considered positive when either IgM antibodies were detected or when a fourfold increase in IgG titers was observed between paired samples. The results of polymerase chain reaction tests were collected for five respiratory viruses (influenza A and B viruses, respiratory syncytial virus, parainfluenza virus, and adenovirus) if available. In cases where viral pneumonia was deemed more likely based on clinical and radiographic findings, bacterial isolates from cultures were not classified as causative.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics are presented as mean (standard deviation [SD]) values for continuous variables and as numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to analyze nonparametric continuous variables, while the t-test was used for parametric continuous variables to assess differences between groups. For categorical variables, the chi-square (χ2) or Fisher’s exact test was used where appropriate. A logistic regression model was employed to calculate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for in-hospital mortality. Statistically significant (P < 0.05) variables from univariate analyses were included in the multivariate analyses. In this study, due to the small number of missing data, we excluded all cases with missing data from the main analysis and conducted a complete case analysis (Supplementary Table 2). A detailed subgroup analysis was performed to explore the association of CAP with in-hospital mortality across various subgroups. These subgroups were defined by sex, age (ages 65–79 and ≥ 80 years), presence of chronic pulmonary disease, ICU admission status, Gram staining results (positive or negative), and pathogen nature (bacterial or non-bacterial). Additionally, temporal trends across three distinct time periods were assessed to investigate changes in the diversity of etiologic microorganisms. Testing for heterogeneity was performed across subgroups to ensure the robustness of the findings. For all analyses, P values were two-tailed, and the significance threshold was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB No.: B-2107-698-003). Because of the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes

Regarding the 4,083 patients hospitalized for CAP, the average age was 70.1 years, and 66.7% were male. The prevalence of chronic lung diseases was notably high at 42.6%, as was the presence of neoplastic disease and immunocompromised status (24.4% and 26.5%, respectively; Table 1). Severe cases requiring ICU admission were reported in 11.5% of patients, with an in-hospital mortality rate of 7.3%.

Stratification by age revealed a higher proportion of female patients among individuals aged 65 years and older (n = 2,962). This older group also had a higher frequency of chronic conditions, with notable increases in cerebrovascular and neuromuscular diseases and chronic lung disease. These patients also had higher rates of ICU admission and mortality than those younger than 65 years (12.2% vs. 9.5%; 8.6% vs. 3.7%, respectively).

Etiologic pathogens of pneumonia

The most frequently identified pathogens in sputum samples from patients hospitalized with CAP were Klebsiella pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Streptococcus pneumoniae (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 1). In blood cultures, K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae were most frequently detected. M. pneumoniae was identified as the causative agent in 24 (0.59%) patients. Two isolates of Candida spp. were identified in the blood culture tests; however, these were considered indicative of concomitant candidemia rather than causative pathogens of pneumonia. Among the 1,439 patients tested for respiratory viral infections, 173 were confirmed positive. Specifically, 95 cases were positive for influenza A, 41 for influenza B, 19 for RSV, 11 for parainfluenza, and 7 for adenovirus.

Age-stratified analysis revealed similar pathogen diversities between adults aged 60 to < 80 years and those aged ≥ 80 years. The diversity of pathogens was also comparable in cases of severe CAP requiring ICU admission (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). However, in patients with chronic lung disease, P. aeruginosa was the most common pathogen identified in sputum cultures (Supplementary Fig. 2). The prevalent bacterial species remained constant over the periods “2005–2009,” “2010–2014,” and “2015–2019”; however, K. pneumoniae surpassed S. pneumoniae as the most common pathogen in 2015 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Clinical presentations according to the pathogen

A comparison of bacterial and viral pneumonia revealed that leukopenia was more common in viral infections, whereas leukocytosis and high procalcitonin levels were associated with bacterial infections. Notably, patients with chronic lung disease had a higher incidence of gram-negative bacterial pneumonia, although the mortality rate between cases of gram-positive and gram-negative bacterial pneumonia did not significantly differ (Supplementary Table 5).

Risk factors for in-hospital mortality

Multivariate analysis showed that the following were significant factors influencing in-hospital mortality: pulse rate, ≥ 125 beats/min; body temperature, < 35°C or > 39.9°C; respiratory rate, ≥ 30 breaths/min; systolic blood pressure of < 90 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure of ≤ 60 mmHg; male sex; immunocompromised status; presence of dyspnea; and C-reactive protein (CRP) elevation. A hemoglobin level of < 10 g/dL was also a strong predictor of mortality (Table 3). Conversely, age over 65 years, elevated blood urea nitrogen levels, high glucose levels, cerebrovascular disease, neoplastic disease, and chronic lung disease did not significantly affect the risk of in-hospital mortality.

DISCUSSION

In this 14-year study conducted at a tertiary care hospital, we examined 4,083 patients hospitalized with CAP whose average age was 70.1 years. This study highlighted a significant prevalence of chronic lung diseases, cancer, and immunosuppression among the patients hospitalized with CAP. Severe pneumonia was present in 10.5% of cases, and advanced age, particularly over 65 years, was associated with higher incidence rates of chronic conditions, severe pneumonia, and mortality. The most frequently isolated pathogens were K. pneumoniae, S. aureus, P. aeruginosa, and S. pneumoniae, identified across all ages and periods. The independent risk factors for in-hospital mortality included increased pulse and respiratory rates, extreme body temperatures, blood pressure deviations, low hemoglobin levels, immunocompromised status, dyspnea, and CRP elevation.

Studies from around the world have also reported the increasing incidence of CAP in aging populations with comorbid chronic diseases. Physiological changes due to aging, such as weakened respiratory muscles and decreased lung elasticity, contribute to increased susceptibility to pneumonia.13 In addition, many older individuals have accompanying underlying diseases, including chronic lung disease, neoplastic disease, and cerebrovascular disease, and often take drugs such as immunosuppressants,14,15 leading to an increase in pneumonia incidence.16 In this study, the patient group aged 65 years or older accounted for 73% of the total study population (2,962 patients). This group also had a high prevalence of factors such as chronic lung disease, neoplastic disease, and the use of immunosuppressive drugs.

Patients with pneumonia presenting to tertiary hospitals may exhibit unique characteristics compared with those treated in the community, as they are often older and have chronic conditions. In this study, K. pneumoniae was the most common causative organism found in sputum samples, followed by P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and S. pneumoniae. The diversity of these pathogens remained relatively stable throughout the 14-year study period. This consistency may reflect the use of standardized diagnostic protocols and uniform microbiological evaluation throughout the study, strengthening the observed trends’ reliability. While K. pneumoniae and S. pneumoniae are well-known primary pathogens of CAP, S. aureus and P. aeruginosa, typically associated with HAP, were also identified as major pathogens in our study. Notably, the relatively high prevalence of P. aeruginosa in our cohort may be attributed to the high proportion of patients with chronic lung diseases (42.6%), known to predispose individuals to gram-negative infections. This further reflects the nature of a tertiary care setting, wherein patients often present with more severe illness and complex comorbidities. According to a prospective multicenter study on CAP in Korea,17 S. pneumoniae was the most common pathogen, followed by influenza viruses, M. pneumoniae, and K. pneumoniae. However, that study excluded patients with neoplastic diseases and those taking immunosuppressants, leading to differences in the study populations.

K. pneumoniae surpassed S. pneumoniae as a common causative pathogen of CAP in this study. This may be attributed to our study being conducted on hospitalized patients in a tertiary medical institution where many severely ill patients are treated, with differences in patients’ underlying conditions and severity. Our cohort included a large proportion of patients with severe CAP, mostly older adults, who had malignancies leading to immunocompromised status or who received frequent outpatient medical services without recent hospitalization. Although immunocompromised patients in this study were classified as having CAP in accordance with current guidelines, their clinical complexity warrants individualized consideration, particularly when interpreting microbiological findings and selecting empirical antibiotics in tertiary care settings (Supplementary Table 6). Additionally, in older patients with CAP, there is an increased prevalence of S. aureus and gram-negative bacteria (Supplementary Table 3).18

In an aging society, the number of older patients with one or more comorbidities increases rapidly. Hence, it is thought that CAP needs a strategy for initial treatment that considers a different clinical picture and causative bacteria than before, especially in the case of hospitalized patients.18,19 Additionally, in tertiary hospitals with multiple older patients with severe pneumonia, those with CAP will likely have patient groups similar to those of the subjects of this study. Therefore, it would be helpful if the diagnosis and treatment of patients with CAP were considered based on this study’s findings.

Our findings also suggest that performing sputum and blood cultures in patients hospitalized with CAP is critical for guiding treatment. Understanding local pathogen diversities can improve empirical antibiotic selection.20 Identifying whether infections are caused by gram-positive or gram-negative bacteria helps optimize early treatment.21 Particularly, gram-negative bacteria, such as K. pneumoniae, were more prevalent in patients with chronic lung diseases, while gram-positive pathogens were associated with more severe clinical presentations.1 In this study, sputum culture tests were performed in 93.8% and blood cultures were performed in 92.8% of patients hospitalized with CAP. Causative microorganisms were identified in 23% of cases using sputum culture tests and in 3.4% of cases using blood culture tests. Although there are relatively few cases identified through definitive culture tests such as blood culture tests, appropriate antibiotic selection based on these tests is important. In addition to typical gram-positive and gram-negative pathogens, Mycobacterium tuberculosis should also be considered as an important differential diagnosis for pneumonia in Korea, especially in hospitalized patients, despite its declining incidence in recent years.22,23

This study has some limitations. First, it exclusively included inpatients. Thus, our cohort may differ from those on patients with CAP. However, in the context of a rapidly aging society, the clinical presentation of CAP is evolving. Our study reflects the unique challenges faced by tertiary hospitals, often encountering more complex cases, including patients with multiple comorbidities and chronic conditions. Second, due to the retrospective nature of this study and its reliance on data extracted from the CDM, limitations in microbiological and etiological assessments existed. Multiplex PCR testing for atypical pathogens including M. pneumoniae, Chlamydia pneumoniae, and Legionella pneumophila was not routinely performed throughout the study period, as such methods only became widely available in Korea in recent years. As a result, the data were not sufficiently comprehensive to allow for a full etiologic assessment of atypical pneumonia pathogens. Moreover, although we identified sputum pathogens in a significant number of cases, we could not determine whether these organisms were definitive causative agents. Furthermore, data on antimicrobial susceptibility profiles were not available in the CDM dataset, limiting our ability to assess resistance patterns. These limitations reduce the precision and clinical applicability of microbiological analysis. Third, the generalizability of our findings is limited by the fact that the study was conducted at a single tertiary care institution in Korea, despite the large sample size and 15-year data collection span using the CDM. Finally, because this study relied on diagnostic codes recorded in the CDM, there may have been limitations in accurately identifying all patients with CAP. Patients with clinical features of pneumonia may have been excluded if appropriate ICD codes were not assigned, potentially introducing selection bias and underestimating the true disease burden. Additionally, the definition of immunocompromised status was also based on CDM data, which may have led to overestimation. While most patients receiving oral corticosteroids met the defined criteria for dose and duration, some patients who had received short-term intravenous corticosteroids were also included. This could have resulted in an inflated proportion of immunocompromised individuals. Nevertheless, considering the tertiary care setting of this study, the high prevalence of immunocompromised patients is consistent with the clinical characteristics of the hospital population.

In this study, patients hospitalized with CAP in a tertiary hospital setting were predominantly older and had significant underlying health conditions, including chronic lung diseases, malignancies, and immunocompromised status. Gram-negative bacteria and S. pneumoniae, which are traditionally associated with CAP, were commonly found in our patient population, likely related to patient age and underlying conditions such as chronic lung diseases. Male sex and immunocompromised status were significant risk factors for in-hospital mortality along with worsening clinical indices. Clinicians are advised to perform careful risk stratification and employ tailored antibiotic strategies to improve outcomes in the treatment of CAP in the previously mentioned vulnerable populations.
