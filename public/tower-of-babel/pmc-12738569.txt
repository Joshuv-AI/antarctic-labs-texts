A robust network for tiny and arbitrary-oriented ship detection in remote sensing images
Authors: Ling Xu, Bin Qiu, Huijun Xu, Shaoyu Ouyang, Xiaoli Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ships in optical remote sensing imagery are often affected by significant background noise, and they typically appear as small targets with large aspect ratios, dense distributions, and arbitrary orientations, which pose substantial challenges for accurate detection. To address these issues, this paper proposes a novel detection network, termed TCA-Net, specifically designed for ship detection in complex remote sensing scenarios. TCA-Net comprises four main modules: a Deformable Attention Pyramid Network (DAPNet), a Multi-Scale Feature Enhancement Network (MFENet), a Multi-Scale Adaptive Pooling Network (MAPNet), and a Rotation Head. First, DAPNet integrates a custom-designed Ship Deformable Convolution unit and Ship Attention modules to effectively capture features of ships with arbitrary shapes and orientations while suppressing multi-scale background noise. Second, MFENet employs parallel Laplacian and dilated convolutions to enhance features of ships across various scales, particularly improving the detection of small targets. Third, MAPNet adaptively generates precise ROI for ships with large aspect ratios and learns multi-scale contextual information to infer relationships in crowded scenes. Finally, the Rotation Head regresses rotated bounding boxes to precisely localize ships at any orientation. Experiments conducted on the public DOTA and HRSC2016 datasets demonstrate that TCA-Net achieves SOTA performance.

Introduction

Automatic ship detection in optical remote sensing imagery plays a critical role in civilian and security-related applications, including maritime traffic monitoring, disaster response, and port management1,2. With the advancement of satellite and airborne imaging technologies, large volumes of high-resolution images can now be acquired efficiently and cost-effectively, making optical imagery the primary data source for ship detection3. Consequently, robust and accurate ship detection has become an important research topic in the remote sensing community4,5.

Early approaches to ship detection relied on traditional machine learning techniques, such as polar coordinate transformations combined with SVMs6 or polarimetric feature analysis7. Although effective in constrained scenarios, these methods were highly sensitive to environmental factors such as illumination changes and atmospheric disturbances. More critically, they struggled to handle the inherently small, arbitrarily oriented, and densely distributed nature of ships in complex maritime environments, leading to poor generalization performance.

With the rise of deep learning, convolutional neural networks have brought remarkable progress by enabling stronger feature extraction capabilities. For example, HSF-Net8 addressed multi-scale detection through unified scale mapping, but its reliance on horizontal bounding boxes (HBBs) introduced redundant information. R2PN9 incorporated rotated proposals and rotation-sensitive pooling to accommodate arbitrary orientations, yet it remained vulnerable to background noise and underperformed in small or crowded ship scenarios. DAPN10 attempted to suppress noise through attention-enhanced pyramid networks but still relied on HBBs, while Rotational Libra R-CNN11 improved crowded-scene detection via balanced sampling strategies but continued to miss small targets. More recent ship-specific methods such as SKNet12, SASOD13, and LRS2-DM14 advanced detection accuracy under challenging conditions, yet they still encounter difficulties when dealing with small ships, vessels with large aspect ratios, or scenes characterized by strong background noise.

Taken together, the challenges of ship detection in remote sensing images can be attributed to several factors. Ships frequently appear at multiple scales, often as small targets with elongated aspect ratios, which easily leads to false alarms and missed detections. They also tend to be densely distributed, while contextual relationships among targets are rarely exploited by existing methods. Furthermore, background noise from waves, clouds, and cluttered port environments exacerbates detection errors, and the arbitrary orientations and irregular shapes of ships pose additional difficulties for conventional detectors.

To overcome these limitations, we propose TCA-Net, a novel detection framework tailored for tiny, crowded, and arbitrarily oriented ships in optical remote sensing imagery. The network integrates four key components to address the challenges above. First, a Deformable Attention Pyramid Network (DAPNet) is designed by introducing a Ship Deformable Convolution (SDC) unit and Ship Attention (SA) modules, enabling adaptive feature alignment with ship structures and effective suppression of multi-scale background noise. Second, a Multi-Scale Feature Enhancement Network (MFENet) is developed by combining Laplacian and dilated convolutions, which sharpen weak features and significantly improve the detection of small ships. Third, a Multi-Scale Adaptive Pooling Network (MAPNet) is constructed to generate precise region proposals for ships with large aspect ratios and to capture contextual relationships in crowded scenes. Finally, a Rotation Head is employed to regress rotated bounding boxes, achieving accurate orientation-aware localization. Through the integration of these modules, TCA-Net provides a comprehensive solution to the fundamental challenges of ship detection in complex remote sensing scenarios.

Related work

Object detector

Currently, most well-known object detectors adopt horizontal bounding box (HBB) to localize targets in natural images. HBB-based detectors generally assume that the target is aligned horizontally and can be categorized into two main groups: one-stage and two-stage methods. One-stage detectors divide the input image into grids and directly predict class labels and bounding box offsets for each grid cell, simultaneously performing feature extraction, classification, and regression in a single step. Representative methods include the YOLO series15–17, SSD18, RetinaNet19, and CornerNet20. These methods offer fast detection speeds but often at the cost of reduced accuracy. Two-stage detectors, also known as region proposal-based methods, first generate candidate regions using region proposal strategies and then perform feature extraction, classification, and bounding box regression on these proposals. Well-known examples include Fast R-CNN21, Faster R-CNN22, R-FCN23, Mask R-CNN24, Cascade R-CNN25, and Dynamic R-CNN26. Although HBB-based detectors have been extensively developed and have achieved strong performance in general object detection, they exhibit significant limitations when applied to ship detection in remote sensing imagery. HBBs fail to provide accurate orientation and aspect ratio information for ships, especially those with large aspect ratios or arranged densely in ports. Moreover, redundant background information included in HBBs often introduces noise that further degrades detection accuracy. To overcome the inherent limitations of HBB-based detectors, a variety of methods employing rotated bounding box (RBB) have emerged, particularly in scene text detection and remote sensing applications. In scene text detection, characters are frequently inclined or arbitrarily oriented, prompting the adoption of RBBs. Early approaches such as TextBoxes++28, RRD28, RRPN++29, and TextOCR30 introduced rotation-sensitive mechanisms to capture these characteristics. However, scene text detectors cannot be directly applied to remote sensing due to the increased complexity of aerial scenes. Consequently, several generic RBB-based detectors for remote sensing imagery have been proposed, including ROI-Transformer31, CAD-Net32, SCRDet33, CFC-Net34, ReDet35, and FSoD-Net36. Nevertheless, ships in remote sensing images often exhibit small sizes, arbitrary orientations, dense arrangements, and large aspect ratios, making general-purpose detectors insufficient for precise ship detection. As a result, specialized ship detection methods have been developed. For example, Yu et al.37, SKNet12, SASOD13, and LRS2-DM14 specifically address the challenges of arbitrary-oriented, small, and densely distributed ships. While these methods have improved detection performance to some extent, they still struggle with small objects under complex conditions.

Small object detection in remote sensing images

Due to the inherent characteristics of small objects and the complexity of external environments in remote sensing imagery, small object detection remains a challenging task38. Lin et al.39 proposed the Feature Pyramid Network (FPN), which effectively enhances the detection performance of small objects in remote sensing images through multi-scale feature representation. Cao et al.40 developed a multi-branch and high-level semantic network (MHN) that alleviates semantic inconsistency across different feature layers, thereby improving detection accuracy. Subsequently, Wang et al.41 introduced a pyramid pooling strategy to strengthen feature fusion between adjacent layers, while Ghiasi et al.42 utilized AutoML techniques to explore the optimal combination of multi-level feature fusion. Qiao et al.43 proposed the novel DetectoRS framework, which integrates a recursive feature pyramid at the macro level and switchable atrous convolution at the micro level, achieving remarkable improvements in small object detection performance. Moreover, Wu et al.44 introduced the Feature- and-Spatial Aligned Network (FSANet), which incorporates a feature-aware alignment module to enhance multi-scale feature extraction and a spatial-aware guidance head for iterative refinement. Wang et al.45 further proposed PG-DRFNet, which designs a dynamic receptive field module combined with positional guidance to improve the representation and localization of small objects. Method

An overview of the proposed TCA-Net is shown in Fig. 1. The network consists of four main sub-networks: DAPNet, MFENet, MAPNet, and the Rotation Head. Given an input image, DAPNet first extracts multi-scale features while suppressing background noise through the Ship Deformable Convolution (SDC) unit and Ship Attention (SA) modules. The resulting feature maps are then enhanced by MFENet to sharpen features across different scales, with a particular focus on small targets. Subsequently, MAPNet uses an Oriented RPN to generate multi-scale rotated region proposals and applies Multi-Scale Adaptive Pooling (MA pooling) to obtain precise ROIs. Finally, the Rotation Head performs classification and five-parameter regression to generate rotated bounding boxes (RBBs) for arbitrary-oriented ship detection. DAPNet

Ships in remote sensing imagery exhibit arbitrary shapes, scales, and orientations and are often obscured by substantial background noise. To effectively extract discriminative features while suppressing irrelevant information, we design DAPNet.

Illustrated in Fig. 1, DAPNet adopts a bottom-up and top-down structure. In the bottom-up process, the SDC unit is used to fully extract features from the input image, forming a hierarchical feature pyramid with increasing channel dimensions and decreasing resolutions {C2, C3, C4, C5}. In the top-down pathway, these features are upsampled to build the final feature pyramid {P2, P3, P4, P5}. During this process, SA modules are incorporated to suppress background noise across different feature levels. Considering that ships in remote sensing imagery can vary significantly in size, high-resolution features {P2, P3} are enhanced by fusing semantic information from lower-resolution features {P4, P5} to improve small ship detection. For larger ships, an additional P6 feature map is generated by downsampling P5 with a stride of 2, which provides more robust representations for detecting large targets. The following subsections detail the SDC unit and SA module.

After obtaining the adaptive sampling features through Eqs. (3)–(5), the predicted offset field and the corresponding standard convolution outputs are further fused within the unit. Specifically, the offset features learned by the 3 Ã—3 standard convolution are first aligned in the channel dimension and then combined with the deformable feature map through an element-wise addition operation. This fusion allows the offset information to serve as an adaptive spatial prior, guiding the deformable convolution to focus on ship regions while suppressing background responses. The motivation behind this design is that ship targets in remote sensing images often exhibit large aspect ratios and arbitrary orientations, which may cause unstable sampling or local feature misalignment when using deformable convolution alone. By integrating the learned offsets with the deformable features, it improve detection robustness for small and densely arranged ships.

Figure 2c illustrates the detailed design of the proposed SDC unit, which includes two key considerations: (1) Conventional convolutional units typically employ batch normalization (BN)47. However, in high-resolution remote sensing scenarios, training often requires small batch sizes due to memory constraints. If the batch size is too large, the system may run out of memory; if it is too small, BN’s estimated mean and variance can deviate significantly from the true distribution, leading to poor convergence and degraded model performance. To address this, Group Normalization (GN)48 is adopted instead. Unlike BN, GN normalizes along the channel dimension within each group and is independent of the batch size, which makes it more stable and effective for high-resolution remote sensing training. (2) While most convolutional units use nonlinear activation functions such as ReLU to introduce nonlinearity, these functions can destroy useful information when the input features lie in a low-dimensional subspace. This loss of information negatively affects detection performance. Therefore, the proposed SDC unit employs a linear activation function to preserve more complete feature representations.

MAPNet

MAPNet aims to generate more precise ROI regions and, to some extent, learn multi-scale contextual relationships among ships, thereby reducing the miss detection and false alarm rates of TCA-Net in densely arranged ship scenarios. The structure of MAPNet is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Due to the fact that ships in remote sensing imagery appear with arbitrary orientations, obtaining accurate detection results requires precise region proposals before feature pooling operations. Therefore, we adopt an Oriented RPN49, which generates multi-scale and arbitrarily oriented proposals. Taking the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_2$$\end{document} feature map as an example, the ROI generation process is shown in Fig.1.

For the DOTA and HRSC2016 datasets used in our experiments, we apply the k-means clustering method to determine the boundary box dimensions and design three sets of horizontal anchors with aspect ratios of (1:2, 2:1, and 1:1) to fully cover every position in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_{2}$$\end{document} feature map.Fig. 3(a) Traditional ROI pooling; (b) MA pooling.

Using the Oriented RPN, multi-scale rotated region proposals are generated. However, focusing only on the proposals themselves, such as the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$E_2$$\end{document} feature region, often makes it difficult even for humans to distinguish whether ship targets exist within them. Therefore, a further pooling operation is needed to produce fixed-size \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K \times K$$\end{document} ROI regions. Traditional ROI pooling22 samples points uniformly along the width and height of the region proposal but neglects the elongated aspect ratios typical of ships in remote sensing images, resulting in sparse sampling along certain directions and redundant background noise, as shown in Fig. 3a. To address this, we design a novel MA pooling method based on bilinear interpolation50, whose sampling pattern is shown in Fig. 3b. MA pooling adaptively samples more uniformly along both width and height directions, yielding more precise ROIs for ship targets. From Fig. 4a, it can be observed that MA pooling gathers uniformly distributed sampling points from multi-scale feature maps and aggregates them into multiple closed-loop rectangular grids, forming compact feature representations. Deep feature maps have a larger receptive field, which helps enhance the model’s classification capability, while shallow feature maps provide finer spatial details, improving the model’s localization ability. Rotation head

The Rotation Head is responsible for identifying ships in remote sensing imagery and generating RBBs to precisely localize them. Building upon the conventional HBB representation, we introduce an additional rotation angle to describe arbitrary-oriented bounding boxes, denoted as RBBs, as shown in Fig. 5. An arbitrary RBB is defined by five parameters \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x, y, w, h, \beta$$\end{document}, where (x, y) represents the center coordinates of the RBB, w and h denote its width and height, respectively, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\beta$$\end{document} is the rotation angle of the width axis relative to the X-axis, constrained within \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$[-90^\circ , 0^\circ )$$\end{document}. The regression process for RBBs is formulated as follows:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} t_{x} = \frac{x - x_{a}}{w_{a}},\ t_{y} = \frac{y - y_{a}}{h_{a}},\ t_{w} = \log \left( \frac{w}{w_{a}}\right) ,\ t_{h} = \log \left( \frac{h}{h_{a}}\right) ,\ t_{\beta } = \beta - \beta _{a}, \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \begin{aligned} t^{'}_{x} = \frac{x^{'} - x_{a}}{w_{a}},\ t^{'}_{y} = \frac{y^{'} - y_{a}}{h_{a}},\ t^{'}_{w} = \log \left( \frac{w^{'}}{w_{a}}\right) ,\ t^{'}_{h} = \log \left( \frac{h^{'}}{h_{a}}\right) ,\ t^{'}_{\beta } = \beta ^{'} - \beta _{a}, \end{aligned} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x, y, w, h, \beta$$\end{document} denote the center coordinates, width, height, and rotation angle of the RBB, respectively; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x, x_a, x^{'}$$\end{document} represent the variables for the ground-truth boxes, anchors, and predicted boxes. The conventional IoU often fails to accurately calculate overlaps for rotated objects. To address this, we adopt the skew IoU strategy, which considers the rotation angle when computing overlaps, thereby improving positive and negative sample assignment accuracy. Finally, to avoid redundant predictions, we use the soft-rotate-NMS technique51. Unlike traditional Soft-NMS, which suppresses overlapping boxes based on their intersection area, soft-rotate-NMS further refines the selection by considering the orientation of adjacent rotated proposals, leading to more reasonable detection results for arbitrarily oriented ships.

In summary, the proposed Rotation Head follows the standard regression formulation of oriented object detectors but integrates these two improvements—skew IoU-based matching and soft-rotate-NMS post-processing—to enhance localization stability and reduce duplicate detections for ships with arbitrary orientations and dense distributions.

Experiment

Datasets and preprocessing

DOTA: The DOTA dataset52 is one of the largest public benchmarks for remote sensing object detection. Its images are sourced from Google Earth, GF-2, and JL-1 satellites, covering 15 object categories (e.g., ships, ports, helicopters). Image resolutions range from approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$800\times 800$$\end{document} to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4000 \times 4000$$\end{document} pixels. Since the number of ship instances in DOTA is relatively limited, we employ data augmentation techniques to expand the training data. In total, we obtain 13,624 images (approximately 165K ship instances) and randomly split them into 1/2 for training, 1/3 for testing, and 1/6 for validation.

HRSC2016: The HRSC2016 dataset53 is a well-known and widely used benchmark for ship detection in remote sensing imagery due to its diverse shooting angles and complex backgrounds. In this work, HRSC2016 is used as an additional benchmark to evaluate the generalization and robustness of TCA-Net. It contains 1,070 images with an average resolution of about \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1000 \times 1000$$\end{document} pixels. Similarly, we apply data augmentation to further increase the number of training samples, and split the dataset into training, testing, and validation sets with a ratio of 3:2:1. Finally, to fairly compare the performance of HBB and RBB detectors, we annotate the training samples using both LabelImg and RoLabelImg, with all ship objects labeled as ship.

Experimental setup and evaluation metrics

Implementation details

The backbone network for TCA-Net is ResNet101 integrated with the proposed SDC unit and SA module, denoted as SS-ResNet101. The deep learning framework used is PyTorch 1.10 with Python 3.8. All experiments are conducted on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU, using the CUDA 12.0 for acceleration. During training, the Momentum Optimizer is employed with a batch size of 32, a weight decay of 0.0001, and a momentum factor of 0.9. The total number of training iterations for DOTA and HRSC2016 is 160K and 50K, respectively, with an initial learning rate of 0.003, which is decreased progressively to allow the model to sufficiently learn target features.

In order to evaluate the computational efficiency and effectiveness of the proposed TCA-Net, we compare its performance with other state-of-the-art methods on the DOTA dataset in terms of AP, parameter count (Para), FLOPs, and FPS. Discussion on TCA-Net subnetworks

This paper designs multiple subnetworks and integrates them into TCA-Net. To verify the effectiveness of each subnetwork, we conduct a model discussion using Faster R-CNN as the baseline, with the DOTA dataset and AP as the evaluation metric.

From Table 4, it can be seen that the baseline achieves an AP of only 69.17%, which does not meet the expected detection performance. DAPNet adopts an FPN-based backbone and integrates the SDC unit and SA module. The SDC unit fully extracts ship features with arbitrary shapes, sizes, and orientations, while the SA module effectively suppresses multi-scale background noise. As a result, DAPNet improves AP by 3.19%. MFENet uses parallel Laplacian and dilated convolutions to sharpen and enhance weak features of small ships, expanding their receptive field and significantly improving detection performance for small targets, resulting in an additional AP gain of 2.45%. MAPNet generates multi-scale rotated ROIs through the Oriented RPN and MA pooling, producing more precise regions for subsequent classification and regression, which increases AP by 2.33%. Finally, the rotation head uses an advanced regression strategy to predict more accurate RBBs, producing well-aligned oriented bounding boxes, which further increases AP by 1.35%. The discussion shows that each subnetwork consistently improves detection performance, which aligns with our initial design concept and confirms that TCA-Net is effective and applicable to ship Conclusion

This paper proposes a novel remote sensing ship detection model named TCA-Net. The model is composed of four subnetworks: DAPNet, MFENet, MAPNet, and the rotation head. First, the SDC unit and SA module are integrated into the FPN to construct DAPNet, which effectively addresses the limitation that conventional detection models cannot fully extract ship features. The SDC unit focuses on solving the problem of extracting features of ships with arbitrary shapes and orientations, while the SA module suppresses multi-scale background noise and reduces the interference of irrelevant features. Second, we design MFENet by combining parallel Laplacian and dilated convolutions, which sharpen and enhance the weak features of small ships, thereby improving their detectability. Third, MAPNet generates multi-scale rotated region proposals through the Oriented RPN and refines them via MA pooling to obtain precise ROIs. Finally, the rotation head uses an advanced regression strategy to generate accurate RBBs for arbitrarily oriented ships in remote sensing imagery. Extensive experiments on large-scale datasets such as DOTA and HRSC2016 demonstrate that TCA-Net achieves superior detection accuracy compared with SOTA methods. However, due to its relatively large computational cost, TCA-Net is currently less suitable for deployment on low-power embedded devices such as UAVs or mobile platforms. In future work, we will focus on lightweighting the network structure of TCA-Net and combining it with image enhancement techniques to enable real-time wide-area ship surveillance on embedded platforms.
