Eco-friendly shape-stabilized phase change materials: Improving thermal performance, reducing pollution, and enabling sustainable energy solutions
Authors: Anas Islam, A.K. Pandey, R. Saidur, Aman Yadav, Wenye Lin, Xiaobin Gu, V.V. Tyagi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryPhase change materials store and release large amounts of latent heat, yet leakage on melting and low thermal conductivity limit their use in thermal energy storage. Confining them in porous supports prevents leakage, but many conventional supports carry high cost and environmental burden. This review synthesizes how biomass-derived porous supports offer a sustainable alternative. It compares three carbonization routes, direct pyrolysis, hydrothermal carbonization, and activation-enabled carbonization, across pore development, PCM loading, leakage resistance, cost, and environmental impact, and examines hybridization with graphene, expanded graphite, metals, and ceramics to raise thermal conductivity. The evidence shows that filler topology and interfacial design govern performance more than filler identity. These insights map a practical, lower-impact route toward scalable bio-based thermal storage for buildings, textiles, desalination, and solar applications.

Introduction

Globally, pollution levels are rising at an alarming rate, directly correlated with extensive industrialization and urbanization. Indeed, researchers have expressed concerns about the rise in pollution levels, as they have immediate effects on the quality of life and human health.1 In addition, pollution affects the urban infrastructure and buildings, as well as the natural habitats of living organisms. As a result, pollution control is of the utmost urgency, along with the emergence of pollution prevention strategies. The former approach relies on reducing pollution by limiting the impact of pollutants after their release, whereas the latter involves changes in the processes that generate pollution, thereby reducing pollution at its source. At this point, renewable energy can be viewed as the ultimate solution, offering a sustainable approach to reducing reliance on fossil fuels and thereby decreasing pollution.2

Further, renewable resources are capable of reducing pollution and protecting ecosystems while producing an improved environment for humans. Solar infrastructure is critical for human energy needs since sunlight is abundantly available.3 Unlike wind or flowing water, which are constrained by geography, solar energy can be harnessed wherever humans live on the globe. Furthermore, solar energy becomes an effective and affordable solution as a technology advances. Solar energy reaches earth as solar radiation; its use involves absorbing and converting it into electricity or heat via photovoltaics. However, due to its intermittent nature, storing solar energy is essential. The stored energy can be used later when sunlight is weak or absent. A better thermal energy storage (TES) material should retain heat well, be safe to use, and should be able to withstand sufficiet number of cycles. Phase change materials (PCMs) are very important in solar energy technology because they store and release large amounts of thermal energy.4 PCMs therefore provide a consistent, uninterrupted power supply even in the absence of sunlight. Their versatility and efficiency make PCMs increasingly important for advancing solar energy technologies and a sustainable energy future.

Nevertheless, several challenges associated with PCMs must be addressed to improve their use in TES applications. A major issue is low thermal conductivity associated with PCMs.5 In addition, leakage and long-term thermal stability are considered major problems when PCMs change from solid to liquid, which can reduce their performance over time and limit their practical TES applications.6 Moreover, the compatibility of PCM materials with containers, along with potential environmental and health risks, represents a secondary but important concern. Overcoming PCM challenges through technological innovation and creative design is fundamental to maximizing the effectiveness of PCMs. Addressing these challenges therefore requires a comprehensive, interdisciplinary endeavor is assumed to address the challenges of PCMs. Cost reductions facilitated by improvements in production techniques and economies of scale, alongside the development of alternative PCM materials, are needed to enable commercial availability. On the other hand, dispersion of nanoparticles into PCMs is helpful in reducing the low thermal conductivity problem, and encapsulation is considered a suitable method to mitigate leakage issues in PCMs over long-term operating periods.7 Moreover, material compatibility problem is addressed by introducing a container material or coating the inside of containers to avoid chemical reactions. Non-toxic PCM formulations are prioritized, and clear handling guidelines are essential to encourage the adoption of safe, environmentally friendly practices.8 In summary, improved heat transfer is essential for developing more compact PCM-based TES systems. This can be achieved through improved system design and the use of simulation tools.9

Bio-based supporting materials represent a promising solution which addresses some of the problems associated with PCMs during the use in TES applications. This approach uses renewable and sustainable waste resources to address the low thermal conductivity problem of PCMs, thereby reducing the need for expensive commercialized nanoparticles and lowering overall costs.10 Bio-based supporting materials help to address low thermal conductivity by improving heat transfer in PCMs. Bio-based supporting materials are also flexible and resilient, which enables them to withstand the volumetric expansion that occurs during phase changes of the PCMs. This prevents mechanical stress and leakage, thereby addressing the leakage issue.11 Bio-based supporting materials exhibit better compatibility with PCM formulations, thereby minimizing potential chemical reactions and container incompatibility. Additionally, bio-based supporting materials ensure the thermal stability of PCMs used for extended periods by providing intrinsic encapsulation, anti-leakage protection, and protection against degradation and phase separation. Bio-based supporting materials are used to develop shape-stabilized PCMs (ss-PCMs), in which the bio-based matrix holds the PCM during phase changes, thereby preventing leakage and improving the overall thermal performance. Some prior research articles on ss-PCMs are systematically discussed below. Rathore et al.12 conducted a review focused on the use of macro-encapsulated PCMs as building materials for energy savings. They considered different strategies for incorporating macro-encapsulated PCMs into building envelopes and studied their effects on indoor thermal behavior and cooling load reduction. It also examined how the container materials affected the encapsulation. This included an encapsulation technique detail, different TES methods used in buildings, and the accessibility of suitable PCMs for encapsulation. In another review work, Zhang et al.13 elaborate on the thermal properties in natural materials and their importance. The examples highlight the methods by which organisms employ trace elements and molecular structures to control material properties which enables adaptive functionality that responds to a wide range of environmental stimuli. The review also discusses the manufacturing technologies and strategies currently used with bio-inspired structural materials which shows how lessons from nature can inform innovative, top-down-to-bottom, combined-material design principles in material science and engineering. As follows, Liu et al.,14 highlighted a vacuum impregnation technique for the development of bio ss-PCMs. The porous supporting material was chemically treated wood, whereas the PCMs were myristic acid, paraffin wax, and polyethylene glycol. The chemical treatment removes lignin and hemicellulose from the wood, reducing its density and creating space for PCM absorption. This procedure improved the thermal properties and durability of the PCM composites. Fabiani et al.15 studied bio-PCMs as promising substitutes for PCM materials, as these compounds are derived from plant or animal fats and shows an improved TES performance. The study focuses on the properties of animal fat-based bio phase change materials as low-cost media for thermal energy storage through differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), and thermal monitoring test results. It has a melting enthalpy of about 28.94 kJ/kg and a double transition temperature range. It emphasizes the importance of bio-PCMs as alternative low-cost media for thermal energy storage applications. However, the low latent heat makes it less effective for advanced applications. Similarly, Rashid et al.16 analyze an outstanding review work of BPCMs which including aspects of bio-PCM mechanisms and mixtures. This paper reviews the approaches to making different types of PCMs and outlines their applications in TES. Pursuant to their analysis, the authors propose numerous techniques to advance bio-PCM composites, thus better equipping them for use across various TES applications. This comprehensive review highlights the potential and versatility of BPCMs, along with plans to improve their performance and enlarge their use. Furthermore, Benhorma et al.17 review the growth of bio-PCMs by focusing on their physical and thermal properties and performance in numerous domestic and industrial applications. These applications include heating, air conditioning, cooling of photovoltaic panels and lithium-ion batteries, thermal management, water treatment, food conservation, and refrigeration of pharmaceutical products. The review also addresses the degradation of bio-PCMs caused by microbial activity, thermal and chemical instability, and repeated cycling, and other long-term consequences, emphasizing the need for further research. The authors highlight these limitations and other research gaps, pointing out areas for future investigation.

The aforementioned review articles emphasize that various research gaps have been identified in the development of bio-based supporting materials for leakage-proof PCMs. Based on the literature, the various synthesis routes of the bio-based supporting materials, including high-temperature pyrolysis, hydrothermal carbonization, and activated carbonization (AC), have not yet been systematically compared. Moreover, the comprehensive assessment of the relative performance and efficiency of the supporting materials also needs to be explored. Additionally, the challenges faced by the newly developed bio ss-PCMs in terms of scalability, cost-effectiveness, and thermal and chemical durability also need to be addressed. Another research gap that needs to be addressed is the potential of the hybridization route for the improvement of the heat transfer properties of the PCMs. The incorporation of metallic nanoparticles and carbon-based nanomaterials and their hybrid composites has been proven to enhance the thermal conductivity. So far, their optimization, compatibility with bio-based materials, stability, and environmental sustainability remain inadequately addressed. Furthermore, the lack of a systematic synthesis of prior studies to recognize and address uncertain challenges in the optimization, design, and application of hybridized bio ss-PCMs underscores the need for in-depth investigation to develop sustainable, efficient, and scalable TES solutions. According to Figure 2 and the above literature search, while numerous reviews exist on PCMs and their applications, a critical examination of the cited prior works reveals specific and addressable gaps that the present review is designed to fill. Existing reviews on macro-encapsulation strategies for building applications12 have focused exclusively on container design and thermal behavior without addressing the synthesis or characterization of bio-based support matrices. Reviews on bio-inspired structural materials18 have approached the topic from a biomimicry perspective without connecting material design to PCM encapsulation performance or thermal energy storage applications. Studies demonstrating vacuum impregnation of chemically treated wood19 have not systematically compared wood-based supports against other bio-derived matrices or evaluated the relative merits of different carbonization routes. Broad reviews of bio-PCM mechanisms and formulations20,21 have been conducted without a structured comparison of synthesis routes or hybridization strategies for thermal conductivity enhancement. Reviews covering bio-PCM applications across multiple sectors21 have not discussed carbonization-based support preparation or the enviro-economic implications of different synthesis routes.

Taken together, these prior reviews share three common gaps that the present work explicitly addresses: (1) the diversity of biomass-derived porous support architectures, (2) the carbonization synthesis routes used to prepare them, and (3) the hybridization strategies employed to overcome their intrinsic thermal conductivity limitations. Most existing reviews treat these three dimensions in isolation or address only one of them, leaving researchers without the integrated, comparative framework needed to make informed material design decisions. The present review directly addresses this gap and offers the following distinct contributions that are not available in the existing literature. First, the present review provides the first systematic side-by-side comparison of direct carbonization, hydrothermal carbonization, and activation-enabled carbonization specifically evaluated as PCM support matrices, using a consistent set of performance metrics that includes pore development, PCM loading fraction, latent heat retention, leakage resistance, thermal cycling stability, process energy requirement, and environmental burden. This comparative analysis, consolidated in Table 1 and Section 2.2.4, offers a practical decision-making framework for support material selection that is absent from the existing review literature. Second, the hybridization strategies covered in Section 2.3 are analyzed not merely as thermal conductivity enhancement techniques as is typical in prior reviews but through the lens of filler topology and scaffold-filler functional division. The synthesized insight that filler network connectivity and interfacial thermal resistance are more decisive than bulk filler conductivity, and that the most effective systems are those in which the conductive phase is structurally integrated into the bio-based scaffold rather than dispersed as isolated particles, represents an original synthesis that has not been articulated in this form elsewhere. Third, unlike most reviews that treat environmental sustainability as qualitative motivation, the present work integrates life cycle, cost-effectiveness, and scalability considerations directly into the materials analysis in Section 3, creating an explicit link between synthesis route choices and their enviro-economic consequences. Fourth, Section 4 catalog applications which explicitly maps material design parameters identified in the synthesis and hybridization sections to the specific thermal performance requirements of each application domain, including energy-efficient buildings, smart textiles, and photovoltaic panel cooling. In section 5 (Life cycle and Sustainability Consideration) we comprehensively discuss raw material sourcing, processing emissions, energy intensity, toxicity risks (nanoparticles, activators), recyclability and end-of-life pathways of the PCMs in TES applications. In Section 6 (Challenges and Future Directions), we identify and deliberate the future research gaps for innovative research studies and briefly discuss viewpoints on the practical design and construction of ss-PCMs. Lastly, the Conclusions section notes that bio ss-PCMs are a strategically vital category of material for next-generation sustainable TES technology. This bidirectional linkage between material design and application need is a structural feature that adds practical value beyond descriptive existing reviews. Bio-based supporting materials for shape-stabilized PCMs

The use of bio-based supports for shape-stable PCMs is a significant advancement in the design of thermal energy storage systems. These supports provide better mechanical and thermal properties, as well as better sustainability characteristics through the use of biodegradable and renewable materials. Modified wood supports and natural fibers used as PCM matrices have been shown to improve PCM retention and enhance thermal conduction.16 Additional material development is necessary to address the common PCM problems of leakage, phase separation, and lack of stability during multiple thermal cycles. Agro-based materials such as beeswax, rice husk, corn cob, and balsa wood, as presented in Figures 3A–3D, are significant in confining the PCM and maintaining the stability of the PCM structure. The heterogeneous nature of bio-based materials may also contribute beneficially to the thermal storage mechanism. The bio-based materials are environmentally friendly compared to the fossil-based materials. However, despite their renewable origin and environmental advantages, bio-based supporting materials in their raw or minimally processed form are not inherently ideal skeletons for shape-stabilized PCMs.34,35 In their native state, most biomass-derived matrices, including wood, agricultural residues, and natural fibers, show several limitations that hinder their direct use as effective PCM supports.36,37,38 First, the pore structure of unmodified bio-based materials is often not optimized for PCM confinement, and PCM retention depends strongly on pore size, pore connectivity, and pore-surface interactions; as a result, leakage during melting remains a central concern in directly impregnated systems.39,40 Second, raw biomass surfaces are rich in polar oxygen-containing functionalities, and the interaction between PCM and the support surface strongly affects loading, compatibility, and leakage resistance, which is why hydrophobic or surface-functional modification is often required for organic PCM systems.25,41 Third, the thermal conductivity of bio-based matrices is typically low, which slows heat storage and release and is repeatedly identified as a major limitation in wood-based and bio-based PCM composites.36,42,43 Fourth, unmodified bio-based matrices may also suffer reductions in mechanical performance after PCM incorporation, and long-term structural robustness must therefore be considered when shape stability is required.37,39,44 These limitations mean that raw bio-based materials generally require deliberate structural or chemical engineering before they can function effectively as PCM skeletons. The main strategies used to overcome these drawbacks include direct carbonization or pyrolysis, hydrothermal carbonization, and activation-based treatments, which convert biomass into porous carbon frameworks with more favorable pore structures and surface characteristics and generally improved thermal transport and shape stability.8,35,45,46 Beyond carbonization, hybridization with highly conductive secondary phases such as graphene, expanded graphite, metals, and ceramic fillers is widely used to further improve heat transfer and overall composite performance. The following subsections therefore introduce the principal bio-based precursor materials and porous support architectures reported in the literature, before discussing in detail the carbonization and hybridization strategies used to transform them into high-performance PCM skeletons.

Bio-porous materials: A green approach

Biodegradable natural polymers and bio-porous materials derived from renewable sources, including agricultural residues, biopolymers, and natural fibers, are widely used to fabricate eco-friendly capsules compared to synthetic encapsulation techniques. For example, chemically treated wood, where lignin and hemicellulose are removed to open up the pore network, can absorb and stabilize large PCM fractions.47 Agricultural by-products such as rice husk48 and corn cob,49 have also been used successfully as porous supports, showing a low cost and sustainable route for thermal energy storage. Earlier studies have looked closely at porous supports for PCM encapsulation and reported clear improvements in both thermal behavior and sustainability. Chen et al.50 reported a flexible wood-based PCM composite with high storage density and directional heat transfer. The composite was made by combining PEG6000 with delignified balsa wood and boron nitride. The composite delivered melting and freezing enthalpies of 209.3 J/g and 214.9 J/g, about 8% higher than pure PEG6000. Adding 33 wt. % boron nitride further improved heat dissipation. The measured thermal conductivity reached about 0.96 W/(m·K) in both the through-thickness and in-plane radial directions. These results point to strong potential for this type of wood-based composite in thermal storage and heat management applications.

In another work, Khosrojerdi et al.51 developed an ss-PCM composite by impregnating an expanded dolomite (ED) support with n-nonadecane PCM under vacuum with magnetic stirring. This green method used ED-based porous support for n-nonadecane PCM targeting textile thermal protection. The results showed that n-nonadecane was effectively absorbed into the porous structure of ED. The composite showed strong thermal stability and effective temperature regulation, with PCM loading as high as 90%. Later, Kaya et al.52 reviewed several bio-based matrices for PCM encapsulation, including starch-based gels and cellulose derivatives, and showed that they can hold PCM effectively while still remaining biodegradable and environmentally friendly. In a similar line of work, Liang et al.8 examined the incorporation of PCM into porous biochar structures, thereby enhancing thermal energy storage properties with no leakage. Additionally, Liu et al.53 investigated the use of bio-based aerogels for PCM encapsulation due to their outstanding thermal insulation performance and lightweight biomass nature.

Synthesis techniques of porous bio-based materials

Synthesis routes for porous bio-based materials are central to building sustainable, high-performance PCM systems. Different methods are used to increase pore volume and improve the functionality of natural substrates, mainly chemical treatment, thermal decomposition, and templating. Chemical treatments such as delignification remove lignin and hemicellulose from wood and natural fibers, which opens the pore network and improves PCM uptake.55 The trade-off is that these processes often depend on harsh chemicals, which raises environmental and safety concerns. Thermal routes such as pyrolysis of agricultural residues can produce highly porous biochar, but controlling the pore size distribution and maintaining consistent batch quality remains challenging.56 Templating techniques offer better control of the material structure and porosity using a scaffold that is easily removed. However, they also tend to be more costly and involve more processing steps. Other techniques that have been employed include direct carbonization, hydrothermal carbonization, and activation. Each of these techniques has its merits and demerits. None of the techniques appears to be universally applicable. The challenge that is being faced is how to balance the properties of the material, scalability, and environmental factors so that the material does not just perform well in the laboratory but also finds its way into the field.

Direct carbonization or pyrolysis process of biomass materials

The direct carbonization process, also known as pyrolysis, is a process in which biomass undergoes a thermal treatment in a high-temperature inert environment to form carbon materials. In this case, organic materials such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin decompose in a way that forms porous carbon materials with variable pore sizes and a large surface area. The carbon materials produced from biomass have a number of adsorption sites that effectively trap phase change materials and prevent leakage during phase transition.57 The benefit is clear, but control is not always easy. Pore size distribution and final product quality can vary, often due to differences in the starting biomass and processing conditions, which then affect how well the PCM performs inside the structure.58 The variability in density and other morphological characteristics can be attributed to differences in base biomass materials and processing conditions. However, there are process-side concerns related to pyrolysis as well. Volatile compounds and fine particulate matter are released during the process. As such, conditions must be well managed to balance efficiency and environmental impact. Nevertheless, pyrolyzed biochar is still a viable choice for use as a matrix in encapsulated PCM. This is due to the porous structure that is able to retain a high fraction of PCM and distribute it well throughout the material. This minimizes leakage during phase change and can improve conductivity and stability in various applications. The porosity and large surface area of biochar would favor the impregnation of PCMs, resulting in a homogeneous dispersion while minimizing leakage during solid-liquid phase transitions. Moreover, incorporating biochar has been shown to improve the thermal conductivity and stability of PCMs across various applications, including building materials, textiles, and thermal energy storage systems. For instance, Das et al.59 reported that hyacinth plant-derived biochar could incorporate paraffin wax, thereby improving its thermal stability by 17.27 times, with conductivity reaching 0.283 W/(m·K). In one of the recent works, Yang et al.60 described a form-stable composite PCM using waste sawdust as the support matrix, incorporating PEG with 5 wt % expanded graphite (EG). The melting temperature and the solidifying temperature of the composite with 5% EG were 58.6°C and 48.5°C, respectively, and the latent heat was 145.3 and 131.4 kJ/kg. Moreover, the thermal conductivity of the PCMs with 5% EG increased by 23.8% compared with the composite without EG. The supercooling extent of PCMs with 5% EG was 19.2% of that of PEG alone. These values of melting temperature, solidification temperature, and latent heat did not show significant change during heating-cooling cycles; therefore, it had excellent thermal stability. The characteristics of biochar are important for the study of its application for phase change energy storage. Liu et al.24 discussed the effect of pyrolysis temperature on the characteristics of biochar matrix, and their corresponding heat energy storage properties during phase change were analyzed in detail for developing biomass-PCM composites. The above idea was demonstrated using corn straw biochar generated under different pyrolysis conditions, which were then impregnated with PEG via the ultrasound-assisted vacuum technique. The results indicate that the pore structure and surface activity of biochar are two critical factors that should be considered for energy storage performance when preparing PCM using bio-PCMs. The PCM composite showed latent heat storage of 100.2 J/g with good shape stability and leakage resistance. Moreover, the photothermal conversion efficiency of 68.95% demonstrates that it is highly efficient for photothermal energy storage.

Such an investigation provides a rationale for improving biochar properties, given its applicability for PCMs to store energy, and emphasizes mass production at low cost. Hydrothermal carbonization of biomass materials

Biomass hydrothermal carbonization has been reported as a potential method for creating porous carbon frameworks, which are effective supporting materials for phase change materials. This method usually takes place in a medium at a temperature of 180°C–250°C under autogenous pressure, transforming carbohydrates and lignocellulosic materials into hydrochar, which has abundant oxygen-containing groups on the surface of the materials.61 These properties of hydrochar materials are effective in retaining the molten PCM through capillary forces, thus enhancing the stability of the composite materials used in thermal energy storage systems. It mimics the natural process of coal formation, but importantly, the technology can reduce the production timeline to several hours and homogenize the final product, transforming the raw feedstock into a solid hydro-char. This is achieved by thermal decomposition of the organic components of the biomass, which are cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin, in subcritical water.62 The produced hydro-char exhibits a high carbon content, significant porosity, and enhanced surface functionality, making it an ideal material for encapsulating PCMs. Moreover, this process does not require the high temperatures required for simple carbonization of raw biomass, thereby saving energy.63 The preparation of wet biomass is a simple procedure that requires no pre-drying. The ability of HTC to process a wide range of biomass feedstocks, including agricultural residues, food waste, and forestry residues, supports the validation of a waste-to-resource approach to waste management. It has been noted that hydro-char derived from HTC offers many advantages, such as a highly well-conditioned porous structure and abundant oxygen-containing functional groups, which enhance loading and stabilize PCMs.64 In particular, the porous structure and surface functionalization of hydro-char provide the conditions necessary for efficient impregnation of PCMs and for overall prevention of leakage during phase transitions. Using LiBr, they broke hydrogen bonds in bamboo, hydrolyzed cellulose and hemicellulose to glucose and xylose, which then dehydrated to form 5-hydroxymethylfurfural (5-HMF), as shown in Figure 7B. At 200°C under acidic conditions, 5-HMF polymerized to create HCS. These HCS were used as microcapsules to encapsulate polyethylene glycol (PEG), forming a novel shape-stabilized composite PCM for energy storage in concrete. Systematic characterization of this composite in simulated concrete pore solutions demonstrated excellent structural stability and thermal performance, indicating its potential to enhance concrete’s thermal energy storage capacity. Yang et al.67 has successfully created a porous carbon skeleton using Agaricus bisporus through a hydrothermal carbonization method. The pore diameter of the mushroom-derived carbon was reduced after carbonization at 180°C for 12 h. However, the pore structure of the carbon material was completely destroyed when the carbonization temperature was raised to 200°C. This decrease in pore structures limits the adsorption capacity of the carbon skeleton material, which in turn would limit the enhancement of the thermal energy storage capacity of phase change composites (PCCs). Hence, the major challenge in developing mushroom-based PCCs is the prevention of pore structure collapse during the carbonization process. In a paper by Hu et al.,68 HTC of carbohydrates, organic molecules, and biomass is discussed. The paper focuses on HTC at high temperatures, which produces carbon materials with a high carbon content and a graphitic structure, while HTC at low temperatures is an eco-friendly approach that produces carbonaceous materials rich in surface functional groups. Parameters such as metal ions and templates influence the low-temperature HTC process and product characteristics, enabling broad applications in environmental, catalytic, electronic, sensing, and biological fields. Despite HTC’s success, challenges remain, such as lowering reaction temperatures, processing waste biomass, understanding low-temperature HTC mechanisms, and designing processes to control material composition.

Sun et al.69 activated biochar derived from hemp bast using hydrothermal carbonization, followed by KOH activation at different impregnation ratios (1, 3, 5 KOH to biochar). The findings revealed that an impregnation ratio of 3 resulted in the highest surface area of 2,879 m2/g. At an impregnation ratio of 1, the number of pores was minimal, indicating that the activation effect had considerable room for improvement. Conversely, an impregnation ratio of 5 led to pore collapse due to excessive activation. Only at an impregnation ratio of 3 was a uniform carbon network formed, resulting in an optimal pore structure and a high specific surface area. Combining biomass-derived carbon materials from HTC with PCMs can enhance thermal management capabilities, owing to the high thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity of the carbon materials. Such an integration may enhance the efficiency and stability of PCMs in their respective applications, which may further increase the scope of application of both HTC-derived carbon materials and PCMs. Studies have shown that hydrochar may be used for enhancing the thermal conductivity and stability of PCMs, thus making them useful for a variety of applications, such as building materials, thermal management systems, and renewable energy storage systems.

Activated carbonization (AC) of bio-mass materials

Activated carbonization is a key technique for the improvement of bio-based materials’ properties via surface area and porosity increase.70 There are two main steps: carbonization of biomass materials and activation, which can be accomplished by physical or chemical means.71 The first step involves the thermal decomposition of biomass in an inert atmosphere to produce char, while the second step is used to obtain additional porosity and surface functional groups, which confer exceptional adsorptive capacity and structural stability to the material. The most common chemical activation agents are KOH, ZnCl2, and H3PO4.72 These chemicals interact with carbonized material to create an extensive network of pores.

For example, Awitdrus et al.73 explored the use of jackfruit peel waste to produce low-cost, environmentally friendly AC electrode materials for supercapacitor devices. The AC synthesis involves a sequential three-step reaction that starts with KOH activation, proceeds to nitrogen carbonization at 600°C, and then undergoes a second activation by CO2 at 850°C, 900°C, and 950°C. The specific capacitance of the supercapacitor cells was determined to be 85 F/g, 191 F/g, and 131 F/g at 850°C, 900°C, and 950°C, respectively. These results highlight the unique physicochemical properties and electrochemical stability of waste biomass-derived AC electrode materials for supercapacitor devices.

On the contrary, physical activation typically involves gases such as CO2 or steam at high temperatures. This causes the existing pores formed during carbonization to open and enlarge. The effect increases the material’s surface area and porosity, as demonstrated by AC. Additionally, the increased surface area and porosity of the activated biochar facilitate the uniform impregnation and retention of PCMs. Moreover, biochar activation improves the thermal conductivity and stability of PCM. Duan et al.74 in which comparison between the different methods of activation of AC derived from Jatropha hulls steam and CO2 were used to activate the AC. According to the study, the carbon yield was 16.6% with steam and 36.6% with CO2 using microwave-assisted methods, whereas conventional methods yielded 13.3% with steam and 18% with CO2. These results underscored the efficiency of microwave-assisted techniques in enhancing carbon yield during AC production from agricultural residues. In a further study, Yuan et al.75 synthesized a novel ZnFe2O4-loaded porous biochar (RZF) with a high surface area of 1,065 m2/g via a one-step carbonation activation process using kitchen leftover rice as the carbon source, with ZnCl2 and FeCl3 as activators. The optimized composite, RZF-500-1:1, was achieved by adjusting the activation ratio (mass ratio of leftover rice to ZnFe2O4) and pyrolysis temperature. The optimized composite exhibited excellent H2S removal capacity, with a breakthrough capacity of 228.29 mg/g at a temperature of 25°C, compared with inactivated biochar, which has a much lower adsorption capacity of about 12.11 mg/g. The study showed that the conditions for desulfurization, such as temperature and the presence of oxygen, affected the breakthrough capacity of H2S. The proposed mechanism for H2S removal involved an adsorption-catalytic oxidation coupled process. Furthermore, the ZnFe2O4-loaded biochar exhibited high regeneration ability, retaining 91.6% of its initial breakthrough capacity after five regeneration cycles. Comparison of carbonization routes

For PCM encapsulation, the key design variable is not BET surface area by itself but the combination of accessible pore volume, pore size distribution, and surface chemistry. Micropores improve capillary retention, but an overly microporous carbon can restrict PCM infiltration and suppress latent heat. Hierarchical carbons with enough mesopore volume usually give the best balance between loading, shape stability, and thermal reliability.31,76

A useful practical inference follows. If the goal is a low-cost support with acceptable loading, direct carbonization is often sufficient, especially when the biomass already has a useful cellular structure. If the goal is maximum encapsulation efficiency and the best leakage control, activation-enabled carbonization is usually the strongest route. Hydrothermal carbonization is rarely the best final support route on its own, but it can be an effective pre-treatment before activation because it improves feed flexibility and can tune the chemistry of the intermediate hydrochar.44,77

Direct carbonization, usually by slow pyrolysis, preserves much of the biomass-derived channel structure and therefore tends to produce carbons with moderate surface area and a useful meso to macroporous framework.24,23 For PCM support applications, this route is attractive because the inherited pore network often supports good impregnation at low cost and with limited processing steps. Its main weakness is that pore development is less tunable than in activated carbons, so the loading ceiling and leakage resistance may plateau earlier.78,79 Hydrothermal carbonization is better viewed as a precursor-engineering route than as a stand-alone pore-forming route.80 Hydrochars are produced at low temperature and high solid yields from wet biomass, but they are often poorly porous prior to a subsequent carbonization or activation step.81 Activation-enabled carbonization, including chemical activation, physical activation, and in situ activation, gives the highest control over surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution.82 This route therefore produces the most capable PCM supports in many studies, especially when the target is high leakage-free loading and strong thermal cycling stability.83 However, it involves more steps, higher energy input, lower final carbon yield, and larger environmental burden when strong activating agents are used.84 Table 1 below focuses on studies whose available metadata report explicit loading, latent heat, or cycle-stability values. Most stand-alone HTC papers are process papers rather than finished PCM-support studies, so the quantitative PCM table is dominated by direct-pyrolysis and activation-enabled carbons.

Across these studies, activation-enabled carbons have shown better PCM loadings and consistent durability in cycling, with leakage-free loadings reaching up to 85 to 92 wt %. Direct carbonization support materials, on the other hand, have shown more moderate, though useful, performance, at times retaining excellent durability in latent heat retention and, in some cases, excellent durability even after less rigorous processing and development. Hydrothermal carbonization takes on a somewhat different place in these comparisons. The main importance of HTC is its ability to engineer the precursors because it treats wet biomass well, improves carbon retention in the intermediate state, and can engineer the chemistry of hydrochar to be conducive to activation.81 It is also worth noting that, across the studies reviewed, HTC is rarely employed as a fully standalone synthesis route for PCM support preparation. In most reported cases, hydrothermal carbonization is combined with a subsequent high-temperature carbonization step, an activation treatment, or the incorporation of conductive fillers such as carbon nanotubes, in order to develop the porosity, graphitization degree, and thermal conductivity that hydrochar alone cannot achieve.25,26,27 This pattern confirms that the practical role of HTC in bio-based ss-PCM fabrication is best understood as an enabling pre-treatment rather than a complete synthesis strategy in its own. The overall evidence therefore suggests that activation mainly improves PCM-support performance by creating a more accessible and better-connected pore network, direct carbonization remains attractive when low cost and process simplicity are prioritized, and HTC is most useful as a preparative step when wet feedstocks or downstream pore tuning are the key constraints.

It is worth noting that beyond direct pyrolysis, hydrothermal carbonization, and activation, several complementary strategies have been reported in recent years for engineering biomass-derived skeletons for PCM confinement. One notable approach is the wood template method, in which the natural channel architecture of wood is used as a sacrificial scaffold to fabricate three-dimensional bionic metallic frameworks. Guo et al.85 employed this strategy to prepare a 3D porous copper skeleton that faithfully inherited the anisotropic channel structure of wood. Because the vertically aligned channels formed a continuous, highly oriented heat transfer network, the resulting paraffin-based composite PCMs achieved thermal conductivities of 6.7 W/m·K in the vertical direction and 2.3 W/m·K in the horizontal direction, representing 26.8 and 9.2 times the conductivity of pure paraffin wax, respectively, while also delivering strong anti-leakage performance. This demonstrates that biomass-templated metallic scaffolds can surpass the thermal conductivity achievable by carbonization alone, at the cost of higher density and more complex fabrication. A second emerging route involves the in situ growth of carbon nanostructures within an existing biomass-derived carbon skeleton, which creates a hierarchical architecture without requiring a separate filler blending step. Tang et al.86 exploited this concept by growing carbon nanotubes (CNTs) in situ within the channels of a wood-derived carbon framework via a catalytic chemical vapor deposition process, thereby constructing a unique layer-pillar structured scaffold. The CNT pillars bridged adjacent carbon layers, simultaneously enhancing capillary confinement, mechanical support, and bidirectional thermal transport. The resulting PEG-based composite PCMs achieved a shape retention ratio of 94.3% under a 10 N compressive load with less than 3% leakage after multiple thermal cycles, while thermal conductivity improvements of 160.87% (axial) and 143.48% (radial) over pure PEG were recorded, along with an electromagnetic interference shielding effectiveness of 39.62 dB. Collectively, these examples illustrate that the design space for biomass-derived PCM skeletons extends well beyond the three carbonization pathways surveyed in sections 2.2.1-2.2.3. In particular, template-assisted metal deposition and in situ carbon nanotube growth represent two orthogonal engineering strategies that can dramatically widen the achievable range of thermal conductivity and mechanical robustness, and their integration with conventional pyrolysis or activation routes is a promising direction for next-generation bio-based shape-stabilized PCMs.

Hybridization approaches for enhanced heat transfer

Hybridization in PCM heat transfer enhancement approaches is a promising solution for overcoming the intrinsic drawbacks of low thermal conductivity in conventional PCMs. Such approaches involve incorporating bio-based porous substrates with high-thermal-conductivity materials such as carbon nanotubes, graphene and metal nanoparticles yielding PCM composites.8 The hybrid PCM composites enhanced the thermophysical properties, responsiveness, and efficiency of PCMs using high-thermal-conductivity additives. For example, improved thermal conductivity can be achieved by incorporating graphene into a biochar matrix, whether through direct or AC of the feedstock, without adversely affecting porosity or encapsulation efficiency.87 Furthermore, metal nanoparticles incorporated into hydrothermally carbonized substrates enable efficient heat transfer, resulting in a homogeneous melt and a more rapid phase transition of PCM. In addition to retaining the high latent heat storage capacity of the base PCMs, these hybrid materials.88 The synthesis of such hybrid composites is essential for enhancing the functionality and reliability of thermal energy storage systems, which are more efficient in three main applications: building energy management, electronic cooling, and renewable electric systems.

Bio-based support PCMs solve the leakage problem well, but they usually still suffer from slow heat diffusion during charging and discharging because most biomass-derived supports are only moderately conductive, even when they have favorable pore structures.89 Hybridization addresses this limit by introducing a second thermally conductive phase into the bio-based scaffold. In the present literature set, the main routes are graphene-family fillers, EG, metallic particles, and ceramic particles. These fillers are not just passive additives. In the strongest systems, they become part of the support architecture. Thus, the scaffold provides support to the PCM and also becomes the continuous heat transfer path.90

A literature-based point of particular interest is that hybridization should be effective in improving the thermal path without compromising the support role of the bio-based matrix. There are three design variables common in the literature. First, the conductive phase should be in a network form rather than in isolated particle form.91 Secondly, it should reduce the interfacial thermal resistance between the PCM and scaffold, as opposed to merely enhancing the intrinsic conductivity of one of the materials.92 Thirdly, enhanced conductivity must be weighed against clogging of pores, latent heat dilution, cost of fillers, and complexity of process.89 These trade-offs explain why the best filler depends on the design goal rather than on conductivity alone.

Graphene-enhanced stabilization

Graphene family fillers are arguably some of the most efficient methods in improving heat transfer in bio-based support PCMs, owing to their high conductivity, low filler loading, and interfacial effects.43 The relevant studies include graphene oxide, rGO, graphene nanoplatelets, and graphene aerogels in cellulose, wood, lignin-derived, and biochar scaffolds.53,91,93,94,95,96 In these studies, graphene does more than simply provide a conductive pathway. It also alters the polarity of the scaffold material, improves wetting of the PCM, decreases interface thermal resistance, and sometimes even enhances the photothermal conversion as well.97

The initial research on cellulose and graphene aerogels in91 also established the fundamental principle, which has dominated later research, namely, that a three-dimensional network of lightweight cellulose/GNP aerogels, maximizes thermal conductivity and maintains shape stability. This network logic reappears in lignin-assisted graphene aerogels,94 nanocellulose and graphene hybrid aerogels,96 and PEG-grafted nano fibrillated cellulose and graphene frameworks.98 Together, these papers suggest that graphene outperforms simple particulate fillers when it is built into the support rather than blended into the PCM phase.

Graphene also performs well in wood-derived systems, where the naturally aligned channels of wood can be turned into directional heat-transfer paths. Graphene-wrapped wood composites,97 graphene-aerogel-based thermal energy storage wood,53 and graphene-modified carbonized bamboo92 all show that the native anisotropy of bio-based supports can be exploited rather than replaced. This is an important inference from the literature. In ordered supports such as wood, the main benefit of graphene is often not only higher conductivity, but a sharper reduction in contact resistance between the scaffold wall and the infiltrated PCM.

Recent quantitative examples reinforce that point. In the PEG and biochar composite modified with surfactant-treated graphene oxide, the final composite reached a latent heat of 106.51 J/g, thermal conductivity of 0.337 W/m·K, and photothermal conversion efficiency of 77.43%while maintaining leak resistance.95 In graphene or reduced-graphene-oxide-modified carbonized bamboo loaded with PEG, the reduced-graphene-oxide system reached an encapsulation ratio of 81.11 wt %, melting enthalpy of 115.62 J/g, and thermal conductivity of 1.03 W/m·K, about 3.7 times that of pure PEG.92 These values show that graphene can improve both heat-transfer rate and effective PCM utilization when the filler is integrated carefully.

The literature also points to a clear limitation. Graphene is highly effective only within a narrow loading and dispersion window. If the graphene network is incomplete, conductivity gains remain modest. If graphene loading is too high, it can block pores, displace PCM, and reduce latent heat more sharply than the conductivity gain justifies.89,95 The most defensible inference is therefore that graphene is best for high-value, multifunctional systems in which low loading, interfacial engineering, and solar or electrothermal conversion matter as much as bulk conductivity.

Expanded graphite based

Expanded graphite is the most mature and practically scalable hybrid filler in this literature. Its main advantage is structural simplicity. Expanded graphite readily forms continuous graphitic pathways, so it improves heat transport in both melting and solidification without requiring the same level of nanoscale dispersion control as graphene.43,90 This helps explain why EG appears across many bio-based supports, including sawdust, activated carbon, carbonized wheat straw, chitosan aerogels, bacterial cellulose, and other biomass-derived porous carbons.60,101,102,103,104

The literature repeatedly demonstrates that EG performs especially well in conjunction with a porous bio-based support material that provides confinement. In these systems, the bio-based material and the EG play complementary roles: the former confines the PCM, and the latter provides long-range heat transfer pathways.105 The waste-sawdust and PEG system in60 provides a useful benchmark. With only 5% EG, there was an increase in thermal conductivity of 23.8%, supercooling was reduced, and there were no significant changes in the transition temperature and latent heat after 200 thermal cycles.60 This paper is important because it demonstrates that significant improvements in heat transfer can be achieved without high loadings.

More advanced hybrids reinforced the same conclusion. Hybrid supports using activated carbon and EG were seen to enhance thermal conductivity as well as charging and discharging and shape stability for nonadecane.101 A waste biomass-based activated carbon and EG hybrid support for the RT24 PCM showed the logic of two-component support material design, where the porous material provides the support and the graphite enhances the thermal conductivity.102 For the cellulose-based material, the EG was also combined with cellulose nanofibers and even CNTs to enhance the interconnectedness of the material and the presence of directional paths.103 This suggests that EG is more effective if the support material has pore volume and the graphite is arranged so that it bridges the transport route rather than blocking it.

The main inference from the literature is that EG is the strongest baseline choice if the aim is to achieve a practical enhancement of conductivity. It is easier to process and generally less expensive than graphene or metal-based additives. Moreover, the enhancement may be substantial enough to achieve a real enhancement of charging-discharging characteristics rather than merely conductivity itself.90 On the flip side, an excessive amount of EG results in a decrease in the fraction of the PCM, a decrease in latent heat density, and an impregnation problem because of the EG occupying the pore spaces.89 Thus, the optimal design is not maximum graphite loading, but the minimum loading needed to create a connected heat-transfer network cycles.

Metallic particles based

Metallic-particle hybridization is represented mainly by silver and, to a lesser degree, copper. These studies are among the strongest performers in terms of conductivity increase, but they are also the least straightforward in practical terms because of cost and density penalties.106,107,108,109 A consistent feature of the better metallic systems is that the particles are deposited on the bio-based scaffold, often by coating or plating, rather than dispersed as a separate bulk filler. This matters because anchored metallic particles bridge local thermal bottlenecks without occupying as much of the pore volume that would otherwise hold PCM.

Silver-based systems dominate the evidence base. In,107 silver nanoparticles deposited on a biological porous-carbon support loaded with PEG produced a composite with a thermal conductivity of 0.676 W/m·K, melting enthalpy of 137.3 J/g, and photothermal conversion efficiency of 82.7%, together with strong anti-leakage performance. Related studies on nano-Ag-coated eggplant-based porous carbon,106 loofah foam,110 navel-orange-peel foam,108 and grapefruit-peel porous carbon111 all point in the same direction: Nano silver enhances conductivity and sometimes simultaneously enhances photothermal conversion. The common justification is that the addition of silver enhances the thermal transport of the support material and simultaneously enhances the efficiency of light absorption in solar-thermal storage systems.

The microtubule-based kapok fiber research in112 is a particularly useful reference as it illustrates this trade-off well. The silver nanoparticles slightly reduced the latent heat from 153.5 J/g to 146.8 J/g but achieved a 92.3% enhancement in thermal conductivity along with great form stability; moreover, it remained robust after 2,000 cycles. This is a great example of a metal nanoparticle payoff when rapid heat storage and release are more important than maintaining the absolute highest latent heat. Copper-based work is less abundant in this review but points in a similar direction. Copper-plated cellulose aerogels and related cellulose-stabilized copper systems suggest that lower-cost metals can also produce effective conductive pathways in bio-based scaffolds.109,113 Still, the literature base is clearly heavier on silver than copper. The strongest inference, therefore, is that metallic-particle hybridization is best suited to specialized, performance-driven composites where fast thermal response and photothermal behavior justify higher material cost and processing complexity.

Xie et al.111 created a new composite PCM by using biomass grapefruit peel-based porous carbon foam as the main structure and Nano-Ag as a surface modifier. The PCM was added using the conventional vacuum immersion technique. The modified carbon foam with Nano-Ag demonstrated an excellent three-dimensional structure and enhanced photo-thermal effects, resulting in improved photo-thermal transformation capacity, thermal conductivity, and thermal stability of the composite PCM. Specifically, thermal conductivity of PCM composite increased by 2.1 times to 0.68 W/m·K. The enthalpy efficiency of the PCM composite also enhanced from 82.0% to 83.7%, and the photo-thermal transformation performance rose from 76.8% to 91.2%. Ceramic particle based

Ceramic-particle hybridization is led by boron nitride in this search, especially boron nitride nanosheets integrated with cellulose, chitosan, alginate, and wood-inspired aerogels.115,116,117,118,119,120 This branch of the literature is significant because it addresses a very different design problem from that addressed in either graphene or silver. In these cases, the goal is not only high conductivity, but high conductivity and electrical insulation, dimensional stability, and sometimes even mechanical robustness. This is why ceramic fillers are particularly interesting in electronics-related thermal management and in cases where conductive carbons or metals might not be desired.115,119

A major result from this literature is that ceramic fillers only become highly effective when their orientation and connectivity are controlled. The cellulose and boron-nitride-nanosheet skeleton in115 is a key paper because it demonstrates anisotropically high conductivity in a robust bio-based scaffold. Inference from this and related studies is straightforward: The ceramic filler should be organized into a directional or networked framework, not scattered randomly in the matrix.116,120 Similar conclusions emerge from boron nitride combined with chitosan118,119 and sodium alginate,117 where the support itself helps organize the filler into a pathway that transfers heat while preserving structural stability and reusability.

In comparison to graphene and EG, ceramic fillers tend to be less frequently chosen for the absolute best conductivity improvements. Ceramic fillers’ main advantage is their well-balanced properties. Boron nitride provides thermal conductivity improvement while maintaining electrical insulation and sometimes enhancing scaffold stiffness and stability.115,119 Such a property makes ceramic hybridization an especially interesting solution in cases of thermal regulation in the vicinity of electronics, battery safety, or wherever electrical conduction cannot be allowed. On the other hand, a disadvantage of using ceramic particles is the need for a more intentional design compared to EG. If the particles are isolated from one another, the enhancement of conductivity will be less effective. If the particles are aligned or otherwise connected, the enhancement of conductivity can be significant in the through-plane direction.115,120

Cross-cutting literature-based inferences

Three broad inferences may be drawn from a consideration of the four hybrid routes taken as a whole.

First, filler topology is more important than filler type. Graphene, EG, silver, and boron nitride are all best used in a connected topology within the bio-based scaffold.91,112,115 A highly conductive filler that remains discontinuous is usually less effective than a moderately conductive filler arranged into a robust network.

Second, the best hybrid systems are those that split the function of scaffold and filler. Thus, the role of the scaffold is that of a mechanical, and hence capillary, confiner, while the role of the filler is that of a heat transfer enhancer.8,105 When the conductive filler invades too much pore volume, latent heat drops and impregnation worsens. This is why coatings, wrapped structures, aerogel backbones, and plated surfaces often outperform simple particulate blending. Third, the optimal hybrid route depends on the constraints of the application. For large-scale applications, EG is the most practical choice owing to its ease of processing and broad effectiveness. For multifunctional applications involving interfacial heat transfer and solar or electrothermal energy conversion, graphene is the preferred choice owing to its superior thermal, optical, and mechanical properties. For applications requiring fast thermal response and high photothermal efficiency, even if at increased cost, metallic particles are optimal. For applications requiring improved thermal conductivity without compromising electrical insulation, ceramic fillers, particularly boron nitride, are optimal.

The literature does not support one universally superior hybridization strategy for bio-based support PCMs. Instead, it shows a hierarchy of design priorities. For scalable thermal storage, EG is usually the most defensible first choice. For multifunctional composites with strong interfacial engineering and solar-thermal response, graphene-based hybridization is often more powerful. For very rapid heat transfer and photothermal amplification, metallic fillers can be highly effective, though rarely the cheapest option. For insulated thermal management, ceramic fillers provide the most balanced route. Across all four approaches, the decisive factor is how well the conductive phase is integrated into the bio-based scaffold so that heat-transfer gains are achieved without sacrificing PCM loading, latent heat, and structural stability. To summarize, selecting the matrix material is the most critical aspect of the meticulous trade-off among multiple characteristics of composite PCMs. Metals are known to be durable and efficient at heat transfer; however, their increased weight and susceptibility to corrosion may not be desirable. Carbon is inexpensive and somewhat weak, even though it is lightweight and reasonably chemically stable. Ceramics are distinguished by their exceptional resistance to heat and chemical stability, yet the issue of brittleness cannot be resolved, and the manufacturing process is complex. All kinds of matrices have their own set of benefits and drawbacks, and it is essential to choose the appropriate one for a given application by carefully considering its needs.

According to the literature search, Table 2 shows that SS-PCM composites employing EG as a supporting matrix exhibit a significant increase in thermal conductivity, achieving 715%121 and 900%130 compared to the base PCM. The advancement in thermal conductivity of the ss-PCM composites demonstrates that carbon-based matrices are noticeably superior to ceramic and metallic matrices in enhancing heat transfer efficiency in the PCMs. The enhancement in thermal conductivity is ascribed to the characteristic high thermal conductivity of graphitic carbon, its continuous, low interfacial thermal resistance, linked lamellar structure, and effective phonon transmission. Ceramic matrices exhibit inadequate thermal conductivity and brittleness, whereas metallic matrices, despite their high conductivity, are limited by susceptibility to corrosion, high density, and poor interfacial compatibility with PCMs. The porous structure of EG facilitates homogeneous PCM encapsulation and strong thermal routes, which render carbon matrices the most promising substrates for high-performance SS-PCM systems.

Enviro-economic aspects of TES: Underlying scopes of bio-based porous PCM composites

The rising demand for sustainable energy solutions has placed TES technologies at the forefront of renewable energy systems. These technologies offer an effective solution for mitigating climate change and global warming and improve thermal effectiveness in residential buildings by 30–35% and decrease overall energy consumption by up to 50%, making them a valuable addition to modern energy systems.149 The integration of bio ss-PCM composites into TES systems offers an effective approach to achieving these environmental goals while also delivering substantial economic benefits. These materials not only contribute to reducing carbon emissions but also help diminish the environmental impact associated with the production and disposal of conventional composite PCMs. For TES to be economically feasible, several factors must be carefully considered. Ensuring that bio-derived or bio-PCM composites have the right thermochemical properties, along with suitable charging and discharging rates and adequate heat absorption capacity, is essential to avoid leakage and corrosion. One of the most important economic benefits that bio ss-PCMs offer is their potential to conserve energy. This is because they have the ability to enhance thermal storage and thermal transfer, thus increasing the efficiency of the system and reducing the energy consumption. Though materials such as paraffin wax are widely used as PCMs, they do not have advanced thermophysical properties. The use of better-quality porous carbon reinforcements such as graphene and carbon nanotubes has significantly enhanced the thermal conductivity of the composites. However, the use of these advanced materials comes at the cost of their expensive construction and processing. On the other hand, cheaper alternatives such as carbon fibers from sisal plants could emerge as cost-effective thermal supports, thus making them suitable for solar energy harvesting and recycling.150

From an economic point of view, porous bio-PCM composites have some benefits. One of the most significant benefits provided by porous bio-PCM composites is that they can decrease energy consumption and costs in buildings. For example, porous bio-PCM composites can be utilized in buildings to maintain constant temperatures. This can decrease energy consumption, since constant temperatures can be maintained without any mechanical heating and cooling devices. This can lead to reduced energy costs and, therefore, an investment return. Furthermore, bio-PCMs can decrease greenhouse gases by 34.4%–59% in the upcoming years, particularly after mass production.151 Ensuring the economic feasibility of bio-PCM composites therefore requires evaluation of their long-term durability. PCM materials must be capable of sustained performance with minimal maintenance to remain economically viable. Another key economic consideration is the evaluation of return on investment. For bio-PCM composites, it is necessary that they are made easily accessible in the market. Moreover, government subsidies and incentives have also been emphasized in terms of future economic considerations, and thus, these technologies have been made cost-effective and viable for TES.8 Moreover, the cost-effectiveness of bio-PCMs is also boosted by their long lifespan of operation and low maintenance needs. Unlike some of the conventional TES systems that deteriorate with time and thus demand frequent maintenance, bio-PCMs have the advantage of maintaining their efficiency for long periods of time without any maintenance. This advantage thus ensures that the cost-effectiveness of the energy-saving technology is maintained throughout its lifespan. Moreover, the use of cheap raw materials such as agricultural by-products in the production of bio-PCMs ensures that the engineering costs of the technology are kept low.

Government incentives, regulatory frameworks, and subsidies are other factors that impact the economic viability of porous bio-PCMs. Policies that promote the growth and use of renewable energy technologies may greatly improve the financial appeal of TES systems. For example, government incentives such as grants and tax credits for energy-efficient building materials can offset some of the costs associated with incorporating PCMs into building materials, making them attractive to investors and consumers. The bio-PCMs market is expected to increase in the future, and this will further reduce costs through economies of scale. A critical but underreported dimension in the enviro-economic evaluation of bio-based ss-PCMs is the relationship between material lifetime and cumulative economic value. The economic case for bio-based ss-PCMs cannot be made on the basis of raw material cost alone; it depends fundamentally on how many thermal cycles the composite can sustain before its latent heat retention, leakage resistance, or scaffold integrity degrades to a level that compromises system performance. The available cycling data across the three carbonization routes is heterogeneous and does not permit a definitive ranking of one route over another in terms of long-term stability. Reported cycle counts range from 50 to 1,000 across both direct and activation-enabled carbonization systems, and several entries report only qualitative stability descriptors such as “good shape stability” or “thermal stability improved” without specifying a cycle count, making quantitative comparison unreliable. This inconsistency in reporting is itself a significant gap in the literature. What can be stated is that both direct carbonization and activation-enabled carbonization have demonstrated acceptable short-to-medium term cycling stability in specific systems, but the relationship between carbonization route, pore architecture, PCM loading, and long-term cycle durability has not been systematically established. The economic implication of this gap is important, without standardized lifetime data reported under consistent cycling conditions, it is not currently possible to calculate a reliable levelized cost of thermal storage for any of the three routes, and cost comparisons based on upfront material cost alone is therefore incomplete and potentially misleading.

From a practical economic standpoint, the levelized cost of storage (LCOS) framework defined as the total life cycle cost of the material divided by the total thermal energy delivered over its operational lifetime provides a more meaningful basis for economic comparison than unit material cost alone. This principle has significant implications for material selection in real applications. A composite that costs more to produce but maintains stable performance over a substantially greater number of cycles may represent better long-term economic value than a cheaper material that degrades earlier. However, the current literature does not yet provide the standardized lifetime data needed to apply this framework reliably across the three carbonization routes reviewed here. Future work should therefore prioritize the systematic reporting of cycle count, latent heat retention percentage, and leakage rate after defined thermal cycling intervals alongside initial material cost and synthesis energy, to enable genuine LCOS comparisons across the bio-based ss-PCM literature. In conclusion, the enviro-economic characteristics of TES point to the promising potential of porous bio-PCMs as a viable means of energy storage in a manner that is environmentally friendly and economically viable. These new materials hold promise for the development of stronger and more energy-efficient structures through the utilization of green resources in a manner consistent with green objectives. Future research and development should focus on bio-PCMs with emphasis on their optimization and new areas of application where these cutting-edge materials may have a significant impact.

Applications of bio-based shape-stabilized PCMs

The integration of PCM in various sectors has been revolutionary in TES applications, leading to innovative ways in energy efficiency and sustainability. This technology has shown promising benefits in managing thermal loads, owing to its latent heat capacity, whereby energy can be stored and released in phases. Therefore, in buildings, integrating PCMs into construction materials enables more stable temperature control within buildings, reducing the need for mechanical heating and air conditioning. In the textiles and wearables sector, smart clothing materials integrated with PCM can regulate body temperature. In the energy sector, the integration of PCM in the cooling system of photovoltaic panels ensures that they are not overheated. This contributes to significant improvements in the efficiency of solar energy conversion by maintaining optimal operating temperatures. These examples collectively highlight the considerable potential of PCMs across diverse sectors.

Energy-efficient buildings

PCMs incorporated into building designs are slowly gaining attention due to their potential for enhancing energy efficiency and maintaining a comfortable temperature range within buildings. These materials are known for their large latent heat absorption and, consequently, for their ability to maintain a constant temperature despite external temperature fluctuations. The impact of PCMs incorporated as a layer is presented with reference to several parameters, such as solar radiation, temperature, wind speed, air-conditioning systems, and the type of PCMs. When determining the effect, Guo et al.152 applied simulation methods such as the Virtual Finite Method and the enthalpy porous model, enabling them to take into account the phase transition process in the PCM layer. From the results, it was clear that the energy consumption could be reduced by applying the PCM layers. However, the performance of the PCMs is highly dependent on the climate, especially the melting temperature. Materials with a melting temperature greater than 20°C perform well under hot weather conditions. A thicker layer may enhance energy consumption reduction, but at the same time, the thickness may increase the density of the walls. The thermal performance of conventional bricks with the use of PCM was investigated by,153 and it was found that if the PCM is located closer to the indoor side, energy savings are maximized, and the best temperature for melting changed with the seasons, ranging from 18°C to 26°C. If the temperature for phase change is not properly matched, the energy demand for cooling in summer increases rather than decreases. An optimal temperature for the melting point at 18°C provided the best benefit, with a reduction in annual thermal load by 17.6%, with 13.2% directly from the latent heat.

Álvarez et al.154 researched various integration strategies for PCMs in different shapes of buildings such as curved shapes or multi-story shapes to enhance the efficiency of PCMs by maintaining temperatures inside a building. However, it is very conditional upon proper installation, compatibility of the PCM with the base material, as well as the actual thermal demand of the building. They researched various options such as ventilated PCM façades or ceiling systems incorporating PCMs that could reduce heat gain inside a building. However, a few practical limitations are still there, like leakage potential, additional weights to the structures, as well as long-term durability. Although this is a promising solution, proper installation techniques, cost reduction, and demonstrated long-term reliability are still required before widespread adoption. In this regard, Castell et al.155 proposed an experimental cubicle setup with conventional and alveolar brick walls, aiming at investigating macro-encapsulated PCMs (RT27 and SP25 A8) in real Mediterranean conditions, as depicted in Figures 12A–12D. For comparison, reference cubicles without PCM were also included. While smoother and lower peak temperatures were recorded in PCM cubicles under free-floating conditions, incomplete night solidification pointed toward an active or passive cooling approach. Under controlled cooling conditions, these cubicles recorded 15% lower energy consumption and reduced CO2 emissions by about 1–1.5 kg/year/m2. This experiment thus validated the technical viability and energy-saving benefits of these materials in building envelope applications. The inherent property of phase change materials, i.e., storing and releasing heat, can be utilized in improving the performance of buildings. Depending on the desired requirement, these materials can be integrated into different areas of the envelope in multiple configurations. These common integration approaches to maximize the building envelope’s heat storage capacity are illustrated in Figure 12E. The use of PCMs is a ground-breaking method for improving the building’s performance by increasing the thermal mass of the structure.156 For instance, as illustrated in Figure 12F, the maximum daily temperature in the reference test cell devoid of PCM attained 36.1°C, whereas the PCM room registered 32.3°C, resulting in a peak reduction of around 3.8°C–4°C. Nighttime temperatures in the PCM chamber were marginally elevated due to the solidification of PCM and the concomitant release of heat. The findings also indicate a pragmatic constraint. If the PCM does not release its stored heat during the night, its storage ability drops after several hot days in a row. It is incapable of absorbing as much heat the following day. Adequate ventilation at night helps restore its performance by allowing proper cooling and solidification.157 Figure 12G presents the implementation of MicroPCM EnFinit 35-based foam concrete panels as an external building envelope for thermal energy management. The incorporation of PCM significantly reduced daytime heat transfer, resulting in indoor air and interior surface temperature reductions of up to 5.5°C and 6.5°C, respectively. To examine the effect of fully bio based PCMs in buildings, Boussaba et al.160 developed an eco-friendly composite using coconut fat as the PCM and a support matrix made of natural clay and cellulose fibers to raise the thermal inertia of building envelopes. The composite showed a thermal conductivity of about 1.06 W/m·K, indicating a clear improvement after integration. The material is low cost and bio derived, and it shows good promise for passive solar thermal energy storage in building envelope applications. Wu et al.161 investigated the hygrothermal behavior of a multilayer building envelope integrating hemp lime concrete (HLC) and PCM. The experiments recreated both outdoor and indoor conditions and evaluated four wall envelope configurations, one without PCM and three with the PCM layer placed at different positions. Adding PCM clearly improved the hygrothermal behavior of the hemp lime composite, with better control of both temperature and relative humidity. The placement of the PCM layer close to the outdoor side resulted in the highest heat storage and release effect, and it produced the greatest reduction in heating and cooling demands. Based on the results, it is suggested that the placement of the PCM layer close to the exterior side will result in better thermal and moisture inertia, and it will produce greater savings. In another study on bio-PCM for building cooling, Guermat et al.162 examined wall systems that used bio-PCMs made from local vegetable oils and purified beeswax for thermal energy storage. The results indicated a clear increase in the storage performance after the integration with the PCM. They also identified the best position for the layer, which reduced the surface temperature inside the walls by a maximum of 3.95°C. These results confirm the use of bio PCMs in the control of temperature in building walls. The use of bio supports with PCMs is a promising solution for the development of environmentally friendly temperature control. Many studies show clear benefits in the use of these materials, such as reduced heating and cooling loads, improved thermal inertia, and energy efficiency in buildings. Among these, coconut fat, hemp lime concrete, and a mixture of vegetable oil and beeswax show a clear potential for use in building walls, mainly for their low costs and sustainability. However, the correct adjustment of the content and temperature of the PCMs is still a pending issue, as incorrect use may produce negative impacts under different climate conditions.

Textiles and wearables

In smart wearables and textiles, PCMs help maintain the wearer’s thermal comfort regardless of environmental conditions. Several bio-based support structures derived from cellulose or chitosan have been developed as flexible, permeable matrices derived from cellulose or chitosan has been developed to accommodate PCMs.163 Cellulose-based composite support structures ensure that thermal energy can be efficiently stored and released, thereby enhancing wearer comfort in textiles. Similarly, chitosan-based matrices enhance durability and flexibility while optimizing thermal management.164 They are generally less prone to breaking while preserving the flexibility of the support structure. The alternative to chitosan or cellulose is to reinforce the PCM with added natural fibers of jute and hemp.165 As a result, it is used in wearable devices and clothing that help the body maintain its temperature, manage moisture, and perform well in various environmental conditions.

For instance, Qian et al.163 developed multifunctional cellulose-based phase change fibers using a light-induced, metal-free surface-initiated atom transfer radical polymerization process that avoids harsh chemical routes. These fibers used cellulose as a support and poly-hexadecyl acrylate as the PCM, and they exhibited a high phase change enthalpy of 73 J/g, a high tensile strength of 10.5 MPa, and super hydrophobicity. The UV-induced grafting of poly-hexadecyl acrylate formed a core containing a phase-change material and a shell containing cellulose-like structures that improves the overall thermal management. This method overcame the challenges of phase change fibers (PCFs) fabrication and enabled numerous applications in other areas. In a related study, Gu et al.166 designed a bilayer polyvinyl butyral composite textile that combines phase change heat storage with radiative cooling for personal comfort in hot conditions. The fabric reflects most incoming sunlight and emits heat strongly to the atmosphere, which supports daytime cooling. It contains about 70 wt. % PCM microcapsules and shows high latent heat, leading to temperature drops of up to about 10°C compared with fabrics without PCM. At the same time, it retains practical features such as flexibility, breathability, and washability. Majd et al.167 investigated bio-textiles with incorporated microencapsulated PCMs in building envelope components with the aim of improving energy performance. Thermal regulation was targeted in the investigation. Bio-textiles with three different types of incorporated PCMs were investigated using DSC, TGA, and thermal tests. The composite material exhibited a high melting point of 25.25°C and latent heat of 194.9 J/g. Thermal tests indicated an increase in specific heat and conductivity of the various textile blends. Based on both economic and environmental considerations, cotton fabric was found to be the best among the investigated textiles due to its low price and CO2 impact. The composite material with an acrylic binder used in the investigation indicates good potential as a textile used in building envelope components. To illustrate the possible applications of the phase change fibers (PFWs), Xu et al.168 created new textile materials that were intended for personal thermal regulation. Instead of the standard flat textile materials, the PWFs were electrospun into a stable honeycomb structure. This significantly enhanced the elasticity and wearing comfort of the materials. The 2D materials were found to have high melting enthalpy, low thermal conductivity, and stable PCM retention after thermal cycling. The authors were not satisfied with the 2D materials and sought to create 3D materials that were cotton-like. This was achieved through the production of the PWFs using the multi needle spinning method. The materials were found to be lightweight, fluffy, and had high porosity. The materials were able to withstand stretching and compression. The 3D materials were found to have lower thermal conductivity than standard cotton and even eiderdown.

Photovoltaic panel cooling

The efficiency of PV panels is heavily dependent on temperature. It has been observed that an increase in temperature results in lower efficiency in converting sunlight into electricity. Hence, there is now much research going into cooling mechanisms that can maintain the temperature at an optimal level. It has been observed that, despite advancements in technology, thermal issues are still an important bottleneck in PV technology. This is because, in addition to increased efficiency and power output, cooling mechanisms can extend the lifespan of these systems. However, they also increase the cost and complexity. In another study, Jamil et al.173 used nanofluids to cool PV systems. The research showed promising results in thermal management and efficiency increase by 8.92°C and 30.23% for PV systems, respectively. Life cycle and Sustainability Considerations

The sustainability of bio-based support PCMs depends on more than the renewable label of the precursor. Across the literature, environmental performance is shaped by feedstock origin, preprocessing needs, synthesis route, energy source, activating chemicals, product yield, and end-of-life handling. As a result, materials derived from wastes or residues can still carry significant environmental burdens if drying demand, chemical activation, wastewater generation, or low-yield processing are not well managed. Life cycle assessment provides a comprehensive framework for the rigorous evaluation of bio-based supporting materials for ss-PCMs, considering thermal performance, environmental integrity, energy efficiency, and long-term sustainability.175 The sustainability of bio-porous matrices composed of waste biomass and integrated with high-conductivity additives depends on numerous interrelated phases, including the acquisition of raw materials, their processing, system operation, and end-of-life management. Each of these phases affects the wide-ranging environmental and economic viability of TES systems.a.Raw material sourcing: The key impact on sustainability lies in securing raw materials. Agricultural residues (such as wheat husk, rice husk, coconut shell, sugarcane waste, and coffee waste), forestry waste, and food-processing by-products are inexpensive, renewable, and carbon-neutral feedstocks.176 On the other hand, commercialized additives adversely affect the environment due to the harmful chemicals they contain.177 Therefore, the aforementioned bio-resources show a substantial decrease in embodied carbon footprint and resource depletion compared with polymeric supports derived from fossil fuels. However, the characteristics of biomass material, i.e., the amount of lignin and cellulose in the biomass material, and ash content of the biomass material also play a role in the efficacy of the PCM encapsulation process.178 The sustainability of the product throughout its entire life cycle also depends on the supply chain’s stability, the neutrality of its land use, and the extent of its competition with food and natural resources.b.Energy intensity: The synthesis route also impacts the energy consumption and the emissions produced. Direct carbonization or pyrolysis is simple and easy to scale up. However, it needs temperatures between 500°C and 800°C in an inert gas environment. This implies that it needs considerable energy and subsequently leads to considerable CO2 emissions if fossil fuels are employed. Hydrothermal carbonization occurs in an aqueous environment and needs temperatures between 180°C and 250°C.179 This indicates that reduced thermal energy is required, enabling the use of wet biomass without prior drying and thereby reducing the upstream energy requirement. However, HTC incurs energy costs associated with pressure and generates process water containing dissolved organics that requires treatment. In AC, chemical activators like KOH, H3PO4, and ZnCl2 are used, along with a stepwise heating procedure, to increase the surface area and volume of the pores. This is a very important step for increased loading of PCMs and leakage resistance.180 However, it is associated with a very high energy and chemical footprint. From a life cycle point of view, the benefits of improved encapsulation efficiency and thermal stability are offset by increased emissions and resource consumption during the processing step.181c.Toxicity risks: This is a critical issue, especially when sophisticated hybridization techniques are employed. Traces of chemicals from the activation process, if not properly neutralized, may impact soil and aquatic environments unfavorably. The incorporation of graphene, EG, nanoparticles, and ceramics may improve the thermal conductivity and charge/discharge rates of the PCMs. However, it may also pose a risk to the health of the workers and the environment.182 The emission of nanoparticles during synthesis, development of ss-PCM composites, and degradation can affect the sustainability of ‘green’ ss-PCMs due to risks of inhalation and ecotoxicity. Life cycle assessment has shown that, although there is an improvement in energy efficiency, there is an environmental drawback due to potential toxicity, and hence ‘green’ synthesis, low loading, and encapsulation in bio-carbon are necessary.183d.Recyclability: The recyclability and circularity of materials are important for durable sustainability. Bio-based porous carbons are more recyclable at a fundamental level compared to polymeric materials due to their thermal stability. In some cases, ss-PCMs can be re-developed by re-impregnating degraded PCM into the same porous scaffold.184 This improves their durability and reduces the use of materials. There might be an increase in the difficulty of the recycling procedure if the material is hybrid and contains metals and ceramic compounds. This is because of the need for the separation of the materials, which might demand more financial and energy resources. The production of ss-PCM composites using materials that can be easily disassembled and the use of green fillers such as bio-derived graphene and ceramic materials meet the requirements of the circular economy.185e.End-of-life pathways: These pathways further differentiate bio ss-PCMs from conventional TES materials. Thermal regeneration, regulated combustion with energy recovery, or utilization in secondary applications such as adsorbents or building fillers are all viable options.186 When bio-carbon matrices are waste, they can effectively sequester carbon for an extended period if stabilized in landfills or reused as soil amendments that are free of contaminants. However, improper disposal of activated and nanoparticle-containing composites is having a detrimental impact on the environment.187 End-of-life strategies are also considered during the design phase, with a focus on degradation, leaching, and regulatory compliance.f.Life cycle sustainability is not determined by any single stage: The sustainability of life cycles is not dependent on a single phase but is a result of a complex interplay between materials, methods of synthesis, strategies for hybridization, and application contexts.188 In energy-efficient structures, such as cooling systems, where photovoltaic panels are used, improved thermal management may result in a considerable decrease in the energy required for building operations and, in turn, justify the higher processing costs.189 However, in applications such as textiles, where materials are ephemeral, it is preferable to use materials that are minimally harmful, energy-efficient, and readily disposable. It is vital to optimize the life cycle of each application.190

The incorporation of bio ss-PCMs is growing across various TES applications; however, several noteworthy challenges must still be addressed to improve their effectiveness and feasibility. This section identifies the key challenges and future directions with a focus on leakage concerns, cost-effectiveness, and scalability. Addressing leakage issue

Leakage remains one of the most persistent barriers to the practical deployment of bio-based support PCMs. Although biomass-derived porous carbons, wood-based scaffolds, cellulose frameworks, and hybrid aerogels can effectively suppress macroscopic flow of molten PCM, complete leakage resistance is still difficult to achieve when pore architecture is not well matched to the PCM, when loading approaches the upper confinement limit, or when repeated thermal cycling progressively weakens the support network. Current evidence shows that leakage is not governed by a single structural parameter, but by the coupled effects of pore size distribution, pore connectivity, interfacial affinity, and long-term framework stability.a.Encapsulation Challenges: A major encapsulation challenge lies in reconciling high PCM loading with reliable shape stability. The earlier works focused primarily on the importance of surface area as a parameter for designing effective supports, while the latter works have shown that accessible pore structures are of greater importance than surface areas. Supports with small pore diameters may cause a reduction in the rate of infiltration, while supports with large pore diameters may cause a reduction in the rate of capillary action during PCM melting, resulting in seepage and loss of latent heat. A hierarchically porous structure with a balanced mesopore volume is considered better than supports with extreme porosity. Another problem that is still unsolved is that there is no standardized method for evaluating the leakage of the systems. Most of the systems are compared and evaluated under different heating conditions, holding times, and number of cycles. Future work should move beyond surface-area maximization toward pore-network engineering, with greater attention to loading thresholds, pore accessibility, and leakage behavior under realistic operating conditions.b.Material Compatibility: The compatibility of the material will be equally important since the nature of the interaction between the PCM and the support material will vary considerably from one type of PCM to another. For PEG-based materials, the presence of oxygen functional groups and hydrogen-bonding interactions will enhance the wettability and infiltration of the PCM. However, if the interaction is too strong, it will limit the crystallization and the latent heat absorption. Paraffin and fatty acids will be more influenced by capillary confinement and accessibility. The compatibility of the material will become more intricate if the PCM is a hybrid material containing fillers that will alter the heat-transfer mechanisms as well as the surface chemistry and accessibility of the material. A significant area of research in the future will be the establishment of design rules that will be specific to the type of PCM and will be able to address the compatibility of the material.c.Thermal Stability: Thermal stability is also viewed from a broader perspective than merely thermal decomposition resistance. In the case of bio-based support PCMs, thermal stability is defined as the retention of latent heat, maintenance of chemical structure, resistance to leakage, and retention of scaffold integrity during repeated melting/solidification cycles. While a large number of reported materials have demonstrated promising short-term cycling stability, long-term stability could still be affected by factors like collapse of pores, oxidation, activation residue, gradual degradation of confinement capability of capillary structures, or aggregation of hybrid composite particles. These factors are more significant when dealing with multifunctional materials that contain metal or photothermal particles, which could introduce additional degradation routes through debonding and overheating. Therefore, future research should focus more on accelerated lifetime tests that involve thermal cycling along with structural and chemical characterization before and after usage. For example, leakage tests, DSC, TGA, FTIR, XRD, and microstructural imaging should be carried out to assess whether short-term stability translates to long-term durability. In summary, leakage in bio-based support PCMs cannot be addressed through pore confinement alone. Long-term leakage suppression is a function of a multi-faceted approach to encapsulation architecture, compatibility between PCMs and supports, and thermal durability.

Cost-effectiveness and scalability

Achieving cost-effectiveness and scalability in the production and application of bio-based support structure-based composite PCMs is another significant challenge. Although reviews of bio-based shape-stabilized PCMs are now numerous, a clearer practical comparison of the preparation routes remains useful. The available literature suggests that the three main carbonization routes should not be treated as interchangeable, because they create different trade-offs in pore development, leakage resistance, processing burden, and deployment relevance.8,34,45 Direct pyrolysis is generally the simplest route and can preserve the native cellular or channel structure of biomass, which is advantageous when the precursor already provides a useful scaffold for PCM confinement and directional heat transfer. This is illustrated by carbonized wood and straw systems, where good PCM loading and improved heat transfer were achieved without overly elaborate processing.191,192 By contrast, hydrothermal carbonization is attractive mainly because it can process wet biomass without predrying and operates at lower temperature, but the resulting hydrochars often require further treatment to develop sufficient porosity for competitive PCM support performance.34,45 Activation-assisted carbonization provides the clearest route to high specific surface area, improved pore accessibility, and higher PCM uptake, but it also introduces added chemical consumption, washing steps, and a higher process burden, especially for chemically activated systems.8

From a performance standpoint, the strongest evidence is for leakage control and thermal reliability rather than for full practical maturity. Biomass-derived porous carbons can effectively stabilize molten PCM through capillary confinement and interfacial interactions when pore structure and loading are properly matched.8,45 In primary studies, activated walnut-shell carbon supported higher PCM loading than its non-activated counterpart and remained thermally reliable after 1,000 heating-cooling cycles, while carbonized maize-straw composites also showed good reliability after 200 cycles.22,192 More broadly, the review literature consistently shows that laboratory-scale cycling stability is often satisfactory, but this should not be equated with demonstrated service lifetime under real operating conditions.34,193 At present, most studies still report bench-scale cycling tests rather than long-term field data, and much less attention has been given to mechanical aging, weather exposure, maintenance behavior, or degradation under realistic use environments.

Economic value is also discussed far less rigorously than thermal performance. Many reviews describe biomass-derived supports as low-cost because the feedstocks are abundant, renewable, or waste-derived, and because some fabrication routes such as melt impregnation or direct carbonization are relatively simple.8,34 However, explicit techno-economic evidence remains scarce. One notable exception is a wood-derived biochar composite study that reported a thermal storage cost of 0.50 CAD per kWhth, while also showing that reactor capital and feedstock procurement were major cost drivers.194 This is useful evidence, but it is not yet enough to support broad claims that one carbonization route is economically optimal across applications. In practice, cost will depend not only on feedstock price, but also on activation chemistry, washing requirements, hybrid filler use, energy input, and whether the added processing yields a performance gain large enough to justify those steps.34,45,194

These observations suggest that the most meaningful practical comparison is not simply which route yields the highest latent heat or the highest thermal conductivity, but which route delivers adequate performance with the lowest additional processing burden for the intended application. Direct pyrolysis appears most attractive when the native biomass structure already provides a useful scaffold, hydrothermal carbonization is most relevant when wet feedstocks or precursor tunability are important, and activation-assisted carbonization is the strongest option when maximum pore development and PCM loading are prioritized.8,34,45,81 At the same time, the literature remains thin on end-of-life management, full-scale manufacturability, and long-term deployment evidence. Despite this limitation, mapping available cycle count data onto application-specific cycling frequencies provides a useful first-order framework for material selection. Building thermal regulation systems typically undergo approximately 300–365 thermal cycles per year, PV panel cooling systems undergo approximately 250–300 cycles per year, and seasonal thermal storage applications undergo fewer than 100 cycles per year. When even conservatively reported cycle stabilities of 200–1,000 cycles are mapped onto these frequencies, the implied material lifetimes range from less than one year in high-frequency applications with poorly performing composites, to over a decade in low-frequency applications with well-engineered supports. These implied lifetimes have profound implications for the economic case of each material in each application, and they highlight that a composite which appears expensive on a per-kilogram basis may in fact represent superior economic value when its full operational lifetime is considered.

This lifetime-application mapping reveals a set of practically actionable material selection principles. For building thermal regulation, where cycling frequency is high and material replacement is costly and disruptive, composites with demonstrated stability over a large number of cycles are clearly preferred regardless of their higher upfront cost. For seasonal thermal storage applications with fewer than 100 cycles per year, composites produced by simpler and less expensive routes may provide equivalent economic value at significantly lower cost. For PV panel cooling, where weight, thermal conductivity, and thermal response speed are critical alongside lifetime, hybrid composites incorporating EG or metallic nanoparticles in a bio-derived scaffold appear to offer a promising balance of performance and cost based on the current literature.107,111 Embedding material lifetime explicitly into cost-effectiveness analyses and reporting it alongside initial material cost, synthesis energy, and end-of-life options would represent a meaningful and distinctive contribution to the bio-based ss-PCM literature that goes beyond the performance-focused reporting that currently dominates the field.

Conclusions

This review examined the main synthesis routes used to prepare bio-based supporting materials for shape-stable PCM composites from waste-derived and biomass-derived precursors, with emphasis on direct carbonization, hydrothermal carbonization, and activation-enabled carbonization. These routes do not offer equivalent advantages. Direct carbonization remains the simplest and most scalable approach, and is often the most practical route for large-volume production of bio-based PCM supports because it requires fewer processing steps and lower chemical input. Its limitation is that the resulting biochars usually show lower surface area and less controllable pore structure than more engineered carbons. Hydrothermal carbonization is best regarded as a feed-flexible and chemistry-tuning route, particularly attractive for wet biomass, but its benefits are offset by pressure-reactor requirements, slurry handling, and the need for downstream upgrading before high-performance PCM supports can be obtained. Activation-enabled carbonization provides the highest degree of pore development and usually the best encapsulation performance, but this improvement is achieved at the expense of higher cost, lower yield, greater process complexity, and a larger environmental burden associated with activating agents, washing, and wastewater treatment. The overall conclusion is therefore that support selection should not be based on porosity alone, but on a balanced assessment of encapsulation efficiency, stability, scalability, and cost.

The review also showed that thermal limitations of bio-based support PCMs can be substantially reduced through hybridization. Graphene-family fillers, EG, metallic particles, and ceramic fillers each improve heat-transfer performance through different mechanisms, but the most successful systems are those in which the secondary filler is integrated into the support architecture to create a continuous thermal pathway without sacrificing PCM loading or leakage resistance. Among these strategies, graphene and EG emerge as the most promising overall. Graphene offers strong multifunctionality by improving interfacial heat transfer, photothermal response, and structural integrity at relatively low loading, whereas EG is the most practical route for scalable conductivity enhancement because it forms efficient graphitic pathways with simpler processing. Metallic fillers, especially silver, can generate large conductivity gains, but their cost and dispersion challenges limit broad deployment. Ceramic fillers such as boron nitride are especially valuable where thermal conductivity must be improved without losing electrical insulation. Across all hybrid routes, the literature consistently indicates that filler topology and interfacial design matter more than filler identity alone.

A structured synthesis of the cost-performance landscape across all material families reviewed, spanning carbonization routes, hybridization strategies, and bio-based feedstocks reveals a practically actionable hierarchy that represents one of the most distinctive contributions of this review. Direct carbonization of waste biomass combined with EG hybridization offers the best overall value for large-scale, cost-sensitive applications such as building thermal regulation and passive solar buffering, while graphene-based and ceramic-based hybridization systems serve higher-performance niches, and silver-based metallic hybridization, despite delivering the strongest conductivity gains, remains the most difficult to justify on cost-sustainability grounds for broad deployment. A critical thing to consider for upcoming work should shift from reporting only peak thermal metrics toward evaluating cost-normalized and application-normalized performance, since the practical advantage of biomass-derived supports depends on whether their sustainability and low feedstock cost are retained after carbonization, activation, and hybrid modification.

Future advances will depend on low-cost biomass feedstocks, scalable synthesis routes, reduced use of expensive nano-additives, and hybrid designs that deliver meaningful performance gains without excessive penalty in cost or manufacturability. Thus, the future of bio-based ss-PCMs lies not in maximizing any single material property, but in developing technically robust, economically viable, and scalable systems that combine stable encapsulation, improved heat transfer, and long-term durability for practical thermal energy storage applications.
