Utility of 18F-Florzolotau PET as a prognostic and monitoring biomarker in a memory clinic cohort
Authors: Jiaying Lu, Jie Wang, Huiwei Zhang, Jie Wu, Yunhao Yang, Min Wang, Xiaoxi Ma, Weiqi Bao, Zhenxu Xiao, Huamei Lin, Xiaowen Zhou, Zizhao Ju, Shumeng Yan, Fangyang Jiao, Xiaoniu Liang, Yang Liu, Li Zheng, Ming Li, Jingjie Ge, Ding Ding, Tzu-Chen Yen, Yihui Guan, Chuantao Zuo, Qianhua Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTau PET is increasingly recognized for its prognostic and disease-monitoring value in Alzheimer’s disease (AD); however, real-world evidence in heterogeneous memory clinic populations remains limited, especially for novel tau positron emission tomography (PET) with 18F-Florzolotau.MethodsWe prospectively enrolled 340 individuals (patients with cognitive complaints and community volunteers) undergoing baseline amyloid PET and tau PET with 18F-Florzolotau, with subsets for clinical follow-up (n = 260; median interval 1.45 [0.96–2.56] years) and repeat tau PET (n = 64; median interval 2.27 [1.47–2.88] years). The incremental prognostic role of tau PET beyond demographic and clinical characteristics was assessed using binary clinical outcomes and continuous cognitive declines. The monitoring utility of tau PET was evaluated through tau accumulation rates versus clinical outcomes, and cognition-tau correlations.ResultsCross-sectionally, tau standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) increased with cognitive impairment severity and correlated with domain-specific deficits. Longitudinally, baseline tau SUVRs provided incremental prognostic value, with higher neocortical SUVR as the sole independent predictor beyond demographic and clinical characteristics, particularly in AD spectrum (HR = 1.62, p = 0.018). Accelerated tau deposition was observed in motor regions including the precentral gyrus (β = 0.07 SUVR/year, p = 0.007) and supplementary motor area (β = 0.08 SUVR/year, p = 0.017) in those clinically progressive individuals than stable counterparts. Critically, these motor regions showed stronger cognition-tau correlations than canonical tau-vulnerable regions (medial temporal lobe and neocortex), establishing them as superior monitoring biomarkers.Conclusions18F-Florzolotau PET provides significant prognostic value and enables AD progression tracking. Motor cortices offered enhanced sensitivity for monitoring clinical deterioration in symptomatic stages, supporting their integration into stage-specific frameworks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13195-025-01938-w.

Background

The landscape of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) has transformed from symptom-based diagnosis towards a clinical-biological approach emphasizing early detection, prognosis and monitoring through biomarkers, with amyloid and tau biomarkers playing important roles [1, 2].

Whilst amyloid positivity can be detected decades before symptoms, its prognostic and monitoring utilities are limited as accumulation plateaus early. Conversely, tau pathology emerges nearer to the onset of cognitive decline and correlates dynamically with progression – positioning tau biomarkers as promising for predicting outcomes and tracking trajectories [3]. Reflecting these insights, the 2024 revision of the Alzheimer’s Association diagnostic and staging criteria [2] stratified AD biomarkers into two principal categories, i.e., early-changing Core 1 for diagnosis and later-changing Core 2 for prognosis/monitoring. Core 2 biomarkers include tau positron emission tomography (PET) and emerging biochemical markers, with only the former currently available for practice [4]. To date, evidence for the prognostic utility of tau PET has largely come from controlled research cohorts [5, 6] and does not reflect the diversity and complexity of memory clinics. A recent study has suggested that the combination of amyloid PET positivity and tau PET positivity (18F-Flortaucipir) defined by visual assessment was associated with a high risk of clinical progression in both preclinical and symptomatic stages of AD [7]. However, the prognostic potential of other tau PET ligands remains less understood, research on their utility for disease monitoring is even more limited.

We conducted a prospective real-world study within a tertiary memory clinic using the second-generation tau PET tracer 18F-Florzolotau (as known as 18F-PM-PBB3, 18F-APN-1607), which exhibits high affinity for tau deposits in both AD [8] and non-AD [9] tauopathies. We aimed to evaluate two key clinical utilities: (1) its incremental prognostic value beyond conventional demographic and clinical predictors, and (2) its utility for longitudinal disease monitoring.

Methods

Participants

This investigation was conducted as part of the Shanghai Memory Study (SMS) – a prospective, longitudinal cohort established at the memory clinic of Huashan Hospital. SMS methodology details are published elsewhere [10].

Participants comprised a prospectively enrolled cohort of patients with cognitive complaints and community volunteers recruited from the local population (May 2019-January 2025). Eligibility required tau PET with 18F-Florzolotau and T1-weighted MRI; amyloid PET was mandatory except for a subset of community volunteers who underwent plasma p-tau217 testing instead [2, 11] due to lower invasiveness.

All participants completed comprehensive face-to-face clinical interviews and a detailed neuropsychological assessment. This assessment included both global and domain-specific cognitive measures (see Supplementary Methods for details). To exclude other causes of cognitive decline, participants underwent laboratory testing (including thyroid function, syphilis serology, vitamin B12/folate levels, and related blood tests) and routine structural neuroimaging (MRI/CT). A panel of cognitive disorder specialists then synthesized all available neurological, psychiatric, neuropsychological, and imaging data to reach a consensus diagnosis. Participants were classified into one of the following categories: (1) Cognitively Unimpaired (CU): Defined by intact performance on neuropsychological testing, no significant cognitive complaints, and a Clinical Dementia Rating global score (CDR-GS) of 0. (2) Subjective Cognitive Decline (SCD): According to the 2014 research framework by Jessen et al., [12] SCD was diagnosed in CU participants with a self-reported perception of cognitive decline, where criteria for MCI or dementia were not met. (3) Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI): Diagnosed according to Petersen’s criteria [13], featuring: (a) cognitive complaints; (b) objective impairment in ≥ 1 cognitive domain; (c) largely preserved functional activities; and (d) absence of dementia. (4) AD Dementia: Diagnosed based on the National Institute on Aging-Alzheimer’s Association criteria (2011) [14] for probable AD dementia, supported by a positive amyloid PET scan. Key criteria included: (a) dementia; (b) impairment in ≥ 2 cognitive domains; (c) no disturbance of consciousness; (d) onset between 40 and 90 years old; (e) impaired activities of daily living and altered behavior; and (f) exclusion of other disorders. (5) Neurodegenerative disorders other than AD: Diagnosed according to established international criteria for specific syndromes (e.g., Gorno-Tempini et al. (2011) criteria for Progressive non-fluent aphasia and Semantic variant primary progressive aphasia [15], Rascovsky et al. (2011) criteria for Behavioral variant frontotemporal dementia [16], the NINDS-AIREN International Workshop criteria for Vascular dementia [17], the Armstrong criteria for Corticobasal syndrome [18], the fourth consensus report of the DLB Consortium for Dementia with Lewy Bodies [19]). Crucially, clinically diagnosed AD dementia cases with negative amyloid PET finding were reclassified to neurodegenerative disorders other than AD, while neurodegenerative disorders other than AD cases with positive amyloid PET finding were excluded to minimize co-pathology confounding. 340 participants were enrolled at baseline (Fig. 1).

Biological profiling

Amyloid status was determined dichotomously (A+/-) by visual assessment via PET (18F-Florbetapir (n = 189), 18F-Florbetaben (n = 121), or 11C-Pittsburgh compound B (n = 7)) or plasma p-tau217 (n = 23, Single Molecular Immunity Detection kits, cutoff: 4.2 pg/mL) [11].

Tau pathology was quantified continuously via 18F-Florzolotau PET standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs, n = 340) in two AD-relevant regions. These regions of interest (ROIs) were selected to align with the recent biological staging scheme [2] where pathology in the medial temporal lobe (MTL) represents an early stage, and spread to neocortical (NEO) regions signifies disease progression. The NEO composite was defined as a meta-ROI representing Braak stages V and VI [8, 20], encompassing widespread association cortices including frontal, parietal, occipital and temporal lobes (for the full list of regions, see Supplementary Table 1) [21, 22]. Tau positivity (T+) was defined using validated in-house thresholds (MTL: 1.33; NEO: 1.15) [8], with positivity in either region considered sufficient.

Detailed protocols for image acquisition and preprocessing are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Clinical follow-up and endpoint definition

The four participants with a baseline CDR-GS of 3 were excluded because their primary outcome was not evaluable (see below). 260 participants (76.5%) met eligibility for longitudinal clinical analysis by completing ≥ 1 follow-up visit (total follow-up duration range: 0.58–5.93 years through April 2025). Among these, 78 attended two follow-up visits, 12 completed three, and two participants completed four or five follow-up evaluations. The median [interquartile ranges (IQRs)] total follow-up duration from baseline to last visit was 1.45 [0.96–2.56] years.

The primary outcome was clinical progression, operationalized as increase in the CDR-GS. Subjects not meeting these criteria were classified as clinically stable. As a secondary endpoint, the annualized rate of cognitive decline was derived by calculating the differential between final follow-up and baseline cognitive assessment scores – subsequently normalized by the duration of the follow-up period in years.

Longitudinal 18F-Florzolotau PET

89 participants (26.2%) met eligibility for longitudinal imaging analysis by completing ≥ 1 18F-Florzolotau PET follow-up scan. Crucially, longitudinal tau-PET analyses were exclusively restricted to the AD spectrum sub-cohort (n = 64) due to fundamentally distinct tau trajectories in non-AD individuals. Among these, the median [IQRs] total follow-up duration from baseline to last visit was 2.27 [1.47–2.88] years, with a range of 0.89–5.33 years. There were 25 participants undergoing two or more repeat scans. The annualized rate of tau accumulation was calculated for MTL and NEO regions, employing the same methodology as that used to assess annualized cognitive decline.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R and Statistical Parametric Mapping. Data are presented as medians [IQRs] for continuous variables and counts (%) for categorical variables. Group comparisons employed Mann-Whitney U or chi-square tests.

Cross-sectional associations between baseline 18F-Florzolotau SUVR and disease severity were assessed using generalized linear models (comparing SUVR across CDR-GS categories) and partial correlation analyses (with cognitive scores), adjusting for age, sex, and education.

For prognostic utility, time-to-clinical progression was visualized with Kaplan-Meier curves stratified by amyloid/tau status. Predictors of progression were identified using univariate Cox models (including age, sex, education, apolipoprotein E [APOE] ε4 status, Montreal Cognitive Assessment [MOCA]); significant demographic/clinical variables were then incorporated into adjusted Cox models to test the independent value of each biomarker. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses with DeLong tests evaluated incremental predictive value over the base demographic/clinical model. Longitudinal cognitive decline was analyzed using linear mixed-effects models. Voxel- and ROI-wise regression models were constructed to assess the predictive value of baseline 18F-Florzolotau PET SUVR for the annualized rate of cognitive decline.

In the subset with longitudinal tau PET, we used linear mixed-effects models to assess tau accumulation over time at the ROI level. The models included participant-specific random intercepts and the following fixed effects: tau PET follow-up interval, clinical group (progressive or stable), and the group × interval interaction term, with adjustments for baseline age, sex, and years of education. The difference in tau accumulation rates between groups was inferred from the significance of the group × interval interaction term. For the voxel-wise analysis, whole-brain, two-sample t-tests were performed to compare annualized SUVR change maps between the clinically progressive and stable groups. These models were also adjusted for follow-up interval, baseline age, sex, and years of education. Furthermore, linear regression models were employed to assess the associations between annualized tau change (at the ROI level) and cognitive decline. These models were adjusted for baseline age, sex, years of education, and tau PET follow-up interval.

A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered significant without multiplicity correction due to the exploratory nature. For voxel-wise analyses, statistical significance was defined as a voxel-wise p < 0.01 (uncorrected) and a cluster threshold of p < 0.05 (uncorrected). More details are available in Supplementary Methods.

Prognostic utility of baseline 18F-Florzolotau PET

Predict clinical progression

Kaplan-Meier analysis revealed significant differences in time-to-progression across binary AT groups in the entire cohort (log-rank p < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Participants with A + T + status exhibited the shortest median time-to-progression of 2.71 years (95% CI 2.29–2.97) and a 2.67-fold higher progression risk compared to A-T- individuals (95% CI 1.58–4.53, p < 0.001; Supplementary Table 4).

Subsequent univariate screening identified older age (HR = 1.03 per year, 95% CI 1.01–1.06, p = 0.001) and lower MOCA score (HR = 0.94 per point, 95% CI 0.92–0.96, p < 0.001) at baseline as significant predictors of clinical progression in the entire cohort (the top panel of Fig. 3B), whereas only older age was significant in the AD spectrum sub-cohort (HR = 1.03 per year, 95% CI 1.01–1.06, p = 0.015; the top panel of Fig. 3D).

In following separate Cox models adjusted for these factors, higher neocortical NEO tau burden showed a trend toward predicting progression in the entire cohort (HR = 1.36 per SUVR unit, 95% CI 0.98–1.87, p = 0.062; the top panel of Fig. 3C) and was a significant independent predictor in the AD spectrum sub-cohort (HR = 1.62 per SUVR unit, 95% CI 1.09–2.42, p = 0.018; the top panel of Fig. 3E). Binary T + status and MTL SUVR did not independently predict progression, although the latter showed a tendency in AD spectrum sub-cohort (HR = 1.43 per SUVR unit, 95% CI 0.95–2.15, p = 0.083).

ROC analysis confirmed that continuous NEO SUVR significantly improved prognostic performance beyond clinical/demographic models in both the entire cohort and the AD spectrum sub-cohort (the bottom panels of Fig. 3C and E), whereas binary T + status did not.

Predict cognitive trajectories

The 3.3.1 analyses indicated that the incremental prognostic value of 18F-Florzolotau PET was more remarkable in the AD spectrum. Therefore, we then looked at its role in predicting cognitive trajectories in the AD spectrum. Detailed cognitive trajectories are presented in Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 4A. Baseline tau SUVRs were predictive of annualized rates of cognitive decline, both globally and within language and attention domains (Fig. 4B-C, Supplementary Fig. 4B-C, Supplementary Table 5).

Longitudinal 18F-Florzolotau PET monitoring in the AD spectrum

Longitudinal cognitive and Tau trajectories

Representative cases (Fig. 5) illustrate parallel tau-clinical progression in AD spectrum participants versus stable trajectories in non-AD individuals throughout the observation period. Subsequent analyses focused exclusively on the AD sub-cohort (n = 64), including 32 clinically progressive and 32 clinically stable individuals at follow-up (Supplementary Table 7, Supplementary Fig. 7). The groups were well-matched on demographics, APOE ε4 status, Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR-SoB), and Functional Assessment Questionnaire score at baseline. The clinically progressive group showed more severe global cognitive deficits as reflected by MOCA score and specific impairments in memory, executive, and language domains, but not in visuospatial or attention.

Disease monitoring

Annual tau accumulation rates correlated with cognitive decline (measured by CDR-SoB). A trend of correlation was observed in canonical tau-vulnerable region, the MTL (β = 2.05 [–0.08 to 4.18], p = 0.059). Notably, the stronger and more robust correlations emerged in the right precentral gyrus (β = 4.33 [0.87 to 7.79], p = 0.015) and the right supplementary motor area (β = 3.37 [0.92 to 5.83], p = 0.008) – regions that were identified in our prior analysis as exhibiting significantly accelerated tau accumulation in the clinical progressive group compared to the stable group (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 9).

Discussion

Our memory-clinic based study revealed 18F-Florzolotau PET could be clinically useful as a prognostic and monitoring biomarker. Here, we found that baseline 18F-Florzolotau SUVR in NEO was an independent prognostic predictor of clinical progression and cognitive trajectories over time beyond demographic/clinical variables. Additionally, we demonstrated that the extent of tau accumulation at follow-up – as identified by repeat 18F-Florzolotau PET scans – closely mirrored the clinical disease course. Interestingly, the right precentral gyrus and supplementary motor area exhibited not only accelerated tau accumulation compared to canonical tau-vulnerable regions (MTL/NEO) but also showed stronger correlations with cognitive decline in our memory clinic AD spectrum, highlighting tau accumulation in these motor areas as particularly sensitive monitoring biomarkers for tracking disease progression in symptomatic AD patients at relatively later stages.

In our unselected patients drawn from a tertiary memory clinic, baseline 18F-Florzolotau SUVR values demonstrated significant cross-sectional associations with multiple cognitive domains as well as global cognition measures. More importantly, our results showed that baseline 18F-Florzolotau PET findings refined the prognostic stratification of patients presenting with cognitive complaints, both in the entire cohort and within the AD spectrum sub-cohort. These findings are consistent with previous studies demonstrating the incremental prognostic value of 18F-RO948 and 18F-Flortaucipir PET imaging in the transition from MCI to dementia [23], and further extend this evidence to a more heterogeneous, real-world memory clinic population. Although the difference was subtle, the NEO region was associated with a slightly better prognosis than the MTL – a finding consistent with the intermediate and advanced disease stages commonly observed in our memory clinic outpatients. It’s worth noting that baseline MOCA score emerged as a promising predictor for clinical progression in the entire cohort but not in the AD spectrum sub-cohort. On the contrary, baseline tau burden in NEO provided better prognostic performance in the AD spectrum sub-cohort than in the entire cohort, underscoring the key role of tau PET in AD management. In addition to binary clinical outcomes, continuous tau SUVRs also predicted trajectories of cognitive decline – both globally and across domain-specific measures – throughout the cohort. This finding is consistent with previous research on the prognostic value of baseline tau PET imaging for cognitive decline, which has been documented for several commonly used tracers – including 18F-Flortaucipir [20, 24–27], 18F-RO948 [20], 18F-GTP1 [28], 18F-MK6240 [29]. Notably, the relationship between baseline tau PET spatial patterns and domain-specific cognitive decline remains incompletely understood. While some studies have reported associations between regional tau deposition and subsequent decline in distinct cognitive domains [25, 27], recent evidence from atypical AD cases suggests a broad spatial overlap between tau-related cognitive decline and canonical default mode network regions, sometimes extending beyond the initial distribution of tau pathology [26]. In our cohort, where most patients already exhibited diffuse tau pathology, domain-specific spatial associations were modest.

Longitudinal 18F-Florzolotau PET results revealed a diffuse, spatiotemporal pattern of tau accumulation, consistent with previous studies showing that tau pathology spreads broadly as AD progresses [30–32]. Prior multicenter research using 18F-Flortaucipir PET identified distinct tau progression profiles primarily in CU and MCI populations [33]. Our findings extend these observations to unselected memory clinic patients predominantly comprising individuals along the clinical spectrum of AD, using the second-generation tau PET imaging. An advantageous feature of 18F-Florzolotau is its high affinity for tau aggregates coupled with low off-target binding, which contributes to a high signal-to-noise ratio and favorable spatial resolution [34]. This high-resolution capability allows for the clear visualization of individual-level SUVR distribution patterns, making it particularly valuable for detailing the topographic spread of tau pathology at various disease stages and may enhance the utility of tau PET for in-vivo staging and monitoring disease progression in individual patients. By integrating 18F-Florzolotau PET with clinical follow-up data, we found that rates of tau accumulation closely mirrored clinical progression, reaffirming the strong temporal association between tau pathology and clinical decline in the AD spectrum [3]. Additionally, our results build on earlier reports with 18F-Flortaucipir [35] and 18F-MK6240 [31], providing preliminary evidence that 18F-Florzolotau can effectively track tau dynamics in parallel with clinical trajectories. Notably, significant correlations were observed between tau accumulation and longitudinal CDR-SoB scores, but not with MOCA. This dissociation likely reflects the greater sensitivity of the CDR-SoB to functional decline in daily activities, as it is a composite measure that incorporates informant reports. In contrast, the MOCA, as a global cognitive screen, may be less sensitive to change in this symptomatic cohort due to its lack of a functional component and potential for floor effects. Despite the promise of 18F-Florzolotau PET as an objective biomarker for disease monitoring, important complexities remain. For instance, the mismatches between tau pathology and clinical severity stages has previously been reported cross-sectionally [8], underscoring the need to further investigate individual heterogeneity and potential asynchrony. Although the modest sample size of our longitudinal 18F-Florzolotau PET subset limited detailed mechanistic analysis, ongoing studies with larger cohorts aim to address this gap and enable more personalized disease monitoring.

While tau deposition occurs gradually throughout the brain in AD [36–38], our clinically progressive patients exhibited significantly accelerated tau accumulation, particularly in the right precentral gyrus and right supplementary motor area – which are typically spared until late-stage AD [39]. Prior longitudinal findings using 18F-MK6240 tau PET imaging have shown that tau accumulation rates may vary by brain region and disease stage, plateauing at high levels [40]. Notably, large neocortical areas, rather than the MTL or inferior temporal cortices, have emerged as suitable markers for tracking tau progression during symptomatic AD stages [40]. These findings are in keeping with our observation of relatively limited differences in 18F-Florzolotau SUVR increases within the MTL between clinically progressive and stable patients. However, we cannot rule out that the limited differences in neocortical regions may reflect a sampling bias, as our cohort predominantly included patients at advanced disease stages. Notably, we identified accelerated tau accumulation in the right precentral gyrus and right supplementary motor area of clinically progressive patients compared to stable counterparts. This regional specificity parallels previous 18F-Flortaucipir research documenting rapidly progressive tau deposition in the frontal lobe with disease progression [41]. These findings support emerging evidence that advanced AD tau pathology extends beyond traditional limbic and association cortices to involve primary motor regions [30, 42]. Critically, our data extend this paradigm by demonstrating that motor regions also showed superior sensitivity as monitoring biomarkers in the current AD patients at our memory clinic. Our findings could be primarily driven by disease-stage–dependent spread of tau pathology. With tau pathology breaching certain thresholds in canonical cortices, its subsequent spread into motor areas may mark a final common pathway for steep clinical decline. This could be particularly evident if the rate of accumulation in canonical regions begins to plateau in later stages, while tau continues to proliferate aggressively in newly vulnerable areas like the precentral gyrus and supplementary motor area [40]. Furthermore, the involvement of tau pathology in motor area could disrupt large-scale brain networks for integrating motor and cognitive functions, thereby accelerating decline. To assess the generalizability of our findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis in a sub-cohort at earlier disease stages (CDR-GS = 0.5 or 1.0). We found that annualized tau accumulation in both canonical (MTL, NEO) and motor regions significantly correlated with clinical decline (Supplementary Table 10). While this reinforces our main conclusion, it also confirms a complementary role for canonical regions at earlier stages. Given the spectrum of disease severity in our cohort, further investigation in dedicated preclinical and prodromal cohorts is warranted to fully establish the stage-specific monitoring role of 18F-Florzolotau PET. Together, the regional heterogeneity observed in our study emphasizes the need for stage-specific tau PET imaging biomarker selection, with MTL, NEO, and motor regions offering distinct prognostic and monitoring value at different stages of AD.

Among the 64 patients with repeated tau PET scans, 9 were with atypical presentations, including 6 in the clinically progressive group (4 Primary Progressive Aphasia, and 2 Posterior Cortical Atrophy) and 3 in the clinically stable group (2 corticobasal syndrome and 1 Posterior Cortical Atrophy). The inclusion of Primary Progressive Aphasia cases likely contributed to the pronounced language impairment observed in the progressive group. However, the potential influence of atypical AD phenotypes in tau PET progression patterns remained unexplored due to limited cases. Future studies in larger, phenotypically diverse cohorts are needed to clarify how specific clinical features map onto distinct tau progression patterns.

A distinctive strength of our study lies in its examination of a genuine memory clinic population, addressing a significant gap in AD research that has typically focused on either CU individuals or those with early-stage dementia in controlled research settings. We believe that our investigation benefits from a broader clinical spectrum, with the notable methodological advantage of incorporating serial tau PET imaging in one-third of participants – an approach rarely implemented in previous research. However, our findings should be interpreted in the context of several limitations. First, the single-center design and use of a single tau PET radiotracer highlight the need for multicenter validation using diverse tau PET ligands to ensure broader generalizability. Second, while tau pathology spreads heterogeneously in both spatial extent and density [32], the traditional ROI-based approach employed in our study may inadequately capture the complexity of cross-regional tau propagation. Third, the follow-up intervals were not strictly standardized, and only a subset of participants underwent repeated longitudinal assessments. These factors limited our ability to accurately model nonlinear patterns of disease progression.

Conclusions

Our study contributes to bridging tau PET research and clinical practice by mapping longitudinal clinical and tau trajectories within a memory clinic cohort. Our findings highlight the relevance of stage-specific biomarker frameworks tailored to clinical settings and may help inform decision-making for personalized monitoring and management across the AD continuum.
