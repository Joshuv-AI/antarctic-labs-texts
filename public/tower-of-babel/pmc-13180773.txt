Recent advances in pathway engineering for the biosynthesis of 1,4-butanediol
Authors: Hongwei Guo, Weigao Wang, Jun Zhao, Hongwen Chen, Guo Chen, Xiaohui Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract1,4-Butanediol (1,4-BDO) is a short-chain diol with extensive applications in various industries. Traditional petroleum-based synthesis methods face challenges such as harsh reaction conditions and environmental pollution. Recent advances in bio-based 1,4-BDO synthesis harness synthetic biology tools to engineer the microbial pathways for sustainable production. Three principal biosynthetic routes, the CoA-dependent route, the lignocellulose-derived sugar-catabolizing route, and the amino acid-catabolizing route, have been rationally optimized using enzyme engineering, and dynamic metabolic regulation strategies. Besides these in vivo strategies, whole-cell transformation and enzyme cascades were engineered in vitro. Despite these advances, industrial scalability remains constrained by unresolved intracellular catabolism, host tolerance limitations, and suboptimal economic feasibility. Future priorities involve elucidating 1,4-BDO degradation mechanisms via multi-omics, designing high-specificity dehydrogenases, and developing precision genetic tools for dynamic flux control. This review critically assesses the pathway engineering breakthroughs and the translational challenges for sustainable 1,4-BDO production.Key points• Three sets of novel pathway designs and engineering strategies were developed for de novo 1,4-BDO production in vivo.• Two biotransformation systems were established for 1,4-BDO production via in vitro enzyme cascades and in vivo whole-cell biocatalysis.• High-efficiency bioproduction of 1,4-BDO still faces multiple core challenges and technical bottlenecks.

Introduction

1,4-Butanediol (1,4-BDO), one of the short-chain diols, is widely utilized in the fields of organic solvents, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, and polymers (Liu et al. 2024b). 1,4-BDO and other diols are employed as platform molecules to synthesize fibers such as polyethylene terephthalate, polytrimethylene terephthalate, and polybutylene terephthalate (PBT), respectively. Compared to other diols, 1,4-BDO is the precursor to PBT which could further be converted to rubber, nylon, plastics, and resins and be nearly utilized in all the industrial fields (Cen et al. 2022). It is estimated that the worldwide market for 1,4-BDO will reach 13.4 billion US dollars by 2030 (Kumar et al. 2024). Consequently, its production and application have garnered significant commercial interest.

Traditionally, the Reppe route has been employed worldwide to synthesize 1,4-BDO from acetylene and formaldehyde. In addition to the Reppe route, processes such as the Lyondell, the butadiene acetoxylation, the Huntsman, and the Davy McKee processes, which utilize various petroleum-derived raw materials, are also employed for the 1,4-BDO synthesis (Silva et al. 2020). However, harsh reaction conditions, environmental pollution, volatile costs of fossil fuels, and safety and toxicity issues remain a continuing concern within these fossil-based synthesis routes (Zhu et al. 2022). Although biomass-based renewable substrates and novel catalysts have been explored and incorporated into the 1,4-BDO synthesis route, the hydroxylation process involved was still energy-intensive under harsh conditions (Flores Fernández et al. 2025; Sun et al. 2025). This has spurred intensive research into the development of sustainable, renewable biosynthetic methods for 1,4-BDO production.

Recently, considerable literature has emerged on engineering microbial factories to develop sustainable and commercially viable processes for 1,4-BDO synthesis using renewable feedstocks. As no natural microbial strains capable of 1,4-BDO synthesis have been identified, engineering microbial hosts for de novo 1,4-BDO synthesis are of great academic challenge and interest. The first heterologous 1,4-BDO synthesis route has been established in the cells of Escherichia coli (Yim et al. 2011), along with advances in synthetic biologic tools and methodologies; serval other host strains, like Corynebacterium glutamicum, Clostridia spp., and Yarrowia lipolytica, also have been engineered to synthesize 1,4-BDO (Guo et al. 2025; Kim et al. 2025; Zha et al. 2024). Laboratory-scale production of 1,4-BDO has been achieved by applying some engineered strains, but intensive efforts are still needed to improve its economic feasibility. This study systematically reviews the recent advances in bio-based 1,4-BDO routes to provide novel insights for developing enhanced 1,4-BDO synthesis methods.

Current advance in engineering metabolic pathways for 1,4-BDO synthesis

Building on recent advances in synthetic biology and metabolic engineering, a series of novel de novo biosynthetic pathways have been developed in model microbial strains, and cell-free enzyme cascades have also been established for 1,4-BDO production (Table 1). Although diverse pathways were designed and explored for 1,4-BDO synthesis previously, based on the substrate utilized, they could be classified into three principal routes: CoA-dependent pathway, lignocellulose-derived sugar-catabolizing pathway, and amino acid-catabolizing pathway. Bio-based 1,4-butanediol synthesis in engineered microbial strains

CoA-dependent pathways

As a non-natural diol, one of the major challenges is to design and verify artificial routes from the known enzyme pools to synthesize 1,4-BDO. An in-house SimPheny™ Biopathway Predictor, a pathway-identification algorithm that integrates all functional group transformations based on the known enzyme catalysis, was employed to predict multiple biosynthetic pathways for 1,4-BDO production from common metabolic intermediates (Yim et al. 2011). As a result, 10,000 possible pathways, each containing 4 to 6 steps, were obtained and subsequently sorted and ranked. Based on criteria such as theoretical yield, pathway length, number of non-natural steps, and thermodynamic feasibility, two artificial routes were supposed as the most potential pathways for synthesizing 1,4-BDO from succinate and α-ketoglutarate (α-KG), respectively. To verify the 1,4-BDO synthesizing capabilities of these proposed pathways, a heterologous pathway comprising native succinyl-CoA (Suc-CoA) synthetase (EcSucCD), succinyl semialdehyde (SSA) dehydrogenase (PgSucD), 4-hydroxybutyrate (4HB) dehydrogenase (Pg4Hbd), and 4-hydroxybutyryl-CoA (4HB-CoA) transferase (PgCat2) from Porphyromonas gingivalis, 4HB-CoA reductase (CaAdhE2), and alcohol dehydrogenase (CaAdhE2) from Clostridium acetobutylicum was introduced into E. coli. The accumulation of TCA cycle intermediates during 1,4-BDO synthesis reduced its efficiency and impaired cell growth. The Optknock algorithm predicted that deleting endogenous alcohol dehydrogenase (EcAdhE), pyruvate formate lyase (EcPflB), lactate dehydrogenase (EcLdh), and malate dehydrogenase (EcMdh) would reduce byproducts (Yim et al. 2011). However, the triple deletion of EcAdhE, EcPlf, and EcLdh impaired anaerobic growth due to low intracellular NADH level and NADH sensitivity of the pyruvate dehydrogenase E3 subunit (EcLpdA). Replacing the native EcLpdA with the non-NADH-sensitive KpLpdAD354K mutant from Klebsiella pneumonia restored the anaerobic growth of the triple deletion strain. Deleting the transcriptional repressor arcA and overexpression of R163L mutant of the citrate synthase (EcGltAR163L, alleviating the feedback suppression of NADH) and ketoglutarate decarboxylase (EcSucA) enhanced metabolic flux of the heterologous 1,4-BDO synthesis. Under microaerobic condition, the obtained ECKh-422 strain exhibited improved growth and exhibited a 20-fold higher 1,4-BDO titer than the control strain. Consequently, an engineered strain with deletions of adhE, pfl, ldh, mdh, and arcA, and overexpression of KpLpdAD354K and EcGltAR163L, could synthesize over fourfold more 1,4-BDO than the control strain (Hwang et al. 2014).

The last two catalytic steps, both by mediated CaAdhE2, were determined as the rate-limiting steps in a 13C-based metabolic flux analysis (Barton et al. 2015). Owing to relatively large size of CaAdhE2 and complexity of engineering its two catalytic domains, Hwang et al. substituted CaAdhE2 with butyraldehyde dehydrogenase (CsBld) and butanol dehydrogenase (CsBdh) from Clostridium saccharoperbutylacetonicum (Hwang et al. 2014). A combined strategy involving random mutagenesis and colorimetric high-throughput screening was implemented to enhance CsBld catalysis, and the CsBldL273T mutant displayed fourfold higher activity than the wild-type CsBld. According to the 3D protein model generated from two most similar aldehyde dehydrogenases, the L273 residue was predicted to participate in NAD(P)H binding. The higher activity of the L273I and L273T mutants was attributed to their increased NAD(P)H binding affinity. Finally, the engineered strain harboring Suc-CoA transferase (PgCat1), PgSucD, Pg4Hbd, PgCat2, CsBldL273T, and CsBdh could accumulate 660 mg·L−1 1,4-BDO, which was 3.6-fold higher than that of the control strain.

The decrease in 1,4-BDO titer during the late fermentation phase prompted a diagnostic experiment, in which exogenous addition of γ-butyrolactone revealed that both Cat2 and AdhE2 were low-expressed and rate-limiting (Burgard et al. 2016). To bypass the catalysis of these enzymes, a short-cut catalysis mediated by the carboxylic acid reductase MAB4714c from Mycobacterium abscessus, converting 4HB to 4HBA, was used to replace Cat2 and Ald (Liu et al. 2024a). As the binding phoshopantetheine group could trigger the Car conformation change from inactive to active, and it was also evidenced that co-expressing phosphopantetheinyl transferase (Sfp) with Car could enhance the enzyme activity (Gahloth et al. 2017; Khusnutdinova et al. 2017), therefore, the modified strain BDO-N4, which harbors MAB4714c along with Sfp from Bacillus subtilis (BsSfp), NAD+ kinase (EcNadk), and formate dehydrogenase (EcFdh), was engineered and constructed. The BDO-N4 strain could accumulate 760 mg·L−1 1,4-BDO, which was comparable to that from the control strain. In addition, the engineered strain showed an increase in biomass alongside enhancement of 1,4-BDO titer, suggesting the high intracellular NADPH supply facilitated diol synthesis.

Glycolate and ethylene glycol (EG), the products of PET digestion by PETase, were used as substrates for 1,4-BDO synthesis. To utilize glycolate, a two-step enzyme catalyzed process mediated by CoA transferase and thiolase was designed (Fig. 1B). It was demonstrated that Pct540 (CoA transferase from Clostridium propionicum) and CnBktB (thiolase from Cupriavidus necator) were the most promising enzymes. The corresponding recombinant, in which CaHbd, and CaCrt (4-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase and crotonase from Clostridium acetobutylicum), TdTer (hydroxylamine reductase from Treponema denticola), Bld, and Bdh were co-expressed, produced 34.9 mg·L−1 1,4-BDO from 2 g·L−1 glycolate. The accumulation of butyrate indicated that CnBktB had inadequate affinity for the glycolyl-CoA. A saturation site-mutagenesis strategy was applied to the V57, L89, M158, and I352 residues of CnBktB. A Suc-CoA auxotrophic strain, in which both endogenous sucA and aceA (isocitrate lyase) were knockout, was constructed and utilized to screen the mutants. Compared to the control strain, the L89S mutant exhibited an enhanced growth rate and higher biomass. In vivo enzyme activity assays evidenced that L89S mutant had higher affinity for glycolyl-CoA and higher catalytic activity. As a result, 1,4-BDO synthesis was improved to 177.6 mg·L−1 (Chen et al. 2025).

Despite the significant improvements in 1,4-BDO synthesis being achievable via these genomic engineering efforts, such iterative modifications proved to be highly time-consuming and labor-intensive. The CRISPR-Cas9 system was employed to introduce a point mutation (R163L) in EcGltA and replace native LpdA with KpLpdAD354K in situ. Also, native succinate semialdehyde dehydrogenase (EcSad) could be deleted by introducing a large DNA fragment containing cat2, bld, and bdh expression cassettes. These genomic modifications were the same as the previous obtained ECKh-422 strain (Yim et al. 2011), and the resultant WΔ5#4 strain accumulated a 1,4-BDO titer comparable to that of the ECKh-422 strain. Using the CRISPRi-dCas9 system, expression of NADP+-dependent succinate semialdehyde dehydrogenase (GadD) and acyl-CoA thioesterase (YbgC and TesB) was repressed by 85–87% at RNA level. These genes inhibition doubled 1,4-BDO titer to 1.8 g·L−1, attributed to reduced levels of succinate and γ-butyrolactone (Wu et al. 2017).

Compared to those succinate-based routes, the α-KG-based route was considered to be thermodynamically more favorable (Yim et al. 2011). A four-enzymes artificial route comprising Pg4Hbd, PgCat2, CsBldL273T, and CsBdh was engineered in an α-KG-overflowing strain of Y. lipolytica (Guo et al. 2025). By dividing these genes into two modules and optimizing the genomic copies of these modules, it was demonstrated that the genomic copy numbers of the 4hbd and cat2 are major determinants of 1,4-BDO titer when the genomic copies bld and bdh exceed 10. It was further observed that the 1,4-BDO synthesis was cell growth-dependent; the high genomic copies of 4hbd and cat2 impaired the growth of engineered yeast. To rebalance the expression of heterologous enzymes and cell growth, a dynamic regulation strategy employing a thiamine-dependent promoter to drive the expression of the CsBld and CsBdh was implemented (Fig. 1C). A combination of strategies, including thiamine dynamic regulation, modular optimization, NADPH enhancement via overexpression of glycerol dehydrogenase (YlGcy1P), deletion of succinate semialdehyde dehydrogenase (Uga2), and overexpression of ketoglutarate decarboxylase from C. glutamicum (CgKgd), was implemented, resulting in 1,4-BDO production of 6217.1 mg·L−1.

Lignocellulose-derived sugars-catabolizing routes

Besides glucose and glycerol, lignocellulosic feedstocks have been proven as economical and sustainable substrates for synthesizing bulk and platform chemicals (Zhao et al. 2020). Although several lignocellulose-utilizing pathways have been reported in many microbial strains, the Weimberg pathway is the most commonly engineered. The key intermediates in these two pathways, 2-keto-3-deoxy-D-xylonate (KDX) and 2-keto-3-L-arabinonate (KDA), which are obtained from D-xylose (D-xyl) and L-arabinose (L-ara) respectively, can be easily converted into 2,5-dioxopentanoate (DOP, also known as KGSA). Upon obtaining DOP, the carbon metabolism could channel into synthesis of α-KG via α-ketoglutarate semialdehyde dehydrogenase (KGSADH) (Francois et al. 2020). Once α-KG obtained, it can be used for synthesizing 1,4-BDO.

To screen and identify the potential genes and gene clusters for converting lignocellulose-derived sugars to α-KG, a growth-dependent α-KG auxotrophic screening platform was constructed and utilized by deleting the endogenous isocitrate dehydrogenase (Icd). Also, for inhibiting endogenous D-xyl, L-ara, and D-galacturonate (D-gla) consumption respectively, the corresponding native xylose isomerase (XylA), 2-dehydro-3-deoxy-D-pentonate aldolase (YjhH and YagE), L-arabinose dehydrogenase (AraA), uronate dehydrogenase (UxaA), and 2-dehydro-3-deoxyglucarate aldolase (GarL) were disrupted, respectively. Based on α-KG replenishment from these lignocellulose-derived sugars in E. coli, a pathway with 100% theoretical molar yield for 1,4-BDO producing was hypothesized, in which DOP was sequentially converted to 1,4-BDO by the combined action of 2-ketoacid decarboxylase (KDC) and alcohol dehydrogenase (Adh). To verify the hypothesis, several KDC and Adh combinations were screened. The best combination was found to be LlKivD (2-ketoacid decarboxylase from Lactococcus lactis) and EcYqhD (a promiscuous alcohol dehydrogenase from E. coli), generating 1.8 g·L−1 1,4-BDO and only 25% theoretical molar yield. However, the 1,4-BDO synthesis was accompanied by accumulation of 3.6 g·L−1 1,2,4-butanetriol (1,2,4-BTO), which was attributed to the low selectivity of the LlKivD and synthesized the 3,4-DHB (3,4-dihydroxybutanal) and 1,2,4-BTO concomitantly (Fig. 3D–E in orange). A set of site-directed mutagenesis experiments targeting the binding pocket was conducted to improve the selectivity of LlKivD for DOP. The V461I mutant doubled the 1,4-BDO titer and reduced byproduct 1,2,4-BTO to 0.9 g·L−1. It was reasoned that the methyl group of I461 residue shrunk the binding pocket of the enzyme and 63% theoretical molar yield was achieved. Using a mixture of glucose and D-xyl, 9.21 g·L−1 1,4-BDO could be synthesized by an engineered E. coli strain harboring xylBCDX operon, LlKivDV461I, and EcYqhD within 36 h in a 1.3-L bioreactor (Tai et al. 2016).

Inspired by glycerol dehydration to 1,3-propanediol, diol dehydratase was engineered to convert the byproduct 1,2,4-BTO to 1,4-BDO to avoid 1,2,4-BTO accumulation (Fig. 3E) (Jiang et al. 2016). Most native diol dehydratases would exhibit low activity toward the non-native triol, which was a major challenge for enzyme engineering. The native diol dehydratase from Klebsiella oxytoca (KoMdlC), engineered by fusing its three subunits (ppdA-C-B) (Jain et al. 2015; Jain and Yan 2011), displayed a 32-fold lower activity toward 1,2,4-BTO than toward 1,2-propanediol (Wang et al. 2017). Co-expression of EcYqhD with ppdA-C-B in vivo failed to detect 4HBA or 1,4-BDO when 1,2,4-BTO was supplemented. The number of hydroxyl groups in the substrate was speculated to impair the catalysis. However, the ppdA-C-BQ336A mutant produced trace amount of 1,4-BDO from 1,2,4-BTO, the S301A/Q336A double mutant produced 1.6-fold more 1,4-BDO. These observations and in silico investigation suggested that enlarging the catalytic pocket of the dehydratase could alleviate substrate inhibition, consistent with in vitro enzyme kinetic parameter characterization (Wang et al. 2017).

Based on prior studies of dehydratase-substrate binding interactions, the distance of O1 of 1,2,4-BTO to the Ca2+ ion, mediated by catalytic pocket residues, plays crucial roles in enzyme activity (Kamachi et al. 2011). The V300 residue of the ppdA-C-B was speculated to influence substrate positioning and the distance of O1 of 1,2,4-BTO to the Ca2+ ion. The distance of V300M was calculated to be 2.6 Å, representing only 36.6% of the distance in the native ppdA-C-B. The S301A/Q336A/V300M triple variant exhibited fivefold enhanced catalytic efficiency compared to the native enzyme. The 1,4-BDO concentration reached to 220 mg·L−1 in a whole-cell biotransformation experiment. A complete 1,2,4-BTO synthesis pathway, consisting of CcXylBCD, LlKivD, and EcYqhD, was introduced into E. coli strain with deletion of xylA, yjhH, and yagE, and subsequent expression the ppdA-C-BS301A/Q336A/V300M mutant. This modified strain could synthesize 209 mg·L−1 1,4-BDO de novo (Wang et al. 2017).

Parallel to engineering of ppdA-C-B, the alcohol dehydratase from Klebsiella pneumoniae (KpGDH) was also engineered to convert polyols to diols. Similar to the mutagenesis of conserved S301 and Q366 in ppdA-C-B to expand substrate spectrum, the corresponding conserved S302 and Q337 in KpGDH were substituted with alanine residues (Fig. 3D). The resulting variants exhibited significantly improved activities toward C4 and C5 polyols. Consistent with previous results from the ppdA-C-B mutants, 1,4-BDO was successfully obtained from 1,2,4-BTO using a combination of the KpGdhS302A/Q337A mutant and commercial alcohol dehydrogenase in vitro. Furthermore, it was demonstrated that 1,4-BDO could also be synthesized from erythritol using either the ppdA-C-B or KpGDH mutants along with EcYqhD (Fig. 3D). When introduced individually into E. coli, neither of the dehydratase mutants resulted in detectable 1,4-BDO production under conditions with erythritol supplementation. In contrast, resting cells expressing the ppdA-C-B and KpGDH mutants individually could yield 16.1 and 11.9 mg·L−1 1,4-BDO from erythritol within 20 h respectively. After optimizing the conditions, 35 mg·L−1 1,4-BDO could be obtained from 80 g·L−1 erythritol (Dai et al. 2018). However, the accumulation of 1,2,4-BTO suggested that the dehydration step of both enzymes remained rate-limiting. By engineering resting cells harboring KpGdh mutant, the whole-cell catalysis was optimized to enhance 1,4-BDO synthesis. Under optimal conditions, the engineered E. coli XGDHt strain could produce 48.9 mg·L−1 1,4-BDO from D-xyl (Dai et al. 2017).

In previous engineered E. coli strains for 1,4-BDO production, supplementing IPTG to control heterologous genes expression was one of the major barriers hindering their industrial-scale application. However, constitutive expression of heterologous genes not only caused intracellular cofactor and ATP imbalance but also impaired the cellular growth. Thus, there was an urgent need for a genetic circuit to regulate the expression of these heterologous genes (Hossain et al. 2020). In line with previous reports, the triple knockout of exogenous xylA, yjhH, and yagE was applied to block native D-xyl consumption. A putative six-enzyme 1,4-BDO synthesis route, comprising CcXylB, EcYjhG/EcYagF, CcXylX/KDX dehydratase from Haloferax volcanii (HvD-KdpD), diol dehydratase from P. putida (PpMdlC)/LlKivD, EcSadh, and EcAdh, was proposed and designed in the triple-knockout host strain (Fig. 3A). After screening, the recombinant expressing heterologous CcXylB, CcXylX, and PpMdlC emerged as the optimal enzyme combination, yielding 0.35 g·L−1 1,4-BDO. To optimize the heterologous gene expression, a cell-density-dependent genetic circuit leveraging a quorum-sensing system to drive the expression of CcXdh, CcXylX, and PpMdlC was implemented. This circuit outperformed other external induction systems, enabling the accumulation of 0.4 g·L−1 1,4-BDO without requiring extracellular inducer supplementation (Liu and Lu 2015).

Bio-based 1,4-butanediol synthesis using cell-free enzyme cascades

To prove this conception, a novel Fe-S-dependent sugar dehydratase from Herbaspirillum seropedicae (HsXylDH2), responsible for converting D-xyl and L-ara to D-xylL and L-araL simultaneously, was identified and characterized. As the hydrolysis of both previously mentioned lactones to their corresponding sugar acids was considered to occur spontaneously, efforts were directed toward screening promiscuous sugar acid dehydratases to synthesize both KDX and KDV from D-xyln and L-arb. The dehydratase from Paralcaligenes ureilyticus (PuDht) was characterized and selected since it possesses the highest activity and total turnover number reported to date. However, no promiscuous enzyme capable of converting KDX and KDA to KGSA (ketoglutarate semialdehyde, also known as 2,5-dioxopentanoate, DOP) has been identified. Instead, the previously reported dehydratase, L-KdpD from C. necator (CnL-KdpD), and D-KdpD from P. putida (PpD-KdpD), were utilized separately (Sutiono et al. 2020). The remaining two steps were considered be fulfilled by previously mentioned LlKdcA and EcAdhZ3.

Therefore, enzyme cascades were constructed to convert D-xyl or L-ara to 1,4-BDO, using HsXylDH2, PuDht, CnL-KdpD/PpD-KdpD, LlKdcA, EcAdhZ3, and formate dehydrogenase from Candida boidinii (CbFdh) which was used for cofactor regeneration additionally. Although a 60% converting rate of 1,4-BDO could be achieved in 32 h using the enzyme cascade, the in vitro production rate was still lower than that of the corresponding fermentation. Through analysis of product spectrum, the accumulation of D-xyln and L-arb was observed, respectively. It was speculated that the hydrolysis of both lactones to corresponding sugar acids might represent the bottlenecks. The promiscuous lactonase from Noviherbaspirillum massiliense (NmLac2) was screened and identified. When NmLac2 was supplemented into the previous enzyme cascades, the reconstructed enzyme reaction achieved 95% of the theoretic yield and a 1,4-BDO converting rate of 3 g·L−1·h−1. After optimizing the concentrations of each enzyme, 18 g·L−1 1,4-BDO was obtained in 16 h using a batch reaction setup (Sutiono et al. 2021).

In line with engineering LlKivD, KdcA from L. lactis, isoenzyme of LlKivD and possessing similar activity and substrate profiles to LlKivD, was also engineered and implemented for 1,4-BDO synthesis from D-xyl. The binding sites of LlKdcA were predicted using in silico docking studies, followed by semi-rational design and screening to enhance catalytic efficiency. It was revealed that both S286 and Q377 were the key residues for enhancing enzyme activity. The S286T/Q377L variant exhibited the highest kcat toward KGSA, which was reasoned to facilitate faster product release from the enzyme complex in the Q377L variant. Interestingly, no previously α-KG-specific decarboxylase exists yet; the S286V/Q377L variant demonstrated a sixfold higher catalysis efficiency toward α-KG. Based on these improvements, a one-pot metal-enzyme cascade transformation was designed to convert D-xyl to 1,4-BDO and succinate. Together with PuDht, PpD-KdpD, and two aldehyde dehydrogenases from E. coli (EcSad and EcAdhp), KdcAS286V/Q377L variant could produce 50 mM 1,4-BDO, which was approximately 100% higher than that generated by the wild-type KdcA (Fig. 4) (Sutiono et al. 2023).

Next, glutamate decarboxylase (EcGadB), γ-aminobutyrate aminotransferase (EcGadT), EcYqhD, and MmCar were harnessed to synthesize 1,4-BDO from glutamate (Glu) in vivo (Wang et al. 2020). As previously reported, the E89Q/Δ452–466 mutant of EcGadB was applied to enhance activity and broaden the optimal catalysis pH (Thu Ho et al. 2013). An engineered E. coli 4BDO-1 strain, harboring high copies of these genes, could synthesize 588.3 mg·L−1 1,4-BDO without additional supplementation of Glu. When 3 g·L−1 of Glu was supplied, the 1,4-BDO titer increased by 47.8% to 869.7 mg·L−1. Additionally, arginine (Arg), glutamine (Gln), and proline (Pro) were incorporated as secondary substrates for 1,4-BDO synthesis through the expression of corresponding arginine decarboxylase (EcAdiA), agmatinase (EcSpeB), putrescine transaminase (EcPatA), 5-aminopentanal dehydrogenase (EcPatD), glutaminase (EcYbaS), and proline dehydrogenase (EcPutA). When 3 g·L−1 of Arg, Gln, and Pro were supplied respectively, the corresponding strains accumulated 1,4-BDO in fermentation broth at 650.1, 903.1, and 648.8 mg·L−1, respectively (Fig. 5B–D).

Although the EcGadBE89Q/Δ452−466 mutant exhibited enhanced activity at neutral pH, the catalytic step remained a bottleneck. To optimize the process, the supply of pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP), the cofactor of both EcGadB and EcGadD, was enhanced. The PLP biosynthetic pathway from the B. subtilis, comprising the PLP synthase subunit (PdsX) and glutamine hydrolase subunit (PdxT), was introduced into the previous 1,4-BDO producing E. coli strain. This not only enhanced the activities of EcGadB and EcGadD, but also improved the metabolic flux from Gln to Glu. Compared to the control strain, co-expression of BsPdsX and BsPdxT doubled 1,4-BDO synthesis, achieving a diol titer of 1.2 g·L−1 when supplemented with Glu. The non-NADH-sensitive EcGltAR163L mutant and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (EcPpc) were also engineered in the BsPdxS- and BsPdxT-overexpressing strain to enhance the metabolic flux from central metabolism to Glu. As a result, the resultant E. coli 4BDO-4 produced 1.4 g·L−1 1,4-BDO in 48 h, which was 2.4-fold higher than that of the control strain (Fig. 5A) (Wang et al. 2020).

Current advance in engineering 1,4-BDO biotransformation

Besides these three principal 1,4-BDO synthesizing routes, two biotransformation exceptions were also designed and engineered for 1,4-BDO synthesis.

Inspired by alkane hydroxylation via monooxygenase, it was hypothesized that ω-hydroxylation could introduce a terminal hydroxyl group on short-chain alkanes. Despite P450 enzymes (CYP153A) being reported to conduct ω-hydroxylation of C8-C12 alcohols, no known enzyme could catalyze this reaction for short-chain alcohols. Given that P450pyr from Sphingomonas exhibits regio- and stereoselective hydroxylation of hydrophobic substrates (Sasaki et al. 2005; Yang et al. 2014), it was engineered to perform ω-hydroxylation on n-butanol for 1,4-BDO synthesis (Yang et al. 2015). Given the structural similarity between n-butanol and 2-methoxyethanol (2-ME), 2-ME was used as a surrogate substrate for high-throughput screening. The product of terminal hydroxylation of 2-ME was unstable and underwent spontaneous decomposition to EG and formaldehyde. Formaldehyde could react with purpald to generate a purple product, whose concentration could be quantified at UV 550 nm. By establishing a relationship between UV absorbance and purple product concentration, the specific activity of the enzyme could be measured (Fig. 7).Fig. 7Engineering P450 for 1,4-BDO synthesis from n-butanol. By docking n-butanol to the P450pyr crystal structure in silico, 22 amino acid residues were identified as potential targets to alter the enzyme’s substrate specificity. First-round iterative saturation mutagenesis (ISM) was performed on all 22 amino acid residues, followed by colorimetric high-throughput screening. Among 4136 mutants, the I83M mutant exhibited the most prominent OD550 value in its reaction solution. In 4 h, 0.25 mM 1,4-BDO could be synthesized without any other hydroxylation byproducts. In the second round of ISM, targeting the remaining 21 residues, the I82T/I83M mutant exhibited the highest OD550 value in its reaction solution. The specific activity of I82T/I83M was 60.5% higher than that of I83M, and 0.4 mM 1,4-BDO was synthesized within 4 h. It was suggested that Met83 mutation led to a closer interaction between Ile102 and Leu132, pushing the C2, C3, and C4 of n-butanol away from the active hemo-O site, thereby promoting terminal hydroxylation at C1. The hydrophilic side chain of Thr82 may disrupt the hydrophobic interaction between Ile102 and Leu132, allowing the hydrophobic end of n-butanol to approach the active hemo-O site more closely. This shorter distance correlates with the higher activity of double mutant (Fig. 7).

A successive enzyme cascade catalysis for 1,4-BDO synthesis using succinate as a substrate was designed. First, succinate was reduced by carboxylic acid decarboxylase (Car) to form SSA, which was then reduced to form 4HB; next, the 4HB underwent reduction to form 4HBA, which was finally reduced to 1,4-BDO by aldo–keto reductase (Akr). Overall, Suc underwent two rounds of Car reduction and reductase/dehydrogenase reduction to synthesize 1,4-BDO. To verify this concept, several Cars and Akrs were selected and screened. Coincided with previous reports, the expression of BsSfp was also a prerequisite for maximizing Car activity (Venkitasubramanian et al. 2007). Among Car and Akr candidates, MmCar and Akr from E. coli (EcYqhD) competed over others, respectively (Ni et al. 2024). In engineered E. coli N01 strain, 22 mg·L−1 1,4-BDO was produced from 10 g·L−1 succinate in 48 h. HPLC analysis suggested that the conversion of succinate to SSA by MmCar might be the rate-limiting step.

The second enzyme cascade designed to enhance SSA synthesis from succinate involved the use of Cat1/SucCD and SucD. After screening 4 Cat1s, 3 SucCDs, and 5 SucDs, it was found that the combination of EcSucCD and PgSucD outperformed other candidates. By increasing the copy numbers of the vector harboring MmCar and EcYqhD to a medium level, and the copy numbers the vector harboring EcSucCD and PgSucD to a high level, the engineered E. coli N04 strain produced 92 mg·L−1 1,4-BDO in 48 h.

To enhance MmCar enzyme activity, 26 residues locating within 10 Å of C4-, C5-, and C6-chain-length carboxylates were selected through in silico docking experiments. Two rounds of ISM were performed, followed by high-throughput screening based on NADPH consumption monitoring. After three rounds of stepwise ISM, the MmCarI922V/C983I/Y952R mutant was obtained exhibiting 108.7% higher activity than the wild-type enzyme. Replacing wild-type MmCar in E. coli N04 strain with MmCarI922V/C983I/Y952R mutant resulted in the production of 135 mg·L−1 of 1,4-BDO. To improve the 1,4-BDO conversion, overexpression of genes enhancing intracellular NADPH and ATP levels was conducted. Co-expression of glucose dehydrogenase from Priestia megaterium (PmGdh) and polyphosphate kinase from Deinococcus geothermalis (DgPpk) significantly increased 1,4-BDO production, achieving 3.7-fold higher NADPH and 2.6-fold higher ATP levels, accompanied by 181 mg·L−1 of 1,4-BDO. Perspective and challenges

Elucidating 1,4-BDO catabolism in host cells via multi-omics approaches

For efficient diol synthesis and accumulation, the deletion of endogenous 1,4-BDO catabolic pathways is a well-recognized prerequisite (Cen et al. 2022); moreover, owing to their high reducing potential, the 1,4-BDO and other short-chain diols are readily subjected to cellular oxidation as energy source when the primary carbon source is depleted. The catabolic pathway of 1,4-BDO has been characterized in P. putida KT2440, and reverse metabolic engineering studies have confirmed that the PQQ-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase in the ped gene cluster (pedE) and PP_2049 (iron-containing alcohol dehydrogenase) play essential roles in this process (Fig. 9). Based on previously characterization of these enzymes, it was assumed that the 1,4-BDO was dehydrogenated to 4HBA by PedE, and PP_2049 would dehydrogenate the 4HB-forming SSA; the enzyme catalyzing 4HBA to 4HB was not be identified (Li et al. 2020; Qian et al. 2023).Fig. 9The 1,4-BDO catabolic pathways in host cells. The endogenous 1,4-BDO catabolic pathway, in which fadH, aldH, and adhA were engaged, was also identified by transcriptomic analysis in C. glutamicum (Kim et al. 2025). The mutants, in which these genes were overexpressed respectively, displayed faster degradation of exogenous 1,4-BDO. Among three enzymes, AldH dominated the degradation of 1,4-BDO, because exogenously added 1,4-BDO was barely detected in the culture broth of AldH-overexpressing strain. Also, the triple knock-out strain accumulated a 1.3-fold higher titer of 1,4-BDO than that of control strain. Combining natural substrate spectrum with the catalysis activity predicting analysis, and enzyme-molecule docking studies, it was suspected that FadH and AdhA would dehydrogenate the 1,4-BDO to 4HBA, which was followed by AldH dehydrogenation of 4HBA to 4HB (Fig. 9).

These enzymes’ identification and characterization suggested that enzymes involved in the catabolism of other diols are likely to catalyze 1,4-BDO oxidation, owing to the structural similarity among different diols and dehydrogenase promiscuity. Pathways dependent on 1,2-PDO oxidoreductase, PQQ-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase, and mycofactocin-associated dehydrogenase have been reported to mediate the EG assimilation in several strains (Shimizu and Inui 2024), highlighting the high potential of screening for 1,4-BDO-catalyzing enzymes with broad substrates spectrum from these engineered 1,4-BDO-producing strains.

Alternatively, growth-based assay-using target metabolite as solo carbon or nitrogen source-coupled with multi-omics analysis represent the most effective strategy to elucidate metabolite assimilation pathways and corresponding cellular response (Cuenca et al. 2016; Vallon et al. 2015). The adaptive laboratory evolution was applied to enable the P. taiwanensis to grow on 1,4-BDO; the genomic sequencing analysis of the engineered strains of P. taiwanensis illustrated that the mutations of PVLB_10545 would play same roles of pedE of P. putida KT2440 on 1,4-BDO catabolism (Op de Hipt et al. 2025). The 1,4-BDO-responsive transporter, YhjX, was successively identified via growth-based assay combined with transcriptomics data mining in E. coli (Szmidt-Middleton et al. 2013), but integrated multi-omics datasets (transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic) are still lacking to validate and characterize dehydrogenase candidates with 1,4-BDO assimilating activity.

It is well established that deciphering and engineering organic solvent tolerance mechanisms of host strains is a powerful strategy to improve the production of target metabolites (Qin et al. 2025b; Xu et al. 2022). Our unpublished data demonstrate that 100–120 g·L−1 1,4-BDO completely inhibits cellular growth of Y. lipolytica in YPD medium. To date, no studies have investigated and reported 1,4-BDO tolerance in heterologous host strains, which limits the development of engineering strategies to enhance 1,4-BDO tolerance and accumulation. Multi-omics approaches can reveal cellular responses to 1,4-BDO stress at the genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, and metabolomic levels. Although tolerance mechanisms are species-specific, genes involved in efflux pumps, stress and multidrug responses, membrane transport, and redox homeostasis are generally recognized as key targets for improving solvent tolerance (Arsov et al. 2021). Therefore, multi-omics studies are highly warranted to identify the potential 1,4-BDO catabolic genes and the cellular tolerance targets for enhancing 1,4-BDO productivity.

Rational and computational engineering key enzymes for 1,4-BDO synthesis

1,4-BDO biosynthesis relies entirely on enzyme promiscuity; however, promiscuous enzymatic activities are typically inefficient due to suboptimal enzyme–substrate binding and conformational fluctuations (Copley 2017). Thus, it is imperative to enhance the activity of promiscuous enzymes, particularly dehydrogenases that catalyze the final step of 1,4-BDO synthesis. In the absence of a detailed understanding of dehydrogenase catalytic mechanisms, random mutagenesis, iterative saturation mutagenesis (ISM), and directed evolution can be employed to improve dehydrogenase activity, albeit these strategies are labor- and time-intensive. High-throughput screening (HTS) technologies can greatly facilitate and accelerate the isolation of enzymes with desired properties from large mutagenesis libraries (Li et al. 2025; Lu et al. 2021). Based on elucidated catalytic mechanisms, rational design strategies can be applied to enhance enzyme activity, modify substrate specificity, and alter cofactor preference of dehydrogenases (Xu et al. 2021). More importantly, machine learning-assisted data-driven enzyme engineering exhibits enormous potential to improve enzyme stability, activity, and robustness (Wen et al. 2025). It is anticipated that dehydrogenases with high activity, stability, and substrate selectivity for 1,4-BDO synthesis can be developed via artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted enzyme engineering.

Dynamic regulation of heterologous 1,4-BDO synthetic routes

Heterologous 1,4-BDO synthesis is highly dependent on the intracellular supply of substrates and cofactors, whose levels dynamically fluctuate according to extracellular environmental conditions and cellular physiological states. Constitutive expression strategies are commonly used to drive the expression of key heterologous enzymes; however, this approach often induces metabolic imbalances and even impairs host cell growth. Dynamic metabolic regulation strategies are universally applied to balance heterologous enzyme catalysis and host cellular growth, thereby enhancing target metabolite synthesis (Zhang et al. 2026). Although a variety of tools have been developed and applied to optimize the production of heterologous metabolites (Qin et al. 2025a), the main barrier to their application for 1,4-BDO synthesis is the lack of 1,4-BDO-responsive dynamic regulatory components. In addition to growth-based assays, environmental stimulus experiments using the target metabolite as the inducer-coupled with multi-omics studies represent the mainstream strategy to identify metabolite-responsive regulatory elements (Wang et al. 2023). Considering the toxic effects of high-titer 1,4-BDO, adaptive evolution to improve host strain tolerance combined with multi-omics mining is the most promising strategy to obtain 1,4-BDO-responsive elements, which can then be engineered for the dynamic regulation of heterologous 1,4-BDO synthetic pathways.

Conclusion

This review elaborates on the recent advances in pathway engineering for sustainable 1,4-BDO biosynthesis, a high-value diol with wide industrial applications, as an alternative to polluting petroleum-based methods. Three core de novo biosynthetic routes, the CoA-dependent route, the lignocellulose-derived sugar-catabolizing route, and amino acid-catabolizing route, have been rationally optimized via enzyme engineering, dynamic metabolic regulation, in vitro whole-cell biotransformation, and enzyme cascade catalysis. The study further identifies key industrialization bottlenecks: unresolved intracellular 1,4-BDO catabolism, limited host tolerance, and poor economic feasibility. It proposes future research priorities, including multi-omics-driven elucidation of the degradation mechanisms, the design of high-specificity dehydrogenases, and the development of precision genetic tools for dynamic flux control, while noting that bio-based 1,4-BDO production is still in the early stage for industrial scalability.
