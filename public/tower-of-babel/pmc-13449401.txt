Integrating multimodal features with deep learning for protein solubility prediction
Authors: Zechen Wang, Lai Heng Tan, Liangzhen Zheng, Jagath C. Rajapakse, Yuguang Mu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Protein solubility prediction holds significant importance in the fields of biotechnology and medicine. With the continual advancements of computational and experimental techniques such as protein design, enzyme mining, and directed evolution, accurate prediction of native and mutant protein solubility has become a key step in accelerating the development of functional proteins. In this study, we extracted physicochemical properties and co-evolutionary features from protein sequences, and further incorporated graph-based protein representations along with surface features as inputs for solubility prediction. Building upon these features, we developed two models, ProSolNet and ProSolNetMut. ProSolNet predicts whether a protein is soluble, while ProSolNetMut predicts solubility changes induced by mutations. Compared with state-of-the-art models, both models achieved higher accuracy in their respective tasks. In addition, we investigated the underlying mechanisms and application potential of the models through interpretability analysis.

Introduction

Obtaining proteins with desired functions is a crucial task in protein engineering [1–3]. However, obtaining adequate amounts of proteins directly from natural sources is often limited by low yields and various technical constraints, making it challenging to meet practical application requirements [4]. To overcome these challenges, expressing recombinant proteins in heterologous hosts has become a widely used strategy. Among available systems, Escherichia coli (E. coli) remains the most popular host [5] due to its ease of genetic manipulation [6, 7], the availability of diverse molecular tools [8], and its capacity for rapid, large-scale cultivation [7]. However, during heterologous overexpression, many proteins tend to aggregate into inclusion bodies composed of misfolded or unfolded species, posing a major challenge in protein engineering [9]. This phenomenon is closely associated with the limited solubility of recombinant proteins.

Previous studies have demonstrated that, for certain proteins, activity and solubility are closely correlated [10]. For instance, some insoluble enzymes in the deoxyxylulose phosphate pathway exhibit significantly lower enzymatic activity compared with an equivalent amount of their soluble enzymes [3]. These findings suggest that solubility may serve as a critical criterion for screening and selecting promising enzyme candidates and their mutants. Therefore, accurately evaluating solubility is essential for guiding protein selection, expression, purification, and downstream applications in both protein and enzyme engineering [3–6]. However, since experimental determination of solubility is often time-consuming and resource-intensive, computational prediction offers an efficient alternative.

In recent years, machine learning and deep learning have been successfully applied to various bioinformatics tasks, including protein structure prediction [11], protein-ligand interaction prediction [12–16], enzyme kinetic parameter prediction [17, 18], and so on. This trend is also evident in the field of protein solubility prediction [10, 19–23], where most models are trained using data derived from public protein repositories such as TargetTrack [24] and the Protein Data Bank (PDB) [25]. A common labeling strategy assigns proteins as soluble if they are experimentally annotated as “soluble” in E. coli, or if they possess downstream indicators consistent with soluble expression [21]. Conversely, proteins that fail during expression or purification are labeled as insoluble [19, 21]. This strategy enables the construction of large-scale solubility datasets and allows predictive models to estimate the likelihood that a target protein can be obtained in a soluble form upon overexpression in E. coli.

Early models primarily relied on features extracted directly from protein sequences. For instance, SoluProt utilized sequence-derived features such as amino acid composition, dipeptide frequency, and physicochemical properties, and trained a gradient boosting classifier for solubility prediction [19]. DeepSoluE employed similar descriptors but adopted a long short-term memory (LSTM) network as the modeling architecture [20]. Unlike SoluProt and DeepSoluE, which relied solely on sequence information, GraphSol constructed protein graphs from predicted contact maps, integrated physicochemical properties and evolutionary information, and employed a graph neural network to predict protein solubility [22]. Recently, significant advances in protein structure prediction have enabled the acquisition of high-quality 3D protein structures through computational approaches [11, 26, 27]. Building on these advances, Tan et al. proposed PROTSOLM, a graph neural network that leverages ESMFold-predicted 3D structures [27] together with fine-tuning strategies, achieving competitive performance on both internal and external test sets for protein solubility predictions [21].

Another challenge in protein and enzyme engineering is assessing the impact of mutations on protein solubility, as even a single-point mutation can lead to a substantial change in solubility [28]. Therefore, developing predictive models for mutation-induced solubility changes is crucial for guiding rational mutation design. To address this need, Yang et al. developed PON-Sol2, a gradient boosting tree-based model that utilizes amino acid composition, conservation, mutation type, neighborhood, and protein type features to characterize mutation-induced solubility changes [29]. More recently, Wang et al. proposed DeepMutSol, a graph neural network that integrates AlphaFold2-predicted 3D structures with embeddings derived from a protein language model as inputs [30]. Although DeepMutSol outperforms PON-Sol2 across multiple evaluation metrics, the model still has room for improvement in distinguishing mutants with different effects on solubility. In particular, it shows limited ability to assign high scores to beneficial mutations, likely due to the scarcity of such samples in the training set.

We speculate that, in addition to data scarcity, limitations in feature modeling represent another key factor constraining the performance of current solubility prediction models. To address these challenges, we extract multimodal features from both the primary and tertiary structures of proteins, including embedding representations from the protein language model Prot_t5_xl_uniref50 (ProtT5) [31], molecular physicochemical properties, protein graph structures, and surface features. Based on these representations and graph neural networks, we developed two models, ProSolNet and ProSolNetMut. ProSolNet predicts whether a protein is soluble, while ProSolNetMut predicts mutation-induced changes in solubility. Compared with baseline models, our proposed models achieve consistently superior performance, improving accuracy (ACC) from 0.794 to 0.827 and from 0.618 to 0.636 on the two corresponding test sets. Furthermore, we evaluate the contribution of different features to solubility prediction. Finally, we discuss the strengths and limitations of current methods and provide perspectives for future research in this field.

Results

Overview of ProSolNet

Fig. 1Overview of ProSolNet, including the feature extraction process and model architecture

Overview of ProSolNet, including the feature extraction process and model architecture

Protein solubility is fundamentally influenced by factors such as the distribution of hydrophobic and hydrophilic residues, surface charge patterns, and local structural packing [1]. Early solubility prediction models relied solely on amino acid sequences, whereas recent advances in protein structure prediction have given rise to structure-based approaches. However, these models have not fully explored the contributions of physicochemical features to solubility prediction.

In this study, global protein features at the sequence level were first extracted, as these features have proven effective in previous works [19, 20]. For structural representation, each protein was encoded as a graph derived from its 3D structure predicted by ESMFold [27], where residues were treated as nodes and edges were constructed between residue pairs whose C\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_{\alpha }$$\end{document}α atoms were within 8 Å. The node features were initialized using ProtT5 embeddings. To further explore the structural features closely related to protein solubility and to enrich the representation of protein features, we incorporated protein surface descriptors generated by MaSIF [32]. MaSIF computes a set of biophysical properties for each vertex of the protein surface mesh, including electrostatic potential, hydrogen bond potential, and hydropathy index. To obtain residue-level surface features, each surface mesh vertex was assigned to its nearest residue based on Euclidean distance, and the surface descriptors were averaged over all vertices mapped to the same residue. The resulting residue-level surface features were then concatenated with the corresponding node features learned from the protein structure graph. To aggregate residue-level features into a global protein representation, we employed an attention-based pooling mechanism that learns adaptive weights for different residues during training. This aggregation allows the model to flexibly combine information from residues with varying contributions to solubility prediction. Finally, the three types of features described above were concatenated to form the overall protein representation, which was then fed into a multilayer perceptron (MLP) to predict the solubility. The overall workflow of ProSolNet is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Performance of ProSolNet

Comparison of ProSolNet with baseline models. a, c, e, and g show the AUC, ACC, MCC, and F1 score on the test set, respectively, while b, d, f, and h present the corresponding metrics on the external test set

We used the training, validation, and test sets of the PDBSol dataset to train and evaluate ProSolNet. Since the model outputs probabilities ranging from 0 to 1, a threshold is required to distinguish between soluble and insoluble proteins. To this end, we evaluated the F1 score at different threshold values on the validation set, as shown in Supplementary Figure S1. When the threshold is set to 0.42, the model achieves the highest F1 score on the validation set. Therefore, 0.42 was selected as the final classification threshold.

We first compared ProSolNet with baseline models [19–21, 33–37] on the test set. ProSolNet attained an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.920, an accuracy (ACC) of 0.827, a Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of 0.655, and an F1 score of 0.840, outperforming PROTSOLM, which reached an AUC of 0.794, an ACC of 0.794, an MCC of 0.588, and an F1 score of 0.801 (Fig. 2a, c, e and g). To further assess the generalization capability of ProSolNet, we conducted evaluations on an external test set. On this dataset, ProSolNet achieved AUC, ACC, and MCC of 0.679, 0.632, and 0.266, respectively, outperforming the baseline models (Fig. 2b, d, and f). However, we observed that ProSolNet achieves a slightly lower F1 score on the external test set compared to PLM_Sol, NetSolP, SoluProt, and DeepSoluE (Fig. 2h). This may be partly explained by the higher sequence identity between the proteins in their training sets and those in the external test set, with some proteins even reaching 100% sequence identity. Such high sequence identity may introduce redundancy between training and test data, potentially contributing to relatively optimistic performance. In contrast, the sequence identity between the training set of ProSolNet and the external test set is strictly below 25%, indicating a more challenging and realistic evaluation setting.

To further evaluate the robustness of ProSolNet, we performed five-fold cross-validation. Specifically, the original training and validation sets were combined, and cross-validation was conducted on the merged dataset. The threshold of 0.42, determined on the validation set, was consistently applied across all folds and evaluation datasets. As shown in Supplementary Table S1, ProSolNet exhibits consistently low standard deviations across folds. In addition, the ensemble model achieves performance comparable to that obtained using the original training and validation split (Supplementary Table S2). These results indicate that ProSolNet is stable under different data splits and produces consistent results across training configurations.

To further assess the practical applicability of ProSolNet, we evaluated the model on the eSOL [38, 39] dataset, which provides experimentally measured solubility values for E. coli proteins. Unlike the PDBSol dataset, eSOL reports continuous solubility percentages derived from in vitro expression experiments, thereby enabling direct comparison between model predictions and experimentally observed solubility. To evaluate classification performance on high-confidence samples, we followed previous studies and adopted commonly used thresholds to define binary labels. Specifically, proteins with solubility greater than 70% were labeled as soluble, while those with solubility lower than 30% were labeled as insoluble [38]. On this subset, ProSolNet achieved an accuracy of 0.693, an AUROC of 0.763, and an MCC of 0.405, demonstrating its ability to distinguish soluble proteins from aggregation-prone ones based on experimental data. In addition, proteins were grouped into bins according to predicted solubility probabilities, and the distribution of experimental solubility values was analyzed across bins (Supplementary Figure S2). As the predicted probability increases, the median experimental solubility shows a clear upward trend, indicating that higher predicted scores are generally associated with higher experimentally measured solubility. Despite the spread within each bin, the overall monotonic trend further supports the ability of ProSolNet to capture solubility-related patterns. These results demonstrate that ProSolNet is not only effective on curated benchmark datasets, but also exhibits meaningful consistency with experimentally measured solubility, highlighting its potential utility in prioritizing candidate proteins for recombinant expression experiments. Interpretability analysis of ProSolNet

To investigate the contribution of different feature types to solubility prediction, we conducted ablation experiments. As shown in Table 1, structural features were more effective than protein-level sequence features, highlighting the advantage of structure-based graph representations for this task. In addition, the contribution of surface features was less pronounced compared with the other feature modalities. This may be because the protein graph representation implicitly captures certain aspects of residue exposure and local surface-related information, thereby reducing the additional benefit provided by explicitly computed surface descriptors.

To investigate the contribution of individual components within each feature modality, we employed the Integrated Gradients (IG) method to quantify their relative importance. Specifically, for the protein graph, we assessed per-residue-type importance using two complementary aggregation strategies. First, we averaged normalized node-level IG attributions (IGA) within each protein and then aggregated them across all proteins in the test set, thereby capturing the typical contribution of each residue type to the model predictions when it occurs in a protein. As shown in Figure 3a, Methionine (M) and Leucine (L) exhibit the strongest influence under this per-protein normalized analysis. Second, we computed a global residue-type importance by accumulating normalized node-level IGA over all proteins, reflecting the overall contribution of each residue type across the entire dataset. Under this global aggregation, Leucine (L) shows the largest overall contribution (Supplementary Figure 3a). However, it should be noted that the second strategy does not normalize for residue frequency, and thus the resulting importance scores may be influenced by the occurrence frequency of specific residues in protein sequences.

Similarly, we also analyzed the importance of individual components within the protein surface features and the sequence-based global features. For protein surface features, we computed normalized per-feature importance scores and observed that hydropathy-related features receive the highest attribution values, indicating that the model relies more strongly on hydropathy information of surface regions in making predictions (Figure 3 d). Among the sequence-based global features, the maximum identity to the E. coli PDB subset exhibits the highest importance, likely because it implicitly captures evolutionary and experimental biases associated with proteins that have been successfully expressed and structurally characterized in E. coli (Supplementary Figure 3b).

To provide an intuitive visualization of the learned representations from different feature modalities in ProSolNet, we extracted intermediate embeddings from hidden layers and visualized their distributions using t-SNE. The sequence-based, structural, surface, and final integrated embeddings are shown in Figs. 3b, 3c, 3e, and 3f, respectively. As illustrated, the embeddings derived from structural and surface features appear to exhibit a clearer clustering tendency between soluble and insoluble proteins compared with sequence-based representations, while the final integrated embeddings show the most visually distinct grouping. We also visualized the attention distribution learned by the model on several proteins, as shown in Fig. 4. After global aggregation across nodes in the protein graph, we observed that the model sometimes assigns relatively higher attention weights to residues located near the N- and C-termini. This pattern reflects how the model internally weights different regions when forming the protein representation. Notably, terminal regions have been reported in previous studies to be associated with solubility-related properties, and the observed attention pattern appears to be consistent with these reports [40, 41].

Another important question is whether using protein structures from different sources during inference affects the prediction performance. To investigate this, we randomly selected 100 soluble and 100 insoluble proteins from the external test set, generated their structures using the AlphaFold server, and used them as inputs to ProSolNet for solubility prediction. We then compared these predictions with those obtained using ESMFold-generated structures. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 4, the use of protein structures from these two different sources has a minor effect on the model’s predictions. Among the 200 samples, only 4 exhibited inconsistent prediction results.

Predicting solubility changes induced by mutations using ProSolNetMut

Architecture of ProSolNetMut. a Overview of the ProSolNetMut framework. b The details of protein encoder module

Protein mutation is a primary strategy for protein modification in protein engineering [42, 43]. In some cases, even a single amino acid substitution can lead to substantial changes in solubility. To address this challenge, we developed ProSolNetMut, a model designed to predict mutation-induced effects on protein solubility using structural and surface features. We adopted the dataset employed in previous works [30], which includes a 10-fold cross-validation set and an independent test set. These datasets were used for training and evaluation of ProSolNetMut. The dataset defines three labels, – 1 (decreased solubility), 0 (no change), and 1 (increased solubility), making it a three-class classification task. Given the limited availability of mutation–solubility data, we applied a symmetry-based data augmentation strategy to enlarge the effective size of the training set. Specifically, if a mutant M is derived from a wild-type sequence, the wild-type sequence can be regarded as the reverse mutant of M. This operation effectively doubles the size of the training set.

The overall framework of ProSolNetMut is illustrated in Fig. 5a. The 3D structures of both the mutant and the wild-type were first fed into a protein encoder (Fig. 5b) to obtain their corresponding embeddings. A difference vector was then computed by subtracting the wild-type embedding from the mutant embedding, and subsequently passed through an MLP to generate the final output.

The goal of this task is to predict whether a mutation results in decreased, unchanged, or increased protein solubility, corresponding to labels of – 1, 0, and 1, respectively. These labels follow an inherent ordinal relationship, reflecting a progressive increase in solubility from – 1 to 1. However, conventional classification approaches based on cross-entropy loss treat these classes as independent and fail to capture this ordered relationship. To address this limitation, ProSolNetMut outputs a continuous value ranging from – 1 to 1, where values closer to 1 indicate increased solubility, whereas values closer to – 1 indicate decreased solubility. To convert the continuous outputs into three discrete classes, two thresholds need to be introduced to separate decrease, no change, and increase in solubility. To determine the optimal thresholds, we collected the predictions from the validation set of each fold in the 10-fold cross-validation and combined them into a unified validation prediction set. Based on this combined set, the threshold combination that maximizes the normalized ACC was selected. As shown in Fig. 6a, the highest normalized ACC is achieved when the two thresholds are set to −0.40 and 0.34. Accordingly, predicted values in the range of – 1 to −0.40 are classified as decreased solubility, values between −0.40 and 0.34 are classified as unchanged solubility, and values between 0.34 and 1 are classified as increased solubility.

Compared with ProSolNet, ProSolNetMut employs a new protein encoder to represent protein structures. In addition, a new GNN module was introduced to replace the original global sequence feature module, thereby enhancing the model’s sensitivity to mutations, as shown in Fig. 5b. This GNN incorporates customized node features, including one-hot encodings of residue type, polarity, aromaticity, hydrophobicity/hydrophilicity, and secondary structure, further improving the model’s ability to capture mutation effects.

Discussion

Modeling protein information is crucial for protein solubility prediction. In this work, we propose a unified modeling framework that integrates multiple heterogeneous features to further improve the performance of protein solubility prediction.

We integrated sequence-level information, graph-based structural features, and explicitly computed surface descriptors to obtain a more comprehensive representation of protein solubility. The improved performance of ProSolNet demonstrates the effectiveness of this multimodal strategy, indicating that combining complementary feature modalities provides a more complete characterization of the factors governing protein solubility. Moreover, incorporating physically meaningful structural and surface features may help alleviate the limitations imposed by the scarcity of experimentally measured solubility data. Ablation experiments reveal that protein graph features contribute more substantially to predictive performance than other feature modalities, suggesting that explicit modeling of residue–residue interactions is important for capturing structural determinants of protein solubility.

Recent advances in protein solubility prediction have further highlighted the importance of improved protein representation learning and multimodal feature integration. In particular, protein language models and deep learning frameworks have been shown to capture rich sequence-level semantics and long-range dependencies, thereby enhancing predictive performance [44, 45]. In addition, integrating sequence, structural, and physicochemical information has been demonstrated to improve model accuracy by providing complementary perspectives on protein properties [46]. These developments are in line with our findings, further supporting the effectiveness of combining diverse feature modalities. Moreover, model generalization remains a critical challenge in protein solubility prediction. Recent studies have pointed out that high sequence similarity between training and test sets may lead to overly optimistic performance estimates, and that improving dataset diversity is essential for building robust models [47]. This observation is also reflected in our results on the external test set, where lower sequence similarity presents a more challenging evaluation scenario. These findings suggest that reducing redundancy and incorporating more diverse protein sequences are important directions for improving model generalization.

A key challenge in predicting mutation effects on protein solubility is the substantial imbalance in experimental data: most mutations decrease solubility, whereas only a small fraction increase it. This imbalance complicates model training and can bias models toward predicting solubility-decreasing mutations, even though mutations indeed tend to reduce solubility in practice. Compared with DeepMutSol [30], which exhibits a relatively ambiguous distinction between neutral and solubility-increasing mutations, ProSolNetMut provides a clearer separation between these two categories (Figure 6c). This improvement is likely attributed to the use of diverse, physically and chemically informed feature representations, combined with a tailored model architecture. However, a major limitation of the current model lies in the limited coverage of protein and mutant space in the training set, which may reduce predictive performance on unseen protein mutants. Future improvements in predicting mutation-induced solubility changes may benefit from incorporating more expressive protein representations, such as large-scale pretrained language models and multimodal feature fusion strategies, as well as leveraging more diverse and high-quality datasets. In addition, developing effective data augmentation strategies may further enhance model generalization, particularly for mutation effect prediction under low sequence similarity conditions.

Compared with experimental assays, computational approaches such as ProSolNet and ProSolNetMut enable rapid and cost-effective high-throughput prediction of protein solubility and mutation-induced solubility changes. This makes them particularly useful for screening large numbers of candidate sequences or mutations prior to experimental testing. Nevertheless, experimental validation remains essential to confirm the reliability of these predictions in real biological systems. Integrating computational predictions with targeted experimental validation represents a promising direction for further enhancing model reliability and practical applicability.

Conclusion

Protein solubility prediction primarily involves two tasks: determining whether a protein is soluble and predicting the effects of mutations on solubility, both of which are essential in protein engineering. In this study, we introduced ProSolNet, a model that integrates protein surface features with global and structural representations to predict protein solubility. This integration enhances both the accuracy and generalization of solubility prediction. We further evaluated the contributions of different features through interpretability analysis and explored how the model captures solubility-related information. To predict mutation-induced changes in protein solubility, we proposed ProSolNetMut, which leverages a new protein encoder to learn differential features between mutants and their corresponding wild-type proteins, thereby enhancing predictive performance, particularly for identifying beneficial mutations. Finally, we discussed our findings in the context of existing studies and provided insights and perspectives for future research in this field.

Methods

Datasets

Datasets for binary solubility classification

For the binary classification task of predicting protein solubility, we adopted the PDBSol dataset established by Tan et al. [21], which integrates data from UniProtKB [48], TargetTrack [24], PDB [25], and PRSP-2K [49] databases. These data were aggregated and curated to construct a large-scale solubility dataset. The solubility labels were derived from experimental annotations and database records. Specifically, proteins successfully expressed in soluble form or reaching downstream experimental stages were labeled as soluble, whereas proteins with failed expression or purification records were labeled as insoluble. After a series of preprocessing steps, including removal of redundant sequences using a 25% identity cutoff, filtering of transmembrane proteins, elimination of sequences with artificial purification tags, and balancing of sequence length distribution, the final dataset contains 64,598 proteins, including 33,763 soluble and 30,835 insoluble samples. In addition, corresponding 3D protein structures predicted by ESMFold are provided for all proteins in the dataset. The PDBSol dataset was randomly divided into training (58,138 samples), validation (3,230 samples), and test sets (3,230 samples). The distributions of soluble and insoluble proteins across these subsets are summarized in Supplementary Table S3.

The external test set used in this work was constructed by Tan et al. [21] and is derived from multiple publicly available datasets, including eSOL [38, 39], NESG-SoluProt [19], and NESG-DSResSol [36]. The eSOL dataset contains experimentally measured protein solubility values, while NESG-SoluProt and NESG-DSResSol are benchmark datasets derived from the NESG resource with different preprocessing. Following commonly adopted practices in previous studies, proteins in the eSOL dataset with solubility values lower than 30% were labeled as insoluble, whereas those with solubility values higher than 70% were labeled as soluble. To ensure independence from the training data, sequences sharing more than 25% identity with the training set were removed. The final external test set contains 7579 proteins, including 3820 soluble and 3759 insoluble proteins, as summarized in Supplementary Table S4.

Datasets for mutation-induced solubility prediction

For the task of predicting solubility changes caused by mutations, we adopted the mutation-induced solubility dataset constructed by Wang et al. [30]. This dataset contains 6,328 mutants derived from 77 proteins, whose wild-type 3D structures were predicted using AlphaFold2 [11]. Among these mutants, 3,136 exhibit decreased solubility, 2166 show no change, and 1026 display increased solubility. Wang et al. randomly divided the dataset into a training set and a test set while maintaining similar ratios among the three mutation categories. The distribution of samples across different classes in the training and test sets is presented in Supplementary Table S5. The training set was further split into ten folds for cross-validation. To ensure a fair comparison with baseline models, we employed the same 10-fold cross-validation splits and test set as in their study.

In our study, the 3D structures of the mutants were generated using PyRosetta [50]. Point mutations were introduced using the MutateResidue mover, followed by structural relaxation with the FastRelax protocol using the standard full-atom Rosetta score function. The relaxed mutant structures were used for subsequent computation.

Protein surface features

We adopted the MaSIF-surface framework to extract protein surface features. Proteins were first protonated using Reduce, and the solvent-accessible surface was computed using MSMS (Michel Sanner’s Molecular Surface) algorithm [53]. The resulting protein surface mesh was then refined using PyMesh to obtain a mesh resolution of 1.0 Å. MaSIF computes physicochemical descriptors on the protein surface through the following components:

Electrostatic potential: Protein structures were first preprocessed using PDB2PQR [54], followed by computation of the Poisson-Boltzmann electrostatic potential using APBS [55]. The resulting electrostatic values were assigned to surface vertices using Multivalue.

Hydrogen-bonding potential: The hydrogen-bonding potential was calculated based on the positions of polar atoms (nitrogen, oxygen, and polar hydrogen). Each surface vertex was assigned a value between – 1 and +1 according to the local atomic orientation, where – 1 indicates an optimal acceptor position and +1 indicating an optimal donor position.

Hydropathy: Hydropathy values were assigned to each surface vertex based on the Kyte–Doolittle scale of its nearest residue and were normalized to represent local hydropathy.

A more detailed description of surface features can be found in the referenced literature [32].

Integrated gradients attribution

Integrated gradients (IG) was used to analyze the importance of different input features. Integrated gradient attribution (IGA) was computed using the Captum library with the model fixed in evaluation mode. Zero-valued tensors were used as baselines for sequence and residue-level graph node features, while mean feature values were used as baselines for protein surface features. IGA was calculated using 100 integration steps, and the resulting scores were used for downstream analyses.

Model training

The training and testing of ProSolNet and ProSolNetMut were implemented using Python and PyTorch. During model training, the maximum number of epochs was set to 15, and an early stopping strategy with a patience of 5 was applied, meaning that training stopped if the performance in the validation set did not improve for five consecutive epochs. The Adam optimizer was used for both models. The loss functions for ProSolNet and ProSolNetMut were BCELoss and weighted MSE, respectively. During the training of ProSolNetMut, samples that increased solubility were assigned a weight of 1.5, whereas other samples were assigned a weight of 1. The final hyperparameters of the models are presented in Supplementary Tables S7-S8.

Baseline protocol

For the binary classification task of protein solubility prediction, SoluProt [19], DeepSoluE [20], SKADE [35], NetSolP [34], DSResSol [36], PLM_Sol [33], PROTSOLM [21], and DeepSolNet [37] were selected as baseline models. Their performance was evaluated on both the test set and the external test set using the publicly available inference implementations reported in the original studies.

In the task of predicting mutation-induced protein solubility changes, PonSol2, DeepMutSol, and ProSolNetMut were trained on the same training dataset, whereas PonSol was trained on a subset of this dataset. All models were evaluated on the same test set. Except for ProSolNetMut, the reported performance of the other three models was directly taken from the references [29, 30].
