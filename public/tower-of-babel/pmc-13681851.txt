Three-dimensional subtalar joint space mapping identifies sinus tarsi–predominant structural compromise in early- versus end-stage varus ankle osteoarthritis
Authors: Nan Mei, Hiroaki Kurokawa, Takuma Miyamoto, Norihiro Tsujimoto, Yinghao Li, Yaokuan Ruan, Fei Chang, Akira Taniguchi, Yasuhito Tanaka, Kawamura Kenji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveSubtalar compensation is increasingly recognized in varus ankle osteoarthritis (OA), yet the breakdown of this compensatory mechanism in advanced disease and the regional degenerative signature within the subtalar joint remain poorly characterized. We applied simulated weight-bearing computed tomography (sWBCT)–based three-dimensional (3D) subtalar joint space width (JSW) mapping to quantify regional structural differences between early- and end-stage disease.MethodsPatients with varus ankle OA were stratified by the Takakura–Tanaka classification into early-stage (stage 2) and end-stage (stage 4) groups and underwent bilateral sWBCT. Conventional radiographic and sWBCT-derived alignment parameters were measured. Subtalar bone-to-bone distance mapping was performed and partitioned into four regions of interest (ROIs): the anterior, middle, and posterior articular facets, and the sinus tarsi interosseous region. For each ROI, distance distribution metrics and surface-burden metrics—defined as the proportion of ROI surface with bone-to-bone distance < 3.0, < 2.0, < 1.0, and < 0.5 mm—were extracted. Associations between alignment parameters and JSW indices were assessed using partial Spearman correlations controlling for disease stage.ResultsEnd-stage OA showed a global reduction in subtalar complex JSW and a greater surface burden of severe narrowing than early-stage OA. Between-group separation was most pronounced and consistent in the sinus tarsi compartment: all eight C-SIN metrics differed significantly between stages with uniformly large effect sizes (η² = 0.257–0.562; all linear mixed model [LMM] q ≤ 0.024). After adjustment for disease stage, the tibiotalar angle on sWBCT (TTA-sWBCT) was independently associated with posterior-facet JSW indices (partial |rp| = 0.44–0.62; all q ≤ 0.036) and with the sinus tarsi severe-narrowing burden (partial |rp| = 0.49–0.58; all q ≤ 0.027).ConclusionsWBCT-based 3D distance mapping identified sinus tarsi interosseous-space compromise as the most prominent structural difference between early- and end-stage varus ankle OA, whereas posterior-facet articular JSW was more closely linked to coronal malalignment after adjustment for disease stage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13018-026-07116-4.

Introduction

Varus ankle osteoarthritis (OA) is a complex three-dimensional (3D) deformity. Current management remains largely tibiotalar-focused and may therefore underrepresent clinically relevant subtalar decompensation. Talar varus and rotation alter load transfer beyond the tibiotalar joint and, through ankle–hindfoot coupling, can drive subtalar malalignment and collapse [1–5]. Growing evidence positions the subtalar joint as a “compensation–decompensation” mechanical modulator in varus ankle OA: subtalar valgus tilt develops in the early-to-intermediate stages, followed by loss of compensation and subtalar varus in end-stage disease [4, 6, 7]. Weight-bearing computed tomography (WBCT) studies have further characterized this progression as a transition from subtalar valgus with calcaneal internal rotation to calcaneal external rotation, hindfoot varus, and peritalar collapse [1–3, 6, 8]. Clinically, increasing talar tilt is recognized as an alignment marker of hindfoot decompensation and disease progression, and correlates strongly with hindfoot alignment, reinforcing the need to incorporate subtalar and hindfoot parameters into comprehensive evaluation [3, 4, 7–10]. Nevertheless, the region-specific structural phenotype of subtalar involvement across early- and end-stage disease remains poorly characterized, and objective 3D metrics capable of quantifying subtalar compensation–decompensation beyond single-plane alignment descriptors are lacking.

Joint space width (JSW) serves as an imaging surrogate of cartilage thickness, contact and loading behavior, providing a structural readout of OA progression, particularly when quantified across the entire articular surface [11–13]. WBCT-based 3D JSW mapping enables distance measurements across the articular surface, and surface-based mapping further permits assessment of regional distribution and narrowing burden—an approach well suited to the heterogeneous contact mechanics of the subtalar joint [14, 15]. To date, however, subtalar involvement in end-stage varus ankle OA has not been well defined using region-specific 3D JSW phenotyping, leaving the structural signature of subtalar compensation–decompensation incompletely understood.

In this study, we used sWBCT-based 3D subtalar JSW mapping to compare regional subtalar structural differences between early- and end-stage varus ankle OA. We hypothesized that end-stage disease would demonstrate greater subtalar JSW narrowing burden compared with early-stage disease, potentially extending beyond the cartilage-bearing articular facets to involve the peritalar space, and that coronal and axial alignment parameters would be associated with subtalar JSW indices.

Materials and methods

Study design and patients

This retrospective, cross-sectional study consecutively enrolled patients with symptomatic varus ankle OA who underwent sWBCT between December 2020 and November 2021. The study was approved by our institutional review board, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Eligible patients had clinically and radiographically confirmed varus ankle OA, completed a standardized sWBCT examination of the ankle and hindfoot, and demonstrated Takakura–Tanaka stage 2–4 disease on standing anteroposterior ankle radiographs. Exclusion criteria were inflammatory or rheumatoid arthritis and a history of prior ankle or subtalar arthrodesis. Eligible patients were stratified into an early-stage group (Takakura–Tanaka stage 2) and an end-stage group (Takakura–Tanaka stage 4).

Stage 3 ankles were not included because CT was not routinely obtained in patients with intermediate-stage disease at our institution. The early-stage group was derived from the contralateral, asymptomatic ankles of patients who underwent bilateral sWBCT prior to surgical intervention on the affected side. Accordingly, the present study was designed as an endpoint comparison between stage 2 and stage 4 rather than as an analysis of the complete disease trajectory.

Radiographic assessment

All participants underwent standardized weight-bearing ankle radiographs (standing anteroposterior and lateral views) and a hindfoot alignment view with the foot positioned in neutral. A foot and ankle surgeon with more than 20 years of clinical experience graded ankle OA according to the Takakura–Tanaka classification (stages 1–4), blinded to the sWBCT findings and clinical information. On the weight-bearing anteroposterior ankle radiograph, the radiographic talar tilt angle (TTA–X-ray), tibial ankle surface angle (TAS), tibial–medial malleolar angle (TMM), and hindfoot alignment parameters were measured (Fig. 1A and B).

Simulated WBCT acquisition

Simulated axial-loading WBCT was performed with participants in the supine position. A 300 N axial compressive load was applied through the plantar surface and maintained throughout CT acquisition [3]. Because partial weight-bearing exceeding 25% of body weight has been shown to reproduce the sagittal foot alignment observed under full weight-bearing, the 300 N load—corresponding to approximately 51.6% of the cohort mean body weight (59.31 ± 8.80 kg)—surpassed this threshold at the cohort level [16, 17]. The scanning range encompassed both lower extremities bilaterally to ensure complete inclusion of the tibiotalar and subtalar joints. Images were reconstructed at a slice thickness of 0.625 mm and exported in DICOM format for subsequent 3D analysis.

3D reconstruction and assessment

DICOM datasets were imported into Mimics (Materialise, Belgium). The tibia, talus, and calcaneus were segmented using a threshold-based method followed by manual correction and refinement. The reconstructed models were then imported into 3-matic (Materialise) for measurements.

Measurement of ankle alignment and bone posture parameters

sWBCT-derived angular alignment and orientation parameters were assessed in the axial (Fig. 1C–E) and coronal (Fig. 1F, G) planes, consistent with previously published methods [1, 18].

3D distance-mapping–based JSW analysis

Three-dimensional surface models of the talus and calcaneus (in STL format) were imported into FEBio Studio (version 2.10.0, FEBio version 4.11; Salt Lake City, UT, USA), and a nearest-neighbor surface distance map was generated to quantify three-dimensional bone-to-bone distance across the subtalar complex (Fig. 2A–C). Detailed segmentation, surface-processing, and distance-mapping procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods.

The calcaneal surface was partitioned into four predefined regions of interest (ROIs): the anterior calcaneal articular facet (C-A), middle calcaneal articular facet (C-M), posterior calcaneal articular facet (C-P), and sinus tarsi interosseous region (C-SIN) (Fig. 2C-E). The anterior, middle, and posterior facets were interpreted as articular JSW regions, whereas C-SIN was interpreted as a measure of peritalar interosseous-space compromise because the sinus tarsi is not a cartilage-covered articular surface. Detailed anatomical ROI boundary definitions and osteophyte-related exclusion procedures are provided in Supplementary Methods.

For each ROI, distance distribution metrics, including mean distance and the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles, were extracted. Surface-burden metrics were defined as the proportion of ROI surface area with bone-to-bone distance < 3.0, < 2.0, < 1.0, and < 0.5 mm. A total subtalar aggregate metric (TOT) was calculated by pooling vertices across all four ROIs and was interpreted as a composite subtalar structural index rather than an independent articular-surface-specific measure.

Previous studies have reported subtalar JSW values of 2.0–3.84 mm in healthy adults [19, 20]. To reduce the influence of non-apposed regions and segmentation outliers in advanced deformity, analyses were restricted to vertices with JSW ≤ 4.0 mm, representing a physiologically plausible upper bound consistent with values reported in asymptomatic adults.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and Python (statsmodels version 0.14). Normality was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test, and continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (Q1, Q3) accordingly. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test when the expected cell count was < 5.

Between-group comparisons of metrics were performed using linear mixed-effects models with patient as a random intercept to account for within-subject dependence from bilateral ankle inclusion. Fixed-effect estimates (β), partial eta-squared (η²), and Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate–adjusted q-values are reported. Effect sizes were interpreted as small, medium, and large using η² thresholds of ≥ 0.01, ≥ 0.06, and ≥ 0.14, respectively.

Associations between alignment parameters and JSW indices were assessed using partial Spearman correlations controlling for disease stage. An a priori sample size estimation (G*Power 3.1.9.2) based on pilot data (n = 5 per group; effect size = 1.53; α = 0.05; power = 0.80) indicated a minimum required sample of 16 ankles [21]. Intra-observer reliability for angular measurements was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients; technical details are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Results

Reliability analyses

Intra-observer reliability for repeated measurements was excellent across both conventional imaging parameters and sWBCT-derived alignment indices (ICC, 0.963–0.997; Supplementary Table S1).

Patient characteristics

Seventeen patients with varus ankle OA were included, contributing 26 ankles (9 early-stage and 17 end-stage). Age, sex distribution, and body mass index (BMI) did not differ significantly between groups (all P ≥ .506; Table 1).

Conventional radiographic and sWBCT alignment parameters differ between early-stage and end-stage varus ankle OA

On weight-bearing ankle radiographs, the end-stage group showed significantly lower TTA–X-ray (P = .001) and TAS (P = .002), and a significantly higher TMM (P = .003), compared with the early-stage group. In contrast, hindfoot alignment on the hindfoot view did not differ significantly between groups and yielded a negligible effect size (P = .958; r = .02; Table 1).

On sWBCT, the end-stage group demonstrated a significantly higher axial calcaneotalar angle (P = .016) and a significantly lower TTA-sWBCT (P = .024). The axial tibiocalcaneal angle and the remaining sWBCT-derived alignment measures—medial calcaneal angle, subtalar posterior facet angle, subtalar inclination angle, and calcaneal inclination angle—showed no significant between-group differences (all P > .05; Table 1).

Global and regional 3D subtalar distance reductions are dominated by the sinus tarsi region

At the total subtalar complex (TOT) level, the end-stage group demonstrated significantly lower JSW distribution metrics and a greater surface burden of narrow joint space than the early-stage group. TOT-MEAN, TOT-P10, TOT-P90, TOT-ROI-3, TOT-ROI-2, and TOT-ROI-1 all showed statistically significant between-group differences that survived FDR correction (with medium-to-large effect sizes; η² = 0.202–0.348; all q ≤ 0.041; Table 2).

Regional analysis identified the sinus tarsi compartment as the dominant site of between-group structural separation. All eight C-SIN metrics differed significantly between groups after FDR correction, with uniformly large effect sizes (η² = 0.257–0.562; all q ≤ 0.024; Table 2), consistently exceeding the effect magnitudes observed at the TOT level.

No statistically significant between-group differences were detected in the C-A or C-M compartments after FDR correction (Supplementary Table S2).

Correlation structure links coronal alignment with subtalar JSW indices

After adjustment for disease stage, TTA-sWBCT showed region-specific associations with subtalar JSW indices. Within the sinus tarsi, TTA-sWBCT was independently associated with severe-narrowing burden indices (C-SIN-ROI-0.5: rp = −0.580, q = 0.009; C-SIN-ROI-1: q = 0.027; C-SIN-P10: rp = 0.494, q = 0.029; Fig. 3A, B).

Discussion

In this study, sWBCT-based 3D subtalar distance mapping identified the sinus tarsi as the dominant site of structural separation between early- and end-stage varus ankle OA. Because the sinus tarsi is not a cartilage-covered articular surface, its metrics should be interpreted as indices of peritalar interosseous-space compromise rather than cartilage-mediated joint-space narrowing. After stage adjustment, TTA-sWBCT remained associated with both sinus tarsi severe-narrowing indices and posterior-facet JSW metrics, supporting the value of regional 3D distance mapping beyond conventional single-plane alignment assessment.

Sinus tarsi–predominant structural compromise in end-stage varus ankle OA

The subtalar joint has been repeatedly implicated in the compensation–decompensation process during the progression of varus ankle OA [22]. Compensatory subtalar eversion may partially offset supramalleolar varus alignment in earlier stages, whereas later disease is accompanied by loss of compensation and peritalar structural collapse [1, 22]. Consistent with this framework, Talaski et al. reported that ankle OA requiring subtalar fusion commonly presents with sinus tarsi impingement symptoms [12]. By contrast, Lisonbee et al. found no significant sinus tarsi narrowing after tibiotalar arthrodesis or total ankle replacement, possibly reflecting their combined anterior-facet–sinus-tarsi ROI and altered postoperative mechanics [13].

Our 3D JSW mapping identified a sinus tarsi–predominant narrowing phenotype in the present native, preoperative cohort, supporting interosseous-space compromise as a structural manifestation of advanced subtalar involvement. Importantly, this interpretation was supported not only by statistical significance but also by large effect sizes: disease stage explained 26–56% of the variance in C-SIN metrics. In the context of a modest sample size, this effect-size pattern suggests that the sinus tarsi interosseous region represents a biologically meaningful site of structural separation between early- and end-stage varus ankle OA. Mechanistically, the sinus tarsi may serve as a clearance space within the talus–calcaneus complex; reduced interosseous distance may reflect diminished capacity for eversion-related accommodation and altered hindfoot loading redistribution [3, 23].

Multiplanar alignment assessment revealed distinct contributions of axial and coronal parameters to subtalar structural status. The axial calcaneotalar angle was higher in end-stage ankle OA but showed no independent association with sinus tarsi metrics after stage adjustment, suggesting a stage-discriminating rather than structure-predicting role. By contrast, coronal malalignment—particularly talar tilt—remained independently associated with subtalar JSW at both the posterior facet and sinus tarsi after stage adjustment, despite the absence of between-group differences in conventional 2D subtalar measures [7, 9, 10]. These findings reinforce varus ankle OA as a three-dimensional deformity in which 3D distance mapping captures structural relationships not detected by single-plane descriptors [2, 23, 24].

Complementary role of posterior-facet JSW as an alignment-sensitive structural marker

Unlike the sinus tarsi, the posterior facet showed no FDR-significant between-stage differences but exhibited the most consistent alignment–structure coupling after stage adjustment. This is consistent with ankle–subtalar mechanical coupling, in which supramalleolar varus deformity may continuously redistribute contact stress at the posterior facet independently of radiographic stage [5, 23]. The sinus tarsi and posterior facet thus capture complementary dimensions of subtalar involvement: between-stage interosseous-space compromise versus alignment-sensitive articular narrowing.

Clinical implications: sinus tarsi as a treatment-relevant compartment in end-stage varus ankle OA

Our findings suggest that, in end-stage varus ankle OA, subtalar compromise may be characterized by sinus tarsi–predominant narrowing rather than uniform collapse. Regional subtalar joint space assessment may therefore help anticipate residual hindfoot symptoms and guide consideration of adjunctive subtalar or hindfoot corrective strategies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single-center study with a modest sample size, which may limit generalizability and reduce power for detecting smaller regional effects outside the sinus tarsi. Although bilateral ankle inclusion introduced within-subject dependence, this was addressed using linear mixed-effects models with patient as a random intercept. A single-ankle sensitivity analysis further supported the robustness of the primary findings. Second, sWBCT was used rather than true standing WBCT. Although simulated loading protocols have been reported to be reproducible, the 300 N axial load represents approximately half of typical single-limb stance loading, and whether the sinus tarsi compartment is fully loaded under this condition remains uncertain [3]. Third, a 4.0-mm distance ceiling was applied to reduce the influence of wide, non-apposed separation values and to focus the analysis on clinically relevant narrowing burden. A no-ceiling sensitivity analysis yielded consistent findings, indicating that the primary sinus tarsi result was not driven by this threshold. Fourth, because stage 3 ankles were not included, the findings represent a cross-sectional comparison between two disease endpoints and should not be interpreted as evidence of a continuous progression pathway or as defining the timing of subtalar compensation failure. Whether sinus tarsi narrowing progresses gradually across disease stages or emerges as a threshold phenomenon requires future studies incorporating intermediate-stage patients and longitudinal follow-up. Fifth, ROI boundaries were delineated by a single observer, and the reproducibility of JSW metric extraction was not directly assessed in the present cohort. Although aggregate JSW metrics are likely less sensitive to vertex-level variability and prior studies have reported good reproducibility for comparable workflows, future work should include formal inter- and intra-observer reliability testing [19]. Finally, ligamentous status was not assessed, and ligamentous laxity may influence both alignment and JSW distribution. In addition, the current 3D distance-mapping workflow relies on specialized segmentation software, which may limit routine clinical translation; streamlined or semi-automated pipelines warrant evaluation in future studies.

Conclusions

sWBCT-based 3D distance mapping enables region-specific characterization of subtalar involvement in varus ankle OA. In this early- versus end-stage comparison, sinus tarsi interosseous-space compromise showed the most consistent between-group separation, whereas posterior-facet articular JSW was independently associated with coronal malalignment after adjustment for disease stage.
