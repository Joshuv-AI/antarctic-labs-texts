IRS: iterative reference selection improves normalization of microbiome sequencing data
Authors: Yiming Shi, Lili Liu, Jun Chen, Kristine M Wylie, Todd N Wylie, Sung Hee Park, Ruiwen Zhou, Yin Cao, Stephanie A Fritz, Molly J Stout, Maria Cristina Vazquez Guillamet, Lei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobiome studies often seek to determine how the absolute abundances of individual taxa change across biological conditions, yet sequencing read counts are sample-specific scaled representations of those abundances. Because sampling depth can differ across samples, fold changes calculated directly from sequencing read counts do not generally represent absolute-abundance fold changes. Normalization methods attempt to account for these between-sample differences in sampling depth, but their accuracy depends on the reference used. In particular, total-sum scaling uses all taxa as the reference and can introduce compositional bias. Reference-based methods instead rely on taxa that are stable across conditions, but contamination of the reference set by differentially abundant (DA) taxa can distort sampling-depth estimation and downstream inference. Here, we present iterative reference selection (IRS), a robust normalization method that iteratively screens and refines a candidate reference set to exclude DA taxa. By deriving a clean reference set, IRS accurately captures between-sample differences in sampling depth and recovers absolute-abundance fold changes. Benchmarking using simulations and datasets with experimental absolute quantification shows that IRS outperforms standard scaling and existing reference-based methods in controlling false discovery rates while maintaining power.

Introduction

Microbiome studies often seek to determine how the absolute abundances of individual taxa change across biological conditions. Because taxon-specific absolute-abundance measurements are not routinely available at scale, most studies rely on high-throughput sequencing to profile many taxa simultaneously [1]. However, sequencing read counts are not measured on the absolute scale of the original specimens. The total microbial load represents the aggregate absolute abundance of all taxa in the original specimen, whereas the total read count refers to the total number of sequencing reads generated for that sample, summed across all taxa. We define sampling depth as the ratio of total read count to total microbial load—i.e. the number of sequencing reads generated per unit of microbial biomass in the original specimen [2]. Each taxon’s absolute abundance is scaled by this sample-specific sampling depth to generate its observed read count. In multiplexed sequencing, libraries are commonly balanced before pooling so that samples contribute reasonably similar numbers of sequenceable DNA molecules rather than amounts directly proportional to their original microbial loads [2]. Consequently, samples with different total microbial loads may receive similar total read counts and therefore have different sampling depths [2, 3]. Although a natural first approach is to compare taxon-specific read counts directly across samples, the resulting fold change reflects both the biological change in the taxon and differences in sampling depth across samples and therefore cannot generally be interpreted as an absolute-abundance fold change [3].

A common alternative is to divide each taxon count by the sample’s total read count, as in total-sum scaling (TSS) [2], and compare the resulting relative abundances. Although TSS removes variation in total read count from the comparison, the resulting proportions are constrained to sum to one. Under this unit-sum constraint, expansion of one or more taxa increases the common denominator and reduces the relative abundances of other taxa, even when their absolute abundances remain unchanged [4, 5]. Such compositional squeezing can attenuate genuine changes and create apparent changes in otherwise stable taxa, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Consequently, relative-abundance fold changes do not generally represent absolute-abundance fold changes. Following Clark-Boucher et al. [6], we use compositional bias to denote the discrepancy between normalized and absolute-abundance log-fold changes. We formalize this discrepancy for reference-set normalization below. Importantly, under the sampling model described below, correcting compositional bias and estimating between-sample differences in sampling depth provide two algebraically linked formulations of the same scale-recovery problem, with absolute-abundance log-fold changes as their common target (see Supplementary Note 1 for details). We therefore formulate normalization primarily as the problem of estimating relative sampling depths and use this perspective throughout the remainder of the paper. Other normalization and transformation methods seek to account for differences in sampling depth include cumulative sum scaling (CSS) [2, 7], the median-ratio approach used by DESeq2 [8], and the centered log-ratio (CLR) transformation [9]. These methods retain all reads, but their ability to recover absolute changes still depends on the assumptions used to define the normalization scale.

Although sequencing data lack an absolute scale, an approximate absolute scale can be established experimentally by combining sequencing with either an external calibration standard or an independent measurement of total microbial load. Specifically, spike-in methods involve adding a known quantity of synthetic or exogenous DNA to a sample prior to sequencing to provide a calibration scale [10, 11]. Alternatively, flow cytometry provides independent quantification of total microbial density by directly counting cells per unit volume or mass [12]. However, these experimental solutions are labor-intensive, costly, and often technically infeasible for large-scale studies. This motivates the need for computational approaches that can approximate absolute-abundance normalization when direct experimental measurements are unavailable or impractical.

When direct measurements of total microbial load are unavailable, reference-based scaling provides a computational alternative inspired by the spike-in logic. Rather than normalizing each sample by the total abundance of all taxa, these methods use an internal reference: a subset of indigenous taxa assumed to be nondifferential, and therefore stable in absolute abundance across conditions. The observed variation of these stable taxa can be used to estimate how sampling depth differs across samples. Normalizing other taxa against this stable reference can then recover their absolute-abundance fold changes without additional laboratory measurements, provided that the stability assumption is valid [13]. Two representative approaches are DACOMP [13] and RSim [14]. DACOMP selects the reference set by finding features with the most stable log-ratios to all other taxa, while RSim identifies a stable reference via pairwise rank similarity. A persistent challenge is reference contamination: selected references may still include differentially abundant (DA) taxa. When this occurs, the resulting normalization does not fully correct the variation in sampling depths, undermining the intended adjustment and potentially degrading downstream inference.

The fundamental challenge is that sequencing data alone do not identify how sampling depth differs across samples. To address this identification problem, we propose iterative reference selection (IRS), a label-informed reference-learning and bias-correction procedure that iteratively identifies taxa with stable absolute abundances and uses them as internal references to estimate these between-sample differences in sampling depth. Correcting observed read-count fold changes for the estimated differences in sampling depth removes the sample-specific scaling and enables recovery of taxon-specific fold changes on the absolute-abundance scale. Existing reference-based methods treat reference identification as a one-shot procedure, whereas IRS frames it as a bias-removal process driven by successive refinement. Together, this work establishes IRS as a robust and general solution for reference-based normalization that improves the accuracy and reliability of differential-abundance analysis (DAA) across a wide range of microbiome datasets and analytical frameworks.

Algorithm: iterative reference selection

The IRS method is a label-informed reference-based normalization procedure. Starting from an initial reference set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${R}_0$\end{document} that may contain both DA and non-DA taxa—such as all taxa passing basic filtering under TSS—IRS proceeds iteratively. At iteration t, given a current reference set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${R}_t$\end{document}, IRS (i) calculates the reference total for each sample; (ii) constructs the taxon-specific effective reference total \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${T}_{i,j}^{R_t}$\end{document} defined below, uses it to normalize the observed counts or as a log offset in count-based models, and tests each taxon for DA; and (iii) updates the reference set to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${R}_{t+1}$\end{document} by retaining taxa that are not significantly differential according to the chosen tests.

Conceptually, each reference set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${R}_t$\end{document} induces a reference shift \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\Delta}^{R_t}$\end{document}, defined as the group-wise log-ratio of the total absolute abundance of reference taxa. When many reference taxa are actually DA, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\Delta}^{R_t}$\end{document} can be far from zero and introduces a bias into all normalized log-fold changes. By repeatedly removing taxa that show strong deviations from the current reference, the aggregate reference shift \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\Delta}^{R_t}$\end{document} tends to move toward zero. As a result, IRS-normalized log-fold changes become closer to the underlying absolute log-fold changes.

In practice, the iteration stops when the reference composition changes minimally over several consecutive iterations, or when a maximum number of iterations (e.g. 50) is reached. In all simulations (Figs 2–4; Extended Data Figs S1–S3) and absolute-abundance datasets analyzed (Figs 5 and 6), IRS stabilizes in a small number of iterations and substantially reduces the discrepancy between relative-abundance and absolute-abundance log-fold changes (Extended Data Fig. S4). These empirical results suggest that, in realistic microbiome settings with heterogeneous effect sizes, iterative removal of DA taxa is effective at suppressing reference-induced bias, yielding a stable reference set for downstream differential analysis.

Initialization of the reference taxa set

IRS starts with all taxa as the reference, which is equivalent to TSS.

As demonstrated in our simulations and Fig. 1, TSS introduces a systematic bias that shifts the log-fold changes but preserves the shape of the mixture components. Consequently, strongly DA taxa remain distinguishable from background noise. In the first step, the test statistic identifies and removes these prominent DA features. Although the initial bias \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\Delta}^R$\end{document} may mask taxa with smaller effect sizes—causing them to remain temporarily in the reference—the exclusion of the strongest DA drivers reduces the systematic reference shift. This refinement unmasks the remaining DA taxa in subsequent iterations, progressively purifying the reference set.

However, a very small reference can lead to unstable normalization. To decide whether to proceed, we apply an empirical diagnostic used in this study. For each sample, compute the reference mass fraction: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${f}_{\mathrm{ref},i}=\frac{\sum_{k\in R}{Y}_{ik}}{\sum_k{Y}_{ik}}$\end{document} and examine its distribution across samples. If \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\mathrm{Median}\left({f}_{\mathrm{ref},i}\right)<0.2$\end{document}, we recommend avoiding reference-based normalization. Additional discussion of this empirical diagnostic and alternative thresholds is provided in the Discussion section.

Bias removal efficiency discussion

For the IRS method to successfully mitigate bias in DAA, several key conditions must hold. First, a robust differential analysis model is required during iterations to accurately identify and remove DA taxa. Second, a sufficiently large proportion of non-DA taxa is needed to maintain stability for normalization. Overly strong DA signals across all taxa could result in large initial bias term \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} ${\Delta}^{R_0}$\end{document} when the TSS method is used to define the initial reference set. This large bias, in turn, causes non-DA taxa to be incorrectly classified as DA and subsequently excluded early in the process. This premature exclusion ultimately leads to a biased reference set in subsequent iterations. A detailed discussion of these considerations is provided in our supplementary materials.

For reference selection, we recommend applying both the Poisson–sandwich GLM and the Kendall rank correlation test. Any taxon deemed significant by either test is removed from the reference set. That is, a taxon remains in the reference set only if both tests produce P-values greater than the chosen threshold.

This combined strategy is motivated by the complementary strengths of the two approaches. The Poisson–sandwich GLM is a Poisson regression model that uses a robust (sandwich) estimator to correct standard errors for overdispersion. In Shi et al. [30], we demonstrated that this model provides high statistical power when applied to microbiome DAA. However, as with other parametric models, it can be sensitive to outliers.

In contrast, the Kendall rank correlation test is a nonparametric method that assesses monotonic associations based on data ranks rather than raw values. As a result, it is inherently robust to outliers and distributional assumptions. Its main limitation, however, is relatively lower statistical power compared with parametric alternatives.

By combining the two methods, we leverage their complementary advantages: the Poisson–sandwich GLM contributes sensitivity and power, while the Kendall test adds robustness against outliers. Unlike standard DAA, where combining tests risks inflating false discoveries, reference selection prioritizes the minimization of contamination. In the context of reference selection, including DA taxa in the reference set can introduce massive bias, while falsely discarding a few truly null taxa merely reduces the reference size without introducing bias. We therefore exclude any taxon that is significant in either test and retain only those that are nonsignificant in both. Because the two methods tend to agree on strongly differential taxa, this dual filter substantially reduces reference contamination while still preserving a sufficiently large and stable reference pool.

Results

Simulation: data generation approach

We benchmarked IRS through simulation studies. Following previous work, we generated microbiome datasets using a Dirichlet-multinomial (DM) model. The model parameters were estimated from real microbiome datasets and were used to define the count-generating mechanism in the simulations. The details are provided in Supplementary Note 2.

Remark on the Dirichlet-multinomial simulator

We use the DM model only as a convenient compositional count generator calibrated to empirical mean composition and overdispersion, not as a mechanistic model of microbial dependence or an estimate of the true underlying microbial dispersion matrix. Consistent with Mosimann [15] (Theorem 2 and the subsequent discussion and examples), correlations among normalized proportions can arise from the constrained relative scale itself. Additional details are provided in Supplementary Note 2.

Simulations: normalization benchmarks

To evaluate normalization performance under varying degrees of compositional bias, we considered the fraction of DA taxa ranging from low (10%) to mild (20%) and high (30%). IRS was compared with traditional scaling methods TSS, CSS [7], CLR [16], GMPR [17], TMM [18], and median [19], as well as reference-based methods DACOMP [13] and RSim [14]. We also included an Oracle reference, which was constructed using the true nondifferential taxa, to represent the best possible performance attainable by any reference-based normalization. TSS can be viewed as a special case of reference-based normalization that uses all taxa as references, while the Oracle reference includes only the true non-DA taxa.

One hundred datasets were generated from a DM distribution. Each dataset contained 200 samples and 1500 operational taxonomic units (OTUs). The details of simulation setting are provided in Supplementary Note 3. Additional small-sample simulations (n = 20, 50, 100) are provided in Supplementary Note 4.

Six normalization metrics precede downstream testing (Fig. 2a–f): (i) log2 fold-change mean-squared error against absolute abundances; (ii) mean absolute log error (MALE) of estimated relative sampling depths; (iii) Spearman correlation between estimated and true relative sampling depths; (iv) systematic bias, the absolute difference in mean log relative-sampling-depth estimation error between groups; (v) reference-set size for IRS, DACOMP, and RSim; and (vi) differential-abundance rate within the reference set—the proportion of truly DA taxa included by mistake (also referred to as “reference contamination” [13]). The detailed formula of each metric is provided in Supplementary Note 5.

Accuracy and stability

We first assessed the ability of each method to recover true absolute fold changes. Under conditions of high signal density (30%), existing methods failed to account for systematic differences in sampling depth across samples, resulting in high mean squared error (MSE) for log2 fold changes. In contrast, IRS effectively removed this bias, achieving minimal MSE (0.16) comparable to the Oracle reference (Fig. 2a). This accuracy was driven by robust estimation of relative sampling depths: IRS consistently yielded relative sampling-depth estimates that strongly correlated with the true relative sampling depths (Spearman \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{upgreek} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document} $\hat{\mathrm{\rho}}\approx 1.0$\end{document}; Fig. 2c) and minimized the MALE (Fig. 2b). Notably, while the performance of competing methods degraded significantly as compositional bias increased, IRS remained stable across all signal densities.

Reduction of systematic bias

To measure this, we adopted the “systematic bias” metric established by the RSim study [14], defined as the absolute difference in mean relative-sampling-depth estimation error between groups. While alternative methods displayed substantial systematic bias (≥0.20) that increased with signal density, IRS maintained negligible bias (0.03–0.05), effectively matching the Oracle benchmark (Fig. 2d). This demonstrated that IRS prevented the confounding of biological signals with technical differences in sampling depth.

Mechanism | minimizing reference contamination

The superior performance of IRS stemmed from its ability to identify a clean reference set. A major challenge for reference-based methods is reference contamination—the accidental inclusion of DA taxa in the reference set. In the high-signal scenario, DACOMP and RSim suffered from elevated contamination rates. IRS, by iteratively refining the candidate set, achieved a substantially lower differential abundance (DA) rate within the reference (0.06 versus competitors ≥0.29) (Fig. 2f). Although IRS selected a slightly smaller reference set than DACOMP (Fig. 2e), its lower contamination rate ensured that the selected references remained independent of experimental covariates, preserving the validity of downstream testing.

Downstream differential abundance analysis

To translate normalization performance into biological discovery, we evaluated how each method affected downstream DA testing. To ensure a comprehensive and model-agnostic assessment, we used DESeq2 (assuming negative binomial distribution) and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test as representative parametric and nonparametric approaches, respectively. Features with Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) adjusted P-values below .05 were considered significantly differential between groups. The evaluation metrics were false discovery rate (FDR), true positive rate (TPR), and F1 score; full formulas are provided in Supplementary Note 5: Simulation evaluation metrics 7–9.

False discovery rate control

We first examined FDR control. Under BH adjustment, the expected FDR should approximate 0.05. Under parametric testing (DESeq2), existing methods exhibited severe FDR inflation due to error in estimating relative sampling depths. Alternative reference-based methods (DACOMP and RSim) outperformed standard scaling, confirming the general utility of anchoring to internal references. Despite this improvement, their performance degraded as signal density increased (Fig. 3a). In contrast, IRS maintained robust FDR control (0.03–0.10) across all settings and closely tracked the Oracle reference (Fig. 3a and b). This confirmed that the clean reference set identified by IRS effectively neutralized the spurious correlations that drove false discoveries.

Sensitivity and overall performance

Controlling FDR often comes at the cost of statistical power. To evaluate this trade-off, we analyzed the TPR and F1 score. In the parametric framework, IRS achieved the highest sensitivity and effectively matched the Oracle benchmark (Fig. 3c). Under the nonparametric Wilcoxon test, TPR values were comparable across most methods (Fig. 3d). This suggested that rank-based tests are inherently more resilient to scaling artifacts, though they are not immune. When combining precision and recall into the F1 score, IRS consistently outperformed all competing methods across both testing frameworks and all signal densities (Fig. 3e and f). Collectively, these results demonstrated that IRS improved valid discovery rates without sacrificing statistical power.

Simulations: differential abundance performance across frameworks

We assessed the integration of our IRS normalization across several established DAA frameworks and compared its performance in addressing compositional bias against each model’s built-in bias-correction strategy.

The evaluation metrics were FDR, TPR, and F1 score with details given in Supplementary Note 5. The DAA methods considered in this evaluation included DESeq2, MaAsLin2, MaAsLin3, ZicoSeq, DACOMP, edgeR (negative binomial and quasi-likelihood), ALDEx2, LDM, LINDA, and ANCOM-BC2 [20–26]. All the listed methods supported a default built-in normalization method. Model details and software versions appear in Supplementary Note 6.

In this section, 100 simulated datasets based on the Adenomas dataset were generated [27]. The details of simulation settings are provided in Supplementary Note 3. Similar patterns and conclusions were observed in additional small-sample simulations (Supplementary Note 4).

Figure 4 compares DAA frameworks run with IRS normalization versus each method’s default normalization in the Adenomas setting. Rows show FDR, TPR, and F1 score; columns show signal density δ =0.1,0.2,0.3. ANCOM-BC2 was run using its default SS-filter and Holm–Bonferroni-adjusted P < .05, whereas the other frameworks were summarized using BH-adjusted P < .05.

False discovery rate control

We first examined whether IRS controlled the FDR inflation commonly observed in standard DAA tools. In the “Adenomas Benchmark” (Fig. 4), methods relying on default normalization frequently exceeded the nominal FDR threshold (0.05), particularly at higher signal densities. When coupled with IRS, FDR performance improved universally. The most dramatic recoveries were observed for MaAsLin2, DACOMP, ALDEx2, and LDM, where severe FDR inflation was corrected to near-nominal levels. Notably, while the default ZicoSeq and DACOMP performed well at low signal density, their control degraded as the signal increased; supplementing them with IRS stabilized performance across all densities. Although negative-binomial based methods (DESeq2, edgeR) remained above the 0.05 threshold due to their inherent distributional assumptions, IRS consistently reduced their error rates compared to default scaling.

Sensitivity and F1 performance

Our gain in precision did not compromise sensitivity. IRS preserved or increased the TPR across all IRS-applicable frameworks. Consequently, when evaluating the F1 score (the balance of precision and recall), IRS-enhanced workflows consistently outperformed their default counterparts. We specifically highlighted the comparison between MaAsLin2 (with IRS) and its successor MaAsLin3 (native). While MaAsLin3 utilized a sophisticated native hurdle model, MaAsLin2 coupled with IRS achieved superior FDR control and higher TPR, particularly in high-signal scenarios. This suggested that modular improvements to normalization can sometimes outperform architectural changes to the statistical model.

Generalizability across cohorts

To ensure that these findings are not artifacts of a specific dataset structure, we expanded the evaluation to the “Multi-Cohort Benchmark,” replicating the analysis using parameters derived from three additional diverse studies (HMP Stool, Asnicar, and MetaCardis Dataset descriptions appear in Supplementary Note 7, and cohort-specific settings and exact parameter values are provided in Extended Figure captions). The results were highly consistent with the Adenomas setting: across all cohorts and signal densities, integrating IRS reduced FDR toward the nominal target, maintained statistical power, and yielded the highest overall F1 scores (Extended Data Figs S1–S3). This confirmed that IRS served as a robust, model-agnostic upgrade for correcting sampling depth variation in microbiome studies.

Real-data validation: Vandeputte_QMP dataset

To assess our method’s performance on real microbiome data, we next analyzed datasets with experimentally measured absolute microbial abundances. In recent years, such measurements have become increasingly available, providing essential ground-truth data for validating DA analyses. A common approach is flow cytometry (single-cell counting). As introduced previously, flow cytometry provides cell counts per gram or milliliter and captures total microbial load differences across samples [12]. It allows linking sequencing-based relative abundances to per-sample absolute bacterial concentrations.

We first analyzed the stool dataset of Vandeputte et al., hereafter referred to as the Vandeputte_QMP (Quantitative Microbiome Profiling) dataset [12]. The study included flow-cytometry–based cell counts alongside 16S rRNA gene sequencing. After standard filtering, the dataset contained 95 samples and 166 taxa (prevalence >5%).

To define a “truth set” of DA taxa, we performed the Wilcoxon rank-sum test on the absolute abundance data. We validated the statistical propriety of this approach via permutation analysis (see Supplementary Note 8), which confirmed strict FDR control. Consequently, the 59 taxa identified as significant in the absolute data constituted the ground truth for downstream benchmarking.

Applying our proposed IRS normalization to the 16S rRNA gene sequence count data, we identified a reference set of 21 taxa. None of them was differentially abundant in the absolute-abundance analysis. Next, we compared competing normalization methods on the 16S data to assess how well each recovered the known positives. Following the same two-step strategy as in our simulations, we (i) evaluated normalization quality across competing methods and (ii) integrated each normalization into differential-abundance models for comparison with their built-in normalization.

We normalized the 16S gene counts using all methods included in the simulation study. We re-analyzed the normalized data with both the Wilcoxon rank-sum test and DESeq2 (negative binomial–based method). Incorporating DESeq2 ensured that our evaluation was not confounded by the choice of statistical framework.

Results are summarized in Fig. 5. We compared the number of significant taxa detected from normalized 16S data with the truth set (those identified using absolute abundances), treating overlap with the truth set as true positives given the effective FDR control of the Wilcoxon test. Using DESeq2 (Fig. 5a, top), IRS achieved the highest overlap, recovering 51 true positives—matching DACOMP (51) and slightly exceeding TMM (50), with all other methods performing worse. Using the Wilcoxon test (Fig. 5a, bottom), IRS again performed best, recovering all 59 true positives, followed closely by DACOMP and TMM.

The log2 fold-changes estimated from absolute abundances versus normalized data using DESeq2 are compared by MSE (Fig. 5b). IRS yielded the lowest MSE among all methods, followed by DACOMP and TMM. The result was consistent with Fig. 5a. In Fig. 5c, we applied IRS within each differential analysis model and compared its performance to the model’s built-in normalization method. Across all models, IRS consistently increased the number of true positives detected relative to built-in normalization. Together, these results aligned with our simulation findings and showed that IRS reliably identified a large, clean set of nondifferential taxa, enabling stable reference-based normalization that effectively reduced the compositional bias.

Real-data validation: MetaCardis dataset (absolute-abundance subset)

To further demonstrate the generality of our conclusions, we analyzed a dataset with experimentally measured absolute microbial abundance—a subset of the MetaCardis cohort [28]. In this subset, microbial load was quantified using flow cytometry. For our analysis, we focused on the type 2 diabetes (n = 536) and healthy control (n = 275) groups and 690 taxa (filtered by prevalence >5%).

Applying the same benchmarking criteria used for the Vandeputte dataset, we first established the ground truth. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test on absolute abundances identified 473 DA taxa. Given the strict FDR control confirmed by our permutation analysis (see Supplementary Note 8), these 473 taxa served as the ground truth for this validation.

When applied to the relative abundance data, IRS identified a reference set of 144 taxa. Notably, only four of these were differentially abundant in the absolute-abundance analysis, indicating that IRS successfully selected stable reference anchors even in complex clinical datasets. We followed the same analysis pipeline as for the Vandeputte et al. dataset. Results shown in Fig. 6 are consistent with our earlier findings. The DESeq2-based analysis showed that IRS detected 258 taxa overlapping with the absolute-abundance truth set, while other methods detected at most 153 true positives (Fig. 6a, top panel). Using the Wilcoxon test, IRS performed comparably to GMPR and TMM in recovering true positives (Fig. 6a, bottom panel). Notably, when comparing log2 fold-change MSE between absolute and normalized abundances, IRS achieved substantially lower MSE than all alternative methods (Fig. 6b). Furthermore, IRS consistently improved detection power across all applicable DA models (Fig. 6c).

The consistency of results across two independent real datasets underscored the robustness and generalizability of IRS and demonstrated that IRS effectively account for variation in sampling depths and improved the accuracy of DAA in diverse experimental settings.

Additional robustness analyses are provided in the Supplementary Information. These include global-null permutation diagnostics (Supplementary Note 9; Supplementary Figs S1–S3), small-sample simulations (Supplementary Note 4; Supplementary Fig. S4), an equal-effect high-signal-density stress test (Supplementary Note 10; Supplementary Fig. S5), and a Vandeputte-calibrated high-DA benchmark (Supplementary Note 11; Supplementary Figs S6–S8).

Discussion

IRS provides a practical framework for learning a stable internal reference and addressing sampling depth variation in microbiome DAA. Because IRS uses the covariate of interest during reference construction, it is best viewed as a supervised component of the DA pipeline rather than as a purely label-agnostic normalization preprocessor. Across diverse simulations and two datasets with absolute-abundance ground truth, IRS accurately estimates relative sampling depths, achieves near-Oracle recovery of absolute-abundance log2 fold changes, reduces systematic bias, and improves FDR control while preserving sensitivity. Its performance is driven by the selection of large, clean reference sets that provide a reliable internal scale for estimating relative sampling depths. Replacing each DAA model’s built-in normalization with IRS improves F1 scores, supporting its use as a practical option when experimental absolute quantification is unavailable.

Reference-based normalization requires a sufficient pool of non-DA taxa. When a majority of taxa are differentially abundant, reference-based normalization methods (TSS, DACOMP, RSim, and IRS) may perform poorly. This failure occurs because a sparse reference set cannot reliably estimate differences in sampling depth across samples. As an empirical diagnostic used in this study, if counts from selected reference taxa contribute under 20% of total read counts, we recommend avoiding normalization based on that selected reference set (including DACOMP, RSim, and IRS). Of note, a survey of 38 two-group 16S datasets reported that 0.8%–40.5% of taxa are differentially abundant [29], implying a substantial pool of non-DA taxa in typical studies. In such settings, IRS converges to a clean reference set. Overall, results indicate that IRS offers robust, reference-based normalization for microbiome data.
