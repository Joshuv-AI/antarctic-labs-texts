Circulating metabolome as key biomarkers for ventilator-associated pneumonia: Case–Control study in a tertiary care hospital of North India
Authors: Ajay Kumar Gautam, Rahul Yadav, Bikram Kumar Gupta, Nidhi Tiwari, Chandan Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is a microbial infection that develops in the lungs when a patient has been intubated for mechanical ventilation for more than 48 h. Diagnosis of VAP remains a challenge despite clinical advances. NMR-based metabolomics might play a pivotal role in overcoming the diagnostic complexity of VAP by investigating biomarkers.Materials and Methods:This case–control study was conducted in 2024–2025 at Sir Sunder Lal Hospital, Varanasi. The participated patients had an intubation period of more than 48 h; a total of 44 patients were recruited, of which 20 patients developed VAP and 24 were non-VAP patients. NMR spectra of serum were recorded, and statistical analysis was done.Results:The 2D scatter plot of PC1 versus PC2 scores clearly shows a distinction between the groups, with the principal component analysis explaining 18.9% of the variance. Partial least-squares discriminant analysis demonstrated R2 and Q2 values of 0.328 and 0.0552, respectively, indicating a probability to model fit. A total of 27 distinct metabolites were identified. Univariate receiver operating characteristic curve analysis identified phenylalanine (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.720), acetoacetate (AUC = 0.706), and glutamate (AUC = 0.690) as significant metabolites. With AUC values within the fair accuracy range, these three metabolites are considered acceptable biomarkers for the diagnosis of VAP.Conclusion:The elevated metabolites indicate lung injury and perturbed inflammatory pathways, which may result from invasive mechanical ventilation. We conclude that the levels of phenylalanine, acetoacetate, and glutamate may serve as biomarkers and aid in the diagnosis of VAP, disease monitoring, and therapeutic stratification.

INTRODUCTION

Critically ill patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) have higher chances of developing nosocomial infections as compared to other hospital areas, especially ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), the most common and serious complication.[1] For VAP, infection develops in the pulmonary parenchyma of the lungs after 48 h of tracheal intubation during mechanical ventilation support.[2] The incidence rates of VAP lie in the range of 20%–36% among the critically ill patients. Various factors influence the range, such as mechanical ventilation, underlying disease severity, older age, supine position, comorbidity, previous medication, trauma, etc. Intubation contributes to more than 95% cases and become primary risk factor.[3] This invasive procedure disrupts the lung microbiome, structural, and physiological state of tissue.

The intubation by endotracheal tube (ETT) may damage the epithelial layer, disrupt the natural protective mechanisms of the upper respiratory tract, and hinder the natural mechanism of coughing, swallowing, and mucociliary clearance, facilitating the direct access of environmental pathogens to the tracheobronchial region.[4] The nasal sinuses, oropharynx, and stomach can act as potential reservoirs for pooled secretions and contain a variety of endogenous pathogens, which may cross the cuff and result in silent microaspiration. The use of an ETT can induce ventilator-associated lung injury (VALI), trigger a systemic inflammatory response, and compromise the host's immune defense against pathogens. In addition, the pathogen on the surface of ETT and in the environment of the ICU may directly inoculate in the lower respiratory tract and cause reinfection. Eventually, the biofilm formation begins around the ETT or in the respiratory tract within hours postintubation and is expected to precede the onset of VAP.[5] This global root cause of VAP makes the treatment of VAP difficult, even with suitable antibiotic therapy, and is responsible for longer stay on ventilation, contributing to a higher risk of reinfection and mortality.[6]

The mortality rate of VAP patients is high (43%), and causative pathogens are Gram-negative bacteria such as Enterobacterales, Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumonia, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Stenotrophomonas maltophilia, and Gram-positive bacteria like Staphylococcus aureus. Fungal colonization with Candida is also observed. The pathogens in the early-onset of VAP include Streptococcus pneumonia, enteric Gram-negative bacilli (Enterobacterales), and Methicillin-susceptible Staphylococcus aureus, which are antibiotic-sensitive and lead to a good prognosis. The main culprits for VAP severity are multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria during the late-onset of VAP, such as P. aeruginosa, methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, A. baumannii, and extended-spectrum beta-lactamase-producing bacteria.[78] Risk factors include previous history of medical procedures of the respiratory system, infection, prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation, multiple trauma, sepsis, burns, central nervous disorder, metabolic disorder, male gender, older age, and smoking. These multiple risk factors have an influence on each other rather than an independent influence on disease severity.[910] Diagnosis of VAP is complex and subtle.

In this study, clinical pulmonary infection score (CPIS), a scoring method is applied to deduce the diagnosis of VAP, which relies on the clinical criteria, radiological, and microbiological methods; lung ultrasound is also considered sometimes. This scoring system is a time-consuming process and prone to error because it is difficult to differentiate the VAP from hospital-acquired pneumonia and other types of pneumonia.[1112] Due to pathobiological heterogeneity, there is no gold standard method, and diagnosis remains the challenging part for initiating the treatment of VAP, which delays antibiotic therapy and leads to longer stays in the ICU and high mortality among critically ill patients.[13] There is a need to develop biomarker-based methods for monitoring VAP.[1415] A systems biological approach can help in identifying certain biomarkers that can serve as a monitoring tool for disease development. NMR spectroscopy is a robust analytical method in the systems biology approach, which gives reproducible data of metabolites with high accuracy.[16171819] Here, we investigate and compare the serum metabolites of patients having VAP with other intubated non-VAP (NVAP) patients by NMR spectroscopy and look out for the perfect biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognosis of VAP.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study protocol

In this case–control study, all patients were recruited from the ICUs under the Department of Anaesthesiology at Centenary Super Specialty Block of Sir Sundarlal Hospital, Institute of Medical Sciences, BHU, Varanasi, a tertiary healthcare hospital in North India. The written consent form was provided to the representatives of the participants. Serum was collected from VAP and NVAP patients. The spectra of all the samples were recorded at the Sophisticated Analytical and Technical Help Institute, BHU. Multivariate analysis was done on the NMR data of metabolites, and suitable biomarkers were discovered.

Patient selection, clinical information, and sample collection

Patients undergoing mechanical ventilation in the ICU were enrolled in the study. After 48 h, an endotracheal or bronchoalveolar lavage fluid sample was collected and sent for microbial investigation. Patients having positive culture reports for pathogen were classified in the VAP group (disease), and other patients having normal throat flora or sterile culture reports were classified in the NVAP group (control). Patients who had pre-existing infection or pneumonia and a history of antibiotic therapy before ICU admission were excluded. The clinical data, such as CPIS score and other clinical findings, were collected at the time of sample collection. Blood was collected in a plain vial and then allowed to rest at room temperature for half an hour. Centrifugation will be done at 2000 g for 10 min. The upper layer of yellow-colored serum was isolated, aliquoted, and stored in a −80°C refrigerator until the whole batch was ready to perform NMR spectroscopy.

Sample preparation

A batch of serum samples stored in-80°C refrigerator was removed, thawed gently for half an hour at 4°C in the refrigerator, followed by cool down to room temperature.[1] Subsequently, serum was taken approximately 500 µl and shifted in 5 mm Wilmad® NMR precision tube. A coaxial insert holding a solution of 0.06 mM trimethylsilyl-2,2,3,3-tetradeuterium propionate (TSP) prepared in 10% deuterium oxide (D₂O) was inserted in the NMR tube to assist as an external standard and chemical shift reference for spectral calibration.

Recording of 1D 1H NMR spectrum of all serum sample

NMR spectrum of all samples recorded at 600 MHz Bruker-Avance Neo, NMR spectrometer with a cryogenically cooled triple-resonance TCI probe was used to get the NMR spectra. A relaxation-edited Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence with water presaturation was used to create one-dimensional (1H 1D) spectra. To achieve macromolecular suppression, a T2 filter was incorporated into the CPMG segment, which was configured with 126 loops (L4) and an individual echo time (2τ) of 0.616 ms, providing a total echo time (TE) of 77.62 ms. Every measurement was carried out at 298 K with a 90° flip angle, a 15 ppm spectral width, and a 5-s relaxation time.[2021] Each NMR spectrum was obtained from 32 transients collected at 2.75 s acquisition time (Tacq) across 128 scans with 64k data points. At 0.00 ppm, the TSP signal served as a reference. Metabolite resonances detected in the 1H NMR spectrum were confirmed through COSY and heteronuclear techniques such as HSQC. For the ¹H–¹≥C HSQC experiments, spectra were acquired using 24 scans across 826 increments, yielding 2k data points. The spectral range was 16 ppm for proton and 165 ppm for carbon.[22]

Spectra processing and integration

1D 1H-NMR spectra of all samples were recorded, and Fourier transformation was used to convert time-domain signals into the frequency domain. To enhance the signal-to-noise ratio, apodization using an exponential multiplication corresponding to a 0.3 Hz line expansion was employed. Then, corrected the phase to make sure symmetric peak shapes and accurate chemical shift representation. Baseline correction was then applied to remove any distortions and preserve spectral integrity. Chemical shift axis calibration was carried out using a reference standard to enable precise metabolite identification. The chemical shifts and relative resonance intensities of low-molecular-weight metabolites were determined by manual spectral integration with respect to TSP, using Chemical shifts and relative resonance intensities of low-molecular-weight metabolites were determined by manual spectral integration with respect to TSP using TopSpin 4.3.0 software (Bruker Corporation, Billerica, Massachusetts, USA). Identification and evidence of minor metabolites resonances shown in the 1H NMR spectrum were further validated by COSY and HSQC experiments.[232425]

Statistical analyses

MetaboAnalyst 6.0 was used to do extensive studies in order to find potential distinguishing metabolites between the VAP and NVAP groups.[26] The dataset, which included 27 metabolite concentrations for 1H NMR, was normalized using log transformation base 10, sum, and Pareto scaling [Supplementary Figure 1].[27] Before normalization, the data set should be subjected to strict filtering criteria. For example, difference filtering is a useful method for boosting metabolite counts that are differentially detected. The simulation study demonstrates that variance-based filtering increases statistical power while preserving control over the false discovery rate (FDR) when paired with an FDR correction. As long as the majority are not anticipated to be differentially expressed, eliminating 50% of probe sets seems appropriate.[28] To examine metabolic variance in 1H NMR spectral data, principal component analysis (PCA) was first performed, followed by partial least squares-discriminant analysis (PLS-DA), and orthogonal PLS-DA (OPLS-DA).. Finally, we used MetaboAnalyst 6.0 to perform pathway analysis.[26] Using pathway database that is Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database,[29] metabolic pathways associated with altered metabolites were identified based on the number of matched metabolites (hits >1) and statistical significance (P < 0.05). Designed for each pathway node, betweenness centrality and network degree were calculated to characterize its topological importance within the metabolic network. A metabolite’s degree is determined by how many associations it forms with the other metabolites in the network. A metabolite’s importance is measured by its betweenness for every pair of node connections. Metabolites with betweenness values >35 and node degrees >5 were considered relevant to distinguish between the NVAP and VAP’s metabolite profiles; their aberrant concentration influences the concentrations of other metabolites in the network. Finally, metabolite set enrichment analysis was conducted to determine whether functionally related metabolites from different pathways showed coordinated alterations and formed integrated metabolic networks. This approach avoids reliance on arbitrary cutoff values, enabling the detection of subtle but meaningful metabolic shifts often missed by conventional analyses. The findings were visualized using corresponding P values and fold enrichment.

Most of the patients had etiology of pulmonary diseases (27.27%) and neurological disorders (25%) [Supplementary Figure 2], of which smoking comorbidity (50%) was high among the disease group. All VAP patients get infected with multidrug-resistant (MDR) pathogens, in which P. aeruginosa and A. baumannii have a high frequency of infection because of their high baseline resistance to carbapenems [Supplementary Figure 3].

For biomarker confirmation, a “univariate receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis” evaluates a single variable’s (or biomarker’s) diagnostic potential using a ROC curve. In order to determine how well a variable distinguishes between those who are ill and those who are not, it plots sensitivity against 1-specificity across a range of potential cutoff points. Its total diagnostic accuracy is summarized by the area under the curve (AUC). Significantly changed metabolites, including phenylalanine, acetoacetate, and glutamate, are shown by the generated ROC curve [Figure 3]. The boxplot clearly distinguishes between VAP and NVAP patients based on their unique metabolic fingerprints [Supplementary Figure 8]. Plotting sensitivity versus 1-specificity across several threshold backgrounds, the ROC curves provided a strong visual depiction of each metabolite’s capacity to distinguish between VAP and NVAP [Figure 4]. Specifically, phenylalanine showed the area AUC at 72%, subsequently acetoacetate at 70.6%, and glutamate at 69%. Additional assessment of trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), creatine, and creatinine showed AUCs of 67.1%, 63.9%, and 62.4%, respectively [Figure 3].

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to identify the potential biomarkers in the serum of VAP patients admitted to a tertiary healthcare center in North India. NMR-based metabolomics provides a metabolic fingerprint for the pathogenesis of disease development and insights into the disrupted underlying metabolic pathways. These system biology findings are also helpful in disease monitoring and stratification of therapy.

We have analyzed 27 metabolites from each group, out of which six metabolites are significantly upregulated, namely phenylalanine, acetoacetate, glutamate, TMAO, creatine, and creatinine. Here, phenylalanine, acetoacetate, and glutamate have AUC values in a fair and acceptable range and show the potential to serve as diagnostic biomarkers.[333435] Remaining biomarkers, namely TMAO, creatine, and creatinine, have low AUC values and cannot be utilized individually but can be used in combination with other biomarkers to improve the diagnostic and prognostic utility.[3637] These altered biomarkers give insights into the altered metabolic pathways in the VAP group.

Phenylalanine is involved in “phenylalanine metabolism” and “phenylalanine, tyrosine, and tryptophan biosynthesis. The tyrosine acts as a precursor for neurotransmitters, which include dopamine, norepinephrine, and epinephrine.[38] Elevated levels of phenylalanine likely hinder phenylalanine hydroxylase (PAH) inhibition and disrupt dopamine/norepinephrine biosynthesis, as observed in the onset of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS).[39] This dysregulation may alter the motor control and drive sedative dependence and prolonged stay on a ventilator, which results in neurological stress conditions and systemic inflammation, often correlated with poor prognosis and disease worsening.[394041] The elevated levels of acetoacetate in VAP patients showed a critical metabolic shift toward alternative energy sources during tissue hypoxia and acidosis, also indicating the patient may get triggered into the onset of ARDS, as circulating acetoacetate is significantly enhanced in ARDS and leads to oxidative stress due to mitochondrial damage.[42] The observed elevated level of glutamate indicates the altered glutamate-glutamine cycle in the VAP patients; these findings also align with recent findings in which disrupted glutamate metabolism showed the hallmark of ARDS and its poor prognosis.[4344] During the pathogenic infection, immune cells in the lung tissue have a higher demand for glutamine to support their proliferation, energy demands, and production of inflammatory signals. In case of VAP, prolonged infection may lead to higher consumption of glutamine and a low systemic level of glutamine and higher glutamate level in serum, reflecting glutamate metabolism. This may lead to inflammatory stress, lowering the activity of the M1 macrophage population as glutamine is exclusively utilized by M1 macrophages for bactericidal activity and increases the risk factor of mortality. Furthermore, elevated levels of glutamate may result from prolonged duration of stimulation of N-methyl-D-aspartate receptors (NMDARs), triggering cell death by influx of calcium ions along with oxygen radicals, damaging mitochondrial function to a greater extent, resulting in acute lung injury.[454647484950] Glutamate participates in ammonia recycling through the urea cycle. The upregulated urea cycle showed the disturbed energy cycle (tricarboxylic acid cycle) and indirectly induced the Warburg effect.[51]

The identified panel of phenylalanine, acetoacetate, and glutamate shows a strong mechanistic correlation with the clinical onset of VAP and related critical conditions like sepsis and ARDS. Specifically, the accumulation of phenylalanine reflects impaired PAH activity due to early immune activation and has been shown to aggravate lung injury by inducing macrophage pyroptosis.[52] Rising acetoacetate levels further corroborate a systemic shift toward a catabolic and pro-inflammatory condition as found in ARDS.[33] In recent findings, elevated levels of glutamate serve as an indirect indicator of systemic proteolysis and inflammatory signaling, characteristic of new-onset sepsis.[33435354] VAP induces metabolic and inflammatory alterations that generate serum biomarker signatures overlapping with the onset of new diseases such as sepsis and ARDS. Elevated phenylalanine, acetoacetate, and glutamate have also been reported in sepsis, bacterial pneumonia, ARDS, and SIRS, reflecting immune activation, mitochondrial stress, and altered amino acid metabolism.[55565758] These shared patterns suggest that VAP involves systemic host metabolic reprogramming rather than localized infection alone. Correlation with biomarker profiles from other diseases highlights the diagnostic potential of metabolite-based assays and supports their utility for early detection and stratification of VAP patients.[596061] Combining serum biomarkers with microbiological testing may improve diagnostic accuracy and clinical decision-making.

The primary limitation of this prospective pilot study is its small sample size and single-center design. The study is conducted in North India only; the given metabolic signatures are prone to influenced by local pathogenic flora and clinical practices. Furthermore, the data collection and analysis are cross-sectional; the absence of longitudinal data prevents us from observing the metabolic trajectory from the onset of mechanical ventilation to the development of VAP, making it difficult to establish biomarkers in the heterogeneous environment of the ICU.

Future research should prioritize longitudinal studies with larger, multicenter cohorts to validate these initial findings and explore the predictive value of the metabolome at multiple time scales during ICU admission. Furthermore, the combination of other omics approaches will facilitate the discovery of robust biomarkers and an in-depth understanding of the pathophysiology of disease.

Finally, this study explores the disrupted underlying pathways in the VAP patients and provides the dysregulated metabolites as biomarkers for diagnosis and prognosis of VAP. These biomarkers, along with other clinical parameters, aid in the diagnosis of VAP and are helpful in the initiation of antibiotic therapy in a short time. NMR-based metabolomics provides a metabolic fingerprint for the pathogenesis of disease development and provides insights into the disrupted underlying metabolic pathways. These system biology findings are also helpful in disease monitoring and stratification of therapy.

CONCLUSION

This pilot study underscores the utility of 1H-NMR-based metabolomics as a robust, non-destructive, and reproducible analytical tool for the rapid identification of biomarkers in the critical care setting. We successfully identified a distinct circulating metabolic signature specifically involving phenylalanine, acetoacetate, and glutamate that serves as a potential biomarker panel for the early diagnosis of VAP. Our findings suggest that lung injury and an exacerbated inflammatory response are central drivers of VAP development. Consequently, treatment strategies aimed at mitigating these processes may reduce VAP incidence. While promising, further large-scale, longitudinal investigations are required to validate this biomarker panel across diverse populations and refine its diagnostic precision for routine clinical application.
