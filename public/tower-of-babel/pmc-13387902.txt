Multimorbidity and Associations with Cognition and Alzheimer's Disease Biomarkers
Authors: Xiaqing Jiang, Sid E O'Bryant, Robert A Rissman, Leigh A Johnson, Meredith N Braskie, Kristine Yaffe, the HABS‐HD Study Team
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMultimorbidity, the coexistence of 2 or more chronic conditions, has been linked to cognitive aging and Alzheimer's disease (AD) and AD‐related dementias, yet the mechanisms remain unclear. We aimed to examine the associations of multimorbidity with cognition and biomarkers across multiple mechanistic pathways.MethodsWe cross‐sectionally analyzed 3,808 dementia‐free participants (mean age 64.9 ± 8.5 years, 62% female) from the Health and Aging Brain Study: Health Disparities. Multimorbidity burden was assessed using a latent construct derived from chronic conditions identified through objective measures, medical history, and self‐report. A latent factor score for cognition was estimated using confirmatory factor analysis and neuropsychological tests. Using linear and logistic regression, we examined the associations of multimorbidity burden with biomarkers of AD (positron emission tomography [PET] amyloid, plasma β‐amyloid 42/40, and phosphorylated tau [p‐tau] measures), neurodegeneration (cortical thickness, hippocampal volume, and plasma neurofilament light and total tau), and cerebral small vessel disease (SVD) (magnetic resonance imaging white matter hyperintensities, cerebral microbleeds, and lacunes).ResultsGreater multimorbidity burden was associated with worse cognition and biomarkers of AD (PET amyloid standardized uptake value ratios and positivity, p‐tau181, and p‐tau217), neurodegeneration (neurofilament light, total tau, cortical thickness, and hippocampal volume), and SVD (white matter hyperintensity volume and presence of lacune and cerebral microbleeds).InterpretationAmong dementia‐free individuals, higher multimorbidity burden was associated with biomarkers for greater AD pathology, neurodegeneration, and SVD. These findings support a more holistic approach to managing chronic disease burden, which has the potential to reduce overall pathophysiological burden and delay cognitive decline. ANN NEUROL 2026;100:370–379

As the population ages in the United States (US), the prevalence of Alzheimer's disease (AD) and AD‐related dementias (ADRD) continues to rise. 1 The aging population contributes to the growing prevalence of multimorbidity, the coexistence of 2 or more chronic conditions. In the United States, approximately 50% of individuals 45 to 64 years old and 81% of those ≥65 have multimorbidity. 2 Importantly, multimorbidity is associated with an increased risk for dementia and cognitive decline. 3 , 4 Key contributors to multimorbidity, such as hypertension, diabetes, obesity, dyslipidemia, and depression, are well‐recognized risk factors for AD/ADRD. 5 However, the mechanisms linking multimorbidity burden to cognition remain poorly understood, particularly AT(N) pathways involving amyloid, tau, and neurodegeneration. 6

Longitudinal cohort and neuropathological studies suggest that the causes of AD/ADRD are multifactorial and often complicated by mixed pathologies. 7 Several individual conditions, such as hypertension, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, stroke, and osteoarticular diseases, have recently been linked to individual AT(N) biomarkers or vascular injury. 4 , 8 , 9 Despite the frequent co‐occurrence of chronic conditions and mixed pathologies, the relationship between overall multimorbidity burden and these mechanistic pathways has yet to be thoroughly investigated. Understanding the relationship between multimorbidity burden and biomarkers of AD neuropathology, neurodegeneration, and vascular pathways could provide valuable insights for prioritizing pathway‐specific strategies relevant to cognitive outcomes.

In a racially and ethnically diverse, community‐based cohort from the Health and Aging Brain Study: Health Disparities (HABS‐HD), we aimed to assess the associations of multimorbidity with cognition and biomarkers across modalities in multiple mechanistic pathways, including AD pathology, neurodegeneration, and cerebral small vessel disease (SVD). Additionally, we assessed whether these associations differed by race/ethnicity and sex.

Methods

Study Population

We conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of the HABS‐HD participants at study baseline. Detailed methods of the HABS‐HD study have been published previously. 10 Briefly, HABS‐HD is an ongoing community‐based cohort study in Fort Worth, Texas. From 2017 to 2024, 4,134 non‐Hispanic White (NHW), Hispanic, and Black individuals ≥50 years were enrolled using a community‐based participatory research approach. Eligible individuals provided blood samples, underwent neuroimaging, and had no severe mental or physical conditions preventing participation. The North Texas Regional institutional review board (IRB) approved the HABS‐HD study protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent. The current secondary analysis of de‐identified data did not require additional IRB approval per determination by the University of California, San Francisco IRB. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.

We excluded 243 participants (5.9%) with dementia, 16 (0.4%) with undetermined cognitive status, and 67 (1.6%) with major brain abnormalities or missing both neuroimaging and plasma biomarkers. Dementia diagnosis was adjudicated through consensus review using the clinician‐administered Clinical Dementia Rating Scale (CDR) sum of boxes ≥2.5 or cognitive z‐scores ≥2 SD below the mean on ≥2 tests. 11 , 12 Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) was determined based on cognitive complaints, a CDR sum of boxes of 0.5 to 2, or cognitive z‐scores ≥1.5 SD below the mean on ≥1 test. We analyzed 3,808 participants without dementia (2,967 cognitively normal and 841 with MCI). In this study, multimorbidity assessment, cognitive testing, plasma biomarkers, and neuroimaging were all collected at the same baseline study visit.

Multimorbidity

Multimorbidity was assessed in 2 ways: first, by estimating a latent construct representing overall multimorbidity burden using formative structural equation modeling (SEM), in which chronic conditions contributed differentially to the latent multimorbidity construct; and second, by counting the total number of chronic conditions. Chronic conditions were identified through objective measures, laboratory values, medical history, and self‐reports. A total of 22 conditions were captured in HABS‐HD, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, obesity, coronary heart disease, arrhythmia, heart failure, thyroid disease, stroke/transient ischemic attack (TIA), anemia, depression/anxiety, cancer, chronic kidney disease, osteoporosis, arthritis, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease/asthma, liver diseases, connective tissue and autoimmune diseases, seizure and other neurological disorders, gastrointestinal disorders, chronic musculoskeletal disorders, and eye conditions. These conditions have been proposed by the US Department of Health and Human Services and other initiatives aimed at consolidating the multimorbidity definition. 13 , 14 Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥140mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90mmHg on 2 occasions, a medical history, or antihypertensive use. Dyslipidemia was defined as low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol ≥120mg/dL, total cholesterol ≥240mg/dL, triglycerides ≥200mg/dL, a medical history, or medication use. Diabetes was defined as hemoglobin A1c ≥6.5%, a medical history, or medication use. We used height and weight from the baseline exam to calculate body mass index (BMI) and defined obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2). Depression was assessed using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) and defined as GDS ≥10 or a medical history of depression. Hyperthyroidism was defined as elevated free thyroxine >1.8ng/dL with thyroid‐stimulating hormone (TSH) <0.4mIU/L, or medication use. Hypothyroidism was defined as TSH ≥4.5mIU/L with normal or low free thyroxine or medication use. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) was defined as estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <60 or a medical history. Anemia was defined as hemoglobin <13.2g/dL in male and <11.7g/dL in female or a medical history. The remaining conditions were defined based on self‐reports of medical history.

Cognitive Assessment

A neuropsychological test battery was administered to assess cognition across 3 domains: (1) executive function, assessed using the Trail Making Test (parts A and B) and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test; (2) verbal fluency, assessed using the FAS and Animal Naming tests; and (3) verbal memory, evaluated using the Wechsler Memory Scale‐III Logical Memory (LM, I and II) and the Spanish English Verbal Learning Test (immediate and 30‐minute delayed recall). Using a second‐order confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) model, latent factors representing each domain were estimated using test scores as indicators and then loaded onto a single higher‐order factor representing overall cognition. This approach accounted for the strong correlations among cognitive domains and captured global cognitive performance while preserving domain‐specific measurement structure. This overall cognitive factor score captured shared variance within and across cognitive domains while minimizing measurement error. Cognitive tests with missing scores (<1% for all tests) were handled using the full information maximum likelihood approach, assuming missingness at random.

Plasma Biomarkers

Fasting blood samples were collected following established guidelines among all participants. 15 EDTA plasma samples were aliquoted after centrifugation and stored within 2 hours (stick‐to‐freezer) at −80°C until assayed at the University of North Texas Health Science Center, Institute for Translational Research Laboratory. Detailed biomarker assay methods have been published. 16 , 17 Plasma was assayed using the ultra‐sensitive Single Molecule Array (Simoa) HD‐X or SR‐X bead‐based immunoassay analyzer (Quanterix, Lexington, MA) for β‐amyloid (Aβ) 40 and 42, total tau (t‐tau), phosphorylated tau (p‐tau)181, p‐tau217, and neurofilament light (NfL). High and low assay controls and an inter‐assay pooled control were included on each plate and run in duplicate to monitor inter‐assay variability. The mean coefficients of variation ranged from 4% to 7%. Biomarker concentrations were quantified via interpolation from a 4‐parameter logistic (4PL) calibration curve with 1/y2 weighting, generated by converting fluorescent signals from the 2‐step immunoassay. The detection limit was 0.196pg/mL for Aβ 40, 0.043pg/mL for Aβ 42, 0.019pg/mL for t‐tau, 0.016pg/mL for p‐tau181, 0.0074pg/mL for p‐tau217, and 0.038pg/mL for NfL. Biomarker values in the analytic sample were within the assay detection limits.

Amyloid Positron Emission Tomography Imaging

Amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) has been conducted among 1,801 participants using Siemens Biograph Vision 450 whole‐body PET/computed tomography (CT) scanners (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany), with acquisition and analysis consistent with the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) 3 protocol. 10 , 18 Each participant received an 8.1 mCi (±10%) Neuraceq bolus (18F‐florbetaben), with a 20‐minute (4 frames, 5 minutes each) dynamic emission acquisition beginning 90 minutes post‐injection. A low‐dose CT scan was obtained for attenuation correction. Emission images were iteratively reconstructed (8 iterations, 5 subsets). Scans were resampled to a voxel size of 1.5mm and smoothed to an apparent resolution of approximately 8mm. A summary cortical region of interest (ROI) was derived from the FreeSurfer‐defined regions (frontal, anterior/posterior cingulate, lateral parietal, and lateral temporal cortex) and normalized to the whole cerebellum reference region to calculate global standardized uptake value ratios (SUVR), consistent with ADNI‐based protocols commonly used in large cohort studies. Amyloid positivity was defined as an SUVR >1.08. 10 , 19

Brain Magnetic Resonance Imaging

All participants underwent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) using a 3 T Siemens MAGNETOM Skyra whole‐body scanner or a 3 T Siemens MAGNETOM Vida scanner. Neuroimaging scans were analyzed and quality checked at the University of Southern California, with details previously described. 10 , 20 , 21 Briefly, T1‐weighted magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradient echo (MPRAGE) and T2‐weighted fluid attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images were acquired to measure white matter hyperintensities (WMH) volume. FreeSurfer (v5.3.0) was used to segment the T1‐weighted volume scans by tissue and parcellate into cortical regions to derive mean cortical thickness in ROIs across the brain. HippoDeep software was used to segment right and left hippocampus and to estimate intracranial volume (ICV). Total hippocampal volume (HCV) was the sum of left and right hippocampal volumes when both were available. The “meta‐ROI” was the surface‐area weighted average of mean cortical thickness across the entorhinal cortex, fusiform, inferior temporal gyri, and middle temporal gyri. 22 Lacune and cerebral microbleeds (CM) (hemosiderin deposits) assessed by a neuroradiologist were reported as either absent or present.

Covariates

Demographic characteristics (age, sex, and race/ethnicity) and educational attainment were obtained from self‐report. The presence of the apolipoprotein E (APOE) ε4 allele was determined using standard techniques. Physical activity was assessed using the Rapid Assessment of Physical Activity questionnaire (score of ≥6 as physically active). Current cigarette smoking was self‐reported. Hazardous alcohol use was defined as an Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test score ≥8. Interview language (English or Spanish) was recorded as the language of test administration.

Statistical Analysis

Participant characteristics were compared across quantiles of multimorbidity burden based on the total number of chronic conditions using χ2 or Kruskal‐Wallis tests. The availability of cognitive tests and biomarkers is reported in Table S1. For biomarkers with >5% missing data, we compared missingness by multimorbidity burden and did not find consistent differences across multimorbidity groups (Table S2). Using a formative SEM approach, multimorbidity was specified as a latent construct that was formed by chronic conditions rather than reflected by them. Given the correlations among cardiovascular risk factors and diseases (eg, hypertension, diabetes, and stroke), these conditions were included as formative indicators of a latent construct representing cardiovascular burden, which together with other chronic conditions formed the multimorbidity factor. The structural model then regressed latent cognition on latent multimorbidity. In the formative model, conditions were allowed to contribute heterogeneously to the latent multimorbidity construct. Conditions with weights that were both negative and nonsignificant in the formative multimorbidity model were excluded, including dyslipidemia, obesity, arrhythmia, thyroid disease, cancer, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease/asthma, liver diseases, connective tissue and autoimmune diseases, seizure and other neurological disorders, gastrointestinal disorders, and eye conditions, as conditions were conceptualized as contributing positively to the overall multimorbidity burden. Model fit was evaluated using robust root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) with its 90% confidence interval (CI), the comparative fit index (CFI), Tucker‐Lewis index (TLI), and standardized root mean square residual (SRMR), based on literature‐recommended cutoffs for adequate fit. 23 , 24 Standardized β were reported with 95% CI. To facilitate interpretation, 13 points were added to the raw multimorbidity latent score so that all values were positive. We reported the multivariable‐adjusted association between latent multimorbidity and cognition and conducted multigroup analyses to test whether this association differed by race/ethnicity using a Wald test. We also assessed the associations between latent multimorbidity and individual cognitive tests using linear regression.

Pairwise associations among latent multimorbidity, cognition, and biomarkers were first assessed using Kendall rank correlation coefficients. Multivariable‐adjusted associations between latent multimorbidity and continuous biomarkers (z‐standardized) were assessed using linear regression. The latent multimorbidity factor score was analyzed as both a continuous variable and a categorical variable by quantile. Because of skewness, plasma biomarkers, WMH volume, and amyloid SUVR were natural‐log transformed. WMH volume and HCV were adjusted for ICV using the residual approach. 25 Associations between multimorbidity and dichotomous biomarkers (amyloid positivity, lacune presence, and CM presence) were examined using logistic regression. Potential covariates were selected a priori based on literature and included in multivariable models based on their potential roles as confounders and their associations with multimorbidity, including demographics, education, APOE genotype, and physical activity. 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 We conducted sensitivity analyses by alternatively modeling multimorbidity burden as the total number of chronic conditions and by additionally adjusting for MCI, BMI, and eGFR for plasma biomarkers and MRI scanner for MRI measurements. Multiple testing was addressed using false discovery rate (FDR) correction with a prespecified q‐value threshold of 0.05. Effect modification by race/ethnicity and sex was assessed using interaction terms between multimorbidity burden and race/ethnicity or sex, with stratified analyses conducted for outcomes showing evidence of interaction. To facilitate discussion, associations between common conditions (prevalence >4%) and biomarkers were explored using linear and logistic regression with FDR adjustment. Statistical analyses were conducted with SAS 9.4 and Mplus 8.11.

Results

The mean age of the 3,808 participants (62% female; 35% NHW, 36% Hispanic, and 29% Black) was 64.9 ± 8.5 years, and 841 (22%) had MCI. The median multimorbidity burden was 4 (IQR = 2–5) conditions (Fig S1), with 408 (11%) having 0 to 1 condition, 1,437 (38%) having 2 to 3 conditions, 1,304 (34%) having 4 to 5 conditions, and 659 (17%) having 6 or more conditions. Participants with greater multimorbidity burden were older, more often female, less educated, less physically active, and more likely to have MCI compared to those with 0 to 1 condition (Table 1). Multimorbidity and the Association with Cognition

The second‐order CFA model of latent cognition demonstrated adequate fit (robust RMSEA = 0.057; 90% CI = 0.051–0.063; robust CFI = 0.987; robust TLI = 0.979; and SRMR = 0.022), with factor loadings shown in Figure 1. The full formative SEM for latent multimorbidity also showed adequate fit (robust RMSEA = 0.053; 90% CI = 0.051–0.055; robust CFI = 0.929; robust TLI = 0.916; and SRMR = 0.045) (Fig 1). The median latent multimorbidity score was 14.15 (IQR = 11.58–16.88) (Fig S2).

After adjusting for demographics, education, APOE, physical activity, and interview language, a higher latent multimorbidity score was associated with worse cognition (standardized β = −0.553; 95% CI = −0.576 to −0.531) (see Fig 1). In the multigroup analysis, we did not find evidence that the association between latent multimorbidity and cognition differed by race/ethnicity based on a Wald test (p = 0.18). The association was attenuated, but remained statistically significant when multimorbidity burden was modeled alternatively as the total number of chronic conditions (standardized β = −0.072; 95% CI = −0.098 to −0.047). Associations between Multimorbidity and Biomarkers

Correlation analyses showed that higher latent multimorbidity was associated with worse cognition and biomarker profiles (Fig S3). After adjustment for demographics, education, APOE, and physical activity, higher latent multimorbidity was associated with biomarkers indicative of greater AD pathology, neurodegeneration, and SVD. Among AD biomarkers, a 1‐IQR increase (5.3 points) in the latent multimorbidity score was associated with higher PET amyloid SUVR (β = 0.08; 95% CI = 0.03–0.13), greater odds of PET amyloid positivity (odds ratio [OR] = 1.35; 95% CI = 1.12–1.62), and higher plasma p‐tau181 (β = 0.13; 95% CI = 0.09–0.17) and p‐tau217 (β = 0.15; 95% CI = 0.11–0.19), but not with plasma Aβ 42/40 (β = −0.02; 95% CI = −0.06 to 0.03). For neurodegeneration, greater multimorbidity was associated with higher NfL (β = 0.15; 95% CI = 0.12–0.19) and t‐tau (β = 0.12; 95% CI = 0.07–0.16), as well as lower cortical thickness (β = −0.10; 95% CI = −0.14 to −0.06) and HCV (β = −0.16; 95% CI = −0.20 to −0.13). Additionally, greater multimorbidity was associated with SVD markers, including greater WMH volume (β = 0.18; 95% CI = 0.14–0.22) and higher odds of lacune (OR = 2.31; 95% CI = 1.71–3.12) and CM presence (OR = 1.24; 95% CI = 1.03–1.5). All associations remained statistically significant after additional adjustment for MCI, BMI, eGFR (for plasma biomarkers), and scanner (for MRI markers), except for CM presence (Table S4). These findings were robust to multiple testing correction using FDR. Modeling latent multimorbidity categorically by quantiles yielded similar results (Fig 2). Results were similar when multimorbidity burden was defined as the total number of chronic conditions, except that the associations with PET amyloid and MRI CM presence were not statistically significant.

We did not find evidence of race/ethnic interactions in biomarker associations, except for MRI WMH volume (p = 0.044). Specifically, the association between higher latent multimorbidity and WMH volume was weaker in Hispanic (β = 0.11; 95% CI = 0.06–0.16) than in NHW (β = 0.20; 95% CI = 0.14–0.26) and Black participants (β = 0.27; 95% CI = 0.18–0.37). We observed some effect modification by sex for plasma NfL (p = 0.031) and MRI WMH (p = 0.005). The associations were stronger in males than females: for WMH volume, β = 0.26 (95% CI = 0.19–0.32) in males versus β = 0.14 (95% CI = 0.09–0.18) in females; for plasma NfL, β = 0.21 (95% CI = 0.15–0.27) in males versus β = 0.13 (95% CI = 0.08–0.17) in females. Discussion

In a large cohort of dementia‐free, middle‐age and older adults in the community, we found that greater multimorbidity burden was associated with worse cognitive function and biomarker levels for greater AD pathology (PET amyloid, p‐tau181, and p‐tau217), neurodegeneration (cortical thickness, HCV, NfL, and t‐tau), and SVD (WMH, lacune, and CM). These associations were not explained by demographics, education, APOE, physical activity, BMI, renal function, or cognitive status and were robust after multiple testing correction.

To date, this is one of the largest studies to investigate multimorbidity with a comprehensive panel of neuroimaging and plasma biomarkers. Our findings reveal a multifaceted relationship between multimorbidity and AT(N) biomarkers. Unlike prior multimorbidity studies that have focused solely on specific AD biomarkers within a single modality, our work uniquely examined AD pathology, neurodegeneration, and SVD biomarkers simultaneously across multiple modalities. 8 , 26 , 29 , 30 , 31 , 32 We found that AD, neurodegeneration, and SVD pathways may all play important roles in the association between multimorbidity and cognition in non‐demented individuals.

Research on multimorbidity and AD pathology is still emerging. Most previous neuropathological and neuroimaging studies have been limited in sample size and have failed to find an association between multimorbidity and amyloid accumulation. 33 , 34 , 35 In our study, associations with both PET amyloid SUVR and amyloid positivity support that multimorbidity is linked to greater cerebral amyloid burden in non‐demented individuals. This aligns with recent findings demonstrating an association between multimorbidity burden and longitudinal Aβ accumulation in the ADNI. 31 Similar to prior reports, including those from the Swedish National Study on Aging and Care in Kungsholmen, the associations we found with plasma p‐tau181 and p‐tau217 further suggest an important yet poorly understood role of tau phosphorylation in linking multimorbidity and cognition. 8 , 32 Our robust findings on cortical thickness, HCV, NfL, and t‐tau are consistent with previous studies that examined neurodegenerative markers separately in smaller samples. 8 , 26 , 29 , 35 Additionally, our results contribute to existing evidence linking multimorbidity to SVD, showing associations not only with WMH volume, but also with lacune and CM. 29

Several considerations are important when evaluating the associations between multimorbidity and AD biomarkers. It is often overlooked that although amyloid PET reliably detects moderate–severe neuritic plaques, it is not sensitive to mild or sparse plaques and does not measure soluble Aβ. 36 Plasma p‐tau may reflect a physiological response to Aβ plaques and soluble Aβ species, becoming abnormal around the same time as amyloid PET. 36 , 37 We demonstrated associations of multimorbidity with both PET amyloid and p‐tau species, supporting a robust relationship with the amyloid pathway. The null association with plasma Aβ42/40 may reflect its comparatively lower analytic accuracy and greater measurement variability. 38 Nevertheless, as one of the earliest AD‐specific biomarkers to become abnormal, the phosphorylated tau fragment serves as an important indicator for tau proteinopathy. 39 Taken together, our findings support a consistent link between multimorbidity and an AD‐specific process potentially involving amyloid and tau pathologies.

Our findings suggest that both AD pathology and neurodegeneration may help explain the association between multimorbidity and worse cognition, although the precise mechanism remains unclear. Although the amyloid cascade hypothesis positions neurodegeneration downstream of amyloid accumulation, emerging evidence suggests neurodegeneration may also arise independently and precede amyloid pathology. 40 , 41 In additional analyses, we found that conditions such as diabetes, obesity, anemia, depression/anxiety, arthritis, and CKD were associated with both AD and neurodegenerative biomarkers, whereas coronary heart disease, and stroke/TIA were linked to neurodegeneration, but not AD pathology (Tables S5–S7). This supports the hypothesis of both amyloid‐dependent and independent pathways of neurodegeneration. The association with neurodegeneration may also be partially related to SVD, given that coronary heart disease and stroke/TIA were also associated with SVD. However, it is still plausible that multimorbidity, neurodegeneration, and cognition are not causally linked, but rather reflect shared mechanisms in multisystem dysregulation associated with aging, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysfunction. Although causality cannot be determined given the study design, our study taps into important gaps in understanding the pathophysiology underlying multimorbidity, AT(N) pathways, and cognition.

Amid the growing population with multimorbidity at increased risk for AD/ADRD, this work has implications for future research. Critical questions remain about the mechanisms by which individual conditions contribute to cognitive aging and AD/ADRD, particularly among different race/ethnic groups. Recent studies have begun to examine multimorbidity clusters in relation to plasma biomarkers. Our work extends this emerging literature by assessing multimorbidity burden in a large, diverse community‐based cohort with multimodal biomarkers of different pathways, while applying a latent multimorbidity approach. More research is needed to delve further into both the individual and joint roles of chronic conditions in AT(N) pathways, including the investigation of condition clusters that share underlying mechanisms and potential synergistic interactions. Understanding the burden and clustering of multimorbidity by mechanism may foster insights into personalized prevention strategies and prioritized targets for intervention. However, individuals with multimorbidity are often excluded from randomized controlled trials for AD/ADRD, 42 even though they might benefit from treatments targeting the processes through which multimorbidity is linked to cognitive outcomes. Future trials could adopt more inclusive, pragmatic designs that enroll individuals with multimorbidity and incorporate analytic strategies such as stratification or explicit modeling of multimorbidity burden, rather than excluding them. Our findings on the associations with biomarkers across multiple pathways further support the need for a broader approach to trials beyond the traditional focus on amyloid. Targeted management of specific multimorbidity profiles, particularly those sharing vascular, inflammatory, or metabolic pathways, may help reduce the aggregate pathological burden leading to AD/ADRD.

This study has several important strengths. The comprehensive, state‐of‐the‐art collection of plasma biomarkers, neuroimaging, and neuropsychological assessments enabled a simultaneous assessment of multiple pathways. The large sample size for AD biomarkers allowed us to detect associations between multimorbidity and both PET amyloid and plasma p‐tau. By considering multimodal biomarkers, we gained unique insights into mechanisms involving AD pathology, neurodegeneration, and SVD. Additionally, the use of SEM and a latent variable approach helped account for measurement error in cognitive tests, enhancing our assessment of cognition and multimorbidity. This study also has some limitations. The cross‐sectional design limits our ability to establish the temporality of multimorbidity and biomarkers, and we interpreted the relationship as associations rather than causality. Longitudinal biomarker assessment will be necessary to validate these findings as follow‐up data in HABS‐HD become available. The assumption of missing at random may be strong for amyloid PET, with 53% missing. However, we complemented the analyses with both plasma p‐tau181 and p‐tau217, which strengthen our findings. The absence of tau PET also limits our ability to assess directly tau pathology and its relationship with multimorbidity. As tau PET imaging in HABS‐HD is underway, follow‐up analyses will further inform the relationship between multimorbidity and disease progression. Plasma biomarker assessment was available in most participants but not all, because of capacity and resource constraints. Some conditions were self‐reported, and the multimorbidity burden may be underestimated. Future studies incorporating condition severity, duration, and cluster‐based multimorbidity patterns may provide more nuanced insights into associations with cognition and AT(N) biomarkers. We did not examine the impact of medications, although further evaluation of how treatments influence biomarkers is warranted. Finally, our findings may not be generalizable to race/ethnic groups beyond NHW, Hispanic, and Black individuals.

In this diverse, community‐based cohort, higher multimorbidity burden was associated with worse cognition and biomarker levels for greater AD pathology, neurodegeneration, and SVD among dementia‐free individuals. These findings support a more holistic approach to managing chronic disease burden, which may help reduce overall pathophysiological burden affecting brain health and potentially delay cognitive decline.
