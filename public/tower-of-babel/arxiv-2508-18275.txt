Algebraic conformal nets, investigation of the locally constant case - M1 internship supervised by Domenico Fiorenza
Quentin Moreau

Abstract
We define the tricategory of algebraic conformal nets, defects, sectors and intertwiners where algebraic refers to the absence of a topology on the relevant algebras and modules. We aim at making these definitions satisfying from a categorical point of view, and in a few cases, e.g., for defects, we propose more general definitions than what can be found in the literature. This allows us in particular to propose an implementation of solitons as defects. Then we focus on the locally constant case where we provide an identification of the tricategory of locally constant algebraic conformal nets, defects, sectors and intertwiners with the tricategory of commutative algebras, algebras, bimodules and bimodule homomorphisms.



1
Introduction

Conformal nets play a pivotal role in the mathematical formulation of quantum field theory.
They have been widely studied and developed by Kawahigashi and Longo
[
1
]
.
A conformal net assigns to each interval of the standard circle a von Neumann algebra acting on a Hilbert space.
Recently, Bartels, Douglas and Henriques
[
2
]
proposed a "coordinate-free" version of conformal nets in which intervals of the standard circle have been replaced by abstract intervals, i.e., manifolds diffeomorphic to a segment.
Henriques showed that these coordinate-free conformal nets are examples of factorization algebras
[
3
]
, a structure that appears in various approaches to quantum field theory.
In particular, Lurie showed that locally constant
n
-dimensional factorization algebras are in correspondence with
E_{n}
-algebras
[
4
, Theorem 5.4.5.9]
.

Bartels, Douglas and Henriques created a whole tricategory of coordinate-free conformal nets, defects, sectors and intertwiners
[
5
]
.
In
[
2
, Section 4.A]
, the same authors give a correspondence between coordinate-free conformal nets and the more usual circle-based conformal nets.
However, they do not extend such a correspondence to the whole tricategory.
Moreover, concrete objects such as the standard circle still play a role in otherwise abstract definitions, leading to a conceptual competition between concreteness and abstractness.
For instance, the definition given by Bartels, Douglas and Henriques
[
6
, Chapter 2.A]
of sectors relies on the standard circle.
Thus, we aim to continue this categorification of conformal nets by providing definitions that are more satisfying from a categorical point of view, while using only concrete objects.

Moreover, conformal nets and defects satisfy algebraic and topological axioms.
For instance, a defect
D
should satisfy the strong additivity property: for any intervals
I
and
J
covering an interval
K
,
D(I)
and
D(J)
topologically generate
D(K)
.
However, when focusing on locally constant conformal nets and defects, such topological properties appear to be simpler or even trivial.
So, in order to focus on the algebraic aspects of the construction, we introduce a notion of algebraic conformal nets, defects, sectors and intertwiners which are released from topological constraints.
Then we reduce to the locally constant case.
This allows us to address the lack of explicit examples from
[
6
]
.
Indeed, in the locally constant case, conformal nets, defects, sectors and intertwiners have a natural algebraic interpretation: we show that in the locally constant case their tricategory is equivalent to the tricategory of commutative algebras, algebras, bimodules and bimodule homomorphisms.

3
The tricategory of algebraic conformal nets

3.1
Algebraic conformal nets

The tricategory of coordinate-free conformal nets, defects, sectors and intertwiners has been defined by Bartels, Douglas and Henriques
[
6
]
.
Their definitions imply topological and algebraic constraints.
In
Section

3
, we build algebraic conformal nets, defects, sectors and intertwiners, in order to capture only the algebraic constraints.
Therefore, our objects are released from the usual topological axioms.
Indeed, a first thing we do is to replace von Neumann algebras by abstract associative algebras, with no topological structure on them.
That is why we introduced
2.1
.

In the definitions of
[
2
]
, the notation
L^{2}
stands for a functor that assigns to each von Neumann algebra
A
a certain Hilbert
A
–
A
-bimodule
L^{2}A
— its Haagerup
L^{2}
-space defined by Haagerup
[
7
]
— such that
L^{2}A\otimes-
is the identity of the object
A
in the bicategory of von Neumann algebras and Hilbert bimodules.
This is necessary because morphisms in this bicategory need to be Hilbert spaces, and the naive
A
–
A
-bimodule
A
would not fit since a von Neumann algebra is generally not a Hilbert space.
However, in our simplified case where we consider associative algebras without topological properties, we do not have this subtlety and an algebra
A
seen as an
A
–
A
-bimodule will be the identity functor on the object
A
in the Morita bicategory
\mathsf{Mor}
of algebras, bimodules and intertwiners.
The bicategory
\mathsf{Mor}
has been introduced by Morita
[
8
]
.
One can find a modern approach to
\mathsf{Mor}
in the work of Brouwer
[
9
, Proposition 2.3.1]
.

Therefore, we define algebraic conformal nets as follows:

Definition 3.1.1

(Algebraic conformal net)
.

An algebraic conformal net
\mathcal{A}
is a functor from the category
\mathsf{INT}
to
\mathsf{Algebra}
such that
\mathcal{A}(\overline{I})=\mathcal{A}(I)^{\mathrm{op}}
for any interval
I
where
\overline{I}
denotes
I
with the reversed orientation.
Let
I
and
J
be subintervals of an interval
K
.
We write
i\colon I\hookrightarrow K
and
j\colon J\hookrightarrow K
the inclusion maps.
Then the following conditions must be met:

1.

Isotony:
\mathcal{A}(f)
is injective for any embedding
f
.

2.

Locality: If
i(I)
and
j(J)
have disjoint interiors in
K
, then
\mathcal{A}(i)(\mathcal{A}(I))
and
\mathcal{A}(j)(\mathcal{A}(J))
are mutually commuting subalgebras of
\mathcal{A}(K)
.

3.

Strong additivity: If
K=I\cup J
then
\mathcal{A}(K)=\mathcal{A}(i)(\mathcal{A}(I))\vee\mathcal{A}(j)(\mathcal{A}(J))
.

Remark
.

Let us preserve the notations from
Definition

3.1.1
.
It is often convenient to not distinguish
\mathcal{A}(I)
and
\mathcal{A}(i)(\mathcal{A}(I))
.
We might want then to write "
\mathcal{A}(I)
or its image" (or only
\mathcal{A}(I)
) where "its image" implicitly refers to its image through
\mathcal{A}(i)
.
In the next definitions, we will make such abuse of notations, but remember that our axioms never impose preservation of inclusion, i.e.,
\mathcal{A}(I)
is not necessarily a subalgebra of
\mathcal{A}(K)
.

Remark
.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a conformal net and
f\colon I\hookrightarrow J
be an orientation-reversing embedding.
Then
f
induces an orientation-preserving embedding
\overline{f}\colon I\hookrightarrow\overline{J}
where
\overline{J}
denotes
J
with the reversed orientation.
Hence,
\mathcal{A}(\overline{f})\colon\mathcal{A}(I)\rightarrow\mathcal{A}(J)^{\mathrm{op}}
is well-defined and induces a linear antihomomorphism
\mathcal{A}(f)\colon\mathcal{A}(I)\rightarrow\mathcal{A}(J)
.

4.1

First, we investigate what objects and morphisms from

For conformal nets, we get:

Definition 4.1.1

(Locally constant conformal nets)
.

A conformal net
\mathcal{A}
is locally constant if
\mathcal{A}
maps any positively-oriented interval to the same algebra
A
and all embeddings of positively-oriented intervals to the identity of
A
.

Since all intervals are diffeomorphic to each other, we see that a locally constant algebraic conformal net is in particular the datum of an algebra
A
together with an involutive antiisomorphism
\overline{(\,\,)}_{A}\colon A\rightarrow A^{\mathrm{op}}
: for every interval
I
one has
\mathcal{A}(I)=A
, for every
embedding
i\colon I\rightarrow J
of intervals with same orientation (both positively or both negatively-oriented) we have
\mathcal{A}(i)=\mathrm{id}_{A}
and for every
embedding
\overline{i}\colon I\rightarrow J
of intervals with opposite orientations we have
\mathcal{A}(\overline{i})=\overline{(\,\,)}_{A}
. For this reason we will simply write
A
instead of
\mathcal{A}
for a locally constant algebraic conformal net.

A first thing we can notice is the following:

Proposition 4.1.2
.

The unique associative algebra
A
in a locally constant conformal net is commutative.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of the locality property.
∎

Since
A
is commutative, we have
A^{\mathrm{op}}=A
, and so
\overline{(\,\,)}_{A}\colon A\rightarrow A
is an involutive algebra automorphism. In particular,
\overline{(\,\,)}_{A}=\mathrm{id}_{A}
is a valid choice. In this case one has that
\mathcal{A}
maps every morphism in
\mathsf{INT}
to the identity of
A
. When this happens we say that
\mathcal{A}
is a locally constant
unoriented
algebraic conformal net. In what follows we will only deal with the unoriented case. The definitions and constructions for the oriented case are verbatim adapted from the unoriented case.

Because of
Proposition

4.1.2
, we will talk about commutative algebras instead of associative algebras when dealing with a locally constant conformal net.

5
Conclusion

We introduced the tricategory
\mathsf{CN}
as an algebraic framework for studying the algebraic properties of circle-based conformal nets.
This setting was designed to be entirely concrete — in order to avoid a mix between circle-based and coordinate-free definitions — and still flexible thanks to the extensive use of categorical tools.
We proposed a way to implement solitons as defects in
\mathsf{CN}
.
Furthermore, we provided a complete description of the locally constant subtricategory
\mathsf{cCN}
of
\mathsf{CN}
, identifying it with the tricategory
\mathsf{ComAlgebra}
.
