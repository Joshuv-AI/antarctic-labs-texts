GPNMB modulates neutrophil extracellular trap formation: therapeutic implications for ischemic stroke
Authors: Ningning Zong, Jian Chen, Yang Geng, Lixuan Yang, Shengnan Xia, Haiyan Yang, Xinyu Bao, Yun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroglia are key immune-competent cells responding immediately to manipulate post-stroke neuroinflammation to shape the prognosis of stroke. GPNMB was reported to be upregulated after ischemic stroke and might influence the outcome. However, its detailed biological function and mechanism remain elusive. Here, we found that GPNMB was remarkably elevated in the ischemic brain and mainly distributed in microglia. Combining GPNMB knockout mice and recombinant GPNMB protein, we found that GPNMB could alleviate ischemic brain injury. Recombinant GPNMB (rGPNMB) administration could reduce neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) formation, while knockout of GPNMB promoted NET formation in ischemic stroke. In addition, we found that CD44 functioned importantly in mediating the role of GPNMB inhibiting NET formation and alleviating ischemic brain injury. Depletion of neutrophils or inhibition of NET formation with DNase I could reduce the neuroprotective impact of GPNMB. Mechanistically, GPNMB might inhibit NET formation partly via modulating the Rac-ROS pathway after binding to the CD44 receptor. Finally, our data indicated that delayed rGPNMB administration retained neuroprotective impact in ischemic stroke. Our study revealed the importance of GPNMB in modulating NET formation and suggested a potential target for manipulating post-stroke neutrophil-associated neuroinflammation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03894-0.Keywords: Ischemic stroke, Microglia, Neutrophil, GPNMB, NETs

Introduction

Ischemic stroke is the leading cause of disability and mortality worldwide, and post-stroke neuroinflammation determines the outcome of ischemic stroke to a large extent [1, 2]. Microglia, resident immune cells of the central nervous system, act as a double-edged sword in post-stroke neuroinflammation depending on the different stages of the disease [3]. Microglia contribute greatly to the immediate post-stroke neuroinflammation response by releasing pro-inflammatory cytokines and subsequently recruiting peripheral immune cells, thereby inducing a more severe secondary inflammatory cascade [4, 5]. Conversely, microglia also play a critical role in resolving neuroinflammation through mechanisms such as engulfing immune cells and damage-associated molecular patterns [6, 7]. Currently, the underlying mechanism governing post-stroke microglia activity is still in its infancy.

Immediately after stroke, various kinds of peripheral cells infiltrate into the damaged brain and exacerbate brain injury [8]. Among these cells, neutrophils are the first-line responders and key contributor to neuroinflammation after ischemia [9]. Among the versatile functions of neutrophils, neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), composed of chromatin, microbicidal agents and proteins, mediate the detrimental role of neutrophils in ischemic stroke [10, 11]. From both clinical and experimental investigation, the levels of NETs in the serum was revealed to be remarkably elevated after ischemic stroke and negatively correlated with the prognosis [12]. Currently, efforts have been spared to develop drugs inhibiting Neutrophil infiltration and NET formation, unfortunately, the drugs have mostly failed in phase III clinical trials [13–15]. Microglia have been revealed to display a close crosstalk with neutrophils to modulate the infiltration and activation of neutrophils [16]. Microglia participate in the recruitment of neutrophils into the infarcted brain [17]. For example, our previous work reported that microglial lncRNA U90926 promoted neutrophil infiltration via upregulating CXCL2 expression [18]. Microglia also phagocytize both apoptotic and live neutrophils, thereby reducing neutrophil infiltration [19]. For example, Li et al. reported that resolvin D1 reprogramed energy metabolism, thereby promoting microglia to phagocytosis of neutrophils after ischemic stroke [20]. However, the study about whether microglia modulate the NET formation of neutrophils and the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, which might provide a novel perspective for manipulating post-stroke neuroinflammation.

Glycoprotein non-metastatic melanoma protein B (GPNMB), an endogenous type 1 transmembrane glycoprotein, is mainly cleaved by the ADAM family, like a disintegrin and metalloproteinase 8 (ADAM8) and ADAM10. Then, the extracellular domain is released and binds to the corresponding receptors to play its biological function [21, 22]. Interestingly, GPNMB has been gradually revealed to modulate neuroinflammation in central nervous system diseases [23]. For example, GPNMB ameliorates neuroinflammation in subarachnoid hemorrhage in mice [24]. In Parkinson’s disease, GPNMB attenuates astrocyte inflammatory responses through the cluster of differentiation 44 (CD44) receptor [21]. However, the precise expression pattern of GPNMB and the mechanisms by which it modulates neuroinflammation in central nervous system disorders, such as ischemic stroke, remain to be fully elucidated. In this study, we found that GPNMB was remarkably upregulated in microglia after ischemic stroke, and the CD44 receptor was highly expressed on neutrophils. Given that CD44 is a principal receptor of GPNMB, we proposed a testable hypothesis that GPNMB may regulate the function of neutrophils after binding to the CD44 receptor, thereby modulating post-stroke neuroinflammation. In this study, we focused on the impacts of GPNMB on ischemic stroke outcome and neutrophil function, as well as the underlying mechanism.

Results

GPNMB is remarkably elevated in ischemic stroke and mainly distributed in microglia

To investigate the potential key molecules modulating microglial activation in ischemic stroke, we reanalyzed our previous single-cell RNA (scRNA) and spatial transcriptomic sequencing of ischemic brain tissue at different time points (Sham, m3h, m12h, m3d) after stroke [25]. We observed a remarkable upregulation of Gpnmb expression in ischemic brain, and Gpnmb was predominantly localized in the ischemic region, including the infarcted core and penumbra (Fig. 1A-C). Consistently, the published scRNA and spatial transcriptomic sequencing data from another mouse stroke model, photothrombosis (PT), also revealed that PT induced obvious elevation of Gpnmb (Fig. S1A-D). Additionally, the qPCR and immunoblot analysis confirmed the upregulation of GPNMB in the penumbra region after ischemic stroke (Fig. 1D-F). Given that GPNMB was mainly cleaved and secreted into the bloodstream to play its functions, Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was performed and revealed a significant elevation of GPNMB protein in the serum after middle cerebral artery occlusion (MCAO), particularly at the third day post-stroke (Fig. 1G). Moreover, GPNMB was significantly upregulated in the ischemic brain of stroke patients compared to the control individuals (Fig. 1H-I). We examined the expression of Gpnmb in the brain, heart, liver, spleen, kidney, and lung tissues of mice, and our findings indicated that Gpnmb mRNA displayed the most pronounced increase in the brain after experimental stroke (Fig. S2). Then, we further investigated the distribution of GPNMB in the ischemic brain. Unsupervised clustering and cell type annotation of our scRNA-seq demonstrated that Gpnmb gene was mainly expressed in microglia and macrophage in ischemic stroke (Fig. S3A-C). Consistently, the immunostaining assay confirmed that GPNMB was mainly colocalized with microglia (Iba1+), mildly in astrocytes (GFAP+), but scarcely in neurons (NeuN+) after MCAO 3 days, and the upregulation of GPNMB was most dramatic in microglia (Fig. 1J-K). PLX5622 was used to deplete microglia in mice prior to MCAO, and the ELISA analysis revealed that the upregulated GPNMB level in the serum of MCAO mice was almost reduced to the baseline level after microglia depletion (Fig. 1L), further confirming that GPNMB was predominantly originates from microglia. And we confirmed the upregulation of Gpnmb level in microglia isolated from the ischemic brain tissue, especially at 3 days post-stroke (Fig. 1M). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that GPNMB was increased in the brain after ischemia, and the GPNMB+ cells were predominant microglia (Fig. 1N-Q). Collectively, these essential results demonstrated that GPNMB was significantly elevated after ischemic stroke and mainly distributed in microglia.

GPNMB protects against ischemic brain injury

To further elucidate the role of GPNMB in the outcome of stroke, global GPNMB knockout (KO) mice (GPNMB−/−) and recombinant GPNMB protein (rGPNMB) were used. At first, we confirmed the efficiency of GPNMB deletion in the GPNMB KO mice (Fig. S4). GPNMB−/− mice displayed worsen ischemic brain injury, as shown by the enlarged infarct volume (Fig. 2A-B), upregulated modified neurological severity score (mNSS) (Fig. 2C) and decreased grip strength (Fig. 2D). Moreover, the Fluoro-JadeB (FJB) (Fig. 2E-F) and TUNEL (Fig. S5A-B) staining demonstrated that GPNMB knockout definitely exacerbated neuronal loss in the ischemic area. In addition, rGPNMB was stereotactic injected into the brain of mice immediately after MCAO surgery, then, neurological functional assessments and 2, 3, 5-triphenyl tetrazolium chloride (TTC) staining were conducted. It is evident that, compared to the control group, the rGPNMB injected mice exhibited a markedly reduced infarction volume (Fig. 2G-H), a lower mNSS scores (Fig. 2I) and an enhanced grip strength (Fig. 2J). In addition, FJB (Fig. 2K-L) and TUNEL (Fig. S5C-D) staining illustrated that rGPNMB alleviated neuronal loss in the ischemic cerebral region. Taken together, our results uncovered the role of GPNMB in protecting against ischemic brain injury.

CD44 is abundantly distributed on neutrophils after MCAO

Currently, CD44 was reported to be the most important receptor of GPNMB, thus we further investigated the expression pattern of CD44 in ischemic stroke. Our scRNA-seq analysis revealed an upregulation of Cd44 expression following MCAO, similar to Gpnmb (Fig. 3A). Spatial transcriptomic analysis also indicated that Cd44 was primarily expressed in the ischemic region, including the infarcted core and penumbra (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, scRNA-seq data demonstrated that Cd44 was extremely highly localized in neutrophils, with relative lower expression in microglia, macrophages and T cells (Fig. 3C-D). Neutrophils displayed a gradual upregulation of CD44 expression after MCAO (Fig. 3E). Similarly, the published scRNA-seq by other researchers indicated a marked increase in Cd44 expression within the infarct area in PT model, particularly in neutrophils (Fig. S6). Flow cytometry analysis illustrated a significant rise in CD44-positive cells post-MCAO, with CD44 expression levels in neutrophils being notably higher than those in microglia and other infiltrated immune cells (Fig. 3F-H). In addition, the flow cytometry analysis also revealed that ischemia induced a significant upregulation of CD44 expression in neutrophils in the blood at 3 days after MCAO (Fig. 3I-J). In vitro, neutrophils were activated by Phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA), and we observed a marked increase in CD44 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3K-N). Additionally, co-immunostaining and co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays confirmed the interaction between CD44 and GPNMB in neutrophils in vitro (Fig. 3O-P), which was further confirmed by immunostaining in vivo (Fig. S7). Thus, our results illustrated that CD44 was significantly increased in neutrophils after ischemia and interacted with GPNMB.

GPNMB reduces the formation of NETs

We further investigated the impact of GPNMB on neutrophils in ischemic stroke. Combined with RNA-seq analysis, we found that rGPNMB exerted no influence on the expression of Il1, Il6, Tnf, while induced a reduction of Hmox1 in PMA-activated neutrophils, suggesting that GPNMB might modulate the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and formation of NETs [26] (Fig. S8). H3Cit is recognized as a key marker for NET formation, and co-staining of H3Cit and Ly6G could specifically mark NETs. Immunoblot (Fig. 4A-B) and immunostaining analysis (Fig. 4C-D) demonstrated that rGPNMB significantly reduced the NET formation in vitro. Moreover, rGPNMB administration could definitely reduce the level of NETs in the ischemic brain, as shown by the immunoblot (Fig. 4E-F), flow cytometry (Fig. 4G-H) and immunostaining analysis (Fig. 4I-J). Consistently, rGPNMB induced downregulation of NETs level in the serum of MCAO mice (Fig. 4K). In contrast, we found that the GPNMB−/− mice displayed elevated level of NETs in the ischemic brain and serum when compared to the WT mice (Fig. 4L-P). In comparison to neutrophils co-cultured with WT microglia, those co-cultured with GPNMB KO microglia demonstrated an enhanced formation of NETs upon stimulation with PMA (Fig. 4Q-R). Furthermore, we found that co-cultured with neutrophils induced death of neuron, and pretreatment neutrophils with PMA exacerbated neuronal death. As expected, rGPNMB could alleviate the neurotoxicity of PMA-activated neutrophils (Fig. 4S-T). In conclusion, our findings indicated that GPNMB inhibited NET formation and reduced the neurotoxicity associated with neutrophils.

CD44 functions importantly in mediating the role of GPNMB inhibiting NET formation

To investigate whether the CD44 receptor contributes to the neuroprotective effect of GPNMB in ischemic brain injury, CD44 KO mice were employed. When rGPNMB was injected into CD44 KO and WT mice after MCAO, we found that rGPNMB could alleviate ischemic brain injury in WT mice, as shown by the reduced infarct volume (Fig. 5A-B) and mNSS scores (Fig. 5C), increased grip strength (Fig. 5D), and less neuronal cell loss (Fig. 5E-F). However, we found that CD44 knockout could partially inhibit the neuroprotective effects of rGPNMB (Fig. 5A-F). Moreover, the ELISA analysis demonstrated that CD44 knockout could alleviate rGPNMB-induced reduction of NETs in the serum of MCAO mice (Fig. 5G). In addition, our results showed that rGPNMB exerted no significant effect on NET formation in PMA-activated neutrophils isolated from CD44 KO mice (Fig. 5H-K). Taken together, our results revealed that the CD44 receptor contributed to the effects of GPNMB on inhibiting NET formation and alleviating ischemic brain injury.

Negative control of NET formation contributes to the neuroprotective effect of GPNMB

To further investigate the effect of GPNMB on neutrophils and NET formation in vivo, the mice received a single dose of anti-Ly6G antibody before MCAO to deplete neutrophils. The depletion efficiency was subsequently assessed by flow cytometry in both peripheral blood and brain tissue (Fig. S9). Our results demonstrated that depletion of neutrophils prior to stroke could reduce the neuroprotective effect of rGPNMB, as shown by the TTC staining (Fig. 6A-B), behavioral assessments (Fig. 6C-D) and FJB staining (Fig. 6E-F). As expected, the immunoblot and immunostaining analysis confirmed similar level of NET formation in both control and rGPNMB treated mice after MCAO once the neutrophils were deleted (Fig. 6G-H). Furthermore, combined with PLX5622 feed mice, we found that rGPNMB still displayed neuroprotective effects in ischemic brain injury despite prior depletion of microglia, further proving the neutrophil contributed importantly to the neuroprotective effect of GPNMB (Fig. S10). Next, DNase I was used to inhibit NET formation and injected immediately following MCAO and at 12 h after MCAO in both WT and GPNMB KO mice. Inhibition of NETs via DNase I could reduce the difference in the outcome between WT and GPNMB KO mice (Fig. 6I-L). These findings suggested that inhibition of NET formation contribute to the neuroprotective effect of GPNMB in ischemic brain injury.

The Rac-ROS pathway is involved in the underlying mechanism of GPNMB controlling NET formation

To investigate the mechanism by which GPNMB modulates NET formation, we conducted a KEGG enrichment analysis of our sequencing data and found significant alterations in small GTPases in PMA-activated neutrophils after rGPNMB treatment (Fig. S11). ROS are essential for NET formation, which are primarily produced by the NADPH oxidase complex, consisting of five oxidase units and the Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate (Rac) GTPase [27]. First, we found that NADPH oxidase activity was suppressed after rGPNMB treatment (Fig. S12A). Therefore, we investigated whether ROS contributed to the impact of GPNMB modulating NET formation. DCFDA is a fluorescent ROS indicator, used to measure ROS production, the flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that rGPNMB could reduce ROS production in PMA-activated neutrophils in vitro (Fig. 7A-B). Similar results were observed when ROS were detected by a luminol dependent chemiluminescence assay (Fig. 7C-D). Moreover, rGPNMB administration also decreased ROS production in neutrophils infiltrated in the ischemic brain in vivo (Fig. 7E-F). Furthermore, the antioxidant N-acetylcysteine (NAC) was applied to inhibit ROS production, and pretreatment of which could alleviate the impact of GPNMB inhibiting NET formation (Fig. 7G-H). The above results suggested that ROS production contributed to the impact of GPNMB modulating NET formation. Then, we further evaluated the activation of Rac GTPase in neutrophils via immunostaining with a Rac-GTP antibody [28], we found that rGPNMB-treated cells displayed weaker activation of Rac (Fig. 7I-J). In addition, Rac inhibitor NSC23766 was further applied to investigate whether GPNMB modulates ROS production via modulating Rac activation. The immunostaining analysis demonstrated that Rac inhibition reduced NET formation and alleviate the impact of GPNMB on NET formation (Fig. 7K-L). Furthermore, using Cl-amidine, an inhibitor of PAD4, Sytox Green (SG) staining and immunoblot analysis indicated that the function of GPNMB modulating NET formation is likely independent of PAD4 involvement (Fig. S12B-E). Collectively, our results revealed that the Rac-ROS pathway participated in mediating the role of GPNMB modulating NET formation.

Delayed rGPNMB administration alleviates the outcome of experimental stroke

Neutrophils immediately infiltrate into the ischemic brain, with the number of neutrophils infiltrating in the parenchyma peaked at 24–48 h after stroke [29]. In this part, we sought to investigate whether delayed administration of rGPNMB at 12 h post-stroke conferred a neuroprotective effect in ischemic brain injury (Fig. 8A). TTC staining and neurological function assessments showed that delayed administration of rGPNMB retained a neuroprotective effect in ischemic stroke (Fig. 8B-E), which was further confirmed by less neuronal damage (Fig. 8F-G) and reduced blood-brain barrier damage (Fig. 8H-K). As expected, delayed administration of rGPNMB definitely decreased NET formation in the ischemic brain tissue and serum of MCAO mice (Fig. 8L-N). These results collectively demonstrated that delayed rGPNMB administration maintained neuroprotective effects, enhancing its potential applicability in clinical translation.

Discussion

Neutrophil infiltration has been identified to be related with poor prognosis of ischemic stroke patients, and NETs contributed greatly to the detrimental role of neutrophils [30, 31]. This study demonstrated that GPNMB was remarkably increased in the ischemic brain and mainly distributed in microglia, which could protect against brain injury. GPNMB inhibited the formation of NETs, and the CD44 receptor contributed importantly to this impact. Mechanistically, the Rac-ROS pathway was found to be involved in mediating the impact of GPNMB inhibiting NET formation. Moreover, our results demonstrated that delayed rGPNMB administration retained neuroprotective impact in experimental stroke.

GPNMB, also referred to as osteoactivin (OA), dendritic cell-heparan integrin ligand, or hematopoietic growth factor-induced neurokinin type 1, is recognized as a type I transmembrane protein, which can be cleaved by certain agents, such as ADAM10, and subsequently released as a secretory protein to exert regulatory effects [32]. GPNMB is widely distributed in bone, hematopoietic systems, and skin [33]. Early investigations predominantly focused on its role in various cancers, including melanoma, renal carcinoma [34, 35]. In nervous system, research has primarily concentrated on the involvement of GPNMB in immune-related diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, and multiple sclerosis [36–38]. Recent studies have revealed that GPNMB expression underwent significant alterations after stroke and played a neuroprotective role in ischemic brain injury [39, 40]. However, the detailed expression pattern, like the distribution of GPNMB in ischemic stroke, and the exact mechanism underlying the neuroprotective effects of GPNMB remain elusive. In this study, combined with scRNA and spatial transcriptomic sequencing, we revealed that GPNMB was remarkably increased in the ischemic brain, and mainly distributed in microglia. Our results also revealed that GPNMB was elevated in the brain of stroke patients compared to the non-neurological controls. We confirmed the neuroprotective effects of GPNMB in ischemic stroke via transgenic mice and recombinant GPNMB protein, and we explored the potential mechanisms underlying the function of GPNMB in experimental stroke. The CD44 receptor contributed greatly to the protective effect of GPNMB in ischemic stroke. In addition, our results uncovered that GPNMB exerted its neuroprotective effects partly via modulating the function of neutrophils.

However, in the present study, only global GPNMB knockout mice were employed. Although microglial depletion using PLX5622, together with flow cytometry analysis, suggested that GPNMB is mainly enriched in microglia, its expression is also detectable in other cell types, including macrophages, indicating potential contributions from non-microglial sources. Therefore, the cell type–specific effects of GPNMB require further clarification using conditional knockout strategies. In addition, in vivo administration of rGPNMB was performed via intracranial stereotactic injection. This approach inevitably introduces procedural confounding factors, such as potential exacerbation of brain injury and neuroinflammation, which may influence the interpretation of the observed effects. Future studies are warranted to explore less invasive and more clinically feasible delivery methods, which may not only provide a clearer evaluation of GPNMB function but also facilitate the development of translational therapeutic strategies targeting NET-mediated injury in ischemic stroke.

Previous studies have revealed that GPNMB signals to multiple cell types via several different receptors, like the Na+/K+ ATPase, αβ-integrins, FGFR1, heparan sulfate proteoglycans, and CD44 [41–44]. Among the receptors, CD44 is considered one of the key receptors mediating the biological function of GPNMB. In this study, combined with CD44 knockout mice, we found that CD44 knockout could reduce the neuroprotective effects of GPNMB in ischemic stroke, indicating that CD44 participated in mediating the protective impact of GPNMB in ischemic brain injury. However, in the present study, only global CD44 KO mice were utilized. We demonstrated, through in vitro experiments using neutrophils derived from CD44 KO mice, that GPNMB-mediated regulation of NET formation requires the involvement of CD44. Nevertheless, the current evidence remains indirect, as definitive approaches such as receptor blockade, competitive binding assays, or cell type-specific deletion are not performed. Therefore, whether and how GPNMB plays its function in stroke via other receptors in ischemic stroke also need further investigation. The interaction between GPNMB and CD44 has been extensively studied, predominantly in astrocytes and macrophages [21, 40]. In this study, combined with scRNA and spatial transcriptomic sequencing, we found that the CD44 receptor was highly expressed on neutrophils after experimental stroke. In addition, combined with Co-IP and co-immunostaining analysis, we confirmed the interaction between GPNMB and CD44 in neutrophils in ischemic stroke. Thus, we hypothesized that GPNMB might modulate the function of neutrophils to exert its neuroprotective effects in ischemic stroke partly via CD44.

The accumulation of NETs contributes to the pathology of ischemic brain injury, and this phenomenon can be blocked by depletion of neutrophils [45]. Our results illustrated that GPNMB could inhibit NET formation in ischemic stroke, and depletion of neutrophils or inhibition of NETs with DNase I could significantly reduce the neuroprotective effect of rGPNMB and the difference in outcome between WT and GPNMB KO mice. Consistently, we found that CD44 knockout in neutrophils alleviated the effects of rGPNMB inhibiting NET formation in vitro. Previous studies have revealed that GPNMB could alleviate post-stroke neuroinflammation, and mainly focused on its impact of inhibiting proinflammatory activation of glial cells [40]. For the first time, we reported that GPNMB may act as a novel mediator linked the crosstalk between microglia and neutrophils, and revealed that GPNMB protected against ischemic brain injury partly via modulating the function of neutrophils, particularly via inhibiting NET formation. However, the exact mechanism about the interaction between GPNMB and CD44 in neutrophils remains unclear, further studies of which might help developing novel drugs targeting NETs-induced injury in the ischemic brain.

Cellular ROS is essential for cellular signaling, which is required for NET formation in neutrophils with almost all stimuli [46]. ROS generated by NADPH oxidase activate PAD4, a vital enzyme for NET formation, which further citrullinates histones and facilitates chromatin decondensation [47]. In addition, ROS could stimulate myeloperoxidase (MPO), resulting in the activation and translocation of neutrophil elastase from neutrophils granules, which in turn leads to chromatin decondensation [48]. Here, we showed that the Rac-ROS pathway was involved in the inhibitory effect of GPNMB on NET formation in experimental stroke. In addition to NET formation, the accumulation of ROS in neutrophils is also important for triggering apoptosis, necroptosis, necrosis [49], whether GPNMB also exerts its neuroprotective impact in ischemic stroke via these or other processes needs our further investigation.

Prominent neutrophil infiltration into the brain parenchyma was first observed at 12 h, peaked at 1–2 days after t-MCAO, and gradually declined thereafter [29, 50]. Thus, we explored whether delayed rGPNMB administration could alleviate ischemic brain injury. Interestingly, we found that administration of rGPNMB at 12 h post-MCAO could also significantly improve the outcome of stroke and inhibited the NET formation, further suggesting that GPNMB is a potential therapeutic target. However, our investigation was limited to examining early changes and did not assess whether GPNMB exerts a long-term impact on neutrophils, which remains an area warranting further exploration in future research.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

The detail information of antibodies and other reagents used in our study are listed in Additional file 2: Table S1 and Additional file 3: Table S2.

Human brain tissue

In our experiment, we used some human brain tissue samples. The brains of ischemic stroke patients or non-neurological diseases were provided by the Chinese Brain Bank Center (approval number: 2021-scuec-034) as described previously [51].

Animals

The C57BL/6J mice were procured from the Institute of Model Animal at Nanjing University (Jiangsu, China). CD44 knockout (CD44−/−) mice on the C57BL/6J background were generously supplied by Professor Sun Cheng from Nantong University. Additionally, the GPNMB knockout (GPNMB−/−) mice were purchased from Cyagen (Jiangsu, China). All mice were housed in a specific-pathogen-free (SPF) environment, with lighting conditions regulated to a 12-hour light/dark cycle. The mice were provided with unrestricted access to food and water. All animal experiments were approved by Animal Care Committee of Nanjing University (Ethical clearance No. 2024AE0107).

Transient-middle cerebral artery occlusion (t-MCAO)

The temporary ischemia model was constructed by 6 − 0 silicon-coated monofilament nylon suture (Doccol Corporation, MA, USA) as described previously [51]. After anesthesia, mice were fixed supinely, and the right common carotid artery, external carotid artery and internal carotid artery were separated. A small incision on the external carotid artery was made, the suture was inserted and moved to the internal carotid artery. A laser Doppler flowmeter (Perimed Corporation, Stockholm, Sweden) was used to monitor cerebral blood flow.The duration of ischemia in the MCAO model was approximately 60 min, then the suture was removed and mice were placed on a heating pad for recovery. The overall mortality rate of t-MCAO mice was approximately 9%. The exclusion criteria were primarily based on laser Doppler flowmetry measurements: animals that died before reaching the predefined endpoints or those in which cerebral blood flow did not decrease to less than 20% of baseline during model induction were excluded from subsequent analyses [52, 53]. The Sham group suffered from similar operations but did not include the insertion of suture. All mice were randomly assigned to different groups, and were housed under identical conditions. Behavioral assessments were conducted by an investigator who was blinded to the group allocation.

Measurement of cerebral infarction volume

The TTC was applied to quantify the cerebral infarction volume [54]. At 24–72 h after MCAO, the brains were removed and then placed on a brain section model and cut into 1 mm thick coronal sections, which were incubated in 2% TTC staining solution prepared in ddH2O. The infarcted part was white while the normal part turned red. The cerebral infarction volume was calculated using ImageJ software. The infarction volume (%) = (contralateral area - ipsilateral non-infarct area) / (2 × contralateral area ) × 100%.

Neurobehavioral tests

The grip strength was measured by allowing the mice to grasp and pull the platform of the grip strength meter (Ugo Basile, Gemonio, Italy). The maximum value of the three tests was recorded as the final data. The mNSS scores was used to evaluate the motion, balance, reflex and sensory of mice, which were measured in sequence and the final total score was calculated. The score range was from 0 to 12 points, and the higher the score, the more severe the neurological dysfunction.

Stereotaxic intracranial injection

After anesthesia, mice were fixed on the stereotaxic instrument (RWD, China) in a prone position. After incision and skull exposure, the injection site was determined by referring to the standard brain atlas of mice to inject the rGPNMB or vehicle as control 2 µg per mice. In this experiment, the injection sites were: 1.5 mm lateral to the midline, 1.1 mm posterior to bregma, and 2.0 mm ventral from bregma. After injection, the needle was retained for 10 min to prevent the reflux of the infused fluid. Then, the incision was sutured and the mice were placed under a heating pad for recovery.

Microglial culture

Primary microglia was extracted and cultured as described previously [55]. The cortexes of neonatal mice were dissected and dissociated, and then cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and 1% antibiotics. After 10–11 days of culture, primary microglia were isolated by gentle shaking (200 rpm for 10 min) and inoculated onto fresh plates. The purity of the obtained primary microglial culture exceeded 95%.

Neutrophil extraction

After removal of the connective tissues, the long bones (femurs and tibias) of mice were harvested, and bone marrow cells were flushed out using RPMI-1640 medium. The collected cells were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min and subsequently resuspended to prepare the single-cell suspension. Neutrophils were then isolated according to the manufacturer’s instructions using a commercial isolation kit (Celbio, China). Briefly, 4 mL of Separation Solution A, 2 mL of Separation Solution B, and 3 mL of the single-cell suspension were sequentially added into centrifuge tube, followed by centrifugation at 800 × g for 25 min using slow acceleration and deceleration settings. The intermediate cell layer was aspirated and the cells were resuspended in 1 × PBS. The suspension was centrifuged at 750 × g for 5 min, followed by washing and centrifugation, finally, the supernatant was discarded and the pellet was resuspended to obtain neutrophils. In this experiment, we stimulated neutrophils in vitro with 100 nM PMA and treated neutrophils with rGPNMB at a final concentration of 10 ng/µL.

Primary neuronal culture

The cortexes of fetal mice were dissected and isolated in Hanks’ balanced salt solution. Following digestion and repeatedly blowing, the resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm mesh filter, centrifuged, and subsequently resuspended. Cells were then seeded onto culture plates pre-coated with poly-D-lysine. Half of the culture medium was changed every two days. Neuronal cells prepared using this protocol were ready for use after one week, achieving a purity exceeding 95%.

Co-culture of neutrophils and primary neurons

We employed a Transwell system to co-culture primary neurons and neutrophils. Primary neurons were seeded into 12-well plates at a density of 6–7 × 10^5 cells per well. After seven days, isolated neutrophils were seeded into each transwell insert at a density of 1–2 × 10^5 cells. After incubating for one hour, specific stimuli were added to the culture medium of neutrophils. Subsequently, the cells were harvested following an additional hour of incubation.

Co-culture of neutrophils and primary microglia

Microglia were collected and seeded into confocal dishes at a density of 1 × 10^6 cells per well. Neutrophils were isolated as described above and co-cultured with microglia at a density of 3 × 10^6 cells per well in RPMI-1640 complete medium. After 30 min of cell seeding, 100 nM PMA was added to each stimulation group. Following 1 h of stimulation, Sytox Green staining was performed to assess NET formation.

Microglia depletion

Mice were fed with AIN-76 A containing 1200 mg PLX5622 diet from 14 days before the MCAO operation and continued to the end of the experiment, which depleted more than 90% microglia in the brain. The control group was fed AIN-76 A diet for the same period of time.

Neutrophil depletion

Twelve hours before MCAO, 125 µg of Ly6G neutralizing antibody (GeneTex, USA) was intraperitoneally injected into mice, which could consume neutrophils in the mice for subsequent experiments. The efficiency of neutrophil depletion was determined by flow cytometric analysis of neutrophils in both peripheral blood and the ipsilateral hemisphere of mice.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells or tissues using TRIzol-chloroform. The quality and quantity of RNA were evaluated using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. (Thermo). And the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Vazyme, China) was used for reverse transcription to synthesize cDNA. Quantitative PCR was performed by using SYBR Green qPCR premix (AG) and the Step One Plus PCR system (Applied Biosystems, CA, USA). The primer sequences used are listed in the Additional file 4: Table S3.

Immunoblot

Brain tissues or cells were lysed using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% protease inhibitor mixture. Protein concentrations of the samples were quantified using bicinchoninic acid assay. Protein samples were normalized to equal concentrations and mixed with loading buffer. The same amount of protein, typically 25–30 µg per well, was loaded into the SDS-PAGE gel wells. Electrophoresis was conducted at 120 V for 90 min, followed by transfer onto membranes at a constant current. After blocking, the membranes were incubated with the primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. On the following day, after the removal of the primary antibody and washing several times by TBST, the appropriate secondary antibody was applied to facilitate chemiluminescent detection. The gray values of the bands were quantified using ImageJ software. β-actin or GAPDH served as the internal reference for this experiment. The antibodies used in this study and their dilution ratios are provided in Additional file 2.

Immunofluorescence staining

After anesthesia, the mice were sequentially perfused transcardially with 1× PBS followed by 4% PFA. The brains were removed for fixation and dehydration. Frozen brain sections with a thickness of 20 μm were prepared for subsequent staining experiments. For cell staining, cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min after stimulation. The cells and brain tissue were permeated with 0.25% Triton X-100 for 20 min, blocked with 2% BSA at room temperature for 2 h, and then incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. On the following day, the sections or cells were washed and incubated with the corresponding secondary antibody for 2 h. Then, the nuclei were stained with DAPI for 15 min and images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope (Olympus, Japan). The antibodies used in this study and their dilution ratios are provided in Additional file 2.

Flow cytometry

After anesthesia, mice were perfused with 1× PBS to eliminate the influence of peripheral blood neutrophils. The mouse brains were harvested, mechanically dissociated, and filtered to obtain single-cell suspensions. Immune cells were isolated using 30–70% percoll (GE Healthcare BioSciences, NJ, USA). The cells were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies targeting mouse CD45, CD11b, Ly6G, F4/80, CD44, H3Cit, GPNMB, and then analyzed with the FACS system (BD Biosciences, CA, USA). The antibodies used in this study and their dilution ratios are provided in Additional file 2. To detect ROS production, the neutrophils were incubated with 20 µM DCFDA, a general oxidative stress indicator, for 30 min at 37 °C. After washing, the cells were analyzed using flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, CA, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The concentrations of H3Cit and GPNMB proteins in the serum of mice were detected using the corresponding ELISA kit (Yobibio, China). In strict accordance with the reagent provider’s protocol, the Tecan microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland) was used to measure the optical density (OD) values at 450 nm wavelength for quantification.

Co-immunoprecipitation

Cells were lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer containing 1mM PMSF and incubated overnight with the anti-GPNMB antibody or normal rabbit IgG at 4 °C. On the second day, the lysates was incubated again with new pre-cleaned protein A/G agarose beads (Millipore) at 4 °C for 2 h. After washing, The SDS-PAGE loading system was prepared by eluting the Co-IP proteins with 1 × immunoblot loading buffer for subsequent immunoblot experimental detection.

Fluoro-jade B staining

After drying the brain slices at 50–60 °C for 1 h, they were incubated in ethanol for 2 min, 0.06% potassium permanganate for 10 min, and 0.1% acetic acid solution containing 0.001% Fluoro-Jade B (Biosensis, TR-150-FJB) for 10 min. After washing and air-drying, the sections were cleared in xylene for 5 min and coverslipped. Following Fluoro-Jade B staining, degenerating neurons exhibited green fluorescence under 488-nm excitation (Olympus, Japan).

Evans blue staining

At 3 days post MCAO, 200 µL of 2% Evans blue dye working solution was injected into each mouse using the tail vein instrument (EWBASIS, China). 3–6 h later, 1 × PBS was used for perfusion, then the brain was removed. The residual Evans blue dye content in the brain tissue of mice was extracted using N, N-dimethylformamide, and the absorbance values at 500, 620, and 740 nm were measured using the Tecan microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland) to calculate Evans blue extravasation in brain tissues. Evans blue extravasation was quantified using the following formula: Evans blue extravasation = [A620 − (A500 + A740)/2] / tissue wet weight.

Sytox green staining

As described in the previous experiment [56], after stimulating the cells, they were incubated with 0.5 µM Sytox Green dye in the 37 °C incubator for 10 min. After washing, the cells were fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, and stained with DAPI. Confocal microscope (Olympus, Japan) can be used for photography to directly observe the NETs.

TUNEL staining

Briefly, sections were first subjected to NeuN immunofluorescence staining according to standard protocols, including incubation with primary and secondary antibodies. After completion of immunostaining, sections were equilibrated in equilibration buffer at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, sections were incubated with a reaction mixture containing terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase enzyme and BrightRed Labeling Mix at 37 °C for 1 h. Following incubation, sections were washed, counterstained with DAPI, and mounted for fluorescence imaging.

Reactive oxygen species detection

In this study, we employed the ROS assay kit from Beyotime to quantify the ROS generated by neutrophils in vitro. Following the manufacturer’s protocol, DCFH-DA was diluted in the culture medium at a 1:1000 ratio. The cells and dye were incubated at 37 °C for 15 min. After washing, fluorescence was determined by microplate reader (Tecan, Switzerland) with excitation of 488 nm and emission of 525 nm. Statistical analysis was performed, and the area under the curve was calculated.

NADP+ enzyme activity assay

NADP⁺ and NADPH levels were measured using MCE assay kit according to the instructions. After the indicated treatments, cells were harvested and lysed with lysis buffer (200 µL per 1 × 10^6 cells). The lysates were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected for subsequent analysis. One portion of the supernatant was used to determine total NADP⁺ + NADPH content, while another portion was incubated at 60 °C for 30 min to decompose NADP⁺, allowing selective measurement of NADPH.

For enzymatic detection, 10 µL of each sample was mixed with 90 µL of G6PDH working solution and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 10 µL of chromogenic reagent was added, and the reaction mixture was incubated in the dark. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

The NADP⁺ level was calculated by subtracting NADPH from total NADP (NADP⁺+ NADPH), and the ratio of NADP⁺ to total NADP was determined accordingly.

Transcriptome sequencing

After stimulating the cells according to the experimental design, total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol, and the quality and quantity of RNA were evaluated using the Nanodrop™ OneC spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA), LabChip GX Touch system (Revvity, China) and Qubit 3.0 fluorometer with the Qubit™ RNA Broad Range Assay kit (Thermo, USA). RNA transcriptome sequencing and subsequent analysis were carried out by the SEQHEALTH (Wuhan, China).

Statistical analysis

Data are displayed as the mean ± standard error of mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments and all statistical tests were performed via GraphPad Prism 8.0. The Student’s t-test, one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni post-hoc test were applied as appropriate. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
