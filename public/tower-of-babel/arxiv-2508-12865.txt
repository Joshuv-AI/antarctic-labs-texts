An empirical determination of the Cosmic Shoreline
P. Meni-Gallardo, E. Pallé

Abstract
The Cosmic Shoreline concept was introduced as a way to separate planets with and without atmosphere, based on the relationship between the cumulative instellation and the escape velocity observed in the Solar System. The exoplanet community has tried to refine the way we understand the cosmic shoreline in order to provide a consistent tool for establishing the hierarchy for exoplanet observations. This is particularly relevant when trying to unveil small exoplanet atmospheres with the JWST or the upcoming ELTs. Here, our goal is to use an empirical approach to refine the Cosmic Shoreline concept. In particular, we used the data provided by the ExoAtmospheres database, using the largest available sample of exoplanets with confirmed atmospheric detections, and the NASA Exoplanet Archive, along with solar system data. We reconcile limitations in the classical shoreline definition by anchoring our Empirical Cosmic Shoreline (ECS) to both Mars and the irradiated super-Earth 55 Cnc e. The resulting relation exhibits a significantly steeper slope than previously theorized. Notably, it aligns with tentative atmospheric detections for planets like GJ 9827 d, L 98-59 d, and Pi Mensae c, while consistently categorising Solar System moons and dwarf planets according to their atmospheric properties. Applied to planets orbiting M dwarfs, prime targets for habitable-zone studies, the ECS suggests that a larger fraction retain atmospheres than predicted by classical models when using standard I XUV I_{\mathrm{XUV}} estimates. However, incorporating revised XUV fluence histories for low-mass M dwarfs ( M ∗ < 0.35 ​ M ⊙ M_{*}<0.35\,M_{\odot} ; Pass et al. 2025) reveals severe atmospheric vulnerability: only seven small planets ( R ≲ 1.7 ​ R ⊕ R\lesssim 1.7\,R_{\oplus} ) orbit securely within the retention zone of these stars. We finally identify high-priority targets for the JWST Rocky Worlds survey and future ELT observations based on their ECS positioning and Transmission Spectroscopy Metrics. Future efforts must focus on expanding the empirical validations of the ECS, particularly through high-precision observations of borderline candidates and systems with well-constrained XUV histories.



1
Introduction

Exoplanetary science has seen a vast development over the past three decades, leading to a large diversity of confirmed planetary systems. With each new progress, the field is focussing more and more on the search and characterisation of small worlds: sub-Neptunes, super-Earths, and Earth-like planets. This pursuit is driving ambitious space missions and the construction of extremely large ground-based telescopes (ELTs), with temperate, Earth-sized habitable zone planets as key targets.
Following JWST’s successful launch, the next milestone is the ELTs’ first light. By the time the first ELTs operate (2030s), current and upcoming missions such as TESS
(
Ricker et al. 2015
)
, CHEOPS
(
Benz et al. 2021
)
, and PLATO
(
Rauer et al. 2014
)
, plus ground-based surveys, should have identified and characterised the most promising potentially habitable planets. If JWST is not able to do it for a few selected targets, high-resolution spectrographs on ELTs, already in advanced design, will be the first instruments capable of probing habitable zone planet atmospheres
(
Palle et al. 2025a
)
. Their role is to go beyond mass and radius, exploring atmospheric structure, composition, surface conditions, and potential biomarkers
(
Des Marais et al. 2002
)
.

M dwarfs dominate the solar neighbourhood
(
Henry et al. 2018
;
Reylé et al. 2021
)
, and their smaller size facilitates the detection of lower-mass planets. However, theory suggests that planets around M stars are highly vulnerable to atmospheric loss
(
Luger & Barnes 2015
;
Wordsworth 2015
;
Dong et al. 2018
)
, raising concerns about their long-term habitability under intense stellar radiation
(
Schaefer et al. 2016
;
Owen & Mohanty 2016
)
. Currently, we lack even the basic knowledge of whether M dwarf rocky habitable zone planets possess atmospheres. The initial JWST results are ambiguous at best
(
Lustig-Yaeger et al. 2023
;
Moran et al. 2023
)
, prompting a major public DDT programme to address this
(
Redfield et al. 2024
)
. This highlights the need for optimal strategies and target selection to maximise the returns of expensive facilities.

To tackle this problem,
(
Zahnle & Catling 2017
;
Zahnle & Catling 2013
)
explored a planet classification based on the relation between the cumulative radiation received by the planets from the hosting stars and the escape velocity, known as the Cosmic Shoreline. Given the lack of information on exoplanet atmospheres at the time, they tried to constrain the parameters that define this concept by using the most reliable data they had: the Solar System planets. They proposed that a line could be drawn that separates whether the planets are able to retain an atmosphere or not. Using this method they classified the Solar System objects in terms of their atmosphere and extended it to the whole template of exoplanets.

Other recent works have built on the original cosmic shoreline idea.
Ji et al. 2025
revisited the concept of cosmic shoreline, focussing on atmospheric retention of rocky planets, by incorporating hydrodynamic escape models for various atmospheric compositions. They computed time-integrated atmospheric loss across a range of stellar and planetary parameters, considering realistic stellar XUV evolution. Their analysis demonstrates that the shoreline is probably not a sharp boundary, but a broad transition zone shaped by non-linear escape physics and an initial volatile inventory.
Pass et al. 2025
also revised estimates of atmospheric retention for rocky exoplanets orbiting mid-to-late M dwarfs by incorporating updated stellar activity lifetimes and XUV emission histories. Using recent observations of stellar rotation and magnetic activity, the authors find that these stars remain in the saturated regime far longer than previously assumed, leading to significantly higher cumulative XUV fluence. As a result, many of the known planets shift above the cosmic shoreline, implying that only the most massive rocky planets are likely to retain an atmosphere. Other authors
Ih et al. 2025
;
Berta-Thompson et al. 2025
, have tried statistical approaches for detailed small planet characterization and to provide refined frameworks for prioritising observational programmes focused on exploring small planet atmospheres.

Despite the complexity proposed by these authors, which we can not still test with the available data, in this paper, we provide an alternative, empirical definition of the cosmic shoreline, based on the cumulative knowledge to date of exoplanet atmospheric observations and solar system exploration data.

The ExoAtmospheres database is a repository of all exoplanet atmospheric detections, collecting information on the properties of the planet, chemical species detected, atmospheric properties, and related literature. Up to now 58 different chemical species have been detected among 274 explored planets, ranging from ultra-hot Jupiters to temperate rocky worlds.

We downloaded the ExoAtmospheres database and created an up-to-date list of which planets have empirical detections of an atmosphere, that takes into account planet type; atmospheric species detections, featureless spectra retrievals, albedos; etc. While it is not possible to infer unequivocally from the ExoAtmospheres data that a planet has no atmosphere (as a non-detection could always be related to poor data or an atmospheric signal below the detection capability of our instrumentation), it is possible to make a list of those planets that do have an atmosphere via the statistically significant detection of a given chemical species.

3
Methods

3.1
Classical Cosmic Shoreline definition

The ”classical” definition of the cosmic shoreline by
Zahnle & Catling 2017
provides a non-linear relation between the cumulative instellation and the escape velocity, which turns into a linear relation in the logarithmic space. The expected slope for this shoreline is 4, but the zero point remains unconstrained.
Zahnle & Catling 2017
used Mars as their zero point value, assuming that this planet marks the boundary between planets with and without atmospheres in our Solar System.

However, when confronted with the latest atmospheric data from exoplanets, this proposed cosmic shoreline does not fit the observations (see Figure
1
and the discussion in the following sections). In this work, we explore the possibility of using the known population of exoplanets to better constrain this zero-point and slope. But first we need to establish which planets have statistically significant atmospheric detections.

3.2
Which planets have an atmosphere?

There are several planets whose combination of high cumulative instellation and low escape velocity places them in the bordeline of the classical cosmic shoreline, but three of them stand out over the rest. The first of them is
\mathrm{GJ\penalty\ 1132\penalty\ b}

(
Berta-Thompson et al. 2015
)
, a warm Earth-sized planet with a radius of
1.16\,\mathrm{R_{\oplus}}
, a mass of
1.65\,\mathrm{M_{\oplus}}
, an equilibrium temperature of
589\,\mathrm{K}
and a period of
1.63\,\mathrm{days}
orbiting a M3.5 dwarf. Although ExoAtmospheres lists a detection of HCN in its atmosphere
(
Swain et al. 2021
)
, this object is controversial because more recent results
(
May et al. 2023
;
Palle et al. 2025b
)
report non-detections of HCN and featureless spectra. Due to this uncertainty, in this work we do not consider GJ 1132b to have an unequivocally detected atmosphere.

Another planet in the borderline is
\mathrm{LTT\penalty\ 9779\penalty\ b}

(
Pearson 2019
)
, an ultra-hot Neptune, with a radius of
0.42\,\mathrm{R}_{J}
, a mass of
0.09\,\mathrm{M}_{J}
, an equilibrium temperature of
2000\,\mathrm{K}
and a period of
0.79\,\mathrm{days}
orbiting a G8 star.
\mathrm{LTT\penalty\ 9779\penalty\ b}
has different atmospheric species detections such as FeH, CO
2
and H
2
O
(
Edwards et al. 2023
)
and upper limits for OH
(
Zhou et al. 2025
)
. Although
\mathrm{LTT\penalty\ 9779\penalty\ b}
is a solid placement for the cosmic shoreline limit, it is not the best option because using
LTT\penalty\ 9779\penalty\ b
causes one other planet,
55\penalty\ Cnc\penalty\ e
, to fall into the non-atmospheric retention region.

\mathrm{55\penalty\ Cnc\penalty\ e}

(
McArthur et al. 2004
)
is a hot super-Earth with a radius of
1.95\,\mathrm{R_{\oplus}}
, a mass of
8.58\,\mathrm{M_{\oplus}}
, an equilibrium temperature of
1996\,\mathrm{K}
and an orbital period of
0.74\,\mathrm{days}
orbiting a G8V star. It has a very high cumulative instellation, low escape velocity and the recent discovery of a secondary atmosphere
(
Hu et al. 2024
)
. Thus,
\mathrm{55\penalty\ Cnc\penalty\ e}
is currently the best solid candidate to establish the empirical limit between exoplanets retaining and non-retaining atmospheres. We describe this process in the following section.

3.3
The empirical Cosmic Shoreline definition

In Figure
1
, we represent all planets with detected atmospheres, based on the ExoAtmospheres database, and we also include the planets and the major moons of the solar system. The classical cosmic shoreline is marked with a red broken line which clearly leaves out a dozen of planet with strong atmospheric detections (
\mathrm{LTT\penalty\ 9779\penalty\ b}
for example). In the figure, we have two ways of defining a new empirical cosmic shoreline based on the fact that
\mathrm{55\penalty\ Cnc\penalty\ e}
has an atmosphere.

The first is to preserve the slope of the classical Cosmic Shoreline, using
\mathrm{55\penalty\ Cnc\penalty\ e}
to define the zero point. This is shown in Figure
1
as a dotted line. The line leaves Mercury just above it, on the non-retention region. However, this line is problematic as many solar system moons that have no atmospheres would fall below it, on the atmospheric retention region.

The second way is by using both Mars and
\mathrm{55\penalty\ Cnc\penalty\ e}
, based on the original conception of Mars as the edge between planets with or without atmosphere in our solar system. This modifies the slope of the classical cosmic shoreline, leading to a new slope of
\approx
5.89. Taking the form of:

\log_{10}(I_{XUV})\approx 5.89\log_{10}(v_{esc})-4.49

(1)

This second boundary line (dash-dotted in the figure), which we will name from now on the Empirical Cosmic Shoreline (ECS) seems to do extremely well in defining the atmospheric retention regions.

First of all, our choice of
\mathrm{55\penalty\ Cnc\penalty\ e}
and Mars as the two points defining the cosmic shoreline, makes three other key planets,
\rm GJ\penalty\ 9827\penalty\ d
,
\rm L\penalty\ 98-59\penalty\ d
and
\rm Pi\penalty\ Mensae\penalty\ c
, sit practically on top of this same line. These three planets have in common tentative detections of possessing an atmosphere –
\rm GJ\penalty\ 9827\penalty\ d

(
Roy et al. 2023
;
Piaulet-Ghorayeb et al. 2024
)
,
\rm L\penalty\ 98-59\penalty\ d

(
Banerjee et al. 2024a
;
Gressier et al. 2024a
)
,
\rm Pi\penalty\ Mensae\penalty\ c

(
García Muñoz et al. 2021
)
– adding support for this boundary at intermediate cumulative instellation values between
\mathrm{55\penalty\ Cnc\penalty\ e}
and Mars. Moreover, using
\mathrm{55\penalty\ Cnc\penalty\ e}
(and
de facto

\rm Pi\penalty\ Mensae\penalty\ c
) has the advantage that the host stars are G-type, and thus the cumulative insolation flux received by the planets should not be underestimated
(
Pass et al. 2025
)
.

At even lower cumulative instellation, Titan and the dwarf planet Eris in our own solar system also fall on top of this ECS line (Figure
1
). Titan definitely possesses an atmosphere
(
Kuiper 1944
;
Kunde et al. 1981
;
Hörst 2017
)
, while the case of Eris is curious. Eris has a thin, transient atmosphere, due to the periodic sublimation of its surface ices of methane and nitrogen along its orbit
(
Hofgartner et al. 2019
)
. This ECS boundary line is also much more consistent with the solar system objects properties, as it places Europa, Callisto, Ganymede, Haumea and Triton, which have no significant atmospheres (certainly not detectable at interstellar distances), in the non-retention region.

The steeper slope of the ECS is also in line with the findings of
Berta-Thompson et al. 2025
, which approach the definition of a cosmic shoreline from an statistical point of view.

3.4
The Atmosphere Retention Metric

Once established our new ECS, it is worth confronting it with more generalised metrics reported in the literature.
Pass et al. 2025
proposed the concept of and Atmosphere Retention Metrics (ARM), also based on the cumulative instellation that planets suffer and the escape velocity, and described as:

ARM\equiv 4\log_{10}(v_{esc})-\log_{10}(I_{XUV})-ZP_{Mars}

(2)

Where
v_{esc}
is the escape velocity,
I_{XUV}
is the cumulative instellation and
ZP
is an arbitrary zero point, which they chose to be located at the Mars ARM value.

Pass et al. 2025
proposed this metric for planets orbiting stars with
\rm M_{*}\leq 0.35M_{\odot}
, and reconstructed their individual cumulative stellar instellation histories, taking into account all the radiation sources that these planets have undertaken during their lifetime. Here, we make use of these new metrics in our figures, but change the slope value to 5.9 and taking as our zero point the ARM value of
\mathrm{55\penalty\ Cnc\penalty\ e}
, such as:

ARM\equiv 5.89\log_{10}(v_{esc})-\log_{10}(I_{XUV})-ZP_{55Cnce}

(3)

By definition, the zero-point of
\mathrm{55\penalty\ Cnc\penalty\ e}
or Mars are exactly the same as they fall in the same line of the ECS (and the same is true in practice for
\rm GJ\penalty\ 9827\penalty\ d
,
\rm L\penalty\ 98-59\penalty\ d
and
\rm Pi\penalty\ Mensae\penalty\ c
).

4.2
Implications for the JWST
Rocky Worlds
survey

Currently, we lack the basic knowledge of whether small planets around M dwarfs, especially those orbiting in the habitable zone of their stars, have atmospheres at all. The first results from the JWST observations are ambiguous at best
(
Lustig-Yaeger et al. 2023
;
Moran et al. 2023
)
, and to try to solve this issue a very large public DDT programme, named
Rocky Worlds

The

One of the major limitations in establishing a definitive ECS is the lack of clear boundaries outside the solar system, i.e. the lack of established non-existence of exo-atmospheres, as high mean molecular weight atmospheres for many planets do not fall within the detectability limits of JWST. However, repeated observations of specific targets could help determine very stringent upper limits to the placement of the ECS. First efforts are already taking place
(
Piaulet-Ghorayeb et al. 2025

. In this sense, it would be important to observe also candidates near but above the ECS which have favourable TSM values, such as
\rm GJ\penalty\ 12\penalty\ b

\rm L\penalty\ 98-59\penalty\ b
or
\rm GJ\penalty\ 486\penalty\ b

These solid ”delimiting” non-detection planets might be as valuable as any eventual atmospheric detection in order to more solidly define the ECS, and in turn help us identify the most robust criterion for target selection to substantially enhance the scientific yield of future observations. As an example,
Bennett et al. 2025a
very recently published a joint analysis of four transits and one emission dataset, all from JWST, and placed very stringent constraints on the possibility of an atmosphere of GJ 1132 b. Empirically establishing GJ 1132 b as bare rock would be a major step into defining the true limits of the ECS (see Figure
1
), and might be well worth a campaign of repeated observations with the JWST.

4.3
Implication for habitability searches

Further than determining the existence or lack of atmospheres for M dwarf planets, another important question is the exploration of small planets in the habitable zone, including the search for possible biomarkers
(
Des Marais et al. 2002
;
Harman et al. 2015
)
. In Figure
4
we plot all the confirmed transiting planets orbiting M dwarfs, with
\rm R<2.5R_{\oplus}
, and with an equilibrium temperature between 250 and 350 K. Currently, only 14 planets meet these criteria. One of these planets is K2-18 b, a sub-Neptune planet, proposed as a possible hycean planet
(
Madhusudhan et al. 2023
)
, which has a confirmed atmosphere but also an on-going intensive debate on its overall nature and detected atmospheric species
(
Schmidt et al. 2025
;
Madhusudhan et al. 2025
;
Hu et al. 2025
)
. Three more sub-Neptune planets TOI-1468 c, TOI-1266 b and TOI-1266 c have similar sizes and TSM to K2-18 b and can be relatively easily explored with JWST in the near future.

For truly small earth-size planets, the c, d, and e planets in the Trappist1 system and Gj-12 b are the best, most accessible targets, but unfortunately all lie on the atmosphere-less part of the ECS. Only TOI-700 e and d are expected to possess an atmosphere, although their TSM metrics make them prohibitive targets for JWST. Thus, based on our ECS results, sub-Neptune planets emerge as the only possible targets for biomarker searches with currently available instrumentation.

One planet deserves special attention. Given its size, its location in the habitable zone, its TSM metrics, and its proximity to our definition of the ECS, Gj-12 b
(
Kuzuhara et al. 2024
;
Turner et al. 2025
)
appears a fundamental planet for JWST to explore in depth, to determine both the possibility of the planet hosting an atmosphere and the exact limits of the ECS.

5
Conclusions

In this study, we have established a new empirical definition of the cosmic shoreline, the ECS, by leveraging atmospheric detections across exoplanetary systems and Solar System objects. Our approach reconciles limitations in the classical shoreline definition
(
Zahnle & Catling 2017
)
by incorporating both Mars—the Solar System’s empirical boundary for atmospheric retention—and the exoplanet 55 Cnc e, which exhibits a secondary atmosphere despite extreme irradiation. The resulting ECS provides a significantly improved fit to the observational data. Notably, it aligns with tentative atmospheric detections for planets like GJ 9827 d, L 98-59 d, and Pi Mensae c, while consistently categorising Solar System moons and dwarf planets according to their atmospheric properties. The slope of 5.89 reflects a steeper dependence on escape velocity than previously theorised, highlighting the role of non-linear atmospheric escape processes and initial volatile inventories.

Our analysis reveals critical implications for planets orbiting M dwarfs, prime targets for atmospheric characterisation with JWST and future ELTs. When applying the ECS with classical
I_{\mathrm{XUV}}
estimates, over half of known M-dwarf planets shift into the atmospheric retention zone—a more optimistic outlook than the classical shoreline suggests. However, incorporating updated cumulative XUV fluence histories for low-mass M dwarfs (
\rm(M_{*}<0.35\,M_{\odot}
)) from
Pass et al. 2025
dramatically reduces the fraction of planets capable of retaining atmospheres. For these systems, only seven small planets (
\rm(R\lesssim 1.7\,R_{\oplus}
)) fall securely below the ECS, emphasising the vulnerability of rocky worlds to prolonged stellar activity. This refinement underscores the need to prioritise targets such as TOI-1452 b and TOI-715 b for the
Rocky Worlds
survey, as they combine favourable TSM with ECS-based retention potential. Conversely, in-depth observing campaigns for planets near but above the ECS could provide essential constraints through non-detections.

Looking ahead, the ECS offers a robust framework for guiding the search for habitable environments. For temperate, Earth-sized planets in the habitable zone, our results suggest that TOI-700 e and d are promising atmospheric candidates, although their low TSM values challenge near-term characterisation. Conversely, TRAPPIST-1 planets c–e and GJ 12 b likely lie in the atmosphere-depleted region, reducing their astrobiological priority. GJ 12 b, however, lies closer to the transition region and its properties make it a prime target for JWST in-depth observations. Future efforts must focus on expanding empirical validations of the ECS, particularly through high-precision observations of borderline candidates and systems with well-constrained XUV histories. Such data will not only refine the shoreline’s slope and zero-point, but also optimise the scientific return of next-generation telescopes in the quest to identify habitable worlds.
