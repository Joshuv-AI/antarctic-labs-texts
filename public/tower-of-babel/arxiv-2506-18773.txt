DPG loss functions for learning parameter-to-solution maps by neural networks
Pablo Cortés Castillo, Wolfgang Dahmen, Jay Gopalakrishnan

Abstract
. We develop, analyze, and experimentally explore residual-based loss functions for machine learning of parameter-to-solution maps in the context of parameter-dependent families of partial differential equations (PDEs). Our primary concern is on rigorous accuracy certification to enhance prediction capability of resulting deep neural network reduced models. This is achieved by the use of variationally correct loss functions. Through one specific example of an elliptic PDE, details for establishing the variational correctness of a loss function from an ultraweak Discontinuous Petrov Galerkin (DPG) discretization are worked out. Despite the focus on the example, the proposed concepts apply to a much wider scope of problems, namely problems for which stable DPG formulations are available. The issue of high-contrast diffusion fields and ensuing difficulties with degrading ellipticity are discussed. Both numerical results and theoretical arguments illustrate that for high-contrast diffusion parameters the proposed DPG loss functions deliver much more robust performance than simpler least-squares losses.



1.
Introduction

The need to recover information on a physical state of interest from a
limited amount of observational data is ubiquitous in scientific and
technological applications. To make such tasks feasible, it is
crucial to leverage the physical laws that the states obey. However,
these are often only partially known. A common way to deal with such
uncertainties is to formulate the mathematical model as a system of
partial differential equations (PDEs) depending on unknown model data,
(such as coefficient fields, initial or boundary conditions, source
terms, or constitutive laws), collected into
parameters

\alpha

from finite- or infinite-dimensional spaces. We have in mind PDE solutions
u(x,\alpha)
depending on a spatial variable
x
in a low-dimensional domain
\varOmega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a parameter variable
\alpha
in a possibly high-dimensional domain
\mathcal{{Q}}
.
For each parameter instance
\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}
, the solution
u(x,\alpha)
, also written as
u(\alpha)
or simply
u
, is
the unique solution of

\mathcal{R}(u,\alpha)=0,

(1)

for some (generally nonlinear) PDE
\mathcal{R}
written in residual form.
The structure and range of
\mathcal{Q}
determine the “design space” or
“solution manifold”
\mathcal{M}
comprised of the states
u(\alpha)
that are
obtained as solutions of (
1
) when
\alpha
traverses
\mathcal{Q}
.
Equation (
1
) implicitly defines a
parameter-to-solution
map
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{M}
.
Generally vector spaces in which
u
is to be found may change from
point to point on the manifold.
At each parameter instance
\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}
, the problem (
1
)
for
u
at the corresponding point on the manifold is referred to as a
“fiber problem.”
Exploration of

\mathcal{M}
or solving inverse tasks like state-estimation or parameter-estimation when using
\mathcal{M}
as a prior, require a large—sometimes prohibitive—number of high-fidelity forward simulations.
This calls for generating reduced models providing efficiently computable surrogates for the parameter-to-solution map
\mathcal{F}
.
The underlying rationale is that evaluating such a surrogate at any given parameter instance is much more efficient than computing a corresponding high-fidelity approximate solution, see e.g.
[
7
,
18
,
27
]
. Generating a surrogate typically takes a substantial computational effort, which though has to be performed only once, and is considered an “offline-cost”.
It amortizes quickly when many parameter queries are needed.

Generating such surrogates is a central instance of
Operator Learning
which has lately been attracting considerable attention,
see e.g.
[
8
,
29
,
30
,
31
]
. In full generality, it aims at approximating mappings between Banach space, hence permitting infinite-dimensional inputs and outputs.
In this latter regard, we are more modest in this paper
and focus on a scenario where
\mathcal{Q}
is a finite-dimensional
diffusion field in a second-order elliptic boundary value problem. However, we address different challenges which are, in our opinion, of paramount importance when striving for
prediction capability
, namely challenges in
controlling prediction error in model-compliant norms even in singularly perturbed regimes of parameters. The second-order elliptic boundary value problem has numerous
applications, a typical one being where
u
represents the pressure in
a porous medium flow and
\mathcal{{R}}
represents the simplest version of
Darcy’s model. In the construction of surrogates,
we use finite elements to discretize the low-dimensional
\varOmega

but use deep neural networks (NN) to discretize
\mathcal{{F}}
from the high-dimensional
\mathcal{{Q}}
. This means that our solution predictions take the form

u_{\theta}(x,\alpha)=\sum_{k}v_{k,\theta}(\alpha)\;\psi_{k}(x)

(2)

where
\psi_{k}(x)
are standard finite element basis functions and the
coefficients
v_{k,\theta}(\alpha)
in the basis expansion are
provided by the NN whose settings are determined by a collection
\theta
.
While many works discretize spatial dependence also using NN
(exceptions include
[
3
,
24
,
26
]
)
we opt for (
2
) to leverage existing finite
element techniques and avoid issues in imposing essential
boundary conditions on unstructured spatial domains.

To put our focus on prediction into perspective, most operator learning approaches are purely data-driven:
one first computes a sufficiently large number
of high-fidelity solutions that are then used as training samples for performing regression over suitable hypothesis classes for operators—see,
e.g.,
[
8
,
24
,
26
,
30
,
29
,
31
]
. The accuracy can then be assessed via estimating the “generalization error” incurred. To mitigate the potentially high cost of computing the high-fidelity solutions, other approaches use
residual-based loss functions
and avoid solving large systems during training. The best known version is PINN (Physics-Informed Neural Network)
[
22
,
28
,
34
]
which employs a Monte-Carlo loss

\theta^{*}\in\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\frac{1}{\#\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\;\#\widehat{\varOmega}}\sum_{\alpha\in\widehat{\mathcal{Q}},x\in\widehat{\varOmega}}\big|\mathcal{R}(u_{\theta}(x,\alpha),\alpha)\big|^{2},

(3)

to learn an approximation
u_{\theta^{*}}
from a hypothesis class
determined by a finite budget
\Theta
of trainable weights
\theta
.
Here
\widehat{\varOmega}
is a finite set of spatial samples (such as
quadrature points in
\varOmega
as well as on
\partial\varOmega
) and

\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}\subset\mathcal{Q}
is a finite collection of model
parameters. It is not possible to summarize the burgeoning PINN
literature here, but we note that error control and error analysis
have received the attention of others:
see
[
1
,
10
,
36
,
38
,
40
]
and
references therein. Most of such literature is however concerned with
parameter-independent problems and aim to solve just a single fixed PDE, while
our focus is on the parameter-to-solution map. While (
3
)
offers great computational convenience (as it only requires evaluation
of residuals
\mathcal{{R}}
at spatial and parametric samples), it has the
following serious drawbacks. Depending on the type of PDE, the
residual
\mathcal{{R}}
need not be a function in
L_{2}(\varOmega)
(a problem
noted in several works
[
3
,
38
,
40
]
). Moreover,
when pursuing an ansatz such as (
3
), the only possible
accuracy assessment would be based on the loss itself, and a loss of
the form (
3
) need neither be an efficient nor a
reliable indicator of the generalization errors.

In this paper, we also opt for employing
residual-based loss functions but insist on these losses being
variationally correct
. A precise definition for our situation is provided in Definition
2.4
, but to convey the idea right away,
when a loss function is variationally correct, its size, at any stage of the optimization process, provides, up to uniform constant factors, a lower and upper bound for the approximation error with respect to the respective model compliant norm (see
[
3
]
). As explained later in detail, this is intimately related to a
stable variational formulation
of the PDE (see also
[
39
]
). Again, related ideas in prior works have been confined to solving a single PDE in low spatial dimensions because corresponding
residual loss functions are only then practically feasible
[
5
,
6
,
15
,
23
,
32
]
.

Even though the presented concepts apply to a much wider scope of PDE models, namely whenever we have a stable Discontinuous Petrov Galerkin (DPG) formulation
[
20
,
21
]
at our disposal, in favor of quantitative accuracy studies, we focus here on the homogeneous Dirichlet problem for second-order elliptic equations with
parameter-dependent
diffusion coefficients. Even when
the range
\mathcal{Q}
of parameters guarantees uniform ellipticity, PINN in the form (
3
) need not be variationally correct. As explained in Section
2
(see also
[
3
]
), one key step to realizing variational correctness is to reformulate the PDE first as a well-posed
first-order system
for the flux and the solution, i.e., for
d+1
scalar solution components. For strictly positive and bounded diffusion coefficients, there exist actually several distinct stable variational formulations for the first-order system, differing by the choice of trial and test space. In this paper, we discuss two such formulations that mark, in some sense, extreme cases.
In the first one, a closed subspace
\mathcal{{X}}
of a graph space, determined by homogeneous boundary conditions, serves as the trial space. It can then be shown that the induced operator maps the trial space
onto
a test space
\mathcal{{Y}}
which equals the
(d+1)
-fold Cartesian product of
L_{2}(\varOmega)
, thus giving
a well-posed variational formulation in
\mathcal{{X}}\times\mathcal{{Y}}
.
This is the

First-Order System Least Squares
(FOSLS) formulation introduced in
[
12
]
. Its residual is well-defined in
L_{2}
and the
L_{2}
-residual is uniformly proportional to the corresponding error
measured in the graph norm. Incidentally, although PINN is not variationally correct for the original second-order formulation, it is for the FOSLS formulation.

How sharp the
L_{2}
-loss is as a lower and upper bound for the graph norm errors, depends, however, on the size-range of the diffusion coefficients. This raises the main issue addressed in this paper, namely, permitting arbitrarily large size variations in the diffusion coefficient. This is sometimes referred to as
high-contrast
problems. To indicate the issue, it is not hard to see that for piecewise constant diffusion (and constant right hand side) the solution tends to zero in regions where the diffusion tends to infinity while the gradient tends to infinity where the diffusion tends to zero—see
[
16
]
and further literature cited there.
The approach in
[
16
]
developed particular reduced bases incorporating these limit features.
In contrast, here we address this issue by considering the so-called
ultraweak
formulation where the trial space is just the
(d+1)
-fold Cartesian product of
L_{2}(\varOmega)
, while the test space
\mathcal{{Y}}
is now a boundary condition-induced closed subspace of the graph space of the
dual
operator (see Example
2.3
). This results in a well-posed formulation in which parameter robustness issues are
manifested
in a hard-to-compute dual norm of
\mathcal{{Y}}
. To overcome these issues, we tap into DPG methodology
[
20
,
21
]
. In quantitative terms this approach is slightly more expensive than the FOSLS formulation.
However, we will show that it is much more robust for “degenerating” parameters
\alpha
than FOSLS. While we have a complete theory establishing the
variational correctness of DPG if the parameter
\alpha
is confined
to a bounded range, we do not have a full theory establishing the
observed robustness of the practically implemented DPG loss. We do show
however, that a closely related ideal DPG loss function is variationally
correct with explicit constants showing
\alpha
-robustness.

The layout of the paper is as follows. Section
2
is devoted to the problem description. Starting with three simple examples in §
2.1
, we outline the issues that motivate the remainder of the developments. In §
2.5
, we define concisely the central notion of variationally correct loss functions based on a functional setting that properly respects the mapping properties of parameter-to-solution maps. In Section
3
we introduce the FOSLS loss function and establish its variational correctness. In essence, this follows
[
3
]
, except that we highlight the dependence of the constants involved on the parameter
\alpha
.
An alternate DPG formulation is then introduced and analyzed in Section
4
along analogous lines. The performance of both formulations with regard to prediction accuracy is studied in
Section
5
.
These findings indicate some advantages of the DPG formulation when the contrast in the diffusion coefficient increases and the conditioning of the FOSLS operator exhibits a stronger degradation. This motivates
a more in-depth robustness study in Section
6
.

2.
A parametric PDE model

2.1.
The model problem and first-order reformulations

Let
\varOmega
be a bounded Lipschitz domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let

\alpha:\varOmega\to\mathbb{R}
be a positive function, and let

a(x)=\alpha(x)^{-1}
. Consider the stationary diffusion equation

\displaystyle-\div(a(x)\nabla u)

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{ in }\quad\varOmega,

(4)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }\quad\partial\varOmega.

Here we are not interested in solving (
4
) for a fixed

a(x)
, but for a parameterized range
\mathcal{Q}
of diffusion
coefficients which will be specified later below. When
a

varies, or equivalently
\alpha
varies, so does the solution
u
,
which we shall write as
u(\alpha)
or
u(x,\alpha)
when we need
to highlight the
\alpha
-dependence. As mentioned in
Section
1
, a central objective in operator
learning is to construct surrogates for the mapping
\alpha\mapsto u(\alpha)
so
as to facilitate an efficient exploration of the corresponding design
space
\mathcal{M}
of viable states
u(\alpha)
,
\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}
.

The boundary value problem (
4
) admits many well-posed variational
formulations (three of which are given below).
Suppose
\mathfrak{u}
is a group variable which contains
u
as
one of the components and suppose that there is a well-posed variational
formulation that uniquely fixes
\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{{X}}
as the solution of a
weak formulation

b_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v})=\ell(\mathfrak{v})\qquad\text{ for all }\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{{Y}},

(5)

where
b_{\alpha}:\mathcal{{X}}\times\mathcal{{Y}}\to\mathbb{{R}}
is a continuous
bilinear form on the product of some Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{{X}}
and

\mathcal{{Y}}
and
\ell\in\mathcal{{Y}}^{\prime}
is the functional capturing the
source
f
. Let the continuous operator generated by
b_{\alpha}
be
denoted by
B_{\alpha}:\mathcal{{X}}\to\mathcal{{Y}}^{\prime}
, i.e.,

(B_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w})(\mathfrak{v})=b_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{w},\mathfrak{v})
for all
\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}
and

\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{{Y}}
. The wellposedness of (
5
), by Babuška’s
theorem, implies that

\|\mathfrak{w}\|_{b_{\alpha}}=\sup_{0\neq\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{{Y}}}\frac{b_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{w},\mathfrak{v})}{\|\mathfrak{v}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}}}

(6)

is an equivalent norm on
\mathcal{{X}}
.
Suppose we are given a candidate
approximation
\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}
to the exact solution
\mathfrak{u}

of (
5
). The error in such an approximation satisfies

\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{b_{\alpha}}=\sup_{0\neq\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{{Y}}}\frac{b_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w},\mathfrak{v})}{\|\mathfrak{v}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}}}=\sup_{0\neq\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{{Y}}}\frac{\ell(\mathfrak{v})-b_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{w},\mathfrak{v})}{\|\mathfrak{v}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}}}=\|\ell-B_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}^{\prime}},

(7)

which we refer to as the
error-residual equation
.
We may now set
\mathcal{{R}}
in (
1
) by

\mathcal{{R}}(\mathfrak{w},\alpha)=\|\ell-B_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}^{\prime}}
and observe that
the solution
\mathfrak{u}
indeed satisfies (
1
) due to (
7
).

The central issues that occupy us in this paper can be described in
simple terms using (
7
). We would like to control the error

\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}
in some desired
\alpha
-independent norm as robustly as
possible while
\alpha
varies in
\mathcal{{Q}}
. To do so, we may use the
residual
\|\ell-B_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}^{\prime}}
in (
7
)
containing only known quantities
\ell
and
\mathfrak{w}
, provided we know

(i)
how to compute (the residual in) the dual norm

\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}^{\prime}}
, and
(ii)
how to obtain
\alpha
-robust
equivalences between the desired
\alpha
-independent norm and

\|\cdot\|_{b_{\alpha}}
(which involves dealing with degenerating
ellipticity when
\alpha
attains very large or very small values
while ranging over
\mathcal{Q}
). The following examples show to what
extent these
two issues can be influenced by the choice of the
variational formulation.

Example 2.1

(Primal formulation)
.

The canonical weak formulation of (
4
) seeks the
solution
\mathfrak{u}=u
in the Sobolev space

{\mathring{H}^{1}}=\{v\in L_{2}(\varOmega):\partial_{i}v\in L_{2}(\varOmega),\text{ for every }i=1,\dots,d
and
v|_{\partial\varOmega}=0\}
, setting

\mathcal{{X}}=\mathcal{{Y}}=\mathring{H}^{1},\qquad b_{\alpha}(u,v)=\int_{\varOmega}\alpha^{-1}\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\;dx,\qquad\ell(v)=\int_{\varOmega}f\,v\;dx

and solving (
5
). Issue
(i)
manifests as the
difficulty to compute the
H^{-1}:=(\mathring{H}^{1})^{\prime}
norm. Issue
(ii)
is evident from the strong
\alpha
-dependence of the

\|\cdot\|_{b_{\alpha}}
-norm in this case, e.g.,

\|\nabla v\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\;\min_{x\in\varOmega}\alpha^{-1}\;\leq\;\|v\|_{b_{\alpha}}^{2}\;\leq\;\|\nabla v\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\;\max_{x\in\varOmega}\alpha^{-1}

shows that attempts to control the error
in an
\alpha
-independent norm, such as

\|u\|_{\mathcal{{X}}}=\|u\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}}=\|\nabla u\|_{L_{2}}
,
or the standard
H^{1}
-norm, are plagued by difficulties. Here and throughout,

\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}}
denotes the norm
of
L_{2}
or its Cartesian products.

Example 2.2

(FOSLS formulation)
.

Given a square-integrable
f\in L_{2}(\varOmega)
,
(
4
) can equivalently be written in the first-order form using
\alpha
as

\displaystyle\alpha(x)\,q+\nabla u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ in }\quad\varOmega,

(8a)

\displaystyle\div q

\displaystyle=f

\displaystyle\text{ in }\quad\varOmega,

(8b)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }\quad\partial\varOmega.

(8c)

It is useful to introduce a notation for the entire first-order
operator in (
8
).

A_{\alpha}(q,u)=\big(\alpha q+\nabla u,\;\div q\big).

(9)

Then (
8
) takes the form

A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{u}=(0,f)
in
\varOmega
for the group variable
\mathfrak{u}=(q,u)
.

Since the weak formulation of (
4
) allows for
f\in H^{-1}(\varOmega)
the above assumption requiring
f
in
L_{2}(\varOmega)
seems to entail a restriction. However, recall that every
f\in H^{-1}(\varOmega)
can be written as
f=f_{\circ}+\div\,g
where
f_{\circ}\in L_{2}(\varOmega)
and
g\in L_{2}(\varOmega)^{d}
. Then the first-order system takes the form
A_{\alpha}(q,u)=(g,f_{\circ})
, thus covering the same range of source terms as (
4
). It is only for the sake of convenience that we continue to work with
f_{\circ}=f,g=0
.

The choice of a pair of trial and test spaces
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
, that give rise to a stable weak formulation of (
9
), is now less canonical.
Equation (
8a
) defines the “flux variable”

q
in terms of derivatives of the “primal variable”
u
. The former
lies in
H(\div)
, the space of vector fields whose components
and divergence are in
L_{2}(\varOmega)
. The latter lies in

\mathring{H}^{1}
in accordance with the weak formulation of (
4
).
Thus, we view
A_{\alpha}
as a mapping

A_{\alpha}:H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1}\longmapsto[L_{2}(\varOmega)]^{d}\times L_{2}(\varOmega),

(10)

and construct a weak formulation of
A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{u}=(0,f)
by
multiplying both sides by
\mathfrak{v}=(r,w)
in

[L_{2}(\varOmega)]^{d}\times L_{2}(\varOmega)
and integrating over
\varOmega
to
arrive at the setting (
5
) with

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{X}=H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1},\qquad\mathcal{Y}=L_{2}(\varOmega)\times[L_{2}(\varOmega)]^{d},\\
b_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v})=\int_{\varOmega}A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{u}\cdot\mathfrak{v}\;dx,\qquad\ell(\mathfrak{v})=\int_{\varOmega}F\cdot\mathfrak{v}\;dx,\end{gathered}

(11)

where
F=(0,f)
. Equation (
5
) in this case can equivalently be written
in component form, with
\mathfrak{u}=(q,u)
and
\mathfrak{v}=(r,w)
as

\int_{\varOmega}(\alpha q+\nabla u)\cdot r+({\rm div}\,q-f)w\,dx=0,\quad w\in L_{2}(\varOmega),\,r\in[L_{2}(\varOmega)]^{d}.

(12)

This is the weak formulation underlying the FOSLS method. It is well known that (
10
) is a continuous bijection and that (
12
) is uniquely solvable for every

\alpha
that is uniformly positive and bounded in

\bar{\varOmega}
. Moreover, it can be shown to yield the same solution as the
canonical weak formulation of (
4
) in the sense that
u

is the weak solution of (
4
) if and only if

q=-a(x)\nabla u
and
u
solves (
12
)—see

[
3
,
12
]
.

The error-residual equation (
7
) now takes the form

\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{b_{\alpha}}=\|F-A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}},\qquad\mathfrak{w}\in H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1}.

(13)

The interest in
the FOSLS formulation (
11
) is now evident: all difficulties
with the issue
(i)
disappears since the norm of

\mathcal{{Y}}=\mathcal{{Y}}^{\prime}
is just the
L_{2}
-norm which is simple to evaluate.
In regards to issue
(ii)
, note that (
11
) implies

\|\mathfrak{w}\|_{b_{\alpha}}=\|A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}}.

Unfortunately, this
is an
\alpha
-dependent norm, which cannot be easily controlled by

\alpha
-independent norms with
\alpha
-robust equivalence
constants—see (
32
) below and
Section
3
. We will see practical
manifestations of this difficulty in our experimental studies in
Section
4
.

Example 2.3

(Ultraweak formulation)
.

This formulation is also based on the first-order
reformulation (
8
). Integrating
(
12
) by parts,

\int_{\varOmega}\alpha q\cdot r-u{\rm div}\,r-q\nabla w-fw\,dx+\int_{\partial\varOmega}(n\cdot r)w+(n\cdot q)w\,ds=0.

Taking
w\in\mathring{H}^{1}
and
r\in H(\div)
as test functions, we arrive at
the problem of finding
(q,u)\in[L_{2}(\varOmega)]^{d}\times L_{2}(\varOmega)
such
that

\int_{\varOmega}q\cdot(\alpha r-\nabla w)-u\,{\rm div}\,r-fw\,dx=0.

(14)

What were previously essential boundary conditions are now natural ones.
Introducing the adjoint operator

A_{\alpha}^{*}(q,u)=\big(\alpha q-\nabla u,\;-\div q\big),

(15)

we may rewrite (
14
) as (
5
) with the following settings:

\begin{gathered}\mathcal{X}=[L_{2}(\varOmega)]^{d}\times L_{2}(\varOmega),\qquad\mathcal{Y}=H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1},\\
b_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v})=\int_{\varOmega}\mathfrak{u}\cdot A_{\alpha}^{*}\mathfrak{v}\;dx,\qquad\ell(\mathfrak{v})=\int_{\varOmega}F\cdot\mathfrak{v}\;dx,\end{gathered}

(16)

with
\mathfrak{u}=(q,u)
and
\mathfrak{v}=(r,w)
. This is often called an
“ultraweak formulation” since all the solution components need
only be regular enough to be in
L_{2}(\varOmega)
. It is well
posed
[
13
]
. If the ultraweak solution
belongs to
H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1}
then it agrees with the
solution to (
12
).

Consider the error-residual equation (
7
) for the
formulation (
16
). It is well known that

A_{\alpha}^{*}:H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1}\to[L_{2}(\varOmega)]^{d}\times L_{2}(\varOmega)

is a continuous bijection (just as (
10
) is). Hence
\mathcal{{Y}}
may
equivalently be normed by

\|\mathfrak{v}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}_{\alpha}}:=\|A_{\alpha}^{*}\mathfrak{v}\|_{L_{2}},\qquad\mathfrak{v}\in H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1}.

(17)

Such norms have been used under the name “optimal test norms”
previously
[
17
,
21
,
41
]
. Let
\mathcal{{Y}}_{\alpha}

denote
H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1}
endowed with the above
norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}_{\alpha}}.
Revising
\mathcal{{Y}}
in (
16
) to

\mathcal{{Y}}_{\alpha},
the norm in (
6
) becomes

\|\mathfrak{w}\|_{b_{\alpha}}=\sup_{0\neq\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{{Y}}_{\alpha}}\frac{b_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{w},\mathfrak{v})}{\|A_{\alpha}^{*}\mathfrak{v}\|_{L_{2}}}=\|\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}},

so (
7
) implies

\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{v}\|_{L_{2}}=\|\ell-B_{\alpha}\mathfrak{v}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}_{\alpha}^{\prime}}.

(18)

An attractive feature of (
18
) is that it has
no
\alpha
-dependent constants and the norm on the left hand
side is the completely
\alpha
-independent
L_{2}
-norm. Thus the previously
mentioned issue
(ii)
disappears. However, the norm on the
right hand side of (
18
) is a non-trivial
dual norm whose numerical evaluation is an issue, i.e., issue
(i)
remains. This can be fixed to some extent using the so-called
ultraweak DPG method, as we shall see in
Section

.

2.2.
Parameter-to-solution map

Since handling
arbitrary variations in the function parameter
\alpha
is beyond the scope of this paper,
we first restrict ourselves to a finite-dimensional parameter set for

\alpha
by subdividing
\varOmega
into finitely many subdomains
\varOmega_{i}
,

i=1,\dots,m_{\alpha}
, and requiring
\alpha
to be a constant in each

\varOmega_{i}
. Namely, using
\chi_{i}(x)
to denote the indicator function of

\varOmega_{i}
, we define the parameter domain to be

\mathcal{Q}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\alpha}}\alpha_{i}\chi_{i}(x)\;:\;\alpha_{i}\geq 0\right\}.

(19)

We are interested in approximating the
parameter-to-solution map
\mathcal{{F}}
defined by

\alpha\overset{{\mathcal{{F}}}}{\longmapsto}\text{ the }(q,u)\text{ solving \eqref{laplaceDP-first-order},}

(20)

i.e.,
\mathcal{{F}}(\alpha)=(q(x,\alpha),u(x,\alpha))
is a function of
x
and
\alpha
defined on the spatio-parametric domain
\varOmega\times\mathcal{Q}
. In a learning context such approximations result from minimizing appropriate loss functions.
In constructing such approximations, even though
\mathcal{{Q}}
is a subset of a vector space of dimension
\dim\mathcal{{Q}}=m_{\alpha}
, the vector space structure is not used. In fact, in many practical problems, the
parameter domain is a bounded subset of
\mathcal{{Q}}
. We keep
\mathcal{{Q}}
unbounded in our studies here to make the learning task more challenging.

The approximation to
\mathcal{{F}}
depends on the particular weak formulation of (
8
) we choose to use. Without specifying the formulation at the moment, suppose
that we are given a basis
\psi_{k}
,
k=1,\dots,m_{h}
, for a finite
element space, built using a mesh of maximal element diameter
h
and
that an approximation of

\mathcal{{F}}(\alpha)=(q(x,\alpha),u(x,\alpha))

is found, for each given
\alpha
in

\mathcal{{Q}}
, in their span

{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}=\left\{\sum_{k=1}^{m_{h}}v_{k}\,\psi_{k}(x):v_{k}\in\mathbb{{R}}\right\}

by some method. Then a computable approximation of the exact
\mathcal{{F}}

in (
20
), which we denote by
{\mathcal{{F}}_{h}}
,
can be implemented as a
map between Euclidean vector spaces,

\mathbb{F}:\mathbb{R}^{m_{\alpha}}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{h}}
. The mapping
of functions
{\mathcal{{F}}_{h}}
is generated from the mapping
\mathbb{{F}}
of vectors
by

{\mathcal{{F}}_{h}}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\alpha}}\alpha_{i}\chi_{i}(x)\Big)=\sum_{k=1}^{m_{h}}\,[\mathbb{{F}}(\bbalpha)]_{k}\,\psi_{k}(x),\qquad x\in\varOmega.

(21)

where
\bbalpha
is the vector of coefficients of
\alpha\in\mathcal{{Q}}
, i.e.,

\bbalpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots)
.
We shall construct such approximate mappings
{\mathcal{{F}}_{h}}
using neural
networks. Two different choices of the finite element space
{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}

(and accompanying loss functions for training the neural networks)
are discussed in Sections
3
and
4

A_{\text{out}}\in\mathbb{{R}}^{m_{h}\times N},A_{m}\in\mathbb{{R}}^{N\times r},W_{m}\in\mathbb{{R}}^{r\times N},A_{\text{in}}\in\mathbb{{R}}^{N\times{m_{\alpha}}},

b_{m}\in\mathbb{{R}}^{r},b_{\text{out}}\in\mathbb{{R}}^{{m_{h}}},b_{\text{in}}\in\mathbb{{R}}^{m_{\alpha}}.

For

\displaystyle L_{\text{out}}:\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{h}},

The training process requires a loss function
\mathcal{{L}}\equiv\mathcal{{L}}_{\alpha}
that estimates
the error in any approximation generated by the NN at a given parameter value

\mathcal{L}:{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}\to\mathbb{R}.

The calculation of
\mathcal{{L}}(v)
, for any

\alpha
even when

is

\alpha
. Although a proper notation indicating so would
be
\mathcal{{L}}_{\alpha}
, we dispense with the subscript to lighten the

The training is carried out through a stochastic minimization process for

\theta^{*}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\theta\in\Theta}\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}_{M}}\mathcal{L}({\mathcal{{F}}_{h,\theta}}(\alpha))\right)

(23)

where
\mathcal{Q}_{M}
is a
collection of
M

\mathcal{Q}
after endowing it with a probability measure
\mu
. Here, in terms of computational vectors
\bbalpha

the summand above is

\mathcal{L}({\mathcal{{F}}_{h,\theta}}(\alpha))=\mathcal{L}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{m_{h}}\,[\mathbb{{F}}_{\theta}(\bbalpha)]_{k}\,\psi_{k}(x)\right)

where
\mathbb{{F}}_{\theta}
is as defined by the NN in (
22
).

In machine learning jargon, the minimization in (
23
) is referred
to as
empirical risk minimization
. When a scheme produces
candidate solution approximations
\mathfrak{w}(\alpha)\in{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
for each

\alpha
in the probability space
\mathcal{{Q}}
with measure
\mu
, its
“ideal risk” is defined as the expectation of the random variable

\mathcal{{L}}\circ\mathfrak{w}
, i.e.,

\mathbb{L}(\mathfrak{w};\,\mathcal{Q}):=\int_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathcal{{L}}(\mathfrak{w}(\alpha))\;d\mu(\alpha)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\big[\mathcal{{L}}(\mathfrak{w}(\alpha))\big].

(24)

The empirical risk is the analogue computed from the
M
random samples in
\mathcal{{Q}}_{M}

drawn from
\mathcal{{Q}}
, defined by

\mathbb{L}(\mathfrak{w},\mathcal{{Q}}_{M}):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}_{M}}\mathcal{L}(\mathfrak{w}(\alpha)).

(25)

It can be viewed as a Monte Carlo approximation to the ideal risk.
Viewing the NN parameter-to-solution map
{\mathcal{{F}}_{h,\theta}}
as a function on the
probability space
\mathcal{{Q}}
, the ideal risk of the NN model is

\mathbb{L}({\mathcal{{F}}_{h,\theta}};\,\mathcal{Q})
. The objective function being minimized
in (
23
), equaling
\mathbb{L}({\mathcal{{F}}_{h,\theta}},\mathcal{{Q}}_{M})
, is then the empirical
risk, a Monte Carlo approximation to the ideal risk.
Desirable criteria while choosing
\mathcal{L}
are described in §
2.5
.

2.4.
The “lifted” problem

To quantify the accuracy of outcomes of the trained model

{\mathcal{{F}}_{h,\theta^{*}}}
from (
23
) in relation to the exact

\mathcal{F}
, as a function of
x\in\varOmega
and
\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}
, one needs an
appropriate model-compliant “continuous reference metric”. As
suggested in
[
3
]
and in agreement with (
24
), we
consider the “lifted” variational problem for a group variable

\mathfrak{u}(x,\alpha)
containing the flux and solution components.
as functions on the spatio-parametric domain
\varOmega\times\mathcal{Q}
.
For each
\alpha\in\mathcal{{Q}}
the solution
\mathfrak{u}(\cdot,\alpha)
is in some trial Hilbert
space
\mathcal{X}
and satisfies a well-posed formulation (
5
) with some trial space
\mathcal{{Y}}
. Generalizing over all parameters using

\mathbb{X}:=L_{2}(\mathcal{Q};\mathcal{X})
, the lifted formulation seeks

\mathfrak{u}\in\mathbb{X}
such that for all

\mathfrak{v}\in\mathbb{Y}:=L_{2}(\mathcal{Q};(\mathcal{Y}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}})
,

b(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v}):=\int_{\mathcal{Q}}b_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v})\;d\mu(\alpha)=\int_{\mathcal{Q}}\ell(\mathfrak{v})\;d\mu(\alpha)

(26)

where

\mu
is a given probability measure on
\mathcal{Q}
.
When the test-spaces
\mathcal{Y}_{\alpha}
depend on
\alpha
, the notation
L_{2}(\mathcal{Q};(\mathcal{Y}_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}})
indicates that, under mild measurability assumptions on the bilinear forms
b_{\alpha}(\cdot,\cdot)
, the space
\mathbb{Y}
is indeed a well-defined Hilbert space, a so-called direct integral: see
[
3
]
. Moreover, it is shown there that (
26
) is well-posed if and only if the individual fiber problems (
5
) are
well-posed (in the sense of the Babuška’s theorem)

uniformly
in
\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}
. The reference norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{X}}
for the continuous problem is reasonable because it measures
errors

\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|^{2}_{\mathbb{X}}=\int_{\mathcal{Q}}\|\mathfrak{u}(\cdot,\alpha)-\mathfrak{w}(\cdot,\alpha)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{X}}\;d\mu(\alpha)=\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\big[\|\mathfrak{u}(\cdot,\alpha)-\mathfrak{w}(\cdot,\alpha)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{X}}\big]

(27)

as an
expectation
over
\mathcal{Q}
.
This underlines the stochastic nature of the learning problem.

A key consideration in our approach is the requirement that
(
27
) be uniformly proportional to
the loss (
24
) when
\mathfrak{w}={\mathcal{{F}}_{h,\theta}}
ranges over the hypothesis class.
Since we will not be able to assess exact values of (
24
)
over the entire parameter range, we use a large (random) test set

\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\rm test}\subset\mathcal{Q}
to gauge accuracy. The errors
in the ideal reference metric
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{X}}
of (
27
) have two sources. The
first is the deviation between the ideal loss and its empirical
counterpart

\mathbb{L}({\mathcal{{F}}_{h,\theta}},\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\rm test})
.
The
second is the error in the
discrete Bochner norm

\|\mathfrak{u}-{\mathcal{{F}}_{h,\theta}}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\rm test})}
where

\mathbb{X}(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\rm test}):=\ell_{2}(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\rm test};\mathcal{{X}})
. The former is
often referred to as the
generalization error
and depends (among
other things) on the size of sufficiently large test set

\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\rm test}\subset\mathcal{Q}
. We do not address the associated
stochastic estimation concepts (see e.g.
[
4
]
)
but concentrate on estimating the empirical risk

\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathcal{{F}}_{h,\theta}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\rm test})}

from corresponding
computable losses

\mathbb{L}({\mathcal{{F}}_{h,\theta}},\widehat{\mathcal{Q}}_{\rm test})
.

2.5.
Variational correctness, reliability, and efficiency

As we have seen, classical boundary value problems like (
8
)
admit many different variational formulations of the form (
5
). The effectiveness of an approximation
\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}

to the exact solution
\mathfrak{u}
in
\mathcal{{X}}

can be measured by the norm
\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{X}}}
. Conforming
finite element methods are based on a variational formulation and use
a finite element subspace
{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}\subset\mathcal{{X}}
.
Following
[
3
]
, we
pursue loss functions that track the error, i.e.,
\mathcal{{L}}(\mathfrak{w})
is
bounded above and below by the true error
\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{X}}},
scaled
by some
\mathfrak{w}
-independent constants, for all candidate
approximations
\mathfrak{w}
. In the finite element literature, such estimates
are well known for
a posteriori
“error estimators”
which are typically based on residuals. Then they do not require knowing the exact solution
\mathfrak{u}
but involve problem data such as source terms whose evaluation may also require some care. We now
borrow the standard terminology for finite element error estimators
into NN loss functions and specify what we mean by their variational
correctness.

Definition 2.4
.

For some class of source functions
f
representable in computations,
the loss function
\mathcal{{L}}:{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}\to\mathbb{{R}}
is said to
be
reliable
in
{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
if there is a
C_{1}>0
such that

C_{1}\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{{X}}}\leq\mathcal{{L}}(\mathfrak{w})

for all
\mathfrak{w}\in{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
. Here
\mathfrak{u}
is the exact solution of (
5
).
If there is a
C_{2}>0
such that

\mathcal{{L}}(\mathfrak{w})\leq C_{2}\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{{X}}}

for all
\mathfrak{w}\in{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
, then the loss function
\mathcal{{L}}
is said to be

efficient
. A loss function that is both reliable and efficient
is said to be
variationally correct
in
{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
.

When a prediction
\mathfrak{w}\in{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
is of the form (
21
), i.e., the expansion coefficients are NNs, the notorious uncertainty of optimization success renders
a priori
estimates based on the expressive power of NNs useless. More useful information on the fidelity of an NN-based prediction of the exact solution
\mathfrak{u}
is drawn from the value of
the loss
\mathcal{{L}}(\mathfrak{w})
. Then the two-sided bounds of a variationally correct loss
function,

C_{1}\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{X}}}^{2}\leq\mathcal{{L}}(\mathfrak{w})\leq C_{2}\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{X}}}^{2},\qquad\mathfrak{w}\in{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}},

(28)

show that
\mathcal{{L}}(\mathfrak{w})
provides an “error certificate,” up to the
values of the constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.

In Definition
2.4
, the solution
\mathfrak{u}\equiv\mathfrak{u}(x,\alpha)
,
the candidate approximation
\mathfrak{w}\equiv\mathfrak{w}(x,\alpha),
as well as the
constants
C_{1}\equiv C_{1}(\alpha),C_{2}\equiv C_{2}(\alpha)
, all depend
on
\alpha
. To indicate this, we often call
\mathcal{{L}}
the “fiber loss” (of the fiber problem) at
\alpha
. Let
\alpha
vary in some selected parameter domain
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
(which is typically a strict compact subset of the
\mathcal{{Q}}
in (
19
)). We say that a variationally correct loss function
\mathcal{{L}}
is
robust over
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
if the constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
do not depend on the parameter
\alpha
as it ranges over
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
. Arguably a useful concept of parameter robustness should also
constrain
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{{X}}}
to include a parameter-independent part, but this is hard to prescribe abstractly.

When
\mathcal{{L}}
is robust over
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
, it is shown in
[
3
]
that (
28
) holds
uniformly for
\alpha\in\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
if and only if

C_{1}\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathbb{X}}^{2}\leq\mathbb{L}(\mathfrak{w};\tilde{\mathcal{Q}})\leq C_{2}\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathbb{X}}^{2},\qquad\mathfrak{w}\in\mathbb{X},

(29)

where

\mathbb{L}
is as in (
24
)
and
\|\cdot\|_{\mathbb{X}}
denotes the Bochner norm of
\mathbb{X}=L_{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{Q}};{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}})
, i.e., if
and only if the “lifted loss”
\mathbb{L}
is variationally correct in
\mathbb{X}
.
Moreover, uniform validity of (
28
) over
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
implies the two-sided bounds

C_{1}\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{M})}^{2}\leq\mathbb{L}(\mathfrak{w};\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{M})\leq C_{2}\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathbb{X}(\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{M})}^{2},\qquad\mathfrak{w}\in\mathbb{X}(\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{M}),

(30)

for any

\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{M}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
of
M
samples, and
for the corresponding computable quantities, involving the discrete
Bochner norm of
\mathbb{X}(\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{M})=\ell_{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{M};{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}})
and the empirical risk
defined in (
25
). Thus, variational correctness of
\mathcal{{L}}
over

{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
and robustness of
\mathcal{{L}}
over
\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
ensures variational
correctness of the lifted loss
\mathbb{L}
over
\mathbb{X}=L_{2}(\tilde{\mathcal{Q}},{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}})
and
ensures that the computable
\mathbb{L}(\mathfrak{w};\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{M})
provides rigorous error
certificates over
any
test set of random samples

\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}_{M}\subset\tilde{\mathcal{Q}}
. Inference on the continuous counterpart

\mathbb{L}(\mathfrak{w};\tilde{\mathcal{Q}})
is then a matter of statistical estimation when
M

increases.

Two-sided inequalities like (
28
) have been developed extensively
for error estimators in the finite element literature
[
2
]
. When the
source
f
is not represented exactly in the finite element
calculations, one can only expect a modified version of (
28
)
to hold with additional data representation errors (often also called
data oscillation terms). In our numerical studies we fix
f
to a simple function
(without the need for such terms) to focus exclusively on
\alpha

dependence. While some finite element error estimators admit
bounds like (
28
) only when
\mathfrak{w}
is a Galerkin approximation, note that we are requiring (
28
) to hold for any
\mathfrak{w}\in{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
.

In the next two sections, we proceed to discuss two variationally
correct loss functions
\mathcal{{L}}
compared in this paper. They are based on the variational formulations (
12
) and (
14
). In both cases,
we will need the following inequality to establish variational
correctness: there exists (an
\alpha
-dependent)
c_{\alpha}>0
such
that

\|(r,w)\|_{L_{2}}\leq c_{\alpha}\|A_{\alpha}(r,w)\|_{L_{2}},\qquad(r,w)\in H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1}.

(31)

Clearly, this inequality is independent of the finite element
discretization and the specifics (including the norms and the spaces)
of different variational formulations used to
treat (
8
). The
inequality (
31
) can be proved using the theory of
mixed systems
[
11
, Ch. II, Prop. 1.3]
. A proof can be
found in many places, such as
[
9
]
,

[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
, or

[
19
, Lemma 4.4]
, and it holds provided that
\alpha

satisfies the so called ellipticity condition, i.e., there
exists two finite positive constants

\alpha_{\text{min}},\alpha_{\text{max}}>0
such that

\alpha_{\text{min}}\leq\alpha(x)\leq\alpha_{\text{max}}\quad\text{ a.e. }x\in\varOmega.

(32)

Then one can make explicit the
\alpha
-dependence
of
c_{\alpha}
, namely,
there exists
[
19
, Lemma 4.4]

some
\alpha
-independent constant

c_{0}>0
such that

c_{\alpha}=c_{0}\left(1+\frac{\max(1,\alpha_{\text{max}})}{\alpha_{\text{min}}}\right).

(33)

One often also uses the graph norm generated by the operator

A_{\alpha}
, namely

(\|\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\|A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}}^{2})^{1/2},
for any

\mathfrak{w}=(r,w)\in H(\div)\times H^{1}.
By triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|\nabla w\|_{L_{2}}+\|\div r\|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\alpha r+\nabla w\|_{L_{2}}+\|\alpha r\|_{L_{2}}+\|\div r\|_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|A_{\alpha}(r,w)\|_{L_{2}}+{\overline{\alpha}}\|r\|_{L_{2}},

showing that

\|\mathfrak{w}\|_{H(\div)\times H^{1}}^{2}\lesssim\|\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\|A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}.

(34)

Here and throughout, when two quantities
A
and
B
admit a bound

A\leq CB
for some constant
C>0
which may depend on
\alpha
but
is independent of
h
, we write
A\lesssim B
, and we write

A\sim B
to mean that both
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A

hold. It is easy to see that

\|\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\|A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\;\sim\;\|\mathfrak{w}\|_{H(\div)\times H^{1}}^{2},\qquad\mathfrak{w}\in H(\div)\times H^{1},

(35)

since the reverse bound of (
34
) can also be
established using the triangle inequality.

3.
The FOSLS loss function

3.1.
The FOSLS solution

We consider the FOSLS method
[
12
]
, based on (
11
) or
(
12
), in its lowest
order form. Since
A_{\alpha}
as an operator in (
10
) is onto, we can symmetrize the variational formulation in (
11
) by writing every test function
\mathfrak{v}
there as
A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}
for some trial function
\mathfrak{w}
to get the following equivalent FOSLS formulation: find
\mathfrak{u}\in\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}
satisfying

b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{w})=\ell^{\mathrm{fos}}(\mathfrak{w}),\qquad\text{ for all }\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}},

(36a)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}=H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1},\qquad\|(r,w)\|_{\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}}^{2}=\|r\|_{H(\div)}^{2}+\|w\|_{\mathring{H}^{1}}^{2}.

(36b)

\displaystyle b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{w}):=(A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{u},A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w})_{L_{2}},\qquad\ell^{\mathrm{fos}}(\mathfrak{w}):=(F,A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w})_{L_{2}}.

(36c)

Here
F=(0,f)
and
(\cdot,\cdot)_{L_{2}}
denotes the inner
product in
L_{2}
or its Cartesian products.

The lowest order FOSLS scheme sets the finite element space
{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
to a subspace of

\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}
described now. Let
\varOmega_{h}
be a conforming simplicial
finite element mesh of
\varOmega
, where
h
denotes the maximal diameter
of elements in the mesh. Let
\mathcal{{P}}_{k}
denote the space of
piecewise polynomials of degree at most
k
on each element of

\varOmega_{h}
(without any inter-element
continuity conditions). Then the lowest order Raviart-Thomas
subspace
[
35
]
of
H(\div)
is

{\mathcal{{R\!T}}_{\!0}}=\left[\mathcal{P}_{0}^{d}+x\mathcal{P}_{0}\right]\cap H(\div).

(37a)

Using also the lowest order Lagrange space on the same mesh,

\mathcal{{U}}_{1}=\mathcal{{P}}_{1}\cap{\mathring{H}^{1}}

(37b)

the FOSLS method sets the finite element space
{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
to

{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}}={\mathcal{{R\!T}}_{\!0}}\times\mathcal{{U}}_{1}.

Given a diffusion parameter
\alpha
, the FOSLS approximation to the
exact solution
\mathfrak{u}=\mathfrak{u}(\cdot,\alpha)=(q(\cdot,\alpha),u(\cdot,\alpha))
in
H(\div)\times{\mathring{H}^{1}}
satisfying (
8
)
is the unique
\mathfrak{u}_{h}\in{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}}
satisfying

(A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{u}^{\rm fos}_{h},A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w})_{L_{2}}=(F,A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w})_{L_{2}},\qquad\text{ for all }\mathfrak{w}\in{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}},

(38)

where
F=(0,f)
and
A_{\alpha}
is as in (
10
). The inner
product in
L_{2}
or its Cartesian products is denoted above and
throughout by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{L_{2}}
and the corresponding norm by

\|\cdot\|_{L_{2}}
. The evaluation of the FOSLS
parameter-to-solution map for each given
\alpha

\alpha\overset{\mathcal{{F}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}}{\longmapsto}\mathfrak{u}^{\rm fos}_{h}(\alpha),\qquad\mathcal{{F}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{u}^{\rm fos}_{h}

can be obtained by solving (
38
).
Note that the set of equations contained in (
38
) for
each test function is the same as the set of normal equations for the
minimization problem

\min_{\mathfrak{w}\in{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}}}\|A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}-F\|^{2}_{L_{2}}.

Thus, for each
\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}
, the FOSLS solution can be characterized as the following unique minimizer:

\mathcal{{F}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{u}^{\rm fos}_{h}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{\mathfrak{w}\in{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}}}\|A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}-F\|^{2}_{L_{2}}.

3.2.
Variational correctness of the FOSLS loss function

For the fiber problem at any
\alpha\in\mathcal{{Q}}
,
the “FOSLS loss function” is defined by

\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\mathfrak{w}):=\|A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}-F\|^{2}_{L_{2}},\qquad\mathfrak{w}\in{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}}.

(39)

The FOSLS parameter-to-solution map is found by performing the
minimization in (
23
) with
\mathcal{{L}}
set to
\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{fos}}

and
{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
set to
\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}
.
As previously mentioned,
we refer to this residual in (
39
)
as “fiber loss.” Note that it explicitly depends on
\alpha
through the
operator
A_{\alpha}
(even though to avoid notational clutter we have chosen to write
\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{fos}}
instead of
\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{fos}}_{\alpha}
).
The next proposition is essentially contained in

[
12
]
but we include a short proof for completeness.

Proposition 3.1
.

For each
\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}
,
\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{fos}}
is variationally correct in

\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}
, i.e., there are
\alpha
-dependent constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0

such that

C_{1}\|\mathfrak{u}(\alpha)-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}}^{2}\leq\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\mathfrak{w})\leq C_{2}\|\mathfrak{u}(\alpha)-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}}^{2},

(40)

for all
\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}.
Here

\mathfrak{u}(\alpha)=(q(\alpha),u(\alpha))\in\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}
is the exact solution
of (
8
) found using (
12
) or
equivalently (
36
).

Proof.

First note that
Since
\mathfrak{u}=\mathfrak{u}(\alpha)
is the exact solution,
F=A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{u}
and

\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\mathfrak{w})=\|A_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{w}-\mathfrak{u})\|^{2}_{L_{2}}
for any
\mathfrak{w}
in

\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{fos}}
. Then (
31
) implies that

\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\|A_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w})\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\leq(c^{2}_{\alpha}+1)\,\|A_{\alpha}(\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w})\|_{L_{2}}^{2}=(c^{2}_{\alpha}+1)\,\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\mathfrak{w}).

Hence by (
35
),

\|\mathfrak{u}-\mathfrak{w}\|^{2}_{\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}}\lesssim\,\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\mathfrak{w})

for all
\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}
, showing reliability of the FOSLS loss
function (the lower inequality of (
40
)).
Furthermore, a simple application of triangle inequality
shows that

\|A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}}\leq\,\max\{1,\alpha_{\text{max}}\}\,\|\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}},

(41)

for all
\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{fos}}.

Thus,
\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{fos}}
is also efficient.
∎

Although FOSLS loss is variationally correct, it is not robust over
the unbounded parameter domain
\mathcal{{Q}}
in (
19
). Let
a

and
b
be two positive constants and consider the bounded parameter
domain

\mathcal{{Q}}^{[a,b]}=\{\alpha\in\mathcal{{Q}}:a\leq\min_{x\in\varOmega}\alpha(x),\;\max_{x\in\varOmega}\alpha(x)\leq b\}.

(42)

From the proof of Proposition
3.1
it is clear that
the FOSLS loss is robust over the bounded parameter domain

\mathcal{{Q}}^{[a,b]}
. Indeed the constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
produced in the
proof depend only on
\alpha_{\text{max}}
and
c_{\alpha}
(which can
depend also on
\alpha_{\text{min}}
per (
33
)), so
C_{1}
and
C_{2}

can also be expressed
using only
a
and
b
, making them independent of

\alpha\in\mathcal{{Q}}^{[a,b]}
. As a consequence, per the discussion in
Section
2.5
, the lifted loss function

\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\mathfrak{w};\mathcal{Q}^{[a,b]}_{M}):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}^{[a,b]}_{M}}\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\mathfrak{w}(\alpha))

(43)

is variationally correct for any set of
M
samples
\mathcal{Q}^{[a,b]}_{M}\subset\mathcal{Q}^{[a,b]}

and admits two-sided bounds as in (
30
) with efficiency and
reliability constants depending solely on
a
and
b
.

4.
The DPG loss function

In this section we introduce the ultraweak DPG method
for (
8
). This method was first presented in

[
19
]
and put in a larger context in the recent
review
[
21
]
. After presenting the discrete method, we present the DPG
loss function and discuss its variational correctness.

4.1.
The DPG solution.

The ultraweak DPG formulation can be described for very general PDEs,
in discretized and undiscretized forms, and in arbitrary order spaces
(see
[
21
, Section 7]
).
It is a mesh-dependent variational formulation
in so-called “broken graph spaces” whose unbroken counterpart was already given in Example
2.3
. For the discretization,
we restrict ourselves here to the simplest
lowest order case for transparent presentation of the ideas. Let

\tr(w)
and
\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{n}}(r)
denote element-wise trace operators defined by

\tr(w)|_{\partial K}=w|_{\partial K}
and

\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{n}}(r)|_{\partial K}=(r\cdot n)|_{\partial K}
. Here and throughout
n

denotes the outward unit normals on element boundaries. Using the
spaces defined in (
37
),
define the following spaces on element boundaries by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{{U}}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{\hat{w}:\;\hat{w}|_{\partial K}=w|_{\partial K}\text{ for some }w\in\mathcal{{U}}_{1}\text{ for all }K\in\varOmega_{h}\}

\displaystyle{\hat{\mathcal{{R\!T}}}_{\!0}}

\displaystyle=\{\hat{q}_{n}:\;\hat{q}_{n}|_{\partial K}=(r\cdot n)|_{\partial K}\text{ for some }r\in{\mathcal{{R\!T}}_{\!0}}\text{ for all }K\in\varOmega_{h}\}.

Since the lowest order spaces
\mathcal{{U}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{{R\!T}}_{\!0}}
are completely
defined by their element-wise traces, the spaces
\hat{\mathcal{{U}}}_{1}
and
{\hat{\mathcal{{R\!T}}}_{\!0}}

are isomorphic to
\mathcal{{U}}_{1}
and
{\mathcal{{R\!T}}_{\!0}}
, respectively. We write a
generic element of
{\hat{\mathcal{{R\!T}}}_{\!0}}
as
\hat{r}\cdot n
or
\hat{r}_{n}

to indicate its dependence on
n
.

The lowest order ultraweak
DPG method uses distinct trial and test
spaces,

\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=\mathcal{{P}}_{0}^{d}\times\mathcal{{P}}_{0}\times\hat{\mathcal{{U}}}_{1}\times{\hat{\mathcal{{R\!T}}}_{\!0}},\qquad\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=\mathcal{{P}}_{2}^{d}\times\mathcal{{P}}_{d+1}.

Recall the formal adjoint of
A_{\alpha}
, given by
(
15
).
As in Example
2.3
,
it is used to norm the test space by

\|(\tau,\nu)\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}}^{2}=\sum_{K\in\varOmega_{h}}\Big(\|A_{\alpha}^{*}(\tau,\nu)\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}+\|(\tau,\nu)\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}\Big)

(44)

for all
(\tau,\nu)\in\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
.
Note that the differential operators within
A_{\alpha}^{*}
are applied
element by element when computing the norm (
44
), which is
referred to as a “broken graph norm”.
The corresponding inner
product is denoted by
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{{Y}}}
and it is an essential
ingredient in computations with the DPG method. Different flavors of
the method are obtained by changing the
\mathcal{{Y}}
inner product, as we
shall see later.

Define the DPG bilinear form by

\displaystyle b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}((q,u,\hat{u},\hat{q}\cdot n),(\tau,\nu))

\displaystyle=\sum_{K\in\varOmega_{h}}\left(\int_{K}(q,u)\cdot A_{\alpha}^{*}(\tau,\nu)+\int_{\partial K}\hat{u}\,\tau\cdot n+\int_{\partial K}\hat{q}\cdot n\,\nu\right),

(45)

for any
(q,u,\hat{u},\hat{q}\cdot n)\in\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
and

(\tau,\nu)\in\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}.
We omit the standard
Lebesgue measure in integrals such as the above
when there can be no confusion.
The DPG method can be presented as
a Petrov-Galerkin formulation or equivalently as a mixed Galerkin
method. We only present the latter (and refer the reader to

[
21
]
for other equivalent forms). Given an
F
in

L_{2}(\varOmega)^{d}\times L_{2}(\varOmega)
(which, as before, will be set to
(0,f)

with
f
as in (
8
)), the DPG method produces
an approximate solution
\mathfrak{u}^{\rm dpg}_{h}=(q_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}},u_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}},\hat{u}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}},\hat{q}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}})
in
\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}

as well as a built-in approximate error representation
e_{h}
in

\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
, that together satisfy the equations

\displaystyle(e_{h},y)_{\mathcal{{Y}}}\;+\;

\displaystyle b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{u}^{\rm dpg}_{h},y)

\displaystyle\,=\,

\displaystyle(F,y)_{L_{2}},\qquad

\displaystyle y\in\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}},

(46a)

\displaystyle b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w},e_{h})

\displaystyle\,=\,

\displaystyle 0,\qquad

\displaystyle\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}.

(46b)

This generates the DPG parameter-to-solution map

\alpha\overset{\mathcal{{F}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}}{\longmapsto}\mathfrak{u}^{\rm dpg}_{h},\qquad\mathcal{{F}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\alpha)=\mathfrak{u}^{\rm dpg}_{h},\quad

for any
\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}
, i.e.,
the application of
\mathcal{{F}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
to any
\alpha
is obtained by
solving (
46
). We want to approximate
\mathcal{{F}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
by a NN.

4.2.
The DPG loss function

The DPG loss function is computed in two steps. First, given any

\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
, we compute a residual representation
\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{h}\in\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
by solving

(\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{h},\mathfrak{v})_{\mathcal{{Y}}}=(F,\mathfrak{v})_{L_{2}}-b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w},\mathfrak{v}),\qquad\text{ for all }\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}.

(47a)

Note that this is the same equation as (
46a
) with

e_{h}
and
\mathfrak{u}^{\rm dpg}_{h}
replaced by
\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{h}
and
\mathfrak{w}
, respectively.
As before we focus first on parameter-dependent DPG fiber loss of
\mathfrak{w}
at
\alpha
, which, using
\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{h}
, we define by

\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}):=\|\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{h}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}}^{2},\qquad\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}},

(47b)

where the
\alpha
-dependent
\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
norm is as defined in (
44
).
We can also write this loss in operator form. One way to do so quickly
is to introduce the mappings

R_{\alpha,h}:\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\to(\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}})^{\prime}
and
B_{\alpha,h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}:\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\to(\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}})^{\prime}
,
defined by
(R_{\alpha,h}y)(z)=(y,z)_{\mathcal{{Y}}}
and

(B_{\alpha,h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\mathfrak{w})(y)=B_{\alpha,h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w},y)
for
all
\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
and
y,z\in\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
. Then (
47a
)
can be expressed as

R_{\alpha,h}\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{h}+B_{\alpha,h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\mathfrak{w}=F

and (
47b
) becomes

\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w})=\|R_{\alpha,h}^{-1}(F-B_{\alpha,h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\mathfrak{w})\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}}^{2},\qquad\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}.

(48)

The key feature that makes this fiber loss function quickly computable is
the block diagonal nature of the matrix of
R_{\alpha,h}
(representing the
Riesz map), with one block per element (so its inverse appearing above
can be computed locally element by element). This is a consequence of
the fact that functions in
\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
have no inter-element continuity
constraints.

4.3.
Variational correctness of the DPG loss function

The variational setting which the ultraweak DPG method is based upon is
more complicated than that of FOSLS. First consider the trial space.
To describe the norm in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space

\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
enclosing the discrete computational trial space
\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
, let

\text{tr}:\mathring{H}^{1}\longrightarrow\prod_{K\in\varOmega_{h}}H^{1/2}(\partial K),\quad(\text{tr }u)\big\rvert_{\partial K}=u\big\rvert_{\partial K},

and

\text{tr}_{n}:H(\div)\longrightarrow\prod_{K\in\varOmega_{h}}H^{-1/2}(\partial K),\quad(\text{tr}_{n}r)\big\rvert_{\partial K}=r\cdot n\big\rvert_{\partial K},

denote element-wise boundary trace operators extended to the indicated
global Sobolev spaces. We define norms of trace and flux functions on
element boundaries using their preimages of the appropriate trace map by

\|\hat{u}\|_{{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h})}}:=\inf_{u\in\text{tr}^{-1}\{\hat{u}\}}\|u\|_{\mathring{H}^{1}},\qquad\|\hat{r}_{n}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h})}:=\inf_{q\in\text{tr}_{n}^{-1}\{\hat{r}_{n}\}}\|q\|_{H(\div)}

and set
\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h})
and
H^{-1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h})
to be the ranges of

\text{tr}(\cdot)
and
\text{tr}_{n}(\cdot)
, respectively.
The well-posedness of
ultraweak DPG formulation can be proven in the trial norm
defined by

\|(r,w,\hat{w},\hat{r})\|_{\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}}^{2}=\|r\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\|w\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\|\hat{w}\|_{\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h})}^{2}+\|n\cdot\hat{r}\|_{H^{-1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h})}^{2}.

(49)

The DPG test space is set to the product of the following spaces

H(\div,\varOmega_{h})=\prod_{K\in\varOmega_{h}}H(\div,K),\qquad H^{1}(\varOmega_{h})=\prod_{K\in\varOmega_{h}}H^{1}(K),

representing piecewise
H(\div)
and
H^{1}
spaces (containing functions
without any interlement continuity constraints). With these definitions,
we can now give the undiscretized DPG formulation, representing the fiber problem at some given parameter
\alpha
, for
the exact solution with four components

\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=(q,u,\hat{u},\hat{q}_{n}).

(50a)

Here
(q,u)
is the exact solution of (
8
),
\hat{u}=\tr(u)
and
\hat{q}_{n}=\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{n}}(q)
. It is well known that
\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
is the unique function in
\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
satisfying

b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{dpg}},\mathfrak{v})=\ell^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{v}),\qquad\text{ for all }\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}},

(50b)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=L_{2}(\varOmega)^{d}\times L_{2}(\varOmega)\times\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h})\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h}),\quad\text{ normed by \eqref{eq:Xdpg-norm},}

(50c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=H(\div,\varOmega_{h})\times H^{1}(\varOmega_{h}),\quad\text{ normed by broken graph norm \eqref{eq:Y-dpg-norm},}

(50d)

\displaystyle b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}((q,u,\hat{u},\hat{q}\cdot n),(\tau,\nu))=\sum_{K\in\varOmega_{h}}\bigg(\langle{\tau\cdot n,\hat{u}}\rangle_{\partial K}+\langle{\hat{q}\cdot n,\nu}\rangle_{\partial K}+\int_{K}(q,u)\cdot A_{\alpha}^{*}(\tau,\nu)\bigg)

(50e)

\displaystyle\ell^{\mathrm{dpg}}((\tau,\nu))=(f,\nu)_{L_{2}},

(50f)

where
\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle_{\partial K}
denotes the duality pairing between
H^{-1/2}(\partial K)
and
H^{1/2}(\partial K)
.
Note that
\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\subset\mathcal{{Y}}
and
\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\subset\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}.
Since the exact problem has zero error, with a zero error representation
e=0
, we can expand
(
50b
) into a mixed method of the form (
46
) to see that
(
46
) is indeed a conforming discretization of the exact formulation (
50
).
The following result is
obtained by putting together known results from DPG
analysis
[
14
,
21
]
.

Proposition 4.1
.

The DPG loss function
\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
is variationally correct in
\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}

when
f
is in
\mathcal{{P}}_{0}
, i.e., there are
\alpha
-dependent
constants
C_{1},C_{2}>0
(that depend on
c_{\alpha}
in
(
31
)) such that

C_{1}\|\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{dpg}}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}}^{2}\leq\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(z)\leq C_{2}\|\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{dpg}}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}}^{2},

for all
\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}.
Here
\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=(q,u,\hat{u},\hat{q}_{n})
is the
exact solution of (
50
).

Proof.

We apply
[
21
, Theorem 6.4]
(see also

[
14
]
) which yields

\displaystyle\gamma\|\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{dpg}}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|\varPi_{h}\|\|\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{h}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}}+\osc(F),

(51)

\displaystyle\|\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{h}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}}

\displaystyle\leq\|b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\|\|\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{dpg}}-\mathfrak{w}\|_{\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}},

provided there is a continuous operator
\varPi_{h}:\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\to\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
, called a Fortin operator,
satisfying

b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}_{h},y-\varPi_{h}y)=0\quad\text{ for all }\mathfrak{w}_{h}\in\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}},y\in\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}},

(52)

and provided the norm
\|b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\|
and the
inf-sup constant
\gamma
of the DPG form over
\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\times\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}

are finite and positive. In (
51
),

\|\varPi_{h}\|
is the operator norm of
\varPi_{h}
and

\osc(F)=\|F\circ(I-\varPi_{h})\|_{(\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}})^{\prime}}.

Since the DPG loss function is defined by (
47b
),
the estimates of (
51
) prove the result
once we verify these conditions and show that
\osc(F)
vanishes.

To obtain the required Fortin operator
\varPi_{h}
, recall that for
any
r\in H(\div)
and
w\in\mathring{H}^{1}
, by
[
21
, Theorems 4.1
and 5.8]
, there are functions
\varPi_{h}^{g}w\in\mathcal{{P}}_{1+d}
and

\varPi_{h}^{d}r\in\mathcal{{P}}_{2}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{K}\varPi_{h}^{g}w-w

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\int_{F}\varPi_{h}^{g}w-w

\displaystyle=0,

(53)

\displaystyle\int_{K}\varPi_{h}^{d}r-r

\displaystyle=0,\qquad

\displaystyle\int_{\partial K}(\varPi_{h}^{d}r-r)\cdot n

\displaystyle=0,

for all mesh elements
K
and all facets
F
of
\partial K
. In the last
equality the integral of

over
\partial K
must in general be
interpreted as a functional action of the
H^{-1/2}(\partial K)
trace

(r\cdot n)|_{\partial K}
on
\partial K
. Setting
\varPi_{h}:\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\to\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}

by
\varPi_{h}(r,w)=(\varPi_{h}^{d}r,\varPi_{h}^{g}w)
the same theorems yield the
bound

\|\varPi_{h}(r,w)\|_{H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1}}\lesssim\|(r,w)\|_{H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1}}.

Now exactly the same argument that proved (
35
)
shows that the above inequality implies

\|\varPi_{h}(r,w)\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}}\lesssim\|(r,w)\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}},\qquad(r,w)\in\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}},

so
\|\varPi_{h}\|\lesssim 1
. Also, (
53
) implies

b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}_{h},(r,w)-\varPi_{h}(r,w))=0
for all

\mathfrak{w}_{h}\in\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
and
(r,w)\in\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
. Furthermore,

\osc(F)=\sup_{y\in\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}}\frac{(F,y-\varPi_{h}y)_{L_{2}}}{\|y\|_{\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}}}

vanishes since (

\displaystyle(F,(r,w)-\varPi_{h}(r,w))_{L_{2}}

\displaystyle=(f,w-\varPi_{h}^{g}w)_{L_{2}}=0

for all
(r,w)\in\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
since

. It only remains to
verify that

\|b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\|\lesssim 1,\qquad 1\lesssim\gamma.

(54)

These are shown in
[
21
, Theorem 7.6 and Example 7.8]
(see
also
[
19
]
) using (
31
).
∎

The DPG parameter-to-solution map is found by performing the
minimization in (
23
) with
\mathcal{{L}}
set to
\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}

and
{\mathcal{{X}}_{h}}
set to
\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
.
Precisely as in the preceding section, we employ the lifted loss function

\mathbb{L}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w};\mathcal{Q}_{M}):=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}_{M}}\mathcal{{L}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}(\alpha)),

(55)

for the empirical risk minimization that trains the NN. As in the

). But we have instead
chosen to devote an entire section (Section

) to
the robustness of the DPG loss.

5.
Performance of neural network predictions

In this section, we show numerical results for both types of
learning methods and compare their performance. In these comparisons we do not take the (substantial, offline) training costs into account but focus solely on the achieved accuracy, reflected by the respective loss functions. Since the evaluation of DPG losses is slightly more expensive, it will be important to understand features that may offset somewhat higher cost.
Our numerical results in this section show
comparable performance for parameter-to-solution map approximations
obtained using NN of the form (
22
), trained either using the FOSLS loss or using the DPG
loss. This then forms the backdrop for further performance gains
described in Section
6
.

In all our numerical results we set
m_{\alpha}=4

\varOmega

square, split into four congruent subdomains, partitioned into a

m_{\alpha}
are

We use a fixed NN architecture

(see

6.
Improved parameter robustness using DPG ideas

The proofs of variational correctnesss of both the FOSLS loss function
and the DPG loss function depended on an inequality independent of
discretizations, namely (
31
). Even if this might
suggest that it is impossible to remove the
\alpha
dependence, we
are able to ameliorate the
\alpha
dependence in the DPG case, as we
shall see in this section. As motivation, we refer again to the
antecedent of the ultraweak DPG method given in
Example
2.3
. There, the optimal test norm
of (
17
),
\|A_{\alpha}^{*}\mathfrak{v}\|_{L_{2}}
for any
\mathfrak{v}

in the (unbroken) graph space yielded an isometry of the operator
induced by the corresponding weak formulation, which yielded an
attractive characterization of error in the
\alpha
-independent

L_{2}
-norm in (
18
) with no
\alpha
-dependent
constants. When moving to the DPG setting, in the broken graph norm
of (
44
), we are forced to add an
L_{2}
-term to make a
norm, namely in

(\|A_{\alpha}^{*}\mathfrak{v}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+\|\mathfrak{v}\|_{L_{2}}^{2})^{1/2}
, for
\mathfrak{v}
in
the broken graph space
\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
. Consequently, we no longer
have (
18
) to characterize the error in an

\alpha
-independent norm. In order to retain as much
\alpha
-robustness as possible, in this section, we weaken the previous DPG norm “towards” the optimal test norm of (
17
), and then see to what extent we can modify resulting fiber loss functionals so as to obtain sharp bounds for the
L_{2}
-errors of the bulk terms (interior variables).

6.1.
Loss functionals built with a weaker test space norm

Instead of (
44
), we now endow the test space

\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=H(\div,\varOmega_{h})\times H^{1}(\varOmega_{h})
by the following norm with an
additional scaling factor
s>0
,

\|(\tau,\nu)\|_{\mathcal{{Y}},s}^{2}=\sum_{K\in\varOmega_{h}}\left(\|A_{\alpha}^{*}(\tau,\nu)\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}+s^{-2}\|(\tau,\nu)\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}\right).

The corresponding inner product is denoted by

(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{{Y}},s}
. The DPG variant using this inner product
is defined exactly as in (
46
) but replacing

(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{{Y}}}
with
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{{Y}},s}
. Such a method was
first studied in
[
25
]
.
Analogous
to (
47
), we define
\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{s}\in\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
by

(\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{s},\mathfrak{v})_{\mathcal{{Y}},s}=(F,\mathfrak{v})_{L_{2}}-b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w},\mathfrak{v}),\qquad\text{ for all }\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}},

(60a)

and a new
s
-dependent fiber loss function by

\hat{\mathcal{{L}}}_{s}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}):=\|{\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{s}}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}},s}^{2},\qquad\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}},\quad\alpha\in\mathcal{Q}.

(60b)

As
s
increases, the test space norm becomes weaker (approaching (
17
)) and this new loss
function gains improved parameter robustness as shown in
Theorem
6.1
below.

The drawback of the loss function
\hat{\mathcal{{L}}}_{s}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
is that it requires us to
solve the infinite-dimensional problem (
60a
) for

\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{s}
. As we shall see below, we are able to get good results when using a
computable version obtained by replacing
\mathcal{{Y}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
with
\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
in (
60a
), i.e,
in place of
\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{s}
, we use
\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{h,s}\in\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
satisfying

(\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{h,s},\mathfrak{v})_{\mathcal{{Y}},s}=(F,\mathfrak{v})_{L_{2}}-b_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w},\mathfrak{v}),\qquad\text{ for all }\mathfrak{v}\in\mathcal{{Y}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}},

(61a)

and

{\mathcal{{L}}}_{s}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}):=\|\varepsilon^{\mathfrak{w}}_{h,s}\|_{\mathcal{{Y}},s}^{2},\qquad\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}_{h}^{\mathrm{dpg}}.

(61b)

We will also consider the dual-parameter functional

{{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{1}},{s_{2}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}):=\frac{s_{2}^{2}{{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{1}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w})-s_{1}^{2}{{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{2}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w})}{s_{2}^{2}-s_{1}^{2}}

(62)

for any two positive parameters
s_{1}<s_{2}
.

6.2.
Theoretical motivation

We present a few theoretical observations that motivate the new loss
function families defined above.

It will prove convenient to express

\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=L_{2}(\varOmega)^{d}\times L_{2}(\varOmega)\times\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h})\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h})

as a Cartesian product of the space of “interior variables” (or bulk variables) in

\mathcal{{X}}_{0}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=L_{2}(\varOmega)^{d}\times L_{2}(\varOmega)
and the space of “interface variables”

\hat{\mathcal{{X}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=\mathring{H}^{1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h})\times H^{-1/2}(\partial\varOmega_{h})
,
i.e., any
\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\in\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
will be written as

\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\in\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=\mathcal{{X}}_{0}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\times\hat{\mathcal{{X}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}},\qquad\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=(\mathfrak{w},\hat{\mathfrak{w}}),\text{ with }\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}_{0}^{\mathrm{dpg}},\;\hat{\mathfrak{w}}\in\hat{\mathcal{{X}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}.

(63)

Any
\hat{\mathfrak{w}}
in
\hat{\mathcal{{X}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
is obtained as the trace of a function in

\mathring{H}^{1}\times H(\div)
, so the following coset definition makes sense:

Z({\hat{\mathfrak{w}}})=\{(r,w)\in H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1}:(\tr w,\mathop{\mathrm{tr}_{n}}r)=\hat{\mathfrak{w}}\}.

By (
31
),
\|A_{\alpha}\cdot\|_{L_{2}}
is
a norm on
H(\div)\times\mathring{H}^{1}
, allowing us to define a minimal
extension operator

E\hat{\mathfrak{w}}=\mathop{\rm argmin}_{(r,w)\in Z({\hat{\mathfrak{w}}})}\|A_{\alpha}(r,w)\|_{L_{2}}.

Writing any
\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\in\mathcal{{X}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
as in (
63
), we
use the above extension to define a trial space norm by

\|\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}}\|_{s,\alpha}^{2}\equiv\|(\mathfrak{w},\hat{\mathfrak{w}})\|_{s,\alpha}^{2}:=\|\mathfrak{w}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+s^{2}\|A_{\alpha}E\hat{\mathfrak{w}}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}.

(64)

Noting that
Z(0)
is a closed subspace of
\mathcal{{X}}_{0}^{\mathrm{dpg}}=L_{2}(\varOmega)^{d}\times L_{2}(\varOmega)
,
we also define the Peetre
K
-functional

K(s,\mathfrak{w})=\inf_{\mathfrak{z}\in Z(0)}\Big(\|\mathfrak{w}-\mathfrak{z}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}+s^{2}\|A_{\alpha}\mathfrak{z}\|_{L_{2}}^{2}\Big)

(65)

defined for any
\mathfrak{w}\in\mathcal{{X}}_{0}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
. In the next theorem, we also use following quantities for positive numbers
s,s_{1},
and
s_{2}
:

\displaystyle k_{s,\alpha}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{c^{2}_{\alpha}}{s^{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{c_{\alpha}^{4}}{s^{4}}+4\frac{c^{2}_{\alpha}}{s^{2}}}\right),

(66)

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{1}},{s_{2}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}})=\frac{s_{2}^{2}{\hat{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{1}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}})-s_{1}^{2}{\hat{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{2}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}})}{s_{2}^{2}-s_{1}^{2}},

(67)

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{1}},{s_{2}}}^{\mathrm{dpg},L}(\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}})=\frac{(1+k_{{s_{1}},\alpha})^{-1}s_{2}^{2}{\hat{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{1}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}})-(1+k_{{s_{2}},\alpha})s_{1}^{2}{\hat{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{2}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}})}{s_{2}^{2}-s_{1}^{2}},

(68)

\displaystyle\hat{{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{1}},{s_{2}}}^{\mathrm{dpg},R}(\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}})=\frac{(1+k_{{s_{1}},\alpha})s_{2}^{2}{\hat{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{1}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}})-(1+k_{{s_{2}},\alpha})^{-1}s_{1}^{2}{\hat{\mathcal{{L}}}}_{{s_{2}}}^{\mathrm{dpg}}(\mathfrak{w}^{\mathrm{dpg}})}{s_{2}^{2}-s_{1}^{2}},

(69)

where
c_{\alpha}
is as in (
31
).

.

Before turning to the proof of these somewhat technical facts, a
few remarks on how to interpret the results in
Theorem
6.1
and explain their relevance are in order. The advantage of the new
loss function
\hat{\mathcal{{L}}}_{s}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
is evident from (

) of

(

when

is very large, it is possible to choose a scaling
factor

, thus making the constants
in (

\alpha

motivates the use of the loss function
{\mathcal{{L}}}_{s}^{\mathrm{dpg}}

defined in (

\hat{\mathcal{{L}}}_{s}^{\mathrm{dpg}}
is not computable).
Yet, a drawback
of (
70
) is that the error is measured in the

\alpha
-dependent norm
\|\cdot\|_{s,\alpha}
, incorporating the (stronger) graph-norm through the element boundary terms. The estimates
of (

(in the correspondingly modified mean squared

from (

\displaystyle\hat{e}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\alpha):=\|A_{\alpha}\big(\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{fos}}_{\theta}(\alpha)-\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{fos}}_{h}(\alpha)\big)\|_{L_{2}}^{2},

\displaystyle e_{0}^{\mathrm{fos}}(\alpha):=\|\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{fos}}_{\theta}(\alpha)-\mathfrak{u}^{\mathrm{fos}}_{h}(\alpha)\|_{L_{2}}^{2},
