Role of Cellular-Ferroptosis-Mediated HMGB1 Nuclear Translocation in Indium-Tin-Oxide-Nanoparticle-Induced Inflammatory Lung Injury
Authors: Yueyue Mu, Jiawei Wu, Xiaomeng Ding, Yanting Pang, Yuna Cao, Jing Qu, Haopeng Zhang, Qing Liu, Ting Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Indium tin oxide (ITO) particles, widely utilized in electronics and energy technologies, pose significant pulmonary risks due to occupational exposure during manufacturing. While emerging evidence links ITO to severe lung pathologies, critical gaps persist in understanding size-dependent effects and molecular mechanisms. This study systematically investigates the size-dependent effects of nanoscale ITO (N-ITO) and submicron-scale ITO (U-ITO) on pulmonary toxicity, focusing on ferroptosis as a key mechanism. In vivo, repeated oropharyngeal exposure of mice to ITO (3.6 and 36 mg/kg) induced dose-dependent lung inflammation and ferroptosis, marked by lipid peroxidation, Fe2+ accumulation, and dysregulation of Gpx4 and Scl7a11 proteins, with pulmonary macrophages identified as primary targets. In vitro, THP-1 macrophages exposed to 6.25–25 μg/mL ITO exhibited size-dependent cytotoxicity, inflammation, and ferroptotic hallmarks. Mechanistically, ITO-triggered ferroptosis promoted HMGB1 secretion and nuclear translocation, which activated the RAGE/p38MAPK pathway, amplifying inflammatory responses. Notably, N-ITO induced stronger ferroptosis and inflammation than U-ITO, correlating with enhanced macrophage uptake due to its nanoscale dimensions. Integrating these findings, we propose an Adverse Outcome Pathway (AOP) framework for ITO-induced lung injury, which cellular uptake may serve as the molecular initiating event and link ferroptosis-driven high mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1) release, RAGE/p38MAPK activation, and chronic inflammation to adverse pulmonary outcomes. This study underscores the necessity of size-specific occupational exposure limits for ITO nanoparticles and provides a mechanistic foundation for risk assessment and management strategies in occupational settings.

Introduction

Indium tin oxide (ITO) particles, composed of approximately 90% indium oxide (In2O3) and 10% tin oxide (SnO2), enabling technologies from touchscreens to solar cells. − However, its widespread industrial use has raised urgent concerns: occupational exposure to ITO particles during manufacturing processes (e.g., grinding and polishing) releases respirable nano- and microscale particles, posing underappreciated pulmonary risks. , Emerging evidence highlights severe lung pathologies in ITO-exposed workers. A case study investigated a Japanese semiconductor manufacturing plant reported interstitial pneumonitis in a worker handling ITO. Nakano et al. documented a case of indium pneumonitis in a worker with a 9-year history of grinding ITO targets, revealing progressive lung destruction via high-resolution chest computed tomography (HRCT). Animal studies corroborate these findings, showing that inhaled ITO particles trigger alveolar inflammation, protein deposition, and epithelial hyperplasia. At the cellular level, ITO particles exhibit toxic effects on lung macrophages and lung epithelial cells. Despite these advances, critical knowledge gaps persist. First, current exposure limits are extrapolated from existing toxicity data, ignoring potential nanospecific bioactivity linked to higher cellular penetration. Second, the molecular mechanisms driving ITO-induced lung injury remain poorly defined.

Macrophages, key players in the innate immune system, are crucial for pathogen clearance, tissue repair, and homeostasis. As frontline defenders against inhaled particles, macrophages are pivotal in activating immune pathways and initiating inflammatory responses. So far, in vitro studies only reveal that ITO exposure reduces macrophage viability and induces apoptosis, but size effect of ITO on macrophage injury and its related mechanism are unknown. Therefore, focusing on the response of macrophages to ITO particles of different sizes and their underlying mechanisms is a critical step in elucidating the early characteristics of lung injury induced by ITO respiratory exposure.

Ferroptosis, a recently identified form of regulated cell death, has emerged as a critical mechanism in lung diseases. Characterized by iron accumulation, glutathione (GSH) depletion, and lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis is implicated in conditions such as acute lung injury, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and pulmonary fibrosis. Kim et al. demonstrated that ferroptosis cells release substantial amounts of oxidized lipid mediators and are more immunogenic than apoptotic cells, leading to the release of inflammatory cytokines and damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), thereby fostering a pro-inflammatory environment. Critically, among the released DAMPs, high mobility group protein B1 (HMGB1) acts as a key alarmin that drives sterile inflammation upon its extracellular release, establishing a direct link between ferroptotic cell death and inflammatory tissue injury. Additionally, ferroptosis cells can release HMGB1 in an autophagy-dependent manner. , HMGB1 acts both as a downstream effector released during ferroptotic cell death and as an upstream amplifier of inflammation, immune activation, and metabolic dysfunction. However, the role of ferroptosis and HMGB1 in ITO-induced lung injury remains poorly understood.

As toxicity research progressed, the concept of an adverse outcome pathway (AOP) was introduced in 2011 as a conceptual framework to describe the link between molecular initiating event (MIE) and adverse outcomes (AOs) at different biological levels. The AOP framework aims to standardize and modularize key events (KEs) that span molecular, cellular, tissue, organ, and individual/population levels, integrating these KEs with MIEs and AOs into a coherent model. The application of the AOP framework simplifies the process of evaluating the toxicity of a chemical substance and facilitates the implementation of risk management strategies.

Based on the existing ITO occupational exposure limits, this study systematically investigated the lung injury effects of repeated respiratory exposure to nanoscale and microscale ITO at the in vitro and in vivo levels with the size effect as the entry point, further focused on ferroptosis to explore the molecular mechanism of ITO particles triggering lung inflammation injury effects, and proposed the AOP framework of ITO triggering lung inflammation based on the clarification of the toxic mode of action, thus providing data support for the formulation of occupational exposure limits for ITO nanoscale particles and providing the basis for the mechanism and strategy for the risk assessment of ITO occupational exposure.

Materials and Methods

Nanomaterials and Characterization

Nanoscale ITO particles (N-ITO) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA), and submicron-sized ITO particles (U-ITO) were purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China), and endotoxin was removed from the solid materials using dry heat and autoclaving. The ITO NPs were prepared as a staining solution with phosphate buffer saline (PBS), stored away from light, and dispersed by ultrasound for 30 min before use. The morphology of ITO NPs was observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-2100, JEOL). The initial particle size distribution of the materials was analyzed using ImageJ1.53a software. A Malvern laser particle size analyzer (Zetasizer Nano-ZS90, Malvern, UK) was used to determine the hydrated particle size and zeta potential of ITO NPs in ultrapure water, PBS, saline, RPMI 1640 complete medium and DMEM complete medium.

Animal Experimentation

Animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Regulations of the People’s Republic of China on the Administration of Laboratory Animals (2017). Animal experiments were approved by the Ethical Qualification Review Committee of the Animal Center of Southeast University. SPF-grade male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks old) used in the experiment were purchased from Viton Lever (Beijing, China, Animal Qualification Certificate No.: SCXK (Zhejiang) 2019–0001), weighing 18–22 g, and were kept in the Specific Pathogen-Free (SPF-grade) Laboratory of the Animal Center of Southeast University (Laboratory Animal Use License No.: SYXK (Su) 2021–0022), and the laboratory temperature was 18–24 °C, relative humidity was 60%, day/night cycle was 12 h/12 h, food and distilled water were supplied sufficiently, and mice were allowed to eat freely. The mice were numbered and weighed after 7 days of acclimatization, and mice that were underweight or overweight were excluded and divided into five groups using the random number table method. (Please refer to Supporting Information Text S1 for detailed dosing protocols). The experimental groups in this study were: control group (PBS), 3.6 mg/kg b.w. U-ITO group, 36 mg/kg b.w. U-ITO group, 3.6 mg/kg b.w. N-ITO group, and 36 mg/kg b.w. N-ITO group (n = 8 per group). Mice were anesthetized with sodium amobarbital (50 mg/kg) and then exposed by oropharyngeal inhalation twice a week for 28 days. The body weight of the mice was recorded, and general vital signs such as hair color were observed. At the end of 28 days, the mice were executed, and the lung tissue of each mouse was collected, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned for subsequent experiments.

Immunostaining (Immunohistochemistry and Immunofluorescence)

Immunohistochemistry: Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in a gradient and washed in distilled water. After soaking in 3% hydrogen peroxide (Maokang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 10 min, they were washed with distilled water. Endogenous peroxidase was blocked by steaming in citrate buffer for 3 min. After the sections were cooled to room temperature, they were washed with water and PBS (Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Dalian, China). Bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added and blocked at room temperature for 30 min to prevent nonspecific staining. The corresponding primary antibodies (IL-1β, Gpx4) (IL-1β was from HuaAn Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China and Gpx4 was from Sanying Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhai, China) were added and incubated overnight in a 4 °C refrigerator. After overnight, the slides were washed with PBS, and the secondary antibody identical to the primary antibody was taken and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. PBS was washed, and the chromogenic agent was added, and the chromogenic slides were rinsed with water to terminate the color development. The nuclei were stained with hematoxylin, and the sections were sealed with neutral gum. Observation was performed under an orthogonal fluorescence microscope (Imager.m2 & Axio 506 color, Zeiss, Germany).

Immunofluorescence: Paraffin sections were deparaffinized in a gradient and washed in PBS. Tissue sections were placed in a repair cassette containing EDTA antigen repair buffer (pH 8.0), and then put into a microwave oven for antigen repair with the program of medium heat for 8 min followed by a cease fire for 8 min and then turn to medium-low heat for 7 min, and then washed with PBS (pH 7.4) after waiting for the slides to cool naturally. After the sections were gently dried, circles were drawn around the tissues with a histochemical pen. BSA (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added to the circle and incubated for 30 min. Thirty minutes later, the primary antibody (CD11b mixed with IL-1β, CD11b mixed with Gpx4, iNOS) was gently shaken off, and the sections were incubated at 4 °C overnight. The sections were washed with PBS and incubated with a secondary antibody identical to the primary antibody at room temperature for 50 min. The sections were washed with PBS and sealed with a drop of antifluorescence quencher containing DAPI. Image acquisition and processing were performed as above.

Transcriptomics

Total RNA was extracted according to the steps in the instruction manual of the Trizol kit (Invitrogen, USA). After extraction, a bioanalyzer and electrophoresis were applied to assess the quality of the RNA to ensure the accuracy and reliability of sequencing. Next, mRNA was isolated from total RNA by DNase digestion of DNA followed by the Oligo (dT) magnetic bead method. mRNA was then fragmented using interrupting reagents, and then the first strand of cDNA was synthesized using random hexameric primers and reverse transcriptase using mRNA as a template. cDNA second strand synthesis was then performed, and the mRNA was deleted to produce double-stranded cDNA (ds cDNA). The ends of ds cDNA were complemented and purified, and then subjected to end plus A (dA-tailing) treatment. The Illumina sequencing junction was ligated at the end of the ds cDNA. The ligated products were screened by agarose gel electrophoresis, PCR amplified, and sequenced using Illumina HiSeq2500­(Gene Denovo, Guangzhou, China).

After sequencing was completed, the data were subjected to differential expression gene analyses (DEGs), GO functional enrichment analysis, and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. DEGs were assessed by gene expression changes of more than 2-fold and adjusted P < 0.05. Standard read counts were calculated by comparing the U-ITO group to the control group, the N-ITO group to the control group, and the U-ITO group to the N-ITO group.

RT-qPCR

The preclipped mouse lung tissues were placed in precooled StarRNA tissue stabilizer (GenStar, Beijing, China). RNA was extracted using TriZol reagent, and an aliquot of RNA was placed in a Mastercycler Gradient PCR system (Eppendorf, USA) for reverse transcription to obtain the template cDNA for RT-qPCR analysis. The experimental primers were designed with reference to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) (Table S1). AceQUniversal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) was used for RT-qPCR amplification (QuantStudio 6 Flex, Applied Biosystems, USA). Data were analyzed using Realtime PCR Software V1.3 to normalize the number of amplified cations according to the intragroup reference gene GAPDH. The experiment was repeated three times for each gene.

Cell Lines and Treatments

Human monocytic leukemia cells (THP-1 cells) (Saikou Biological Co., Guangzhou, China) are of human origin, and second, THP-1 cells can be induced to differentiate into macrophages in order to facilitate the study of the type of polarization of macrophages and their role in the primary response to immunity after treatment with ITO particles.

RPMI 1640 complete medium (Kaiji Biological Co., Jiangsu, China) was used as the medium for THP-1 cells, which were cultured in a 37 °C cell culture incubator (Thermo, USA) containing 5% CO2. After centrifugation of logarithmic growth phase THP-1 cells were resuspended in RPMI-1640 medium, cells were counted under a inverted microscope­(OLYMPUS, Japan), the density of THP-1 cells was adjusted to be 5 × 105 cells/mL, and PMA (Maokang Biological Co., Shanghai, China) was added so that the final concentration of PMA was 100 ng/mL, the cell degree was seeded on 96-well plates, laser confocal quadruple compartment dishes, 12-well plates, and 6-well plates as desired, and cultured overnight in a cell culture incubator.

The in vitro dose was determined based on the results of the previous previous toxicity studies and preexperiment, and the formal experimental groups were divided into: blank control (Control), U-ITO (6.25, 12.5, and 25 μg/mL), and N-ITO (6.25, 12.5, and 25 μg/mL) groups.

Cell Viability

Cell viability was determined using a neutral erythrocyte, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) toxicity assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. U-ITO and N-ITO were administered at concentrations of 0, 6.25, 12.5, and 25 μg/mL over 24 h.

THP-1 Cell Pro-Inflammatory Phenotype Assay

Changes in the pro-inflammatory phenotype of THP-1 cells were detected using a flow cytometer (FACSCantoTM, BD, USA) and a laser confocal microscope (FV 1000, Olympus, Japan) after staining with 0, 6.25, 12.5, and 25 μg/mL of U-ITO and N-ITO for 24 h.

ELISA to Detect Alterations in the Cytokines IL-1β and TNF-α

The levels of cellular inflammatory factors IL-1β and TNF-α were detected according to the instructions of the commercialized kit (Ruixin Biological Co., Quanzhou).

Fluorescence Detection of Intracellular Ferrous Ions (Fe2+)

After exposure to 0, 6.25, 12.5 and 25 μg/mL of U-ITO and N-ITO for 24 h, the cells were observed using a FerroOrange probe (Fe2+ indicator, Maokang Biologicals Co., Shanghai, China) at an excitation wavelength of 532 nm and an emission wavelength of 572 nm using a Zeiss microscope (Vert.A1, ZEISS, Germany) at an excitation wavelength of 532 nm.

Cellular Lipid Peroxidation Fluorescence Detection

Observation of cellular lipid peroxidation using C11-BODIPY581/591 lipid peroxidation probe (Thermo Fisher, Shanghai, China).

Detection of Intracellular MDA and GSH Levels

Malondialdehyde (MDA) and reduced GSH were mainly used to evaluate the level of ferroptosis occurring in THP-1 cells. MDA was determined using Solarbio kit instructions (Solarbio Science & Technology, Beijing, China). GSH was determined using a kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Absorbance was determined by a fully automated microplate analyzer (EPOCH, BIOTEK, USA).

Western Blot

Cell lysates configured in the ratio of RIPA:PMSF = 100:1­(RIPA was from Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China; PMSF was from GenStar, Beijing, China) were added to the treated THP-1 cells. Subsequently, 15–20 μg of denatured proteins were subjected to immunoblotting according to standard methods after protein concentration was determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein quantification kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Samples were separated using 12.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (EpiZyme, Shanghai, China), and proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, USA). After one hour of closure at room temperature, the membrane was incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C, followed by 2 h of incubation with secondary antibody (Abcloal, Wuhan, China). After staining with ECL luminescence solution (EpiZyme, Shanghai, China), the membranes were exposed to an automated chemiluminescence detector (Tanon 5200, Shanghai Tianneng Technology Co., Ltd.). Quantification was performed using Image J (National Institutes of Health, USA). The brands and catalog numbers of all antibodies used in Western blot experiments are listed in Table S2.

Solubility at Different pH Values

The working solutions were configured according to the method described in the literature. Indium standard solutions of 0, 0.625, 1.25, 2.5, 5.0, 10 and 20 μg/mL were prepared, and the absorbance was measured in an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (Model GFA-7000A, Shimadzu, Japan) and the indium standard curve was plotted. After taking 2.5 μL of ITO NPs, adding 1997.5 μL of working solution, ultrasonically dispersing and centrifuging with a high-speed centrifuge, 1 mL of the supernatant was taken and detected by the atomic absorption spectrophotometer, and the ion content measured in the supernatant was calculated by using the standard curve.

In Vitro Oxidizing Capacity

The in vitro oxidizing ability of ITO NPs was assessed using 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) as described in the literature. To 96 μL of 25 μg/mL 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein (DCF) working solution, 4 μL of 5 mg/mL ITO NPs were added and incubated for 2 h away from light. The DCF fluorescence emission spectra were collected in the range of 500–600 nm using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (F-4600, Hitachi High-Technologies Corporation) at an excitation wavelength of 490 nm.

Detection of Indium in Cells by Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS)

After seeding THP-1 cells at 4 × 105 cells/mL, they were exposed to ITO. Cell samples were then collected at various intervals: 1.5, 3, 6, 12, and 24 h after treatment. Discard the original medium, PBS washed three times, the cells were digested and then centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min to collect the cells, 1 mL of PBS was added to resuspend the cells, and the cells were counted using a hemocyte counting plate. After counting, the cells were again centrifuged at 1000 g for 5 min to collect the cells, after which the supernatant was discarded and nitrated by adding 1 mL of 65% concentrated nitric acid and left in the dark overnight. On the next day, slowly open the cap of the centrifuge tube, slowly pour the digested sample into a beaker and add 2 mL of 30% hydrogen peroxide, place it on a graphite heating plate preheated to 200 °C to heat up the acid and remove it when there is about 1 mL of liquid left, cool it down and add 1% HNO3 to make it volume to 6 mL, and then put it in the refrigerator at 4 °C for preparation of the measurement after being filtered with a 0.22 μm filter. The indium standard solution and the sample to be tested were tested according to the AAS procedure, and the content of indium in the sample to be tested was calculated using the standard curve.

Statistical Analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (X̅ ± SD), and all data were from three or more independent replicated experiments. SPSS 23.0 and Excel 2021 were used for preliminary sorting and analysis of experimental data. t test, one-way ANOVA and Dunnett’s method were used for statistical comparison. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Physicochemical Characterization of ITO Particles

TEM revealed that ITO particles exhibited a roughly hexagonal morphology (Figure A). Quantitative analysis of 100 particles demonstrated that the particle sizes of U-ITO and N-ITO were mainly distributed in the ranges of 40–160 nm and 10–40 nm respectively (Figure B). Hydration dynamics and stability were assessed in aqueous and physiological media (Table S3). Notably, ITO particles exhibited minimal aggregation in RPMI 1640 and DMEM, as evidenced by hydrodynamic sizes approximating primary particle dimensions (TEM) and elevated zeta potential values (absolute magnitude) compared to DI water or PBS, indicating enhanced stability in this medium.

Lung Damage Effects in Mice after Multiple Oropharyngeal Inhalation Exposures to ITO Particles

In murine exposure studies, 28-day oropharyngeal inhalation of ITO nanoparticles induced pronounced histopathological alterations in lung tissue (Figure D). N-ITO provoked dose-dependent interstitial thickening, alveolar septal widening, inflammatory cell infiltration, and architectural disruption, with maximal severity in the 36 mg/kg b.w. N-ITO cohort. In the present study, PAS staining and Masson staining were further used to assess glycogen/glycoprotein exudation and collagen fiber deposition in lung tissue, respectively. The results of PAS staining (Figure E) showed that the ITO particulate-treated group (especially the 36 mg/kg b.w. N-ITO group) showed a significant increase in fine bronchial tubes, terminal fine bronchioles, and glycogen/glycoproteins in the alveoli, and the positive areas in the N-ITO group were was significantly larger than that of the U-ITO group in a dose-dependent manner. Masson’s trichrome staining highlighted collagen fiber deposition around vasculature and airways, both exacerbated in nanoparticle-exposed groups (Figure F).

Immunohistochemistry staining results (Figure A–D) showed that both IL-1β and TNF-α protein expression were significantly up-regulated in the ITO particulate-treated group compared with the control group in a dose-dependent and particle-size-dependent manner, with the expression in the N-ITO group significantly higher than that in the U-ITO group. When particulate matter enters the lungs, lung macrophages first recognize and phagocytose these particles, possibly inducing an inflammatory response and releasing inflammatory cytokines. To further characterize the changes of inflammatory factors in macrophages, this study used immunofluorescence double labeling to observe the expression of IL-1β in mouse lung macrophages (Figure E). Macrophage-specific IL-1β immunofluorescence further corroborated N-ITO-driven inflammation, with signal intensity in alveolar macrophages exceeding adjacent parenchymal regions, suggesting macrophage polarization as a primary contributor to pulmonary inflammation induced by ITO.

Transcriptomics of Lung Tissue from Mice Exposed to ITO Particles by Multiple Oropharyngeal Inhalation

DEGs analysis revealed distinct transcriptional profiles among control group, U-ITO group and N-ITO group (Figure A). Compared with control, U-ITO exposure induced 220 DEGs (173 up-regulated, 47 down-regulated), whereas N-ITO treatment caused more pronounced changes with 874 DEGs (581 up-regulated, 293 down-regulated). Notably, solute carrier family 7 member 11 (Slc7a11) emerged as the top up-regulated gene in the N-ITO group, with U-ITO also showing significant induction of this transporter. Besides, both U-ITO and N-ITO significantly up-regulated key ferroptosis-initiating genes Hmox1 (heme oxygenase 1), which also suggested ITO particles may activate ferroptosis pathways (Figure S1A). GO enrichment analysis showed that DEGs obtained after U-ITO and N-ITO exposure were the most enriched in “biological process”, followed by “cellular component” and the least affected in “molecular function” related functional terms (Figure S1B).

Subsequent KEGG pathway analysis of the DEGs revealed activation of immune/inflammatory pathways in both U-ITO and N-ITO treatment groups compared to control (Figure B), including “Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction”, “Chemokine signaling pathway”, “lL-17 signaling pathway” and so on. In addition to immune response- and inflammation-related pathways, we noted that N-ITO and U-ITO caused simultaneous changes in “Ferroptosis” signaling pathway. Therefore, we further screened the expression of inflammation-related and ferroptosis-related genes, including inflammation-related IL-17a, Tgfb1, IL-1b, IL-1a and Smad3, as well as ferroptosis-related Slc7a11, Slc40a1, Hmox1 and Fth1. Figure C shows that both U-ITO and N-ITO can cause up/down regulation of genes related to inflammation and ferroptosis. More importantly, the changes caused by N-ITO are more obvious than those caused by U-ITO in terms of the fold change of gene expression. RT-qPCR validation demonstrated inflammation-related and Ferroptosis-related genes showed consistent up/down regulation trends with RNA-seq both in N-ITO and U-ITO treatments (Figure D), corroborating transcriptomic findings. The interaction network analysis delineating crosstalk between inflammatory and ferroptosis pathways revealed direct protein–protein interactions connecting validated pro-inflammatory genes (e.g., IL-17a, Tgfb1) with core ferroptosis regulators (e.g., Hmox1) (Figure E), which suggesting that ferroptosis may be associated with the development of inflammation in ITO treated mouse.

Repeated Oropharyngeal Inhalation Exposure to ITO Particles Induced Ferroptosis in the Lung Tissue of Mice

Ferroptosis, an iron-dependent cell death pathway mediated by lethal lipid peroxidation, was investigated through its key regulators Slc7a11 and Gpx4. , To further investigate whether ferroptosis occurs in mouse lungs after exposure to ITO particles, this study used immunohistochemistry to detect the expression of Slc7a11 and Gpx4 in mouse lung tissues. The results showed that both U-ITO and N-ITO caused a significant dose-dependent decrease in Slc7a11 and Gpx4 compared with the control group (Figure A–D). To further confirm the specific site of ferroptosis, we performed CD11b and Gpx4 double immunofluorescence labeling, which also showed that U-ITO and N-ITO caused a decrease in the intensity of Gpx4 immunofluorescence in macrophages at both doses (Figure E). These results suggest that ITO particles may affect the redox function of Gpx4 by decreasing its expression in macrophages, leading to the occurrence of cellular ferroptosis.

ITO Particles Induce Inflammatory Response in THP-1 Cells

Based on previous studies, we focused on lung macrophages and selected THP-1 as a cell model to further verify the cell viability and inflammatory response of lung macrophages to ITO of different sizes in vitro. Neutral red staining revealed dose-dependent cytotoxicity in THP-1 cells exposed to ITO particles and N-ITO exhibited greater toxicity than U-ITO at the same concentration (Figure S2A). In addition, the changes in cell viability were further confirmed by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) content assay (Figure S2B). The results showed that both ITO particles significantly decreased cell viability at all concentrations except for the 6.25 μg/mL U-ITO group, and the cell viability of the 25 μg/mL N-ITO group was significantly lower than that of the U-ITO group.

The polarization balance of M1/M2 macrophages plays a key role in organ inflammation and injury. To investigate ITO particle-induced macrophage polarization, we analyzed CD86-positive M1 macrophages in THP-1 cells through flow cytometry. Both U-ITO and N-ITO particle treatments significantly increased M1 macrophage populations compared to control after 24-h exposure, with N-ITO demonstrating higher induction than U-ITO (Figure A). Consistent with this polarization shift, immunofluorescence revealed intensified iNOS expression in ITO-treated group, particularly in N-ITO-exposed cells which showed greater fluorescence intensity versus U-ITO (Figure B). Then by ELISA assays, it was found that the release of both IL-1β and TNF-α was significantly increased after ITO particles treatment compared with the control group, and the N-ITO group resulted in a significantly higher release of inflammatory factors than the U-ITO group (Figure C). These collective findings demonstrate that ITO particles, particularly in nanoform, potently drive macrophage polarization toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype and subsequent inflammatory mediator production.

ITO Particles Induce Ferroptosis in THP-1 Cells

To verify whether ITO particle treatment resulted in ferroptosis in THP-1 cells, this study used Fer-1­(Ferrostatin-1), an inhibitor of ferroptosis, for pretreatment. Compared with the ITO particle-treated group, cell viability was significantly increased in both U-ITO and N-ITO groups after Fer-1 pretreatment (Figure A,B), suggesting that ITO particles may induce ferroptosis in THP-1 cells. It has been shown that the occurrence of ferroptosis is dependent on the accumulation of intracellular Fe2+. Therefore, this study further used FerroOrange probe to observe the changes of intracellular Fe2+ after ITO particles treatment. The results showed that the fluorescence intensity of intracellular Fe2+ was significantly enhanced with increasing dose after 24 h of ITO particles treatment compared with the control (Figure C). At the same dose, the change in the fluorescence intensity of Fe2+ was significantly higher in the N-ITO group than in the U-ITO group. Lipid peroxidation is an important feature of cellular ferroptosis, and we examined the indicators GSH and MDA associated with lipid peroxidation. Compared with the control group, the content of MDA was significantly elevated at 12.5 and 25 μg/mL ITO particle treatment (Figure D). The intracellular GSH content decreased in a dose-dependent manner after 24 h of ITO particle treatment, which was significant only at 25 μg/mL (Figure E). At the same concentration, the changes of GSH and MDA contents in the N-ITO group were slightly greater than those in the U-ITO group. Our study also used C11-BODIPY581/591 probe to observe cellular lipid peroxidation. The results showed that the intensity of red fluorescence in the cells was gradually weakened and the intensity of green fluorescence was gradually enhanced with the increase of the treatment dose of ITO particles, which means lipid peroxidation in THP-1 cells (Figure F). At the same dose, the change in fluorescence intensity was significantly greater in the N-ITO group than in the U-ITO group. In addition, the result of ferroptosis-related protein Gpx4 and Slc7a11 expression showed that the expression of intracellular Gpx4 and Slc7a11 was decreased in a concentration-dependent manner compared with the control group (Figure G). These results suggest that ITO particles may lead to lipid peroxidation in THP-1 cells, thus inducing ferroptosis.

Ferroptosis Is Involved in ITO Particle-Induced Inflammatory Response in THP-1 Cells

In this study, we demonstrated that ITO particle treatment resulted in ferroptosis and inflammatory response in THP-1 cells, but the relationship between cellular ferroptosis and inflammatory response was unclear. Therefore, we further explored the association between ferroptosis and cellular inflammation using ferroptosis inhibitor Fer-1. Based on the results of previous experiments, 25 μg/mL of ITO particles was selected as the experimental dose, and THP-1 cells was pretreated with 5 μmol/mL Fer-1 for 2 h before coincubation with ITO particles for 24 h. Flow cytometry analysis revealed that Fer-1 pretreatment significantly reduced the M1-type macrophage increase caused by ITO particle treatment (Figure A). Laser confocal microscopy observations showed that Fer-1 pretreatment significantly reduced the change in iNOS immunofluorescence intensity after both N-ITO and U-ITO treatment (Figure B). These results suggested that Fer-1, an inhibitor of ferroptosis, could alleviate ITO particles-induced M1-type polarization of THP-1 cells. To further explore the relationship between ferroptosis and cellular inflammatory factor secretion, we detected the release of IL-1β and TNF-α using an ELISA kit (Figure C). The results showed that Fer-1 pretreatment significantly reduced IL-1β and TNF-α levels induced by ITO particles. In summary, Fer-1 was able to alleviate ITO particles-induced M1-type polarization and secretion of inflammatory factors in THP-1 cells, suggesting that cellular ferroptosis may contribute to cellular inflammatory responses. Next, we further focused on exploring how ferroptosis is involved in ITO-induced cellular inflammation.

ITO Particle-Induced Ferroptosis in THP-1 Cells Mediates HMGB1 Secretion and Nuclear Translocation

DAMPs are endogenous molecules released by the body’s own cell death and represent endogenous danger signal. HMGB1 is a typical DAMPs as an important late inflammatory mediator whose release is associated with cellular ferroptosis. , In this study, we first examined the changes in the content of HMGB1 in the supernatant of THP-1 cells after treatment with ITO particles (Figure A). The results showed that ITO particles increased the level of HMGB1 released by THP-1 cells in a dose-dependent manner. At the same dose, the N-ITO group induced significantly higher HMGB1 release than the U-ITO group. To further investigate the relationship between ferroptosis and HMGB1 release in THP-1 cells, the present study used Fer-1 to pretreat THP-1 cells (5 μmol/mL, 2 h) and then exposed them to ITO particles treatment solution for 24 h. The results showed that Fer-1 pretreatment significantly reduced the elevated HMGB1 levels induced by both sizes of ITO particles (Figure B).

Studies have shown that under stress conditions, HMGB1 is transferred from the nucleus to the cytoplasm and then released to the outside of the cell. So this study explored the expression of HMGB1 in the nucleus and cytoplasm after ITO particles treatment. The results, as shown in Figure C, showed a concentration-dependent decrease in HMGB1 expression in the nucleus after 24 h of ITO particles treatment. HMGB1 expression in the N-ITO group was significantly lower than that in the U-ITO group. The expression level of HMGB1 in the cytoplasm showed a dose-dependent increase compared with the control group, which was significantly higher at 12.5 and 25 μg/mL. HMGB1 Secreted by Ferroptosis in THP-1 Cells Promotes Cellular Inflammatory Responses by Activating p38MAPK through Binding to the RAGE Receptor

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) has been reported to induce HMGB1 secretion from mouse monocyte macrophages and maintain high serum concentrations for the following 24 h. Meanwhile, HMGB1 also stimulates monocyte macrophages to produce pro-inflammatory factors, including IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α. Therefore, this study investigated the expression of total HMGB1 protein and inflammatory protein IL-1β in THP-1 cells after ITO particles treatment. The results, as shown in Figure A, showed that the intracellular expression levels of total HMGB1 and IL-1β proteins were dose-dependently increased after ITO particles treatment compared with the control group. The expression of HMGB1 and IL-1β proteins in the N-ITO-stained group was significantly higher than that in the U-ITO group. These results tentatively suggest that HMGB1 plays a role in ITO particle-induced cellular inflammatory responses.

HMGB1 nuclear translocation can bind to the receptor for advanced glycosylation end products (RAGE) and activate p38 MAPK to promote cellular inflammatory responses. It has been shown that blocking HMGB1 binding to RAGE can inhibit p38 MAPK activation, thereby attenuating the inflammatory response. , Therefore, this study investigated the changes in the expression of RAGE, p38 MAPK and IL-1β proteins in THP-1 cells after ITO particles treatment. AOP Construction for ITO Particle-Induced Lung Injury

To further identify the molecular initiating events of ITO-induced ferroptosis and subsequent inflammation, and to understand why N-ITO induces more intense inflammatory responses than U-ITO, we first examined the key physicochemical properties related to the bioeffects of metal oxides, including size, metal ion release, and in vitro oxidative capacity, then correlated them with key ferroptosis and inflammation end points. As shown in Figure A, the In3+ release of N-ITO was higher than that of U-ITO only at pH = 3. Whereas, under other conditions, the amount of indium released from the two was close. Moreover, Figure B showed that the in vitro oxidative capacity of N-ITO was higher than that of U-ITO. Then, we further investigated the Pearson correlation of size, In3+ release, and in vitro oxidizing capacity with cellular responses in THP-1 cells (Figure C), including the following indicators of cellular effects: cell viability, MDA content, GSH depletion, Fe2+, inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α), ferroptosis and inflammation-related proteins (Gpx4, Scl7a11, HMGB1, RAGE, IL-1β). The results of Pearson correlation showed that particle size was positively correlated with cell viability, GSH amount, and ferroptosis-related proteins, whereas it was negatively correlated with MDA content, Fe2+, inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α), and inflammation-related proteins. The absolute values of the correlation coefficients (R) were greater than 0.8, indicating a good correlation, except for Fe2+ and Gpx4. The correlations between In3+ release and cellular response indices were lower, with most of the absolute values of R in the range of 0.2–0.4. The correlation between in vitro oxidative capacity and cellular response indicators was high, with most of the absolute R values ranging from 0.6 to 1.0. These results suggest that particle size is the main factor influencing the difference in inflammation-inducing effects between the two particles.

Existing studies point out that smaller particle sizes tend to be accompanied by greater cellular uptake. AAS (Figure D) showed that the indium content of the cells increased gradually with the duration of exposure, reaching a peak at 12 h. At the same end point of staining, intracellular indium content was higher in the N-ITO group than in the U-ITO group, and cellular uptake of small-sized N-ITO was higher than that of U-ITO, which might explain why N-ITO has a higher capacity to induce inflammation than U-ITO. The results demonstrate that chloroquine pretreatment reverses the ITO particle-induced upregulation of ferroptosis-related genes, elevation of MDA, and reduction of GSH (Figure E,F). On this basis, we constructed AOP of ITO particle-induced lung injury using cellular uptake as the molecular initiation event and combined with relevant mechanisms in the prophase (Figure G).

Discussion

Indium tin oxide (ITO) nanoparticles are extensively utilized in various fields due to their superior electrical and optical properties. However, the rapid expansion of the global electronics industry raises concerns about the environmental release of ITO during production, use, and disposal, particularly regarding occupational exposure. Reports indicate that ITO exposure may result in lung damage, including conditions like alveolar proteinosis and emphysema. − Occupational assessments reveal that workers are primarily exposed to ITO particles in the size range of 20–50 nm, yet most existing studies have focused on larger micrometer-sized particles. To address this gap, our study investigated both nanoscale and micrometer-sized ITO particles using a repeated oropharyngeal aspiration model in mice. While the oropharyngeal aspiration model differs from actual occupational inhalation in terms of particle deposition and clearance patterns due to its bolus administration rather than simulating chronic, low-concentration exposure, this well-established method provides a controlled and efficient means to deliver precise doses of particles directly into the respiratory tract, making it particularly suitable for elucidating clear dose–response relationships and underlying mechanisms. The 3.6 mg/kg exposure dose in this study was based on the OEL (0.1 mg/m3), which was converted from human to mouse exposure dose using a safety factor and a weight factor. A 10-fold exposure dose (36 mg/kg) was set as the high dose group. The primary objective of this dose-range design was to establish a pronounced toxicological response, which served as a necessary basis for elucidating the size effect and toxic mechanisms underlying lung injury induced by ITO particles. Furthermore, we aimed to construct an AOP framework for ITO-induced lung inflammation, and to provide a foundation for the risk assessment of lung injury in ITO occupational exposure.

The toxicity of metal oxide nanoparticles is closely linked to their physicochemical properties. Thus, we first comprehensively analyzed the pristine size, hydrated particle size, and zeta potential of ITO particles. In our study, both N-ITO and U-ITO particles were spherical. Notably, the absolute zeta potential was higher in complete medium compared to ultrapure water, PBS, and saline, indicating enhanced stability of ITO particles in the complete medium. This increased stability may be attributed to the formation of protein coronas, as the complete medium contains serum proteins that facilitate rapid protein adsorption on the particle surface, thereby enhancing stability.

In the present study, the histopathological results of lungs of mice exposed to micro- and nanoscale ITO showed pathological changes such as inflammatory infiltration, increased mucus secretion, and proliferation of collagen fibers. This finding is consistent with the results of other studies. For example, Nagano et al. observed pathological damage such as alveolar protein deposition, macrophage and inflammatory cell infiltration, and increased lung weight by inhalation of ITO particles; while Lim et al. found that ITO particles induced broncho-interstitial pneumonitis and lung hyperplasia in male rats. Mice exhibited collagen fiber proliferation after 28 days of exposure to ITO particles, and prolonged or repeated exposure may lead to more severe pulmonary fibrosis. Clinical reports corroborate this progression, where chronic occupational ITO exposure leads to radiologically evident interstitial infiltrates and fibrosis, and histologically confirmed cholesterol granulomas with peri-lesional fibrosis. In addition, the expression of the inflammatory proteins IL-1β and TNF-α was elevated more markedly in lung tissues as the ITO particle size decreased. Notably, N-ITO showed more pronounced pathological damage than U-ITO at the same exposure dose. Similarly, Liu et al. found that exposure of rats to ITO caused an increase in IL-1β and TNF-α in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. These studies and our results suggest that particle size may be an important factor influencing the toxicity of ITO particles, with smaller particle sizes being more toxic. More importantly, we identified pulmonary macrophages as the main site of lung tissue inflammation by immunofluorescence double labeling, which may be a consequence of pulmonary macrophages as the first line of defense after the invasion of foreign particles and phagocytosing most of the ITO particles deposited in the alveoli.

In this study, RNA-Seq was used to further explore the mechanism of inflammatory injury caused by ITO particles. DEG analysis showed that both U-ITO and N-ITO up-regulated the ferroptosis-related genes Slc7a11 and Hmox1, suggesting that exposure to ITO particles may activate the ferroptosis pathway. KEGG enrichment analysis further showed that both sizes of ITO particles induced significant alterations of ferroptosis and inflammation-related pathways, with the smaller size N-ITO triggered a greater number of changes in ferroptosis-related genes. These results suggest that ITO particles may lead to ferroptosis in mouse lungs, and ferroptosis may be closely related to the development of inflammatory injury in the lungs. Slc7a11 and Gpx4 are key regulatory proteins for ferroptosis, and inhibition of these proteins has been shown to induce ferroptosis. For example, Wang et al. found that plumbagin treatment of colon cancer cells (SW480 and HCT116) resulted in a decrease in Slc7a11 protein expression and an increase in Fe2+ levels, as well as a significant decrease in GSH and Gpx4, which promoted ferroptosis and inhibited cell proliferation and invasion. Shao et al. on the other hand, found that treatment of nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells with choline erucinate resulted in an increase in ferrous iron, lipid peroxidation and reactive oxygen species, accompanied by downregulation of Slc7a11 and upregulation of transferrin and transferrin receptor. The results of the present study showed that Slc7a11 and Gpx4 protein expression of pulmonary macrophages were significantly down-regulated after ITO particle exposure, consistent with the above studies. It should be noted that transcriptome sequencing showed significant up-regulation of Slc7a11 gene expression, which was opposite to the protein expression trend. This observed discrepancy between mRNA upregulation and protein downregulation of SLC7A11 is biologically plausible and often reflects post-transcriptional regulation. , The upregulation of Slc7a11 mRNA may represent a compensatory transcriptional response to oxidative stress upon ITO exposure. However, the decisive factor for ferroptosis is the functional protein level. The concurrent downregulation of Slc7a11 and Gpx4 proteins strongly suggests the activation of translational suppression and/or protein degradation pathwayskey regulatory nodes in ferroptosis execution. For instance, iron overload and elevated ROS could promote proteasomal degradation or inhibit translation of these key antiferroptotic proteins. Thus, the net protein loss, consistent with other ferroptotic markers (iron accumulation, lipid peroxidation), ultimately drives the cell fate. This multilayered regulation underscores the complexity of ferroptosis and strengthens our conclusion based on functional protein data. Overall, ITO particles not only caused inflammatory damage in mouse lungs but also induced lung ferroptosis, especially in lung macrophages. However, the relationship between ferroptosis and inflammation still needs to be further investigated.

Further, THP-1 cells were used as the in vitro lung macrophage model to explore the characteristics of ITO particle-induced inflammatory injury in the lungs and the related mechanisms of ferroptosis. The in vitro concentrations selected in this study (6.25–25 μg/mL) were established with consideration of their dose-metric relevance to both the in vivo animal exposure and real-world occupational scenarios. The animal low dose (3.6 mg/kg) was derived from the occupational exposure limit (OEL) for indium tin oxide (0.1 mg/m3), with the detailed interspecies conversion provided in Text S1. To bridge the in vivo and in vitro doses, we employed an alveolar surface area-based dosimetry model. Mice received eight weekly instillations of ITO at 3.6 mg/kg per dose. For a 25 g mouse, the cumulative lung deposition was 0.72 mg (0.09 mg × 8). Given the mouse alveolar surface area of 82.2 cm2, the particle load was 8.76 μg/cm2. Scaling to a 24-well plate (2 cm2, 500 μL medium) yielded an equivalent in vitro concentration of approximately 35 μg/mL. This value is of the same order of magnitude as our selected concentrations (6.25–25 μg/mL), with the latter being slightly lower to enable investigation of early mechanistic events under subtoxic conditions. Macrophages are key regulators of innate and adaptive immune responses, and their polarization status is influenced by microenvironmental stimuli, which are mainly classified into pro-inflammatory M1 and anti-inflammatory M2. The M1/M2 balance is crucial for maintaining tissue homeostasis. In order to verify the ITO-induced inflammatory response of lung macrophages at the in vitro level, we explored whether they promoted the polarization of THP-1 cells to M1-type macrophages. The results showed that ITO particles promoted M1 polarization of THP-1 cells in a dose-dependent manner, and iNOS protein expression was significantly increased at an exposure dose of 25 μg/mL. This is similar to the study of Zhao et al. who found that TiO2 nanoparticles of 25 nm activated mouse peritoneal macrophages to polarize toward M1 type and increased mRNA expression of iNOS. Le et al. also showed that ZnO nanoparticles promoted the polarization of RAW264.7 cells toward M1-type macrophages. The activated macrophages always secrete various inflammatory mediators, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. To investigate whether ITO particles induce an inflammatory response in THP-1 cells, we measured cytokine levels in the cell culture supernatants using ELISA. The results demonstrated that ITO particles increased the release of IL-1β and TNF-α in a dose- and size-dependent manner. This finding aligns with the results of Zhang et al., who reported that TiO2 nanoparticles significantly and dose-dependently elevated IL-1β and TNF-α levels in THP-1 cells. Additionally, William et al. observed that small-sized silver nanoparticles (Ag-NPs) caused porcine brain microvessel endothelial cells to release more IL-1β compared to large-sized Ag-NPs. Therefore, our study confirms that ITO particle exposure triggers inflammatory responses in THP-1 cells, as evidenced by increased M1 polarization and elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines, with smaller-sized N-ITO particles resulting in more severe inflammatory reactions.

Ferroptosis is a type of iron-dependent regulated cell death associated with a variety of diseases and characterized by the accumulation of intracellular iron and lipid peroxides. Compared with other cell death types, ferroptosis has unique morphological, biochemical, and genetic features. Our in vivo studies and transcriptomic analyses suggest that ITO particles activate the ferroptosis pathway, alter the expression of ferroptosis-associated genes, and reduce the levels of the key proteins Slc7a11 and Gpx4. Therefore, we further verified whether ITO particles induced ferroptosis in THP-1 cells. Fer-1 is a commonly used inhibitor of ferroptosis by regulating oxidative stress and iron metabolism in cells. The decrease in cell viability caused by ITO particles was restored after Fer-1 pretreatment, suggesting that the cells may have experienced ferroptosis. To further confirm this, we examined the intracellular levels of Fe2+, ROS, lipid peroxidation products GSH, and MDA. The results showed that the levels of intracellular Fe2+, ROS, and MDA increased in a dose-dependent manner after ITO particle staining, whereas the level of GSH decreased. Small-sized N-ITO induced more Fe2+, ROS, and MDA production than large-sized U-ITO and led to a more substantial decrease in GSH. These results are consistent with ferroptosis-related studies. Shen et al. found that myeloid differentiation protein 2 (MD2) was able to induce ferroptosis in neuronal cells, as evidenced by the up-regulation of Fe2+ and MDA and the down-regulation of GSH. Zhang et al, on the other hand, explored the effects of ZnO nanoparticles on cellular ferroptosis using HUVECs, L02 and RAW264.7 cell models and found that they induced ferroptosis by perturbing the iron homeostasis, leading to elevated ROS and MDA levels and GSH depletion. Similarly, Yan et al. demonstrated that PM2.5 triggered ferroptosis in L02 cells, characterized by excessive ROS production, MDA accumulation, and GPX4 downregulation. Our in vivo studies showed that ITO particles decreased the expression of Slc7a11 and Gpx4 in lung tissues. In in vitro experiments, ITO particles induced a concentration-dependent decrease in the expression of Slc7a11 and Gpx4 in THP-1 cells, and protein expression was lower in the N-ITO-treated group than in the U-ITO group. This is consistent with the in vivo findings indicating that ITO particles induced ferroptosis in lung tissues. Similar to existing studies, Mo et al. noted that tetrahydrocortisone citrate (TetC) induced ferroptosis in A549 cells by inhibiting the expression of Slc7a11 and Gpx4. In addition, Chen et al. found that Gigantol induced ferroptosis through inhibition of the Slc7a11-Gpx4 axis, thereby exerting an antilung cancer effect. Thus, ITO particles resulted in decreased Slc7a11 and Gpx4 expression, further validating the occurrence of cellular ferroptosis. Next, we will explore the relationship between ferroptosis and the inflammatory response.

DAMPs are endogenous danger signals that activate the innate immune system and regulate inflammatory responses and cell death. HMGB1 acts as a nuclear and secreted protein involved in transcription, proliferation and inflammatory processes. Under stress conditions such as injury or infection, HMGB1 is released into the extracellular compartment. , Studies have shown that LPS treatment increases HMGB1 gene expression in RAW264.7 cells and promotes its release from the nucleus to the cytoplasm. In this study, ITO particles treatment of THP-1 cells showed a significant increase in the secretion of total HMGB1 factor at concentrations of 12.5 and 25 μg/mL, and a dose-dependent decrease in the expression of HMGB1 in the nucleus, and a dose-dependent increase in the cytoplasmic expression of HMGB1. After Fer-1 pretreatment, the supernatant HMGB1 in THP-1 cells was significantly decreased, the expression level of HMGB1 in the nucleus increased, and the increase of HMGB1 in the cytoplasm was suppressed. Similarly, Wen et al. showed that HMGB1 release was increased after treatment of human fibrosarcoma cells (HT1080), human pancreatic adenocarcinoma cells (PANC1), and mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEF) with four ferroptosis activators (erastin, sorafenib, RSL3, and FIN56), whereas the release of HMGB1 increased in comparison with the ferroptosis inhibitors (Fer-1, liproxstatin-1 and baicalein) cotreatment inhibited HMGB1 release. Wang et al. also showed that methotrexate (MTX) induced ferroptosis in HepG2 and AML-12 cells while significantly increasing HMGB1 expression, while Fer-1 and DFP pretreatment decreased HMGB1 expression. This translocation likely represents a combined process involving both active signal-mediated and passive release mechanisms. In the early stages of ferroptosis, the intense oxidative stress triggered by ITO exposure may induce post-translational modifications of HMGB1, such as acetylation, which promotes its active nuclear export via binding to the exportin CRM1a view supported by several studies on oxidative stress-regulated acetylation and nucleocytoplasmic shuttling of HMGB1. − As ferroptosis progresses, the loss of plasma and nuclear membrane integrity may then facilitate the passive release of HMGB1. ITO particles, by inducing ferroptosis, may first activate a modification-dependent active nuclear export mechanism, leading to HMGB1 accumulation in the cytoplasm, which subsequently promotes its eventual extracellular release to initiate and amplify inflammatory responses. From what has been discussed above, we clarified that the release of HMGB1 in THP-1 is the downstream events of ferroptosis induced by ITO. More importantly, combined with the results of the subsequent HMGB1 intervention experiments, we further confirmed that ITO particles induced ferroptosis in THP-1 cells, which may promote cellular inflammatory responses by mediating the release of HMGB1.

RAGE induces inflammatory responses by interacting with multiple molecules in the cell membrane. HMGB1 binds to RAGE and initiates multiple cellular responses including inflammation. Binding of HMGB1 to RAGE activates signaling pathways such as MAPK, NF-κB, and JAK/STAT, which promotes inflammatory responses. , The results of this study showed that ITO particles significantly increased the expression of RAGE, p-p38 MAPK and IL-1β. Similarly, Liang et al. found that HMGB1 stimulation of human bronchial epithelial cells (HBECs) resulted in a significant increase in the expression and secretion of TNF-α, IL-1β, and MMP-9, and induced elevated RAGE protein expression. Blockade of RAGE and inhibition of the p38 MAPK pathway significantly reduced the secretion of these inflammatory factors. To verify the mechanism that ITO particles activate the RAGE-p38 MAPK pathway by promoting HMGB1 expression leading to inflammatory injury in the lungs, we pretreated with FPS-ZM1 (a RAGE blocker) and detected the expression of RAGE, p38 MAPK and IL-1β. The results showed that FPS-ZM1 pretreatment successfully reversed the changes in protein levels induced by ITO particles. Consistent with this, Mi et al. showed that FPS-ZM1 reversed the elevation of HUVEC cytokines and apoptosis caused by hypoxia and reoxygenation. Li et al. found that the FPS-ZM1-treated group had reduced protein expression of HMGB1 and RAGE, restoration of inflammatory cell infiltration, and decreased expression of inflammatory factors, when compared to the group of asthmatic mice. In summary, the present study suggests that ITO particles activate the RAGE-p38 MAPK pathway by promoting HMGB1 release, which in turn promotes inflammatory injury in the lung.

AOP elucidates relationships at the biological level of organization (including molecular, subcellular, cellular, tissue, organ, organism, and whole populations), linking MIEs to AOs through causality. An important application area of AOP is the assessment of and decision-making about risks associated with materials, which provides a more efficient and cost-effective alternative method to assess the potential risks of inhaling particles.

In the context of nanomaterials, “MIE” refers to the initial molecular event activated by a stressor stimulus. Due to the structural properties and chemical diversity of nanomaterials, the triggered MIEs can be varied and complex. By correlation matrix analysis of ITO particle size, In3+ release, and in vitro oxidative capacity with indicators of cellular effects, we found that particle size was an important factor influencing cellular effects. Existing research indicates that higher cellular uptake is often the fundamental reason why nanoscale materials are more toxic than micrometer-scale materials, and it is also the molecular initiation event of the toxic cascade reaction. To verify this conjecture, we precisely quantified the induced content in THP-1 after exposure to two different sizes of ITO. Our findings underscore the pivotal role of particle size in determining the cellular uptake. more precisely, smaller ITO NPs exhibit a higher propensity to trigger MIEs, that is higher cellular uptake of ITO. This phenomenon aligns with established principles of nanotoxicology, wherein diminished particle dimensions (<100 nm) facilitate increased cellular internalization via energy-dependent endocytic pathways, such as clathrin-mediated endocytosis or macropinocytosis. Following pretreatment of THP-1 cells with chloroquine, reversal of ferroptosis-related markers was observed after 24-h coculture with ITO particles. This indicates that inducing cellular uptake of ITO particles represents an upstream mechanism of their ferroptosis induction and constitutes the molecular initiating event within the entire AOP framework.

Finally, based on our experimental data, we have preliminarily an AOP with cellular uptake as the MIE, covering a range of molecular, cellular and tissue-level events. Specifically, the ingested ITO may, due to its relatively high surface reactivity, trigger a series of cellular stress responses such as intracellular GSH depletion and MDA increase, and subsequently lead to the occurrence of cellular lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. As a downstream response of ferroptosis, HMGB1 is activated and transferred from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, and further activates the RAGE-p-p38MAPK pathway, thereby causing an inflammatory response and the ultimate adverse outcome - lung injury. The established AOP framework, anchored by cellular uptake as the MIE, provides a mechanistic bridge between ITO uptake and pulmonary toxicity, offering transformative insights for risk assessment of ITO nanomaterials. The subacute exposure model established in this study represents a crucial first step toward understanding the long-term disease progression. The AOP model constructed here provides key insights for future long-term exposure studies, assessing fibrosis risk, and identifying intervention targets. By delineating the causal chain from initial particle-cell interactions (MIE) to ferroptosis-driven inflammation and tissue injury, this model enables quantitative prediction of hazard potential based on early biomarkers (e.g., intracellular GSH/MDA levels, HMGB1 translocation) rather than relying solely on terminal histopathological end points. Such a paradigm shift aligns with the 3R (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) principles in toxicology, minimizing animal testing through mechanistically informed in vitro-to-in vivo extrapolation.

Conclusion

This study starts from the size difference between nanoscale and micrometer-scale ITO to explore the size effect of ITO respiratory exposure causing lung injury. At the same time, focusing on cell ferroptosis, an important mode of cell death, this study deeply explores its mechanism of action in ITO particle-induced lung injury. The in vivo study suggested that ITO particles (3.6 and 36 mg/kg) induced an inflammatory response and the development of ferroptosis in the lungs of mice after multiple oropharyngeal inhalation exposures, and pulmonary macrophages were the main sites of ITO-induced inflammation and ferroptosis. In in vitro studies, it was observed that 6.25–25 μg/mL ITO particles could produce significant inflammatory responses of THP-1 cells. The ferroptosis, which characterized by lipid peroxidation, accumulation of Fe2+, and abnormal expression of key ferroptosis proteins (Gpx4 and Scl7a11), played an important role in ITO-induced inflammation. The secretion and nuclear translocation of HMGB1 mediated by cellular ferroptosis, which further activates p38MAPK by binding to the RAGE receptor on the cell membrane, is an intermediate mechanism by which ITO-induced ferroptosis promotes cellular inflammatory responses. More importantly, the results of both in vivo and in vitro studies have confirmed that N-ITO can cause stronger ferroptosis and inflammation compared to U-ITO, which is related to its higher uptake by lung macrophages. The size-dependent lung injury of N-ITO provides a theoretical basis of particle size specificity for the improvement of occupational exposure limits of ITO. Taking cell uptake as the MIE and further integrating the key events in the mechanism, this study constructed the AOP of ITO induced inflammatory lung injury, helping for providing a strategy for the risk assessment of lung injury caused by occupational exposure to ITO.
