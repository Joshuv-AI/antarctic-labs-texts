Osculating Geometry and Higher-Order Distance Loci
Sandra Di Rocco, Kemal Rose, Luca Sodomaco

Abstract
. We discuss the problem of optimizing the distance function from a given point, subject to polynomial constraints. A key algebraic invariant that governs its complexity is the Euclidean distance degree , which pertains to first-order tangency. We focus on the data locus of points possessing at least one critical point of the distance function that is normal to a higher-order osculating space. We study the higher-order distance degree of a morphism as an intersection-theoretic invariant involving jet bundles and higher-order polar classes. Our approach builds on foundational definitions and results developed by Piene, particularly regarding higher-order polar loci. We give closed formulas for generic maps, Veronese embeddings, and toric embeddings. We place particular emphasis on the Bombieri-Weyl metric, revealing that the chosen metric profoundly influences both the degree and birationality of the higher-order projection maps. Additionally, we introduce a tropical framework that represents these degrees as stable intersections with Bergman fans, facilitating effective combinatorial computation in toric settings.



1.
Introduction

Motivation and Background

Metric algebraic geometry investigates the geometry of algebraic varieties through notions of distance and approximation
[
BKS24
]
. At its core lies the problem of identifying points on a variety that are closest to a given point in ambient space, typically with respect to the Euclidean norm.
A key measure of the intrinsic complexity of these problems is the
Euclidean distance degree (EDD)
. This algebraic invariant counts the number of complex critical points of the squared Euclidean distance function when restricted to a given variety. Introduced in the foundational work
[
DHO+16
]
, the EDD is central in the theory of optimization over algebraic varieties and has catalyzed recent advances at the interface of geometry and computation.

The classical EDD reflects first-order geometry: critical points are governed by tangent spaces and orthogonality conditions between the variety and the direction vector from a data point. However, many applications across data science, signal processing, and numerical optimization require a more refined notion of proximity, one that accounts for curvature, torsion, or higher-order contact. For instance, in manifold learning
[
MM10
,
FMN16
,
FIK+18
,
AK24
,
KWW25
]
, trajectory planning
[
BH75
,
Zha02
]
, nonlinear regression, and geometric data fitting
[
CP05
,
SW89
]
, it is not sufficient to minimize the Euclidean distance merely; how a model surface curves toward the data is crucial for both interpretability and accuracy. Such problems require critical configurations that exhibit higher-order contact, modeled via osculating spaces rather than tangents alone.
This motivates the study of algebraic invariants that generalize Euclidean distance degrees and reflect higher order tangency. Foundational work in this direction was developed by Piene in
[
Pie22
]
, where she introduces and studies higher-order polar classes and reciprocal loci in a broad algebro-geometric setting. Her results are instrumental to the developments presented in this paper. We focus on the case of nonsingular varieties, which often allows for local computations and more streamlined arguments. In particular, in Sections
3
,
4
, and
5
, we adopt and adapt some of Piene’s definitions and results within our framework. Further related recent contributions include
[
BW24
,
Hor24
,
BRW25
]
.

Geometric Setup and State of the Art.

We start by fixing an
(n+1)
-dimensional real vector space
V_{\mathbb{R}}
and a positive definite quadratic form
q\colon V_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{R}
. We denote by
Q
the quadric hypersurface defined by
q.
For any data point
u\in V_{\mathbb{R}}
, we denote by
d_{u}
the distance function from
u
, defined by
d_{u}(v)\coloneqq\sqrt{q(u-v)}
for all
v\in V_{\mathbb{R}}
. For any fixed subset
S\subseteq V_{\mathbb{R}}
such that
u\notin S
, it is natural to study the set of points
x\in S
attaining a local minimum of
d_{u}
restricted to
S
. In this paper, we restrict ourselves to subsets
S
that are affine cones over real algebraic varieties. As a prototypical example, we focus on affine cones over projective toric varieties
[
DDRP14
]
, and in particular varieties of rank at most one symmetric tensors, seen as homogeneous polynomials
[
Ban38
]
. More precisely, we let
Y_{\mathbb{R}}
be the image of a real morphism
f\colon X_{\mathbb{R}}\to\mathbb{P}(V_{\mathbb{R}})=\mathbb{P}_{{\mathbb{R}}}^{n}
and
S=C(Y_{\mathbb{R}})
be the affine cone over
Y_{\mathbb{R}}
. Firstly, despite being naturally a problem defined over the real numbers, the algebraic study of the locus of critical points of
d_{u}
requires working over the field of complex numbers. Secondly, we observe that a similar study may be carried out for more general affine varieties that are not cones. However, we restrict ourselves to affine cones over projective varieties to apply classical results of intersection theory in projective space. Motivated by these facts, we consider the complex vector space
V\coloneqq V_{\mathbb{R}}\otimes\mathbb{C}
, the Zariski closure
Y
of
Y_{\mathbb{R}}
in
\mathbb{P}(V)=\mathbb{P}^{n}
, and its affine cone
C(Y)\subseteq V
. In particular, we will always work with morphisms
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
, where
Y=f(X)
. Additionally, since the squared distance function
d_{u}^{2}
is polynomial, we consider it as a polynomial function
d_{u}^{2}\colon V\to\mathbb{C}
. We emphasize that the real quadratic form
q
induces only a bilinear symmetric form over
V
and not a Hermitian inner product. We denote by
Q
the nonsingular quadric hypersurface in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
defined by the quadratic form
q
.

A generic complex data point typically has a nonsingular critical point for the distance function restricted to a reduced variety. Introducing additional conditions on the nature of critical points leads to richer algebraic structures known as
data loci
. For example,
conditional ED data loci
encode data points with at least one critical point on a prescribed subvariety
[
Hor17
,
HR22
,
DRGS25
]
. Other well-studied data loci are
\varepsilon
-offsets
, representing points with a critical point at distance
\varepsilon
,
bisector hypersurfaces
, defined by data having two equidistant critical points
[
HW19
,
OS20
]
, and
Voronoi cells
, consisting of all data points closest to a fixed point lying on a given variety
[
CRSW22
]
.

In this work, we introduce and study the
higher-order distance degree
\operatorname{DD}_{k}(f,Q)
and
higher-order distance loci

\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)
of a morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
with respect to a fixed nonsingular quadric hypersurface
Q\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
. This invariant describes the algebraic complexity for
f
to satisfy a prescribed osculating condition. In the following, we summarize the main steps that motivate our definition.

For a fixed integer
k\geq 1
and a point
p\in X
, the
k
th osculating space

\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
is defined as the projective span of all partial derivatives of the components of
f
at
p
up to order
k
, equivalently, the image of the
k
th jet map at
p
. This space captures all infinitesimal directions of the morphism
f
at
p
up to order
k
and is the smallest projective linear subspace with this property. For
k=1
, the osculating space
\mathbb{T}_{p}^{1}(f)
coincides with the projective tangent space
\mathbb{T}_{p}(f)
, which has dimension
m=\dim X
at nonsingular points. If
f
is an embedding, the tangent space has constant dimension by definition. However, for
k\geq 2
, the dimension of the osculating spaces
\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)
may vary. We say that a morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
is
globally
k
-osculating
if the dimension of
\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)
is constant across all points of
X
(see Definition
2.2
).

.

In particular, a globally
1
-osculating parametrization is a closed embedding.
Globally
k
-osculating morphisms are particularly well-suited to geometric analysis, as they admit a natural system of invariants known as
higher-order polar classes

p_{k,i}(f)
for
i\in\{0,\ldots,m\}
. Introduced by Piene in
[
Pie22
]
, these classes are subvarieties of codimension
i
in
X
that encode the geometric behavior of the
k
th order osculating spaces. In Sections
2
and
3
, we recall the geometric framework of osculating functions and the main definitions and results on higher-order polar geometry from
[
Pie22
]
, which forms the foundation for our main results.

Section
4
recalls the notion of higher-order normality with respect to a quadric
Q
. Given a morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
and a point
u\in V
, we say that
[v]=f(p)\in f(X)
is
critical of order
k
for the squared distance function
d_{u}^{2}
if

[\nabla d_{u}^{2}(v)]\in\mathbb{N}_{p}^{k}(f,Q)\coloneqq\langle\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)^{\perp},f(p)\rangle\,,

where
\langle S\rangle
denotes the projective span of a subset
S\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
, and
\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)^{\perp}
is the
polar subspace
of the
k
th order osculating space
\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)
with respect to the quadric
Q
, see (
4.2
). We refer to
\mathbb{N}_{p}^{k}(f,Q)
as the
k
th order normal space
of
f
at
p
.

The central object of study is the
k
th order distance locus

\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)
, defined as the projection to the second factor
\mathbb{P}^{n}
of the incidence variety

\operatorname{DC}_{k}(f,Q)\coloneqq\overline{\left\{([v],[u])\in\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}\,\middle|\,[v]\in f(X)\text{ is critical of order }k\text{ for }d_{u}^{2}\right\}}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}\,.

For
k=1
, this construction recovers the classical
projective ED correspondence

\operatorname{DC}(f,Q)\coloneqq\operatorname{DC}_{1}(f,Q)
and the
Euclidean distance degree

\operatorname{DD}(f,Q)\coloneqq\operatorname{DD}_{1}(f,Q)
, defined as the degree of the surjective projection
{\rm pr}_{2}|_{\operatorname{DC}(f,Q)}\colon\operatorname{DC}(f,Q)\to\mathbb{P}^{n}=\operatorname{DL}_{1}(f,Q)
; see
[
DHO+16
, Theorem 4.4]
.
The description of higher-order distance loci
\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)
becomes increasingly intricate for
k\geq 2
: indeed, for generic
[u]\in\mathbb{P}^{n}
, a critical point of order
k
may not exist on
f(X)
. Hence, unlike the case
k=1
, the data locus
\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)
forms a proper algebraic subvariety of
\mathbb{P}^{n}
, reflecting the rarity and structural complexity of higher-order critical interactions.

To quantify its complexity, we consider the surjective morphism
\varphi_{2,k}\colon\operatorname{DC}_{k}(f,Q)\to\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)
induced by
{\rm pr}_{2}
and we define the
k
-th order distance degree
of
(f,Q)
as

\operatorname{DD}_{k}(f,Q)\coloneqq\deg\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)\cdot\deg\varphi_{2,k}\,,

as formalized in Definition
4.7
. In particular, when the pair
(f,Q)
is in
general
k
-osculating position
(see Definition
4.9
), the morphism
\varphi_{2,k}
is generically finite over its image and
\operatorname{DD}_{k}(f,Q)
attains its maximum value, called the
generic
k
th order distance degree of
f
and denoted by
\operatorname{gDD}_{k}(f)
(see Proposition
4.11
). In particular, this invariant is independent of
Q
.

Main Results.

The main result of Section
5
provides a closed formula for the
k
th order distance degree of a generic polynomial map. Its proof is given in Section
5.2
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}
be
n+1
generic polynomials in
\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}
and consider a nonnegative integer
k\leq d
. Assume that
n>\binom{m+k}{k}-1
and let
f\colon\mathbb{P}^{m}\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
be the associated globally
k
-osculating morphism. Then for any nonsingular quadric hypersurface
Q\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
such that
(f,Q)
is in general
k
-osculating position, the morphism
\varphi_{2,k}
is birational and

\operatorname{DD}_{k}(f,Q)=\deg\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{\binom{m+k}{k}}{i}(d-k)^{i}d^{m-i}\,.

This result relies on an interesting interplay between generic and special metric properties. We begin by exploring the connection between higher-order osculating optimization and a concrete, widely studied problem in data approximation: the so-called best (symmetric) rank-one tensor approximation
[
Ban38
,
EY36
,
dSL08
,
OSS14
]
.
In Definition
5.4
, we introduce
higher-order eigenvectors
of symmetric tensors, a generalization of the notions introduced in
[
Lim05
,
Qi05
]
. In Proposition
5.7
, we relate them to higher-order critical points of the nearest-point problem to the Veronese variety with respect to the Bombieri-Weyl quadratic form
Q_{\mathrm{BW}}
, see Definition
5.1
.
Furthermore, we compute the higher-order distance degrees of Veronese embeddings with respect to the Bombieri-Weyl quadratic form, and examine how the geometry of the higher-order distance loci
\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)
varies with the choice of metric. In Section
5.1
, we prove the following result.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\nu_{m}^{d}\colon\mathbb{P}^{m}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d})
be the degree-
d
Veronese embedding of
\mathbb{P}^{m}
, and equip
\mathbb{R}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}
with the Bombieri-Weyl inner product. For all
k\leq d
,

(1)

If
(m,k)=(1,d-1)
, then
\deg\varphi_{2,d-1}=2
and
\deg\operatorname{DL}_{d-1}(\nu_{1}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})=d-1
.

(2)

If
(m,k)\neq(1,d-1)
, then
\varphi_{2,k}
is birational and
\operatorname{DD}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})=\deg\operatorname{DL}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
equals the coefficient of the monomial
h_{1}^{m}h_{2}^{\binom{m+k}{k}-m-1}
in the expansion of

(1.1)

(-1)^{\binom{m+k}{k}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}(dh_{1}+h_{2}+k(d-k)h_{1}^{2}+kh_{1}h_{2})^{j}\in\frac{\mathbb{Z}[h_{1},h_{2}]}{\langle h_{1}^{m+1},h_{2}^{\binom{m+d}{d}}\rangle}\,.

Furthermore, for all
k\leq d
, if
(\nu_{m}^{d},Q)
is in general
k
-osculating position, then
\varphi_{2,k}
is birational and

(1.2)

\deg\operatorname{DL}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q)=\operatorname{gDD}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d})=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{\binom{m+k}{k}}{i}(d-k)^{i}d^{m-i}\,.

When
k=1
, Equation (
1.1
) specializes to the distance degree
\operatorname{DD}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
computed in
[
DHO+16
, Corollary 8.7]
, a consequence of
[
CS13
, Theorem 5.6]
, see also
[
OO13
, Theorem 3.4]
and
[
FO14
, Theorem 12]
for related formulas in a more general setting.

In Section
6
, we focus on the case of
k
-regular embeddings
, that is, embeddings
f\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
whose
k
th order osculating spaces attain the maximal possible dimension globally. For such morphisms, we derive explicit formulas that are particularly well-suited to computational applications. We demonstrate their use in the context of embeddings of varieties of dimension at most three, with special focus on toric embeddings.

Beyond the projective-geometric and intersection-theoretic frameworks, we consider a tropical approach, which is suited to both combinatorial and computational perspectives.
In Section
7
, we study the tropicalization of the higher-order conormal variety
\Trop W_{k}(f,Q)
associated with a morphism
f
. This viewpoint offers a powerful framework for analyzing the behavior of higher-order critical loci under degenerations and for computing higher-order polar degrees combinatorially. We show that higher-order polar degrees can be expressed in terms of stable intersections between
\Trop W_{k}(f,Q)
and Bergman fans
\operatorname{Berg}(M^{n-j+1}_{n+1}\times M^{j+m_{k}-m+2}_{n+1})
corresponding to uniform matroids, obtaining the following tropical formula, whose proof is given in Section
7.1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
f
be a monomial
k
-osculating embedding of generic
k
-osculating dimension
m_{k}
.
The generic
k
th order distance degree of
f
is the sum of the degrees

\operatorname{gDD}_{k}(f)=\sum_{j=0}^{m+n-m_{k}-1}\int\Trop W_{k}(f,Q)\cdot\operatorname{Berg}(M^{n-j+1}_{n+1}\times M^{j+m_{k}-m+2}_{n+1})\,.

When
f
is a toric embedding, we further make these results effective and give a combinatorial description of
\Trop W_{k}(f,Q)
in Proposition
7.7
.
We accompany our theoretical findings with an easy-to-use
Julia
-based software implementation, which enables the computation of higher-order distance degrees and polar degrees in this toric setting, similar to results from
[
HS18
]
.

In the following, we present an example that provides a clear illustration of how we can explicitly compute and visualize all our constructions, including osculating spaces, higher-order normal bundles, polar loci, and distance degrees. Additionally, it connects our intersection-theoretic framework with issues related to low-rank tensor approximation, which we revisit in Section
5
.

Illustrative Example

Consider the space
V=(\mathbb{C}^{2})^{*}=\mathbb{C}[x_{0},x_{1}]_{1}
of linear forms in
x=(x_{0},x_{1})
,
X=\mathbb{P}(V)=\mathbb{P}^{1}
, and the morphism
f\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\to\mathbb{P}(S^{3}V)\cong\mathbb{P}^{3}
defined by
f([\ell])\coloneqq[\ell^{3}]
for every
\ell\in V
. Its image
f(\mathbb{P}^{1})
is a nonsingular twisted cubic in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. In coordinates we write
\ell(x)=t_{0}x_{0}+t_{1}x_{1}
, hence
\ell^{3}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{3}\binom{3}{i}(t_{0}x_{0})^{3-i}(t_{1}x_{1})^{i}
and we define
f([t_{0}:t_{1}])\coloneqq[t_{0}^{3}:t_{0}^{2}t_{1}:t_{0}t_{1}^{2}:t_{1}^{3}]
.
In particular,
f
corresponds to the Veronese embedding
\nu_{1}^{3}\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{3}
, and its image consists of all cubic binary forms of rank at most one.
Working in the affine patch
\{t_{0}\neq 0\}
and using the local coordinate
t=\frac{t_{1}}{t_{0}}
, the map
f
reads as
t\mapsto(1,t,t^{2},t^{3})
. Given
p=[1:t]
, we consider the matrix

A_{p}^{(2)}(f)=\begin{pNiceMatrix}

whose rows correspond to all partial derivatives of order at most
2
of the components of
f
. On the one hand
\mathbb{T}_{p}^{2}(f)=\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{rowspan}A_{p}^{(2)}(f))
. One verifies that
\dim\operatorname{rowspan}A_{p}^{(2)}(f)=3
, hence
\dim\mathbb{T}_{p}^{2}(f)=2
. In Figure
1
, we display the tangent and second-order osculating spaces of
f
at some points.
On the other hand, the right kernel of
A_{p}^{(2)}(f)
is one-dimensional and is generated by the vector
\eta=(-t^{3},3\,t^{2},-3\,t,1)^{\mathsf{T}}
.

Consider a quadric hypersurface
Q\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{3}
and let
M_{Q}
be the positive-definite symmetric matrix defining the corresponding quadratic form in
S^{3}\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then the
2
nd order distance locus
\operatorname{DL}_{2}(f,Q)
is equal to the union of the normal spaces

\mathbb{N}_{p}^{2}(f,Q)=\langle\mathbb{T}_{p}^{2}(f)^{\perp},f(p)\rangle=\langle[M_{Q}\eta],f(p)\rangle

for every
p\in\mathbb{P}^{1}
, in particular it is a projective surface in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
. Applying Proposition
4.11
and Corollary
4.12
, we have
\operatorname{gDD}_{2}(f)=\mu_{2,0}(f)+\mu_{2,1}(f)=3+3=6
.

Firstly, let
Q_{\mathrm{ED}}
be the standard Euclidean quadric of equation
\sum_{i=0}^{3}u_{i}^{2}=0
, hence
M_{Q_{\mathrm{ED}}}
is the identity matrix. We verified in
Macaulay2

[
GS97
]
that
\operatorname{DL}_{2}(f,Q_{\mathrm{ED}})
is a surface of degree
6=\operatorname{gDD}_{2}(f)
(displayed in light blue on the left-hand side of Figure
2
) and that
\varphi_{2,2}
is a birational morphism.
If instead we consider
Q_{\mathrm{BW}}\colon\sum_{i=0}^{3}\binom{3}{i}u_{i}^{2}=0
, corresponding to the
Bombieri-Weyl
inner product in
S^{3}\mathbb{R}^{2}
, then two interesting properties hold: firstly
\operatorname{DD}_{2}(f,Q_{\mathrm{BW}})=4<6=\operatorname{gDD}_{2}(f)
, equivalently there is a “
2
nd-order ED defect” equal to
2
, a behaviour similar to the classical ED defects studied in
[
MRW20
]
. Secondly, the morphism
\varphi_{2,2}
is not birational, indeed
\operatorname{DL}_{2}(f,Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
is the nonsingular quadric surface of equation
u_{1}^{2}-u_{0}u_{2}+u_{2}^{2}-u_{1}u_{3}=0
(displayed in yellow on the right-hand side of Figure

), where
[u_{0}:u_{1}:u_{2}:u_{3}]
are homogeneous coordinates in
\mathbb{P}^{3}
, while
\deg\varphi_{2,2}=2
. The smaller value of
\operatorname{DD}_{2}(f,Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
follows because each
2
nd order normal line
\mathbb{N}_{p}^{2}(f,Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
meets the twisted cubic

at another point

such that

,

, and the vectors

are orthogonal, implying that
\mathbb{N}_{p}^{2}(f,Q_{\mathrm{BW}})=\mathbb{N}_{p^{\prime}}^{2}(f,Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
. We explain this graphically on the right-hand side of Figure

, in particular with the points

and

.

2.
Osculating functions

Notation.
Let
V
be an
(n+1)
-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{C}
, or more generally over
\mathbb{K},
an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero. We denote by
\mathbb{P}^{n}
the projective space
\mathbb{P}(V)
, while
(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}
denotes the dual projective space of hyperplanes in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Given a multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
, we define
|\alpha|\coloneqq\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{m}
, and for any vector
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
, we call
x^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{m}^{\alpha_{m}}
.

Let
X
be a smooth irreducible variety over
\mathbb{K}
and let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
be an algebraic morphism.
It is determined by the subset
V=f^{*}(H^{0}(\mathbb{P}^{n},\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1))\subseteq H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))
where
\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)\coloneqq f^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(1)).
In particular
f(p)=(\sigma_{0}(p),\ldots,\sigma_{n}(p))
for a given basis
(\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
of
V.

If
V=H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))
the morphism
f
is said to be linearly normal.
Let
p\in X
and
\sigma\in V
. Recall that any
\sigma\in V\subseteq H^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))
can be represented in a neighborhood of
p
as a polynomial in local coordinates
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
.
The
k
th jet
of
\sigma
at
p
is the
\binom{m+k}{k}
-tuple

j_{k,p}(\sigma)\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{|\alpha|!}\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}\sigma}{\partial x^{\alpha}}(p)\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\\
0\leq|\alpha|\leq k\end{subarray}}\in\mathcal{P}^{k}_{p}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))\coloneqq H^{0}\left(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)\otimes\frac{\mathcal{O}_{X,p}}{\mathfrak{m}_{p}^{k+1}}\right)\cong\mathbb{K}^{\binom{m+k}{k}}\,,

where
\mathfrak{m}_{p}
denotes the maximal ideal at
p
. The vector space
\mathcal{P}^{k}_{p}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))
is the fiber at
p
of a vector bundle
\mathcal{P}^{k}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))
, called the
k
th jet bundle
of the line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)
. Jet sheaves, also called sheaves of principal parts, are defined as follows. Consider the two projections
{\rm pr}_{i}\colon X\times X\to X
for
i=1,2.
The principal part sheaf of order
k
is the coherent sheaf given by:

\mathcal{P}^{k}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))\coloneqq({\rm pr}_{1})_{*}\left({\rm pr}_{2}^{*}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))\otimes\frac{\mathcal{O}_{X\times X}}{\mathcal{I}_{\Delta}^{k+1}}\right)\,,

where
\Delta
denotes the diagonal in
X\times X.
Since the variety
X
is smooth these sheaves are locally free of rank
\binom{m+k}{k}
, see
[
Gro67
, §16]
.

Gluing together the maps
j_{k,p}
yields a morphism of vector bundles on
X
:

(2.1)

j_{k}\colon V\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}\to\mathcal{P}^{k}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))\,.

Throughout the remainder of this paper, we adopt the shorthand
\mathcal{P}^{k}(f)\coloneqq\mathcal{P}^{k}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)).

Definition 2.1
.

For every point
p\in X
, the
k
th osculating space of
f
at
f(p)\in f(X)
is

\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)\coloneqq\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{im}j_{k,p})\,.

If
f
is a closed embedding, by convention we identify
X
with its image
f(X)
and write
p
instead of
f(p)
. Moreover, when
f
is a closed embedding, the first osculating space of
X
at the point
p
coincides with the projective tangent space of
f
at
p
:

\mathbb{T}_{p}^{1}(f)=\mathbb{T}_{p}(f)\,.

Furthermore, osculating spaces form an ascending sequence of inclusions:

(2.2)

\{p\}\subseteq\mathbb{T}_{p}(f)=\mathbb{T}_{p}^{1}(f)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}\,.

For a generic
p\in X
, the rank of
j_{k,p}
is constant. This motivates the following definition.

Definition 2.2
.

Given
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
, let
X_{k}^{\circ}\subseteq X
be the dense open subset of
X
of points
p
such that
\operatorname{rank}j_{k,p}
is constant. We define the
generic
k
-osculating dimension
of
f
as
m_{k}\coloneqq\operatorname{rank}j_{k,p}-1
for any
p\in X_{k}^{\circ}
.
We say that the morphism
f
is
globally
k
-osculating
if
X_{k}^{\circ}=X
. In this case,
m_{k}
is referred to as the
k
-osculating dimension
of
f
.

Remark 2.3
.

The generic
1
-osculating dimension of a morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
is
m_{1}=m
. Indeed, the open dense subset
X_{1}^{\circ}\subseteq X
coincides with the nonsingular locus of
X
, and
\mathbb{T}_{p}^{1}(f)=\mathbb{T}_{p}(f)=\mathbb{P}^{m}
for every
p\in X_{1}^{\circ}
. Furthermore, the morphism
f
is globally
1
-osculating if and only if it is a closed embedding.

Let
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
be a system of local coordinates around the point
p=(0,\ldots,0)
. After choosing a basis
(\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
of
V
one can define the
\binom{m+k}{k}\times(n+1)
matrix

(2.3)

A^{(k)}_{p}(f)\coloneqq\left(\frac{1}{|\alpha|!}\frac{\partial^{|\alpha|}\sigma_{\beta}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}(p)\right)_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m}\\
\ 0\leq|\alpha|\leq k\\
0\leq\beta\leq n\end{subarray}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c}j_{k,p}(\sigma_{0})^{\mathsf{T}}&j_{k,p}(\sigma_{1})^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&j_{k,p}(\sigma_{n})^{\mathsf{T}}\end{array}\right)

obtained by concatenating horizontally the
n+1
column vectors
j_{k,p}(\sigma_{\beta})^{\mathsf{T}}
of length
\binom{m+k}{k}
for every
\beta\in\{0,\ldots,k\}
.
A basis of
\operatorname{im}j_{k,p}
is given by
m_{k}+1
linearly independent vectors in the column span of
A^{(k)}_{p}(f)
.
Notice that
X_{k}^{\circ}=\{p\in X\mid\operatorname{rank}A_{p}^{(k)}(f)=m_{k}+1\}
and that this identity is independent of the choice of a basis of
V.

Definition 2.4
.

The morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
is
k
-regular
if
X_{k}^{\circ}=X
and if the vector bundle morphism
j_{k}
in (
2.1
) is surjective, equivalently
m_{k}+1=\binom{m+k}{k}
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a globally
k
-osculating morphism for some
k\geq 1
. Then it is
\ell
-osculating for all
\ell\leq k.

Similarly, if
f
is
k
-regular, then it is
\ell
-regular for all
\ell\leq k
.

Proof.

Assume that
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a globally
k
-osculating morphism for some
k\geq 1
, and let
\ell\leq k
. Notice that

A_{p}^{(k)}(f)=\begin{pmatrix}A_{p}^{(\ell)}(f)\\
B_{p}(f)\end{pmatrix}

where
B_{p}(f)
is an
\left(\binom{m+k}{k}-\binom{m+\ell}{\ell}\right)\times(n+1)
matrix for every
p\in X
. Assuming that there exists a special point
p
where
A_{p}^{(\ell)}
does not have the maximal generic rank while
A_{p}^{(k)}
has the maximal generic rank is impossible.
Moreover, if
A_{p}^{(k)}(f)
has maximal rank, then
A_{p}^{(\ell)}(f)
will also have maximal rank.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

A globally
1
-osculating morphism is always
1
-regular. For
k\geq 2
, this is no longer the case. See Example
2.9
below.

2.1.
A gallery of toric examples

For varieties endowed with a maximal dimensional torus action, i.e., toric varieties, the torus action leads to remarkably simple criteria dictated by the size of the associated polytope.

Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a nonsingular, globally
k
-osculating, torus-equivariant embedding of a toric variety
X
of dimension
m
.
Then
f
is a monomial map which over the algebraic torus is given by
x\mapsto(x^{a_{0}},\ldots,x^{a_{n}})
for a subset
A=\{a_{0},\ldots,a_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}^{m}
.
Recall that
P(f)=\operatorname{conv}(A)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a full-dimensional polytope whose vertices are in one-to-one correspondence with the fixed points by the torus action. In particular the variety
X
has an affine cover
X=\bigcup_{v\in\operatorname{vert}P(f)}U_{v}
where
U_{v}\subseteq\mathbb{C}^{m}
is a Zariski open subset containing
p_{v}
for every
v\in\operatorname{vert}P(f)
. Since
X
is nonsingular, for every vertex
v
the first lattice points along the edges containing
v
form a lattice basis
\mathcal{B}_{v}
for which
(v)_{\mathcal{B}_{v}}=0
. Moreover there is an associated affine patch
U_{v}\ni p_{v}
and local coordinates
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})
for which
p_{v}=0
and

f|_{U_{v}}\colon x\mapsto(x^{(a_{0})_{\mathcal{B}_{v}}},\cdots,x^{(a_{n})_{\mathcal{B}_{v}}})

Let
p={\boldsymbol{1}}
be the generic point in the torus.
For every
k\geq 1
, we denote by
A^{(k)}
the matrix
A_{{\boldsymbol{1}}}^{(k)}(f)
defined in (
2.3
). We note that the construction of these matrices is also explained in
[
DDRP14
, Section 5.2]
.

The augmented matrix
A^{(k)}
determines the generic
k
-osculating rank. More generally, for every fixed point
p_{v}\in X
, we denote by
A_{v}^{(k)}
the matrix
A_{p_{v}}^{(k)}(f)
. The following result characterizes globally
k
-osculating toric embeddings.

Proposition 2.7
.

A nonsingular toric embedding
f
is globally
k
-osculating of osculating dimension
m_{k}
if and only if
\dim\mathbb{T}_{p_{v}}^{k}(f)=\operatorname{rank}A_{v}^{(k)}-1=\operatorname{rank}A^{(k)}-1
for every fixed point
p_{v}
.

Proof.

From the description above, it is clear that
m_{k}+1=\operatorname{rank}A^{(k)}.
Note that if the locus of points where the
k
th osculating space has a lower dimension than
m_{k}
is nonempty, the torus action ensures that it contains fixed points. It follows that to ensure global
k
th osculation is enough that the
k
th osculation dimension at each fixed point is
m_{k}.

∎

A special class of toric varieties that are always globally
k
-osculating consists of the Segre-Veronese embeddings of a product of projective spaces. Consider two
r

{\boldsymbol{m}}=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{r})
and
{\boldsymbol{d}}=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{r})
, with
m_{1}\leq\cdots\leq m_{r}
. Define
d\coloneqq d_{1}+\cdots+d_{r}
. Let
V_{1},\ldots,V_{r}
be vector spaces of dimensions
m_{1}+1\leq\cdots\leq m_{r}+1

\mathbb{P}^{\boldsymbol{m}}=\prod_{i=1}^{r}\mathbb{P}(V_{i})

\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{\boldsymbol{m}}}({\boldsymbol{d}})
induces an embedding

(2.4)

\sigma\nu_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\boldsymbol{d}}\colon\mathbb{P}^{\boldsymbol{n}}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{r}\mathrm{Sym}^{d_{i}}V_{i}\right)\,,

whose image
SV_{\boldsymbol{n}}^{\boldsymbol{d}}=\sigma\nu_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\boldsymbol{d}}(\mathbb{P}^{\boldsymbol{n}})
is the
Segre-Veronese variety
.
When
r=1
, we use the notation
{\boldsymbol{m}}=m
,
{\boldsymbol{d}}=d
,
\nu_{m}^{d}\coloneqq\sigma\nu_{m}^{d}
, and
V_{m}^{d}\coloneqq SV_{m}^{d}
is the
Veronese variety
.

[

, Proposition 2.5]
.

{\boldsymbol{n}}
and
{\boldsymbol{d}}
, and for all integer

, the Segre-Veronese embedding
\sigma\nu_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{\boldsymbol{d}}
is globally
k
-osculating of
k
-osculating dimension

(2.5)

m_{k}=\begin{cases}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{|{\boldsymbol{s}}|=i\,,\,{\boldsymbol{s}}\leq{\boldsymbol{d}}}\binom{n_{1}+s_{1}-1}{s_{1}}\cdots\binom{n_{r}+s_{r}-1}{s_{r}}&\text{if $1\leq k\leq|{\boldsymbol{d}}|$}\\
\prod_{\ell=1}^{r}\binom{n_{\ell}+d_{\ell}}{d_{\ell}}-1&\text{if $k>|{\boldsymbol{d}}|$.}\end{cases}

[

.

Let

and

) is

.

3.
Higher-order polar classes

The definition of polar classes is classical in Algebraic Geometry, going back to Severi
[
Sev02
]
and Todd
[
Tod37
]
.
A generalization to higher-order polar classes has been recently given by Piene in
[
Pie22
]
. We follow her definitions and results, adapting them to our setting.

With notation as above, assume that the morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
is globally
k
-osculating, and thus a closed embedding. Then the image
\operatorname{im}j_{k}
and the kernel
\ker j_{k}
of the morphism
j_{k}
in (
2.1
) are also vector bundles over
X
of ranks
m_{k}+1
and
n-m_{k}
respectively.
We then obtain the following short exact sequence of vector bundles over
X
:

(3.1)

0\to\ker j_{k}\hookrightarrow V\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{im}j_{k}\to 0\,.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a globally
k
-osculating morphism and let
0\leq i\leq\dim X=m
be an integer. The
i
th polar class of
f
of order
k
is the Chern class:

(3.2)

p_{k,i}(f)=c_{i}(\operatorname{im}j_{k})\,.

The
i
th polar degree of
f
of order
k
is
\mu_{k,i}(f)\coloneqq\deg p_{k,i}(f)
.

In
[
Pie22
, Section 3]
, Piene defines the higher-order polar loci

(3.3)

P_{k,i}(f,L)\coloneqq\overline{\{x\in X_{k}^{\circ}\mid\dim(\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)\cap L)>m_{k}-\operatorname{codim}(L)\}}
for all
i\in\{0,\ldots,\dim X=m\}
,

where
L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
is a linear subspace of dimension
n-m_{k}+i-2
, and proves the identity (
3.2
) in
[
Pie22
, Theorem 3.1]
, without any smoothness assumption on
X
nor the assumption of global osculation for
f
. In our approach, we consider globally
k
-osculating morphisms
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
and adopt the definition given by (
3.2
) directly. For completeness, in Proposition
3.7
we include a self-contained proof of the equivalence between
p_{k,i}(f)
and the classes of the loci
P_{k,i}(f,L)
for a generic
L
, using slightly different methods under the assumption of global
k
-osculation.
Higher order polar loci are closely related to higher order conormal and dual varieties, which have been introduced in the earlier work by Piene
[
Pie83
]
.

Definition 3.2
.

[
Pie83
, §2]

Let
p\in X_{k}^{\circ}
and
H\in(\mathbb{P}^{N})^{\vee}.
Then
H
is
k
-osculating at
f(p)
if
H\supset\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f).
The
k
th
conormal variety
of
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
is the Zariski closure
W_{k}(f)\coloneqq\overline{W_{k}^{\circ}(f)}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee},
where:

(3.4)

W_{k}^{\circ}(f)\coloneqq\{(x,H)\in\mathbb{P}^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}\mid\text{$x=f(p)$ for some $p\in X_{k}^{\circ}$ and $H$ is $k$-osculating at $x$}\}\,.

Remark 3.3
.

Since
\dim\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)=m_{k}
for every
p\in X_{k}^{\circ}
, we have

(3.5)

\dim W_{k}(f)=n-1+m-m_{k}\,.

Let the morphism
f
be globally
k
-osculating. Then
f
is a closed embedding and, using (
2.1
),

W_{k}^{\circ}(f)=W_{k}(f)=\mathbb{P}((\ker j_{k})^{\vee})\subseteq X\times(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}\,.

In particular, if
k=1
, then
W(f)=W_{1}(f)
is the classical conormal variety of dimension
n-1
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}
.

Let
{\rm pr}_{1}
and
{\rm pr}_{2}
be the projections of
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}
to the factors
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and
(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}
, respectively.

Definition 3.4
.

[
Pie83
, §2]

The
dual variety of order
k
of
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{N}
is
X_{k}^{\vee}\coloneqq{\rm pr}_{2}(W_{k}(f))
.

Consider the surjective morphisms
\pi_{1,k}\colon W_{k}(f)\to X
and
\pi_{2,k}\colon W_{k}(f)\to X_{k}^{\vee}
induced by the projections
{\rm pr}_{1}
and
{\rm pr}_{2}
. More precisely
\pi_{i,k}={\rm pr}_{i}\circ\iota_{k}
for
i=1,2
, where
\iota_{k}\colon W_{k}(f)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}
is the inclusion.
Given
H\in X_{k}^{\vee}
, the
contact locus of
H
of order
k
is

(3.6)

\operatorname{Cont}_{k}(H,X)\coloneqq\pi_{1,k}(\pi_{2,k}^{-1}(H))=\overline{\{p\in X_{k}^{\circ}\mid H\supset\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)\}}\,.

For a generic
H\in X_{k}^{\vee}
, the dimension of
\operatorname{Cont}_{k}(H,X)

is equal to the dimension of a generic fiber of
\pi_{2,k}
. From (
3.5
) it follows that

\dim X_{k}^{\vee}=n-(m_{k}-m+1)-\dim\operatorname{Cont}_{k}(H,X)
for a generic hyperplane
H\in X_{k}^{\vee}
.

Generally, one expects that
\dim X_{k}^{\vee}=n-(m_{k}-m+1)
, or equivalently that the generic contact locus is zero-dimensional.

Definition 3.5
.

The
dual defect of order
k
of the morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
is defined as

(3.7)

\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)\coloneqq\dim\operatorname{Cont}_{k}(H,X)
for a generic
H\in(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}
.

We say that
X
is
dual defective of order
k
if
\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)>0
. Otherwise,
X
is
dual nondefective of order
k
.

Example 3.6
.

Consider the embedding
f_{1}\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{6}

W(f_{1})

\dim W_{2}(f_{1})=6-1+2-5=2

\dim X_{2}^{\vee}=2
and
\deg X_{2}^{\vee}=6

\operatorname{def}_{2}(f_{1})=0.

\diamondsuit

When the morphism

is governed by nonvanishing higher-order polar classes.
The following results are proven in

. We give a self-contained argument in the simpler case of globally osculating morphisms on smooth varieties.

Proposition 3.7
.

[
Pie22
, Theorem 3.5]

Let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a globally
k
-osculating morphism. The following holds.

(1)

p_{k,i}(f)=[P_{k,i}(f,L)]
for a generic subspace
L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
of dimension
n-m_{k}+i-2
.

(2)

p_{k,i}(f)=0
for
i>m-\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)
.

(3)

p_{k,m-\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)}(f)\neq 0
and
\deg X_{k}^{\vee}=\mu_{k,m-\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)}(f)
.

Proof.

Recall that
\operatorname{im}j_{k}
is globally generated by the sections of
\mathcal{O}_{X}^{\oplus(n+1)}=X\times V
via the vector bundle map
j_{k}
, and
\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{im}j_{k}=m_{k}+1
. Using
[
GH78
, §III.3, pp. 411–414]
, for every integer
i\geq 0
, the
i
th Chern class
c_{i}(\operatorname{im}j_{k})
is the rational class
[D(j_{k}(\sigma_{1}),\ldots,j_{k}(\sigma_{m_{k}-i+2}))]
of the degeneracy locus

D(j_{k}(\sigma_{1}),\ldots,j_{k}(\sigma_{m_{k}-i+2}))\coloneqq\{p\in X\mid j_{k}(\sigma_{1})(p)\wedge\cdots\wedge j_{k}(\sigma_{m_{k}-i+2})(p)=0\}

for the generic global sections
j_{k}(\sigma_{1}),\ldots,j_{k}(\sigma_{m_{k}-i+2})\in H^{0}(X,\operatorname{im}j_{k}).

This is equivalent to requiring that
\dim(\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)\cap L)>i-2=m_{k}-\operatorname{codim}(L)
for a generic linear space
L
of codimension
m_{k}-i+2
.

Consider the
k
th dual variety
X_{k}^{\vee}
introduced in Definition
3.4
, in particular

X_{k}^{\vee}=\overline{\{H\in(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}\mid\text{$\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f)\subseteq H$ for some $p\in X_{k}^{\circ}$}\}}\,.

Note that a generic linear subspace
L
of dimension
\operatorname{codim}(X_{k}^{\vee})-1=m_{k}-m+\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)
does not meet
X_{k}^{\vee}
and hence
j_{k}(\sigma)\neq 0
for a generic linear combination
\sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{m_{k}-m+\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)+1}\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}.

From what proven above it follows that
p_{k,m-\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)+1}(f)=c_{m-\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)+1}(\operatorname{im}j_{k})=0.
Similarly one sees that
p_{k,m-\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)}(f)=c_{m-\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)}(\operatorname{im}j_{k})\neq 0
and that
\mu_{k,m-\operatorname{def}_{k}(f)}(f)=\deg X_{k}^{\vee}
.
∎

Let
H
and
H^{\prime}
be hyperplanes in
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and
(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}
, respectively, and let
[H]
and
[H^{\prime}]
be the corresponding generating classes in the Chow rings
A^{*}(\mathbb{P}^{n})
and
A^{*}((\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee})
. We use the shorthand

(3.8)

h={\rm pr}_{1}^{*}([H])=[H\times(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}]\,,\quad h^{\prime}={\rm pr}_{2}^{*}([H^{\prime}])=[\mathbb{P}^{n}\times H^{\prime}]\,.

Recalling (
3.5
), the class
[W_{k}(f)]\in A^{*}(\mathbb{P}^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee})
can be written as

(3.9)

[W_{k}(f)]=\sum_{i=0}^{n-m_{k}+m-1}\delta_{k,i}(f)h^{n-i}(h^{\prime})^{m_{k}-m+1+i}\,.

The numbers
\delta_{k,i}(f)
are the multidegrees of
W_{k}(f)
.
Given
0\leq i\leq n-m_{k}+m-1
, let
L_{1}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
and
L_{2}\subset(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}
be generic subspaces of dimensions
n-i
and
m_{k}-m+1+i
, respectively. In particular
[L_{1}]=[H]^{i}
and
[L_{2}]=[H^{\prime}]^{n-m_{k}+m-1-i}
. Then
\delta_{k,i}(f)
is the cardinality of the finite intersection
W_{k}(f)\cap(L_{1}\times L_{2})
, or equivalently

(3.10)

\delta_{k,i}(f)=\int{\rm pr}_{1}^{*}([L_{1}])\cdot{\rm pr}_{2}^{*}([L_{2}])\cdot[W_{k}(f)]=\int h^{i}(h^{\prime})^{n-m_{k}+m-1-i}[W_{k}(f)]\,.

We can now prove a higher-order version of the celebrated formula by Kleiman in
[
Kle86
, Prop. (3), p. 187]
. In particular, we show a correspondence between the
k
th order polar degrees
\mu_{k,j}(f)
and the multidegrees
\delta_{k,j}(f)
of the rational equivalence class
[W_{k}(f)]
.

Proposition 3.8
.

Consider a morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n},
that is globally
k
-osculating of
k
-osculating dimension
m_{k}<n
. For all
0\leq j\leq\dim X=m
, we have
\mu_{k,j}(f)=\delta_{k,m-j}(f)
.

Proof.

Since the morphism
f
is
k
-osculating of
k
-osculating dimension
m_{k}
, in particular
X
is nonsingular and the variety
W_{k}(f)
has the structure of a projective bundle over
X
, hence it is also a nonsingular variety.
Consider the surjective morphisms
\pi_{k,1}\colon W_{k}(f)\to X
,
\pi_{k,2}\colon W_{k}(f)\to X_{k}^{\vee}
. Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a generic projective subspace of dimension
n-m_{k}+j-2
. Then
L_{k}^{\vee}=L^{\vee}
and
\dim L_{k}^{\vee}=n-1-\dim L=m_{k}-j+1
. In particular
[L_{k}^{\vee}]=[H^{\prime}]^{n-1-m_{k}+j}
, using the notation in (
3.8
).
Following
[
Pie22
, Proposition 3.4]
a similar approach in
[
DRGS25
, Lemma 3.24]
, one can show that

(3.11)

P_{k,j}(f)=\pi_{k,1}(\pi_{k,2}^{-1}(L_{k}^{\vee}))\,.

Let
\iota_{k}\colon W_{k}(f)\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}\times(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}
be the inclusion. Then

\displaystyle\mu_{k,j}(f)

\displaystyle=\deg P_{k,j}(f)=\int[H]^{m-j}\cdot[P_{k,j}(f)]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{(*)}}}{{=}}\int[H]^{m-j}\cdot[\pi_{1,k}(\pi_{2,k}^{-1}(L_{k}^{\vee}))]=\int[H]^{m-j}\cdot\pi_{1,k*}(\pi_{2,k}^{*}([L_{k}^{\vee}]))

\displaystyle=\int[H]^{m-j}\cdot\pi_{1,k*}(\pi_{2,k}^{*}([H^{\prime}]^{n-1-m_{k}+j}))\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{(**)}}}{{=}}\int\pi_{1,k*}(\pi_{1,k}^{*}([H]^{m-j})\cdot\pi_{2,k}^{*}([H^{\prime}]^{n-1-m_{k}+j}))

\displaystyle=\int\pi_{1,k*}(\pi_{1,k}^{*}([H])^{m-j}\cdot\pi_{2,k}^{*}([H^{\prime}])^{n-1-m_{k}+j})=\int\pi_{1,k*}(\iota_{k}^{*}(h^{m-j})\cdot\iota_{k}^{*}((h^{\prime})^{n-1-m_{k}+j}))

\displaystyle=\int\pi_{1,k*}(\iota_{k}^{*}(h^{m-j}\cdot(h^{\prime})^{n-1-m_{k}+j}))=\int\pi_{1,k*}(h^{m-j}\cdot(h^{\prime})^{n-1-m_{k}+j}\cdot[W_{k}(f)])

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathclap{(***)}}}{{=}}\int h^{m-j}\cdot(h^{\prime})^{n-1-m_{k}+j}\cdot[W_{k}(f)]=\delta_{k,m-j}(f)\,.

In
(*)
, we applied (
3.11
). In
(**)
, we applied the general projection formula in
[
Ful98
, Theorem 3.2]
. In
(***)
, we used the fact that
h^{m-j}(h^{\prime})^{n-1-m_{k}+j}[W_{k}(f)]
is a zero-dimensional cycle, so the degree is preserved under projection.
∎

4.
Higher-order distance loci

In this section, we introduce a
higher-order distance degree
,
[
DHO+16
]
. Furthermore, we define the
higher-order distance loci
. These concepts and their geometry strongly depend on the choice of an underlying metric. Our ambient space is an
(n+1)
-dimensional vector space
V
, and
x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}
are homogeneous coordinates in
\mathbb{P}(V)\cong\mathbb{P}^{n}
. To introduce a notion of “normality”, we fix a nonsingular quadric hypersurface
Q=\mathbb{V}(q)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
, where
q\in\mathrm{Sym}^{2}V^{*}
. The hypersurface
Q
is the
isotropic quadric
associated with
q
. A standard choice is to consider
q(x)=q_{\mathrm{ED}}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}x_{i}^{2},
but there is no need to restrict to the standard choice.
The quadric
Q
induces the
polarity
or
reciprocity
map

(4.1)

\begin{matrix}\partial_{q}\colon&\mathbb{P}^{n}&\longrightarrow&(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}\\
&p=[p_{0}:\cdots:p_{n}]&\longmapsto&\left[\frac{\partial q}{\partial x_{0}}(p):\cdots:\frac{\partial q}{\partial x_{n}}(p)\right]\,.\end{matrix}

Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
be a subspace. We define

(4.2)

L^{\perp}\coloneqq\partial_{q}(L)^{\vee}=\left\{y\in\mathbb{P}^{n}\ \bigg|\ \text{$\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{\partial q}{\partial x_{i}}(p)y_{i}=0$ for every $p\in L$}\right\}\,.

Observe that
\mathbb{P}(L)^{\perp}=\mathbb{P}(L^{\perp})
considering the product
\langle u,v\rangle_{Q}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{\partial q}{\partial x_{i}}(u)v_{i}=0.

Observe also that if
\dim L=r
, then
\dim L^{\perp}=n-r-1
. In particular, if
p=[p_{0}:\cdots:p_{n}]\in\mathbb{P}^{n}
, then
p^{\perp}
is the polar hyperplane
H_{p}\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
of equation
\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{\partial q}{\partial x_{i}}(p)x_{i}=0
. Furthermore
(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\perp}=\emptyset
.

Definition 4.1
.

[
Pie22
]

Consider a morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
, a nonsingular quadric hypersurface
Q\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
, and let

p\in X_{k}^{\circ}.
The
k
th order normal space of
(f,Q)
at
p
is

\mathbb{N}_{p}^{k}(f,Q)\coloneqq\langle(\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f))^{\perp},f(p)\rangle\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}\,.

When
k=1
, we call it the
normal space of
(f,Q)
at
p
and we denote it by
\mathbb{N}_{p}(f,Q)
.

In particular, if
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
is globally
1
-osculating, then the normal space of
(f,Q)
at
p
is
\mathbb{N}_{p}(f,Q)=\langle\mathbb{T}_{p}(f)^{\perp},p\rangle
.

Remark 4.2
.

Given a point
p\in\mathbb{P}^{n}\setminus Q
and the inclusion
\iota\colon\{p\}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
, the normal space of
(\iota,Q)
is
\mathbb{N}_{p}(\iota,Q)=\langle H_{p},p\rangle=\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Furthermore, when
f=\mathrm{id}\colon\mathbb{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
, the normal space of
(\mathrm{id},Q)
at

is
\mathbb{N}_{p}(\mathrm{id},Q)=\langle(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\perp},p\rangle=\{p\}.
The analog of the sequence (
2.2
) for higher-order normal spaces is

\{p\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{p}^{k}(f,Q)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{p}^{2}(f,Q)\subseteq\mathbb{N}_{p}(f,Q)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}\,.

Remark 4.3
.

Observe that, as
p
varies in
X
, the dimension of
\mathbb{N}_{p}^{k}(f,Q)
might change.
Even if we assume that the map
f
is
k
-osculating, the dimension of the span
\langle(\mathbb{T}_{p}^{k}(f))^{\perp},p\rangle
drops if

which happens if

intersects

nontransversally.

Definition 4.4
.

[
Pie22
, p. 245]

Consider a morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
and a nonsingular quadric hypersurface
Q\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
. The
k
th order distance correspondence of

is the incidence correspondence

\operatorname{DC}_{k}(f,Q)\coloneqq\overline{\left\{([v],[u])\in\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}\mid\text{$[v]=f(p)$ for some $p\in X_{k}^{\circ}$ and $[\nabla d_{u}(v)]\in\mathbb{N}_{p}^{k}(f,Q)$}\right\}}\,.

The following lemma is proven similarly to the first part of
[
DHO+16
, Theorem 4.1]
.

Lemma 4.5
.

Consider a morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
and a nonsingular quadric hypersurface
Q\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Assume that
X
is irreducible of dimension
m
and that
f(X)\not\subseteq Q
. Then
\operatorname{DC}_{k}(f,Q)
is irreducible of dimension
m+n-m_{k}
in
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
. The projection of
\operatorname{DC}_{k}(f,Q)
onto the first factor
\mathbb{P}^{n}
is locally trivial over
f(X_{k}^{\circ})\setminus Q
with fibers of dimension

.

Definition 4.6
.

Consider a morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
and a nonsingular quadric hypersurface
Q\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
. The
k
th order distance locus of
(f,Q)
is

(4.3)

\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)\coloneqq{\rm pr}_{2}(\operatorname{DC}_{k}(f,Q))=\overline{\bigcup_{p\in X_{k}^{\circ}}\mathbb{N}_{p}^{k}(f,Q)}\,,

where
{\rm pr}_{2}
is the projection of
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
onto the second factor. The
distance loci sequence
is:

\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)\subseteq\operatorname{DL}_{k-1}(f,Q)\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\operatorname{DL}_{1}(f,Q)=\mathbb{P}^{n}\,.

In the following, we denote by
\varphi_{1,k}\colon\operatorname{DC}_{k}(f,Q)\to f(X)
and
\varphi_{2,k}\colon\operatorname{DC}_{k}(f,Q)\to\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)
the surjective morphisms induced by the projections
{\rm pr}_{1}
and
{\rm pr}_{2}
of
\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
onto its factors.

Definition 4.7
.

Consider a morphism
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
and a nonsingular quadric hypersurface
Q\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}
. Assume that the morphism
\varphi_{2,k}
is generically finite. The
k
th order distance degree of
(f,Q)
is

\operatorname{DD}_{k}(f,Q)\coloneqq\deg\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)\cdot\deg\varphi_{2,k}\,,

where
\deg\varphi_{2,k}=\deg\varphi_{2,k}^{-1}(u)
for a generic

.

For
k=1
we use the shorthand
\operatorname{DC}_{1}(f,Q)=\operatorname{DC}(f,Q)
, while
\varphi_{1,1}=\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2,1}=\varphi_{2}
. In this case
\dim\operatorname{DC}(f,Q)=n
and
\operatorname{DL}_{1}(f,Q)=\mathbb{P}^{n}
.
Hence, the classical ED degree of
(f,Q)
introduced in
[
DHO+16
]
is
\operatorname{DD}_{1}(f,Q)=\deg\varphi_{2}
. This is not generally the case for
k\geq 2
, see the running example in the introduction.

We always have the chain of inclusions

In fact, there always exists an integer
k\geq 1
such that
f(X)=\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q).
The following lemma examines the two extreme cases: when

and when
\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)=\mathbb{P}^{n}.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let
f
be a globally
k
-osculating morphism and
Q
a generic quadric. Then

(1)

There always exists a
k
for which
f(X)=\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)
. Moreover
(X,f)=(\mathbb{P}^{m},\nu_{m}^{k})
is the
k
th Veronese embedding if and only if
f(X)=\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)
and
m_{k}=\binom{m+k}{k}-1.

(2)

If
\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)=\mathbb{P}^{n}
for some
k\geq 1
then
\operatorname{DL}_{\ell}(f,Q)=\mathbb{P}^{n}
for every
\ell\in[k].

Proof.

(1) Given a closed embedding
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}
we have
m_{k}=n
for a large enough

It follows that
\operatorname{rank}\operatorname{im}j_{k}=n+1
and thus
j_{k}
is an isomorphism of vector bundles, imposing
\operatorname{im}j_{k}\cong\mathcal{O}_{X}^{\oplus(n+1)}.
This implies that the morphism
\varphi_{2,k}
is birational, hence
\operatorname{DC}_{k}(f,Q)\cong f(X)
and
\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)=f(X).

If
(X,f)=(\mathbb{P}^{m},\nu_{m}^{k})
is the Veronese embedding, then

is
k
-regular, namely
\operatorname{im}j_{k}=\mathcal{P}^{k}(f)\cong\mathcal{O}_{X}^{\oplus(m_{k}+1)}
with

, and the map

is an isomorphism. This implies, as above, that

is birational and that its image is

. Viceversa if

and

and thus the projectivized jet bundle is a product. Applying

(2) Assume now that

the morphism
\varphi_{2,k}=\cdots=\varphi_{2,1}

∎

Proposition 4.11

Proof.

Consider a morphism

is the
i
th Segre class of
\mathcal{E}
, see
[
Ful98
]
. In the following, we denote by
s(\mathcal{E})
the total Segre class of
\mathcal{E}
. These facts and equation (
4.4
) yield the relation
c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}_{k}(f,Q)^{\vee}}(1))^{m+n-m_{k}}=s_{m}(\mathcal{E}_{k}(f,Q)^{\vee})
. It follows that

s(\mathcal{E}^{k}(f,Q)^{\vee})=s((\ker j_{k})\oplus\mathcal{O}_{X}(-1))=s(\ker j_{k})s(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-1))=c(\ker j_{k})^{-1}c(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-1))^{-1}\,.

From the
k
-jet exact sequence (
4.5
) we derive the identity
c(\ker j_{k})^{-1}=c(\operatorname{im}j_{k})
, hence

(4.8)

c(\ker j_{k})^{-1}c(\mathcal{O}_{X}(-1))^{-1}=c(\operatorname{im}j_{k})\sum_{i=0}^{m}c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))^{i}

which implies that

(4.9)

c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}_{k}(f,Q)^{\vee}}(1))^{m+n-m_{k}}=s_{m}(\mathcal{E}_{k}(f,Q)^{\vee})=\sum_{i=0}^{m}c_{i}(\operatorname{im}j_{k})\cdot c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))^{m-i}\,.

The hypothesis of global
k
-osculation implies that
\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)
is very ample. Hence, the top self intersection
c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))^{m}
is always positive. For the same reason, the other terms
c_{i}(\operatorname{im}j_{k})\cdot c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))^{m-i}=0
are equal to zero if
c_{i}(\operatorname{im}j_{k})=0
, otherwise they are positive. Therefore, the top self intersection
c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}_{k}(f,Q)^{\vee}}(1))^{m+n-m_{k}}
of the tautological line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}_{k}(f,Q)^{\vee}}(1)
is always positive. For this reason, we conclude that the morphism
\varphi_{2,k}
is generically finite over its image and

(4.10)

c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathcal{E}_{k}(f,Q)^{\vee}}(1))^{m+n-m_{k}}=\deg\operatorname{im}\varphi_{2,k}\cdot\deg\varphi_{2,k}^{-1}(u)

for a generic
u\in\operatorname{im}\varphi_{2,k}=\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)
. The right-hand side of (
4.10
) is equal to
\operatorname{DD}_{k}(f,Q)
by Definition
4.7
.
The formula in (
4.7
) follows from (
4.8
) and (
4.9
).
∎

We conclude this section with the first application of Proposition
4.11
to Veronese embeddings. The coming result generalizes the formula in
[
DHO+16
, Proposition 7.10]
for
k=1
.

Corollary 4.12
.

Consider the Veronese embedding
\nu_{m}^{d}\colon\mathbb{P}^{m}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\binom{m+d}{d}-1}
. Then for every
k\in[d]

(4.11)

\operatorname{gDD}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d})=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{\binom{m+k}{k}}{i}(d-k)^{i}d^{m-i}\,.

Proof.

The generic
k
th order distance degree of
\nu_{m}^{d}
is, by Proposition
4.11
, equal to

(4.12)

\operatorname{gDD}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d})=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\mu_{k,i}(\nu_{m}^{d})=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\int_{X}c_{i}(\operatorname{im}j_{k})\,.

Since
\nu_{m}^{d}
is
k
-regular for all
k\in[d]
, we have
\operatorname{im}j_{k}=\mathcal{P}^{k}(\nu_{m}^{d})=\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{m}}(d-k)^{\oplus\binom{m+k}{k}}
. Hence, letting
h=c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{m}}(1))
, we have
c(\mathcal{P}^{k}(\nu_{m}^{d}))=(1+(d-k)h)^{\binom{m+k}{k}}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\binom{\binom{m+k}{k}}{i}(d-k)^{i}h^{i}
, in particular

\int_{X}c_{i}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(\nu_{m}^{d}))=\int_{X}\binom{\binom{m+k}{k}}{i}(d-k)^{i}h^{i}\cdot(dh)^{m-i}=\binom{\binom{m+k}{k}}{i}(d-k)^{i}d^{m-i}\,,

giving the desired identity.
∎

5.
Osculating eigenvectors of symmetric tensors

We start by setting some preliminary notation. Given a vector
v=(v_{0},\ldots,v_{m})\in\mathbb{C}^{m+1}
, we use the shorthand
v^{\alpha}
for the product
v_{0}^{\alpha_{0}}\cdots v_{m}^{\alpha_{m}}
for all
\alpha=(\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m+1}
. Furthermore we define
|\alpha|=\alpha_{0}+\cdots+\alpha_{m}
. For all
k\in\mathbb{N}
we define the vector
v^{k}\coloneqq(v^{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m+1},|\alpha|=k}\in\mathbb{C}^{\binom{m+k}{k}}
.

A symmetric tensor of order
d
over
\mathbb{C}^{m+1}
is an element of the tensor space
\mathrm{Sym}^{d}(\mathbb{C}^{m+1})^{*}
, which we identify with the space
\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}
of homogeneous polynomials of degree
d
in
m+1
variables. In particular, we write an element
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}
as

(5.1)

f(x_{0},\ldots,x_{m})=\sum_{|\alpha|=d}\binom{d}{\alpha}f_{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\,,

where
\binom{d}{\alpha}=\frac{d!}{\alpha_{0}!\cdots\alpha_{m}!}
. In particular, we identify
f
with the vector
(f_{\alpha})_{|\alpha|=d}\in\mathbb{C}^{\binom{m+d}{d}}
.
The monomials
(x^{\alpha})_{|\alpha|=d}
form a basis for the Veronese embedding
\nu_{m}^{d}
.

The geometry induced by a specific quadric hypersurface may differ from the generic behavior. In the following, we analyze the geometry of the Bombieri-Weyl metric.

Definition 5.1
.

Given
f=(f_{\alpha})_{|\alpha|=d}
and
g=(g_{\alpha})_{|\alpha|=d}
with real coordinates, the Bombieri-Weyl inner product between
f
and
g
is

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}\coloneqq\sum_{|\alpha|=d}\binom{d}{\alpha}f_{\alpha}g_{\alpha}\,.

In particular, the Bombieri-Weyl norm of
f=(f_{\alpha})_{|\alpha|=d}
is the square root of the quadratic form
q_{\mathrm{BW}}(f)\coloneqq\sum_{|\alpha|=d}\binom{d}{\alpha}f_{\alpha}^{2}
, while
\sqrt{q_{\mathrm{BW}}(f-g)}
is the Bombieri-Weyl distance between the polynomials
f
and
g
. In this section, we aim to study the higher-order critical points of the Bombieri-Weyl distance minimization problem

(5.2)

\min_{\ell\in(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})^{*}}q_{\mathrm{BW}}(f-\ell^{d})\,,\quad f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}\,.

As for the general case, we relax the assumptions that
f
and
\ell
have real coefficients; therefore, we study the higher-order critical points of the complex-valued polynomial function
q_{\mathrm{BW}}(f-\ell^{d})
. Let
q(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{m}x_{i}^{2}
be the standard Euclidean quadratic form in
(\mathbb{R}^{m+1})^{*}
and
S_{q}^{m}=\mathbb{V}(q(x)-1)\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m+1}
the
m
-dimensional unit sphere associated with
q
. Consider the map

(5.3)

S_{q}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}\,,\quad(v,\lambda)\mapsto\lambda\langle v,x\rangle^{d}\,.

Then the subset
\{\ell^{d}\mid\ell\in(\mathbb{C}^{m+1})^{*}\}\subseteq\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}
is the Zariski closure of the image of the map in (
5.3
). More precisely, it equals the affine cone over the Veronese variety
V_{m}^{d}\subseteq\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d})
defined in (
2.4
). Furthermore, two important identities coming from the definition of the Bombieri-Weyl quadratic form are

(5.4)

\displaystyle\begin{split}q_{\mathrm{BW}}(\langle v,x\rangle^{d})&=q(v)^{d}\quad\forall\,v\in\mathbb{C}^{m+1}\\
\left\langle\prod_{j=1}^{d}\langle x,v_{j}\rangle,\prod_{j=1}^{d}\langle x,w_{j}\rangle\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}&=\prod_{j=1}^{d}\langle v_{j},w_{j}\rangle\quad\forall\,v_{j},w_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{m+1}\,.\end{split}

Remark 5.2
.

The minimization problem (
5.2
) can be rephrased as

(5.5)

\min_{v\in S_{q}^{m}}\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}q_{\mathrm{BW}}(f-\lambda\,\ell^{d})\,,\quad f\in\mathbb{R}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}\,,

where
\ell=\ell(v)\coloneqq\langle x,v\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{m+1})^{*}
.
Let
\langle\ell^{d}\rangle
be the line spanned by
\ell^{d}
. Then the projection of
f
onto
\langle\ell^{d}\rangle
is denoted by
P_{\langle\ell^{d}\rangle}(f)
. We also consider the orthogonal complement
\langle\ell^{d}\rangle^{\perp}
of
\langle\ell^{d}\rangle
with respect to the Bombieri-Weyl inner product. By the Pythagorean identity, we have that

(5.6)

q_{\mathrm{BW}}(f)=q_{\mathrm{BW}}(P_{\langle\ell^{d}\rangle}(f))+q_{\mathrm{BW}}(P_{\langle\ell^{d}\rangle^{\perp}}(f))\,.

Furthermore, we have that
\min_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}}q_{\mathrm{BW}}(f-\lambda\,\ell^{d})=q_{\mathrm{BW}}(P_{\langle\ell^{d}\rangle^{\perp}}(f))
.
Hence, we can say that the original problem (
5.5
) is now rephrased as

(5.7)

\min_{v\in S_{q}^{m}}q_{\mathrm{BW}}(P_{\langle\ell^{d}\rangle^{\perp}}(f))\Longleftrightarrow\max_{v\in S_{q}^{m}}q_{\mathrm{BW}}(P_{\langle\ell^{d}\rangle}(f))\,,

and the equivalence is an immediate consequence of (
5.6
).
The identity (
5.4
) allows us to write
P_{\langle\ell^{d}\rangle}(f)=\langle f,\ell^{d}\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}\,\ell^{d}\quad\forall\,v\in S_{q}^{m}
, where
q_{\mathrm{BW}}(\ell^{d})=1
thanks to (
5.4
). But then the second part of (
5.7
) is in turn equivalent to

(5.8)

\max_{v\in S_{q}^{m}}\langle f,\ell^{d}\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}\,.

In particular,
\langle f,\ell^{d}\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}
is a polynomial objective function of degree
d
in the coordinates of
v
. In short, minimizing the Bombieri-Weyl distance between
f
and the affine cone
CV_{m}^{d}
is equivalent to maximizing the function
\langle f,\ell^{d}\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}
for
v\in S_{q}^{m}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

The Veronese embedding
\nu_{m}^{d}\colon\mathbb{P}^{m}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d})
is
k
-regular for all
k\leq d
. Furthermore, for all
\ell\in(\mathbb{C}^{m+1})^{*}\setminus\{0\}
and
k\leq d
, we have

(5.9)

\mathbb{T}_{[\ell^{d}]}^{k}(\nu_{m}^{d})=\{[\ell^{d-k}g]\mid g\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{k}\}\,.

Definition 5.4
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}
and
k\leq d
. A nonzero vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{m+1}
is a
normalized eigenvector of
f
of order
k
if
q(v)=1
and there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
such that

(5.10)

\frac{(d-k)!}{d!}\nabla_{k}f(v)=\lambda\,v^{k}\,,

where
\nabla_{k}f\coloneqq\left(\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}\right)_{|\alpha|=k}\in\mathbb{C}^{\binom{m+k}{k}}
.
The value
\lambda
is the
eigenvalue of
f
of order
k
associated with
v
. The pair
(v,\lambda)
is called a
normalized eigenpair
of
f
of order
k
.

Remark 5.5
.

If
d
and
k
are odd or
d
is odd and
k
is even, then if
(v,\lambda)
is a solution of (
5.10
), then also
(-v,-\lambda)
is. Instead, if
d
is even and
k
is odd or
d
and
k
are even, then if
(v,\lambda)
is a solution of (
5.10
), then also
(v,-\lambda)
is.

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}
and
k\leq d
. For every
v\in\mathbb{C}^{m+1}
, we define
\ell=\ell(v)\coloneqq\langle x,v\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{m+1})^{*}
. For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m+1}
with
|\alpha|=k

\langle f,\ell^{d-k}x^{\alpha}\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}=\frac{(d-k)!}{d!}\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}(v)\,.

Proof.

Writing
f
as in (
5.1
) and applying Definition
5.1
, we get

\displaystyle\langle f,\ell^{d-k}x^{\alpha}\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\gamma|=d}\sum_{|\beta|=d-k}\binom{d-k}{\beta}\left\langle\binom{d}{\gamma}x^{\gamma},x^{\alpha+\beta}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}f_{\gamma}v^{\beta}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\gamma|=d\\
\gamma-\alpha\geq 0\end{subarray}}\binom{d-k}{\gamma-\alpha}f_{\gamma}v^{\gamma-\alpha}

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-k)!}{d!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\gamma|=d\\
\gamma-\alpha\geq 0\end{subarray}}\binom{d}{\gamma-\alpha}f_{\gamma}v^{\gamma-\alpha}=\frac{(d-k)!}{d!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\gamma|=d\\
\gamma-\alpha\geq 0\end{subarray}}\binom{d}{\gamma}f_{\gamma}\left[\prod_{j=0}^{m}\frac{\gamma_{j}!}{(\gamma_{j}-\alpha_{j})!}v^{\gamma-\alpha}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-k)!}{d!}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\gamma|=d\\
\gamma-\alpha\geq 0\end{subarray}}\binom{d}{\gamma}f_{\gamma}\left[\frac{\partial^{k}x^{\gamma}}{\partial x^{\alpha}}(v)\right]=\frac{(d-k)!}{d!}\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}(v)\,,

namely the desired identity.
∎

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
f\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}
and
k\leq d
. For every
v\in\mathbb{C}^{m+1}
, we define
\ell=\ell(v)\coloneqq\langle x,v\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{m+1})^{*}
. A vector
\lambda\,\ell^{d}
with
v\in S_{q}^{m}
and
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
is a critical point of order
k
of the function
q_{\mathrm{BW}}(f-\lambda\,\ell^{d})
if and only if
(v,\lambda)
is a normalized eigenpair of
f
of order
k
.

Proof.

Consider the linear form
\ell(v)=\langle x,v\rangle\in(\mathbb{C}^{m+1})^{*}
.
The tensor
\lambda\,\ell^{d}
is critical of order
k
of
q_{\mathrm{BW}}(f-\lambda\,\ell^{d})
if and only if
\langle f-\lambda\,\ell^{d},w\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}=0
for every
w\in\mathbb{T}_{[\ell^{d}]}^{k}(\nu_{m}^{d})
. Using Lemma
5.3
and linearity of the inner product, we can simplify this problem by imposing that

\langle f-\lambda\,\ell^{d},\ell^{d-k}x^{\alpha}\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}=0\quad\forall\,\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m+1}\,\ |\alpha|=k\,.

For any
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m+1}
with
|\alpha|=k
, we compute
\langle f,\ell^{d-k}x^{\alpha}\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}
and
\langle\ell^{d},\ell^{d-k}x^{\alpha}\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}
. The former is computed in Lemma
5.6
. The latter is computed applying Lemma
5.6
replacing
f
with
\ell^{d}
:

\displaystyle\langle\ell^{d},\ell^{d-k}x^{\alpha}\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}

\displaystyle=q(\ell)^{d-k}\langle\ell^{k},x^{\alpha}\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}=\sum_{|\beta|=k}\binom{d}{\alpha}^{-1}v^{\beta}\left\langle\binom{d}{\beta}x^{\beta},\binom{d}{\alpha}x^{\alpha}\right\rangle_{\mathrm{BW}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|\beta|=k}\binom{d}{\alpha}^{-1}v^{\beta}\binom{d}{\alpha}\delta_{\beta,\alpha}=v^{\alpha}\,,

where the first and second equalities follow from (
5.4
) and
\delta_{\beta,\alpha}\neq 0
if and only if
\beta=\alpha
, in which case
\delta_{\beta,\beta}=1
.
Summing up, the point
\lambda\,\ell^{d}
with
v\in S_{q}^{m}
is critical of order
k
of
q_{\mathrm{BW}}(f-\lambda\,\ell^{d})
if and only if
\frac{(d-k)!}{d!}\frac{\partial^{k}f}{\partial x^{\alpha}}(v)=\lambda\,v^{\alpha}
for all
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{m+1}
with
|\alpha|=k
, or equivalently, if and only if
(v,\lambda)
is a normalized eigenpair of
f
.
∎

In Proposition
5.7
, it is fundamental to use the Bombieri-Weyl quadratic form
q_{\mathrm{BW}}
.
If we replace
Q_{\mathrm{BW}}
with a generic quadric
Q
, then
\operatorname{DD}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q)=\operatorname{gDD}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d})
, and the latter is computed in Corollary
4.12
. Our next goal is to compute
\operatorname{DD}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
instead.

Proposition 5.8
.

Let
\nu_{m}^{d}
be the degree-
d
Veronese embedding of
\mathbb{P}^{m}
, and equip
\mathbb{R}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d}
with the Bombieri-Weyl inner product. For all
k\leq d
, the
k
th order distance degree
\operatorname{DD}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
equals the coefficient of the monomial
h_{1}^{m}h_{2}^{\binom{m+k}{k}-m-1}
in the expansion of (
1.1
).
Furthermore, the
k
th order distance locus of
(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
is irreducible of dimension
m+\binom{m+d}{d}-\binom{m+k}{k}
.

Proof.

By Proposition
5.7
, the
k
th order critical points of the Bombieri-Weyl distance function from
f
restricted to
\nu_{m}^{d}(\mathbb{P}^{m})
correspond to the
k
th order eigenvectors of
f
.
Consider the
\binom{m+k}{k}\times 2
matrix

(5.11)

M_{k}(x,f)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\nabla_{k}f(x)&x^{k}\end{pmatrix}\,.

We consider the pair
([x],[f])\in Z\coloneqq\mathbb{P}^{m}\times\mathbb{P}(\mathbb{C}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{m}]_{d})
. Note that the columns of
M_{k}(x,f)
have bidegrees
(k,0)
and
(d-k,1)
in the coordinates of
x
and
f
, respectively. This means that the transpose of
M_{k}(x,f)
defines the fiber
\varphi_{[x],[f]}
over
([x],[f])
of the vector bundle morphism

(5.12)

\varphi\colon\mathcal{E}\to\mathcal{F}\,,\quad\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\mathcal{O}_{Z}^{\binom{m+k}{k}}\,,\quad\mathcal{F}\coloneqq\mathcal{O}_{Z}(k,0)\oplus\mathcal{O}_{Z}(d-k,1)\,.

The degeneracy locus
D(\varphi)\coloneqq\{z\in Z\mid\operatorname{rank}\varphi_{z}\leq 1\}
coincides with the
k
th order distance correspondence
\operatorname{DC}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
introduced in Definition
4.4
. Observe that
\operatorname{DC}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})\neq\emptyset
and

\displaystyle\dim\operatorname{DC}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})=m+\binom{m+d}{d}-\binom{m+k}{k}=\dim Z-(\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{E}-1)(\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{F}-1)

coincides with the expected dimension of
D(\varphi)
. Therefore the degree of
D(\varphi)
, or the degree of
\operatorname{DC}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
, can be computed applying Porteous’ formula
[
ACGH85
, (4.2), p. 86]
:

(5.13)

\deg D(\varphi)=(-1)^{\binom{m+k}{k}-1}c_{\binom{m+k}{k}-1}(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F})\,,

where

\displaystyle c_{t}(\mathcal{E}-\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle=\frac{c_{t}(\mathcal{E})}{c_{t}(\mathcal{F})}=\frac{1}{(1+kh_{1})(1+(d-k)h_{1}+h_{2})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+dh_{1}+h_{2}+k(d-k)h_{1}^{2}+kh_{1}h_{2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}(dh_{1}+h_{2}+k(d-k)h_{1}^{2}+kh_{1}h_{2})^{j}\,.

The
k
th order distance locus
\operatorname{DL}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})=\varphi_{2,k}(\operatorname{DC}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}}))
is irreducible of dimension
m+\binom{m+d}{d}-\binom{m+k}{k}
by Lemma
4.5
, or of codimension
\binom{m+k}{k}-m-1
. Hence
\operatorname{DD}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
equals the coefficient of the monomial
h_{1}^{m}h_{2}^{\binom{m+k}{k}-m-1}
in the previous expansion.
∎

We determine close formulas for
\operatorname{DD}_{k}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
for small values of
m
.

Corollary 5.9
.

For all
k\leq d
, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{DD}_{k}(\nu_{1}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})

\displaystyle=k(d-k+1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{DD}_{k}(\nu_{2}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})

\displaystyle=\frac{(d-k)^{2}}{2}\binom{k+2}{k}^{2}-\frac{(d-k)(3d-5k)}{2}\binom{k+2}{k}+(d-2k)^{2}\,.

Proof.

Let
m=1
and
k\leq d
. By Proposition
5.8
,
\operatorname{DD}_{k}(\nu_{1}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
is the coefficient of
h_{1}h_{2}^{k-1}
in the expansion of

(5.14)

(-1)^{k}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}(dh_{1}+h_{2}+kh_{1}h_{2})^{j}\in\frac{\mathbb{Z}[h_{1},h_{2}]}{\langle h_{1}^{2},h_{2}^{d+1}\rangle}\,.

Observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}(dh_{1}+h_{2}+kh_{1}h_{2})^{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}\sum_{i_{1}+i_{2}+i_{3}=j}\binom{j}{i_{1},i_{2},i_{3}}d^{i_{1}}k^{i_{3}}h_{1}^{i_{1}+i_{3}}h_{2}^{i_{2}+i_{3}}\,.

To extract the coefficient of
h_{1}h_{2}^{k-1}
, we need to impose that
i_{1}+i_{3}=1
, hence
(i_{1},i_{3})\in\{(1,0),(0,1)\}
and

i_{2}=j-1
, therefore, from the inner sum we extract the two summands
jdh_{1}h_{2}^{j-1}+jkh_{1}h_{2}^{j}
. The coefficient of
h_{1}h_{2}^{k-1}
is obtained selecting
j\in\{k-1,k\}
, hence

\operatorname{DD}_{k}(\nu_{1}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})=(-1)^{k}[(-1)^{k-1}(k-1)k+(-1)^{k}kd]=k(d-k+1)\,.

Now consider
m=2
and
k\leq d
. In this case
\operatorname{DD}_{k}(\nu_{2}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})
is the coefficient of
h_{1}^{2}h_{2}^{\binom{k+2}{2}-3}
in the expansion of

(5.15)

(-1)^{\binom{k+2}{k}-1}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j}(dh_{1}+h_{2}+k(d-k)h_{1}^{2}+kh_{1}h_{2})^{j}\in\frac{\mathbb{Z}[h_{1},h_{2}]}{\langle h_{1}^{3},h_{2}^{\binom{d+2}{d}}\rangle}\,.

Observe that

\displaystyle(dh_{1}+h_{2}+k(d-k)h_{1}^{2}+kh_{1}h_{2})^{j}=\sum_{|i|=j}\binom{j}{i}d^{i_{1}}k^{i_{3}+i_{4}}(d-k)^{i_{3}}h_{1}^{i_{1}+2i_{3}+i_{4}}h_{2}^{i_{2}+i_{4}}\,.

To extract the coefficient of
h_{1}^{2}h_{2}^{\binom{k+2}{2}-3}
, we need to assume that
i_{1}+2i_{3}+i_{4}=2
. This implies that

Proof of Theorem

.

By definition, the Veronese embedding

which is a generalization of a result attributed to Lasker, see

We consider first the case

\mathbb{T}_{[\ell^{d}]}^{k}(\nu_{1}^{d})^{\perp}=\{[(\ell^{\perp})^{k+1}g]\in\mathbb{P}^{d}\mid g\in\mathbb{C}[x_{0},x_{1}]_{d-k-1}\}

Now consider a generic point

\operatorname{DD}_{d-1}(\nu_{1}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})=2(d-1)

Now assume that
(m,k)\neq(1,d-1)

for all

\mathbb{N}_{[x_{0}^{d}]}^{d}(\nu_{m}^{d},Q_{\mathrm{BW}})=\{[x_{0}^{d}]\}

th order eigenvectors of

, where

Lemma 5.10

Let
\mathbb{P}(V)\subseteq\mathbb{P}(W)
be projective spaces of dimensions
n
and
r
, respectively. Consider the morphism
f=\pi\circ f^{\prime}\colon X\to\mathbb{P}(V)
, where

k
-osculating of
k
-osculating dimension

\pi\colon\mathbb{P}(W)\dashrightarrow\mathbb{P}(V)

(1)

f
is globally
k
-osculating of
k
-osculating dimension

and

\operatorname{gDD}_{k}(f)=\operatorname{gDD}_{k}(f^{\prime})\,.

(2)

Let

be a quadric hypersurface such that
(f,Q)
is in general
k
-osculating position. Then
Q
induces a nonsingular quadric hypersurface

such that
(f^{\prime},Q^{\prime})
is in general
k
-osculating position and

(5.18)

\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f,Q)\cong\operatorname{DL}_{k}(f^{\prime},Q^{\prime})\cap\mathbb{P}(V)\,.

Proof.

The proof of part
(1)
follows from the identity
\mu_{k,j}(f)=\mu_{k,j}(f^{\prime})
, as established in
[
Pie22
]
and
[
Pie78
]
. For completeness, we provide a self-contained argument using the notation and methods developed herein.

Consider the
k
th jet morphisms
j_{k}\colon X\times V\to\mathcal{P}^{k}(f)
and

and
f^{\prime}
, respectively. Choose bases
(\sigma_{0},\ldots,\sigma_{n})
of

and
(\tau_{0},\ldots,\tau_{r})
of

The generic
k
th osculating dimension of
f
(and similarly
f^{\prime}
) corresponds to the rank of the matrices

\displaystyle A^{(k)}_{p}(f)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c}j_{k,p}(\sigma_{0})^{\mathsf{T}}&j_{k,p}(\sigma_{1})^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&j_{k,p}(\sigma_{n})^{\mathsf{T}}\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle A^{(k)}_{p}(f^{\prime})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c|c}j_{k,p}(\tau_{0})^{\mathsf{T}}&j_{k,p}(\tau_{1})^{\mathsf{T}}&\cdots&j_{k,p}(\tau_{r})^{\mathsf{T}}\end{array}\right).

Since
\pi
is generic, the columns of
A^{(k)}_{p}(f)
are generic linear combinations of those of
A^{(k)}_{p}(f^{\prime})
. Hence,

\operatorname{rank}(A^{(k)}_{p}(f))=\min\{r,m_{k}\}=m_{k}

by definition of

. It follows that
\mathbb{T}^{k}_{p}(f)\cong\mathbb{T}^{k}_{p}(f^{\prime})
for all

. The isomorphism is induced by
\pi
, in particular

(5.19)

\pi(\mathbb{T}^{k}_{p}(f^{\prime}))=\mathbb{T}^{k}_{p}(f)\,.

Let
\Pi\colon W\to V
be the linear map defining
\pi
.
At the level of vector bundles, the map
\Pi
induces a surjection
\operatorname{im}j_{k}(f^{\prime})\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{im}j_{k}(f)
, which is an isomorphism due to equal rank. Since
\pi|_{f^{\prime}(X)}\colon f^{\prime}(X)\to f(X)
is an isomorphism, we have
(\pi|_{f^{\prime}(X)})^{*}(\operatorname{im}j_{k}(f))\cong\operatorname{im}j_{k}(f^{\prime})
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mu_{k,j}(f^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{X}c_{j}(\operatorname{im}j_{k}(f^{\prime}))=\int_{X}c_{j}\left((\pi|_{f^{\prime}(X)})^{*}\operatorname{im}j_{k}(f)\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{X}(\pi|_{f^{\prime}(X)})^{*}\left(c_{j}(\operatorname{im}j_{k}(f))\right)=\int_{X}c_{j}(\operatorname{im}j_{k}(f))=\mu_{k,j}(f),

using the projection formula

. Consider the linear map

k
-osculating position. Denote by

. We define a nonsingular quadric

over

Proof of Theorem

the associated globally
k
-osculating morphism.

f_{0},\ldots,f_{n}

f=\pi\circ\nu_{m}^{d}
, where
\nu_{m}^{d}\colon\mathbb{P}^{m}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\binom{m+d}{d}-1}
denotes the Veronese embedding, and

(1) with

. Furthermore, by Lemma

(2) there exists a nonsingular quadric

such that

, in particular

. The final identity follows by (

6.
Higher-order distance degrees of regular embeddings

In this subsection, we apply the formula (
4.7
) to compute the generic
k
th order distance degree of a
k
-regular embedding
f\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
. The
k
-regularity of
f
implies that
\mu_{k,i}(f)
equals the degree of
c_{i}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))
for all
i
and
k
.

In Corollary
4.12
, we already computed the generic higher-order distance degree of the Veronese embedding
\nu_{m}^{d}\colon\mathbb{P}^{n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{\binom{m+d}{d}-1}
, using that its jet bundles split as a sum of line bundles. More in general, given a
k
-regular embedding
f\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
, the short exact sequences

(6.1)

\begin{gathered}0\to\Omega_{X}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}^{1}(f)\longrightarrow\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)\to 0\\
0\to\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{X}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}^{k}(f)\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(f)\to 0\quad\forall\,k\geq 2\end{gathered}

allow us to compute the Chern classes of the vector bundles
\mathcal{P}^{k}(f)
via a recursive formula, provided that the Chern classes of
\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{X}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)
and of
\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{X}
are known. Regarding the first ones, the Chern classes of the tensor product between a vector bundle
\mathcal{F}
of rank
r
and a line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on a nonsingular variety
X
are given by the following formula.

(6.2)

c_{i}(\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{L})=\sum_{j=0}^{i}\binom{r-j}{i-j}c_{j}(\mathcal{F})c_{1}(\mathcal{L})^{i-j}\,.

In the following, we write the total Chern classes of
\mathcal{T}_{X}
and
\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)
as
c(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))=1+L
and
c(\mathcal{T}_{X})=c(X)=1+\sum_{i=1}^{m}c_{i}
. Note that
c_{i}(\Omega_{X})=(-1)^{i}c_{i}
for all
i
. From the first exact sequence in (
6.1
) we get
c(\mathcal{P}^{1}(f))=c(\Omega_{X}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))\cdot c(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))
. Using Lemma
6.2
we get for all
i\geq 1

\displaystyle c_{i}(\mathcal{P}^{1}(f))

\displaystyle=c_{i}(\Omega_{X}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))+c_{i-1}(\Omega_{X}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))\cdot L

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{i}\binom{m-j}{i-j}c_{j}(\Omega_{X})\cdot L^{i-j}+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\binom{m-j}{i-1-j}c_{j}(\Omega_{X})\cdot L^{i-j}

\displaystyle=c_{i}(\Omega_{X})+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\left[\binom{m-j}{i-j}+\binom{m-j}{i-1-j}\right]c_{j}(\Omega_{X})\cdot L^{i-j}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{i}c_{i}+\sum_{j=0}^{i-1}\binom{m+1-j}{i-j}(-1)^{j}c_{j}\cdot L^{i-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{i}\binom{m+1-j}{i-j}(-1)^{j}c_{j}\cdot L^{i-j}\,,

which corresponds to
[
Hol88
, §3]
. Then, using the second exact sequence in (
6.1
), we have
c(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))=c(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{X}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))\cdot c(\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(f))
, hence

\displaystyle c_{i}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}c_{\ell}(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{X}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))c_{i-\ell}(\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(f))

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\binom{\binom{m+k-1}{k}-j}{\ell-j}c_{j}(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{X})\cdot L^{\ell-j}\right]c_{i-\ell}(\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(f))\,,

where we used the fact that
\operatorname{rank}\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{X}=\binom{m+k-1}{k}
for all
k\geq 1
. Summing up, we have the following recursive formula:

(6.3)

\begin{cases}c_{i}(\mathcal{P}^{1}(f))=\sum_{j=0}^{i}\binom{m+1-j}{i-j}(-1)^{j}c_{j}\cdot L^{i-j}\\
c_{i}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))=\sum_{\ell=0}^{i}\left[\sum_{j=0}^{\ell}\binom{\binom{m+k-1}{k}-j}{\ell-j}c_{j}(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{X})\cdot L^{\ell-j}\right]c_{i-\ell}(\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(f))\,.\end{cases}

We implemented a
Macaulay2
code
[
GS97
]
that computes the Chern classes
c_{i}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))
for any input
k
and
m
using (
6.3
); see
[
DRRS25
]
. Our code computes the generic
k
th order distance degree of any
k
-regular embedding
f\colon X\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
of a nonsingular projective variety
X
of dimension
m
.

In the following, we derive closed formulas for the classes
c_{i}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))
for
m\in[3]
, using the following lemma, whose proof is a direct application of the splitting principle
[
Ful98
, Remark 3.2.3]
. More involved identities can be derived for larger
m
.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mathcal{F}
be a vector bundle on a nonsingular surface
X
. Then for any
k\geq 1
,

c(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\mathcal{F})=\begin{cases}1+k\,c_{1}(\mathcal{F})&\text{if $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{F}=1$}\\[3.0pt]
1+\binom{k+1}{2}c_{1}(\mathcal{F})+\binom{k+1}{3}\frac{3k+2}{4}c_{1}^{2}(\mathcal{F})+\binom{k+2}{3}c_{2}(\mathcal{F})&\text{if $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{F}=2$}\\[3.0pt]
1+\binom{k+2}{3}c_{1}(\mathcal{F})+\frac{1}{2}\binom{k+3}{3}\binom{k+1}{3}c_{1}^{2}(\mathcal{F})+\binom{k+3}{4}c_{2}(\mathcal{F})&\\[2.0pt]
+\binom{k+3}{5}\frac{5\,k^{4}+20\,k^{3}-5\,k^{2}-50\,k-12}{54}c_{1}^{3}(\mathcal{F})&\\[2.0pt]
+\binom{k+3}{5}\frac{5\,k^{2}+20\,k+6}{6}c_{1}(\mathcal{F})c_{2}(\mathcal{F})+\binom{k+3}{4}\frac{2\,k+3}{5}c_{3}(\mathcal{F})&\text{if $\operatorname{rank}\mathcal{F}=3$.}\end{cases}

Proposition 6.2
.

Consider a
k
-regular embedding
f\colon C\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
of a nonsingular projective curve
C
. Write
c(X)=1+c_{1}
and
c(\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))=1+L
. Then

(6.4)

\operatorname{gDD}_{k}(f)=\int_{C}(k+2)L-\binom{k+1}{2}c_{1}\,.

Proof.

We claim that, for all
k\geq 1
,

(6.5)

c(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))=1+(k+1)L-\binom{k+1}{2}c_{1}\,.

Assuming the claim is true, then

\operatorname{gDD}_{k}(f)=\mu_{k,0}(f)+\mu_{k,1}(f)=\int_{C}L+c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))=\int_{C}(k+2)L-\binom{k+1}{2}c_{1}\,,

which is the desired formula.
To prove the claim, consider the recursive formula (
6.3
). The first equation yields
c(\mathcal{P}^{1}(f))=1+2L-c_{1}
, which is identity (
6.5
) for
k=1
. Now assume (
6.5
) true for
k-1
. Using the second exact sequence in (
6.1
), we obtain that

\displaystyle c(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))

\displaystyle=c(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{C}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{C}(1))c(\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(f))=(1+L+c_{1}(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{C}))\left(1+kL-\binom{k}{2}c_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=(1+L-kc_{1})\left(1+kL-\binom{k}{2}c_{1}\right)=1+(k+1)L-\binom{k+1}{2}c_{1}\,,

thus proving the identity (
6.5
) at the step
k
. This completes the proof.
∎

Example 6.3
.

We apply Proposition
6.2
for the Veronese embedding
\nu_{1}^{d}\colon\mathbb{P}^{1}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{d}
, which is
k

k\leq d
. Let
h

.
\diamondsuit

Proposition 6.4
.

Consider a
k
-regular embedding
f\colon S\hookrightarrow\mathbb{P}^{n}
of a nonsingular projective surface
S
. Write
c(S)=1+c_{1}+c_{2}
and
c(\mathcal{O}_{S}(1))=1+L
. Then

(6.6)

\operatorname{gDD}_{k}(f)=\int_{X}\alpha_{1}\,L^{2}+\alpha_{2}\,c_{1}L+\alpha_{3}\,c_{1}^{2}+\alpha_{4}\,c_{2}\,,

where

(6.7)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\alpha_{1}&=1+\binom{k+2}{2}+3\binom{k+3}{4}\,,\quad\alpha_{2}=-\binom{k+2}{2}\binom{k+2}{3}\,,\\
\alpha_{3}&=\frac{1}{2}\binom{k+1}{3}\binom{k+3}{3}\,,\quad\alpha_{4}=\binom{k+3}{4}\,.\end{split}

Proof.

We claim that, for all
k\geq 1
,

(6.8)

\begin{aligned} c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))&=\binom{k+2}{2}L-\binom{k+2}{3}c_{1}\\
c_{2}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))&=\frac{1}{2}\binom{k+1}{2}\binom{k+3}{2}L^{2}-2k\binom{k+3}{4}c_{1}L+\frac{1}{2}\binom{k+1}{3}\binom{k+3}{3}c_{1}^{2}+\binom{k+3}{4}c_{2}\,.\end{aligned}

Assuming the claim is true, then

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{gDD}_{k}(f)&=\mu_{k,0}(f)+\mu_{k,1}(f)+\mu_{k,2}(f)=\int_{X}L^{2}+c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))L+c_{2}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))\\
&=\int_{X}\left[1+\binom{k+2}{2}+3\binom{k+3}{4}\right]L^{2}-\left[\binom{k+2}{3}+2k\binom{k+3}{4}\right]c_{1}L+\frac{1}{2}\binom{k+1}{3}\binom{k+3}{3}c_{1}^{2}+\binom{k+3}{4}c_{2}\,,\end{aligned}

which, after simplifying, is equal to the desired formula.
To prove the claim, consider the recursive formula (
6.3
). The first equation yields the identities
c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{1}(f))=3L-c_{1}
and
c_{2}(\mathcal{P}^{1}(f))=3L^{2}-2c_{1}L+c_{2}
, thus proving (
6.8
) for
k=1
. Now assume (
6.8
) true for
k-1
. Observe that
\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{S}
has rank
k+1
for all
k\geq 1
.
Using Lemma
6.1
, we get the Chern classes

(6.9)

c_{1}(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{S})=-\binom{k+1}{2}c_{1}\,,\quad c_{2}(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{S})=\frac{3k+2}{4}\binom{k+1}{3}c_{1}^{2}+\binom{k+2}{3}c_{2}\,.

Applying Lemma
6.2
, we obtain that

\begin{aligned} c(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{S}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{S}(1))&=\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}c_{i}(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{S}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{S}(1))=\sum_{i=0}^{k+1}\sum_{j=0}^{i}\binom{k+1-j}{i-j}c_{j}(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{S})\cdot L^{i-j}\\
&=1+(k+1)L+c_{1}(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{S})+\binom{k+1}{2}L^{2}+kc_{1}(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{S})L+c_{2}(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{S})\\
&=1+(k+1)L-\binom{k+1}{2}c_{1}+\binom{k+1}{2}L^{2}-k\binom{k+1}{2}c_{1}L+\frac{3k+2}{4}\binom{k+1}{3}c_{1}^{2}+\binom{k+2}{3}c_{2}\,.\end{aligned}

Using the second exact sequence in (
6.1
) and the induction step, we obtain that

\begin{aligned} c(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))&=c(\mathrm{Sym}^{k}\Omega_{S}\otimes\mathcal{O}_{S}(1))\cdot c(\mathcal{P}^{k-1}(f))\\
&=\left(1+(k+1)L-\binom{k+1}{2}c_{1}+\binom{k+1}{2}L^{2}-k\binom{k+1}{2}c_{1}L+\frac{3k+2}{4}\binom{k+1}{3}c_{1}^{2}+\binom{k+2}{3}c_{2}\right)\\
&\quad\cdot\left(1+\binom{k+1}{2}L-\binom{k+1}{3}c_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\binom{k+2}{2}L^{2}-2(k-1)\binom{k+2}{4}c_{1}L+\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{3}\binom{k+2}{3}c_{1}^{2}+\binom{k+2}{4}c_{2}\right)\,.\end{aligned}

Expanding the product, one verifies that

\begin{aligned} c_{1}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))&=\left[(k+1)+\binom{k+1}{2}\right]L-\left[\binom{k+1}{2}+\binom{k+1}{3}\right]c_{1}=\binom{k+2}{2}L-\binom{k+2}{3}c_{1}\,,\\
c_{2}(\mathcal{P}^{k}(f))&=\left[(k+2)\binom{k+1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{2}\binom{k+2}{2}\right]L^{2}\\
&\quad-\left[k\binom{k+1}{2}+\binom{k+1}{2}^{2}+(k+1)\binom{k+1}{3}+2(k-1)\binom{k+2}{4}\right]c_{1}L\\
&\quad+\left[\frac{3k+2}{4}\binom{k+1}{3}+\binom{k+1}{2}\binom{k+1}{3}+\frac{1}{2}\binom{k}{3}\binom{k+2}{3}\right]c_{1}^{2}+\left[\binom{k+2}{3}+\binom{k+2}{4}\right]c_{2}\,,\end{aligned}

which, after simplifying, correspond to the identities in (
6.5
) at the step
k
.
∎

7.
Tropical geometry of distance optimization

In this chapter, we present a new combinatorial framework for studying distance optimization, based on tropical geometry. To enable this analysis, we introduce an additional parameter
t
to the coefficients of our variety
X
, and replace our base field of complex numbers
\mathbb{C}
with the valued field of complex Puiseux series
\mathbb{C}\{\{t\}\}
.
The limiting behavior of
X
as
t
goes to
0
is captured by the tropicalization
\Trop X
, a polyhedral complex that encodes many of the geometric properties of
X
.
Studying this limiting behavior allows us to characterize both higher distance degrees and the sensitivity of critical points of the distance function with respect to the parameter
t
.

We begin by fixing some notation.
Throughout,
\mathcal{C}
denotes an algebraically closed field with a nontrivial, non-Archimedean valuation
\nu\colon\mathcal{C}^{*}\to\mathbb{R}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{C}^{\circ}\coloneqq\big\{x\in\mathcal{C}\mid\nu(x)\geq 0\big\}
and by
\mathcal{C}^{\circ\circ}\coloneqq\big\{x\in\mathcal{C}\mid\nu(x)>0\big\}
the valuation ring of
\mathcal{C}
and its unique maximal ideal, respectively. The residue field
\mathcal{C}^{\circ}/\mathcal{C}^{\circ\circ}
is denoted by
\widetilde{\mathcal{C}}
.

We recall that a nonzero
formal Puiseux series
is of the form

x(t)=\sum_{k=k_{0}}^{\infty}c_{k}t^{\frac{k}{N}}\text{ for some }k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z},\,N\in\mathbb{N},\,c_{k}\in\mathbb{C},\,c_{k_{0}}\neq 0\,.

The valuation
\nu(x(t))=\frac{k_{0}}{N}
is defined as the smallest exponent of
t
in
x(t)
with nonzero coefficient, it is a measure of the degree to which
x(t)
depends on
t
.

From now on, let
f\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{C})
denote the closed embedding of an
m
-dimensional variety.
We denote by
\Trop X
the tropicalization of the cone over
f(X)
.

\displaystyle\Trop X\coloneqq\overline{\{\nu(x)\ \colon\ x\in(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{n+1}\mid\ [x]\in f(X)\}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n+1},

This is the closure of the image under the element-wise valuation map, taken in Euclidean topology.

Recall the notation given in the introduction and at the beginning of Section
4
. Consider
q\in\mathrm{Sym}^{2}V^{*}
, the nonsingular quadric hypersurface
Q=\mathbb{V}(q)\subseteq\mathbb{P}(V)=\mathbb{P}^{n}
, and the squared distance function
d_{u}^{2}(x)=q(u-x)
, seen as a function
d_{u}^{2}\colon V\to\mathbb{C}
.
Throughout most of this section, we assume
Q=Q_{\mathrm{ED}}=\mathbb{V}(x_{0}^{2}+\cdots+x_{n}^{2})
, which is most relevant for Euclidean distance optimization.
Note that, identifying the projective space
\mathbb{P}^{n}
and its dual
(\mathbb{P}^{n})^{\vee}
through the reciprocity map (
4.1
), induces a closed embedding
\mathrm{id}\times\partial_{q}^{-1}\colon W_{k}(f)\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{P}^{n}
of the conormal variety, and denote the embedded variety by
W_{k}(f,Q)\coloneqq\mathrm{id}\times\partial_{q}^{-1}(W_{k}(f))
.
The varieties
W_{k}(f,Q)
and
W_{k}(f,Q_{\mathrm{ED}})
are related through a linear change of coordinates induced by
M_{Q}
.
We now show that the tropicalization
\Trop W_{k}(f,Q)
contains crucial information on the
k
th-order polar degrees of
f
for arbitrary
k
.

We first note that, by the construction of

we have the inclusion
\{{\boldsymbol{1}}\}\times\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{1}}}^{k}(f)^{\perp}\subseteq W_{k}(f,Q_{\mathrm{ED}})
.
By
[
DDRP14
, Lemma 5.2]
it holds
\mathbb{T}_{{\boldsymbol{1}}}^{k}(f)=\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{rowspan}A^{(k)}),
and one checks directly that, following (
4.2
), the orthogonal complement
\mathbb{P}(\operatorname{rowspan}A^{(k)})^{\perp}
is equal to the kernel
\mathbb{P}(\ker(A^{(k)}))
.
Together, we obtain the inclusion

\{{\boldsymbol{1}}\}\times\mathbb{P}(\ker(A^{(k)}))\subseteq W_{k}(f,{Q_{\mathrm{ED}}})\,

Now
W_{k}(f,Q_{\mathrm{ED}})
is stable under the
(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{m}
-action
t\cdot(x,T)=(t^{A}\cdot x,t^{-A}\cdot T)
for all
(x,T)\in W_{k}(f,Q_{\mathrm{ED}})
. This finishes the proof.
∎

Before providing an algorithm for computing higher-order polar degrees, we recall basic results on the tropicalization of certain unirational varieties.
Let
C
and
D
be integer matrices of size
r\times d
and
s\times r
respectively. The rows of
D
are
w_{1},\ldots,w_{s}\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
.
We denote by
\lambda_{C}
the map defined by
C
, and by
\mu_{D}
the monomial map specified by
D
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{C}\colon(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{d}

\displaystyle\dashrightarrow(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{r}

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle\longmapsto C\,v

(7.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mu_{D}\colon(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{r}&\longrightarrow(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{s}\\
x&\longmapsto(x^{w_{1}},\ldots,x^{w_{s}}).\end{split}

The composition of these maps gives the unirational variety

Y_{C,D}=\overline{\operatorname{im}(\mu_{D}\circ\lambda_{C})}
in
(\mathcal{C}^{*})^{s}
.
Its tropicalization
\Trop Y_{C,D}
is obtained by tropicalizing the map
\mu_{D}\circ\lambda_{C}
.
The tropical linear space
\Trop(\operatorname{im}\lambda_{C})
is computed

of the matroid of

]
. An implementation can be found in polymake

]
.
The monomial map

tropicalizes to the linear map

Linear projections of balanced polyhedral complexes, and the underlying weights, are described in

Lemma 7.6
.

The tropical variety
\Trop Y_{C,D}
is the image, as a balanced fan, of the Bergman fan
\Trop(\operatorname{im}\lambda_{C})
under the linear map

Proposition
