Don’t Retrieve, Generate: Prompting LLMs for Synthetic Training Data in Dense Retrieval
Aarush Sinha

Abstract
Training effective dense retrieval models often relies on hard negative (HN) examples mined from the document corpus via methods like BM25 or cross-encoders (CE), processes that can be computationally demanding and require full corpus access. This paper introduces a different approach, an end-to-end pipeline where a Large Language Model (LLM) first generates a query from a passage, and then generates a hard negative example using only that query text. This corpus-free negative generation contrasts with standard mining techniques. We evaluated this LLM Query → \rightarrow LLM HN approach against traditional LLM Query → \rightarrow BM25 HN and LLM Query → \rightarrow CE HN pipelines using E5-Base and GTE-Base models on several BEIR benchmark datasets. Our results show the proposed all-LLM pipeline achieves performance identical to both the BM25 and the computationally intensive CE baselines across nDCG@10, Precision@10, and Recall@100 metrics. This demonstrates that our corpus-free negative generation method matches the effectiveness of complex, corpus-dependent mining techniques, offering a potentially simpler and more efficient pathway for training high-performance retrievers without sacrificing results. We make the dataset including the queries and the hard-negatives for all three methods publicly available 1 1 1 https://huggingface.co/collections/chungimungi/arxiv-hard-negatives-68027bbc601ff6cc8eb1f449 .

Introduction

Dense retrieval models, which encode queries and documents into low-dimensional vectors, are foundational to modern information retrieval systems
Karpukhin et al. 2020
;
Xiong et al. 2020
;
Reimers and Gurevych 2019
. These models typically require fine-tuning on task-specific data using contrastive objectives like triplet loss, which pulls relevant pairs closer while pushing away irrelevant (negative) passages
Henderson et al. 2017
.

A key factor in effective fine-tuning is the quality of negative examples
Xiong et al. 2020
. While random negatives are easy to generate, hard negatives semantically related but irrelevant passages provide stronger learning signals
Zhan et al. 2021
. Traditionally, these are mined from the corpus using methods like BM25 or neural cross-encoders (CEs) for re-ranking
Nogueira and Cho 2020
;
Qu et al. 2021
. However, these approaches require access to the full corpus and are resource-intensive, particularly CE-based mining which demands heavy GPU inference and scales poorly with corpus size.

Large Language Models (
Brown et al. 2020
;
Grattafiori et al. 2024
;
Kavukcuoglu 2025
) have become exceedingly good at understanding and generating text by following user instructions. We propose an alternative, corpus-free pipeline that utilizes the generative abilities of LLMs particularly the Gemini2.5-Pro to generate full training triplets. Our approach includes:

1.

Query Generation:
Given a positive passage, an LLM generates a corresponding query, similar to prior work like LLM Query.

2.

Hard Negative Generation:
Conditioned only on the generated query, the LLM produces two semantically challenging but irrelevant passages.

This query-only conditioning for negative generation avoids any corpus lookup or re-ranking, simplifying the pipeline and improving scalability.

We evaluate this end-to-end
LLM Query
\rightarrow
LLM HN
pipeline against two baselines:
LLM Query
\rightarrow
BM25 HN
and
LLM Query
\rightarrow
CE HN
, using E5-Base and GTE-Base models
Li et al. 2023
across BEIR datasets
Thakur et al. 2021
. Notably, our method achieves
comparable performance
across nDCG@10, Precision@10, and Recall@100, especially with GTE-Base.

In summary, we:

•

Propose a fully LLM-based training data generation pipeline.

•

Introduce a novel query-only, corpus-free hard negative generation method.

•

Demonstrate empirical parity with traditional corpus-mining approaches on BEIR.

•

Highlight significant efficiency and simplicity benefits over existing pipelines.

Related Work

Large neural models have been increasingly applied across the information retrieval pipeline. For query understanding, techniques like query expansion or generating hypothetical documents aim to improve retrieval effectiveness
Zhu et al. 2023
;
Ding et al. 2024
. Generative models also serve as powerful re-ranking components, refining initial candidate lists through pairwise or listwise comparisons
Ma et al. 2023
;
Qin et al. 2024
.

A significant area of research involves using generative models to create synthetic data for training dense retrievers, particularly valuable when labeled data is scarce. Approaches like InPars
Bonifacio et al. 2022
generate relevant documents for given queries, while others focus on unsupervised data augmentation
Meng et al. 2023
or specialized data for conversational contexts
Chen et al. 2024
or training smaller models
Tamber et al. 2025
.

Most relevant to our work is the exploration of generative models for creating challenging negative samples. SyNeg
Wang et al. 2024a
, for instance, employs multi-attribute prompting and reflection, often mixing synthetic negatives with traditionally mined ones. Other works optimize training data distributions considering negative samples
Ma et al. 2024
or explore negative generation for specific contexts like conversational retrieval
Jin et al. 2023
. Our approach differs by employing an end-to-end generative pipeline where hard negatives are synthesized conditioned solely on a generated query, explicitly avoiding corpus interaction for negative selection and aiming for performance parity through this simplified process.

Methodology

3.1
LLM Prompting

Our approach is straightforward. To construct our corpus, we collect 7,250 abstracts from arXiv across a variety of domains, including mathematics, physics, computer science, and others. We utilize the Gemini 2.5 (Pro -03-25) via Google AI Studio

Generate 2 passages for each query. Ensure that the passages are hard negatives in terms of information retrieval. The passage should be max of 100 words but no less than 75.

5
Results

We evaluated our proposed end-to-end generative pipeline (LLM Query + LLM HN), using an LLM for both query generation and corpus-free hard negative generation. This was compared against baselines using the same LLM queries but with BM25-mined (LLM Query + BM25 HN) or cross-encoder-mined (LLM Query + CE HN) hard negatives. Evaluations employed
E5-Base
and
GTE-Base
models on 10 BEIR datasets, measuring nDCG@10, Precision@10 (P@10), and Recall@100.

Detailed results are presented in Table
1
. The primary finding concerns the
GTE-Base
model: our proposed pipeline (LLM Query + LLM HN) achieved performance virtually identical to both the BM25 HN and CE HN baselines across all datasets and metrics (e.g., average nDCG@10: 0.495 for all methods, with negligible differences in the 4th decimal place for CE HN averages). For
E5-Base
, performance was also highly similar across the three methods (average nDCG@10: 0.441 for all). Minor differences (e.g., 0.001 nDCG@10 on COVID) were observed between our method and the baselines on individual datasets, but overall parity was maintained. As expected,
GTE-Base
consistently outperformed
E5-Base
(e.g., average nDCG@10 0.495 vs 0.441).

This performance parity, especially for
GTE-Base
, suggests that all three negative generation strategies may provide a sufficiently strong training signal for the models to approach their performance ceiling on these benchmarks and metrics. Therefore, our corpus-free negative generation approach demonstrates effectiveness comparable to established, computationally intensive corpus-dependent mining techniques in this experimental setup.

6
Conclusion

We presented an end-to-end pipeline using Large Language Models to generate queries and corresponding hard negatives conditioned solely on the query text, bypassing corpus mining for negatives. Our experiments demonstrate that this approach achieves retrieval performance identical to strong baselines using traditional BM25 or cross-encoder negative mining, particularly with the GTE-Base model across several benchmarks (nDCG@10, P@10, R@100).

This performance parity highlights the potential of our method to significantly simplify the training pipeline and reduce computational overhead associated with corpus indexing or re-ranking, without sacrificing retrieval effectiveness. Our corpus-free negative generation strategy offers a viable and efficient alternative for training dense retrievers, especially valuable when corpus access or mining resources are limited. Future work could explore generalization across different models, generative sources, and dataset types.

7
Future Work

Our future research involves extending the evaluation framework considerably. We aim to assess the adaptability of our method by applying it to a wider spectrum of Large Language Models, exemplified by models like Claude and the GPT family. A parallel and crucial objective is to test its robustness and scalability more thoroughly by utilizing a significantly larger text corpus, ensuring its efficacy under increased data volume and complexity.

8
Limitation

Our experiments were conducted using specific dense retriever architectures (E5-Base and GTE-Base). The performance equivalence observed might not directly generalize to significantly larger or different types of retrieval models without further investigation. Another limitation is our corpus being only 7250 abstracts. This relatively small and domain-specific initial set for query generation might limit the generalization of our findings, as the resulting synthetic training data may not fully reflect the challenges present when training or evaluating retrievers on much larger and more varied corpora. Dependency on a specific, experimental version of Gemini 2.5 (Pro -03-25) limits the study, as future model updates could alter generative behavior, while discontinuation would impede reproducibility.

Appendix A
What is a Hard Negative according to Gemini2.5-Pro ?

To ensure the Large Language Model understood the specific requirements for generating hard negatives relevant to information retrieval, we first prompted Gemini 2.5 Pro for a definition. This initial step helped ground the subsequent generation task. Figure
2
below presents the verbatim response received from the model, outlining its understanding of hard negatives, including their types, importance, and common mining techniques. This definition formed the conceptual basis used when prompting the model to generate the actual hard negative examples for our experiments, as described in Section 3.1.

Hard Negatives (IR Context)

Yes, absolutely. I know what a
hard negative
is in the context of Information Retrieval (IR) and Machine Learning.

Here’s a breakdown:

1.

Basic Definitions:

•

Positive Example:
A document that
is
relevant to a given query.

•

Negative Example:
A document that
is not
relevant to the given query.

2.

Types of Negatives:

•

Easy Negative:
A document that is clearly and obviously irrelevant to the query. It likely shares very few keywords or semantic concepts. A model can usually distinguish these easily.

–

Example:
Query: "best laptops for programming". Easy Negative: An article about gardening tips.

•

Hard Negative:
A document that is
irrelevant
to the query but is
difficult
for the model to distinguish from a positive example. It often shares significant keyword overlap, topic similarity, or might be ranked highly by a simpler retrieval model (like BM25) even though it doesn’t truly satisfy the user’s intent.

–

Example:
Query: "best python libraries for data analysis". Hard Negative: An article about popular Python libraries for
web development
(e.g., Django, Flask). It mentions "Python" and "libraries" but is contextually wrong. Another hard negative could be an outdated article about data analysis libraries.

3.

Why are Hard Negatives Important?

•

Training Better Models:
When training ranking models or embedding models for IR (especially dense retrieval models), using only easy negatives doesn’t challenge the model enough. It learns to separate obviously different things but fails on subtle distinctions.

•

Improving Discrimination:
Hard negatives force the model to learn finer-grained differences between relevant and irrelevant documents. It has to look beyond simple keyword matching and understand context, recency, specificity, and user intent better.

•

Better Embeddings:
In embedding-based retrieval, using hard negatives during training (e.g., in triplet loss or contrastive loss) pushes the embeddings of irrelevant but similar documents further away from the query embedding, while pulling relevant document embeddings closer. This creates a more discriminative embedding space.

4.

How are Hard Negatives Found (Mining)?

•

Top K from Initial Retrieval:
Taking top-ranked documents from a baseline model (like BM25) that are known (via labels) to be non-relevant.

•

Using Embeddings:
Finding documents whose embeddings are close to the query embedding but are known to be non-relevant.

•

User Behavior:
Documents that were presented to users but not clicked, or clicked but resulted in very short dwell times (indicating they weren’t actually relevant).

•

Adversarial Generation:
Techniques specifically designed to find or generate examples that fool the current model.

In summary, hard negatives are crucial for training robust and effective IR models because they represent the challenging cases where the model might otherwise make mistakes, pushing it to develop a deeper understanding of relevance.

To provide further insight into the characteristics of the training data components produced by each pipeline, Figure
3
visualizes the semantic relationships. Cosine similarities were computed using the
sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2
representation model, comparing the query, the original passage, and the corresponding hard negative example for each of the three methods.

Several observations emerge from this comparison. Firstly, the similarity between the query and the source passage (blue bars) differs depending on the query generation technique, reflecting the distinct nature of the queries produced by our generative approach versus LLM Query. Secondly, the Query vs. Hard Negative similarities (orange bars) indicate that all three methods successfully identify or create negative examples that possess considerable semantic closeness to the query, confirming their status as challenging distractors rather than simple, easily distinguishable negatives.

The most significant distinction, however, lies in the Passage vs. Hard Negative similarities (grey bars). The negative example produced by our synthetic generation method exhibits markedly lower similarity to the original source passage compared to the negatives retrieved from the document collection via BM25 or cross-encoder re-ranking. The BM25-retrieved negative, in particular, displays a very high degree of similarity to the source passage, likely indicating significant lexical or fine-grained topical overlap found within the corpus. In contrast, the synthetically generated negative, while semantically related to the query, is considerably less similar to the specific content of the original passage.

This pattern strongly corroborates the design of our proposed method, where negative examples are constructed based solely on the query text, independent of the original passage content. It highlights a fundamental difference in the nature of the negative examples used for training: while the corpus-mining approaches find existing documents that closely resemble the positive passage, our approach generates novel distractors focused on query relevance. This analysis underscores that although the different pipelines yield equivalent final retrieval performance (as shown in Section
5
), the underlying training data possesses demonstrably distinct characteristics, reinforcing the viability of achieving strong results without direct corpus interaction during negative selection.
