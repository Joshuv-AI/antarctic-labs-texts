Assessing awareness of digital literacy among medical students in Azad Jammu and Kashmir
Authors: Hifza Ishtiaq, Brekhna Jamil, Nowshad Asim, Bushra Sherwani, Hina Munir, Humera Adeeb
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDigital literacy is a critical competency in 21st-century medical education, enabling students to leverage technology (e.g., electronic health records, virtual simulations) for clinical practice, research, and lifelong learning. However, resource-constrained regions like Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK), Pakistan, with limited digital infrastructure and training capacity, lack data on students’ digital readiness.ObjectivesTo assess digital literacy awareness among undergraduate medical students in AJK and examine associations between digital literacy and student demographics.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 377 students from four medical colleges in AJK using a validated 24 item questionnaire covering seven digital literacy domains. Stratified random sampling ensured representative demographics. Data were analyzed using SPSS v26, with descriptive statistics, independent t-tests, and one-way ANOVA and Pearson correlations.ResultsStudents reported high confidence in basic skills such as learning new technologies (73.2%) and using communication platforms (57.8%) but limited awareness of programming (11.1%) and cybersecurity (43%). Male students had higher beginner-level scores than females (24.83 ± 4.51 vs. 23.48 ± 4.30; p = 0.003). First-year students demonstrated significantly higher advanced-level awareness compared with final-year students (36.88 ± 5.71 vs. 33.67 ± 6.05; p = 0.014). Age showed a small negative correlation with beginner (r = − 0.125) and advanced (r = − 0.116) scores. Male, first-year, and younger students performed better in digital literacy assessments (p < 0.05).ConclusionMedical students in AJK possess foundational digital literacy and ethical awareness but lack proficiency in advanced domains such as programming, AI, and cybersecurity. Tailored curricular reforms and institutional support are urgently needed to prepare future physicians for technology-driven healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-08357-3.

Introduction

The 21 st century has brought a technological revolution that is reshaping the healthcare landscape. As such, medical professionals must now be competent not only in clinical knowledge but also in digital literacy [1]. Digital literacy encompasses a broad set of skills, including information literacy, communication and collaboration through digital platforms, digital content creation, safety, problem-solving, and awareness of digital tools such as artificial intelligence (AI) and cybersecurity frameworks [2].

In medical education, digital literacy is increasingly recognized as an essential component of competency-based training. The World Federation for Medical Education (WFME) and the World Health Organization (WHO) both advocate for integrating digital competencies into undergraduate medical curricula to prepare students for evolving digital clinical environments [3]. In technologically advanced countries, such integration has significantly enhanced medical training outcomes, improving access to medical knowledge, patient care systems, and interdisciplinary collaboration. However, low and middle income countries (LMICs) face unique challenges in achieving such integration due to limited infrastructure, insufficient training opportunities, and lack of institutional policy [4].

Pakistan, an LMIC, exemplifies these challenges. Despite policy frameworks that encourage digital innovation in education, many institutions lack the resources and trained faculty to implement effective digital literacy programs [5]. In particular, regions like Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK) lag behind more urbanized provinces due to underdeveloped educational technology infrastructure and constrained budgets. AJK, home to four public-sector medical colleges, serves a critical population yet remains unassessed in terms of its digital literacy preparedness. Comparable challenges are reported in South Asian countries with similar socio-economic contexts. In South Asia, medical and nursing students show familiarity with digital platforms but limited exposure to advanced domains such as AI and cybersecurity due to poor infrastructure and uneven access [17]. Studies from rural India also reveal a digital divide, with students demonstrating basic digital literacy but lacking higher-order competencies, mirroring the situation in AJK [18].

Globally, research shows that digital competence among medical students varies based on demographic variables such as gender, age, academic year, and institutional support [6]. For example, male students often report greater familiarity with technology, while senior students may have more academic exposure to digital tools. However, there is also evidence that first-year students may exhibit higher digital awareness due to their more recent engagement with school-level information technology education.

This study addresses this gap by assessing digital literacy awareness in AJK’s medical students and identifying demographic factors that could inform targeted interventions.

This paper presents findings from a cross-sectional survey conducted among 377 medical students from four institutions in AJK. The results offer valuable insights into current digital literacy levels, highlight areas requiring urgent curricular reform, and contribute to the broader discourse on digital transformation in LMIC medical education settings.

This study is conceptually informed by established models of digital literacy, particularly Eshet-Alkalai’s (2004) and Martin’s (2006) frameworks, which view digital literacy as a multidimensional construct. These models guided the survey design and interpretation of results [13, 20]. Additionally, Davis’s Technology Acceptance Model (1989) suggests that perceived usefulness and ease of use drive technology adoption, offering insight into why younger and male students (with greater exposure and confidence) might show higher digital competence. These frameworks informed our survey design and interpretation of competencies across domains such as software use, cybersecurity, programming, AI literacy, and communication technologies [21].

Study objectives

This study aimed to assess digital literacy awareness among undergraduate medical students in AJK and examine associations between digital literacy and student demographics.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a descriptive cross-sectional study conducted across four recognized medical colleges in Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK), Pakistan: Azad Jammu & Kashmir Medical College, Muzaffarabad, Poonch Medical College, Rawalakot, Mohtarma Benazir Bhutto Shaheed Medical College, Mirpur, and Mohi-ud-Din Islamic Medical College, Mirpur.

Study population and sample size

The study population consisted of undergraduate medical students enrolled in the above institutions. Sample size was calculated using Cochran’s formula, assuming 50% prevalence, 95% confidence level, and 5% margin of error. The adjusted sample size was 323; to account for non-response, 384 students were targeted. A total of 377 valid responses were included in the analysis.

Sampling technique

Stratified random sampling was used, with proportional representation based on academic year and gender.

Data collection tool

Digital literacy was assessed using a validated 24-item questionnaire adapted from Aydınlar et al. (2024), covering seven domains: Software & Multimedia, Hardware & Technical Problem Solving, Communication & Collaboration, Ethics, Security, Artificial Intelligence, and Interest-Knowledge. The instrument was reviewed by experts and piloted (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.87). Responses were collected on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree).

A descriptive cross-sectional study design was adopted to quantitatively assess digital literacy awareness among undergraduate medical students enrolled in four recognized medical colleges in Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK), Pakistan. This non-experimental design enabled the researchers to evaluate the current level of digital competencies in a defined population at a single point in time. The approach was guided by a quantitative methodology, facilitating statistical interpretation of data collected through a standardized, structured questionnaire.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected using Google Forms distributed via institutional communication channels. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Incomplete responses were excluded.

Ethical considerations

Ethical Clearance was obtained from KMU-IHPER Ethics Board (Ref: 1–12/IHPER/MHPE/KMU/25 − 11 dated: 10-04-2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS v26. Descriptive statistics (frequencies, means, SDs) summarized participant responses. Independent-samples t-tests assessed gender differences, one-way ANOVA compared academic years, and Pearson correlations examined associations with age. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Digital literacy assessment

Beginner-level awareness

Students reported relatively high confidence in foundational digital skills. A majority agreed that they could learn new technologies easily (73.2%) and communicate using platforms such as Zoom or Microsoft Teams (57.8%). Awareness of basic hardware components was reported by 58.8% of respondents. However, only 11.1% indicated familiarity with any programming language, and 35.3% felt capable of troubleshooting routine technical issues (Table 2).

Digital Literacy Assessment

Intermediate-Level awareness

Moderate levels of awareness were observed in information searching and digital collaboration. More than half of participants (51.2%) reported confidence in conducting online searches, and 52.5% agreed that ICT supported peer collaboration. Awareness of professional networking tools (e.g., LinkedIn) was limited, with a substantial proportion reporting neutral responses. Awareness of AI-related concepts was reported by 46.9% of students, suggesting general familiarity but not necessarily domain-specific understanding.

Advanced-Level awareness

Awareness of digital ethics was comparatively higher than awareness of cybersecurity or data-handling concepts. Over half of the respondents stated that they avoided digital plagiarism (53.8%) and were aware of ethical considerations related to patient data (53.1%). Moderate familiarity with digital copyright laws (39.3%) and data protection legislation (41.1%) was observed.

In contrast, cybersecurity-related competencies were limited. Only 43.0% of students reported awareness of internet fraud, and 36.9% understood how mobile applications store and process data. Indicators of interest and engagement with technology were relatively higher, with more than half reporting that they follow emerging technologies (55.2%) and routinely verify the reliability of online medical information (53.0%).

Associations between digital literacy and sociodemographic variables

Gender

Male students had significantly higher beginner-level scores than female students (24.83 ± 4.51 vs. 23.48 ± 4.30; p = 0.003). No significant gender differences were observed at intermediate or advanced competency levels.

Academic year

There was a statistically significant difference in advanced-level awareness across academic years (p = 0.014). First-year students scored higher (36.88 ± 5.71) than final-year students (33.67 ± 6.05). Differences in intermediate-level scores approached significance but did not meet the threshold (p = 0.07).

Age

Age demonstrated a small but statistically significant negative correlation with beginner-level (r = − 0.125; p < 0.05) and advanced-level (r = − 0.116; p < 0.05) competencies. There was no significant correlation with intermediate-level competencies.

Multivariate analysis

Multiple linear regression, adjusting for gender, age, and academic year, identified male gender and earlier academic year as significant predictors of higher overall digital literacy scores (p < 0.05). Age remained a weak but significant negative predictor. Detailed results are shown in Tables 3 and 4.

Discussion

This section examines how sociodemographic factors such as age, gender, and year of study influenced digital literacy levels among medical students in Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK). The findings suggest that medical students in AJK demonstrate moderate levels of digital literacy, particularly in basic digital tasks and communication technologies [10]. This is consistent with global reports, which found that while students are generally comfortable with using digital platforms, significant gaps persist in higher-order competencies such as cybersecurity, ethical digital conduct, and AI [12, 13].

However, in contrast to students in high-income settings, who increasingly receive structured digital health training [10], the students in AJK appear to rely largely on self-learning and informal exposure. This highlights a structural deficit in formal curricular integration rather than just individual learner gaps.

At the beginner level, students displayed high self-reported confidence in basic technological skills (e.g., using Zoom, Microsoft Teams). Yet, familiarity with deeper ICT concepts such as programming languages and troubleshooting was notably weak. This reflects a known phenomenon described by Ng [14], where students can be proficient technology users but not necessarily developers or critical problem-solvers. The gender difference observed, where male students scored higher, aligns with UNESCO [6] and literature [19], who reported persistent digital access and confidence gaps in conservative and resource-limited settings. These findings reinforce the importance of targeted interventions aimed at female students to bridge systemic inequities rather than assuming access alone will equalize skill acquisition.

The study found strong peer collaboration skills but weak AI awareness among students. This duality is significant while collaborative competencies align with trends reported in the literature [21], limited conceptual grasp of AI suggests that medical curricula in AJK have not kept pace with global technological shifts.

Encouragingly, students demonstrated strong awareness of digital ethics. However, moderate proficiency in cybersecurity practices, particularly data storage and privacy, raises significant concerns in the context of rising telehealth and electronic health records usage. This pattern mirrors findings from the literature that warned that ethical intention does not always translate into practical data security competence among future healthcare providers [20].

Interestingly, the higher digital literacy scores among first year students suggest that digital nativity is a stronger predictor of competence than accumulated years in medical school [20]. This finding contradicts assumptions that seniority alone results in greater digital mastery and highlights the dynamic evolution of student digital readiness with incoming cohorts.

Pakistan’s national strategies, such as the Digital Pakistan Vision and the Telemedicine Project, focus heavily on infrastructure development [13]. However, technological infrastructure without parallel investments in human capital especially structured digital literacy training produces uneven outcomes [14].

The current findings confirm this imbalance despite access to devices and internet, critical digital competencies remain underdeveloped among medical students. This suggests that policy success metrics must move beyond “access” and “connectivity” to include “competency development” as a core indicator.

Internationally, digital literacy is now embedded as a professional competency within undergraduate medical education [4, 10]. While institutions in Europe and North America report rising AI, cybersecurity, and ethics integration into curricula, AJK’s medical colleges are still early in this transformation [12, 13].

Similar patterns are evident across South Asia. In Bangladesh and Nepal, students show basic digital familiarity but limited advanced skills due to curricular and infrastructural constraints [17, 22]. Rural India also demonstrates a digital divide, paralleling AJK and highlighting regional challenges in preparing health professionals for technology-driven education [18].

The readiness among AJK students to engage with digital platforms, despite curricular gaps, represents an opportunity: educational policymakers must leverage this intrinsic motivation through structured, locally contextualized digital training modules rather than relying on passive technology adoption.

Conclusion

Medical students in Azad Jammu and Kashmir possess moderate awareness in basic digital tasks and show strong interest in technology and digital ethics, and weaker competencies in advanced domains, such as cybersecurity, programming, and AI. Younger and early-year students reported higher awareness across several domains. These findings highlight gaps that may be addressed through structured digital literacy training within undergraduate medical curricula. Further research incorporating performance-based measures is recommended to complement self-reported assessments and provide a more comprehensive understanding of digital competence in this context.

Limitations of the study

The study relied entirely on self-reported awareness rather than objective, performance-based assessments. This may introduce social desirability bias and could overestimate or underestimate actual competence. As a cross-sectional study, findings represent a single point in time and cannot capture how digital literacy evolves across medical training years. The sample was limited to medical colleges in Azad Jammu and Kashmir (AJK); therefore, results may not be generalizable to other provinces, private-sector institutions, or international LMIC contexts with differing infrastructure. Indeterminant variables such as prior ICT exposure, device ownership, and internet access were not assessed and may influence digital literacy outcomes. Finally, the study did not include qualitative methods, which could have provided deeper contextual insights into students’ digital learning experiences.

Recommendations

To overcome these limitations, future research should consider longitudinal studies to track the evolution of digital literacy throughout medical education. Intervention-based studies are also recommended, particularly those that implement and evaluate digital training modules to support curriculum development. Furthermore, comparative studies across various regions or countries could provide broader insights into national or global trends in digital literacy. Finally, adopting a mixed-methods approach, incorporating qualitative interviews or case studies, would offer a more nuanced understanding of students’ attitudes, challenges, and motivations.
