Regular walking is associated with greater lumbar spine bone mineral density in elderly men with adequate vitamin D levels
Authors: Mohamed Zouch, Anis Zribi, Walid Ibrahim Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sport practices play a major role in reducing age-related bone loss and preventing osteoporosis and associated fractures in later life. Normal vitamin D levels are crucial in maintaining bone mass and reducing bone turnover, especially in elderly adults. This study investigates whether regular walking combined with normal vitamin D3 levels improves areal bone mineral density (aBMD) and bone mineral content (BMC) at weight-bearing and non—weight-bearing bones in older men compared to controls. Sixteen individuals who walked regularly (8–12 h/week) and fourteen controls (less than 2 h/week) aged between 60 and 75 years were recruited. Bone parameters (aBMD, BMC) were assessed using dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), bone turnover and various hormonal markers were measured. vitamin D3 levels were higher in walkers than controls, other hormonal and bone markers did not show any significant differences between the two groups. Walkers exhibited greater BMC and aBMD in the total hip, lumbar spine, dominant upper limb, and dominant and non-dominant whole and distal radius. However, exclusively the lumbar spine aBMD remained significantly higher in walkers after controlling for vitamin D3 levels and total sports participation. These findings suggest that regular walking may be associated with enhanced lumbar spine bone mineral density and maintained bone health in this vulnerable population. providing a strong argument for making such activities easily accessible.

Introduction

Physical activity has been recognized as a significant determinant of stimulation of bone formation by osteogenic action, particularly when it involves impacts transmitted to the skeleton1 by reducing bone loss related to age and preventing osteoporosis and associated fractures later in life2. High-impact loading activities promote bone formation stimulating osteogenic pathways and enhancing skeletal strength3–5. However, these activities are particularly poorly suited for people over 60 years of age, due to an increased risk of injury and cardiovascular strain. In contrast, regular walking, a moderate-impact sport, could benefit the skeleton. It helps enhance muscle strength and coordination reducing the risk of falls and related hip fractures6. Furthermore, walking is a low-stress and low-cost activity appropriate for older adults that may provide sufficient mechanical loading to maintain bone mineral density (BMD) in loaded regions such as the hip and lumbar spine7. Beyond mechanical loading, vitamin D3 plays an essential role in calcium homeostasis and bone mineralization. Adequate levels of vitamin D plays a crucial role in maintaining health bone mass and reducing bone turnover, especially in elderly adults8. In contrast, Inadequate Vitamin D are associated with increased bone loss and increased risk of bone fracture related to the fall rate9 moreover, vitamin D deficiency is widespread and represents a global health problem10. Therefore, it is assumed that people with low vitamin D3 levels have lower physical performance and more significant deterioration in physical function11.

Several studies showed a dynamic interaction between regular physical activity and adequate vitamin D levels, suggesting their combined effect may have synergistic benefits for bone health and functional mobility in older adults11]– [12. However, this promising area of study is limited by a narrow focus, with much of the evidence drawn from studies primarily involving female participants or those using vitamin D supplementation rather than natural sources13]– [14. As a result, there remains a significant gap in understanding how habitual physical activity interacts with naturally occurring vitamin D levels, as measured by serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3, in diverse, free-living populations across genders, ages, and lifestyles15. A significant gap remains in our understanding of the relationship between routine physical activity and vitamin D levels (as measured by serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3) across diverse populations. Furthermore, research studying the combined effect of regular walking and serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 levels on bone mineral density and bone turnover in older men remain limited. Elucidating this interaction could pave the way for non-pharmacological, lifestyle-based strategies to preserve bone health in this overlooked population. Therefore, the current cross-sectional study aimed to verify whether the combined of this sport and adequate vitamin D3 level might have optimal osteogenic potential and be expressed by greater values of areal bone mineral density and bone mineral content in loaded bone sites in elderly men.

Materials and methods

Subjects

Thirty healthy, age-matched elderly Caucasian men (60–75 years) recruited from local walking clubs and community centers. Sixteen participants were regular walkers (≥ 8–12 h/week for at least two years), while fourteen served as non-active controls (< 2 h/week). All subjects with bone disease, endocrine disorders, chronic alcoholism, active smoking (> 5 cigarettes/day), or previous six months treatments affecting bone metabolism (e.g., corticosteroids, bisphosphonates, anabolics) were excluded. These criteria reduced the risk of confounding physiological factors and ensured homogeneity.

All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the [Declaration of Helsinki / national/institutional ethical standards]. All participants provided their signed informed consent.

All measured parameters and questionnaires were completed by all recruited participants from the second week of March 2022 to the end of Jun 2022.

Anthropometric parameters

Weight assessment was performed by a Seca electronic scale (Seca; model #770) to the nearest 0.1 kg. Height assessment was performed by a wall-mounted stadiometer (Seca; model S-220) to the nearest 0.001 m. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated by the following formula: BMI = Weight/ Height².

Bone measurements and body composition

Body composition parameters: fat mass (FM, kg), lean mass (LM, kg), the areal bone mineral density (aBMD, g/cm²), bone mineral content (BMC, g) of the whole body, lumbar spine (L2-L4), total hip, dominant (D) and non-dominant (ND) radius and upper and lower limbs, were measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA, Hologic Delphi 4500 QDR, 70453 w).

The aBMD and BMC variation coefficient, evaluated by duplicate measurements in ten older men was less than 1% in the total hip, radius, lumbar spine, and whole body and 2,1% in the upper and lower limbs.

Calcium intake

The dietary calcium intake of each participant was measured by Fardelonne’s self-administered method of recording food for a typical day. The standard calcium value in elderly adults is between 1200 and 1500 mg/day16.

Physical activity parameters

Current and previous physical activity was determined by quantification of physical activity (QUANTAP) interview-administered survey, a computer-assisted tool designed to quantify physical activity over a lifetime17.

We quantified the duration of annual current sports practice, the duration of total sports practice, the duration of total physical activity, the duration of previous total sports practice, the duration of previous total physical activity, the duration of total previous occupational activity, the energy expenditure of total sports practice, the energy expenditure of total physical activity and the energy expenditure of total occupational activity.

Biochemical and bone markers study

All subjects have been performed a phosphocalcic analysis: blood and urinary calcium, blood phosphorus, 25-hydroxycholecalciferol (vitamin D3), parathyroid hormone (PTH), estradiol, leptin, total testosterone and sex steroid transport protein in the blood (sex hormone binding globulin: SHBG). The subject with endocrinopathy is excluded from the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Statistica software (version 6.0, 2001; Statsoft, Maisons-Alfort, France). Data were expressed as means ± SD and differences were considered significant at the p < .05 level.

Age, anthropometric, sports, physical and occupational activity, energy expenditure, dietary calcium intake, LM, FM, aBMD, BMC, biochemical and bone markers study data were analyzed by unpaired samples Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test, depending on the distribution of the variables. Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Lilliefors tests were used to identify the distribution type. In the second step, analyses of covariance were performed using occupational activity or total sports practices and vitamin D3 as covariates to compare aBMD, BMC, bone markers between groups.

Discussion

This study investigated the combined effect of regular walking and serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D3 levels on bone mineral density and bone turnover in elderly men. Our findings revealed that habitual walkers exhibited higher aBMD and BMC values at multiple skeletal sites compared with controls, but after adjustment for total sports participation and vitamin D₃ levels, only the lumbar spine difference remained significant. These observations highlight the site-specific effects of moderate physical activity and the potential modulatory role of vitamin D on skeletal health in aging men.

The persistence of lumbar spine BMD improvement aligns with evidence that the spine experiences repetitive axial and shear loading during gait, which produces cyclic compressive forces through the vertebral column19 (Zhang et al., 2024). Although walking generates vertical ground-reaction forces measured at ~ 1.2× body weight than running measured up to 2.5× body weight20, the frequency and directionality of loading may still activate trabecular bone remodeling in metabolically active vertebrae21. In contrast, cortical-rich sites such as the hip or forearm require higher strain rates to trigger significant osteogenesis22 (Ng et al., 2023). These biomechanical factors likely explain the seletive preservation of lumbar aBMD.

Our findings are in accordance with Cheng et al. (2022), who reported that walking in older adults is associated with higher spinal BMD, especially when combined with sufficient vitamin D levels and outdoor activity23. A 12-month longitudinal study demonstrated that regular walking increased aBMD in the lumbar spine and femoral neck among postmenopausal women, suggesting that even low-impact activities can support skeletal health in this population24. Similarly, a higher aBMD of the lumbar spine and trochanter was observed in postmenopausal women who engaged in more than 90 min of moderate physical activity per week (walking, aerobic, etc.) than in sedentary women25. Moreover, Jianzhong et al. (2023) showed that physical activity ranging from moderate to vigorous is positively associated with total spine aBMD in postmenopausal women and that high levels of physical activity significantly increase total spine aBMD26. However, Lim et al. (2023) showed no significant difference in the aBMD of the total hip, femoral neck, lumbar spine and total body between the moderate exercise group and older sedentary people27. In a meta-analysis of men and women over the age of 50 who practiced walking, Palombaro et al. (2005) showed that this sport has no positive effect on the aBMD of the femoral neck and calcaneus, and therefore cannot limit age-related bone loss28.

Contradictory findings have been reported regarding the osteogenic capacity of walking. Variations in participant age, walking duration and intensity, vitamin D status, and baseline physical activity levels likely explain these discrepancies. Our study population (60–75 years) represents a cohort where even modest mechanical stimuli may preserve trabecular mass, especially under conditions of hormonal stability and adequate nutrient supply.

Participants in the walking group had significantly higher serum 25(OH)D₃ levels, consistent with prior studies showing that outdoor physical activity enhances dermal synthesis of vitamin D via UVB exposure29. In contrast, the control group had relatively low physical condition, which is responsible for lower vitamin D3 levels29. However, the fluctuations in vitamin D levels remain of low amplitude as the PTH does not vary30.

The total duration of occupational activity was higher in walkers than in controls. However, unlike controls, most walkers performed manual work throughout their lives. We considered it necessary to correct the bone parameters based on the total duration of the occupational activity. We found that significant differences in the bone densitometric parameters remained unchanged, similar to without adjustment. This result exhibited a non-persistent effect of occupational activities having similar levels of moderate physical exertion in both walkers and controls, which could not differentiate bone density and turnover between the two groups. Contrary to our findings, there is evidence that occupational activity positively affects bone health. A few studies have reported a positive association between physical activity in the workplace and aBMD in the hip and femur31,32. These findings contrast other studies that provided no evidence that occupational activity is associated with higher hip aBMD in both sexes33,34 and positive associations only with standing at the age of 30 years33.

Based on the present study findings, our population groups do not show significant differences in daily calcium intake, which is considered insufficient compared to the recommended intake for men over 60 years (1500 mg/d) 35 but is comparable to the intake found in several studies in elderly peoples36,37.

Leptin concentrations, a hormone produced by adipocytes that regulates body weight by exerting negative feedback on food intake, are higher in control subjects than in walkers. This difference could be due to current sports activity, which is higher in walkers, and/or to fat mass, which tends to be higher in the controls. This result confirms the data from the literature showing that leptin levels are lower in exercised individuals (long-distance runners) than sedentary people, and these levels correlate with body fat38.

We found that bone markers adjusted or no for total sports practice and vitamin were similar between walkers and controls. This result disagrees with a previous study that has shown that vitamin D3 supplementation in athletes leads to stimulating bone remodeling and has preventive effects on bone under intensive physical effort39. These differences in results could be due to the difference in methodology. However, our population is older and has normal vitamin D, without taking vitamin D3 supplementation dose.

Our study has some limitations. First, the modest sample size of n = 30 reduces statistical power, as well as generalizability. Second, the cross-sectional study design eliminates the possibility of making causal inferences about the connection between walking activity and bone health. Third, dietary calcium exposure was estimated with a validated assessment, yet overall dietary control with standardized monitoring of meals was not enacted. Fourth, the data collection took place during March to June of 2022, thus no account can be made fully to exclude seasonal variability in exposure to sunlight with resultant vitamin D synthesis.

Conclusion

This study offers useful information about the association between frequent walking, within-range vitamin D3 levels, and lumbar spine aBMD in elderly men. Despite the cross-sectional design, the findings support walking as a beneficial activity for maintaining bone health in this vulnerable population, providing a strong argument for making such activities easily accessible. A follow-up longitudinal study with larger populations and detailed dietary intake assessment, sun exposure, and additional bone-loading activities deserves consideration to establish causality and shed additional light on mechanisms involved.
