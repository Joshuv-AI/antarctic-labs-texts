Efficacy of Antibiotics for Chronic Low Back Pain With Disc Herniation
Authors: Flavia M. Cicuttini, Anita E. Wluka, Feng Pan, Richard O’Sullivan, Karin Leder, Allen C. Cheng, Donna M. Urquhart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionAre antibiotics an effective and safe intervention for reducing pain in individuals with chronic low back pain and disc herniation compared with placebo?FindingsThis randomized clinical trial of 170 participants found that treatment with amoxicillin-clavulanate, 500 mg/125 mg twice per day for 90 days, did not result in greater pain reduction than placebo at 12 months.MeaningThese findings suggest that antibiotics should not be used in the treatment of chronic low back pain with disc herniation.

Introduction

Low back pain (LBP) is a global epidemic and the greatest cause of disability burden worldwide.1 Although a variety of treatments are available, evidence-based options are limited and the use of harmful treatments is increasing.2 Clinical guidelines have recommended that pharmacologic treatments be considered if the response to first-line treatment options is inadequate.3,4 Antibiotic therapy is one pharmacologic treatment for chronic LBP that has received substantial interest.5,6,7 This attention has resulted from low-virulent bacteria being identified in the spinal discs of individuals with LBP8,9 and clinical trials showing a substantial effect of antibiotics on chronic LBP with disc herniation and Modic changes (bone changes detected on magnetic resonance imaging [MRI]).10,11 Moreover, recent reviews6,12 have suggested that patients may benefit from antibiotics when other therapies have failed and the risks outweigh the benefits, and internet searches and discussions about antibiotic therapy for LBP on social media forums are substantial and ongoing.13,14

However, the efficacy of antibiotics for LBP remains unclear and highly controversial. The results of clinical trials are conflicting because they have examined different types of antibiotics and different patient subgroups with LBP. Two trials10,11 examining amoxicillin-clavulanate found a clinically significant improvement in pain and disability,10 but a trial15 using amoxicillin, which has a narrower antibacterial coverage than amoxicillin-clavulanate combined, found no benefit. Although all trials recruited participants with chronic LBP and disc herniation, there were differences in patient subgroups based on the type of Modic changes present.10,15

Uncertainty about the efficacy of antibiotics for LBP, combined with limited effective therapies, is driving their ongoing use, despite the potential for harm, because increased antibiotic prescribing could substantially contribute to antimicrobial resistance, the leading cause of death globally.16,17 There is an urgent need to address the limitations of previous trials and determine the efficacy of antibiotics for chronic LBP. Thus, the aim of this trial is to examine whether amoxicillin-clavulanate, a broad-spectrum antibiotic, is effective in reducing pain at 12 months compared with an identical placebo in a broad patient group with chronic LBP and disc herniation. In addition, we aimed to examine whether amoxicillin-clavulanate is effective in a subgroup of individuals with disc herniation and Modic changes.

Methods

This study was a community-based, double-blind, placebo-controlled randomized clinical trial with a 2-arm, parallel group, superiority design. The trial was registered at the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry before recruitment, and trial reporting was guided by the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guidelines.18 The study was conducted through the School of Public Health and Preventive Medicine, Monash University. The trial was performed in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki,35 and ethics approval was obtained from the Alfred Hospital and Monash University human research ethics committees. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol has been previously published19 and is available in Supplement 1.

The trial procedures were modified due to the COVID-19 pandemic. Victoria was under COVID-19 restrictions during this time, which substantially limited community movement and access to health services, requiring the study to be conducted via telemedicine. Participants who had been recruited continued in the trial, with study assessments and reviews performed by telehealth, and the recruitment of new participants was paused during severe restrictions but resumed once these were lifted.

Participants

We recruited male and female participants aged 18 to 60 years with chronic LBP, defined as pain between the lower borders of the rib cage and the gluteal folds that had persisted for more than 3 months20 and with the presence of a disc herniation on MRI, between January 20, 2016, and May 27, 2021. Disc herniation was defined as disruption of the annular fibers, leading to localized displacement of disc material beyond the limits of the intervertebral disc space at lumbar spinal levels. MRI of the lumbar spine was conducted using a 3.0-T magnetic resonance unit and sagittal T1-, T2-, and fat suppressed T2-weighted scanning was performed. Exclusion criteria included the following: (1) specific pathological entity, such as metastasis, osteoporosis, or fractures; (2) any contraindication or allergy to antibiotics; (3) antibiotic therapy in the past 3 months; (4) a compromised immune system; (5) any infection, including osteomyelitis, systemic infection, or an infection requiring ongoing antibiotics; (6) kidney disease; (7) planned surgery in the next 6 months; (8) major coexisting illness, such as a neurologic, cardiovascular, or psychiatric condition, which might confound assessment of function; (9) another significant musculoskeletal condition, such as severe hip or knee osteoarthritis or recent fracture or ligament injury; (10) pregnancy, planning or trying to become pregnant, or breastfeeding; or (11) inability to give informed consent, including individuals who were unable to read, speak, or understand English.

Potential participants were screened over the phone using a questionnaire and then attended an assessment at the School of Public Health and Preventive Medicine, Monash University, to provide informed consent and further examination of their eligibility. A full blood examination and electrolyte and liver function tests were performed to exclude conditions such as liver impairment or renal failure, and a C-reactive protein blood test was performed to detect the presence of inflammation. A lumbar spine MRI was also performed at baseline to determine whether a disc herniation was present and at baseline and 12 months to assess the presence of Modic changes.

Randomization and Blinding

Participants were allocated in a 1:1 ratio to the intervention or placebo group using computer-generated random numbers prepared by a statistician who was not otherwise involved in the trial. Allocation concealment was ensured via a central automated allocation procedure, with security in place to ensure allocation data could not be accessed or influenced by any person. Allocation concealment and double-blinding were ensured by medications being dispensed by a Therapeutic Goods Administration–licensed facility that specializes in product manufacturing of investigational products for the pharmaceutical industry, use of a placebo tablet that was identical to the study medication, and questionnaires being administered by research assistants who were blinded to group allocation. The trial was double-blinded, with participants, investigators, and statisticians blinded to treatment allocation.

Intervention

Participants in the intervention arm received the amoxicillin-clavulanate (500 mg/125 mg) tablets twice per day for 90 days, and those in the control group received microcrystalline cellulose tablets (Pharmaceutical Packaging Professionals Pty Ltd), which were identical in size, color, coating, and packaging to the active tablets. All participants were provided with usual care by their treating health practitioners.

Outcomes

Outcome data were collected from the beginning of randomization on January 20, 2016, until the end of the 12-month follow-up on July 26, 2022. The primary outcome measure was pain intensity measured using the Low Back Pain Rating Scale (LBPRS) and 100-mm visual analog scale (VAS). The LBPRS, a valid measure of LBP,21 consists of three 11-point box scales assessing current pain and the worst and average pain within the last 2 weeks. LBPRS scores range from 0 to 10, with 0 indicating no pain and 10 indicating worst pain imaginable.

The secondary outcome measure of disability was assessed using the Roland Morris Disability Questionnaire (RMDQ), which is a validated instrument designed to assess self-rated disability caused by LBP.22 We examined work absenteeism and hindrance using the Short Form Health and Labour Questionnaire, a validated questionnaire used to assess productivity losses due to injury involved in paid or unpaid work.23 Global improvement was measured using a 6-point Likert scale, ranging from much worse to completely recovered, with higher scores indicating greater improvement.24 General health status, depression, and fear of movement or reinjury were measured using the EuroQoL 5-dimension 5-level (EQ-5D-5L) instrument,25 Beck Depression Inventory,26 and Tampa scale,27 respectively.

Although the outcomes presented in the current study were prespecified in the published protocol, the primary outcome was refined from the original, unpublished protocol to focus on pain only. Disability and work outcomes, initially included within the primary outcome set, were reclassified as secondary outcomes. This change was made to minimize issues associated with multiple primary end points and provide a clearer interpretation of the main treatment effect.

Other Assessments

Height, weight, and body mass index (calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters) were measured at baseline. We recorded any associated compensation claims and data on medication use.

Adverse Events

Adverse events were monitored during the study with regular blood tests to assess participants’ kidney and liver function at 2, 6, and 12 weeks and by participants reporting any adverse events to the research staff if they occurred. Serious adverse events, defined as any untoward medical occurrence in a participant that results in death, life-threatening event, hospitalization, persistent or substantial disability or incapacity, congenital birth defect, or medically important events that based on clinical judgment pose a substantial hazard and might require intervention to prevent one of the study outcomes,28 were determined by study physicians and reported to the ethics committees.

Statistical Analysis

We calculated that with 85 patients in each arm of the trial there would be 90% power to detect a difference of 0.50 SDs in our primary outcome measure of pain intensity. For the LBPRS and assuming a between-person SD of 3.0,10 a difference of 1.5 units could be detected with 90% power. A clinically significant difference in pain intensity was regarded as being at least 1.5 to 2.0 units.29,30 All analyses were adjusted for the baseline value of the relevant outcome variable, which would further increase the power by an amount depending on the size of the baseline to follow-up correlation in the outcome.

Summary statistics comparing randomized arms at baseline were tabulated. Intention-to-treat analyses of primary and secondary continuous outcomes were performed by linear regression adjusting for the baseline of the outcome variable. Logistic regression was performed for binary outcomes. We used regression models to examine the effect of Modic change status, as per the primary analysis, adjusting for baseline values of the outcome. Multiple imputation by chained equations31 was used to impute missing pain, disability, and work data at 3 and 12 months by treatment arm. SPSS Statistics, version 22.0 (IBM Inc) was used for statistical analyses. A 2-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was performed from June to November 2024.

Adherence to trial medication, defined as taking more than 80% of the prescribed medication, was assessed by pill count and self-report and was used to define treatment received. Given that there can be potential bias due to postrandomization nonadherence with a per-protocol analysis, we performed complier average causal effect (CACE) analyses using instrumental variable methods.32 Randomization was used as an instrument for treatment received, thereby preserving the benefits of randomization while appropriately accounting for nonadherence. Continuous outcomes were analyzed using instrumental-variable regression with 2-stage least squares, adjusted for the corresponding baseline outcome. Binary outcomes were analyzed using instrumental-variable probit regression; probit coefficients were transformed to approximate odds ratios (ORs) by multiplying by 1.6 and exponentiating.

Results

A total of 415 participants were assessed for eligibility, and 170 (41.0%) were randomly assigned to amoxicillin-clavulanate (n = 85) or placebo (n = 85) (Figure 1). The mean (SD) age of the participants was 44.4 (10.8) years, the mean (SD) body mass index was 28.7 (5.8) (calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters). Of the participants, 101 (59.4%) were male and 69 (40.6%) were female (Table 1).

There were no differences in pain intensity at 12 months for individuals with Modic changes for LBPRS (adjusted difference, 0.06; 95% CI, −0.79 to 0.91) or VAS (adjusted difference, −4.39; 95% CI, –14.40 to 5.66) scores or disability at 12 months on the RMDQ (adjusted difference, −0.95; 95% CI, −2.85 to 0.96) (eTables 3 and 4 in Supplement 2). This was also the case for the subgroup without Modic changes on MRI. The CACE analyses showed that there were no differences in pain intensity, disability, or work absence or hindrance in individuals who received the intervention when adherence was accounted for (eTable 5 in Supplement 2).

Three serious adverse events were reported: 2 (2.4%) in the amoxicillin-clavulanate group (an allergic reaction and radiculopathy) and 1 (1.2%) in the placebo group (diarrhea) (eTable 6 in Supplement 2). The allergic reaction and diarrhea were considered possibly related to treatment; both participants discontinued medication but completed follow-up, whereas the radiculopathy was deemed unrelated. Adverse events were reported in 34 participants (40.0%) in the amoxicillin-clavulanate group and 20 (23.5%) in the placebo group. Six participants withdrew (3 from each group), and 31 (18.2%) ceased use of medication, including 23 (27.1%) in the amoxicillin-clavulanate group (14 [16.5%] from adverse events) and 8 (9.4%) in the placebo group (2 [2.4%] from adverse events).

Of the 152 participants who completed 12-month outcomes, 128 (84.2%) provided data on medication adherence, with 78 providing pill count data and 50 self-report data. Based on pill count, 24 of 29 participants (82.8%) in the amoxicillin-clavulanate group and 39 of 49 participants (79.6%) in the placebo group were more than 80% adherent to the study medication, and 21 of 23 participants (91.3%) in the amoxicillin-clavulanate group and 17 of 18 participants (94.4%) from the placebo group were adherent based on self-report.

Discussion

This clinical trial showed no benefit of antibiotic treatment for chronic LBP with disc herniation. No significant improvements in pain intensity at 3 or 12 months were found for amoxicillin-clavulanate compared with placebo for individuals with chronic LBP and disc herniation or in the subgroup with type 1 or 2 Modic changes. Amoxicillin-clavulanate was generally well tolerated; however, 16.5% of participants in the intervention group compared with 2.4% in the placebo group ceased medication due to adverse events. This study showed that the use of antibiotics for chronic LBP with disc herniation, a common subgroup thought to benefit from this treatment, is not effective.

Our results do not support the findings of previous studies10,15 that examined the efficacy of antibiotics for CLBP. The study by Albert et al10 found amoxicillin-clavulanate to be more effective than placebo for chronic LBP with disc herniation and type 1 Modic changes. In contrast, Braten and colleagues15 found no effect of amoxicillin. Although this was a high-quality randomized clinical trial, the study’s findings may be explained by the lack of coverage of the relevant bacteria with amoxicillin. In our study, we used amoxicillin-clavulanate because it provides broad coverage and targets low virulent bacteria identified in spinal discs of people with LBP,8,9 but we found no significant effect for pain at 12 months.

In contrast to the study by Albert et al,10 we recruited participants with chronic LBP and disk herniation but did not specifically select for Modic changes. This is because a previous review33 found that disc herniation is the most common diagnosis among individuals with LBP and bacterial infection, and there is a relationship between the presence of bacteria and LBP with disc herniation. However, in a subgroup analysis, we found no effect of amoxicillin-clavulanate in individuals with type 1 or 2 Modic changes. On the basis of the numbers in our study with Modic changes, it is unlikely that we missed a clinically significant effect. Other differences between our population and that of Albert et al10 is that they recruited patients from 2 secondary spine centers in hospitals in Denmark and found very high adherence to amoxicillin-clavulanate treatment (>90%) compared with our study and the other trial by Al Falahi et al11 using this treatment. This is may limit the generalizability of these findings.

LBP is the greatest cause of disability burden worldwide, and effective management options are limited.1,34 Although the potential for antibiotics to be an effective treatment has been an appealing prospect, the results of this trial do not support this hypothesis. These findings are important in informing updates of clinical practice guidelines for LBP, which to date have been unable to make recommendations for or against the use of antibiotics. Moreover, given evidence from meta-analyses and reviews6,7,12 that suggest patients may benefit from antibiotics where the risks outweigh the benefits and the significant consumer interest in antibiotic treatment for LBP based on internet searches and social forums,13,14 these results are significant in informing both clinicians and patients and reducing the potential for real harm, especially antibiotic resistance. The World Health Organization considers antibiotic resistance to be a global health threat,17 with 5 million deaths per year estimated to be associated with drug-resistant bacteria.16

Limitations

The study has several limitations. We had planned to conduct nose, axilla, and groin swabs and collect fecal samples to examine antimicrobial resistance; however, these plans were ceased early in the study due to the difficulty in recruiting participants with the inclusion of these procedures. A total of 18 of the 170 participants (10.6%) did not provide data on the 12-month primary outcome, which could have introduced bias if the data were not missing at random. However, we performed multiple imputations to address this. Although participants were recruited in a variety of ways, most were community-dwelling individuals recruited via social media and advertising and other sources rather than patients presenting to hospitals or medical specialists with more complex chronic LBP. Although this study deviated from the original protocol, it is consistent with the published protocol in specifying a single primary outcome, allowing a focus on the main treatment effect. Our trial was not primarily powered to examine the efficacy of amoxicillin-clavulanate for individuals with Modic changes, and although this may have influenced our results, it was a planned before the trial commencement.

Conclusions

This randomized clinical trial examined whether amoxicillin-clavulanate is effective in reducing pain in a broad patient group with chronic LBP and disc herniation. The findings did not demonstrate the efficacy of antibiotics and provide important data to prevent their inappropriate and harmful use.
