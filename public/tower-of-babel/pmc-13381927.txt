Decreased CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte count predicts 28-day all-cause mortality in severe AECOPD patients admitted to the ICU: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Wenbo Zhang, Yuping Zhang, Wanqing Huo, Junlong Wang, Yifan Yao, Huaqi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Studies report a high 28-day all-cause mortality rate in patients with acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD). The specific prognostic value of lymphocyte subsets for critically ill patients remains unclear. We aimed to evaluate the association between lymphocyte subsets and 28-day all-cause mortality in patients with AECOPD admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). We conducted a retrospective cohort study on patients with severe AECOPD admitted to our hospital from January 2022 to December 2025. Comparisons were made between the survivors and non-survivors. We used restricted cubic splines, and Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves to describe the association between lymphocyte-related indicators and 28-day all-cause mortality. To prevent overfitting and robustly identify independent risk factors, Boruta feature selection and LASSO regression were sequentially utilized, followed by multivariable logistic regression to construct a prognostic model. The study enrolled 382 patients. During the 28-day follow-up, 115 (30.1%) patients died, and 267 (69.9%) survived. Among all examined immune cells, the CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte count demonstrated the strongest predictive performance. CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocytes exhibited the best predictive performance with a cutoff value of 157.61/µL. A prognostic model incorporating CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocytes and other key clinical features showed excellent discriminative ability with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.860. A marked decline in CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocytes (< 157.61/µL) is a significant and readily accessible independent risk factor for 28-day all-cause mortality in patients with AECOPD admitted to the ICU. A nomogram based on the multivariable logistic regression model provides a practical tool for early risk stratification and targeted immune monitoring in critical care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-48465-8.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a major cause of death worldwide1. It imposes a significant burden on the healthcare system. An acute exacerbation of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (AECOPD) is defined as an acute worsening of dyspnea, cough, or sputum production within 14 days. These events are commonly associated with increased inflammation caused by respiratory infections, air pollution, or other factors. Mortality is much higher in critically ill patients who require admission to an intensive care unit (ICU) as opposed to those in general wards. Studies show 2% to 19% of hospitalized AECOPD patients need ICU with an in-hospital death rate of 20% to 40%2. For those that are admitted to ICU, the 30-day death rate is extremely high and ranges from 16.9–48.8%3. Early and accurate identification of the high-risk patients is important. This facilitates the personalized allocation of medical resources and treatment strategies.

Lymphocytes play an important part in the host immune defense system. They maintain immune homeostasis against external pathogens. Under normal physiological conditions, lymphocytes circulate between the blood and lung tissue. This dynamic trafficking is regulated by chemokines and contributes to the maintenance of immune homeostasis4. During acute infections (e.g., early-stage AECOPD), lymphocytes rapidly migrate from the blood to sites of infection, such as the lungs. This is a vital adaptive defense response. Research has shown that in chronic inflammatory diseases such as in the case of the lungs (COPD) this migration pattern changes. This results in the abnormal accumulation and functional exhaustion of effector T cells in the lungs5. With critical illness or persistent Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS), this defence mechanism may fail. A cytokine storm can induce widespread pathological apoptosis, which results in a rapid decline in circulating lymphocyte counts6. This persistent depletion impairs the body’s ability to fight off infections. It is also strongly associated with multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS) and adverse clinical outcome7. Lymphopenia is also independently associated with poor prognosis in a wide range of disorders, such as in cases of COPD, chronic heart failure, sepsis and cerebral infarction8–10.

Previous studies have demonstrated that a reduction in CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts is closely associated with the development and progression of acute exacerbation in patients with COPD. This may result from the combined effects of autoimmune dysfunction caused by the disease and iatrogenic factors. First, the progression from stable COPD to AECOPD is characterized by a marked impairment of cellular immunity. Previous studies have demonstrated that AECOPD patients exhibit a significant decline in CD4 + T cells and an inverted CD4+/CD8 + ratio, which strongly correlates with elevated systemic inflammatory markers (such as CRP and NLR) and drives disease exacerbation11. This immune impairment originates in the stable phase of COPD, where a reduction in tolerogenic PD-L1 + dendritic cells disrupts immune homeostasis, leading to Th17-skewed chronic inflammation and impaired CD4 + T cell function12. Second, patients admitted to the ICU with severe AECOPD routinely receive high-dose systemic glucocorticoids. Although high-dose systemic glucocorticoids are essential for managing acute symptoms, they induce apoptosis in dendritic cells and downregulate MHC class II expression, thereby significantly impairing T helper cell activation13.Although lymphopenia has been identified as a predictor of mortality in critically ill patients, lymphocytes comprise multiple subpopulations with distinct immunological functions, and no specific quantitative indicators for assessing immune status have been established in patients with severe COPD. There are no specific quantitative indicators in patients with severe COPD. To date, the specific roles of different T lymphocyte subsets in the progression and prognosis of severe AECOPD remain controversial14,15. Existing prognostic models for AECOPD typically rely on routine complete blood counts, using general indicators such as the total lymphocyte count or neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR)11,16–18. Although these markers reflect systemic inflammation, they lack the resolution needed to identify the specific underlying immune exhaustion. Moreover, the specific roles of distinct T lymphocyte subsets in the progression and prognosis of severe AECOPD remain largely unelucidated and controversial. To address this knowledge gap, our study adopted a novel approach involving high-resolution immune profiling of lymphocyte subsets. We aimed to systematically characterize the full panel of lymphocyte subsets (including CD3 + CD4+ T cells, CD3 + CD8+ T cells, B cells, and NK cells) and directly compare their prognostic value with that of routine complete blood count indices for predicting 28-day all-cause mortality in patients with AECOPD admitted to the ICU. We also used robust machine learning feature selection algorithms (Boruta and LASSO) to eliminate multicollinearity among a large number of clinical variables. We anticipate that this study will facilitate early risk stratification and targeted immune monitoring in clinical practice.

Data collection

We extracted clinical data from the electronic medical records of all enrolled patients within the first 24 h of ICU admission. These data included demographic characteristics (age, sex), vital signs, comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, coronary heart disease), laboratory test results (complete blood count, clinical biochemistry, coagulation function, arterial blood gas analysis, and lymphocyte subset detection), and medications. We also calculated the Systemic Inflammatory Response Index (SIRI), Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), and Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte Ratio (NLR). The overall physiological status of each patient was assessed using the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score. SIRI = Neutrophil count×Monocyte count/Lymphocyte count; SII = Neutrophil count×Platelet count/Lymphocyte count; NLR = Neutrophil count/Lymphocyte count.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and median (interquartile range, IQR) for non-normally distributed data. Intergroup comparisons were performed using the independent samples t-test, Mann-Whitney U test, or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test, with Fisher’s exact test applied for small sample sizes. Variables with missing values > 30% were excluded, and those with missing values ≤ 30% were imputed using the multiple imputation method. We performed restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis to investigate the association between lymphocyte-related indices and the risk of 28-day all-cause mortality. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) were used to compare the predictive ability of various indices for 28-day all-cause mortality. Subgroup analyses were performed to identify potential confounding factors. To develop and validate the 28-day all-cause mortality risk prediction model, all enrolled patients were randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). We evaluated and compared the predictive performance of seven different machine learning algorithms using ROC curves, AUC values, and calibration curves; the latter were used to assess the accuracy of risk estimates. Furthermore, we used Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to interpret the model and visualize the contribution of each feature to the prediction19. Finally, we constructed a nomogram to provide a practical quantitative tool for clinical application. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2).

Sample size calculation

To verify the adequacy of the sample size for developing the multivariable prediction model, we performed sample size calculation based on the criteria proposed by Riley et al.20. The calculation parameters were set as follows: an expected Nagelkerke R² of 0.2, an expected event prevalence of 30%, and six independent predictors identified in our final model. To ensure satisfactory predictive performance and reduce the risk of overfitting (a shrinkage factor > 0.9), the calculation indicated that a minimum of 353 patients (with approximately 106 outcome events) were required. Our final study cohort included 382 patients, which met the sample size requirements for developing the prediction models.

Results

Participant selection and baseline characteristics

We initially screened 694 patients with AECOPD who met the preliminary inclusion criteria. After excluding patients with missing prognosis data(135) and lymphocyte subsets (177), a total of 382 patients were included in the study. Of these, 267 patients survived and 115 died within the 28-day follow-up period (Fig. 1). Most patients were male (317, 83.0%), and there was no significant difference in sex distribution between the two groups. However, significant differences were observed in age, vital signs (diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure), and APACHE II scores between the two groups. Additionally, significant differences were observed in laboratory test results [white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin (Hb), platelet count (PLT), lymphocyte count (Lymph), aspartate aminotransferase (AST)] and derived inflammatory indices (SIRI, SII, NLR). With respect to clinical treatment, non-survivors were more likely to receive invasive mechanical ventilation and vasoactive agents. No significant statistical differences were found in the use of glucocorticoids or tracheotomy between the two groups. Baseline characteristics of the included participants are detailed in Table 1.

RCS analysis of lymphocyte indicators and 28-day all-cause mortality

Survivors had significantly higher levels of all lymphocyte-related indices than non-survivors. Using restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, we identified a significant association between several lymphocyte-related indices and 28-day all-cause mortality. Specifically, lower levels of these indices were associated with a higher risk of 28-day all-cause mortality. We adjusted for potential confounding factors including the APACHE II score, myocardial infarction (MI) history, SIRI, and other variables with significant baseline differences between the two groups. After this adjustment, the association between routine complete blood count-derived lymphocyte counts, B cell counts, and the risk of 28-day all-cause mortality was no longer statistically significant. In contrast, total lymphocyte counts, T lymphocyte counts, CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts, CD3 + CD8+ T lymphocyte counts, and NK cell counts were significantly associated with the risk of 28-day all-cause mortality (Fig. 2A, B).

ROC analysis of lymphocyte indicators

We generated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves for all lymphocyte-related indices (Fig. 2C) to assess their predictive value for 28-day all-cause mortality in patients with severe AECOPD. CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts yielded an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.799. This index outperformed total lymphocyte counts (AUC: 0.781), T lymphocyte counts (AUC: 0.791), CD3 + CD8+ T lymphocyte counts (AUC: 0.748), NK cell counts (AUC: 0.745), and routine complete blood count-derived lymphocyte counts (AUC: 0.726). B cell counts exhibited the lowest predictive value (AUC: 0.621). These results indicate that CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts offer a significant advantage as a single predictor of 28-day all-cause mortality. At an optimal cutoff value of 157.61/µL, CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts had a sensitivity of 0.784 and a specificity of 0.699.

Logistic regression analysis and subgroup analysis of lymphocyte indicators

We stratified each lymphocyte-related index into high and low groups according to its median value. Unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression analyses were performed to assess the association between these indices and 28-day all-cause mortality, with adjustments made for the following covariates: age, sex, APACHE II score, coronary heart disease, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, smoking status, and glucocorticoid dosage. The results demonstrated that all lymphocyte-related indices were identified as protective factors for 28-day all-cause mortality in these patients. In the unadjusted model, the odds ratio (OR) for CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts was 0.15 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.08–0.24), and that for NK cell counts was 0.17 (95% CI: 0.10–0.29). After covariate adjustment, the adjusted OR for CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts was 0.23 (95% CI: 0.12–0.42), and that for NK cell counts was 0.21 (95% CI: 0.11–0.30). Subgroup analyses revealed that CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts were consistently identified as statistically significant protective factors in most subgroups, including those stratified by age, APACHE II score, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, smoking status, and glucocorticoid dosage. Detailed results for other lymphocyte-related indices are provided in Appendix 1. The phenomenon that some subgroup indicators lose statistical significance may be related to the small sample size of the corresponding subgroup.

Variable selection using Boruta and LASSO algorithms

Patients were randomly assigned to a training set (n = 267) and testing set (n = 115) in the ratio of 7:3. Multiple imputation was applied to handle missing values in both the training and test sets. There were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between the training and test sets. Variable selection was performed using the training set only. In the Boruta algorithm, green variables represent confirmed important predictors of 28-day all-cause mortality, yellow variables denote tentative predictors potentially associated with the outcome, and red variables indicate non-important variables that were excluded from further analysis. Initially, the Boruta algorithm identified 21 candidate variables, among which was the APACHE II score. To further refine this list and eliminate multicollinearity among the candidate variables, we applied the LASSO algorithm (Fig. 3). The most important variables were identified by intersecting the results from the five imputed datasets. Ultimately, the APACHE II score, CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts, serum urea level, SII, AST level, and SIRI were identified as the final key predictors.

Construction and verification of machine learning prediction models

Incorporating the six key predictors identified by the Boruta and LASSO algorithms, we trained and compared seven predictive models using both traditional statistical methods and advanced machine learning algorithms (Fig. 4A). All seven models exhibited robust predictive performance, with AUC values ranging from 0.825 to 0.860 in the test set. The traditional logistic regression model outperformed the more complex “black-box” machine learning algorithms, exhibiting the highest discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.860 in the test set. Given its superior predictive performance and inherent interpretability, the logistic regression model was selected as the final prognostic model. The clinical utility of this prognostic model was further validated using decision curve analysis (DCA), which revealed a significant net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the calibration curve showed excellent agreement between the predicted and observed 28-day all-cause mortality risk in the test set (Fig. 4C).

Model interpretability analysis and clinical application of nomogram

The relative importance of each variable in the logistic regression model is presented in Fig. 5A. The APACHE II score was the strongest predictor, followed by CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts. The SHAP summary plot for the logistic regression model is presented in Fig. 5B. The APACHE II score and serum urea level had a positive impact: higher values (represented by red dots) were associated with higher SHAP values (distributed to the right of the x-axis). This indicates that higher levels of these indicators are strongly associated with an adverse prognosis (a higher risk of 28-day all-cause mortality). In contrast, CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts had a negative impact: higher counts were associated with lower SHAP values (distributed to the left of the x-axis). This suggests that higher levels of these immune cells are a protective factor for patient survival.

To facilitate clinical application, we constructed a nomogram based on the multivariable logistic regression model (Fig. 5C). This nomogram incorporates six independent predictors: the APACHE II score, AST level, CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts, SII, serum urea level, and SIRI. This nomogram is easy to use in clinical practice. Each predictor value is assigned a specific number of points on the top scale. Summing these points to obtain a total point score allows clinicians to easily read the patient’s estimated risk of 28-day all-cause mortality from the bottom risk axis. Notably, the scoring scale for CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts is inverted relative to that for the other predictors. Lower counts correspond to higher scores, reflecting that impaired immune function is a key risk factor for 28-day all-cause mortality. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we characterized the clinical features of patients with severe AECOPD admitted to the ICU. We found that lymphocyte subset counts were more accurate than routine complete blood count-derived lymphocyte counts for predicting prognosis, independent of baseline demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and medication use. Our most notable finding is that a marked decline in CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts (< 157.61/µL) is an independent risk factor for 28-day all-cause mortality in these patients. By integrating machine learning feature selection algorithms (Boruta and LASSO), we successfully reduced complex clinical data to six core predictors: the APACHE II score, CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts, serum urea level, SII, AST level, and SIRI. A highly interpretable prognostic nomogram derived from the validated logistic regression model was therefore developed to facilitate individualized risk stratification in clinical practice.

A key finding of our study is the critical role of CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts in predicting the risk of 28-day all-cause mortality. Our results demonstrate that a reduction in CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts is an independent risk factor for 28-day all-cause mortality (optimal cutoff value: 157.61/µL). Notably, although this cutoff value was statistically determined using the Youden index, it is highly consistent with established clinical definitions of immunodeficiency. A CD4 + T cell count below 200/µL is universally recognized as severe immunocompromise, a threshold that traditionally defines a high risk of opportunistic infections21,22. That our objectively determined cutoff value falls well within this severe immunodeficiency range (< 200/µL) provides strong biological plausibility for our findings.

AECOPD is not merely a local airway inflammatory process but a systemic inflammatory response. CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocytes (T helper cells, Th cells) act as the “commanders” of the immune system23: they orchestrate the immune response by recognizing pathogens, stimulating B cells to produce antibodies, and activating CD8 + cytotoxic T lymphocytes to eliminate infected cells. Depletion of these cells impairs both cellular and humoral immunity. When CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts drop to a critical threshold (e.g., 157.61/µL), patients effectively develop immune paralysis or compensatory anti-inflammatory response syndrome (CARS)6. This implies that the body loses its ability to eliminate pathogenic bacteria and viruses. Consequently, patients are unable to control the primary infection and are at a significantly increased risk of developing secondary hospital-acquired infections (e.g., ventilator-associated pneumonia), which leads to poor clinical outcomes. Our study identifies this critical risk threshold, which suggests that while anti-inflammatory therapy remains the mainstay of treatment, clinicians should also monitor and assess patients’ immune status closely.

Based on these findings, we developed and validated a 28-day all-cause mortality prognostic model that integrates immune-inflammatory indicators and the APACHE II score. Using the Boruta algorithm combined with LASSO for variable selection, we identified six independent prognostic factors: the APACHE II score, CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts, serum urea level, SII, AST level, and SIRI. After evaluating seven machine learning algorithms (e.g., XGBoost, LightGBM), we found that the logistic regression algorithm exhibited the best predictive performance (AUC = 0.860) and excellent calibration in the test set. Although machine learning algorithms (e.g., XGBoost, GBM) handle non-linear relationships well, traditional logistic regression performed best in our dataset24. Notably, our study demonstrated that logistic regression (LR) outperformed complex machine learning algorithms, including XGBoost and LightGBM, in predicting 28-day all-cause mortality in this patient cohort. This finding is likely attributable to our rigorous variable selection approach using the Boruta and LASSO algorithms, which identified only variables with strong linear associations with the primary outcome measure. When the underlying data structure is predominantly linear, LR is theoretically the optimal approach, whereas more complex algorithms may exhibit suboptimal performance. Furthermore, for clinical application, the nomogram derived from the logistic regression model offers superior interpretability and clinical utility. It allows clinicians to intuitively quantify the contribution of each variable to the overall mortality risk, making it more amenable to clinical implementation than complex “black-box” machine learning models. The decision curve analysis (DCA) further confirmed that the model yields significant net clinical benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities.

The APACHE II score was once again confirmed to be a robust predictor, which is consistent with the findings of most previous studies and validates the reliability of our study data25–27. The SII and NLR have also been identified as independent risk factors for all-cause mortality in patients with COPD in previous studies, and our findings are highly consistent with these prior results3,28–31. Additionally, elevated serum urea levels and abnormal AST levels may indicate impaired clearance of toxic substances, including metabolic byproducts and reactive oxygen species. Collectively, these indicators constitute a multidimensional assessment system that encompasses systemic inflammation, immune status, and target organ dysfunction.

While previous studies have investigated similar prognostic factors for AECOPD, they have several notable limitations. Peng et al. utilized the MIMIC database to identify PaO2, vasoactive agent use, tracheal intubation, and admission NLR/PIN as prognostic predictors2. However, admission PaO2 levels are highly dependent on oxygen therapy modalities and oxygen concentration, which were not controlled for in their study. Furthermore, it remains unclear whether vasoactive agent use and tracheal intubation were initiated within the first 24 h of admission, which may introduce potential selection bias. Cojocaru et al. identified the SII and NLR as independent risk factors but did not conduct further in-depth analyses, which limits the clinical utility of their findings3. Our study addresses these key knowledge gaps to a significant extent. We exclusively utilized laboratory and clinical data obtained within the first 24 h of ICU admission. This approach enables early risk stratification at the time of admission, allowing clinicians to formulate more individualized and targeted treatment strategies.

Our study also has several inherent limitations. Firstly, as a single-center retrospective cohort study, selection bias is unavoidable, and the enrolled patients may not be fully representative of the broader severe AECOPD population. And we cannot ensure that biological specimens were collected immediately at the time of ICU admission, only that they were obtained within a 24-hour time window. Secondly, while we met the minimum sample size requirements for developing the prognostic model, the overall sample size remains relatively modest. Thirdly, because all enrolled patients were critically ill, they cannot tolerate pulmonary function testing. Therefore, only a small number of people can retrieve the pulmonary function reports, but by reviewing their medical records, it is found that they have all been treated with inhaled medications in the past. Finally, we acknowledge that while lymphocyte subset analysis is a routine procedure in large tertiary care centers, it is not universally available in all clinical settings, particularly in resource-limited ICUs, due to the requirement for specialized equipment and trained laboratory personnel. Nevertheless, in centers with access to this technology, flow cytometry exhibits high cost-effectiveness when used for patients with severe AECOPD. It offers a rapid turnaround time with a moderate associated cost, which further supports its clinical utility in critical care settings.

Conclusion

In patients with severe AECOPD admitted to the ICU, lymphocyte subsets—specifically total lymphocyte counts, T lymphocyte counts, CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts, CD3 + CD8+ T lymphocyte counts, and NK cell counts—are independently associated with 28-day all-cause mortality. Notably, CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts (< 157.61/µL) emerge as the most important independent prognostic predictor, and its predictive value is superior to that of routine complete blood count-derived lymphocyte counts for predicting 28-day all-cause mortality. By integrating robust machine learning feature selection algorithms, we constructed a reliable prognostic model that incorporates CD3 + CD4+ T lymphocyte counts and five other key clinical indicators. The nomogram derived from the multivariable logistic regression model provides clinicians with a practical and efficient tool for early risk stratification and individualized treatment decision-making in patients with severe AECOPD admitted to the ICU.
