Deep self-attention reinforcement learning adaptive gait planning and control for lower limb rehabilitation exoskeletons robot
Authors: Pengfei Zhang, Xiangyang Li, Binrui Wang, Xueshan Gao, Tao Liu, Bin Zhang, Feifei Qin, Yiyi Zhang, Siwei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAiming at the gait planning challenges of lower limb rehabilitation exoskeletons for adapting to user-specific gait characteristics, this study proposes an adaptive gait planning framework based on an improved deep deterministic policy gradient (DDPG) algorithm. We integrate self-attention mechanism into DDPG network, optimize attention parameters via cosine annealing SGD, and adopt Bayesian optimization to adjust hyperparameters adaptively. Simulation and experimental results show that the proposed algorithm outperforms traditional DDPG in gait trajectory generation, tracking accuracy and stability under variable gait parameters and unsteady human-robot interaction. This method improves the adaptive planning capability of exoskeletons, and provides a feasible scheme for personalized lower limb rehabilitation training.Subject areas: Biomechanics, Machine learning

Introduction

With the worsening global aging population and the continuous increase in the number of stroke patients,1,2 there are now over 13.7 million stroke patients worldwide,3 making stroke one of the leading causes of death among adults.4 Consequently, post-stroke rehabilitation has become increasingly important.5 Studies have shown that training with exoskeleton rehabilitation robots can induce sustained changes in human behavior,6 leading to a growing demand for efficient assistive training devices in the field of motor function rehabilitation.7 Against this backdrop, lower limb exoskeleton robots have become a focus of research due to their significant potential in assisting walking and rehabilitation training.8,9,10,11,12 Exoskeleton rehabilitation robots not only help patients adjust incorrect gaits but also provide assistive motion training during the rehabilitation period, thereby speeding up the recovery process. Despite these advancements, the interaction effects between exoskeleton robots and the human body, as well as their compliance and personalized adaptability in real-world applications, remain challenging areas of research.13,14 To enhance the assistive performance of exoskeleton robots, in-depth research and optimization of their control strategies and gait planning are essential.15 In this article, gait planning refers to the process of directly and in real time generating joint trajectories based on personalized human motion intention and human-robot interaction.

In the research on control technologies for lower-limb rehabilitation exoskeleton robots, gait planning serves as a core technology that plays a pivotal role in enhancing the usability and collaborative capability of human-robot interaction.16 This technology enables the generation of safe and self-adaptive joint control trajectories containing parameters such as angle, velocity, and torque based on the patient’s motor status and rehabilitation requirements. Accurate gait planning not only ensures patient safety but also improves the effectiveness of active motion training. For this purpose, human-machine interaction sensors such as force sensors,17 displacement sensors, inertial measurement unit (IMU) sensors,18 electroencephalogram (EEG) signals,19 and electromyogram (EMG) signals play a significant role in gait planning by perceiving human motion behavior.20 Compared to sensors based on EEG and EMG signals, kinematics-based sensors exhibit significant advantages in practical applications. Their signals are stable and reliable, less susceptible to external environmental influences such as muscle fatigue or electrical noise,10 and these sensors can more intuitively reflect the user’s motion state and intentions, thus reducing errors in the conversion process from biological signals to actual motion.

In the field of gait planning research for lower limb exoskeleton robots, various methods such as adaptive admittance control,21 rapid parametric gait planning methods,22 vision-assisted autonomous gait pattern planning,23 Dynamic Movement Primitives (DMPs) algorithm, and neural network predictions of gait have been proposed for gait planning, showing certain potential. Some researchers have used DMP for modeling the swinging leg,12,24 but have not optimized the parameters within the DMP model, resulting in insufficient generalization capability for gait planning. The DDPG algorithm combines the representational power of deep learning with the decision-making ability of reinforcement learning,25 capable of performing effective online parameter optimization for the gait planning model while maintaining the continuity of policy evaluation. Deep reinforcement learning has made some progress in the motion planning of mobile robots,26 but it has not yet been well applied in lower limb exoskeleton rehabilitation robots. This is because exoskeleton robots require real-time interaction with the human body, making gait planning more challenging than for robot dogs. Recently, Wang et al. proposed a computed torque control strategy with a double closed-loop servo mechanism for a servo pneumatic muscle-driven mechanical leg, which improves dynamic performance and anthropomorphic characteristics through variable-axis joint design and force-position mapping.27

Li proposed a hierarchical reinforcement learning framework based on the DDPG algorithm for optimal motion control of quadruped robots.28 Qiu used the DDPG algorithm for adaptive gait generation in hexapod robots,29 both employing the traditional DDPG algorithm for robot gait planning; yet, there remain aspects to be optimized. Firstly, deep reinforcement learning needs to improve the efficiency of capturing key information when faced with multisensory information from lower limb exoskeleton rehabilitation robots. Secondly, hyperparameter optimization is a crucial step in machine learning and deep learning,30 which can enhance learning efficiency and reduce training time, as the performance of algorithms heavily depends on the settings of these parameters. The environment faced in lower limb exoskeleton gait planning is more complex,13,23 and each patient has a personalized way of walking and needs. Zheng combined the sensitivity amplification control with deep reinforcement learning, thereby reducing the dependence on model accuracy while adapting to the dynamically changing human-exoskeleton interaction dynamics.31 Guo applied the twin delayed deep deterministic policy gradient (DDPG) algorithm to design a motion controller for the lower limb exoskeleton rehabilitation robot, enabling it to perform accurate gait trajectory planning and joint motion control in the stair-climbing environment.32 However, most methods lack efficient multi-source sensor data processing or personalized hyperparameter adaptation, failing to fully address the complexity of human-robot interaction in rehabilitation. The multi-head self-attention mechanism is employed for adaptive feature recalibration, dynamically weighting sensor channels according to real-time gait context. This enables the model to focus on salient inputs, which is crucial for achieving responsive and personalized adaptation and has been shown to enhance policy learning within the actor-critic framework.33

The gait planning task for lower-limb rehabilitation exoskeletons faces multiple core challenges: human-robot interaction during rehabilitation training is highly complex and subject-specific, requiring real-time and accurate recognition of human motion intention from multi-source heterogeneous sensor information; to achieve this, dynamic weighting of multi-modal sensor information adapted to gait phases must be performed; moreover, planning stable and natural gait in continuous action space also needs to overcome the sensitivity of deep reinforcement learning to hyperparameters and the difficulty of manual tuning. To address these challenges, this paper proposes an enhanced DDPG algorithm that integrates a self-attention mechanism and Bayesian optimization. This combined design is tailored to the task characteristics of lower-limb exoskeleton gait planning: the self-attention mechanism enables dynamic weighting of multi-source sensor information to focus on key motion features, while Bayesian optimization adaptively tunes hyperparameters, thereby improving training efficiency and policy stability in continuous action space. Specifically, the self-attention mechanism dynamically weights sensor channels according to real-time gait context, focusing on key features related to gait requirements and reward feedback; Bayesian optimization then adaptively adjusts hyperparameters based on these attention-guided reward signals, enabling the DDPG network to converge faster and more stably toward the reward function. This combined strategy significantly enhances learning efficiency and adaptability in complex human-robot interaction scenarios: the self-attention mechanism strengthens exploration and feature selection in high-dimensional perceptual environments, and Bayesian optimization accelerates convergence and mitigates the value-estimation bias present in traditional DDPG through adaptive, guided hyperparameter search. In practical implementation, a self-attention layer is first integrated into the DDPG framework to achieve adaptive fusion of multi-source information, thereby enhancing the extraction of key motion features and the understanding of human-robot interaction intention; second, Bayesian optimization is employed for automatic hyperparameter tuning, which overcomes the limitations of manual adjustment while ensuring algorithm stability and substantially improving overall performance.34

Results

Gait planning simulation and experiment

We constructed a human musculoskeletal model in OpenSim: https://simtk.org/projects/opensim for simulation, which was integrated with the improved DDPG algorithm implemented in PyCharm to evaluate the gait trajectory planning capability for lower limb exoskeleton applications. To compare the impact of different gait planning algorithms and hyperparameter optimization strategies on gait planning performance, we designed four sets of simulation experiments: (1) Baseline DDPG with manual hyperparameters, denoted as DDPG, (2) Baseline DDPG with Bayesian-optimized hyperparameters, denoted as DDPG(BO-Hp), (3)MHA-DDPG with its own optimized hyperparameters, denoted as MHA-DDPG (self-optimized Hp), and (4) MHA-DDPG with the same optimized hyperparameters as (2) and denoted as: improved DDPG. Each set of experiments was conducted over 50 episodes, with each episode containing 1,000 time steps. We established the dynamic model of the lower limb rehabilitation robot in OpenSim and configured its mechanical and dynamical parameters to ensure the reliability of the simulation results. Figure 1C illustrates the variation trend of reward values during lower limb exoskeleton gait planning using various DDPG algorithms. Compared with the reward curve of the traditional DDPG, the improved DDPG exhibits higher cumulative rewards throughout the training process, especially after the first 10,000 time steps (the cycle for the agent to make a single decision), where it demonstrates faster learning capability and a higher reward growth rate. Furthermore, after exceeding 20,000 time steps, the reward values of the improved DDPG tend to stabilize and remain at a high level, reflecting its significant advantages in learning stability and policy optimization. The performance of MHA-DDPG (self-optimized Hp) is second only to the improved DDPG, with a rapid increase within the first ten time steps followed by steady growth and gradual stabilization; in contrast, DDPG (BO-Hp) has the lowest initial reward, exhibits severe fluctuations within the first 20 time steps, and its learning efficiency and stability are inferior to the former two algorithms.

As shown in Figure 1D, within 1,000 time steps, the total reward for gait planning using the improved DDPG is −8.97, with an average reward of −0.0091; the total reward for the traditional DDPG is −139.13, with an average of −0.1391. The improved DDPG maintains a higher reward level with smaller fluctuations, indicating better training stability. The real-time reward fluctuation amplitude of MHA-DDPG (self-optimized Hp) is between that of the improved DDPG and the traditional DDPG, with its total and average rewards outperforming both the traditional DDPG and DDPG (BO-Hp), and its training stability approaching that of the improved DDPG; while DDPG (BO-Hp) has the largest real-time reward fluctuation amplitude, with its total and average rewards falling between those of the improved DDPG and the traditional DDPG, and its stability being inferior to the former two algorithms.

Figure 1C focuses on the short-term performance within the first 50 time steps. The improved DDPG shows a rapid increase in rewards after the 10th time step and stabilizes around −50 by the 50th time step; in contrast, the reward values of the traditional DDPG remain below −100, reflecting its slower learning speed and lower reward level. MHA-DDPG (self-optimized Hp) stabilizes its reward around −100 by the 50th time step, with a learning speed second only to the improved DDPG and a smaller fluctuation amplitude than DDPG (BO-Hp); while DDPG (BO-Hp) still has a reward below −100 by the 50th time step, ranking the poorest in short-term learning efficiency and stability among the four algorithms.

In contrast, Figure 1E presents the training process over a longer period (800 time steps). The improved DDPG approaches convergence at approximately the 100th time step, with its reward approaching 0 and maintaining high stability; meanwhile, the traditional DDPG fails to converge within 800 time steps, exhibiting significant reward fluctuations and remaining within the range of −100 to −150. MHA-DDPG (self-optimized Hp) approaches convergence at approximately 200–300 time steps, with its reward stabilizing around −50; its convergence speed is slower than that of the improved DDPG but much faster than those of the traditional DDPG and DDPG (BO-Hp); while DDPG (BO-Hp) still fails to converge by the 800th time step, with its reward maintained between −50 and −100, and its fluctuation amplitude smaller than that of the traditional DDPG but still significantly inferior to the former two algorithms. This comparison highlights that the improved DDPG achieves not only higher learning efficiency in the short term but also superior convergence performance and stability over longer training cycles, followed by MHA-DDPG (self-optimized Hp). Although DDPG (BO-Hp) outperforms the traditional DDPG, there remains a noticeable gap in its overall performance compared to the former two algorithms.

Adaptive trajectory tracking control simulation

To verify the ability of the improved DDPG algorithm to represent the ideal trajectory of patients, we asked the subjects to wear the exoskeleton and conduct a walking experiment in the zero-torque mode of the exoskeleton. For new users, personalization avoids retraining from scratch and adopts transfer learning: load the pre-trained model, input user-specific data (height, weight, and basic gait features), and fine-tune for 50 episodes to adapt to individual needs efficiently. We collected the gait profiles of the subjects in the natural walking state and used them as the ideal gait profiles. The subject was 173 cm tall, weighed 75 kg, and was 28 years old. During the walking process, the subject was required to walk 100 steps with a comfortable and stable gait. The collected gait profiles were used for offline training of DDPG and served as the reference trajectory of the exoskeleton during online motion planning. In this subsection, we compared the ability of the improved DDPG algorithm and the traditional DDPG algorithm to represent the reference trajectory. Non-steady-state gait trajectory planning

In the simulation experiments, the irregular amplitude and unstable step frequency interaction force curve shown in Figure 3F were introduced to verify whether the improved DDPG could accurately plan the gait curves based on unstable human-robot interaction information.

Observations from Figures 3G and 3H indicate that within 1,000 time steps, both algorithms exhibit consistent periodic trajectories. However, at specific transition phases, particularly around the 100th, 400th, 600th, and 900th time steps, the traditional DDPG shows noticeable fluctuations and spikes. In contrast, the improved DDPG demonstrates smoother transitions during these critical stages and more precise control over peak trajectory values. These results suggest that the improved DDPG algorithm can provide more stable and expected gait trajectory planning for the lower limb exoskeleton in response to human-robot interaction forces with different gait frequencies and stride lengths, reducing the instability of dynamic characteristics, which may enhance the comfort of exoskeleton usage.

During the critical gait transition phases (Figures 4B–4E), the conventional DDPG algorithm (blue curve) exhibited significant oscillations in human-exoskeleton interaction forces, with force amplitudes fluctuating violently between −30 N and 40 N. Biomechanical analysis revealed insufficient inertia compensation during stance-swing phase transitions in bilateral hip/knee joints, manifested as: high-frequency oscillations induced by abrupt joint acceleration changes, generating multiple transient peaks exceeding 30 N. In contrast, the improved DDPG algorithm (red curve) effectively constrained force fluctuations within the −10 N–10 N range. Quantitative analysis (Figure 4F) further validated the superiority of the enhanced algorithm, demonstrating 52.9%, 40.0%, 72.9%, and 46.6% reductions in mean interaction forces at left hip, right hip, left knee, and right knee joints respectively. These improvements indicate effective suppression of gait cycle-related high-frequency disturbances. By reducing human metabolic cost and enhancing human-robot collaboration efficiency, the modified algorithm significantly optimizes the human-machine integration characteristics of the exoskeleton system.

Discussion

The improved DDPG algorithm proposed in this study demonstrates significant advantages in lower limb exoskeleton gait planning, exhibiting exceptional robustness particularly when handling non-steady-state gaits. Experimental results confirm that the algorithm achieves superior angle tracking accuracy, with a RMSE for the left hip joint of only 0.085°, significantly outperforming the traditional DDPG’s 5.38°. Furthermore, quantitative analysis reveals that, compared to traditional methods, the enhanced algorithm reduces the mean interaction forces at the left hip, right hip, left knee, and right knee joints by 52.9%, 40.0%, 72.9%, and 46.6%, respectively. The improved algorithm effectively suppresses the severe force fluctuations caused by traditional DDPG, thereby enhancing the human-robot co-adaptation of the exoskeleton system.

Compared to our previous study,21 the improved algorithm achieves a breakthrough in reducing human-robot interaction forces. In the experiments of ref. 21, the peak interaction force reached approximately 15 N and was accompanied by a certain degree of unstable fluctuation. In this study, owing to the optimization of the control strategy, the peak interaction force during robot walking was reduced by approximately 60% compared to Reference [21]. This lower and more stable human-robot interaction force indicates a higher level of fusion between the human and the robot, reducing walking resistance and making the planned trajectories more closely align with natural human walking patterns, which enhances patient comfort while ensuring the safety of rehabilitation training.

These performance improvements are primarily attributed to the deep optimization of the algorithmic architecture and the simulation environment. By incorporating a self-attention mechanism, the improved algorithm achieves dynamic weighting of multi-source sensor information, allowing it to automatically focus on key motion features based on real-time gait contexts. This significantly enhances the model’s capability for feature extraction and recognition within complex human-robot interaction environments. Concurrently, the use of Bayesian optimization for adaptive hyperparameter tuning overcomes the limitations of manual adjustment, ensuring faster convergence and higher policy stability within continuous action spaces. Additionally, the research framework deeply integrates the improved DDPG algorithm with a musculoskeletal model during the simulation phase, achieving closed-loop optimization of the training process by simulating active muscle control and limb compliance. Together, these advantages ensure precise planning and adaptive capabilities across various gait patterns, providing an efficient and robust technical solution for personalized lower limb rehabilitation training.

Limitations of the study

Despite these achievements, this study still has certain limitations in actual rehabilitation training. The key lies in the lack of gait data of stroke patients at different real rehabilitation stages. In future clinical applications, the control strategy we proposed needs to be combined with the walking data of real patients, so that the controller based on DDPG can better adapt to the characteristics of pathological gait, and make personalized fine-tuning of the amplitude and frequency of the standard gait according to the individual differences of patients (such as the degree of limb movement disorder and rehabilitation progress) to meet their actual needs for passive training.

In the future, we will further combine the human musculoskeletal model to construct a highly realistic human-machine system model, and combine multi-modal sensing data to optimize the end-to-end control architecture, so as to better realize the transfer from simulation to clinical personalized rehabilitation training.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participant

A healthy male subject (age 28 years, height 173 cm, weight 75 kg) participated in the gait acquisition experiment. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant ethical guidelines, and it obtained the approval of all ethical and experimental procedures and protocols from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital, College of Medicine, Zhejiang University (Approval No.: (2020)IIT-80). Written informed consent was obtained from the participant prior to the experiment. The participant was instructed on how to wear and operate the exoskeleton robot and walked 100 steps with a comfortable, stable gait in zero-torque mode to record natural gait trajectories.

Simulation model

A human musculoskeletal model from OpenSim was used as the plant for reinforcement learning simulations. The model included lower limb segments actuated by internal joint torques; the pelvis was fixed to an external frame. Joint angle limits and torque saturation were applied to ensure gait stability. No animals, plants, or microbial strains were used in this study.

Method details

Robotic system design

As shown in Figure S1, the rehabilitation robot consists of a gantry structure, weight reduction system, lower limb exoskeleton system, sensor system, and dual-wheel drive system. The lower limb exoskeleton system includes two mechanical legs, each equipped with disc motors at the hip and knee joints to drive the joints. Two servo motors at the bottom center of the robot act as drive wheels to move the entire robot. The robot’s main control system includes two Stm32 controllers, displacement sensors, force sensors, speed sensors, and encoders. The drive units include the servo motor of the weight reduction system, the exoskeleton motors, and the hub motors. The lower limb exoskeleton is used to drive the patient’s leg walking training, with disc motors installed at the hip and knee joints. These motors receive gait trajectory control commands from the controller via the CAN bus, collecting angular and torque information from four channels: left hip joint, right hip joint, left knee joint, and right knee joint. Additionally, the exoskeleton platform’s sensors include interaction force sensors between the four leg rods. The Stm32 controller communicates with Labview via a WIFI module, enabling the collection of multisensory information and displaying various data curves on the interface.

Improved DDPG algorithm

The DDPG algorithm’s autonomous motion decision-making, as illustrated in Figure S2, involves the Actor policy network making action selections based on the environment, while the Critic evaluation network assesses the value of each action to guide the policy network’s action choice. The task of the Critic network is to estimate the action-value function Q(st,at), which is used to measure the expected cumulative reward of taking action a in states. The state vector st is mathematically defined as a 12-dimensional vector containing multi-source sensor information from the lower-limb rehabilitation exoskeleton, characterizing the system’s dynamic environment at time t. Its specific composition includes the angles θlh, θrh, θlk, θrk and angular velocities θ˙lh,θ˙rh,θ˙lk,θ˙rk of the four joints (left/right hip, left/right knee), along with the measured human-robot interaction forces flh, frh, flk, frk at the corresponding four joints. Angle and angular velocity data are obtained from joint encoders, interaction force data come from force sensors integrated into the leg rods, and attitude information is provided by IMU sensors. To ensure the stability and convergence speed of deep reinforcement learning training, each dimension of the sensor data is normalized before being fed into the network using min-max normalization, expressed as xnorm=x−xminxmax−xmin, where x is the raw sensor value, and xmin and xmax are the predefined physical range for that dimension, for instance, the hip joint angle range is [−30°, 40°], the knee joint angle range is [0°, 70°], and the interaction force is scaled according to the sensor’s calibrated range (e.g., [−50 N, 50 N]). The action vector at is mathematically defined as the 4-dimensional control command output by the policy network (Actor) in the DDPG algorithm, directly corresponding to the target angles θlhd,θrhd,θlkd,θrkd for the four joints of the lower-limb exoskeleton, with the physical unit of degrees (°). This output is a position control command (target joint angle). At the end of the policy network, the raw output is scaled to the aforementioned feasible joint angle range using a ReLU activation function and linear mapping, and is then sent via the CAN bus by the controller (STM32) to the joint disc motors for execution, with the control cycle synchronized with the algorithm’s time step. DDPG reward function design

To address the issue of value estimation bias in traditional DDPG algorithms for lower-limb exoskeleton gait planning, this paper introduces a self-attention mechanism and Bayesian optimization to enhance model performance. Within this framework, the reward function guides the learning process of the DDPG algorithm, aiming to minimize human-robot interaction forces and gait angle errors. This reward function is constructed as a weighted sum of the two objectives (Equation 10). Its design is based on the dual goals of gait rehabilitation: ensuring trajectory accuracy for effective training while minimizing interaction forces for safety and comfort, adopting the scalarization method commonly used in multi-objective optimization.

In terms of robustness, the design mechanism of the reward function is mainly reflected in three aspects: First, the weight coefficients have clear physical meanings and can be flexibly adjusted according to different rehabilitation stages or individual patient differences. For example, in the early rehabilitation phase, wθ can be increased to enhance trajectory tracking capability, while in the later adaptation phase, wf can be increased to promote human–robot collaboration. Second, the linear weighted form mathematically provides a convex combination optimization structure for the two competing objectives, helping to balance the conflicts among multiple objectives during training, thereby avoiding policy oscillations or convergence difficulties caused by objective conflicts. Third, This design supports adaptation to non-steady-state walking conditions walking conditions, enabling continuous guidance of the algorithm to achieve coordinated optimization of interaction forces and tracking errors in dynamically changing environments such as varying stride frequencies and lengths.

Reducing human-robot interaction force means less resistance between the patient and the robot when using the exoskeleton, leading to a more integrated human-robot training. Meanwhile, accurately tracking the ideal angle trajectory is vital for ensuring the accuracy and safety of the gait, which helps to prevent potential harm caused by abnormal gait representations. Self-attention mechanism.

The sensors of the robot system include sensors for hip/knee joint angles, human-robot interaction forces, speed, and IMU attitudes. In the study of gait planning for lower limb exoskeleton robots, the introduction of a self-attention mechanism into the DDPG framework can effectively enhance the model’s ability to process critical sensor data. The traditional DDPG employs a fixed network architecture to process all sensor inputs, which limits its ability to adaptively focus on key signals (such as human-robot interaction forces during the stance phase or joint angles during the swing phase) and suppress noise.

To address this, this study introduces a multi-head self-attention mechanism to dynamically weight different sensor channels based on their real-time importance. The core of this mechanism lies in using the attention function Attention(Q,K,V)=softmaxQKTdkV to compute the correlations between features, and through gradient optimization, the model automatically strengthens key information highly relevant to specific gait phases (such as joint angles and interaction torques) during training, while suppressing interference from noise or non-stationary signals, thereby enhancing the feature representation capability for potentially personalized gait planning. The self-attention module is composed of queries (Q), keys (K), and values (V). It first computes the similarity W between Q and K through the operation Q ⋅ KT.
