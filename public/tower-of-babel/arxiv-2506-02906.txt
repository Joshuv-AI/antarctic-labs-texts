Power Enhancement of Permutation-Augmented Partial-Correlation Tests via Fixed-Row Permutations
Tianyi Wang, Guanghui Wang, Zhaojun Wang, Changliang Zou

Abstract
Permutation-based partial-correlation tests guarantee finite-sample Type I error control under any fixed design and exchangeable noise, yet their power can collapse when the permutation-augmented design aligns too closely with the covariate of interest. We remedy this by fixing a design-driven subset of rows and permuting only the remainder. The fixed rows are chosen by a greedy algorithm that maximizes a lower bound on power. This strategy reduces covariate-permutation collinearity while preserving worst-case Type I error control. Simulations confirm that this refinement maintains nominal size and delivers substantial power gains over original unrestricted permutations, especially in high-collinearity regimes.



Keywords
: Collinearity; Exchangeable noises; Group invariance test; Partial-correlation test; Permutation methods.

1
Introduction

We consider a fixed-design linear model of the form
Y=X\beta+Z\theta+\epsilon
, where
(X,Z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
is the design matrix,
\epsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the noise vector, and
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the response vector. The objective is to evaluate the partial correlation between
Y
and
X
by testing the null hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{0}:\beta=0
against the alternative hypothesis
\mathcal{H}_{1}:\beta\neq 0
.

Classical F- and t-tests provide valid inference under the assumption of normal noises or in large samples. Permutation and rank-based tests relax the normality assumption, offering validity in finite or large samples under specific noise structures, such as symmetry
(
Hartigan 1970
)
and rotational invariance
(
Meinshausen 2015
)
. A comprehensive review of early developments in this area is presented in Section A of
Lei and Bickel 2021b
.

Recent advancements have focused on permutation-based tests that maintain finite-sample validity for any fixed design matrix and arbitrary
exchangeable
noise, enhancing robustness across diverse noise distributions. A summary of key examples is provided in Table 1 of
Guan 2024
. Notably, the Cyclic Permutation Test (CPT)
(
Lei and Bickel 2021a
)
uses a pool of linear statistics of the response vector and ensures that their joint distribution is invariant under cyclic permutations, but it only maintains the nominal significance level
\alpha\in(0,1)
when
n>(1/\alpha-1)p
.
Wen et al. 2025
proposed the Residual Permutation Test (RPT) which projects residuals onto subspaces orthogonal to both the original and permuted design matrices, ensuring valid size control when
n>2p
; however, by replacing unknown noise-projection thresholds with computable upper bounds, its p-value is slightly inflated, rendering RPT mildly conservative in finite samples. In contrast, the Permutation-Augmented Linear Model Regression Test (PALMRT)
(
Guan 2024
)
pairs test statistics from both the original and permuted regression models, guaranteeing worst-case coverage of
2\alpha
when
n>2p
, and it empirically controls Type I error.

1.1
Trivial Power Due to Collinearity

To illustrate the concept, we first consider a specific instance of PALMRT using a pair of F-statistics. Let
H_{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be the projection matrix onto the column space
\mathcal{C}(M)
of
M
. For a set of
n\times n
permutation matrices
\{P_{\pi_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{B}
, each pair of F-statistics is given by:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} T_{i,0}&=\|(I-H_{X,Z,P_{\pi_{i}}Z})Y\|_{2}^{2}=\|(I-H_{X,Z,P_{\pi_{i}}Z})\epsilon\|_{2}^{2},\\
T_{0,i}&=\|(I-H_{P_{\pi_{i}}X,Z,P_{\pi_{i}}Z})Y\|_{2}^{2}=\|(I-H_{P_{\pi_{i}}X,Z,P_{\pi_{i}}Z})(X\beta+\epsilon)\|_{2}^{2}.\end{aligned}

(1)

A larger difference, with
T_{i,0}<T_{0,i}
, provides stronger evidence against
\mathcal{H}_{0}
. PALMRT uses the following conformal p-value:

\displaystyle p_{\text{val}}=\frac{1}{B+1}\left[1+\sum_{i=1}^{B}\mathbb{I}(T_{i,0}>T_{0,i})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{I}(T_{i,0}=T_{0,i})\right],

(2)

which ensures that
pr(p_{\text{val}}\leq\alpha\mid\mathcal{H}_{0})<2\alpha
for any fixed design and arbitrary exchangeable noise, as shown in
Guan 2024
.

However, permutation-augmented spaces for the projections of
Y
can introduce collinearity. Specifically, when
X\in\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi_{i}}X,Z,P_{\pi_{i}}Z)
, we have
T_{i,0}\geq T_{0,i}
regardless of the value of
\beta
, failing to reject
\mathcal{H}_{0}
when
\beta\neq 0
. This
(near-)collinearity
issue is also noted in
Guan 2024
(see Section 9, Discussions). To further clarify this issue, consider the following example:

Example 1

(Paired Designs)
.

Consider a paired design:

\displaystyle(X,Z)=(X,Z_{1},\dots,Z_{p})=\begin{pmatrix}1&0_{1\times p}\\
0_{p\times 1}&I_{p}\\
1&1_{1\times p}\\
0_{(n-p-2)\times 1}&0_{(n-p-2)\times p}\end{pmatrix},

where
a_{n\times p}
denotes an
n\times p
matrix with all entries equal to
a
, and
I_{p}
is the identity matrix of size
p\times p
. Let
P_{\pi_{i}\mid 1\leftrightarrow(k+1)}
be any permutation matrix that swaps the first and
(k+1)
-th rows. It follows that
X=P_{\pi_{i}\mid 1\leftrightarrow(k+1)}(Z_{k}-X)+Z_{k}
, which implies
X\in\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi_{i}\mid 1\leftrightarrow(k+1)}X,Z_{k},P_{\pi_{i}\mid 1\leftrightarrow(k+1)}Z_{k})
. This collinearity becomes more pronounced as
p
increases. As shown in Figure
1
, the probability of collinearity,
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi_{i}}X,Z,P_{\pi_{i}}Z))
, increases with
p
and leads to trivial power for large
p
.

Comparison of PALMRT’s performance with and without row-selection in a paired design with standard Gaussian noise (
Y=4X+\epsilon
) for varying
p
. The left panel illustrates the probability of collinearity, while the right panel shows the test power. The significance level is set to
5\%
.

1.2
Our Contribution

In this paper, we introduce a refinement of PALMRT that addresses the trivial power issues caused by near-collinearity in permutation-augmented regression. The core idea is to perform random permutations by fixing certain rows of the design matrix (i.e., permuting the rest). Specifically, we use random restricted permutation matrices
P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}
, where a subset of row indices
\mathcal{R}
is fixed (see Section
2.1
). These rows are selected greedily to maximize the angle between
X
and the space
\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}X,Z,P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)
, thereby mitigating collinearity; see Section
2.3
. This row-selection strategy guarantees a lower bound on the test power. Although design-dependent, the resulting procedure retains worst-case coverage of
2\alpha
at any significance level
\alpha
. This follows because all restricted permutation matrices generate a
subgroup
, and uniformly sampling permutations from this subgroup preserves exchangeability—so the conformal-test validity argument still applies.

In Example
1
, an intuitive approach is to select a row set
\mathcal{R}=\{2,\ldots,p+2\}
. This ensures
P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}Z=Z
for any
P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}
, and thus
X
only falls into
\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}X,Z,P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)
when
P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}X=X
(which occurs with probability
1/(n-p-1)
). It is implicitly assumed that
X\notin\mathcal{C}(Z)
, as otherwise
\beta
would not be identifiable. Figure
1
demonstrates that PALMRT with the proposed row-selection strategy effectively reduces the probability of collinearity across various values of
p
, thereby enhancing the test power. Notably, our row-selection strategy successfully identifies the target row set
\{2,\ldots,p+2\}
in most cases.

1.3
Related Literature

Permutation methods have long offered exact, distribution-free inference, yet only recently has their behavior under random resampling been fully characterized.
Hemerik and Goeman 2018
showed that exactness is retained for any subgroup.
Ramdas et al. 2023
pushed this further: a randomization device yields valid p-values for any subset and any sampling distribution. A different line of work demonstrates that using a strategically chosen subgroup can substantially improve power and reduce computation:
Koning and Hemerik 2024
constructed “representative” subgroups that dominate random sampling, and
Koning 2024
showed that tiny, well-selected subgroups can even outperform the full group in multiple-testing settings.

Our work brings these subgroup ideas to permutation-augmented partial-correlation testing. A key difference from earlier work is that even with full-group sampling, the test can still suffer from trivial power whenever permutation-augmented regressors align too closely with the covariate of interest. Our solution is to restrict permutations to a design-driven subgroup with fixed-rows, preserving finite-sample (worst-case) validity of the conformal p-value.

2
Methodology

2.1
Restricted Permutation Matrices and Subgroup

Let
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. A permutation
\pi:[n]\rightarrow[n]
corresponds to an
n\times n
permutation matrix
P_{\pi}=(p_{ij})
where
p_{ij}=1
if
\pi(i)=j
and
p_{ij}=0
otherwise. Applying
P_{\pi}
to a design matrix
X
permutes the rows of
X
according to
\pi
. For the identity permutation
\pi_{0}
(where
\pi_{0}(i)=i
for
i\in[n]
),
P_{\pi_{0}}=I_{n}
. The set of all
n\times n
permutation matrices forms a symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
of order
n!
under matrix multiplication.

Given a subset of row indices
\mathcal{R}\subseteq[n]
, a
restricted
permutation matrix
P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}
is a permutation matrix
P_{\pi}
with the restriction that
p_{ii}=1
for all
i\in\mathcal{R}
. The collection of all such restricted permutation matrices forms a
subgroup

\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}}
, a subset of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
that is closed under matrix multiplication.

2.2
Our Procedure

We describe our procedure for a generalized class of paired statistics
(T_{i,0},T_{0,i})
, including paired F-statistics as a special case (see Eq.(
1
)). Specifically, for any permutation matrix
P_{\pi_{i}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
, define

\displaystyle T_{i,0}=T(P_{\pi_{i}},P_{\pi_{0}};X,Z,\epsilon),\quad T_{0,i}=T(P_{\pi_{0}},P_{\pi_{i}};X,Z,\epsilon),

where
T(\cdot,\cdot;X,Z,\epsilon)
is a function that satisfies the transferability Condition
1

(
Guan 2024
)
.

Condition 1

(Transferability)
.

For any permutation matrices
P_{\pi_{i}},P_{\pi_{j}},P_{\sigma}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
,

\displaystyle T(P_{\pi_{i}},P_{\pi_{j}};X,Z,P_{\sigma}\epsilon)=T(P_{\sigma}^{-1}P_{\pi_{i}},P_{\sigma}^{-1}P_{\pi_{j}};X,Z,\epsilon).

We begin by fixing a subset of row indices
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}(X,Z)\subseteq[n]
(see Section
2.3
), typically depending on the design
(X,Z)
. It then proceeds by randomly sampling
P_{\pi_{1}\mid\mathcal{R}},\ldots,P_{\pi_{B}\mid\mathcal{R}}
from the collection
\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}}
. A p-value is computed as in Eq.(
2
), where the random permutation matrices
P_{\pi_{i}}
in
(T_{i,0},T_{0,i})
are replaced by the restricted counterparts
P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}
. Importantly,
\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}}
forms a subgroup, regardless of the design-dependent nature of
\mathcal{R}
, underpinning the procedure’s finite-sample validity.

Theorem 1
.

Consider any fixed design matrix
(X,Z)
and assume arbitrary exchangeable noise
\epsilon
. Under Condition
1
, for any row set
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}(X,Z)
,

pr(p_{\text{val}}\leq\alpha\mid\mathcal{H}_{0})<2\alpha

for all
\alpha>0
.

Remark 1
.

The worst-case bound of
2\alpha
is inherited from PALMRT and aligns with the bounds for predictive coverage established for multisplit conformal prediction methods
(
Vovk et al. 2018
;
Barber et al. 2021
)
.
In certain special designs, this bound could be tightened. For example, consider Example
1
using paired F-statistics with a selected row set
\mathcal{R}
. If additionally
P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}Z=Z
for all
P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}}
(as typically observed in our simulations), we can show that
pr(p_{\text{val}}\leq\alpha\mid\mathcal{H}_{0})\leq\alpha
; see Appendix
A.2
for a proof.

2.3
Greedy Row-Selection Strategy

Theorem
1
permits any design-dependent selection of fixed rows. We propose a strategy motivated by F-statistics (see Eq.(
1
)) that enhances power by reducing collinearity.

Proposition 1
.

Consider fixed designs and assume exchangeable noises. For paired F-statistics
(T_{i0},T_{0i})
and any candidate row set
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}(X,Z)
,

pr(p_{\text{val}}\leq\alpha)\geq pr\big(\max_{P_{\pi}\in\mathcal{S}_{n}}g(P_{\pi},\epsilon;X,Z)\leq\beta^{2}\cdot F_{D_{\mathcal{R}}}^{-1}(\alpha+O(B^{-1/2}(\log B)^{1/2});X,Z)\big)+O(B^{-1}),

where
g
is a design-dependent function of
P_{\pi}
and
\epsilon
(see Eq.(
A1
) in the Appendix),
D_{\mathcal{R}}=\|(I-H_{P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}X,Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z})X\|_{2}^{2}
is the squared residual norm obtained by projecting
X
onto
\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}X,Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)
, and
F_{D_{\mathcal{R}}}^{-1}(\alpha;X,Z)
is the
\alpha
-quantile of
D_{\mathcal{R}}
.

Motivated by Proposition
1
, we seek the row set
\mathcal{R}
that maximizes the lower bound on power. Up to permutation sampling errors
O(B^{-1/2}(\log B)^{1/2})
, we maximize
F_{D_{\mathcal{R}}}^{-1}(\alpha;X,Z)
—the
\alpha
-quantile of the squared residual norm
D_{\mathcal{R}}
. Maximizing this quantile enlarges the angle between
X
and the projection space
\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}X,Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)
, thereby minimizing collinearity.

Exploring every subset
\mathcal{R}\subseteq[n]
is computationally impractical, so we adopt a greedy strategy that adds one row at a time, each time choosing the candidate that gives the largest quantile. A naive implementation would begin with
\mathcal{R}=\emptyset
. This is unsafe: in Example
1
(see Section
1.2
), fixing any single index in the target row set
\{2,\ldots,p+2\}
already forces
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))\geq\alpha
for large
p
(see Appendix
A.3
for a proof), and Proposition
2
(i) then implies
F_{D_{\mathcal{R}}}^{-1}(\alpha;X,Z)=0
. The procedure would therefore produce only the trivial power lower bound. Thus, we first build an initial shield that keeps
X
safely outside
\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)
before the greedy search begins.

0.

Input
: design matrix
(X,Z)
, significance level
\alpha
, budget
T
.

Output
: fixed-row set
\mathcal{R}
.

1.

Initial shield.

a)

Set
\mathcal{R}^{0}\leftarrow\emptyset
.

b)

While
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))>\alpha
:

i)

For each
k\not\in\mathcal{R}^{0}
, estimate
\mu_{\mathcal{R}^{0}\cup\{k\}}(Z)
.

ii)

Let
k^{*}={\arg\min}_{k}\mu_{\mathcal{R}^{0}\cup\{k\}}(Z)
and update
\mathcal{R}^{0}\leftarrow\mathcal{R}^{0}\cup\{k^{*}\}
.

2.

Power-oriented augmentation.

a)

For
t\in[T]
:

i)

For each
k\not\in\mathcal{R}^{t-1}
, estimate
Q^{t}(k)=F_{D_{\mathcal{R}^{t-1}\cup\{k\}}}^{-1}(\alpha)
.

ii)

Let
k^{*}={\arg\max}_{k}Q^{t}(k)
and
Q^{t}=Q^{t}(k^{*})
. Update
\mathcal{R}^{t}\leftarrow\mathcal{R}^{t-1}\cup\{k^{*}\}
.

b)

t^{*}=\arg\max_{0\leq t\leq T}Q^{t}
.

3.

Return:

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{t^{*}}
.

Algorithm 1

Greedy row-selection

Stage I (initial shield)
: The idea is to make
Z
and
P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z
similar, thereby pushing
X
away from
\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)
because of
X\notin\mathcal{C}(Z)
. Define
\mu_{\mathcal{R}}(Z)=E\{\textrm{rank}(Z-P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)\}
. Proposition
2
(ii) shows that
\mu_{\mathcal{R}}(Z)
decreases as
\mathcal{R}
grows, and therefore we suggest adding rows one-by-one until the safety condition
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))<\alpha
is met. The resulting set is denoted as
\mathcal{R}^{0}
.

Stage II (power-oriented augmentation)
: We perform
T
greedy iterations to enlarge the separation between
X
and the space
\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}X,Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)
, thereby improving power. At iteration
t\in[T]
, for every candidate row
k\not\in\mathcal{R}^{t-1}
, compute the quantile
F_{D_{\mathcal{R}^{t-1}\cup\{k\}}}^{-1}(\alpha;X,Z)
. We then select
k^{*}
that maximizes the quantile, updating
\mathcal{R}^{t}=\mathcal{R}^{t-1}\cup\{k^{*}\}
and storing this best quantile as
Q^{t}
. After
T
steps we select the iterate
\mathcal{R}^{t}
whose
Q^{t}
is the largest among
\{Q^{0},Q^{1},\ldots,Q^{T}\}
, ensuring a power lower bound no smaller than that of PALMRT.

Proposition 2
.

For any fixed design
(X,Z)
and row set
\mathcal{R}
, (i)
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))\geq\alpha
implies
F_{D_{\mathcal{R}}}^{-1}(\alpha;X,Z)=0
; (ii)
\mu_{\mathcal{R}}(Z)\geq\mu_{\mathcal{R}\cup\{k\}}(Z)
for any
k\in[n]\backslash\mathcal{R}
.

This monotonicity justifies the greedy construction in Stage I and ensures that each added row strictly improves the collinearity shield.

Remark 2
.

The rank criterion
\mu_{\mathcal{R}}(Z)
, the safety probability
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))
, and the squared residual-norm quantile
F^{-1}_{D_{\mathcal{R}}}(\alpha;X,Z)
can all be approximated via Monte Carlo simulation of restricted permutation matrices
P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}}
. In particular, verifying
X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)
reduces to checking whether
\textrm{rank}(\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))=\textrm{rank}(\mathcal{C}(X,Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))
.

.

3
Simulation Studies

We compare our fixed-row PALMRT to two baselines—the original PALMRT without row-selection and CPT—evaluating finite-sample size and power. Throughout, all experiments use
n=100
observations, a nominal level
\alpha=5\%
, and
B=2,000
permutations for PALMRT both with and without row-selection. CPT follows the implementation details recommended by
Lei and Bickel 2021a
, i.e., a genetic pre-ordering of
10,000
optimization steps followed by
19
cyclic permutations. For our greedy search we set a budget of
T=20
when
p=15
and
T=30
when
p=40
. Quantities such as
F^{-1}_{D_{\mathcal{R}}}(\alpha;X,Z)
,
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))
, and
\mu_{\mathcal{R}}(Z)
are estimated according to Remark
2
with
2,000
restricted permutations.

We consider three representative design matrices that span the spectrum from negligible to severe collinearity:

•

Gaussian: entries of
(X,Z)
are i.i.d.
N(0,1)
with
p=15
covariates, where collinearity is negligible.

•

Paired: the high-collinearity construction of Example
1
with
p=15
.

•

Mixture: entries are drawn i.i.d. from
0.95\delta_{0}+0.05N(0,1)
with
p=40
, creating moderate collinearity, where
\delta_{0}
represents the Dirac function at
0
.

Noise vectors
\epsilon
are generated from four distributions—standard Gaussian,
t_{3}
, standard Cauchy, and a sparse multinomial perturbation (
N(0,I_{n})+10^{4}\times\text{Multinomial}(1;(n^{-1},\ldots,n^{-1}))\times(-1)^{\text{Bernoulli}(0.5)}
)—to cover light- and heavy-tailed as well as discrete noise regimes.

For every design-noise combination, we carry out
50
Monte Carlo replicates; within each replicate, we generate
2,000
independent noise vectors to estimate the empirical rejection probability—whether for size or power.

A.1
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Without loss of generality, label the fixed rows by
\mathcal{R}=\{n-s+1,\dots,n\}
for some design-dependent integer
s=s(X,Z)
. Let
\mathcal{R}_{i}=\{n-i+1,\dots,n\}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}_{i}}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}P&0_{(n-i)\times i}\\
0_{i\times(n-i)}&I_{i}\end{pmatrix}:P\in\mathcal{S}_{n-i}\right\},

which forms a subgroup of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. Note that
\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}_{0}}=\mathcal{S}_{n}
. By Condition
1
, for each
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
,
\{T(P_{\pi_{l}\mid\mathcal{R}_{i}},P_{\pi_{k}\mid\mathcal{R}_{i}},X,Z,\epsilon)\}
is identically distributed as
\{\tilde{T}_{lk}\}=\{T(\tilde{P}_{\pi_{l}\mid\mathcal{R}_{i}},\tilde{P}_{\pi_{k}\mid\mathcal{R}_{i}},X,Z,\epsilon)\}
, where
P_{\pi_{0}\mid\mathcal{R}_{i}}=I_{n}
,
\{P_{\pi_{l}\mid\mathcal{R}_{i}}\}_{l=1}^{B}
and
\{\tilde{P}_{\pi_{l}\mid\mathcal{R}_{i}}\}_{l=0}^{B}
are randomly sampled from
\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}_{i}}
. Thus, this distributional identity property is preserved for
\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}}
with design-dependent
s
.
Following similar arguments used in
Guan 2024
, the collection
\{f(l,\tilde{T})=1+\sum_{k\neq l}(1\{\tilde{T}_{kl}>\tilde{T}_{lk}\})\}_{l=0}^{B}
is exchangeable for different
l
, and thus the probability of
p_{\text{val}}\leq\alpha
can be bounded by the size of
\{l:f(l,\tilde{T}\leq\alpha(B+1))\}
.
∎

A.2
Tight Bound in Paired Designs

Statement.
If additionally
P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}Z=Z
for all
P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}}
, then
pr(p_{\text{val}}\leq\alpha\mid\mathcal{H}_{0})\leq\alpha
.

Proof.

When
P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}Z=Z
for all
P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}\in\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}}
, we can rewrite
T_{i,0}
and
T_{0,i}
as

\displaystyle T_{i,0}

\displaystyle=\|(I-H_{X,Z,P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}Z})Y\|_{2}^{2}=\|(I-H_{X,Z})Y\|_{2}^{2},

\displaystyle T_{0,i}

\displaystyle=\|(I-H_{P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}X,Z,P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}Z})Y\|_{2}^{2}=\|(I-H_{P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}(X,Z)})Y\|_{2}^{2}.

Now we denote
T_{i,0}
,and
T_{0,i}
as
T_{0}
and
T_{i}
, respectively:

\displaystyle p_{\text{val}}=\frac{1}{B+1}\left[1+\sum_{i=1}^{B}\mathbb{I}(T_{0}>T_{i})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{I}(T_{0}=T_{i})\right].

According to the transferability condition, we have

\displaystyle T_{i}=\|(I-H_{X,Z})P_{\pi_{i}\mid\mathcal{R}}^{-1}Y\|_{2}^{2}.

Under
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, the exchangeability of
\epsilon
and the same technique used in Theorem 2 of
Hemerik and Goeman 2018
allow us to conclude that
T_{0},\dots,T_{B}
are exchangeable. Consequently, by the standard analysis of permutation tests, we have

\displaystyle pr(p_{\text{val}}\leq\alpha\mid\mathcal{H}_{0})\leq\alpha.

∎

A.3
Necessity of the Initial Shield

Statement.
In paired designs, fixing any single index in the target row set
\{2,\ldots,p+2\}
already forces
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))\geq\alpha
for
p\geq\alpha(n-1)+1
.

Proof.

For indices
j,k\in[n]\backslash\mathcal{R}
, define
\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}}
as the subset of permutations that move the
k
th row to the
j
th row while keeping the rows in
\mathcal{R}
fixed. The cardinality of this subset is given by
|\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}|=(n-|\mathcal{R}|-1)!
. Furthermore, for any distinct indices
k_{1},k_{2}\in[n]\backslash\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k_{1}\rightarrow j}\cap\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k_{2}\rightarrow j}=\emptyset
.

When
\mathcal{R}=\{p+2\}
, note that for
P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow 1}\in\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow 1}
, we have

\displaystyle X=P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow 1}Z_{k},\quad\forall k\in\{2,\dots,p+1\},

which implies
X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow 1}Z)
. Consequently,

\displaystyle pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))\geq p\frac{(n-2)!}{(n-1)!}=\frac{p}{n-1}.

When
\mathcal{R}=\{j\},j=2,\dots,p+1
, we obtain

\displaystyle X=P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow 1}(Z_{k}-Z_{j})+Z_{j},\quad\forall k\in\{2,\dots,p+1\}\backslash\{j\}.

Similarly, we conclude that
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))\geq(p-1)/(n-1).

In summary, attempting to fix any single index in the target row set
\{2,\ldots,p+2\}
yields
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))\geq\alpha
, for
p\geq\alpha(n-1)+1
.
∎

A.5
Proof of Proposition
2

Proof.

(i) Notice that
\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)\subseteq\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}X,Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)
for any fixed design
(X,Z)
and row set
\mathcal{R}
. Consequently, if
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))\geq\alpha
, then

\displaystyle pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}X,Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))\geq pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))\geq\alpha.

Furthermore, when
X\in\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}X,Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z)
, we have
D_{\mathcal{R}}=0
. By the definition of quantile,
pr(X\in\mathcal{C}(P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}X,Z,P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R}}Z))\geq\alpha
implies that
F_{D_{\mathcal{R}}}^{-1}(\alpha;X,Z)=0
.

(ii) Without loss of generality, suppose that
\mathcal{R}=\{n-s+1,\dots,n\}
. For
k\in[n]\backslash\mathcal{R}
, note that
\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}}
can be decomposed as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}}=\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow 1}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}\cup\{k\}}\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow n-s}.

Besides, for any distinct indices
j_{1},j_{2}\in[n]\backslash\mathcal{R}
,
\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j_{1}}\cap\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j_{2}}=\emptyset
. For any
j,k\in[n]\backslash\mathcal{R}
, we can construct a bijection between
\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}
and
\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}\cup\{k\}}
. Let
P_{k\leftrightarrow j}
be the permutation that swaps the
k
th and
j
th rows while leaving all other rows unchanged. Then:

\displaystyle P_{k\leftrightarrow j}\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}=\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R}\cup\{k\}}.

Moreover, for any distinct
P_{\pi_{1}\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j},\ P_{\pi_{2}\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}\in\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}
, we have
P_{k\leftrightarrow j}P_{\pi_{1}\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}\neq P_{k\leftrightarrow j}P_{\pi_{2}\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}
. Thus, we conclude that
P_{k\leftrightarrow j}
is the bijection.

Next, we demonstrate that for any permutation
P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}\in\mathcal{S}_{n\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}
, we have

\displaystyle\textrm{rank}(Z-P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}Z)\geq\textrm{rank}[Z-P_{k\leftrightarrow j}P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}Z].

Observe that the difference between
Z-P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}Z
and
Z-P_{k\leftrightarrow j}P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}Z
appears only in the
k
th and
j
th rows. Without loss of generality, consider
j=1
. Denote
Z^{k}
as the
k
th row of
Z
.

\displaystyle Z-P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow 1}Z=\begin{pmatrix}Z^{1}-Z^{k}\\
\vdots\\
Z^{k}-Z^{\pi(k)}\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix},\quad Z-P_{k\leftrightarrow 1}P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow 1}Z=\begin{pmatrix}Z^{1}-Z^{\pi(k)}\\
\vdots\\
0\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}.

If
\textrm{rank}(Z-P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow 1}Z)<\textrm{rank}(Z-P_{k\leftrightarrow 1}P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow 1}Z)
, then

\displaystyle Z^{1}-Z^{k},Z^{k}-Z^{\pi(k)}\in\mathcal{C}(Z^{2}-Z^{\pi(2)},\dots,Z^{k-1}-Z^{\pi({k-1})},Z^{k+1}-Z^{\pi({k+1})},\dots),

but
Z^{1}-Z^{\pi(k)}
does not. This leads to a contradiction because
Z^{1}-Z^{\pi(k)}=(Z^{1}-Z^{k})+(Z^{k}-Z^{\pi(k)})
.

Thus we conclude that for any
k\in[n]

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathcal{R}}(Z)=\frac{1}{n-s}\sum_{j=1}^{n-s}E[\textrm{rank}(Z-P_{\pi\mid\mathcal{R},k\rightarrow j}Z)]\geq\mu_{\mathcal{R}\cup\{k\}}(Z).

∎
