Reappraising the Elatina series: Solar dynamo clocking and inference of orbital periods
Frank Stefani, Tom Weier, Gerrit M. Horstmann, George Mamatsashvili

Abstract
We reconsider the 680 million year old Elatina series of sedimentary laminae from South Australia that show a remarkably stable periodicity with a main period of around 12 years, which is close to the Schwabe cycle, and a second period of 314 years that has been coined Elatina cycle. By analyzing the residuals of the series’ minima from a linear trend, and deriving Dicke’s ratio, we first show that the series exhibits a high degree of phase stability, except one single break point which may indicate a 90 ∘ phase jump. We discuss the data in terms of a recently developed synchronization model of the solar dynamo. This model is then employed to infer those orbital periods of Venus, Earth, Jupiter and Saturn that would be required to jointly explain the moderately changed Schwabe cycle, and the Elatina cycle when interpreted as a prolonged Suess-de Vries cycle. Assuming pairwise conservations of the sum of the angular momenta of Jupiter/Saturn and Venus/Earth, respectively, we find solutions of the underlying inverse problem which amount to approximately 1 percent angular momentum increase of Jupiter and a 0.005 per cent angular momentum increase of Earth. The plausibility of such changes over a period of seven hundred million years is discussed in light of solar system dynamics.

I
Introduction

Over four decades ago, Williams published
the remarkable results of his
survey into
a series of sedimentary laminae of
light-colored silt or fine sand in a dark clay matrix
in the 680 million year old Elatina formation at Pichi Richi pass, South Australia

(
G. E. Williams 1981
;
G. E. Williams 1985
;
G. E. Williams & C. P. Sonett 1985
)
. While the entire drilling project entailed
a series spanning around 19000 “varve years”,
most detailed information was drawn from
a sequence of 1337 varves obtained from enlarged
photographs of thin sections.
When interpreted as a proxy of solar activity,
a stunning feature of this
series is its extreme cleanliness which makes it
comparable, or even
superior, to more recent sequences of
cosmogenic isotopes
(
N. Brehm et al. 2021
)
, with the caveat that rhythmites are at first thickness sequences and not time series.
Quite early, this lead to speculations

(
J. Goguel 1982
;
G. Williams 1982
)

on whether the data are indeed a proxy of
solar activity, or rather of tidal effects, in which case
the laminae would
not represent yearly data but those related to the
(approximately) fortnightly spring tides
(see also
K. J. Zahnle & J. C. G. Walker 1987
and the discussion by

F.-L. Deubner 1990
,

F. L. Reineck & G. E. Williams 1990
and
R. Mazumder 2005
).

In this context, the problem of how to
comprehend the strong and clear nexus between solar activity and
climate went not unnoticed by the authors.
Williams tried to explain it by the low level of oxygen
in the Precambrian atmosphere, which would have resulted
in a strongly reduced height of the ozone layer,
allowing UV radiation to penetrate much deeper into the
atmosphere
(
G. E. Williams 1985
)
. Changes of local
ozone due to variations of solar UV might then have directly influenced ground-level temperature.
Actually, this idea does not contradict other possible
mechanism as, e.g., the top-bottom influence of
UV-variations on the cyclone paths,
as argued for
by
S. Prasad et al. 2004
in an attempt to
explain the influence
of solar activity on sediment layers in the
Lake Lisan region in much more recent times.

After Williams’ groundbreaking work,
it was mainly
Bracewell who analyzed in detail the
dominant periods contained in the Elatina series

(
R. N. Bracewell & G. E. Williams 1986
;
R. N. Bracewell 1988a
;
R. N. Bracewell 1988b
)
.
Decisive in this respect
was his idea to de-rectify the
\sim
12 -year
Schwabe-type cycle to a
\sim
24-year Hale-type cycle.
Using this method, the author was able
to constrain the main Hale cycle to 23.7 years,
with a very low
standard deviation of 0.2 years. This remarkable
precision of the “Elatina oscillator”, and
the resulting high Q-factor
of 120, let him to argue in favor of
some clock mechanism behind solar activity.
Interestingly, though citing Dicke’s seminal
work
(
R. H. Dicke 1978
)
on the distinction between
random walk and clocked processes, Bracewell
did not analyze the Elatina series
from this perspective.

At the end of the ’80ies, Williams, for one,
changed his mind and became an outspoken proponent
of the “tidal theory” of the Elatina series
(
G. E. Williams 1989
)
.
Indeed, the inferred numbers for the
lunar orbit and the length of day in the Precambrian
turned out to be quite realistic
(
F.-L. Deubner 1990
)
.
With that shift in attitude the original
“solar theory” lost some of its appeal, although
it is still pursued until recently

(
M. I. Tyasto et al. 2020
)
.

Our motivation to take up the “sunny trail”,
originally paved by Williams, Sonett and Bracewell,
stems from our efforts to establish a
synchronization model of the solar dynamo
that relies on the tidal action of the
orbiting planets.
This idea traces back to early work of

C.-C. Hung 2007
;
N. Scafetta 2012
;
I. R. G. Wilson 2013
;
V. P. Okhlopkov 2016
.
After the initial application of simple ODE and 1D-PDE
solar-dynamo models
(
F. Stefani et al. 2016
;
F. Stefani et al. 2018
;
F. Stefani et al. 2019
;
F. Stefani et al. 2021
)
,
the synchronization theory has matured over the
last years
(
F. Stefani et al. 2023
;
M. Klevs et al. 2023
;
G. M. Horstmann et al. 2023
;
F. Stefani et al. 2024
)
.
An account of its history, including
the debates with opponents,
can be found in

F. Stefani et al. 2025
.

In a nutshell, the recent version of the model builds
on the observation that
the typical periods of magneto-Rossby waves

(
M. Dikpati et al. 2017
;
M. Dikpati et al. 2020
;
B. Raphaldini & C. F. M. Raupp 2015
;
B. Raphaldini et al. 2019
;
T. V. Zaqarashvili et al. 2010
;
T. V. Zaqarashvili et al. 2021
)

at the solar
tachocline form a perfect resonance ground for the
two-planet spring tides with periods of (approximately) 118, 199 and
292 days, as exerted by the three pairs of the
tidally dominant planets Venus, Earth and Jupiter

(
G. M. Horstmann et al. 2023
;
F. Stefani et al. 2024
)
. While one beat
period of those three waves has a period of
1.723-yr period, which is well-known in solar physics as the QBO
(
F. Stefani et al. 2025
)
, the other dominant beat period
is 11.07 years which agrees well
with the average Schwabe cycle. The longer-term
Suess-de Vries cycle arises in this model
as a secondary beat period between the 22.14.year Hale
cycle and the rosette-shaped motion of Sun
around the barycenter of the solar system.
While this motion is known to be dominated by the
19.86-year cycle of Jupiter and Saturn,
the precise mechanism of dynamo-influencing
spin-orbit coupling is not
completely understood yet, despite promising ideas

(
I. R. G. Wilson 2013
;
J. E. Solheim 2013
;
J. H. Shirley 2006
;
J. H. Shirley 2023
)
.

With
P_{V}
,
P_{E}
,
P_{J}
,
P_{S}
,
P_{Sch}
,
P_{Hal}
,
P_{Bar}
,
P_{SdV}
denoting, respectively, the orbital periods of Venus, Earth, Jupiter, and Saturn, the Schwabe and Hale cycles, and the barycentric and Suess-de Vries cycles,
the three governing equations of the synchronization model
are as follows:

\displaystyle P_{Hal}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2P_{Sch}=\left(\frac{3}{P_{V}}-\frac{5}{P_{E}}+\frac{2}{P_{J}}\right)^{-1}\;,

(1)

\displaystyle P_{Bar}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{J}P_{S}}{P_{S}-P_{J}}\;,

(2)

and

\displaystyle P_{SdV}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{Hal}P_{Bar}}{P_{Hal}-P_{Bar}}\;.

(3)

Quite reassuring for the validity of this model was
the astonishing
coincidence of the spectral peaks arising from it
with those of climate-related sediment data from Lake Lisan

(
S. Prasad et al. 2004
)
(see Fig. 9 in
F. Stefani et al. 2024
).
The largely coinciding spectra
are indeed important in the context of the present work
as they show a
similar ordering of the dominant
periods as in the Elatina series,
just with modified numbers.
Also relevant is the high level of self-consistency
of the double-synchronization model, whose explanatory
capability ranges now from Rieger-type periods,
via the QBO to the Schwabe/Hale and finally
the Suess-de Vries cycle.

(a) Series of varve thicknesses in mm, as

Figure 1 of
R. N. Bracewell & G. E. Williams 1986
. (b) De-rectified

.

With this background, we feel well-prepared to
re-analyze the data from the Elatina series, now with
the main focus on phase stability and clocking, and its
potential explainability in terms of planetary periods.
When accepting the synchronized solar dynamo as the mediator, it becomes evident that for explaining the
observed period changes the orbits of the
planets must change as well. The problem is only: how much?
And further: how consistent are the derived orbital changes
with the solar system dynamics
over a time-span of 700 million years?

In order to make the paper self-contained,
we start with illustrating the
main data from the Elatina series.
Then we will analyze phase stability and clocking
in terms of Dicke’s ratio. Based on that,
we will infer the planetary orbits. As will be shown,
only minor changes of the angular momenta
of the planets
(around 1 percent for Jupiter and 0.005
per cent for Earth) are required to accommodate
the significant changes of the two observed
periods (7 per cent for Hale and 63 per cent
for Suess-de Vries).
In the discussion we will come back to the
caveat that the Elatina series might also be
interpreted in terms of ebb-tides deposits.

II
The Elatina series and its clocked character

In absence of the original data we digitized, similarly as

M. I. Tyasto et al. 2020
, the Elatina series data as published
by
R. N. Bracewell & G. E. Williams 1986
.
Figure 1a, which corresponds mainly to Figure
1 of
R. N. Bracewell & G. E. Williams 1986
,
shows the series of measured varve thicknesses
over 1337 “varve years”.
Even better digitization results can be
obtained from the de-rectified series of Figure
2 in
R. N. Bracewell & G. E. Williams 1986
which are shown here
in Figure 2b. Since we are mainly interested
in the series of minima and maxima, we consider
the accuracy of our digitizations to be sufficient.
As for the values of the modified Hale cycle we will,
at any rate, rely on the value 23.7 years as thoroughly
derived by
R. N. Bracewell 1988a
.
We will also use his value of 314 years for the
Elatina cycle, which will be identified with a
modified Suess-de Vries cycle, whose present
period is appr. 200 years.

After having graphically inferred the sequences of
minima and maxima of
the Elatina series, we analyze them now with
particular view on phase stability and clocking.
The first step for this is the determination
of the so-called residual

\delta_{n}=t_{n}-n\tilde{P}_{Sch}
, i.e. the distance
of the
n
-th
instant
t_{n}
of a maximum or minimum
from the corresponding instant that
would follow from a purely
linear trend when choosing
\tilde{P}_{Sch}

as a first guess of the Schwabe-type period.
For the cycle minima, those residuals
are shown as the violet curve
in Figure 2(a), for which we have chosen

\tilde{P}_{Sch}=11.95
years, a bit longer than
11.85 years as advocated by

R. N. Bracewell & G. E. Williams 1986
. Indeed, our choice leads to
a relatively horizontal appearance of the
curve, at least piecewise,
while a certain jumpy behavior becomes
already visible around the year 600.

For assessing phase stability and clocking,
in the following we will utilize
Dicke’s ratio

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\sum_{i=2}^{N}\delta^{2}_{i}}{\sum_{i=2}^{N}(\delta_{i}-\delta_{i-1})^{2}}

(4)

between the mean square of the residuals

\delta_{i}
and the mean square of the

differences

\delta_{i}-\delta_{i-1}

between two subsequent residuals. As
shown by
R. H. Dicke 1978
, the dependence of this ratio on
the number
N
of cycles taken into account
reads
(N+1)(N^{2}-1)/(3(5N^{2}+6N-3))
for a random walk
process, and
(N^{2}-1)/(2(N^{2}+2N+3))

for a clocked process. Hence,
for a random-walk process,
D

converges for
N\rightarrow\infty
towards

N/15
, while for a clocked process it converges
towards the constant value 0.5.
Both theoretical curves are shown in Figure 2(b), together with
Dicke’s ratio computed for the actual
residuals of the minima.
Note that the number
N
,
shown on the upper abscissa,
is increasing when
going from right to left, i.e. when
decreasing the starting year from
the terminal year 1337.

What becomes immediately visible in the violet curve of
Figure 2(b) are two distinct features. Starting close to
the right end (i.e., for low
N
), the curve first
clings to the (gold) clocked-process curve, but then jumps up
around the year 1200, swinging quickly above
the (light blue) random-walk curve. This overshoot effect is known
for processes that are governed by long-term
periodicities (see Figure 2 in
F. Stefani et al. 2019
).
Moving further
to the left, the curve remains rather horizontal, falling
below the (linearly increasing)
random-walk curve. Yet, around the year 600, it suddenly
jumps up again, before continuing further as a horizontal
curve.

To separate the different effects, we
subtract the long-term periods from the residuals’ curve in
Figure 2(a). Even by eyesight, those periods
appear similar to the so-called “Elatina cycle”
of 314 years
(
R. N. Bracewell & G. E. Williams 1986
)
and its first overtone.
The black curve in Figure 2(a) represents an
optimal fit of the violet curve
with two sine-functions whose periods
turn out to be 323 and 161 year, respectively.
Note that this result is by no means self-evident.
While the Elatina cycle of 314 years was originally inferred
from
amplitude variations
, we obtain here
a similar value
from the
variations of the residuals

(note that the same procedure provided also
the modern-day Suess-de Vries cycle, see
Figure 2 in
F. Stefani et al. 2020
).
It suggests itself to think here about the
usual Gnevyshev-Waldmeier rule of anticorrelation
between amplitude and duration
of solar cycles
(
Y. A. Nagovitsyn et al. 2019
)
.

After having subtracted the optimal double-sine fit,
we arrive at the
green curve in Figure 2(a), which generally shows less
variance, while the jump close to the year 600
becomes even more clearly expressed.
Remarkably, Dicke’s ratio following from it
(green curve in Figure 2(b))
clings now closely to the clocked-process, at least
down to the starting year 600. Then it undergoes
a significant jump, pointing again to
some break in the process here.

In a first attempt to explain this jumpy behavior,
we consider the hypothetical possibility that
one cycle somewhere
between the years 588-658 might
have been missed (indeed, there are five
consecutive cycles with a remarkably long
average duration of 14 years).
To assess this possibility, we
“smuggle” in one putative additional minimum
at the year 624.
The results of this “trickery” are shown in Figure 3.
As expected, close to the year 600,
the green curve in Figure 3(a) moves up.
The optimal fit of the Elatina period
changes slightly to 320.4 years. As for
Dicke’s ratio we see in Figure 3(b) that the jump close
to the year 600 still remains, although being slightly reduced in comparison with that in
Figure 2(b). Evidently, just inserting
one additional minimum, i.e. by
introducing a phase jump by 180
∘
, is not the right
solution.

But what about a phase jump of 90
∘
?
In frame of solar dynamo theory, this option
may sound very weird. For a clocked, but noisy,
process
one can easily understand some irregular breakdowns
after which the normal cycle comes back, just with a
180
∘
phase jump.
By contrast,
90
∘
phase jumps were only discussed so far
by
H. Vos et al. 2004
in their analysis
of algae-related proxies of the solar cycle in the early
Holocene. Yet, those
90
∘
phase jumps were explained by the authors
in terms of the optimal growth condition of the
investigated algae, and not by the underlying solar cycle.

We were, therefore, much surprised when recently stumbling
about 90
∘
phase jumps in the context of our
refined synchronization model of the solar dynamo

(
F. Stefani et al. 2025
)
in which
the QBO of 1.723 year played a key role.
In this model, the 11.07-year period shows up only
as an envelope in the
\alpha
-effect.
In absence of full-fleshed derivation of
the
\alpha
-effect, at least
we showed that the maximum
of this envelope may undergo shifts by 5.5 years when
the time average is changed.

In a first attempt to assess a possible 90
∘
phase-jump effect
for the Elatina series, we have replaced,
before the year 600, the minima of the series
by the maxima.
The results of this procedure are shown in Figure 4.
Remarkably, the former jump in Dicke’s ratio
close to the year 600 completely disappears,
as if a 90
∘
phase jump at this point would
perfectly describe the clocked process.
We will come back to this point in the conclusion,
where we will also discuss its ramification for the
choice between solar and tidal theories of the
Elatina series.

For the moment we just note that the Elatina series
shows a very strong sign of phase-stability and clocking,
just with one break point around the year 600, which needs
special consideration.

III
Modified orbital periods

Having obtained, in the last section,
strong evidence for a clocked solar dynamo
in the late Precambrian,
we ask now what changes of the planetary orbital period
would be required to accommodate the
modified Hale and Suess-de Vries
cycles as inferred from the
Elatina series. While it is not our goal here to enter
the most ambitious business of planetary system
dynamics for several hundred million years
(see, e.g.,
G. J. Sussman & J. Wisdom 1992
and
J. Laskar 2008
),
we will occasionally have a side view on some
known results in order to assess the plausibility
of our results.

The equation to start from is the expression of
the angular momentum
L_{i}
of a planet with
orbital period
P_{i}
and eccentricity
e_{i}
,
under the simplifying assumption of negligible inclination
and a mass
m_{i}

that is very small compared to the mass
M

of the central star:

\displaystyle L_{i}=m_{i}(GM/\sqrt{2\pi})^{2/3}P^{1/3}_{i}\sqrt{1-e^{2}_{i}}\;.

(5)

Evidently, the angular momentum depends both
on the orbital period
P_{i}
and the eccentricity
e_{i}
.
The accurate determination of their joint
time evolution, under the mutual influence of
the other planets, is most ambitious and will
not be considered here. We only note that, according
to the Laplace-Lagrange secular evolution theory,
the eccentricity of Jupiter is restricted
to values between 0.0256 and 0.0611,
while that of Saturn to
0.0121-0.0845 (see Table 10.4 in
R. Fitzpatrick 2012
).
Typically, both eccentricities evolve
in opposite direction
(
T. A. Michtchenko & S. Ferraz-Mello 2001
)
in order
to fulfill the conservation of the total angular
momentum. According to Equation (5), for Jupiter this
would amount to an angular momentum variation of
0.15 per cent.

III.1
Assuming unchanged eccentricity

In order to get a first clue about the typical
changes of the individual
orbital periods and angular momenta,
we start with the
(admittedly not very realistic)
case of unchanged eccentricities.
Modified eccentricities will be considered
further below.

Specifically, we assume 23.7 years
for the Hale cycle, and identify the 314-year Elatina period
(as thoroughly derived by
R. N. Bracewell 1988a
)
with a modified Suess-de Vries cycle.
While the former identification, in view of the relatively
minor change of only 7 per cent,
suggests itself,
the second one is certainly contentious.
Indeed, serious doubts seem appropriate when
one tries to explain a 63 per cent increase
from the today’s 193-year Suess-de Vries
by slightly changed orbital periods.

Yet, we should keep in mind the
high sensitivity of the beat periods in
Equations (1) and (3)
with respect to the small differences between
large numbers appearing in the respective
denominators. In the following, we will show that
only minor changes of the planetary orbits
are required for
accommodating simultaneously

the changes of the Hale and the Suess-de Vries
cycle.

Obviously, our inverse problem
of inferring the four periods, and eccentricities,
of
Venus, Earth, Jupiter and Saturn from only
two (modified) Hale and Suess-de Vries cycles
is massively underdetermined. To make the problem
tractable, we split it into two parts.
First, we solve Equation (3) to express
P_{Bar}

in terms of
P_{Hal}=23.7
years and
P_{SdV}=314
years,
leading to

\displaystyle P_{Bar}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P_{Hal}P_{SvD}}{P_{Hal}+P_{SvD}}=22.04\,{\mbox{years}}\;.

(6)

After having thus derived
P_{Bar}
,
we now employ Equation (2) in order to
obtain
P_{J}
and
P_{S}
. This is only possible
when taking into account a second equation, for which
we use the conservation of the sum of the angular momenta of
Jupiter and Saturn.
Assuming further, as mentioned above, the two
eccentricities to be unchanged
from the present ones, we
obtain from Equation (5)
the relation

\displaystyle P_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m_{J}\sqrt{1-e^{2}_{J,t}}}{m_{S}\sqrt{1-\epsilon^{2}_{S,t}}}(P^{1/3}_{J,t}-P^{1/3}_{J})+P^{1/3}_{S,t}\right)^{3}\;,

(7)

where
P_{J,t}=11.86
years,
e_{J,t}=0.049
,

P_{S,t}=29.46
years,

e_{S,t}=0.054
denote the periods and
eccentricities
of Jupiter and Saturn as of
today
.
With this two-planet angular momentum
constraint (7) we
solve, in
Figure 5, Equation (2) graphically.
From the intersection point of the
green and the black curves, we read off

P_{J}=12.21
years and,
correspondingly,
P_{S}=27.38
years.
For Jupiter this amounts to an increase of
P_{J}
by
2.95 per cent while for Saturn
P_{S}
decrease by
7.06 per cent. In view of the 1/3 power in
Equation (5), these values corresponds
to angular momentum changes of
0.98 and 2.35 per cent, respectively.

Before discussing the plausibility of
such values in terms
of celestial mechanics, let us first
complete our solution by determining
the corresponding period changes
for Earth and Venus. For that purpose we
enter again Equation (1) with the modified Jupiter
period
P_{J}=12.21
years
and
P_{Hal}=23.7
years.
Once more, we need a second equation for the simultaneous
determination of
P_{V}
and
P_{E}
.
In contrast to the physically well justified
pairwise angular momentum
constraint for the two most massive
planets Jupiter and Saturn, the application of this
principle for Venus and Earth is
less plausible. If we still apply it,
and also keep the present eccentricities

e_{V,t}=0.007
and
e_{V,t}=0.017

unchanged, we get

\displaystyle P_{E}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m_{V}\sqrt{1-e^{2}_{V,t}}}{m_{E}\sqrt{1-e^{2}_{E,t}}}(P^{1/3}_{V,t}-P^{1/3}_{V})+P^{1/3}_{E,t}\right)^{3}\;.

(8)

The results are illustrated in Figure 6.
What becomes immediately clear is the high sensitivity
of
P_{Hal}
with respect to very minor changes of

P_{V}
and
P_{E}
. Indeed, the resulting orbital period
of Venus,
P_{V}=0.61505
years, represents only
a decrease of 0.023 per cent with respect to
today’s value
P_{V}=0.61519
years.
For the Earth, the corresponding value is
0.015 per cent (1.00015 vs 1 year).
For the angular momenta those values amount
to 0.0077 and 0.005 per cent, respectively.

While, in principle, we should also
consider changes of the sidereal year

as the basic unit of time
by which
any physical period is measured on Earth,
we see now that this is basically unimportant
for the interpretation of the final results.

III.2
Robustness check with modified eccentricities

Since the assumption of constant eccentricities as used
above is certainly not physical, in the following
we assess the robustness of our
inversion. Although the present eccentricity
value of Jupiter is already
close to the maximum as predicted by
Laplace-Lagrange secular perturbation theory,
we nevertheless check what would happen if this value were
increased
to 0.1 or even 0.2. It might be worthwhile to
assess those high values, particularly
in view of recent theories that try to explain
the late Precambrian “snowball earth” in terms of
higher frequency of impacts from the asteroid belt

(
M. Fu et al. 2024
)
. Those are known to be
strongly fostered by high
eccentricities of Jupiter
(see, e.g., Figure 1 in
J. Horner & B. W. Jones 2012
).

Therefore in Figure 7 we redo the computations
of the previous subsection, but use a high
eccentricity of Jupiter
of 0.1 and 0.2 and zero eccentricity for Saturn, as well
as vice versa. With that pairwise choice
of high and low eccentricities we pay credit
to their typical anti-phase evolution

(
T. A. Michtchenko & S. Ferraz-Mello 2001
;
T. Ito & K. Tanikawa 1996
)
.

Figure 7:
Graphical solution of Equation (2) under the
pairwise angular-momentum constraint (7) for

(0.1 and 0.2)
for either Jupiter and Saturn, and zero

Remarkably, the solutions of our
attribution problem turn out to be pretty robust against
the inclusion of much higher eccentricities. Specifically,
we obtain
orbital periods of Jupiter between 12.208 and 12.215 years,
and orbital periods of Saturn between 27.37 and 27.39 years,
which represents in either case less than 0.1 per cent variation.
Hence, the derived periods seem to be
relatively safe, provided that
the Elatina series indeed represents a proxy
of solar activity, and that our
synchronization theory is basically correct.

We just mention here that the
corresponding results for Venus and Earth
are very robust against
changing eccentricities,
for which Laskar’s
very long and highly accurate simulations had
found variations
between 0 and 0.06 (for Earth). Note again
that,
owing to the chaotic behavior, the precision of
the solution decreases rapidly
for simulation times beyond 40 Myr
(
J. Laskar et al. 2011
)
.

IV
Conclusions

In this paper, we have reconsidered the Elatina series
of sedimentary laminae from South Australia
which, presumably, represents
an amazingly accurate proxy of solar activity
in the Precambrian around 680 Million years ago.
We relied on, and reconfirmed, the existence of a
Hale-type cycle with a
mean period of 23.7 years, and a longer-term “Elatina cycle”
of 314 years
as found previously by
R. N. Bracewell & G. E. Williams 1986
. Our
first focus laid on
the phase stability of the Schwabe cycle which we
assessed by analyzing Dicke’s ratio for the
residuals of the cycle minima. When subtracting the strong

\sim
314-yr cycle, and its first overtone,
we found indeed a nearly perfect clocking within two
time-segments divided approximately
at the “varve year” 600,
where five relatively long intervals occur consecutively.
While a tentative insertion of one additional
minimum
at this instant did not much improve Dicke’s ratio,
we found that the exchange of cycle minima by
maxima at this point
leads to a nearly perfect convergence of Dicke’s ratio against the theoretical value of 0.5
for a clocked process. While such a replacement
may seem heavily contrived,
it could make perfect sense in light of the recently
revealed possibility of 90
∘
phase jumps in the solar cycle.

Note also that a 90
∘
phase jump provides another strong argument for
the “solar theory” of the Elatina series, since - as far as we are aware of - the competing “tidal theory”
(
K. J. Zahnle & J. C. G. Walker 1987
;
G. E. Williams 1989
;
F.-L. Deubner 1990
)

would not offer any possibility to explain it.
In summary, whatever the specific role of this break point
might be, the overall behavior of Dicke’s ratio gives
strong support for the synchronization theory of
the solar dynamo.

Thus encouraged, we have asked what planetary
orbits would be required to explain the observations.
For that purpose we tentatively interpreted the
314-yr cycle as nothing else than a modified
Suess-de Vries cycle. Our task was, then, to
explain simultaneously a 7 per cent change of the Hale cycle
and a 63 per cent change
of the Suess-de Vries cycle. Assuming
pairwise angular momentum conservation of
Jupiter/Saturn and Venus/Earth, respectively, our
inverse problem turned out to be
unique when keeping the ellipticities constant. For
Jupiter we then arrived at a 3 per cent increase
of its orbital period, which amounts to 1 per cent for the angular momentum.
The corresponding orbital period change for the Earth
turned out to be 0.015 per cent, corresponding to an
angular momentum change of 0.005 per cent
(the values for Venus are similar).

The mathematical reason for the major
effect of relatively
minor modifications of
orbital periods on the
Hale and (even more so) the Suess-de Vries cycle
lies in the fact that both cycles represent

beat periods

which include small differences
between large numbers in the
denominator.

While - as non-experts in celestial mechanics - we
are not in a position to numerically
validate our results, they seem not to be
completely unrealistic.
Admittedly, the usual eccentricity swaps
between Jupiter and Saturn, as computed in frame of the
Laplace-Lagrange secular evolution theory

(
R. Fitzpatrick 2012
)
,
imply only (individual) angular momentum changes
of around 0.15 per cent for Jupiter and 0.34
per cent for Saturn, which are markedly below
the respective values of 1 per cent and 2.3 per cent
as derived by us. Yet, it should
be emphasized that this theory is only approximate
in nature and capable of predicting the secular evolution
of the solar system with reasonable accuracy up to a million
or so years. Over longer timescales,
it becomes inaccurate because the true long-term dynamics of the
solar system contains chaotic elements resulting from resonances.
As shown by
D. Psychoyos & J. D. Hadjidemetriou 2004
, Jupiter-Saturn-like
planetary pairs in 5:2 resonance
can easily develop variations of their semi-major axes
in the order of a few per cent (with that of “Saturn”
being larger, see Figure 8 in
D. Psychoyos & J. D. Hadjidemetriou 2004
).
This applies already for typical eccentricity variation
in the order of 0.1, while unstable orbits,
including escapes, can also develop
for slightly different parameters.

Certainly, the inferred relation of 2.24 of the periods
of Saturn and Jupiter differs markedly from the
present value of 2.48, which is close to the
mentioned 5:2 resonance.
One might be tempted here to speculate about a rather smooth evolution between the 2:1 resonance in the early phase of the
solar system evolution and the present

\approx
5:2 resonance. However, this
could be misleading since
that transition
is typically assumed to have
occurred much earlier. Another speculation
is related to the impact theory
(
M. Fu et al. 2024
)

of the “snowball Earth”
period to which the Elatina formation belongs.
As shown by
J. Horner & B. W. Jones 2012
a strongly
enhanced eccentricity
of Jupiter of 0.1 could have increased the
impact frequency
of asteroids by a factor of 2.
It is legitimate to ask then whether the
significant period change as argued for here
could indeed be connected with a corresponding
change of the eccentricity.
