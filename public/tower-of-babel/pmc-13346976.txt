Integrated respiratory metabolism and hemolymph proteome of caste specific features during honeybee (Apis mellifera L.) development
Authors: Xuancheng Dong, Beibei Ma, Yu Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As a typical eusocial insect, honeybee exhibits a close relationship between social caste and developmental patterns. However, the respiratory metabolism and hemolymph proteomic profiles of the three honeybee castes during development remain unclear. Here, employing respirometry detection approach and high-resolution mass spectrometry, we for the first time build up the comprehensive dataset of respiratory metabolic rates, physical body weights and hemolymph proteome profiles of worker bees, drones, and queens at different developmental stages, including the larval, pupal, and adult stages. Furthermore, a total of 2,198 proteins were identified, including 1,508 in worker bees, 1,466 in drones, and 1,146 in queens. The integrated data provides a crucial foundation for in-depth analysis of the biological characteristics of honeybees and offers critical support for subsequent research.

Background & Summary

Honeybees (Apis mellifera ligustica) are essential pollinators for ecosystems and agriculture. They enable the reproduction of nearly 90% of wild flowering plants and 35% of global food crops1, contributing €153 billion annually (9.5% of global agricultural output)2,3. Furthermore, bee products like honey, wax, and propolis drive significant economic value across the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetics industries.

A honeybee colony consists of a queen, workers, and drones with distinct roles. Queens and workers develop from fertilized eggs, while drones develop from unfertilized eggs4. The queen lays thousands of eggs daily and maintains colony cohesion through queen mandibular pheromones, particularly 9-oxo-(E)−2-decenoic acid5–7. Worker bees perform all labor tasks—cleaning, brood care, wax production, and foraging—with their roles changing as they age8,9. Drones contribute only genetic material and die shortly after mating10–12.

Honeybee development follows complete metamorphosis through egg, larval, pupal, and adult stages9, with caste-specific variations in duration. Pupation lasts approximately 8 days in queens, 12 days in workers, and 14–15 days in drones5,13. Development is regulated by both genetic programming and nutrition; female larvae develop into queens or workers based on their diet—royal jelly or bee bread14,15. Developmental transitions involve significant physiological changes, particularly in respiratory metabolism, which determines energy balance16. Temperature and physiological state substantially influence honeybee respiratory metabolic rates16–20. Larval metabolic rates increase during growth, decrease sharply during pupation, and rise again before eclosion21. In adult bees, metabolic rates correlate positively with body weight16, and flight metabolism can reach up to 48 times the resting state, reflecting the relationship between energy demand and behavioral patterns18.

In the insect open circulatory system, hemolymph plays a central role in substance transport, immune defense, and developmental regulation22–24. In honeybees, hemolymph delivers metabolites to reproductive organs like ovaries to support egg-laying25, defends against pathogens through coagulation and hemocyte phagocytosis26–28, and adjusts its proteome in response to environmental stress29–31. Hemolymph proteome composition varies by caste and developmental stage. Nutrient storage proteins such as vitellogenin are significantly more abundant in queens than workers32,33; worker larvae are enriched in growth-related enzymes, while adult workers are dominated by immune-related proteins34. Additionally, protein-folding and glycolysis pathways differ across honeybee strains35. These findings suggest that hemolymph proteome dynamics may underlie metabolic adaptations.

Although patterns in honeybee social division of labor and metabolic characteristics have been documented, a comprehensive understanding integrating respiratory metabolism across castes (worker, drone, queen) and developmental stages (larva, pupa, adult) with hemolymph proteome composition remains lacking. We combined high-precision respirometry with label-free quantitative proteomics and bioinformatics to: (i) correlate body weight and energy metabolic rates among honeybee castes and developmental stages; (ii) analyze hemolymph protein profiles across developmental stages of workers, drones, and queens. These findings provide a foundation for assessing colony health and advancing selective breeding, with implications for sustainable apiculture and ecological conservation.

Methods

Sample collection

All honeybee colonies used in this experiment were maintained at the apiary of the Institute of Apicultural Research, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. Three healthy Apis mellifera ligustica colonies of comparable strength were selected. Each colony served as one biological replicate.

Due to the development duration specialized in honeybee castes, we selected the representative samples based on the morphological development of honeybee. Queens and workers typically complete caste differentiation by the 3rd day of the larval stage, and by the 4th day, larvae can be clearly distinguished as workers, drones, and queens. The 1st day of the pupal stage and the day of adult emergence mark distinct transitions between developmental stages. Therefore, the 4th day of the larval stage, the 1st day of the pupal stage, and the 1st day after adult emergence were selected as representative stages for larvae, pupae, and adults, respectively (Fig. 1).

Body Weight Measurement

The body weight of each sample was measured using an electronic analytical balance (PL 203, Mettler Toledo, USA). Each group consisted of 30 individuals, and the weight of each bee was recorded separately (Fig. 2a–c). GraphPad Prism 9 (Graphpad Software, LLC, USA) was used for graphing and Ordinary one-way ANOVA was adopted for data analysis.

Respiratory Metabolism Measurement

To assess respiratory metabolism, an eight-channel respiratory metabolism measurement system was employed. Briefly, bee individuals of the three castes at the same developmental stage were placed in respiration chambers of the SSI Insect Respiratory Metabolism Measurement System (Sable System, USA). Magnesium perchlorate (Alfa Aesar, UK), UKDRIERITE (Capitol Scientific, USA), and a carbon dioxide absorbent (Molecular Products, UK) were used as reagents.

The CO₂ production rate of each individual was recorded (n = 24). Each measurement lasted for 5 minutes and was repeated three consecutive times. The average of the three readings was calculated as the CO₂ production rate (Fig. 2d). The graphs were created using GraphPad Prism 9 software (Graphpad Software, LLC, USA), and the data analysis was performed using Ordinary two-way ANOVA.

Hemolymph Collection

To collect hemolymph accurately, specific approaches were applied to honeybees at different developmental stages. Because the physiological structure, cuticle hardness, and hemolymph distribution vary greatly across stages, these tailored methods ensure efficient collection while strictly avoiding contamination from the digestive tract (Fig. 1). For larvae, the cuticle was punctured at the head with a sterile needle, as the head capsule is relatively thin, rich in hemolymph, and puncturing this region minimizes the risk of gut rupture. The larval body was then gently pressed to facilitate exudation, and the fluid was collected using a 0.5 mm capillary tube36. For pupae, individuals were placed dorsally to align the head and abdomen. A needle was used to puncture approximately 1 mm into the base of the antennae, which remains a soft cuticular area during this stage. Subsequent gentle twisting of the thorax and abdomen facilitated hemolymph outflow37. For adult bees, removing one antenna exposes a natural opening at the antennal socket, allowing for collection without piercing the body cavity. Protein Extraction

To extract proteins from pooled hemolymph samples and determine the protein concentration accurately. A 20 µL pooled hemolymph sample collected from individuals at the same developmental stage from same colony was mixed with 100 µL of lysis buffer. Ultrasonicate at 4 °C for 10 minutes to allow complete release of proteins from the sample. Centrifuge at 4 °C and 12,000 g for 20 minutes, transfer the clear middle supernatant to a new tube, then add 5 volumes of acetone to precipitate the proteins at −20 °C for 20 minutes, followed by centrifugation at 4 °C and 12,000 g for 20 minutes. The supernatant was discarded, and the protein pellet was dissolved in urea solution. After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 15 minutes at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay39 with a standard curve generated from bovine serum albumin according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Peptide Preparation

Based on the measured protein concentration, 50 μg of protein was used for digestion. Added one-tenth volume of 100 mM DTT. After incubating at room temperature for 1 hour, 100 mM IAA at five times the volume of DTT was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark for 1 hour. Dilute the urea concentration in the sample to less than 500 mM using 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate solution containing 10 mM glycine. Trypsin was then added at a sample-to-enzyme ratio of 50:1 (v/v), and digestion was performed overnight (12–14 hours) at 37 °C. The reaction was terminated with 1 μL of FA. Perform desalting using C18 solid-phase extraction (SPE) columns. The eluate was transferred to a rotary evaporator (RVC 2–18, Marin Christ, DE) for desiccation. The dried peptides were re-dissolved in 50 μL of 0.1% FA solution. The peptide concentration was determined using a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop 2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), adjust the final concentration to 0.5 μg/μL.

LC-MS/MS Analysis

Proteins were analyzed using a tandem mass spectrometer (Orbitrap Exploris 240, Thermo Scientific, USA) coupled with a high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Easy-nLC 1200, Thermo Scientific, USA). The sample was first loaded onto the pre-column (Acclaim PepMap100 column, 2 cm × 100 μm, C18, 3 μm, Omics technology Co., Ltd, China) and then separated through the chromatographic column (EASY-Spray column, 15 cm × 150 μm, C18, 2 μm, Omics technology Co., Ltd, China). The HPLC injection flow rate was set at 500 nL/min, with an injection volume of 4 μL. Mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% FA, and mobile phase B consisted of 80% ACN with 0.1% FA. The elution was performed over 60 minutes using the following gradient: 0–2 min, 92% A and 8% B; 2–40 min, 88% A and 12% B; 40–51 min, 70% A and 30% B; 51–54 min, 55% A and 45% B; 54–60 min, 10% A and 90% B. Full MS scans were acquired using electrospray ionization at a resolution of 120,000 over a scan range of 350–1500 m/z, and automatic gain control (AGC) target was 2.0e6, maximum IT was 50 ms. dd-MS2 scans were performed using higher-energy collisional dissociation mode, with a resolution of 15,000, AGC target was 7.5e4, and a normalized collision energy of 30%.

Proteomics data processing and statistical analysis

The raw mass spectrometry data were processed using PEAKS Studio 8.5 (Bioinformatics Solutions Inc., Waterloo, Canada). Searches were conducted against two databases: the Apis mellifera protein database (NCBI, April 2024; 31,658 entries) and the contaminant database (cRAP, April 2022; 115 entries). Search parameters were set as follows: precursor ion mass tolerance of 20 ppm; fragment ion mass tolerance of 0.05 Da; trypsin as the proteolytic enzyme allowing up to 2 missed cleavages; fixed modification of carbamidomethylation of cysteine (C, + 57.02 Da); and variable modification of oxidation of methionine (M, + 15.99 Da). The false discovery rate (FDR) was controlled below 1% at both the peptide and protein levels. Only proteins with two or more unique peptides were considered identified. The label-free approach was implemented with Peaks Studio 8.5, applying a mass error tolerance of 20 ppm and a retention time shift tolerance of 6.0 minutes. Peptide abundance was calculated by summing peak areas of each feature. Protein expression levels were derived from the corresponding peptide ion intensities measured across three technical replicates. Data normalization was performed to adjust for inter-run variability and to account for systematic biases introduced during sample processing. Variations in protein expression across different developmental stages were assessed by one-way analysis of variance. A change in protein abundance was considered statistically significant if it reached a minimum fold change of 1.5 with a p-value below 0.05.

Technical Validation

To ensure the reliability and quality of the proteomics data, comprehensive technical validation was performed for both qualitative and quantitative analyses. As depicted in Fig. 3, the precursor ion mass error distribution (Fig. 3a) exhibited a narrow peak centered around 0 ppm, indicating high mass accuracy of the identified peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs). The protein coverage analysis (Fig. 3b) demonstrated high-quality identification, with 64.96% of the proteins exhibiting sequence coverage greater than 10%, a high level that ensures the reliability of protein identification and provides a robust foundation for subsequent quantitative analysis. The vast majority of peptides generated by enzymatic digestion fall within the optimal length range of 7–25 amino acids, primarily contributed by + 2 and + 3 charge state peptides (Fig. 3c), reflecting high-quality sample preparation and thorough digestion. Additionally, the detection of longer peptides with + 4 to + 6 charge states demonstrates the instrument’s excellent ionization stability and detection sensitivity, confirming its high performance in covering a broad variety of peptide species. Furthermore, the missed cleavage analysis (Fig. 3d) revealed a low proportion of peptides with multiple missed cleavages, confirming efficient enzymatic digestion.
