Galaxy mass profiles with convolutional neural networks
J. Sarrato-Alós ,, C. Brook ,, A. Di Cintio ,, J. Expósito-Márquez ,, M. Huertas-Company , ,, Andrea V. Macciò , ,

Abstract
Context. Determining the dynamical mass profiles of dispersion-supported galaxies is particularly challenging due to projection effects and the unknown shape of their velocity anisotropy profile. Aims. Our goal is to develop a machine learning algorithm capable of recovering dynamical mass profiles of dispersion-supported galaxies from line-of-sight stellar data. Traditionally, this task relies on time-consuming methods that require profile parameterization and assume dynamical equilibrium and spherical symmetry. Methods. We train a convolutional neural network model using various sets of cosmological hydrodynamical simulations of galaxies. By extracting projected stellar data from the simulated galaxies and feeding it into the model, we obtain the posterior distribution of the dynamical mass profile at ten different radii. Additionally, we evaluate the performance of existing literature mass estimators on our dataset. Results. Our model achieves more accurate results than any literature mass estimator while also providing enclosed mass estimates at radii where no previous estimators exist. We confirm that the posterior distributions produced by the model are well-calibrated, ensuring they provide meaningful uncertainties. However, issues remain, as the method loses performance when trained on one set of simulations and applied to another, highlighting the importance of improving the generalization of ML methods trained on specific galaxy simulations.



1
Introduction

Understanding the dynamical mass distribution within galaxies is crucial for testing the cold dark matter paradigm. In rotation-supported galaxies, mass is inferred from rotational velocities, whereas dispersion-supported galaxies require kinematic studies based on spectroscopic data to trace stellar motions. However, these measurements are subject to projection effects, making it difficult to reconstruct the full three-dimensional structure of the galaxy.

Dynamical modelling provides a robust approach to address these challenges. Advanced techniques, often combined with Monte Carlo simulations, yield robust mass estimates with well-calibrated uncertainties. However, these models are still affected by the mass-anisotropy degeneracy; unknown velocity anisotropies can bias the inferred mass distribution, with some methods being more sensitive to these effects than others. Moreover, the computational expense and the need for careful system modelling further complicate the process.

In contrast with dynamical modelling, several authors
(
Walker et al. 2009
;
Wolf et al. 2010
;
Amorisco & Evans 2012
;
Campbell et al. 2017
;
Errani et al. 2018
, e.g.,)
have developed simple formulae to estimate the dynamical mass of galaxies enclosed within specific radii, where they have found velocity anisotropy and/or other factors to minimise uncertainty. These estimators rely on the line-of-sight velocity dispersion of the stellar component and on the half-light radius, aiming to obtain an unbiased mass estimate that holds for a broad range of galaxy properties and that is minimally affected by line-of-sight projection effects.

In recent years, machine learning has proven to be an effective tool for analysing complex data and uncovering underlying patterns. These methods have been applied to various astrophysical problems, including dynamical mass estimation and density profile determination using line-of-sight data. For example,
de los Rios et al. 2023
used convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to analyse mock photometry and interferometry images from cosmological hydrodynamical simulations of spiral galaxies, successfully inferring 20 points of their dynamical mass profiles. Similarly,
Expósito-Márquez et al. 2023
employed 2-d probability distribution functions of projected stellar positions and kinematics as inputs to a CNN to estimate the inner slopes (150 pc from the centre) of dark matter density profiles in dwarf galaxies. This approach resulted in a model which was able to differentiate between cusps and cores in cosmological hydrodynamical simulations.
Ho et al. 2019
applied a CNN model with a similar input structure based on probability distribution functions of positions and velocities of galaxies to estimate the dynamical masses of galaxy clusters. Also,
Nguyen et al. 2023
developed a graph neural network capable of accurately recovering the dark matter density profiles of mock spherical, dynamically equilibrated dwarf galaxies.

In this paper we build a CNN based on that used in
Expósito-Márquez et al. 2023
, and we train it on a mixed dataset of cosmological hydrodynamical simulations of dispersion-supported galaxies spanning a broad range of stellar mass and other galactic properties. Our model uses projected stellar information to estimate the dynamical mass profile of galaxies providing the posterior distribution of the values of ten points of the dynamical mass profile of the galaxy. We compare the capabilities of our model with five existing literature mass estimators
(
Walker et al. 2009
;
Wolf et al. 2010
;
Amorisco & Evans 2012
;
Campbell et al. 2017
;
Errani et al. 2018
)
. We find our model provides improved scatter in the estimation of dynamical mass, on top of its ability to provide new points of the mass profile at radii where no literature mass estimators have been proposed.

The structure of this article is the following: In Section
2
we describe the simulations used to test the literature mass estimators and train the CNN, and also the process we follow to prepare the dataset to be inputted into the model. In Section
3
we introduce the existing mass estimators in the literature, shortly explaining their derivation and assumptions. We describe our CNN model in Section
4
, where we show its architecture and explain the structure of inputs and outputs. In Section
5
we present the results of our work. First in Section
5.1
we show the performance of literature mass estimators on our dataset of simulated galaxies and then in Section
5.2
we compare with the results of our model. We summarise our conclusions in Section

2
Simulation Dataset

Training a CNN to estimate galaxy dynamical mass requires an extensive dataset of realistic simulated galaxies from which we can extract stellar data to use as input for the network, as well as calculate the enclosed mass profile we aim to estimate. We decide to use cosmological hydrodynamical simulations over idealised simulations, choosing to train our model on a more realistic dataset for which we have limited sampling capability, rather than on a less realistic dataset that we can generate and sample faster and more uniformly.

Since we are restricted to a dataset of previously run simulations, we aim for it to sample the space of galaxy properties as completely as possible, providing a large enough number of galaxies for the study. We find that a set of simulations based on the NIHAO project
(
Wang et al. 2015
)
fulfils these requirements. In order to assess possible biases in the model we also select other suites of simulations to compare the performance of the model. For that, we also make use of publicly available data for the AURIGA
(
Grand et al. 2024
)
and the FIRE-2
(
Wetzel et al. 2023
)
simulations.

2.2
The AURIGA project

The cosmological parameters used in the simulations from the AURIGA project come from
Planck Collaboration et al. 2014
, just like in the NIHAO project.

The galaxy formation model implemented in simulations from the AURIGA project includes magnetic fields, primordial line metal cooling which takes account self-shielding, stellar feedback and thermal feedback from radio and quasar accretion modes of black holes. The AURIGA simulation suite effectively reproduces the properties of real galaxies, particularly Milky Way-like systems.

The simulations also produce realistic rotation curves and star formation histories. 2.4
Training Dataset

Our dataset comprises 496 zoom-in cosmological simulations extracted from the NIHAO project, 66 zoom-in simulations from the AURIGA project and 17 zoom-in simulations from the FIRE project. Besides the fiducial NIHAO dataset, we include simulations of NIHAO galaxies with different stellar formation density thresholds
n_{\rm th}

(
Dutton et al. 2020
)

(
Blank et al. 2019
)
and high resolution runs of 6 NIHAO simulations
(
Buck et al. 2019
)

For all simulations in the NIHAO+AURIGA dataset, we select all galaxies with stellar masses ranging from
M_{*}=10^{5.5}
to
M_{*}=10^{11}
that contain at least 50 star particles and whose mass fraction in high resolution particles is higher than 95%. Out of such galaxies, we select the ones that are considered dispersion-supported systems according to the criterion
\kappa_{\textrm{co}}<0.5
.
\kappa_{\textrm{co}}

Correa et al. 2017

\kappa_{\textrm{co}}=\frac{1}{K_{\textrm{star}}}\sum_{i}^{r<\textrm{30 kpc}\;\;;\;\;j_{\textrm{z,i}}>0}\frac{m_{\textrm{i}}}{2}\left(\frac{j_{\parallel,\textrm{i}}}{R_{\perp,\textrm{i}}}\right)^{2}

(1)

where
K_{\textrm{star}}
is the total kinetic energy of stars, and the sum is over all star particles within 30 kpc of the centre of the galaxy and with positive values of the specific angular momentum (
j_{\parallel}
) along the direction of the total angular momentum of the stellar component of the galaxy.
m
is the mass of each stellar particle and
R_{\perp}
is the distance from the star to the rotation axis of the stellar component. We further improve the quality of the dataset by manually removing ongoing mergers and heavily disrupted galaxies identified through visual inspection. Applying these filters results in a sample of 1976 galaxies from the NIHAO project, 1066 from the AURIGA project, and 192 from the FIRE project.

We sample stars randomly from each galaxy with a sample size between 200 and
10^{4}
, depending on the total number of star particles in the original galaxy. Then we project the stellar data of galaxies along 64 lines-of-sight uniformly distributed along the unit sphere. We carry on this process for galaxies of all three simulations in order to construct datasets where we can test the performance of models over all lines-of-sight. For each projection we also calculate the projected half-light radius (half-stellar number

3
Mass Estimators

Figure 3
:
Scheme of our CNN architecture. The CNN extracts the spatial and dynamical information of the galaxy from the projected stellar data and compresses it through a series of convolution and pooling operations. The CNN joins the information of the spatial and dynamical branches and further reduces the dimensionality for producing a N parameter output, representing the value estimated for the dynamical mass of the galaxy enclosed within N different radii. After training the CNN, the 32 neurons of penultimate layer are used as inputs to train a normalising flow model. The flow model learns a series of transformations to a N-dimensional gaussian PDF, which are conditioned on the inputs, and outputs a posterior N-dimensional joint PDF for the N enclosed masses.

Several works in the literature have developed simple formulae for calculating galaxy dynamical masses enclosed within different radii from projected quantities only. These estimators make use of the projected half-light radius
R_{\textrm{h}}
of the galaxy and the line-of-sight velocity dispersion of the stellar component either in all the galaxy, at a specific distance from the centre or within a given radius. We introduce here the most common mass estimators that we test on our simulations in Sec.
5.1
.

The first of such mass estimators is that of
Peñarrubia et al. 2008
. They find that in the case of isotropic (
\beta(r)=0
) stellar components following a King profile inside a
Navarro et al. 1997
halo, the error of using the central stellar component’s projected velocity dispersion for calculating the dynamical mass of galaxies is minimised within the King core radius (
R_{c}
). Their mass estimator takes the form
M(<R_{c})=1.44G^{-1}R_{c}\sigma_{los}^{2}(0)
.

Walker et al. 2009
make the less restrictive assumption that the anisotropy profile is flat throughout all the galaxy, and that the stellar component follows a Plummer spherical profile. They argue that the error on mass estimation introduced by the constant anisotropy is small when using the estimator
M(<R_{h})=2.5G^{-1}R_{h}\left\langle\sigma_{los}^{2}\right\rangle
that uses the line-of-sight stellar velocity dispersion over the whole galaxy.

Wolf et al. 2010
derived analytically a new mass estimator that minimises the effect of a non-uniform, three-parameter anisotropy profile when calculating the mass within
r_{3}
, the radius where the log-slope of the stellar density profile is 3. Through a series of approximations they transform their mass estimator in order to apply it to calculate the mass within
4/3R_{h}
:
M(<4/3R_{h})=4G^{-1}R_{h}\left\langle\sigma_{los}^{2}\right\rangle
.

For stellar components modelled using the Michie-King distribution function,
Amorisco & Evans 2012
find
M(<1.67R_{h})=5.85G^{-1}R_{h}\sigma_{los}^{2}(R_{h})
.

Campbell et al. 2017
used the APOSTLE simulations
(
Sawala et al. 2016
)
to derive a mass estimator for larger radii
M(<1.77R_{h})=5.99G^{-1}R_{h}\left\langle\sigma_{los}^{2}(<1.04R_{h})\right\rangle
.

For a similar radius,
Errani et al. 2018
found that
M(<1.8R_{h})=3.5\times 1.8G^{-1}R_{h}\left\langle\sigma_{los}^{2}\right\rangle
minimises the errors introduced by the shape of the tidally-stripped dark matter halo (cusp or core) in which lays a Plummer sphere stellar component.

Some mass estimators have been tested on cosmological simulations. Apart from their own estimator,
Campbell et al. 2017
tested Walker’s and Wolf’s mass estimators on fully cosmological hydrodynamical simulations from the APOSTLE project.
González-Samaniego et al. 2017
tested Walker’s, Wolf’s and Campbell’s mass estimators on 10 dwarf galaxy cosmological hydrodynamical simulations from the FIRE-2 project
(
Hopkins et al. 2018b
)
. Both studies obtained different results for the biases and specially for the scatters of the three mass estimators.

Neural Network

We use the python’s package
tensorflow

Abadi et al. 2015

4.1
Input

The inputs of our neural network must represent the line-of-sight projected dynamical data of stars in galaxies in an uniform way such that we can use it to represent the data of both smaller and larger galaxies with enough detail. At the same time, the inputs must include information about the size of the galaxies, since galaxy size correlates with mass.

We construct the inputs using the x and y line-of-sight projected positions of stars in the sky, and their velocities along the line-of-sight
v_{los}
, for each projection of each galaxy in the dataset. Additionally we use the projected half-light radius
R_{h}
, the 98th percentile of the line-of-sight velocity
v_{los,p98}
, and the line-of-sight velocity dispersion of stars
\sigma_{los}
. We calculate these quantities for each projection using only the stars in the sub-sample corresponding to the projection, rather than all stars in the galaxy.

We choose to use two different 2-d arrays with dimensions 64x64 as inputs. The arrays represent samples of 2-d joint probability distribution functions (PDFs) of projected stellar data. We construct the PDFs using bivariate kernel density estimation (KDE), which is described in Appendix
A
. We find that these maps impact more positively the training of the model when we construct them using only stars within a radius of 0.5
R_{h}
. The input arrays are described next.

•

Input 1: A 2-d PDF of the projected positions of stars in the sky, normalised by
R_{h}
(The two dimensions of the array are {x/
R_{h}
, y/
R_{h}
}). We sample the PDF in a grid between -0.5 and 0.5 in both dimensions to create a 64x64 array.

•

Input 2: A 2-d PDF in the space {
R_{xy}
/
R_{h}
,
v_{los}
/
v_{los,p98}
} (where
R_{xy}=\sqrt{x^{2}+y^{2}}
). We sample the PDF in a grid between -0.5 and 0.5 in the
R_{xy}
/
R_{h}
dimension and between -1 and 1 in the
v_{los}
/
v_{los,p98}
dimension, obtaining a 64x64 array.

•

Input 3: Since literature mass estimators make use of line-of-sight velocity dispersion
\sigma_{los}
and projected half-light radius
R_{h}
, we decide to also input these values to the final layers of the network. On top of that, we also include the value
v_{los,p98}
, as we have used it for scaling one dimension of the array in input 2. We structure this data in a one-dimensional array of size 3.

Architecture

The architecture of the CNN is shown in in Fig.
3
. It is formed by two branches which take as inputs both arrays described in section
4.1
. The first input (projected position map {x/
R_{h}
, y/
R_{h}
}) is randomly rotated and/or flipped when fed into the model as a technique to improve rotational invariance and to avoid over-fitting. The arrays are passed twice through a combination of convolutional layer plus pooling layer, and then we apply a dropout layer to minimise potential over-fitting. After this, the results of both branches are flattened and concatenated, and we use a series of fully-connected dense layers until we reduce the output to N values where N is the number of mass profile points we intend to fit (in this case, 10). We again apply dropout between specific layers. Also, we concatenate the projected half-light radius
R_{h}
,the line-of-sight velocity dispersion of the galaxy
\sigma_{los}
and the 98th percentile of line-of-sight velocities
v_{los,p98}
to the outputs of the first dropout layer.

We utilise the outputs of the CNN to train a normalising flow model
(
Papamakarios et al. 2021
)
with the Python package
ltu-ili

(
Ho et al. 2024a
, Learning the Universe Implicit Likelihood Inference)
. Specifically, the 32 values from the penultimate layer of the CNN are employed as inputs to train a Masked Autoregressive Flow
(
Papamakarios et al. 2018
, MAF)
for neural posterior estimation. The model learns a series of transformations conditioned on the input data, applied to a base Gaussian distribution, to produce a posterior distribution for the target variables (dynamical enclosed masses in this study). The MAF model outputs a N-dimensional joint PDF representing the dynamical mass of a galaxy within N different radii, rather than single values for each radius. This approach offers advantages such as accounting for correlations between different dimensions of the output variables and enabling the estimation of uncertainties.

4.3
Output

The output of our CNN is a set of N values representing the galaxy’s dynamical mass enclosed within a given radius. We train the model to reproduce the galaxy masses by minimising a Mean Square Error (MSE) loss function:

MSE=\frac{1}{n_{data}}\sum_{i}^{n_{data}}\sum_{j}^{N}(y_{real,i,j}-y_{pred,i,j})^{2}

(2)

Where
y_{real,i,j}
is the
j
-th label of the
i
-th galaxy projection in our dataset of total size
n_{data}
and
y_{pred,i,j}
is the neural network prediction for the same value. In our case,
y_{real,i,j}
is the mass enclosed inside the
j
-th radius we study for the
i
-th galaxy projection of the dataset, numerically calculated from the galaxy simulation.

This process ensures the information encoded in the 32 neurons of the penultimate layer of the CNN model contains relevant information about the masses we aim to estimate, and therefore it is suitable to use as an input for the MAF model.

The output of the MAF is a N-dimensional joint PDF, where each dimension represents the galaxy’s dynamical mass enclosed within a given radius. The MAF also needs to undergo a training process so that it can learn the transformations that generate correct posterior distributions for the target data. In order to train a model that outputs a meaningful PDF
\mathbb{P}
, we use the negative log-likelihood (
L
) of the distribution as a loss function:

L=-\ln L_{*}=\sum_{i}-\ln\left[\mathbb{P}\left(y_{real,i}|\theta\right)\right]

(3)

Where
y_{real,i}
is the set of labels of the
i
-th galaxy projection in our dataset and
\mathbb{P}
is the PDF generated by the MAF using transformations defined by the trainable parameters
\theta
. Since the output distribution is N-dimensional, we evaluate it over a set of N labels
y_{real,i}
, which are the galaxy enclosed masses within N different radii.

5
Results

5.1
Mass estimators

We present the results of applying the literature mass estimators described in Section
3
to our dataset. We skip the estimator described in
Peñarrubia et al. 2008
as we find the assumption of isotropic velocities to fail greatly on these simulated galaxies.

Fig.
4
shows the probability distribution function of the ratio between the enclosed mass calculated by the literature mass estimators and the true enclosed mass for the galaxies in our training dataset. We also show the results of applying literature mass estimators to galaxies from each individual suite of simulations. We find all literature mass estimators to produce reasonably precise dynamical mass estimations, showing 1-
\sigma
dispersion values ranging from 0.26 for Wolf and Errani through 0.30. The accuracy of estimators also varies from one to another, and the estimators acting at the innermost radii present biases towards underpredicting the enclosed mass (
\sim
15% for Walker and
\sim
10% for Wolf). The trends observed are similar when analysing only AURIGA or NIHAO simulations. Nevertheless, FIRE simulations deviate slightly from the behaviour described above, exhibiting lower 1-
\sigma
dispersion and lower bias at inner radii mass estimations.

The varying performance of literature mass estimators on simulated galaxies depending on their properties or the specifics of the simulation is a topic worth exploring, but it is out of the scope of the present study. See
Campbell et al. 2017
for such study on the Walker’s and Wolf’s estimators using APOSTLE simulations. Notably, the errors associated with each mass estimator in this work are generally larger than those reported in previous studies. This discrepancy arises from the application of these estimators to a diverse set of hydrodynamical simulations, as opposed to a dataset consisting solely of N-body simulations or mock data
(
Errani et al. 2018
, e.g.)
, or to a very uniform set of hydrodynamical simulations
(
Campbell et al. 2017
;
González-Samaniego et al. 2017
)
.

We train our model separately on NIHAO and AURIGA projections of galaxies, and also on a combined dataset including both. We carefully separate our dataset in training and validation sets, making sure all projections of one singular galaxy lay only in one of the sets. We train our model with a batch size of 64, using the Adam optimiser with an initial learning rate of
10^{-3}
. We reduce the learning rate after each 10 epochs by a factor of 2, and we end the training when the validation loss has not improved for 15 epochs straight. The training typically terminates after
\sim
50 epochs. Next we extract the information from the penultimate layer of the CNN and we use it to train the MAF model created with the python package ltu-ili
(
Ho et al. 2024b
)
using 50 hidden features, 5 flow transformations, a batch size of 32 and a learning rate of
10^{-4}
.

Once the model is trained, we sample the joint PDF obtained through our CNN+MAF model 1000 times for each galaxy projection. We obtain estimates of the dynamical enclosed mass at 10 different radii by calculating the median of the resulting PDF along each dimension, and we assign them uncertainties using the 16th and 84th percentiles. In Fig.

Just like for the literature mass estimators, we explore the accuracy of the CNN+MAF model using the ratio between the predicted and the real mass, only we now obtain predictions at several radii on the fly using the CNN+MAF model. In the left panel of Fig.
6
we show the accuracy of the model trained on NIHAO galaxies only, and we show the same for the model trained and tested on AURIGA galaxies in the right panel. We compare the model results with the accuracy of literature mass estimators in each set, and we observe that
our CNN+MAF model outperforms all of them, both in terms of bias and scatter, while providing mass estimates at several radii which literature mass estimators are unable to explore
. We notice that the median ratio between predicted and real enclosed mass remains constant and close to unity for both the training and validation set both for NIHAO and AURIGA galaxies. However, there are differences between the results for both simulations: our model predictions show increased scatter for NIHAO galaxies at the innermost radii, which we do not find in AURIGA. This may be a consequence of the absence of cored dark matter profiles among AURIGA galaxies, which makes dynamical mass profile shapes more uniform in comparison to NIHAO galaxies, among which we find both cusps and cores
(
Di Cintio et al. 2014
;
Tollet et al. 2016
;
Di Cintio et al. 2017
)
. Also, only in the case of training on NIHAO we find more scatter on the validation set than on the training set, a hint of possible over-fitting. Constructing a mixed dataset to train on both simulations at the same time produces an intermediate result (Fig.
7
), both in terms of inner scatter and possible slight over-fitting, while the model still shows no bias and improves with respect to literature mass estimators at all radii.

Although all three models (trained on NIHAO only, AURIGA only, and on both at the same time) outperform literature mass estimators, not all of them generalize well across all simulated galaxies. Fig.
8
illustrates the results of applying these models to the validation sets of NIHAO and AURIGA, respectively. We find that a model trained exclusively on one simulation set struggles to make accurate predictions for the other. In contrast, the model trained on both simulation suites together is able to predict masses accurately for each individual set.

To assess whether training on both NIHAO and AURIGA galaxies at the same time improves the model’s ability to generalize to an entirely different set of simulations, we apply the three training models to FIRE galaxies and present the results in Fig.
9
. The models trained solely on NIHAO and AURIGA exhibit systematic differences when applied to FIRE galaxies, particularly at the innermost radii. However, training on a combination of NIHAO and AURIGA produces more balanced predictions (although they present more scatter than literature mass estimators), indicating that expanding the training set can improve generalization. Furthermore, we observe that small changes in the train/test split lead to variations in the median predictions, indicating that the model can converge to different states. While it consistently performs well on NIHAO and AURIGA galaxies, its performance on FIRE presents some variability.

As previously discussed, our model generates full posterior distributions for the enclosed masses. To evaluate the calibration of these distributions, we employ the Test of Accuracy with Random Points
(
Lemos et al. 2023
, TARP)
methodology, which assesses the statistical coverage of the posterior distributions. Statistical coverage refers to the proportion of times the true value of a parameter lies within a given credible interval of the posterior distribution. A well-calibrated posterior ensures that, for example, a 90% credible interval contains the true value approximately 90% of the time. In Fig.
10
we present the results of the TARP test for both the training and validation sets of the model trained on combined NIHAO and AURIGA galaxies. We find that the posteriors produced by our model are highly well-calibrated for galaxies in the training set, with deviations from the expected coverage being less than 3% across all confidence levels. Moreover, the calibration is nearly identical for all galaxies in the training set, regardless of their origin (NIHAO or AURIGA). For the validation set, the results are similarly robust, though deviations slightly exceed 3% for AURIGA galaxies at credibility levels around 60%. In contrast, deviations for NIHAO galaxies in the validation set, as well as for the combined NIHAO+AURIGA validation set, remain below 3%. Notably, the TARP results reveal more pronounced differences between galaxies from the two simulation suites in the validation set. Additionally, we apply the TARP test to the posteriors generated for FIRE galaxy projections. These posteriors exhibit increasing overconfidence as the credibility level rises, a trend consistent across multiple model trainings. In some cases, deviations from the expected coverage reach up to 30%. Further details and additional checks of the posterior calibration are provided in Appendix
C

6
Conclusions

In this work, we aim to evaluate the performance of existing literature galaxy mass estimators, as well as to construct a novel machine-learning model able to predict the enclosed mass of dispersion supported galaxies at any radius from line-of-sight stellar data. To do so, we use a large dataset from en extensive number of zoom-in cosmological hydrodynamical simulations from the projects NIHAO
(
Wang et al. 2015
)
, AURIGA
(
Grand et al. 2024
)
and FIRE
(
Wetzel et al. 2023
)
.

We find existing mass estimators to work reasonably well on our dataset, producing estimates for NIHAO and AURIGA galaxies with dispersion of 0.25-0.30 on the ratio between the predicted and actual mass calculated from the simulation. We notice the two innermost tested literature mass estimators (Walker and Wolf) systematically underestimate the enclosed mass for these galaxies by more than 10%. We also note that the errors associated to literature mass estimators, when applied on our large set of hydrodynamical simulations, are larger than what quoted from previous studies, likely due to the latter using a more self-similar and uniform data set
(
Campbell et al. 2017
;
Errani et al. 2018
;
González-Samaniego et al. 2017
)
.

We developed a machine-learning model consisting on a convolutional neural network and a masked autoregressive flow, which outperforms literature mass estimators when applied to simulations from the same suites it has been trained on. The model not only improves mass estimates at radii where mass estimators have been proposed in the literature (both in terms of scatter and bias), but it also provides estimates of the dynamical mass enclosed at any radii, where no mass estimators exist. Our model trained on a mixed dataset of NIHAO and AURIGA simulated galaxies presents less than 1% bias in recovering the true enclosed galaxy mass at any given radius, with 1-
\sigma
scatter below 20% for radii over the projected half-light radius. A key advantage of our model is its ability to generate full posterior distributions for the enclosed masses. These posteriors are well-calibrated across all credibility levels, meaning that the probability intervals (e.g., 68%, 90%) reliably contain the true mass values at the expected rates. Additionally, the posteriors are well-calibrated for all marginals, ensuring that the one-dimensional projections of the posterior distributions for individual parameters (e.g., mass at a specific radius) are also statistically reliable. This calibration highlights the robustness and versatility of our model for dynamical mass estimation.

We trained our model separately on galaxy projections from the NIHAO and AURIGA projects, and we find similarly good results for both of them. One key difference is the predictions of enclosed masses at inner radii show more scatter for NIHAO galaxies, which we attribute to the greater variety of inner dark matter density profile slopes in this simulation suite
(
Di Cintio et al. 2014
;
Tollet et al. 2016
)
, compared to galaxies from the AURIGA project. We find that training exclusively on galaxies from one simulation suite and applying the model to another can lead to discrepancies in the predictions. However, when we combine NIHAO and AURIGA simulations for training and test the model on FIRE galaxies, the model still captures the overall trends despite some remaining variability. Although the scatter is higher compared to literature mass estimators, this outcome highlights opportunities for further refining the model, like the application of domain adaptation techniques.

The lack of generalization power of the model might be due to different factors. Since we provide the model with the half-light radius and stellar velocity dispersion along the line-of-sight, and given that these properties can vary across different simulations (as shown in Fig.
2
), it is possible that the model may learn biased relationships between these features and the enclosed mass values. Another possibility is there is a bias in the maps used as inputs for the CNN. Biases could appear as a consequence of the varying spatial resolution of simulations, and also by a sub-optimal kernel width choice for transforming the distribution of projected stellar positions and velocities into a PDF we can sample on arrays.

At this stage, our CNN-based model can be applied to real galaxies, but this relies on the assumption that our simulation training set closely matches the properties of the observed galaxies.

In future works, in order to improve the model’s generalizability, we plan on creating a new model by following three different strategies. The first one consists in changing the neural network model used from a CNN to a Graph Neural Network
(
Scarselli et al. 2009
)
, which eliminates the need to use a gaussian kernel for creating the inputs. The next option revolves around changing the normalizing flow model to one which predicts a likelihood
(
Alsing et al. 2018
;
Alsing et al. 2019
, Neural Likelihood Estimation,)
rather than directly outputting the posterior distribution. This would allow us to discard predictions on a new set of data never seen by the model if we find the likelihood value to be too low. Finally, another option is to apply domain adaptation techniques like Domain Adversarial Neural Networks
(
Ganin et al. 2016
)
, which aim to force the model to use information which is invariant through different domains (simulation suites), improving its generalization ability.

Our CNN+MAF method is able to estimate the mass profiles of dispersion-supported galaxies with better accuracy and with fewer assumptions than traditionally used galaxy mass estimators, at very low computational cost, on top of providing estimates of their dynamical masses across a wide range of radii.

Appendix A
Bivariate Kernel Density Estimation

Kernel density estimation (KDE) is a non-parametric technique used to estimate the PDF of a random variable. Bivariate KDE extends this concept to two dimensions, estimating the joint PDF of two random variables.

KDE estimates the value of the underlying PDF at the point x according to

f_{h}(x)=\frac{1}{n\left|\mathbb{H}\right|^{1/2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\left[\mathbb{H}^{-1/2}\left(x-\mathbf{X_{i}}\right)\right]

(4)

Where
\mathbf{X_{1}},

\mathbf{X_{2}},
…,
\mathbf{X_{n}}
conform a sample of size n of the random variable(s) whose PDF we aim to estimate. In the case of bivariate KDE,
\mathbf{X_{i}}
represents two values
\mathbf{X_{i}}=\left(\mathbf{X_{i,1}},\mathbf{X_{i,2}}\right)
, which are joint samples of the two random variables for which we want to estimate the PDF.
K
is a kernel function and
\mathbb{H}
is a bandwidth matrix whose dimensions match those of
\mathbf{X_{i}}
(in this case, 2x2).

Effectively, the KDE evaluates the PDF at
x
by adding the contributions of each point
\mathbf{X_{i}}
, weighted by the kernel function
K
, based on the distance between
x
and
\mathbf{X_{i}}
, scaled by the bandwidth matrix
\mathbb{H}
.

We use a 2D Gaussian kernel that follows

K(\mathbf{u})=(2\pi)^{-3/2}\left|\mathbb{H}\right|^{1/2}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u}^{T}\mathbb{H}\mathbf{u}\right)

(5)

where
\mathbf{u}=x-\mathbf{X_{i}}
. The bandwidth matrix
\mathbb{H}
is calculated by multiplying the covariance matrix of the data by a factor
n^{-1/6}
, where n is the size of the sample.
