Investigating the role of temperature and moisture on the degradation of 3D-printed polymethyl methacrylate dental materials through molecular dynamics simulations
Authors: Ravinder S Saini, Sunil Kumar Vaddamanu, Doni Dermawan, Seyed Ali Mosaddad, Artak Heboyan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to comprehensively investigate the degradation behavior of 3D printed polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) dental materials, with a specific focus on the influential factors of temperature and moisture, by employing molecular dynamics simulations. Owing to their aesthetic properties, 3D-printed PMMA dental materials play a pivotal role in dental applications. However, understanding their degradation mechanisms, particularly in the context of temperature and moisture variations, is crucial for their long-term durability. A Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) was utilized for the molecular dynamics simulations. The simulation setup included temperature variations from 300 to 600 K and relative humidity (RH) levels ranging from 20 to 100%. Various mechanical properties and structural changes were analyzed to determine the degradation behavior. Energetic profiling during equilibration and the subsequent temperature variations were studied. The spatial distribution of the mean squared displacement, non-bond energy, Young’s modulus, bending stress, and volume expansion coefficient of the particles were quantitatively analyzed, revealing temperature- and moisture-dependent trends. The study concluded that temperature and moisture significantly affected the degradation behavior of 3D-printed PMMA dental materials. Higher temperatures and increased humidity levels contribute to reduced mechanical strength and altered structural properties, emphasizing the importance of controlling environmental conditions during fabrication.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-77736-5.Keywords: Material degradation, Moisture, Molecular dynamics simulation, PMMA, TemperatureSubject terms: Dentistry, Materials science

Introduction

Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) is an excellent material for dentistry and 3D printing, particularly because of its good aesthetic and fabrication properties. Dentistry is one of the fields in which 3D technology has been rapidly developing over the past decade, and in recent years, attention has shifted towards the long-term performance and durability of 3D-printed PMMA dental materials1. Environmental factors such as ambient temperature and moisture have a tremendous influence on the degradation reactions of these materials over time. An understanding of PMMA degradation mechanisms is important for the development of dental materials as well as for the durable usage of dental restorations2. The photocuring process essentially leads to polymerization and cross-linking of the material used. However, this process is integral to the fabrication of 3D-printed PMMA dental materials and incurs degradation flaws (photooxidation, hydrolysis, and mechanical degradation due to cyclic loading and stress concentration) with their incorporation3–5.

Hydrolytic degradation is another aging mechanism that has been well-studied in PMMA. Water molecules penetrate the polymer matrix and catalyze the breakage of chemical bonds between chains, thereby reducing the molecular weight, inducing dimensional changes, and decreasing the mechanical properties6–8. In particular, higher soaking temperatures during processing, sterilization, and presence in the oral cavity during chewing will promote thermal degradation, which is the breakage of the polymer chains, induces reduced molecular weight, decreases mechanical strength, and changes the optical properties9,10. Another chemical attack on PMMA dental restorative materials is exposure to acidic or alkaline oral fluids in the oral environment. Under acidic conditions, scission of polymer chains occurs in the polymer molecular chains due to the acidic breakage and unreactive breaking of chemical bonds between atoms in the same molecule, whereas under alkaline conditions, acidulate is deprotonated to enol or enolate and weakens the acid schedule. On the other hand, owing to the framework of PMMA, the absorbance of the PMMA structure reduces the mechanical strength, causes roughened surface morphologies, and alters the aesthetic properties11,12. Moreover, multiple mechanical wear and fatigue fractures resulting from the accumulation of cyclic loading and mechanical stresses applied during mastication and physiological oral movements occur during long-term clinical use, which is a part of the degradation mechanism13–15. Owing to the simultaneity of multiple degradation mechanisms in PMMA restorative materials, an overall understanding of the predictability of the life of dental restorations is necessary.

The impact of environmental effects and external conditions on polymer degradation has been investigated in more detail in materials science, particularly the effects of temperature and humidity. Research findings outside the dental context provide important insights into the general behavior of polymers16–18. Temperature has a major influence on the stability and mechanical properties of polymers. An increase in temperature promotes degradation processes, such as thermal oxidation and hydrolysis, which may result in a change in the physical and mechanical properties19–21. Moisture, another pivotal environmental factor, plays a substantial role in polymer degradation. It can induce plasticization and water absorption, resulting in dimensional changes, reduced mechanical strength, and increased susceptibility to degradation, such as hydrolytic and microbial attacks22–24. Research on polymer degradation in various contexts has illuminated the intricate relationship between polymer materials and their surrounding environment.

Despite the wealth of the existing literature, specific research gaps persist, necessitating further exploration. First, the unique conditions of the oral environment introduce challenges that require a more nuanced understanding. Previous studies have often focused on broad temperature ranges; however, the oral environment involves dynamic temperature variations. Investigating how these fluctuations affect the degradation kinetics of 3D-printed PMMA dental materials is crucial for a more accurate representation of real-world scenarios. Second, the influence of moisture, both in terms of quantity and quality, on the degradation of 3D-printed PMMA dental materials remains an area that requires further exploration25,26. The oral cavity is a complex, moist environment influenced by factors such as saliva composition, variations in fluid flow, and exposure to different types of liquids27. Investigating how these variables affect degradation is essential to obtain comprehensive insights. Moreover, while molecular dynamics simulations offer a tool for investigating material behavior at the molecular level, their application in the degradation of 3D-printed PMMA dental materials is an evolving area28. Optimizing simulation methodologies and validating results against experimental data will contribute to the robustness of predictions and the reliability of molecular dynamics simulations in this specific context29.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations are a powerful computational technique used to study the physical movements of atoms and molecules over time30,31. By solving Newton’s equations of motion, MD allows researchers to observe the behavior of materials at an atomic level, capturing essential details about molecular interactions, structural changes, and thermodynamic properties32–34. This approach is beneficial for investigating complex systems, such as polymers, where traditional experimental methods may face limitations in temporal or spatial resolution. 3D printing techniques, particularly those based on fused deposition modeling (FDM) and stereolithography (SLA), have facilitated the precise control of PMMA’s microstructure and properties35,36. The ability to tailor the geometric and mechanical characteristics of PMMA through 3D printing offers exciting possibilities for advancing dental materials and other applications. Despite its advantages, the environmental degradation of PMMA remains a concern, especially under varying temperature and humidity conditions. Thus, elucidating the relationship between environmental factors and the mechanical behavior of 3D-printed PMMA is essential for enhancing its reliability and longevity.

The goal of this study was to apply molecular dynamics to understand the degradation of 3D-printed polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) dental materials, particularly with respect to temperature and moisture. This research describes the dynamics of PMMA in terms of atomic interactions, with the objective of understanding how high temperatures and exposure to moisture cause degradation. The study covered a range of temperatures relevant to 3D-printed PMMA dental materials and included different temperature points and associated gradients to explore how temperature affected the degradation pathway. In addition, the presence of varying levels of moisture triggers hydrolytic degradation, and there is a lack of understanding of how moisture influences hydrolytic degradation as well as the subsequent stability of 3D printed PMMA that needs to be put into the highly dynamic moisture environment of the oral cavity. Therefore, this study examined how changes in mechanical properties (parameters including the spatial distribution of particles, mean squared displacement, non-bond energy, Young’s modulus, bending stress, and volume expansion) responded to changes in temperature or moisture. Overall, the spatial, kinetic, and mechanical properties of dental materials are key factors for understanding the overall stability and performance of dental materials. This study provides vital data on how temperature- and moisture-induced degradation causes changes in the mechanical behavior of 3D-printed PMMA.

Methodology

MD simulations provided insight into the atomic-level molecular scenarios underlying PMMA chain deterioration. The real-time behavior of PMMA dental materials at atomic-level molecular resolution has been unavailable for a long time. We employ a Large-scale Atomic/Molecular Massively Parallel Simulator (LAMMPS) and examine the structural changes, molecular interactions, and degradation processes underlying the reshaping of the material toward uncovering their material responses under different environmental stimuli37.

Description of the simulation setup

We designed a simulated system in which six stoichiometrically arranged linear isotactic PMMA chains, each with 100 monomers, were self-assembled, similar to the prototype crystal proposed by Shaffer et al.38 The simulation began with the construction of six polymer chains within a cubic simulation box measuring 80 Å on each side (80 Å × 80 Å × 80 Å) at a temperature of 600 K. These dimensions were meticulously selected to ensure that the polymer density remained below the equilibrium bulk density. Additionally, approximately 800 water molecules were introduced into the simulation box to achieve a humidity level of 20%. Correspondingly, at 100% humidity, approximately 4000 water molecules were included within the same box dimensions. The initial PMMA conformations, created via the Fortran 90 program, applied a random and self-avoiding walk algorithm consisting of the structural linkage between the monomers of PMMA chains39. Considering computational efficiency, we used a united atom model for the methylidyne, methylene, and methyl groups; all three hydrogen atoms in the PMMA chains were consolidated into carbon atoms bonded with the connecting PMMA chains. The atomic interactions of PMMA are sufficiently sensitive to the bond angle, and the force-field parameters used here are from the previously established united atom potentials to describe a van der Waals and Coulombic force field developed by Okada et al.40 The rationale behind opting for a united atom model stems from the need to strike a balance between computational efficiency and the complexity of the simulations necessary to study the PMMA system comprehensively. By consolidating multiple hydrogen atoms into carbon atoms bonded with the connecting PMMA chains, we significantly reduce the computational burden associated with handling individual hydrogen atoms. This allows us to perform simulations on a larger scale and over longer timeframes, facilitating the exploration of a wider range of temperature and humidity conditions. However, it’s important to acknowledge that this simplification comes with certain limitations. Specifically, the united atom model sacrifices some level of detail and accuracy, particularly in capturing the nuances of interactions involving hydrogen atoms. While this approach enables efficient exploration of mechanical properties and general behaviors of PMMA, it may overlook finer molecular-level interactions, potentially affecting the fidelity of our results, especially in scenarios where hydrogen atoms play a significant role. Nonetheless, for the purposes of our study focusing on mechanical responses and overall degradation trends of PMMA under varying environmental conditions, the united atom model provides a pragmatic compromise between computational feasibility and model complexity.

The role of temperature and moisture in the simulation setup

Temperature and moisture play pivotal roles in shaping the degradation behavior of 3D-printed PMMA dental materials. The simulation began with the construction of three polymer chains within a cubic simulation box, with each side measuring 60.0 Å. We used a range of chemical potentials from µ_1 to µ_2 (0.5 to 2.0 kJ/mol) to explore the adsorption isotherms comprehensively. The temperature was initially set to T = 600 K. The choice to set the initial temperature at T = 600 K in our molecular dynamics simulations is deliberate and aligns with the goals of our study. Simulating at a higher temperature, such as 600 K, provides a valuable opportunity to explore the behavior of polymers under elevated temperatures. These conditions may be encountered during critical stages of material processing, sterilization procedures, or exposure to external factors. A three-dimensional periodic boundary condition was applied to simulate an extended system, allowing for a more realistic representation of material interactions. The primary species considered for adsorption were water molecules, which are relevant to the study of moisture effects on PMMA. The conjugate gradient method was employed to minimize the total potential energy and ensure a stable starting point for the simulations. A 100 ps equilibration period was followed, which is crucial for eliminating potential overlaps within the polymer chains and allowing the system to reach a more energetically favorable state. Subsequently, thermal annealing was conducted in five NVT simulation stages, each lasting for 500 ps. This process involved cycling the temperature from 600 K to 300 K and back to 600 K, a protocol designed to remove undesirable interactions and attain the lowest energy state. The same method was applied to raise the temperature from 600 K to 900 K. The equilibrium of the system was then maintained through a 10 ns NPT simulation at 600 K and zero pressure, ensuring that the simulated systems did not become entrapped in metastable states. Furthermore, the Relative Humidity (RH) parameter introduced a key environmental parameter; that is, changes in temperature and RH cause differential thermal stresses that have a strong structural effect as well as an impact on degradation pathways. To overcome this issue, we used one of the most sophisticated simulation techniques available, Grand Canonical Ensemble (NVTµ) simulations, which allows changes in the number of water molecules and simulates changes in RH42. We anticipate that this study will examine an RH range representative of the oral environment, which can range from 20 to 100%. This range is relevant for dynamic moisture variations in the oral environment and for developing a more comprehensive understanding of the response of the material under appropriate conditions to different parts of its lifecycle and clinical scenarios.

Data analysis techniques and methods for quantifying degradation behavior

The degradation characteristics of 3D-printed PMMA dental materials were quantified by analyzing the autocorrelation function of the end-to-end vector to the PMMA chain during the NPT equilibration at 600 K. It was confirmed that the system relaxed to its minimum energy configuration after 10 ns. This cumulative 10 ns system cooling was subsequently divided into smaller time intervals (10 ns each) to carry out all simulation studies by gradually cooling the system from 600 K to 300 K at 10 K per 10 ns and facilitating a detailed characterization of the temperature-dependent degradation behavior of 3D printed PMMA dental materials. The moisture requirement in the force field setup was essential for simulating the dynamic movement of the oral environment and its impact on the structural integrity and mechanistic pathways for PMMA degradation. This is also why all molecular dynamics simulations were performed under realistic temperature and moisture conditions, as demonstrated in this study. It is theorized that the inclusion of realistic temperature and moisture environments in this study will more accurately represent the behavior of the material under realistic conditions.

Results and discussion

The molecular dynamics simulations provided insights into the individual effects of temperature and humidity on PMMA’s mechanical properties. However, it is important to note that the real-world application of PMMA involves simultaneous exposure to mechanical loads, such as biting forces, along with temperature and humidity variations. This study did not capture these interactions, which could significantly influence the material’s degradation and performance. For instance, the combined effect of tensile or compressive stresses, in conjunction with moisture absorption and thermal expansion, may lead to accelerated material fatigue and deterioration that is not fully understood in our current analysis. While our results reveal significant insights into the temperature-dependent structural changes of PMMA, it is essential to highlight that these findings do not encompass the long-term aging and deterioration of the material. In real-world dental applications, factors such as repeated mechanical stress, environmental interactions, and chemical degradation contribute to the material’s aging process. Therefore, while our study offers foundational knowledge regarding immediate responses to temperature variations, the implications for long-term performance remain to be thoroughly investigated.

Among the findings from molecular dynamics simulations, it is crucial to report some details regarding the energy features of the PMMA chain, specifically the potential and total energy parameters. The average potential energy, with a value of -595.87 kcal/mol, is an indicator of the intermolecular forces and interactions between the molecules of the polymer. This result is in line with those of other polymer simulation studies. In fact, most studies deal with the potential energy content, which is a significant parameter for better understanding the equation of motion in such materials44,45. Considering these statements, it is worth pointing out that a negative value of potential energy can be used as an index of the stability of the polymer examined, especially in this case, where PMMA was found in an energetically stable configuration. This indicates that our simulated polymer chain had a rigid structure, as predicted by the computational simulations that obtained negative values of the potential energy. The total energy is the sum of all values required for the system to exist. In this case, it was equal to 781.88 kcal/mol. This is of paramount importance, especially because it reports the total potential and kinetic energies. In fact, two components should be summed to guarantee this value, that is, the rest and relative motion energy of the PMMA molecules. This parameter is important because it serves as a global index of the system stability. For example, other studies on polymer materials have shown that this metric is crucial to better understand the stability and behavior of these compounds46. By having this specific value, it is possible to enrich the description of the overall energy of PMMA in the course of the simulated times. Finally, the average kinetic energy, equal to 1243.23 kcal/mol, is the value of the energy needed by the particles within the PMMA chain to move randomly. Essentially, a higher value of kinetic energy implies a greater amount of energy is necessary for the particles to rotate and vibrate. This quantity is particularly helpful in describing the dynamic features of a material. Other studies on polymer dynamics have reported the importance of kinetic energy for the interpretation of molecular movement and, in this way, material flexibility47,48. With this information, it is possible to obtain a better sense of the mobility and vibrational performance of the PMMA chain during the course of the molecular dynamics simulation.

A higher moisture content causes peak shifts, which reflects a change in the intermolecular distance. Because the aging effect makes the thermal energy very small, the PMMA molecules tend to obtain much more locally ordered conformations, which can be interpreted as a decrease in the RDF values. Peaks in the RDF at certain radial distance ranges, like 3–4 Å and 5–6 Å, shed more light on the peculiar structural features within the PMMA polymer. These peculiar features might be inter- and intra-molecular rather than bulk. They may provide information on certain interactions or conformations that become more significant or suppressed by changes in temperature. Such subtle features help us build a picture of the complex interplay between the temperature and the spatial arrangement of PMMA. Between 0.5 Å and 1.5 Å, the RDF values show sharp peaks at higher temperatures that flatten out with decreasing temperature, which is consistent with relatively short-range interactions. There is clearly an intense intermolecular force and continuous contact with the neighboring PMMA chains. The peaks appearing at higher temperatures can be attributed to the higher molecular mobility, as the polymer segments can be brought into closer proximity at higher temperatures. The increase in the intermolecular interaction and structural rigidity of the molecule with a decrease in temperature led to reduced mobility of the polymers. The increase in temperature causes thermal motion, which increases the chain flexibility and reduces the attraction between molecules owing to thermal excitation52. The structure beyond 2 Å, where the RDF peaks of PMMA appear, sheds light on the medium-to-long-range structural organization of this polymer. The peak at 4 Å in the RDF may signify structural motifs and spatial correlations of the polymer chains in this range. For instance, there was clear growth at 4 Å as the temperature decreased. Such temperature-dependent changes in the RDF peaks are important for polymer engineering, such as for engineering PMMA-based materials with 3D printing. Varying the temperature during the synthesis process could affect the polymer chain distributions in the product, ultimately impacting the mechanical and thermal properties of the final product. Modifying the temperature parameters (oxygen atom density within a certain radius) according to the RDF analysis could be used as a strategy in the synthesis of PMMA with optimized properties for use in certain applications.

By closely examining the radial distance range between 0.5 Å and 2 Å, it is clear that with humidity, the RDF peaks are shifted towards higher values. This is a direct indication of an increase in short-range intermolecular forces and, thus, a greater tendency for the PMMA chains to be in close contact with each other under high-humidity conditions. Therefore, the increased RDF values at 100% RH indicate greater molecular aggregation because the water molecules might help bring the PMMA chains closer to each other53. Looking more at distances greater than 2 Å, the RDF peaks were able to provide insights into the medium-to-long-range structural organization of PMMA. Most notably, at 60% RH and above, we observed an increase in the RDF value for all relaxation times, and many peaks hit each peak with higher values. This is a clear indication that something was occurring in PMMA at these humidity levels. This is likely related to water molecules interacting with the PMMA chains and how the water molecules might structure the polymer conformation and arrangement54. In our RDF analysis, we observed that the peaks in the RDF plots shifted significantly with increasing moisture content, indicating changes in the spatial arrangement of the polymer chains. Specifically, as moisture content increased, the first peak of the RDF for the PMMA-water interactions exhibited a notable shift towards shorter distances (Fig. 4b). This shift suggests that water molecules are more effectively penetrating the amorphous regions of PMMA, leading to enhanced hydrogen bonding and subsequent molecular aggregation. The increased proximity of water molecules to the PMMA chains correlates with the increased density of molecular interactions, as reflected in the height of the RDF peaks. This supports our conclusion that higher moisture content facilitates molecular aggregation within the PMMA matrix.

The temperature-dependent MSD, shown in Fig. 3a, indicates a gradual increase in MSD as temperature rises from 300 K to 600 K over the time frame from t0 = 0 ns to tn = 5 ns.​ This increase in MSD aligns with standard polymer physics principles, where higher temperatures promote molecular mobility. As the temperature goes from 300 K to 600 K, the MSD gradually increases. An experimental study indicated that the PMMA starts to melt at ~ 440 K43. Experimental evidence on polymeric materials has shown that thermal energy has a positive impact on molecular mobility55,56, and it is expected that MSD will increase as the temperature rises. In particular, it is as good as linearly increased from 0.5 at 300 K up to 18 saturation value at 600 K, thus indicating that molecular mobility is higher at higher temperatures, in accordance with standard polymer physics principles, where elevated temperatures should yield more flexible chains and thus higher diffusion coefficients57. The first step (300 K to 450 K) demonstrated a less dramatic increasing trend, with values as low as ten, indicative of a more constrained molecular motion. This behavior stems from the limited availability of thermal energy to polymeric chains at lower temperatures58. Above 460 K, the MSD values behaved more steeply, reaching the 18-saturation value, thus indicating higher molecular mobility at higher temperatures. This stems from a significant increase in molecular mobility, the consequence of substantial chain entanglement relaxation, and increased segmental motion. Moreover, in the low-humidity regime (20–40%), the MSD values also increased by moderate amounts, indicating a small impact on molecular mobility. As previously discussed, studies on polymer dynamics have shown that water molecules can act as plasticizers, with water molecules penetrating the amorphous regions of the polymers where plastic behavior occurs and the polymer chains can be free to move59. A similar timeframe (t0 = 0 ns to tn = 5 ns) was employed for the humidity variation. However, the MSD value increased drastically at humidity above 40% and nonlinearly with humidity, increasing from 2 for 20% humidity to 18 for 100% humidity. This nonlinearity indicates that the effect of relative humidity on the molecular dynamics of PMMA becomes more pronounced at high relative humidity than at low humidity because more water molecules permeate the polymer and increase the water uptake; hence, the polymer chain-to-chain interaction changes. The combined effects of temperature and humidity can be inferred from the impacts of these two parallel trends. Over most of the humidity range, an increase in temperature always increased the MSD of PMMA. The effects of the two components were additive but also slightly synergistic; any impact of temperature was enhanced at higher humidity levels, and any effect of humidity was enhanced at higher temperatures. The decision to present fixed MSD values was aimed at providing a concise overview of PMMA behavior under varying conditions. We aimed to simplify the analysis to highlight trends in material behavior, especially concerning diffusion properties. By presenting fixed MSD values, we intended to offer a snapshot of material dynamics to facilitate interpretation and comparison across different scenarios. However, we recognize that this approach may oversimplify the complex dynamics of PMMA, particularly at elevated temperatures where diffusion plays a significant role. Despite being a solid-state material, PMMA has significant amorphous regions where the polymer chains exhibit substantial mobility. MSD helps us quantify this mobility and understand how external conditions (temperature and humidity) influence it. This is particularly relevant for 3D-printed PMMA dental materials, where mechanical performance under varying environmental conditions is critical. While classical force fields do not allow for chemical bond rearrangement, they are effective in studying the mechanical and thermal behavior of polymers under moderate conditions. Our simulations aimed to capture these aspects rather than phase transitions, providing insights into how temperature and moisture affect the structural and mechanical properties of PMMA.

Non-bond energy

Examination of the non-bond energy underscores the impact of temperature on intermolecular interactions. An increased temperature accelerates van der Waals and electrostatic forces, leading to an elevated non-bond energy. However, the correlation between humidity and non-bonded energy is not a well-established or commonly studied relationship in traditional scientific contexts. Moisture, which acts as a plasticizing agent, contributes only to hydrogen bonding, thereby influencing the overall energy landscape60. In addition to its role in facilitating hydrogen bonding, moisture’s impact on van der Waals interactions is crucial for comprehensively understanding PMMA’s behavior in humid environments. The plasticizing effect of moisture can decrease the glass transition temperature of the polymer, leading to a more flexible material that responds differently to mechanical stresses. This interplay between hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces under varying humidity levels warrants a more thorough analysis, as it directly influences the mechanical properties and durability of PMMA in practical applications. The resulting decrease in the non-bonded energy suggests the weakening of the intermolecular forces, allowing for greater molecular mobility and reduced cohesive energy within the PMMA structure62. It is noteworthy that the non-bonded energy values exhibit a non-linear upward trend, indicating a complex relationship between temperature and the molecular dynamics of PMMA. This nonlinearity may be attributed to the intricate interplay of various factors, such as the activation of specific molecular conformations or the occurrence of phase transitions within the polymer matrix at certain temperature ranges. The non-bonded energy of PMMA is significantly influenced by temperature fluctuations and the presence of phase transitions. As temperature increases from 300 K to 600 K, PMMA transitions from a glassy state to a rubbery state, leading to enhanced molecular mobility and alterations in chain conformations. These changes facilitate variations in intermolecular interactions, including van der Waals and electrostatic interactions, contributing to the observed non-linear variations in non-bond energy. The transition through these phases results in an evolving energetic landscape that directly impacts the material’s mechanical properties and thermal response.

Limitations

Experimental comparison constraints

While molecular dynamics (MD) simulations provide valuable insights into atomic-scale behavior, direct replication of experimental conditions poses challenges. Our reliance on MD to study temperature and humidity effects on PMMA’s mechanical properties revealed a discrepancy, as experimental data typically indicate expansion at 300 K, whereas our simulations predicted contraction. This divergence can be attributed to model simplifications, incomplete equilibration during the cooling process, limitations of the force field, and potentially unaccounted effects of humidity that influence PMMA’s volume.

Cooling process and equilibration timeframes

The cooling process may have introduced limitations in the study. The observed fluctuations in energy during cooling suggest that the system may not have fully equilibrated within the simulation timeframe. This incomplete equilibration could affect the accuracy of the obtained results, particularly in terms of structural and mechanical properties. Additionally, the 200-ns simulation duration may not have been sufficient to achieve complete equilibration, especially at higher temperatures where the system’s dynamics are more pronounced. Therefore, while the cooling process served to stabilize the system, longer equilibration periods and potentially alternative equilibration strategies may be necessary to ensure the reliability of the simulation outcomes. We recognize that the predicted contraction at 300 K contrasts with experimental data indicating expansion. This discrepancy underscores the limitations of molecular dynamics simulations, including modeling assumptions, environmental condition variances, and potential inadequacies in the equilibration process. Future work should focus on refining the simulation parameters, incorporating longer equilibration times, and possibly utilizing advanced force fields to better capture the thermodynamic properties of PMMA. The study emphasizes the effects of temperature and humidity on PMMA, but it is essential to recognize the limitations posed by the classical force fields employed in our simulations. The inability to model chemical bond rearrangement restricts our understanding of PMMA’s behavior in high-stress settings or extreme thermal conditions, where bond degradation may occur. This intrinsic limitation necessitates caution when extrapolating our findings to scenarios involving potential material failure or phase transitions. To address these concerns, future investigations should leverage advanced simulation techniques that incorporate quantum mechanics or reactive force fields, allowing for a more nuanced exploration of PMMA’s degradation pathways under critical conditions.

Broad RDF analysis in PMMA-water system interactions

The broad RDF analysis lacked specificity and did not fully capture the various non-covalent interactions at play in the PMMA-water system, including van der Waals forces, electrostatic interactions, and hydrogen bonding. While hydrogen bonding plays a significant role in the cohesive forces between water and PMMA, humidity also alters the strength and orientation of van der Waals interactions and electrostatic forces between polymer chains and surrounding water molecules. These interactions are crucial for a comprehensive understanding of PMMA’s response to humidity. Future studies should incorporate quantitative measures of van der Waals and electrostatic interactions to provide a more detailed picture of humidity’s effect on the polymer’s structural and mechanical behavior. Specifically, analyzing interactions between atom pairs like oxygen-oxygen (water and PMMA carbonyl oxygen) could reveal the full scope of these non-covalent interactions beyond hydrogen bonding.

Fixed MSD analysis in capturing dynamic material behavior

The presentation of fixed MSD values overlooks the dynamic nature of PMMA, particularly at higher temperatures and in the presence of water molecules. MSD should ideally be depicted as a function of time to capture the evolving nature of material diffusion accurately. By not incorporating this temporal aspect, the analysis fails to provide a comprehensive understanding of how PMMA molecules move and rearrange over time. This limitation hampers the depth of insight into diffusion phenomena and may lead to incomplete interpretations of material behavior. Thus, future studies should aim to incorporate dynamic MSD analyses to better capture the temporal evolution of PMMA properties.

Linear regime assumption

Equation (5) in our study represents the relationship between stress and strain in the linear region of the stress-strain curve, where Hooke’s law applies. However, it’s crucial to acknowledge that the behavior of polymers, including PMMA, extends beyond the linear regime, encompassing non-linear deformations, yielding, and possible failure mechanisms. Our analysis primarily focused on the linear response of PMMA to deformation, and as such, our calculations of Young’s modulus may not fully capture the material’s behavior under all conditions.

Macroscopic deformation representation

In MD simulations, the deformation of materials is represented at the atomic level, providing detailed insights into atomic interactions and structural changes. However, translating this atomic-scale deformation into macroscopic stress-strain curves akin to experimental measurements presents challenges. While stress and strain tensors can be extracted from MD simulations, creating comprehensive stress-strain curves that span the full range of material behavior, including non-linear regimes, is not straightforward.

Strain rate considerations

Our study did not explicitly address the effect of strain rate on the mechanical properties of PMMA. In experimental settings, strain rate plays a crucial role in determining material response, particularly in dynamic loading scenarios. However, due to the nature of MD simulations, which typically operate at constant deformation rates, our analysis may not fully capture the dynamic behavior of PMMA under varying strain rates. Due to computational constraints and the limitations of our MD model, including periodic boundary conditions in all directions, conducting traditional bending tests as performed in experimental settings was not feasible. Therefore, our analysis of bending stress was based on the atomic-scale behavior of PMMA under varying environmental conditions, providing insights into its mechanical response at the molecular level. However, we recognize the importance of considering specimen dimensions and experimental testing methodologies for future studies to ensure a more comprehensive analysis of bending stress.

Modeling assumptions and force field accuracy

MD simulations rely on force fields to describe interatomic interactions within materials. While these force fields are continually improving, they are subject to inherent limitations and inaccuracies, particularly in capturing complex phenomena such as polymer behavior under extreme temperature and humidity conditions. As such, the accuracy of our results may be influenced by the choice of force field and the assumptions inherent in the MD model. Our study presupposed a homogeneous stoichiometric configuration of PMMA polymer chains; however, actual 3D-printed materials are likely to exhibit microstructural flaws, voids, and irregularities. These microstructural changes can significantly impact degradation pathways through mechanisms such as localized stress concentration, altered moisture absorption, and modified chemical interactions with environmental agents.

Simplified model and interpretational challenges

One limitation of our approach is the oversimplification inherent in our model of the volume expansion coefficient. While we attempted to capture the combined effects of temperature and humidity on PMMA’s volume, the fluctuations observed in the coefficient values raise questions about the accuracy and completeness of our simulation. While our study utilized the united atom model to enhance computational efficiency, it is important to consider the implications of this simplification on the accuracy of our results. The reduction in explicit representation of hydrogen atoms may impact the accuracy of hydrogen bonding interactions, which are critical for understanding PMMA’s mechanical behavior and degradation mechanisms. Additionally, the interpretation of negative volume expansion coefficients in certain situations may not align with physical intuition, suggesting potential inaccuracies or artifacts in our computational framework. Further refinement and validation of our model are necessary to address these limitations and enhance the reliability of our results. Addressing these limitations is essential for ensuring the robustness and applicability of our findings, and future studies could explore alternative simulation techniques or experimental validation to complement and enhance our understanding of PMMA’s mechanical behavior under diverse environmental conditions.

Biological agents and environmental complexity

While our study focused on the effects of temperature and humidity on PMMA, it is essential to recognize that various biological agents, including enzymes, bacteria, and salivary components, also influence the oral environment. These agents can contribute to the deterioration of PMMA through mechanisms such as hydrolysis and oxidation. The complexity of these interactions poses challenges for accurate representation in molecular dynamics simulations, as it requires detailed knowledge of the biochemical properties and dynamics of these agents. Future studies should consider integrating biological factors into the simulation models to provide a more comprehensive understanding of PMMA’s behavior in oral environments. By examining the synergistic effects of environmental conditions and biological agents, researchers can enhance the predictive accuracy of PMMA’s performance in clinical applications.

Cyclic environmental conditions

Our study primarily focused on unidirectional changes in temperature and humidity. However, PMMA materials in the oral cavity are subjected to recurrent cyclic variations that can significantly impact their performance. These daily fluctuations may lead to time-dependent breakdown processes that are not captured in our current simulations. Cyclic exposure to varying temperatures and humidity levels can result in cumulative mechanical fatigue, increased moisture absorption, and altered chemical interactions with biological agents, all of which can exacerbate material degradation. Future research should incorporate models that simulate these cyclic conditions to better understand the long-term effects of PMMA in real-world dental applications.

Impact of time-dependent mechanical stress

Our study primarily focused on the effects of temperature and humidity on PMMA degradation over a relatively short simulation period. However, in real-world dental applications, PMMA is subjected to continuous mechanical stress from activities such as biting and chewing. This time-dependent mechanical loading can significantly impact the material’s fatigue properties and long-term degradation behavior. Current simulations do not account for the complexities of cyclic loading and the resultant mechanical fatigue, which may lead to microstructural changes and eventual failure of the material. Future research should prioritize the investigation of time-dependent stress effects on PMMA, employing fatigue testing methods and long-duration simulations to provide a more accurate representation of PMMA’s performance in clinical settings.

Influence of additives and fillers

Our study primarily focuses on pure PMMA without accounting for the presence of additives or fillers commonly used in real-world applications, especially in dentistry. These components can markedly affect the thermal and mechanical characteristics of PMMA, potentially altering its behavior under various environmental conditions. The absence of these considerations in our simulations represents a significant limitation, as the effects of additives could influence the material’s degradation pathways and overall performance. Future studies should aim to incorporate multi-component systems to assess how these additives and fillers modify the behavior of PMMA under thermal and mechanical stress.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our molecular dynamics simulations have provided significant insights into the degradation of 3D-printed PMMA dental materials under varying temperature and humidity conditions. Our analysis, particularly through RDF studies, indicates that the structural organization of PMMA is significantly influenced by temperature, highlighting a temperature-dependent relationship in the spatial arrangement of the polymer chains. Additionally, the observed humidity-induced shifts in the RDF peaks underscore the crucial role of short-range intermolecular forces such as van der Waals forces, electrostatic interactions, and hydrogen bonding in shaping the structural conformation of PMMA in the presence of water molecules. These intermolecular forces collectively alter the mechanical and structural properties of PMMA under humid conditions, with humidity enhancing van der Waals interactions and modulating electrostatic forces between polymer chains and water molecules. The MSD values revealed temperature-dependent trends, illustrating the relationship between temperature, molecular mobility, and non-bonded energy of PMMA. Notably, the non-linear increase in MSD with humidity, particularly at elevated relative humidity, demonstrates that the combined synergistic effects of temperature and humidity enhance the molecular mobility of PMMA. Furthermore, both the non-bonded energy and Young’s modulus of PMMA were shown to be responsive to environmental factors, reflecting the elastic interplay between temperature and humidity and their impact on the mechanical behavior of the polymer. Our findings indicate that the bending stress of PMMA is highly sensitive to these environmental conditions, with a notable decline in performance correlated with increased temperature and humidity. This sensitivity to hydraulic expansion highlights the importance of considering moisture exposure in material design, particularly for applications demanding high durability, such as dental prosthetics. To mitigate the effects of hydraulic expansion, we recommend exploring strategies such as incorporating hydrophobic additives and optimizing cross-linking. For future research, it will be essential to integrate experimental validation to confirm our simulation findings and further investigate the implications of hydraulic expansion on the mechanical behavior of PMMA under various environmental conditions. Our study lays the groundwork for developing improved PMMA formulations or alternatives better suited to specific applications. Overall, this research contributes valuable knowledge for effectively utilizing PMMA in diverse fields, particularly in dentistry and structural engineering.
