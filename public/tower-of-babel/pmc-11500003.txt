Architecture and geomorphology of fluvial channel systems in the Arabian Basin
Authors: Dicky Harishidayat, Saleh Al-Dossary, Abdullatif Al-Shuhail
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe architecture and geomorphology of fluvial channel system plays an important role in the interpretation of its sedimentary processes and characterization of the ability for subsurface storage. In the Arabian Basin, this system is considered a new petroleum play after a century of carbonate-play dominance with enormous petroleum reserves. In addition, the presence of publicly available 3D seismic refection data in the Arabian Basin is very limited, providing a condition that fluvial channel system knowledge becomes publicly less understood. Therefore, our study utilizes 3D seismic reflection data and aims to (1) interpret seismic facies and architecture, (2) map the unit thickness and lateral distribution, and (3) calculate morphometry of the Late Carboniferous to Early Permian fluvial channel systems in the Arabian Basin, and discuss its sedimentary processes, development and petroleum prospectivity. Seismic facies and seismic attribute analysis revealed three distinct fluvial channel systems including their crevasse-splay and flood plain. The channels are characterized by isolated bright on the RMS amplitudes, spectral decomposition, variance and sweetness with sinuous geomorphology and lobes (crevasse-splay) and variation of amplitude values in hummocky to sub-parallel and parallel (flood plain) reflection configuration. The morphometric analysis shows three clusters on the scaling relationship between maximum channel width and meander belt width, as well as the minor variation on the channel sinuosity index. These results indicated the dominance of the bed-load sediment transport mechanism in the fluvial depositional environment with a single-story channel body architecture. These factors provide the ability for fluvial channel systems in the studied area to act as petroleum reservoir as well as potential source rock, cap rock and stratigraphic traps. Furthermore, a single-story channel body indicated poorly connected among them, both in lateral and vertical extends that required horizontal wellbore for drainage the reservoir. Our study revealed that fluvial channel systems in the Arabian Basin has potentiality both in petroleum prospectivity as well as subsurface storage (carbon dioxide, waste, hydrogen). In addition, the workflow presented here could be applicable to characterize the fluvial channel system from 3D seismic reflection data in other basins, worldwide.Subject terms: Geology, Geomorphology, Geophysics, Sedimentology, Geomorphology, Carbon capture and storage, Energy storage, Fossil fuels

Introduction

Fluvial channels deposits are well-known for their ability to store hydrocarbons in the deep subsurface, as well as to store carbon dioxide in the recent energy transition era. The 3D subsurface characterization itself mainly comes from 3D seismic reflection data (including acquisition, processing and interpretation techniques) that have been around since the 1980s1–4. The 3D seismic interpretation techniques (identification and extraction) of planform morphology from the fluvial channel is called seismic geomorphology which integrates traditional horizon and faults interpretation with slicing techniques (time-horizon-stratal) and seismic attributes, as well as cross-sectional interpretation5–9. The application of seismic geomorphology (with seismic stratigraphy) in subsurface records plays an important role to reconstruct the sedimentary dynamics of depositional system and their relationships with source-to-sink system, thus provide valuable information for characterizing petroleum prospectivity and subsurface storage5,9–12.

One typical fluvial channel infill is sandstone filled as product of bed-load sediment transport mechanism, which is usually characterized by isolated geomorphology of seismic amplitude that has rock property contrast (density, velocity, etc.) with other fluvial geomorphology counterparts (e.g., flood plain shale), and in planform morphology has sinuosity (high and low) with cross-cutting lateral development13–19. This characteristic of isolated morphology (contrast in rock properties with surrounding, discontinuous sand-bodies surrounded by shale) provide favorable place for hydrocarbon reservoirs, groundwater reservoirs and subsurface storage20–22.

Seismic geomorphology studies (utilizing 3D seismic reflection data) of fluvial channel system are still under-represented compared to those of other fluvial depositional systems, some of which exist in Asia e.g.,13,23–28, Africa e.g.,29–31, Europe e.g.,14,15,32–34, Australia e.g.,35–37, and North–South America e.g.,17,18,38–41. However, seismic geomorphology studies of the fluvial channel system in the Arabian Basin are still publicly underexplored because of the restriction on the 3D seismic reflection data mainly acquired by the petroleum industry in this region. In addition, the focus on carbonate petroleum systems at the Arabian Basin, that hold enormous estimated proven reserves of oil and gas, is also the reason why that fluvial channel system is still publicly underexplored. The specific research question from the fluvial channel system in the Arabian Basin; What is the characteristic of seismic facies and architecture? How is the vertical and lateral distribution? What is geometry? How is the development and evolution? How is the ability in terms of petroleum prospectivity and subsurface storage?.

Therefore, our study is utilize onshore 3D seismic reflection data from the Central Arabian Basin in the Late Carboniferous to Early Permian Period (Fig. 1a,b) of the Paleozoic4,42 and aims to (1) interpret seismic facies and architecture, (2) map the unit thickness and lateral distribution, and (3) calculate morphometry of its fluvial channel systems. Our results are discussed in relation to sedimentary processes, sequence stratigraphy and petroleum prospectivity in this fluvial channel systems with the proposed depositional model. Consequently, our study would be of public interest and database for seismic geomorphology aspects (fluvial depositional system, in particular) in the Arabian Basin, as well as other similar systems globally. In addition, our study could contribute to the detailed spatial and temporal resolution of the Arabian fluvial depositional system for hydrocarbon exploration-development, and subsurface storage.

Geological setting

The Arabian Basin is an asymmetric Upper Precambrian-Phanerozoic sedimentary basin that has a maximum depth of ~ 8 km (Fig. 1a,b)43. It is bounded by the Precambrian Arabian Shield in the west (cropping out), foredeep setting of the Zagros collision zone in the east, Arabian Sea in the south, and the intraplate of Palmyra-Sinjar troughs with Bitlis suture zone in the north (Fig. 1a)43. Several tectonic regimes occurred in the Arabian Basin, including a series of subsidence and uplift periods, plate motion, deformation, and a stable platform that influenced the structural, stratigraphic configuration, and sediment dispersal of thick accumulated Upper Precambrian-Phanerozoic sedimentary strata43–47.

During the Late Carboniferous to Early Permian, the study area in the Arabian Basin was located at the Gondwana continental margin between 30 and 15° south with a transition climate from temperate to subtropical—tropical climate in relatively semi-arid conditions45,48,49. It moved from the south to the north (continental drift)43,49,50. On this period, the study area experienced uplift and erosion during the Hercynian orogeny. Continental siliciclastic fluvial depositional systems (non-marine and terrestrial) reshaped the geomorphology of the paleo-landscape through a fluvial channel sediment routing system43,45,47–51.

The sediment source for this siliciclastic continental system was mainly from the exposed Precambrian Arabian Shield (plutonic, volcanic, and metamorphic) in the west and some paleo-highs in the Central Arabian Basin, with mainly alluvial, eolian dunes and braided-meandering fluvial channels43,46,50,51. These events of uplift, erosion, and deformation were caused by collision along the northern margin of Gondwana continent together with its clockwise rotation43,46,49.

The Paleozoic tectonic events ended in the Late Permian, when continental breakup started to move together with the onset of passive margin development along the northeast plate boundary (the newly opened Neo-Tethys ocean), which marked the deposition of major carbonate systems in the region43,48,50.

Data and methods

Data

The dataset used in this study is onshore 3D seismic reflection data, with a Common Depth Point (CDP) bin size of 25 × 50 m and CDP fold of 480. The inline and crossline receiver intervals are 50 and 200 m, respectively. Far offsets are 3000 m, vibroseis point intervals of 100 × 100 m, and a time sampling rate of 2 ms. The data covers an area of approximately 300 km2. The main seismic processing steps include 3D geometry, noise removal, velocity/residual statics, surface-consistent amplitude, deconvolution, azimuthal sorting–stacking, and post-stack migration. The seismic reflection data is shown in the normal polarity of the Society of Exploration Geophysicists (SEG) convention with zero phase amplitude. This means that the increase in acoustic impedance (from low velocity and density layers to higher layers) is represented by a positive amplitude at the apex of the peak wavelet (Fig. 2a). The seismic data has moderate quality with relatively less noise in the studied interval (Fig. 2a).

Methods

Traditional seismic interpretation with recent advances in 3D seismic interpretation techniques has been implemented in this study utilizing industrial standard software and workstation. Seismic facies analysis (including seismic character) together with faults and horizons (including time structure maps) are the basis for further advanced analysis in this study (Fig. 2a,b,c,d). Seismic facies analysis includes reflection termination, reflection configuration, reflection continuity, amplitude characteristic, and bedding frequency, which lead to the geological interpretation of seismic reflection data9,52–58.

Furthermore, Barnes59, Chopra and Marfurt60, Hart61 has classified some optimum seismic attributes to delineate the channel system, these are; (1) Root Mean Square (RMS) amplitude, which is a function of the square root of the sum of squared amplitudes within the interval window, (2) spectral decomposition and color blending, which provides a direct measure of relative amplitude within a given frequency band of 20 Hz (blue), 30 Hz (red) and 40 Hz (green) dominant frequencies in this studied interval (Fig. 3a), (3) variance, which measures the fit between seismic traces to produce the volume of discontinuity, and (4) sweetness, which is derived by dividing the reflection strength by the square root of the instantaneous frequency42,60–67. Most of those seismic attributes are detecting the isolated sand bodies of the channels system that usually tend to generate stronger amplitude than its surrounding shale.

(a) Frequency spectrum of the seismic reflection data at the studied interval, and morphometric parameters measured in this study using (b) RMS amplitud example and (c) its schematic drawing.

The seismic slicing technique of volume attributes is performed in all units in the studied interval (Fig. 2). These are time slices that represent a map view at a fixed time—Two Way Travel Time (TWTT) of seismic reflection data. In addition, horizon slices along interpreted horizons (the same picked reflectors) and stratal slices representing proportional slices between two reference horizons from the volume attributes are used (Fig. 2e).

Once channel geometry has been identified from the previous methods, seismic geomorphology analysis is performed following the fluvial channel morphometric criteria regarding the channel width and meander belt width (Lateral; Fig. 3b–d) of Harishidayat et al.15, Wood18, Ethridge and Schumm19, Gibling68, Pettijohn et al.69, Potter70. In addition, the sinuosity (the ratio between channel length and meander wavelength in plan-view of one channel wavelength; Fig. 3c) follows the criteria defined by Wood18, Gibling68, Schumm71, Stølum72, Charlton73. Furthermore, the scaling relationship (R2 and R; coefficient determination and correlation, respectively) of these two fluvial channel morphometrics is used to infer basin and dimensional bodies types69,70, the interpretation of sedimentary processes and the development of the fluvial channel system15,74–76. In addition, stratal architecture and facies are used to infer fluvial basin accommodation, sedimentation, and avulsion frequency21,77–81.

Results

Seismic stratigraphy, morphology, and facies

Three horizons (H1–H3) have divided the studied interval into four seismic units from the lowermost older (U1) to the uppermost younger (U4) with reflection termination patterns originating from local erosional truncation of the isolated bright amplitudes (Fig. 2a).

The first unit consists of sub-parallel to hummocky reflections configuration (Fig. 2a,b), low continuity to discontinuity, dominance with isolated bright amplitude (Fig. 2a,c) and low bedding frequency (Fig. 2). The second unit is bounded by continuous and high amplitudes of horizons 1 and 2 (H1 and H2) with relatively sub-parallel to hummocky reflection configuration (Fig. 2a,b), isolated bright amplitude (Fig. 2a,c), and low continuity to discontinuity and low bedding frequency (Fig. 2). The third unit consists of sub-parallel to hummocky reflection configuration (Fig. 2a,b), relatively moderate continuity of the reflector, and isolated bright amplitude (Fig. 2a,c) with moderate bedding frequency (Fig. 2). The last unit is characterized by hummocky reflection configuration (Fig. 2a,b), low continuity to discontinuity, isolated bright amplitude (Fig. 2a,c), and relatively low bedding frequency (Fig. 2).

Most of the faults in the studied area are normal faults, with relatively small displacements (Fig. 2d). Some faults are found in all the units, whereas other faults are mainly located in Units 1 or 2 (Fig. 2d). The faults are oriented relatively in the north–south direction (Fig. 4a–c).

Time structure maps of (a) Horizon 1, (b) Horizon 2, (c) Horizon 3. Thickness maps of (d) seismic unit 2 and (e) seismic unit 3.

Unit 1 is bounded by Horizon 1 at the top and dominated by an anticline with north–south structure orientation, where the low area of this Horizon 1 is in the north and the high area is in the south (Fig. 4a). Horizon 2 bounds unit 2 at the top and has similar characteristics to horizon 1 but is located shallower in the stratigraphic interval (Figs. 2 and 4b). Horizon 3 bounds unit 3 at the top and is characterized by a relatively gently dipping structure in the northwest to southeast direction of the apex main orientation (Fig. 4c). Furthermore, unit 2 has stratigraphic thickness ranging from 60 to 100 ms TWTT with the thick interval located in the west and northwest, whereas the thinner interval is in the south and eastern side of the studied area (Fig. 4d). In addition, unit 3 has a thicker interval on the northwestern side of the studied area with a relatively thin interval in the east (Fig. 4e).

The seismic facies of depositional element in this study revealed 3 distinct facies (Table 1). Seismic Facies 1 (SF1) is characterized by isolated bright amplitude (medium to high amplitudes) with concave-up shape (in section view), isolated high RMS amplitude (in plan-view), and elongate features (in plan-view) that are interpreted as channels (Table 1a). Seismic Facies 2 (SF2) is characterized by parallel to sub-parallel (low to high amplitude) with a continuous reflector (in section view), medium RMS amplitude (in plan-view), and having relatively homogeneous features (in plan-view) that is interpreted as a flood plain (Table 1b). Furthermore, Seismic Facies 3 (SF3) is characterized by wide isolated bright amplitude (with lateral amplitude variation) with sub-parallel (in section view), isolated high RMS amplitude (in plan-view) and mounded—lobe shapes (in plan-view) that are interpreted as crevasse-splay (Table 1c).

These features are interpreted as flood plain—seismic facies 2 (Table 1b).

Another amplitude character on this fluvial channel system 1 is related to the lobe shape of isolated medium to high RMS amplitude (plan-view) and wider shape compared to seismic facies 1 (Table 1c and Fig. 5f). This feature is associated with seismic facies 1 and has thicknesses ranging from 5 to 30 ms TWTT that usually thicken to the middle body and thinner to both sides of the edges (Section view; Table 1c and Fig. 5g–l). The distribution of this feature is scattered all over the studied area with the direction that parallel to the channel system (seismic facies 1). These features are interpreted as crevasse-splay—seismic facies 3 (Table 1c).

Morphometric analysis of the fluvial channel geomorphology

Three fluvial channels morphometric parameters analyses were performed in this study: maximum channel width, meander belt width, and sinuosity (Fig. 3b,c). These three fluvial channel morphometric parameters indicated relatively small variations in the channels of fluvial channel systems 1, 2 and 3 in the studied intervals.

Fluvial channel system 1 has a maximum channel width in the range of 202–308 m with a meander belt width ranging from 1590 to 1924 m (Fig. 8a,b), whereas the crevasse-splay itself has a maximum width of 350 m (Fig. 5f). Fluvial channel system 2 in the studied area has a maximum channel width in the range of 245–290 m with a meander belt width ranging from 771 to 1050 m (Fig. 8a,b), whereas the crevasse-splay itself has a maximum width of 430 m (Fig. 6e). Fluvial channel system 3 has a maximum channel width in the range of 311–340 m with a meander belt width ranging from 589 to 1377 m (Fig. 8a,b), whereas the crevasse-splay itself has a maximum width of 490 m (Fig. 7f). These indicated a clear difference between fluvial channel systems in the studied area.

Stratigraphic evolution of the fluvial channel system 1 (older; red), 2 (younger; green) and 3 (youngest; blue) based on (a) maximum channel width, (b) meander belt width, and (c) sinuosity with its (d) paleocurrent direction.

Furthermore, the sinuosity of fluvial channel system in the studied area is in the range of 0.95–1.14 (Fig. 8c). A very minor sinuosity of 1.30 is found in fluvial channel system 2 (green point in Fig. 8c). Using fluvial channel classification of sinuosity by Wood18, Schumm71, Stølum72, Charlton73, the 0.95–1,14 sinuosity is low sinuosity (Straight channel), whereas the 1.30 is medium sinuosity (Less meander channel). Schumm82 defined a strong relationship between the sinuosity and dominant grain size transported by the flow in the fluvial channel system; therefore, channels in the studied area are developed through the bed-load sediment transport mechanism (sinuosity in the range of 0.95–1.25) with a very minor mixed-load sediment transport mechanism (sinuosity of 1.25–2.0) in the relatively north to south flow direction (Fig. 8d). In addition, based on the Charlton73 classification, most of fluvial channels in the studied area are classified as low sinuosity channels (sinuosity in the range of 0.95–1.2) with very minor meander channels (sinuosity of 1.30) (Fig. 8c).

Despite the relatively small variation of these three fluvial channel systems in the studied area, the scaling relationship between the two morphometric parameters (maximum channel width and meander belt width) has revealed the clustering of these 3 fluvial channel systems into three distinct categories: fluvial channel system 1 (green color), fluvial channel system 2 (orange color), and fluvial channel system 3 (blue color) (Fig. 9). In addition, the coefficient determination (R2) and coefficient correlation (R) for those three different fluvial channel systems are relatively low (maximum value is 0.318 and 0.563, respectively), revealed non-linear correlation (Fig. 9).

Discussion

Sedimentary processes and development of the fluvial channel systems

The fluvial channel is the principal sediment conduit for transporting clastic sediments from the sediment source to the basin area in a fluvial depositional environment19,21,83–87. The three seismic units as well as the three seismic facies in the studied area are characterized by mainly sub-parallel to hummocky reflections configurations, low to high continuity, dominance of isolated bright amplitude and low to medium bedding frequency (Table 1 and Fig. 2), elongated and lobe shape (Table 1 and Figs. 5f, 6e, 7f) that indicate a channel system with its crevasse-splay, respectively.

The sediment transport mechanism in this fluvial channel systems is dominated by a bed-load sediment transport mechanism indicated by isolated bright amplitude, low continuity, and low bedding frequency in hummocky seismic reflection configuration (Figs. 2, 5, 6 and 7). In plan-view, this bed-load transport mechanism revealed from bright—isolated RMS amplitude and spectral decomposition with sinuous geomorphology (sinuosity index in the range of 1.0–1.4) indicated sandy material of the channel (seismic facies 1) compared to its shale counterpart (flood plain; seismic facies 2) in fluvial depositional system with low and soft RMS amplitude and spectral decomposition (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). In addition, flooding and/or channel-bank erosion occurred, as indicated by isolated bright amplitude with lobe shape attached to the channel (Table 1 and Figs. 5, 6, 7) produced crevasse-splay deposit (seismic facies 3 on the Table 1 and Figs. 5, 6, 7). Furthermore, based on width fluvial channel classification by Gibling68, most fluvial channels in the studied area are classified as medium channel that have a minimum width of 100 m (Fig. 8a). Based on channel body connectivity, the fluvial channel systems in the studied area are characterized by a single-story channel body (Table 1 and Figs. 5, 6, 7, 11) where the main channel body (medium channels classification by ) is isolated from other bodies, representing topographic low, symmetric or asymmetric (medium channel width) with the maximum vertical thickness located close to one of the margins19,68,86,88–90.

Depositional model of the three fluvial channel systems in the study area with a single-story channel body architecture and geological parameters interpretation.

The development of fluvial channel systems in the studied area can be divided into three different stages (Fig. 11). The first stage (oldest) was the deposition of fluvial channel system 1. The seismic architecture indicated the dominance of relatively fine material of the shale with continuous and medium to high seismic amplitude values in a bedding stacking pattern (Figs. 5g–l and 11a). Furthermore, the channel density on this fluvial channel system 1 is low, whereas the flood plain deposit accumulation is relatively high (Figs. 5 and 11a). In addition, this fluvial channel system 1 has an average maximum channel width of 256 m, meander belt width of 1750 m (Figs. 8 and 9), and sinuosity of 1.11 (Fig. 8c). The coefficient determination (R2) and coefficient correlation (R) for this channel system is relatively low—nonlinear correlation (0.318 and 0.563, respectively), indicated that channel width and meander belt width are developed from different influences (local and regional factors, e.g., collapsing the channel walls and faults, respectively). These results indicated a proxy of relatively low rate of sediment supply with dominant bed-load sediment transport mechanism, and more frequent flooding processes77–80,91. The second stage was the deposition of fluvial channel system 2 with higher channel density and direction (both channels and crevasse-splay of 178 °N) compared to fluvial channel system 1 (Fig. 11a,b respectively). Furthermore, the seismic facies and architecture of fluvial channel system 2 are characterized by relatively sub-parallel to hummocky reflection configuration, isolated bright amplitude, low continuity, and low bedding frequency with lateral—vertical separation of low to high seismic amplitude in a bedding stacking pattern (Figs. 6f–h and 11b). This fluvial channel system 2 has an average maximum channel width of 266 m, meander belt width of 881 m (Figs. 8 and 9), and sinuosity of 1.09 (Fig. 8c). Its coefficient determination (R2) and coefficient correlation (R) is 0.005 and 0.070, respectively (Fig. 9). These indicated nonlinear correlation that channel width and meander belt width are developed from different influences (local and regional factors, e.g., collapsing the channel walls and faults, respectively). In addition, the stratigraphic thickness of this fluvial channel system 2 shows a relatively thinner area in the south (Fig. 4d). These results indicated a proxy of higher sediment supply with dominant bed-load sediment transport mechanism, and that basin accommodation is decreasing in volume; thus, the area in the south is more exposed to the surface of erosion21,77,78,91 providing variation in paleo-topography. The fluvial channel system 2 is bounded at the top by H2, a continuous and high amplitude horizon that could indicate a basin-wide surface of very fine-grained suspension depositional system (Figs. 2 and 11b). The last stage was the deposition of fluvial channel system 3, where this youngest fluvial channel system has more channel density than fluvial channel systems 2 and 3 (Fig. 11a–c, respectively). In addition, the seismic facies and architecture of fluvial channel system 3 contain a hummocky reflection configuration, low continuity, isolated bright amplitude, relatively low bedding frequency with lateral variation that indicates the degradation effect of seismic amplitude (Figs. 7g–l and 11c). This fluvial channel system 3 has an average maximum channel width of 327 m, meander belt width of 1096 m (Figs. 8 and 9), and sinuosity of 1.07 (Fig. 8c). Its coefficient determination (R2) is 0.008, whereas coefficient correlation (R) is 0.089 (Fig. 9). These nonlinear correlations infer that channel width and meander belt width are developed from different influences (local and regional factors, e.g., collapsing the channel walls and faults, respectively). The stratigraphic thickness of this fluvial channel system 3 is thicker compared to the fluvial channel system 2 (Fig. 4d,e, respectively) and more avulsion processes in the south. These results indicated a proxy of higher sediment supply with dominant bed-load sediment transport mechanism, variations in paleo-topography, and frequent flooding processes77–81,91.

These sediment supply, avulsion processes, and sediment transport mechanism interpretation are supported by regional geological setting where the sediment source was mainly located in the north and northwest (with some Precambrian Arabian Shield paleo-high) of the studied area with a fluvial channel sediment routing system transferring the sediments to the Basin in the south (Fig. 11). This is also supported by Paleozoic regional study of Alsharhan and Kendall49, which described the Carboniferous and Early Permian periods deposits in the Arabian plate as having thickening trends to the basin in the southern part (for 250–500 m), whereas the thinning trend is toward the north and northeast area (minimum thickness of 100–150 m).

Morphometry comparison with other fluvial channel systems

The fluvial channel systems in the studied area are divided into three distinct classes by morphometric parameters and stratigraphic stacking position (proxy to the geological age). Comparison to another fluvial channel systems indicated similarity and disagreement (Fig. 12); these are a fluvial channel system described in Alqahtani et al.13, Harishidayat et al.15, Wood18, Tan et al.23.

Cross-plots showing the relationship between meander belt width and maximum channel width comparison with channels in other fluvial systems.

The fluvial channel systems in the studied area have a maximum channel width of 202 m and 340 m for fluvial channel system 1 and fluvial channel system 3, respectively (Figs. 9 and 12). They are both comparable to the fluvial channel systems in the Norwegian Barents Sea fluvial channel system documented in Harishidayat et al.15 and the Bohai Bay basin (offshore China) fluvial channel system types 3, 4 and 5 in Tan et al.23 (Fig. 12). In addition, they are also comparable to the Southeast Asia fluvial channel system (unit 6) documented in Alqahtani et al.13 (Fig. 12). The meander belt widths of the fluvial channel systems in the studied area are 589 m and 1924 m for fluvial channel system 3 and fluvial channel system 1, respectively. They are comparable with a fluvial channel system in the Norwegian Barents Sea fluvial channel system, Bohai Bay basin (offshore China) fluvial channel system types 3, 4 and 5, and northern Gulf of Mexico (U.S.A) fluvial channel system classes 1, 2 and 3 described above (Fig. 12). This agreement indicates that our interpretation of the fluvial channel systems in the studied area are consistent with and supported by existing knowledge and morphometric database from other established fluvial channel systems worldwide.

The cross-plots showing relationship between maximum channel width and meander belt width indicated that the fluvial channel systems in the studied area is neither in the cluster with the fluvial channel system of Alqahtani et al.13 nor that of Wood18 (Fig. 12). This might be due to the relatively gentle paleo-morphology that provided less power to increase the maximum channel width and meander belt width compared to the fluvial channel systems of Alqahtani et al.13 and Wood18. In addition, some local basin factors also influence the development of fluvial channel systems, e.g., subsidence could provide less sediment supply and channel density, uplift could provide higher sediment supply and channel density, collapsing of the channel wall could widen the channel width and meander belt width, etc.).

Petroleum prospectivity of the fluvial channel systems

The fluvial channel systems in the studied area are dominated by isolated bright amplitude, low continuity, and low bedding frequency in a hummocky seismic reflection configuration in addition to sinuous geomorphology (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). These results indicated that sandstone is the dominant filling deposit of the channel and crevasse-splay (seismic facies 1 and 3; Table 1) in the studied area that could act as a favorable reservoir with shale as a background deposit (i.e., flood plain deposit). The sandstone in the fluvial channel systems of the studied area has an isolated seismic amplitude with lateral variation values from relatively high seismic amplitude. This includes high RMS amplitude with bright spectral decomposition and maximum values of sweetness and variance attributes in the middle of the features and relatively low amplitude on both sides of the channel body margin for hundreds of meters (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). These results indicated that the channel reservoir in the studied area has lateral variation in rock properties; good porosity in the middle of the channel that degraded towards the sides of the channel body margin as can be recognized in Fig. 1115,20,41,92–95. In addition, as a single-story channel body, the maximum thickness of the channel reservoir in the studied area is located on one side of the channel body where the point-bar ends (Fig. 11). Stratigraphically, fluvial channel system 1 has less channel density in a single-story channel body architecture that provides a low net to gross ratio between clean sand and shale (Fig. 11a). Conversely, fluvial channel systems 2 and 3 have more channel density in a single-story channel body architecture that provides relatively high net to gross ratios (Fig. 11b,c).

Furthermore, the flood plain shale (seismic facies 2; Table 1) as a lithological background on this fluvial channel system is characterized by a continuous and high amplitude (Horizons 1–3) with a relatively parallel reflection configuration (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). The shale has a relatively low RMS amplitude, soft color on the RGB blending of the spectral decomposition with minimum values of sweetness and variance seismic attributes (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). This fluvial shale, together with its fluvial subtropical-tropical organic content (woody materials) could be a potential source rock (types III–IV) that produce hydrocarbons in the fluvial channel systems of the studied area93,95–98. In addition, fluvial channel system 1 has a relatively low net to gross ratio, thus having a higher probability for the presence of fluvial shale compared to other fluvial channel systems in the studied area (Fig. 11). Furthermore, this fluvial shale (Horizons 1 to 3) and in between channels has the potential for cap rock that act as a boundary for hydrocarbons to migrate vertically and horizontally, respectively.

The petroleum trapping mechanism in the fluvial channel systems of the studied area is a combination of structural and stratigraphic traps. The elongate ridge (anticline) with a relatively north–south structure orientation plunging from north to south could act as a structural trap (Fig. 4a–c). On the other hand, the fluvial shale (seismic facies 2; Table 1) mainly acts as an impermeable stratigraphic layer and matrix for hydrocarbons to traps (Figs. 5, 6 and 7). 3D seismic attribute analysis revealed the presence of a fluid flow feature (most likely gas) in the fluvial channel system of the studied area (Figs. 10 and 13a). This fluid flow feature is characterized by a maximum value of variance seismic attribute with a vertical feature99–103, and a velocity push-down effect (i.e., reduction in velocity and increase in the travel time) on the seismic amplitude104–106. This vertical fluid flow feature appeared in the three fluvial channel systems of the studied area with 1.5–1.6 km lateral dimensions (Fig. 13a) and tend to migrate through the faults system.

(a) 3D representation of the fluvial depositional system in the studied area with a time slices of RMS amplitude that shows fluvial system 1 (see Fig. 5 for more detailed interpretation) and gas chimney (vertical brown to orange body) present in all fluvial systems in the area (See Fig. 10 for the interpretation), together with a box probe of seismic amplitude. (b) Channel architecture and proposed reservoir drainage strategy.

As a single-story channel body, the three fluvial channel systems in the studied area are mainly poorly connected both in vertical and lateral extents, except for some cross-cutting channels (Figs. 11 and 13). Therefore, the drainage reservoir strategy will need to utilize horizontal wellbore with a wellbore spacing (meander belt width) of 500–2000 m to be able to extract the hydrocarbons fully geometrically in both vertical and lateral dimension (Figs. 9 and 13b). The faults displacement in our study area-interval is very low (as we can see on the Fig. 2) so that having very minor to no roles in reservoir appraisal and compartmentalization, as well as petroleum trapping mechanism.

Conclusion

This work focused on architecture and geomorphology of the fluvial channel systems in the Arabian Basin and concluded the following:

Three distinct fluvial channel systems were preserved within the stratigraphy (youngest: fluvial channel system 3 and oldest: fluvial channel system 1) of the studied interval and classified as single-story channel body.
