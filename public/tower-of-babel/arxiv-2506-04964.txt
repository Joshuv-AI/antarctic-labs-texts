An improved bound for strongly regular graphs with smallest eigenvalue − m -m
Jack H. Koolen, Chenhui Lv, Greg Markowsky, Jongyook Park

Abstract
In 1979, Neumaier gave a bound on λ \lambda in terms of m m and μ \mu , where − m -m is the smallest eigenvalue of a primitive strongly regular graph, unless the graph in question belongs to one of the two infinite families of strongly regular graphs. We improve this result. We also indicate how our methods can be used to give an alternate derivation of Bruck’s Completion Theorem for orthogonal arrays.

Introduction

Sims (see
[
3
, Theorem 8.6.4]
) showed that for
m
an integer at least 2, except for a finite number of exceptions, any primitive strongly regular graph with smallest eigenvalue
-m
belongs to one of the two infinite families of strongly regular graphs. Neumaier, building on results of Bruck and Bose, made the statement of Sims explicit by completing the following theorem.

Theorem 1.1

(cf.
[
3
, Theorem 8.6.3]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a primitive strongly regular graph with smallest eigenvalue
-m
, where
m
is a positive integer. Let

f(m,\mu)=\frac{1}{2}m(m-1)(\mu+1)+\mu-m-1
.Then the following hold:

(i)

(Bruck
[
4
]
) If
\mu=m(m-1)
and
\lambda>f(m,\mu)
, then
\Gamma
is the
collinearity graph of a partial geometry
\mathrm{pg}(K,R,T)
with
T=R-1
; that is, a
Latin square graph
\mathrm{LS}_{m}(\lambda-m(m-3))
.

(ii)

(Bose
[
1
]
) If
\mu=m^{2}
and
\lambda>f(m,\mu)
, then
\Gamma
is the
collinearity graph of a partial geometry
\mathrm{pg}(K,R,T)
with
T=R
; that is, the block
graph of a
2
-
(\lambda(m-1)-m(m-1)(m-2)+m,m,1)
design.

(iii)

(Neumaier
[
15
, Theorem 4.7]
) If
\mu\neq m(m-1)
and
\mu\neq m^{2}
, then
\lambda\leq f(m,\mu)
.

Note that Neumaier also showed that for a primitive strongly regular graph with smallest eigenvalue
-m
, the parameter
\mu
is bounded by
(2m-3)m^{3}
(see
[
15
, Theorem 3.1]
). These two facts imply that the second largest eigenvalue is
bounded by a function in
m
, and hence the valency is also bounded by a function in
m
, unless the graph belongs
to one of the two infinite families mentioned in the theorem.

In this paper, we improve the result of Neumaier as follows.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a primitive strongly regular graph with smallest
eigenvalue
-m
, where
m
is a positive integer. Let
f(m,\mu)=\frac{8}{3}m(\mu-1)-\frac{2}{3}\mu+3m-\frac{10}{3}
. Then the following hold:

(i)

If
\mu=m(m-1)
and
\lambda>f(m,\mu)
, then

\Gamma
is the collinearity graph of a partial geometry

\mathrm{pg}(K,R,T)
with
T=R-1
; that is, a Latin square
graph
\mathrm{LS}_{m}(\lambda-m(m-3))
.

(ii)

If
\mu=m^{2}
and
\lambda>f(m,\mu)
, then

\Gamma
is the collinearity graph of a partial geometry

\mathrm{pg}(K,R,T)
with
T=R
; that is, the block graph of a

2
-
(\lambda(m-1)-m(m-1)(m-2)+m,m,1)
design.

(iii)

If
\mu\neq m(m-1)
and
\mu\neq m^{2}
, then

\lambda\leq f(m,\mu)
.

We should mention that Metsch
[
13
]
has already shown Theorem
1.2
under the additional condition that the strongly regular graph in question has geometric parameters lying within a particular range of values. Without this additional condition, Metsch’s method and result are not valid. Our contribution is showing that this additional condition is not required, and giving a more elementary and general method for proving the result. We have endeavored to keep the paper as simple as possible; as a consequence, our result is slightly weaker than Metsch’s when the graph in question does satisfy the additional requirement. However, with a bit of extra effort (which we do not expend in this paper) our methods can be adapted to prove exactly Metch’s result in the case that he proved it.

In comparison to Neumaier’s result (Theorem
1.1
), our main result (Theorem
1.2
) provides a better bound when
m\geq 6
. For
m\leq 5
, Neumaier’s bound is typically better, except in a few exceptional cases; see Remark
5.4
below for more details.

We now give an outline of the proof. The condition
\lambda>f(m,\mu)
ensure that the strongly regular graph is the point graph of a partial linear space, using a result of Metsch (See Theorem
2.1
).
Using the vertex-line incidence matrix, we show that a strongly regular graph satisfying the condition
\lambda>f(m,\mu)
has parameters which would match those of the point graph of a partial geometry. Then we use the method of
[
9
]
to show that our strongly regular graph must in fact be geometric (that is, it must be the point graph of a partial geometry). Finally, Theorem
1.2
follows easily from this.

The paper is organized as follows. The next section reviews (without proofs) the part of the theory of strongly regular and distance-regular graphs that is relevant to us. Following this, in Section
3
, we have a short discussion of the relation between classical parameters, Delsarte cliques, and strongly regular graphs. Section
4
applies many of the methods developed in
[
9
]
in a much more general setting, and it revealed there that these methods really are about the presence or absence of Delsarte cliques in a graph; this interpretation was not noted in
[
9
]
. In Section
5
, we finally are in a position to prove our main result. We have also included an appendix, Appendix
A
, which deals with the connection between our results and Bruck’s well-known completion theorem for orthogonal arrays.

2
Preliminaries

All graphs under consideration are assumed to be simple and connected, unless otherwise stated. We also assume graphs are not complete, to avoid the continuous need to qualify statements. The following are standard notations. Let
\Gamma
be a graph with vertex set
V(\Gamma)
. For subsets
A,B
of
V(\Gamma)
, we will use the notation
d(A,B)
to denote the distance between them; that is, the length of a shortest path with one endpoint in each set. When either or both sets are singletons, we will drop the braces, so for instance
d(x,y)
is the distance between vertices
x
and
y
, and
d(x,B)
is the length of a shortest path connecting vertex
x
with a vertex in
B
. The
diameter

D
of
\Gamma
is the maximum distance among two vertices of
\Gamma
. For vertex
x
, we let
\Gamma_{i}(x)=\{y\in V(\Gamma):d(x,y)=i\}

(0\leq i\leq D)
, and write
\Gamma(x):=\Gamma_{1}(x)
.

An
amply regular graph
with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
is a
k
-regular graph on

v
vertices, with the property that every pair of adjacent vertices have exactly
\lambda

common neighbors and every pair of vertices at distance two have exactly
\mu
common
neighbors. A
strongly regular graph
is an amply regular graph with diameter 2, so that each pair of distinct vertices have either
\lambda
or
\mu
common neighbors, depending on whether or not they are adjacent. A strongly regular graph with given parameters will be referred to as
srg(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
. To dispense with trivialities, we will be concerned only with
primitive
strongly regular graphs, which are connected strongly regular graphs whose complement is also connected. A connected graph
\Gamma
with diameter
D
is called

distance-regular
if there are integers
b_{i},c_{i}

(0\leq i\leq D)
such that for any two vertices
x,y\in V(\Gamma)
with
d(x,y)=i
, there are precisely
c_{i}

neighbors of
y
in

\Gamma_{i-1}(x)
and
b_{i}
neighbors of
y
in
\Gamma_{i+1}(x)

(cf.
[
2
, page 126]
). In particular, a distance-regular graph
\Gamma
is regular with valency

k:=b_{0}
and we define
a_{i}:=k-b_{i}-c_{i}
for notational convenience. Distance-regular graphs therefore come with a set of parameters
(b_{0}=k,b_{1},\ldots,b_{D-1};c_{1},\ldots,c_{D})
, referred to as the
intersection array
.

Strongly regular graphs are simply distance-regular graphs with diameter
D=2
, although they have a number of important properties not shared with the more general class of distance-regular graphs. For this and other reasons, we prefer to use the ”
\lambda
and
\mu
” notation rather than the “
a_{i}
’s,
b_{i}
’s, and
c_{i}
’s” notation when discussing strongly regular graphs. We will refer to the sets of parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
(for strongly regular graphs) or
(b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{D-1};c_{1},\ldots,c_{D})
(for distance-regular graphs) as the
standard parameters
of the graph, a designation whose purpose will become more clear when we introduce the classical parameters of such graphs.

An important property of distance-regular graphs is that they have exactly
D+1
distinct eigenvalues, which is in general far fewer than the
v
eigenvalues possessed by an arbitrary graph on
v
vertices (note that eigenvalues here refers to eigenvalues of the adjacency matrix and that the eigenvalues different from the valency are called
nontrivial
). For this to occur, some at least of the eigenvalues of a distance-regular graph must have relatively high multiplicity. The spectral structure of a distance-regular graph carries a great deal of information about the graph, and for this reason is a major part of the theory.

Strongly regular graphs with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)=(4t+1,2t,t-1,t)
are
known as
conference graphs
. A strongly regular graph is either a conference graph
or has all eigenvalues being integers (cf.
[
8
, Lemma 10.3.3]
).
In this paper, we always assume that
\Gamma
is not a conference graph.

In certain classes of
k
-regular graphs, the bound
|C|\leq 1+\frac{k}{-\theta_{min}}
must be obeyed, where
C
is any clique and
\theta_{min}
is the smallest eigenvalue of the graph, necessarily negative. This is known as the
Delsarte bound
, as it was proved by Delsarte in
[
7
]
for strongly regular graphs, later to be generalized by Godsil to distance-regular graphs; see
[
2
, Proposition 4.4.6]
for two short proofs of this. It was further generalized to
2
-walk regular graphs in
[
5
]
, although we will not need this generalization. We will refer to a clique in
\Gamma
as a
Delsarte clique
if its order is equal to
1+\frac{k}{-\theta_{min}}
, in other words if achieves equality in the Delsarte bound.

An
incidence structure
is a tuple
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
where
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}
are non-empty disjoint sets and
\mathcal{I}\subseteq\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{L}
. The elements of
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{L}
are called
points
and
lines
, respectively. If
(p,\ell)\in\mathcal{I}
, then we say that
p
is
incident
with
\ell
, or that
p
is on the line
\ell
. The
order of a point
is the number of lines it is incident with, and the
order of a line
is the number of points it contains.

A
partial linear space
is an incidence structure such that each pair of distinct points are both incident with at most one line, and a
linear space
is a partial linear space such that every pair of points is contained in a unique line; these requirements justify the term ”lines” in the definitions. A
partial geometry

pg(K,R,T)
is a partial linear space with the property that every line contains exactly
K
points, every point lies on exactly
R
lines, and given a line
L
and point
x\notin L
there are exactly
T
lines containing
x
and intersecting
L
(in the literature, one also meets the notation
pg(s,t,\alpha)
, where
K=s+1,R=t+1,T=\alpha
). We will assume that
K,R>1
and
T>0
. Given a
pg(K,R,T)
, call it
X
, we may dualize it; in other words we may create a new partial geometry
Y
whose points are the lines of
X
and whose lines are the points of
X
, with a point lying on a line in
Y
precisely when the corresponding line contains the corresponding point in
X
. In the process we obtain a
pg(R,K,T)
.

Incidence structures relate to graphs in the following way. The
point graph

\Gamma
of an incidence structure
(\mathcal{P},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
is the graph with vertex set
\mathcal{P}
, and two distinct points are adjacent if and only if they are on a common line. General distance-regular and strongly regular graphs need not be related to incidence structures, but many are the point graphs of partial linear spaces, or of partial geometries. Knowledge that a graph is associated to such a structure gives access to powerful results on the structure of the graph, and therefore a sufficient condition for a strongly regular graph to be of this type is of great value. The most important result on this was proved by Metsch in
[
13
]
.

Theorem 2.1

(cf.
[
14
, Result 2.1]
,
[
13
]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be an amply regular graph with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
.
Assume that there exists a positive integer
\sigma
such that the following two conditions are satisfied:

(1)

(\sigma+1)(\lambda+1)-k>(\mu-1){\sigma+1\choose 2};

(2)

\lambda+1>(\mu-1)(2\sigma-1)
.

Define a line as a maximal clique with at least
\lambda+2-(\mu-1)(\sigma-1)
vertices.
Then
X=(V(\Gamma),{\mathcal{L}},\in)
is a partial linear space, where
\mathcal{L}
is the set of all lines,
\Gamma
is the point graph of
X
, and the symbol
\in
means that the relation required for the incidence structure is merely given by inclusion. Moreover, each vertex lies on at most
\sigma
lines, and every pair of adjacent vertices in
\Gamma
lie in a unique line.

Note that the condition
(1)
of Theorem
2.1
implies that each vertex lies on at most
\sigma
lines because it means that there are no
(\sigma+1)
-claws (see, for example
[
11
, Lemma 1.6]
).
Eventually, we will apply this theorem in order to prove our main result.

The point graph of a partial geometry
pg(K,R,T)
is automatically strongly regular, and is in fact a
srg(K+K(K-1)(R-1)/T,R(K-1),(R-1)(T-1)+K-2,RT)
. We will say that a strongly regular graph which is the point graph of a partial geometry is
geometric
. Note, however, that it is possible for a strongly regular graph to have parameters of this form but which is not the point graph of a partial geometry; it is even possible that no partial geometry with the corresponding parameters exist (an example is the Cameron graph, see the introduction of
[
9
]
for a discussion of this). If
\Gamma
is a strongly regular graph with parameters of the correct form, we will say that
\Gamma

has geometric parameters
. To be precise,
\Gamma
has geometric parameters if it is
srg(K+K(K-1)(R-1)/T,R(K-1),(R-1)(T-1)+K-2,RT)
, where
R,K,T
are positive integers, and we further require that
T\leq R
and
T\leq K
, conditions which are forced by the definition of a
pg(R,K,T)
.

Another important result for us on partial geometries was proved by Neumaier.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
15
, Theorem 4.5]

If a
pg(K,R,T)
has
T\leq R-2
, then

K-1\leq(R-T)^{2}(2T-1),

and equality implies
T=1
or
R=2T+1
.

Note that the dualization process described earlier allows us to interchange
K
and
R
in this result, and in fact we have stated the dual of the result as it was originally stated in
[
15
]
.

We now introduce the SPLS(
\sigma
) property, which was first introduced in

[
12
]
. Let
\Gamma
be a strongly regular graph with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
, and let
\sigma\geq 2
be an integer. We say that
\Gamma
has the
SPLS(\sigma)
property if
\Gamma
is the point graph of a partial linear space
(V(\Gamma),\mathcal{L},\in)
, where the set of lines
\mathcal{L}
are the maximal cliques with at least
\lambda+2-(\mu-1)(\sigma-1)
vertices,
\lambda\geq(2\sigma-1)(\mu-1)
, and each vertex lies on at most
\sigma
lines. Note that these conditions come directly from the conditions in Theorem
2.1
; in particular, if
\Gamma
satisfies the conditions of that theorem for some
\sigma
, then it has the
SPLS(\sigma)
property. We note further that if
\Gamma
has the
SPLS(\sigma)
property, then a lower bound on the size of each line is
\lambda+2-(\mu-1)(\sigma-1)\geq\sigma(\mu-1)+2
.

The notion of
classical parameters
is important in the theory of distance-regular graphs (see
[
2
, Chapter 6]
or
[
6
, Section 3.1.1]
). These are parameters of the form
(D,b,\alpha,\beta)
, where
D
must be a positive integer and
b
an integer not equal to
0
or
-1
. Here
D
is the diameter of the graph and
b,\alpha,\beta
are other parameters which have more subtle interpretations. The intersection array of a distance-regular graph with classical parameters can be entirely generated from the classical parameters, according to the following formulas (note that
[j]_{b}
here denotes the
Gaussian
b
-number
, namely
[j]_{b}:=1+b+b^{2}+\ldots+b^{j-1}
with
[0]_{b}:=0
).

\begin{gathered}b_{i}=\Big([D]_{b}-[i]_{b}\Big)\Big(\beta-\alpha[i]_{b}\Big)\qquad(i=0,\ldots,D-1)\\
\hskip-19.91684ptc_{i}=[i]_{b}\Big(1+\alpha[i-1]_{b}\Big)\qquad~~~~~~~~(i=1,\ldots,D)\end{gathered}

(2.0.1)

From
[
2
, Corollary 8.4.2]
, we know that the eigenvalues of
\Gamma
are

[D-i]_{b}(\beta-\alpha[i]_{b})-[i]_{b}=\frac{b_{i}}{b^{i}}-[i]_{b},\quad 0\leq i\leq D.

(2.0.2)

We note that if
b\geq 1
, then the eigenvalues
\theta_{i}=\frac{b_{i}}{b^{i}}-[i](0\leq i\leq D)
of
\Gamma
are in the natural ordering, i.e.,

\theta_{D}<\theta_{D-1}<\ldots<\theta_{1}<\theta_{0}=k
.

Not every distance-regular graph has classical parameters, but many do; the theoretical reason for the abundance of graphs with classical parameters within the class of distance-regular graphs is at this point not clear at all. However, when a distance-regular graph has classical parameters, then these parameters fit very naturally with the Delsarte bound and Delsarte cliques, if there are any. That is the topic of the next section.

3
Delsarte cliques and classical parameters

The purpose of this section is to clarify the relation between Delsarte cliques and classical parameters, but we begin by showing that every strongly regular graph has classical parameters, as well as clarifying the relationship between the two sets of parameters in this context.

Lemma 3.1
.

A distance-regular graph
\Gamma
with classical parameters
(2,b,\alpha,\beta)
is strongly regular with standard parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
, where

\begin{gathered}v=1+(b+1)\beta+\frac{b\beta(\beta-\alpha)}{(\alpha+1)}\\
\hskip-88.2037ptk=(b+1)\beta\\
\hskip-75.39963pt\lambda=\beta+\alpha b-1\\
\hskip-59.75095pt\mu=(\alpha+1)(b+1)\\
\end{gathered}

(3.0.1)

Furthermore, the eigenvalues distinct from
k
are
\beta-\alpha-1
and
-(b+1)
.

On the other hand, if
\Gamma
is a
srg(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
, and
r
is an eigenvalue of
\Gamma
distinct from
k
, we may define

\begin{gathered}b=-r-1\\
\hskip 1.42271pt\alpha=\frac{-\mu}{r}-1\\
\hskip-17.07182pt\beta=\frac{-k}{r},\end{gathered}

(3.0.2)

and
\Gamma
has classical parameters
(2,b,\alpha,\beta)
.

Proof:
For the first part of the theorem, note that the formulas for
k,\mu,
and
\lambda
can be easily deduced from the definitions in (
2.0.1
), and that
v
follows from these by
v=1+k+\frac{k(k-\lambda-1)}{\mu}
. To calculate the eigenvalues, we note that it is well known from the theory that the nontrivial eigenvalues
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
of
\Gamma
are the positive and negative roots of the quadratic
x^{2}-(\lambda-\mu)x-(k-\mu)
, and therefore we have the fundamental relations
-\theta_{1}\theta_{2}=k-\mu
and
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}=\lambda-\mu
. It may be easily checked that
\beta-\alpha-1
and
-(b+1)
satisfy these two fundamental relations, and it follows that they must therefore be the two roots of the quadratic,
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
, in some order.

For the second part of the theorem, we see from the previous paragraph that if
\Gamma
has classical parameters
(2,b,\alpha,\beta)
then
b
must be equal to
-r-1
, where
r
is either
\theta_{1}
or
\theta_{2}
. The values of
\alpha
and
\beta
are therefore forced by the formulas in (
3.0.1
), and the result follows. ∎

This lemma shows that we may pass back and forth between the standard and classical parameters, and shows in fact that there are always exactly two possible choices for the classical parameters of a given strongly regular graph, depending upon which of the nontrivial eigenvalues we choose to be
r
. If
r\geq 0
, then
b\leq-1
(with equality only for complete multipartite graphs) and
\alpha<-1,\beta<0
; if, on the other hand, we have
r<-1
, then
b>0,\alpha>-1
, and
\beta>0
. We will adopt the convention of always choosing
r
to be negative, essentially the positive set of classical parameters, although we note that it is still possible that
\alpha<0
(for example, the Clebsch graph with standard parameters
(16,5,0,2)
has classical parameters
(2,b,\alpha,\beta)=(2,2,-\frac{1}{3},\frac{5}{3}))
. We will therefore use the notation
srgc(b,\alpha,\beta)
to denote a strongly regular graph with these classical parameters (we do not need to give the diameter, since any strongly regular graph has diameter
2
).

The following lemma shows why we prefer classical parameters to standard ones in relation to Delsarte cliques. It is true for all distance-regular graphs, so we prove it in this more general setting. We will let
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{min}
denote the second largest and smallest eigenvalues of our graph, respectively.

Lemma 3.2
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a distance-regular graph with classical parameters
(D,b,\alpha,\beta)
, with
b\geq 1
and
D\geq 2
(included in this is that
\Gamma
is
srgc(b,\alpha,\beta))
, and that
\Gamma
contains a Delsarte clique,
C
. Then
|C|=\beta+1
and
|\Gamma(x)\cap C|=\alpha+1
whenever
d(x,C)=1
. In particular,
\alpha
is a non-negative integer, and
\beta
is a positive integer. Furthermore, if
D=2
then
d(x,C)=1
for every
x\in V(\Gamma)\backslash C
.

Proof:
Note that
k=b_{0}=[D]_{b}\beta
and
b_{1}=([D]_{b}-1)(\beta-\alpha)
. As mentioned
earlier,
\theta_{1}=\frac{b_{1}}{b}-1
,
\theta_{min}=-[D]_{b}
. Now the lemma follows
immediately from
[
2
, Proposition 4.4.6]
. ∎

We now give the requirement on the classical parameters for a strongly regular graph to have geometric parameters.

Lemma 3.3
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is
srgc(b,\alpha,\beta)
, with
b,\alpha,\beta
nonnegative integers and
\alpha\leq b,\beta
. Then
\Gamma
has geometric parameters, with
b+1=R,\beta+1=K,\alpha+1=T
.

Proof:
Straightforward calculation using Lemma
3.1
shows that
\Gamma
is
srg(K+K(K-1)(R-1)/T,R(K-1),(R-1)(T-1)+K-2,RT)
, and the result follows. ∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a
\text{srg}(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
with smallest eigenvalue
-m
, where
m
is a positive integer. Then
\Gamma
has geometric parameters if and only if
\frac{k}{m}
is an integer and
\mu\leq m^{2}
.

Proof.

As mentioned earlier, the nontrivial eigenvalues
\theta_{1}>\theta_{2}
of
\Gamma
are the positive and negative roots of the quadratic equation
x^{2}-(\lambda-\mu)x-(k-\mu)=0
, and therefore satisfy the relations
\theta_{1}\theta_{2}=\mu-k
and
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}=\lambda-\mu
.

We first assume that
\Gamma
has geometric parameters. Then
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}=\lambda-\mu=K-T-1+(-R)
and
\theta_{1}\theta_{2}=\mu-k=-R(K-T-1)
imply that
\theta_{1}=K-T-1
and
\theta_{2}=-R
(as
T\leq R
). Since
k=R(K-1)
and
m=R
, we know that
\frac{k}{m}=K-1
is an integer. Also we have that
\mu=RT\leq R^{2}=m^{2}
, where the inequality holds as
T\leq R
.

Now, we assume that
\frac{k}{m}
is an integer and
\mu\leq m^{2}
. Note that by Lemma
3.1
,
\Gamma
is a
srgc(b,\alpha,\beta)
with
b=m-1
,
\alpha=\frac{\mu}{m}-1
and
\beta=\frac{k}{m}
. Since
\theta_{1}+\theta_{2}=\lambda-\mu
and
\theta_{2}=-m
are integers,
\theta_{1}
is also an integer, and this implies that
\frac{k}{m}-\frac{\mu}{m}
is an integer as
\theta_{1}\theta_{2}=k-\mu
. Then the assumption that
\frac{k}{m}
is an integer implies that
\frac{\mu}{m}
is an integer. Thus,
b,\alpha
and
\beta
are all integers. Indeed, they are all nonnegative integers.
Note that the assumption
\mu\leq m^{2}
is equivalent to
\alpha=\frac{\mu}{m}-1\leq m-1=b
. Clearly,
\mu\leq k+m
holds, and this is equivalent to
\alpha=\frac{\mu}{m}-1\leq\frac{k}{m}=\beta
. Thus, by Lemma
3.3
,
\Gamma
has geometric parameters.
This finishes the proof.
∎

4
Strongly regular graphs that arise as the point graph of a partial linear space

In this section, we apply the methods developed in
[
9
]
to a general setting, in order to provide a collection of lemmas which are required for Theorem
1.2
. Delsarte cliques, and the vertices contained in them, are of principal importance throughout. We will assume that
\Gamma
is the
point graph of a partial linear space
X=(V(\Gamma),\mathcal{L},\in)
, where the set of lines
\mathcal{L}
consists
of a certain collection of maximal cliques of
\Gamma
.

Let
\Gamma
be a strongly regular graph with classical parameters
(b,\alpha,\beta)
as defined in the previous section, and let
m=-\theta_{min}
(which agrees with the notation in
[
15
]
). For each
x\in V(\Gamma)
, let
\tau(x)
denote the number of lines in the partial linear space
X
containing
x
. The following lemma, giving us a lower bound on
\tau(x)
, is important.

Lemma 4.1
.

\tau(x)\geq m
for all
x
.

Proof:
Recall from Lemma
3.2
that the maximal size of a line is
\beta+1
, and by the definition of a partial linear space any two distinct lines containing
x
can only intersect at
x
. Therefore, any collection of
m^{\prime}<m
lines containing
x
would contain less than
m^{\prime}\beta
vertices in
\Gamma(x)
, however there are
k=m\beta
vertices in
\Gamma(x)
, so there must be some vertices in
\Gamma(x)
not contained in any of these
m^{\prime}
lines. Since every edge is contained in a unique line, we see that there must be a line containing
x
which is not among our collection of
m^{\prime}
lines. It follows that there must be at least
m
lines containing
x
. ∎

It follows also from this argument that, if
\tau(x)=m
for some
x
, then every line containing
x
must be a Delsarte clique, containing exactly
1+\beta
vertices. We will call such a vertex (with
\tau(x)=m
) a
Delsarte vertex
, and let
V_{D}
denote the set of Delsarte vertices in
V(\Gamma)
.

We also will need the following estimate on the number of lines.

Lemma 4.2
.

We have
\min(g,|V_{D}|)\geq|V(\Gamma)|-|{\cal L}|
, where
g
is the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
-m
of the adjaceny matrix
A
of
\Gamma
.

Proof:
We recall that
{\cal L}
denotes the set of lines in the partial linear space. Let
M
denote the
line-vertex incidence matrix
of
\Gamma
. That is,
M
is a
|{\cal L}|\times|V(\Gamma)|
matrix whose rows are indexed by the elements of
{\cal L}
and columns are indexed by the vertices of
\Gamma
, with
M_{ij}=1
when vertex
x_{j}
is contained in line
L_{i}
and 0 otherwise. Since every edge is contained in a unique line, we will have

M^{T}M=A+diag(\tau(x_{1}),\tau(x_{2}),\ldots,\tau(x_{n})).

where
V(\Gamma)=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and
diag(\cdot)
denotes the diagonal matrix with the given values on the diagonal. It is clear that
rank(M)\leq\min(|{\cal L}|,|V(\Gamma)|)
, and the same must be true of
A+diag(\tau(x_{1}),\tau(x_{2}),\ldots,\tau(x_{n}))
.

We know that the smallest eigenvalue of
A
is
-m
of multiplicity
g
, and the eigenvalues of
diag(\tau(x_{1}),\tau(x_{2}),\ldots,\tau(x_{n}))
are simply the quantities
\{\tau(x_{j})\}
, all of which are at least
m
by Lemma
4.1
.

Suppose
v
is a unit 0-eigenvector of
M^{T}M
. Then
v^{T}(A+diag(\tau(x_{1}),\tau(x_{2}),\ldots,\tau(x_{n})))v=0
. However, we must also have
v^{T}Av\geq-m
and
v^{T}diag(\tau(x_{1}),\tau(x_{2}),\ldots,\tau(x_{n}))v\geq m
. These inequalities must therefore be equalitites, and thus
v
must necessarily be a
m
-eigenvector of
diag(\tau(x_{1}),\tau(x_{2}),\ldots,\tau(x_{n}))
and a
(-m)
-eigenvector of
A
. We see that
|V_{D}|
, which is the dimension of the
m
-eigenspace of
diag(\tau(x_{1}),\tau(x_{2}),\ldots,\tau(x_{n}))
, is at least as big as the dimension of the 0-eigenspace of
M^{T}M
, which is
|V(\Gamma)|-rank(M)\geq|V(\Gamma)|-|{\cal L}|
. The same is true of
g
, the dimension of the
(-m)
-eigenspace of
A
. The result follows. ∎

The following lemma combines the previous lemma with a simple upper bound on
|{\cal L}|
in order to give a lower bound on
|V_{D}|
in the case
\mu\geq 2
. In fact, the estimate does hold when
\mu=1
, but gives no information since always
\sigma\geq 2
. As such, we need a new argument that holds when
\mu=1
, and this is given by the subsequent Lemma
4.4
below.

Lemma 4.3
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a srg
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
. If
\mu\geq 2
and
\Gamma
has the
SPLS(\sigma)
property, then

|V_{D}|\geq\Big(1-\frac{\sigma}{\sigma(\mu-1)+2}\Big)|V(\Gamma)|.

In particular, if
\mu\geq 3
then
|V_{D}|\geq\frac{1}{2}|V(\Gamma)|
, and if
\mu=2
then
|V_{D}|>2
.

Proof:
Note that
|{\cal L}|
can be upper bounded by summing the number of lines containing each vertex and dividing by the minimum size of a line. In other words

|{\cal L}|\leq\frac{\sum_{x\in V(\Gamma)}\tau(x)}{(\lambda+2)-(\mu-1)(\sigma-1)}\leq\frac{|V(\Gamma)|\sigma}{((2\sigma-1)(\mu-1)+2)-(\sigma-1)(\mu-1)}=\frac{|V(\Gamma)|\sigma}{\sigma(\mu-1)+2}.

By Lemma
4.2
, we know that
|V_{D}|\geq|V(\Gamma)|-|\cal L|
, and hence we have

|V_{D}|\geq\Big(1-\frac{\sigma}{\sigma(\mu-1)+2}\Big)|V(\Gamma)|.

For the final statement, if
\mu\geq 3
then the fraction
\frac{\sigma}{\sigma(\mu-1)+2}
is less than
\frac{1}{2}
, so the result follows. If
\mu=2
then
1-\frac{\sigma}{\sigma(\mu-1)+2}=\frac{2}{\sigma+2}
. The conditions
\sigma\geq 2
and
\lambda\geq(2\sigma-1)(\mu-1)=2\sigma-1
, required for the
SPLS(\sigma)
property, imply that
|V(\Gamma)|>\lambda+2>\sigma+2
. Thus,
|V_{D}|\geq\frac{2}{\sigma+2}|V(\Gamma)|>2
. ∎

If
\mu=1
the previous lemma is not useful, but fortunately there is a simple condition which gives that
\Gamma
is geometric, and this is sufficient for our purposes.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose
\Gamma
is a srg
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)
with
\mu=1
. Then
\Gamma
is not geometric, and
(\lambda+1)(\lambda+2)\leq k
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a geometric strongly regular graph, and fix a vertex
x\in V(\Gamma)
. By Lemma
3.2
, every vertex not adjacent to
x
has at least one neighbour on each of the
\frac{k}{\lambda+1}
lines containing
x
, and these neighbours are all distinct. Therefore,
\mu\geq\frac{k}{\lambda+1}\geq 2
. This yields our first conclusion.

For the second, assume
\mu=1
and
(\lambda+1)(\lambda+2)>k
. This implies that
\Gamma
has the

\text{SPLS}(\lambda+1)
property, since in this case we clearly have
\lambda+1\geq 2
, and all the conditions of Theorem
2.1
are satisfied for
\sigma=\lambda+1
. Now we have

|{\cal L}|\leq\frac{\sum_{x\in V(\Gamma)}\tau(x)}{(\lambda+2)-(\mu-1)(\sigma-1)}\leq\frac{|V(\Gamma)|(\lambda+1)}{(\lambda+2)}<|V(\Gamma)|

Hence, there exists at least one Delsarte vertex in
\Gamma
(by Lemma
4.2
).
Since
\mu=1
, for each vertex
x
of
\Gamma
, any two nonadjacent vertices in
\Gamma(x)
cannot have a common neighbor in
\Gamma(x)
. Also, clearly each vertex in
\Gamma(x)
has exactly
\lambda
neighbors in
\Gamma(x)
as
\Gamma(x)
is
\lambda
-regular. Hence,
\Gamma(x)
is a disjoint union of
\frac{k}{\lambda+1}

cliques, each of order
\lambda+1
. In other words, each vertex is contained in

\frac{k}{\lambda+1}
maximal cliques of order
\lambda+2
. Therefore, if a Delsarte
vertex exists, every vertex in
\Gamma
is a Delsarte vertex, and thus
\Gamma
is
geometric. This contradicts our earlier conclusion, and the result follows. ∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a
\mathrm{srgc}(b,\alpha,\beta)
with
b\geq 1
.
Suppose that
\Gamma
contains two distinct Delsarte vertices.
Then
0\leq\alpha\leq b
, and
\alpha
is an integer.

Proof.

Let
x,z
be two vertices at distance two from each other such that
x
is a Delsarte vertex, and there exists a Delsarte clique
C
containing
z
such that
x
has at least one neighbor in
C
. By Lemma
3.2
,
x
has exactly
\alpha+1
neighbors in
C
. Therefore,
\alpha
is a non-negative integer. Since
x
is a Delsarte vertex, there are
m
lines through
x
. By Lemma
3.1
, we know that
m=b+1
. Let
C_{1},C_{2},\ldots,C_{b+1}
denote these lines, each of which is a Delsarte clique.

Note that
x
has exactly
\alpha+1
neighbors in
C
. Furthermore, each clique
C_{i}
intersects
C
in at most one vertex for
i=1,2,\ldots,b+1
. This implies that
\alpha+1\leq b+1
, and hence
\alpha\leq b
. Thus, we conclude that
0\leq\alpha\leq b
, and
\alpha
is an integer.

Next, let
y
be a Delsarte vertex in
\Gamma
distinct from
x
. Assume first that
d(x,y)=1
. There exists a Delsarte clique
C
that contains
y
but not
x
. As
C
is a maximal clique, there exists a vertex
z\in C
not adjacent to
x
. By the above argument, we obtain
0\leq\alpha\leq b
, and
\alpha
is an integer.

Now assume
d(x,y)=2
. In this case, let
z=y
, and the conclusion follows as before. This completes the proof. ∎

Remark 4.6
.

The proofs of Lemmas
4.1
,
4.2
, and
4.3
do not make use of the diameter of
\Gamma
, and therefore all hold true for any graphs which are distance-regular, and with the same proofs. Lemma
4.4
requires diameter 2, and Lemma
4.5
requires
m=b+1
, and this is not true for arbitrary distance-regular graphs, so these lemmas do require that the graph is strongly regular. In
[
12
]
, the authors presented versions of Lemmas
4.4
and
4.5
valid for distance-regular graphs of arbitrary diameter with classical parameters.

5
Proof of Theorem
1.2

In this section, we will finally prove our main result. In Theorem
5.1
, we give a sufficient condition for a
srgc(b,\alpha,\beta)
to have the SPLS
(\sigma)
property. In Lemma
5.2
, we will show that a srgc
(b,\alpha,\beta)
which has the SPLS
(\sigma)
property has geometric parameters. In Theorem
5.3
we will show, under weak conditions, that a srgc
(b,\alpha,\beta)
having geometric parameters is in fact geometric. At the end of the section, we will combine everything to prove Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a srgc(
b,\alpha,\beta
) with
\mu\geq 2
. If

\displaystyle\beta>\max\Big\{

\displaystyle\frac{8}{3}(b+1)(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-\frac{2}{3}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-4b\alpha,

(5.0.1)

\displaystyle\frac{8}{3}(b+1)(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-\frac{5}{3}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-b\alpha\Big\},

(5.0.2)

then
\Gamma
has the SPLS(
\lceil\frac{4b+1}{3}\rceil
) property.

Proof:
By definition of the SPLS(
\sigma
) property, we only need to show that the conditions (1) and (2) in Theorem
2.1
are all satisfied for
\sigma=\lceil\frac{4b+1}{3}\rceil
. Clearly,
\sigma=\lceil\frac{4b+1}{3}\rceil\geq 2
as
b\geq 1
. By Lemma
3.1
, we have
\lambda+1=\beta+b\alpha,\mu-1=b\alpha+b+\alpha
and
k=\beta(b+1)
. Therefore, the conditions in Theorem
2.1
can be written as

\displaystyle\beta>\frac{\sigma+1}{2(\sigma-b)}((b\alpha+b+\alpha)\sigma-2b\alpha)\quad

\displaystyle(a)

\displaystyle\beta>(b\alpha+b+\alpha)(2\sigma-1)-b\alpha\quad

\displaystyle(b)

Let
\sigma_{0}:=\frac{4b+1}{3}
be a real number. Then
\sigma_{0}\leq\sigma\leq\sigma_{0}+\frac{2}{3}
, and hence
\frac{\sigma+1}{\sigma-b}\leq\frac{\sigma_{0}+1}{\sigma_{0}-b}=4
.

As
(b\alpha+b+\alpha)\sigma-2b\alpha>0
and
b\alpha+b+\alpha=\mu-1\geq 1
always hold, to show that conditions (a) and (b) hold, it suffices to show the following two inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\beta>2(b\alpha+b+\alpha)(\sigma_{0}+\frac{2}{3})-4b\alpha=\frac{8}{3}(b+1)(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-\frac{2}{3}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-4b\alpha,

\displaystyle\beta>(b\alpha+b+\alpha)(2\sigma_{0}+\frac{4}{3}-1)-b\alpha=\frac{8}{3}(b+1)(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-\frac{5}{3}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-b\alpha.

Therefore, if
\begin{aligned} \beta>\max\Big\{&\frac{8}{3}(b+1)(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-\frac{2}{3}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-4b\alpha,\\
&\frac{8}{3}(b+1)(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-\frac{5}{3}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-b\alpha\Big\},\end{aligned}

then
\Gamma
has the SPLS
(\lceil\frac{4b+1}{3}\rceil)
property by Theorem
2.1
. ∎

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a srgc
(b,\alpha,\beta)
with
b\geq 1
an integer and
\mu=(\alpha+1)(b+1)\geq 2
. If
\Gamma
has the SPLS(
\sigma
) property for some
\sigma
, then
\alpha\text{ and }\beta
are integers with
0\leq\alpha\leq b
. In particular
\Gamma
has geometric parameters.

Proof:
By Lemma
4.3
, there are at least two Delsarte vertices. Therefore, by Lemma
4.5
,
0\leq\alpha\leq b
and
\alpha
is an integer; by Lemma
3.2
,
\beta
is a positive integer. Clearly,
\alpha\leq\beta
(see, (
3.0.2
)). It follows that
\Gamma
has geometric parameters by Lemma
3.3
. ∎

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\Gamma
be a srgc
(b,\alpha,\beta)
with
b\geq 2
an integer and
\mu=(\alpha+1)(b+1)\geq 2
. If
\beta\geq\frac{5}{2}b(b\alpha+b+\alpha)
and
\Gamma
has the SPLS
(\sigma)
property for some
\sigma\leq 2b
, then
\Gamma
is a geometric strongly regular graph.

Proof:
Assume
\Gamma
satisfies the conditions of the theorem, but is not geometric. By Lemma
5.2
we have
0\leq\alpha\leq b
and
\alpha
is an integer. We also have
\mu=(b+1)(\alpha+1)\geq b+1\geq 3
.

We will let
V^{c}_{D}
denote the set of vertices which are not Delsarte vertices; that is,
V^{c}_{D}=V(\Gamma)-V_{D}=\{x\in V(\Gamma)~|~\tau(x)>m=b+1\}
. As
\Gamma
is not geometric,
V^{c}_{D}\neq\phi
. For any vertex
x
of
\Gamma
, let
\tau_{D}(x)
denote the number of Delsarte cliques containing
x
; note that
\tau_{D}(x)\leq\tau(x)
, with equality precisely when
x
is a Delsarte vertex. Fix integer
\gamma
with
0\leq\gamma\leq m-2=b-1
, and set
\delta=m-\gamma=b+1-\gamma
, note that
2\leq\delta\leq b+1
. We now separate the analysis into two cases, the first when there is
x\in V^{c}_{D}
with
\tau_{D}(x)\geq\gamma+1
, and the second when there is no such
x
. We will obtain a bound on
\beta
in both cases, and later will show how to choose
\gamma
in order to optimize this bound.

Case 1:
Suppose there is
x\in V^{c}_{D}
with
\tau_{D}(x)\geq\gamma+1
. Then we will be able to show that

\beta\leq\binom{\delta}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-(\delta-2)\delta\alpha\text{, for $2\leq\delta\leq b+1$}.

To prove this, let
\tau(x)=b+2+\kappa
for some
\kappa\geq 0
. Since
\Gamma
has the SPLS
(\sigma)
property, we must have
\tau(x)\leq\sigma\leq 2b
. Note that
\tau_{D}(x)\geq\gamma+1
implies that there are at least
\gamma+1
Delsarte cliques containing
x
. We fix
\gamma+1
Delsarte cliques containing
x
, and we count the vertices of
\Gamma(x)
which lie outside of the
\gamma+1
Delsarte cliques containing
x
. By the inclusion-exclusion principle, we have

k-\beta(\gamma+1)\geq(\tau(x)-\gamma-1)(\lambda+1-(\gamma+1)\alpha)-\binom{\tau(x)-\gamma-1}{2}(\mu-1).

As
k=\beta(b+1),\lambda=\beta+b\alpha-1,\mu=(b+1)(\alpha+1),\tau(x)-\gamma-1=\delta+\kappa
and
\gamma+1=b+2-\delta
, we have

\displaystyle\beta(b-\gamma)

\displaystyle\geq(\tau(x)-\gamma-1)(\beta+b\alpha-(\gamma+1)\alpha)-\binom{\tau(x)-\gamma-1}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)

\displaystyle=(\delta+\kappa)(\beta-2\alpha+\delta\alpha)-\binom{\delta+\kappa}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)

(5.0.3)

We wish to replace the final expression of (
5
) by
\delta(\beta-2\alpha+\delta\alpha)-\binom{\delta}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)
, and this is justified by the following sequence of equivalent inequalities. Note that clearly it is true when
\kappa=0
, so we may assume that
\kappa>0
.

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (\delta+\kappa)(\beta&-2\alpha+\delta\alpha)-\binom{\delta+\kappa}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)\geq\delta(\beta-2\alpha+\delta\alpha)-\binom{\delta}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)\\
&\iff\kappa(\beta-2\alpha+\delta\alpha)\geq\Big(\binom{\delta+\kappa}{2}-\binom{\delta}{2}\Big)(b\alpha+b+\alpha)\\
&\iff\kappa(\beta-2\alpha+\delta\alpha)\geq\frac{\kappa^{2}+2\delta\kappa-\kappa}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)\\
&\iff\beta-2\alpha+\delta\alpha\geq\frac{\kappa+2\delta-1}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)\\
&\iff\beta\geq(2-\delta)\alpha+\frac{\kappa+2\delta-1}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)\end{aligned}

As
\tau(x)\leq 2b
,
\kappa=\tau(x)-b-2\leq b-2
and
\beta\geq\frac{5}{2}b(b\alpha+b+\alpha)
, the above holds.

In other word, we have

\beta(b-\gamma-\delta)\geq\delta(\delta-2)\alpha-\binom{\delta}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha).

Recall that
\delta=m-\gamma=b+1-\gamma
, and substituting in this final expression for
\delta
on the left yields
-\beta\geq(\delta-2)\delta\alpha-\binom{\delta}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)
. Multiplying by
-1
gives our bound in Case 1.

Case 2:
Suppose
\tau_{D}(x)\leq\gamma
for all
x\in V^{c}_{D}
. Then we will show that

\beta\leq\frac{(b\alpha+b+\alpha)b\alpha\delta+(b+1)(b+\alpha+1)+1}{(b\alpha+b+\alpha)\delta(\delta-1)-(b+1)b}\text{, for $2\leq\delta\leq b+1$}.

(5.0.4)

To see this, note that by Lemma 4.3,
|V^{c}_{D}|\leq\frac{\sigma}{\sigma(\mu-1)+2}|V(\Gamma)|<\frac{1}{\mu-1}|V(\Gamma)|
. We will now establish a lower bound on
|V^{c}_{D}|
. Consider
\Gamma(x)
for any
x\in V^{c}_{D}
. If
y\in\Gamma(x)\setminus V^{c}_{D}
, then
y
is a Delsarte vertex. By our assumption, there are at most
\beta\gamma
such vertices in
\Gamma(x)
. Therefore

|\Gamma(x)\cap V^{c}_{D}|\geq k-\beta\gamma=\beta(b+1-\gamma)=\beta\delta.

Next, we want to count the number of vertices
z\in\Gamma_{2}(x)\cap V^{c}_{D}
. By the same arguement as before, each
y\in\Gamma(x)\cap V^{c}_{D}
must also have at least
\beta\delta
neighbours in
V^{c}_{D}
. Out of these, we must remove
x
and those already in
\Gamma(x)
. There are at most
\lambda+1=\beta+b\alpha
of those.

Finally, for each
z\in\Gamma_{2}(x)\cap V^{c}_{D}
, it is counted at most
\mu
times via elements of
\Gamma(x)
. Thus, a lower bound on
|V^{c}_{D}|
is given by

|V^{c}_{D}|\geq 1+\beta\delta+\frac{\beta\delta(\beta\delta-\beta-b\alpha)}{(b+1)(\alpha+1)}>\frac{\beta\delta(\beta\delta-\beta-b\alpha)}{(b+1)(\alpha+1)}.

Note that
|V(\Gamma)|=1+k+\frac{k(k-\lambda-1)}{\mu}=1+\beta(b+1)+\frac{\beta b(\beta-\alpha)}{\alpha+1}
.

By
|V^{c}_{D}|<\frac{|V(\Gamma)|}{\mu-1}=\frac{|V(\Gamma)|}{b\alpha+b+\alpha}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\beta\delta(\beta\delta-\beta-b\alpha)}{(b+1)(\alpha+1)}<\frac{1}{b\alpha+b+\alpha}\left(\frac{\beta b(\beta-\alpha)}{\alpha+1}+\beta(b+1)+1\right)

\displaystyle\iff\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle\beta\delta(\beta\delta-\beta-b\alpha)(b\alpha+b+\alpha)

\displaystyle<(b+1)b\beta(\beta-\alpha)+(b+1)^{2}(\alpha+1)\beta+(b+1)(\alpha+1)

\displaystyle\overset{\text{divide by }\beta}{\iff}

\displaystyle(b\alpha+b+\alpha)(\delta-1)\delta\beta-(b\alpha+b+\alpha)b\alpha\delta

\displaystyle<(b+1)b\beta-(b+1)b\alpha+(b+1)^{2}(\alpha+1)+\frac{(b+1)(\alpha+1)}{\beta}

\displaystyle\iff\hskip 5.69046pt

\displaystyle((b\alpha+b+\alpha)(\delta-1)\delta-(b+1)b)\beta

\displaystyle<(b\alpha+b+\alpha)b\alpha\delta+(b+1)(\alpha+b+1)+\frac{(b+1)(\alpha+1)}{\beta}

\displaystyle<(b\alpha+b+\alpha)b\alpha\delta+(b+1)(\alpha+b+1)+1.

The final inequality is true because
\beta\geq\frac{5}{2}b(b\alpha+b+\alpha)
and
b\geq 2,\alpha\geq 0
. Dividing through to isolate
\beta
on the left gives the bound (
5.0.4
) we have claimed for Case 2.

It is evident that exactly one of these two cases must hold, so combining the estimates we have

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle\leq\max\mathopen{\Biggl\{}\binom{\delta}{2}(b\alpha+b+\alpha)-(\delta-2)\delta\alpha,

\displaystyle\phantom{\leq\max\mathopen{\Biggl\{}}\frac{(b\alpha+b+\alpha)b\alpha\delta+(b+1)(b+\alpha+1)+1}{(b\alpha+b+\alpha)\delta(\delta-1)-(b+1)b}\Biggr\}

(5.0.5)

Appendix A
Appendix: Applications to orthogonal array

Our methods provide another approach to proving Bruck’s Completion Theorem, as we now explain. An
orthogonal array

\mathrm{OA}(m,n)
is an
m\times n^{2}
array
with entries from an
n
-element set, such that all
n^{2}
ordered pairs formed
by any two rows are distinct. An
\mathrm{OA}(m,n)
is equivalent to a set of

m-2
mutually orthogonal Latin squares.

Given an
\mathrm{OA}(m,n)
, we define the
Latin square graph

\mathrm{LS}_{m}(n)
as follows: the vertices are the
n^{2}
columns of the
orthogonal array, and two vertices are adjacent if they agree in one coordinate
position. This graph is strongly regular with parameters
(v,k,\lambda,\mu)=(n^{2},\,m(n-1),\,(m-1)(m-2)+n-2,\,m(m-1))
, with second largest eigenvalue

n-m
and smallest eigenvalue
-m
. A necessary condition for the existence of an orthogonal array

\mathrm{OA}(m,n)
is that
m\leq n+1
. An
\mathrm{OA}(n+1,n)
is
called a
full orthogonal array
. The
deficiency
of an

\mathrm{OA}(m,n)
is defined as
\delta=n-m+1
, representing the
number of rows missing from a full orthogonal array. The complement of

\mathrm{LS}_{m}(n)
has the same parameters as
\mathrm{LS}_{\delta}(n)
.

We remark that certain references refer to any strongly regular graph with parameters of the form
(n^{2},\,m(n-1),\,(m-1)(m-2)+n-2,\,m(m-1))
as a Latin square graph, regardless of whether or not it corresponds to an orthogonal array. However, we will stick to the definition given above.

The result of Bruck, Theorem
1.1

(i)
(cf.
[
4
]
), implies that if
n>f(\delta)
,
where
f
is a fixed polynomial of degree 4, then the complement is itself a
Latin square graph. In this case, the two graphs can be combined to obtain a full
orthogonal array. Metsch
[
13
, Corollary 2.6]
later improved
Bruck’s bound by providing a polynomial
f
of degree 3.

Following the proof strategy of Bruck and Metsch, and applying Theorems
5.1

and
5.3
, we obtain a bound similar to that of Metsch, namely the following
corollary.

Corollary A.1
.

Let
m
and
n
be positive integers, and define
\delta=n-m+1
. Suppose there
exists an orthogonal array
\mathrm{OA}(m,n)
. If
n>\frac{8}{3}\delta^{3}-\frac{16}{3}\delta^{2}+2\delta+\frac{2}{3}
,
then the orthogonal array can be extended to a full orthogonal array.

Here we provide an outline of the proof; for full details, one may refer to
[
4
]
or
[
13
, Corollary 2.5]
.

Proof (sketch):
If
\delta=1
, the statement is clearly true
[
13
, Corollary 2.5]
, so we
may assume
\delta\geq 2
. Assume we have an orthogonal array

\mathrm{OA}(m,n)
, and let
\delta=n-m+1
. Then we can construct the Latin
square graph
\mathrm{LS}_{m}(n)
. The complement of
\mathrm{LS}_{m}(n)
, which we denote
by
\Gamma
, has the same parameters as the Latin square graph

\mathrm{LS}_{\delta}(n)
, which has smallest eigenvalue
-\delta
, and where
n>\frac{8}{3}\delta^{3}-\frac{16}{3}\delta^{2}+2\delta+\frac{2}{3}
. If
\delta=2
, by
the classification of strongly regular graphs with smallest eigenvalue
-2
(cf.
[
3
, p. 5]
),
\Gamma
is
\mathrm{LS}_{2}(n)
, which is geometric. If
\delta\geq 3
, then
by Theorems
5.1
and
5.3
, we can also show that the graph
\Gamma
is geometric. The
set of all lines of
\Gamma
can be partitioned into
\delta
classes, each forming a
partition of
V(\Gamma)
. For each such partition, we assign the same label to all
vertices lying on the same line. In this way, we naturally obtain a new line of the
orthogonal array. Since there are
\delta
such partitions, we obtain
\delta
new lines,
thereby extending the orthogonal array to a full orthogonal array.
