Mortality attributable to modifiable lifestyle factors in the Spanish cohort of the European prospective investigation into cancer and nutrition (EPIC) study
Authors: Lluís Cirera, José María Huerta, Conchi Moreno-Iribas, Ana Jiménez, Olatz Mokoroa, Marcela Guevara, Naroa Andueza, María José Sánchez, Dafina Petrova, Leila Luján-Barroso, Diego Salmerón, María Dolores Chirlaque

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is consistent evidence on the relationship of unhealthy habits with mortality risk. The population attributable fraction (PAF) is a useful quantifier of the number of new disease cases attributable to modifiable risk factors. Our objective was to obtain the hazard ratios (HRs) and the PAFs for the major individual lifestyle factors of mortality in the Spanish cohort of the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC) study.MethodsProspective study which included 40 307 EPIC-Spain participants (62.5% women). A total of 7 262 cases of all-cause mortality (53.9% were men) occurred during an average follow-up of 25.1 years. We calculated PAFs separately for men and women, using adjusted HRs of mortality (and 95% CI) from multivariate Cox regression models for binary categories of smoking, high alcohol consumption, obesity markers, poor Mediterranean diet adherence, and low physical activity.ResultsMost lifestyle factors studied were significantly associated with mortality in the study cohort. In final multivariate analyses, smoking registered the largest PAFs in men (21.0%, 95%CI 19.1–22.7%), while general obesity (BMI) accounted for the largest PAF in women (10.8%, 7.5–13.8%). Attributable fractions were similar in both sexes for poor adherence to the Mediterranean diet, whereas the lowest PAFs were estimated for physical activity. High alcohol consumption and central obesity did not pose a risk in women.ConclusionsShifting from any risk factor to a healthier lifestyle would reduce mortality in both sexes. Background

There is consistent evidence on the relationship of unhealthy habits on mortality risk [1, 2], mainly for tobacco smoking [3], high alcohol intake [4], poor diet quality [5], low physical activity [6], and obesity [7], as major risk factors for leading chronic diseases [8]. Likewise, relevant papers have shown the independent and complementary effect of central and general adiposity to determine the risk of mortality [7, 9].

Special concerns on unhealthy factors relies in the definition of risk thresholds, ranks, and reference levels. That was the case for alcohol consumption threshold related to adverse health effects [10, 11], even considering abstinence in relation to digestive cancersrisk [12], whereas there seems to be a consistent protective effect on cardiovascular risk of moderate alcohol intake [11, 13–16]. Also, for body mass index categories (BMI) related to all-cause mortality risk [7, 17].. Variability affects the definition of the Mediterranean dietary pattern, hampering interpretability and direct between-study comparisons [18, 19]. The same heterogeneity applies for physical activity exposures in epidemiologic studies [6, 20].

The population attributable fraction (PAF) is a useful quantifier of the number of new disease cases attributable to modifiable risk factors. PAF has a causal interpretation, defined as the fraction of cases that would have not occurred if the exposure had been eliminated [21]. Therefore, important papers have considered to obtain PAFs with premature mortality endpoint in a cohort design [2], and others underlined international comparisons applying PAFs with relevant risk outcomes from literature [22].

Our aim was to quantify the population attributable fractions and the magnitude of association between major lifestyle factors and all-cause mortality in the Spanish cohort of the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition (EPIC) study.

Methods

Study sample

Recruitment procedures and data collection of the EPIC study have been described elsewhere [23, 24]. The EPIC-Spain cohort comprised 41 437 volunteer participants (62% female), mostly aged 30–65 years at the time of enrolment (1992–1996), and were largely blood donors. Community-dwelling adults between 40 and 70 years old residing in the selected areas were eligible to participate with the sole exclusion of pregnant women. Baseline data on diet, lifestyles, and medical and reproductive history were gathered during in-person interviews using a study-specific questionnaire. In addition, participants underwent a physical examination to obtain anthropometric information. The cohort covered five Spanish areas from the North/Atlantic Ocean (Asturias, Guipúzcoa, and Navarre) and the South/Mediterranean Sea (Murcia and Granada) environments [25]. The manuscript has been prepared following the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational prospective studies (https://www.strobe-statement.org).

Identification of all-cause mortality cases

Vital status of the cohort was updated up to 31 December 2020 by record linkage with the cause-of-death database of the Spanish National Statistics Institute. The endpoint status was all-cause natural deaths (all mortality codes of the ICD-10th Revision, except external causes –codes V–Y). Cases for whom the cause of death was unknown (n = 380) were excluded, whereas those who died due to external causes (n = 331) were censored in the analyses at their date of death.

Assessment of anthropometry, diet, lifestyle, social position, and clinical information

Height (in m), weight (in kg), and waist circumference (WC) (in cm) were measured by trained personnel during a physical examination following standard procedures [26]. Body adiposity was considered with two markers as central and general adiposity expressed as waist circumference (WC) and body mass index (BMI). Central obesity was categorized as a WC ≥ 102 cm in men, or ≥ 88 cm in women [27]. BMI was estimated as weight (in kg) divided by squared height (in m) and grouped into WHO categories of low weight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5 to < 25 kg/m2), overweight (25 to < 30 kg/m2), and general obesity (≥ 30 kg/m2).

Detailed information on habitual diet of the previous year was assessed with a validated diet history method, administered face-to-face by trained dietitians [23].Total daily energy (kcal/day) and nutrient intakes were calculated and further harmonized within the EPIC Nutrient Database project [28]. Adherence to the Mediterranean diet was assessed by the adapted relative Mediterranean Diet score (arMED) defined within the EPIC-Spain cohort. As detailed elsewhere [29], the arMED is a scale which assigns 0–2 points to each one of six key dietary components considered to fit the Mediterranean dietary pattern (fruits, vegetables, legumes, fish, olive oil and cereals), based on tertiles of intake (g/2000 kcal). For the two components considered not to fit the traditional MD (meat and dairy products), the scoring is reversed (i.e.2 points were given to intakes in the lowest third, and 0 points to intakes in the upper third of the distribution). Following such definition, the arMED score was computed for the whole cohort by summing the points estimated for each one of the eight items and further categorized into low (0–5 points), medium (6–9 points), and high adherence (10–16 points) (Supplementary Table 1). In order to study the specific effect of alcohol on mortality, alcohol consumption was considered separately from the diet score, and coded as high intake (> 20 g/day in men and > 10 g/day in women), versus lower intake [29].

Questionnaire information was collected at baseline on own education, father’s occupation at childhood, smoking status, and medical and female reproductive history. A hierarchical combination of education and occupation was computed to assess the life-course social position, and grouped into upper, middle and lower strata [30]. Smoking status at baseline was defined as never smoker; former smoker; current smoker (1 or more cigarettes/day), and unknown. Prevalence of chronic diseases was self-reported. Women were asked about ever use of oral contraceptives and hormonal replacement therapy, and reproductive history. Missing values in categorical exposure variables (< 1%) were considered a separate group, whereas participants with missing values in covariates were excluded.

Time spent in physical activities (PA) was assessed at baseline using a validated questionnaire [31]. Sum of household and recreational physical activity was expressed in metabolic equivalents of task (MET) in minutes a week, and divided into < 600 and ≥ 600 MET·min/week categories [32, 33].

Definition of the exposure

Individual factors of exposure were binary defined, taking the healthiest category as the reference. Smoking exposure was current versus never and former smoker. Mediterranean diet adherence was high versus medium or low arMED scores. The recommended alcohol intake was ≤ 20 g/day in men and ≤ 10 g/day in women, versus higher intakes. General obesity was defined as ≥ 30 kg/m2 versus a BMI < 30 kg/m2. Central obesity was defined as waist circumference ≥ 102 cm in men or ≥ 88 cm in women, versus lower circumference values. PA was defined as low (< 600 MET·min/week) versus recommended (≥ 600 MET·min/week).

Ethics

All participants gave their informed consent, and the Ethics Committee of the International Agency for Research on Cancer granted approval to the project. All procedures have been conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistics

We excluded participants with prevalent cardiovascular and cancer diseases (< 2%), and presented descriptive statistics of participants’ baseline characteristics based on means and standard deviations or frequencies, and percentages (as appropriate), for men and women separately.

We estimated hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for all natural causes of death for unhealthy binary exposures (smoking, high alcohol intake, general and central obesity, low-medium adherence to the Mediterranean diet, and low physical activity). We applied proportional hazards Cox regression models, stratified by age (in 5-year categories) and study centre (to account for heterogeneity in methods and population characteristics across centres). Models included all exposure variables, and adjustment for total energy using cubic splines with 5 knots. Besides, women models included oral contraceptives, hormonal replacement therapy, and reproductive history. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using the Schoenfeld residuals. When this assumption was rejected for a covariate, this covariate was considered as a time-dependent covariate in the adjusted model to estimate the adjusted HR for the other variables. For time-dependent covariates, the average HR was considered. Sensitivity analyses were performed excluding dead participants with less than 2 years of follow-up, or including, one by one, participants with diabetes, hypertension, or hyperlipidaemia, or any of all three prevalent chronic diseases. A sensitivity analysis was also performed showing HR of mortality by additional strata of lifestyle exposures (smoking status, adherence to the Mediterranean diet, general obesity and physical activity) overall, and by age group (< 50, 50–59, and ≥ 60 years). Finally, we stratified for the potential effect modification of high versus middle-lower social position on the lifestyle factors by sex.

We calculated the population attributable fractions (PAF) for each of the risk factors applying the exposure prevalence among death cases in the cohort to the adjusted HR, as the estimator of the relative risk (RR) using the Miettinen’s formula [34]:

The 95% CI of PAF were calculated applying the respective extremes of 95% CI of HR.

Analyses were performed with Stata/SE v.14.2 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). P-values of < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 40 307 participants (62.5% women) were followed up for a mean of 25.1 years (standard deviation: ± 4.3 y) and 1 009 104 person-years, resulting in 7262 cases of all-cause natural deaths (3 912 cases in men [25.9%) and 3 350 cases in women (13.3%]). At baseline, both sexes had similar height, weight, total energy intake, Mediterranean diet adherence, higher sum of household and recreational physical activities, social position, and showed higher prevalences of diabetes and hypertension. Men were elder, and more likely to be overweight and hyperlipidaemic, whereas women presented more obesity and had a lower alcohol intake. There were half as many active smokers at baseline among women than among men (18.9% versus 40,5%). Baseline participants’ characteristics are described in Table 1.

After a mutual adjustment for all factors, ‘current smokers’ versus ‘former’/‘non-smokers’ showed one of the biggest HRs, and much similar magnitude and significance in both sexes (HR, 95%CI: 1.76, 1.65—1.88 in men; and 1.74, 1.55—1.94 in women). Risks were also very close for ‘low’/‘medium’ adherence to the Mediterranean diet versus ‘high adherence’ in men and women (HRs: 1.14 versus 1.12, respectively). Less physical activity was associated with mortality, especially in women (1.72, 1.16—2.56). General obesity showed a significant risk among women (1.30, 1.19—1.42) and men (1.13, 1.04—1.22). Central obesity (HR = 1.17, 1.08 −1.27) and higher alcohol intake (HR = 1.13, 1.06 −1.22) were significantly positively associated with mortality only among men (Table 2). The association of waist circumference in women was borderline but not statistically significant (HR = 1.09, 0.99—1.19; p = 0.07).

Smoking showed the largest proportion of attributable deaths in men (PAF = 21.0%, 95%CI: 19.1%—22.7%), while general obesity accounted for the largest fraction among women (10.8%, 7.5%—13.8%) (Table 3). Medium–low adherence to the Mediterranean diet registered a similar impact on mortality in men and women (8.7%, 3.9—13.1%, and 7.8%, 2.6—12.6%, respectively). General obesity contributed significantly to mortality risk in both sexes (10.8% in women and 4.1% in men), but it was particularly relevant among women due to its higher prevalence (47.0%) and HR estimate. Central obesity accounted for 7.0% of natural deaths among men, but it was not significantly associated with death in women. The HR estimate for alcohol consumption did not reach statistical significance in women. Finally, PA had the lowest PAFs significance in both sexes (1.7% in men and 0.3% in women).

Sensitivity analyses excluding the first 2 years of follow-up (Supplementary Table 2), or the stepwise exclusion of prevalent chronic diseases (Supplementary Table 3), endorsed the robustness of the results, showing almost no differences in the estimates magnitude or statistical significance. Results were not modified either by strata of social position, except for general obesity in men (Supplementary Table 4). When using an alternative categorisation of lifestyle exposures with additional levels and stratifying the analyses by age group, results remained similar with few exceptions (Supplementary Tables 6, 7, 8 and 9). Noteworthy, smoking status did not statistical significance among women who were former smokers, more specifically for those being 50 years or older at recruitment. Also, a ‘high’ score for Mediterranean diet adherence presented a significantly lower risk of mortality as compared to medium or low scores both in men and women. For general obesity, an effect for a BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2 was observed for women 50 years or older (HR = 1.33 in the 50–59 year group, and 1.34 for an age ≥ 60 years). The significance of mortality risk associated with central obesity in men was more remarkable among age groups over 50 years old. Waist circumference remained non-significant in women after stratifying by age categorisation (Supplementary Tables 7—9). On the contrary, alcohol drinking was associated with an increased risk of mortality among younger women (< 50 years; HR, 95%CI: 1.29, 1.06—1.56).

Discussion

In this study, we have focused on the prospective relationship between major individual lifestyle factors and mortality risk. We have showed that unhealthy lifestyle behaviours in adulthood were positively associated with all-cause mortality. The unhealthy lifestyle factors of smoking, lower adherence to the Mediterranean diet, high alcohol intake, general and central obesity, and low household and recreational physical activity all contributed independently to mortality risk in both sexes, with the exception of alcohol consumption and central obesity in women. Smoking accounted for 21% of death cases among men, followed by a low or medium Mediterranean diet score (8.5% of deaths), and central obesity (7.2% of death cases), whereas in women, obesity, as defined by an excess BMI, accounted for the largest proportion of avoidable deaths (11%), followed by a poorer diet (7.8% of preventable deaths).

Indicators and categories of modifiable lifestyle factors

Related to smoking data, as the exposures were only assessed at baseline, it is possible that some former smokers had resumed smoking after recruitment, leading to underestimate the benefits related to smoking cessation due to exposure misclassification in the reference group. Similarly, some current smokers could have quit smoking during the follow-up time, which would have led to underestimation of hazards related to continuing smoking. Moreover, some former smokers might have quit smoking because of ill health; thus, they could have had higher mortality than those who continued smoking, although we did not find evidence of reverse causation after excluding deaths within the first 2 years of follow-up. Similarity, it is likely that some current smokers had quit smoking during the follow-up, which would have led to underestimate hazards related to continuing smoking. Evaluation of the smoking impact of and cessation among older adults is challenging, as the magnitude of general mortality increases in the elderly [3]. As previously shown, the hazardous effects of smoking and benefits of cessation persist even in older ages [35].

Literature have generally reached a consensus for a limit of recommended or tolerable alcohol intake of up to 1 unit a day for women and up to 2 units a day for men [36], which would be roughly equivalent to < 6 g/day of ethanol for women and < 12 g/d for men. We have maintained these recommended limits in accordance with the WHO strategy [37]. Our results underlined a potential mortality reduction, solely significant among men, probably due to their higher prevalence of consumption at baseline. However, in the periodical Spanish Health Surveys, the alcohol consumption among women, has stayed stable between 8 to 9 g/d within the past decade [38]. The positive and non-statistically significant HR in women was possibly affected by underreporting of high alcohol consumption (0.6% in ≥ 60 g/d) and a general low prevalence of consumption that could lower statistical power to detect a significant risk. Nevertheless, the finding of a significant association for alcohol drinking (> 1 unit/day of alcohol) among younger women in our study (Supplementary Table 8) reinforces the public health approach to keep alcohol consumption below risk threshold levels.

We included both general and central body measures for obesity, as proxy markers of adiposity, due to their independent effect shown on mortality [17]. Related to the central adiposity markers, we have selected WC, as it is considered easier to measure and interpret than the waist-to-hip ratio in clinical practice [39]; although without success, partly due to the high proportion of larger WC in women (Supplementary Graphs 1), and the BMI and WC correlation and covariance (Supplementary Graphs 2 and 3) to account for adiposity in a mutually adjusted statistical modelling. Reanalysis of the mutually adjusted model excluding BMI in women showed an independent a significant risk for WC (HR 1.26, SE 0.05, p < 0.0005, 95%CI 1.16—1.36; data not shown).

Since the arMED score was computed using study-specific tertiles of dietary intake in the EPIC-Spain cohort, it is thus a relative index. We have therefore described the main components of the Mediterranean diet in the Spanish cohort in Supplementary Table 1, to allow for external comparisons.

Our physical activity recommendation was aligned to the WHO recommendation of 150–300 min of moderate activity (ranging from 3 to 6 MET), equivalent to a minimum of 450–500 MET·min/week. Our 600 MET·min/week limit was in-between the Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans [40] and WHO [33] recommendations of 500–1000 MET·min/week, and was selected for greater comparability with the European/Spanish National Health Surveys [38]. Despite only a small fraction of participants reported physical activity levels below the cut-off of 600 MET·h/week, an extra 2.6% of deaths among men and 0.5% deaths among women could have been prevented by adopting a more active lifestyle.

Cornerstones of the estimation

Before performing an attributable mortality analysis, it is necessary to reflect critically on the three cornerstones of the estimation: observed mortality, exposure prevalence, and associated risk. It is essential to keep in mind the impact of time, such as age, latency of causes of death, or duration of exposure, and the relationship between exposure and health outcomes. If prevalence diminishes in the population, attributable mortality would be underestimated and vice versa [41]. Regarding to risk aspect, the use of more recent and nationally representative relative risks tended to decrease estimates of deaths attributable to smoking for men and to increase PAF estimates for women in the USA [42] and in specific cohorts of France, Italy, and Spain [10].

Attributable fractions for unhealthy factors in neighbouring countries of Western Europe

For international assessments, we opted to compare unhealthy PAF factors with neighbouring Western European countries. Most of the relevant papers were published and based on the Global Burden Disease (GBD) study, from which data were obtained with different sources for prevalence exposure and relative risk estimations.

For tobacco smoking regarding all-cause mortality according to GBD with nationally representative survey exposure data for a similar period (1990–2019) and own PAF calculation, PAF by women and men, reached 7.8% and 18.9% in France, 8.2% and 20.5% in Italy, and 25.4% and 5.6% in Spain, respectively [43].

Regarding alcohol intake, the general population of France have established a lower PAF (using Levin’s approach) for all-cause mortality of 3% in men and 2% in women, with an higher average alcohol intake of 28 g/day and 13 g/day, respectively [44]. Another alcohol study taking abstainer prevalence as reference with alcohol-related mortality conditions in four European countries (not including Spain), has shown an 2.6% mortality increase in men and a 2.0% increase in women, with inconsistencies to WHO consumption data [45]. All-cause mortality for alcohol intake versus abstention in 2016, using GBD data showed a (Levin’s) PAF of 5.6% for men and 1.8% for women, in Spain; 8.0% for men and 2.9% for women, in France; and 4.2% for men, and a non-significant 1.8% for women, in Italy [46]. In our study, the non-significant HR in women for higher versus lower alcohol intake of all-cause mortality was aligned to a recent meta-analysis, where fully adjusted RR for occasional drinking (< 1.3 g/day) or low-medium alcohol volumes (1.3 to < 25 to < 45 g/day) did not achieve risk levels versus lifetime non-drinkers [4].

Overweight and obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) for all-cause mortality in participants aged 20 or more in 2017 have shown PAF of 7.5% in France; of 9.9% in Italy; and 9.8% in Spain [47]. A similar positive association was detected in a more recent meta-analysis on central obesity, which have stated that WC and other central indices were positively and significantly associated with a higher all-cause mortality risk [9].

Concerning PA, a previous study showed an all-cause mortality reduction of 6.6% (95% CI 2.2–10.7) among active participants from the EPIC-Spain cohort for both sexes, with a different methodological adjustment [48].

It would be of interest to apply the estimated HRs for unhealthy factors to updated prevalence data from the most recent National Health Survey in Spain to obtain PAFs with improved external validity in the Spanish population.

Repeated lifestyle measurement in large scale cohorts

A Chinese study measured 3 times in 6 years assessed lifestyle trajectories by a composite score of risk factors. Changing lifestyle to a healthy trajectory showed a reduced risk of cardiovascular disease only when BMI decreased, and reduced all-cause mortality risk only when BMI was time-stable [49]. USA long-term study of 3 grouping of individual risk factors and 3 trajectories were related to all-cause mortality and longevity, has concluded that participants who have met public health recommendations lived longer [50]. In the ongoing EPIC-Europe study, two investigations have addressed the association of changes in lifestyles at baseline and follow-up with chronic disease and mortality by a summary score of classic risk factors, as components of a less and more healthy behaviours in a Healthy Lifestyle Index (HLI). One previous EPIC study showed an inverse relationship of the HLI with all-cause mortality after giving equal weight to each component [10]. The one other study contrasted giving equal weights to each component (the “total” HLI) to a disease-specific weighed HLI, and found that the total HLI had a lower discriminatory power [51]. In the light of our results, changes in lifestyles in the general population may effectively reduce a large proportion of premature mortality in adult and elderly subjects.

This study has several strengths, as this is a large observational analytical study using longitudinal data in a prospective design, which use all-cause death as outcome to reduce the potential of outcome misclassification bias. Furthermore, a wide set of variables was available for analysis, such as information on tobacco smoking, detailed alcohol intake, Mediterranean diet, a validated physical activity index, measured anthropometric data, and informed chronic diseases, as well as reproductive status and hormonal drug therapy in women. However, the study also has some limitations. As in most longitudinal observational studies, data on potential confounders were only available at baseline, so it was not possible to control for changes of exposures at different times during follow-up. As this is an historic cohort set up in the early 1990 s, that participants could have modified their habits throughout follow-up, towards less alcohol, less smoking, and more physical activity, particularly after retirement. In addition, it is likely that healthy participants could have maintained their weight within a narrower range, whereas those who were affected by diseases could have undergone weight loss. We studied all-cause mortality rather than cause-specific mortality, but this would assure a more accurate and reliable estimate of total effect. The literature shows heterogeneous and conflicting results when studying major or specific causes of death, as some papers have established negative relationships with cardiovascular mortality, while others found positive associations with cancer mortality using specific and standard categories of alcohol intake and BMI [8, 12, 17].

Conclusions

The population attributable fraction is a useful measure for assessing the impact of interventions on disease risk. Shifting from any risk factor to a healthier lifestyle would reduce mortality in both sexes in the EPIC-Spain cohort, except for non-moderate alcohol consumption among women. Furthermore, in women only BMI (not central obesity) accounted for mortality risk associated with excess body adiposity.
