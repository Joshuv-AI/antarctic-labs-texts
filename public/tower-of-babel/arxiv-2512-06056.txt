Diophantine Analysis of a Digital Anomaly
Samer Seraj

Abstract
The arithmetic-digital anomaly of 5 ÷ 2 = 2.5 5\div 2=2.5 has been observed several times in the past. We generalize it to an exponential Diophantine equation and inequality in the general number base, which is the object of our analysis. First, we produce a near-parametrization of all solutions using a modification of the standard parametrization of Pythagorean triples. We use this parametrized function to find all solutions where the numerator and denominator are coprime, and we construct infinite families where they are not coprime. Next, we use a variant of Baker’s theorem from transcendental number theory to prove that each number base admits only finitely many solutions. Lastly, we use the a ​ b ​ c abc conjecture to conditionally show that only finitely many solutions have a numerator with k k digits, for each k ≥ 3 k\geq 3 . A conjecture is offered for k = 2 k=2 .

Observe the arithmetic-digital property of the computation,

\frac{5}{2}=2.5,

where the numerator is the fractional part and the denominator is the integer part of the common value. This curiosity raises questions about which numerator-denominator pairs satisfy the same property, and in which number bases. Formally, we are looking at the exponential Diophantine equation (

The problem has been studied previously outside the research setting. For example, a search on the website,

Definition 1.1
.

The positive integers
(x,y,B,k)
form a

if

\frac{x}{y}=y+\frac{x}{B^{k}},

(1.1)

where

, and

B^{k-1}\leq x<B^{k}.

(1.2)

Example 1.2
.

The original motivating example satisfies

\frac{5}{2}=2+\frac{1}{2}=2+\frac{5}{10^{1}},\;10^{0}\leq 5<10^{1}.

To gain perspective, we see that some immediate consequences of Definition
1.1
are, by contradiction:

•

x>y
, because otherwise
x\leq y
would lead to
\frac{x}{y}\leq 1<y+\frac{x}{B^{k}}
.

•

, because otherwise

would lead to the left hand side of (

•

, because otherwise

Along the way, our tools will include a modification of the standard parametrization of Pythagorean triples, a variant of Baker’s theorem from transcendental number theory, and the

Parametrizing Digital Anomalies

Lemma 2.1
.

The quadruple of positive integers
(x,y,B,k)
satisfies (
1.1
) if and only if

(x+2B^{k}y)^{2}+(2B^{2k})^{2}=(x+2B^{2k})^{2}.

(2.1)

Proof.

An expansion of (
2.1
) quickly shows that it is equivalent to (
1.1
). Instead, we display how to devise (
2.1
) from (
1.1
) in the first place. The idea is to begin by using the quadratic formula to isolate

\displaystyle\frac{x}{y}=y+\frac{x}{B^{k}}

\displaystyle\iff B^{k}x=B^{k}y^{2}+xy

\displaystyle\iff B^{k}y^{2}+xy-B^{k}x=0

\displaystyle\iff y=\frac{-x\pm\sqrt{x^{2}+4B^{2k}x}}{2B^{k}}

\displaystyle\iff(x+2B^{k}y)^{2}=x^{2}+4B^{2k}x

\displaystyle\iff(x+2B^{k}y)^{2}+(2B^{2k})^{2}=x^{2}+4B^{2k}x+4B^{4k}

\displaystyle\iff(x+2B^{k}y)^{2}+(2B^{2k})^{2}=(x+2B^{2k})^{2}.

∎

A

\displaystyle\ell(2mn)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell_{0}}{2}\left[2(m_{0}+n_{0})(m_{0}-n_{0})\right]=\ell(m_{0}^{2}-n_{0}^{2})=b,

\displaystyle\ell(m^{2}+n^{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell_{0}}{2}\left[(m_{0}+n_{0})^{2}+(m_{0}-n_{0})^{2}\right]=\ell_{0}(m_{0}^{2}+n_{0}^{2})=c.

We make some comments about Theorem

•

It is necessary that
tn
is a square.

•

Since the described set of
(t,m,n)
are in bijection with the set of
(x,y,B^{k})
over all digital anomalies
(x,y,B,k)
, via the correspondence in (

•

The result parametrizes

, but it does not necessarily uniquely determine

Corollary 2.8
.

For any digital anomaly
(x,y,B,k)
,

B^{k}<\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}\leq B^{k+1}.

(2.12)

Consequently,

k=\left\lceil\log_{B}\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}\right\rceil-1.

Proof.

While figuring out the double inequality in (
2.12
) was the result of experimentation with manipulations that cannot be recounted here, it can be proven quickly in a backwards manner, since the result has already been articulated:

\displaystyle B^{k}<\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}

\displaystyle\iff B^{k}(m-n)^{2}<mn

\displaystyle\iff m\sqrt{tn}(m-n)^{2}<mn

\displaystyle\iff t(m-n)^{4}<n,

which we know from (
2.8
). Secondly,

\displaystyle\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}\leq B^{k+1}

\displaystyle\iff mn\leq B^{k+1}(m-n)^{2}

\displaystyle\iff m^{2k}n^{2k}\leq[(B^{k})^{2}]^{k+1}(m-n)^{4k}

\displaystyle\iff m^{2k}n^{2k}\leq[(m\sqrt{tn})^{2}]^{k+1}(m-n)^{4k}

\displaystyle\iff m^{2k}n^{2k}\leq m^{2k+2}t^{k+1}n^{k+1}(m-n)^{2k}

\displaystyle\iff n^{k-1}\leq m^{2}t^{k+1}(m-n)^{4k},

which is (
2.9
). For the consequence,
[
2
, p. 69]
tells us that

\lceil\alpha\rceil=\beta\iff\beta-1<\alpha\leq\beta,

for real numbers
\alpha,\beta
, so

\displaystyle B^{k}<\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}\leq B^{k+1}

\displaystyle\iff k<\log_{B}\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}\leq k+1

\displaystyle\iff\left\lceil\log_{B}\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}\right\rceil=k+1.

This is equivalent to what we sought.
∎

Corollary

is not immediately useful to us, since

is not parametrized in terms of

and

. However, it allows for the formulation of a heuristic argument for why digital anomalies with larger

are increasingly rarer as

The heuristic argument involves considering how

might be equal to a different power
C^{j}
, and how that affects the bounds in (

). First, we try to make

less than
k
. For a given triple
(t,m,n)
to produce a digital anomaly
(x,y,B,k)
, it is necessary that

is a perfect

power and that (

) holds. Then, for any positive integers
C
and

such that
j<k
and
C^{j}=B^{k}
, since

j<k\iff k+1<k\left(1+\frac{1}{j}\right),

we find that

C^{j}=B^{k}<\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}\leq B^{k+1}<(B^{k})^{1+\frac{1}{j}}=(B^{k})^{\frac{j+1}{j}}=(B^{\frac{k}{j}})^{j+1}=C^{j+1}.

So, every positive integer
j
such that
B^{k}=C^{j}
and
j<k
works, which means we can always go to the bottom by letting the exponent be
j=1
.

However, going from
k=1
to higher exponents

shows that
\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}
gets squeezed into a very tight interval, as follows. Suppose

B^{1}<\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}\leq B^{2}

and that

also works. Then

B^{1}=C^{j}<\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}\leq C^{j+1}=(B^{\frac{1}{j}})^{j+1}=B^{1+\frac{1}{j}}\leq B^{2},

where we see that the upper bound of

is closer to

than the original

. As

increases, the interval in which

has to lie becomes narrower, from

to
\left(1,1+\frac{1}{j}\right]
.

As a side note, we remark that the highest exponent

for some integer

, is the greatest common divisor

of the multiplicities of the prime power factors of

. Due to the fact that the common divisors of a set of integers are precisely the factors of their greatest common divisor, we know that

is a perfect

power for some positive integer

if and only if
j
divides
g
. However, there is no guarantee that

is the highest exponent that will satisfy the inequalities (
2.12
).

Infinite Families

In this section, we look at families of digital anomalies

\gcd(x,y)=1

Theorem 3.1

The set of all digital anomalies
(x,y,B,k)
, such that

Suppose

n
must be a square, so we let
y^{2}=n=s^{2}
for some integer
s=y\geq 2
, which turns (
3.2
) into

(x,y,B^{k})=(s^{2}+1,s,(s^{2}+1)s).

(3.3)

We can see that (
3.3
) satisfies (
1.1
) by checking,

\frac{x}{y}=\frac{s^{2}+1}{s}=s+\frac{1}{s}=s+\frac{s^{2}+1}{(s^{2}+1)s}=y+\frac{x}{B^{k}}.

To prove the inequality (
1.2
), we find
k
first. Since
B^{k}=s(s^{2}+1)
and
\gcd(s,s^{2}+1)
are coprime, we find that
s
and
s^{2}+1
must both be perfect
k^{\text{th}}
powers. Then,
s^{2}
and
s^{2}+1
are consecutive positive integers that are both perfect
k^{\text{th}}
powers, which is impossible for
k\geq 2
due to the widening gap. So,
k=1
, and we prove (
1.2
),

B^{k-1}=B^{0}=1\leq x=s^{2}+1<(s^{2}+1)s=B^{k}.

Therefore, the parametrization is complete.
∎

Example 3.2
.

The first two examples with
\gcd(x,y)=1
are:

•

If
s=2
is substituted into (
3.1
), then
(x,y,B,k)=(5,2,10,1)
. So, the original observation that
\frac{5}{2}=2.5
in base
10
is the first example with coprime
x
and
y
.

•

If
s=3
, then
(x,y,B,k)=(10,3,30,1)
. We can write
10_{10}=a_{30}
, since
9<10<30
means that
10
is a digit in base
30
but not in base
10
. Then, the computation works out as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{a}{3}\right)_{30}=\left(\frac{10}{3}\right)_{10}=3_{10}+\left(\frac{1}{3}\right)_{10}

\displaystyle=3_{10}+\left(\frac{10}{30}\right)_{10}

\displaystyle=3_{30}+\left(\frac{a}{10}\right)_{30}=(3.a)_{30}.

Although it is challenging to find
all
digital anomalies
(x,y,B,k)
where
\gcd(x,y)
has a fixed value
d>1
, we can parametrize an infinite family of such solutions in each case.

Theorem 3.3
.

For each integer
d>1
, an infinite family of digital anomalies
(x,y,B,k)
such that
\gcd(x,y)=d
is given by

(x,y,B,k)=((s^{2}+d)d^{2},sd,(s^{2}+d)s,1),

(3.4)

where
s
is any positive integer that is coprime to
d
and
s\geq d^{2}+1
.

Proof.

It is not difficult to verify that this family works, which we will do at the end of the proof. First, we will show how to construct it. Suppose
(x,y,B,k)
is a digital anomaly. Using the parametrization (
2.7
) from Theorem
2.7
,

\displaystyle\gcd(x,y)

\displaystyle=\gcd(tm(m-n)^{2},\sqrt{tn}(m-n))

\displaystyle=(m-n)\cdot\gcd(tm(m-n),\sqrt{tn}).

To get
\gcd(x,y)
to get closer to becoming
d
, we take
t=1
and
m-n=d
because it forces

(m-n)\cdot\gcd(tm(m-n),\sqrt{tn})=d\cdot\gcd(md,\sqrt{n})=d\cdot\gcd(d,\sqrt{n}),

due to
m
and
n
being coprime.
Now it suffices to let
\sqrt{n}=s
be an integer that is coprime to
d
, so that

\gcd(x,y)=d\cdot\gcd(d,\sqrt{n})=d\cdot\gcd(d,s)=d.

So, using
t=1
,
m=n+d
, and
n=s^{2}
, (
2.7
) becomes

\displaystyle(x,y,B^{k})

\displaystyle=(tm(m-n)^{2},\sqrt{tn}(m-n),m\sqrt{tn})

\displaystyle=((s^{2}+d)d^{2},sd,(s^{2}+d)s),

which is (
3.4
) by taking
k=1
.

We can verify that (
1.1
) holds here,

\frac{x}{y}=\frac{(s^{2}+d)d^{2}}{sd}=sd+\frac{d^{2}}{s}=\frac{(s^{2}+d)d^{2}}{(s^{2}+d)s}=y+\frac{x}{B^{k}}.

Since we chose
k=1
, this makes the inequality constraint in (
2.8
) into

t(m-n)^{4}<n\iff d^{4}<s^{2}\iff d^{2}<s\iff d^{2}+1\leq s,

which is simply a lower bound on
s
in terms of the fixed
d
. Lastly, we note that (
2.9
) is trivially true when
k=1
is substituted into it.
∎

Example 3.4
.

The smallest case of the families described in Theorem

substitutes

and
s=2^{2}+1=5
into (

(x,y,B,k)=((s^{2}+d)d^{2},sd,(s^{2}+d)s,1)=(108,10,135,1).

We can check, using base

4
Finite Classes

Our final area of exploration will be to look at the set of digital anomalies for a fixed base
B
or a fixed exponent
k
.

For analyzing fixed bases, the following is a significant lemma that is taken from Alan Baker’s famous work in transcendental number theory.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{u}
be algebraic numbers, not
0
or
1
, for some positive integer
u
. Let
\log\alpha_{1},\ldots,\log\alpha_{u}
denote the principal value of each logarithm. Let
K
be the field generated by
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{u}
over the rationals
\mathbb{Q}
and let
d
be the degree of
K
. For each
\alpha_{i}
, let
A_{i}\geq e
be an upper bound on the absolute values of the relatively prime integer coefficients of the minimal polynomial of
\alpha_{i}
, where
e
is Euler’s constant. Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{u}
be integers, not all
0
, and let
C\geq e
be an upper bound on
\{|c_{1}|,\ldots,|c_{u}|\}
. Denoting

\Lambda=c_{1}\log\alpha_{1}+\cdots+c_{u}\log\alpha_{u},

it holds that

\log|\Lambda|>-16(ud)^{2(u+2)}(\log A_{1}\cdots\log A_{u})\log C,

(4.1)

which consists of natural logarithms of positive real numbers.
[
1
, p. 20]

As a direct consequence, we obtain the following special case for our purposes.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
p_{1},\ldots,p_{u}
be distinct primes. Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{u}
be integers, not all
0
, and let
C\geq e
be an upper bound on the set
\{|c_{1}|,\ldots,|c_{u}|\}
. Denoting

\Lambda=c_{1}\log p_{1}+\cdots+c_{u}\log p_{u}=\log(p_{1}^{c_{1}}\cdots p_{u}^{c_{u}}),

where all logarithms are real and in the natural base, it holds that

\log|\Lambda|>-2(16u)^{2(u+2)}(\log p_{1}\cdots\log p_{u})\log C.

(4.2)

For future reference, we will use
D
to denote the positive constant

D=2(16u)^{2(u+2)}(\log p_{1}\cdots\log p_{u}),

which depends only on the
p_{i}
and
u
(the number of
p_{i}
).

Proof.

In Lemma
4.1
, we take each
\alpha_{i}
to be
p_{i}
, which has the minimal polynomial
x-p_{i}
, so
A_{i}=p_{i}
is acceptable in all but the case of the prime
2
. If the prime
p_{i}=2
appears, then
A_{i}=p_{i}=2<e
, whereas we are required to have
A_{i}\geq e
. To get a consistent bound, we always choose the
A_{i}
corresponding to the smallest appearing prime
p_{i}
to be
A_{i}=p_{i}^{2}
, which is always greater than both
p_{i}
and
e
. Then,

\log A_{i}=\log(p_{i}^{2})=2\log p_{i},

which explains our extra factor of
2
in
D
in (
4.2
), compared to the expression for the lower bound in (
4.1
) in Lemma
4.1
,

The degree of
K
is just
d=1
, since the primes
p_{i}
are all in
\mathbb{Q}
.

Lastly, we can let
C=\max\{e,|c_{1}|,\ldots,|c_{u}|\}
, where
e
is included among the elements, just in case all of the integers
c_{i}
are too small by all being in the interval
[-2,2]
, since we require
C\geq e
.
∎

A variation of this special case of Lemma
4.1
is mentioned in
[
3
, p. 1229]
, but that article appears to have missed the necessity of our extra factor of
2
in
D
.

Theorem 4.3
.

For each base
B\geq 2
there exist finitely many digital anomalies
(x,y,B,k)
.

Proof.

Suppose
B\geq 2
is a fixed integer, and that
(x,y,B,k)
is a digital anomaly. By Theorem
2.7
, there exist positive integers
(t,m,n)
in terms of which
x,y
can be written and

B^{k}=m\sqrt{tn}\implies B^{2k}=m^{2}nt.

So, the prime factors of
m
and
n
are all from among the prime factors of
B
. Moreover, it is known that
m,n
are coprime, so the set of prime factors of
m
are disjoint from the set of prime factors of
n
. Let the prime factorizations of
B,m,
and
n
be
r_{1}^{c_{1}}\cdots r_{u}^{c_{u}}
,
p_{1}^{a_{1}}\cdots p_{v}^{a_{v}}
, and
q_{1}^{b_{1}}\cdots q_{w}^{b^{w}}
, respectively, where the primes in each individual prime factorization are distinct, and the multiplicities are all non-zero.

The general hope is to put a bound above each of
t,m,n
in terms of a constant depending only on
B
, regardless of
k
, so that there are only finitely many possibilities remaining. From (
2.8
) and
m>n
, we know that

\displaystyle t\leq t(m-n)^{4}<n

\displaystyle\implies t<n,

(4.3)

\displaystyle(m-n)^{4}\leq t(m-n)^{4}<n

\displaystyle\implies m<n^{\frac{1}{4}}+n\leq 2n.

(4.4)

Combining these yields the chain

t<n<m<2n.

This means that it suffices to bound
m
or
n
in terms of a constant depending only on
B
because, then, the same will follow for the other two parameters from the triple
(t,m,n)
. This strategy is achievable using Lemma
4.2
, as it will be shown.

To get an expression
\Lambda
that is a linear combination of logarithms, with integer coefficients, we consider

\log\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)=\log m-\log n=\sum_{i=1}^{v}{a_{i}\log p_{i}}+\sum_{j=1}^{w}{(-b_{j})\log q_{i}},

(4.5)

which is desirable because it has the multiplicities of the primes factor of
m
and
n
as the coefficients of the logarithms, and it suffices to bound them. Since
m>n
, the expression in (
4.5
), which we will call
\Lambda
, is positive. Using the well-known fact that
\log(x+1)<x
for all
x>0
, we get

|\Lambda|=\Lambda=\log\left(\frac{m}{n}\right)=\log\left(1+\frac{m-n}{n}\right)<\frac{m-n}{n}.

Then, (
4.4
) tells us that

m-n<n^{\frac{1}{4}}\implies\frac{m-n}{n}<n^{-\frac{3}{4}}.

Therefore, we have the elementary upper bound

\log|\Lambda|<\log\frac{m-n}{n}<\log(n^{-\frac{3}{4}})=-\frac{3}{4}\log n.

On the other hand, Lemma
4.2
gives the lower bound

\log|\Lambda|>-D\log C,

where

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle=2(16(v+w))^{2(v+w+2)}(\log p_{1}\cdots\log p_{v}\cdot\log q_{1}\cdots\log q_{w}),

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\max\{e,a_{1},\ldots,a_{v},b_{1}\ldots,b_{w}\}.

In order to make
D
depend purely on
B
, we replace it with the larger number,

D_{B}=2(16u)^{2(u+2)}(\log r_{1}\cdots\log r_{u}),

which is acceptable since
u\geq v+w
, and
\{p_{1},\ldots,p_{v}\}
and
\{q_{1},\ldots,q_{w}\}
are disjoint subsets of the set of prime factors
\{r_{1},\ldots,r_{u}\}
of
B
. Then,

\log|\Lambda|>-D\log C>-D_{B}\log C.

Combining the upper and lower bound for
\log|\Lambda|
creates our fundamental squeeze:

-D_{B}\log C<\log|\Lambda|<-\frac{3}{4}\log n\implies\log n<\frac{4}{3}D_{B}\log C.

(4.6)

While this is an upper bound on
n
,
C
still depends on the
a_{i}
and the
b_{j}
, which are not yet bounded. There are three (possibly overlapping) cases of
C
: it is
e
or it is
\max\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{w}\}
or it is
\max\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{v}\}
. These are handled as follows:

1.

If
C=e
, then (
4.6
) implies

\log n<\frac{4}{3}D_{B}\log e=\frac{4}{3}D_{B}\implies n=e^{\log n}<e^{\frac{4}{3}D_{B}},

which is an upper bound on
n
, in terms of a constant that depends only on
B
.

2.

Suppose
C=\max\{b_{1},\ldots,b_{w}\}=b_{j}
for some index
j
. Letting
p
be the smallest prime factor of
B
, we get

n\geq q_{j}^{b_{j}}\geq p^{b_{j}}=p^{C},

which leads to

C\log p=\log(p^{C})\leq\log n<\frac{4}{3}D_{B}\log C\implies\frac{C}{\log C}<\frac{4D_{B}}{3\log p}.

This proves that
\frac{C}{\log C}
is bounded above in terms of a constant, assuming
B
is fixed. Recall that, over real
z
,

\frac{d}{dz}\frac{z}{\log z}=\frac{\log z-1}{(\log z)^{2}}

is positive for all real
z>e
, so
\frac{z}{\log z}
is strictly increasing there. We know that
C
is an integer above
e
in this case, which implies the existence of an upper bound
W_{B}
for
C
, using only
B
. Finally, (
4.6
) produces

n=e^{\log n}<e^{\frac{4}{3}D_{B}\log C}=C^{\frac{4}{3}D_{B}}\leq W_{B}^{\frac{4}{3}D_{B}},

which is a constant upper bound for
n
, as long as
B
is known.

3.

Suppose
C=\max\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{v}\}=a_{i}
for some index
i
. In this case, we use (
4.4
) to get

\displaystyle m<2n

\displaystyle\implies\log m<\log 2+\log n<\log 2+\frac{4}{3}D_{B}\log C

(4.7)

\displaystyle\implies\log\frac{m}{2}=\log m-\log 2<\frac{4}{3}D_{B}\log C.

(4.8)

Again, we let
p
be the smallest prime factor of
B
. Similar to the second case, we get
m\geq p^{C}
, so

C\log\frac{p}{2}\leq\log\frac{p^{C}}{2^{C}}\leq\log\frac{p^{C}}{2}\leq\log\frac{m}{2}<\frac{4}{3}D_{B}\log C,

giving

\frac{C}{\log C}<\frac{4D_{B}}{3\log\frac{p}{2}},

where the upper bound is a constant when
B
is fixed. As before, the strictly increasing property of
\frac{z}{\ln z}
for real
z>e
yields an implicit upper bound
V_{B}
for
C
that depends only on
B
, so (
4.8
) produces

m=2\cdot\frac{m}{2}=2e^{\log\frac{m}{2}}<2e^{\frac{4}{3}D_{B}\log C}=2C^{\frac{4}{3}D_{B}}\leq 2V_{B}^{D_{B}},

which is a constant upper bound for
m
if
B
is known.

The bounds in each case are large, so calculations on the “finitely many” cases would still be computationally intensive.
∎

Lastly, we apply the
abc
conjecture to the case of fixed exponents
k
to derive a conditional result.

Conjecture 4.4

(
abc
conjecture)
.

Let
\epsilon>0
be a fixed real number. Then, the set of triples
(a,b,c)
of coprime positive integers such that
a+b=c
with
a<b
and

\text{Rad}(abc)<c^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}}

is finite. Here,
\text{Rad}(\gamma)
denotes the radical of the positive integer argument
\gamma
, which is the product of the distinct prime factors of
\gamma
.
[
9
, p. 213]
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Assuming the
abc
conjecture, the collection of digital anomalies
(x,y,B,k)
is finite for each fixed
k\geq 3
.

Proof.

Suppose
(x,y,B,k)
is a digital anomaly for some
k\geq 3
. By Theorem
2.7
, we know that positive integers
t,m,n
exist such that

B^{k}=m\sqrt{tn}\implies B^{2k}=m^{2}tn\implies mn\mid B^{k},

so the set of prime factors of
mn
is a subset of that of
B
. Moreover, recall from (
4.4
) that

m<2n\implies m-n<n.

This allows us to set
(a,b,c)=(m-n,n,m)
in the
abc
conjecture. Then,

\displaystyle\text{Rad}((m-n)nm)

\displaystyle\leq\text{Rad}(B^{k}(m-n))

(4.9)

\displaystyle=\text{Rad}(B(m-n))\leq B(m-n),

since powers in the argument of the radical function are irrelevant. Now, we obtain the following estimates for
B
using (
2.12
) and
m-n
using (
4.4
), along with
n<m
,

\displaystyle B^{k}\leq(m-n)^{2}B^{k}<mn<m^{2}\implies

\displaystyle B<m^{\frac{2}{k}},

\displaystyle m-n<n^{\frac{1}{4}}<m^{\frac{1}{4}}.

Together, for
k\geq 3
, they yield

B(m-n)<m^{\frac{2}{k}}m^{\frac{1}{4}}\leq m^{\frac{2}{3}+\frac{1}{4}}=m^{\frac{11}{12}},

Since
\frac{11}{12}=\frac{1}{\left(\frac{12}{11}\right)}=\frac{1}{\left(1+\frac{1}{11}\right)}
, we can take
\epsilon=\frac{1}{11}
to get

\text{Rad}((m-n)nm)<m^{\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}}.

By the
abc
conjecture, there are finitely many triples for
\epsilon=\frac{1}{11}
. This means the set of pairs
(m,n)
produced from the set of digital anomalies
(x,y,B,k)
is finite for each fixed
k\geq 3
, which bounds
m
and
n
above by a constant using only
k
. Since
0<t<n
, we know that
t
is bounded above as well, so the set of triples
(t,m,n)
is finite. By Theorem
2.7
,
x
and
y
can be written in terms of
(t,m,n)
and
B=(m\sqrt{tn})^{\frac{1}{k}}
is also determined when
k
is fixed.

Therefore, contingent on the truth of the
abc
conjecture, fixing
k\geq 3
yields a finite set of digital anomalies.
∎

Unfortunately, applying the
abc
conjecture to the
k=2
case does not appear to be feasible, as it is difficult to find a suitable constant
\epsilon>0
. We can come close with an argument based on (
2.12
) and (
4.9
):

\displaystyle B^{k}<\frac{mn}{(m-n)^{2}}

\displaystyle\implies B^{2}(m-n)^{2}<mn<m^{2}

\displaystyle\implies\text{Rad}((m-n)nm)\leq B(m-n)<m.

This is equivalent to taking
\epsilon=0
in the exponent
\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}
of
m
in the upper bound, whereas
\epsilon>0
remains elusive. To that end, we offer a conjecture for
k=2
.

Conjecture 4.6
.

The only digital anomalies
(x,y,B,k)
for which
k=2
are
(18,4,6,2)
and
(1323,36,42,2)
.

In fact, the author is unaware of any other examples for which
k>1
, so these might be the only solutions outside
k=1
. This possibility leaves the door open for stronger results than those derived here.
