Differentiable neural network representation of multi-well, locally-convex potentials
Reese E. Jones, Adrian Buganza Tepole, Jan N. Fuhg

Abstract
Multi-well potentials are ubiquitous in science, modeling phenomena such as phase transitions, dynamic instabilities, and multimodal behavior across physics, chemistry, and biology. In contrast to non-smooth minimum-of-mixture representations, we propose a differentiable and convex formulation based on a log-sum-exponential (LSE) mixture of input convex neural network (ICNN) modes. This log-sum-exponential input convex neural network (LSE-ICNN) provides a smooth surrogate that retains convexity within basins and allows for gradient-based learning and inference. A key feature of the LSE-ICNN is its ability to automatically discover both the number of modes and the scale of transitions through sparse regression, enabling adaptive and parsimonious modeling. We demonstrate the versatility of the LSE-ICNN across diverse domains, including mechanochemical phase transformations, microstructural elastic instabilities, conservative biological gene circuits, and variational inference for multimodal probability distributions. These examples highlight the effectiveness of the LSE-ICNN in capturing complex multimodal landscapes while preserving differentiability, making it broadly applicable in data-driven modeling, optimization, and physical simulation.

Across all these domains, a differentiable surrogate of the underlying potential (or probability distribution) is indispensable for gradient‑based simulation, optimization, and learning.

These non‑differentiable regions impede gradient- and Hessian-based solvers in mechanics, break adjoint‐based sensitivity analysis, and preclude direct use in modern deep‑learning frameworks that rely on automatic differentiation.

As the name suggests, the

Convexity of a potential conveys a number of theoretical properties that lead to guarantees of solvability and stability

Among general representations of convex functions, the so-called

2
Related work

Finding arbitrary potentials to fit experimental data through machine learning architectures has gained interest in several areas of computational physics.
In most applications, potentials are expected to be at least locally convex.
Convexity in data-driven potentials can be enforced
a priori
with input convex neural networks (ICNNs)
[
27
,
40
]
, and similar architectures
[
41
,
42
]
designed to ensure convexity with respect to their inputs.
In solid mechanics, global polyconvexity of strain energy potentials is desirable to guarantee the existence and uniqueness of boundary‑value problems
[
43
]
.
However, when the underlying material contains, for example, phase‑transforming crystals or snap‑through instabilities, the effective energy develops multiple local minima
[
44
,
10
]
.
A straightforward way to capture such behavior in a data-driven way is to model the energy as the point-wise minimum of several convex wells modeled via ICNNs
[
45
,
46
]
.
As discussed, the problem with this approach is that the potential is non‑differentiable along the intersection of the multiple wells.
Also, physical transitions/saddle points are generally smooth with a physical scale appropriate for the physics of the transition.
Furthermore, the point-wise minimum approach requires explicit knowledge of the number of distinct wells.

An alternative strategy for modeling multi-well energy landscapes in physics-informed settings involves sparse system identification, where symbolic or interpretable models are inferred directly from data using sparsity-promoting priors
[
47
]
.
While promising, these approaches may struggle to scale or generalize to high dimensions.

Our own previous work has leveraged ICNNs to model polyconvex strain energy functions, and introduced gating mechanisms to detect spatial or temporal regions of dissipation versus elastic response
[
35
,
48
]
.
This framework preserved the necessary convexity in conservative regimes while allowing transitions to inelastic behaviors through learned internal-state dynamics.

Within the related context of multimodal probability density distributions, efforts to create mixture models such as Gaussian mixture models are common
[
49
]
, and several data-driven enhancements have been proposed in this regard
[
50
,
51
]
.
While GMMs and their machine learning variants offer smooth representations, they do not typically enforce convexity of the underlying components, which is an essential feature in many physical systems.

3
Neural network architecture

To represent multi-well potentials that are locally convex, we develop a neural network based on a log-sum-exponential (LSE) mixing of input convex NNs.

Log sum exponent layer

Previous representations
[
45
,
46
]
rely on a simple minimum of a set of functions
\{f_{i}(\mathsf{x})\}

\min\left(f_{1}(\mathsf{x}),\ldots,f_{n}(\mathsf{x})\right).

(1)

We want a differentiable approximation of the minimum function with a learnable length-scale of transition.
The LSE function

\operatorname{LSE}(\{f_{i}(\mathsf{x})\},\rho)=-\frac{1}{\rho}\log\left(\frac{1}{N_{\text{modes}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{modes}}}\exp(-\rho f_{i}(\inputvector))\right)\quad,

(2)

is smoothly differentiable and approaches
\min_{i}(f_{i})
as
\rho\rightarrow+\infty
, see Boyd and Vandenberghe
[
22
]
(Section 3.1.5).
This transition is illustrated in Fig.
1
.
Here
\rho
is analogous to the inverse temperature in the Helmholtz free energy.
To allow for the number of wells to be discoverable, we reframe Eq.  (
2
) as a mode-weighted function:

\operatorname{LSE}_{\alpha}(\{f_{i}(\mathsf{x})\},\rho)=-\frac{1}{\rho}\log\left(\frac{1}{N_{\text{modes}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{modes}}}\varsigma(\alpha_{i})\exp(-\rho f_{i}(\inputvector))\right)\ ,

(3)

where
\alpha_{i}
are mode weights and
\varsigma
is a gating activation function. In order to preserve convexity, we require that
\varsigma(\alpha_{i})>0
and
\rho>0
. Herein we employed a shifted and scaled sigmoid function for
\varsigma(x)=\operatorname{sigmoid}(10(x/2-1))
such that
1>\varsigma(\alpha_{i})>0
.
Alternatively, we can recast Eq.  (
3
) as

\displaystyle\operatorname{LSE}_{\alpha}(\{f_{i}(\mathsf{x})\},\rho)=-\frac{1}{\rho}\log\left(\frac{1}{N_{\text{modes}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{modes}}}\exp(-\rho( f_{i}(\inputvector) -\log(\varsigma(\alpha_{i}))))\right)\quad,

(4)

which further elucidates the role of the weighing function in the approximation of Eq.  (
2
).
To enable local convexity, i.e., convexity near distinct modes/wells, each
f_{i}
in Eq.  (
3
) is an input convex neural network (ICNN).

Illustration of the effect of length-scale
\rho
on the
\operatorname{LSE}

Input convex neural network

For each mode function
f_{i}(\mathsf{x})
, we employ an ICNN, which is a fully connected neural network with a skip connection and constraints on specific weights.
For an input vector
\mathsf{x}
, the ICNN is a feedforward stack of
N_{\ell}
layers:

\displaystyle\mathsf{h}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\left(\mathsf{V}_{1}\mathsf{x}+\mathsf{b}_{1}\right)

\displaystyle\mathsf{h}_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}\left(\mathsf{V}_{k}\mathsf{x}+\mathsf{W}_{k}\mathsf{h}_{k-1}+\mathsf{b}_{k}\right)\qquad k=2,\ldots,N_{\ell}-1

(5)

\displaystyle\mathsf{y}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathsf{V}_{N_{\ell}}\mathsf{x}+\mathsf{W}_{N_{\ell}}\mathsf{h}_{N-1}+\mathsf{b}_{N_{\ell}}

with weights
\mathsf{W}_{k}
and
\mathsf{V}_{k}
, activation functions
\sigma_{k}
and
k\in[1,N_{\ell}]
.
The weights and biases form the set of trainable parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\{\mathsf{W}_{k},\mathsf{V}_{k},\mathsf{b}_{k}\}
.
The output
\mathsf{y}=\mathsf{N}\!\mathsf{N}(\mathsf{x};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
is convex with respect to the input because the weights
\mathsf{W}_{k}
are constrained to be non-negative, and the activation functions
\sigma_{k}
are chosen to be convex and non-decreasing
[
27
]
and set as softplus in this work.

Log-sum-exponential input convex neural network

The LSE-ICNN is simply

\operatorname{LSE}_{\alpha}(\{\mathsf{N}\!\mathsf{N}_{i}(\mathsf{x})\},\rho)=-\frac{1}{\rho}\log\left(\frac{1}{N_{\text{modes}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\text{modes}}}\varsigma(\alpha_{i})\exp(-\rho\, \NN_{i}(\inputvector))\right)\ ,

(6)

where
\rho
is a learnable parameter in addition to the weights of the ICNN modes
\{\mathsf{N}\!\mathsf{N}_{i}\}
and mode weights
\{\alpha_{i}\}
.

Sparse regression

The parameters of LSE-ICNN are
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\{\rho,\{\alpha_{i},{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{\text{modes}}}\}
.
We use the usual mean squared error (MSE) loss function augmented with an L1 penalty
\epsilon
on the weights
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\{\alpha_{i}\}

\mathcal{L}=\underbrace{\|\mathsf{y}-\operatorname{LSE}(\mathsf{x};{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}}_{\operatorname{MSE}({\boldsymbol{\theta}};\mathsf{D})}+\varepsilon|\boldsymbol{\alpha}|_{1}\quad,

(7)

which is common in sparse regression
[
52
]
to minimize the number of non-zero components.
In practice, we start with a generous estimate of the number of modes
N_{\text{modes}}
for the application and then, through the L1 regularization of
\alpha_{i}
remove unnecessary terms during training with gradient descent.

Multimodal distributions

Representing the probability distribution of stochastic bifurcating dynamics in chemical and other physical systems represents a challenge.
The Schlögl system involves three species and two reactions and is a canonical chemical reaction that exhibits bistability
[
57
,
58
]
.

The Schlögl model is a prototypical bistable chemical reaction system involving a single dynamic species
X
and two reservoir species
A
and
B
, which are maintained at constant concentrations. The model consists of the following reactions:

\displaystyle A+2X

\displaystyle\underset{k_{2}}{\overset{k_{1}}{\rightleftharpoons}}3X

(17)

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle\underset{k_{4}}{\overset{k_{3}}{\rightleftharpoons}}X

(18)

Assuming constant concentrations
[A]=a
and
[B]=b
, the concentration
x(t)=[X](t)
evolves according to the ordinary differential equation:

\frac{dx}{dt}=k_{1}ax^{2}-k_{2}x^{3}+k_{3}b-k_{4}x\quad.

(19)

In the data-generating model, the reaction kinetics transition probabilities drive a Gillespie stochastic simulation algorithm
[
59
,
60
]
.
The output of the SSA for the Schlögl system is multimodal and depends on the particular realization of the stochastic diffusion process and the initial conditions.
More details can be found in
[
61
,
62
]
.
Fig.
8

The higher concentration mode is broader/more diffuse than the lower concentration mode, which is more apparent in the kernel density estimate (KDE) shown in Fig.

To model this data, we use variational inference (VI)
[
63
]
with an LSE-ICNN representation of the probability density function (PDF)
\pi(y)
of
y
, the single observed species:

\log\pi(y)\approx\log q(y;{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\equiv\text{LSE}(y;{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\quad.

(20)

In this learning framework
q
plays the role of the posterior density
\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ |\ y)
given data
y
as in Bayes rule:

\underbrace{\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}}\ |\ y)}_{\text{posterior}}\propto\underbrace{\pi(y\ |\ {\boldsymbol{\theta}})}_{\text{likelihood}}\underbrace{\pi({\boldsymbol{\theta}})}_{\text{prior}}

(21)

Minimizing the (negative) evidence lower bound (ELBO) provides an optimization problem

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(t)=\operatorname{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\Bigl[-\text{ELBO}({\boldsymbol{\theta}};y)\Bigr]=\operatorname{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\Bigl[-\mathbb{E}_{q}\left[\log\pi(y(t)\ |\ {\boldsymbol{\theta}})\right]+\operatorname{D}_{\text{KL}}(q(y;{\boldsymbol{\theta}})||\pi(y))\Bigr]

(22)

for calibrating the parameters of the LSE-ICNN posterior distribution.
In Eq.  (

and the prior
\pi(y)

The KL divergence term provides a regularization of the main MSE-like objective.

Waddington’s epigenetic landscape is a classical metaphor in developmental biology
[
65

This metaphor can be translated into a dynamical systems framework using gene circuit models.
Consider two genes,

and
x_{2}

\dot{\mathbf{x}}=\begin{bmatrix}\dot{x}_{1}\\
\dot{x}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}ax_{1}(p_{1}-x_{1})+\dfrac{b}{S^{n}+x_{2}^{n}}-\dfrac{bnx_{1}^{n-1}x_{2}}{(S^{n}+x_{1}^{n})^{2}}\\[10.0pt]
ax_{2}(p_{2}-x_{2})+\dfrac{b}{S^{n}+x_{1}^{n}}-\dfrac{bnx_{2}^{n-1}x_{1}}{(S^{n}+x_{2}^{n})^{2}}\end{bmatrix}

(32)

The first term in each component,

for

a
controls the growth rate and
p_{i}
sets the preferred expression level.
The second term,

(

x_{i}
that is suppressed by increasing levels of
x_{j}

The final term in each equation captures nonlinear degradation due to the opposing gene, which becomes saturated in the limit of large

This coupling structure induces bistability or multistability depending on the parameter regime.
For small

For larger

[

and
x_{2}

In that model, each gene self-activates and represses the other, encoding a binary fate decision in hematopoietic stem cells.

5
Conclusion

In this work, we employ ICNNs as convex basis functions inside the LSE formulation, enabling a fully data-driven approximation of well-behaved multi-well energies.
We demonstrated that the LSE-ICNN is an effective representation across a variety of disciplines in computational physics.

Furthermore, we introduce a learnable transition scale and gating parameters associated with each well.
The inverse temperature-like parameter allows the LSE-ICNN to fit the range of smooth to sharp transitions.
The gating weights act as an adaptive mechanism that determines the relevance of each mode during training, effectively allowing the model to infer the number of active wells from data.
This feature is particularly valuable in situations where the number of energy minima is not known
a priori
.

Overall, our method provides a new paradigm for learning energy functions that are differentiable, convex within basins, and adaptive to complex multimodal structures such as: multimodal probability densities, loss of polyconvexity in materials with phase-changes or instabilities during microstructure deformation, and biological networks with multiple stable wells guiding cell differentiation.

In future work, we intend to continue the development of the LSE-ICNN in dynamic and dissipative applications, which include further thermodynamic considerations to guarantee positive energy dissipation. By bridging physical priors with flexible neural architectures, the LSE-ICNN opens new possibilities for robust, data-driven modeling of complex energy landscapes in computational physics.
