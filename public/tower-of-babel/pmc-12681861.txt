A person-centered approach to digital health technologies for cancer prevention: Qualitative findings on use of technology-based interventions in Houston, Texas
Authors: Preena Loomba, Scherezade Mama, Thuan Le, Stacy Mitchell, Elizabeth Dominguez, Karen Basen-Engquist, Larkin L. Strong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPhysical inactivity, poor nutrition, and obesity are significant, yet preventable contributors to morbidity and mortality among adults. This qualitative study examined internet access and technology use, and barriers and facilitators to engaging in digital cancer prevention programs.MethodsWe conducted 30–60-min semi-structured interviews of Hispanic adults (n = 12) and cancer survivors (n = 10) from two lifestyle intervention trials (September 2021–April 2022) in Houston, Texas, who transitioned from in-person to virtual delivery during the COVID-19 pandemic. Interviews examined in-person versus virtual experiences and were thematically analyzed.ResultsParticipants (95 % women, mean age for Hispanic adults = 39.8 ± 7.9 years, 54.6 ± 5.4 years for cancer survivors) shared their experiences transitioning to virtual programs and discussed performance and effort expectancies related to using digital health technologies. Participants emphasized the need for social interactions and lack of connectivity in current virtual offerings. Factors such as prior experience and tech-related anxiety were also discussed as important for the uptake of technology-based programs.ConclusionsQualitative findings highlight the complex experiences of cancer prevention program participants using digital health technologies during the COVID-19 pandemic and can inform interventions and policies aimed at reducing health inequalities linked to the digital divides.

Introduction

Regular exercise and consuming a healthy diet offer numerous health benefits (Clinton et al., 2020). Nonetheless, most adults in the U.S. do not engage in sufficient amounts of physical activity (PA) and consume poor-quality foods (Liu and Mozaffarian, 2024). Disparities in PA and healthy eating are pronounced among racial and ethnic minorities and those with lower socioeconomic status (Bhattacharyya et al., 2024; Patel et al., 2022). For example, only 43 % of Hispanic/Latinos meet the PA recommendations (Elgaddal et al., 2022), and over 78 % consume fewer than five servings of fruits and vegetables daily (Lee et al., 2022). These patterns are correlated with obesity rates, as 79 % of Hispanic/Latino adults are overweight or obese compared with 67 % of non-Hispanic White adults (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Adult Obesity Prevalence Maps, 2025).

Additionally, cancer survivors can substantially benefit from lifestyle interventions that improve overall health and quality of life and reduce recurrence and morbidity risk; however, they continue to experience notable health disparities and diminished quality of life (Mama et al., 2020; Howlader et al., 2014). While both regular exercise and adequate nutrition can help restore physical function and reduce morbidity among cancer survivors, most cancer survivors do not engage in sufficient PA or maintain a healthy diet, further elevating their health risks (Zhang et al., 2015; Campbell et al., 2019). As advances in screening, early detection, and cancer treatment continue (Santos Salas et al., 2024), the number of cancer survivors is expected to rise.

Technology-based tools, such as wearables, mobile applications, and telemedicine, can be leveraged to effectively promote healthy behaviors, particularly PA (Alley et al., 2024), and can broaden access to resources (Shaffer et al., 2023). The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated the use of digital platforms to promote PA, as restrictions on public spaces and fitness centers limited access to traditional resources (Liu et al., 2022; Wunsch et al., 2022). Importantly, individuals who utilized web-based or mobile applications for home-based exercises during the pandemic were more likely to achieve recommended levels of moderate-to-vigorous and strength-based activity (Fang et al., 2022).

Technology adoption among adults is influenced by multiple factors, such as sociodemographic characteristics, health status, attitudes, technology attributes, and social influences (Bertolazzi et al., 2024). Research shows that use of digital resources varies by population; for example, while Hispanics have high rates of social media and smartphone use (Pew Research Center, 2015), they are less likely than non-Hispanic Whites to participate in telehealth activities (Weber et al., 2020). Barriers to adoption include limited access (Jaworski et al., 2022), low education level and income (Doetsch et al., 2017), high device costs, and financial constraints. In addition, age-related physical impairments (sensory, motor, cognitive) can hinder adoption. Patients with chronic conditions are more likely to benefit from telemedicine programs (Rodríguez-Fernández et al., 2022). Well-designed technology-based interventions that promote exercise and healthy eating have the potential to reduce inequities and enhance inclusive access to health benefits. Minimizing barriers such as travel, transportation, and scheduling constraints can improve participation in health programs. Effectively addressing disparities and promoting overall health necessitates careful consideration of contextual and cultural nuances within diverse populations.

The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) model integrates constructs from eight major information systems and technology acceptance models and extends health behavior theories to better explain technology adoption (Viswanath et al., 2003). It posits that technology adoption is primarily driven by behavioral intentions, shaped by four key factors: performance expectancy (expected benefits), effort expectancy (ease of use), social influence, and facilitating conditions (FAC) (Table 1 and Fig. 1). The model identifies technology anxiety as a key factor influencing the relationships between the core constructs and shaping technology adoption (Viswanath et al., 2003). It has been widely applied, yielding varied results across different studies and contexts (Lai, 2020; Shi et al., 2025). Materials and methods

Study design and sample

This qualitative study interviewed participants from two lifestyle intervention trials, Tu Salud, ¡Si Cuenta! (TSSC)/Your Health Matters and Active Living After Cancer (ALAC). The TSSC study was designed to evaluate the effectiveness of a 6-month family-based intervention for promoting positive changes in PA and healthy eating behaviors in dyads of adult Hispanic family members (John et al., 2025). In this study, 225 adult family dyads (n = 450 adults) were enrolled between June 2018 and March 2020. Dyads were randomized to the TSSC intervention or to a comparison condition focused on promoting health and safety within the home. Both interventions were delivered by trained and certified community health workers and involved six in-person monthly sessions that took place at participants' homes. The TSSC intervention was designed to enhance motivation and social support within dyads for positive health behavior change.

Active Living After Cancer (ALAC) is a 12-week evidence-based program that evaluates changes in PA, quality of life, and physical functioning among post-treatment cancer survivors and caregivers in which a group facilitator uses a structured curriculum to teach cognitive and behavioral skills for increasing PA, lead moderate-intensity PA, and present topics related to cancer survivorship (e.g., fatigue, communication with health care providers, cancer screenings, and nutrition). The program has been shown to increase participants' PA and improve their quality of life and physical functioning (Mama et al., 2024). ALAC participants who completed at least one session in person and at least one session virtually were contacted by email or phone to participate in this study.

Between September 2021 and April 2022, we recruited purposive samples from the two programs. For TSSC, 26 eligible dyads were identified, and participants were contacted until 12 consented, with only one participant per dyad included; each participant had completed at least five sessions (including at least two virtual sessions). For ALAC, 31 cancer survivors were contacted; 10 consented to participate. The study was approved by The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center Institutional Review Board and conducted in accordance with its ethical guidelines.

Interviews

Semi-structured interviews were conducted by telephone or Zoom videoconference between September 2021 and April 2022. Participants completed a brief questionnaire to assess demographics and internet access and usage. The 30–60-min interviews were conducted using a semi-structured interview guide, in English or Spanish. Questions addressed participants' experiences and satisfaction with participating in the intervention virtually, factors that facilitated or hindered their ability to connect via Zoom, and aspects of virtual vs in-person delivery that they liked or disliked. Questions focused on participants' experiences during the COVID-19 pandemic and thus did not fully address how virtual programming could be improved once in-person opportunities had resumed. Additionally, participants were asked about their overall experiences and satisfaction with the programs and recommendations for improvement. Interviews were conducted by a trained bilingual research member of the TSSC and ALAC teams. All interviews were digitally recorded, transcribed, and translated using an outside vendor.

Qualitative statistical analysis

Descriptive and summary statistics were examined to characterize demographics and internet access and use. Interview transcripts were compared with digital recordings for accuracy. Thematic analysis was used to examine participants' perspectives on switching to virtually delivered programs by systematically identifying and organizing patterns and themes in the data. The analysis followed a two-step process. First, a list of codes was generated on the basis of study objectives and open-ended responses using a content analysis approach. Next, thematic content analysis identified key themes. Coding was iterative in nature, involving multiple readings of the interview transcripts. During the initial review, new codes were identified and added to the preliminary codebook. As each transcript was analyzed, recurring constructs and corresponding quotes were identified and grouped into themes. The second reading focused on refining these themes by comparing each transcript with the updated codebook. This process involved confirming, modifying, or rejecting themes as necessary. In interpreting these themes, we incorporated the framework within the UTAUT model. A final review ensured thematic saturation, confirming that no new themes emerged. Co-authors engaged in collaborative discussions throughout the coding process, reviewing and refining the emerging themes. The final 12 themes were grouped by UTAUT constructs. All interviews were coded and managed using ATLAS.ti (v24).

The mean ages of the TSSC and ALAC program participants were 39.8 years and 54.6 years, respectively (Table 2). Internet access and use and experience with telehealth services

Participants were asked about digital options for accessing the internet and experience with scheduling telemedicine appointments for medical/cancer care. Over half (58 %) of TSSC participants reported having multiple alternatives (e.g., laptop, tablet) besides their cellphone for accessing the internet, compared to 40 % of ALAC program participants. During the COVID-19 pandemic, 33 % of TSSC participants and 40 % of ALAC program participants reported having a telehealth medical/cancer appointment. Among those who utilized telemedicine services, 75 % of TSSC participants and 50 % of ALAC participants were very satisfied with their telehealth experience.

Performance expectancy (PEX) – In-person. Participants expressed a strong preference for in-person classes, emphasizing the importance of accessibility and interpersonal connections. They found the program content both engaging and useful. TSSC participants especially appreciated the in-person weigh-ins, which enabled them to observe measurable progress in their weight. Cancer survivors participating in the ALAC program valued the support received from fellow survivors during the in-person classes. They received comfort by engaging with other cancer survivors and appreciated the depth and quality of in-person interactions. Overall, participants perceived that in-person classes facilitated deeper engagement, which improved the overall learning experience.

PEX – Virtual. Participants in both programs expressed a mix of positive and negative attitudes regarding their virtual participation in TSSC and ALAC. Although participants showed a clear preference for in-person, they acknowledged the constraints posed by the COVID-19 pandemic and recognized the unique advantages of virtual learning. Participants reported that virtual program participation offered flexibility, convenience, and adaptability during the pandemic. They found the format user-friendly, allowing safe engagement from home.

Despite facing initial technical challenges, participants reported successfully navigating the obstacles, for example, taking notes and listing steps to access Zoom, and overall valuing the opportunity to continue their learning virtually. The online experience was regarded as practical and efficient in creating a successful and enriching learning environment. The skills and experiences gained with technology proved valuable for other areas of their life, such as staying connected with family, attending other meetings, and building confidence in using digital tools.

Participants also reported significant challenges during the virtual classes, including unreliable internet connectivity, technical difficulties with the device or software, and disruptions with incoming calls during the class. This hindered effective communication and the flow of online sessions. These challenges were particularly acute for participants with limited technological experience and those from rural areas who faced additional complications due to poor internet signals, leading to frequent disruptions and frustrations during sessions. Participants in both groups found virtual classes to be beneficial; however, they expressed a need for more personal connection, which they found rewarding during the in-person classes.

PEX – Delivery improvements. Participants suggested improvements to virtual offerings to increase engagement. Suggestions for improvement included providing more training on the video conference technology and maintaining communication during the session. A few participants also suggested that the virtual classes be kept to a reasonable length (e.g., no more than 90 min), as these may become tedious with frequent interruptions.

Effort expectancy (EEX) – Virtual. Participants reported that one of the key benefits of virtual sessions is convenience, particularly for those without transportation or those residing far away. They appreciated the virtual format for eliminating travel, which allowed them more time to attend to their children or other responsibilities. Technology allowed for greater flexibility, making communication easier and reducing the hassles of traffic and additional costs.

Participants in both programs described the lack of ease associated with using digital health tools compared to in-person learning. They found the virtual sessions to be less engaging and motivating than in-person interactions, often leaving them feeling bored, low on energy, and unable to concentrate during the sessions. They cited distractions at home, limited social interaction, and communication challenges as major drawbacks of virtual classes. Poor signals, phone interruptions, and inadequate equipment also negatively impacted the experience. The absence of physical presence and hands-on activities made the experience of participating in the classes less fulfilling. The remote nature of the program hindered full participation in exercises and food demonstration, while the lack of measurements and interactions diminished motivation for some. A rural cancer survivor reported that lack of reliable internet made it difficult to connect via Zoom. Participants in both interventions reported that reconnecting after internet failures was challenging.

The majority of participants indicated a preference for in-person classes. They perceived that virtually delivered programs were less effective, which left them feeling dissatisfied, particularly regarding challenges in completing tasks and staying engaged.

Social influence (SIN). When asked about the experience of using virtual platforms with respect to social connection, although cited less often, participants reported favorable experiences, finding it convenient and easy to connect with others, share experiences, and learn from each other. Virtual classes were considered inclusive and accessible, particularly by those who faced barriers in attending in-person sessions. Participants in the ALAC program reported that the technology enabled cancer survivors from various locations to engage in sessions, providing comfort and support in a virtual setting. Despite the pandemic restrictions, the technology fostered a sense of community and mutual support.

FAC. Specific FAC influenced participants' ability to engage effectively in the programs. Participants who had previously used digital technology in different contexts, such as attending online classes or telehealth appointments or using messaging platforms, reported greater ease in navigating the virtual classes. Familiarity and comfort with technology appeared to positively influence their engagement in the sessions.

Technology anxiety. Struggling to navigate the technology and internet connectivity issues were recurring themes for participants during the sessions. Participants in both programs reported facing difficulties with the technology, leading to anxiety. For these participants, the experience was akin to stepping into unfamiliar territory, made even more challenging by COVID-19 pandemic–induced limitations on in-person meetings.

Qualitative findings among those with access to digital options to access online programs

Study participants' ability to continue receiving vital information remotely in lieu of restrictions that limited in-person meetings was seen as a key benefit of the new virtual option. An additional analysis examined participants' experiences in digital health programs, comparing those with multiple digital access options (e.g., access through devices other than just a mobile phone) to those with limited access options. Participants with multiple access options adapted more readily to virtual participation, valuing its flexibility while still desiring in-person sessions for stronger emotional connections. They readily addressed technical challenges, experiencing fewer difficulties and requiring minimal assistance to troubleshoot problems. In contrast, those with limited access struggled more with virtual technology, primarily due to technical issues, limited digital literacy, and lack of motivation. This group expressed a stronger preference for in-person meetings, citing better interpersonal connections, a greater need for social support when using technology, and increased frustration and anxiety with digital platforms. Detailed themes, matched with the UTAUT model constructs, and supporting quotes are presented in the Supplemental table for this additional analysis.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to explore barriers and facilitators to the use of digital health technologies in exercise and healthy eating programs that were delivered virtually during the COVID-19 pandemic to Hispanic adults and cancer survivors. We found that participants' engagement with digital health programs was influenced by key determinants of technology use and closely aligns with constructs of UTAUT. The theoretical framework provided a valuable lens through which to interpret the study's findings, offering insights into the complex dynamics of digital health technology adoption.

Most participants viewed switching to virtual platforms positively while also noting the challenges of this approach and the need to improve engagement. The ability to maintain effective communication and the exchange of vital information during the pandemic was recognized as a key advantage (positive PEX). Virtual classes enabled convenient social interactions that often translated into improved personal interactions that extended beyond sessions (SIN). However, there were challenges, including navigating technology, connectivity issues, tech anxiety, and lack of experience with the platform (negative EEX, FAC). Comparing the two formats, participants noted that certain aspects of in-person activities (e.g., exercising or hands-on activities) were not sufficiently replicated in a virtual format. Digital health interventions, when intentionally designed for virtual delivery, can capitalize on unique opportunities that are not available in traditional in-person settings. Interventions such as IM@Home, a digital, live (synchronous) mind-body and exercise intervention developed in response to the COVID-19 pandemic to effectively address symptom relief and patient support needs (Mao et al., 2025), demonstrated that virtual programming can complement and enhance in-person care rather than simply replace it.

The results from this study align with prior research on barriers and facilitators to digital healthcare engagement. Importantly, past studies have observed that all three core predictors (PEX, EEX, SIN) were positively correlated with attitudes and use of digital tools, including app acceptance (Liu et al., 2019) and video consultations in cancer care. Patients' discomfort with new technology is a significant barrier to adopting online telemedicine programs and services (Nurtsch et al., 2024). These findings align with our observations that EEX was a major challenge for adopting programs delivered virtually. Evidence comparing in-person and virtual healthcare and supportive experiences for adult cancer survivors is limited, making it difficult to understand the differences, benefits, and challenges of these care modalities (Shaffer et al., 2023). Further research in this area is needed to drive innovation and support integration of digital health interventions in health care and programming.

Research suggests that in-person interactions provide culturally tailored support and stronger peer connections (Kiemen et al., 2023). While remote therapy can be beneficial, more research is needed to assess cancer survivors' preferences for in-person sessions. The influence of EEX underscores the need for user-friendly, easily integrated technology, especially for those less familiar with using technology. Cancer survivors' digital literacy can improve with both informational and emotional support, emphasizing the value of active engagement and social support over passive browsing. As noted by Liu et al. (2019), the perceived usefulness (PEX) may become a more salient determinant of acceptance than EEX as technologies mature and users gain experience. In our cohort of cancer survivors, the continued importance of EEX and SIN may reflect participants' relatively early digital health experience; however, as virtual platforms become increasingly user-friendly, reliable, and pervasive, survivors' acceptance of digital health technologies will likely depend on anticipated outcomes and personal benefit. These reflections underscore the need to balance the convenience of virtual participation with the unique benefits of in-person interaction, particularly in supportive group contexts.

Despite age differences, participants in both TSSC and ALAC shared similar barriers and facilitators to using digital health technologies. Prior research indicates that past experience and comfort with technology were key predictors of engagement and telehealth usage (Holtz et al., 2022). We found that familiarity with digital tools facilitated participation and may have moderated their perceptions about virtual programs (FAC), a conclusion that has also been supported by previous studies (Bagchi et al., 2022). Digital access barriers amplify technology-related challenges and support needs, underscoring the necessity to design digital health tools that reduce rather than reinforce existing disparities. Barriers like unequal access (Jaworski et al., 2022), low digital literacy (Arias López et al., 2023), age-related challenges (Jaworski et al., 2022), and socioeconomic factors can hinder adoption among vulnerable populations. To mitigate these risks, targeted interventions should improve digital health literacy, provide tailored technical support, and address infrastructure inequities. These improvements can enhance accessibility and convenience while ensuring that individual users remain central to the process of technology adoption.

This study enhances our understanding of factors influencing digital health technology use among an understudied Hispanic population, despite some limitations. Participants were primarily middle-aged or older Hispanic women from an urban area who participated in a research study (TSSC) or a community program (ALAC), which may limit generalizability. Nonetheless, the findings may provide a foundation for future hypothesis-driven research in these and other age-related cohorts. This study was conducted during widespread pandemic-related restrictions on in-person care; therefore, the findings reported here may not fully extend to post-pandemic settings with restored in-person care access. Interviews reflected experiences from the early stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, when virtual technology was less familiar; therefore, responses may differ in the current environment. Nonetheless, many of the perceptions pertaining to PEX, EEX, and SIN influence remain relevant. Future research involving virtual programming should consider contextual and implementation factors and account for differences in previous technology experience.

Conclusions

The COVID-19 pandemic unexpectedly accelerated the adoption of digital health technologies, enabling clinical and cancer prevention services to continue through virtual platforms. Our research provides valuable insights into the barriers and facilitators of transitioning from in-person to virtual programming among two underserved population subgroups. Importantly, leveraging the distinct strengths of digital modalities, such as scalability and real-time support, can enable digital health interventions to complement and enhance in-person care and supports innovative approaches to behavior change and survivorship care. The findings suggest that digital health approaches, when designed with attention to users' capabilities and strategies for engagement, can be a viable approach for intervening with underserved communities in cancer prevention.
