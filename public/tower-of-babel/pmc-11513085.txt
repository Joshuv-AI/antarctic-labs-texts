A modified transactional model of stress and coping on depressive symptoms among informal caregivers of persons with dementia
Authors: Qi Yuan, Tee Hng Tan, Peizhi Wang, Daniel Poremski, Edimansyah Abdin, Harish Magadi, Richard Goveas, Li Ling Ng, Mythily Subramaniam
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInformal caregivers are crucial to the care of persons with dementia (PWD), but their role is often reported as challenging. This study aims to examine caregiver stress and depressive symptoms among informal caregivers of PWD using a modified transactional stress and coping model. Path analysis was conducted among a sample of informal caregivers of PWD in Singapore (n = 281) using information on functional dependence and memory and behavioral problems of PWD, and self-reported measurements on caregivers’ knowledge of dementia, perceived positive aspects of caregiving, social support, coping patterns, caregiving self-efficacy, caregiving burden and depressive symptoms. The initial model reflecting our assumptions for the transactional stress and coping model showed a poor fit (model 1 - CFI = 0.858, TLI = 0.665, RMSEA = 0.118). Logical modifications were made until sufficient model fit was achieved (model 2 - CFI = 0.987, TLI = 0.955, RMSEA = 0.043). We then removed the insignificant paths in model 2 and obtained our final model (model 3 - CFI = 0.990, TLI = 0.974, RMSEA = 0.033). The final model supported our hypotheses, with some adjustments. This study advances our understanding of caregiver distress by modifying the transactional stress and coping model, including (1) the key role of caregiver self-efficacy in the primary appraisal process, (2) the dynamic assessment of coping resources across all stages of the model, and (3) the importance of coping patterns. Future studies could explore the generalizability of these findings.Keywords: Dementia, Informal caregivers, Depressive symptoms, Transactional model of stress and coping, Path analysisSubject terms: Psychology, Risk factors

Introduction

The progressive deterioration in cognitive and physical abilities experienced by person with dementia (PWD)1,2 makes them increasingly dependent on others. The support they require to complete activities of daily living increases as the disease progresses. According to previous studies, most of this assistance is provided by informal caregivers of PWD such as their family members or friends3. However, the experience of caring for PWD is not easy, but rather challenging for caregivers. Typical challenges could stem directly from the PWD, via challenging behaviours4; from the conflicts between caregivers time-consuming roles5; or from the caregiving process, such as social isolation due to the high involvement in daily care6. These challenges can lead to negative consequences for caregivers. Previous systematic reviews suggest that the prevalence of depression among informal caregivers of PWD was between 31.2 and 34% 7,8, which is much higher than the prevalence of 5% of depression among general adults globally9.

The relationship between caring for a PWD and caregiver stress is complex. The transactional model of stress and coping10 is one of the most supported theories for understanding this process11. This model generally proposes that stress is an interaction between individuals and environmental factors. Individuals go through a two-stage appraisal process to determine whether the situations or experiences are stressful and whether they could lead to negative mental health outcomes. In the primary appraisal, they assess whether the situation is stressful. If the situation is assessed to be stressful, secondary appraisal is initiated to assess whether current resources are sufficient to manage these stressful events10,12. If resources are insufficient, coping options are considered and different coping strategies might result in different outcomes10. Putting this model in the context of caregiver stress, the interaction between PWD and their caregivers will trigger a primary appraisal among caregivers to determine if these scenarios are threatening and challenging, and then as a secondary appraisal whether they can be managed by the current resources. If the resources are not sufficient, caregivers will then use different coping strategies.

The transactional model was tested among caregivers of PWD and showed a good fit13. However, subsequent research has highlighted that key questions are missing from this model as applied to caregiver stress. The first question concerns the primary appraisal process - how do caregivers determine whether the situation is threatening or not? A previous study of cancer patients and their caregivers suggested that self-efficacy mediates the relationship between symptom distress and the appraisal process14. Putting this in the scenario of dementia caregiving, self-efficacy is about the confidence to cope with caregiving tasks after assuming the role of caregiver. This kind of confidence could come from their ability to deal with the behavioural problems of PWD or from the social support they receive, that would greatly reduce the chances of caregiving events being categorized as challenging and threatening. From this perspective, we hypothesize that caregivers’ primary appraisal is indeed a weighing between the stressors they encounter (i.e., caregiving scenarios) and their confidence in handling these stressors, and it is only when caregivers are not confident that these stressors are judged as challenging and threatening – this is also where caregiving burden is from. Meanwhile, caregiving self-efficacy is based on a dynamic and thorough assessment of oneself, and as caregivers develop more resources and skills, they will become more confident. In other words, as caregivers become more confident in caregiving, less events will be categorized as stressful during the primary appraisal. This assumption is consistent with self-efficacy theory, which emphasizes the importance of an individual’s perception of his or her own abilities as a key determinant of successful outcomes, and that self-efficacy can be increased through repeated mastery experiences. This, in turn, reduces related defensive behaviours15.

A second limitation of the transactional model concerns the role of available coping resources. In the current model, available coping resources only work during secondary appraisal to determine if the stressors might turn to stress. However, previous studies on positive aspects of caregiving suggested that informal caregivers of PWD would grow during this process through their own adaptations, and this will lead to gains and enhanced perceived positive aspects16–18, which might help to buffer the impact of caregiving stressors17. As a result, the assessment of available coping resources should also be dynamic since these resources might grow over time. After caregivers gain more knowledge and experiences, they would be more confident in handling caregiving-related stressors. In this case, coping resources might also play an important role during the primary appraisal as a result of their enhanced confidence, and during the coping process as they are indeed part of the coping (e.g., social support). As such, we hypothesize that available coping resources are important across all three stages from primary appraisal to the coping mechanisms.

A possible third issue concerns the coping process in the traditional transactional model of stress and coping. In one of our published studies, we found that rather than using different coping strategies, informal caregivers of PWDs tend to adopt a specific coping pattern, which was identified as a combination of different coping strategies19. For example, we identified three different coping patterns, namely low-, medium- and high-coping patterns; and caregivers with the high-coping pattern tended to use all different coping strategies frequently; those in the medium-coping pattern group also relied on all different coping strategies but less frequently; finally, those in the low-coping pattern group relied mainly on acceptance coping strategies and very minimally on any other coping strategies19. This concept of coping patterns has also been reported in other populations, such as caregivers of frail older adults20 or even among young adults21 or the general population22 during the COVID-19 pandemic. In light of this, previous studies that examined how specific types of coping strategies, such as adaptive coping, might affect caregiver stress23 may be biased and might no longer be appropriate - as many people tend to use both adaptive and maladaptive coping strategies simultaneously. Instead, a new model is needed that adopts the concept of coping patterns rather than the traditional model of specific coping strategies.

The current study aims to examine the mechanism behind caregiving appraisal and depressive symptoms through a modified model of stress and coping in which we include the concept of self-efficacy, dynamic resource assessment and coping patterns (Fig. 1). Our hypotheses were as follows:

Methods

Participants and procedures

Informal caregivers who were most involved in the care management of the PWD were recruited from the outpatient clinics of a tertiary mental health service provider and a geriatric clinic of a general hospital in Singapore. Participants were either actively approached by the study team members in the clinics or referred by collaborating clinicians. A flyer with a brief summary of the study was used to recruit potential participants. The following criteria were used to select participants – (1) Singapore residents (including Singapore Citizens and Permanent Residents), (2) aged 21 years and above, (3) being the primary informal caregiver to a PWD; and (4) able to communicate in either English, Mandarin or Malay. Caregivers were excluded if they had difficulty in understanding the informed consent (as a mechanism to screen their cognitive levels) or if they failed to visit the PWD on a weekly basis. Data were collected via interviewer-administered questionnaire in their preferred language, this is also to ensure their understanding. In total, 282 caregivers completed their assessments between Jan 2017 to Dec 2018. After excluding one participant who skipped questions on coping strategies, the total sample included in the current study was 281. More details of this study are documented elsewhere4,19,24.

The study was approved by the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board in Singapore (reference number: 2016/00921). The study was conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to the data collection.

Measurements

Caregiving stressors

In the current study, caregiving-related stressors mainly refer to the functional dependence and behavioral problems of the PWD, as well as the weekly hours spent on caregiving reported by the caregivers. Functional dependence was measured by the Activities of Daily Living Scale (ADL)25 and the Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale (IADL)26. The number of endorsed ADLs and IADLs were summed up to get a total functional dependence score27. The memory and behaviour problems of PWD was assessed by the memory (7 items) and behavior disruption (8 items) domains of the Revised Memory and Behaviour Problems Checklist (RMBPC)28. In the current study, the internal reliability of the two subscales together was 0.74. Lastly, weekly hours spent on caregiving were collected via asking them how much time they spent on taking care of the PWD during the past week.

Appraisal-related measurements

Caregiving self-efficacy was proposed as the core component of primary appraisal and it was assessed by the Revised Scale for Caregiving Self-efficacy29. This scale has 15 items in total, and it covers three components including self-efficacy for obtaining respite, for responding to disruptive patient behaviours, and for controlling upsetting thoughts about caregiving. Individuals were asked to rate their level of confidence (from 0 to 100%) in performing each activity under the above-mentioned three domains. According to our previous analysis, the original factor structure proposed by the developer had acceptable fit for our sample, and the internal reliability statistics of the three domains varied from 0.893 to 0.921 30.

Caregiver burden was selected to represent the stress after the two appraisal processes and it was measured using the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI)31. ZBI consists of 22 items on the perception of caregiving, with the responses to each item coded on a scale from 0 (never) to 4 (nearly always). In our previous analysis, we had confirmed a 3-factor structure of 17 items among the current sample4, and this version was used in the final analysis.

Available coping resources

Caregivers’ dementia knowledge was assessed using the dementia knowledge assessment scale (DKAS)32. In our previous analysis, we found that the original 4-factor structure was not suitable, instead another 3-factor structure with a total of 23 items was more suitable among the current sample33. As such this 23-item version was used in our analysis.

Positive aspects of caregiving was measured by the positive aspects of caregiving scale (PAC)34. This scale has a total of 9 items, and each of them is scored on a 5-point Likert scale from ‘1-disagree a lot’ to ‘5-agree a lot’, with a total score ranging from 9 to 45. A higher score reflects a more positive perception of the caregiver’s experiences. In our previous analysis, this scale fit well among the current sample; and its internal reliability was 0.883 35.

Perceived social support was assessed through the 8-item perceived Social Support Scale from Pearlin et al.36. Content of this scale covered topics on trust, caring from others, ability to confide in someone, and expression of support to caregiver by others36. Individuals were required to rate on a 4-point Likert, from ‘1-strongly agree’ to ‘4-strong disagree’. The total score of the scale ranged from 4 to 32, with a higher score indicating higher perceived social support. This scale has been used in Singapore before, showed very good internal reliability (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.90)37. In the current study, its internal reliability was 0.779.

Coping patterns

Coping strategies were assessed using the 28-item Brief COPE38. This scale consists of 14 subscales, including active coping, planning, positive reframing, acceptance, humour, religion, emotional support, instrumental support, self-distraction, denial, venting, substance use, behavioural disengagement, and self-blame. Each subscale is measured by two items, using a 4-point Likert scale ranging from ‘1 = I haven’t been doing this at all’ to ‘4 = I’ve been doing this a lot’. In the current study, since we our focus was on the concept of coping patterns, we did not calculate the item or domain scores. Instead, the concept of coping patterns from our previous publication19 were adopted in the current analysis, including low-coping (i.e., relying mainly on acceptance coping strategies and very minimally on any other coping strategies), medium-coping (i.e., using all different coping strategies but less frequently) and high-coping patterns (i.e., using all different coping strategies frequently). Since only caregivers in the low-coping pattern group showed significant difference on depressive symptom compared with the other two groups (medium- & high-coping) from our previous study19, we combined the other two groups in the current study.

Mental health outcome

Depressive symptoms among caregivers of PWD was the final outcome in the proposed model and it was measured by the 20-item Centre for Epidemiological Study Scale (CES-D)39. Caregivers were asked to rate their feelings in the past week on a 4-point Likert scale (ranging from 0 to 3, with ‘0’ representing ‘rarely or none of the time and ‘3’ representing ‘most or all of the time). The total score ranges from 0 to 60, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. In the current study, a CES-D score of 16 and above was categorized as having potential depression40–42. The internal reliability of CES-D in this study was 0.91 19.

Additionally, we also collected socio-demographic information of the caregivers, such as their age, gender, ethnicity, education level, marital status, and employment status; and a few caregiving variables like their relationship to the PWD and having a domestic helper or not.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V29 43, to analyze and present the sample characteristics. To test the hypothetical model, a series of path analyses were conducted using the Lavaan package in R 4.2.0 44. The analysis was conducted in the following steps – 1) a path analysis was run to test the hypothesized model shown in Figs. 1 and 2) another path analysis was run after checking the modification index to incorporate reasonable adjustments to refine the model; 3) a last path analysis was run after removing the insignificant paths. Criteria for good model fit were set at (1) the comparative fit index (CFI) > 0.95; (2) the Tucker-Lewis index (TLI) > 0.95; and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) < 0.06 45.

The path analysis of the original model which purely reflected our assumptions to the transactional stress and coping model (model 1) resulted in a poor model fit (χ2(df) = 93.500 (19), CFI = 0.858, TLI = 0.665, RMSEA = 0.118, and SRMR = 0.085). As such we checked the modification index and incorporated the following paths which were logically reasonable – (1) functional dependence and memory and behavioural problems could directly lead to caregiving burden; (2) weekly hours spent on caregiving had a direct effect on caregiving burden and depressive symptoms; (3) self-efficacy could affect depressive symptoms directly; and (4) the correlation between memory and behavioural problems and dementia knowledge. The modified model was in line with our hypotheses, and the second path analysis (model 2) yielded a good model fit (χ2(df) = 19.840 (13), CFI = 0.987, TLI = 0.955, RMSEA = 0.043, and SRMR = 0.046). The model fit improvements were significant compared to model 1 (Δχ2(df) = 73.659(6), P < 0.001). In the final model (model 3), we removed the insignificant paths from model 2, and the results were good (χ2(df) = 23.149 (18), CFI = 0.990, TLI = 0.974, RMSEA = 0.033, and SRMR = 0.049). Discussion

This study largely confirmed the modified transactional stress and coping model we proposed among informal caregivers of PWD - this includes the key role of caregiver self-efficacy in the primary appraisal process, the dynamic assessment of coping resources across all stages of the model, and the importance of coping pattern. Most of the hypothesized relationships were supported across the sample, with some minor adjustments on the relationships between certain variables in the tested models.

The key role of caregiving self-efficacy in primary appraisal

Our study confirmed the key role of caregiving self-efficacy in the primary appraisal process. More specifically, caregiving self-efficacy partially mediated the relationship between caregiving stressors and caregiving resources with caregiving burden, with direct relationships between caregiving stressors and caregiving burden as well. The definition of self-efficacy among family caregivers concerned their ability to manage behaviours and other caregiving stress, control upsetting thoughts, assist with ADL and IADL, access community supports etc46. In the current model, caregiving stressors represented the environmental factors while caregiving resources contributed to their internal assessment of their own abilities, and the interactions between these two led to the outcome of the appraisal. However, threatening stressors that fell beyond the existing resources would still lead to caregiver stress (caregiving burden in our model). This is consistent with descriptions of the transactional stress and coping model10.

The dynamic resources assessment process

As hypothesized, the assessment of available resources among informal caregivers of PWD was a dynamic process. This could be seen in our third path model. In this model, we found that coping resources including PAC, social support and low coping pattern contributed to all stages of the model and that different resources also worked differently – (1) PAC, social support and low coping pattern were positively associated with caregiving self-efficacy during the primary appraisal process; (2) PAC and low coping pattern were negatively associated with caregiving burden; and (3) social support and low coping pattern were negatively associated with caregiver reported depressive symptoms. Unexpectedly, dementia knowledge was only positively associated with caregiving burden in the final model. There are two possible explanations – firstly, in our model there were significant correlations between the memory and behaviour problems of PWD and caregivers’ knowledge level, suggesting the possibility that caregivers’ knowledge may indeed stem from their experiencing these problems. This is consistent with findings from qualitative studies which suggested that knowledge of dementia and caregiving was indeed one of the major gains from their experiences of providing care to PWD16,47,48. Second, the items included in the DKAS were mainly about the misconceptions and symptoms of dementia33. There were some items about caregiving consideration, such as the importance of exercise and planning for end-of-life care, but knowledge of these items could not help caregivers to cope with the behavioral problems of PWD and the burden associated with them (the single most important stressor). These are just our assumptions; future studies especially mixed methods studies are needed to further investigate this issue. Nevertheless, our study highlighted the limitations of the existing DKAS assessment tool32,33, and it might be helpful to include items on knowledge about coping with caregiving stressors and self-care in the assessment tool and to re-evaluate the relationship between knowledge on dementia and caregiving with self-efficacy, caregiving burden and depressive symptoms.

The importance of coping patterns

Coping patterns – as a concept to characterise how caregivers would respond towards challenging scenarios – as expected, play an important role across all stages of the modified transactional stress and coping model. More specifically, low-coping pattern was positively associated with caregiving self-efficacy, and negatively associated with caregiving burden and depressive symptoms among informal caregivers. In our previous analysis, we found that caregivers with low-coping patterns indeed tend to rely on coping strategies including acceptance while rarely using other coping strategies19. The current study further corroborates this point and emphasizes the importance of acceptance in the scenarios of dementia caregiving. Considering the potential effectiveness of acceptance and commitment therapy in this aspect, we believe such training might be beneficial to informal dementia caregivers as well49.

There are other interesting findings. First, weekly caregiving hours reported by caregivers were not associated with self-efficacy. Instead, they were significantly associated with caregiving burden and depressive symptoms. The caregiving self-efficacy measure has three domains, including obtaining respite, responding to disruptive behavior, and controlling upsetting thoughts29; none of these appear to be related to weekly caregiving hours - respite services may help reduce their caregiving hours, but obtaining respite here is more about the ability to access the respite service, these two are different concepts. Nevertheless, as informal caregivers are usually very busy caring for their PWD19, it could be helpful to increase their access to respite services (including providing financial subsidies) and to teach them how to access such services. Second, in addition to the indirect effect of caregiving self-efficacy on depressive symptoms via caregiving burden, it was also directly negatively associated with depressive symptoms, suggesting that for caregivers of PWD, fostering caregiving self-efficacy could also positively affect their lives. Lastly, though functional dependence was negatively associated with caregiving self-efficacy, it was also negatively associated with caregiving burden in the current study. This is contradictory to our expectations. One possible explanation was that PWD who showed more functional dependence, may be at a later stage of their disease with less behavioural problems. This can be partially seen from our previous analysis which found that PWD at the severe stage tended to show high functional dependence and fewer memory and behavioral issues, and this group accounted for 32% of our sample24. However, this is just an assumption, future qualitative studies are needed to further understand this phenomenon.

The findings of our study also have practical implications. First, our study highlighted the importance of caregiving self-efficacy for informal caregivers of PWD50, as its impact is not only related to caregiving but also to their overall life. Secondly, the building of self-efficacy could come from different channels as multiple components help to increase self-efficacy levels. In this case, the intervention focusing on this aspect should be comprehensive, as this might achieve the best effects. Our study, on the other hands, also provided the theoretical evidence on how to design such interventions. Third, our study also revisited the role of coping strategies during this process and led to the point that coping patterns are a more realistic concept during this process - especially the low-coping pattern (reliance on acceptance while rarely using other coping strategies)19. Interventions are needed to promote this awareness among informal caregivers and to encourage caregivers to gradually shift to the low-coping pattern rather than focusing on teaching positive coping strategies.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to incorporate self-efficacy theory into transactional stress and coping theory to better understand stress among informal caregivers of PWD. It is also the first to incorporate the concepts of dynamic resource appraisal and coping patterns. However, it is important to note the limitations of the current study. First, this is a cross-sectional study, which does not allow us to draw conclusions about causal relationships. Future longitudinal studies are needed to further confirm the relationships tested in this study. One approach could be to collect data at two time points, with an interval of one year, and then determine the temporal order of different variables. Secondly, our study sample was primary informal caregivers of PWD in Singapore, which may limit its applicability to other populations. Finally, because convenience sampling was used, there may be selection bias. Nevertheless, our study provided informative insights on how to best develop future interventions for them.

Conclusion

This study tested a modified transactional stress and coping model among informal caregivers of PWD in Singapore and largely confirmed (1) the key role of caregiver self-efficacy in the primary appraisal process, (2) the dynamic assessment of coping resources across all stages of the model, and (3) the importance of coping patterns. The findings of this study further emphasises the importance of building caregiving self-efficacy for informal caregivers and highlights the different components that may contribute to it. Future studies could consider testing our findings among caregivers in other scenarios to understand the generalisability of these findings. Researchers might also consider developing multi-component interventions based on our study to improve caregiving self-efficacy among informal caregivers and test the impact of such interventions on caregiving burden and mental health outcomes.
