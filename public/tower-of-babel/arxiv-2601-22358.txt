Capacity of Two-User Wireless Systems Aided by Movable Signals
Matteo Nerini, Bruno Clerckx

Abstract
Movable signals have emerged as a third approach to enable smart radio environments (SREs) , complementing reconfigurable intelligent surfaces (RISs) and flexible antennas. This paper investigates their potential to enhance multi-user wireless systems. Focusing on two-user systems, we characterize the capacity regions of the multiple access channel (MAC) and broadcast channel (BC) . Interestingly, movable signals can dynamically adjust the operating frequency to orthogonalize the user channels, thereby significantly expanding the capacity regions. We also study frequency optimization, constraining it in a limited frequency range, and show that movable signals provide up to 45% sum rate gain over fixed signals.



I
Introduction

Smart radio environments (SREs)
promise major advances in wireless communications by allowing the channel to be shaped and treated as an optimization variable
[
1
,
2
]
.
The main enabling technology of
SREs
is
reconfigurable intelligent surface (RIS)
, extensively investigated in recent years
[
3
]
.
A
RIS
consists of an array of reflecting elements with reconfigurable
electromagnetic (EM)
properties that can be tuned to manipulate impinging wavefronts.
By deploying a
RIS
in the environment and adjusting its
EM
properties, we can shape the propagation conditions, enhancing desired signals while mitigating interference.

SREs
enabled by
RIS
rely on modifying the
EM
characteristics of the environment and can therefore be referred to as
EM
-domain
SREs
.

A second approach for enabling
SREs
operates in the space domain, by adjusting the distances between transmit antennas, receive antennas, and nearby objects through flexible antennas.
Several flexible antenna technologies have been proposed, including fluid and movable antennas, which exploit the fact that even sub-wavelength displacements can lead to substantially different fading realizations in rich scattering environments
[
4
,
5
]
.
To provide greater adaptability, pinching antennas allow the antennas to be repositioned along a waveguide
[
6
]
.

A third approach for enabling
SREs
lies in the frequency domain.
Beyond the
EM
properties of objects in the environment and the spatial distances, the signal frequency also constitutes a controllable parameter of the wireless channel.
Recent work has introduced movable signals, a technique in which the signal spectrum can be “moved” along the frequency axis to reshape the channel
[
7
,
8
]
.
Unlike conventional systems, where the frequency is selected within a relatively narrow range, with movable signals the frequency is reconfigured within a much wider range (e.g.,
[f_{\text{min}},f_{\text{max}}]
with
f_{\text{max}}=1.8f_{\text{min}}
), resulting in significant channel shaping capabilities.
Compared to
RISs
, movable signals do not rely on reconfiguring the
EM
properties of surrounding surfaces but instead exploit the frequency selectivity of the environment.
Compared to flexible antennas, movable signals do not alter distances but instead the wavelength, inducing similar channel variations.
Movable signals therefore offer a third appealing approach for enabling
SREs
, requiring the sub-bands available for transmission to be suitably distributed along the frequency spectrum (such as through carrier aggregation
[
9
]
), without the need for reconfigurable or movable hardware.

Previous work on movable signals has shown that dynamically reconfiguring the operating frequency enables effective beam-steering capabilities for serving a receiver in
line-of-sight (LoS)

[
7
,
8
]
.
Inspiring related works also studied the frequency-dependent beamforming capabilities of leaky-wave antennas and arrays equipped with
true time delay (TTD)
devices
[
10
,
11
,
12
,
13
]
.
This property has been leveraged to serve multiple users by assigning each user a distinct frequency, either via leaky-wave antennas
[
10
,
11
]
or via arrays including
TTD
devices
[
12
,
13
]
.
However, serving multiple users via orthogonal frequency channels is known to be suboptimal.
How to simultaneously serve multiple users on the same frequency, assumed to be reconfigurable with high flexibility, remains less explored.

In this paper, we fill this gap by studying the fundamental limits of two-user systems aided by movable signals, in which the users operate on the same frequency.

First
, we characterize the capacity region of wireless systems where a multi-antenna base station serves two users in
LoS
using movable signals.
We show that movable signals can make the two channels orthogonal by suitably choosing the operating frequency, therefore achieving a highly enhanced capacity region.

Second
, we provide transmission strategies allowing us to exactly achieve the boundary of the capacity region, considering both the uplink and the downlink, namely the
multiple access channel (MAC)
and the
broadcast channel (BC)
.

Third
, we show how to reconfigure the operating frequency when it is constrained within a limited frequency range.
Numerical results show that, even under a constrained frequency range, movable signals can approach the sum-rate capacity and significantly outperform conventional systems.
In contrast to conventional frequency allocation over a narrow range that cannot meaningfully alter the
LoS
channel, movable signals enable channel orthogonalization and substantial capacity gains.

II
System Model

In this section, we present the system model.
We first introduce the two-user uplink and downlink transmission models, also referred to as
MAC
and
BC
, respectively, and then describe the wireless channel model, assumed to be
LoS
as it is relevant in millimeter waves and terahertz communications.

Fig. 1:
Two-user multiple access channel (MAC).

For the uplink, we consider a
MAC
where
K=2
single-antenna users transmit to an
N
-antenna base station, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
The transmitted signals are denoted as
x_{ul,k}\in\mathbb{C}
, and are subject to the power constraints
\mathbb{E}[|x_{ul,k}|^{2}]=P_{k}
, where
P_{k}
is the transmitted signal power of user
k
, for
k=1,2
.
Therefore, the received signal
\mathbf{y}_{ul}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
is given by

\mathbf{y}_{ul}=\sum_{k=1}^{2}\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}x_{ul,k}+\mathbf{n}_{ul},

(1)

where
\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
is the wireless channel between user
k
and the base station, for
k=1,2
, and
\mathbf{n}_{ul}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
is the
additive white Gaussian noise (AWGN)
at the base station with noise power
\sigma^{2}
.

For the downlink, we consider the corresponding
BC
, where an
N
-antenna base station transmits to
K=2
single-antenna users.
The transmitted signal is denoted as
\mathbf{x}_{dl}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
, and is subject to the power constraint
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}_{dl}\|^{2}]=P
, where
P
is the transmitted signal power.
In general, we have
\mathbf{x}_{dl}=\mathbf{x}_{dl,1}+\mathbf{x}_{dl,2}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{dl,1},\mathbf{x}_{dl,2}\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 1}
are two statistically independent signals intended for the two users, subject to
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}_{dl,1}\|^{2}]=P_{1}
and
\mathbb{E}[\|\mathbf{x}_{dl,2}\|^{2}]=P_{2}
, with
P_{1}+P_{2}=P
.
Accordingly, the received signal at user
k
, which is denoted as
y_{dl,k}\in\mathbb{C}
, writes as

y_{dl,k}=\mathbf{h}_{dl,k}\mathbf{x}_{dl}+n_{dl,k},

(2)

where
\mathbf{h}_{dl,k}\in\mathbb{C}^{1\times N}
is the wireless channel between the base station and user
k
, and
n_{dl,k}\in\mathbb{C}
is the
AWGN
at user
k
with noise power
\sigma^{2}_{k}
, for
k=1,2
.

To model the wireless channels
\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}
and
\mathbf{h}_{dl,k}
, we assume that the atenna array at the base station is a
uniform linear array (ULA)
located along the
x
-axis and centered in
x=0
, such that the
n
th transmitting antenna has
x
coordinate

x_{n}=(n-(N+1)/2)d_{A}
,
where
d_{A}
is the antenna spacing.
We denote as
d_{k}
the distance between user
k
and the center of the base station array, and as
\theta_{k}\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
the angle of the user
k
direction with respect to the base station array normal, as show in Fig.
1
.
Hence, the distance between user
k
and antenna
n
at the base station is

d_{n,k}=d_{k}-\left(n-\frac{N+1}{2}\right)d_{A}\sin\left(\theta_{k}\right),

(3)

assuming far-field propagation.
Depending on
d_{n,k}
, the entries of the uplink channels
\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}
and
\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}
are given by
\left[\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}\right]_{n}=e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d_{n,k}}
, where
\lambda=c/f
is the wavelength,
c
the speed of light, and
f
the frequency.
Following (
3
), we can write more explicitly

\left[\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}\right]_{n}=e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\left[d_{k}-\left(n-\frac{N+1}{2}\right)d_{A}\sin\left(\theta_{k}\right)\right]},

(4)

for
n=1,\ldots,N
and
k=1,2
.
In a system aided by movable signals, the frequency
f
can be dynamically optimized to shape the wireless channels.
The downlink channels are
\mathbf{h}_{dl,k}=\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}^{T}
, for
k=1,2
, assuming that channel reciprocity holds between the uplink and the downlink.

III
Capacity of Multiple Access Channel

In this section, we derive the capacity region of the two-user
MAC
aided by movable signals described in Section
II
.
This region is defined as the set of all rate pairs
(R_{1},R_{2})
such that users
1
and
2
can simultaneously and reliably communicate at rates
R_{1}
and
R_{2}
, respectively
[
14
, Chapter 12]
.

III-A

Characterization of the Capacity Region

We begin by noticing that the rate of user
k
is upper bounded by its rate in a single-user
single-input multiple-output (SIMO)
system, i.e.,

\displaystyle R_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}\det\left(\mathbf{I}_{N}+\frac{P_{k}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}^{H}\right)

(5)

\displaystyle=\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P_{k}}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}\right)

(6)

\displaystyle=\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P_{k}}{\sigma^{2}}N\right),

(7)

for
k=1,2
, where in (
6
) we used Sylvester’s determinant theorem and in (
7
) we exploited
\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}=\|\mathbf{h}_{ul,k}\|^{2}=N
.
In addition, the sum rate
R_{1}+R_{2}
is upper bounded by the rate achievable when the two users “cooperate” forming a two-antenna array, where antenna
k
has a power constraint
P_{k}
, for
k=1,2
.
By introducing the
multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO)
channel
\mathbf{H}_{ul}=[\mathbf{h}_{ul,1},\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}]\in\mathbb{C}^{N\times 2}
and the transmit covariance matrix
\mathbf{Q}_{ul}=\text{diag}(P_{1},P_{2})\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 2}
, such an upper bound is given by

\displaystyle R_{1}+R_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}\det\left(\mathbf{I}_{N}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{H}_{ul}\mathbf{Q}_{ul}\mathbf{H}_{ul}^{H}\right)

(8)

\displaystyle=\log_{2}\det\left(\mathbf{I}_{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{Q}_{ul}\mathbf{H}_{ul}^{H}\mathbf{H}_{ul}\right).

(9)

Therefore, the capacity region is bounded by the constraints in (
7
) and (
9
).

To enhance the capacity region with movable signals, we observe that the upper bound in (
9
) is globally maximized when the channels
\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}
and
\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}
are orthogonal, i.e., when
\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}=0
.
In this case, (
9
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle R_{1}+R_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}\det\left(\mathbf{I}_{2}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}P_{1}N&0\\
0&P_{2}N\end{bmatrix}\right)

(10)

\displaystyle=\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P_{1}N}{\sigma^{2}}\right)+\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P_{2}N}{\sigma^{2}}\right),

(11)

becoming equivalent to (
7
).
Therefore, if it were possible to reconfigure the signal frequency such that
\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}=0
, the capacity region would be bounded solely by (
7
).

Interestingly, it is possible to orthogonalize the channels
\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}
and
\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}
by reconfiguring the signal frequency, as shown in the following.
Since we want to set the frequency such that
\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}=0
, we begin by explicitly writing
\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}
as a function of the wavelength
\lambda
as

\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left(e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\left[d_{2}-\left(n-\frac{N+1}{2}\right)d_{A}\sin(\theta_{2})\right]}\right.\\
\left.\times e^{-j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}\left[d_{1}-\left(n-\frac{N+1}{2}\right)d_{A}\sin(\theta_{1})\right]}\right),

(12)

where we applied (
4
), which can be simplified as

\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}=e^{j\alpha}\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}nd_{A}\left(\sin\left(\theta_{1}\right)-\sin\left(\theta_{2}\right)\right)},

(13)

where
\alpha
is a constant independent on the index
n
, defined as

\alpha=2\pi(d_{2}-d_{1}+(N+1)d_{A}(\sin(\theta_{2})-\sin(\theta_{1}))/2)/\lambda
.
Looking at (
13
), we notice that since
e^{j\alpha}\neq 0
for any value of
\lambda
, we have
\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}=0
if and only if

\sum_{n=1}^{N}e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}nd_{A}\left(\sin\left(\theta_{1}\right)-\sin\left(\theta_{2}\right)\right)}=0,

(14)

or, equivalently,

\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}nd_{A}\left(\sin\left(\theta_{1}\right)-\sin\left(\theta_{2}\right)\right)}=0.

(15)

Therefore, we can exploit the property of finite geometric series
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}r^{n}=(1-r^{N})/(1-r)
, for any
r\neq 1
, to rewrite (
15
) as

\frac{1-e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}Nd_{A}\left(\sin\left(\theta_{1}\right)-\sin\left(\theta_{2}\right)\right)}}{1-e^{j\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d_{A}\left(\sin\left(\theta_{1}\right)-\sin\left(\theta_{2}\right)\right)}}=0,

(16)

which is satisfied under two conditions.

First, the numerator in (
16
) must be zero, i.e., we must have

\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}Nd_{A}\left(\sin\left(\theta_{1}\right)-\sin\left(\theta_{2}\right)\right)=2\pi L,

(17)

with
L\in\mathbb{Z}
.
The optimal wavelength should therefore be set as a function of the angles
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
as
\lambda^{\star}=Nd_{A}|\sin(\theta_{1})-\sin(\theta_{2})|/L
, with
L\in\mathbb{N}
.
Equivalently, the signal frequency should be set as

f^{\star}=\frac{Lf_{A}}{N\left|\sin\left(\theta_{1}\right)-\sin\left(\theta_{2}\right)\right|},

(18)

with
L\in\mathbb{N}
, where we have introduced
f_{A}=c/d_{A}
, indicating that there are infinitely many possible values of
f^{\star}
, depending on the values of
L
.

Second, the denominator in (
16
) must be different from zero, i.e., we need

\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}d_{A}\left(\sin\left(\theta_{1}\right)-\sin\left(\theta_{2}\right)\right)\neq 2\pi M,

(19)

with
M\in\mathbb{Z}
, which requires
\theta_{2}\neq\theta_{1}
and

f^{\star}\neq\frac{Mf_{A}}{\left|\sin\left(\theta_{1}\right)-\sin\left(\theta_{2}\right)\right|},

(20)

with
M\in\mathbb{N}
.
This means that the two channels can be made orthogonal by adjusting the frequency only when
\theta_{2}\neq\theta_{1}
, which is intuitive since if the channels are perfectly aligned, they remain aligned for any frequency value.
Besides, the optimal frequency must be a multiple of
f_{A}/(N|\sin(\theta_{1})-\sin(\theta_{2})|)
but not a multiple of
f_{A}/|\sin(\theta_{1})-\sin(\theta_{2})|
, otherwise the
N
additive complex terms in
\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}
have all the same phase and
|\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}|=N
.

III-B

Achievability of the Capacity Region

We have seen that by dynamically setting the signal frequency to
f^{\star}
fulfilling (
18
) and (
20
), the capacity region is only constrained by
R_{k}\leq\log_{2}(1+P_{k}N/\sigma^{2})
, for
k=1,2
.
To exactly achieve
R_{k}=\log_{2}(1+P_{k}N/\sigma^{2})
, for
k=1,2
, both users can transmit with their respective powers
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
on the frequency
f^{\star}
.
Then, since
f^{\star}
makes the two channels
\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}
and
\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}
orthogonal, the base station can recover the two transmitted symbols by applying matched filtering to the received signal
\mathbf{y}_{ul}
.
In detail, the base station computes the signal used for detection
\mathbf{z}=[z_{1},z_{2}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 1}
as

\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{H}_{ul}^{H}\mathbf{y}_{ul},

(21)

and
z_{k}
is used to detect the transmitted signal
x_{ul,k}
with a rate
R_{k}=\log_{2}(1+P_{k}N/\sigma^{2})
.
The resulting capacity region is shown in Fig.
2
, where we fix
P_{1}/\sigma^{2}=P_{2}/\sigma^{2}=10
dB and consider three different values of
N
.

As a final remark, we observe that the sum rate capacity, defined as the maximum sum rate
R_{1}+R_{2}
achievable in this
MAC
system, is given by

C_{ul}=\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P_{1}N}{\sigma^{2}}\right)+\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P_{2}N}{\sigma^{2}}\right).

(22)

IV
Capacity of Broadcast Channel

In this section, we derive the capacity region of the two-user
BC
aided by movable signals introduced in Section
II
.

IV-A

Characterization of the Capacity Region

An upper bound on the rate of any user is achieved by allocating all the power
P
to that user as in a single-user
multiple-input single-output (MISO)
system, namely

\displaystyle R_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}\mathbf{h}_{dl,k}\mathbf{h}_{dl,k}^{H}\right)

(23)

\displaystyle=\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P}{\sigma_{k}^{2}}N\right),

(24)

for
k=1,2
.
By allocating all the power
P
to user
k
, we obtain
R_{k}=\log_{2}(1+PN/\sigma_{k}^{2})
, while the rate of the other user is zero.
These two extreme cases can be bridged by allocating a power
P_{1}
to user 1 and
P_{2}
to user 2, such that
P_{1}+P_{2}=P
, which limits the two rates as

\displaystyle R_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}N\right),

(25)

\displaystyle R_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P-P_{1}}{\sigma_{2}^{2}}N\right).

(26)

Interestingly, this pair of constraints holds tightly when the frequency is reconfigured to orthogonalize the two channels as derived in Section
III
, i.e., such that
\mathbf{h}_{dl,1}\mathbf{h}_{dl,2}^{H}=0
, as shown in the following subsection.
Therefore (
25
) and (
26
) characterize the boundary of the capacity region, where all the points on the boundary can be achieved by using different values of power
P_{1}
.

Capacity region of a two-user
MAC
aided by movable signals, with
P_{1}/\sigma^{2}=P_{2}/\sigma^{2}=10
dB.

IV-B

Achievability of the Capacity Region

To achieve the rate pairs
R_{1}=\log_{2}(1+P_{1}N/\sigma_{1}^{2})
and
R_{2}=\log_{2}(1+(P-P_{1})N/\sigma_{2}^{2})
, the base station can perform matched beamforming with the appropriate power allocation by operating at a frequency
f^{\star}
which fulfills (
18
) and (
20
).
In detail, the base station allocates a power
P_{1}
to the symbol intended for user 1
s_{1}\in\mathbb{C}
, which becomes subject to
\mathbb{E}[|s_{1}|^{2}]=P_{1}
, and the remaining power to the symbol for user 2
s_{2}\in\mathbb{C}
, such that
\mathbb{E}[|s_{2}|^{2}]=P-P_{1}
.
Then, the base station computes the transmitted signal by precoding the two symbols
\mathbf{s}=[s_{1},s_{2}]\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times 1}
as

\mathbf{x}_{dl}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathbf{H}_{dl}^{H}\mathbf{s},

(27)

where
\mathbf{H}_{dl}=[\mathbf{h}_{dl,1}^{T},\mathbf{h}_{dl,2}^{T}]^{T}\in\mathbb{C}^{2\times N}
.
Alternatively, we can equivalently interpret the transmitted signal as
\mathbf{x}_{dl}=\mathbf{x}_{dl,1}+\mathbf{x}_{dl,2}
, where
\mathbf{x}_{dl,1}=\mathbf{h}_{dl,1}^{H}s_{1}/\sqrt{N}
and
\mathbf{x}_{dl,2}=\mathbf{h}_{dl,2}^{H}s_{2}/\sqrt{N}
.
Since
f^{\star}
makes the two channels
\mathbf{h}_{dl,1}
and
\mathbf{h}_{dl,2}
orthogonal, each user can recover its symbol with no interference from the other stream.
Their rates will therefore be
R_{1}=\log_{2}(1+P_{1}N/\sigma_{1}^{2})
and
R_{2}=\log_{2}(1+(P-P_{1})N/\sigma_{2}^{2})
.
The resulting capacity region is shown in Fig.
3
, where we consider
P/\sigma_{1}^{2}=P/\sigma_{2}^{2}=10
dB and three different values of
N
.

The sum rate capacity is achieved by finding the power allocation
P_{1}
for user
1
that solves

\displaystyle\underset{P_{1}}{\mathsf{\mathrm{max}}}\;\;

\displaystyle\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P_{1}}{\sigma_{1}^{2}}N\right)+\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{\left(P-P_{1}\right)}{\sigma_{2}^{2}}N\right)

(28)

\displaystyle\mathsf{\mathrm{s.t.}}\;\;\;

\displaystyle 0\leq P_{1}\leq P.

(29)

By assuming
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sigma_{2}^{2}=\sigma^{2}
for simplicity, (
28
)-(
29
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\underset{P_{1}}{\mathsf{\mathrm{max}}}\;\;

\displaystyle\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P}{\sigma^{2}}N+\frac{P_{1}\left(P-P_{1}\right)}{\sigma^{4}}N^{2}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle\mathsf{\mathrm{s.t.}}\;\;\;

\displaystyle 0\leq P_{1}\leq P,

(31)

giving that
P_{1}
needs to maximize
P_{1}(P-P_{1})
, which is a concave function maximized in
P_{1}=P/2
.
By setting
P_{1}=P_{2}=P/2
, the sum rate capacity is therefore given by

C_{dl}=2\log_{2}\left(1+\frac{P}{2\sigma^{2}}N\right).

(32)

aided by movable signals, with

V
Frequency Optimization for Movable Signals with Limited Frequency Range

We have characterized the capacity region of two-user systems aided by movable signals and showed how to optimally select the signal frequency.
While we have assumed so far that the signal frequency can be arbitrarily set, the frequency is selected within a limited frequency range in practice.
In this section, we show how to select the signal frequency when it is confined within a range
[f_{\text{min}},f_{\text{max}}]
, and assess the performance of movable signals under this practical constraint.

Sections
III
and
IV
have shown that the rates of the two users are jointly maximized when the two channels are orthogonal, both in the uplink and in the downlink.
Therefore, we optimize the signal frequency so as to minimize the cosine similarity between the two channels.
For the uplink, this is formulated as

\underset{f}{\mathsf{\mathrm{min}}}\;\;\left|\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}\right|\;\;\mathsf{\mathrm{s.t.}}\;\;f\in\left[f_{\text{min}},f_{\text{max}}\right].

(33)

while for the downlink the objective is
|\mathbf{h}_{dl,1}\mathbf{h}_{dl,2}^{H}|
.
By expressing
\mathbf{h}_{ul,2}^{H}\mathbf{h}_{ul,1}
as a function of
\lambda=c/f
as discussed in Section
III
, this problem can be equivalently reformulated as

\underset{f}{\mathsf{\mathrm{min}}}\;\;\frac{\left|1-e^{j2Nf\beta}\right|}{\left|1-e^{j2f\beta}\right|}\;\;\mathsf{\mathrm{s.t.}}\;\;f\in\left[f_{\text{min}},f_{\text{max}}\right],

(34)

where we introduced
\beta=\pi d_{A}(\sin(\theta_{1})-\sin(\theta_{2}))/c
, which in turn is equivalent to

\underset{f}{\mathsf{\mathrm{min}}}\;\;\frac{\left|\sin\left(Nf\beta\right)\right|}{\left|\sin\left(f\beta\right)\right|}\;\;\mathsf{\mathrm{s.t.}}\;\;f\in\left[f_{\text{min}},f_{\text{max}}\right],

(35)

following the Euler identity.
By analyzing the objective function
|\sin(Nf\beta)/\sin(f\beta)|
, we observe that all its local minima occur at points where the numerator vanishes while the denominator does not.
Consequently, every local minimum corresponds to a zero of the function and is therefore a global minimum.
If at least one solution
f^{\star}
satisfying (
18
) and (
20
) lies within the interval
[f_{\text{min}},f_{\text{max}}]
, that value is selected, since it perfectly orthogonalizes the channels and globally minimizes the objective function.
Otherwise, since no local minimum of
|\sin(Nf\beta)/\sin(f\beta)|
lies within
[f_{\text{min}},f_{\text{max}}]
, the extreme value theorem implies that the minimum over the interval occurs either in
f_{\text{min}}
or
f_{\text{max}}
.
We therefore compare
|\sin(Nf_{\text{min}}\beta)/\sin(f_{\text{min}}\beta)|
and
|\sin(Nf_{\text{max}}\beta)/\sin(f_{\text{max}}\beta)|
, and select
f_{\text{min}}
if the former is no larger than the latter, while
f_{\text{max}}
otherwise.

VI
Conclusion

We have investigated the fundamental limits of two-user wireless systems aided by movable signals, a recently proposed approach for enabling
SRE
.
By dynamically reconfiguring the operating frequency, movable signals can effectively orthogonalize the user channels and suppress inter-user interference.
Therefore, they enable significantly enhanced capacity regions in both
MAC
and
BC
.
Future work may extend these results to more complex wireless scenarios, such as with more than two users each equipped with multiple antennas, and in
non-line-of-sight (NLoS)
.
