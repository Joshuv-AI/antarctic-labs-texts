Clinician Perspectives of Oxygen Control in Children With Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia at Home: An International Survey of Clinicians From 44 Countries
Authors: Thy A. N. Pham, James X. Sotiropoulos, Abhijeet Rakshasbhuvankar, Vineet Bhandari, Georg M. Schmölzer, R. Kishore Kumar, Satoshi Kusuda, Huayan Zhang, Thu‐Tinh Nguyen, Ratchada Kitsommart, Han‐Suk Kim, Li Ma, Fan Yang, Azanna Ahmad Kamar, Setya Dewi Lusyati, Kee Thai Yeo, Juin Yee Kong, Lloyd Tooke, Dominic Fitzgerald, Kate Gonski, Janneke Dekker, Gina Lim, Victor Javier Lara‐Díaz, Ju Lee Oei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is the most common complication of preterm birth and may lead to difficulties with oxygen control even at home. How oxygenation is managed outside the hospital and in high and low resourced settings is unclear.AimsTo determine clinician perspectives for managing oxygenation after hospital discharge in infants with BPD.MethodsAn opportunistic, anonymous, online survey was developed in English and translated into Mandarin, Vietnamese and Korean. The survey was disseminated three times to neonatal clinicians between December 2022 and August 2023. Responses were classified using the World Bank Income Classification.ResultsOverall, 610 clinicians from 44 countries responded, with 453 (74%, 453/610) from high‐ and upper‐middle‐income countries (HMIC) and 153 (25%, 153/610) from low‐ and lower‐middle‐income countries (LMIC). Most clinicians (84%, 510/610) would advocate discharge on home oxygen therapy (HOT), but this was less possible in LMIC (65% vs. 91%, p < 0.001). Most clinicians perceived HOT to improve growth (73%, 370/510) and neurodevelopment (72%, 366/510). HMIC respondents were more likely to use home oximetry (78% vs. LMIC 67%, p = 0.012). There were wide variations in oxygen saturation targets, pre‐discharge oximetry use, and parameters for weaning. Most clinicians perceived novel technologies such as wearable oximetry with parent‐led oxygen control as important (77%, 460/596) and useful (84%, 499/595) for improving oxygen management at home.ConclusionMost neonatal clinicians would prescribe HOT for infants with BPD but are limited by barriers to sufficient resourcing, especially in LMIC. Evaluation of cost‐effective novel monitoring techniques may improve accessibility and control of HOT.

Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is the most common complication of preterm birth [1]. It is defined as the need for respiratory support, including supplemental oxygen, at 36 weeks postmenstrual age [2]. Despite improvement in preterm infant survival, the rates of BPD have increased [3]. In Australia and New Zealand, up to 80% of surviving preterm infants less than 26 weeks' gestation now develop BPD [4].

Infants with BPD may need prolonged treatment with oxygen but their oxygen needs may fluctuate over time and under different conditions for example, ill‐health. Preterm infants may also have precarious respiratory regulation, with some experiencing intermittent hypoxic events until 43–44 weeks postmenstrual age [5]. However, many are ready for discharge home at an earlier age, for example, 36 weeks postmenstrual age [6] and there are concerns about oxygenation at home because untreated hypoxemic spells increase the risk of poor outcomes including pulmonary hypertension, neurodevelopmental impairment and death [7].

Regulating oxygenation requires adjustment of supplemental oxygen under close monitoring of oxygen saturations (SpO2) with oximetry to prevent the adverse sequelae of clinically undetectable hyperoxia and hypoxia [8, 9, 10]. The NeOProM individual participant data prospective meta‐analysis of 4965 infants from five randomised clinical trials (RCTs) with a median gestation age of 26 weeks found that infants who were nursed in lower SpO2 (85%–89%) were more likely to die or develop necrotising enterocolitis but those in higher SpO2 (91%–95%) were more likely to develop retinopathy of prematurity [11].

Such close monitoring is difficult in the home setting. International recommendations to guide clinicians on managing oxygenation in BPD infants at home vary considerably (Table 1). Home oximetry monitoring for infants discharged with HOT, for example, is not recommended in Australian guidelines [15] and none of the guidelines account for oxygen monitoring for infants with BPD who are not discharged on HOT. A consequence of this variability is treatment non‐compliance and self‐weaning from oxygen without medical guidance, potentially contributing to poor outcomes [17, 18]. Some of the most important barriers to oxygen monitoring are technical. Oximeters are expensive and also wired, which restricts patient movement. Oximetry data cannot be transmitted to the clinicians for real‐time clinical advice. Caregivers develop alarm fatigue [19]. As a result, some babies are hospitalised until they are completely weaned from supplemental oxygen [20] which incurs increased health care costs and displaces the family from their home.

Since management of an infant with BPD is dependent on available technology and local resources, we sought to determine how clinicians in different settings, including high‐ and low‐income countries, managed oxygenation for infants with BPD outside of the home environment. We hypothesised that clinician opinions regarding oxygenation would be similar worldwide, but that their actual practice would be limited by available resources.

Methods

Study Design

An opportunistic online survey was disseminated to clinicians (neonatologists, respiratory physicians, paediatricians and paediatric trainees, pulmonary hypertension specialists) through email lists, established clinician and national networks with Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) [21]. There were 26 multiple choice and 15 optional free‐text items (Appendix A in the Supporting Information). Emails were sent three times between December 2022 and August 2023. The survey was developed in English and back translated into Mandarin, Vietnamese and Korean by native speakers. Anonymous responses were collected online and securely stored on a REDCap database at UNSW, Sydney, Australia. Responses were merged into a common English dataset. We followed the Consensus‐based checklist for Reporting Of Survey Studies (CROSS) reporting guidance (Appendix B in the Supporting Information). The numbers and characteristics of survey non‐responders could not be monitored due to the opportunistic dissemination method employed.

Ethics Considerations

The survey was approved under the low‐or‐negligible risk review pathway by the South Eastern Sydney Local Health District Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC, 2022/01859).

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analysis was conducted using R (R version 4.3.0, https://www.r‐project.org). Descriptive statistics were used to report and present survey results. Univariate null‐hypothesis significant testing was conducted to identify factors associated with certain survey responses. Chi‐squared test (or Fisher's exact for cell counts fewer than 5) was performed for categorical variables. All data were categorical. Two‐tailed statistical significance was denoted at 5% level. Missing data were not imputed.

Data Transformation

Survey responses were analysed based on respondents, country of practice, and World Bank Income Classification of these countries [22]. The World Bank uses the Atlas method to categorise countries using the Gross National Income per capita. Responses were initially classified into high‐income countries (HIC), upper‐middle‐income countries (UMIC), lower‐middle‐income countries (LOMIC) and low‐income countries (LIC). Levels for HIC and UMIC were collapsed into high‐income and upper‐middle‐income countries (HMIC). Levels for LIC and LOMIC were collapsed into low‐income and lower‐middle‐income countries (LMIC). Variables not in the Chinese dataset (n = 18) were excluded from the English, Korean and Vietnamese dataset to later bind the four datasets together for analyses. Responses (n = 4) with undisclosed country of practice were included in total counts but omitted from comparative analyses.

Results

Demographics

A total of 610 survey responses from 44 countries were received. Of the total responses, 453 responses were from HMIC and 153 responses from LMIC (Appendix C in the Supporting Information). Country of practice was not provided by four respondents. There were 54 respondents from Oceania, 43 from North America, 15 from Europe, 452 from Asia, and 42 from the Middle East/Africa. Most respondents were practising neonatologists with more than 10 years' experience (Table 2).

Views on Home Oxygen Therapy

Most clinicians (84%, 510/610) would discharge infants with BPD on HOT but LMIC clinicians were less likely to do so (65% vs. 91%, p < 0.001). Perceived benefits for HOT included better growth (73%, 370/510), neurodevelopment (72%, 366/510), respiratory outcomes (68%, 346/510), feeding (62%, 314/510), and sleep (49%, 248/510) (Figure 1). Most clinicians followed up infants discharged on HOT as outpatients (88%, 530/600) with support from parent education packages (77%, 389/506), home visits and outreach programmes (64%, 326/507), and public health nurses (32%, 163/506).

Both clinicians from HMIC and LMICs provide reasons for managing infants with BPD on HOT, but clinicians from HMICs are more likely to attribute benefits of better growth, better neurodevelopment, less respiratory problems including apnoea and infection, better feeding, better sleep and other reasons than clinicians from LMICs. HMIC, high‐income and upper‐middle‐income countries; LMIC, low‐income and lower‐middle‐income countries.

Oximetry

Most clinicians (98%, 491/501) conducted pre‐discharge oximetry on infants with BPD to determine if they required HOT. Various methods were used, including overnight oximetry (43%, 219/501), spot oximetry, undefined brief (19%, 99/501), or more prolonged (over days and weeks, 56%, 287/501) measurements, car seat challenges (9%, 44/501), and polysomnography (PSG) (5%, 26/501). After hospital discharge, most clinicians (75%, 451/602) would conduct intermittent home oximetry if infants were discharged on HOT, with those from HMIC more likely to use oximetry than those from LMIC (78% versus 67%, p = 0.012). Oximetry data were collected through various means including review of stored oximetry data during outpatient visits (39%, 200/508), via telehealth consultations (17%, 87/508), through downloads sent by parents by post or email (13%, 63/508), and by a variety of other methods, including oral or written records by parents, community nurses, or home care companies (31%, 158/508).

SpO2 Targets

Most clinicians (89%, 450/506) used 24‐h SpO2 trends to adjust HOT, varying from SpO2 < 85% to > 95% (Table 3). SpO2 ranges varied significantly, from 93%–95% (40%, 180/451) to < 85% (0.7%, 3/451) (Figure 2). Clinicians from LMICs were more likely to use lower SpO2 (90%–92%) targets than those from HMIC (93%–95% and > 95%).

Oximetry parameters for weaning, increasing and instituting HOT were variable (Figure 3). These included use of parental feedback from pulse oximeter readings (67%, 342/510), clinical judgement (66%, 337/510), review of continuous home oximetry data (64%, 324/510), spot‐check outpatient oximetry (37%, 187/510), overnight hospital oximetry (20%, 103/510), and PSG (9%, 46/510). Clinicians from LMIC were less likely to use oximetry to adjust HOT.

Clinicians from HMIC and LMIC consider different parameters when deciding how to wean oxygen for infants with BPD on HOT. The most common parameters considered are continuous home oximetry data, clinical and parent feedback from oximetry. Clinicians from HMIC are more likely to consider parameters including spot‐check oximetry, overnight oximetry, polysomnography and parent feedback from the oximeter for weaning off oxygen compared to clinicians from LMIC. HMIC, high‐income and upper‐middle‐income countries; LMIC, low‐income and lower‐middle‐income countries.

Novel Methods for Management of Oxygenation

Most respondents agreed that management of oxygenation was limited by technology, and that cheaper, less intrusive, and more accessible monitoring technologies were required. These included wearable oximeters allowing management of oxygenation to be led by families (77%, 460/596) (Table 4), especially in LMICs (85% versus 75%, p = 0.012) who cited accessibility (72%, 359/499), ease of responding to parents' concerns (75%, 375/499), and ease of adjusting SpO2 (81%, 405/499) as primary factors (Figure 4). However, barriers to using continuous wearable oximetry included concerns about potential caregiver stress, loss of privacy, and difficulties with data storage.

Discussion

We found significant variations in clinical practice for the management of oxygenation for infants with BPD in the home setting. The major barriers to consistent practice were lack of information for optimum SpO2 targets and difficulties in providing accessible and informed monitoring of oxygen levels away from the hospital. BPD is a global problem but respondents from LMICs were particularly limited by lack of resources and were thus less likely to discharge infants on HOT or continue surveillance of infants with BPD at home [23].

Our study shows that clinicians appreciate the potential benefits of HOT but health inequity and deficits in health infrastructure remain an important barrier to implementation. An oxygen cylinder costs approximately USD $62 [24] and an oxygen concentrator ranges from USD $76 to USD $3383 [25]. Whilst the median total cost of HOT versus hospitalisation for the same period is lower (USD $5195 vs. USD $46 920, respectively) [26], infants with BPD on HOT are more likely to also have higher medical costs, including the need for more medications, specialist visits and general health care [27]. LMIC settings may also limit HOT availability because of infrastructure problems, such as the lack of reliable power sources or difficulties in the transport of oxygen cylinders to the patient's home [28].

We found great variability in clinical targets for optimum SpO2, which emphasises the need for more robust data. Ranges for optimum SpO2 varied from < 85% to > 95%, and the majority were based on NeOProM data [11], obtained from in‐hospital monitoring, which is not applicable to the convalescent infant in the home setting.

There were also differences in how oxygenation was monitored in both LMICs and HMICs. Clinicians in LMICs were less likely to use oximetry to guide HOT probably because of the costs of monitoring including access to overnight pulse oximetry or PSG. Despite this, there is little evidence that one method improves outcomes or patient experience more than another [29] and consideration should also be given to an individualised approach including the use of subjective parameters like parental feedback when deciding when or how to wean oxygen.

Finally, most respondents agreed that novel technologies such as wearable oximetry allowing parent‐led control of oxygenation would be acceptable and beneficial for infant outcomes. Our findings support current literature, where pulse oximetry can reassure [15], identify acute clinical deteriorations, assist weaning and mitigate stressors of outpatient care [13]. Rather than view carer stress as a limitation, it should be anticipated and addressed with a comprehensive care bundle for BPD focusing on carer support. Current literature supports home oximetry monitoring for BPD infants on HOT with potential benefits of reassurance [30], convenience and reduction of duration of HOT [31]. However, home oximetry necessitates informed family participation, staffing resources and software, and current technological options lead to a high rate of drop‐outs from families [32].

Limitations

Despite repeated dissemination via clinician networks and international contacts, European and LIC respondents were relatively low. Thus, survey findings may not represent all clinicians from these categories and each country. We used a convenience sampling method and as such, we were unable to assess the characteristics and number of non‐responders. This limits the generalisability of results but may be addressed in future country‐specific surveys. The broad nature of health systems assessed encompasses systems with vastly different populations, infrastructure, and capacities; this further limits the generalisability of results. Several statistical tests were conducted without correction for multiplicity. Statistically significant differences in responses between LMIC and HMIC respondents should be interpreted with caution. Finally, this self‐reported questionnaire could have recall bias as clinicians were asked questions on current practice for BPD infants.

Future Directions

Future country‐ and context‐specific, Europe‐centric, and LMIC‐specific surveys could be considered to better understand barriers to HOT and wearable oximetry, and how to mitigate them in diverse settings. To ensure best practice and outcomes for BPD infants, standardised, consensus‐based international guidelines must be developed, along with investigating new methods of monitoring HOT using novel technologies to assess oxygen levels outside hospital.

Conclusion

The results of this survey demonstrated varied clinician practice between HMIC and LMIC for BPD infants but a consensus that HOT improves outcomes for these infants despite difficulties with monitoring and logistics. Most clinicians want to assess the wellbeing of BPD infants and perceive novel technologies such as wearable pulse oximetry as acceptable and useful. Importantly, these technologies could play a significant role in alleviating carer stress and concerns, improving accessibility and improving supplementary oxygen control for BPD infants at home.
