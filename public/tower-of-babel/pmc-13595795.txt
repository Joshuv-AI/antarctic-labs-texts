Psychosocial burden in pediatric familial mediterranean fever: A study of quality of life, sleep habits, maternal mental health, and clinical correlates
Authors: Sema Nur Taşkın, Tuğba Karaca Ahat, Burcu Bozkaya Yücel, Şeyda Doğantan, Gülçin Otar Yener, Ayşenur Paç Kısaarslan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFamilial Mediterranean Fever (FMF) is a chronic pediatric autoinflammatory disease that imposes not only biological challenges but also notable psychosocial stress on families. However, psychosocial outcomes such as quality of life, sleep habits, and maternal psychological well-being have rarely been evaluated comprehensively in relation to clinical and genetic factors. This multicenter study aimed to provide a holistic assessment of psychosocial outcomes—specifically children’s quality of life, sleep patterns, and maternal mental health—in pediatric FMF and to examine their potential associations with MEFV exon 10 mutation burden, age, and sex.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 155 children aged 5–12 years with FMF and their mothers, recruited from three pediatric rheumatology centers in Türkiye. All assessments were conducted during attack-free periods under regular colchicine treatment. Quality of life was assessed using both the child- and parent-report versions of the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL). Maternal psychological status was evaluated using the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-I/II) and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), while children’s sleep habits were assessed using the Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ). Participants were stratified according to MEFV exon 10 mutation burden (single vs. double pathogenic variants), age group, and sex.ResultsParent-reported PedsQL scores were significantly lower in children carrying homozygous or compound heterozygous MEFV exon 10 mutations than in those with a single exon 10 mutation (54.9 ± 15.5 vs. 61.2 ± 16.2, p = 0.016). Similarly, parent-reported PedsQL scores were lower in girls than in boys (55.2 ± 14.9 vs. 61.0 ± 16.7, p = 0.029). In contrast, child-reported PedsQL scores did not differ according to mutation burden, age group, or sex (all p > 0.05). Maternal STAI-I, STAI-II, and BDI scores and children’s CSHQ scores did not differ according to mutation burden, age group, or sex (all p > 0.05). The discrepancy between parent- and child-reported outcomes suggests that psychosocial outcomes may be perceived differently by parents and children.ConclusionsIn this cohort of clinically stable, attack-free children with FMF, parent-reported health-related quality of life was lower in children with two pathogenic MEFV exon 10 variants and in girls, whereas child-reported health-related quality of life did not differ according to mutation burden, age, or sex. Maternal anxiety and depressive symptom scores and children’s sleep habit scores were also similar across these subgroups. These findings highlight the importance of considering both child and caregiver perspectives when evaluating psychosocial outcomes in pediatric FMF.

Introduction

Familial Mediterranean fever (FMF) is the most common monogenic autoinflammatory disease, predominantly affecting populations in the Mediterranean basin, including Türkiye. It is caused by mutations in the MEFV gene, which encodes the pyrin protein responsible for regulating inflammasome activation and interleukin-1β (IL-1β)–mediated inflammation [1]. FMF typically manifests during childhood and is characterized by recurrent episodes of fever and serositis, accompanied by elevated acute-phase reactants. If left untreated, persistent inflammation may lead to long-term complications [2, 3].

Beyond its characteristic recurrent attacks, FMF has been associated with impaired quality of life, sleep disturbances, and an increased prevalence of anxiety and depressive symptoms in children and adolescents [4–6]. Recurrent pain, fatigue, and the unpredictability of attacks may negatively impact school attendance and social participation. Recent studies have shown that both children with FMF and their parents experience a significant decline in quality of life, with caregivers reporting higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms [4, 7]. Furthermore, sleep disturbances are frequently observed in FMF and have been reported to correlate with a greater prevalence of psychological symptoms [6].

A marked allelic heterogeneity has been identified in the MEFV gene, with the most clinically relevant pathogenic variants located in exon 10 [1]. Homozygous or compound heterozygous exon 10 mutations have been associated with a more severe clinical phenotype. This mutational pattern has been suggested as an indicator of a higher inflammatory burden and disease progression. Homozygosity for the M694V mutation, in particular, has been associated with exaggerated inflammatory responses, increased attack frequency and long-term complications, most notably amyloidosis [2, 8, 9]. However, the influence of MEFV mutation burden—defined as the presence of one versus two pathogenic exon 10 variants—on quality of life, and sleep parameters in children has been scarcely investigated, and its clinical implications remain unclear.

In childhood FMF, psychosocial experiences may differ by age and sex. Younger children are thought to be more vulnerable to psychosocial stressors, whereas girls may report a greater emotional and psychological burden [10]. Accordingly, evaluating clinical and psychosocial outcomes according to age and sex may provide a more comprehensive understanding of the multidimensional impact of FMF on children and their families.

Despite increasing recognition of the psychosocial impact of FMF, studies comprehensively evaluating quality of life, parental psychological status, and sleep behavior in children with FMF remain limited [4, 6, 11, 12]. Psychosocial outcomes in pediatric chronic diseases are increasingly recognized as critical dimensions of health psychology and family-centered care, yet these domains have not been systematically incorporated into FMF research or clinical practice. In particular, exploring the possible relationship between MEFV exon 10 mutation burden and psychosocial as well as functional outcomes is clinically important for the early identification of potentially vulnerable subgroups. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess quality of life, parental anxiety and depressive symptoms, and children’s sleep habits in patients with FMF, and to investigate the associations of these parameters with MEFV exon 10 mutation burden, age, and sex. It was anticipated that a higher mutation burden might be related to less favorable psychosocial and functional outcomes.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted between July 2024 and July 2025 in collaboration with the Pediatric Rheumatology Departments of Eskişehir City Hospital, Başakşehir Çam and Sakura City Hospital, and Samsun Training and Research Hospital. The study included children aged 5–12 years with FMF who fulfilled the Eurofever/PRINTO classification criteria, and whose diagnosis was confirmed by pediatric rheumatologists at each participating center, together with their mothers [13]. For subgroup analyses, participants were categorized into two age groups (5–7 years and 8–12 years). The age range and subgrouping were predefined in the study protocol according to the age-specific validated versions of the PedsQL child self-report questionnaire. The upper age limit of 12 years was selected to maintain a developmentally more homogeneous pediatric cohort and to minimize additional psychosocial heterogeneity associated with adolescence.

To minimize the influence of disease activity and treatment-related confounding factors, only patients who had been diagnosed with FMF for at least six months, had been receiving regular colchicine therapy for at least three months, had remained free of FMF attacks during the preceding three months, had no history of infection during the previous four weeks, and had erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), and serum amyloid A (SAA) values within the normal reference ranges of the participating centers’ laboratories were included. Thus, a clinically stable and relatively homogeneous cohort was intentionally established. All eligible patients who met the inclusion criteria during the study period were consecutively enrolled. No participants were excluded because of missing or incomplete questionnaire data.

The study was approved by the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of Eskişehir City Hospital (Decision No: ESH/BAEK 2024/36, Date: July 17, 2024) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from the parents of all participants.

Data collection and assessment tools

Sociodemographic and clinical data—including age, sex, and MEFV gene mutation status—were obtained from medical records. A set of standardized assessment instruments, well established in the literature and validated for Turkish populations, were used for the evaluations [4, 14–17]. These included the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL 4.0) for assessing children’s quality of life; the State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-I and STAI-II) and the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) for evaluating maternal anxiety and depressive symptoms; and the Children’s Sleep Habits Questionnaire (CSHQ) for assessing children’s sleep behaviors and identifying potential sleep disturbances [18–22].

Pediatric quality of life inventory (PedsQL 4.0)

The PedsQL 4.0 was used to assess children’s health-related quality of life. The PedsQL was administered using age-specific child self-report versions (5–7 and 8–12 years) and maternal proxy-report versions, as appropriate. All PedsQL scores were reverse-scored and linearly transformed according to the standard PedsQL scoring algorithm and are presented on a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating better health-related quality of life. For children aged 5–7 years, the child self-report questionnaire was completed using the standard interviewer-administered format, in which a trained researcher read each item and response options aloud while the child selected the response independently. Children aged 8–12 years completed the child self-report questionnaire independently. The mothers completed the maternal proxy-report questionnaires [14, 18, 19].

State-trait anxiety inventory (STAI-I and STAI-II)

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI), developed by Spielberger et al., was used to assess maternal anxiety [20]. The inventory consists of two 20-item subscales: the State Anxiety Inventory (STAI-I), which assesses anxiety experienced at the time of assessment, and the Trait Anxiety Inventory (STAI-II), which assesses an individual’s general tendency to experience anxiety. Each subscale yields a total score ranging from 20 to 80, with higher scores indicating greater levels of state and trait anxiety, respectively. The scale was adapted and validated for Turkish populations by Öner and Le Compte. The Turkish version has demonstrated good reliability, with reported internal consistency coefficients ranging from 0.83 to 0.87, test–retest reliability coefficients from 0.71 to 0.86, and item reliability coefficients from 0.34 to 0.72 [15].

Beck depression inventory (BDI)

The BDI was used to evaluate maternal depressive symptoms. Higher BDI scores indicate greater severity of depressive symptoms. BDI scores were classified into predefined score ranges as minimal (0–9), mild (10–16), moderate (17–29), and severe (30–63), according to the Turkish-validated version of the inventory [17, 21].

Children’s sleep habits questionnaire (CSHQ)

The CSHQ was used to assess children’s sleep behaviors and identify potential sleep disturbances. Sleep duration, sleep quality, and sleep-related problems were assessed solely by maternal report using the CSHQ. Higher CSHQ total scores indicate poorer sleep habits and greater sleep disturbance. Sleep disturbance was defined as a total CSHQ score > 41, according to the validated Turkish version of the questionnaire [16, 22].

Administration of the assessment instruments

All questionnaires were administered during routine outpatient visits in a face-to-face setting. All questionnaires were completed under the supervision of a trained investigator using a standardized administration procedure across all participating centers. In addition, all completed questionnaires from the participating centers were centrally reviewed by the same developmental pediatrician to ensure consistency in the assessment process. The investigator was available only to clarify procedural questions and did not influence or guide participants’ responses. Participants were informed about the general purpose of the study but were not informed of the specific study hypotheses. Although children were present during the outpatient visit, mothers were instructed to complete the questionnaires independently without discussing the questionnaire items with their children. Only mothers were included in the psychosocial assessments to ensure a more homogeneous study population and minimize variability related to differences in parental caregiving roles and psychological responses.

Genetic analysis

Results of the MEFV gene analysis were retrieved from patients’ medical records. Genetic testing had been carried out using the next-generation sequencing (NGS) method. Based on the mutation status of exon 10 in the MEFV gene, patients were classified into two groups: Group 1 consisted of patients with a single heterozygous exon 10 mutation, whereas Group 2 included those with homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations in the same region.

Statistical analysis

No a priori sample size calculation was performed because all eligible patients meeting the inclusion criteria during the predefined study period were consecutively enrolled. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, while numerical variables were expressed as mean and standard deviation, or as median and minimum–maximum values when appropriate. The normality of the distribution of numerical variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

For comparisons of categorical variables between groups, Chi-square tests were applied. The Pearson Chi-square test was used when expected cell counts were sufficient, whereas the Fisher’s Exact test was used in cases with low expected frequencies. For comparisons of numerical variables between two groups, the Independent Samples t-test was used when parametric assumptions were met, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used otherwise. STAI-I and STAI-II scores were analyzed as continuous variables for group comparisons. BDI scores were analyzed as continuous variables for group comparisons, while predefined BDI score ranges were additionally used for categorical comparisons. Effect sizes were calculated to facilitate the interpretation of group differences. Cohen’s d was used for continuous variables, whereas Cramér’s V (or the phi coefficient for 2 × 2 contingency tables) was used for categorical variables. In addition, a multiple linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between MEFV exon 10 mutation burden and parent-reported PedsQL scores. Parent-reported PedsQL score (0–100 scale) was entered as the dependent variable, while MEFV exon 10 mutation group was included as the primary independent variable, with age group and sex included as prespecified covariates.

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 155 children diagnosed with FMF who carried at least one pathogenic MEFV exon 10 variant were included in the study. Among them, 89 patients (57.4%) had a single heterozygous mutation in exon 10 (Group 1), whereas 66 patients (42.6%) exhibited homozygous or compound heterozygous mutations (Group 2). The mean age of the cohort was 8.7 ± 2.6 years (range, 5–12 years). Fifty-eight children (37.4%) were aged 5–7 years and 97 (62.6%) were aged 8–12 years. A total of 65 participants (41.9%) were female and 90 (58.1%) were male. There were no significant differences in age or sex distribution between mutation groups (p > 0.05; Table 1).

Comparisons according to MEFV exon 10 mutation burden

Parent-reported total PedsQL scores were lower in children carrying two pathogenic exon 10 variants than in those carrying a single pathogenic exon 10 variant (54.9 ± 15.5 vs. 61.2 ± 16.2, p = 0.016, Cohen’s d = 0.40). Child-reported PedsQL scores did not differ according to mutation burden (60.9 ± 14.6 vs. 65.0 ± 16.7, p = 0.108, Cohen’s d = 0.26).

STAI-I (p = 0.205), STAI-II (p = 0.584), BDI (p = 0.104), and CSHQ (p = 0.922) scores did not differ between the single- and double-mutation groups. Likewise, BDI category distribution (p = 0.274) and sleep pattern (p = 0.465) were comparable between the groups (Table 2).

Comparisons by sex

The age distribution was similar between girls and boys (p = 0.603). Parent-reported total PedsQL scores were lower in girls than in boys (55.2 ± 14.9 vs. 61.0 ± 16.7, p = 0.029, Cohen’s d = 0.36). Child-reported PedsQL scores did not differ between the sexes (60.5 ± 15.8 vs. 65.3 ± 15.8, p = 0.063, Cohen’s d = 0.30).

STAI-I (p = 0.615), STAI-II (p = 0.097), BDI (p = 0.411), and CSHQ (p = 0.669) scores were comparable between girls and boys. Likewise, BDI category distribution (p = 0.392) and sleep pattern (p = 0.684) did not differ between the sexes (Table 3).

Multiple linear regression analysis

A multiple linear regression analysis was performed with parent-reported PedsQL score as the dependent variable and MEFV exon 10 mutation group, age group, and sex as independent variables. After adjustment for age group and sex, children with two pathogenic exon 10 variants had significantly lower parent-reported PedsQL scores than those with a single variant (B = − 6.35, 95% CI: −11.46 to − 1.24, p = 0.015). Male sex was associated with higher parent-reported PedsQL scores compared with female sex (B = 5.45, 95% CI: 0.38 to 10.52, p = 0.035), whereas age group was not significantly associated with parent-reported PedsQL score (B = − 1.86, 95% CI: −7.08 to 3.36, p = 0.482). The overall model was statistically significant (R² = 0.069, adjusted R² = 0.050, p = 0.013) (Table 5).

Discussion

In this multicenter study, health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in children with FMF was assessed using both child self-reports and parent (maternal) proxy reports. Our findings demonstrated that parent-reported PedsQL scores differed significantly according to MEFV exon 10 mutation burden and sex. In contrast, child self-reported PedsQL scores did not differ according to mutation burden, age, or sex. This parent–child discrepancy suggests that parent and child reports may capture different aspects of disease burden and psychosocial well-being. Mean maternal STAI-I and STAI-II scores were approximately 46 and 47, respectively, and did not differ according to mutation burden, age group, or sex.

The discrepancy between parent- and child-reported outcomes suggests that maternal assessments may capture different aspects of psychosocialburden than children’s self-reports, a pattern that has also been highlighted in previous pediatric chronic disease studies [23–26]. As only mothers were included in this study, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting maternal perceptions. Furthermore, the association between MEFV exon 10 mutation burden and lower parent-reported HRQoL remained statistically significant after adjustment for age group and sex. Children carrying two pathogenic exon 10 variants had parent-reported PedsQL scores approximately 6.4 points lower than those carrying a single variant.

Health-related quality of life (PedsQL – parent and child reports)

Discrepancies between parental and child assessments are frequently observed in the pediatric chronic disease literature. It has been reported that parents of children with chronic illnesses tend to rate their children’s quality of life lower than the children themselves, and domain-specific inconsistencies between parent and child reports have been documented [23]. MEFV exon 10 mutations are associated with a more severe clinical phenotype, more frequent attacks, higher inflammatory burden, and long-term complications such as amyloidosis [2]. In this context, the lower quality of life scores observed among parents of children carrying two mutations may be related to concerns regarding the potential disease prognosis and treatment course. These findings were further supported by the multiple linear regression analysis, in which double MEFV exon 10 mutation status remained significantly associated with lower parent-reported PedsQL scores after adjustment for age group and sex. Male sex was also associated with higher parent-reported PedsQL scores, whereas age group was not significantly associated with the outcome.

Furthermore, in our study, parent-reported HRQoL scores assessed through the PedsQL parent form also showed significant differences by sex. Parents of boys reported significantly higher quality of life scores compared with those of girls. One possible explanation is that maternal perceptions of children’s health-related quality of life may differ according to the child’s sex. However, parental attitudes toward the child’s sex were not directly assessed in the present study. Therefore, this interpretation should be made with caution. Hijkoop et al. reported that, even among healthy children, both parent- and child-reported PedsQL scores were lower in girls than in boys [24]. Similarly, Muilekom et al. demonstrated that HRQoL scores were lower in pediatric patients with chronic diseases compared to the general population and that female sex was associated with lower HRQoL scores [25].

The lack of significant differences in child self-reported (PedsQL-Child) quality of life scores across mutation burden, age, or sex groups may be explained, at least in part, by developmental factors influencing children’s perception of illness. In younger children, subjective health perception and illness awareness are still developing, which may limit their ability to distinguish subtle differences in health-related quality of life between clinical subgroups [26]. Previous studies have similarly shown that younger children with chronic diseases may not perceive disease-related symptoms and long-term health implications as clearly as their parents [25, 27]. This may contribute to less variation in self-reported quality of life scores. Additionally, the process of “normalization,” whereby chronic illness becomes integrated into everyday life, may contribute to relatively more favorable self-reported quality of life in children. The clinically stable, attack-free status of the cohort and the relatively narrow age range may also have reduced between-group differences in child-reported HRQoL. Although the PedsQL child self-report has been shown to be reliable and valid in children aged 5–7 years when administered using the standard interviewer-administered format, younger children may still have a more limited capacity to recognize, interpret, and communicate subjective aspects of health-related quality of life than older children [14, 26]. Moreover, because children aged 5–7 years completed the PedsQL self-report using the standard interviewer-administered format, whereas those aged 8–12 years completed it independently, direct comparisons of child-reported PedsQL scores between the two age groups should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, the discrepancies observed between parent- and child-reported outcomes should not be interpreted as evidence that parental reports are inherently more sensitive. Rather, parent and child reports likely provide complementary perspectives on children’s health-related quality of life, reflecting differences in developmental stage as well as individual perceptions. Taken together, the observed sex-related differences in parent-reported PedsQL scores may have multiple explanations. One possible explanation is that maternal perceptions of children’s health-related quality of life may vary according to the child’s sex. However, because parental attitudes toward the child’s sex were not directly evaluated in the present study, no conclusions regarding sex-specific parental perceptions can be drawn. Therefore, parent-reported assessments should be interpreted with appropriate caution, taking potential perceptual biases into account to support holistic and child-centered clinical evaluation. Interestingly, significant differences according to mutation burden and sex were observed only in mother-reported PedsQL scores, whereas child-reported quality of life, maternal anxiety and depressive symptoms, and children’s sleep quality did not differ significantly between groups. Although reporting bias associated with maternal proxy reports cannot be excluded, the observed discrepancy is likely multifactorial and cannot be attributed solely to reporting bias.

Anxiety (STAI-I and STAI-II)

Maternal state and trait anxiety scores did not differ according to MEFV exon 10 mutation burden, age group, or sex in our cohort. Existing literature suggests that FMF may have psychological effects not only on affected children but also on their caregivers. Sönmez et al. reported higher levels of anxiety and depressive symptoms among children with FMF compared with healthy peers, with greater psychological burden associated with increasing disease severity [4]. Similarly, Gezgin Yıldırım et al. and Aydın et al. reported substantial stress, anxiety, and caregiver burden among parents, particularly mothers, emphasizing the broader family impact of the chronic and unpredictable course of FMF [11, 28].

In our cohort, maternal anxiety levels were broadly similar across mutation burden and demographic subgroups. Previous studies have suggested that factors such as the chronic nature of the disease, caregiving responsibilities, and uncertainty regarding disease progression may contribute to parental anxiety [4, 11, 28]. However, these factors were not directly assessed in the present study and therefore should be considered possible explanations rather than conclusions derived from our findings. Therefore, incorporating maternal anxiety into clinical evaluations may support the early identification of families at psychosocial risk and contribute to a more comprehensive FMF follow-up strategy.

Depressive symptoms (beck depression inventory)

In our study, maternal depressive symptoms varied in severity, with mean BDI scores falling within the mild-to-moderate predefined score ranges. Minimal, mild, moderate, and severe BDI score ranges were observed in 38%, 25%, 12%, and 25% of participants, respectively. The finding that nearly one-quarter of mothers fell within the severe BDI score range suggests heterogeneity in maternal depressive symptom severity within the cohort. Although mothers of children carrying two pathogenic MEFV exon-10 variants showed a tendency toward higher BDI scores compared with those of children with a single mutation, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Overall, maternal depressive symptoms did not differ significantly according to mutation burden. Previous studies have suggested that caregiver burden, coping difficulties, and uncertainty related to living with a chronic disease may contribute to depressive symptoms among caregivers [11, 29]. However, these factors were not directly assessed in the present study and therefore should be considered as possible explanations rather than conclusions derived from our findings.

Previous studies also support this interpretation. The prevalence of depression among parents of children with FMF has been reported to be significantly higher than that of healthy controls [30]. Additionally, a Turkish adolescent FMF cohort demonstrated that a disease-management education intervention significantly reduced depressive symptoms and improved quality of life in affected adolescents; however, this study assessed depressive symptoms in children rather than in their mothers [5]. Given that FMF is a chronic disease requiring lifelong treatment adherence, these findings suggest that psychosocial factors related to caring for a child with a chronic illness may contribute to depressive symptoms among caregivers [2, 11, 29]. However, caregiver burden, coping strategies, and family stress were not directly assessed in the present study; therefore, these mechanisms should be regarded as possible explanations rather than conclusions derived from our findings. Accordingly, systematic evaluation of parental—particularly maternal—psychological well-being may help identify families who could benefit from additional psychosocial support during FMF management.

Sleep habits

In our cohort, sleep disturbances were found to be common among children with FMF, yet sleep scores did not significantly differ according to sex, age group, or MEFV exon-10 mutation burden. Notably, sleep disturbances were observed even though all participants had remained attack-free during the preceding three months. This finding may suggest that sleep difficulties in FMF are not necessarily confined to periods of overt clinical attacks; however, the cross-sectional design does not allow us to determine their persistence over time or their independence from inflammatory activity. Although children’s emotional and behavioral symptoms were not directly assessed in this study, it is conceivable that factors related to chronic disease experience—such as anticipatory worry about future attacks, family-related stress, or caregiving demands—may contribute to the observed sleep difficulties. Future prospective studies incorporating psychological assessments of both children and parents will be essential to clarify these potential mechanisms. However, sleep disturbances are also relatively common in the general pediatric population, particularly among school-aged children and adolescents [31]. Therefore, the high prevalence of disturbed sleep observed in our cohort should be interpreted within this broader context and should not be attributed solely to FMF. Developmental, behavioral, and environmental factors that were not evaluated in the present study may also have contributed to the observed sleep disturbances.

In children diagnosed with FMF, impaired sleep quality has been reported to negatively affect fatigue levels and health-related quality of life; furthermore, the chronic disease burden appears to extend beyond the patient and also impacts the parents’ quality of life and psychological well-being [12, 32]. In a large cohort study conducted by İncesu et al., the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index scores of children with FMF were found to be significantly higher than those of healthy controls, and a strong negative correlation was observed between fatigue level and sleep quality. However, no significant differences in sleep or fatigue scores were detected according to MEFV mutation type (homozygous, heterozygous, or compound heterozygous) [32]. Similarly, Makay et al. reported that sleep problems were significantly more frequent in children with FMF compared to healthy peers [33]. It is worth noting that Durcan et al. showed that sleep problems in pediatric and adolescent FMF patients were significantly associated with both anxiety and depression levels, indicating that beyond inflammatory processes, the psychological burden of the disease may also adversely influence sleep quality [6]. Taken together, these findings underscore the necessity of adopting a multidimensional framework that considers both biological and psychosocial contributors when evaluating sleep quality in FMF.

Sleep disturbances may reflect complex interactions among pain, psychosocial factors, and inflammatory processes. Previous studies have demonstrated that poor sleep quality in patients with FMF is associated with disease activity and inflammatory markers, while pediatric evidence supports a close relationship between sleep disturbances and pain in children with chronic illnesses [34, 35]. Therefore, routine assessment of sleep habits and the provision of basic sleep hygiene recommendations, such as maintaining regular sleep schedules and limiting screen use before bedtime, may be considered as part of comprehensive care for children with FMF [36].

Limitations and clinical implications

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and does not capture changes in psychosocial or sleep-related outcomes over the disease course. Psychosocial assessments relied largely on maternal self-report and proxy report, which may introduce reporting bias and shared method variance. Paternal assessments were not included; therefore, the findings primarily reflect maternal perspectives. Sleep was assessed solely by maternal report using the CSHQ, without objective measures such as actigraphy or polysomnography, and no structured psychiatric interview was performed to identify potentially unrecognized psychiatric conditions in children or their mothers. Although restricting enrollment to clinically stable, attack-free patients reduced the potential influence of acute inflammation, it also limits the generalizability of the findings to children with active disease, colchicine-resistant FMF, or psychiatric comorbidities. In addition, a validated FMF disease severity score such as the ISSF was not available, and detailed measures of disease activity, treatment adherence, and caregiver burden were not systematically assessed. The absence of healthy controls and children with FMF without pathogenic exon 10 variants limits comparisons with healthy populations and other FMF genotypes. Similarly, the absence of children with other chronic diseases limits comparisons with broader chronic disease populations. Together, these limitations restrict the generalizability and specificity of the observed psychosocial findings. Finally, residual confounding related to unmeasured clinical, socioeconomic, and disease-related factors cannot be excluded.

Despite these limitations, the multicenter design and inclusion of participants from different regions strengthen the clinical relevance of the study. The findings emphasize the importance of considering psychosocial well-being alongside biological disease control in pediatric FMF. Brief screening for sleep difficulties, maternal psychological symptoms, and broader family psychosocial needs during routine follow-up may help identify children and families who require further evaluation. When clinically indicated, referral to child and adolescent psychiatry, psychology, developmental pediatrics, or other appropriate services may support a practical family-centered approach to care.

Conclusion

This multicenter study demonstrated that parent-reported health-related quality of life was lower in children with two pathogenic MEFV exon 10 variants and in girls, whereas child self-reported health-related quality of life did not differ according to mutation burden, age, or sex. Maternal STAI-I and STAI-II scores did not differ according to mutation burden, age group, or sex. Maternal depressive symptoms and sleep disturbances among children with FMF were also frequently observed. However, these psychosocial parameters did not significantly differ according to mutation burden, age, or sex.

Sleep disturbances were frequently observed in this clinically stable, attack-free cohort, suggesting that sleep-related difficulties may be present even in the absence of overt clinical attacks. However, longitudinal studies with repeated assessments during different phases of disease activity are needed to determine their temporal course and relationship with inflammatory activity. Future studies integrating psychosocial, genetic, and inflammatory factors may further clarify the mechanisms underlying psychosocial outcomes in pediatric FMF.
