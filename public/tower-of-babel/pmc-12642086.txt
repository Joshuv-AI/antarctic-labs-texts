Is sub-endometrial injection a superior method for the administration of intrauterine PRP?: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Parijot Kumar, Ray O’Sullivan, Harriet Walsh, Kevin Marron, Conor Harrity
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeEndometrial platelet-rich plasma (PRP) is proposed to treat thin endometrium and implantation failure. Different techniques are described, and nomenclature is often confusing, with infusion or injection being used interchangeably, not discriminating between methods. Sub-endometrial injection, by hysteroscopic or ultrasound guidance, differs from intracavity infusion, but whether this method is superior has not been subject to review.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was performed to compare sub-endometrial injection against placebo or intra-cavity infusion in a defined assisted reproduction population, with subgroup analysis to assess for any differences between USS-guided or Hysteroscopic Techniques. Primary outcomes were clinical pregnancy (CPR), live birth (LBR), and miscarriage rates (MR).ResultsSignificant increases in CPR (OR = 5.14, p < 0.001) and LBR (OR = 4.60, p < 0.001) were seen in appropriately selected patients with sub-endometrial PRP injection compared to placebo, with a reduction in MR (OR = 0.60, p = 0.036). The benefit of injection compared to infusion appears highest for CPR in those with a resistant thin endometrium (p = 0.03).ConclusionsSub-endometrial administration may improve the efficacy of PRP in selected patients. More work is needed to identify the optimal candidates, and given the additional administration challenges, whether injection should be a first-line intervention, or considered after unsuccessful infusion, remains open to debate, with more studies needed to confirm this.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12958-025-01455-3.

Introduction

The application of platelet-rich plasma (PRP) to reproductive medicine is promising new therapy for addressing endometrial dysfunction, either a resistant thin endometrium, or unexplained recurrent implantation failure (RIF). PRP contains high concentrations of growth factors, including platelet–derived growth factor (PDGF), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and transforming growth factor–beta (TGF–β), which stimulate cellular proliferation, angiogenesis, and tissue repair [1–3]. These growth factors enhance endometrial stromal and epithelial cell regeneration, and modulate inflammatory responses, potentially improving receptivity [2, 4, 5]. An endometrium which fails to reach a threshold thickness of 7 mm during the implantation window remains a significant challenge, contributing to cycle cancellations and failures [4, 6, 7]. Several adjunctive therapies have been proposed such as sildenafil citrate, pentoxifylline, L-arginine and granulocyte–colony stimulating factor (G–CSF), but with unconvincing results. When these modalities fail there are limited options, but evidence is accumulating for the use of endogenous growth factors in PRP as a promising intervention [8, 9]. Initial PRP use was reported around 1970 in haematology, with expansion into surgical patients for wound healing, and later into reproductive medicine. The first intracavitary use was reported in 2015 from China, in 5 challenging patients with 4 achieving pregnancy [10]. Following this, further studies have reported varying benefit, with meta-analyses demonstrating significant positive outcomes for both a non-responsive thin endometrium and unexplained RIF [11–18]. A recent Cochrane review demonstrated an improvement in clinical pregnancy and live birth rates with PRP infusion, but highlighting significant heterogeneity and a risk of bias across included studies, concluding the current evidence is of low certainty and that further high-quality RCTs are needed [18].

Some patients will still not respond adequately to intracavity PRP infusion, leading clinicians to seek alternative options. Injection of PRP directly into the sub-endometrial zone is a novel technique which has gained increasing interest, delivering the growth factors to the target area in a more accurate and efficient manner, maximizing local bioavailability [19, 20]. While early infusion studies utilised PRP instillation without imaging guidance [6, 19], more recent injection protocols incorporate advanced visualisation during administration to improve placement location, which may provide additional benefits beyond sub-endometrial delivery. One method utilises hysteroscopic guidance via an operative scope, allowing targeted injections into specific endometrial regions keeping the needle under direct visualisation at all times [21]. This approach also allows the concomitant treatment of any co-existing pathology, such as polyps or adhesions. Disadvantages are the requirement of specialized equipment, an operative setting, appropriate analgesia, increased operator skill and additional cost. An alternative but less utilised technique is an ultrasound–guided transvaginal injection approach, using real-time ultrasonography to enable visualisation and precise deposition of PRP near the endometrial-myometrial junction. This could allow the most accurate observation of needle depth and position, optimizing the spatial distribution of growth factors [19, 20]. Ultrasound–guided PRP injection increased the median endometrial thickness from 5.8 to 6.5 mm (P = 0.007) in a recent study, enabling successful embryo transfers in previously cancelled cycles.

Histological evidence demonstrates PRP enhances αvβ3 integrin and Ki67 expression, both well-established markers of endometrial receptivity and proliferation [5, 6]. Doppler ultrasound assessments demonstrate improved endometrial vascularity post–PRP administrationn, quantified using Applebaum criteria, correlating with higher implantation rates [20] Both ultrasound–guided injection and intrauterine infusion had higher clinical pregnancy and live birth rates than controls in a 2021 study [19]. A 2025 study demonstrated PRP’s superiority over G–CSF, with a 30% increase in clinical pregnancy rates [7]. Recent reviews comparing endometrial PRP with placebo consistently demonstrate improve clinical pregnancy and live birth rates, with lower miscarriage rates [11, 16, 18, 22]. Whether the extra invasiveness, costs, and technical skills required to perform sub-endometrial injection over infusion are justified by improved outcomes remains subject to debate. Additionally, no comparison in benefit between the less resource intensive ultrasound-guided technique and hysteroscopic injection has been reported. A detailed systematic review comparing these sub-endometrial injection techniques against either placebo or infusion has not yet been performed, but is needed due to the growth in use of these treatments. Indeed, confusion in the literature exists over the nomenclature of endometrial PRP techniques, with several previous studies and reviews interchangeably using the terms infusion and injection, when only a standard intracavity installation technique was utilised [23–26]. This was understandable during the emergence of the treatment, when only infusion was available, but for clarity and consistency, the term injection appears better reserved for only direct sub-endometrial administration. The first SRMA using clearly defined sub-endometrial injection techniques for PRP administration is presented, with detailed subgroup analysis comparing the different modalities of visualisation, and indications for use, allowing a better understanding of the current evidence for this controversial therapy.

Methods

Protocol and registration

The SRMA was conducted following the PRISMA guidelines. The review protocol was registered with PROSPERO prior to commencement, with reference number CRD42025100551, highlighting the underlying commitment to transparency and methodological precision.

Eligibility criteria

Included studies assessed endometrial PRP use by confirmed sub-endometrial injection in women with RIF, defined by the absence of clinical pregnancy after ≥ 2 high-quality embryo transfers in ≥ 2 IVF cycles, or a non-responsive thin endometrium. Randomized controlled trials, cohort studies, or case–control studies comparing sub-endometrial PRP with prior to ART, either intrauterine infusion, or placebo/no treatment were eligible. The primary outcomes were clinical pregnancy rate (CPR), live birth rate (LBR), and miscarriage rate (MR).

Definitions

Clarity in terminology was needed to ensure consistency when comparing techniques. Injection was defined as a sub-endometrial needle-guided administration of PRP directly into the basal layer, under either hysteroscopic or USS guidance. Infusion was defined as an intracavity instillation of PRP into the uterus via catheter, without penetration of the endometrium. RIF was defined as ≥ 3 unsuccessful ET and thin endometrium was considered as a thickness < 7 mm on USS. In terms of outcomes, clinical pregnancy was defined as the presence of a gestational sac, with or without a fetal heartbeat, confirmed by transvaginal ultrasound. The CPR was reported as per embryo transfer or initiated cycle to reflect early treatment success [27–29]. The LBR reflects the proportion of clinical pregnancies resulting in a live birth [27–29]. MR was categorised as the number of miscarriages as a proportion of the total clinical pregnancies [28–30]. These definitions align with international recommendations (ESHRE, ASRM) and allow for consistent, outcome-focused reporting once a pregnancy is achieved. Reporting LBR and MR per clinical pregnancy ensures these outcomes are directly comparable and mutually exclusive, together accounting for 100% of clinical pregnancies. Thus, enhancing both clinical relevance and methodological transparency.

Information sources and search

A comprehensive search of PubMed, Scopus, and Embase databases was performed. Selected search terms were chosen to capture a broad spectrum of studies related to the research question, including: “PRP”, “Injection”, “Infusion”, “Intrauterine”, “endometrium” and outcome terms including ‘pregnancy,’ ‘miscarriage,’ ‘recurrent implantation failure’ (RIF), ‘assisted reproductive technology’ (ART), ‘intra cytoplasmic sperm injections’ (ICSI), ‘implantation’. These terms were used in various combinations, connected by the Boolean operators ‘OR’ as well as ‘AND’ to ensure a wide-ranging search. The strategy had no language restrictions and was inclusive of literature published from 2010–2025. To ensure literature saturation, we manually searched the references of included studies and relevant review articles.

Study selection

The study selection process was systematically structured. Two independent reviewers (PK and CH) screened the titles and abstracts yielded from the initial search. After the preliminary filtration, full–text articles were retrieved and assessed against our inclusion criteria. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer (ROS/HW/KM) when necessary. This process was documented, ensuring reproducibility and minimizing the risk of selection bias.

Data collection process

A standardized data extraction form was developed and pilot-tested to ensure all relevant data were captured accurately. The data extraction process was independently conducted by two reviewers (PK and CH). Extracted information included study design, sample size, baseline characteristics, method of administration, details of PRP treatment (dosage, timing related to embryo transfer, frequency of administration), additional medications used, and outcome measures. Authors of the studies were contacted to resolve any ambiguities in the reported data or retrieve missing information.

Risk of bias in individual studies

The risk of bias was assessed in individual studies the Newcastle-Ottawa score for observational studies, allowing evaluation of potential sources of bias across several domains. Each domain was rated as ‘low risk,’ ‘high risk,’ or ‘unclear risk’ of bias. The outcome of these assessments was reviewed within the team to ensure a balanced and critical evaluation of each study’s methodological quality.

Summary measures and synthesis of results

The principal summary measures were the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for dichotomous outcomes. The Mantel–Haenszel method was used to synthesize the data, then tested for statistical heterogeneity using the I² statistic, with I² values of 25%, 50%, and 75% representing low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. A random-effects model was chosen in anticipation of inter-study variability. The approach to data synthesis prioritized methodological appropriateness and relevance to clinical practice.

Additional analyses

To explore potential sources of heterogeneity, and to ensure the robustness of the study findings, subgroup analyses were planned based on the technique, indication for use, and study design. Sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the influence of individual studies on the overall results. Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and quantitatively with Egger’s regression test, where applicable. Subgroup analysis was performed based on the technique for sub-endometrial injection (hysteroscopic or ultrasound–guided), study type (RCT vs. Observational), and the indication for PRP use (unexplained RIF or Endometrial factors).

Results

Study selection

The search strategy was extensive, resulting in a significant number of studies. The initial screening process adhered strictly to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, allowed us to reduce the records to the most relevant articles (Fig. 1). After rigorous full – text review, 7 studies were identified [4, 19, 21, 31–34]. These collectively assessed a total of 1165 patients, with 397 cases administered sub-endometrial PRP by injection, 461 using standard intrauterine infusion, and 307 controls with no treatment.

Risk of bias within studies

A risk of bias assessment was conducted across the included studies, revealing a heterogeneous landscape of study quality. Due to studt design there were prevalent issues with lack of randomisation and blinding of participants and personnel. This deficiency indicates a notable risk of performance bias, and studies were assessed in the relevant domains. The detailed evaluation, critical for understanding the strength of evidence, is represented in a summary graph (Fig. 2).

Synthesis of results

Sub-endometrial PRP injection Vs No treatment

Clinical Pregnancy Rate (CPR): CPR per embryo transfer is an important primary outcome measure and reported by all 4 included studies (n = 553) comparing PRP injection with no treatment. The analysis demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in CPR in PRP treated patients across the studies (Fig. 3). The overall effect size showed a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 2.97 (95% CI: 1.67 to 4.66, p < 0.001), and a significant increase in the odds of clinical pregnancy with PRP treatment. The analysis demonstrated low heterogeneity (I2 = 24%), an objective measure which reflects consistency of the results. This data suggests that PRP treatment prior to embryo transfer is associated with improved clinical pregnancy outcomes among selected ART patients with a previous confirmed diagnosis of RIF or endometrial dysfunction using high grade embryos.

Clinical Pregnancy Rate (CPR) for Sub – Endometrial PRP versus Placebo

Live Birth Rate (LBR): Live Birth Rate is perhaps the most important quality indicator in Assisted Reproduction and was reported in all 4 studies with 554 participants (Fig. 4). The effect of PRP injection on LBR was also significant, with an OR of 3.89 (95% CI: 1.56 to 9.70, p = 0.004). The analysis revealed moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 54%), suggesting variability among included trials, perhaps in study design or population characteristics. The live birth outcomes were significantly improved with concomitant PRP administration, underscoring the consistent positive impact of PRP on increasing LBR across diverse research settings for this poor prognosis patient group, which is both clinically and statistically significant.

Miscarriage Rate (MR): The MR was reported in the patients that achieved pregnancy in 4 included studies (n = 194). The analysis demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in miscarriage rate for the PRP group compared to controls (Fig. 5), with an OR of 0.45 (95% CI: 0.23 to 0.90, p = 0.02) The trials demonstrated low heterogeneity (I2 = 3%), again suggesting consistency in the effect of PRP on reducing miscarriages across different studies.

Sub-endometrial PRP injection Vs intra-cavity infusion

Clinical Pregnancy Rate (CPR): Interestingly, a statistically significant improvement in CPR was identified in patients treated with PRP injection compared to infusion across 5 included studies with 554 (Fig. 6). The overall effect size showed a pooled odds ratio (OR) of 1.59 (95% CI: 1.08 to 2.32, p < 0.02), indicating a statistically significant benefit (Fig. 3a). The analysis demonstrated low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%), reflecting reduced variability in study outcomes. This suggests that endometrial PRP injection prior to embryo transfer may be associated with improved clinical pregnancy outcomes in selected ART patients with a diagnosis of either unexplained RIF or endometrial dysfunction compared to intracavity infusion as well as placebo. More detailed analysis is needed to identify which patients may achieve the most benefit.

Clinical Pregnancy Rate (CPR) for Sub – Endometrial PRP versus Intracavitary Infusion

Live Birth Rate (LBR): Live Birth Rate was reported in 3 studies (n = 300), and no statistically significant benefit from PRP injection on LBR compared to infusion was noted (Fig. 7), with an OR of 1.33 (95% CI: 0.78 to 2.28, p = 0.30). The analysis revealed low heterogeneity (I2 = 8%). In this instance, there is an absence of evidence to determine if endometrial injection of PRP could be superior to infusion for improving live birth rate in selected patients, with both techniques demonstrating a similar benefit.

Miscarriage Rate (MR): MR data was reported in 3 studies (n = 167), and meta-analysis demonstrated no difference in miscarriage rates between the different PRP administration techniques (Fig. 8), with an OR of 1.13 (95% CI: 0.59 to 2.18, p = 0.71) The trials demonstrated low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%).

Overall, these findings highlight the potential efficacy of sub-endometrial PRP injection compared to placebo in enhancing CPR with ART in these difficult to treat patient groups. More studies, especially randomised trials, is needed to compare LBR data between injection and infusion. The low heterogeneity observed indicated consistency in the effect of PRP across the different studies evaluated here. More detailed subgroup analysis is needed to determine if there are any specific patient groups that may benefit from PRP injection rather than infusion, with no overall difference in live birth or miscarriage rates noted between the groups.

Subgroup analysis: hysteroscopic vs. ultrasound – guided

Variations in cost, procedure time, and equipment required, make it important to identify if there are any differences between hysteroscopic and ultrasound – guided sub – endometrial injection techniques in terms of efficacy, or specific indications which respond better to either method. No interventional study has yet directly compared these 2 techniques, so subgroup analysis of existing studies is the only current method to analyse if potential differences exist. Both techniques significantly improve the CPR, LBR and MR when compared to placebo (Table 3), with no difference in efficacy seen between the methods, suggesting both techniques are equally valid options, with the choice depending on operator preference.

Subgroup analysis: indication for PRP

When assessing current indications for the use of endometrial PRP injection, two diverse patient groups are identified. One cohort consists of unexplained RIF, where transfer of multiple high-grade embryos has been unsuccessful without any identifiable cause. The other category is patients with ultrasonic or hysteroscopic evidence of endometrial dysfunction, where the endometrium does not develop an appropriate thickness or trilaminar pattern, despite adequate stimulation. These distinct groups of endometrial disorders have different aetiology and may necessitate different treatment options, hence the need for a detailed subgroup analysis between these patient cohorts. When the indication for PRP injection is endometrial dysfunction, the injection technique significantly outperforms intracavity infusion in terms of CPR (OR 2.07, p = 0.03) (Fig. 9). Although a similar difference was noted in LBR (OR 2.06, p = 0.3), the wider confidence intervals due to the sample size mean there is an absence of evidence as to whether this potential improvement is real (Supp 4). Interestingly, when the indication for PRP administration is RIF, there was no difference in CPR (OR 0.95, Fig. 9) or LBR (OR = 0.95, supp 4) between the injection and infusion techniques, suggesting no additional benefit by sub-endometrial administration when implantation failure is the primary indication. There was no difference in miscarriage rates based on the two clinical indications (Supp 5). When sub-endometrial injection is compared to no-intervention, the technique results in improvements in clinical outcomes across all indications (Supp 1,2,3).

Subgroup analysis: study design

None of the included studies were randomised controlled trials, reducing the methodological quality of included studies, Analysis of large cohort studies is currently the best way to assess the available evidence to analyse and appraise this new treatment options until further data is presented. The study results highlight the potential benefit of PRP injection, and promote the need to design of future RCTs.

Discussion

A persistent non-responsive thin endometrium, and unexplained implantation failure remain challenging aspects of assisted reproduction. The increasing utilisation of endometrial PRP, and a trend towards sub-endometrial administration is an interesting yet controversial adjunct for these problems. This review demonstrates a significant benefit following sub-endometrial PRP injection for some of the primary outcome measures, but caution is needed. Compared to placebo, significant increases in CPR and LBR are seen in these complex cases, along with an additional reduction in MR. Interestingly, when injection was compared to the more widely utilised infusion, direct sub-endometrial administration led to a higher CPR, but there is an absence of evidence to determine if this results in an improved LBR. Until more studies with increased patient numbers are available, it is difficult to determine if these trend are reproducible. Although promising, there is a need for randomised trials and better study design, to exclude potential sources of bias before this can become a mainstream approach.

Harnessing growth factors and cytokines from autologous blood may enhance endometrial regeneration, improve fertility outcomes, and support early pregnancy development. PRP has demonstrated efficacy in increasing endometrial thickness, a critical factor for implantation. Studies report an average increase of 0.6–1.0 mm following intrauterine infusion, with thickness frequently > 7 mm in patients that have been previously unresponsive to treatment [2, 35, 36]. A 2023 study in patients with thin endometrium reported an increase to 7.4 mm, enabling progression to embryo transfer rather than cycle cancellation in all cases [35]. A meta–analysis of 730 patients demonstrated that endometrial PRP improved endometrial thickness by 0.79 mm compared to controls, with the mechanism involving the action of growth factors like VEGF and EGF to stimulate angiogenesis and cellular proliferation [2, 37, 38]. These factors counteract endometrial fibrosis and promote stromal cell regeneration, particularly in cases associated with diminished ovarian reserve or chronic endometritis [1, 6]. Notably, PRP outperforms other adjuvant therapies such as low-dose aspirin or vaginal oestrogen, which show limited efficacy [2, 35]. Ashermann’s syndrome, characterized by intrauterine adhesions and fibrosis, poses significant challenges, and surrogacy is often considered when this is refractory to treatment. Endometrial PRP injection may complement surgical adhesiolysis as an adjunctive treatment by promoting endometrial regeneration. Preclinical murine studies show that PRP administration reduces fibrosis markers, collagen deposition, and restores normal endometrial morphology, leading to higher implantation and live birth rates [39]. A pilot study demonstrated improved endometrial function post-PRP with Ashermann’s syndrome, reporting successful pregnancies despite persistent thin endometrium [40]. Results are mixed, however, with a 2021 trial reporting no significant difference in endometrial thickness or pregnancy rates between PRP-treated Ashermann’s patients and controls [41]. A 2024 meta–analysis of 874 patients demonstrated that endometrial PRP could reduce moderate to severe adhesion recurrence by 52%, with 37% improvements in CPR [42]. These discrepancies in outcomes highlight the need for further trials. Current evidence suggests that PRP may enhance residual endometrial cell proliferation post-surgical adhesiolysis, but that its role may be adjunctive rather than standalone [39, 43].

PRP has potential to treat patients with unexplained RIF following embryo transfer, often associated with inadequate endometrial receptivity. An RIF study reported that endometrial PRP increased CPR from 20 to 37% and LBR from 2 to 19% [36]. A 2024 randomized trial reported a 68.8% LBR in PRP–treated frozen embryo transfer cycles versus 11.7% in controls [44]. These improvements have been attributed to modulation of inflammatory cytokines and upregulated implantation markers like integrins [2, 45]. The timing of administration appears to be crucial, with Intrauterine treatment 48 h before embryo transfer aligning with the window of endometrial receptivity, allowing the growth factors to enhance decidualization and vascularization [44, 46]. A double-blind trial demonstrated a 20% pregnancy rate in PRP-treated RIF patients versus 13.3% in controls, with no adverse effects [46]. PRP may become a potential intervention for the treatment of RIF where embryo quality has been demonstrated to be good and endometrial dysfunction is suspected [36, 44]. Emerging data also suggests that PRP may reduce the miscarriage risk in susceptible patients. A pilot study of 40 high risk RPL patients undergoing IVF treatment reported a 15% live birth rate in the PRP treated group compared to 0% in controls, but statistical significance was not reached due to small sample size [45]. The cytokine composition of PRP, including TGF–β and interleukin-1 supports decidualization and placental development, which has the potential to reduce the risk of early pregnancy failure [2, 45]. In patients with thin endometrium, the role of PRP in restoring endometrial integrity may perhaps indirectly reduce the miscarriage risk by improving the quality of embryo implantation [1, 6]. The impact on miscarriage risks remains controversial, with a meta–analysis linking endometrial PRP to higher clinical pregnancy rates, but finding no significant impact on miscarriage or live birth rates, underscoring the need for targeted research [37, 42]. The current evidence remains preliminary and further exploration of PRP in recurrent miscarriage protocols is needed.

The advantage of injection over infusion appears to be most promising for patients with endometrial dysfunction. In these cases the clinical pregnancy rate was significantly higher following sub-endometrial PRP rather than an intracavity flush. This improvement would not be unexpected, as this administration technique directly targets the regenerative zone, allowing injection under vision into the endo-myometrial junction, which has been histologically demonstrated to be the regenerative layer containing both stem cells and progenitor cells critical for endometrial repair [2, 23, 47, 48]. With intracavitary infusion, PRP distribution may be only superficial, limiting its efficacy due to lack of penetration to these deeper regenerative layers [2, 32]. An enhanced level of growth factor delivery may be achieved with injection, leading to higher localized concentrations of important cytokines (VEGF, FGF, and EGF) at the target site, promoting angiogenesis and cellular proliferation [2, 32, 48]. In RIF cases, there was no difference in outcome between the two techniques, with infusion appearing less invasive but equally effective, suggesting that for this indication, direct delivery to the basal layer is not needed to provide benefit. Perhaps the immune modulating effects can be equally achieved by a lower cost and less technical infusion technique, perhaps being the preferred method of administration for these patients. Regarding the injection techniques, direct comparison of hysteroscopic and ultrasound–guided methods shows no difference in outcomes. Further study is needed to determine if there are specific circumstances where a certain method may be preferred, or if both are equally effective for all patients. Hysteroscopy allows direct visualization of the needle and puncture sites, but ultrasound offer comparable efficacy without requiring operative settings, reducing costs [21]. To improve consistency, standardization of injection protocols is needed. Identification of the optimal needle gauge, number of injection sites, and total volume will help to achieve this. The optimal dosing of PRP has not been definitively established, with variability in platelet concentrations (0.6-1.0 × 1011/mL) and injection volumes (1-8mL) used in various protocols, potentially complicating direct cross–study comparisons [4, 6]. Whether any impact on efficacy is influenced by dosing has not been specifically assessed. The timing and frequency of injections also varies, with most protocols using a single doses, but refractory cases may require 2–4 treatments [6]. Whether additional doses confer increased benefit needs study to determine the optimal frequency. Co-treatment has been proposed, combining PRP with adjuvant therapies in an attempt to improve outcomes. Administration of subcutaneous G–CSF alongside PRP has been suggested to amplify leukocyte mediated tissue repair [19], but there is a lack of data to determine if this is actually beneficial. Additionally, enrichment of PRP suspensions with autologous endometrial stromal/epithelial cells demonstrated a synergistic effect on angiogenesis and endometrial development [4]. Whether this technique, or the additional use of exosome derived growth factors can truly improve outcomes needs further study.

Endometrial PRP appears to have a good safety profile and tolerability. The infection risk is < 1% across studies, comparable to standard intrauterine procedures [49, 50]. A concern about using an injectable technique is the potential for increased patient discomfort, but published data is reassuring, demonstrating good tolerance, with only mild discomfort reported during cervical manipulation in 15–20% of cases, easily managed with local anaesthesia [49, 50]. No significant systemic sides effects have been reported in published data, and the autologous nature of the preparation removes the risk of allergic or immune reactions [1, 6]. Despite the encouraging safety data, there remains a lack of long–term outcomes, with limited data on obstetric outcomes beyond live birth rate. A major limitation of this study is that only observational trials were identified. Although several RCTs have been designed and published assessing endometrial infusion of PRP, there is no randomised trial assessing sub-endometrial injection. Although the risk of bias assessments are reassuring, and heterogeneity is low, there will be need for well-designed prospective RCTs to fully answer questions regarding patient selection and potential bias. Direct studies comparing infusion again injection, hysteroscopic with ultrasound-guided administration are also needed. Published data only assesses outcomes following assisted reproduction treatments. There is no evidence to assess the impact no spontaneous conception or unexplained infertility.

Despite the issues, sub-endometrial PRP injection has the potential to represent a paradigm shift in managing thin endometrium, offering targeted regenerative therapy with measurable improvements in endometrial parameters and assisted reproductive technology outcomes. Current evidence still positions this technique as an experimental intervention for refractory cases, with further standardization, randomised trials, and long–term follow-up studies remaining essential. The technique also provides hope for unexplained RIF, and more detailed understanding of the immunological changes that result from administration may help identify potential cases. As reproductive specialists increasingly adopt vision-guided PRP delivery, its integration into assisted reproductive technology protocols may help to reduce cycle cancellations for endometrial dysfunction and improve patient prognosis.

Conclusion

The administration of sub-endometrial PRP represents a promising adjunct to the therapeutic options aimed at enhancing endometrial function and improving reproductive outcomes. This reviews identifies that delivering PRP directly into the sub-endometrial space may enhance its efficacy in improving clinical pregnancy rates in selected patient populations, particularly those with a refractory thin endometrium. However, this technique introduces additional complexity in terms of administration, requiring greater technical precision and potentially more invasive manipulation. Given these considerations, questions remains whether sub-endometrial PRP should be utilized as a first-line approach, or reserved for cases where previous intrauterine infusion has been unsuccessful. The limited volume of high-quality comparative studies prevents drawing firm conclusions at this stage, with further well-designed, randomized trials needed to evaluate the relative efficacy, safety, and cost-effectiveness of sub -endometrial injection compared to intrauterine PRP infusion.
