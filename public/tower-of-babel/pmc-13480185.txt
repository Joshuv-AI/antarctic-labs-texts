Differential roles of peer bullying and childhood maltreatment in suicidal ideation and attempts among Chinese adolescents
Authors: Zixuan Xu, Wenhua An, Jie Hu, Ziyan Yin, Chenxi Zhang, Lengyi Zhang, Yuqin Dai, Yanmei Zhang, Yizhen Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe progression from suicidal ideation to attempt is a multi-stage process, yet it is unknown how different victimization contexts contribute uniquely to each stage. We compared the associations of school-based peer bullying and family-based childhood maltreatment with suicidal ideation, suicide planning, and suicide attempts in adolescents to identify the primary intervention target for halting suicidality at each step.MethodsA multicenter, school-based survey of 18,910 Chinese students aged 12–22 years was conducted. Participants were categorized into four groups: none, peer bullying only, childhood maltreatment only, and both. Logistic regression and population attributable fraction analyses were applied.ResultsOverall, 10.9% reported peer bullying only, 18.7% maltreatment only, and 8.9% both. Peer bullying showed a stronger association with suicidal ideation than maltreatment (OR 1.35; 95% CI:1.14–1.60), particularly among females < 15 years (OR 1.68; 95% CI:1.21–2.32). Childhood maltreatment had a stronger link to suicide attempts (OR 1.34; 95% CI: 1.06–1.70) and a higher PAF (13% vs. 2% for peer bullying). Among ideators, maltreatment increased progression to attempts (OR 1.70, 95% CI: 1.32–2.19). Notably, co-exposure sharply raised the risk of suicide attempts (PAF: 25%).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13034-026-01093-6.

Introduction

Suicide is a critical public health issue, accounting for over 720,000 annual deaths worldwide [1]. Although the overall suicide rate has recently declined, adolescent suicide remains a significant concern. From 2019 to 2021, suicide rose from the fourth to the third leading cause of death among adolescents [1, 2]. In China, suicide rates among adolescents aged 10–19 surged from 1.59 to 2.83 per 100,000 individuals between 2017 and 2021, posing new challenges for prevention efforts [3]. Given the irreversible nature of suicide, suicidal behavior—encompassing a continuum from suicidal ideation (SI) and planning (SP) to suicide attempts (SA) —represents a critical precursor to suicide mortality and provides key opportunities for timely intervention. Contemporary theories increasingly conceptualize suicidal behavior as a staged process rather than a single, homogeneous outcome. The ideation-to-action (I2A) framework posits that the emergence of suicidal ideation and the transition from ideation to suicidal behavior are governed by partially distinct mechanisms. Prominent I2A theories, including the Three-Step Theory (3ST), the Integrated Motivational–Volitional (IMV) model, and the Interpersonal-Psychological Theory of Suicide, emphasize that psychological pain, social disconnection, and feelings of entrapment primarily contribute to suicidal ideation, whereas exposure to volitional or capability-enhancing factors facilitates the progression from ideation to planning and attempts [4–6]. Accordingly, identifying stage-specific risk factors within the same study is essential for informing targeted and effective prevention strategies.

Peer bullying and childhood maltreatment, as two prevalent forms of childhood adversity, both demonstrate significant associations with adolescent suicidal behaviors. Yet, they differ fundamentally in their perpetrators and developmental contexts: peer bullying typically involves aggression among age-mates, whereas childhood maltreatment refers to harm inflicted by adult caregivers, most often parents, within a caregiving relationship. Therefore, from an I2A perspective, these two forms of adversity may differentially influence specific stages of suicidal behavior. Peer bullying may primarily contribute to the development of suicidal ideation by increasing perceived burdensomeness, thwarted belongingness, and psychological distress during a critical period of social development. In contrast, childhood maltreatment may exert a stronger influence on later stages of suicidal behavior by disrupting emotional regulation, increasing impulsivity, and fostering long-term vulnerability to self-destructive behaviors [7, 8].

Nevertheless, empirical findings regarding their relative impact across stages remain inconsistent. A study in Western countries reported that peer bullying was associated with a higher suicide risk than maltreatment [9], whereas another study found childhood maltreatment to be more strongly linked to both suicidal ideation and suicide attempts [10]. Moreover, evidence suggests that childhood maltreatment and peer bullying are not mutually exclusive but often reinforce each other and frequently co-occur [11–13]. The failure to disentangle their independent and joint effects may obscure their stage-specific contributions to suicidal behavior, limiting the development of precise prevention strategies.

Additionally, the relationship between childhood adversity and suicidal behaviors may vary different demographic and developmental dimensions. For instance, gender differences have been observed, with females showing a higher prevalence of suicide attempts following childhood maltreatment [14–16]. Age-related differences in adolescence may also exist; however, evidence across developmental stages remains limited. A study across 48 countries found that adolescents aged 12 to 15 who were bullied by peers are three times more likely to attempt suicide than those who were not bullied [17]. Yet, because this study focused solely on early adolescence, it provides no basis for comparisons with middle or late adolescence. Overall, the lack of research covering a broader range of developmental stages restricts our understanding of potential age-group variations and hinders the development of age-specific interventions.

To address these gaps, we conducted a nationwide cross-sectional study among Chinese middle and high school students with three objectives. First, we examined the independent and joint effects of peer bullying and childhood maltreatment on adolescent suicidal behaviors by categorizing participants into “bullying-only” and “maltreatment-only” groups to isolate each effect. Second, we compared their associations across the three stages of suicidal behaviors and identified the more critical form of victimization, while also assessing variations by gender and stage of adolescence. Finally, we estimated population attributable fractions (PAFs)—a measure of the proportion of suicidality cases preventable by eliminating specific exposures—to complement odds ratios and identify high-impact risk factors, thereby informing suicide prevention priorities and resource allocation.

Methods

Study population

The study, conducted in China from April to December 2021, focused on adolescents’ psychological and behavioral issues. A multi-stage stratified cluster sampling method was used. In Stage 1, five provinces (Jiangsu, Guangdong, Yunnan, Gansu, and Hubei) were randomly selected from different regions in China. Stage 2 involved randomly selecting two cities per province. Stage 3 selected one urban district and one rural county per city. Stage 4 randomly chose one junior and one senior high school per district or county. Stage 5 involved randomly selecting four to six classes per grade (7th to 12th) based on school enrollment size. All students in these classes were invited to participate voluntarily. Informed consent was obtained from participants or their guardians (for students under 14), and the study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, adhering to the latest version of the Helsinki Declaration.

Assessment of suicide behavior

Suicidal behavior was assessed using three items adapted from the Global School-Based Student Health Survey [18, 19]. Suicidal ideation (“Have you ever seriously considered committing suicide?“), Suicide plans (“Have you ever made a specific plan about how you would kill yourself?“), and Suicide attempts (“Have you ever tried to kill yourself?“). If the response was “no”, the questioning stopped; if “yes”, the frequency was recorded, with responses classified based on the past year as either “no” (0 times) or “yes” (1 to 4 times) [20, 21]. Two distinct methodologies were employed for the classification of suicidal behaviors: (1) the three suicidal behaviors (SI, SP, SA) were classified using non-exclusive criteria, with each behavior categorized separately based on its presence irrespective of co-occurring behaviors; (2) suicidal behaviors were categorized into three mutually exclusive stages: suicidal ideation (suicidal ideation only), suicide planning (both suicidal ideation and plans, but no attempts), and suicide attempt (presence of suicidal ideation, plans, and attempts) [22].

Assessment of peer bullying victimization

Traditional bullying victimization was measured using an adapted version of the Olweus Bully Victim Questionnaire (OBVQ), consisting of six items. Participants were asked, “How often have your peers exhibited these behaviors towards you in the last six months?” Responses were coded as: 1 = never, 2 = once or twice, 3 = two or three times a month, 4 = about once a week, and 5 = several times a week. Adolescents experiencing any item two or three times a month or more were classified as victims of traditional bullying [23, 24]. The Cronbach’s alpha for the OBVQ in this study was 0.79.

Cyberbullying was assessed with the question, “In the past six months, have any students ridiculed, abused, threatened you, or maliciously spread your privacy online through text messages, emails, or chat platforms? " Students responding “yes” were identified as cyber victims [25].

A student was considered peer bullied if they reported experiencing any type of bullying.

Assessment of childhood maltreatment

Childhood maltreatment was assessed using the Childhood Trauma Questionnaire-Short Form (CTQ-SF) [26]. The instrument comprises five subscales: physical abuse, emotional abuse, sexual abuse, physical neglect, and emotional neglect. Each subscale contains 5 items (25 items total), rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never, 2 = occasional, 3 = sometimes, 4 = frequent, 5 = always). In this study, childhood maltreatment was identified if scores on any of the subscales exceeded the following thresholds: physical abuse ≥ 10 points, emotional abuse ≥ 13 points, sexual abuse ≥ 8 points, emotional neglect ≥ 15 points, or physical neglect ≥ 10 points. Participants with scores meeting or exceeding these thresholds in any subscale were classified as having experienced childhood maltreatment; Otherwise, they were classified as not having experienced childhood maltreatment [27]. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the CTQ-SF-RC scale in the study was 0.65.

Assessment of covariates

Covariates in this study included general demographic information, sexual orientation, and experiences of being left behind, as potential confounders in the relationship between bullying victimization, childhood maltreatment, and suicidal behavior. Demographic variables included age (continuous), sex (male or female), grade (junior high school or senior high school), school district (urban or rural), family structure (core family—living with two biological parents, or others), single-child family (yes or no), father’s and mother’s educational level (primary school or less, junior high school, senior high school, and college or more), and family income (monthly average in RMB: <1999, 2000–3999, 4000–5999, 6000–7999, > 8000). Sexual orientation (heterosexual or LGBQ) was assessed using a single item on a 4-point scale: 0 = Heterosexual, 1 = Lesbian/gay, 2 = Bisexual, and 3 = Questioning [28, 29]. The left-behind experience (yes or no) was operationalized as responding affirmatively to the question, “Did your father and/or mother move to a town or district for work and not live with you for at least six months?” [30, 31].

Missing data

In the data preprocessing stage, we first assessed the extent of missingness for all variables. Variables with more than 15% missing data were excluded from the analyses due to concerns about potential bias and unreliable imputation. For the remaining variables, missing values were handled using multiple imputation to reduce bias and improve statistical efficiency. Multiple imputation was conducted using the chained equations approach (multiple imputation by chained equations, MICE), with 10 imputations. All analytic variables and covariates related to the missingness mechanism were included in the imputation model. The final estimates were pooled across imputed datasets using Rubin’s rules.

Statistical analysis

We first described the characteristics of the study sample and the distribution of suicidal behaviors. Participants were then categorized into four groups: those who experienced neither peer bullying nor childhood maltreatment, those exposed to peer bullying only, those exposed to childhood maltreatment only, and those exposed to both. In our study, two different approaches to handling suicidal behaviors were adopted. For the multinomial logistic regression analyses, suicidal behaviors were treated as mutually exclusive categories, which was utilized to assess the differential associations of peer bullying and childhood maltreatment with three distinct categories of suicidal behaviors (SI, SP, SA). Three statistical models were fitted: Model 1 (with no exposure as the reference group), Model 2 (peer bullied only as the reference group), and Model 3 (childhood maltreatment only as the reference group). All models controlled for the demographic information, sexual orientation, and left-behind experiences. To further assess the distinct roles of peer bullying and childhood maltreatment, we additionally employed a non-mutually exclusive approach to categorizing suicidal behaviors, and, that is, we conducted binary logistic regression among adolescents reporting suicidal ideation to evaluate their risk of progressing to suicide attempts. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported to present the results of the regression analyses. To quantify the associations observed in the cross-sectional data, we calculated risk differences (RDs) and population attributable fractions (PAFs). These were descriptive metrics comparing each mutually exclusive exposure group (e.g., bullying only) to the reference group with no exposure. A sensitivity analysis was conducted with stratification by age and gender to explore potential associations. The significance level was defined as a two-tailed test with P < 0.05. SPSS 26.0 and R 4.3.3 was used for all statistical analyses.

Results

Prevalence and characteristics of suicidal behavior

Among 18,910 participants aged 12–22 years with a mean age of 15 years, 50.7% were female, and 4,130 (21.8%) reported having experienced suicidal behavior in the past year. Specifically, 1,863 (9.9%) reported SI, 1,369 (7.2%) reported making SP, and 898 (4.7%) reported SA. The characteristics according to suicidal behavior are detailed in Table 1. Participants exhibiting suicidal behavior tended to be younger, more likely to be female, urban residents, from non-nuclear family structures, identified as LGBQ, and had left behind experience, childhood maltreatment or peer bullying; their parents were also more likely to have higher education levels and higher economic incomes (all P < 0.05).

General characteristics of participants and proportion of suicidal behaviors

Associations between peer bullying, childhood maltreatment, and suicidal behaviors

Among the 18,910 participants, 2,066 (10.9%) reported exposure to peer bullying only, 3,538 (18.7%) to childhood maltreatment only, and 1,679 (8.9%) to both peer bullying and childhood maltreatment. As shown in Fig. 1, exposure to either form of victimization was associated with an increased risk of suicidal behaviors. Notably, individuals exposed to both peer bullying and childhood maltreatment exhibited the highest risk across all stages of suicidality, with adjusted odds ratios (aORs) of 3.05 (95% CI: 2.59–3.59) for SI, 5.08 (95% CI: 4.28–6.01) for SP, and 13.69 (95% CI: 11.27–16.64) for SA.

Comparative associations of peer bullying and childhood maltreatment with suicidal behaviors

After comparing individuals exposed to peer bullying only with those exposed to childhood maltreatment only in relation to mutually exclusive suicidal behaviors, we found that peer bullying showed a stronger association with SI (OR = 1.35, 95% CI: 1.14–1.60) (Fig. 1). This association remained significant among females (OR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.14–1.79) and those in early adolescence (under 15 years of age) (OR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.18–1.93) after stratifying by gender and age (Additional Fig. 2, Additional Fig. 3). In contrast, childhood maltreatment showed a stronger association with SA (OR = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.06–1.70) (Fig. 1), and this association was observed only among those in early adolescence (OR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.02–1.89) and did not persist after gender stratification (Additional Fig. 1, Additional Fig. 2, Additional Fig. 3). No significant difference was found in the risk of SP between these two groups (P = 0.079) (Fig. 1).

Associations of peer bullying and childhood maltreatment with suicidal progression

Stage-specific transitions across the suicidality continuum

To further examine how different victimization patterns influence the escalation of suicidality, we analyzed mutually exclusive suicidal stages (None→SI, SI→SP, SP→SA, and SI→SA) across different categories of victimization exposure. The results are presented in Table 2. These findings indicate that peer bullying plays a more prominent role in the onset of suicidal ideation (OR = 2.23, 95% CI: 1.93–2.57), whereas childhood maltreatment is more strongly associated with the escalation from ideation to attempts (OR = 2.55, 95% CI: 2.06–3.16), and adolescents exposed to both bullying and maltreatment consistently demonstrated the highest risks across all transition stages, with OR ranging approximately from 1.25 to 4.36.

Discussion

The present study examined the relationship between peer bullying, childhood maltreatment and adolescent suicidal behaviors using data from a nationally representative sample in China. Peer bullying demonstrated a stronger association with suicidal ideation in adolescents, particularly among females in early adolescence (under 15 years of age). However, while peer bullying appeared to play a greater role in being associated with the emergence of initial suicidal ideation, the eventual progression to suicide attempts was more strongly associated with parental maltreatment, with a higher OR and PAF compared to peer bullying. Notably, co-exposure to both peer bullying and childhood maltreatment markedly increased the risk of suicide attempts, accounting for approximately one-quarter of all suicide attempts cases.

Our findings demonstrated that peer bullying and childhood maltreatment exerted differential effects on the three stages of suicidal behaviors. In contrast to our findings, which indicated that peer bullying was more strongly associated with suicidal ideation than childhood abuse, Salmon et al. [10] identified childhood maltreatment as the more influential factor. One possible explanation for this discrepancy lies in the distinct psychological and physiological mechanisms underlying different types of adverse experiences. Peer bullying tends to disrupt emotional responses rapidly over a short period—being associated with feelings of shame and thwarted belongingness—whereas childhood maltreatment, particularly emotional abuse, may induce long-term neuroendocrine alterations. Specifically, such maltreatment can lead to dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which may be related to in abnormal glucocorticoid secretion, increased behavioral impulsivity, and elevated pain tolerance thresholds [16], consequently potentially contributing to more far-reaching effects on the individual’s suicidal risk. Moreover, it may be attributable to methodological differences in exposure classification. While Salmon’s study treated bullying and maltreatment as non-mutually exclusive, potentially conflating their effects, our study employed mutually exclusive categories—“bullying-only” and “maltreatment-only”—allowing for a clearer assessment of each risk factor’s independent association with suicidal outcomes. Consistent with our findings, Salmon’s study similarly found that childhood maltreatment was associated with a significantly higher risk of suicide attempts compared to peer bullying, and no significant difference was observed between these two risk factors in relation to suicide planning. Unlike the transient mood swings caused by bullying, the cumulative feelings of hopelessness stemming from abuse may more readily be linked to a higher likelihood of impulsive actions such as suicide attempts when triggered by later stressors, particularly among adolescents with pre-existing suicidal ideation.

After stratifying by developmental stage and gender, the significant association between peer bullying and suicidal ideation (compared to childhood maltreatment) was observed only among early adolescent girls (under 15 years of age). This may be attributed to the heightened neurodevelopmental vulnerability during early adolescence, a period marked by continued brain maturation and processes such as synaptic pruning, which may increase sensitivity to negative social experiences and are closely linked to cognitive and psychiatric risks [32]. In addition, gender differences further shape distinct stress response patterns: due to greater susceptibility to internalizing disorders (such as depression and anxiety) [33], adolescent females tend to adopt rumination as a coping strategy more frequently than males when confronted with peer bullying [34]. When exposed to peer bullying, they tend to dwell on negative emotions and internalize the experience as self-blame or self-denial. The interplay of these developmental and psychological factors may explain why the association between peer bullying and suicidal ideation particularly prominent in early adolescent girls. Although childhood maltreatment showed a significantly stronger association with suicide attempts than peer bullying in the overall sample, this association did not reach statistical significance in gender-stratified analyses. Nevertheless, the direction and magnitude of the effect were consistent across both male and female subgroups. The lack of statistical significance is likely attributable to reduced precision and wider confidence intervals in the stratified models, rather than a true absence of effect. Additionally, the non-significant interaction between gender and childhood maltreatment suggested that gender did not significantly moderate this association (Additional Table 1).

In examining risk differences (RDs) and population attributable fractions (PAFs) for suicidal behaviors across exposure groups—only peer bullying, only childhood maltreatment, and co-exposure to both—several key findings emerged. For suicidal ideation, PAFs were relatively low and comparable across groups, with the highest observed in the peer bullying–only group (RD = 5%, PAF = 5%). Given the limited population-level impact of each individual exposure, further research is warranted to identify additional, potentially more impactful risk factors to improve the efficiency of prevention strategies. For both suicide planning and attempts, co-exposure to peer bullying and childhood maltreatment showed the greatest impact, with PAFs of 11% and 25%, respectively—substantially higher than those associated with either exposure alone. These findings highlight the critical need to address both forms of adversity concurrently in suicide prevention efforts. Preventing co-occurring exposure could potentially be associated with a reduction of up to one-quarter of suicide attempts. Therefore, adolescents affected by both peer bullying and childhood maltreatment should be prioritized in intervention strategies, with coordinated actions from families, schools, and communities to mitigate overlapping victimization and its severe consequences.

Implication

This study has important implications for suicide prevention and mental health intervention among adolescents by elucidating the distinct and stage-specific roles of peer bullying and childhood maltreatment in suicidal behaviors.

From a preventive perspective, the strong association between peer bullying and suicidal ideation—particularly among girls in early adolescence—highlights the critical role of school-based mental health strategies. Universal and selective anti-bullying programs, combined with early screening for emotional distress and interventions targeting emotion regulation and peer relationships, may be particularly effective in reducing the onset of suicidal ideation [35]. Given the heightened neurodevelopmental sensitivity to social stressors during early adolescence, timely school-based identification and support may help interrupt early suicidal trajectories before more severe behaviors emerge [36].

In contrast, childhood maltreatment demonstrated a stronger association with suicide attempts and contributed a larger population attributable fraction, underscoring the importance of trauma-informed approaches within clinical and social service settings [37]. Adolescents with histories of maltreatment may require enhanced monitoring and targeted interventions focusing on impulsivity, affect regulation, and stress reactivity—especially during periods of acute psychosocial stress. This finding emphasized the need for closer integration between child protection systems, mental health services, and primary care to identify and support adolescents at elevated risk of transitioning from suicidal ideation to attempts [38].

Notably, adolescents exposed to both peer bullying and childhood maltreatment experienced the highest risk of suicide attempts, accounting for approximately one quarter of cases at the population level. This substantial burden underscores the importance of integrated, multi-sectoral prevention strategies that address both family- and peer-related adversities simultaneously. Coordinated interventions involving schools, families, and community mental health services may be particularly critical for this high-risk group.

In addition, the findings of this study point to important directions for future research. Future work should move beyond the identification of risk factors and focus on identifying and testing protective factors that may buffer these associations. Specifically, research could examine whether and how factors such as positive teacher–student relationships, strong family cohesion, effective emotion regulation skills, high self-efficacy, and a supportive school climate attenuate the impact of peer bullying on suicidal ideation, or reduce the risk of progression from suicidal ideation to suicide attempts among adolescents with a history of childhood maltreatment. Examining the moderating effects of these protective factors is essential for the development of precise and effective resilience-enhancing interventions that can be tailored to high-risk adolescents, thereby strengthening psychological resilience and interrupting the developmental pathways leading to suicidal behaviors.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths, including the use of a nationally representative sample of Chinese adolescents, the classification of peer bullying and childhood maltreatment into mutually exclusive categories to more accurately capture their independent effects on suicidality, and the use of stratified analyses to identify the high-risk group. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference between peer bullying, childhood maltreatment, and adolescent suicidal behaviors. However, the study’s large, multi-center sample enhances statistical power and generalizability. Second, the analyses did not account for the school-level clustering inherent in the multi-stage stratified cluster sampling design. This may lead to an overstatement of statistical precision (i.e., overly narrow confidence intervals), and the findings should therefore be interpreted with appropriate caution. Third, reliance on retrospective self-reports at a single time point may underestimate exposure to peer bullying and childhood maltreatment experiences, compared to prospective, multiple measurements. That said, such retrospective measures often capture more typical instances and help reduce false positives. Fourth, childhood maltreatment was operationalized as a binary variable based on the moderate-to-severe CTQ-SF cut-off scores. This approach may have obscured variability in maltreatment severity and excluded the potential effects of mild maltreatment. Finally, the study did not examine the effects of specific subtypes of bullying and maltreatment with suicidal behavior, which future research could address this gap to enable more targeted prevention and intervention strategies.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides evidence of the specific risk-enhancing effects of peer bullying and childhood maltreatment on three stages of adolescent suicidal behaviors. Peer bullying is more strongly associated with suicidal ideation, while childhood maltreatment ultimately serves as the critical precipitating factor for suicide attempts. Tailored interventions for different stages of suicidal behavior are recommended. Given that the PAF of suicide attempts for adolescents with both peer bullying and childhood maltreatment is as high as 25%, strategies aimed at preventing the co-occurrence of peer bullying and childhood maltreatment is crucial to yield significant public health benefits.
