Biomimetic phycocyanin lipid-based nanoparticles ameliorate placental dysfunction and restore angiogenic balance in a pre-eclampsia model
Authors: Hongxia Yuan, Jian He, Jiahua Niu, Min Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Pre-eclampsia (PE) is a life-threatening pregnancy disorder associated with substantial maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality worldwide. Despite its clinical significance, current treatment strategies remain largely limited to symptomatic management, underscoring the urgent need for targeted interventions that address underlying pathophysiology. Lipid-based nanoparticles (LBNPs) have emerged as promising platforms for placental drug delivery. However, achieving precise and efficient site-specific accumulation remains a major challenge. Herein, we developed trophoblast cell membrane–hybridized, phycocyanin (PC)-loaded lipid-based nanoparticles (LBNP-PC@M) to enhance placental targeting. By integrating the intrinsic bioactivity of phycocyanin with the biomimetic targeting capability of trophoblast membranes, LBNP-PC@M facilitates preferential accumulation in the placenta and subsequent localized release of the therapeutic cargo, thereby improving angiogenic balance and metabolic homeostasis. In an inflammation-induced PE model, LBNP-PC@M treatment enhances placental vascularization, mitigates fetal growth restriction, and alleviates key pathological features associated with PE. Multi-omics analyses, including transcriptomics and metabolomics, indicate that LBNP-PC@M modulates pathways related to angiogenesis, oxidative stress responses, and amino acid metabolism, while partially restoring dysregulated histamine metabolism in PE placentas. Collectively, this study presents a biomimetic nanoplatform for placenta-targeted delivery of bioactive compounds, offering a promising strategy for modulating placental dysfunction and advancing therapeutic approaches for PE and related placental disorders.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04498-9.

Introduction

Pre-eclampsia (PE) is defined as the new-onset of hypertension and proteinuria after 20 weeks of gestation [1]. It affects approximately 4–5% of pregnancies globally and remains a leading cause of maternal and fetal morbidity and mortality [2, 3]. Defective placental development is the central pathogenesis of PE. During normal pregnancy, extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs) migrate deeply into the maternal decidua and myometrium, replacing the endothelial lining of spiral arteries and transforming these vessels into high-capacity, low-resistance conduits that ensure adequate blood flow to the developing placenta. This process is essential for establishing efficient maternal–fetal circulation and maintaining a stable intrauterine environment. In PE, by contrast, multiple risk factors including genetic abnormalities or immune disorders impair the invasion of placental EVTs into the uterine myometrium, hindering spiral artery remodeling and disturbing placental blood flow. Cumulative damage further induces placental hypoxia and oxidative stress, triggering aberrant secretion of antiangiogenic factors such as soluble Fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1). The upregulated sFlt-1 sequesters vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and placental growth factor (PlGF), impairing vasodilation and endothelial function, thereby driving the systemic manifestations of PE [4].

Due to the incomplete understanding of PE’s underlying mechanism, there is currently no recognized therapy for predicting or slowing its progression. Clinically, premature delivery remains the only definitive cure for PE [1]. Other therapeutic interventions are limited to symptom control, including pravastatin for blood pressure management and magnesium sulfate for seizure prevention [5]. Nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems have revolutionized the field of biomedicine, offering unique advantages such as improved drug stability, controlled release, and site-specific targeting [6], [7]. Among various nanocarriers, ionized lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) and lipid-based nanoparticles (liposome) [8] have gained considerable attention for placental delivery due to their biocompatibility, high loading capacity, and ability to protect cargo from degradation [9–12]. Previous preclinical studies have attempted to deliver siRNA, small-molecule inhibitors, or VEGF mRNA to the placenta to downregulate sFlt-1 or promote angiogenesis [13–18] in PE. However, those approaches face significant challenges such as non-specific delivery. To enhance delivery efficiency and site-specific release, modified LNPs have also been explored, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) antibody-conjugated LNPs to reduce liver accumulation and improve placenta uptake [19]. However, the efficacy of targeted LNP systems could be affected by the altered receptor expression in diseases like tumor. To address these challenges, biomimetic nanocarriers engineered with cell membrane coatings have emerged as a promising strategy, leveraging the inherent homotypic recognition and immune evasion properties of natural cell membranes [20, 21].

Phycocyanin (PC) is a pigment-binding protein derived from microalgae, with well-documented antioxidant [22, 23], anticancer [24], anti-inflammation [25] and reno-protection [26] properties. Studies have shown that PC exerts its biological functions by activating Nrf2/HO-1 pathway through its subunit phycocyanobilin (PCB) [25]. HO-1 (Heme oxygenases-1) is a key enzyme that catalyzes heme into biliverdin, exerting potential antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects. It is mainly produced in macrophages residing in the liver and spleen and is inducible in response to heme, pro-inflammatory cytokines, or growth factors [27]. Nevertheless, HO-1 is highly expressed in placentas of human, mice and rats, playing a critical role in placental development, including trophoblast invasion, spiral artery remodeling, and maintenance of vascular homeostasis [28]. Notably, reduced HO-1 expression has been implicated in PE pathogenesis, either directly or through its metabolites [29, 30]. HO-1 negatively regulates sFlt-1 and soluble endoglin (sENG), two key antiangiogenic factors in PE, thereby alleviating endothelial dysfunction and vascular impairment [31–33]. Decreased HO-1 expression in PE may result from placental hypoxia or hemolysis, leading to local accumulation of hemoglobin and excessive oxidative stress [29]. Given these properties, PC represents a promising therapeutic agent for PE. However, its poor bioavailability and lack of tissue specificity limit its clinical application [34].

Herein, we designed trophoblast cell membrane-hybridized PC-loaded lipid-based nanoparticles (LBNP-PC@M) to achieve targeted delivery of PC to the placenta. Lipid-based carriers exhibit excellent biocompatibility, improved stability for protein therapeutics, and efficient fusion with biological membranes, making them particularly suitable for protein delivery and biomimetic membrane hybridization strategies. Their lipid composition closely resembles that of natural cell membranes, which facilitates membrane coating and enhances cellular uptake [35]. Specifically, the trophoblast cell membrane hybridization endows the nanoparticles with biomimetic targeting capability, while the LBNP core ensures stable encapsulation and controlled release of PC. We systematically characterized the physicochemical properties, targeting efficiency, and therapeutic efficacy of LBNP-PC@M in vitro and in vivo. Multi-omics analyses were employed to decipher the underlying molecular and metabolic mechanisms. Our results demonstrate that LBNP-PC@M efficiently targets the placenta, restores angiogenesis, rebalances metabolic homeostasis, and alleviates PE-related pathologies, providing a safe and effective precision therapeutic strategy for PE.

Results

Preparation and characterization of LBNP-PC@M

The LBNP-PC@M fabrication process involved LNP-PC synthesis via thin-film hydration and ultrasonic emulsification, followed by trophoblast membrane hybridization through extrusion (Fig. 1a). UV-Vis-NIR spectroscopy confirmed PC incorporation into LBNPs, with a characteristic absorbance peak at 620 nm for both LBNP-PC and LBNP-PC@M (Fig. 1b). The encapsulation efficiency (EE) of LBNP-PC was determined by the absorbance at 620 nm according to concentration-absorbance curve of PC (Fig. S1), which is around 20.8%. The modest EE may be attributed to PC’s limited lipophilicity, which restricts its partitioning into the hydrophobic lipid matrix and consequently reduces encapsulation efficiency. Blank LBNPs exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of around 100 nm, while LBNP-PC and LBNP-PC@M had diameters of 200 nm and 230 nm, respectively (Fig. 1c-e, S2), consistent with TEM (transmission electron microscope) observations of core-shell structures with a distinct membrane layer (Fig. 1f). The increase in nanoparticle size following PC loading can be attributed to the incorporation of phycocyanin, a relatively large and hydrophilic protein, into the lipid architecture. This incorporation may induce swelling of the lipid bilayer and alter the internal structural organization, leading to an increase in the hydrodynamic diameter. Furthermore, subsequent coating with cell membrane components introduces an additional lipid bilayer or multilayered structure on the nanoparticle surface, thereby further increasing the overall particle size.

We next evaluated the physicochemical stability of LBNP-PC@M under various conditions. The nanoparticles maintained good colloidal stability in water, PBS, and cell culture medium, with minor variation in hydrodynamic size over time (Fig. S3a, b). In vitro release profile revealed that nanoparticles exhibited a sustained release behavior, with cumulative release reaching approximately 25% at 12 h and 40% at 36 h. A gradual increase in drug release was observed over time, indicating a controlled diffusion process rather than rapid leakage. (Fig. S3c). To visualize the internalization of LBNP-PC@M by cells, we labeled the components of LBNP-PC@M with DiA and DiL. The natural PC has spontaneous emission fluorescence at 620 nm, and DiA-labeled LBNPs and DiL-labeled trophoblast membranes confirmed the colocalization of PC, LBNP core, and cell membrane (PCC, Pearson’s correlation coefficient = 0.65), verifying successful LBNP-PC@M integration and endocytosis by HTR8 cells (Fig. 1g, h). The PCC value (0.65) reflects the intrinsic structural features of membrane-hybridized nanoparticles, arising from heterogeneous membrane coverage, partial lipid fusion, and the nanoscale spatial distribution of fluorescent probes. Coomassie blue staining confirmed that LBNP-PC@M retained most of the specific proteins from the trophoblast cell membrane (Fig. 1i), suggesting preserved placental recognition ability. Additionally, LBNP-PC@M expressed the trophoblast cell membrane marker HLA-C, indicating its potential for placental targeting (Fig. 1j). These results collectively supported the formation of membrane hybrid lipid-based nanoparticles. Compared with free PC, LBNP-PC@M significantly enhanced cellular uptake efficiency (Fig. 1k, l). This improvement can be attributed to the biomimetic membrane coating, which facilitates homotypic recognition, and the lipid-based structure that promotes membrane fusion and endocytosis.

LBNP-PC@M exhibits superior pro-angiogenic effects in vitro

Given the critical role of impaired angiogenesis in PE pathogenesis, we evaluated the proangiogenic effects of LBNP-PC@M in vitro. PC has been reported to promote wound healing by enhancing cell migration [36–38]. To reveal the benefits of PC in mitigating PE, cell viability assay was performed to determine the effects of LBNP-PC@M in HTR8 (Human Trophoblast Cells) and HUVEC (Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells). The results demonstrated that LBNP-PC@M significantly enhanced HUVEC viability (121 ± 8% of control) (Fig. 2a), indicating that LBNP-PC@M promotes the proliferation of vascular endothelial cells without exhibiting cytotoxicity toward trophoblast cells. The differential responses of HUVEC and HTR8 to LBNP-PC@M may be attributed to the fact that under normal physiological conditions, trophoblast cells are regulated by multiple hormones and cytokines. When the cells were incubated with different concentrations of LBNP-PC@M, 100 µg/mL had a promoting effect on HUVEC cells (Fig. 2b). The cell viability assay proved that LBNP-PC@M benefited vascular endothelial cells viability.

Angiogenesis is a vital process for vascular endothelial cells and placental development. To mimic PE-related impaired angiogenesis, HUVECs were treated with tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) to induce inflammatory injury. The migration assay showed that TNF-α significantly inhibited cell migration, while LBNP-PC@M reversed the inhibitory effect on HUVEC migration (migration area increased 52.7 ± 0.7%) more effectively than LBNP-PC (migration area increased 40 ± 6.5%) (Fig. 2c, d). Tube formation assays further confirmed the superior proangiogenic activity of LBNP-PC@M. TNF-α drastically disrupted tube formation, as shown by the reduced number of meshes, tube length, and junctions compared with the control group. In contrast, LBNP-PC@M remarkably restored angiogenic parameters (number of meshes, master segments, tube length, and junctions) to levels comparable to the control group (Fig. 2e-l), outperforming LBNP-PC. LBNP-PC also demonstrated certain alleviating effects, which could be ascribed to the intrinsic anti-inflammatory effects of PC in vitro. However, LBNP-PC@M exhibited superior effects compared to LBNP-PC, indicating its advantages in angiogenesis. These results collectively confirm that LBNP-PC@M effectively promotes angiogenesis in vitro by enhancing endothelial cell migration and tube formation, supporting its potential as a PE therapeutic.

LBNP-PC@M stabilizes tight junctions and regulates angiogenic signaling pathways

During early placentation, extravillous trophoblasts (EVTs) migrate from the anchoring villi into the decidua and progressively invade the spiral arteries. Through this process, EVTs infiltrate the vessel walls and replace endothelial cells, ultimately leading to vascular remodeling from an endothelial to a trophoblast-lined structure. In PE, this process is disrupted, as impaired trophoblast invasion and insufficient spiral artery remodeling result in defective placental angiogenesis and restricted maternal–fetal nutrient exchange [39, 40]. Given that cellular tight junctions (TJs) play a crucial role in maintaining vascular permeability and barrier integrity [41], we next evaluated the effects of LBNP-PC@M on endothelial and epithelial barrier function. To this end, transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) assays were performed in an inflammation-induced in vitro model of PE (Fig. 3a). From our results, TNF-α treatment significantly reduced ΔTEER (endpoint minus initial value) in both HUVEC and HTR8 monolayers, indicating disrupted cell-cell adhesion. However, LBNP-PC@M effectively restored monolayer integrity and barrier function (Fig. 3b, c). In addition, immunofluorescence results also showed that TNF-α devastated the TJs in the in-vitro PE model while LBNP-PC@M recovered the expression of TJs marker ZO-1 (Fig. 3d, e, S4). Western blot confirmed that LBNP-PC@M upregulated the TJs family CLDN1 and Occludin in TNF-α induced PE model in vitro (Fig. 3f, S5, S6), further strengthen the regulatory functions of PC in cell-cell interference. Interestingly, a modest increase in CLDN1 is observed in the LBNP group and LBNP-PC also showed an increase in occludin and ZO-1 (Fig. 3f, S6). This effect may be partially attributed to the lipid components, which could influence membrane stability or tight junction protein expression in endothelial cells.

Clinically, placental hypoxia in PE induces abnormal secretion of HIF-1α, which promotes the release of antiangiogenic proteins such as sFlt-1, leading to endothelial dysfunction and reduced vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression [5]. To evaluate trophoblast-related functions including invasion and cellular response, TNF-α was given to HTR8 cells to induce inflammatory PE model in vitro, and qPCR results showed that TNF-α treated group had significantly increased HIF-1α and sFlt-1expressions, while LBNP-PC@M treatment effectively downregulated these two factors (Fig. 3g, h). Vascular endothelial malfunction induced by elevated sFlt-1 impairs angiogenesis, characterized by truncated expressions of VEGF. To assess vascular endothelial function and angiogenic responses, HUVEC was treated with TNF-α to mimic in vitro angiogenic impairments. The protein level of VEGF, including VEGF-A and VEGF-B were significantly restored by LBNP-PC@M after TNF-α inducement, further confirming its proangiogenic effects in PE (Fig. 3i-l).

LBNP-PC@M enhances placental targeting and improves PE-related pathologies in vivo

Cell membrane derived from trophoblast cells inherited their recognition ability of the placenta and decidua [42], as HLA-C expressed by trophoblast cells can be recognized by decidual NK cells [43]. To verify the placental targeting ability of LBNP-PC@M, pregnant mice (E16.5) were injected with LBNP-PC@M and organic distribution was analyzed 6 h later (Fig. 4a). Given the intrinsic fluorescence of PC, we can directly calculate the drug accumulation among different organs. Compared with LBNP-PC, LBNP-PC@M significantly increased PC accumulation in the placenta, while no significant statistical differences were observed in other organs (Fig. 4b-i, Fig.S7). The fluorescence signal observed in the liver and kidneys may be associated with hepatic uptake and renal clearance of nanoparticle degradation products. The placenta-to-kidney and placenta-to-liver ratios of LBNP-PC@M were significantly higher than those of LBNP-PC, confirming enhanced placental targeting specificity (Fig. 4d, e). Specifically, no fluorescence was imaged in fetus, indicating LBNP-PC@M achieved excellent target release while minimized the potential influence in fetus (Fig. S8, 9, 10). Though fluorescence intensity in fluorescence imaging does not directly correlate with absolute nanoparticle accumulation, as it can be influenced by tissue optical properties and nanoparticle microenvironment, this excellent placenta targeting feature showed promising applications of LBNP-PC@M in placental disorders. Most importantly, no accumulation or abnormalities were observed in fetal organs, strengthening the biosafety of LBNP-PC@M in fetal developments under the experimental conditions (Fig. S10).

Next, we used LPS to establish pre-eclampsia in vivo. Pregnant mice were injected with LPS on E7.5 [17], followed by LBNP-PC@M administration from E11.5 to E13.5. Body weight was recorded daily, and samples were collected on E17.5 (Fig. 4j). According to body weight recording, LPS injection caused significant weight loss in pregnant mice, indicating possible fetal growth restriction (FGR). LBNP-PC@M treatment led to a moderate weight gain (Fig. 4k). This might help validating the relief of pre-eclampsia upon LBNP-PC@M administration. The decreased urine protein indicated the relief of PE by LBNP-PC@M (Fig. 4l) as proteinuria is another key characteristic of PE. Serum sFlt-1 level also proved the contributive effect of LBNP-PC@M on PE (Fig. 4m). In inflammation-induced PE mice, sFlt-1 was significantly increased. After treatment, sFlt-1 was reduced. Fetal development assessments revealed that LBNP-PC@M partially reversed LPS-induced FGR, restoring fetal and placental weight and size (Fig. 4n-p). The relatively modest magnitude of change may be attributed to the inherent variability of the inflammation-induced PE model and the multifactorial regulation of fetal growth. Importantly, the observed trend toward improvement is consistent with the overall restoration of placental function and angiogenesis, supporting the therapeutic efficacy of LBNP-PC@M.

Overall, these results confirm the favorable placental targeting and therapeutic potential of LBNP-PC@M in promoting placental development.

LBNP-PC@M exhibits high biocompatibility and biosafety in vivo

In vitro biosafety assays showed that LBNP-PC@M increased HUVEC viability and had no toxic effects on HTR8 cells. To evaluate in vivo biosafety, pregnant mice were injected with PC, LBNP, LBNP-PC, or LBNP-PC@M for 10 days during the second trimester, and blood and major organs were collected on E17.5 (Fig. 5a). Hematological analysis showed no significant differences in white blood cells (WBC), neutrophils, lymphocytes, hemoglobin (HGB), red blood cells (RBC), platelets (PLT), or monocytes between the treatment groups and the control group (NT). Biochemical parameters including alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (UREA), creatinine (CREA), and uric acid (UA) were also comparable to the NT group (Fig. 5b-m). Additionally, morphological results of the major organs including heart, liver, spleen, lung and kidney all had no visible morphological abnormalities among all groups, which further proved the safety of LBNP-PC@M in vivo (Fig. 5n).

LBNP-PC@M promotes placental angiogenesis via Nrf2/HO-1 axis activation

Proangiogenesis effect has always been one of the most important goal to alleviate PE [44]. We then investigated the proangiogenic effect of LBNP-PC@M as a therapeutic option for the PE in vivo. From H&E staining, LPS injection caused a decreased density of trophoblast cells in labyrinth zone (which contains maternal blood sinusoids and fetal blood capillaries), indicating impaired angiogenesis and maternal-fetal exchange, while LBNP-PC@M increased trophoblast cell density (Fig. 6a). IHC results presented the downregulated expression of VEGF and CD31 (key markers for vasculature [45, 46]) in LPS-induced PE. With the treatment of LBNP-PC@M, VEGF and CD31 regained its expression (Fig. 6a, b, c, S11). E-cadherin (E-cad) is an important protein responsible for cell-cell adhesive junction and trophoblastic invasion [47]. In normal trophoblast differentiation, the extravillous cytotrophoblast undergoes an epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), during which epithelial cells loss some expressions of E-cad and dysregulations of EMT is associated with impaired trophoblast cells invasion and spiral artery remodeling [48]. In PE, E-cad is reported to be upregulated [49]. From our results, E-cad is also upregulated in LPS-induced PE, indicating the defects in trophoblast invasion. However, LBNP-PC@M downregulated E-cad, re-balancing the trophoblast function (Fig. 6a, d). In vitro study also showed the altered expression of β-catenin, the downstream of E-cad (Fig. S12, 13). The restored β-catenin in LBNP-PC@M evidenced the activation of Wnt signaling pathway in placenta, which was important in angiogenesis [50]. Moreover, HIF-1α expression was also modified with LBNP-PC@M treatment. From our results, HIF-1α had higher expression in junction zone of placenta, which is responsible for immune privilege and vascular remodeling. In PE a higher expression of HIF-1α represents placental hypoxia [51]. LBNP-PC@M not only regulated HIF-1α to a normal level but also upregulated HO-1, a key protector against inflammation and oxidative stress [52] (Fig. 6a, e, f). The regulatory effects of LBNP-PC@M in HIF-1α and HO-1 presented the promoting effects of PC in treating placental diseases. Transcriptomic analysis reveals multi-pathway regulation by LBNP-PC@M

To gain insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of LBNP-PC@M, transcriptomic analysis was performed on PE placentas. Specifically, LPS-introduced PE placentas were used to compare with NT group to identify pathogenic differences. Then, LBNP-PC@M (PC in short in this section) treated group were compared with LPS group to investigate the involved pathways.

Comparison of LPS-induced PE placentas (LPS group) with normal placentas (NT group) identified 2804 upregulated and 2991 downregulated genes (Fig. 7a). KEGG enrichment analysis showed that upregulated genes were enriched in oxidative phosphorylation, apoptosis and Reactive oxygen species (ROS), indicating the possible damages in placenta (Fig. 7b), while downregulated genes were mainly enriched in amino acid metabolism, cell adhesion and VEGF pathway (Fig. 7c), consistent with placental hypoxia and dysfunction in PE. Subsequently, LBNP-PC@M was applied to reverse PE progression. Comparison of LBNP-PC@M-treated placentas (PC group) with LPS-induced PE placentas identified 3489 upregulated and 3011 downregulated genes (Fig. 7d). The KEGG enrichment analysis of upregulated genes mainly concentrated on energy metabolism including glutathione and histidine (Fig. 7e) and cell adhesion, while downregulated genes concentrated on pathways like autophagy, TNF signaling pathway, TGF-beta signaling pathway and HIF-signaling pathway (Fig. 7f). PCA analysis of three group favored the distinct gene profiles (Fig. 7g). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed that compared with NT group, LPS had reduced immune response and glutathione metabolism and increased oxidative phosphorylation (Fig. 7h-j). In contrast, LBNP-PC@M treatment upregulated glutathione metabolism, amino acid metabolism, and cell adhesion pathways (Fig. 7k-m). Metabolomic profiles of placenta from PE patients have also reported the downregulated glutathione metabolism pathway [55], indicating that LBNP-PC@M may have a promising effect in relieving PE through metabolism regulation. The representative heatmap of apoptosis, glutathione metabolism and cell adhesion pathway also showed the differential expressions among three groups (Fig. 7n-p). Consistent with transcriptomic analysis, western blot indicated that LPS induced-PE placenta had higher expression in HIF-1α, TNF-α, NLRP3 and Caspase3, indicating the activation of HIF-signaling, inflammatory pathway and apoptotic pathway. However, LBNP-PC@M deregulated those factors and activated NQO-1, indicating its function through HO-1 axis and other multiple pathways (Fig. 7q-v). In conclusion, the transcriptome profiles have unveiled a possible mechanism of PC in alleviating PE, through which PC may alleviate PE by restoring metabolic homeostasis and improving energy metabolism. Moreover, pathways including cell adhesion, HIF-1α and TNF pathway may all be under regulation of PC, proving the multifaceted functions of PC in biomedicine.

Mammalian placental development requires dynamic and proper metabolic regulation [56]. Transcriptome analysis has revealed that LBNP-PC@M treatment altered multiple metabolic pathways including glutathione, arginine, TCA and so on. To further investigate the impact of PE in placental metabolism and the potential target metabolite of LBNP-PC@M, we conducted metabolism analysis using mice placentas. From our results, LPS and LBNP-PC@M group showed distinct clusters of metabolites compared with NT, indicating the influence of PE in placenta metabolism and the possible metabolic regulation of LBNP-PC@M in PE placenta (Fig. 8a, S16). Then we focused on the differential metabolites between pairs. We noticed that among top differential metabolites, LPS significantly altered histamine compared with NT, while LBNP-PC@M significantly upregulated histamine compared with LPS (Fig. 8b, c). In physiological gestation, histamine is assumed to be a key mediator in feto-maternal interface, based on the functions of histamine in female steroids and vasoactive, cell growth and differentiation promoting effects [57, 58]. It is possible that LBNP-PC@M repaired placental development in PE through regulating histamine.

Discussion

PE remains a leading cause of maternal and perinatal mortality globally, accounting for approximately 70,000 maternal deaths and 500,000 fetal losses annually [65]. Current management strategies focus on premature delivery due to the lack of effective treatments to halt its progression. Nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems have emerged as a transformative tool in biomedicine, offering unique advantages for placental disorder treatment. By modifying nanoparticles with placental cell-surface ligands or homing molecules, selective recognition and uptake by placental cells can be achieved, enabling targeted delivery of therapeutic cargos. These cargos typically fall into two categories: proangiogenic agents (e.g., VEGF mRNA [66]) to restore placental vascular function, or inhibitors of pathogenic factors to block PE-driven signaling cascades [67]. The advantages of nanoparticle-based therapeutic systems primarily lie in their ability to enhance drug stability, prolong circulation time, and enable site-specific delivery. In the context of placental disorders, targeted delivery is particularly critical to maximize therapeutic efficacy while minimizing fetal exposure. However, nucleic acid therapeutics are inherently susceptible to degradation, require stringent dose control to minimize off-target effects, and typically modulate only a single pathway, such as VEGF upregulation-limiting their therapeutic scope. Furthermore, the ethical constraints of testing novel therapies in pregnant populations, given potential fetal harm, limit the available translational pathways, exacerbating the gap between preclinical promise and clinical application [68].

PC, a natural antioxidant and anti-inflammatory microalgae-driven protein, has been used as an effective ingredient of pharmaceuticals. Beyond its well-documented biomedical functions [69], the molecular mechanism of PC action has been increasingly clarified: its chromophore subunit, phycocyanobilin (PCB), contains a conserved binding motif for Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1). This interaction disrupts the Keap1-Nrf2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) complex, releasing Nrf2 to translocate to the nucleus and drive transcription of heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1). This reaction not only prevents heme-induced cellular sensitization to oxidative stress and apoptosis but also generates metabolites with independent cytoprotective effects.

In placental development, HO-1 is highly expressed and fulfills essential physiological roles: it is enriched in trophoblast cells, where it modulates EVTs invasion via upregulation of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ (PPARγ) [30, 70], a key regulator of trophoblast differentiation and spiral artery remodeling. In PE, this protective pathway is dysregulated: autoantibodies (e.g., angiotensin II type 1 receptor agonistic autoantibodies, endothelin-1 agonistic autoantibodies) activate pro-oxidative signaling cascades, leading to excessive HO-1 consumption. Reduced HO-1 expression diminishes heme metabolite production, triggering a cascade of antiangiogenic events—most notably upregulation of sFlt-1 [71]. This soluble VEGF receptor sequesters free VEGF and placental growth factor (PlGF), neutralizing their proangiogenic activity and exacerbating placental vascular dysfunction [72].

Against this backdrop, we developed a trophoblast cell membrane-hybridized PC lipid-based nanoparticle (LBNP-PC@M) system for precision PE therapy. This delivery platform integrates three critical features: (i) lipid-based nanoparticles (LBNPs) for stable encapsulation of PC, preventing premature cargo release; (ii) trophoblast cell membrane hybridization for biomimetic targeting, leveraging the inherent homotypic recognition of placental cells; and (iii) site-specific PC release in the placenta to reverse PE-associated pathogenic changes. The structural characteristics of the membrane-fused lipid nanoparticles endow LBNP-PC@M with relatively slow and sustained release behavior. Such a sustained release behavior is beneficial for maintaining drug stability and prolonging therapeutic exposure, which may enhance the overall bioavailability of PC in subsequent biological applications. Technically, LBNP-PC@M addresses the core limitations of current PE therapies by utilizing cell membrane hybridization to preserve key biological properties of trophoblast cells: this design not only mitigates maternal immune clearance but also significantly enhances placental accumulation–properties that could be extended to other placental disorders (e.g., fetal growth restriction, placental abruption) requiring targeted drug delivery.

Nevertheless, the current encapsulation efficiency (EE) of PC remains relatively low (approximately 20%), likely due to its high hydrophilicity. Although the present formulation has demonstrated encouraging in vitro and in vivo outcomes, further optimization is required to enhance EE and facilitate its translational application. For instance, approaches such as the double-emulsion solvent evaporation method and cold homogenization method have been reported to significantly improve encapsulation efficiency [73]. Strategies including modifying lipid composition [74], optimizing hydration conditions, or introducing active loading strategies [75] can enhance protein loading efficiency and further improve the translational potential.

Transcriptomic analysis further expanded our understanding of PC’s therapeutic role, revealing previously unrecognized effects on placental development. In LPS-induced PE models, we observed significant downregulation of pathways involved in amino acid metabolism (including glutathione and histidine metabolism)—key for maintaining redox homeostasis, energy production and pathways including VEGF and cell adhesion. LBNP-PC@M treatment reversed the apoptotic pathways and metabolic defects, suggesting PC may also act as a metabolic modulator to restore placental nutrition supply. This finding adds a new dimension to PC’s mechanism of action, highlighting its ability to target both signaling cascades and metabolic pathways—two interconnected drivers of PE-associated placental dysfunction. Further metabolism analysis exhibited the potential role of PC in regulating histamine in PE. Histamine maintenance is critical for feto-maternal communication and placental development [58, 76]. In PE, disturbed histamine degradation and subsequent histamine accumulation can be caused by reduced DAO activity arising from trophoblast dysfunction and excessive placental oxidative stress. Elevated histamine levels can induce vasoconstriction and endothelial dysfunction, which further aggravate placental oxidative stress and vascular impairment [58]. In this context, PC exhibits potent antioxidant activity by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 signaling pathway, thereby mitigating oxidative stress in placental tissues. The restoration of redox homeostasis may help preserve trophoblast function and promote the recovery of DAO expression, ultimately contributing to the normalization of histamine metabolism at the maternal–fetal interface. Thus, LBNP-PC@M might promote placenta development through histamine metabolism by restoring DAO level in placenta. Further investigation about the crosstalk between Nrf2/HO-1 and DAO is required.

While the current study is founded on an inflammation-driven PE model, it is crucial to take into account the potential applicability of our transcriptomic and metabolomic findings to other well-established PE models, such as the sFlt-1 overexpression model. These models vary in their primary pathogenic drivers. LPS-induced models mainly reflect inflammatory responses, while sFlt-1-based models more closely reproduce the angiogenic imbalance observed in clinical PE [77, 78]. Despite these disparities, evidence indicates that key downstream pathological features, including oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and impaired angiogenesis, are common across multiple PE models. In this regard, the pathways identified in our study, especially those associated with oxidative stress regulation, angiogenic signaling, and metabolic reprogramming, may represent common mechanistic nodes involved in PE progression. Notably, the modulation of the Nrf2/HO-1 axis and associated metabolic pathways observed herein could also be relevant in sFlt-1-driven pathology, where oxidative stress and vascular dysfunction play central roles. Nevertheless, direct validation in alternative models is necessary to determine the degree to which these molecular signatures are conserved. Future studies comparing multi-omics profiles across different PE models will further clarify the generalizability of these findings and support the translational potential of this therapeutic strategy.

From clinical translation, the safety and efficacy data of LBNP-PC@M demonstrated substantial translational potential. The central challenge in the treatment of PE lies in achieving an optimal balance between maternal therapeutic benefits and fetal safety. This study demonstrates that LBNP-PC@M not only alleviates fetal growth restriction (FGR) in PE model mice, but also induces no maternal hepatic or renal dysfunction following long-term administration. These findings suggest that combining natural PC protein-based therapeutics with biomimetic delivery systems may represent a promising strategy to address the “maternal-fetal safety dilemma.” The inherent no-toxicity of natural biopharmaceuticals, coupled with the precision of targeted delivery, minimizes fetal drug exposure and establishes a critical safety basis for future clinical translation.

Taken together, this study presents a biomimetic lipid-based nanoparticle platform for targeted modulation of placental function in a PE model. By integrating phycocyanin within a membrane-hybridized lipid carrier, the system demonstrated favorable placental accumulation and exerted protective effects on trophoblast and endothelial cell function under inflammatory conditions. Our findings suggest that PC may alleviate PE by restoring angiogenic balance and modulating metabolic homeostasis. In particular, the regulation of histamine metabolism through restoration of DAO expression may represent a previously underappreciated mechanism contributing to placental function recovery. These findings highlight the multifaceted therapeutic potential of PC when combined with advanced nanodelivery systems.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The current study is based on an inflammation-driven model and does not include comprehensive assessment of systemic clinical parameters such as blood pressure. In addition, although minimal fetal exposure was observed under the present conditions, further investigation using more sensitive approaches and alternative PE models will be required to fully evaluate transplacental transport and long-term safety.

Overall, this work provides a foundation for the development of placenta-targeted nanotherapeutics and offers new insights into the modulation of angiogenesis and oxidative stress in pre-eclampsia-related pathology.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a biomimetic nanoplatform (LBNP-PC@M) by hybridizing trophoblast cell membranes with PC-loaded LBNPs for targeted PE therapy. LBNP-PC@M exhibits superior placental targeting, controlled drug release, and therapeutic efficacy in vitro and in vivo. Mechanistically, LBNP-PC@M restores angiogenesis by activating the Nrf2/HO-1 axis, downregulating antiangiogenic factors, and stabilizing endothelial tight junctions. Multi-omics analyses reveal that LBNP-PC@M modulates key pathways involved in TNF, HIF signaling, cell adhesion, and metabolic homeostasis, particularly restoring histamine metabolism. Importantly, LBNP-PC@M exhibits excellent biocompatibility and biosafety, without inducing adverse effects on maternal organs or fetal development. This work provides a promising precision therapeutic strategy for PE, leveraging the advantages of biomimetic nanotechnology and natural therapeutic agents. Future studies will focus on optimizing the encapsulation efficiency of PC, investigating the long-term effects on fetal development, and translating this platform to clinical applications.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human trophoblast cell line HTR8 was kindly provided by East China Normal University (ECNU) and cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cell lines were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Synthesis of PC-loaded LBNPs (LBNP-PC)

Cholesterol (5 mg), lecithin (20 mg) and DSPE-PEG (2000) (5 mg) were dissolved in 4 ml trichloromethane in a round-bottom flask. After complete dissolution via ultrasonic water bath, the solution was transferred to a rotary evaporator to remove trichloromethane at 40 °C, forming a uniform lipid film on the flask wall. Subsequently, 5 mL of PC solution (5 mg/mL) was added to the flask and the mixture was ultrasonicated to achieve complete emulsification. The crude LBNP-PC suspension was centrifuged at 50,000×g for 1 h at 4 °C to collect sediments, which were then resuspended in 1 mL phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter for sterilization.

Preparation of trophoblast cell membrane-hybridized LBNP-PC (LBNP-PC@M)

HTR8 cell membrane were extracted using Membrane and Cytosol Protein Extraction Kit (Beyotime, P0033) following manufactory’s instructions. The prepared cell membrane was then mixed with LBNP-PC (1:1) and extruded with liposome extruder (200 nm and 100 nm) for 10 times. The final product, LBNP-PC@M, was stored at 4 °C for further use.

Characterization of LBNP-PC and LBNP-PC@M

The hydrodynamic diameter and polydispersity index (PDI) of LBNP, LBNP-PC, and LBNP-PC@M were measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS, Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS90) at 25 °C. Samples were diluted in PBS prior to measurement. The morphology and core–shell structure of nanoparticles was examined by transmission electron microscopy. Samples were negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid before imaging. UV–Vis–NIR spectroscopy (Shimadzu UV-2600) was used to confirm PC loading, with characteristic absorbance recorded in the range of 450–700 nm.

PC loading analysis

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) of PC in LBNPs was determined by UV–Vis spectroscopy. Briefly, LBNP-PC was centrifuged at 50,000 ×g for 1 h at 4 °C to separate free PC in the supernatant. The absorbance of PC at 620 nm was measured, and the concentration was calculated using a pre-established standard calibration curve (Figure S1). EE (%) was calculated as: EE (%) = [(total PC−free PC)/total PC] × 100%.

In vitro drug release

The in vitro release profile of PC from LBNP-PC@M was evaluated using a dialysis method. Briefly, nanoparticle suspensions (equivalent to 1 mg PC) were placed into dialysis bags (MWCO 70 kDa) and immersed in 40 mL of PBS (pH 7.4). The system was maintained at 37 °C with gentle shaking (100 RPM). At predetermined time points (0, 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48 and 72 h), 1 mL of release medium was collected and quantified by UV–Vis spectroscopy at 620 nm. The cumulative release percentage was calculated based on a standard calibration curve.

Stability study

The storage stability of LBNP-PC@M was evaluated over 3 days. Nanoparticles were stored in water, PBS and cell culture medium (1640) at room temperature. At predetermined time points, samples were collected to measure hydrodynamic size using dynamic light scattering. All measurements were performed in triplicate.

In vitro dosing regimens

For in vitro experiments, cells were treated with free PC, LBNP-PC, or LBNP-PC@M at equivalent PC concentrations of 100 µg/mL unless otherwise specified. For inflammatory injury models, TNF-α was applied at a concentration of 100 ng/mL for 24 h prior to nanoparticle treatment.

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8. HTR8 and HUVEC cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells/well and incubated overnight. After treatment with LBNP, LBNP-PC and LBNP-PC@M for 48 h or different concentrations of LBNP-PC@M for 48 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was expressed as a percentage relative to untreated controls.

Cell migration assay

Cells were seeded in a 6 well plate and allowed to form a confluent monolayer. A sterile micropipette tip was used to create a scratch across the monolayer, which was then washed with PBS to remove cell debris. TNF-α was applied to mimic inflammation-induced PE model. Fresh medium containing the corresponding treatments was added, and the scratch width was observed and imaged at predetermined time points. Migration areas were quantified using ImageJ software.

Tube formation assay

24-well plate was coated with 200 µL Matrigel (Beyotime, C0372) and incubated to solidify. HUVECs (200,000 cells/well) were seeded onto the Matrigel with or without treatments in cells pre-treated with TNF-α. Pictures were captured every 2 h. The number of tubes (meshes) and other angiogenic parameters were quantified using the ImageJ Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin.

Transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) assay

TEER was measured using a R/V Meter for Epithelium. HTR8 or HUVEC cells were seeded on Transwell inserts (0.4 μm pore size) independently and cultured until a confluent monolayer formed. TEER values were recorded before and after treatment. The final TEER values were calculated by subtracting the blank insert value and multiplying by the membrane area (Ω·cm²). Changes in TEER (ΔTEER) were defined as the difference between endpoint and initial values.

In vitro fluorescence microscopy

For cellular uptake studies, LBNPs were labeled with DiA and trophoblast membranes with DiL. Cells were seeded on glass-bottom dishes and incubated with labeled nanoparticles for 4–6 h at 37 °C. After incubation, cells were washed three times with PBS and nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33,342. Images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope. Colocalization analysis was performed using ImageJ software to calculate Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC).

For immunofluorescent staining, cells were seeded onto tissue culture (TC)-treated glass coverslips placed in a 24-well plate. After pre-treated with TNF-α, cells were then cultured with LBNP-PC@M formula for 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were rinsed twice with pre-cooled PBS to remove residual medium and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at room temperature for 30 min. Then the fixed cells were incubated with 0.5% Triton X-100 solution for 20 min at room temperature for cell membrane permeabilization, followed by the blockage of non-specific binding sites with 5% BSA solution for 1 h. After blocking, the cells were rinsed for three times with TBST buffer (PBS supplemented with 0.5% Triton X-100 and 0.5% Tween-20) for 5 min per wash to eliminate excess blocking reagent. The cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the relevant primary antibodies. Following three additional TBST rinses (5 min per wash), the cells were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies and DAPI for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Finally, the coverslips were carefully mounted onto glass slides using anti-fade mounting medium, and fluorescent images were acquired using a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal laser scanning microscope.

Animal model

Pregnant ICR mice (10 weeks in age) used in this study were purchased from Charles River Laboratories and maintained in standard SPF environment. All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (Approval No. ZJU20251009) and conducted in strict accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The PE model was established by intraperitoneal injection of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) at a dose of 1 µg/kg on gestational day 7.5 (E7.5) [17]. Mice were randomly divided into five groups. Body weight of pregnant mice was recorded daily from E8.5 to E16.5. For in vivo studies, LBNP-PC or LBNP-PC@M was administered via tail vein injection at a dose equivalent to 25 mg/kg PC per injection. Treatments were performed once daily from gestational day E11.5 to E13.5. For biodistribution studies, nanoparticles were injected at the same dose and fluorescence signals were analyzed 6 h post-injection.

RNA extraction and Quantitative Real-Time PCR (qPCR)

Cells were collected and lysed and placenta tissue were grinded in the tissue homogenizer at 60 Hz, 45 s per round 3 times in Trizol Reagent. Total RNA Isolation Kit (Vazyme, RC113-01) was applied. The subsequent reverse transcription and qPCR were executed exactly as the instructions (Vazyme, Q711-01). The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal reference. Primer sequences are listed in supplementary Table 1.

Western blot

Cell or tissue lysates (20 µg) were denatured, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After washing with TBST three times, membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Chemiluminescent signals were detected using a Servicebio SCGW-3000 Chemiluminescent Imaging System. Antibodies and the dilution ratio were in supplementary Table 2.

H&E staining and Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Placentas were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4 °C for 24 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned into 5 μm slices. For H&E staining, sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, stained with hematoxylin for 2 min and eosin for 1 min, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with neutral balsam. For IHC, deparaffinized and rehydrated sections were subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 95 °C for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% H₂O₂ for 10 min at room temperature, and non-specific binding was blocked with 5% goat serum for 30 min. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Placental morphology was observed using a 3D Histech MIDI panoramic scanner.

Placental transcriptome analysis

The placental transcriptome analysis was provided by BGI Tech. Briefly, placentas was prepared for RNA extraction and mRNA enrichment by oligo-dT magnetic beads and fragmented. The constructed library is amplified using PCR with high-fidelity polymerases to enrich for DNA fragments with adapters. Sequencing is performed using Illumina HiSeq. After data quality control, the clean reads are aligned to a reference transcriptome and differentially expressed genes were revealed. Subsequently, the functional enrichment analysis is performed to reveal the biological progressions, pathways, or networks which are significantly associated with the differentially expressed genes using tools such as GO (Gene Ontology) and KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise stated. GraphPad Prism 10 software was used for graph generation and statistical analysis. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare differences among more than two groups. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).
