On the classical Reinforcement problem and Optimisation
E. Cristoforoni, C. Nitsch, C. Trombetti

Abstract
In the present survey, we consider the classical reinforcement problem for elliptic boundary value problems originally studied by Sanchez-Palencia in 1969. We focus on the seminar papers by Brezis, Caffarelli, & Friedman, and by Acerbi & Buttazzo, and discuss the related optimisation problems proposed by Friedman and by Buttazzo.

1
Introduction

Many problems in mathematical physics, such as in elasticity, electrostatics, or thermal conduction, are governed by elliptic (or parabolic) equations or variational inequalities whose coefficients have a jump across a smooth surface
S
. These models describe the behaviour of a physical quantity,
u
, in a medium,
D
, composed of two phases,
\Omega
and
\Sigma
. The two phases come into contact across the smooth interface
S=\partial\Omega\cap\partial\Sigma
, and have different physical properties, such as elasticity or diffusivity coefficients, described by positive parameters
k_{\Omega}
and
k_{\Sigma}
. Let
f
be a given function. The prototype equation one should think of is the following

\begin{cases}-k_{\Omega}\Delta u=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
-k_{\Sigma}\Delta u=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
u|_{\Omega}=u|_{\Sigma}&\text{on }S,\\[5.0pt]
k_{\Omega}\partial_{\nu}u|_{\Omega}=k_{\Sigma}\partial_{\nu}u|_{\Sigma}&\text{on }S\setminus\partial D,\\[5.0pt]
u=0&\text{ on }\partial D,\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\nu
is normal to
S
. The condition

u|_{\Omega}=u|_{\Sigma}

across the interface
S
, is a continuity condition which models the fact that the two phases
\Omega
and
\Sigma
are in perfect contact with each other, while the condition

k_{\Omega}\partial_{\nu}u|_{\Omega}=k_{\Sigma}\partial_{\nu}u|_{\Sigma}\quad\text{on }S\setminus\partial D,

(1.2)

models the continuity of the flux across the interface. Condition (
1.2
) is known as transmission, or diffraction, condition.

More generally, let
A_{1},A_{2}
be a positive definite symmetric matrices. We can consider the following diffraction problem

\begin{cases}-k_{\Omega}\divv(A_{1}\nabla u)=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
-k_{\Sigma}\divv(A_{2}\nabla u)=f&\text{in }\Sigma,\\[5.0pt]
u|_{\Omega}=u|_{\Sigma}&\text{on }S\setminus\partial D,\\[5.0pt]
k_{\Omega}\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}u|_{\Omega}=k_{\Sigma}\partial_{\nu_{A_{2}}}u|_{\Sigma}&\text{on }S\setminus\partial D,\\[5.0pt]
u=0&\text{ on }\partial D,\end{cases}

(1.3)

and
\partial_{\nu_{A}}u
is the derivative in the conormal direction
\nu_{A}=A\nu
.
A weak solution to (
1.1
) is simply a function
u\in H^{1}_{0}(D)
such that

k_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}A_{1}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx+k_{\Sigma}\int_{\Sigma}A_{2}\nabla u\cdot\nabla\varphi\,dx=\int_{D}f\varphi\,dx,

for every
\varphi\in H^{1}_{0}(D)
, or, equivalently, the minimiser in
H^{1}_{0}(D)
of the energy

k_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}A_{1}\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\,dx+k_{\Sigma}\int_{\Sigma}A_{2}\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\,dx-2\int_{D}fv\,dx.

Existence and regularity for the solutions to (
1.3
) have been widely studied in the ’60s, in particular, if
A_{1}\in C^{1,\gamma}(\bar{\Omega}),A_{2}\in C^{1,\gamma}(\bar{\Sigma})
,
f\in C^{0,\gamma}(\bar{\Omega})\cap C^{0,\gamma}(\bar{\Sigma})
, and both the interface
S
and the outer boundary
\partial D
are of class
C^{1,1}
, then, the solution
u
, restricted to each phase, is
C^{2}
with first derivative continuos up to the boundary with possibly the exception of the points in
S\cap\partial D
. We refer to
[
48
, §3.16]
for the precise statement (see also
[
58
,
47
,
48
]
).

Of particular interest in many physical models is the case in which the thickness of one of the phases, say
\Sigma
, is much smaller than the characteristic size of
D
, and the constants
k_{\Omega}
and
k_{\Sigma}
differ widely. Mathematically, this consists of the study of the
asymptotic behaviour of the solutions
of (
1.3
) in the
limit
as both the
thickness
of
\Sigma
goes to zero and the
ellipticity constant

k_{\Sigma}
goes either to
zero
or to
infinity
.
In
elasticity
(or elasto-plasticity), the magnitude of the ellipticity constant is related to the material’s hardness: a small ellipticity constant corresponds to a
hard
material, whereas a large one corresponds to a
soft
material. In this context,
D
often represents the cross-section of a torqued beam composed of two (or more) phases. Then,
\Sigma
is seen as a small quantity of
extremely hard
(or
extremely soft
) material added to the remaining material,
\Omega
, to
reinforce
the beam. Given the previous interpretation, these types of problems are often referred to as
reinforcement problems
, sometimes further divided into
interior
reinforcement and
boundary
reinforcement. Of particular significance in the case of boundary reinforcement is also the point of view of thermal conduction, in which the set
\Omega
represents a conductor surrounded by an extremely thin layer,
\Sigma
, of extremely good (or extremely bad)
insulating material
, in such framework, the solution
u
in
D
represents the (steady-state)
temperature
of the configuration.

The survey is structured as follows.
We start with a brief history of the problem, outlining the original result by Sanchez-Palencia. We then, in
subsection 2.1
and
subsection 2.2
, prove the celebrated results by Brezis, Caffarelli & Friedman
[
11
]
and by Acerbi & Buttazzo
[
AB86
]
respectively. In
subsection 2.3
, we give an overview of subsequent developments with particular attention to the case of different boundary conditions. Finally, in
section 3
we discuss the optimisation problem naturally arising from the limit equations.

2
A brief history of the problem

Consider the problem of describing the temperature of a system, D, composed of a thin metal sheet in contact with a relatively poor conductor, such as soil or insulating material. This problem is briefly described in the second edition of the book
Conduction of Heat in Solids
by Carslaw, prepared by Jaeger in 1959 (see
[
22
]
). Let
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}\cap\set{z=0}
be a bounded planar set and let
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}=\omega\times(0,\varepsilon)
represent a thin metal sheet of constant thickness
\varepsilon>0
, whose faces are both covered by insulating material
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=D\setminus\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
so that the interface
S_{\varepsilon}
consists exactly of the set
\omega\times\set{0,\varepsilon}
(see
Figure 1
).

Figure 1:
Thin metal sheet
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
covered by insulating material
\Omega_{\varepsilon}=D\setminus\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
.

For simplicity’ sake, let
k_{\varepsilon}=k_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}
and
k=k_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}
. The steady-state temperature,
u_{\varepsilon}
, will be a solution to

\begin{cases}-k\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=f&\text{in }\Omega_{\varepsilon}\\[5.0pt]
-k_{\varepsilon}\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{in }\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\\[5.0pt]
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}=u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }S_{\varepsilon},\\[5.0pt]
k\partial_{\nu}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}=k_{\varepsilon}\partial_{\nu}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }S_{\varepsilon}\setminus\partial D,\\[5.0pt]
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{ on }\partial D.\end{cases}

(2.1)

In the book, the assumption made is that the metal sheet is so thin that the temperature in the sheet can be taken to be constant across its thickness. Then, considering the hat balance in the sheet, they simplify equation (
2.1
) by substituting the equation in
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
and the transmission condition on
S_{\varepsilon}
, with an effective boundary condition, of Wentzel type, relating the "two-dimensional" Laplacian on the flat surface of the sheet with the outward derivative in the direction orthogonal to it, that is

\begin{cases}-k\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=f&\text{in }\Omega_{\varepsilon},\\[5.0pt]
\partial_{z}u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{in }\Sigma_{\varepsilon},\\[5.0pt]
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}=u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }S_{\varepsilon},\\[5.0pt]
\varepsilon k_{\varepsilon}\left(\partial^{2}_{xx}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\partial^{2}_{yy}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\right)=-k\partial_{z}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }\omega\times\set{0},\\[5.0pt]
\varepsilon k_{\varepsilon}\left(\partial^{2}_{xx}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}+\partial^{2}_{yy}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\right)=k\partial_{z}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }\omega\times\set{\varepsilon},\\[5.0pt]
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{ on }\partial D.\end{cases}

Notice that, while
\varepsilon
is assumed to be small, the ratio
k_{\varepsilon}/k
, which represents the ratio of the thermal conductivities, is, by the physical assumptions on the problem, really high.

To our knowledge, the first rigorous mathematical proof of this phenomenon can be found in the work of Sanchez-Palencia
[
62
]
, published in 1969 (see also the paper by Hung and Sanchez-Palencia
[
46
]
, published in 1974). In the paper, the author studies the limit behaviour of the function
u_{\varepsilon}
, solution to (
2.1
), in the limit as
\varepsilon
goes to zero and
k_{\varepsilon}
diverges to infinity, the limit temperature then will be the solution to a boundary value problem depending on the limit,
\eta
, of the quantity

\varepsilon k_{\varepsilon}
. In particular, when
\eta
is strictly positive and finite, the function
u_{\varepsilon}
converges to the solution to the following boundary value problem

\begin{cases}-k\Delta u=f&\text{in }D\setminus\left(\omega\times\set{0}\right),\\[5.0pt]
u|_{\set{z>0}}=u|_{\set{z<0}}&\text{on }\omega\times\set{0},\\[5.0pt]
-\eta\left(\partial^{2}_{xx}u+\partial^{2}_{yy}u\right)=k\left(\partial_{z}u|_{\set{z>0}}-\partial_{z}u|_{\set{z<0}}\right)&\text{on }\omega\times\set{0},\\[5.0pt]
\partial_{\nu}u=0&\text{on }\partial\omega\times\set{0},\\[5.0pt]
u=0&\text{ on }\partial D.\end{cases}

When
\eta=0
, the effect of the plate becomes negligible, and the limit problem reduces to the usual Poisson problem

\begin{cases}-k\Delta u=f&\text{in }D,\\[5.0pt]
u=0&\text{on }\partial D.\end{cases}

On the other hand, if
\eta=+\infty
, in the limit, function
u
will be forced to be constant in
\omega\times\set{0}
and the limit boundary value problem will be the following

\begin{cases}-k\Delta u=f&\text{in }D\setminus\left(\omega\times\set{0}\right),\\[5.0pt]
u|_{\set{z>0}}=c=u|_{\set{z<0}}&\text{on }\omega\times\set{0},\\[5.0pt]
u=0&\text{ on }\partial D,\end{cases}

with the extra integral condition

\displaystyle\int_{\omega}\partial_{z}u|_{\set{z>0}}=\int_{\omega}\partial_{z}u|_{\set{z<0}}.

As noted by the author, from the point of view of electrostatics, the case
\eta=+\infty
corresponds to the definition, given in
[
60
]
(see also
[
59
, §4.16]
), of an infinitely conducting and infinitely thin plate.

In 1970, Sanchez-Palencia
[
63
]
(see also
[
64
]
) studied the opposite case, namely, a thin sheet of insulating material in contact with a good conductor. Mathematically, this corresponds to the study of the limit behaviour of the function
u_{\varepsilon}
, solution to (
2.1
), in the case in which both
\varepsilon
and
k_{\varepsilon}
go to zero. In such a case, the limit problem will depend on the limit,
\alpha
, of the quantity
k_{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon
. When
\alpha
is finite, the limit function will usually be discontinuous across
\omega\times\set{0}
, and will satisfy

\begin{cases}-k\Delta u=f&\text{in }D\setminus\left(\omega\times\set{0}\right),\\[5.0pt]
\partial_{z}u|_{\set{z>0}}=\partial_{z}u|_{\set{z<0}}&\text{on }\omega\times\set{0},\\[5.0pt]
k\partial_{z}u=\alpha\left(u|_{\set{z>0}}-u|_{\set{z<0}}\right)&\text{on }\omega\times\set{0},\\[5.0pt]
u=0&\text{ on }\partial D.\end{cases}

When
\alpha=+\infty
, the effect of the insulating sheet becomes negligible and the limit problem reduces to the usual Poisson problem in
D
.

Another interesting physical problem, described in the book by Carslaw and Jaeger, is the case of
a thin surface skin of poor conductor
,
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
, surrounding a body
\Omega
. Let
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
be the set of points at distance at most
\varepsilon>0
from
\Omega
, then, if
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, the temperature
u_{\varepsilon}
, solution to (
2.1
), can be approximated by substituting the equation in
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
with an effective boundary condition of Robin type, that is

\begin{cases}-k\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=f&\text{in }\Omega\\[5.0pt]
k\partial_{\nu}u_{\varepsilon}+\frac{k_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

This approximation is consistent with the results on boundary reinforcement by Caffarelli and Friedman
[
19
]
in elasto-plasticity, as well as the results of Brezis, Caffarelli & Friedman that we will discuss in the next section. In particular, if we denote by
\alpha
the limit of the quantity
k_{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon
, when
\alpha>0
is finite, the function
u_{\varepsilon}
, solution to (
2.1
), will converge to the solution of

\begin{cases}-k\Delta u=f&\text{in }\Omega\\[5.0pt]
k\partial_{\nu}u+\alpha u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

As before, when
\alpha=+\infty
, the effect of the surface layer
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
will become negligible. The case
\alpha=0
may be a bit more involved and will be analysed in
Theorem 2.5
.

2.1
The result by Brezis, Caffarelli & Friedman

In this section, we discuss the results proved by Brezis, Caffarelli & Friedman in
[
11
]
. In the paper, the authors prove convergence results for boundary and interior reinforcement problems for elliptic equations as well as the corresponding variational inequalities. As the techniques used in the various results are essentially the same, we will focus on the boundary reinforcement for elliptic equations.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded open set with
C^{1,1}
boundary, let
h\colon\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
be a strictly positive Lipschitz function. For every
\varepsilon>0
let

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}=\Set{\sigma+t\nu(\sigma)\colon\sigma\in\partial\Omega,\,t\in(0,\varepsilon h(\sigma))},

where
\nu
denotes the outward unit normal vector to
\partial\Omega
, an let
D_{\varepsilon}=\bar{\Omega}\cup\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
(see
Figure 2
).

Figure 2:
Reinforcement of
\Omega
with a thin set of variable thickness
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
.

Our regularity assumptions on
\Omega
ensure us that, for every
\varepsilon<\bar{\varepsilon}
sufficiently small, the map

(\sigma,t)\to\sigma+t\nu(\sigma)

is invertible on
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
, that is for every
x\in\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
there exist unique
\sigma(x)\in\partial\Omega
and
t(x)\in(0,\varepsilon h(\sigma))
, such that

x=\sigma(x)+t(x)\nu(\sigma(x)),

moreover
t(x)
coincides with the distance,
d(x)
, of
x
from
\Omega
, and
\sigma(x)
is the orthogonal projection on
\partial\Omega
.

Let
A_{1},A_{2}
be bounded and uniformly elliptic matrices with coefficients in
C^{1,\gamma}(\bar{\Omega})
and
C^{1,\gamma}(\bar{\Sigma}_{\bar{\varepsilon}})
, for some
\gamma>0
, respectively and denote by
\nu_{A_{1}}=A_{1}\nu
and
\nu_{A_{2}}=A_{2}\nu
the conormal vector to
\partial\Omega
from inside and outside
\Omega
respectively. Let
f\colon\bar{D}_{\bar{\varepsilon}}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function in
C^{1,\gamma}(\bar{\Omega})\cup C^{1,\gamma}(\bar{\Sigma}_{\bar{\varepsilon}})
, and let
k,k_{\varepsilon}
be two positive numbers with

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}k_{\varepsilon}=0.

For every
\varepsilon\in(0,\bar{\varepsilon})
, let
u_{\varepsilon}
be the solution to the diffraction problem

\begin{cases}-k\divv(A_{1}\nabla u_{\varepsilon})=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
-k_{\varepsilon}\divv(A_{2}\nabla u_{\varepsilon})=f&\text{in }\Sigma_{\varepsilon},\\[5.0pt]
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega}=u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
k\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega}=k_{\varepsilon}\partial_{\nu_{A_{2}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\partial D_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

(2.2)

We will study the limit of the function
u_{\varepsilon}
, as
\varepsilon
goes to zero, following the original proof by Brezis, Caffarelli, & Friedman. As mentioned in the introduction, such a limit will depend on the limit

\alpha=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{k_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\in[0,+\infty].

Remark 2.1
.

We remark that in the original paper
[
11
]
, the set
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
(denoted as
\Omega_{1}
in the paper) is not described as the
normal
graph of the function
h
, but rather as the
conormal
graph instead. This difference will result in a slightly more involved boundary condition in the limit problem; on the other hand, it will give us a clearer distinction between the role of the geometry and that of the operator. By the ellipticity of
A_{2}
we have that
\gamma_{A_{2}}=\nu_{A_{2}}\cdot\nu>c>0
uniformly on
\partial\Omega
and, by the regularity of
\Omega
we can write
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
as

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}=\Set{\sigma+t\nu_{A_{2}}(\sigma)\colon\sigma\in\partial\Omega,\,t\in(0,h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma))},

for some positive function
h_{\varepsilon}
. Using the regularity of
\Omega
and
h
, one may easily verify that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}=\gamma_{A_{2}}(\sigma),

(2.3)

uniformly on
\partial\Omega
.

Remark 2.2
.

We remark that by a change of variable, we can rewrite the integrals on
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
as

\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}v(x)\,dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}v(\sigma+t\nu_{A_{2}})\gamma_{A_{2}}(\sigma)(1+tq(t,\sigma))\,dt\,d\sigma,

where
q
is a bounded function. Moreover, by (
2.3
), if
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small we have

\dfrac{1}{C}\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}v(\sigma+t\nu_{A_{2}})\,dt\,d\sigma\leq\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}v(x)\,dx\leq C\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}v(\sigma+t\nu_{A_{2}})\,dt\,d\sigma.

(2.4)

from the previous equation, if
v\in H^{1}(\Sigma_{\varepsilon})
with zero trace on
\partial D_{\varepsilon}
we immediately have that

\int_{\partial\Omega}v^{2}\,d\sigma\leq\varepsilon C\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dx.

(2.5)

Indeed

v(\sigma)=-\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}}\partial_{\nu_{A_{2}}}v(\sigma+t\nu_{A_{2}})\,dt,

so that

\int_{\partial\Omega}v^{2}\,d\sigma\leq\int_{\partial\Omega}h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nu_{A_{2}}\rvert^{2}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dt\,d\sigma\leq\varepsilon C\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dx.

The main tool in the study of the limit behaviour of
u_{\varepsilon}
are the following
H^{2}
-estimates.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
u_{\varepsilon}
be the solution to (
2.2
). There exists a positive constant
C>0
, such that, for every
\varepsilon
sufficiently small, the following estimates hold

\int_{D_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}^{2}\,dx\leq C\left(1+\dfrac{\varepsilon^{2}}{k_{\varepsilon}^{2}}\right),

(2.6)

\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx+k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx\leq C\left(1+\dfrac{\varepsilon}{k_{\varepsilon}}\right),

(2.7)

\int_{\Omega}\lvert D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx+k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx\leq C\left(1+\dfrac{\varepsilon}{k_{\varepsilon}}\right),

(2.8)

where
D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}
is the Hessian of
u_{\varepsilon}
.

The previous lemma is based on classic elliptic estimates for diffraction problems in which we keep track of the ellipticity constant
k_{\varepsilon}
in
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
.

Case 1.
\alpha\in(0,+\infty]

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
u_{\varepsilon}
be the solution to equation (
2.2
). If

\alpha=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{k_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon}\in(0,+\infty],

then
u_{\varepsilon}
converges, uniformly in compact subsets of
\Omega
, to the function
u
solution to

\begin{cases}-k\divv(A_{1}\nabla u)=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\dfrac{hk}{\alpha\gamma_{A_{2}}}\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}u+u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(2.9)

Proof.

Let
u
be the solution to (
2.9
) and let
w_{\varepsilon}=u_{\varepsilon}-u
, then in
\Omega

w_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

\begin{cases}-k\divv(A_{1}\nabla w_{\varepsilon})=0&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\dfrac{hk}{\alpha\gamma_{A_{2}}}\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}w_{\varepsilon}+w_{\varepsilon}=R_{\varepsilon}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(2.10)

where

R_{\varepsilon}=\dfrac{hk}{\alpha\gamma_{A_{2}}}\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}u_{\varepsilon}+u_{\varepsilon}.

If we prove that
R_{\varepsilon}
converges to zero in
L^{2}(\Omega)
as
\varepsilon
goes to zero, we can use the Green function representation of the solution of (
2.10
) to prove that
w_{\varepsilon}
converges uniformly to zero in compact subsets of
\Omega
.

Recall that, by our assumptions, the functions
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega}
and
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}
are
C^{2}
with continuous first derivatives up to
\partial\Omega
. Let
\sigma\in\partial\Omega
, using the transmission conditions on
\partial\Omega
, we have

u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)+\dfrac{k}{k_{\varepsilon}}h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega}(\sigma)=u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)+h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)\partial_{\nu_{A_{2}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}(\sigma).

(2.11)

Notice that, by (
2.3
),

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)k}{k_{\varepsilon}}=\dfrac{h(\sigma)k}{\alpha\gamma_{A_{2}}(\sigma)},

uniformly on
\partial\Omega
.

From the Dirichlet boundary condition, we have

u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)=u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)-u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+h_{\varepsilon}\nu_{A_{2}})=-\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}\partial_{\nu_{A_{2}}}u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu_{A_{2}})\,dt,

while

\partial_{\nu_{A_{2}}}u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu_{A_{2}})=\int_{0}^{t}\partial^{2}_{\nu_{A_{2}}^{2}}u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+s\nu_{A_{2}})\,ds+\partial_{\nu_{A_{2}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}(\sigma).

Substituting in the previous equation, we get

u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)=-h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)\partial_{\nu_{A_{2}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\sigma_{\varepsilon}}(\sigma)-\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}\int_{0}^{t}\partial^{2}_{\nu_{A_{2}}^{2}}u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+s\nu_{A_{2}})\,ds\,dt,

so that, substituting the formula for
u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)
, in the right-hand side of (
2.11
), we have

u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)+\dfrac{k}{k_{\varepsilon}}h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega}(\sigma)=-\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}\int_{0}^{t}\partial^{2}_{\nu_{A_{2}}^{2}}u_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+s\nu_{A_{2}})\,ds\,dt.

Then if we take take square integral on
\partial\Omega
using the change of variable (
2.4
), and use the
H^{2}
-estimate (
2.8
), we have

\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\lvert u_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{k}{k_{\varepsilon}}h_{\varepsilon}\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega}\right\rvert^{2}\,d\sigma\leq C\varepsilon^{3}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert D^{2}u_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{2}\,dx\leq C\varepsilon^{2}\dfrac{\varepsilon}{k_{\varepsilon}}\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon}{k_{\varepsilon}}+C\right),

(2.12)

by the assumption on
\alpha
,
\varepsilon/k_{\varepsilon}
is bounded so that

\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\lvert u_{\varepsilon}+\dfrac{k}{k_{\varepsilon}}h_{\varepsilon}\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega}\right\rvert^{2}\,d\sigma\leq C\varepsilon^{2}.

On the other hand, by standard Sobolev inequalities and using estimates (
2.7
) and (
2.8

\Omega
we can assume
v\in W^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. recall that, when
\varepsilon
is sufficiently small, every
x\in\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
can be uniquely written as
x=\sigma(x)+d(x)\nu(\sigma(x))
, where
d(x)
is the distance from
\Omega
and
\sigma(x)
is the orthogonal projection of
\partial\Omega
, let
h(x)=h(\sigma(x))
and

\phi_{\varepsilon}(x)=\begin{cases}1&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
1-\dfrac{d(x)}{\varepsilon h(x)}&\text{if }x\in\Sigma_{\varepsilon},\\[5.0pt]
0&\text{if }x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus D_{\varepsilon}.\end{cases}

Then
0\leq\phi_{\varepsilon}\leq 1
and the function
v_{\varepsilon}=v\phi_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,p}_{0}(D_{\varepsilon})
, and
v_{\varepsilon}
converges to
v\chi_{\Omega}
in
L^{p}(\Omega)
. For every
t\in(0,1)
we have

\begin{split}\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})&=G(v)+k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\gamma(x,v\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}+\phi_{\varepsilon}\nabla v)\,dx\\[15.0pt]
&\leq G(v)+k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\left[t\lvert v\rvert^{p}\gamma\left(x,\dfrac{\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}}{t}\right)+(1-t)\phi_{\varepsilon}^{p}\gamma\left(x,\dfrac{\nabla v}{1-t}\right)\right]\,dx\\[15.0pt]
&\leq G(v)+k_{\varepsilon}t^{1-p}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert v\rvert^{p}\gamma(x,\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon})\,dx+c_{2}k_{\varepsilon}(1-t)^{1-p}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{p}\,dx.\end{split}

(2.23)

By direct computations, in
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
we have

\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon}=-\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon h}\nu+\dfrac{d}{\varepsilon h^{2}}\nabla h=d\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon h^{2}}\nabla h-(1-d)\dfrac{1}{(1-d)\varepsilon h}\nu,

so that, by convexity and
p
-homogeneity

\begin{split}k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert v\rvert^{p}\gamma(x,\nabla\phi_{\varepsilon})\,dx&\leq k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert v\rvert^{p}\left[\dfrac{d}{(\varepsilon h^{2})^{p}}\gamma(x,\nabla h)+\dfrac{(1-d)^{1-p}}{(\varepsilon h)^{p}}\gamma(x,\nu)\right]\,dx\\[15.0pt]
\varepsilon&\leq\dfrac{k_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{p-1}}c_{2}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert v\rvert^{p}h^{1-2p}\lvert\nabla h\rvert^{p}\,dx+\dfrac{k_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{p-1}}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\dfrac{\lvert v\rvert^{p}}{h^{p}}\gamma(x,\nu)\,dx.\end{split}

(2.24)

Joining (
2.23
) and (
2.24
) and passing to the limsup for
\varepsilon
going to zero, we have that for every
t\in(0,1)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\geq G(v)+\alpha t^{1-p}\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\dfrac{\lvert v\rvert^{p}}{h^{p}}\gamma(x,\nu)\,dx,

(2.25)

Finally, using
subsection 2.2
, and passing to the limit for
t
going to
1
, we have

\limsup_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\geq G(v)+\alpha\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\lvert v\rvert^{p}}{h^{p-1}}\gamma(\sigma,\nu)\,d\sigma=G_{\alpha}(v).

We now prove the liminf inequality. Let
v\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
v_{\varepsilon}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
v_{\varepsilon}
converging to
v
in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. If

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})=+\infty,

the inequality is trivial, hence we can assume
\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\leq C
for every
\varepsilon
so that
v_{\varepsilon}\in W^{1,p}_{0}(D_{\varepsilon})
for every
\varepsilon
. Moreover,

k\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx+k_{\varepsilon}c_{1}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx\leq\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\leq C,

whence
v\in W^{1,p}(\Omega)
and, up to a subsequence (not relabelled),
v_{\varepsilon}
converges to
v
weakly in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
.

If
\alpha<+\infty
, by semicontinuity,

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}G(v_{\varepsilon})\leq G(v),

and we are left to prove that

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\gamma(x,\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\,dx\leq\alpha\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\gamma(\sigma,\nu)}{h(\sigma)^{p-1}}\lvert v\rvert^{p}\,d\sigma.

(2.26)

For every
\sigma\in\partial\Omega
let

\nu_{\gamma}(\sigma)=\dfrac{1}{p}\nabla_{z}\gamma(\sigma,\nu(\sigma)).

Then, by Euler’s theorem on homogeneous functions, we have that

\nu_{\gamma}\cdot\nu=\gamma(\sigma,\nu)\geq c_{1}>0,

so that, arguing as in
subsection 2.1
, we can describe
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
as

\Sigma_{\varepsilon}=\Set{\sigma+t\nu_{\gamma}(\sigma)\colon\sigma\in\partial\Omega,\,t\in(0,h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma))},

for some positive function
h_{\varepsilon}
, with

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\dfrac{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}=\gamma(\sigma,\nu),

(2.27)

uniformly on
\partial\Omega
. By the change of variable
x=\sigma+t\nu_{\gamma}
, we have

\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\gamma(x,\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\,dx=\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}\gamma(\sigma+t\nu_{\gamma},\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\gamma(\sigma,\nu)(1+tq(t,\sigma))\,dt\,d\sigma,

where
q
is bounded. Notice that,

\lvert\gamma(\sigma+t\nu_{\gamma},\nabla v_{\varepsilon})-\gamma(\sigma,\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\rvert\leq\omega(h_{\varepsilon})\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p},

so that

k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\gamma(x,\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\,dx=k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}\gamma(\sigma,\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\gamma(\sigma,\nu)\,dt\,d\sigma+r_{\varepsilon},

with
r_{\varepsilon}
going to zero with
\varepsilon
. By
subsection 2.2
we have

\gamma(\sigma,\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\geq\dfrac{\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{z}\gamma(\sigma,\nu)\rvert^{p}}{\lvert\nu\cdot\nabla_{z}\gamma(\sigma,\nu)\rvert^{p}}\gamma(\sigma,\nu)=\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nu_{\gamma}\rvert^{p}\gamma(\sigma,\nu)^{1-p},

hence

k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\gamma(x,\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\,dx\geq k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\partial\Omega}\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\cdot\nu_{\gamma}\rvert^{p}\gamma(\sigma,\nu)^{2-p}\,dt\,d\sigma+r_{\varepsilon}.

(2.28)

On the other hand, for
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-almost every
\sigma\in\partial\Omega

\lvert v_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)\rvert^{p}=\left\lvert\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu_{\gamma})\cdot\nu_{\gamma}\,dt\right\rvert^{p}\leq\lvert h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)\rvert^{p-1}\int_{0}^{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu_{\gamma})\cdot\nu_{\gamma}\rvert^{p}\,dt.

(2.29)

Using (
2.28
) and (
2.29
) we get

\displaystyle k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\gamma(x,\nabla v_{\varepsilon})\,dx\geq\dfrac{k_{\varepsilon}}{\varepsilon^{p-1}}\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\lvert\dfrac{h_{\varepsilon}(\sigma)\gamma(\sigma,\nu)}{\varepsilon h}\right\rvert^{1-p}\dfrac{\gamma(\sigma,\nu)}{h(\sigma)^{p-1}}\lvert v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,d\sigma+r_{\varepsilon}.

(2.30)

Finally, using (
2.27
) and passing to the limit in (
2.30
), by the convergence of
v_{\varepsilon}
to
v
in
L^{p}(\partial\Omega)
, we have (
2.26
).

If
\alpha=+\infty
, we have that for every
\alpha_{0}>0
and
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small,
k_{\varepsilon}>\alpha_{0}\varepsilon^{p-1}
, hence

\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\geq G(v_{\varepsilon})+\alpha_{0}\varepsilon^{p-1}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\gamma(x,v_{\varepsilon})\,dx.

Using the liminf inequality proved for the case
\alpha<+\infty
and passing to the limit for
\varepsilon
going to zero, we have

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\geq G(v)+\alpha_{0}\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{\gamma(\sigma,\nu)}{h(\sigma)^{p-1}}\lvert v\rvert^{p}\,d\sigma.

Then, passing to the limit for
\alpha_{0}
going to infinity, we have

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\geq\begin{cases}G(v)&\text{if }v\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega),\\[5.0pt]
+\infty&\text{if }v\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\setminus W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega),\end{cases}

that is,

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\geq G_{\infty}(v).

∎

In
[
10
]
, the authors generalised the result to the case of
non homogeneous
reinforcement, that is, they considered the case of the
p
-Laplacian equation with different exponent in
\Omega
and
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
. Moreover, they allow
k_{\varepsilon}
to be a function with possibly different asymptotic behaviours with respect to
\varepsilon^{p-1}
on different subsets of
\partial\Omega
. Another direct generalisation of the result is the one contained in
[
55
]
, where the authors study the case of a right-hand side in
L^{1}
.

Convergence of minima

It is well known that the
\Gamma
-convergence of a sequence of functionals is strictly related to the convergence of its minimum points and values, for instance, we have the following proposition (we refer the reader to
[
28
, Theorem 7.8]
).

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
X
be a metric space and let
F,F_{\varepsilon}\colon X\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
functions such that

i)

F_{\varepsilon}
is equi-coercive, that is, for every
c>0
the exists a compact subset
K_{c}\subset X
such that

\Set{x\in X}{\,F_{\varepsilon}(x)\leq c}\subseteq K_{c},

for every
\varepsilon>0
;

ii)

F_{\varepsilon}

\Gamma
-converges to
F
.

Then,
F
admits minimum in
X
and

\min_{X}F=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\inf_{X}F_{\varepsilon}\right).

Moreover, if
x_{\varepsilon}
if a family in
X
such that

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}F_{\varepsilon}(x_{\varepsilon})=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left(\inf_{X}F_{\varepsilon}\right)

and
x_{\varepsilon}
converges to
x_{0}
, then

F(x_{0})=\min_{X}F.

Let
f\in L^{p^{\prime}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we want to apply the previous proposition to prove that, when
\alpha
is strictly positive, the minimisers of the functional

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(v)=F_{\varepsilon}(v)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}fv\,dx

converge to the ones of

\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}(v)=G_{\alpha}(v)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}fv\,dx.

By
subsection 2.2
,
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}

\Gamma
-converges to
\mathcal{G}_{\alpha}
so that we just need to show the equi-coercivity of
\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}
.

Proposition 2.16
.

Let
\alpha>0
. Let
v_{\varepsilon}
be a family in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\leq c

for some
c>0
, then
v_{\varepsilon}
is relatively compact in
L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

By our assumptions on
F_{\varepsilon}
, we have

k\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx+k_{\varepsilon}c_{1}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx+\int_{D_{\varepsilon}}fv_{\varepsilon}\,dx\leq\mathcal{F}_{\varepsilon}(v_{\varepsilon})\leq c.

Then, using Hölder’s Young’s and inequalities, we have that for every
\delta>0

\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx+k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx\leq c_{\delta,f}+\delta\int_{D_{\varepsilon}}\lvert v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx.

(2.31)

For every
t\in[0,\varepsilon h]
and
\sigma\in\partial\Omega
we have

\lvert v_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+t\nu)\rvert^{p}=\left\lvert\int_{t}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}\partial_{\nu}v_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+s\nu)\,ds\right\rvert^{p}\leq\left(\varepsilon h(\sigma)\right)^{p-1}\int_{0}^{\varepsilon h(\sigma)}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}(\sigma+s\nu)\rvert^{p}\,ds,

so that, integrating, we get

\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx\leq C\varepsilon^{p}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx\leq C\varepsilon k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx

(2.32)

and

\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,d\sigma\leq C\varepsilon^{p-1}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx\leq Ck_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx,

(2.33)

where we used the fact that, by the assumption on
\alpha>0
, we have that
\varepsilon^{p-1}/k_{\varepsilon}
is bounded. Using a Poincaré-type inequality with trace term (also known as Friedrichs’ inequality) and (
2.33
), we have

\int_{\Omega}\lvert v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx\leq C\left[\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,d\sigma\right]\leq C\left[\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx+k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx\right].

(2.34)

Then, using (
2.32
) and (
2.34
) in (
2.31
), for a suitable choice of
\delta>0
we finally have

\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx+k_{\varepsilon}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert\nabla v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx\leq C.

Hence
v_{\varepsilon}
in uniformly bounded in
W^{1,p}(\Omega)
and, by (
2.32
),

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}\lvert v_{\varepsilon}\rvert^{p}\,dx=0,

which proves the assertion.
∎

2.3
Subsequent developments and other boundary conditions

The basic result of the previous section can be summarised as follows. Given a smooth
C^{1,1}
bounded open set
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and thin layer
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
described by normal graph on
\partial\Omega
of the function
\varepsilon h
, where
h\colon\partial\Omega\to(0,+\infty)
is a Lipschitz function, for any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the solution
u_{\varepsilon}
to the diffraction problem

\begin{cases}-\divv(A_{1}\nabla u_{\varepsilon})=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
-\varepsilon\divv(A_{2}\nabla u_{\varepsilon})=f&\text{in }\Sigma_{\varepsilon},\\[5.0pt]
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega}=u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega}=\varepsilon\partial_{\nu_{A_{2}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{on }\partial D_{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

(2.35)

with Dirichlet boundary condition on the boundary of
D_{\varepsilon}=\bar{\Omega}\cup\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
, converges to the solution to the Robin boundary value problem

\begin{cases}-\divv(A_{1}\nabla u)=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}u+\dfrac{\gamma_{A_{2}}}{h}u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(2.36)

where
\gamma_{A_{2}}=\nu_{A_{2}}\cdot\nu
and
\nu_{A_{1}},\nu_{A_{2}}
are the conormal directions
A_{1}\nu
,
A_{2}\nu
on
\partial\Omega
from "inside" and "outside"
\Omega
respectively.

In many applications, however, it could be useful to work with less regular sets, for instance, let
\Omega
be a bounded open set with Lipschitz boundary and let
D_{\varepsilon}
be a generic family of bounded open sets containing
\Omega
such that

d_{\varepsilon}=\sup\Set{d(x,\Omega)\colon x\in D_{\varepsilon}},

goes to zero as
\varepsilon
goes to zero. Under these mild assumptions, in
[
15
]
, the authors derive the necessary and sufficient condition to have that the solution to the diffraction problem (
2.35
), for any
f
, converges, in
L^{2}
, to the solution to a boundary value problem of the type (
2.36
). The boundary parameter
\gamma_{A_{2}}/h
, however, must be replaced with a (capacitary) measure
\mu_{A}
, which is supported by
\partial\Omega
and depends uniquely on the family
D_{\varepsilon}
(and on the elliptic operator). The limit problem can be formally written as

\begin{cases}-\divv(A_{1}\nabla u)=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\partial_{\nu_{A_{1}}}u+\mu_{A}u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

and has to be interpreted, variationally, as the first order optimality condition for the minimisation in
H^{1}(\Omega)
of the energy

\int_{\Omega}A_{1}\nabla v\cdot\nabla v\,dx+\int v^{2}\,d\mu_{A}-2\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx.

As the necessary and sufficient condition is quite involved, we refer the reader to
[
15
, Theorem 6.1]
for the precise statement. We remark, however, that the simple condition

d_{\varepsilon}<c\varepsilon,

for some
c>0
, is a sufficient condition for the limit to hold. We also refer the reader, for instance, to
[
21
,
20
]
and the references therein, for the reinforcement of fractal sets, to
[
6
,
5
]
for the
partial
reinforcement of a piece-wise flat subset of the boundary, and to
[
36
]
for the case of a vectorial problem.

Other generalisations include, for instance, the case of the reinforcement with a thin layer with
oscillating thickness
considered by
[
16
]
(see also
[
57
,
53
]
for more recent results on the topic); the reinforcement for the
torsion problem in multiconnected domains
studied in
[
9
]
and
[
57
]
, were the function
u_{\varepsilon}
is required to have a constant, unprescribed, trace on the inner boundaries and a prescribed flux condition. The case of different boundary conditions on
\partial D_{\varepsilon}
as also been studied.

In particular, in
[
54
]
, the authors consider the case of
Neumann boundary conditions
. Namely, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth bounded set,
\Sigma_{\varepsilon}
an uniform layer of thickness
\varepsilon>0
,
D_{\varepsilon}=\bar{\Omega}\cup\Sigma_{\varepsilon},
and consider the boundary value problem

\begin{cases}-k\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=f&\text{in }\Omega_{\varepsilon}\\[5.0pt]
-k_{\varepsilon}\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=f&\text{in }\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\\[5.0pt]
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}=u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
k\partial_{\nu}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}=k_{\varepsilon}\partial_{\nu}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\partial_{\nu_{\varepsilon}}u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{ on }\partial D_{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

(2.37)

where
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
satisfies the compatibility condition

\int_{\Omega}f\,dx=\int_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}f\,dx=0,

for every
\varepsilon>0
. The authors prove that, if
k_{\varepsilon}
goes to zero as
\varepsilon
goes to zero, and
u_{\varepsilon}
is a solution to (
2.37
), denoting by
(u_{\varepsilon})_{\Omega}
its mean in
\Omega
, the function
u_{\varepsilon}-(u_{\varepsilon})_{\Omega}
converges, uniformly in the compact subset of
\Omega
, to the solution to

\begin{cases}-k\Delta v=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\partial_{\nu}v=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v\,dx=0.\end{cases}

It is also possible to consider the case in which
k_{\varepsilon}
goes to infinity as
\varepsilon
goes to zero. The limit problem will then depend on the limit
\eta
of
\varepsilon k_{\varepsilon}
. In particular, letting
v_{\varepsilon}
be the solution to (
2.37
) with zero mean in
D_{\varepsilon}
, if
\eta
is finite, then
v_{\varepsilon}
converges, strongly in
H^{1}(\Omega)
to the solution of the following Wentzel problem

\begin{cases}-k\Delta v=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
k\partial_{\nu}v+\eta\Delta_{\partial\Omega}v=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v\,dx=0,\end{cases}

where
\Delta_{\partial\Omega}
is the tangential Laplacian on
\partial\Omega
. If
\eta=+\infty
,
v_{\varepsilon}
converges to
w-(w)_{\Omega}
, where
w
is the solution to

\begin{cases}-k\Delta w=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
w=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

We also remark that in the same paper, the authors also consider mixed Dirichlet-Neumann boundary conditions for particular geometries, and use
\Gamma
-convergence to study semi-linear equations with Dirichlet boundary conditions.

The case of
Robin boundary conditions
, on the other hand, has been more recently considered by
[
52
]
(see also
[
31
,
1
,
2
]
). Namely, consider the boundary value problem

\begin{cases}-k\Delta u_{\varepsilon}=f&\text{in }\Omega_{\varepsilon}\\[5.0pt]
-k_{\varepsilon}\divv(A_{2}\nabla u_{\varepsilon})=f&\text{in }\Sigma_{\varepsilon}\\[5.0pt]
u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}=u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
k\partial_{\nu}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Omega_{\varepsilon}}=k_{\varepsilon}\partial_{\nu_{A_{2}}}u_{\varepsilon}|_{\Sigma_{\varepsilon}}&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
k_{\varepsilon}\partial_{\nu_{\varepsilon,A_{2}}}u_{\varepsilon}+\beta u_{\varepsilon}=0&\text{ on }\partial D_{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

(2.38)

where
\nu_{\varepsilon,A_{2}}=A_{2}\nu_{\varepsilon}
is the conormal to
\partial D_{\varepsilon}
, and
\beta
is a positive constant. The authors prove that, if
k_{\varepsilon}
goes to zero as
\varepsilon
goes to zero, and we denote by
\alpha
the limit of
k_{\varepsilon}/\varepsilon
, the function
u_{\varepsilon}
, solution to (
2.38
), converges (strongly in
L^{2}(\Omega)
) to the solution of

\begin{cases}-k\Delta u=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
k\partial_{\nu}u+\dfrac{\beta\alpha\gamma_{A_{2}}}{\beta+\alpha\gamma_{A_{2}}}u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\\[5.0pt]
\end{cases}

Actually, the problem was originally studied for the parabolic case, but the same result holds for the elliptic case.
We notice that this type of effective boundary condition was already mentioned in the book by Carslaw and Jaeger (
[
22
]
) in the discussion about the case of a
"
thin surface skin of poor conductor
". In
[

" introduced in
[

(see also

is small in
all
directions, it is enough to assume that
A_{2,\varepsilon}
is bounded and small in the normal direction to
\partial\Omega
. That is,
\nu
is an eigenvector of eigenvalue
A_{2,\varepsilon}(x)\nu\cdot\nu=k_{\varepsilon}\gamma_{A_{2}}(\sigma(x))
, where
\sigma(x)
denotes the orthogonal projection on
\partial\Omega
. We also refer the reader to
[
23
]
(see also
[
49
]
) for the
2
-dimensional case in which the eigenvalue of

in the tangential direction is allowed to diverge.

The corresponding eigenvalue problems have also been extensively studied, see for instance
[

,

3
The optimisation point of view

In
[
18
]
, Buttazzo proposed the study of optimisation problems related to the limit equation of the reinforcement problem with Dirichlet boundary conditions

\begin{cases}-\Delta u_{h}=f&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\partial_{\nu}u_{h}+\dfrac{1}{h}u_{h}=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(3.1)

As discussed in the previous section, from a physical perspective, the solution
u_{h}
to equation (
3.1
) is an approximation of the steady-state temperature in a conductor
\Omega
, surrounded by a thin layer of insulating material. The function
f
represents the
heat source
in
\Omega
, while the function
h
represents the
shape
of the insulating layer. The optimisation problem proposed in
[
18
]
can then be stated as

Given
\Omega
and
f
, what is the "best" distribution of insulator around
\partial\Omega
?

To give a mathematical formalisation of the problem, an optimality criterion must be defined. A natural choice is the following. For every
h\colon\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
consider

\mathcal{E}(v,h)=\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{v^{2}}{h}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}-2\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx,

the variational energy associated to (
3.1
), then

\mathcal{E}(h)=\min_{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)}\mathcal{E}(v,h)=\mathcal{E}(u_{h},h)=-\int_{\Omega}fu_{h}\,dx.

We will say that a configuration
h_{1}
is
better
than a configuration
h_{2}
if

\mathcal{E}(h_{1})\leq\mathcal{E}(h_{2}).

Notice that, when the heat source is
uniformly distributed
(i.e.
f
is constant), the energy
\mathcal{E}(h)
is proportional to the opposite of the average temperature in
\Omega
, hence a better configuration of insulating material corresponds to a higher average temperature.

The optimisation problem consists in minimising the energy
\mathcal{E}
among non-negative functions
h\colon\partial\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
which satisfy the integral constraint

\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=m.

(3.2)

The integral constraint is to be interpreted as an approximated measure constraint on the insulating layer. Indeed, if we define

\Sigma_{\varepsilon h}=\Set{\sigma+t\nu\colon\sigma\in\partial\Omega,\,t\in(0,\varepsilon h)},

then we have that

\lvert\Sigma_{\varepsilon h}\rvert=\varepsilon\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

We refer the reader to
[
4
]
for numerical results on the problem.

In
[
18
]
, the author also proposed the investigation of other optimality criteria such as

\int_{\Omega}\lvert u_{h}-u_{\text{obj}}\rvert^{2}\,dx,

where
u_{\text{obj}}
is a desired temperature, or more generally

\int_{\Omega}g(x,u_{h})\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}j(\sigma,h,u_{h})\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\sigma),

for suitable choices of functions
g
and
j
. However, to our knowledge, this type of optimisation problem is still open for the limit equation in the Dirichlet case and is partially solved in the Robin one (see
[
26
]
) under the weaker constraint

\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\leq m.

The corresponding optimisation problem for the eigenvalue, that is to minimise the principal eigenvalue,
\lambda(h)
, of the problem

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda u&\text{in }\Omega,\\[5.0pt]
\partial_{\nu}u+\dfrac{1}{h}u=0&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

(3.3)

under the integral constraint (
3.2
), was originally proposed by Friedman in
[
38
]
. We notice that from a thermal insulation point of view, the eigenvalue
\lambda(h)
represents the rate of decay of the temperature. We refer the reader to
[
8
,
7
]
for numerical results on the problem.

3.1
Optimisation of variational energy

Fix
m>0
,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a bounded Lipschitz open set, and let

\mathcal{H}_{m}(\partial\Omega)=\Set{h\in L^{1}(\partial\Omega)\colon\,h\geq 0,\,\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\sigma=m}.

We are interested in the minimisation problem

\min\Set{\mathcal{E}(v,h)\colon v\in H^{1}(\Omega),\,h\in\mathcal{H}_{m}(\partial\Omega)}

(3.4)

studied in
[
18
]
(see also
[
13
,
14
]
)

Proposition 3.1
.

For every
v\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
there exists
h_{v}\in\mathcal{H}_{m}(\partial\Omega)
solution to

\min_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{m}(\partial\Omega)}\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{v^{2}}{h}\,d\sigma.

(3.5)

Moreover, if
v
is not identically zero, the solution is unique and

h_{v}=m\dfrac{\lvert v\rvert}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v\rvert\,d\sigma}.

Proof.

If
v
is identically zero, every function
h
is a solution to (
3.5
). Assume
v\neq 0
, by Hölder’s inequality we have that for every
h\in\mathcal{H}_{m}(\partial\Omega)

\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v\rvert\right)^{2}\leq\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{v^{2}}{h}\,d\sigma\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\sigma=m\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{v^{2}}{h}\,d\sigma,

that is

\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{v^{2}}{h}\,d\sigma\geq\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v\rvert\right)^{2}

for every
h\in\mathcal{H}_{m}(\partial\Omega)
. On the other hand, setting

h_{v}=m\dfrac{\lvert v\rvert}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v\rvert\,d\sigma},

by direct computation, we have

\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{v^{2}}{h_{v}}\,d\sigma=\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v\rvert\right)^{2}.

Finally, the uniqueness comes from the equality case in Hölder’s inequality.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

We remark that the
subsection 3.1
still holds if we consider
h
in the larger space

\mathcal{H}_{m}^{-}(\partial\Omega)=\Set{h\in L^{1}(\partial\Omega)\colon\,h\geq 0,\,\int_{\partial\Omega}h\,d\sigma\leq m}

Using
subsection 3.1
we have that problem (
3.4
) is equivalent to the auxiliary problem

\min\Set{\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dx+\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v\rvert\right)^{2}-2\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx\colon\,v\in H^{1}(\Omega)}.

(3.6)

The following Poincaré-type inequality holds

Proposition 3.3
.

There exists
C>0
such that, for every
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)

\int_{\Omega}v^{2}\,dx\leq C\left[\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dx+\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v\rvert\right)^{2}\right].

(3.7)

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that (
3.7
) fails. Then, there exists a sequence
v_{k}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
with

\int_{\Omega}v_{k}^{2}\,dx=1

(3.8)

and

\lim_{k\to\infty}\left[\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{k}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v_{k}\rvert\right)^{2}\right]=0.

Hence, up to a subsequence,
v_{k}
converges to zero in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, which is in contradiction with (
3.8
).
∎

Theorem 3.4
.

Fix
m>0
and
f\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Then, problem (
3.4
) admits at least a solution
(u_{m},h_{m})\in H^{1}(\Omega)\times\mathcal{H}_{m}(\partial\Omega)
, where
u_{m}
is a minimizer to (
3.6
), and, if
u_{m}|_{\partial\Omega}\not\equiv 0
,

h_{m}=m\dfrac{\lvert u_{m}\rvert}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert u_{m}\rvert\,d\sigma}.

Moreover if
\Omega
is connected
u_{m}
is unique.

Proof.

As observed, problem (
3.4
) is equivalent to (
3.6
). Let

F(v)=\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dx+\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v\rvert\right)^{2}-2\int_{\Omega}fv\,dx,

and let
v_{k}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
be a minimising sequence for problem (
3.6
). Without loss of generality, we can assume

F(v_{k})\leq F(0)=0,

so that, using Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities on the source term

2\int_{\Omega}fv_{k}\,dx,

we have that, for every
\delta>0
, there exists
C_{\delta}>0
such that

\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{k}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v_{k}\rvert\right)^{2}\leq C_{\delta}+\delta\int_{\Omega}v_{k}^{2}\,dx.

By the Poincarè-type inequality (
3.7
), we have that

\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{k}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v_{k}\rvert\right)^{2}\leq C_{\delta}+\delta C\left[\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{k}\rvert^{2}\,dx+\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v_{k}\rvert\right)^{2}\right],

so that
v_{k}
is bounded in
H^{1}(\Omega)
. Up to a subsequence
v_{k}
converges, weakly in
H^{1}(\Omega)
, to a function
u_{m}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
. By the
H^{1}(\Omega)
-lower semicontinuity of
F

we have that
u_{m}
is a solution to (
3.6
).

Assume
\Omega
is connected. To prove the uniqueness of the solution, let
u_{1},u_{2}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
be solutions to (
3.6
). By direct computations, we have

\begin{split}F\left(\dfrac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right)-\dfrac{F(u_{1})+F(u_{2})}{2}=&-\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\left\lvert\dfrac{\nabla u_{1}-\nabla u_{2}}{2}\right\rvert^{2}\,dx+\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\lvert\dfrac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right\rvert\,d\sigma\right)^{2}+\\[15.0pt]
&-\dfrac{1}{2m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert u_{1}\rvert\,d\sigma\right)^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert u_{2}\rvert\,d\sigma\right)^{2}.\end{split}

(3.9)

By the convexity of the boundary term

v\mapsto\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v\rvert\,d\sigma\right)^{2},

the right-hand side of (
3.9
) is strictly negative unless
u_{1}-u_{2}
is constant. On the other hand, by the minimality of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, the left-hand side of (
3.9
) is non-negative. Hence,
u_{1}=u_{2}+c
. Similarly, if
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
have different sign on a subset
\Gamma
of
\partial\Omega
with
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\Gamma)>0
, we have

\lvert u_{1}+u_{1}\rvert<\lvert u_{1}\rvert+\lvert u_{2}\rvert

on
\Gamma
and the right-hand side of (
3.9
) is strictly negative. Hence
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
have the same sign and

\begin{split}-\dfrac{c^{2}}{4m}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\Omega)=&-\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\lvert\dfrac{u_{1}-u_{2}}{2}\right\rvert\,d\sigma\right)^{2}=\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\left\lvert\dfrac{u_{1}+u_{2}}{2}\right\rvert\,d\sigma\right)^{2}+\\[15.0pt]
&-\dfrac{1}{2m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert u_{1}\rvert\,d\sigma\right)^{2}-\dfrac{1}{2m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert u_{2}\rvert\,d\sigma\right)^{2},\end{split}

which, by (
3.9
) and the minimality of
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
, is non-negative,
so that
c=0
and
u_{1}=u_{2}
.
∎

3.2
Spectral optimisation

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz set, and denote by
\lambda(h)
the principal eigenvalue of problem (
3.3
) on
\Omega
. We have the following variational characterisation

\lambda(h)=\min_{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\set{0}}\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dx+\int_{\partial\Omega}\dfrac{v^{2}}{h}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v^{2}\,dx}.

We are interested in the minimisation problem

\lambda_{m}(\Omega):=\min\Set{\lambda(h)\colon\,h\in\mathcal{H}_{m}},

(3.11)

studied in
[
13
]
, we also refer the reader to
[
32
]
for the minimisation of the first eigenvalue of the reinforcement problem with Robin boundary conditions, and to
[
27
]
for the case of the
p
-Laplacian.

Let

J_{m}(v)=\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dx+\dfrac{1}{m}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\lvert v\rvert\,dx\right)^{2}}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v^{2}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}.

Arguing as for problem (
3.4
), we have that problem (
3.11
) is equivalent to the auxiliary problem

\min_{v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\set{0}}J_{m}(v),

(3.12)

which, by the Poincaré-type inequality (
3.7
) and the direct methods of the calculus of variations, admits a non-negative solution
\bar{u}_{m}
. We have the following theorem

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
\bar{u}_{m}
be a solution to problem (
3.12
) and let

\bar{h}_{m}=m\dfrac{\bar{u}_{m}}{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\Omega}\bar{u}_{m}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}}.

Then
\bar{h}_{m}
is a solution to problem (
3.11
), and the couple
(\bar{u}_{m},\bar{h}_{m})
is a solution to (
3.3
)

We have the following properties for the minimum eigenvalue as a function of
m>0

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded Lipschitz set. Then the function
m\in(0,+\infty)\mapsto\lambda_{m}(\Omega)
is continuos and strictly decreasing, moreover

\lim_{m\to+\infty}\lambda_{m}(\Omega)=0,

\lim_{m\to 0^{+}}\lambda_{m}(\Omega)=\lambda^{D}(\Omega),

the first Dirichlet eigenvalue of
\Omega
.

Symmetry-breaking

When
\Omega
is a ball, an interesting phenomenon has been observed in
[
13
]
(see also
[
32
]
), namely minimisers of
J_{m}
are not always radial functions, hence optimisers
\bar{h}_{m}
to problem (
3.11
) can be non-constant (see
Figure 4
). This surprising phenomenon is called
symmetry-breaking
and is contained in the following result.

Figure 4:
Example of non-radial solution to (
3.12
) and corresponding configuration of insulating material.

Theorem 3.8
.

Let
\Omega=B_{R}
be a ball of radius
R>0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
n\geq 2
. There exists
m_{0}>0
such that the solutions to the auxiliary minimisation problem (
3.12
) are

i)

radial, if
m>m_{0}
,

ii)

non-radial, if
0<m<m_{0}
.

In particular
\bar{h}_{m}
is non-constant if
m<m_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\mu(B_{R})
be the principal eigenvalue of the Neumann problem on
B_{R}
an let
\bar{v}
be an associated
L^{2}(B_{R})
-normalised eigenfunction, then
0<\mu(B_{R})<\lambda^{D}(B_{R})
hence, by proposition
subsection 3.2
, there exists a unique
m>0
, such that

\lambda_{m}(B_{R})=\mu(B_{R}).

Let
m_{0}
be such a value. For every
m>0
, let
\bar{u}_{m}
be a non-negative solution to (
3.12
) with normalised
L^{2}
-norm. Notice that, by the minimum principle,
\bar{u}_{m}
can only vanish on the boundary of
B_{R}
and that the trace of
\bar{u}_{m}
cannot be identically equal to zero as
\lambda_{m}(B_{R})<\lambda^{D}(B_{R})
. In particular, if
u_{m}
is radial, then it is strictly positive. Conversely, if
m\neq m_{0}
, the only positive solutions are the radially symmetric ones. Indeed, a solution to (
3.12
) is strictly positive if and only if it is a strictly positive solution to

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda_{m}(B_{R})u&\text{in }B_{R},\\[7.0pt]
\partial_{\nu}u=-\dfrac{1}{m}\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}u\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&\text{on }\partial B_{R}.\end{cases}

(3.13)

In particular, given a positive solution to (
3.13
), we can construct a positive and radial solution by considering the average along the angular coordinates. On the other hand, any two positive solutions to (
3.13
) can be combined linearly to obtain a solution to the homogeneous Neumann eigenvalue problem, so that positive solutions to (
3.13
) are unique up to a multiplicative constant.

We begin by proving that a strictly positive solution can exist only if
m\geq m_{0}
, so that, in particular, if
m<m_{0}
, no radial solution exists. Let
m\neq m_{0}
so that
\lambda_{m}(B_{R})\neq\mu(B_{R})
, and assume that
\bar{u}_{m}
is strictly positive, than it satisfies

\int_{B_{R}}\nabla\bar{u}_{m}\nabla\varphi\,dx+\dfrac{1}{m}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\bar{u}_{m}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\varphi\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=\lambda_{m}(B_{R})\int_{B_{R}}\bar{u}_{m}\varphi\,dx,

for every
\varphi\in H^{1}(B_{R})
. We have that
\bar{u}_{m}
and the Neumann eigenfunction
\bar{v}
are orthogonal, indeed

(\mu(B_{R})-\lambda_{m}(B_{R}))\int_{B_{R}}\bar{u}_{m}\bar{v}\,dx=-\dfrac{1}{m}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\bar{u}_{m}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\int_{\partial B_{R}}\bar{v}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0,

where we used that, since
B_{R}
is a ball

\int_{\partial B_{R}}\bar{v}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0.

As
\bar{u}_{m}
is strictly positive the function
\varphi_{\delta}=\bar{u}_{m}+\delta\bar{v}
is strictly positive for
\delta
sufficiently small, so that using
\varphi_{\delta}
as a test function for
\lambda_{m}(B_{R})
and using the orthogonality of
\bar{u}_{m}
and
\bar{v}
, we have

\lambda_{m}(B_{R})\leq J_{m}(\varphi_{\delta})=\dfrac{\lambda_{m}(B_{R})+\delta^{2}\mu(B_{R})}{1+\delta^{2}},

that is

\lambda_{m}(B_{R})\leq\mu(B_{R})

and, by monotonicity,
m>m_{0}
.

We now need to prove that non-radial solutions only exist if
m\leq m_{0}
. Let
m\neq m_{0}
and assume that a non-radial solution exists. Let
\bar{u}_{m}(r,\theta)
be such a solution in polar coordinates. By spherical symmetrisation (see
[
66
,
33
]
) we can assume that
\bar{u}_{m}
is
spherically symmetric
in a direction
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, that is

\bar{u}_{m}(r,\theta_{1})\geq\bar{u}_{m}(r,\theta_{2})\quad\text{for every }0<r<R\,\text{ and }\lvert\theta_{1}-\theta_{0}\rvert\leq\lvert\theta_{2}-\theta_{0}\rvert.

Indeed, by the properties of the rearrangement, the integral of the gradient decreases while the
L^{2}
-norm and the boundary integral remain the same. Moreover
\bar{u}_{m}
satisfies

\begin{cases}-\Delta\bar{u}_{m}=\lambda_{m}(B_{R})\bar{u}_{m}&\text{in }B_{R},\\[7.0pt]
\partial_{\nu}\bar{u}_{m}=-\dfrac{1}{m}\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\bar{u}_{m}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&\text{on }\partial B_{R}\cap\set{\bar{u}_{m}>0},\\[10.0pt]
\partial_{\nu}\bar{u}_{m}\geq-\dfrac{1}{m}\displaystyle\int_{\partial B_{R}}\bar{u}_{m}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}&\text{on }\partial B_{R}\cap\set{\bar{u}_{m}=0}.\end{cases}

(3.14)

Multiplying equation (
3.14
) by the Neumann eigenfunction
\bar{v}
and integrating by parts we have

(\mu(B_{R})-\lambda_{m}(B_{R}))\int_{B_{R}}\bar{u}_{m}\bar{v}\,dx=\int_{\partial B_{R}}\partial_{\nu}\bar{u}_{m}\bar{v}\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}.

(3.15)

Without loss of generality we can also assume that
\bar{v}
is spherically symmetric in the direction
\theta_{0}
, as a consequence
\bar{v}
is antisymmetric in the direction orthogonal to
\theta_{0}
and, as
\bar{u}_{m}
is spherically symmetric in direction
\theta_{0}
and non-constant on
\partial B_{R}

\int_{B_{R}}\bar{u}_{m}\bar{v}\,dx>0.

Finally, by the symmetry of
\bar{u}_{m}
and the boundary conditions in (
3.14
), we can deduce that also
-\partial_{\nu}\bar{u}_{m}
is spherically symmetric in direction
\theta_{0}
, hence the right hand side of (
3.15
) is non positive and
\lambda_{m}(B_{R})\geq\mu(B_{R})
, that is
m<m_{0}
.
∎

Let

, where instead of the Neumann eigenvalue, they considered

k_{1}(\Omega)=\min\Set{\dfrac{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v\rvert^{2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}v^{2}\,dx}\colon\,v\in H^{1}(\Omega)\setminus\set{0},\,\int_{\partial\Omega}v\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}=0}.

Finally, the authors conjectured that, denoting by
m_{0}(\Omega)
the largest value of
m_{0}
such that
Theorem 3.9
holds, then

m_{0}(\Omega)\geq m_{0}(\Omega^{*}),

where
\Omega^{*}
is the ball of the same volume. That is, among sets of given volume, the ball should be the set for which the symmetry-breaking phenomenon is less likely to happen. To our knowledge, such a problem remains open.
