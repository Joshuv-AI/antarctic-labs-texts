1.
Introduction

Consider a two-dimensional energy balance model coupled with the basic equations of geophysical flows, namely, the primitive equations of ocean dynamics. This system integrates an advection-diffusion model for the ocean’s interior temperature (subject to a dynamic boundary condition) with the three-dimensional primitive equations. We study two settings: the deterministic case and the stochastic case, in which the stochastic force is modelled by a cylindrical Wiener process
(W_{t})_{t}
on the boundary. Both settings are of great interest due to the presence of dynamic boundary conditions and the fact that we deal with unique, global, strong solutions.

More specifically, we investigate global, strong well-posedness results for this system, described in the deterministic setting by the set of equations

(1.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \varepsilon\partial_{t}v^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla v^{\varepsilon}-\Delta v^{\varepsilon}+\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}p^{\varepsilon}&=0,&\quad\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
\partial_{z}p^{\varepsilon}&=-T,&\quad\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau)\\
\mathrm{div}\,u^{\varepsilon}&=0,&\quad\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
\partial_{t}T+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla T-\Delta T&=0,\quad&\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
T|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=\rho,\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
\partial_{t}\rho+v^{\varepsilon}|_{\Gamma_{u}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho-\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}\rho+(\partial_{z}T)|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=R(x,\rho),\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
v^{\varepsilon}(0)&=v_{0},\quad T(0)=T_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Here,
\mathcal{O}=G\times(0,1)
denotes the spatial domain, where
G=(0,1)^{2}
represents the horizontal domain, and where
\Gamma_{u}=G\times\{1\}
as well as

\Gamma_{b}=G\times\{0\}
denote the
upper (surface) or lower (bottom) boundary of
\mathcal{O}
, respectively. Furthermore,
u^{\varepsilon}=(v^{\varepsilon},w^{\varepsilon}):\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3}
denotes the velocity of the ocean and
p^{\varepsilon}:\mathcal{O}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denotes ocean’s pressure on a climate, slow time-scale (several years), where
\varepsilon\sim\nicefrac{{\tau_{\text{fast}}}}{{\tau_{\text{slow}}}}
characterises the separation between the fast (weather) and slow (climate) time-scales; see
Section 3
for more details. Moreover,

T:\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the temperature of the ocean and
\rho=T|_{\Gamma_{u}}:G\to\mathbb{R}
the temperature evaluated at the surface. The operators
\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}
and
\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}
denote the horizontal Laplacian and
the horizontal gradient, that is
\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}:=\partial^{2}_{x}+\partial^{2}_{y}
and
\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}:=(\partial_{x},\partial_{y})^{T}
. Moreover,
R(x,\rho)
is a reaction term modelling the net radiative balance at the
surface described in detail below.

In the stochastic setting, the force is given through a cylindrical Wiener process
(W_{t})_{t}
, acting via a given process
H_{\rho}
on the temperature on the boundary. In this case, the system reads

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \varepsilon\,\mathrm{d}v^{\varepsilon}+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla v^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}t-\Delta v^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}t+\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}p^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{d}t&=0,&\quad\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
\partial_{z}p^{\varepsilon}&=-T,&\quad\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau)\\
\mathrm{div}\,u^{\varepsilon}&=0,&\quad\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
\,\mathrm{d}T+u^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla T\,\mathrm{d}t-\Delta T\,\mathrm{d}t&=0,\quad&\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
T|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=\rho,\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
\,\mathrm{d}\rho+\overline{v}^{\varepsilon}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho\,\mathrm{d}t-\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}\rho\,\mathrm{d}t+(\partial_{z}T)|_{\Gamma_{u}}\,\mathrm{d}t&=R(x,\rho)\,\mathrm{d}t+H_{\rho}\,\mathrm{d}W,\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
v^{\varepsilon}(0)&=v_{0},\quad T(0)=T_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

Note that here the transport term on the boundary is replaced by the transport along the average of the velocity given by
\overline{v}^{\varepsilon}=\int_{0}^{1}v^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi
.

Both systems (
1.1
) as well as (
1.2
) are supplemented by the boundary conditions

(1.3)

\displaystyle(\partial_{z}v^{\varepsilon})|_{\Gamma_{u}\cup\Gamma_{b}}=w^{\varepsilon}|_{\Gamma_{u}\cup\Gamma_{b}}=0,\hskip 10.00002pt(\partial_{z}T)|_{\Gamma_{b}}=0\ \text{ and }\ u^{\varepsilon},p^{\varepsilon},T,\rho\hskip 10.00002pt\text{ are periodic on }\ \partial G\times[0,1].

Note that the domain
G=(0,1)^{2}
, when endowed with periodic boundary conditions, can be naturally identified with the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
.

Energy balance model serves often as a simplified climate model. For more information on deterministic energy balance models and their applications, we refer
e.g., to
[
27
,
5
,
10
,
17
]
.
The first pioneering work on stochastic climate models goes back to K. Hasselmann in
[
31
]
, where noise arises as a result of the interaction of the many
components constituting the climate system, in particular as the effect of the faster component on the slower ones; see
[
34
]
for a formulation of Hasselmann’s programme
in modern mathematical language. For further information on stochastic energy balance models, we refer to
[
26
,
22
,
18
]
. To account for contributions of both slow and fast processes in the noise term, we may consider
W=(W^{1},W^{2}),

where
W^{1}
and
W^{2}
are independent cylindrical Wiener processes, and set
H_{\rho}=(H_{\rho}^{1},\sqrt{\varepsilon}H_{\rho}^{2}).
Here,
H_{\rho}^{1}
captures unresolved variability arising from slow processes, while
H_{\rho}^{2}
, scaled by
\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, represents the cumulative effect of fast (weather-scale) processes on the slow climate dynamics, according with standard multiscale stochastic modeling approaches; see
[
17
]
.
For simplicity of notation and exposition, in the remainder of this work we avoid explicitly considering the two-component formulation of
H_{\rho}
and treat a single effective noise term.

A central aspect in our system is the analysis of the
dynamic boundary condition
for the surface temperature, given by
T|_{\Gamma_{u}}=\rho
. Dynamic boundary conditions have, of course, been previously studied, both in the weak and strong sense. We refer here e. g., to the work of Denk, Prüss and Zacher
[
21
]
, who considered dynamic boundary conditions
for diffusion, surface diffusion, the linearised Stefan problem, and linearised free boundary value problems for the Navier-Stokes equations. Furthermore, Prüss, Racke, and Zheng

[
45
]
obtained a strong well-posedness result for the Cahn-Hillard equation with dynamic boundary condition.

Let us also mention that Díaz and Tello
[
24
]
proved the existence of a weak solution to certain energy balance models, where the fluid is
passive
, in the sense that the velocity
u
of the
fluid is prescribed and not part of the problem.

There is a large literature on stochastic fluid systems or stochastic heat systems with noise or with noise on the boundary. We refer here for example to the work of
Cerrai and Freidlin
[
12
]
or Debussche, Fuhrmann and Tessitore
[
16
]
, Brzezniak, Goldys, Peszat and Russo
[
8
]
, Binz, Hieber, Hussein and Saal
[
6
]
. Regarding the primitive equations with additive or multiplicative noise, we refer to
[
30
]
and
[
15
,
29
]
.
For further results focusing on linear boundary conditions and solutions with
\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{H}^{1})
regularity, we refer to
[
7
,
48
]
.
Note that in contrast to the above cited studies, we consider
dynamic boundary conditions in the strong sense subject to noise
.

The aim of this article is threefold: firstly, we couple the energy balance model with an
active
fluid, meaning that the ocean’s velocity is not a given function but governed by the
coupling with the incompressible primitive equations. More precisely, the coupling is given by a dynamic boundary condition for the temperature on the boundary subject to a transport term on the
upper boundary
\Gamma_{u}
of the form
v|_{\Gamma_{u}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho
. Here,
v
denotes the horizontal component of the (unknown) velocity
u=(v,w)
.

Secondly, we aim to prove the existence of a
unique, global, strong
solution to the system (
1.1
) for
arbitrary large
initial data belonging to

\mathrm{W}_{\mathrm{per}}^{2(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}),p}\times\mathrm{W}_{\mathrm{per}}^{2(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}),p}
for
p\in[2,\infty)\setminus\{3\}
, satisfying the horizontal solenoidal divergence-free condition. In the special case of
p=2
, we thus
obtain global strong well-posedness of solutions for arbitrary large data in
\mathrm{H}^{1}\times\mathrm{H}^{1}
.
This is rather surprising, since we would be unable to do so when the geophysical fluid would be replaced, for example, by the Navier-Stokes equations.

Thirdly, concerning equations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), let us recall that we are dealing with

dynamic boundary conditions in the strong sense subject to noise
. It seems that, independent of the system under consideration, there are no results known concerning strong solutions
for noise on the boundary subject to dynamic boundary conditions. Our final aim is to prove the existence of a unique, global, strong solution
in the pathwise sense with regularity
\mathrm{C}(\mathrm{H}^{1})\cap\mathrm{L}^{2}(\mathrm{H}^{2})
for arbitrary large data in
\mathrm{H}^{1}\times\mathrm{H}^{1}
.

At this point, some words about our approach are in order. We will comment on the physics behind our model in
Section 2
and
Section 3
. In order to establish global, strong well-posedness results for the system (
1.1
) and (
1.2
), we associate with the temperature equations the differential
operator
\mathcal{A}(D,D_{\mathrm{H}})
, defined by

\mathcal{A}(D,D_{\mathrm{H}})(T,\rho):=(\Delta T,-\gamma\partial_{z}T+\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}\rho),

subject to the boundary condition
(\partial_{z}T)|_{\Gamma_{b}}=0
. Here,
\gamma
denotes the trace operator and
\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}
the two-dimensional Laplace operator with periodic boundary conditions.
For
p\in(1,\infty)
, consider the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-realization of
\mathcal{A}
in
\mathrm{X}^{(T,\rho)}_{0}:=\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathrm{L}^{p}(G)
, defined by

(1.4)

\displaystyle A(T,\rho)=\mathcal{A}(D,D_{\mathrm{H}})(T,\rho)\mbox{ with }\mathrm{D}(A)=\bigr\{(T,\rho)\in\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{2,p}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{2,p}(G)\ \colon T|_{\Gamma_{u}}=\rho,\ (\partial_{z}T)|_{\Gamma_{b}}=0\bigl\}.

We will then show in
Section 5
that the operator
A
admits a bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus on
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathrm{L}^{p}(G)
with angle
\Phi_{A}<\pi/2
.
That is,
A
is sectorial with spectral angle
\varphi_{A}<\Phi_{A}
, and there exists
\Phi_{A}\in(\varphi_{A},\pi/2)
such that the following holds.
Let
\Sigma_{\Phi_{A}}:=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}:|\arg\lambda|<\Phi_{A}\}
,
\rho(\lambda):=\frac{\lambda}{(1+\lambda)^{2}},
and

\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{0}(\Sigma_{\Phi_{A}}):=\{f\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}(\Sigma_{\Phi_{A}}):\exists\,C,\varepsilon>0\text{ s.t. }|f(\lambda)|\leq C\,|\rho(\lambda)|^{\varepsilon},\;\lambda\in\Sigma_{\Phi_{A}}\}.

Then
A
has a bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus of angle
\Phi_{A}
if

\|f(A)\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathrm{L}^{p}(G))}\leq C_{\Phi_{A}}\sup_{z\in\Sigma_{\Phi_{A}}}|f(z)|
for all
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\infty}_{0}(\Sigma_{\Phi_{A}}).

This property is crucial because in the case where
\Phi_{A}\leq\pi/2
it implies maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity for
A
, a key tool in our analysis.

This yields, as a first step, the existence of a unique, local, strong solution to system (
4.1
).
Energy estimates are now crucial for extending this solution to a global one for arbitrary large data. Note that the presence of the non-linear boundary transport term

v^{\varepsilon}|_{\Gamma_{u}}\cdot\nabla_{H}\rho
naturally leads to the appearance of the term
\int_{G}v^{\varepsilon}|_{\Gamma_{u}}\cdot\nabla_{H}\rho\cdot\rho
in the classical energy estimate used for
the global well-posedness result. This term, however, does not vanish, as
v^{\varepsilon}|_{\Gamma_{u}}
no longer satisfies the (horizontal) divergence-free condition.
The control of this term thus crucially depends on obtaining a uniform
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
bound for
\rho
. We derive such a bound by a suitable maximum principle, hereby
adapting a result of Ewald and Temam
[
25
]
to our setting. This enables us to
close the energy estimates and to prove global well-posedness for arbitrary large data belonging to
\mathrm{W}_{\mathrm{per}}^{2(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}),p}\times\mathrm{W}_{\mathrm{per}}^{2(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}),p}
, for
p\in[2,\infty)\setminus\{3\}
.

To investigate the effect of noise in the temperature equation on the boundary, we treat the fluid and temperature equations separately, and first consider the temperature as a
stochastic evolution equation.
The boundedness of the
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus of
A
on
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathrm{L}^{p}(G)
has now another important consequence for the noise on the boundary.
In fact, the linearised problem enjoys stochastic maximal regularity, see
[
43
,
1
,
2
]
.
To handle the nonlinear problem, we employ the Da Prato-Debussche trick (
[
13
,
14
]
). The central idea is to split the solution into two components: a rough part that captures the full irregularity of the boundary noise via the stochastic convolution of the linearised system, and a smoother remainder that satisfies a deterministic evolution equation with random coefficients. This transformation has two advantages: first, it isolates the irregular behaviour of the noise in an explicitly controlled component; second, it reduces the original stochastic PDE with nonlinearities into a random PDE where classical energy estimates can be applied. In this way, the nonlinear terms act only on the more regular remainder, and we can establish the existence of unique, global, strong solutions in the pathwise sense.

Regarding open problems, let us note that our assumption of spatial periodicity for the horizontal domain
G
is a simplification. The most realistic domain would require more complex geometries (e. g., spherical caps or annuli) and considerably more technical effort. The present work should thus be viewed as a first analytical step in the simplified periodic setting of the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
, providing a foundation for future studies on more realistic domains.

This article is organized as follows. In
Section 3
, we present the formulation of our 2D-EBM coupled to an active 3D ocean, described by the primitive equations.
In
Section 4
, we provide an equivalent formulation of the model along with the fundamental elements of our functional setting. We also state there our main results regarding
the existence and uniqueness of a global strong solution as well as additional regularity properties for the deterministic setting. In
Section 5
, we prove the local well-posedness by establishing a
bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus for the operator associated with the temperature and its dynamic boundary condition. Next, in
Section 6
, we establish the
global well-posedness of the strong solution to (
1.1
). Our proof is based on a maximum principle for the ocean’s temperature
(obtained under the additional hypothesis of an
\mathrm{L}^{\infty}
- bound on the initial temperature), the maximal
L^{p}
-regularity estimate for the linearized system and energy estimates. Finally, in
Section 7
, we provide a proof of the global existence result concerning the stochastic system (
1.2
).

2.
Description behind existing energy balance models

Modelling the Earth’s climate system is, naturally, a highly challenging task. Scientists therefore rely on a hierarchy of climate models, ranging from elementary elementary averaged models that elegantly describe the main drivers of Earth’s climate to fully coupled general circulation models that incorporate fluid dynamics as well as
chemical and biological processes. Among these, energy balance models , though not coupled to a fluid, occupy a fundamental role. These were were introduced independently by Budyko and Sellers in 1969
[
9
,
46
]
.
They assume that the Earth’s surface temperature
T
evolves according to the balance between the radiation absorbed by the planet and the radiation it emits. Ultimately,
while EBMs simplify many of the intricate processes governing climate, they remain indispensable for their clarity, analytical tractability, and capacity to reveal the
underlying structure of Earth’s climate dynamics. For more information we refer to the articles
[
5
,
10
,
17
,
18
]
.

Let us now describe various energy balance models in more detail. We denote by
\Gamma_{u}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
an open set representing a simplified description of Earth’s surface.
A two-dimensional energy balance model (2D-EBM) is given by the following equation

c(x)\,\partial_{t}\rho=\operatorname{div}\bigl(\kappa(x)\nabla\rho\bigr)+R_{a}(x,\rho)-R_{e}(x,\rho),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }\Gamma_{u}\times(0,\tau),

where
\rho(x,t)
denotes the Earth’s surface temperature at the point
x\in\mathcal{O}
and time
t\in(0,\tau)
,
c(x)
represents the heat capacity, and

\operatorname{div}\bigl(\kappa(x)\nabla\rho\bigr)
models the heat diffusion in a simplified way. For a more detailed discussion on this topic, see
[
44
]
.
The functions
R_{a}
and
R_{e}
denote the radiation absorbed and emitted by the planet, respectively. The parametrizations of these terms vary slightly among different authors;
we discuss the details in
Section 3
. A one-dimensional energy balance model (1D-EBM) is obtained by averaging the 2D-EBM along the longitude direction,
resulting in a temperature
\overline{\rho}
that depends only on the latitude, see
[
27
]
.

In 1990, Watts and Morantine (
[
40
]
) proposed a two-dimensional (latitude-depth) upwelling-diffusion ocean model coupled with a 1D-EBM. Their main goal was to study the
interaction between Earth’s surface and the deep ocean, particularly starting from the analysis rapid climate change recorded paleoclimatic data. Starting from the work of Watts and Morantine,
Díaz and Tello (
[
24
]
) investigated a 2D-EBM coupled to a 3D ocean model, considering, however, a
passive
field
u\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
for the ocean’s velocity.
The domain is simplified to
\mathcal{O}=G\times(0,1)\subset\mathbb{R}^{3},

with
G\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
representing the horizontal component of the ocean. Denoting by
\Gamma_{u}=G\times\{1\}
the upper boundary (i. e., the ocean’s surface), the model
proposed by Díaz and Tello takes the form:

(2.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}T+u\cdot\nabla T-\Delta T&=0,&\quad\text{in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\[2.84526pt]
T|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=\rho,&\quad\text{in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\[2.84526pt]
\partial_{t}\rho-\divH\bigl(\kappa(x)\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho\bigr)+\bigl(\partial_{z}T\bigr)|_{G\times\{0\}}+F(x,\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho)&=R(x,\rho),&\quad\text{in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\[2.84526pt]
\overline{T}(0)&=\overline{T}_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

where
F\colon\Gamma_{u}\times\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
represents, in a simplified manner, the transport along the boundary.
The main improvements introduced by Díaz and Tello compared to previous models are: the inclusion of an additional spatial dimension in the EBM on the boundary and to the ocean’s
interior temperature, the ocean model now incorporates not only upwelling (as in Watts and Morantine’s model) but also horizontal transport via the term
u\cdot\nabla T
,
and the addition of a simplified boundary transport term
F(x,\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho)
.

3.
The new model: coupling to a geophysical flow

In this section, we describe the coupling of the energy balance model to the primitive equations, which are a basic model in geophysical fluid dynamics for oceanic and atmospheric dynamics.
We assume that the velocity of the fluid is governed by the primitive equations. These are derived from the
Navier-Stokes equations under the assumption of hydrostatic balance for the pressure. We refer to works of Majda
[
39
]
and Vallis
[
47
]
for details.

The primitive equations have been introduced in a series of
papers by Lions, Temam and Wang
[
36
,
37
,
38
]
. In their pioneering work, they proved the existence of a weak solution to the
primitive equations; its uniqueness remains an open problem until today. Since then, both the compressible and incompressible primitive equations have been the subject of
intensive mathematical investigations. A celebrated result of Cao and Titi
[
11
]
shows that the incompressible primitive equations, subject to Neumann boundary conditions, are
globally strongly well-posed in the three-dimensional setting for arbitrarily large data in
\mathrm{H}^{1}
. For different approaches and results, including those in
critical spaces, we refer e. g., to
[
35
,
33
,
28
]
.

Consider a cylindrical domain
\mathcal{O}=G\times(0,1),
with
G=(0,1)^{2}
. This domain models the ocean, with the upper boundary
\Gamma_{u}=G\times\{1\}
representing the ocean’s surface. The surface temperature, denoted by
\rho=T|_{G\times\{1\}},

is governed by the two-dimensional energy balance model (2D-EBM)

(3.1)

\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}\rho+v|_{\Gamma_{u}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho+(\partial_{z}T)|_{\Gamma_{u}}=R(x,\rho),\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }G\times(0,\tau).

In the above, all physical constants (such as the heat capacity and diffusion constants) have been normalized to one. In (
3.1
), the term
\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}\rho
models the horizontal diffusion of temperature,
v|_{\Gamma_{u}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho
captures the horizontal advection at the surface, and
(\partial_{z}T)|_{\Gamma_{u}}
represents the vertical heat flux from the ocean interior to the surface. The reaction term is given by

(3.2)

R(x,\rho)=Q(x)\,\beta(\rho)-|\rho|^{3}\rho,\hskip 10.00002ptx\in\Gamma_{u},\;\rho\in\mathbb{R},

where the solar radiation
Q\in C^{1}_{b}(G)
is positive. The outgoing radiation is modelled via the Stefan-Boltzmann law. The co-albedo function
\beta
is parametrized as

(3.3)

\beta(\rho)=\beta_{1}+(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})\,\frac{1+\tanh(\rho-\rho_{\mathrm{ref}})}{2},

with
0<\beta_{1}<\beta_{2}
corresponding to the co-albedo values for ice-covered and ice-free conditions, respectively. The parameter
\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}
denotes a critical reference temperature, usually chosen as
\rho_{\mathrm{ref}}=-263K
, at which ice turns white (see
[
22
]
). We remark that our EBM is of Sellers-type, i.e. with Lipschitz continuous co-albedo. The other class of EBM the Budyko type, characterised discontinuous co-albedo. For the latter, it is known that even for the EBM alon, uniqueness of the solution does not hod; see, for instance,
[
23
]
for a discussion of the non-uniquenss of weak solutions.

The temperature
T\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
in interior of the ocean is coupled to the surface model through the dynamic boundary condition at
\Gamma_{u}
. We assume that the temperature
T
satisfies a Heat equation and is transported along the full velocity field and the surface energy balance (
3.1
) acts as a dynamic boundary condition for the temperature
T
at
\Gamma_{u}
. Hence we arrive at the system

(3.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}T+u\cdot\nabla T-\Delta T&=0,\quad&\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
T|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=\rho,\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
\partial_{t}\rho+v|_{\Gamma_{u}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho-\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}\rho+(\partial_{z}T)|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=R(x,\rho),\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
{T}(0)&={T}_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Our aim is to study an
active
fluid, meaning that the ocean velocity
u
is not prescribed but instead determined by the
incompressible primitive equations
, which are governed by the system

(3.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}v+u\cdot\nabla v-\Delta v+\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}p&=0,&\quad\text{in }\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
\partial_{z}p&=-T,&\quad\text{in }\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
\mathrm{div}\,u&=0,&\quad\text{in }\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
v(0)&=v_{0}.\end{aligned}\right.

Here
u=(v,w)
where
v\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
denotes the horizontal part of the velocity field and
w\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes its vertical part. Moreover,
p\colon\mathcal{O}\to\mathbb{R}
denotes the pressure of the ocean. We emphasize that the primitive equations of the ocean (
3.5
)
describe the
fast
fluid motions, whereas the temperature model
(
3.4
) evolves on a
slow
time scale.

To couple equations (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) togheter, it is natural to separate the
characteristic time scales and apply a nondimensionalization. Let
\tau^{\prime}
denote the fast (weather/seasonal) time, and introduce the slow time variable

\tau=\varepsilon\tau^{\prime},\hskip 20.00003pt0<\varepsilon\ll 1,

where
\varepsilon\sim\nicefrac{{\tau_{\text{fast}}}}{{\tau_{\text{slow}}}}\ \text{(e.\ g., days--months over years--decades).}

We then define the rescaled velocity and pressure fields of the ocean by

u^{\varepsilon}(t^{\prime},x,y,z):=u(\varepsilon t,x,y,z),\hskip 20.00003ptp^{\varepsilon}(t^{\prime},x,y,z):=p(\varepsilon t,x,y,z),

and consider the combined systems, consisting of the rescaled primitive equations and the climate model (
3.4
), given by the equations (
1.1
) for the deterministic model, and equations (
1.2
) for the stochastic model, where the transport term is replaced by the transport along the averaged velocity field.

Summarizing, the system (
1.1
) combines a two-dimensional reaction-diffusion model on the surface with a three-dimensional fluid dynamic model for the ocean interior.
The 2D-EBM captures the primary mechanisms of radiative balance through the parametrization in (
3.2
)–(
3.3
), while the primitive equations account for the
full dynamical behaviour of the ocean. In particular, the advective term
v^{\varepsilon}|_{\Gamma_{u}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho
emphasizes the role of horizontal transport in redistributing heat along the surface.

By treating the 2D-EBM as a dynamic boundary condition, the model naturally couples the surface radiative processes with the interior dynamics. This coupling is fundamental in
understanding how surface phenomena (such as albedo feedbacks) influence the overall oceanic temperature distribution.

Lastly, we introduce an additive noise term to our model on the dynamic boundary condition to capture the unresolved variability arising from fast weather processes
interacting with slower climate dynamics. Physically, this noise represents random fluctuations in the boundary heat flux, for example, variations in solar radiation or
atmospheric conditions, that directly affect the ocean’s surface temperature. Such fluctuations are critical in accurately modelling the stochastic nature of the climate system,
as originally suggested by Hasselmann
[
31
]
.

From an analytical point of view, incorporating noise into the dynamic boundary condition, as done in (
1.2
), adds a new additional difficulty.
In the deterministic setting, analytical tools such as
the maximum principle allow us to obtain energy estimates. However, these techniques do not extend to the stochastic framework. We thus modify the transport term on the
boundary in our stochastic model by replacing the velocity on the boundary by its mean velocity. This allows us to close the energy estimates and to prove existence and uniqueness of
global, strong solutions for arbitrary large data in
\mathrm{H}^{1}
also for the stochastic model in the pathwise sense.

4.
Preliminaries and Main Results

Let
\tau>0
be fixed. In this section we present our main results regarding the unique, strong and global solvability of the deterministic equations (
1.1
) as well as
of the stochastic model (
1.2
). Both systems are supplemented with the dynamic boundary conditions (
1.3
). For simplicity of the notation we denote in the following the rescaled oceanic velocity
u^{\varepsilon}
and the rescaled pressure
p^{\varepsilon}
by
u
and
p
respectively. Moreover, as it does not affect the analysis, we set the scaling constant
\varepsilon\equiv 1
.

4.1.
The deterministic model

Consider the system (
1.1
) supplemented with the boundary conditions (
1.3
). The equation of state
\partial_{z}p=-T
yields

p(t,x,y,z)=p_{s}(t,x,y)-\int_{0}^{z}T(\cdot,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,

where
p_{s}
denotes the surface pressure, i. e.,
p_{s}(t,x,y)=p(t,x,y,z=0)
. Moreover, the vertical velocity is determined by the divergence-free condition, namely,

w(t,x,y,z)=-\int_{0}^{z}\divH v(t,x,y,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Using this, as well as the boundary conditions for
w
, we rewrite (
1.1
) as

(4.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}v+v\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}v+w(v)\cdot\partial_{z}v-\Delta v+\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}p_{s}&=\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\int_{0}^{z}T(\cdot,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,&\quad\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
\divH\overline{v}&=0,&\quad\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
\partial_{t}T+u\cdot\nabla T-\Delta T&=0,\quad&\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
T|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=\rho,\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
\partial_{t}\rho+v|_{\Gamma_{u}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho-\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}\rho+(\partial_{z}T)|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=R(x,\rho),\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
v(0)&=v_{0},\quad T(0)=T_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

supplemented by the boundary conditions (
1.3
).

4.2.
The stochastic model

Consider the system (
1.2
) supplemented with the boundary conditions (
1.3
).
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
be a probability space with a filtration
\mathcal{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t}
. An

\mathcal{F}
-cylindrical Brownian motion on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{U}
is a bounded linear operator
W:\mathrm{L}^{2}((0,\infty);\mathcal{U})\to\mathrm{L}^{2}(\Omega)
such that for all
f,g\in\mathcal{U},t^{\prime}\geq t\geq 0
:

(i)

The random variable
W(t)f:=\mathcal{W}(\mathds{1}_{[0,t]}\otimes f)
is centred Gaussian and
\mathcal{F}_{t}
-measurable.

(ii)

\mathbb{E}[W(t^{\prime})f\cdot W(t)g]=t\left<f,g\right>_{\mathcal{U}}
.

(iii)

The random variable
W(t^{\prime})f-W(t)f
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{t}
.

If
\mathcal{U}
is separable and
(e_{n})_{n}
an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{U}
, then
\beta_{n}(t):=W(t)e_{n}
is a standard
\mathcal{F}
-Brownian motion, and we have the representation

\displaystyle W(t)f=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(t)\left<f,e_{n}\right>_{\mathcal{U}}.

Hence,
W(t):\mathcal{U}\to L^{2}(\Omega)
,
W(t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\beta_{n}(t)\left<\cdot,e_{n}\right>_{\mathcal{U}}
defines a family of linear operators.

To deal with the noise in the temperature equation we treat the fluid equation and the temperature equation separately and rewrite the equations concerning the temperature as a stochastic evolution equation of the form

(4.2)

\,\mathrm{d}\begin{pmatrix}T\\
\rho\end{pmatrix}-A\begin{pmatrix}T\\
\rho\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}t=\begin{pmatrix}-u\cdot\nabla T\\
-\overline{v}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho+R(x,\rho)\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}t+\begin{pmatrix}0\\
H_{\rho}\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}W,

where the operator
A
is introduced in (
1.4
). Secondly, we investigate the linearized system

(4.3)

\,\mathrm{d}\begin{pmatrix}Z_{T}\\
Z_{\rho}\end{pmatrix}-A\begin{pmatrix}Z_{T}\\
Z_{\rho}\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}t=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
H_{\rho}\end{pmatrix}\,\mathrm{d}W,

by means of stochastic maximal regularity due to van Neerven, Veraar and Weiss
[
43
]
. This is possible since
-A+\omega
admits a bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus on
\mathrm{X}_{0}=\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathrm{L}^{p}(G)

of angle strictly less than
\pi/2
for
\omega>0
sufficiently large. This will be established in
Section 5
. Defining the remainder term
(\tilde{T},\tilde{\rho})^{\top}:=(T,\rho)^{\top}-(0,Z_{\rho})^{\top}
we see that
(\tilde{T},\tilde{\rho})
satisfies the equations

(4.4)

\partial_{t}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{T}\\
\tilde{\rho}\end{pmatrix}-A\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{T}\\
\tilde{\rho}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}-u\cdot\nabla\tilde{T}\\
-\overline{v}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}(\tilde{\rho}+Z_{\rho})+R(x,\tilde{\rho}+Z_{\rho})\end{pmatrix},

which can be regarded as a deterministic evolution equation by the maximal regularity properties of
Z=(0,Z_{\rho})
. By defining
(T,\rho)^{\top}=(\tilde{T},\tilde{\rho})^{\top}+(0,Z_{\rho})^{\top}
, we conclude that in order to solve (
1.2
) it is sufficient to consider the deterministic system

(4.5)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}v+v\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}v+w(v)\cdot\partial_{z}v-\Delta v+\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}p_{s}&=\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\int_{0}^{z}\tilde{T}(\cdot,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi,&\quad\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
\divH\overline{v}&=0,&\quad\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
\partial_{t}\tilde{T}+u\cdot\nabla\tilde{T}-\Delta\tilde{T}&=0,\quad&\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
\tilde{T}|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=\tilde{\rho},\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}+\overline{v}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}(\tilde{\rho}+Z_{\rho})-\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}\tilde{\rho}+(\partial_{z}\tilde{T})|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=R(x,\tilde{\rho}+Z_{\rho}),\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
v(0)&=v_{0},\quad\tilde{T}(0)=\tilde{T}_{0},\end{aligned}\right.

subject to the boundary conditions (
1.3
), where
Z=(0,Z_{\rho})
is the given solution of the linear stochastic evolution equation (
4.3
).

In conclusion, our approach outlined above to solving the stochastic model relies on the application of the Da Prato-Debussche trick. Specifically, to study the existence of a unique, strong, global solution to (
1.2
) in the pathwise sense, we proceed as follows:
(a) we consider the semilinear auxiliary stochastic evolution equation (
4.2
) for temperature;
(b) we linearize (
4.2
) to obtain (
4.3
), whose solution enjoys stochastic maximal regularity;
(c) we then consider, pathwise, the remainder between the solutions of (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), which satisfies the deterministic, non-autonomous evolution equation (
4.5
). The solution to this final equation is then obtained using, in part, the techniques adopted for the deterministic model (
4.1
).

4.3.

Let us introduce several function spaces, which are used in the sequel. Given

and
p,q\in(1,\infty)
, we
denote by

the fractional Sobolev spaces and by

the Bessel potential spaces, where

is an open set.
As usual, we set

,
\mathrm{H}^{s}(\mathcal{O}):=\mathrm{H}^{s,2}(\mathcal{O})
and note that
\mathrm{H}^{s,q}(\mathcal{O})
coincides with the
classical Sobolev space
\mathrm{W}^{m,q}(\mathcal{O})
whenever
s=m\in\mathrm{N}
. For more information on these function spaces we refer e.g. to
[
3
]
.

We also introduce the terminology needed to describe periodic boundary conditions on

as well as on

. Given

and

we define the spaces

where horizontal periodicity is modelled by the function spaces
\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\overline{\mathcal{O}})
and
\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(\overline{G})
defined in
[
33
]
. Of course, we interpret

as

. For more information regarding spaces equipped with periodic boundary conditions, see also
[
33
,

]
. Next, we define the space of
hydrostatically solenoidal vector fields
by

\mathrm{L}^{q}_{\overline{\sigma}}(\mathcal{O})=\overline{\{v\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}(\overline{\mathcal{O}};\mathbb{R}^{2})\colon\divH\overline{v}=0\}}^{\|\cdot\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}(\mathcal{O})}}.

Their role in the analysis of the primitive equations parallels that of the solenoidal vector fields for the Navier-Stokes equations.

5.
Linear theory and local well-posedness: deterministic setting

In this section, we establish the maximal regularity results concerning the decoupled linear equations associated with (
4.1
) and prove local well-posedness of the coupled
system. Let
0<\tau\leq\infty
given a well as
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. The linear system for the velocity is given by

(5.1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}v-\Delta v+\omega_{1}v+\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}p_{s}&=f_{1},&\quad\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau),\\
\divH\overline{v}&=0,&\quad\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
(\partial_{z}v)|_{\Gamma_{u}\cup\Gamma_{b}}&=0,\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
&=v_{0},\quad&\text{ in }&\mathcal{O},\end{aligned}\right.

and the linear equations for the temperature are given by

(5.2)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}T-\Delta T+\omega_{2}T&=f_{2},\quad&\text{ in }&\mathcal{O}\times(0,\tau)\\
T|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=\rho,\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\
\partial_{t}\rho-\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}\rho+\omega_{2}\rho+(\partial_{z}T)|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=f_{3},\quad&\text{ in }&G\times(0,\tau),\\

T(0)&=T_{0},\quad&\text{ in }&G.\end{aligned}\right.

In order to state the well-posedness result for the linearized primitive equations (
5.1
), we define the time trace space

\mathrm{X}^{v}_{\gamma}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{aligned} \{&v_{0}\in\mathrm{W}_{\mathrm{per}}^{2(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}),p}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\overline{\sigma}}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R}^{2})\ \text{ and }\ (\partial_{z}v_{0})|_{\Gamma_{u}\cup\Gamma_{b}}=0\},\quad&&p>3,\\
&\mathrm{W}_{\mathrm{per}}^{2(1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}),p}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\overline{\sigma}}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R}^{2}),\quad&&1<p<3,\ p\neq\nicefrac{{3}}{{2}}.\end{aligned}\right.

We then recall the following result from
[
28
]
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)\setminus\{\frac{3}{2},3\}
. Then there exists
\omega_{1}\geq 0
such that the system (
5.1
) admits a unique, strong solution
v
and
p_{s}
satisfying

v\in\mathbb{E}^{v}_{1,\infty}:=\mathrm{H}^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\overline{\sigma}}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{2,p}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R}^{2})\cap\mathrm{L}^{p}_{\overline{\sigma}}(\mathcal{O};\mathbb{R}^{2}))\ \text{ and }\ p_{s}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\dot{\mathrm{H}}_{\mathrm{per}}^{1,p}(G))

if and only if the data
(f_{1},v_{0})
satisfies

f_{1}\in\mathrm{L}^{p}((0,\infty)\times\mathcal{O})=:\mathbb{E}_{0,\infty}^{v}\ \text{ and }\ v_{0}\in\mathrm{X}^{v}_{\gamma}.

In that case, there exists a constant
C>0
such that
v
and

satisfy the estimate

\|v\|_{\mathbb{E}^{v}_{1,\infty}}+\|\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}p_{s}\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times G)}\leq C\bigr(\|f_{1}\|_{\mathbb{E}^{v}_{0,\infty}}+\|v_{0}\|_{\mathrm{X}^{v}_{\gamma}}\bigl).

To establish our well-posedness result for (
4.1
), we associate with its left-hand side the differential operator
\mathcal{A}(D,D_{\mathrm{H}})
defined by

\mathcal{A}(D,D_{\mathrm{H}})(T,\rho):=(\Delta T,-\gamma\partial_{z}T+\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}\rho),

subject to the boundary condition

. Here,
\gamma
denotes the trace operator and
\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}
denotes the two-dimensional Laplace operator with periodic boundary conditions. For
p\in(1,\infty),
consider the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-realization of
\mathcal{A}
in the ground space
\mathrm{X}^{(T,\rho)}_{0}:=\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathrm{L}^{p}(G)
, which is defined by

(5.3)

\displaystyle A(T,\rho)

\displaystyle:=\mathcal{A}(D,D_{\mathrm{H}})(T,\rho),

\displaystyle\mathrm{D}(A)

\displaystyle:=\bigr\{(T,\rho)\in\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{2,p}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathrm{H}_{\mathrm{per}}^{2,p}(G)\ \colon T|_{\Gamma_{u}}=\rho,\ (\partial_{z}T)|_{\Gamma_{b}}=0\bigl\}.

It was shown in
[

]
that the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-realization

of
\mathcal{A}
in the ground space
\mathrm{X}^{(T,\rho)}_{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}},0}=\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathrm{W}^{1-\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}},p}(G)
has the property of maximal

-regularity. In the following, we show that the operator

admits a bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus of angle strictly less than

. A similar result holds
true when considering the
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-realization of
\mathcal{A}
in the ground space
\mathrm{X}_{s,0}^{(T,\rho)}:=\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathrm{W}_{\mathrm{per}}^{s,p}(G)
for

, see
Remark 5.3
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
p\in(1,\infty)
. Then there exists
\omega_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
such that for all
\omega>\omega_{0}
the operator
-A+\omega
admits a bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus on
\mathrm{X}^{(T,\rho)}_{0}=\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})\times\mathrm{L}^{p}(G)
of angle
\Phi_{A}<\pi/2

Proof.

The proof is based on a similarity transform of the operator
A
and the fact that the bounded
\mathcal{H}^{\infty}
-calculus is preserved under similarity,
see e.g.,
[
20
]
. We divide the proof into two steps.

Step 1
.

The aim of the first step is to construct an invertible mapping
S\colon\mathrm{X}_{0}^{(T,\rho)}\to\mathrm{X}_{0}^{(T,\rho)}
such that the operator
G:=S^{-1}AS
has diagonal domain. To this end,
consider the auxiliary problem

(5.4)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \Delta\theta&=0,&\quad\text{ in }&\mathcal{O},\\
\theta|_{\Gamma_{u}}&=\varphi,\quad&\text{ in }&G,\\
(\partial_{z}\theta)|_{\Gamma_{b}}&=0,\quad&\text{ in }&G,\\
\end{aligned}\right.

with
\varphi\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(G)
. We denote its solution operator by
\mathrm{L}_{0}\colon\mathrm{L}^{p}(G)\rightarrow\mathrm{L}^{p}(\mathcal{O})
and show that
\mathrm{L}_{0}
is well-defined and bounded. Notice that for
\varphi\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(G)

there exists a unique solution
\theta\in\mathrm{H}^{2,p}_{\mathrm{per}}(\mathcal{O})
of (
5.4
) and as a consequence
\mathrm{L}_{0}
exists as a densely defined operator in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(G)
with domain
\mathrm{C}^{\infty}_{\mathrm{per}}(G)
. This allows us to define a unique adjoint
\mathrm{L}_{0}^{\prime}\colon\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})\to\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})
with
\nicefrac{{1}}{{p}}+\nicefrac{{1}}{{p^{\prime}}}=1
. To show that
\mathrm{L}_{0}
is well-defined and bounded, we prove that
\mathrm{L}_{0}^{\prime}
can be extended to a bounded operator from
\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(G)
to
\mathrm{L}^{p^{\prime}}(\mathcal{O})

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho|_{(x,t)=(x_{0},t_{0})}\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt(\partial_{z}T)|_{(x,t)=(x_{0},t_{0})}\geq 0,\hskip 10.00002pt\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho|_{(x,t)=(x_{0},t_{0})}=0,\hskip 10.00002pt\Delta_{\mathrm{H}}\rho|_{(x,t)=(x_{0},t_{0})}\leq 0.

In particular, we conclude that

∎

Proof of
Theorem 4.1
(b).

By an iterative application of

(a), local solutions
(v,T,\rho)
of (

) exists on a maximal time interval

. If

, i. e., the solution does not exist globally, the maximal existence time

is characterized by the condition

\lim\limits_{\tau\to a_{\mathrm{max}}}\|(v,T,\rho)\|_{\mathbb{E}_{1,\tau}}=\infty.

Therefore, to prove global well-posedness for

, we show that the bounds derived in
Proposition 6.4
are sufficient to control the maximal regularity norm of the solution

. By maximal
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-regularity, there exists a constant
C>0
such that, for
\tau<a_{\mathrm{max}}
, it holds

\displaystyle\|(v,T,\rho)\|_{\mathbb{E}_{1,\tau}}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigl(\|\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\int_{0}^{z}T(\cdot,\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi-v\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}v-w(v)\cdot\partial_{z}v\|_{\mathbb{E}_{0,\tau}^{v}}

\displaystyle+\|(-v\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}T-w(v)\cdot\partial_{z}T,R(x,\rho)-v|_{\Gamma_{u}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}\rho)\|_{\mathbb{E}_{0,\tau}^{T}}\bigr).

Concerning the convection term in the primitive equations as well as the transport term in the temperature equation, we verify that

\displaystyle\|v\cdot\nabla_{\mathrm{H}}v+w(v)\cdot\partial_{z}v\|^{2}_{\mathbb{E}_{0,\tau}^{v}}
