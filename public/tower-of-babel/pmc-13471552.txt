X-ray-mediated isolated microspore mutagenesis: a system for salt-tolerant line development in wheat (Triticum aestivum L.)
Authors: Wenlu Tan, Hui Li, Huijun Guo, Yongdun Xie, Huiyuan Li, Jiayu Gu, Hongchun Xiong, Yuping Ding, Le Xu, Linshu Zhao, Luxiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEstablishing a technical system for in vitro mutagenesis and salt tolerance screening of wheat microspores is crucial for accelerating the homozygous stabilization of mutant progeny, creating novel salt‑tolerant wheat germplasm, and enabling the effective utilization of saline‑alkali land. Due to technical limitations of in vitro microspore culture in wheat, studies combining wheat microspore culture with in vitro mutagenesis and salt stress screening have not been reported. Based on a previously established genotype‑dependent wheat microspore culture system, our laboratory developed a technical system integrating microspore‑based X‑ray mutagenesis and salt stress screening, thereby providing a new technical approach for accelerating the development of salt‑tolerant wheat germplasm.ResultsTo determine the optimal X-ray irradiation dose, microspores from the cultivars Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 were exposed to 0, 3, 5, 10, 15, and 20 Gy of X-ray radiation. The LD₅₀ values for Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 were 3 Gy and 4 Gy, respectively. To determine the NaCl concentration for in vitro salt tolerance screening of microspores, callus induction and differentiation were carried out on media supplemented with a range of NaCl concentrations. Adding 51 mM NaCl to the induction medium reduced regenerated plants per anther by 62.95% (Shi 4185) and 42.76% (HeNong 6425) relative to controls, while adding 85 mM NaCl to the differentiation medium decreased the regenerated plants by 46.65% (Shi 4185) and 44.02% (HeNong 6425), respectively. Microspores of Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 were irradiated with X‑rays (3 Gy) and then subjected to salt screening at the induction (51 mM NaCl) and differentiation (85 mM NaCl) stages. Under this dual salt-stress treatment, the regenerated plants per anther of Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 was reduced by 84.82% and 81.60%, respectively, compared with untreated controls. Overall, 68 and 33 doubled haploid (DH) lines were obtained from Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425, respectively. Through salt tolerance evaluation at the germination, seedling, and 5 dS/m saline‑alkali field stages, 15 salt‑tolerant lines were obtained from Shi 4185 and two from HeNong 6425, with per‑plant yield increases of 56.54%-105.81% and 54.01%-56.07%, respectively, compared with the wild type. Exome sequencing of five salt‑tolerant Shi 4185 DH lines revealed higher numbers of SNP and InDel mutation sites than those in regenerated lines without mutagenesis and salt stress treatment; Under 342 mM NaCl stress, the expression levels of salt tolerance genes TaNHX, TaPDI, and TaMYB in these five lines were significantly higher than those in the wild type Shi 4185.ConclusionsThe integrated technical system combining microspore culture, X‑ray irradiation, and in vitro salt stress screening established in this study provides technical support for the development of novel salt‑tolerant wheat germplasm.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13007-026-01549-y.Keywords: Wheat, Microspore culture, X-ray, NaCl salt stress screening, Salt-tolerant lines

Introduction

Wheat accounts for roughly one-third of global grain production and serves as a major source of human energy and nutrition [1, 2]. With the world population projected to reach 9 billion by 2050, global food demand is expected to increase by approximately 70% [3]. However, opportunities to expand arable land are limited due to constraints on land and water resources, posing a major challenge to food security.

Globally, saline-alkali soils cover about 1.1 × 10⁹ hm², of which nearly 86% have potential for agricultural development [4]. These lands represent a “potential granary” for expanding cultivated areas and improving productivity. When soil electrical conductivity (ECe) reaches ≥ 4 dS/m, yields of most crops are significantly reduced [5]. Therefore, breeding salt-tolerant cultivars has become an effective and sustainable approach to enhance saline-alkali land utilization and maintain crop yields under salt stress.

In breeding salt-tolerant varieties, enriching the existing germplasm resources is of paramount importance. Mutagenesis is an efficient strategy to induce novel genetic variations, enhancing both mutation efficiency and genetic diversity [6], serving as an effective means to increase heritable variation for salt tolerance. Common mutagenesis approaches include physical mutagenesis (e.g., γ-rays, X-rays), chemical mutagenesis (e.g., ethyl methanesulfonate [EMS], bleomycin [PYM]), and space mutagenesis [7]. According to the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) Mutant Variety Database, approximately 17% of mutant varieties have been generated using X-ray mutagenesis [8]. X-ray irradiation is increasingly favored for its adjustable energy, flexible operation, and high safety.

Through mutagenesis, several salt-tolerant wheat varieties, such as Yuandong 3, have been successfully bred [9]. In addition, Lethin et al. [10] reported EMS-induced salt-tolerant wheat lines with significantly higher spike grain numbers and tiller counts than wild types under salt stress (7.2–9.8 dS/m). Similarly, the wheat salt-tolerant mutant lines obtained through EMS mutagenesis exhibited a 10%–30% yield increase under salt stress (3–7 dS/m) compared to the wild type [11], while X-ray–induced lines achieved yield increases of 101.5% and 121.2% relative to the salt-tolerant control Lumai 10 under 10 dS/m salt stress [12].

The combination of mutagenesis with haploid technology can shorten the time required to obtain homozygous target mutants [13–15]. Several studies have integrated mutagenesis with anther culture to produce germplasm exhibiting salt and drought tolerance or high anther culture ability [16–18]. For example, our laboratory previously combined mutagenesis with anther in vitro culture and salt stress screening, successfully developing the salt-tolerant variety H6756 after nine years [19]. Using microspores as mutagenesis receptors effectively avoids interference from diploid tissues, such as the anther wall and prevents chimera formation [20]. Due to its advantages such as large-scale processing and rapid homozygosity of mutants, microspore mutagenesis has become an effective means for genetic improvement and germplasm development in cruciferous crops [21–24]. In gramineous plants, microspore mutagenesis coupled with low-nitrogen or salt stress in the culture media. This approach successfully generated a salt-tolerant barley line demonstrating a significantly higher per-plant yield than the wild type, and a mutant exhibiting high nitrogen uptake efficiency in field low-nitrogen environments [25, 26].

Due to the limitations of in vitro microspore culture in wheat, research combining wheat microspore culture with in vitro mutagenesis and salt stress screening to develop novel materials has rarely been reported. In our previous work, we developed an efficient in vitro culture system for wheat microspores based on free dispersion culture and achieved favorable outcomes [27], thereby laying the groundwork for subsequent in vitro mutagenesis research on microspores. This study used X-ray irradiation on in vitro wheat microspores to establish a mutagenesis system and, through integration with salt stress screening, generate novel salt-tolerant materials, offering a new technical approach for creating salt-tolerant wheat germplasm.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

The major wheat cultivars Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425, which showed high plant regeneration rates in previous microspore culture studies [27], were used as materials. After seed vernalization at 4 °C for 38 days, the seeds were planted in the greenhouse of the North Experimental Field, Institute of Crop Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS), with seven seedlings per pot. Greenhouse conditions were maintained at 22 ± 3 °C during the day and 15 ± 3 °C at night, under an 18 h/6 h (light/dark) photoperiod and a light intensity of 200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹.

Microspores culture

Wheat spikes containing microspores at the mid-to-late uninucleate stage [28] were stored at 4 °C in darkness for 2 days. Before inoculation, the spikes were peeled and surface-sterilized with 5% NaClO for 30 min, followed by 3–4 rinses with sterile distilled water in a laminar flow hood. Using forceps, the anthers were carefully picked from wheat spikes and placed onto a solid mannitol pretreatment medium. After 5 days of pretreatment, the anthers were transferred to liquid induction medium and incubated at 28 ± 1 °C in complete darkness. During this process, microspores were released into the medium and developed into embryoids. After 35 days, the regenerated embryoids or calli were transferred to differentiation medium and cultured in darkness for 7 days, followed by transfer to a light incubator (23 ± 2 °C, 18 h light/6 h dark, 2000 lx). The compositions of the induction and differentiation media followed the protocols of Broughton [29] and Zhao et al. [30].

X-ray irradiation treatments

X-ray irradiation was performed using a self-shielded X-ray irradiator (Changzhou Sairui Instrument Technology Co., Ltd.), enabling safe, reproducible treatments without additional radiation protection. Anthers containing microspores at the late uninucleate stage were inoculated onto solid medium supplemented with mannitol. After 3 days, they were subjected to X-ray irradiation at doses of 0, 3, 5, 10, 15, and 20 Gy with a dose rate of 2.4 Gy/min. Two days later, the irradiated anthers were transferred to liquid induction medium, from which microspores were isolated and cultured. Each treatment was performed in triplicate. NaCl stress screening

To determine the suitable NaCl concentration for in vitro salt stress screening of microspores, three screening approaches were employed. In the first, during the induction phase, microspores were cultured in induction media supplemented with 0, 17, 51, 85, or 120 mM NaCl. After 35 days of in vitro culture, the resulting embryoids or calli were transferred to differentiation media free of NaCl for further development. Screening during the differentiation phase employed microspores cultured for 35 days in NaCl-free induction medium. The resulting embryoids or calli were transferred to differentiation media containing 0, 85, 171, 256, or 342 mM NaCl for growth. Finally, concurrent screening during induction and differentiation phases used microspores cultured in induction media supplemented with 17, 51, 85, or 120 mM NaCl for 35 days. Embryoids and calli at each NaCl concentration were transferred to differentiation media containing 85, 171, 256, or 342 mM NaCl for culture. Using cultures without NaCl in the induction and differentiation media as the control. Each treatment was performed in triplicate. The number of regenerated plants per anther was determined following 20 days of differentiation.

Generation and cultivation of new materials

Anthers of wheat cultivars Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 were inoculated onto solid mannitol pretreatment medium. After 3 days of in vitro culture, the anthers were subjected to X‑ray irradiation at 3 Gy. Two days after irradiation, the anthers were transferred to liquid induction medium supplemented with 51 mM NaCl. Following 35 days of in vitro culture, embryoids and calli derived from isolated microspores were transferred to differentiation medium containing 85 mM NaCl. Regenerated plants (H1) were obtained after 20 days of differentiation culture. Ploidy level was determined using a CyFlow Ploidy Analyzer (Sysmex Partec). Haploid plants (H1) were doubled by immersion in a 0.2% colchicine solution containing 5% DMSO. Regenerated H1 plants, after undergoing artificial or natural chromosome doubling, were vernalized for 38 days and transplanted into the field at the Crop Science Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (CAAS). Upon maturation, H2 seeds were harvested on a single-plant basis. These H2 seeds were then sown in the same CAAS field, and the harvested H3 seeds were used for subsequent salt tolerance evaluation.

Germination-stage salt tolerance evaluation

Following a previously published method [31], 20 seeds of each line were placed ventral side down on a hydroponic rack and cultured in a hydroponic box containing a 256 mM NaCl solution at 22–25 °C under a 16 h/8 h(light/dark) photoperiod for 10 days. Water treatment served as the control. Each treatment had three replicates. Germination was defined as the point at which the plumule reached half the seed length and the root length was ≥ 2 mm. Field evaluation of salt tolerance

Field salt tolerance trials were conducted at the Salt Tolerance Testing Base of the Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences in Dongying, Shandong Province (ECe = 5 dS/m) (Fig. S1). Each line was sown in three randomized rows, with 50 seeds per row. At maturity, agronomic traits including plant height (PH), effective spike number (ESN), spike length (SL), spikelet number per spike (SNS), grain number per spike (GNS), thousand-grain weight (TGW), and yield per plant (YP) were recorded for salt tolerance assessment. The mean value of each trait was calculated per row for subsequent data analysis.

DNA extraction, exome sequencing, and read mapping

Five salt‑tolerant lines (S008, S022, S037, S044, S066) derived from Shi 4185 through in vitro X‑ray mutagenesis combined with salt stress screening were randomly selected. Leaf genomic DNA was extracted, and its integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, followed by quantification using a Qubit fluorometer. DNA samples that passed quality control were used for library construction and subsequently subjected to paired‑end sequencing on the DNBSEQ‑T7 platform.

Raw sequencing data were processed using Fastp (v0.23.4) for quality control and filtering with the following parameters: -n 15 -q 15 -u 40 [33]. The filtering criteria were: (1) removal of adapter sequences; (2) removal of paired reads if the number of N bases in either read exceeded 15; (3) removal of paired reads if the proportion of low‑quality bases (Q ≤ 15) in either read exceeded 40%. High‑quality clean reads were accurately aligned to the reference genome (Chinese Spring genome v2.1) using the BWA‑MEM algorithm [34]. Finally, SNP and insertion‑deletion (InDel) sites for each sample were detected using GATK and bcftools [35].

RNA extraction and target gene quantification

Five salt‑tolerant lines (S008, S022, S037, S044, S066) derived from Shi 4185 through in vitro X‑ray mutagenesis combined with salt stress screening were randomly selected. Seedlings grown under normal conditions for 10 days were subjected to 342 mM NaCl stress. Leaf samples were collected 6 h after treatment. Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol® method, and cDNA was synthesized using the SynScript® III RT SuperMix for qPCR kit. The relative expression levels of TaNHX, TaPDI, and TaMYB were determined by qRT-PCR with Actin as the reference. The PCR program was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. Each experiment was performed with three replicates, and three plants were used per replicate. The normalized expression levels of target genes were calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method [36]. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Statistical analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was tested using Levene’s test. When variances were homogeneous (P > 0.05), one‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s HSD test was applied. When variances were heterogeneous, Welch’s ANOVA was performed, and upon significant differences (P < 0.05), the Games‑Howell post hoc test was conducted.

Nonlinear regression using the “Inhibitor vs. response, three parameters” model was employed to estimate the median lethal dose (LD₅₀). The standard error of the LD₅₀ and the 95% confidence interval based on profile likelihood were also calculated. Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Results

Dose selection for X-ray irradiation of microspores

Microspores of Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 were exposed to 0, 3, 5, 10, 15, and 20 Gy X-rays. The number of regenerated plants declined progressively with increasing dose (Fig. S2). Based on the number of regenerated plants per anther at different doses, dose–response curve fitting analysis was performed (Fig. 1), and the LD₅₀ values for Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 were 3 Gy and 4 Gy, with estimated ranges (95% CI) of 2–5 Gy and 3–7 Gy, respectively.

In the non-irradiated controls, regenerated plants per anther were 1.15 for Shi 4185 and 0.61 for HeNong 6425 (Fig. 1). At 3 Gy, these regenerated plants fell to 0.58 and 0.32, representing reductions of 49.31% and 46.93% relative to controls. At 5 Gy, the regenerated plants dropped further to 0.22 and 0.21. Regenerated plants remained achievable, but at a low differentiation rate. At doses ≥ 15 Gy resulted in regenerated plants under 0.1, indicating severe inhibition of microspore regeneration. Therefore, 3 Gy was chosen as the irradiation dose for subsequent X‑ray treatment of wheat microspores.

Determination of NaCl concentrations for in vitro screening

Supplementing induction and differentiation media with NaCl inhibited plants regeneration in a concentration-dependent manner (Fig. S3). In NaCl-free media, regenerated plants per anther were 1.15 for Shi 4185 and 0.61 for HeNong 6425. When NaCl was added only to the induction medium, 51 mM NaCl reduced regenerated plants by 62.95% in Shi 4185 and 42.76% in HeNong 6425 relative to the NaCl-free control; at 120 mM NaCl, Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 regenerated fewer than 0.1 plants per anther. (Fig. 2). When NaCl was applied only during differentiation, 85 mM NaCl reduced regenerated plants by 46.65% and 44.02% for Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425, respectively; at 342 mM NaCl, no plants were recovered from Shi 4185 and only one from HeNong 6425. Thus, 51 mM NaCl in the induction medium or 85 mM NaCl in the differentiation medium each reduced regenerated plants to roughly half that of the control.

Applying NaCl during both induction and differentiation produced a markedly stronger inhibitory effect than single-stage stress (Fig. 2). The combination of 17 mM (induction) + 85 mM (differentiation) reduced regenerated plants by 54.41% for Shi 4185 and 63.80% for HeNong 6425, and the other 15 treatment combinations suppressed regeneration by ≥ 80% in both varieties. Under 17 mM (induction) + 171 mM (differentiation) and 51 mM (induction) + 85 mM (differentiation), both Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 exhibited reductions in regenerated plants of 80%-90%. In addition, when the culture media already contained 85–120 mM NaCl (induction) or 256–342 mM NaCl (differentiation), all 12 dual-stage treatment combinations resulted in a reduction of more than 90% in regenerated plants for both cultivars.

Single-stage stress, using either 51 mM NaCl in the induction medium or 85 mM NaCl in the differentiation medium, reduced the regenerated plants to approximately 50% of the control. In contrast, both 51 mM (induction) and 85 mM (differentiation) NaCl decreased it by more than 80%. Therefore, the dual-stage NaCl treatment with 51 mM at induction and 85 mM at differentiation was chosen as the stress condition for the subsequent in vitro screening of salt-tolerant plants.

Generation of doubled haploid (DH) lines and identification of salt tolerance

Microspores of Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 were irradiated with 3 Gy, followed by dual-stage salt screening using induction medium supplemented with 51 mM NaCl and differentiation medium with 85 mM NaCl. Salt screening produced 113 regenerated green seedlings from Shi 4185 and 63 from HeNong 6425. Following artificial and spontaneous chromosome doubling, 68 doubled haploid (DH) lines were established from Shi 4185 and 33 DH lines from HeNong 6425 (Table 2).

Field evaluation of salt tolerance among the 68 lines (Table 5) revealed that 15 lines had per‑plant yields 56.54%–105.81% higher than the wild type Shi 4185 and were statistically indistinguishable from Dekang 961. This indicates a strong salt tolerance in these 15 lines under field saline conditions. Their performance in salt tolerance assessments at both the germination and seedling stages was also superior to that of the wild type (Table 4 and Fig. S4). Comprehensive analysis of yield‑related traits in these lines revealed that eight lines (S021, S037, S044, S046, S050, S054, S058, S062) had significantly higher grain number per spike (72.25, 71.16, 74.51, 70.80, 71.90, 70.86, 70.49, 71.74) and thousand‑grain weight (44.87, 44.68, 45.02, 44.70, 45.03, 46.36, 44.63, 44.12) than the wild type ( GNS: 60.53; TGW: 37.65). Among them, line S044 exhibited the highest yield per plant (24.86 g), representing a 105.81% increase over the wild type. Despite showing no significant difference in grain number per spike compared with the wild type, five lines (S011, S043, S063, S065, S066) exhibited significantly higher thousand‑grain weight (43.53, 44.15, 43.33, 46.69, 43.82), which likely accounts for their higher yield per plant. Line S022 showed no significant difference in thousand‑grain weight (41.57) from the wild type, but had a significantly higher grain number per spike (74.25) reaching the level of the salt‑tolerant cultivar Dekang 961 (66.19), resulting in a significantly increased yield per plant.

Discussion

Optimal irradiation doses for microspores in different crops

In plant physical mutagenesis studies, the median lethal dose (LD₅₀) is typically used to define the optimal irradiation threshold [37].In this study, microspores of wheat cultivars Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 were subjected to X-ray irradiation ranging from 0 Gy to 20 Gy. The regenerated plants rate progressively declined with increasing irradiation dose, indicating that X-ray exposure exerts a pronounced inhibitory effect on wheat microspore regeneration. This observation is consistent with previous findings in cruciferous plants, where both X-ray and γ-ray treatments suppressed microspore viability and regeneration capacity [38, 39]. However, microspore sensitivity varies markedly across species. Macdonald et al. [38] reported an LD₅₀ of 10.5 Gy for B. napus microspores following X-ray irradiation, based on the number of calli induced after irradiation. In the present study, assessing regenerated plants after irradiation yielded LD₅₀ values of 3 Gy for Shi 4185 and 4 Gy for HeNong 6425, both lower than that of rapeseed microspores.

The above differences in sensitivity may be attributed to the characteristics of haploid cells and differences in species genomes. Compared with diploid cells, microspores (haploid cells) exhibit higher radiosensitivity, a trait closely linked to their DNA repair pathways. Lacking homologous chromatids, haploid cells cannot efficiently employ homologous recombination (HR) and instead depend on non‑homologous end joining (NHEJ) for DNA damage repair, a pathway that is more error‑prone and confers higher radiosensitivity [40, 41]. Furthermore, factors such as genome size, nuclear DNA content, and chromatin structure also influence radiosensitivity across species [42]. With a genome of approximately 15 Gb [43], wheat has a much larger genome than rapeseed (approximately 1.8 Gb) [44]. The larger genome harbors more potential DNA damage sites, rendering it more susceptible to DNA damage at a given irradiation dose and thus more radiosensitive.

Effects of NaCl stress during different in vitro culture stages

Salt tolerance screening in vitro can be performed by applying NaCl stress during the induction or callus differentiation stages, or by gradually increasing the salt concentration through subcultures to obtain tolerant regenerants [45–47]. Conducting salt stress treatment concurrently during both the induction and differentiation stages plays a critical role in improving screening efficiency for salt tolerance [48]. Under salt stress conditions, microspore cells activate corresponding cellular defense mechanisms, thereby enhancing their salt adaptation [49]. Lu et al. [25] demonstrated that simultaneous NaCl stress during both the induction and differentiation stages in barley microspore culture effectively produced salt-tolerant lines with significantly improved yield under saline conditions than the wild type. Consistent with these findings, we observed that combined NaCl stress during both stages caused a more substantial inhibition of plant regeneration compared with single-stage salt stress. Therefore, 51 mM NaCl in the induction medium and 85 mM NaCl in the differentiation medium were employed as stress conditions for screening salt tolerance in wheat microspores. Under these combined concentrations, the number of regenerated plants per anther of Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 decreased by more than 80%. Using this screening system, 17 salt-tolerant lines were obtained, exhibiting significantly higher yield per plant under salt stress compared with their wild-type counterparts.

Effectiveness of approaches for creating novel salt-tolerant materials

Applying salt stress during embryo induction or callus differentiation enables early screening for salt tolerance at the cell or tissue level. However, salt tolerance exhibited at the cell or tissue level is not always stably transferred to the whole‑plant level [50]. In the present study, among the DH lines obtained through in vitro salt tolerance screening, some lines proved to be salt‑sensitive under salt stress at the germination, seedling, or field stages, suggesting that the tolerance mechanisms at the callus and whole‑plant levels may differ. Studies have shown that under salt stress, salt tolerance at the callus level relies on osmotic adjustment and cellular antioxidant defense mechanisms, whereas at the whole‑plant level, it is associated with the regulation of K⁺ and Na⁺ ions [51–53].

Under salt stress, seed germination capacity determines early plant morphogenesis [54], Meanwhile, the extent of leaf wilting and chlorosis is closely linked to Na⁺ exclusion efficiency, Na⁺ tolerance, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity [55], These indicators have been demonstrated to be reliable for evaluating salt tolerance in wheat [56, 57]. However, our evaluation at the germination and seedling stages showed that some lines tolerant at germination became sensitive at the seedling stage, consistent with Peng et al. [58]. This is likely because germination-stage tolerance depends on seed imbibition and osmotic adjustment, while seedling-stage tolerance relies mainly on Na⁺ efflux [59, 60]. Nevertheless, through a combined analysis of germination rates and a weighted calculation of leaf salt injury levels at the seedling stage, this study identified 35 salt-tolerant Shi 4185 DH lines and 3 HeNong 6425 DH lines that performed well in both stages (Table 4).

Furthermore, evaluating crop salt tolerance under field conditions with natural salt stress more effectively assesses the validity of in vitro salt tolerance screening [61].Houshmand et al. reported that salt-tolerant genotypes of durum wheat, derived from field and in vitro assessment methods, showed comparable yields under field salt stress conditions [62]. This study conducted a field‑based salt tolerance evaluation of DH lines derived from microspore mutagenesis combined with in vitro salt screening, revealing that 17 lines showed yield per plant comparable to the salt‑tolerant cultivar Dekang 961. These comprised 15 lines from Shi 4185 and 2 from HeNong 6425, thereby yielding novel salt-tolerant wheat lines. Further multi-year, multi-location trials will be performed to confirm the stability of their salt tolerance.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that genomic variation, known as somaclonal variation, occurs during plant tissue culture [63, 64]. For example, in vitro mutagenesis of barley microspores revealed that a certain level of genomic mutation can be induced during microspore regeneration, and EMS mutagenesis during microspore culture significantly increased the mutation frequency [14]. In the present study, variations including SNPs (55.38%), Ins (20.91%), and Del (16.35%) were detected within the exome‑targeted capture regions in the DH line obtained through microspore culture. On this basis, X‑ray irradiation combined with in vitro salt stress screening further increased the degree of variation; the proportions of SNPs, Ins, and Del in the five salt‑tolerant lines were all higher than those in the line regenerated solely through microspore culture, indicating that in vitro X‑ray mutagenesis combined with salt stress induced genomic‑level variations. Future studies will include non‑irradiated lines subjected solely to in vitro salt stress as controls to further clarify the mutagenic effect of X‑ray irradiation.

Plant salt tolerance is a complex trait regulated by multiple genes. Previous studies have shown that TaNHX sequesters excess Na⁺ from the cytoplasm into the vacuole, thereby maintaining ion homeostasis [65]. TaPDI alleviates oxidative damage under salt stress by promoting the scavenging of excess reactive oxygen species (ROS) [66]. TaMYB transcription factors regulate the expression of salt‑responsive genes directly or indirectly, thereby participating in ABA signaling, osmotic adjustment, and ion homeostasis [67]. In the present study, five salt‑tolerant lines obtained through in vitro X‑ray mutagenesis combined with salt stress screening showed significantly higher expression levels of TaNHX, TaPDI, and TaMYB than those in the wild type Shi 4185. Among them, some lines exhibited expression levels similar to those of Dekang 961 for specific genes. These results indicate that the salt‑tolerant lines can rapidly activate key pathways involved in ion transport, osmotic adjustment, and ROS scavenging at the early stage of stress, thereby enhancing stress adaptation. The coordinated upregulation of multiple salt tolerance‑related genes under salt stress provides a molecular basis for the enhanced salt tolerance of these lines.

Conclusion

In this study, the median lethal doses (LD₅₀) of X‑ray irradiation for microspores of Shi 4185 and HeNong 6425 were determined to be 3 Gy and 4 Gy, respectively. A dual‑phase salt stress system consisting of 51 mM NaCl in the induction medium and 85 mM NaCl in the differentiation medium was established as applicable for in vitro salt tolerance screening of wheat microspores. Furthermore, using the integrated approach of microspore culture, X‑ray irradiation, and in vitro salt stress screening, 17 salt‑tolerant wheat lines were generated.
