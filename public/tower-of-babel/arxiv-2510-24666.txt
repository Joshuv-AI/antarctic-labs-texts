1.
Introduction

Let
M
be a smooth manifold,
T_{x}M
be its tangent space at
x\in M
,
T^{\ast}_{x}M
be its cotangent space at
x
,
TM=\{(x,y):x\in M,y\in T_{x}M\}
be its tangent bundle and
T^{\ast}M=\{(x,\alpha):x\in M,\alpha\in T^{\ast}_{x}M\}
be its cotangent bundle.
A
C^{0}
-Finsler structure
of
M
is a continuous function
F:TM\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that
F(x,\cdot):T_{x}M\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is an asymmetric norm for every
x\in M
(see Definition
2.6
).

We will use the term “Finsler geometry” according to its more usual definition.
Its basic theory follows closely the way that Riemannian geometry was developed, mainly because its objects are smooth and differential calculus is applicable directly (see
[
7
]
).
On the other hand, we need additional techniques when dealing with some geometrical structures on smooth manifolds that generalizes Riemannian and Finsler structures.

In this work, we are interested to study minimizing paths on
C^{0}
-Finsler manifolds.
In Riemannian (and Finsler) geometry, we have geodesic fields on tangent bundles (slit tangent bundles) as a basic tool.
Although general
C^{0}
-Finsler and
C^{0}
-sub-Finsler manifolds don’t admit such type of vector fields, we can restrict ourselves to structures
F
that satisfies some kind of horizontal smoothness.
We can define a control system where its vector fields are continuously differentiable and represent
F
.
Instead of using the Euler-Lagrange equations on the energy functional in order to obtain geodesics, we can apply the Pontryagin maximum principle (“PMP” in short) on this control system in order to obtain extremals of
(M,F)
.
This is an approach that is used in works such as
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
5
,
6
,
8
,
9
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
23
]
, and an overview of them can be found in
[
20
,
22
]
.

Let us summarize this work.

A
C^{0}
-Finsler structure being
of Pontryagin type
is the way
we define the family of continuously differentiable vector fields
\{X_{u}\}
of the control theory that is useful for our purposes (see Definition
2.27
and
[
22
]
).
This family is given by unit vector fields defined on an open subset
U
of
M
, and the main idea is that
\{X_{u}\}
satisfy the minimum requirements of the PMP for the problem of minimizing paths parametrized by arc length.
As a consequence of applying the PMP to this control system, we get the extended geodesic field
\mathcal{E}
, which is a multivalued vector field on the
slit cotangent bundle

T^{\ast}U\backslash 0=\{(x,\alpha):x\in U,\alpha\in T^{\ast}_{x}U\backslash\{0\}\}
.
Its integral curves
(x(t),\alpha(t))
are the Pontryagin extremals of
(U,F)
and
x(t)
are extremals of
(U,F)
.
Every minimizing curve of
(M,F)
parametrized by arc length with its trace on
U
is an extremal of
(U,F)
.
In the particular case where
F
is a Finsler structure, the fiberwise Legendre transform from
TM\backslash 0\rightarrow T^{\ast}M\backslash 0
is a smooth map that identifies the geodesic spray on
TM\backslash 0
and the extended geodesic field on
T^{\ast}M\backslash 0
.
In other words, if we consider the identification given by the Legendre transform, then the geodesic spray on
TM\backslash 0
is included in the theory of extended geodesic fields on
T^{\ast}M\backslash 0
.
The proof of all these facts can be found in
[
22
]
.

Lie groups
G
endowed with left-invariant
C^{0}
-Finsler structures
F
are of Pontryagin type.
The control system is given by the unit left-invariant vector fields.
In this work, we study Lie groups endowed with left-invariant strongly convex
C^{0}
-Finsler structures.
In this case,
\mathcal{E}
is a locally Lipschitz vector field defined on
T^{\ast}G\backslash 0
(see
[
22
]
), what ensures that for a given
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
(x_{0},\alpha_{0})\in T^{\ast}G\backslash 0
, there exist a unique maximal Pontryagin extremal
(x(t),\alpha(t))
satisfying
(x(t_{0}),\alpha(t_{0}))=(x_{0},\alpha_{0})
.
We prove here that these maximal Pontryagin extremals are defined in
\mathbb{R}
(see Theorem
4.6
).

Let
\mathfrak{g}
be the Lie algebra of
G
. Left-invariant
C^{0}
-Finsler structures
F
on
G
are determined by their restriction
F_{e}
to
\mathfrak{g}
as it happens for left-invariant Riemannian structures.
In the case where
F
is a left-invariant strongly convex
C^{0}
-Finsler structure on
G
, we have that
F_{e}
is a strongly convex asymmetric norm (see Definition
2.11
).
We define a mollifier smoothing
F_{\varepsilon}
of
F
, which is a left-invariant version of the mollifier smoothing on
C^{0}
-Finsler structures defined on
[
15
]
.
In order to do so, we first define the mollifier smoothing
\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}
of
F_{e}
, and then we spread
\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}
through left-invariance on
G
, obtaining
F_{\varepsilon}
.
Given
(x_{0},\alpha_{0})\in T^{\ast}G\backslash 0
and
t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, consider the Pontryagin extremals
t\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto(x(t),\alpha(t))
and
t\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto(x_{\varepsilon}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t))
of
(G,F)
and
(G,F_{\varepsilon})
respectively satisfying
(x(t_{0}),\alpha(t_{0}))=(x_{\varepsilon}(t_{0}),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t_{0}))=(x_{0},\alpha_{0})
.
The main result of this work states that given a compact interval
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
,

(x_{\varepsilon}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t))
converges uniformly to
(x(t),\alpha(t))
on
[a,b]
when
\varepsilon
converges to zero (see Theorem
8.5
).

Let us make comments about some subjects related to this work.

If
(M,F)
is a Finsler manifold, and we consider
(x_{0},y_{0})\in TM
, then there exist an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and a unique maximal geodesic
x:I\rightarrow M
such that
x(0)=x_{0}
and
\dot{x}(0)=y_{0}
.
We can ask if Theorem
4.6
can be used to prove this result for Lie groups endowed with left-invariant strongly convex
C^{0}
-Finsler structures.
The answer is that the existence and uniqueness result of this initial value problem, that holds for Finsler manifolds, doesn’t hold in our case.
As a counterexample, we can
consider the Lie group
G=\{(x^{1},x^{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:x^{2}>0\}
,
(x^{1},x^{2})\cdot(z^{1},z^{2})=(x^{2}z^{1}+x^{1},x^{2}z^{2})
, which is isomorphic to the connected component of the identity element
e
of the affine group
\text{Aff}(\mathbb{R})
.
Let
F
be the left-invariant strongly convex
C^{0}
-Finsler structure given in Section 9.2 of
[
22
]
, which extremals can be explicitly calculated using the PMP (see also
[
16
]
).
We have that given the initial condition
(x_{0},y_{0})=(x_{0},(0,1))\in TG\cong G\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
, there exist infinitely many geodesics
x(t)\in G
satisfying
x(0)=x_{0}
and
\dot{x}(0)=y_{0}
, and even local uniqueness of this initial value problem doesn’t hold.
On the other hand, for every
(x_{0},\alpha_{0})\in T^{\ast}G
, there exists a unique Pontryagin extremal
(x(t),\alpha(t))
such that
(x(0),\alpha(0))=(x_{0},\alpha_{0})
.
This example show that, in general, it is more natural to study extremals of
C^{0}
-Finsler manifolds using Hamiltonian formalism and the PMP on
T^{\ast}M\backslash 0
than a structure like geodesic fields on
TM\backslash 0
.

We can define a distance function
d_{F}
on a connected
C^{0}
-Finsler manifold
(M,F)
, where
d_{F}(x_{1},x_{2})
is the infimum of the lengths of piecewise continuously differentiable paths that connect
x_{1}
to
x_{2}
(in this order).
Notice that, in general,
d_{F}
is asymmetric.
Although given
(x_{0},y_{0})\in TM
we don’t have the local existence and uniqueness of an extremal
x(t)
for the initial value problem
x(0)=x_{0}
and
\dot{x}(0)=y_{0}
, the boundary value problem is much more well-behaved, even for general
C^{0}
-Finsler structures.
We have that if
x_{1},x_{2}\in(M,F)
are such that
d_{F}(x_{1},x_{2})
is small enough, then there exist a minimizing path connecting
x_{1}
and
x_{2}
(see
[
19
]
for the original result and Remark 2.6 of
[
22
]
for an adaptation of this result for
C^{0}
-Finsler manifolds).
This result is only about existence, it doesn’t state anything about uniqueness.
The technique of the proof of this boundary value problem is metric geometry, which is another important area in studying
C^{0}
-Finsler manifolds.
For another instance of the use of metric geometry in
C^{0}
-Finsler geometry, we can calculate minimizing paths explicitly in some specific
C^{0}
-Finsler manifolds, even when PMP isn’t applicable (see
[
14
]
).

In
[
15
]
, the authors introduced a mollifier smoothing for general
C^{0}
-Finsler structures, which is done in two steps:
The first step is a vertical smoothing on each tangent space.
In order to assure that this smoothing is a Minkowski norm (and therefore strongly convex), an additional term is introduced in the bump function.
The second part is a horizontal smoothing, which is done on coordinate open subsets
\{U_{\beta}\}
of
M
, weighted with respect to a partition of the unity subordinated to
\{U_{\beta}\}
.
If
F
is a Finsler structure, the authors prove that the geometrical objects of
F_{\varepsilon}
such as several connections of the Finsler geometry and the flag curvatures converge uniformly to the corresponding objects of
F
on compact subsets when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
(see Section 8 of
[
15
]
).
In this work, the mollifier smoothing on
F_{e}
is similar to the vertical mollifier smoothing defined in
[
15
]
.
The only difference is that here we suppose that the asymmetric norm
F_{e}
is strongly convex, and the smoothing is a Minkowski norm without the additional term.
On the other hand, the horizontal part of the mollifier smoothing defined in
[
15
]
is replaced by an extension of the smoothing of
F_{e}
through the Lie group by left-invariance.
Considering these differences, it is natural to ask whether the convergence of geometrical objects that hold in the previous work are also valid here.
The answer is positive, but due to the length of this work, we will leave the proof for a future work.

Besides Theorems
4.6
and
8.5
, the main contribution of this manuscript is to show that the mollifier smoothing of
C^{0}
-Finsler structures and the uniform convergence of Pontryagin extremals works extremely well together, especially because the smoothing occurs on the tangent bundle of
G
and the convergence occurs on the cotangent bundle of
G
.
This is an evidence that the mollifier smoothing and the extended geodesic field are important objects in studying this kind of geometry.
We also emphasize the importance of developing the strong convexity hypothesis properly in order to make it useful for this kind of transition between the cotangent and the tangent bundle.

Now we explain how this work is organized.
In Section

Although most of the subjects are known in the literature, some few results are new according to our best knowledge, and they are indicated along the section.
In Section

We give three equivalent characterizations of strong convexity in the general case and one additional characterizations for the smooth case (Minkowski norms).
We give special attention to the modulus of convexity, which will be important afterward.
In Section
4
, we present the Lie algebra level version
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
of extended geodesic fields when

is a Lie group.
We also present its version for strongly convex asymmetric norm

, which is given by
\mathfrak{a}\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\backslash 0\mapsto\mathfrak{a}([u(\mathfrak{a}),\cdot])\in\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}
, where

is the dual space of
\mathfrak{g}
and

that maximizes

.
As a consequence, we prove that the maximal Pontryagin extremals on Lie groups endowed with left-invariant strongly convex

(see Theorem

).
In Section

we define the mollifier smoothing

on left-invariant strongly convex

-Finsler structures

.
It is a simplified version of the vertical smoothing presented in
[

]
in terms of calculations.
The main result states that

are strongly convex with respect to the same norm if

In Section

In Section

In this section, we present the preliminaries that are necessary for the development of this work.
Throughout this manuscript,
\mathbb{V}

-dimensional vector space over
\mathbb{R}
endowed with an inner product

.
We denote its dual space by

2.1.
Convex functions and asymmetric norms

In this subsection we present the prerequisites on convex functions and asymmetric norms.
We define strongly convex asymmetric norms and we prove that Minkowski norms are strongly convex.
For asymmetric norms and convex functions, see
[
10
,
21
,
24
]
.
For strongly convex asymmetric norms, see
[
22
]
.
Finally, for Minkowski norms, see
[

]
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
U
be a non-empty convex subset of
\mathbb{V}
and
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function.

(1)

We say that
f
is
convex
if

\displaystyle f(ty+(1-t)z)\leq tf(y)+(1-t)f(z),

for all
t\in[0,1]
and any
y,z\in U.

(2)

We say that
f
is
strictly convex
if

\displaystyle f(ty+(1-t)z)<tf(y)+(1-t)f(z),

for all
t\in(0,1)
and any
y,z\in U
with
y\neq z.

(3)

We say that
f
is
strongly
(or
uniformly
)
convex with modulus

\gamma,
when there is a constant
\gamma>0
where for any
y,z\in U
and all
t\in[0,1],
we have

f(ty+(1-t)z)\leq tf(y)+(1-t)f(z)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t(1-t)\|y-z\|^{2}.

Definition 2.2
.

Consider
f:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, where
S
is a non-empty subset of
\mathbb{V}
.
The epigraph
\epi f
of
f
is a subset of
\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{R}
defined by

\epi f=\{(x,r)\in\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{R}:\,\,f(x)\leq r,\,\,x\in S,\,\,r\in\mathbb{R}\}.

Remark 2.3
.

If
f:X\rightarrow Y
is a function and
y\in Y
, we denote the inverse image
f^{-1}(y)
by
\left\{f=y\right\}
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
S\subset\mathbb{V}
be a non-empty convex set and
f:S\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
.
Then
f
is a convex function iff
\epi f
is a convex subset of
\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{R}
.
In particular, if
r\in\mathbb{R}
and
f
is a convex function, then

\displaystyle\epi f\cap(\mathbb{V}\times\{r\})

\displaystyle=\{(x,r)\in\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{R}:f(x)\leq r,x\in S\}

\displaystyle=\{x\in\mathbb{V}:f(x)\leq r,x\in S\}\times\{r\}

\displaystyle\cong\{x\in\mathbb{V}:f(x)\leq r,x\in S\}

are convex sets.

Theorem 2.5
.

Let
f:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a convex function and
\varphi:f(\mathbb{V})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a non-decreasing convex function.
Then
\varphi\circ f
is a convex function.

Definition 2.6
.

An
asymmetric norm
on
\mathbb{V}
is a function
F:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
that satisfies the following conditions:

(1)

F(y)=0
only if
y=0;

(2)

F(\mu y)=\mu F(y)
for every
\mu\in[0,\infty)
and
y\in\mathbb{V}
(positive homogeneity);

(3)

F(y+z)\leq F(y)+F(z)
for every
y,z\in\mathbb{V}
(triangle inequality).

It is straightforward that every norm is an asymmetric norm.

Proposition 2.7
.

An asymmetric norm
F:(\mathbb{V},\|\cdot\|)\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a Lipschitz map.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{B}=\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
be a basis of
\mathbb{V}
and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
be the sum norm given by
\|y^{i}e_{i}\|_{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|y^{i}|
.
Set
\tilde{C}=\max_{i=1,\ldots,n}\{F(e_{i}),F(-e_{i})\}
.
Then every
y\in\mathbb{V}
is written as
y^{i}\tilde{e}_{i}
, where
\tilde{e}_{i}\in\{e_{i},-e_{i}\}
and
y^{i}\geq 0
, and we have that
F(y)\leq\tilde{C}\|y\|_{1}
due to the positive homogeneity and triangle inequality of
F
.
Finally, the inequality
F(y)\leq\tilde{C}\|y\|_{1}
and the equivalence of the norms
\|\cdot\|
and
\|\cdot\|_{1}
implies that there exists
C>0
such that
|F(y)-F(z)|\leq C\|y-z\|
for every
y,z\in\mathbb{V}
, what settles the proof.
∎

Definition 2.8
.

Let
F:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be an asymmetric norm.
The open ball centered at
y
and radius
r
is the subset

B_{F}(y,r)=\{z\in\mathbb{V}:F(z-y)<r\}.

The closed ball centered at
y
and radius
r
is the subset

B_{F}[y,r]=\{z\in\mathbb{V}:F(z-y)\leq r\}.

The sphere centered at
y
and radius
r
is the subset

S_{F}[y,r]=\{z\in\mathbb{V}:F(z-y)=r\}.

Remark 2.9
.

Let
F,\hat{F}:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be asymmetric norms. Then
F
and
\hat{F}
are equivalent asymmetric norms, that is, there exist positive constants
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
such that

(1)

\displaystyle c_{1}\hat{F}(y)\leq F(y)\leq c_{2}\hat{F}(y),

for every
y\in\mathbb{V}
.
Indeed,
F
and
\hat{F}
are continuous by Proposition
2.7
, and it is enough to set
c_{1}=\min\limits_{y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]}\frac{F(y)}{\hat{F}(y)}
and
c_{2}=\max\limits_{y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]}\frac{F(y)}{\hat{F}(y)}
due to the positive homogeneity of
F
and
\hat{F}
.

We now introduce the definition of subdifferential and subgradient (see
[
21
]
).
We will restrict and adapt the theory for our necessities.
Let
f:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a convex function.
A vector
\alpha_{y}{}^{\sharp}
is said to be a
subgradient of
f
at

y\in\mathbb{V}
if

(2)

\displaystyle f(z)\geq f(y)+\langle\alpha_{y}{}^{\sharp},z-y\rangle,\quad\forall\,z\in\mathbb{V}.

The set of all subgradients of
f
at
y
is called the
subdifferential of
f
at
y
and it is denoted by
\partial f(y).

\partial f(y)
is also called the set of functional supports of
f
at
y
.
Since
f(y)\in\mathbb{R}
for every
y\in\mathbb{V}
, we have that
\partial f(y)
is a non-empty set for every
y\in\mathbb{R}
.

Using the Riesz-Fréchet Representation Theorem we can identify the subgradient
\alpha_{y}{}^{\sharp}
of
f
at
y
with the linear functional
\alpha_{y}=\langle\alpha_{y}{}^{\sharp},\cdot\rangle.
Thus (
2
) can be rewritten as

(3)

\displaystyle f(z)\geq f(y)+\alpha_{y}(z-y),\quad\forall\,z\in\mathbb{V}.

From now on, the elements of
\partial f(y)
will be linear functionals
\alpha_{y}
in
\mathbb{V}^{\ast}
(compare to
[
22
]
).
In particular, if
f
is differentiable at
y
, then
\partial f(y)=\{df_{y}\}
.

Strict convexity of asymmetric norms is defined analogously to the strict convexity of norms.

Definition 2.10
.

We say that an asymmetric norm
F
is strictly convex if

(4)

\displaystyle F((1-t)y+tz)<(1-t)F(y)+tF(z)

for every
y,z\in S_{F}[0,1]
,
y\neq z
, and
t\in(0,1)
.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
\check{F}
be an asymmetric norm on
\mathbb{V}.
We say that an asymmetric norm
F
is
strongly convex with respect to

\check{F}
if

F^{2}(z)\geq F^{2}(y)+\alpha(z-y)+\check{F}^{2}(z-y)

for every
y,z\in\mathbb{V}
and
\alpha\in\partial F^{2}(y)
.

Remark 2.12
.

Frequently the asymmetric norm
\check{F}
of Definition
2.11
isn’t important because all asymmetric norms are equivalent (see (

)) and given any other asymmetric norm
\hat{F}
on
\mathbb{V}

.

The relation of Definition

with the classical one (see Definition

) will be presented in Theorem

.

Now we present a smooth version of strongly convex asymmetric norms (see Proposition

).

Definition 2.13
.

A function
F:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a
Minkowski norm
if

(1)

F
is smooth in
\mathbb{V}\backslash\{0\}
;

(2)

F(\mu y)=\mu F(y)
for every
\mu\in[0,\infty)
and
y\in\mathbb{V}
;

(3)

If
(y^{1},\ldots,y^{n})
is a coordinate system of
\mathbb{V}
with respect to a basis of
\mathbb{V}
, then the
n\times n
hessian matrix

\left(g_{ij(y)}\right):=\left(\left[\frac{1}{2}F^{2}(y)\right]_{y^{i}y^{j}}\right),\hskip 14.22636pti,j=1,\ldots,n,

is positive definite for every
y\in\mathbb{V}\backslash\{0\}
, where the subscript
y^{i}y^{j}
stands for the partial derivatives with respect to
y^{i}
and
y^{j}
.

Proposition 2.14
.

Let
F
be a Minkowski norm and
y\in\mathbb{V}\backslash\{0\}
.
Then

(1)

y^{i}F_{y_{i}}(y)=F(y)
;

(2)

y^{j}F_{y_{i}y_{j}}(y)=0
.

Proposition 2.15
.

Every Minkowski norm is an asymmetric norm.

2.2.
The maximizer
u(\alpha)
of
\alpha\in\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\backslash\{0\}
on

In this subsection,
\mathbb{V}
is endowed with a strictly convex asymmetric norm
F
.
The strict convexity of

implies that for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\backslash\{0\}
, there exist a unique

that maximizes
\alpha
on
S_{F}[0,1]
.
In this subsection we study the map

.
The references for this subsection are
[
21
,
22
]
.

Definition 2.16
.

Let
F
be an asymmetric norm on
\mathbb{V}.
The
dual asymmetric norm

F_{\ast}:\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
of

is defined by

\displaystyle F_{\ast}(\alpha)=\sup_{y\in S_{F}[0,1]}\alpha(y).

Proposition 2.17
.

If
F
is a strictly convex asymmetric norm on
\mathbb{V}
, then
F_{\ast}{}^{2}
is differentiable.

Lemma 2.18
.

Let
F:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a strictly convex asymmetric norm.
Then
F(dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha))=2F_{\ast}(\alpha)
for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{V}^{\ast}.

Proposition 2.19
.

If
F:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a strictly convex asymmetric norm and
\alpha\in\mathbb{V}^{\ast},
then

\displaystyle u(\alpha)=\frac{dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)}{F(dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha))}=\frac{dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)}{2F_{\ast}(\alpha)}

is the unique point in
S_{F}[0,1]
that maximizes
\alpha
in
S_{F}[0,1],
where
dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}:\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
is identified with the differential
dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}:\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}.

Theorem 2.20
.

If
F
is a strongly convex asymmetric norm on
\mathbb{V}
with respect to
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\|\cdot\|
, then the mapping
dF_{\ast}{}^{2}:\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
is Lipschitz, with Lipschitz constant
\frac{4}{\gamma}
.

Now we prove that
\alpha\mapsto u(\alpha)
, when restricted to compact subsets of
\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\backslash\{0\}
, is Lipschitz.
This result wasn’t found elsewhere, and its proof is included here for convenience.

Lemma 2.21
.

Let
F:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a strongly convex asymmetric norm with respect to
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\|\cdot\|.
The application that associates every
\alpha\in\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}
to the vector
u(\alpha)\in\mathbb{V}
is Lipschitz over compact subset of
\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}.

Proof.

Let
K_{\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}}
a compact subset of
\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}.
We must show that there exists a constant
\mathcal{K}>0,
such that

\displaystyle\|u(\alpha)-u(\beta)\|\leq\mathcal{K}\|\alpha-\beta\|_{\ast},\,\forall\,\alpha,\beta\in K_{\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}}.

Let

(5)

\displaystyle C_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\frac{1}{F_{\ast}(\alpha)}:\alpha\in K_{\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}}\right\}>0,

(6)

\displaystyle C_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{F_{\ast}(\alpha):\alpha\in K_{\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}}\right\}>0,

(7)

\displaystyle C_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\big\|dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)\big\|:\alpha\in K_{\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}}\right\}>0,

(8)

\displaystyle L_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{F_{\ast}(\alpha):\alpha\in S_{\|\cdot\|_{\ast}}[0,1]\right\}.

Note that
L_{1}
is a Lipschitz constant of
F_{\ast}
.
Then, given
\alpha,\beta\in K_{\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}},
we have

\displaystyle\|u(\alpha)-u(\beta)\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Bigg\|\frac{dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)}{2F_{\ast}(\alpha)}-\frac{dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\beta)}{2F_{\ast}(\beta)}\Bigg\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\Bigg\|\frac{F_{\ast}(\beta)dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)-F_{\ast}(\alpha)dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\beta)}{F_{\ast}(\alpha)F_{\ast}(\beta)}\Bigg\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}C_{1}^{2}\big\|F_{\ast}(\beta)dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)-F_{\ast}(\alpha)dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\beta)\big\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}C_{1}^{2}\big\|F_{\ast}(\beta)dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)-F_{\ast}(\alpha)dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)\big\|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}C_{1}^{2}\big\|F_{\ast}(\alpha)dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)-F_{\ast}(\alpha)dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\beta)\big\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}C_{1}^{2}\big\|dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)\big\|\big|F_{\ast}(\alpha)-F_{\ast}(\beta)\big|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}C_{1}^{2}F_{\ast}(\alpha)\big\|dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)-dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\beta)\big\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}C_{1}^{2}C_{3}|F_{\ast}(\alpha)-F_{\ast}(\beta)|

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}C_{1}^{2}C_{2}\|dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\alpha)-dF_{\ast}^{\ 2}(\beta)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(C_{1}^{2}C_{3}L_{1}+C_{1}^{2}C_{2}\frac{4}{\gamma}\right)\|\alpha-\beta\|_{\ast},

where the last inequality is due to Theorem
2.20
. Setting

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}=\frac{1}{2}\left(C_{1}^{2}C_{3}L_{1}+C_{1}^{2}C_{2}\frac{4}{\gamma}\right),

we have that

(9)

\displaystyle\|u(\alpha)-u(\beta)\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}\|\alpha-\beta\|_{\ast},

for every
\alpha,\beta\in K_{{\mathbb{V}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}}},
what settles the proof.
∎

2.3.
Almost radial growth rates of
F^{2}
for asymmetric norms
F
on
\mathbb{V}

In Lemma 4 of
[
15
]
, we have already calculated the almost radial growth rates of
F
.
In this subsection we obtain a slight variation of that result.
Consider an asymmetric norm
F
on
\mathbb{V}
.

Define

(10)

r_{M}=\max_{y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]}F(y)=\max_{y\in B_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]}F(y)

and

(11)

r_{m}=\min_{y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]}F(y).

Then

\max_{y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]}F(y)=\min_{y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}\left[0,\frac{r_{M}}{r_{m}}\right]}F(y).

Let

(12)

\mathcal{A}:=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\|y\|\in\left[\frac{1}{2},\frac{2r_{M}}{r_{m}}\right]\right\}.

Lemma 2.22
.

Let
F
be an asymmetric norm on
{\mathbb{R}^{n}}
and
v\in\mathcal{A}.
Set

\varepsilon\in\left(0,\frac{r_{m}\|v\|}{4nr_{M}}\right],

where
r_{M}
and
r_{m}
are defined by (
10
) and (
11
) respectively. Then

\frac{r_{m}^{2}}{32}t<F^{2}\left(w+t\frac{v}{\|v\|}\right)-F^{2}(w)<\left(\frac{2r_{m}r_{M}^{3}+12r_{M}^{4}}{r_{m}^{2}}\right)t

for every
w\in B_{\|\cdot\|}[v,\varepsilon]
and
t\in(0,1)
, and the first inequality holds also for
t>0
.

Proof.

Let us prove the first inequality. In Lemma 4 of
[
15
]
, we proved the inequality

(13)

\displaystyle F\left(w+t\frac{v}{\|v\|}\right)-F(w)>\frac{r_{m}}{8}t

under the same hypotheses, with the exception that in that result we supposed
v\neq 0
instead of
v\in\mathcal{A}
.
Then

\displaystyle F^{2}\left(w+t\frac{v}{\|v\|}\right)-F^{2}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(F\left(w+t\frac{v}{\|v\|}\right)+F(w)\right)\left(F\left(w+t\frac{v}{\|v\|}\right)-F(w)\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle F(w)\left(F\left(w+t\frac{v}{\|v\|}\right)-F(w)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|w\|\frac{F(w)}{\|w\|}\left(F\left(w+t\frac{v}{\|v\|}\right)-F(w)\right)

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|v\|r_{m}\left(\frac{r_{m}}{8}t\right)\geq\frac{r_{m}^{2}}{32}t,

where the strict inequality is due to
\|w\|\geq\frac{1}{2}\|v\|
, (
11
) and (
13
).

Now we prove the second inequality.
Fix
v\in\mathcal{A}.
Given
w\in B_{\|\cdot\|}[v,\varepsilon],
we have that

\displaystyle\|w\|-\|v\|\leq\|w-v\|\leq\varepsilon\leq\frac{r_{m}\|v\|}{4nr_{M}}.

Using
\|v\|\leq\frac{2r_{M}}{r_{m}},
we get

\displaystyle\|w\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{r_{m}+4r_{M}}{2r_{m}}.

Then

\displaystyle F^{2}\left(w+tv\right)-F^{2}(w)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle F^{2}(w)+2tF(w)F(v)+t^{2}F^{2}(v)-F^{2}(w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2tF(w)F(v)+t^{2}F^{2}(v)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle 2tF(w)F(v)+tF^{2}(v)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\left(2F(w)F(v)+F^{2}(v)\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle t\left[2\cdot\frac{r_{m}r_{M}+4r_{M}^{2}}{2r_{m}}\cdot\frac{2r_{M}^{2}}{r_{m}}+\left(\frac{2r_{M}^{2}}{r_{m}}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\left(\frac{2r_{m}r_{M}^{3}+12r_{M}^{4}}{r_{m}^{2}}\right),

since
t\in(0,1).

Therefore,

\displaystyle F^{2}\left(w+tv\right)-F^{2}(w)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2r_{m}r_{M}^{3}+12r_{M}^{4}}{r_{m}^{2}}\right)t

for every
v\in\mathcal{A},

w\in B_{\|\cdot\|}[v,\varepsilon]
and
t\in(0,1).

∎

2.4.
The extended geodesic field

In this subsection we define Pontryagin type
C^{0}
-Finsler manifolds and extended geodesic fields
(see Sections 3 and 4 of
[
22
]
).
In short, a Pontryagin type
C^{0}
-Finsler manifold has its structure determined by a control system of unit continuously differentiable vector fields and the extended geodesic field are determined applying the PMP in this control system.

Definition 2.23
.

A
C^{0}
-Finsler structure
of
M
is a continuous function
F:TM\rightarrow[0,\infty)
such that its restriction to each tangent space is an asymmetric norm.
The pair
(M,F)
is a
C^{0}
-Finsler manifold
.
If the restriction of
F
to every tangent space of
M
is a strongly convex asymmetric norm, then
F
is called a
strongly convex
C^{0}
-Finsler structure
.

We denote the open ball, the closed ball and the sphere in
(T_{x}M,F|_{T_{x}M})
with center at
a\in T_{x}M
and radius
r>0
by
B_{F}(x,a,r)
,
B_{F}[x,a,r]
and
S_{F}[x,a,r]
respectively.

Definition 2.24
.

A
Finsler structure
of
M
is a function
F:TM\rightarrow[0,\infty)

with the following properties:

(1)

F
is smooth on the slit tangent bundle
TM\setminus 0=\{(x,y)\in TM\,:\,y\neq 0\}.

(2)

For every
x\in M,

F(x,\cdot):T_{x}M\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is a Minkowski norm.

The pair
(M,F)
is a Finsler manifold.

Remark 2.25
.

Since every Minkowski norm is an asymmetric norm, we have that a Finsler structure is a
C^{0}
-Finsler structure.

Remark 2.26
.

A
C^{0}
-Finsler structure
F:TM\rightarrow[0,\infty)
induces the fiberwise dual
C^{0}
-Finsler structure
F_{\ast}:T^{\ast}M\rightarrow[0,\infty)
, that is,
F_{\ast}(x,\cdot):T^{\ast}_{x}M\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is defined as

F_{\ast}(x,\alpha)=\max_{y\in S_{F}[x,0,1]}\alpha(y).

Definition 2.27
.

A
C^{0}
-Finsler manifold
(M,F)
is of
Pontryagin type at

p\in M
if there exist a neighborhood
U
of
p,
a coordinate system
\phi=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n}):U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n},
with the respective natural coordinate system
d\phi=(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n},y^{1},\ldots,y^{n}):TU\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2n}
on the tangent bundle, and a family of
C^{1}
unit vector fields

\displaystyle\left\{x\mapsto X_{u}(x)=(y^{1}(x^{1},\ldots x^{n},u),\ldots,y^{n}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n},u)):u\in S^{n-1}\right\}

on
U
parametrized by
u\in S^{n-1}
such that

(1)

u\mapsto X_{u}(x)
is a homeomorphism from
S^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
onto
S_{F}[x,0,1]
for every
x\in U;

(2)

(x,u)\mapsto(y^{1}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n},u),\ldots,y^{n}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n},u))
is continuous;

(3)

(x,u)\mapsto\left(\frac{\partial y^{1}}{\partial x^{i}}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n},u),\ldots,\frac{\partial y^{n}}{\partial x^{i}}(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n},u)\right)

is continuous for every
i=1,\ldots,n.

We say that
F
is of
Pontryagin type
on
M
if it is of Pontryagin type for every
p\in M
. The pair
(M,F)
is called Pontryagin type
C^{0}
-Finsler manifold.

Remark 2.28
.

Definition
2.27
does not depend on the choice of the coordinate system (see Remark 3.2 of
[
22
]
).

Now we present the definition of extended geodesic field
\mathcal{E}
on
T^{\ast}U
of a Pontryagin type
C^{0}
-Finsler manifold.

The
Hamiltonian

H_{u}:T^{\ast}U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

corresponding to the control
u
is defined by
H_{u}(x,\alpha)=\alpha(X_{u}(x))
.
Denote the canonical symplectic form on
T^{\ast}U
by
\omega
, which is the exterior derivative of the tautological
1
-form on
T^{\ast}U
.
Let
\vec{H}_{u}
be a vector field on
T^{\ast}U
given by
d(H_{u})(x,\alpha)=\omega(\cdot,\vec{H}_{u}(x,\alpha))
.
For each
(x,\alpha)\in T^{\ast}U\backslash 0
, define

\mathcal{C}(x,\alpha)=\left\{u\in S^{n-1}:\alpha(X_{u}(x))=\max\limits_{v\in S^{n-1}}\alpha(X_{v}(x))\right\}.

The
extended geodesic field
is the multivalued vector field defined on
T^{\ast}U\backslash 0
given by

\mathcal{E}(x,\alpha)=\{\vec{H}_{u}(x,\alpha):u\in\mathcal{C}(x,\alpha)\}.

Represent
X_{u}
in a coordinate system
(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})
of
U
by

X_{u}(x)=f^{i}(x,u)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}=(f^{1}(x,u),\ldots,f^{n}(x,u)).

In the coordinate system
(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n},\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
of
T^{\ast}U
corresponding to
(x^{1},\ldots,x^{n})
,
\mathcal{E}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(x,\alpha)=\left\{f^{i}(x,u)\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{i}}-\alpha_{j}\frac{\partial f^{j}}{\partial x^{i}}(x,u)\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{i}}:u\in\mathcal{C}(x,\alpha)\right\}.

The extended geodesic field is obtained as a consequence of PMP applied to the control system given by Definition
2.27
.
An absolutely continuous curve
\gamma:[a,b]\rightarrow T^{\ast}U
such that
\gamma^{\prime}(t)\in\mathcal{E}(\gamma(t))
for almost every
t\in[a,b]
is called a
Pontryagin extremal
of
(U,F).

If
\gamma(t)=(x(t),\alpha(t))
is a Pontryagin extremal of
(U,F)
, then

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}(x(t),\alpha(t)):=\max_{u\in S^{n-1}}\alpha(t)\big(X_{u}(x(t))\big)=F_{\ast}(x(t),\alpha(t))

is constant.
We also have that if
x(t)
is a length minimizer on
U,
then there exists
\alpha(t)
such that
(x(t),\alpha(t))
is a Pontryagin extremal of
(U,F)
.

Remark 2.29
.

Suppose that we have a control system on an open subset
U
of
M
(not necessarily a coordinate open subset) such that every
u\in S^{n-1}
corresponds to a continuously differentiable vector field

on
U
.
Suppose that Item (1) of Definition
2.27
is satisfied for every

.
In addition, suppose that for every

, there exist a coordinate system on a neighborhood

of

such that Items (2) and (3) of Definition
2.27
are satisfied on

.
Then Items (2) and (3) of Definition
2.27
are satisfied with respect to any coordinate system on

due to
Remark
2.28
,
and
\mathcal{E}
can be defined on

independently of the choice of coordinates.
Finally, we have that for any minimizing path
x(t):[a,b]\rightarrow U
, there exist an absolutely continuous
\alpha(t)
such that
(x(t),\alpha(t))
is a Pontryagin extremal (see Corollary 12.1 of
[
4
]
).

Now we present this theory for Lie groups (see
[
20
]
).
Let
G
be a Lie group. We denote the neutral element of

by

. Recall that its Lie algebra and its dual space are denoted by

and
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}

For every

.
Let

be a left-invariant

-Finsler structure, that is,

Denote

We have that Lie groups endowed with a left-invariant

-Finsler structure is of Pontryagin type, where

is replaced by
\mathfrak{u}\in S_{F_{e}}[0,1]

are left-invariant vector fields such that

The next result, which proof can be found in Theorem 8 of

\gamma^{\prime}(t)\in\mathcal{E}(\gamma(t))
can be represented on
S_{F_{e}}[0,1]\times\mathfrak{g^{\ast}}.

Theorem 2.30
.

Let
(G,F)
be a Lie group endowed with a left invariant
C^{0}
-Finsler structure
F.
Let
t\in I\mapsto(x(t),\alpha(t))
be a Pontryagin extremal of
(G,F)
corresponding to the control
\mathfrak{u}(t)
, and
\mathfrak{a}(t)
be the pullback

of
\alpha(t)
to
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}.
Then
\mathfrak{a}(t)
is an absolutely continuous function and
\mathfrak{u}(t)\in\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{a}(t)):=\mathcal{C}(e,\mathfrak{a}(t))\subset S_{F_{e}}[0,1]
is a measurable control satisfying

\displaystyle\dot{\mathfrak{a}}(t)=-\mbox{\rm ad}^{\ast}(\mathfrak{u}(t))(\mathfrak{a}(t))=\mathfrak{a}(t)([\mathfrak{u}(t),\cdot]),

where

is the infinitesimal coadjoint representation on

be a measurable control such that Eq. (

) is satisfied. Then, given

and

, there exists a unique Pontryagin extremal
(x(t),\alpha(t))
corresponding to the control
\mathfrak{u}(t)
such that
\mathfrak{a}(t)=d\big(L^{\ast}_{x(t)}\big)_{e}(\alpha(t))
and

Definition 2.31
.

A solution
(\mathfrak{u}(t),\mathfrak{a}(t))
of
\dot{\mathfrak{a}}=-\mbox{\rm ad}^{\ast}(\mathfrak{u})(\mathfrak{a}),
where
\mathfrak{u}(t)\in\mathcal{C}(\mathfrak{a}(t))
is a measurable function, is also called a
Pontryagin extremal
of
(G,F)
and
\mathfrak{a}(t)
is called the
vertical part
of a Pontryagin extremal.

2.5.
Mollifier smoothing of convex functions

In this section we will present the mollifier smoothing of convex functions, but before that, we will define the standard mollifier function and the mollifier smoothing.

We will restrict ourselves to the mollifier smoothing of continuous functions in Euclidean spaces, but this smoothing can be done for locally integrable functions. For more details, see Appendix C of
[

]
.

Let
U
be an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n},

\varepsilon>0
and
U_{\varepsilon}=\{y\in U:\dist(y,\partial U)>\varepsilon\},
where
\partial U
is
the boundary of
U.
Let
\|\cdot\|
be the Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}.

Definition 2.32
.

(1)

Define
\eta\in\mathit{C}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
by

\displaystyle\eta(y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}C\exp\left(\frac{1}{\|y\|^{2}-1}\right),\quad&{\rm{if\quad}}\|y\|<1;\\
0,&{\rm{if}}\quad\|y\|\geq 1,\par\end{cases}

where the constant
C>0
is chosen so that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta\ dx=1;

(2)

For each
\varepsilon>0,
define

\displaystyle\eta_{\varepsilon}(y)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{n}}\eta\left(\frac{y}{\varepsilon}\right).

We call
\eta
of
standard mollifier.

Note that
\eta_{\varepsilon}
is
\mathit{C}^{\infty},

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta_{\varepsilon}\ dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta\ dy=1\quad{\rm and}\quad{\rm{supp}}(\eta_{\varepsilon})=B_{\|\cdot\|}[0,\varepsilon],

where
\rm{supp}(\eta_{\varepsilon})
is the support of
\eta_{\varepsilon}.

Definition 2.33
.

If
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a continuous function, its
mollifier smoothing

\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast f
is the convolution of
\eta_{\varepsilon}
and
f
in
U_{\varepsilon}.
That is,

\displaystyle(\eta_{\varepsilon}*f)(y):=\int_{U}\eta_{\varepsilon}(y-z)f(z)dz=\int_{B_{\|\cdot\|}[0,\varepsilon]}\eta_{\varepsilon}(z)f(y-z)dz,

for every
y\in U_{\varepsilon}.

Before presenting the properties of
\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast f
, we will first introduce the definition of a multi-index.

Definition 2.34
.

(1)

A vector of the form
\nu=(\nu_{1},\ldots,\nu_{n}),
where each component
\nu_{i}
is a non-negative integer, is called a
multi-index
of order

|\nu|=\nu_{1}+\cdots+\nu_{n};

(2)

Given a multi-index
\nu,
define

D^{\nu}f(y):=\frac{\partial^{|\nu|}f(y)}{\partial(y^{1})^{\nu_{1}}\cdots\partial(y^{n})^{\nu_{n}}},

where
f
is a real valued function defined in an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Theorem 2.35
.

(Properties of mollifiers). Let
f:U\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function, then the following statements are true:

(1)

(\eta_{\varepsilon}*f)\in\mathit{C}^{\infty}(U_{\varepsilon})
;

(2)

(\eta_{\varepsilon}*f)\rightarrow f
as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
(pointwise);

(3)

(\eta_{\varepsilon}*f)\rightarrow f
uniformly on compact subsets of
U.

Proof.

See Theorem 6 of Appendix C of
[
12
]
.
∎

Remark 2.36

\displaystyle D^{\nu}(\eta_{\varepsilon}*f)(y)=\int\eta_{\varepsilon}(z)D^{\nu}f(y-z)dz=(\eta_{\varepsilon}*D^{\nu}f)(y).

Lemma 2.39
.

If
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a strongly convex function with module
\gamma
, then
\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast f
is also strongly convex with modulus
\gamma.

Proof.

We have

f(ty+(1-t)z)\leq tf(y)+(1-t)f(z)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t(1-t)\|y-z\|^{2},

for every
y,z\in\mathbb{R}.
Then

\displaystyle(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast f)(ty+(1-t)z)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\eta_{\varepsilon}(w)f(ty+(1-t)z-w)dw

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\eta_{\varepsilon}(w)f(t(y-w)+(1-t)(z-w))dw

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int\eta_{\varepsilon}(w)\left[tf(y-w)+(1-t)f(z-w)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t(1-t)\|y-z\|^{2}\right]dw

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast f)(y)+(1-t)(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast f)(z)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t(1-t)\|y-z\|^{2}.

This proves that

is strongly convex with modulus

3.
Strongly convex asymmetric norms and strongly convex functions

We begin recalling the following well known result about continuously differentiable strongly convex functions.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
U\subset\mathbb{V}
be a non-empty open convex set and
f:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a continuously differentiable function on
U
. Let
(y^{1},\ldots,y^{n})
be a coordinate system of
\mathbb{V}
relative to a basis of

\mathbb{V}.
Then the following properties are equivalent:

(1)

The function
f
is strongly convex in
U
with modulus
\gamma>0.

(2)

For any
y,z\in U

f(z)\geq f(y)+df_{y}(z-y)+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|z-y\|^{2};

(3)

For any
y,z\in U

(df_{z}-df_{y})(z-y)\geq\gamma\|z-y\|^{2}.

(4)

When
f
is twice continuously differentiable in
U
, the above properties are also equivalent to:

The Hessian
\Hess f
of
f
is strongly (or uniformly) positive definite at every point of
U
. More precisely,

(\Hess f)_{y}(v,v):=f_{y^{i}y^{j}}(y)v^{i}v^{j}\geq\gamma\|v\|^{2},\forall y\in U,\forall v\in\mathbb{V}.

Proof.

See Chapter 1 of
[
24
]
.
∎

In Theorem
3.2
and Proposition
3.4
, we prove versions of Theorem
3.1
for strongly convex asymmetric norms.
In Proposition
3.7
, we prove that

is a strongly convex asymmetric norm, iff there exist

such that

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
F
be an asymmetric norm on
\mathbb{V}
and
\gamma
be a positive constant. Then

F^{2}
is strongly convex with module
\gamma
(according to Definition
2.1
) if, and only if,

F
is strongly convex with respect to
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\|\cdot\|
(according to Definition
2.11
).

Proof.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose that
F^{2}
is strongly convex with modulus
\gamma.
Then

F^{2}(ty+(1-t)z)\leq tF^{2}(y)+(1-t)F^{2}(z)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t(1-t)\|y-z\|^{2},

for all
t\in[0,1]
and any
y,z\in\mathbb{V}.

Let be
y\in\mathbb{V}
and
\alpha_{y}\in\partial F^{2}(y)
be fixed, but arbitrary. Fix
t=t_{0}\in(0,1).
Given
z\in\mathbb{V},
we get from (
3
) that

\displaystyle F^{2}(t_{0}z+(1-t_{0})y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle F^{2}(y)+t_{0}\alpha_{y}(z-y).

By the Classical Definition of strongly convex function, we have

\displaystyle t_{0}F^{2}(z)+(1-t_{0})F^{2}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t_{0}(1-t_{0})\|z-y\|^{2}\geq F^{2}(t_{0}z+(1-t_{0})y).

From the last two inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle t_{0}F^{2}(z)+(1-t_{0})F^{2}(y)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t_{0}(1-t_{0})\|z-y\|^{2}\geq F^{2}(y)+t_{0}\alpha_{y}(z-y),

what gives

\displaystyle F^{2}(z)\geq F^{2}(y)+\alpha_{y}(z-y)+\frac{1}{2}\gamma(1-t_{0})\|z-y\|^{2}.

Note that the above inequality holds for every
t_{0}\in(0,1).
Considering
t_{0}\to 0
we get

\displaystyle F^{2}(z)\geq F^{2}(y)+\alpha_{y}(z-y)+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|z-y\|^{2},

which proves that
F
is a strongly convex asymmetric norm with respect to
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\|\cdot\|.

(\Leftarrow)
Suppose that
F
is strongly convex with respect to
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\|\cdot\|.
Then

F^{2}(w)\geq F^{2}(p)+\alpha_{p}(w-p)+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|w-p\|^{2},

for every
p,w\in\mathbb{V}
and
\alpha_{p}\in\partial F^{2}(p).

Given
t\in[0,1]
and
y,z\in\mathbb{V},
we have

(17)

\displaystyle tF^{2}(y)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle tF^{2}(p)+t\alpha_{p}(y-p)+t\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|y-p\|^{2}

and

(18)

\displaystyle(1-t)F^{2}(z)\geq(1-t)F^{2}(p)+(1-t)\alpha_{p}(z-p)+(1-t)\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|z-p\|^{2}.

Consider
p=ty+(1-t)z.
Then
y-p=(1-t)(y-z)
,
z-p=t(z-y)
, and adding (
17
) and (
18
), it is straightforward that

\displaystyle F^{2}(ty+(1-t)z)\leq tF^{2}(y)+(1-t)F^{2}(z)-\frac{1}{2}\gamma t(1-t)\|y-z\|^{2}.

This completes the proof of the result.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

If

is a strongly convex asymmetric norm with respect to

is strictly convex. In fact, by Theorem

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
F
be an asymmetric norm on
(\mathbb{V},\|\cdot\|)
which is smooth on
\mathbb{V}\backslash\{0\}
. If
F
is Minkowski norm on
\mathbb{V}
, then there exist
\gamma>0
such that

(19)

\displaystyle g_{ij(y)}v^{i}v^{j}:=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}F^{2}}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}(y)v^{i}v^{j}\geq\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|v\|^{2}

for every
y\in\mathbb{V}\backslash\{0\}
and
v\in\mathbb{V}
.
Moreover,
F
is strongly convex with respect to every norm
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\|\cdot\|
that satisfies (
21
).
Reciprocally, if
F
is a strongly convex asymmetric norm with respect to
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\|\cdot\|
, then
F
is a Minkowski norm such that (
21
) is satisfied.

Proof.

Before we go into the proof of this result, we make some remarks on Taylor expansions.
Let
f:[a,x]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a
C^{k}
-function and suppose that its
k
-th derivative
f^{(k)}
is differentiable on
(a,x)
(not necessarily
C^{1}
).
Then the Taylor expansion with Lagrange remainder holds, that is, there exist a
c\in(a,x)
such that

(20)

\displaystyle f(x)=\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{f^{(l)}(a)}{l!}(x-a)^{l}+\frac{f^{(k+1)}(c)}{(k+1)!}(x-a)^{k+1}

holds.
This result is usually stated with
f
being of class
C^{k+1}
on
[a,b]
, but this isn’t necessary if we see the proof of this result.
In fact, we only need that
f^{(k)}
is differentiable on
(a,x)
when applying the Rolle’s Theorem on the function

t\mapsto f(x)-\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{f^{(l)}(t)}{l!}(x-t)^{l}-\left(\frac{x-t}{x-a}\right)^{k+1}r_{k}(x),

where
r_{k}(x)
is the remainder term

r_{k}(x)=f(x)-\sum_{l=0}^{k}\frac{f^{(l)}(a)}{l!}(x-a)^{l}.

Let
F:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
be a Minkowski norm.
Parametrize
\mathbb{V}
by coordinates
(y^{1},\ldots,y^{n})
.
Then
F^{2}
is
C^{\infty}
on
\mathbb{V}\backslash\{0\}
and
C^{1}
on
\mathbb{V}
due to the positive homogeneity of
F
.
We have that
g_{ij(y)}dy^{i}\otimes dy^{j}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}F^{2}}{\partial y^{i}\partial y^{j}}(y)dy^{i}\otimes dy^{j}
is a family of inner products on
\mathbb{V}
parametrized by
y\in\mathbb{V}\backslash\{0\}
such that
g_{ij(y)}=g_{ij(\lambda y)}
for every
\lambda>0
and
y\in\mathbb{V}\backslash\{0\}
(see
[
7
]
).
Therefore, the family of inner products above is equal to the family
\{g_{ij(y)}dy^{i}\otimes dy^{j}\}_{y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]}
.
Due to the compactness of
S^{n-1}
and the continuity of
g_{ij(y)}
, there exist
\gamma>0
such that

(21)

\displaystyle g_{ij(y)}v^{i}v^{j}\geq\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|v\|^{2}

for every
y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]
(and therefore
y\in\mathbb{V}\backslash\{0\}
) and
v\in\mathbb{V}
.

Let
y_{0},y_{1}\in\mathbb{V}
be distinct points and consider
\gamma:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
given by
\gamma(t)=(1-t).y_{0}+t.y_{1}
.
If
0\not\in\gamma((0,1))
, then
F^{2}(\gamma(t))
is smooth in
(0,1)
and
C^{1}
in
[0,1]
.
Then

(22)

\displaystyle F^{2}(y_{1})

\displaystyle=F^{2}(y_{0})+d(F^{2})_{y_{0}}(y_{1}-y_{0})+g_{ij}(c)(y^{i}_{1}-y^{i}_{0})(y^{j}_{1}-y^{j}_{0})

(23)

\displaystyle\geq F^{2}(y_{0})+d(F^{2})_{y_{0}}(y_{1}-y_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|y_{1}-y_{0}\|^{2},

where (
22
) is (
20
) applied to
F^{2}(\gamma(t))
and (
23
) is due to (
21
).
On the other hand, if
\gamma(t_{0})=0
with
t_{0}\in(0,1)
, then we consider (
23
) applied to
F^{2}(\gamma(t))|_{[0,t_{0}]}
and
F^{2}(\gamma(t))|_{[t_{0},1]}
, what implies

(24)

\displaystyle F^{2}(y_{1})\geq\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|y_{1}\|^{2}

and

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq F^{2}(y_{0})-d(F^{2})_{y_{0}}(y_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|y_{0}\|^{2}

(25)

\displaystyle=F^{2}(y_{0})-d(F^{2})_{y_{0}}(y_{1})+d(F^{2})_{y_{0}}(y_{1}-y_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|y_{0}\|^{2}

because
d(F^{2})_{0}\equiv 0
.
Notice that
y_{1}=-\frac{\|y_{1}\|}{\|y_{0}\|}y_{0}
because
y_{0}
and
y_{1}
lies in the opposite sides of a line that contains the origin.
Then

(26)

\displaystyle-d(F^{2})_{y_{0}}(y_{1})=\frac{\|y_{1}\|}{\|y_{0}\|}d(F^{2})_{y_{0}}\left(y_{0}\right).

It is immediate from the positive homogeneity of
F
and from (
24
) that

(27)

\displaystyle d(F^{2})_{y_{0}}(y_{0})=2F^{2}(y_{0})\geq\gamma\|y_{0}\|^{2}.

Plugging (
26
) and (
27
) into (
25
), we have

(28)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq F^{2}(y_{0})+\gamma\|y_{0}\|\|y_{1}\|+d(F^{2})_{y_{0}}(y_{1}-y_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|y_{0}\|^{2}

respectively. Summing up (
24
) and (
28
), we have that

\displaystyle F^{2}(y_{1})

\displaystyle\geq F^{2}(y_{0})+d(F^{2})_{y_{0}}(y_{1}-y_{0})+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|y_{1}-y_{0}\|^{2}

because
y_{1}
is a positive multiple of
-y_{0}
, what proves the strong convexity of

with respect to
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\|\cdot\|
.

The reciprocal is direct consequence of Theorem
3.1
applied to

restricted to a convex neighborhood of

in

.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Proposition

isn’t a consequence of Item (

In fact, if

Before proving Proposition

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
f:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a convex function such that
f(y)>0
for every
y\neq 0
.
Let
r\geq 1
and suppose that
f
is a positively homogeneous of degree
r
, that is,
f(\lambda y)=\lambda^{r}f(y)
for every
\lambda>0
.
Consider
y\in\mathbb{V}\backslash\{0\}
,
\alpha\in\partial f(y)
and
C\in\mathbb{R}
.
If
C\alpha\in\partial f(y)
, then
C=1
.

Proof.

Suppose that
C\alpha\in\partial f(y)
. Then

f(\lambda y)\geq f(y)+(\lambda-1)C\alpha(y)\Leftrightarrow\left(\lambda^{r}-1\right)f(y)\geq(\lambda-1)C\alpha(y).

If
\lambda>1
, we have

\frac{\lambda^{r}-1}{\lambda-1}f(y)\geq C\alpha(y)\Rightarrow\lim_{\lambda\rightarrow 1^{+}}\frac{\lambda^{r}-1}{\lambda-1}f(y)=r.f(y)\geq C\alpha(y)

due to L’Hospital rule.
Analogously, if
\lambda\in(0,1)
, we have

\frac{\lambda^{r}-1}{\lambda-1}f(y)\leq C\alpha(y)\Rightarrow r.f(y)\leq C\alpha(y).

Therefore,
C=\frac{r.f(y)}{\alpha(y)}
is the unique value such that
C\alpha\in\partial f(y)
. But
\alpha\in\partial f(y)
, what implies
C=1
and settles the lemma.
∎

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
F
be an asymmetric norm.
Then
F
is strongly convex with respect to
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\|\cdot\|
iff there exists
\mathcal{R}>0
satisfying the following condition: for every
y\in S_{F}[0,1]
and
\alpha_{y}\in\partial F^{2}(y)
there exists
\tilde{y}\in\mathbb{V}
such that

\displaystyle S_{F}[0,1]\subset B_{\|\cdot\|}[\tilde{y},\mathcal{R}],\quad y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[\tilde{y},\mathcal{R}]

and
T_{y}(S_{\|\cdot\|}[\tilde{y},\mathcal{R}])=\{\alpha_{y}=\alpha_{y}(y)\}
.

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Fix
y\in S_{F}[0,1]
and
\alpha_{y}\in\partial F^{2}(y)
. We claim that there exist
y^{\prime}
and
\mathcal{R}_{y}>0
such that
S_{F}[0,1]\subset B_{\|\cdot\|}[y^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{y}]
,
y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[y^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{y}]
and
T_{y}(S_{\|\cdot\|}[y^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{y}])=\{\alpha_{y}=\alpha_{y}(y)\}
.

As
F
is a strongly convex asymmetric norm with respect to
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\|\cdot\|,
then

(29)

\displaystyle F^{2}(z)\geq F^{2}(y)+\alpha_{y}(z-y)+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|z-y\|^{2},

for every
z\in\mathbb{V}
.

Let
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathbb{V}
and consider
z\in\mathbb{V}
.
Set
y=y^{i}e_{i}
,
z=z^{i}e_{i}
and
(\alpha_{y})_{i}=\alpha_{y}(e_{i})
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Then

\displaystyle F^{2}(y)+\alpha_{y}(z-y)+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\|z-y\|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F^{2}(y)+(\alpha_{y})_{i}(z^{i}-y^{i})+\frac{1}{2}\gamma\sum_{i=1}^{n}(z^{i}-y^{i})^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F^{2}(y)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}(z^{i}-y^{i})+\frac{(\alpha_{y})_{i}}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}\right]^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(\alpha_{y})_{i}^{\ 2}}{2\gamma}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle F^{2}(z).

For every
z\in S_{F}[0,1],
we have
F^{2}(z)=F(z)=1=F(y)=F^{2}(y),
what implies

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}(z^{i}-y^{i})+\frac{(\alpha_{y})_{i}}{\sqrt{2\gamma}}\right]^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(\alpha_{y})_{i}^{\ 2}}{2\gamma}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[(z^{i}-y^{i})+\frac{(\alpha_{y})_{i}}{\gamma}\right]^{2}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(\alpha_{y})_{i}^{\ 2}}{2\gamma}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[z^{i}-\left(y^{i}-\frac{(\alpha_{y})_{i}}{\gamma}\right)\right]^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{y})_{i}^{\ 2}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\Bigg\|z-\left(y-\frac{((\alpha_{y})_{1},\ldots,(\alpha_{y})_{n})}{\gamma}\right)\Bigg\|\leq\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{y})_{i}^{\ 2}}.

Therefore if we set

\displaystyle y^{\prime}=y-\frac{((\alpha_{y})_{1},\ldots,(\alpha_{y})_{n})}{\gamma}\,\text{ and }\,\mathcal{R}_{y}=\frac{1}{\gamma}\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\alpha_{y})_{i}^{\ 2}},

it is immediate that
S_{F}[0,1]\subset B_{\|\cdot\|}[y^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{y}]
and
y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[y^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{y}]
.
Notice also that
S_{\|\cdot\|}[y^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{y}]
is the inverse image of a regular value of the smooth function
f
defined by

z\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[(z^{i}-y^{i})+\frac{(\alpha_{y})_{i}}{\gamma}\right]^{2}.

Therefore,
T_{y}(S_{\|\cdot\|}[y^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{y}])=\left\{\alpha_{y}=\alpha_{y}(y)\right\}
because
\nabla f(y)
is proportional to
((\alpha_{y})_{1},\ldots,(\alpha_{y})_{n})
, what implies that
T_{y}(S_{\|\cdot\|}[y^{\prime},\mathcal{R}_{y}])
is parallel to
\ker(\alpha_{y})
, what settles the claim.

Now we will determine
\mathcal{R}>0
such that for every
y\in S_{F}[0,1]
and
\alpha_{y}\in\partial F^{2}(y)
, there exist a
\tilde{y}
satisfying

Theorem 4.1
.

-Finsler structure

be a compact subset of
\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}.

Proof.

Let
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
\mathfrak{g}
.

Let

(30)

\displaystyle\hat{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\big|c_{ij}^{\ \ k}\big|:\,i,j,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\right\}\mbox{ and}

\displaystyle\tilde{C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max\left\{\|\mathfrak{a}\|_{\ast}:\,\mathfrak{a}\in K_{\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}}\right\},

where

are the Lie algebra structure constants.

Given
\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{b}\in K_{\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}}

(31)

\displaystyle\big\|\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\mathfrak{a})-\tilde{\mathcal{E}}(\mathfrak{b})\big\|_{\ast}=\big\|\mathfrak{a}[u(\mathfrak{a}),\cdot]-\mathfrak{b}[u(\mathfrak{b}),\cdot]\big\|_{\ast}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\big\|\mathfrak{a}[u(\mathfrak{a}),\cdot]-\mathfrak{a}[u(\mathfrak{b}),\cdot]\big\|_{\ast}+\big\|\mathfrak{a}[u(\mathfrak{b}),\cdot]-\mathfrak{b}[u(\mathfrak{b}),\cdot]\big\|_{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\big\|\mathfrak{a}[u(\mathfrak{a})-u(\mathfrak{b}),\cdot]\big\|_{\ast}+\big\|(\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{b})[u(\mathfrak{b}),\cdot]\big\|_{\ast}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]}\big|\mathfrak{a}[u(\mathfrak{a})-u(\mathfrak{b}),y^{j}e_{j}]\big|+\max_{y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]}\big|(\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{b})[u(\mathfrak{b}),y^{j}e_{j}]\big|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\max_{y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]}\big|y^{j}\big|\big|\mathfrak{a}[u(\mathfrak{a})-u(\mathfrak{b}),e_{j}]\big|+\max_{y\in S_{\|\cdot\|}[0,1]}\big|y^{j}\big|\big|(\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{b})[u(\mathfrak{b}),e_{j}]\big|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\big|\mathfrak{a}[u(\mathfrak{a})-u(\mathfrak{b}),e_{j}]\big|+\big|(\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{b})[u(\mathfrak{b}),e_{j}]\big|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\|\mathfrak{a}\|_{\ast}\big\|[u(\mathfrak{a})-u(\mathfrak{b}),e_{j}]\big\|+\|\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{b}\|_{\ast}\big\|[u(\mathfrak{b}),e_{j}]\big\|\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\|\mathfrak{a}\|_{\ast}\big\|[(u^{i}(\mathfrak{a})-u^{i}(\mathfrak{b}))e_{i},e_{j}]\big\|+\|\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{b}\|_{\ast}\big\|[u^{i}(\mathfrak{b})e_{i},e_{j}]\big\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(\tilde{C}\big|u^{i}(\mathfrak{a})-u^{i}(\mathfrak{b})\big|\big\|[e_{i},e_{j}]\big\|+\big|u^{i}(\mathfrak{b})\big|\|\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{b}\|_{\ast}\big\|[e_{i},e_{j}]\big\|\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\tilde{C}\big\|u(\mathfrak{a})-u(\mathfrak{b})\big\|\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{ij}{}^{k}e_{k}\right\|

\displaystyle+\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\big\|u(\mathfrak{b})\big\|\|\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{b}\|_{\ast}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}c_{ij}{}^{k}e_{k}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\tilde{C}\mathcal{K}+\frac{1}{r_{m}}\right)\hat{C}n^{3}\|\mathfrak{a}-\mathfrak{b}\|_{\ast},

where the last inequality is due to (
9
), (
11
), (
30
) and

(32)

\displaystyle\max_{\mathfrak{b}\in K_{\mathfrak{g}^{\ast}\setminus\{0\}}}\|u(\mathfrak{b})\|\leq

\displaystyle\max_{y\in S_{F_{e}}[0,1]}\|y\|=\frac{1}{r_{m}}.

Therefore

is a Lipschitz constant of
\tilde{\mathcal{E}}
.

Theorem 4.3

We claim for a contradiction assuming that

Let

be a sequence in

As

is a continuation of

5.
Mollifier smoothing of strongly convex asymmetric norms

In this section, we define the mollifier smoothing
\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}
of a strongly convex asymmetric norm

, which is a version of the vertical mollifier smoothing defined in
[

]
.
We prove that for a sufficiently small
\varepsilon

and

are strongly convex with respect to the same norm (see Theorem

Let

be a strongly convex asymmetric norm with respect to
\sqrt{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\|\cdot\|
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The mollifier smoothing of

is defined by

\left(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2}\right)(y)=\int\eta_{\varepsilon}(z)F^{2}(y-z)dz

for

where

(38)

\displaystyle\tau\in\left(0,\frac{r_{m}}{8nr_{M}}\right),

and
r_{M}
and
r_{m}
are defined as in (

) and (

), respectively.
Notice that
F^{-1}([r_{M}/2,2r_{M}])\subset\mathcal{A},
where

is defined as (

The following lemma will be used to construct the sphere of the mollifier smoothing.

Lemma 5.1
.

\left(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2}\right)
has the following properties for every
\varepsilon\in\left(0,\tau\right):

(1)

\Hess\left(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2}\right)_{y}(v,v)\geq\gamma\|v\|^{2}
for every

and

(2)

The radial derivative of

on

(3)

\left(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2}\right)(y)<r_{M}^{2}
for every

with

(4)

\left(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2}\right)(y)>r_{M}^{2}
for every

with

In particular we have that
\left\{\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2}=r_{M}^{2}\right\}
is a smooth hypersurface in

such that its radial projection onto

is a diffeomorphism.

Proof.

(1)
By Lemma
2.39
, we have that
\left(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2}\right)
is strongly convex with the same modulus
\gamma
of

and this item is consequence of Item (4) of Theorem

.

(2) Consider

Then

\displaystyle\frac{r_{m}^{2}}{32}t

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\left(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2}\right)\left(y+t\frac{y}{\|y\|}\right)-\left(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2}\right)(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\|\cdot\|}[0,\varepsilon]}\eta_{\varepsilon}(z)\left[F^{2}\left(y+t\frac{y}{\|y\|}-z\right)-F^{2}(y-z)\right]dz

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{B_{\|\cdot\|}[y,\varepsilon]}\eta_{\varepsilon}(y-z)\left[F^{2}\left(z+t\frac{y}{\|y\|}\right)-F^{2}(z)\right]dz

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2r_{m}r_{M}^{3}+12r_{M}^{4}}{r_{m}^{2}}\right)t

for

, where the inequalities are due to Lemma
2.22
. Thus,
\frac{r_{m}^{2}}{32}
is a lower bound and
\frac{2r_{m}r_{M}^{3}+12r_{M}^{4}}{r_{m}^{2}}
is an upper bound for
\frac{y^{i}}{||y||}(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2})_{y^{i}}(y)
.

(3) Consider
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\|y\|\leq 1/2
.
Then

(39)

\displaystyle\left(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2}\right)(y)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int\eta_{\varepsilon}(z)F^{2}(y-z)dz

where

on the support of
\eta_{\varepsilon}
.
Then

, what implies

, and

\left(\eta_{\varepsilon}\ast F^{2}\right)(y)<r^{2}_{M}

holds due to (

(4) Consider

such that
\|y\|\geq\frac{2r_{M}}{r_{m}}
.
The proof follows analogously to Item (3).
We have that

.
We will prove that
(x_{\varepsilon}(t),\alpha_{\varepsilon}(t))

Define

S_{\tilde{F}_{\varepsilon}}[0,1]

.

The Pontryagin extremal
(x(t),\alpha(t))

u(\mathfrak{a}(t))
, what implies,
\dot{x}(t)=d(L_{x(t)})_{e}(u(\mathfrak{a}(t)))

If we write

\displaystyle\dot{x}(t)=u^{j}(\mathfrak{a}(t))X_{j}(x(t)),\quad x(0)=x_{0}.

\displaystyle\dot{x}_{\varepsilon}(t)=u_{\varepsilon}^{j}(\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon}(t))X_{j}(x_{\varepsilon}(t)),\quad x_{\varepsilon}(0)=x_{0}.

Lemma

states that for every

Theorem 8.5

G

-Finsler structure

Let

\varepsilon\in(0,\tau_{B^{\ast}})

(x_{0},\alpha_{0})\in T^{\ast}G\backslash 0

(x(t),\alpha(t))

t_{i}

\phi^{-1}(B_{d_{G}}(x(t_{i}),\frac{\mu}{3}))\cap\left(\{t_{i}\}\times\{x_{0}\}\times\mathcal{U}\right)

\displaystyle d_{G}(\phi(t_{i},x_{0},u_{\varepsilon}(\mathfrak{a}_{\varepsilon}(t))),\phi(t_{i},x_{0},u(\mathfrak{a}(t))))

\displaystyle=d_{G}(x_{\varepsilon}(t_{i}),x(t_{i}))<\frac{\mu}{3}

Now consider

\displaystyle d_{G}(x_{\varepsilon}(t),x_{\varepsilon}(t_{i}))+d_{G}(x_{\varepsilon}(t_{i}),x(t_{i}))+d_{G}(x(t_{i}),x(t))

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{3}+\frac{\mu}{3}+\frac{\mu}{3}=\mu
