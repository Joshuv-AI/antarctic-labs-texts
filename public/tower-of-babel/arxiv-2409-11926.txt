Coarsest Fourier-reflexive Partitions for the Lee, Homogeneous and Subfield Metric
Jessica Bariffi 1, Giulia Cavicchioni 2, Violetta Weger 1

Abstract
. MacWilliams identities relate the weight enumerators of a code with those of its dual and are classically formulated with respect to the Hamming weight. For other metrics, however, these identities often fail when considering the weight partition of the ambient space. It is known that MacWilliams identities hold for enumerators associated with Fourier-reflexive partitions, and that orbits of subgroups of the linear isometry group always yield such partitions. This raises the question whether, for metrics beyond the Hamming metric, there exist meaningful partitions that lie strictly between the weight partition and the orbit partition: finer than the latter, yet still coarse enough to retain useful MacWilliams-type identities. In this work, we study this question for finite chain rings endowed with additive metrics. For the Lee metric, we show that the partition induced by the action of the full group of linear isometries is already the coarsest Fourier-reflexive partition refining the weight partition. In particular, no intermediate partition exists that is both finer than the Lee weight partition and Fourier-reflexive. We refer to this partition as the Lee partition and show that it allows the recovery of all additive weight enumerators over the ring. In contrast, for the homogeneous metric and for the subfield metric, we identify new, significantly coarser symmetrized partitions that remain Fourier-reflexive and still allow the recovery of the corresponding weight enumerators. We prove that these partitions are the coarsest such symmetrized partitions for which MacWilliams-type identities hold. As an application, we derive linear programming bounds based on the resulting MacWilliams identities.

1.
Introduction

The MacWilliams identities have been a major breakthrough in understanding the theory of linear codes and mark one of the most celebrated results. In a nutshell, the MacWilliams identities state that the weight enumerator of a code is related to, and completely determines, the weight enumerator of the dual code.
The original result has been proven in
[
1
]
by Jessie MacWilliams and several generalizations and reformulations, through association schemes, generating functions or graphs, have appeared since
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
]
. Other generalizations of the MacWilliams identities have been discussed in
[
7
]
and further extensions to different code families, like convolutional codes, quantum codes and others, followed
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.
The MacWilliams identities also allow to state a Linear Programming (LP) bound, giving the tightest known upper bound on the size of a code with prescribed minimum distance and length. For an overview we refer the interested reader to
[
13
]
.

This powerful original result restricts itself to classical codes over finite fields endowed with the Hamming metric. However, as soon as we change the metric, the situation becomes more complex. While the MacWilliams identities are still valid for codes over
\mathbb{Z}/{4}\mathbb{Z}
equipped with the Lee metric
[
14
]
, in 2015, Shi et al.
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
proved their failure over
\mathbb{Z}/{8}\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover, Abdelghany and Wood
[
17
]
showed the nonexistence of any version of the MacWilliams identities for Lee weight enumerators over
\mathbb{Z}/{m}\mathbb{Z}
for any positive integer
m\geq 5
.
A similar result holds for the homogeneous weight as well: apart from the trivial cases
\mathbb{Z}/{p}\mathbb{Z}
, for a prime
p
, where the homogeneous metric coincides with the Hamming metric, the homogeneous MacWilliams identities fail
[
18
,
19
]
. In
[
20
,
21
]
, the rarity of weights that satisfy the MacWilliams identities is observed.
Changing the partition of a code and, more precisely, moving away from enumerating codewords of the same weight, has already been suggested by MacWilliams herself for Lee-metric codes over finite fields
[
22
]
. Similarly, this idea has been supported for Lee-metric codes using association schemes in
[
23
,
24
]
where the existence of MacWilliams-type identities have been shown.
The study of partitioning the ambient space according to a pre-defined property dates back to Zinoviev and Ericson
[
25
]
in 1996. They studied additive codes and analyzed for which enumerators of the code and its dual MacWilliams-type identities are fulfilled, which laid the foundation of
Fourier-reflexive
partitions. In
[
18
]
, Gluessing-Luerssen, studied partitions of additive codes over finite Frobenius rings and showed that MacWilliams identities exist if the partition of the underlying ambient space is Fourier-reflexive. In a follow-up work
[
26
]
, she proved that partitioning a code in the homogeneous metric into the codewords of the same homogeneous weight yields to a failure of the MacWilliams identities. Similarly, for codes in the sum-rank metric,
[
27
]
showed the failure of the classical MacWilliams identities and the existence of MacWilliams-type identities using a finer partition.

In this paper, we focus on codes over a finite chain ring
\mathcal{R}
endowed with an additive weight (i.e., the weight of a codeword is given by the sum of the weights of its entries over
\mathcal{R}
).
For additive weights on codes

\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{n}
, it is natural to consider partitions of the ambient space

\mathcal{R}
that induce symmetrized partitions of

\mathcal{R}^{n}
. MacWilliams-type identities for such symmetrized partition enumerators were studied in
[
18
]
, where it was shown that they hold whenever the underlying partition of
\mathcal{R}
is Fourier-reflexive. Classical examples include the Hamming weight partition, the singleton partition, and partitions induced by the orbits of subgroups of the additive automorphism group.
These examples illustrate a fundamental trade-off. The Hamming weight partition is very coarse and satisfies MacWilliams identities, but it does not allow to recover other additive weight enumerators. At the opposite extreme, the singleton partition is Fourier-reflexive and allows the recovery of all additive weights, but it is maximally fine and becomes computationally infeasible as the alphabet size grows. Orbit partitions arising from isometry groups lie between these two extremes and are guaranteed to be Fourier-reflexive, but they are often still unnecessarily fine.
The central goal of this work is therefore to identify, for a given additive metric, the coarsest symmetrized partition that is Fourier-reflexive and still determines the corresponding weight enumerator. We address this problem for finite chain rings endowed with the Lee metric, the homogeneous metric, and the subfield metric. In particular, we show that for the Lee metric no such intermediate partition exists: the orbit partition induced by the full isometry group is already the coarsest Fourier-reflexive refinement. In contrast, for the homogeneous and subfield metric we construct new, significantly coarser symmetrized partitions that retain MacWilliams-type identities while still allowing the recovery of the respective weight enumerators.
The paper is organized as follows. Section
2
will serve as introduction and recap of the main definitions and results needed throughout the paper. We start with the notion of a linear code over a finite chain ring endowed with an additive weight (where we specifically introduce the Hamming, Lee, homogeneous and subfield weight). We then recap the theory of group characters stating some of their main properties, such as the Schur orthogonality. The section is completed by introducing the MacWilliams identities in their original form together with the Krawtchouk coefficients. Instead of partitioning tuples by weight, in Section
3
we present novel Fourier-reflexive partitions with respect to the Lee, homogeneous and subfield weight.
As a first step we consider a finite chain ring endowed with any additive metric and partition the code into codewords of the same (Lee weight) decomposition which we refer to as the
Lee partition
of the code.
We show that the Lee decomposition enumerator of a code is fully determined by the Lee decomposition enumerator of its dual code, i.e., MacWilliams-type identities exist for any additive weight over any finite chain ring, and, in particular, for codes in the Lee metric. Even though the result holds for any additive metric over a finite chain ring, for some metrics there are coarser and more natural partitions to choose. Thus, in a next step we introduce partitions for the homogeneous weight and the subfield weight, respectively, that are more suited and coarser and still allow for MacWilliams-type identities. To complete the study, we derive linear programming bounds for the Lee, homogeneous and subfield metric in Section
4
. Finally, concluding remarks are given in Section
5
.

Preliminaries

In this section we introduce the main definitions and basic results needed throughout this paper. We denote by
p
a prime number.
Let
\mathcal{R}
be a finite chain ring with maximal ideal generated by
\gamma
, let

be its nilpotency index and

be the size of the residue field, for a positive integer
r
. While the main result holds for any finite chain ring, we often focus on integer residue rings.
We denote by
p
a prime number and for a positive integer
s
we let
\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}

(\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
the set of units modulo

.
Note that a finite integer residue ring
\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}
is a finite chain ring with
q=p
,
\gamma=p,r=1
and nilpotency index
s
.
Given a positive integer
n
and an
\ell
-tuple of nonnegative integers
k:=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell})
satisfying that
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}k_{i}=n

(2)

\displaystyle\binom{n}{k}:=\binom{n}{k_{1},\ldots,k_{\ell}}=\frac{n!}{k_{1}!\ldots k_{\ell}!}

the multinomial coefficient. Moreover, given a statement
A
, we denote by
\mathbbm{1}_{A}
its indicator function, that is,

(3)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}_{A}=\begin{cases}1&A\text{ is true,}\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Furthermore, for a positive integer
m
, we denote by

2.1.
Codes and Additive Weights over Finite Chain Rings

In this section we introduce linear codes over finite chain rings and their main properties. In this paper, we exclusively focus on commutative chain rings in order not to distinguish between left and right ideals.

Definition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a finite chain ring. An
\mathcal{R}
-linear code

\mathcal{C}
of length
n
is an
\mathcal{R}
-submodule of
\mathcal{R}^{n}
. The free module
\mathcal{R}^{n}
is called the
ambient space
of
\mathcal{C}
and the elements of
\mathcal{C}
are called
codewords
.

The fundamental theorem of finite abelian groups implies that any
\mathcal{R}
-module (i.e., any
\mathcal{R}
-linear code
\mathcal{C}
) is isomorphic to the following direct sum of
\mathcal{R}
-modules

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\cong(\mathcal{R}/\gamma^{s}\mathcal{R})^{k_{0}}\times(\mathcal{R}/\gamma^{s-1}\mathcal{R})^{k_{1}}\times\cdots\times(\mathcal{R}/\gamma\mathcal{R})^{k_{s-1}}.

The unique
s
-tuple
(k_{0},k_{1},\ldots,k_{s-1})
is called the
subtype
of
\mathcal{C}
and
k_{0}
is called its
free rank
.

A code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{n}
can be represented by a
generating set
, that is a subset of codewords that generates
\mathcal{C}
as an
\mathcal{R}
-submodule. We call a generating set a
minimal generating set
if it is minimal with respect to inclusion. The cardinality of a minimal generating set of
\mathcal{C}
is called the
rank

K
and can be computed as
K=\sum_{i=0}^{s-1}k_{i}
.

We endow the ambient space
\mathcal{R}^{n}
with the standard inner product, i.e., for
x,y\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
we set

(5)

\displaystyle\left\langle{x,y}\right\rangle:=xy^{\top}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}.

Definition 2
.

Given a linear code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{n}
, the
dual

\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
of
\mathcal{C}
is defined as

(6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}^{\perp}:=\left\{{x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}\;|\;\left\langle{x,c}\right\rangle=0\text{ for every }c\in\mathcal{C}}\right\}.

In ring-linear coding theory, the notion of duality is in general not well-defined. However, in
[
5
]
, Wood proved that the dual of a code over a Frobenius ring, and hence over a finite chain ring, is well-defined. Thus, it holds that
(\mathcal{C}^{\perp})^{\perp}=\mathcal{C}
.
It is straightforward to see that a parity-check matrix
H
of
\mathcal{C}
is a generator matrix of
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
. Moreover, if
\mathcal{C}
is of rank
K
and subtype
(k_{0},\dots,k_{s-1})
then
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
has rank
n-k_{0}
and subtype
(n-K,k_{s-1},\dots,k_{1})
.

One of the main goals of coding theory is to correct errors that occur during transmission in a received message. A linear code is capable of correcting a certain amount of errors which are measured using a given metric induced by a weight function over the ring
\mathcal{R}
.

Definition 3
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
denote a finite chain ring. A
weight
over
\mathcal{R}
is a function
\mathrm{wt}\colon\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{Q}
satisfying

(1)

\mathrm{wt}(0)=0
and
\mathrm{wt}(x)>0
for all
x\neq 0
;

(2)

\mathrm{wt}(x)=\mathrm{wt}(-x)
;

(3)

\mathrm{wt}(x+y)\leq\mathrm{wt}(x)+\mathrm{wt}(y)
.

A weight over a finite chain ring
\mathcal{R}
naturally induces a
distance
which is a function
\mathrm{d}\colon\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{R}\to\mathbb{Q}
defined by
\mathrm{d}(x,y):=\mathrm{wt}(x-y)
.
The coordinate-wise extensions of the weight and distance function will also be denoted by
\mathrm{wt}
and
\mathrm{d}
which we call
(additive) weight
and
(additive) distance
, respectively. In particular, given
x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
, we define

(7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{wt}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathrm{wt}(x_{i}).

It is easy to see that such an additive distance over
\mathcal{R}
satisfies the properties of a metric. We will now introduce the main additive weight functions which are of interest in the course of this paper. Each of the weights over
\mathcal{R}
induce a distance function as described above.
The most prominent weight in coding theory is the Hamming weight.

Definition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a finite chain ring. The
Hamming weight
of an
n
-tuple
x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
is given by the cardinality of the support of
x
, i.e.,

(8)

\displaystyle\wt_{\mathsf{H}}(x):=\left|\,{\left\{{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\;|\;x_{i}\neq 0}\right\}}\,\right|.

We now focus on the case where
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}
is a finite integer residue ring modulo
p^{s}
. Hence, we consider codes to be submodules of
(\mathbb{Z}/p^{s}\mathbb{Z})^{n}
.
The Lee weight, introduced in 1958 by Lee
[
28
]
, is a generalization of the Hamming weight over the binary.

Definition 5
.

The
Lee weight of an element

a\in\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}
is given by

(9)

\displaystyle\wt_{\mathsf{L}}(a):=\min\left\{a,\left|\,{p^{s}-a}\,\right|\right\}.

Similarly, we define the
Lee weight of an
n
-tuple

x\in(\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z})^{n}
additively by

(10)

\displaystyle\wt_{\mathsf{L}}(x):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\wt_{\mathsf{L}}(x_{i}).

The following upper and lower bounds hold trivially for any
a\in\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}
and
x\in(\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z})^{n}

(11)

\displaystyle 0\leq\wt_{\mathsf{L}}(a)\leq\left\lfloor{p^{s}/2}\right\rfloor\quad\text{and}\quad\wt_{\mathsf{H}}(x)\leq\wt_{\mathsf{L}}(x)\leq\left\lfloor{p^{s}/2}\right\rfloor\wt_{\mathsf{H}}(x).

In 1997, Constantinescu and Heise first introduced the homogeneous weight for integer residue rings
[
29
]
, which was later extended to finite rings in
[
30
]
and
[
31
]
. For this we consider again a finite chain ring
\mathcal{R}
with socle
\mathcal{S}.
Recall that the socle
\mathcal{S}
of a ring is defined as the sum of the minimal nonzero submodules. For example, for
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}
we have
\mathcal{S}=\langle p^{s-1}\rangle
. More generally, if the maximal ideal of
\mathcal{R}
is
\langle\gamma\rangle
, we have
\mathcal{S}=\langle\gamma^{s-1}\rangle.

The homogeneous weight extends the Hamming weight coinciding with it for finite fields and with the Lee weight for
\mathbb{Z}/{4}\mathbb{Z}
. In the following we define the homogeneous weight in its normalized form.

Definition 6
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a finite chain ring with residue field of size
q
. The
homogeneous weight
of
a\in\mathcal{R}
is defined as

(12)

\displaystyle\wt_{\mathsf{hom}}(a)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }a=0,\\
1&\text{if }a\not\in\mathcal{S},\\
\frac{q}{q-1}&\text{if }a\in\mathcal{S}\setminus\{0\}.\end{cases}

The homogeneous weight extends additively to tuples
x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
, i.e.,

(13)

\displaystyle\wt_{\mathsf{hom}}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\wt_{\mathsf{hom}}(x_{i}).

Lastly, let us consider finite fields, more precisely finite extension fields
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
. In
[
32
]
, the authors introduced the
subfield metric
over
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
for quantum error correcting codes. This metric is defined similarly to the homogeneous metric over finite chain rings. Namely, it gives weight one to nonzero elements in the underlying base field and all other nonzero elements in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
are of weight
\lambda\geq 1
.

Definition 7
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}
and
\lambda\geq 1
. The
\lambda
-
subfield weight
of
a
is defined as

(14)

\displaystyle\wt_{\lambda}(a)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }a=0,\\
1&\text{if }a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\times},\\
\lambda&\text{if }a\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{p}.\end{cases}

The
\lambda
-subfield weight extends additively to vectors
x\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{r}}^{n}
, i.e.,

(15)

\displaystyle\wt_{\lambda}(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\wt_{\lambda}(x_{i}).

2.2.
Group Characters

We now recall the required basics on group characters. In a nutshell, a group character defines a representation of a group
G
in terms of a complex function. Working over cyclic groups, characters are used in combination with the discrete Fourier transform. Throughout this section, let
\mathbb{C}^{*}\coloneqq\left\{{z\in\mathbb{C}\;|\;\left|\,{z}\,\right|=1}\right\}
.

Definition 8
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group. A
character

\chi
of
G
is a complex-valued map
\chi:G\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{*}
, such that for every
\alpha,\beta\in G
it holds

(16)

\displaystyle\chi(\alpha+\beta)=\chi(\alpha)\chi(\beta).

We denote by
\widehat{G}
the set of all characters of
G
, where

\widehat{G}=\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbb{Z}}(G,\mathbb{C}^{*})

forms a group along with the addition

(17)

\displaystyle(\chi_{i}+\chi_{j})(g)=\chi_{i}(g)\chi_{j}(g),\quad\text{for all }\chi_{i},\chi_{j}\in\widehat{G}\text{ and }g\in G.

This group is called the
character group
of
G
. The identity element of
\widehat{G}
is the
principal character

\chi_{0}
, given by
\chi_{0}(g)=1
for all
g\in G
.
It is well known that
G
and
\widehat{G}
are isomorphic, though not naturally so
[
33
]
. Consequently, we have
|G|=|\widehat{G}|
. Moreover,
G
is naturally isomorphic to the double character group
\widehat{\widehat{G}}
.

Example 9
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a finite chain ring of characteristic
p^{s}
. It is well-known that there exists a subring
\mathcal{T}\subseteq\mathcal{R}
such that
\mathcal{R}
is isomorphic to a direct product of multiple copies of
\mathcal{T}
as abelian groups
[
34
]
. Therefore
\mathcal{R}=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mathcal{T}
, where
\mathcal{T}
is in particular a Galois ring and it is unique up to isomorphism. Given
\xi
a
p^{s}
-th root of unity, for any
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{t}),\ x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{t})\in\mathcal{R}
, a character
\chi_{a}(x)
of
\mathcal{R}
has the form

(18)

\displaystyle\chi_{a}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}\xi^{\trace(a_{i}x_{i})}=\xi^{\trace(a_{1}x_{1}+\dots+a_{t}x_{t})}=\xi^{\trace(\left\langle{a,x}\right\rangle)}\ .

We can derive expressions for characters in
\mathcal{R}^{n}
. Given
a=(a^{(1)},\ldots,a^{(n)}),x=(x^{(1)},\ldots,x^{(n)})\in\mathcal{R}^{n},
where
a^{(i)}=(a^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,a^{(i)}_{t})\in\mathcal{R}
all the characters are of the form

(19)

\displaystyle\chi_{a}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\xi^{\trace\left(\left\langle{a^{(i)},x^{(i)}}\right\rangle\right)}\ ,

where
\xi
is a
p^{s}
-th root of unity.

An important property of characters is the Schur orthogonality. Given a character
\chi
of a group
G
, the Schur orthogonality gives an explicit formula for the sum of the evaluations of
\chi
in every element of the group
G
.

Lemma 10

(Schur Orthogonality over a Group)
.

Let
G
be a finite group and
\chi
a character of
G
. Then it holds that

(20)

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g)=\begin{cases}\left|\,{G}\,\right|&\text{ if }\chi\text{ is the principal character,}\\
0&\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}

Proof.

If
\chi
is a principal character, we have
\chi(g)\equiv 1
for every
g\in G
and therefore
\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g)=\left|\,{G}\,\right|
.
Thus, let us assume that
\chi
is a non-principal character of
G
. This means that there exists an element
h\in G
such that
\chi(h)\neq 1
. Since
G
is a group, we have

(21)

\displaystyle\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g)=\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g+h)=\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g)\chi(h)=\chi(h)\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g),

which is equivalent to
(1-\chi(h))\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g)=0
. As
\chi(h)\neq 1
it must hold that
\sum_{g\in G}\chi(g)=0
.
∎

A similar result exists for subgroups (or linear codes).
However, in that case, we distinguish between characters whose kernel is contained in the given subgroup and those whose kernel is not (see for instance
[
5
]
). More formally, let
H\subset G
be a subgroup of a finite abelian group
G
. Define the
annihilator
of
H\subset G
as

(22)

\displaystyle(\widehat{G}:H):=\left\{{\chi\in\widehat{G}\;|\;\chi(h)=1\text{ for all }h\in H}\right\}.

Definition 11
.

Let
\chi
be a character of
G
and let
H
be a subgroup of
G
. Then
\chi
is said to be
generating for
H
, or simply
generating
, if
\chi\not\in(\widehat{G}:H)
, that is,
H\not\subset\ker(\chi)
.

The orthogonality relation in Lemma
10
extends to a subgroup as follows.

Lemma 12

(Schur Orthogonality over a Subgroup)
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and
H\subset G
be a subgroup. Then the following Schur orthogonality property holds

(23)

\displaystyle\sum_{h\in H}\chi(h)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\chi\text{ is generating for $H$,}\\
\left|\,{H}\,\right|&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

As a consequence of Schur’s Lemma
10
, we highlight two relevant properties of characters that will be extensively used to prove MacWilliams-type identities.

Corollary 13
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian group and let
H
be a subgroup of
G
. If
\chi
is a generating character for
H
(i.e.,
\chi\not\in(\widehat{G}:H)
) it holds that

•

\sum_{x\in H}\chi(x)=0
;

•

\sum_{x\in H\setminus\{0\}}\chi(x)=-1
.

In particular, if
\mathcal{R}
is a finite chain ring and
I
is an ideal in
\mathcal{R}
, for a generating character
\chi
over
\mathcal{R}
we have
\sum_{x\in I}\chi(x)=0
, while
\sum_{x\in I\setminus\{0\}}\chi(x)=-1.

2.3.
Partitions and Induced Partitions

We now turn our attention to partitions of a finite abelian group
G
.
Following
[
35
]
, a partition
\mathcal{P}=P_{1}\mid P_{2}\mid\dots\mid P_{m}
of
G
consists of non-empty pairwise disjoint sets
P_{i}
,
1\leq i\leq m
, that cover
G
. The sets of a given partition are called
blocks
.

Definition 14
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
be two partitions of
G
. The partition
\mathcal{P}
is called
finer
than
\mathcal{Q}
if the blocks of
\mathcal{P}
are contained in the blocks of
\mathcal{Q}
. We also say that
\mathcal{Q}
is
coarser
than
\mathcal{P}
and we write
\mathcal{P}\leq\mathcal{Q}
.

The partition of a group
G
can be extended to a partition of the
n
-fold cartesian product of the group
G
as follows
[
8
,
18
]
.

Definition 15
.

Let
G
be a finite abelian, nontrivial group and consider a partition
\mathcal{P}=P_{1}\;|\;\cdots\;|\;P_{m}
of
G
. For
g,g^{\prime}\in G^{n}
, define the equivalence relation
g\sim g^{\prime}
if
|\left\{{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\;|\;g_{i}\in P_{j}}\right\}|=|\left\{{i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\;|\;g^{\prime}_{i}\in P_{j}}\right\}|
for all
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
. The resulting partition
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym},n}
on
G^{n}
is called the
induced symmetrized partition
.

We now define a partition of the set of characters
\widehat{G}
of a finite abelian group which we refer to as the
dual partition
.

Definition 16
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=P_{1}\mid P_{2}\mid\dots\mid P_{m}
be a partition of a finite abelian group
G
. Let
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}=Q_{1}\mid Q_{2}\mid\dots\mid Q_{\ell}
denote the partition of
\widehat{G}
where
\chi,\chi^{\prime}\in\widehat{G}
lie in the same block
Q_{j}
, for
j\in\{1,\ldots,\ell\}
, if and only if, for every
i=\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
, it holds

(24)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in P_{i}}\chi(x)=\sum_{x\in P_{i}}\chi^{\prime}(x).

We call
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
the
dual partition
of
G
. Furthermore, a partition
\mathcal{P}
is called
self-dual
if
\mathcal{P}=\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
.

In
[
18
]
, Gluesing-Luerssen referred to a partition
\mathcal{P}
as
Fourier-reflexive
if
\widehat{\!\widehat{\mathcal{P}}}=\mathcal{P}
, and showed that a partition
\mathcal{P}
is Fourier-reflexive if and only if
\mathcal{P}
and
\widehat{\mathcal{P}}
consist of the same number of blocks. This definition aligns with the
Fourier-invariant
partitions introduced in
[
25
]
. Additionally,
[
18
, Theorem 3.3]
shows that an induced symmetrized partition
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym},n}
is Fourier-reflexive if
\mathcal{P}
is.

In the following, we focus on self-dual partitions which, by definition, are Fourier-reflexive.

Definition 17
.

Let
\mathcal{P}=P_{1}\mid P_{2}\mid\dots\mid P_{m}
and
\mathcal{Q}=Q_{1}\mid Q_{2}\mid\dots\mid Q_{m}
be two partitions of the finite abelian group
G
. For
a\in Q_{j}
, let
\chi_{a}
be a character of
G
. If the sum
\sum_{x\in P_{i}}\chi_{a}(x)
does not depend on the choice of
a\in Q_{j}
, we define the
Krawtchouk coefficient of
i
with respect to
j
as

(25)

\displaystyle K_{j}(i)=\sum_{x\in P_{i}}\chi_{a}(x)\ \text{ for any }a\in P_{j}\ .

Identifying the group
G
with its character group
\widehat{G}
, the partition
\mathcal{Q}
induces a partition on
\widehat{G}
. Zinoviev and Ericson
[
25
, Lemma 1]
showed that the Krawtchouk coefficients are well-defined if and only if the partitions are Fourier-invariant. Furthermore, the existence of the Krawtchouk coefficients depends on the partition of the group rather than the group itself.
In coding theory, partitions are often induced by a weight function on the ambient space. Given a finite chain ring
\mathcal{R}
and an additive weight
\wt
on
\mathcal{R}^{n}
, we can partition
\mathcal{R}^{n}
into tuples of the same weight. That is, we consider a partition

\mathcal{P}^{\wt}=P^{\wt}_{0}\;|\;\cdots\;|\;P^{\wt}_{\left|\,{\image(\wt)}\,\right|-1}
where

(26)

\displaystyle P^{\wt}_{i}\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}\;|\;\wt(x)=i\}.

2.4.
MacWilliams Identities over Finite Chain Rings

We finally recap the MacWilliams identities for the Hamming metric and the non-existence results in the Lee, homogeneous in this section.

Again, let
\mathcal{R}
be a finite chain ring and let
\mathrm{wt}\colon\mathcal{R}^{n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Q}
be an additive weight over
\mathcal{R}
. Furthermore, we consider a linear code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{n}
. From now on, we will denote by
M_{\mathrm{wt}}
the maximum value of
\mathrm{wt}
on
\mathcal{R}
.
The
\mathrm{wt}
-weight distribution
of
\mathcal{C}
is a tuple which specifies the number of codewords of each possible weight
i\in\left\{{0,\ldots,nM_{\wt}}\right\}
, i.e.,

(27)

\displaystyle A^{\wt}(\mathcal{C}):=\big(A_{0}^{\mathrm{wt}}(\mathcal{C}),A_{1}^{\mathrm{wt}}(\mathcal{C}),\dots,A_{nM_{\mathrm{wt}}}^{\mathrm{wt}}(\mathcal{C})\big),

where
A_{i}^{\mathrm{wt}}(\mathcal{C}):=\left|\,{\left\{{c\in\mathcal{C}\;|\;\wt(c)=i}\right\}}\,\right|
. If
\mathrm{wt}
and
\mathcal{C}
are clear from the context, we will simply write
A_{i}
instead of
A_{i}^{\mathrm{wt}}(\mathcal{C})
. Moreover, the weight distribution determines the
\mathrm{wt}
-
weight enumerator
of
\mathcal{C}
defined as

(28)

\displaystyle\mathsf{wwe}_{\mathcal{C}}=\sum_{i=0}^{nM_{\mathrm{wt}}}A_{i}^{\mathrm{wt}}(\mathcal{C})X^{nM_{\mathrm{wt}}-i}Y^{i}\ .

That is, for instance, the Lee weight enumerator of a code
\mathcal{C}
is denoted by
\mathsf{Lwe}_{\mathcal{C}}
, the homogeneous weight enumerator by
\mathsf{Homwe}_{\mathcal{C}}
and the subfield weight enumerator by
\lambda\mathsf{we}_{\mathcal{C}}
.
Although the weight distribution does not entirely characterize a code, it provides significant information. The research on this topic dates back to MacWilliams’ work
[
1
]
, when she proved the famous
MacWilliams identities
(
29
). The identities establish a relation between the Hamming weight enumerators of a linear code over a finite field and its dual.

Theorem 18

(MacWilliams Identities
[
1
, Lemma 2.2]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be a linear code over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and let
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
be its dual code. Let
(A_{i})_{i=0,\dots,n}
and
(B_{i})_{i=0,\dots,n}
be the Hamming weight distributions of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
, respectively. For any
0\leq\nu\leq n
it holds

(29)

\sum_{j=0}^{n-\nu}\binom{n-j}{\nu}A_{j}=q^{n-\nu}\sum_{j=0}^{\nu}\binom{n-j}{n-\nu}B_{j}\ .

There are several techniques to prove the MacWilliams identities each resulting in a distinct set of equations, but equivalent to the MacWilliams identities. One of the most widely used methods involves the Poisson summation formula
[
36
]
.
On the other hand, Delsarte proposed an interesting approach based on association schemes and Krawtchouk coefficients
[
37
]
.

Theorem 19
.

Let
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
be a linear code and let
(A_{i})_{i=0,\dots,n}
and
(B_{i})_{i=0,\dots,n}
be the Hamming weight distributions of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
, respectively. For any
0\leq j\leq n
,

(30)

\displaystyle B_{j}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{i=0}^{n}A_{i}K_{j}(i),

where the
Krawtchouk
coefficients are given by
K_{j}(i)=\sum_{k=0}^{j}(-1)^{k}(q-1)^{j-k}\binom{i}{k}\binom{n-i}{j-k}
.

For the partition of
(\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z})^{n}
into tuples of given Lee weight, however, the MacWilliams identities fail, whenever
p^{s}\geq 5
.

Theorem 20

(
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

For any
p^{s}\geq 5
there exist linear codes
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
over
\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}
satisfying
\mathsf{Lwe}_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}=\mathsf{Lwe}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}
and
\mathsf{Lwe}_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}_{1}}\neq\mathsf{Lwe}_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}_{2}}
.

Note that Theorem
20
has been shown for any
\mathbb{Z}/{m}\mathbb{Z}
with
m\geq 5
.
A similar result holds for the homogeneous weight as well.

Theorem 21

(
[
19
, Theorem 3.2]
)
.

For any prime number
p
and any positive integer
s\geq 2
with
p^{s}>5
, there exist linear codes
\mathcal{C}_{1},\mathcal{C}_{2}
over
\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}
satisfying that
\mathsf{Homwe}_{\mathcal{C}_{1}}=\mathsf{Homwe}_{\mathcal{C}_{2}}
and
\mathsf{Homwe}_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}_{1}}\neq\mathsf{Homwe}_{\mathcal{C}^{\perp}_{2}}
.

Both, the Lee and homogeneous weight enumerator, can be interpreted as symmetrized weight partitions on
\mathcal{R}^{n}
induced by the weight partition on
\mathcal{R}
and are not Fourier-reflexive. This motivates the need to consider a different partition of the ambient space.

3.
Partition-based MacWilliams Identities

As discussed in the previous section, the MacWilliams identities generally fail for some weight enumerators like, for instance, the Lee weight enumerator and the homogeneous weight enumerator (see Theorems
20
and
21
, respectively). However, using partitions different from the weight partition yields MacWilliams-
type
identities provided that the partition is Fourier-reflexive, as established in
[
18
]
. In this section we make use of these results and present for different additive metrics over
\mathcal{R}
each a symmetrized partition that is Fourier-reflexive (and hence satisfies the MacWilliams-type identities) and allows to retrieve the weight enumerator in the metric considered. Additionally, we show that the partitions presented for each metric are the coarsest such partition. Hence, we refer to such a partition as the
coarsest Fourier-reflexive symmetrized partition
with respect to the metric considered.

We start by introducing the set-up that we use throughout this section for all metrics. Let
\mathcal{R}
denote a finite chain ring and
\wt
a weight function over
\mathcal{R}
extending additively to

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym},n}=P^{\wt}_{\pi^{(0)}}\;|\;\cdots\;|\;P^{\wt}_{\pi^{(D-1)}}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\wt}(\mathcal{C}):=\left(\mathcal{D}^{\wt}_{\pi^{(0)}}(\mathcal{C}),\ldots,\mathcal{D}^{\wt}_{\pi^{(D-1)}}(\mathcal{C})\right).

If the code

In the following subsections we focus on the Lee, homogeneous and subfield metric. For each of them, we define an induced symmetrized partition using the above notation and show that this partition is Fourier-reflexive and allows to recover the weight enumerator.

3.1.
Symmetrized Lee Partition for Additive Weights

Although the MacWilliams identities for the symmetrized Lee partition enumerator are well-established
[
23
,
18
,
22
]
, in this section we propose an alternative, yet equivalent, formulation of the MacWilliams-type identities for the Lee decomposition enumerator presented in
[
18
, Theorem 3.5]
. Specifically, we explicitly calculate the Krawtchouk coefficients, providing a set of linear equations that enable us to recover the Lee decomposition enumerator as a function of the Lee decomposition enumerator of its dual, for any linear code over any finite chain ring and any additive weight.

In this section, if the characteristic of the ring is different from 2, we label the
q^{s}
elements of
\mathcal{R}
as follows:

(38)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}=\{\alpha_{0}=0,\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{M},\alpha_{M+1},\dots,\alpha_{q^{s}-1}\},

where
M\coloneqq\left\lfloor{q^{s}/2}\right\rfloor
and
\alpha_{q^{s}-i}
represents the additive inverse of
\alpha_{i}
, i.e.,
\alpha_{q^{s}-i}=-\alpha_{i}
. We refer to
\alpha_{q^{s}-i}
as the
opposite
of
\alpha_{i}
. To enable the recovery of the Lee weight enumerator, we introduce the Lee partition of
\mathcal{R}
, in which each block consists of an element and its opposite.

Definition 22
.

Given a finite chain ring
\mathcal{R}
of characteristic greater than 2, for any
0\leq i\leq M
we set
I^{\mathsf{L}}_{i}\coloneqq\{\alpha_{i},-\alpha_{i}\}
to be the subset of
\mathcal{R}
containing
\alpha_{i}
and its opposite. We then define the
Lee partition
of
\mathcal{R}
as

(39)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{L}}=I^{\mathsf{L}}_{0}\;|\;I^{\mathsf{L}}_{1}\;|\;\cdots\;|\;I^{\mathsf{L}}_{M}\ .

If the characteristic of
\mathcal{R}
is 2, then
\left|\,{\mathcal{R}}\,\right|=2^{r}
and every element is its own additive inverse, meaning
\alpha_{i}=-\alpha_{i}
for all
i
. In this case, there is no need for relabeling, and the Lee partition

(40)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{L}}=I^{\mathsf{L}}_{0}\;|\;I^{\mathsf{L}}_{1}\;|\;\cdots\;|\;I^{\mathsf{L}}_{2^{r}}\ .

consists of the singletons, that is
I_{i}^{\mathsf{L}}=\left\{{\alpha_{i}}\right\}
.

Following the notation introduced at the beginning of the section, we denote by
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
the induced symmetrized Lee partition of
\mathcal{R}^{n}
.

Proposition 23
.

The symmetrized Lee partition
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
is Fourier-reflexive.

Proof.

According to
[
18
, Theorem 3.3]
, if contains
\{0\}
as a block and
\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{L}}
is Fourier-reflexive, then
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
is Fourier-reflexive. Therefore, it suffices to prove that
\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{L}}
is Fourier-reflexive.
If the characteristic of the ring is greater than 2, the Lee partition can be interpreted as the partition induced by the orbits of the action of the group
\{\pm 1\}
on
\mathcal{R}
. It is shown in
[
18
, Theorem 2.6]
that any partition of a finite group induced by the orbits of a subgroup of its automorphism group is Fourier-reflexive. On the other hand, if the characteristic of the ring is 2, then the Lee partition consists of singleton blocks, which is clearly a Fourier-reflexive partition
[
5
,
18
]
. Hence, the claim follows.
∎

Corollary 24

(MacWilliams-type Identities for the Symmetrized Lee Weight Enumerator)
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a finite chain ring, with residue field size
q=p^{r}
and nilpotency index
s
. For a positive integer
n
, consider a linear code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{n}
and let
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
denote its dual. For a given Lee decomposition
\rho\in\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}
, the number of codewords in the dual
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
of Lee decomposition
\rho
is given by

(41)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{C}^{\perp})=\frac{1}{\left|\,{\mathcal{C}}\,\right|}\sum_{\pi\in\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}}K^{\mathsf{L}}_{\pi}(\rho)\mathcal{D}_{\pi}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{C}).

Here,
K^{\mathsf{L}}_{\pi}(\rho)
denotes the value of the Krawtchouk coefficient of
\rho
with respect to
\pi
and, for a given
p^{s}
-th root of unity
\xi
, it is given by

(42)

\displaystyle K^{\mathsf{L}}_{\pi}(\rho)\coloneqq\sum_{t\in\mathrm{Comp}^{\mathsf{L}}_{\pi}(\rho)}\Bigg(\prod_{i=0}^{M}\binom{\pi_{i}}{t_{i0},\ldots,t_{iM}}\kappa^{\mathsf{L}}\Bigg),

where

(43)

\displaystyle\kappa^{\mathsf{L}}=\begin{cases}\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}\left(\xi^{-\trace(\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j})}+\xi^{\trace(\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j})}\right)^{t_{ij}}\xi^{\trace(\alpha_{i}\alpha_{M})}&\text{if }p=2,\\
\prod_{j=1}^{M}\left(\xi^{-\trace(\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j})}+\xi^{\trace(\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j})}\right)^{t_{ij}}&\text{otherwise.}\\
\end{cases}

For any
k\in\left\{{0,\dots,M}\right\}
,
\alpha_{k}
denotes any element in
I_{k}^{\mathsf{L}}
and
\binom{\pi_{i}}{t_{i0},\ldots,t_{iM}}
denotes the multinomial coefficient.

Proof.

Note that, for
a\in P^{\mathsf{L}}_{\pi}
, the sum
\sum_{x\in P^{\mathsf{L}}_{\rho}}\chi_{a}(x)
runs over all
x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
with given Lee decomposition
\rho
and is only dependent on the Lee decomposition, but not on the composition of the tuples themselves. Therefore, without loss of generality, we assume that the first
\pi_{0}
entries of
a
have weight
0
, the next
\pi_{1}
entries equal to
\pm\alpha_{1}
. We then find
\pi_{2}
entries equals to
\pm\alpha_{2}
and so on.
In the first
\pi_{0}
positions, an
n
-tuple
x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
of Lee decomposition
\rho
can have
t_{0j}<\rho_{j}
positions lying in
j\in\left\{{0\dots,M}\right\}
, where
\sum_{j=0}^{M}t_{0j}=\pi_{0}
. Repeating the argument iteratively for each
i\in\left\{{0,\dots,M}\right\}
, we deduce that the number of
x
satisfying these conditions is then given by the product of multinomial coefficients

(44)

\displaystyle\prod_{i=0}^{M}\binom{\pi_{i}}{t_{i0},\ldots,t_{iM}}.

The formula follows from the values of the Krawtchouk coefficients with the multiplicities provided by the composition
t\in\mathrm{Comp}^{\mathsf{hom}}_{\pi}(\rho)
.
∎

In particular, if
\mathcal{R}=\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}
, i.e.,
r=1
, for all
i\in\left\{{0,\dots,M}\right\}
, the sets
I_{i}^{\mathsf{L}}
reduce to
I_{i}^{\mathsf{L}}=\left\{{\pm i}\right\}
,
\trace=\mathrm{id}
, and MacWilliams-type identities for the Lee metric codes hold. In this case, we obtain,

(45)

\displaystyle\kappa^{\mathsf{L}}=\begin{cases}\prod_{j=1}^{M-1}\left(\xi^{-ij}+\xi^{ij}\right)^{t_{ij}}\xi^{iM}&\!\!\!\!\text{if }p=2,\\
\prod_{j=1}^{M}\left(\xi^{-ij}+\xi^{ij}\right)^{t_{ij}}&\!\!\!\!\text{else.}\\
\end{cases}

A concrete example to illustrate the calculations of the identities for Lee-metric codes
\mathcal{C}\subseteq(\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z})^{n}
is given in
A.1
.

Compared to other partitions (like, for instance, partitioning a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{n}
by the codewords of the same weight), the partition into codewords of the same Lee decomposition is rather fine.
The finer a partition is, the more complex the enumerator and the identities become.

Lemma 25
.

Given a finite chain ring
\mathcal{R}
and a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{n}
.
The symmetrized Lee partition is the coarsest symmetrized Fourier-reflexive partition that allows retrieving the Lee weight enumerator of
\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{sym},n}
be a symmetrized, Fourier-reflexive partition of
\mathcal{R}^{n}
induced by
\mathcal{Q}=J_{0}\;|\;\cdots\;|\;J_{N}
of
\mathcal{R}
. Assume
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{sym},n}
is coarser than
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
. That is, its number of blocks is strictly smaller than the number of blocks of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{sym},n}^{\mathsf{L}}
. This also implies that
\mathcal{Q}
is coarser than
\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{L}}
, i.e.,
N<M
. Therefore, there exists a block
J_{i}
of
\mathcal{Q}
whose elements do not all share the same Lee weight. Hence, by definition of the symmetrized weight, there exist
x,\tilde{x}\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
with
\wt_{\mathsf{L}}(x)\neq\wt_{\mathsf{L}}(\tilde{x})
but both contained in one block of
\mathcal{Q}_{\text{sym},n}
, which makes it impossible to recover the Lee weight enumerator.
∎

Note that the Lee partition is always finer than the partition induced by any weight
\wt
on
\mathcal{R}
. This is because, for any weight
\wt
, the weight of each element must be equal to the weight of its opposite. Consequently, the blocks of the partition of
\mathcal{R}^{n}
induced by
\wt
contain the blocks of the Lee partition.
Thus, for an
n
-tuple
x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
we can compute its weight
\wt(x)
using its Lee decomposition
\pi^{\mathsf{L}}(x)
, i.e.,

(46)

\displaystyle\wt(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{M}\pi^{\mathsf{L}}_{i}(x)\wt(i).

Hence, the MacWilliams identities for the symmetrized Lee partition are valid for any additive weight over a finite chain ring
\mathcal{R}
. However, the symmetrized Lee partition is not always the most practical partition to choose. Indeed, for other metrics, there exist coarser partitions of the ambient space that still allow for MacWilliams-type identities and allow us to recover the weight enumerator of the code.

3.2.
Symmetrized Homogeneous-Unit Partition

We now focus on the homogeneous metric over
\mathcal{R}
, where we assume that
\mathcal{R}
has nilpotency index
s>1
. In fact, when
s=1
,
\mathcal{R}
is a finite field, and the homogeneous metric reduces to the Hamming metric for which MacWilliams identities exist.

Similar to the Lee metric,
n
-tuples with a given homogeneous weight can be decomposed in different ways.
A natural choice is the
symmetrized homogeneous weight partition
induced by the homogeneous weight partition on
\mathcal{R}
, classifying the elements
x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}
of given homogeneous weight.
That is, we partition
\mathcal{R}
into blocks containing all elements of the same homogeneous weight, i.e.,

(47)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{\hom}=Q_{0}\;|\;Q_{1}\;|\;Q_{\lambda}\;\text{ where }\;Q_{i}=\left\{{a\in\mathcal{R}\;|\;\wt_{\mathsf{hom}}(a)=i}\right\}.

Hence, we can define the

\mathcal{Q}^{\hom}_{\mathrm{sym},n}
of a code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{n}
, partitioning
\mathcal{C}
into the codewords of the same homogeneous decomposition.
However, this partition does not lead to well-defined Krawtchouk coefficients and hence the MacWilliams identities fail
[

]

One main reason is that, given an element

of homogeneous weight
1
and a
p^{s}

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathcal{R}\\
\wt_{\mathsf{hom}}(x)=1\end{subarray}}\xi^{\trace(ax)}

is not independent on

In fact, its value depends on whether

are units or zero divisors. Therefore, we refine it by distinguishing between zero components, units, nonzero components lying in the socle
\mathcal{S}=\left\langle{\gamma^{s-1}}\right\rangle
of
\mathcal{R}
, and components lying in
\left\langle{\gamma}\right\rangle
but not in
\mathcal{S}
, i.e., we define the sets

\displaystyle Z:=\left\{{0}\right\},\;U:=\mathcal{R}^{\times},\;S:=\mathcal{S}\setminus\left\{{0}\right\},\;

\displaystyle\text{and}\;R:=\mathcal{R}\setminus\left(Z\cup U\cup S\right)=\langle\gamma\rangle\setminus\langle\gamma^{s-1}\rangle.

We observe that the cardinalities of the sets
Z,U,S
and

(49)

\displaystyle\left|\,{Z}\,\right|=1,\;\left|\,{U}\,\right|=q^{s-1}(q-1),\;\left|\,{S}\,\right|=q-1,\;\text{and}\;\left|\,{R}\,\right|=q^{s-1}-q.

This immediately partitions
\mathcal{R}
into these four sets. We call this partition of

the

and denote it by

(50)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{hom}}:=Z\;|\;U\;|\;S\;|\;R.

Let

denote the induced

, where
\pi^{(i)}\in\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{hom}}_{n}
, for every

Furthermore, note that every

While some Fourier-reflexive partitions over
\mathcal{R}
naturally arise from a partition of orbits of a subgroup of its automorphism group (e.g., the symmetrized Lee weight partition), this is not generally true for the homogeneous-unit partition
\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{hom}}

\mathrm{Aut}^{\hom}(\mathcal{R})=U^{n}\rtimes S_{n}

Proposition 26
.

The homogeneous-unit partition
\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{hom}}
can be seen as a partition given by the orbits of a subgroup

if and only if
s\in\left\{{1,2,3}\right\}

Proof.

In the case where

coincides with the Hamming weight partition (that is, if

s\in\left\{{2,3}\right\}

\mathcal{P}^{\mathsf{hom}}

\mathcal{R}

However, if

\displaystyle R=\left(\left\langle{p}\right\rangle\cup\cdots\cup\left\langle{p^{s-2}}\right\rangle\right)\setminus\left\langle{p^{s-1}}\right\rangle.

If, by contradiction,

cannot be represented as the partition of orbits.
∎

4.
Linear Programming Bound

One of the tightest bounds for the size of linear codes is given by the Linear Programming (LP) bound. Such bounds have been introduced by Delsarte
[
13
]
and usually stem from association schemes. However, given the MacWilliams identities, one can easily derive an LP bound. For the homogeneous and the subfield metric no such LP bound has been stated. An exception is the Lee metric, where an association scheme using the Lee decomposition has been employed to derive a Lee-metric LP bound
[
23
,
24
]
. For the homogeneous metric over a Frobenius ring a different approach to derive an LP bound has been presented in
[
8
]
.
In this Section, we derive LP bounds stemming from the MacWilliams identities presented in Section
3
.

Given a finite chain ring
\mathcal{R}
, an additive weight
\mathrm{wt}:\mathcal{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{Q}
and an
\mathcal{R}
-linear code
\mathcal{C}
, recall the definition of the
decomposition enumerator
of
\mathcal{C}

(78)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\pi}^{\wt}(\mathcal{C})=\left|\,{\left\{{c\in\mathcal{C}\;|\;\pi^{\wt}(c)=\pi}\right\}}\,\right|

with respect to the weight
\wt
. Moreover, if
\left|\,{\mathbb{D}^{\wt}_{n}}\,\right|=D
, we denote by
\pi^{(0)}=(n,0,\dots,0)
the decomposition of the all-zero vector, and all the other decompositions in
\mathbb{D}^{\wt}_{n}
by
\pi^{(1)},\dots,\pi^{(D-1)}
.

The MacWilliams-type identities for the Lee decomposition enumerator, given in Corollary
24
, allow us to straightforwardly derive an LP bound for any
\mathcal{R}
-linear code and any additive weight. Moreover, the MacWilliams-type identities given in
Corollary

28
and
Corollary

33
allow to derive an LP bound for the homogeneous and subfield metric respectively, which is less complex (i.e., that involves fewer variables).

From now on,
B_{\mathcal{R}}(n,\dist_{\wt})
denotes the maximum number of codewords of an
\mathcal{R}
-linear code of length
n
and minimum
\mathrm{wt}
-distance
\dist_{\wt}
.

Theorem 35

(Linear Programming Bound)
.

For a linear code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{R}^{n}
of minimum distance
\dist_{\wt}
it holds that

(79)

\displaystyle B_{\mathcal{R}}(n,\dist_{\wt})\leq\max\left(\sum_{i=0}^{D-1}\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(i)}}^{\wt}(\mathcal{C})\right),

where the maximum is taken over all
\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(i)}}^{\wt}(\mathcal{C})
subject to the following conditions:

•

\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(0)}}^{\wt}(\mathcal{C})=1
;

•

\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(i)}}^{\wt}(\mathcal{C})\geq 0
for all
1\leq i\leq D-1
and
\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(i)}}^{\wt}(\mathcal{C})=0
for all
i\in I
;

•

\sum_{i=1}^{D-1}\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(i)}}^{\wt}(\mathcal{C})K_{\pi^{(j)}}(\pi^{(i)})\geq 0
,

where
I=\{i\ \;|\;\ x\in\mathcal{R}^{n}\text{ such that }\pi(x)=\pi^{(i)}\text{ and }\mathrm{wt}(x)<\dist_{\wt}\}
.

If
\wt
is an additive weight of
\mathcal{R}^{n}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\pi}^{\wt}(\mathcal{C})
is the Lee partition enumerator, the LP bound requires a large number of variables. Thus, the program cannot run efficiently and is not computationally feasible.

4.1.
Lee metric

The Lee scheme, introduced in
[
23
]
and investigated in
[

,

,

Theorem

Throughout this section, let
\mathcal{R}=(\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z})^{n}

Given a
\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}

of length

denote a codeword. In the Hamming metric, whenever we multiply each component of

\displaystyle\pi^{\mathsf{L}}(r\cdot c)=(\pi^{\mathsf{L}}_{0},\pi^{\mathsf{L}}_{{\sigma_{r}}(1)},\dots,\pi^{\mathsf{L}}_{\sigma_{r}(M-1)})=:\sigma(\pi^{\mathsf{L}}(r\cdot c)),

\sigma_{r}(i)=k
such that

Lemma 36

(
[
40
, Lemma 1]
)
.

Given a Lee decomposition
\pi\in\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}
, a unit element

For any Lee decomposition

(81)

\displaystyle S(\pi):=\left\{{\sigma_{r}(\pi)\;|\;r\in(\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z})^{\times},\ \sigma_{r}(i)=k\text{ with }kr\equiv i\pmod{p^{s}}}\right\}.

Since

, an additional constraint arises in the formulation of the LP problem for linear Lee-metric codes.

Let

denote the maximum number of codewords in a linear code of length

and minimum Lee-distance
\dist_{\mathsf{L}}
over
\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}
.

Theorem 37

(Linear Programming Bound for Lee-metric Codes)
.

For a linear code
\mathcal{C}\subseteq(\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z})^{n}
of minimum Lee distance

it holds that

(82)

\displaystyle B_{\mathbb{Z}/{p^{s}}\mathbb{Z}}(n,\dist_{\mathsf{L}})\leq\max\left(\sum_{i=0}^{D-1}\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(i)}}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{C})\right),

where the maximum is taken over all
\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(i)}}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{C})
subject to the following conditions:

•

\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(0)}}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{C})=1
;

•

\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(i)}}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{C})\geq 0
for all

and
\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(i)}}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{C})=0
for all

•

\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(i)}}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{C})=\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(j)}}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{C})
for all
\pi^{(j)}\in S(\pi^{(i)})
;

•

and

(83)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{D-1}\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(i)}}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{C})K^{\mathsf{L}}_{\pi^{(j)}}(\pi^{(i)})\geq\begin{cases}-\binom{n}{\pi^{(i)}_{1},\ldots,\pi^{(i)}_{M}}2^{n-\pi^{(i)}_{0}-\pi^{(i)}_{M}}&\text{if }p=2,\\

\pi^{(0)}

.

However, note that this formulation of the LP problem still does not result in an efficient program due to the high number of variables involved.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated MacWilliams-type identities for codes over finite chain rings equipped with additive metrics. Our approach is based on studying partitions of the ambient space that refine the corresponding weight partitions while remaining Fourier-reflexive. For the Lee metric, we showed that the partition induced by the action of the full group of linear isometries is already the coarsest Fourier-reflexive refinement of the weight partition. In contrast, for the homogeneous and subfield metric, we identified new symmetrized partitions that are significantly coarser than the orbit partitions induced by subgroups of the linear isometry group, yet remain Fourier-reflexive. These partitions are, moreover, the coarsest symmetrized partitions for which MacWilliams-type identities hold. The presented identities can be used to formulate LP bounds in each of the metrics.

In this work we laid the main focus on the Lee, homogeneous and subfield metric. Even though the MacWilliams-type identities for the Lee partition enumerator allows to derive the weight enumerator of any additive weight over any finite chain ring, for specific weights there are more natural choices on how to partition the code. Future work, therefore, includes the study of other weights, such as the Bachoc weight
[
41
]
, Krotov weight
[
42
,
43
]
or the overweight
[
44
]
.

A.2.
Homogeneous Weight-Unit Partition

Let us now consider an example for the MacWilliams-type identities for the homogeneous weight-unit partition stated in Theorem
21
.

Example 39
.

Given
q=2
and
s=4
, consider the code
\mathcal{C}\subset(\mathbb{Z}/{16}\mathbb{Z})^{3}
generated by
G=\begin{pmatrix}8&1&8\end{pmatrix}
. Notice, that there are four distinct homogeneous weight-unit decompositions occurring in
\mathcal{C}
, namely,

\displaystyle\pi^{(0)}=(3,0,0,0),\quad\pi^{(1)}=(2,0,0,1),\quad\pi^{(2)}=(2,0,1,0),\quad\pi^{(3)}=(0,1,2,0),

with the corresponding homogeneous weight-unit enumerators given by

(98)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(0)}}^{\mathsf{hom}}(\mathcal{C})=1,\quad\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(1)}}^{\mathsf{hom}}(\mathcal{C})=6,\quad\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(2)}}^{\mathsf{hom}}(\mathcal{C})=1,\quad\mathcal{D}_{\pi^{(3)}}^{\mathsf{hom}}(\mathcal{C})=8.

Note that the dual code of
\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
, is generated by

(99)

\displaystyle G^{\perp}=\begin{pmatrix}1&8&0\\
0&8&1\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\rho=(1,1,1,0)\in\mathbb{D}_{3}^{\mathsf{hom}}
be a homogeneous weight-unit decomposition in
(\mathbb{Z}/{16}\mathbb{Z})^{3}
. By calculating the codewords of
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
, we observe that
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{hom}}(\mathcal{C}^{\perp})=16
.
For every
\pi\in\left\{{\pi^{(0)},\pi^{(1)},\pi^{(2)},\pi^{(3)}}\right\}
let us now compute the Krawtchouk coefficient
K^{\mathsf{hom}}_{\pi}(\rho)
as stated in Theorem
28
.

Let us start by choosing
\pi=\pi^{(0)}
. Notice that there is only one composition
\rho
with respect to
\pi^{(0)}
, i.e.,

(100)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Comp}^{\mathsf{hom}}_{\pi^{(0)}}(\rho)=\Big\{(\underbrace{1,1,1,0}_{t_{Z}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{t_{U}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{t_{S}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{t_{R}})\Big\}.

Hence, we obtain

(101)

\displaystyle K^{\mathsf{hom}}_{\pi^{(0)}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in\left\{{Z,U,S,R}\right\}}\binom{\pi_{i}}{t_{iZ},\ldots,t_{iR}}=\binom{\pi_{Z}}{t_{Z}}(q^{s-1}(q-1))^{t_{ZU}}

(102)

\displaystyle=\binom{3}{1,1,1,0}8=48.

Now, let
\pi=\pi^{(1)}
.
Note that the set of compositions
\mathrm{Comp}^{\mathsf{hom}}_{\pi}(\rho)
of
\rho
with respect to
\pi
consists of only three elements, i.e.,

(103)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Comp}^{\mathsf{hom}}_{\pi^{(1)}}(\rho)=\Big\{

\displaystyle t=(\underbrace{1,1,0,0}_{t_{Z}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{t_{U}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{t_{S}},\underbrace{0,0,1,0}_{t_{R}}),

(104)

\displaystyle s=(\underbrace{1,0,1,0}_{s_{Z}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{s_{U}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{s_{S}},\underbrace{0,1,0,0}_{s_{R}})

(105)

\displaystyle v=(\underbrace{0,1,1,0}_{v_{Z}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{v_{U}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{v_{S}},\underbrace{1,0,0,0}_{s_{R}})\Big\}.

Recalling that
q-1=1
and omitting all the powers of
1
, we obtain

(106)

\displaystyle K^{\mathsf{hom}}_{\pi^{(1)}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\binom{\pi_{Z}}{t_{Z}}\binom{\pi_{R}}{t_{R}}\left(q^{s-1}\right)^{t_{ZU}}+\binom{\pi_{Z}}{v_{Z}}\binom{\pi_{R}}{v_{R}}(q^{s-1})^{v_{ZU}}

(107)

\displaystyle=\binom{2}{1,1,0,0}\binom{1}{0,0,1,0}8+\binom{2}{0,1,1,0}\binom{1}{1,0,0,0}8

(108)

\displaystyle=32.

Moreover, if
\pi=\pi^{(2)}
, we have

(109)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Comp}^{\mathsf{hom}}_{\pi^{(2)}}(\rho)=\Big\{

\displaystyle t=(\underbrace{1,1,0,0}_{t_{Z}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{t_{U}},\underbrace{0,0,1,0}_{t_{S}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{t_{R}}),

(110)

\displaystyle s=(\underbrace{1,0,1,0}_{s_{Z}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{s_{U}},\underbrace{0,1,0,0}_{s_{S}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{s_{R}})

(111)

\displaystyle v=(\underbrace{0,1,1,0}_{v_{Z}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{v_{U}},\underbrace{1,0,0,0}_{v_{S}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{s_{R}})\Big\}.

It follows that

(112)

\displaystyle K^{\mathsf{hom}}_{\pi^{(2)}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\binom{\pi_{Z}}{t_{Z}}\binom{\pi_{S}}{t_{S}}\left(q^{s-1}\right)^{t_{ZU}}+\binom{\pi_{Z}}{s_{Z}}\binom{\pi_{S}}{s_{S}}(-1)^{s_{SU}}+\binom{\pi_{Z}}{v_{Z}}\binom{\pi_{S}}{v_{S}}(q^{s-1})^{v_{ZU}}

(113)

\displaystyle=\binom{2}{1,1,0,0}\binom{1}{0,0,1,0}8+\binom{2}{1,0,1,0}\binom{1}{0,1,0,0}(-8)+\binom{2}{0,1,1,0}\binom{1}{1,0,0,0}8

(114)

\displaystyle=16.

Lastly, for
\pi^{(3)}
, we have

(115)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Comp}^{\mathsf{hom}}_{\pi^{(2)}}(\rho)=\Big\{

\displaystyle t=(\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{t_{Z}},\underbrace{1,0,0,0}_{t_{U}},\underbrace{0,1,1,0}_{t_{S}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{t_{R}}),

(116)

\displaystyle s=(\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{s_{Z}},\underbrace{0,1,0,0}_{s_{U}},\underbrace{1,0,1,0}_{s_{S}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{s_{R}})

(117)

\displaystyle v=(\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{v_{Z}},\underbrace{0,0,1,0}_{v_{U}},\underbrace{1,1,0,0}_{v_{S}},\underbrace{0,0,0,0}_{s_{R}})\Big\},

which yields to

(118)

\displaystyle K^{\mathsf{hom}}_{\pi^{(2)}}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\binom{\pi_{U}}{t_{U}}\binom{\pi_{S}}{t_{S}}\left(-q^{s-1}\right)^{t_{SU}}+\binom{\pi_{U}}{s_{U}}\binom{\pi_{S}}{s_{S}}0^{s_{UU}}+\binom{\pi_{U}}{v_{U}}\binom{\pi_{S}}{v_{S}}(-1)^{v_{US}}(-q^{s-1})^{v_{ZU}}

(119)

\displaystyle=\binom{1}{1,0,0,0}\binom{2}{0,1,1,0}(-8)+\binom{1}{0,0,1,0}\binom{2}{1,1,0,0}8

(120)

\displaystyle=0.

Finally, inserting these values in Equation (
62
), yields

(121)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\left|\,{\mathcal{C}}\,\right|}\sum_{\pi\in\mathbb{D}^{\mathsf{L}}_{n}}K^{\mathsf{L}}_{\pi}(\rho)\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{L}}(\mathcal{C})=\frac{1}{16}(48\cdot 1+6\cdot 32+1\cdot 16+0\cdot 8)=16,

which corresponds with
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{\mathsf{hom}}(\mathcal{C}^{\perp})
.

A.3.
Subfield-Trace Partition

Lastly, we give an example to show the calculations of the MacWilliams-type identities of Corollary
33
.

Example 40
.

Given the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{27}=\mathbb{F}_{3}[\alpha]
where
\alpha^{3}=\alpha+2
. Using the subfield-trace partition (as stated in (
)) we partition
\mathbb{F}_{27}
into the following four blocks

\displaystyle I^{\lambda}_{0}=\left\{{0}\right\},\quad I^{\lambda}_{1}=\mathbb{F}_{27}^{\times}=\left\{{1,2}\right\},\quad I^{\lambda}_{2}=\left\{{\alpha,\alpha+1,\alpha+2,2\alpha,2\alpha+1,2\alpha+2}\right\},\quad

\displaystyle\text{and }\quad I^{\lambda}_{3}=\mathbb{F}_{27}\setminus\left(I^{\lambda}_{0}\cup I^{\lambda}_{1}\cup I^{\lambda}_{2}\right)

Consider the code
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{F}_{27}^{3}
generated by
G=\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}
. By computing the codewords of
\mathcal{C}
we observe that they can be partitioned into six distinct subfield-trace decompositions, namely

(3,0,0,0),(0,2,1,0),(0,0,2,1),(0,0,1,2),(0,1,0,2)\text{ and }(0,0,0,3).

Each of the six subfield-trace decompositions are represented by
1,2,6,4,2
and
12
codewords, respectively.
The dual
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
of
\mathcal{C}
has a generator matrix of the form

(122)

\displaystyle G^{\perp}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&2\\
0&1&\alpha\end{pmatrix}.

It is easily checked that the vectors
(1,0,2)
and
(2,0,1)
are the only two codewords of
\mathcal{C}^{\perp}
of subfield-trace decomposition equal to
\rho=(1,2,0,0)
. Thus,
\mathcal{D}_{\rho}^{\lambda}(\mathcal{C}^{\perp})=2
.

We now verify that indeed
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}\sum_{\pi\in\mathbb{D}^{\lambda}_{n}(\rho)}K^{\lambda}_{\pi}(\rho)\mathcal{D}_{\pi}^{\lambda}(\mathcal{C})=2
. We show detailed calculations of the Krawtchouk coefficient only for
\pi^{(0)}=(3,0,0,0)
and
\pi^{(1)}=(0,2,1,0)
. Let us first assume, that
\pi=\pi^{(0)}
. Similar to the examples of the Lee and the homogeneous metric, we observe that

(123)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Comp}^{\lambda}_{\pi}(\rho)=\{(\underbrace{1,2,0,0}_{\pi_{0}},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{\pi_{1}},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{\pi_{2}},\underbrace{0,\ldots,0}_{\pi_{3}})\}.

That is, for
t\in\mathrm{Comp}^{\lambda}_{\pi}(\rho)
, we have
t_{00}=1,t_{01}=2
and
t_{02}=\ldots=t_{33}=0
. This yields

(124)

\displaystyle K^{\lambda}_{\pi}(\rho)=\binom{3}{2,1}(3-1)^{2}=12,\quad\text{and}\quad K^{\lambda}_{\pi}(\rho)\mathcal{D}_{\pi}^{\lambda}(\mathcal{C})=12\cdot 1=12.

\pi=\pi^{(1)}
