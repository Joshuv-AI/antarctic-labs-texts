Inventory Consensus Control in Supply Chain Networks using Dissipativity-Based Control and Topology Co-Design
Shirantha Welikala, Hai Lin, Panos J. Antsaklis

Abstract
Recent global and local phenomena have exposed vulnerabilities in critical supply chain networks (SCNs), drawing significant attention from researchers across various fields. Typically, SCNs are viewed as static entities regularly optimized to maintain their optimal operation. However, the dynamic nature of SCNs and their associated uncertainties have motivated researchers to treat SCNs as dynamic networked systems requiring robust control techniques. In this paper, we address the SCN inventory consensus problem, which aims to synchronize multiple parallel supply chains, enhancing coordination and robustness of the overall SCN. To achieve this, we take a novel approach exploiting dissipativity theory. In particular, we propose a dissipativity-based co-design strategy for distributed consensus controllers and communication topology in SCNs. It requires only the dissipativity information of the individual supply chains and involves solving a set of convex optimization problems, thus contributing to scalability, compositionality, and computational efficiency. Moreover, it optimizes the robustness of the SCN to various associated uncertainties, mitigating both bullwhip and ripple effects. We demonstrate our contributions using numerical examples, mainly by comparing the consensus performance with respect to standard steady-state control, feedback control, and consensus control strategies.

Addressing this gap, in this paper, we focus on the inventory consensus control problem, aiming to synchronize multiple parallel supply chains within an SCN. In particular, we propose a dissipativity-based consensus control and communication topology co-design scheme that both optimizes the communication topology and minimizes the bullwhip and ripple effects in a control-theoretic sense. The resulting co-design ensures a robust synchronization of the supply chains, thereby enhancing the overall SCN resilience and efficiency.

Contributions

Despite these promising developments, existing distributed control solutions in SCNs assume the underlying communication topology as prespecified/fixed, thereby leaving a crucial avenue to mitigate bullwhip and ripple effects unexplored.
Motivated by this idea, in this paper, we propose to co-design the distributed controllers and the communication topology to ensure SCN consensus and optimize communication topology while also minimizing bullwhip and ripple effects.
In particular, we propose a dissipativity-based control solution that holistically addresses these challenges in an energy-efficient manner.
To the best of our knowledge, dissipativity theory, which is well known for energy-efficient control solutions
[
39

, has not been applied before to control problems in SCNs.
We exploit the inherent dissipativity properties of supply chains to efficiently design large-scale SCNs, leveraging the compositionality of dissipativity
[

By formulating the problem using linear matrix inequalities (LMIs), we enable computationally efficient co-design of distributed controllers and communication topology
[
41

42
]
. Additionally, our dissipativity- and LMI-based approach offers scalability, decentralizability, and compositionality - enhancing the overall solution’s practicality.
To showcase the effectiveness of the proposed control solution, we implemented a generic SCN simulator

III
Inventory Consensus Control Problem Formulation

As mentioned in the Introduction, coordinating several parallel supply chains to maintain a consensus improves the overall performance and robustness of the supply chain network (SCN)
[
19
]
. Maintaining such a consensus can also be viewed as a synchronizing action across the SCN - which is particularly vital when individual supply chains are physically identical and supply the same product. Another scenario where consensus/synchrony is crucial is when different supply chains are dedicated to providing different products but at some fixed ratio.
In this section, we formulate the inventory consensus control problem as a networked system control problem so that the dissipativity-based control and topology co-design technique in Prop.
2
can be applied.

\tilde{\Sigma}_{i,k},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N},k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}
includes a transportation process
\bar{\Sigma}_{i,k}
and a subsequent inventory/storage process
\Sigma_{i,k}
.

At each supply chain link
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i,k},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N},k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}
, the inventory level, placed order magnitude, and received delivery amount at time step
t\in\mathbb{N}
are denoted by
x_{i,k}(t)
,
u_{i,k}(t)
, and
y_{i,k}(t)
, respectively, and the transportation delay and inventory decay rate parameters are denoted by
\tau_{i,k}
and
\rho_{i,k}
respectively. Using these notations, for each supply chain link
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i,k},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N},k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}
, the transportation dynamics can be written as

y_{i,k}(t)=u_{i,k}(t-\tau_{i,k})-\bar{w}_{i,k}(t),

(9)

where
\bar{w}_{i,k}(t)
is the observed transportation loss (waste) at time step
t\in\mathbb{N}
, and the inventory dynamics can be written as

x_{i,k}(t+1)=(1-\rho_{i,k})x_{i,k}(t)+y_{i,k}(t)-u_{i,k+1}(t)-w_{i,k}(t),

(10)

where
w_{i,k}(t)
is the observed inventory loss (waste) at time step
t\in\mathbb{N}
. Note that, for
k=n
(i.e., for the supply chain link
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i,n}
at the edge of the supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i}
), we consider

u_{i,n+1}(t)\triangleq d_{in}(t),

(11)

where
d_{in}(t)
represents the uncontrollable customer demand. Besides, the remaining order magnitudes
\{u_{i,k}(t):k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}\}
are considered as the controllable inputs.

The transportation delays
\{\tau_{i,k}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{N},k\in\mathbb{N}_{N}\}
are assumed to be time-invariant and integer-valued (i.e., an integer multiple of the time step size used). Similarly, the inventory decay rates (also called the “perish rates,” and typically falls in the interval
[0,1]
) are assumed to be time-invariant. Note that these two assumptions are mild as any violations can be compensated by the respective loss (waste) terms introduced in the transportation (
9
) and inventory (
10
) dynamics. Note also that these loss terms can also compensate for any modeling imperfections in (
9
)-(
10
).

Using the previous assumption that
\tau_{i,k}\in\mathbb{N}
, we next express the transportation dynamics (
9
) in an LTI system form. For this purpose, we introduce transportation states:

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{i,k}^{(1)}(t)\triangleq

\displaystyle u_{i,k}(t-\tau_{i,k}),

(12)

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{i,k}^{(2)}(t)\triangleq

\displaystyle u_{i,k}(t-\tau_{i,k}+1),

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{i,k}^{(\tau_{i,k})}(t)\triangleq

\displaystyle u_{i,k}(t-1).

Then, by vectorizing these states
\bar{x}_{i,k}(t)\triangleq[\bar{x}_{i,k}^{(l)}(t)]_{l\in\mathbb{N}_{\tau_{i,k}}}^{\top}
, (
9
) can be expressed as a
\tau_{i,k}
-dimensional LTI system:

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{i,k}(t+1)=

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{i,k}\bar{x}_{i,k}(t)+\bar{B}_{i,k}u_{i,k}(t),

(13)

\displaystyle y_{i,k}(t)=

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{i,k}\bar{x}_{i,k}(t)-\bar{w}_{i,k}(t),

where

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{i,k}\triangleq

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix},\ \ \bar{B}_{i,k}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
0\\
1\end{bmatrix},

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{i,k}\triangleq

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0&0\end{bmatrix}.

We are now ready to express the dynamics of an entire supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
in a compact form by considering vectorized versions of (
10
) and (
13
) (across all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}
). First, let us introduce vectorized transportation states, inventory levels, order magnitudes, delivery amounts, transportation losses, inventory losses, and customer demands associated with the supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
as:

\bar{x}_{i}(t)\triangleq[\bar{x}_{i,k}^{\top}(t)]_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}^{\top}
,

x_{i}(t)\triangleq[x_{i,k}(t)]_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}^{\top}
,

u_{i}(t)\triangleq[u_{i,k}(t)]_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}^{\top}
,

y_{i}(t)\triangleq[y_{i,k}(t)]_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}^{\top}
,

\bar{w}_{i}(t)\triangleq[\bar{w}_{i,k}]_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}^{\top}
,

w_{i}(t)\triangleq[w_{i,k}(t)]_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}^{\top}
and

d_{i}(t)\triangleq[0,0,\cdots,0,d_{in}(t)]^{\top}
,
respectively. Using these notations, for each supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
, the transportation dynamics can be written as

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{i}(t+1)=

\displaystyle\bar{A}_{i}\bar{x}_{i}(t)+\bar{B}_{i}u_{i}(t),

(14)

\displaystyle y_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\bar{C}_{i}\bar{x}_{i}(t)-\bar{w}_{i}(t).

where

\bar{A}_{i}\triangleq\text{diag}(\bar{A}_{i,k}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{n})
,

\bar{B}_{i}\triangleq\text{diag}(\bar{B}_{i,k}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{n})
and

\bar{C}_{i}\triangleq\text{diag}(\bar{C}_{i,k}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{n})
,
and the inventory dynamics can be written as

x_{i}(t+1)=A_{i}x_{i}(t)+y_{i}(t)-B_{i}u_{i}(t)-d_{i}(t)-w_{i}(t),

(15)

where

A_{i}\triangleq\text{diag}((1-\rho_{i,k}):k\in\mathbb{N}_{n})
and

B_{i}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\\
0&0&0&\cdots&0\end{bmatrix}.

Finally, it is worth noting the dimensions:
\bar{x}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{\tau_{i}}
where
\tau_{i}\triangleq\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}\tau_{i,k}
(i.e., the total delay in the supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i}
), and
u_{i}(t),y_{i}(t),\bar{w}_{i}(t),x_{i}(t),d_{i}(t),w_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

III-B

Steady-State Control

For generality, the disturbance vectors
\bar{w}_{i}(t)
,
w_{i}(t)
and
d_{i}(t)
in (
14
)-(
15
) are assumed to be random vectors with known fixed means
\overline{\bar{w}}_{i}
,
\overline{w}_{i}
and
\overline{d}_{i}
, respectively. And thus, we can express
\bar{w}_{i}(t)=\overline{\bar{w}}_{i}+\tilde{\bar{w}}_{i}(t)
,
w_{i}(t)=\overline{w}_{i}+\tilde{w}_{i}(t)
and
d_{i}(t)=\overline{d}_{i}+\tilde{d}_{i}(t)
, where
\tilde{\bar{w}}_{i}(t)
,
\tilde{w}_{i}(t)
and
\tilde{d}_{i}(t)
represent the corresponding zero mean disturbance vectors, respectively.

On the other hand, let us denote the steady-state values of the transportation state vector
\bar{x}_{i}(t)
in (
14
) and the inventory state vector
x_{i}(t)
in (
15
) by
\overline{\bar{x}}_{i}
and
\overline{x}_{i}
, respectively. It is worth mentioning that the steady-state inventory state vector
\overline{x}_{i}
is a design variables that need to be prespecified. For example, in strategies like “just-in-time,” the steady-state inventory values are selected such that
\overline{x}_{i}=\mathbf{0}

[
19
]
.

To support the said mean disturbance levels and steady-state inventory levels, each supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
is assumed to select its control input (order magnitudes) as

u_{i}(t)=\overline{u}_{i}+\tilde{u}_{i}(t),

(16)

where
\overline{u}_{i}\triangleq[\overline{u}_{i,k}]_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}^{\top}
is the steady-state control input (i.e., the steady-state ordering magnitudes) and
\tilde{u}_{i}(t)
is a virtual control law. The details of
\tilde{u}_{i}(t)
will be provided later on. However, the following proposition provides the steady-state control input
\overline{u}_{i}
and the steady-state transportation state
\overline{\bar{x}}_{i}
.

Proposition 3

For each supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
described by (
14
)-(
15
), under fixed deterministic disturbances (i.e.,
\bar{w}_{i}(t)=\overline{\bar{w}}_{i}
,
w_{i}(t)=\overline{w}_{i}
, and
d_{i}(t)=\overline{d}_{i},\forall t\in\mathbb{N}
), there exists an equilibrium point such that

\displaystyle\bar{x}_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\overline{\bar{x}}_{i}=\bar{D}_{i}\overline{u}_{i},

(17)

\displaystyle x_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\overline{x}_{i}\mbox{ (prescribed)},

under steady-state control input
\overline{u}_{i}\triangleq[\overline{u}_{i,k}]_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}}^{\top}
with

\overline{u}_{i,k}=\sum_{j=k}^{n}(\rho_{i,k}\overline{x}_{i,k}+\overline{\bar{w}}_{i,k}+\overline{w}_{i,k})+\overline{d}_{i},\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}

(18)

and
\tilde{u}_{i}(t)=\mathbf{0}
in (
16
) with
\bar{D}_{i}\triangleq\text{diag}(\mathbf{1}_{\tau_{i,k}}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{n})
.

Proof:

Under the given conditions,
u_{i}(t)=\overline{u}_{i}
in (
16
). Consequently, starting from (
14
), the resulting steady-state transportation state
\overline{\bar{x}}_{i}
can be obtained as

\displaystyle\overline{\bar{x}}_{i}=

\displaystyle\ \bar{A}_{i}\overline{\bar{x}}_{i}+\bar{B}_{i}\overline{u}_{i}=(\mathbf{I}-\bar{A}_{i})^{-1}\bar{B}_{i}\overline{u}_{i}=\bar{D}_{i}\overline{u}_{i}.

(19)

The above last step is due to
(\mathbf{I}-\bar{A}_{i,k})^{-1}
being an upper-triangular matrix of ones, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}_{n}
.

On the other hand, starting from (
15
), the resulting steady-state inventory state
\overline{x}_{i}
can be obtained as

\displaystyle\overline{x}_{i}=

\displaystyle\ A_{i}\overline{x}_{i}+(\bar{C}_{i}\overline{\bar{x}}_{i}-\overline{\bar{w}}_{i})-B_{i}\overline{u}_{i}-\overline{d}_{i}-\overline{w}_{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ (\mathbf{I}-A_{i})^{-1}\left((\bar{C}_{i}\overline{\bar{x}}_{i}-\overline{\bar{w}}_{i})-B_{i}\overline{u}_{i}-\overline{w}_{i}-\overline{d}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ (\mathbf{I}-A_{i})^{-1}\left((\bar{C}_{i}\bar{D}_{i}-B_{i})\overline{u}_{i}-\overline{\bar{w}}_{i}-\overline{w}_{i}-\overline{d}_{i}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ (\mathbf{I}-A_{i})^{-1}\left((\mathbf{I}-B_{i})\overline{u}_{i}-\overline{\bar{w}}_{i}-\overline{w}_{i}-\overline{d}_{i}\right).

(20)

The above last two steps are are due to (
19
) and
\bar{C}_{i}\bar{D}_{i}=\mathbf{I}
, respectively.
Now, since
\overline{x}_{i}
is prespecified, the required steady-state control
\overline{u}_{i}
can be obtained using (
20
) as

\overline{u}_{i}=(\mathbf{I}-B_{i})^{-1}\left((\mathbf{I}-A_{i})\overline{x}_{i}+\overline{\bar{w}}_{i}+\overline{w}_{i}+\overline{d}_{i}\right).

(21)

Finally, using the facts that
(\mathbf{I}-B_{i})^{-1}
is an upper-triangular matrix of ones and
(\mathbf{I}-A_{i})=\text{diag}(\rho_{i,k}:k\in\mathbb{N}_{n})
, it is easy to see that (
21
) is equivalent to (
18
).
∎

The result (
18
) established in Prop.
3
intuitively means that the order magnitude of each supply chain link has to compensate for the total mean waste of the downstream supply chain and the mean customer demand.

III-C

Error Dynamics

We now provide the error dynamics of each supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
around its equilibrium point under steady-state control (
16
) with (
21
). Let us define the error states

\tilde{\bar{x}}_{i}(t)\triangleq\bar{x}_{i}(t)-\overline{\bar{x}}_{i}
and

\tilde{x}_{i}(t)\triangleq x_{i}(t)-\overline{x}_{i}
, and
total disturbance input

r_{i}(t)\triangleq\tilde{\bar{w}}_{i}+\tilde{w}_{i}(t)+\tilde{d}_{i}(t)
. Then, using (
14
) and (
15
) (together with (
19
) and (
20
)), we get

\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{x}_{i}(t+1)\\
\tilde{\bar{x}}_{i}(t+1)\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathrm{x}_{i}(t+1)}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}A_{i}&\bar{C}_{i}\\
\mathbf{0}&\bar{A}_{i}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathcal{A}_{i}}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{x}_{i}(t)\\
\tilde{\bar{x}}_{i}(t)\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathrm{x}_{i}(t)}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-B_{i}\\
\bar{B}_{i}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathcal{B}_{i}}\tilde{u}_{i}(t)+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}-\mathbf{I}\\
\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathcal{D}_{i}}r_{i}(t).

(22)

To stabilize (and dissipativate, if necessary) (
22
), a local state feedback controller
\mathcal{L}_{i}
can be included by defining:

\tilde{u}_{i}(t)\triangleq\mathcal{L}_{i}\mathrm{x}_{i}(t)+\tilde{\tilde{u}}_{i}(t),

(23)

where the virtual control input
\tilde{\tilde{u}}_{i}(t)
is yet to be designed based on other global control needs like achieving supply chain inventory consensus.
For the use of such global controllers, at each supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{n}
, we also include the inventory error
\tilde{x}_{i}(t)
as a
virtual output
:

\mathrm{y}_{i}(t)\triangleq\tilde{x}_{i}(t)=\mathcal{C}_{i}\mathrm{x}_{i}(t),

where
\mathcal{C}_{i}\triangleq\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{I}&\mathbf{0}\end{bmatrix}
. Consequently, the error dynamics of each supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
takes the compact form

\displaystyle\mathrm{x}_{i}(t+1)=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}_{i}+\mathcal{B}_{i}\mathcal{L}_{i})\mathrm{x}_{i}(t)+\eta_{i}(t),

(24)

\displaystyle\mathrm{y}_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i}\mathrm{x}_{i}(t),

where we define the
virtual input
as

\eta_{i}(t)\triangleq\mathcal{B}_{i}\tilde{\tilde{u}}_{i}(t)+\mathcal{D}_{i}r_{i}(t).

(25)

The remaining task is to design the virtual control input
\tilde{\tilde{u}}_{i}(t)
(also called the global controller), which we will address in the sequel. Before moving on, it is worth noting the dimensions of some key variables:
\mathrm{x}_{i}(t),\eta_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}}
where
n_{i}\triangleq n+\tau_{i}
, and
r_{i}(t),\tilde{u}_{i}(t),\tilde{\tilde{u}}_{i}(t),\mathrm{y}_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

III-D

Inventory Consensus Control

The goal of inventory consensus is to maintain some coordination among the supply chain inventories so that

x_{i}(t)-x_{j}(t)=\rho_{ij},\quad\forall i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{N},i\neq j.

(26)

Clearly,
\rho_{ij}\triangleq\overline{x}_{i}-\overline{x}_{j}
, and since
\mathrm{y}_{i}(t)=x_{i}(t)-\overline{x}_{i}
, (
26
) can be restated as

\mathrm{y}_{i}(t)-\mathrm{y}_{j}(t)=\mathbf{0},\quad\forall i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{N},i\neq j.

(27)

To achieve this goal, at each supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},\char 16\relax\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
, we propose to use a distributed inventory consensus controller:

\tilde{\tilde{u}}_{i}(t)\triangleq\mathcal{L}_{ii}\mathrm{y}_{i}(t)+\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}\backslash\{i\}}\mathcal{L}_{ij}(\mathrm{y}_{i}(t)-\mathrm{y}_{j}(t)).

(28)

Typically (e.g., see
[
19
]
), each
\mathcal{L}_{ij}
term in (
28
) is constrained to be scalars
1
or
0
depending on whether the information from supply chain
\Sigma_{j}
can be obtained at
\Sigma_{i}
or not, respectively. However, we propose to treat
\mathcal{L}_{ij}
as a free coupling weight matrix. Nevertheless, in our framework, if
\mathcal{L}_{ij}=\mathbf{0}
, it still implies that information from
\Sigma_{j}
is not necessary at
\Sigma_{i}
. Therefore, it is clear that: (1) the proposed consensus control law (
28
) is distributed, (2) the overall control solution is far more flexible, and thus, can lead to less conservative behaviors, and (3) via determining
\mathcal{L}_{ij}
terms, we can synthesize the required information flow topology.

Under (
28
), the closed-loop error dynamics of each supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{x}_{i}(t+1)=

\displaystyle(\mathcal{A}_{i}+\mathcal{B}_{i}\mathcal{L}_{i})\mathrm{x}_{i}(t)+\eta_{i}(t),

(29)

\displaystyle\mathrm{y}_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{i}\mathrm{x}_{i}(t),

\displaystyle\eta_{i}(t)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{i}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}\mathcal{K}_{ij}\mathrm{y}_{j}(t)+\mathcal{D}_{i}r_{i}(t),

with
\mathcal{K}_{ii}\triangleq\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}\mathcal{L}_{ij}
and
\mathcal{K}_{ij}\triangleq-\mathcal{L}_{ij},\forall j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}\backslash\{i\}
. It is worth noting that
\mathcal{L}\triangleq[\mathcal{L}_{ij}]_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}
is fully determined by
\mathcal{K}\triangleq[\mathcal{K}_{ij}]_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}
.

Before moving on, motivated by the said coordination goal (
27
), we also define a
performance output

z_{i}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for each supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
as

z_{i}(t)\triangleq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}(\mathrm{y}_{i}(t)-\mathrm{y}_{j}(t))\equiv\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}\mathcal{E}_{ij}\mathrm{y}_{j}(t)

(30)

where
\mathcal{E}_{ij}\triangleq(\mathbf{1}_{\{i=j\}}-\frac{1}{N})\mathbf{I}_{n},\forall i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}
. This performance objective is utilized in the sequel to design the optimally robust consensus controller
\mathcal{K}
along with the required information flow topology (i.e., the communication topology).

III-E

Networked System View

Using the aforementioned notions of
virtual inputs

\eta(t)\triangleq[\eta_{i}^{\top}(t)]_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}^{\top}
,
virtual outputs

\mathrm{y}(t)\triangleq[\mathrm{y}_{i}^{\top}(t)]_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}^{\top}
,
total disturbance inputs

r(t)\triangleq[r_{i}^{\top}(t)]_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}^{\top}
and
performance outputs

z(t)\triangleq[z_{i}^{\top}(t)]_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}^{\top}
, the closed-loop error dynamics of the SCN (consisting of
N
parallel supply chains) can be viewed as a networked system as shown in Fig.
3
. Note that it is of the same form as the interconnected system considered in Fig.
1
. In particular, here, the corresponding interconnection relationship takes the form:

\begin{bmatrix}\eta(t)\\
z(t)\end{bmatrix}=M\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{y}(t)\\
r(t)\end{bmatrix}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}M_{\eta\mathrm{y}}&M_{\eta r}\\
M_{z\mathrm{y}}&M_{zr}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathrm{y}(t)\\
r(t)\end{bmatrix},

(31)

where
M_{\eta\mathrm{y}}=\mathcal{B}\mathcal{K}
with
\mathcal{B}\triangleq\text{diag}(\mathcal{B}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{N})
,

M_{\eta r}=\mathcal{D}
with
\mathcal{D}\triangleq\text{diag}(\mathcal{D}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{N}_{N})
,

M_{z\mathrm{y}}=\mathcal{E}\triangleq[\mathcal{E}_{ij}]_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}
and
M_{zr}=\mathbf{0}
.
It is worth noting the dimensions:
\eta(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{n}}
where
\bar{n}=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}n_{i}
, and
z(t),\mathrm{y}(t),r(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{nN}
.

Fig. 3
:
Closed-loop error dynamics of the SCN as a networked system.

Through this networked system view of the closed-loop SCN, our goal is to synthesize the interconnection matrix block
M_{\eta\mathrm{y}}
to obtain
\mathcal{L}_{ij}
matrices as required in the used inventory consensus controller (
28
). Since
\mathcal{L}_{ij}
matrices are fully determined by the corresponding
\mathcal{K}_{ij}
matrices, and each
\mathcal{K}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, the format of the matrix block
M_{\eta\mathrm{y}}
is such that

M_{\eta\mathrm{y}}\triangleq[M_{\eta\mathrm{y}}^{ij}]_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}}

where

M_{\eta\mathrm{y}}^{ij}=\mathcal{B}_{i}\mathcal{K}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times n},\quad\forall i,j\in\mathbb{N}_{N}.

(32)

Consequently, we require each column of
M_{\eta\mathrm{y}}^{ij}
to be in the column space of
\mathcal{B}_{i}
. This constraint, together with the facts that
\mathcal{B}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{i}\times n}
and
\text{rank }\mathcal{B}_{i}=n
, ensures that we can uniquely determine each
\mathcal{K}_{ij}
using the corresponding
M_{\eta\mathrm{y}}^{ij}
.

Overall, the resulting inventory consensus controller (
28
) (through synthesizing
M_{\eta\mathrm{y}}
) should not only stabilize the closed-loop SCN but also attenuate the effects of disturbances
r(t)
on the consensus performance
z(t)
. As the considered disturbances in our approach include both demand and intermediate uncertainties (e.g., waste), such an attenuation will minimize both the bullwhip effect and the ripple effect in the closed-loop SCN (
29
)-(
31
).

Consequently, when synthesizing
M_{\eta\mathrm{y}}
(i.e., the consensus controllers), we enforce a prespecified bound
\bar{\gamma}
on the squared
L_{2}
-gain
\gamma^{2}
from disturbances
r(t)
to consensus performance
z(t)
in the closed-loop SCN. In other words, we enforce the constraint:

\frac{\|z(\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}^{2}}{\|r(\cdot)\|_{\mathcal{L}_{2}}^{2}}\leq\gamma^{2}\mbox{ with }\gamma^{2}\leq\bar{\gamma}.

(33)

According to Rm.
1
, this specification (
33
) can be met by enforcing
\textbf{Y}\equiv\scriptsize\begin{bmatrix}\gamma^{2}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&-\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}
-EID (from
r(t)
to
z(t)
) along with the constraint
\gamma^{2}\leq\bar{\gamma}
in the closed-loop SCN (
29
)-(
31
).

IV-A

Local Controller Design

The following theorem formulates the local controller design problem as an LMI problem. The goal of each local controller is to enforce a certain EID property, which will then be exploited to design the global controller.

Theorem 1

At each supply chain
\tilde{\Sigma}_{i},i\in\mathbb{N}_{n}
, the error dynamics (

X_{i}
-EID (from
\eta_{i}(t)
to
\mathrm{y}_{i}(t)
) when the local controller

(

\displaystyle\mbox{Find:}

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}_{i},P_{i},X_{i}

(34)

Evaluated SCN Control Strategies

We considered five different SCN control strategies:
(i) Local steady-state control (LSSC),
(ii) Local state-feedback control (LSFC),

Discussion

From the reported observations, it is clear that the proposed DCC methods outperform conventional methods like LSSC, LSFC, and GCC in terms of maintaining a consensus among the supply chains in the SCN - in the presence of considerable uncertainties and failures. It is worth highlighting that the proposed DCC-U method provides the fastest convergence to a consensus from the applied diverse initial conditions and injected transportation and inventory failures. The final state of the SCN observed under the proposed DCC-U strategy in a simulation is shown in Fig.
14
.

The observed superior consensus control performance of the DCC-U method is partly due to the specifically designed communication topology shown in Fig.
10
. In this communication topology, several key features can be observed:

1.

It is suboptimal to use all the communication links.

2.

More information is required at the inventories located at the corners of the SCN as opposed to those located at the middle of the SCN.

3.

Communication links between non-adjacent inventories are preferred as opposed to those between adjacent inventories.

These observations provide crucial yet non-trivial intuitions regarding suitable communication topologies for distributed consensus control in SCNs to minimize the adverse bullwhip and ripple effects.

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we developed a robust distributed inventory consensus control solution for supply chain networks. Maintaining such a consensus among inventories synchronizes parallel supply chains in the SCN while enhancing coordination and robustness of the overall SCN, mitigating both bullwhip and ripple effects.
For this purpose, we modeled the SCN as a networked system comprised of dynamic subsystems with some disturbance inputs and performance outputs. We then used the dissipativity theory to develop a co-design strategy for distributed consensus controllers and their underlying communication topology. The proposed dissipativity-based approach, due to its omission of exact dynamic models, provides a co-design that is inherently robust to disturbances. Moreover, the formulated co-design problem is convex, enabling efficient solutions. The resulting consensus control solutions outperformed the conventional static steady-state control, local dynamic control, as well as global consensus control strategies. Moreover, the resulting optimal communication topologies provide useful insights into effective communication topologies for consensus control of SCNs. Future work is directed towards including the design of adaptive control laws to estimate and update the involved consensus controller parameters like the mean transportation and inventory loss values and mean customer demand values.
