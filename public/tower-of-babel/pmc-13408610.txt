Assessment of Dental Anxiety Using Doll Placement Test in a Miniature Dental Setup among 3–7-year-old Children with Verbal Communication Challenges Compared to Children with Verbal Communication Skills
Authors: D Meera Fathima, Jayanthi Mungara, Poornima Vijayakumar, Deebiga Karunakaran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective communication builds trust between the child, dentist, and parents, helping reduce fear and anxiety during dental treatment. Children with limited verbal communication often experience greater anxiety due to difficulty expressing themselves and understanding instructions.AimThe study was conducted to assess and compare the dental anxiety levels of children with limited verbal communication skills to those with normal verbal communication skills using a Doll Placement Test in a miniature dental setup. The study also investigates correlating the measured anxiety levels with the children's behavior in the dental operatory.Materials and methodsBased on the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 82 children were selected and divided into two groups. Group A (n = 41) children with normal verbal communication skills and group B (n = 41) with limited verbal communication skills. Demographic and medical details were collected using a standardized questionnaire. Anxiety was measured using a Doll Placement Test, where the doll's position reflected the child's perceived anxiety. Behavior during the dental visit was assessed with the Modified Frankl's Behavior Rating Scale. Data were analyzed using SPSS v29.0. The chi-square test examined the association between anxiety and behavior. Relationships between variables were analyzed by Pearson's correlation.ResultsIn Group B, 82.9% preferred a doll position, indicating a need for parental presence (p = 0.036). In Group A, 82.9% showed definitely positive behavior in the dental chair (p = 0.002). A definite positive correlation was observed between doll placement and actual behavior between the two groups (p < 0.001).ConclusionThe Doll Placement Test in a miniature dental setup is a simple, effective tool for predicting children's dental anxiety regardless of communication ability. Children with limited verbal skills showed higher anxiety and a stronger need for parental presence, significantly correlating with their observed behavior.Keywords: Assessment of dental fear and anxiety, Behavior, Communication challenges, Doll Placement Test, Miniature dental office model

Introduction

Dental fear and anxiety are widely recognized as significant barriers to the acceptance and utilization of dental care, provoking physical, cognitive, emotional, and behavioral responses in individuals.1 Dental anxiety refers to feelings of worry, stress, or apprehension associated with dental settings often involving fear of pain or unpleasant experiences, as well as a perceived loss of control.2

Dental anxiety is influenced by a combination of factors.3 It may stem from observing fear in parents or peers, prior distressing dental visits, or insufficient dental awareness during childhood.3 Individual personality traits, such as increased worry or self-consciousness, can heighten susceptibility.4 Negative depictions of dental treatment in the media, ineffective coping mechanisms, concerns about physical appearance,5 and the sense of vulnerability when lying in a reclined position can further contribute. Additionally, sensory elements such as the sight of needles, the sound of dental instruments, distinctive smells like eugenol, or vibrations during procedures can intensify anxiety within the dental environment.3–9

Common fears that contribute to dental anxiety include apprehension about pain, potential injury, exposure to mercury, impersonal or insensitive treatment, feelings of embarrassment, concerns about radiation, fear of the unknown, lack of trust in the dental team, fear of choking, and a perceived loss of control while seated in the dental chair.10

According to Milgrom et al.,11 the “Seattle System” developed at the University of Washington identifies four distinct categories of patients with dental anxiety12: (1) anxiety related to specific dental stimuli, (2) distrust of dental personnel, (3) generalized dental anxiety, and (4) anxiety related to the anticipation of catastrophe.

In pediatric dentistry, dental fear and anxiety often manifest as uncooperative behavior on the dental chair, posing considerable challenges for delivering safe and effective treatment.11 Moreover, heightened anxiety can trigger stress responses among dental professionals, leading to strained interactions between dentists, young patients, and their caregivers.13,14 As a child's initial dental experience often shapes attitudes toward future dental care, unaddressed dental anxiety may contribute to dental neglect persisting into adulthood.15

It is therefore vital for pediatric dentists to recognize both obvious and subtle signs of fear and anxiety in children and to apply appropriate behavior management strategies. Prior interactions involving the dentist, child, and parent, supported by subjective and objective assessments, can assist in the timely diagnosis and effective management of dental anxiety.

Various methods are available for assessing dental fear and anxiety in children, including behavioral ratings, psychometric scales, physiological measures, and projective techniques.16 Projective techniques employ picture-based tools such as the RMS Scale2, RMS-PS,2 Chotta Bheem–Chutki Scale,16 facial index scale (FIS),17 Venham's pictorial test (VPT),18 the dental subscale of the children's fear survey schedule,19 and the Modified Dental Anxiety Scale.20 Physiological parameters21,22 such as pulse rate, heart rate, autonomic nervous activity, muscle activity, and biomarkers23 such as salivary or serum stress markers provide additional objective measures of anxiety.

An innovative projective technique is the Doll Placement Test conducted within a miniature dental setup that visually replicates a pediatric dental operatory. This method provides valuable insights into children's perceptions and feelings about dental visits and has been effectively used to assess dental anxiety among preschool-aged children in earlier studies.24–27

Effective communication is equally important, requiring active listening, empathy, and trust-building to overcome fear and anxiety.28 The concept of “Pediatric Dentists” emphasizes age-appropriate, individualized, development-based communication between pediatric dentists and children to foster trust and cooperation. Lamb highlighted that ineffective dentist–patient communication can contribute to significant patient attrition,29 underlining the importance of building a strong, trust-based relationship among the dentist, child, and caregiver.

Children with limited verbal communication skills often experience higher levels of dental anxiety and face additional challenges in accessing effective dental care.30 Innovative assessment methods, such as the Doll Placement Test, may help bridge this communication gap and enhance patient–dentist interaction for children with special healthcare needs.

In light of these factors, the present study was conducted to evaluate dental anxiety levels in children with limited verbal communication skills compared to their peers with typical communication abilities, using the Doll Placement Test. Additionally, the study aimed to correlate the measured anxiety levels with the children's behavior during their first dental visit.

A total of 82 eligible children were included in the study and equally allocated into two groups according to their communication skills: group A (control): 41 children with normal communication skills (from Ragas Dental College OPD) and group B (experimental): 41 children with limited communication skills (referred from NIEPMD), including those with speech/hearing impairments or mild autistic traits.

Informed consent was obtained from parents/guardians. A specially customized miniature dental clinic model, with dolls symbolizing the dentist, child, and parents, was utilized to perform the Doll Placement Test as a projective tool to evaluate dental anxiety. Children were asked to arrange the dolls inside or outside the operatory (Figs 1 and 2).

During the actual dental visit, child behavior was assessed independently using the Modified Frankl Behavior Rating Scale, categorizing behavior as definitely positive (++), positive (+), negative (−), or definitely negative (−–).

Statistical Analysis

Anxiety levels, as assessed using the Doll Placement Test, and behavior ratings, as evaluated by the Modified Frankl's Behavior Rating Scale, were recorded, tabulated, and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 29.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic characteristics and Doll Placement Test patterns. The chi-square test was applied to assess the association between anxiety levels (placement patterns) and behavior ratings during dental treatment, both within and between the groups. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to analyze the relationship between anxiety scores and behavior outcomes. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Dental anxiety in children is a complex, multifactorial condition influenced by psychosocial and environmental factors. For most pediatric patients, fear is triggered by specific stimuli, which can be addressed by clinicians through appropriate management strategies tailored to the child's needs.9 Understanding anxiety triggers and related behavioral patterns is essential for pediatric dentists to adopt techniques that improve cooperation and comfort during treatment.

Children with limited communication skills, such as those with speech and hearing impairments, developmental delays, or mild autistic traits, face distinct challenges in expressing fear, understanding dental procedures, and cooperating in the operatory. This makes them more prone to heightened anxiety and uncooperative behavior. Although subjective scales such as the Facial Index Scale (FIS)19 and the Venham picture scale20 are effective for verbal children, alternative approaches are required for those with limited speech. In this study, the Doll Placement Test in a Miniature Dental Office Model—originally developed by Ozaki et al.24and later validated by Singh et al.,25 Suresh et al.,26 and Shinji et al.27 was used to objectively assess dental anxiety.

A total number of 82 children aged between 3 and 7 years were included, divided equally between those with normal communication skills and those with limited communication abilities. Four doll placement patterns were used to represent increasing levels of anxiety. Placement of the child on the dental chair with the dentist present and the parents outside the operatory (pattern 1) was associated with lower anxiety levels, whereas placing the child or the dentist outside the operatory (patterns 3 and 4) indicated higher anxiety levels. The findings showed that 82.9% of children with communication challenges preferred pattern 2, indicating a strong need for parental presence inside the operatory, compared to 56.1% of children with normal communication skills. Only 7.3% of children with communication challenges chose pattern 1, whereas 31.7% of their peers did so, highlighting how parental separation increases anxiety for children with limited communication.

Behavioral outcomes assessed with the Modified Frankl's Behavior Rating Scale demonstrated that 82.9% of children with normal communication skills showed definitely positive behavior during treatment, whereas only 46.3% of children with communication challenges did so. An additional 43.9% of children with communication difficulties displayed positive but cautious cooperation, suggesting that heightened anxiety may not always present as overtly negative behavior but rather as hesitancy or resistance that requires additional support. Negative and definitely negative behaviors did not differ significantly between the groups.

A significant difference in age was observed between the groups (p = 0.027), with younger children (under 5 years) comprising the majority in the control group, whereas older children (5 years and above) were more prevalent in the communication-challenged group. This indicates delayed recognition and intervention for speech and hearing problems, which may lead to later introduction to dental care settings and greater unfamiliarity, consistent with previous research.33,34 A strong positive relationship was found between the anxiety levels inferred from the Doll Placement Test and the behavior observed during treatment, as determined by Pearson correlation analysis. The correlation coefficients were r = 0.635 for the control group and r = 0.683 for the communication-challenged group, both statistically significant (p < 0.001). These results indicate that higher anxiety levels are associated with less cooperative behavior, consistent with previous findings.25–27

The dentist doll was designed with a friendly face, casual attire, and a white coat to reflect literature suggesting that clinic design and dentist appearance can influence acceptance of dental care.31,32 Although the model was symbolic, it successfully simulated the clinical setting for the purpose of the test. Innovative approaches such as the PJS pictorial scale35 and virtual reality aids with integrated sign language36 further emphasize the growing need for tailored assessment tools for children with communication challenges.

The study highlights that the Doll Placement Test in a miniature dental office model can serve as a practical tool for assessing dental anxiety and predicting likely behavior in children with and without communication difficulties. However, limitations include a lack of analysis of broader psychosocial variables such as parental education, family income, or previous dental experiences, as well as the cross-sectional design, which did not capture how anxiety and behavior might evolve with repeated visits. The involvement of parents in helping children choose doll placement may also have introduced bias.

These findings underscore the importance of parental presence, gradual familiarization, use of visual aids, positive reinforcement, and training in adaptive behavior management techniques for dentists treating children with special communication needs. With such tailored approaches, dental anxiety can be reduced, cooperation improved, and the overall dental experience made more positive for children who require extra support.

Conclusion

The results of this study support the Doll Placement Test conducted in a miniature dental setup, demonstrating its practicality and effectiveness in assessing dental anxiety levels in children, including those with limited verbal communication abilities. The clear correlation between the placement patterns and observed cooperative behavior indicates that this simple method can help predict how a child may respond in the dental chair, allowing dental professionals to plan suitable management strategies in advance.

Clinically, these results emphasize the importance of allowing parental presence during dental procedures, especially for children with communication challenges, as this can provide emotional security and reduce fear. Additional approaches, such as previsit orientation programs, role-playing activities, visual aids, and positive reinforcement techniques, can help familiarize children with the dental environment and enhance their cooperation.

To improve outcomes for this vulnerable patient group, pediatric dentists should receive specialized training in adaptive communication, behavioral management, and strategies for treating children with speech and hearing impairments or developmental delays.

Future research should aim to include larger and more diverse samples, integrate objective physiological markers of anxiety, and adopt longitudinal study designs to track changes in dental anxiety and behavior over time. Exploring additional psychosocial variables could also provide a more comprehensive understanding of the complex factors influencing dental fear in children with communication challenges.

By implementing these strategies, dental teams can contribute to more positive, less stressful dental experiences, ultimately promoting better oral health and long-term treatment compliance among children who face communication barriers.
