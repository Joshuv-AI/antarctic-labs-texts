Identifying priority zones for carbon management in the Central Yunnan agglomeration
Authors: Yao Fu, Chang Liu, Xi Yang, Zhenghui Li, Ruiwu Zhou, Tao Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo support the dual goals of carbon neutrality and peak carbon, along with the regional coordinated development strategy, this paper analyzes the spatio-temporal dynamics of carbon sequestration, carbon emissions, and carbon offset effect at the county scale in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. A carbon management evaluation system was also established. Integrated zoning for carbon compensation and management was conducted based on the carbon offset effect, the economic-social-ecological composite index, and the coupling coordination degree of each county. Over the past two decades, both carbon sequestration and carbon emissions have shown an upward trend in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. Although the total carbon sequestration can offset the overall carbon emissions, analysis at the county level reveals significant spatial disparities in the carbon offset effect. A multi-core structure was observed, exhibiting a spatial pattern of "low in the center and high at the periphery." Based on the carbon offset effect and local economic, social, and ecological conditions, the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration was categorized into six zones, and differentiated management strategies were proposed for each. This study and its zoning scheme are crucial for clarifying regional carbon responsibilities and for developing and implementing tailored carbon strategies and planning.Keywords: Carbon neutrality, Carbon sequestration, Carbon emissions, Carbon offset effect, Carbon regulationSubject terms: Urban ecology, Sustainability

Introduction

People have deeply learned that human-induced climate change is a threat to human wellbeing and health of the planet. The changing climate impacts socioeconomic systems across the world, and presents a fundamental threat to human health and ecosystem stability1–3. The increase in levels of greenhouse gases emitted by human activity, such as carbon dioxide (CO2), methane and nitrous oxide, is the most significant contributors to climate change with the acceleration of global industrialization and urbanization. To avoid and mitigate the climate risks, reducing carbon emissions and carrying out carbon transform are great challenges for the whole world. China has actively contributed to global climate governance, it set a “dual-carbon” target in 2020, it has been making strenuous efforts to meet the goals with carbon emissions peaked in 2030 and eventually neutralized in 2060.

The scientific foundation for effective climate governance lies in robust carbon compensation mechanisms and zoning, which allocate differentiated responsibilities based on regional carbon accounting4–8. Given the diverse trajectories of socio-economic development across regions, carbon emission productivity displays substantial variations. This divergence is influenced by factors such as industrial structures, technological capabilities, and energy consumption patterns. Simultaneously, as regions pursue economic growth, the ecological degradation and environmental pollution caused by carbon emissions manifest characteristics of external extension and jointness. These phenomena not only pose challenges to local sustainable development but also give rise to complex inter-regional carbon responsibility issues, which are essential considerations in the design and implementation of carbon compensation mechanisms.

Zoning for carbon compensation and carbon management is a new field in this context, carbon compensation refers to compensating for carbon emissions generated by individuals, organizations, or nations through supporting emission reduction or carbon sequestration projects, which is aiming to achieve ecological compensation that promotes the coordinated development of population, society, economy, resources, and environment9–11. Zoning for carbon compensation and carbon management refers to a governance approach that divides geographical spaces into distinct carbon management units based on regional resource endowments, carbon emission profiles, ecological carrying capacity, and development needs. It formulates differentiated strategies for emission reduction, carbon sequestration enhancement, and climate adaptation. Its significance lies not only in enhancing carbon reduction efficiency but also in fostering regional synergy and sustainable development. It is one of the important research topics in this field, which is beneficial for the implementation of coordinated regional management policies12–15.

The governance of carbon removal requires integrated and highly coordinated governmental functions and actions, as it spans multiple sectors, administrative levels, and national borders16. Urban agglomerations are the core space for regional development, and their concentrated growth facilitates long-term strategic planning17,18. Investigating the dynamics of carbon accounting and method for carbon zoning within urban agglomerations, in essence, aims to achieve "minimization of emission reduction costs and maximization of carbon sink benefits" through spatial reconstruction, and it contributes to establishing a coordinated development model encompassing large, medium, and small cities.

The Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration, a strategic hub in Southwest China, serves as an ideal case to address these research gaps. As a critical component of national development strategies and the intersection of the "One Belt and One Road" and the Yangtze River Economic Belt, the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration’s transition to low-carbon development is of paramount importance19. Its alpine topography and multi-ethnic context further underscore the need for tailored carbon management strategies that balance economic development with ecological preservation.

Moreover, county is the basic unit of urban and rural social and economic development in China, to further specifies and implements low-carbon design strategies and green development measures, considering the disparities in population, economy, resources, environment and other aspects among different cities at the county—scale level is of great significance for formulating fair and reasonable emission reduction plans10,11,20–22. However, limited research has been conducted on carbon accounting and compensation at the county scale.

A comprehensive technical framework for "carbon accounting-assessment-zoning" suitable for mountainous urban agglomerations has been developed, by integrating remote sensing inversion, nighttime light data, and a coupling coordination degree model, it conducts a county-level spatiotemporal analysis of carbon sequestration, carbon emissions, and the carbon offset effect in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration from 2000 to 2020. Subsequently, it develops an integrated carbon management zoning framework by synthesizing the carbon offset effect with a composite economic-social-ecological index and coupling coordination degree. The objectives are to: (1) elucidate the evolving spatial patterns of the carbon accounting; (2) identify distinct functional zones for carbon compensation; and (3) propose differentiated governance strategies. This research aims to provide a scientific basis for optimizing resource allocation and advancing the development of a carbon–neutral mountainous urban agglomeration.

This study innovates by conducting a fine-grained, practical county-scale spatiotemporal analysis of carbon accounting, which enables comparative carbon accounting across administrative units and is essential for crafting equitable and implementable low-carbon policies. Furthermore, it develops an integrated carbon compensation zoning framework that synthesizes social, economic, and ecological indicators to propose differentiated governance strategies. Through a systematic investigation of the unique environmental and urban development dynamics characteristic of these areas, this research not only advances our understanding of carbon management challenges and opportunities in mountainous settings but also offers critical insights and a demonstrative model for the development of effective carbon management strategies in similar geographies worldwide.

Methods

Study area

The Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration is located in Yunnan Province, China, which is a key area of the western development and a crucial nexus linking Southeast Asia and South Asia. The Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration covers 49 counties (cities and districts), including Kunming, Qujing, Yuxi, Chuxiong Yi Autonomous Prefecture and 7 counties (cities) in the north of Honghe Hani and Yi Autonomous Prefecture(Fig. 1). It spans a total land area of 111,400 km2, and the total forest area spans approximately 61,400 km2.By the end of 2020, the permanent population reached 21.49 million, the GDP was RMB 1507.3 billion yuan, the value of the primary sector, the secondary sector, and the tertiary sector amounts to RMB 158.2 billion yuan, 541.1 billion yuan and 802.6 billion yuan respectively.

Data sources

The indicators of economic development and security subsystems for 49 counties (cities, districts) within the Central Yunnan agglomeration from 2000 to 2020 were retrieved from the China Urban Statistical Yearbook and the Yunnan Statistical Yearbook. Meanwhile, energy emissions data in Yunnan province during the same period (2000–2020) were sourced from the Yunnan Statistical Yearbook.

The annual mean PM2.5 values from 2000 to 2020 were obtained from the Satellite-derived PM2.5 dataset, which was provided by the Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group at Washington University in St. Louis (https://sites.wustl.edu/acag/datasets/surface-pm2-5/#V5.GL.02). The green space data spanning from 2000 to 2020 were acquired from the Land Cover Dataset, as processed by Yang and Huang23.

For the NPP data, it was obtained by strictly following the user guides of the MOD17A3 data (https://search.earthdata.nasa.gov/search), which was provided by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA)24,25.

Regarding nighttime light data, the DMSP/OLS nighttime data from 2000 to 2011 were retrieved from the NOAA/NGDC website (https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/eog/dmsp.html), while the NPP-VIIRS nighttime data from 2012 to 2020 were sourced from the NOAA/NGDC website (https://www.ngdc.noaa.gov/eog/viirs/index.html).

The Mann–Kendall test27 was employed to quantify the significance of trends of carbon sequestration, carbon emissions, and the offset effect time series of each county in central Yunnan urban agglomeration. Results

Spatio-temporal variation of carbon sequestration

In the past two decades, the carbon sequestration of the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration has exhibited a fluctuating increasing trend (Fig. 2). From 2000 to 2020, the carbon sequestration increased from 44.7 million tons to 50.7 million tons, with an average annual growth rate of approximately 0.2 million tons. Substantial variations in carbon sequestration exist among different prefectures, with Chuxiong Prefecture exhibiting the highest level accounting for around 27% of the total carbon sequestration within the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration and maintaining an annual average carbon sequestration of 12.7 million tons. Conversely, Yuxi demonstrates comparatively lower levels of carbon sequestration constituting about 14% of the total carbon sequestration within this urban agglomeration and displaying an annual average carbon sequestration of 6.7 million tons.

Temporal variations of carbon sequestration in Central Yunnan urban agglomeration from 2000 to 2020.

The carbon sequestration of the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration exhibits a distribution characteristic of low in the middle-high around (Fig. 3), which is consistent with other major urban agglomerations, notably the Guangdong-Hong Kong-Macao Greater Bay Area8. It is essentially consistent with the distribution of forest coverage in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. The carbon sequestration of 15 counties (cities, districts) exceeded 1.2 million tons in 2000, with a total of 24.6 million tons, accounting for approximately 55% of the overall carbon sequestration in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration (Fig. 3a). In 2020, the number of counties (cities, districts) with carbon sequestration exceeding 1.2 million tons increased to 17, with a total carbon sequestration of 30.7 million tons, which accounted for approximately 61% of the overall carbon sequestration within the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration (Fig. 3b).

According to the analysis results of the carbon sequestration trend from 2000 to 2020 (Fig. 3c), most counties (cities, districts) exhibited an upward trend in carbon sequestration. The variation in carbon sequestration among counties (cities, districts) exhibits a distribution pattern that is high in the center and low around the periphery, while also being higher in the east and lower in the west. The average annual carbon sequestration growth of 7 counties (cities, districts) of Kunming City and Yuxi City in the central region of urban agglomeration are less than 0.0015 million tons/year. The central regions, such as Wuhua and Guandu, exhibit a higher proportion of construction land and a lower proportion of ecological land, resulting in a relatively diminished capacity for carbon sequestration. Xuanwei and Huize, located in Qujing City, exhibit the highest growth rate in carbon sequestration, surpassing 0.025 million tons/year. The annual carbon sequestration growth rate in the remaining 40 cities ranges from 0.0015 to 0.0135 million tons/year. The peripheral counties(districts) of Chuxiong, Dayao, Huize, and Xuanwei serve as crucial ecological barriers for the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. These regions experience minimal human interference, boast abundant ecological land and vegetation resources, and exhibit a robust capacity for carbon sequestration within their ecosystems. Carbon sequestration capacity of these regions has consistently grown from 2000 to 2020.

Spatio-temporal variation of carbon emissions

The total carbon emissions of the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration exhibited an upward trend between 2000 and 2020, surging from 17.2 million tons in 2000 to 48.2 million tons in 2020, it increased nearby 2 times. The average annual growth rate amounted to approximately 1.3 million tons (Fig. 4). The growth of carbon emissions can be roughly divided into two stages: the initial stage witnessed a rapid surge from 2000 to 2012, during which carbon emissions peaked at 53.4 million tons in 2012, with an average annual increase of 2.8 million tons. Subsequently, post-2012, carbon emissions exhibited relative stability, experiencing a gradual decline followed by a slight resurgence, with an average annual growth rate of 0.1 million tons.

Temporal variations of carbon emissions in Central Yunnan urban agglomeration from 2000 to 2020.

Significant regional disparities exist in carbon emissions, with Kunming being the highest contributor, accounting for approximately 57% of the total carbon emissions in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration, its annual average carbon emissions amount to 22.3 million tons/year. The Chuxiong Prefecture exhibits the lowest carbon emissions, constituting approximately 7% of the total carbon emissions within the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration, with annual average carbon emissions of 2.5 million tons/year.

The carbon emissions of counties in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration have consistently increased from 2000 to 2020, exhibiting a pronounced concentration in the eastern regions and a comparatively lower distribution in the western areas. The spatial distribution pattern of the multi-core structure, centered in the southern counties of Kunming, Chuxiong City in Chuxiong Prefecture, Hongta District in Yuxi City, and Qilin District in Qujing City, is becoming increasingly evident (Fig. 5). This is in line with the economic development and population distribution of the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. The rapid economic development, dense population, and extensive industrial land in these regions contribute to their high carbon emission rate during the study period. Relevant studies have demonstrated a positive correlation between carbon emissions and both GDP and population18,19,26,30,31. Furthermore, economic development and population growth serve as significant driving forces behind the escalating levels of carbon emissions17,19,32.

In 2000, the carbon emissions in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration were generally characterized by low levels (Fig. 5a). Only four districts in Kunming have carbon emissions exceeding 2 million tons, the total carbon emissions from these four districts reached 10.1 million tons, which accounts for 59% of the overall carbon emissions in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. Wuhua District of Kunming has the highest carbon emissions of 3.1 million tons.

The carbon emissions of all counties (cities, districts) in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration exhibited a collective increase in 2020 (Fig. 5b), characterized by a growing multi-core structure. The number of counties with carbon emissions exceeding 2 million tons has witnessed a significant increase, reaching a total of 9 counties. Among them, Kunming accounts for 6 counties, while Chuxiong, Qujing, and Yuxi each have one county.

The cumulative carbon emissions of the 9 counties reached a total of 28.4 million tons, constituting approximately 59% of the overall carbon emissions within the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. Notably, Guandu District in Kunming exhibited the highest level of carbon emission, amounting to 6.7 million tons.

According to the trend analysis results of carbon emissions (Fig. 5c), most cities exhibit an upward trajectory in carbon emissions, the carbon emission growth rates exhibit substantial variations across different counties (cities, districts). Chenggong and Guandu in Kunming demonstrate the highest growth rate of carbon emissions, surpassing 0.15 million tons/year. These counties are important transportation hubs of the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration, which possess favorable conditions for economic development. The rapid pace of economic development, the accelerated process of urbanization, and the exponential growth in population have collectively contributed to a substantial surge in energy demand and consumption, consequently leading to a significant increase in carbon emissions.

Spatio-temporal variation of offsetting effect

The carbon offset effect of each county (city, district) is assessed by quantifying the disparity between carbon sequestration and emissions. A positive disparity indicates carbon emissions are sufficiently offset by carbon sequestration, while a negative disparity signifies an excess of carbon emissions that cannot be adequately offset by carbon sequestration.

As shown in Fig. 6, although the overall carbon emissions of the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration continued to rise during the study period, this upward trend was offered by its carbon sequestration capacity. However, according to the results of trend analysis, there was an downward trend in the offset effect of carbon sequestration on carbon emissions in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration from 2000 to 2020, which decreased from 27.5 million tons to 2.5 million tons, showing an average annual decrease of 1.028 million tons.

Temporal variations of carbon offset effect in Central Yunnan urban agglomeration from 2000 to 2020.

During the study period, although the total carbon sequestration in the urban agglomeration of central Yunnan is able to offset the overall carbon emissions, there are evident spatial disparities in carbon offset effect among different counties (Fig. 7). The spatial pattern of carbon offset effects has evolved from a single-core structure in 2000 to a multi-core structure in 2020. Among the 39 cities studied, carbon offset effects showed a declining trend, with 17 cities experiencing statistically significant reductions in carbon offset capacity. The districts with negative carbon offset effect are mainly located in Kunming, the center of the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. In 2000, only 8 counties (cities, districts) exhibited a negative carbon offset effect, with the lowest recorded in Wuhua District of Kunming at − 2.972 million tons. In 2020, 17 counties (cities, districts) experienced negative carbon offset effects, with the lowest value observed in Guandu District of Kunming at − 6.495 million tons, representing approximately 2 times greater than that observed in 2000.

(a) Map of carbon offset effect in Central Yunnan urban agglomeration in 2000; (b) Map of carbon offset effect in Central Yunnan urban agglomeration in 2010; (c) Trend of carbon offset effect in Central Yunnan urban agglomeration from 2000 to 2020, the counties with bold labels have a trend that is significant at the 95% level, the counties with italics labels did not reach a significant level.

The regions with negative carbon offset effects are characterized by rapid economic development, high population density, and extensive industrial land use. These factors collectively drive up carbon emissions and accelerate their growth rate. Additionally, these districts feature a large proportion of construction land and relatively scarce ecological land, presenting significant challenges in effectively offsetting carbon emissions through natural carbon sequestration processes.

In contrast, counties such as Yongren, Luquan, and Fuyuan, located on the northern periphery of the urban agglomeration, experience slower economic growth and have smaller populations. As a result, their energy demands and carbon emissions remain low. With limited construction land and abundant ecological areas, human activities in these regions have minimal impact on the natural environment. The local ecosystems possess a strong carbon sequestration capacity, enabling them to efficiently offset carbon emissions.

Such significant spatial heterogeneity in carbon offset capacity within the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration, underscoring the need for differentiated carbon responsibility allocation. This aligns with global-scale findings, Hahn, et al.33 demonstrate substantial disparities in national carbon budget appropriation and accountability due to varying historical emissions and development paths. These consistent patterns across scales confirm that the principle of "common but differentiated responsibilities" is fundamental to equitable climate governance, from global and national levels down to regional urban agglomerations.

Integrated zoning for carbon compensation and management

The composite index of economic, social, and ecological factors and the degree of coupling coordination

Economic development index, social security index, ecological environment index and composite index of the economic development, social security, and ecological environment subsystems of 49 counties (cities, districts) within the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration for the year 2020 have been calculated. The economic index ranges from 0.056 to 0.854, with an average of 0.194, exhibiting a spatial distribution characterized by higher values in the southern regions and lower values in the northern regions (Fig. 8a).

(a) Map of economic development index of Central Yunnan urban agglomeration in 2020; (b) Map of social security index of Central Yunnan urban agglomeration in 2020; (c) Map of ecological environment index of Central Yunnan urban agglomeration in 2020; (d) Map of composite index of Central Yunnan urban agglomeration in 2020; (e) Map of “economic-social-ecological” coupling coordination degree of Central Yunnan urban agglomeration in 2020.

The economically advantageous areas of the urban agglomeration are predominantly concentrated in the central urban areas of Kunming, Yuxi, Qujing, Honghe, and Chuxiong. The aforementioned areas exhibit evident advantages in terms of per capita GDP, while simultaneously demonstrating high levels of economic efficiency and energy efficiency. Conversely, the northeastern part of urban agglomeration exhibits the lowest economic performance according to the economic index.

The social security index exhibits a wide range of values (Fig. 8b), spanning from 0.026 to 0.886, with an average value of 0.153. The spatial distribution exhibits an eastward high-westward low pattern. The advantageous areas of the social index within the urban agglomeration are mainly distribute in its central region, and in the central urban areas of Chuxiong, Qujing, Yuxi, and Honghe. The aforementioned districts possess robust financial backing, an abundant supply of educational institutions, healthcare facilities, and other essential resources, as well as relatively convenient living conditions. The disadvantaged areas of the social index are primarily situated in the peripheral county units of Chuxiong, which exhibit inadequate provision of public service infrastructure.

The ecological environment index of the entire central Yunnan urban agglomeration exhibits a high level (Fig. 8c), between 0.411 and 0.953, with an average value of 0.764. Its spatial pattern characterized by higher values in the northern regions and lower values in the southern regions, as well as lower values in the peripheral areas and higher values in the central areas. The advantageous areas of the ecological index in the urban agglomeration are primarily distributed in the northeastern and northwestern regions, which boast abundant forests, lakes, and other ecological resources. These areas serve as a crucial guarantee for the ecological security of the urban agglomeration.

The disadvantageous areas are primarily distributed in the central regions of urban agglomerations. These counties (cities, districts) exhibit a low per capita green land area, carbon emissions and discharge of sewage have exerted significant adverse impacts on the regional ecological environment during the process of economic development.

The composite index of "economy-society-ecology" exhibits a spatial pattern characterized by being "high in the central and low in the peripheral areas" (Fig. 8d). The average value of the composite index is 0.371, with the highest value reaching 0.78 and the lowest value recorded at 0.249. There are 12 counties (cities, districts) that above average level, most of them are situated in the central part of the urban agglomeration, located in Kunming City, or in the government residences of Qujing City, Yuxi City, and Chuxiong Prefecture. These counties (cities, districts) possess social security advantages, thereby conferring them with an advantageous position in regional comprehensive evaluation. There are 37 counties below the average, exhibiting a low overall score due to their deficiencies in one or more aspects of ecological environment, social security, and economic development.

The coupling coordination degree of "economy-society-ecology" in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration had an average value of 0.489 in 2020, with a range from 0.347 to 0.877, indicating a mild imbalance in coupling coordination (Fig. 8e). The spatial distribution of urban agglomeration’s coupling coordination degree exhibits a pattern of "higher in the central and low in the peripheral regions". The counties (cities, districts) with a coupling coordination degree greater than 0.5 exhibit a state of coordinated development. The coupling coordination degree of 15 counties (cities, districts) exceeds 0.5, with Kunming accounting for 8, and Qujing, Yuxi, Chuxiong, and Honghe accounting for 1, 2, 1, and 3 respectively. These counties (cities, districts) have advantages in economic development and social security in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration, resulting in a relatively high degree of coupling coordination among the economy, society, and ecology subsystems. The counties (cities, districts) with a coupling coordination degree less than 0.5 are characterized by an uncoordinated development status. There are 34 counties (districts, cities) with a coupling coordination degree of less than 0.5. The composite index of "Economic-Social-Ecological" represents the integrated development status of the counties (cities, districts), while the coupling coordination degree represents the level of coordination among economic development, social security, and ecological environment subsystems. The composite index will be high when the economic, social, and ecological indicators are all at a high level. The coupling coordination degree is high when the differences of the three index values are small, and vice versa. The Yunnan Province features a subtropical plateau monsoon climate, characterized by warm and humid conditions with abundant rainfall. This climatic condition is highly conducive to vegetation growth and endows the region with significant natural resource advantages. The ecological environment indexes of counties (cities, districts) in the urban agglomeration of central Yunnan are consistently at a high level. The Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration ranks 17th out of the 19 urban agglomerations in China in terms of GDP and per capita GDP, indicating a significant development gap. Therefore, the ecological index value of most counties (cities, districts) surpasses that of the economic and social indices, indicating a mild imbalance in coupling coordination within the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. The low coupling coordination degree in these regions can be attributed to the immense pressure from economic development and social security concerns; however, it is worth noting that these areas possess abundant ecological resources.

Integrated zoning for carbon compensation and management

The integrated zoning for carbon compensation and management is established based on carbon offset effect, the economic-social-ecological composite index, and the coupling coordination degree. It consists of two main components. Firstly, based on the carbon offset effect, it is categorized into the carbon payment zone, carbon balance zone and carbon benefit zone with decreasing priority in terms of carbon management. Secondly, based on the composite index and coupling coordination degree of economic-social-ecological factors, it is divided into the carbon emission reduction zone, carbon control zone, and carbon optimization zone, also with a decreasing priority in terms of carbon management. According to that, in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration, there are six distinct zones for carbon compensation and management as depicted in Fig. 9.

Integrated zoning for carbon compensation and management in Central Yunnan urban agglomeration.

There are 8 districts designated as the payment-carbon-emission reduction zone, including 6 districts in Kunming, 1 district in Yuxi and 1 district in Qujing, most of them are located in the central of urban agglomeration. The carbon offset effect of this zone is negative, ranges from − 6.5 to − 1.6 million tons, while its "economic-social-ecological" composite index and coupling coordination degree are high. Aim of this zone is maintaining residents’ living standards, efforts should be made to reduce regional carbon emissions. These districts are either the main urban areas of Kunming or the government seats of Yuxi and Qujing, they play a pivotal role as a comprehensive transportation hub in the region, offering abundant prospects for further development. In 2020, the GDP of these 8 districts constituted 35% of the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration’s total GDP, while the secondary industry in these 8 districts contributes 52% to the overall added value of the secondary industry in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. Due to their rapid economic development, high population density, and ongoing urban expansion, these regions experience an increase in contribution areas accompanied by a decrease in ecological land. Consequently, their capacity for carbon sequestration is low and fails to offset carbon emissions. Therefore, these areas fall under the category of the payment-carbon-emission reduction zone.

The payment-carbon-controlled zone encompass 6 districts and counties, namely Songming, Jinning, Chengjiang around the payment area, and Kaiyuan, Mengzi, and Chuxiong in the southern or western edge of the urban agglomeration. The carbon offset effect of this zone is negative too, ranges from − 0.8 to − 0.2 million tons. Its carbon offset effect outperforms that of the payment-carbon-emission reduction zone, yet its carbon sequestration capacity remains insufficient to fully offset its carbon emissions. And its "economic-social-ecological" composite index is lower than 0.4, while the coupling coordination degree is higher than 0.5. The optimization of development strategy seeks to achieve carbon balance while driving economic, social, and environmental advancement.

The three counties (districts) have been designated as the payment-carbon-optimized zone, including Tonghai of Yuxi, Luliang and Malong in Qujing. Their carbon offset effects are close to equilibrium but still remain negative, range from − 0.125 to − 0.052 tons. Unlike the payment-carbon-controlled zone, the payment-carbon-optimized zone demonstrates not only lower "economic-social-ecological" composite index but also weaker coupling coordination degree. Therefore, while implementing emission reduction measures, this region must emphasize coordinated development among social, economic, and ecological dimensions.

Only Gejiu City is classified as balanced-carbon-controlled zone, which is characterized by a fundamental equilibrium between carbon sequestration and carbon emissions. Moreover, its "economy-society-ecology" composite index is lower than 0.4 and coupling coordination degree exhibit a high level of performance. Building upon the existing advantages in carbon balance, these districts have the potential to enhance carbon design strategies and achieve sustainable long-term low-carbon development.

The balanced-carbon-optimized zone encompass 17 counties (cities, districts), including Dongchuan, Fumin, Shilin and others, they are primarily distributed in the northwestern and eastern fringes of urban agglomeration, as well as along the peripheries of payment zones. These counties (cities, districts) exhibit lower composite index of "economy-society-ecology" and coupling coordination degree, it is essential for these counties (cities, districts) to preserve the existing carbon balance advantages while promoting coordinated development across economic, social, and environmental dimensions.

The benefits-carbon-optimized zone encompass 14 counties (cities, districts), they are primarily distributed on the periphery of the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. In 2020, these counties achieved an impressive average carbon offset effect of 19.804 million tons, resulting in substantial carbon income. Despite their low "economic-social-ecological" composite index, there is potential to enhance economic and social service facilities while preserving the existing advantage in carbon sequestration. This zone is distributed in the periphery of the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration, with a large ecological land area, bordered by the Jinsha River basin ecological protection zone to the north, the Ailao Mountain and Wuliangshan ecological barrier as well as the Honghe River basin ecological protection zone to the southwest, and the Nanpan River basin ecological protection zone to the east. The forest area of this zone constitutes approximately 53% of the total forest area within the entire central Yunnan urban agglomeration, exhibiting a robust capacity for carbon sequestration. The economy of the region is relatively underdeveloped, characterized by a dispersed population, resulting in low carbon demand and carbon emissions. The carbon sequestration it achieves is sufficient to compensate for carbon emissions. However, carbon emissions of this zone have a lower contribution to economic development, and its social security facilities are relatively limited.

Discussion

The carbon zoning results of this study provide governance insights for allocating carbon removal responsibilities, which are highly complementary to the discontinuous fair-share allocation framework proposed by Robiou du Pont et al.34. Their work quantifies different combinations of responsibility and capability principles for distributing positive and negative emissions, providing a critical ethical and modeling foundation for defining the core question of 'who should pay for carbon removal’.

The significant differences in the social and economic development levels among different counties (cities, districts) have led to substantial disparities in carbon emission productivity. Zoning of carbon compensation and carbon management can clearly define the division of carbon responsibilities among regions in terms of space, and implement differentiated low-carbon strategies for each division9,12–14. The spatially clarified classification of carbon compensation management types enables the implementation of nearby carbon trading according to the differences in regional carbon responsibilities.

The payment-carbon-emission reduction zone bear the highest-priority mission of emission reduction in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration. These districts possess perfect infrastructure and abundant social security resources, their economic and social indices far surpass those of other counties (cities, districts), thus resulting in a higher composite index and coupling coordination degree. Consequently, they are currently experiencing a phase of coordinated development. These regions need to systematically promote low-carbon transformation while ensuring the stability of social development and the quality of residents’ lives. Firstly, strengthen the top-level design, strictly set the threshold for industrial carbon emission intensity31, reshape the industrial structure by phasing out backward production capacities and promoting energy-saving and carbon-reducing technologies4,35,36. Simultaneously, establish a production system centered around low-carbon energy sources and environmentally friendly materials to cut pollutant emissions from the source32. The payment-carbon-controlled zone and the payment-carbon optimized zone both have relatively low composite indices, but the former is better coordinated. To boost economic development and achieve a reasonable distribution of carbon pressure, the extension of industrial chain upgrading can be pursued. Additionally, improving infrastructure in the payment-carbon optimized zone will enhance coordination among economic growth, social welfare, and ecological conservation.

Relying on the carbon zoning framework, scientifically plan the industrial layout, and orderly transfer some industries from the payment zones to carbon balance zones (such as Jinning and Fumin). At the same time, leverage the economic advantages of the payment-carbon-emission reduction zone, establish a horizontal ecological compensation mechanism, and guide high-energy-consuming enterprises within the areas to achieve emission reduction goals by purchasing carbon sinks. For example, Chuxiong Yi Autonomous Prefecture is rich in forest resources but lags in economic development. High-carbon enterprises can subscribe to its forestry carbon sink indicators, which not only fulfills their emission reduction responsibilities but also helps the economic growth of ethnic minority areas.

For ecologically sensitive areas such as Dianchi Lake, strictly demarcate the carbon sink red line in the lake buffer zone, prohibit the layout of high-water-consuming and high-emission industries (such as the phosphorus chemical industry), and vigorously promote wetland ecological restoration projects. At the same time, strengthen the construction of the urban forest system, expand the extent of nature reserves, and enhance the overall carbon sequestration capacity of the city. In addition, through policy guidance and publicity and education, cultivate residents’ low-carbon consumption concepts, and promote green travel modes such as new energy vehicles, shared bicycles, or walking, to form a good atmosphere of the whole society’s participation in emission reduction.

The balanced-carbon zones, with the dynamic balance between carbon sequestration and carbon emissions, take economic integration development as the core task and make every effort to upgrade the social security system. The counties adjacent to the payment-carbon zones, relying on their geographical advantages and resource endowments, give priority to undertaking industrial transfers, deepen regional economic linkage, drive employment growth and the improvement of public services, and achieve the coordinated development of the economy and social security.

Based on the carbon zoning plan, focus on cultivating green industrial chains. For example, guide the counties within balance zone of Yuxi to focus on low-carbon equipment manufacturing fields such as new energy batteries and carbon capture equipment, and create a highland for green technology export facing South Asia and Southeast Asia. At the same time, give priority support to counties (cities, districts) in the balanced-carbon-optimized zone with relatively weak economic and social security foundations, such as Eshan and Yimen. Through policy preferences and resource integration, promote their leapfrog development. In addition, areas with convenient transportation and balanced development, such as Gejiu in the balanced-carbon-controlled zone, give full play to the advantages of coordinated economic, social and ecological development. Relying on unique climate and forest resources, build ecological and livable cities with Yunnan characteristics and low-carbon concepts, attract population agglomeration, and promote the construction of urban ecological civilization12,14,37.

Most of the counties in the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration are classified to the benefits-carbon optimized zone, which is rich in carbon sink resources. Through innovative development models, they transform ecological advantages into economic momentum and achieve a virtuous cycle between regional development and ecological protection. Based on the carbon zoning frame, establish a multi-level carbon compensation trading network, and encourage enterprises and local governments to promote the market-oriented allocation of ecological resources through means such as carbon credit trading within the province, between provinces, and international carbon sink cooperation, such as the "mutual recognition of carbon credits along the China—Laos Railway".

In terms of infrastructure construction, make overall plans for the new energy transportation corridor, improve the layout of the intercity fast charging network, optimize the regional transportation system, and promote public transportation and intelligent transportation systems to significantly reduce carbon emissions in the transportation sector38. At the same time, strengthen regional communication, deeply tap the potential of ecological resources, create a carbon sink—ecological tourism industry cluster, and launch characteristic products such as carbon sink-themed research tours and carbon neutral travel to attract domestic and foreign tourists and promote the green upgrading of the tourism industry8.

In terms of industrial development, strongly support the benefits-carbon-optimized zone to explore new ecological economic models. For example, promote the composite business model of "under-forest economy with carbon sink", combine carbon sink monitoring with mushroom cultivation and Chinese medicinal material cultivation to achieve a win–win situation of ecological and economic benefits. Relying on mountainous resources, build distributed photovoltaic and energy storage microgrids to gradually replace traditional coal-fired power supply. Taking Qujing’s "hydropower-aluminum and photovoltaic" integration project as a demonstration, make full use of Yunnan’s abundant hydropower resources to promote the zero-carbon transformation of key industries, cultivate new growth points of the low-carbon economy, and ultimately achieve the coordinated progress of regional development and ecological protection.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered. First, a key methodological constraint arises from our use of NPP. While this approach captures the gross carbon sequestration potential of all vegetation, it does not represent the net ecosystem carbon sink, which is determined by Net Ecosystem Productivity (NEP = NPP—Heterotrophic Respiration). As heterotrophic respiration releases a significant portion of fixed carbon, our estimates overstate the region’s actual net sequestration intensity. This critical distinction reinforces that climate strategies cannot rely solely on natural sinks but must prioritize deep industrial emission reductions.

Second, the scope of our assessment is intentionally focused. While our NPP-based estimates inherently include both natural and planted forests, they cannot distinguish their individual contributions. Furthermore, engineered carbon removal approaches (such as technical ways of carbon capture) and indirect carbon reduction through ecosystem services (such as energy-saving effects and water-saving effects) are excluded due to the current lack of systematic, county-level deployment data8,16.

Our approach directly addresses the implementation gap by providing a foundational baseline that enables initial policy interventions while being deliberately designed for iterative refinement as data availability improves. Future research should therefore focus on integrating soil respiration observations to enable NEP-based accounting and on developing frameworks that incorporate technological carbon removal pathways, thereby progressively enhancing the accuracy and comprehensiveness of regional carbon neutrality planning.

Conclusions

This paper calculates the dynamic changes in carbon sequestration, carbon emissions, and carbon offset effects within the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration from 2000 to 2020. The results indicate a consistent upward trend in both carbon sequestration and carbon emissions within the Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration during the study period, with growth rates of 0.2 million tons/year and 1.3 million tons/year, respectively. The growth rate of carbon emissions significantly outpaces carbon sequestration capacity, so the carbon offset effect has shown a significant decline, with a mean annual decrease of 1.028 million tons. Although the carbon sequestration in entire central Yunnan urban agglomeration can effectively offset carbon emissions, significant disparities in carbon offset effect were observed across counties (cities, districts). The carbon offset effect exhibits a multi-core structure characterized by lower central values and higher peripheral performance. To accomplish 'dual carbon goals’, the reasonable planning of carbon zoning is of paramount importance for formulating carbon strategies and implementing schemes tailored to local conditions. Therefore, this paper assesses the actual effectiveness of carbon offset and establishes a comprehensive carbon zoning evaluation system that considers factors of economic development, social security, and ecological environment. The Central Yunnan Urban Agglomeration is categorized into six zones: The payment-carbon-emission reduction zone, the payment-carbon-controlled zone, the payment-carbon-optimized zone, the balanced-carbon-controlled zone, the balanced-carbon-optimized zone, the benefits-carbon-optimized zone, and the priority of carbon management in the above zones decreases sequentially. Furthermore, this paper proposes distinct management strategies tailored to each zone. The method in this paper can be referenced, our findings provide valuable insights for zoning management and regional coordinated development. It holds significant implications for achieving a new paradigm of high-quality development.
