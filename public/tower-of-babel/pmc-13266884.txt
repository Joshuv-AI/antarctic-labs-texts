Systemic hypothermia in non-aortic cardiac surgery: a narrative review
Authors: Daniel Kyrillos Ragheb, Nika Samadzadeh Tabrizi, Anthony L Zaki, Alexander D Shapeton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveSystemic hypothermia is commonly used in most cardiac operations in order to reduce metabolic demand and augment both myocardial and other end-organ protection. With modern improvements in perfusion and protection strategies, some surgeons now routinely perform non-aortic surgery under normothermic conditions. However, there is limited evidence and conflicting reports on the “optimal” temperature conditions for cardiac surgery, resulting in a paucity of guidelines and a wide variety in practice. We aim to give a thorough review of the current evidence comparing hypothermia and normothermia in cardiac surgery.MethodsA narrative review was conducted comparing studies that involved hypothermia and/or normothermia within cardiac surgery. Search terms included: temperature cardiac surgery, temperature cardiopulmonary bypass, normothermia surgery, hypothermia surgery, normothermia cardiac, hypothermia cardiac, normothermia cardiopulmonary bypass. Applicable manuscripts and abstracts from PubMed during the timeframe of 1/1/1990 to 1/1/2025 were included, including a range of experimental, observational, prospective, and randomized studies. In addition, prior narrative reviews and meta-analyses were included. Evidence was collected and synthesized across bleeding/transfusion need, neurologic outcomes, arrhythmias, renal function, inflammation and wound healing, and anesthetic pharmacology.Key Content and FindingsCooling effectively decreases metabolic rate and provides additional end-organ protection. Large, observational datasets have reported limited but meaningful improvements in short- and long-term survival with hypothermia. However, more modern studies across observational and randomized cohorts evaluating individual organ outcomes show limited differences between hypothermia and normothermia in cardiac surgery patients. The most meaningful difference is consistently demonstrated in greater coagulopathy in hypothermic patients. Neurologic and renal outcomes in hypothermia are conflicting between studies, with no consistent difference. There is likely a modest impact of hypothermia on worsening wound healing and higher arrhythmogenicity. Lastly, hypothermia has a known prolongation of effects and clearance of anesthetics, but in a predictable manner.ConclusionsNo single temperature strategy is universally superior for non-aortic cardiac surgery. Hypothermia has benefits in myocardial and end-organ protection, but with a cost in coagulopathy. Temperature management should be individualized, balancing these two considerations with the modest impacts noted in renal function, neurologic outcomes, wound healing, and anesthetics.Keywords: Hypothermia, normothermia, temperature management, cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB)

Introduction

Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) is a critical component of most cardiac surgeries, allowing diversion of blood away from the heart and lungs while preserving vital organ perfusion. In addition, many cardiac surgeries require a still and bloodless surgical field, which is achieved with cold cardioplegic arrest. This induces diastolic arrest and lowers myocardial oxygen demand by reducing contractility and ventricular distention. Together, CPB and cold cardioplegia maintain systemic perfusion while protecting the myocardium through cooling, decompression, and electrical silence.

Systemic hypothermia is often used as an adjunct to CPB and cold cardioplegia to further enhance myocardial protection (1-4). Lower body temperatures reduce metabolic demand, providing additional organ protection to vulnerable organs, particularly the brain and kidneys. Beyond the traditional “mild” or “moderate” hypothermia, “deep hypothermia” can be employed in selected settings. In a review of 490 consecutive patients undergoing aortic surgery, deep hypothermic circulatory arrest at a mean bladder temperature of 18.7 ℃ provided cerebral protection for a limited duration even without adjunctive perfusion strategies (5). Additionally, in patients with aortic insufficiency or significant coronary artery disease, antegrade cardioplegia may not effectively reach myocardial territories. Thus, cardiac protection may be reduced in comparison to those with patent coronaries and a competent aortic valve. In such instances, the benefit of hypothermia, namely, reduction of metabolic demand, may be especially beneficial.

While hypothermia during CPB offers physiological and clinical benefits, it is also associated with drawbacks such as coagulopathy, altered anesthetic pharmacodynamics, and variable effects on end-organ function. Advances in perfusion techniques, myocardial protection strategies, and intraoperative monitoring have enabled many centers to perform routine cardiac surgery, such as coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), under normothermic conditions without apparent compromise in safety or efficacy. This shift has reignited debate over whether hypothermia continues to offer measurable benefits in contemporary cardiac surgery. Existing studies comparing normothermia and hypothermia report inconsistent and often conflicting outcomes, and currently, no formal guidelines define the optimal temperature strategy during CPB (6). However, it is important to note that current guidelines do provide guidance on other aspects of temperature management, including keeping maximum target arterial blood temperature below 37 ℃ and gradients between the arterial outflow and venous inflow below 10 ℃.

Objectives

This narrative review examines current evidence comparing hypothermic and normothermic CPB strategies in non-aortic cardiac surgery to clarify their impact on patient outcomes and inform optimal intraoperative temperature management. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0212/rc).

Methods

A narrative review was conducted comparing studies that involved hypothermia and/or normothermia within cardiac surgery. Search terms included: temperature cardiac surgery, temperature cardiopulmonary bypass, normothermia surgery, hypothermia surgery, normothermia cardiac, hypothermia cardiac, normothermia cardiopulmonary bypass (Table 1). Applicable manuscripts and abstracts from PubMed during the timeframe of 1/1/1990 to 1/1/2025 were included, including a range of experimental, observational, prospective, and randomized studies. In addition, prior narrative reviews and meta-analyses were included. Evidence was collected and synthesized across bleeding/transfusion need, neurologic outcomes, arrhythmias, renal function, inflammation and wound healing, and anesthetic pharmacology.

Current trends

Traditionally, systemic hypothermia during CPB is achieved at 28–34 ℃ to reduce whole-body oxygen consumption and mitigate ischemic injury, with metabolic rate decreasing by approximately 6–7% per ℃ drop in core temperature (7,8). This physiologic effect may directly correspond to a survival benefit, as demonstrated in a study by Hendriks et al. of 5,672 patients undergoing CABG over a period of 18 years (9). They found that patients undergoing mild hypothermia (defined as 32–35 ℃) had the highest 30-day (98%) and 5-year survival (88%), substantially exceeding outcomes in the normothermia group (93% and 72%, respectively).

This survival benefit is likely mediated by mechanisms at the cellular level. In porcine models, hypothermia has been demonstrated to significantly reduce cardiac lymphatic flow, myocardial necrosis, and myocardial cell abnormalities (1). The same study also showed decreased synthesis of tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNFα), an inflammatory cytokine, and a lower concentration of cardiac troponin I, a marker of myocardial injury. Reduced TNFα production was linked to decreased cytotoxic effects on cardiomyocytes, resulting in less cell membrane rupture, interstitial edema, and necrosis. The authors hypothesize that this may partially explain the cardioprotective effects of hypothermia.

Hypothermia has also been associated with adverse outcomes, including prolonged action of neuromuscular blockade and anesthetic agents (10-12), arrhythmia (13-15), myocardial stunning (16), impaired platelet function and coagulopathy (17-19), high inflammatory response (20), worse neurologic outcomes (21,22), and reduced renal perfusion and renal dysfunction (23,24). In a propensity-matched study of over 6,500 patients undergoing isolated CABG, isolated valve, or combined CABG and valve surgeries from 2011 to 2018, Bianco and colleagues at the University of Pittsburgh compared outcomes between mild hypothermia (32–35 ℃) and normothermia (>35 ℃) during CPB (25). The study found no significant differences in postoperative stroke, readmission, reoperation, and long-term survival over a median follow-up of 4.4 years. However, hypothermia was associated with a higher prevalence of acute renal failure and longer intensive care unit stays, even after accounting for differences in patient demographics and surgical complexity (25). A larger study of 142,541 patients from the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Adult Cardiac Surgery Database compared those undergoing moderate (≤34 ℃), mild (>34 ℃), and no hypothermia (>36 ℃). Authors found that hypothermia, both mild and moderate, was protective against operative mortality when compared to normothermia (26). Given these conflicting findings, there remain practice variations in the utilization of hypothermia in routine non-aortic surgery.

Thus, the benefits and risks of hypothermia remain controversial. Overall, systemic hypothermia during routine and low-risk cardiac surgery continues to be widely practiced. A Canadian survey reported that 94% of centers used hypothermia for low-risk cases and 97% for high-risk cases (27). Similarly, analysis of the STS Database found that 96.5% of surgeons routinely performed CABG under hypothermic conditions (26).

Coagulopathy and transfusion requirements

Hypothermia has been consistently associated with a higher risk of bleeding and allogenic blood transfusions (28). Specifically, hypothermia can adversely affect multiple components of the coagulation cascade, including platelet adhesion, thrombin generation, and fibrinogen availability (19,29,30). As temperature decreases, coagulation factor activity and platelet function become progressively impaired, resulting in delayed clot formation (18). Additional mechanistic insights have been provided by Mitrophanov et al., who used computational modeling in human subjects to demonstrate a dose-dependent prolongation in thrombin generation, clotting, thrombin peak, and prothrombin times (31,32).

Studies of human whole blood further corroborate the impact of temperature on the coagulation cascade. Whelihan et al. isolated venous blood from healthy humans and measured thrombin generation at 37, 32, and 27 ℃ (30). In this study, fibrinogen consumption and factor XIII activation were greatest at 37 ℃, with a stepwise decline. Fibrin formation similarly decreased with cooling. Using thromboelastography, the same study demonstrated slower clot formation at colder temperatures. Another thromboelastography-based study further supports these findings, showing greater postoperative hypocoagulopathy among patients with lower nadir temperatures (33). These laboratory abnormalities translate into changes in standard clinical coagulation measures. In an early study, Rohrer and Natale evaluated pooled plasma at 37, 34, 31, and 28 ℃ and observed a stepwise prolongation of both prothrombin time and partial thromboplastin time with each decrement in temperature (34).

Platelet dysfunction is another key contributor of hypothermia-induced coagulopathy. Studies from as early as the start of the 1990’s demonstrated that this effect is largely mediated by impaired platelet aggregation due to reduced thromboxane A3 release, which is necessary for formation of the initial “platelet plug” observed in normal coagulation (35,36). Hypothermia-induced platelet dysfunction appears to be only partially reversible with rewarming (37).

The relationship between temperature and coagulopathy appears to be dose-dependent, with colder temperatures—more aggressive hypothermia—correlating with worse clinical outcomes. In a study of 245 patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery, intermediate (31–33 ℃) or moderate (28–30 ℃) hypothermia was associated with greater blood loss and transfusion needs compared to mild hypothermia (34–36 ℃) {mild: reference; intermediate [odds ratio (OR): 4.5]; moderate (OR: 5.82)} (38). Patients in the lower temperature groups also required greater supportive measures, including blood products, inotropes, and ventilatory support. This study demonstrates an observational difference between temperature groups. However, given the observational design, lack of randomization, and potential confounding by surgical complexity, causality could not be definitively established.

The impact of hypothermia on clinical outcomes can be quite impressive. A meta-analysis demonstrated that even a 1 ℃ drop in temperature was linked to a significant increase in blood loss [~16%, 95% confidence interval (CI): 4–26%] and transfusion needs (~22%, 95% CI: 3–37%) (39). In another meta-analysis of 44 studies involving 6,731 patients undergoing hypothermia or normothermia, Ho and Tan demonstrated that hypothermia was associated with higher transfusion of red blood cells [relative risk (RR) 1.19], fresh frozen plasma (RR 1.54), and platelets (RR 2.53) (28). In off-pump CABG, when intraoperative efforts were taken to limit hypothermia, patients required fewer red blood cell transfusions, achieved earlier extubation, had shorter intensive care unit and hospital stays, and had less chest tube drainage when compared to patients without similar temperature control strategies (40). Similar patterns have been observed during hypothermic circulatory arrest, where platelet dysfunction and impaired coagulation cascade can exacerbate perioperative bleeding and transfusion requirements (18). In contrast, the aforementioned study by Bianco and colleagues showed that although cardiac surgical patients undergoing mild hypothermia had higher blood transfusion rates, the difference was not statistically significant (25).

Neurologic outcomes

Stroke and other neurologic complications remain among the most devastating complications following cardiac surgery. Systemic cooling is thought to provide neuroprotection by reducing the brain’s metabolic demand, whereas patients undergoing active warming during bypass via water-bath heater-cooler to maintain normothermia may be subject to higher rates of new, postoperative central neurologic deficits. In a randomized study of 138 patients undergoing CABG, those managed with hypothermia (<28 ℃) had significantly fewer new postoperative central neurologic deficits compared to patients undergoing active warming (>35 ℃) (41). However, the process of cooling and rewarming in itself may adversely lead to edema, disturbances in cerebral perfusion, and neurologic deficits (42). In concordance with these conflicting data, more recent meta-analyses have not demonstrated a significant difference in stroke rates between hypothermic and normothermic strategies, with insufficient evidence to link temperature management in either direction with perioperative stroke (43,44).

Postoperative neurocognitive outcomes are another topic of interest, with most studies reporting no meaningful differences between hypothermia and normothermia (28,45,46). Even when differences have been observed, they often fail to reach statistical significance. For instance, Wong et al. found improved postoperative neuropsychological test performance in patients undergoing normothermic CPB in comparison to hypothermic CPB, but statistical significance was achieved in only one of seven tests performed (21).

Additional limited evidence supports neurologic benefits of normothermia. In a prospective study of 144 patients randomized 1:1 to normothermic (36 ℃) or hypothermic (32 ℃) CPB, cognitive processing was assessed using a P300 auditory-evoked potential. Patients undergoing hypothermic CPB had prolonged potentials and impaired cognitive processing at both one week and four months (47). Another study observed a similar trend toward higher stroke rates (1.3% versus 1.0%) among patients undergoing hypothermia, although this difference was not statistically significant (22).

Many of the purported benefits of normothermia have also been demonstrated in hypothermic CPB. For example, in a randomized trial conducted from 1995 to 1998, patients over 60 years undergoing CABG were cooled to 32 ℃ and then either maintained at this temperature (32 ℃) or rewarmed immediately to 37 ℃ (48). Patients who sustained hypothermia had improved neurocognitive performance compared to the rewarming group at one week postoperatively, and this difference persisted at three months (48). A follow-up study of the same cohort confirmed modest but sustained neurocognitive benefits in the hypothermia group (49). With conflicting reports and only modest evidence in either direction, these data indicate that there is no definitive evidence to suggest that hypothermia is consistently associated with either neurologic deficits or improvements after cardiac surgery.

Arrhythmias

Temperature management during CPB may also influence arrhythmogenicity and the risk of new-onset postoperative atrial fibrillation. Physiologic studies suggest that mild hypothermia can increase susceptibility to arrhythmias (15), with experimental rabbit models confirming associated electrochemical alterations (13). However, clinical data involving patients undergoing cardiac surgery remains limited and equivocal (28). One study suggests that lower temperatures may be disadvantageous (14). In a randomized single-center study, Adams et al. compared patients undergoing mild (34 ℃) and moderate (28 ℃) hypothermia during CPB for CABG and found a higher prevalence of postoperative atrial fibrillation among those undergoing moderate hypothermia (49% versus 22%) (14). Notably, this study dates to 2000, and the degree of “moderate” hypothermia studied reflects cooling well below contemporary norms. Ultimately, clinical evidence remains sparse and inconclusive.

Renal function

Renal impairment is another important postoperative complication following cardiac surgery. In a prospective study of 1,072 patients, Kourliouros and colleagues used multivariate regression and propensity adjustment to demonstrate that lower arterial CPB temperatures during first-time CABG were independently associated with a higher risk of acute renal injury (23). In contrast, another study reported a numeric difference in the other direction, with a higher prevalence of significant renal dysfunction among patients undergoing normothermia (37 ℃) compared to mild hypothermia (34 ℃) (17% versus 9%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (24). An in-depth discussion of rewarming is outside the scope of this discussion, but it is worth noting that active rewarming to 37 ℃ is well known to be associated with worsened renal outcomes (24). Overall, evidence supporting a renoprotective effect of normothermia remains limited.

Inflammation and healing

Temperature may also influence systemic inflammation. Outside of cardiac surgery, intraoperative hypothermia has been clearly linked to poor wound healing. This relationship was most clearly established in a randomized controlled trial of 200 patients undergoing non-cardiac surgery at 34.7 versus 36.6 ℃, in which normothermic patients had a substantially lower rate of surgical site infections (50). As a result, maintenance of normothermia to reduce surgical site infections has been incorporated into the World Health Organization recommendations (51).

In cardiac surgery, Nesher et al. analyzed inflammatory and myocardial injury markers in patients undergoing off-pump CABG (20). Patients were randomized to either traditional warming or Allon thermoregulation, a non-invasive warming technology with constant temperature feedback (20). Patients maintained at normothermia with Allon thermoregulation had lower interleukin-6 and troponin-I levels, whereas inadequate maintenance of normothermia in the former group was associated with higher inflammatory and injury markers (20). Nonetheless, this area remains poorly studied in cardiac surgery, and it is not clear whether a greater inflammatory response is clinically significant. Prior studies do not report a difference in the rate of surgical site infections comparing patients undergoing hypothermia to those undergoing normothermia (52), suggesting that modest temperature variations may have minimal clinical impact in this context.

Anesthesia considerations in hypothermia

The choice between normothermia and hypothermia in cardiac surgery has important implications for anesthetic management, particularly with respect to drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics. As discussed above, temperature-related changes occur in metabolic demand, neurologic function, and renal performance, which can all influence anesthetic delivery, effect, and clearance in patients undergoing cardiac surgery.

Temperature-sensitive enzymes are significantly affected by hypothermia, leading to prolonged action of many neuromuscular blocking agents and anesthetics. For example, in the early 1990s, Heier et al. demonstrated that a 2 ℃ reduction in temperature (36.5 versus 34.5 ℃) doubles the duration of action of vecuronium in both spontaneous recovery and provoked recovery (10), an effect also observed with rocuronium (12). More recently, a randomized study by Lee et al. showed that sugammadex reversal following rocuronium resulted in a significantly slower recovery of neuromuscular function in hypothermic patients compared to normothermic controls (53). Similarly, Leslie et al. reported prolonged duration of action for atracurium in patients at 34 versus 37 ℃ (54). These effects extend across age groups. In a cohort of 10 infants undergoing CPB, Withington and colleagues showed that cisatracurium infusion rates could be reduced by approximately 60% in hypothermic (21.9 ℃) patients while maintaining equivalent neuromuscular blockade compared to their normothermic (33.6 ℃) counterparts (55). Although cardiac surgery patients are not routinely extubated while hypothermic, these findings provide additional insight into the effects of temperature regulation and pharmacokinetics.

Hypothermia also alters the pharmacology of sedative and hypnotic agents. In the same study by Leslie and colleagues, the concentration of propofol was 28% higher in hypothermic patients compared with normothermic controls (54). Again, this is seen with midazolam, a benzodiazepine often used for sedation. In a randomized crossover study of healthy patients, Hostler and colleagues found that midazolam clearance decreased by 11.1% per 1 ℃ drop in temperatures below 36.5 ℃ (56). This finding is corroborated in rat models by Empey et al., demonstrating reduced clearance of both midazolam and fentanyl in mild hypothermia (57). Inhaled anesthetics are similarly affected. In a study of 33 children with congenital cardiac disease undergoing cardiac surgery, isoflurane requirements decreased in a graded fashion as temperature declined from 37 to 34 and 31 ℃ (58). Collectively, these findings indicate that across age groups and drug classes, hypothermia predictably prolongs anesthetic effects compared with normothermia, with important implications for perioperative management.

Conclusions

Although the optimal temperature strategy during CPB remains controversial, growing evidence suggests that both hypothermia and normothermia confer distinct benefits and drawbacks. Hypothermia produces marked physiologic effects, most notably enhanced myocardial protection, but at the expense of worsened coagulopathy and prolonged anesthetic drug clearance. Potential differences in neurocognitive, electrophysiologic, renal, immune, and other systemic outcomes have also been described. The inconclusiveness of available data highlights the need for further investigation and supports an individualized approach to temperature management. Hypothermic conditions may be preferential in higher-risk patients, such as those undergoing combined CABG and valve surgeries necessitating prolonged CPB times or presenting for CABG following myocardial infarction and stunned myocardium. Future studies should focus on the development of a decision-making framework for temperature selection during CPB in contemporary cardiac surgery. Such an algorithm must account for patient risk profile, operative complexity, anticipated ischemic burden, coagulopathy, end-organ vulnerability, and temperature-dependent pharmacologic effects. This may provide a more standardized and physiologically informed approach to temperature management in cardiac surgery.
