Contraceptive practices among spouses of individuals with schizophrenia: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Jag Mohan Prajapati, Dheerendra K. Mishra, Daisy Rure
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:The reproductive and sexual health needs of spouses of individuals with schizophrenia often go unaddressed in psychiatric care. These caregivers face considerable burden, which can affect contraceptive use and elevate risks related to fertility and sexually transmitted infections (STIs).Aim:To assess the prevalence and patterns of contraceptive use, preferred methods, and reproductive health concerns, including fertility issues and STI history, among spouses of individuals with schizophrenia.Materials and Methods:This cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary psychiatric facility in India and included 60 spouses of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia. Data were collected using a semi-structured questionnaire covering sociodemographic details, clinical profiles of patients, and reproductive health practices of spouses. Descriptive statistics were used for analysis.Results:Participants had a mean age of 35.58 ± 7.11 years, with equal representation of both genders. Most belonged to lower socioeconomic strata (78.3%) and had marriages lasting over 10 years. The average illness duration among patients was 5.08 ± 3.52 years, with 66.7% receiving irregular treatment. Sexual dysfunction was reported in 41.7% of patients, and 36.7% had comorbid substance use. Only 35% of spouses used contraception, predominantly barrier methods (15%) and hormonal methods (11.7%). Fertility problems were noted in 8.3%, and 15% had a history of STIs.Conclusion:Contraceptive use is low, and reproductive health risks are notable among spouses of individuals with schizophrenia. The study underscores the need for integrated, family-focused psychiatric services that also address caregivers’ sexual and reproductive health.

Schizophrenia is a chronic and debilitating psychiatric disorder characterized by disturbances in thought, perception, emotion, and behavior. Its pervasive impact extends beyond the affected individual, often imposing substantial psychological, social, and economic burdens on caregivers and family members, particularly spouses.[1] While considerable research has explored the caregiving experiences, emotional challenges, and expressed emotions of spouses living with individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia, relatively little attention has been directed toward their reproductive health and contraceptive practices.[23] Given the intimate and interconnected nature of marital relationships, understanding the sexual and reproductive health behaviors of these spouses is crucial in promoting holistic care.[4]

Family planning and contraceptive practices are integral to sexual and reproductive health. They allow individuals and couples to anticipate and attain their desired number of children and the spacing and timing of their births.[5] In India, where social structures are predominantly family-centric, reproductive decisions are deeply intertwined with cultural, religious, and gendered expectations.[6] The presence of a severe mental illness in one partner, such as schizophrenia, can disrupt normative family planning trajectories, often leading to compromised sexual relationships, fertility concerns, and irregular or absent contraceptive use.[7]

Studies suggest that mental illnesses such as schizophrenia can affect sexual functioning and intimacy, often due to negative symptoms, medication side effects (such as hyperprolactinemia or reduced libido), and interpersonal difficulties.[8] Consequently, these challenges may influence the couple’s decision or ability to engage in contraceptive discussions or practices. Moreover, the caregiving burden on spouses—particularly female spouses—can lead to psychological exhaustion, stigma, and social withdrawal, which may further reduce engagement with reproductive health services.[9]

Despite the potential implications, research focusing on the contraceptive behaviors of spouses of individuals with schizophrenia is scarce.[10] Existing literature on reproductive health in psychiatric populations tends to emphasize the needs of individuals with mental illness themselves, often overlooking the perspectives and health behaviors of their partners.[11] In particular, there is a lack of data from low- and middle-income countries like India, where cultural norms, healthcare access, gender dynamics, and mental health stigma shape reproductive decisions in unique ways.

This study seeks to address a significant gap in the literature by systematically examining contraceptive practices within this unique caregiver population. This study aims to evaluate the prevalence of contraceptive use, the preferred methods, and associated factors such as fertility concerns and a history of sexually transmitted infections (STIs) among spouses of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

This was a cross-sectional, descriptive study conducted to evaluate contraceptive practices, preferred methods, and associated reproductive health issues—including fertility concerns and history of STIs—among spouses of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia.

Study setting

The study was conducted at the Department of Psychiatry of Shyam Shah Medical College Rewa, a tertiary care teaching hospital, which provides both outpatient and inpatient services for individuals with severe mental illnesses.

Study population

The study population comprised spouses of patients diagnosed with schizophrenia, attending outpatient or inpatient psychiatric services during the study period.

Sample size and sampling technique

A total of 60 spouses were enrolled in this study using purposive, convenience sampling over the defined recruitment period. Given the exploratory nature of our objectives and the limited pool of eligible participants at our tertiary care centre, the sample size was determined by feasibility while aiming for adequate power to detect medium-sized differences in quality-of-life outcomes between groups with and without clinically significant depressive or anxiety symptoms. Spouses accompanying patients to outpatient appointments, follow-up visits, or inpatient wards were approached consecutively and invited to participate after providing informed consent.

Data collection tool

A semi-structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire was developed for the study. Items were selected and adapted to address key psychosocial and clinical domains relevant to spouses of individuals with schizophrenia.

Content validity was ensured through consultation with senior psychiatrists and clinical psychologists. The questionnaire was reviewed for clarity and comprehensibility, and a pilot test was conducted with a small group of participants (n = 5), following which minor modifications were made based on participant feedback.

The tool included three major sections:

Sociodemographic profile

Age, gender, education, occupation, socioeconomic status, duration of marriage, and number of children.

Clinical profile of spouse suffering from schizophrenia, e.g., duration of study, disease severity based on PANSS rating scale, treatment profile, etc.

Contraceptive practices

Type of contraception used (if any), consistency of use, reasons for non-use, and preferred methods.

Reproductive health indicators

Self-reported history or presence of sexually transmitted diseases (STDs/STIs), fertility problems (e.g., inability to conceive after one year of unprotected intercourse), and past gynecological or urological consultation.

Data collection procedure

Eligible participants were informed about the nature and objectives of the study. Written informed consent was obtained. Interviews were conducted in a private and confidential setting by trained investigators. Each interview took approximately 20–30 min.

Statistical analysis

Data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using SPSS (Statistical Package for the Social Sciences) by IBM Corp., located in Armonk (N.Y., USA). Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation) were used to summarize sociodemographic and reproductive health data. A P- value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to systematically evaluate the prevalence and pattern of contraceptive use, preferred contraceptive methods, and associated factors such as fertility issues and history of STIs among spouses of individuals diagnosed with schizophrenia. One of the most striking findings of the study is the low prevalence of contraceptive use, with only 35% of spouses reporting any use of contraception. Among those who used contraceptives, barrier methods such as condoms (15%) were the most commonly employed, followed by hormonal methods (11.7%), and very limited use of intrauterine devices (1.7%). This trend indicates a preference for short-term and easily accessible methods, while long-term or provider-dependent methods remain underutilized. This underuse may be attributed to several factors, including lack of access to reproductive health services, poor integration of sexual health within psychiatric care, and possible lack of awareness or misconceptions regarding contraceptive methods.[12] A similar trend has been observed in previous studies conducted in South Asia and other low-resource settings, where mental illness within the family can often lead to the marginalization of reproductive discussions and services.

Most spouses were within the reproductive age range (mean age 35.6 years) and belonged to the lower socioeconomic class (78.3%). Educational status varied, with over 50% having secondary education, yet this did not significantly translate into greater contraceptive use. These findings support existing evidence that socioeconomic status plays a pivotal role in healthcare access and reproductive autonomy (NFHS-5, 2021).[13] Lower economic status is often associated with reduced access to family planning services, a lack of female agency in reproductive decisions, and minimal exposure to sexual health education.[1415] Furthermore, the duration of marriage (mean 12.2 years) and the number of children (over 50% having 1–2 children) indicate that fertility goals may have already been partially achieved in this group, yet contraceptive use remains low, suggesting possible unmet needs for spacing or limiting births.

From the clinical perspective, the majority of patients had been diagnosed with schizophrenia for less than 5 years, and 66.7% were on irregular treatment. This irregularity may contribute to poor patient functioning and increased caregiver stress—factors that can severely disrupt intimacy, communication, and reproductive decision-making in a marital relationship.[1617]

Importantly, sexual dysfunction was reported in 41.7% of the patients, which is consistent with global literature indicating that both schizophrenia and its pharmacological treatment (especially antipsychotics) can impair sexual functioning.[1819] This dysfunction can act as a natural deterrent to sexual activity, potentially explaining low contraceptive use in some cases. However, it may also mask underlying needs, such as fear of unplanned pregnancy or STIs, especially when the partner has co-occurring substance use (36.7%)—as reported in a significant subset of the sample.

Fertility problems were reported in 8.3% of the spouses, while 15% had a history of STIs. Both figures are concerning and point toward inadequate sexual health services and a lack of preventive care among caregivers. The presence of STIs may reflect high-risk sexual behavior, possibly influenced by the patient’s comorbidities (e.g., substance use), or it may indicate a lack of condom use and limited access to STI screening and treatment.[20] These issues are often neglected in mental health settings where caregivers’ health needs are not routinely addressed.[21]

Limitations and future directions

Limitations include a small sample size, a single-center design, and a cross-sectional nature, limiting generalizability and causality. Future research should adopt longitudinal, multicentric, and dyadic designs to explore evolving contraceptive behaviors and evaluate integrated intervention models addressing both partners’ reproductive and mental health needs.

CONCLUSION

This study highlights low contraceptive use among spouses of individuals with schizophrenia, with a preference for short-term methods and links to sexual dysfunction, fertility concerns, and sociodemographic factors. The findings reveal low contraceptive use, limited method diversity, and reproductive health risks such as fertility problems and STIs, all within a context of caregiving burden and psychosocial stressors.

Addressing these issues calls for a paradigm shift in mental health services, moving beyond a sole focus on individual psychopathology toward integrated, family-centered, and culturally sensitive models of care. Such a holistic approach should encompass mental, sexual, and reproductive health, thereby promoting the overall well-being of both patients and their caregivers.
