Iatrogenic superior mesenteric artery dissection repaired with open septectomy and retrograde balloon control
Authors: Sarah A. Loh, Andres Doval Rojas, David Kuwayama, Britt H. Tonnessen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Complications of mesenteric artery stenting include distal embolization, branch perforation, thrombosis, and dissection. We present the case of a middle-aged woman with acute mesenteric ischemia who was treated with superior mesenteric artery (SMA) stenting resulting in an iatrogenic dissection that was later discovered on computed tomography angiography. SMA dissections pose a particular challenge for endovascular or open therapy, often extending into the distal artery and branches. This case serves a dual purpose: (1) to describe our successful technique with SMA septectomy and retrograde balloon control—an uncommonly performed procedure, and (2) to share lessons learned from our experience with the diagnosis and management of iatrogenic SMA dissection.

Superior mesenteric artery (SMA) dissections are uncommon and may occur spontaneously or from an iatrogenic mechanism. Although many SMA dissections can be managed conservatively, persistent symptoms warrant more aggressive intervention.1 Procedural options include endovascular stenting, patch angioplasty, or mesenteric bypass.2, 3, 4, 5 This case used a hybrid approach for a symptomatic, iatrogenic SMA dissection using open septectomy with retrograde balloon control of the previously stented SMA. The patient provided written consent for the publication of their case and images.

Case report

A 53-year-old female patient presented with several hours of acute abdominal pain. She reported antecedent history of several months of weight loss and postprandial pain. Of note, she had presented to the emergency room several days prior with similar symptoms but no laboratory derangements. Medical history was significant for hypertension, smoking and recent cocaine use. Physical examination revealed a body mass index of 19.6 kg/m2 and diffuse abdominal guarding. Blood work demonstrated a leukocytosis of 20,000 cells/μL and elevated lactate of 6.9 mmol/L. Computed tomography angiography (CTA) revealed celiac artery occlusion and a high-grade proximal SMA stenosis without calcification. She was brought emergently for SMA angiogram via left brachial access. Aortogram demonstrated high-grade stenosis of the SMA and meandering mesenteric collaterals. The origin of the SMA was noncalcified and did not appear to be stenotic (Fig 1, A). The SMA was dilated with a 4-mm balloon followed by placement of two 6-mm balloon-expandable stents. The stent was intentionally not extended into the aorta owing to the location of the stenosis and a normal SMA origin. Additional angioplasty was performed with the 4-mm balloon for residual stenosis distal to the stents with some radiographic improvement (Fig 1, B). The decision was made not to extend the stents further distal owing to the smaller diameter of the middle SMA and reluctance to cover branches. One week later, she was taken back to the operating room for evacuation of incision hematoma. A follow-up CTA demonstrated SMA dissection with pseudoaneurysm distal to the stents, suspected to be iatrogenic (Figs 1, C, D). Multiple branches originated from a very compressed true lumen adjacent this new pseudoaneurysm. Interestingly, the subsequent radiology report suggested fibromuscular dysplasia (FMD) of the renals owing to subtle beading of the mid portions.

A trial of conservative management with anticoagulation and diet challenges were initiated, and total parenteral nutrition was added. Her postoperative course was also confounded by dysesthesia and allodynia of the abdominal wall unrelated to meals, necessitating pain management. However, she was unable to consistently tolerate oral intake with persistent SMA dissection with severely compressed true lumen on follow-up imaging. She had no recurrence of leukocytosis or elevated lactate. She remained inpatient on total parenteral nutrition owing to the medical issues and social barriers to safe discharge. Owing to patient factors (malnutrition, pain management, and waxing and waning dietary tolerance) and surgeon prudence, the decision was made to proceed with redo laparotomy and open SMA septectomy was scheduled 6 weeks after her initial procedure.

The midline laparotomy was reopened. The superior mesenteric vein and SMA were approached anteriorly at the base of the mesentery and dissected circumferentially. The prior SMA stents were palpated. The pseudoaneurysmal segment of the SMA dissection was visualized, and retrograde micropuncture access was obtained distal to the dissected area (Fig 2, A). Proximal control of the SMA was obtained with a 6 mm short-shaft balloon over an 0.018″ wire. Angiogram demonstrated pooling of contrast within the dilated false lumen cul-de-sac (Fig 2, B). Control of multiple mesenteric branches was obtained with Yasargil clips and vessel loops. Arteriotomy of the SMA was performed, revealing a thickened, mature dissection flap (Fig 3, A). The flap was sharply excised, with good visualization of satisfactory proximal and distal end points and the SMA was reconstructed with a bovine patch. Before completing the patch suture line, the balloon was deflated and removed (Fig 3, B). One-month postoperative CTA demonstrated a widely patent SMA and stents without residual dissection (Figs 3, C, D). She was discharged on clopidogrel long term and has had no recurrence of intestinal angina 1 year after index procedure.

Discussion

Isolated SMA dissection is a rare condition that poses a particular challenge for endovascular or open therapy and often extends into the mid to distal SMA. This case demonstrates a successful hybrid approach for the repair of a flow-limiting SMA dissection. Although there are no formal guidelines or randomized controlled trials on the management of SMA dissection, the majority of acute cases can be managed conservatively with pain control, bowel rest, and anti-impulse therapies.1,4,6 The choice to use anticoagulation vs antiplatelet therapy for the initial treatment of isolated SMA dissection is an area of equipoise. In a single-center review of 77 patients with celiac or SMA dissections, 95% were successfully managed with antiplatelet therapy (30%), a short course of anticoagulation (52%), or observation (13%) without the need for endovascular or operative intervention.3 A 2021 systematic review found that 74% of patients were not treated with antithrombotic agents for isolated celiac or SMA dissections.6 Remarkably, a 2019 meta-analysis by Ahn et al7 showed (1) no difference in resolution of SMA dissections and Prior to 2) no impact on subsequent aneurysmal degeneration when comparing those treated with antithrombotic drugs vs none.

The possibility of FMD in the renal artery was mentioned on a follow-up CTA report, raising the possibility of FMD in the SMA as well. FMD typically creates a classic beaded appearance in afflicted arteries and rarely involves the SMA. This patient had a proximal SMA stenosis that could be explained by FMD or by soft atheromatous plaque in a heavy smoker. Relevant to our case, FMD can render arteries more vulnerable to dissection, either spontaneously or with manipulation.8 The patient remains on antiplatelet clopidogrel long-term for stent patency and in the setting of FMD.

Intervention is pursued in patients with SMA dissection in the setting of worsening clinical symptoms or evidence of bowel ischemia. Radiographic findings that may prompt intervention include aneurysmal degeneration or severe true lumen compression. Classification of SMA dissections have been described. The dissection in this case would be Yun type IIA, described as a patent false lumen without reentry.9 However, there is currently no definitive evidence that the classification systems can predict outcomes or direct optimal management.10 Endovascular options that have been described include SMA angioplasty and stenting2,11 with or without adjuvant catheter-directed papaverine12 or thrombolytic6 infusions. Stenting is the most common invasive treatment for SMA dissection—self-expanding stents in this situation provide less radial force and may be a more suitable alternative to standard balloon-expandable stents commonly used for treating atherosclerotic disease.13

This case also highlights the importance of recognizing iatrogenic dissections intraoperatively and having a heightened threshold to rescan patients with recurrent abdominal pain after visceral endovascular procedures. In retrospect, the stenosis distal to the stent likely represented a dissection. Angiographic filling of the distal SMA and the palpable intraoperative pulse provided false reassurance. Intravascular ultrasound could have readily identified the dissection on the table. Intravascular ultrasound examination is a useful adjunct for sizing and evaluating the composition and degree of stenosis. Iatrogenic SMA dissections are uncommonly reported. Several case reports have described sheath and wire trauma, during power injection, and angioplasty for other visceral artery pathology.14, 15, 16 We caution that management for iatrogenic SMA dissections cannot necessarily be extrapolated from the data on spontaneous SMA dissections.

SMA thrombectomy with open septectomy and saphenous vein bypass have been described.3, 4, 5,17,18 Open septectomy in this case was performed 6 weeks after the initial presentation, at a time when the dissection flap would be more mature and the vessel potentially less friable.17 However, earlier reoperation should be considered when patient's symptoms are intractable. We selected an open surgical repair because the dissection extended into a small caliber mid-SMA with multiple branches and open septectomy with patch closure would allow preservation of branches. There was also concern that extensive stenting of the SMA would not provide durable patency and limit future open options. Open surgery for SMA dissection is feasible for cases of acute or chronic mesenteric ischemia and is a reasonable alternative to endovascular therapy.

Conclusions

Iatrogenic SMA dissection can occur with endovascular manipulation. Persistently symptomatic SMA dissections may require interventional treatment. This case demonstrates the feasibility of a hybrid approach involving open SMA septectomy and retrograde balloon control, which resulted in a favorable outcome for the patient.
