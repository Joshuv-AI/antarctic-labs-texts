Modelling Nonstationary Time Series using Trend-Stationary Hypothesis
Zhandos Abdikhadir Department of MathematicsHong Kong Baptist University

Abstract
This paper challenges the prevalence of unit root models by introducing the Linear Trend-Stationary Trigonometric ARMA (LTSTA), a novel framework for modelling nonstationary time series under the trend-stationary hypothesis. LTSTA decomposes series into three components: (1) a deterministic trend (modeled via continuous piecewise linear functions with structural breaks), (2) a Fourier-based deterministic seasonality component, and (3) a stochastic ARMA error term. We propose a heuristic approach to determine the optimal number of structural breaks, with parameter estimation performed through an iterative scheme that integrates a modified dynamic programming algorithm for break detection and a standard regression procedure with ARMA errors. The model’s performance is evaluated through a case study on US Real GDP (2002–2025), where it accurately identifies breaks corresponding to major economic events (e.g., the 2008 financial crisis and COVID-19 shocks). Additionally, LTSTA outperforms well-established univariate statistical models (SES, Theta, TBATS, ETS, ARIMA, and Prophet) on the CIF 2016 forecasting competition dataset across MAE, RMSE, sMAPE, and MASE metrics. The LTSTA model provides an interpretable alternative to unit root approaches, particularly suited for time series with predominant deterministic properties where structural break detection is critical. Keywords: Time series, trend-stationary hypothesis, segmented linear regression, Fourier series, ARMA, forecasting

1
Introduction

Popularized by George E. P. Box and Gwilym M. Jenkins in their seminal work
(
Box and Jenkins, 1970
)
, Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) models are a foundational framework for analyzing and forecasting nonstationary time series. These models are based on the idea that nonstationarity (or a unit root) can be removed through finite-order differencing, thereby transforming unit root processes into a stationary form. Afterwards, the stationarized time series can be described using autoregressive (AR) and moving average (MA) terms. The AR terms capture the linear dependence on past observations, while the MA terms account for the linear dependence between current values and prior forecast errors.

The influential findings of
Nelson and Plosser, 1982
revealed strong evidence that most economic time series follow unit root processes, further supporting the relevance of ARIMA models in describing a wide range of time series movements and generating reliable short-term forecasts. While differencing nonstationary processes remains a cornerstone of time series analysis,
Perron, 1989
challenged the traditional unit root hypothesis. Instead, he proposed that many economic time series are trend-stationary, meaning they can be described with stationary processes that fluctuate around a deterministic trend characterized by shocks or structural breaks. For example, Perron used the Great Crash of 1929 and the 1973 oil crisis as the primary sources of these shocks when evaluating the deterministic trend component in his analysis of macroeconomic time series.

These structural changes typically arise from economic crises, policy shifts, technological disruptions, or pandemics. Such events may disrupt the data-generating process and challenge fixed-parameter models like classical ARIMA. Several methods that account for structural changes have been proposed to address this limitation. Among these approaches, Markov Switching Models (MSM,
Hamilton, 1989
) allow parameters to stochastically switch between discrete states like economic expansions and recessions. In contrast, the Self-Exciting Threshold Autoregressive (SETAR) and Threshold Autoregressive (TAR) models
(
Tong and Lim, 1980
)
assume that regimes change deterministically when the series crosses a threshold value estimated from its own lagged values or exogenous variables, respectively.

In this paper, we propose a model based on the unpopular Perron’s idea that univariate nonstationary time series can be described by trend-stationary processes. The proposed model is termed as the Linear Trend-Stationary Trigonometric ARMA (LTSTA). This paper presents the core concepts of the LTSTA and demonstrates its application through a case study of US Gross Domestic Product (GDP) data. To evaluate the forecasting performance of the proposed model, we compare it against several well-established methods using four evaluation metrics across 72 time series from a forecasting competition. The paper concludes with a discussion of the empirical findings and their implications.

2
Linear Trend-Stationary Trigonometric ARMA

2.1
Nonstationary Time Series

Before introducing the proposed model, we review the foundational concepts and key characteristics of modelling nonstationary time series. Formally, a time series
\{Y_{t}\}
is strictly stationary if the joint probability distribution of any finite subset of observations is invariant under time shifts. This ensures that the statistical properties of the time series remain constant over time, enabling us to describe its behaviour. However, this notion of strict stationarity is too strong and rarely encountered in real-world applications. A more practical approach is to assume weak stationarity, where:

•

The mean
\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=\mu
is constant over time,

•

The variance
\mathrm{Var}(Y_{t})=\sigma^{2}
is constant over time, and

•

The autocovariance
\mathrm{Cov}(Y_{t},Y_{t-k})=\gamma(k)
depends only on the lag
k
.

One of the reasons behind the assumption of weak stationarity is that if we want to construct the best linear predictor (in the mean squared error sense) for the strictly stationary time series, we only need to know the mean and autocovariance function without more detailed knowledge of the joint distribution
(
Brockwell and Davis, 2016
)
. This principle is complemented by Wold’s Representation Theorem
(
Wold, 1938
)
, which provides a complete characterization of weakly stationary processes through their infinite moving average representation. Let
\{Y_{t}\}
be a weakly stationary process with
\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}]=0
and covariance function,
\gamma(j)=\mathbb{E}[Y_{t}Y_{t-j}]
,
\forall j
. Then,
\{Y_{t}\}
admits the decomposition:

Y_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_{j}\varepsilon_{t-j}+\eta_{t}

(1)

where:

1.

\{\varepsilon_{t}\}
is a white noise process satisfying
\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_{t}]=0
,
\mathrm{Var}(\varepsilon_{t})=\sigma^{2}_{\varepsilon}
,
\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon_{t},\varepsilon_{s})=0
for
t\neq s
,

2.

The coefficients
\{\psi_{j}\}
are square-summable
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\psi_{j}^{2}<\infty
with
\psi_{0}=1
,

3.

\eta_{t}
is deterministic (perfectly predictable from its past),

4.

\{\varepsilon_{t}\}
and
\{\eta_{t}\}
are uncorrelated.

Under suitable conditions, a weakly stationary process can be represented in the infinite autoregressive form, meaning that it can be written as a weighted sum of past observations plus white noise:

Y_{t}=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\pi_{j}Y_{t-j}+\varepsilon_{t}

(2)

For convenience, the term stationarity refers specifically to weak stationarity unless otherwise stated. Although the representations (
1
) and (
2
) of stationary time series are elegant and describe the historical data well, they are often impractical as they require estimating an infinite number of parameters. Thus, a parsimonious representation of stationary time series using finite autoregressive and moving average components is preferred
(
Box et al., 2015
)
. A process
\{Y_{t}\}
is said to be ARMA
(p,q)
with non-zero mean
\mu
if it can be defined as:

Y_{t}-\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_{i}(Y_{t-i}-\mu)+\varepsilon_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_{j}\varepsilon_{t-j}

(3)

or

\phi(B)(Y_{t}-\mu)=\theta(B)\varepsilon_{t}

where
\{\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{p}\}
are the autoregressive coefficients with
\phi_{p}\neq 0
;
\{\theta_{1},\dots,\theta_{q}\}
are the moving average coefficients with
\theta_{q}\neq 0
;
B
is the backward shift operator such that
B^{j}(X_{t})=X_{t-j}
;
\phi(B)=1-\phi_{1}B-\dots-\phi_{p}B^{p}
and
\theta(B)=1-\theta_{1}B-\dots-\theta_{q}B^{q}
are autoregressive and moving average operators respectively.

An ARMA
(p,q)
process must satisfy causality and invertibility conditions to ensure it is well-defined and useful for inference. Causality means
Y_{t}
depends only on past/present white noise, while invertibility allows
\varepsilon_{t}
to be expressed via past observations. Mathematically, these require the roots of
\phi(z)
and
\theta(z)
to lie outside the unit circle
|z|>1
, respectively. Additionally, if an ARMA process is causal, it is automatically stationary, as the conditions of stationarity and causality are identical.

While ARMA processes are suited for stationary time series, they can be extended to describe the nonstationary behaviour observed in many real-world applications. The most common indicator of nonstationarity in time series is a mean that evolves over time. It turns out that if the roots of the autoregressive operator lie on the unit circle, the nonstationary (unit root) process can be transformed into stationary form by applying finite-order differencing. This procedure eliminates the time-dependent trend component, making the transformed series suitable for ARMA modelling. Such processes are referred to as
difference-stationary
processes and can be described by ARIMA models. A nonstationary process
\{Y_{t}\}
is said to be ARIMA
(p,d,q)
if its
d
-th difference
X_{t}=(1-B)^{d}Y_{t}
is a stationary
\text{ARMA}(p,q)
process. In general, it can be written as:

\phi(B)(1-B)^{d}Y_{t}=\delta+\theta(B)\varepsilon_{t}

(4)

where
\delta
is the drift term, and all other terms follow the definitions given in equation (
3
).

Another way to describe the nonstationary time series is to assume that the process is
trend-stationary
. Instead of differencing, we can replace
\mu
of the stationary process (
3
) with a deterministic time trend
T_{t}
. After subtracting the trend component from the original values, we obtain
\{Z_{t}\}
which follows a stationary zero-mean ARMA
(p,q)
process:

Y_{t}=T_{t}+Z_{t}

(5)

This approach works well when the nature of the time series is known as a priori. For example, one may set
T_{t}
to be an exponential function of time when the series exhibits multiplicative growth patterns, such as in population dynamics or compound interest scenarios. However, most time series do not follow such simplistic trends and require deep domain expertise to adequately specify the trend component, making difference-stationary ARIMA models generally preferable. To address this limitation, we introduce a method that provides a flexible trend component, which enables modelling nonstationary time series under the trend-stationary hypothesis.

2.2
The Model

We propose a
Linear Trend-Stationary Trigonometric ARMA
(LTSTA) approach, which decomposes a univariate nonstationary time series into a deterministic trend
T_{t}
, a deterministic seasonal component
S_{t}
, and a stochastic error term
Z_{t}
. The trend component uses a piecewise linear function to capture long-term movements and sudden significant shocks; the seasonality component uses a trigonometric approach through a Fourier series to describe periodic patterns; and the error term follows a zero-mean ARMA(
p,q
) process to account for temporal dependence. If the time series
\{Y_{t}\}
is assumed to experience
m
structural changes (
m+1
regimes), the model can be written as:

Y_{t}=T_{t}+S_{t}+Z_{t}

(6)

T_{t}=\begin{cases}\beta^{(1)}_{0}+\beta^{(1)}_{1}t,&t\leq c_{1}\\
\beta^{(2)}_{0}+\beta^{(2)}_{1}t,&c_{1}<t\leq c_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
\beta^{(m+1)}_{0}+\beta^{(m+1)}_{1}t,&c_{m}<t\end{cases}

(7)

S_{t}=\sum_{k=1}^{N}\left(a_{k}\cos\left(\frac{2\pi kt}{P}\right)+b_{k}\sin\left(\frac{2\pi kt}{P}\right)\right)

(8)

Z_{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{p}\phi_{i}Z_{t-i}+\varepsilon_{t}+\sum_{j=1}^{q}\theta_{j}\varepsilon_{t-j}

(9)

where
\beta^{(i)}_{0}
and
\beta^{(i)}_{1}
are the intercept and the slope of the
i^{th}
regime for
i=1,\ldots,m+1
;
c_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,m
represents the time indices of the breaks, which are treated as unknown (for convenience, we set
c_{0}=0
and
c_{m+1}=n
);
P
is the period of the seasonal pattern (e.g.,
P=12
for monthly data with yearly seasonality);
N
is the number of harmonic terms;
a_{k}
and
b_{k}
determine the amplitude of each cosine and sine wave at frequency
k
for
k=1,\ldots,N
; the terms of the ARMA(
p,q
) process
\{Z_{t}\}
are defined as in equation (
3
).

Since the underlying structure of the time series is typically unknown, the linear representation of the trend component provides a parsimonious approximation. We assume the seasonality and error terms undergo no structural shifts, which is reasonable as these components rarely change in practice. Additionally, the implementation of the deterministic seasonality component fully satisfies Wold’s representation for stationary time series (
1
), with
\eta_{t}
replaced by
S_{t}
.

We impose a continuity constraint on the trend to ensure smooth transitions between adjacent regimes. This restriction enhances interpretability by eliminating abrupt shifts, enabling clearer analysis of trend evolution across different regimes. Additionally, the continuity constraint reduces the number of trend parameters by expressing each subsequent regime’s intercept (excluding the first regime) in terms of the breakpoints and slopes of the current and preceding regimes. Specifically, for
i=1,2,\ldots,m
:

\beta_{0}^{(i)}+\beta_{1}^{(i)}c_{i}=\beta_{0}^{(i+1)}+\beta_{1}^{(i+1)}c_{i},

(10)

or

\beta_{0}^{(i+1)}=\beta_{0}^{(1)}+\sum_{k=1}^{i}\left(\beta_{1}^{(k)}-\beta_{1}^{(k+1)}\right)c_{k}

The proposed framework incorporates concepts analogous to those in well-established time series models such as Facebook’s Prophet
(
Taylor and Letham, 2018
)
and the Trigonometric Box-Cox ARMA Trend Seasonal (TBATS,
Livera et al., 2011
). For a general model with
m+1
regimes, we must estimate the location of
m
breaks and
m+2
beta parameters for the trend component,
2N
parameters for the seasonality component, and
p+q+1
parameters for the stochastic error term that follows ARMA
(p,q)
process. The parameters of the model are estimated via an iterative scheme.

2.3
Preliminary Estimation

Considerable research has been conducted on breakpoint estimation across statistical applications. Seminal work by
Bai and Perron, 1998
developed general methods for detecting multiple structural changes, including estimation algorithms and asymptotic theory. Modern computational tools like the
ruptures
library
(
Truong et al., 2020
)
further extend these approaches, offering diverse detection techniques supporting parametric and nonparametric cost functions with various penalties. The fundamental principle underlying most breakpoint detection methods is to minimize a cost function that evaluates within-segment goodness-of-fit while controlling model complexity. Consider a non-seasonal structural model (where
S_{t}=0
) with uncorrelated error terms
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}
, which can be expressed as:

Y_{t}=T_{t}+\varepsilon_{t}

(11)

where the trend component satisfies representation (
7
) with continuity constraints (
10
). The parameter estimation method considered is based on Least Squares (LS). Suppose that the number of breaks
m
is known. Given an observed time series
\{Y_{t}\}
of sample size
n
, the beta parameters
(\beta^{(1)}_{0},\beta^{(1)}_{1},...,\ \beta^{(m+1)}_{1})
and breaks
(c_{1},...,c_{m})
are estimated by minimizing:

\sum_{t=1}^{n}(Y_{t}-T_{t})^{2}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\sum_{t=c_{i}+1}^{c_{i+1}}\left[Y_{t}-\left(\beta^{(1)}_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{i}(\beta^{(k)}_{1}-\beta^{(k+1)}_{1})c_{k}\right)-\beta^{(i+1)}_{1}t\right]^{2}

(12)

A primary challenge in optimizing (
12
) arises from the second summation term on the right-hand side, which introduces non-smoothness in the cost function, making direct optimization methods not possible. Additionally, the model’s nonlinearity in parameters makes the minimization even more sophisticated. Since breaks are discrete-valued, both problems can be addressed by prespecifying all possible partition locations, estimating the corresponding beta parameters via Ordinary Least Squares (OLS), and selecting the partition that represents the smallest Sum of Squared Residuals (SSR).

For illustration, consider a short time series
\mathbf{Y}=(Y_{1},Y_{2},Y_{3},Y_{4},Y_{5})^{\prime}
with a single structural break. The potential break positions are
Y_{2}
,
Y_{3}
, and
Y_{4}
. The model can be expressed in matrix form as:

\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{T}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}

(13)

where
\mathbf{T}=(T_{1},\ldots,T_{5})^{\prime}
is the trend vector and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=(\varepsilon_{1},\ldots,\varepsilon_{5})^{\prime}
is the error vector. The parameterized representation of the trend vector
\mathbf{T}
depends on the break location. For example, for a break at
c_{1}=3
, the trend vector
\mathbf{T}
is written as:

\mathbf{T}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\
1&2&0\\
1&3&0\\
1&3&1\\
1&3&2\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\beta_{0}^{(1)}\\
\beta_{1}^{(1)}\\
\beta_{1}^{(2)}\end{pmatrix}

(14)

where the design matrix
\mathbf{X}
contains information about the break and
\boldsymbol{\beta}=\bigl(\beta_{0}^{(1)},\beta_{1}^{(1)},\beta_{1}^{(2)}\bigr)^{\prime}
is the vector of parameters of the corresponding conditioned model. The OLS estimates of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
, denoted as
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
, are calculated as:

\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}=(\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{X})^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{\prime}\mathbf{Y}

(15)

Therefore, we estimate
\boldsymbol{\beta}
for all possible specifications of the design matrix
\mathbf{X}
(three in this case) and compute the corresponding sum of squared residuals:

\text{SSR}=(\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})^{\prime}(\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*})

(16)

The optimal breakpoint is identified as the specification that yields the minimum SSR. However, this process becomes computationally slow for moderate-sized time series samples especially when the number of breaks exceeds two. Fortunately, there exist methods that improves the search process of the optimal breakpoints.

The optimal time series partition can be obtained through a
dynamic programming algorithm
that solves the optimization problem iteratively
(
Perron and Qu, 2006
)
. This method accelerates computation by reusing results from previous partitions, offering significant speed advantages over exhaustive grid search without substantial loss of accuracy. The algorithm was originally designed for structural models without continuity constraints; we therefore propose a modified version of the standard approach.

Let
\text{SSR}_{k}(i,j)
denote the sum of squared residuals of the optimal partition of the time series with time indices
t\in[i,j]
containing
k
breakpoints, and let
h
be the minimal permissible segment length. Increasing
h
improves the algorithm’s computational efficiency but may yield suboptimal partitions; we, therefore, recommend
h=2
when estimation accuracy is prioritized. The dynamic programming algorithm for finding the optimal partition with
m
breaks within a series of length
n
proceeds in two stages:

1.

Initialization (
k=1
):

Compute
\text{SSR}_{1}(1,i)
for
i=2h,2h+1,\ldots,n
via full grid search over all possible single-break partitions. Store these values.

2.

Iterative Computation (
k=2
to
m
):

For each
k=2,\ldots,m
, compute
\text{SSR}_{k}(1,i)
for
i=(k+1)h,(k+1)h+1,\ldots,n
as:

\text{SSR}_{k}(1,i)=\underset{j=kh,\ldots,i-h}{\min}\left[\text{SSR}_{k-1}(1,j)+\text{SSR}_{1}(j+1,n)\right]

(17)

where the position of breaks for
\text{SSR}_{k-1}(1,j)
is retrieved from the previous step, and only
\text{SSR}_{1}(j+1,n)
requires additional grid search.

While this method does not guarantee an optimal partition due to interdependence between regimes imposed by continuity constraints, it yields results nearly identical to those obtained by exhaustive evaluation of all possible partitions.

The dynamic programming algorithm performs well when the number of breaks is known. A heuristic approach to determine the optimal number of partitions involves applying the dynamic programming method for a sufficiently large specified
m_{\max}
to obtain segmentations with
1
to
m_{\max}
breaks. Notably, reapplying dynamic programming for each case is unnecessary, since the solution for
m_{\max}
optimal breaks inherently contains information about all preceding optimal segmentations. By comparing these
m_{\max}+1
models (including the no-break case), we then select the most parsimonious specification. Unfortunately, traditional selection methods such as the Akaike Information Criterion (
AIC
,
Akaike, 1974
) and Bayesian Information Criterion (
BIC
,
Schwarz, 1978
) cannot be directly applied, as such metrics tend to overfit by favouring excessively complex models, particularly in the presence of autocorrelated errors
(
Bai and Perron, 2003
)
. For this problem, our objective extends beyond simply identifying the number of breaks required to achieve stationary residuals. We must determine the optimal number of breaks that accurately characterize the trend component while maintaining an appropriate balance with model complexity.

The initial approach to determining the number of structural changes involves examining three key elements: the plot of fitted trend values against actual observations, the corresponding residual series, and its autocorrelation function (ACF) plot. Visual inspection of these plots helps to identify the number of breaks needed for trend-free stationary residuals. This approach works reasonably good for well-behaved time series where structural breaks are visually evident, allowing us to confidently select a single model. Other models either fail to capture the full trend (underfitting) or mistake noise for signal (overfitting). However, this method becomes unreliable when multiple plausible candidate models exist and the optimal one must be selected.

One way to solve this problem is to use the
elbow
method. Essentially, we select a model with
m
breaks when adding additional breaks (
m+1,\ldots,m_{\max}
) no longer provides a significant reduction in the cost function. We implement a modified version of the
L
-method
(
Salvador and Chan, 2004
)
, which fits two continuous linear segments to the evolution of the cost function and identifies their intersection point. This knee point shows where the rate of improvement in the cost function slows significantly and, therefore, determines the optimal number of breaks. As before, this method performs well for well-behaved time series but typically struggles when trends are less pronounced. Nevertheless, it generally provides reliable estimates for the number of breaks needed to capture the overall time series structure.

To address this limitation, we analyze the change in SSR by computing the improvement ratio
r_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,m_{\max}
and the absolute improvement
d_{j}
for
j=m_{L}+1,\ldots,m_{\max}
between successive models, defined as:

r_{i}=1-\frac{\text{SSR}_{i}}{\text{SSR}_{i-1}}

(18)

d_{j}=\text{SSR}_{j-1}-\text{SSR}_{j}

(19)

where
m_{L}
is the number of breaks identified by the L-method discussed previously. For example, when using the ratio-based approach, we calculate the improvement ratio for each model and reject break counts where the ratio falls below the specified ratio threshold
\tau_{r}
(for instance, with
\tau_{r}=0.2
we discard models showing less than 20% SSR improvement). The chosen model is the last acceptable one before the first rejected model. The same selection logic applies when evaluating SSR absolute improvements based on the absolute threshold
\tau_{d}
.

The thresholds are derived as the sample mean of the corresponding improvement metrics. This approach stems from the empirical observation that for overparameterized models, the metrics shows nearly the same values as additional breaks begin capturing noise rather than meaningful structural changes. Consequently, the sample mean serves as a natural threshold, formally defined as:

\tau_{r}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{m_{\max}}r_{i}}{m_{\max}}

(20)

\tau_{d}=\frac{\sum_{j=m_{L}+1}^{m_{\max}}d_{j}}{m_{\max}-m_{L}}

(21)

Unlike ratio-based approaches, SSR absolute improvements are unbounded which makes the initial large values substantially inflate the threshold. To address this, we implement the L-method, which identifies the number of breaks
m_{L}
needed to approximately capture the time series’ overall shape. This helps filter out initial extreme values, enabling the computation of a more robust threshold. Generally, the ratio-based method is not as effective as the absolute improvement method because it selects models based solely on relative SSR ratios, ignoring whether the absolute improvements are meaningful.

The effectiveness of these techniques heavily depends on the specification of
m_{\max}
, as it affects both the location of the knee point in the L-method and consequently the computation of the thresholds. We recommend selecting
m_{\max}
as approximately three times the number of breaks visually identifiable from the time series plot. If the time series contains significant outliers, the SSR becomes unreliable because of its high sensitivity to extreme values. In such cases, the Sum of Absolute Residuals provides a more robust alternative. Overall, the aforementioned techniques—including examination of visual plots and analysis of SSR—help us identify a reasonable number of breaks. This process can be automated by utilizing SSR absolute improvements, with
m_{\max}
set to 10, 20, and 30 for short, moderate, and long time series, respectively. While this automated approach yields results nearly identical to manual analysis, we strongly recommend selecting the optimal number of breakpoints through careful evaluation of all available techniques, as this parameter significantly impacts model performance.

Note that breakpoints are not treated as true parameters in this framework. Because the estimation of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is conditional on breakpoints, a consequence of the dynamic programming algorithm (
17
), it is impossible to obtain the full covariance matrix of the estimates
(
Muggeo, 2003
)
. Consequently, all parameter inferences are necessarily conditional on the identified breaks. This approach fundamentally treats breakpoints estimation as a model selection problem rather than as a standard parameter estimation procedure. Although this section has focused on the estimation of the nonseasonal model with uncorrelated errors, these results remain applicable to the complete estimation of the model (
6
).

2.4
Modelling with LTSTA

The dynamic programming algorithm (
17
) is designed for pure structural change models (
11
), and directly applying the dynamic programming method to partial structural change model (
6
) presents two key challenges. First, certain partitions cannot generate stationary error terms. Second, the method necessitates fitting an ARMA model during every iteration – a computationally demanding process that becomes even more complex because the ARMA order is unknown. On the other hand, the breakpoints detected within the pure structural change framework can serve as initial estimates for the proposed model. This validity arises because seasonal and ARMA components primarily capture fluctuations that negligibly influence trend-level shifts. Before presenting the estimation method for the model (
6
), we first analyse the estimation approach for the seasonal and ARMA components of the series with a known trend component
T_{t}
. The detrended series
W_{t}
are given by:

W_{t}=Y_{t}-T_{t}=S_{t}+Z_{t}

(22)

where
S_{t}
and
Z_{t}
retain their definitions from (
8
) and (
9
) respectively.

To estimate the parameters of the seasonal component
(a_{1},b_{1},\ldots,a_{N},b_{N})
and the ARMA process
(\sigma_{\varepsilon}^{2},\phi_{1},\ldots,\phi_{p},\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{q})
, we employ the following procedure. First, the limiting orders of the both components must be specified. We constrain the maximum number of harmonic terms
N
to
P/2
(equivalent to using seasonal dummy variables) while fixing the maximum orders of the autoregressive and moving average terms to
p_{\max}=2
and
q_{\max}=2
respectively. We assume the white noise process
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}
follows a Gaussian distribution, enabling parameter estimation via standard Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE). For determining the orders of both seasonal and ARMA components, we employ the bias-corrected Akaike Information Criterion (AICc) proposed by
Hurvich and Tsai, 1989
, defined as:

\mathrm{AICc}=-2\ln L+\frac{2kn}{n-k-1}

(23)

where
L
is the maximized likelihood of the model;
n
is the sample size;
k
is the number of estimated parameters. Consequently, we select the model with the lowest AICc score which parameters are estimated through standard MLE procedure: first obtaining initial parameter estimates via conditional sum of squares (CSS), followed by iterative numerical optimization.

Bai and Perron, 2003
proposed an iterative estimation scheme for partial structural change models of the form (
6
), which achieves rapid convergence by repeatedly applying the dynamic programming algorithm until parameters and breaks stabilize. In our context, the presence of continuity constraints increases the dynamic programming algorithm’s complexity compared to unconstrained cases, prompting us to propose the following modified scheme to address this computational challenge:

1.

Initial Estimation of the Trend Component:

Run the dynamic programming algorithm for the pure structural change model (
11
) using sufficiently large
m_{\max}
to identify the optimal number of breaks
m
, then extract both the initial breakpoints
(c_{1}^{*},\ldots,c_{m}^{*})
and their corresponding
\boldsymbol{\beta}^{*}
parameters, which collectively define the initial estimate of the trend component
T_{t}^{*}
.

2.

Initial Estimation of the Unstructured Components:

Calculate the detrended series
\{Y_{t}-T_{t}^{*}\}
. Estimate the orders and parameters of the seasonal and ARMA components using the previously described procedure, obtaining the initial estimated components
S_{t}^{*}
and
Z_{t}^{*}
.

3.

Refinement of the Breakpoints:

Compute the adjusted series
\{Y_{t}-S_{t}^{*}-Z_{t}^{*}\}
, then apply the dynamic programming algorithm to the adjusted series to determine the final optimal breaks
(\hat{c}_{1},\ldots,\hat{c}_{m})
.

4.

Final Parameter Estimation:

Re-estimate the parameters for the trend
\hat{T}_{t}
, seasonal
\hat{S}_{t}
, and error
\hat{Z}_{t}
components using the same orders as in Step 2 and refined breakpoints from Step 3.

This procedure accounts for seasonality and error components via a two-stage dynamic programming method. While subsequent iterations would theoretically improve results, empirical evidence shows that breakpoints are usually identical to those obtained for pure structural model in Step 1. Additionally, we can relax the assumption that seasonality and error components remain structurally unchanged using the estimated breakpoints from the preliminary estimation. This applies, for example, when trend changes significantly affect seasonal patterns or when error terms exhibit varying volatility across different regimes.

The proposed model is an extension of ARMA with exogenous variables. The exogenous variables are constructed from time indices to represent the trend and seasonality components. Consequently, parameter estimation, forecast generation, and confidence interval construction follow standard procedures. Following model specification and parameter estimation, diagnostic checks must be conducted to confirm that the residuals exhibit Gaussian white noise properties, specifically homoscedasticity, normality, and no autocorrelation. Significant deviations from these assumptions suggest potential misspecification, necessitating model refinement. To address this problem, power transformations such as the Box-Cox transformation
(
Box and Cox, 1964
)
usually help enhance linearity, improve normality, stabilize variance, and reduce outlier influence.

The
f
-step-ahead optimal linear forecast of the trend-stationary process estimated by LTSTA are generated by forecasting the stationary ARMA process representing the error terms and then adding the projected deterministic components to obtain the final point forecasts
(
Hamilton, 1994
)
. As previously noted, parameter inference is conditional on the estimated breakpoints, which do not account for breakpoint uncertainty. This narrows the confidence intervals for future forecasts and leads to inaccurate uncertainty quantification. This challenge can be resolved using either bootstrapping techniques or more advanced approaches like conformal prediction
(
Vovk et al., 2005
)
, which provides robust estimation of prediction confidence intervals. Note that the predicted trend component is heavily determined by the final regime’s parameters, making forecast accuracy highly sensitive to the estimation of the last regime. In practice, models containing breaks near the series endpoint should typically be avoided. In the following section, we present a practical implementation of the LTSTA model and conduct an empirical evaluation of its forecasting performance against established benchmarks across multiple time series.

3.2

In this study, we compare the forecasting performance of the proposed LTSTA model against several well-established univariate statistical methods using a dataset from the Computational Intelligence in Forecasting 2016 (CIF 2016) competition

Among these, 57 series have a forecast horizon of 12, while the remaining 15 series have a forecasting horizon of 6. The time series are well-behaved and do not require preliminary power transformations. We evaluate the forecasting performance of the proposed model against established benchmark methods using four key metrics:

(26)

The Mean Absolute Error (MAE) provides robust, interpretable error measurement by averaging absolute deviations equally. Finally, the Mean Absolute Scaled Error (MASE) benchmarks performance against a naïve model’s in-sample accuracy, enabling scale-free comparisons. By combining these different metrics, we balance their strengths and weaknesses as well as get a more complete picture of forecast performance, as each one captures errors in a unique way.

Although these empirical results are promising, LTSTA has considerable disadvantages. First, the optimal number of breaks is highly sensitive to the choice of
m_{\max}
, making it difficult in practice to select a number that adequately guards against underfitting. Second, the LTSTA’s reliance on dynamic programming makes it computationally intensive relative to conventional statistical models. Third, this estimation approach fails to deliver reliable parameter inference or confidence intervals. Moreover, it struggles to capture the behavior of highly nonlinear time series with smooth transitions as we assume that the trend is captured through linear functions, and thus the last regime can be wrongly specified. Finally, if we are concerned about long-term forecasting, the LTSTA model will potentially perform poorly because it assumes that the future horizon does not contain breakpoints and the trend will remain the same.

On the other hand, LTSTA offers significantly greater interpretability than other models by providing an intuitive decomposition of the time series into trend, seasonality, and error components. The trend component reveals structural changes in the time series, such as those caused by the Global Financial Crisis or COVID-19, while also quantifying the average rate of change within each regime. The seasonality component employs Fourier series to deliver a compact representation of periodic patterns, while the stochastic error term uses ARMA processes to capture the series’ random structure. This framework excels at modeling time series that exhibit some sort of regularity, as it emphasizes deterministic rather than purely stochastic representation. For instance, it performs particularly well for economic indicators such as gross domestic product, retail sales, and employment data, which typically exhibit stable linear structures and are dominated by deterministic components rather than stochastic fluctuations. This serves as the primary reason for its superior performance over other models in the CIF 2016 competition, since most time series were artificially generated with predominantly deterministic characteristics.

4
Conclusion

This paper introduces the Linear Trend-Stationary Trigonometric ARMA (LTSTA) approach, which models nonstationary time series under the trend-stationary hypothesis. This hypothesis posits that nonstationary series can be characterized by a stationary component fluctuating around a deterministic trend. LTSTA decomposes the time series into: (1) a deterministic trend component modeled via piecewise linear functions, (2) a deterministic seasonal component represented by Fourier series, and (3) a stochastic error component following an ARMA process. This framework prioritizes model interpretability, enabling intuitive time series modeling. We implement a modified dynamic programming algorithm to identify trend breakpoints and propose methods for selecting the optimal number of breaks. The model is fully estimated through an iterative scheme compatible with standard ARMA procedures incorporating exogenous variables. While these techniques face challenges—including unreliable parameter inference and computational intensity—they yield promising results for nonstationary time series with predominant deterministic properties. This was demonstrated through both a case study on US Real Gross Domestic Product and a comparative analysis using traditional univariate statistical models and the CIF 2016 forecasting competition dataset. By combining interpretable deterministic components with flexible stochastic modelling, LTSTA offers a principled framework for analyzing trend-stationary time series across economics and other applied domains.
