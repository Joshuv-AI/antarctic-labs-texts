Mitochondrial transfer via ADSC-EVs reprograms glucose/glutamine metabolism to restore TCA cycle-driven M2 polarization in diabetic wounds
Authors: Yue Zhang, Fei Han, Yixuan Yuan, Mengyang Li, Linbin Yi, Yujie Xiao, Dongliang Zhang, Zhi Zhang, Yunchuan Wang, Hongtao Wang, Dahai Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic diabetic wounds are trapped in a persistent inflammatory state, largely due to macrophage failure to transition from pro-inflammatory (M1) to pro-reparative (M2) phenotypes. Here, we show that adipose-derived stem cell extracellular vesicles (ADSC-EVs) deliver functional mitochondria into diabetic wound macrophages, thereby restoring tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle-driven M2 polarization. Mechanistically, ADSC-EV-mediated mitochondrial transfer reactivates pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and pyruvate carboxylase (PC), increases TCA cycle flux, and suggests enhanced glutamine anaplerosis, as evidenced by ¹³C-glucose isotope tracing. This metabolic rewiring restores oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), elevates oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and suppresses glycolysis. Consequently, ADSC-EV treatment reduces M1 macrophages and increases M2 macrophages, lowers pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, MCP1, p < 0.0001), and upregulates IL-10 in vitro, p < 0.0001). In a diabetic mouse wound model, a single course of ADSC-EVs accelerates wound closure at day 14 (p < 0.05), enhances re-epithelialization and collagen deposition, and reduces local oxidative stress and inflammation. Mitochondria‑depleted Rho-ADSC-EVs show markedly diminished effects, confirming that functional mitochondrial transfer is the primary driver. Introduction

The aging population presents a growing burden on global healthcare systems, with rising rates of chronic diseases such as diabetes, a condition strongly linked to impaired wound healing [1]. The 5-year mortality rate among patients with diabetic foot disease is on a par with those suffering from some types of cancer (31%) [2]. Furthermore, chronic diabetic wounds are unable to advance through the four well-coordinated stages of wound healing. Instead, they get stuck in the inflammatory phase, characterized by overactive inflammatory signaling, heightened protease activity, increased levels of reactive oxygen species, diminished growth factor function, and reduced stem cell activity-all of which ultimately result in hindered healing [3, 4]. Among the myriad cellular defects, macrophage dysfunction stands out as a central driver of this healing arrest.

Macrophages, which are long-lived phagocytic cells belonging to the innate immune system, exert vital functions in immune defense, inflammatory responses, and the maintenance of tissue homeostasis. They exhibit remarkable phenotypic diversity and plasticity [5, 6]. During wound healing, macrophages rapidly adapt to their activation state in response to the tissue microenvironment to ensure optimal healing response [7, 8]. The onset of this process is triggered by the recruitment of monocytes in circulation to the injured area, where they initially acquire a pro-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic (M1) phenotype, functioning to bolster microbial clearance and the formation of blood vessels within the affected tissue [9]. Once the immediate threat is resolved, macrophages transition to a pro-repair (M2) phenotype to facilitate tissue regeneration [10]. In chronic wounds, however, the switch from a pro-inflammatory to a pro-reparative phenotype is perturbed, culminating in persistent inflammation [11, 12]. Therefore, deciphering the mechanisms underlying macrophage phenotypic switching remains a frontier of wound healing research.

Immune cell metabolism is intricately linked to activation states and functions, and metabolic intermediates can influence gene expression and phenotypic activation [13–15]. In macrophages, pro-inflammatory polarization critically depends on glycolysis, whereas anti-inflammatory M2 polarization requires functional oxidative phosphorylation and an intact TCA cycle [16, 17]. The TCA cycle not only supports OXPHOS but also supplies metabolites such as α-ketoglutarate (αKG), which epigenetically regulates M2-associated gene expression [18–20].

However, in diabetic wounds, macrophages exhibit a broken TCA cycle, characterized by succinate accumulation and impaired isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) activity, locking them in a glycolytic, pro-inflammatory state [21–23]. Mitochondrial dysfunction lies at the core of this metabolic arrest [24, 25].

Mitochondrial transfer is an emerging field of research[26–28], holding a critical position in intercellular communication, signal transduction, and cellular metabolism, while also exerting an impact on the biological functions of recipient cells [29, 30]. Mitochondrial transfer between cells, like mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) to alveolar epithelial cells or from astrocytes to neurons, enhances the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) capacity of recipient cells and is indispensable for tissue repair processes [31–33]. While it is known that diabetic macrophages harbor TCA cycle defects, it remains entirely unknown whether restoring mitochondrial integrity to these cells via EV-mediated transfer can reset TCA flux and override the inflammatory lock.

Here, we show that ADSC-EVs directly transfer functional mitochondria into diabetic macrophages, reactivating PDH/PC-dependent TCA anaplerosis and OXPHOS, thereby enforcing M2 polarization and rescuing wound healing. Our findings provide mechanistic insight into how mitochondrial transfer regulates macrophage metabolism at the molecular level and highlight its therapeutic potential for diabetic wound healing.

Results

Persistent inflammation and metabolic dysregulation in diabetic wounds

Wound healing is governed by a tightly regulated sequence of cellular signaling events that mediate skin barrier restoration [3, 34]. To clarify the mechanisms that underlie the impaired healing of diabetic wounds, we performed comparative analyses between diabetic and non-diabetic (C57) murine models. Initial evaluation of day 12 post-injury wounds revealed marked differences in macrophage polarization. Immunofluorescence staining of paraffin-embedded wound sections demonstrated a significantly elevated proportion of CD86+ M1 macrophages in diabetic wounds compared to that in C57 controls (p < 0.0001), whereas C57 wounds were enriched with CD206+ M2 macrophages (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1A-D). Consistent with these findings, protein analysis of wound tissues showed marked upregulation of the M1 phenotypic marker iNOS, along with a concurrent downregulation of the M2 markers Arg-1 and CD206 in diabetic wounds (Fig. 1E, F). Collectively, these results demonstrate a pronounced deficit in M2 macrophages, a cell population critical for tissue repair and remodeling in diabetic wounds. In contrast, non-diabetic wounds exhibit an efficient transition to the pro-regenerative M2 phenotype.

Emerging evidence indicates that, while M1 macrophages secrete pro-inflammatory mediators to eliminate necrotic debris, persistent M1 activation disrupts angiogenesis and extracellular matrix synthesis, thereby impeding the critical transition to the anti-inflammatory, pro-repair M2 phenotype and perpetuating inflammatory dysregulation [9, 35, 36]. Supporting this paradigm, quantitative enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) corroborated this inflammatory imbalance, demonstrating increased pro-inflammatory cytokines (Tnf-α, IL-1β, IL-6, MCP1) alongside suppressed anti-inflammatory IL-10 in diabetic wounds (Fig. 1G). Ultrastructural analysis using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) revealed pronounced mitochondrial swelling and organelle damage in diabetic mice, in sharp contrast to the intact mitochondrial architecture observed in C57 controls (Fig. 1H). Immunohistochemistry further confirmed the upregulation of IL-6 and Tnf-α[37] in diabetic wounds (Fig. 1I, J).

Given the pivotal role in cellular energetics[38], we conducted targeted metabolomics to uncover the profound metabolic perturbations in diabetic wounds (Fig. 1K). Diabetic wounds exhibited elevated levels of glycolytic metabolites (Fig. 1L) and accumulation of inflammatory TCA cycle intermediates, such as succinate, acetyl-CoA, and isocitrate (Fig. 1M). These findings suggest that the defective phenotypic transition of macrophages in diabetic wounds results in inflammatory dysregulation and metabolic disturbances, thereby contributing to delayed wound healing.

Diabetic wounds exhibit substantial heterogeneity

To mechanistically dissect impaired healing in diabetic wounds, we conducted single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) of wound samples from diabetic mice. From 58,562 high-quality cells, dimensionality reduction combined with graph-based clustering identified 36 distinct clusters that were annotated into 13 major cell types, including vascular endothelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages, based on canonical marker genes [39] (Fig. 2A). Comparative cellular landscape analysis demonstrated significant alterations in natural killer cell, neutrophil, and macrophage populations between diabetic and control wounds (Fig. 2B). Further multi-omics integration through clustering heatmaps, Gene Ontology, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes analyses uncovered profound pathway heterogeneity. This was apparent in IL-17, TNF, and IFN-γ signaling, along with dysregulation in PD-L1 expression, GTPase activity, Wnt signaling, and cell adhesion pathways (Supplementary Fig. S1). This suggests comprehensive perturbations in inflammatory responses, immune regulation, and metabolic processes in diabetic wound healing.

Given the abnormal activation of M1 macrophages and inflammatory imbalance in diabetic wounds (Fig. 1), we focused on macrophages, which formed 12 subclusters representing distinct molecular states or subtypes (Fig. 2C). Subclusters 1, 2, 5, 6, and 7 demonstrated pronounced transcriptional differences, highlighting the functional diversity of macrophage populations in diabetic wounds (Fig. 2D). Subcluster 2 exhibited a strong pro-inflammatory signature, marked by elevated expression of Lyz2, Plac8, Ifitm3, and Chil3. Subcluster 5 displayed a distinct transcriptional profile involving apoptosis, inflammation, and immune regulation (Mfge8, Cd207, Epcam, Cst3, and Rgs2), whereas subcluster 7 was characterized by a pro-apoptotic gene signature (Lgals7, Krt5, and Cst6) (Fig. 2E). These findings reveal that diabetic wound macrophages exist in multiple dysregulated states, with distinct subpopulations contributing to inflammation, immune dysfunction, and apoptotic pathways, collectively driving impaired wound healing.

We revealed striking differences in the macrophage activation states and metabolic profiles between diabetic and C57BL/6 mouse wounds. Diabetic wounds were characterized by a predominance of M1-like macrophages exhibiting glycolytic gene signatures (Fig. 2F, H), whereas C57BL/6 wounds showed an enrichment of M2-like and OXPHOS gene expression patterns (Fig. 2G, J). TCA cycle enzyme analysis demonstrated downregulation of isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) and upregulation of aconitate decarboxylase (Acod1), a hallmark of M1-like polarization (Fig. 2I), suggesting profound metabolic rewiring in diabetic macrophages [20]. Mechanistically, impaired IDH activity may perpetuate glycolytic gene transcription and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines via the NF-κB and PI3K/AKT signaling pathways [40], thereby creating a self-reinforcing cycle of M1-like activation. These findings align with established models, in which successful wound healing requires a metabolic shift from M1-like/glycolytic to M2-like/oxidative phenotypes in macrophages [16]. Our single-cell resolution analysis not only confirmed this paradigm, but also revealed that TCA cycle disruption in diabetic macrophages locks them in a pro-inflammatory, glycolytic state, ultimately contributing to the chronic inflammatory microenvironment that impairs wound healing.

ADSC-EVs mediate mitochondrial transfer to macrophages

MSCs are capable of spontaneously transferring mitochondria to other cell types, such as alveolar epithelial cells and neurons during tissue repair [31, 32]. MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (MSC-EVs) can release functional mitochondria and restore metabolic programs in damaged cells [33, 41, 42]. However, the therapeutic potential of mitochondrial transfer to diabetic wounds remains unclear. We established a co-culture system of ADSC-EVs and macrophages and systematically validated mitochondrial transfer using complementary experimental approaches.

To investigate mitochondrial transfer, we employed Rhodamine-6G- a mitochondrial fluorescent tracer that irreversibly inhibits mitochondrial function while preserving paracrine activity [31] (Supplementary Fig. S2). EVs were extracted from the conditioned media of untreated or Rhodamine-6G-pretreated ADSCs using differential ultracentrifugation. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) confirmed that Rhodamine-6G treatment did not alter the EV morphology or production yield (Fig. 3A). Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) identified three distinct size populations: 100–200 nm (exosomes), 200–400 nm (microvesicles), and larger particles (> 500 nm), corresponding to the mitochondrial dimensions (Fig. 3B, left). The Rhodamine-6G-treated group showed complete absence of a mitochondrial-sized population (Fig. 3B, right). Western blot analysis revealed the strong expression of canonical EV markers (CD63 and CD81, p < 0.0001) in ADSC-EVs (Fig. 3E, F), and ultrastructural analysis revealed the presence of both typical exosomes and multivesicular bodies (Fig. 3I, left), as well as intact double-membrane mitochondrial structures within EVs (Fig. 3I, right).

To establish mitochondrial incorporation within EVs, we employed a rigorous dual-labeling approach. ADSCs were pre-labeled with MitoTracker Deep Red FM prior to EVs isolation, followed by flow cytometric analysis for simultaneous detection of mitochondrial content and EVs surface markers [31, 41]. This revealed substantial co-localization of ADSC-derived mitochondria with characteristic EVs markers, as evidenced by dual-positive populations for both CD44/MitoTracker (22.1 ± 7.4%) and CD63/MitoTracker (17.7 ± 8.7%) (Fig. 3C). In contrast, EVs derived from Rhodamine-6G-pretreated ADSCs showed negligible mitochondrial signals (Fig. 3D). The presence of functional mitochondria within EVs was further validated by western blot analysis of Tom20, a definitive mitochondrial outer membrane protein, in ADSC-EVs (Fig. 3G, H).

To eliminate potential interspecies variability in mitochondrial function and enable the precise investigation of exogenous mitochondrial regulation, we used a human macrophage model by differentiating THP-1 monocytes with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (Supplementary Fig. S2 C, D). Using this isogenic system, we observed rapid mitochondrial transfer from ADSC-EVs to macrophages within 10 min using live-cell imaging with subsequent mitochondrial fusion events (indicated by yellow merged signals in MitoTracker Red-labeled samples; Supplementary Video 1, Fig. 3J). This transfer was also confirmed by the significant upregulation of mitochondrial markers (VDAC, hsp60, Tom20) in recipient macrophages (Fig. 3K, L). Flow cytometry revealed an enhanced increase in MitoTracker fluorescence intensity in ADSC-EV-treated macrophages compared to that in controls (Fig. 3M, N), demonstrating efficient mitochondrial uptake. These multimodal analyses provide definitive evidence that ADSC-EVs serve as effective mitochondrial donors for macrophages, substantially augmenting their mitochondrial content.

Because ADSC-EV-mediated mitochondrial transfer promotes macrophage M2 polarization and reduces intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels, we investigated the impact of ADSC-EV-mediated mitochondrial transfer on macrophage polarization. Macrophages maintained under physiological glucose conditions (5.5 mM) served as normoglycemic controls, whereas those exposed to high glucose (33 mM) comprised the hyperglycemic group. To control for potential osmotic effects, a hyperosmotic control was established using medium supplemented with 19.5 mM mannitol (total osmolarity equivalent to the hyperglycemic condition) while retaining normoglycemic glucose levels (5.5 mM). The experimental groups consisted of macrophages cultured under high-glucose conditions and treated with either ADSC-EVs or Rho-ADSC-EVs. Flow cytometry revealed that high-glucose conditions significantly elevated the percentage of M1 (iNOS+) macrophages and reduced the percentage of M2 (CD206+) macrophages compared with the normoglycemic and hyperosmotic control groups. ADSC-EV treatment effectively reversed this trend, whereas Rho-ADSC-EVs exerted a diminished effect, implicating mitochondrial cargo as the principal mediator (Fig. 4A, B).

Dual immunofluorescence staining for CD86+ (red) and CD206+ (green) further confirmed that ADSC-EV treatment significantly reduced the red/green ratio compared to that in the hyperglycemic group (Fig. 4C). ELISA analysis of M1-associated cytokines and the M2 anti-inflammatory cytokine demonstrated that ADSC-EVs treatment notably reduced the concentrations of IL-1β, Tnf-α, IL-6, and MCP1, while increasing IL-10 expression (Fig. 4D). Similarly, reverse transcription (RT)-polymerase chain reaction (PCR) results indicated that high-glucose conditions activated M1-associated genes (IL-1β, Tnf-α), whereas ADSC-EVs treatment upregulated M2-associated genes (Arg-1, Nrp1, Vegf, CXCR4) (Supplementary Fig. S3). Western blot analysis further confirmed that ADSC-EVs- and Rho-ADSC-EVs reduced iNOS expression and upregulated the expression of M2 phenotypic markers (Arg-1 and CD206), with ADSC-EVs showing the most pronounced effect (Fig. 4E, F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that ADSC-EVs- and Rho-ADSC-EVs promote M2 macrophage polarization and reduce inflammatory cytokine release, with the mitochondria playing a central role in enhancing these effects.

Metabolic dysregulation in diabetes drives pathological ROS accumulation, triggering cellular mechanisms such as protein kinase C activation and NF-κB-driven vascular inflammation [43, 44]. Excessive ROS production is a critical pathological mechanism underlying cellular damage in diabetic wounds [45]. We further evaluated the effect of ADSC-EVs on intracellular ROS levels. Immunofluorescence staining revealed that both ADSC-EVs- and Rho-ADSC-EVs reduced ROS production in high-glucose-treated macrophages, with ADSC-EVs exhibiting superior efficacy, owing to the presence of functional mitochondria (Fig. 4G, H).

ADSC-EV-mediated mitochondrial transfer ameliorates mitochondrial function and reprograms energy metabolism in macrophages

To evaluate the cytoprotective effects of ADSC-EV-mediated mitochondrial transfer, we first quantified mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) in macrophages via JC-1 staining. High-glucose conditions markedly diminished MMP compared to the normoglycemic controls, whereas ADSC-EV treatment restored MMP to near-baseline levels, suggesting the mitigation of glucose-induced mitochondrial injury (Fig. 5A). Given the pivotal role of mitochondria in bioenergetics, we investigated metabolic flux using Seahorse XF analysis. High glucose levels suppress key parameters of oxidative phosphorylation, including basal respiration, proton leakage, and nonmitochondrial oxygen consumption. ADSC-EVs significantly rescued these deficits, whereas Rho-ADSC-EVs (mitochondria-depleted controls) exhibited only partial efficacy (Fig. 5B, C). Parallel assessment of the extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) revealed that high-glucose hyperactivated glycolysis, a phenotype normalized by ADSC-EV treatment (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these data demonstrated that ADSC-EVs counteracted high glucose-induced metabolic dysfunction by delivering functional mitochondria, thereby shifting macrophage energetics from glycolytic to oxidative metabolism.

Western blot analysis demonstrated that mitochondrial transfer mediated by ADSC-EVs enhanced the expression of critical mitochondrial proteins, such as the outer membrane marker VDAC, matrix chaperone Hsp60, and import receptor Tom20, in high-glucose-treated macrophages (Fig. 5E-F). Concurrently, mitochondrial biogenesis was enhanced, as evidenced by increased levels of the master regulator PPARGC1B (PGC-1β) and the translational elongation factor GFM1 (Fig. 5G-H). Functional validation via ATP quantification demonstrated a significant increase in cellular ATP levels following mitochondrial transfer, corroborating the restoration of oxidative metabolic activity (Fig. 5J). These results collectively establish that ADSC-EVs not only deliver functional mitochondrial components but also activate endogenous biogenesis pathways to sustain metabolic homeostasis under high-glucose stress.

Mitochondrial ROS (mtROS), a dominant contributor to cellular oxidative stress and tissue damage[46, 47], are tightly regulated by mitochondrial dynamics (fusion/fission) and MMP[48]. Using mtROS-specific fluorescent probes, we observed that ADSC-EV treatment significantly attenuated mtROS accumulation in macrophages exposed to high glucose (HG), indicating mitochondrial functional restoration (Fig. 5I, M). Phagocytic functional assays revealed distinct responses across experimental groups: while hyperosmotic and untreated controls exhibited baseline phagocytic activity, HG stimulation markedly enhanced macrophage phagocytosis. ADSC-EV treatment reduced phagocytic capacity compared to Rho-ADSC-EV controls. Given that heightened phagocytosis characterizes pro-inflammatory M1 polarization, whereas M2 macrophages prioritize tissue remodeling, these data, consistent with our previous findings, suggest that ADSC-EVs drive macrophages toward an M2-like reparative phenotype (Fig. 5K, L). Collectively, these results demonstrate that the mitochondria delivered by ADSC-EVs are internalized and utilized by macrophages, thereby improving mitochondrial function and energy metabolism.

ADSC-EV-mediated mitochondrial transfer accelerates diabetic wound healing in vivo

To validate our in vitro findings, we established a diabetic wound healing model in mice (blood glucose > 16.7 mmol/L, confirming successful induction of diabetes [49]. Full-thickness circular excisional wounds with a diameter of 1 cm were established on the dorsal region and administered with ADSC-EVs, Rho-ADSC-EVs (mitochondria-depleted control), or phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Longitudinal assessment of wound closure revealed that treatment with ADSC-EVs notably improved the wound healing rate in comparison to both control groups (Fig. 6A, G). Although Rho-ADSC-EVs showed some therapeutic benefits, their effect was markedly attenuated relative to that of intact ADSC-EVs (p < 0.01), demonstrating that the pro-healing effects are largely dependent on functional mitochondrial transfer.

Histopathological evaluation of wound tissues on day 12 by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Masson’s trichrome staining [50] demonstrated superior tissue regeneration in the ADSC-EV-treated groups. HE staining showed that the ADSC-EV-treated wounds exhibited the shortest wound length and complete re-epithelialization (Fig. 6B, left). Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated well-arranged collagen fibers and hair follicle structures in the ADSC-EV-treated wounds (Fig. 6B, right). The Rho-ADSC-EV group showed partial improvement, whereas the PBS controls exhibited delayed epithelial migration and disordered extracellular matrix organization. These histological findings provide compelling morphological evidence that functional mitochondrial transfer constitutes the principal mechanism underlying ADSC-EV-mediated wound healing, promoting both structural and functional skin regeneration through enhanced re-epithelialization and extracellular matrix remodeling.

Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that ADSC-EV treatment markedly modulated the wound immune microenvironment by reducing CD86+ M1 macrophages and increasing CD206+ M2 macrophages compared to the PBS controls (Fig. 6D, E, J, K). Furthermore, ADSC-EVs treatment attenuated oxidative stress (Fig. 6F) and suppressed key inflammatory mediators IL-6 and Tnf-α (Fig. 6C, H, I). These findings collectively demonstrate that mitochondrial transfer via ADSC-EVs orchestrates an anti-inflammatory switch in macrophage polarization, reduces oxidative damage, and establishes a pro-regenerative microenvironment critical for diabetic wound healing.

ADSC-EV-mediated mitochondrial transfer boosts macrophage TCA cycle through dual glucose and glutamine metabolism

To precisely delineate the metabolic consequences of mitochondrial transfer, we performed stable isotope tracing with 13C6-glucose coupled with metabolic flux analysis (Fig. 7). ADSC-EV treatment significantly increased the enrichment of 13C6-citrate and 13C3-citrate compared to mitochondrial-depleted Rho-ADSC-EVs controls, demonstrating enhanced flux through both pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) and pyruvate carboxylase (PC) pathways (Fig. 7A). The combined enrichment of citrate isotopologs (13C2 + 13C3) also increased, indicating elevated citrate synthase (CS) activity and TCA cycle turnover (Fig. 7B, C). We also observed decreased 13C enrichment in α-ketoglutarate (αKG) downstream metabolites such as 13C2-succinate and 13C2-malate, suggesting that the metabolism of unlabeled ¹²C₅-glutamine may provide a substantial anaplerotic contribution (Fig. 7D). These results established that ADSC-EV-delivered mitochondria enhanced glucose-derived pyruvate oxidation and TCA cycle flux, thereby creating a metabolically active state conducive to tissue repair.

Glutamine metabolism represents a metabolic hallmark of M2 macrophage polarization, in which glutamate metabolism serves as a central metabolic node [51]. This process involves coordinated transcriptional regulation of key metabolites (glutamate, αKG, and ornithine) and their metabolic enzymes (e.g., GATM, ARG1, OAT, GOT2, and GPT2). The present findings suggest that glutamine may fuel the TCA cycle and provide metabolic support for macrophage polarization. Nevertheless, direct ¹³C-glutamine tracing experiments are still required in future studies to further clarify the specific role of glutamine-mediated anaplerosis.

Discussion

Macrophages serve as pivotal mediators of skin wound healing, where their functional plasticity facilitates phenotypic adaptation to microenvironmental cues. Dysregulation of the transition from pro-inflammatory (M1) to pro-reparative (M2) states substantially compromises tissue regeneration. Our investigation systematically addressed this clinical challenge through three integrated approaches: (1) single-cell-resolution mapping of macrophage heterogeneity in diabetic versus physiological healing; (2) mechanistic dissection of the tissue injury-metabolism-polarization axis; and (3) therapeutic exploitation of mitochondrial transfer via ADSC-EVs. ADSC-EV-mediated mitochondrial transfer improved mitochondrial function and facilitated M2 macrophage polarization, conferring significant therapeutic benefits. These effects are primarily driven by the enhancement of glucose and glutamine metabolism, which augment TCA cycle activity, thereby promoting anti-inflammatory responses and tissue regeneration during wound repair. This metabolic reprogramming strategy holds particular promise for chronic diabetic wounds in which macrophage polarization is profoundly dysregulated.

Chronic diabetic wounds represent one of the most severe complications of diabetes and serve as the leading cause of non-traumatic lower limb amputations, with a post-amputation 5-year mortality rate exceeding 70%2,4. Comparative analysis of diabetic and physiological wound healing revealed a striking deficit in M2 macrophages, which are key mediators of tissue repair and remodeling in diabetic lesions. Conversely, these wounds demonstrated sustained M1-like pro-inflammatory macrophage activation, indicating a state of chronic inflammation. Using our single-cell RNA sequencing approach, we identified discrete macrophage subpopulations (clusters 1, 2, 5, 6, and 7) that exhibited hallmark M1 activation signatures and glycolytic metabolic programming in diabetic wounds.

During the early stages of wound healing, glycolysis ensures rapid ATP production in macrophages, enabling critical effector functions, such as microbial defense and cytokine synthesis [16]. As healing progresses, macrophages must transition to an OXPHOS-dominated metabolism to generate sufficient ATP for tissue repair and remodeling [13, 34, 52]. Mitochondria serve as central hubs for this metabolic shift, and their functional integrity is critical for proper OXPHOS activity [53, 54]. Under diabetic conditions, chronic hyperglycemia promotes the pathological phosphorylation of dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1) at Ser616, triggering excessive mitochondrial fission and bioenergetic failure [55–57]. These fragmented mitochondria show impaired TCA cycle function and reduced efficiency of the electron transport chain. This may explain the observed deficit in OXPHOS-dependent M2 macrophages, a hallmark of impaired diabetic wound healing [58, 59].

Mitochondrial transfer is an innovative strategy for diseases involving mitochondrial dysfunction [60–62]. It not only replaces damaged mitochondria but also restores normal metabolic programs in recipient cells [63]. However, isolated mitochondria face several challenges: maintaining functional integrity in high extracellular Ca²⁺, achieving efficient transmembrane transfer and internalization, and avoiding oxidative stress or inflammatory responses [64]. MSC transplantation has shown promise for treating various diseases, with mitochondrial transfer increasingly being recognized as a key mechanism underlying their therapeutic effects [27, 65].

Compared with isolated mitochondria, EVs derived from stem cells offer several advantages. Their lipid bilayer structure protects the mitochondria from extracellular Ca²⁺ damage and facilitates rapid fusion with recipient cell membranes, enhancing mitochondrial delivery [66, 67]. Umbilical cord MSCs transfer mitochondria to liver neutrophils via EVs. This induces mitochondrial fusion and restores neutrophil functionality, thereby mitigating ischemia‑reperfusion injury [68]. Similarly, MSC-EVs transfer mitochondria to pulmonary epithelial cells, enhancing barrier integrity in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [31], while neural stem cell-derived EVs deliver mitochondria to neurons, enhancing neuronal viability and restoring metabolic function [32]. Our assessment of mitochondrial transfer relies on indirect fluorescence intensity and protein expression measurements rather than absolute organelle quantification, and this should be interpreted with due caution. Consistent with these findings, we demonstrated that mitochondrial cargo within ADSC-EVs was internalized by macrophages, significantly improving wound healing in diabetic mice. In vitro experiments verified that ADSC-EVs facilitated M2 polarization, reduced inflammatory cytokine release, and ameliorated oxidative stress, highlighting the central role of mitochondria in their therapeutic effects.

Mechanistic studies have demonstrated that hyperglycemia induces mitochondrial dysfunction in macrophages through mtROS overproduction and membrane potential dissipation. In this process, oxygen consumption by macrophages decreases and glycolysis increases. However, the internalization of exogenous mitochondria via ADSC-EVs restores mitochondrial function, enhances cellular respiration, and reduces glycolysis. Additionally, ADSC-EVs promoted mitochondrial biogenesis and increased ATP production. The transferred mitochondria can deliver mtDNA encoding 13 essential proteins of the ETC, rapidly initiating OXPHOS to alleviate energy deficits in recipient cells [28, 69]. Even minimal quantities of exogenous mtDNA can integrate into the recipient’s mitochondrial network, thereby effectively rescuing cells from mtDNA depletion [28]. We hypothesize that although transferred mitochondria account for a small proportion of the total mitochondrial pool, their role in promoting mitochondrial biogenesis is critical to enhance the macrophage energy supply.

Our findings demonstrate that stem cell-EVs mediate functional mitochondrial integration into the endogenous networks of recipient cells, exerting beneficial effects. However, the underlying mechanisms remain elusive. A small fraction of exogenous mitochondria can orchestrate global metabolic and functional reprogramming. Elucidating how this happens presents a fundamental challenge. Our ¹³C‑glucose tracing data show increased TCA cycle flux and suggest possible anaplerotic contributions from glutamine, as indicated by the dilution of ¹³C‑labeled succinate and malate. We acknowledge that this interpretation is indirect. The relative contributions of glucose versus glutamine to TCA cycle intermediates may vary depending on metabolic context, and other anaplerotic substrates (e.g., fatty acids) could also contribute. Future studies using direct ¹³C‑glutamine tracing will be needed to confirm the specific role of glutamine anaplerosis. Nevertheless, our data clearly demonstrate that ADSC‑EV‑mediated mitochondrial transfer enhances overall TCA cycle activity, which is consistent with the metabolic shift toward M2 polarization. This enables metabolic plasticity in the resolution of inflammation. This explains why Rho-ADSC-EVs (lacking mitochondria) fail to fully reverse polarization despite their paracrine effects.

EV-mediated mitochondrial delivery has emerged as a transformative therapeutic platform. Its efficacy has been demonstrated in targeting molecular pathways such as HSP27 to preserve blood-brain barrier integrity during ischemia-reperfusion injury[67]. Our work establishes a foundational framework for clinical translation in diabetic wound therapy, particularly through the selective targeting of metabolically compromised cells. This is a strategy supported by the preferential integration and functional rescue observed in mitochondrial-deficient models (Rho⁰ cells and C macrophages) [70, 71]. Crucially, this approach exhibits intrinsic selectivity. Exogenous mitochondria robustly restore oxidative metabolism in dysfunctional cells. However, their persistence remains limited in normal microenvironments, suggesting a built-in safety mechanism against off-target effects.

Nevertheless, several challenges must be addressed before clinical application. First, scalability and GMP (good manufacturing practice) compliance remain major hurdles. Producing ADSC‑EVs with consistent mitochondrial cargo requires serum‑free, xeno‑free culture systems, along with robust quality controls such as mitochondrial membrane potential and mtDNA copy number per EV. Second, safety concerns need further evaluation. Allogeneic mitochondrial transfer carries potential risks of immune rejection or unintended transfer of damaged mitochondria. While our Rho‑ADSC‑EV control suggests that mitochondria‑depleted EVs have minimal effects, long‑term biodistribution and immunogenicity studies are still lacking. Third, regulatory and standardization issues are unresolved.

Despite these challenges, the therapeutic potential is substantial. Future research should focus on spatiotemporal control of mitochondrial transfer and its functional adaptation to metabolic demands. Bridging the translational divide between animal models and human pathophysiology will be imperative to accelerate clinical adoption.

Methods

Sex as a biological variable

Our study examined male and female animals, and similar findings are reported for both sexes.

Cell culture

The human monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1 was commercially sourced from the China Center for Type Culture Collection. Cells were propagated in RPMI-1640 culture medium (Gibco) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 100 IU/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin, and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. For macrophage differentiation, THP-1 monocytes were stimulated with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) in complete medium over 48 h, inducing characteristic morphological conversion from non-adherent to adherent phenotypes. To model diabetic pathophysiology, differentiated macrophages underwent 24-h preconditioning in high-glucose milieu (33 mM D-glucose) preceding functional assays.

Isolation, expansion, and phenotypic characterization of ADSCs

Subcutaneous adipose tissue specimens were procured from consenting patients undergoing elective liposuction procedures. Minced tissue fragments underwent enzymatic digestion using 1 mg/mL collagenase type I (Gibco) at 37 °C for 60 min with constant agitation. The resultant digestate was sequentially filtered through 100-µm nylon meshes (Millipore) and centrifuged at 200 × g for 5 min. Pelleted stromal vascular fraction cells were resuspended in specialized ADSC growth medium (Gibco) and maintained at 37 °C/5% CO2, with medium replenishment every 72 h.

EVs isolation from ADSCs

EVs were harvested from ADSC-conditioned medium through differential ultracentrifugation [32]. The protocol initiated with cellular debris removal via centrifugation (2,000 × g, 45 min, 4 °C). The clarified supernatant subsequently underwent ultracentrifugation (100,000 × g, 3 h, 4 °C) to sediment EVs. The pelleted EVs were washed with PBS and reconstituted in PBS (10 µL per 106 ADSCs). EV protein content was quantified by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Beyotime), with experimental dosing standardized to EVs derived from 1 × 106 ADSCs per 104 macrophages (10 µL volume).

For generation of mitochondria-depleted ADSCs (Rho-ADSCs), cultures were exposed to 1 µg/mL Rhodamine-6G (Sigma-Aldrich) for 48 h in uridine-supplemented medium (50 µg/mL) containing 2.5 mM sodium pyruvate [72], followed by 48-h serum-free conditioning. Rho-ADSC-derived EVs (Rho-ADSC-EVs) were isolated using identical ultracentrifugation parameters as control ADSC-EVs.

TEM

EVs were immobilized in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 30 min at ambient temperature (22–25 °C), subjected to three PBS washes, and adsorbed onto Formvar/carbon-coated copper grids. Specimens underwent dual-contrast enhancement through sequential staining with 1% tannic acid and 2% glutaraldehyde, followed by imaging using a JEOL JEM-1400Plus transmission electron microscope operated at 80 kV.

NTA

EV size distribution and concentration were quantified using NTA on a Nanosight NS300 platform (Malvern Panalytical) configured with a 488-nm excitation laser and scientific CMOS (sCMOS) detector. Per sample, six consecutive 60-s video captures were acquired under controlled fluid flow at 25 °C, with data processed using NTA 3.4 software following manufacturer-recommended calibration protocols.

Metabolic and functional assays

ATP measurement

Post-confluent human macrophage cultures were exposed to ADSC-EVs (or relevant controls) for 24 h to assess mitochondrial transfer efficiency. Total intracellular ATP content was determined via luminescent ATP detection assay (Beyotime) according to manufacturer specifications, with measurements normalized to total cellular protein.

OCR assessment

To examine changes in the bioenergetic profile of human macrophages after exposure to ADSC-EVs, we determined the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) with a Seahorse XFe96 analyzer (Agilent). Macrophages were plated into XF96 microplates at a density of 30,000 cells per well and co-cultured with ADSC-EVs for 24 h. Before the assay, any EVs that had not been internalized by the cells were eliminated by replacing the culture medium. For OCR measurement, the cells were treated sequentially with the following compounds: 1.5 µM oligomycin (an inhibitor of ATP synthase), 1 µM FCCP (a mitochondrial uncoupling agent), and a mixture of 1.25 µM rotenone and 2.5 µM antimycin A (inhibitors of the ETC). The resulting data were normalized against cell numbers and analyzed using Seahorse Wave Software.

ECAR assay

Human macrophages were plated in XF96 plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well and allowed to equilibrate in XF medium containing 2 mM glutamine, 10 mM glucose, and 1 mM pyruvate. Glycolytic flux was quantified via the Seahorse XFe96 analyzer using sequential injections of oligomycin (10 µM), 2-DG (50 mM), and HCl (100 mM). Data (mpH/min/µg protein, normalized to BCA) derived from 3-min measurement cycles defined basal ECAR, glycolytic capacity, and reserve.

MMP analysis

MMP was assessed utilizing the JC-1 fluorescent probe (Beyotime). Cells were incubated with JC-1 staining solution at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by two washes with JC-1 buffer. Subsequent analysis was performed using either fluorescence microscopy or flow cytometry. MMP levels were quantified as the ratio of red fluorescence intensity (from aggregates, indicating high potential) to green fluorescence intensity (from monomers, indicating low potential).

Quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the specimens using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The purity and concentration of RNA were checked by spectrophotometry, with the A260/A280 ratio being greater than 1.8. First-strand complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 1 µg of total RNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara). Quantitative PCR amplification was performed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The specificity of amplification was confirmed by melting curve analysis. All reactions were run in triplicate, with suitable negative controls included.

Gene expression levels were normalized to Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), a housekeeping gene, and quantified using the comparative ΔΔCt method. The sequences of the primers (synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology), which generated amplicons ranging in size from 112 to 267 bp, are provided in Table 1. Primer efficiency, ranging from 90% to 110%, was validated through standard curve analysis.

ROS measurement

Intracellular and mitochondrial ROS levels were fluorometrically quantified using specific molecular probes. For cytosolic ROS detection, primary human macrophages were loaded with 2 µM dihydroethidium (DHE; Beyotime) at 37 °C/5% CO2 for 30 min, then imaged with an FSX100 epifluorescence microscope (Olympus). Mitochondrial superoxide generation was monitored via MitoSOX™ Red (5 µM; Thermo Fisher Scientific) under identical incubation parameters. Fluorescence intensities were quantified by threshold-based segmentation in ImageJ software (NIH) and normalized to Hoechst 33,342-stained nuclei counts.

ELISA

Cell culture supernatants were harvested and clarified by centrifugation (300 g, 10 min) to eliminate cellular particulates. Concentrations of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-6, MCP-1, and IL-10 were quantified using ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific) performed in strict adherence to manufacturer protocols. Briefly, 100 µL aliquots of supernatant or standard solutions were transferred to pre-coated antibody wells and subjected to 2-h incubation at ambient temperature (23 ± 2 °C). Following three wash cycles, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated detection antibodies were introduced for 60 min. Chromogenic development was initiated with 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) substrate, terminated by 2 N sulfuric acid (H2SO4) addition. Absorbance at 450 nm (reference 570 nm) was recorded using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (BioTek Instruments). Analyte concentrations were interpolated from assay-specific standard curves generated for logistic regression.

Flow cytometry

For EV characterization [73], isolated EVs were conjugated to 4-µm aldehyde/sulfate latex beads (Thermo Scientific) by incubating 100 µL EVs fraction with 0.25 µL beads for 20 min at RT. The mixture was stabilized with 1 mL PBS containing 0.1% BSA overnight at 4 °C. Bead-EV complexes were washed by centrifugation (2,000 × g, 10 min) and stained with fluorescent antibodies against CD63-FITC (1:1,000) and CD44-PE-Cy7 (1:1,000; both from Abcam) for 45 min at 4 °C. EV-positive populations were gated based on forward/side scatter parameters of the 4-µm bead population using a BD FACSAria™ III flow cytometer.

For macrophage phenotyping, cells were stained with anti-iNOS-APC (1:1,000), anti-CD206-PE (1:1,000), and anti-F4/80-FITC (1:1,000; all from Abcam) for 30 min at 4 °C. Following washing, the cells were analyzed using the BD FACSAria™ III flow cytometer. Data were processed using FlowJo software (v10.8.1), with compensation performed using single-stained controls and fluorescence-minus-one controls for gating.

Cellular uptake study

The intracellular uptake kinetics of human macrophages were quantitatively assessed by flow cytometry (BD FACSAria™ III system) through measurement of DiI MFI using a 488-nm argon-ion laser [74]. In brief, macrophages were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h of culture, the cells were incubated with DiI-labeled fluorescent microspheres (Beyotime) at a final concentration of 100 ng/mL, with the incubation lasting for 1 h. Subsequently, the cells were collected in 500 µL of PBS and subjected to flow cytometric analysis.

Mitochondrial transfer assay

Live-cell imaging

ADSCs were labeled with 200 nM MitoTracker Deep Red (Thermo Scientific) for 45 min, washed, and cultured in a serum-free medium for 48 h to generate EVs. Macrophages were counterstained with 200 nM MitoTracker Green (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and co-cultured with ADSC-derived EVs. Mitochondrial transfer was visualized using confocal microscopy (Nikon NIS-Elements, Tokyo, Japan) and quantified via flow cytometry.

Flow cytometry

Single-cell populations were gated while excluding debris (n = 4). Untreated macrophages (gray histogram) showed baseline fluorescence, whereas Rho-ADSC-EV-treated (green) and ADSC-EV-treated (red) macrophages exhibited rightward shifts in FITC fluorescence intensity, indicating differential mitochondrial uptake. Isotype controls were incorporated, and cell viability was over 95%, which was verified through trypan blue exclusion assay.

Cellular immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized using 0.3% Triton X-100, and blocked with 10% goat serum. Primary antibodies targeting CD86 and CD206 (1:200, Abcam) were incubated overnight at 4 °C, after which Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:100, Abcam) were applied for 1 h at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and images were captured using an FSX100 microscope (Olympus).

13C6-Glucose LC‐MS metabolomics

Human macrophages were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 4 × 105 cells per well and randomly assigned to three groups: control, ADSC-EV-treated, and Rho-ADSC-EV-treated. After being cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) for 24 h, the cells were incubated with uniformly labeled [¹³C₆]-glucose (25 mM; Sigma) in fresh DMEM for 4 h. Post-incubation, the cells were rinsed three times with ice-cold PBS and lysed in 1 mL of pre-cooled 80% methanol at −80 °C for 2 h. The cell lysates were subsequently centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was measured using a BCA assay, and the supernatant was dried by evaporation with a SpeedVac concentrator.

13C enrichment was analyzed by LC-MS on a Q-Exactive HF-X system (Thermo Scientific), with chromatographic peaks identified by retention time and accurate mass (TraceFinder 4.1 software). Metabolite quantification was performed by peak area integration and normalized to cell number and total protein content. Isotopolog distributions were determined by summing all mass spectral peaks for each metabolite, with 13C enrichment expressed as the molar percentage enrichment relative to the total carbon pool.

Western blot analysis

Protein lysates were fractionated by SDS-PAGE (10% resolving gel) and electrophoretically transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes underwent 2-h blocking at ambient temperature with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with primary antibodies (all 1:1,000; Abcam) targeting: iNOS, Arg-1, CD206, CD63, CD81, Tom20, PPARGC1B, GFM1, VDAC, and Hsp60. β-Actin (1:3,000) served as the loading control. After three TBST washes, membranes were probed with HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:3,000; Boster) at 37 °C for 60 min. Protein bands were visualized via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL Prime; Millipore) using a FluorChem M imaging system (Alpha Innotech), with densitometric quantification performed in ImageJ (NIH) after normalization to β-actin expression.

In vivo wound healing in mice

Six-week-old male Balb/c mice were procured from the Laboratory Animal Center of Fourth Military Medical University (Xi’an, China). Diabetes mellitus was induced via intraperitoneal streptozotocin (STZ) administration (50 mg/kg/day for 5 consecutive days) following established protocols [75]. One week post-induction, after maintenance on high-fat/high-glucose diet (60% kcal fat, 20% fructose water), tail vein blood glucose levels exceeded 16.7 mmol/L in all subjects, confirming diabetic status for subsequent wound healing experiments.

All mice received 10-mm full-thickness dorsal wounds using sterile surgical scissors. From postoperative day 1, the wounds received daily subcutaneous injections of either of the following: (1) 100 µg ADSC-EVs in 0.5 mL PBS, or (2) PBS vehicle control (n = 5/group) for 3 consecutive days. Wound closure was monitored via daily calibrated photography (specific camera/software) until sacrifice at predetermined endpoints, and the harvested tissues were processed for histological/molecular analysis.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence staining of tissue

Skin tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in either paraffin or optical cutting temperature compound, and sectioned at 4 μm for HE, Masson’s trichrome, and immunostaining with anti-CD86, -CD206, and -F4/80 antibodies (1:200; Abcam). For immunofluorescence, sections were permeabilized, blocked, and incubated with primary antibodies, followed by ROS detection (CellROX; Beyotime) and DAPI nuclear counterstaining. Images were captured with an Olympus FSX100 microscope and analyzed utilizing ImageJ software (NIH).

Electron microscopy

Skin specimens underwent primary fixation in 2.5% glutaraldehyde/0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) at 4 °C for 48 h, followed by post-fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h at ambient temperature. After triple-washing in PBS (10 min/wash), samples were progressively dehydrated through an ethanol gradient (50%, 70%, 90%, 100%) and embedded in Epoxy Embedding Medium EPON 812 Resin (Sigma-Aldrich). Ultrathin Sect. (50 nm) were prepared using a Leica EM UC7 ultramicrotome equipped with a diamond knife, transferred to 200-mesh copper grids, and subjected to sequential staining: 2% uranyl acetate in methanol (15 min) and Reynolds’ lead citrate (5 min). Mitochondrial architecture was characterized using a Zeiss EVO MA10 field-emission scanning electron microscope equipped with an X-MaxN 50 EDS detector, operating at 15 kV accelerating voltage. For each cohort, ≥ 20 representative fields were imaged at 10,000–30,000× magnification by investigators blinded to group allocation.

Single-cell preparation from skin samples

Skin specimens were maintained in sterile PBS on ice and processed within 3 h of excision. Following sequential disinfection in 10% povidone-iodine, 70% ethanol, and PBS (1 min each), tissues were digested with 5 mg/mL Dispase II in Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution at 4 °C overnight. The epidermis was removed mechanically, and the remaining tissue was minced into fragments (< 1 mm) before secondary digestion in 3.3 mg/mL Dispase II (37 °C, 90 min, 200 rpm). The obtained cell suspension was passed through 70-µm and 40-µm filters, then centrifuged at 500 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, and resuspended in PBS containing 0.04% ultrapure BSA. Final cell viability exceeded 90% (trypan blue exclusion) with concentration adjusted to 1 × 103 cells/µL.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Cells derived from wounds of db/db mice and C57BL/6J wild-type mice were prepared for flow cytometric analysis. CD45+CD11b+F4/80+ cell populations were index-sorted into 384-well plates containing lysis buffer (0.5 µL nuclease-free water, 0.2% Triton X-100, and 4 U murine RNase inhibitor [NEB]). Single-cell gene expression libraries were constructed using Chromium 3’ v2/v3 kits (10x Genomics), with barcoded gel bead-in-emulsions (GEMs) generated using the Chromium Controller. Following RT, cDNA libraries were quantified with a Qubit 3.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and quality-checked using 2100 Bioanalyzer HS DNA chips (Agilent). Subsequent sequencing was performed on a NovaSeq S4 platform (Illumina), producing 40,000–50,000 reads per cell that covered 1,000–2,000 transcripts per cell.

MS assay

Protein samples underwent extraction, reduction, alkylation, and enzymatic digestion with trypsin. Chromatographic separation was accomplished using nanoflow reverse-phase liquid chromatography (nanoLC) on a C18 column, employing a linear gradient of acetonitrile (0.1% formic acid) at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Mass spectrometric analysis was carried out on a Q-Exactive HF-X hybrid quadrupole-Orbitrap mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) running in data-dependent acquisition mode. Full-scan MS spectra (m/z 350–1800) were obtained at a resolution of 60,000, after which high-energy collisional dissociation was performed on the 20 most intense precursor ions (normalized collision energy: 30%). Raw data were analyzed using MaxQuant (v2.0.3) with the UniProt proteome database, with a 1% false discovery rate threshold applied at both the peptide and protein levels. Label-free quantification was utilized for differential expression analysis.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test; all data groups passed both tests (p > 0.05), justifying the use of parametric methods. For comparisons between two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was applied. For comparisons involving more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software). A significance threshold of p < 0.05 was used for all analyses. We chose to report mean ± SD rather than standard error of the mean (SEM) because SD better reflects the variability within each experimental group, which is more appropriate for the in vitro and in vivo experiments presented in this study.
