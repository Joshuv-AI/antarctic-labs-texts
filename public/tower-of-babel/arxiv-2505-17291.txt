Optimal Transport with Heterogeneously Missing Data
Linus Bleistein, Aurélien Bellet, Julie Josse

Abstract
We consider the problem of solving the optimal transport problem between two empirical distributions with missing values. Our main assumption is that the data is missing completely at random (MCAR), but we allow for heterogeneous missingness probabilities across features and across the two distributions. As a first contribution, we show that the Wasserstein distance between empirical Gaussian distributions and linear Monge maps between arbitrary distributions can be debiased without significantly affecting the sample complexity. Secondly, we show that entropic regularized optimal transport can be estimated efficiently and consistently using iterative singular value thresholding (ISVT). We propose a validation set-free hyperparameter selection strategy for ISVT that leverages our estimator of the Bures-Wasserstein distance, which could be of independent interest in general matrix completion problems. Finally, we validate our findings on a wide range of numerical applications.

Introduction

Data missingness is one of the most common challenges faced by data science practitioners: for some individuals, certain features are not observed and are represented as

15\%
of the rows are removed when
d=3
; for
d=100
, this percentage increases to
99.5\%

, where missing values are replaced by a fixed value such as the mean. Between these extremes lies a range of more sophisticated models that impute missing values by leveraging observed data
[
13
,
50
,
51
,
49
,
34
]
.

In practice, missing values are handled in different fashions depending on the final objective, be it supervised learning, parameter estimation, or matrix completion. When the final goal is a supervised learning task, a relative empirical consensus has emerged in the last few years
[
43
,
39
]
, backed by theoretical findings
[
33
,
3
,
27
]
: the specific choice of imputation matters less than the choice of a well-suited and expressive predictive model
[

. For instance,
Morvan and Varoquaux 2024
showed in a recent study that even substantial improvements in imputation accuracy lead to only marginal gains in downstream prediction accuracy across a wide range of datasets.

When the goal is
not
to predict outcomes in a supervised fashion—a setting in which model expressivity can compensate for data missingness—but to estimate statistics or to compare empirical distributions, the picture is more subtle. For instance, naive constant imputation will bias higher order moments of a distribution by lowering its variance: hence, the distributions will be distorted by the imputation method. Put otherwise, in such settings, one ideally seeks an imputation method that preserves the moments of the underlying data
[
51
,
9

Comparing distributions requires a well-defined notion of distance. Optimal Transport (OT), first introduced by
Monge 1781

how can we solve the optimal transport problem with incomplete data to mitigate the bias introduced by missingness?

Contributions and Outline.

Our contribution is threefold. First, in Section
4
, we provide a precise analysis of the effect of naive imputation on the optimal transport problem, showing that it implicitly biases the cost function. Second, in Section
5
, we devise a consistent, dimension-free estimator of the Bures-Wasserstein distance by leveraging recent results in high-dimensional covariance estimation, and in particular a novel non-commutative Bernstein inequality
[
42
]
. The modular structure of our approach further allows us to derive new generalization bounds for domain adaptation with missing values. Third, in Section
6
, we move beyond the Gaussian setting to address general entropic regularized optimal transport between distributions supported on compact sets. We establish the consistency of a two-step procedure based on iterative singular value thresholding using recent cost-sensitivity bounds on entropic regularized optimal transport
[
28
]
, and show that our Bures-Wasserstein estimator enables a highly effective validation set-free strategy for hyperparameter selection. Finally, Section
7
presents a comprehensive set of numerical experiments.

4
Bias and Implicit Regularization of Optimal Transport with Missing Data

As shown in Figure
1
, handling missing values through naive imputation distorts the geometry of the optimal transport problem. In this section, we rigorously quantify this effect by building on recent results in linear regression with missing data
[
3
]
, showing that naive imputation introduces an
implicit regularization
into the optimal transport formulation. Let

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{\mathbf{M}}\in\argmin\limits_{\mathbf{\Pi}\in\Pi}\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\sum\limits_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{\Pi}_{ij}\lVert\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\texttt{NA}}-\mathbf{y}_{i}^{\texttt{NA}}\rVert^{2}_{\mathbf{M}}\,|\,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y}\Big].

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\overline{\mathbf{M}}:=\mathbf{M}-\mathbf{P}\mathbf{M}\mathbf{Q}=[(1-p_{i}q_{j})m_{ij}]_{i,j=1}^{d}
. The transport map
\overline{\mathbf{\Pi}}_{\mathbf{M}}
solves the optimal transport problem

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{\Pi}\in\Pi}\textnormal{Tr}\Big[\mathbf{\Pi}^{\top}\big(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{X}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{Y}\big)\Big].

This proposition shows that, on average, naive imputation implicitly regularizes the cost function of the optimal transport problem by reducing the cost of matchings that are both geometrically aligned—according to the metric defined by
\mathbf{M}
—and infrequently observed, that is, when
\mathbf{x}_{i}^{\top}\overline{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{y}_{i}>0
. Taking this analysis a step further, we also derive a lower bound on the Wasserstein distance computed from incomplete data.

Proposition 4.2
.

Assume for the sake of clarity that
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}=\textnormal{diag}(\sigma^{2}_{1},\dots,\sigma^{2}_{d})
with
\sigma_{i}>0
for all
i=1,\dots,d
and
\mathbf{M}=\textnormal{diag}(m_{1},\dots,m_{d})
. Then if
\mathbf{P}\neq\mathbf{I}_{d}

\displaystyle\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu^{\textnormal{{NA}}},\mu)\geq\lVert(\mathbf{P}-\mathbf{I}_{d})\mathbf{m}_{x}\rVert_{\mathbf{M}}^{2}+\mathscr{B}_{\mathbf{M}}^{2}(\mathbf{P})>0

where
\mathscr{B}_{\mathbf{M}}^{2}(\mathbf{P}):=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{d}m_{i}\Big[\sigma_{i}-\sqrt{p_{i}}\sqrt{\sigma^{2}_{i}+(1-p_{i})(\mathbf{m}_{x})^{2}_{i}}\Big]^{2}
.

This result shows in particular that
\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu^{\textnormal{{NA}}},\mu)>\mathbf{W}_{2}^{2}(\mu,\mu)=0
if at least one variable
i
is not perfectly observed
and

m_{ii}>0
, which highlights the importance of the interaction between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{M}
. As expected, this lower bound collapses to
0
when
\mathbf{P}\to\mathbf{I}_{d}
. Crucially, even if
\mathbf{m}_{x}=\mathbf{0}
(i.e., naive imputation is unbiased), the Wasserstein distance is still bounded away from
0
due to the difference in variance induced by constant imputation. These results motivate our next contributions, in which we develop estimators of the Wasserstein distance that are
asymptotically invariant
to missing data—that is, they converge to the true value of
\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{M}}(\mu,\eta)
as
n,m\to\infty
.

5.1
Estimating the Bures-Wasserstein Distance with Missing Values

We build upon the literature on covariance matrix estimation with missing data

\displaystyle\mathbf{P}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{P}^{-1}\big)\textnormal{diag}\big(\mathbf{S}^{\textnormal{{NA}}}_{xn}\big)+\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{S}^{\textnormal{{NA}}}_{xn}\mathbf{P}^{-1},\textnormal{ where }\mathbf{S}^{\textnormal{{NA}}}_{xn}=n^{-1}\sum\big(\mathbf{x}^{\textnormal{{NA}}}_{i}-\mathbf{m}^{\textnormal{{NA}}}_{xn}\big)\big(\mathbf{x}^{\textnormal{{NA}}}_{i}-\mathbf{m}^{\textnormal{{NA}}}_{xn}\big)^{\top}

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbf{\Sigma}}_{x}:=\mathbf{P}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{P}^{-1}\big)\textnormal{diag}\big(\mathbf{S}^{\textnormal{{NA}}}_{xn}\big)+\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{S}^{\textnormal{{NA}}}_{xn}\mathbf{P}^{-1}+\mathbf{P}^{-1}\big(\mathbf{I}_{d}-\mathbf{P}^{-1}\big)\textnormal{diag}\big(\mathbf{m}_{xn}^{\textnormal{{NA}}}\mathbf{m}_{xn}^{\textnormal{{NA}}\top}\big).

\displaystyle A_{2}(t):=\lVert\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}\Big(2+\lVert\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{m}\big(\eta^{\texttt{NA}},t\big)\Big)

and

\displaystyle A_{3}(t):=\lVert\mathbf{P}^{-1}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}\Big(2+\lVert\mathbf{\Sigma}_{y}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}\Big[\lVert\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}+\mathbf{\Delta}_{n}\big(\mu^{\texttt{NA}},t\big)\Big]\lambda^{-1}_{\textnormal{min}}\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)\Big).

Remarkably, our bound is
entirely dimension-free
, including the terms in
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1},m^{-1})
. We obtain the same convergence rates as in the complete data setting
[
29
]
. The price of missingness remains reasonably small, affecting the convergence rate of our estimator only up to constants depending on
\lVert\mathbf{P}^{-1}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}
and
\lVert\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}
. However, these constants can become arbitrary large as the probability of observing a variable tends to
0
. Our bounds can be extended to entropic regularized optimal transport between Gaussians using closed form formulas available in the literature
[
12
]
. We use this extension in our experiments.

5.2
Application to Domain Adaptation with Missing Data

Our Bures-Wasserstein estimator enables effective domain adaptation in the presence of missing data.
We consider a setup inspired by
Courty et al. 2016
and
Flamary et al. 2019
. We have access to a model
\widehat{f}_{n_{\ell}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{Y}
pretrained by empirical risk minimization on a
source
domain, which maps points
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to predicted outcomes
y\in\mathcal{Y}
. This model is given
without its training set
, a setup often referred to as hypothesis transfer
[
31
,
1
]
which has become increasingly prevalent in the era of large models. We have access to two datasets: a curated unlabeled dataset
\mathbf{X}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{t}\times d}
from a
target
distribution
\mathcal{P}_{t}
(different from the source distribution
\mathcal{P}_{s}
) on which we wish to apply the model, and a small unlabeled dataset
\mathbf{X}^{\texttt{NA}}_{s}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{s}\times d}
with
n_{s}\ll n_{t}
that is similar to the pre-training dataset from the source domain but whose data is partially missing. This may arise, for instance, when the model trainer prefers not to release their curated high-quality data, or when some individuals have partially or fully opted out of a data release agreement. In the latter case, individuals who have fully opted out are excluded from the dataset, while those who have agreed to a partial release have their undisclosed features reported as missing. As in
Flamary et al. 2019
, we make the following assumption to connect the two distributions.

Assumption 3
.

The source and target distributions satisfy
\mathcal{P}_{t}=m_{\sharp}\mathcal{P}_{s}

where
m(x,y)=(\mathbf{T}_{\ell}^{\star}(x),y)
.

We assume similar missingness mechanisms as above, and consider an empirical risk minimization framework for a classification task. Let
\mathcal{H}_{K}
be a reproducing Hilbert space associated with a symmetric kernel
K:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
K_{x}(\cdot)=K(\cdot,x)\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
and
f(x)=\langle f(x),K_{x}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}
for all
f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Let

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{s}(f):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{s}}\Big[\mathscr{L}\big(Y,f(X)\big)\Big]\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\mathscr{R}_{t}(f):=\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{P}_{t}}\Big[\mathscr{L}\big(Y,f(X)\big)\Big]

be the expected risk of a predictor
f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}
in the source domain and target domain. We borrow the following assumptions from
Flamary et al. 2019
. The function
\mathscr{L}:\mathcal{Y}\times\mathcal{Y}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a loss function which is
M_{\mathscr{L}}
-Lipschitz with respect to its second variable. Assume that the Bayes predictors lie in
\mathcal{H}_{K}
, i.e.,

f_{\star}^{s}:=\argmin_{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{R}_{s}(f)\in\mathcal{H}_{K}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad f_{\star}^{t}:=\argmin_{f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}}\mathscr{R}_{t}(f)\in\mathcal{H}_{K}

and additionally satisfy
\lVert f_{\star}^{s}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\leq 1
and
\lVert f_{\star}^{t}\rVert_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\leq 1
. We assume that the original model has been trained on a possibly large dataset with
n_{\ell}
labeled examples, that is

\displaystyle\widehat{f}_{n_{\ell}}\in\argmin\limits_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{K}}\frac{1}{n_{\ell}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n_{\ell}}\mathscr{L}\big(f(\mathbf{x}_{i}),y_{i}\big).

Finally, assume that the eigenvalues
(\zeta_{k})_{k\geq 0}
of the integral operator
T_{K}
of
\mathcal{H}_{K}
decay at rate
k^{-2\beta}
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Assume the Bayes predictors
f^{t}_{\star},f^{s}_{\star}
satisfy
\mathscr{R}_{t}(f^{t}_{\star})=\mathscr{R}_{s}(f^{s}_{\star})
i.e. the Bayses risk is equal in both domains. For any
t>0
and
n_{s},n_{t}
large enough, up to terms in
\mathcal{O}(n_{s}^{-1})
and
\mathcal{O}(n_{t}^{-1})
, we have with probability at least
1-4e^{-t}
:

\displaystyle\mathscr{R}_{t}(\widehat{f}_{n_{\ell}}\circ\widehat{\mathbf{T}})-\mathscr{R}_{t}(f^{t}_{\star})\lesssim n_{\ell}^{-\frac{2\beta}{1+2\beta}}+\frac{t}{n_{\ell}}+\frac{B_{1}(t)}{\sqrt{n_{s}}}+\frac{B_{2}(t)}{\sqrt{n_{t}}}+B_{3}(t)\mathbf{\Delta}_{n_{s}}(\mu^{\texttt{NA}},t)+B_{4}(t)\mathbf{\Delta}_{n_{t}}(\eta,t)

where

\displaystyle B_{1}(t):=M_{f}M_{\mathscr{L}}\lVert\mathbf{P}^{-1}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}\sqrt{K_{2}^{x}\Big(1+2\sqrt{K_{2}^{x}\lVert\mathbf{P}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}\lVert\mathbf{\Sigma}_{x}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}t}+2t\Big)}

\displaystyle B_{2}(t):=M_{f}M_{\mathscr{L}}\lVert\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\rVert^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big(\mathbf{r}\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\big)+2\sqrt{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}t}+2t\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle B_{3}(t):=\frac{M_{f}M_{\mathscr{L}}K^{x}_{4}\kappa(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t})\lVert\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1/2}_{n_{s}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n_{t}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{n_{s}}^{-1/2}\big)}}

and

\displaystyle B_{4}(t):=M_{f}M_{\mathscr{L}}\frac{\kappa(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{s})}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\textnormal{min}}\big(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{s}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{t}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{s}^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)}}.

This theorem adapts
Flamary et al. 2019
to the presence of missing data. As for the estimation of covariance matrices, we obtain similar rates - up to missingness dependent constants - as in the complete data case.

6
Consistent Estimation of Entropic Optimal Transport with Missing Data

6.1
Consistent Estimation Through Matrix Completion

We now move beyond the Gaussian and linear Monge cases, and address the general entropic-regularized optimal transport problem. We propose a two-step algorithm: first impute missing values in
\mathbf{X}^{\texttt{NA}}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{\texttt{NA}}
, then solve optimal transport on the completed data. We consider a noisy setting:

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}^{\texttt{NA}}=\big(\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{N}_{x}\big)\odot\Omega_{x}\textnormal{ and }\mathbf{Y}^{\texttt{NA}}=\big(\mathbf{Y}+\mathbf{N}_{y}\big)\odot\Omega_{y},

(Noisy Data)

in which
\mathbf{N}_{x},\mathbf{N}_{y}
are two noise matrices. We make the following assumption on the noise.

Assumption 4
.

The entries of
\mathbf{N}_{x}=(\xi^{x}_{ij})_{i,j}
and
\mathbf{N_{y}}=(\xi^{y}_{ij})_{ij}
are i.i.d. and satisfy
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{x}_{ij})=\mathbb{E}(\xi^{y}_{ij})=0
, and
\mathbb{E}\big((\xi^{x}_{ij})^{2}\big)\leq b
,
\mathbb{E}\big((\xi^{y}_{ij})^{2}\big)\leq b
for all
i,j
.

Our approach leverages iterative singular value thresholding (ISVT)
[
37
,
30
]
, a celebrated spectral algorithm for noisy matrix completion, combined with recent cost-sensitivity bounds on entropic regularized optimal transport
Keriven 2022
, to derive a consistent estimator of the entropic-regularized Wasserstein distance.
ISVT solves the matrix completion problem by recovering a low-rank approximation of the full matrix from partial observations with a single hyperparameter
\lambda\geq 0
—the full algorithm is given in Appendix
D
. In our setting, we apply ISVT independently to impute the missing entries in
\mathbf{X}^{\texttt{NA}}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{\texttt{NA}}
, and define
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
as the resulting cost matrix. We make two additional assumptions on the data.

Assumption 5
.

The measures
\mu
and
\eta
have bounded support, that is
\lVert X\rVert_{2}\leq\mathcal{R}_{x}
and
\lVert Y\rVert_{2}\leq\mathcal{R}_{y}
almost surely, and their covariance matrices have dimensions
k_{\mu}\leq d
and
k_{\eta}\leq d
respectively.

Under this Assumption, the cost matrix
\mathbf{C}
has bounded entries. We let
c_{\textnormal{max}}
and
c_{\textnormal{min}}
be its maximal and minimal entries.

Theorem 6.1
.

Under Assumptions
2
and
5
, we have with high probability that

\displaystyle\Big\lvert\mathbf{W}_{\widehat{\mathbf{C}},\varepsilon}(\mu^{\texttt{NA}}_{n},\eta^{\texttt{NA}}_{m})-\mathbf{W}_{\mathbf{C},\varepsilon}(\mu_{n},\eta_{m})\Big\rvert\lesssim K_{\varepsilon}\Bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}k_{x}\rho_{\mu}(n)}{\lVert\mathbf{P}^{-1}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}n}}+\sqrt{\frac{\lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}k_{y}\rho_{\eta}(m)}{\lVert\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}m}}.\Bigg)

where
\rho_{n}(\mu):=(\mathcal{R}_{x}\vee\sigma)^{2}\lVert\mathbf{P}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}+\mathcal{R}_{x}^{2}\log(n\wedge d)+b^{2}\log(n+d)
,
\rho_{m}(\eta):=(\mathcal{R}_{y}\vee\sigma)^{2}\lVert\mathbf{Q}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}+\mathcal{R}_{y}^{2}\log(m\wedge d)+b^{2}\log(m+d)

and
K_{\varepsilon}:=\exp(\varepsilon^{-1}(2c_{\textnormal{max}}-c_{\textnormal{min}}))
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}\big[\mathbf{\Pi}_{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{C})\,|\,\mathbf{\Pi}_{\varepsilon}(\widehat{\mathbf{C}})\big]\lesssim\varepsilon^{-1}\frac{M_{x}}{n^{1/4}}\Big(K^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+K_{\varepsilon}\frac{M_{x}}{n^{1/4}}\Big)+\varepsilon^{-1}\frac{M_{y}}{m^{1/4}}\Big(K^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}+K_{\varepsilon}\frac{M_{y}}{m^{1/4}}\Big)

where
K_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}:=\exp\Big(\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}(3c_{\textnormal{max}}-7c_{\textnormal{min}})\Big)
,
M_{x}:=\Big(\frac{\lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}k_{x}\rho_{\mu}(n)}{\lVert\mathbf{P}^{-1}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}}\Big)^{1/4}
and
M_{y}:=\Big(\frac{\lVert\mathbf{M}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}k_{y}\rho_{\eta}(m)}{\lVert\mathbf{Q}^{-1}\rVert_{\textnormal{op}}}\Big)^{1/4}
.

The first part of our theorem shows that the consistency of the estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{C}}
translates to the consistency of the optimal transport cost. As expected, our bound is additive in both sample sizes
n
and
m
. Remarkably, the second part of our theorem shows that our estimator of the transport plan is also asymptotically consistent. Remark, however, that its convergence speed in
\mathcal{O}(n^{-1/4})
is considerably slower than estimation of the Wasserstein distance—a fact already noticed by
Keriven 2022
.

6.2
Cross-Validation Free Hyperparameter Selection Using the Bures-Wasserstein Criterion

Figure 2
:
Value of the entropic regularized optimal transport problem as a function of
\Lambda\geq 0
when
90\%
of observations are missing.

Like other matrix completion algorithms, ISVT depends on hyperparameters (specifically,
\lambda
) that require careful tuning for optimal performance.
Hyperparameter selection is generally carried out as follows: 1) a subset of the observed entries, which we call
validation entries
, is selected and removed, leaving remaining
training entries
; 2) the matrix completion algorithm is run on the
training entries
only; 3) a validation criterion is evaluated, either on the validation entries alone or on the full matrix). Cross-validation can be applied on top of this procedure, partitioning the observed entries into multiple folds and rotating the validation set across folds. In any case, this approach introduces a sharp trade-off between the sizes of validation and training sets, since removing validation entries reduces the amount of data available for training and may distort the structure of the matrix we ultimately aim to complete.

We propose a novel hyperparameter selection strategy for matrix completion that leverages our estimator of the Bures-Wasserstein criterion. Instead of setting aside a validation set, we compute—for each candidate hyperparameter—the estimated Bures-Wasserstein criterion between the imputed data and the original data with missing values. Missingness probabilities are estimated from the data. Since the Bures-Wasserstein score (and, by extension, our debiased estimator) provides a lower bound on the true optimal transport distance
[
19
]
, it serves as a meaningful proxy for tracking variations in the true distance. Formally speaking, for a matrix completion algorithm
\mathscr{A}_{\Lambda}:(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\texttt{NA}\})^{n\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with hyperparameters
\Lambda\in\mathcal{G}_{\Lambda}\subset\mathbb{R}^{h}
, our procedure operates by selecting

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\star}\in\argmin\limits_{\Lambda\in\mathcal{G}_{\Lambda}}\widehat{\mathbb{BW}}\Big[\mathscr{A}_{\Lambda}\Big(\mathbf{X}^{\texttt{NA}}\Big),\mathbf{X}^{\texttt{NA}}\Big].

Intuitively, this strategy favors methods which impute data in a distributionally consistent manner. Figure
2
compares the true value of the entropic optimal transport problem with our Bures-Wasserstein proxy and the standard Frobenius criterion (computed on observed entries) on toy data, displaying the strong consistency of our criterion (see Appendix
D
for experimental details).

8
Conclusion

In this work, we introduced two complementary strategies for computing optimal transport distances in the presence of heterogeneous missingness: a debiasing approach for the Bures-Wasserstein distance and a matrix completion method based on ISVT for entropic-regularized optimal transport. We displayed the effectiveness of our methods on a wide set of numerical experiments.

Limitations and Future Work.

We see a series of limitations and opportunities for future work.

1.

Our analysis relies on the MCAR assumption, which may not always reflect real-world data, even though we have partially studied the effect of MNAR missingness. It would be interesting to design more complex estimators and to obtain theoretical guarantees in the MNAR case.

2.

In the same spirit as recent papers by
Perez-Lebel et al. 2022
and
Morvan and Varoquaux 2024
, we advocate for a large scale study of the effect of different imputation strategies on distributional tasks. Applications include generative modeling, statistical testing and quantile regression.

3.

Beyond the entropic regularized optimal transport problem, many variants of the optimal transport problem—such as robust optimal transport, Gromov-Wasserstein and sliced optimal transport—are of high interest and could be adapted to accommodate missing values.

We purposely do not implement applications requiring extensive computational power, such as generative modeling and shape registration.

Appendix A
Supplementary Results and Mathematical Background

A.1
Basic Properties of Random Vectors

Definition A.1

(sub-Gaussian Random Variable)
.

A centered real-valued random variable
X
is said to be sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
\sigma^{2}
if its moment generating function satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp(s\big(X-\mathbb{E}(X)\big))\Big]\leq\exp\Big(\frac{\sigma^{2}s^{2}}{2}\Big)

for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
.

Definition A.2
.

A random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is said to be sub-Gaussian if for all
u\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, the random variable
u^{\top}\big(X-\mathbb{E}(X)\big)
is sub-Gaussian.

The sub-Gaussian property is equivalent to the tail decay property

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(X-\mathbb{E}\big(X\big)>t\Big)\leq\exp\Big(\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big)\,\,\textnormal{ and }\,\,\mathbb{P}\Big(X-\mathbb{E}\big(X\big)<-t\Big)\leq\exp\Big(\frac{-t^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\Big).

We recall the following important result.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent sub-Gaussian random variables with variance proxies
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}
. Then for all
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the random variable
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\big(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}(X_{i})\big)
is sub-Gaussian with variance proxy
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}^{2}a_{i}^{2}
.

Remark 1
.

Note that this implies that
n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}(X_{i})\big)
is
n^{-2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}^{2}
sub-Gaussian.

A proof is given by
[
46
]
, Corollary 1.7. We now define sub-exponential random variables.

Definition A.4
.

A random variable
X
is said to be sub-exponential with parameter
\lambda
if its moment generating function satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\exp(s\big(X-\mathbb{E}(X)\big))\Big]\leq\exp\Big(\frac{s^{2}\lambda^{2}}{2}\Big)

for all
\lvert s\rvert\leq\lambda^{-1}
.

We end this section by recalling Bernstein’s inequality for sub-exponential random variables.

Theorem A.5

(
Vershynin 2010
, Corollary 5.17)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be independent sub-exponential random variables and let
K:=\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\lVert X_{i}\rVert_{\psi_{1}}.
Then for all
t>0
, with probability at least
1-e^{-t}

\displaystyle\Big\lvert n^{-1}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\Big(X_{i}-\mathbb{E}(X_{i})\Big)\Big\rvert\leq CK\Big(t^{-\frac{1}{2}}n^{-\frac{1}{2}}\vee t^{-1}n^{-1}\Big)

for a universal constant
C>0
.

D.3
Details on Cross-Validation in Matrix Completion

The typical procedure is to construct a new mask
\Omega_{\textnormal{train}}
that starts from the initial mask
\Omega
, and removes a fixed share
\delta_{\textnormal{val}}\in[0,1]
of the observed values uniformly at random. Hence the number of missing values is greater in
\Omega_{\textnormal{train}}
than in
\Omega
. The indices of the removed entries are stored in a new mask
\Omega_{\textnormal{val}}
, which can be seen as a held-out validation set. This new mask has hence a share
\delta_{\textnormal{val}}
of entries equal to
1
. Finally, the imputation model is trained only on values kept in
\Omega_{\textnormal{train}}
and evaluated by computing the relative error in Frobenius norm

\displaystyle\textnormal{RF}(\lambda):=\frac{\Big\lVert\Omega_{\textnormal{val}}\odot\texttt{isvt}_{\lambda}\Big[\mathbf{X}^{\texttt{NA}}\odot\Omega_{\textnormal{train}}\Big]-\Omega_{\textnormal{val}}\odot\mathbf{X}^{\texttt{NA}}\Big\rVert_{2}}{\lVert\Omega_{\textnormal{val}}\odot\mathbf{X}^{\texttt{NA}}\rVert_{2}},

on the entries in
\Omega_{\textnormal{val}}
. This metric is commonly used in matrix completion
