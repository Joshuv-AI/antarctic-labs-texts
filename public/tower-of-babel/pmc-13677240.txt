Dietary phosphatidylinositol promotes ovarian maturation in Pacific white shrimp (Penaeus vannamei) via altered lipid mobilization and hormonal regulation
Authors: Long Zhang, Tong Chang, Xiaolong Liang, Fenglu Han, Chang Xu, Erchao Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhosphatidylinositol (PI) is an important phospholipid involved in membrane structure, lipid transport, and cellular signaling, but its nutritional role in regulating ovarian maturation in crustacean broodstock remains poorly understood. To elucidate the effects and mechanisms by which dietary PI modulates ovarian development in female broodstock of Pacific white shrimp (Penaeus vannamei), five isonitrogenous, isolipidic, and semi-purified diets containing 0.0, 0.2%, 0.4%, 0.6%, or 0.8% PI (dry-matter basis) were formulated and fed to shrimp for 28 d. A total of 160 seven-month-old females (initial body weight 39.70 ± 1.80 g) at ovarian stage I were allocated to five treatments with four replicates each and eight shrimp per replicate. Compared with the control group (0.0 PI), the 0.6% PI diet significantly increased the gonadosomatic index (P < 0.001) and reduced the hepatosomatic index (P = 0.030) and shortened each of the ovarian stages I–III by approximately two days (stage I, P = 0.056; stage II, P = 0.159; stage III, P = 0.007). Mean oocyte area in the ovary (P < 0.001) and lipid-droplet area in the hepatopancreas (P < 0.001) were both greatest in the 0.6% PI group compared with the other dietary groups. Ovarian total cholesterol was highest in the 0.6% group (P = 0.029). Serum high-density lipoprotein and low-density lipoprotein declined in most PI-supplemented groups, reaching their lowest levels at 0.6% (P < 0.05). Endocrine profiling showed increased estradiol at 0.6% and 0.8% (P < 0.001), elevated progesterone across all supplemented groups (P = 0.007), and reduced molt-inhibiting hormone at 0.2% and 0.6% (P = 0.043). Transcriptomics identified 421 differentially expressed genes enriched for lipid synthesis and transport and for transforming growth factor (TGF)-β/Notch signaling, while metabolomics revealed upregulation of D-sphingosine, palmitic acid, and stearic acid, with decreased levels of purines (guanine and hypoxanthine) and B-vitamin intermediates (riboflavinand and pantothenate). Multi-omics integration indicated potentiation of sphingolipid metabolism and fatty-acid biosynthesis, alongside heightened consumption within purine metabolism, as a potential regulatory pathway promoting ovarian maturation. In summary, dietary PI may facilitate ovarian maturation by coordinating lipid mobilization and steroidogenesis, potentially interacting with developmental signaling pathways. A recommended supplementation range of 0.442%–0.586% (dry-matter basis) is suggested based on dose-response modeling, with 0.6% yielding the significant response. These findings provide new insights into broodstock nutrition strategy in shrimp aquaculture.Keywords: Penaeus vannamei, Phosphatidylinositol, Ovarian development, Broodstock nutrition, Hormonal regulation

Introduction

In shrimp aquaculture, optimizing broodstock reproductive performance remains a fundamental challenge limiting seed quality and production efficiency. As the industry continues to expand globally—particularly across the Asia-Pacific and Latin American regions—the demand for improved broodstock performance continues to increase (FAO, 2024). However, the intensive farming of shrimp presents complex challenges, including genetic degradation, reduced fecundity, and declining reproductive performance (Emerenciano et al., 2022; Ruben et al., 2025). These issues underscore the critical need for targeted research on reproductive regulation, especially the mechanisms underlying ovarian maturation, which remains a bottleneck in broodstock management despite the remarkable adaptability and growth advantages of this species.

Ovarian maturation involves sophisticated biological mechanisms, including vitellogenin synthesis, oocyte maturation, and cytoplasmic accumulation, processes that are regulated by nutritional, environmental, and endocrinological factors (Nagaraju, 2011; Subramoniam, 2011). Among various regulatory factors, nutrition has emerged as a pivotal modulator of reproductive physiology. Precise nutritional modulation of reproductive performance in Pacific white shrimp (Penaeus vannamei) broodstock represents an economically viable and efficacious approach. Dietary manipulation, especially through modification of lipids and sterol compositions, has been shown to increase the gonadosomatic index (GSI), promote ovarian histological development, and increase fertilization and hatching rates (Hoa et al., 2009; Kian et al., 2004; Liang et al., 2022; Wouters et al., 2001a). Nevertheless, despite its potential, the specific nutritional strategies and molecular mechanisms that govern ovarian development in P. vannamei broodstock remain inadequately characterized.

Phospholipids, which serve as the primary structural components of biological membranes, have multiple reproductive functions and influence embryonic and larval development in crustaceans. These complex lipids not only participate in vitellogenin formation and transportation but also provide essential energy substrates and structural foundations for embryonic development (Liang et al., 2022; Ravid et al., 1999). Furthermore, phospholipids regulate metabolic processes associated with ovarian development by modulating membrane fluidity, influencing membrane protein functionality, and facilitating the generation of signaling molecules (Liang et al., 2022; Maneii et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2013). During ovarian maturation, a significant increase in phospholipid accumulation has been documented (Chansela et al., 2012; Liang et al., 2022; Ravid et al., 1999; Wouters et al., 2001a). Dietary supplementation with 40 g/kg soybean lecithin improved the growth and reproductive performance of female P. vannamei broodstock, with phosphatidylinositol (PI) accounting for approximately 9.74% of the phospholipids (Maneii et al., 2019). This finding highlights the importance of phospholipids, including PI, in modulating broodstock growth and ovarian development.

Phosphatidylinositol has several functions in cellular signal transduction and membrane trafficking. Phosphatidylinositol and its phosphorylated derivatives (PIP, PIP2, PIP3, etc.) form a signaling network that regulates diverse biological processes (Balla, 2013). In mammalian systems, the PI signaling pathway, via PI3K/Akt/mTOR cascade reactions, modulates follicular development and oocyte maturation (Li et al., 2021; Maidarti et al., 2020). Additionally, PI participates in calcium ion signal transduction and cytoskeletal reorganization, processes that are instrumental during oocyte meiosis and fertilization (Hoshino et al., 2004; Kalous et al., 2023).

Despite extensive investigations into the role of PI in mammalian ovarian development, the understanding of its function and mechanistic actions in crustaceans, particularly in P. vannamei, remains substantially limited. Preliminary studies indicate that PI concentrations in crustacean ovaries increase significantly during gonadal development, suggesting a potential intimate association with ovarian maturation (Liang et al., 2022; Wouters et al., 2001b). However, the effects of dietary PI supplementation on ovarian development in P. vannamei and the underlying molecular mechanisms have not been comprehensively elucidated. In P. vannamei, PI potentially facilitates ovarian development through cellular signal transduction, regulation of lipid metabolism, and modulation of hormonal secretion. Given the potential influence of PI on ovarian development, an exploration of its effects on female P. vannamei broodstock is imperative. This study aimed to investigate the impact of dietary PI supplementation on lipid metabolism, reproductive hormones, and potential pathways of ovarian development in female P. vannamei broodstock, thereby providing insights for optimizing broodstock nutrition to promote ovarian development.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All experimental procedures involving animals in this research adhered strictly to the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals from East China Normal University (No. 20201001; approved on December 15, 2020).

Experimental diets

In this study, experimental diets were formulated with defatted fish meal, casein, and gelatin as the primary protein sources. Fish oil, soybean oil, PI, and cholesterol constituted the main lipid sources, whereas corn starch served as the predominant carbohydrate source. Based on previous relevant investigations, five isonitrogenous and isolipidic semi-purified diets were formulated, each containing 53.21% crude protein (Li et al., 2023) and 13.53% crude lipids (Liang et al., 2024). The dietary PI concentrations on a dry-matter basis were 0.0, 0.2%, 0.4%, 0.6%, and 0.8% (active PI). Phosphatidylinositol ingredient used in this study had a purity of 50%; accordingly, the corresponding inclusion levels were 0, 4, 8, 12, and 16 g/kg diet. For clarity, diets were designated as 0.0, 0.2%, 0.4%, 0.6%, and 0.8% according to the intended active PI levels (0.0−0.8%). Following the diet preparation protocol, the formulated diets were air-dried at room temperature and subsequently stored at −20 °C (Liang et al., 2022). Moisture, crude protein, crude lipid, and ash were determined according to the AOAC (2023). The moisture content was determined gravimetrically by desiccation to a constant mass in an oven (WFO-520, Tokyo Rikakikai Co., Ltd., Bunkyo-Ku, Tokyo, Japan) at 105 °C (method 934.01), whereas the crude protein content was analyzed via the Dumas combustion methodology (Dumas nitrogen/protein analyzer, rapid N exceed, Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Langenselbold, Hesse, Germany; method 968.06). The crude lipid fraction was assessed via Soxhlet extraction (method 920.39), and the ash content was determined via incineration in a muffle furnace (Muffle furnace, SX2-4-10 N, Shanghai Yiheng Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China; method 942.05). Organic matter was calculated on a dry-matter basis as 100% minus the ash content. The formulation and proximate composition of the experimental diets are presented in Table 1.

Feeding trial

Postlarval d-10 (P10) juveniles of the P. vannamei, were obtained from a local company in Dongfang, Hainan, China, and reared on a commercial diet to 7 months of age, reaching broodstock size. Experimental shrimps were maintained in black polyethylene tanks (bottom diameter × height, 4.0 m × 1.0 m) and acclimated to the control diet for 7 d. Following acclimation, 170 females at ovarian developmental stage I (39.70 ± 1.80 g) were randomly selected. Ten shrimp were randomly subsampled to determine the hepatosomatic index (HSI [5.85 ± 0.23]%) and GSI (0.50 ± 0.13)%. Thereafter, the remaining 160 females were randomly and evenly stocked into black polyethylene tanks (bottom diameter × height, 1.0 m × 0.9 m); both control and treatment groups comprised four replicates, with eight shrimp per replicate. A 28-d feeding trial was conducted. Every 7 d, the total biomass per tank was recorded; the daily ration was then set at 5.5% of this biomass and administered in seven equal feedings at 07:30, 10:00, 13:00, 15:00, 18:00, 21:00, and 24:00. Uneaten feed and feces were siphoned daily, followed by an 80% water exchange, continuing until the end of the trial. Throughout the experiment, water quality was maintained at 25 to 28 °C, pH 7.8 to 8.4, salinity 30 to 31, dissolved oxygen 5 to 6 mg/L, ammonia nitrogen 0.10 to 0.30 mg/L, and nitrite 0.03 to 0.10 mg/L, under a 12 h light:12 h dark photoperiod. All tanks across treatments followed the same feeding and water-exchange regimen, and water-quality variables were monitored and maintained within narrow ranges.

Sampling and analytical measurements

Upon completion of the acclimation period, hepatopancreas and ovarian tissues were randomly sampled from 10 shrimp to determine the initial hepatosomatic and gonadosomatic indices. After each daily water exchange, ovarian developmental stage was assigned to each shrimp using macroscopic criteria, including ovarian size/extent and color visible through the carapace: stage I (transparent and filamentous), stage II (enlarged, translucent, or white), stage III (further enlarged, pale yellow, and separated between the abdominal segments and the carapace) and stage IV (enlarged, yellow, and connected between the abdominal segments and the carapace) (Liang et al., 2022). All staging assessments were performed throughout the trial by a single trained evaluator to minimize observer bias. Upon completion of the feeding trial, shrimp from all experimental groups were subjected to a 24-h fasting period, followed by brief anesthesia in an ice bath for 10 s prior to sampling. Body weight was initially measured for each group, after which the samples were collected on ice. Hemolymph was extracted from the pericardial cavity via 1-mL disposable sterile syringes and allowed to coagulate at ambient temperature before centrifugation. Eyestalks were excised via sterile surgical scissors, rapidly flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and preserved at −80 °C for subsequent analyses. After weighing the hepatopancreas and ovaries of each shrimp, tissue samples from the same location were excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and prepared for histological observation. The remaining hepatopancreatic and ovarian tissues were rapidly immersed in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 °C for further analysis. The following formulae were employed for the index calculations:

Weight gain (WG, %) = 100 × (Final body weight – Initial body weight)/Initial body weight;

Specific growth rate (SGR, %/d) = 100 × [ln (Final body weight) – ln (Initial body weight)]/Days;

Feed intake (FI, %) = 100 × (Total feed offered/Total days)/[(Initial body weight + Final body weight)/2];

GSI (%) = 100 × (Wet ovary weight/Wet body weight);

HSI (%) = 100 × (Wet hepatopancreas weight/Wet body weight).

To ensure comprehensive biochemical analysis and data reliability, eyestalks, hemolymph, hepatopancreas, and ovarian tissues were subjected to preliminary processing. After the feeding trial, hemolymph was collected individually from two shrimp per tank using sterile syringes and allowed to clot at room temperature. Serum was obtained by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C (refrigerated benchtop centrifuge, 3–18 KS, Sigma Laborzentrifugen GmbH, Osterode am Harz, Lower Saxony, Germany), and the supernatant was used for subsequent biochemical and hormonal assays. For tissue assays, two shrimp per tank were randomly selected. Eyestalks, hepatopancreas, and ovaries were dissected and processed individually (no pooling). Each tissue sample was weighed and homogenized in prechilled 0.85% saline at a 1:9 (w/v) ratio using a tissue grinder (Tissuelyser-24, Shanghai Jingxin Industrial Development Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) at 60 Hz for 30 s, followed by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected for biochemical and hormonal measurements.

Histological examination of the hepatopancreas and ovaries

For each shrimp, evenly thick slices of the hepatopancreas were cut, frozen, and then stained with oil red O. Three hepatopancreas slices were randomly selected from each treatment group, with each slice coming from a different shrimp. From each slice, randomly chosen areas of the same size were analyzed for the relative area of lipid droplets using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 (Media Cybernetics, Inc., Rockville, MD, USA) for quantitative analysis.

For each shrimp, the same region of the ovary was excised, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by dehydration, washing, and balancing with ethanol, toluene, and xylene. The samples were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned into 5 μm thick slices using a rotary microtome. After staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), the slices were examined under a fluorescence microscope. Three ovarian slices were randomly selected from each treatment group, with each slice originating from a different shrimp. From each slice, randomly chosen areas of the same size were analyzed using Image-Pro Plus 6.0 software to quantify the oocyte area.

Accumulation and transport of critical lipids between the hepatopancreas and ovaries

Biochemical indices (total protein [TP; A045-4-2], triglyceride [TG; A110-1-1], and total cholesterol [T-CHO; A111-1-1]) in hepatopancreas and ovary were measured from individual shrimp, with two shrimp sampled per tank (no pooling). Serum lipoproteins (very low-density lipoprotein [VLDL; H249-1-2], low-density lipoprotein [LDL-C; A113-1-1], and high-density lipoprotein [HDL-C; A112-1-1]) were determined from individual serum samples collected from two shrimp per tank (no pooling). The assay kits used for these analyses were procured from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). All the analytical procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the manufacturer's protocols. The VLDL standards were prepared at 0.0, 0.4, 0.8, 1.6, 3.2, and 6.4 μmol/mL. The intra-assay coefficient of variation was < 10% and the inter-assay coefficient of variation was < 12%. The assay's dynamic range was 0.05 to 10.00 μmol/mL.

Hormonal profile analysis

Serum hormones (17β-estradiol [E2; FT-X9147X], progesterone [PROG; FT-X9S1235X], and methyl farnesoate [MF; FT-X9108X]) were measured from individual shrimp serum samples, with two shrimp samples per tank (no pooling). Eyestalk-derived hormones (gonad-inhibiting hormone [GIH; FT-Y10582X] and molt-inhibiting hormone [MIH; FT-P91071T]) were quantified from supernatants prepared from individual eyestalk tissues, with two shrimp sampled per tank (no pooling). The quantification of E2, PROG, MF, GIH, and MIH was performed via shrimp enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits, procured from Shanghai FanTai Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and all analytical procedures were executed in strict adherence to the manufacturer's instructions. For all kits, standard curves exhibited linear regressions with correlation coefficients R ≥ 0.9900, showed no cross-reactivity with other structurally related soluble analogues, and had intra- and inter-assay coefficients of variation < 15%.

Serum metabolomic analysis

To assess the metabolic effects of dietary PI, serum samples from the control and 0.6% PI groups (n = 3 per group) were analyzed via untargeted metabolomics. After protein precipitation, the metabolites were extracted with methanol:acetonitrile (1:1, v/v) and analyzed via a Thermo Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) coupled with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 Å, 1.8 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm; Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA). Data were acquired in both positive and negative ion modes via data-dependent acquisition (DDA). The raw data were processed with MS-DIAL (v4.9.221218) for peak extraction, alignment, and annotation. Metabolites were identified based on the PerSonalbio Next-Generation Metabolomics Database, which integrates in-house libraries, mzCloud, LIPIDMAPS, HMDB, MoNA, and NIST_2020_MSMS, using an MS1 tolerance of 0.01 Da, MS2 tolerance of 0.05 Da, and a minimum identification score of 70.

Metabolite identification analysis was performed via RStudio (version 2024.09.0+375) for compounds with a total score ≥ 60. Univariate statistical analysis was conducted on metabolites with a quality control relative standard deviation (QC RSD) ≤ 0.3. Each metabolite was initially subjected to the Shapiro-Wilk test for normality and Levene's test for homogeneity of variance. Based on these results, the independent samples t-test, Welch's t-test, or Mann-Whitney U-test was selected as appropriate, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple testing. After skewness was calculated, the data underwent logarithmic transformation prior to orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). Differentially abundant metabolites were selected based on established criteria (P < 0.05, variable importance in projection [VIP] > 1) and the corresponding false discovery rate (adjusted P-value) was calculated for each metabolite. The identified differential metabolites were subsequently mapped to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways for functional annotation and biological interpretation.

Ovarian transcriptomic analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the ovarian tissues of three shrimp from both the control and 0.6% PI groups via TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific‌ Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA). The RNA concentration and integrity were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Wilmington, DE, USA) and a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA). The sequencing libraries were prepared via the TruSeq RNA Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA), which includes mRNA enrichment, fragmentation, complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis, adapter ligation, and PCR amplification. Libraries with an average insert size of 400 to 500 bp were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Read counts were calculated for each gene, and transcripts per million (TPM) values were derived based on read counts and gene length. Data normalization and model fitting were performed via the DESeq2 package, with Benjamini-Hochberg correction applied to account for multiple testing. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using the criteria of adjusted P < 0.05 and |log2 (fold change)| ≥ 1. The STRINGdb package was used to query the STRING database for DEGs and construct a protein-protein interaction (PPI) network with an interaction score threshold of ≥ 400. The top 10% of the hub genes were subsequently identified and characterized by calculating network metrics, including degree, betweenness centrality, and closeness centrality. Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted via the Enricher function of the clusterProfiler package. The analysis utilized GO terms and KEGG pathway annotations to calculate gene lists and counts for each term or pathway, followed by P-value calculation via hypergeometric distribution. Notably, human disease-related pathways were excluded prior to KEGG enrichment analysis.

Integrated multiomics analysis

Integration of transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets was conducted through KEGG-based mapping of shared enzyme and pathway identifiers to identify correlated gene-metabolite pairs. Spearman correlation analysis was performed via preprocessed differential matrices, with significant associations defined as |correlation| > 0.8 and P < 0.01 (adjusted by Benjamini-Hochberg). The KEGG co-enrichment analysis and nine-quadrant plots were used to visualize functional convergence.

Weighted co expression network analysis (WCNA) was performed via normalized transcriptomic (TPM) and metabolomic data to identify modules and hub elements associated with ovarian development. Modules significantly correlated with the GSI (P < 0.05) were retained for further analysis. Cross-omics correlation networks were constructed based on bi-weight mid correlations, and hub nodes were identified via combined centrality metrics (degree and betweenness) weighted via principal component analysis (PCA). Nodes highly correlated with the GSI (|r| > 0.8, adjusted P < 0.05) were defined as key regulators potentially involved in ovarian maturation.

Gene expression analysis

Six samples were randomly selected from eight ovarian specimens per experimental group. Total RNA was subsequently extracted from the ovarian tissues via TRIzol reagent (15,596,018; Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Carlsbad, CA, USA). The RNA concentration and integrity were subsequently quantified via a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. One microgram of total RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA via a reverse transcription kit (Biosharp, Hefei Lanjieke Technology Co., Ltd., Hefei, Anhui, China). Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed using ChanQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd., Nanjing, Jiangsu, China). The reaction was conducted in a 20 μL volume under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 62 °C for 30 s. The melting curve analysis was generated at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 60 s, and 95 °C for 15 s. β-Actin and 18 S rRNA served as endogenous reference genes. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated via the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001). The primer sequences utilized for qRT-PCR amplification are presented in Table 2. Statistical analysis

Outlier detection and removal from experimental data (excluding omics data) were performed via the boxplot method based on the interquartile range (IQR). Following Tukey's criterion, data points less than Q1 – 1.5 × IQR or greater than Q3 + 1.5 × IQR were identified as outliers and subsequently removed. The cleaned data were first tested for normality via the Shapiro-Wilk test and for homogeneity of variance via Bartlett's test. After confirming that the data met the assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed. The mathematical model for the one-way ANOVA is:

Where Yij is the observation from the j-th experimental unit in the i-th treatment group; μ is the overall mean across all groups; Ti is the deviation of the i-th treatment mean from the overall mean; and eij is the random error term, assumed to follow a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance s2.

Duncan's multiple range test is conducted to determine the significance of differences among treatment groups, with different superscript letters indicating statistically significant differences (P < 0.05). Values in the tables are presented as means with their corresponding between-group standard error of the mean (SEM). In addition, orthogonal polynomial contrasts were applied across the different dietary PI inclusion levels to assess dose-dependent trends. For selected variables, dose-response regression models were further fitted as follows:

Where Yi is the observed mean corresponding to the i-th level of PI inclusion; β0 is the intercept term, representing the theoretical expected value of the trait when PI inclusion is 0; β1 is the linear regression coefficient, describing the linear trend in the trait per unit increase in PI level; β2 is the quadratic regression coefficient, capturing the curvature of the dose–response relationship and thus characterizing potential nonlinear (e.g. increase-decrease) biological responses; PIi is the actual PI inclusion level (% dry matter) for the i-th treatment; and εi is the random error term, assumed to follow a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance s2.

All data processing and statistical analyses were performed using RStudio (version 2024.09.0+375).

Results

Growth performance, ovarian and hepatopancreatic development, and histology

Phenotypic analysis of ovarian development revealed that PI supplementation significantly affected both the GSI and HSI (Table 3). Compared with the control group, the 0.2%, 0.6%, and 0.8% PI groups showed significantly higher GSI, with the highest value observed in the 0.6% PI group (P < 0.001). In contrast, the HSI was significantly lower in the 0.6% PI group than in the other dietary groups (P = 0.030), whereas no significant differences were observed among the 0.0, 0.2%, 0.4%, and 0.8% PI groups (P > 0.05). Ovarian developmental progression varied among dietary PI treatments. The control group generally showed slower ovarian progression, whereas the 0.6% PI group exhibited the shortest durations for stages I–III. Among the recorded stages, stage III duration differed significantly among treatments, with a shorter duration in the 0.4% and 0.6% PI groups than in the 0.0 and 0.2% PI groups (P = 0.007).

To thoroughly analyze the interaction patterns of DEGs at the protein level and further explore their potential regulatory mechanisms, a PPI network was constructed. This network was established via the STRING database (version 12.0) with Pacific white shrimp (P. vannamei, taxon ID: 6689) as the background organism. Interactions with a combined score ≥ 400 were selected to ensure medium confidence reliability. The resulting PPI network incorporated 78 DEGs with 158 interaction relationships, revealing a complex and tightly interconnected structure. Node attributes were integrated with regulatory direction (up/downregulation), log2 (fold change) values, and adjusted P-value. Topological parameters, including degree, betweenness centrality, and closeness centrality, were calculated to identify key regulatory genes. On the basis of the degree metric, the top 10% highly connected genes were selected, and 11 potential hub genes were identified: LOC113822899 (CYP 9e2), LOC113821695 (nuclear receptor subfamily 4 group A member 2), LOC113818649 (cAMP-dependent protein kinase type II regulatory subunit), LOC113820574 (ATP-citrate synthase), LOC113815940 (fatty acid synthase), LOC113823591 (NADH dehydrogenase ubiquinone), LOC113822901 (sarcosine dehydrogenase), LOC113803122 (beta-alanyl-dopamine/carcinine hydrolase), LOC113804373 (ornithine aminotransferase precursor), and LOC113821163 (regulator of G protein signaling family member locomotion defects) and LOC113799962 (Fig. 4B).

Gene Ontology enrichment analysis was conducted separately for upregulated and downregulated differentially expressed genes, with the top ten GO terms selected for each direction (P < 0.05; Fig. 4C). The upregulated genes were enriched predominantly in GO terms such as fatty acid biosynthetic process, lipid transport, lipid transporter activity, and ATPase-coupled transmembrane transporter activity. Additionally, the upregulated genes were significantly enriched in RNA polymerase II transcription regulatory region sequence-specific DNA binding and iron ion transport. Enrichment analysis of the downregulated genes revealed significant GO terms such as transport, transmembrane transport, lipid binding, and mitochondrial electron transport, cytochrome c to oxygen. Notably, the pathways lipid binding and lipid transport were also significantly enriched among the downregulated genes.

To further elucidate the metabolic and signaling pathways associated with the DEGs, KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed separately for the upregulated and downregulated DEGs. After excluding pathways related to human diseases, the top ten most significantly enriched pathways were selected for visualization (P < 0.05; Fig. 4D). The upregulated genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to signal transduction and cellular responses, including efferocytosis, transforming growth factor (TGF)-beta signaling pathway, glucagon signaling pathway, bile secretion, and alanine, aspartate and glutamate metabolism. Additionally, several regulatory pathways, such as the notch signaling pathway, polycomb repressive complex, glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis-heparan sulfate/heparin, cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, and nitrogen metabolism, were significantly enriched. The downregulated genes were enriched primarily in core metabolic pathways, particularly those involved in lipid synthesis and amino acid metabolism, specifically one carbon pool by folate, biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids, fatty acid elongation, tyrosine metabolism, glycerophospholipid metabolism, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, cysteine and methionine metabolism, and arginine and proline metabolism. Furthermore, drug metabolism-other enzymes and ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters were also significantly enriched.

Discussion

Phosphatidylinositol, an essential polar lipid, is involved in multiple cell signal transduction pathways and plays a crucial role in regulating biological growth and development. This study systematically investigated the effects of different PI concentrations on ovarian development in P. vannamei and the potential underlying molecular mechanisms. The results showed that appropriate PI supplementation (particularly at 0.6%) was associated with promoted ovarian development in P. vannamei, accelerated oocyte maturation processes, and markedly increased the GSI. This promoting effect was closely associated with PI-regulated changes in lipid metabolism, transport, hormone synthesis, and multiple signaling pathways.

In this study, shrimp in the 0.6% PI group exhibited the highest GSI, largest mean oocyte area, and fastest ovarian development rate, suggesting that appropriate PI supplementation was associated with enhanced ovarian development. Histological analysis revealed that the oocytes in the 0.6% group had larger volumes, greater accumulation of vitellogenin, and more intact cortical rod structures, and their formation marked the entry of the oocytes into the maturation phase (Khayat et al., 2001; Kruevaisayawan et al., 2010). Studies by Li et al. (2025) and Preechaphol et al. (2010) demonstrated that the integrity of cortical rod structures is closely related to the quality of shrimp oocytes and serves as an important indicator for evaluating oocyte maturity. In the present study, the time required for ovarian development was significantly shorter in the 0.6% group, particularly during early developmental stages (stages I–III), with each stage shortened by nearly two days. These findings suggest that PI may promote oocyte development through facilitation of the vitellogenesis process. Concurrently, significant lipid droplet accumulation was observed in the hepatopancreas, whereas the HSI decreased, suggesting that PI supplementation was associated with enhanced lipid deposition and potential mobilization toward the ovary. Research on the Mud crab (Scylla paramamosain) (Chen et al., 2023) revealed that lipid droplet accumulation in the hepatopancreas is positively correlated with lipid synthesis and secretion capacity, with these synthesized lipids primarily supporting ovarian development. A similar study on P. vannamei (Liang et al., 2024) has also demonstrated that appropriate lipid accumulation helps provide sufficient energy and structural materials for gonadal development, whereas excess lipid accumulation or lipid deficiency may inhibit ovarian development. It should be noted that the present experiment was conducted under a relatively intensive management regime, characterized by frequent feeding and high daily water exchange, to maintain stable water quality and minimize confounding environmental effects. Such optimized conditions may attenuate stress-related metabolic variation and thereby highlight diet-induced metabolic and endocrine responses. However, they may also limit direct extrapolation to less intensively managed commercial systems.

Crustacean ovarian development requires substantial accumulation of nutrients, including proteins, lipids, and cholesterol (Feng et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023; Ma et al., 2024; Zhu et al., 2024). This study reveals that PI elicits pronounced tissue-specific responses. A pattern of hepatopancreatic sterol depletion and neutral lipid mobilization, coupled with preferential ovarian cholesterol enrichment, suggests that substrates are routed toward steroidogenesis and membrane-domain biogenesis rather than mere bulk accumulation. Given the substantial metabolic demands for protein synthesis and steroidogenesis during ovarian development, these biochemical alterations provide direct biochemical evidence supporting the observed phenotypic manifestations. Research has confirmed that nutrient accumulation underpins oocyte development, with proteins providing raw materials for vitellogenin synthesis, whereas cholesterol serves as a precursor for estrogen and progesterone synthesis (Kumar et al., 2018; Ma et al., 2024; Wolin et al., 1973). Other studies have also demonstrated that cholesterol is an important precursor for estradiol and progesterone synthesis and that its content directly affects the ovarian development process in crustaceans (Guo et al., 2022). The present results revealed elevated expression of osbp, which regulates cholesterol and sterol transport (Olkkonen et al., 2006). Additionally, increased sphingolipid metabolism may contribute to this process. Concomitantly, the HSI declined significantly, while the relative area of lipid droplets increased, indicating effective mobilization of hepatopancreatic reserves and their circulatory delivery to the ovary. Together with serum lipoprotein concentrations, these observations support a transport dynamic characterized by maintained secretory output and accelerated receptor-mediated clearance at the ovarian sink, rather than simple intravascular accumulation. Notably, all experimental diets contained a fixed and relatively high cholesterol level (1.65%) to avoid sterol limitation. Under this standardized background, PI supplementation did not uniformly increase tissue cholesterol; rather, it induced a tissue-specific redistribution pattern. Total cholesterol in the hepatopancreas was significantly reduced in PI-supplemented groups, whereas ovarian cholesterol peaked at 0.6%. This pattern suggests that PI may enhance the mobilization and transport of cholesterol from storage tissues to the ovary, where it can be utilized for steroid hormone biosynthesis. Such a redistribution mechanism may partly explain the concurrent elevation of estradiol and PROG observed at the optimal PI level. Future studies manipulating both PI and dietary cholesterol levels would be required to further elucidate their interactive effects on lipid trafficking and reproductive endocrinology. Given that MTP is pivotal to the assembly and secretion of triacylglycerol-rich lipoproteins, in concert with the upregulation of osbp and oil red O-demonstrated accumulation of hepatopancreatic lipid droplets, it was that under enhanced MTP-mediated assembly capacity, sterol and lipid fluxes are rapidly exported for peripheral utilization (Olkkonen et al., 2006; White et al., 1998). Within the ovary, the preferential enrichment of cholesterol correlates with synchronous increases in E2 and PROG, suggesting that cholesterol serves as the rate-limiting substrate and a CYP-catalyzed cascade driving an upward shift in steroidogenic flux (Thongbuakaew et al., 2016). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that the PI establishes a metabolic foundation for accelerated ovarian development by enhancing nutrient accumulation and transport mechanisms.

To further substantiate this mechanistic pathway, transcriptomic analysis revealed widespread upregulation of genes associated with fatty acid biosynthesis and lipid transport in the 0.6% treatment group. This transcriptional response correlated with the significantly elevated lipid metabolites observed in the metabolomic profile, including D-sphingosine, stearic acid, palmitic acid, and other lipid species, thereby forming a comprehensive molecular framework linking gene expression to metabolic outcomes. During crustacean ovarian development, fatty acids not only constitute components of cell membranes but also serve as energy reserves and precursors for signaling molecules (Chansela et al., 2012). Research has demonstrated that during ovarian development in S. paramamosain, genes related to fatty acid synthesis and lipid transport are significantly upregulated, promoting lipid accumulation in oocytes (Chen et al., 2023). The upregulation of fatty acids, particularly long-chain saturated fatty acids (such as stearic acid and palmitic acid), is closely associated with ovarian development. These fatty acids not only constitute essential components of cell membranes but also participate in energy storage and the synthesis of signaling molecules (Liang et al., 2024; Zeng et al., 2023). Wouters et al. (2001b), in their study on female wild P. vannamei at different sexual maturation stages, demonstrated that the serum concentrations of specific fatty acids fluctuated significantly across ovarian developmental stages. Notably, saturated fatty acids (SFAs), particularly palmitic acid (C16:0), tend to increase during late vitellogenesis. These findings suggest that these fatty acids play crucial roles in the vitellogenin formation process. Similarly, Ravid et al. (1999), in their investigation of green tiger prawn (Penaeus semisulcatus), reported significant changes in lipid accumulation patterns during ovarian development. Stearic acid and palmitic acid, as predominant saturated fatty acids, potentially participate in the synthesis and transport of vitellogenin precursors.

The upregulation of D-sphingosine, a key intermediate in sphingolipid metabolism, in the 0.6% group reflects the active state of sphingolipid metabolism. Sphingolipids, a class of structurally complex bioactive lipids, not only serve as essential components of cell membranes but also participate in cell signal transduction and cell fate determination (Merrill et al., 2001). Research by Hannun and Obeid (2008) demonstrated that sphingolipid metabolites (such as ceramides and sphingosine-1-phosphate [S1P]) function as intracellular second messengers, regulating critical biological processes, including cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. In crustacean reproductive systems, the dynamic equilibrium of sphingolipid metabolism plays a crucial regulatory role in ovarian development and oocyte maturation. Studies have shown that sphingolipid metabolic intermediates such as S1P are closely involved in the promotion of oocyte meiotic resumption and embryonic developmental potential through the activation of the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. For example, in mammalian models, S1P releases oocytes from meiotic arrest by binding to G protein-coupled receptors (S1PRs) or directly regulating calcium ion release (Yuan et al., 2022), and similar regulatory mechanisms may also operate in crustaceans. Furthermore, the metabolic balance between ceramide (Cer) and S1P is critical for cell fate determination, as imbalances can lead to oocyte mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative stress, thereby reducing embryonic survival rates (Guo et al., 2013; Jamil and Cowart, 2023). During the ovarian maturation cycle of P. vannamei, sphingolipid metabolites such as glucosylceramide (GlcCer) and sphingomyelin (SM) accumulate significantly in the late oocyte stage, and their concentration changes are positively correlated with yolk deposition efficiency (Liang et al., 2022). Transcriptome analysis of the Chinese mitten crab (Eriocheir sinensis) revealed synchronized upregulation of sphingolipid metabolism-related genes with lipid transport- and vg synthesis-related genes in the hepatopancreas, suggesting that sphingolipids may coordinate energy reserves and embryonic development by regulating vg synthesis and transport (Chen et al., 2019).

Notably, the regulation of the lipoprotein transport system by PI may be related to the physiological properties of phospholipids. As an amphipathic molecule, PI can enhance the emulsification properties of lipids, promote lipid solubility in aqueous phases, and facilitate lipid transport in body fluids (Zhang et al., 2022). Additionally, PI may influence lipid transport efficiency through the modulation of LPL activity. Contrary to prior reports describing a global elevation of circulating lipoproteins, we observed no significant differences in VLDL across groups, whereas HDL-C and LDL-C declined in most supplementation groups. This pattern is better explained by a “maintained secretion-accelerated receptor-side clearance” model: at the ovarian interface, the LDLR family and SR-BI mediate endocytosis and selective uptake, thereby enhancing the clearance efficiency of LDL/HDL-borne lipid flux and yielding lower apparent circulating concentrations (Acton et al., 1996; Brown and Goldstein, 1986). Concomitantly, phospholipid enrichment, potentially by potentiating the cooperative action of LPL, may accelerate TG hydrolysis and lipoprotein particle remodeling, thereby favoring peripheral tissue utilization and clearance of lipid-laden particles, rather than necessarily increasing their circulating levels (Jiang et al., 2023; Nishitsuji et al., 2010).

This cascade of metabolic and transport processes is intimately linked with endocrine regulation. The hormonal system plays a pivotal regulatory role in crustacean ovarian development (Jayasankar et al., 2020). This study shows that E2 increased at 0.6% and 0.8%, PROG rose across all supplementation groups, and MIH declined at 0.2% and 0.6%, whereas MF and GIH exhibited no significant differences among groups. Collectively, these results indicate that PI promotes ovarian maturation mainly through enhancing steroidogenic throughput and stage-specific relief of inhibitory tone along the molting axis, rather than via generalized downregulation of GIH or upregulation of MF (Nagaraju, 2011; Nakatsuji et al., 2009). Within an inter-organ reallocation characterized by hepatopancreatic sterol depletion and ovarian cholesterol accretion, substrate availability in the ovarian compartment increases and, together with upregulation of CYP enzymes and nuclear receptor hubs, drives E2/PROG biosynthesis (Ikonen, 2008; Thongbuakaew et al., 2016). Integrating transcriptomic and metabolomic evidence, PI may additionally potentiate vitellogenin biosynthesis and the oocyte maturation program by acting in concert with the PI3K/Akt-mTOR axis and cell-cycle regulators (e.g., APC7, CKIα) (Kalous et al., 2023; Li et al., 2021).

Estradiol, which functions as a critical gonadotropic hormone, stimulates vitellogenin gene expression and promotes the synthesis of vitellogenin precursors in the hepatopancreas (Coccia et al., 2010; Liu et al., 2025). Progesterone predominantly participates in the processes of vitellogenesis (Medesani et al., 2015) and ultimately oocyte maturation (Subramoniam, 2011). Methyl farnesoate in crustaceans not only regulates molting but also contributes to the modulation of vitellogenin synthesis (Su et al., 2020). Notably, the stimulatory effect of PI on hormone synthesis appears to be closely linked to its regulatory influence on cholesterol metabolism. Steroid hormones in crustaceans (such as estradiol and progesterone) are synthesized using cholesterol as a precursor through catalysis by CYP enzyme cascades (Thongbuakaew et al., 2016). Concurrently, PPI network analysis revealed LOC113822899 (encoding a CYP) (Thongbuakaew et al., 2016) and LOC113821695 (encoding a nuclear receptor transcription factor) (Gong et al., 2016; Subramoniam, 2017) as pivotal hub genes that directly participate in the biotransformation of cholesterol to sex hormones. Research has demonstrated that cholesterol transport to the gonads constitutes the rate-limiting step in sex steroid synthesis (Manna et al., 2016; Thongbuakaew et al., 2016). This study indicates that PI may enhance ovarian cholesterol delivery by promoting the synthesis and remodeling of circulating lipoprotein classes (HDL-C, LDL-C, and VLDL), thereby increasing the capacity for sex-steroid biosynthesis. While MIH primarily regulates the molting process, it has also been shown to participate in reproductive regulation (Nakatsuji et al., 2009; Zhou et al., 2019).

Based on established metabolic and hormonal regulatory networks, PI further promotes oocyte division and maturation through the activation of cell cycle regulation and critical signaling pathways. Gene expression analysis revealed that multiple key cell cycle regulatory factors, including apc7 and ckialpha, were significantly upregulated in the 0.6% group.

As a core component of the APC/C, the upregulation of APC7 may facilitate meiotic progression through APC/C-mediated regulation (Yu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2016). This molecular change manifests phenotypically in histological observations, where oocytes in the 0.6% group presented larger volumes, increased yolk protein accumulation, more intact cortical rod structures, and significantly shortened ovarian development times. The upregulation of CkIalpha further elucidates the signaling pathway mechanism by which PI promotes ovarian development. This kinase regulates cell cycle protein expression through modulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, thereby controlling ovarian cell proliferation and differentiation (Jiang, 2017; Mazzoldi et al., 2019). The Wnt signaling pathway is a conserved and evolutionarily ancient signaling pathway (Holstein, 2012), and Wnt may increase ecdysteroid production (Mykles, 2021) while acting synergistically with vg1 (Skromne and Stern, 2001). Moreover, the significant upregulation of mTOR signaling pathway-related genes indicates that PI activated mTOR-dependent protein synthesis and cell growth programs, which corresponds with the observed changes in L-valine levels via metabolomics analysis. Branched-chain amino acids such as valine not only serve as substrates for protein synthesis but also function as signaling molecules to activate mTOR pathways, promoting cell growth and proliferation (Monirujjaman and Ferdouse, 2014).

The pro-ovarian development effects of PI also involve fine-tuned regulation of gene expression and regulatory mechanisms. The significant upregulation of the expression of the spliceosome-associated protein CWC15 and Nop60 B highlighted the potential involvement of RNA processing and modification in this process. Nop60 B is involved in RNA pseudouridylation, which is crucial for maintaining RNA stability and translation efficiency (Garus and Autexier, 2021). In female Drosophila, Nop60 B expression peaks in adult ovaries, which may indicate that RNA modification has a conserved regulatory role in ovarian development (Riccardo et al., 2007). The changes in the expression of the trr gene resulting from the loss of PHDs support the possibility that PI regulates ovarian development through epigenetic mechanisms. This gene encodes a histone methyltransferase, participates in epigenetic regulation, and affects chromatin structure and gene expression. Histone modifications play key roles in global DNA demethylation and chromatin reprogramming in primordial germ cells (Sasaki and Matsui, 2008), and histone variants have specific functions in meiotic recombination (Kimmins and Sassone-Corsi, 2005). Phosphatidylinositols may regulate the expression of specific gene sets by affecting histone modification patterns, thereby optimizing gene expression programs related to ovarian development.

This study further observed significant upregulation of genes associated with the TGF-β and Notch signaling pathways in the 0.6% PI group. These pathways are known to play crucial roles in ovarian development. In mammals, the TGF-β signaling pathway contributes to follicular development and oocyte maturation through the regulation of cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. The Notch signaling pathway plays a pivotal role in regulating follicular granulosa cell differentiation and oocyte-follicle interactions (He et al., 2025; Patton et al., 2021). Liu et al. (2021) reported that activation of the TGF-β signaling pathway in P. vannamei promotes vitellogenin synthesis and ovarian development. Additionally, Liu et al. (2024a) reported the essential role of the Notch signaling pathway in the ovarian development of olive flounder (Paralichthys olivaceus). Notably, the present study found significant upregulation of genes associated with multiple amino acid metabolic pathways (such as alanine, aspartate, and glutamate metabolism) in the 0.6% PI group, whereas genes related to glycine, serine, and threonine metabolism were significantly downregulated. Amino acid metabolism is intimately linked with crustacean ovarian development, providing substrates for vitellogenin synthesis and participating in energy metabolism and signal transduction (Li et al., 2023). Research has demonstrated that metabolic patterns of different amino acids vary across the stages of crustacean ovarian development, reflecting the changing requirements for specific amino acids during this developmental process (Feng et al., 2022, 2023).

Metabolomic analysis revealed distinct metabolic reprogramming patterns during PI-promoted ovarian development. Nucleoside metabolites, including guanine, hypoxanthine, guanosine, and inosine, were significantly downregulated in the 0.6% group, suggesting directional alterations in purine metabolism. Purine metabolism provides fundamental substrates for nucleic acid synthesis, energy metabolism, and signal transduction, playing crucial roles in multiple biological processes (Chua and Fraser, 2020). These metabolic changes may reflect a metabolic reprogramming strategy that prioritizes energy allocation from maintenance metabolism to ovarian developmental processes, which would be adapted to the physiological demands of dramatically increased nucleic acid and protein synthesis rates during ovarian development.

Changes in vitamin metabolism may further support this metabolic reprogramming mechanism. The downregulation of pantothenic acid (vitamin B5) and riboflavin (vitamin B2) levels may reflect dramatically increased demands for vitamin B metabolism during late-stage ovarian development, potentially resulting in significant depletion of these vitamins in peripheral tissues. Pantothenic acid, as a precursor of coenzyme A, participates in key metabolic pathways, including fatty acid β-oxidation and synthesis (Combs and McClung, 2017), and its consumption is consistent with the active lipid metabolism observed in this study. Riboflavin, which serves as a precursor of flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD) and flavin mononucleotide (FMN), participates in oxidation-reduction reactions and energy metabolism (Fieschi et al., 1995; Liu et al., 2024b), and changes in riboflavin levels may reflect the dynamic regulation of redox balance during ovarian development. Studies using the fruit fly (Drosophila melanogaster) as a model have shown that B vitamin deficiency significantly shortened the lifespan of wild-type females and impaired reproductive function, involving energy metabolic reorganization during reproductive development (Yu et al., 2023).

Through the integration of transcriptomic and metabolomic datasets, this investigation suggests three pivotal pathways—sphingolipid metabolism, purine metabolism, and fatty acid biosynthesis—that may constitute the fundamental mechanisms by which the PI facilitates ovarian development. Furthermore, via WCNA in conjunction with GSI correlation analysis, three functional modules were identified as being intimately associated with ovarian development were identified: module 18, module 1, and module 11. Notably, module 1 was positively correlated with the GSI, suggesting that genes and metabolites within this module potentially enhance ovarian maturation; conversely, modules 18 and 11 were negatively correlated with the GSI, indicating their potential involvement in the negative regulation of ovarian developmental processes. This modular analytical approach has been previously validated in studies of various aquatic organisms as an efficacious method for illuminating the molecular regulatory networks underlying complex biological processes (Li et al., 2018). Subsequent refined screening identified 18 crucial genes and 17 metabolites significantly correlated with the GSI. These molecular entities may illustrate multiple potential pathways through which PI could modulate ovarian development but also provide novel perspectives for comprehending reproductive physiology in crustaceans.

Based on the above findings, it was suggested potential mechanisms by which PI may regulate ovarian development in P. vannamei. First, as a key component of cell membranes, PI enhances signal transduction efficiency by influencing membrane fluidity and the function of signaling receptors. Specifically, PI may promote oocyte growth and development through the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway. This hypothesis is consistent with the identification of multiple genes related to cell cycle regulation (such as apc7 and kohtalo). Second, PI affects the expression of ovary-specific genes by regulating transcription factor activity and epigenetic modifications. The identification of multiple transcription regulation-related genes (such as Lost PHDs of trr and transcription elongation regulator 1) supports this mechanism. Third, the PI provides material and energy support for rapid ovarian development by optimizing lipid, amino acid, and nucleotide metabolism. This is evidenced by the identified metabolites (such as stearic acid, L-valine, and guanine) and related metabolic enzymes. Finally, the PI may coordinate the endocrine regulation of ovarian development by influencing steroid hormone synthesis and signal transduction. This study systematically reveals the molecular network and metabolic pathways through which PI regulates ovarian development in P. vannamei for the first time. The 18 key genes and 17 metabolites identified not only provide a molecular basis for understanding the mechanisms by which PI promotes ovarian development but also offer new intervention targets for optimizing ovarian development in P. vannamei.

Conclusion

This study suggests that dietary supplementation with PI may significantly enhance ovarian development and maturation in P. vannamei, with the recommended optimal inclusion level ranging from 0.442% to 0.586% on a dry matter basis. Phosphatidylinositol supplementation was associated with promoted oocyte maturation, increased GSI, and accelerated ovarian development, which was accompanied by increased lipid accumulation and nutrient transport from the hepatopancreas to the ovaries. Concurrently, PI modulated reproductive hormone levels by upregulating the expression of stimulatory hormones (E2 and PROG) and downregulating the expression of inhibitory neuropeptides (MIH), which may facilitate vitellogenesis. Multiomics analysis suggested that PI may influence key molecular pathways—including fatty acid biosynthesis, sphingolipid metabolism, and purine metabolism—and identified regulatory hub genes and metabolites associated with lipid metabolism, cell cycle progression, and hormonal biosynthesis. Based on the above findings, a potential mechanism by which phosphatidylinositol may regulate ovarian development in P. vannamei is proposed (Fig. S1). In conclusion, these findings provide mechanistic insights into the role of the PI in modulating ovarian development, maturation, and offer a theoretical basis for optimizing broodstock nutritional strategies aimed at improving ovarian developmental status in shrimp aquaculture.
