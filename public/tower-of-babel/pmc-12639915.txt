Comparative meta-transcriptomic analysis of nasal and anal microbiota in respiratory syncytial virus mono and co-infected children
Authors: Iman Dandachi, Laila Alawdah, Waleed Aljabr
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory syncytial virus is among the most common viruses causing respiratory infections in children. It has been suggested that imbalance of the respiratory microbiota could be involved in the prognosis of RSV illness. The aim of this work is to explore dysbiosis of nasal/anal microbiota in children upon RSV infection, compared to controls.ResultsFifty-nine subjects were enrolled in this study and included: 23 controls, 17 infected only with RSV, and 19 RSV infected together with other respiratory viruses. Multi-infected subjects had non-statistically significant, higher rates of ICU admission (8 versus 3), oxygen supplementation (13 versus 9), and longer hospital stay (20 versus 15 mean days), compared to those single infected with RSV (P > 0.05). Meta-transcriptomic analysis revealed that the nasal microbiota of multi-infected RSV patients had significantly lower alpha richness compared to RSV mono-infected and control group. Principal component analysis revealed the distinct clustering of the nasal microbiome based on the infectious category (P < 0.05). Following the control of confounders i.e. age, gender, comorbidities and antimicrobial intake, Pseudomonas were significantly increased in RSV patients compared to controls. Lactobacillus, Bacteroides, and Staphylococcus were significantly decreased in multi-infected compared to the control subjects. For the anal microbiota, multi-RSV-infected patients had significantly lower alpha richness according to the observed indices, when compared only to the control group. RSV patients had both significantly lower abundance of Spirochaetes and Actinobacteria compared to the control group (P < 0.05). However, following the control of confounders, no significant difference was observed (P > 0.05).ConclusionOur study revealed that multi-infected RSV subjects revealed the highest difference in microbial composition of the nasal microbiota compared to single infected and control subjects. Whether the observed microbiome difference is related to increased disease severity is yet to be explored in future studies. Moreover, the potential use of Lactobacillus and Bacteroides as probiotics for the treatment and prevention of RSV infection should be explored in future research.Keywords: Meta-transcriptomics, RSV, Pediatrics, Microbiota

Introduction

Respiratory tract infections are among the most common causes of death globally. Among others, Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) has been regarded as a significant cause of respiratory illnesses that poses a major burden on health systems worldwide, due to its wide range of disease prognosis [1]. Globally, it has been estimated that in children aged between 0–5 years, in 2019, 33 million cases of RSV caused acute lower respiratory tract infections have been recorded, including 26,300 hospital death and 3.6 million hospitalizations [2]. The clinical manifestation of RSV in children ranges from an acute respiratory infection to a severe respiratory syndrome [3]. When infected with RSV, children with neuromuscular disease, tracheostomy, congenital airway abnormalities are at higher risk of developing severe complications [4]. Up to date, there is a lack of approved drug for the treatment of RSV infection, with this latter being only supportive [4]. For prophylaxis, it is only recently that an RSV vaccine has been approved by FDA, for use in children and elderlies [5]. Mechanisms underlying the wide variability of RSV infection’ clinical presentation are still incompletely understood; recent insights suggested that an imbalance in the airway microbiota might be involved [1, 6]. The complex interplay between RSV and members of the nasopharyngeal microbiota can have an impact on the disease severity, by modulating vulnerability to RSV infection, favoring bacterial superinfection as well as indirectly inducing subsequent recurrent asthma and wheezing in later childhood [10]. The relation between viral infection and microbiota imbalance is not restricted to the one of the respiratory tract. Indeed, studies have shown that between the respiratory and the gastrointestinal tract microbiomes, a systemic microbial crosstalk exists, through immune and circulatory responses [7]. Targeting the airway and gut microbiota imbalance in response to the viral infection through probiotics and prebiotics administration, is one way suggested by scientists as a feasible intervention to reduce the burden of respiratory viruses [8], prevent lung damage [9], as well as to improve respiratory health [10]. Several reports worldwide have explored differences in airway and to a lesser extent in gut microbiota between RSV infected and control subjects; nevertheless, scarce studies compared mono infection with RSV to RSV co-infection with other viruses, in addition to control subjects. The aim of this work is thus to explore differences, in the microbial composition of the nasal and anal microbiomes, between single-infected, co-infected RSV and control subjects, through meta-transcriptomic-next-generation sequencing approach.

Methodology details

Samples and data collection

Fifty-nine nasal and fifty anal swab samples were collected from admitted patients at the pediatric hospital of KFMC during 2023–2024, and were stored at −80 °C for later use. These included twenty-three controls and thirty-six RSV confirmed patients (< 18 years). Of the thirty-six RSV infected subjects, 17 were infected only with RSV “single infection group”, and 19 were infected with RSV together with other respiratory viruses as “multi-infection group”. Control subjects are defined as patients who were hospitalized for a non-respiratory and a non-gastrointestinal infection, are non-cancerous, not admitted to any intensive care unit (ICU) and don’t have any immunocompromised underlying condition. RSV subjects are defined as patients with a positive RSV test and who are non-cancerous and immunocompetent.

For patient enrollment, the attended physician at the Pediatric hospital notifies the research team once an RSV case has been admitted to the hospital. Following the signed informed consent from the subject guardians, fresh nasal and anal swab samples were collected using the virus collection and transport kit (CITOTEST) containing the viral transport media (VTM). Thereafter samples were directly taken on ice to the research laboratory and stored at −80 °C to be processed i.e. subjected to RNA extraction, within 24 h. Moreover, accordingly, the clinical and demographic data of included subjects were collected. These included: age, gender, underlying comorbidities, viral respiratory tests, ICU admission, length of hospital stay, oxygen supplementation, symptoms and antimicrobials. For instance, in our hospital, respiratory samples are tested by the multiplex real-time polymerase chain reaction using the Biofire Film Array instrument and the multiplex respiratory panel (Biomerieux diagnostics). This respiratory panel identifies the most common fourteen respiratory pathogens and include: coronaviruses HKU1, 229E, OC43, NL63, adenovirus, human rhinovirus/enterovirus, human metapneumovirus, Influenza A and B viruses, parainfluenza virus 1–4, human bocavirus, as well as RSV A and B. It is based on this test that our RSV subjects were divided into single infection group and multi infection groups. Ages of included pediatrics were categorized as infancy (0–12 months), toddler (13 months – 2 years), early childhood (3–5 years), and middle childhood (6–11 years) [11].

RNA extraction and DNase treatment

From all samples, RNA was extracted and subjected to Dnase treatment using the QIAamp Viral RNA Mini Kit (Qiagen), and the TURBO DNA-free Kit (Thermofisher Scientific), respectively. Following vortexing of the sample, the extraction was conducted using 140 µl of the transport media in which the swab was immersed, and with a final elution volume of 60 µl. Extracted RNA was then quantified using the Qubit RNA High Sensitivity, Broad Range Assay kit (32,852; Invitrogen). RNA extracts with concentrations “out of range” in the qubit were considered not eligible for sequencing and were not progressed to the next step.

Sequence-independent, single-primer amplification (SISPA) and sequencing

For the meta-transcriptomic identification of viral and bacterial transcripts, SISPA was performed [12]. Amplified SISPA products were then quantified using the Qubit double-stranded DNA high-sensitivity assay kit (Q32851; Invitrogen), and purified in a 1:1 ratio using the AMPure XP beads (A63882; Beckman Coulter). The library was then prepared according to the “sequencing by ligation” protocol with native barcodes for multiplexing (SQK-LSK109; Oxford Nanopore Technologies) and added to a R9.4.1 flow cell that was connected to MInIT device, where the sequencing was initiated via MinKNOW.

Bioinformatic analysis

Obtained Fast5 files were based-called using Guppy (ONT-guppy-cpu-4.4.1-win64). Obtained reads were first mapped to the human Genome assembly “GRCh38.p14” using minimap2, for the removal of host reads. Meta-transcriptomic analysis was then performed using Kraken2 [13]. Alpha diversity was assessed using: Chao1 and observed indices for richness and Shannon and Simpson indices for richness and evenness (diversity) of microbial taxa. For the assessment of beta diversity, principal coordinate analysis (PCOA) using the Bray Curtis index, based on permutational multivariate analysis of variance was performed [14]. Taxa abundance differences comparison among different categories was conducted using linear discriminant analysis (LEFSe), and the Wilcoxon rank sum test. Multiple linear regression analysis to find significantly different taxa following the control of confounders: age, gender, presence of underlying comorbidities, and antimicrobial intake was also performed.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients across the three study groups. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables, such as length of hospital stay, were summarized using mean and standard deviation using Microsoft Excel. For comparisons among the three groups, the Kruskal Wallis test was performed using SRplot [15]. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

General characteristics of the studied population

In our study, equal gender distribution, presence of underlying comorbidities, and antibiotic intake was observed among the three categories. Only control subjects were significantly older than RSV infected patients, and had significantly lower mean of length of hospital stay (P < 0.05). Patients co-infected with other respiratory viruses had a non-statistically significant longer hospital stays, higher rate of ICU admission and oxygen supplementation (Table 1). Only a six-month-old female patient co-infected with rhinovirus have died from hypercapnic respiratory failure secondary to central hypoventilation. Moreover, only one RSV single-infected male patient took “ribavirin”.

Nasal and anal microbial communities’ richness and diversity.

For nasal samples, a total of 9,419,710 reads (average 159,656) with mean quality phred score of 10 were obtained. Control and single-infected patients had significantly higher alpha richness compared to co-infected patients based on the chaos and observed indices. The alpha diversity was however, non-significantly different between all three categories based on the Shannon and Simpson indices (Fig. 1A). PCOA analysis revealed that nasal microbial communities formed separate clusters (P < 0.05) based on patients’ category but not on the level of age, gender, presence of comorbidities or antimicrobials uptake (Fig. 2A). For anal samples, a total of 36,133,669 reads (average 708,503) were obtained with mean quality phred score of 10. No significant differences in alpha richness/diversity was observed between all groups. This is only except for the observed index, where co-infected RSV patients had significantly lower alpha richness compared to control patients (Fig. 1B). Moreover, the three microbial communities didn’t cluster at any level based on the PCOA analysis (Fig. 2B).

Alpha richness of the anal microbiota was significantly lower in the multi-infected group only compared to the controls as per observed index

Nasal and anal microbial communities’ composition

In the nasal microbiome, the top detected phyla were Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes. Co-infected RSV patients had significantly lower abundance of Actinobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Fusobacteria and Proteobacteria, and significantly higher abundance of Firmicutes (P < 0.05), compared to single-infected patients. Compared to control subjects, co-infected patients showed significantly lower abundance of Firmicutes, and Fusobacteria (P < 0.05). Single-infected patients on the other hand, had significantly lower abundance of Firmicutes and higher abundance of Fusobacteria compared to control subjects (P < 0.05). The top families detected were Corynebacteriaceae, Staphylococcaceae, and Streptococcaceae. On the other hand, the top genera detected were Acinetobacter, Corynebacterium, Moraxella, Staphylococcus and Streptococcus (Fig. 3A). In the anal microbiome, Firmicutes followed by Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes were the top phyla detected. Mono and co-infected RSV patients had significantly lower abundance of Actinobacteria, and Spirochaetes compared to control subjects (P < 0.05). Co-infected patients had significantly lower abundance of Proteobacteria compared to single-infected. Bacteroidaceae, and Enterobacteriaceae, were among the top families detected; whereas the top genera detected were Bacteroides, Escherichia, and Klebsiella (Fig. 3B).

Composition of the (A) nasal and (B) anal microbiome at the phylum, family and genus levels. The figure shows the top abundant taxa in the nasal and anal microbiome for the three subjects categories of this study

Differential abundance of taxa among the three patients’ categories

Wilcoxon rank test revealed that the nasal microbiome of RSV single-infected patients, at the family level, had significantly lower relative abundance of Fusobacteriaceae and Leptotrichiaceae, but increased relative abundance of Pseudomonadaceae, and Enterobacteriaceae compared to control subjects. Similarly, at the genera level, these subjects had a significant increase in Pseudomonas, Citrobacter, and Stenotrophomonas and a significant decrease in Leptotrichia, Fusobacterium and Streptococcus, respectively (Fig. 4). When co-infected RSV patients were compared to single-infected or control subjects, significant differences were seen at high numbers of family and genera (P < 0.05). For the nasal microbiome, significant abundance differences were seen at the level of family and genera, between all groups (P < 0.05).

Taxonomic differences at the (A) family and (B) genera levels between single-infected and control subjects. Red color signifies significant increased abundance. Blue color signifies significant decreased abundance

Microbial biomarkers in all three categories

In the nasal microbiome, when single-infected patients were compared to control subjects, Lefse analysis revealed no specific family or genus that is differentially abundant in the infected group. However, when multi-infected RSV patients were compared to control subjects, the differential expression of Enterobacteriaceae, Pseudomonadaceae, and Escherichia, Pseudomonas at the family and genera level, respectively was observed. On the other hand, Mycobactericeae, Bulkholdericeae, families were differentially abundant in multi-infected patients compared to those single-infected; whereas at the genus level, Mycobacterium, and Moraxella were observed (Fig. 5). In the anal microbiome, when compared to control subjects, single-infected patients didn’t show differentially abundant families or genera, nor significant differences were observed versus multi-infected subjects. Multi-infected patient, on the other hand, showed only differential abundance in the Pasteurellaceae and Listeriaceae families, as well as the Haemophilus genus, versus control subjects (Fig. 6).

Multiple linear regression analysis

After controlling for confounders including: age, gender, presence of comorbidities, and antimicrobial intake, multiple linear regression analysis revealed no significant difference between the three groups in the anal microbiome, but only significant differences were observed at the level of the nasal microbiome. For instance, at the phylum, family and genera levels, the biggest difference was observed when multi-infected patients were compared to control subjects and the least difference was recorded when single-infected were compared to control (Fig. 7).

Virome of the nasal and anal microbiome

Among the seventeen single-infected RSV patients, RSV reads were detected in eight nasal samples, and among the nineteen multi-infected subjects, RSV was detected in seven. Co-infecting viruses: human metapneumovirus, rhinovirus, and parainfluenza virus were detected in four samples. In anal samples, RSV was detected in the reads of four single-infected patients. In addition, the co-infecting enterovirus was detected in one sample.

Discussion

In Saudi Arabia, RSV is the most common virus detected in respiratory illnesses. In fact, Saudi Arabia is among the countries that reported the highest prevalence of RSV in infants less than 12 months of age in the MENA region [16]. In recent years, studies have revealed the pivotal role of the respiratory microbiota inside the human body [17]. In fact, studies have shown that upon infection, the respiratory microbiota is altered, leading to an increase in the relative abundance of opportunistic pathogens and consequently increased disease severity [18, 19]. Beside the respiratory microbiota, the gut microbiota during viral infection has gained increased interest in the last years. It has been found that intestinal dysbiosis can modulate immune responses, and affect the mechanisms of viral entry, exacerbating thus the respiratory viral infection and increasing airways inflammation as well as disease severity [20, 21]. For instance, several gut microorganisms were found to be able to activate macrophages, B cells, as well as the Toll like receptors on T cells, dendritic and epithelial cells. Accordingly, this activation can modulate the production of antibodies, cytokines and chemokines that are key players in the clearance of the RSV virus [22, 23]. Analyzing therefore both the nasal and anal microbiota is essential in identifying the microbial perturbations that occur upon RSV infection and their possible effect on the disease severity and/or prognosis using both shotgun and functional metagenomic studies.

In the nasal microbiota, our study revealed a significantly lower alpha richness, only between multi-infected RSV patients and both single-infected and control subjects. Alpha diversity, on the other hand, was not significantly different between the three categories. These findings are in agreement with other studies that found that RSV-infected patients have decreased alpha richness compared to controls and none significant alpha diversity according to the Shannon index [24–26]. Interestingly, according to Ragopala et al., the higher alpha richness could be protective against severe disease of RSV, however this observation needs to be validated against a large number of samples [26]. Moreover, in this study, PCOA analysis using the Bray–Curtis dissimilarity index, revealed that the three microbiota were significantly different. In their study, Schippa et al., found also significant difference between RSV patients and controls using the same analysis [25]. Grier et al., found also similar results and showed also that the observed difference is greater between control and severe disease of RSV compared to mild illness [27]. In our study, multi-infected patients had higher rates of ICU admission, oxygen supplementation, as well as longer in-hospital stays, compared to single-infected subjects, however this difference was not statistically significant. Whether RSV co-infection with other respiratory viruses negatively affect disease prognosis is not yet to be explored in the literature. While some studies suggested the less likelihood of developing severe disease [28], others have suggested an increased severity [29–31]. More studies are needed to elucidate factors behind each observation and the mechanisms underlying it.

Following multiple linear regression analysis while controlling for confounders, our study revealed that at the phyla level, significant differences in abundance were observed only when multi-infected RSV subjects were compared to the other two groups. Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes were in a significantly lower abundance. Other phyla including Firmicutes and Fusobacteria, and Actinobacteria had significant lower abundance only when multi-infected were compared to control subjects. Differences in the phyla relative abundances between control and RSV infected patients was contradictory in the literature. While some studies found no significant differences at the phyla level, others have revealed some differences [25, 32, 33].

At the genus level, several studies in the literature reported the significant increase in Haemophilus, Moraxella, Streptococcus, Klebsiella and Pseudomonas in RSV patients and the significant decrease of Corynebacterium, Staphylococcus and Bacteroides compared to controls [7]. In our study, significant differences were also observed in these genera when RSV subjects were compared to control, however, following the controlling of confounders, only Pseumodomonas were significantly increased in single and multi-infected patients compared to control, and Lactobacillus, Bacteroides, and Staphylococcus were significantly decreased in multi-infected compared to the controls. Interestingly, in one study, it was found that the treatment of a 3 weeks old BALB/c mice with Lactobacillus rhamnosus CRL1505 resulted in significant reduction of tissue injuries and lung viral loads after challenge with RSV. CRL1505 strain’ ability to provide protective effect was attributed to its capacity to modulate differentially the respiratory antiviral immune response [8]. In another study, it was shown that nasal priming with Corynebacterium pseudodiphtheriticum 090104 boosted the respiratory innate immunity in infant mice; improving thus their resistance to primary RSV infection and secondary pneumococcal pneumonia [10]. For Staphylococcus, it has been reported that Staphylococcus epidermidis in the nasal cavity, is negatively associated with an increased risk of severe respiratory infection [34]. Indeed, it has been suggested that one of the novel therapeutic and prophylactic approaches for these infections is the direct supplementation of S. epidermidis into the nasal cavity [34]. The development and administration of probiotics is increasingly gaining interest as “adjuvant treatments” that control nasal dysbiosis and accordingly improve respiratory infections prognosis. Indeed, it has been found that the administration of synbiotics (probiotic and prebiotics) decreased significantly the incidence of respiratory infections in newborns compared to the placebo administered ones [35, 36]. More specifically, in their study, Ji et al. revealed that in neonatal mice, probiotics protected against infection with RSV, constituting thus a promising candidate for the prevention and treatment of RSV infection, that is worth more future research and development [37].

For the anal microbiota, our study showed no significant differences in alpha/beta diversity, nor in alpha richness between the groups. This is except only for the observed richness index that revealed a significantly lower alpha richness in multi-infected RSV patients compared to control subjects. In the literature, one study also found no significant difference in alpha diversity between control and RSV subjects [38]. Another study conducted by Russel et al., found that compared to RSV infected patients, healthy controls had similar to our results, significantly higher alpha richness [39]. As for the beta diversity, in contrast to our findings, both Harding and Russel et al., reported that using the Bray–Curtis and Unifrac distances dissimilarity indices, RSV and healthy patients, have significantly an overall different gut microbial composition [38, 39]. For instance, looking at specifically different taxonomic levels in the gust microbiota of RSV patients, studies in the literature were controversial, with no consistent results on which taxa had significant increase or decrease in abundance, between RSV patients and controls [7]. In our study, following the control of confounders using the multiple linear regression analysis, no significant difference at any taxa level was observed between the three categories of subjects. In their review, Yagi et al., suggested that given the low number of studies that explored the gut microbiome composition upon RSV infection, no definitive conclusion can be made [7].

It should be mentioned that out of thirty-six RSV patients included in this study, RSV reads were detected only in 15 samples (41.6%). The SISPA protocol used in this study has been previously proven to be successful in the identification of various viral pathogens including Lassa, Dengue and Chikungunya viruses [40, 41]. The observed discrepancy in our findings could be attributed to the large bacterial background obtained upon sequencing that impeded the sequencing of the virus to a sufficient reading coverage and depth. Indeed, in their study, Alrezaihi et al., found that upon modifying the SISPA protocol by the addition of SARS-CoV-2 leader sequences, the whole genome of the virus was successfully amplified and sequenced, and the lineages were identified. This is while allowing simultaneously, the effective exploration of the bacterial nasopharyngeal microbiome [42].

Our study has several limitations. The first one is the small sample size within each category of subjects, which might limit the statistical power to detect significant associations, especially the ones related to clinical outcomes such as ICU admission, need for oxygen supplementation and length of hospital stay that were increased in our findings in RSV co-infected group compared to the mono-infected ones, but with no statistical significance. The relatively small sample size could be in part attributed to challenges in subjects’ recruitment. In some cases, the subject guardians chose not to give consent and participate in the study, in other cases the child himself decline to provide the sample, despite the acceptance and obtained informed consent from his guardians. These recruitment challenges contributed to the small cohort and hindered the collection of a larger sample size. The second limitation of this study relies in its single center design. As such our findings might not be generalizable to other different populations with different genetic backgrounds, diets and environmental exposures. Nevertheless, it is worth mentioning that King Fahad Medical City is a tertiary and referral hospital, and one of the largest medical complexes in the middle eastern region that serves a wide and diverse population of the Riyadh city. As such, our findings may still offer valuable insights that are relevant to similar population within our area.

Taken together, our study revealed significant differences in microbial composition in the nasal but not in the anal microbiota, upon RSV infection; this is especially in the co-presence of other respiratory viruses. Need of oxygen supplementation, ICU admission and longer hospital stays, were higher in multi-infected RSV patients compared to single-infected, with no significant statistical difference. Accordingly, whether the altered nasal microbiota has effect or not on the disease severity upon co-infection with several respiratory viruses, should be explored in future studies, taking into account, a larger, multi-center cohort, in order to achieve sufficient power for clinical correlation. Moreover, in order to assess if the observed microbiome dysbiosis is a causative or a consequential factor for the severity of RSV infection, studies exploring the microbiome diversity at different time points “longitudinal sampling” during the disease, including the recovery stage, are warranted. Future studies should also aim to understand the metabolic pathways that are influenced by microbial dysbiosis, as well as unveiling the causal relationship between specific microbiota changes (e.g., Lactobacillus significant decrease in abundance) and immune response or disease severity, using functional metagenomics and animal models studies, respectively.
